Abstract
. A surge of interest has emerged in weakly supervised semantic segmentation due to its remarkable efficiency in recent years. Existing approaches based on transformers mainly focus on exploring the affinity matrix to boost CAMs with global relationships. While in this work, we first perform a scrupulous examination towards the impact of successive affinity matrices and discover that they possess an inclination toward sparsification as the network approaches convergence, hence disclosing a manifestation of over-smoothing. Besides, it has been observed that enhanced attention maps tend to evince a substantial amount of extraneous background noise in deeper layers. Drawing upon this, we posit a daring conjecture that the undisciplined over-smoothing phenomenon introduces a noteworthy quantity of semantically irrelevant background noise, causing performance degradation. To alleviate this issue, we propose a novel perspective that highlights the objects of interest by investigating the regions of the trait, thereby fostering an extensive comprehension of the successive affinity matrix. Consequently, we suggest an adaptive re-activation mechanism (AReAM) that alleviates the issue of incomplete attention within the object and the unbounded background noise. AReAM accomplishes this by supervising high-level attention with shallow affinity matrices, yielding promising results. Exhaustive experiments conducted on the commonly used dataset manifest that segmentation results can be greatly improved through our proposed AReAM, which imposes restrictions on each affinity matrix in deep layers to make it attentive to semantic regions.



1.
introduction

Figure 1
.
Visualization of attention map generation. The proposed AReAM enables the attention map to contract towards more semantic regions.

Semantic segmentation is a fundamental task in computer vision that aims to classify pixels in an image into their semantic classes. Although deep neural networks have achieved great success in approaching this task, they often require costly pixel-wise annotations due to the data-hungry nature. To alleviate this problem, weakly supervised semantic segmentation (WSSS)
(
Zhang et al. 2021b
)
is introduced to utilize annotations that are relatively easy to obtain, such as bounding boxes
(
Lee et al. 2021d
)
, scribbles
(
Lin et al. 2016
;
Su et al. 2022b
)
, points
(
Bearman et al. 2016
)
and image-level class labels
(
Ahn and Kwak 2018
)
. Image-level class labels are more accessible since they already exist in many large-scale datasets, but they only indicate the presence of categories, making it challenging to learn high-quality semantic segmentation models. Therefore, we focus on WSSS using image-level class labels in this paper.

Previous solutions of WSSS with image-level labels commonly generate Class Activation Maps (CAMs)
(
Zhou et al. 2016
)
as initial pseudo labels to locate objects in an image. The refined pseudo-labels are then employed as ground-truth annotations for training a fully-supervised semantic segmentation network. Consequently, the integrity of pseudo labels is indispensable to the success of WSSS. CAMs can be derived from either Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) or visual transformers. Since convolution only perceives local information, CNN-generated CAMs tend to identify the most discriminative part of an object rather than encompassing the entire region
(
Ahn and Kwak 2018
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. In contrast to CNNs, visual transformers exhibit strong capability in modeling long-range contexts owing to the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanism
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
. Recent approaches attempt to dispatch affinity matrix in transformer blocks to compensate for the global relationship of naive CAMs, resulting in significant performance improvements. Nevertheless, we experimentally discovered that the affinity matrixes in self-attention blocks tend to project disparate weights into similar patterns as the layer depth increases, thereby diminishing the global relationships contributed to the CAMs (See Figure
2b
).

In this work, we first embark on a meticulous analysis of the aforementioned issue. A visual examination of the affinity matrices reveal that it possesses an inclination toward sparsification as it approaches convergence, hence disclosing a manifestation of over-smoothing. Meanwhile, by observing the successively augmented CAM attention maps (See Figure
5
), we discover that the deeper results tend to evince a substantial amount of extraneous background noise. Drawing upon this analysis, we posit a daring conjecture that the unbounded over-smoothing phenomenon introduces a noteworthy quantity of semantically irrelevant background noise, causing the performance degradation in deeper layers.

To address the undisciplined over-smoothing characteristic of the affinity matrix, we propose an Adaptive Re-Activation Mechanism (AReAM) that alleviates the issue of incomplete and excessive activations by restraining the high-level affinity matrixes with shallow global relationships. Specifically, AReAM entails weighing the affinity matrices of distinct layers based on the mean normalized inverse entropy of the successively enhanced CAM, which results in allocating inferior weights to layers with greater entropy values. During the backward process, the weights are inverted from the forward stage, with an emphasis on overseeing the deeper layers with more significant entropy values. This strategy enables an adaptive semantic oversight embedding, fostering compelling results.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We make an early attempt to investigate the impact of the affinity matrix that the undisciplined over-smoothing of the affinity matrix bears the main responsibility for the collapse of the global relationships and impeding the final performance.

•

This work proposes an adaptive approach termed AReAM based on the entropy of the successive enhanced attention map. Our method employs the adequate shallow affinity matrix fusion to supervise the over-smoothing deep affinity matrix, resulting in superior gains.

•

We conduct ample experiments on existing public datasets, demonstrating the simplicity and efficacy of our approach. Our method not only completes missing target regions but also effectively manages the global background interference noise.

2.
related work

2.1.
Weakly Supervised Semantic Segmentation (WSSS)

Current methods for WSSS using image-level labels often rely on Class Activation Maps (CAMs) as pseudo segmentation labels for fully supervised training, yet they may fail to capture complete object regions.
A number of prior works that deal with this problem are dedicated to promoting the quality of CAMs via various training schemes, such as seed region expansion
(
Kolesnikov and Lampert 2016
;
Huang et al. 2018
)
, adversarial erasing
(
Wei et al. 2017
;
Hou et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
and online attention accumulation
(
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Jiang et al. 2021
)
.

Wang et al. 2020a
imposes consistency regularization on CAMs from the perspective of network optimization.

Wei et al. 2018
enlarges the receptive field of convolution so as to transfer surrounding discriminative signals to non-discriminative object regions.
Apart from mining within an image, cross-image semantic relations
(
Sun et al. 2020a
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
are also exploited to recognize more comprehensive object patterns.
Moreover, some recent works
(
Du et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2022
)
leverage contrastive learning between pixels and prototypes to facilitate integral activations of object regions.
Another line of work
(
Ahn and Kwak 2018
;
Ahn et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Ru et al. 2022
;
Su et al. 2022a
)
focuses on learning pairwise affinities for CAM refinement. AffinityNet
(
Ahn and Kwak 2018
)
predicts semantic affinities between a pair of adjacent pixels from reliable regions. The predicted affinities are then viewed as transition probabilities to realize random walk on CAMs. IRNet
(
Ahn et al. 2019
)
further detects boundaries among classes so that the affinity propagation can be estimated more accurately. SEAM
(
Wang et al. 2020b
)
evaluates inter-pixel affinities from feature maps of the classification network. Pairwise affinities are also learned from self-attention
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Ru et al. 2022
)
to improve object appearance continuity.

2.2.
Visual Transformers for WSSS

Owing to the favourable capability of capturing long-range dependencies, visual transformers have achieved impressive performances over various visual tasks
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
;
Strudel et al. 2021
;
Xie et al. 2021
)
.
Some latest works
(
Gao et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Ru et al. 2022
)
also introduce visual transformers to the field of WSSS.
Inspired by the fact that the class token in a standard transformer can be arranged to a class-agnostic attention map,
TS-CAM
(
Gao et al. 2021
)
proposes to combine CAMs with the attention map for more accurate object localization.
MCTformer
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
introduces multiple class tokens to disentangle context information among different object categories. Consequently, the learned attention from class tokens to patch tokens can produce class-specific attention maps.
Instead of focusing on manipulating class tokens, TransCAM
(
Li et al. 2023
)
explicitly leverages the attention weights from the transformer branch for subsequent semantic propagation.
AFA
(
Ru et al. 2022
)
manages to derive reliable affinity labels to supervise the inherent affinities from self-attention.
In addition, considering the architecture difference between CNNs and transformers, some works
(
Chefer et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
adopt the Deep Taylor Decomposition
(
Montavon et al. 2017
)
principle, based on which attention and relevancy scores are integrated across multiple layers for generating class-aware activation maps.

2.3.
Re-Activation Mechanism

Since image-level labels only indicate presence or absence of certain object categories, activation regions identified by the classifier are usually incomplete and sparse. To this end, many existing methods manage to activate non-discriminate object regions.
Erasing strategies are commonly applied either on images
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
)
or feature maps
(
Hou et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
to form dense localization maps. However, erasing-based approaches generally lack constraints on distinguishing non-discriminate regions and background regions.
Several methods have been proposed to address this issue by taking advantage of saliency maps
(
Fan et al. 2018
;
Fan et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
)
.

3.
preliminaries

3.1.
Generate Coarse Attention with CAM

We employ class activation maps (CAMs)
(
Zhou et al. 2016
)
to obtain initial pseudo segmentation labels. The feature map derived from the transformer encoder is denoted as
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times H\times W}
, where
D
is the feature dimension,
H
and
W
are the spatial dimensions, respectively. The class activation map
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
(
C
is the number of foreground categories) is generated as follows:

(1)

\mathbf{M}{c,:,:}=\operatorname{ReLU}(\sum_{d=1}^{D}{\mathbf{W}_{d,c}}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{d,:,:})

Here
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times C}
denotes the classifier weights, and
\operatorname{ReLU}(\cdot)
is applied to exclude negative activations. The resultant coarse attention is then scaled to the range of
[0,1]
by means of
\operatorname{Min-Max}
normalization:

(2)

\hat{\mathbf{M}}{c,:,:}=\frac{\mathbf{M}{c,:,:}-\min(\mathbf{M}{c,:,:})}{\max(\mathbf{M}{c,:,:})-\min(\mathbf{M}{c,:,:})}

Existing CAM methodologies often concentrate on local discriminative areas, which are insufficient in generating attention maps for weakly supervised semantic segmentation tasks. In recent years, transformer-based techniques have gained popularity in modelling global relationships via long-range dependencies, which complement CAMs effectively. As a result, an increasing number of methodologies are employing the affinity matrix in Transformers to complement the deficient global relationships in CAMs, allowing attention maps to better encapsulate the corresponding objects. We will elaborate the process in the next subsection.

3.2.
Attention Map Refinement With Affinity Matrix

Given an input image, it is partitioned into
H\times W
patches, which are flattened and linearly projected to a sequence of
N=H\times W
tokens. Transformers typically employ the
multi-head self-attention
(MHSA)
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
to capture long-range dependencies among these tokens. Specifically, for each head
h
in each transformer layer
l
, the token sequence is projected to a query matrix
\mathbf{Q}^{(l,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D_{k}}
, a key matrix
\mathbf{K}^{(l,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D_{k}}
and a value matrix
\mathbf{V}^{(l,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D_{v}}
through linear layers, where
D_{k}
is the embedding dimension of queries and keys, and
D_{v}
is the embedding dimension of values. The widely used scaled dot-product attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
computes dot products of each query with all keys and scales them by dividing
\sqrt{d_{k}}
, yielding the pair-wise affinity matrix
\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. The output matrix
\mathbf{O}^{(l,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D_{v}}
is therefore a weighted sum of values
\mathbf{V}^{(l,h)}
, where the softmax normalized affinity matrix
\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)}
is used as weight coefficients. The aforementioned self-attention mechanism can be formulated as:

(3)

\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)}=\frac{{\mathbf{Q}^{(l,h)}}{\mathbf{K}^{(l,h)}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{D_{k}}}

(4)

\mathbf{O}^{(l,h)}=\sigma(\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)})\mathbf{V}^{(l,h)}

where
\sigma
is the softmax operation. To aggregate attention maps from different layers, we average the self-attention probabilities across multiple heads and then compute the averaged results over all layers. The averaged affinity matrix
\bar{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is calculated as follows:

(5)

\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\frac{1}{N_{h}}\sum_{l}{\sum_{h}{\sigma(\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)})}}

where
N_{l}
is the number of selected transformer layers, and
N_{h}
is the number of heads in each transformer layer.

Discussion
:
Figure
2a
depicts the mIoU scores of the initial CAMs (w/o att.) and the attention-refined CAMs (w/ att.). Specifically, we choose attention maps from all layers and aggregate them by taking a simple average, following the method proposed in
(
Gao et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. CAMs are subjected to various background thresholds, and we report the optimal mIoU. Figure
2a
illustrates that applying an average affinity matrix (
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
) to CAM (
\hat{\mathbf{M}}{c,:,:}
) delivers a remarkable improvement in the accuracy of the original CAM. For instance, the accuracy of MCTFormer
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
increases from 47.2% to 60.5% (+13.3%), while the accuracy of Conformer
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
surges from 50.4% to 64.3% (+13.9%). However, upon a closer examination of the gain that the affinity matrix brings to the CAM at each layer, we found that it has a sustained and significant benefit in the shallow layers (the deficiency of Conformer at layer
0
is due to the lack of interaction with the convolution branch compared with the rest of layers), but it dramatically impairs the accuracy of the existing CAM in the deep layers, as depicted in Figure
2b
.
Consequently, we summarize that
the affinity matrix plays a crucial role in enhancing the generation of attention maps, while its efficacy across different layers in transformer block is not uniform. Uncovering these underlying factors and utilizing these global relationships adaptively is still an uncharted territory, demanding further exploration.

4.
methodology

Building upon the insights above, we present a detailed account of our adaptive and robust solution, which is based on the underlying analysis that we proposed.

4.1.
Analysis

(I) Consistent patterns in deeper layers
:
Recall that the global relationships of the affinity matrix in different layers do not always benefit attention maps. While delving into the underlying analysis, we present the outcomes of a detailed examination and study pertaining to or affecting the sense of sight regarding the affinity matrix in Figure
3
. The observed results from Figure
3
indicate that diagonal correlations are predominant in the early layers, whether using the MCTformer or Conformer approach. However, as the layer depth going deeper, such as in the 8th and 11th layers, the affinity matrix progressively converges towards particular patterns.

Visualization of affinity matrix in different layers.

To further explore the effect of the affinity matrix for weakly-supervised semantic segmentation, we employ the Hellinger distance to quantify the distance between two probability distributions. Let
\hat{A}^{(l)}=[\hat{A}^{(l)}_{i,j}],i\in\{1,\dots,N\},j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
be an affinity matrix at layer
l
, where each row
a_{i,:}
is normalized by
\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)
to represent the attention distribution between the
i
-th token and all other tokens as
\hat{A}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{N_{h}}\sum_{h}\sigma(A^{(l,h)})
. The Hellinger distance of the
i
-th distribution and the
j
-th distribution is then computed as:

(6)

\mathcal{H}_{l}(\hat{A}^{(l)}_{i,:},\hat{A}^{(l)}_{j,:})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(\sqrt{\hat{A}^{(l)}_{i,k}}-\sqrt{\hat{A}^{(l)}_{j,k}})^{2}}}

Our experiments are conducted on PASCAL VOC
train
dataset by fine-tuning a transformer-based network MCTformer
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
and a hybrid network Conformer-S
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
.

In Figure
4
(b), we illustrate the Hellinger distance of attention distributions from layer
0
to layer
11
. As shown in Figure
4
(b), for MCTformer, the layer-wise distance gradually gets lower as the layer gets deeper, while for Conformer, it remains stable until layer
10
, but rapidly drops at layer
11
and layer
12
.The stability displayed in Conformer at shallow layers is probably due to the coupled convolution branch, which is helpful to diversify global relationships.

(II) Undisciplined over-smoothing bring global noise
:
Given that the token-wise affinity matrix demonstrates sparsity with particular patterns in deeper layers, it is intuitive to wonder whether the CAM attention map, which is augmented by the affinity matrix, would exhibit such characteristic as well. Figure
5
illustrates that as the affinity matrix becomes smoother, the enhanced attention is gradually polluted with global noise. Empirical analysis reveals that when the global relationship among all tokens is consistently dependent on a subset of tokens, minor perturbations can engender irrelevant background attention noise.

To summarize, the self-attention mechanism inclines to encode different token relationships into sparse patterns when layers are deep, and we argue that this tendency can be consistently observed in the attention distribution, which is a previous step for calculating token representations.
Although previous works
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2022
)
have explored the affinity matrix to refine rough CAMs, they simply take the average of affinity matrices derived from different layers, totally neglecting the over-smoothing issue that existed in deep layers, as we have discussed. While there are few works investigate the phenomenon of the over-smoothing in token representation, they simply ascribe the performance degradation to the over-smoothing. However, in this work, we hypothesize that
the undisciplined over-smoothing of the affinity matrix bears the main responsibility for performance degradation
, as will be further substantiated in the experimental section.

). At inference stage, we simply average optimized affinity matrices as the final global relationships to compensate for CAMs.

4.3.
Adaptive Re-Activation Mechanism

In this section, we introduce our proposed AReAM framework in detail.

Compensating CAMs with Affinity Matrices.

Following mathematical notation in sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we denote the normalized class activation maps as
\hat{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
and the affinity matrix in layer
l
as
\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{HW\times HW}
. As illustrated in Figure
7
, for a presented category
c\in\{1,\dots,C\}
, we first flatten the corresponding class activation map
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{c,:,:}
to a sequence, then perform matrix multiplication on
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{c,:,:}
and the transpose of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
, which can be formulated as:

(7)

\mathbf{\hat{P}}^{(l)}=\Gamma^{-1}(\sigma(\sum_{c}(\Gamma(\mathbf{\hat{M}}_{c,:,:})\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(l)})^{\top}))

(8)

w^{(l)}=1-\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{\hat{P}}^{(l)}_{i,j}log\mathbf{\hat{P}}^{(l)}_{i,j}

(9)

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\Gamma^{-1}(\Gamma(\mathbf{\hat{M}}_{c,:,:})\cdot(\sum_{l}w^{(l)}\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(l)})^{\top})

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
represents the reshape operator that flattens a two-dimensional map to a one-dimensional sequence, and
\Gamma^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse operation of
\Gamma(\cdot)
.
The idea behind matrix multiplication is quite intuitive. Specifically, each row of the affinity matrix is a probability distribution that reflects the attention of a specific token to other tokens, including itself, and the dot-product operation between this probability and the flattened CAM reactivates elements by perceiving panoramic contextual information.

Generate Reliable Segmentation Labels.

Although CAMs are greatly promoted by the propagation with attention maps, they still suffer from inaccuracy, especially at boundaries of different categories, since the whole network is only supervised by image-level classification labels.
To enhance pixel-level contextual consistency, some approaches
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2022
)
incorporate dense CRF
(
Krahenbuhl and Koltun 2011
)
to introduce low-level image features for further refinement.
In addition, some single-stage networks
(
Araslanov and Roth 2020
;
Ru et al. 2022
)
adopt pixel-adaptive convolution
(
Su et al. 2019
)
as a considerably faster alternative of dense CRF to refine segmentation labels during the training phase. We draw the inspiration from
(
Su et al. 2019
)
to generate piece-wise smooth results as follows:

(10)

\mathbf{Y}^{*}(:,i,j)=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{N}(i,j)}\kappa(i,j,x,y)\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(:,x,y)

where pixel
(x,y)
is sampled from the neighbor set of pixel
(i,j)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(i,j)
.
The function
\kappa(i,j,x,y)
is a kernel that reflects pairwise potentials between pixel
(i,j)
and pixel
(x,y)
.
Inspired by
(
Ru et al. 2022
)
, we take image intensities and spatial locations into account and define our pairwise terms as follows:

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa_{I}\equiv\kappa_{I}(i,j,x,y)&=-\frac{{|I(i,j)-I(x,y)|}^{2}}{c_{I}{\sigma_{I}}^{2}}\\
\kappa_{L}\equiv\kappa_{L}(i,j,x,y)&=-\frac{{|L(i,j)-L(x,y)|}^{2}}{c_{L}{\sigma_{L}}^{2}}\end{split}

where
I(i,j)
and
L(i,j)
denote image intensities and spatial locations at pixel
(i,j)
,
\sigma_{I}
and
\sigma_{L}
are locally computed standard deviations of intensities and locations,
c_{I}
and
c_{L}
are constants that control the degree of color similarity and spatial nearness. The above formulation is inspired by the observation that adjacent pixels with similar colors are likely to be categorized into the same class.
The kernel function
\kappa(i,j,x,y)
is therefore a linear combination of normalized
\kappa_{I}
and
\kappa_{L}
:

(12)

\kappa(i,j,x,y)=w_{I}\frac{\exp(\kappa_{I})}{\sum_{(x,y)}{\exp(\kappa_{I})}}+w_{L}\frac{\exp(\kappa_{L})}{\sum_{(x,y)}{\exp(\kappa_{L})}}

where
w_{I}
and
w_{L}
are weight factors that adjust the importance of each term.
We use multiple dilated
3\times 3
convolution kernels to extract neighbors of a given pixel. The number of iterations is set to 10 to balance the training efficiency and segmentation accuracy.

In practice, we define two thresholds
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, satisfying
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<1
, to generate reliable segmentation labels. The segmentation label
\mathbf{Y}_{s}
at pixel
(i,j)
is defined as:

(13)

\mathbf{Y}_{s}(i,j)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\operatorname{argmax}(\mathbf{Y}^{*}(:,i,j))&\text{if }\max(\mathbf{Y}^{*}(:,i,j))>\alpha_{1}\\
0&\text{if }\max(\mathbf{Y}^{*}(:,i,j))<\alpha_{2}\\
255&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
\operatorname{argmax}(\cdot)
extracts the semantic label at which the prediction value is maximized. Besides, we use label
0
to represent the background, and label
255
to indicate uncertain regions.

Deep-level Affinity Optimization.

The generated reliable segmentation labels
\mathbf{Y}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
are used to derive reliable affinity labels
{\mathbf{Y}_{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{HW\times HW}
between two pixels, formulated as follows:

(14)

\mathbf{Y}_{a}(ij,kl)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}1&\text{if }\mathbf{Y}_{s}(i,j)=\mathbf{Y}_{s}(k,l)\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}_{s}(:,:)\neq 255\\
0&\text{if }\mathbf{Y}_{s}(i,j)\neq\mathbf{Y}_{s}(k,l)\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}_{s}(:,:)\neq 255\\
255&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Specifically, for pixels
(i,j)
and
(k,l)
that are sampled from confident foreground and background regions, their affinity label is
1
if their predicted segmentation labels are the same, otherwise
0
. Note that if either of two pixels is in uncertain regions, they are simply ignored during optimization.

The reliable affinity labels are considered as pseudo ground truth labels to supervise attentions in deep transformer layers. Recall that in section
3.1
, we denote the self-attention matrix generated from each layer as
\mathbf{A}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{HW\times HW}
. The attention adjustment loss for each layer
\mathcal{L}_{aff}^{(l)}
is then calculated as:

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{aff}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{+}}\sum_{\mathbf{Y}_{a}(ij,kl)=1}(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(\mathbf{A}^{(l)}(ij,kl)))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{-}}\sum_{\mathbf{Y}_{a}(ij,kl)=0}\operatorname{sigmoid}(\mathbf{A}^{(l)}(ij,kl))

where
N^{+}
and
N^{-}
are number of positive pairs and number of negative pairs in the affinity matrix, respectively. Instead of applying
\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)
function, we employ
\operatorname{sigmoid}(\cdot)
function to mimic the gating mechanism. In other words, if the affinity label between the pixel
(i,j)
and any other pixel
(k,l)
is
1
, their attention should be encouraged and therefore the activation at pixel

5.1.
Setup

Datasets and Evaluation Metrics.

Our approach is evaluated on the PASCAL VOC 2012
(
Everingham et al. 2009
)
dataset and the MS COCO 2014
(
Lin et al. 2014
)
dataset. PASCAL VOC 2012 comprises 20 distinct foreground object classes. Following the common practice
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Ru et al. 2022
)
, we adopted an enlarged set of 10,582 images with image-level annotations provided by
(
Hariharan et al. 2011
)
for training. It also has 1,449 validation images and 1,456 test images. Testing results are obtained from the official evaluation server. MS COCO 2014 dataset contains 80 foreground object classes. It has 82,081 images for training and 40,137 images for validation. Note that we only leverage image-level classification labels throughout the whole training process. We report the mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) as the evaluation metric.

Implementation Details.

For Conformer-based experiments, we adopt Conformer-S
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
pretrained on ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
as the backbone. We employ standard data augmentation techniques during training, including random rescaling within the range of
[0.5,2.0]
, random horizontal flipping, color jittering, and random cropping to
384\times 384
. We use AdamW
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
as the optimizer and the batch size is
8
. The total number of epochs is
12
. The learning rate for the backbone’s parameters is linearly ramped up during the first epoch to
5e-5
, then decayed with a cosine schedule
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2016
)
, while the learning rate for other parameters is multiplied by 10. Two background thresholds
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are 0.35 and 0.55, respectively.
For MCTformer-based experiments, we adopt DeiT-S
(
Touvron et al. 2021
)
pretrained on ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
as the backbone.
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are 0.15 and 0.35, respectively. Hyper-parameters of pixel-adaptive refinement are the same as in
(
Ru et al. 2022
)
.

Undisciplined over-smoothing
vs.
Semantic-aware contraction
.
In Figure
8
, the first row illustrates the affinity matrix in different layers of vanilla MCTformer, while the second row exhibits the corresponding affinity matrix after
AReAM
is applied. It is observed that the diagonal of the affinity matrix is highlighted at the beginning, but as the layer deepens, particular columns are instead emphasized, indicating that tokens in deep layers incline to pay attention to sparse specific tokens.
Recall that we make a comprehensive analysis in Section
4.1
for the degradation of attention augmentation in deep layers.
We argue that the reason for this phenomenon can be attributed to undisciplined over-smoothing instead of the characteristic of over-smoothing.
As shown in Figure
9
, the undisciplined affinity matrix introduces a lot of background noise (see the first row of each case), which impedes the overall performance of semantic segmentation. As can also be demonstrated in Figure
8
, the over-smoothing still exists in deep layers, but the pattern has been changed in a segmentation-friendly way. It is worth noting that segmentation results can be greatly improved through our proposed
AReAM
(see the second row of each case), which imposes restrictions on each affinity matrix in deep layers to make it attentive to semantic objects. These results reveal that over-smoothing does exist in deep layers, but it is not necessarily a barrier to attention generation.
AReAM
addresses this issue by changing attentive tokens to more semantic tokens in deep layers.

Moreover, since parameters in shallow layers also participate in optimization, semantic segmentation results at shallow layers are simultaneously facilitated. At inference time, we aggregate affinity matrices from all layers to refine CAMs. An issue may arise here: why not perform CAM propagation with the layer that gives the best performance? First of all, the
best
layer can not be ensured with prior knowledge and it is tedious to search such a layer. Secondly, referring back to Figure
10
, the result with aggregation is comparable (w/o AReAM) or is better (w/ AReAM) than that with the best layer. We assume the gain with AReAM is due to the fact that all affinity matrices have been finely calibrated and they can be complementary to each other, which is beneficial to boost the performance of semantic segmentation.

6.
conclusion

This work proposes a novel perspective that highlights the objects of interest by investigating the regions of the trait, fostering an extensive comprehension of the successive affinity matrix. An adaptive re-activation mechanism (AReAM) is suggested to address the issue of incomplete attention within the object and chaotic background noise by supervising high-level attention with shallow affinity matrices based on the discovery that the undisciplined over-smoothing of the affinity matrix bears the main responsibility for the collapse of the global relationships. The proposed AReAM strategy is effective and flexible, as demonstrated by extensive experiments on existing public datasets, can also be employed in many other transformer-based attention generation tasks.
