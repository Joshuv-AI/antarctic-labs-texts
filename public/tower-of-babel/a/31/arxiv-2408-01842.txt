On a novel fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz space
Kasmi Ayoub 1 , Azroul El Houssine 2 , and Shimi Mohammed 3

Abstract
. In this work, we aim to explore whether a novel type of fractional space can be defined using Orlicz spaces and fractional calculus. This inquiry is fruitful, as extending classical results to new contexts can lead to a better and deeper understanding of those classical results. Our main objective is to introduce a new fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz space, denoted by 𝒪 α , G ​ ( Λ , ℝ ) \displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{\alpha,G}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}) . We are interested in several qualitative properties of this space, such as reflexivity, completeness, and separability. Additionally, we establish a continuous embedding results of this space into a suitable Orlicz space and the space of continuous functions. As an application, we use the mountain-pass theorem (MPT) to ensure the existence of a nontrivial weak solution for a new class of fractional-type problems in Orlicz space.

Introduction

Fractional calculus constitutes a significant research area because it extends traditional calculus by allowing the use of non-integer order derivatives and integrals, providing powerful tools for modelling and solving complex problems across various fields including applied mathematics, physics, biology, engineering, economics, and others (see for instance

On other hand, the mathematical analysis of complex phenomena relies on suitable functional spaces with specific properties. Among these spaces, Orlicz-Sobolev spaces
[

,
16
,
22
,
15

provide a powerful framework for extending the analysis beyond classical Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, allowing for a wider range of functions and growth behaviors. Their main advantage lies in their ability to capture the finer properties of functions, where traditional Sobolev spaces sometimes struggle to represent them optimally. In addition, Orlicz-Sobolev spaces are easier to handle analytically because of their convexity and modularity properties, making them easier to handle mathematically.

Alongside, fractional Sobolev spaces, introduced by Nikol’skij
[

in 1951, are an extension of classical Sobolev spaces to accommodate fractional (non-integer) orders of differentiability offering a bridge between classical analysis and more complex, non-local phenomena. For
\displaystyle s\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle p\in(1,+\infty),
these spaces are defined as

W^{s,p}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega):\tfrac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{\tfrac{{\tiny N}}{p}+s}}\in L^{p}(\Omega\times\Omega)\right\},

where
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an open set. They are useful in various fields such as partial differential equations, image processing, and fractal geometry.

In the last few years, based on the same approach given in aforementioned spaces, some scholars have extended the Orlicz-Sobolev spaces to include tha fractional case, see for example

and the references therein.

In

Jiao and Zhou proposed an other way to define the Sobolev space in the fractional case. They introduced the space
\displaystyle E_{0}^{\alpha,p}
as a set of functions
\displaystyle v\in

\displaystyle L^{p}\left(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
having an

\displaystyle\alpha
-order Caputo fractional derivative
\displaystyle{}_{0}^{C}D_{t}^{\alpha}v
in
\displaystyle L^{p}\left(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
and
\displaystyle v(0)=v(T)=0
with
\displaystyle\Lambda=[0,T]
. More recently, José el al.
[

introduced a new space using a novel derivative called "
\displaystyle\psi
-Hilfer fractional derivative
\displaystyle{}^{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{D}_{0+}^{\alpha,\beta;\psi}
", which generalises other classical fractional derivatives, as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{p}^{\alpha,\beta;\psi}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}v\in L^{p}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R});{}^{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{D}_{0+}^{\alpha,\beta;\psi}v\in L^{p}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}),\\
\mathbf{I}_{0+}^{\beta(\beta-1)}v(0)=\mathbf{I}_{T-}^{\beta(\beta-1)}v(T)=0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})}

It is therefore a natural inquiry to explore the possibility of defining a new different type of fractional space using Orlicz spaces and fractional calculus. This exploration is valuable because extending classical results to new contexts can lead to a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of these results.

Thus, Inspired by the above interesting works, our primary goal in this work is to define the new fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz space

reflexivity, separability, and establish some embeddings results.

2.1.
Orlicz spaces

We devote the present subsection to listing briefly some fundamental terms and characteristics of Orlicz spaces. We would like to refer the reader to
[
1
,
19
,
15
,
22
]
and the references therein.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega
be an open subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Assume that
\displaystyle G:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a
\displaystyle\mathrm{N}
-function, meaning that
\displaystyle G
is convex and continuous for all
\displaystyle s>0
, which satisfies

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{G(s)}{s}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\frac{G(s)}{s}=\infty.

Equivalently,
\displaystyle G
admits the representation :
\displaystyle G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(t)dt
, where
\displaystyle g
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfies the following conditions :

•

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}g(s)=\infty~
and
\displaystyle~g(0)=0\text{; }

•

If
\displaystyle s>0
, then
\displaystyle g(s)>0
;

•

\displaystyle g
is increasing;

•

\displaystyle g
is right continuous.

We define the Orlicz space for the function G described before by

L^{G}(\Omega)=\left\{v:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable }\int_{\Omega}G(\gamma|v(s)|)ds<\infty,\text{ for some }\gamma>0\right\}.

The space
\displaystyle L^{G}(\Omega)
is a Banach space equipped with the Luxemburg norm

(2.1)

\|v\|_{G}=\inf\left\{\gamma>0:\int_{\Omega}G\left(\frac{|v(s)|}{\gamma}\right)ds\leqslant 1\right\}.

The formula
\displaystyle\overline{G}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\overline{g}(t)dt
defines the conjugate
\displaystyle N
-function of
\displaystyle G
, where
\displaystyle\overline{g}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is given by
\displaystyle\overline{g}(s)=\sup\{t:g(t)\leqslant s\}
.

In addition, the Hölder inequality in Orlicz space is given by the following formula

(2.2)

\left|\int_{\Omega}vuds\right|\leqslant C\|v\|_{G}||u\|_{\overline{G}}\quad\forall v\in L^{G}(\Omega)\text{ and }u\in L^{\overline{G}}(\Omega),

where
\displaystyle C
is a positive constant (see
[
16
, Theorem 13.13]
).

The function
\displaystyle G
is said to satisfy the
\displaystyle\Delta_{2}
-condition (see
[
15
]
) if there exists
\displaystyle k>0
such that

(2.3)

G(2s)\leqslant kG(s),\forall s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Given an N-function
\displaystyle G
which satisfies the global
\displaystyle\Delta_{2}
-condition. Then, we have

(2.4)

\bar{G}(g(s))\leq cG(s)\text{ for all }s\geq 0,

where
\displaystyle c>0
.

Furthermore, the function
\displaystyle G
has the following properties
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle\bullet
For any
\displaystyle 0<r<1
and
\displaystyle s>0
, it holds

(2.5)

G(sr)\leqslant rG(s).

\displaystyle\bullet
Every N-function
\displaystyle G
satisfies

(2.6)

G(s)\leq sg(s)\leq G(2s),\quad\text{ for all }s\geq 0.

In this paper we assume that

(2.7)

1<g^{-}:=\inf_{s>0}\frac{sg(s)}{G(s)}<g^{+}:=\sup_{s>0}\frac{sg(s)}{G(s)}<+\infty\text{. }

In addition, we presume that
\displaystyle G
satisfies the subsequent requirement the function

(2.8)

[0,\infty)\ni s\mapsto G(\sqrt{s})\text{ is convex}.

Relation (
2.8
) assures that
\displaystyle L^{G}(\Omega)
is an uniformly convex space consequently is a reflexive space (see
[
15
, Proposition 2.2]
).

For all
\displaystyle\phi\in L^{G}(\Lambda)
, we define the modular

\rho(\phi)=\int_{\Lambda}G\left(|\phi(x)|\right)dx.

If
\displaystyle\left(\phi_{n}\right),\phi\in L^{G}(\Lambda)
then the relations shown below are valid

(2.9)

\|\phi\|_{G}>1\Rightarrow\|\phi\|_{G}^{g^{-}}\leqslant\rho(\phi)\leqslant\|\phi\|_{G}^{g^{+}};

(2.10)

\|\phi\|_{G}<1\Rightarrow\|\phi\|_{G}^{g^{+}}\leqslant\rho(\phi)\leqslant\|\phi\|_{G}^{g^{-}};

(2.11)

\left\|\phi_{n}-\phi\right\|_{G}\rightarrow 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho\left(\phi_{n}-\phi\right)\rightarrow 0;

(2.12)

\left\|\phi_{n}\right\|_{G}\rightarrow\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho\left(\phi_{n}\right)\rightarrow\infty.

Definition 2.1

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

Definition 2.2
.

The Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives
\displaystyle D_{a+}^{\alpha}v
and
\displaystyle D_{b-}^{\alpha}v
of order
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n}\mathbf{I}_{a+}^{n-\alpha}v(x)

(2.16)

respectively, where
\displaystyle n=[\alpha]+1
.

Definition 2.3
.

The left-sided and right-sided Caputo fractional derivatives

(2.18)

{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{b-}^{\alpha}v(x):=D_{b-}^{\alpha}\left[v(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{v^{(k)}(b)}{k!}(b-t)^{k}\right](x)

respectively, where
\displaystyle D_{a+}^{\alpha}(\cdot)
and
\displaystyle D_{b-}^{\alpha}(\cdot)
are defined in

and
\displaystyle(\ref{211})

3.
Fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz space

The present section is devoted to the new fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz functional framework and the establishment of there basic properties.

Given
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1
and let
\displaystyle G
be an
\displaystyle N
-function and we denote
\displaystyle\Lambda=[0,T]
. Then, we introduce the left-sided fractional Caputo-derivative Orlicz space
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{\alpha,G}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{O}^{\alpha,G}
as follows :

(3.1)

\mathcal{O}^{\alpha,G}=\mathcal{O}=\left\{v\in L^{G}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})~~\text{ such that }~~{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v\in L^{G}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})\right\}.

We equip this space with the following norm

(3.2)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}}=\|v\|_{G}+[v]_{\mathcal{O}},

where
\displaystyle[\cdot]_{\mathcal{O}}
is defined by

(3.3)

[v]_{\mathcal{O}}=\inf\left\{\gamma>0:\int_{\Lambda}G\left(\frac{\left|{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)\right|}{\gamma}\right)dx\leqslant 1\right\}\text{.}

Remark 3.1
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is non-empty. Indeed, let us consider the constant function
\displaystyle v(x)=C
,
\displaystyle G(x)=e^{x}
and let
\displaystyle\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Clearly
\displaystyle v\in L^{G}(\Lambda)
, it remains to show that
\displaystyle{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v\in L^{G}(\Lambda).

{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\int_{0}^{T}(x-t)^{-\alpha}v^{\prime}(t)dt=0.

Then

\psi(x)={}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)=0.

Hence, we have
\displaystyle\psi\in L^{G}(\Lambda)
, which implies that
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is non-empty.

Note that The fractional derivative of a constant is not necessarily zero. However, the Caputo derivative of a constant is always zero, which is a distinctive feature compared to other types of fractional derivatives.

Next, for any
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{O}
, we define the modular function

\rho_{G}(v)=\int_{\Lambda}\left[G(|v(x)|)+G\left(\left|{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)\right|\right)\right]dx.

It is obvious to check that
\displaystyle\rho_{G}
is a convex modular on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, the norm
\displaystyle(\ref{32})
can be defined as follows:

(3.4)

\|v\|=\inf\left\{\gamma>0:\rho_{G}\left(\frac{v}{\gamma}\right)\leqslant 1\right\}.

We also define the closure of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}\left(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}\right)
, in the norm
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
defined in (
3.4
) by

\mathcal{O}_{0}=\left\{v\in\mathcal{O}:v(0)=v(T)=0\right\}.

On the other hand, for any
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{O}_{0}
, we define the convex modular as follows

\rho_{G}^{0}(v)=\int_{\Lambda}G\left(\left|{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)\right|\right)dx.

The norm associated with
\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{0}
is given by

\|v\|_{0}=[v]_{\mathcal{O}}=\inf\left\{\gamma>0:\rho_{G}^{0}\left(\frac{v}{\gamma}\right)\leqslant 1\right\}.

Note that in the case
\displaystyle g(t)=p|t|^{p-2}t
, the space
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
coincide with the fractional derivative space
\displaystyle E_{0}^{\alpha,p}
introduced in
[

Theorem 3.1
.

The space
\displaystyle(\mathcal{O},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{O}})
defined by
\displaystyle(\ref{O})
is a Banach space.

Proof.

We define the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow L^{G}(\Lambda)\times L^{G}(\Lambda)=\Pi

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(v(x),{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v(x)\right).

For any
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{O}

\|\mathcal{T}(v)\|_{\Pi}=\|v\|_{G}+\|{}^{C}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}v\|_{G}=\|v\|_{G}+[v]_{\mathcal{O}}=\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}}.

Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is an isometry. Since
\displaystyle\Pi
is a Banach space

]

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is also a Banach space.

\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v_{k}),v_{k}\right\rangle=\int_{\Lambda}g\left({}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)\right){}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)dt-\int_{\Lambda}a(t,v_{k}(t))v_{k}(t)dt.

Then by
\displaystyle(\ref{45})
, we get

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{J}\left(v_{k}\right)-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right),v_{k}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathcal{J}\left(v_{k}\right)\right|+\left|\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right)\right|\left|v_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\right).

However, with Proposition
3.1
and
\displaystyle(\ref{Gg})
, we get

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(v_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}G\left({}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)\right)dt-\int_{\Lambda}A(t,v_{k}(t))dt

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}A(t,v_{k}(t))dt.

In addition

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v_{k}),v_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}g\left({}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)\right){}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)dt-\int_{\Lambda}a(t,v_{k}(t))v_{k}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leqslant k\int_{\Lambda}{}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v_{k}(t)dt-\int_{\Lambda}a(t,v_{k}(t))v_{k}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leqslant k\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}a(t,v_{k}(t))v_{k}(t)dt.

By
\displaystyle(\ref{47})
,
\displaystyle(\ref{48})
and
\displaystyle(a_{2})
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(v_{k}\right)-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right),v_{k}\right\rangle\geqslant

\displaystyle\left(1-\tfrac{k}{\mu}\right)\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}A\left(t,v_{k}(t)\right)dt+\tfrac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Lambda}a\left(t,v_{k}(t)\right)v_{k}(t)dt

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-\tfrac{k}{\mu}\right)\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}.

Since
\displaystyle\mu>k
it follows that
\displaystyle{v_{k}}
is bounded in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
is reflexive space, there exists some
\displaystyle v
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
and some sub-sequence which for simplicity we still denote by
\displaystyle v_{k}
, with
\displaystyle v_{k}\rightharpoonup v
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
, as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
.

(4.10)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right)-\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v),v_{k}-v\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right),v_{k}-v\right\rangle-\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v),v_{k}-v\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right)\right\|\left\|v_{k}-v\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}-\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v),v_{k}-v\right\rangle.

By
\displaystyle(\ref{410})
, we find

\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right)-\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v),v_{k}-v\right\rangle\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

From Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, we get that
\displaystyle v_{k}
is bounded in
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\Lambda)
, additionally, we might presume that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|v_{k}-v\right\|_{\infty}=0.

Hence, we have

\int_{\Lambda}\left[a\left(t,v_{k}(t)\right)-a(t,v(t))\right]\left(v_{k}(t)-v(t)\right)dt\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Furthermore, a simple calculation reveals that

\left\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\left(v_{k}\right)-\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(v),v_{k}-v\right\rangle\geqslant\left\|v_{k}-v\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}-\int_{\Lambda}\left(a\left(t,v_{k}(t)\right)-a(t,v(t))\right)\left(v_{k}(t)-v(t)\right)dt.

So
\displaystyle\left\|v_{k}-v\right\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. That is
\displaystyle\left\{v_{k}\right\}
converges strongly to
\displaystyle v
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}
.
\displaystyle\Box

Now, we are in position to establish the proof of the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem

4.1
. It is simple to observe that
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(0)=0.
It remains to show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
satisfies the geometric conditions of the mountain pass theorem.

From
\displaystyle(\ref{IN})
, we infer

\max_{t\in\Lambda}|v(t)|\leq M\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}},\forall v\in\mathcal{O}_{0},

with
\displaystyle M=\tfrac{C\left(\overline{G}(1)\right)^{\frac{1}{g+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}x^{\frac{\alpha}{g}}
. Next let
\displaystyle C_{1}=\tfrac{1}{C}
, it follows by the inequality from above
and
\displaystyle(\ref{43})
, if
\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\leq C_{1}

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}A(t,v(t))dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Lambda}A\left(t,\frac{v(t)}{|v(t)|}\right)|v(t)|^{\mu}dt

\displaystyle\leq MTC^{\mu}\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{\mu}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}G\left({}^{C}{\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}}v(t)\right)dt-\int_{\Lambda}A(t,v(t))dt

\displaystyle\geq\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-MTC^{\mu}\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{\mu},\quad\text{ if }\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\leq C_{1},

Therefore

\mathcal{J}(v)\geq C_{1}^{g^{\pm}}-MTC_{1}^{\mu}C^{\mu}.

Let us consider
\displaystyle R<\min\left\{C_{1},\left(\tfrac{1}{MTC^{\mu}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\mu-g^{\pm}}}\right\}
and
\displaystyle\beta=R^{g^{\pm}}-MTC^{\mu}R^{\mu}
, then

\mathcal{J}(v)\geq\beta~~\text{ with }\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}=R.

Thus
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
fulfils the first geometric condition of the mountain pass theorem.

Now, for each
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
and
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{O}_{0}
, from Lemma
4.3
, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\xi v)

\displaystyle\leqslant\xi\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}A(t,\xi v(t))dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\xi\|v\|_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}^{g^{\pm}}-\ell|\xi|^{\mu}\int_{\Lambda}|v(t)|^{\mu}dt+T\ell.

Since
\displaystyle\mu>k
, by passing to the limit when
\displaystyle\xi\rightarrow+\infty
, we obtain
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\xi v)\rightarrow-\infty
, so the second geometric condition of the mountain pass theorem is affirmed by taking
\displaystyle e=\xi v
, with
\displaystyle\xi
sufficiently large, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(e)\leq 0.

Therefore
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
satisfies the
mountain pass condition, and thus
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
possess a nontrivial critical point which is a nontrivial weak solution of
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
.
