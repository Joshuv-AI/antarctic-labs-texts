1.
Introduction

In 1849, de Polignac
[
25
]
conjectured that every odd number greater than 3 is the sum of a prime and a power of 2.
However, he
[
27
]
found that 127 and 959 are two counterexamples soon. In 1934, Romanov
[
30
]
proved that there
is a positive proportion of the odd numbers which can be represented by the form
p+2^{k}~~(p\in\mathbb{P},~k\in\mathbb{N})
,
where
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{N}
stand for the set of primes and natural numbers, respectively.
Later, the opposite direction was achieved by van der Corput
[
6
]
, who illustrated that the odd numbers
which can not be written as the form
p+2^{k}~~(p\in\mathbb{P},~k\in\mathbb{N})
still possess a positive lower density.
In the same year, Erdős
[
13
]
constructed an arithmetic progression of odd numbers none of whose elements can be
represented by the sum of a prime and a power of 2. It is easy to find that van der Corput’s result follows from the one of Erdős.

In 2004, the first author and Sun
[
5
]
consider the quantitative version of the Romanov theorem. They showed that the lower asymptotic density of the odd numbers represented by the form
p+2^{k}~~(p\in\mathbb{P},~k\in\mathbb{N})
is larger than
0.0868
. In their paper, they pointed out that Erdős’ construction leads to the fact that the upper asymptotic density of the odd numbers with those forms is less than
0.49999991
. There are a few improvements of the lower asymptotic density, such as
[
12
,
15
,
16
,
20
,
26
]
. As far as we know, the best numerical values of upper asymptotic density at present is
0.49095
due to Habsieger and Roblot
[
15
]
. In literatures, the best numerical value of the lower asymptotic density is
0.107648
obtained by Elsholtz and Schlage–Puchta
[
12
]
. However, by a review of Alessandro regarding the paper of Elsholtz and Schlage–Puchta
[
12
]
on MathSciNet, the lower asymptotic density is at least
0.110114
.

Recently, Del Corso et al.
[
8
]
thought that if there is some absolute constant
c_{0}
such that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\{n:n\leqslant x,~n=p+2^{m},~m\in\mathbb{N},~p\in\mathbb{P}\}}{x}=c_{0},

then
0.437...
is probably a candidate of it. Their calculations follow the heuristic argument of Bombieri as well as a large amount of numeric operations.

Define the representation function
f(n)
to be

f(n)=\#\left\{(p,k):n=p+2^{k},~p\in\mathbb{P},~k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Noting that (in the proof of the Romanov theorem)

\sum_{n\leqslant x}f^{2}(n)\ll x,

Turán asked Erdős (written communication) whether the function
f(n)
is the unbounded one.
Erdős
[
13
]
gave an affirmative answer to the question of Turán by showing the following stronger result:

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(n)}{\log\log n}>0.

(1.1)

The unboundedness of the function
f(n)

for integers
n
having at most two prime factors was then established by Lu
[
19
]
,
following a remark of Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
14
]
which claimed that
f(n)
is greater than 2
for infinitely many integers
n
with at most two prime factors.
Further, for any subset
\mathscr{A}
of
\mathbb{N}
, let

f_{2^{\mathscr{A}}}(n)=\#\left\{(p,a):n=p+2^{a},~p\in\mathbb{P},~a\in\mathscr{A}\right\}.

With this notation, we shall have
f(n)=f_{2^{\mathbb{N}}}(n)
.
Erdős’ proof can be extended to the result

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f_{2^{\mathscr{A}}}(n)\log n}{\mathscr{A}\left(\frac{\log n}{\log 2}\right)\log\log n}>0,

where
\mathscr{A}(x)=|\mathscr{A}\bigcap[1,x]|
. So if one takes
the subset
\mathscr{A}
to be the prime set
\mathbb{P}
, we just
miss unboundedness fact of the representation function

f_{2^{\mathbb{P}}}(n)
. Based on this observation, the first author
of the present paper
[
3
]
posed the following conjecture:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{2^{\mathbb{P}}}(n)=\infty.

This conjecture was solved by the second author and Zhou
[
10
]
earlier.

It turns out that the magnitude of the function
f_{2^{\mathscr{A}}}(n)
is quite difficult to decide. For example,
Erdős
[
13
]
conjectured
f(n)=o(\log n)
. However, as Erdős
[
13
]
said, he can even not prove that not all the integers

n-2^{k}~~\left(1\leqslant k<\frac{\log n}{\log 2}\right)

are primes for sufficiently large numbers
n
. A careful checking of the prime table (up to
203775
)
by Erdős indicated that the largest exceptional integer should be 105. For integers
n\leqslant x
,
Vaughan
[
31
]
proved that the number of the exceptional integers is
O(x\exp(-c_{1}\log x\log\log\log x/\log\log x))

for some constant
c_{1}>0
by the Montgomery sieve
[
23
]
(i.e., the arithmetic large sieve).
Under the assumption of the extended Riemann Hypothesis, Hooley
[
18
]
proved that the exceptional numbers
are less than
O(x^{1-\lambda+\varepsilon})
for any sufficiently small positive number
\varepsilon
,
where
\lambda=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\right)
. Narkiewicz
[
24
]

improved this to
O(x^{1-\lambda/\log 2+\varepsilon})
under the same assumption. Elsholtz
[
11
]
considered a related problem.

In the same paper, Erdős made the following anecdotal conjecture.

Conjecture (Erdős 1950).

Let
c
be any constant and
x

sufficiently large,

a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdot\cdot\cdot<a_{t}\leqslant x,~t>\log x.

Then there exists an integer
n
so that the number of solutions
of
n=p+a_{i}

(p\in\mathbb{P},1\leq i\leq t)
is greater than

c
.

The case
a_{i}=2^{i}
of the conjecture was proved by Erdős himself as we mentioned above (Turán’s question). In a former note

[
9
]
, the second author gave a slight generalization of Erdős’ theorem. To be precise,
suppose that
\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdot\cdot\cdot\}
is a set satisfying
\mathscr{A}(x)>\log x
and

a_{i}\mid a_{i+1}
for all sufficiently large
x
and
i
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f_{\mathscr{A}}(n)}{\sqrt{\log\log n}}>0,

(1.2)

where

f_{\mathscr{A}}(n)=\#\left\{(p,a):n=p+a,~p\in\mathbb{P},~a\in\mathscr{A}\right\}.

After the second author
and Zhou
[
10
]
proved the conjecture for the case

a_{i}=2^{p_{i}}
, where
p_{i}
is the
i
-th prime. The authors of the
present paper
[
4
]
recognized that the complete proof of
Erdős’ conjecture actually follows directly from a new
achievement of the distributions of the primes established by
Maynard–Tao
[
21
,
28
]
. In fact, we proved the following more
stronger result.

Theorem A.
(Chen–Ding)
Let
x\geq 2
and the positive integers
a_{i}
satisfy

a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdot\cdot\cdot<a_{t}\leqslant x,~t>\log x.

Then there exists infinitely many integers
n
so that the number
of solutions of
n=p+a_{i}

(p\in\mathbb{P},1\leq i\leq t)
is
greater than
\frac{1}{8}\log\log x-1.6
.

In Theorem A, we do not know the magnitude of the least
n
. In
this paper, we give a quantitative result of the Erdős
conjecture.

Now, let us state the following theorem which is the main purpose of our paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
, there are two positive constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
depending on
\alpha
such that if
x
is a sufficiently large number,
t>(\log x)^{\alpha}
,
t
pairs of positive integers
a_{i},~\ell_{i}
satisfy

a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdot\cdot\cdot<a_{t}\leqslant x,

and

0<\ell_{i}<0.9\log\log x,\quad(\ell_{i},a_{i})=1\quad(1\leqslant i\leqslant t),

then there are at least
x^{1/\alpha}\exp(-c(\log x)^{\alpha})

integers
n\in\left[(x\log\log x)^{1/\alpha},2(x\log\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)
so that at least
c^{\prime}\log\log n
of the representations

\ell_{i}n=p+a_{i}\quad(p\in\mathbb{P},1\leqslant i\leqslant t)

are available.

The quantitative result of the Erdős conjecture mentioned above is a special case of Theorem
1.1
by taking
\ell_{i}=1
for all
i
.

For two sequences
\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots\}
and
\mathscr{L}=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\cdots\}
(
\ell_{i}
not necessarily different) with
(\ell_{i},a_{i})=1
, let
f_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{L}}(n)
be the extended representation function defined as

f_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{L}}(n)=\#\left\{i:\ell_{i}n=p+a_{i},~a_{i}\in\mathscr{A},~\ell_{i}\in\mathscr{L},~p\in\mathbb{P},~1\leq i\leq\mathscr{A}(n)\right\}.

With this definition we shall have
f_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{L}}(n)=f_{\mathscr{A}}(n)
for
\ell_{i}=1
. From Theorem
1.1
, we have the following corollaries.

Corollary 1.2
.

For
\alpha>0
, let

\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots\}

and

\mathscr{L}=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\cdots\}\quad\text{(not~necessarily~different)}

be two sequences of positive
integers with
\mathscr{A}(m)>(\log m)^{\alpha}
for infinitely
many positive integers
m
and
\ell_{m}<0.9\log\log m
for sufficiently large integers
m
. We have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{f_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{L}}(n)}{\log\log n}>0.

Remark 1.1
.

Taking
\mathscr{A}=\{2^{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\ell_{i}=1
, then Corollary
1.2
reduces to

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{f(n)}{\log\log n}>0,

which is the old result obtained by Erdős in 1950. Taking
\mathscr{A}=\{2^{p}:p\in\mathbb{P}\}
and
\ell_{i}=1
, then Corollary
1.2
reduces to

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{f_{2^{\mathbb{P}}}(n)}{\log\log n}>0,

which offers a much more stronger result obtained by the second author and Zhou formerly.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots\}
be a strictly increasing sequence of positive
integers with
\mathscr{A}(x)>(\log x)^{\alpha}
for all
sufficiently large
x
. Then there are two positive constants
c
and
c^{\prime}

depending only on
\alpha
such that for each
sufficiently large
x
, there are at least
x\exp(-c(\log x)^{\alpha})
integers
n\leq x
with

f_{\mathscr{A}}(n)\geq c^{\prime}\log\log x.

Corollary 1.4
.

Given an integer
\ell\geq 1
. Let

f_{\ell}(n)
be the number of solutions of
n=p+2^{m^{\ell}}
with

p\in\mathbb{P}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there are two positive
constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
depending only on
\ell

such that for each
sufficiently large
x
, there are at least
x\exp(-c(\log x)^{1/\ell})
integers
n\leq x
with

f_{\ell}(n)\geq c^{\prime}\log\log x.

Remark 1.2
.

If
\ell\geq 2
, then

|\{n\leq x:f_{\ell}(n)\geq 1\}|\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\cdot(\log x)^{1/\ell}=\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-1/\ell}}.

It follows that
the set of integers
n
that can be represented as the form

n=p+2^{m^{\ell}}
with
p\in\mathbb{P}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}

has asymptotic density zero. But Corollary
1.4
shows that

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{f_{\ell}(n)}{\log\log n}>0.

By employing a deep result of Maynard
[

, we
prove the following result which is used to prove Theorem

. This is of interest itself.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
. Then there exist a positive constant

C_{0}
depending only on
\alpha
such that for sufficiently

x
and
C_{0}\leq k\leqslant(\log x)^{\alpha}
, if

\{u_{1}n+v_{1},\cdots,u_{k}n+v_{k}\}
is a set of

\#\left\{x\leq n<2x:|\{u_{1}n+v_{1},\dots,u_{k}n+v_{k}\}\cap\mathbb{P}|\geqslant C_{0}^{-1}\log k\right\}\gg x\exp(-C_{0}(\log x)^{\alpha}).

Remark 1.3
.

The constraint

in Theorem
1.5
could be relaxed to

and

, where

.

It should be mentioned that we do not require

(for the definition of the admissible set of linear functions, see the first paragraph of Section 2 below). This will bring convenience in future applications. Below, we give an application of it as an illustration of the convenience. Let

up to

be the Euler totient function.

.

Let
\varepsilon
be an arbitrarily small positive number,

a positive
integer and let

be numbers satisfying that at least

and

is sufficiently large. Suppose that

such that

\displaystyle\pi(qn+y;q,a)-\pi(qn;q,a)\gg\log y,

(1.3)

where the implied constant depends only on

.

Corollary

is an expansion of Maynard’s result

provided that at least one of

(1.4)

for at least

2.
Clusters of primes in subsets

The proof of Theorem
1.5
follows from a quantitative
result of the admissible sets involving the distributions of
primes established by Maynard
[
22
]
. Firstly, we introduce
the notion of the admissible sets of linear functions. Let

\mathcal{L}=\{u_{i}n+v_{i}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}
be a set of linear functions with
u_{i}\geqslant 1

(1\leqslant i\leqslant k)
. We call
\mathcal{L}
an admissible
set of linear functions, if for each prime
p
there is some

m_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

p\nmid\prod_{i=1}^{k}(u_{i}m_{p}+v_{i}).

Below, we give a lemma which illustrates how to pick out the
admissible sets from an arbitrarily given set of linear functions.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
u_{1}n+v_{1},\dots,u_{s}n+v_{s}
are arbitrarily different
s(\geqslant 5)

linear functions with
(u_{i},v_{i})=1
and
u_{i}\geqslant 1

(1\leqslant i\leqslant s)
, then there are at least

\gg\frac{s}{\log s}
of them constituting an admissible set of
linear functions, where the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}
be the
i
-th prime.
If
p_{i}\mid u_{j}
, then let
\ell_{i,j}=v_{j}
. If
p_{i}\nmid u_{j}
,
then let
\ell_{i,j}=u_{i,j}v_{j}
, where
u_{j}u_{i,j}\equiv 1\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p_{i})
. For
p_{1}
, there is a residue class modulo
p_{1}

containing at most
\lfloor s/p_{1}\rfloor
of

\ell_{1,1},\ell_{1,2},...,\ell_{1,s}
. So at least
s-\lfloor s/p_{1}\rfloor
of

\ell_{1,1},\ell_{1,2},...,\ell_{1,s}

occupy at
most
p_{1}-1
residue classes modulo
p_{1}
. Denoting by

s_{1}=s-\lfloor s/p_{1}\rfloor
, without loss of generality, we may
assume that

\ell_{1,1},\ell_{1,2},...,\ell_{1,s_{1}}

occupy at most
p_{1}-1
of the residue classes modulo
p_{1}
.
Similarly, at least
s_{2}=s_{1}-\lfloor s_{1}/p_{2}\rfloor
elements of

\ell_{2,1},\ell_{2,2},...,\ell_{2,s_{1}}

occupy at most
p_{2}-1
residue classes modulo

p_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume

\ell_{2,1},\ell_{2,2},...,\ell_{2,s_{2}}

occupy at most
p_{2}-1
of the residue classes modulo
p_{2}
.
Continuing this process, let
t
be the least integer with

s_{t}<p_{t+1}
, where
s_{0}=s
. We have

s_{j}=s_{j-1}-\lfloor s_{j-1}/p_{j}\rfloor\quad(1\leqslant j\leqslant t).

Now we have

\displaystyle s_{t}\geqslant s_{t-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{t}}\right)\geqslant\cdots\geqslant s\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{t}}\right)\gg\frac{s}{\log p_{t}}

(2.1)

by the Mertens estimate. By the definition of
t
we have

s_{t-1}\geqslant p_{t}
, thus

\displaystyle s_{t}\geqslant s_{t-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{t}}\right)\geqslant p_{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{t}}\right)=p_{t}-1.

(2.2)

From equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), it can be concluded
that

s_{t}\gg\frac{s}{\log p_{t}}\geqslant\frac{s}{\log(s_{t}+1)}\geqslant\frac{s}{\log s}.

Finally, we show that

\{u_{1}n+v_{1},\dots,u_{s_{t}}n+v_{s_{t}}\}

is an admissible set of linear functions.
For any positive integer
i\leq t
, since

\{\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2},\dots,\ell_{i,s_{t}}\}\subseteq\{\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2},\dots,\ell_{i,s_{i}}\},

it follows that

\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2},\dots,\ell_{i,s_{t}}

occupy at most
p_{i}-1
of the residue classes modulo
p_{i}
. For

i>t
, we have
p_{i}\geq p_{t+1}>s_{t}
and

\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2},\dots,\ell_{i,s_{t}}

occupy at most
p_{i}-1
of the residue classes
modulo
p_{i}
. In all cases, there exists an integer
m_{i}
such
that

m_{i}+\ell_{i,j}\not\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p_{i}),\quad 1\leq j\leq s_{t}.

If
p_{i}\mid u_{j}
, then by
(u_{j},v_{j})=1
, we have
p_{i}\nmid v_{j}
.
Thus,
p_{i}\nmid u_{j}m_{i}+v_{j}
. If
p_{i}\nmid u_{j}
, then

u_{j}m_{i}+v_{j}\equiv u_{j}m_{i}+u_{i,j}u_{j}v_{j}\equiv u_{j}(m_{i}+\ell_{i,j})\not\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p_{i}).

In all cases, we have

u_{j}m_{i}+v_{j}\not\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p_{i}),\quad 1\leq j\leq s_{t}.

That is,

p_{i}\nmid(u_{1}m_{i}+v_{1})\cdots(u_{s_{t}}m_{i}+v_{s_{t}}).

Therefore,

\{u_{1}n+v_{1},\dots,u_{s_{t}}n+v_{s_{t}}\}

is an
admissible set of linear functions.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.7
.
∎

In order to prove Theorem
1.5
, we will employ a deep result of
Maynard
[
22
]
. For convenience of the readers, we use the
same symbols used by Maynard as far as possible (but there are
some minor adjustments of them). Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of
integers and
\mathcal{P}
a subset of primes. Let
\mathcal{L}=\{u_{i}n+v_{i}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}
and
L_{i}(n)=u_{i}n+v_{i}

\mathcal{A}[x]=\#\{n\in\mathcal{A}:x\leqslant n<2x\},

\mathcal{A}[x;q,a]=\#\{n\in\mathcal{A}:x\leqslant n<2x,n\equiv a\ ({\rm{mod}}\ q)\},

L_{i}(\mathcal{A})=\{L_{i}(n):n\in\mathcal{A}\},~~\varphi_{L_{i}}(q)=\varphi(qu_{i})/\varphi(u_{i}),

\mathcal{P}_{L_{i},\mathcal{A}}[x]=\#\{u_{i}n+v_{i}\in\mathcal{P}:x\leqslant n<2x,n\in\mathcal{A}\},

Hypothesis 1

The type

Lemma 5.10

Proof.

Since

, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant N\\
\lfloor\beta n+\gamma\rfloor\equiv a_{1}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ q_{1})\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left(q_{2}\lfloor\beta n+\gamma\rfloor+a_{2}\right)=\beta^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant\lfloor\beta N+\gamma\rfloor\\
m\equiv a_{1}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ q_{1})\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left(q_{2}m+a_{2}\right)+O\left(N^{1-\kappa_{1}}\right),

where the implied constant depends only on
\beta
and
\gamma
.

The proof of Theorem
5.15
is actually a trivial adjustment of the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 5.1]
(i.e., Lemma
5.13
) by making the following replacement in it.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant M\\
m\equiv a_{1}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ q_{1})\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\Lambda(q_{2}m+a_{2})e(\beta km){\mathrel{\to}^{n=q_{2}m+a_{2}}}e\left(-\frac{\beta ka_{2}}{q_{2}}\right)\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant q_{2}M+a_{2}\\
n\equiv q_{2}a_{1}+a_{2}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ q_{1}q_{2})\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\Lambda(n)e\left(\frac{\beta kn}{q_{2}}\right).

We remark that in this equality the additional condition
(q_{2}a_{1}+a_{2},q_{1})=1
appeared in Theorem
5.15
is necessary, which shall arise naturally in the verification of condition (2) in
Hypothesis 1
. Summarizing the above analysis and statements, it can be concluded as the following theorem.

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
\beta>1
and
0\leqslant\gamma<\beta
be fixed real numbers such that
\beta
is irrational with bounded partial quotients. For
\alpha>0
, let

\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots\}

and

\mathscr{L}=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\cdots\}\quad\text{(not~necessarily~different)}

be two sequences of positive
integers with
\mathscr{A}(m)>(\log m)^{\alpha}
for infinitely
many positive integers
m
and
\ell_{m}<0.9\log\log m
for sufficiently large integers
m
. Then we have

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
n\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\frac{f_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{L}}(n)}{\log\log n}>0

where
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\lfloor\beta k+\gamma\rfloor\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is the Beatty sequence.

Perhaps, some further refinements of our previous results can be taken in the scene of the Piatetski–Shapiro sequences
[
29
]

\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
for some
c
slightly larger than
1
due to the well–distributed property of its elements. We shall admit that we do not proceed this work. We believe that an elaborate treatment of the exponential sums involving the Piatetski–Shapiro sequences
\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
to make the constant
c
as large as possible would be of some interest.

6.
Final remarks

There is an outstanding conjecture named
Prime k-tuples conjecture
of Dickson which states as following.

Conjecture 6.1

(weak form)
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{k}
be an admissible set, then there are infinitely many integers
n
such that all of
n+a_{1},n+a_{2},...,n+a_{k}
are primes.

Based on the conjecture of Dickson and recent progress towards this direction, especially the result of Maynard
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
, we can make the following reasonable conjecture. It can be viewed as an extension of the Dickson conjecture.

Conjecture 6.2
.

For any arbitrarily small constant
\delta>0
, there exists an integer
x_{0}=x_{0}(\delta)
such that
for every
x\geq x_{0}
, if
a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{k}
is an admissible set
with
k\leq(\log x)^{1-\delta}
and
a_{i}\leqslant x^{1-\delta}
,
then there is a positive integer
n\leq x

such that all of the
k
shifts
n+a_{1},n+a_{2},...,n+a_{k}

are primes.

Replacing Theorem
1.5
by Conjecture
6.2
, we shall have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f_{\mathscr{A}}(n)}{(\log n)^{1-\delta}}>0.

Certainly, it covers the result

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(n)}{(\log n)^{1-\delta}}>0.

However, the decision of whether
f(n)=o(\log n)
seems to be far beyond the present mathematics, even under the strong assumptions. We end this article by quoting the original words of Erdős. ‘
The conjecture
f(n)=o(\log n)
if true is probably rather deep
’.
