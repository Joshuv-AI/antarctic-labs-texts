Maximum speed of dissipation
Swetamber Das, Jason R. Green

Abstract
Physical systems powering motion or creating structure in a fixed amount of time dissipate energy and produce entropy. Here, we derive speed limits on dissipation from the classical, chaotic dynamics of many-particle systems: the inverse of the entropy irreversibly produced bounds the time to execute a physical process for deterministic systems out of equilibrium, Δ ​ t ≥ k B / s ¯ i \Delta t\geq k_{B}/\bar{s}_{i} . We relate this statistical-mechanical speed limit on the mean entropy rate to deterministic fluctuation theorems. For paradigmatic classical systems, such as those exchanging energy with a deterministic thermostat, there is a trade-off between the time to evolve to a distinguishable state and the heat flux, q ¯ ​ Δ ​ t ≥ k B ​ T \bar{q}\Delta t\geq k_{B}T . In all these forms, the inequality constrains the relationship between dissipation and time during any nonstationary process including transient excursions from steady states.



When a piece of macroscopic matter is set in motion, it will irreversibly lose energy due to frictional or viscous heat generation
Callen 1985
.
If the system undergoes these dissipative transitions on finite timescales, it will not only expel energy, but also waste free energy and produce entropy.
But, how quickly does a, potentially finite-size, classical system of particles incur these thermodynamic costs as it evolves in the presence of an environment?
Are there limits on the speed at which these observables accumulate during finite time processes?
Answering this question is important to both synthetic and biological systems.
For example, the microscopic machinery of the cell includes nanoscale motors, such as kinesin, that perform essential functions on finely-tuned timescales
Goel and Vogel 2008
.
Given one second to power motion and haul molecular cargo, kinesin will dissipate about
650k_{B}T
of energy to its environment
Bustamante et al. 2005
, where
k_{B}
is Boltzmann’s constant and
T
is the temperature.
One might then hypothesize that the dissipation of energy and the production of entropy could constrain the time it takes for such systems to transition between nonequilibrium states
Bryant and Machta 2020
;
Skinner and Dunkel 2021
.

Recently, there have been advances from stochastic thermodynamics in predicting the speed of irreversible processes driven by gradients in temperature, pressure, and concentration.
These thermodynamic speed limits
Shiraishi et al. 2018
;
Hasegawa and Van Vu 2019
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
Nicholson et al. 2018
;
Falasco and Esposito 2020
;
Nicholson et al. 2020
are giving new understanding of the trade-off between time and dissipation.
In some forms, these purely stochastic results are analogues of quantum speed limits
Deffner and Campbell 2017
, which bound the speed at which physical systems (or their observables) evolve between two distinguishable states.
While thermodynamic speed limits on fluxes of energy and entropy are an emerging feature of stochastic thermodynamics, another important feature is fluctuation theorems. However, fluctuation theorems were originally derived for mechanical systems out of equilibrium
Evans and Searles 2002
with the techniques of dynamical systems theory
Gaspard 1998
;
Dorfman 1999
.
Still open is the question of whether thermodynamic speed limits on dissipation have counterparts that derive from the physical dynamics of mechanical systems.
That is, despite progress in classical speed limits and the classical limit of quantum speed limits
Shanahan et al. 2018
;
Okuyama and Ohzeki 2018
;
Poggi et al. 2021
;
Das and Green 2023a
, there are not speed limits on thermodynamic dissipation that derive from atomistic dynamics.

In this Letter, we derive a classical speed limit on entropy production for any continuous dynamical system.
We show the mean entropy production puts a bound on the time for a classical many-body system to transition between two nonequilibrium states as defined by deterministic fluctuation theorems.
This trade-off between the time to evolve between two distinguishable states and the entropy production predicts how much entropy or energy will be dissipated by a physical process over a finite period of time or how much time will it take for a process to dissipate an amount of entropy or energy.
Like early fluctuation theorems
Evans and Searles 1994
;
Gallavotti and Cohen 1995
;
Cohen and Gallavotti 1999
, this bound can be expressed in terms of Lyapunov exponents, the fundamental quantities characterizing deterministic chaos.
These quantities have been used to analyze rare trajectories
Tailleur and Kurchan 2007
, jamming
Banigan et al. 2013
, nonequilibrium self-assembly
Green et al. 2013
, equilibrium and nonequilibrium fluids
Evans and Morris 1990
;
Bosetti and Posch 2014
;
Das and Green 2017
, and critical phenomena
Das and Green 2019
. The finite system size, finite-time behaviors in these examples are subject to the speed limits on dissipation we derive here.

Dissipation from phase space volume contraction.–

To derive speed limits on dissipation in nonequilibrium processes, we ask: how many distinct physical states are visited by a classical trajectory during a time
\Delta t=t-t_{0}
?
Equilibrium statistical physics is successful through its methods for counting indistinguishable microscopic states for a given set of physical conditions
Callen 1985
; the thermodynamic entropy is a measure of the number of states,
S=k_{B}\ln\mathcal{V}
using phase space volume as a surrogate for the count.
To work away from equilibrium, we use the relationship between the dissipation of energy and the contraction of phase space volume
Gaspard 1998
;
Dorfman 1999
to construct measures distinguishability between physical states along deterministic trajectories.

To that end, consider a
d
-dimensional system of
N
classical particles at a point,
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{M}
, in phase space
\mathcal{M}
.
As the system evolves under chosen physical conditions, the state traces a trajectory,
\Omega=\{\bm{x}(t);t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{f}\}
, governed by the equations of motion
\dot{\bm{x}}=\bm{F}[\bm{x}(t)]
in an
n=2dN
-dimensional phase space,
\mathcal{M}
, Fig.
1
(a).
Infinitesimal phase space volumes,
\delta\mathcal{V}
, surrounding the phase point have an intrinsic rate at which they deform – the divergence of the flow,
\Lambda:=\delta\dot{\mathcal{V}}/\delta\mathcal{V}=\gradient\cdot\dot{\bm{x}}=\sum^{2dN}_{i}\partial F_{i}/\partial x_{i}
, From this divergence, one can calculate the Gibbs entropy rate
Andrey 1985
;
Ruelle 1996
;
Ruelle 1997

\dot{S}_{G}/k_{B}=-\int d\bm{x}\rho(\bm{x},t)\Lambda(\bm{x},t)=\langle\Lambda\rangle,

(1)

which, in deterministically thermostatted systems, is the thermodynamic entropy production rate
Jepps and Rondoni 2010
;
Harish and Patra 2021
.
The smooth probability density over the phase space
\rho(\bm{x},t)
is the solution of Liouville’s equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\dot{\bm{x}}\nabla\rho=-\rho\Lambda
.
The system is dissipative if the time averaged
\Lambda
is negative in the long-time limit
Ramshaw 2017
.

The divergence of the flow
\Lambda
is the rate of phase space compression due to dissipative forces and the theoretical link between dynamics, entropy production, and distinguishable states that we need for speed limits.
At a given phase point, the divergence is a property of the linearized dynamics in which infinitesimal volumes
\delta\mathcal{V}
evolve over time.
These phase space volumes are readily simulated with the linearized dynamics of model nonlinear dynamical systems
Pikovsky and Politi 2016
and molecular dynamics techniques
Evans and Morriss 2008
.

As a measure of the distinguishability of physical states, we consider the infinitesimal volume at two times,
\Gamma(t,t_{0}):=\delta\mathcal{V}(t)/\delta\mathcal{V}(t_{0})
.
To define a phase space volume, we choose a complete set of
n
linearly independent unit vectors
\{\ket{\delta\bphi_i}
}, normalized
\ket{\delta\bphi_i}=\ket{\delta\bx_i}/\|\delta\bm{x}_{i}\|
so that their outer product defines a classical density matrix,
{\bm{\xi}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\outerproduct{\delta\bphi_i}{\delta\bphi_i}

Das and Green 2022
.
At any time
t
, the determinant of
{\bm{\xi}}
represents the volume
\delta\mathcal{V}(t)
of an
n
-sphere in phase space and the trace
\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t)
gives its radius (Supplementary Material, SM
I
).
In terms of the radius
\Tr{\bm{\xi}}
, the ratio of volumes or
survival factor
is:

\displaystyle{\Gamma(t,t_{0})}:=\frac{\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t)}{\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t_{0})}=e^{2n^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Lambda(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}.

(2)

The rate
\Lambda
at given phase space point does not generally have a definite sign.
However, for dissipative systems, there are finite time intervals
\Delta t=t-t_{0}
, in which the
net
contraction
\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,\Lambda dt^{\prime}
is negative.

Classical speed limit on phase space contraction.—

Choosing values of the survival factor define conditions for speed limits on dissipation. Say the survival factor in Eq.
2
vanishes
\Gamma=0
in a time interval
\Delta t
and
\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Lambda dt^{\prime}\leq 0
.
The bilinear approximation of
e^{2x}
for
x\leq 0
gives the inequality
e^{2x}\geq(1+x)/(1-x)
, which we apply to Eq.
2
to get:

\displaystyle\Delta t\bar{\Lambda}/n\geq 1,

(3)

a trade-off between time and the net phase space contraction rate,
\bar{\Lambda}=-\Delta t^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Lambda dt
.
This bound is a speed limit on the phase space contraction of continuous-time deterministic dynamical system, and our first main result. It applies to systems that are dissipating energy to the surroundings so that the integrated phase space volume rate,
\bar{\Lambda}
, is net negative (SM
III
). By using the radius of spheres in phase space, rather than the volume, the dimension of phase space
n
appears, making the bound system-size intensive. For
N
-particle systems in 3D,
n=6N
.
Figure
1
(b) shows
\Lambda
as a function of time for a singly thermostatted oscillator. The time windows mark intervals satisfying this speed limit.

Another route to the distinguishability of physical states that we use to derive this speed limit (and its relatives below) comes from
deterministic
fluctuation theorems. Up to a constant
2/n
, the survival factor
\Gamma
in Eq.
2
appears in the transient fluctuation theorem: the ratio of probabilities to obtain
A
and
-A
as the finite time average of
\Lambda
over a time interval
\Delta t
is
Evans et al. 1993
;
Evans and Searles 2002
:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\Delta t}(\bar{\Lambda}=-A)}{p_{\Delta t}(\bar{\Lambda}=A)}=e^{\Delta tA}.

(4)

The net change in phase space volume
A=-\bar{\Lambda}(t)
sets both the relative probability of phase space contraction (compared to expansion) in the fluctuation theorem and the speed limit.

Comparing to quantum dynamics, this speed limit is analogous to the Margolus-Levitin bound: a quantum mechanical system with a finite average energy cannot evolve between two distinguishable states faster than
\Delta t_{\perp}\geq(2\langle E\rangle)^{-1}

Margolus and Levitin 1998
.
Rearranging
\Delta t^{-1}\leq\bar{\Lambda}
,

\bar{\Lambda}
is the maximum number of distinguishable states the system can pass through per unit time.
The minimum time for the system to evolve between distinguishable states is
\Delta t
, where the states here are distinguishable in the sense that
\Gamma=0
.
Here, the dissipation
\bar{\Lambda}
sets a similar speed limit on classical dynamical systems: A classical system with a finite average dissipation rate cannot evolve between two distinguishable states faster than
\Delta t\geq\bar{\Lambda}^{-1}
.
When there is no dissipation,
\bar{\Lambda}=0
, the time to move to a distinguishable state diverges because all states are indistinguishable under the chosen criterion.
A weaker form of the inequality in Eq.
3
follows from the linear approximation,
e^{x}\geq 1+x
, which gives
\Delta t\bar{\Lambda}/n\geq 1/2
and a factor of two that one might expect comparing to the Margolus-Levitin speed limit.
For a systems with a time-independent
\Lambda
and constant rate of phase space contraction, such as the damped harmonic oscillator and the Lorenz-Fetter model, the inequality also simplifies to
-\Delta t\Lambda/n\geq 1
.

A more general speed limit results if we fix
\eta:=p_{\Delta t}(-A)/p_{\Delta t}(A)
to a nonzero value. In this case, it gives the minimum time
\Delta t_{\eta}
to dissipate an amount determined by
\eta
.
If we choose condition
0\leq\Gamma=\eta<1
, we find the speed limit (SM
III
):

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{\Lambda}}{n}\geq\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}\quad\quad(\bar{\Lambda}\leq 0).

(5)

The inequality gives the minimum phase space contraction for a given time interval
\Delta t_{\eta}
.
It saturates for time intervals in which the average contraction rate is sufficiently small such that terms containing higher powers of
\bar{\Lambda}
(cubic and beyond) can be neglected:
e^{x}\approx\frac{2+x}{2-x}\approx 1+x+x^{2}/2
.
If
\eta=1
, the minimum time to transition between distinguishable states vanishes.
A similar relation exists for the expansion of phase volume with
\bar{\Lambda}\geq 0
(SM
III
).

Dissipation-time trade-off.—
Building on the connection to fluctuation theorems, we can derive statistical mechanical speed limits. Consider an ensemble of deterministic trajectories evolving over a time interval
\Delta t=t-t_{0}
.
What is the minimum time for the dissipation to reach a threshold value?
To find this first passage time, we define the survival factor as the ensemble average of
\Gamma
over the entire phase space:

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma(t,t_{0})\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t)}{\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t_{0})}\right\rangle=\left\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2\Lambda/ndt^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

(6)

Geometrically, the ratio in
\langle\Gamma\rangle
is the squared radius of a phase space hypersphere at a time
t_{0}
and at a later time
t
when the volume has been compressed by the flow.
Time integrating, we can define the mean first passage time of this dissipative process:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2\Lambda/ndt^{\prime}}\right\rangle dt\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2\langle\Lambda\rangle/ndt^{\prime}}.

(7)

Jensen’s inequality gives a lower bound on the this time, which is the mean time for dissipation to attain a threshold value in a time-interval
\Delta t
.

For time intervals that are short enough to treat
\langle\Lambda\rangle
as constant, this lower bound simplifies to
n/2\langle\Lambda\rangle=nk_{B}/2\dot{S}_{G}
.
Within this approximation, there is a tradeoff relation between the Gibbs entropy rate and the mean first passage time:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{G}\mathcal{T}\geq\frac{1}{2}nk_{B}.

(8)

During a nonequilibrium process, the minimum Gibbs entropy produced in a time
\mathcal{T}
is
nk_{B}/2\mathcal{T}
.
The bound saturates for the systems with constant
\Lambda
(e.g., the damped harmonic oscillator and the Lorenz-Fetter model
Lorenz 1963
).
A stronger form of the bound is
\dot{S}_{G}\mathcal{T}\geq nk_{B}
. This form is similar to the dissipation-time tradeoff for purely stochastic processes
Falasco and Esposito 2020
.
However, Eq.
8
applies to mechanical systems and explicitly includes the dimensionality of the phase space
n
, ensuring the entropy production rate is intensive in both time and system size.
Both inequalities are in terms of an intrinsic timescale
\mathcal{T}
and only valid for short time intervals in which the dissipation is time independent.
Because
\Lambda
is generally a function of time, so we derive a speed limit on the entropy production more akin to the Margolus-Levitin bound.

Classical speed limit on entropy production.–

Another approach is to instead consider time intervals
\Delta t
(as measured by an external clock) in which the system produces more entropy than it consumes by a factor,
\eta:=P_{-}(\Delta t)/P_{+}(\Delta t)
.
That is, we can use the integrated fluctuation theorem as a condition for the statistical distinguishability of states.
The ratio of total probabilities of negative and positive values of
\bar{\Lambda}
in a time interval
\Delta t
across the phase space is the ensemble average of Eq.
4
:

\displaystyle\frac{P_{-}(\Delta t)}{P_{+}(\Delta t)}=\left\langle e^{\Delta t\bar{\Lambda}}\right\rangle_{\bar{\Lambda}<0}=:\eta.

(9)

This choice of the value
\eta
defines a statistical distinguishability criterion based upon how much more likely a set of trajectory segments is to produce than consume entropy.
Like Eq.
4
, this integrated fluctuation theorem measures second law violations of a small system over a short time interval
t
.
From this fluctuation theorem, we can derive a classical speed limit on the Gibbs entropy rate using
\langle\Gamma\rangle
from Eq.
6
and Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle{\langle\Gamma(t,t_{0})\rangle}\geq e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2\langle\Lambda\rangle/ndt^{\prime}}.

(10)

Applying the bilinear approximation as before, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t_{\eta}\overline{\langle\Lambda\rangle}}{n}\geq\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta},

(11)

where
\overline{\langle\Lambda\rangle}=-\Delta t_{\eta}^{-1}\int dt\langle\Lambda\rangle\geq 0
.

This last inequality can be expressed in terms of the entropy production rate using Eq.
1
. For simplicity, we take
\eta=0
and express this result:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{s}_{i}\geq k_{B},

(12)

in terms of the time-averaged entropy production rate per degree of freedom,
\bar{s}_{i}=\overline{\langle\Lambda\rangle}/n\geq 0
, in transitioning between distinguishable states.
The Gibbs entropy rate is positive for strongly chaotic and dissipative dynamical systems with no vanishing Lyapunov exponents
Ruelle 1996
;
Ruelle 1997
.
Rearranging, the entropy production rate per phase space dimension sets the speed limit on second law “violating” trajectory segments.
Importantly, this speed limit explicitly accounts for the system-size extensivity of the entropy rate
Cohen and Rondoni 1998
.
Assuming
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\dot{S}_{G}}/n
is well-defined in the thermodynamic limit, this is a bound on dissipation of physical systems transitioning between macroscopic states in finite time.
However, the speed limit has no restriction on the number of zero Lyapunov exponents; the rate
\Lambda(t)
is the sum over all instantaneous Lyapunov exponents,
\Lambda(t)=\sum_{i}^{n}\lambda_{i}(t)
.
In fact, we can simply restrict the sum to positive exponents, in which case the speed limit is instead set by the finite-time KS entropy
Green et al. 2013
, another signature of deterministic chaos
\Delta t_{\eta}h_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{KS}}\geq 1
.

Classical speed limit on energy dissipation in deterministically thermostatted dynamical systems.—
The bounds above holds for any dynamical system, and they take on particular significance in their connection to nonequilibrium thermodynamics.
Dynamics subject to deterministic thermostats are models of nonequilibrium processes with expressions for the entropy production in terms of microscopic dynamical properties
Jepps and Rondoni 2010
;
Hoover and Hoover 2020
, providing a direct connection between Newtonian single-particle dynamics and Gibbs’ statistical ensembles
Hoover and Hoover 2020
.
Deterministic thermostats are also often used to investigate nonequilibrium steady states
Evans and Morriss 2008
.

For physical systems interacting with deterministic thermostats (e.g., Gaussian, Nosé-Hoover), the negative Gibbs entropy rate is identified as the thermodynamic entropy production rate
Hoover et al. 2016b
;
Hoover et al. 2016a
;
Ramshaw 2017
;
Hoover and Hoover 2020
;
Ramshaw 2020
.
For time-reversible thermostats at temperature
T
, the thermodynamic entropy production rate of the system is
{\dot{S}_{i}/k_{B}:=-\dot{S}_{G}/k_{B}=-\langle\Lambda\rangle=-\dot{\mathcal{Q}}/k_{B}T}
, where energy is dissipated as heat at a mean rate
\dot{\mathcal{Q}}
.
Using this identity, transforms the speed limit on the Gibbs entropy rate to a bound:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\eta}\overline{q}\geq k_{B}T,

(13)

on the time to transition between distinguishable states in terms of the time-averaged rate of heat loss/gain for the system,
\bar{q}=(n\Delta t_{\eta})^{-1}\int dt\dot{\mathcal{Q}}
, and the thermostat temperature.

Bounds on transport coefficients and intrinsic timescales.—

For specific models, the Gibbs entropy rate is directly related to transport coefficients.
For example, a harmonic oscillator damped with
\gamma=\kappa/m\geq 0
and friction coefficient
\kappa
has a contraction rate
\Lambda=\gradient\cdot\dot{\bm{x}}=-\gamma
and survival factor
\Gamma=e^{-2\gamma\Delta t_{\eta}/n}
.
For this model, the inequality in Eq.
3
becomes
\Delta t_{\eta}\geq 2/\gamma
.
Decreasing the friction coefficient or increasing the mass,
m
, of the oscillator, slows down the rate of dissipation and increases the time to dissipate a given amount.

For the Gaussian and Nosé-Hoover thermostats, the bound in Eq.
3
extends to transport coefficients because the electrical conductivity, diffusion, and viscosity are functions of
\langle\Lambda\rangle

Klages et al. 2000
;
Klages 2007
. As an example, consider the Lorentz gas, a system of moving charged particles (neglecting Coulomb interactions) in a two-dimensional array of fixed hard disk scatterers driven by an external electric field
\mathbf{E}
and thermostatted by a reservoir at temperature
T

van Beijeren and Dorfman 1995
.
The electrical conductivity is
\sigma(t)=T/\mathbf{|E|^{2}}\overline{\langle\Lambda(t)\rangle}
. From Eq.
11
, the electric field and temperature set the lower bound:
\Delta t_{\eta}\sigma(t)\geq nT/\mathbf{|E|^{2}}
.

Overall, the speed limits on dissipation here are statistical and mechanical, resolving the natural tension in irreversible processes between the entropy produced and the minimum time to produce it.
They are a quantitative statement of this relationship expressing the fact that any increase in the entropy production necessarily comes with a decrease in the minimum time to evolve between two distinguishable physical states.
These results bridge the deterministic, potentially chaotic, dynamics of physical systems, avoiding any hypotheses about chaos and instead making measures of chaos explicit.

Equation of motion for the trace of the density matrix

A perturbation vector in the tangent space of a classical tranjectory evolves according to:

d_{t}\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}={\bm{A}}[\bm{x}(t)]\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}.

(1)

For many-body systems, these vectors are perturbations to positions and momenta
\delta\bm{x}_{i}=(\delta\bm{q}^{i},\delta\bm{p}_{i})
.
The stability matrix,
{\bm{A}}:={\bm{A}}[\bm{x}(t)]=\gradient\bm{F}
generating these dynamics has the elements
({\bm{A}})^{i}_{j}=\partial\dot{x}^{i}(t)/\partial x^{j}(t)
.
Dirac’s notation here represents a finite-dimensional vector (its transpose) with the ket (bra):
\ket{\delta\bx(t)}:=[\delta x^{1}(t),\delta x^{2}(t),\ldots,\delta x^{n}(t)]^{\top}\in T\mathcal{M}
. Solving the equation of motion
Pikovsky and Politi 2016
:

\displaystyle\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{+}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\bm{A}}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}}\ket{\delta\bx_i(t_0)},

(2)

gives the perturbation vector at a time
t
(with the time ordering operator
\mathcal{T}_{+}
because stability matrices at two times do not generally commute).

To span the volume
\delta\mathcal{V}
, we can choose a complete set of
n
linear independent unit vectors
\{\ket{\delta\bphi_i}
} and
normalize them
\delta{\bm{\phi}}_{i}=\delta\bm{x}_{i}/\|\delta\bm{x}_{i}\|
so that the outer product defines a classical density matrix,
{\bm{\xi}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\outerproduct{\delta\bphi_i}{\delta\bphi_i}
. As the basis vectors form complete set of independent vectors, the determinant of
{\bm{\xi}}
gives the volume
\delta\mathcal{V}
span by them in the phase space.

In our previous work, see Ref.
Das and Green 2022
, we have shown that the determinant of
{\bm{\xi}}
denoted by
|{\bm{\xi}}|
has the following equation of motion:

\displaystyle d_{t}\ln|{\bm{\xi}}|=2\Tr{\bm{A}}=2\Lambda.

(3)

This gives rate of change of phase space volume under any non-conservative dynamics.
The evolution of its trace
\Tr{\bm{\xi}}
is governed by:

\displaystyle d_{t}\ln\Tr{\bm{\xi}}=2\Lambda/n.

(4)

For a single tangent vector
\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}
, the trace
\Tr{\bm{\xi}}_{i}(t)=\bra{\delta\bx_i(t)}\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}
gives the rate at which the perturbation represented by the vector
\ket{\delta\bx_i(t)}
evolves in time.

The solution to Eq.
4
reads:

\displaystyle\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t)=\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t_{0})=e^{2n^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Lambda(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}.

(5)

We use this solution to define the survival factor
\Gamma(t,t_{0})=\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t_{0})/\Tr{\bm{\xi}}(t_{0})
which leads to the dissipation-time bound in the main text.

III
Derivation of the speed limit on dissipation

Consider the survival factor,

\displaystyle\Gamma(t,t_{0})=e^{2\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\Lambda/n}.

(9)

Let
\Delta t_{\eta}
is the time interval in which
\Gamma
reduces by a factor of
0\leq\eta<1
. This leads to

\displaystyle\eta=e^{2\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\Lambda/n}.

(10)

Applying a bilinear approximation of
e^{2x}
for
x\leq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta\geq\frac{1-\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{\Lambda}/n}{1+\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{\Lambda}/n},

(11)

where
\bar{\Lambda}=-\Delta t_{\eta}^{-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\Lambda
. Rearranging the inequality,

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{\Lambda}/n\geq\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}.

(12)

A similar relation can be found if we consider the expansion of phase volume with
\bar{\Lambda}\geq 0
.
In dissipative systems, there may be time intervals in which
\Gamma
increases implying that
\eta\geq 1
. The bound in Eq.
5
(main text) then takes the following form:

\displaystyle\quad\Delta t_{\eta}\bar{\Lambda}/n\geq\frac{\eta-1}{\eta+1};\quad(0\leq\bar{\Lambda}<1).

(13)

This inequality holds for small average expansion rates,
0<\bar{\Lambda}<1
.

IV
Singly-thermostatted harmonic oscillator: The 0532 model

This recently introduced system belongs to the family of Nosé-Hoover thermostats. It is one of the simplest example to investigate thermodynamic dissipation in the thermostatted dynamics of the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator. The system is given by oscillator coordinate
q
, velocity
p
, and friction coefficient
\zeta
at a thermostat temperature
T
:

\displaystyle\dot{q}

\displaystyle=p,\quad\dot{p}=-q-\zeta\left(\alpha p+\frac{\beta p^{3}}{T}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\frac{p^{2}}{T}-1\right)+\beta\left(\frac{p^{4}}{T^{2}}-\frac{3p^{2}}{T}\right),

with parameters,
\alpha
and
\beta
, set to
0.05
and
0.32
. These equations impose a
weak
control of the second and fourth moments of
p
. The control variable
\zeta
is responsible for stabilizing the kinetic energy of the system.

The system is deterministic, time-reversible, and has been shown to be ergodic through extensive numerical tests. At equilibrium
Patra et al. 2016
;
Hoover et al. 2016a
the model reproduces the Gibbs distribution
f(q,p,\zeta)\propto e^{-q^{2}/2T}e^{-p^{2}/2T}e^{-\zeta^{2}/2T}
in units with
k_{B}=1
. Moreover, in these ergodic thermostats, the time-averaged heat current
\overline{p^{2}}/2
measured directly from the model has been found to be in excellent agreement with the Green-Kubo linear-response theory
Patra et al. 2016
.

To study thermodynamic entropy production, a localized temperature gradient can be introduced using a smooth temperature profile
T=1+\epsilon\tanh(q)
with
0\leq\epsilon<1
. The localized temperature within the system thus varies between
1-\epsilon
and
1+\epsilon
:

\displaystyle T(-\infty)=1-\epsilon\leq T(q)\leq 1+\epsilon=T(+\infty).

(14)

For a nonzero value of
\epsilon
, the system is out of equilibrium. The temperature gradient
dT/dq=\epsilon/\cosh^{2}(q)
generates a heat transfer from (to) the system to (from) the thermostat making the dynamics dissipative and irreversible.

The phase space volume contraction rate
\Lambda
is given by:

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{\partial\dot{q}}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial\dot{p}}{\partial p}+\frac{\partial\dot{\zeta}}{\partial\zeta}=-\zeta\left(\alpha+\frac{3\beta p^{2}}{T}\right).

(15)

At any time
t
, the instantaneous rate of heat loss/gain
\dot{h}
by the system can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{h}=q\dot{q}+p\dot{p}=-\zeta\left(\alpha p^{2}+\frac{\beta p^{4}}{T}\right).

(16)

To fix the kinetic energy, the time average of the square of thermostatted variable,
\bar{\zeta}^{2}
needs to remain a constant. This means that
\dot{\zeta}
must vanish at all times and we can write:

\displaystyle\zeta\dot{\zeta}=\zeta\alpha\left(\frac{p^{2}}{T}-1\right)+\zeta\beta\left(\frac{p^{4}}{T^{2}}-\frac{3p^{2}}{T}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

We can rearrange this equation to find:

\displaystyle-\zeta\left(\alpha+\frac{3\beta p^{2}}{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\zeta}{T}\left(\alpha p^{2}+\frac{\beta p^{4}}{T}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\Lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{h}}{T},

(17)

from Eqs.
15
and
16
. This shows that the local dissipation rate equals the instantaneous rate of heat loss divided by the thermostat temperature at any time
t
.
We now take ensemble average both sides of Eq.
17
and identify
\langle\Lambda\rangle
as the Gibbs entropy rate
\dot{S}_{G}
to find the following identities:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{G}=\langle\Lambda\rangle=\frac{\dot{\mathcal{Q}}}{T},

(18)

where
\dot{\mathcal{Q}}=\langle\dot{h}\rangle
. These ensemble average can be replaced by time averages as the system is ergodic. The Gibbs entropy rate gives the thermodynamic entropy production rate in thermostatted Hamiltonian systems of Nosé-Hoover and Gaussian types.

. We plot them on the

-\bar{\Lambda}

, which consists of trajectory segments with nearly equal
\bar{\Lambda}
