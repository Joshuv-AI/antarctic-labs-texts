Fixing the Kawarabayashi-Thomas-Wollan Flat Wall
Dan Arnon

Abstract
Recent papers by Kawarabayashi, Thomas and Wollan ( [ 1 , 2 ] ) provide major improvements over Robertson and Seymour’s original proof of the structure theorem for finite graphs that exclude a given graph ( [ 4 ] ). This structure theorem constitutes a central step in the proof of the Wagner Conjecture. The new papers provide a significant reduction of the size bounds in the theorem as well as providing a simpler, shorter and more accessible proof. The first paper [ 1 ] gives a new proof of the Flat Wall Theorem, a central stepping stone to the proof of the structure theorem itself in [ 2 ] . More than that, the paper redefines an important notion, that of a flat wall . Unfortunately, this new notion is too strong. As a result, the new Flat Wall Theorem (Theorem 5.2 in [ 1 ] ) is incorrect. I give a counterexample in Appendix A . A follow-on lemma in the same paper (Lemma 6.1 in [ 1 ] , about the transitivity of flatness) is also incorrect, a fact that was noticed by Dimitrios Thilikos et al in [ 5 ] . However, those authors appear to have missed the main issue, which is Theorem 5.2. Nevertheless, their notion of a tight rendition is a crucial ingredient of the fix to the main problem as presented here. This paper provides a weaker definition of the notion of a flat wall, provides a correction to the proof of the Flat Wall Theorem and a new proof of flatness transitivity. The notion of a tight rendition as presented here differs a little from [ 5 ] but is defined much more simply, and the notion of a proper cycle is introduced. The notions of certificates and tilted walls in [ 5 ] turn out of be unnecessary and transitivity is preserved in its original simplicity and generality. Most importantly, it looks like the new weaker definition of flatness is all that is really necessary to carry through the structure theorem in [ 2 ] .

1.1
Loops in
\mathbb{S}^{2}

We start with some terminology and basic facts about subsets of the unit 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}

\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
We refer to them as

A loop

\Delta_{L}^{1}

Given two loops

L

L^{\prime}

or
L\subset\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1}
.
Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be non-crossing. Without loss of generality, we can assume
L\subset\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{0}
.
Since
\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{0}
is simply connected, we have one region of
L
that is contained in
\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{0}
. Say
\Delta_{L}^{0}\subseteq\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{0}
.

The converse is also true. If
\Delta_{L}^{i}\subset\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{j}
for some
i,j\in\{0,1\}
then
L\subset\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{j}
and so

and
L^{\prime}
are non-crossing.
Since
\Delta_{L}^{i}\subset\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{j}
implies
\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-j}\subset\Delta_{L}^{1-i}
, the non-crossing relationship is symmetric.

Lemma 1
.

Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be non-crossing and let
i,i^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
. Then either:

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{i}\subseteq\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad\text{or}\quad\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}\subseteq\Delta_{L}^{i}\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{i}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}\subseteq L\cap L^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\text{or}\quad\Delta_{L}^{i}\cup\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}=\mathbb{S}^{2}

Proof.

Since
L
and
L^{\prime}
are non-crossing, we have some
j,k\in\{0,1\}
such that
\Delta_{L}^{j}\subseteq\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{k}
.
It follows that
\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-k}\subseteq\Delta_{L}^{1-j}
.

If
j=i
and
k=i^{\prime}
we are done due to the first inclusion, and if
j\neq i
and
k\neq i^{\prime}
we are done due to the second inclusion. If
j=i
and
k\neq i^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{i}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{L}^{j}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-k}

\displaystyle\subseteq\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{k}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-k}=L^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{i}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{L}^{j}\cap\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-k}

\displaystyle\subseteq\Delta_{L}^{j}\cap\Delta_{L}^{1-j}=L

Finally if
j\neq i
and
k=i^{\prime}
then

\Delta_{L}^{i}\cup\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}=\Delta_{L}^{1-j}\cup\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{k}\supseteq\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{1-k}\cup\Delta_{L^{\prime}}^{k}=\mathbb{S}^{2}

Societies and their renditions

Let

a set of vertices of

Societies arise naturally when we try to draw graphs on two dimensional surfaces with a connected boundary (i.e. surfaces from which the interior of a closed disk has been removed),

Let

A

such that

•

.
As we saw, the orientation of

in

•

•

is a function assigning a subgraph

of

, to each cell

,
such that

–

–

, and

–

]

1.3
Graph terminology vs. topological terminology

Like tree decompositions, the power of renditions comes from the interplay they engender between the structure of the underlying graph and the geometry of the rendition.
To minimize confusion, I try to use separate terminologies for graph terms and the rendition-related topological terms.

•

A subset of a disk homeomorphic to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
will be referred to as a
loop
(the usual term is
simple loop
, but all the loops here will be simple).
A cycle in a graph will be referred to as a
cycle
.

•

Loops in an oriented disk will be
oriented
and have
(clockwise) orientations
. Directed cycles in a graph will be
directed
and have
(clockwise) directions
.

•

Graphs have
vertices
and renditions have

When there is no risk of confusion, I will use the term

to refer to a graph vertex that is associated to a node through the
\pi
-mapping,
and often ignore
\pi

\mathcal{N}\subseteq V(G)
and
\pi
is the identity.

•

If
H
is a subgraph of
G
, the nodes of
H
are denoted
N(H)

N(H)=V(H)\cap\pi(\mathcal{N})=V(H)\cap\mathcal{N}

If

is a subset of
\Delta
, the nodes of

are denoted

. So

.

•

A subset of a disk

homeomorphic to the closed unit interval is usually referred to as a path in
\Delta
, but here will be referred to as a
segment
. The term

will be reserved to paths in a graph.
The interior of a segment in
\Delta
will be referred to as an

•

By abuse of notation, we will say that an edge (or vertex) of

resides in a cell if it belongs to the flap of that cell.

2.1
Fixing the definition of flatness

In
[
1
]
, the definition of the term

is too restrictive. It ignores the possibility that the induced graph

of the torso

is constructed. See Appendix

The following weaker definition seems to be sufficient for the purpose of
[

,
2
]

Let

; both ends of

and all of the internal vertices of

.
If

is a wall with boundary

that is obtained by an edge subdivision of an elementary wall

, then a subpath

of

is a peg interval of

.

.

Definition 9

Let

be a graph and

a wall with boundary

. We say that

of

2.

3.
