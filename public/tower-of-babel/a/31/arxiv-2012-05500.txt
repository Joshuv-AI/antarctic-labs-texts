Precise asymptotics on the Birkhoff sums for dynamical systems Thanks: *Corresponding author
Lulu Fang, Hiroki Takahasi, Yiwei Zhang*

Abstract
. We establish two precise asymptotic results on the Birkhoff sums for dynamical systems. These results are parallel to that on the arithmetic sums of independent and identically distributed random variables previously obtained by Hsu and Robbins, Erdős, Heyde. We apply our results to the Gauss map and obtain new precise asymptotics in the theorem of Lévy on the regular continued fraction expansion of irrational numbers in ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) .



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
37A44, 37A50, 60F10.

1.
Introduction

It is of general interest to investigate various probabilistic limit laws as stochastic properties for deterministic dynamical systems. The current paper is a contribution to this topic.
We establish
two precise asymptotics on the Birkhoff sums for
dynamical systems.
Let us first introduce its background from probability theory.

1.1.
Precise asymptotics for i.i.d. case

The concept of
precise asymptotics
was initially introduced by Hsu and Robbins

[
10
]
under the heading of complete convergence. Since then, an abundance of papers have appeared in the setting of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d. for short) random valuables, see a survey paper
[
15
]
for more detailed information. Meanwhile, the researches on precise asymptotic topics also turn out to be closely relevant to the deviation theory
[
25
]
, and have great applications ranging from stochastic volatility models
[
12
]
to statistical analysis
[
25
]
.

Let us briefly state the background on precise asymptotics for the i.i.d. case as follows. Denote by
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with
E|X_{i}|=0
, and write the arithmetic sum
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. The following theorem plays a fundamental role in precise asymptotics.
The first part
was obtained by Hsu and Robbins
[
10
]
, while the second part was obtained later by Erdős
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
10
,
11
]
)
.

If
EX_{1}^{2}<\infty
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|>n\varepsilon)<\infty.

(1.1)

Conversely, if the sum (
1.1
) is finite for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
EX_{1}^{2}<\infty
and the sum is finite for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Theorem
1.1
can be viewed as a refined result on the rate of convergence in the law of large numbers: not only the terms
\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|>n\varepsilon)
have to tend to 0 as
n
tends to infinity, but the sum of them has to converge, which contains more information.

By using more general results linking the integrability of the summands to the rate of convergence in the law of large numbers, a series of paper (see for examples Spitzer
[
28
]
, Katz
[
17
]
) pursued Theorem
1.1
further. Baum and Katz
[
5
]
provided necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of the series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r/p-2}\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|\geq\varepsilon n^{1/p})<\infty

for general
0<p<2
and
r\geq p
.

Another way to view these sums is to note that
\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|>n\varepsilon)
is non-decreasing, and tends to infinity as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
. It is therefore of interest to find the rate at which this occurs. This amounts to finding appropriate normalization of functions of
\varepsilon
that yield nontrivial limits. In this direction, Heyde
[
16
]
proved that

Theorem 1.2

(
[
16
]
)
.

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|\geq\varepsilon n)=EX^{2},

whenever
EX^{2}<\infty
.

Extensions of Theorem
1.2
for more general values of
r
and
p
have also been investigated in
[
9
,
13
,
29
]
. Such extensions include the rate estimations of

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{2(r-p)}{2-p}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r/p-2}\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|\geq\varepsilon n^{1/p})

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|\geq\varepsilon n^{1/p})

with
r\geq 2
and
0<p<2
. In the view of central limit theorem, there are no analogous result for
p=2
. However, there are further results by replacing
n^{1/p}
by

\sqrt{n\log n}
or
\sqrt{n\log\log n}
, see
[
14
]
.

1.2.
Statements of main results

As a comparison, we will adapt some of the precise asymptotic results
mentioned above to the dynamical systems setting where the independence is usually absent.
We consider an ergodic measure-preserving system

(X,T,m)

and a measurable observable
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
with
\int fdm=0
.
Put the Birkhoff sum

S_{n}f:=f+f\circ T+\cdots+f\circ T^{n-1}.

By the Birkhoff ergodic theorem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_{n}f(x)}{n}=0\ \ \ \ m\text{-a.e. }\,x\in X.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)=\Lambda_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)+\Lambda_{n}^{-}(\varepsilon)
, where

\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)=m\left\{x\in X:\frac{S_{n}f(x)}{n}\geq\varepsilon\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{-}_{n}(\varepsilon)=m\left\{x\in X:\frac{S_{n}f(x)}{n}\leq-\varepsilon\right\}.

Let
\Lambda(\varepsilon):=\Lambda^{+}(\varepsilon)+\Lambda^{-}(\varepsilon)
with

\Lambda^{+}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \Lambda^{-}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda^{-}_{n}(\varepsilon).

With these notations, the main theorems are as follows.

Theorem 1.3

(Main Theorem)
.

Suppose the following hypothesis hold:

•

CLT
: there exists
0<\sigma<\infty
such that

\frac{S_{n}f}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1).

•

LD
:
there exist constants
\delta>0
,
M>0
,
C>0

and a
C^{2}
function
I:(-\delta,\delta)\to[0,\infty)
such that the following holds:

–

I(0)=0
,
I^{\prime}(0)=0
,
I^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
.

–

for any
n\geq 1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta)
such that
\varepsilon>M/n
,

\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq Ce^{-I(\varepsilon)n}\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{-}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq Ce^{-I(-\varepsilon)n}.

Then we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\Lambda(\varepsilon)=\sigma^{2}.

(1.2)

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.3
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}=2.

Let us comment on the two key assumptions in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
CLT
requires that the limiting variance
\sigma
does not vanish, which is often assumed in previously known results on the central limit theorem. One way to verify the positivity
of the limiting variance is to use Livsič theorems on measurable rigidity (see e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
24
]
).
For example,
for expanding Markov interval maps with infinitely many branches
(see Section
3.1
for the definition)
this can be shown by the Livsič theorem of Aaronson and Denker
[
1
]
.
For these maps,
Morita
[
23
]
earlier verified
the positivity of the limiting variance and hence
CLT
for a large class of observables.

LD
is a refinement of large deviations
from the mean
0
. It implies that
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta)
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\Lambda_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)\leq I(\varepsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\Lambda_{n}^{-}(\varepsilon)\leq I(-\varepsilon).

(1.3)

There is a wealth of results
for various kinds of dynamical systems
which establish the existence of rate functions defined on a small neighborhood of the mean, and
replace the inequalities in (
1.3
) by equalities
using the rate functions. These large deviations results, as well as
(
1.3
)
take the limit
n\to\infty
, and therefore
never implies
LD
.

Stronger bounds than those in
LD
have been verified for a large class of uniformly hyperbolic dynamical systems.
For expanding Markov interval maps with finitely many branches
(see Section
3.1
for the definition)
and Hölder continuous observables with mean
0
,
Chazotttes and Collet
[
8
, Lemma A.1]

obtained such bounds under the assumption of
CLT
:
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,

\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)
is bounded from both sides by
constant multiples of
e^{-I(\varepsilon)n}/\sqrt{n}
.
These bounds are in agreement with the i.i.d. case in
[
4
, Theorem 1]
. Waddington
[
31
, Theorem 1]
obtained a corresponding result
for Anosov flows.
An important assumption in

[
8
,
31
]
is that the dynamical systems are modeled by topological Markov shifts over a finite alphabet.
In
[
30
]
,
LD
was shown to hold for the Gauss map, that is an expanding Markov interval map with infinitely many branches.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. Our strategy is to modify the proofs by Heyde
[
16
]
and Spătaru
[
29
]
in the i.i.d. case, by using
LD
and
CLT
to compensate the lack of independence.
One key step in their proofs
is to deduce an accurate upper bound estimation of
\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|>n\varepsilon)
. Two upper bound formulas were previously obtained in
[
16
, p. 175]
and

[
29
, Lemma 2]
. These formulas heavily rely on the independence, and
it is difficult to check their validity in the dynamical systems setting. Therefore, we put
LD
and
CLT
as assumptions, and deduce from them a new upper bound of
\mathbb{P}(|S_{n}|>n\varepsilon)
.

Section
3
provides applications to expanding Markov interval maps including the Gauss map.
We will apply Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
to this setting and obtain a new precise asymptotics in Lévy’s theorem
[
19
]
for the regular continued fraction expansion of irrational numbers in
(0,1)
,
see Theorem
3.2
.

2.
Proof of main results

This section is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
Let us begin with some useful lemmas. The first one is the classical Euler-Maclaurin formula, see Theorem 7.13 in
[
2
, p. 149]
.

Lemma 2.1

(the Euler-Maclaurin formula)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a<b
.
Assume that
f
has a continuous derivative
f^{\prime}
on
[a,b]
. Then we have

\sum^{b}_{n=a}f(n)=\int^{b}_{a}f(x)dx+\int^{b}_{a}f^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)dx+\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2},

where
\psi(x)=x-\lfloor x\rfloor-1/2
. Furthermore, if the improper integrals
\int^{\infty}_{a}f(x)dx
and
\int^{\infty}_{a}f^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)dx
are convergent and
f(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
, then

\sum^{\infty}_{n=a}f(n)=\int^{\infty}_{a}f(x)dx+\int^{\infty}_{a}f^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)dx+\frac{f(a)}{2}.

The second lemma is the Pólya theorem, see Theorem 9.1.4 in
[
3
, p. 290]
.

Lemma 2.2

(the Pólya theorem)
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables. Assume that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
F_{n}(x)\to F(x)
as
n\to\infty
, where
F_{n}
and
F
are distribution functions of
Y_{n}
and
Y
respectively. If
F
is a continuous function, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{n}(x)-F(x)\right|=0.

We denote by
\Phi(\cdot)
the distribution function of the standard normal random variable, namely

\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{x}_{-\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{-x}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt.

The following result gives the lower bound and the upper bound for
\Phi(x)
. See Lemma 6.1.6 in
[
6
, p. 162–163]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
x>0
,

\frac{x}{x^{2}+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\leq\Phi(-x)\leq\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We will first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\rho^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Let
\rho>0
be small. It follows from Lemma
2.3
that the improper integrals

\int^{\infty}_{0}\Phi(-\rho\sqrt{x})dx\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \int^{\infty}_{0}x^{-1/2}\Phi^{\prime}(-\rho\sqrt{x})\psi(x)dx

are convergent as
|\psi(x)|\leq 1/2
.
Since
\Phi(\cdot)
has continuous derivative on
\mathbb{R}
and
\Phi(-y)\to 0
as
y\to\infty
,
applying
f(x)=\Phi^{\prime}(-\rho\sqrt{x})
to Lemma
2.1
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)dx-\frac{\rho}{2}\int^{\infty}_{0}x^{-1/2}\Phi^{\prime}\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)\psi(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}.

(2.1)

From Lemma
2.3
, we see that
x\cdot\Phi(-\rho\sqrt{x})\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.
Applying the integral by path formula to the first term in the right-hand side of (
2.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)dx

\displaystyle=0+\int^{\infty}_{0}\Phi^{\prime}\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)\cdot\frac{\rho\sqrt{x}}{2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}x}{2}}\rho\sqrt{x}dx=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{2}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt=\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}.

For the second term in the right-hand side of (
2.1
), note that
|\psi(x)|\leq 1/2
, we have

\left|\frac{\rho}{2}\int^{\infty}_{0}x^{-1/2}\Phi^{\prime}\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)\psi(x)dx\right|\leq\int^{\infty}_{0}\Phi^{\prime}\left(-\rho\sqrt{x}\right)d(\rho\sqrt{x})=\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore,

\rho^{-2}/2\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}+1.

Multiplying
\rho^{2}
and letting
\rho\to 0
yields the desired equation.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
K>0
be fixed. Then

\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\ \rho^{2}\cdot\sum_{n\geq K/\rho^{2}}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)=0.

Proof.

We derive from Lemma
2.3
that

\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\rho\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{(-\rho^{2}n)/2}

and then

\rho^{2}\cdot\sum_{n\geq K/\rho^{2}}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{n\geq K/\rho^{2}}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{(-\rho^{2}n)/2}.

Note that

\int^{\infty}_{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}e^{-y^{2}/2}dy<\infty

and

\sum_{n\geq K/\rho^{2}}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{(-\rho^{2}n)/2}\leq C_{K}\int^{\infty}_{K/\rho^{2}}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{x}}\cdot e^{(-\rho^{2}x)/2}dx=\frac{C_{K}}{2}\cdot\rho\cdot\int^{\infty}_{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}e^{-y^{2}/2}dy,

where
C_{K}>0
is a constant depending on
K
. Hence we obtain

\rho^{2}\cdot\sum_{n\geq K/\rho^{2}}\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\rho\cdot\frac{C_{K}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}e^{-y^{2}/2}dy.

Putting
\rho\to 0^{+}
, the desired result follows.
∎

To complete the proof of
Theorem
1.3
, it suffices to show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\Lambda^{+}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\Lambda^{-}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}.

In what follows, we only prove the first equation since the second one can be obtained by means of similar arguments. To this end, we write

\varepsilon^{2}\Lambda^{+}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)-\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)\Big)+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right).

(2.2)

From Lemma
2.4
, it then follows that the second term on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) converges to
\sigma^{2}/2
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.
So we only need to prove that the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) tends to zero as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. We first treat

\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{K(\varepsilon)}\Big(\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)-\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)\Big),

where
K>8
is an integer and
K(\varepsilon):=\lfloor K/\varepsilon^{2}\rfloor
.
By
CLT
, we can put

\Delta_{n}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left|m\left\{x\in X:\frac{(S_{n}f)(x)}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\leq y\right\}-\Phi(y)\right|,

(2.3)

and it follows from Lemma
2.2
that
\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Combining this with the definition of
K(\varepsilon)
, we see that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{K(\varepsilon)}\Big|\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)-\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)\Big|\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{K(\varepsilon)}\Delta_{n}=0.

(2.4)

We are now in a position to show

\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n>K(\varepsilon)}\Big|\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)-\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)\Big|=0.

From
Lemma
2.5
, we have

\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n>K(\varepsilon)}\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)=0.

It remains to prove

\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n>K(\varepsilon)}\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)=0.

(2.5)

For
0<\varepsilon<1
and
K>M
, we have
K/\varepsilon^{2}>M/\varepsilon
. Let
n>M/\varepsilon
be fixed, then
M/n<\varepsilon
.
It follows from
LD
that
\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq Ce^{-I(\varepsilon)n},

which implies that

\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n>K(\varepsilon)}\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq C\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-e^{-I(\varepsilon)}}\cdot e^{-K\cdot I(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2}}.

(2.6)

LD
gives

I(\varepsilon)\to 0
,
I^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\to 0,

I^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon)\to I^{\prime\prime}(0)>0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Hence

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1-e^{-I(\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{I(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{I^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}>0.

(2.7)

Combining (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), we deduce that

\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n>K(\varepsilon)}\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq C\cdot\limsup_{K\to\infty}e^{-K\cdot\frac{I^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}}=0.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

It suffices to show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}=1.

(2.8)

Split

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}=I(\varepsilon)+I\!I(\varepsilon)-I\!I\!I(\varepsilon)+IV(\varepsilon),

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\sum^{L(\varepsilon)}_{n=1}\frac{1}{n}\big(\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)-\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)\big);

\displaystyle I\!I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n};

\displaystyle I\!I\!I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)}{n},

with
L(\varepsilon)=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-2}\rfloor
, and

\displaystyle IV(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Phi\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\right)}{n}.

In what follows, we will deal with
these four terms one by one. The condition
CLT
will only be used for an estimation of
I(\varepsilon)
and
LD
will only be used for an estimate of
I\!I(\varepsilon)
. To be more specific,

For
I(\varepsilon)
, recall that
\Delta_{n}
was given in (
2.3
), and Lemma
2.2
yields

\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Thus

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\Delta_{k}}{k}=0.

So we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\big|I(\varepsilon)\big|}{-\log\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum^{L(\varepsilon)}_{n=1}\frac{\Delta_{n}}{n}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log L(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}\cdot\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\log L(\varepsilon)}\sum^{L(\varepsilon)}_{n=1}\frac{\Delta_{n}}{n}=0.

(2.11)

This means

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\big|I(\varepsilon)\big|}{-\log\varepsilon}=0.

For
I\!I(\varepsilon)
, let
0<\varepsilon<\min\{\delta,1,M^{-1}\}
. For any
n>L(\varepsilon)
,
it follows from
LD
that

\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq Ce^{-I(\varepsilon)n}<\frac{C}{I(\varepsilon)n},

which implies that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}I\!I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{\Lambda^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{C}{I(\varepsilon)}\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{C}{I(\varepsilon)L(\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{2C}{I^{\prime\prime}(0)}.

The last inequality is deduced from (
2.7
). Hence

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{I\!I(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}=0.

For
I\!I\!I(\varepsilon)
, let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
and put
\rho=\varepsilon/\sigma
. The upper bound in Lemma
2.3
gives

\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\rho\sqrt{n}}e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}n}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{2}{\rho^{3}n\sqrt{n}},

and hence

\displaystyle I\!I\!I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{\Phi\left(-\rho\sqrt{n}\right)}{n}\leq\frac{2}{\rho^{3}\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{L(\varepsilon)+1}}\sum_{n>L(\varepsilon)}\frac{1}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\rho^{3}\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{L(\varepsilon)\sqrt{L(\varepsilon)+1}}<\frac{2}{\rho^{3}\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot 2\varepsilon^{3}=\frac{4\sigma^{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Then we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{I\!I\!I(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}=0.

For
IV(\varepsilon)
, it follows from
[
29
, Proposition 1]
that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{IV(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}=1.

Combining these four estimates on
I(\varepsilon)-IV(\varepsilon)
and (
2.9
), we eventually obtain (
2.8
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.∎

3.
Expanding Markov interval maps and continued fractions

In this section we give applications of Theorem
1.3
and
Theorem
1.4
.

3.1.
Expanding Markov interval maps

Let
S
be a countable set and let
m
be the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

An expanding Markov interval map
is a map
T\colon\bigcup_{a\in S}\varDelta_{a}\to[0,1]
such that the following holds:

(a)

\{\varDelta_{a}\}_{a\in S}
is a family
of subintervals of
[0,1]
with pairwise disjoint interiors
such that
m\left([0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{a\in S}\varDelta_{a}\right)=0
.

(b)

For each
a\in S
,
T|_{\varDelta_{a}}
is a
C^{2}
diffeomorphism onto its image
with bounded derivatives.

(c)

T\varDelta_{a}\supset\varDelta_{b}

holds for all
a\in S
,
b\in S
.

(d)

There exist an integer
p\geq 1
and a constant
\lambda>1
such that

\inf_{a\in S}\inf_{x\in\varDelta_{a}}|(T^{p})^{\prime}x|\geq\lambda.

(e)

(Rényi’s condition)

\sup_{a\in S}\sup_{x\in\varDelta_{a}}\frac{|T^{\prime\prime}x|}{|T^{\prime}x|^{2}}<\infty.

An expanding Markov map
T

is said to be
with finitely many branches

if
S
is a finite set.
Otherwise it is said to be

with infinitely many branches
.

It is known as a folklore theorem originating in the 1950s that expanding Markov interval maps admit a unique invariant probability measure
\nu
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
m
, see for example
[
27
]
. Moreover,
\nu
is ergodic.

Let
T
be an expanding Markov interval map with finitely many branches.
From the result of Chazotttes and Collet
[
8
, Lemma A.1]
,
LD
holds
for a Hölder continuous observable
f
with
\int fd\nu=0

under the assumption of
CLT
.
It is well-known that
\sigma>0
holds if and only if
the cohomological equation
f=\psi\circ T-\psi+\int fd\nu
has no solution in
L^{2}(\nu)
.
Since
f
is Hölder continuous,
by the Livsič theorem
[
20
,
21
]
,
any solution of the cohomological equation
in
L^{2}(\nu)
has a version which is Hölder continuous. It follows that

\sigma=0
holds if and only if
f
is cohomologous to a constant.
In the case
f=\log|T^{\prime}|
,

\sigma=0
holds if and only if

\nu
is the measure of maximal entropy
[
7
]
.

For maps with infinitely many branches we have the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T
be an expanding Markov interval map with infinitely many branches, and let
\nu
be the
T
-invariant probability measure
that is ergodic and absolutely continuous with respect to
m
.
Assume
\int\log|T^{\prime}|d\nu<\infty
.
Then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}=2,

where

\Lambda_{n}=\left\{x\in(0,1)\colon\left|\frac{1}{n}\log|(T^{n})^{\prime}(x)|-\int\log|T^{\prime}|d\nu\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}.

Proof.

The
CLT
for
f=\log|T^{\prime}|-\int\log|T^{\prime}|d\nu
holds as a consequence of the result of Morita
[
23
, Theorem 4.1]
, or Aaronson and Denker
[
1
, Corollary 2.3]
.
The argument in the proof of
Theorem
3.2
below to show the
LD

for the Gauss map
works verbatim to show

LD
in this general setting.
Hence, Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

yield the desired equalities.
∎

3.2.
The Gauss map and continued fractions

An interesting example of an expanding Markov interval map with infinitely many branches is the Gauss map

G\colon x\in(0,1]\mapsto\frac{1}{x}-\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x}\right\rfloor\in[0,1).

Each
x\in(0,1)
admits a
continued fraction expansion
of the form

x=\dfrac{1}{a_{1}(x)+\dfrac{1}{a_{2}(x)+\ddots}}:=[a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),\cdots],

(3.1)

where
a_{n}(x)
are positive integers.
Such a representation of
x
can be generated by the Gauss map
G
, in the sense that

a_{1}(x)=\lfloor 1/x\rfloor
and
a_{n+1}(x)=a_{1}(G^{n}(x))
for all
n\geq 1
. For any
x\in(0,1)
, its continued fraction expansion is finite (i.e., there exists
k\geq 1
such that
G^{k}(x)=0
) if and only if
x
is rational. For any irrational number
x\in(0,1)
, we denote by

\frac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}=[a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),\cdots,a_{n}(x)]

the
n
th
convergent
of
x
, with
n\geq 1
and
p_{n}(x)
and
q_{n}(x)
are relatively prime. These convergents are rational numbers and give the best approximations to
x
among all the rational approximations with denominator up to
q_{n}
. Moreover, it is well known that

\frac{1}{2q_{n+1}^{2}(x)}\leq\left|x-\frac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{q_{n}^{2}(x)}.

(3.2)

In other words, the order of
q_{n}^{-2}(x)
dominates the speed of
p_{n}(x)/q_{n}(x)
approximation.
The result of Lévy
[
19
]
states that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log q_{n}(x)}{n}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{12\log 2}=:\gamma\quad\text{ $m$-a.e. $x\in(0,1)$.}

(3.3)

We obtain precise asymptotics on
q_{n}
beyond (
3.3
).
For
\varepsilon>0
and
n\geq 1
, put

\Gamma_{n}(\varepsilon)=m\left\{x\in(0,1):\left|\frac{\log q_{n}(x)}{n}-\gamma\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{-\log\varepsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{n}(\varepsilon)}{n}=2.

Proof.

We view
G
as a dynamical system acting on the set of irrational numbers in
(0,1)
.
Then
G
leaves invariant the Gauss measure

d\mu_{G}=\frac{1}{\log 2}\cdot\frac{dx}{1+x}.

By Lévy’s theorem and the ergodic theorem,

\int\log|G^{\prime}|d\mu_{G}=2\gamma.

We apply Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

to
(G,\mu_{G},\log|G^{\prime}|)
.

CLT
was established by Misevic̆ius
[
22
]
.
To verify
LD
we
introduce the Lyapunov spectrum
\alpha\in[2\log((\sqrt{5}+1)/2),\infty)\mapsto b(\alpha)\in[0,\infty)
by

b(\alpha)=\dim_{H}\left\{x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\colon\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|(G^{n})^{\prime}(x)|=\alpha\right\},

where
\dim_{H}
denotes the Hausdorff dimension on
[0,1]
.
The Lyapunov spectrum for the Gauss map was analyzed
by Kesseböhmer and Stratmann
[
18
]
, Pollicott and Weiss
[
26
]
.
It was shown to be analytic,
b(\alpha)=0
if and only if
\alpha=2\gamma
.
Using the Lyapunov spectrum, we
define
I\colon[2\log((\sqrt{5}+1)/2)-2\gamma,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
by

I(\varepsilon)=(\varepsilon+2\gamma)(1-b(\varepsilon+2\gamma)).

(3.4)

Then
I
is
C^{2}
(analytic) and
I(0)=0
,
I^{\prime}(0)=0
.
By these and
[
30
, Main Theorem]
,
the function
I
in (
3.4
) satisfies all the hypotheses in
LD
but

I^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
, which we now verify below.

Lemma 3.3
.

I^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
.

Proof.

A direct calculation gives

I^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon)=-2b^{\prime}(\varepsilon+2\gamma)-b^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon+2\gamma)(\varepsilon+2\gamma).

Substituting
\varepsilon=0
gives

I^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2b^{\prime\prime}(2\gamma)\gamma.

(3.5)

To evaluate
b^{\prime\prime}(2\gamma)
,
we introduce a pressure function
\beta\in(1/2,\infty)\mapsto P(\beta)

by

P(\beta)=\sup\left\{h(\nu)-\beta\int\log|G^{\prime}|d\nu\colon\nu\in\mathcal{M}(G),\ \int\log|G^{\prime}|d\nu<\infty\right\},

where
\mathcal{M}(G)
denotes the set of
G
-invariant Borel probabiity measures.
The pressure function is convex and analytic

[
18
,
26
]
.
For each
\alpha>2\log((\sqrt{5}+1)/2)
,
let
\beta(\alpha)
denote the solution of the equation
P^{\prime}(\beta)+\alpha=0.
We have

b(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}(P(\beta(\alpha))+\alpha\beta(\alpha)).

(3.6)

Differentiating (
3.6
) twice gives

b^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=-\frac{-\beta^{\prime}(\alpha)\alpha^{3}-2P(\beta(\alpha))\alpha}{\alpha^{4}}.

(3.7)

By the implicit function theorem
applied to the function
P^{\prime}(\beta)+\alpha
,
\alpha\mapsto\beta(\alpha)
is differentiable
and
\beta^{\prime}(\alpha)=-1/P^{\prime\prime}(\beta(\alpha))<0
.
Since
P(\beta(2\gamma))=0
, substituting
\alpha=2\gamma
into (
3.7
) we obtain

b^{\prime\prime}(2\gamma)=-\frac{-\beta^{\prime}(2\gamma)}{2\gamma}<0,

and therefore
I^{\prime\prime}(0)>0.

∎

Since the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\mu_{G}}{dm}
is bounded from above and zero,
and
\log q_{n}(x)/\log|(G^{n})^{\prime}x|
is uniformly bounded from above and zero over all
n
and

x
, Theorem
3.2
follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎
