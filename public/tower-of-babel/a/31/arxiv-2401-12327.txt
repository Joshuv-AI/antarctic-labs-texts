Streams and Graphs of Dynamical Systems.
Roberto De Leo, James A. Yorke

Abstract
While studying gradient dynamical systems, Morse introduced the idea of encoding the qualitative behavior of a dynamical system into a graph. Smale later refined Morse’s idea and extended it to Axiom-A diffeomorphisms on manifolds. In Smale’s vision, nodes are indecomposable closed invariant subsets of the non-wandering set with a dense orbit and there is an edge from node M M to node N N (we say that N N is downstream from M M ) if the unstable manifold of M M intersects the stable manifold of N N . Since then, the decomposition of the non-wandering set was studied in many other settings, while the edges component of Smale’s construction has been often overlooked. In the same years, more sophisticated generalizations of the non-wandering set, introduced by Birkhoff in 1920s, were elaborated first by Auslander in early 1960s, by Conley in early 1970s and later by Easton and other authors. In our language, each of these generalizations involves the introduction of a closed and transitive extension of the prolongational relation, that is closed but not transitive. In the present article, we develop a theory that generalizes at the same time both these lines of research. We study the general properties of closed transitive relations (which we call streams ) containing the space of orbits of a discrete-time or continuous-time semi-flow and we argue that these relations play a central role in the qualitative study of dynamical systems. All most studied concepts of recurrence currently in literature can be defined in terms of our streams. Finally, we show how to associate to each stream a graph encoding its qualitative properties. Our main general result is that each stream of a semi-flow with “compact dynamics” on a connected space has a connected graph. The range of semi-flows covered by our theorem goes from 1-dimensional discrete-time systems like the logistic map up to infinite-dimensional continuous-time systems like the semi-flow of quasilinear parabolic reaction-diffusion partial differential equations.

Introduction

This article is about the following two fundamental ingredients in the qualitative description of a dynamical system: the invariant sets of the system and the dynamical relations between them.
The foundational example we have in mind, introduced and studied by Smale in
[
39
]
, is the flow of a Morse gradient vector field on a compact manifold.
In this case, each invariant set is a fixed point and there are only finitely many of them.
Heteroclinic trajectories of the system determine a relation between invariant sets that we express as “being downstream from” and can be encoded into a graph.

We aim at providing a natural framework that unifies and widely generalizes the following two fundamental and seminal ideas and results on the subject.

1. Smale’s Graph of a Dynamical System.
In
[
40
]
, as a generalization of the example above, Smale introduced the concept of
graph of a dynamical system
and studied the case of Axiom-A diffeomorphisms on a compact manifold.
In that case, given
an Axiom-A diffeomorphism
F
, the nodes of the associated graph are (finitely many) closed, disjoint invariant subsets
N_{i}
of the non-wandering set
NW_{F}
such that: i)
F|_{N_{i}}
is topologically transitive; ii)
\cup N_{i}=NW_{F}
.
There is an edge from node
N_{1}
to node
N_{2}
if and only if the unstable manifold of
N_{1}
has non-empty intersection with the stable manifold of
N_{2}
.
In our terminology, we say that
N_{1}
is downstream from
N_{2}
.
This decomposition of
NW_{F}
in “nodes” was called by Smale its
spectral decomposition
and it has been widely generalized, e.g. to
maps on the interval
[
18
]
, homeomorphisms on compact
[
1
,
4
]
and non-compact
[
13
,
30
]
metric spaces and on non-metrizable spaces
[
36
]
.
On the contrary, the edges – and so the whole graph point of view – did not receive, in the authors knowledge, much attention by the dynamical system community,
with the following noticeable exceptions: an article by D. Mizin
[
32
]
(in Russian; see Chap. 9 in Osipenko’s book
[
37
]
for a presentation in English of Mizin’s results); several works by Fiedler and Rocha on the graphs of the semi-flows of some class of semi-linear parabolic PDEs
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
; two recent works by the present authors on the logistic map and the Lorenz system
[
16
,
17
]
.
The graph introduced by Mizin is the same we introduced independently in
[
16
]
.

2. Quasi-orders and Auslander’s generalized recurrence.
In
[
5
]
,
as a generalization of the concept of non-wandering point of a semi-flow
F
,
Auslander studied the smallest quasi-order (i.e. reflexive and transitive) relation
\succcurlyeq_{A}
that extends the orbit relation (i.e., for all
x
in the phase space,
x\succcurlyeq_{A}y
for all
y
in the orbit of
x
) and is topologically closed.
Auslander used
\succcurlyeq_{A}
to generalize the set
NW_{F}
as follows:
x
is a
generalized recurrent point
if and only if either
x
is a fixed point or there is a
y
such that
x\succcurlyeq_{A}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{A}x
.
Thanks to the transitivity of the relation, the set of all these generalized recurrent points has the nice property, similar to the spectral decomposition of Smale, to naturally decompose into the disjoint union of closed invariant sets (which we still call
nodes
).
Notice that nodes in Smale’s spectral decomposition (i.e. “non-wandering nodes”) can be sometimes not disjoint (e.g. see Example
4.2.9
and
4.2.10
) while nodes of Auslander’s generalized recurrent set, as mentioned above, are always disjoint.
More details, with a thorough discussion about relations and generalized recurrence, can be found in a recent work by Akin and Auslander
[
2
]
.

In a nutshell, in this work we extend Auslander’s idea by considering
all
(rather than just the smallest one) closed quasi-orders that extend the orbit relation of
F
(we call these relations
streams
), and by showing that their qualitative features can be encoded into a graph, similarly to how Smale did for Axiom-A systems.
Each stream
S
has a set
{\cal R}_{S}
of
S
-recurrent points, defined in the same way we defined above the generalized recurrent points, and on
{\cal R}_{S}
is defined a natural equivalence relation that decomposes it into the disjoint union of closed forward-invariant sets.
We show that, besides Auslander’s generalized recurrent points, also Conley’s chain-recurrent points
[
11
]
and Easton’s strong-recurrent points
[
20
]
arise naturally as the sets of recurrent points of a stream, that we call respectively the
chains
and the
summable chains
streams.
In particular, we show that several common properties of these sets derive from general properties of streams.
Finally, we extend Smale’s graph idea to streams by associating to each stream
S
a directed graph
\Gamma_{S}
as follows:
the nodes of
\Gamma_{S}
are the equivalence classes of the recurrent points of
S
and there is an edge from node
N_{1}
to node
N_{2}
if and only if
N_{1}\succcurlyeq_{S}N_{2}
(Defs.
5.2.1
and
5.2.2
).

We point out that some authors already used similar relations in the study of general properties of dynamical systems.
For instance Akin, inspired by
[
5
]
, based his monography on topological dynamical systems
[
1
]
on streams (although he does not use this terminology).
Duarte and Torres used similar relations in the framework of set-valued dynamical system
[
19
]
.
More recently, Akin and Auslander used streams (again, not with this terminology) in their work on compactification of locally compact dynamical systems
[
2
]
.

Our main results are the following. Assume that the semi-flow
F
has a compact global trapping region (Definition
2.1.1
; notice that this condition is trivially satisfied when
X
is compact). Then:

1.

The graph of the prolongational relation of
F
is connected (Theorem
1
).

2.

The graph of each stream of
F
is connected (Theorem
2
).

3.

Given any stream
S
of
F
, for each node
N
there is at least a top node
T
(i.e.
T
has no incoming edges) and a bottom node
B
(i.e.
B
has no outgoing edges) such that
T\succcurlyeq_{S}N\succcurlyeq_{S}B
(Theorem
3
).

4.

The nodes and graph of the chains stream of a continuous-time semi-flow coincide with those of its time-1 map (Theorem
4
).
This extends a result by Hurley
[
27
]
that the set of the recurrent points of the two systems coincide.

5.

If the smallest (i.e. Auslander’s) stream has at most countably many nodes, then the chains streams and Auslander’s stream (and so every stream in between, including all summable chains streams) are equal to each other (Theorem
5
).
In particular, their nodes and graphs are equal.

Moreover, in Section
4.4
we classify the prolongational graph of T-unimodal maps (Definition
4.4.1
), namely continuous unimodal maps without wandering intervals and without non-topological attracting periodic orbits.
This extends and clarifies some aspect of our classification of the graph of the logistic map in
[
16
]
and of the graph of T-unimodal maps with a chaotic attractor in
[
3
]
.

2
Setting, main definitions and tools

Throughout the article,
X
will denote a
metrizable
connected topological space.
We will usually denote by
x,y,z
points in
X
and by
d
any distance function compatible with the topology of
X
.

The main object of interest of this work is a discrete-time or continuous-time semi-flow, as defined below.

Definition 2.0.1
.

A
semi-flow
on a topological space
X
is a continuous map

F:{\mathbb{T}}\times X\to X
, where either
{\mathbb{T}}=0,1,2,\dots
(discrete time) or
{\mathbb{T}}=[0,\infty)
(continuous time), satisfying the following properties:

1.

F^{0}(x)=x\text{ for each }x\in X
;

2.

F^{t_{1}+t_{2}}(x)=F^{t_{2}}(F^{t_{1}}(x))\text{ for each }x\in X\text{ and }t_{1},t_{2}\geq 0
.

Notice that the discrete case consists in the iterations of the time-1 map
F^{1}
.
Discussions here will usually focus on discrete time semi-flows, and we leave it to the reader to adapt the notation to the continuous-time case.

Our motivational example is the (continuous-time) semi-flow induced by a (finite- or infinite-dimensional) ODE

\dot{x}=f(x),

whose solutions are unique and defined for all times
t\geq 0
.
In this case,
F^{t_{0}}(x_{0})
is defined as the value at time
t=t_{0}
of the solution of the ODE that passes through
x_{0}
at time
t=0
.

The following definitions are crucial for this article.

Definition 2.0.2
.

Given a semi-flow
F
, we write
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
if
y=F^{t}(x)
for some
t\geq 0
and we say that
y
is
F
-downstream from

x
.
We write
x\overset{F}{=}y
if
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{F}x
.
We call
orbit space
of
F
the set

\text{\bf\boldmath${\cal O}_{F}$}=\{(x,y):x\succcurlyeq_{F}y\},

namely the binary relation on
X
whose symbol is
\succcurlyeq_{F}
.
The orbit of any given point
x
under
F
is

\text{\bf\boldmath${\cal O}_{F}(x)$}=\{y:(x,y)\in{\cal O}_{F}\}.

In analogy with the orbit space, we call
limit space
of
F
the binary relation

\text{\bf\boldmath$\Omega_{F}$}=\{(x,y):\text{ there is }t_{n}\to\infty\text{ as }n\to\infty\text{ such that }F^{t_{n}}(x)\to y\}.

The limit set of a point
x
under
F
is the set

\text{\bf\boldmath$\Omega_{F}(x)$}=\{y:(x,y)\in\Omega_{F}\}.

We say that
x
is
fixed
for
F
, or that
x
is a
fixed-point
of
F
, if
{\cal O}_{F}(x)=\{x\}
; that
x
is
periodic
if either
x
is fixed or there is a
y\neq x
such that
x\overset{F}{=}y
; that
x
is
recurrent
(or, to emphasize the map,
F
-recurrent) if
x\in\Omega_{F}(x)
.
Finally, we say that a function
L\in C^{0}(X)
is a
Lyapunov function
for
F
if
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
implies
L(x)\geq L(y)
.

2.1
Compactness and Trapping regions

In this article, we focus on the case of semi-flows with “compact dynamics”.
By this, we mean semi-flows such that each of their orbit are contained within a compact set.
Of course we could just assume
X
to be compact.
Such a strong assumption, though, would rule out cases important in applications, as examples below will show.
In fact, even local compactness would be too strong because it would rule out infinite-dimensional dynamical systems (e.g. parabolic PDEs).
Hence, from now on, we do not make any extra assumption on
X
and we rather focus on the case when all orbits of
F
are attracted by some compact set
Q\subset X
, as illustrated below.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
Q\subset X
.
We say that
Q
is a
trapping region
for
F
if it is closed and forward-invariant under
F
.
Set
{N_{\varepsilon}(Q)}=\{y:d(y,Q)<\varepsilon\}
.
A trapping region
Q
is:

•

stable
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that

F^{t}(N_{\delta}(Q))\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Q)
for all
t\geq 0
;

•

uniformly asymptotically stable
if there is an
\eta>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,\eta)
, there is
\tau>0
such that

F^{t}(N_{\delta}(Q))\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Q)
for all
t\geq\tau
;

•

globally attracting

if, for every
x
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
T
such that
x\in N_{\varepsilon}(Q)
for all
t\geq T
.

We say that a trapping region
Q
is a
global trapping region
if it is globally attracting and uniformly asymptotically stable.
Finally, we say that
F
is a
semi-flow with compact dynamics
if
F
has a compact global trapping region.

Notice that
X
is, trivially, a global trapping region for every semi-flow on
X
.
Hence, when
X
is compact, each semi-flow on
X
has compact dynamics.
We leave to the reader the proof of the next statement.

Proposition 2.1.2
.

Let
F
be a semi-flow with compact dynamics and let
Q
be a compact global trapping region for
F
.
Then, for each
x
,
\Omega_{F}(x)
is a non-empty subset of
Q
.

Proposition 2.1.3
.

Let
F
be a semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Then, for every
x
, there is an
F
-recurrent point in
\Omega_{F}(x)
.

Proof.

Let
y\in\Omega_{F}(x)
.
If
y
is not
F
-recurrent, then
y\not\in\Omega_{F}(y)
and so
\Omega_{F}(y)
is a proper subset of
\Omega_{F}(x)
.
Let
\Lambda
be the set of all subsets of
\Omega_{F}(x)
that are the limit set of some point in
\Omega_{F}(x)
.
The collection of such sets is partially ordered by inclusion.
By Zorn’s lemma, there is a maximal ordered collection
{\cal C}
of these sets.
They are all compact, so their intersection
\Omega^{*}
is compact and non-empty.
Since the intersection of invariant sets is invariant, for each
z\in\Omega^{*}
we have that
\Omega_{F}(z)\subset\Omega^{*}
.
By maximality,
\Omega_{F}(z)
must be in
{\cal C}
and it cannot be strictly smaller than
\Omega^{*}
.
Hence,
z\in\Omega_{F}(z)=\Omega^{*}
.
∎

Finally, we present a technical lemma that will be relevant in Section
6.1
.

Definition 2.1.4
.

Given any set
A\subset X
, we use the notation

{\cal I}_{F}(A)=\bigcup_{t\in[0,\infty)}F^{t}(A).

The reader can verify the following:

Proposition 2.1.5
.

{\cal I}_{F}(A)
is the smallest
F
-invariant set containing
A
.

Lemma 2.1.6
.

Assume that
X
is completely metrizable and let
d
be a complete metric for
X
.
Let
F
have compact dynamics and let
Q
be a compact global trapping region for
F
.
Let
\eta>0
be small enough that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\eta)
, there exists
\tau_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

F^{t}(Q_{\varepsilon})\subset Q_{\varepsilon/2}
for all
t\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
.
Then

\bigcap_{\varepsilon\in(0,\eta)}{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})=Q.

In other words, for every
\delta>0
, there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})\subset Q_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Let
y\in\cap_{\varepsilon\in(0,\eta)}{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})\setminus Q
.
Then we can find sequences
z_{i}\in X
with
d(z_{i},Q)\to 0
and
t_{i}>0
such that
F^{t_{i}}(z_{i})=y
.
For each
z_{i}
there is at least a
q_{i}\in Q
such that
d(z_{i},Q)=d(z_{i},q_{i})
.
Since
Q
is compact, the
q_{i}
have a converging subsequence.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
q_{i}\to\bar{q}\in Q
.

Since
d(z_{i},q_{i})\to 0
and
q_{i}
converges, the sequence
z_{i}
is Cauchy and so it converges to
\bar{q}
.
If the sequence of the corresponding
t_{i}
were bounded, we could extract a converging subsequence
t_{i}\to\tau
and, by continuity,
F^{\tau}(\bar{q})=y
.
This is impossible because
Q
is forward-invariant, so necessarily
t_{i}\to\infty
.
Therefore, there are points
z_{i}
arbitrarily closed to
\bar{q}
whose orbit passes through
y
after an arbitrarily long time.

We show now that this leads to a contradiction.
Indeed, set an
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,\eta)
such that
\varepsilon_{0}<2d(y,Q)
.
Then
F^{t}(Q_{\varepsilon_{0}})\subset Q_{\varepsilon_{0}/2}
for all
t\geq\tau_{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
Now, take a
z_{i}
with
d(z_{i},\bar{q})<\varepsilon_{0}
such that
t_{i}>\tau_{\varepsilon_{0}}
(since
t_{i}\to\infty
, almost all
z_{i}
satisfy this condition).
Then
F^{t_{i}}(z_{i})\in Q_{\varepsilon/2}
, so that
d(F^{t_{i}}(z_{i}),Q)<\varepsilon_{0}/2<d(y,Q)
, against the assumption that
F^{t_{i}}(z_{i})=y
.
∎

While having compact dynamics is a quite restrictive condition, there are many important classes of semi-flows that satisfy it.
We present some of them in the examples below.

Example 2.1.7

(
Logistic map).

Consider the logistic map

\ell_{\mu}(x)=\mu x(1-x),

where
\mu\in(2,4)
and
X=(0,1)
, and set
c_{k}(\mu)=\ell^{k}_{\mu}(1/2)
.
Then
Q=[c_{2}(\mu),c_{1}(\mu)]
is a compact global trapping region for
\ell_{\mu}
.

Example 2.1.8

(
Lorenz system).

Consider the Lorenz ODE system in
X={\mathbb{R}}^{3}

\begin{cases}\dot{x}&=-\sigma x+\sigma y\\
\dot{y}&=-xz+rx-y\\
\dot{z}&=\phantom{+}xy-\beta z\end{cases}.

The convex function

L(x,y,z)=rx^{2}+\sigma y^{2}+\sigma(z-2r)^{2}

is a Lyapunov function for the flow
F^{t}
of the Lorenz system far enough from the origin.
More precisely,

\frac{d}{dt}L(F^{t}(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}))\Big|_{t=0}<0

for all
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
far enough from the origin.
Hence, every ball of large enough radius centered at the origin is a compact global trapping region for
F
.

Example 2.1.9

(
Reaction-Diffusion PDEs).

One of the simplest infinite-dimensional dynamical systems to which our machinery applies is the reaction diffusion scalar PDE

u_{t}=u_{xx}+f(x,u,\nabla u),\;u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),

where
x\in(0,1)
.
PDEs of this type arise from many phenomena in chemistry reactions, population dynamics, biophysics and more (see
[
23
]
for a large number of references).
It turns out that the most convenient setting for these PDEs is the functional space
X=L^{2}(0,1)
of all square-summable functions on the unit interval.
It can be proved, indeed, that the weak version of the PDE above admits a continuous semi-flow

F:{\mathbb{R}}\times L^{2}(0,1)\to L^{2}(0,1)
.
Moreover, for some choice of the right-hand-side
f
, the corresponding semi-flow
F
has a compact global trapping region.
This is the case, for instance, when
f(x,u,0)\cdot u<0
for large
|u|
and
f(x,u,p)
has subquadratic growth in
|p|
.
In this case, indeed, one can prove that there is a ball
Q
in the subset of
H^{1}_{0}
functions (namely
L^{2}
functions whose weak first derivative is also
L^{2}
and that are limit of continuous functions on
(0,1)
with compact support) such that, for each
x
, there is a
T
such that
F^{t}(x)\in Q
for each
t\geq T
.
Since
H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
is a compact subset of
L^{2}(0,1)
, then
Q
is compact as a subset of
L^{2}
(see
[
38
]
, Chapter 11).
Hence, Q is a compact global trapping region for this system.
The graphs of many of this type of PDEs are under investigation by Fedler and Rocha
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

Example 2.1.10

(
Navier-Stokes).

Consider the 2D Navier-Stokes PDEs on the 2-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
, given by

\begin{cases}\dot{u}-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=f&\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\end{cases},

where
\nu
is a positive parameter and the unknowns
u
and
p
are a square-integrable vector field and a square-integrable function on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
.
As in the example above, this PDE defines a continuous semi-flow on
X=L^{2}
and one can prove that there is a ball
Q
in the
H^{1}
norm that each trajectory
enters in finite time. Notice that a set that is bounded with respect to the
H^{1}
norm is compact in the
L^{2}
topology.
This ball
Q
is a compact trapping region for the semi-flow (see
[
38
]
, Chapter 12).

Example 2.1.11

(
Ricci flow).

V. and T. Ivancevic showed in
[
28
]
that every reaction-diffusion model in literature can be seen as a special case of the celebrated Ricci PDE on manifolds

\dot{g}=Ricci_{g},

where
g
is a smooth metric on a manifold
M
and
Ricci_{g}
is the corresponding Ricci manifold, a
(0,2)
tensor obtained as contraction of the curvature tensor of
g
.
This shows that Ricci flow has in many cases a compact global trapping region.

2.2
Chains

Since
X
is metrizable, given a semi-flow
F
we can use distances to define finite sequences of points called chains, as defined below.
Chains play a major role in defining the main types of recurrence.
Below we define chains only for discrete semi-flows.
In Section
6.1
we show that this is enough for our goals (see Theorem
4
).

Definition 2.2.1
.

A
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-bchain
(or simply
\varepsilon
-bchain
, when there is no ambiguity) from point
x
to point
y
of length
k
is a finite sequence
C
of
k
points
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
, with
k>1
, such that:

1.

d(z_{1},x)\leq\varepsilon
;

2.

d(z_{k},y)\leq\varepsilon
;

3.

d(F(z_{i}),z_{i+1})\leq\varepsilon
for
i=1,\dots,k-1
.

We say that an
\varepsilon
-bchain
C
is:

1.

an
\varepsilon
-chain
if
z_{1}=x
and
z_{k}=y
.

2.

an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha
chain
if
z_{k}=y
and
F(z_{i})=z_{i+1}
for all
i=2,\dots,k-1
.

3.

an
\varepsilon
-
\omega
chain
if
z_{1}=x
and
F(z_{i})=z_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\dots,k-2
.

4.

an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain
if
F(z_{i})=z_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\dots,k-2
.

Finally, we say that
C
is an
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-
\Sigma
chain
if

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}d(x_{i},F(x_{i}))<\varepsilon.

Sometimes, when
x=y
, we refer to an
\varepsilon
-chain as an
\varepsilon
-loop based at
x
.

In other words, an
\varepsilon
-bchain is like an
\varepsilon
-chain where it is allowed to make a jump
before
the first application of
F
; an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha
chain is an
\varepsilon
-bchain with a single jump, the very first one; an
\varepsilon
-
\omega
chain is an
\varepsilon
-bchain with a single jump, the very last one; an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain is an
\varepsilon
-bchain with just two jumps: the very first one and the very last one.

The concept of chains for maps goes back at least to Birkhoff
[
8
]
. To the authors’ knowledge, chains for (semi-)flows (see Definition
6.2.1
) were introduced by Conley
[
12
]
to define the chain-recurrent set.
Later Bowen
[
10
]
, while studying the shadowing phenomenon, considered also the case of
infinite chains and called them
pseudo-orbits
. Some authors still use the term chains for finite truncations of pseudo-orbits
[
31
,
7
]
.
Nowadays, though, the terms
chains
and
pseudo-orbits
are often used as synonyms
[
25
,
42
]
.
Finally,
\Sigma
chains were introduced by Easton, under the name of
strong chains
, in
[
20
]
.

As a first application of chains, we provide a characterization of recurrent points in metric spaces.
We leave its proof to the reader.

Proposition 2.2.2
.

A point
x
is recurrent under
F
if and only if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
\varepsilon
-
\omega
chain from
x
to itself.

3
Quasi-orders and “being downstream”

The relation
\succcurlyeq_{F}
is clearly reflexive and transitive.
It is therefore natural generalizing the concept of
being downstream

by considering the following class of relations.

Definition 3.0.1
.

A quasi-order on
X
is a binary relation
D\subset X\times X
that is reflexive and transitive, namely:

1.

(x,x)\in D
for every
x
;

2.

if
(x,y)\in D
and
(y,z)\in D
, then
(x,z)\in D
.

We sometimes use the notation
x\succcurlyeq_{D}y
to say that
(x,y)\in D
.
We call
\succcurlyeq_{D}
the symbol of
D
.
Then reflexivity means that
x\succcurlyeq_{D}x
for all
x
and transitivity that, if
x\succcurlyeq_{D}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{D}z
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{D}z
.

Example 3.0.2
.

The orbit space
{\cal O}_{F}
of a semi-flow
F
is a quasi-order on
X
.
The symbol
\succcurlyeq_{F}
introduced in Definition
2.0.2
is precisely the symbol of this quasi-order.

The following definition generalizes Definition
2.0.2
to quasi-orders.

Definition 3.0.3
.

Given a relation
D
, we use the notation

\Down_{D}(x)=\{y:x\succcurlyeq_{D}y\},\;\;\Up_{D}(y)=\{x:x\succcurlyeq_{D}y\},

and we say that the points of
\Down_{D}(x)
are
D
-downstream
from
x
(or simply
downstream
when there is no ambiguity) and points in
\Up_{D}(x)
are
D
-upstream
from
x
(or simply
upstream
when there is no ambiguity).
Finally, for a
M\subset X
we set

\Down_{D}(M)=\cup_{x\in M}\Down_{D}(x),\;\;\Up_{D}(M)=\cup_{x\in M}\Up_{D}(x).

We say that
M
is
D
-invariant
if
\Down_{D}(M)\subset M
.
We refer sometimes to the set
\Up_{D}(M)
as the
D
-basin
of
M
.

Proposition 3.0.4
.

Let
D
be a quasi-order. Then

\Down_{D}(\Down_{D}(x))=\Down_{D}(x)
and

\Up_{D}(\Up_{D}(x))=\Up_{D}(x)
for every
x
.
In particular, for every
M\subset X
,
\Down_{D}(M)
is
D
-invariant.

Proof.

It is an immediate consequence of transitivity.
∎

Notice that, in general, the set of all points downstream from a closed set of points is not closed.

Example 3.0.5
.

Let
F
be the flow of the vector field
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
on
R^{2}
.
Consider the quasi-order
{\cal O}_{F}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
and set
C=\{xy=-1:y>0\}
.
The set
C
is a closed subset of the plane but the sets

\Down_{{\cal O}_{F}}(C)=\{(x,y):y>0,xy\geq-1\},

\Up_{{\cal O}_{F}}(C)=\{(x,y):y>0,xy\leq-1\}

are not.

3.1
Closed quasi-orders and Lyapunov functions

It turns out that strong properties hold for closed quasi-orders, namely quasi-orders that are closed in the product topology of
X\times X
.

A first important property is that, when
D
is closed, the sets
\Down_{D}(C)
and
\Up_{D}(C)
are closed for every compact set
C
.

Proposition 3.1.1
.

If
K\subset X
is compact, then
\Down_{D}(K)
and
\Up_{D}(K)
are closed.
In particular, when
X
is compact, if
C
is closed then
\Down_{D}(C)
and
\Up_{D}(C)
are closed.

Proof.

Let
x_{n}\in\Up_{D}(K)
be a sequence converging to a point
\bar{x}\in X
.
Then there exists a sequence
y_{n}\in K
such that
(x_{n},y_{n})\in D
.
Since
K
is compact, there is a subsequence
y_{n_{k}}
converging to a
\bar{y}\in K
.
Hence, in
D
,
(x_{n_{k}},y_{n_{k}})\to(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.
Since
D
is closed,
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in D
, namely
\bar{x}\in\Up_{D}(K)
.
Hence,
\Up_{D}(K)
is closed.
Mutatis mutandis, the same argument works for
\Down_{D}(K)
.
∎

Notice that in the proof we never used the transitivity of the relation.
Hence, this result holds also for the prolongational relation (see Section
4
).

A second important property is that every closed quasi-order can be defined through a collection of continuous functions as described below.

Definition 3.1.2
.

A function
L\in C^{0}(X)
is a
Lyapunov function
for a quasi-order
D
if
x\succcurlyeq_{D}y
implies
L(x)\geq L(y)
.
We denote by
{\cal L}_{D}\subset C^{0}(X)
the set of all Lyapunov functions of
D
.

Notice that constant functions are Lyapunov for every quasi-order and that, if
L_{1},L_{2}\in{\cal L}_{D}
, then
a_{1}L_{1}+a_{2}L_{2}\in{\cal L}_{D}
for all
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 0
.
Hence, the proposition below follows.

Proposition 3.1.3
.

For any quasi-order
D
, the set
{\cal L}_{D}
is a non-empty closed convex cone.

Example 3.1.4
.

The set of Lyapunov functions of the quasi-order
{\cal O}_{F}
coincides with the set of Lyapunov functions of the semi-flow
F
.

One can also run things backward and use collections of functions to define quasi-orders, as illustrated below.

Given a collection
{\cal S}\subset C^{0}(X)
, we set

{\cal D}_{\cal S}=\{(x,y):L(x)\geq L(y)\text{ for all }L\in{\cal S}\}.

Notice that, if
C_{\cal S}
is the convex cone generated by
{\cal S}
(namely the smallest closed convex cone containing
{\cal S}
), then

{\cal D}_{\cal S}={\cal D}_{C_{\cal S}}
.

Proposition 3.1.5
.

The set
{\cal D}_{\cal S}
is a topologically closed quasi-order on
X
. Moreover, if
{\cal T}\subset{\cal S}
, then
{\cal D}_{\cal S}\subset{\cal D}_{\cal T}
.

We leave to the proof to the reader.

Example 3.1.6

(The largest and smallest quasi-order.)
.

{\cal D}_{\emptyset}=X\times X.

{\cal D}_{C^{0}(X)}=\emptyset.

Auslander showed that, when
X
is separable and locally compact (in particular, when
X
is compact), for every closed quasi-order
D
, the set
{\cal L}_{D}
contains enough functions to fully characterize
D
,
as illustrated below.
The proof, by Auslander, that we include for reader’s convenience, is a variant of the standard proof of Urhyson’s Lemma.

Lemma A

(Auslander, 1963
[
5
]
, Theorem 4)
.

Let
X
be separable and locally compact and let
D
be a closed quasi-order on
X
.
Then, if
x\not\succcurlyeq_{D}y
(equivalently, if
(x,y)\not\in D
), there exists a function
L\in{\cal L}_{D}
such that
L(x)<L(y)
.

Proof.

Assume first that
X
is compact.
We will show below that one can build an infinite countable family of closed sets
U_{r}
, labeled by rational (dyadic) numbers
r
in
[0,1]
such that:

1.

x\in\cap_{r}U_{r}
;

2.

y\not\in\cup_{r}U_{r}
;

3.

if
r<r^{\prime}
, then
U_{r}\subset interior(U_{r^{\prime}})
;

4.

U_{r}
is
D
-invariant, namely
\Down_{D}(U_{r})=U_{r}
.

With the sets
U_{r}
, one can define the function
L
in the proposition claim as follows:

L(z)=\inf\{r:z\in U_{r}\}.

A standard argument shows that
L
, by point (3) above, is continuous. Moreover:

•

L(x)=0
by point (1) above;

•

L(y)=1
by point (2) above;

•

if
z\succcurlyeq_{D}z^{\prime}
, then
L(z)\geq L(z^{\prime})
by points (3) and (4) above.

Now we will show how to build the sets
U_{r}
.
Let
A_{0}
and
B
disjoint closed neighborhoods of, respectively,
x
and
y
such that
\Down(A_{0})\cap B=\emptyset
.
Such neighborhoods exist because
D
is closed. Indeed, assume that there is no such neighborhoods
A_{0}
and
B
.
This means that there is a sequence
y_{i}
converging to
y
and
x_{i}
converging to
x
such that
x_{i}\succcurlyeq_{D}y_{i}
for all
i
.
Since
D
is closed, this implies that
x\succcurlyeq_{D}y
, against the hypotheses.

Set
U_{0}=\Down(A_{0})
.
Since
X
is compact,
U_{0}
is closed by Proposition
3.1.1
.
Moreover, we also have that
x\in U_{0}
,
\Down(U_{0})=U_{0}
and
U_{0}\cap B=\emptyset
.

Now, let
A_{\frac{1}{2}}
be a closed neighborhood of
U_{0}
such that
U_{0}\subset interior(A_{\frac{1}{2}})
and
\Down(A_{\frac{1}{2}})\cap B=\emptyset
.
As above, set
U_{\frac{1}{2}}=\Down(A_{\frac{1}{2}})
.
Then, by the same argument above,
U_{\frac{1}{2}}
is closed,
\Down(U_{\frac{1}{2}})=U_{\frac{1}{2}}
,
U_{\frac{1}{2}}\cap B=\emptyset
and
U_{0}\subset interior(U_{\frac{1}{2}})
.

By repeating this construction, we can build sets
U_{\frac{3}{4}},U_{\frac{7}{8}},\dots
.
To build
U_{\frac{1}{4}}
, we proceed as follows.
Let
B_{\frac{1}{2}}=closure(X\setminus U_{\frac{1}{2}})
.
Then
U_{0}\cap B_{\frac{1}{2}}=\emptyset
and we can repeat the first step of our construction with
B_{\frac{1}{2}}
in place of
B
.
We denote by
U_{\frac{1}{4}}
the compact set we get this way.
Notice that
U_{\frac{1}{4}}\subset interior(U_{\frac{1}{2}})
.
This way, we can build a set
U_{r}
for each dyadic
r
with the four properties listed above.

Consider now, the general case, when
X
is a non-compact, locally compact and separable space.
Recall that every separable metric space is Lindelof and that every locally compact Lindelof space is
hemicompact.
Hence, there are compact sets
X_{i}\subset X
,
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
X_{i}\subsetneq X_{i+1}
for all
i
and
X=\cup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{i}
.
Let
i_{0}
be any integer such that
x,y\in X_{i_{0}}
. Then, by the argument above, there is a Lyapunov function
L
for
D
in
X_{i_{0}}
.
In order to prove the theorem, it is enough to prove that we can extend
L
to a Lyapunov function for
D
on
X_{i_{0}+1}
. By induction,
indeed, this allows to extend
L
to the whole
X
.

We can accomplish this by noticing that closed neighborhoods
A^{\prime}_{0}
of
x
and
B^{\prime}
of
y
such that
\Down_{D}(A^{\prime}_{0})\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset

exist in
X
(notice, though, that in this case
A^{\prime}_{0}
is not necessarily compact and so
\Down_{D}(A^{\prime}_{0})
is not necessarily closed).
Hence, in
X_{i_{0}}
, we can elect to choose
A_{0}=A^{\prime}_{0}\cap X_{i_{0}}
and
B=B^{\prime}\cap X_{i_{0}}
. By repeating this argument at every step of the construction
of the sets
U_{r}
, we build a Lyapunov function
L_{i_{0}}
for
D
in
X_{i_{0}}
.
Then we can repeat the same construction in
X_{i_{0}+1}
by setting
A_{0}=A^{\prime}_{0}\cap X_{i_{0}+1}
and
B=B^{\prime}\cap X_{i_{0}+1}
and so on.
This way, we end up with a Lyapunov function
L_{i_{0}+1}
for
D
in
X_{i_{0}+1}
such that, by construction,

L_{i_{0}+1}|_{X_{i_{0}}}=L_{i_{0}}
.

∎

The following theorem, implicitly contained in
[
5
]
, shows that closed quasi-orders are completely determined by their set of Lyapunov functions.

Theorem A

(Auslander, 1963
[
5
]
)
.

Let
X
be separable and locally-compact and let
D
be a closed quasi-order on
X
.
Then

D={\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{D}}.

Proof.

Let
L\in{\cal L}_{D}
.
By definition,
L(x)\geq L(y)
for all pairs
(x,y)\in D
, so
D\subset{\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{D}}
.
By Lemma A above, if
(x,y)\not\in D
, there is some
L\in{\cal L}_{D}
such that
L(x)<L(y)
.
Hence
(x,y)\not\in{\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{D}}
, namely
{\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{D}}\subset D
.
∎

We conclude this discussion on general closed quasi-orders by showing that, for each relation, there is a smallest closed quasi-order that contains it.

Proposition 3.1.7
.

The set of all quasi-orders on
X
is closed under intersection.

Proof.

The intersection of closed sets is closed.
We leave to the reader to verify that
the intersection of two quasi-orders is a quasi-order.
∎

Definition 3.1.8
.

We say that a relation
R
extends a relation
R^{\prime}
if
R^{\prime}\subset R
.

Corollary 3.1.9
.

Given any relation
R
on
X
, there is a smallest closed quasi-order
D
on
X
extending
R
.

Proof.

The set
D
is the intersection of all closed quasi-orders that contain
R
.
This set is non-empty because
X\times X
is a closed quasi-order.
∎

4
Closing
{\cal O}_{F}
: the prolongational relation
{\cal P}_{F}
and the non-wandering set
NW_{F}
.

The orbit space
{\cal O}_{F}
is a quasi-order but, in general, is not closed.
Hence, in general,
{\cal O}_{F}
is strictly contained in
{\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{F}}
, that is the smallest closed quasi-order extension of
{\cal O}_{F}
.

Example 4.0.1
.

Let
F
be the flow of the ODE
x^{\prime}=1
on the real line.
Then
{\cal O}_{F}(x)=[x,+\infty)
and
{\cal O}_{F}=\{(x,y):y\geq x\}
.
In this case,
{\cal O}_{F}
is a closed quasi-order.

Example 4.0.2
.

Let
F
be the flow of the ODE
x^{\prime}=-\sin(\pi x)
on the
[0,1]
segment.
Then
{\cal O}_{F}(0)=\{0\}
,
{\cal O}_{F}(1)=\{1\}
and
{\cal O}_{F}(x)=(0,x]
for each
x\in(0,1)
(see Figure
8
, left).
Hence, in this case
{\cal O}_{F}
equals the closed triangle with vertices
(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)
minus the segments
(0,1]\times\{0\}
and
\{1\}\times[0,1)
.
In particular,
{\cal O}_{F}
is not closed.

Having a topologically closed relation is preferable – for instance, closedness is essential to prove all general theorems we present in this article.
A natural option is considering the relation obtained by taking the topological closure of
{\cal O}_{F}
.

4.1
The prolongational relation

Definition 4.1.1
.

We denote by
{{\cal P}}_{F}
the relation
\overline{{\cal O}_{F}}
, the topological closure of
{\cal O}_{F}
, and by
\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}
the corresponding symbol.
We call this relation the
prolongational relation
.

This relation was first introduced and studied by Auslander and Guerin
[
6
]
as a tool to study weak mixing in minimal flows.
By definition, a point belongs to
{{\cal P}}_{F}
if and only if there is a sequence
(x_{n},y_{n})\subset{\cal O}_{F}
such that
(x_{n},y_{n})\to(x,y)
in the product topology.
This means that, arbitrarily close to
x
and
y
, it is possible to find, respectively, points
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
such that
y^{\prime}\in{\cal O}_{F}(x^{\prime})
.
The reader can verify the following elementary fact.

Proposition 4.1.2
.

Let
d
be any metric on
X
compatible with the topology of
X
.
Then
(x,y)\in{{\cal P}}_{F}
if and only if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
y
.

The set
\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
coincides with the so-called
first prolongation
of
x

[
43
,
5
]
,
which justifies the name
prolongational relation
for
{\cal P}_{F}
.
Notice that, since
{{\cal P}}_{F}
is not transitive, in general
\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}\left(\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)\right)
is strictly larger than
\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
.

Example 4.1.3
.

Let
X=[0,2]
and let
F
be the flow of the ODE

x^{\prime}=|\sin(\pi x)|.

Notice that
\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(1)=[1,2]
and, for each
x\in(0,1)
,

\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\{1\}.

Hence, for each
x\in(0,1)
,

\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}\left(\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)\right)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup[1,2].

The following technical elementary lemma, that will be used in several proofs of this section, shows that some weaker notion of transitivity does hold for
{{\cal P}}_{F}
.

Lemma 4.1.4
.

Assume that
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}y
.
Then
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}{\cal O}_{F}(y)\cup\Omega_{F}(y)
.

Proof.

We prove first that, if
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}y
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}F^{n}(y)
for every
n\geq 0
.
This is an immediate consequence of the continuity of
F^{n}
.
Indeed, fix a
n\geq 0
and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Then we can find an
\eta>0
such that
d(z,y)<\eta
implies
d(F^{n}(z),F^{n}(y))<\varepsilon
.
If
\eta>\varepsilon
, set
\eta=\varepsilon
.
Let now
c_{1},\dots,F^{k}(c_{1})
be an
\eta
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
y
, namely
d(c_{1},x)<\eta
and
d(F^{k}(c_{1}),y)<\eta
.
Then
c_{1},\dots,F^{k+n}(c_{1})
is an
\varepsilon
-
{\alpha\omega}
chain from
x
to
F^{n}(y)
.
Hence,
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}{\cal O}_{F}(y)
.

Let now
z\in\Omega_{F}(y)
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
N>0
such that
d(F^{N}(y),z)<\varepsilon
.
By continuity, we can also find
\eta>0
such that
d(w,y)<\eta
implies
d(F^{N}(w),F^{N}(y))<\varepsilon
.
Now, set
\eta=\varepsilon
if
\eta>\varepsilon
.
Then, by the triangular inequality, every
\eta
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
y
can be extended to a
2\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
z
.
Hence,
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}\Omega_{F}(y)
.
∎

Lemma 4.1.5
.

Assume that
x\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}y
and
y\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
N_{\varepsilon}>0
, with
N_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, such that every
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
y
has length larger than
N_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Suppose, by contradiction, that there is an integer
N>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can find an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chain from
x
to
y
whose length is not larger than
N
.
Take a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and corresponding
\varepsilon_{n}
-
\alpha\omega
chains
(z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,z_{k_{n}}^{(n)})
from
x
to
y
.
There is at least one integer
m
with
1\leq m\leq N
such that infinitely many of these chains have length
m
.
Passing to a suitable subsequence
n_{j}
, we have a sequence of
\varepsilon_{n_{j}}
-
\alpha\omega
chains of length
m
from
x
to
y
.
Therefore, as
\varepsilon_{n_{j}}\to 0
,
z_{1}^{(n_{j})}\to x
and
z_{m}^{(n_{j})}\to F^{m-1}(x)
.
Hence, it must be that
y=F^{m-1}(x)
, against the theorem’s assumption that
y
is not in the orbit of
x
.
∎

Proposition 4.1.6

(
Properties of
{{\cal P}}_{F}
).

For any semi-flow
F
,
{{\cal P}}_{F}
is forward-invariant under the induced action of
F
on
X\times X
given by
F^{t}(x,y)=(F^{t}(x),F^{t}(y))
.
Moreover, for every
x
, the following holds:

1.

\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
is forward-invariant under
F
;

2.

\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)\;\supset\;{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Omega_{F}(x)
;

3.

if
\Omega_{F}(x)\neq\emptyset
,

{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(\Omega_{F}(x))\supset\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
;

4.

{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(y)\;\supset\;\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
for all
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
;

5.

if
F^{t}
is open for all
t\geq 0
(for instance if
F
is a flow or an open map), then

\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(y)
for all
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.

Proof.

(1) Let
y\in\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
.
We need to show that
F(y)\in\Down_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(x)
.
This follows immediately from Lemma
4.1.4
.

(2) This is an immediate consequence of the facts that
{\cal O}_{F}\subset{{\cal P}}_{F}
and that
{{\cal P}}_{F}
is closed.

(3) Let

F(y_{i})\to x
as
i\to\infty
.
Since

is a forward-invariant neighborhood of
NW_{F}
, for

large enough

lies entirely in

.
By continuity,

.

We now prove that

.
First of all, notice that, since

and
F^{n_{i}-1}(c_{i})\to\bar{y}
, we have that

.
Moreover, since

is an open map, for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta>0
such that, for every
z\in N_{\delta}(\{x\})
, there is a
w\in N_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y})
such that
F(w)=z
.
Notice that we can assume without loss of generality that
\delta\leq\varepsilon
.
Now, fix an
\varepsilon>0
, let
\delta>0
be the
\delta
mentioned above and let
(z_{0},\dots,F^{n}(z_{0}))
be a
\delta
-
\alpha\omega
chain from

to
\bar{y}
.
Then there is

and
(w_{0},z_{0},\dots,F^{n}(z_{0}))
is an
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega

to itself.
Hence,
\bar{y}\in NW_{F}
.
Moreover, it follows from our argument that

and
\bar{y}
are
{{\cal P}}_{F}
-equivalent, so
\bar{y}\in N
.
Hence, each node is invariant and so is the whole non-wandering set.
∎

Let

Let

When

(1) Via

.

Definition 4.3.12
.

A prolongational graph
\Gamma_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}
is
connected
if, whenever

with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
closed and disjoint sets forward-invariant under

C_{1}
to a node of
C_{2}
or viceversa.

Notice that, since
C_{1}
is
F
-forward-invariant, if a point of a node lies in
C_{1}
then the whole node does, and similarly for
C_{2}
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
F
has a connected global trapping region
C
that is a neighborhood of
NW_{F}
.
Then, if
F
has compact dynamics,
\Gamma_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}
is a connected graph.

Notice that
C
does not need to be globally attracting.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3.10
, the restriction of
F
to
C
has the same prolongational graph than
F
.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
X
is connected.
We denote by
Q
a compact global trapping region of
F
.
Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be two
F
-forward-invariant, closed and disjoint sets such that
NW_{F}=C_{1}\cup C_{2}
.
Since
NW_{F}
and all of its nodes are closed and
NW_{F}\subset Q
, then they are also compact.
Hence, by Proposition
3.1.1
(and the comment underneath),
B_{1}=\Up_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(C_{1})
and
B_{2}=\Up_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}(C_{2})
are closed.
Since

is globally attracting, every

has a non-empty limit set
\Omega_{F}(x)\subset Q
.
By case (5) of Proposition

,
\Omega_{F}(x)\subset NW_{F}
and therefore every

is upstream from some non-wandering node.
Hence,
B_{1}\cup B_{2}=X
.
Since

is connected, there must be a point
x_{0}\in B_{1}\cap B_{2}
.
Therefore, there exist points
x_{i}\in C_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that
x_{0}\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}x_{i}
.

Denote by
N_{i}\subset C_{i}
the node containing
x_{i}
,
i=1,2
and by
N_{0}
the node containing
\Omega_{F}(x_{0})
.
By case (3) of Proposition
4.1.6
, either
x_{i}\in{\cal O}_{F}(x_{0})
or
\Omega_{F}(x_{0})\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}x_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
In the first case, by case (1) of Proposition
4.2.6
,
{\cal O}_{F}(x_{i})
is a
{{\cal P}}_{F}
-equivalent set.
Hence
{\cal O}_{F}(x_{i})\subset\Omega_{F}(x_{i})=\Omega_{F}(x_{0})
, so that
x_{i}\in\Omega_{F}(x_{0})
.
In any case, therefore, we have that
\Omega_{F}(x_{0})\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}x_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Then,

N_{0}\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}N_{1}\subset C_{1}\text{ and }N_{0}\succcurlyeq_{{\cal P}_{F}}N_{2}\subset C_{2}.

Since
N_{0}
belongs to either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
, this means that there is either an edge from
C_{1}
to
C_{2}
or viceversa.
Hence,
\Gamma_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}
is connected.
∎

4.4
The prolongational graph of T-unimodal maps

In this final subsection we discuss in some detail the graph of some special classes of unimodal maps.
While doing so, we sharpen the main result in
[
16
]
in the context of the prolongational relation.

Definition 4.4.1
.

A continuous map
f:[a,b]\to[a,b]
is
unimodal
if there exists a unique point
c\in(a,b)
such that
f
is monotonically increasing (resp. decreasing) in
[a,c]
and monotonically decreasing
(resp. increasing) in
[c,b]
.
Point
c
is called the
critical point
of
f
.
We say that an attracting periodic orbit
\gamma
of
f
is
non-topological
if
c
is not in the basin of attraction of
\gamma
.
We say that an interval
J\subset[a,b]
is a
wandering interval
if
f^{m}(J)\cap f^{n}(J)=\emptyset
for every
n>m\geq 0
and
f^{n}(J)
does not lie in the basin of a periodic orbit.
We say that
f
is
T-unimodal
if
f
has no wandering intervals and no non-topological attracting periodic orbits.

The “T” in
T-unimodal
alludes to the fact that the graph of all these unimodal maps is a tower (see Definition
4.4.5
).
T-unimodal maps include the tent map family
[
3
]
as well as every S-unimodal map
[
16
,
15
]
.
See Example 5.5 in
[
3
]
for an example of a non-trivial T-unimodal map that is not topologically conjugate neither to a tent map neither to a S-unimodal map.

The theorem below summarizes the most relevant well-known results on invariant sets of unimodal maps.

Theorem B

(Gukenheimer, 1979
[
24
]
, Jonker and Rand, 1980
[
29
]
)
.

Let
f
be a unimodal map with critical point
c
.

The repellors of
f
can be only of the following two types:

1.

a periodic orbit;

2.

a Cantor set on which
f
acts as a subshift of finite type with a dense orbit.

The attractors of
f
can be only of the following three types:

1.

a periodic orbit;

2.

a cycle of intervals containing
c
;

3.

a Cantor set on which
f
acts as an adding machine.

The map
f
is transitive, i.e. has a dense orbit, in each of the repellors and attractors types above.
Attractors of type 2 and 3 contain
c
.
If there is no attractor of type 2 or 3, then one of the attracting periodic orbits has
c
in its basin of attraction.
When the attractor is of type 3, and only in this case,
NW_{f}
has (countably) infinitely many nodes, whose unique accumulation point is the attractor.

For a thorough discussion and proofs of the statement above, see also Thm 4.1 and 4.2 in
[
18
]
.

Theorem
B
has the following two fundamental consequences.

Corollary 4.4.2
.

Every T-unimodal map
f
has a unique attractor.

Proof.

By definition,
f
has no non-topological attracting periodic orbit.
Hence, by Theorem
B
,
c
must be in the basin of attraction of the attractor, so there can be only one attractor.
∎

Corollary 4.4.3
.

The spectral decomposition of
NW_{\ell_{\mu}}
in Theorem
B
coincides with the decomposition of
NW_{\ell_{\mu}}
into nodes of the prolongational relation
{{\cal P}}_{\ell_{\mu}}
.
In other words, every node of
{{\cal P}}_{\ell_{\mu}}
is of one of the five types listed in Theorem
B
.

Proof.

Each of the invariant non-wandering sets in Theorem
B
has a dense orbit.
Hence, each of them is a node of
{{\cal P}}_{\ell_{\mu}}
.
∎

In
[
16
]
we studied, in case of the logistic map family
\ell_{\mu}
, the graph
\Gamma_{\ell_{\mu}}

defined below (see also
[
15
]
for more general statements on S-unimodal maps).
We did not give a particular name to it, so in this article we use the name given to it by Mizin.

Definition 4.4.4

(Mizin, 2002
[
32
]
; De Leo & Yorke, 2020
[
16
]
)
.

Given a map
f:X\to X
, the
structural graph
of
f
is the graph
\Gamma_{f}
whose nodes are the chain-recurrent nodes of
f
, namely sets
N
of points
x
such that,
for each
\varepsilon>0
, there are
\varepsilon
-chains from
x
to each other point of
N
(for more details on chain-recurrence see Section
6.1
).
There is an edge from node
M
to node
N
if and only if there is a bi-infinite trajectory
b
such that
\alpha(b)\subset M
and
\omega(b)\subset N
.

Notice that
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chains are
\varepsilon
-chains and so each non-wandering point is also chain-recurrent and each non-wandering node is contained in a chain-recurrent node.

Definition 4.4.5
.

A directed graph
\Gamma
is a
tower
if it has no loops and there is an edge between any two nodes.

The following is our main result on CR graphs of the logistic map.

Theorem C

(De Leo, Yorke 2021
[
16
]
)
.

The structural graph of the logistic map
\ell_{\mu}
is a tower for each
\mu\in[0,4]
.

In other words, chain-recurrent nodes of
\ell_{\mu}
can be sorted in a linear order
N_{0},N_{1},\dots,N_{p}
(where
p
can be infinite) so that there is an edge from
N_{i}
to
N_{j}
if and only if
j>i
.
In this ordering, node
N_{0}
is always the repelling fixed point 0 and
N_{p}
is always the attractor.
Notice that the cases
\mu\leq 2
and
\mu=4
are trivial because, for those values, there is a single node (the fixed point
0
for
\mu\leq 2
and the whole interval
[0,1]
for
\mu=4)
.

Below we translate these result in terms of properties of
\Gamma_{{{\cal P}}_{\ell_{\mu}}}
:

1) Nodes.

In
[
16
]
, we provided the classification below of chain-recurrent nodes of the logistic map family.
Notice that by “periodic window” we indicate the maximal range of
\mu
for which exists a given non-wandering repelling Cantor set (see Figure
7
for a picture of the logistic map family’s period-3 window).

Theorem D

(De Leo, Yorke 2021
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\ell_{\mu}
be a logistic map.
Then:

1.

If
\mu
is not an end-point of a periodic window of the logistic map family, then its chain-recurrent nodes coincide with its non-wandering nodes.

2.

If
\mu
is the left end-point of a periodic window, then the (one-sided) attractor is a periodic orbit belonging to a repelling Cantor set.
The attracting chain-recurrent node is equal to this Cantor set.

3.

If
\mu
is the right end-point of a periodic window, then the (one-sided) attractor is a cycle of intervals having a non-empty intersection with a repelling Cantor set. In this case, the chain-recurrent node contains the attractor, part of its basin of attraction and the repelling Cantor set.

2) Edges.
As shown in next proposition, when

is compact, as is the case for the logistic map family, there is a strict relation between the edges
of the structural graph and the edges of the prolongational graph.

Proposition 4.4.6
.

Let
\Gamma_{F}
be the structural graph of a map
F

be distinct nodes of
\Gamma_{F}
, each of which contains a single non-wandering node

. Then:

1.

If there is an edge in
\Gamma_{F}
from
M
to

, then there is an edge in
\Gamma_{{{\cal P}}_{F}}
from
\hat{M}
to
\hat{N}
.

2.

Assume that

is compact and let
\hat{M},\hat{N}
be adjacent.
Then there is an edge in
\Gamma_{F}
from
M
to

.

Proof.

1) Under the hypotheses, there is a bitrajectory

with
\omega(b)\subset N
and
\alpha(b)\subset M
.
By Proposition

,
\omega(b)\subset\hat{N}
and
\alpha(b)\subset\hat{M}

Via

∎

In
[

]
, we extended all main results of

]
to T-unimodal maps whose attractor is a cycle of intervals by showing that our proofs ultimately only rely on purely topological properties, first and foremost the absence of wandering intervals and of non-topological attracting periodic orbits.
The same ideas apply to the case of the other two types of attractors, so that Theorem

actually apply more generally to T-unimodal maps.
Hence, we can reformulate the results above in the following way.

Proposition 4.4.7
.

Let

be a T-unimodal map and let

be its non-wandering nodes sorted according to their distance from

, so that

and

Then:

for each

with

;

,

.

5
Streams

The prolongational relation
{{\cal P}}_{F}
is closed and extends
{\cal O}_{F}
but is not, in general, a quasi-order (see Example
4.2.10
).
The main goal of the present article is to show that
closed quasi-order extensions
D
of
{\cal O}_{F}
, which extend at the same time the roles of
{\cal O}_{F}
(as a quasi-order) and
{{\cal P}}_{F}
(as a closed relation), play a particularly important role in dynamics.

Definition 5.0.1
.

We call
F
-stream
(or simply
stream
, when there is no ambiguity) on
X
a closed quasi-order
S
that is an extension of
{\cal O}_{F}
.
A
substream
of
S
is an
F
-stream
S^{\prime}
such that
S^{\prime}\subset S
.

Proposition 5.0.2
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream on space
X
.
Then
{\cal L}_{S}\subset{\cal L}_{F}
.
Hence, in particular:

1.

S\supset{{\cal P}}_{F}
;

2.

{\cal R}_{S}\supset NW_{F}
;

3.

{\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{F}}
is the smallest
F
-stream on
X
.

Proof.

Let
L\in{\cal L}_{S}
.
Since
{\cal O}_{F}\subset S
,
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
implies
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
which, in turn, means that
L(x)\geq L(y)
.
Hence,
L\in{\cal L}_{F}
.
∎

The smallest stream of a semi-flow
F
was introduced and studied first by Auslander in 60s
[
5
]
and so we denote it, in the remainder of the article, by
{\cal A}_{F}
.

Proposition 5.0.3
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream. Then:

1.

\Down_{S}(x)\supset{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x))
;

2.

\Down_{S}(x)\supset{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(y)
for all
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.

Proof.

(1) If
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
because
S
is an extension of
{\cal O}_{F}
.
If
y\in\Omega_{F}(x)
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
because
y
is the limit of a sequence of points on
{\cal O}_{F}(x)
and
S
is closed.
If
y\in\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x))
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
by transitivity.

(2) This is an immediate consequence of transitivity.
∎

5.1
Recurrent points and nodes of a stream

Recurrent and non-wandering points are generalizations of periodic points.
Similarly, nodes of
{{\cal P}}_{F}

are a generalization of periodic orbits of
F
(which are the nodes of
{\cal O}_{F}
).
Streams allow to define a natural and quite general analogue of periodic orbits in the following way.

Definition 5.1.1
.

Given a stream
S
of
F
, we write
x\overset{S}{=}y
, and we say that
x
and
y
are
S
-equivalent
, if
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{S}x
.

We say that
x
is
S
-recurrent
if either
x
is fixed or there is a
y\neq x
such that
x\overset{S}{=}y
.
We denote by
{\cal R}_{S}
the set of all
S
-recurrent points.
We say that a set
M\subset{\cal R}_{S}
is
S
-equivalent if all points of
M
are mutually
S
-equivalent.

The set
{\cal R}_{S}
comes with a natural equivalence relation, unavailable in
NW_{F}
due to the lack of transitivity of
{{\cal P}}_{F}
.

Proposition 5.1.2
.

The relation
\overset{S}{=}
is an equivalence relation on
{\cal R}_{S}
.

Proposition 5.1.3
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be two
F
-streams and assume
S\subset S^{\prime}
.
Then
{\cal R}_{S}\subset{\cal R}_{S^{\prime}}
.

We leave the proof to the reader.

Definition 5.1.4
.

Let
D^{\prime}
be a sub-quasi-order of
D
and let
N
and
N^{\prime}
nodes of, respectively,
D
and
D^{\prime}
.
We say that
N
extends
N^{\prime}
if
N\supset N^{\prime}
.

Definition 5.1.5
.

We call
nodes
the equivalence classes of
{\cal R}_{S}
with respect to
\overset{S}{=}
.
If
x\in{\cal R}_{S}
, we denote by
\Node_{S}(x)
the node containing
x
.

Note that, unlike the nodes of
{{\cal P}}_{F}
, nodes of a stream are all mutually disjoint because they are defined via an equivalence relation.

Example 5.1.6
.

Every point in
{\cal R}_{{\cal O}_{F}}
is
F
-periodic
and the nodes of
{\cal R}_{{\cal O}_{F}}
are precisely the periodic orbits of
F
.
Not all recurrent points of
F
, though, are in
{\cal R}_{{\cal O}_{F}}
since, in general, there are recurrent points that are not periodic.
For instance, let
F
be a rotation on the circle by an irrational angle.
Then the orbit of any point is dense, namely every point is recurrent for
F
, but no point is actually periodic.

Proposition 5.1.7
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream. Then:

1.

For each
x
,
\Omega_{F}(x)
is a
S
-equivalent set. In particular, there is a node
N
of
S
such that
\Omega_{F}(x)\subset N
.

2.

If
x
is
F
-recurrent, then
{\cal O}_{F}(x)\subset\Omega_{F}(x)\subset\Node_{S}(x)
.

Proof.

Since
S\supset{{\cal P}}_{F}
, the claim is an immediate consequence of Proposition
4.1.17
and Proposition
4.2.6
.
∎

Proposition 5.1.8
.

Let
S
be a stream.
Then each Lyapunov function
L\in{\cal L}_{S}
is constant on each node of
S
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a node. If
N
is a fixed point, there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, let
x,y\in N
with
x\neq y
. Then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{S}x
, so that, by Definition
3.1.2
,

L(x)\geq L(y)
and
L(y)\geq L(x)
.
Hence,
L
is constant on
N
.
∎

Proposition 5.1.9
.

Any intersection of
F
-streams is an
F
-stream.

Proof.

Quasi-orders and closed sets are closed under intersection.
∎

Corollary 5.1.10
.

Given any set
R\subset X\times X
and semi-flow
F
, there is a smallest
F
-stream containing
R
.
In particular,
{\cal A}_{F}
coincides with the smallest
F
-stream containing
{{\cal P}}_{F}=\overline{{\cal O}_{F}}
.

5.2
The Graph of a stream

The qualitative properties of a stream can be encoded in a graph as follows.

Definition 5.2.1
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream.
Given two sets
A,B\subset X
, we write
A\succcurlyeq_{S}B
if and only if
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
for each
x\in A
and
y\in B
.

Definition 5.2.2

(
Graph of a stream).

Given an
F
-stream, the graph of
S
, denoted by
\Gamma_{S}
, is the directed graph whose nodes are the nodes of
{\cal R}_{S}
and such that there is an edge from a node
N_{1}
to a node
N_{2}\neq N_{1}
if and only if
N_{1}\succcurlyeq_{S}N_{2}
.

Notice that, due to transitivity, if there is an edge from
N_{1}
to
N_{2}
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
for every
x\in N_{1}
and
y\in N_{2}
.
Moreover, again by transitivity, edges are themselves transitive, in the sense that
N_{1}\succcurlyeq_{S}N_{2}
plus
N_{2}\succcurlyeq_{S}N_{3}
implies

N_{1}\succcurlyeq_{S}N_{3}
.
Hence, when we draw the graph of a stream, we usually draw it with only a minimal set of edges, where the rest can be inferred.

Proposition 5.2.3
.

The graph of a stream
S
has no cycles.

Proof.

If
x_{1}\succcurlyeq_{S}\dots\succcurlyeq_{S}x_{n}\succcurlyeq_{S}x_{1}
for some integer
n>1
, then all these
x_{i}
are mutually
S
-equivalent and so they all belong to the same node.
∎

Definition 5.2.7
.

A stream graph
\Gamma_{S}
is

Theorem 2
.

Let

be a
F
-stream and assume that

has a connected global trapping region

Then, if

is a connected graph.

Proof.

Each

-stream

Notice that

\Omega

Suppose now that

5.3
\Omega
streams.

An immediate consequence of stream’s transitivity is that, for all
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
,

\Down_{S}(x)\supset{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(y).

It turns out that streams for which the sets at the left and right hand sides above are equal enjoy rather special properties.
In this section we illustrate some of them.

Definition 5.3.1
.

We say that an
F
-stream
S
is a
\Omega

if

\Down_{S}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(y)

for every

Proposition 5.3.2
.

Let

be a semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Then

is an
F
-
\Omega
stream if and only if

\Down_{S}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x)).

Proof.

By Proposition
5.0.3
, it is enough to prove that, if

, then either
x\succcurlyeq_{F}z
or
\Omega_{F}(x)\succcurlyeq_{S}z
.
Let
z\in\Down_{S}(x)
and suppose that
z\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Then, since
S
is an
\Omega
stream,
y\succcurlyeq_{S}z
for all
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Since
F
has a compact global trapping region,
\Omega_{F}(x)\neq\emptyset
.
Let
w\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Then there is a sequence
y_{n}\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
such that
y_{n}\to w
.
Since
y_{n}\succcurlyeq_{S}z
, then
w\succcurlyeq_{S}z
as well since

is closed.
Hence,
\Omega_{F}(x)\succcurlyeq_{S}z
.
∎

Next example shows that the compactness hypothesis in the previous proposition cannot be dropped.

Definition 5.3.4
.

We say that a node

F
-stream

F

Next propositions shows that every dynamical node contains non-wandering points.

Proposition 5.3.5
.

Every forward-invariant node of a stream is dynamical.

Proof.

If a node is forward-invariant, it contains the limit-sets of each of its points.
By the compactness hypothesis, every limit-set is non-empty.
By the Proposition
2.1.3
, each limit-set contains
F
-recurrent points.
∎

Corollary 5.3.6
.

A node

.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the fact that non-wandering nodes are forward-invariant.
∎

Proposition 5.3.7
.

Let
S
be an
F

\Omega

If

-recurrent, then

\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x))\cap{\cal R}_{S}=\Down_{S}(x)\cap{\cal R}_{S}

3.

is closed and forward-invariant under

Every node of

Proof.

(1)
If

.

Assume now that

is
S
-recurrent but not

Then there is a

S

.
Now, let

Since

\Omega

Hence, either

When either

When

and

Since, by hypothesis,

In the second case, it follows immediately that

(2)
Since

Since, by Proposition

-recurrent point in

Notice that, since

\Omega

The case when

Then there is a

Let

5.4
Subgraphs and Towers

Graphs of streams can be highly complicated.
Even in case of the logistic map, one of the simplest possible non-trivial dynamical system, for certain parameters the non-wandering set has infinitely many nodes, although its phase space
X=[0,1]
is compact.
Moreover, as shown in the example below,
one can easily modify locally the logistic map so to obtain a unimodal map without wandering intervals and with an arbitrary (at most countable number) of attractors (all but one consisting of non-topological attracting orbits), saddles and repellors.

).

Example 5.4.1
.

Recall that the prolongational graph of a logistic map
\ell_{\mu}

In this case, it coincides with the graph of the smallest stream of
\ell_{\mu}
(see the bottom of Section

Now, set
c_{k}=\ell^{k}_{\mu}(1/2)
.
Notice that the dynamics of
\ell_{\mu}
in the interval

is trivial:

0
is fixed and every other point enters the trapping region
Q=[c_{2},c_{1}]
in finite time.
All non-trivial dynamics takes place inside
Q
.
Hence, by modifying
\ell_{\mu}
in

, one obtains a smooth unimodal function
f
whose graph contains, as a subgraph (see Definition
5.4.2
), the graph of
\ell_{\mu}
.
In Figure

we show an example of such unimodal maps.
The map
f
whose graph is shown in the picture has a single non topological attracting fixed point, obtained by modifying a logistic map
\ell_{\mu}
in a neighborhood of 0.
In this particular case
\mu=3.5
, so that
\ell_{\mu}
has three nodes: the fixed node
0
(repelling), the other fixed node
p
(saddle) and a period-2 periodic orbit (attracting).
The blue part of the graph of

coincides with the graph of
\ell_{\mu}
.
Close to zero, we choose

so that
0<f^{\prime}(0)<1
and that

increases strictly monotonically until it meets the blue branch.
This way, 0 is attracting while the other intersection

of the red branch of the graph is repelling.
Hence,

(and therefore
{\cal A}_{f}
) has four nodes: the fixed point 0 (attracting, non-topological),
\phi
(repelling),
p
(saddle) and the period-2 orbit (attracting).
With the same trick, one can get unimodal maps

with
infinitely many non-topological attracting periodic orbits.
Assume that one starts with a logistic map
\ell_{\mu}
has infinitely many nodes. Then the graph of
{\cal A}_{g}

has infinitely many repelling nodes, infinitely many attracting nodes and infinitely many saddle nodes.
An example of such graph (with a single saddle but potentially infinitely many repelling and attracting nodes) is shown in Figure

(right, bottom).

The situation gets much more complicated in dimension larger than one.
Indeed, while unimodal maps with infinitely many nodes are non-generic, Sheldon Newhouse showed
[

]
that, on any compact manifold of dimension larger than one and for any

, there are open sets
{\cal U}\subset\text{Diff}^{r}(M)
such that a

-generic map of

has infinitely many attractors.

Hence, it is particularly important to identify important building blocks of stream graphs.
Towers, first introduced in Section

, are perhaps the most important one.
In this section, we present a general result about them.

Definition 5.4.2
.

Given a directed graph
\Gamma
, the directed graph
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a
subgraph
of
\Gamma
if the set of nodes of
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a subset of the set of nodes of
\Gamma
and, if there is an edge from node
N
to node
M
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, there is an edge from node
N
to node
M
in
\Gamma
.
Given a directed graph
\Gamma
, we call
tower
any acyclic subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
such that there is an edge between each
M,N\in\Gamma^{\prime}
.
A tower is
maximal
if it is not a subgraph of a larger tower.

Definition 5.4.3
.

A node
N
of a stream
S
is a
top node
if there are no
S
-nodes upstream of it and is a
bottom node
if there are no
S
-nodes downstream of it.

While it is tempting thinking of bottom nodes as attractors, the following example shows that this is not always the case.

Example 5.4.4
.

Consider the scalar ordinary differential equation

\frac{dx}{dt}=-x^{2}\sin\frac{\pi}{x}

where the right-hand side is defined to be 0 at
x=0
. For integers
n\neq 0
, the points
x=\pm\frac{1}{n}
are fixed points and are attracting for
n
odd and repelling for
n
even. Notice that all trajectories with
x(0)>1
are attracted to the point
x=1
while those with
x(0)<-1
are attracted to the point
x=-1
.
Hence there are countably many nodes, and there is an edge between adjacent nodes, one of which is an attractor and a top node and another is a repellor and a bottom node.
The graph is a connected curve that includes the node
x=0
. However, there are no edges to or from that node. Hence it is both a top and a bottom node. This graph is plotted as a saw tooth plot with top nodes higher than the bottom nodes they connect to. An infinite number of nodes converge to the node at
x=0
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream and assume that either
X
is compact or that
S
is an
\Omega
stream.
Then each node
N
of
S
is contained in a maximal tower of
\Gamma_{S}
.

Proof.

In order to prove the claim, we need to work in a compact environment.
Let
Q
be a compact global trapping region of
F
.
If the
F
-stream
S
is a
\Omega
stream, then every node of
S
lies in
Q
(see case 5 of Lemma
5.5.2
) and so it is enough to prove the claim for the restriction of
F
to
Q
.
If
S
is not a
\Omega
stream, then we must assume that
X
is compact.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
X
is compact.

We prove below that there is a top node
T\succcurlyeq_{S}N
and we leave the analogous proof for a bottom node to the reader.
By Zorn’s Lemma, there is a maximal tower
{\cal T}
of nodes such that
N\in{\cal T}
.
For each node
M\in{\cal T}
, let
{\cal T}(M)=\{A\in{\cal T}:A\succcurlyeq_{S}M\}
.
Then
{\cal T}(M)
is an ordered chain.
The set
U(M)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\cup_{A\in{\cal T}(M)}A
is compact and the collection of all
U(A)
for
A\in{\cal T}
is a nested set of compact sets.
Let
V\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\cap_{A\in{\cal T}(M)}U(A)
.

V
is non-empty and compact and consists of points that are mutually
S
-equivalent.
Hence,
V
is contained in a node
T\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\Node(V)\in{\cal T}
and all points of
T
are in
V
.
If there were a node
B\succcurlyeq_{S}T
, with
B\neq T
, then it would not be in the chain and so the chain would not be maximal, a contradiction.
Hence,
T
is a top node.
∎

5.5
Trapping regions of streams

Recall that a trapping region for
F
is a closed set
Q
such that
F^{t}(Q)\subset Q
for all
t\geq 0
.
By analogy, we provide the following definition in case of streams.

Definition 5.5.1
.

Given a stream
S
, we say that a closed set

is a trapping region for
S

if
\Down_{S}(Q)\subset Q
.

Notice that, since
{\cal O}_{F}\subset S
, each trapping region for an
F
-stream

is also a trapping region for
F
.

Lemma 5.5.2
.

Let
S
be an
F
-stream on
X
and let
Q
be a trapping region for
S
.
The following hold:

1.

if
S^{\prime}
is a substream of
S
, then
Q
is a trapping region for
S^{\prime}
;

2.

if a node
N
of
S
has some point in
Q
, then
N\subset Q
;

3.

there is no edge in
\Gamma_{S}
from any node in
Q
to any node outside
Q
;

4.

if
S
is an
\Omega
stream and
Q
is a global trapping region for
F
, then
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
, with
x,y\not\in Q
, if and only if
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
;

5.

if
S
is an
\Omega
stream and
Q
is a global trapping region for
F
,
{\cal R}_{S}\subset Q
.

Proof.

(1)
If
S^{\prime}
is a substream of
S
, then
\Down_{S^{\prime}}(Q)\subset\Down_{S}(Q)\subset Q
.

(2)
Let
x\in N\cap Q
and let
y\in N
.
Then
y\in\Down_{S}(x)\subset Q
.

(3)
Let
N
be a node of
S
inside
Q
and let
M
be another node of
S
.
Since there is an edge from
N
to
M
, then
M\subset\Down_{S}(N)
.
Hence,
M\subset Q
.

(4)
Since
S
is a
\Omega
stream,
\Down_{S}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x))
.
Since
Q
is globally attracting, either
\Omega_{F}(x)
is empty or
\Omega_{F}(x)\subset Q
.
Hence,
\Down_{S}(\Omega_{F}(x))\subset Q
and so, since
y\in\Down_{S}(x)
, necessarily

, namely
x\succcurlyeq_{F}y
.

(5)
Let
x\in{\cal R}_{S}
.
If
x
is periodic, then
\Omega_{F}(x)={\cal O}_{F}(x)
and, since
\Omega_{F}(x)\subset Q
, then
x\in Q
.
If
x
is not periodic, then there is a
y\in{\cal R}_{S}
with
y\neq x
,
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
and
y\succcurlyeq_{S}x
.
Assume that
x\not\in Q
.
Then it must be also
y\not\in Q
by case (3) and so, by case (4),
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
and
x\in{\cal O}_{F}(y)
.
But then
x
is periodic and so it must lie in
Q
, against the assumption.
∎

Proposition 5.5.3
.

Let
{\cal S}_{S}
be the set of substreams of an
\Omega
stream
S
and let
Q
be a trapping region for
S
that is globally attracting.
Then
S_{1},S_{2}\in{\cal S}_{S}
coincide if and only if their restriction to
Q
coincide.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
S_{1}\cap(Q\times Q)=S_{2}\cap(Q\times Q)
.
We need to prove that
(x,y)\in S_{1}
if and only if
(x,y)\in S_{2}
when either
x\not\in Q
or
y\not\in Q
(or both).

Suppose that
(x,y)\in S_{1}
.
By case 4 of the lemma above, when both
x,y
are outside of

,
(x,y)
belongs to all substreams of
S
.
If
x\in Q
, since
Q
is a trapping region for
S
, we must have that
y\in Q
.
The last case is when
x\not\in Q
and
y\in Q
.
In this case, since
S
is an
\Omega
stream, either
(x,y)\in{\cal O}_{F}
, in which case it belongs to all streams, or
y\in\Down_{S_{1}}(\Omega_{F}(x))
.
Since
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
coincide inside

, then
(x,y)\in S_{2}
.
∎

6
Streams of chains

The most important generalizations of recurrent points in literature are all based on chains: generalized recurrence, first considered by Auslander in 1963
[
5
]
, is based on
\varepsilon
-
\alpha\omega
chains; chain-recurrence, introduced by Conley in 1972
[
11
,
33
]
, is based on
\varepsilon
-chains; strong chain-recurrence, introduced in 1977 by Easton
[
20
]
, is based on
\varepsilon
-
\Sigma
chains (see
[
44
]
for several examples and recent results on these three recurrence generalizations).
In this section we show that each of those generalizations are the set of recurrent points of suitable streams.

6.1
The chains streams

Recall that, given a map
f
on
X
, a
(f,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
y
of length
n+1
is a sequence
(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})
such that
c_{0}=x
,
c_{n}=y
and
d(f(c_{i}),c_{i+1})<\varepsilon
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
.

The following technical lemma will be used several times in the rest of the section.

Lemma 6.1.1

(Hurley, 1991
[
26
]
, Lemma 1.2)
.

Let
f:X\to X
be continuous and let
x\in X
.Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any integer
p>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that:

1.

d(x,y)<\delta\implies d(f^{k}(x),f^{k}(y))<\varepsilon\;\text{ for every }\;k=0,\dots,p;

2.

for every
(f,d,\delta)
-chain
C=(c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
based at
x
with
n\leq p
, the chain
C^{\prime}
consisting in the pair of points
c_{0},c_{n}
is a
(f^{n},d,\varepsilon)
-chain.
Equivalently,

d(f^{n}(c_{0}),c_{n})<\varepsilon.

If
f
is uniformly continuous, then
\delta
can be chosen independently on
x
.

Proof.

(1)
We leave it to the reader.

(2)
The case
p=1
is a tautology and holds for every
\delta<\varepsilon
.
Assume now that the claim holds for all
p\leq p_{0}
.
Since we already know that the claim is true for all
n=1,\dots,p_{0}
, it is enough to prove that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that
d(f^{p_{0}+1}(c_{0}),c_{p_{0}+1})<\varepsilon
.

Consider a chain
(c_{0},\dots,c_{p_{0}+1})
and notice that

d(f^{p_{0}+1}(c_{0}),c_{p_{0}+1})\leq d(f^{p_{0}+1}(c_{0}),f(c_{p_{0}}))+d(f(c_{p_{0}}),c_{p_{0}+1}).

By continuity, there is an
\eta>0
such that
d(y,z)<\eta
implies
d(f(y),f(z))<\varepsilon/2
.
By the inductive assumption,
there is a
\delta_{1}>0
such that, if
(c_{0},\dots,c_{p_{0}})
is a
(f,d,\delta_{1})
-chain, then
d(f^{p_{0}}(c_{0}),c_{p_{0}})<\eta
.
Therefore, if
(c_{0},\dots,c_{p_{0}})
is a
(f,d,\delta_{1})
-chain, then
d(f^{p_{0}+1}(c_{0}),f(c_{p_{0}}))<\varepsilon/2
.

Assume now that
(c_{0},\dots,c_{p_{0}+1})
is a
\varepsilon/2
-chain.
Then
d(f(c_{p_{0}}),c_{p_{0}+1})<\varepsilon/2
.
Finally, let
\delta=\min\{\varepsilon/2,\delta_{1}\}
.
Then, for every
\delta
-chain based at
x
of length at least
p_{0}+2
,

d(f^{p_{0}+1}(c_{0}),c_{p_{0}+1})<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.

∎

Corollary 6.1.2

(Hurley, 1991)
.

Let
y\in\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(x)
and
y\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Then, if
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0^{+}
and
C_{n}
is a
(F,d,\varepsilon_{n})
-chain from
x
to
y
, the length of the
C_{n}
diverges as
\varepsilon_{n}
goes to 0.

Definition 6.1.3
.

Given a discrete-time semi-flow
F
on
X
and a metric
d
compatible with the topology of
X
, we call
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-chains stream
the relation

{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}=\{(x,y):\text{ there is a $(F,d,\varepsilon)$-chain from $x$ to $y$}\}.

We call
(F,d)
-infinitesimal chains stream
(or simply
chains stream
) the relation

{\cal C}_{F,d}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}.

Next two propositions grant that the relations defined above are indeed streams.

Proposition 6.1.4
.

{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
is a stream for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
is a quasi-order.

By definition
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
contains the diagonal of
X\times X
. Moreover,
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
is transitive because the concatenation of two
F
-
\varepsilon
-chain is an
F
-
\varepsilon
-chain.

{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
is closed.

Assume that
(x_{n},y_{n})\to(x,y)
for some sequence of pairs belonging to
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
.
For each
n>0
, denote by
C_{n}
any
\varepsilon
-chain from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
.
Then, for
n
large enough, we can assume that
d(x,x_{n})<\varepsilon
and
d(y,y_{n})<\varepsilon
and so the chain
\{x\}+C_{n}+\{y\}
is an
\varepsilon
-chain from
x
to
y
.
In particular,
(x,y)\in{\cal C}_{\varepsilon,F}
.

{\cal O}_{F}\subset{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}.
Each
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
can be joined to
x
by a
0
-chain (orbit segment).
∎

Notice that, in general, the
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
are not
\Omega
streams, as the example below shows.

Example 6.1.5
.

Let
F(x)=x^{2}
on
X=[0,1]
.
This is a discrete version of the case discussed in Example
5.2.4
: the end-points are fixed while every other point moves leftward, asymptoting to 0.
Since
X
is compact,
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
does not depend on
d
and we denote it by
{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, every point close enough to 0 (resp. to 1) is
{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}
-equivalent to 0 (resp. to 1).
For
\varepsilon\geq 1
, every finite sequence of points in
X
is an
\varepsilon
-chain and so

{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}=X\times X
.
For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, points close enough to
1/2
are further away than
\varepsilon
from their image, so that the node containing 0 is disjoint form the node containing 1.
In this case,
{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}
has two nodes (
N_{0}
, containing 0, and
N_{1}
, containing 1) and its graph is equal to the one of
{\cal C}_{F}
, namely a tower with two nodes.
Nevertheless, for

with

, we have that

although
1\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}}(0)
.
Hence, by Proposition
5.3.2
,
{\cal C}_{F,\varepsilon}
cannot be a
\Omega
stream.

Lemma 6.1.6
.

For every
x
and
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
,

{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(y)\supset\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(x).

Proof.

Let
z\in\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(x)
,
z\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Let
n>0
be such that
y=F^{n}(x)
.
By case 2 of Lemma
6.1.1
, there is a
\delta>0
such that, for every
(F,d,\delta)
-chain
(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})
based at
x
,
d(y,c_{n})=d(f^{n}(x),c_{n})<\varepsilon
.
Let
(c_{0},\dots,c_{N})
,
N\geq n+1
, be a
(F,d,\delta)
-chain from
x
to
z
.
Such chain exists by Corollary
6.1.2
.
Its subchain
(c_{0},\dots,c_{n+1})
is a
(F,d,\delta)
-chain based at
x
and so
d(F^{n+1}(x),c_{n+1})<\varepsilon
.
Since
F^{n+1}(x)=F(y)
, this means that
(y,c_{n+1},\dots,c_{N})
is a
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-chain from
y
to
z
.
Since this holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, then
z\in\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(y)
.
∎

Proposition 6.1.7
.

{\cal C}_{F,d}
is an
\Omega
stream.

Proof.

{\cal C}_{F,d}
is a stream because streams are closed under intersections (Proposition
5.1.9
).
It is an
\Omega
stream as an immediate consequence of Proposition
5.0.3
(2) and the lemma above.
∎

Proposition 6.1.8
.

Assume
X
be locally compact and let
F
have compact dynamics.
Then each compact global trapping region of
F
is a trapping region for
{\cal C}_{F,d}
.

Proof.

Denote by
Q
a compact global trapping region of
F
.
We need to prove that
\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}(Q)\subset Q
.

Let
(x,y)\in{\cal C}_{F,d}
with
x\in Q
and
y\not\in Q
and set
\rho=d(y,Q)
.
Notice that
\rho>0
since
Q
is compact.
Since
X
is locally compact, we can assume that
Q_{\rho}
has compact closure – if not, we set
\rho
to a smaller value for which that holds.

Recall that
{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})
denotes the smallest
F
-invariant subset of
X
containing
Q_{\varepsilon}
.
By Lemma
2.1.6
, there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})\subset Q_{\rho/2}
.
Hence, if
x\in Q_{\varepsilon}
,
F^{t}(x)\in Q_{\rho/2}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Since
{\cal I}_{F}(A)\subset{\cal I}_{F}(B)
for
A\subset B
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<\rho
.
Recall also that, by definition, there is a
N>0
such that
F^{t}(Q_{\varepsilon})\subset Q_{\varepsilon/2}
for all
t\geq N
.
Finally, by Lemma
6.1.1
, there is an
\eta>0
such that, for every
(F,d,\eta)
-chain
(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})
with
n\leq N
, we have that
d(F^{n}(c_{0}),c_{n})<\varepsilon/2
.
Since
\overline{Q_{\rho}}
is compact, each
F^{t}
is uniformly continuous on it and so the
\varepsilon
above does not depend on the starting and ending point of the chain as long as they lie in
\overline{Q_{\rho}}
.

Let now
(z_{0},\dots,z_{N},\dots,z_{2N},\dots,z_{kN+q})
be a
(F,d,\eta)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
Since
z_{0}=x\in Q
,

and

, so

.
Since
z_{N}\in Q_{\varepsilon}
,

and

, so

.
By repeating this same argument a finite number of times we get that

.
Finally, since

and
z_{kN}\in Q_{\varepsilon}
, we have that
F^{q}(z_{kN})\in{\cal I}_{F}(Q_{\varepsilon})\subset Q_{\rho/2}
and

, so that

.
Since
\rho/2+\varepsilon/2<\rho
, we get a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 6.1.9
.

Let
X
be locally compact and assume that
F
has a compact global trapping region

.
Then
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}\subset Q
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition

and case 5 of Lemma

.
∎

The streams
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
depend, in general, on the particular metric

used on

.
Nevertheless, as proved below, when

is locally compact and

has compact dynamics,

is purely topological.

Lemma 6.1.10
.

Let
X
be compact and let

be any two metrics generating the topology of

.
Then
{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}={\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
for any discrete-time semiflow

on

.

Proof.

Suppose that there are
x,y\in X
such that

is
{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}
-downstream but not
{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
-downstream from

.

Let
C_{i}
be a sequence of
(d_{1},\varepsilon_{i})
-chains from

to

, with
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
.
Since

is not
{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
-downstream from

, there is a
\delta>0
such that, for each

, there is a point
x_{k_{i}}
on

such that

d_{2}(f(x_{k_{i}-1}),x_{k_{i}})>\delta.

Since
X
is compact, we can assume without loss of generality that these

converge to a point

.
This means that, for every
\eta>0
, we can find an

such that

d_{1}(f(x_{k_{i}-1}),x_{k_{i}})<\eta
and
d_{1}(z,x_{k_{i}})<\eta
,
so that
d_{1}(z,f(x_{k_{i}-1}))<2\eta
.
In particular, also

with respect to the

distance. Since

and
d_{2}
are equivalent,
x_{k_{i}}\to z
and

and

.
This means that, for every
\eta>0
, we can find an

large enough such that

and

.
On the other side, we also have (see above) that

for every

.
These three inequalities are incompatible with the triangular inequality for
\eta

Hence, we must have that
{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}={\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
.
∎

Proposition 6.1.11
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and let

be any two equivalent metrics on

.
If

has compact dynamics,

{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}={\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
.

Let
Q
be a compact global trapping region for

.
By Proposition
6.1.8
,

{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}
and
{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
.
By Proposition
6.1.7
, we know that both

We now show that they are identical.

Consider first the case

.
By case 4 of Lemma

,
(x,y)\in{\cal C}_{F,d_{i}}
,

, if and only if

, which is a condition independent on the distance used on

.
Hence,
{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}
and
{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}

.
The case

and

cannot happen for either

or

.
Consider now the case where

and

Since

.
Since each pair
(x,y)
with
y\in\Omega_{F}(x)
is in both
{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}
and
{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
,
we are now left with the case when
x,y\in Q
.
The fact that
(x,y)\in{\cal C}_{F,d_{1}}
if and only if
(x,y)\in{\cal C}_{F,d_{2}}
is proven in Lemma
6.1.10
.
∎

When
{\cal C}_{F,d}
is the same for all
d
, we just write
{\cal C}_{F}
.

The following proposition generalizes an analogue result of Douglas Norton in
[
35
]
, stated for
X
compact.

Proposition 6.1.12
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and let
F
be a semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Then each node of
{\cal C}_{F}
is
F
-invariant.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1.8
and Lemma
5.5.2
,
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F}}\subset Q
.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
X
is compact.
Then, by Norton’s Proposition 3.8 in
[
35
]
, the claim follows.
∎

6.2
Continuous-time chains streams.

So far, we only considered the case of discrete-time chains.
Here, we prove that this can be done without loss of generality because the time-1 map
f=F^{1}
of a continuous-time semi-flow
F^{t}
completely determines the nodes and edges of the graph of
F^{t}
.
Our results extend, within a compact dynamics context, the following important result by Mike Hurley:

Theorem E

(Hurley, 1995
[
27
]
)
.

Let
F
be a continuous-time semi-flow on a compact metric space
X
and let
f=F^{1}
be the corresponding time-1 discrete-time semi-flow.
Then
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}={\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{f,d}}
.

Notice that the result above is not stated explicitly in
[
27
]
but is rather a corollary of a more general result (Theorem 5 in
[
27
]
) that holds, in general metric spaces,
for a stronger version of chain-recurrence, where the “
\varepsilon
” of an
\varepsilon
-chain is not a constant but rather a strictly positive function.
In case of a compact metric space, this general result reduces to Theorem
E
.

We start with the following definition.

Definition 6.2.1
.

Given a continuous-time semi-flow
F
on
X
and a metric
d
compatible with the topology of
X
, given
\varepsilon>0
and
T>0
, a
(F,d,\varepsilon,T)
-chain
of length
n+1
from
x
to
y
is a sequence of
n+1
points
c_{0},\dots,c_{n}
together with a finite sequence of positive real numbers
t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}
such that:

1.

c_{0}=x
,
c_{n}=y
;

2.

d(F^{t_{i}}(c_{i}),c_{i+1})\leq\varepsilon
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
;

3.

t_{i}\geq T
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
.

The following technical lemma is a continuous-time analogue of Lemma
6.1.1
.

Lemma 6.2.2

(Hurley, 1995)
.

Let
F
be a continuous-time semi-flow on
X
.
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
T>0
and
p>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that:

1.

d(x,y)<\delta\implies d(F^{t}(x),F^{t}(y))<\varepsilon\;\text{ for every }\;t\in[0,T];

2.

for every
(F,d,\delta,T)
-chain
C
with
p+1
points
c_{0},\dots,c_{p}
and times
t_{0},\dots,t_{p-1}
, the chain
C^{\prime}
with points
c_{0},c_{p}
and time
\tau=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}t_{i}
is a
(F,d,\varepsilon,\tau)
-chain.
Equivalently,

d(F^{\tau}(c_{0}),c_{p})<\varepsilon.

If
f
is uniformly continuous, then
\delta
can be chosen independently on
x
.

Proof.

(1)
We leave it to the reader.

(2)
The proof goes exactly as for the discrete-time version, case 2 of Lemma
6.1.1
.
∎

Definition 6.2.3
.

We call
(F,d,\varepsilon,T)
-chains stream
the relation

{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon,T}={\cal O}_{F}\cup\{(x,y):\text{ there is a $(F,d,\varepsilon,T)$-chain from $x$ to $y$}\}.

We call
(F,d,T)
-infinitesimal chains stream

the relation

{\cal C}_{F,d,T}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon,T}.

We call
(F,d)
-infinitesimal chains stream
(or simply
chains stream
) the relation

{\cal C}_{F,d}=\bigcap_{T>0}{\cal C}_{F,d,T}.

By analogy, given a discrete-time semi-flow
f
, we call
(f,d,\varepsilon,N)
-chain
of length
n+1
from
x
to
y
a sequence of
n+1
points
c_{0},\dots,c_{n}
together with a finite sequence of positive integers
k_{0},\dots,k_{k-1}
such that:

1.

c_{0}=x
,
c_{n}=y
;

2.

d(f^{k_{i}}(c_{i}),c_{i+1})\leq\varepsilon
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
;

3.

k_{i}\geq N
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
.

We call
(f,d,\varepsilon,N)
-chains stream
the relation

{\cal C}_{f,d,\varepsilon,N}={\cal O}_{f}\cup\{(x,y):\text{ there is a $(f,d,\varepsilon,N)$-chain from $x$ to $y$}\}

and
(f,d,N)
-infinitesimal chains stream

the relation

{\cal C}_{f,d,N}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}{\cal C}_{f,d,\varepsilon,N}.

The reader can verify that all the relations above are indeed streams.

Proposition 6.2.4
.

Let
F
be a continous-time semiflow.
Then
{\cal C}_{F,d}
is an
\Omega
stream and, for every
T>0
,
{\cal C}_{F,d,T}
is an
\Omega
stream.

Proof.

We start with the case of
{\cal C}_{F,d,T}
.
Let
y=F^{\bar{t}}(x)
.
We need to prove that

{\cal O}_{F}(x)\cup\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}(y)\supset\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}(x).

Let
z\in\Down_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}(x)\setminus{\cal O}_{F}(x)
and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there is a
(F,d,T,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
z
with points
(c_{0},\dots,c_{N})
and times
(t_{0},\dots,t_{N-1})
.
Notice that, since a
(F,d,T,\varepsilon)
-chain is also a
(F,d,T^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-chain for all
T^{\prime}<T
, we can assume without loss of generality that
T>\bar{t}
.

By Lemma
6.2.2
, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for every
(F,d,T,\delta)
-chain
(c_{0}=x,c_{1},c_{2})
with times
(t_{0},t_{1})
, we have that
d(F^{t_{0}+t_{1}}(x),c_{2})<\varepsilon
, namely

d(F^{t_{0}+t_{1}-\bar{t}}(y),c_{2})<\varepsilon.

We can assume without loss of generality that
\delta\leq\varepsilon
.
Since
\bar{t}<T\leq t_{0}
, then the sequence
(y,c_{2},\dots,c_{N})
with times
(t_{0}+t_{1}-\bar{t},t_{2},\dots,t_{N-1})
is a
(F,d,T,\varepsilon)
-chain from
y
to
z
.

Since every
{\cal C}_{F,d,T}
is an
\Omega
stream, then
{\cal C}_{F,d}=\cap_{t\geq 0}{\cal C}_{F,d,T}
is an
\Omega
stream as well.
∎

Proposition 6.2.5
.

Let
f
be a discrete-time semiflow.
Then, for every
N>0
,
{\cal C}_{f,d,N}
is an
\Omega
stream.

Proof.

The argument is identical to the one used to prove Proposition
6.2.4
.

∎

We now state, for continuous-time chains, some statement we proved in case of discrete-time and that can be proved with the very same proof of their discrete-time version.

Proposition 6.2.6
.

Let
F
be a continuous-time semiflow on
X
and set
f=F^{1}
.
If
X
is locally compact and
F
has a compact global trapping region
Q
, the following holds:

1.

Q
is a trapping region for
{\cal C}_{F,d}
, for
{\cal C}_{F,d,T}
for all
T>0
and for
{\cal C}_{f,d,N}
for all
N>0
;

2.

{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}
, each
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}
,
T>0
, and each
{\cal C}_{f,d,N}
for all
N>0
are subsets of
Q
;

3.

{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d}}
, each
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}
,
T>0
, and each
{\cal C}_{f,d,N}
for all
N>0
are
F
-invariant;

4.

{\cal C}_{F,d}
, each
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}
,
T>0
, and each
{\cal C}_{f,d,N}
for all
N>0
are independent on the metric.

Under assumptions that grant the metric independence of a relation, we will use for them the notation
{\cal C}_{F}
,
{\cal C}_{F,T}
and
{\cal C}_{f,N}
.

Next two lemmas show that, for all that concerns infinitesimal chains, it is enough to consider the time-1 map
f=F^{1}
.

Lemma 6.2.7
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,d,T}}
, there are
(F,d,\varepsilon,T)
-chains of arbitrarily large length from
x
to itself.

Proof.

Fix any integer
n>0
.
By hypothesis, there is at least a
(F,d,\varepsilon,T)
-chain
C
from
x
to itself.
By concatenating
C
with itself enough times, the result is a
(F,d,\varepsilon,T)
-chain of length larger than
n
.
∎

In several statements below, starting from next one, we will use the notation
{\bf\boldmath\lfloor T\rfloor}
to indicate the largest integer not larger than
T
.

Lemma 6.2.8
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and let
F
be a continuous-time semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Let
x
and
y
be
{\cal C}_{F,T}
-equivalent
and set
f=F^{1}
and
N=\lfloor T\rfloor
.
Then
x
and
y
are
{\cal C}_{f,N}
-equivalent.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a compact global trapping region for
F
.
By Proposition
6.2.6
,

Q
is also a trapping region for both streams
{\cal C}_{F,T}
and
{\cal C}_{f,N}
.
Hence, their recurrent points are entirely contained in
Q
and so it is enough to consider the analogue problem for the restriction of
F
to
Q
.
Hence, in the reminder of the proof we assume, without loss of generality, that
X
is compact.

We will prove that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
The same argument then can be used to show that there is a
(f,\varepsilon,N)
-chain from
y
to
x
.

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and let
\delta>0
satisfy point (1) of Lemma
6.2.2
and point (2) of Lemma
6.1.1
with
p=N
.
We can assume without loss of generality that
\delta\leq\varepsilon
.
Let
C
be a
(F,T,\delta)
-loop based at
x
with points
(c_{0},\dots,c_{r})
and times
(t_{0},\dots,t_{r-1})
such that
c_{i}=y
for some
0<i<r
.
Set

S=t_{0}+\dots+t_{r-1}
.
Notice that, if
S/N
is irrational, since rationals are dense, we can change
t_{r-1}
to a new time
t^{\prime}_{r-1}
so that the new chain
(c_{0},\dots,c^{\prime}_{r-1},c_{r})
is still a
(F,T,\delta)
-loop based at
x
but this time its period
S^{\prime}
is such that
S^{\prime}/N
is rational.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
S/N
is rational.

Following Hurley
[
27
]
, we build a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-loop
C^{\prime}
based at
x

in the following way.
For every
j
, set
s_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}t_{j}
.
The
s_{j}
are precisely the times at which, on the chain
C
, there are jumps – precisely, a jump from
F^{t_{j}}(c_{j})
to
c_{j+1}
.
We start
C^{\prime}
by setting
c^{\prime}_{0}=c_{0}
.
Then, after Hurley, for each
k
, we follow the rule below:

1.

if there is no jump in
C
in the interval
(kN,(k+1)N]
, then we set
c^{\prime}_{k+1}=f^{N}(c^{\prime}_{k})=F^{N}(c^{\prime}_{k})
;

2.

if there is a jump in
C
at
s_{j}\in(kN,(k+1)N]
, then we set

c^{\prime}_{k+1}=F^{(k+1)N-s_{j}}(c_{j+1})
.

The two cases above cover all possible cases because, since in
C
jumps take place at least
T\geq N
time units apart, there can be at most one jump in each interval
(kN,(k+1)N]
.

Once the
(F,T,\delta)
-loop gets back to
x
, not necessarily this is the case for the
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain.
Nevertheless, recall that
S/N
is rational, namely there are integers
m,n>0
such that
mS=nN
.
The chain obtained by repeating
m
times the sequence
(c_{0},\dots,c_{r})
and the relative times
(t_{0},\dots,t_{r-1})
is still a
(F,T,\delta)
-loop.
Hence, after applying the construction above to this new loop, the next-to-last of the
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain coincides with the next-to-last point of the
(F,T,\delta)
-loop.
Since
\delta\leq\varepsilon
, this shows that the
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain can be completed to a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-look based at
x
by adding
x
as the last point of the chain.
∎

Corollary 6.2.9
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and
F
is a continuous-time semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Set
f=F^{1}
, fix a
T>0
and set
N=\lfloor T\rfloor
.
Then
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,T}}={\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{f,N}}
and each node of
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,T}}
is a node of
{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{f,N}}
and viceversa.

Lemma 6.2.10
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and
F
is a continuous-time semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Set
f=F^{1}
, fix a
T>0
and set
N=\lfloor T\rfloor
.
Then, if
x
is
{\cal C}_{F,T}
-upstream of
y\in{\cal R}_{{\cal C}_{F,T}}
,

x
is
{\cal C}_{f,N}
-upstream of
y
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a
(F,T,\delta)
-chain from
x
to
y
and let
D
be a
(F,T,\delta)
-chain loop from
y
to itself.
Let
D^{n}
be the concatenation of
D
with itself
n
times.
Then by concatenating
C
with
D^{n}
we can get a chain from
x
to
y
of arbitrary length.
Hence, by using the very same procedure of the previous lemma, we can prove that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
∎

Corollary 6.2.11
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and
F
is a continuous-time semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Set
f=F^{1}
, fix a
T>0
and set
N=\lfloor T\rfloor
.
Then
\Gamma_{{\cal C}_{F,T}}=\Gamma_{{\cal C}_{f,N}}
.

The results above show already that all that the qualitative description of the dynamics of a continuous-time semi-flow
F
with compact dynamics is all encoded in the powers of its time-1 map.
Below we show that, in fact, the first power of the time-1 map is enough.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
X
is locally compact and
f
is a discrete-time semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Then, for any integer
N>0
,
{\cal O}_{f}\cup{\cal C}_{f,N}={\cal C}_{f}
.
If
f=F^{1}
for some continuous-time semi-flow
F
, then we have also that
{\cal C}_{F}={\cal O}_{F}\cup{\cal C}_{f}
.
In particular,
\Gamma_{{\cal C}_{F}}=\Gamma_{{\cal C}_{f}}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
6.2.8
, we can assume without loss of generality that
X
is compact.

First notice that, for any
N>0
,
{\cal C}_{f}\subset{\cal O}_{f}\cup{\cal C}_{f,N}
because every
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain
C
can be seen as a
(f,\varepsilon)
-chain – just break each jumpless segment in pieces of length 1.
To complete the proof, we need to prove that, given any
N>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, if
x
can be joined to
y
by a
(f,\eta)
-chain for every
\eta>0
, then we can join
x
to
y
with a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain.

So, let
\varepsilon>0
, set
p=2N
and let
\delta>0
be the
\delta
whose existence is granted by Lemma
6.1.1
(2) and
C
a
(f,\delta)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
Recall that, by possibly concatenating
C
with some
(f,\delta)
-chain from
y
to itself, we can assume that
C
has at least
N
points.
Let
c_{0},\dots,c_{r}
,
r\geq N
, be the points of
C
.
Then, by Lemma
6.1.1
(2), each pair
c_{kN},c_{(k+1)N}
is a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain.
If
r=qN
for some integer
q>0
, then
c_{0},c_{N},\dots,c_{qN}
is a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
Otherwise,
qN<r<(q+1)N
for some
q>0
.
In this case, we use as the final segment of the new chain the pair
c_{(q-1)N},c_{r}
.
Since
2N>r-(q-1)N>N
, even this pair is a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain.
Hence, the chain
c_{0},c_{N},\dots,c_{(q-1)N},c_{r}
is, in any case, a
(f,N,\varepsilon)
-chain from
x
to
y
.
This proves that
{\cal O}_{f}\cup{\cal C}_{f,N}={\cal C}_{f}
.

The second claim of the theorem comes from the fact that
{\cal C}_{F,T}={\cal O}_{F}\cup{\cal C}_{f,\lfloor T\rfloor}
(Corollary
6.2.9
) and that
{\cal O}_{F}\cup{\cal C}_{f,N}={\cal O}_{F}\cup{\cal C}(f)
for every integer
N>0
(by the first claim of this theorem).
∎

6.3
The
\Sigma
chains streams

This subsection is based on Easton’s definition of
\Sigma
chains given in Definition
2.2.1
.

Definition 6.3.1
.

Given a metric
d
compatible with the topology of
X
, we call
(F,d)
-
\Sigma
chains stream
the relation

\Sigma_{F,d}=\{(x,y):\text{for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a $(F,d,\varepsilon)$-$\Sigma$chain from $x$ to $y$}\}.

Proposition 6.3.2
.

\Sigma_{F,d}
is an
\Omega
stream.

Proof.

\Sigma_{F,d}
is a quasi-order.

By definition
\Sigma_{F,d}
contains the diagonal of
X\times X
.
Now, suppose that
(x,y),(y,z)\in\Sigma_{F,d}
and fix an
\varepsilon>0
.
By choosing an
\varepsilon/2
-
\Sigma
chain from
x
to
y
and another one from
y
to
z
, their concatenation is an
\varepsilon
-
\Sigma
chain from
x
to
y
.
Hence,
\Sigma_{F,d}
is transitive.

\Sigma_{F,d}
is closed.

Assume that
(x_{n},y_{n})\to(x,y)
for some sequence of pairs belonging to
\Sigma_{F,d}
.
For each
n>0
, denote by
C_{n}
any
\varepsilon/2
-
\Sigma
chain from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
.
Then, for
n
large enough, we can assume that
d(x,x_{n})<\varepsilon/4
and
d(y,y_{n})<\varepsilon/4
and so the chain
\{x\}+C_{n}+\{y\}
is an
\varepsilon
-
\Sigma
chain from
x
to
y
.
In particular,
(x,y)\in\Sigma_{F,d}
.

{\cal O}_{F}\subset\Sigma_{F,d}
. Each
y\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
can be joined to
x
by a
0
-chain (orbit segment).

\Sigma_{F,d}
is an
\Omega
stream.
The same argument used to prove that
{\cal C}_{F}
is an
\Omega
stream applies to this case, since every
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-
\Sigma
chain from
x
to
y
is also a
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-chain between the same points.
∎

Notice that, unlike chains streams,
\Sigma
chains streams do depend on the distance
d
used in their definition.

Example 6.3.3
.

Let
F
be the flow of an ODE
x^{\prime}=g(x)
on
X=[0,1]
, where
g
is a non-positive Lipschitz function on
X
whose set of zeros coincide with the middle-third Cantor set
C
.
Since the measure of
C
with respect to the Euclidean distance
e
on
X
is zero, there are
(F,e,\varepsilon)
-
\Sigma
chains from 0 to 1 for every
\varepsilon>0
, so that all points of
X
are
\Sigma_{F,e}
-equivalent to each other.
Now, let
d
be a distance topologically equivalent to
e
but for which the measure of
C
is strictly positive.
Then, for
\varepsilon
small enough, no
(F,d,\varepsilon)
-
\Sigma
chain can join 0 to 1, so that
\Sigma_{F,e}\neq\Sigma_{F,d}
(see
[
44
]
for several other examples).

6.4
The smallest stream

By Proposition
5.0.2
, we know that each semi-flow
F
has a smallest stream, namely the smallest closed quasi-order containing
{\cal O}_{F}
(and, therefore, containing
{{\cal P}}_{F}
).
This relation, that we denote by
{\cal A}_{F}
, has been first introduced and studied by Auslander in
[
5
]
.
In that article, under the assumption that
X
is separable and locally compact, Auslander introduced
{\cal A}_{F}
as the set
of all pairs
(x,y)
such that
L(x)\geq L(y)
for all Lyapunov functions
L\in{\cal L}_{F}
.
Hence,
{\cal A}_{F}={\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{F}}
.
In the proposition below, we show that
{\cal A}_{F}
can also be seen as a stream of chains, in particular a
\Sigma
chains stream.
We also drop Auslander’s assumptions on
X
and we rather transfer them to
F
.

Proposition 6.4.1
.

Assume that
F
has a separable and locally compact global trapping region
Q
.
Denote by
{\cal M}_{X}
the set of all distances on
X
compatible with its topology.
Then the following holds:

1.

{\cal A}_{F}=\displaystyle\bigcap_{d\in{\cal M}_{X}}\Sigma_{F,d}
;

2.

{\cal R}_{{\cal A}_{F}}=\displaystyle\bigcap_{d\in{\cal M}_{X}}{\cal R}_{\Sigma_{F,d}}
;

3.

{\cal A}_{F}
is an
\Omega

Chains streams with countably many nodes

Definition 6.5.1

The following is the last of our main results and shows that, when the smallest stream has only countably many nodes,
it coincides with the chains stream, that is usually easier to deal with.

Theorem 5
.

Let

be a semi-flow with compact dynamics.
Then, if

{\cal A}_{F}={\cal C}_{F}
.

Proof.

Recall that
{\cal A}_{F}={\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{F}}
. Hence, it is enough to show that
{\cal C}_{F}={\cal D}_{{\cal L}_{F}}
, namely that there is an
\varepsilon

x

L(x)\geq L(y)

Since

In turn, this amounts to show that
L(x)\geq L(y)
for every

If

y\not\in{\cal O}_{F}(x)
.
Since

has a compact globally attracting trapping region,
\Omega_{F}(x)
is not empty and is contained in a node

{\cal C}_{F}
.
Similarly, since

{\cal T}
has at most countably many nodes.
Denote by
{\cal T}^{\prime}

{\cal T}
that are not isolated.
These nodes subdivide the set of all isolated nodes of
{\cal T}
into at most countably many sequences

ordered with respect to
{\cal C}_{F}
.
In each
{\cal T}_{i}

Hence, any

{\cal T}

Denote by
({\cal T}^{*})^{\prime}

{\cal T}^{*}

These nodes subdivide the set of all isolated nodes of
{\cal T}^{*}

{\cal C}_{F}

\alpha_{0}

Final comments

An interpretation of streams in terms of modeling.

No model of a natural phenomenon can be able to reproduce exactly its dynamics.
In other words, we can safely assume that, given a semi-flow
F
, the dynamics of
F
represents the real behavior only within some
\varepsilon>0
, depending on the degree of accuracy of the model.
The smaller
\varepsilon
, the more accurate the model is, but we can safely assume that
\varepsilon>0
.
This means that what gives us the most reliable information on the dynamics of the system we are modeling is, more than the qualitative dynamics of
F
(that would be described by the graph of the prolongational relation), the graph of some
F
-stream.

For instance, suppose we are modeling some phenomenon using a discrete-time model and that we do not have precise information on the amount of the error of our model at every step, except perhaps that the error is “not too large”.
Then the graph of the chains stream
{\cal C}_{F}
is one of the most suitable graphs to describe the qualitative behavior of the system.
Indeed, by its very definition, the nodes and edges of the chains graph describes the behaviour of a point under
F
in presence of arbitrarily small perturbations.
Recall that, when the number of nodes is countable, as happens in most applied cases, the chains stream actually coincides with the Auslander stream
{\cal A}_{F}
.
In case some quantitative information on the error is available, then streams such as
{\cal C}_{F,d,\varepsilon}
might be more suitable to describe the actual dynamics of the system described by
F
. For instance, if we model a motion in the
[0,1]
segment as in Figure
8
(left) and we know that the model suffers by some “large” error for
x
close to
0.5
, then, depending on the amount of the error, it could happen that the corresponding stream looks like the one in
Figure
8
(center), where the new node means that, due do the large errors, the true dynamics of the natural system is compatible with the presence of a third node about
0.5
.
Possibly a more accurate model could tell whether such a node exists or not.

An interpretation of streams in terms of controlled systems.

Given an
F
-stream
S
, our interpretation of
x\succcurlyeq_{S}y
is that, under possibly some kind of control, it is possible to start at
x
and end up at
y
.

The relation
{\cal O}_{F}
is the one that represents the system in absence of controls: it is possible to end up in finite time in
y
when starting at
x
if and only if
y
is in the orbit of
x
.
Any stream, including
{\cal A}_{F}
, describes the behavior of the system under some kind of control.

For instance, consider the three streams shown in Figure
8
.
The first (left) describes what happens in the system when arbitrarily small controls can be applied at any point.
There are two effects.
The first is that it is possible to leave from the fixed point 1 and reach any other point of the interval – this is why
1\succcurlyeq_{S}x
for every
x\in[0,1]
.
The second is that it is possible to jump on the fixed point 0 in finite time from the orbit of any point
x<1
.

The second (center) describes what happens in the system when, besides the “infinitesimal control” above, one applies some finite control in a region in the middle.
Although the flow tends to move all points towards 0, with this finite control one can keep indefinitely a trajectory within the intermediate node and can reach any point within it.

The third describes what happens when one applies some finite control close enough to a pre-existing node.
In this case, rather than having a new node, a previously existing node “extends” into a larger node.
Notice that this node is forward-invariant but not invariant.

In this context, also the prolongational relation plays a role.
Its graph shows from which node to which node it is possible to get by using only two controls, a first (arbitrarily small) “push” at the very beginning of the motion and a second (arbitrarily small) “push” at its very end.
