The uniform existence time and Zero-Alpha limit problem of the Euler-Poincaré equations
Min Li, Zhaoyang Yin

Abstract




Abstract:
We consider the Cauchy problem of the Euler-Poincaré equations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a varying dispersion parameter
\alpha
. Based on the convex entropy structure and the modified commutator estimates, we have proved that the Euler-Poincaré equations have a uniform existence time with respect to
\alpha
in Sobolev spaces
H^{s}.
Combined with the Bona-Simth method, we obtain convergence of the solutions to the Euler-Poincaré equations as
\alpha\to 0
in the same space where the initial data are located.

Keywords:
Euler-Poincaré equations; Zero-Alpha limit; uniform existence time; convergence.

MSC (2010):
35Q35, 35Q51, 35L30

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for Euler-Poincaré equations

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}m+u\cdot\nabla m+\nabla u^{T}\cdot m+(\mathrm{div}u)m=0,\qquad&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
m=(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)u,\qquad&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0},\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(EP)

here
u,~m
represent the velocity and momentum respectively,
\nabla u^{T}=\bigl(\partial_{i}u^{j}\bigr)
is the transpose of the Jacobian
\nabla u=\bigl(\partial_{j}u^{i}\bigr)
, and
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{0}],\alpha_{0}>0
is a dispersion parameter with the length scale. To avoid any confusion, the first equation can be written in the component-wise form as

\partial_{t}m^{i}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}u^{j}\partial_{j}m^{i}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}\partial_{i}u^{j}m^{j}+\bigl(\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}\partial_{j}u^{j}\bigr)m^{i}=0,\qquad i=1,2,\cdots,d.

The equations (
EP
) were first introduced by Holm, Marsden, and Ratiu in
[
17
,
18
]
as a high dimensional generalization of the following Camassa-Holm equation for modeling and analyzing the nonlinear shallow water waves :

\displaystyle m_{t}+um_{x}+2u_{x}m=0,\ m=u-u_{xx}.

(CH)

Indeed, when
d=1
the Euler-Poincaré equations are the same as the Camassa-Holm equation (
CH
). Also, the Euler-Poincaré equations were investigated as the system describe geodesic motion on the diffeomorphism group with respect to the kinetic energy norm in
[
16
]
.

For
d=1
, the equation (
CH
) was introduced by Camassa and Holm
[
5
]
as a bi-Hamiltonian model for shallow water waves. Most importantly, CH equation has peakon solutions of the form
Ce^{-|x-Ct|}
which aroused a lot of interest in physics, see
[
8
,
30
]
. There is an extensive literature about the strong well-posedness, weak solutions and analytic or geometric properties of the CH equation, here we name some. Local well-posedness and ill-posedness for the Cauchy problem of the CH equation were investigated in
[
9
,
12
,
13
]
. Blow-up phenomena and global existence of strong solutions were discussed in
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. The existence of global weak solutions and dissipative solutions were investigated in
[
3
,
4
,
31
]
, more results can be found in the references therein.

As part of the well-posedness theory, the continuity properties of the data-to-solution map is important. Recently, starting from the research of Himonas et al.
[
14
,
15
]
, the continuity properties of the data-to-solution maps of the Camassa-Holm type equations are gradually attracting interest of many authors, see
[
22
,
23
]
. Most of the non-uniform constinuity results are established only on a bounded set near the origin. To overcome this limitation, Inci obtained a series of nowhere uniform continuity results including many Camassa-Holm type equations
[
19
,
20
]
. And for the incompressible Euler equation, Bourgain and Li
[
2
]
showed that the data-to-solution map is nowhere-uniform continuity in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
s\geq 0
by using an idea of localized Galilean boost. Based on Galilean boost method and the symmetric structure, we prove that the data-to-solution map of the Euler-Poincaré equations (
EP
) is not uniformly continuous on any open subset
U\subset B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),s>\max\{1+\frac{d}{2},\frac{3}{2}\}

[
21
]
.

The first rigorous analysis of the Euler-Poincaré equations (
EP
) was done by Chae and Liu
[
6
]
, they eatablished the local existence of weak solution in
W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ p>d
and local existence of unique classical solutions in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ s>\frac{d}{2}+3
. Yan and Yin
[
33

further discussed the local existence and uniqueness of the solution to (
EP
) in Besov spaces. On the other hand, Li, Yu and Zhai
[
28

proved that the solutions to (
EP
) with a large class of smooth initial data blows up in finite time or exists globally in time, which settled an open problem raised by Chae and Liu
[
6

. Later, Luo and Yin have obtained a new blow-up result in the periodic case by using the rotational invariant properties of the equation
[
26
]
. For more results of Euler-Poincaré equations, see
[

34

The Zero-Alpha limit problem has been studied by Chae and Liu in their pioneering paper
[
6
]
. They prove that as the dispersion parameter vanishes, the weak solution converges
to a solution of the zero dispersion equation with sharp rate as
\alpha\to 0
, provided that
the limiting solution belongs to
C([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
s>d/2+3
. This result has been extended to the modified two-component Euler–Poincaré equations by Yan and Liu
[
32

.

It is worth to mention that, in Chae and Liu’s work, the uniform existence time
T
with respect to
\alpha
is based on assumptions and has not been proven. In fact, by the standard energy method, the existence time
T_{\alpha}
of the solution would shrink to zero as
\alpha\to 0
. On the other hand, in dealing with the convergence problem of the solutions of (
EP
) as
\alpha\to 0
, previous results required the assumption that the space of initial data possesses better regularity than the space where convergence is established. Naturally, this leads to the emergence of the following

Problem 1.

For the solutions of Euler–Poincaré system (
EP
) with the same initial data, do there exist uniform existence time
T>0
with respect to
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{0}]
?

Problem 2.

As
\alpha\to 0,
does the solution of (
EP
) converge to the solution of zero dispersion equation (
\alpha=0
) in the same space where the initial data are located?

For simplicity, we first transform (
EP
) into a transport type system. According to Yan
[

, the Euler-Poincaré equations are equivalent to

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u=-(I-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}

\displaystyle\Big(\alpha^{2}\ \mathord{{\rm div}}~\big(\underbrace{\nabla u\nabla u+\nabla u\nabla u^{T}-\nabla u^{T}\nabla u-\nabla u(\mathord{{\rm div}}~u)+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u:\nabla u)\mathbf{I}}_{\mathcal{M}(u,u)}\big)+\underbrace{u(\mathord{{\rm div}}~u)+\nabla u^{T}u}_{\mathcal{N}(u,u)}\Big),

(1.1)

Based on the structure of
\mathcal{M}(u,u)
and
\mathcal{N}(u,u)
, we define the following bilinear symmetric form

\begin{cases}&\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla u\nabla v+\nabla u\nabla v^{T}-\nabla u^{T}\nabla v-\nabla u(\mathrm{div}v)+(\nabla u:\nabla v)\mathbf{I}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\nabla v\nabla u+\nabla v\nabla u^{T}-\nabla v^{T}\nabla u-\nabla v(\mathrm{div}u)\big),\\
&\mathcal{N}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\big(u~\mathord{{\rm div}}~v+\nabla v^{T}u+v~\mathord{{\rm div}}~u+\nabla u^{T}v\big).\end{cases}

(1.2)

It should be noted that,
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
is a bilinear matrix-valued function of
(\nabla u,\nabla v),
and

is a bilinear vector-valued function of
(u,\nabla u;v,\nabla v)
in (
1.2
). And by intentionally constructed, they are symmetric on

\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}(v,u),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{N}(u,v)=\mathcal{N}(v,u).

With these definitions in hand, for all
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{0}]
we can rewrite (

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u=-(I-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}\Big(\alpha^{2}\mathord{{\rm div}}~\mathcal{M}(u,u)+\mathcal{N}(u,u)\Big),\qquad&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0},\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

(
EP_{\alpha}
)

Note that, for the fixed

2
Littlewood-Paley analysis

We first present some facts about the Littlewood-Paley decomposition, the nonhomogeneous Besov spaces and their some useful properties (see
[
1
]
for more details).

Let
\mathcal{B}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\xi|\leq 4/3\}
and
\mathcal{C}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:3/4\leq|\xi|\leq 8/3\}.

Choose a radial, non-negative, smooth function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto[0,1]
such that it is supported in
\mathcal{B}
and
\chi\equiv 1
for
|\xi|\leq 3/4
. Setting
\varphi(\xi):=\chi(\xi/2)-\chi(\xi)
, then we deduce that
\varphi
is supported in
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\chi(\xi)+\sum_{j\geq 0}\varphi(2^{-j}\xi)=1\quad\mbox{ for any }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We should emphasize that the fact
\varphi(\xi)\equiv 1
for
4/3\leq|\xi|\leq 3/2
will be used in the sequel.

For every
u\in\mathcal{S^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the inhomogeneous dyadic blocks
{\Delta}_{j}
are defined as follows

{\Delta_{j}u=}\begin{cases}0,&if\quad j\leq-2;\\
\chi(D)u=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\chi\mathcal{F}u),&if\quad j=-1;\\
\varphi(2^{-j}D)u=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\varphi(2^{-j}\cdot)\mathcal{F}u\big),&if\quad j\geq 0.\end{cases}

In the inhomogeneous case, the following Littlewood-Paley decomposition makes sense

u=\sum_{j\geq-1}{\Delta}_{j}u\quad\text{for any}\;u\in\mathcal{S^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The nonhomogeneous low-frequency cut-off operator
S_{n}
will be often used in the subsequent sections, which is defined by

\displaystyle S_{n}u=\sum_{j\leq n-1}{\Delta}_{j}u\quad\text{for any}\;u\in\mathcal{S^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(2.1)

Definition 2.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
(p,r)\in[1,\infty]^{2}
. The nonhomogeneous Besov space
B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\;\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty\Big\},

where

\displaystyle\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\geq-1}2^{sjr}\|\Delta_{j}f\|^{r}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}},

if
1\leq r<\infty
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=

\displaystyle\sup_{j\geq-1}2^{sj}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

if
r=\infty
.

In particular, by the Fourier–Plancherel formula, Besov space
B^{s}_{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

coincides with the standard Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
The following Bernstein’s inequalities will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Ball and
\mathcal{C}
be an annulus. There exist constants
C>0
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, any positive real number
\lambda
and any function
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm{supp}}\hat{f}\subset\lambda\mathcal{B}\;\Rightarrow\;\|D^{k}f\|_{L^{q}}:=\sup_{|\alpha^{2}|=k}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{q}}\leq C^{k+1}\lambda^{k+(\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q})}\|f\|_{L^{p}},

\displaystyle{\rm{supp}}\hat{f}\subset\lambda\mathcal{C}\;\Rightarrow\;C^{-k-1}\lambda^{k}\|f\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\Delta^{k}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C^{k+1}\lambda^{k}\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

Lemma 2.2

(See
[
1
]
)
.

Let
(s_{1},s_{2},p,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[1,\infty]^{2}
, and
s_{1}<s_{2},~0<\theta<1
, then we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{B^{\theta s_{1}+(1-\theta)s_{2}}_{p,r}}\leq

\displaystyle\|u\|_{B^{s_{1}}_{p,r}}^{\theta}\|u\|_{B^{s_{2}}_{p,r}}^{1-\theta},

\displaystyle\|u\|_{B^{\theta s_{1}+(1-\theta)s_{2}}_{p,1}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{s_{2}-s_{1}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\Bigr)\|u\|_{B^{s_{1}}_{p,\infty}}^{\theta}\|u\|_{B^{s_{2}}_{p,\infty}}^{1-\theta}.

Then, we give some important product estimates which will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.3

(See
[
1
]
)
.

For
(p,r)\in[1,\infty]^{2}
and
s>0
,
B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an algebra. Moreover, for any
u,v\in B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\|uv\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\leq C(\|u\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|v\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}).

In addition, if

Lemma 2.4

(
Modified Commutator Estimates).

Let
\varLambda^{s}_{\alpha}:=(1-\Delta)^{s/2}(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1},
and denote
\varLambda^{s}=\varLambda^{s}_{0}=(1-\Delta)^{s/2}.
For any
s>0
and

\displaystyle\|[\varLambda^{s}_{\alpha},v\cdot\nabla]u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\big(\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{H^{s}}+\|\nabla v\|_{H^{s-1}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}}\big).

if
s<1+\frac{d}{2}
, and
\nabla v\in L^{\infty}\cap H^{d/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we also have

\displaystyle\|[\varLambda^{s}_{\alpha},v\cdot\nabla]u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}\cap H^{d/2}}\|u\|_{H^{s}}.

Proof.

Since the Bessel potential

The rest of the proof follows directly from Lemma 2.100 of
[
1

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varLambda^{s}(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}\big(\mathord{{\rm div}}~\mathcal{M}(u_{n},u_{n})+\Delta\mathcal{N}(v_{n},v_{n})\big)\cdot\varLambda^{s}\delta\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varLambda^{s}(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{N}(\delta,\delta)\cdot\varLambda^{s}\delta\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varLambda^{s}(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{N}(2v_{n},\delta)\cdot\varLambda^{s}\delta\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\|\mathord{{\rm div}}~u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\varLambda^{s}\delta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{H^{s-1}\cap L^{\infty}}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{H^{s}\cap L^{\infty}}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{2}C\|\mathord{{\rm div}}~\mathcal{M}(u_{n},u_{n})+\Delta\mathcal{N}(v_{n},v_{n})\|_{H^{s}}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\bigl(\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}^{3}+\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}\cap H^{s-1}}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{H^{s}}\|\delta\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad C\big(\|u_{n}\|_{H^{s}}+\|v_{n}\|_{H^{s+1}}\big)\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\big(\|u_{n}\|_{H^{s+2}}+\|v_{n}\|_{H^{s+3}}\big)\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}

(3.36)

As indicated by the growth rate
\|u_{n},v_{n}\|_{H^{s+k}}\leq C_{\textit{\tiny T}}\|S_{n}u_{0}\|_{H^{s+k}}\leq C2^{kn},
it can be inferred from (
3.36
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\delta\|_{{H}^{s}}\leq

\displaystyle\quad C2^{n}\|\delta\|_{H^{s}}+C\alpha^{2}2^{3n}

and the Gronwall’s inequality implies

\displaystyle\|\delta(t)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{2}2^{3n}te^{C2^{n}t}\leq C\alpha^{2}2^{3n}e^{C2^{n}},\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(3.37)

Back to the decomposition (
3.16
), with the combining results of (
3.34
) and (
3.37
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{S}_{t}^{\alpha}(u_{0})-\mathbf{S}_{t}^{0}(u_{0})\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\alpha^{2}2^{3n}e^{C2^{n}}+C\|(\mathrm{Id}-S_{n})u_{0}\|_{{H}^{s}},\qquad\forall(t,n)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{N}^{+}.

(3.38)

From (
3.38
), we can conclude the theory by such that: given
u_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any
\epsilon>0,
we first find a large
N
such that
C\|(\mathrm{Id}-S_{N})u_{0}\|_{{H}^{s}}<\epsilon/2.
Then, for the fixed
N
, we could choose a small
\alpha_{\textit{\tiny N}}
such that
C\alpha^{2}_{\textit{\tiny N}}2^{3\textit{\tiny N}}\exp(C2^{\textit{\tiny N}})<\epsilon/2,
thus
\|\mathbf{S}_{t}^{\alpha_{\textit{\tiny N}}}(u_{0})-\mathbf{S}_{t}^{0}(u_{0})\|_{H^{s}}<\epsilon.
As a result, we obtain

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\|\mathbf{S}_{t}^{\alpha}(u_{0})-\mathbf{S}_{t}^{0}(u_{0})\|_{H^{s}}=0,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

this complete the proof of Theorem
2
.
