Active Exploration via Autoregressive Generation of Missing Data
Tiffany (Tianhui) Cai

Abstract
We pose uncertainty quantification and exploration in online decision-making as a problem of training and generation from an autoregressive sequence model, an area experiencing rapid innovation. Our approach rests on viewing uncertainty as arising from missing future outcomes that would be revealed through appropriate action choices, rather than from unobservable latent parameters of the environment. This reformulation aligns naturally with modern machine learning capabilities: we can i) train generative models through next-outcome prediction rather than fit explicit priors, ii) assess uncertainty through autoregressive generation rather than parameter sampling, and iii) adapt to new information through in-context learning rather than explicit posterior updating. To showcase these ideas, we formulate a challenging meta-bandit problem where effective performance requires leveraging unstructured prior information (like text features) while exploring judiciously to resolve key remaining uncertainties. We validate our approach through both theory and experiments. Our theory establishes a reduction, showing success at offline next-outcome prediction translates to reliable online uncertainty quantification and decision-making, even with strategically collected data. Semi-synthetic experiments show our insights bear out in a news-article recommendation task, where article text can be leveraged to minimize exploration.

Introduction

This paper aims to create a bridge between active exploration methods and recent breakthroughs in sequence modeling, enabling decision-making systems to learn from unstructured prior information (e.g., text) while actively gathering outcomes to resolve key uncertainties. Our work targets a critical subroutine in many sequential decision-making algorithms – the use of posterior sampling of latent model parameters to assess uncertainty and drive exploration. We replace this posterior sampling step with an
autoregressive generation
step.

At the core of our approach is a shift in perspective, where uncertainty stems from potentially observable outcomes rather than unobservable latent parameters. Traditional probabilistic modeling of latent environment parameters can face substantial challenges in settings with high dimensional and/or unstructured data. These challenges include i) constructing informed priors incorporating diverse and unstructured information sources, ii) performing probabilistic inference under the current model of uncertainty, and iii) updating beliefs as more (possibly unstructured) data is gathered. By viewing uncertainty instead as arising from missing outcomes that
would
be revealed through appropriate action choices, in the style of Rubin
[
69
]
, our approach directly aligns with modern machine learning capabilities: we can i) train generative models through next-outcome prediction rather than fit explicit priors over latent parameters, ii) assess uncertainty through autoregressive generation rather than parameter sampling, and iii) adapt to new information through in-context learning
[
11
]
rather than explicit posterior updating.

Concretely, we make several contributions. First, we formulate a challenging exploration problem that showcases our ideas. Second, we develop concrete algorithms,
with an autoregressive implementation of
Thompson sampling
[
81
,
72
]
serving as our primary example. Third, we provide theoretical support for our work’s core premise, showing formally that success at offline sequence prediction translates into reliable online decision-making and uncertainty quantification. Finally, we validate these ideas through semi-synthetic experiments on a news-article recommendation task, demonstrating how article text features can be leveraged to minimize exploration. These contributions are discussed in detail in the remainder of this section. We defer a detailed discussion of related work to Section
8
.

In the

A range of exploration algorithms, including Bayes UCB, Knowledge Gradient, Information Directed Sampling, and Thompson Sampling, can be implemented using posterior sampling of parameters as a subroutine

The posterior sampling via autoregressive generation subroutine we present can serve as a drop-in replacement for the posterior sampling subroutine in all of these algorithms.
While other algorithms may require multiple generations (or posterior parameter samples) to select an action, Thompson sampling requires only a single generation step per decision: the agent simply behaves
as if
the generated data were real, using both the observed and generated outcomes to fit an optimal policy and selecting the best action according to that policy. For this reason, we focus much of the presentation on Thompson sampling.

As more outcomes are observed within an online decision-making task, the agent learns “in-context” by conditioning the sequence model on more observed outcomes; no retraining of the sequence model is needed. This is a significant departure from alternatives like Bayesian neural networks

Such incremental training is challenging: it is difficult to ensure that new observations are properly incorporated through gradient updates without either underfitting recent data or catastrophically forgetting information from earlier interactions.

Theoretical grounding (Sections
2
,
5
,
6
)

Our autoregressive generation approach is grounded with formal theoretical guarantees. In Section
2
, we begin by describing the viewpoint under which missing data about the potential outcomes of actions not yet taken is the source of the decision-maker’s uncertainty and defines posterior sampling in this context. Under this viewpoint, the correctness of autoregressive generation is a tautological consequence of the law of total probability, but only in settings where the sequence model perfectly matches the true joint distribution of potential outcomes. Our theory goes much further by accommodating learned sequence models that make accurate predictions but are not exactly optimal. Section
5
shows the quality of generated posterior samples generated by our algorithm is controlled by the model’s offline prediction performance, even when sampling is performed conditional on strategically collected observations. The regret bound in Section
6
ensures performance competitive with an oracle
informed
Thompson sampling, which optimally incorporates unstructured prior information
Z
, up to an error that depends on the excess offline prediction loss of the sequence model. These results formally
reduce a challenging online decision-making problem to one of offline learning a sequence prediction model with low loss.

Experimental validation (Section
7
)

We demonstrate that our theoretical insights bear out in simulations. Our methods not only match oracle Bayesian procedures in synthetic settings, but do so without relying on an explicit latent parameter specification. Moreover, our approach scales to a news article recommendation setting, where the best performance requires leveraging unstructured prior information like article headlines. Even when incorporating and fine-tuning a language model, we find our autoregressive generation procedure implements accurate uncertainty quantification (credible intervals) and drives exploration with low regret.

Intellectual foundations of our work.

Our efforts to perform inference solely through (potentially) observable quantities echoes a classical vision in statistics, advocated by Dawid
[
20
]
and Geisser
[
30
]
. Modern sequence models enable this vision by efficiently modeling joint distributions over collections of outcomes. Geisser understood this approach, but found it “too burdensome to be worthwhile” except in limited cases
[
30
, Page 52]
with the tools available at the time. Much of Geisser’s textbook on predictive inference instead relied on classical Bayesian modeling of latent factors. This pattern repeats in causal inference, where Rubin’s potential outcomes framework emphasized missing potential outcomes as the fundamental quantity of interest, yet his seminal paper
[
69
]
still relied on Bayesian parametric models for implementation.

Core premises and motivating vision.

In contrast to the rapid rate of progress in self-supervised learning (e.g., sequence modeling of natural language),
online decision-making has not enjoyed similar advances outside of simulations due to the necessity of interactive data collection.
We focus on the following distinct challenges in interactive decision-making: quantifying uncertainty, reasoning about the value of gathering information, and handling distribution shifts from interactive data collection. This paper establishes a reduction, showing that success in offline sequence prediction translates into effective online decision-making and uncertainty quantification. The utility of this reduction rests on two implicit premises that warrant explicit discussion. First, one can achieve strong performance at offline sequence prediction – a premise we take seriously given the remarkable empirical success of sequence models across domains ranging from natural language and code to protein sequences and audio data
[
11
,
48
,
80
,
49
, see e.g.]
, supported by evidence that predictive performance improves reliably with increased model and dataset size (so-called scaling laws
[
40
,
34
]
). Second, there exist sufficient domains and organizations (e.g., online platforms) with the historical data needed to train such predictive models. A third motivating consideration, more speculative in nature, is our conjecture that future systems will increasingly learn from unstructured outcomes amenable to sequence modeling — whether they be written responses from students using digital tutoring systems, medical scans and lab results from patients under treatment, audio recordings of customer service calls, detailed traces of user behavior in web applications, or other applications yet to emerge.

Main idea: the missing-data view of uncertainty and autoregressive implementations of posterior sampling

This section presents our main insights in an abstract way, relying on later sections to showcase their concrete application to informed exploration problems. Here, we focus exclusively on how to quantify uncertainty in a bandit-style decision-making task given access to an appropriate sequence model – that is, one that makes accurate probabilistic predictions about sequences of outcomes, given the task-specific prior information. For now, we defer questions of how such a model is trained, or how imperfections in the model impact downstream online decision-making performance. These are a core focus of later sections, with Sections
3
and
4
describing problem structures and procedures that enable offline learning of such a model, and Sections
5
and
6
showing that strong offline prediction performance ensures effectiveness for decision-making and uncertainty quantification in the online problem.

Brief formulation of a bandit task.

We consider a bandit-style decision-making task in which an agent sequentially selects actions from a finite, set
\mathcal{A}
across time periods
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
, adapting future actions in response to feedback. Specifically, at timestep
t
, the agent selects action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
, observes a vector of outcomes
Y_{t}
, and associates with this a reward
R(Y_{t})\in[0,1]
by applying a fixed, known function
R(\cdot)
. The agent also has access to (rich, unstructured) prior information
Z=(Z^{(a)})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
associated with each action. The goal, informally, is to select a rule for adaptively selecting actions that maximizes cumulative expected reward earned. This set of interactions correspond to a single “day” in Figure
2
and a single online bandit
task
in Figure
3
.

Missing data viewpoint.

Rather than viewing uncertainty as stemming from latent model parameters, the problem formulation and algorithms in our paper take a more direct view focused on potentially observable quantities. We say a bandit task
\mathcal{B}=(Z,\tau)
consists of prior action information
Z
and a two dimensional array or “table” of potential outcomes

\tau=(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)})_{a\in\mathcal{A}},

(1)

where each
Y_{t}^{(a)}
represents what would be observed if action
a
were taken at time
t
. In particular,
Y_{t}=Y_{t}^{(A_{t})}
. We index rows of the table by actions and columns by time, writing
\tau[a,t]=Y_{t}^{(a)}
. We treat the task
\mathcal{B}
as a random variable, drawn from some probability distribution, and aim to employ tools of probabilistic inference. As previewed in Figure
3
, we will eventually discuss problem structures where offline data can be used to learn about the task distribution.

In this view, all decisions are made under uncertainty about missing entries in this
potentially
observable data
\tau
. As decisions are made sequentially, each observed outcome exactly reveals one entry in
\tau
, resolving uncertainty about that entry while potentially informing predictions about the remaining unobserved entries. The agent may also resolve substantial uncertainty by leveraging prior information (
Z
), insofar as it is predictive of missing entries in
\tau
.

Various quantities of interest to the decision-maker can be written as functions of the potential outcomes
\tau
. The long-run mean reward of action
a
is

\mu_{a}(\tau)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{t}^{(a)}),

and the action with maximal reward is

A^{*}(\tau)\in\textnormal{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\,\mu_{a}(\tau).

(Assume ties are broken in some deterministic fashion). In contextual generalizations of the problem, the table
\tau
also contains decision-time specific context information; see Appendix
C
.

Posterior sampling by autoregressive generation.

By imputing unobserved entries in the potential outcomes table, our algorithms will generate approximate posterior samples of the table. Formally, we call
\hat{\tau}_{t}
an
exact posterior sample
of the potential outcomes table
\tau
at time
t
if

\hat{\tau}_{t}\sim\mathbb{P}\left(\tau\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\right),

(2)

where

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=\big\{\{Z^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\,,\,A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1},Y_{t-1}\big\}

(3)

denotes the history of observations available at the start of time
t
, including unstructured prior information
Z
. If the generated table
\hat{\tau}_{t}
is an exact posterior sample, functions of the generated table, like the associated mean-rewards
\mu_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{t})
or optimal action
A^{*}(\hat{\tau}_{t})
, are also exact posterior samples of those quantities. Selecting the action to be a posterior sample of the optimal action, as
A_{t}=A^{*}(\hat{\tau}_{t})
, is known as Thompson sampling, sometimes called probability matching.

Figure
5
depicts posterior samples constructed by autoregressive generation. The generation procedure views
\tau
as an
|\mathcal{A}|\times T
array, with rows representing distinct actions and columns indexing potential outcomes associated with the action. For an action
a
, given the prior information
Z^{(a)}
and observed outcomes
\{Y_{1}^{(a)},Y_{3}^{(a)}\}
, we generate the missing entries the corresponding row of
\tau
using some autoregressive sequence model
p
(which is believed to reflect the true outcome distribution). We sample a first unobserved potential outcome as
\hat{Y}_{2}^{(a)}\sim p(\,\cdot\,\mid Y_{1}^{(a)},Y_{3}^{(a)})
, then sample the next conditioned on both real and generated observations as
\hat{Y}_{4}^{(a)}\sim p(\,\cdot\,\mid Y_{1}^{(a)},Y_{3}^{(a)},\hat{Y}_{2}^{(a)})
, continuing until the row is complete. Repeating this process across actions generates a table
\hat{\tau}_{t}
without missing entries. For each action
a
, generating multiple versions of its row in the potential outcomes table
\tau
allows us to assess uncertainty in its mean reward
\mu_{a}(\tau)
.

Remark 2

(Distinguishing epistemic and aleotric uncertainty)
.

Not all approaches to generating and leveraging missing data will effectively capture uncertainty. Our implementation makes specific choices in how it generates and processes potential outcomes, designed to distinguish between different types of uncertainty in a principled way. Autoregressive generation captures the total uncertainty in the potential outcomes array. On the other hand, averaging over generated rewards helps separate two key forms of uncertainty: idiosyncratic variation in individual outcomes
Y
(“aleotoric” uncertainty) is averaged out, while uncertainty about an action’s overall performance
\mu_{a}(\tau)
(“epistemic” uncertainty) is preserved. In order to distinguish these types of uncertainties in particular it is critical that the missing outcomes in
\tau
are (a) imputed probabilistically, (b) imputed jointly rather than individually, and (c) that we average over rewards from a long sequence. Section
7.3
demonstrates that recently proposed approaches that do not have these three properties can have poor performance, with regret that grows linearly over time even with a perfect sequence model.

Correctness of autoregressive generation.

While the missing data viewpoint applies very broadly, the specific autoregressive generation procedure above uses two assumptions. First, independence across actions is required to justify the way we have performed generation separately across rows of the potential outcomes table.

Assumption 1

(Independence across actions)
.

There exists some (unknown) distribution
p^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\big(Z^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\big)\sim p^{*}\quad\textnormal{independently across}\quad a\in\mathcal{A}.

Without this assumption, we would need a more sophisticated generative model that shares information across actions.

Next, we make an exchangeability assumption. This justifies the deliberate discarding of temporal information in the generation procedure: it conditions its generation on past observed
outcomes
but not the timestep in which each outcome was observed. Essentially, the procedure is modeling the joint distribution of potential outcomes as a set
\{Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\}
, rather than treating them as a temporally
ordered sequence.

Assumption 2

(Exchangeability)
.

For any
a\in\mathcal{A}
and permutation
\sigma
,
\big(Z^{(a)},Y_{\sigma(1)}^{(a)},Y_{\sigma(2)}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{\sigma(T)}^{(a)}\big)
has the same joint distribution as
\big(Z^{(a)},Y_{1}^{(a)},Y_{2}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\big)
.

The missing-data viewpoint on uncertainty, and its quantification through autoregressive generation, extends beyond the exchangeable setting we focus on here. However, exchangeability plays an essential role in the bandit problems we study. On one hand, exchangeability encodes a temporal stability that makes the bandit learning problem meaningful: it ensures the existence of a “best action”
A^{*}
that an agent could hope to identify and consistently play, while also making early observations informative about future performance. Natural violations of exchangeability arise when there is some reason a priori to distinguish different columns of the potential outcomes table, such as contextual information in contextual bandits or timing information in non-stationary problems. (See Appendix
C
.) On the other hand, exchangeability allows for richer forms of uncertainty than independence would permit. Under independence, with a well-trained sequence model matching the true distribution
p^{*}
, the law of large numbers would eliminate meaningful uncertainty about actions’ mean rewards, making exploration unnecessary. Instead, exchangeability assumptions allow for uncertainty in an action’s overall performance
*
*

*

One can interpret draws from an exchangeable distribution as proceeding in two stages. First, the long run empirical distribution
p_{a}(y)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{t}^{(a)}=y)
is sampled (determining for instance, the average reward of the arm). Then, the outcomes sequences determined by sampling
without replacement
from the empirical distribution. For instance if outcomes are just binary, nature samples a (fractional) success probability and then distributes the successes randomly among the
T
outcomes. This is essentially a latent variable model where
p_{a}(\cdot)
acts as the (potentially very complex) latent factor. A technical detail is that, conditioned on the latent
p_{a}(\cdot)
, outcomes are sampled
without
replacement in the second step instead of independently
with
replacement as is common in Bayesian modeling. De Finneti’s theorem essentially shows this technical difference vanishes for infinitely exchangeable sequence models (i.e.
T=\infty
).
, which is gradually resolved as individual outcomes are observed.

Under these assumptions, the autoregressive procedure can be justified by an explicit expression for the posterior distribution of
\tau
. At time
t
some entries of each row of
\tau
have been observed. Let
\mathcal{M}_{a}\subset\{1,\ldots,T\}
denote the set of columns whose entries are missing in row
a
. To simplify notation, we permute each row so that observed entries always precede missing ones; this matches the implicit ordering used for generation in Figure
5
. Due to exhangeability (Assumption
2
), this is reordering has no impact on the joint distribution of missing entries. Formally, for any row
a
, introduce a total order
\prec_{a}
where
i\prec_{a}j
whenever (a)
i<j
, or (b) entry
i\notin\mathcal{M}_{a}
has been observed but
j\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
has not. Then the posterior distribution takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\propto\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\prod_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{a}}p^{*}(\tau[a,j]\mid Z^{(a)},\{\tau[a,k]\}_{k\,\prec_{a}\,j}).

(4)

This expression follows from independence across actions (Assumption 1) and the chain rule of probability. It lets us decompose the problem of sampling from the joint distribution of missing potential outcomes into the problem of sequentially generating individual missing potential outcomes associated with an action, conditioned on
Z^{(a)}
, observed outcomes, and previous generated outcomes.

The informed meta-bandit problem

Having established how sequence models can drive uncertainty quantification, we now formulate a class of sequential decision-making problems that are particularly well-suited to this approach. We study
informed meta-bandit problems
– settings where an agent repeatedly encounters multi-armed bandit tasks
\mathcal{B}=(Z,\tau)
. Each task is enriched with unstructured features
Z
providing prior information (like text). The agent must assess and resolve key uncertainties by combining both this unstructured prior information with interactively gathered outcomes.

While our formulation applies broadly to informed meta-bandit problems, we ground our discussion in the concrete example of news article recommendation, as depicted in Figure
2
. In this setting, each day presents a new task: the agent receives a batch of recently published articles and must sequentially recommend them to a fixed subpopulation of
T
users
*
*

*

While this is not a formal part of the model, we think these users as a pre-filtered segment with similar demographics or tastes; contextual variants of the model are treated in Appendix
C
.
throughout the day.

We treat this as an
offline
meta learning problem, depicted in Figure
3
, where the agent is assumed to have access to a large offline dataset collected from previous bandit tasks (e.g. data on historical articles) and uses this to design a fixed policy that is deployed on future tasks. The key challenge is synthesizing four distinct sources of information:

1.

An extensive offline dataset from previous similar tasks.

2.

An external foundation model capable of processing unstructured information.

3.

Unstructured prior information (e.g. article text) associated with the current bandit task.

4.

Limited actively gathered data associated with the current bandit task.

We describe all four information sources, beginning with those available to train models offline.

Offline learning and offline information sources.

The objective in the offline learning stage is to design (or learn) a policy
\pi
that, when applied to a fresh randomly drawn task
\mathcal{B}

Two information sources are available during offline learning. The first is a large offline dataset

\mathcal{D}^{\textnormal{offline}}=\big\{Z^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\big\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{offline}}},

collected from articles released on previous days (i.e. present in past bandit tasks). Each example in this dataset corresponds to a single article

with the set of observed user interactions

. It is pooled across article

that characterizes the online decision-making tasks. It is worth noting that, in realistic settings, we may only have access to short sequences

for some

The second information source is a foundation model, like a large language model pretrained on internet data. Such models provide powerful capabilities for processing unstructured text inputs and contain rich world knowledge. However, it is important not to confuse the foundation model itself with the autoregressive sequence model introduced in Sec.
2
: while foundation models excel at processing text, they are not inherently equipped to predict sequences of user interactions in specialized domains like recommender systems. Instead, we will train a multimodal model that
extends
a foundation model to predict outcomes of interactive decisions conditioned on text inputs.

Online decision-making task and additional online information sources.

In the online decision-making phase, the agent encounters multi-armed bandit tasks
\mathcal{B}=(Z,\tau)
, assuemd to be drawn randomly from some distribution satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2

\pi

4
Informed exploration and posterior sampling via autoregressive generation

In this section, we substantiate our main insight in two phases. In an
offline learning phase
, we train an autoregressive sequence model to predict successive outcomes (
Y

\mathcal{D}^{\textnormal{offline}}
. In an
online decision-making phase
, this sequence model powers exploration and decision-making without requiring retraining. Within each bandit task, our algorithm learns in-context by conditioning on observations, assesses uncertainty through autoregressive generation of missing data, and drives exploration by acting on these generated samples. While this approach extends to several exploration strategies, we primarily illustrate ideas using Thompson sampling as it only requires generation of a single trajectory.

4.1.1
Potential sequence model architectures

The choice of architecture for the sequence model
p_{\theta}
is flexible. Our theoretical results in Sections
5
and
6
establish that any architecture achieving strong predictive performance is suitable. Below, we describe three architectural approaches that offer different tradeoffs between flexibility and domain-specific structure:

Sequence model extension.

The most flexible approach directly extends a pretrained large language model to handle both (unstructured) prior information
Z^{(a)}
and outcomes
Y_{1:T}^{(a)}
. This architecture processes
Z^{(a)}
as a sequence of tokens and represents outcomes either as text tokens or as special tokens added to the model’s vocabulary. Through fine-tuning of select model weights, the model learns to predict the joint sequence
(Z^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)})
, capturing complex dependencies between text features and outcomes.

Separate-encoder architecture.

A more structured approach uses separate encoders for text features and outcomes, connected by a prediction head. The first encoder uses a language model to embed
Z^{(a)}
, while the second processes the sequence of outcomes
Y_{1:T}^{(a)}
. The encoder’ outputs are combined through an MLP layer to make predictions. This architecture aims to offer a balance between flexibility and efficiency.
The version of this model used in our experiments (Section
7
) is visualized in Figure
7
. The language model is finetuned to optimize the embedded representation for this task, but the second tower is a hardcoded function that extracts sufficient statistics – suitable because the
Y
values follow a distribution with known sufficient statistics.

Structured Bayesian models.

When strong domain knowledge exists about the outcome distribution, we can incorporate it directly into the model architecture. For binary outcomes (
Y_{t}^{(a)}\in\{0,1\}
), we can implement a Beta-Bernoulli model where neural networks map text features to prior parameters:

p_{\theta}(Y_{t+1}^{(a)}=1\mid Z^{(a)},Y_{1:t}^{(a)})=\frac{\alpha_{\theta}(Z^{(a)})+\sum_{j=1}^{t}Y_{j}^{(a)}}{\alpha_{\theta}(Z^{(a)})+\beta_{\theta}(Z^{(a)})+t}

(6)

Here,
\alpha_{\theta}(Z^{(a)})
and
\beta_{\theta}(Z^{(a)})
are learned functions that transform text features into Beta prior parameters and (
6
) is the corresponding “posterior predictive” distribution. This architecture explicitly encodes conjugate Bayesian updating while allowing the prior to be fit from data in the style of “empirical Bayes” methods
[
15
]
. For a more thorough discussion on the connection to Bayesian sequence modeling, see Appendix
D

Each architecture represents a different point on the spectrum between flexibility and structure. The language model extension offers maximum flexibility but potentially requires more data and computation. The structured Bayesian model is potentially more sample-efficient when its assumptions align with the true data generation process. The two-tower approach, intuitively, provides a compromise.

4.2
Online decision-making using the learned sequence model

After a sequence model is trained on offline data, it is deployed and used for decision-making. We primarily illustrate ideas using an autoregressive implementation of Thompson sampling in Algorithm
1
. This implementation itself combines three distinct ideas:

1.

Real-time learning “in-context”: a replacement of posterior updating.
The algorithm learns from the outcomes of arm selections without requiring retraining of the sequence model during deployment on a specific bandit task. Instead, learning occurs “in-context” by conditioning the sequence model on newly observed outcomes, similar to how large language models incorporate new information by conditioning on tokens beyond the initial prompt.

2.

Assessing uncertainty by autoregressive generation: a replacement for posterior sampling of latent parameters.
To explore efficiently, an agent must comprehend uncertainty about potential outcomes.
For this purpose, we apply autoregressive generation, as described in Figure
5
, Section
2
to produce posterior samples of the full potential outcomes table.

3.

Acting on uncertainty in the style of Thompson sampling.
While, in general, repeated posterior sampling is required to assess uncertainty, Algorithm
3
requires only a single posterior sample
\hat{\tau}_{t}
of the potential outcomes table to reach a decision at any timestep
t
. In the style of Thompson sampling, after generating
\hat{\tau}_{t}
, the agent
acts as if all uncertainty had been resolved and generated data were real
. In this simple setting without contexts, this entails computing the mean
\mu_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{t})
of the observed and imputed rewards for each arm

A_{t}=A^{*}(\hat{\tau}_{t})\in\textnormal{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\big\{\mu_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{t})\big\}.

Through this process, actions that are optimal under some likely generation of the missing rewards according to
p_{\theta}
have a chance of being selected and explored. Once no plausible sample of missing rewards could result in an action being optimal, it is essentially written off.

5
Theory part 1: Autoregressive generation from optimized sequence models approximates posterior sampling

When can we trust that autoregressive generation will produce faithful posterior samples to guide exploration? While it is natural to expect high-quality samples when the sequence model exactly matches the data-generating distribution, this section establishes a far more general guarantee: the quality of generated posterior samples is controlled by the model’s offline prediction performance, even when sampling is performed conditional on strategically collected observations. This provides a concrete foundation for our algorithm’s core premise—that successfully optimizing next-outcome prediction on a large offline dataset enables reliable posterior sampling during online exploration.
The practical significance of this reduction stems from remarkable empirical advances in sequence modeling. While theoretical understanding is limited, scaling laws indicate consistent improvements in predictive loss as model and dataset size increase
[
40
,
34
]
, and
engineering effort and hardware investments in AI have produced consistent success
[
11
,
48
,
80
,
49
, see e.g.]
.

5.1
Offline optimized sequence models

Our approach is predicated on the offline learning phase successfully training a high-quality sequence model for outcomes. This does not mean exactly recovering the true data-generating process
p^{*}
, but in the sense of achieving low log loss

\displaystyle\ell(p_{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\bigg[-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}\big(Y_{t}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)},Y_{1:t-1}^{(a)}\big)\bigg]

(7)

\displaystyle=\ell(p^{*})+\mathbb{E}_{Z^{(a)}\sim P_{Z}}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(p^{*}\big(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\;\Big\|\;p_{\theta}\big(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\right)\right].

(8)

For completeness, see Lemma
1
in Appendix
A.1
for the derivation of this identity. It expresses the excess loss of a sequence model,
\ell(p_{\theta})-\ell(p^{*})
, as the average KL divergence between its predictions and those of the true data generating model
p^{*}
.
Informally, we say a sequence model has been successfully
optimized
for offline predictions if
\ell(p^{*})-\ell(p_{\theta})\leq\epsilon
, for some small
\epsilon
. Inspired by amazing successes of sequence prediction across many domains, we are hopeful that sequence models can be optimized in this manner given sufficient offline training data (i.e. many meta-examples
|\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{offline}}|
) and sufficient compute resources. (In practice, the loss is approximated on an out-of-sample validation data; this is precisely the measure of performance tracked in scaling laws.)

Remark 3

(What is known in theory)
.

While developing a complete theory of next-outcome prediction for our setting remains an exciting direction for future work, existing theoretical perspectives offer some insights. Classical statistical learning theory implies that, when optimizing over suitable finite-dimensional model classes, the excess loss scales as
O(1/|\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{offline}}|)
with the number of sequence used in training. However, the most straightforward versions of these results depend pessimistically on both sequence length and model dimension. For sequence length, they suggest error terms scaling linearly with
T
(since each prediction could incur maximum possible loss) despite that for exchangeable sequences, later predictions should become easier as more data is observed. Some specialized asymptotic theory for exchangeable sequences formalizes this intuition, showing the dependence on sequence length enters only logarithmically
[
18
, see e.g.]
. However, adapting these results to modern practice—particularly our approach of fine-tuning large language models—remains a significant theoretical challenge, as classical bounds that scale with parameter dimension become vacuous despite the remarkable empirical success of these methods.

5.2
A reduction from online posterior sampling to offline prediction

Our main result formalizes how offline prediction quality controls the quality of generated samples, even under adaptive data collection. Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=\big\{Z\,,\,A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1},Y_{t-1}\big\}
denotes the history of observations available at the start of time

Z=\{Z^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
). The proof is given in Appendix
A.2
.

Proposition 1
.

If, after employing some policy
\pi
for
t-1
time periods, Algorithm
1

with sequence model

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}(\hat{\tau}_{t}\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\,\|\,\mathbb{P}\left(\tau\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\right)\right)\right]}_{\textnormal{degradation in online generated samples}}\leq\underbrace{|\mathcal{A}|\left[\ell(p^{*})-\ell(p_{\theta})\right].}_{\textnormal{degradation in offline predictions}}

Moreover, the data processing inequality implies that for any function

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}(f(\hat{\tau}_{t})\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\,\|\,\mathbb{P}(f(\tau)\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\right)\right]\leq|\mathcal{A}|\left[\ell(p^{*})-\ell(p_{\theta})\right]

Note that the expectation in the proposition is taken over the history. This result relates offline and online performance. When the model achieves near-optimal prediction loss, the bound implies that in an information-theoretic sense it is difficult to distinguish our algorithm’s samples from true posterior samples. The second part of the proposition extends this guarantee to any derived quantity, like mean rewards or optimal actions, that we might compute from these samples.

This result formalizes deep properties of our algorithm. First, it shows that incremental errors in autoregressive generation do not severely compound across iterations. Second, it shows that a single measure of offline sequence prediction loss directly controls the quality of posterior generations
for
any

[

6
Theory Part II: exploration using optimized sequence models results in low regret

This section continues to provide theoretical foundation for our algorithm’s core premise – showing that successfully optimizing next-outcome prediction offline dataset reliable online exploration with low regret. We begin with a formal definition of the regret measure.

6.1
Performance measure: average regret

We measure the agent’s performance through expected per-period regret:

\Delta(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(R(Y_{t}^{(a)})-R(Y_{t}^{(A_{t})})\right)\right].

(10)

The expectation above is taken over the joint draw of unstructured prior information and potential outcomes
(Z^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
as well as any additional randomness in the actions selected by policy
\pi
. Expected regret measures the expected degradation in decision-quality due to the need to explore, benchmarked against an omniscient agent that always plays the best action in hindsight for the subpopulation of
T
users (see further discussion in the next paragraph).

Benchmarking against the best action in hindsight is very common in adversarial formulations. Less stringent versions of regret that move the maximum outside a certain conditional expectation are sometimes called Pseudo-regret
[
13
,
44
]
. In versions of our formulation where both are well-defined, the distinction between these are insignificant; see Appendix
E
.

In the literature, our expected regret measure is most akin to “Bayesian” regret, but because of the recurring nature of the meta-learning bandit problem (depicted in Figure
2
), the expectation has a physical rather than philosophical meaning:
\Delta(\pi)
is the average regret the system would incur if
\pi
was deployed across many independent draws of bandit tasks (i.e. many distinct days of news recommendation).

As a first step to analyzing regret, we offer a different interpretation of learned sequence models.

6.2
Interpreting learned sequence models as simulators

Our analysis relies on interpreting a learned (potentially non-exchangeable) sequence model as inducing a simulator for bandit tasks, as in the definition below. One could use such a simulator to train policies offline using reinforcement learning techniques, or, as we do, to perform online planning. Let
O_{t}^{(a)}
denote the sequence of potential outcomes for action
a
that have been observed in the history
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
, ordered by the time period in which they were observed.

Definition 1

(Bandit simulator)
.

A sequence model
p_{\theta}
, together with the true marginal distribution
P_{Z}
of
Z^{(a)}
, induces a stochastic simulator of bandit policies as follows. First draw
Z^{(a)}\sim P_{Z}
independently for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, at any time
t
, given the history
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
, algorithm
\pi
selects some action
A_{t}
and observes an outcome

Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1},A_{t}\sim p_{\theta}\big(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(A_{t})},\,(Y_{j})_{j\in O_{t}^{(A_{t})}}\big),

which is added to the history.

It is not hard to show that, according to this definition the simulator induced by
p^{*}
perfectly matches the true data generating process.

6.3
A general reduction: optimized simulators reflect true regret

As in Section
5.1
, we consider sequence models that have been successfully
optimized
for offline prediction performance. We show that online decision-making algorithms designed to have low regret under the simulated environment induced by the sequence model (Definition
1
) will incur low expected regret when deployed in the real environment.
Our goal is to reduce a complex online decision-making problem to an offline sequence prediction problem – an area witnessing remarkable advances in recent years. As in Section
5
, we do not aim to provide new theory about the sample complexity of offline sequence modeling in this work, beyond the existing results and open questions mentioned in Remark
3
.

Let
\Delta(\pi;p_{\theta})
denote the expected regret policy
\pi
incurs under the simulated environment and reserve
\Delta(\pi;p^{*})
for the true regret defined earlier in (
10
).

Proposition 2
.

For any policy
\pi
and any sequence model
p_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Delta\big(\pi;p^{*}\big)}_{\textnormal{Deployment regret}}\leq\underbrace{\Delta\big(\pi;p_{\theta}\big)}_{\textnormal{Regret under simulator}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{(|\mathcal{A}|/2)\left\{\ell(p_{\theta})-\ell(p^{*})\right\}}}_{\textnormal{Penalty for sub-optimal simulator}}.

(11)

The proof is given in Appendix
A.3
. Similar statements apply to simulated quantities beyond regret. Informally, we expect
\ell(p_{\theta})-\ell(p^{*})
go to zero as the size of the training set (i.e., the number of offline actions observed,
|\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{offline}}|
) grows, as long as the sequence model class chosen is sufficiently flexible.

6.4

As a corollary of the general reduction above, we attain a regret guarantee for our algorithm when deployed with an offline-optimized sequence model.

6.5

Example 1

(Prior Sensitivity of Thompson sampling)
.

Consider a pair of Bayesian generative models that differ only in their prior distributions:

1.

True Bayesian model:
A sequence
(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)})\mid Z^{(a)}\sim p^{*}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})
is generated by first drawing a latent parameter
U^{(a)}\sim\nu_{*}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})
, then sampling iid outcomes
Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\mid U^{(a)}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}q(\,\cdot\,\mid U^{(a)})
.

2.

Model with an incorrect prior:
A sequence
(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{T}^{(a)})\mid Z^{(a)}\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})
follows the same generative process but with prior
U^{(a)}\sim\nu_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})
, while maintaining identical likelihood
q(\,\cdot\,\mid U^{(a)})
.

Then,

\displaystyle\ell(p^{*})-\ell(p_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z^{(a)}\sim P_{Z}}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{p^{*}}\big(Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\;\Big\|\;\mathbb{P}_{p_{\theta}}\big(Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z^{(a)}\sim P_{Z}}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{p^{*}}\big(U^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\;\Big\|\;\mathbb{P}_{p_{\theta}}\big(U^{(a)},Y_{1:T}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)}\big)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z^{(a)}\sim P_{Z}}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\nu_{*}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})\;\Big\|\;\nu_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})\right)\right]

The inequality is the data-processing inequality and the final equality uses the chain rule together with the fact that both generative models share a common likelihood. To simplify further, assume prior information
Z^{(a)}
takes on only one possible value. Then, Corollary
2
becomes:

\displaystyle\Delta\big({\pi_{\textnormal{PS-AR}}}(p_{\theta});\,p^{*}\big)\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{A}|\log(|\mathcal{A}|)}{2T}}}_{\textnormal{Regret bound for Thompson sampling}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2}D_{\rm KL}(\nu_{*}\;\big\|\;\nu_{\theta})}}_{\textnormal{Penalty for misspecified prior}}.

(12)

Our upper bound suggests that misspecified priors have persistent effects that do not diminish with the time horizon. The per-period expected regret remains inflated by a non-vanishing KL divergence term. This is unavoidable in general. In Appendix
A.5
, we provide a lower bound example where even with minimal prior misspecification (differing only by a
\log(T)
term), the per-period regret remains constant, aligning with known lower bounds in related settings
[
50
]
.

The persistent impact of misspecified priors differs markedly from passive learning, where prior effects typically fade with more data. The distinction arises from the active nature of our exploration problem: an agent may completely avoid certain arms based on their unstructured prior information (
Z^{(a)}
), never gathering the evidence needed to correct mistaken prior beliefs. While this can be beneficial—allowing a news recommender to quickly filter out poorly written articles—it risks permanently discarding valuable arms due to incorrect prior inferences from these features.

Sometimes, the impact of misspecifed prior beliefs
does
fade. Prior work has shown that Thompson sampling with diffuse or optimistic priors can achieve vanishing per-period regret as
T
grows
[
2
,
3
]
, even under misspecification. These approaches induce exploration by increasing uncertainty, ensuring all arms are tested. However, this conservative strategy sacrifices the efficiency gains possible through effective use of unstructured prior information (
Z
). There appears to be a fundamental tension between leveraging prior information to reduce exploration costs and maintaining enough uncertainty to avoid permanent mistakes.

7.3
Important subtleties in autoregressive generation.

Figure 10
:
Comparing to Alternative Sampling Approaches.
All methods use the same
p_{\theta}
model, but PS-AR has the lowest regret at longer horizons, in the synthetic setting.

Our implementation of autoregressive generation in Section
4.2
makes three critical choices:

1.

Potential outcomes are computed autoregressively, meaning we condition each newly generated outcome on those previously generated. This captures persistent patterns in performance, where actions that have demonstrated strong results are more likely to continue performing well.

2.

Autoregressive generation is performed through random sampling rather than selecting the most likely next outcome. This preserves the model’s implicit understanding of uncertainty, whereas always choosing the most likely outcome would collapse this uncertainty.

3.

We compute summary statistics of arm performance across repeated selections, such as mean reward, rather than examining variability in individual selections. This approach better captures uncertainty about an arm’s overall performance, which is most relevant for exploration.

These implementation choices are critical to the algorithm’s correctness. Figure
10
demonstrates that two alternative approaches, including one recently proposed in several published papers, lead to poor decision-making by either over- or under-exploring. Each tested variant modifies lines 3 and 4 of Algorithm
3
, where we average autoregressively imputed and observed rewards to form a “posterior sample” of the mean reward, while keeping the remainder of the algorithm unchanged. See Appendix
B
for additional details on the experimental setup. Our analysis reveals two key findings:

The importance of averaging out idiosyncratic noise:

The method labeled
Generate one reward
implements a proposal from recent works
[
58
,
55
,
28
]
. These works propose generating only the outcome/reward from
the next
arm draw, and takes the action with the highest sample reward. While this captures overall
predictive uncertainty
in the next outcome -- including both uncertainty in an arm’s overall quality (epistemic uncertainty) and uncertainty in the idiosyncratic noise of a single outcome (aleatoric uncertainty)--it fails in settings with substantial idiosyncratic uncertainty
*
*

*

In domains like recommender systems, there is substantial “noise” in individual user interactions. The aforementioned papers apply their method to benchmark problems with almost no reward noise.
. Figure
10
shows this approach incurs linear regret due to endless randomized exploration.

The importance of autoregressive generation:

The method labeled
Generate many rewards, but not sequentially
averages rewards across many independent (non-autoregressive) draws of the next outcome and takes the action with the highest average sampled reward. With sufficient generations, this average converges to the mean of the predictive distribution, effectively selecting the action currently believed to be best without purposeful exploration. Such “greedy” algorithms perform poorly in the long run because they fail to gather the data needed to resolve uncertainty about arm quality.

8
Related Work

Distinctive contributions.

While there has been intense study on sequence modeling, bridging foundation modeling and sequential decision making, and the use of neural networks in multiarmed bandit problems, our work makes distinct contributions that do not seem to have precedent in the literature. First, we focus on a new problem: informed meta-bandit problems where unstructured prior information (like text) must be leveraged to minimize exploration costs. Second, in developing principled algorithms for this setting, we show that autoregressive generation of missing outcomes enables principled uncertainty quantification and exploration without explicit parameter inference. Third, our theoretical guarantees establish a powerful reduction, showing that success in offline sequence prediction directly translates to effective online decision-making.

Core ideas from statistical sequence prediction.

As mentioned in the introduction, our work builds on a classical vision in statistics where inference is performed solely through potentially observable quantities, rather than through latent parameters. This perspective traces back to de Finetti’s work on exchangeable sequences
[
23
]
and appears across multiple literatures
[
19
,
30
,
7
,
27
,
26
,
33
,
8
,
9
,
25
,
62
,
45
,
64
]
. Among these, our initial efforts on this direction were especially inspired by
Osband et al. 2024
, who uses sequence prediction loss to
evaluate
the quality of epistemic uncertainty quantification. Several authors recently proposed neural network architectures for learning the joint distribution / posterior predictives
[
58
,
55
,
28
]
. While some of these papers include Bayesian bandit experiments, they do not use autoregressive generation to quantify epistemic uncertainty (see Section
7.3
for further discussion). Moreover, none of these papers share the distinctive contributions listed above.

Sequential decision-making with foundation models.

Recent work leveraging foundation models for sequential decision-making broadly falls into three categories. The first involves training sequence models on expert demonstrations. Some of these works try to directly imitate the typical expert, while others uses goal-conditioned action selection to improve upon demonstrated behavior
[
36
,
17
,
21
]
; While effective in some settings, this approach can provably fail when reward signals are noisy
[
10
,
53
]
. The second category explores zero-shot decision making by carefully prompting pretrained foundation models without additional training data
[
35
,
86
, see e.g.]
. In our informed exploration problem, we also aim to leverage the strong initial performance of foundation models, though we emphasize systematic improvement through online interactions. The third category develops generative models as learned environment simulators
[
31
,
38
,
32
, see e.g.]
. Section
6
interprets our sequence models as simulators, but our work distinctively uses these simulator models for online autoregressive generation to quantify epistemic uncertainty and enable principled exploration.

Sequence modeling in bandits.

A particularly relevant approach in this space is the Decision Pretrained Transformer (DPT)
[
46
]
, which implements Thompson sampling by pretraining a sequence model to predict the reward-maximizing action. This elegant approach works by observing that optimal in-context prediction of the reward-maximizing action necessarily models its posterior distribution. Our work develops a broader framework for uncertainty quantification through autoregressive generation of missing outcomes, an idea that is applicable well beyond Thompson sampling. While the works share connections, their core insights and implementations differ substantially, each with distinct advantages. DPT increases computational efficiency by directly modeling optimal actions, while our approach provides unique theoretical guarantees linking
offline
sequence modeling to
online
performance. These differences appear to stem from core design choices. DPT trains to predict the identity of the best action on histories from a behavior policy and faces distribution shift when deployed with histories generated by Thompson sampling. In contrast, we predict reward/outcome sequences for each single actions, reducing dependence on order and frequency with which an action is chosen. Given these complementary strengths, it would be interesting to explore hybrid approaches, where our models could serve as offline simulators to distill DPT-style policies, which reduce online computation.

Autoregressive predictive models are also employed by
Liu et al. 2023
, but with very different goals and insights. Their work recognizes that Thompson sampling can over-explore in nonstationary environments, where constant environmental changes introduce fresh uncertainty that may not be worth resolving. They address this through a clever conditioning of imagined reward trajectories in their Bayesian model. While our work shares the use of generating potential rewards, we do so with an entirely different objective - replacing explicit Bayesian inference with a more scalable approach rather than moderating Thompson sampling’s exploration. Later works that approximate predictive sampling with neural networks
[
91
]
rely on deep ensembles trained via online SGD, whereas our methods do not modify the sequence model at decision-making time: they are specifically designed to perform in-context learning.

Other approaches to neural network inference in bandit problems.

Beyond approaches that leverage sequence modeling, there are several approaches to implement Thompson sampling with neural networks. The first places a Bayesian prior on the neural network weights themselves, either through Bayesian linear regression on the final layer
[
67
,
78
]
or full Bayesian neural networks
[
89
]
. A second thread uses ensembles of neural networks to approximate posterior sampling
[
61
,
52
,
65
]
, including more efficient variants like Epinets
[
64
,
90
,
63
]
and HyperModels
[
22
,
47
]
that reduce computational overhead. Our work offers a different perspective entirely: rather than placing uncertainty on network parameters or using ensembles, we model uncertainty through autoregressive generation of missing outcomes, allowing us to leverage the full power of modern sequence models while maintaining principled uncertainty quantification. Moreover, we emphasize informed meta-problems where leveraging rich priors from unstructured information is crucial. This setting poses fundamental challenges for existing approaches. Ensemble methods typically rely on bootstrapping data to inject variance, but at the start of a decision problem, there is no data to bootstrap, leaving no basis for principled exploration. While randomized prior functions
[
61
]
attempt to address this through random initialization, our approach is fundamentally different — extracting meaningful priors implicitly through training and scaling laws, and updating beliefs through in-context learning rather than incremental gradient descent.

Meta bandit-learning.

Prior work on meta bandit-learning has primarily focused on parametric settings, studying how to fit hyperparameters for Thompson sampling
[
6
,
42
,
82
]
or linear UCB algorithms
[
16
]
. While these approaches demonstrate convergence to oracle performance given sufficient meta-examples, they differ fundamentally from our work which tackles informed exploration with unstructured information and relies on autoregressive generation rather than parameter inference. An alternative approach learns exploration policies from scratch using reinforcement learning, potentially discovering novel exploration strategies but also potentially requiring more complex training procedures and larger sample sizes than our sequence prediction framework.

9
Conclusion

By viewing uncertainty through the lens of missing data rather than latent parameters, our work establishes a principled bridge between modern sequence models and exploration algorithms, demonstrating through algorithms, theory, and experiments that success in offline sequence prediction translates to effective online decision-making and uncertainty quantification.

Several important challenges remain open. While enormous investments in both software and hardware are going toward speeding up autoregressive generation (e.g. for language model inference), we still expect that generating repeated posterior samples online is computationally expensive. Future work could address this computational overhead by distilling exploration policies into neural networks through imitation learning
[
57
]
. Second, our framework assumes access to sufficient historical data for offline learning. In practice, available data may be limited and partially confounded by previous action selection policies. While many deployed systems face and overcome similar challenges, carefully evaluating our methods’ robustness to these issues at scale remains important. Finally, as discussed in Remark
3
, developing a complete theory of offline next-outcome prediction, particularly for modern neural sequence models, remains an exciting direction for theoretical work.

In this work, exchangeability of the data generating distribution served as a vehicle for ensuring i) sufficient offline training data (through bootstrap augmentation) and ii) precise equivalence between posterior sampling and autoregressive generation.
We believe our missing data view of uncertainty extends well beyond the setting studied in this work, including to sequences with predictable time-dependence.
We are optimistic that operationalizing this viewpoint beyond the setting studied in this paper will bear fruit.

Proof.

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\psi,*}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\right)\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\psi,h}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\psi,*}\left[D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\psi,*}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}^{+}_{t-1}\right)\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\psi,\theta}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}^{+}_{t-1}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\psi,*}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\right)\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\psi,\theta}\left(\mathcal{H}_{|\mathcal{A}|T}^{+}\in\cdot\right)\right).

A.5
The tightness of our regret bound with misspecified priors

In Example
1
we showed the following bound on regret under Thompson sampling applied with a misspecified prior:

\displaystyle\Delta\big({\pi_{\textnormal{PS-AR}}}(p_{\theta});\,p^{*}\big)\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{A}|\log(|\mathcal{A}|)}{2T}}}_{\textnormal{Regret bound for Thompson sampling}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2}D_{\rm KL}\left(\nu_{*}\;\Big\|\;\nu_{\theta}\right)}}_{\textnormal{Penalty for mispecified prior}}.

(16)

As noted in the discussion following the example, our upper bound suggests that misspecified priors have persistent effects that do not diminish with the time horizon. The per-period expected regret remains inflated by a non-vanishing KL divergence term. Here we show this is unavoidable in general.

Example 2
.

Now we special case of Example
1
where the KL divergence term in (
16
) is nearly constant (just
O(\log(T))
) and yet per-period regret is also inflated by a constant.

Let the latent variable
U^{(a)}\in\{1/4,1/2,3/4\}
take on one of three values and take the likelihood
q(\cdot\mid U^{(a)})={\rm Bern}(U^{(a)})
to be a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
U^{(a)}
. Take
R(y)=y
so there is not distinction between rewards and outcomes. Let
Z^{(a)}\in\{\mathtt{Safe},\mathtt{Risky}\}
take one of two values with equal probability.

1.

When
Z^{(a)}=\mathtt{Safe}
the arm is known to have 50% success probability. That is,
U^{(a)}=1/2
with probability 1 (under both
\nu_{*}
and
\nu_{\theta}
).

2.

When
Z^{(a)}=\mathtt{Risky}
, the value of the latent success probability
U^{(a)}
is uncertain. Under the true data generating process, it is equally likely to take on a large or small value, with
\nu_{*}(\{3/4\}\mid\mathtt{Risky})=1/2=\nu_{*}(\{1/4\}\mid\mathtt{Risky})
. Under the mispecified process, risky arms are unlikely to have high success probability, with
\nu_{\theta}(\{3/4\}\mid\mathtt{Risky})=1/T^{2}
and
\nu_{\theta}(\{1/4\}\mid\mathtt{Risky})=1-1/T^{2}
.

Following the calculation in Example
1
, one can conclude

\displaystyle\ell(p^{*})-\ell(p_{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{P_{Z}(\mathtt{Risky})}_{=1/2}\left((1/2)\log\big(\frac{1/2}{1/T^{2}}\big)+(1/2)\log\big(\frac{1/2}{1-1/T^{2}}\big)\right)=O(\log(T)).

The idea of this construction is that
\mathtt{Risky}
have a good chance of high upside under
p^{*}
, so, when
T
is large, an optimal policy will start by exploring them to learn their two quality. Under the mispecified model, Risky arms have an
O(1/T^{2})
chance of realizing their high upside, and therefore only an
O(1/T^{2})
chance of being optimal. Thompson sampling which samples arms according to the posterior probability they ar optimal, is likely to never play a risky arm across all
T
periods. An agent employing Thompson sampling begins with a small (just
O(1/T^{2})
) chance of picking a risky arm in each period. So long as they do not play a risky arm, the posterior does not change, as there is no uncertainty about safe arms. Therefore, the probability of picking a risky arm remains very small (just
O(1/T^{2})
) unless, by a fluke, such an arm happens to be chosen. Proceeding along these lines, it is possible to show, at least when
|\mathcal{A}|=O(1)
, that the per-period regret of misspecified TS is constant, with

\Delta\big({\pi_{\textnormal{PS-AR}}}(p_{\theta});\,p^{*}\big)\geq\Omega(1).

B.1
Two practical ideas used to implement PS-AR

(1) Bootstrapping in offline learning.

In some settings (as was the case with our experiments was the news experiments), we may not observe
T
recommendation outcomes for each action in the offline dataset (first mentioned in the paragraph on “Information sources available offline” in Section
3

2

\tilde{Y}_{1}^{(a)},\dots,\tilde{Y}_{T}^{(a)}

. We then use
\big\{Z^{(a)},\tilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(a)}\big\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{mini-batch}}}

The second practical trick we use is when

B.5
Modeling Best Arm Rather Than Modeling Arm Reward

Lee et al. 2023b
introduce Decision Pretrained Transformers as an alternative approach of using sequence models to implement Thompson sampling. Their procedure trains a sequence model to predict the best action, mimicking an expert/optimal policy, given current state and recent interactions. This section aims to make a fair empirical comparison between our TS PS-AR algorithm and the approach introduced in
Lee et al. 2023b
.

Experimental details.

In order to make the most fair comparisons between modeling best actions (which we call “TS Action”) and modeling rewards (“TS PS-AR”), we use nearly identical model architecture and training procedures. The only adjustments we make are i) while the TS PS-AR sequence models output a distribution over the next outcome
Y
, the TS Action sequence models output a softmax distribution over the set of action candidates, ii) the training target is set to the best action, and iii) we must sample offline trajectories collected by a behavior policy to use for training (“in-context datasets”). For collecting these offline trajectories we follow a procedure identical to that used in
Lee et al. 2023b
for the multi-armed bandit setting.
*
*

*

For each new bandit task, we load a random batch of arms and their corresponding sequences of rewards. This batch of size 5000 is split into multiple bandit environments, each with 10 arms. Actions are sampled according to a mixture of a Dirichlet and a point mass (see Section 4 of
[
46
]
for more details).

We train these action-based sequence models for 2500 epochs and select the best learning rate out of {1e-1,1e-2,1e-3,1e-4}.

Bayesian sequence models

This section connects our offline learning procedure to empirical Bayes methods (Type-II maximum likelihood), which is an approach used to fit a Bayesian model to observed data

D.1
Offline learning as empirical Bayes

Standard Bayesian mixture models define exchangeable sequence models. This is formalized in Example

Example 3

(Bayesian models define exchangeable sequence models)
.

Bayesian mixture models define exchangeable sequence models: the model places a prior on a latent variable
U^{(a)}

\displaystyle U^{(a)}\sim P(U^{(a)}\in\,\cdot\,\mid Z^{(a)})\quad\quad\textnormal{then},\quad\quad Y_{1}^{(a)},\dots,Y_{T}^{(a)}\mid U^{(a)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}P(Y_{t}^{(a)}\in\,\cdot\,\mid U^{(a)},Z^{(a)}).

The

2

To keep the presentation simple, we illustrate empirical Bayes in a simple case. Consider a Beta-Bernoulli Bayesian model without

\displaystyle\mu_{a}\sim\textnormal{Beta}(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})~~\textnormal{then}~~Y_{1}^{(a)},Y_{2}^{(a)},\dots\big|\,\mu_{a}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\textnormal{Bernoulli}(\mu_{a}).

It is well known that the above Bayesian model (

\displaystyle p_{\theta}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(a)}=1\mid Y_{1:t}^{(a)}\big)=\frac{\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{t}Y_{i}^{(a)}}{\alpha+\beta+t}\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\theta=(\alpha,\beta).

Here, the true sequence model

evaluated at

For the above sequence model class

Appendix E
Finite vs infinite population formulations and Thompson Sampling variants

This section discusses the intimate connections between (large) finite-population formulations that were discussed in the main body of the paper and infinite-population formulations that are more common in the Bayesian bandit literature. We do this in the special case of the Bayesian mixture model from Example
3
.

We emphasize that
from our perspective, the main advantages or disadvantages of the finite population view are conceptual.
In terms of advantages: (1) the definitions do not require any explicit assumptions around mixture modeling or latent variables. and (2) The finite nature of the problem lets us visualize the procedure as in Figure
5
, without abstract reference to limits across infinite sequences.

E.1
Review of Thompson sampling in infinite populations

Thompson sampling is most often defined for a Bayesian mixture model, e.g., as in Example
3
.
Following that example, we consider in the subsection the canonical example of exchangeable sequences: a mixture model wherein the outcomes are i.i.d conditioned on a latent variable
U^{(a)}
.
That is,
p^{*}(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{t}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)})=\intop\prod_{t=1}^{T}P(Y_{t}^{(a)}\mid Z^{(a)},U^{(a)}=u)P(U^{(a)}=u)du
. The unknown latent variable represents the decision-maker’s uncertainty about an action’s performance.

The literature typically defines the “true arm means” as

\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}=\intop R(y)\cdot P(y\mid Z^{(a)},U^{(a)})dy.

The subscript highlights that this has the interpretation of a long-run average reward across an infinite population of users (or infinite set of rounds). By the law of large numbers (applied conditional on
(Z^{(a)},U^{(a)})
, one has

\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{t}^{(a)}).

The true best arm is defined as

A^{*}_{\infty}\in\textnormal{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}~\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}

Randomness in the latent parameters
(U^{(a)})
means
\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}
and
A^{*}_{\infty}
are random variables whose realizations are uncertain even given the history
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}.
Thompson sampling selects an action by probability matching on
A^{*}_{\infty}
, defined by the property

\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=a\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}(A^{*}_{\infty}=a\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}.

(22)

Per-period Bayesian regret over
T
periods is defined as

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(R\big(Y^{(A_{\infty}^{*})}_{t}\big)-R\big(Y^{(A_{t})}_{t}\big)\right)\right]

(23)

E.2
Thompson sampling in finite populations

One can define the true mean of a finite population as

\mu_{T}^{(a)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R\big(Y_{t}^{(a)}\big).

Note in the main paper we use the notation
\mu_{a}(\tau)
to denote the above mean, but we change the notation here to make the contrast to
\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}
more clear.
The true best arm for this finite population is defined as

A^{*}\in\textnormal{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}~\mu_{T}^{(a)}.

As in Corollary
1
, Thompson sampling selects an action by probability matching on the (finite-population) optimal action
A^{*}
, defined by the property

\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=a\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}(A^{*}=a\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}.

(24)

Per-period Bayesian regret over
T
periods is defined as

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(R\big(Y^{(A^{*})}_{t}\big)-R\big(Y^{(A_{t})}_{t}\big)\right)\right]

(25)

It is not hard to show that (
25
) is a more stringent notion of regret than in (
23
), since
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R\big(Y^{(A^{*})}_{t}\big)\geq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R\big(Y^{(A^{*}_{\infty})}_{t}\big)
by definition of
A^{*}
. Both definitions are widely used, with the more stringent finite-population version being more common in the adversarial bandit literature; see
[
44
]
.

E.3
The gap between finite and infinite population formulations is small

We analyze the gap between the two formulations in the case of a mixture model.
By a sub-Gaussian maximal inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}-\mu_{T}^{(a)}\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}-\mu_{T}^{(a)}\right|\bigg|\,\{U^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\right]\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(|\mathcal{A}|)}{T}}.

To justify the last inequality, note that since the function
R
takes values in
[0,1]
,
R\big(Y_{t}^{(a)}\big)-\mu^{(a)}_{\infty}
is subgaussian with variance proxy
1
, conditional on
\mathcal{U}
(by Hoeffing’s Lemma). Since it is the average of independent sub-Gaussian random variables,

\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}-\mu_{T}^{(a)}
is subgaussian with variance proxy
\frac{1}{T}
, conditional on
\{U^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
. The last step follows then from applying the subgaussian maximal inequality, conditional on
\{Z^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}},\{U^{(a)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

It follows easily that the infinite population optimum
A^{*}_{\infty}
is near optimal for finite populations:

0\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{T}^{(a)}-\mu^{(A^{*}_{\infty})}_{T}\right]\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log(|\mathcal{A}|)}{T}}.

Analogously, the finite population optimum is near-optimal in infinite populations:

0\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}~\mu_{\infty}^{(a)}-\mu^{(A^{*})}_{\infty}\right]\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log(|\mathcal{A}|)}{T}}.

Supported by this theory, we do not focus on the distinction between
A^{*}
and
A^{*}_{\infty}
.

12(a)
in Appendix
B.1.1
. The implementation that performs full imputation can be interpreted as Thompson sampling for a finite population.
The implementation that performs forward generation of fixed-length
