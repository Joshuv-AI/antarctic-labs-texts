Operator-valued Schatten spaces and Quantum entropies
Salman Beigi & Milad M. Goodarzi

Abstract
. Operator-valued Schatten spaces were introduced by G. Pisier as a noncommutative counterpart of vector-valued ℓ p \ell_{p} -spaces. This family of operator spaces forms an interpolation scale which makes it a powerful and convenient tool in a variety of applications. In particular, as the norms coming from this family naturally appear in the definition of certain entropic quantities in Quantum Information Theory (QIT), one may apply Pisier’s theory to establish some features of those quantities. Nevertheless, it could be quite challenging to follow the proofs of the main results of this theory from the existing literature. In this article, we attempt to fill this gap by presenting the underlying concepts and ideas of Pisier’s theory in a self-contained way which we hope to be more accessible, especially for the QIT community at large. Furthermore, we describe some applications of this theory in QIT. In particular, we prove a new uniform continuity bound for the quantum conditional Rényi entropy.

Interpolation theory: The complex method

Recently, interpolation theory has proven useful in establishing properties of quantum entropic quantities and studying properties of quantum channels. In this section, we present a brief overview of the complex interpolation method due to Calderón
[
9
]
. We refer the reader to
[
7
,
28
]
for more details on interpolation theory.

Banach spaces in this paper are all considered to be complex.
For Banach spaces
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})
,
unless otherwise explicitly stated, the equality
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
means that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are the same spaces (up to linear isomorphism) with
equivalent norms
in the sense that there are absolute constants
c,C>0
such that
c\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}
.

We denote by
B(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
the Banach space of all bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, equipped with the usual operator norm

\|T\|_{B(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}:=\sup\big\{\|Tx\|_{\mathcal{Y}}:x\in{X},\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 1\big\}.

When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
, let
B(\mathcal{X})=B(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. We denote the dual of
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{X}^{*}=B(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{C})
.

We write
\mathcal{X}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
if there is a continuous embedding from
\mathcal{X}
into
\mathcal{Y}
.
A couple of Banach spaces
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is said to be
compatible
if there is a Hausdorff topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
such that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}
.
For such a compatible couple we may consider
\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}
as a linear subspace of
\mathcal{V}
, and for any
v\in\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}
define

\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}}:=\inf\{\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}}:x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y},v=x+y\}.

This norm turns
\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}
into a Banach space.

In order to prepare the setup, consider the strip

\mathbb{S}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq\text{Re}(z)\leq 1\},

in the complex plane, and let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space. A function
f:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
is said to be
holomorphic
in the interior of
\mathbb{S}
, if for every
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
the complex function
z\mapsto\langle x^{*},f(z)\rangle
is holomorphic in the interior of
\mathbb{S}
. It is not hard to verify that the
maximum principle
holds for such holomorphic functions. Namely, for a bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
that is holomorphic in the interior of
\mathbb{S}
, we have

\sup_{z\in S}\|f(z)\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\max\big\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}},\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}\big\}.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
be a compatible couple of complex Banach spaces. We denote by
\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
the space of all bounded continuous functions
f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}
that are holomorphic in the interior of
\mathbb{S}
such that

t\mapsto f(it)\in\mathcal{X}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ t\mapsto f(1+it)\in\mathcal{Y}

are continuous functions. We equip
\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
with the norm

\|f\|_{\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}:=\max\big\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}},\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\big\}.

Using the maximum principle mentioned above, it is not difficult to verify that
\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})

is a Banach space.

We are now ready to define the complex interpolation spaces
[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}
,
0\leq\theta\leq 1
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
be a compatible couple of Banach spaces.
For every
0<\theta<1
, we define
[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}
to be the linear space

[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}:=\big\{f(\theta):f\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\big\},

endowed with the norm

\|v\|_{[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}}:=\inf\big\{\|f\|_{\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}:f(\theta)=v,f\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\big\}.

It can be verified that
[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}
is a Banach space.

From the definition it is clear that
[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}]_{\theta}=\mathcal{X}
with identical norms. Moreover, we have (
[
7
, Theorem 4.2.1]
)

[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}=[\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X}]_{1-\theta}.

Reiteration theorem
is another interesting property of interpolation spaces. Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
be a compatible couple and let
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}
,
0<\theta<1
. Then, a special case of the reiteration theorem (
[
7
, Theorem 4.6.1]
) says that if
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{1}
as vector spaces, for
0\leq\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\leq 1
we have

[\mathcal{X}_{\theta_{0}},\mathcal{X}_{\theta_{1}}]_{\theta}=\mathcal{X}_{(1-\theta)\theta_{0}+\theta\theta_{1}},\qquad 0<\theta<1.

(2.1)

Interpolation spaces behave nicely under taking the Banach dual as well. Suppose that one of the spaces
\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}
of a compatible couple
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
are finite-dimensional (or more generally, reflexive). Then, letting
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}
,
0<\theta<1
, we have

\mathcal{X}_{\theta}^{*}=[\mathcal{X}^{*}_{0},\mathcal{X}^{*}_{1}]_{\theta},

where
\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}
is the Banach dual of
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}
(see
[
7

Remark 2.2
.

For every
v\in[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}
we have

\displaystyle\|v\|_{[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}}=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}}{{f(\theta)=v}}}\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big)^{1-\theta}\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\Big)^{\theta}.

To see this, we note that by definition

\|v\|_{[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]_{\theta}}:=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}}{{f(\theta)=v}}}\max\big\{\sup_{t}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}},\sup_{t}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\big\},

and

\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big)^{1-\theta}\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\Big)^{\theta}\leq\max\big\{\sup_{t}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}},\sup_{t}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\big\}.

On the other hand, given
f\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
with
f(\theta)=v
, the map
f_{\alpha}(z)=e^{\alpha(z-\theta)}f(z)
, for arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, also belongs to
\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
and satisfies
f_{\alpha}(\theta)=v
. Moreover, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\|f_{\alpha}(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}=e^{-\alpha\theta}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\|f_{\alpha}(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=e^{\alpha(1-\theta)}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Then, optimizing over the choice of
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\max\big\{\sup_{t}\|f_{\alpha}(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}},\sup_{t}\|f_{\alpha}(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\big\}=\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big)^{1-\theta}\Big(\sup_{t}\|f(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\Big)^{\theta}.

Putting these together, the desired equality follows.

3.
Operator spaces

The space of bounded operators
B(\mathcal{H})
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
naturally appears in the study of a quantum system described by
\mathcal{H}
. If the quantum system interacts with another quantum system described by an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space, then we need to study operators acting on the tensor product Hilbert space. Such an operator can be thought of as an
n\times n
matrix whose entries belong to
B(\mathcal{H})
. From this point of view, not only
B(\mathcal{H})
, but also matrices with entries in
B(\mathcal{H})
become relevant in quantum information theory. Operator space theory is a theoretical ground for the study of such objects. In this section we give a brief overview of operator space theory and refer to
[
39
]
for more details.

We denote by
\ell_{2}^{n}
the Hilbert space of
n
-tuples of complex numbers, and let
\ell_{2}=\ell_{2}^{\infty}
. For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathcal{H}^{n}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}=\ell_{2}^{n}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H}
where
\otimes_{2}
denotes the
Hilbertian tensor product
. The space of all
m\times n
matrices with entries in a linear space
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
M_{m,n}(\mathcal{X})
. For simplicity we denote
M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
by
M_{m,n}
. The (operator) norm of a matrix
A\in M_{m,n}
—when identified with an operator from
\ell_{2}^{n}
into
\ell_{2}^{m}
—is denoted by
\|A\|_{m,n}
.
When
m=n
, we let
M_{n}(\mathcal{X})=M_{n,n}(\mathcal{X})
.

An element
T\in B(\mathcal{H})
is positive (positive semidefinite) if it is of the form
T=X^{*}X
for some
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
, where
X^{*}
is the adjoint of
X
. We shall write
T\geq 0
to denote that
T
is positive.

For each
n\geq 1
, the space
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
of
n\times n
matrices with entries in
B(\mathcal{H})
is equipped with a norm naturally induced by
B(\mathcal{H})
. Indeed, there is a natural way to turn
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
into a
C^{*}
-algebra by identifying it with
B(\mathcal{H}^{n})
. To see this, let us denote a generic element of
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
by
[X_{ij}]
where
X_{ij}\in B(\mathcal{H})
. Then, for
[X_{ij}],[Y_{ij}]
in
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
, we put

[X_{ij}]\cdot[Y_{ij}]=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{ik}Y_{kj}\right]\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ [X_{ij}]^{*}=[X_{ji}^{*}].

Next, to introduce the norm on
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
, we think of
X=[X_{ij}]\in M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
as an element of
B(\mathcal{H}^{n})
—which is algebraically isomorphic to
M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
—and define

\|X\|_{n}:=\|X\|_{M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))}=\sup\left\{\bigg(\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{ij}(v_{j})\Big\|^{2}\,\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}:v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{H},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|v_{j}\|^{2}\leq 1\right\}.

We can similarly define norms on
M_{m,n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
.

The above matrix norms satisfy two crucial properties. For
A,B^{*}\in M_{m,n}
and
X\in M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))
, by considering matrix multiplication in the usual way, we can think of
AXB
as an element of
M_{m}(B(\mathcal{H}))
.

7

7

Equivalently,
AXB
can be understood as
(A\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})X(B\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})
where

is the identity operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
.
Also, for
X\in M_{m}(B(\mathcal{H}))
and
Y\in M_{n}(B(\mathcal{H}))

Then, it is readily verified that the norms on

, satisfy the following properties:

(i)

\|AXB\|_{m}\leq\|A\|_{m,n}\|X\|_{n}\|B\|_{n,m}
for all

,
X\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
;

(ii)

\left\|X\oplus Y\right\|_{m+n}=\max\{\|X\|_{n},\|Y\|_{m}\}
for all
X\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
,
Y\in M_{m}(\mathcal{X})
.

We note that the above two properties hold not only in
B(\mathcal{H})
, but also in all of its linear subspaces. Conversely, Ruan’s theorem
[

]
states that any Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, with a hierarchy of norms
\|\cdot\|_{n}
on
M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
,
n\geq 1
, satisfying properties (i) and (ii)
8
8

8

In view of Ruan’s theorem, these spaces are sometimes termed as
abstract operator spaces
as opposed to concrete operator spaces (see Definition
3.1
).
“embeds” into
B(\mathcal{H})
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A (concrete) operator space is a subspace of
B(\mathcal{H})
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
that is closed in operator norm topology.

Ruan’s theorem may be precisely formulated by using the notion of completely bounded maps. Let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be a linear map between operator spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
. The
n
-th
amplification

\phi_{n}:M_{n}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow M_{n}(\mathcal{Y})
of
\phi
is defined by
\phi_{n}([X_{ij}])=[\phi(X_{ij})]
for
[X_{ij}]\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
. Then
\phi
is said to be

if
\|\phi\|_{cb}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\phi_{n}\|<\infty
.
We say that
\phi
is a

if each

is an isometry.

Using this terminology, by Ruan’s theorem any Banach space
\mathcal{X}
with a sequence of norms

on

for

3.1.
Dual operator space structure

There is a well-behaved notion of dual space in the category of operator spaces and completely bounded maps. Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be two operator spaces, and let
(CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),\|\cdot\|_{cb})
be the Banach space of all completely bounded maps from
\mathcal{X}
into
\mathcal{Y}
. Then,
CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
may be endowed with a natural operator space structure which we now describe. To this end, we first identify
M_{n}(CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
with
CB(\mathcal{X},M_{n}(\mathcal{Y}))
as follows: for
\Phi=[\phi_{ij}]\in M_{n}(CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
, let
\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow M_{n}(\mathcal{Y})
be given by
\widetilde{\Phi}(X):=\big[\phi_{ij}(X)\big]
. Then we can define a norm on
M_{n}(CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
by letting

\|\Phi\|_{n}:=\big\|\widetilde{\Phi}\big\|_{CB(\mathcal{X},M_{n}(\mathcal{Y}))}.

It is not hard to verify that
CB(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
with this sequence of norms satisfies Ruan’s axioms (properties (i) and (ii) above), and by Ruan’s theorem is an operator space.

Considering the special case
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{C}
, we find that
CB(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{C})
is an operator space. On the other hand, one may observe that
CB(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{C})
coincides with
\mathcal{X}^{*}=B(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{C})
with identical norms as Banach spaces. Therefore, the Banach dual
\mathcal{X}^{*}
of any operator space has a canonical operator space structure.

3.2.
Column and row operator spaces

Here we present two important examples of operator spaces which will play pivotal roles in the construction of vector-valued Schatten spaces in the next section.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two Hilbert spaces. Then the map

\displaystyle X\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
X&0\end{bmatrix}

is an isometric embedding of
B(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
into
B(\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Therefore,
B(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
is naturally endowed with an operator space structure. Note that the induced norm on
M_{n}\big(B(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})\big)
coincides with the norm of
B(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{n},\mathcal{H}_{2}^{n})
. This means that to understand the operator space structure of
B(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
we can indeed ignore the above embedding and directly work with
B(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{n},\mathcal{H}_{2}^{n})

[
39
, p. 22]
.

Considering spacial cases of the above construction, we find that both
B(\mathbb{C},\mathcal{H})
and
B(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{C})
are operator spaces. Indeed, for any Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the Banach spaces

\mathcal{H}_{c}:=B(\mathbb{C},\mathcal{H})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{H}_{r}:=B(\mathcal{H}^{*},\mathbb{C})

have operator space structures.
\mathcal{H}_{c}
is called the
column operator space
while
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is called the
row operator space

[
38
, p. 25]
.

We note that elements of
\mathcal{H}_{c}
and
\mathcal{H}_{r}
can be identified with those of
\mathcal{H}
. To this end, for any
v\in\mathcal{H}
define
v^{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{c}
and
v^{r}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
by

v^{c}(\alpha)=\alpha v,\qquad v^{r}(w)=\langle w,v\rangle,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C},w\in\mathcal{H}^{*}.

Then,
v^{c}\mapsto v^{r}
provides an isometry between
\mathcal{H}^{c}
and
\mathcal{H}^{r}
as Banach spaces. Nevertheless, the operator space structures of
\mathcal{H}^{c}
and
\mathcal{H}^{r}
do not coincide under this identification. To verify this, suppose that
n\leq\dim\mathcal{H}
, and define
v_{ij}\in\mathcal{H}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
as follows: let
v_{ij}=0
if
j\neq 1
and let
\{v_{11},\dots,v_{n1}\}\subset\mathcal{H}
be orthonormal. Then we have

\displaystyle\big\|\big(v_{ij}^{c}\big)\big\|_{M_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{c})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}}}{{\sum|\alpha_{k}|^{2}=1}}}\Big\|\Big(\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}v_{ij}\Big)\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}}}{{\sum|\alpha_{k}|^{2}=1}}}\big\|\big(\alpha_{1}v_{i1}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\big(v_{i1}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i}\|v_{i1}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n},

while

\displaystyle\big\|\big(v_{ij}^{r}\big)\big\|_{M_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{r})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}}}{{\sum\|w_{k}\|^{2}=1}}}\Big\|\Big(\sum_{j}\langle w_{j},v_{ij}\rangle\Big)\Big\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}}}{{\sum\|w_{k}\|^{2}=1}}}\big\|\big(\langle w_{1},v_{i1}\rangle\big)\big\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}}}{{\sum\|w_{k}\|^{2}=1}}}\Big(\sum_{i}|\langle w_{1},v_{i1}\rangle|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}}}{{\sum\|w_{k}\|^{2}=1}}}\|w_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{*}}

\displaystyle=1,

where in the penultimate equality we use the fact that
\{v_{11},\dots,v_{n1}\}
is orthonormal.

For simplicity of notation, we denote

\mathcal{C}=(\ell_{2})_{c},\quad\mathcal{R}=(\ell_{2})_{r}.

In the finite-dimensional case of
\mathcal{H}=\ell_{2}^{n}
, we denote the associated spaces by
\mathcal{C}^{n},\mathcal{R}^{n}
.
Note that
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{R}
can be identified with the spaces of column and row vectors, respectively. In what follows, we denote elements of
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}
with superscripts
v^{c},w^{r}
to highlight that they are column and row vectors, respectively.

Remark 3.2
.

In the following, we will also need
(\ell^{*}_{2})_{c},(\ell_{2}^{*})_{r}
. Nevertheless, since these spaces can again be identified by column and row vectors, we abuse the above notations and denote them by
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}
, respectively.

3.3.
Interpolation of operator spaces

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
be a compatible couple of Banach spaces. If further
\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}
are operator spaces, then
M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
and
M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
with their specific norms form a compatible couple of Banach spaces. Thus, we may consider the complex interpolation space
[M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{1})]_{\theta}
, for every
n\geq 1
, and endow
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}
with a matrix norm structure as follows:

\big\|[x_{ij}]\big\|_{M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{\theta})}:=\big\|[x_{ij}]\big\|_{[M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{1})]_{\theta}},\ \ \ [x_{ij}]\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{\theta}).

It can be shown that these norms satisfy Ruan’s axioms, so
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}
turns into an operator space
[
37
,
39
]
. Moreover, we have the following analogue of Theorem
2.5
.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
37
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})

is the (Banach) dual of
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
.

.

We argued that

3.4.
Haagerup tensor product and interpolation

Haagerup tensor product provides a special operator space structure for the tensor product of two operator spaces. Its construction is an important step towards understanding operator-valued Schatten spaces and is reviewed in this subsection.

Let
X=[X_{ij}]\in M_{n,m}(\mathcal{X})
and
Y=[Y_{ij}]\in M_{m,n}(\mathcal{Y})
, where
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are two operator spaces. Then the element
X\odot Y\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})
is defined by

X\odot Y:=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{ik}\otimes Y_{kj}\right].

Using this notation for
Z\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})
, we define its
Haagerup matrix norm
by

\|Z\|_{h,n}:=\inf\big\{\|X\|_{n,m}\|Y\|_{m,n}:Z=X\odot Y\big\},

where the infimum is taken over all
m
and all
X,Y
as above.

We verify that
\|\cdot\|_{h,n}
satisfies the triangle inequality and is thus an actual norm
[
15
]
. For
Z_{i}\in M_{n}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y})
,
i=1,2
, and arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, let
X_{i}\in M_{n,m_{i}}(\mathcal{X})
and
Y_{i}\in M_{m_{i},n}(\mathcal{Y})
be such that
Z_{i}=X_{i}\odot Y_{i}
and

\displaystyle\|X_{i}\|_{n,m_{i}}\|Y_{i}\|_{m_{i},n}\leq\|Z_{i}\|_{h,n}+\epsilon.

(3.2)

After scaling
X_{i},Y_{i}
, we can assume that
\|X_{i}\|_{n,m_{i}}=\|Y_{i}\|_{m_{i},n}
. Next, define

\widetilde{X}=\big[X_{1},X_{2}\big]\in M_{n,m_{1}+m_{2}}(\mathcal{X}),\qquad\widetilde{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}\\
Y_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in M_{m_{1}+m_{2},n}(\mathcal{Y}).

Then, we have
Z_{1}+Z_{2}=\widetilde{X}\odot\widetilde{Y}
, and

\displaystyle\|Z_{1}+Z_{2}\|_{h,n}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\widetilde{X}\big\|_{n,m_{1}+m_{2}}\big\|\widetilde{Y}\big\|_{m_{1}+m_{2},n}

\displaystyle=\big\|\widetilde{X}\widetilde{X}^{*}\big\|_{n}^{1/2}\big\|\widetilde{Y}^{*}\widetilde{Y}\big\|_{n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\big\|X_{1}X_{1}^{*}+X_{2}X_{2}^{*}\big\|_{n}^{1/2}\big\|Y^{*}_{1}Y_{1}+Y^{*}_{2}Y_{2}\big\|_{n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big\|X_{1}X_{1}^{*}+X_{2}X_{2}^{*}\big\|_{n}+\big\|Y^{*}_{1}Y_{1}+Y^{*}_{2}Y_{2}\big\|_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big\|X_{1}X_{1}^{*}\big\|_{n}+\big\|X_{2}X_{2}^{*}\big\|_{n}+\big\|Y^{*}_{1}Y_{1}\big\|_{n}+\big\|Y^{*}_{2}Y_{2}\big\|_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big\|X_{1}\big\|^{2}_{n}+\big\|X_{2}\big\|^{2}_{n}+\big\|Y_{1}\big\|^{2}_{n}+\big\|Y_{2}\big\|^{2}_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|Z_{1}\|_{h,n}+\|Z_{2}\|_{h,n}+2\epsilon,

where in the last line we use (
3.2
) and
\|X_{i}\|_{n,m_{i}}=\|Y_{i}\|_{m_{i},n}
. Then,
\|Z_{1}+Z_{2}\|_{h,n}\leq\|Z_{1}\|_{h,n}+\|Z_{2}\|_{h,n}
since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary.

It can also be verified that this matrix norm structure satisfies Ruan’s axioms. Thus, after completion in this norm, we obtain an operator space structure on
\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Y}
called the
Haagerup tensor product
which we denote by
\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}
.

The Haagerup tensor product is associative, that is,
(\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y})\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Z})
completely isometrically. Nonetheless, it is
not
commutative as in general
\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}
carry different norms.

In the following proposition we collect some properties of Haagerup tensor product in regard to column and row Hilbert spaces. This proposition is a key step towards understanding the construction of operator-valued Schatten spaces in the next section. Its proof is included in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}
be two Hilbert spaces. Then,

(i)

we have the complete isometries

\mathcal{H}_{c}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{K}_{c}=(\mathcal{H}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{K})_{c}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{K}_{r}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{H}_{r}=(\mathcal{H}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{K})_{r},

(ii)

and

\mathcal{S}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}_{c}\otimes_{h}(\mathcal{H}^{*})_{r}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}_{r}\otimes_{h}(\mathcal{H}^{*})_{c}.

In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}=C\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{C}
completely isometrically.

(iii)

Moreover,
M_{1,d}(\mathcal{C}^{d})=M_{d,1}(\mathcal{R}^{d})=\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{d}
and
M_{1,d}(\mathcal{R}^{d})=M_{d,1}(\mathcal{C}^{d})=\mathcal{S}_{2}^{d}
isometrically.

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{1})
be two compatible couples of operator spaces. Then it can be verified that

(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{1})
is also a compatible couple of operator spaces. The following theorem states that an interpolation space obtained from this couple can be written as the Haagerup tensor product of the interpolation spaces obtained from respective components. This theorem plays a pivotal role in identifying the norms of operator-valued Schatten spaces as variational expressions.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
37
,
24

)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{1})
be two compatible couples of operator spaces. Then, for every
0<\theta<1
, we have a complete isometry

[\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{1}]_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}\otimes_{h}[\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{1}]_{\theta}.

(3.3)

(
i.e., the Haagerup tensor product and interpolation are commuting operations.
)

The proof that follows is essentially borrowed from

C

subharmonicity lemma

Proof.

To simplify notation, we denote
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}

\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{1}]_{\theta}

\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{Z}_{0},\mathcal{Z}_{1}]_{\theta}

\mathcal{Z}_{j}=\mathcal{X}_{j}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{j}

3.3
) is tantamount to

\|W\|_{M_{n}(\mathcal{Z}_{\theta})}=\|W\|_{M_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{\theta}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{\theta})},\ \ \ n\geq 1.

We assume that all the spaces are finite-dimensional,

t
since
A^{(b)}(b+it)A^{(b)}(b+it)^{*}\leq F(b+it)F(b+it)^{*}
.

Now, define the holomorphic function
G(z)
on
\mathbb{S}
by

G(z):=F(z)^{-1}\cdot h(z).

By (
3.9
) and the preceding paragraph we obtain

\displaystyle G(b+it)

\displaystyle=F(b+it)^{-1}\cdot A^{(b)}(b+it)B^{(b)}(b+it)

\displaystyle=S^{(b)}(b+it)B^{(b)}(b+it).

This implies that
G(z)
is bounded, and by (
3.11
) for almost every
t
we have

\|G(b+it)\|_{M_{d,1}(\mathcal{Y}_{b})}\leq\|B^{(b)}(b+it)\|_{M_{d,1}(\mathcal{Y}_{b})}\leq\|h(b+it)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{b}}.

(3.14)

Finally, using (
3.8
), (
3.13
), (
3.14
), and part (ii) of Lemma
B.3
, together with the identities just above the lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\|h(\theta)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\|F(\theta)\|_{M_{1,d}(\mathcal{X}_{b})}\|G(\theta)\|_{M_{d,1}(\mathcal{Y}_{b})}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\sum_{b}\int P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\Big(\|F(b+is)\|_{M_{1,d}(\mathcal{X}_b)} \|G(b+is)\|_{M_{d,1}(\mathcal{Y}_b)} \Big)\differential s

\displaystyle\leq\exp\sum_{b}\int P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\Big( (1+\epsilon) \|h(b+is)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_b}\Big)\differential s

\displaystyle\leq\exp\sum_{b}\int P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\bigg( (1+\epsilon)\Big(\sup_t \|h(b+it)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_b}\Big)\bigg)\differential s

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)\Big(\sup_{t}\|h(it)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{0}}\Big)^{1-\theta}\Big(\sup_{t}\|h(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\Big)^{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\max\big\{\sup_{t}\|h(b+it)\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{b}}:b=0,1\big\}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)^{2}\|W\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}}.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the desired inequality is obtained.
∎

4.
Operator-valued Schatten spaces

In this section, building upon the previous constructions and results, we finally introduce the family
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, for an operator space
\mathcal{X}
. Indeed, we first introduce the endpoint spaces
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{X}]
as certain Haagerup tensor products of
\mathcal{X}
with the column and row operator spaces, and define
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
,
1<p<\infty
, to be an interpolation space between them. Then, by employing Theorem
3.7
, we establish an identification of
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
which allows us to view
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]
,
\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{X}]
and
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
in a unified way. Next, we make use of this identification to show that the family
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
,
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, is an interpolation scale. In the final part of this section we establish variational characterizations of the norms of
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
and
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
.

4.1.
Definition and basic properties

For an operator space
\mathcal{X}
, we first put

\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]:=\mathcal{C}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{X}]:=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{C}.

(4.1)

These definitions are consistent with, and indeed motivated by, the identities
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}=C\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{C}
(see Proposition
3.6
(ii)). In fact, by taking
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}
we get
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathbb{C}]=\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathbb{C}]=\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

Recalling the notation developed in Subsection
3.2
, if
\{e_{i}^{c}:\,i\}
and
\{e^{r}_{i}:\,i\}
are the orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
and
\mathcal{R}^{n}
, respectively, corresponding to the standard basis of
\ell_{2}^{n}
, then one can identify
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{n}[\mathcal{X}]
with
M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
via the natural completely isometric map

[X_{ij}]\mapsto\sum_{i,j}e_{i}^{c}\otimes X_{ij}\otimes e_{j}^{r}.

(4.2)

One can also identify
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{n}[\mathcal{X}]
with
M_{n}(\mathcal{X})
similarly. However, this latter identification will not be a complete isometry.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. The
\mathcal{X}
-valued
p
-Schatten space

\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
is defined to be the operator space

\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]:=\big[\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}],\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{X}]\big]_{\frac{1}{p}}.

\mathcal{S}_{p}^{n}[\mathcal{X}]
for
n\geq 1
is defined similarly.

In the following theorem, we obtain an identification of
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
for
1<p<\infty
that complements the identities in (
4.1
), and allows us to treat
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]
,
\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{X}]
and
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
(
1<p<\infty
) on an equal footing. Before proceeding further, we introduce a piece of notation:

\mathcal{R}(\theta):=[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}]_{\theta}=[\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}]_{1-\theta},\ \ \ \mathcal{R}(0):=\mathcal{R},\ \ \ \mathcal{R}(1):=\mathcal{C}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. Then we have completely isometrically

\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]=\mathcal{R}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{p}\big)\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}\big(\tfrac{1}{p}\big).

Proof.

By associativity of the Haagerup tensor product and an iterated use of Theorem
3.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{C}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{C}]_{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}]_{\frac{1}{p}}\otimes_{h}[\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R},\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{C}]_{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}]_{\frac{1}{p}}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}]_{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{p}\big)\otimes_{h}\mathcal{X}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}\big(\tfrac{1}{p}\big),

as desired.
∎

Note that for
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}
, this result yields the completely isometric identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{p}=\mathcal{R}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{p}\big)\otimes_{h}\mathcal{R}\big(\tfrac{1}{p}\big),

which is the analogue of Proposition
3.6
(ii) for
1<p<\infty
. Note also that one can view the elements of
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
as
\mathcal{X}
-valued matrices via a similar identification as in (
4.2
). However, the identification will not be a complete isometry unless
p=\infty
.

In the next result, we collect some basic properties of the operator-valued Schatten spaces which will be used in the sequel. For the sake of completeness, the proofs are given in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}
be operator spaces, and
1\leq p_{0},p_{1},q_{0},q_{1}\leq\infty
. Then the following statements hold true.

(i) Interpolation: If
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
is a compatible couple, then we have completely isometrically

\mathcal{S}_{p_{\theta}}[\mathcal{X}_{\theta}]=\big[\mathcal{S}_{p_{0}}[\mathcal{X}_{0}],\,\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}}[\mathcal{X}_{1}]\big]_{\theta},

(4.3)

where
p_{\theta}
is given by
\frac{1}{p_{\theta}}=\frac{1-\theta}{p_{0}}+\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}
.
In particular, if
q_{\theta}
is given by
\frac{1}{q_{\theta}}=\frac{1-\theta}{q_{0}}+\frac{\theta}{q_{1}}
, we have completely isometrically

\mathcal{S}_{p_{\theta}}[\mathcal{S}_{q_{\theta}}]=\big[\mathcal{S}_{p_{0}}[\mathcal{S}_{q_{0}}],\,\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}}[\mathcal{S}_{q_{1}}]\big]_{\theta}.

(4.4)

(ii) Duality: Let
1<p\leq\infty
and
p^{\prime}
be given by
1=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. Then we have completely isometrically

\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}[\mathcal{X}^{*}].

(iii) Fubini’s theorem: For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have completely isometrically

\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{p}]=\mathcal{S}_{p}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2}).

4.2.
Pisier’s variational formula

Here, we finally prove the promised characterization of the norm in the space
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
. Its proof is based on the so-called Pisier’s variational formula.

Theorem 4.4

(Pisier’s formula
[
38
]
)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Then for every
T\in\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]
we have

\|T\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{X}]}=\inf\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}},

where the infimum is taken over all
A,B\in\mathcal{S}_{2p}
and
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{X}]
such that
T=AXB
. As explained above, we understand the identity
T=AXB
by thinking of
T,X
as
\mathcal{X}
-valued matrices.

Proof.

Considering
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{d}[\mathcal{X}]
that is easier to work with, we note that
\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}^{d}_{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}=\|X\|_{M_{d}(\mathcal{X})}
.
Then, using Theorem
4.2
and the definition of Haagerup tensor product, we need to evaluate
\|A\|_{M_{1,n}(\mathcal{R}^{d}(1-\theta))}
and
\|B\|_{M_{n,1}(\mathcal{R}^{d}(\theta))}
, where
\theta=\frac{1}{p}
. We first pad
A
with zeros if necessary in order to assume that
n=d
.
Next, by the definition of the interpolation of operator spaces we have

\displaystyle M_{1,n}(\mathcal{R}^{n}(1-\theta))

\displaystyle=\big[M_{1,n}(\mathcal{R}^{n}),M_{1,n}(\mathcal{C}^{n})\big]_{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=\big[\mathcal{S}_{2}^{n},\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{n}\big]_{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{2p}^{n},

where the second equality follows from part (iii) of Proposition
3.6
, and the third equality follows from (
3.1
) and the reiteration theorem. Therefore,
\|A\|_{M_{1,n}(\mathcal{R}^{n}(1-\theta))}=\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}^{n}}
. Similarly, it can shown that
\|B\|_{M_{n,1}(\mathcal{R}^{n}(\theta))}
equals the
2p
-norm of
B
. Then the desired result follows.
∎

Theorem 4.5

(
[
38
,
20
]
)
.

Let
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
and define
r
by

\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}.

Then we have the following:

(i)

For every
X
in
\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
define

\|X\|_{(p,q)}:=\inf\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}},

(4.5)

where the infimum is taken over all
A,B\in\mathcal{S}_{2r}
and
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{q}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})
such that
X=AYB
. Then we have
\|X\|_{(p,q)}=\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}
. We note that the multiplication
AYB
is understood as before, and indeed is equal to
(A\otimes I_{\ell_{2}})Y(B\otimes I_{\ell_{2}})
, where
I_{\ell_{2}}\in B(\ell_{2})
is the identity operator.

(ii)

For every
X
in
\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{p}]
define

\|X\|_{(q,p)}:=\sup\|AXB\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})},

(4.6)

where the supremum is taken over all
A,B\in\mathcal{S}_{2r}
such that
\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}},\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\leq 1
. Then we have
\|X\|_{(q,p)}=\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{p}]}
.

We note that it is not immediate that
\|\cdot\|_{(q,p)}
defined by (
4.6
) satisfies the triangle inequality. By the above theorem, however,
\|\cdot\|_{(q,p)}
is really a norm and satisfies the triangle inequality.

Proof.

(i) We first show that
\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\leq\|X\|_{(p,q)}
. Suppose that
X\in\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
,
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{q}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})
and
A,B\in\mathcal{S}_{2r}
are such that
X=AYB
. Suppose also that
Y=CZD
is an arbitrary factorization of
Y
with
C,D\in\mathcal{S}_{2q}
and
Z\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
. Then,
X=AC\cdot Z\cdot DB
and by Hölder’s inequality we have
AC,DB\in\mathcal{S}_{2p}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}

\displaystyle\leq\|AC\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\|Z\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|DB\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{(Theorem \ref{Pisier's formula})}

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|C\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2q}}\|Z\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|D\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2q}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\ \ \ \ \quad\ \text{(H\"{o}lder's inequality)}.

Taking infimum over all
C,D,Z
as above, and using Theorem
4.4
gives

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}},

where in the second line we use Proposition
4.3
(iii). Finally, taking infimum over the choices
A,B,Y
as above, we obtain
\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\leq\|X\|_{(p,q)}
as desired.

To prove the reverse inequality, let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary. Then, there exist a
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]
and
A,B\in\mathcal{S}_{2p}
such that
X=AYB
and
\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\leq\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}+\epsilon
.
We note by the use of polar decomposition and absorbing unitaries into
Y
without changing its norm, we may assume that
A,B
are positive.
In this case,
A^{\frac{p}{q}},B^{\frac{p}{q}}\in\mathcal{S}_{2q}
and
A^{\frac{p}{r}},B^{\frac{p}{r}}\in\mathcal{S}_{2r}
are well-defined. Hence,

\displaystyle\|X\|_{(p,q)}

\displaystyle=\|AYB\|_{(p,q)}

\displaystyle=\|A^{\frac{p}{r}}\cdot(A^{\frac{p}{q}}\cdot Y\cdot B^{\frac{p}{q}})\cdot B^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{(p,q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|A^{\frac{p}{q}}\cdot Y\cdot B^{\frac{p}{q}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}(\ell_{2}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})}\|B^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}

\displaystyle=\|A^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|A^{\frac{p}{q}}\cdot Y\cdot B^{\frac{p}{q}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\ \ \ \ \qquad\quad\quad\text{(Proposition \ref{interpolation}(iii))}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|A^{\frac{p}{q}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2q}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B^{\frac{p}{q}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2q}}\|B^{\frac{p}{r}}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(Theorem \ref{Pisier's formula})}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}^{\frac{p}{r}}\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}^{\frac{p}{q}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}^{\frac{p}{q}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}^{\frac{p}{r}}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}+\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, it follows that
\|X\|_{(p,q)}\leq\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}[\mathcal{S}_{q}]}
, and the proof of (i) is complete.

(ii) We prove this part by exploiting the duality relation
\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{p}]=\mathcal{S}_{q^{\prime}}[\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}]^{*}
given in Proposition
4.3
(ii). Using this fact along with part (i) for
q^{\prime}\leq p^{\prime}
, we compute:

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q}[\mathcal{S}_{p}]}

\displaystyle=\|X\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q^{\prime}}[\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}]^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y}\frac{|\tr(Y^* X)|}{\|Y\|_{\mathcal{S}_{q^{\prime}}[\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}]}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y}\frac{|\tr(Y^* X)|}{\|Y\|_{(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y}\ \sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A,B,Z}}{{Y=AZB}}}\frac{|\tr(Y^* X)|}{\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Z\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,B,Z}\frac{\big|\tr(B^* Z^* A^*X)\big|}{\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Z\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,B,Z}\frac{\big|\tr\big(Z^*(A^* X B^*)\big)\big|}{\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|Z\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,B}\ \frac{\|A^{*}XB^{*}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}}{\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,B}\ \frac{\|AXB\|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}}{\|A\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2r}}}

\displaystyle=\|X\|_{(q,p)},

as desired.
∎

We conclude this section with the following technical remark which will come in handy later on. For the sake of completeness we include the proofs in Appendix
A
.

5.
Applications in quantum information theory

In this section we present some applications of the above tools in quantum information theory. Here, we denote Hilbert spaces (mostly finite-dimensional) by
\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y}
where subscripts
X,Y
indicate quantum subsystems. We add a subscript to an operator
T_{X}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{X})
to emphasize that it acts on subsystem
X
, and
I_{X}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{X})
is the identity operator. A density matrix (quantum state) is a positive operator with normalized trace. For a density matrix
\rho_{XY}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{X}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{Y})
, its marginal states
\rho_{X}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{X})
and
\rho_{Y}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
are defined via partial trace. Quantum operations (super-operators) are completely positive trace-preserving (cptp) maps
\Phi:B(\mathcal{H}_{X})\to B(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
. We denote the identity super-operator acting on subsystem
X
by
\mathcal{I}_{X}
.

Recall that for a distribution
p_{X}
over discrete set
\mathcal{X}
, its (Shannon) entropy is defined by

H(X)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log p(x).

In the quantum case, for a density matrix
\rho_{X}
acting on the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{X}
, its (von Neumann) entropy is defined by

H(X)=-\tr(\rho_X\log\rho_X).

For certain applications it is advantageous to define other forms of entropies that somehow approximate Shannon or von Neumann entropies. A well-known such quantity is the Rényi entropy that for a parameter
\alpha
is given by

H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\tr(\rho_X^\alpha),

which in the classical case (when
\rho_{X}
is diagonal with values
p(x),x\in\mathcal{X}
, on the diagonal) turns into
H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\big(\sum_x p(x)^\alpha\big)
. Rényi entropy is an approximation of the usual entropy since

\lim_{\alpha\to 1}H_{\alpha}(X)=H(X).

Now,
L_{p}
-spaces naturally appear in the study of entropic quantities since Rényi entropy can be stated in terms of the norm in these spaces:

H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\|\rho_{X}\|_{\alpha}.

Therefore, the properties of
L_{p}
-spaces are applicable in the understanding of these entropic quantities.
We emphasize that although Rényi quantities can also be defined for
\alpha<1
, hereafter we assume that
\alpha\geq 1
so that we can use the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
is really a norm.

5.1.
Quantum conditional Rényi entropy

Given a bipartite distribution
p_{XY}
, the condition entropy
H(X|Y)
is defined by
H(X|Y)=\sum_{y}p(y)H(X|Y=y)
as the average entropy of the conditional distribution
p_{X|Y=y}
. Generalization of this definition to the quantum case is tricky since conditional states do not have a well-defined interpretation. Nevertheless, it can be verified that

\displaystyle H(X|Y)=H(XY)-H(Y),

(5.1)

and this formula is readily generalized to the quantum case as the definition of quantum conditional entropy. The definition of conditional Rényi entropy in the quantum case is even more tricky since (
5.1
), called the
chain rule
, no longer holds for
\alpha\neq 1
. Thus, we should take a different approach to define the quantum conditional Rényi entropies.

Umegaki’s divergence (also called quantum relative entropy) is another important quantity in information theory which can be considered as a parent quantity for entropy. For a density matrix
\rho_{X}
, and positive operator
\sigma_{X}
, that is not necessarily normalized, we define their Umegaki’s divergence by

D(\rho_{X}\|\sigma_{X}):=\begin{cases}\tr\rho_{X}(\log\rho_{X}-\log\sigma_{X}),\ \ \ \ \text{if}\ \ {\rm supp}\,\rho_{X}\leq{\rm supp}\,\sigma_{X},\\[7.22743pt]
+\infty,~~~~~\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Considering the case where
\rho_{X},\sigma_{X}
are diagonal, divergence in the classical case is also derived. Moreover, letting
\sigma_{X}=I_{X}
to be the identity operator, we find that

D(\rho_{X}\|I_{X})=-H(\rho_{X})
. The conditional entropy can also be stated in terms of divergence:

H(X|Y)=-D(\rho_{XY}\|I_{X}\otimes\rho_{Y}).

Even more, we have

\displaystyle H(X|Y)=-\inf_{\sigma_{Y}}D(\rho_{XY}\|I_{X}\otimes\sigma_{Y}),

(5.2)

where the infimum is taken over all density matrices
\sigma_{Y}
. This identity can be proven using
D(\rho_{XY}\|I_{X}\otimes\sigma_{Y})=D(\rho_{XY}\|I_{X}\otimes\rho_{Y})+D(\rho_{Y}\|\sigma_{Y})
that is easy to verify, and the fact that
D(\rho_{Y}\|\sigma_{Y})\geq 0
for any two density matrices
\rho_{Y},\sigma_{Y}
. Equation (
5.2
) suggests that having a good definition of quantum Rényi divergence, we can define a conditional version of quantum Rényi entropy.

Rényi divergence in the classical case is given by

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p_{X}\|q_{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{x}p(x)^{\alpha}q(x)^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\|q_{X}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}p_{X}\Big\|_{\alpha},

and we have
H_{\alpha}(p_{X})=-D_{\alpha}(p_{X}\|I_{X})
as expected. Next, inspired by (
5.2
) we may define the conditional Rényi entropy by

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X|Y)=-\inf_{q_{Y}}D_{\alpha}(p_{XY}\|I_{A}\otimes q_{Y}).

(5.3)

Trying to generalize these definitions to the quantum case a complication arises: since two density matrices
\rho_{X},\sigma_{X}
do not necessarily commute,
\sigma_{X}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}
and
\rho_{X}
can be “multiplied” in different orders. This amounts to different notions of quantum Rényi divergence, yet here we limit ourselves to the so-called
sandwiched

,

]
defined by

D_{\alpha}(\rho_{X}\|\sigma_{X})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\|\sigma_{X}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho_{X}\sigma_{X}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\Big\|_{\alpha},

Based on this and following (
5.3
) we define the quantum conditional Rényi entropy by

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\sigma_{Y}}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{XY}\|I_{X}\otimes\sigma_{Y})

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\sigma_{Y}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log\|\Big(I_{X}\otimes\sigma_{Y}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\Big)\rho_{XY}\Big(I_{X}\otimes\sigma_{Y}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\Big)\Big\|_{\alpha}.

For

\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}

is the Hölder conjugate of

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\log\Big(\inf_{\sigma_Y} \Big\| \Big(I_X\otimes\sigma_Y^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha'}}\Big)\rho_{XY}\Big(I_X\otimes\sigma_Y^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha'}}\Big)\Big\|_\alpha\Big)

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\log\Bigg(\inf_{\stackrel{T_Y\geq 0}{\|T_Y\|_{\alpha'}=1}} \Big\| \Big(I_X\otimes T_Y^{-\frac 12}\Big)\rho_{XY}\Big(I_X\otimes T_Y^{-\frac 12}\Big)\Big\|_\alpha\Bigg).

Then, comparing this equation with (

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X|Y)=-\alpha^{\prime}\log\|\rho_{YX}\big\|_{(1,\alpha)}.

This is a key identity that connects operator-valued Schatten spaces to information theoretic quantities.

This holds since using

(iv) Let

. Let
\sigma_{XX^{\prime}}
be a

It is well-known that the conditional von Neumann entropy satisfies a
uniform
continuity bound
[
1
,
49
]
, i.e., for two quantum states
\rho_{XY},\sigma_{XY}
satisfying
\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{XY}-\sigma_{XY}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
we have

\big|H(X|Y)_{\rho}-H(X|Y)_{\sigma}\big|\leq 2\epsilon\log d_{X}+(1+\epsilon)h\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\Big),

where
h(x)=-x\log x-(1-x)\log(1-x)
is the binary entropy function. The crucial point in this continuity bound is that it is independent of the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
. Such a continuity bound for the quantum conditional Rényi entropy is first found in
[
30
]
(see also
[
19
,
25
]
for related results). Here, we prove another such continuity bound that is stronger than the result of
[
30
]
in some regimes of parameters.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\rho_{XY},\sigma_{XY}
be two quantum states satisfying
\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{XY}-\sigma_{XY}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
. Then we have

\big|H_{\alpha}(X|Y)_{\rho}-H_{\alpha}(X|Y)_{\sigma}\big|\leq\alpha^{\prime}\log(1+2\epsilon d_X^{2/\alpha'}).

We note that our bound diverges as
\alpha\to 1
, yet it is stronger than the bound of
[
30
]
for large values of
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let us assume with no loss of generality that
\|\rho_{XY}-\sigma_{XY}\|_{1}=2\epsilon
. Then, there are density matrices
\mu_{XY},\nu_{XY}
such that

\rho_{XY}-\sigma_{XY}=\epsilon(\mu_{XY}-\nu_{XY}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Big|\|\rho_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}-\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{YX}-\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\|\mu_{YX}-\nu_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\big(\|\mu_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}+\|\nu_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}\big).

On the other hand, as a consequence of part (iv) of Proposition
5.1
we have

\|\mu_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)},\|\nu_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}\leq d_{X}^{1/\alpha^{\prime}}.

Hence,

\Big|\|\rho_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}-\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}\Big|\leq 2\epsilon d_{X}^{1/\alpha^{\prime}}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\rho_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}}{\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}+2\epsilon d_{X}^{1/\alpha^{\prime}}}{\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2\epsilon d_{X}^{1/\alpha^{\prime}}}{\|\sigma_{YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\epsilon d_{X}^{2/\alpha^{\prime}},

where in the last inequality we once again use part (iv) of Proposition
5.1
. Taking the logarithm of both sides and multiplying by
\alpha^{\prime}
yields

H_{\alpha}(X|Y)_{\sigma}-H_{\alpha}(X|Y)_{\rho}\leq\alpha^{\prime}\log(1+2\epsilon d_X^{2/\alpha'}).

∎

The following theorem is a generalization of the chain rule for the von Neumann entropy.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 1
such that

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}=\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}.

Then for any tripartite density matrix
\rho_{XYZ}
we have

H_{\alpha}(XY|Z)\geq H_{\beta}(X|YZ)+H_{\gamma}(Y|Z).

This theorem is first proven in
[
13
]
. The following proof, that is much simpler than the original proof in
[
13
]
, is found by the first author of this paper and independently by McKinlay and Tomamichel (private communications). See also
[
33
]
for other results in this direction.

Proof.

Note first that the condition on
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
may be rewritten as
{\alpha^{\prime}}={\beta^{\prime}}+{\gamma^{\prime}}
. Then, for
\theta:=\gamma^{\prime}/\alpha^{\prime}=1-\beta^{\prime}/\alpha^{\prime}
we have

\theta\in[0,1]
and

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{1-\theta}{1}+\frac{\theta}{\gamma},\qquad\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{1-\theta}{\beta}+\frac{\theta}{1}.

Now by invoking Proposition
4.3
(i) we have

\mathcal{S}_{1}\big[\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}[\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}]\big]=\Big[\mathcal{S}_{1}\big[\mathcal{S}_{1}[\mathcal{S}_{\beta}]\big],\mathcal{S}_{1}\big[\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}[\mathcal{S}_{1}]\big]\Big]_{\theta}.

That is, the
(1,\alpha,\alpha)
-norm is obtained via interpolation between the
(1,1,\beta)
- and
(1,\gamma,1)
-norms.
Therefore, by Remark
2.3
we have

\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,\alpha,\alpha)}\leq\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,1,\beta)}^{1-\theta}\cdot\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,\gamma,1)}^{\theta}.

On the other hand, by Proposition
4.3
(iii) we have

\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,\alpha,\alpha)}=\|\rho_{Z,YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)},

and

\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,1,\beta)}=\|\rho_{ZY,X}\|_{(1,\beta)}.

Furthermore, as shown in
[
11
]
we have

\|\rho_{ZYX}\|_{(1,\gamma,1)}=\|\rho_{ZY}\|_{(1,\gamma)}.

Putting all these together we arrive at

\|\rho_{Z,YX}\|_{(1,\alpha)}\leq\|\rho_{ZY,X}\|_{(1,\beta)}^{1-\theta}\cdot\|\rho_{ZY}\|_{(1,\gamma)}^{\theta}.

Now taking the logarithm of both sides and multiplying by
-\alpha^{\prime}
, the desired inequality is obtained.
∎

5.2.
Rényi coherent information

The coherent information
I(X\rangle Y)
of a state
\rho_{XY}
is defined by

I(X\rangle Y)=H(Y)-H(XY)=-H(X|Y).

Then, given a cptp map
\Phi:B(\mathcal{H}_{X})\to B(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
, its coherent information is defined by

\displaystyle I^{\rm{coh}}(\Phi)=\max_{\rho_{RX}}I(R\rangle Y)_{\sigma},

(5.5)

where
I(R\rangle Y)_{\sigma}
is computed with respect to

\sigma_{RY}=\mathcal{I}_{R}\otimes\Phi(\rho_{RX}),

and
\mathcal{H}_{R}
is an auxiliary reference Hilbert space.
It is well-known that
Q(\Phi)
, the quantum capacity of
\Phi
, i.e., its optimal rate for sending quantum information with asymptotically vanishing error is equal to
[
47
]

Q(\Phi)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}I^{\rm{coh}}(\Phi^{\otimes n}),

where
\Phi^{\otimes n}
is the
n
-fold tensor product of
\Phi
.

Since coherent information is expressed in terms of conditional entropy, we may define Rényi coherent information based on the Rényi conditional entropy.
Indeed, based on (
5.5
) we may define the
\alpha
-Rényi coherent information of a cptp map by
[
17
]

\displaystyle I^{\rm{coh}}_{\alpha}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho_{RX}}I_{\alpha}(R\rangle Y)_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}\log\Big( \max_{\rho_{RX}} \| \Phi\otimes\mathcal I_R (\rho_{XR}) \|_{(1, \alpha)} \Big).

Proposition 5.4
.

I^{{\rm coh}}_{\alpha}(\Phi)=\alpha^{\prime}\log\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}\|_{(1,1)\rightarrow(1,\alpha)}
.

Proof.

We need to show that

\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}\|_{(1,1)\rightarrow(1,\alpha)}=\max_{\rho_{RA}}\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho_{XR})\|_{(1,\alpha)},

where the maximum is taken over density matrices
\rho_{XR}
. Since
\|\rho_{XR}\|_{(1,1)}=\|\rho_{XR}\|_{1}=1
for any density matrix
\rho_{XR}
, it suffices to show that the maximum in

\max_{F_{XR}:\|F\|_{1}=1}\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(F_{XR})\|_{(1,\alpha)},

is achieved at a positive
F
. To prove this we follow similar steps as in the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 12]
.

Let
F_{XR}
be such that
\|F\|_{1}=1
. Then, by the polar decomposition there exists a unitary
U_{XR}
and density matrix
\rho_{XR}
such that
F=U\rho
. Now, the block matrix

\begin{bmatrix}U\rho^{1/2}\\
\\
\rho^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{1/2}U^{*}&\rho^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U\rho U^{*}&U\rho\\
\rho U^{*}&\rho\end{bmatrix},

is positive. Then, since
\Phi
is completely positive,

\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho U^{*})&\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho)\\
\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho U^{*})&\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho)\end{bmatrix},

is positive.
Therefore, using
[
8
, Proposition 1.3.2]
there exists a contraction
K_{YR}
such that

\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho)=\big(\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho U^{*})\big)^{1/2}K_{YR}\big(\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho)\big)^{1/2}.

Hence, according to
[
11
, Lemma 9]
we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(F)\|_{(1,\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho U^{*})\|_{(1,\alpha)}^{1/2}\cdot\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho)\|_{(1,\alpha)}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(U\rho U^{*})\|_{(1,\alpha)},\|\Phi\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}(\rho)\|_{(1,\alpha)}\Big\}.

The proof concludes once we note that both
\rho
and
U\rho U^{*}
are density matrices.
∎

Now that we represent the
\alpha
-Rényi coherent information of a channel in terms of an operator norm, we may use all the tools for studying operators norms, such as convexity, interpolation techniques and log-Sobolev inequalities to investigate the problem of quantum channel capacity. In particular, it is tempting to use the
\alpha
-Rényi coherent information to prove strong converse bounds on quantum channel capacity. A natural open question in this direction is whether
I_{\alpha}^{\rm{coh}}(\cdot)
is
additive
for certain (e.g., degradable) cptp maps or not, i.e., does
I_{\alpha}^{\rm{coh}}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})
equal to
I_{\alpha}^{\rm{coh}}(\Phi_{1})+I_{\alpha}^{\rm{coh}}(\Phi_{2})
?

We finish this subsection by mentioning some related results to the above additivity problem. Motivated by the problem of the additivity of the
minimum output entropy
of cptp maps, it is shown in
[
11
]
that the completely bounded
(p\to q)
-norm of cptp maps is multiplicative. The proof of this result is heavily based on the theory of operator-valued Schatten norms. It is also shown that the
entanglement-assisted
capacity of cptp maps can be expressed in terms of the so-called completely
p
-summing norms
[
21
]
that is again related to the theory of operator-valued Schatten norms. Inspired by
[
11
]
, the theory of
logarithmic-Sobolev
inequalities of completely bounded norms is developed in
[
6
]
. Also, extending
[
6
]
, estimations of the decoherence rate of
non-primitive
quantum Markov semigroups is derived in
[
3
]
. See also
[
2
]
for some related results.

5.3.
A correlation measure

In this subsection we define yet another information theoretic invariant based on operator-valued Schatten spaces. Here, we briefly explain the main properties of this invariant that is first introduced in
[
34
]
and refer to the original paper for more details.

For a bipartite density matrix
\rho_{XY}
define

W_{\alpha}(X|Y)=\Big\|\rho_{YX}-\rho_{Y}\otimes\frac{I_{X}}{d_{X}}\Big\|_{(1,\alpha)},

where
d_{X}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}
is the dimension of subsystem
X
. Similar to
H_{\alpha}(X|Y)
, the definition of
W_{\alpha}(X|Y)
is also in terms of the
(1,\alpha)
-norm, so it is expected that these two quantities are related. Indeed, it is shown in
[
34
, Proposition 9]
that

\Big|W_{\alpha}(X|Y)-e^{-\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}H_{\alpha}(X|Y)}\Big|\leq d_{X}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}}.

We note that if
\rho_{XY}=\rho_{X}\otimes\rho_{Y}
is a product density matrix and
\rho_{X}=I_{X}/d_{X}
, then
W_{\alpha}(X|Y)=0
. Thus, assuming that marginal density matrix
\rho_{X}
is maximally mixed, then
W_{\alpha}(X|Y)
can be thought of as a correlation measure. As a correlation measure it is expected that
W_{\alpha}(X|Y)
satisfies the data processing inequality: if
\Phi:B(\mathcal{H}_{Y})\to B(\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{\prime})
is a cptp map, then

W_{\alpha}(X|Y^{\prime})\leq W_{\alpha}(X|Y).

This inequality is proven in
[
34
, Theorem 8]
based on similar ideas as in the proof of part (iii) of Proposition
5.1
.

Theorem 5.5
.

[
34
, Corollary 14]

Let
\rho_{XY}
be a bipartite density matrix and let
P:\mathcal{H}_{X}\to\mathcal{H}_{X_{0}}
be an orthogonal projection where
\mathcal{H}_{X_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{X}
is a subspace. Then for any
1\leq\alpha\leq 2
we have

\mathbb{E}_{U}\Big[\Big\|\frac{d_{X}}{d_{X_{0}}}(PU_{X}\otimes I_{Y})\rho_{XY}(U^{*}_{X}P\otimes I_{Y})-\frac{I_{X_{0}}}{d_{X_{0}}}\otimes\rho_{Y}\Big\|_{1}\Big]\leq 2^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-1}d_{X_{0}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}}W_{\alpha}(X|Y).

Proof of this theorem for
\alpha=1
is easy. For
\alpha=2
, it is proven essentially based on ideas from
[
14
]
. Then, the key observation is that, by Theorem
2.5
, from the boundary cases
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
we can infer the theorem for all values of
1\leq\alpha\leq 2
. See
[
34
]
for more details.

Theorem
5.5
is called a decoupling theorem as it says that by applying a random unitary and a projection on a bipartite density matrix, the two parts become decoupled. Such decoupling results have several applications in information theory, e.g., in channel coding and privacy amplification
[
14
,
34
,
43
]
.

X=[v_{1}^{r}\dots v_{m}^{r}]\quad\text{ and }\quad Y=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}^{c}\\
\vdots\\
w_{m}^{c}\end{bmatrix}.

Then,

\displaystyle\|X\|_{M_{1,m}(\mathcal{R}^{d})}=\big\|[v_{1}^{r}\dots v_{m}^{r}]\big\|_{1,md}=\Big(\sum_{i}\|v^{r}_{i}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(A.1)

and

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{M_{m,1}(\mathcal{C}^{d})}=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}w^{c}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
w^{c}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{md,1}=\Big(\sum_{i}\|w^{c}_{i}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

(A.2)

On the other hand,

corresponds to

.
Therefore,

equals the infimum of

\Big(\sum_{i}\|v^{r}_{i}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i}\|w^{c}_{i}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

subject to
\psi(Z)=\sum_{i}w^{c}_{i}v^{r}_{i}
. An application of Hölder’s inequality shows that this is equal to
\|\psi(Z)\|_{\mathcal{S}_{1}^{d}}
.

Part (iii) follows readily once we note that

and
M_{d,1}(\mathcal{R}^{d})
are naturally identified with the space of

matrices, and by definition, their norms are the same as the norm of
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{d}
. The isometries
M_{1,d}(\mathcal{R}^{d})=M_{d,1}(\mathcal{C}^{d})=\mathcal{S}_{2}^{d}
are proven essentially in (
A.1
) and (
A.2
).
∎

Appendix B
Auxiliary lemmas in the proof of Theorem
3.7

B.1.
A selection lemma

Suppose that
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
are norms, respectively, on
M_{d,n}
,
M_{n,d^{\prime}}
and
M_{d,d^{\prime}}
such that

\gamma(X)=\inf\big\{\alpha(A)\cdot\beta(B):X=AB,A\in M_{d,n},B\in M_{n,d^{\prime}}\big\}.

Our goal here is to show that
almost optimizing
pairs
(A,B)
can be chosen to be measurable functions.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
X(s)
be a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
with values in
M_{d,d^{\prime}}
.
Then for every
\epsilon>0
there exist measurable functions
A(s),B(s)
such that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle X(s)=A(s)B(s),

\displaystyle\alpha(A(s))\leq 1+\epsilon,

\displaystyle\beta(B(s))\leq\gamma(X(s)).

To prove this lemma we make use of a special case of the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski selection theorem
[
41
]
:

Theorem B.2
.

(Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski selection theorem)
Let
\emph{cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the set of all nonempty closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\emph{cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a multi-function such that the set
\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\psi(s)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}
is measurable for every closed subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there is a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f(s)\in\psi(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

Based on the above selection theorem we define
\psi(s)
to be the set of all almost optimizer pairs
(A,B)
satisfying

\displaystyle X(s)=AB,

\displaystyle\alpha(A)\leq 1+\epsilon,

\displaystyle\beta(B)\leq\gamma(X(s)).

We need to show that
\psi(\cdot)
satisfies the hypotheses of the above selection theorem. It is straightforward to show that
\psi(s)
is non-empty and closed for every
s
. Thus, we need to verify that
\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\psi(s)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}
is measurable. To this end, consider the map

\Psi:(s,A,B)\mapsto\Big(\gamma(X(s)-AB),\,\alpha(A),\,\gamma(X(s))-\beta(B)\Big).

Since
\Psi
is continuous, the set

\mathcal{E}:=\Psi^{-1}\Big(\{0\}\crossproduct[0,1+\epsilon]\crossproduct[0,+\infty)\Big)

is closed.
Now, if
U
is a closed set of pairs
(A,B)
, then
\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U)
is closed. Next, observe that

\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\psi(s)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U)),

where
\pi_{1}
denotes the projection map onto the first component. Therefore, to see the measurability of
\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\psi(s)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}
, it suffices to prove that
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U))
is measurable.

Note that any Euclidean space can be written as a countable union of compact sets. Then, since the intersection of a closed and a compact set is compact, the closed set
\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U)
can be written as

\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}K_{i}

where
K_{i}
’s are compact. On the other hand, since a continuous image of a compact set is compact,
\pi(K_{i})
’s are compact too. This implies that

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{E}\cap(\mathbb{R}\crossproduct U))=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\pi_{1}(K_{i})

is a countable union of compact sets, and is therefore measurable.
∎

B.2.
A subharmonicity lemma

Let
\mathbb{D}
be the unit disk in the complex plane and
\partial\mathbb{D}
be its boundary. It is well-known that having a sufficiently nice function
f
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, there is a unique
harmonic
function on
\mathbb{D}
that matches
f
on the boundary. This harmonic function can be represented in terms of the
Poisson kernel
for
\mathbb{D}
, that is given by

P(z,e^{it}):=\real\left(\frac{e^{it}+z}{e^{it}-z}\right)=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|e^{it}-z|^{2}},

for
z=re^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{D},e^{it}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
.
In particular, if
f\in L_{1}(\partial\mathbb{D})
and

F(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})P(z,e^{it})\differential t,

(B.1)

then,

\lim_{r\rightarrow 1}F(re^{i\theta})=f(e^{i\theta}),\qquad\text{a.e. on}\ \partial\mathbb{D}.

Recall that
\mathbb{S}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq\text{Re}(z)\leq 1\}
. The Poisson kernel for
\mathbb{S}
may be obtained by carrying
P
over to
\mathbb{S}
using the conformal map
\phi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}

defined by
12
12

12

See, e.g.,
[
42
,
44
]
for more information on the Poisson kernel as well as conformal maps.

\phi(z)=\frac{1}{i\pi}\log(i\frac{1+z}{1-z}).

(B.2)

Indeed, for any holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, the function
f\circ\phi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is also holomorphic. Thus, using (
B.1
) we have

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\circ\phi(e^{it})\cdot P\big(\phi^{-1}(z),e^{it}\big)\differential t.

We note that
\phi
maps the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
onto the boundary of
\mathbb{S}
. Then, letting
\phi(e^{it})=b+is
with
b\in\{0,1\}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and rewriting the integral in terms of
s
, we find that

f(z)=\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(b+is)\cdot\frac{P\big(\phi^{-1}(z),\phi^{-1}(b+is)\big)}{2\cosh(\pi s)}\differential s.

Consequently, the Poisson kernel of
\mathbb{S}
is given by the functions
P_{b}(x+iy,s)
for
b=0,1
, where

\displaystyle P_{b}(x+iy,s)

\displaystyle:=\frac{P\big(\phi^{-1}(z),\phi^{-1}(b+is)\big)}{2\cosh(\pi s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{2\big(\cosh(\pi(y-s))-\cos(\pi(x-b))\big)}.

We note that
P_{b}(x+iy,s)\geq 0
and it can be verified that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{0}(x+iy,s)\differential s=1-x,\qquad\quad\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{1}(x+iy,s)\differential s=x.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
be a compatible couple and let
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}]_{\theta}
for
0<\theta<1
.

(i)

Let
f(z)\in\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1})
. Then we have

\log\|f(\theta)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}}\leq\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\|f(b+is)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\differential s.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1}
are finite-dimensional (or more generally, reflexive), then the same inequality holds whenever
f(z)
is bounded on
\mathbb{S}
and holomorphic in its interior (though not necessarily continuous up to the boundary).

The first part of this lemma is proven in
[
7
, Lemma 4.3.2]
. Here, we prove the second part for the sake of completeness.

Proof of
(ii)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}
be the Banach dual of
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}
. Then, there is
\ell\in\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}
such that

\|\ell\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}}=1,\qquad\|f(\theta)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}}=|\langle\ell,f(\theta)\rangle|.

Since
\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}=[\mathcal{X}^{*}_{0},\mathcal{X}^{*}_{1}]_{\theta}
, for any
\epsilon>0
there is
g(z)
such that
g(\theta)=\ell
and

\max\big\{\sup_{t}\|g(it)\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}_{0}},\sup_{t}\|g(1+it)\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}_{1}}\big\}\leq\|\ell\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\theta}}+\epsilon=1+\epsilon.

Now define
\psi(z):=\langle g(z),f(z)\rangle
. We note that
\psi(z)
is holomorphic and bounded. Then, by the subharmonicity of holomorphic functions we have

\displaystyle\log\|f(\theta)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\log|\psi(\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{b}(\theta,s)\log|\psi(b+is)|\differential s

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\big(\|g(b+is)\|_{\cX_b^*}\cdot\|f(b+is)\|_{\cX_b}\big)\differential s

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\big((1+\epsilon) \|f(b+is)\|_{\cX_b}\big)\differential s

\displaystyle\leq\log(1+\epsilon)+\sum_{b=0}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P_{b}(\theta,s)\log\|f(b+is)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\differential s.

The desired inequality follows once we note that
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary.

Appendix C
Factorization of operator-valued functions

This section is dedicated to the Wiener–Masani theorem on factorization of operator-valued functions
[
46
,
32
,
12
]
. This theorem is used in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, and is one of the main technical tools in the development of the theory of operator-valued Schatten spaces. Thus, we devote this section to the statement and proof of this theorem.
We follow the approach of
[
18
]
for the proof, that is based on characterization of
invariant subspaces
of the shift operator. To this end, we first need a number of ingredients.

C.1.
Hardy spaces

Following the notation of Section
2
, let
\mathbb{D}
be the unit disk in the complex plane and let
\partial\mathbb{D}
be its boundary, equipped with the normalized Lebesgue measure. Then,
L_{p}=L_{p}(\partial\mathbb{D})
denotes the Banach space of all measurable functions
f:\partial{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that the norm

\|f\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(e^{it})|^{p}\differential t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},~~~~~\qquad\qquad 1\leq p<\infty,\\[7.22743pt]
\esssup|f|,~~~~~\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ p=\infty,\end{cases}

is finite. We note that by Hölder’s inequality, we have
L_{q}\subseteq L_{p}
for
p<q
.

Definition C.1
.

The
Hardy space

H_{p}
is the closed subspace of
L_{p}
consisting of all functions
f\in L_{p}
for which there is a function
F
holomorphic in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
whose
radial limit
coincides with
f
almost everywhere.
13
13

13

The radial limit of
F
at
e^{it}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
equals
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}F(re^{it})
.

There is another description of
H_{p}
in terms Fourier coefficients and Fourier series that goes as follows. For each function
f\in L_{1}
, let

a_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})e^{-nit}\differential t,

and

f(e^{it})\sim\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{nit},

be the Fourier coefficients and the Fourier series of
f
, respectively. Then,
H_{p}
is the subset of
L_{p}
consisting of all functions
f
whose Fourier coefficients
a_{n}
vanish for
n<0
.

Define the operator
\mathfrak{S}
by
(\mathfrak{S}f)(e^{it})=e^{it}f(e^{it})
. Consequently, for
n\geq 1
we have

(\mathfrak{S}^{n}f)(e^{it})=e^{nit}f(e^{it})
.
The operator
\mathfrak{S}
is called the
shift operator
since it shifts the Fourier coefficients.

Definition C.2
.

A function
f\in H_{2}
is said to be
outer
if the linear span of the set
\{\mathfrak{S}^{n}f:n\geq 0\}
is dense in
H_{2}
.

It is well-known that every outer function
f
can be written in the form

f(z)=\gamma\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{e^{it}+z}{e^{it}-z}k(t)\differential t\right),

where
\gamma
is a complex number of modulus
1
, and
k(t)=\log|f(e^{it})|
almost everywhere; see, e.g.,
[
29
,
42
]
for more information on outer functions.

C.2.
Vector-valued Hardy spaces

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable (usually finite-dimensional) Hilbert space. An
\mathcal{H}
-valued function
F:\partial{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathcal{H}
is said to be measurable if for every
v\in\mathcal{H}
the complex function
e^{it}\mapsto\langle v,F(e^{it})\rangle
is measurable.
Abusing the notation, let
L_{p}(\mathcal{H})=L_{p}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{H})
be the Banach space of all measurable functions
F:\partial{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that the norm

\|F\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\|F(e^{it})\|^{p}\differential t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},~~~~~\qquad\qquad 1\leq p<\infty,\\[7.22743pt]

Definition C.3

C.3.
Invariant subspaces

The shift operator
\mathfrak{S}
can also be defined on the space of vector-valued functions:
(\mathfrak{S}F)(e^{it})=e^{it}F(e^{it})
.

Definition C.4
.

A closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of

is said to be

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, where

We may also consider operator-valued (matrix-valued) functions. We say that such a function

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a simply invariant subspace of
L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
that contains no nontrivial doubly invariant subspace. Then there exist an auxiliary Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a measurable operator-valued function
U
such that

(i)

U(e^{it}):\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is an isometry for any
e^{it}\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{M}
consists of functions
e^{it}\mapsto U(e^{it})F(e^{it})
for
F\in H_{2}(\mathcal{K})
.

We note that vector-valued Hardy spaces
H_{2}(\mathcal{K})
are simply invariant subspaces and do not contain any nontrivial doubly invariant subspace. The above lemma says that all such subspaces are essentially vector-valued Hardy spaces.

Proof.

For each
n
, define
\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\mathfrak{S}^{n}(\mathcal{M})
. As
\mathcal{M}
is invariant, we have

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{0}\supseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}\supseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}\supseteq\cdots.

We note that

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}=\bigcap\limits_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{M}_{n}

is doubly invariant, so by assumption
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}=0
.

For any
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{N}_{n}
be the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
(with respect to the inner product of
L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
). Thus, we have
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\mathcal{N}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}=0
, we have

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{N}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}\oplus\cdots.

(C.3)

Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{E_{1},E_{2},\cdots\}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{N}_{0}
, and note that
\mathcal{B}_{n}:=\mathfrak{S}^{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0})=\{\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{k}:k\geq 1\}
, for
n\geq 1
, is a subset of
\mathcal{M}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. This means that for every
j,k
and
n\geq 1
we have

\langle E_{j},\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{nit}\big\langle E_{j}(e^{it}),E_{k}(e^{it})\big\rangle\differential t=0.

Moreover, as
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is orthonormal, the same equation holds when
n=0
and
j\neq k
. More generally, we observe that

\langle\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j},\mathfrak{S}^{m}E_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{(m-n)it}\big\langle E_{j}(e^{it}),E_{k}(e^{it})\big\rangle dt=\delta_{jk}\cdot\delta_{mn}.

This equation shows that all the nontrivial Fourier coefficients of the functions
e^{it}\mapsto\big\langle E_{j}(e^{it}),E_{k}(e^{it})\big\rangle
are zero, and hence it is almost everywhere identical to the constant function
\delta_{jk}
.

We claim that for each
n
, the orthonormal set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is a basis for
\mathcal{N}_{n}
. To this end, we need to show that
\mathcal{B}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}=\spn\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Letting
F\in\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
, by definition there is
G\in\mathcal{M}
such that
F=\mathfrak{S}^{n+1}G
. On the other hand, for
\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
we have

\langle\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j},F\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j},\mathfrak{S}^{n+1}G\rangle=\langle E_{j},\mathfrak{S}G\rangle=0,

where we used the fact that
\mathfrak{S}G\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
and that
E_{j}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
and is orthogonal to
\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
, which means that
\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{B}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}
.

Next, let
F\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Then, there is
G\in\mathcal{M}
such that
F=\mathfrak{S}^{n}G
. Since
G\in\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{N}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{M}_{1}
, there are
G_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
and
G_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
G=G_{0}+\mathfrak{S}G_{1}
. Therefore

F=\mathfrak{S}^{n}G_{0}+\mathfrak{S}^{n+1}G_{1}.

By definition we have
\mathfrak{S}^{n+1}G_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
. Also, since
G_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}=\spn\mathcal{B}_{0}
, we have
\mathfrak{S}^{n}G_{0}\in\spn\mathcal{B}_{n}
. As a result,
F\in\spn\mathcal{B}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
for any
F\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Thus, using
\mathcal{B}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}
proven above, and the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
, we find that
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\spn\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Putting (
C.3
) and
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\spn\mathcal{B}_{n}
together, we find that every
F\in\mathcal{M}
can be expanded as

F=\sum_{j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{nj}\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j},\ \ \ \quad\sum_{n,j}|a_{nj}|^{2}<\infty.

(C.4)

Now, let
\mathcal{K}
be a Hilbert space isomorphic to
\mathcal{N}_{0}
with orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots\}
. Every function in
L_{2}(\mathcal{K})
can be written in the form
\sum f_{j}e_{j}
with

,
j=1,2,\dots
, being coordinate functions (see (
C.1
)).
Define
\hat{U}:L_{2}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow L_{2}(\mathcal{H})

\hat{U}\Big(\sum f_{j}e_{j}\Big)=\sum f_{j}E_{j},

meaning that
\hat{U}\Big(\sum f_{j}e_{j}\Big)(e^{it})=\sum f_{j}(e^{it})E_{j}(e^{it})
.
Then,
\hat{U}
is a well-defined linear operator. Moreover, since
\langle E_{j}(e^{it}),E_{k}(e^{it})\rangle=\delta_{jk}

\hat{U}
is an isometry.

For every
e^{it}
we define a linear map
U(e^{it}):\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
as follows. For every
v\in\mathcal{K}
let
\hat{v}\in L_{2}(\mathcal{K})
be the constant function
\hat{v}(e^{it})=v
. Then, define

U(e^{it})v:=(\hat{U}\hat{v})(e^{it}).

It is easy to verify that
e^{it}\mapsto U(e^{it})
is measurable.
We claim that
U(e^{it})
is an isometry for almost every
t
. To show this, fix

. Using the fact that
\hat{U}
is an isometry, for every

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{U}\hat{v},\hat{U}(\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{w})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{v},\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{nit}\langle v,w\rangle\differential t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle v,w\rangle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{nit}\differential t

\displaystyle=\delta_{n0}\langle v,w\rangle.

(C.5)

On the other hand, the definition of
\hat{U}
implies that
\hat{U}(\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{w})=\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{U}(\hat{w})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{U}\hat{v},\hat{U}(\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{w})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{U}\hat{v},\mathfrak{S}^{n}\hat{U}(\hat{w})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{nit}\langle\hat{U}\hat{v}(e^{it}),\hat{U}\hat{w}(e^{it})\rangle\differential t.

Consequently, comparing to the previous equation, all the nontrivial Fourier coefficients of the function

vanish. This means that this function is constant almost everywhere. Thus, we have, almost everywhere,

\displaystyle\langle U(e^{it})v,U(e^{it})w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\langle U(e^{is})v,U(e^{is})w\rangle\differential s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\langle\hat{U}\hat{v}(e^{is}),\hat{U}\hat{w}(e^{is})\rangle\differential s

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{U}\hat{v},\hat{U}\hat{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,w\rangle,

where in the last line we use (
C.5
). This means that
U(e^{it})
is an isometry almost everywhere (hence one can modify
U(e^{it})
on a set of measure zero so that
U(e^{it})
is an isometry for any

).

We note that
H_{2}(\mathcal{K})=\big\{\sum f_{j}e_{j}:\,f_{j}\in H_{2},\|f_{j}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\big\}
, and every
f_{j}\in H_{2}
can be written as
f_{j}(e^{it})=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{nj}e^{nit}
. Therefore, (
C.4
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{U}H_{2}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sum_{j}f_{j}E_{j}:f_{j}\in H_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\sum\|f_{j}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sum_{j}\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{nj}\mathfrak{S}^{n}E_{j}:\sum|a_{nj}|^{2}<\infty\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}.

Finally, from the definition of

U(e^{it})e_{j}=(\hat{U}e_{j})(e^{it})=E_{j}(e^{it})

\displaystyle(\hat{U}F)(e^{it})

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum f_{j}E_{j}\Big)(e^{it})

\displaystyle=\sum f_{j}(e^{it})E_{j}(e^{it})

\displaystyle=\sum f_{j}(e^{it})U(e^{it})e_{j}

\displaystyle=U(e^{it})\Big(\sum f_{j}(e^{it})e_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=U(e^{it})F(e^{it}).

That is,
\mathcal{M}
consists of functions of the form
e^{it}\mapsto U(e^{it})F(e^{it})

C.4.
Wiener–Masani factorization theorem

In the following we assume that
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional with orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
. For every
e^{it}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
we assume that
T(e^{it}):\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is a linear map such that
e^{it}\mapsto T(e^{it})
is an operator-valued measurable function. We further assume that entries of
T
(in the basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
) are integrable, i.e., belong to
L_{1}
.

Suppose that
T(e^{it})
is positive, and uniformly bounded almost everywhere, i.e., there exists a constant
c>0
such that

0\leq T(e^{it})\leq cI,\qquad\text{ almost everywhere}.

Let
Q(e^{it})=\sqrt{T(e^{it})}
. We note that with the above assumptions
Q
can be written as the limit of measurable functions (consider the truncated Taylor expansion of
x\mapsto\sqrt{x}
at point
c
), and is measurable itself.
We also note that
Q
can be considered as an element of
B(L_{2}(\mathcal{H}))
.
Indeed, for every
F\in L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
we have
(QF)(e^{it})=Q(e^{it})F(e^{it})
and

\displaystyle\|QF\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big\langle Q(e^{it})F(e^{it}),Q(e^{it})F(e^{it})\big\rangle\differential t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\langle F(e^{it}),T(e^{it})F(e^{it})\rangle\differential t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}c\langle F(e^{it}),F(e^{it})\rangle\differential t

\displaystyle=c\|F\|_{2}^{2}.

Define

\mathcal{M}(T)=\overline{\spn\{\mathfrak{S}^{n}(Qe_{j}):n\geq 0,\,j=1,\dots,d\}},

where
Qe_{j}\in L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
is given by
(Qe_{j})(e^{it})=Q(e^{it})e_{j}
. Observe that by definition,
\mathcal{M}(T)
is a closed and invariant subspace of
L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
. In fact,
\mathcal{M}(T)
is the smallest invariant subspace of
L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
generated by columns of
Q
.

Lemma C.7
.

Let
T(e^{it})
be a measurable operator-valued function as above, and
\lambda(e^{it})
be a positive measurable function such that

\log\lambda(e^{it})\in L_{1},

(C.6)

and

0<\lambda(e^{it})I\leq T(e^{it})\leq cI,\qquad\text{a.e. on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$},

for some
c>0
. Then
\mathcal{M}(T)
does not contain any nontrivial doubly invariant subspace.

Proof.

Observe that by (
C.6
) there is
\psi\in H_{2}
such that
|\psi(e^{it})|=\sqrt{\lambda(e^{it})}
. To construct such a function we use the Poisson kernel of
\mathbb{D}
to define

g(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\real\frac{e^{it}+z}{e^{it}-z}\log\sqrt{\lambda(e^{it})}\differential t.

We note that
g(e^{it})=\log\sqrt{\lambda(e^{it})}
almost everywhere, and that
g(z)
is harmonic. Then, considering its harmonic conjugate, there is a holomorphic function
\hat{g}(z)
with
\real\hat{g}(z)=g(z)
. Now, letting
\psi(z)=e^{\hat{g}(z)}
, we have almost everywhere

|\psi(e^{it})|=|e^{\hat{g}(it)}|=e^{\real\hat{g}(it)}=e^{g(it)}=\sqrt{\lambda(e^{it})}.

We say that
P\in H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
is a
polynomial
if its Fourier expansion (
C.2
) consists of finitely many
n\geq 0
terms, i.e.,
P
is a polynomial in variable
e^{it}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{H}
. For such a polynomial
P\in H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
we have
\psi P\in H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
where
(\psi P)(z)=\psi(z)P(z)
.
Define

\mathcal{N}=\overline{\spn\{\psi P:\text{$P$ polynomial}\}}.

Observe that
\mathcal{N}\subseteq H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
is an invariant subspace.

Next, consider the subspace
\mathcal{W}=\spn\{QP:\,\text{$P$ polynomial}\}\subseteq L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
where as before
Q=\sqrt{T}
. We note that by definition
\overline{\mathcal{W}}=\mathcal{M}(T)
.
Define
U
from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{N}
by
U(QP)=\psi P
. Since
\sqrt{\lambda(e^{it})}I\leq Q(e^{it})
almost everywhere, we have

\displaystyle\|\psi P\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big\|\psi(e^{it})P(e^{it})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lambda(e^{it})\big\|P(e^{it})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big\|(QP)(e^{it})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|QP\|^{2}_{2}.

This means that
\|U\|\leq 1
, and
U
can be extended to
U:\mathcal{M}(T)\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
while
\|U\|\leq 1
.

We claim that
U
is one-to-one. To prove this, suppose that
F\in\mathcal{M}(T)
and
UF=0
. Since
F\in\mathcal{M}(T)
, there is a sequence of polynomials
\{P_{n}:\,n\geq 1\}\subset H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}QP_{n}=F\ \ \ \qquad\text{(in $L^{2}(\mathcal{H})$)},

and since
QF=0
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi P_{n}=0\ \ \ \qquad\text{(in $L^{2}(\mathcal{H})$)}.

Define
R_{m}=\{e^{it}:\lambda(e^{it})\geq 1/m\}
, and let
J_{m}
be the characteristic function of
R_{m}
. We compute

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{m}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lambda(e^{it})J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lambda(e^{it})\big\|P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle=\|\psi P_{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|_{2}^{2}\,\differential t=0,\ \ \ \quad\forall m.

(C.7)

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\big\|F-QP_{n}\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big\|F(e^{it})-Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|F(e^{it})-Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\Big(\|F(e^{it})\big\|-\|Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|\Big)^{2}\,\differential t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\Big(\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|^{2}+\big\|Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}-2\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|\cdot\big\|Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|\Big)\differential t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t-\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|Q(e^{it})P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t-2\|F\|_{2}\cdot\frac{c}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|P_{n}(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t,

where the penultimate inequality follows by ignoring a non-negative term and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality follows from
Q(e^{it})\leq\sqrt{c}I
. Taking the limit of both sides as
n\rightarrow\infty
, using (
C.7
) and the non-negativity of the remaining term, we obtain

\|J_{m}F\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}J_{m}(e^{it})\big\|F(e^{it})\big\|^{2}\,\differential t=0,\ \ \ \quad\forall m.

Now, the monotone convergence theorem implies
\|F\|_{2}^{2}=\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\|J_{m}F\|_{2}^{2}=0
.
This means that
F=0
. Thus,
U:\mathcal{M}(T)\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is one-to-one, as claimed.

Finally, suppose that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(T)
is a nontrivial doubly invariant subspace. Then
\overline{U\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}\subseteq H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
is a doubly invariant subspace. But
H_{2}(\mathcal{H})
does not contain nontrivial doubly invariant subspaces. This implies that
\overline{U\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}=0
. Hence,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=0
since
U
is one-to-one.
∎

Proof.

Since

almost everywhere on

and

, it is evident that the function

lies in

. On the other hand, it follows from (

) that

. These facts imply that the outer function

, defined by

\lambda(z):=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{e^{it}+z}{e^{it}-z}\log\tilde{\lambda}(e^{it})\differential t\right),

lies in
H_{\infty}
, and

\big\|\lambda(e^{it})A^{-1}(e^{it})\big\|=1,\qquad\text{a.e.\ on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$}.

(C.9)

Let
h
be an arbitrary but fixed vector in
\mathcal{H}
, and define
\psi_{h}(e^{it})=\overline{\lambda(e^{it})}A^{-1}(e^{it})^{*}h
. Then,
\psi_{h}\in L_{2}(\mathcal{H})
since
\|\psi_{h}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}
by (
C.9
).

Now let
\Psi_{h}
be the bounded linear functional on

corresponding to

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Psi_{h}(F)

\displaystyle=\big\langle e^{-it}\psi_{h}(e^{it}),F(e^{it})\big\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathcal{H})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it}\big\langle\psi_{h}(e^{it}),F(e^{it})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\differential t.

We are going to show that
\Psi_{h}
vanishes on

. To this end, note first that the last expression above is indeed a Fourier coefficient, with negative index, of the function

. As a result, for every function

we have

where

, since

. Thus,
\Psi_{h}
vanishes on

, which in turn implies that
\Psi_{h}
vanishes on the entire

as by assumption
A(z)
is outer.

Let

be an arbitrary (but fixed) vector in
\mathcal{H}
, and

Consequently, letting

\mathbb{D}

∎

We can now state and prove the Wiener–Masani theorem.

Theorem C.9

(Wiener–Masani)
.

Let
T(e^{it})
be a measurable operator-valued function as above, and
\lambda(e^{it})
be a positive measurable function such that

\log\lambda(e^{it})\in L_{1},

(C.10)

and

0<\lambda(e^{it})I\leq T(e^{it})\leq cI,\qquad\text{a.e. on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$},

(C.11)

for some
c>0
. Then there exists an outer operator-valued function
A(z)\in H_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
such that
A(z)
is invertible for all
z
in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
, and

T(e^{it})=A^{*}(e^{it})A(e^{it}),\qquad\text{a.e. on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$}.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.7
,
\mathcal{M}(T)
does not contain any doubly invariant subspace. Therefore, by Lemma
C.5
, there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a function
U(e^{it}):\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, that is an isometry almost everywhere, such that
\mathcal{M}(T)=UH_{2}(\mathcal{K})
.

Recall that
Qe_{j}\in\mathcal{M}(T)
for every basis element
e_{j}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, for each
j
, there is
A_{j}\in H_{2}(\mathcal{K})
such that
Qe_{j}=UA_{j}
. Define
A(e^{it}):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
by
A(e^{it})e_{j}=A_{j}(e^{it})
. Observe that
A
is a holomorphic operator-valued function since
A_{j}\in H_{2}(\mathcal{K})
. Moreover, using the fact that
U
is an isometry almost everywhere, we have

\displaystyle A^{*}(e^{it})A(e^{it})

\displaystyle=A^{*}(e^{it})U^{*}(e^{it})U(e^{it})A(e^{it})

\displaystyle=Q^{*}(e^{it})Q(e^{it})

\displaystyle=T(e^{it}),\qquad\text{a.e.\ on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$}.

(C.12)

Now we show that
A(e^{it})
is outer. Let
F\in H_{2}(\mathcal{K})
be arbitrary. Then,
UF\in UH_{2}(\mathcal{K})=\mathcal{M}(T)
, so for every
n\geq 1
there is a polynomial
P_{n}
such that
\|QP_{n}-UF\|_{2}\leq 1/n
. Next, using the fact that
U
is an isometry almost everywhere we have

\|AP_{n}-F\|_{2}=\|UAP_{n}-UF\|_{2}=\|QP_{n}-UF\|_{2}\leq 1/n.

This shows that
\{AP:P\text{ polynomial}\}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
and
A
is outer.

It follows from (
C.4
) and our assumptions on
T(e^{it})
that
A(e^{it})
is invertible almost everywhere on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, and
\|A^{-1}(e^{it})\|^{2}=\|T^{-1}(e^{it})\|
. On the other hand, by (
C.11
) we have

\displaystyle 2\log\|A^{-1}(e^{it})\|

\displaystyle=\log\|T^{-1}(e^{it})\|

\displaystyle\leq\log\lambda(e^{it})^{-1},\qquad\text{a.e. on $\partial{\mathbb{D}}$}.

This, together with (
C.10
), yields that
\log\|A^{-1}(e^{it})\|\in L_{1}
. Finally, Lemma
C.8
is applied to conclude that
A(z)
is invertible for all
z
in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
.
∎
