Abstract
. We derive an explicit distribution for the threshold sequence of the symmetric binary perceptron with Gaussian disorder, proving that the critical window is of constant width.



1.
Introduction

We fix a real number
\kappa>0
throughout the rest of this paper.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{n}=\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Given an infinite sequence
M=(M_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

S_{j}(M):=\{x\in\Sigma_{n}\colon|\langle x,M_{j}\rangle|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\},\qquad S^{m}(M):=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}S_{j}(M).

Furthermore, define the
threshold

\tau=\tau(M)
as the first index such that

S^{\tau-1}(M)\neq\emptyset\text{ ~and }S^{\tau}(M)=\emptyset,

or
+\infty
if it does not exist.
Let
G=(G_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent standard
n
-dimensional Gaussian vectors, i.e.,
G_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
. The
threshold for the symmetric binary perceptron with Gaussian disorder
is
\tau=\tau(G)
(note
\tau(G)<+\infty
almost surely).

We note that the threshold as defined in
Definition

1.1
is up to rescaling equivalent to the
storage capacity
of the binary perceptron model. We have chosen to work with Gaussian disorder as it marginally simplifies certain computations. The limiting distribution can immediately be extended to Rademacher disorder, although various quantitative error terms will differ and the mean and variance of an associated log-normal distribution
Z^{\ast}
must be slightly changed.

We will require the following basic special functions.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
Z,Z_{1},Z_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be independent standard Gaussians. Let
p=\mathbb{P}[|Z|\leq\kappa]
and define the critical value

\alpha_{c}:=-\log 2/\log p.

We also define

\mu_{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{|Z|\leq\kappa}]}{p},\quad\beta=-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{c}}}{2}(1-\mu_{2}).

Finally we define the
pair probability function

q(\gamma)=\mathbb{P}\big[|\sqrt{\gamma}Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\gamma}Z_{2}|\leq\kappa\wedge|\sqrt{\gamma}Z_{1}-\sqrt{1-\gamma}Z_{2}|\leq\kappa\big].

We note that
-1/2<\beta<0
follows from
[
3
, (3.8)]
. We now state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
and
\tau=\tau(G)
be as in
Definition

1.1
. There exists
\theta>0
such that the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
. Let
Z^{\ast}\sim\mathcal{N}(\frac{1}{4}\log(1-4\beta^{2}),-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-4\beta^{2}))
and choose
k
with
k+\alpha_{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have

\bigg|\mathbb{P}[\tau\leq k+\alpha_{c}n]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-e^{Z^{\ast}}p^{k}}{2}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg|\leq n^{-\theta}.

Remark 1.4
.

The polynomial rate specified by
Theorem

1.3
is essentially the limit of our method. Combining the statement of
Theorem

1.3
with the tails bounds given by Altschuler
[
3
, Theorem 2]
, one can prove that there exists
C
such that for any fixed
q\geq 1
and
n
large in terms of
q
:

\mathbb{E}|\tau-\alpha_{c}n|^{q}\leq C^{q}.

We also note that
Theorem

1.3
disproves
[
3
, Conjecture 1]
since the upper tail
\mathbb{P}[\tau\geq k+\alpha_{c}n]
is of exponential type in
k>0
.

1.1.
Previous work

A toy model for neural networks, the (asymmetric) perceptron model has been a model of substantial interest, in part stemming from conjectures on the asymptotic threshold due to Krauth and Mezard
[
13
]
. Despite progress on a number of related problems, progress on the perceptron has proved considerably more difficult. In this direction, groundbreaking work of Ding and Sun
[
8
]
establishes the conjectural lower bound for the threshold and recent work of Xu
[
19
]
has established an analog of Friedgut’s sharp threshold theorem
[
11
]
for such models (see work of Nakajima and Sun
[
15
]
for an alternate proof). In particular these sharp threshold results establish that for general perceptron models,
\tau
typically lives within an interval of length
o(n)
.

The symmetric perceptron model, introduced in work of Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
4
]
, has proven to be considerably more tractable at a mathematical level due the availability of the moment method applied directly to the number of solutions (while for the asymmetric perceptron model a substantially more involved conditioning scheme is required). It provides a test-bed for various conjectural phenomena since the solution space geometries of symmetric and asymmetric perceptron are expected to behave similarly. In particular, work of Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
4
]
established that w.h.p.
\tau<(\alpha_{c}+\epsilon)n
and with positive probability that
\tau>(\alpha_{c}-\epsilon)n
.
The determination of the threshold (with scaling window
o(n)
) at
\alpha_{c}n
with high probability was proven independently by Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
and Perkins and Xu
[
17
]

[
4

and Perkins and Xu

. Both the works of Abbe, Li, and Sly

We also note that there has recently been work on understanding efficient algorithms for the symmetric perceptron; in particular work of Abbe, Li, and Sly

1.2.
Stopping time and Poisson sampling heuristic

The key ingredient our in work is an early stopping time argument, which combined with an understanding of the geometry of the remaining solutions is sufficient to derive a distribution for the threshold. More precisely, define the (non-random) cutoff time
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\eta\log n\rfloor
for some sufficiently small constant
\eta>0
. By mimicking the techniques of Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
(for Gaussian disorder), one can prove that the number of solutions to the perceptron at
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
follows a log-normal distribution; we note here that the fact that the second and first moments match so close to the threshold is a critical insight found in the work of Altschuler
[
3
]
. Furthermore, the moment computations of Altschuler (in particular the “frozen” nature of typical solutions) can be used to prove that all pairs of solutions are essentially orthogonal (e.g. have dot product bounded in magnitude by
n^{1/2}\log n
).

The crucial idea at this point is to note that since w.h.p. at
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
there are a small polynomial number of solutions, the pairwise dot product of solutions being near zero can be translated to near-independence of the event of whether these solutions will survive the next slices. In particular, the total variation distance between the true distribution and a natural product distribution is polynomially small. The desired result then follows noting that there are only logarithmically more steps and thus the computation of the threshold sequence reduces to a computation with product distributions: we have some set
S
of solutions at time
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
and each solution is retained for
t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
extra slices with probability
p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}
, so an independence heuristic suggests that
\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{Bin}(|S|,p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}})=0]
is roughly the chance that we empty the solution set in
t
steps, where
\mathrm{Bin}
denotes a binomial random variable. In our regime, this can be approximated via a Poissonian heuristic that gives rise to the expression in
Theorem

1.3
(after incorporating the fact that
|S|/\mathbb{E}|S|
satisfies a log-normal distribution). We note that the strange factor of
2
arising in the distribution in
Theorem

1.3
stems from the fact that
v\in S
if and only if
-v\in S
.

We note that in implementing the above sketch in order to give polynomial error rates, the primary difficulty is quantifying the work of Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
(and adjusting it to both handle when the number of hyperplanes is near the threshold and to allow for Gaussian disorder). In order to provide a quantitative rate we transform the necessary cycle count distributional claims under conditional models to distributional questions about unconditioned Gaussian polynomials (via orthogonal invariance of Gaussians and various
expectation and variance computations) and then provide quantitative rates via Stein’s method (in paricular through the use of exchangeable pairs), rather than the method of moments used in
[
2
]
.
We note one curious feature is that the distribution of solutions at
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
, while still being log-normal, does not have the same mean and variance parameters as the Rademacher case derived in
[
2
]
. The difference ultimately arises from the fact that if
M\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, then
M^{2}
has nontrivial variance whereas
M^{2}
is constant if
M\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
. (This difference manifests in the need to track the degenerate
2
-cycle count corresponding to
k=1
in
Definition

3.2
; we note that the
k=1
expression in the Rademacher case would be deterministic, hence the difference.)

We note that the methods of this paper appear to more generally applicable; in particular, our approach can likely be adapted give a precise characterization of the threshold for solutions in constrained
k
-XORSAT which was shown to have an
O(1)
-size scaling window by Pittel and Sorkin
[
18
]
(see also
[
9
,
7
]
). We intend to return to this subject in future work.

For positive semidefinite

, the standard Gaussian vector with identity covariance. For a discrete random variable
X
with nonzero probabilities
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
for its atoms, its entropy is

H(X)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\log p_{i}.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence between

\operatorname{KL}(X\parallel Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\log\frac{q_{i}}{r_{i}}

their KL divergence is

\operatorname{KL}(q\parallel r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{r(x)}dx.

For real matrices
A
and

\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A^{T}B),\qquad\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^{T}A)}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}.

\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{|v_{i}|=1}|A(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})|.

Finally for

\langle D^{k}f(x),(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\rangle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]}\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{i_{k}}}(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\ldots(u_{k})_{i_{k}}

M_{r}(g)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|D^{r}g(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

For a matrix

Proof of
Theorem

1.3

In order to derive our main result we will assume the following pair of structural properties of the solution space and its size. Here and beyond, we fix some
\eta>0
that will be chosen small in terms of only
\kappa
at the end of the argument. For now, we leave it unspecified in the various statements that appear, merely using that it is sufficiently small in the proofs.

Proposition 2.1
.

There exists
\gamma>0
such that the following holds. Let
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\eta\log n\rfloor
. For
n
sufficiently large, we have the following pair of estimates on the size of the solution space
X(G):=|S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}(G)|
for
n
sufficiently large.

(1)

With probability at least
1-n^{-1}
there do not exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}(G)
such that
|\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle|\in[n^{1/2}\log n,n-1]
.

(2)

Let
Z^{\ast}=\mathcal{N}(\frac{1}{4}\log(1-4\beta^{2}),-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-4\beta^{2}))
. Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\frac{X(G)}{\mathbb{E}X(G)}\geq e^{t}\bigg]=\mathbb{P}[Z^{\ast}\geq t]\pm n^{-\gamma}.

We will also require the following tail bounds on the number of solutions. The second appears explicitly as
[
3
, Theorem 3]
which in turns builds closely on work of Perkins and Xu
[
17
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
3
, Theorem 3]
)
.

We have the following.

(1)

For all
t\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}[|S_{t}(G)|\geq 1]\leq 2^{n}p^{t}.

(2)

There exists a constant
c=c_{\ref{prop:tails}}>0
such that following holds. Fix
H\geq c^{-1}
. Suppose that
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
H
. Then

\mathbb{P}[S_{\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-H\log n\rfloor}(G)=\emptyset]\leq\exp(-cH\log n).

We require the following basic bound on the total variation distance between a pair of normal distributions.

Lemma 2.3
.

For positive definite symmetric matrices
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we have

2\operatorname{TV}(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{1}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{2}))^{2}\leq\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{1})\parallel\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\big(\log(\det(\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}))-d+\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}\Sigma_{1})\big).

Proof.

The first inequality is by Pinsker’s inequality (see e.g.
[
6
, Lemma 2]
), holding for general distributions. The equality is a straightforward Gaussian integral (see e.g.
[
10
, Page 13]
).
∎

Theorem

1.3
given these inputs.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
,
\eta
and
X(G)
be as in
Proposition

2.1
. Let

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{X(G)\geq\mathbb{E}[X(G)]\cdot\exp((\log n)^{3/4})\}\cup\{X(G)\leq\mathbb{E}[X(G)]\cdot\exp(-(\log n)^{3/4})\}

and

\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{|\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle|\in[n^{1/2}\log n,n-1]:x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}\}.

We now reveal set
S=S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}(G)\cap\{x:x_{1}=1\}
and note that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, and
X(G)
are deterministic given
S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}(G)
. We note that
Proposition

2.1
implies that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}]\leq n^{-\Omega(1)}
(since
|Z^{\ast}|\leq(\log n)^{3/4}
occurs with super-polynomially good probability). We condition on a revelation of
S
such that
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}
holds.

We now consider
G_{t}
for
t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+2\eta\log n]
and consider the distribution induced by

\{\langle G_{t},x_{i}\rangle/\sqrt{n}:x_{i}\in S,t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+2\eta\log n]\}.

Note that
G_{t}
are independent for
t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+2\eta\log n]
. Let
M
denote a
X(G)/2
by
n
matrix where each row corresponds to an entry of
S
. Notice that for each
t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+\lceil 2\eta\log n\rceil]
we have that

\{\langle G_{t},x_{i}\rangle/\sqrt{n}:x_{i}\in S\}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,MM^{T}/n).

The crucial point is that since
MM^{T}/n
is sufficiently diagonally dominant we will be able to prove that the distribution is close in total variation distance to
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{|S|})
. To prove this, note that

\displaystyle\lVert MM^{T}/n-I_{|S|}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert MM^{T}/n-I_{|S|}\rVert_{\operatorname{F}}^{2}\leq|S|^{2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in S\\
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\end{subarray}}|\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle|^{2}/n^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|S|^{2}\cdot n^{-3/4}\leq n^{-1/2}

due to
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}
holding and the fact that
S
does not simultaneously contain any pair
\{x,-x\}
.

This immediately implies that all the eigenvalues of
MM^{T}/n
are of the form
1\pm n^{-1/4}
and therefore by
Lemma

2.3
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(\mathcal{N}(0,I_{|S|}),\mathcal{N}(0,MM^{T}/n))

\displaystyle\leq(\operatorname{Tr}(MM^{T}/n)-|S|+\log(\det(MM^{T}/n)))/2

\displaystyle\leq|S|n^{-1/4}+|S|\log(1+n^{-1/4})\leq n^{-1/5}.

The last inequality comes from
|S|=X(G)/2\leq\mathbb{E}X(G)\cdot\exp((\log n)^{3/4})\leq n^{O(\eta)}
by the choice of
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
, and assuming
\eta
is small enough.

As we are restricting attention to
t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+\lceil 2\eta\log n\rceil]
we have that

\operatorname{TV}((\{\langle G_{t},x_{i}\rangle/\sqrt{n}:x_{i}\in S\})_{t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+\lceil 2\eta\log n\rceil]},\mathcal{N}(0,I_{|S|})^{\otimes\lceil 2\eta\log n\rceil}))\leq n^{-1/6}.

Since
S
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}
, this implies that for
t\in(\tau_{\mathrm{pre}},\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}+\lceil 2\eta\log n\rceil]
we have

\mathbb{P}[S_{t}=\emptyset|S_{\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}]=(1-p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}})^{X(G)/2}\pm n^{-1/6}.

We now choose a constant
\rho>0
which is sufficiently small with respect to
\eta
. We first isolate the case where
t\in[\lceil\alpha_{c}n-\rho\log n\rceil,\lceil\alpha_{c}n+\rho\log n\rceil]
. Notice that we therefore have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[S_{t}=\emptyset]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[S_{t}=\emptyset\cap(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c})]\pm\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[S_{t}=\emptyset\cap(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c})]\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(1-p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}})^{X(G)/2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}}]\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}\cdot X(G)}{2}\bigg)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}}\bigg]\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}.

The final equality requires justification: note that
(1-p)^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}})^{X(G)}\leq 1
,
1-x=e^{-x+O(x^{2})}
for all
|x|\leq 1/2
, and

\exp(p^{2(t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}})}\cdot X(G))\leq 1+n^{-\Omega(1)}

by using that
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}
holds and some basic computation.

Let
Z^{\ast}
be as in
Proposition

2.1
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}\cdot X(G)}{2}\bigg)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}\cdot X(G)}{2}\bigg)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}}\geq u\bigg]du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-e^{Z^{\ast}}\mathbb{E}[X(G)]p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}}{2}\bigg)\geq u\bigg]du\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{-e^{Z^{\ast}}\mathbb{E}[X(G)]p^{t-\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}}}{2}\bigg)\bigg]\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-e^{Z^{\ast}}2^{n-1}p^{t})]\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}.

This implies the desired result for
t
in the specified interval.

For
t\leq\alpha_{c}n-\gamma\log n
, we have that

\mathbb{P}[|S_{t}|=\emptyset]\leq\mathbb{P}[|S_{\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\gamma\log n\rfloor}|=\emptyset]\leq n^{-\Omega(1)},

using what we have established for
t=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\gamma\log n\rfloor
and explicitly computing
\mathbb{E}\exp(e^{-Z^{\ast}}2^{n-1}p^{t})
, which implies the desired result in this range. Finally for
t\geq\alpha_{c}n+\gamma\log n
, we have that

\mathbb{P}[|S_{t}|\neq\emptyset]\leq\mathbb{E}[|S_{t}|]\leq p^{(\gamma\log n)/2}\leq n^{-\Omega(1)}

from
Proposition

2.2
(1) which immediately implies the desired result in this range. This finishes the proof.

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|\tau^{\ast}-\alpha_{c}n|^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{|\tau-\alpha_{c}n|\leq H\log n}]+n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|\tau^{\ast}-\alpha_{c}n|^{q}+n^{-\Omega(1)}

\displaystyle\leq C^{q}

Note that bounds derived on the upper tail for

has subexponential tails by direct calculation.

2.1.
Reduction to normalized second moment computation

We now formally state the second moment computation that we perform in the remainder of the paper and use these results to prove
Proposition

2.1
. We first define the key random variables used for small graph conditioning used in the definition of the moments.

Definition 2.4
.

The
2k
-cycle count
of a sequence of vectors
M=(M_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is

C_{k}(m,M)=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\bigg)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\

where we let

. Then the
K
-weighted cycle count
is

Y_{K}(m,M)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{2(2\beta)^{k}C_{k}(m,M)-(2\beta)^{2k}}{4k}.

Remark 2.5
.

Notice that for
k=1
, this is a shifted sum of squares of Gaussians. Such an expression is trivial in the Rademacher setting and hence is not required in the work of Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2

.

We also define certain planted conditional distributions that are key in computing the moments.

Definition 2.6
.

For
t\in[-n,n]
with
t\equiv n\pmod{2}
define
v_{t}=(1,\ldots,1,-1,\ldots,-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where there are
(n+t)/2
many
1

\Delta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{\otimes n}
. Let
\Delta_{1}
be

conditional on the event

. Let
\Delta_{2}(t)
be
x\sim\Delta_{1}
conditional on the further event
\{|\langle x,v_{t}\rangle|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\}
. Then let

, which has its vectors drawn from
\Delta_{0}
, and let
G^{(1)}=(G^{(1)}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
have vectors drawn independently from
\Delta_{1}
and
G^{(2)}(t)=(G^{(2)}_{j}(t))_{j\geq 1}
have vectors drawn independently from
\Delta_{2}(t)
.

We have the following weighted moment estimate.

Lemma 2.7
.

There exists
\gamma>0
such that the following holds. Let
m=\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\eta\log n\rfloor
and suppose

,

, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X(G)}{\exp(Y_{K}(m,G)\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G)\geq-L])}\bigg]\geq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}X(G)

Lemma 2.8

Let

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[g(V(G^{(0)}))]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{K}))}[g(Z)]|

We prove each of the items of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\{x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)\colon

For the second item, let

3.2.
Quantification of Gaussian convergence

In order to prove the required Gaussian convergence of the random variables

, we will prove

or

using multidimensional Stein’s method machinery adapted from Meckes
[

[

in the Gaussian case, we will be brief with certain basic calculations.

To reduce to
\Delta_{0}
, we introduce a slightly more general cycle count which allows us to plug in different conditional distributions.

Definition 3.2
.

Given
M
and
-n<t<n
with
t\equiv n\pmod{2}
, we define

\displaystyle\sigma_{j,i}(t,M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+t)/2}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{(n+t)/2}M_{j,i^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{j,i}(t)=M_{j,i}-\sigma_{j,i}(t,M),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }1\leq i\leq\frac{n+t}{2},

\displaystyle\sigma_{j,i}(t,M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-t)/2}\sum_{i^{\prime}=(n+t)/2+1}^{n}M_{j,i^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{j,i}(t)=M_{j,i}-\sigma_{j,i}(t,M),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }\frac{n+t}{2}<i\leq n.

If we are further given a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

, we define

\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,M,\sigma)=\frac{1}{(mn)^{k/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m]\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\bigg(\widetilde{M}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}(t)+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}\bigg)\bigg(\widetilde{M}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}(t)+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}\bigg)-\mathbbm{1}_{k=1}\sqrt{mn}.

The key point of this definition is that

C_{k}(m,M)=\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,M,\sigma(t,M))

and the distributions of
\widetilde{M}(t)
and
\sigma(t,M)
are independent in all
relevant cases (namely
M=G^{(0)},G^{(1)},G^{(2)}(t)
). Furthermore, across all relevant cases we have that the distribution of
\widetilde{M}(t)
is identical, so the distributions of
C_{k}(m,M)
and
\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,M))
are identical. Thus it suffices to understand the differences in the models via the parameters
\sigma
, which merely encode the average of the first
(n+t)/2
and last
(n-t)/2
elements of each vector. Therefore, we prove the following, which essentially shows the resulting statistics
V(M)
are the same up to shifting of means.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
m=\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\eta\log n\rfloor
and
K=\lceil\eta\log n\rceil
. Given
1\leq k\leq K
and
|t|\leq\sqrt{n}\log n
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(1)}))-C_{k}(m,G)\big]

\displaystyle=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1}))(2\beta)^{k},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)\big]

\displaystyle=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/4}))2\cdot(2\beta)^{k},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\big[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(1)}))-C_{k}(m,G)\big]

\displaystyle=O(n^{-5/6}\operatorname{Var}[C_{k}(m,G)]),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\big[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)\big]

\displaystyle=O(n^{-5/6}\operatorname{Var}[C_{k}(m,G)]).

Proof.

We define

\rho_{1}^{(0)}=\sigma_{1,1}(t,G^{(0)}),\rho_{2}^{(0)}=\sigma_{1,n}(t,G^{(0)})

and similar for
G^{(1)},G^{(2)}(t)
. Note that
\sigma_{1,i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,(n+t)/2\}
will give exactly
\rho_{1}
and
\sigma_{1,i}
for all
i\in\{(n+t)/2+1,\ldots,n\}
will give exactly
\rho_{2}
. Furthermore, as we vary
j
in
\sigma_{j,i}
, we do not have the exact same value but we have an independent value that is drawn from the same distribution. For instance, note that
\rho_{1}^{(0)}=(G_{1,1}+\cdots+G_{1,(n+t)/2})/((n+t)/2)
and
\rho_{2}^{(0)}
some Gaussians, while
(\rho_{1}^{(1)},\rho_{2}^{(1)})
have the distribution of
(x,y)=(\rho_{1}^{(0)},\rho_{2}^{(0)})
conditioned on
|(n+t)x/2+(n-t)y/2|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}
, and
(\rho_{1}^{(2)},\rho_{2}^{(2)})
have the distribution of
(x,y)=(\rho_{1}^{(1)},\rho_{2}^{(1)})
further conditioned on
|(n+t)x/2-(n-t)y/2|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}
.

We first note that

(3.5)

\mathbb{E}\rho_{1}^{(b)}=\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}^{(b)}=0

for all
b\in\{0,1,2\}
, and

(3.6)

\mathbb{E}\rho_{1}^{(b)}\rho_{2}^{(b)}=0

for
b\in\{0,2\}
. This can be seen from symmetry. Note that
b=1
does have some covariance. Additionally, we have

\mathbb{E}(\rho_{1}^{(0)})^{2}=\frac{1}{(n+t)/2},\quad\mathbb{E}(\rho_{2}^{(0)})^{2}=\frac{1}{(n-t)/2}.

Finally, we can compute

\mathbb{E}(\rho_{1}^{(2)})^{2}=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/4}))\frac{2\mu_{2}}{n+t},\quad\mathbb{E}(\rho_{2}^{(2)})^{2}=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/4}))\frac{2\mu_{2}}{n-t},

using that the distribution of
(\rho_{1}^{(2)},\rho_{2}^{(2)})
is that of
(\rho_{1}^{(0)},\rho_{2}^{(0)})
, which are independent Gaussians, conditional on two explicit linear inequalities. (The error term comes from the possible deviation in
t
; if

these are easily seen to be precise equalities.)

We first consider
Equations

3.2
and
3.4
. For the expectation, note that
G,G^{(2)}(t)
are independent and
G,G^{(2)}(t)
are mean
0
(the latter since we are in the symmetric perceptron), so for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(mn)^{k/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m]\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\big(\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))\big)\big(\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(mn)^{k/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m]\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m)\cdots(m-k+1)}{(mn)^{k/2}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}}\bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}\widetilde{G}_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}+\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))\sigma_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}(t,G^{(2)}(t))]\bigg).

Now if any
i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}
are not in the same group
\{1,\ldots,(n+t)/2\},\{(n+t)/2+1,\ldots,n\}
then the corresponding terms are
0
by
Equation

3.6

to be in one group or the other to have nonzero contribution. Additionally, the choice of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))]

\displaystyle=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/4}))\alpha_{c}^{k/2}(2(\mu_{2}-1)^{k})=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/4}))2(2\beta)^{k}

We used that
\mathbb{E}\widetilde{G}_{j,1}\widetilde{G}_{j,2}=-2/(n+t)
and similar for the other index group. This establishes

\mathbb{E}C_{1}(m,G)=0
so we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{m}{\sqrt{mn}}

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\widetilde{G}_{1,i}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\sigma_{1,1}(t,G^{(2)}(t))^{2}+\mathbb{E}\sigma_{1,n}(t,G^{(2)}(t))^{2}\bigg)-\sqrt{mn}

\operatorname{Var}[C_{k}(m,G)]=(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1}))2k

X_{j,i}^{(0)}=Y_{j,i}^{(0)}=\widetilde{G}_{j,i}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[\widetilde{C}_{k}(m,t,

\displaystyle G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)]

(3.7)

\widetilde{G}_{j,i},\sigma_{j,i}

Therefore, we easily find that (using independence of the random variables associated to different

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\bigg[\bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}X_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}^{(b_{2\ell-1})}X_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}^{(b_{2\ell})}-\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}Y_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}^{(b_{2\ell-1})}Y_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}}^{(b_{2\ell})}-\mathbb{E}[\cdot]\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}X_{j_{\ell}^{\prime},i_{\ell}^{\prime}}^{(b_{2\ell-1})}X_{j_{\ell}^{\prime},i_{\ell+1}^{\prime}}^{(b_{2\ell})}-\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}Y_{j_{\ell}^{\prime},i_{\ell}^{\prime}}^{(b_{2\ell-1})}Y_{j_{\ell}^{\prime},i_{\ell+1}^{\prime}}^{(b_{2\ell})}-\mathbb{E}[\cdot]\bigg)\bigg]

where

\widetilde{G}^{2}
. In the case
r=2k
, note that we must have
b_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\in[2k]
and then the fact that we subtract
X,Y
means that the term is in fact precisely
0
. Thus we may assume
r<2k
. Therefore we may suppose these
r\geq 0
edges form
s\geq 0
connected components which are all paths. The number of choices for
i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
is then easily seen to be
(O(k))^{O(s)}\cdot(O(n))^{4k-r-s}
. When
s=0
, we have
r=0
, and we can save an extra factor of
n
compared to the bound
(O(n))^{4k}
for the counting given above: if
(j_{\ell})_{\ell\in[k]}
and
(j_{\ell}^{\prime})_{\ell\in[k]}
share no values, then the two parts of the covariance are independence and we obtain a
0
contribution, so we can ignore the bulk of those terms. The number of ways to choose

to have such an overlap is at most
k^{2}m^{2k-1}n^{2k}=(O(n))^{4k-1}
.

Therefore, we deduce

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[\widetilde{C}_{k}

\displaystyle(m,t,G,\sigma(t,G^{(2)}(t)))-C_{k}(m,G)]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,s}\frac{2^{2k}}{(mn)^{k}}\cdot 2^{2k}\cdot(O(1/n))^{2k-r}\cdot\min\{(O(k))^{O(s)}(O(n))^{4k-r-s},(O(n))^{4k-1}\}

\displaystyle\leq(O(1))^{k}\cdot\min\{(k^{O(1)}/n)^{s},1/n\}\leq n^{-5/6}\operatorname{Var}[C_{k}(m,G)].

We used
k=\eta\log n
where recall
\eta>0
will be chosen sufficiently small. This completes the justification of
Equation

3.4
for
k>1
;
k=1
is easily checked by hand.

For

3.1
and
3.3
the argument is similar, but the expectation computation is complicated by the fact that
\mathbb{E}\rho_{1}^{(1)}\rho_{2}^{(1)}\neq 0
. In fact, instead of conditioning on the values
M_{1}+\cdots+M_{(n+t)/2},M_{(n+t)/2+1}+\cdots+M_{n}
that appear, it is easier to only condition on the total sum

(since we are only conditioning on information related to it for this computation), and to modify
Definition

3.2
appropriately. Additionally, since the value

plays no role if we compute the expectation this way, we actually have the better error term listed in
Equation

3.1
. However, we do omit these details here (note that
[

, Lemma 3.2]
implies that our claimed mean shift is in fact what comes out of doing the necessary computations).
∎

Finally, in order to prove the Gaussian convergence of
C_{k}
when the matrix is sampled from
\Delta_{0}
, we rely on an argument based on exchangeable pairs and Stein’s method. Let us first define the notion of a pair of exchangeable random variables.

Definition 3.4
.

X^{\prime}
and

are exchangeable random variables if
(X^{\prime},X)
and
(X,X^{\prime})
have the same distribution.

The key probability theoretic statement we will use is a multivariate version of the method of exchangeable random variables for proving convergence to a Gaussian. This form is due to Meckes
[
14
]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Specialization of
[
14
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
(X,X^{\prime})
be an exchangeable pair of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that there is an invertible matrix
\Lambda
and a random matrix
E^{\prime}
such that

•

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X^{\prime}-X\big|X}}\right]=-\Lambda X

•

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X^{\prime}-X)(X^{\prime}-X)^{T}\big|X}}\right]=2\Lambda+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E^{\prime}\big|X}}\right].

Then for
g\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(3.8)

\big|\mathbb{E}g(X)-\mathbb{E}g(Z)\big|\leq\|\Lambda^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{4}M_{2}(g)\mathbb{E}\|E^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+\frac{1}{9}M_{3}(g)\mathbb{E}\lVert X^{\prime}-X\rVert_{2}^{3}}}\right]

where
Z
is a standard Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We will need the following bound on moments of standard Gaussian random variables (which is a special case of a phenomenon called
hypercontractivity
).

Theorem 3.6

(
[
16
, Theorem 9.21]
)
.

Let
f
be a polynomial in
n
variables of degree at most
d
. Let
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
either be a vector of independent Rademacher random variables or a vector of independent standard Gaussian random variables. Then for any real number
q\geq 2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[|f(\vec{x})|^{q}\big]^{1/q}\leq\big(\sqrt{q-1}\big)^{d}\mathbb{E}\big[f(\vec{x})^{2}\big]^{1/2}.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
m=\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}=\lfloor\alpha_{c}n-\eta\log n\rfloor
and
K=\lceil\eta\log n\rceil
. We define
V(M)
as in
Lemma

2.8
and consider
n
sufficiently large. For
g\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{K})
, we have

\big|\mathbb{E}g(V(G^{(0)}))-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{K})}g(Z)\big|\leq(M_{2}(g)+M_{3}(g))n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

We now define the setup for our exchangeable pairs. Let

G=G^{(0)}=(g_{j,i})_{(j,i)\in[m]\times[n]}

and

G^{\prime}=(g_{i,j})_{(j,i)\in([m]\times[n])\setminus I}\cup(g_{I})

where
I
is an index sampled from
[m]\times[n]
uniformly at random and the
g_{I}
is an independent standard Gaussian. The exchangeable pair of random variables we will consider is:

V=\bigg(\frac{C_{1}(m,G)}{\sqrt{2\cdot 1}},\ldots,\frac{C_{K}(m,G)}{\sqrt{2K}}\bigg),\qquad V^{\prime}=\bigg(\frac{C_{1}(m,G^{\prime})}{\sqrt{2\cdot 1}},\ldots,\frac{C_{K}(m,G^{\prime})}{\sqrt{2K}}\bigg).

Since
I
is a uniformly random element from
[m]\times[n]
notice that

\mathbb{E}[V^{\prime}-V|V]=-\text{diag}\bigg(\frac{2k-\mathbbm{1}_{k=1}}{mn}\bigg)_{k\in[K]}V

so define

\Lambda=\text{diag}\bigg(\frac{2k-\mathbbm{1}_{k=1}}{mn}\bigg)_{k\in[K]}.

In order to apply
Theorem

3.5
, we take

E^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}[(V^{\prime}-V)(V^{\prime}-V)^{T}-2\Lambda|G]

and note that this satisfies the required condition for
E^{\prime}
as
V
is a measurable function of
G
.

We bound each of the terms appearing in
Equation

3.8
. First,
\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=mn
. Second, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert X^{\prime}-X\rVert_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}(C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime}))^{2}\bigg)^{3/2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq K^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}|C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime})|^{3}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq n^{1/5}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime})\bigg|^{2}\bigg]\bigg)^{3/2}

\displaystyle=n^{1/5}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}(2k/(mn)\mathbb{E}[C_{k}(m,G)^{2}])^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-11/4}

where we have applied Holder’s inequality,
Theorem

3.6
, and computed the variance using orthogonality of various monomials in order to prove the desired result (note that all but at most
2k/(mn)
fraction of the monomials are canceled in
C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime})
).

Finally, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert E^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathbb{E}\lVert E^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(2\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq K}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[(C_{k_{1}}(m,G)-C_{k_{1}}(m,G^{\prime}))(C_{k_{2}}(m,G)-C_{k_{2}}(m,G^{\prime}))|G]^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{1\leq k\leq K}\operatorname{Var}\big[\mathbb{E}[(C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime}))^{2}|G]\big]\bigg)^{1/2}

where we have used that
2k(E^{\prime})_{k,k}=\mathbb{E}[(C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime}))^{2}|G]-\mathbb{E}[(C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime}))^{2}]
. We now handle the off-diagonal and on-diagonal contribution separately. For ease of notation, given an index
I\in[m]\times[n]
we define

\displaystyle C_{k}(m,M,I)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\bigg)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m]\text{ distinct}\\
I\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}\{(j_{\ell},i_{\ell}),(j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}M_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}M_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}},

\displaystyle C_{k}^{\ast}(m,M,I)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\bigg)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m]\text{ distinct}\\
I\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}\{(j_{\ell},i_{\ell}),(j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{(j,i)\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}\{(j_{\ell},i_{\ell}),(j_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\}\setminus I}M_{j,i}.

We consider the off-diagonal contribution first. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[(C_{k_{1}}(m,G)-C_{k_{1}}(m,G^{\prime}))(C_{k_{2}}(m,G)-C_{k_{2}}(m,G^{\prime}))|G]^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I)+C_{k_{1}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{m^{2}n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I)\bigg)^{2}+\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k_{1}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)\bigg)^{2}\bigg].

Note that as
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, we have that
C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I)
and
C_{k_{1}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}^{\ast}(m,G,I)
are mean zero random variables. We will bound

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I)C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I)\bigg)^{2}\bigg].

Expanding, each term is of the form
C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I_{1})C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I_{1})C_{k_{1}}(m,G,I_{2})C_{k_{2}}(m,G,I_{2})
. A term contributes precisely if the monomial in
G
has all even powers. Furthermore, any
G_{j,i}
appears at most
4
times, which means any contribution is of size at most
3^{4K}=(O(1))^{K}
. Therefore, we are primarily interested in counting the number of nonzero terms that arise. We have a multigraph
H
coming from layering two cycles of length
k_{1}
and two cycles of length
k_{2}
, with possible overlaps (including two guaranteed overlaps at edges
I_{1},I_{2}
). Let there be
v
vertices, and let
H^{\prime}
be the multigraph formed by halving the multiplicities of
H
. Note that each vertex has at least two outgoing edges to different vertices in
H^{\prime}
, and the total multiplicity of edges is
2k_{1}+2k_{2}
. We deduce that there are at most
2k_{1}+2k_{2}
vertices, with equality precisely when
H^{\prime}
is a disjoint union of cycles. However,
H^{\prime}
must be connected and can be seen to not be a single cycle since
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
. Thus in fact
v\leq 2k_{1}+2k_{2}-1
.

If
v\leq 2k_{1}+2k_{2}-8\log\log n
then there are at most
(8k)^{8k}n^{v}\leq n^{2k_{1}+2k_{2}-4}
choices of term, using
k\leq\eta\log n
. Otherwise let
r=2k_{1}+2k_{2}-v
and note
1\leq r\leq 8\log\log n
. We see that
H^{\prime}
has at most
O(r)
many vertices of degree at least
3
, where we include multiplicity (since there are
2k_{1}+2k_{2}
edges with multiplicity,
2k_{1}+2k_{2}-r
vertices, and each vertex has at least two distinct neighbors). Notice that between such vertices,
H^{\prime}
must have paths and there are at most
K^{r^{2}}
ways to choose the sizes of all these paths. After doing so, there are
4^{O(K)}
ways to assign these edges to various cycles. Finally, there are at most
e^{O(K)}
ways to order these into cycles: outside of the
r
higher degree vertices, we have bounded degree so a bounded number of choices for the next edge at every stage, and among
r\leq 8\log\log n=O(\log K)
vertices we have at most
O(K)
choices, for
K^{O(\log K)}
choices. Since
K^{r^{2}}=K^{O((\log K)^{2})}
we easily find a total of at most
(O(1))^{K}n^{2k_{1}+2k_{2}-1}
total terms.

Plugging in, we find the contribution to
(\mathbb{E}\lVert E^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}})^{2}
from these off-diagonal terms is at most

\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{2}}\cdot(O(1))^{K}n^{2k_{1}+2k_{2}-1}\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{(mn)^{k_{1}}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1}{(mn)^{k_{2}}}\bigg).

The total contribution is at most
n^{-5+1/6}
. The situation for
C^{\ast}
is even simpler, and we can obtain a bound of
n^{-5+1/6}
analogously. Additionally, this analysis is easily seen to hold when
k_{1}=1
.

For the on-diagonal contribution, the analysis is quite similar. For
k=1
, straightforward computation gives a contribution to
(\mathbb{E}\lVert E^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}})^{2}
of at most
O(n^{-6})
.

For
k\geq 2
, note that we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\big[\mathbb{E}[(C_{k}(m,G)-C_{k}(m,G^{\prime}))^{2}|G]\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{2}}\operatorname{Var}\bigg[\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}(C_{k}(m,G,I)^{2}+C_{k}^{\ast}(m,G,I)^{2})\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{m^{2}n^{2}}\bigg(\operatorname{Var}\bigg[\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k}(m,G,I)^{2}\bigg]+\operatorname{Var}\bigg[\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k}^{\ast}(m,G,I)^{2}\bigg]\bigg).

As before we consider the
C
term, since the
C^{\ast}
term can be analyzed analogously. We have

\operatorname{Var}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k}(m,G,I)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k}(m,G,I)^{2}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{I\in[m]\times[n]}C_{k}(m,G,I)^{2}\bigg]^{2}.

Again view this as an expansion into
4
overlapping cycles as in the off-diagonal case. Here however as the two cycles in each pair coming from
k_{1},k_{2}
are isomorphic, one can overlap them perfectly, which was not allowed in the prior analysis (which was used to argue that in fact
v\leq 2k_{1}+2k_{2}-1
, ruling out
v=2k_{1}+2k_{2}
). In this remaining case, we can see the contribution is exactly cancelled however by the subtracted term. The proof can thus proceed similarly to the off-diagonal case, and we obtain a bound
n^{-5+1/6}
in this case as well.

Now we deduce

\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{4}\mathbb{E}\lVert E^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}\lVert X^{\prime}-X\rVert_{2}^{3}\bigg)\leq mn(\sqrt{\log n}(4n^{-5+1/6})^{1/2}+n^{-11/4})\leq n^{-1/3},

and therefore
Theorem

3.5
finishes.
∎

The result is ultimately a straightforward combination of

Proof of

By

\displaystyle=2n\sum_{1\leq t\leq n/2-\sqrt{n}\log n}2^{n}\exp(nH(t/n))(q(t/n))^{m}

Next, notice that the function

where we have used

Proof of

2.2
.

Notice that linearity of expectation and conditioning gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(G)^{2}\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G)\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G)\geq-L])]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n\leq t\leq n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}2^{n}\binom{n}{(n+t)/2}\mathbb{P}[v_{n},v_{t}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)]\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\geq-L])].

We first handle the range when
n^{1/2}\log n\leq|t|\leq n-1
. By
Equation

2.3
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{1/2}\log n\leq|t|\leq n-1\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}2^{n}\binom{n}{(n+t)/2}\mathbb{P}[v_{n},v_{t}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)]\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\geq-L])]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2L)\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{1/2}\log n\leq|t|\leq n-1\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}2^{n}\binom{n}{(n+t)/2}\mathbb{P}[v_{n},v_{t}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2\eta}\exp(-(\log n)^{3/2})\leq\exp(-(\log n)^{4/3})

which is decaying faster than any polynomial. We next handle the case where
t=\pm n
; notice the contribution in this case is

\displaystyle 2\cdot 2^{n}\mathbb{P}[v_{n}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)]\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(n))\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(n))\geq-L])]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[X(G)]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(1)})\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(1)})\geq-L])]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[X(G)]\leq n^{-\Omega(1)}\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}.

We used
Equation

2.5
of
Lemma

2.8
with an appropriate test function
g
(an exponential of a linear function with smooth cutoff) to bound the quantity
\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(1)})\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(1)})\geq-L])]
by a constant, similar to in the proof of
Equation

2.1
. The final inequality follows since
\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}
is such that
\mathbb{E}X(G)=n^{\Theta(\eta)}
is of polynomial size.

Finally we handle the case where
|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n
. For the sake of brevity, we define
\widetilde{Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(t))\geq-L])]
. Now

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}2^{n}\binom{n}{(n+t)/2}\mathbb{P}[v_{n},v_{t}\in S_{\tau_{\operatorname{pre}}}(G)]\widetilde{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{4^{n}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/2}\bigg)q\bigg(\frac{n+t}{2n}\bigg)^{m}\widetilde{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=q(1/2)^{m-\alpha_{c}n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{4^{n}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/4}\bigg)q\bigg(\frac{n+t}{2n}\bigg)^{\alpha_{c}n}\widetilde{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=q(1/2)^{m-\alpha_{c}n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{4^{n}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/4}\bigg)\bigg(q\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)}{8n^{2}}\pm n^{-5/4}\bigg)^{\alpha_{c}n}\widetilde{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=q(1/2)^{m-\alpha_{c}n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{4^{n}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/6}\bigg)\bigg(q\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)}{8n^{2}}\bigg)^{\alpha_{c}n}\widetilde{Y}(0).

We used
3.8
in the first line, used that
q(\cdot)
has bounded first, second, third derivatives in the neighborhood of
1/2
in the second and third lines, and that
\widetilde{Y}(t)=(1\pm n^{-2/11})\widetilde{Y}(0)
(which is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

2.8
and an argument identical to that in the proof of
Equation

2.1
) in the final line.

Continuing,

\displaystyle q(1/2)^{m-\alpha_{c}n}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{4^{n}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/6}\bigg)\bigg(q\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)}{8n^{2}}\bigg)^{\alpha_{c}n}\widetilde{Y}(0)

\displaystyle=4^{n}p^{2m}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\pm n^{-1/6}\bigg)\bigg(1+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)}{8n^{2}p^{2}}\bigg)^{\alpha_{c}n}

\displaystyle=4^{n}p^{2m}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\leq n^{1/2}\log n\\
(n+t)/2\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)\alpha_{c}}{8np^{2}}\pm n^{-2/13}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq(1+n^{-1/7})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+\frac{t^{2}q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)\alpha_{c}}{8np^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1+n^{-1/7})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+\frac{t^{2}\kappa^{2}e^{-\kappa^{2}}\alpha_{c}}{\pi np^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1+n^{-1/7})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}+\frac{t^{2}\alpha_{c}(1-\mu_{2})^{2}}{2n}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1+n^{-1/7})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}(1-4\beta^{2})}{2n}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-1/8})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}(1-4\beta^{2})}{2}\bigg)~dx

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-1/8})\mathbb{E}[X(G)]^{2}\widetilde{Y}(0)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4\beta^{2}}}

where we have used that
q(1/2)=p^{2}
, the values of
q^{\prime\prime}(1/2),(1-\mu_{2})p
and identities relating this value to
\beta
via
Items

1
and
6
of
Lemma

3.1
, and finally using the Euler–Maclaurin formula to transfer from the final sum to an integral.

The final remaining task is therefore to bound

\widetilde{Y}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(0))\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(0))\geq-L])].

Note that

Y_{K}(m,G^{(2)}(0))
is approximately normally distributed as
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\ast},\sigma^{2})
with

\mu^{\ast}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(2\beta)^{k}}{\sqrt{2k}}\cdot\frac{2(2\beta)^{k}}{\sqrt{2k}}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(2\beta)^{2k}}{4k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{3(2\beta)^{2k}}{4k},\qquad\sigma^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(2\beta)^{2k}}{2k}.

Via an argument essentially identical to that in the proof of
Equation

2.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}(0)

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-1/4})\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2Z)]

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-1/4})\exp(-2\mu^{\ast}+2\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-1/4})\exp\bigg(-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(2\beta)^{2k}}{2k}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)})\sqrt{1-4\beta^{2}}.

Finally, putting everything together we have

\mathbb{E}[X(G)^{2}\exp(-2Y_{K}(m,G)\mathbbm{1}[Y_{K}(m,G)\geq-L])]\leq n^{-\Omega(1)}\mathbb{E}X(G)^{2}+e^{-(\log n)^{4/3}}+(1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)})\mathbb{E}X(G)^{2},

so the desired result follows immediately.
∎
