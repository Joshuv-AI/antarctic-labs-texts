Harmless label noise and informative soft-labels in supervised classification
Daniel Ahfock, Geoffrey J. McLachlan

Abstract
Manual labelling of training examples is common practice in supervised learning. When the labelling task is of non-trivial difficulty, the supplied labels may not be equal to the ground-truth labels, and label noise is introduced into the training dataset. If the manual annotation is carried out by multiple experts, the same training example can be given different class assignments by different experts, which is indicative of label noise. In the framework of model-based classification, a simple, but key observation is that when the manual labels are sampled using the posterior probabilities of class membership, the noisy labels are as valuable as the ground-truth labels in terms of statistical information. A relaxation of this process is a random effects model for imperfect labelling by a group that uses approximate posterior probabilities of class membership. The relative efficiency of logistic regression using the noisy labels compared to logistic regression using the ground-truth labels can then be derived. The main finding is that logistic regression can be robust to label noise when label noise and classification difficulty are positively correlated. In particular, when classification difficulty is the only source of label errors, multiple sets of noisy labels can supply more information for the estimation of a classification rule compared to the single set of ground-truth labels.



1
Introduction

Many supervised learning algorithms operate on the assumption that the training set labels are the ground-truth labels. In practice, ground-truth labels may not be readily obtainable, and manual annotation is used to construct the training dataset
(
Frénay and Verleysen 2014
)
. In medical applications, clinicians will often classify patients into different groups on the basis of preliminary examinations. In machine learning, it is convenient to crowdsource labels for image and speech recognition tasks through an online platform. Due to the subjective nature of the process, and the inherent difficulty of classifying some observations, the manually collected labels may not be equal to the ground-truth labels. This mismatch is often referred to as label noise, and this phenomenon can have interesting statistical implications
(
McLachlan 1972
;
Bouveyron et al. 2019
;
Cannings et al. 2020
)
. A primary concern is the robustness of an estimated classification rule with respect to label noise
(
Cappozzo et al. 2019
;
Vranckx et al. 2021
)
. A closely related issue is that when each member of a group of experts provides a class assignment, the agreement is not necessarily unanimous. The situation when there is heterogeneity amongst the supplied labels is referred to as soft-labelling, as there is no definitive class assignment for each feature vector
(
Quost et al. 2017
)
. Extracting the maximum amount of information from conflicting label sets is a challenging task in statistical machine learning
(
Dawid and Skene 1979
;
Smyth et al. 1995
;
Raykar et al. 2009
;
Yan et al. 2010
)
.

In the framework of model-based classification, the ground-truth labels are commonly treated as latent variables in a finite-mixture model
(
Fraley and Raftery 2002
;
McLachlan and Peel 2000
)
. The data generating process for a
g
-component mixture model can be represented as a hierarchical model,

\displaystyle\bm{Z}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(1,\bm{\pi}),

\displaystyle\bm{Y}\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=1

\displaystyle\sim f(\bm{y};\bm{\omega}_{i}),

where
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{g})^{\mathsf{T}}
gives the mixing proportions and
\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\omega}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{\omega}_{g}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}
is a vector of component specific parameters. The latent variable
\bm{Z}
is considered the ground-truth label as the feature vector
\bm{Y}
is then sampled from the
i
th class conditional distribution
f(\bm{y};\bm{\omega}_{i})
given that
\bm{Z}_{i}=1

(i=1,\ldots,g)
. We propose to treat the manual label
\bm{Z}^{\prime}
as a random variable to model the manual labelling process. Label noise occurs when
\bm{Z}^{{}^{\prime}}\neq\bm{Z}
.

We develop a probabilistic model for manual annotation where label noise is positively associated with classification difficulty. Using this model, we analyse the relative value of noisy manual labels compared to the ground-truth labels in the context of logistic regression. As a starting point, Section
2.2
highlights that when the manual labels
\bm{Z}^{\prime}
are sampled according to the posterior probabilities of class membership, the use of noisy labels can be as efficient as the use of the ground-truth labels
\bm{Z}
. In Section
2.3
we introduce a random-effects model for group labelling to account for imperfect knowledge of the posterior class-probabilities. In Section
3
we derive the asymptotic relative efficiency of logistic regression using the noisy labels to logistic regression using the ground-truth labels. The key finding is that multiple sets of noisy labels can supply more information for the estimation of a classification rule relative to the single set of ground-truth labels. Section
4
presents simulation results regarding the asymptotic relative efficiency. In Section
5
we assess the proposed random effects model on the Gastroentology dataset introduced in Figure (
1
) (a) and present the results of experiments on the Wisconsin breast cancer dataset. Finally, conclusions and directions for future work are given in Section
6
.

2
Manual labelling

2.1
Prior work

A comprehensive survey of label noise in supervised learning is given in
Frénay and Verleysen 2014
. Early statistical methods for manual labels treated the ground-truth label as a latent variable to be estimated on the basis of the observed noisy labels
(
Dawid and Skene 1979
;
Smyth et al. 1995
)
. More recently, there has been a focus on constructing generative models for the noisy labels in order train a classifier
(
Jin and Ghahramani 2003
;
Bouveyron and Girard 2009
;
Yan et al. 2010
;
Raykar et al. 2009
)
. In a related branch of work on the analysis of crowdsourced labels, methods have been developed for filtering out bad actors who deliberately mislabel instances, and ranking annotators when domain expertise varies in the group
(
Raykar and Yu 2012
;
Hovy et al. 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2013
)
. We will assume that the annotators are of comparable skill level and none act maliciously. This assumption will likely be violated in crowdsourcing applications with low barriers to entry, but is more plausible when manual labels are collected from qualified experts in a medical study.

Prior work on modelling group labelling typically makes the assumption that label noise occurs uniformly within a class
(
Dawid and Skene 1979
;
Raykar et al. 2009
;
Jin and Ghahramani 2003
;
Donmez et al. 2010
;
Song et al. 2020
)
. Feature dependent label noise is considered in
Yan et al. 2010
to allow for different experts in the group to specialise in separate areas of the feature space. The behaviour of logistic regression has been studied under the assumption of fixed class-conditional label noise rates. In
Michalek and Tripathi 1980
and
Bi and Jeske 2010
it is demonstrated that with fixed class-conditional noise, logistic regression is not necessarily consistent. The increase in the expected error rate due to the noise is also derived. Under class-conditional uniform noise,
Song et al. 2020
show that the noisy labels can be treated as the response in a modified generalized linear model and compare the relative value of the noisy labels to the ground-truth labels.

The idea that label noise is more concentrated around classification decision boundaries has been explored in the context of robust estimation
(
Xu et al. 2006
;
Rebbapragada and Brodley 2007
;
Blanchard et al. 2016
)
. However, there appears to be little statistical work modelling this phenomenon and quantifying the information loss relative to the ground-truth labels. In practice, information loss, manual adulteration, and data-entry errors can introduce label noise into the dataset. In order to simplify the theoretical analysis we make the assumption that only contributing factor to label errors is classification difficulty as measured by the posterior probabilities of class membership.

2.2
Label noise

Here we show that label noise does not necessarily lead to a loss in statistical information. The mixing proportions
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{g})^{\mathsf{T}}
and component specific parameters
\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\omega}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{\omega}_{g}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}
can be put together into the single parameter
\bm{\Psi}=(\bm{\pi}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{\omega}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\bm{\Omega}
. We assume that the parameter space
\bm{\Omega}
is such that the mixture model is identifiable, suitable conditions for this to hold are discussed in
Cheng and Liu 2001
. Let
\tau_{i}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})
represent the posterior probability of membership in the
i
th class

\displaystyle\tau_{i}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\pi_{i}f(\bm{y};\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\sum_{h=1}^{g}\pi_{h}f(\bm{y};\bm{\omega}_{h})}\quad(i=1,\ldots,g),

(1)

and
\bm{\tau}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})=(\tau_{1}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi}),\ldots,\tau_{g}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi}))^{\mathsf{T}}
give the vector of
g
posterior class-probabilities. To model the subjective nature of manual labelling, suppose the annotated label
\bm{Z}^{\prime}
is distributed as a multinomial random variable according to the posterior probabilities of class membership (
1
),

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{\prime}\mid\bm{Y}=\bm{y}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(1,\bm{\tau}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})).

(2)

Under this labelling model, the probability of a labelling error is dependent on the posterior class-probabilities
\bm{\tau}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\text{pr}(\bm{Z}^{\prime}\neq\bm{Z}\mid\bm{Y}=\bm{y})

\displaystyle=1-\text{pr}(\bm{Z}^{\prime}=\bm{Z}\mid\bm{Y}=\bm{y})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\text{pr}(Z_{i}^{\prime}=1,Z_{i}=1\mid\bm{Y}=\bm{y})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\{\tau_{i}(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})\}^{2}.

Broadly speaking, the greater the classification difficulty, the greater the probability of a labelling error. In the two-class problem, the probability of a labelling error approaches 0.5 near the decision boundary.

The manual label model (
2
) is particularly interesting as the joint distribution of the manual label
\bm{Z}^{\prime}
and the feature vector
\bm{Y}
is the same as the joint distribution of the ground-truth label
\bm{Z}
and the feature vector
\bm{Y}
. The joint distributions
(\bm{Y},\bm{Z}^{\prime})\sim g(\bm{y},\bm{z}^{\prime};\bm{\Psi})
and
(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})\sim f(\bm{y},\bm{z};\bm{\Psi})
are equal as

\displaystyle g(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})g(\bm{z}^{\prime}\mid\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})f(\bm{z}^{\prime}\mid\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{y};\bm{\Psi}),

where the substitution
g(\bm{z}^{\prime}\mid\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})=f(\bm{z}^{\prime}\mid\bm{y};\bm{\Psi})
is possible as the manual labels
\bm{Z}^{\prime}
are sampled according to the posterior probabilities of class membership (
1
). As such, the joint distribution of
(\bm{Y},\bm{Z}^{\prime})
is the same as the joint distribution of
(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})
. Consequently, given
n
independently and identically distributed observations using the manual labelling model,
\{(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{Z}_{j}^{{}^{\prime}})\}_{j=1}^{n}
, the distribution of the maximum likelihood estimate will be the same as if using a dataset with the ground-truth labels
\{(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{Z}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
. It follows that that the expected error rate of a classifier trained using
\bm{Z}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{n}^{\prime}
will be the same as a classifier trained using the ground-truth labels
\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{n}
. This is a proof of concept that label noise can be harmless when the label noise process is governed by the posterior probabilities of class membership.

2.3
Group labelling

Suppose that there are
m
individuals in the labelling group, and they each supply a label for each of the
n
feature vectors in the dataset. In an idealised scenario the ground-truth labels, feature vectors and manually assigned labels are treated as
n
independently and identically distributed observations from the model:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(1,\bm{\pi}),

(3a)

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{j}\mid Z_{ji}=1

\displaystyle\sim f(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\omega}_{i}),

(3b)

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{jk}^{\prime}\mid\bm{Y}_{j}=\bm{y}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(1,\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi}))\quad(k=1,\ldots,m).

(3c)

The random vector
\bm{Z}_{j}=(Z_{j1},\ldots,Z_{jg})^{\mathsf{T}}
represents the ground-truth label for the feature vector
\bm{y}_{j}
. The random vector
\bm{Z}_{jk}^{\prime}=(Z_{jk1},\ldots,{Z}_{jkg})^{\mathsf{T}}
represents the label assigned to the feature vector
\bm{y}_{j}
by the
k
th individual in the group of experts. Let
S_{ji}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(Z_{jki}=1)
, represent the number of votes that entity
j
belongs to class
i
over the
m
experts in the group, and
\bm{S}_{j}=(S_{j1},\ldots,S_{jg})^{\mathsf{T}}
give the vector of vote counts for each of the
g
classes. It follows from the model (
3a
),(
3b
),(
3c
) that
S_{ji}\sim\text{Binomial}(m,\tau_{i}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi}))
and the vector of aggregated class counts
\bm{S}_{j}
has the distribution

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(m,\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi})).

(4)

To relax the assumption about the accuracy of manual annotation, we adopt a random effects model for the class label counts
\bm{S}_{j}
. The Dirichlet-Multinomial model
(
Johnson et al. 1997
)
is an extension of the Multinomial distribution that can be used to account for the fact that the group of experts does not have exact knowledge of the posterior probabilities of class membership
\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi})
. We introduce the overdispersion parameter
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
to give the proposed Dirichlet-Multinomial model for the aggregated manual labels

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Dirichlet-Multinomial}(m,\alpha_{0}\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi})).

(5)

The model (
5
) has the hierarchical representation

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{0}\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi})),

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{j}\mid\bm{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(m,\bm{p}_{j}).

The overdispersion parameter
\alpha_{0}
controls how concentrated the draws of
\bm{p}_{j}
are around the true posterior class-probabilities
\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\Psi})
. As
\alpha_{0}\to\infty
the excess variance goes to zero, and the Dirichlet-Multinomial model (
5
) converges to the multinomial distribution (
4
).
The Dirichlet-Multinomial model still enforces a relationship between the probability of a labelling error and classification difficulty, but relaxes the assumptions on the accuracy of the manual annotation compared to (
4
). An important difference between our model and existing work is that we make an explicit connection between probability of labelling error and classification difficulty using the posterior class-probabilities.

3
Relative efficiency

We now consider the relative value of the aggregated noisy manual labels
\bm{S}_{1},\ldots,\bm{S}_{n}
compared to the ground-truth labels
\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{n}
for training a discriminative classifier
(
Ng and Jordan 2002
)
. We will consider a binary classification problem where the posterior probabilities of class membership
\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})
are described by a logistic regression model with parameter
\bm{\beta}=({\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}
,

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\text{pr}(Z_{j1}=1\mid\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})}{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})}.

(6)

For example, this will be the case for two-class normal discrimination with equal covariance matrices.

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
represent the
n
observed feature vectors,
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
represent the ground-truth labels,
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\{\bm{z}_{jk}^{\prime}\}_{j=1,\ldots,n}^{k=1,\ldots,m}
represent the manual labels, and
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{s}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{z}_{jk}^{\prime}\}_{j=1}^{n}
represent the class vote counts from manual annotation. The logistic regression log-likelihood functions using the ground-truth labels
\mathcal{Z}
and the aggregated noisy manual labels
\mathcal{S}
are respectively,

\displaystyle\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j1}(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})-\log\left\{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j1}(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})-m\log\left\{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}_{j})\right\}.

(8)

We compare the use of
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
and
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})
for the estimation of
\bm{\beta}
. Let
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{G}
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
be the estimated coefficients from the maximisation
L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
and
L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})
respectively, and
\widehat{R}_{G}
and
\widehat{R}_{M}
be the corresponding estimated classification rules. The goal is to determine the asymptotic relative efficiency

\displaystyle\rm{ARE}

\displaystyle=\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\ \dfrac{\mathbb{E}\{{\rm{err}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{G};\bm{\beta})\}-\rm{err}(\bm{\beta};\bm{\beta})}{\mathbb{E}\{{\rm{err}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M};\bm{\beta})\}-\rm{err}(\bm{\beta};\bm{\beta})},

where
\text{err}(\bm{\beta}^{*};\bm{\beta})
denotes the conditional error rate when using the estimate
\bm{\beta}^{*}
when the true parameter is
\bm{\beta}
. The key assumptions for the analysis are summarised below.

Assumption 1
.

The observations
(\bm{Y}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{Z}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{S}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})
are independently and identically distributed for
j=1,\ldots,n
, and
\text{cov}(\bm{Y}_{j})=\bm{\Sigma}
for some symmetric positive definite matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. Furthermore, the conditional distribution of
\bm{Z}_{j}
given
\bm{Y}_{j}
is given by the logistic regression model (
6
), and the aggregated manual labels
\bm{S}_{j}
are distributed according to the Dirichlet-Multinomial model (
5
).

Using the ground-truth labels,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{G}-\bm{\beta})\to N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{LR}^{-1})
, where
\bm{I}_{LR}
is the Fisher information in the logistic regression
(
Efron 1975
)
. Theorem
1
gives the asymptotic distribution of the estimator using the noisy manual labels
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that the conditions of Assumption 1 are satisfied. Then
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
is a consistent estimator of
\bm{\beta}
, and
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}-\bm{\beta})\to N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{M}^{-1})
, where
\bm{I}_{M}
is the Godambe information matrix. The Godambe information matrix is equal to

\displaystyle\bm{I}_{M}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{m+\alpha_{0}}\bm{I}_{LR},

where
\bm{I}_{LR}
is the Fisher information matrix for the logistic regression using the ground-truth labels,
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
.

Proof.

Under the imperfect supervision model (
5
), the logistic regression on the manual labels,
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})
is misspecified. We first show that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
remains a consistent estimator. Under the Dirichlet-Multinomial model for the manual labels,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{S}_{j}\mid\bm{y}_{j}]=m\{\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}
for all values of the overdispersion parameter
\alpha_{0}
.
It follows that the expected value of the score statistic is still zero despite the model misspecification,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{S}}\left[\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}\mid\bm{Y}}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\{S_{j1}-m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}\bm{y}_{j}\mid\bm{Y}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\{m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})-m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}\bm{y}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\{0\}\bm{y}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

Under the conditions of Assumption 1, we can apply Theorem 3 in
Fahrmeir 1990
for the asymptotic behaviour of M-estimators in misspecified generalised linear models. It can be seen that the conditions of Assumption 1 are sufficient to meet the regularity conditions by appealing to Corollary 3 in
Fahrmeir and Kaufmann 1985
. By Theorem 3 in
Fahrmeir 1990
,
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
is a consistent estimator of
\bm{\beta}
and symptomatically normally distributed. The asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}
is given by
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M}-\bm{\beta})\to N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{M}^{-1})
,
where the Godambe information is given by
\bm{I}_{M}=\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{G}\bm{H}^{-1}
, where

\displaystyle\bm{H}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\beta}\partial\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\right\},

\displaystyle\bm{G}

\displaystyle=\text{cov}\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\right),

where the expectation and variance are taken over a single feature vector
\bm{Y}_{j}
and vector of label counts
\bm{S}_{j}
. As
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})
has the form of a logistic regression model,

\displaystyle\bm{H}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\{1-\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}\bm{y}_{j}\bm{y}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]=m\bm{I}_{LR},

(9)

where
\bm{I}_{LR}
is the Fisher information for
\bm{\beta}
in the log-likelihood
\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
that uses the ground-truth labels. Under the Dirichlet-Multinomial model the variance of the class count is

\displaystyle\text{var}[S_{j1}\mid\bm{y}_{j}]

\displaystyle=m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\{1-\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}\dfrac{m+\alpha_{0}}{1+\alpha_{0}}.

Using the law of total-variance,

\displaystyle\bm{G}

\displaystyle=\text{cov}\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\text{cov}\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\right)\mid\bm{Y}\right]+\text{cov}\left(\mathbb{E}_{S\mid Y}\left[\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\log L_{LR}(\bm{\beta};\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Y})\mid\bm{Y}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\text{var}(S_{j1})\bm{y}_{j}\bm{y}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]+\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m+\alpha_{0}}{1+\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[m\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\{1-\tau_{1}(\bm{y}_{j};\bm{\beta})\}\bm{y}_{j}\bm{y}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m+\alpha_{0}}{1+\alpha_{0}}m\bm{I}_{LR}.

(10)

Combining (
9
) and (
10
), the Godambe information
\bm{I}_{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{I}_{M}

\displaystyle=\bm{H}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1+\alpha_{0}}{m+\alpha_{0}}m\bm{I}_{LR}\bm{I}_{LR}^{-1}\bm{I}_{LR}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{m+\alpha_{0}}\bm{I}_{LR}.

∎

Under the Dirichlet-Multinomial model (
5
), logistic regression using the noisy labels
\mathcal{S}
provides a consistent estimator of
{\bm{\beta}}
. This is not necessarily the case when label noise process is uniform within each class
(
Michalek and Tripathi 1980
;
Bi and Jeske 2010
)
. Theorem
1
connects the Godambe information
\bm{I}_{M}
from the set of noisy labels
\mathcal{S}
to the Fisher information
\bm{I}_{LR}
from the the ground-truth labels
\mathcal{Z}
. From this relationship we can derive the asymptotic relative efficiency of
\widehat{R}_{M}
compared to
\widehat{R}_{G}
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose the conditions of Assumption 1 are satisfied. The asymptotic relative efficiency of
\widehat{R}_{M}
compared to
\widehat{R}_{G}
is

\displaystyle\rm{ARE}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{m+\alpha_{0}}.

Proof.

The asymptotic excess error can be expanded as

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\ n\left[\mathbb{E}\{\rm{err}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\beta})\}-\rm{err}(\bm{\beta};\bm{\beta})\right]

\displaystyle={\rm{trace}}\{\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})\bm{I}^{-1}(\bm{\beta})\},

where
\bm{I}(\bm{\beta})
is the information matrix for a single observation, and

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\nabla^{\mathsf{T}}{\rm{err}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\beta})\right]_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\bm{\beta}}.

The ARE can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\rm{ARE}

\displaystyle=\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\ \dfrac{\mathbb{E}\{{\rm{err}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{G};\bm{\beta})\}-\rm{err}(\bm{\beta};\bm{\beta})}{\mathbb{E}\{{\rm{err}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{M};\bm{\beta})\}-\rm{err}(\bm{\beta};\bm{\beta})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{{\rm{trace}}(\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})\bm{I}_{LR}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}))}{{\rm{trace}}(\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})\bm{I}_{M}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}))}.

Using Theorem
1
and substituting
\bm{I}_{M}=\tfrac{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{m+\alpha_{0}}\bm{I}_{LR}
,

\displaystyle\rm{ARE}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{{\rm{trace}}(\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})\bm{I}_{LR}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}))}{\tfrac{m+\alpha_{0}}{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{\rm{trace}}(\bm{J}(\bm{\beta})\bm{I}_{LR}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}))}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m(1+\alpha_{0})}{m+\alpha_{0}}.

∎

Remark 1
.

The limiting value of the Godambe information as
m\to\infty
is

\lim_{m\to\infty}\bm{I}_{M}=(1+\alpha_{0})\bm{I}_{LR}.

The limiting value of the ARE as
m\to\infty
is
(1+\alpha_{0})
. The parameter
\alpha_{0}
gives an upper bound on efficiency gain that can be obtained given imperfect manual labels. Under the Dirichlet-Multinomial model, the information supplied by the
m
sets of noisy labels is bounded below by
\bm{I}_{LR}
as

\lim_{\alpha_{0}\to 0^{+}}\bm{I}_{M}=\bm{I}_{LR}.

Consequently the ARE is bounded below by one.
∎

Previous evidence for the benefits of incorporating multiple noisy labels in supervised learning has largely been empirical
(
Sheng et al. 2008
;
Natarajan et al. 2013
;
Raykar et al. 2009
;
Yan et al. 2010
)
, Theorems
1
and
2
give important supporting theory on the robustness and effectiveness of noisy manual labels.

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Dirichlet-Multinomial}(m,\alpha_{0}\bm{\tau}(\bm{y}_{j};{\bm{\beta}}))\quad(j=1,\ldots,n),

(11)

is a function of the true value of
\bm{\beta}

6
Conclusion

Label noise is a practical consideration in supervised learning that can have interesting statistical effects
(
McLachlan 1972
;
Frénay and Verleysen 2014
;
Cannings et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2020
)
. A consequence of Bayes’ theorem is that noisy labels sampled according to the posterior probabilities of class membership supply as much information as the ground-truth labels. Based on this observation we proposed a random effects model for manual labelling that takes into account that the experts do not have perfect knowledge of the posterior probabilities of class membership. Under the proposed model, the sandwich information matrix associated with the use of the noisy manual labels is a multiple of the Fisher information matrix associated with the use of the ground-truth labels. We derived the asymptotic relative efficiency of logistic regression using the noisy manual labels compared to logistic regression using the ground-truth labels, and found that the classifier trained using multiple sets of noisy manual labels can outperform the classifier trained using the single set of ground-truth labels.

A limitation of the Dirichlet-Multinomial model is that the information in the noisy labels
\bm{I}_{M}
is bounded below by
\bm{I}_{LR}
. The model is not flexible enough to allow for the
m
sets of noisy labels to be less informative than the single set of ground-truth labels. The Dirichlet distribution is conjugate to the Multinomial, and was viewed as a natural choice for the random effect to capture imperfect labelling that is mathematically tractable. Extensions to this work could develop richer models that allow for
\bm{I}_{M}
to be less than
\bm{I}_{LR}
with very noisy labels. There is empirical evidence that multiple noisy labels can be less informative than the single set of ground-truth labels
(
Sheng et al. 2008
)
.

A second area where improvements could be made is allowing for correlated labelling errors. In the present model, the manual labels are drawn independently for each instance in the training set. It is plausible that labelling errors will be correlated for similar instances in the training set. Correlated labelling errors could be modelled by allowing for correlated random effects in the Dirichlet-Multinomial model. Copulas could be used to introduce the correlation. However, we expect that derivations of the Godambe information and the asymptotic relative efficiency as in Section
3
will become more challenging to calculate.

Modelling manual annotation by a group of experts is useful as it can allow for more information to be extracted than by simply taking the majority vote
(
Raykar et al. 2009
;
Yan et al. 2010
;
Song et al. 2020
)
. When group disagreement is related to classification difficulty, it is possible to use this association to increase the accuracy of a classifier.
