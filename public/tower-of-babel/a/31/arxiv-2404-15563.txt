Highly Squeezed States in Ring Resonators: Beyond the Undepleted Pump Approximation
Colin Vendromin, Yan Liu, Zhenshan Yang, J. E. Sipe

Abstract
We present a multimode theory of squeezed state generation in resonant systems valid for arbitrary pump power and including pump depletion. The Hamiltonian is written in terms of asymptotic-in and -out fields from scattering theory, capable of describing a general interaction. As an example we consider the lossy generation of a highly squeezed state by an effective second-order interaction in a silicon nitride ring resonator point-coupled to a waveguide. We calculate the photon number, Schmidt number, and the second-order correlation function of the generated state in the waveguide. The treatment we present provides a path forward to study the deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states in resonant systems.

I
Introduction

Continuous-variable (CV) squeezed states of light exhibit an uncertainty below vacuum noise in one quadrature
[
1
]
making them an essential resource for implementing CV quantum information processing
[
2
]
, and with post-selection strategies they can be used to create
non-Gaussian states of light
[
3
,
4
]
, an important resource for quantum computing.

Squeezed states are typically generated through nonlinear optical processes such as spontaneous parametric down conversion (SPDC) and spontaneous four-wave mixing (SFWM), the first relying on the presence of a
\chi^{(2)}
susceptibility, and the second on the
\chi^{(3)}
susceptibility
[
5
,
6
]
. In the usual treatment of these processes, the pump field or fields are treated classically, with the nonlinear process being considered sufficiently weak that those fields can be treated as undepleted.
In this case the resulting squeezed state can be approximately written as
[
7
]

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\exp(\frac{\beta}{2} \int dk_1 k_2 \phi(k_1, k_2) a^\dagger_{k_1}a^\dagger_{k_2} -{\rm H.c.})\ket{vac},

(1)

where
\beta
is an overall squeezing parameter, and
\phi(k_{1},k_{2})
is the normalized biphoton wavefunction that describes the production of pairs of photons; one with wavenumber
k_{1}
and the other
k_{2}
. The ket in Eq. (
1
) is equivalent to a lowest-order Magnus expansion
[
8
]
that treats the pump as classical and undepleted. If the squeezing parameter
\beta
is sufficiently weak, then time-ordering corrections that arise in the higher-order terms of the Magnus expansion are expected to be negligible. However, time-ordering effects become important when the amount of squeezing increases
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, and the approximate ket in Eq. (
1
) then becomes a poorer and poorer approximation.

Including time-ordering corrections, the ket can obtained
under the assumption that the pump is classical and undepleted, and that the generated light is Gaussian. As a result, the full Gaussian ket can be written generally as a squeezed vacuum state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\exp(\frac{1}{2}\int dk_1 dk_2 J(k_1,k_2)a^\dagger_{k_1}a^\dagger_{k_2} - {\rm H.c.})\ket{vac},

(2)

where the squeezing matrix
J(k_{1},k_{2})
can be obtained from the Heisenberg equations of motion
[
10
]
. If time-ordering corrections are negligible this ket reduces to Eq. (
1
), with the squeezing matrix just being proportional to the biphoton wavefuction
J(k_{1},k_{2})\approx\beta\phi(k_{1},k_{2})
.

However, if the nonlinear process is strong enough that pump depletion needs to be considered, the generated light can be expected to be non-Gaussian due to entanglement between the pump and the generated fields
[
12
,
13
]
. In this case a squeezed vacuum state such as in Eq. (
2
) does not provide a complete description of the features of the generated light, such as pump depletion. To go beyond the undepleted pump approximation one strategy that has been employed is to replace the vacuum state in Eq. (
2
) with a non-Gaussian ket, which starts from vacuum initially and evolves such that it remains close to the vacuum. This treatment was recently investigated in waveguides
[
12
]
and for a single-mode SPDC Hamiltonian
[
13
]
, and offers a potential route to the deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states of light. In fact experiments have already demonstrated high conversion efficiencies where the undepleted pump approximation is no longer valid
[
14
,
15
]
, and non-Gaussianity may become important.

In this paper we extend such a treatment to resonant systems.
As an example we study a ring resonator, a system of interest in its own right due to its resonant enhancement of nonlinear processes and the precise control over the frequencies involved, as well as its integrability on photonic chips
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. We develop a multimode Hamiltonian theory of non-Gaussian state generation in such systems, including scattering loss, by employing
the asymptotic-in and -out fields used in scattering theory
[
19
]
.

We apply the theory to the particular case of generating a highly squeezed state by an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction in a ring resonator
[
20
,
15
]
. For this interaction one relies on the
\chi^{(3)}
response of the medium, but there is one pump field in a mode
C
that is continuous wave (CW) and so strong that it can be treated as classical and undepleted; in the presence of the material
\chi^{(3)}
this leads to an effective
\chi^{(2)}
through which a much weaker pump in the mode
P
can generate two photons in the mode
S
(see Fig.
1
(a)). There is no issue with phase-matching, since the pumps and the generated photons are close in wavelength. The formalism we present can also be applied to a standard
\chi^{(2)}
interaction as well.

Although the theory we present is capable of studying non-Gaussian states, our focus in this paper is on highly squeezed states that include the effects of pump depletion. The treatment of the non-Gaussian corrections to the squeezed state will be handled in a subsequent communication, as will the effects of self- and cross-phase modulation, and the excitation of fields in additional ring resonances. While in this paper we specialize to ring resonator systems, most of the formalism can be applied to other systems as well, such as waveguides.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we write
the system Hamiltonian, including the SFWM interaction in a ring resonator, in terms of the asymptotic-in and -out fields for our system. In Sec.
III
we introduce the input and output kets that are used to describe the generation of light in the ring resonator system. In Sec.
IV
we derive the non-Gaussian unitary operator that connects the input ket to the output ket. In Sec.
V
we show how to obtain the first-order solution for the ket. In Sec.
VI
we numerically study the generation of a highly squeezed state by an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction in a ring resonator with loss. In Sec.
VII
we conclude.

II
The Hamiltonian

Our system of interest is a ring resonator point-coupled to an actual waveguide and a phantom waveguide (see Fig.
1
(b)), where the phantom waveguide is used to describe scattering losses
[
21
]
. We define the “input and output channels” of the actual waveguide (shown in red) as the parts of that
waveguide that are to the left and to the right of the coupling point, respectively; likewise, the input and output channels of the phantom waveguide (shown in white) are the parts of that waveguide that are
to the right and to the left of the coupling point, respectively.

Figure 1
:
(a) SFWM with a weak pump at
\omega_{P}
and a strong pump at
\omega_{C}
. (b) Schematic of a ring resonator point-coupled to an actual waveguide (bottom red) and phantom waveguide (top white). (c) Asymptotic-in field associated with the input channel of the actual waveguide (left), and asymptotic-out fields associated with the output channel of the actual waveguide (center) and phantom waveguide (right), respectively
[
19
,
21
]
.

As a basis for expanding the electromagnetic field in the full system of waveguides and ring, we employ the asymptotic-in and -out mode fields
[
19
,
21
]
; they are sketched in Fig.
1
(c), and are eigenstates of the full linear Hamiltonian of the system. Each asymptotic-in or -out mode field has an amplitude in both waveguides and in the ring. In each diagram, the black arrow corresponds to a freely propagating field that is either incoming (asymptotic-in) or outgoing (asymptotic-out), with its amplitude equal to that of a mode field in an isolated waveguide. The white arrows indicate either outgoing (asymptotic-in) or incoming (asymptotic-out) fields in the other channels, and their amplitudes depend on the coupling parameters between the ring and the waveguides
[
21
]
. Asymptotic-in mode fields are also defined for the phantom channel, but they will not appear in our calculations.

The asymptotic-in and -out mode fields can be identified by three parameters. We use a discrete index
n
to indicate the actual output channel or the phantom output channel, the waveguide wavenumbers

k
, and an index
J
that identifies the range of wavenumbers
k
in the
waveguide that correspond to a range of frequencies near a ring resonance,
where
J=P,\,S,\,C
. We assume the ring resonances have
sufficiently high quality factors that the ranges over which
k
varies for each
J
can be considered distinct; we leave this implicit in the integrals below.

For the system considered here, the displacement field operator
can be written in terms of the asymptotic-in mode fields as

[
19
]

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,J}\int dkb_{nJk}\bm{D}^{\text{{in}}}_{nJk}(\bm{r})+{\rm H.c.},

(3)

where
b_{nJk}
is the operator that annihilates an asymptotic-in photon associated with input channel
n
, with wavenumber
k
in the ring resonance
J
, and
\bm{D}^{\text{{in}}}_{nJk}(\bm{r})
is the mode field associated with that photon (see the left sketch in Fig.
1
(c) for the asymptotic-in mode field associated with the actual input channel). Similarly, the displacement field operator can be written in terms of the asymptotic-out mode fields as

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,J}\int dka_{nJk}\bm{D}^{\text{{out}}}_{nJk}(\bm{r})+{\rm H.c.},

(4)

where
a_{nJk}
is the operator that annihilates an asymptotic-out photon associated with output channel
n
, with wavenumber
k
in the ring resonance
J
, and
\bm{D}^{\text{{out}}}_{nJk}(\bm{r})
is the mode field associated with that photon (see the middle or right sketch in Fig.
1
(c)). The input and output operators satisfy the commutation relations

\displaystyle[a_{nJk},a^{\dagger}_{nJk^{\prime}}]=[b_{nJk},b^{\dagger}_{nJk^{\prime}}]=\delta(k-k^{\prime}),

(5)

with all others zero; the linear Hamiltonian can be written in terms of the input or output operators as

\displaystyle H_{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,J}\int dk\hbar\omega_{nJk}b^{\dagger}_{nJk}b_{nJk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,J}\int dk\hbar\omega_{nJk}a^{\dagger}_{nJk}a_{nJk},

(6)

where
\omega_{nJk}
is the dispersion relation for the waveguide containing channel
n
, ranging only over wavenumbers
k
near ring resonance
J
; see Appendix
A
for details.

We now include a third-order nonlinear interaction described by
the Hamiltonian
[
22
]

\displaystyle H_{\textit{NL}}=-\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{0}}\int d\bm{r}\Gamma^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})D^{i}(\bm{r})D^{j}(\bm{r})D^{k}(\bm{r})D^{l}(\bm{r}),

(7)

where
i
,
j
,
k
, and
l
are Cartesian components, as usual summed over when repeated, and the tensor
\Gamma^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})
is related to the more familiar third-order tensor
\chi^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})
by
[
22
]

\displaystyle\Gamma^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})=\frac{\chi^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})}{\epsilon^{2}_{0}\epsilon^{4}(\bm{r})},

(8)

where we have assumed that the relative dielectric function
\epsilon(\bm{r})
does not depend on frequency over the range of frequencies within each ring resonance, and have neglected any dependence of
\chi_{3}^{ijlk}(\bm{r})
over the frequencies of interest.

Our process of interest is SFWM, where one
pump photon in resonance
P
and one in resonance
C
are annihilated, and two photons in
resonance
S
are generated (see Fig.
1
(a)). The asymptotic-out fields for the actual and phantom waveguides are used for the generated photons, while only the asymptotic-in mode field for the actual waveguide is used for the pump photons; hence we can drop the index
n
on the asymptotic-in mode fields and operators, understanding the actual input channel to be identified. If we consider only the terms responsible for SFWM in Eq. (
7
), the nonlinear Hamiltonian becomes
[
21
]

\displaystyle H_{NL}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\int dk_{1}dk_{2}dk_{3}dk_{4}K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})

\displaystyle\times a^{\dagger}_{nSk_{1}}a^{\dagger}_{n^{\prime}Sk_{2}}b_{Pk_{3}}b_{Ck_{4}}+{\rm H.c.},

(9)

where the nonlinear coefficient for SFWM is given by

\displaystyle K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{r}\Gamma^{ijkl}_{3}(\bm{r})\left[D^{\text{{out}},i}_{nSk_{1}}(\bm{r})\right]^{*}

\displaystyle\times\left[D^{\text{{out}},j}_{n^{\prime}Sk_{2}}(\bm{r})\right]^{*}D^{\text{{in}},k}_{Pk_{3}}(\bm{r})D^{\text{{in}},l}_{Ck_{4}}(\bm{r}).

(10)

We take the integral in Eq. (
10
) to range over the ring where the fields are concentrated, and the effect of the nonlinearity is most significant.
The sums over
n
and
n^{\prime}
, which range over the actual
and phantom output channels,
take into account the four combinations of two photons exiting the ring through two output channels. That is, both photons can leave through the phantom or actual output channel, or one through each of the channels.
The nonlinear coefficient
K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
gives the strength of the interaction corresponding to each combination. It is symmetric with respect to exchanging
the order of both
n
and
n^{\prime}
and
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
; that is,

\displaystyle K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=K^{n^{\prime}n}(k_{2},k_{1},k_{3},k_{4}).

(11)

But it is not symmetric with respect to only exchanging the order of
n
and
n^{\prime}
or
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
; that is,

\displaystyle K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\neq K^{n^{\prime}n}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}).

(12)

III
Input and output kets

The unitary time-evolution operator for the system,
\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})
, is a solution to the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime})}{dt}=H\mathcal{U}(t,t^{\prime}),

(13)

satisfying
\mathcal{U}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})=I
for all
t^{\prime}
, where
I
is the identity operator.
We write the full Hamiltonian
H
as the sum of the linear and nonlinear contributions,

\displaystyle H=H_{L}+H_{NL},

(14)

where

H_{L}
is given by Eq. (
6
) and
H_{NL}
is given by Eq. (
9
). We assume that times
t_{on}
and
t_{\textit{off}}
can be identified such that: (a) For
t<t_{on}
the excitations of the quantum fields incident on the ring are sufficiently far from it that
H_{NL}
has no effect on the evolution of the ket, and that (b) for
t>t_{\textit{off}}
all the field excitation has propagated sufficiently far from the ring that, again,
H_{NL}
has no effect on the evolution of the ket.

Consider then the evolution of a ket from some early time
t_{0}<t_{\textit{on}}
to some later time
t_{1}>t_{\textit{off}}
. The evolution operator can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{0})=\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{\textit{off}})\mathcal{U}(t_{\textit{off}},t_{\textit{on}})\mathcal{U}(t_{\textit{on}},t_{0}).

(15)

The evolution operator from
t_{0}
to
t_{\textit{on}}
and
t_{\textit{off}}
to
t_{1}
only involves the linear Hamiltonian, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t_{\textit{on}},t_{0})

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-iH_{L}(t_{\textit{on}}-t_{0})/\hbar},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{\textit{off}})

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-iH_{L}(t_{1}-t_{\textit{off}})/\hbar}.

(17)

We can then write Eq. (
15
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{0})={\rm e}^{-iH_{L}(t_{1}-t_{\textit{off}})/\hbar}\mathcal{U}(t_{\textit{off}},t_{\textit{on}}){\rm e}^{-iH_{L}(t_{\textit{on}}-t_{0})/\hbar}.

(18)

We now suppose that an initial ket
\ket{\psi(t_0)}
is specified at a time
t_{0}<t_{\textit{on}}
. We define the “input ket”

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{in}}\equiv{\rm e}^{-iH_{L}(0-t_{0})/\hbar}\ket{\psi(t_0)},

(19)

which is the ket that the initial ket would evolve to at
t=0
were
H_{NL}=0
. And starting with the ket
\ket{\psi(t_1)}
characterizing the state at a time
t_{1}>t_{\textit{off}}
, we define the “output ket”

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{out}}\equiv{\rm e}^{iH_{L}(t_{1}-0)/\hbar}\ket{\psi(t_1)},

(20)

which is the ket at
t=0
that would evolve to

\ket{\psi(t_1)}
at
t=t_{1}
were
H_{NL}=0
. Since the evolution of the ket from from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t_1)}=\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{0})\ket{\psi(t_0)},

(21)

in terms of the input and output kets
this can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{out}}=U(t_{1},t_{0})\ket{\psi_{in}},

(22)

where we defined

\displaystyle U(t_{1},t_{0})\equiv{\rm e}^{iH_{L}t_{1}/\hbar}\mathcal{U}(t_{1},t_{0}){\rm e}^{-iH_{L}t_{0}/\hbar}.

(23)

Then taking the limit

\displaystyle U(\infty,-\infty)=\lim_{t_{1}\rightarrow\infty,t_{0}\rightarrow-\infty}U(t_{1},t_{0}),

(24)

we obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{out}}=U(\infty,-\infty)\ket{\psi_{in}}.

(25)

It is then convenient to define

\displaystyle\overline{U}(t)\equiv U(t,-\infty),

(26)

for all times
t
. We can then write

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{out}}=|\overline{\psi}(\infty)\rangle,

(27)

where

\displaystyle|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{U}(t)\ket{\psi_{in}},

(28)

and

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{in}}=|\overline{\psi}(-\infty)\rangle.

(29)

Taking the derivative of Eq. (
28
) and using Eqs. (
13
), (
23
), and (
26
) we obtain

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle=\overline{H}_{NL}(t)|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(30)

where we have defined the interaction picture nonlinear Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{NL}(t)\equiv{\rm e}^{iH_{L}t/\hbar}H_{NL}{\rm e}^{-iH_{L}t/\hbar},

(31)

and as
t
ranges from
-\infty
to
\infty
in Eq. (
30
) the ket evolves from
\ket{\psi_{in}}
to
\ket{\psi_{out}}
.

IV
Unitary evolution

In Appendix A we simplify the nonlinear Hamiltonian Eq. (
31
) by treating the strong CW pump classically, and discretize the
k
involved for the weak pump and generated fields to implement numerical calculations. The result can be written as

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{NL}(t)

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{l}\Lambda_{\mu\nu l}(t)a^{\dagger}_{\mu}a^{\dagger}_{\nu}b_{l}+{\rm H.c.},

(32)

where for convenience we removed the discrete labels
P
and
S
on the input and output operators, and they satisfy the usual commutation relations
[b_{l},b^{\dagger}_{l^{\prime}}]=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}
and
[a_{\mu},a^{\dagger}_{\nu}]=\delta_{\mu\nu}
. Here
\Lambda_{\mu\nu l}(t)
is the nonlinear coefficient for the effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction, where
l
labels a discrete wavenumber
k_{l}
for the pump, and
\mu
and
\nu
are discrete indices for the generated photons (see Eq. (
83
)). Each Greek index
\mu
, for example, refers to two properties of the photon, its discrete wavenumber
k_{i}
and the output channel
n
. The sum over
\mu
in Eq. (
32
) represents two sums; one over the actual and phantom output channel and the second over the discrete wavenumbers
k_{i}
.

We now turn to the unitary evolution in Eq. (
30
). Here we do not consider a general ket
\ket{\psi_{in}}
, but restrict ourselves to
an input ket that is a coherent state in the actual input channel and vacuum in all other channels,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{in}}

\displaystyle=D_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{in})\ket{vac},

(33)

where
D_{b}(\bm{\beta}(t))
is the unitary displacement operator, with displacement parameter
\bm{\beta}_{in}\equiv\bm{\beta}(-\infty)
. It is written in terms of the input operators as

\displaystyle D_{b}(\bm{\beta}(t))=\exp([\bm\beta(t)]^{\rm T}\bm b^\dagger- [\bm\beta^*(t)]^{\rm T}\bm b),

(34)

where
\bm{\beta}(t)=[\beta_{1}(t),\beta_{2}(t),\ldots]^{\rm T}
and
\bm{\beta}^{*}(t)=[\beta^{*}_{1}(t),\beta^{*}_{2}(t),\ldots]^{\rm T}
are column vectors of displacement parameters, and
\bm{b}=[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots]^{\rm T}
and
\bm{b}^{\dagger}=[b^{\dagger}_{1},b^{\dagger}_{2},\ldots]^{\rm T}
are column vectors of input operators. We then seek a solution of (
30
)
of the form

\displaystyle|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle=U_{a}(t)U_{b}(t)|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(35)

where
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
describes the effect of a non-Gaussian unitary operator acting on the vacuum state, as discussed below, and

U_{a}(t)
and
U_{b}(t)
are Gaussian unitary operators, given by

\displaystyle U_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t)){\rm e}^{i\theta_{a}(t)},

(36)

\displaystyle U_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=D_{b}(\bm{\beta}(t)){\rm e}^{i\theta_{b}(t)},

(37)

where
\theta_{b}(t)
and
\theta_{a}(t)
are real functions of time,
S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))
is a multimode squeezing operator, and
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
is a multimode rotation operator.

The unitary squeezing operator
S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))
is given by
[
23
]

\displaystyle S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))

\displaystyle=\exp(\frac{1}{2} {\bm a^\dagger}^{\rm T}\bm J(t) {\bm a^\dagger} - {\rm H.c.}),

(38)

where
\bm{a}=[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]^{\rm T}
and
\bm{a}^{\dagger}=[a^{\dagger}_{1},a^{\dagger}_{2},\ldots]^{\rm T}
are column vectors of output operators and
\bm{J}(t)
is the symmetric time-dependent squeezing matrix, with
\bm{J}(t)=\bm{J}^{\rm T}(t)
. The unitary rotation operator
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
is given by
[
23
]

\displaystyle R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))

\displaystyle=\exp(i {\bm a^\dagger}^{\rm T}\bm\phi(t) \bm a),

(39)

where
\bm{\phi}(t)
is
the Hermitian time-dependent rotation matrix,
with
\bm{\phi}(t)=\bm{\phi}^{\dagger}(t)
. The parameters
\bm{\beta}(t)
,
\bm{J}(t)
, and
\bm{\phi}(t)
are subject to the following initial conditions
\bm{\beta}(-\infty)=\bm{\beta}_{in}
,
\bm{J}(-\infty)=0
, and
\bm{\phi}(-\infty)=0
, and also
\theta_{b}(-\infty)=\theta_{a}(-\infty)=0
; further, we
take

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}(-\infty)\rangle=|vac\rangle.

(40)

This guarantees that the initial condition (
29
) is satisfied.

Returning to Eq. (
35
), our goal is to define the parameters in the Gaussian unitary operators in Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
37
) so that the ket
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
isolates the non-Gaussian behavior of the full ket in Eq. (
35
). Now regardless of how the parameters in those Gaussian unitary operators are chosen to depend on time, we find that for Eq. (
35
) to satisfy the full Schrödinger equation in Eq. (
30
) we require

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle=H_{\textit{eff}}(t)|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(41)

where we have introduced the effective Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\textit{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=H_{I}(t)+U^{\dagger}_{a}(t)H_{a}(t)U_{a}(t)-i\hbar U^{\dagger}_{a}(t)\frac{dU_{a}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle+U^{\dagger}_{b}(t)H_{b}(t)U_{b}(t)-i\hbar U^{\dagger}_{b}(t)\frac{dU_{b}(t)}{dt},

(42)

with

\displaystyle H_{a}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\hbar\Lambda_{\mu\nu l}(t)[\bm{\beta}(t)]_{l}a^{\dagger}_{\mu}a^{\dagger}_{\nu}+{\rm H.c.},

(43)

\displaystyle H_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\hbar\Lambda^{*}_{\mu\nu l}(t)[\bm{V}(t)\bm{W}^{\rm T}(t)]_{\mu\nu}\left(b^{\dagger}_{l}-[\bm{\beta}^{*}(t)]_{l}\right)+{\rm H.c.},

(44)

\displaystyle H_{I}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\hbar\Lambda^{*}_{\mu\nu l}(t)\bigg([\bm{V}(t)]_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}[\bm{V}(t)]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}a_{\mu^{\prime}}a_{\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\left([\bm{V}(t)]_{\mu\nu^{\prime}}[\bm{W}(t)]_{\nu\mu^{\prime}}+[\bm{W}(t)]_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}[\bm{V}(t)]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\right)a^{\dagger}_{\mu^{\prime}}a_{\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+[\bm{W}(t)]_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}[\bm{W}(t)]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{\mu^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{\nu^{\prime}}\bigg)b^{\dagger}_{l}+{\rm H.c.}.

(45)

Here repeated indices are summed over, and the matrices
\bm{V}(t)
and
\bm{W}(t)
are defined in terms of the parameters appearing in the Gaussian unitary operators in Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
37
); see Appendix B for details.

The Hamiltonians appearing above each capture part of the physics:

H_{a}(t)
describes the generation of photon pairs by an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction modulated by a time-dependent pump amplitude
\bm{\beta}(t)
;

H_{b}(t)
determines the rate of depletion of the pump amplitude
\bm{\beta}(t)
, which is being driven by the generation of photon pairs;

H_{I}(t)
, which only contains non-Gaussian terms, describes the quantum correlations between the pump and the generated light.

To achieve our goal of moving all the non-Gaussian behavior to
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
, we must choose the time dependence of the parameters appearing in the Gaussian operators in Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
37
) so that

\displaystyle H_{\textit{eff}}(t)=H_{I}(t).

(46)

It is easy to show that this condition is satisfied if we have

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{dU_{a}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=H_{a}(t)U_{a}(t),

(47)

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{dU_{b}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=H_{b}(t)U_{b}(t).

(48)

We show in Appendix
B
that this can be achieved by
appropriately choosing the parameters
\bm{V}(t)
,
\bm{W}(t)
,
\bm{\beta}(t)
, and
\theta_{b}(t)
so that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{V}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-2i\bm{\zeta}(t)\bm{W}^{*}(t),

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-2i\bm{\zeta}(t)\bm{V}^{*}(t),

(50)

\displaystyle i\frac{d[{\bm{\beta}}(t)]_{l}}{dt}

\displaystyle=[\bm{\gamma}(t)]_{l},

(51)

\displaystyle\theta_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}[\bm{\gamma}(t^{\prime})]_{l}[{\bm{\beta}}^{*}(t^{\prime})]_{l}+{\rm c.c.},

(52)

are satisfied, where the matrix
\bm{\zeta}(t)
drives the production of photon pairs in the squeezed state,

\displaystyle[\bm{\zeta}(t)]_{\mu\nu}=\Lambda_{\mu\nu l}(t)[\bm{\beta}(t)]_{l},

(53)

and the vector
\bm{\gamma}(t)
determines the rate of pump depletion,

\displaystyle[\bm{\gamma}(t)]_{l}=\Lambda^{*}_{\mu\nu l}(t)[\bm{V}(t)\bm{W}^{\rm T}(t)]_{\mu\nu}.

(54)

We solve the Eqs. (
49
), (
50
), and (
51
) numerically for the parameters
\bm{V}(t)
,
\bm{W}(t)
, and
\bm{\beta}(t)
, which are subject to the initial conditions
\bm{\beta}(-\infty)=\bm{\beta}_{in}
,
\bm{W}(-\infty)=0
, and
\bm{V}(-\infty)=\bm{I}
, where
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix. The solutions are put into Eq. (
52
), which is then integrated to obtain the phase
\theta_{b}(t)
. In Appendix
C
we show how to extract the squeezing matrix
\bm{J}(t)
and rotation matrix
\bm{\phi}(t)
from the solutions
\bm{V}(t)
and
\bm{W}(t)
, and in Appendix
D
we give an equation for the phase
\theta_{a}(t)
in Eq. (
36
). This procedure provides a solution for the Gaussian parameters of the unitary operators in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), and is valid for high pump power and nonlinear interaction strength.

Still necessary for a full solution, of course, is to include the non-Gaussian evolution of
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
. Then the full solution can be written as the squeezed, displaced, and rotated version of non-Gaussian terms acting only on the vacuum state,

\displaystyle|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle=U_{a}(t)U_{b}(t)|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(55)

where recall Eq. (
40
). An attractive feature of this approach is that we can work in the Gaussian limit by setting

|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle\rightarrow|vac\rangle
, and still study the effects of pump depletion and the dynamics of the joint spectral amplitude in squeezed state generation.

That is what we do for
the remainder of this
paper. We put

\displaystyle|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle\rightarrow U_{a}(t)U_{b}(t)|vac\rangle\equiv|\overline{\psi}_{G}(t)\rangle,

(56)

and we have

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}={\rm e}^{i\theta_{a}(t)}S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))\ket{vac}\otimes{\rm e}^{i\theta_{b}(t)}\ket{\bm\beta(t)}.

(57)

where
\ket{\bm\beta(t)}=D_{b}(\bm{\beta}(t))\ket{vac}
is a coherent state.
The Gaussian state is a product of a squeezed vacuum state written in terms of the output operators and a coherent state written in terms of the input operators, and from (
56
) its dynamics is governed by

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}}{dt}=\left(H_{a}(t)+H_{b}(t)\right)\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}.

(58)

This of course is an approximate result, and there are consequences. For example,
the operator
Q={\bm{a}^{\dagger}}^{\rm T}\bm{a}/2+{\bm{b}^{\dagger}}^{\rm T}\bm{b}
commutes with
H_{L}
and
H_{NL}
, and therefore with the full Hamiltonian in Eq. (
14
), and so it is a conserved quantity.
It therefore also commutes with
\overline{H}_{NL}(t)
, which governs the evolution of the full
|\overline{\psi}(t)\rangle
according to Eq. (
30
).
However, it does
not
commute with the Hamiltonian
H_{a}(t)+H_{b}(t)
that governs the evolution of the its approximation
|\overline{\psi}_{G}(t)\rangle.
Nonetheless, in Appendix
E
we show that
\langle\overline{\psi}_{G}(t)|Q|\overline{\psi}_{G}(t)\rangle

is
constant as
\ket{\overline\psi_G(t)}
evolves according to Eq. (
58
).

V
First-order solution

If the energy of the pump is sufficiently low or the nonlinear coefficients
\Lambda_{\mu\nu l}(t)
are sufficiently weak, then the elements of the matrix
\bm{\zeta}(t)
in Eq. (
53
) will be much less than unity,

[\bm{\zeta}(t)]_{\mu\nu}\ll 1
for all
\mu
and
\nu
. In this case the solution to Eq. (
49
) and Eq. (
50
) can be approximately written as
[
10
]

\displaystyle\bm{V}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\bm{I},

(59)

\displaystyle\bm{W}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-2i\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\bm{\zeta}(t^{\prime}).

(60)

Consequently, the pump depletion rate in Eq. (
51
) is negligible

\displaystyle\frac{d[{\bm{\beta}}(t)]_{l}}{dt}

\displaystyle\approx-2i\Lambda^{*}_{\mu\nu l}(t)\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}[\bm{\zeta}(t^{\prime})]_{\mu\nu}\approx 0,

(61)

since it depends on the product of the
nonlinear coefficients, which are assumed to be small. The Hamiltonian
H_{b}(t)
in Eq. (
44
) can be set to zero,

H_{b}(t)\rightarrow 0
, since the coefficients in
H_{b}(t)
are given by
d[{\bm{\beta}}(t)]_{l}/dt
. Similarly the phase
\theta_{b}(t)
in Eq. (
52
) is negligible,
\theta_{b}(t)\approx 0
.

With these approximations, the Gaussian ket
|\overline{\psi}_{G}(t)\rangle
in Eq. (
57
) can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}=S_{a}(\bm{J}(t))\ket{vac}\otimes\ket{\bm\beta_{in}},

(62)

which satisfies the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}}{dt}=H_{a}(t)\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}.

(63)

Treating
H_{a}(t)
as a small quantity we can construct a first-order solution to Eq. (
63
) using the Magnus expansion
[
8
]
, and keeping only the first term we obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\overline{\psi}_G(t)}\approx\exp(-i\int_{-\infty}^tdt' [\bm\zeta(t')]_{\mu\nu} a^\dagger_\mu a^\dagger_\nu- {\rm H.c.})\otimes\ket{\bm\beta_{in}}.

(64)

The higher-order terms in the expansion involve the commutator between the Hamiltonian at two different times, such as
[H_{a}(t),H_{a}(t^{\prime})]

[
8
]
. The ket in Eq. (
64
) is the solution when we assume that
[H_{a}(t),H_{a}(t^{\prime})]=0
so that time-ordering effects are negligible. It was pointed out earlier for waveguides
[
12
,
10
]
that this assumption becomes invalid when the power of the pump is increased. We demonstrate
below that this also holds for a squeezed state generated in a ring resonator. Comparing Eq. (
64
) and Eq. (
62
) we immediately obtain the approximate first-order solution for the squeezing matrix

\displaystyle\bm{J}(t)\approx-2i\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\bm{\zeta}(t^{\prime}).

(65)

This is the expression for
\bm{W}(t)
in Eq. (
60
), so we can write
\bm{J}(t)\approx\bm{W}(t)
. To obtain the form in Eq. (
62
) we have put
\theta_{a}(t)=0
, which is valid for a weak nonlinearity (see Appendix
D
).

VII
Conclusion

In conclusion, we presented a multimode theory of Gaussian state generation for an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction, in terms of asymptotic-in and -out fields, that includes scattering loss and pump depletion. We showed that the full ket for our system can be written as a Gaussian unitary acting on non-Gaussian
ket that satisfies a Schrödinger equation with an effective Hamiltonian that describes the quantum correlations between the pump and the generated light. In order for this to be valid, we required that the squeezing, rotation, and displacement parameters of the Gaussian unitary were solutions to a set of coupled differential equations. As an example we studied the lossy generation of a highly squeezed state using an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction in a silicon nitride ring resonator, and we compared this full Gaussian solution to a lowest-order Magnus expansion for the ket. In future work, we will study the evolution of the non-Gaussian ket, the effects of self- and cross-phase modulation, and the application to more general resonant systems. This work opens up a path to study the deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states in resonant systems.

Appendix A
Effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction in a ring resonator

In this Appendix we derive the nonlinear Hamiltonian for an effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction in a ring resonator.

We begin with the nonlinear Hamiltonian for SFWM in Eq. (
9
). In the interaction picture it takes the form

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{NL}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\int dk_{1}dk_{2}dk_{3}dk_{4}

\displaystyle\times K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}){\rm e}^{-i\Omega_{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})t}

\displaystyle\times a^{\dagger}_{nSk_{1}}a^{\dagger}_{n^{\prime}Sk_{2}}b_{Pk_{3}}b_{Ck_{4}}+{\rm H.c.},

(70)

where we have used the definition in Eq. (
31

defined

\displaystyle\Omega_{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\equiv\omega_{Ck_{4}}+\omega_{Pk_{3}}-\omega_{n^{\prime}Sk_{2}}-\omega_{nSk_{1}}.

(71)

In order to build the effective
\chi^{(2)}
interaction, the pump in the mode

is treated classically with a cw field that is undepleted. The operators

are replaced with the amplitudes
[

(72)

where
P_{C}
is the cw power and
v_{C}
is the group velocity of the cw pump in the actual input channel.
Under this approximation, Eq. (
70
) becomes

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{NL}(t)

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\int dk_{1}dk_{2}dk_{3}\Lambda^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},t)

\chi^{(2)}

\displaystyle\Lambda^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi P_{C}}{\hbar\omega_{C}v_{C}}}K^{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},K_{C})

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{-i\Omega_{nn^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},K_{C})t}.

(74)

We consider a ring resonator that is point-coupled to an actual waveguide that couples the light out of the ring and a phantom waveguide that handles the scattering loss; recall Fig.
1

Appendix C
Extracting
\bm{J}(t)
and
\bm{\phi}(t)
from
\bm{V}(t)
and
\bm{W}(t)

We begin by doing a numerical polar decomposition of
\bm{V}(t)
. Note that Eq. (
91
) is already in the form of a polar decomposition, where
\cosh(\bm u(t))
is a Hermitian positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix and
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
is a unitary matrix. And since the Hermitian PSD matrix in a polar decomposition is always unique, the diagonal elements of
\cosh(\bm u(t))
, which we identify by the set
\{s_{i}(t)\}
, will be unique. Further, since the matrices
\cosh(\bm u(t))
and
\bm{u}(t)
commute they can be diagonalized by the same unitary transformation. So we can write

\displaystyle\cosh(\bm u(t))=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}(s_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t).

(103)

and

\displaystyle\bm{u}(t)=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}(r_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(104)

where the unitary matrix
\bm{F}(t)
is the same in both decompositions, and the set
\{r_{i}(t)\}
are the diagonal elements of
\bm{u}(t)
. Since Eqs. (
103
) and (
104
) are diagonalizations of PSD Hermitian matrices, we have

\displaystyle s_{i}(t)\geq 0

(105)

and

\displaystyle r_{i}(t)\geq 0.

(106)

But a condition stronger than Eq. (
105
) holds. Writing
Eq. (
104
) as

\displaystyle\bm{u}(t)=\bm{F}(t)\bm{r}(t)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(107)

where
\bm{r}(t)
is a diagonal matrix we have

\displaystyle\cosh(\bm u(t))

\displaystyle=\cosh(\bm F(t) \bm r(t) \bm F^{\dagger}(t))

\displaystyle=\bm{F}(t)\cosh(\bm r(t))\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(108)

or

\displaystyle\cosh(\bm u(t))=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}(\cosh r_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t).

(109)

Comparing with Eq. (
103
) we see that

\displaystyle s_{i}(t)=\cosh(r_i(t)),

(110)

and so, given Eq. (
106
), we have

\displaystyle s_{i}(t)\geq 1.

(111)

A first consequence of this, which follows from using it in Eq. (
103
),
is that
\cosh(\bm u(t))
is
invertible and then so is
\bm{V}(t)
; thus from the numerical
polar decomposition of
\bm{V}(t)

we not only extract a unique Hermitian matrix
\cosh(\bm u(t))
but also a
unique unitary matrix
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
. A second consequence is that from Eq. (
110
) we can write Eq. (
104
) as

\displaystyle\bm{u}(t)=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}({\rm arccosh}(s_{1}(t)),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t)

(112)

where by “
{\rm arccosh}
” we mean the non-negative
{\rm arccosh}
, by virtue of Eq. (
106
).
In summary, then, from the numerical polar decomposition of
\bm{V}(t)
we immediately obtain the unique matrices
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
and
\cosh(\bm u(t))
, from the diagonalization Eq. (
103
) we determine the unitary matrix
\bm{F}(t)
and the
\{s_{i}(t)\}
, and then from Eq. (
112
) we construct
\bm{u}(t)
.

With
\bm{u}(t)
in hand we can then form

\displaystyle\sinh(\bm u(t))

\displaystyle=\sinh(\bm F(t) \bm r(t) \bm F^{\dagger}(t))

\displaystyle=\bm{F}(t)\sinh(\bm r(t))\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(113)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\sinh(\bm u(t))=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}(\sinh(r_1(t)),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(114)

which can be written as

\displaystyle\sinh(\bm u(t))

\displaystyle=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}\left(\frac{\sinh(r_1(t))}{r_{1}(t)},\ldots\right)

\displaystyle\times{\rm diag}(r_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(115)

or

\displaystyle\sinh(\bm u(t))=\bm{K}(t)\bm{u}(t),

(116)

where we have used Eq. (
104
) and let

\displaystyle\bm{K}(t)=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}\left(\frac{\sinh(r_1(t))}{r_{1}(t)},\dots\right)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(117)

with

\displaystyle K_{i}(t)=\frac{\sinh(r_i(t))}{r_{i}(t)}

(118)

Forming
K_{i}(t)
seems problematic if
r_{i}(t)=0
. Of course, in such an instance the natural choice would be to take
K_{i}(t)
to be the limit of Eq. (
118
) as
r_{i}(t)\rightarrow 0
, which would give
K_{i}(t)=1
. But one could worry about issues of uniqueness. In fact, we show below that if
r_{i}(t)=0
we can take
K_{i}(t)
to be any nonzero number, and the final result of the following calculation of
\bm{J}(t)
will be the same.

The matrix
\bm{K}(t)
is clearly invertible, since we can identify

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{-1}(t)=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{K_{1}(t)},\ldots\right)\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t),

(119)

and furthermore we can numerically construct
\bm{K}^{-1}(t)
since we know
\bm{F}(t)
and the
r_{i}(t)
. From Eq. (
116
) we then have

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{-1}(t)\sinh(\bm u(t))=\bm{u}(t),

(120)

and from Eq. (
92
)
we have

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{-1}(t)\bm{W}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{u}(t){\rm e}^{i\bm{\alpha}(t)}{\rm e}^{-i\bm{\phi}^{\rm T}(t)}=\bm{J}(t){\rm e}^{-i\bm{\phi}^{\rm T}(t)},

(121)

so

\displaystyle\bm{J}(t)=\bm{K}^{-1}(t)\bm{W}(t){\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}^{\rm T}(t)}.

(122)

All the quantities on the right-hand-side are known, and so we can determine
\bm{J}(t)
.

To investigate what happens to
\bm{J}(t)
when some of the eigenvalues
r_{i}(t)
are zero, consider

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{-1}(t)\bm{W}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{F}(t){\rm diag}\left(\frac{\sinh(r_1(t))}{K_{1}(t)},\ldots\right)

\displaystyle\times\bm{F}^{\dagger}(t){\rm e}^{i\bm{\alpha}(t)}{\rm e}^{-i\bm{\phi}^{\rm T}(t)},

(123)

where we used Eqs. (
120
) and (
114
).
Now we can see that for any
i
for which
r_{i}(t)=0
we will have
\sinh(r_i(t))/K_{i}(t)=0
, regardless of which nonzero value
K_{i}(t)
is set.
So the eigenvalues
r_{i}(t)
that are zero do not contribute to the matrix
\bm{J}(t)
.

At this point we have identified
\bm{J}(t)
and
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
; we still must extract
\bm{\phi}(t)
. The
\bm{\phi}(t)
appearing in
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
of (
39
) is a Hermitian matrix, and so can be diagonalized,

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(t)=\bm{M}(t){\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t),\dots)\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t),

(124)

where
\bm{M}(t)
is a unitary matrix. Thus we have

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{i\bm{M}(t){\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)}

\displaystyle=\bm{M}(t){\rm e}^{i{\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t),\ldots)}\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t).

(125)

And so numerically diagonalizing the identified
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
would naturally lead us to identify
\bm{\phi}(t)
as given by Eq. (
124
). But although the eigenvalues of
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
are well-defined, the values of the phases appearing in those eigenvalues are not. For we could just as easily write

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}

\displaystyle=\bm{M}(t){\rm e}^{i{\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t)+2\pi m_{1}(t),\ldots)}\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{i\bm{M}(t){\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t)+2\pi m_{1}(t),\ldots)\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)},

(126)

where
m_{1}(t)
, etc., are any integers. So instead of Eq. (
124
) we could just as well claim that
\bm{\phi}(t)

is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{M}(t){\rm diag}(\chi_{1}(t)+2\pi m_{1}(t),...)\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t).

(127)

However, this ambiguity is benign, because both Eq. (
124
) and Eq. (
127
) lead to the same effective
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
.

To see this, note that if we posit Eq. (
127
) we have

\displaystyle[\bm{\phi}(t)]_{kl}=[\bm{M}(t)]_{kp}(\chi_{p}(t)+2\pi m_{p}(t))[\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)]_{pl},

(128)

and so

\displaystyle R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))={\rm e}^{ia^{\dagger}_{k}[\bm{M}(t)]_{kp}(\chi_{p}(t)+2\pi m_{p}(t))[\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)]_{pl}a_{l}}.

(129)

Now put

\displaystyle c_{p}(t)=[\bm{M}^{\dagger}(t)]_{pl}a_{l}=[\bm{M}^{*}(t)]_{lp}a_{l},

(130)

so

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{q}(t)=[\bm{M}(t)]_{jq}a^{\dagger}_{j}.

(131)

Thus

\displaystyle[c_{p}(t),c^{\dagger}_{q}(t)]=\delta_{pq},

(132)

and the new operators
c_{p}(t)
and
c^{\dagger}_{q}(t)
satisfy equal time commutation relations of raising and lowering harmonic oscillator operators. In terms of them, we can write (
129
) as

\displaystyle R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{ic^{\dagger}_{p}(t)(\chi_{p}(t)+2\pi m_{p}(t))c_{p}(t)}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{ic^{\dagger}_{p}(t)\chi_{p}(t)c_{p}(t)}{\rm e}^{2\pi im_{p}(t)c^{\dagger}_{p}(t)c_{p}(t)}.

(133)

As basis kets we can choose the eigenkets
|\{n\},t\rangle
of all the number operators
c^{\dagger}_{q}(t)c_{q}(t)
,
where

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{q}(t)c_{q}(t)|\{n\},t\rangle=n_{q}|\{n\},t\rangle.

(134)

Then

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{2\pi im_{p}(t)c^{\dagger}_{p}(t)c_{p}(t)}|\{n\},t\rangle={\rm e}^{2\pi im_{p}(t)n_{p}}|\{n\},t\rangle=|\{n\},t\rangle,

(135)

and since any ket
|\varphi(t)\rangle
can be written as a superposition of the
|\{n\},t\rangle
, from (
133
) and (
135
) we have

\displaystyle R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))|\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{ic^{\dagger}_{p}(t)(\chi_{p}(t)+2\pi m_{p}(t))c_{p}(t)}|\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{ic^{\dagger}_{p}(t)\chi_{p}(t)c_{p}(t)}|\varphi(t)\rangle.

(136)

Thus the result we find for
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
will be the same whether we use Eq. (
124
) or Eq. (
127
) in identifying the logarithm of
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
to construct
R_{a}(\bm{\phi}(t))
; any logarithm of
{\rm e}^{i\bm{\phi}(t)}
will do.
