Affine equivariant Tyler’s M-estimator applied to tail parameter learning of elliptical distributions
Esa Ollila, Daniel P. Palomar, Frédéric Pascal

Abstract
We propose estimating the scale parameter (mean of the eigenvalues) of the scatter matrix of an unspecified elliptically symmetric distribution using weights obtained by solving Tyler’s M-estimator of the scatter matrix. The proposed Tyler’s weights-based estimate (TWE) of scale is then used to construct an affine equivariant Tyler’s M-estimator as a weighted sample covariance matrix using normalized Tyler’s weights. We then develop a unified framework for estimating the unknown tail parameter of the elliptical distribution (such as the degrees of freedom (d.o.f.) ν \nu of the multivariate t t (MVT) distribution). Using the proposed TWE of scale, a new robust estimate of the d.o.f. parameter of MVT distribution is proposed with excellent performance in heavy-tailed scenarios, outperforming other competing methods. R-package is available that implements the proposed method.



I
Introduction

We model the observed
p
-variate observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random samples from an unspecified centered (i.e., symmetric around the origin) elliptically symmetric (ES) distribution
[
1
,
2
]
. A continuous random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has centered ES distribution if
it possesses a probability density function (pdf) of the form

f(\mathbf{x})=C_{p,g}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}g(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}),

where
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is called the density
generator,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0
is the positive definite symmetric matrix parameter, called the
scatter matrix
, and
C_{p,g}
is a normalizing constant ensuring that
f(\mathbf{x})
integrates to 1. We let
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{E}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},g)
to denote this case. For example, the centered multivariate normal (MVN) distribution
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is obtained when

g(t)=\exp(-t/2)
while the multivariate
t
(MVT) distribution with
\nu>0
degrees of freedom (d.o.f.) is obtained when

g(t)=(1+t/\nu)^{-(p+\nu)/2}.

(1)

Parameter
\nu>0
is a tail parameter of the density.
For
\nu\to\infty
, the MVT distribution reduces to the MVN distribution, while
\nu=1
corresponds to the multivariate Cauchy distribution. Also, many other subclasses of ES distributions are parametrized by a density generator indexed by an additional tail parameter that is unknown in practice and needs to be estimated. Learning this unknown parameter is also one of the goals of this paper.

We are interested in estimating the scale parameter of the scatter matrix, defined as the mean of its eigenvalues,

\eta=\frac{\tr(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}{p}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i},

(2)

where
\lambda_{i}>0
denotes the
i
th eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
Formally,
\eta\equiv\eta(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is a
scale
parameter if it verifies
\eta(\mathbf{I})=1
and
\eta(a\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=a\eta(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for all
a>0

[
3
]
.

Tyler’s M-estimator
[
4
]
is a popular robust M-estimator of the scatter matrix that has been extensively studied both in signal processing and statistics literature (e.g,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
). Tyler’s M-estimator is
defined as the solution to the fixed-point equation

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cfrac{p}{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\triangleq\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}};\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}).

(3)

Note that me way also write the map
\mathcal{H}(\cdot;\cdot)
in the form

\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}};\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\ \mbox{ with }\ \hat{w}_{i}=\cfrac{p}{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}},

where
\hat{w}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n,
are referred to as
Tyler’s weights
.
Tyler’s M-estimator is unique only up to a scaling factor, and therefore a common convention is to consider a solution that verifies
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
. Thus, Tyler’s M-estimator is actually an estimator of a
shape matrix
(normalized scatter matrix)
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, defined by

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/\eta=p\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/\tr(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

and verifying
\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=p
.

In this paper, we propose an estimator of the scale
\eta
based on Tyler’s weights
\hat{w}_{i}
. The proposed scale estimate along with Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
are then jointly used for constructing affine equivariant robust estimates of the scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and the covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}=\cov(\mathbf{x})
(or their shrinkage versions). These developments are described in
Section II
. Then, in
Section III
, we propose a unified framework allowing to estimate the tail parameter of the elliptical distribution using the proposed scale statistic
\hat{\eta}
. In the case of the MVT distribution, this leads to a new estimate of the d.o.f. parameter based on Tyler’s weights. Finally, Section
IV
demonstrates the relevance of the proposed approach on simulated data, with concluding remarks in Section
V
. In the R package
fitHeavyTail

[
14
]
, the function
fit_Tyler
implements this method.

II
Estimate of scale, scatter, and covariance matrix based on Tyler’s weights

Assuming that
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{E}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},g)
has finite 2nd-order moments, then its covariance matrix,
\mathbf{R}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
satisfies

\mathbf{R}=\theta\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\quad\mbox{for }\quad\theta=\frac{\mathbb{E}[r^{2}]}{p},

(4)

where
r^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
is the squared Mahalanobis distance of
\mathbf{x}
w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, whose pdf is given by

f_{r^{2}}(t)=Ct^{p/2-1}g(t),

(5)

where
C=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p/2-1}g(t)\mathrm{d}t
. Hence pdf of
r^{2}
has a one-to-one correspondence with density generator
g
.
From (
4
), we notice that the scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is proportional to the covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}
(assuming
\mathbf{R}
exists).
In the MVN case,
\theta=1
, while for the MVT distribution with density generator as in (
1
) one obtains
\theta=\nu/(\nu-2)
for all
\nu>2
.

II-A

Estimate of scale

As mentioned earlier, Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
loses information of the scale
\eta
. However, it is yet possible to construct an estimate of
\eta
from Tyler’s weights.
Our Tyler’s weights-based estimate (TWE) of scale is defined as the harmonic mean of reciprocal of weights,
1/\hat{w}_{i}
’s, that is,

\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\bigg)^{-1}=\bigg(\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}]^{-1}\bigg)^{-1}.

(6)

One can esily verify that this is a proper scale estimate in the sense that if
\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}^{*}
is computed on scaled observations,
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}=c\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, then
\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}^{*}=c^{2}\cdot\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}
. This follows because Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
in (
3
) with trace constraint is invariant to scaling the data, so
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.

The proposed estimate (
6
) can also be motivated from the following result derived in the high-dimensional random matrix theory (RMT) regime, where
p,n\to\infty
with
n>p
and their ratio tending to constant:
p/n\to c\in(0,1).

Namely, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
be Tyler’s M-estimator in (
3
) verifying
\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
. Then, it was shown in
[
11
,
13
]
that
\max_{\ell}|\eta\hat{w}_{\ell}-1|\to 0
almost surely. The authors in
[
11
]
derived this result for the case that data is i.i.d. Gaussian
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
while
[
13
]
extended these results for more general distributions. Thus since
1/\hat{w}_{i}
concentrate on
\eta
,
(
6
) is a natural robust estimator of scale. Many other robust scale statistics could be constructed from
1/\hat{w}_{i}
’s, such as the median, trimmed mean, etc. In the next subsection, we illustrate why the proposed harmonic mean (
6
) is the most natural.

II-B

Affine equivariant estimate of scatter matrix

Using the scale estimate
\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}
and Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
(with
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
), we can form an estimate of the scatter matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

(7)

referred to as
TWE of scatter matrix
.
Thus
\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}
is scale statistic derived from
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}
since
\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}=\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}})/p
. Equivalently, the trace of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}
can be easily computed as the harmonic mean of Tyler’s quadratic form:

\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}})=\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}]^{-1}\bigg)^{-1}.

Recalling (
6
) we can write (
7
) in the following more intuitive form:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{v}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top},

(8a)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{w}_{i}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{\ell}}=\frac{[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}]^{-1}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}[\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}]^{-1}}

(8b)

where
\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{n}
are normalized Tyler’s weights that verify
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{v}_{i}=1
. Eq. (
8a
) and (
8b
) illustrate that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}
is a
weighted sample covariance matrix (SCM)
with weights
\hat{v}_{i}
.

Finally, we draw the parallel of Tyler’s M-estimating equation and our estimator (
8
). First, note that Tyler’s M-estimating equation (
3
) verifies broader invariance than just invariance with respect to scaling of the data matrix. Namely, denoting the unit-norm normalized data by
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}/\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, one can easily verify that the fixed-point equation in
(
3
) can be rewritten as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}};\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\})
, so based on normalized data.
Furthermore, since
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
, one has:

\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cfrac{p}{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\,\tr\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\right)=p,

or equivalently
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}]^{-1}=1
since
\tr\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\right)=1.
It follows that Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
with
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
is the solution to the following fixed-point equation:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{w}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\ \mbox{ with }\ \tilde{w}_{i}=\frac{p\,[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}]^{-1}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell}]^{-1}},

(9)

where
\tilde{w}_{\ell}
’s are the normalized Tyler’s weights computed on normalized (unit norm) observations
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
’s. Thus, while Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
can be interpreted as a weighted SCM based on normalized data
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}
as shown in (
9
), TWE of scatter
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}
in (
8
) can be viewed as weighted SCM of actual (non-normalized) data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
.

It is worthwhile to point out that most robust estimators of scatter are affine equivariant in the sense that an affine transformation on the data
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, induces following transformation on the estimate:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\{{\bf A}\mathbf{x}_{i}\})={\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}){\bf A}^{\top},\ \forall\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\mbox{ invertible.}

(10)

For example, robust Maronna’s
[
15
]
M-estimators, S-estimators
[
16
]
, or MM-estimators
[
17
]
are affine equivariant.
However, Tyler’s scatter matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is not affine equivariant since (
10
) only holds up to multiplicative scalar factor as shown in (
11
).
Affine equivariance is desirable since if
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{E}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},g)
, then
{\bf A}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{E}_{p}(\mathbf{0},{\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\bf A}^{\top},g)
.
Hence the transformed data shares the same elliptical distribution, but the scatter matrix parameter is mapped to
{\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\bf A}^{\top}
.
Thus a natural requirement to be imposed on any scatter matrix estimator is that it should verify this same equivariance principle under transformations
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mapsto{\bf A}\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
This is shown next.

Lemma 1
.

TWE of scatter matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}
is affine equivariant:
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}(\{{\bf A}\mathbf{x}_{i}\})={\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}){\bf A}^{\top}
,
\forall
invertible
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that Tyler’s M-estimator (with
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=p
) is equivariant in the sense that if
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\})
denotes Tyler’s M-estimator (verifying
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*})=p
) computed on data
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}={\bf A}\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, then

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*}=c{\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf A}^{\top},\ c=\frac{p}{\tr({\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf A}^{\top})}

(11)

for all invertible
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
Now let
\hat{w}_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
denote the corresponding Tyler’s weights.
Then the scale estimate (
6
) computed as the harmonic mean of reciprocals of weights
1/\hat{w}_{i}^{*}=[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}]^{\top}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}^{*}_{\textup{TWE}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}^{*}\Big)^{-1}=\bigg(\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}{\bf A}^{\top}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*})^{-1}{\bf A}\mathbf{x}_{i}]^{-1}\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=c^{-1}\bigg(\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}]^{-1}\bigg)^{-1}=c^{-1}\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}

(12)

where in the 2nd to last identity we simply utilized (
11
). Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}^{*}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{*}=c^{-1}\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}\cdot c{\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf A}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bf A}(\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}){\bf A}^{\top}={\bf A}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}{\bf A}^{\top}.

∎

In the case of large dimensional data, one can also consider a shrinkage Tyler’s M-estimator of the scatter matrix as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE},\beta}=\beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}+(1-\beta)\hat{\eta}_{\textup{TWE}}\mathbf{I},

(13)

where the data adaptive shrinkage parameter
\beta\in[0,1]
is computed as described in
[
18
, Sect. IV.C]
. However, unlike the estimator
in
[
18
]
, the shrinkage TWE in (
13
) provides an estimator of scatter instead of shape matrix.

II-C

An estimator of covariance matrix

If the density generator
g
(and hence the underlying ES distribution) is specified, then the value of
\theta
in (
4
) can be determined, and we can use relationship (
4
) to obtain a covariance matrix estimator as

\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{TWE}}=\theta\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{TWE}}.

For example, if the data has an MVN distribution, then

\theta=1
while
\theta=\nu/(\nu-2)
in the case of an MVT distribution with
\nu
d.o.f.
However, often the underlying parametric family is known, but the underlying tail parameter, say
\nu
, indexing the density generator is unknown.
As is shown in
Section III
, we can form an estimate of
\nu
, denoted
\hat{\nu}_{\text{TWE}}
, using Tyler’s weights.
Since
\theta=h(\nu)
(
cf.
Eq. (
15
) below), a
TWE of covariance matrix
can be computed as

\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\textup{TWE}}=\hat{\theta}_{\textup{TWE}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\textup{TWE}}
,
where
\hat{\theta}_{\text{TWE}}=h(\hat{\nu}_{\text{TWE}})
.

III
Estimating the tail parameter of ES distribution

From (
4
) we can induce the following relationship between the scale parameter
\eta_{\text{cov}}=p^{-1}\tr(\mathbf{R})
of the covariance matrix and scale
\eta=p^{-1}\tr(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
of the scatter matrix:

\eta_{\text{cov}}=\theta\eta\Leftrightarrow\theta=\eta_{\text{cov}}/\eta.

(14)

Note that a natural estimate of
\eta_{\text{cov}}
is
p^{-1}\tr(\mathbf{S})
, where
\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
denotes the sample covariance matrix (SCM). On the other hand, if density generator
g
is specified up to unknown tail parameter
\nu
, thus indexed by
g_{\nu}(\cdot)
, then
\theta
in (
4
) is a following function of the tail parameter
\nu
:

\displaystyle\theta=\int_{0}^{\infty}tf_{r^{2}}(t;\nu)\mathrm{d}t\triangleq h(\nu)

(15)

where the pdf
f_{r^{2}}(\cdot)
, defined in (
5
), is one-to-one with
g_{\nu}(\cdot)
. We do not need numerical integration in most practical cases as often closed-form expression for
h(\nu)
can be derived. Then, after solving the inverse mapping,
\nu=h^{-1}(\theta)=h^{-1}(\eta_{\text{cov}}/\eta)
, Algorithm
1
offers a unified approach for estimating the tail parameter of an ES distribution:

Input:
Data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

Output:
Estimated tail parameter
\nu

1.

Compute Tyler’s M-estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
and weights
\hat{w}_{i}
’s in (
3
);

2.

Compute
\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}
in (
6
), and set
\hat{\theta}_{\text{TWE}}=p^{-1}\tr({\mathbf{S}})/\hat{\eta}_{\text{TWE}}
;

3.

Using (
15
), estimate
\nu
as
\hat{\nu}_{\text{TWE}}=h^{-1}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{TWE}})
.

Algorithm 1

Distribution tail parameter learning

As an example, if
\mathbf{x}
follows an MVT distribution with
\nu>2
d.o.f., one has that
\theta=h(\nu)=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}
, which
unfolds the relation:

\nu=h^{-1}(\theta)=\frac{2\theta}{\theta-1}\mbox{ for }\theta>1.

(16)

Note that
\nu>2
is required for the covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}
to exist. The obtained estimator
\hat{\nu}_{\text{TWE}}
is closely related to estimator in
[
18
, Alg. 1]
, referred to as
OPP estimator
for short. OPP is an iterative approach that iteratively (re-)computes the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
of the MVT distribution with
\nu
given by the current estimate of d.o.f. parameter
\nu^{(k)}
. It then computes
\hat{\theta}=\tr({\mathbf{S}})/\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
which provides an update
\nu^{(k+1)}=h^{-1}(\hat{\theta})
via (
16
). The algorithm iterates for
k=0,1,2\ldots
until convergence,
starting from an initial start
\nu^{(0)}=\hat{\nu}_{\text{kurt}}
, where
\hat{\nu}_{\text{kurt}}
is an estimate of
\nu
based on elliptical kurtosis, proposed in
[
18
]
, and referred to as
kurtosis estimator
. We also proposed an improved version of OPP estimator in
[
19
]
, which, however, is impractical for large
n
and
p
. ML estimation of
\nu
via the Expectation-Maximization (EM) approach is considered in
[
20
]
. This method is unfortunately rather unstable
[
21
]
.

V
Concluding remarks

We proposed a new robust estimator of scale parameter of an elliptical distribution based on the weights from Tyler’s M-estimator, which was further used to construct an affine equivariant Tyler’s M-estimator.
We then proposed a unified framework to estimate the tail parameter of an elliptical distribution.
Finally, it should be noted that this method generalizes to complex-valued data in a straightforward manner.
