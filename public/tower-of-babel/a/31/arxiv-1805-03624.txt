1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main results

Let
\alpha
be a real number. An
\alpha
-Presburger sentence
(short: an
\alpha
-PA sentence) is a statement of the form

Q_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{1}}\;\dots\;Q_{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{r}}\;\;\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{r}),

(1.1)

where
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}\in\{\forall\hskip 0.85355pt,\exists\hskip 0.85355pt\}
are
r

alternating quantifiers
,
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{r}
are
r
blocks of integer variables,
and
\Phi
is a Boolean combination of linear inequalities in
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{r}

with coefficients and constant terms in
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
.
As the number
r
of alternating quantifier blocks and the dimensions

n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
increase, the truth of
\alpha
-PA sentences becomes harder to decide.
In this paper, we study the computational complexity of deciding
\alpha
-PA
sentences.

Sentences of the form (
1.1
) have nice geometric interpretations in many
special cases.
Assume the formula
\Phi
is a conjunction of linear equations
and inequalities, then
\Phi
defines a convex polyhedron
P
defined over
\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]
. For
r=1
and
Q_{1}=\exists
,
a sentence of the form (
1.1
) asks for existence of an integer point in
P
:

\exists\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{x}\in\hskip 1.70709ptP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

(1.2)

In a special case of
r=2
,

Q_{1}=\forall
and
Q_{2}=\exists
, the sentence
S
can ask whether
projections of integer points in a convex polyhedron
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k+m}

cover all integer points in another polyhedron
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

\forall\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{x}\in R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}\;\;\exists\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\;\;:\;\;(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in P\hskip 0.85355pt.

(1.3)

Here both
P
and
R
are defined over
\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]
.
When
\alpha
is rational, the classical problems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are repectively known as
Integer Programming
and
Parametric Integer Programming
(see eg.
[
Schr
]
,
[
Len
]
,
[
Kan
]
).
Further variations on the theme and increasing number of quantifiers
allow more general formulas with integer valuations of the polytope algebra. For a survey of this area, see Barvinok
[
Bar
]
.

Recall that classical Presburger arithmetic (
PA
) is the first-order theory of
(\mathbb{Z},<,+)
, introduced by Presburger in
[
Pre
]
. When
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, then deciding the truth of
\alpha
-PA sentence is equivalent to deciding whether or not a PA sentence is true. The latter decision problem has been studied extensively and we review some of their results below. The focus of this paper is the case when
\alpha
is irrational, which is implicitly assumed whenever we mention
\alpha
-PA.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
is the field of real algebraic numbers. We think of
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
as being given by its defining
\mathbb{Z}[x]
-polynomial of
degree
d
, with a rational interval to single out a unique root.
We say that
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
is
quadratic
if
d=2
.
Similarly, the elements
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
are represented in the form
\gamma=c_{0}+c_{1}\alpha+\ldots+c_{d-1}\alpha^{d-1}
, where
c_{0},\ldots,c_{d-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For example,
\alpha=\sqrt{2}
is quadratic and given by
\{\alpha^{2}-2=0,\alpha>0\}
. Thus
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]=\{a+b\hskip 0.85355pt\sqrt{2},\,a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. For
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
, the
encoding length

\ell(\gamma)
is the
total bit length of the
c_{i}
’s defined above.
Similarly, the encoding length
\ell(S)
of an
\alpha
-PA sentence
S
is defined to be the total bit length
of all symbols in
S
, with integer coefficients and constants represented in binary.

The only existing result that directly relates to the complexity of deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences is the following theorem due to Khachiyan and Porkolab, which extends Lenstra’s classical result
[
Len
]
on Integer Programming in fixed dimensions.

{thmC}

[
[
KP
]
]
For every fixed
n
, sentences of the form

\exists\hskip 0.85355pt\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,:\,A\mathbf{y}\leq\overline{b}
with
A\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}^{m\times n},\overline{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}^{m}

can be decided in polynomial time.

Note that the system
A\mathbf{y}\leq\overline{b}
in Theorem
1.1
can involve arbitrary algebraic irrationals. This is a rare positive result on irrational polyhedra. Indeed, for a non-quadratic
\alpha
, this gives the only positive result
on deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences that we know of.

In this paper we establish that Theorem
1.1
is an exception and in general deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences is often substantially harder. We first consider the case of
\alpha
being quadratic. In this situation the theory
\alpha
-PA is decidable, and in this paper we prove both lower and upper bounds on the complexity of deciding the truth of a given
\alpha
-PA sentence. In the following theorems, the constants
K,C
vary from one context to another.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
be a quadratic irrational number,
and let
r\geq 1
. An
\alpha
-PA sentence
S
with
r

alternating quantifier blocks can be decided in time at most

K\hskip 1.70709pt2^{2^{\,\iddots^{\;2^{\,C\hskip 0.85355pt\ell(S)}}}}\quad\text{\rm$\bigl(\hskip 0.85355pt$tower of height \hskip 1.70709pt$r\hskip 0.85355pt\bigr)$},

where the constants
K,\hskip 0.85355ptC>0
depend only on
\alpha
.

In the opposite direction, we have the following lower bound:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
be a quadratic irrational number,
and let
r\geq 4
. Then deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences
with
r
alternating quantifier blocks and at most
c\hskip 0.85355pt\hskip 0.85355ptr
variables and inequalities requires space at least:

K\hskip 1.70709pt2^{2^{\,\iddots^{\;2^{\,C\hskip 0.85355pt\ell(S)}}}}\quad\text{\rm$\bigl(\hskip 0.85355pt$tower of height \hskip 1.70709pt\hskip 0.85355pt$r-3\hskip 0.85355pt\bigr)$},

where the constants

only depend on
\alpha
.

These results should be compared with the triply exponential
upper bound and doubly exponential lower bounds for PA (discussed in Section
1.2
).
In the borderline case of
r=3
, the problem is especially
interesting.
We give the following lower bound, which only needs a few variables:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
be a quadratic irrational number.
Then deciding
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{6}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{4}\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{11}

\alpha
-PA sentences with at most
K
inequalities is
PSPACE
-hard,
where the constant
K
depends only on
\alpha
. Furthermore,
for
\alpha=\sqrt{2}
, one can take
K=10^{6}
.

This should be compared with Grädel’s theorem on

\Sigma^{\textup{{P}}}_{2}
-completeness for
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}
integer sentences
in PA (also discussed in Section
1.2
). The sudden jump from the polynomial hierarchy in PA is due to the power of irrational quadratics.
Specifically, any irrational quadratic
\alpha
has an infinite periodic continued fraction, and this allows us to work with Ostrowski representations of integers in base
\alpha
. Because of this we are able to code string relations such as shifts, suffix/prefix and subset, which were not at all possible to define in PA.
Such operations are rich enough to encode arbitrary automata computation, and in fact Turing Machine computation in bounded space. Section
1.3
further discusses our method.

The situation is even worse when
\alpha
is a non-quadratic irrational number. In this case
\alpha
-PA is undecidable and we prove here that just four alternating quantifier blocks are enough.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\textsf{{alg}}}
be a non-quadratic irrational number.
Then
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}

\alpha
-PA sentences are undecidable, where
k=20000
.

1.2.
Previous results

In this paper, we build on earlier related works on certain expansions of the real ordered additive group.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R},<,+,\mathbb{Z},x\mapsto\alpha x)
. Denote by
T_{\alpha}
the first-order theory of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
; this is the first-order theory of the real numbers viewed as a
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
-vector space with a predicate for the set of the integers. This theory is an extension of Presburger Arithmetic, and it is not hard to see that
T_{\alpha}
contains an
\alpha
-PA sentence if only if the
\alpha
-PA sentence is true.

It has long been known that
T_{\alpha}
is decidable when
\alpha
is rational. This is arguably due to Skolem
[
Sko
]
and was later
rediscovered independently by Weispfenning
[
W2
]
and Miller
[
Mil
]
. More recently, Hieronymi
[
H2
]
proved that
T_{\alpha}
is still decidable when

\alpha
is quadratic. Thus it was known that checking
\alpha
-PA sentences is decidable for quadratic
\alpha
.

When
\alpha
is irrational non-quadratic, the theory
T_{\alpha}
is undecidable by Hieronymi and Tychonievich
[
HTy
]
. This result itself does not imply undecidability of
\alpha
-PA sentences in this case, as
T_{\alpha}
is a proper extension of
\alpha
-PA. However, the undecidability of
\alpha
-PA can be obtained by a careful analysis of the proof in
[
HTy
]
. In Theorem
4
, we not only give an explicit proof of this result, but also precisely quantify this result by showing that four alternating quantifier blocks are enough for undecidability. While our argument is based on the ideas in
[
HTy
]
, substantial extra work is necessary to reduce the number of alternations to four from the lower bound implicit in the proofs in
[
HTy
]
.

While this paper is the first systematic study of the complexity of decision problems related to
\alpha
-PA, there is a large body of work for Presburger arithmetic. A quantifier elimination algorithm for PA was given by
Cooper
[
Coo
]
to effectively solve the decision problem.
Oppen
[
Opp
]
showed that such sentences can be decided in at most triply
exponential time (see also
[
RL
]
). In the opposite direction,
a nondeterministic doubly exponential lower bound was obtained
by Fischer and Rabin
[
FR
]
(see also
[
W1
]
). We also refer the reader to Berman
[
Ber
]
for the precise complexity of Presburger arithmetic.
As one restricts the number of alternations, the complexity of PA drops by roughly one exponent (see
[
Für
,
Sca
,
RL
]
), but still remains exponential.

For a bounded number of variables, two important cases are known to be polynomial time decidable,
namely the analogues of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) with rational
polyhedra
P
and
R
. These are classical results by Lenstra
[
Len
]

and Kannan
[
Kan
]
, respectively. Scarpellini
[
Sca
]
showed that all

\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{n}
-sentences are still polynomial time decidable for every
n
fixed.
However, for two alternating quantifiers, Schöning proved in
[
Schö
]
that
deciding
\hskip 0.85355pt\exists y\hskip 0.85355pt\forall x\hskip 0.85355pt:\hskip 0.85355pt\Phi(x,y)
is
NP
-complete.
Here
\Phi
is any Boolean combination of linear inequalities in two variables,
instead of those in the particular form (
1.3
).
This improved an earlier result by Grädel in
[
Grä
]
, who also showed
that PA sentences with
m+1
alternating quantifier blocks and
m+5
variables
are complete for the
m
-th level in the Polynomial Hierarchy
PH
.
In these results, the number of inequalities (atoms) in
\Phi
is still part of the input, i.e., allowed to vary.

Much of the recent work concerns the most restricted PA sentences for which the number of alternations (
r+2
), number of variables
and
number
of inequalities in
\Phi
are all fixed.
Thus, the input is essentially a bounded list of integer coefficients
and constants in
\Phi
, encoded in binary.
For
r=0
, such sentences are polynomial time decidable by Woods
[
Woo
]
.
For
r=1
, Nguyen and Pak
[
NP
]
showed that deciding
\exists^{1}\forall^{2}\exists^{2}
PA-sentences with at most
10
inequalities is
NP
-complete.
More generally, they showed that such sentences with
r+2
alternations,
O(r)
variables and inequalities are complete for the
r
-th level in
PH
.
Thus, limiting the “format” of a
PA formula does not reduce the complexity by a lot. This is our main motivation
for the lower bounds in Theorems
2
and
3

for
\alpha
-PA sentences.

1.3.
Proofs outline

Let S1S be the monadic second order theory of
(\mathbb{N},+1)
, where
+1
denotes the usual successor function, and let WS1S be the weak monadic second order theory of
(\mathbb{N},+1)
, that is the monadic second order logic of
(\mathbb{N},+1)
in which quantification over sets is restricted to quantification over finite subsets. The main results of
[
H2
]
states that for quadratic
\alpha
, one can decide
T_{\alpha}
-sentences by translating them into corresponding S1S-sentences, and then decide the latter. Since
\alpha
-PA sentences form a subset of all
T_{\alpha}
-sentences, this method can be used to decide
\alpha
-PA sentences. Thus, upper complexity bounds for S1S can theoretically be transferred to deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences. Moreover, the work in
[
H2
]
also shows that one can translate S1S-sentences into
T_{\alpha}
-sentences. However, no efficient direct translation between
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
-sentence and S1S-sentence was given in
[
H1
,
H2
]
.
Ideally, one would like to do this translation with as few extra alternations of quantifiers as possible.
In Theorems
1
and
2
, we explicitly quantify this translation. We strengthen the result from
[
H2
]
by showing that one can translate
\alpha
-PA sentences to WS1S-sentences. The translation then allows to us find upper and lower complexity bounds for deciding
\alpha
-PA sentences.

The most powerful feature of
\alpha
-PA sentences is that we can talk about Ostrowski representation of integers, which will be used throughout the paper as the main encoding tool. We first obtain the upper bound in Theorem
1
by directly translating
\alpha
-PA sentences into the statements about automata using Ostrowski encoding and using known upper bounds for certain decision problems about automata. Next, we show the lower bound for three alternating quantifiers
(Theorem
3
) by a general argument on the
Halting Problem with polynomial space constraint, again using Ostrowski encoding. We generalize the above argument to get a lower bound for
r\geq 3
alternating quantifier blocks (Theorem
2
). For the latter result, we first translate WS1S-sentences to
\alpha
-PA sentences with only one extra alternation,
and then invoke a known tower lower bound for WS1S. Finally in the proof of Theorem
4
, we again use the expressibility of Ostrowski representation to reduce the upper bound of the number of alternating quantifier blocks needed for undecidability of
\alpha
-PA sentences. The use of Ostrowski representations allows us to replace more general arguments from
[
HTy
]
by explicit computations, and thereby reduce the quantifier-complexity of certain
\alpha
-PA sentences.

A

\begin{gathered}(p_{-1},q_{-1})\hskip 1.70709pt=\hskip 1.70709pt(1,0);\;(p_{0},q_{0})\hskip 1.70709pt=\hskip 1.70709pt(a_{0},1);\;\\

2.1.
Periodic continued fractions

Lagrange’s theorem states that
an irrational number
\alpha
is quadratic if and only if it has a periodic continued fraction expansion (see
[
RS
, Chapter III.1 Theorem 1 and 2]
). In this situation, we write

\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},\dots,a_{m},\overline{c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{k_{\alpha}-1}}],

where
c_{0},\dots,c_{k_{\alpha}-1}
is the repeating block in the continued fraction expansion with the
minimum
period
k_{\alpha}
.

Let
\gamma=[\overline{c_{0};c_{1},\dots,c_{k-1}}]
.
It is easy to see that
\gamma=(c\alpha+d)/(e\alpha+f)
for some
c,d,e,f\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore,
\alpha
-PAs sentences can be expressed in
\gamma
-PA sentences and vice versa. Since
\alpha
is constant, so are
c,d,e
and
f
. Thus, for our complexity purposes, we can always assume that our quadratic irrational
\alpha
is purely periodic, i.e.,

\alpha=[\overline{a_{0};a_{1},\dots,a_{k_{\alpha}-1}}]

(2.10)

with the minimum period
k_{\alpha}
.

Fact 8
.

Let

.
There exist

such that for every

(p_{n+i},q_{n+i})\;=\;c_{i}(p_{n},q_{n})+d_{i}(p_{n+1},q_{n+1}).

The coefficients

can be computed in time

.

Proof 2.3

3.
\alpha
-Presburger formulas

Fix some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. An
\alpha
-PA formula
is of the form

Q_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}\;\dots\;Q_{r}\mathbf{y}_{r}\;\;\Phi(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{r},\mathbf{x}),

where
\Phi
is a Boolean combination of linear inequalities in
\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{r}},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with coefficients and constant terms in
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
and
\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{r},\mathbf{x}
are integer variables; or any logically equivalent first-order formula in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\{+,0,1,<,\lambda_{p}:p\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\}
where
\lambda_{p}
is a unary function symbol for multiplication by
p\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
. We will denote a generic
\alpha
-PA formula as
F(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{x}
are the free variables of
F
, i.e., those not associated with a quantifier. An
\alpha
-PA sentence
is an
\alpha
-PA formula without free variables.

Given an
\alpha
-PA formula
F(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathbf{x}|}
, we say
F(\mathbf{X})
holds (or is true) if the statement obtained by replacing the free variables in
F
by
\mathbf{X}
and letting the quantified variables
\mathbf{y}_{i}
range over
\mathbb{Z}^{n_{i}}
, is true.
We say that a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is
\alpha
-PA definable
(or an
\alpha
-PA set
) if there exists an
\alpha
-PA formula
F(\mathbf{x})
such that

S=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathbf{x}|}\ :\ F(\mathbf{X})\}.

When
\alpha=0
, then a
\alpha
-PA formula is just a classical PA-formula. Hence
0
-PA is just PA, and therefore decidable. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=(\mathbb{R},<,+,\mathbb{Z},x\mapsto\alpha x)
. As pointed out in the introduction, the first-order theory
T_{\alpha}
of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
contains all true
\alpha
-PA sentence. Since
T_{\alpha}
is decidable by
[
H2
]
, we have:

Theorem 11
.

Let
\alpha
be quadratic. Then
\alpha
-PA is decidable.

The main difference between the situation when
\alpha
is rational and when it is irrational, is that when
\alpha
is irrational,
\alpha
-PA formulas can express properties of the
\alpha
-Ostrowski representation of natural numbers. This increases the computational complexity of the decision procedure of
\alpha
-PA in comparison to the one for PA.

3.1.
\alpha
-PA formulas for working with Ostrowski representation

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number, not necessarily quadratic. In this section, we will show that various properties of Ostrowski representations can be expressed using
\alpha
-PA formulas.

By Fact
5
the convergents
\{p_{n}/q_{n}\}
of
\alpha
can be characterized by the best approximation property.
Namely,
u/v
with
v>1
is a convergent
p_{n}/q_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if

\forall\hskip 0.85355ptw,z\;(0<z<v)\to|z\alpha-w|>|v\alpha-u|.

(3.1)

Here
\gcd(u,v)=1
is implied, since if
k=\gcd(u,v)>1
, then
|\alpha v/k-u/k|<|\alpha v-u|
and
0<v/k<v
. Now consider two consecutive convergents
(u,v)=(p_{n},q_{n})
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=(p_{n+1},q_{n+1})
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any integers
0<z<v^{\prime}
and
w
, first we have
|z\alpha-w|>|v^{\prime}\alpha-u^{\prime}|
. If
|z\alpha-w|<|v\alpha-u|
, then first we must have
v<z<v^{\prime}
. Then among all such pairs
(w,z)

|z\alpha-w|
must necessarily be another convergent of
\alpha
, which is impossible since we assumed that
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are consecutive. Thus, a necessary and sufficient condition for
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
to be consecutive convergents is simply:

\begin{gathered}\textbf{C}_{\forall\hskip 0.85355pt}(u,v,u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\;\coloneqq\;0<v<v^{\prime}\;\land\;\forall\hskip 0.85355ptw,z\;\big(0<z<v^{\prime}\to\\
|z\alpha-w|\geq|v\alpha-u|>|v^{\prime}\alpha-u^{\prime}|\big).\end{gathered}

(3.2)

Note that
\textbf{C}_{\forall\hskip 0.85355pt}
is a
\forall
-formula.
More generally, consider the
\alpha
-PA formula:

\begin{gathered}\textbf{C}_{\forall\hskip 0.85355pt}(u_{0},v_{0},\dots,u_{k},v_{k})\;\coloneqq\;1<v_{0}<v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}\;\land\\
\forall\hskip 0.85355ptw,z\;\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k}\Big(0<z<v_{i+1}\to|z\alpha-w|\geq|v_{i}\alpha-u_{i}|>|v_{i+1}\alpha-u_{i+1}|\Big).\end{gathered}

(3.3)

Then

for some

Define the following quantifier-free

\displaystyle\textbf{After}(u,v,u^{\prime},v^{\prime},Z,Z^{\prime})\;\coloneqq

\displaystyle\bigl(-\alpha v+u<\alpha Z-Z^{\prime}<-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle\lor\;\bigl(-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}<\alpha Z-Z^{\prime}<-\alpha v+u\bigr).

\displaystyle\bigl(-\alpha v+u-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}<\alpha Z-Z^{\prime}<-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}\bigr)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lor\;\bigl(-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}<\alpha Z-Z^{\prime}<-\alpha v+u-\alpha v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}\bigr).

4.
Quadratic irrationals: Upper bound

In this section we prove Theorem
1
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be an irrational quadratic number.
We will show that for every
\alpha
-PA formula
F(\mathbf{x})
, there is a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{A}
accepts precisely those words that are Ostrowski representations of numbers satisfying
F
. This will then allow to use automata-based decision procedure to decide
\alpha
-PA sentence. It should be emphasized that the tower height in Theorem
1
only depends on the number of alternating quantifiers, but not on the number of variables in the sentence.

4.1.
Finite automata and Ostrowski representations

We first remind the reader of the definitions of finite automata and recognizability. For more details, we refer the reader to Khoussainov and Nerode
[
KN
]
. Let
\Sigma
be a finite set. We denote by
\Sigma^{*}
the set of words of finite length on
\Sigma
.

{defi}

A

(NFA)
\mathcal{A}

\Sigma

(S,I,T,F)
, where
S
is a finite non-empty set, called the set of states of
\mathcal{A}

I
is a subset of
S

is a non-empty set, called the transition table of
\mathcal{A}
and
F
is a subset of
S
, called the set of final states of
\mathcal{A}
. An automaton
\mathcal{A}=(S,I,T,F)
is
deterministic
(DFA) if
I
contains exactly one element, and for every
s\in S
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
there is exactly one
s^{\prime}\in S
such that
(s,\sigma,s^{\prime})\in T
.
We say that an automaton
\mathcal{A}
on
\Sigma

accepts
a word
w=w_{n}\dots w_{1}\in\Sigma^{*}
if there is a sequence
s_{n},\dots,s_{1},s_{0}\in S
such that
s_{n}\in I
,
s_{0}\in F
and for

,
(s_{i},w_{i},s_{i-1})\in T
. A subset

is

by

if

is the set of
\Sigma
-words that are accepted by
\mathcal{A}
. We say that

is

By the

It is well known that recognizability by NFA and DFA are equivalent:

(

B_{1},\dots B_{4}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
to code the computation on
U(x)
.
The
A_{i}
’s code the current state of the Turing machine.
That is, for
(s,t)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, we have
(s,t)\in A_{i}
if and only if at step
s
of the computation,
U
is in state
q_{i}
and its head is over the
t
-th cell of the tape.
The
B_{j}
’s code which symbols are written on the tape at a given step of the computation.
We have
(s,t)\in B_{j}
if and only if at step
s
of the computation, the symbol
\sigma_{j}
is written on
t
-th cell of the tape. The computation
U(x)
then halts if and only if there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{8}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
B_{1},\dots B_{4}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that:

a)

A_{i}
’s are pairwise disjoint;
B_{j}
’s are pairwise disjoint.

b)

(0,0)\in A_{1}
, i.e., the computation starts in the initial state.

c)

There exists some
(u,v)\in A_{2}
, i.e., the computation eventually halts.

d)

For each
s\in\mathbb{N}
, there is at most one
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(s,t)\in\cup_{i}A_{i}
, i.e., at each step of the computation,
U
can only be in exactly one state.

e)

If
x=x_{0}\dots x_{n}\in{\bf\Sigma}^{*}
, then for every
0\leq t\leq n
, we have
x_{t}=\sigma_{j}\iff(0,t)\in B_{j}
, i.e., the first rows of the
B_{j}
’s code the input string
x
.

f)

Whenever
(s,t)\in B_{j}
,

f1)

if
(s,t)\notin A_{i}
for all
i\in[8]
, then
(s+1,t)\in B_{j}
. That is, if the current head position is not at
t
, then the
t
-th symbol does not change.

f2)

if
(s,t)\in A_{i}
for some

and
\delta(i,j)=(\delta^{1}_{ij},\delta^{2}_{ij},\delta^{3}_{ij})\in[8]\times[4]\times\{\pm 1\}
, then
(s+1,t)\in B_{\delta^{2}_{ij}}
and
(s+1,t+\delta^{3}_{ij})\in A_{\delta^{1}_{ij}}
. That is, if the head position is at
t
, and the state is
i
, then a transition rule is applied.

We use the predicate
Member
to code membership
(s,t)\in A_{i},B_{j}
.
By Theorem
36
, there should exist tuples
\bm{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{i4}),\,\bm{Y}_{j}=(Y_{j1},\dots,Y_{j4})\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
that represent
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
.
In other words, we have

(s,t)\in A_{i}\iff\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t)\;,\quad(s,t)\in B_{j}\iff\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j},s,t).

For the input condition
e)
, there exist
\bm{Z}_{j}=(Z_{j1},\dots,Z_{j4})\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
so that

x_{t}=\sigma_{j}\iff\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Z}_{j},0,t)\quad\forall\;0\leq t\leq n.

Note that
\bm{Z}_{j}
can be explicitly constructed from the input
x
(see Theorem
36
’s proof).
Now the sentence
\varphi_{x}
that encodes halting of
U(x)
is:

\displaystyle\varphi_{x}\;\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\exists\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{8},\,\bm{Y}_{1},\dots,\bm{Y}_{4}\in\mathbb{N}^{4},\,u,v\in\mathbb{N}\quad\forall s,t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\neq i^{\prime}}\lnot\big(\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t)\;\land\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i^{\prime}},s,t)\big)

\displaystyle\;\land\;

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\lnot\big(\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j},s,t)\;\land\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j^{\prime}},s,t)\big)

\displaystyle\;\land\;

\displaystyle\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{1},0,0)\;\;\land\;\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{2},u,v)

\displaystyle\;\land\;

\displaystyle\Big[\big(\bigvee_{i}\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t)\big)\;\land\;\big(\bigvee_{i}\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t^{\prime})\big)\;\to\;t=t^{\prime}\Big]

\displaystyle\;\land\;

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j}\big(\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Z}_{j},0,t)\to\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j},0,t)\big)

\displaystyle\;\land\;

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j}\Big(\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j},s,t)\to\big[\,\bigwedge_{i}\lnot\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t)\;\land\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{j},s+1,t)\,\big]

\displaystyle\;\lor\;\bigvee_{i}\big[\,

\displaystyle\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{i},s,t)\;\land\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{Y}_{\delta^{2}_{ij}},s+1,t)\;\land\;\textbf{\bf Member}(\bm{X}_{\delta^{1}_{ij}},s+1,t+\delta^{3}_{ij})\,\big]\;\Big).

Since
Member
is
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}
-definable, the sentence
\varphi_{x}
is
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{*}
.
Whether
U(x)
halts or not is undecidable, and so is
\varphi_{x}
.
A direct count shows that

appears at most
200
times in
\varphi_{x}
.
From the last estimate in the proof of Lemma

, we see that

\varphi_{x}
is at most a
\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\exists\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}\forall\hskip 0.85355pt^{k}

\square

The main theorem by Khachiyan and Porkolab in
[

]

is the following general integer optimization result
on convex semialgebraic sets.

{thmC}

[
[
KP
]
]
Consider a first order formula
F(\mathbf{y})
over the reals of the form:

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:Q_{1}\hskip 0.85355pt\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\;\dots\;Q_{m}\hskip 0.85355pt\mathbf{x}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}}\;P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{m}),

where

is a Boolean combination of equalities/inequalities of the form

with

and
g_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{w}]
.
Let

be fixed, and suppose that the set

S_{F}\hskip 1.70709pt\coloneqq\hskip 1.70709pt\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:F(\mathbf{y})=\text{\rm true}\}

is convex. Then we can either decide in polynomial time that
S_{F}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}=\varnothing
,
or produce in polynomial time some
\mathbf{y}\in S_{F}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.

This immediately implies Theorem
1.1
.
Here there is no restriction on the number of
g_{i}
’s and their degrees.
The coefficients of

’s are encoded in binary. Note that convexity is crucially important in the theorem.
In Manders and Adleman
[

]
, it is shown that given

,
