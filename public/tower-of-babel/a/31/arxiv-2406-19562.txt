The Pinnacle Sets of a Graph
Chassidy Bozeman, Christine Cheng, Pamela E. Harris, Stephen Lasinis, Shanise Walker

Abstract
. We introduce and study the pinnacle sets of a simple graph G G with n n vertices. Given a bijective vertex labeling λ : V ⁡ ( G ) → [ n ] \lambda\,:\,V(G)\rightarrow[n] , the label λ ⁡ ( v ) \lambda(v) of vertex v v is a pinnacle of ( G , λ ) (G,\lambda) if λ ⁡ ( v ) > λ ⁡ ( w ) \lambda(v)>\lambda(w) for all vertices w w in the neighborhood of v v . The pinnacle set of ( G , λ ) (G,\lambda) contains all the pinnacles of the labeled graph. A subset S ⊆ [ n ] S\subseteq[n] is a pinnacle set of G G if there exists a labeling λ \lambda such that S S is the pinnacle set of ( G , λ ) (G,\lambda) . Of interest to us is the question: Which subsets of [ n ] [n] are the pinnacle sets of G G ? Our main results are as follows. We show that when G G is connected, G G has a size- k k pinnacle set if and only if G G has an independent set of the same size. Consequently, determining if G G has a size- k k pinnacle set and determining if G G has a particular subset S S as a pinnacle set are NP-complete problems. Nonetheless, we completely identify all the pinnacle sets of complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, cycles and paths. We also present two techniques for deriving new pinnacle sets from old ones that imply a typical graph has many pinnacle sets. Finally, we define a poset on all the size- k k pinnacle sets of G G and show that it is a join semilattice. If, additionally, the poset has a minimum element, then it is a distributive lattice. We conclude with some open problems for further study.

Our results.

In Section 2, we present preliminary results about pinnacle sets of graphs. First, we prove that

is the pinnacle set of some graph with
n

In Section 3, we identify the pinnacle sets of complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, cycles and paths. In Section 4, we present two techniques for creating new pinnacle sets from old ones. The first one involves switching the labels of two vertices. Remarkably, by just performing a sequence of switches, we prove that if

The second technique involves the use of what we call an

Our next result establishes a connection between pinnacle sets and independent sets of a graph.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
\lambda
be a labeling of
G
. Let
S
be the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda)
and let
V_{S}
contain all the vertices whose labels are in
S
. Then
n\in S
. Moreover,
V_{S}
is an independent set of
G
.

Proof.

Let
z
be the vertex such that
\lambda(z)=n
. Since
n
is the largest label assigned by
\lambda
, we have that
\lambda(z)>\lambda(y)
for every neighbor
y
of
z
. Thus,
n\in S
.

Next, when
V_{S}
has only one vertex,
V_{S}
is clearly an independent set.
So assume
V_{S}
has two or more vertices.
Let
u,v\in V_{S}
.
Then either
\lambda(u)>\lambda(v)
or
\lambda(v)>\lambda(u)
.
If
u
and
v
are adjacent, either
\lambda(u)
or
\lambda(v)
is not a pinnacle of
(G,\lambda)
, a contradiction.
Thus, no two vertices of
V_{S}
are adjacent so
V_{S}
is an independent set of
G
.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

Basic labeling.
In our proofs on pinnacle sets, we apply a certain type of labeling on a subgraph

of

The close connection between independent sets and pinnacle sets suggest that certain problems about pinnacle sets are computationally hard. Consider the following decision problems:

A graph

We establish that both problems are NP-complete. Before we do so, consider the well-known NP-complete problem (see

.

remains NP-complete even when the input graph

Proof.

is already known to be in NP so we just have to show that the additional restriction in the proposition does not affect the computational complexity of the problem.

Suppose

is the input to

Add a new vertex

is a yes-instance of

is also a yes-instance of

is a yes-instance of

3.
Pinnacle sets for certain graph families

Although we have just shown that
PinnacleSetExistence
is NP-complete,
for certain graph families, we can still give a complete characterization of its pinnacle sets.
We present such results for complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, cycles, and paths.
Let
\mathsf{Pin}(G)
contain all pinnacle sets of
G

\mathsf{pin}(G)=|\mathsf{Pin}(G)|

3.1.
Complete graphs

We show that
\{n\}
is the only pinnacle set of

, the complete graph on

Lemma 3.1
.

If
n\geq 1
, then
\mathsf{Pin}(K_{n})=\{n\}
and
\mathsf{pin}(K_{n})=1
.

Proof.

For any labeling of
K_{n}
, each vertex is adjacent to the vertex labeled
n
, and is therefore not a pinnacle. Thus,
\{n\}
is the only pinnacle set for
K_{n}
and
\mathsf{pin}(K_{n})=1
. ∎

3.2.
Complete bipartite graphs

We now characterize all of the pinnacle sets of

, the complete bipartite graph on
m+n
vertices.
In what follows, whenever
a<b
are integers we let
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
and whenever
a=b
we let
[a,b]=\{a\}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
m\geq n\geq 1
, then
\mathsf{Pin}(K_{m,n})=\{[k,m+n]:n+1\leq k\leq m+n\}
and
\mathsf{pin}(K_{m,n})=m
.

Proof.

Let
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m}\}
and
W=\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\}
be the disjoint sets of vertices of
K_{m,n}
. We now label the vertices of
K_{m,n}
so that
[n+k^{\prime},n+m]
, where
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq m
, is the pinnacle set. Let
\lambda(v_{j})=j
for
j=1
to
k^{\prime}-1
and let
\lambda(v_{j})=n+j
for
j=k^{\prime}
to
m
. Assign the remaining labels in
[k^{\prime},n+k^{\prime}-1]
to the vertices of
W
. Let
V^{\prime}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k^{\prime}-1}\}
and
V^{\prime\prime}=V-V^{\prime}
. Notice that for every
v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime},w\in W,v^{\prime\prime}\in V^{\prime\prime}
,
\lambda(v^{\prime})<\lambda(w)<\lambda(v^{\prime\prime})
. Thus, none of the labels in
[1,k^{\prime}-1]\cup[k^{\prime},n+k^{\prime}-1]
are pinnacles while
every
label in
[n+k^{\prime},n+m]
is a pinnacle. Since
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq m
, it follows that
[n+1,n+m],[n+2,n+m],\ldots,[n+m,n+m]
are all pinnacle sets of
K_{m,n}
.

For the reverse inclusion, suppose
S
is a pinnacle set of
(K_{m,n},\lambda)
. Without loss of generality, assume
v\in V
so that
\lambda(v)=m+n
. An immediate consequence is that for each
w\in W
,
\lambda(w)
is not a pinnacle because
v
and
w
are adjacent.
Let
s
be the smallest label in
S
and
\lambda(v^{\prime})=s
. If
v^{\prime}\in W
, then
v^{\prime}
and
v
would be adjacent and
\lambda(v)>\lambda(v^{\prime})
, contradicting that
s
is a pinnacle. Hence
v^{\prime}\in V
.
It must also be the case that for every
w\in W
,
\lambda(w)<s
.
Pick
t\in[s,m+n]
.
Since
s\leq t
, the vertex
v^{\prime\prime}
that has
\lambda(v^{\prime\prime})=t
is in
V
. Since
v^{\prime\prime}
is adjacent to only the vertices in
W
,
t
is also a pinnacle. It follows that
every
label in
[s,m+n]
is a pinnacle; i.e.,
S=[s,m+n]
. Finally, by Corollary
2.5.2
,
|S|\leq m
because the largest independent set of
K_{m,n}
is
m
. Hence,

S\in\{[k,m+n]:n+1\leq k\leq m+n\}
, as desired.

The count for the number of distinct pinnacle sets follows directly from the construction above.
∎

In the special case of
m=1
, the complete bipartite graph
K_{1,n-1}
is a star graph with
n
vertices. The previous result immediately implies the following.

Corollary 3.2.1
.

If
n\geq 1
, then
\mathsf{Pin}(K_{1,n-1})=\{[k,n]:2\leq k\leq n\}
and
\mathsf{pin}(K_{1,n-1})=n-1
.

3.3.
Cycle graphs

Let

Lemma 3.3
.

If

is a pinnacle set of

, then

.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.5.1
, the size of a pinnacle set of a graph is the size of an independent set of the graph.
The maximum size of an independence set of a cycle on

vertices is exactly

.
∎

The following definition and lemma will play a key role in subsequent arguments.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
. Define

\ell(s_{i})=|\{x\in[n]\setminus S:x<s_{i}\}|
, the number of elements in
[n]
that are less than
s_{i}
and are not in
S
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}\}
with
s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{k}
be a pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda)
. Let
\lambda(v_{j})=s_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
, and let
G_{j}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices whose labels are less than or equal to
s_{j}
. If
some
s_{i}
has
\ell(s_{i})\leq i
and
\deg(v_{j})\geq 2
for
j=1,\ldots,i
, then
G_{i}
contains a cycle.

Proof.

By definition,
G_{i}
has
s_{i}=\ell(s_{i})+i
vertices. But
\ell(s_{i})\leq i
, so
G_{i}
has at most
i+i=2i
vertices.
Let
V_{i}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i}\}
and
N[V_{i}]
be the closed neighborhood of
V_{i}
. Since
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i}
are pinnacles of
(G,\lambda)
, every vertex in
N[V_{i}]
is in
G_{i}
and, consequently,
\deg_{G}(v)=\deg_{G_{i}}(v)
for each
v\in V_{i}
. But
V_{i}
remains an independent set of
G_{i}
so it follows that
G_{i}
has at least
\sum_{j=1}^{i}\deg(v_{j})\geq 2i
edges. Since the number of edges of
G_{i}
is at least the number of its vertices,
G_{i}
has to have a cycle. ∎

In Theorems
3.6
and
3.9
, we fully characterize the pinnacles sets of cycles and paths, respectively. To determine if a set

that are not in

for which there are too few such labels (as described in the theorems), we consider the subgraph induced on

contains a proper subgraph that contains a cycle (in Theorem

contains a proper subgraph that is all of

Remark 3.10
.

As noted in the introduction, our work generalizes the notion of the pinnacle set of a permutation

of
[n]
in
[
8
]
. If we think of
\pi
as a labeling of
P_{n}

Consequently, in their work, the pinnacle set of
\pi
need not contain
n
; it can even be an empty set.
In our work, however, the pinnacle set of
every
labeling of
P_{n}
contains
n
.

Davis et al.
[
8
]
characterized the non-empty subsets of positive integers that are pinnacle sets of permutations. We rephrase their statement below to match our discussion.

Theorem 3.11

(

8
]
, Theorem 1.10 and Proposition 2.3)

Let

be a non-empty set of positive integers with

and
s_{k}=n
. Then
S
is a pinnacle set of a permutation of
[n]
if and only if both these statements are true:

(i)
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k-1}\}
is also a pinnacle set of
a permutation of
[s_{k-1}]
and

(ii)
k<n/2
.

Note that Davis et al.’s characterization is recursive. To prove that
S
is a pinnacle set of a permutation of

[n]
, one has to verify that

(remember that
s_{k}=n
),
k-1<s_{k-1}/2
,
\ldots
,
1<s_{1}/2
and that
\{s_{1}\}
is a pinnacle set of a permutation of

[s_{1}]
, which is always true when
1<s_{1}/2
. Now, the

inequalities can be summarized as
s_{i}>2i
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
,
which bears a strong resemblance to the inequalities in Corollary
3.9.1
. Thus, Davis et al.’s characterization of the pinnacle sets of a permutation of

[n]
is very similar but not the same as our characterization of the pinnacle sets of
P_{n}
when

.

4.
New pinnacle sets from old ones

We now present two methods for creating new pinnacle sets from old ones. The first one simply swaps the labels of two vertices while the second one relabels two or more vertices.

4.1.
Switching labels

Suppose that in the labeled graph
(G,\lambda)
,
\lambda(w)=p
is a pinnacle while
\lambda(x)=p^{\prime}
is not a pinnacle and
p<p^{\prime}
.
This means that all the neighbors of
w
have labels smaller than
p
while
x
has a neighbor whose label is larger than
p^{\prime}
.
Swap the labels of
w
and
x
and keep all the other vertices’ labels the same.
Call the new labeling
\lambda^{\prime}
.
Then,

\lambda^{\prime}(w)=p^{\prime}
is a pinnacle of
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
while
\lambda^{\prime}(x)=p
is not.
It is also possible that the status of the other labels changed; i.e., they were not pinnacles in
(G,\lambda)
but became pinnacles in
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
or vice versa.
In the next lemma, we show that when
p^{\prime}=p+1
, such status changes do not occur. For example, if
\{3,6,8,10\}
is a pinnacle set of
G
, then so are
\{4,6,8,10\},\{3,7,8,10\}
and
\{3,6,9,10\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
a pinnacle set of
G
with
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{k}
. Suppose that
p_{i+1}-p_{i}>1
for some
i
,
1\leq i<k
. Then
(P\setminus\{p_{i}\})\cup\{p_{i}+1\}
is also a pinnacle set of
G
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a labeling of
G
so that
P
is the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda)
. Assume
\lambda(w)=p_{i}
and
\lambda(x)=p_{i}+1
. Define
\lambda^{\prime}
as the labeling that swaps the labels of
w
and
x
and keeps the labels of all other vertices fixed. That is,
\lambda^{\prime}(w)=p_{i}+1
,
\lambda^{\prime}(x)=p_{i}
and
\lambda^{\prime}(v)=\lambda(v)
for
v\neq w,x
.
Let
P^{\prime}
denote the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
.
We will now argue that
P^{\prime}=(P\setminus\{p_{i}\})\cup\{p_{i}+1\}
.

Notice that for any vertex
v
that is not
w
nor
x
nor a neighbor of the two vertices, the status of
\lambda(v)=\lambda^{\prime}(v)
in
(G,\lambda)
and
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
are the same. That is,
\lambda(v)\in P
if and only if
\lambda^{\prime}(v)\in P^{\prime}
because the labels of
v
and that of its neighbors are the same in both
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
. So let us consider
w
and its neighbors first and then
x
and its neighbors next.

Since
\lambda(w)=p_{i}\in P
but
\lambda(x)=p_{i}+1\not\in P
, it must be the case that
w
and
x
are not adjacent. It also means that all the neighbors of
w
have
\lambda
-labels smaller than
p_{i}
and are not in
P
. These neighbors’ labels did not change in
\lambda^{\prime}
but
\lambda^{\prime}(w)>\lambda(w)
. Thus,
\lambda^{\prime}(w)=p_{i}+1\in P^{\prime}
and the neighbors’
\lambda^{\prime}
-labels are again not in
P^{\prime}
.

On the other hand,
\lambda(x)=p_{i}+1\not\in P
because some neighbor of
x
has a label larger than
p_{i}+1
. This neighbor’s label did not change in
\lambda^{\prime}
but
\lambda^{\prime}(x)<\lambda(x)
so
\lambda^{\prime}(x)=p_{i}\not\in P^{\prime}
.
Next consider some neighbor
y
of
x
such that
\lambda(y)\in P
. Then
\lambda(y)
is larger than
p_{i}+1
while all of its neighbors have labels that are at most
p_{i}
. But
\lambda^{\prime}(y)=\lambda(y)
and all of its neighbors’
\lambda
-labels did not change in
\lambda^{\prime}
except for
x
where
\lambda^{\prime}(x)<\lambda(x)
. (Note that
w
is not a neighbor of
y
since
\lambda(w)\in P
.) Thus,
\lambda^{\prime}(y)\in P^{\prime}
too.

Finally, suppose some neighbor
z
of
x
has
\lambda(z)\not\in P
, then there are two possibilities: (i)
\lambda(z)<\lambda(x)
or (ii)
\lambda(z)>\lambda(x)
and
z
has another neighbor whose label is larger than
\lambda(z)
. For case (i),
\lambda(z)<p_{i}
because
x
and
w
are not adjacent. Thus,
\lambda(z)=\lambda^{\prime}(z)<\lambda^{\prime}(x)
so
\lambda^{\prime}(z)\not\in P^{\prime}
. For case (ii), since
\lambda(x)=p_{i}+1
, the
\lambda
-labels of
z
and this neighbor are both larger than
p_{i}+1
. Both their labels did not change in
\lambda^{\prime}
so
\lambda^{\prime}(z)\not\in P^{\prime}
.

We have shown that the only change from
P
to
P^{\prime}
is that
p_{i}\not\in P^{\prime}
but
p_{i}+1\in P^{\prime}
. Thus,
P^{\prime}=(P\setminus\{p_{i}\})\cup\{p_{i}+1\}
.
∎

leads us to an interesting fact about pinnacle sets.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices and
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
a pinnacle set of
G
with
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{k}
. Let
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
be another subset of
[n]
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{k}
. When
p_{i}\leq q_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, then
Q
is a pinnacle set of
G
as well.

Proof.

Let
j
be the largest index such that
p_{j}<q_{j}
. It has to be the case that
1\leq j<k
because
p_{k}=q_{k}=n
by Proposition
2.2
.
Thus,
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j},q_{j+1},q_{j+2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
.
Now
p_{j}<q_{j}<q_{j+1}
so
q_{j+1}-p_{j}>1
. According to Lemma
4.1
, it follows that

\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j-1},p_{j}+1,q_{j+1},q_{j+2},\ldots,q_{k}\}

is a pinnacle set of
G
. Applying this idea iteratively implies that the sets

\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j-1},p_{j}+2,q_{j+1},q_{j+2},\ldots,q_{k}\},

\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j-1},p_{j}+3,q_{j+1},q_{j+2},\ldots,q_{k}\},

\vdots

\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j-1},q_{j},q_{j+1},q_{j+2},\ldots,q_{k}\},

are all pinnacle sets of
G
. Repeating the procedure for the remaining indices, it follows that
Q
is a pinnacle set of
G
. ∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
be a pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda)
with
p_{0}\leq p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{k}
and

. Suppose that
p_{i}-p_{i-1}>1
for some

,
1\leq i\leq k
. Let
\lambda(w)=p_{i}
and
\lambda(x)=p_{i}-1
. If

and
x
are not adjacent, then
(P\setminus\{p_{i}\})\cup\{p_{i}-1\}
is a pinnacle set of
G
.

Proof.

Since
p_{i}\in P
, it follows that all the neighbors of
w
have labels smaller than
p_{i}
and are not in
P
.
In fact, their labels are smaller than
p_{i}-1
because
w
and
x
are not adjacent.
On the other hand, since
p_{i}-1\not\in P
,
x
must have a neighbor whose label is larger than
p_{i}-1
. The said label has to be larger
p_{i}
because
w
and
x
are not adjacent. Vertex
x
may also have neighbors whose labels are smaller than
p_{i}-1
.

Define
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
as the label obtained from
\lambda
by swapping the labels of
x
and
w
and let
P^{\prime\prime}
be the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda^{\prime\prime})
.
We note that when
v\neq w,x
nor a neighbor of these two vertices, the status of
\lambda(v)=\lambda^{\prime\prime}(v)
in
P
and
P^{\prime\prime}
are the same.

Consider
w
. Its neighbors’ labels did not change in
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
so they are still smaller that
p_{i}-1
. But
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(w)=p_{i}-1
so
p_{i}-1\in P^{\prime\prime}
and none of
w
’s neighbors’ labels are in
P^{\prime\prime}
. Now
x
has a neighbor whose label is at least
p_{i}+1
and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(x)=p_{i}
so
p_{i}\not\in P^{\prime\prime}
. For the other neighbors
z
of
x
, we now argue their status is the same in
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
. If
\lambda(z)\in P
, then
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z)=\lambda(z)>p_{i}
and will not be affected by the change in label of

(and
w
) so it will be in
P^{\prime\prime}
. If
\lambda(z)\not\in P
, then there are two possibilities: (i)
\lambda(z)<\lambda(x)
or (ii)
\lambda(z)>\lambda(x)
and
z
has a neighbor whose label is larger than
\lambda(z)
. For case (i), since
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z)=\lambda(z)
but
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(x)>\lambda(x)
, it follows that
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z)\not\in P^{\prime\prime}
. For case (ii),
\lambda(z)
and its neighbor had
\lambda
-labels larger than

. Their labels did not change in

. It follows that
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z)=\lambda(z)\not\in P^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, the pinnacle set of

is

.

4.2.
Using ordered tree partitions of a graph

We begin by introducing the notion of ordered tree partitions of a graph and some of its properties. We will use it in Theorem
4.10
to relabel vertices so that we can derive a new pinnacle set from an old one.

Let
I=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\}
be the independent set of
G
so that the labels
\lambda(v_{i})
,
i=1,2,\ldots,k
are exactly the pinnacles of
(G,\lambda)
.
Intuitively, this means we can partition
G
into
k
parts, where the
i
th part is the “region of influence” of
v_{i}\in I
. That is, if
u
is in this
i
th part, then there is a path from
u
to
v_{i}
so that the labels of the vertices along the path are increasing. Thus,
v_{i}
’s region of influence is a connected subgraph of
G
and
\lambda(v_{i})
is the only pinnacle in that region. We shall describe this region by a tree
T_{i}
rooted at
v_{i}
and call the sequence of trees
(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k})
with some extra conditions an
ordered tree partition of
G
. We define the structure of an ordered tree partition of
G
without a labeling first.

The reader should be aware that when we discuss the relationship between two vertices
u
and
v
, there are two contexts: their relationship in
G
and their relationship in the ordered tree partition of
G
. When the context is
G
, we will say
u
is
adjacent
to
v
or
u
is
neighbors
with
v
, etc. On the other hand, when the context is a rooted tree in the ordered tree partition, we will say
u
is a
child
of
v
or
u
is a
parent
of
v
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph. An
ordered tree partition
of
G
is a sequence
\mathcal{T}=(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k})
of subgraphs of
G
where each
T_{i}
is a tree rooted at
r_{i}
so that

(i)

\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}\}
is an independent set of
G

(ii)

(V(T_{1}),V(T_{2}),\ldots,V(T_{k}))
is a partition of
V(G)
and

(iii)

when
u
is neighbors in
G
with at least one vertex in
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}\}
, then
u
is a child of
r_{h}
with
h=\min\{i:\mbox{$u$ is adjacent to $r_{i}$ in $G$}\}
.

See Figures
7
and
8
for some examples. We emphasize that the ordering of the trees in
\mathcal{T}
is important because of condition (iii). The next proposition states some of its implications.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k})
be an ordered tree partition of
G
, and let
v\in V(T_{i})
with
v\neq r_{i}
.
When
v
is a child of
r_{i}
, then
v
is not adjacent to
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{i-1}
in
G
.
On the other hand, when
v
is not a child of
r_{i}
, then
v
is not adjacent to
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}
in
G
.

Lemma 4.8

Let
\lambda

For

\lambda(r_{i})=q_{i}

(1)

If

be a neighbor of

\lambda(v)

\lambda(v)

(G,\lambda)

(2)

Otherwise, let

and set

.

This

G
with exactly

are the only vertices with no parents. For

, let

denote the tree whose root is

.

Since
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}\}
contains all the vertices whose labels are pinnacles of
(G,\lambda)

G
. Moreover, because every vertex not in
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}\}

(V(T_{1}),V(T_{2}),

\ldots,V(T_{k}))

Next, consider an arbitrary tree

and

. By transitivity, every vertex in

q_{i}

\sum_{j=1}^{i}|V(T_{j})|\leq q_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. ∎

5.
The pinnacle posets of a graph

Inspired by Theorem
4.2
, we define a partial ordering (poset) on the pinnacle sets of a graph with the same size. Let
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
contain all the size-
k
pinnacle sets of the graph
G
.
For ease of discussion, we assume that the elements of a pinnacle set are ordered from smallest to largest.
For any two sets
P,Q\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
with
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
, define the relation
\preceq
as follows:
P\preceq Q
if and only if
p_{i}\leq q_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
.
It is easy to check that
\preceq
is reflexive, anti-symmetric and transitive so
\mathcal{P}(G,k)=(\mathsf{Pin}(G,k),\preceq)
is a partial order. We note that Davis et al. (
[
8
]
, Section 4) suggested a similar poset for the pinnacle sets of a permutation but did not explore it.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(D,\leq)
be a finite poset and
d\in D
. We say that
d
is a
maximal element
of
\mathcal{D}
if there is no
e\in D
such that
d\leq e
and
d\neq e
. Additionally, if
d
is the
only
maximal element of
\mathcal{D}
or, equivalently, for every
c\in D
,
c\leq d
,
then
d
is the
maximum element
of
\mathcal{D}
. Similarly,
d
is a
minimal element
of
\mathcal{D}
if there is no
c\in D
such that
c\leq d
and
c\neq d
? Futhermore, if
d
is the
only
minimal element of
\mathcal{D}
or, equivalently, for every
e\in D
,
d\leq e
, then
d
is the
minimum element
of
\mathcal{D}
. Below, we show that when
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
, the poset
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a maximum element.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices. Assume
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a maximum element: the set
M_{n,k}=\{n-k+1,n-k+2,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

When
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
,
G
has a pinnacle set of size
k
. According to Theorem
2.5
,
M_{n,k}
is one of them. Furthermore, for any pinnacle set
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
,
p_{k}\leq n,p_{k-1}\leq n-1,\ldots,p_{1}\leq n-k+1
. That is,
P\preceq M_{n,k}
. Thus,
M_{n,k}
is the maximum element of
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
.
∎

The poset
\mathcal{D}=(D,\leq)
is a
join semilattice
if for any two elements
c,d\in D
, their
least upper bound
or
join
exists. Similarly, if for
c,d\in D
, their
greatest lower bound
or
meet
exists, then
\mathcal{D}
is a
meet semilattice
. We denote the join and meet of
c
and
d
as
c\lor d
and
c\land d
respectively.

When
\mathcal{D}
is both a join and meet semilattice, then
\mathcal{D}
is a
lattice
. Finally,
\mathcal{D}
is a
distributive lattice
if for any three element
c,d,e\in D
,
c\land(d\lor e)=(c\land d)\lor(c\land e)
or, equivalently,
c\lor(d\land e)=(c\lor d)\land(c\lor e)
. For example, for any set
A
, its power set
\mathcal{P}(A)
forms a distributive lattice under the subset relation. For
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(A)
,
A_{1}\lor A_{2}=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
while
A_{1}\land A_{2}=A_{1}\cap A_{2}
. The fact that the union and intersection operations distribute over each other is why
(\mathcal{P}(A),\subseteq)
a distributive lattice and not just a lattice. We are now ready for our next result.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices. Assume
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a join semilattice. For any two pinnacle sets
P,Q\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
with
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
, their join is
P\lor Q=\{\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}:i=1,2,\ldots,k\}.

Proof.

Let
P,Q\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
with
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
. Define
\max(P,Q)
as the set
\{\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\},i=1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Let us argue that
\max(P,Q)
is also a size-
k
pinnacle set of
G
.
For
i=1,2,\ldots,k-1
,
p_{i}<p_{i+1}
and
q_{i}<q_{i+1}
by definition. If
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}=p_{i}
then
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}<p_{i+1}\leq\max\{p_{i+1},q_{i+1}\}
. Similarly, if
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}=q_{i}
then
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}<q_{i+1}\leq\max\{p_{i+1},q_{i+1}\}
. Thus,
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}<\max\{p_{i+1},q_{i+1}\}
which implies that the sequence
(\max\{p_{1},q_{1}\},\max\{p_{2},q_{2}\},\ldots,\max\{p_{k},q_{k}\})
is strictly increasing. That is, no two elements of
\max(P,Q)
are equal so
\max(P,Q)
has size
k
. Furthermore,
every
element of
\max(P,Q)
is from
P\cup Q
so
\max(P,Q)
is a size-
k
subset of
[n]
. Now,
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
,
p_{i}\leq\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}
. From Theorem
4.2
, since
P
is a size-
k
pinnacle set of
G
, so is
\max(P,Q)
.

We have established that
\max(P,Q)\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
. Clearly,
P\preceq\max(P,Q)
and
Q\preceq\max(P,Q)
so
\max(P,Q)
is an upper bound of
P
and
Q
. Now, suppose
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}\}\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
is another upper bound of
P
and
Q
.
It follows that for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
,
\max\{p_{i},q_{i}\}\leq s_{i}
. Consequently,
\max(P,Q)\preceq S
and
P\lor Q=\max(P,Q)
.
That is,
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a join semilattice.
∎

Let us refer to the minimal elements of
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
as the
bottom size-
k
pinnacle sets of
G
. A natural question to ask is: when
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
, is
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
a meet semilattice too? Figure
10
implies the answer is no. It shows a graph that has two bottom size-3 pinnacle sets.
Nonetheless, there are exceptions. Figure
11
displays the posets of size-
k
pinnacles sets of the path
P_{9}
for
k=2
,
3
and
4
. All of them have minimum elements. Corollaries
3.6.1
and
3.9.1
imply that the posets of the size-
k
pinnacle sets of cycles and paths have minimum elements provided
\mathsf{Pin}(C_{n},k)
and
\mathsf{Pin}(P_{n},k)
are non-empty.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 2
be positive integers such that

. Then
\{3,5,7,\ldots,2k-1,n\}
is the minimum pinnacle set of
\mathcal{P}(C_{n},k)
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 2
be positive integers such that
n\geq 2k-1
. Then
\{2,4,\ldots,2k-2,n\}
is the minimum pinnacle set of
\mathcal{P}(P_{n},k)
.

Indeed, when
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a minimum element, we can say something even stronger about the poset’s structure.

Corollary 5.4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices. Assume
\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
. If
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a minimum element, then
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a distributive lattice. For any two pinnacle sets
P,Q\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
with
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
, their meet is
P\land Q=\{\min\{p_{i},q_{i}\}:i=1,2,\ldots,k\}.

Proof.

Again, let
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
be size-
k
pinnacle sets of
G
. Let
\min(P,Q)=\{\min\{p_{i},q_{i}\},i=1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Similar to the proof of Theorem
5.2
, it is straightforward to argue that
\min(P,Q)
is a size-
k
subset of
[n]
. Assume
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a minimum element, say
B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
. By definition,
B\preceq P
and
B\preceq Q
so
b_{i}\leq\min\{p_{i},q_{i}\}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Since
B
is a size-
k
pinnacle set of
G
, by Theorem
4.2
,
\min(P,Q)
is also a size-
k
pinnacle set of
G
.

By definition,
\min(P,Q)\preceq P
and
\min(P,Q)\preceq Q
so
\min(P,Q)
is a lower bound of
P
and
Q
. If some
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}\}\in\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)
has
S\preceq P
and
S\preceq Q
, then
s_{i}\leq\min\{p_{i},q_{i}\}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
so
S\preceq\min(P,Q)
. Thus,
P\land Q=\min(P,Q)
and
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a meet semilattice. Together with Theorem
5.2
, we conclude that
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a lattice.

Finally, to prove that
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is actually a distributive lattice, we need to show that for any three pinnacle sets
P,Q,S
in
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
,
P\land(Q\lor S)=(P\land Q)\lor(P\land S)
. It is equivalent to showing that
\min(P,\max(Q,S))=\max(\min(P,Q),\min(P,S))
, which in turn is equivalent to showing that for

\min\{p_{i},\max\{q_{i},s_{i}\}\}=\max\{\min\{p_{i},q_{i}\},\min\{p_{i},s_{i}\}\}

and
s_{i}
are integers and the
\max

\min

the last equality has to be true. Therefore,
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
is a distributive lattice. ∎

Minimum pinnacle sets

The structure of
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
implies that the bottom size-
k
pinnacle sets of
G
play an important role. Once determined, we know
all
of the size-
k
pinnacle sets of
G
. That is, every size-
k
subset
P
of
[n]
is a pinnacle set of
G
if and only if there is some bottom size-
k
pinnacle set
B
of
G
such that
B\preceq P\preceq M_{n,k}
. The characterization is even more succinct when
G
has a
unique
bottom size-
k
pinnacle set, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a minimum element. Below, we present two such cases. For the first case,
G
has
k
connected components. By Corollary
2.5.2
, the smallest-sized pinnacle set of
G
is also
k
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Suppose
G
has
n
vertices and
k
connected components. Let the sizes of these components be
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{k}
respectively with
h_{1}\leq h_{2}\leq\cdots\leq h_{k}
. Then
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
where
p_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}h_{j}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
is the minimum element of
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
.

Proof.

Let
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}
be the connected components of
G
where
|V(H_{i})|=h_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. As stated in the theorem, let
h_{1}\leq h_{2}\leq\cdots\leq h_{k}
. For each
H_{i}
, pick a vertex
r_{i}
and run the breadth-first search (BFS) algorithm starting at
r_{i}
. Let
T_{i}
denote the resulting BFS-tree rooted at
r_{i}
. By construction, all the neighbors of
r_{i}
in
H_{i}
will be its children in
T_{i}
. It is straightforward to check that
(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k})
is an ordered tree partition of
G
. Consequently, by Lemma
4.9
,
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
is a pinnacle set of
G
.

Let
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots q_{k}
be a pinnacle set of
G
. Let
\lambda^{\prime}
be a labeling of
G
so that
Q
is the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
.
We will now argue that
P\preceq Q
. Let
w_{i}
be the vertex such that
\lambda^{\prime}(w_{i})=q_{i}
. Let
F_{i}
be the connected component that contains
w_{i}
, and let its size be
f_{i}
. Since there are
k
components and the pinnacle set of
(G,\lambda^{\prime})
has size
k
, it has to be the case that
q_{i}
is the largest label assigned by
\lambda^{\prime}
to the vertices in
F_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. In fact, we can say something stronger – because
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{i}
, the largest label assigned by
\lambda^{\prime}
to all the vertices in
\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}F_{j}
is
q_{i}
. Thus,
\sum_{j=1}^{i}f_{j}\leq q_{i}
.

But
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{k}
is a reordering of
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}
and the sizes of the latter are ordered from smallest to largest. It follows that
\sum_{j=1}^{i}h_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}f_{j}
because the
i
smallest components of
G
have sizes
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{i}
respectively. Hence

5.2.
Counting distinct
k
-size pinnacle sets.

Let
\mathsf{pin}(G,k)
be the number of size-
k
pinnacle sets of
G
; i.e.,
\mathsf{pin}(G,k)=|\mathsf{Pin}(G,k)|
. We show how to compute
\mathsf{pin}(G,k)
when
G
has a unique bottom size-
k
pinnacle set.

Theorem 5.8
.

If
B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
is only the bottom pinnacle of size
k
of the graph
G
, then

\mathsf{pin}(G,k)=\sum_{i_{k-1}=b_{k-1}}^{b_{k}-1}\cdots\sum_{i_{2}=b_{2}}^{i_{3}-1}\sum_{i_{1}=b_{1}}^{i_{2}-1}1

is the total number of distinct pinnacle sets of size
k
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.1
,
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{j-1},i,b_{j+1},\dots,b_{k}\}
is also a pinnacle set where
i\in[b_{j},b_{j+1}-1]
. Notice that every time
b_{j}
is incremented by 1 we obtain a new pinnacle set of size
k
. In order to count the total number of distinct pinnacle sets of size
k
, we let
i_{j}=b_{j}
for
1\leq j<k
and proceed to increment
i_{1}
until
i_{1}=i_{2}-1
. We select
i_{t}
such that
i_{t}
can be incremented by at least 1 and
t
is as small as possible. When
i_{t}
is incremented by 1, we reset
i_{j}=b_{j}
for
1\leq j<t
. We then repeat the process of incrementing the smallest
i
such that
i
can be incremented. This iterative process is repeated until
i_{k-1}=b_{k}-1,i_{k-2}=i_{k-1}-1,\dots,i_{1}=i_{2}-1
. This yields the total number of distinct pinnacle sets of size
k
along with the following summation as desired:

\sum_{i_{k-1}=b_{k-1}}^{b_{k}-1}\cdots\sum_{i_{2}=b_{2}}^{i_{3}-1}\sum_{i_{1}=b_{1}}^{i_{2}-1}1.\qed

As an application of Theorem

.

Proof.

Assume that for all
\ell\leq n

\displaystyle\mathsf{pin}(C_{n+1},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{k-1}=2k-1}^{(n+1)-1}\sum_{i_{k-2}=2k-3}^{i_{k-1}-1}\cdots\sum_{i_{2}=5}^{i_{3}-1}\sum_{i_{1}=3}^{i_{2}-1}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{k-1}=2k-1}^{n}\sum_{i_{k-2}=2k-3}^{i_{k-1}-1}\cdots\sum_{i_{2}=5}^{i_{3}-1}\sum_{i_{1}=3}^{i_{2}-1}1

6.
Future work

Given our introduction of the pinnacle sets of a graph along with their orderly structure, much remains unknown about these sets. In this section we present some directions for further study.

Having characterized when a graph has a pinnacle set of a given size, an initial natural question is:

Question 6.1
.

Given a graph and a graph operation, how does the size of a pinnacle set change?

We provide a result when we delete an edge of a graph.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
S
is a pinnacle set of
G
, then for any edge
e
of
G
, the pinnacle set
T
of
G-\{e\}
satisfies
|S|\leq|T|\leq|S|+1
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary edge
e=(u,v)
of
G
with
u
labeled
a
and
v
labeled
b
, and assume without loss of generality that
a<b
.
Then, either both
a
and
b
are non-pinnacles, or exactly one of them is a pinnacle.
In the first case, as
a<b
, we must have a vertex
w
adjacent to
v
with label
x
satisfying
x>b
, which makes
v
a non-pinnacle. Deleting edge
e
, means that
b
remains a non-pinnacle as its corresponding vertex
v
is still adjacent to vertex
w
with label
x
. However, label
a
may have now become a pinnacle. This implies that
|S|\leq|T|\leq|S|+1
, as desired.
In the second case, we assume
b
is a pinnacle. Then deleting edge
e
continues to ensure that
b
is a pinnacle. Once again label
a
may have now become a pinnacle. This again implies that
|S|\leq|T|\leq|S|+1
, as desired.
∎

One might begin this study by considering the graph operations of subdividing an edge, edge contraction, and the join of two graphs.

In Theorem
5.8
we gave a formula for the number of pinnacle sets of size
k
provided there is a unique bottom pinnacle set.
However, as we showed in Figure
10
not all graphs have a unique bottom pinnacle set, and hence Theorem
5.8
does not hold. So the following questions remain:

Question 6.2
.

What must be true of
G
and
k
such that
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has a unique bottom pinnacle set of size
k
?

Or more generally:

Question 6.3
.

Fix
b\geq 1
. What must be true of
G
and
k
such that
\mathcal{P}(G,k)
has precisely
b
bottom pinnacle sets of size
k
? In this case, how many distinct pinnacle sets of size
k
does
G
have?

One place to begin this study is to consider the bottom pinnacle set(s) of trees and determining if there is a unique bottom element. Another consideration is determining graph families that have many bottom pinnacle sets.
We can also ask questions regarding the number of labelings with a given pinnacle set.

Question 6.4
.

Given a graph
G
with
n
vertices and a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, how many labelings of
G
have pinnacles set
S
?

We conclude by answering this question for a cycle graph on
n
vertices and when
S
consists of the largest
k
integers in
[n]
. To formally state this result we recall that a
composition
of an integer
s
with
t
parts is an ordered
t
-tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{t})
with strictly positive integer entries whose coordinate sum equals
s
. Namely
a_{i}>0
for all
i\in[t]
and
\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{t}=s
.
Let
\mathrm{comps}(s,t)
denote the set of compositions of
s
into
t
parts.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
k\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and let
S=[n-k+1,n]
be a size
k
pinnacle set of the cycle graph
C_{n}
.
Then the number of labelings of
C_{n}
with pinnacle set
S
is given by

(k-1)!\cdot n\cdot 2^{n-2k}\cdot\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathrm{comps}(n-k,k)}\binom{n-k}{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}}.

Proof.

As in Figure
4
, order the vertices
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
C_{n}
clockwise with
v_{n}
placed at the top of the circle, and followed by
v_{1}
.
We know that pinnacles cannot be adjacent, so we first select the position at which the pinnacles will be placed. To begin we position the pinnacle
n
, at the vertex
v_{n}
.
To know where the remainder of the pinnacles lie, it is equivalent to know the number of vertices between any two pinnacles. Since there are
k
pinnacles, there are
k
sets of vertices who lie completely between any two pinnacles. We call those sets of vertices lying between two pinnacles the set of gaps.

Moreover, since there are
k
pinnacles, there are
n-k
non-pinnacle values to place in those gaps. Each composition of
n-k
with exactly
k
parts gives all of the possible sizes of the gaps, and the way of selecting non-pinnacle numbers to place within the gaps is given by a multinomial.
Thus the number of ways of selecting the size of the gaps and selecting among the non-pinnacle numbers to fill those gaps with is given by

\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathrm{comps}(n-k,k)}\binom{n-k}{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}}.

Now for each composition
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})
of
n-k
and the choice of what non-pinnacle numbers go into each gap, we must arrange these numbers so as to not create any pinnacles within the gaps.
For each gap we claim this can be done in
2^{a_{i}-1}
ways.
To see this, notice that since the
a_{i}
numbers are all distinct, you can place the smallest number at the
j
th vertex on that path of vertices (where
1\leq j\leq a_{i}
). Then you must select
j-1
numbers to place to the left and order them in decreasing order. This can be done in
\binom{a_{i}-1}{j-1}
ways.
The remaining numbers must be placed to the right of where we placed the smallest value and all placed in increasing order. There is only one way to do this. Now we must account for all of the possible places we can put the smallest number, hence the total number of ways to arrange the
a_{i}
numbers so as to not create any pinnacles is given by

\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}}\binom{a_{i}-1}{j-1}=2^{a_{i}-1}

as claimed.

Once we know what numbers go in each gap, arranging the numbers in each gap so as to not create any pinnacles can be done independently. Hence, for any composition, the total number of ways to arrange the non-pinnacle values is given by

\prod_{i=1}^{k}2^{a_{i}-1}=2^{n-2k}.

To complete the proof we note that we can permute the position of the pinnacles, not including the pinnacle
n
, which we anchored at vertex
v_{n}
. This can then be done in
(k-1)!
ways.
To conclude we note that we can rotate the labeling in order to account for all possible locations of where to place
n
within the cycle graph. This can be done in
n
ways.
Taking these two factors into account gives the final count for the total number of labelings of
C_{n}
with pinnacle set
[n-k+1,n]
:

(k-1)!\cdot n\cdot 2^{n-2k}\cdot\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathrm{comps}(n-k,k)}\binom{n-k}{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}}.\qed
