A General Framework for Importance Sampling with Latent Markov Processes
Cheng-Der Fuh, Yanwei Jia, Steven Kou

Abstract
Although stochastic models driven by latent Markov processes are widely used, the classical importance sampling method based on the exponential tilting method for these models suffers from the difficulty of computing the eigenvalue and associated eigenfunction and the plausibility of the indirect asymptotic large deviation regime for the variance of the estimator. We propose a general importance sampling framework that twists the observable and latent processes separately based on a link function that directly minimizes the estimator’s variance. An optimal choice of the link function is chosen within the locally asymptotically normal family. We show the logarithmic efficiency of the proposed estimator under the asymptotic normal regime. As applications, we estimate an overflow probability under a pandemic model and the CoVaR, a measurement of the co-dependent financial systemic risk. Both applications are beyond the scope of traditional importance sampling methods due to their nonlinear structures.

Introduction

Stochastic models with latent processes, in which the increments of the processes of interest (observed processes) often
depend on other possibly unobserved processes

, are ubiquitous. The latent process usually describes the random environment in which the processes of interest evolve.
For example, when studying a firm whose revenue depends on the business cycle fluctuation, the revenue is the process of interest while the macroeconomic state (expansion or contraction) is latent;
when estimating the risk measure of a portfolio, the portfolio value, as the process of interest, may be affected by the volatilities of each asset, which are latent.
Other examples with latent variables include production mode in supply chain and inventory management and unobserved sub-populations during a pandemic; see, e.g.,
Tang et al. 2008

We are interested in simulating events with small probabilities, e.g., default and overflow events, for such models and computing the related probabilities. Calculating these small probabilities is indispensable to risk management and guiding public policy issues.

Due to the complexity of such stochastic models, an analytical calculation of such probabilities is often unavailable, and one needs to apply simulation methods.

Simulation-based methods have been widely used in derivative pricing
(
Glasserman et al. 1999
,
Haugh and Kogan 2004
)
, portfolio evaluation
(
Glasserman et al. 2002
,
Haugh et al. 2006
)
.
For a general review of the applications in financial engineering, see
Haugh 2003
,
Glasserman 2004
. Applications in other domains include inventory planning and routing
(
Cáceres-Cruz et al. 2012

, and epidemiological analysis

.

However, plain Monte Carlo methods are inefficient in computing small probabilities because the estimator’s variance gradually dominates its mean, and many repeated trials are needed to achieve acceptable accuracy. Consequently, importance sampling based on exponential tilting was proposed by
Siegmund 1976
as a variance reduction technique in rare-event simulation for the case of independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables.
3
3

3

The idea of importance sampling appeared even earlier, e.g., in
Ulam et al. 1947
,
Kahn and Harris 1951
.
The basic idea is to sample under an alternative probability distribution by changing the probability measure; see, e.g.,
Asmussen and Glynn 2007
for a comprehensive introduction.

The research question of this paper is: Can we design a general importance sampling algorithm to do rare-event simulation for stochastic models with latent processes?
For tractability, we shall restrict to Markovian systems and attempt to provide a general importance sampling framework that is tractable and easy to implement for stochastic models with latent Markov processes, known as Markov random walks. Moreover, we are especially interested in the event of a moderately small probability
(
10^{-2}
to
10^{-4}
) that is more common in finance, economics, hypothesis testing, and epidemic applications. The basic idea is to enlarge the searching space of the parameterized sampling distribution by tilting the latent and observed processes separately and using a suitably chosen link function.

The simulation time for estimating a moderately small probability is crucial for many applications
(
Glasserman et al. 2002
,
Fuh et al. 2011
)
. For example, a financial firm needs to evaluate the risk profile associated with
thousands portfolio positions on a daily basis, to meet the stress-testing requirement of Basel regulation,
while the probabilities of interest are often moderately small.
Hence, even a moderate improvement in either variance reduction or computational time in each simulation can have a huge impact because the number of simulations needed can be massive.

Motivation and Our Contribution.

A central issue in importance sampling is how to choose the change of probability measures optimally, especially for complex stochastic processes.
For general Markov random walks,

Collamore 2002
proposes a method to approximate the optimal change of measure by doing an exponential tilting method based on the large deviation (LD)
principle; he proves that the asymptotically optimal variance reduction is achieved by using LD-based tilting as the probability of the rare event approaches zero.
See
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
,
Ney and Nummelin 1987b
for the LD principle for Markov chains and Markov random walks.

However, two challenges remain. (1) Since the classical LD-based tilting only aims at maximizing the exponential decay rate of the variance within a parameterized exponential family of tilting probabilities, not the original variance or asymptotic variance, the titling measure given by the LD may not be close to the optimal one for the moderately small (
10^{-2}
to
10^{-4}
) probabilities.
(2) It is generally difficult to calculate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions required by the exponential tilting method.
If the latent Markov process has finite state space, one needs to find the eigenvalue of a (non-negative) matrix.
However, it becomes a functional eigenvalue problem in infinite-dimensional space when the underlying latent Markov process has continuous general state space.
So far, only affine processes are known to admit log-affine eigenfunctions analytically
(
Zhang et al. 2015
)
.

More precisely, the contribution of the paper is twofold.
First, in terms of theory, we propose a duo-exponential tilting framework for general stochastic models with latent Markov processes that can be described as Markov random walks. This tilting family aims to minimize the estimator’s variance directly (instead of only the exponential decay rate of the variance) within a parameterized duo-exponential family. Our simple idea is to change the distribution of
the underlying process and observed process
separately
through exponential embedding and a link function,
instead of changing the two distributions
jointly
as in the classical LD-based tilting methods. The proposed tilting family is large enough to include classical exponential tilting (e.g.,
Collamore 2002
) as its special case; this connection implies that, in theory, the variance reduction of our methods should be as good as existing methods.

We provide an asymptotically optimal link function under the local asymptotic normal (LAN) family.In particular, the asymptotically optimal link function is related to the derivative of the eigenfunction in the classical exponential tilting algorithms at only one point (zero), and there is no need to find the whole eigenfunction. We show the logarithmic efficiency of the proposed estimator under the asymptotic normal regime. We also discuss ways to choose the link function outside the LAN family.
Numerical examples show the proposed method performs better than existing importance sampling algorithms.

4

Besides the simulation literature mentioned before, this paper is also related to two strands of other literature.
First, there is a strand of literature on moderate deviation rare event simulations, which also
aims to minimize the estimator’s variance directly instead of only the exponential decay rate of the variance.
See
Do and Hall 1991
,
Fuh and Hu 2004
,
Fuh et al. 2011
for i.i.d. random variables, and by
Andradóttir et al. 1995
,
Andradóttir et al. 1996
,
Fuh and Hu 2007
for finite-state Markov chains. They are special cases of our general framework because we consider Markov random walk with latent processes.
Second, when searching for the desirable tilting parameters, our algorithm minimizes the estimator’s variance by using a pre-sampling stochastic approximation algorithm. The stochastic approximation has been used in importance sampling for some special Markov processes; see, e.g.,

Egloff et al. 2005
,
Ahamed et al. 2006
,
Kawai 2009
.
We complement this strand of literature by studying general state space Markov processes and treating the searching step as a separate stage rather than an adaptive procedure.

Second, in terms of application, the generality of our family enables us to study complicated nonlinear problems to which the current exponential titling method cannot be applied.
(1) We apply our method to a compartmental pandemic model (SIR model, short for susceptible-infectious-removed, cf.,
Allen 2017
) to estimate the overflow probability of hospitalized population, where the evolution of the (observed) quantity of interest
(infectious) also depends on other latent processes (susceptible) in a nonlinear way. Due to the nonlinear structure, usual eigenfunction-based importance sampling techniques are not applicable, while our method is flexible to adapt to this model and achieves significant variance reduction. (2) We apply our method to compute the conditional value-at-risk (CoVaR) in
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
under the vector autoregression model with generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (VAR-GARCH), aimed at studying the co-dependent systemic risk motivated by the 2007-2008 financial crisis. In the VAR-GARCH model, the profit/return process is observed; while the innovation process is
latent.
Due to the conditioning and multivariate feature, the joint event may not be in the standard form of large deviation theory. However, since the model is within the LAN family, we can provide the asymptotic optimal link function for importance sampling. To the best of our knowledge, no other literature addresses the importance sampling techniques for the two applications.

Managerial Insights.

This paper yields both methodological and policy insights. The methodological insight is twofold.
First, for complicated models (e.g., SIR and VAR-GARCH), we are not aware of existing importance sampling methods, but our method can deal with these complex problems.
Second, for simple models (e.g., Heston’s model), compared with various existing importance sampling methods, our improvement in terms of efficiency ratio is significant.
Furthermore, our method is more convenient as it does not rely on the state-dependent change of measure, and there is no need to compute the whole eigenfunction.

The policy insight is also twofold.
First, our pandemic model simulation suggests that the infected population’s overflow probability is sensitive to the transmission rate.
Even one basis point change can affect the probability dramatically. It implies any marginal effort to limit the transmission may have large impacts,
and calls for a more accurate estimation of such parameters to better evaluate the risk associated with a pandemic.
Second, our CoVaR simulation confirms the empirical finding in
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
that VaR and CoVaR have no significant relationship cross-sectionally at a fixed quantile level. Hence institutions’ exposure to systemic risk due to institutional connections may not align with individual risk measures.
Our study points to a new observation regarding tail dependence: Each institution’s VaR and CoVaR may follow a similar trend with respect to the level of risk, but such tread varies across different institutions.
This suggests that an individual-specific factor could approximate the dependence of the system well.

We discuss the simulation efficiency in terms of a modified definition of logarithm efficiency, and provide an example to demonstrate the benefit of the proposed duo-exponential tilting in Section

2
Basic Setting and Review of the Classical Exponential Titling

2.1
The framework of Markov random walks

In this subsection, we describe a general framework
of a discrete-time dynamic system with latent Markov process, called the Markov random walk, cf.

Ney and Nummelin 1987a
and
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
. The observable process is a random walk:
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where the distribution of increment is governed by a latent Markov process

\{X_{n},n\geq 0\}
on a general state space
\mathcal{X}
, with transition probability kernel
p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})
. For
A\subset\mathcal{X},B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{n}=x)=\int_{A}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),\ \mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}\in B|X_{n}=x,X_{n+1}=x^{\prime})=\int_{B}\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y,

where
\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})
is the conditional probability density of
Y_{n+1}
given
(X_{n},X_{n+1})
with respect to
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then
\{(X_{n},S_{n}),n\geq 0\}
is still a Markov process with transition probability kernel

(X_{n+1},S_{n+1})\in(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}s^{\prime})|X_{n}=x,S_{n}=s\sim p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\rho(s^{\prime}-s|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}.

(1)

The problem of interest is to estimate the expectation of a functional with an initial distribution
Law(X_{0},S_{0})=\nu\otimes\delta_{0}
(or
\delta_{x}\otimes\delta_{0}
, where
\otimes
stands for the product measure), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[F(S_{\tau})]\ (\text{ or }\mathbb{E}_{x}[F(S_{\tau})]),

(2)

where
\nu
is an initial distribution of the Markov chain
\{X_{n},n\geq 0\}
,
F
is a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\tau
is a stopping time with respect to the natural filtration of
\{(X_{n},S_{n}),n\geq 0\}
. In this paper,
\tau
is assumed to be a first passage time or a fixed constant, and
F(S_{\tau})
is called “the event of interest”.

In most applications, the problem of interest depends only on the observed process (random walk)
S_{n}
, e.g., the events
S_{n}>b
(a
fixed-time event
) or

\tau_{b}<T
(a
first-passage-time event
), where
\tau_{b}=\inf\{n\geq 0:S_{n}>b\}
. These two quantities are related to overflow probability or bankruptcy. We use these two events to
illustrate our theory, though our framework applies to more general events.
Although
X_{n}
may not explicitly relate to the objective, it still governs the evolution of the quantities of interest.
Thus, we call
X_{n}
a “latent process” and
S_{n}
the “observed process” because it is directly relevant to the quantity of interest.
For instance,
X_{n}
can be an hidden regime that affects the quantities of interest or an adjoin process like the volatility process in computing VaR.

2.2
Review of the classical exponential tilting family

Let
X_{n}
be the latent process (underlying Markov chain), and
Y_{n}
be the observed process (additive part).
Consider the eigenvalues–eigenfunctions framework in
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
.
More precisely, we define an operator as

(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}g)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{\alpha^{\top}Y_{1}}g(X_{1})]=\int p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})g(x^{\prime})\int e^{\alpha^{\top}y}\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y=\int g(x^{\prime})e^{\psi(x,x^{\prime},\alpha)}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),

(3)

where
g(x)
is a function the operator applies on;
\top
denotes transpose, and
\psi(x,x^{\prime},\alpha)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\alpha^{\top}Y_{1}}|X_{0}=x,X_{1}=x^{\prime}]=\log\int e^{\alpha^{\top}y}\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y
is the conditional cumulant generating function of the additive part, given the state
(x,x^{\prime})
. Here
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the same dimension as the observed variable
Y_{1}
and

\alpha^{\top}Y_{1}
is the inner product.
Under some regularity conditions to be specified in Section
3
, let
e^{\Lambda(\alpha)}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
, and
r(\cdot,\alpha)
be the associated positive right eigenfunction
5
5

5

More references on the properties of eigenvalues and related Perron-Frobenius theory can be found in
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
,
Ney and Nummelin 1987b
,
Glynn 1994
,
Glynn and Desai 2023
.
such that

E_{x}[e^{\alpha^{\top}Y_{1}}r(X_{1},\alpha)]=e^{\Lambda(\alpha)}r(x,\alpha).

(4)

Following
Collamore 2002
,
we can define the induced tilting probability
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\frac{r(X_{n},\alpha)}{r(X_{0},\alpha)}e^{\alpha^{\top}S_{n}-n\Lambda(\alpha)}.

(5)

Under the new probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
in (
5
),
the transition probability is

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}|x,s)=\frac{r(x^{\prime},\alpha)}{r(x,\alpha)}e^{-\Lambda(\alpha)+\alpha^{\top}(s^{\prime}-s)}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\rho(s^{\prime}-s|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime},

(6)

and an unbiased estimator of (
2
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}}\bigg[F(S_{\tau})\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}\frac{r(X_{i-1},\alpha)}{r(X_{i},\alpha)}e^{-\alpha^{\top}Y_{i}+\Lambda(\alpha)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}}\bigg[F(S_{\tau})\frac{r(X_{0},\alpha)}{r(X_{\tau},\alpha)}e^{-\alpha^{\top}S_{\tau}+\tau\Lambda(\alpha)}\bigg].

Under the tilting probability

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|X_{n}=x\sim\frac{r(x^{\prime},\alpha)}{r(x,\alpha)}e^{-\Lambda(\alpha)+\psi(x,x^{\prime},\alpha)}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}y|X_{n}=x,X_{n+1}=x^{\prime}\sim e^{\alpha^{\top}y-\psi(x,x^{\prime},\alpha)}\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y.

A distinct feature is that the exponential tilting family is characterized by one parameter

\alpha
.
In other words, the change of transition probability of the underlying Markov process and the conditional probability is indexed by only one parameter
\alpha
.

A widely used way to select the tilting parameter is based on the large deviation (LD) principle
(
Collamore 2002
)
, denoted by
\alpha_{LD}
. More precisely, the probability of interest is embedded in a regime where
F(S_{\tau})
converges to a constant when a particular parameter tends to infinity (e.g.,
\frac{S_{n}}{n}
converges due to the law of large numbers as
n\to\infty
) and the rate of convergence is described by a rate function. Usually, the LD-based methods could achieve asymptotic optimality in terms of either
logarithmic efficiency or bounded relative error
(
Asmussen and Glynn 2007
)
.
The LD theory and the rate function are profoundly related to the eigenvalue problem (
4
).

There are two main drawbacks of applying the LD-based exponential tilting.
First, the LD-based exponential tilting only aims to maximize the variance’s exponential decay rate, not the original variance or asymptotic variance. When the probability of the event of interest is only moderately small (
10^{-2}
to
10^{-4}
), the titling probability measure given by the LD may not be close to the optimal one.
Second, in general, it is difficult to calculate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions (
4
) required by the classical exponential tilting method, except when the
latent Markov process has finite state space or is affine (see
Zhang et al. 2015
for affine processes). A functional eigenvalue problem is typically very challenging to solve.
To avoid these drawbacks, we propose a broader titling family that is more flexible and easier to implement,
so that importance sampling can be performed for more complex applications.

3
A General Duo-Exponential Tilting Family

In our proposed algorithm,
it is not necessary to compute eigenvalues and eigenfunctions
; hence the computational cost can be reduced.
It also performs importance sampling for more general stochastic models, to which the eigenvalues are unavailable.
Furthermore, the algorithm tries to minimize the importance sampling estimator’s variance directly rather than the asymptotic exponential rate function. Such optimization will incur more computational cost but will also provide a more refined approximation to the optimal variance minimizing tilting distributions. The proposed algorithm tilts the transition probability and the distribution of innovations separately.

For given
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the following exponential embedding for the random walk part.

\rho_{\theta}(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y=e^{\theta^{\top}y-\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta)}\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y,

(7)

where
\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta)
is the same function defined after (
3
) by replacing
\alpha
by
\theta
, and
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta)<\infty,\text{ for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\}
.
Next, we apply exponential tilting for the transition probability kernel of the latent process
X_{n}
. For given
\eta\in H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a given parameter space, define

p_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=e^{k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)-\phi(x,\eta)}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),

(8)

where
\phi(x,\eta)=\log\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{k(x,X_{1},\eta)}]
,
and
H=\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\phi(x,\eta)<\infty,\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}\}
.
We call
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)
a
link function
with parameter
\eta
, that maps
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
, which will be specified later.
Generally, the choice of
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)
should be tailored to specific models and objectives. It determines which exponential family to be embedded for the transition probability. We will discuss some prototypical choices of
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)
and its linkage to some well-known exponential families in Section
4.1
.

Combining (
7
) and (
8
) enables us to define the new transition probability
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}
for Markov process
\{(X_{n},S_{n}),n\geq 0\}
as

\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}|x,s)=e^{\theta^{\top}(s^{\prime}-s)+k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)-\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta)-\phi(x,\eta)}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\rho(s^{\prime}-s|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}.

(9)

Thus, an unbiased estimator of
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[F(S_{\tau})]
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}}[F(S_{\tau})e^{-\theta^{\top}S_{\tau}+\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}-k(X_{i-1},X_{i},\eta)+\psi(X_{i-1},X_{i},\theta)+\phi(X_{i-1},\eta)}].

Since the estimator is unbiased, it suffices to minimize
the variance of the Monte-Carlo estimator and hence the second moment, i.e.,

\displaystyle G(\theta,\eta)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}}[F^{2}(S_{\tau})e^{-2\theta^{\top}S_{\tau}+\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}-2k(X_{i-1},X_{i},\eta)+2\psi(X_{i-1},X_{i},\theta)+2\phi(X_{i-1},\eta)}]

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[F^{2}(S_{\tau})e^{-\theta^{\top}S_{\tau}+\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}-k(X_{i-1},X_{i},\eta)+\psi(X_{i-1},X_{i},\theta)+\phi(X_{i-1},\eta)}].

In (
10
)
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
is the expectation related to the original
probability
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
. The optimal parameters
(\theta,\eta)
can be found by solving

\min_{\theta,\eta}G(\theta,\eta).

3.1
.

3.1
Regularity conditions

{assumption}

1.

\Theta
is an open set containing 0, such that for a given
\theta\in\Theta
,
\mathbb{E}[e^{\theta^{\top}Y_{1}}|X_{0}=x,X_{1}=x^{\prime}]<\infty
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

2.

H
is an open set containing 0, such that for a given
\eta\in H
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{k(x,X_{1},\eta)}]<\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

3.

When
\mathcal{X}
is general (unbounded) state space, for all
(\theta,\eta)
lying in a compact set, the functions
\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta),k(x,x^{\prime},\eta),\phi(x,\eta)
and their partial derivatives
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}(x,x^{\prime},\theta),\frac{\partial k}{\partial\eta}(x,x^{\prime},\eta),\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}(x,\eta)
all have linear growth in
x,x^{\prime}
. There exist positive constant
L,C_{0}>0
such that for any
\ell\leq L
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{\ell|X_{1}|}\right]\leq e^{C_{0}\ell(1+|x|)}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[e^{\ell|X_{0}|}\right]<\infty
.

Assumption
3.1

(i)
and
(ii)
are necessary to guarantee the existence of two “cumulant generating functions” and hence necessary for the feasibility of the exponential tilting.
This assumption is common in exponential tilting literature. The condition
(iii)
is the growth and moment condition needed for (unbounded) general state Markov chain to guarantee the finiteness of quantities in our methods.
Next, we state two assumptions commonly imposed in the Markov random walk literature.

{assumption}

The underlying Markov chain
\{X_{n},n\geq 0\}
is aperiodic, irreducible and
Harris recurrent (i.e., (
25
) holds).

Assumption
3.1
is a standard assumption of the underlying Markov chain
\{X_{n},n\geq 0\}
, cf.
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
, which also guarantees the existence of the stationary probability measure
\pi
. The Harris recurrent condition is defined in Section
8
(cf.
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
).

{assumption}

When the additive part does not degenerate, i.e.,
Y_{n}|X_{n-1},X_{n}
is not deterministic, there exists a measurable function
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\int_{A\times B}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\rho(y|x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y\geq h(x)\mu(A\times B)
.

Assumption
3.1
is often called “minorization condition” (see, e.g.,
Ney and Nummelin 1987a
). It is a common assumption in the study of Markov random walk, and together with Assumption
3.1
(i)
implies that
\{(X_{n},S_{n}),n\geq 0\}
is Harris recurrent and admits a regenerative scheme. More discussions on this assumption can be found in E-Companion

may affect the theoretical properties of the algorithms, but not the implementation of algorithms.

{assumption}

The solution to eigenvalue-eigenfunction problem (

There are sufficient conditions to ensure Assumption

;

3.3
A two-stage algorithm for duo-exponential tilting importance sampling

Once the link function is determined, we describe our importance sampling algorithm through a two-stage process. The first stage is to search for the optimal tilting parameters and the second stage is to do the sampling under the alternative probability. In the first stage, we can follow the procedures in
Kawai 2009
to directly minimize (
10
) based on the stochastic gradient descent algorithm. Alternatively, other stochastic optimization algorithms can also be applied here, e.g., sample average approximation
(
Kim et al. 2015
)
. The second stage follows the standard importance sampling procedure and is straightforward once the tilting parameters are fixed. In summary, we present the proposed Algorithm 1 in E-Companion
9
.

Because the stage 1 is a stochastic program, the solution accuracy also depends on the sample size we draw, which is proportional to the computational cost. The accuracy of the numerical approximation of the tilting parameters may affect the variance reduction that can be achieved at the second stage.
Thus, two cases may arise. (1) Optimizing over
\theta,\eta
can significantly improve the variance reduction, hence the extra computation cost is worthy.
(2) The variance reduction is insensitive to
\theta,\eta
, hence it is not necessary to spend too much time to calculate the optimal parameters. Such trade-off is further demonstrated in Section
5
measured by the efficiency ratio.

4.2
Approximating the optimal exponential martingale for diffusion processes

Consider a large class of diffusion processes

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t},\ X_{0}=x_{0}.

The problem of interest is to estimate
\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[F(X_{\tau})]
for a stopping time
\tau
, where
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
. To simulate the continuous-time diffusion processes, we employ the Euler-Maruyama scheme so that the underlying Markov process has a conditional normal transition probability:

X_{n+1}|X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(X_{n}+b(X_{n})\Delta t,\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{n})\Delta t\right).

Hence these processes still belong to our general framework of Markov random walks (
1
) with a degenerate additive part
S_{n}=X_{n}
.

For this class of stochastic models, it is challenging to do LAN analysis because its stationary distribution is usually not easy to obtain. Moreover, neither the eigenvalue–eigenfunction problems nor the Poisson equation can be solved explicitly.
However, we can provide a useful exponential tilting formula by approximating a zero variance importance sampling as follows.

A natural change of measure is obtained via an exponential martingale and Girsanov theorem. The exponential martingale
L_{t}
satisfies

\mathrm{d}L_{t}=L_{t}U_{t}^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{t},\ L_{0}=1
, and the choice of
U_{t}
determines the alternative distribution.
For a given
T>0
, the associated probability measure is
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}=L_{T}.

Under
{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}
, for a given
0<t<T
,
W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}=W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}U_{s}\mathrm{d}s
is the standard Brownian motion and
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=[b(X_{t})+\sigma(X_{t})U_{t}]\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}
.
Thus, an unbiased estimator is
\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[F(X_{\tau})\frac{1}{L_{\tau}}]=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}[F(X_{\tau})]
.
How to minimize the variance of importance sampling estimator
\operatorname{Var}^{\mathbb{Q}}[F(X_{\tau})\frac{1}{L_{\tau}}]
becomes an optimal control problem.
In theory, the optimal titling, which leads to a zero-variance tilting, can be constructed by a feedback control;
namely, there exists a function
g^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
U^{*}(t,X_{t})=\sigma^{\top}(X_{t})g^{*}(t,X_{t})
gives the optimal tilting (see E-Companion
10
).
Thus, to sample
X_{\tau}
under the zero-variance tilting measure
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, after the discretization, we have

X_{n+1}|X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(X_{n}+b(X_{n})\Delta t+\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{n})g^{*}(t_{n},X_{n})\Delta t,\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{n})\Delta t\right).

However, it may be impractical to solve the control problem.

We can still approximate the unknown optimal feedback control within a linear space spanned by some basis functions,
so that
g^{*}(t,x)\approx B(x)\eta
, where
B:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
.
The basis function is typically taken as the linear or quadratic function, or based on the original functional form of
b,\sigma
.
Thus, to approximate the zero-variance tilting measure
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, we sample under

X_{n+1}|X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(X_{n}+b(X_{n})\Delta t+\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{n})B(X_{n})\eta\Delta t,\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{n})\Delta t\right).

Although this is a heuristic approximation, it may still achieve significant variance reduction; see the SIRD model in Section
6.1
,
where we also explain how to choose these basis function for this particular case therein.

From the derivation in E-Companion
10
, we see the optimal
g^{*}
actually depends on the value function of the event of interest, i.e.,
f(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{\tau})|X_{t}=x]
. In particular,
g^{*}(t,x)=\frac{1}{f(t,x)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,x)
. Hence the proposed approximation of the tilting family
B(x)\eta
can also be viewed as an approximation to
\frac{1}{f(t,x)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,x)
. The idea to approximate the value function in simulation has also been adopted by
Fouque and Tullie 2002
and
Henderson and Glynn 2002
;
both suggest to approximate the value function
f
by the value function under a similar but more tractable model and then compute the exponential tilting (e.g, geometric Brownian motions to approximate the Heston’s models, diffusion approximation to queues).
Henderson and Glynn 2002
further uses the approximated value function to construct a martingale process that mimics the event of interest, to be used as a control variate. Our approach is closer to
Fouque and Tullie 2002
who also uses the approximated value function to do importance sampling, but we differ by considering a simple parametric family and hence reduce computational cost.
In addition, the value functions, both in our approach (see more details in E-Companion
10
) and the one in
Henderson and Glynn 2002
, satisfy a Poisson equation (and in the continuous-time diffusion process, it becomes the Feynman-Kac formula), which, unfortunately, is not related to the one in Theorem
4.4
.

The above approximation is equivalent to define the tilting probability measure using the link function
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)=\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(x)x^{\prime}
.
More precisely, the change of measure (
8
) becomes

p_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=\exp\{\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(x)x^{\prime}-\phi(x,\eta)\}p(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),

where
\phi(x,\eta)=\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(x)\left(x+b\left(x\right)\Delta t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(x)\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x)B(x)\eta
.
Thus, an unbiased estimator is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}^{\mathbb{P}_{\eta}}\bigg[F(X_{\tau})\exp\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}

\displaystyle-\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})X_{i}+\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})\left(X_{i-1}+b\left(X_{i-1}\right)\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{i-1})B(X_{i-1})\eta\bigg\}\bigg].

The resulting tilting parameter
\eta
is chosen to minimize

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}^{\mathbb{P}_{\eta}}\bigg[F^{2}(X_{\tau})\exp\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}

\displaystyle-\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})X_{i}+\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})\left(X_{i-1}+b\left(X_{i-1}\right)\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(X_{i-1})\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{i-1})B(X_{i-1})\eta\bigg\}\bigg].

Efficiency Criteria and an Ilustrative Example

5.1
Theoretical efficiency

In the conventional rare event simulation, we consider a sequence of events indexed by the rarity, or equivalently, the probabilities of such events, which tend to zero.
Then some theoretical efficiency criteria such as bounded relative error or logarithmic efficiency can be used;
see
Asmussen and Glynn 2007
for more details.

However, when we consider the asymptotic normal regime that is properly indexed as in Definition
4.2
, the probabilities of such events do not converge to zero.
This necessitates a different notion of efficiency.
More precisely,
we need to take one more limit to ensure the probabilities of the limiting normal distributed variables also go to zero.
In particular, we can define the logarithmic efficiency under the asymptotic normal regime as follows.

Definition 5.1

(Logarithmic Efficiency for Asymptotic Normal Regime)

(i). For a fixed-time event indexed by
l
(
l\to\infty
) under the asymptotic normal regime indexed by
n
(
n\to\infty
), suppose the probability of the event, denoted as

z(n,\ell)
, satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}z(n,\ell)=\Phi(-\ell)
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal distribution. Then an unbiased estimator
\hat{Z}(n,\ell)
is said to have logarithmic efficiency, if,
for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(n,\ell)\right)}{z(n,\ell)^{2-\varepsilon}}=0.

(18)

(ii) For a first-passage-time event
l
(
l\to-\infty
) under the asymptotic normal regime indexed by
b
(
b\to\infty
), suppose the probability of the event can be represented by
z(b,\ell)
, satisfying
\lim_{b\to\infty}z(b,\ell)=\Phi(\ell)
. Then an unbiased estimator
\hat{Z}(b,\ell)
is said to have logarithmic efficiency, if,
for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{\ell\to-\infty}\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(b,\ell)\right)}{z(b,\ell)^{2-\varepsilon}}=0.

(19)

This definition is adapted from the conventional definition of logarithmic efficiency in the rare event simulation to fit the asymptotic normal regime within
the LAN family. It essentially imposes restrictions that the rarity goes to zero as the same rate as that of the tail probability of a normal distribution.
For more comparison with the large deviation regime, see E-Companion
11
.
We can show that the asymptotic optimal tilting within the LAN family is also logarithmic efficient.

5.2
Numerical criteria and an illustrative example

There is a trade-off between accuracy (as measured by the standard deviation) and time consumption
(as measured by the CPU time),
when evaluating the numerical performance of different simulation methods,
Following
Glynn and Iglehart 1989
, we shall use the following ratio to strike a balance between the two criteria
12
12

12

Other criteria include difficulty in implementation, robustness to model parameters, adaptiveness to model specifications, etc.
Since they are difficult to measure, we do not compare algorithms based on these criteria.

More precisely, the efficiency ratio of an algorithm relative to the plain Monte Carlo on the same sample size is defined as

\text{Efficiency ratio}:=\frac{\text{standard deviation reduction ratio}}{\sqrt{\text{time consumption ratio}}}=\frac{\text{standard deviation}_{MC}/\text{standard deviation}_{a}}{\sqrt{\text{time consumption}_{a}/\text{time consumption}_{MC}}}.

The square root is used because increasing the sample size by
N
times reduces the standard deviation by
\sqrt{N}
times.

To give a numerical example, consider Heston’s stochastic volatility (SV) model:

\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\mu S_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{X_{t}}S_{t}\mathrm{d}W^{S}_{t},~~~\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\kappa(\alpha-X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\sqrt{X_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{X}_{t},

6
Two Applications

We consider two applications, modeling pandemics in Section
6.1
and measuring financial systemic risk in Section
6.2
,
which are both challenging for the classical importance sampling methods.

There are two major challenges. First, the system dynamics are not affine. Thus, the eigenfunctions are not easy to compute and usual regularity conditions may not be satisfied.
For example, in the pandemic model, the whole population is divided into several interacting subgroups; hence, the entire system becomes nonlinear.
Second, the quantity of interest may not be standard and applying the existing large deviation principle is difficult.
For example, to study systemic financial risk, one is interested in the risk of one institute conditioned on the other institute’s defaults,
which involves estimating the probability of a joint event of one first passage time and the terminal value of another correlated stochastic process.
Despite these challenges, we shall see that our proposed method can still be applied in these applications by specifying suitable link functions and achieving significant variance reduction.

6.1
An overflow probability in a pandemic model

Although pandemic modeling has drawn a lot of attention due to the recent outbreak of COVID-19, the simulation of pandemic models has not been well addressed in the importance sampling literature,
mainly due to the complicated nonlinear dynamic system interactions. Here we consider a stochastic variant of the celebrated SIR model in epidemics as the first application.

The total population is classified into four types: susceptible (
S_{t}
), infected (
I_{t}
), recovered (
R_{t}
) and deceased (
D_{t}
), which are modeled as follows.

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}S_{t}&=-\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}\mathrm{d}t-\sqrt{\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}}\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}I_{t}&=(\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}-(\beta+\gamma)I_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}}\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t}-\sqrt{\beta I_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{t}-\sqrt{\gamma I_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{3}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}R_{t}&=\beta I_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\beta I_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}D_{t}&=\gamma I_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma I_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{3}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}N_{t}&=-\mathrm{d}D_{t}.\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

Here
\alpha
stands for the transmission rate,
\beta
the recovery rate,
\gamma
the death rate, and
N_{t}
the number of the total population,
W^{1}_{t},W_{t}^{2},W^{3}_{t}
are three independent Brownian motions.
In the standard deterministic SIRD model, Brownian motions
W^{1}_{t},W_{t}^{2},W^{3}_{t}
disappear.
The deterministic counterpart without the deceased group is first introduced in
Kermack and McKendrick 1927
and has been extended to the stochastic case. For a review on stochastic epidemic models and the volatility structure in (
20
), see
Allen 2017
.

The SIRD model and its variants have been widely adopted in the study of pandemic to predict the infected number, for example,
Caccavo 2020
,
Athayde and Alencar 2022
,
Sebbagh and Kechida 2022
. We consider a particular form of such quantity: The time that the existing cases exceed the maximum capacity of a given medical system. Mathematically, it is the first passage time
\tau_{c}=\inf\{t\geq 0:I_{t}\geq c\}
for a given
c>0
. In particular, we want to estimate the overflow probability
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{c}<T)
for a fixed number
T<\infty
given initial condition
(N_{0}-I_{0},I_{0},0,0)
, where
I_{0}
is the number of initial cases and
N_{0}
is the total number of population. In addition, note that

is an absorbing state and hence can be used to terminate the simulation earlier.

The model parameters are taken from those in
Anastassopoulou et al. 2020
with
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0.319,0.1,0.00147)
and the the reproduction number

R_{0}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta+\gamma}=3.1438.

Since
N_{t}=S_{t}+I_{t}+R_{t}
and
\mathrm{d}D_{t}=-\mathrm{d}N_{t}
, the above system can be represented by a three-dimensional vector
X_{t}=(S_{t},I_{t},R_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, such that

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}

b(X_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}\\
\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}-\beta I_{t}-\gamma I_{t}\\
\beta I_{t}\end{pmatrix},\ \sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}&-\frac{\alpha S_{t}I_{t}}{N_{t}}&0\\

Due to the nonlinear structure in the drift term, it is infeasible to compute eigenfunctions and eigenvalues or to solve the Poisson equation.
Hence existing methods cannot be applied easily. However, our method only requires a link function
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)=\eta^{\top}B^{\top}(x)x^{\prime}

4.2

Under the tilted probability, the drift term becomes
\tilde{b}(X_{t})=b(X_{t})+\sigma\sigma^{\top}(X_{t})B(X_{t})\eta
.

Next, we explain the heuristics on how to choose the basis function based on the structure of this process. Recall that the dynamic system is characterized by three parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
with distinct physical meanings.

7
Conclusion

We propose a general importance sampling framework for a stochastic system driven by latent Markov processes.
We focus on Markov random walk, i.e., a random walk whose increment is driven by an underlying general state Markov process. The basic idea is to twist the transition probability of the underlying process and the observed process separately based on link functions. Such a duo-exponential tilting family essentially enlarges the search space of sampling distributions and includes the traditional one-parameter exponential tilting as a special case. We provide asymptotically optimal link functions under the LAN family. Furthermore, compared to the existing method of exponential tilting, our framework does not require the large deviation principle. Thus, it does not need eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, which are difficult to get for complex systems. Also, the existence of the asymptotically optimal link functions requires slightly weaker regularity conditions on the stochastic processes compared to the conventional large deviation-based methods. In particular, when the typical regularity conditions hold for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions,
we show that our asymptotically optimal link function can be regarded as a local, linearized approximation to the eigenfunction. Therefore, we only need to compute the derivative of the eigenfunction at one point instead of finding a whole function. We show the logarithmic efficiency of the proposed estimator under the asymptotic normal regime.
We apply our algorithms to a pandemic model and the computation of CoVaR in finance, where the conventional importance sampling methods cannot be easily used.

The proposed algorithm produces better numerical results than existing importance sampling algorithms and achieves significant variance reduction in two applications for events with a moderately small probability (
10^{-2}\sim 10^{-4}
). Moreover,
the simulation results shed new light on the impact of reducing the transmission rate in controlling the overflow probability in the pandemic model and on
a cross-sectional relationship between VaR and CoVaR.

Bounded Relative Error and Logarithmic Efficiency under the Asymptotic Normal Regime

Conventionally, in the study of rare event simulation, the properties of bounded relative error and logarithmic efficiency are often used as criteria. More specifically, let
\{A(\alpha)\}
be a sequence of events of interest, indexed by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
or
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, assume that
z(\alpha)=\mathbb{P}\left(A(\alpha)\right)\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
, and for each
\alpha
let
\hat{Z}(\alpha)
be an unbiased estimator for
z(\alpha)
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{Z}(\alpha)]=z(\alpha)
.

A sequence of estimators
\{\hat{Z}(\alpha)\}
is called to have
bounded relative error
as
\alpha\to\infty
, if

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(\alpha)\right)}{z(\alpha)^{2}}<\infty.

(27)

It is called to have
logarithmic efficiency
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(\alpha)\right)}{z(\alpha)^{2-\varepsilon}}=0,

(28)

for all
\varepsilon>0
. See
Asmussen and Glynn 2007
for more introduction to these concepts.

Note that, we often call the event “rare”, because we implicitly consider the limiting behavior of a sequence of events, whose probabilities tend to zero. Only when
z(\alpha)\to 0
, the notion of bounded relative error makes sense, and the logarithmic efficiency is a weaker property than bounded relative error.

Fixed-time events.

We consider the simulation of the probability of a fixed-time event in the form of
\{S_{n}>c_{n}\}
.
Under the large-deviation regime, the sequence of events of interests typically have the form of
A(n,\ell)=\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu>\ell\}
, indexed by
n
. Under the original probability
\mathbb{P}
,
\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu\to 0
almost surely. Hence
z(n,\ell)=\mathbb{P}\left(A(n,\ell)\right)\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
for any fixed
\ell
. Therefore, it naturally fits in the framework of rare event simulation.
In contrast, under the asymptotic normal regime (see Definition
4.2
), the sequence of events of interests have the form of
A(n,\ell)=\{\sqrt{\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}}(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu)>\ell\}
, indexed by
n
. Under the original probability
\mathbb{P}
,
\sqrt{\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}}(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu)\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in distribution for some
\sigma>0
. Therefore,
z(n,\ell)=\mathbb{P}\left(A(n,\ell)\right)\to\Phi(-\ell)\in(0,1)
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal distribution. In other words, events
under the asymptotic normal regime do not directly fit into the conventional framework of rare event simulation because the limiting probability is not zero. Consequently, the concept of bounded relative error and logarithmic efficiency are not discussed in the literature of moderate-deviation simulation, e.g.,
Fuh and Hu 2004
.

The above discussion motives us to redefine bounded relative error and logarithmic efficiency in the simulation under the asymptotic normal regime
by letting
\ell\to+\infty
(after first taking limit
n\to\infty
) to have a truly rare event. More precisely, a family of estimators
\{\hat{Z}(n,\ell)\}
is called to have bounded relative error, if

\limsup_{\ell\to+\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(n,\ell)\right)}{z(n,\ell)^{2}}<\infty.

It is called to have
logarithmic efficiency
if

\limsup_{\ell\to+\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(n,\ell)\right)}{z(n,\ell)^{2-\varepsilon}}=0,

for all
\varepsilon>0
.

First-passage-time events.

Finally, we examine the simulation of the probability of another typical event involving the first-passage time in the form of
\{\tau_{b}<T\}
. In particular,
under the asymptotic normal regime, the sequence of events of interests have the form of
A(b,\ell)=\{\tau_{b}<\sqrt{\frac{b\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{3}}}\ell+\frac{b}{\mu}\}=\{\frac{\tau_{b}-\frac{b}{\mu}}{\sqrt{b\sigma^{2}/\mu^{3}}}<\ell\}
, with
\mu>0
,
b>0
. It is well known that
z(b,\ell)=\mathbb{P}\left(A(b,\ell)\right)\to\Phi(\ell)
as
b\to\infty
for any given
\ell
, e.g., see
Fuh 2004
.
In this case, we are motivated to call a family of estimators
\{\hat{Z}(b,\ell)\}
to have bounded relative error, if

\limsup_{\ell\to-\infty}\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(b,\ell)\right)}{z(b,\ell)^{2}}<\infty.

It is called to have
logarithmic efficiency
if

\limsup_{\ell\to-\infty}\limsup_{b\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{Z}(b,\ell)\right)}{z(b,\ell)^{2-\varepsilon}}=0,

for all
\varepsilon>0
.

c=\frac{4\kappa e^{-\kappa\Delta t}}{\sigma^{2}(1-e^{-\kappa\Delta t})}
, and
d=\frac{4\kappa\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}
in this subsection. Then

X_{t_{k+1}}|X_{t_{k}}\sim C\chi^{2}_{d}\left(cX_{t_{k}}\right).

We choose the link function as
k(x,x^{\prime},\eta)=\eta x^{\prime}
, and then the resulting tilting probability is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\exp\{\theta Y_{t_{k+1}}+\eta X_{t_{k+1}}-\phi(X_{t_{k}},\eta)-\psi(X_{t_{k}},X_{t_{k+1}},\theta)\},

where

\phi(x,\eta)=\frac{cC\eta x}{1-2\eta C}-\frac{d}{2}\log(1-2\eta C),

\psi(x,x^{\prime},\theta)=\left([\mu-\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\kappa(\alpha-x)]\Delta t+\frac{\rho}{\sigma}(x^{\prime}-x)\right)\theta+\frac{1}{2}x(1-\rho^{2})\Delta t\theta^{2}

are two cumulant functions. More precisely, under the proposed sampling distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\eta}
, the transition probability and conditional distribution are given by

X_{t_{k+1}}|X_{t_{k}}\sim C\operatorname{Gamma}(\frac{d}{2},\frac{1}{2}-\eta C,\frac{cX_{t_{k}}}{1-2\eta C}),

Y_{t_{k+1}}|X_{t_{k+1}},X_{t_{k}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left([\mu-\frac{1}{2}X_{t_{k}}-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\kappa(\alpha-X_{t_{k}})]\Delta t+\frac{\rho}{\sigma}(X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_{k}})+\theta X_{t_{k}}(1-\rho^{2})\Delta t,X_{t_{k}}(1-\rho^{2})\Delta t\right),

where we use
\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta,\delta)
to denote a “non-central” Gamma distribution, which has the same distribution as
\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha+N,\beta)
, with
N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\frac{\delta}{2})
.

SIRD Model

Recall the notations from the main text Section

. We further use
b_{t}
to denote
b(X_{t})
and similarly to
\tilde{b}_{t},\sigma_{t},B_{t}
. Moreover, let

Under the alternative measure, the process is sampled from

\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{t_{k}}-\frac{\alpha_{-}S_{t_{k}}I_{t_{k}}}{N_{t_{k}}}\Delta t\\

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\eta}}\bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{c}<\infty\}}\exp\{-\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{c}-1}\big(\eta^{\top}B_{t_{k}}^{\top}X_{t_{k+1}}-\eta^{\top}B_{t_{k}}^{\top}b_{t_{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\top}B_{t_{k}}^{\top}\Sigma_{t_{k}}B_{t_{k}}\eta\big)\}\bigg].

We present two additional examples where the Poisson equation has an analytical solution.
The first is an affine process in an autoregressive model. The second one is a finite-state-space-affine process in a Markov switching model.
It is difficult to find the eigenfunctions for the second example; thus, the LD-based simulation method cannot be applied easily.

Example 14.1

Consider a discrete-time affine model as described in
Le et al. 2010
.
\{X_{t}\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0\}
is the underlying Markov process whose transition probability satisfies

\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta X_{t+1}}|X_{t}]=(\alpha+\beta X_{t})\eta+\frac{1}{2}(\iota+\gamma X_{t})\sigma_{x}^{2}\eta^{2},

and the additive part
\{Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 1\}
satisfies

\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\theta Y_{t+1}}|X_{t},X_{t+1}]=(a_{1}X_{t}+a_{2}X_{t+1}+a_{3})\theta+\frac{1}{2}(b_{1}X_{t}+b_{2}X_{t+1}+b_{3})^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}.

is affine, with

. Suppose
X_{t}
admits a stationary distribution
\pi

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X_{0}]=\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}

where

On the other hand, consider the eigenvalue problem:

We notice that one eigenfunction is given by

, where

.

This is because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{\theta Y_{1}}r(X_{1})]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\exp\left\{AX_{1}+\theta(a_{1}x+a_{2}X_{1}+a_{3})+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}(b_{1}x+b_{2}X_{1}+b_{3})}{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\exp\left\{(A+\theta a_{2}+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}b_{2}}{2})X_{1}\right\}\right]e^{(\theta a_{1}+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}b_{1}}{2})x+\theta a_{3}+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}b_{3}}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+(\theta a_{1}+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}b_{1}}{2})x+\theta a_{3}+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}\theta^{2}b_{3}}{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\Lambda(\theta)+Ax}.

We provide a detailed argument for interchanging the order between the expectation and differentiation. To this end, we present the following variant of the dominated convergence theorem:

Lemma 15.1

Let

such that
g_{n}\geq 0,h_{n}\geq 0
almost surely for all
n

f_{n}\to f_{\infty}
almost surely, and
|f_{n}|\leq g_{n}+h_{n}
. In addition, we assume
g_{n}\to g_{\infty}
in
L^{1}
and almost surely, and
\mathbb{E}[h_{n}]\to 0
. Then
f_{n}\to f_{\infty}
in
L^{1}
.

Proof.

By Fatou’s lemma,
\mathbb{E}[|f_{\infty}|]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|f_{n}|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|g_{\infty}|]<\infty
, hence
f_{\infty}\in L^{1}
.

Note that
|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|\leq g_{n}+h_{n}+|f_{\infty}|
, hence we apply Fatou’s lemma again to
g_{n}+h_{n}+|f_{\infty}|-|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|\geq 0

\mathbb{E}\left[\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(g_{n}+h_{n}+|f_{\infty}|-|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|\right)\right]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}+h_{n}+|f_{\infty}|-|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|].

That is,

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\infty}+|f_{\infty}|+\liminf_{n\to\infty}h_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[g_{\infty}]+\mathbb{E}[|f_{\infty}|]-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|].

Therefore,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|]+\mathbb{E}[\liminf_{n\to\infty}h_{n}]\leq 0
. This proves
\mathbb{E}[|f_{n}-f_{\infty}|]\to 0
, i.e.,
f_{n}\to f_{\infty}
in
L^{1}
.
\hfill\Box
