Can-SAVE: Mass Cancer Risk Prediction via Survival Analysis Variables and EHR
Petr Philonenko, Vladimir Kokh, Pavel Blinov

Abstract
Specific medical cancer screening methods are often costly, time-consuming, and weakly applicable on a large scale. Advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods greatly help cancer detection but require specific or deep medical data. These aspects prevent the mass implementation of cancer screening methods. For this reason, it is a disruptive change for healthcare to apply AI methods for mass personalized assessment of the cancer risk among patients based on the existing Electronic Health Records (EHR) volume. This paper presents a novel Can-SAVE cancer risk assessment method combining a survival analysis approach with a gradient-boosting algorithm. It is highly accessible and resource-efficient, utilizing only a sequence of high-level medical events. We tested the proposed method in a long-term retrospective experiment covering more than 1.1 million people and four regions of Russia. The Can-SAVE method significantly exceeds the baselines by the Average Precision metric of 22.8%±2.7% vs 15.1%±2.6%. The extensive ablation study also confirmed the proposed method’s dominant performance. The experiment supervised by oncologists shows a reliable cancer patient detection rate of up to 84 out of 1000 selected. Such results surpass the medical screening strategies estimates; the typical age-specific Number Needed to Screen is only 9 out of 1000 (for colorectal cancer). Overall, our experiments show a 4.7-6.4 times improvement in cancer detection rate (TOP@1k) compared to the traditional healthcare risk estimation approach.



I
Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Over the past decades, the world has seen a dramatic increase in yearly cases of oncology detection
[
1
]
. On the one hand, this is due to factors of worsening environment
[
2
]
. Paradoxically, on the other hand, this is due to the general tendency of an increase in life expectancy and substantial improvement in medical diagnostic methods
[
3
]
. While oncology remains a severe disease with no definite cure, there is significant progress in treatment methods, especially effective in the early stages. Successful cure and sustained remission are much more probable for the first stages than similar success in the late ones.

Early detection of cancer development remains an acute problem because the disease can stay asymptomatic for a long time. There are special cancer screening methods (e.g., biological material tests and medical imaging), but they are often costly, time-consuming, and weakly applicable on a large scale. Many artificial intelligence (AI) methods exist in the literature to assess personalized cancer risk. However, these methods require the use of specific or deep medical data. It allows the model to be sensitive to the target disease but also imposes substantial restrictions on mass implementation.

Thus, applying AI methods for widespread, personalized cancer risk assessment represents a significant shift in healthcare. This approach leverages the easily accessible and extensive Electronic Health Records (EHR)
[
4
]
volume to more effectively evaluate cancer risk among patients. By utilizing intelligent methods on this data in the background, it becomes possible to estimate each patient’s instant risk without their active involvement or additional examinations. During further patient interactions with the healthcare system, high-risk individuals can be tested explicitly in a preventive manner to verify the presence of a disease. If the cancer is confirmed, early detection allows for more time and increased chances of successful treatment. Implementing such methods on a population level can lead to a substantial improvement in early-stage cancer detection. Moreover, this method allows for a more precise ranking of patients within current cancer screening protocols, enabling prioritized testing for those at high risk of cancer while maintaining the same budget.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a novel Can-SAVE method for personalized cancer risk prediction based on time-to-event survival analysis models as domain-specific variables for ensembling in a gradient-boosting algorithm. Among the others, our method stands out because of the minimum data greedy policy, which requires only a history of high-level medical events, such as a stream of service codes and diagnoses from EHR. This feature significantly broadens the method’s applicability, making it suitable for clinics with different levels of data completeness.

•

We present the proposed method’s results from the most extensive nationwide study, including four regions with more than 1.1 million people. Our numerical and medical-related experiments show a 4.7-6.4 times improvement in cancer detection rate (TOP@1k) compared to the traditional healthcare risk estimation approach.

•

We discover the crucial set of EHR-level factors indicating cancer development.

II
Related Works

The problem of cancer risk assessment based on Electronic Health Record (EHR) data evokes excellent interest among researchers where the EHR is the systematized collection of patient and population electronically-stored health information in a digital format
[
5
]
. In the literature, the question is repeatedly raised in various formulations. For example, some studies try to determine the cancer risk by analyzing genetic data and gene mutations
[
6
]
, conducting invasive studies of the target organs
[
7
]
, or examining biological markers
[
8
]
. Other possible methods include family history
[
9
]
, lifestyle
[
10
]
, analysis of bad habits
[
11
]
and interactions with harmful substances
[
12
]
, etc. However, these and many other methods require specialized medical data or significant depth of patient history to obtain good predictive power. For example, in
[
13
]
, to train the model, 17 specific laboratory variables are required, among which are 25-OH vitamin
D
and bicarbonate values; in
[
14
]
, the breast cancer assessment model relies on the Breast Cancer Risk Assessment Tool (BCRAT) parameters from EHR; in
[
15
]
, a patient’s PSA analysis over last 10 years is needed to predict the risk of prostate cancer, and so on.

Specialized medical parameters and examinations increase the model’s sensitivity to the identification of target diseases. However, it significantly narrows such model applicability for mass screening. In other words, applying such models to an existing volume of EHR data in other clinics is difficult since they have different levels of data granularity and history depth. To better illustrate the concept, let us describe the challenges that must be addressed when training and implementing such a model. Firstly, a clinic must set upstream monitoring of required medical parameters for a group of patients in question. Such change is often coupled with increasing financial costs, which is hardly acceptable, especially for small clinics. Secondly, reaching the minimum required number of observations takes a long time.

Therefore, there is a line of research on using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for medical risk prediction
[
16
]
. With the current digitalization of medical processes, this is very accessible data available in most medical institutions. In addition, these are standardized data used in many countries worldwide. Such data is exploited to assess the patient’s risks for various disease groups, including oncology ones
[
17
]
.

Modern approaches increasingly use deep learning (DL) methods such as convolutional (CNN) or recurrent (RNN) neural networks
[
18
]
and Transformer-based architectures
[
19
]
. It allows such models to reveal deeply hidden patterns and interconnections in the data. However, it complicates the communication process between doctor and model since such models can only be used in black-box mode and often cannot explicitly share new medical knowledge. These facts complicate interpreting the results and finding an answer to the question: ”Why did the model make such a decision?”. For example, when obtaining a second medical opinion based on an AI model, the doctor would like to consider not only the model prediction but the explanation of this prediction. In the case of cancer risk assessment, such the explanation may be on which significant features the AI model relies on in decision-making. In addition, using complex architectures can require a large amount of training data and potent hardware (e.g., high-performance computing systems and GPUs). All of this can make significant limitations on the reproducibility and scalability of the method.

The situation is quite different for methods of classical machine learning (ML) and survival analysis
[
20
]
. These methods make it possible to build interpretable models, even on a small amount of data and with potentially high predictive power. The survival analysis methods are initially based on a time-to-event estimation approach, which involves studying the time it takes for a specific event (event of interest) to occur, such as the onset of a disease, the occurrence of a complication, or death. Consequently, it allows us to solve the problem by appropriate methods. A fundamental characteristic is a survival function determining the probability of the event of interest at any time
t
. For example, to assess a survival function, the Kaplan-Meier estimator
[
21
]
can help. This estimator is a commonly used and extensively studied method, but one does not consider the predictors’ effects and displays the general survival experience of patients over time. For this case, there are survival regression methods in survival analysis. For example, they are the Cox proportional-hazards
[
20
]
and Accelerated Failure Time (AFT)
[
22
]
models. Each method has advantages and disadvantages, but we only note that using the Cox model requires testing the proportional hazards assumption, which is strong.

Such models are less computationally heavy, can be combined into an ensemble (as additional features of the ML model), and can be reproducible in most medical institutions. Hence, our study proposes a method based on the synergy of effective ML methods and methods from the survival analysis. To solve the problem, we formulate the task of a binary classification.

There are ongoing attempts to develop and implement a universal standard for maintaining and storing medical data, for example, FHIR

This circumstance creates a request for the proposed method to be applicable to the data of the overwhelming majority of medical organizations. III-C

Baseline Method based on RNN and Pre-Trained BERT

As a baseline, we rely on the available modern methods in the literature. We consider the paper
[
18
]
that solves the classification problem to predict the ICD-10 code through a sequence of embeddings based on unstructured medical text records. This method is relevant to our work. In addition, RNN is the natural way for sequence processing. The
Baseline method
is as follows:

1.

Determine the target of the sequence. We assign
target=1
if any diagnosis in the range C00-C97 (note as C-diagnosis) occurs and assign
target=0
otherwise;

2.

Set
t_{end}=t_{target}-\texttt{30 days}
(as a rule, medical examinations 30 days before the C-diagnosis are performed to only state the presence of cancer in the patient, e.g., CT, MRI, or a tumor marker) if
target=1
else
t_{end}=t_{\texttt{MAX date}}-\delta
; Set
t_{start}=t_{end}-\Delta
;

3.

Leave medical events that occurred in the period
[t_{start},t_{pred}]
but not more than
L
;

4.

Prepare an embedding for each medical event in the sequence using a pre-trained language model. For this:

(a)

A code of event is replaced by its corresponding text description (e.g., the diagnosis
I11.9
through the ICD-10 classifier is replaced by
Hypertensive heart disease without (congestive) heart failure
);

(b)

Apply the Profile model
[
27
]
to the text description. This model is a BERT-based language model pre-trained on medical texts.

As a result, a CLS-embedding (1x768) for each medical event is performed;

5.

Each CLS-embedding is expanded by concatenating the sex and age at the medical event, and any other available features (frequency and number for every type of visits). As a result, we built a personalized patient embedding of a medical event;

6.

Feed this sequence of expanded embeddings and
target
to the input of the RNN.

The algorithmic complexity of this solution is determined to be comparable to the standard pipeline for recurrent neural networks.

IV
The Can-SAVE Method

In this section, we describe our method based on an synthesis of traditional machine learning and survival analysis methods. We further introduce the main definitions, describe the models used, and describe the resulting Can-SAVE method.

IV-A

Survival Data

The survival analysis theory
[
20
]
is based on assessing the
survival function
S(t)
, which determines the probability of some underlying event of interest occurrence at the time
t
(time-to-event approach). In our work, such an
event of interest
is the occurrence of the C-diagnosis.
The
survival function,
S(t)
, can be mathematically represented as
S(t)=P(T>t)
, where
T
represents the time to the event of interest and
P(T>t)
denotes the probability that the event has not occurred by time
t
.
S(t)
satisfies the following statements:
S(0)=1
and
S(+\infty)=0
.

In this case, we consider patients diagnosed with cancer as
complete observations
since we know the sex, age, and other parameters at the time of the C-diagnosis. Other patients can be considered
incomplete (censored) observations
since parameters of the patient at the C-diagnosis time are unknown. We only know the patient’s parameters when the C-diagnosis has not yet occurred. In our paper, censored observations corresponds to the
randomly right-censored

[
20
]
case. Figure
3
shows an example of such data. Based on the introduced definitions, we describe the following methods.

Fig. 3:
Example of censored data.
Line length
is the depth of the EHR history.

Red line
(complete observation): the sequence of medical events ends by the C-diagnosis.

Green line
(randomly right-censored observation):
the sequence of medical events ends at the
t_{\texttt{MAX date}}
while the C-diagnosis has not been occurred. It is assumed that the C-diagnosis would occur at
[t_{\texttt{MAX date}},+\infty)
.

IV-B

Survival Models

Kaplan-Meier estimator

[
21
]
.
This is a non-parametric estimation of the survival function
S(t)
. Using this estimator has been a gold standard in survival analysis for many years because this estimate is classical, well-studied, and prevalent
[
20
]
. In terms of the problem, we construct the Kaplan-Meier estimator
\hat{S}_{KM}(t)
as follows:

\centering\hat{S}_{KM}(t_{j})=\hat{S}_{KM}(t_{j-1})\cdot\left(1-\frac{d_{j}}{n_{j}}\right),\@add@centering

(1)

where
t
is the patient’s age (in years) (
t_{0}=0
),
\hat{S}_{KM}(0)=1
,
\hat{S}_{KM}(+\infty)=0
,
d_{j}
is the number of patients diagnosed with cancer in
t_{j}
and
n_{j}
is the number of patients not yet diagnosed with cancer before
t_{j}
.

Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) model

[
22
]
.
This is another semi-parametric method for constructing an estimator of the survival function
S(t)
. Unlike the Kaplan-Meier estimator, the AFT model is a multivariate regression method to assess the probability of the terminal event (the C-diagnosis occurrence). Each patient is an observation with a vector of individual parameters at the time
t_{pred}
.

We denote a sample
W
of
n
patients to train the AFT model:

W=\{(t_{1},\delta_{1},\overline{x}^{1}),(t_{2},\delta_{2},\overline{x}^{2}),...,(t_{n},\delta_{n},\overline{x}^{n})\},

where

\delta_{j}
is the
target
of the
j
-th patient (i.e.,
\delta_{j}=1
if the C-diagnosis occurs, otherwise
\delta_{j}=0
);

t
is the age of the patient at the
t_{pred}
;

\overline{x}^{j}
is a vector of individual parameters (covariates) describing the patient at the time
t
(e.g., covariates can describe the total number of visits, the number of unique diagnoses, the year, and others).

Then, the main idea behind the AFT model is the selection of parametrizations for the non-negative basic distribution
S_{0}(t)
and the non-negative function
r(x;\beta)
in order to construct the function
\hat{S}_{AFT}(t;x,\beta,S_{0},r)=\hat{S}_{AFT}(t)
. This function estimates the probability of the C-diagnosis for a set
(t,\delta,\overline{x})
:

\centering\hat{S}_{AFT}(t)=S_{0}\left(\int_{0}^{t}r(x(s);\beta))ds\right).\@add@centering

(2)

Minimizing the likelihood function determines parameters
\beta
in equation (
2
). The selection of
S_{0}
and
r
is based on maximizing the model’s performance. In the paper, we consider the Concordance Index (C-index)
[
28
]
as the metric of the model performance. The C-index is a generalization of the ROC AUC score on the censoring case.

IV-C

Can-SAVE Method

Our hypothesis is as follows: By utilizing survival analysis methods, it is possible to construct essential features for the ML-based model that can enhance the predictive capability of the overall model. Next, we propose the
Can-SAVE method
in detail:

1.

Determine the target of the sequence. We assign
target=1
if the C-diagnosis occurs and assign
target=0
otherwise;

2.

Set
t_{end}=t_{target}-\texttt{30 days}
if
target=1
else
t_{end}=t_{\texttt{MAX date}}-\delta
; Set
t_{start}=t_{end}-\Delta
. Aim of these offsets is to minimize the noise of a target determination. These offsets can be changed if necessary;

3.

Leave medical events that occurred in the
[t_{start},t_{pred}]
;

4.

Provide feature engineering. To assess the patient risk, we compute a set of individual characteristics (features) on available information over previous visits. Below we present the groups of features used in the paper:

(a)

Socio-demographic:
age, sex, BMI, birth month;

(b)

Visit frequency:
visits, diagnoses/visits, unique diagnoses, unique services, visits in last months, etc.;

(c)

Diagnoses frequency:
a quantity for each ICD-10 group (e.g., 4 diagnoses in I00-I99);

(d)

Medical service frequency:
a quantity for each service group (e.g. kidney U/S performed 3 times);

(e)

Time between visits:
time from the first/last visit, avg. time between visits, time from the first/last occurrence in each ICD-10/service group, etc.;

(f)

Seasonality:
month, season, weather;

(g)

Diagnoses indicators:
binary for each ICD-10 group (e.g., False in E10-E14);

(h)

Medical service indicator:
binary for each group (True in lung x-ray);

(i)

Survival models:
features based on the Kaplan-Meier and AFT models.

More than 700 features are constructed for each patient. The most significant features included in the final model are described in
Feature Importance
;

5.

Feed features and targets to the framework’s input as a task of binary classification (e.g., CatBoost, LightAutoML, sklearn, etc.).

The algorithmic complexity of the proposed solution is determined to be comparable to the pipeline of the ML-stacking approach.

We provide technical details regarding the training of the Baseline method. In parentheses, we indicate the search set used for optimal selection. To ensure consistency, we set the sequence length of medical events for each patient to

(from the range [8, 256]). The neural network architecture comprises four unidirectional GRU layers (from the set {GRU, LSTM}), each consisting of 128 units (from the range [16, 512]), with time distributed between the layers. The penultimate hidden layer is fully connected and utilizes the ReLU activation function (from the set {ReLU, sigmoid, tanh}). The Sigmoid activation function is applied to the last layer. A recurrent dropout of 0.5 is applied to each GRU layer (from the range [0.0, 0.5]). These optimal hyperparameters were obtained using the ”Optuna” framework (with 10 iterations).

For training the proposed Can-SAVE method, we employed various frameworks, including scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, LightAutoML (LAMA), and CatBoost. VI
Supervised Retrospective Study

VI-A

Design of Experiment

This section presents the detailed results of a retrospective experiment conducted under the supervision of specialized oncologists. The design of the retro-experiment was as follows:

1.

Obtaining EHRs of patients (out-of-sample);

2.

Computation of risk for these patients using the proposed Can-SAVE method in out-of-time period;

3.

Construction of the sample ”Can-SAVE”: selection of the TOP patients with the highest risk (e.g.,
TOP=1000
) computed by the proposed Can-SAVE method;

4.

Construction of the sample ”Traditional Examination”: selection of the TOP patients using existing protocols for routine medical examinations;

5.

Sending both samples to the oncologists to verify the occurrence of cancer for the selected patients;

6.

Analysis of the obtained results.

The described procedure ensured that risk assessment and result verification were conducted separately by the parties involved in the experiments, thereby increasing the reliability of the obtained results. More formally, this can be referred to as a
blind supervised retrospective (out-of-time & out-of-sample) experiment
.

VI-B

Results

Firstly, we present the results obtained from the retrospective experiment described above. This retro-experiment was conducted in four regions of Russia and involved over a million unique patients, making it one of the largest oncological studies utilizing EHRs. The results are summarized in Table

These findings suggest that the combination of the proposed Can-SAVE method with the existing medical examination process has the potential to significantly enhance the detection rates of oncological diseases.

Furthermore, we conducted an analysis of the patients identified during the retrospective experiment. We identified the cancer nosologies that were detected in the TOP patients and present them in Table

In the retro-experiment, the Can-SAVE method exhibited the highest sensitivity to neoplasms of the Digestive organs, Respiratory and intrathoracic organs, Skin, and Respiratory and intrathoracic organs (detected in all four regions). Additionally, the Can-SAVE method showed high sensitivity to neoplasms of the Breast, Genitourinary system, and Thyroid (detected in three out of four regions).

VII
Conclusion

Our paper discusses the problem of early cancer risk detection among patients. The task’s relevance and importance align with national healthcare projects implemented within World Health Organization initiatives. We propose a novel method to address such a problem with the primary data from EHRs commonly available in most medical facilities, namely codes of medical diagnoses and services. During the method development process, we rely on the depersonalized EHR dataset from one of the largest clinics. Each record is a detailed sequence of patient medical events.

We considered two methods as possible solutions. The first is the Baseline method, based on traditional RNN and a BERT language model pre-trained on medical texts. The second is the proposed method, which ensembles machine learning and survival analysis methods. We have compared the proposed methods in a comprehensive set of numerical experiments. The primary metric for the proposed method significantly outperforms the Baseline.

Additionally, our method exceeds age baselines by a significant margin. According to the conducted retro-experiment, our method is sensitive to almost all nosologies of cancer. Moreover, in the blind retrospective out-of-time experiment, we have clearly shown that the proposed method is reliable in cancer patient detection (up to 84 out of 1000 selected). Such results exceed the estimates from traditional approaches, e.g., the best Number Needed to Screen (9 out of 1000 screenings). This result means our method would greatly help a healthcare system and society with early cancer detection.

The proposed method allows several practical use-case scenarios. First, it is a tool for differentiating patients into risk groups for further management decisions and diagnostic actions. Second, our method allows patient risk ranking within existing lists of patients for examination. Such a procedure improves patient prioritization, saves valuable time, and maintains the budget for planned examinations.

Despite the impressive results, we believe even further enhancement of the method is feasible. The current model training process consists of separate model training stages that may hinder the synergy effect. Therefore, training in an end-to-end manner can significantly increase overall performance.
