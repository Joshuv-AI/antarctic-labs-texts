Abstract
Spectral functions on Euclidean Jordan algebras arise frequently in convex models. Despite the success of primal-dual conic interior point solvers, there has been little work on enabling direct support for spectral cones , i.e. proper nonsymmetric cones defined from epigraphs and perspectives of spectral functions. We propose simple logarithmically homogeneous barriers for spectral cones and we derive efficient, numerically stable procedures for evaluating barrier oracles such as inverse Hessian operators. For two useful classes of spectral cones - the root-determinant cones and the matrix monotone derivative cones - we show that the barriers are self-concordant, with nearly optimal parameters. We implement these cones and oracles in our open source solver Hypatia, and we write simple, natural formulations for four applied problems. Our computational benchmarks demonstrate that Hypatia often solves the natural formulations more efficiently than advanced solvers such as MOSEK 9 solve equivalent extended formulations written using only the cones these solvers support.



1
Introduction

A conic problem minimizes a linear function over the intersection of an affine subspace with a Cartesian product of primitive proper cones (i.e. irreducible, closed, convex, pointed, and full-dimensional conic sets).
Any convex problem can be represented in conic form.
An advantage of using conic form is that under mild assumptions, a conic problem has a simple and easily checkable certificate of optimality, primal infeasibility, or dual infeasibility
(
Permenter et al. 2017
)
.

A class of functions that commonly arise in convex optimization applications are spectral functions on Euclidean Jordan algebras such as the real vectors and real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrices.
In this context, a spectral function is a real-valued symmetric function of the (real) eigenvalues.
Examples include the geometric mean (or root-determinant), the entropy (e.g. von Neumann entropy), and the trace of the inverse (e.g. the A-optimal design criterion).
Indeed, many disciplined convex programming (DCP) functions are spectral functions
(
Grant et al. 2006
;
Grant and Boyd 2014
)
.
We define
spectral cones
as proper cones defined from epigraphs of convex homogeneous spectral functions or epigraphs of perspective functions of convex spectral functions.
These cones allow simple, natural conic reformulations of a wide range of convex optimization problems.
Despite this, to our knowledge there has been little prior work enabling direct support for various spectral cones in primal-dual conic solvers.

Many conic solvers (such as CSDP
(
Borchers 1999
)
, CVXOPT
(
Andersen et al. 2011
)
, ECOS
(
Domahidi et al. 2013
;
Serrano 2015
)
, MOSEK
(
MOSEK ApS 2020
)
, SDPA
(
Yamashita et al. 2003
)
) implement primal-dual interior point methods (PDIPMs).
Conic PDIPMs require easily computable oracles for a logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier (LHSCB) function for the proper cone of the conic problem.
Complexity analysis of idealized PDIPMs shows that they converge to
\varepsilon
tolerance in
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\nu}\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations, where
\nu
is the barrier parameter of the LHSCB.

Currently, most PDIPMs are specialized for the nonnegative, second order, and positive semidefinite (PSD) cones, which are cones of squares of Euclidean Jordan algebras.
However, these symmetric cones limit modeling generality and often require the construction of large extended formulations (EFs)
(
Coey et al. 2021b
)
.
For many spectral cones (e.g. for the negative entropy function), equivalent EFs using only symmetric cones do not exist, and when they do, they can be impractically large.
Nonsymmetric conic PDIPMs (for example, by
Nesterov et al. 1996
;
Nesterov 2012
;
Skajaa and Ye 2015
) can handle a much broader class of cones.
In
Coey et al. 2021a
, we generalize the method by
Skajaa and Ye 2015
, enhance its practical performance, and test our implementation in our open-source conic solver Hypatia
(
Coey et al. 2021b
)
.

A key feature of Hypatia is its generic cone interface, which allows specifying a proper cone
\mathcal{K}
by implementing a small list of oracles.
Once
\mathcal{K}
is defined, both
\mathcal{K}
and its dual cone
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
may be used in any combination with other recognized cones in Hypatia to construct conic models.
The oracles to implement are: an initial interior point
t\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
, a feasibility test for the cone interior
\intr(\mathcal{K})
(and optionally for the dual cone interior
\intr(\mathcal{K}^{\ast})
), and several derivative oracles for an LHSCB for the cone.
The LHSCB oracles needed for ideal performance are the gradient, the Hessian operator (i.e. the second order directional derivative applied once to a given direction), the inverse of the Hessian operator, and the third order directional derivative (applied twice to a given direction).
Fast and numerically stable procedures for evaluating oracles are crucial for practical performance in conic PDIPM solvers such as Hypatia.

Our first main contribution is to define simple logarithmically homogeneous barriers for spectral cones and derive efficient and numerically stable barrier oracle procedures.
For example, for the case where the spectral function of the cone is separable, we show how to apply the inverse Hessian operator of the barrier function very cheaply using a closed-form formula, without the need to compute or factorize an explicit Hessian matrix (which can be expensive and prone to numerical issues).
Similarly, for the negative log-determinant and root-determinant spectral cones, we derive highly-efficient specialized oracle procedures.

Our second main contribution is to show that for two important subclasses of spectral cones - the root-determinant cones and the matrix monotone derivative (MMD) cones - the barriers we propose are LHSCBs.
These LHSCBs have parameters that are only a small additive increment of one larger than the parameter of the LHSCB for the cone of squares domain of the cone, hence the parameters are near-optimal.
MMD cones allow modeling epigraphs of a variety of useful separable spectral functions, in particular the trace (or sum) of the negative logarithm (i.e. the negative log-determinant), negative entropy, and certain power functions.
Furthermore, the dual cones of the MMD cones allow modeling epigraphs of even more separable spectral functions, such as the trace of the inverse, exponential, and more power functions.

These two contributions enable efficient and numerically stable implementations of the MMD cone and the log-determinant and root-determinant cones in nonsymmetric conic PDIPMs.
We define these cones through Hypatia’s cone interface.
Our MMD cone implementation is parametrized by both a Jordan algebra domain and an MMD function, allowing the user to define new domains and MMD functions.
An MMD function is easily specified by implementing a small set of oracles for its univariate form: the function itself, its first three derivatives, and its convex conjugate, as well as an interior point for the corresponding MMD cone.
We predefine five common MMD functions and three typical Jordan algebra domains: the real vectors, real symmetric matrices, and complex Hermitian matrices.

We use these new spectral cones in Hypatia to formulate example problems from distribution estimation, experiment design, quantum information science, and polynomial optimization.
The natural formulations (NFs) using these cones are simpler and smaller than equivalent EFs written in terms of the handful of standard cones recognized by either ECOS or MOSEK 9 (i.e. the common symmetric cones and the three-dimensional exponential and power cones).
Our computational experiments demonstrate that, across a wide range of sizes and spectral functions, Hypatia can solve the NFs faster than Hypatia, MOSEK, or ECOS can solve the equivalent EFs.
Furthermore, to illustrate the practical impact of our efficient oracle procedures, we show that our closed-form formula for the MMD cone inverse Hessian product is faster and more numerically reliable than a naive direct solve using an explicit Hessian factorization.

Jordan algebras

Jordan algebraic concepts provide a useful and straightforward abstraction for spectral functions, cones, and our barrier results in later sections.
We follow the notation of
Faraut and Koranyi 1998
where possible.

An algebra over the real or complex numbers is a vector space

For

.
We refer to

\displaystyle w_{a}\circ w_{b}

\displaystyle=w_{b}\circ w_{a},

(1a)

\displaystyle w_{a}\circ(w_{a}^{2}\circ w_{b})

\displaystyle=w_{a}^{2}\circ(w_{a}\circ w_{b}).

(1b)

For example, for

and
V=\mathbb{H}^{d}

Given

\displaystyle L(w_{a})w_{b}

\displaystyle=w_{a}\circ w_{b}

(2)

Given

\displaystyle P(w)=2L(w)^{2}-L(w^{2}).

(3)

For any positive integer

\displaystyle P(w)^{k}

\displaystyle=P(w^{k}).

(4)

It is standard to assume the existence of a multiplicative identity

Note that

.
A point

if and only if

Equation

Faraut and Koranyi 1998
, Proposition II.3.1)

Henceforth we consider only the finite dimensional

Jordan algebras.
A Jordan algebra

is Euclidean if
\langle w_{a}\circ w_{b},w_{c}\rangle=\langle w_{b},w_{a}\circ w_{c}\rangle

w_{a},w_{b},w_{c}\in V

We call
\mathcal{Q}

.
The cone

is proper (closed, convex, pointed, and solid) because

In addition,

For example, for

, the cone of squares is

For convenience, we often write

If

Furthermore

implies that

This also implies by

(with

2.1
Spectral decomposition

In a Euclidean Jordan algebra
V
, an
idempotent
is an element
c\in V
such that
c^{2}=c
.
Two idempotents
c_{1},c_{2}
are

if
c_{1}\circ c_{2}=0

Let
d
be the rank of
V
.

c_{1},\ldots,c_{d}
is a
complete
system of orthogonal idempotents if
c_{1},\ldots,c_{d}
are all idempotents, pairwise orthogonal, and
\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}c_{i}=e
.
An idempotent is

The number of elements in any Jordan frame is called the

of
V
.
For example, the rank of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{S}^{d}
, or
\mathbb{H}^{d}

.

For any

, there exist unique real numbers (not necessarily distinct)

and a unique Jordan frame

(
Faraut and Koranyi 1998

w=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}c_{i}.

(5)

We call

of

The

is
\det(w)=\prod_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}

is
\tr(w)=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}

(
Faraut and Koranyi 1998
, Section II.2, page 29)
.
For example, for
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Jordan frame is the standard unit vectors and

is its own vector of eigenvalues.
For
V=\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we can think of the Jordan frame as the rank one PSD matrices from a full symmetric eigendecomposition.

Henceforth, we define the inner product on

\langle w_{a},w_{b}\rangle=\tr(w_{a}\circ w_{b})
.
Under this inner product,

Vieira 2007

Thus for

\langle P(w)r_{1},r_{2}\rangle=\langle P(w^{1/2})r_{1},P(w^{1/2})r_{2}\rangle=\langle r_{1},P(w)r_{2}\rangle.

(6)

Spectral functions and derivatives

Let
V
be a Jordan algebra of rank
d
.
A real-valued function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

A symmetric function

\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}

\varphi(w)=f(\lambda(w))

\lambda(w)=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})

(
Baes 2007
, Definition 8)
.
Note that
\varphi
is convex if and only if

(
Davis 1957
)

In this section, we give expressions for certain derivatives and directional derivatives of

that are useful for the barrier oracles we derive in
Section

5
.
We express these derivatives at a point
w\in V
(satisfying certain assumptions as necessary below) with spectral decomposition
Equation

5
, and we let the direction be
r\in V
with Peirce decomposition
Equation

8
.
The gradient is
\nabla\varphi(w)\in V
and the second and third order directional derivatives are
\nabla^{2}\varphi(w)[r]\in V
and
\nabla^{3}\varphi(w)[r,r]\in V

We begin with the general nonseparable case in

Section

3.1
The nonseparable case

Let

We use subindices to denote particular components of these derivatives.
According to

\nabla^{2}\varphi(w)[r]=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{(\nabla f)_{i}-(\nabla f)_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}r_{i,j}+\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{2}f)_{i,j}r_{i}c_{j}.

15
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\nabla\varphi(w(t))

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\varphi(w(t))[r]

(17a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{(\nabla f(t))_{i}-(\nabla f(t))_{j}}{w_{i}(t)-w_{j}(t)}r_{i,j}(t)+\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{2}f(t))_{i,j}r_{i}(t)c_{j}(t).

(17b)

We differentiate
Equation

17
once more.
From
Vieira 2016
and
Sun and Sun 2008
, for
i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=r_{i}(t),

(18a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}c_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=s_{i}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:j\neq i}\frac{r_{i,j}(t)}{w_{i}(t)-w_{j}(t)}.

(18b)

Using the chain rule,
Equation

18a
implies:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\nabla f(t))_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{2}f(t))_{i,k}r_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,

(19a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\nabla^{2}f(t))_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{3}f(t))_{i,j,k}r_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket.

(19b)

Applying the chain and product rules, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{w_{i}(t)-w_{j}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{j}(t)-r_{i}(t)}{(w_{i}(t)-w_{j}(t))^{2}}

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i\neq j,

(20a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}r_{i,j}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(4c_{i}(t)\circ(c_{j}(t)\circ r))

(20b)

\displaystyle=4c_{i}(t)\circ(s_{j}(t)\circ r)+4s_{i}(t)\circ(c_{j}(t)\circ r)

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i\neq j,

(20c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle c_{i}(t),r\rangle c_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle s_{i}(t),r\rangle c_{j}(t)+r_{i}(t)s_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket.

(20d)

Finally, letting
s_{i}\coloneqq s_{i}(0)
for all
i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
, these results imply that:

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\varphi[r,r]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\nabla\varphi(w(t))\big|_{t=0}

(21a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{(\nabla f)_{i}-(\nabla f)_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}\biggl(4c_{i}\circ(s_{j}\circ r)+4s_{i}\circ(c_{j}\circ r)-\frac{r_{i}-r_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}r_{i,j}\biggr)+{}\\
&\hphantom{{}={}}\sum_{i,j,k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{(\nabla^{2}f)_{i,k}-(\nabla^{2}f)_{j,k}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}r_{k}r_{i,j}+{}\\
&\hphantom{{}={}}\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{2}f)_{i,j}(\langle s_{i},r\rangle c_{j}+r_{i}s_{j})+\sum_{i,j,k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{3}f)_{i,j,k}r_{i}r_{k}c_{j}\end{split}

(21b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{(\nabla f)_{i}-(\nabla f)_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}\biggl(4c_{i}\circ(s_{j}\circ r)+4s_{i}\circ(c_{j}\circ r)-\frac{r_{i}-r_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}r_{i,j}\biggr)+{}\\
&\hphantom{{}={}}\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{2}f)_{i,j}(2r_{j}s_{i}+\langle s_{i},r\rangle c_{j})+\sum_{i,j,k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}(\nabla^{3}f)_{i,j,k}r_{i}r_{k}c_{j}.\end{split}

(21c)

The derivative expressions simplify significantly for
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
V=\mathbb{S}^{d}
, the form of
Equation

15
is well-known
(
Faybusovich and Zhou 2021
)
and the form of
Equation

21c
appears in
Sendov 2007
.

3.2
The separable case

The spectral function
\varphi
induced by
f
is
separable
if
f
is a separable function, i.e.
f(\lambda)=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}h(\lambda_{i})
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For convenience, if
w\in V
, we also define
h:V\to V
as
h(w)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}h(w_{i})c_{i}
.
This allows us to write
\varphi(w)=\tr(h(w))=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}h(\lambda_{i})
.
Note that
\varphi
is convex if and only if
h
is convex.
For example, if
h(w)=-\log(w)
then
\varphi(w)=\tr(-\log(w))=-\logdet(w)
; we consider this special case in
Section

3.3
.

We specialize the derivatives from
Equations

14
,
15
and
21c
, maintaining the simplifying assumption of distinct eigenvalues.
Since
(\nabla^{2}f)_{i,j}=(\nabla^{3}f)_{i,j,k}=0
unless
i=j=k
, we have:

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\nabla h(w_{i})c_{i},

(22a)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi(w)[r]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{\nabla h(w_{i})-\nabla h(w_{j})}{w_{i}-w_{j}}4c_{i}\circ(c_{j}\circ r)+\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\nabla^{2}h(w_{i})P(c_{i})r,

(22b)

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\varphi(w)[r,r]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:i<j}\frac{\nabla h(w_{i})-\nabla h(w_{j})}{w_{i}-w_{j}}\biggl(4c_{i}\circ(s_{j}\circ r)+4s_{i}\circ(c_{j}\circ r)-\frac{r_{i}-r_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}r_{i,j}\biggr)+{}

(22c)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{}={}}\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\nabla^{2}h(w_{i})(2r_{i}s_{i}+\langle s_{i},r\rangle c_{i})+\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\nabla^{3}h(w_{i})r_{i}^{2}c_{i}.

(22d)

3.3
The negative log-determinant case

The negative log-determinant function
\varphi(w)=-\logdet(w)
is a separable spectral function.
We let
w\succ 0
and drop the assumption of distinct eigenvalues.
For convenience, we let
\hat{r}\coloneqq P(w^{-1/2})r\in V
.
First, note that (similar to
Vieira 2007
):

\displaystyle\langle w^{-1},r\rangle=\langle P(w^{-1/2})e,r\rangle\overset{\mathclap{\scriptscriptstyle\ref{eq:moveP}}}{=}\langle e,P(w^{-1/2})r\rangle=\tr(\hat{r}).

(23)

Due to
Faraut and Koranyi 1998
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}(\tr(\hat{r}))=\nabla_{w}(w^{-1})[r]=-P(w^{-1})r.

(24)

Adapting the result in
Faybusovich and Tsuchiya 2017
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}(P(w^{-1})r)[r]=-2P(w^{-1/2})\hat{r}^{2}.

(25)

Now, the gradient of
\varphi
is
(
Faraut and Koranyi 1998
, Propositions III.4.2(ii))
:

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi(w)

\displaystyle=-w^{-1},

(26)

so the second and third order directional derivatives are:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi(w)[r]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\scriptscriptstyle\ref{eq:Dinv}}}{=}P(w^{-1})r,

(27a)

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\varphi(w)[r,r]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\scriptscriptstyle\ref{eq:Pinv}}}{=}-2P(w^{-1/2})(P(w^{-1/2})r)^{2}.

(27b)

Note that unlike the separable spectral function case in
Section

3.2
, here we do not need the explicit eigenvalues of
w
.

4
Cones and barrier functions

In this paper we are concerned with a class of proper cones that can be characterized as follows:

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\cl\{\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{E}:\zeta(\tilde{u})\geq 0\}\subset\tilde{V},

(28)

where
\zeta:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}
is a concave, (degree one) homogeneous function and
\mathcal{E}
is some convex cone in the space
\tilde{V}
.
In particular, we define
\zeta
in terms of a
C^{3}
-smooth spectral function
\varphi
that is defined on the interior of a cone of squares
\mathcal{Q}
of a Jordan algebra
V
of rank
d
.

4.1
The homogeneous case

First, we suppose that
\varphi
is convex and homogeneous.
Then
\zeta(u,w)\coloneqq u-\varphi(w)
is concave and homogeneous, and we let
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q})
and
\tilde{V}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times V
.
This defines a convex cone that is the closure of the epigraph set of
\varphi
:

\mathcal{K}_{h}\coloneqq\cl\{(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q}):u\geq\varphi(w)\}.

(29)

Note that if
\varphi
is concave, we can analogously define a cone from the hypograph set of
\varphi
.
In
Section

7
, we consider the root-determinant cone, which is the hypograph of the concave root-determinant function.
To check membership in
\intr(\mathcal{K}_{h})
, we first determine whether
w\in\intr(\mathcal{Q})
(which is equivalent to positivity of the eigenvalues), and if so, whether
\zeta(\tilde{u})>0
.

4.2
The non-homogeneous case

Now we suppose that
\varphi
is convex and non-homogeneous.
We define the perspective function of
\varphi
,
\per\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q})\to\mathbb{R}
, as
(\per\varphi)(v,w)\coloneqq v\varphi(v^{-1}w)
.
This is a homogeneous and convex function
(
Boyd et al. 2004
, Section 3.2.6)
.
We let
\zeta(u,v,w)\coloneqq u-(\per\varphi)(v,w)
, with
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q})
and
\tilde{V}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times V
.
This defines a convex cone that is the closure of the epigraph set of the perspective function of
\varphi
:

\mathcal{K}_{p}\coloneqq\cl\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q}):u\geq v\varphi(v^{-1}w)\}.

(30)

Equivalently, we can view
\mathcal{K}_{p}
as the closed conic hull of the epigraph set of
\varphi

(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, Chapter 5)
.
In
Section

6
, we consider the special case where
\varphi
is a separable spectral function with matrix monotone first derivative.
The membership check for
\intr(\mathcal{K}_{p})
is similar to that of
\intr(\mathcal{K}_{h})
except we first check whether
v>0
.

4.3
Dual cones

A proper cone is
primitive
if it cannot be written as a Cartesian product of two or more lower dimensional proper cones.
The dual cone of a primitive, proper cone
\mathcal{K}
is another primitive, proper cone:

\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\coloneqq\{z:\langle s,z\rangle\geq 0,\forall s\in\mathcal{K}\}.

(31)

Recall that when
\mathcal{K}
is defined through Hypatia’s generic cone interface, both
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
become available for constructing conic models.

We assume that
\varphi
is convex, and we derive the dual cones of the epigraph cones
\mathcal{K}_{h}
and
\mathcal{K}_{p}
(these steps can be adapted for analogous hypograph cones if
\varphi
is concave).
We define the convex conjugate function
\varphi^{\ast}:V\to\mathbb{R}\cup\infty
of
\varphi
as the modified Legendre-Fenchel transformation (similar to
Zhang 2004
):

\varphi^{\ast}(r)=\textstyle\sup_{w\in\dom(\varphi)}\{-\langle w,r\rangle-\varphi(w)\},

(32)

which is a convex function.
The conjugate of a symmetric function is also a symmetric function
(
Baes 2007
, Lemma 29)
, and the conjugate of a spectral function induced by a symmetric function
f
is the spectral function induced by
f^{\ast}

(
Baes 2007
, Theorem 30)
.
Thus for
\varphi(w)=f(\lambda(w))
we have the conjugate function
\varphi^{\ast}(w)=f^{\ast}(\lambda(w))
.

For the epigraph-perspective cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}
in
Equation

30
,
Zhang 2004
and
Rockafellar 2015
derive the dual cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}
:

\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}=\cl\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\mathbb{R}\times V:v\geq u\varphi^{\ast}(u^{-1}w)\}.

(33)

We can view
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}
as the epigraph set of the perspective function of the conjugate of
\varphi
, but with the epigraph and perspective components swapped (compare to
Equation

30
).
Depending on the natural domain of
\varphi^{\ast}
, the
w
component of
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}
is not necessarily restricted to lie in
\mathcal{Q}
; in
Section

6.2
we discuss several example spectral functions, some of which have conjugates defined on all
V
and others only on
\intr(\mathcal{Q})
.

For
\mathcal{K}_{h}
in
Equation

29
, we derive the dual cone
\mathcal{K}_{h}^{\ast}
as follows.
Since
\varphi
is homogeneous in this case,
(\per\varphi)(v,w)=v\varphi(v^{-1}w)=\varphi(w)
.
Therefore the corresponding perspective cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}
for
\varphi
is not a primitive cone, as it can be written as a (permuted) Cartesian product of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
and
\mathcal{K}_{h}
:

\mathcal{K}_{p}=\cl\{(u,v,w)\in\tilde{V}:v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq},(u,w)\in\mathcal{K}_{h}\}.

(34)

Since the dual cone of a Cartesian product of cones is the Cartesian product of their dual cones, we have (since
\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{\ast}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
):

\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}=\cl\{(u,v,w)\in\tilde{V}:v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq},(u,w)\in\mathcal{K}_{h}^{\ast}\}.

(35)

By
(
Lasserre 1998
, Theorem 2.1)
, the homogeneity of
\varphi
implies that
\varphi^{\ast}
can only take the values zero or infinity.
Hence by
Equation

33
, we know:

\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}=\cl\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\mathbb{R}\times V:v\geq 0,u\varphi^{\ast}(u^{-1}w)<\infty\}.

(36)

Since
Equations

35
and
36
describe the same cone, we can conclude that the dual cone of
\mathcal{K}_{h}
is:

\mathcal{K}_{h}^{\ast}=\cl\{(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}_{>}\times V:\varphi^{\ast}(u^{-1}w)<\infty\}.

(37)

4.4
Barrier functions and oracles

A logarithmically homogeneous barrier (LHB) function
\Gamma
for a proper cone
\mathcal{K}\subset\tilde{V}
is
C^{2}
-smooth and satisfies
\Gamma(\tilde{u}_{i})\to\infty
along every sequence
\tilde{u}_{i}\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
converging to the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
, and:

\displaystyle\Gamma(\theta\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\tilde{u})-\nu\log(\theta)

\displaystyle\forall\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{K},\theta\in\mathbb{R},

(38)

for some
\nu\geq 0

(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, Definition 2.3.2)
.
If
\Gamma
is also self-concordant, then it is an LHSCB with parameter
\nu\geq 1
(or a
\nu
-LHSCB) for
\mathcal{K}
.
For self-concordance,
\Gamma
must be
C^{3}
-smooth and satisfy
(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
, Definition 2.1.1)
:

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla^{3}\Gamma(\tilde{u})[\tilde{p},\tilde{p},\tilde{p}]\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2(\nabla^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{u})[\tilde{p},\tilde{p}])^{3/2}

\displaystyle\forall\tilde{u}\in\intr(\mathcal{K}),\tilde{p}\in\tilde{V}.

(39)

The best known interior point algorithms need at most
O(\sqrt{\nu}\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations to converge to a solution within
\varepsilon
tolerance
(
Nesterov and Todd 1997
)
.

The LHB we consider for a cone of the form
Equation

28
is:

\Gamma(\tilde{u})\coloneqq-\log(\zeta(\tilde{u}))+\Psi(\tilde{u}),

(40)

where
\Psi
can be thought of as an LHSCB for the domain of
\zeta
or
\cl(\mathcal{E})
.
The negative logarithm function
-\log
is the standard LHSCB for
\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
, with parameter
\nu=1
.
Similarly, the spectral function
-\logdet
(see
Section

3.3
) is the standard LHSCB for a cone of squares
\mathcal{Q}
of
V
, with
\nu=d
(the rank of
V
).
For
\mathcal{K}_{h}
we let
\Psi(\tilde{u})=-\logdet(w)
, hence
\Gamma
has parameter
\nu=1+d
.
Since an LHSCB for a Cartesian product of cones is the sum of LHSCBs for the primitive cones, for
\mathcal{K}_{p}
we let
\Psi(\tilde{u})=-\log(v)-\logdet(w)
, hence
\Gamma
has parameter
\nu=2+d
.
Note that although
\Gamma
is an LHB, it is not necessarily self-concordant; in
Sections

6.4
and
7.3
we prove that
\Gamma
is an LHSCB for some useful special cases.

We now define four barrier oracles that Hypatia’s PDIPM uses; for ideal performance, these oracle implementations should be efficient and numerically stable.
For an interior point
\tilde{u}\in\intr(\mathcal{K})
and a direction
\tilde{p}\in\tilde{V}
, the gradient
g
, the Hessian product
H
, the inverse Hessian product
\bar{H}
, and the third order directional derivative
T
are:

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla\Gamma(\tilde{u}),

(41a)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{u})[\tilde{p}],

(41b)

\displaystyle\bar{H}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\nabla^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{u}))^{-1}[\tilde{p}].

(41c)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla^{3}\Gamma(\tilde{u})[\tilde{p},\tilde{p}].

(41d)

Note
g,H,\bar{H},T\in\tilde{V}
.
In later sections, we use subscripts to refer to subcomponents of these oracles, for example the
w
component of the gradient oracle is
g_{w}\coloneqq\nabla_{w}\Gamma(\tilde{u})\in V
.
Ideally,
H
applies the positive definite linear operator
\nabla^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{u}):\tilde{V}\to\tilde{V}
without constructing an explicit Hessian, and similarly,
\bar{H}
applies the (unique) inverse operator
(\nabla^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{u}))^{-1}:\tilde{V}\to\tilde{V}
without constructing or factorizing an explicit Hessian.

We note that for the standard LHSCB
\Psi
for a cone of squares, efficient and numerically stable procedures for these four oracles are well-known.
The same cannot be said for the LHB
\Gamma
currently.
In
Section

5
, we derive these oracles for
\mathcal{K}_{p}
(noting that they can be adapted easily for
\mathcal{K}_{h}
).
In the special cases for which we show
\Gamma
is an LHSCB, the oracles can be computed particularly efficiently.

5
Barrier oracles for epigraph-perspective cones

We consider the epigraph-perspective cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}
defined in
Equation

30
.
Recall that we let
\tilde{p}=(p,q,r)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times V
and
\tilde{u}=(u,v,w)\in\intr(\mathcal{K}_{p})
, and we define
\zeta
and
\Gamma:\intr(\mathcal{K}_{p})\to\mathbb{R}
from
Equation

40
as:

\displaystyle\zeta(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle\coloneqq u-v\varphi(v^{-1}w),

(42a)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\log(\zeta(\tilde{u}))-\log(v)-\logdet(w).

(42b)

In this section, we derive expressions and evaluation procedures for the
g
,
H
,
T
, and
\bar{H}
oracles (defined in
Section

4.4
) corresponding to the LHB
\Gamma
for
\mathcal{K}_{p}
.
We note that the oracles for
\mathcal{K}_{h}
in
Equation

29
are simpler because no perspective operation is needed for a homogeneous
\varphi
; they can be obtained by fixing
v=1
and
q=0
and ignoring the
v
components in the oracle expressions in this section.

Without assuming any particular form for
\varphi
, we write
g
,
H
, and
T
in
Section

5.1
and
\bar{H}
in
Section

5.2
in terms of the derivatives of
\varphi
.
If
\varphi
is a spectral function, these derivatives can be computed using the expressions from
Section

3
.
In the case that
\varphi
is a separable spectral function (see
Section

3.2
), we derive a more specialized procedure for
\bar{H}
in
Section

5.3
, which is no more expensive than
H
.
Finally, in
Section

5.4
, we specialize the four oracles for the negative log-determinant function (i.e.
\varphi(w)=-\logdet(w)
; see
Section

3.3
) and we discuss implementations.

5.1
Derivatives

First, we express the derivatives of
\zeta
in terms of those of
\varphi
.
We define the function
\mu:\mathbb{R}_{>}\times\intr(\mathcal{Q})\to\intr(\mathcal{Q})
and its first directional derivative
\xi\in V
in the direction
(q,r)
as:

\displaystyle\mu(v,w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq v^{-1}w,

(43a)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla\mu(v,w)[(q,r)]=\nabla_{v}\mu(v,w)q+\nabla_{w}\mu(v,w)[r]=v^{-1}(r-q\mu(v,w)).

(43b)

For convenience, we fix the constants
\mu\coloneqq\mu(v,w)
,
\varphi\coloneqq\varphi(\mu)
, and
\zeta\coloneqq\zeta(\tilde{u})
.
Let
\nabla\varphi
,
\nabla^{2}\varphi
, and
\nabla^{3}\varphi
be the derivatives of
\varphi
evaluated at
\mu
, and let
\nabla\zeta
,
\nabla^{2}\zeta
, and
\nabla^{3}\zeta
be the derivatives of
\zeta
evaluated at
\tilde{u}
.
Using
Equation

43
, the directional derivatives of
\zeta
can be written compactly as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\zeta

\displaystyle=1,

(44a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\zeta

\displaystyle=-\varphi+\nabla\varphi[\mu],

(44b)

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}\zeta

\displaystyle=-\nabla\varphi,

(44c)

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta[\tilde{p}]

\displaystyle=p-q\varphi-v\nabla\varphi[\xi],

(44d)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}\zeta[\tilde{p}])_{v}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi,\mu],

(44e)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}\zeta[\tilde{p}])_{w}

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi],

(44f)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\zeta[\tilde{p},\tilde{p}]

\displaystyle=-v\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi,\xi],

(44g)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{3}\zeta[\tilde{p},\tilde{p}])_{v}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{3}\varphi[\xi,\xi,\mu]+\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi,\xi]-2v^{-1}q\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi,\mu],

(44h)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{3}\zeta[\tilde{p},\tilde{p}])_{w}

\displaystyle=2v^{-1}q\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi]-\nabla^{3}\varphi[\xi,\xi],

(44i)

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\zeta[\tilde{p},\tilde{p},\tilde{p}]

\displaystyle=-v\nabla^{3}\varphi[\xi,\xi,\xi]+3q\nabla^{2}\varphi[\xi,\xi].

(44j)

Using
Equation

44
, we now derive the directional derivatives of
\Gamma
.

The Hessian of

For convenience, we let:

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\nabla^{2}_{w,w}\Gamma)^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{w,u}\Gamma,

\displaystyle Y_{v}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\nabla^{2}_{w,w}\Gamma)^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{w,v}\Gamma,

It can be verified (for example, by analogy to the block symmetric matrix inverse formula) that the inverse Hessian product oracle

in

\displaystyle=\bar{Z}_{v,u}(p-\langle Y_{u},r\rangle)+\bar{Z}_{v,v}(q-\langle Y_{v},r\rangle),

6.1
Matrix monotonicity

A function
f
is
matrix monotone
(or operator monotone) if
w_{a}\succeq w_{b}\succeq 0
implies
f(w_{a})\succeq f(w_{b})
for all
w_{a},w_{b}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
for all integers
d
.
The following integral representation result is attributed to
Löwner 1934
(see e.g.
Kwong 1989
and
Furuta 2008
).
A function
f:\mathbb{R}_{>}\to\mathbb{R}
is matrix monotone in
\mathbb{R}_{>}
if and only if it has the representation:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x}{x+t}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)=\alpha+\beta x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\biggl(1-\frac{t}{x+t}\biggr)\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t),

(77)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
and
\rho
is a positive measure on
\mathbb{R}_{>}
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-1}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)<\infty
.

This result implies that, for a cone of squares
\mathcal{Q}
of a Jordan algebra, and for
w\in\intr(\mathcal{Q})
with the spectral decomposition
w=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}c_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle f(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}f(w_{i})c_{i}

(78a)

\displaystyle=\alpha e+\beta w+\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\biggl(1-\frac{t}{w_{i}+t}\biggr)c_{i}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)

(78b)

\displaystyle=\alpha e+\beta w+\int_{0}^{\infty}(e-t(w+te)^{-1})\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t).

(78c)

This is similar to the representation in
Faybusovich and Tsuchiya 2017
.

7.1
Cone definition

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is a cone of squares of a Jordan algebra
V
with rank
d
.
Let
\varphi:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
denote the root-determinant function (or the geometric mean of the eigenvalues):

\varphi(w)\coloneqq\det(w)^{1/d}=\prod_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}w_{i}^{1/d},

\tilde{u}\coloneqq(u,w)\in\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Q}

The function

\displaystyle\zeta(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varphi(w)-u.

We define the root-determinant cone
\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}
and its dual cone as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Q}:u\leq\det(w)^{1/d}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}_{\leq}\times\mathcal{Q}:-d^{-1}u\leq\det(w)^{1/d}\}.

(98b)

We note that

is a hypograph modification of the epigraph cone
\mathcal{K}_{h}
in
Equation

29
, and it is a primitive proper cone.

\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}^{\ast}
can be derived by modifying the steps we use to derive
\mathcal{K}_{h}^{\ast}
in
Equation

37
and using the convex conjugate of the negative root-determinant function.

8.1
Hypatia solver

Hypatia’s generic cone interface allows specifying a
vectorized
proper cone
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
for some dimension
q
.
The nonnegative cone is already in vectorized format.
For the real symmetric PSD cone
\mathbb{S}_{\succeq}^{d}
, we use the standard
svec
transformation, which rescales and stores only the elements of the matrix triangle in a vector of dimension
d(d+1)/2
.
For the complex Hermitian PSD cone
\mathbb{H}_{\succeq}^{d}
, we perform a modified
svec
transformation to a
d^{2}
-dimensional vector, storing each real diagonal element as a single element and each complex off-diagonal element in the triangle as two (rescaled) consecutive real elements (the real part followed by the imaginary part).
These transformations preserve inner products and the self-duality of cones of squares.

We adapt these transformations to enable vectorization of spectral cones.
For example, for the epigraph-perspective cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}
in
Equation

30
, the vectorization is
(u,v,\vect(w))\in\mathbb{R}^{2+q}
, where
\vect(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the appropriate vectorization of

.
Fortunately, the dual cone of this vectorized cone is the analogous vectorization of the dual cone
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\ast}
in
Equation

33
.

Hypatia’s primal general conic form over a vector of variables
x
minimizes a linear function
\langle c,x\rangle
subject to linear equality constraints
b-Ax=0
and conic constrains
h-Gx\in\mathcal{K}
.
Here, the vectorized proper cone
\mathcal{K}
is specified as a Cartesian product
\mathcal{K}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{K}_{K}
of
K
proper cones recognized by Hypatia.
This means for each
\mathcal{K}_{i}
, we must have either
\mathcal{K}_{i}
or
\mathcal{K}_{i}^{\ast}
defined through Hypatia’s cone interface.

For the domains
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, and
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, we implement vectorizations of the MMD cone
\mathcal{K}_{\mmd}
, the log-determinant cone
\mathcal{K}_{\logdet}
, and the root-determinant cone (
\mathcal{K}_{\rtdet}
).
1

8.2
Natural and extended formulations

To assess the computational value of our new cones and efficient oracles, we compare the performance of Hypatia on NFs over
\mathcal{K}_{\mmd}

, and

Coey et al. 2021b
.

Computational methodology

We perform all instance generation, computational experiments, and results analysis with Ubuntu 21.10, Julia 1.8.0-DEV.862, and Hypatia 0.5.3.
3
3

For each example problem in

.

Coey et al. 2021b
