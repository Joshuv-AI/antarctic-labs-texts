Stochastic homogenization of HJ equations: a differential game approach
Andrea Davini and Raimundo Saona and Bruno Ziliotto

Abstract
. We prove stochastic homogenization for a class of non-convex and non-coercive first-order Hamilton-Jacobi equations in a finite-range of dependence environment for Hamiltonians that can be expressed by a max-min formula. We make use of the representation of the solution as a value function of a differential game to implement a game-theoretic approach to the homogenization problem.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the asymptotic behavior, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, of the solutions of a stochastic Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) equation of the form

(HJ
ε
)

{\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}}+H\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},D_{x}u^{\varepsilon},\omega\right)=0\quad\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$,}

for every fixed
T>0
, where
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz Hamiltonian which can be represented by a max-min formula. The dependence of the equation on the random environment
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
enters through the Hamiltonian
H(x,p,\omega)
, whose law is assumed to be
stationary
, i.e., invariant by space translation, and
ergodic
, i.e., any event that is invariant by space translations has either probability
0
or
1
.
Under the additional assumptions that the random variables
\big(H(\cdot,p,\cdot)\big)_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
satisfy a finite range of dependence condition and that the underlined dynamics is oriented, we show homogenization for equation (
HJε
), see Theorem
2.2
. Furthermore, by exploiting an argument taken from
[
27
, Section 5]
, we are able to extend the homogenization result to a class of Lipschitz Hamiltonians that can be put in max-min form only locally in
p
, see Theorem
2.4
.
The full set of assumptions and the precise statements of our homogenization results are presented in Section
2
. In what follows, we want to emphasize that the Hamiltonians we consider are non-coercive and non-convex in
p
.

The coercivity of
H
in the momentum is a condition which is often assumed in the homogenization theory of first-order HJ equations.
Its role is to provide uniform
L^{\infty}
-bounds on the derivatives of the solutions to the equation (
HJε
) and to an associated “cell” problem.
The first homogenization results for equations of the form (
HJε
) for coercive Hamiltonians were established in the periodic setting in the pioneering work
[
34
]
and later extended to the almost–periodic case in
[
32
]
.
The generalization of these results in the stationary ergodic setting was established in
[
38
,
36
]
under the additional assumption that the Hamiltonian is convex in
p
.
By exploiting the metric character of first order HJ equations, homogenization has been extended to the case of quasiconvex Hamiltonians in
[
24
,
9
]
.
The question whether homogenization holds in the stationary ergodic setting for coercive Hamiltonians that are non-convex in the momentum remained an open problem for about fifteen years, until the third author provided in
[
40
]
the first counterexample to homogenization in dimensions greater than
1
.
Feldman and Souganidis generalized that example and showed in
[
28
]
that homogenization can fail for Hamiltonians of the form
H(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq G(p)+V(x,\omega)
whenever
G
has a strict saddle point.
This has shut the door to the possibility of having a general qualitative homogenization theory in the stationary ergodic setting in dimension
d\geqslant 2
, at least without imposing further mixing conditions on the random environment.
On the positive side, homogenization of equation (
HJε
) for coercive and non-convex Hamiltonians of fairly general type has been established in dimension
d=1
in
[
11
,
29
]
, and in any space dimension for Hamiltonians of the form
H(x,p,\omega)=\big(|p|^{2}-1\big)^{2}+V(x,\omega)
in
[
10
]
.
Further positive results in random environment satisfying a finite range of dependence condition have been obtained in
[
7
]
for Hamiltonians that are positively homogeneous of degree
\alpha\geqslant 1
, and for Hamiltonians with strictly star–shaped sublevel sets in
[
28
]
.
Despite all these significant progresses, the general question of which equations of the form (
HJε
) homogenize in the non-convex case remains largely uncharted.

When the coercivity condition of
H
in
p
is dropped, one loses control on the derivatives of the solutions of equation (
HJε
) and of the associated “cell” problem, which are no longer Lipschitz continuous in general. As a consequence, homogenization of (
HJε
) is known to fail even in the periodic case, regardless to the fact that the Hamiltonian is or is not convex in
p
, see for instance the introductions in
[
19
,
20
]
and some examples in
[
15
]
.
In this generality, supplementary conditions need to be assumed to compensate the lack of coercivity of the Hamiltonian.
In the periodic and other compact settings, homogenization results in this vein have been obtained in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
3
]
and more recently in some convex situations in
[
13
]
, for a class non-convex Hamiltonians in dimension
d=2
in
[
19
]
, and in other non-convex cases in
[
17
,
15
]
.
When
H(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq|p|+\langle V(x,\omega),p\rangle
, equation (
HJε
) is known in the literature (up to a sign change) as the
G
–equation
, for which homogenization has been established, both in the periodic in
[
20
,
39
,
37
]
, and in the stationary ergodic case in
[
35
,
21
]
, under a smallness condition on the divergence of
V
, but without imposing that
|V|<1
, meaning that
H
is not assumed to be coercive in
p
.

This paper furnishes a new and fairly general class of non-convex and non-coercive Hamiltonians for which the equation (
HJε
) homogenizes. In our first result, corresponding to Theorem
2.2
, we prove homogenization for a class of non-convex and Lipschitz Hamiltonians that arises from Differential Game Theory. More specifically, we will consider Hamiltonians of the form

(H)

H(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq\max_{b\in B}\min_{a\in A}\left\{-\ell(x,a,b,\omega)-\langle f(a,b),p\rangle\right\}\qquad\text{for all }(x,p,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega,

where the main assumptions are that the law of
\ell
has finite range of dependence, in the same spirit as
[
7
,
28
]
, and that there exists a direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\delta>0
such that

\langle f(a,b),e\rangle{\geqslant}\delta\qquad\hbox{ for all $a\in A$, $b\in B$.}

This latter condition is inspired by the joint work
[
30
]
of the third author, addressed to the study of the asymptotic of a discrete-time game on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
We refer the reader to
[
30
, Section 4]
for a discussion on the relation between these discrete games and the topic of stochastic homogenization.
Interestingly, the assumption (
f
) precludes a Hamiltonian of the form (
H
) from being coercive, see Remark
2.3
, and this is one significant originality that distinguishes our work from most of the contributions on the topic of stochastic homogenization.

Another important novelty relies on the proof technique.
Indeed, thanks to the form (
H
) of the Hamiltonian, we can represent the
solution of (
HJε
) as the value function of a differential game, as explained in
[
27
]
, and implement a game-theoretic approach.
Such an approach has been rarely used in the context of homogenization of non-convex HJ equations (see e.g.
[
15
]
in the periodic setting), and, up to our knowledge, this is the first time that it is considered to prove a positive result in the stochastic case.
By considering optimal strategies, generated paths, and the dynamic programming principle, we manage to show that the solutions of (
HJε
) exhibit a concentration behavior assymptotically and that their mean satisfies an approximate subadditive inequality. The homogenization result is finally derived by exploiting the local Lipschitz character of the solutions of (
HJε
).

The probabilistic arguments that we use are related to the works
[
8
,
28
,
7
]
, where the authors prove homogenization for several classes of first and second order nonconvex Hamilton-Jacobi equations.
In order to do so, they consider an auxiliary stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation, called the
metric problem

[
8
]
(resp.,
[
7
,
28
]
), whose solutions can be interpreted as the minimal cost of going from one point of the space to another point (resp., to a planar surface).
By analogy with techniques employed in first-passage percolation
[
2
,
33
]
, they combine Azuma’s inequality with a subadditive argument to prove homogenization of the metric problem, and provide convergence rates and concentration estimates.
Then, they resort to a PDE approach to relate the metric problem and the original Hamilton-Jacobi equation.
Compared to their technique, our proof presents several key differences.

(1)

The concentration and subadditive inequalities techniques are applied to the value of a zero-sum differential game, which is a fairly different problem than the metric problem.

(2)

Our arguments rely mainly on a game-theoretic approach, by exploiting the monotonicity property (in the preferred direction
e
) of the optimal trajectories, rather than on PDE arguments.

(3)

We deal with non-coercive Hamiltonians, while Hamiltonians in
[
8
,
7
,
28
]
are coercive.

This yields several difficulties, including that the space-Lipschitz constant of the solutions of (
HJε
) are not uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon
.

In our second result, corresponding to Theorem
2.4
, we succeed to extend the homogenization result to a class of non-convex and non-coercive Lipschitz Hamiltonians that are not necessarily defined by a max-min formula as the ones considered above.
This makes the game-theoretic approach even more notable, in the sense that we are able to deal with Hamiltonians that do not come
a priori
from a differential game.
For such an extension, we exploit an argument introduced in
[
27
, Section 5]
to put these Hamiltonians in the form (H) when
p
is constrained within a ball
B_{R}
, but its use to prove a homogenization result in presence of a non-coercive Hamiltonian is not trivial and, as far as we know, new.
The difficulty relies on the fact that, due to the lack of coercivity of the Hamiltonian, the Lipschitz constants in
x
of the solutions to (
HJε
) are not uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
, but explode with rate
1/\varepsilon
.
From the game-theoretic viewpoint, this corresponds to a set of controls for Player 2 of the kind
B_{R/\varepsilon}
.
In view of this, we had to tailor the proof of Theorem
2.2
in a form that is suited for this extension, by paying particular attention that the constants that come into play in the crucial estimates at the base of our arguments depend on parameters that can be controlled when
\varepsilon
goes to
0
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we present the notation, the standing assumptions and the statements of our homogenization results, namely Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
.
In Section
3
we present the reduction homogenization strategy we will follow to prove Theorem
2.2
.
Some proofs are deferred to Appendix
B
.
In Section
4
we prove the probabilistic concentration result.
Section
5
is devoted to the proofs of Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
.
Appendix
A
contains the deterministic PDE results, along with their proofs, that we use in the paper.

2.
Assumptions and main result

Throughout the paper, we will denote by
d\in\mathbb{N}
the dimension of the ambient space.
We will denote either by
B_{r}(x_{0})
or
B(x_{0},r)
(respectively,
\overline{B}_{r}(x_{0})
or
\overline{B}(x_{0},r)
) the open (resp., closed) ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
r>0
centered at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
When
x_{0}=0
, we will more simply write
B_{r}
(resp.,
\overline{B}_{r}
).
The symbol
|\cdot|
will denote the norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, for any
k\geqslant 1
.
We will write
\varphi_{n}\rightrightarrows\varphi
in
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
to mean that the sequence of functions
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
uniformly converge to
\varphi
on compact subsets of
E
.
We will denote by
\mbox{\rm C}(X),\,\mbox{\rm UC}(X),\,\mbox{\rm BUC}(X)
, and
\mbox{\rm Lip}(X)
the space of continuous, uniformly continuous, bounded uniformly continuous, and Lipschitz continuous functions on a metric space
X
, respectively.

We will denote by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
a probability space, where
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure and
{\mathcal{F}}
is the
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{P}
–measurable subsets of
\Omega
.
We will assume that
\mathbb{P}
is
complete
in the usual measure theoretic sense.
We will denote by
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and equip the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\times\Omega
with the product
\sigma
-algebras
{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes{\mathcal{F}}
, respectively.

We will assume that
\mathbb{P}
is invariant under the action of a one-parameter group
(\tau_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
of transformations
\tau_{x}\colon\Omega\to\Omega
.
More precisely, we assume that:
the mapping
(x,\omega)\mapsto\tau_{x}\omega
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
to
\Omega
is measurable;

\tau_{0}=id
;

\tau_{x+y}=\tau_{x}\circ\tau_{y}
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
and
\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{x}(E)\right)=\mathbb{P}(E)
, for every
E\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Lastly, we will assume that the action of
(\tau_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is
ergodic
, i.e., any measurable function
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathbb{P}(\varphi(\tau_{x}\omega)=\varphi(\omega))=1
for every fixed
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is almost surely equal to a constant.

A random process
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
stationary
with respect to
(\tau_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
if
f(x,\omega)=f(0,\tau_{x}\omega)
for all
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
.
Moreover, whenever the action of
(\tau_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is ergodic, we refer to
f
as a
stationary ergodic
process.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a (possibly uncountable) family of jointly measurable functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
.
We will say that the random variables
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
exhibit
long-range independence
(or, equivalently, have
finite range of dependence
) if there exists
\rho>0
such that, for all pair of sets
S,\widehat{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that their Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(S,\widehat{S})>\rho
, the generated
\sigma
–algebras
\sigma(\{X_{i}(x,\cdot):i\in\mathcal{I},x\in S\})
and
\sigma(\{X_{i}(x,\cdot):i\in\mathcal{I},x\in\widehat{S}\})
are independent, in symbols,

(FRD)

\sigma(\{X_{i}(x,\cdot):i\in\mathcal{I},x\in S\})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\sigma(\{X_{i}(x,\cdot):i\in\mathcal{I},x\in\widehat{S}\})\qquad\hbox{whenever\ \ $d_{H}(S,\widehat{S})>\rho$}.

In this paper, we will be concerned with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of the form

(2.1)

\partial_{t}u+H(x,D_{x}u,\omega)=0,\quad\hbox{in}\ (0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the Hamiltonian
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be stationary with respect to shifts in
x
variable, i.e.,
H(x+y,p,\omega)=H(x,p,\tau_{y}\omega)
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\omega\in\Omega
, and to belong to the class
\mathscr{H}
defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be in the class
\mathscr{H}
if it is jointly measurable and it satisfies the following conditions, for some constant
\beta>0
:

(H1)

|H(x,p,\omega)|\leqslant\beta\left(1+|p|\right)\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \text{for all }(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(H2)

|H(x,p,\omega)-H(x,q,\omega)|\leqslant\beta|p-q|\qquad\qquad\text{for all }x,p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(H3)

|H(x,p,\omega)-H(y,p,\omega)|\leqslant\beta|x-y|\qquad\quad\quad\text{for all }x,y,p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumptions (H1)-(H3) guarantee well-posedness in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for every fixed
T>0
, of the Cauchy problem associated with equation (
2.1
) when the initial datum is in
\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Furthermore, the solutions are actually in
\mbox{\rm UC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Solutions, subsolutions and supersolutions of (
2.1
) will be always understood in the viscosity sense, see
[
14
,
16
,
18
,
22
]
, and implicitly assumed continuous, if not otherwise specified.

The purpose of this paper is to prove a homogenization result for equation (
2.1
) for a subclass of stationary Hamiltonians belonging to
\mathscr{H}
that arise from Differential Game Theory and that can be expressed in the following max-min form:

(H)

H(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq\max_{b\in B}\min_{a\in A}\left\{-\ell(x,a,b,\omega)-\langle f(a,b),p\rangle\right\}\qquad\text{for all }(x,p,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega.

Here
A,B
are compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for some integer
m
, and the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\times\Omega
is equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes{\mathcal{F}}
.
The mapping
f\colon A\times B\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuous vector field, and the
running cost

\ell\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a jointly measurable function satisfying the following assumptions:

(
\ell_{1}
)

\ell(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\omega)\in\mbox{\rm BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
;

(
\ell_{2}
)

there exists a constant
\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)>0
such that

|\ell(x,a,b,\omega)-\ell(y,a,b,\omega)|\leqslant\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)\,|x-y|\qquad\hbox{for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $a\in A$, $b\in B$ and $\omega\in\Omega$;}

(
\ell_{3}
)

\ell
is stationary with respect to
x
, i.e.,

\ell(x,a,b,\omega)=\ell(0,a,b,\tau_{x}\omega)\qquad\hbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $a\in A$, $b\in B$ and $\omega\in\Omega$.}

Throughout the paper, we will denote by
\mathscr{H}_{dG}
the subclass of Hamiltonians in
\mathscr{H}
that can be put in the form (
H
) with
f
and
\ell
satisfying assumptions (
f
) and (
\ell_{1}
)–(
\ell_{2}
), respectively.
A Hamiltonian
H
belonging to
\mathscr{H}_{dG}
will be furthermore termed stationary to mean that assumption (
\ell_{3}
) is in force.

The specific form (
H
) of the Hamiltonian allows to represent solutions to equation (
2.1
) via suitable formulae issued from Differential Games, see Appendix
A.3
for more details.
In the sequel, we shall denote by
\|\ell\|_{\infty}
the
L^{\infty}
–norm of
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\times\Omega
, which is finite due to (
\ell_{1}
), (
\ell_{3}
), and the ergodicity assumption on
\Omega
.

To prove our homogenization result, we will assume the following additional crucial assumptions:

(
\ell_{4}
)

(long-range independence)
the random variables
\left(\ell(\cdot,a,b,\cdot)\right)_{(a,b)\in A\times B}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
exhibit long-range independence, i.e., there exists
\rho>0
such that (
FRD
) holds with
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq A\times B
and
X_{i}\coloneqq\ell(\cdot,a,b,\cdot)
where
i=(a,b)
;

(
f
)

(oriented dynamics)
the dynamics given by
f\colon A\times B\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
oriented
, i.e., there exists
\delta>0
and a direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that

\langle f(a,b),e\rangle\geqslant\delta\qquad\hbox{for all $(a,b)\in A\times B$.}

Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
H
be a stationary Hamiltonian belonging to
\mathscr{H}_{dG}
and satisfying hypotheses (
\ell_{4}
) and (
f
).
Then, the HJ equation (
HJε
) homogenizes, i.e., there exists a continuous function
{\overline{H}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, called effective Hamiltonian, such that, for every uniformly continuous function
g
on
\mathbb{R}
, there exists a set
\Omega_{g}
of probability
1
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{g}
, the solutions
u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,\omega)
of (
HJε
) satisfying
u^{\varepsilon}(0,\,\cdot\,,\omega)=g
converges, locally uniformly on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, to the unique solution
\overline{u}
of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\overline{u}+{\overline{H}}(D_{x}\overline{u})=0&\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\\
\overline{u}(0,\,\cdot\,)=g&\hbox{in $\mathbb{R}$}.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
{\overline{H}}
satisfies (H1) and (H2).

Remark 2.3
.

We stress that a Hamiltonian of the form (
H
) with
f
satisfying condition (
f
) is never coercive. Indeed,

\lim_{t\to-\infty}H(x,te,\omega)=+\infty,\quad\lim_{t\to+\infty}H(x,te,\omega)=-\infty\qquad\hbox{for every $(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega$.}

By exploiting suitable Lipschitz bounds for solutions to (
HJε
) with Lipschitz initial data and a localization argument inspired by
[
27
, Section 5]
, we are able to extend the homogenization result stated above to the subclass of Hamiltonians in
\mathscr{H}
described in the statement of the next theorem.
This new subclass has nonempty intersection with
\mathscr{H}_{dG}
, but it is not fully contained in it.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
G
be a stationary Hamiltonian belonging to
\mathscr{H}
and satisfying the following assumption:

(G1)

the random variables
\left(G(\cdot,p,\cdot)\right)_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
exhibit long-rate independence, i.e., there exists
\rho>0
such that (
FRD
) holds with
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
X_{i}\coloneqq H(\cdot,p,\cdot)
where
i=p
.

Then, the homogenization result stated in Theorem
2.2
holds for any Hamiltonian
H
of the form

H(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq G(x,\pi(p),\omega)+\langle p,v\rangle
, where
v
is a non-null vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear map such that
\pi(v)=0
.

Examples of Hamiltonians
G
lying in
\mathscr{H}
and satisfying
(G1)
are the ones of the form
G(x,p,\omega)\coloneqq G_{0}(p)+V(x,\omega)
, where
G_{0}
belongs to
\mathscr{H}
and
V\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a stationary function, globally bounded and Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which satisfies (
FRD
) with
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\left\{0\right\}
and
X_{0}\coloneqq V
.

3.
Reduction arguments for homogenization

In this section we describe the reduction strategy that we will follow to prove Theorem
2.2
.
The first step consists in noticing that, in order to prove homogenization for equation (
HJε
), it is enough to restrict to linear initial data instead of any
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The precise statement is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
H
satisfy hypotheses (H1)-(H3) and denote by
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
the unique continuous solution of equation (
HJε
) satisfying
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(0,x,\omega)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
for all
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
and for every fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Assume there exists a function
\overline{H}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following convergence takes place for every
\omega
in a set
\Omega_{\theta}
of probability
1
:

(3.1)

\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,x,\omega)\ {\rightrightarrows}\ \langle\theta,x\rangle-t\overline{H}(\theta)\quad\hbox{in $[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$ as $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$.}

Then,
\overline{H}
satisfies condition (H1)-(H2).
Furthermore, for every fixed
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a set
\Omega_{g}
of probability
1
such that the unique function
u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,\omega)\in\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
which solves (
HJε
) with initial
condition
u^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot,\omega)=g
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
converges, locally uniformly in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, to the unique solution
\overline{u}\in\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
of

(3.2)

\partial_{t}\overline{u}+\overline{H}(D_{x}\overline{u})=0\qquad\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}

with the initial condition
\overline{u}(0,\cdot)=g
, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{g}
.

This reduction argument, which is of deterministic nature, was already contained in the pioneering work
[
34
]
on periodic homogenization, at least as far as first-order HJ equations are concerned.
This holds, in fact, even in the case when equation (
HJε
) presents an additional (vanishing) diffusive and possibly degenerate term.
A proof of this can be found in
[
23
]
and is given for Hamiltonians that are coercive in the
p
-variable.
Such a class does not include the kind of Hamiltonians we consider here, as pointed out in Remark
2.3
.
Yet, the extension follows by arguing as in
[
23
]
, with the only difference that one has to use a different Comparison Principle, namely Theorem
A.3
, in place of
[
23
, Proposition 2.4]
.
We refer the reader to
Appendix

B
for the detailed argument.

Theorem
3.1
yields, in particular, that the effective Hamiltonian
\overline{H}
is identified by the following almost sure limit:

\overline{H}(\theta)\coloneqq-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,0,\omega)\qquad\hbox{for every fixed $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

The second step in the reduction consists in observing that, in order to prove the local uniform convergence required to apply
Theorem

3.1
, it is enough to prove it for a fixed value of the time variable, that we chose equal to
1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\omega\in\Omega
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed, and assume that

(3.3)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{y\in B_{R}}|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,y,\omega)-\langle\theta,x\rangle+\overline{H}(\theta)|=0\qquad\hbox{for every $R>0$.}

Then, for every
T>0
,

(3.4)

\tilde{u}_{\theta}^{\varepsilon}(t,x,\omega)\rightrightarrows\langle\theta,x\rangle-t\overline{H}(\theta)\quad\text{in }[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

Since
\omega
will remain fixed throughout the proof, we will omit it from our notation.
Let us fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We first take note of the following scaling relations

\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,x)=\varepsilon\tilde{u}^{1}_{\theta}(t/\varepsilon,x/\varepsilon)=t(\varepsilon/t)\tilde{u}^{1}_{\theta}(t/\varepsilon,x/\varepsilon)=t\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon/t}_{\theta}(1,x/t)\qquad\text{for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Fix
T>0
.
Then, for every fixed
r\in(0,T)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{r\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{y\in B_{R}}\left|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,y)-\langle\theta,y\rangle+t\overline{H}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{r\leqslant t\leqslant T}\;\sup_{y\in B_{R}}\left|t\big(\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon/t}_{\theta}(1,y/t)-\langle\theta,y/t\rangle+\overline{H}(\theta)\big)\right|

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant T\sup_{\varepsilon/T\leqslant\eta\leqslant\varepsilon/r}\;\sup_{z\in B_{R/r}}\left|\tilde{u}^{\eta}_{\theta}(1,z)-\langle\theta,z\rangle+\overline{H}(\theta)\right|\,.

By (
3.3
), the right-hand side goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.
On the other hand, in view of Proposition
A.6
-(i) and of the fact that
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(0,x)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant r}\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,y)-\langle\theta,y\rangle+t\overline{H}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant r|\overline{H}(\theta)|+\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant r}\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,y)-\langle\theta,y\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant r\left(|\overline{H}(\theta)|+\|\ell\|_{\infty}+\|f\|_{\infty}\right)\,.

Assertion (
3.4
) follows from this and (
3
) by the arbitrariness of the choice of
r\in(0,T)
.
∎

In order to simplify some arguments, we find convenient to work with solutions with zero initial datum.
We can always reduce to this case, without any loss of generality, by setting
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,x,\omega)\coloneqq\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(t,x,\omega)-\langle\theta,x\rangle
for all
(t,x,\omega)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
.
The function
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
is the unique continuous function which solves equation (
HJε
) with
H_{\theta}\coloneqq H(\cdot,\theta+\cdot,\omega)
in place of
H
and which satisfies the initial condition
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(0,x,\omega)=0
for all
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
.
Note that the Hamiltonian
H_{\theta}
is still given by the max-min formula (
H
) where
\ell
is replaced by
\ell_{\theta}(x,a,b,\omega)\coloneqq\ell(x,a,b,\omega)+\langle f(a,b),\theta\rangle
.
Furthermore,
\ell_{\theta}
satisfies the same conditions (
\ell_{1}
)–(
\ell_{4}
).

The last reduction remark consists in noticing that the following rescaling relation holds

u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,x,\omega)=\varepsilon u_{\theta}(1/\varepsilon,x/\varepsilon,\omega)\qquad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\varepsilon>0$,}

where we have denoted by
u_{\theta}
the function
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
with
\varepsilon=1
.

In the light of all this, the proof of
Theorem

2.2
is thus reduced to show that, for every fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a set
\Omega_{\theta}
of probability
1
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\theta}
, we have

(3.6)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\;\sup_{y\in B_{R}}|u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,y,\omega)+\overline{H}(\theta)|=\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\;\sup_{y\in B_{tR}}\left|\frac{u_{\theta}(t,y,\omega)}{t}+\overline{H}(\theta)\right|=0\quad\hbox{for all $R>0$,}

for some deterministic function
\overline{H}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

4.
Probabilistic concentration

In this section we shall prove, by making use of Azuma’s martingale inequality, that
u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
is concentrated.
For this, we will take advantage of the fact that
u_{\theta}
can be expressed via the Differential Game Theoretic formula (
A.17
) with initial datum
g\equiv 0
and running cost
\ell_{\theta}(x,a,b,\omega)\coloneqq\ell(x,a,b,\omega)+\langle f(a,b),\theta\rangle
.
Here is where assumptions (
FRD
) and
(f)
play a crucial role, by ensuring altogether that the solution of (
HJε
) is robust with respect to local perturbations of the cost function
\ell
.

Throughout this section, we will weaken the conditions on the running cost
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and we will assume
\ell
to be only jointly measurable and such to satisfy conditions (
\ell_{1}
)-(
\ell_{2}
).
In particular, no continuity and stationarity conditions with respect to
x
will be required.

We start by recalling a classic theorem on concentration of martingales, also known as Azuma’s inequality.

Lemma 4.1

(Concentration of martingales
[
12
,
31
]
)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a martingale and
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a real sequence such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
|X_{n}-X_{n+1}|\leqslant c_{n}
almost surely.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
M>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|X_{n}-X_{0}|\geqslant M)\leqslant 2\exp\left(\frac{-M^{2}}{2\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}c_{m}^{2}}\right)\,.

The probabilistic concentration result mentioned before is stated as follows.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exists a constant
c=c(\rho,\delta,\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell),\left\|f\right\|_{\infty})>0
, only depending on
\rho
,
\delta
,
\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)
and
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
, such that, for all
M>0
and
t\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)-U_{\theta}(t)|\geqslant M\sqrt{t}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-cM^{2}\right)\,,

where
U_{\theta}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)]
denotes the expectation of the random variable
u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
.

Remark 4.3
.

We have tailored the proof of Proposition
4.2
in such a way that the constant
c
appearing in the statement does not depend on
\|\ell\|_{\infty}
. This is crucial in view of the extension of the homogenization result provided in Theorem
2.4
.

To prove
Proposition

4.2
, we need a technical lemma first.
The result is deterministic, hence the dependence on
\omega
will be omitted.
It expresses that, thanks to the condition (
f
) on the dynamics, we can control the variation of the value function as we change the running cost on a strip that is orthogonal to the direction
e
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any given pair
R<\widehat{R}
in
\mathbb{R}
, let us define the strip between
R
and
\widehat{R}
as follows:

S_{R,\widehat{R}}\coloneqq\{x:\langle x,e\rangle\in[R,\widehat{R}]\}\,.

Let
\ell,\widehat{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel–measurable bounded running costs and let
f\colon A\times B\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a continuous vector field satisfying condition (
f
).
For every fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us denote by
u_{\theta}(t,x)
,
\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,x)
the value functions defined via (
A.17
) with initial datum
g\equiv 0
and running cost
\ell_{\theta}(x,a,b,\omega)\coloneqq\ell(x,a,b,\omega)+\langle f(x,a,b),\theta\rangle
and
\widehat{\ell}_{\theta}(x,a,b,\omega)\coloneqq\widehat{\ell}(x,a,b,\omega)+\langle f(x,a,b),\theta\rangle
, respectively.
If
\ell=\widehat{\ell}
on
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{R,\widehat{R}}\right)\times A\times B
, we have

|u_{\theta}(t,x)-\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,x)|\leqslant\frac{R-\widehat{R}}{\delta}\|\ell-\widehat{\ell}\|_{\infty}\qquad\hbox{for all $(t,x)\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

We point out the generality of the previous statement: no continuity conditions on the running costs
\ell,\,\widehat{\ell}
are assumed in the above statement, and the functions
u_{\theta},\,\widehat{u}_{\theta}
are still well defined.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
and
x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
R<\widehat{R}
be arbitrary in
\mathbb{R}
and consider the strip
S_{R,\widehat{R}}
.
Fix controls
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
and
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
and consider the solution
y_{x}\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{y}_{x}(s)=f(\alpha[b](s),b(s))\qquad\hbox{in $[0,t]$}\\
y_{x}(0)=x.\end{cases}

From the orientation of the game, the map
s\mapsto\langle y_{x}(s),e\rangle
is strictly increasing in
[0,t]
.
More precisely,

(4.1)

\dfrac{d}{ds}\langle y_{x}(s),e\rangle=\langle\dot{y}_{x}(s),e\rangle=\langle f(\alpha[b](s),b(s)),e\rangle\geqslant\delta\qquad\hbox{for all $s\in[0,t]$}.

If
\langle y_{x}(0),e\rangle=\langle x,e\rangle\geqslant\widehat{R}
, we derive that the curve
y_{x}
always lies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{R,\hat{R}}
and the assertion trivially follows since
\ell=\widehat{\ell}
on
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{R,\widehat{R}}\right)\times A\times B
.
Let us then assume that
\langle y_{x}(0),e\rangle<\widehat{R}
and define two exit times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
as follows:

t_{1}\coloneqq\inf\left\{s\in[0,t]:\langle y_{x}(s),e\rangle>R\right\}\,,\qquad t_{2}\coloneqq\sup\left\{s\in[0,t]:R<\langle y_{x}(s),e\rangle<\widehat{R}\right\},

where we agree that
t_{1}=t_{2}=t
when the sets above are empty.
Notice that
t_{2}-t_{1}\leqslant(\widehat{R}-R)/{\delta}
.
Indeed, if
t_{2}-t_{1}>0
, then
t_{1}<t
and, by continuity of
y_{x}
and (
4.1
), we have
\langle y_{x}(t_{1}),e\rangle=R
.
From (
4.1
), we infer

\displaystyle\widehat{R}-R\geqslant\langle y_{x}(t_{2})-y_{x}(t_{1}),e\rangle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\langle f(\alpha[b](s),b(s)),e\rangle\,ds\geqslant(t_{2}-t_{1})\delta\,,

as it was claimed.
Also, if
t_{2}<t
, then, from (
4.1
), we have that
\langle y_{x}(t),e\rangle>\widehat{R}
for every
t\in(t_{2},t)
.
Consider deterministic running costs
\ell,\widehat{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\ell=\widehat{\ell}
on
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{R,\widehat{R}}\right)\times A\times B
.
Then, in view of the previous remarks, we get

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\ell_{\theta}(y_{x}(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t))

\displaystyle-\widehat{\ell}_{\theta}(y_{x}(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t))\Big)\,ds\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\Big(\ell(y_{x}(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t))-\widehat{\ell}(y_{x}(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t))\Big)\,ds\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant(t_{2}-t_{1})\|\ell-\widehat{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{\widehat{R}-R}{\delta}\|\ell-\widehat{\ell}\|_{\infty}\,.

The assertion easily follows from this by arbitrariness of the choice of the control
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
and of the strategy
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
.
∎

In the proof of
Proposition

4.2
, we look at the conditional expectation of
u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
given the running cost in a neighborhood of zero.
Taking a suitable sequence of increasing neighborhoods, we define the corresponding martingale and note that, by
Lemma

4.4
, it has bounded differences. Then, applying Azuma’s inequality, see
Lemma

4.1
, we get the result.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Fix
t>0
.
Recall the long-range independence parameter
\rho
.
Denote
n\coloneqq\lceil t\rceil
and
C\coloneqq\lceil\|f\|_{\infty}/\rho\rceil
, where
\lceil\cdot\rceil
stands for the upper integer part.
Note that, for all
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
and
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
, the solution
y_{0}\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{y}_{0}(s)=f(\alpha[b](s),b(s))\qquad\hbox{in $[0,t]$}\\
y_{0}(0)=0\end{cases}

satisfies that
|y_{0}(s)|\leqslant\rho\,C\,n
for all
s\in[0,t]
.
For
r\in\{1,2,\ldots,Cn\}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{r}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
\{\ell(y,a,b,\cdot):a\in A,b\in B,\langle y,e\rangle\leqslant\rho r\}
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{0}
be the trivial
\sigma
-algebra.
We claim that, for all
0\leqslant r<Cn
, we have that

(4.2)

|\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r+1}]-\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r}]|\leqslant\frac{8\rho^{2}}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)\,.

Indeed, recall that

S_{r,\widehat{r}}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle y,e\rangle\in[r,\widehat{r}]\}

and consider
\widehat{\ell}
defined by
\widehat{\ell}(x,a,b,\omega)=\ell(x-2\rho e,a,b,\omega)
for
(x,a,b,\omega)\in S_{\rho r,\rho(r+2)}\times A\times B\times\Omega
, and
\widehat{\ell}(x,a,b,\omega)=\ell(x,a,b,\omega)
otherwise.
Then, by
Lemma

4.4
, almost surely we have that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left|u_{\theta}(t,0,\omega)-\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,0,\omega)\right|\leqslant{\frac{2\rho}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)2\rho=\frac{4\rho^{2}}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)}.

where
u_{\theta}
(resp.
\widehat{u}_{\theta}
) is the value function associated via (
A.17
) with the running cost
\ell_{\theta}\coloneqq\ell+\langle f(a,b),\theta\rangle
(resp.
\widehat{\ell}_{\theta}\coloneqq\widehat{\ell}+\langle f(a,b),\theta\rangle
) and initial datum
g\equiv 0
.
By definition,
\widehat{u}_{\theta}
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\widehat{\ell}(x,a,b,\cdot):a\in A,b\in B,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, which coincides with the
\sigma
-field generated by
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\{\ell(x,a,b,\cdot):a\in A,b\in B,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{\rho r,\rho(r+2)}\}
.
By long-range independence, the
\sigma
-field generated by
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\{\ell(x,a,b,\cdot):a\in A,b\in B,\langle x,e\rangle\geqslant\rho(r+2)\}
is independent of the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{r+1}
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\ell}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{r+1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\ell}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{r}\right]\,,

and so we obtain that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{r+1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{r}\right]\,.

Finally, using successively (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we prove the claim:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r+1}]-\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r}]|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r+1}]-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{u}_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r}]|+\frac{8\rho^{2}}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\rho^{2}}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)\,.

For
r\leqslant Cn
, denote
W_{r}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{r}]
.
The process
(W_{r})_{r\leqslant Cn}
is a martingale, and
W_{0}=\mathbb{E}[u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)]=U_{\theta}(t)
.
Moreover, since
u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
is
\mathcal{F}_{Cn}
-measurable, we have that
W_{Cn}(\cdot)=u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
.
By Azuma’s inequality, see
Lemma

4.1
, applied to the martingale
(W_{\tau})_{\tau\leqslant C\,n}
and (
4.2
), for all
M\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)-U_{\theta}(t)|\geqslant M) =\mathbb{P}(|W_{Cn}-W_{0}|\geqslant M) \leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{M^{2}}{2Cn\left(\frac{8\rho^{2}}{\delta}\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)\right)^{2}}\right)\,.

Therefore, there exists a constant
c>0
, only depending on
\rho
,
\delta
,
\mbox{\rm Lip}(\ell)
, and
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
(through
C
), but not on
\theta
, such that, for all
M>0
and
t\geqslant 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)-U_{\theta}(t)|\geqslant M\sqrt{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-cM^{2}\right)\,,

as it was asserted.
∎

5.
Proof of the homogenization results

This section is devoted to the proof of our homogenization results stated in Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.
We will denote by
H
a stationary Hamiltonian belonging to the class
\mathscr{H}
, and by
u_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot,\omega)
the solution of the equation (
HJε
) with
\varepsilon=1
,
H_{\theta}\coloneqq H(\cdot,\theta+\cdot\,,\omega)
in place of
H
, and initial condition
u_{\theta}(0,x,\omega)=0
for all
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
.

Note that, since the initial condition is zero, the function
u_{\theta}
is stationary, i.e., for all
(t,x,\omega)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
u_{\theta}(t,x+y,\omega)=u_{\theta}(t,x,\tau_{y}\omega)
,
\mathbb{P}
–almost surely in
\Omega
.

We also recall that
U_{\theta}(t)
denotes the expectation of the random variable
u_{\theta}(t,0,\cdot)
.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

In this subsection, we will furthermore assume that
H
belongs to the class
\mathscr{H}_{dG}
, so that
u_{\theta}(t,x,\omega)
can be represented via (
A.17
) with initial datum
g\equiv 0
and running cost
\ell_{\theta}(x,a,b,\omega)\coloneqq\ell(x,a,b,\omega)+\langle f(x,a,b),\theta\rangle
.
This allows us to make use of the results obtained in Section
4
.

According to Section
3
, the proof of Theorem
2.2
boils down to establishing the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

For every fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a set
\Omega_{\theta}
of probability
1
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\theta}
, we have

(5.1)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{y\in B_{R}}|u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,y,\omega)+\overline{H}(\theta)|=\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{y\in B_{tR}}\left|\frac{u_{\theta}(t,y,\omega)}{t}+\overline{H}(\theta)\right|=0\quad\hbox{for all $R>0$,}

for some deterministic function
\overline{H}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

The proof of Proposition
5.1
is based on the following fact.

Proposition 5.2
.

For all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
U_{\theta}(t)/t
converges as
t
goes to infinity.

The proof of Proposition
5.2
consists in showing that
U_{\theta}(t)
, satisfies an approximated subadditive inequality.
We postpone this proof and proceed to show how to exploit this fact to prove
Proposition

.

v(t_{0},x_{0})-w(t_{0},x_{0})-2\eta\phi(x_{0})-\frac{2b}{T-t_{0}}=\sup_{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x,t,x)>\frac{\theta}{2}.

In particular, such a point
(t_{0},x_{0})
actually lies in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
t_{0}>0
, since (
A.7
) implies
v(t_{0},x_{0})-w(t_{0},x_{0})>0
.
For every fixed
\eta\in(0,\delta)
, choose
\varepsilon_{\eta}>0
small enough so that
(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})
and
(s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
both belong to
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\eta})
. The function

\varphi(t,x)\coloneqq w(s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+\frac{|x-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\eta\left(\phi(x)+\phi(y_{\varepsilon})\right)+\frac{|t-s_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{b}{T-t}

is a supertangent to
v(t,x)
at the point
(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})
and
v
is a subsolution of (
HJ
), while the function

\psi(s,y)\coloneqq v(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})-\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\eta\left(\phi(x_{\varepsilon})+\phi(y)\right)-\frac{|t_{\varepsilon}-s|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\frac{b}{T-s}

is a subtangent to
w(s,y)
at the point
(s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
and
w
is a subsolution of (
HJ
). We infer

(A.8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon}-s_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}+H\left(x_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}+\eta D\phi(x_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle-c,

(A.9)

\displaystyle\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon}-s_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}+H\left(y_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}-\eta D\phi(y_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle c,

where we have set

c\coloneqq\frac{b}{T^{2}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad p_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}.

By subtracting (
A.8
) from (
A.9
), we get, according to assumptions
(
H2′
)-(
H4
),

\displaystyle 0<2c\leqslant H\left(y_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}-\eta D\phi(y_{\varepsilon})\right)-H\left(x_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}+\eta D\phi(x_{\varepsilon})\right)\leqslant\omega\big(|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|(1+|p_{\varepsilon}|)\big)+2\omega\left(\eta\right).

Sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we have that
\omega\big(|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|(1+|p_{\varepsilon}|)\big)
vanishes in view of (
A.6
).
Sending
\eta\to 0^{+}
, we have that
\omega\left(\eta\right)
vanishes.
But this is a contradiction since
0<c
.
∎

Next, we provide a result which is usually used to prove the finite speed of propagation of first order HJ equations, see for instance
[
18
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian satisfying (
H4
) and condition
(H2)
, for some constant
\beta>0
.
Let
v\in\mbox{\rm USC}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
w\in\mbox{\rm LSC}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be, respectively, a sub and a supersolution of (
HJ
). Then the function
u\coloneqq v-w
is an upper semicontinuous subsolution to

(A.10)

\partial_{t}u-\beta|Du|=0\qquad\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be supertangent to
u
in a point
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let us assume that
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a strict local maximum point of
u-\varphi
. Let us choose
r>0
such that the open ball
B\coloneqq B_{r}\big((t_{0},x_{0})\big)
of radius
r>0
centred at
(t_{0},x_{0})
is compactly contained in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(t_{0},x_{0})
is the only maximum point of
u-\varphi
in
\overline{B}
. Let us introduce the function

\Phi(t,x,s,y)\coloneqq v(t,x)-w(s,y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\frac{|t-s|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\varphi(t,x)\qquad\hbox{ for $(t,x,s,y)\in\overline{B}\times\overline{B}$.}

By uppersemicontinuity of
\Phi
and compactness of the domain, the maximum of
\Phi
on
\overline{B}\times\overline{B}
is attained at (at least) a point
(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon},s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\in\overline{B}\times\overline{B}
. In view of
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
, we infer that

(A.11)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon},s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=(t_{0},x_{0},t_{0},x_{0})\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}=0

Choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
small enough so that
(x_{\varepsilon},t_{\varepsilon})
,
(y_{\varepsilon},s_{\varepsilon})
belong to
B
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
. The function

\psi_{1}(t,x)\coloneqq w(s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+\frac{|x-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{|t-s_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\varphi(t,x)

is a supertangent to
v(t,x)
at
(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})
and
v
is a subsolution to (
HJ
), hence

(A.12)

\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon}-s_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}+\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})+H\left(x_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}+D_{x}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})\right)\leqslant 0,

where we have set
\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}
. Analogously,

\psi_{2}(s,y)\coloneqq v(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})-\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\frac{|t_{\varepsilon}-s|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})

is a subtangent to
w(s,y)
at the point
(s_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
and
w
is a supersolution to (
HJ
), hence

(A.13)

\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon}-s_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}+H\left(y_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}\right)\geqslant 0,

By subtracting (
A.13
) from (
A.12
) and by taking into account conditions (
H4
)-(
), we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})+H\left(x_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}+D_{x}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})\right)-H\left(y_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})-\beta|D_{x}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})|+H\left(x_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}\right)-H\left(y_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})-\beta|D_{x}\varphi(t_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})|-\omega\big(|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|(1+|p_{\varepsilon}|)\big).

Now we send
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
to get, in view of (
A.11
),

0\geqslant\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})-\beta|D_{x}\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})|,

as it was to be shown.
∎

With the aid of the previous proposition, we can prove the following version of the Comparison Principle for unbounded sub and supersolutions.

Theorem A.3
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian satisfying assumptions
(H2)
and (
H4
).
Let
v\in\mbox{\rm USC}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
w\in\mbox{\rm LSC}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be, respectively, a sub and a supersolution of (
HJ
).
Then,

v(t,x)-w(t,x)\leqslant\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(v(0,\cdot)-w(0,\cdot)\big)\qquad\hbox{ for every $(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

Proof.

Up to adding to
v
a suitable constant and to the trivial case
\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(v(0,\cdot)-w(0,\cdot)\big)=+\infty
, we can assume, without any loss of generality, that

\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(v(0,\cdot)-w(0,\cdot)\big)=0
. In view of Proposition
A.2
, the function
u\coloneqq v-w
is an upper semicontinuous subsolution of (
A.10
) satisfying
u(0,\cdot)\leqslant 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
, strictly increasing and bounded function satisfying
\Psi(0)=0
. It is easily seen, due to the positive 1–homogeneity of equation (
A.2
), that the function
(\Psi\circ u)(t,x)
is a bounded, upper semicontinuous subsolution to (
A.2
). By applying Theorem
A.1
we derive that
(\Psi\circ u)\leqslant 0=\Psi(0)
in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The assertion follows by the strict monotonicity of
\Psi
.
∎

A.2.
Existence results and Lipschitz estimates for solutions

Throughout this subsection, we will assume that the (deterministic) Hamiltonian
H
satisfying the following assumptions for some constants
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}>0
:

(H1
′
)

|H(x,p,\omega)|\leqslant\beta_{1}\left(1+|p|\right)\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \text{for all}\ (x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(H2
′
)

|H(x,p,\omega)-H(x,q,\omega)|\leqslant\beta_{2}|p-q|\qquad\qquad\text{for all}\ x,p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(H3
′
)

|H(x,p,\omega)-H(y,p,\omega)|\leqslant\beta_{3}|x-y|\qquad\quad\quad\text{for all}\ x,y,p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We begin with the following existence and uniqueness result, where the uniqueness part is guaranteed by Theorem
A.3
.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For every
T>0
, the following problem

(HJP)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+H(x,Du)=0&\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\\
&u(0,\cdot)=g(\cdot)&\hbox{on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\end{aligned}\right.

has a unique viscosity solution in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, this solution belongs to
\mbox{\rm UC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

The case
g\in\mbox{\rm BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is proved in
[
18
, Theorem 7.1]
.
Let us then assume
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Pick

\psi\in\mbox{\rm C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mbox{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|\psi-g\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. In view of the previous step, the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) with
\tilde{H}(x,p)\coloneqq H(x,D\psi(x)+p)
and
g-\psi
in place of
H
and
g
, respectively, admits a unique solution
\tilde{u}(t,x)
in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore,

\tilde{u}(t,x)
in
\mbox{\rm UC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We derive that
u(t,x)\coloneqq\tilde{u}(t,x)+\psi(x)
belongs to
\mbox{\rm UC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and is a solution of the original Cauchly problem (
HJP
).
∎

We proceed to show suitable Lipschitz bounds for the solution of (
HJP
) when the initial datum is Lipschitz.

Theorem A.5
.

Let
g\in\mbox{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
u
be the unique continuous function in
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
which solves the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) for every
T>0
.
Then
u
is Lipchitz in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for every
T>0
. More precisely

\|D_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\left(\beta_{3}t+\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)\right),\qquad\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\beta_{1}\left(1+\|Dg\|_{\infty}\right).

Proof.

(i) Let us fix
{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set

v_{h}(t,x)\coloneqq u(t,x+{h})-\beta_{3}|{h}|t,\quad w_{h}(t,x)\coloneqq u(t,x+{h})+\beta_{3}|{h}|t\qquad\hbox{for every $(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

By exploiting assumption (H2
′
), it is easily seen that
v_{h}
and
w_{h}
are, respectively, a viscosity sub and supersolution to (
HJ
).
Indeed, the following inequalities hold in the viscosity sense:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{h}+H(x,Dv_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x+h)-\beta_{3}|h|+H(x,Du(t,x+h))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x+h)+H(x+h,Du(t,x+h))\leqslant 0\qquad\hbox{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

thus showing that
v_{h}
is a subsolution of (
HJ
). The assertion for
w_{h}
can be proved analogously.
By the Comparison Principle, see Theorem
A.3
, we infer that, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle u(t,x)-v_{h}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle u(0,x)-v_{h}(0,x)=g(x)-g(x+h)\leqslant\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)|h|

\displaystyle w_{h}(t,x)-u(t,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle w_{h}(0,x)-u(0,x)=g(x+h)-g(x)\leqslant\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)|h|,

namely

(A.14)

|u(t,x+h)-u(t,x)|\leqslant\left(\beta_{3}t+\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)\right)|h|\qquad\hbox{for all $(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$,}

thus showing the first assertion.

(ii) Let us first assume that
g\in\mbox{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mbox{\rm C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By assumption (H1
′
) we have

(A.15)

|H(x,Dg(x))|\leqslant\beta_{1}\left(1+|Dg(x)|\right)\leqslant\beta_{1}\left(1+\|Dg\|_{\infty}\right)\qquad\hbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

For notational simplicity, let us denote by
M
the most right-hand side term in the above inequality. It is easily seen that the functions

\underline{u}(t,x)\coloneqq g(x)-Mt,\quad\overline{u}(t,x)\coloneqq g(x)+Mt,\qquad(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

are, respectively, a sub and a supersolution of (
HJP
) for every
T>0
. By the Comparison Principle, see Theorem
A.3
, we infer that

\underline{u}(t,x)\leqslant u(t,x)\leqslant\overline{u}(t,x)\ \hbox{for all $(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

i.e.,

\|u(t,\cdot)-g\|_{\infty}\leqslant Mt\qquad\hbox{for all $t\geqslant 0$.}

By applying the Comparison Principle again we get

(A.16)

\|u(t+h,\cdot)-u(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|u(h,\cdot)-u(0,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant Mh=\beta_{1}\left(1+\|Dg\|_{\infty}\right)\,h\quad\hbox{for all $t,h\geqslant 0$,}

meaning that
u
is
\beta_{1}\left(1+\|Dg\|_{\infty}\right)
–Lipschitz in
t
.

The case
g\in\mbox{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be obtained by approximation. Let us denote by
g_{n}
the mollification of
g
via a standard convolution kernel and by
u_{n}
the solution to the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) with
g_{n}
in place of
g
. Since
\|Dg_{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|Dg\|_{\infty}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we deduce from what proved above that the functions
u_{n}
are
\beta_{1}\left(1+\|Dg\|_{\infty}\right)
–Lipschitz in
t
and
\left(\beta_{3}t+\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)\right)
–Lipschitz in
x
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the Ascoli–Arzelà Theorem and the stability of the notion of viscosity solution, we infer that the functions
(u_{n})_{n}
are converging, locally uniformly in
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, to the solution
u
of the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) with initial datum
g
. We derive that
u
satisfies (
A.16
) as well, as it was to be shown.
∎

A.3.
Differential Games

Throughout this subsection, we will work with a deterministic Hamiltonian
H
of the form

H(x,p)\coloneqq\max_{b\in B}\min_{a\in A}\left\{-\ell(x,a,b)-\langle f(a,b),p\rangle\right\}\qquad\text{for all }(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
A,B
are compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for some integer
m
,
f\colon A\times B\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is continuous vector field, and the running cost
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded and continuous function satisfying assumption (
\ell_{2}
) appearing in Section
2
. We shall denote by
\|\ell\|_{\infty}
the
L^{\infty}
–norm of
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\times B
.
The Hamiltonian
H
clearly satisfies the properties (H1)–(H3) listed in Section
2
.

Next, we provide a formula borrowed from Differential Game Theory to represent such solutions, see
[
27
]
. To this aim, we first observe that
u(t,x)
is a viscosity solution of the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) if and only
\check{u}(t,x)\coloneqq-u(T-t,x)
is a viscosity solution of equation (
HJ
) with
\check{H}(x,p)\coloneqq H(x,-p)
in place of
H
in the sense adopted in
[
27
]
, see items (a)-(b) at the end of page 774, and satisfying the terminal condition
\check{u}(T,x)=-g(x)
, cf.
[
27
, problem (HJ)]
.
Let us denote by

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}(T)\coloneqq\left\{a\colon[0,T]\to A\,:\,a\text{ measurable}\right\},\qquad\mathbb{B}(T)\coloneqq\left\{b\colon[0,T]\to B\,:\,b\text{ measurable}\right\}.

The sets
A
and
B
are to be regarded as action sets for Player 1 and 2, respectively.
A nonanticipating strategy for Player 1 is a function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{B}(T)\to\mathbb{A}(T)
such that,
for all
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{B}(T)
and
\tau\in[0,T]
,

b_{1}(\cdot)=b_{2}(\cdot)\quad\text{in }[0,\tau]\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha[b_{1}](\cdot)=\alpha[b_{2}](\cdot)\quad\text{in }[0,\tau]\,.

We will denote by
\alpha\in\Gamma(T)
the family of such nonanticipating strategies for Player 1.
For every
(t,x)\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us set

(A.17)

v(t,x)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))\,ds+g(y_{x}(t))\right\},

where
y_{x}\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the solution of the ODE

(ODE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{y}_{x}(s)=f(\alpha[b](s),b(s))\qquad\hbox{in $[0,t]$}\\
y_{x}(0)=x.\end{cases}

The function
v
defined by (
A.17
) is usually called
value function
.

Proposition A.6
.

(i)

Let
g\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
v\in W^{1,\infty}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every fixed
T>0
. More precisely, for every
x,\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t,\hat{t}\in(0,T)
, we have

\displaystyle|v(t,x)|\leqslant T\|\ell\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle|v(t,x)-v(\hat{t},\hat{x})|\leqslant\left(\beta t+|\theta|+\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)\right)|x-\hat{x}|+\left(\|\ell\|_{\infty}+\|f\|_{\infty}\mbox{\rm Lip}(g)\right)|t-\hat{t}|\,;

(ii)

Let
g\in\mbox{\rm BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
v\in\mbox{\rm BUC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every fixed
T>0
.

Proof.

Item (i) follows directly from
[
27
, Theorem 3.2]
after observing that
v(t,x)=-V(T-t,x)
, where
V
is the function given by (
A.17
)
with
-\ell
and
-g
in place of
\ell
and
g
, respectively.
Let us prove (ii).
Let
(g_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of functions in
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.
For every fixed
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, any fixed strategy
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
and every control
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
, let us set

J[\alpha,b,g](t,x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))\,ds+g(y_{x}(t)),

where
y_{x}\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the solution of (
ODE
). We have

J[\alpha,b,g_{n}](t,x)-\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}\leqslant J[\alpha,b,g](t,x)\leqslant J[\alpha,b,g_{n}](t,x)+\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}.

By taking the infimum with respect to
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
and then the supremum with respect to
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
, we infer

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}J[\alpha,\hat{b},g_{n}](t,x)-\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}J[\alpha,\hat{b},g](t,x)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}J[\alpha,b,g_{n}](t,x)+\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}.

This means that
|v_{n}(t,x)-v(t,x)|\leqslant\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
v_{n}
and
v
denote the value function associated to
g_{n}
and
g
, respectively. As a uniform limit of a sequence of equi–bounded Lipschitz functions, we conclude that
v
belongs to
\mbox{\rm BUC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Via the same argument used in the proof of Proposition
A.6
-(i), we derive from
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
the following fact, known as Dynamic Programming Principle.

Theorem A.7

(Dynamic Programming Principle)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0<\tau<T
be fixed. Then,

(A.18)

v(T,x)=\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(T-\tau)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(T-\tau)}\left\{\int_{0}^{T-\tau}\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))\,ds+v(\tau,y_{x}(T-\tau))\right\}\,.

The following holds.

Theorem A.8
.

Let
g\in\mbox{\rm BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For every fixed
T>0
, the unique continuous solution of (
HJP
) is given by
(
A.17
).

Proof.

When
g
is additionally assumed Lipschitz continuous, the assertion follows
directly from
[
27
, Theorem 4.1]
via the same argument used in the proof of Proposition
A.6
-(i) and in view of what remarked at the beginning of this subsection.
Let us assume
g\in\mbox{\rm BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and pick a sequence of functions
g_{n}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. Let us denote by
v
and
v_{n}
the value functions associated via (
A.17
) to
g
and
g_{n}
, respectively. Arguing as in the proof of
Proposition
A.6
-(ii), we derive

|v_{n}(t,x)-v(t,x)|\leqslant\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From the previous step we know that
v_{n}
solves
(
HJP
) with initial datum
g_{n}
. By the stability of the notion of viscosity solution, we conclude that
v
solves (
HJP
).
∎

We now extend the previous result to the case of initial data that are not necessarily bounded. The result is the following.

Theorem A.9
.

Let
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For every fixed
T>0
, the unique viscosity solution in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) is given by the representation formula (
A.17
).

Proof.

Let us pick
\psi\in\mbox{\rm C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mbox{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|\psi-g\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. In view of Theorem
A.8
, the unique solution
\tilde{u}(t,x)
in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the Cauchy problem (
HJP
) with
\tilde{H}(x,p)\coloneqq H(x,D\psi(x)+p)
and
g-\psi
in place of
H
and
g
, respectively, is given by the formula (
A.17
) with

\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))+\langle f(\alpha[b](s),b(s)),D\psi(y_{x}(s))\rangle
and
(g-\psi)(y_{x}(t)
in place of
g(y_{x}(t))
.
For every fixed strategy
\alpha\in\Gamma(t)
and every control
b\in\mathbb{B}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))+\langle f(\alpha[b](s),b(s)),D\psi(y_{x}(s))\rangle\Big)\,ds=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))+\dfrac{d}{ds}\psi(y_{x}(s))\right)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))\,ds+\psi(y_{x}(t))-\psi(x).

We infer

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))+\langle f(\alpha[b](s),b(s)),D\psi(y_{x}(s))\rangle\Big)\,ds+(g-\psi)(y_{x}(t))\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma(t)}\inf_{b\in\mathbb{B}(t)}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\ell(y_{x}(s),\alpha[b](s),b(s))\,ds+g(y_{x}(t))\right\}-\psi(x).

The conclusion readily follows after observing that the function
u(t,x)\coloneqq\tilde{u}(t,x)+\psi(x)
is the continuous solution of the original Cauchly problem (
HJP
).
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3.1

In this section we prove Theorem
3.1
. The result is of deterministic nature, with
\omega
treated as a fixed parameter. We will therefore omit it from our notation. We will need the following fact.

Proposition B.1
.

Let us assume that all the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1

are in force. Let
g\in\mbox{\rm UC}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and, for every
\varepsilon>0
, let us
denote by
u^{\varepsilon}
the unique function in
\mbox{\rm C}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
that solves (
HJε
)
subject to the initial condition

u^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=g
. Set

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\ \sup\{u^{\varepsilon}(s,y)\,:\,(s,y)\in(t-r,t+r)\times B_{r}(x),\ 0<\varepsilon<r\,\},

\displaystyle u_{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\ \inf\{u^{\varepsilon}(s,y)\,:\,(s,y)\in(t-r,t+r)\times B_{r}(x),\ 0<\varepsilon<r\,\}.

Let us assume that
u^{*}
and
u_{*}
are finite valued. Then

(i)

u^{*}\in\mbox{\rm USC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and it is a viscosity subsolution of (
3.2
);

(ii)

u_{*}\in\mbox{\rm LSC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and it is a viscosity supersolution of (
3.2
).

We postpone the proof of Proposition
B.1
and we first show how we employ it to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let us prove that
\overline{H}
satisfies condition (H1). To this aim, we remark that the functions
u^{+},\,u^{-}
defined as

u^{\pm}(t,x)\coloneqq\langle\theta,x\rangle\pm\beta\left(1+|\theta|\right)t,\qquad\hbox{$(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

are, respectively, a continuous super and subsolution of (
HJε
) satisfying
u^{\pm}(0,x)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
, for every
\varepsilon>0
in view of assumption (H1).
By Theorem
A.3
we infer
u^{-}\leqslant\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}\leqslant u^{+}
in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Hence

|\overline{H}(\theta)|=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(1,0)|\leqslant\beta\left(1+|\theta|\right),

as it was to be shown.
Let us now show that
\overline{H}
satisfies condition (H2). Fix
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set

u_{\theta_{2}}^{\varepsilon,\pm}(t,x)\coloneqq\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta_{2}}(t,x)+\langle\theta_{1}-\theta_{2},x\rangle\pm\beta|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|t,\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The function
u^{\varepsilon,+}_{\theta_{2}}
and
u^{\varepsilon,-}_{\theta_{2}}
are, respectively, a super and a subsolution of (
HJε
), in view of assumption (H2), which satisfy
u^{\varepsilon,\pm}_{\theta_{2}}(0,x)=\langle\theta_{1},x\rangle
. By Theorem
A.3
we derive that

u^{\varepsilon,-}_{\theta_{2}}\leqslant\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta_{1}}\leqslant u^{\varepsilon,+}_{\theta_{2}}
in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence

|\overline{H}(\theta_{1})-\overline{H}(\theta_{2})|=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta_{1}}(1,0)-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta_{2}}(1,0)|\leqslant\beta|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|.

We now proceed to prove the second part of the assertion.
Let us first assume
g\in{\mbox{\rm C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mbox{\rm Lip}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Take a constant
M
large enough so that

M>\|H(x,Dg(x))\|_{\infty}.

Then the functions
u_{-}(t,x)\coloneqq g(x)-Mt
and
u_{+}(t,x)\coloneqq g(x)+Mt
are,
respectively, a Lipschitz continuous sub and supersolution of
(
HJε
) for every
\varepsilon>0
. By the Comparison Principle stated in Theorem
A.3
, we get
u_{-}\leqslant u^{\varepsilon}\leqslant u_{+}
in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
every
\varepsilon>0
. By the definition of relaxed semilimits we
infer

u_{-}(t,x)\leqslant u_{*}(t,x)\leqslant u^{*}(t,x)\leqslant u_{+}(t,x)\quad\text{for all $(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

in particular,
u_{*}
,
u^{*}
satisfy

u_{*}(0,\cdot)=u^{*}(0,\cdot)=g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Proposition
B.1
, we know that
u^{*}
and
u_{*}
are, respectively, an upper
semicontinuous subsolution and a lower semicontinuous supersolution of
the effective equation (
3.2
). We can therefore
apply Theorem
A.3
again
to obtain
u^{*}\leqslant u_{*}
on

[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the opposite inequality holds by the definition of upper and
lower relaxed semilimits, we conclude that the function

\overline{u}(t,x)\coloneqq u_{*}(t,x)=u^{*}(t,x)\qquad\hbox{for all $(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}

is the unique continuous viscosity solution of (
3.2
) such that
\overline{u}(0,\cdot)=g
on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore, by Theorem
A.9
, we also know that
\overline{u}
belongs to
\mbox{\rm UC}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The fact that the relaxed
semilimits coincide implies that
u^{\varepsilon}
converge locally
uniformly in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\overline{u}
, see for instance
[
1
, Lemma
6.2, p. 80]
.

When the initial datum
g
is just uniformly continuous on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
result easily follows from the above by
approximating
g
with a sequence
(g_{n})_{n}
of initial data belonging to

{\mbox{\rm C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mbox{\rm Lip}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, if we denote by
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
u^{\varepsilon}
the solution of (
HJε
) with initial datum
g_{n}
and
g
, respectively, we have, in view of Theorem
A.3
,

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}-u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\qquad\hbox{for every $\varepsilon>0$.}

The assertion follows from this by the stability of the notion of viscosity solution.
∎

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

The fact that
u^{*}
and
u_{*}
are upper and lower semicontinuous on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an immediate consequence of their definition.
Let us prove (i), i.e., that
u^{*}
is a subsolution of (
3.2
).
The proof of (ii) is analogous.

We make use of Evans’s perturbed test function method, see
[
26
]
.
Let us assume, by contradiction, that
u^{*}
is not a subsolution of (
3.2
). Then there exists a function
\phi\in\mbox{\rm C}^{1}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
that is a strict supertangent of
u^{*}
at some point
(t_{0},x_{0})\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for which the subsolution test fails, i.e.,

(B.1)

\partial_{t}\phi(t_{0},x_{0})+\overline{H}(D_{x}\phi(t_{0},x_{0}))>2\delta

for some
\delta>0
. For
r>0
define
V_{r}\coloneqq(t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r)\times B_{r}(x_{0})
. Choose
r_{0}>0
to be small enough so that
V_{r_{0}}
is compactly contained in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u^{*}-\phi
attains a strict local maximum at
(t_{0},x_{0})
in

(B.2)

\max_{\partial V_{r}}(u^{*}-\phi)<\max_{\overline{V}_{r}}(u^{*}-\phi)=(u^{*}-\phi)(t_{0},x_{0}).

Let us set
\theta\coloneqq D_{x}\phi(t_{0},x_{0})
and for every
\varepsilon>0
denote by
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
the unique continuous function in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
that solves (
HJε
) subject to the initial condition
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(0,x)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
. We claim that there is an
r\in(0,r_{0})
such that the function

\phi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\coloneqq\phi(t,x)+\tilde{u}_{\theta}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\left(\langle\theta,x\rangle-t\overline{H}(\theta)\right)

is a supersolution of (
HJε
) in
V_{r}
for every
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Indeed, by a direct computation we first get

(B.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+H\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},D_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)=\partial_{t}\phi+\overline{H}(\theta)+\partial_{t}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}+H\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},D_{x}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}+D_{x}\phi-\theta\right)

in the viscosity sense in
V_{r}

Using (

\overline{H}
and the fact that

Moreover, by taking into account (H2), we can further reduce

in the viscosity sense. Plugging these relations into (

\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
is a solution of (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+H\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},D_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)>\delta+\partial_{t}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}+H\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},D\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}\right)=\delta>0

in the viscosity sense in

, thus showing that
\phi^{\varepsilon}
is a supersolution of (
HJε
) in
V_{r}
.
Now we need a comparison principle for equation (

) in
V_{r}

to infer that

\sup_{V_{r}}(u^{\varepsilon}-\phi^{\varepsilon}){\leqslant}\max_{\partial V_{r}}(u^{\varepsilon}-\phi^{\varepsilon}).

Since condition (H2) is in force, the validity of this comparison principle is guaranteed by

Now notice that, by the assumption (

in

Taking the limsup of the above inequality as

∎
