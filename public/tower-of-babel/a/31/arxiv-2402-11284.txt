Abstract
. The p p -modulus of curves, test plans, upper gradients, charts, differentials, approximations in energy and density of directions are all concepts associated to the theory of Sobolev functions in metric measure spaces. The purpose of this paper is to give an analogous geometric and “fragment-wise” theory for Lipschitz functions and Weaver derivations, where ∞ \infty -modulus of curve fragments, ∗ \ast -upper gradients and Alberti representations play a central role. We give a new definition of fragment-wise charts and prove that they exists for spaces with finite Hausdorff dimension. We give a replacement for p p -duality in terms of Alberti representations and ∞ \infty -modulus and present the theory of ∗ \ast -upper gradients. Further, we give new and sharper results for approximations of Lipschitz functions, which yields the density of directions. Our results are applicable to all complete and separable metric measure spaces. In the process, we show that there are strong parallels between the Sobolev and Lipschitz worlds.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Analysis on metric spaces, emerging at the turn of the millennium, is based on the deep observation of the importance that collections of curves in the metric space play in order to perform analysis.
Upper gradients – introduced and developed through the works of Heinonen–Koskela, Shanmugalingam, Cheeger and others
[
27
,
12
,
38
,
25
]
– control the oscillation of functions along curves, and lead to a theory of Poincaré inequalities and Sobolev spaces in metric spaces.

In his seminal work
[
12
]
, Cheeger proved a Rademacher type theorem in this context, giving rise to a fruitful first order calculus: doubling metric measure spaces
(X,d,\mu)
supporting a Poincaré inequality can be covered by countably many
charts

(U,\varphi)
, where
U\subset X
is Borel and
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Lipschitz, such that every Lipschitz function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
admits a unique differential
\mathrm{d}_{x}f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
(U,\varphi)
, characterized by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)-\mathrm{d}_{x}f(\varphi(y)-\varphi(x))|}{d(x,y)}=0,

for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in U
.
Metric measure spaces satisfying the conclusion of Cheeger’s differentiation theorem are now known as
Lipschitz differentiability spaces
.

Lipschitz differentiability on metric spaces has since seen a flurry of interest, and multiple research directions have developed, beginning with the work of Keith
[
32
]
who gave a weaker sufficient condition, the Lip-lip inequality, for the conclusion of Cheeger’s theorem to hold.
The work of the first named author
[
8
]
showed that Lipschitz differentiability spaces necessarily possess a rich structure of 1-rectifiable curves (known as Alberti representations, see below) which determine the differentiable structure.
Stronger variants which require (
1.1
) to hold for all Lipschitz functions taking values in an RNP Banach space have been used to establish non-embedding results
[
13
]
.
It has been shown that such RNP differentiability spaces are covered by countably many isometric images of spaces satisfying the Poincaré inequality
[
10
,
20
]
.
It is known
[
36
]
that differentiability of
real
valued Lipschitz functions alone does not guarantee any Poincaré inequality.
Notions of
partial
differentiability have been introduced in terms of Weaver derivations
[
40
,
26
,
35
]
.

On the other hand, Sobolev space theory can be developed in general metric measure spaces
[
28
,
23
,
24
]
, and many important properties of Sobolev spaces (e.g. their reflexivity and the density of Lipschitz functions) hold without assuming any structure on the metric space
[
3
]
. In this general context, in
[
22
]
, the second and third named authors introduced
p
-weak charts with respect to which Sobolev functions admit differentials satisfying (
1.1
) along curves in the space. The charts and the associated weak differentiable structure in
[
22
]
exist whenever the space has finite Hausdorff dimension, and give an explicit curvewise description of the differential, and minimal upper gradient, of a Sobolev function.

In this paper, we combine techniques from Sobolev space theory and Lipschitz differentiability spaces to obtain a weak notion of charts that generalizes both (
1.1
) and
p
-weak charts in
[
22
]
, and exists in spaces of finite Hausdorff dimension (see Definition
1.1
and Theorem
5.5
).
Our results give a geometric interpretation of the partial differentiability of Lipschitz functions previously studied via Weaver derivations (see Theorem
1.4
).

In order to study Lipschitz differentiability spaces, one must first note that there need not be any curves in the space.
Indeed, a Cantor set in the real line of positive Lebesgue measure is easily observed to be a Lipschitz differentiability space.
Instead, one must consider curve
fragments
: bi-Lipschitz maps
\gamma:\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\to X
from a compact set
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
An Alberti representation
\mathcal{A}=\{\mu_{\gamma},\mathbb{P}\}
of a measure
\mu
on a metric space
X
is a disintegration of
\mu
into 1-rectifiable measures
\mu_{\gamma}\ll\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\operatorname{im}(\gamma)}
:

\displaystyle\mu(E)=\int_{\operatorname{Fr}(X)}\mu_{\gamma}(E)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\gamma),\quad E\subset X\textrm{ Borel,}

where
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
is the space of curve fragments and
\mathbb{P}
is a positive measure on
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
(cf. Definition
2.16
). Independent collections of Alberti representations (see Definition
2.19
) play a central role in the theory: they formalize the notion of the “independent directions” a measure sees, and act as an analogue of
p
-independence in
[
22
]
. Moreover, admitting
n
independent Alberti representations is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the existence of an
n
-dimensional chart
[
8
, Theorem 6.6]
in a Lipschitz differentiability space. (See Section
2
for the terminology and definitions employed in the introduction.)

Definition 1.1
.

A fragment-wise chart
(U,\varphi)
of dimension
n
consists of a Borel set
U\subset X
with
\mu(U)>0
and a Lipschitz function
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the following.

(i)

Independence
:
\mu|_{U}
admits
n

\varphi
-independent Alberti representations;

(ii)

Maximality
: If
V\subset U
and
\mu|_{V}
admits
k
independent Alberti representations, then
k\leq n
.

A result of Bate–Kangasniemi–Orponen
[
9
]
implies the Hausdorff dimension bound
n\leq\dim_{H}U
for any fragment-wise chart
(U,\varphi)
of dimension
n
.
This bound also follows from a deep result of De Philippis and Rindler
[
16
]
;
previous bounds in terms of the Assouad dimension can be found in Schioppa
[
35
]
and
[
8
]
.
From such a bound, standard arguments yield that
spaces of finite Hausdorff dimension can be covered by a countable number of fragment-wise charts
, see Theorem
5.5
.
An alternative proof of this fact can be found following
[
22
]
.

In order to combine the Sobolev space theory with Alberti representations, we use the notion of a
\ast
-upper gradient, introduced in
[
10
]
(see (
1.2
)), to accommodate for the gaps in a curve fragment. This acts as a replacement for upper gradients of Sobolev functions, which were introduced in
[
27
]
.
The proofs of our results employ elements of plan-modulus duality of
[
4
]
and the close connection between 1-plans and Alberti representations.
A novel aspect of our work is connecting the defined notions and expressing many results in terms of
\ast
-upper gradients and the
\infty
-modulus of curve fragments.
From a historical perspective this is quite natural.
Indeed, in
[
38
]
Sobolev spaces were introduced together with weak
p
-upper gradients, defined in terms of
p
-modulus of curves.
Thus, whereas weak
p
-upper gradients and
p
-modulus of curves are the natural concepts for Sobolev functions,
\ast
-upper gradients and
\infty
-modulus of curve fragments are the corresponding natural notions for Lipschitz functions.

1.2.
Fragment-wise differentials and density of directions

Throughout the paper, we consider a metric measure space
X=(X,d,\mu)
, i.e. a complete separable metric space
(X,d)
equipped with a Borel regular measure
\mu
that is finite on bounded sets. Given
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
, we say that a Borel function
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
is a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
, if

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\rm osc}_{\gamma}f\leq\int_{\operatorname{im}(\gamma)}\rho\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}+\operatorname{LIP}(f)\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)

for every
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
. We refer to Section
2.3
for the relevant definitions and the fact that, for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
, there exists a minimal
\ast
-upper gradient, denoted
|Df|_{\ast}
. (For Sobolev functions, there exists a minimal
weak
upper gradient
[
28
, Section 6]
, but in our case, weak
\ast
-upper gradients are always a.e. equal to
\ast
-upper gradients, see Remark
2.9
.)

Let
U\subset X
be a Borel set with
\mu(U)>0
and
\varphi\in\operatorname{LIP}(X;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. A Borel map
\bm{\xi}:U\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
is a fragment-wise differential of
f
with respect to
(U,\varphi)
, if

(1.3)

\displaystyle(f\circ\gamma)_{t}^{\prime}=\bm{\xi}((\varphi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}_{t})\quad a.e.\ t\in\gamma^{-1}(U)\quad\textrm{for }\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}-a.e.\ \gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X).

If any two fragment-wise differentials of
f
agree
\mu
-a.e., we say that
f
admits a
unique
fragment-wise differential, denoted
\mathrm{d}f:U\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
, with respect to
(U,\varphi)
.

Our first theorem establishes the existence of differentials and pointwise norms associated to charts, which give rise to measurable cotangent bundles discussed later on.
It is a representation in the spirit of
[
14
, Corollary 1.10]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
(U,\varphi)
is a fragment-wise chart of dimension
n
. Then every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
admits a unique fragment-wise differential
\mathrm{d}f:U\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
. Moreover for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in U
there exists a norm
|\cdot|_{x}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
such that
x\mapsto|\xi|_{x}
is Borel for every
\xi\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
and

(1.4)

\displaystyle|Df|_{\ast}(x)=|\mathrm{d}_{x}f|_{x}\quad\mu-a.e.\ x\in U

for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
.

The differential satisfies the usual calculus rules, see Section
5.2
. As a geometric consequence of the analytical structure provided by fragment-wise charts we have the following theorem which states that, at a generic point in the chart, there exist curve fragments arbitrarily close to any given direction. This result generalizes
[
14
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(U,\varphi)
be a fragment-wise chart of dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\mu
-a.e.
x\in U
, the set of directions

\displaystyle S_{x}(\varphi)=\bigg\{\frac{(\varphi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)}{|(\varphi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)|}:\ \gamma_{t}=x\bigg\}\subset S^{n-1}

is dense in
S^{n-1}
.

This theorem is proven in a stronger form as Theorem
6.4
.

1.3.
Lipschitz differentiability spaces and Weaver derivations

A countable covering of
X
by fragment-wise charts gives rise to a differentiable structure as well as
tangent
and
cotangent
bundles
TX
and
T^{*}X
, respectively, see Section
5.4
. The differential of
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
given by Theorem
1.2
naturally defines an element
\mathrm{d}f\in L^{\infty}(T^{*}X)
in the space of essentially bounded sections of
T^{*}X
.

An important property of the linear map
f\mapsto\mathrm{d}f:\operatorname{LIP}(X)\to L^{\infty}(T^{*}X)
is its weak
∗
-continuity (cf. Theorem
5.8
and Remark
5.10
), which hints at a connection with
Weaver derivations
(see Section
2.6
). Introduced originally by Weaver
[
40
]
, Weaver derivations have inspired the theory of tangent modules in
[
23
]
, and turn out to be closely connected with Alberti representations and metric 1-currents
[
37
,
35
]
. The space
\mathcal{X}(\mu)
of Weaver derivations equipped with the norm
\|\bm{b}\|_{\mathcal{X}(\mu)}=\||\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}
is a Banach space. See Section
2.6
, and (
2.3
) for the definition of the pointwise norm
|\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}
of a Weaver derivation
\bm{b}\in\mathcal{X}(\mu)
.

In Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
below, we identify Weaver derivations with
vector fields
on
X
, i.e. sections of the tangent bundle
TX
, and use this identification to characterize Lipschitz differentiability spaces in the spirit of
[
35
]
by using the minimal
\ast
-upper gradients, respectively.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
X=(X,d,\mu)
admits a fragment-wise differentiable structure. Then the map
\iota:L^{\infty}(TX)\to\mathcal{X}(\mu)
defined by

\displaystyle\iota(\bm{X})(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathrm{d}f(\bm{X}),\quad f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)

is an isomorphism of normed
L^{\infty}(\mu)
-modules satisfying

(1.5)

\displaystyle|\iota(\bm{X})|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}=|\bm{X}|\quad\mu-a.e.

for every
\bm{X}\in L^{\infty}(TX)
.

In the claim below, a modulus of continuity is a non-decreasing continuous function
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\omega(0)=0
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space. The following are equivalent.

(i)

X
is a Lipschitz differentiability space;

(ii)

\operatorname{Lip}f=|Df|_{\ast}

\mu
-a.e. for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
;

(iii)

there is a collection
\{\omega_{x}\}_{x\in X}
of moduli of continuities such that

\operatorname{Lip}f\leq\omega(|Df|_{\ast})\quad\mu-a.e.

for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
.

In closing, we remark that the equivalence (ii)
\iff
(iii) demonstrates a self-improving property of the inequality
\operatorname{Lip}f\leq\omega(|Df|_{\ast})
. In the case
\omega(t)=Ct
it follows from the self-improvement of Keith’s Lip–lip condition
[
35
,
14
]
.

1.4.
Approximation

Both the theory of Sobolev spaces and that of Weaver differentials rests heavily on finding good approximations of functions. Indeed, for us, the proof of the density of directions, Theorem
6.4
, is based on the following approximation result.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{LIP}_{bs}(X)
. Then there exists a sequence
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}_{bs}(X)
with
\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})\leq\operatorname{LIP}(f)
and
|f_{j}|\leq|f|
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

(1)

f_{j}\to f
pointwise everywhere (and consequently uniformly on compact sets);

(2)

\operatorname{Lip}_{a}f_{j}\to|Df|_{\ast}
,
\operatorname{Lip}f_{j}\to|Df|_{\ast}
, and
|Df_{j}|_{\ast}\to|Df|_{\ast}
pointwise
\mu
-a.e.

This approximation result is of independent interest and brings together again the two strands of research on Lipschitz algebras and Sobolev spaces. It has many predecessors that have appeared in these different contexts.

In the case of differentiability spaces, such approximations were implicitly used in
[
8
, Lemma 6.3]
and explicitly used in
[
35
, Theorem 1.21]
, see also
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
for a recent application. In those cases, an embedding to
\ell^{\infty}
was used as an auxiliary tool. In our case, we use an embedding into the free space over
X
, see Subsection
2.2
and specifically Remark
2.1
.

Independent from the differentiability space case, methods to approximate Sobolev functions appeared earlier in Cheeger’s work
[
12
, Sections 4 and 5]
, where no embedding was employed. That work was very similar to a much older argument of Ziemer, see
[
12
, Remark 5.26]
, see also
[
27
, Proposition 2.17]
. Recently, these techniques were used in
[
21
]
to give a new proof of density of energy of Lipschitz functions. This density in energy was earlier discovered by entirely different means
[
6
]
.

Theorem
1.6
is the Lipschitz analogue of the result in
[
21
]
and is based on similar ideas. It gives a more direct way of obtaining the approximations from
[
8
, Lemma 6.3]
and
[
35
, Theorem 1.21]
in the Lipschitz differentiability category, while also giving slightly sharper conclusions. It also elucidates how the different approximations appearing in the literature are really modifications of the same one in disguise.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and conventions

In this paper, we will only consider complete and separable metric measure spaces
X
that are equipped with a Radon measure
\mu
that is finite on bounded sets.

We say that a function
f:X\to Y
is Lipschitz, if
\operatorname{LIP}(f)=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{d(f(x),f(y))}{d(x,y)}<\infty
. We denote by
\operatorname{LIP}(X)
the collection of Lipschitz functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, and treat it simply as a set without a topology. Further,
\operatorname{LIP}(X,Y)
denotes the space of Lipschitz functions
f:X\to Y
. We denote by
\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)
the collection of bounded Lipschitz functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}}=\max(\|f\|_{\infty},\operatorname{LIP}(f))
. We let
\operatorname{LIP}_{bs}(X)
be the space of Lipschitz functions with bounded support, and we write

\operatorname{Lip}_{a}f(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\operatorname{LIP}(f|_{B(x,r)})\text{ and }\operatorname{Lip}f(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\sup_{y\in B(x,r)\setminus\{x\}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}.

For isolated points
x\in X
, we set
\operatorname{Lip}_{a}f(x)=\operatorname{Lip}f(x)=0
.

If
\mu
is a Radon measure on
X
, then the pushforward of
\mu
under a measurable map
f:X\to Y
is denoted
f_{*}\mu
and is given by
f_{*}\mu(A)=\mu(f^{-1}(A))
.

2.2.
Lipschitz free space

Let
\operatorname{LIP}_{0}(X)
be the space of Lipschitz functions vanishing at a given base point
x_{0}\in X
, equipped with the Lipschitz norm
\operatorname{LIP}(\cdot)
. For every
x\in X
the associated evaluation map
\iota_{x}(f):=f(x)
belongs to
\operatorname{LIP}(X)^{*}
. Note that
\iota_{x}
is moreover continuous with respect to pointwise convergence. The Lipschitz free space
\mathcal{F}(X)
is defined as

\mathcal{F}(X)=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{\iota_{x}:\ x\in X\}\subset\operatorname{LIP}(X)^{*}

equipped with the dual norm. The canonical embedding
\iota:X\hookrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, given by
x\mapsto\iota_{x}
, is an isometry. The Lipschitz free space
\mathcal{F}(X)
over
X
is a predual of
\operatorname{LIP}_{0}(X)
– i.e.
\mathcal{F}(X)^{*}=\operatorname{LIP}_{0}(X)
; moreover on bounded sets, the
w^{*}
-topology on
\operatorname{LIP}_{0}(X)
induced by

\mathcal{F}(X)
coincides with the topology of pointwise convergence, cf.
[
40
, Theorem 2.2.2]
. We refer to
[
40
]
for the more detailed definition of and thorough introduction to
\mathcal{F}(X)
and its properties.

We next define the
semihull

\operatorname{sh}(X)
of
X
. Set

\displaystyle\operatorname{sh}(X)=\bigcup\{[x,y]:\ x,y\in X\},

where
[x,y]=\{\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x}):\ t\in[0,1]\}
is the line segment in
\mathcal{F}(X)
connecting the isometric images of
x
and
y
. Every element
v\in\operatorname{sh}(X)\setminus X
has two representations
v=\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x})=\iota_{y}+(1-t)(\iota_{x}-\iota_{y})
that is, the representation is unique up to switching
x
and
y
. We equip
\operatorname{sh}(X)
with the metric from
\mathcal{F}(X)
. It directly follows that
X\subset\operatorname{sh}(X)
isometrically.

The semihull provides a convenient technical tool which will be used in Section
3
to obtain lower semicontinuity of suitable integrals over fragments of curves. Its key property is a canonical extension of functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
to functions
\operatorname{sh}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
which preserves lower semicontinuity.

Remark 2.1
.

The standard Kuratowski embedding
X\hookrightarrow\ell^{\infty}
is not appropriate for our canonical extension scheme. Indeed, the natural “convex” extension we use (see Section
3.1
)
is not well-defined for the Kuratowski embedding, and we do not know whether a canonical semicontinuity preserving extension exists for it.

The following lemma gives a concrete description of convergence in
\operatorname{sh}(X)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
v=\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x})
and suppose
(v_{j})\subset\operatorname{sh}(X)
converges to
v
. Then there exist sequences
(x_{j}),(y_{j})\subset X
and
(t_{j})\subset[0,1]
with
v_{j}=\iota_{x_{j}}+t_{j}(\iota_{y_{j}}-\iota_{x_{j}})
such that the following holds.

(1)

If
v\in\operatorname{sh}(X)\setminus\iota(X)
, then
t_{j}\to t
and
(x_{j},y_{j})\to(x,y)
;

(2)

If
v\in\iota(X)
, then up to a subsequence, we have one of the following options: (a)
(x_{j},y_{j})\to(x,y)
, (b)
x_{j}\to x
and
t_{j}\to 0
, or (c)
y_{j}\to y
and
t_{j}\to 1
.

Proof.

Assume first that
v=\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x})\notin\iota(X)
, i.e.
x\neq y
and
t\in(0,1)
. Choose a representation
v_{j}=\iota_{x_{j}}+t_{j}(\iota_{y_{j}}-\iota_{x_{j}})
such that

is minimal. The functions
f=\operatorname{dist}(\{x,y\},\cdot)
,
\tilde{f}=f-f(x_{0})
are 1-Lipschitz,
\tilde{f}
vanishes at
x_{0}
, and

v_{j}(\tilde{f})-v(\tilde{f})=tf(x_{j})+(1-t)f(y_{j})
. Consequently

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\min\{t,1-t\}[\operatorname{dist}(\{x,y\},x_{j})+\operatorname{dist}(\{x,y\},y_{j})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle tf(x_{j})+(1-t)f(y_{j})=|v_{j}(\tilde{f})-v(\tilde{f})|\leq\|v-v_{j}\|\to 0,

i.e. the sequences
(x_{j})
and
(y_{j})
converge either to
x
or
y
. By the minimality of
d(x,x_{j})+d(y,y_{j})
it follows that
x_{j}\to x
and
y_{j}\to y
. Similarly, if
g=d(x,\cdot)
and
\tilde{g}=g-g(x_{0})
, then
v_{j}(\tilde{g})-v(\tilde{g})=d(x_{j},x)+t_{j}(d(y_{j},x)-d(x_{j},x))-td(y,x)\to 0
, implying
t_{j}\to t

Suppose next that
v\in\iota(X)
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
v=\iota_{x}
and
t\leq 1/2
. Considering the functions
f=\operatorname{dist}(\{x,y\},\cdot)
as above we obtain that
x_{j}\to x
or
y_{j}\to x
. Up to relabeling
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
we may assume that
x_{j}\to x
. If a subsequence of
(y_{j})
converges to
x
then (a) is satisfied. If not, there exists
\delta>0
such that
d(y_{j},x)\geq 2\delta
for all
j
. Consequently
d(x_{j},y_{j})\geq\delta
for all large

. It follows that the functions
g_{j}=\frac{d(x_{j},\cdot)}{d(x_{j},y_{j})}
,
\tilde{g}_{j}=g_{j}-g_{j}(x_{0})
are uniformly Lipschitz and
v_{j}(\tilde{g}_{j})-v(\tilde{g}_{j})=t_{j}-\frac{d(x,x_{j})}{d(y_{j},x_{j})}\to 0
. Since
\frac{d(x,x_{j})}{d(y_{j},x_{j})}\to 0
, it follows that
t_{j}\to 0
, completing the proof

Remark 2.3
.

Define the
edge-length function

l_{\operatorname{sh}}:\operatorname{sh}(X)\to[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle l_{\operatorname{sh}}(\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x}))\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=d(x,y).

In particular,
l_{\operatorname{sh}}(x)=0
for
x\in X\subset\operatorname{sh}(X)
.
This is well-defined and
\{l_{\operatorname{sh}}>0\}=\operatorname{sh}(X)\setminus X
. It follows from Lemma
2.2
that
l_{\operatorname{sh}}
is lower semicontinuous.

2.3.
Curve fragments and
\ast
-upper gradients

A curve fragment in
X
is a bi-Lipschitz map
\gamma:\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\to X
from a compact set
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\subset\mathbb{R}
, called the domain of
\gamma
. The biLipschitz constant of
\gamma
is denoted
\operatorname{biLip}(\gamma)
and it is the smallest constant
L
so that
L^{-1}|a-b|\leq d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))\leq L|a-b|
holds for all
a,b\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
.

Let
I_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{dom}(\gamma))=[a,b]
be the smallest interval containing
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
and, writing
\displaystyle[a,b]\setminus\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)=\bigcup_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})
, the gap of the curve fragment
\gamma
is defined as

\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)=\sum_{i}d(\gamma_{b_{i}},\gamma_{a_{i}}).

We also call the intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
appearing in this context the gaps of the curve fragment
\gamma
. The gaps are indexed by a finite or countable set.
We define an extension
\bar{\gamma}:I_{\gamma}\to\operatorname{sh}(X)
of
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\gamma_{t}\ ,&t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\\
\frac{t-a_{i}}{b_{i}-a_{i}}\gamma_{b_{i}}+\frac{b_{i}-t}{b_{i}-a_{i}}\gamma_{a_{i}},&t\in(a_{i},b_{i}).\end{array}\right.

The
metric speed

|\bar{\gamma}_{t}^{\prime}|
exists for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I_{\gamma}
, see
[
7
, Theorem 4.1.6]
.
In particular, curve fragments also admit a metric speed: for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
the limit

|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|:=\lim_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\ni s\to t}\frac{d(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{s})}{|t-s|}

exists.
At any non isolated point of
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
, this value is independent of the chosen extension.

If
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz, then it can be extended to a Lipschitz function
\bar{f}:\operatorname{sh}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, the derivative
(\bar{f}\circ\bar{\gamma})^{\prime}(t)
exists for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I_{\gamma}
. Consequently, for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
the limit

(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t):=\lim_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\ni s\to t}\frac{f(\gamma_{s})-f(\gamma_{t})}{s-t}

exists. Again, the limit is independent of the choice of extension at non-isolated points of the domain
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
.

The line integral of a Borel function
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
over
\gamma
satisfies

\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s:=\int_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\rho(\gamma_{t})|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\operatorname{im}(\gamma)}\rho\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Remark 2.4
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\ell^{\ast}(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|\mathrm{d}t+\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)=\ell(\bar{\gamma}).

The curve
\bar{\gamma}
can be parametrized by constant speed, see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 4.2.1]
.
Consequently also the curve fragment
\gamma
has a bi-Lipschitz reparametrization
\tilde{\gamma}:\operatorname{dom}(\tilde{\gamma})\to X
with
[0,1]=I_{\tilde{\gamma}}
such that
|\tilde{\gamma}_{t}^{\prime}|=\ell^{*}(\gamma)
for every
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\tilde{\gamma})
.

The set of curve fragments on
X
is denoted by
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
and is given a topology as follows. Using the injective map
\gamma\mapsto{\rm graph}(\gamma)=\{(t,\gamma_{t}):\ t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\}
,
we identify
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
with a subset of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{R}\times X)
(the space of closed bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}\times X
) and equip it with the Hausdorff metric. The following lemma follows by a straightforward application of the definitions and Lemma
2.2
and we omit the proof.

Lemma 2.5
.

We have that
\bar{\gamma}|_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}=\gamma
and
\ell(\bar{\gamma})=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\operatorname{im}(\gamma))+\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
. Moreover,
\gamma_{j}\to\gamma
in
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
if and only if
\bar{\gamma}_{j}\to\bar{\gamma}
in
\operatorname{Fr}(\operatorname{sh}(X))
.

Next we define curve fragment analogues of upper gradients, called
\ast
-upper gradients.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
. A Borel function
g:X\to[0,\infty]
is a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
if

(2.1)

{\rm osc}_{\gamma}f\leq\int_{\gamma}g\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{LIP}(f){\rm gap}(\gamma)

for every
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
.

Here, we use the notation
\displaystyle{\rm osc}_{\gamma}f=\sup_{x,y\in\operatorname{im}(\gamma)}|f(x)-f(y)|
. Note that
\ast
-upper gradients are only defined for Lipschitz functions, and that
\operatorname{LIP}(f)
is always a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
.

2.4.
\infty
-Modulus and minimal
\ast
-upper gradients

We relax the notion of
\ast
-upper gradients by introducing negligible families of curve fragments in the sense of the
\infty
-modulus

\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}
. The resulting class of
weak
\ast
-upper gradients
behaves better with respect to limits, and automatically contains minimal elements, making it a suitable analogue of minimal
p
-weak upper gradients in the context of curve fragments; compare
[
28
,
11
]
. However, we will also see that compared to the theory for finite
p
, the exponent
p=\infty
is special: a minimal
\ast
-upper gradient also exists.

The modulus
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma)
of a family of curve fragments
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
is defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma)=\inf\left\{\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}:\ \int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s\geq 1\textrm{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}.

The
\infty
-modulus is an outer measure on
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
, and all the basic properties of
\operatorname{Mod}_{p}
for finite
p
remain valid for
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}
, see
[
19
]
. Families
\Gamma_{0}\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
of zero
\infty
-modulus have a particularly simple description, given below; compare
[
19
, Lemma 5.7]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

A family
\Gamma_{0}\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
satisfies
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma_{0})=0
if and only if there exists a Borel set
N\subset X
with
\mu(N)=0
such that
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma_{N}^{+}
, where

\Gamma_{N}^{+}:=\{\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X):\ \mathcal{H}^{1}(N\cap\operatorname{im}(\gamma))>0\}.

Proof.

The proof follows closely that of
[
19
, Lemma 5.7]
. If
\mu(N)=0
, let
\rho=\infty\chi_{N}
, and we have
\int_{\gamma}\chi_{N}ds=\infty\mathcal{H}^{1}(N\cap\operatorname{im}(\gamma))=\infty
. Further,
\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}=0
, and thus
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma_{N}^{+})=0
. This gives the converse direction of the lemma.

If on the other hand
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma_{0})=0
, then for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
\rho_{\epsilon}
with
\|\rho_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\leq\epsilon
and
\int_{\gamma}\rho_{\epsilon}ds\geq 1
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
. Let
N=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{n^{-1}}^{-1}((n^{-1},\infty))
. From the definition of the
L^{\infty}(\mu)
-norm it is direct to see
\mu(N)=0
. Now, if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
, we have some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\operatorname{im}(\gamma))<n
. Then,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\rho_{n^{-1}}^{-1}((n^{-1},\infty))\cap\operatorname{im}(\gamma))>0
since otherwise
\int_{\gamma}\rho_{n^{-1}}ds\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\operatorname{im}(\gamma))n^{-1}<1
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(N\cap\operatorname{im}(\gamma))>0
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{N}^{+}
. Since this holds for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
, the claim follows.
∎

Definition 2.8
.

A Borel function
\rho\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of a function
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
if (
2.1
) holds for every curve fragment outside a family
\Gamma_{0}\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
with
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}(\Gamma_{0})=0
.

Remark 2.9
.

In contrast with finite exponents, a weak
\ast
-upper gradient
\rho
of
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
has a
\mu
-representative which is a genuine upper gradient. Indeed, if
\Gamma_{0}\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
is the set where (
2.1
) fails for
\rho
and
N\subset X
is the Borel
\mu
-null set with
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma_{N}^{+}
, then

\tilde{\rho}=\operatorname{LIP}(f)\chi_{N}+\rho\chi_{X\setminus N}

is the required representative.
Indeed, if
\gamma
is any curve fragment, then we can divide the domain as
\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}K_{i}\cup B
, where
K_{i}
are compact sets such that
\gamma(K_{i})\subset N
or
\gamma(K_{i})\cap N=\emptyset
, and
B
is a null set. Then
\tilde{\rho}
is an
\ast
-upper gradient for each
\gamma|_{K_{i}}
. From this one can conclude that
\tilde{\rho}
is an
\ast
-upper gradient for
\gamma
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
. Then there exists a minimal
\ast
-upper gradient
|Df|_{\ast}
of
f
in the following sense:
|Df|_{\ast}
is a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
and, if
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
, then
|Df|_{\ast}\leq\rho

\mu
-a.e. Moreover, any two minimal
\ast
-upper gradients of
f
agree
\mu
-a.e.

In the proof below we use the observation that a Borel function
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
if and only if

\displaystyle|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)|\leq\rho(\gamma_{t})|\gamma^{\prime}_{t}|\quad a.e.\ t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)

for
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}
-a.e.
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
. The proof also uses the concept of a measure theoretic infimum, which is also sometimes referred to as a lattice infimum.

Definition 2.11
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is any collection of measurable functions, then the measure theoretic infimum
{\rm essinf}\mathcal{F}
is a measurable function
\rho
such that
\rho\leq f
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and such that if
\rho^{\prime}
is any other such function, then
\rho^{\prime}\leq\rho
(a.e.).

Similarly, the measure theoretic supremum
{\rm esssup}\mathcal{F}
is a measurable function
\rho
such that
\rho\geq f
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and such that if
\rho^{\prime}
is any other such function, then
\rho^{\prime}\geq\rho
(a.e.).

It can be shown that the measure theoretic infimum exists for
\sigma
-finite spaces, and that there exists always a sequence of
\rho_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{n}={\rm essinf}\mathcal{F}
. The proof is quite standard, see e.g.
[
22
, Appendix A.2, Essential supremum]
. By replacing functions by their negations, we can obtain the same results for measure theoretic suprema.

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

Two minimal weak
\ast
-upper gradient must necessarily agree
\mu
-almost everywhere: if
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are both pointwise minimal, then
\rho_{1}\leq\rho_{2}
and
\rho_{2}\leq\rho_{1}

\mu
-a.e. Thus it suffices to show its existence.

We show the following
lattice property
of weak
\ast
-upper gradients: if
\rho_{i}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
,
i=1,2,\ldots
are weak
\ast
-upper gradients of
f
, then
\rho_{\infty}=\inf_{i}\rho_{i}
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
. Indeed, if
\Gamma_{i}\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
is the
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}
-null set where (
1.2
) fails for
\rho_{i}
and
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\cup\cdots
, then for
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{0}
there exist null sets
N_{i}\subset\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
such that
|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)|\leq\rho_{i}(\gamma_{t})|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|
for
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\setminus N_{i}
. It follows that

|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)|\leq\rho_{\infty}(\gamma_{t})|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|

for
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\setminus(N_{1}\cup N_{2}\cup\cdots)
. Thus
\rho_{\infty}
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
as claimed.

Now let
\rho
be the measure theoretic infimum over all weak
\ast
-upper gradients of
f
, and let
\rho_{i}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
be a sequence from this family such that
\rho=\inf_{i}\rho_{i}

\mu
-a.e. By the preceding argument
\rho
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
, and by Remark
2.9
we may choose a representative of
\rho
that is a genuine
\ast
-upper gradient. The definition of measure theoretic infimum implies that
\rho\leq g

\mu
-a.e. for any weak
\ast
-upper gradient
g
of
f
.
∎

The lattice property established in the proof above yields the basic locality properties of the minimal
\ast
-upper gradient by standard arguments, see
[
28
, Chapter 6.3]
or
[
11
, Chapter 2.2]
. We record below the basic properties needed later on without proof, and refer the interested reader to the references above.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
f,g\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
. Then

(1)

|D(f+g)|_{\ast}\leq|Df|_{\ast}+|Dg|_{\ast}

\mu
-a.e.;

(2)

|Df|_{\ast}=|Dg|_{\ast}

\mu
-a.e. on
\{f=g\}
; and

(3)

|Dh|_{\ast}=\chi_{\{f<g\}}|Df|_{\ast}+\chi_{\{f\geq g\}}|Dg|_{\ast}

\mu
-a.e. for
h=\min\{f,g\}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Suppose
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}(X)
converges pointwise to
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
and
\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})<\infty
. If
\rho_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f_{j}
for each
j
and
(\rho_{j})
converges pointwise
\mu
-a.e. to
\rho\in L^{\infty}
with
\sup_{j}\|\rho_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}<\infty
, then
\rho
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
.

Proof.

Let
N=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}N_{i}\subset X
be the Borel set, where

N_{0}:=\{x\in X:\lim_{j\to\infty}\rho_{j}(x)\neq\rho(x)\text{ or }\rho(x)>\sup_{j}\|\rho_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\}

and
N_{j}
is a Borel set so that for
\gamma\not\in\Gamma_{N_{j}}^{*}
the function
\rho_{j}
is a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f_{j}
. The latter is guaranteed by Lemma
2.7
.

Then, whenever
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{N}^{+}
, we have that (a)
\rho_{j}
is a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f_{j}
along
\gamma
, and (b)
\rho_{j}\circ\gamma\to\rho\circ\gamma
pointwise a.e. with
\sup_{j}\|\rho_{j}\circ\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\operatorname{dom}(\gamma))}<\infty
. By the dominated convergence theorem we have

\displaystyle|f(\gamma_{t})-f(\gamma_{s})|=

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}|f_{j}(\gamma_{t})-f_{j}(\gamma_{s})|\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\bigg(\int_{\gamma}\rho_{j}\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s+C\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)

for all
t,s\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
, where
C=\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})
. From this and the Lebesgue differentiation theorem it follows that
|(f\circ\gamma)_{t}|\leq\rho(\gamma_{t})|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|
a.e.
t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
for
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{N}^{+}
. Thus
\rho
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
.
∎

The same conclusion remains true when the
\mu
-a.e. convergence
\rho_{j}\to\rho
is weakened to
w^{\ast}
-convergence in
L^{\infty}(\mu)=L^{1}(\mu)^{\ast}
. The argument uses a slightly non-standard version of Mazur’s lemma.

Lemma 2.14
.

Suppose
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}(X)
converges pointwise to
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
and
\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})<\infty
. If
\rho_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f_{j}
for each
j
and
(\rho_{j})

w^{\ast}
-converges in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
to
\rho
, then
\rho
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
.

Proof.

First, by Banach-Steinhausen, we get
\sup_{j}\|\rho_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}<\infty
.
We will show that there exists non-negative numbers
\alpha_{k}(n),\dots\alpha_{N_{n}}(n)
,
N_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
(with
n\leq N_{n})
and with
\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)=1
, so that

\tilde{\rho}_{n}=\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)\rho_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho

converges pointwise almost everywhere and with
\sup_{j}\|\tilde{\rho}_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}<\infty
. If this is true, then it is direct to verify that
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient for
\tilde{f}_{n}=\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)f_{j}
. Indeed, for
\operatorname{Mod}_{\infty}
-a.e. curve fragment
\gamma
, and any
s,t\in\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}_{n}(\gamma_{t})-\tilde{f}_{n}(\gamma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)|f_{j}(\gamma_{t})-f_{j}(\gamma_{s})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)\bigg(\int_{\gamma}\rho_{j}\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\tilde{\rho}_{n}\mathrm{d}s+C\operatorname{gap}(\gamma),

where
C=\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})
. From this, we get that
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
is a weak
\ast
-upper gradient for
\tilde{f}_{n}
. Now, the claim follows from Lemma
2.13
. We are left to prove the existence of
\alpha_{j}(n)
and
N_{n}
such that the previous claims hold.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and fix
x_{0}\in X
. Since
L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},n),\mu)\subset L^{2}(B(x_{0},n),\mu)\newline
\subset L^{1}(B(x_{0},n),\mu)
, we have that the sequence
\rho_{j}\chi_{R}\to\rho\chi_{R}
weakly in
L^{2}(B(x_{0},n),\mu)
. Thus, by Mazur’s lemma, together with passing to a subsequence, we can find non-negative values
\beta^{n}_{k}(k),\dots\beta^{n}_{M^{n}_{k}}(k)
,
k\leq M^{n}_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\sum_{j=k}^{M^{n}_{k}}\beta_{j}(k)=1
, so that

\tilde{\rho}^{n}_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{M^{n}_{k}}\beta_{j}(k)\rho_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho

converges almost everywhere in
B(x_{0},n)
. So, for some index
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
we have
m_{n}\geq n
and
\mu(\{x\in B(x_{0},n):|\tilde{\rho}^{n}_{m_{n}}(x)-\rho(x)|\geq\frac{1}{n}\})\leq 2^{-n}
. Set
N_{n}=M_{m_{n}}
, and
\alpha_{j}(n)=\beta_{j}(m)
for
j\in[m,M_{m}]
, and otherwise
\alpha_{j}(n)=0
for
j\in[n,m]
.

By the following Borel-Cantelli-type argument we have that almost everywhere

\tilde{\rho}_{n}=\sum_{j=n}^{N_{n}}\alpha_{j}(n)\rho_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho.

Indeed, let
A_{n}=\{x\in B(x_{0},n):|\tilde{\rho}^{n}_{m_{n}}(x)-\rho(x)|\geq\frac{1}{n}\}=\{\{x\in B(x_{0},n):|\tilde{\rho}_{n}(x)-\rho(x)|\geq\frac{1}{n}\}.
Then for the limit superior set
\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n\geq m}A_{n}
we have

\mu\left(\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n\geq m}\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\right)=0,

and for every
x\not\in\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}
, we get pointwise convergence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\rho}_{n}(x)=\rho(x)
.
∎

2.5.
Plans and Alberti representations

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a finite measure on
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
. The
barycenter

\mathbb{P}^{\#}
of
\mathbb{P}
is the Borel regular measure on
X
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\#}(E):=\int\int_{\gamma}\chi_{E}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\gamma),\quad E\subset X\textrm{ Borel.}

Definition 2.15
.

A finite measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
is called a 1-plan, if
\mathbb{P}^{\#}=\rho\mu
for some
\rho\in L^{1}(\mu)
.

These 1-plans are closely related to Alberti representations, defined next.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure. An Alberti representation
\mathcal{A}=\{\mu_{\gamma},\mathbb{P}\}
of
\mu
consists of a finite measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
and, for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\gamma
, a finite measure
\mu_{\gamma}\ll\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\operatorname{im}(\gamma)}
such that the following hold. For every Borel set
E\subset X
we have that

(a)

\displaystyle\gamma\mapsto\mu_{\gamma}(E)
is Borel, and

(b)

\displaystyle\mu(E)=\int_{\operatorname{Fr}(X)}\mu_{\gamma}(E)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\gamma)
.

At this juncture, we remark, that there are subtle differences in the definitions and conventions regarding Alberti representations. Our definition follows that of
[
8
]
, which is essentially equivalent to
[
35
]
. For other definitions see e.g.
[
13
,
1
,
21
]
. We record the close relationship between 1-plans and Alberti representations in the next remark.

Remark 2.17
.

If
\mathbb{P}
is a 1-plan on
X
and
\rho
is a Borel representative of
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\#}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
, then
\mu|_{\{\rho>0\}}

\mathcal{A}=\{\mu_{\gamma},\mathbb{P}\}

.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{A}=\{\mu_{\gamma},\mathbb{P}\}
is an Alberti representation of
\mu

there exists a compact

is the map

,

Such a

with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\#}
.
Note that

is concentrated on subcurves of

.

Depending on the application, there are advantages to both Alberti representations and plans. We highlight some crucial features to pay attention to. For example, Alberti representations are easier to restrict to subsets (we can just replace

with

when

may not be a curve fragment), whereas it is easier to take superpositions of plans (we can add
\mathbb{P}^{1}+\mathbb{P}^{2}
, but it is more difficult to combine

and
\mu_{\gamma}^{2}
in order to preseve b) in Definition
2.16
). Plans are a bit more flexible, since they do not impose (b) in Definition
2.16
, however, a plan may not “see” all of the measure
\mu
. Moreover, for Alberti representations, a crucial technical issue is that
\mu_{\gamma}
only “sees” a part of the curve fragment
\gamma
. These formal details are reflected in the details of the arguments employing the different notions, and have some subtle consequences for the formulations of different results. A reader interested in these subtleties can see the arguments in
[

,
8
]
.

Denote by
C(v,\vartheta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ v\cdot w>\cos\vartheta|w|\}
the (open) cone of width
\vartheta\in(0,\infty)
in the direction

. We say that a

-tuple

of cones is

are linearly independent whenever

,
j=1,\ldots,k
and
\varepsilon
-separated

), if

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j}^{k}\lambda_{j}w_{j}\bigg\|>\varepsilon\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\|\lambda_{j}w_{j}\|

,
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,

.

Remark 2.18
.

If

-separatedness pointwise for

Observe that

-separated for some Borel

.

Suppose

2.6.
Weaver derivations

A Weaver derivation is a bounded linear map

\bm{b}:\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)\to L^{\infty}(\mu)

that (a) satisfies the Leibniz rule
\bm{b}(fg)=f\bm{b}(g)+g\bm{b}(f)

\mu
-a.e. for every
f,g\in\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)
,
and (b) is weak
∗
-continuous in the following sense: if a sequence
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)

with
\sup_{j}\|f_{j}\|_{\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}}<\infty
converges to
f\in\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)
pointwise, then
\bm{b}(f_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w^{*}}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\bm{b}(f)
in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
. The collection
\mathcal{X}(\mu)
of Weaver derivations on
X
is a Banach space when equipped with the norm
\|\bm{b}\|_{\mathcal{X}(\mu)}=\||\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}
, where

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\operatorname{esssup}\{|\bm{b}(f)|:\ \operatorname{LIP}(f)\leq 1\}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)

is the pointwise norm defined as a measure theoretic supremum, see Definition
2.11
.

It is a standard fact that the estimate

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\bm{b}(f)|\leq|\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}\operatorname{Lip}_{a}f\quad\mu-a.e.

holds for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}_{\infty}(X)
. Indeed, in order to introduce some of the common techniques involved in the study of Weaver derivations, we will sketch this argument.

The Leibniz rule together with weak
∗
-continuity implies locality: If
A
is a positive measure subset and
f|_{A}=g|_{A}
, then
\bm{b}(f)|_{A}=\bm{b}(g)|_{A}
almost everywhere, see e.g.
[
26
, Lemma 13.4]
. Now, for every ball
B(x,r)
, we can take
f_{x,r}
to be the McShane extension of
f|_{B(x,r)}
, which is
\operatorname{LIP}(f|_{B(x,r)})
-Lipschitz. Then, locality implies that
\bm{b}(f_{x,r})|_{B(x,r)}=\bm{b}(f)|_{B(x,r)}
almost everywhere. From (
2.3
) we thus get

|\bm{b}(f)|_{B(x,r)}|\leq|\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}\operatorname{LIP}(f|_{B(x,r)}).

Then, taking a countable basis of the topology given by balls
\{B(x_{i},r_{i}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, we see that for a.e.
x\in X
we have

|\bm{b}(f)|(x)\leq|\bm{b}|_{\mathcal{X},\mathrm{loc}}(x)\inf\{\operatorname{LIP}(f|_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}):x\in B(x_{i},r_{i})\}.

Now, the final part is to observe that
\inf\{\operatorname{LIP}(f|_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}):x\in B(x_{i},r_{i})\}=\operatorname{Lip}_{a}(f)(x)
, whenever
\{B(x_{i},r_{i}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
forms a basis for the topology.

Remark 2.20
.

The locality of Weaver derivation can also be used to define
\bm{b}(f)
for any
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
by
\bm{b}(f):=\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{b}(f_{j})
, where
f_{j}=(1-\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,B(x_{0},j))_{+}f
for some arbitrary
x_{0}\in X
. The pointwise norm bound (
2.4
) remains valid for
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
and we will tacitly use this extension in the sequel.

3.1.
Curve fragments, line integrals, and lower semicontinuity

In the proof of Theorem
1.6
, but also of the representation Theorem
4.1
, we will need semicontinuity properties of the line integral. However, in contrast to the situation with curves, the line integral of a continuous function need not be lower semicontinuous with respect to convergence of curve fragments, compare
[
31
, Proposition 4]
. Nevertheless here we obtain the semicontinuity of a closely related quantity, called the
\ast
-integral in
[
10
]
, defined by

\displaystyle\int^{\ast}_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s:=\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma)

for Borel functions
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
and
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
. Here the weighted gap-functional
\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma)
is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\rho(\gamma_{a_{i}})+\rho(\gamma_{b_{i}})}{2}d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}}),

where
\bigcup_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})=I_{\gamma}\setminus\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)
. We also recall the edge length functional
l_{\operatorname{sh}}
and the extension
\bar{\gamma}:I_{\gamma}\to\operatorname{sh}(X)
of
\gamma
(cf. Remark
2.3
and Section
2.2
, respectively), and define
\operatorname{gap}_{\delta}
for
\delta\geq 0
by

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}_{\delta}(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\chi_{\{l_{\operatorname{sh}}>\delta\}}\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}})>\delta}d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}}),

where
(a_{i},b_{i})
are the gaps of the curve fragment
\gamma
; recall the terminology in Subsection
2.3
.
Observe that
\operatorname{gap}(1,\gamma)=\operatorname{gap}(\gamma)=\operatorname{gap}_{0}(\gamma)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
(\gamma_{j})\subset\operatorname{Fr}(X)
converges to
\gamma
in
\operatorname{Fr}(X)
, and
(\rho_{j})
is a pointwise non-decreasing sequence of lower semicontinuous functions with (pointwise) limit
\rho
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\left(\int_{\gamma_{j}}\rho_{j}\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{gap}(\rho_{j},\gamma_{j})\right)

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}_{\delta}(\gamma)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\operatorname{gap}_{\delta}(\gamma_{j}).

We will prove this proposition after a bit of preliminary work. Given any function
\rho:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
we may define the extension
\bar{\rho}:\operatorname{sh}(X)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x}))\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t\rho(y)+(1-t)\rho(x).

Using the properties of
\mathcal{F}(X)
it is not difficult to see that the extension is well-defined. The extensions
\bar{\gamma}
and
\bar{\rho}
can be used to identify the
\ast
-integral with a line-integral over the extended curve in
\operatorname{sh}(X)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\gamma:\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)\to X
be a curve fragment and
\bar{\gamma}
the associated extension. Let
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
be a Borel function and let
\bar{\rho}
be it’s extension. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma)=\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\bar{\rho}\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Given
\gamma
with the decomposition using gaps,
I_{\gamma}\setminus\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)=\bigcup_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})
, consider the extended curve
\bar{\gamma}
and note that, for each
i
,

\int_{\bar{\gamma}|_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}}\bar{\rho}\mathrm{d}s=d(\gamma_{b_{i}},\gamma_{a_{i}})\int_{0}^{1}[t\rho(\gamma_{b_{i}})+(1-t)\rho(\gamma_{a_{i}})]\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\rho(\gamma_{a_{i}})+\rho(\gamma_{b_{i}})}{2}d(\gamma_{b_{i}},\gamma_{a_{i}})

so that

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\bar{\rho}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\rho\mathrm{d}s+\int_{[a,b]\setminus\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\bar{\rho}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\rho\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{gap}(\rho,\gamma),

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous, then
\bar{\rho}:\operatorname{sh}(X)\to[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let
v=\iota_{x}+t(\iota_{y}-\iota_{x})
with
t\leq 1/2
and
v_{j}\to v
in
sh(X)
. Let
(x_{j},y_{j})
and
(t_{j})
be as in Lemma
2.2
. If
x\neq y
and
t\in(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(v)=

\displaystyle t\rho(y)+(1-t)\rho(x)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}t_{j}\rho(y_{j})+\liminf_{j\to\infty}(1-t_{j})\rho(x_{j})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\to\infty}[t_{j}\rho(y_{j})+(1-t_{j})\rho(x_{j})]=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\bar{\rho}(v_{j}).

In the second case we have that
v=\iota_{x}
and
t=0
, Thus
t_{j}\to 0
and
x_{j}\to x
. In this case

\bar{\rho}(v)=\rho(x)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}(1-t_{j})\rho(x_{j})\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}[t_{j}\rho(x_{j})+(1-t_{j})\rho(y_{j})]=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\bar{\rho}(v_{j}).

This completes the proof.
∎

We now provide the proof of the main result of this subsection.

3.2.
Construction of an approximating sequence

Suppose
f\in\operatorname{LIP}_{bs}(X)
. By Remark
2.9
,
|Df|_{\ast}
has a Borel representative
\rho
that is a genuine
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
. Since
f
has bounded support,
\rho
vanishes outside some bounded set. We fix
\rho
for this construction.

If
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

E_{j,l}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\rho^{-1}\Big(\frac{l-1}{2^{j}},\frac{l}{2^{j}}\Big],

for
l=1,\ldots,N_{j}
, where
N_{j}
is the unique integer in
(2^{j}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)},2^{j}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}+1]
.
Given
j,l
, let
K_{j,l}
be a compact subsets of
E_{j,l}
, such that

\mu(E_{j,l}\setminus K_{j,l})\leq 2^{-j^{2}}.

Set
\displaystyle K_{j}:=\bigcup_{l=1}^{N_{j}}K_{j,l}
. For any compact set
A\subset X
and
r>0
, let
B(A,r)=\{x\in X:d(x,A)<r\}
. Let
k_{j}
be such that

2^{-k_{j}}<\frac{\min_{l\neq\tilde{l}}\operatorname{dist}(K_{j,l},K_{j,\tilde{l}})}{2}.

Then, for each
k\geq k_{j}
define the functions
\rho_{j,l}^{k}:X\to[0,1]
,

\rho_{j,l}^{k}:=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,X\setminus B(K_{j},2^{-k}))}{\operatorname{dist}(x,X\setminus B(K_{j},2^{-k}))+\operatorname{dist}(x,K_{j,l})},

which vanish outside
B(K_{j},2^{-k})
and equal
1
on
K_{j,l}
. Finally set

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{k}:=\operatorname{LIP}(f)-\sum_{l=1}^{N_{j}}\Big(\operatorname{LIP}(f)-\frac{l}{2^{j}}\Big)_{+}\rho_{j,l}^{k}.

Remark 3.4
.

We have for every
k\geq k_{j}
that
\rho_{j}^{k}=\min\{l/2^{j},\operatorname{LIP}(f)\}
on
K_{j,l}
,
\rho_{j}^{k}=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
outside
B(K_{j},2^{-k})
and
\rho_{j}^{k}\leq\operatorname{LIP}(f)
everywhere. Furthermore,
(\rho_{j}^{k})_{k}
is a non-decreasing sequence of continuous functions converging pointwise to the function

\rho_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\operatorname{LIP}(f)\chi_{X\setminus K_{j}}+\sum_{l=1}^{N_{j}}\frac{l}{2^{j}}\chi_{K_{j,l}},

which satisfies
\rho\leq\rho_{j}
and is thus a
\ast
-upper gradient of
f
. Further,
\rho_{j}\geq\rho_{j}^{k}\geq 2^{-j}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
k=k(j,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(x)|\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}})\leq 2^{-k}}\frac{\rho(\gamma_{a_{i}})+\rho(\gamma_{b_{i}})}{2}d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}})

\displaystyle+\operatorname{LIP}(f)\operatorname{gap}_{2^{-k}}(\gamma)+\varepsilon

for all
\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
with start and endpoints
x
and
y
, respectively, and

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})=\operatorname{conv}{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\setminus\operatorname{dom}(\gamma).

The proof scheme is based on a contradiction argument that has appeared much earlier. Indeed, Cheeger attributes it to Ziemer in
[
12
, Remark 5.22]
. For the proof we define the function
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}:\operatorname{sh}(X)\to[0,\infty)
by

\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}:=\max\{\overline{\rho_{j}^{k}},\operatorname{LIP}(f)\chi_{\{l_{\operatorname{sh}(X)}>2^{-k}\}}\}

for each

, where
\overline{\rho_{j}^{k}}
denotes the extension of
\rho_{j}^{k}
into
\operatorname{sh}(X)
, cf. Section
3.1
, and note that

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\gamma}\rho_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sum_{d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}})\leq 2^{-k}}\frac{\rho(\gamma_{a_{i}})+\rho(\gamma_{b_{i}})}{2}d(\gamma_{a_{i}},\gamma_{b_{i}})+\operatorname{LIP}(f)\operatorname{gap}_{2^{-k}}(\gamma).

Remark 3.6
.

The line-integral above has the following geometric interpretation: from the perspective of the curve fragment
\gamma
, the line integral of
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}
over
\bar{\gamma}
penalizes gaps in
\gamma
with length above a certain threshold, while short gaps only contribute an amount determined by
\rho_{j}^{k}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Observe that, as the maximum of two lower semicontinuous functions,
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}
is lower semicontinuous, cf. Lemma
3.3
and Remark
2.3
. Moreover the sequence
(\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k})_{k}
increases pointwise to
\widehat{\rho}_{j}:=\chi_{X}\rho_{j}+\operatorname{LIP}(f)\chi_{\operatorname{sh}(X)\setminus X}
as

, see Remark
3.4
. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\gamma}\rho_{j}\mathrm{d}s+\operatorname{LIP}(f)\operatorname{gap}(\gamma),\quad\gamma\in\operatorname{Fr}(X).

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and suppose by contradiction that, for every
k\geq k_{j}
, there exists
\gamma_{k}\in\operatorname{Fr}(X)
with start and endpoints

respectively, such that

(3.1)

|f(y_{k})-f(x_{k})|\geq\int_{\bar{\gamma}_{k}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s+\varepsilon.

Choose
\gamma_{k}
to be a minimal curve-fragment such that (
3.1
) holds, where curve fragments are ordered according to the inclusions of their graphs. By Zorn’s lemma, since the intersection of any chain of curve fragments satisfying the previous inequality will also satisfy that same inequality (by lower-semicontinuity of the integrand in (
3.1
)), such a minimal curve fragment exists. Denote
C_{k}=\operatorname{dom}(\gamma_{k})
and
\operatorname{conv}C_{k}=[a_{k},b_{k}]
.

We claim that
\operatorname{im}(\gamma_{k})\subset\overline{B}(K_{j},2^{1-k})
. The idea here is that
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
on the parts of the fragment lying outside
\overline{B}(K_{j},2^{1-k})
and thus they can be removed while preserving (
3.1
).

Suppose that
F_{k}:=\gamma_{k}^{-1}(X\setminus\overline{B}(K_{j},2^{1-k}))\neq\varnothing
. If

is isolated in
C_{k}
, let
t_{0}^{\pm}\in(C_{k}\setminus\{t_{0}\})\cup\{a_{k},b_{k}\}
,

\sigma\in\{-,+\}.
Either

, in which case

, or
\gamma_{k}(t_{0}^{\sigma})\notin\overline{B}(K_{j},2^{-k})
, in which case
\rho_{j}^{k}(t_{0}^{\sigma})=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
. In both cases we have
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(t)))=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
for all

. Consequently
\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(t))=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
for

, in particular

\displaystyle\varepsilon+\int_{\overline{\gamma}_{k}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s\leq

\displaystyle|f(y_{k})-f(x_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|f(y_{k})-f(\gamma_{k}(t_{0}^{+}))|+\int_{\overline{\gamma}_{k}|_{[t_{0}^{-},t_{0}^{+}]}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s+|f(\gamma_{k}(t_{0}^{-}))-f(x_{k})|.

It follows that the proper sub-fragment

also satisfies (

\gamma_{k}
.

Therefore, we have that every point in

is an accumulation point of of
C_{k}
and
F_{k}
is relatively open. Thus, there exist

\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(t))=\operatorname{LIP}(f)
for all

|f(\gamma_{k}(b_{k}^{\prime}))-f(\gamma_{k}(a_{k}^{\prime}))|\leq\int_{\overline{\gamma}_{k}|_{[a_{k}^{\prime},b_{k}^{\prime}]}}\widehat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s
, and that

\gamma_{k}
.

Thus
\operatorname{im}(\gamma_{k})\subset\overline{B}(K_{j},2^{1-k})
for all

2^{-j}\ell^{\ast}(\gamma_{k})\leq\varepsilon+\int_{\bar{\gamma}_{k}}\hat{\rho}_{j}^{k}\mathrm{d}s\leq|f(y_{k})-f(x_{k})|\leq\operatorname{LIP}(f)\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{spt}(f))

so that
\sup_{k\geq k_{j}}\ell^{\ast}(\gamma_{k})=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}L<\infty
. By Remark

Then the sequence
\bar{\gamma}_{k}:[0,1]\to\operatorname{sh}(X)
of uniformly Lipschitz curves satisfies
\bar{\gamma}_{k}([0,1])\subset\operatorname{sh}(\overline{B(K_{j},2^{-k})})
and thus, it is not hard to see that for each

is precompact. By the Arzelá–Ascoli Theorem
[

The representation is based on the disintegration of a measure with respect to a mapping

\Phi_{\ast}\mu

[

Set

4.1.
Plan-modulus duality

The proof of Theorem

[

[

]

]
and the following shows that these are related.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a finite measure on a metric space

\Gamma
a compact family of curve fragments. Then one of the following two conditions holds.

(1)

\mu
-null Borel set

for every

there exists a compact set

and a 1-plan

\Gamma|_{K}
.

In the proof we use a variant of the barycenter called the

, define a measure

on

\mathbb{P}_{b}(E)=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\operatorname{dom}(\gamma)}\chi_{E}(\gamma_{t})\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\gamma),\quad E\subset X\textrm{ Borel}.

ensures suitable upper semicontinuity properties for the functional

defined in the proof below. Note moreover that

Suppose (2) does not hold. Then for each

is singular with respect to
\mu
.

(which could be taken compact by the inner regularity of

would yield a 1-plan, because

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}|_{K})^{\#}(E)=\int\int_{\gamma}\chi_{E}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}|_{K}=\int\int_{\gamma|_{\gamma^{-1}(K)}}\chi_{E}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{\#}|_{K}(E)

.

To complete the proof we follow the argument in
[
34
, Lemma 9.4.3]
to show that if

Let
X
be a Lipschitz differentiability space.
Suppose
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}(X)
converges pointwise to
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
and
\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})<\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\int g\operatorname{Lip}f\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int g\operatorname{Lip}f_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu

for every
g\in L^{1}(\mu)
.

Proof.

By
[
35
, Theorem 1.15]
we have that
\operatorname{Lip}f=\operatorname{esssup}\{\bm{b}(f):\|\bm{b}\|_{\mathcal{X}(\mu)}\leq 1\}

\mu
-a.e. for every
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
. Theorem
1.4
and the properties of the measure theoretic supremum further imply that there is a sequence
(\bm{X}_{m})\subset L^{\infty}(TX)
with
\|\bm{X}_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(TX)}\leq 1
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}f=\sup_{m}\mathrm{d}f(\bm{X}_{m})\quad\mu-a.e.

It follows that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the sets
E_{m}=\{\mathrm{d}f(\bm{X}_{m})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{Lip}f\}
cover
X
up to a
\mu
-null set. For each fixed
m
, the lower semicontinuity of differentials (Theorem
5.8
) implies that

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\int_{E_{m}}g\operatorname{Lip}f\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int_{E_{m}}g\mathrm{d}f(\bm{X}_{m})\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int g\operatorname{Lip}f_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu

for every non-negative
g\in L^{1}(\mu)
. Since the sets
E_{m}
cover
X
and
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, the claim follows.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Assume that (i) holds, i.e. that
X
is a Lipschitz differentiability space. By
[
8
, Theorem 6.6]
,
X
can be covered up to a
\mu
-null set by compact Cheeger charts
(U,\varphi)
such that
\mu|_{U}
admits
n_{U}

\varphi
-independent Alberti representations, and the dimension
n_{U}
of
(U,\varphi)
is the maximal number of independent Alberti representations of
\mu|_{V}
for any
V\subset U
with
\mu(V)>0
. Thus
(U,\varphi)
is a fragment-wise chart. Moreover, for any
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
, the fragment-wise and Cheeger differentials of
f
with respect to
(U,\varphi)
agree, cf.
[
8
, Lemma 3.8 or Corollary 6.7]
. To prove the implication (i)
\implies
(ii) it suffices to show that
\operatorname{Lip}f=|Df|_{\ast}

\mu
-a.e. on
U
for each such Cheeger chart
(U,\varphi)
.

Since the claim is local, we may assume without loss of generality that
\operatorname{spt}f
is bounded. Let
(f_{j})\subset\operatorname{LIP}(X)
be a sequence given by Theorem
1.6
with

\displaystyle\sup_{j}\operatorname{LIP}(f_{j})<\infty

converging pointwise to
f
such that
\operatorname{Lip}f_{j}\to|Df|_{\ast}
. By Lemma
7.3
we have

\displaystyle\int_{U}\operatorname{Lip}f\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{U}\operatorname{Lip}f_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{U}|Df|_{\ast}\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since
\operatorname{Lip}f
is am
\ast
-upper gradient, we have by Proposition
2.10
the inequality
|Df|_{\ast}\leq\operatorname{Lip}f

\mu
-a.e., this implies the claimed equality.

The implication (ii)
\implies
(iii) is trivial, and thus it remains to prove (iii)
\implies
(i). Assume that (iii) holds. Applying (iii) to the function
f=\operatorname{dist}(S,\cdot)
we obtain that
\operatorname{Lip}f(x)=0

\mu
-a.e.
x\in S
, since
|Df|_{\ast}\chi_{S}=0
by Lemma
2.12
. However, if
S
is
\eta
-porous for some
\eta>0
then
\operatorname{Lip}f(x)\geq\eta
for every
x\in S
. Thus (iii) implies that porous sets are
\mu
-null. By
[
8
, Lemma 8.3]
it follows that
X
can be expressed as
X=N\cup\bigcup_{i}X_{i}
where
\mu(N)=0
and
\dim_{H}X_{i}<\infty
for each
i
. Thus
X
can be covered by a countable collection of fragment-wise charts, cf. Theorem
5.5
. To complete the proof it suffices to show that every fragment-wise chart is a Cheeger chart.

Let
(U,\varphi)
be a fragment-wise chart (of dimension
n
) in
X
. In particular

0<I(\varphi,x)\leq\inf_{\|\lambda\|_{\ast}=1}\operatorname{Lip}(\lambda\circ\varphi)(x)\quad\mu-a.e.\ x\in U,

so that uniqueness of Cheeger differentials holds on
U
(cf.
[
8
, Lemma 3.3]
). Let
f\in\operatorname{LIP}(X)
, and let
\mathrm{d}f
be the fragment-wise differential of
f
with respect to
(U,\varphi)
. If
\Psi
is given by Proposition
4.5
for
\psi=(\varphi,f)\in\operatorname{LIP}(X,\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, then
\mathrm{d}f:U\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
satisfies
\Psi((\mathrm{d}_{x}f,-1),x)=0

\mu
-a.e.
x\in U
. By our assumption (iii), we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(f-\bm{\xi}\circ\varphi)(x)\leq\omega_{x}\left(\Psi((\bm{\xi},-1),x)\right)

a.e. for every piecewise constant
\bm{\xi}
. By approximating
\mathrm{d}_{x}f
by such
\bm{\xi}
we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(f-\mathrm{d}_{x}f\circ\varphi)(x)\leq\omega_{x}\left(\Psi((\mathrm{d}_{x}f,-1),x)\right)=0

\mu
-a.e.
x\in U
. Consequently
\mathrm{d}f
is the Cheeger differential of
f
by definition.
∎
