Dynamic Contextual Pricing with Doubly Non-Parametric Random Utility Models
Elynn Chen, Xi ChenLan Gao ♯, Jiayu Li

Abstract
In the evolving landscape of digital commerce, adaptive dynamic pricing strategies are essential for gaining a competitive edge. This paper introduces novel doubly nonparametric random utility models that eschew traditional parametric assumptions used in estimating consumer demand’s mean utility function and noise distribution. Existing nonparametric methods like multi-scale Distributional Nearest Neighbors (DNN and TDNN) , initially designed for offline regression, face challenges in dynamic online pricing due to design limitations, such as the indirect observability of utility-related variables and the absence of uniform convergence guarantees. We address these challenges with innovative population equations that facilitate nonparametric estimation within decision-making frameworks and establish new analytical results on the uniform convergence rates of DNN and TDNN, enhancing their applicability in dynamic environments. Our theoretical analysis confirms that the statistical learning rates for the mean utility function and noise distribution are minimax optimal. We also derive a regret bound that illustrates the critical interaction between model dimensionality and noise distribution smoothness, deepening our understanding of dynamic pricing under varied market conditions. These contributions offer substantial theoretical insights and practical tools for implementing effective, data-driven pricing strategies, advancing the theoretical framework of pricing models and providing robust methodologies for navigating the complexities of modern markets. Keywords: Dynamic Contextual Pricing; Doubly Nonparametric Estimation; Distributional Nearest Neighbours; Random Utility Models; Regret Bound;

Introduction

The key in dynamic pricing is to model and estimate consumer demand function accurately. In practice, consumer demand is influenced not only by the price of a product but also by specific customer and product information, such as browsing history, zip codes, and product features. In digital sales environments, the economic model of random utility augmented with a wealth of sales data can offer crucial insights into consumer behavior, particularly consumers’ preferences and their reactions to different prices under varying contextual conditions.
By implementing contextual pricing with random utility models, firms can not only gain deeper insights into the market, but also set dynamic prices tailored to individual customer profiles and product characteristics, potentially maximizing long-term revenue and gaining a competitive edge in the market.

Literature in dynamic contextual pricing often assumes a parametric form for either the mean utility (see, e.g.,
Shah
et al. (2019)
;
Fan
et al. (2022)
;
Luo
et al. (2024)
), which represents the average preference of consumers, or the market noise (see, e.g.,
Javanmard and
Nazerzadeh (2019)
;
Cohen
et al. (2020)
;
Xu and
Wang (2021)
), which captures the unobservable influences on demand. However, relying on parametric estimation poses significant disadvantages, primarily the risk of model misspecification. Parametric models make strong assumptions about the underlying data structure, and if these assumptions do not hold, the model’s predictions can be inaccurate, leading to suboptimal pricing strategies.

To address these challenges, our paper explores
contextual pricing with doubly nonparametric random utility models
. These models estimate both the mean utility function and the market noise’s distribution in a nonparametric manner, avoiding the pitfalls of model misspecification. The learning and decision process in these models is particularly challenging as the quantities of interest, including consumers’ true utility and the underlying market noise, are not directly observable. Sophisticated non-parametric estimation techniques designed for offline learning are not directly applicable, and they often lack the necessary theoretical guarantees for dynamic environments. We propose an innovative online approach to estimate and optimize under these models, overcoming the inherent challenges and offering a more robust framework for dynamic pricing.

Doubly Nonparametric Random Utility Models

The proposed
doubly nonparametric random utility model
are characterized by (

2
) without any parametric characterizations on
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
or
F(\cdot)
. It operates within a broad economic framework of random utility models.
In this paper, we consider the setting in which products are sold individually, with each sale resulting in a binary outcome indicating success or failure. At each decision point
t\in[T]
, a
d
-dimensional contextual covariate
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is independently and identically drawn from a fixed, but a priori unknown, distribution.
The expected market value of the product at time
t
is determined by the observed contextual covariate
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
.
A
general random utility model
for the market value
v_{t}
of the product is given by

v_{t}=\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t},

(1)

where
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
is the
unknown function
of the mean utility, and
\varepsilon_{t}
are i.i.d. noises following an
unknown distribution

F(\cdot)
with
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{t}]=0
.
We assume that the market value
v_{t}
is within an interval
[B_{v},B-B_{v}]
with constants
0<B_{v}<B

Given a posted price of
p_{t}
for the product at time
t

y_{t}:=\mathbbm{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t})
that indicates whether the a sale occurs (
y_{1}=1
) or not (
y_{1}=0
).
The model is equivalent to the probabilistic model:

y_{t}=\begin{cases}0,&\;\text{with probability}\quad F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})),\\
1,&\;\text{with probability}\quad 1-F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})).\end{cases}

(2)

Given a posted price
p_{t}
, the expected revenue at time
t
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is

\rev_{t}(p_{t}):=p_{t}\cdot\left(1-F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right).

(3)

The
doubly nonparametric contextual pricing
operates within a general economic framework of random utility models, which diverge significantly from approaches that directly model the revenue function.
For example,
Chen and
Gallego (2021)
directly models the unknown expected revenue as
\rev_{t}(p_{t})=p_{t}\cdot d(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},p_{t})
with
d(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},p_{t})\in[0,1]
representing the personalized demand function.

Bu
et al. (2022)
further assumes that
d(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},p_{t})
is separable in terms of
p_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
.
In contrast, we utilize the random utility model where the unknown expected revenue is given by (
3
) and the demand function is assumed to be
d(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},p_{t})=1-F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))
. The finer granularity in demand decomposition allows for separate analyses of the mean utility function and noise distribution, thus providing more precise control over the estimation errors of
F
and
\mathring{\mu}
and improving their statistical guarantees.

In addition, the structure of the random utility model facilitates an advanced analysis of how regret is influenced quadratically by the difference between optimal and estimated prices, which are closely tied to the estimation accuracy of
F
and
\mathring{\mu}
.
This connection leads to improved regret bounds under various scenarios. For further details on these relationships and their implications, refer to the discussions in Remark
3
in Section
3.2

The optimal price
p_{t}^{*}
is the price that maximizes
\rev_{t}(p_{t})
.
For a policy
\pi
that sets price
p_{t}
for
t
, its regret over the time horizon of
T
is defined as

\displaystyle\Reg(T;\pi)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}^{*}\mathbbm{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t}^{*})-p_{t}\mathbbm{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t})\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\equiv\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\left[\rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})-\rev_{t}(p_{t})\right].

The goal of this paper is to design a pricing policy that minimizes
\Reg(T;\pi)

\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\rev_{t}(p_{t})
.

1.2
Main Contributions

This paper distincts from existing literature by adopting a
fully nonparametric
approach to model both the mean utility function,
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
, and the noise distribution,
F(\cdot)
, in the context of dynamic pricing with random utility. This approach eliminates the risk of model misspecification and enhances the flexibility and accuracy of our pricing strategy, which is particularly beneficial in complex and evolving market environments.
We employ state-of-the-art nonparametric methods, notably the Distributional Nearest Neighbors (DNN) and the Two-scale Distributed Nearest Neighbor (TDNN) techniques, which are known for their simplicity in implementation and minimal assumptions. These estimators not only provide the flexibility needed in dynamic pricing but also achieve optimal convergence rates, improving as we scale up the model’s complexity and data fidelity.

However, existing DNN and TDNN methods, traditionally used for offline regression, are not directly applicable to the dynamic and online nature of pricing decisions due to their design limitations. Key challenges include the indirect observability of utility-related variables and the absence of robust convergence guarantees in such a dynamic context. To overcome these limitations, we employed two new population equations, (
5
) and (
11
), which enable the nonparametric estimation of both
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot)
using only binary response data
y_{t}
. This innovative approach bypasses the need for direct measurements of variables like
v_{t}
or
\epsilon_{t}
, enhancing the robustness and accuracy of our estimations, and thereby substantially reducing the regret associated with pricing decisions.

Our theoretical developments include a new uniform convergence rate in Theorem
5
for the DNN and TDNN estimators.
Employing the elegant U-Statistic representation
(
Lee, 2019
)
of DNN and TDNN, Theorem
5
establishes that the DNN and TDNN estimators achieve the
minimax optimal rate
of uniform convergence for nonparametric regression under the second-order smoothness condition and the fourth-order smoothness condition, respectively
(
Stone, 1982
)
.

Moreover, our theoretical analyses reveal new insights into the interdependencies between the estimation accuracy for
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot)
, and their impact on the overall performance of the pricing strategy within the
doubly nonparametric setting
. Firstly, our analysis shows that the regret bounds for our
doubly nonparametric
dynamic pricing model intricately depend on the trade-offs between the dimensionality of contextual variables (
d
) and the smoothness properties of the noise distribution (
m
), as detailed in the last row of Table

(
Chen and
Gallego, 2021
;
Bu
et al., 2022
)
, offering more precise control over varying smoothness levels of

and
\mathring{\mu}

Specifically, when the revenue function exhibits

Literature and Organization

We explore the extensive literature on contextual dynamic pricing, focusing on the underlying economic models and nonparametric techniques. Generally, this literature splits into two categories: contextual dynamic pricing under random choice models and direct modeling of demand or revenue functions. The latter is often treated as a special case of bandit problems where the reward function corresponds to the revenue function. Additionally, we review related work in dynamic pricing, including recent developments that incorporate inventory control, fairness, and privacy considerations. These areas could potentially integrate with our
doubly nonparametric contextual pricing
approach in future research directions.

2.1
Mean Utility Function Estimation

To address the challenge of not directly observing random utility
v_{t}
, we observe the following property for both single-product dynamic pricing.
Specifically, if a uniform random policy
p_{t}\sim\Unif(0,B)
with
B
being the upper bound for the market value is adopted, we can recover the mean utility function in an average sense. Denote by
g_{t}:=By_{t}
.
It can be observed that given the contextual covariate
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
,
g_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[By_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{t}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}[By_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\varepsilon_{t}]\;|\;\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right]=B\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{t}}\Big[\frac{\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}}{B}\,\Big|\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\Big]=\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}).

(5)

Therefore, if the seller randomly sets i.i.d. price
p_{t}{\sim}\Unif(0,B)
for each period
t
in the exploration phase
{\cal T}_{k,exp}
, then the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
can be estimated by regressing
g_{t}
on
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
based on observations
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}:=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},g_{t})\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
.

Distributional Nearest Neighbors.

To non-parametrically estimate the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
, we apply the method of Distributional Nearest Neighbors (DNN,
Steele (2009)
;
Biau
et al. (2010)
) which is flexible and applicable even with a limited amount of data.
DNN is a variation of
K
-Nearest Neighbours (
K
-NN) and automatically assigns monotonic weights to the nearest neighbors in a distributional fashion on the entire sample.
It incorporates the idea of bagging by averaging all 1-NN estimators, each constructed from randomly subsampling
s
observations without replacement. Here
s
is the subsampling scale diverging with the total sample size.

Suppose a sample
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{t})\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
of size
n_{k,exp}
is collected in the exploration phase of the
k
th episode. Since
g_{t}:=By_{t}
satisfies (

Considering a new contextual covariate observation
\boldsymbol{x}
, our goal is to fit a non-parametric mean function from the sample to estimate the underlying truth
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
. We reorder the observations
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},g_{t})\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
according to
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
’s Euclidean distance to
\boldsymbol{x}
and denote the reordered sample as
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{(1)},g_{(1)}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{(n_{k,exp})},g_{(n_{k,exp})})\}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(1)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(2)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\cdots\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(n_{k,exp})}-\boldsymbol{x}\|,

(6)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm, and the ties are broken by assigning the smallest rank to the observation with the smallest natural index.

g_{(1)}
is defined as the
1
-NN estimator of
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
based on the sample
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},g_{t})\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
.
For simpler notation, denote
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}:=(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},g_{t})
for
1\leq t\leq n_{k,\exp}
.
For
1\leq s\leq n_{k,exp}
, the DNN estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)
with subsampling scale
s
for estimating
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
is formally defined as the average over all
1
-NN estimators based on the
s
-scale subsamples, that is,

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)={n_{k,exp}\choose s}^{-1}\underset{1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\\
\leq i_{s}\leq n_{k,exp}}{\sumop\displaylimits}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{s}}),

(7)

where
g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{s}})
is the
1
-NN estimator of
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
based on the subsample
(\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{s}})
.
Specifically, with a abuse of notation,
g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{s}}):=g_{(1)}
from the reordered sample
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{(1)},g_{(1)}),\cdots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{(s)},g_{(s)})\}
of the subsample
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},g_{i_{1}}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{s}},g_{i_{s}})\}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(1)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(2)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\cdots\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{(s)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|.

(8)

The subsampling scale
s
will be chosen to balance the trade-off between bias and variance.
In addition, it is shown in
Steele (2009)
that the DNN estimator also admits an equivalent representation of a weighted nearest neighbor that

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)={n_{k,exp}\choose s}^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n_{k,exp}-s-1}{n_{k,exp}-i\choose s-1}\,g_{(i)},

(9)

where
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{(1)},g_{(1)}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{(n_{k,exp})},g_{(n_{k,exp})})\}
is the overall ordered sample according to the ordered distance in (
6
).

A nice feature of the above DNN estimator is that the weights on the nearest neighbors are characterized exclusively by the total sample size
n_{k,exp}
and the subsampling scale
s
.
Therefore, the equivalent representation in (
9
) can be exploited for easy implementation, while the representation in (
7
) is crucial in theoretical analysis for the uniform convergence rate of the estimator.

Two-scale Distributional Nearest Neighbour.

When the mean utility function and density function of covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
have a higher degree of smoothness, two-scale DNN (TDNN,
Demirkaya et al. (2022)
) can be applied to eliminate the first-order bias of the DNN estimator by aggregating two DNN estimators with distinct subsampling scales
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
. Specifically, a TDNN estimator of the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
is given by

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})=\alpha_{1}\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1})+\alpha_{2}\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{2}),

(10)

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
satisfy
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
and
\alpha_{1}s_{1}^{-2/d}+\alpha_{2}s_{2}^{-2/d}=0
(this equation serves to remove the first-order bias). From these two equations, we derive the coefficients
\alpha_{1}=1/(1-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d})
and
\alpha_{2}=-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d}/(1-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d})
.

In general, if the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
and density function of covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
are (
2l
)-times differentiable for an integer
l\geq 2
, we can construct an
l
-scale DNN estimator to eliminate the bias up to the
(l-1)
-th order by constructing a linear combination of
l
DNN estimators with distinct subsampling scales, utilizing a similar technique in (
10
).

2.2
Utility Noise Distribution Estimation

To non-parametrically estimate the cumulative distribution function
F(\cdot)
of the random noise
\varepsilon_{t}
, the key observation is that it can be expresses through a conditional expectation:

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{t}\leq z)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{P}\big(\varepsilon_{t}\leq p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\,|\,p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=z\big)\big]

(11)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{P}\big(v_{t}\leq p_{t}\,|\,p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=z\big)\big]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}[y_{t}|p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=z].

Thus, the Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression estimator can be applied to estimate
F(\cdot)
.
Given sample
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{t},p_{t}\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
collected in the exploration phase and any estimated utility function
\mu(\cdot)
, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\left\lvert n_{k,exp}b_{k}\right\rvert^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}K\left(\frac{p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-z}{b_{k}}\right)y_{t},

(12)

\displaystyle\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\left\lvert n_{k,exp}b_{k}\right\rvert^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}K\left(\frac{p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-z}{b_{k}}\right),

where
K(\cdot)
is a chosen
m
-th order kernel function and
b_{k}
is the bandwidth that will be chosen appropriately later.
Based on the sample
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{t},p_{t}\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
and any estimated utility function
\mu(\cdot)
, we apply the Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator of
F(z)
defined by

\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\mu)=1-\frac{\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\mu)}{\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\mu)}.

(13)

In Section
2.1
, we have introduced the DNN and TDNN estimators of the mean utility function. For instance, if the DNN estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};s)
is applied for each period
t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}
based on the collected sample
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{t}\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
, the estimator of the distribution
F(z)
in (
13
) can be written as

\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}\right)=1-\frac{\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN})}{\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{DNN})}.

(14)

Analogously, the Nadaraya-Watson estimator of
F(z)
based on the TDNN estimator of the mean utility function is given by

\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}\right)=1-\frac{\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN})}{\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN})}.

(15)

2.3
Optimal Decision

In the
k
-th episode, the estimators
\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\cdot)
for the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}\right)
for the noise distribution
F(z)
, derived in the exploration phase,
will be employed to inform data-driven optimal decision for dynamic pricing throughout the exploitation phase.

Oracle Optimal Decision.

Under the oracle setting where the true
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot)
are known, the optimal offered price
p_{t}^{*}
can be determined according to

p_{t}^{*}=\underset{p_{t}\geq 0}{\argmax}~p_{t}[1-F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})].

(16)

Under Assumption
4
, the oracle optimal price is given by

\displaystyle p_{t}^{*}=h\circ\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),\text{where}\quad h(v)

\displaystyle:=v+\phi^{-1}(-v)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\phi(z):=z-\frac{1-F(z)}{F^{(1)}(z)}.

(17)

Here
h\circ\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
denotes the composite function
h(\mathring{\mu}(\cdot))
and
F^{(1)}
is the first-order derivative of
F
.

Feasible Optimal Decision.

In reality, we can only estimate
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot)
from collected data.
In the
k
-th episode of Algorithm
1
, we obtain the estimated mean utility function
\widehat{\mu}_{k}
using either the DNN or TDNN estimator and calculate the kernel estimator
\widehat{F}_{k}(z)
based on (
14
) or (
15
).
Accordingly, the data-driven optimal offered price
\widehat{p}_{t}
for
t\in{\cal T}_{k,com}
in the exploitation phase is obtained by optimizing the estimated reward. That is,
\widehat{p}_{t}=\underset{p_{t}>0}{\argmax}~p_{t}[1-\widehat{F}_{k}(p_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})]
.
As a result, it can be obtained that for any
t\in{\cal T}_{k,com}
,

\widehat{p}_{t}=\widehat{h}_{k}\circ\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),

(18)

where

\widehat{h}_{k}(v)=v+\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(-v)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)=z-\frac{1-\widehat{F}_{k}(z)}{\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z)}.

(19)

Here
\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z)
represents the first-order derivative of
\widehat{F}_{k}(z)
and is defined by

\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z)=\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})=-\frac{\widehat{a}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})-\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})}{\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})^{2}},

(20)

where
\widehat{a}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})
and
\widehat{\xi}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})
are defined in (
12
) and their corresponding first derivatives are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{-1}{n_{k,exp}b_{k}^{2}}\sumop\displaylimits_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}K^{(1)}\left(\frac{p_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-z}{b_{k}}\right)y_{t},

\displaystyle\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{-1}{n_{k,exp}b_{k}^{2}}\sumop\displaylimits_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}K^{(1)}\left(\frac{p_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-z}{b_{k}}\right).

3
Theoretical Guarantees

In this section, we establish the regret analysis of the proposed Algorithm
1
for doubly robust learning and decision in contextual dynamic pricing. For the theoretical analysis, we first introduce some notations and present necessary assumptions.
For any candidate mean utility function
\mu:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, let
\xi(z;\mu)
be the density function of
Z=p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
evaluated at
Z=z

and define
F(z;{\mu}):=1-\mathbb{E}[y_{t}|p_{t}-{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=z]
.
Therefore, the underlying true noise distribution
F(z)=F(z;\mathring{\mu})
according to (
11
).
We next introduce the assumptions about the market noise
\varepsilon_{t}
, the distribution function
F(u;\mu)
, density function
\xi(u;\mu)
, and the kernel function
K(x)
.

Assumption 1

(Smoothness of
F(z;\mu)
and
F^{(1)}(z;\mu)
)
.

The true distribution of the market noise
F(z)
is
\ell_{F}
-Lipschitz.
Let
{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}:=[-B_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon}]
be the support of the market noise, it is assumed that, for any
\delta>0
,
\sup_{\|\mu-\mathring{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta,z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}}|F^{(1)}(z;\mu)-F^{(1)}(z)|\leq\ell_{F}\delta
.
Moreover, it holds that
\min_{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B),\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})]}F^{(1)}(z)\geq c_{F}
for some constant
c_{F}>0
.

Assumption 2

(Smoothness of
\xi(\cdot;\mu)
)
.

There exists an integer
m\geq 2
and a constant
\ell_{\xi}
such that for all
\mu\in{\cal N}_{0}:=\{\mu:\|\mu-\mathring{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq(T^{-\frac{2m+1}{(4m-1)(d+4)}}\land T^{-\frac{1}{d+8}})\sqrt{d\log T}\}
, the density function
\xi(\cdot;\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{(m)}
and
\xi^{(m)}(\cdot;\mu)
is
\ell_{\xi}
-Lipschitz continuous on
{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, there exist constants
B_{1}>0
and
B_{2}>0
such that for any
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
,
\max(\|\xi(\cdot;{\mu})\|_{\infty},\|\xi_{(}\cdot;\mu))^{(1)}\|_{\infty})\leq B_{1}
and
\min_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}}\xi(z;\mu)\geq B_{2}
.
In addition, define
a(z;\mu):=\xi(z;\mu)F(z;\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and we assume
a^{(m)}(z;\mu)
is
\ell_{\xi}
-Lipschitz on
{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
.

Assumption 3

(Condition for
K(x)
)
.

The kernel function
K(x)
and
K^{(1)}(x)
are both
\ell_{K}
-Lipschitz continuous with bounded support. In addition,
K(x)
is an order-m kernel, that is,
\intop\nolimits K(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
,
\intop\nolimits x^{j}K(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0
for
j\in[m-1]
, and
\intop\nolimits|x^{m}K(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.

Assumption 4

(Regularity condition)
.

There exists positive constants
L_{\phi}
and
B_{u}
such that
\phi^{(1)}(z)\geq L_{\phi}
for all
z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}
with
\phi(z)
defined in (
17
).

Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
are standard assumptions for the smoothness of the relevant functions in nonparametric estimation and inference. The rate of convergence
(T^{-\frac{2m+1}{(4m-1)(d+2)}}\land T^{-\frac{1}{2(d+4)}})\sqrt{d\log T}
in Assumption
2
can be satisfied for the DNN or TDNN estimators (with a further improved neighborhood size) with appropriately chosen parameters.
Assumption
4
guarantees that
\phi(x)
is strictly increasing and, thus, the uniqueness of the optimal solution of (
16
).
It is weaker than that in
Javanmard and
Nazerzadeh (2019)
since
1-F(u)
is log-concave under the further restriction of
L_{\phi^{\prime}}\geq 1
.

The regret analysis unfolds in two steps. Firstly, we will develop the uniform convergence rate of both the DNN and TDNN estimator in estimating the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}
, as well as the convergence rate of the Nadaraya-Watson estimator for estimating the noise distribution
F(\cdot)
. Secondly, building upon the uniform convergence rate of the estimators, we will obtain an upper bound for the regret of Algorithm
1
.

3.1
Statistical Convergence Rate of Learning

We will establish statistical uniform convergence of non-parametric estimators for both the mean utility function and the market noise distribution.
The finite-sample uniform bound for the DNN and TDNN estimators of
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
are presented below.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose density function
f(\cdot)
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
are two times continuously differentiable with bounded second-order derivatives on the compact support
\mathcal{X}
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
. The density
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is bounded away from 0 and
\infty
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then we have for the DNN estimator that with probability
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq Cs^{-2/d}+B\sqrt{\frac{2s[\log\delta^{-1}+\log d+d\log n]}{n}}.

(21)

Furthermore, assume density function
f(\cdot)
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
are four times continuously differentiable with bounded second, third, and fourth-order derivatives on the compact support
\mathcal{X}
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
. Then we have for TDNN estimator
D_{n}(s_{1},s_{2})(\boldsymbol{x})
with
c_{1}s_{2}\leq s_{1}\leq c_{2}s_{2}
that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})|

(22)

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{1}^{-\min\{3,\,4/d\}}+CB\sqrt{\frac{2s_{1}[\log\delta^{-1}+\log d+d\log n]}{n}}.

Here
C
is a constant depending on the underlying density function
f(\cdot)
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
, the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
, the ratio of
s_{1}/s_{2}
, and the dimensionality d, which may take difference values from line to line.

The proof of Theorem
5
can be found in Section
C
of the supplementary material. The uniform convergence rate results in theorem
5
and the proofs are new to the literature and hold independent interest. Regarding the the uniform convergence of DNN estimator, the first error term
s^{-2/d}
provides an upper bound for the bias, while the second error term corresponds to bound for the variance. In a similar manner, in the uniform bound for TDNN estimator, the first error term
s_{2}^{-4/d}
is related to the bias and the second error term is associated with the variance. We can observe from Theorem
5
that the TDNN estimator preserves the same order of variance as the DNN estimator but reduces the bias. This improvement is achieved by TDNN’s formulation of a linear combination of two DNN estimators, targeted at eliminating the first-order bias. For any general integer
l\geq 2
, one can employ an
l
-scale DNN estimator, which is defined as a linear combination of
l
DNN estimators, each with a distinct subsampling scale to remove the bias up to the
(l-1)
-th order. This construction requires the mean utility function and density function of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
to have bounded derivatives up to the
2l
-th order.

Building upon the uniform convergence rate for DNN estimators, we obtain the following result on the accuracy of the kernel estimator
\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}\right)
defined in (
14
) for estimating the noise distribution
F(z)
.

Theorem 6
.

Assume DNN estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}
is applied with subsampling size
s=O(n_{k,\exp}^{\frac{d}{d+4}})
.
Under condition of Theorem
5
for DNN estimator and Assumptions
1
–
4
, there exist constants
C_{1}>0
and
C_{2}>0
such that for any episode
k
satisfying
n_{k,exp}^{\frac{2m}{2m+1}}\geq\max(C_{1}d(\frac{\log n_{k,exp}}{2m+1}+1),3\log n_{k,exp})
with probability at least
1-2\delta
with
\delta\in(4\exp\{-C_{2}n_{k,exp}^{\frac{m}{2m+1}}/\log n_{k,exp}\},1/2]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon},{\mu}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}\right)-F(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq B_{F}n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{m}{2m+1}}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log\delta^{-1}})

(23)

\displaystyle+B_{F}{n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{2}{d+4}}\sqrt{d\log n_{k,exp}}}.

Theorem

in Appendix

and Theorem

The first term in the upper bound comes from kernel approximation of the noise distribution, while the second term comes from the upstream estimation error of DNN estimator for mean utility

For existing work that assumes a parametric model of
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})

However, Theorem

When TDNN estimator is applied to estimate the nonparametric mean regression function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and then the same Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator for the error distribution

, we can obtain the following parallel result in Theorem

below for the kernel estimator

Theorem 7
.

Assume TDNN estimator

is applied with subsampling scales
s_{1}=O(n_{k,\exp}^{\frac{d}{d+8}\lor\frac{1}{7}})

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon},{\mu}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}\left(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}\right)-F(z)\right\rvert

3.2
Regret Analysis of Decision Making

The following result establishes an upper bound for the cumulative regret of Algorithm
1
over a time horizon
T
when DNN estimator is applied in the mean utility function estimation. The proofs of Theorem
8
and
9
are provided in Appendix
A
, respectively.

Theorem 8
.

Assume the DNN estimator is applied with subsampling size
s=O(n_{k,exp}^{\frac{d}{d+4}})
to estimate the mean utility function.
Suppose condition of Theorem
5
for DNN estimator and Assumptions
1
–
4
hold. Let the exploration phase size in the
k
th episode be
n_{k,exp}=n_{k}^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}\lor\frac{d+4}{d+8}}
in Algorithm

, then for any
T\geq[\max(C_{1}d(1+\frac{\log T}{2m+1}),3\log T)]^{\frac{2m+1}{m}(\frac{4m-1}{2m+1}\land\frac{d+8}{d+4})}
, the total regret satisfies

\Reg(\pi,T)\leq CT^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}\lor\frac{d+4}{d+8}}[(\log T)^{2}+d\log T].

(25)

Remark 1
.

Theorem
8
requires that the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
is twice differentiable and the error distribution
F(\cdot)
has
m
-th order derivatives.
The regret upper bound results from a synergistic learning of
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot)
.
Specifically, learning a twice-differentiable
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
incurs a regret bound of
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}})
, while learning an
m
-th differentiable

incurs a regret bound of
\widetilde{\cal O}(T^{\frac{d+4}{d+8}})
.
Overall, the regret bound is dominated by
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}})
as
1\leq d\leq\frac{6}{m-1}
and is instead dominated by
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{d+4}{d+8}})
as
d>\frac{6}{m-1}
.

If the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
is further fourth differentiable, the more sophisticated TDNN estimator can be utilized to estimate
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
in Algorithm
1
, achieving a lower regret bound due to its debiasing capability. The corresponding regret bound of incorporating the TDNN estimator are detailed in Theorem
9
below.

Theorem 9
.

Assume the TDNN estimator is applied with subsampling sizes
s_{1}=O(n_{k,exp}^{\frac{d}{d+8}\lor\frac{1}{7}})
and

for mean utility function estimation. Suppose condition of Theorem

for TDNN estimator and Assumptions

–
4

in Algorithm
1
, then for any
T\geq[\max(C_{1}d(1+\frac{\log T}{2m+1}),3\log T)]^{\frac{2m+1}{m}(\frac{4m-1}{2m+1}\land\frac{d+16}{d+8}\land\frac{13}{7})}
, the total regret satisfies

\Reg(\pi,T)\leq CT^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}\lor\frac{d+8}{d+16}\lor\frac{7}{13}}[(\log T)^{2}+d\log T].

(26)

Remark 2
.

The overall regret of doubly-nonparametric learning and pricing is a trade-off between two components: the term of order
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{d+8}{d+16}\lor\frac{7}{13}})
corresponds to learning a fourth differentiable mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
, while the term of order
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}})
corresponds to learning an
m
-th differentiable distribution function
F(\cdot)
.

When
F(\cdot)
is comparatively more difficult to learn, i.e. less smooth in the sense that
m<1+\min(\frac{12}{d},9)
, the overall regret is dominated by
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}})
.
When
\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
is comparatively more difficult to learn, i.e. higher-dimensional such that
m\geq 1+\min(\frac{12}{d},9)
, the the overall regret is dominated by
\widetilde{{\cal O}}(T^{\frac{d+8}{d+16}\lor\frac{7}{13}})
where the term
\frac{7}{13}
corresponds to the setting of
d=1
.

Remark 3
.

The structure of the random utility model leads to (
16
) and (
17
), facilitating an advanced analysis of regret, which demonstrates a high-order dependence on the square of the difference between the optimal and estimated prices.
To elaborate, consider the revenue function
\rev_{t}(p)
defined in (
3
) along with its first and second derivatives,
\rev_{t}^{(1)}(\cdot)
and
\rev_{t}^{(2)}(\cdot)
respectively.
As
p_{t}^{*}
is the price that maximizes revenue, it holds that
\rev_{t}^{(1)}(p_{t}^{*})=0
. A Taylor expansion around
p_{t}^{*}
yields

\rev_{t}(p_{t})=\rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\rev_{t}^{(2)}(p)(p_{t}-p_{t}^{*})^{2},

for some
p
between
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{*}
.
Additionally, according to (
17
), the
(p_{t}-p_{t}^{*})^{2}
is directly linked to the square of the estimation errors in
F
and
\mathring{\mu}
.

This nuanced relationship results in improved regret bounds under various scenarios, surpassing those reported in
Chen and
Gallego (2021)
and
Bu
et al. (2022)
. Unlike the linear dependence on the difference between optimal and estimated prices observed in the discretization-based methods used in these studies, our model’s regret depends quadratically on these differences, leading to potentially tighter regret bounds under comparable conditions.

Remark 4
.

Theorem
8
and
9
require only second- and fourth-order smoothness, respectively, of the true utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
. When the smoothness of
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
reaches a general level
\ell
for an even integer
\ell
, it is possible to develop a
(\ell/2)
-scale DNN estimator analogous to the TDNN framework that achieves better convergence rates.

Remark 5
.

When we consider the policy set
\Pi:=\{\pi:\pi(p_{t})=\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(-\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\}
with
\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)=z-\frac{1-\widehat{F}_{k}(z)}{\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z)}
, where
\widehat{\mu}_{k}
,
\widehat{F}_{k}
, and
\widehat{F}^{(1)}_{k}
are estimators for the mean utility function, noise distribution, and derivative of the noise distribution in the

and its derivative

Remark 6
.

Our analysis can be generally extended to a broader range of nonparametric estimators for the mean utility function, such as kernel estimators, random forest, and neural network methods, provided that these estimators achieve a similar uniform convergence rate as presented in Theorem

5
Conclusion

This paper advances the field of dynamic pricing by introducing and exploring doubly nonparametric random utility models. By forgoing parametric assumptions for both the mean utility function and the noise distribution, our approach reduces the risk of model misspecification and increases the reliability of pricing strategies. Employing advanced nonparametric estimation techniques like Distributional Nearest Neighbors and Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimation, we enhance the accuracy of dynamic pricing decisions in complex market environments. Our theoretical contributions, which include new analytical results and insights into the interaction between model components, offer valuable guidance for both academic research and practical applications. This work not only deepens the understanding of nonparametric estimation in pricing models but also provides a robust framework for businesses to optimize pricing dynamically and effectively in data-driven markets.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{t}(\pi)\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}^{*}\mathbbm{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t}^{*})\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}\mathbbm{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t})\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}^{*}\mathbbm{1}(\varepsilon_{t}+\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\geq p_{t}^{*})\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}\mathbbm{1}(\varepsilon_{t}+\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\geq p_{t})\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{*}\left(1-F(p_{t}^{*}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right)-p_{t}\left(1-F(p_{t}-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right)

\displaystyle:=\rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})-\rev_{t}(p_{t}),

where

is defined as the expected revenue under price

.
Note that

. It follows by Taylor expansion that

(38)

for some

between
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{*}
.

,
we claim that, for any

p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{*}

(39)

which is derived by the fact that

\left\lvert\rev_{t}^{(2)}(p)\right\rvert=\left\lvert-2F^{(1)}(p-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))-pF^{(2)}(p-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right\rvert,

and
p_{t},p_{t}^{*}\in[0,B]

Combining (
37
), (
38
) and (
39
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{t}(\pi)\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2+B)C/2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(p_{t}(\pi)-p_{t}^{*})^{2}\right]

Let
\widehat{p}_{t}:=p_{t}(\widehat{\pi})
be the price given by Algorithm

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{t}(\widehat{\pi})\;|\;{\cal H}_{t-1}^{+}\right]

We can obtain by (

) as well as the definitions of
p_{t}^{*}
and
\widehat{p_{t}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\reg_{t}(\widehat{\pi})\right]

\displaystyle\leq{(2+B)C}/{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{h}(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))-h(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2+B)C\Big\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{h}(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))-h(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right)^{2}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(h(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))-h(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))\right)^{2}\right]\Big\}.

B.1
Proof of Lemma

Recall that
F(u;\mu):=1-\mathbb{E}[y_{t}\;|\;p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=u]
and

.
The proof builds on the intuition that the kernel estimator
\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\mu)
converges to the population conditional expectation

and further
F(z;\mu)
approaches
F(z)
as
\mu
approaches
\mathring{\mu}

By the triangular inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon},\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\mu)-F(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon},\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\mu)-F(z;\mu)\right\rvert

(51)

\displaystyle+\sup_{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon},\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert F(z;\mu)-F(z)\right\rvert.

In the sequel, we will bound each of the two terms on the right hand side of (
51
).

First, we concern ourselves with the accuracy of kernel estimator
\widehat{F}_{k}(u;\mu)
for a fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
.
Applying the same technique of proving Lemma 4.2 in
Fan
et al. (2022)

(52)

Next, regarding the second term on the right hand side of (
51
), recalling the definition of the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{k}
given in (
28
), we have for any
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
,

\displaystyle F(z;{\mu})

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}\;|\;p_{t}-{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

(53)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\;|\;p_{t}-{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(r_{n_{k,exp}}+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}-{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\;|\;p_{t}-{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

\displaystyle=F(z-r_{n_{k,exp}}),

and similarly,

\displaystyle F(z;\mu)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}\;|\;p_{t}-{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

(54)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}-\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\;|\;p_{t}-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathbb{P}(-r_{n_{k,exp}}+\varepsilon_{t}\geq p_{t}-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\;|\;p_{t}-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=z)

\displaystyle=F(z+r_{n_{k,exp}}).

Therefore, by Assumption
1
that

is

-Lipschitz, we have

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon},{\mu}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left\lvert F(u;{\mu})-F(u)\right\rvert\leq\ell_{F}r_{n_{k,exp}}.

(55)

A combination of (
51
), (
52
) and (
55
) yields Lemma
11
. This concludes the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
13
.

Recall the definitions of estimator
\widehat{h}_{k}(v)
in (
19
) and the population quantity
h(v)
in (
17
) that

\widehat{h}_{k}(v)=v+\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(-v),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)=z-\frac{1-\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})}{\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})},

(59)

and

h(u)=v+\phi^{-1}(-v),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\phi(z)=z-\frac{1-F(z)}{F^{(1)}(z)}.

(60)

Observe that

\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon},B-B_{\varepsilon}]}|\widehat{h}_{k}(v)-h(v)|=\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}|\widehat{\phi}^{-1}_{k}(v)-\phi^{-1}(v)|.

(61)

Thus it suffices to study the upper bound for
\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}|\widehat{\phi}^{-1}_{k}(v)-\phi^{-1}(v)|
.

Step. I.

First, we derive a uniform upper bound for
\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)-\phi(z)\right\rvert
, which can be decomposed by

\displaystyle\underset{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}}{\sup}\;\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)-\phi(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\underset{u\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}}{\sup}\left\lvert\frac{\left(1-\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})\right)\left(\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})-F^{(1)}(z)\right)}{\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})F^{(1)}(z)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\underset{z\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}}{\sup}\left\lvert\frac{\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})-F(z)}{F^{(1)}(z)}\right\rvert.

By Assumption
1
, we have (noting that
\phi
is an increasing function by Assumption
4
)

\min_{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B),\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})]}F^{(1)}(z)\geq c_{F}

and applying the continuity of
F^{(1)}(u)
yields that for some constant
\delta_{F}>0
,

\min_{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]}F^{(1)}(z)\geq c_{F}/2

Therefore, it follows from Lemma
12
that for
\widehat{\mu}_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
, with probability at least
1-4\delta
,

\min_{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]}\widehat{F}^{(1)}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})\geq c_{F}/4,

as long as
n_{k,exp}^{\frac{m-1}{2m+1}}\geq(8c_{F})^{-1}B_{f}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log\delta^{-1}})
and
r_{n_{k,exp}}\leq c_{F}B_{f}^{-1}/8
.

Combining the above results with Lemmas
11
and
12
that bound
\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})-F(z)\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{k}^{(1)}(z;\widehat{\mu}_{k})-F^{(1)}(z)\right\rvert
,
we have with probability
1-6\delta
,

\displaystyle\underset{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]}{\sup}\;\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)-\phi(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16B_{f}}{c_{F}^{2}}\cdot\left(n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{m-1}{2m+1}}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)})+r_{n_{k,exp}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{4B_{F}}{c_{F}}\left(n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{m}{2m+1}}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)})+r_{n_{k,exp}}\right).

(62)

Step. II.

We bound
\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}|\widehat{\phi}^{-1}_{k}(v)-\phi^{-1}(v)|
through
\underset{z\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]}{\sup}\;\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)-\phi(z)\right\rvert
.
The computation here involves obtaining the inverse of
\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)
, which is not necessarily monotone.
To deal with this challenge, we will show in the sequel that
\widehat{\phi}_{k}(z)
is very ‘close’ to
\phi
in some main interval of interest, which contains
[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]
and depends only on
F
. Since
\phi^{(1)}(z)\geq L_{\phi}>0
, it holds that
\phi(\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F})\leq B_{\varepsilon}-B-L_{\phi}\delta_{F}
and similarly,
\phi(\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F})\geq-B_{\varepsilon}+L_{\phi}\delta_{F}
. Therefore, we have
[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]\subset[\phi(\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F}),\phi(\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F})]
. Moreover, by (
62
), we can deduce that as
n_{k,exp}^{\frac{m-1}{2m+1}}\gtrsim\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log\delta^{-1}})
and
r_{n_{k,exp}}\lesssim 1
,

\widehat{\phi}_{k}\big(\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F}\big)\leq B_{\varepsilon}-B

and

\widehat{\phi}_{k}\big(\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}\big)\geq-B_{\varepsilon}.

By continuity of
\phi
and
\widehat{\phi}_{k}
, it follows that

[B_{\varepsilon}-B,B_{\varepsilon}]\subset\phi\big([\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]\big)\cap\widehat{\phi}_{k}\big([\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]\big).

For
v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]
, we define the inverse of
\widehat{\phi}_{k}
as

\widehat{\phi}^{-1}_{k}(v):=\inf\left\{u\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]:\widehat{\phi}_{k}(u)=v\right\}.

(63)

Now we are ready to upper bound
\underset{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}{\sup}\;\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}^{-1}_{k}(v)-\phi^{-1}(v)\right\rvert
.
From Assumption
4
that
\phi^{(1)}(u)\geq L_{\phi}
, it holds that
\left\lvert\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2})\right\rvert\geq L_{\phi}\left\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rvert
and hence with probability at least
1-6\alpha
,

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(v)-\phi^{-1}(v)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\phi}^{-1}\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}\left\lvert\phi(\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(v))-\phi(\phi^{-1}(v))\right\rvert

(64)

\displaystyle=L_{\phi}^{-1}\sup_{v\in[B_{\varepsilon}-B,-B_{\varepsilon}]}\left\lvert\phi(\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(v))-\widehat{\phi}_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{-1}(v))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\phi}^{-1}\sup_{u\in[\phi^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}-B)-\delta_{F},\phi^{-1}(-B_{\varepsilon})+\delta_{F}]}\left\lvert\widehat{\phi}_{k}(u)-\phi(u)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\phi}^{-1}\frac{16B_{f}}{c_{F}^{2}}\cdot\left(n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{m-1}{2m+1}}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\alpha)})+r_{n_{k,exp}}\right)

\displaystyle+L_{\phi}^{-1}\frac{4B_{F}}{c_{F}}\left(n_{k,exp}^{-\frac{m}{2m+1}}\sqrt{\log n_{k,exp}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log(1/\alpha)})+r_{n_{k,exp}}\right).

13
.

Appendix C
Proofs of Uniform Finite-Sample Bounds of DNN or TDNN

Recall that the DNN and TDNN estimators are constructed using the sample
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},g_{t})\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{k,exp}}
of size
n_{k,exp}
, where
g_{t}=By_{t}
with
y_{t}
being an Bernoulli random variable.
To simplify the notation, we denote
n=n_{k,exp}
in this section when there is no ambiguity.

C.1
Proof of Theorem
5

We first prove the result (
21
) for the DNN estimator. The main idea of proof is to apply a bias-variance decomposition for the DNN estimator. The bias term can be bounded using existing result in
Demirkaya et al. (2022)
and the variance term can be addressed by applying the bound differences inequality in empirical theory and concentration inequality for U-statistic.
Observe that the estimation error can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})\right)+\left(\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})\right).

(67)

For the second term related to the bias, it follows from applying a similar technique of proving Theorem 1 in
Demirkaya et al. (2022)
that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})\big|\leq Cs^{-2/d},

(68)

where
C
is a constant depending on the underlying density function
f(\cdot)
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
, the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and the dimensionality
d
.

For the first term related to the variance in (
67
), observe that
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)
is a U-statistic of order
s
. The main idea is to apply the concentration inequality for U-statistics and the fact that there are only finite number of distinct
K
-NN sets over
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
for any
K
, given
n
i.i.d samples.
First, by Hoeffding inequality of U-statistic (
Ai
et al. (2022)
), noting that
0\leq g_{t}:=By_{t}\leq B
, we have for any
t>0
.

\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2B^{2}s}\Big).

(69)

In addition, note that Lemma 3 in
Jiang (2019)
shows that there are at most
dn^{d}
distinct
K
-NN sets over
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
K
, given
n
i.i.d. samples. Therefore, for fixed
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{n}
and
s
, the DNN estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)
can take at most
dn^{d}
distinct possible values in view of its L-statistic representation in (
9
). Consequently, applying union bound and (
69
) yields for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})\big|\geq t\Big)\leq 2dn^{d}\exp\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2B^{2}s}\Big),

(70)

Therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s)-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})\big|\geq B\sqrt{\frac{2s[\log\delta^{-1}+\log d+d\log n]}{n}}\bigg)\leq 2\delta,

(71)

which combining with (
68
) derives the desired uniform convergence rate for DNN estimator given in (
21
).

Next we deal with the TDNN estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})
. Recall that the TDNN estimator is a linear combination of two DNN estimators with distinct subsampling scales
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, that is,

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})=\alpha_{1}\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1})+\alpha_{2}\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{2}),

where
\alpha_{1}=1/(1-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d})
and
\alpha_{2}=-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d}/(1-(s_{1}/s_{2})^{-2/d})
. Similarly, we have the bias-variance decomposition

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})-\alpha_{1}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})-\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})\right)

(72)

\displaystyle+\left(\alpha_{1}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})+\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})\right).

By Corollary 1 in
Demirkaya et al. (2022)
, it holds that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\alpha_{1}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})+\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})-\mathring{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})\big|\leq Cs^{-\min\{3,\,4/d\}},

(73)

where
C
is a constant depending on the underlying density function
f(\cdot)
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
, the mean utility function
\mathring{\mu}(\cdot)
and the dimensionality
d
.

Moreover, we have for the variance term that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})-\alpha_{1}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})-\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{1}|\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1})-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})\big|+|\alpha_{2}|\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\DNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{2})-\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})\big|.

Therefore, it follows from (
71
) that with probability
1-4\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\TDNN}(\boldsymbol{x};s_{1},s_{2})-\alpha_{1}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{1}})-\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s_{2}})\big|

(74)

\displaystyle\leq B\big(|\alpha_{1}|\sqrt{s_{1}}+|\alpha_{2}|\sqrt{s_{2}}\big)\sqrt{\frac{2[\log\delta^{-1}+\log d+d\log n]}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}B\sqrt{\frac{2s_{1}[\log\delta^{-1}+\log d+d\log n]}{n}}

where
C_{d}
is a constant depending on the dimensionality
d
and the ratio of
s_{1}/s_{2}
. Substituting (
73
) and (
74
) into (
72
) yields the desired result (
22
) for TDNN estimator. The proof of Theorem
5
is completed.
