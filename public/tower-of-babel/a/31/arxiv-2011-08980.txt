Linear Phase Retrieval for Near-Field Measurements with Locally Known Phase Relations
Alexander Paulus 1 , Jonas Kornprobst 1 , Josef Knapp 1 , and Thomas F. Eibert 1

Abstract
A linear and thus convex phase retrieval algorithm for the application in phaseless near-field far-field transformations is presented. The formulation exploits locally known phase relations among sets of measurement samples, which can in practice be acquired with multi-channel receivers. Due to the linearity of the formulation, a reliable phaseless transformation is achieved, which completely avoids the problem of local minima — the Achilles heel of most existing phase retrieval techniques. Furthermore, the necessary number of measurements are kept close to that of fully-coherent antenna measurements. Comparisons with an already existing approach exploiting local phase relations demonstrate the accuracy and reliability for synthetic data.

In the present paper, in contrast to
[
38

In this way, the problem formulation features more flexibility while keeping the computational costs low. The presented method exploits partial coherence in the measured data while preserving linearity, and thus convexity. The approach perfectly matches the problem arising from phaseless NF antenna measurements when a multi-channel receiver with multiple probe antennas is used. No synchronization between the AUT and the probes is required, and no coherence among sequentially acquired samples is assumed. However, due to the receiver architecture, local coherence between the channels of the receiver at each respective measurement position is given. This can either be achieved with vectorial measurement hardware, or with adequate scalar equipment, however, requiring a larger number of channels. Note that the formulation can also be applied to (incomplete) holographic measurements with a reference antenna. Furthermore, the need for a fixed reference antenna is removed, diminishing the problem of limited dynamic range and mutual shadowing of probe, AUT, and reference antenna.

We start by briefly revising the common NFFFT source reconstruction problem and discuss the model of partially coherent observations. For the purpose of comparability, we recapitulate an existing formulation for exploiting partially coherent measurements, followed by the novel linear phase retrieval formulation. Results for simulation data are discussed.

II
Phase Retrieval With Partial Coherence

For the sake of simplicity and without loss of generality, we consider NF antenna measurement setups where the unknown AUT is transmitting and the field is sampled by one or multiple probe antennas in receive mode. The AUT is represented by a vector of complex-valued source coefficients
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the probe antennas acquire the complex-valued signals
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and the solution of the relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\bm{b}

(1)

represents the main task of an NFFFT with full phase information.
In the case of phaseless measurements, only
\left|\bm{b}\right|
is known and (
1
) deteriorates into a nonlinear system of equations. In both cases, the desired fields, e.g., the FF, can be computed from
\bm{z}
, once it has been determined.

Let us now assume that we have acquired three sets of NF measurements,
\bm{b}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}}
,
\bm{b}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{2}}
, and
\bm{b}_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{3}}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\bm{b}_{1}
has been acquired with some random phase, but the second and third set of samples
\bm{b}_{2/3}
have been measured
simultaneously
by a two-channel receiver equipped with two probe antennas. Consequently, both sets feature the same number of entries,
m_{2}=m_{3}
, and there is a known phase relation between the
k
th entry in
\bm{b}_{2}
and
\bm{b}_{3}
for
k\in\left\{1,...,m_{2}\right\}
.

II-A

Interferometry via Linear Combinations

It is well known that the phase difference between two complex scalars
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
can be computed analytically from four magnitude measurements of the linear combinations
[
33
]

\displaystyle o_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|a_{1}\right|^{2},\hskip 14.22636pto_{3}=\left|a_{1}+a_{2}\right|^{2},

\displaystyle o_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|a_{2}\right|^{2},\hskip 14.22636pto_{4}=\left|a_{1}+\mathrm{j}a_{2}\right|^{2}

(2)

via

\displaystyle\angle\left(a_{1}\overline{a_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{atan}\left[\dfrac{o_{4}-o_{1}-o_{2}}{o_{3}-o_{1}-o_{2}}\right],

(3)

where
\overline{a_{2}}
denotes the complex conjugate of
a_{2}
.
Coming back to our example from above, we can thus formulate the task of a phaseless NFFFT with knowledge of the partial coherence between
\bm{b}_{2}
and
\bm{b}_{3}
as

\displaystyle\left|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}\\
\mathbf{A}_{2}\\
\mathbf{A}_{3}\\
\mathbf{A}_{2}+\mathbf{A}_{3}\\
\mathbf{A}_{2}+\mathrm{j}\mathbf{A}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}\,\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{1}\\
\bm{b}_{2}\\
\bm{b}_{3}\\
\bm{b}_{2}+\bm{b}_{3}\\
\bm{b}_{2}+\mathrm{j}\bm{b}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\right|,

(4)

with elementwise magnitude operations on both sides of the equation.
Note that when neglecting the added rows of linear combinations in the forward operator and the right-hand-side, the common, fully-incoherent problem is obtained. In terms of the notation in (
4
), the knowledge of the phase differences thus directly translates into additional measurement rows. The overall structure remains the same.

A simple approach of exploiting partial coherence is thus to solve (
4
) with a phase retrieval algorithm of your choice. As reported in
[
2
,
1
]
, the additional rows related to the phase differences increase the chance of a successful transformation. Still, success is not guaranteed and only limited theoretical
insight into the behavior of the formulation is available
[
39
]
.

II-B

Linear Formulation for Partial Coherence

Here, we follow an idea presented in
[
38
]
and derive a flexible formulation with low computational cost.
In order to illustrate the new approach, we consider an example with
m_{1}=1
,
m_{2}=2=m_{3}
, and

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{1}=\left|b_{11}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{11}},\hskip 2.84544pt\bm{b}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\left|b_{21}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{21}}\\
\left|b_{22}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{22}}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 2.84544pt\bm{b}_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}\left|b_{31}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{31}}\\
\left|b_{32}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{32}}\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

We can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\bm{z}=\bm{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{1}^{\text{T}}&\bm{b}_{2}^{\text{T}}&\bm{b}_{3}^{\text{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left|b_{11}\right|&0&0\\
0&\left|b_{21}\right|&0\\
0&0&\left|b_{22}\right|\\
0&\left|b_{31}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\left(\varphi_{31}-\varphi_{21}\right)}&0\\
0&0&\left|b_{32}\right|\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\left(\varphi_{32}-\varphi_{22}\right)}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{11}}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{21}}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\varphi_{22}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left(\left|\bm{b}\right|\right)\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\left(\varphi_{31}-\varphi_{21}\right)}&0\\
0&0&\mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{j}\left(\varphi_{32}-\varphi_{22}\right)}\\
\end{bmatrix}\bm{\psi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\bm{\psi},

(6)

leading to the linear formulation

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{1}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}_{2}\right)\tilde{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\hskip 14.22636pt\tilde{\bm{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{z}^{\text{T}}&\bm{\psi}^{\text{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+q}

(7)

with the stacked vector of unknowns
\tilde{\bm{z}}
consisting of the unknown AUT representation
\bm{z}
and a vector
\bm{\psi}
of all remaining unknown phase terms. The matrices
\mathbf{P}_{1/2}
in (
7
) are defined via unit and zero matrices
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{0}
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{1}\tilde{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{z}}=\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{2}\tilde{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{z}}=\bm{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{q},

(9)

and extract the respective parts from the stacked vector of unknowns. The diagonal matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
contains all measured magnitudes while the actual information about partial coherence is inside the sparse matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times q}
, containing all known phase differences.
The more phase differences are added, the less independent phase terms remain unknown and the fewer columns in
\mathbf{C}
exist.
Note that for (
7
) to yield the correct solution, we have to enforce that
\left|\psi_{k}\right|=1
,
\forall k\in\left\{1,...,q\right\}
. However, this condition is nonlinear and leads to a nonconvex problem. Still, we find an approximate and linear formulation by writing

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{1}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}_{2}\right)\tilde{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

(10)

\displaystyle\hskip-51.21504pt\text{s.t.}\,\,\,\psi_{s}=1,

which only enforces the magnitude constraint on the
s
th phase unknown and fixes its phase to zero. In this way, a linear and thus convex phase retrieval formulation for the case of partially coherent observations is obtained. Note that, in contrast to (18) in
[
38
]
, the here presented notation is more flexible and allows for an arbitrary combination of incoherent and coherent measurements. Also, the large computational effort per matrix-vector product of (21) in
[
38
]
, requiring a pseudo-inverse, is avoided. As a downside of (
10
), more unknowns are introduced.

Transformation Results

III-A

Randomly Generated Data

As a first indicator for the performance of the formulation in (
10
), synthetic data randomly taken from a complex-valued normal distribution was generated and processed. The reference formulation in (
4
) was solved via the phase retrieval algorithm described in
[
1
]
, which essentially is a nonconvex optimization-based phase retrieval algorithm similar to the widely known Wirtinger flow
[
16
]
. The algorithm features an iterative line search algorithm according to
[
40

and computes descent directions via the L-BFGS method
[
41
]
. Due to its nonconvexity, the formulation heavily depends on the initial guess, which was computed via
40
power iterations of a spectral method
[
16
]
. While (
10
) was solved via a direct inversion in Matlab
[
42
]
, at most
2000
iterations were allowed for the iterative solution of (

The model with three sets of measurements introduced in Section

was utilized, with

and

. The number of measurements with local phase relations

was varied within the range of
$0.05$n
to

For each ratio of the total number of measurements to unknowns,

It becomes evident from Fig.

IV
Conclusion

A reliable linear formulation for the task of phase retrieval with partially coherent measurements was presented and investigated. Suitable near-field data can be obtained by receivers with two or more coherent channels and appropriate probe arrays. The presented equations are highly flexible with respect to the form and availability of the knowledge of partial coherence and allow for a computationally efficient implementation. While effectively dropping a portion of the available magnitude restrictions, the algorithm features superior reliability compared to an existing nonconvex approach once a sufficient number of measurements has been acquired. The required number of samples is in the order of that needed for a conventional transformation with full phase information. Only in the regime of few available measurement samples, existing nonconvex approaches should be preferred as they do not drop information and may, by chance, return a more accurate result.
