Berry–Esseen-type estimates for random variables with a sparse dependency graph
Maximilian Janisch, Thomas Lehéricy

Abstract
We obtain Berry–Esseen-type bounds for the sum of random variables with a dependency graph and uniformly bounded moments of order δ ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ] \delta\in(2,\infty] using a Fourier transform approach. Our bounds improve the state-of-the-art in the regime where the degree of the dependency graph is large. As a Corollary of our results, we obtain a Central Limit Theorem for random variables with a sparse dependency graph that are uniformly bounded in L δ L^{\delta} for some δ ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ] \delta\in(2,\infty] .



1
Introduction

Consider

(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
a family of
L^{2}
real random variables (r.v.) indexed by a finite set
V
of cardinality
N\in\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, and let
S\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sum_{k\in V}Y_{k}
. We do not assume that they are independent.
We want to study if and at what speed the random variable

W\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{S-\mathbb{E}[S]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S]}}

(1.1)

converges to a normal distribution. Our results are non-asymptotic, meaning that
V
is fixed; our results can therefore be directly adapted to hold for triangular arrays
(Y_{m,k})_{m\in\mathbb{N},k\in\{1,\dots,N(m)\}}
of random variables for a fixed sequence
(N(m))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
going to
\infty
.

In the simplest situation,
V=\{1,\dots,N\}
and the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). The central limit theorem ensures that
W

converges, in distribution as
N\to\infty
, to a standard-normally distributed random variable. If the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
have a finite third moment we can bound the speed at which the distribution of
W
converges to the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The usual metric used to quantify this speed of convergence is the
Kolmogorov distance
, defined for two real random variables
X,Y
with distributions
\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathbb{P}_{Y}
as

d_{\text{Kol}}(X,Y)=d_{\text{Kol}}(X,\mathbb{P}_{Y})=d_{\text{Kol}}(\mathbb{P}_{X},Y)=d_{\text{Kol}}(\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathbb{P}_{Y})\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)\right|.

Note that
d_{\text{Kol}}(X,Y)\leq 1
for every
(X,Y)
.
Other metrics can be used, for example the Wasserstein-1 distance.
Berry and Esseen proved independently that

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{1}\right|^{3}]}{\mathbb{V}[Y_{1}]^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(1.2)

Here
C
is a constant independent of
V
and
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
. Esseen showed that (
1.2
) holds for
C=7.6
. The constant has been improved to
C\leq 0.5583
, cf.
[
She13a
]
.
The case of independent but non-identically distributed r.v. is well-known: the central limit theorem was generalized by Lindeberg in 1922 under his eponymous condition, and

[
She13a
]
gives for
C=0.5583

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq C\frac{\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}\right|^{3}]}{(\mathbb{V}[S])^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(1.3)

For a different constant, (
1.3
) was proven in
[
Ess44a
]
.

The case where
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not independent is much more difficult. In general, we do not even have convergence (as
N\to\infty
) of
W
to a normal distribution if we replace the condition of independence of the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
by pairwise independence. Indeed, as shown in
[
Jan88a
]
, there exists a stationary sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of pairwise independent real
L^{\infty}
-random variables with strictly positive variance such that
W
defined above converges in distribution to
0
as
n\to\infty
, not to a standard normal distribution. In fact, the situation is even worse: For any fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a sequence of identically distributed real
L^{\infty}
-random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that any sub-family
(Y_{n_{1}},Y_{n_{2}},\dots,Y_{n_{m}})
of
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
is independent, but such that
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not satisfy the Central Limit Theorem. See
[
Pru98a
]
for a proof of this result.

The need to specify the
way
the random variables depend on each other is therefore crucial. We are interested in dependency graphs
[
BR89a
,
FMN17a
]
, a slight generalization of which
is the notion of local dependence
[
CS04a
]
;
other notions include weighted dependency graphs
[
F“’er18a
]
, Markov chains and fields,
or functions of independent random variables
[
Dan14a
]
, among others. Our focus lies in Berry–Esseen-type bounds, which naturally yield a Central Limit Theorem, albeit with suboptimal conditions; for Central Limit Theorems for random variables which are only partially independent, see for example
[
Ore58a
]
and more recently
[
Jan21d
]
.

Dependency graph.

A simple graph
G
on
V
(its
vertex set
) is an ordered tuple
(V,E)
, where
E
is a set of pairs (called
edges
) of distinct vertices of
V
. If two vertices belong to the same edge, we say that they are
adjacent
to each other. If a vertex
v
belongs to an edge
e
, we say that
v
is incident to
e
. The
degree
of a vertex is the number of edges it belongs to.
We also consider multigraphs, where multiple edges are allowed: this corresponds to allowing
E
to be a multiset. In the case of multigraphs, we also allow loops, i.e. edges whose endpoints are the same vertex.

We say that a multigraph
G
is a
dependency graph of
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
if and only if the following property holds: If
V_{1},V_{2}\subset V
are disjoint and there exist no edge with one vertex in
V_{1}
and the other in
V_{2}
, then the families of random variables
(Y_{k})_{k\in V_{1}}
and
(Y_{k})_{k\in V_{2}}
are independent. For a deeper introduction to dependency graphs, see for example the remarks following
[
FMN13a
, Definition 9.1.1]
.

Dependency graphs can describe independent random variables:
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
is independent if and only if they admit the graph on
V
with no edge as a dependency graph.

Our main results concerns
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))
when the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
admit a dependency graph. The bound is state-of-the-art when the variables are in
L^{\infty}
, and beats the state-of-the-art in certain regimes when the variables are in
L^{\delta}
for some
\delta<\infty
, see Section
3
.
For conciseness, we write
a\lesssim b
to mean
a\leq Kb
for an absolute constant
K\geq 0
.

Throughout the text, we assume that the family
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
has a dependency graph of maximal degree
D\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Assume that for some
\delta\in(2,\infty)
, all
Y_{k}
are in
L^{\delta}
. Define the
renormalized standard deviation

\xi_{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\left(\frac{N}{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[S]}{N(D+1)}},

(1.4)

where

\mathcal{A}_{\delta}=\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}-c_{k}\right|^{\delta}]

for an arbitrary family of real numbers
(c_{k})_{k\in V}
.

Proposition
6.5
guarantees that
\xi_{\delta}\in[0,1]
and that
\xi_{\delta}
is a decreasing function of
\delta
.
In what follows we fix the sequence
(c_{k})_{k\in V}
.
Reasonable choices are
c_{k}=0
or
c_{k}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]
, but other values could be considered, for example
c_{k}\in\argmin_{c}\|Y_{k}-c\|_{\delta}
.

The Berry–Esseen inequality (
1.3
) for independent random variables with finite moments of order
L^{\delta}
for some
\delta\in(2,3]
is, up to the absolute constant, equivalent to the following Theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Cf.
[
Pet75a
, Theorem V.3.6]
)
.

Let
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
be a family of
\left|V\right|=N
independent random variables (in particular they admit the empty dependency graph with
D=0
). Assume that
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta\in(2,3]
. Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\lesssim\xi_{\delta}^{-\delta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2}}.

(1.5)

We conjecture that (
1.5
) remains true even for random variables with a dependency graph, if the term
\frac{1}{N}
is replaced with
\frac{D+1}{N}
.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Let
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
be a family of
\left|V\right|=N
random variables admitting a dependency graph of degree
D\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Assume that there exists
\delta\in(2,3]
such that
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}<\infty
. Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\lesssim\xi_{\delta}^{-\delta}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2}}.

(1.6)

While the conjecture remains out of reach of current techniques, both ours and Stein’s method, our results provide evidence in favor of the conjecture. In particular, our results support the heuristic that the substitution of
1/N
by
(D+1)/N
is the correct adaptation. We briefly present arguments in favor of the conjecture at the end of Section
4
.

We present our results in Section
2
, and investigate the regime where they do better than the current state-of-the-art (mostly obtained by Stein’s method) in Section
3
. The optimality of our results is discussed in Section
4
. Section
5
gathers some applications of our results: Central Limit Theorems in Section
5.1
, U-statistics in Section
5.2
, and volatility estimation for prices of financial assets in Section
5.3
. We gather the proof of our main results in Section
6
. A concise explanation of our proof ideas can be found in Section
6.1
.

2
Main results

We assume that the random variables all belong to some
L^{\delta}
for
\delta\in(2,\infty]
, equipped with
\|\cdot\|_{\delta}
, the
L^{\delta}
norm. Define
v=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S]}
. Recall
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}
and
\xi_{\delta}
as in Definition
1.1
. In Theorem
2.1
, which has the sharpest bound, we consider the case
\delta=\infty
; in Theorem
2.5
, the case
\delta\in[3,\infty)
; and in Theorem
2.6
, the case
\delta\in(2,3]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
v>0
and that

\max_{k\in V}\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq L
for some
L\in(0,\infty)
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq\max\left\{68.5\frac{(D+1)^{2}\mathcal{A}_{3}}{v^{3}}\ ,\ 22.88\frac{L(D+1)}{v}\right\}.

(2.1)

This result is essentially optimal, as discussed in section
4
.

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
gives a strictly better conclusion (except for the constant) than
[
FMN17a
, Corollary 30]
, which gives the bound

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 76.36\frac{L^{3}N(D+1)^{2}}{v^{3}}.

(2.2)

Indeed, in this case, define

M_{\delta}=\max_{k\in V}\,\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\delta}\leq L\qquad,\qquad\sigma_{\delta}=\frac{1}{M_{\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{v^{2}}{N(D+1)}}\in[0,1].

Then

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{(D+1)^{2}\mathcal{A}_{3}}{v^{3}},\frac{L(D+1)}{v}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\xi_{3}^{3}}\ ,\ \frac{1}{\xi_{\delta}}\frac{L}{\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{3}^{3}},\frac{1}{\sigma_{3}}\frac{L}{M_{3}}\right\}

(2.3)

since
\xi_{\delta}(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}/N)^{1/\delta}=\sigma_{3}M_{3}
and
\sigma_{3}\leq\xi_{3}\leq 1
, and

\displaystyle\frac{L^{3}N(D+1)^{2}}{v^{3}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{3}^{3}}\left(\frac{L}{M_{3}}\right)^{3}\geq\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{3}^{3}},\frac{1}{\sigma_{3}}\frac{L}{M_{3}}\right\}

since
L\geq M_{3}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Under the same assumptions of Theorem
2.1
with
c_{k}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]
,
[
Rin94a
]
obtains

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{v}\left((D+1)L+\sqrt{\frac{N}{v^{2}}}(D+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}L^{2}+\frac{n}{v^{2}}(D+1)^{2}L^{3}\right).

(2.4)

This is a Corollary of Theorem
2.1
because
\mathcal{A}_{3}\leq NL^{3}
. Therefore the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is less or equal than an absolute constant times the maximum of the first term of (
2.4
) and the third term of (
2.4
).

At the cost of a more complicated inequality, Theorem
2.1
can be improved to have a more precise upper bound that includes the third centered moment of
S
(or more generally moments up to any fixed order). This is done in the following Theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that
v>0
and
\max_{k\in V}\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq L
as in Theorem
2.1
. Let
\rho=|\mathbb{E}[(S-\mathbb{E}[S])^{3}]|
.
Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 0.607148\frac{\rho}{v^{3}}+116.84\frac{(D+1)^{3}\mathcal{A}_{4}}{v^{4}}\\
+\max\left\{16.57\frac{L(D+1)}{v}\ ,\ 22.47\sqrt{\frac{(D+1)^{3}\mathcal{A}_{4}}{v^{4}}}+1.596\frac{\rho}{v^{3}}\right\}\ .

(2.5)

If the bound of Theorem
2.1
is smaller than
1
, then the right-hand side of (
2.5
) is smaller than that of Theorem
2.1
, multiplied by
1.06164
. When
c_{k}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]
for every
k\in V
, this multiplicative constant is instead
0.85771
.

We expect the bound of Theorem
2.4
to always be better than that of Theorem
2.1
, but were only able to establish this up to a multiplicative constant of
1.06164
.

We finally treat the case where the random variables only need to have a finite third moment (meaning
\mathcal{A}_{3}<\infty
), or even just a finite
2+\varepsilon
-th moment for some
\varepsilon>0
(meaning
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta>2
).

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume
v>0
and
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta\in[3,\infty)
. Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq\max\left\{18.96\,\xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}\ ,\ \frac{227.5}{\xi_{\delta}^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\right\}\ .

(2.6)

Theorem 2.6
.

Assume
v>0
and
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta\in(2,3)
.
Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 8.015\ \xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}.

(2.7)

Remark 2.7
.

We conjecture that the bounds of Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
are not optimal, see Conjecture
1.3
. Consequently, we have done little to optimize the constants in the theorems above. We are confident that many of the techniques used to improve the bounds of the i.i.d. Berry–Esseen theorem (see e.g.
[
She13a
]
) can be adapted to our setting.

Remark 2.8
.

The asymptotic behaviour of the bounds of Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
as
N\to\infty
is not always obvious, since
\xi_{\delta}
implicitely depends on
N
. For the same reason, it is not always clear which
\delta
gives the best bound.

As noted after Definition
1.1
,
\xi_{\delta}\in[0,1]
and
\xi_{\delta}
decreases with
\delta
. This means that the terms
\xi_{\delta}^{-\delta/(\delta+1)}
and
\xi_{\delta}^{-3}
become larger as
\delta\to\infty
. Compare this to Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, where only
\xi_{3}
and
\xi_{4}
are needed.
On the other hand, a larger
\delta
gives a better exponent on
((D+1)/N)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}
.

If the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are identically distributed and
\frac{\mathbb{V}[S]}{N(D+1)}
is fixed, then
\xi_{\delta}
does not depend on
N
, and only the term
(D+1)/N
matters. In this case, fixing
\delta
as large as possible gives the best asymptotic bounds as
N/(D+1)\to\infty
— it is useful to note that the maximum in (
2.6
) equals the first term when
N/(D+1)
is large enough.

The situation is different when the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not identically distributed. Let us highlight that the definition of
\xi_{\delta}
involves

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}],

the average
L^{\delta}
norm of the
(Y_{k}-c_{k})_{k\in V}
. This is significantly better than a more naive bound using
\sup_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}]
instead of
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}/N
, particularly in the case where the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not identically distributed.

Let us give an example where taking
\delta
as large as possible is not the best decision. Consider independent
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
with
Y_{k}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1/k)
and
c_{k}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]=1/k
for every
k\geq 1
. Then
\sup_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}]\geq\mathbb{E}[|Y_{2}-1/2|^{\delta}]=2^{-\delta}
, but

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}=\frac{\ln N}{N}(1+o_{N}(1))=o_{N}(1)\quad,\quad\mathbb{V}[S]=\ln N(1+o_{N}(1))\quad,\quad\xi_{\delta}=\left(\frac{\ln N}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta}}(1+o_{N}(1)).

In this case, Theorem
2.5
gives

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\lesssim\max\left\{(\ln N)^{-\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}\ ,\ \frac{N^{1-\frac{3}{\delta}}}{(\ln N)^{\frac{3(\delta-2)}{2\delta}}}\right\}.

The bound is non-trivial only when
\delta=3
, in which case
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\lesssim(\ln N)^{-1/8}
. On the other hand, the bound of Theorem
2.6
becomes

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\lesssim(\ln N)^{-\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}.

The better bound is attained for
\delta=3
, and is then identical to that of Theorem
2.5
.

Remark 2.9
.

The best bound in the example in the previous remark is given by Theorem
2.1
:

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\lesssim(\ln N)^{-1/2}.

In fact, for most applications, Theorem
2.1
gives better bounds than Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
if it can be applied.

3
Comparison to the state-of-the-art

Our approach, which is close to that of the first proof of Berry–Esseen, and of the state-of-the art in the i.i.d. case, centers around bounding the Fourier transform (or characteristic function)
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi W}]
of
W
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi X_{n}}]
converges pointwise (i.e. for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
) towards
\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}/2}
as
n\to\infty
if and only if
X_{n}
converges in distribution towards
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Hence, it does not come as a surprise that any uniform bound on the difference between the Fourier transform of
W
and that of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
on a neighborhood of
0
translates to a bound on
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))
, see Section
6.1
. We can in fact derive much more than a Berry–Esseen-type bound from a good enough control on the Fourier transform of
W
, which is the main appeal of this method. Its drawback is that it is hard to control the Fourier transform of
W
when the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not independent.

An alternative approach was pioneered by Stein
[
Ste72a
]
and met resounding success thanks to its polyvalence. Stein’s method works with distances of the form

\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{F}}(W,G)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{E}[f(W)]-\mathbb{E}[f(G)]|

for
\mathcal{F}
some class of functions that is large enough for the above expression to indeed define a distance: for example, the set of all
1
-Lipschitz functions defines the Wasserstein-1 distance
W_{1}
, and the set of all
\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,t]}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
defines the Kolmogorov distance. The right-hand side is then re-written by introducing well-chosen couplings, then bounded, typically by Taylor expansion and using a Lipschitz property for
f
and its derivative. As an example,
[
Ros11a
, Theorem 3.5]
or
[
BR89a
]
yields, for
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
having a dependency graph of maximum degree
D
,

W_{1}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq\frac{D^{2}}{\mathbb{V}[S]^{3/2}}\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}|^{3}]+\sqrt{\frac{26}{\pi}}\frac{D^{3/2}}{\mathbb{V}[S]}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}^{4}]}.

(3.1)

Bounds in the Kolmogorov distance are trickier to obtain, crucially because the functions
\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,t]}
have a discontinuity. An easy, but suboptimal bound is derived from (
3.1
) together with
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq 2\sqrt{CW_{1}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))}
where
C=1/\sqrt{2\pi}
is the maximum of the density of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Such a bound is found in
[
Pen03a
]
:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Pen03a
, Theorem 2.4]
)
.

Let
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
be a finite family of real
L^{4}
-random variables admitting a dependency graph with maximal degree
D\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, if
\mathbb{V}[S]>0
,

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 6\frac{(D+1)}{\mathbb{V}[S]^{\frac{3}{4}}}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]\right|^{3}]}+6\frac{(D+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathbb{V}[S]}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]\right|^{4}]}.

Surprisingly, it is better in some cases than
[
CS04a
, Theorem 2.7]
, which is proven using the slightly more general framework of local dependence:

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
\delta\in(2,3]
.
Let
V
be a set of cardinality
N
and
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
be a family of random variables that admit a dependency graph of maximum degree
D
, such that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]=0
and
\|Y_{k}\|_{\delta}\leq\theta<\infty
for every
k\in V
. Then

d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq 75N(D+1)^{5(\delta-1)}\theta^{\delta}=75\sigma_{\delta}^{-\delta}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2}}(D+1)^{4\delta-4},

where
\sigma_{\delta}
is as in Remark
2.2
with
c_{k}=0
for every
k\in V
.

The dependence in
N
is optimal (i.e. just as good as in the classical Berry–Esseen inequality, Theorem
1.2
). However, the exponent of
D
is significantly worse, and there are regimes (namely
D
large) where our Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
do significantly better than Theorem
3.2
. In addition,
\sigma_{\delta}
is used instead of
\xi_{\delta}
, which is worse since we always have

. Nevertheless, for

constitutes, as far as we know, the state-of-the-art for Berry–Esseen-type bounds in the context of dependency graphs.

The former state-of-the-art using the Fourier transform method, using a similar starting point as our results, was as follows. We strictly improve this result.

Theorem 3.3

(
[

, Theorem 39]
)
.

Let
(Y_{n,k})_{1\leq k\leq n}

for some

, where, for every

(3.3)

4
Optimality

In the previous section, we based our comparison on the example where the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
were grouped into cliques of
D+1
of r.v. with maximal correlation inside the cliques and independence between cliques. This is the case where we expect
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))
to be largest. This example can thus give us insight on how close to optimal our bound is. In the i.i.d. case, the known optimality of the Berry–Esseen bound ensures that for every
C_{-}<\frac{\sqrt{10}+3}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx 0.4097
(we fix one for the rest of this section), there exist bounded i.i.d. random variables
(Z_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with unit variance and zero expectation such that (cf.
[
Ess56a
]
)

\displaystyle d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{Z_{0}+\dots+Z_{n-1}}{\sqrt{n}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)>C_{-}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|Z_{0}|^{3}]}{\mathbb{V}[Z_{0}]^{3/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

(4.1)

As before,
consider the family
(Y_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
, where
N=n(D+1)
, and for every
0\leq k\leq n-1
,

Y_{k(D+1)+1}=Y_{k(D+1)+2}=\dots=Y_{k(D+1)+D+1}=Z_{k}.

Then

S=(D+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}Z_{k},

with
\mathbb{V}[(D+1)Y_{1}]=(D+1)^{2}\mathbb{V}[Z_{0}]
and
\mathbb{E}[|(D+1)Y_{1}|^{3}]=(D+1)^{3}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{0}|^{3}]
. Applying the “reverse Berry–Esseen bound” (
4.1
) to
S
gives

\displaystyle d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)>C_{-}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|Z_{0}|^{3}]}{\mathbb{V}[Z_{0}]^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\ .

On the other hand, Theorem
2.1
gives that there exists a universal constant
C^{\prime}
such that

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\max\left\{\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left|Z_{0}\right|^{3}]}{\mathbb{V}[Z_{0}]^{\frac{3}{2}}},\frac{\|Z_{0}\|_{\infty}}{\mathbb{V}[Z_{0}]}\right\}.

Theorem
2.1
thus gives a matching bound up to a multiplicative constant, meaning that it is, in its full generality, almost optimal.

More generally, for
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
with
\max_{k\in V}\|Y_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq L
, as long as
\mathbb{V}[S]\geq\mathfrak{c}(D+1)N
for some
\mathfrak{c}>0
independent of
N
and
D
,
Theorem
2.1
implies

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{A}_{3}}{N},L\right\}.

This is as good as (
1.2
) up to multiplicative constant when substituting
1/N
by
(D+1)/N
, which we expect to be the correct surrogate. There is not much room for improvement. However, in Theorem
2.5
and
2.6
where the
Y_{k}
are not uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}
, we expect that our exponents can be improved upon, see Conjecture
1.3
.
Since the exponents of
N
and
D
play a much larger role for the quality of the bound than the numerical constant in front of them, it is thus of little interest to find the best possible numerical constant.

We can now explain Conjecture
1.3
. The optimal exponent of
N
is already known. Indeed, Theorem
3.2
ensures that, for
D
fixed, the exponent of
N
is that of the Berry–Esseen inequality for independent random variables. The exponent of
D
in Theorem
3.2
is however not optimal, as is demonstrated by the comparison in Section
3
. To obtain the Conjecture, we thus use the heuristic of substituting
N
with
(D+1)/N
. This heuristic is underpinned by the following two observations: The relevant quantity in the Theorems of Section
2
is
(D+1)/N
instead of
N
; and the example above gives the same results as the i.i.d. case with
N
replaced by
(D+1)/N
.

Remark 4.1
.

In the independent case, Theorem
2.5
gives as Corollary a result which is similar to the classical Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem.
Indeed, recall that for a sequence
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent random variables, for which we assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]=0
for every
k
, and letting
S_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{k}
, the
Lyapunov condition

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}\right|^{\delta}]=0\quad\text{for some }\delta>2

(4.2)

implies
S_{N}/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in distribution as
n\to\infty
.
Assuming that there are some
0<a<A
such that
\|Y_{k}\|_{\delta}\in(a,A)
for every
k
, (
4.2
) is equivalent to

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{N^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}=0\ .

(4.3)

This is precisely the condition which ensures that the right-hand side of (
2.6
) converges to
0
as
N\to\infty
.
Indeed, the statement

\lim_{N\to\infty}\xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}=0

(4.4)

is equivalent to
\lim_{N\to\infty}\xi_{\delta}^{-\delta}N^{\frac{2-\delta}{2}}=0
, which for
D=0
reduces to
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}\frac{N}{\mathbb{V}[S]^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}=0
. By assumption,
\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}\in[a^{\delta},A^{\delta}]
, so that (
4.3
) implies (
4.4
). Similarly (details left to the interested reader), one can show that (
4.3
) implies
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\xi_{3}^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{D+1}{N}}=0
.

Remark 4.2
.

The power for
\xi_{\delta}
in (
2.6
) is optimal in the sense that for any
\delta\geq 2
, multiplying the right-hand-side of (
2.6
) by
g(\xi_{\delta})
for any function
g
that converges to
0
as
\xi_{\delta}\to 0
makes the inequality wrong. It does not mean that the right-hand side of (
2.6
) is optimal, but that a different exponent on
N
and on
D
would be required in order to improve the bound.
To see that the exponent of
\xi_{\delta}
is optimal, let
\delta\geq 3
and let
(Y_{k})_{k\geq 2}
be an independent sequence of random variables such that

\mathbb{P}[Y_{k}=k^{\frac{1}{\delta}}]=\mathbb{P}[Y_{k}=-k^{\frac{1}{\delta}}]=\frac{1-\mathbb{P}[Y_{k}=0]}{2}=\frac{k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-(k-1)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{2k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}.

By the Mean Value Theorem, for every
k\geq 2
, there exists
\zeta(k)\in[k-1,k]
such that
k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-(k-1)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}=\frac{2}{\delta}\zeta(k)^{\frac{2}{\delta}-1}
. In particular,
k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-(k-1)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\leq k^{\frac{2}{\delta}-1}
.
Then
\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}\right|^{\delta}]=k^{1-\frac{2}{\delta}}(k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-(k-1)^{\frac{2}{\delta}})\leq 1
and
\mathbb{V}[Y_{k}]=k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-(k-1)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}
. In particular,
\mathbb{E}[S_{N}]=0
and
\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]=N^{\frac{2}{\delta}}
for every
N\geq 2
.

We claim that
(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}])/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}
does not converge in distribution to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
.
To prove this, note that the Feller condition
\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{V}[Y_{k}]/\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]=0
is satisfied, because
\mathbb{V}[Y_{k}]
is uniformly bounded in
k
while
\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. On the other hand, the Lindeberg condition

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}^{2}[Y_{k}^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]]]=0\quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0

(4.5)

is not satisfied. Indeed, pick
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
. Then
Y_{k}^{2}[Y_{k}^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]]\neq 0\iff k^{\frac{2}{\delta}}>\frac{N^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{2}\iff k>N2^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}
. Let
N_{\dagger}
denote the smallest integer strictly greater than
N2^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}
. Then

\frac{1}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}^{2}[Y_{k}^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]]]=N^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}\left(N^{\frac{2}{\delta}}-N_{\dagger}^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)=1-\left(\frac{N_{\dagger}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}.

But
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{N_{\dagger}}{N}=2^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}
and therefore (
4.5
) cannot be satisfied. By the Lindeberg Central Limit Theorem, if the Feller condition is satisfied, then
(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}])/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in distribution as
N\to\infty
if and only if the Lindeberg condition is satisfied. We conclude that
d_{\text{Kol}}((S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}])/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]},\mathcal{N}(0,1))\not\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

To see why the exponent of
\xi_{\delta}
in (
2.6
) is optimal, observe that we can apply Theorem
2.5
with
D=0
,
A=2
, and
\xi_{\delta}=\mathrm{const}\cdot N^{\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}}
. The right-hand side of (
2.6
) is thus a positive constant: we cannot multiply the right-hand side of (
2.6
) by any
g(\xi_{\delta})
with
g(\xi_{\delta})\to 0
as
\xi_{\delta}\to 0
, because it would contradict the previous observation.

The same example also shows that the power of
\xi_{\delta}
in (
2.7
) is optimal.

5
Applications

5.1
Central Limit Theorems and Weak Laws of Large Numbers

To further illustrate our results, in this section we prove some Central Limit Theorems and Weak Laws of Large Numbers that follow from our results.

Corollary 5.1
.

Consider a triangular array
(Y_{N,k})_{N\in\mathbb{N},k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
such that for every
N
,

(Y_{N,k})_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
has a dependency graph of maximum degree
D(N)
. Fix a real-valued family
(c_{N,k})_{N\in\mathbb{N},k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
. Define
S_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{N,k}
,
v_{N}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}
,
W_{N}=(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}])/v_{N}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{N,k}-c_{N,k}|^{\delta}]
and

\xi_{\delta}(N)=\left(\frac{N}{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}{N(D(N)+1)}}\ .

Assume that
v(N)>0
for every
N
large enough. If

\delta\in(2,3)\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{\xi_{\delta}(N)}\left(\frac{D(N)+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta}}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

(5.1)

or

\delta\in[3,\infty)\quad\text{ and }\quad\xi_{\delta}(N)^{-1}\left(\frac{D(N)+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta}}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\text{ and }\quad\xi_{\delta}(N)^{-3}\sqrt{\frac{D(N)+1}{N}}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

(5.2)

or

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|Y_{N,k}-c_{N,k}\|_{\infty}<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{D(N)+1}{v_{N}}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{N(D(N)+1)^{2}}{v_{N}^{3}}\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

(5.3)

then

W_{N}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(5.4)

in distribution as
N\to\infty
.

In particular, we also obtain the Weak Law of Large Numbers, i.e.

\frac{S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}]}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}\to 0

in probability as
N\to\infty
whenever either (
5.3
) holds, or
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)>0
and either (
5.1
) or (
5.2
) holds.

Proof of Corollary
5.1
.

(
5.1
), (
5.2
) or (
5.3
) ensures that the right-hand side of Theorem
2.6
,
2.5
or
2.1
converges to
0
as
N\to\infty
, which proves (
5.4
).

The first step towards the Weak Law of Large Numbers is to check that
v_{N}\to\infty
. This is implied by the last condition of (
5.3
). In the other cases,

\frac{1}{\xi_{\delta}(N)}\left(\frac{D(N)+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta}}=\frac{1}{v_{N}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}N^{\frac{1}{\delta}}(D(N)+1)^{1-\frac{1}{\delta}}\geq\frac{1}{v_{N}}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}.

thus
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)^{1/\delta}/v_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
under (
5.1
) or (
5.2
).
Together with
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(N)>0
, it implies
v_{N}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.
By Slutzky’s Theorem together with (
5.4
) we have convergence in distribution of
\frac{S_{N}-\mathbb{E}[S_{N}]}{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}
to
0
. We conclude by observing that convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Since
\xi_{\delta}(N)\leq 1
for every
\delta\in(2,\infty)
, conditions
5.1
and
5.2
imply in particular
\frac{D(N)+1}{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Furthermore,
v_{N}^{2}\leq N(D(N)+1)(\sup_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\|Y_{N,k}-c_{N,k}\|_{\infty})^{2}
, so that condition
5.3
also implies
\frac{D(N)+1}{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Remark 5.3
.

To illustrate the sharpness of condition
5.3
, consider for example
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
i.i.d. with

\mathbb{P}[X_{0}=1]=\mathbb{P}[X_{0}=-1]=\frac{1}{2}.

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}]=0,\mathbb{V}[X_{0}]=1
.
Fix a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
(for example
f(N)=\lfloor N^{2/3}\rfloor
) such that
f(N)\leq N
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{N}{f(N)^{2}}=0.

Consider the triangular array of random variables
(Y_{N,k})_{N\in\mathbb{N},k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
given by
Y_{N,k}=X_{0}
if
k\leq f(N)
and
Y_{N,k}=X_{k-f(N)}
if
k>f(N)
.
For every fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
(Y_{N,k})_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
thus admits a dependency graph such that
D+1=f(N)
. Furthermore,
\sup_{N,k}\|Y_{N,k}\|_{L^{\infty}}=1<\infty
.
Let
S_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{N,k}
. The variance of
S_{N}
is

\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]=f(N)^{2}\mathbb{V}[X_{0}]+(N-f(N))\mathbb{V}[X_{1}]=f(N)^{2}+N-f(N).

By the classical Central Limit Theorem,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-f(N)}}\sum_{k=f(N)+1}^{N}Y_{N,k}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ in distribution as }N\to\infty.

Since
f(N)^{2}/N\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{f(N)^{2}+N-f(N)}}\sum_{k=f(N)+1}^{N}Y_{N,k}\to 0\text{ in distribution as }N\to\infty.

Furthermore,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{f(N)^{2}+N-f(N)}}\sum_{k=1}^{f(N)}Y_{N,k}=\frac{f(N)}{\sqrt{f(N)^{2}+N-f(N)}}X_{0}\to X_{0}\text{ almost surely as }N\to\infty.

Hence,

\frac{S_{N}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S_{N}]}}\to X_{0}\text{ in distribution as }N\to\infty,

but
X_{0}
is not
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
distributed. The reason that Corollary
5.1
doesn’t apply here is that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{D+1}{v}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{\sqrt{f(N)^{2}+N-f(N)}}=1\neq 0
.

5.2
Application to
U
-statistics

Throughout the rest of the text, for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every set
A
, we write
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\mathbb{Z}\cap[a,b]
and
\binom{A}{k}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\{B\subset A:\left|B\right|=k\}
.

We apply Theorems
2.1
,
2.5
and
2.6
to
U
-statistics
, a class of statistics that is especially important in estimation theory.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a measurable space,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of
\mathcal{S}
-valued random variables and
f:\mathcal{S}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
a measurable function. The
U
-statistics
of
f
are defined, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as

U_{n}=U_{n}(f)=U_{n}(f,(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\frac{1}{\ell!\binom{n}{l}}\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}f(X_{\alpha}),

(5.5)

where

\Lambda_{n,\ell}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\left\{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{\ell}:\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\}\in\binom{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}{\ell}\right\}

and

X_{\alpha}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}(X_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,X_{\alpha_{\ell}})\qquad\text{ for }\qquad\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}.

Examples
.

1.

If
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R},\ell=1
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto x
, the U-statistics are the
sample means

U_{n}=\frac{X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}}{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\bar{X}_{n}.

(5.6)

2.

If
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R},\ell=2
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},(x,y)\mapsto(x-y)^{2}/2
, the U-statistics are the
sample variances

\begin{split}U_{n}&=\frac{1}{4\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(X_{k}-\bar{X}_{n})^{2}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\hat{\Sigma}_{n}.\end{split}

(5.7)

When using U-statistics as estimators, a question of importance is whether they are consistent, and if they are, at what speed they converge to the quantity that they estimate. A satisfying answer to this question is usually given in the form of a Central Limit Theorem.
Janson
[
Jan21e
]
proved a Central Limit Theorem when the
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are
m
-dependent
:

Definition 5.5
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{Z}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. A family of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in V}
is called
m
-dependent
if and only if, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
(X_{n})_{n\leq k}
and
(X_{n})_{n\geq k+m+1}
are independent.

Independence is equivalent to
0
-dependence. A
m
-dependent family of random variables always admits a dependency graph with maximum degree at most
2m
, namely the graph in which
i,j\in V
are connected if and only if
1\leq\left|i-j\right|\leq m
.
This graph has maximal degree less or equal than
2m
. On the other hand, if
(X_{n})_{n\in V}
, with
V\subset\mathbb{N}
finite, is a family of random variables admitting a dependency graph of maximal degree less or equal than
2m
, there need not exist a permutation
\sigma:V\to V
such that
(X_{\sigma(n)})_{n\in V}
is
m
-dependent.

Theorem 5.6

(
[
Jan21e
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a stationary sequence of
m
-dependent random variables with values in a measurable space
\mathcal{S}
. Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f:\mathcal{S}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

f(X_{\alpha})\in L^{2}\quad\text{ for every }\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}.

Let
V_{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}l!\binom{n}{l}U_{n}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}f(X_{\alpha})
denote the
“non-normalized U-statistic”
. Then there exists a
\sigma^{2}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{V}[V_{n}]}{n^{2l-1}}=\sigma^{2},

(5.8)

and

\frac{V_{n}-\mathbb{E}[V_{n}]}{n^{l-\frac{1}{2}}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\quad\text{in distribution as }n\to\infty.

(5.9)

Remark 5.7
.

Using that
\frac{\Gamma(x+1)x^{\varepsilon}}{\Gamma(x+1+\varepsilon)}=1+O(1/x)
as
x\to\infty
for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, cf.
[
GR14a
, 8.328, 2.]
,

\frac{\mathbb{V}[V_{n}]}{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}=\left(\frac{n!}{(n-l)!}\right)^{2}=n^{2l}(1+O(1/n))

as
n\to\infty
. Therefore,
(
5.8
)
is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]=\sigma^{2}.

and (
5.9
)
is equivalent to

\frac{U_{n}-\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad\text{in distribution as }n\to\infty.

Under additional assumptions, we can supplement Theorem
5.6
with an estimate of the speed at which
(V_{n}-\mathbb{E}[V_{n}])/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[V_{n}]}
converges towards
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The idea, which is used both in
[
Jan21e
, Section 9]
and in Corollary
5.10
below, relies on writing the U-statistic as a sum of the r.v.
(f(X_{\alpha}))_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}
, which have an explicit dependency graph. Every Berry–Esseen-type estimate on r.v. with a dependency graph then directly yields an estimate on the speed of convergence of the U-statistic.

Let us describe the dependency graph. For generality we consider
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with a dependency graph of maximum degree
m
— this includes the case of
m
-dependence. Consider the graph on the vertex set
V=\Lambda_{n,\ell}
such that
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}),\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\ell})\in V
are connected if and only if there exist
i,j\in\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket
such that either
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{j}
, or
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
are connected in the dependency graph of the
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. This graph has cardinality
N\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\left|V\right|=\binom{n}{l}\ell!=\frac{n!}{(n-\ell)!}
and maximal degree

D\leq\ell^{2}(m+1)\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!-1=\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}N-1.

(5.10)

Indeed, for any fixed
\alpha
, to construct any adjacent
\beta
we first need to choose at least one pair of coordinates
(i,k)\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}^{2}
and
\beta_{k}
such that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{k}
are connected in the dependency graph of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
or
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{k}
. For this, there are at most
\ell^{2}(m+1)
possibilities. Then one has to choose the other
\ell-1
coordinates, for which there are
\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!
possibilities, since order matters. Finally one has to subtract
1
because
\alpha
is not adjacent to itself.

This idea applies in more general situations than considered in
[
Jan21e
]
. Constrained, resp. exactly constrained U-statistics
[
Jan21e
, Section 3]
differ only in the set of all
\alpha
such that
f(X_{\alpha})
appears in
U_{n}
, as well as in the dependency graph that they induce on the family
(f(X_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
. Furthermore, we need not assume that the
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be
m
-dependent as in
[
Jan21e
]
: it suffices that they have a dependency graph of maximal degree
2m
. We also do not assume that the
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are stationary — this is used by Janson to determine the variance of the U-statistic, something that we do not consider. Finally, it is possible to consider the case where
m
depends on
n
, whereas
m
is fixed in
[
Jan21e
]
.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
(X_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
be random variables with values in a measurable space
\mathcal{S}
that admit a dependency graph of maximal degree
m
. Let
(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}
be an arbitrary family of real numbers,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f:\mathcal{S}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
measurable such that
\max_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}\|f(X_{\alpha})-c_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}L\in(0,\infty)
.
Recall
U_{n}
defined by (
5.5
) and
V_{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}l!\binom{n}{l}U_{n}
as in Theorem
5.6
.
Define furthermore
\mathbf{a}_{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}/\left|\Lambda_{n,\ell}\right|\right)^{1/\delta}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(n-\ell)!/n!\right)^{1/\delta}
.
Then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{U_{n}-\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 227.5\sqrt{\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}}\Xi_{\infty}^{-3},

(5.11)

where

\Xi_{\infty}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{1}{L}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[V_{n}]}{\ell^{2}(m+1)n^{2l-1}}}\in(0,1].

The proof is a straightforward application of Theorem
2.1
. We refer the reader to the proof of Corollary
5.10
which follows the same structure.

5.8
is similar to
[
Jan21e
, Theorem 3.14]
.

Remark 5.9
.

Theorem

give trivial bounds when used on the family

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
(X_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
,
\ell
,
(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}
be as in Corollary
5.8
. Let
f:\mathcal{S}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
measurable such that
\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}\|f(X_{\alpha})-c_{\alpha}\|_{\delta}^{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\in(0,\infty)
for some

U_{n}
and

be as in Corollary
5.8
.
Define furthermore
\mathbf{a}_{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}/\left|\Lambda_{n,\ell}\right|\right)^{1/\delta}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(n-\ell)!/n!\right)^{1/\delta}
.

Then if
\delta\in(2,3)
and

,

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{U_{n}-\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 8.015\left(\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}\Xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}},

(5.12)

where

\Xi_{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{1}{\mathbf{a}_{\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[V_{n}]}{\ell^{2}(m+1)n^{2l-1}}}\in(0,1]

(5.13)

If instead
\delta\in[3,\infty)
and

, then, using the same notation as in (

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{U_{n}-\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\\
\leq\max\left\{18.96\ \left(\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\delta-2}{2(\delta+1)}}\Xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}},227.5\ \sqrt{\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}}\Xi_{\delta}^{-3}\right\}.

(5.14)

Proof.

Recall that the r.v.
(f(X_{\alpha}))_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{n,\ell}}
have a dependency graph with cardinality

and maximal degree

.
Therefore, since

,

N(D+1)\leq\frac{\ell^{2}(m+1)}{n}N^{2}\leq\ell^{2}(m+1)n^{2l-1}.

(5.15)

If

, Theorem
2.6
gives

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{U_{n}-\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[U_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 8.015\left(\xi_{\delta}\left(\frac{N}{D+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{\delta+1}},

5.3
Application to estimating stock volatility

Let
(\mathscr{R}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process and
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
an unbounded, strictly increasing sequence of (deterministic) times with
t_{0}=0
. Define
\kappa_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
and
X_{k}=\mathscr{R}_{t_{k}}-\mathscr{R}_{t_{k-1}}
for every
k\geq 1
. We assume that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=\mathfrak{e}\kappa_{k}
and
\mathbb{V}[X_{k}]=\nu\kappa_{k}
.

We can estimate
\mathfrak{e}
and
\nu
by weighted linear regression. The least square estimators, which are the maximum likelihood estimators assuming that the
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are independent and normally distributed, are

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\qquad,\qquad\hat{\nu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{X_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{k}}-\frac{t_{n}}{n}\hat{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}^{2}\ .

\hat{\nu}_{n}
can be made unbiaised by replacing
n
by
n-1
in the denominator of the fractions.

Example 5.12
.

In the Black–Scholes model, a stock price is given by
\exp(\mathscr{R}_{t})
where
(\mathscr{R}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion with variance
\nu
and drift
\mathfrak{e}
. The family
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
then consists in returns computed between unevenly spaced epochs.
In practice,
\hat{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
converges too slowly, so less direct methods, like the Capital Asset Pricing Model, are used. Furthermore, we are mainly interested in
\nu
, since
\mathfrak{e}
plays no role when pricing options.

In a simple model, the
X_{k}
are i.i.d., and we can establish a Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem for
\hat{\nu}_{n}
under the condition that
X_{k}\in L^{4}
together with some control on
\kappa_{k}
and the moments of
X_{k}
.
For more realism, the i.i.d. assumption needs to be relaxed. If we keep the independence property, a common approach (GARCH model, the RiskMetrics variance model) is to have the variance of the
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
evolve stochastically with time, with the
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
being independent conditionally on the variance process. If we instead relax the independence assumption, it is still possible to obtain a Strong Law of Large Numbers if one assumes that the
X_{k}
are merely pairwise independent, using
[
Jan21c
, Corollary 2.1 and Remark 3]
.

We use our results to study the case where each
X_{k}
is allowed to depend on a small number of other
X_{j}
. For simplicity, we make the unrealistic assumption that
\mathbb{V}[X_{k}]=\nu\kappa_{k}
, precisely the assumption that the RiskMetrics and GARCH models relax. This means that our estimations are only valid on short timescales where the returns can be assumed to be homoscedastic. A strong point of Corollary
5.13
is that it gives an upper bound on the error of the least square estimator when the number of samples is limited.

Corollary 5.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu\in(0,\infty)
,
\delta>4
,
(t_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
strictly increasing with
t_{0}=0
and define
\kappa_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
for every
1\leq k\leq n
. We consider a family
(X_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
with a dependency graph of maximal degree
m
such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=0
,
\mathbb{V}[X_{k}]=\nu\kappa_{k}
and
X_{k}\in L^{\delta}
for every
k
.
Write

\mathcal{T}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{2^{\frac{\delta-1}{2}}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\kappa_{i}^{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t_{n}}{n}\right)^{\delta}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{X_{i}}{\kappa_{i}}\right|^{\delta}\right]

and assume that

\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]\geq\frac{\mathcal{K}^{2}}{n}

for some
\mathcal{K}>0
.
Then, if
\delta\in(4,6)
, we have for
\tilde{\delta}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{\delta}{2}
,

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 8.015\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{K}t_{n}}\right)^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}\mathcal{T}^{\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}(4(m+1))^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}-1}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}n^{-\frac{\tilde{\delta}-2}{2(\tilde{\delta}+1)}}.

(5.16)

If instead
\delta\in[6,\infty)
, then

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq\max\bigg\{18.96\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{K}t_{n}}\right)^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}\mathcal{T}^{\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}(4(m+1))^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}-1}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}n^{-\frac{\tilde{\delta}-2}{2(\tilde{\delta}+1)}},\\
227.5\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{K}t_{n}}\right)^{3}\mathcal{T}^{\frac{3}{\tilde{\delta}}}\frac{(4(m+1))^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg\}.

Assume
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{T}<\infty
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}n/t_{n}<\infty
. Then if
\delta\in(4,6)
and
m+1=o(n^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}-2}{2\tilde{\delta}-2}})
, or
\delta\in[6,\infty[
and
m+1=o(n^{\frac{1}{4}})
,

\frac{\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)

in distribution as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 5.14
.

Note that
\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]\geq\frac{\mathcal{K}^{2}}{n}
for some
\mathcal{K}>0
is true if
t_{n}=n
and the
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. in
L^{4}
with
\mathbb{V}[X_{1}]>0
. Indeed, in that case
\hat{\nu}_{n}
is simply the usual variance estimator
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}
, and the representation
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}=\frac{1}{2\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset\binom{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}{2}}(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}
gives

\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]=\mathbb{V}[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}])^{4}]}{n}-\frac{\mathbb{V}[X_{1}]^{2}(n-3)}{n(n-1)}\geq\frac{\mathcal{K}^{2}}{n}

for some
\mathcal{K}>0
depending only on
X_{1}
.

Remark 5.15
.

Even if the
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
were independent, we would need
X_{k}
to be in
L^{4}
to establish convergence of
\hat{\nu}_{n}
to a normal distribution, and in order to obtain Berry–Esseen estimates on the speed of convergence one would need finite
\delta
-th moments for some
\delta>4
. Corollary
5.13
matches these requirements.

Proof of Corollary
5.13
.

Note that

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{n}=\frac{1}{nt_{n}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}}{\kappa_{i}}\left(\frac{t_{n}}{n}X_{i}-\kappa_{i}X_{j}\right).

Write
Y_{i,j}=\frac{X_{i}}{\kappa_{i}}\left(\frac{t_{n}}{n}X_{i}-\kappa_{i}X_{j}\right)
.
From the Cauchy-Schwarz and AM-GM inequalities, for every
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{i,j}\right|^{\frac{\delta}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\kappa_{i}^{\delta}}\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{i}\right|^{\delta}]\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{t_{n}}{n}X_{i}-\kappa_{i}X_{j}\right|^{\delta}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2^{\delta-1}}{\kappa_{i}^{\delta}}\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{i}\right|^{\delta}]\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{t_{n}}{n}X_{i}\right|^{\delta}+\left|\kappa_{i}X_{j}\right|^{\delta}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{\delta-3}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{i}\right|^{\delta}]+\left(\frac{t_{n}}{n\kappa_{i}}\right)^{\delta}\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{i}\right|^{\delta}]+\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{j}\right|^{\delta}]\right).

We therefore have

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{i,j}\right|^{\frac{\delta}{2}}\right]\leq n2^{\frac{\delta-1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\kappa_{i}^{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t_{n}}{n}\right)^{\delta}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{X_{i}}{\kappa_{i}}\right|^{\delta}\right]=n^{2}\mathcal{T}.

The graph on
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{2}
, in which two pairs
(i,j)
and
(k,\ell)
are connected if and only if
\{i,j\}\cap\{k,\ell\}\neq\emptyset
is a dependency graph for the random variables
(Y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. This graph has
N=n^{2}
vertices and maximal degree
D\leq 4(m+1)n-1
.

Assume first that
\delta\in(4,6)
. Then we can use Theorem
2.6
applied to the family
(Y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
:

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq 8.015\xi_{\delta}^{-\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\tilde{\delta}+1}}\left(\frac{D+1}{N}\right)^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}-2}{2(\tilde{\delta}+1)}},

(5.17)

where

\xi_{\tilde{\delta}}=\mathcal{T}^{-\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}\left[nt_{n}\hat{\nu}_{n}\right]}{N(D+1)}}\geq\mathcal{T}^{-\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}}}\frac{t_{n}}{2n}\sqrt{\frac{n\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]}{m+1}}\geq\mathcal{T}^{-\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}}}\frac{\mathcal{K}t_{n}}{2n\sqrt{m+1}}

(5.18)

and we used the assumption
\mathbb{V}[\hat{\nu}_{n}]\geq\frac{\mathcal{K}^{2}}{n}
in the last inequality.
Combining (
5.17
) with (
5.18
) gives the first part of the Theorem.
If
\delta\in[6,\infty)
, we proceed instead with Theorem
2.5
.
∎

6
Proof of the main results

6.1
Overview of the proofs

In this section, we present a proof of Theorems
2.1
to
2.6
. The two main methods to prove Berry–Esseen-type bounds for random variables are either Stein’s method (see e.g.
[
Pen03a
]
), or a range of mutually similar analytical methods relying on bounding the Fourier transform of the sum. The latter approach gives the current benchmark for i.i.d. random variables, and it is the one we use here. For a more detailed overview of related work, see section
3
.

The starting point of the proofs, established in Section
6.2
, is a bound of the cumulants of the sum
S
of
N\in\mathbb{N}
random variables
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
indexed by a set
V
with a dependency graph of maximum degree
D
. We establish this bound by an elementary refinement of a similar estimate from
[
FMN13a
]
.
Recall that the cumulants
(\kappa^{(r)}(X))_{r\geq 1}
of a bounded random variable
X
are such that for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\ln\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{zX}]=\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{\kappa^{(r)}(X)}{r!}z^{r}.

In particular,
\kappa^{(1)}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X],\kappa^{(2)}(X)=\mathbb{V}[X]
and
\kappa^{(3)}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^{3}]
; higher order cumulants can still be expressed in terms of the moments of
X
but with more complex formulas.

Assuming that the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are uniformly bounded by the same constant
L
, our bound looks as follows: For every
r\geq\delta>1
,

\kappa^{(r)}(S)\leq(2(D+1))^{r-1}r^{r-2}L^{r}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{L^{\delta}},

where
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}
is as in Definition
1.1
.
This bound is not optimal, but it is close enough to optimal for our purposes, as we justify with the following two observations.
Firstly, although the dependency in
N
and
D
may be improved upon on a case-by-case basis, it is the best one can achieve in general. Indeed, if we let
(X_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be i.i.d. and
Y_{(D+1)i+1}=Y_{(D+1)i+2}=\dots=Y_{(D+1)(i+1)}=X_{i}
for every
0\leq i<N/(D+1)
, then

\kappa^{(r)}(S)=(D+1)^{r}\kappa^{(r)}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{N}{D+1}-1}X_{i}\right)=N(D+1)^{r-1}\kappa^{(r)}(X_{1}).

Noting that
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}/L^{\delta}\leq N
gives the same dependency in
D
and
N
as our bound.
Secondly, if
\|X_{i}\|_{\infty}=L
then
\limsup_{r}(\kappa^{(r)}(X_{1})/r!)^{1/r}=L
. In comparison, our bounds gives
2\mathrm{e}L
. The factor
2\mathrm{e}
only worsens the final Berry–Esseen bounds by a constant factor; since we did not try to get the best possible constants it is of little impact.

In Section
6.3
, we translate our bound on the cumulants of
S
to a Taylor expansion-type bound on the Fourier transform of
S
in a neighborhood of zero, see Lemma
6.2
: for every
s
small enough and for some
C
that depends on
N
,
D
,
\mathcal{A}_{3}
,
\mathbb{V}[S]
and
L
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}sW}\right]-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\right|\leq Cs^{3}e^{Cs^{3}-\frac{s^{2}}{2}},

(6.1)

where we recall
W=(S-\mathbb{E}[S])/\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S]}
.
Fourier analysis is then used to derive a bound on
d_{\text{Kol}}(W,\mathcal{N}(0,1))
.
We use the following elementary inequality that implies the classical Berry–Esseen bound (albeit with a suboptimal constant), cf.
[
Fel71a
, Chapter XVI, (3.13), (5.4)]
: for every random variable
X
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and
\mathbb{V}[X]=1
and for every
T>0
,

d_{\text{Kol}}(X,\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}sX}\right]-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\right|\frac{1}{|s|}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{24}{T\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}.

(6.2)

One must then optimize over
T
. Indeed, the first term of the right-hand side of (
6.2
) increases with
T
, whereas the second term decreases with
T
.
This is the content of Section
6.4
and Section
6.5
, proving Theorems
2.1
and
2.4
respectively. The proof of these two Theorems are nearly identical, only differing in the estimate on the Fourier transform of
S
.

The case where the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not bounded requires some more work. The key idea is to re-use the estimates from the bounded case by “truncating” the
Y_{k}
. Fixing some
L>0
, and letting
Y^{(L)}_{k}=Y_{k}\mathds{1}_{|Y_{k}|\leq L}
(the actual formula is slightly different), we can apply our results to
S^{(L)}=\sum_{k}Y^{(L)}_{k}
. From there, a straightforward approach (used in
[
FMN17a
]
) is to bound the Kolmogorov distance between
S
and
S^{(L)}
, and to optimize over
L
to get the best possible bound. We choose a different approach: instead, we bound the difference between the Fourier transform of
S
and that of
S^{(L)}
in a neighborhood of zero, which gives an estimate reminiscent of (
6.1
) (see Lemma
6.4
), and then proceed with the same proof method as Theorem
2.1
. The cases
\delta\geq 3
and
\delta\in(2,3]
are handled separately, in Sections
6.6
and
6.7
respectively.

We tried to obtain the best constants that our proof methods could give, but only when it did not add too many technicalities. Better constants could be obtained by being more careful in the estimates that we use, or under additional hypotheses, or by using an alternative Fourier-transform-to-Kolmogorov-distance bound, like the one used in
[
She13a
]
(which gives the best Berry–Esseen bound for i.i.d. random variables as of 2012). A better bound on the cumulants of
S
would also naturally translate to a better Berry–Esseen estimate.

6.2
Cumulants of the sum

Recall the notations introduced in the introduction.
Fix some real-valued family
(c_{k})_{k\in V}
. For
L>0
and
k\in V
, define

Y^{(L)}_{k}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}(Y_{k}-c_{k})\mathds{1}_{\left|Y_{k}-c_{k}\right|\leq L}\ .

Recall

S\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sum_{k\in V}Y_{k}\qquad,\qquad v\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S]}\ ,

and define in a similar way

S^{(L)}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sum_{k\in V}(Y^{(L)}_{k}+c_{k})\qquad,\qquad v_{L}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[S^{(L)}]}\ .

Recall also
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}=\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}]
from Definition
1.1
, and
W=(S-\mathbb{E}[S])/v
.

We start by proving a bound on the cumulants of
S^{(L)}
.
Recall the definition of cumulants: for every real-valued random variable
Y
, if
\ln\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{cY}]
is well-defined for
c\in\mathbb{C}
in a neighborhood of zero,
then it is analytic:

\ln\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{cY}]=\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{\kappa^{(r)}(Y)}{r!}c^{r}

for some coefficients
\kappa^{(r)}(Y)
, called the
r
-th cumulants
of
Y
. In particular,
\kappa^{(1)}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]
and
\kappa^{(2)}(Y)=\mathbb{V}[Y]
. Furthermore, for every constant
c
we have
\kappa^{(r)}(Y+c)=\kappa^{(r)}(Y)
for every
r\neq 1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For every
\delta\in[1,\infty)
, for every
r\in[\delta,\infty)
with
r>1
and for every
L>0
,

\left|\kappa^{(r)}(S^{(L)})\right|\leq r^{r-2}(2(D+1))^{r-1}L^{r}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{L^{\delta}}.

(6.3)

This is an improvement of the bound (
6.4
) in
[
FMN13a
, Theorem 9.8]
, which fails to take advantage of the hypothesis
\delta>1
, so that
[
FMN13a
]
obtained only the special case of Lemma
6.1
where
\delta=1
:

\left|\kappa^{(r)}(S^{(L)})\right|\leq\left(\sum_{k\in V}\|Y_{k}\|_{1}\right)r^{r-2}(2(D+1))^{r-1}L^{r-1}\ .

(6.4)

Proof.

First note that the cumulants of
S^{(L)}
are well-defined because
|S^{(L)}-\sum_{k\in V}c_{k}|
is almost surely bounded by
NL
.
Consider
r\geq\delta
.
Let
\pi
be a partition of
\{1,2,\dots,r\}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
an element of
V^{r}
. Observe that every dependency graph of
(Y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in V}
is also a dependency graph of
(Y^{(L)}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in V}
.
Consider

\prod_{B\in\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in B}Y^{(L)}_{\alpha_{i}}\right].

This number is denoted by
M_{\pi}
in
[
FMN13a
, Section 9.4.1]
, and we keep this notation. Using the generalized Hölder inequality, for every
B\in\pi
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in B}Y^{(L)}_{\alpha_{i}}\right]\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in B}Y^{(L)}_{\alpha_{i}}\prod_{i\notin B}1\right]\right|\leq\left(\prod_{i\in B}\|Y^{(L)}_{\alpha_{i}}\|_{r}\right)\left(\prod_{i\notin B}\|1\|_{r}\right)=\prod_{i\in B}\|Y^{(L)}_{\alpha_{i}}\|_{r}\ .

By using the arithmetico-geometric inequality,

M_{\pi}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\|Y_{\alpha_{i}}^{(L)}\|_{r}\leq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{\alpha_{i}}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right].

Replacing the bound used in
[
FMN13a
, Section 9.4.2]
by the above, improved bound, and continuing with the same simplification as in their (47),

\displaystyle\left|\kappa^{(r)}\left(S^{(L)}-\sum_{k\in V}c_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r-1}\sum_{\alpha\in V^{r}}\frac{1}{r}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{\alpha_{i}}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right]\right)\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]},

(6.5)

where
\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]}
denotes the number of spanning trees in the graph
G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]
(for the Definition of
G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]
see
[
FMN13a
, Section 9.3.2]
).
The term
\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]}
is invariant by permutation of the
(\alpha_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
: therefore, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
,

\sum_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})\in V^{r}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{\alpha_{i}}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right]\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]}=\sum_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})\in V^{r}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{\alpha_{1}}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right]\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]}.

(6.6)

Furthermore, for any fixed
\alpha_{1}\in V
, the sum of
\mathrm{ST}_{G[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}]}
over
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})\in V^{r-1}
is bounded from above by
r^{r-2}(D+1)^{r-1}
by
[
FMN13a
, Corollary 9.17]
. Combining this with (
6.5
) and (
6.6
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\kappa^{(r)}\left(S^{(L)}-\sum_{k\in V}c_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq r^{r-2}(2(D+1))^{r-1}\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{k}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right]\ .

Since
|Y^{(L)}_{k}|\leq L
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{k}^{(L)}\right|^{r}\right]\leq L^{r-\delta}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{(L)}_{k}|^{\delta}]\leq L^{r-\delta}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}]
.
The Lemma then follows by the above observation that cumulants of order
r\geq 2
are not changed by additive constants.
∎

Next, we gather some useful estimates on
|\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{E}[S]|
and
|\mathbb{V}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{V}[S]|
.
By Hölder’s and Markov’s inequality, defining
Z^{(L)}_{k}=(Y_{k}-c_{k})\mathds{1}_{|Y_{k}-c_{k}|>L}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Z^{(L)}_{k}|]\leq\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\delta}\mathbb{P}(|Y_{k}-c_{k}|>L)^{1-1/\delta}\leq\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\delta}^{\delta}L^{1-\delta}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}[\left|S-S^{(L)}\right|]\leq\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[\left|Y_{k}-Y^{(L)}_{k}-c_{k}\right|]=\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{(L)}_{k}|]\leq L^{1-\delta}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}.

(6.7)

Next, since
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are independent (and thus uncorrelated) whenever there is no edge between
i
and
j
in the dependency graph, and since
Y_{k}=Y^{(L)}_{k}+Z^{(L)}_{k}+c_{k}
,

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbb{V}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{V}[S]\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i\sim j}\lvert\Cov(Y^{(L)}_{i},Z^{(L)}_{j})\rvert+\sum_{i\sim j}\lvert\Cov(Z^{(L)}_{i},Z^{(L)}_{j})\rvert

where the sum is over
i,j\in V
with
i\sim j
, and we write
i\sim j
when
i
and
j
are adjacent in the dependency graph of
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
or when
i=j
. For every
i,j\in V
,

\displaystyle\lvert\Cov(Y^{(L)}_{i},Z^{(L)}_{j})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Y^{(L)}_{i}\mathds{1}_{|Y_{j}-c_{j}|>L}]\mathbb{V}[Z^{(L)}_{j}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\left(Y^{(L)}_{i}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{|Y_{j}-c_{j}|>L}]\mathbb{E}[\left(Z^{(L)}_{j}\right)^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y^{(L)}_{i}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{|Y_{j}-c_{j}|>L}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z^{(L)}_{j}\right)^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|Y_{j}-c_{j}|^{\delta}]L^{2-\delta}

using the arithmetico-geometric inequality, the fact that
\mathbb{E}[(Z^{(L)}_{j})^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|Y_{j}-c_{j}|^{\delta}]L^{2-\delta}
by the same argument used above to bound
\mathbb{E}[|Z^{(L)}_{j}|]
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y^{(L)}_{i}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{|Y_{j}-c_{j}|>L}\right]\leq L^{2}\mathbb{P}(|Y_{j}-c_{j}|>L)\leq\mathbb{E}[|Y_{j}-c_{j}|^{\delta}]L^{2-\delta}.

Similarly,

\lvert\Cov(Z^{(L)}_{i},Z^{(L)}_{j})\rvert\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Z^{(L)}_{i}]\mathbb{V}[Z^{(L)}_{j}]}\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{V}[Z^{(L)}_{i}]+\mathbb{V}[Z^{(L)}_{j}]\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-c_{i}|^{\delta}]+\mathbb{E}[|Y_{j}-c_{j}|^{\delta}]\right)L^{2-\delta}.

We finally get

\lvert\mathbb{V}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{V}[S]\rvert\leq\sum_{k\in V}3L^{2-\delta}(\mathrm{deg}(k)+1)\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta}]\leq 3L^{2-\delta}(D+1)\mathcal{A}_{\delta}

(6.8)

where
\mathrm{deg}(k)
is the degree of
k
in the dependency graph of
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
.

6.3
Fourier transform of the sum

The core estimate is a bound that allows us to compare the Fourier transform of the sum
S
to that of a normally distributed random variable with same expectation and variance. Lemma
6.2
contains such a bound for the sum
S^{(L)}
of the random variables
(Y^{(L)}_{k})_{k\in V}
, which are obtained by “truncating” the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
, see the beginning of Section
6.2
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Write
\delta^{\prime}=\min(\delta,3)
. Let
L>0
such that
v_{L}>0
.
For every
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|\xi|\leq v_{L}/(2\mathrm{e}L(D+1))
, writing
K_{L}=C(D+1)^{2}(L/v_{L})^{3}\mathcal{A}_{\delta^{\prime}}/L^{\delta^{\prime}}
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\frac{S^{(L)}-\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}]}{v_{L}}}\right]\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}-1\right|\leq K_{L}|\xi|^{3}\mathrm{e}^{K_{L}|\xi|^{3}},

with

C\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}4\mathrm{e}^{3}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{r^{r-2}}{r!\mathrm{e}^{r}}\in[5.17,5.18].

(6.9)

This implies that
(S^{(L)}-\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}])/(N^{1/3}(D+1)^{2/3}v_{L})
has a “zone of control” of index
(3,3)
with respect to the reference law
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
(i.e.
\alpha=2,c=1/{\sqrt{2}}
)
in the sense of
[
FMN17a
, Definition 5]
.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\frac{S^{(L)}-\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}]}{v_{L}}}\right]\exp\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{r=3}^{\infty}\frac{\kappa^{(r)}(S^{(L)})}{r!}\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\xi}{v_{L}}\right)^{r}\right)\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\exp(z).

By the improved cumulant bound (

) with

\displaystyle\lvert z\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=3}^{\infty}r^{r-2}(2(D+1))^{r-1}L^{r}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta^{\prime}}}{L^{\delta^{\prime}}}\frac{\left|\xi\right|^{r}}{r!v_{L}^{r}}=Z\left(2\mathrm{e}(D+1)L\frac{\left|\xi\right|}{v_{L}}\right),

The claim follows from

∎

Lemma 6.4

For every

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\frac{S-\mathbb{E}[S]}{v}}\right]-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\right|\leq\underbrace{\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\frac{S-\mathbb{E}[S]}{v}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\frac{S^{(L)}-\mathbb{E}[S]}{v}}\right]\right|}_{\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\mathrm{I}}\\

.

(6.12)

where the last inequality follows from (
6.7
).

6.11
), we have that

(6.13)

where

For the last term, since

\displaystyle\mathrm{III}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathrm{i}s\frac{\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{E}[S]}{v}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v_{L}s}{v}\right)^{2}+\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right|\leq\frac{|s|}{v}|\mathbb{E}[S^{(L)}]-\mathbb{E}[S]|+\frac{s^{2}}{2}\frac{|v_{L}^{2}-v^{2}|}{v^{2}}.

6.4
Proof for bounded random variables

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.1
, which contains a Berry–Esseen-type bound on
S
when the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are a.s. uniformly bounded by some common constant.
The proof relies on one hand on Lemma
6.2
, and on the other hand on a Fourier-transform-to-Kolmogorov-distance inequality. Various options exist for this second step, differing by the constant they yield. We chose (
6.2
), which allows for a short and relatively elementary proof. To improve the constant, one could refine our estimate for

\int_{-T}^{T}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi W}\right]-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\right|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}{|\xi|}

where we recall

,
for example by a better bound on
|\mathrm{e}^{z}-1|
and a change of variable
u^{2}/2=\xi^{2}/2+z
(where
z
is given in the proof of Lemma
6.2
). Using another Fourier-transform-to-Kolmogorov-distance inequality would also improve upon the constant, for example
[
She13a
]
which gives the state-of-the-art in the i.i.d. case as of 2012.

Let us now proceed with the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
Recall that we consider random variables
Y_{k}
such that

\max_{k\in V}\|Y_{k}-c_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq L.

Then
S^{(L)}=S
almost surely and in particular
v=v_{L}
. Let

. By Lemma
6.2
, for every
T\leq v/(2\mathrm{e}L(D+1))
such that

,

\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{T}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi W}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi G}\right]\right|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}{|\xi|}

\displaystyle\leq K\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}(1-2KT)}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{K\sqrt{2\pi}}{(1-2KT)^{3/2}}

where
K=K_{L}=C(D+1)^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\delta^{\prime}}L^{3-\delta^{\prime}}/v^{3}
is given in Lemma
6.2
.

K
is minimal when taking
\delta=3
. Indeed,

\mathcal{A}_{\delta^{\prime}}L^{3-\delta^{\prime}}=\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{\delta^{\prime}}]L^{3-\delta^{\prime}}\geq\sum_{k\in V}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{k}-c_{k}|^{3}]=\mathcal{A}_{3}.

Using (
6.2
),

\displaystyle d_{\text{Kol}}(W,G)\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{K\sqrt{2\pi}}{(1-2KT)^{3/2}}+\frac{24}{\pi\sqrt{2\pi}T}=K\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left((1-\alpha)^{-3/2}+\frac{24}{\pi\alpha}\right)\ ,

where we write

reached at

. For every

the term in brackets is bounded from above by

Using this in (

) yields

\displaystyle d_{\text{Kol}}(W,G)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{KI\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}},I\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\alpha_{0}\frac{\mathrm{e}L(D+1)}{v}\right\}\ .

Replacing

by its expression (with

Improvement with third cumulant, bounded case

We now prove Theorem

6.6
Proof with a finite third moment

We handle the case where the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are not assumed to be almost surely uniformly bounded.
In this section, we prove Theorem
2.5
, which contains our Berry–Esseen-type estimate in the case
\delta\in[3,\infty)
. Recall
W=(S-\mathbb{E}[S])/v
and
\xi_{\delta}
as in Definition
1.1
, and let
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Proposition 6.5
.

For all
1\leq\delta\leq\tilde{\delta}<\infty
,

\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{\delta}}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}}}.

In particular,

\xi_{\tilde{\delta}}\leq\xi_{\delta}.

We also have
\xi_{\delta}\in[0,1]
for every
\delta\in[2,\infty)
.

Proof.

Write
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
for the probability space on which the
(Y_{k})_{k\in V}
are defined.
Let
\mathsf{Q}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in V}\delta_{k}

be the uniform probability measure on
V
and let
f:V\times\Omega\to[0,\infty)\,,\,(k,\omega)\mapsto\left|Y_{k}(\omega)-c_{k}\right|^{\delta}
. Then

\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\delta}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{\delta}}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}}}\qquad\iff\qquad\left(\int_{V\times\Omega}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{Q}\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\delta}}\leq\int_{V\times\Omega}f^{\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\delta}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{Q}\otimes\mathbb{P},

which is a consequence of Jensen’s inequality.

It remains to prove that
\xi_{\delta}\in[0,1]
for every
\delta\in[2,\infty)

. For

\leq\mathcal{A}_{2}\sup_{i\in V}\sum_{j\in V:j\sim i}1\leq\mathcal{A}_{2}(D+1)\ .

It follows that

.
∎
