Abstract
. DBSCAN ∗ \operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*} and HDBSCAN ∗ \operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*} are well established density based clustering algorithms. However, obtaining the clusters of very large datasets is infeasible, limiting their use in real world applications. By exploiting the geometry of Euclidean space, we prove that it is possible to systematically construct the DBSCAN ∗ \operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*} and HDBSCAN ∗ \operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*} clusters of a finite X ⊂ ℝ n X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} from specific subsets of X X . We are able to control the size of these subsets and therefore our results make it possible to cluster very large datasets. To illustrate our theory, we cluster the Microsoft Building Footprint Database of the US, which is not possible using the standard implementations.

Introduction

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space. A
clustering
of
X
often refers to finding disjoint subsets (
clusters
) of
X
such that points within a subset are closer to each other than to points in the others. A variant of this is density based clustering, where in addition, the points in a cluster must satisfy a given density condition. This clusters
X
into “high density” regions. Density based clustering has received interest from many fields because of its applicability to a vast range of real world problems
[
1
,
16
,
5
,
18
,
2
,
15
]
.

A widely used density based clustering algorithm is
\operatorname{DBSCAN}

[
6
]
, we work with
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
which is a modification of the original algorithm.
Given parameters
\varepsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
first considers the set
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)\subset X
of points
p\in X
for which the cardinality of
B_{\varepsilon}(p)
at least
k+1
. It then clusters
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
by adding
p,q\in\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
to the same cluster if
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
(see
Section

2
).
Formally, we consider the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters as the connected components of a graph with vertex set
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
and
(p,q)
an edge if
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
(see
2.3
).

Since it is often unclear clear how to choose
\varepsilon
,
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
evaluates
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters over the full range of
\varepsilon>0
and, by evaluating how this hierarchy of clusters evolves, selects the most prevalent clusters of the dataset
[
3
]
.
A core part of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
is the construction of this hierarchy using a complete weighted graph
(X,\omega)
, where
\omega(p,q)
is the maximum of
d(p,q)
and the least
\varepsilon>0
such that
p,q\in\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
(see
2.4
).
The
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters of parameter
\varepsilon
are then the non-trivial connected components of a graph
(X,\omega)_{\varepsilon}
obtained by removing all edges of weight strictly greater than
\varepsilon
.

An essential component of
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
and
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
are
k
nearest neighbour searches, but their complexity grows quadratically and the memory requirements increase with the size of the dataset. This makes it impractical, or even impossible, to use these algorithms for cluster analysis of very large datasets.
Although there has been significant effort to improve their performance
[
7
,
14
,
8
,
10
,
9
,
17
,
13
,
4
]
, the tested data and parameters are incomparable to those used in real world applications. A thorough benchmark
[
11
]
shows that, even when running overnight, it is only feasible to cluster datasets with 5 million points and with
k\leq 5
.
For comparison, the Microsoft Building Footprint Database of the US
[
12
]
has 125 million points and one requires
k\geq 1,000
to delineate geographical areas of interest.

In this article we introduce a new framework to obtain the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
and
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters of very large datasets.
In both cases, we offer an alternative to the explored approaches of optimising and approximating the steps of the algorithms in order to do this. Instead, we provide constructions that avoid the lengthy
k
nearest neighbour searches and prove that they produce the same clusters.

Our starting point is to demonstrate how it is possible to use the natural partition of Euclidean space into cubes to produce
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters subject to minimising the number of distance calculations (see
Section

3
).
In particular, our construction allows us to skip
k
nearest neighbour calculations in highly dense areas of
X
, which are a common challenge for implementations such as sklearn.DBSCAN. In fact, since our proof is constructive we use it to derive an algorithm
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
to construct the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters, which is parallel and scales to very large datasets.

For
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
, we construct a weighted graph
(X,\omega^{\prime})
and prove that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the connected components of
(X,\omega^{\prime})_{\varepsilon}
precisely equal those of
(X,\omega)_{\varepsilon}
, see
5.10
. To mitigate the asymptotic complexity of calculating
k
nearest neighbours the main idea is to construct this graph from small independent subsets
S_{i}\subset X
, whilst ensuring that the connected components are preserved (see Definitions
5.3
and
5.6
).

As with
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
, we use our proof to give an algorithm,
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
, that constructs the
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters, see
Section

5
.
We show that it is possible to iterate through progressively less dense areas of the dataset; partition the data of each iteration into independent, manageable pieces; remove parts of the data that are redundant for the processing of subsequent iterations; and recover the
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters of the full dataset by processing the small pieces of each iteration.

Reconstructing a global property of a graph relying only on local properties is a very hard task. In our case, a central factor that makes it possible to achieve this is, is our careful selection of the
S_{i}
and the identification and explicit description of reduced subsets
\partial S_{i}
that contain the relevant points that interact with other
S_{j}
.

In fact, we develop theory that allows us to study finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in a similar way to how one treats open sets in classical topology, see
Section

4
. This machinery enables us to incorporate Euclidean geometry onto finite sets, for example, define notions such as boundary and neighbourhood of a set; calculate distances between sets and prove that it is attained near the boundary (see
4.1
and
4.2
). This point of view is instrumental in our construction of
(X,\omega^{\prime})
and in the proof that it has the desired property. It proves a great computational asset by controlling the number of distance calculations when clustering a real world dataset.

To put our framework into practice, we include examples where we cluster two real world datasets, using
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
in
Section

6
, and
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
in
Section

7
. Our largest dataset is the entire Microsoft Building Footprint Database of the United States
[
12
]
which we cluster with
k=1,900
.

2.
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
and
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}

Given an edge-weighted graph
G
, we write
V(G)
for the set of vertices of
G
,
E(G)
for the set of edges of
G
and
\omega(G)
for the weight function
\omega(G)\colon E(G)\to[0,\infty)
. We will often omit the argument of
G
whenever it is clear from the context. We will write
\pi_{0}(G)
for the set of connected components of
G
.

Let
G,H
be two weighted graphs. The
union

G\cup H
is the weighted graph with vertex set
V(G)\cup V(H)
, edge set
E(G)\cup E(H)
and weights

\omega(p,q)=\begin{cases}\min\{\omega(G)(p,q),\omega(H)(p,q)\}&(p,q)\in E(G)\cap E(H)\\
\omega(G)(p,q)&(p,q)\in E(G)\setminus E(H)\\
\omega(H)(p,q)&(p,q)\in E(H)\setminus E(G).\end{cases}

If
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
and
G
is a weighted graph, the graph
G_{\varepsilon}
will denote the subgraph of
G
with vertex set
V(G)
, set of edges
E(G_{\varepsilon})=\{(p,q)\in E(G):\omega(G)(p,q)\leq\varepsilon\}
and with weight
\omega(G_{\varepsilon}):=\omega(G)|_{E(G_{\varepsilon})}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G_{\varepsilon,k}
denote the subgraph of
G_{\varepsilon}
induced by vertices of degree at least
k
.

Given a finite metric space
(X,d)
, let
G(X,d)
be the complete graph on
X
with weight
\omega(p,q)=d(p,q)
.

Remark 2.1
.

In the context of topological data analysis,
G(X,d)_{\varepsilon}
is sometimes referred to as the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood graph
of
X
and it is the
1
-skeleton of the well-known Čech complex of
(X,d)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Given a point
p\in X
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we denote by
B_{\varepsilon}(p)
the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon
with centre
p
. The cardinality of a set
A
will be denoted by
|A|
. Given
A,B\subset X
and
p\in X
define the
distance to
A
from
p
as

d(p,A)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a),

and the
distance between
A
and
B
as

d(A,B)=\inf_{a\in A}d(a,B).

For
\varepsilon\geq 0
define the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood
of
A\subset X
as

B_{\varepsilon}(A)=\{p\in X:d(p,A)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Throughout this article we work with a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this section we describe two density based clustering algorithms:
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
and
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
.

2.1.
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}

In this section, we fix
(X,d)
a finite metric space and
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
is an algorithm that clusters regions of
X
with minimum local density
k+1
. The local density of
X
is measured using the cardinality of balls with radius
\varepsilon
.

Definition 2.2
.

A point
p\in X
is called a
core point
if
B_{\varepsilon}(p)
contains at least
k
points excluding
p
, that is, if

|B_{\varepsilon}(p)|>k.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
the set of core points and define the set of
noise points

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)=X\setminus\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
.

Notice that
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)=V(G(X,d)_{\varepsilon,k})
.

Definition 2.3
.

The set of
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}(\varepsilon)

clusters
is

\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)=\{V(C):C\in\pi_{0}(G_{\varepsilon,k})\}.

When
\varepsilon
is clear from the context, we write
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}
instead of
\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)
,
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)
, respectively.

2.2.
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}

\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
evaluates
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}(\varepsilon)
clusters over the full range
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
. By considering how these clusters evolve and persist as
\varepsilon
varies, it is possible to quantify the relative density of a cluster compared to surrounding regions.
The final
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters are
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters which prevail the most through all scales. These clusters may come from different
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
scales.

We recall the construction of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
and its persistence score, described as in
[
10
]
.
Again, for this section, we fix
(X,d)
a finite metric space.

Definition 2.4
.

A
nearest neighbour
of
p\in X
is an element
p_{1}\in X
such that
d(p,p_{1})=d(p,X\setminus\{p\})
.
For
j>1
, a
j
-th nearest neighbour
of
p
is an element
p_{j}\in X
which satisfies
d(p,p_{j})=d(p,X\setminus\{p,p_{1},\cdots,p_{j-1}\})
, where
p_{i}
is an
i
-th nearest neighbour for
1\leq i\leq j-1
.
For
p\in X
, we will write
\operatorname{core}_{k}(p)
for the distance from
p
to a
k
-th nearest neighbour.

We define the
reachability distance

\rho\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
as

\rho(p,q)=\operatorname{max}\{\operatorname{core}_{k}(p),\operatorname{core}_{k}(q),d(p,q)\}

if
p\neq q
and
\rho(p,p)=0
.

To describe
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
we first note that for any finite metric space
(X,d)
and
\varepsilon>0
,

E(G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon})=E(G(X,d)_{\varepsilon,k}).

Consequently, for
p\neq q\in X
,

(2.1)

p,q\in\Gamma\text{ for some }\Gamma\in\pi_{0}(G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon})\Leftrightarrow p,q\in\tilde{\Gamma}\text{ for some }\tilde{\Gamma}\in\pi_{0}(G(X,d)_{\varepsilon,k}).

Thus, by varying
\varepsilon
from
0
to
\operatorname{diam}(X)
,
G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon}
produces a hierarchy of the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters.
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
first derives a summary of this hierarchy by
defining an equivalence relation on these clusters.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. For
0\leq\gamma\leq\varepsilon
, the inclusion

i_{\gamma}\colon G(X,d)_{\gamma,k}\hookrightarrow G(X,d)_{\varepsilon,k},

gives the induced map

(i_{\gamma})_{*}\colon\pi_{0}(G(X,d)_{\gamma,k})\hookrightarrow\pi_{0}(G(X,d)_{\varepsilon,k}).

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, set

\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon,m)=\{C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon):|C|\geq m\}.

Define an equivalence relation on

\bigcup_{\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)}\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon,m)

by
C\sim D
, with
C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(\delta,m)
and
D\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon,m)
and without loss of generality
\delta\leq\varepsilon
, if and only if

(i_{\delta})_{*}(C)=D\text{ and }|(i_{\gamma})_{*}^{-1}|=1

for every
\gamma\in[\delta,\varepsilon]
.

Notice that for an equivalence class
[C]
and
\gamma\in[0,\infty)

|[C]\cap\mathcal{D}^{*}(\gamma,m)|\in\{0,1\}.

Set
C_{\gamma}
to be the unique element of
[C]\cap\mathcal{D}^{*}(\gamma,m)
if the latter is non empty, and
\emptyset
otherwise. Define the
persistence score
of an equivalence class
[C]
by

\Sigma([C])=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|C_{\gamma}|}{\gamma^{2}}.

This is a weighted average of the density of the clusters
C_{\gamma}
within an equivalence class.

The final step in
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
is to choose final clusters amongst the equivalence classes. For an equivalence class
[C]
, define

p([C])=\bigcup_{\varepsilon\geq 0}C_{\varepsilon}.

Note that since
X
is a finite metric space there are only a finite number of equivalence classes, say
\{[C^{1}],\ldots,[C^{\alpha}]\}
. We now select
I\subset\{1,\ldots,\alpha\}
which maximises

\sum_{i\in J}\Sigma[C^{i}]

amongst all
J\subset\{1,\ldots,\alpha\}
with

p([C^{i}])\cap p([C^{j}])=\emptyset

whenever
i\neq j\in J
.
The set of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}

clusters
is

\mathcal{H}^{*}=\{p([C^{i}]):i\in I\}.

3.
Reconstructing
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
using covering cubes

In this section we describe
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
, our parallel algorithm that produces
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters. This algorithm relies on the natural partition of Euclidean space into cubes.

From now on,
X
will denote a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 2
. We will consider subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with either the
l_{2}
metric, denoted by
d
, or the reachability distance derived from
d
, denoted by
\rho
. Whilst our ideas are applicable to any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we restrict to this setting for simplicity.

Definition 3.1
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, define
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
to be the collection of cubes of the form

\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\quad j_{i}\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}\leq x_{i}\leq(j_{i}+1)\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}\right\},

with
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

We say that
S,T\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
are
adjacent
if
S\cap T\neq\emptyset
. For
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, define the
m
-extension of
S
by

S^{m}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{max}{|x_{i}-s_{i}|}\leq m\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}\text{ for some }s\in S\right\}.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
be the smallest integer such that
\mathfrak{m}\geq 2\sqrt{n}
. Note that
S^{\mathfrak{m}}
is the smallest extension of
S
satisfying
B_{\varepsilon}(S)\subset S^{\mathfrak{m}}
.

For
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

\mathcal{I}(A)=\{S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon):S\cap A\neq\emptyset\}.

For
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
Y_{A}
for
Y\cap A
.

For the rest of the section we fix
\varepsilon>0
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}
are the core points of
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
from
2.2
.

Note that, because of the choice of side length of cubes
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
,

p\in S,\ q\in S^{1}\Longrightarrow d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon.

In particular,

(3.1)

p,q\in S_{\mathcal{C}}\Longrightarrow p,q\in C\text{ for some }C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}.

\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
constructs a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C}),\mathcal{E})
whose connected components are in bijective correspondence with the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters:
S,T\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
are in the same connected component of
\mathcal{G}
if and only if all of the elements of
S_{\mathcal{C}}
and
T_{\mathcal{C}}
belong to the same
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
cluster.
This relies on the observation in
eq.

3.1
.

The idea behind the construction of this graph arises from algebraic topology. In terms of covering spaces, a
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
cluster is equivalent to a connected component of
\cup\mathfrak{B}
, where one regards

\mathfrak{B}=\{B_{\varepsilon/2}(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{C}}

as a covering of
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We consider two other coverings of
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathfrak{U}=\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
(a refinement of
\mathfrak{B}
) and
\mathfrak{W}=\{S^{\mathfrak{l}}\}_{S\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})}
(refined by
\mathfrak{B}
), with
\mathfrak{l}
the smallest integer
\mathfrak{l}\geq\sqrt{n}
.
The connected components of
\cup\mathfrak{U}
and
\cup\mathfrak{W}
are an under and over approximation of the connected components of
\cup\mathfrak{B}
. In general, both of
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{W}
are significantly smaller than
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathfrak{B}
, especially around highly dense areas of
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, working with a regular tiling by cubes is more efficient than working with arbitrary balls.

The first step is to identify the core points
\mathcal{C}
and the cubes containing them
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
. For this we:

•

Categorise each cube
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
as “dense”, “sparse” or “locally dense” depending on the cardinality of a neighbourhood of
S
.
This is done so that “dense” cubes only contain core points and “sparse” cubes only contain noise points.

•

For “locally dense”
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
, identify the core points
p\in S
, obtain their
\varepsilon
-neighbours
B_{\varepsilon}(p)\cap X
, and identify the cubes containing any
\varepsilon
-neighbours of points in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
.

In the second step we build the graph
\mathcal{G}
:

•

Begin with
\mathcal{G}_{0}=(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C}),\mathcal{E}_{0})
, where
(S,T)\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
if
S\cap T\neq\emptyset
; that is, the connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
correspond to those of
\cup\mathfrak{U}
. We use the results from the first step and the cubes from
\mathfrak{W}
to remove and add edges to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
and obtain
\mathcal{G}
.

This approach, and our specific choice of side length of the cubes in
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
, allows us to identify large portions of
X
for which the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters can be partially constructed without the need for any pairwise distance calculations.

The first step only requires us to consider a neighbourhood of a cube and can be computed in parallel. Most of the second step only requires either the neighbourhood of a cube, or a connected component of a graph, and each can be processed in parallel.

Note for computational reasons it is more convenient to use the covers
\{S^{1}\}_{S\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})}
and
\{S^{\mathfrak{\mathfrak{m}}}\}_{S\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})}
, instead of
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{W}
, which we do below.

3.1.
Categorising cubes

Observation 3.2
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
. If
|S_{X}^{1}|>k
, then
S_{X}\subset\mathcal{C}
. If
|S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{X}|\leq k
or
S_{X}=\emptyset
, then
S_{X}\subset\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the fact that if
S
and
T
are adjacent cubes in
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
, then
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
for any
p\in S
and
q\in T
. The second is satisfied since, for any
p\in S
,
B_{\varepsilon}(p)\subset S^{\mathfrak{m}}
.
∎

This observation motivates the following definition:

Definition 3.3
.

We say that
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
is

(1)

dense
if
S_{X}\neq\emptyset
and
|S^{1}_{X}|>k
,

(2)

sparse
if
|S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{X}|\leq k
or
S_{X}=\emptyset
,

(3)

locally dense
if
S_{X}\neq\emptyset
,
|S^{1}_{X}|<k
and
|S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{X}|>k
.

By
3.2
, we only have to determine the core points of locally dense cubes, which significantly reduces the number of pairwise distance calculations.

3.2.
Identifying core points in locally dense cubes and their
\varepsilon
-neighbours

We use the following observation to identify the core points in a locally dense cube and their
\varepsilon
-neighbours.

Observation 3.4
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
be locally dense. A
p\in S
is a core point if and only if

|B_{\varepsilon}(p)\cap(S_{X}^{\mathfrak{m}}\setminus S^{1})|>k-|S^{1}_{X}|.

This implies that for every locally dense cube
S\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
and
p\in S
we only need to calculate
d(p,q)
for
q\in S^{\mathfrak{m}}\setminus S^{1}
. If we deduce that
p\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
, we store these
\varepsilon
-neighbours of
p
in a list
\mathcal{B}_{p}
and add the elements of
S^{1}_{X}\setminus\{p\}
. We also store a list
\mathcal{B}_{S}
of the cubes
T
such that
T_{X}\cap B_{\varepsilon}(S_{\mathcal{C}})\neq\emptyset
.

3.3.
Building the graph

Let us first consider the graph
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C}),\mathcal{E}_{1})
with
(S,T)\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
if

(1)

S,T
are adjacent or,

(2)

S,T
are locally dense,
T\in\mathcal{B}_{S}
and
S\in\mathcal{B}_{T}
or

(3)

S
is locally dense,
T
is dense and
T\in\mathcal{B}_{S}
.

This graph is easy to construct using the previous results of this section. However,
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
only approximates the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters.

The first problem is if
S,T\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
are as in
Item

2
, we cannot guarantee the existence of
p\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
and
q\in T_{\mathcal{C}}
with
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
. Compare this to
Items

1
and
3
, where finding such
p,q
is guaranteed. We prune
\mathcal{G}_{1}
to correct this.

Precisely, we calculate
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
and for each
\Gamma\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
we remove edges
(S,T)
with
S,T
non adjacent and such that for every
p\in S
,

B_{\varepsilon}(p)\cap T_{\mathcal{C}}=\emptyset.

We do this in parallel across the connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
to construct
\mathcal{G}_{2}=(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C}),\mathcal{E}_{2})
. By construction, if
(S,T)\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
, then there are
p\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
and
q\in T_{\mathcal{C}}
with
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
. In particular,
p,q\in C
for some
C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
. By
eq.

3.1
,
S_{\mathcal{C}}\cup T_{\mathcal{C}}\subset C
.

Conversely, suppose that
p\in S_{C}
and
q\in T_{\mathcal{C}}
with
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
. If
S,T
are adjacent or one of them is locally dense, then by definition
(S,T)\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and this edge is not pruned when constructing
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

The remaining problem is if
S,T\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
are both dense and belong to different connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
. For every pair of such cubes with
T\subset S^{\mathfrak{m}}\setminus S^{1}
, we calculate pairwise distances to determine if there are
p\in S_{X}\subset\mathcal{C}
and
q\in T_{X}\subset\mathcal{C}
with
d(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
. If so, we add
(S,T)
to form a final graph
\mathcal{G}
and stop processing the connected components containing
S
and
T
.

We handle this case last to avoid as many pairwise distance calculations involving dense cubes as possible; this is crucial as their cardinality can be very large. By doing this step last we increase the likeliness that two dense cubes will be connected by a path constructed earlier in the process.

4.
Geometric constructions relative to covering cubes

In this section, we fix
\varepsilon>0
. Recall from
3.1
the set of cubes
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
and its properties. We also fix
A\subset X
and recall that
\mathcal{I}(A)=\{S\text{ cube}:S_{A}\neq\emptyset\}
.
We use the elements of
\mathcal{Q}
to define standard notions from topology (interior, boundary and closure) of
A
relative to
\mathcal{Q}
.
These constructions are a fundamental component of our alternate construction of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters.

At the end of the section we demonstrate how these constructions allow us to easily extend
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
to
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}
, a parallel algorithm that obtains the
\operatorname{DBSCAN}
clusters, whilst minimising pairwise distance calculations.

Definition 4.1
.

A cube
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
is an
interior cube
of
A
if
S^{1}_{X}\subset A
and
T\in\mathcal{I}(A)
for every cube
T\subset S^{1}
. The
interior of
A
is the union of interior cubes,

\operatorname{int}(A)=\bigcup_{S\text{ interior}}S.

A cube
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
is a
boundary cube
of
A
if
S
is not an interior cube.
The
boundary of
A
is defined as

\partial A=\bigcup\limits_{S\text{ boundary}}S,

and the
closure of
A
is

\bar{A}=\operatorname{int}(A)\cup\partial A=\bigcup\limits_{S\in\mathcal{I}(A)}S.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
N\geq 0
. We define the
N
-extension
of
Y
to be

Y^{N}=\bigcup\limits_{S\in\mathcal{I}(Y)}S^{N}

From now on we will write
\mathfrak{n}
for the smallest integer satisfying
\mathfrak{n}\geq\sqrt{n}-1
. Notice that
\mathfrak{n}\geq 1
.

We now show that the distance to a point outside
A
is attained near the boundary of
A
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
x\in X\setminus A
and
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For any
p\in A\setminus(\partial A)^{\mathfrak{n}+r}_{A}
, there exists
a\in(\partial A)_{A}
such that

d(p,x)>d(a,x)+r\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}.

In particular, for any
x\in X\setminus A
and
p\in A
, there exists
a\in(\partial A)^{\mathfrak{n}}_{A}
such that

d(a,x)\leq d(p,x).

Proof.

Let
x,p
be as in the hypothesis and
S\in\mathcal{Q}
with
p\in S
.

First note that

(4.1)

S^{\mathfrak{n}+r}\subset\operatorname{int}(A).

Indeed
S\subset\operatorname{int}(A)
because
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
and
p\not\in\partial A
. If
i\geq 0
is maximal such that
S^{i}\subset\operatorname{int}(A)
and
i<\mathfrak{n}+r
, there exists
T\in\mathcal{Q}
with
T\subset S^{i+1}\cap\partial A
and this would imply

S\subset T^{i+1}\subset(\partial A)^{i+1}\subset(\partial A)^{\mathfrak{n}+r},

contradicting the hypothesis.
Note that
eq.

4.1
implies

(4.2)

T\in\mathcal{Q},\ T\subset S^{\mathfrak{n}+r+1}\Longrightarrow T\in\mathcal{I}(A)\text{ and }T\not\in\mathcal{I}(X\setminus A).

Let
[p,x]
denote the line segment from
p
to
x
and let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{j}=1
and
p_{i}=(1-t_{i})p+t_{i}x
be such that

[p_{i-1},p_{i}]\in\{S\cap[p,x]:S\in\mathcal{I}([p,x])\}.

We write
Q_{i}
for the cube satisfying
[p_{i-1},p_{i}]=Q_{i}\cap[p,x]
.

Let
i
be maximal such that
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{I}(A)
and
Q_{i}\not\in\mathcal{I}(X\setminus A)
. In particular,
Q_{i}\subset\partial A
and by
eq.

4.2

Q_{i+1}\not\subset S^{\mathfrak{n}+r+1}
. Since
p\not\in(\partial A)^{\mathfrak{n}+r}\supset(Q_{i})^{\mathfrak{n}+r}
we have the strict inequality

(4.3)

d(p,p_{i})>(\mathfrak{n}+r+1)\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}.

Let
a\in(\partial A)_{A}\cap Q_{i}
, so that

(4.4)

d(p_{i},a)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
p,p_{i},x
all lie in the same line segment, we have

d(p,x)=d(p,p_{i})+d(p_{i},x).

Combining this and
eqs.

4.4
and
4.3
and using the reverse triangle inequality,

\displaystyle d(p,x)

\displaystyle>(\mathfrak{n}+r+1)\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}+d(x,a)-d(a,p_{i})

\displaystyle\geq(\mathfrak{n}+r+1)\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}+d(x,a)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\geq r\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{n}}+d(x,a),

using the fact that
\mathfrak{n}+1\geq\sqrt{n}
.
∎

We next show that in order to find the closure of the extension of a set, we only need to extend its boundary.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, then
(\bar{A})^{r}=\operatorname{int}(A)\cup(\partial A)^{r}.

Proof.

Certainly
(\bar{A})^{r}\supset\operatorname{int}(A)\cup(\partial A)^{r}.

To prove the other containment, let
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
. If
S\subset\partial A
, then
S^{r}\subset(\partial A)^{r}
. Otherwise, suppose
S\subset\operatorname{int}(A)
and let
T\in\mathcal{Q}
be such that
T\subset S^{r}
. Notice that if
T\in\mathcal{I}(A)
, then
T\subset\bar{A}\subset\operatorname{int}(A)\cup(\partial A)^{r}
.

Now suppose
T\not\in\mathcal{I}(A)
. Then there exists
t>0
such that
T^{t-1}_{A}=\emptyset
and
T^{t}_{A}\neq\emptyset
.
Since
S\subset T^{r}
and
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
,
t<r
. Let
Q\subset T^{t}
be such that
Q\in\mathcal{I}(A)
. Then
Q\subset\partial A
and so
T\subset(\partial A)^{t}\subset(\partial A)^{r}
.
∎

Finally, we note that to find the
\varepsilon
-neighbours of
A
it is sufficient to extend the boundary
\mathfrak{m}
times.

Corollary 4.4
.

B_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\bar{A}^{\mathfrak{m}}=\operatorname{int}(A)\cup(\partial A)^{\mathfrak{m}}.

Proof.

For any
S\in\mathcal{I}(A)
,
B_{\varepsilon}(S)\subset S^{\mathfrak{m}}
and so
S^{\mathfrak{m}}\subset\bar{A}^{\mathfrak{m}}
. In consequence,

B_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\bigcup\limits_{S\in\mathcal{I}(A)}S^{\mathfrak{m}}\subset\bar{A}^{\mathfrak{m}}.

∎

4.1.
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}

The original density-based clustering algorithm
\operatorname{DBSCAN}
was first introduced in
[
6
]
. In contrast to
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
,
\operatorname{DBSCAN}
makes a distinction between non-core points which are
\varepsilon
-neighbours of core points and those that are not.

Definition 4.5
.

An

is a

if
x\in B_{\varepsilon}(C)
for some

.

assigns any border point

to the first cluster

.

Using
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
and the results from
Section

4
, it is straightforward to obtain

clusters.

Remark 4.6
.

Fix
C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
. By
4.4
, the border points of
C
are contained in
(\partial C)^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{N}}
. By
4.2
,
x\in(\partial C)^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{N}}
is a border point of

if there exists

such that

. For locally dense

, we previously calculated the

-neighbours of

and so we can immediately add those that belong to

. For dense cubes

, we can immediately add

to

. Thus we only need to restrict to pairwise distance calculations between core points in a dense cube

and noise points in

.

5.
Reconstructing

using cluster boundaries

In this section we use the theory from
Section

4

.
In fact, we construct a weighted graph

satisfying

\{V(C):C\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})\}=\{V(C):C\in\pi_{0}(G(X,\rho)_{\alpha})\},

for every

. The definition of the persistence score and the process used to choose the final clusters from

can be applied to

to produce the

We construct

by constructing smaller weighted graphs restricted to subsets

and combine them to form

.
Since our central requirement is that

satisfies

(1)

(the search space for

and contains the

-nearest neighbours of elements in

(2)

we can independently construct graphs for each

and have minimal interaction between different

(3)

we can prescribe reduced subsets of

that contain the relevant points that interact with other
C_{j}
.

In order to meet the above constraints we

•

select sets
C_{i}
with a uniform bound
\operatorname{core}_{k}\leq\varepsilon
so that taking

to be an extension of
C_{i}
(as in
Section

4
); that is,
C_{i}\subset\mathcal{C}(\varepsilon)

item

1
;

•

iteratively construct our graph using an increasing sequence of
\varepsilon
and choose the

at each iteration so that the

do not intersect other
C_{j}

item

2
.

•

ensure

is a union of
\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)
clusters. Otherwise, since all the elements in a
\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)
cluster are in the same connected component at scale
\varepsilon
, we would need to search other

for edges of weight in
[0,\varepsilon]

item

2
.

•

ensure
C_{i}

is
a
\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)
cluster. Otherwise,

could be dispersed throughout

and its extension could be the whole of
X

item

2
.

•

prove that an extension of

, for each
C_{i}
in an iteration, contains all the points that interact with previous iterations. By construction, the
C_{i}
of one iteration do not interact with each other
item

3
.

Having established that
\mathcal{F}
must be constructed iteratively using
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters to partition

,
\mathcal{D}^{*}(\varepsilon_{1})

We then wish to repeat this for a choice of

. However, increasing the scale can result in clusters that are too large for local calculations to be efficient or feasible. This would also not take into account the graphs obtained in the initial step. Instead, we consider a subset of

consisting of
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon_{1})
and an extension of the boundary of each cluster in

The key result is to show that the core points that play a role in forming

We define the subset

containing this extension of each cluster in

and
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon_{1})
of

The main inductive argument is

To complete the hierarchical clustering, we define

Remark 5.1

One could also construct approximations of the

From now on, let

Let

Let

\varepsilon>0

\rho^{F(Y,\varepsilon)}(p,q)=\rho^{Y}(p,q).

Example:
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
to cluster building data of the US

Recall that, for a fixed value of
\varepsilon
, the connected components of
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
(with more than one element) coincide with the connected components of
G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon}
, used to construct the
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
clusters, see
eq.

2.1
.
This fact allows us to construct
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
in steps by first separating the data into
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters and then obtaining the connected components of
G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon}
restricted to each
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
cluster.

More explicitly the steps of
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
are:

(1)

Cluster
X_{1}=X
using
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
with an initial choice of
\varepsilon_{1}
.

(2)

For each cluster
C\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(X_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
, we calculate the reachability distance
\tilde{\rho}
using
only
points in
C^{\mathfrak{m}}
and obtain the weighted graph
G(C,\tilde{\rho})_{\varepsilon_{1}}
.

(3)

Combine an extended boundary of each cluster and
\mathcal{N}(X_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
to form
X_{2}
. Repeat steps 1 and 2 on
X_{2}
for a choice of
\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{1}
.

(4)

Combine an extended boundary of each cluster in
\mathcal{D}^{*}(X_{2},\varepsilon_{2})
and
\mathcal{N}(X_{2},\varepsilon_{2})
to form
X_{3}
.

(5)

Calculate
\tilde{\rho}
in
X_{3}
and the weighted graph
G(X_{3},\tilde{\rho})
.

(6)

Combine all weighted graphs produced at each step to produce our final graph
G
.
5.10
guarantees that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the connected components of
G_{\varepsilon}
agree with those of
G(X,\rho)_{\varepsilon}
.

This procedure can be iterated any number of times, we use
3
for illustration only.
At each step, each
\operatorname{S-DBSCAN}^{*}
cluster is processed independently and in parallel.

One of the fastest implementations of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
is sklearn.
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
,
[
10
]
.
The speed of this algorithm comes from combining several high performance algorithms used to optimise the steps of
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
with high time complexity.
A central tool used in these optimisations are
k
-d trees
, which are state-of-the-art in nearest neighbour type searches.

A
k
-d tree partitions
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into non-overlapping regions, indexed by a given
reference tree

\tau_{r}\subset X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Given a
query point

p\in\tau_{q}\subset X
, one can use the partition to efficiently find the nearest neighbours in
\tau_{r}
to
p
.
To further optimise performance one can build a second partition based on the
query tree

\tau_{q}
.

In
[
10
]
, query and reference trees are used to calculate
\operatorname{core}_{k}
and a
minimum spanning tree
.
In practice, given a weighted graph
H
, to obtain the connected components of
H_{\varepsilon}
, for every
\varepsilon
, a standard and efficient technique is to store a minimum spanning tree of
H
. This is done using the dual tree Borůvka algorithm.

The performance and memory usage of calculating
\operatorname{core}_{k}
and the Borůvka algorithm worsens as the size of the reference and query trees increase.
In fact, in
[
11
]
it can be seen that clustering very large datasets using sklearn.
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
becomes infeasible.
An underlying reason for this is that these trees are constructed using the entire dataset.
A common solution is to process the points in the query tree in batches.
However, given the size of our dataset and our choice of
k
, the reference trees are simply too large and batch processing the query trees alone does not make the task tangible.

To solve this issue
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
partitions
X
to create many smaller trees.
The difficulty lies in partitioning the reference trees whilst ensuring that the correct
\operatorname{core}_{k}
and the correct weights of a spanning graph are produced.
Since
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters naturally partition
X
they can be used to achieve this.
We take this further and iteratively use
\operatorname{DBSCAN}^{*}
clusters to incrementally construct a spanning graph.
After each iteration we can remove points from the dataset that are now redundant, namely the interiors of clusters of previous iterations.
Moreover, the calculations for each cluster are independent and are processed in parallel.
A crucial point is that, the minimum spanning trees of each cluster are combined into a graph with a relatively small amount of edges that, by
5.10
, allows us to recover the connected components required for
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
.

In practice, from one iteration to the next we only add edges
(p,q)
with weight
\varepsilon_{i-1}<\omega(p,q)\leq\varepsilon_{i}
because this is sufficient to complete a small spanning graph.
Further, we only add an edge
(p,q)
if
p
and
q
were not in the same connected component in a previous iteration.

We also note that
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
can be used recursively: if a particular cluster cannot be processed directly, the same approach can be applied to the cluster.

To demonstrate our theory, we built an implementation of
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
and ran it on the same example datasets than
Section

6

Since this was for demonstration purposes only it consists of unoptimised Python scripts. As a point of comparison, we also ran sklearn.
\operatorname{HDBSCAN}^{*}
.

7.2.
Final remarks

There is a lot of potential to implement
\operatorname{S-HDBSCAN}^{*}
in parallel and to employ sophisticated algorithms that optimise its performance. Moreover, the number of points that we removed in this final iteration motivates further study in how to optimally choose the
\varepsilon_{i}
to have a greater impact by removing larger interiors in each iteration.
