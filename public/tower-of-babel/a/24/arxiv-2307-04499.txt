First order synthesis for data words revisited
Julien Grange, Mathieu Lehaut

Abstract
We carry on the study of the synthesis problem on data words for fragments of first order logic, and delineate precisely the border between decidability and undecidability.



1
Introduction

The reactive synthesis problem, which dates back to Church
[
4
]
, is about generating a
correct-by-construction
program with respect to a given specification.
It is often formulated as a two-player game between an uncontrollable Environment and the System, who alternate picking an input and an output letter, respectively.
This creates an infinite execution, and the goal of the System is to make every execution satisfy the specification, whatever Environment does.
If the System has a strategy to ensure this result, it then corresponds to a program that is sure to respect the specification.
The original problem is decidable and was solved by Büchi and Landweber
[
3
]
, and several improvements and extensions have since been studied. However, it only encompasses finite alphabets, which is inadequate for representing executions of distributed systems involving a number of processes which is not fixed; this occurs in communication protocols, distributed algorithms, multi-agent systems, swarm robotics, or with ad-hoc networks.

We thus consider an extension of this problem which deals with alphabets whose size is not fixed.
In those cases it is more adequate to use data words to represent executions, where a data word consists of a sequence of pairs (action, data), the action coming from a finite alphabet while the data, which comes from an infinite alphabet (or at least, an alphabet whose size is not fixed), specifies who performed said action.

We will assume that the sets of actions of both players are disjoint, but this does not necessarily need to be the case for the processes.
We will consider two distinct cases, depending on process ownership.
In the first case, all processes are shared, meaning that both players can perform their respective actions on any process.
Consider for instance the modelling of a drone fleet, where a process corresponds to a single drone, inputs correspond to atmospheric conditions and outputs to possible movements of the drone. In that case, it makes sense to have both System and Environment play their actions on shared processes.
In the second case, each player has their own processes, on which only they can play an action.
This is for instance useful for modelling a single machine with several components, where each component is considered to be its own process, some being sensors/receptors (belonging to Environment) while other parts (System’s processes) perform the machine’s output.

Finally, it remains to chose a formalism to express the specification which should be satisified by System.
As always, there is a trade off between expressiveness of the formalism and tractability of its synthesis problem.
Many specification languages for data words have been studied, and the synthesis problem has been investigated for some of them, such as register automata
[
6
]
and the Logic of Repeating Values
[
5
]
.
Here we follow the steps of
[
1
]
and consider first order logic
FO
and its fragment
\textsc{FO}^{2}
where only two reusable variables are allowed.
First order logic for words is well understood, and its extension to data words is easy to define, which makes it a good candidate for our purpose.
On data words,
FO
has acess to a binary predicate
\sim
such that
x\sim y
if positions
x
and
y
belong to the same process. On top of that, a unary predicate for each action marks the positions where this action has been played (as is usually the case for words), and we will study variations where relative positions will either be accessible via a binary predicate
<
, its successor relation
+1
, both or none of them.

The satisfiability problem, which can be seen as a very restricted synthesis problem in the case where Environment never acts, has been showed to be decidable in the two-variable fragment with
<
and
+1
(
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<,+1]
)
[
2
]
.
The synthesis problem itself has previously been studied in
[
1
]
for various of fragments of
FO
, with both decidable and undecidable results, depending on the variety of the considered fragment.
The aim of this paper is to extend those results and get a better understanding on where the border between decidability and undecidability lies exactly.

2
Preliminaries

Data words.

Let
A
be a finite alphabet of
actions
, partitioned into System actions
A_{\mathrm{S}}
and Environment actions
A_{\mathrm{E}}
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
) be a finite set of
processes
belonging to System (resp. Environment), and let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
be a finite set of processes which can be activated by both players. These sets are assumed to be pairwise disjoint, and we define
\mathbb{P}:=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}\cup\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}\cup\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
. Let us refer to
A_{\mathrm{S}}\times(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}\cup\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}})
as
\Sigma_{S}
and to
A_{\mathrm{E}}\times(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}\cup\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}})
as
\Sigma_{E}
. A
data word
is a finite or infinite sequence
w=(a_{0},p_{0})(a_{1},p_{1})\dots
over
\Sigma:=\Sigma_{S}\cup\Sigma_{E}
.

Intuitively, a data word represents the trace of an execution of a distributed system.
Each pair
(a,p)
(called a
position
) indicates that action
a
has been performed by process
p
. Note that the above definition prevents a player from playing on processes owned by their opponent.

First order logic.

Let
A
be a finite alphabet of actions and
V
be a set of variable names.
We define formulas of first order logic with
<
and
+1
on data words (
\textsc{FO}[\sim,<,+1]
) as follows:

\displaystyle t:=~

\displaystyle a(x)\mid x=y\mid x<y\mid x=y+1\mid x\sim y\mid\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}(x)\mid\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}(x)\mid\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}(x)

\displaystyle\varphi:=~

\displaystyle t\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\exists x.\varphi\mid\forall x.\varphi

where
a\in A
and
x,y\in V
.

A data word
w=(a_{0},p_{0})(a_{1},p_{1})\dots
is represented as a structure whose domain has an element for each position
(a_{i},p_{i})
, and an element for each process in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
, resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
), which is in the interpretation of the unary predicate
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
, resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
). Note that this allows to quantify on processes on which no action has been played.
For every action
a\in A
, the unary predicate
a
is interpreted as the set of positions with action
a
. The binary predicate
<
is interpreted as the linear order on positions and
+1
as its successor relation. Finally,
(a_{i},p_{i})\sim(a_{j},p_{j})
holds if and only if
p_{i}=p_{j}
; on top of that,
\sim
also relates each process
p
to all positions
(a,p)
.
Beyond that, we consider the usual semantics for first order logic, e.g. see
[
7
]
.

We then define various fragments of
\textsc{FO}[\sim,<,+1]
depending on which binary predicates are allowed, and how many variables are available.

\textsc{FO}[\sim,<,+1]
is the whole fragment with
\sim
,
<
, and
+1
, while
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
only allows
\sim
out of the three predicates.
Furthermore, we also study the two-variable restriction of
FO
, in which one can only use two (reusable) variables in the whole formula, i.e.
V=\{x,y\}
.
We denote this variation by
\textsc{FO}^{2}
, and combine it with the previous notations; for instance
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<]
is the two-variable fragment with predicates
\sim
and
<
, but without
+1
.
Note that unless restricted to two variables, first order logic can define
+1
using
<
, but not the other way around.

Synthesis.

Let us now define the synthesis problem. A data word is seen as an execution, or history, in a game opposing System and Environment.
A
strategy

\mathcal{S}
for System is a function
\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma_{S}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
which, given some finite data word representing the history of the game, either outputs some move in
\Sigma_{S}
(i.e. plays an action on some process which can be activated by System) or passes its turn.
An execution
w=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
is said to be
\mathcal{S}
-compatible if

•

for all
0\leq i<|w|
,
\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma_{S}
implies
\sigma_{i}=\mathcal{S}(\sigma_{0}\dots\sigma_{i-1})
, and

•

if
w
is finite, then
\mathcal{S}(w)=\varepsilon
.

With this definition we intuitively allow Environment to block System from playing anytime Environments wants to play, which could potentially be forever.
To prevent pathological cases, we will consider only
fair
executions for
\mathcal{S}
, i.e. executions
w=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
such that if there are infinitely many
i
such that
\mathcal{S}(\sigma_{0}\dots\sigma_{i-1})\neq\varepsilon
, then there are infinitely many positions in
\Sigma_{S}
. In other words, Environment can delay actions from System, but cannot silence them forever.
Finally, for a given formula
\varphi
, we say that
\mathcal{S}
is
winning
for
\varphi
if all executions which are
\mathcal{S}
-compatible and fair for
\mathcal{S}
satisfy
\varphi
.

In this paper we focus on two specific configurations for processes: when they are shared, and when they are partitioned between players.

Shared.

We say that the processes are
shared
when
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}=\emptyset
, i.e. when all processes can be affected both by System and Environment. The synthesis problem for logic
\mathcal{L}
with shared processes is denoted
\textsc{SharedSynth}(\mathcal{L})
: it amounts, given an alphabet
A
of actions and a formula
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}
, to decide whether there exists a winning strategy
\mathcal{S}
for
\varphi
with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}=\emptyset
and some (finite)
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
. Since the identity of the shared processes does not matter and only the cardinality of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
is relevant, we say in that case that
\mathcal{S}
is a
|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}|
-winning strategy for
\varphi
.

Partitioned.

We say that the processes are
partitioned
if
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}=\emptyset
. In that case, each player has their own pool of processes on which they can play, but that their opponent cannot use. The synthesis problem for logic
\mathcal{L}
with partitioned processes is denoted
\textsc{PartSynth}(\mathcal{L})
. As above, it is the problem of deciding, given
A
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}
, whether System has a winning strategy for
\varphi
with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}=\emptyset
and some arbitrary finite sets
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
. Similarly, we say in that case that
\mathcal{S}
is a
(|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}|,|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}|)
-winning strategy for
\varphi
.

Known results.

Data words were introduced in
[
2
]
. Bojańczyk et al. proved that the satisfiability problem for
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<,+1]
on data words is decidable. Note that this corresponds to the synthesis problem for
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<,+1]
when both
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}}
are empty. They also showed that as soon as a third variable is available, this problem becomes undecidable, even without the order (i.e. for
\textsc{FO}^{3}[\sim,+1]
).

Decidability of the satisfiability problem for the two-variable logic in this setting is what prompted Bérard et al. to consider the synthesis problem on data words, for serveral fragments of first order logic
[
1
]
. They proved that the synthesis problem for
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
is decidable when processes are partitioned and when the number of Environment processes is fixed. In constrast, they established undecidability results for
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
and
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<,+1]
when processes are shared.

\textsc{FO}[\sim]
with processes partitioned between players

First, we turn to the case where processes are partitioned between System and Environment. It has been shown in
[
1
]
that in that case, the synthesis problem for
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
is decidable when System has an arbitrary number of processes, but Environment only has access to a fixed number of processes. We extend this result by lifting this restriction: we show in Theorem

that
\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}[\sim])
is decidable.

As a first step towards proving this result, let us show that beyond a certain threshold (which depends only on the formula), having access to more processes in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
is always a boon for the Environment. Note that this is note true for small cardinalities of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}
: it is not hard to design a game where Environment wins if
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}=\emptyset
but loses as soon as
|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{E}}|\geq 1
.

We now have the tools to prove our main decidability result:

Theorem 2
.

\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}[\sim])
is decidable.

Proof.

Let
(A,\varphi)
be an input for
\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}[\sim])
, and let
n^{\text{min}}_{\text{E}}
be the corresponding integer whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
1
.

Theorem 15 from
[
1
]
states that there exists some integer
n^{\text{min}}_{\text{S}}
such that System has a
(n^{\text{min}}_{\text{S}},n^{\text{min}}_{\text{E}})
-winning strategy for
\varphi
iff System has a
(n_{S},n^{\text{min}}_{\text{E}})
-winning strategy for
\varphi
for any (every)
n_{S}\geq n^{\text{min}}_{\text{S}}
.

We argue in the following that System has an
(n_{S},n_{E})
-winning strategy for
\varphi
for some
n_{S},n_{E}\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if System has an
(n_{S},n_{E})
-winning strategy for
\varphi
for some
n_{S}\leq n^{\text{min}}_{\text{S}}
and

. Thus there are only a finite number of process configurations to explore in order to decide whether System has a
(n_{S},n_{E})
-winning strategy for
\varphi
for some

. When the numbers

and

of processes are fixed, one can duplicate the letter from the alphabet (for instance, multiplying

by the number of System processes), and reduce the synthesis problem on data words to the decidable synthesis problem on plain words

, and thus decide the problem in each of this finitely many fixed configurations. It is important to note that the bounds

and

are computable.

Let us now show that one need not look further than

-winning strategy for

and

.

•

If

•

If
n_{S}>n^{\text{min}}_{\text{S}}
and

, then in virtue of Lemma

(n_{S},n^{\text{min}}_{\text{E}})
-winning strategy for
\varphi

[
1

4
Undecidability results

When System and Environment processes are partitioned, we have seen that when one is only allowed to check whether two positions belong to the same process, the synthesis problem is decidable for
FO
. In this section, we show that as soon as we are able to compare the relative positions of two processes, this is no longer the case, even when restricting ourselves to the two-variable setting, and when having access only to one positional relations (
<
or
+1

\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<])
(Theorem
3
) and
\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,+1])
(Theorem
4
) are undecidable.

When processes are shared, the prospect is even darker: it was shown in
[
1
]
that the synthesis problem in undecidable for
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
. We argue that this is already the case in the two-variable fragment:
\textsc{SharedSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim])
is already undecidable (Theorem
5
).

4.1
Undecidability of
\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<])
and
\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,+1])

Let us start by considering the case where the only positional relation is the order. We show in the following that when System and Environment processes are separated, the synthesis problem is undecidable in this setting.

Theorem 3
.

\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<])
is undecidable.

Proof.

We prove this theorem by reduction from the halting problem for two-counter Minsky machines.
A
two-counter Minsky machine

has a finite set of states
\mathcal{Q}
(containing an initial state
q_{0}
and an halting state
q_{h}

and

where
\mathcal{T}^{++}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}

\mathcal{T}^{--}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}

c_{i}

. A

of

is a triple

, where

indicates the current state of the machine and

the value of counter
c_{i}
. A

is a sequence starting in the initial configuration
(q_{0},0,0)
, and such that two successive configurations

and

satisfy the following condition: either

for any

[
8
]

Given such a machine

, we exhibit a formula

, computable from

, with the following property: there exists a halting run for

iff System has an
(n_{S},1)

We consider the signature
A:=A_{E}\uplus A_{S}
where
A_{E}:=\{\text{ok}_{E},\text{ko}_{E}\}

\begin{cases}t_{0}:q_{0}\xrightarrow{c_{0}++}q_{0}\\

The halting run (

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(0,\text{ok}_{S})}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(\bullet,\text{ok}_{E})}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\displaystyle(0,q_{0})(0,t_{0})(0,\text{inc}_{0})(0,\text{ok}_{S})}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(\bullet,\text{ok}_{E})}

\text{ok}_{S}
by System followed by an
\text{ok}_{E}
by Environment.

Following these two letters, we get a succession of the pattern

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\_,q)(\_,t)(\_,l)(\_,\text{ok}_{S})}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(\_,\text{ok}_{E})}

is equal to the number of System processes on which an

has been played, but no

\text{inc}_{i}

\mathcal{T}^{--}_{i}

\text{inc}_{i}
has been played, but no
\text{dec}_{i}

is not zero - that is, if there exists a process on which an
\text{inc}_{i}
and no

have been played.

Let us now explain how players are prevented from cheating to their advantage. The mechanism of fraud prevention is always the same: if System cheats, then Environment immediately responds by playing
\text{ko}_{E}
, and on the other way around, System plays
\text{ok}_{S}
when detecting a fraud from Environment. Note that there is by definition an asymmetry between the players, and that the

Environment can cheat in only three ways:

[E1]

[E2]

by playing multiple

[E3]

by playing an
\text{ok}_{E}

Let us now described the ways System can cheat:

[S1]

[S2]

[S3]

[S4]

by playing some

[S5]

by playing an
\text{inc}_{i}

\text{dec}_{i}
or

[S6]

by playing
\text{inc}_{i}

\text{inc}_{i}

[S7]

by playing
\text{dec}_{i}
on a process on which an
\text{dec}_{i}
has already been played

[S8]

by playing
\text{dec}_{i}

\text{inc}_{i}

[S9]

On top of that,

ensures a player loses the game if they refuse to play when their turn comes.

The following notations will be useful:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{Q}(x)&:=\quad\bigvee_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ q(x)\\

The following formula
\Phi_{\text{ko}_{S}}
is satisfied when there is an irregularity on Environment’s part since System last played an
\text{ok}_{S}

[E2]

[E3]

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{ko}_{S}}:=\exists x,

\displaystyle\text{ok}_{S}(x)\land\big(\forall y,x<y\to\neg\text{ok}_{S}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\land\exists y,x<y\land\text{ok}_{E}(y)\land\Big[\big(\exists x,y<x\land\text{ok}_{E}(y)\big)\lor\neg\big(\exists y,x<y\land\text{ok}_{S}(y)\big)\Big]

\text{ok}_{E}

will ensure that Environment wins if they output a

while

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{ko}_{E}}:=\exists x,

\displaystyle\text{ok}_{E}(x)\land\big(\forall y,x<y\to\neg\text{ok}_{E}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\land\Big[\Psi_{\text{bad sequence}}(x)\lor\Psi_{\text{bad target}}(x)\lor\Psi_{\text{bad source}}(x)\lor\Psi_{\text{bad upkeep}}(x)\lor\Psi_{\text{bad zero test}}(x)\Big]

[S3]

[S8]

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{bad sequence}}(x):=

\displaystyle\exists y>x,\ \begin{aligned} \Big[\quad&\mathcal{Q}(y)\ \land\ \exists x>y,\mathcal{Q}(x)\\
\lor\ &\mathcal{T}(y)\ \land\ \exists x>y,\big(\mathcal{Q}(x)\lor\mathcal{T}(x)\big)\\

\displaystyle\lor\ \big(\exists y>x,\ A_{S}(y)\big)\ \land\ \neg\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{Q}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\lor\ \big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{U}(y)\ \lor\ \text{ok}_{S}(y)\big)\ \land\ \neg\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{T}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\lor\ \big(\exists y>x,\ \text{ok}_{S}(y)\big)\ \land\ \neg\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{U}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\neg\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{T}(y)\big)\ \land\ \bigvee_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big[\big(\exists y>x,\ q(y)\big)\ \land\ \exists x,\big([\forall y>x,\ \neg\mathcal{T}(y)]\ \land\ \bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathcal{T}\text{ doesn't}\\

\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\quad\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathcal{T}\text{ doesn't}\\

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=0,1}\begin{aligned} \Big[\quad&\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{T}^{++}_{i}(y)\big)\ \land\ \big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{U}(y)\land\neg\text{inc}_{i}(y)\big)\\

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=0,1}\Big[\big(\exists y>x,\ \mathcal{T}^{==0}_{i}(y)\big)\ \land\ \exists x,\big[\text{inc}_{i}(x)\ \land\ \neg(\exists y,\ y\sim x\ \land\ \text{dec}_{i}(x))\big]\Big]

\displaystyle\varphi_{M}:=

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{bad prefix E}}\ \lor\ \Phi_{\text{E blocks}}\ \lor\ \Phi_{\text{E plays after ko}}\ \lor\ \big[\exists x,\text{ko}_{S}(x)\land\Phi_{\text{ko}_{S}}\big]

.
First, consider a halting run of

. It is straighforward to see that System has an

-winning strategy for
\varphi_{M}
, where

is the total number of transition of

used in the run (or

if no such transitions are used): start by playing
\text{ok}_{S}
on any process, and wait for Environment to play
\text{ok}_{E}
on their process. Once this is done, play according to the run, in the sequence

(taking care to play
\text{inc}_{i}
on a new process each time, and
\text{dec}_{i}
on a process which has an
\text{inc}_{i}
but no
\text{dec}_{i}
), and after each such sequence wait for the acknowledgement from Environment, in the form of an
\text{ok}_{E}
. Then stop when reaching
q_{h}
. If Environment blocks, block as well; if Environment deviates by playing
\text{ok}_{E}
too soon, play
\text{ko}_{S}
as soon as possible and then block; if Environment plays
\text{ko}_{E}
, block. It is easily shown by induction on the length of the run that such a strategy is winning for
\varphi_{M}
, and indeed require only
n_{S}
processes.

Conversely, suppose that System has an
(n_{S},1)
-winning strategy for
\varphi_{M}
, for some

. We argue this entails the existence of a halting run in
M
. Let us inspect the data word
w
produced by this winning strategy against an Environment which respects the aforementioned rules (namely, which waits their turn to play
\text{ok}_{E}
, unless System cheats, i.e. if the current data word satisfies
\Phi_{\text{ko}_{E}}
, in which case Environment plays a
\text{ko}_{E}
and stops). Since Environment respects the format and makes no false allegation,
\varphi_{M}
cannot be satisfied because of the subformulas
\Phi_{\text{bad prefix E}}
,
\Phi_{\text{E blocks}}
,
\Phi_{\text{E plays after ko}}
or

. Hence, the second part of
\varphi_{M}
must hold:
\Phi_{\text{bad prefix S}}
,
\Phi_{\text{S blocks}}
and
\Phi_{\text{S plays after ko}}
are false, no
\text{ko}_{E}
was played, and

holds: System’s part of
w
must start with an
\text{ok}_{S}
, followed by a sequence of patterns
q\to t\to\text{inc}_{i}/\text{dec}_{i}/\text{noop}\to\text{ok}_{S}
interspered with
\text{ok}_{E}
from Environment, until it reaches a position marked with
q_{h}
. Since Environment played no
\text{ko}_{E}
, at no point was
\Phi_{\text{ko}_{E}}
satisfied, which means that one can reproduce in a run of
M
the sequence of states and transitions played by System in

: by induction on the number of patterns

played by System, one shows that there is a corresponding run in

whose last configuration is
(q,v_{0},v_{1})
, where

is the last letter from
\mathcal{Q}
played by System and

is the number of processes from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{S}}
marked with one
\text{inc}_{i}
and no
\text{dec}_{i}

\mathcal{T}^{--}_{i}
and
\mathcal{T}^{==0}_{i}
comes from the fact
\Psi_{\text{bad upkeep}}(x)
and
\Psi_{\text{bad zero test}}(x)
were never satisfied, for otherwise Environment would have immediately played a
\text{ko}_{E}
. Considering the whole of

, we get a run of
M
ending in
q_{h}
, thus concluding the proof.
∎∎

Let us now argue that this problem remains undecidable if one has access to the successor relation on positions, rather that to the order itself:

Theorem 4
.

\textsc{PartSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,+1])
is undecidable.

First of all, note that Theorems
3
and
4
are not derivable from one another, as
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<]
and
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,+1]
have orthogonal expressive power: with only two variables, the successor relation is not definable from the order.

Sketch of proof.

The main idea is to note that in the proof of Theorem
3
, the encoding of a run follows a sequence of patterns of size 5 (
q\to t\to\text{inc}_{i}/\text{dec}_{i}/\text{noop}\to\text{ok}_{S}\to\text{ok}_{E}
). Furthermore, as soon as an
\text{ok}_{E}
or
\text{ok}_{S}
is issued, the suspected violation can be found in the positions immediately preceding it (in the case of
\text{ok}_{E}
) or is easily seen globally (in the case of an
\text{ok}_{S}
issued to report an instance of
[E2]
or
[E3]
, which can be delayed for arbitraily long by Environment). As a consequence of this locality, all the formulas in the proof can be reformulated with
+1
instead of
<
.
∎∎

4.2
Undecidability of
\textsc{SharedSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim])

Let us now turn to the case where processes are shared between both players. In this context, not much can be done:
\textsc{SharedSynth}(\textsc{FO}[\sim])
was shown to be undecidable in
[
1
]
.
As we now show, this problem is already undecidable when we restrict ourselves to two variables.

Theorem 5
.

\textsc{SharedSynth}(\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim])
is undecidable.

Sketch of proof.

We give a guideline to adapt the proof of undecidability of
\textsc{SharedSynth}(\textsc{FO}[\sim])
(
[
1
]
, Theorem 17) to the case where only two variables are available.
That proof was a reduction from the halting problem for two-counter Minsky machines, but in a way that is quite different from the reduction from the proof of Theorem
3
: the idea was to encode the value of each counter as the number of processes having a particular number of actions played by both System and Environment.
Namely, to increment a counter, System and Environment would cooperate to pick a fresh process and perform two
a
’s (from System) and two
b
’s (from Environment) on it.
To decrement the same counter, one such process would further receive two additional
a
’s and
b
’s, and from this point on this particular process would never be involved again.
A limited kind of alternation enforced by the formula made sure System and Environment followed this construction; otherwise they immediately lost the game.

The winning condition in the previous proof was given as a condition on the locations of the corresponding parameterized vector game, but it is straightforward to go back to
\textsc{FO}[\sim]
formulas from those.
The number of variables needed depends on the largest value appearing in the count of the number of letters for a given process or in the count of the number of processes for a given location.
For instance, we need two different variables to express that a process has at least two
a
’s, or that it has exactly one
a
(by stating it has at least one and not at least two):

\varphi_{|a|\geq 2}(x):=\exists y,\ y\sim x\ \land\ a(y)\ \land\ \exists x,\ x\sim y\ \land x\neq y\ \land\ a(x)\,.

Similarly if we want to specify that there are at least two such processes then we need at least two different variables.

The counting of processes for a given location is not a problem for the proof in the case of
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim]
, as we only check for the existence of exactly zero (one variable needed), exactly one (two variables needed), at least zero (no variable needed), and at least one (one variable needed) processes in a particular location.
The only hurdle lies in the counting of actions, as we need to count up to four occurrences for each letter (as seen above, four
a
’s and four
b
’s).
The trick to overcome this difficulty is to use multiple letters to play the same role, instead of just one:
a
is split into
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, and
b
into
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
.
And indeed, although it was not possible in
\textsc{FO}^{2}
to express the existence of a process with four
a
’s or more, we can now use a formula stating the existence of a process with at least two
a_{1}
’s and two
a_{2}
’s with only two variables:
\varphi_{|a_{1}|\geq 2}(x)\land\varphi_{|a_{2}|\geq 2}(x)
.
The incrementation of a counter is now encoded by playing exactly one
a_{1}
, one
a_{2}
, one
b_{1}
, and one
b_{2}
(instead of exactly two
a
’s and two
b
’s) on a new process, and to decrement a counter, we double the count of each letter as was done previously.
We now only need to count the number of occurrences of each letter up to two, which can be done with two variables instead of four.
The rest of the proof is unchanged.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have answered the questions left open in
[
1
]
. It appears that when positions between two processes can be compared, the synthesis problem quickly becomes undecidable. As a next step, it thus seems natural to consider the case of partitioned processes for an intermediate logic between
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim]
and
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\sim,<]
:
\textsc{FO}^{2}[\lesssim]
, where one can compare only positions pertaining to the same process.
