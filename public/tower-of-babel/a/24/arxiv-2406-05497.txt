Holonomy of Parabolic Geometries Near Isolated Higher-Order Fixed Points
Jacob W. ERICKSON

Abstract
For Cartan geometries admitting an automorphism with isotropy satisfying a particular, loosely dynamical property on their model geometries, we demonstrate the existence of an open subset of the geometry with trivial holonomy. This property, which generalizes characteristics of isotropies corresponding to isolated higher-order fixed points in parabolic geometries that are known to require a nearby open subset to have vanishing curvature, only relies upon the behavior of the isotropy in the model geometry, and therefore applies regardless of initial curvature assumptions, such as regularity or normality. Along the way to proving our main results, we also derive a couple of results for working with holonomy, relating to limits of sequences of developments and the existence of antidevelopments, that are useful in their own right. To showcase the effectiveness of the techniques developed, we use them to completely characterize all almost c-projective and almost quaternionic structures that admit a nontrivial automorphism with a higher-order fixed point, as well as all nondegenerate partially integrable almost CR structures that admit a higher-order fixed point with non-null isotropy.

Introduction

In each dimension greater than two, a celebrated theorem of Ferrand and Obata tells us that, up to conformal isomorphism, there are only two examples of conformal structures of Riemannian signature admitting automorphism groups that act non-properly: the standard conformal sphere and Euclidean space. A similar result, due to Schoen and Webster, holds for strictly pseudo-convex CR structures: if the CR automorphisms of such a structure act non-properly, then it must be isomorphic to either the standard CR structure on the sphere or a left-invariant CR structure on the Heisenberg group. Eventually, it was realized that Cartan geometries—a class of geometric structures that extend the spirit of Klein’s Erlangen program somewhat analogously to the way that Riemannian manifolds extend Euclidean geometry—are the natural setting for this type of result, and in
[
7
]
, Frances gave a generalization of both results, extending the idea to all parabolic Cartan geometries of real rank one, assuming the curvature satisfies a standard regularity condition. A convenient and concise history of this result can be found in
[
13
]
.

​These Ferrand–Obata type theorems provide striking examples where the global structure of a given Cartan geometry is almost completely determined by the behavior of its automorphisms. Naturally, though, we are led to ask: how far can we take this? How much can we say about the global structure of a Cartan geometry just from the behavior of its automorphisms, or even from the behavior of a
single
automorphism?

In the vein of this latter question, we specifically want to look at automorphisms of parabolic geometries with
higher-order fixed points
. These are automorphisms with isotropies, over some fixed point of the geometry, lying in the unipotent radical of the parabolic subgroup used as the stabilizer subgroup for the corresponding model geometry. For many of the most common types of parabolic geometries, such as projective and conformal geometry, these just amount to automorphisms whose derivatives on the base manifold are the identity transformation on the tangent space over some fixed point.

The existence of a higher-order fixed point often guarantees that the curvature of the geometry vanishes over some open subset containing the higher-order fixed point in its closure. Results in this direction essentially go all the way back to
[
15
]
in the projective case and
[
9
]
in the conformal case, though a more unified approach was introduced in
[
2
]
and expanded upon in
[
11
,
14
]
, among other places. Notably, however, these prior results are all local in nature, unless we make quite strong assumptions on the geometry, such as real-analyticity or—as explored in
[
12
,
15
]
—metrizability.

In this paper, we will provide a way to extend these existing local results to semi-global ones when the higher-order fixed point is isolated. Our main result, Theorem
4.4
, proves that if an automorphism has isotropy satisfying a kind of dynamical property in the model geometry that applies to the cases with isolated higher-order fixed points where local results are known, then there is a certain invariant open subset of the geometry on which the
holonomy
must be trivial. If we know a bit more about the topology of this open subset, then we can even prove genuine global results along the lines of Ferrand–Obata type theorems. To showcase this, we specifically prove such results for c-projective structures, quaternionic structures, and CR structures; as a courtesy to those unfamiliar with Cartan geometries, we state these theorems in their “native” languages, though in the rest of the paper, we will almost exclusively work from the unified perspective provided by the Cartan machinery.

Theorem A
.

Suppose that
(M,J,[\nabla])
is an almost c-projective structure.
If
(M,J,[\nabla])
admits a nontrivial automorphism
\alpha
with a higher-order fixed point

\mathbb{CP}^{m}

.

2.1
Relevant model geometries

Cartan geometries are modeled on homogeneous geometries in the sense of Klein, though we present these geometries in a way that emphasizes the role of the Lie group as a principal bundle over the homogeneous space.

Definition 2.1
.

A

(G,H)

is a Lie group and

The models relevant to Theorems

2.2
Generalities for Cartan geometries

In essence, the idea of a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
is to specify a
\mathfrak{g}

\omega_{{}_{G}}\colon X_{g}\in T_{g}G\mapsto\operatorname{L}_{g^{-1}*}X_{g}\in\mathfrak{g}

.

Let
(G,H)
be a model. A

over a (smooth) manifold

({G},\omega)
, where

is a principal

q_{{}_{H}}\colon{G}\to M
and
\omega

\mathfrak{g}

such that

•

Throughout, we will want to compare different Cartan geometries of the same type. To do this, we will use local diffeomorphisms that preserve the Cartan-geometric structure, called
geometric maps
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given two Cartan geometries
({G},\omega)
and
({Q},\upsilon)
of type
(G,H)
, a
geometric map

\varphi\colon({G},\omega)\to({Q},\upsilon)
is an
H
-equivariant smooth map
\varphi\colon{G}\to{Q}
such that
\varphi^{*}\upsilon=\omega
.

A geometric map
\varphi\colon({G},\omega)\to({Q},\upsilon)
always induces a corresponding local diffeomorphism between the base manifolds of
{G}
and
{Q}
, given by
q_{{}_{H}}({g})\mapsto q_{{}_{H}}(\varphi({g}))
for each
{g}\in{G}
. We find it convenient and natural to not waste symbols to distinguish between these maps; throughout, whenever we have a geometric map
\varphi
, we will also denote its induced map on the base manifolds by the same symbol
\varphi
. The meaning should always be clear from context.

Of course, some geometric maps tell us more than others. We say that a geometric map
\varphi\colon({G},\omega)\to({Q},\upsilon)
is a
geometric embedding
when
\varphi
is injective, and when
\varphi
is bijective, we further say that it is a
(geometric) isomorphism
. A geometric isomorphism from
({G},\omega)
to itself is then called a
(geometric) automorphism
.

Geometric maps are quite rigid: if the base manifold of
({G},\omega)
is connected, then a geometric map
\varphi\colon({G},\omega)\to({Q},\upsilon)
is uniquely determined by the image
\varphi({g})
of an arbitrary single element
{g}\in{G}
. As such, the
automorphism group

\Aut({G},\omega)
, consisting of all automorphisms of
({G},\omega)
, will act freely on
{G}
.

Following our convention of not distinguishing between geometric maps and the corresponding induced maps on the base manifolds, when we talk about fixed points of an automorphism
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
, we will mean fixed points of the induced map on the base manifold; since
\Aut({G},\omega)
acts freely, automorphisms will not have actual fixed points in the bundle
{G}
. We denote by

\mathrm{Fix}_{M}(\alpha):=\{q_{{}_{H}}({g})\in M\mid\alpha(q_{{}_{H}}({g}))=q_{{}_{H}}({g})\}

the set of fixed points for
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
.

For each
{g}\in{G}
such that
q_{{}_{H}}({g})\in\mathrm{Fix}_{M}(\alpha)
, there is some
a\in H
such that
\alpha({g})={g}a
. This element
a\in H
is called the
isotropy
of
\alpha
at
{g}
.

Definition 2.6
.

For
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
and
{e}\in{G}
such that
\alpha({e})\in{e}H
, the
isotropy
of
\alpha
at
{e}
is the unique element
a\in H
such that
\alpha({e})={e}a
.

For the applications considered in the paper, we will primarily focus on automorphisms
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
of parabolic geometries
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,P)
with isotropy
a\in P_{+}<P
at some
{e}\in{G}
. In that case, we say that
\alpha
has a
higher-order fixed point
at
q_{{}_{P}}({e})
.

Another core idea for Cartan geometries is that of
curvature
, which tells us when the geometry locally differs from the Klein geometry.

Definition 2.7
.

Given a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
, its
curvature
is the
\mathfrak{g}
-valued two-form given by
\Omega:=\mathrm{d}\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}[\omega,\omega]
.

When the curvature vanishes in a neighborhood of a point, then the geometry is locally equivalent to that of the Klein geometry near that point. In other words, when
\Omega
vanishes on some neighborhood of an element
{e}\in{G}
, we can find a geometric embedding

\psi\colon\ \bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U),\omega_{{}_{G}}\bigr)\hookrightarrow({G},\omega)

from a neighborhood
q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)
of the identity
e\in G
in the Klein geometry such that
\psi(e)={e}
. When the curvature vanishes everywhere, we say that the geometry is
flat
; such geometries are equivalent to locally homogeneous geometric structures in the sense of
[
10
]
.

2.3
Development and holonomy

Again, we recommend
[
5
]
for an overview of our techniques involving holonomy, as well as
[
16
, Chapter 3, Section 7]
for a review of basic results on developments of paths.

We would like to pretend that Cartan geometries
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
“are” their model geometries. The notions of
development
and
holonomy
allow us to do this somewhat judiciously.

Definition 2.8
.

Given a (piecewise smooth)
3
3

3

Throughout, whenever we refer to a “path”, we will always mean a piecewise smooth path.
path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
in a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
, the
development

\gamma_{G}
of
\gamma
is the unique (piecewise smooth) path
\gamma_{G}\colon[0,1]\to G
such that
\gamma_{G}(0)=e
and
\gamma^{*}\omega=\gamma_{G}^{*}\omega_{G}
.

Development allows us to identify paths in a Cartan geometry with paths in the model, and if we fix a pretend “identity element”
{e}\in{G}
, we can even give a kind of correspondence between elements of
{G}
and elements of
G
.

Definition 2.9
.

For a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
and points
{e},{g}\in{G}
, we say that
g\in G
is a
development of
{g}
from
{e}
if and only if there exists a path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
and
h\in H
such that
\gamma(0)={e}
,
\gamma(1)h={g}
, and
\gamma_{G}(1)h=g
.

Developments of elements in
{G}
are usually not unique. Thankfully, the
holonomy group

\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
tells us precisely how this happens: if
g
is a development of
{g}
from
{e}
, then the set of all possible developments from
{e}
to
{g}
is precisely
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)g
.

Definition 2.10
.

For a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
, the
holonomy group
of
({G},\omega)
at
{e}\in{G}
is the subgroup of
G
given by

\Hol_{e}({G},\omega):=\left\{\gamma_{G}(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}\in G\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}\text{ is a path such that,}\\
\text{for }h_{\gamma}\in H,\,\gamma(0)={e}=\gamma(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}\end{array}\right\}.

Because
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
completely encapsulates the ambiguity in taking developments from
{e}
, we can assign unique developments to elements of
{G}
when
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)=\{e\}
. In other words, whenever
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)=\{e\}
, we get a geometric map
\mathrm{dev}_{e}\colon({G},\omega)\to(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
, which we could reasonably call the
developing map based at
{e}
, given by
\gamma(1)h\mapsto\gamma_{G}(1)h
for (piecewise smooth) paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
starting at
\gamma(0)={e}
and
h\in H
. When the geometry is flat and the base manifold is simply connected, the holonomy group is always trivial, and the induced map of
\mathrm{dev}_{e}
on the base manifold is precisely the developing map in the usual sense for locally homogeneous geometric structures.

Crucially, it follows that the curvature vanishes over an open set if and only if loops that are null-homotopic in that open set have trivial holonomy.

3
Some useful tools for working with holonomy

To make appropriate use of holonomy and development for our present purposes, we will need a way to tell when a sequence of holonomies converges, as well as a way to reverse the process of development for sufficiently small paths. In this section, we will provide a couple of results that will allow us to do this, which are useful in their own right and will almost certainly see significant use in subsequent works.

3.1
Convergence of developments

For a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
, an inner product
\mathrm{g}
on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
determines a Riemannian metric
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
on
{G}
given by
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\mathrm{g}(\omega(\xi_{1}),\omega(\xi_{2}))
; we call such a Riemannian metric on
{G}
an
\omega
-constant Riemannian metric
. Given an element
{e}\in{G}
, we can use an arbitrary
\omega
-constant Riemannian metric
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
to imbue the set
\Path_{e}({G})
of (piecewise smooth) paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
in
{G}
starting at
\gamma(0)={e}
with a useful topology.

Definition 3.1
.

The
first-order compact-open topology
on

\Path_{e}({G}):=\bigl\{\gamma\in C^{0}([0,1];{G})\mid\gamma\text{ is a piecewise smooth path and }\gamma(0)={e}\bigr\},

the space of piecewise smooth paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
starting at
\gamma(0)={e}
, is the topology induced by the metric

d_{\mathrm{g}_{\omega}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}(\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}_{\omega}}(\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t)))+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t))-\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t))\|_{\mathrm{g}},

where the second supremum is taken over all
t\in[0,1]
such that both
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t)
and
\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t)
are well defined.

This is essentially just an extension of the usual
C^{1}
compact-open topology on smooth paths so that it is defined on piecewise smooth paths instead.

When we say that a sequence of paths converges in
\Path_{e}({G})
, we will mean that the sequence converges with respect to the first-order compact-open topology.

Lemma 3.2

(continuous dependence on parameters)
.

Suppose
(f_{\lambda})
is a net of functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathfrak{g}
that converges uniformly to some function
f_{\infty}\colon[0,1]\to\mathfrak{g}
. If there exists a solution
\gamma_{\infty}\in\Path_{e}({G})
to the ODE given by
\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{\infty})=f_{\infty}
, and solutions
\gamma_{\lambda}\in\Path_{e}({G})
to
\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{\lambda})=f_{\lambda}
for each
\lambda
, then
(\gamma_{\lambda})
converges to
\gamma_{\infty}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
.

Proof.

As the labeling implies, this result is effectively just a version of the usual “continuous dependence on parameters” lemma that one typically encounters for ODEs, except that it is adapted to our current setting. The main tool we will need is the “fundamental lemma” 1.5.1 from
[
4
, Chapter 2]
, which tells us that, with the norm
\|\cdot\|
on a Banach space
E
, a subset
U\subseteq E
, and an interval
I
, if a function
f\colon I\times U\to E
is
k
-Lipschitz,
\gamma\colon I\to U
solves the ODE given by
\dot{\gamma}(t)=f(t,\gamma(t))
, and
\bar{\gamma}\colon I\to U
satisfies
\|\dot{\bar{\gamma}}(t)-f(t,\bar{\gamma}(t))\|\leq\varepsilon
for all
t\in I
, then for
\tau\in I
, the distance
\|\gamma(t)-\bar{\gamma}(t)\|
satisfies the bound

\|\gamma(t)-\bar{\gamma}(t)\|\leq\|\gamma(\tau)-\bar{\gamma}(\tau)\|{\rm e}^{k|t-\tau|}+\varepsilon\frac{{\rm e}^{k|t-\tau|}-1}{k}.

The strategy, in essence, is just to apply this “fundamental lemma” in finitely many coordinate balls inside charts along
\gamma_{\infty}
. This will show that
\gamma_{\lambda}
converges uniformly to
\gamma_{\infty}
in each of the finitely many balls, hence it converges to
\gamma_{\infty}
uniformly, and since the derivatives converge uniformly by assumption, it follows that
\gamma_{\lambda}
converges to
\gamma_{\infty}
in the first-order compact-open topology.

To be more explicit, let us cover
\gamma_{\infty}([0,1])
in finitely many coordinate balls
B_{0},\dots,B_{n}
, and cover
[0,1]
by closed subintervals
I_{0},\dots,I_{n}
with
\max(I_{i})\in I_{i+1}
and
\gamma_{\infty}(I_{i})\subseteq B_{i}
for each
i
. Since
f_{\infty}(t)
is constant in
{g}\in{G}
, we can find
k_{i}>0
for which the coordinate expressions for the smooth vector fields
\omega^{-1}(f_{\infty}(t))
are
k_{i}
-Lipschitz on
\bar{B}_{i}
with respect to the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{\text{coord}}
induced by the coordinates; for clarity, note that this norm only gives a metric on the chart, not all of
{G}
, but since
\bar{B}_{i}
is compact, the restriction of the coordinate metric to
\bar{B}_{i}
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the restriction of an
\omega
-constant metric, so uniform convergence on
\bar{B}_{i}
with respect to this metric implies uniform convergence with respect to an
\omega
-constant one and vice versa. By compactness of
[0,1]
and
\bar{B}_{i}
,

\varepsilon_{i}(\lambda):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,1]\\
{g}\in\bar{B}_{i}\end{subarray}}\bigl\|\omega_{g}^{-1}(f_{\lambda}(t))-\omega_{g}^{-1}(f_{\infty}(t))\bigr\|_{\text{coord}},

exists for each
i
and
\lambda
, and it converges to
0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
because
f_{\lambda}
converges uniformly to
f_{\infty}
by assumption. Starting with
t_{0}:=0
, so that
\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t_{0})-\gamma_{\infty}(t_{0})\|_{\text{coord}}=0
, the “fundamental lemma” of
[
4
]
tells us that, for
\lambda
sufficiently far along toward
\infty
,

\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t)-\gamma_{\infty}(t)\|_{\text{coord}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}(\lambda)\frac{{\rm e}^{k_{0}t}-1}{k_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}(\lambda)\frac{{\rm e}^{k_{0}}-1}{k_{0}}

for all
t\in I_{0}
, so as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
,
\gamma_{\lambda}|_{I_{0}}
converges uniformly to
\gamma_{\infty}|_{I_{0}}
. Next, choose
t_{1}\in I_{0}\cap I_{1}
; by taking
\lambda
to be sufficiently far along toward
\infty
, we have just seen (now using the coordinate metric in
B_{1}
rather than
B_{0}
) that we can get
\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t_{1})-\gamma_{\infty}(t_{1})\|_{\text{coord}}
as close to
0
as we would like, so using the “fundamental lemma” of
[
4
]
, we get that

\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t)-\gamma_{\infty}(t)\|_{\text{coord}}\leq\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t_{1})-\gamma_{\infty}(t_{1})\|_{\text{coord}}{\rm e}^{k_{1}}+\varepsilon_{1}(\lambda)\frac{{\rm e}^{k_{1}}-1}{k_{1}}

for all
t\in I_{1}
, hence
\gamma_{\lambda}|_{I_{1}}
converges uniformly to
\gamma_{\infty}|_{I_{1}}
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Iteratively, we repeat this process, picking
t_{i}\in I_{i-1}\cap I_{i}
and then using the uniform convergence over
I_{i-1}
and the “fundamental lemma” from
[
4
]
to show that, for
\lambda
sufficiently far along toward
\infty
, we get

\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t)-\gamma_{\infty}(t)\|_{\text{coord}}\leq\|\gamma_{\lambda}(t_{i})-\gamma_{\infty}(t_{i})\|_{\text{coord}}\,{\rm e}^{k_{i}}+\varepsilon_{i}(\lambda)\frac{{\rm e}^{k_{i}}-1}{k_{i}}

for all
t\in I_{i}
, hence
\gamma_{\lambda}|_{I_{i}}
converges uniformly to
\gamma_{\infty}|_{I_{i}}
for each of the finitely many
i
. As noted above, this implies that
\gamma_{\lambda}
converges uniformly to
\gamma_{\infty}
on all of
[0,1]
, and since, by assumption, the derivatives converge uniformly as well,
\gamma_{\lambda}
converges to
\gamma_{\infty}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
.∎

This naturally leads to the following corollary, which seems implicit in much of the existing literature.

Proposition 3.3
.

The development map
(\cdot)_{G}\colon\Path_{e}({G})\to\Path_{e}(G)
given by
\gamma\mapsto\gamma_{G}
is continuous.

Proof.

Since
\Path_{e}({G})
is a metric space, it suffices to prove that if
\gamma_{k}\rightarrow\gamma_{\infty}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
, then
(\gamma_{k})_{G}\rightarrow(\gamma_{\infty})_{G}
in
\Path_{e}(G)
. But
\gamma_{k}\rightarrow\gamma_{\infty}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
implies that
\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{k})=\omega_{{}_{G}}((\dot{\gamma}_{k})_{G})
converges uniformly to
\omega(\dot{\gamma}_{\infty})=\omega_{{}_{G}}((\dot{\gamma}_{\infty})_{G})
, so by Lemma
3.2
,
(\gamma_{k})_{G}\rightarrow(\gamma_{\infty})_{G}
in
\Path_{e}(G)
. ∎

In particular, it follows from Proposition
3.3
that a sequence of paths converging to
{e}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
must have developments converging to the identity in
G
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
(\gamma_{k})
is a sequence in
\Path_{e}({G})
. If
(\gamma_{k})
converges in
\Path_{e}({G})
to the constant path at
{e}
, then the developments
(\gamma_{k})_{G}(1)
converge to the identity element
e\in G
.

For the purposes of using this lemma, it will be helpful to keep two particular facts in mind.

First, the endpoint of the development of a path does not depend on parametrization: if
\tau
is a (piecewise smooth) reparametrization of
[0,1]
with
\tau(0)=0
and
\tau(1)=1
, then
\gamma^{*}\omega=\gamma_{G}^{*}\omega_{G}
implies

(\gamma\circ\tau)^{*}\omega=\tau^{*}\gamma^{*}\omega=\tau^{*}\gamma_{G}^{*}\omega_{G}=(\gamma_{G}\circ\tau)^{*}\omega_{G},

so
(\gamma\circ\tau)_{G}=\gamma_{G}\circ\tau
and
(\gamma\circ\tau)_{G}(1)=\gamma_{G}(\tau(1))=\gamma_{G}(1)
. Therefore, even if a sequence of paths in
\Path_{e}({G})
converges to the constant path
modulo parametrization
, the endpoints of their developments will still converge to the identity in
G
.

Second, the lemma is applicable even when the converging paths we are considering are on the base manifold: if
(\gamma_{k})
is a sequence of paths in
{G}
such that
\gamma_{k}(0)\in{e}H
and the paths
q_{{}_{H}}(\gamma_{k})
in the base manifold converge to the constant path at
q_{{}_{H}}({e})
to first order, then we can find paths
\beta_{k}\colon[0,1]\to H
such that
\gamma_{k}(0)\beta_{k}(0)^{-1}={e}
and
\gamma_{k}\beta_{k}^{-1}\rightarrow{e}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
. In this situation, we should take particular note of how the sequence
(\beta_{k})
of paths in
H
plays a similar role to what we call
ballast sequences
—the original term “holonomy sequence”, as used in
[
2
,
7
,
14
]
, among several other places, is clearly inappropriate given the present context, since they are not sequences of holonomies for the geometry—in Section
4
below: while the paths
\gamma_{k}
might not converge to anything, we can use the paths
\beta_{k}
to counterbalance them so that the end result
\gamma_{k}\beta_{k}^{-1}
still converges.

3.2
Existence of antidevelopments

It would be convenient to be able to reverse the process of development: given a piecewise smooth path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to G
with
\gamma(0)=e
, we would like to be able to construct a corresponding path
\gamma_{G}
in
{G}
such that
\gamma
is the development of
\gamma_{G}
. We can reasonably call such a path—if it exists—an
antidevelopment
of
\gamma
.

Definition 3.5
.

For a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
and a path
\gamma\in\Path_{e}(G)
, an
antidevelopment
for
\gamma
starting at
{e}\in{G}
is a path
\gamma_{G}\in\Path_{e}({G})
such that
(\gamma_{G})_{G}=\gamma
.

Unlike with developments, antidevelopments do not always exist. Conceptually, we can regard this asymmetry as a consequence of the fact that
\omega
-constant metrics
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
for a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
are not necessarily complete, whereas
\omega_{G}
-constant metrics on
G
are always complete. In other words, there will often be paths that “escape”
{G}
despite having finite length with respect to some
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
, but this can never happen for finite-length paths in
G
because
\omega_{G}
-constant metrics on
G
must be complete.

However, if we fix a particular element
{e}\in{G}
and an
\omega
-constant metric
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
, then we can always find a sufficiently small radius
r_{e}>0
for which the metric ball
B_{r_{e}}({e})
has compact closure. This radius will usually depend on the choice of element
{e}
unless
\mathrm{g}_{\omega}
is complete, but it will always be greater than
0
. Paths starting at
{e}
of length less than
r_{e}
will be trapped inside the compact subspace
\overline{B_{r_{e}}({e})}
, so if
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to G
is a path such that
\gamma(0)=e
whose length with respect to
\mathrm{g}_{\omega_{G}}
is less than
r_{e}
, then its antidevelopment
\gamma_{G}
will not be able to escape
\overline{B_{r_{e}}({e})}
. In particular, we have the following.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
and
\mathrm{g}
is an inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
. For each
{e}\in{G}
, there exists an
r_{e}>0
such that every path
\gamma\in\Path_{e}(G)
whose length with respect to
\mathrm{g}_{\omega_{G}}
is less than
r_{e}
has a well-defined antidevelopment
\gamma_{G}
starting at
{e}
.

In summary, sufficiently short paths
\gamma\in\Path_{e}(G)
will always have antidevelopments at a given point
{e}\in{G}
.

4
Isotropies that “ensnare” a region of the model

In
[
2
,
14
]
, the overall strategy for demonstrating local flatness near a given higher-order fixed point was to find an open region of the geometry whose points were pulled toward that higher-order fixed point in a particular way. Here, we will establish two criteria to capture the behavior underlying these results when the higher-order fixed point is
isolated
, meaning that some neighborhood of it contains no other higher-order fixed points with the same isotropy up to conjugation. These criteria—codified in Definition
4.2
—are entirely determined by the behavior of the isotropy in the relevant model geometry, which we will not require to be parabolic in general. Because this behavior only depends on the isotropy, this allows us to transfer this desired behavior to every Cartan geometry admitting an automorphism with such isotropy.

4.1
Criteria for the isotropy

To understand the expected behavior of automorphisms near an isolated higher-order fixed point, which we will take as inspiration for the ideas below, it is useful to look at some examples on low-dimensional model geometries. We have sketched such examples for real and complex projective geometry in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Sketches of automorphisms with an isolated higher-order fixed point for 2-dimensional real projective geometry
(\PGL_{3}\mathbb{R},P)
(left) and 1-dimensional complex projective geometry
(\PGL_{2}\mathbb{C},P)
(right).

Given an automorphism
\alpha
with a higher-order fixed point, the results in
[
2
,
14
]
suggest we should look for particular types of
ballast sequences
to find an open set on which the curvature vanishes.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
be an automorphism for a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
. A sequence
(b_{k})
in
H
is called a
ballast sequence for
\alpha
at
{g}\in{G}
with attractor
{e}\in{G}
if and only if there exists a sequence
({g}_{k})
in
{G}
such that
{g}_{k}\rightarrow{g}
and
\alpha^{k}({g}_{k})b_{k}^{-1}\rightarrow{e}
.

In particular, for isolated higher-order fixed points, the techniques in
[
2
,
14
]
suggest looking for ballast sequences
(b_{k})
contained in the Levi subgroup
G_{0}<P
of our parabolic isotropy
P
such that the restriction
\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}|_{\mathfrak{g}_{-}}
of the adjoint action to
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
converges to
0
. We should think of this as an assumption on the behavior of the derivatives of the automorphism on the tangent bundle over the base manifold
M
. To understand why, suppose that
(b_{k})
is such a ballast sequence at
{g}
, so that
\alpha^{k}({g}_{k})b_{k}^{-1}\rightarrow{e}
for some sequence
({g}_{k})
in
{G}
converging to
{g}
and
\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}(v)\rightarrow 0
for all
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{-}
. For sufficiently small
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{-}
, we therefore get a well-defined sequence of elements
\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}_{k}
converging to
\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}
, where
\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr)
denotes the time-1 flow along the
\omega
-constant vector field
\omega^{-1}(v)
, so that

\alpha^{k}\bigl(\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}_{k}\bigr)b_{k}^{-1}=\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}v)\bigr)\bigl(\alpha^{k}({g}_{k})b_{k}^{-1}\bigr)\rightarrow{e}.

Taking such
v
to be in a bounded neighborhood
V
of
0\in\mathfrak{g}_{-}
, we can make
\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}(V)
as close to
0
as we would like for
k
sufficiently large, so for every neighborhood of
q_{{}_{P}}({e})
, we may find a large enough
k
that
\alpha^{k}q_{{}_{P}}\bigl(\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(V)\bigr){g}_{k}\bigr)
is contained in that neighborhood. In other words, the existence of such a ballast sequence for each
{g}\in q_{{}_{P}}^{-1}(U)
over some open set
U\subseteq M
guarantees that the iterates
\alpha^{k}
, viewed as maps on the base manifold, converge
locally uniformly
to the constant map at
q_{{}_{P}}({e})
on
U
. Moreover, for whatever
w\in\mathfrak{g}_{-}
we choose, we can define
\exp\bigl(t\omega^{-1}(w)\bigr)\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}_{k}
for sufficiently small
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\alpha^{k}\bigl(\exp\bigl(t\omega^{-1}(w)\bigr)\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}_{k}\bigr)b_{k}^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad=\exp\bigl(t\omega^{-1}(\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}w)\bigr)\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}v)\bigr)\bigl(\alpha^{k}({g}_{k})b_{k}^{-1}\bigr)\rightarrow{e};

restricting our possible choices of
w
to a bounded set in
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
, we can again get all
\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{k}}(w)
as close to
0
as we would like, so the corresponding curves
t\mapsto\alpha^{k}\bigl(q_{{}_{P}}\bigl(\exp\bigl(t\omega^{-1}(w)\bigr)\exp\bigl(\omega^{-1}(v)\bigr){g}_{k}\bigr)\bigr)
converge to first order to the constant curve at
q_{{}_{P}}({e})
. Thus, we are looking for automorphisms
\alpha
with a region
U
over which iterates of the derivatives of
\alpha
converge locally uniformly to the zero vector over some point of the base manifold.

Another necessary component in the techniques of
[
2
,
14
]
is the presence of certain paths that get shrunk to a point of the base manifold by the automorphism. The flat regions constructed in those papers are covered by such shrinking paths, so in particular, there should be at least one of them. We will only need to specify one such path, which we will call
\zeta_{U}
, in the base manifold of the model geometry, and moreover, we will not need it to be the quotient image of an exponential path, though (for convenience) we will choose for it to be so in all of the applications in Section
5
.

Combining these two criteria leads us to the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

We say that
a\in H

ensnares
an
a
-invariant connected open subset
U\subseteq G/H
if and only if the following two criteria hold:

•

Over
U
, the derivative maps
a^{k}_{*}\colon T(G/H)\to T(G/H)

4.2
Existence of ensnared region with trivial holonomy

Since we can pull in concatenations of the form
\zeta_{U}\star q_{{}_{H}}(\delta)
to be as short as we want, we can show that they always have well-defined antidevelopments when there exists an automorphism with ensnaring isotropy.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
,
{e}\in{G}
, and
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
such that
\alpha({e})={e}a
for some
a\in H
that ensnares

, then every path of the form
\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta
, where
\hat{\zeta}_{U}\in\Path_{e}(G)
is a lift of
\zeta_{U}
and
\delta\colon[0,1]\to q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)
such that
\delta(0)=\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)
, has a well-defined antidevelopment
\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}
starting at
{e}
.

Proof.

Since
a^{k}((\zeta_{U}\circ\tau_{k})\star q_{{}_{H}}(\delta))=a^{k}q_{{}_{H}}\bigl(\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\circ\tau_{k}\bigr)\!\star\delta\bigr)
converges to the constant path at
q_{{}_{H}}(e)
to first order, there exist
\beta_{k}\colon[0,1]\to H
such that
\beta_{k}(0)=a^{k}
and
a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}
converges, modulo parametrization, to the constant path at

in
\Path_{e}(G)
. As a result, for each
r>0
, there is some
k\geq 0
for which
a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}
has length less than

By Lemma

, there exists an

for which all paths in

of length less than

have well-defined antidevelopments starting at

. Thus,

has an antidevelopment
\bigl(a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)_{G}
starting at
{e}
for all sufficiently large

, hence

\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}:=\alpha^{-k}\bigl(a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)_{G}\beta_{k}

is a well-defined path starting at
\alpha^{-k}({e})\beta_{k}(0)={e}a^{-k}a^{k}={e}
with development

\displaystyle\bigl(\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}\bigr)_{G}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha^{-k}\bigl(a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)_{G}\beta_{k}\bigr)_{G}=\beta_{k}(0)^{-1}\bigl(\bigl(a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)_{G}\bigr)_{G}\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=a^{-k}\bigl(a^{k}\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)\beta_{k}=\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta.

Whenever
({G},\omega)
has an automorphism
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
with isotropy

, we can use Lemma

to construct an

given by the endpoints of antidevelopments for concatenations of the form
\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta
together with their right-translations by elements of

. Note that

is necessarily an open subset of
{G}
: for each
\delta(1)\in q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)

\delta(1)
stay in the open set

, so each

q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}
.

We should think of
q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}
as the analogue in
{G}
for the open subset
q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)
in

to what

does for the ensnaring isotropy

. Indeed, as we mentioned in the proof of the lemma, each path of the form
\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta

\beta_{k}\colon[0,1]\to H

in

modulo parametrization. Consequently, the derivatives

on the base manifold must converge locally uniformly to

over the connected open set

implies

—as necessarily happens when

has trivial holonomy.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
with an automorphism
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
and an element
{e}\in{G}
such that
\alpha({e})={e}a
for some
a\in H
ensnaring
U\subseteq G/H
. If
a^{k}ga^{-k}\rightarrow e
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
implies
g=e
, then
\Hol_{(\hat{\zeta}_{U})_{G}(1)}\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G},\omega\bigr)=\{e\}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}
is a path such that

\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)h^{-1}=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)_{G}(1)

for some
h\in H
. We want to show that
\gamma_{G}(1)h^{-1}=e
. To do this, consider the path

\gamma_{\zeta_{U}}:=(\hat{\zeta}_{U})_{G}\star\gamma\star\operatorname{R}_{h}\overline{(\hat{\zeta}_{U})}_{G},

where
\overline{\eta}
is the
reverse
of the path
\eta
, given by
\overline{\eta}(t):=\eta(1-t)
, and
\operatorname{R}_{h}
denotes right-translation by
h
.

Since
\alpha^{k}_{*}\rightarrow 0_{q_{{}_{H}}({e})}
locally uniformly over
U_{G}
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, the paths
\alpha^{k}q_{{}_{H}}(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})
converge, modulo parametrization, to
q_{{}_{H}}({e})
to first order as before, so there exist paths
\beta_{k}\colon[0,1]\to H
with
\beta_{k}(0)=a^{k}
such that, again modulo parametrization,
\alpha^{k}\gamma_{\zeta_{U}}\beta_{k}^{-1}
converges to
{e}
in
\Path_{e}({G})
. Thus, by Lemma
3.4
, the developments

\bigl(\alpha^{k}\gamma_{\zeta_{U}}\beta_{k}^{-1}\bigr)_{G}(1)=\beta_{k}(0)(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}=a^{k}(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}

must converge to the identity element
e\in G
. Note, in particular, that the endpoint

\displaystyle\alpha^{k}(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}}(1))\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}=\alpha^{k}\bigl(\operatorname{R}_{h}\overline{\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)}_{G}(1)\bigr)\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}=\alpha^{k}({e}h)\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}={e}a^{k}h\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}

converges to
{e}
, so
a^{k}h\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}\rightarrow e
, hence

a^{k}\bigl((\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)h^{-1}\bigr)a^{-k}=(a^{k}(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)\beta_{k}(1)^{-1})\bigl(a^{k}h\beta_{k}(1)^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}\rightarrow ee^{-1}=e.

But by hypothesis, this implies that
(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)h^{-1}=e
, and

\displaystyle(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)_{G}\star\gamma\star\operatorname{R}_{h}\overline{\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)}_{G}\bigr)_{G}(1)=\bigl(\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)_{G}\bigr)_{G}\!(1)\gamma_{G}(1)\bigl(\operatorname{R}_{h}\overline{\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)}_{G}\bigr)_{G}(1)

\displaystyle=\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)\gamma_{G}(1)h^{-1}\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)^{-1}h,

so
\gamma_{G}(1)h^{-1}=\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)^{-1}(\gamma_{\zeta_{U}})_{G}(1)h^{-1}\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)=\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)^{-1}\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)=e
.
∎

It is, perhaps, worth explicitly pointing out that Theorem
4.4
starkly improves previous results that exhibit local flatness near an isolated higher-order fixed point: with considerably weaker assumptions, we have still obtained a large open subset

containing

in its closure, and not only does the curvature vanish on this subset, but its holonomy is trivial, even without additional curvature restrictions or considerations other than the behavior of the isotropy on the model geometry. Moreover, if we also happen to know a bit about the topology of

Corollary 4.5
.

In the setting of Theorem
4.4
, if

is simply connected, then there exists a geometric embedding
\sigma\colon\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U),\omega_{{}_{G}}\bigr)\hookrightarrow({G},\omega)
with
\sigma\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)\bigr)=q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}
.

Proof.

Naturally, we want to try to define
\sigma\colon q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)\to q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}\subseteq{G}
by

\sigma(\delta(1)h):=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}(1)h

for each path
\delta\in\Path_{\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)}\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)\bigr)
and each

. If
\sigma
is well defined, then it is
H

\omega(\sigma_{*}\dot{\delta}(1))=\omega_{{}_{G}}(\dot{\delta}(1))
by definition, so it is necessarily geometric. It remains, then, to prove that
\sigma
is well defined and that
\sigma
is injective.

If we have two paths
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\Path_{\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)}\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)\bigr)
with the same endpoint

, then we can concatenate
\delta_{1}
with the reverse of
\delta_{2}
to get a loop
\delta_{1}\star\bar{\delta}_{2}
based at
\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)
. In particular, to prove that
\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta_{1}\bigr)_{G}(1)=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta_{2}\bigr)_{G}(1)
for all such paths

and
\delta_{2}

\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}(1)=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\bigr)_{G}(1)
for each loop

.

For each loop

from

to the constant loop

, since

is simply connected. From Lemma

: the distribution

is integrable because the curvature

\sigma(c)_{s}(0)

\sigma(c)_{s}(1)

\displaystyle\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\bigr)_{G}(1)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\beta^{-1}\bigr)_{G}(1)\beta(1)=\bigl(\hat{\zeta}_{U}\star\delta\beta^{-1}\bigr)_{G}(1)

\hat{\zeta}_{U}(1)\mathrm{dev}_{(\hat{\zeta}_{U})_{G}(1)}\colon\ q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)_{G}\to q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U)

Throughout this section, we will need a notion of codimension; it will be convenient to use the following terminology, which comes from considerations of transversality.

Definition 5.1
.

For a smooth manifold
M
and a closed subset
N\subseteq M
, we will say that
N
has
codimension at least two
if and only if, for each path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma(0)\not\in N
, there exists a homotopy
c\colon[0,1]^{2}\to M
,
(s,t)\mapsto c_{s}(t)
such that
c_{0}=\gamma
,
c_{s}(0)=\gamma(0)
for all
s\in[0,1]
, and
c((0,1]\times[0,1])\cap N=\varnothing
.

Closed submanifolds and subvarieties
N\subseteq M
will, of course, have codimension at least two in this sense if
\dim(M)-\dim(N)\geq 2
. Moreover, the complement of
N
must be dense in
M
if
N
satisfies this definition: for each point
p\in M
, we can find a sequence of points of the form
c_{s_{i}}(t_{i})\not\in N
with
s_{i}\rightarrow 0
such that
c_{s_{i}}(t_{i})\rightarrow p
.

5.1
General result

In this subsection, we will prove the following result, from which Theorems

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
({G},\omega)
be a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over a connected smooth manifold
M
,
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
, and
{e}\in{G}
such that
\alpha({e})={e}a
for some
a\in H
. Suppose that the isotropy
a\in H
ensnares

, that
U
is simply connected, and that
\mathrm{Fix}_{G/H}(a)
has codimension at least two in
G/H
. If
a^{k}ga^{-k}\rightarrow e
as

implies

, then
({G},\omega)
geometrically embeds onto a dense open subset of the Klein geometry
(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})

(G,H)
.

By Corollary

, the hypotheses of the theorem tell us that there is a geometric embedding
\sigma\colon\bigl(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(U),\omega_{{}_{G}}\bigr)\hookrightarrow({G},\omega)
. It turns out that this is already sufficient to prove the theorem:

to get a geometric isomorphism between

and a dense open subset of

Intuitively, the image
\sigma(U)=U_{G}
of

should already make up nearly all of

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over a connected smooth manifold

Suppose
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}

\gamma(0)={e}

\gamma(0)h_{\gamma}=\gamma(1)

\gamma_{G}(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}=e

, and since

Because

has codimension at least two in

such that

,

for all

, and
c((0,1]\times[0,1])\subseteq{F}
; see Figures

for each

\sigma(c_{s})_{G}=c_{s}(0)^{-1}c_{s}=c_{s}
for all

converges to

as

. In particular,

\sigma\bigl(c_{s}(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}\bigr)=\sigma(c_{s}(1))h_{\gamma}^{-1}\rightarrow\gamma(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}=\gamma(0)=\sigma(e),

so since

is an embedding, we must have
c_{s}(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}\rightarrow\gamma_{G}(1)h_{\gamma}^{-1}=e
, hence
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)=\{e\}
.
∎

Recall that, whenever
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)=\{e\}
, the developing map

\mathrm{dev}_{e}\colon\ ({G},\omega)\to(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})

based at
{e}
is a well-defined geometric map given by
\gamma(1)h\mapsto\gamma_{G}(1)h
for every

and piecewise smooth path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{G}
starting at

. Therefore, if we can show that
\mathrm{dev}_{e}
is injective, then the proof of Theorem

will be done.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
\psi\colon({G},\omega)\to({Q},\upsilon)
is a geometric map and that
V\subseteq{Q}
is a dense subset. If
\psi|_{\psi^{-1}(V)}
is injective, then
\psi
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose that
\psi({g})=\psi({g}^{\prime})
for some
{g},{g}^{\prime}\in{G}
. Since
\psi
is a local diffeomorphism, there exists an open neighborhood
W
of
{g}^{\prime}
such that
\psi|_{W}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image. If
({g}_{i})
is a sequence in
\psi^{-1}(V)
converging to
{g}
, which must exist because density of
V
in
{Q}
implies density of
\psi^{-1}(V)
in
{G}
, then
\psi({g}_{i})\rightarrow\psi({g})=\psi({g}^{\prime})
, so
\psi|_{W}^{-1}(\psi({g}_{i}))\rightarrow\psi|_{W}^{-1}(\psi({g}^{\prime}))={g}^{\prime}
. But
\psi|_{\psi^{-1}(V)}
is injective, so
\psi|_{W}^{-1}(\psi({g}_{i}))={g}_{i}\rightarrow{g}
; since
{G}
is Hausdorff, this means
{g}={g}^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

In the setting of Proposition
5.3
, the developing map
\mathrm{dev}_{e}\colon({G},\omega)\to(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
is a geometric embedding.

Proof.

As we did in the proof of Proposition
5.3
, we may assume that
e\in{F}
and
\sigma(e)={e}
. By definition, we have that
\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma)=\gamma_{G}
whenever
\gamma(0)={e}
, so
(\mathrm{dev}_{e}\circ\sigma)(\gamma_{G})=\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma)=\gamma_{G}
whenever
\gamma_{G}
is contained in
{F}
. Hence,
\mathrm{dev}_{e}\circ\sigma
is the identity map on
{F}
.

It just remains to show that
\mathrm{dev}_{e}\colon{G}\to G
is injective. To this end, suppose
\mathrm{dev}_{e}({g})\in{F}
, and let
\gamma\in\Path_{e}({G})
be a path such that
\gamma(1)\in{g}H
. Then,
\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma)
is a path in
G
with
\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma(0))=\mathrm{dev}_{e}({e})=e
and
\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma(1))=\gamma_{G}(1)\in\mathrm{dev}_{e}({g})H
. Since
G\setminus{F}
has codimension at least two, there exists a homotopy
c\colon[0,1]^{2}\to G
such that
c_{0}=\mathrm{dev}_{e}(\gamma)=\gamma_{G}
,
c_{s}(0)=e
for all
s\in[0,1]
, and
c((0,1]\times[0,1])\subseteq{F}
. By continuity,
\sigma(c_{s}(1))\rightarrow\sigma(c_{0}(1))=\sigma(\gamma_{G}(1))
as
s\rightarrow 0
, and by Lemma
3.2
,
\sigma(c_{s}(1))\rightarrow\gamma(1)
as
s\rightarrow 0
. Because
{G}
is Hausdorff, this means that
\gamma(1)=\sigma(\gamma_{G}(1))\in\sigma({F})
, hence that
{g}\in\sigma({F})
. Thus,
\mathrm{dev}_{e}^{-1}({F})=\sigma({F})
, so because
\mathrm{dev}_{e}|_{\sigma({F})}
is injective,
\mathrm{dev}_{e}
is injective by Lemma
5.4
.
∎

In summary, under the hypotheses of Theorem
5.2
, we have shown that the developing map
\mathrm{dev}_{e}
gives a geometric embedding from
({G},\omega)
onto a dense open subset of the Klein geometry
(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
, so the proof is complete.

5.2
Proof of Theorem
A

Recall that, in this case, our model geometry is
(\PGL_{m+1}\mathbb{C},P)
, which corresponds to complex projective geometry over
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
. We want to prove that, given a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(\PGL_{m+1}\mathbb{C},P)
over a connected smooth manifold
M
and a nontrivial geometric automorphism
\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)
such that
\alpha({e})\in{e}P_{+}
for some
{e}\in{G}
, there exists a geometric embedding of
({G},\omega)
onto a dense open subset of the Klein geometry
(\PGL_{m+1}\mathbb{C},\omega_{{}_{\PGL_{m+1}\mathbb{C}}})
.

For
\alpha({e})={e}a
, let us write

a=\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

for some
\beta^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. In view of Theorem
5.2
, it suffices to prove that
a
ensnares the complement
U=\mathbb{CP}^{m}\setminus\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{CP}^{m}}(a)
, that
U
is simply connected, and that
\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{CP}^{m}}(a)
has codimension at least two.

For arbitrary

q_{{}_{P}}(g)=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{CP}^{m},

with
r\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we have

a^{k}(q_{{}_{P}}(g))=a^{k}\begin{pmatrix}r\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\beta\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}r\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r+k\beta(x)\\
x\end{pmatrix},

which is equal to

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\dfrac{1}{r+k\beta(x)}x\end{pmatrix}

whenever
r+k\beta(x)\neq 0
. In particular, fixed points are precisely those points for which
\beta(x)=0
, and if
\beta(x)\neq 0
, then
a^{k}(q_{{}_{P}}(g))
must go to
q_{{}_{P}}(e)
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. Since

\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{CP}^{m}}(a)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{CP}^{m}\mid\beta(x)=0\right\}

is a complex projective hyperplane in
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
,
U=\mathbb{CP}^{m}\setminus\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{CP}^{m}}(a)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, hence it is simply connected. Moreover, since
\beta(x)=0
implies
\mathrm{Re}(\beta(x))=0
and
\mathrm{Im}(\beta(x))=0
,
\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{CP}^{m}}(a)
has (real) codimension 2 in
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
.

Now, all that is left to do is prove that
a
ensnares
U
. For this, note that
\zeta_{U}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{CP}^{m}
, given by
t\mapsto\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
ty\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
for some
y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that
\beta(y)\in(0,+\infty)\subset\mathbb{R}
, shrinks into itself under forward iterates of
a
, so that
a^{k}(\zeta_{U}(t))=\smash{\zeta_{U}\bigl(\tfrac{t}{1+k\beta(y)t}\bigr)}
. Thus, for
\tau_{k}\colon t\mapsto\tfrac{t}{1+k\beta(y)(1-t)}
, we get
a^{k}(\zeta_{U}(\tau_{k}(t)))=\smash{\zeta_{U}\bigl(\tfrac{t}{1+k\beta(y)}\bigr)}
, hence
a^{k}(\zeta_{U}\circ\tau_{k})
converges to
q_{{}_{P}}(e)
to first order.
Moreover, since
a^{k}(q_{{}_{P}}(g))
converges to
q_{{}_{P}}(e)
for each
q_{{}_{P}}(g)\in U
, we may assume that
r+k\beta(x)\neq 0
for all
k\geq 0
after replacing
q_{{}_{P}}(g)
with
a^{n}(q_{{}_{P}}(g))
for suitably large
n
. This allows us to work in the coordinate chart
r=1
, and we know that in those coordinates,
a^{k}
is given by
x\mapsto\tfrac{1}{1+k\beta(x)}x
. Therefore, for
\xi\in T_{q_{P}(g)}U
and considering
a^{k}_{*}\xi
as a point derivation on the vector of coordinate functions
x
,

\displaystyle\bigl(a^{k}_{*}\xi\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\xi\left(\frac{1}{1+k\beta(x)}x\right)=\frac{1}{1+k\beta(x)}\left(\xi(x)-\frac{k\xi(\beta(x))}{1+k\beta(x)}x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+k\beta(x))^{2}}\big(\xi(x)+k(\beta(x)\xi(x)-\xi(\beta(x))x)\big),

so since
(1+k\beta(x))^{2}
grows quadratically in
k
while the numerator
\xi(x)+k(\beta(x)\xi(x)-\xi(\beta(x))x)
only grows linearly in
k
,
a^{k}_{*}\xi\rightarrow 0_{q_{P}(e)}
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. Indeed, for all sufficiently small open subsets
V\subset TU\subseteq T(G/P)
,
\pi\colon T(G/P)\to G/P
the natural projection from the tangent bundle to
G/P\cong\mathbb{CP}^{m}
,
\|\cdot\|
a norm on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and
k>(\inf_{x\in\pi(V)}|\beta(x)|)^{-1}
, we have

\left\|\frac{1}{1+k\beta(x)}x\right\|=\frac{\|x\|}{|1+k\beta(x)|}\leq\frac{\sup_{x\in\pi(V)}\|x\|}{\inf_{x\in\pi(V)}|\beta(x)|k-1}\rightarrow 0

and

\displaystyle\|(a^{k}_{*}\xi)(x)\|={}

\displaystyle\left\|\xi\left(\frac{1}{1+k\beta(x)}x\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\inf_{x\in\pi(V)}|\beta(x)|k-1)^{2}}\Bigl(k\bigl(\sup_{x\in\pi(V)}|\beta(x)|\sup_{\xi\in V}\|\xi(x)\|

\displaystyle+\sup_{\xi\in V}|\xi(\beta(x))|\sup_{x\in\pi(V)}\|x\|\bigr)+\sup_{\xi\in V}\|\xi(x)\|\Bigr)\rightarrow 0

for
x
in
\pi(V)
and
\xi\in V
over the chart
r=1
, so the convergence of
a^{k}_{*}
to
0_{q_{{}_{P}}(e)}
is locally uniform over
U
. In other words,
a
ensnares
U
, so the proof of Theorem
A
is complete.

5.3
Proof of Theorem

This proof is largely the same as the one for Theorem
A
. In this case, the model becomes
(\PGL_{m+1}\mathbb{H},P)

\alpha\in\Aut({G},\omega)

\alpha({e})\in{e}P_{+}
for some

, that there exists a geometric embedding of
({G},\omega)
onto a dense open subset of the Klein geometry

\alpha({e})={e}a
, we write

a=\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

for some
\beta^{\top}\in\mathbb{H}^{m}

5.2

a
ensnares
U=\mathbb{HP}^{m}\setminus\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{HP}^{m}}(a)

q_{{}_{P}}(g)=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{HP}^{m},

a^{k}(q_{{}_{P}}(g))=a^{k}\begin{pmatrix}r\\

x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r+k\beta(x)\\
x\end{pmatrix},

which is equal to

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
x(r+k\beta(x))^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

, we can assume

. Indeed, since the convergence comes from the disparity of degrees in the polynomials in

ensnares

5.4
Proof of Theorem

This time, our model geometry is given by
(\PU(\mathrm{h}_{p,q}),P)
, basically corresponding to the natural CR structure on the null-cone for
\mathrm{h}_{p,q}
in
\mathbb{CP}^{p+q+1}
