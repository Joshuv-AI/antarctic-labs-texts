CMA-ES with Adaptive Reevaluation for Multiplicative Noise
Kento Uchida, Kenta Nishihara, Shinichi Shirakawa

Abstract
. The covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) is a powerful optimization method for continuous black-box optimization problems. Several noise-handling methods have been proposed to bring out the optimization performance of the CMA-ES on noisy objective functions. The adaptations of the population size and the learning rate are two major approaches that perform well under additive Gaussian noise. The reevaluation technique is another technique that evaluates each solution multiple times. In this paper, we discuss the difference between those methods from the perspective of stochastic relaxation that considers the maximization of the expected utility function. We derive that the set of maximizers of the noise-independent utility, which is used in the reevaluation technique, certainly contains the optimal solution, while the noise-dependent utility, which is used in the population size and leaning rate adaptations, does not satisfy it under multiplicative noise. Based on the discussion, we develop the reevaluation adaptation CMA-ES (RA-CMA-ES), which computes two update directions using half of the evaluations and adapts the number of reevaluations based on the estimated correlation of those two update directions. The numerical simulation shows that the RA-CMA-ES outperforms the comparative method under multiplicative noise, maintaining competitive performance under additive noise.

1.
Introduction

The covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES)
(
Hansen and
Ostermeier, 1996
)
is a probabilistic model-based evolutionary algorithm that employs a multivariate Gaussian distribution as the sampling distribution.
CMA-ES is known as a powerful optimization method for continuous black-box optimization.
The update procedure of CMA-ES consists of three steps: sampling the solutions from the multivariate Gaussian distribution, evaluating the solutions on the objective function, and updating the distribution parameters.
All the hyperparameters of the CMA-ES have recommended default values depending only on the search dimensions
d
and population size
\lambda

(
Hansen, 2016
)
, which is given as
\lambda=4+\lfloor 3\ln d\rfloor
by default.
Based on the concept of stochastic relaxation
(
Ollivier
et al., 2017
)
, the update rule of CMA-ES is partially considered as the natural gradient descent to maximize the expectation of the utility function
(
Akimoto
et al., 2010
)
, and the ranking-based weights in CMA-ES are regarded as the estimated utility function values.

Objective functions often contain noise in real-world applications of evolutionary algorithms.
This noise disturbs the update direction of the distribution parameters and deteriorates the performance of the CMA-ES.
Several variants of the CMA-ES have been developed to improve the performance in noisy optimization problems.
The major categories of noise-handling methods for evolution strategies are categorized into three approaches: the reevaluations of solutions
(
Beyer and
Sendhoff, 2007
;
Heidrich-Meisner
and Igel, 2009
)
, population size adaptation
(
Hellwig and Beyer, 2016
;
Nishida and
Akimoto, 2018
)
, and learning rate adaptation
(
Nomura
et al., 2023
)
.
Recently, adaptation methods of the population size
(
Nishida and
Akimoto, 2018
)
and the learning rate
(
Nomura
et al., 2023
)
for CMA-ES have been developed.
These methods maintain the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the update directions of the distribution parameters and were evaluated using benchmark functions with additive Gaussian noise and demonstrated outstanding performance.
However, reevaluation adaptation, which adapts the number of evaluations for each solution, has not been actively investigated.

In this study, we discuss the difference between these noise-handling methods from the stochastic relaxation perspective.
We introduce two types of utility functions:
the noise-dependent utility
, which considers the maximization of the expectation under the joint distribution of the sampling and noise distributions, and
the noise-independent utility
, which is designed to maximize the expectation under the sampling distribution.
The population size and the learning rate adaptations employ the noise-dependent utility, whereas the reevaluation adaptation uses the noise-independent utility.
We also derive that the set of maximizers with noise-dependent utility does not contain the optimal solution certainly under multiplicative noise.
By contrast, the set of maximizers with noise-independent utility always contains the optimal solution under any type of noise.

We also propose a novel CMA-ES with a reevaluation adaptation mechanism, termed RA-CMA-ES.
Considering the success of adaptation using update directions, RA-CMA-ES computes two update directions using half of the evaluations and adapts the number of reevaluations based on the estimated correlation of those two update directions.
RA-CMA-ES also employs the learning rate adaptation to improve the estimation accuracy of the correlation.
We evaluated the performance of RA-CMA-ES on the benchmark functions under the additive noise and multiplicative noise.
Experimental results show that the RA-CMA-ES outperforms the comparative methods under multiplicative noise, while maintaining competitive performance under additive noise.

Related Works

2.1.
CMA-ES

The CMA-ES is a black-box optimization method for noiseless objective functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The CMA-ES employs a multivariate Gaussian distribution

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)},(\sigma^{(t)})^{2}\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

which is parameterized by the mean vector
\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and step-size
\sigma^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
CMA-ES also has two evolution paths,
\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which are initialized as
\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
.

In each iteration, CMA-ES generates
\lambda
solutions
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
from the multivariate Gaussian distribution as

(2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)}+\sigma^{(t)}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\kern 5.0pt.

The CMA-ES then evaluates the solutions on the objective function and computes their rankings. In the following, we denote the index of
i
-th best solution as
i\!:\!\lambda
.
The CMA-ES assigns the weights
w_{1},\cdots,w_{\mu}
to the
\mu=\lfloor\lambda/2\rfloor
best solutions as

(5)

\displaystyle w_{i}=\frac{\ln((\lambda+1)/2)-\ln i}{\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}\ln((\lambda+1)/2)-\ln k}\kern 5.0pt.

Next, the CMA-ES updates two evolution paths using the weighted averages
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\sum^{\mu}_{i=1}w_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i:\lambda}
and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{z}}=\sum^{\mu}_{i=1}w_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{i:\lambda}
as

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{\sigma})\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(t)}+\sqrt{c_{\sigma}(2-c_{\sigma})\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{z}}

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{c})\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(t)}+h_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}\sqrt{c_{c}(2-c_{c})\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}\kern 5.0pt,

where
c_{\sigma}
and
c_{c}
are accumulation factors and
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}=(\sum^{\mu}_{i=1}w_{i}^{2})^{-1}
is the variance
effective selection mass and
h_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}
is the Heaviside function.
We use the Heaviside function introduced in
(
Hansen and Auger, 2014
)
as

(8)

\displaystyle h_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad\frac{\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}}{1-(1-c_{\sigma})^{2(t+1)}}<\left(2+\frac{4}{d+1}\right)d\\
0&\text{otherwise}\kern 5.0pt.\end{cases}

Finally, CMA-ES updates the distribution parameters as

(9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)}+c_{m}\sigma^{(t)}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}

(10)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(t)}\exp\left(\frac{c_{\sigma}}{d_{\sigma}}\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\sigma}^{(t+1)}\|}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\|]}-1\right)\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{C}^{(t+1)}&=\left(1+c_{1}\delta^{(t+1)}\right)\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}+c_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(t+1)}\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{c}^{(t+1)}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}-\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}\right)\\
&\hskip 80.0pt+c_{\mu}\sum^{\mu}_{i=1}w_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{i:\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}_{i:\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}-\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}\right)\kern 5.0pt,\end{split}

where
c_{m}
,
c_{1}
, and
c_{\mu}
are the learning rates,
d_{\sigma}
is the damping factor, and
\delta^{(t+1)}=(1-h_{\sigma}^{(t+1)})c_{c}(2-c_{c})
.
The expectation
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\|]
is typically approximated by
\sqrt{d}(1-\frac{1}{4d}+\frac{1}{21d^{2}})
.

CMA-ES has recommended settings for each hyperparameter
(
Hansen, 2016
;
Hansen and Auger, 2014
)
, which makes the CMA-ES a pseudo hyperparameter-free optimization method.

2.2.
Noise-Resilient Variants of CMA-ES

In this section, we introduce several variants of the CMA-ES efficient on noisy optimization problems.
We consider the minimization of the expectation of the noisy objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
that receives the solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the noise
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
generated from
P_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
, that is,

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})]\kern 5.0pt,

where we denote
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim P_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}
for short.

Uncertainty Handling CMA-ES

Uncertainty handling CMA-ES (UH-CMA-ES)
(
Hansen et al., 2009
;
Heidrich-Meisner
and Igel, 2009
)
is a variant of CMA-ES for noisy optimization problems.
UH-CMA-ES measures the uncertainty level to incorporate the noise handling.
For
\lambda_{\mathrm{reev}}
solutions selected from
\lambda
solutions randomly, UH-CMA-ES performs the evaluation process twice and obtains two evaluation values
{f}_{i,1}
and
{f}_{i,2}
for each solution
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
The evaluation processes for the other
\lambda-\lambda_{\mathrm{reev}}
solution are performed once, and the evaluation values are copied as
{f}_{i,1}={f}_{i,2}
.
Subsequently, the UH-CMA-ES computes the uncertainty level using the ranking in the union set
\{{f}_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{\lambda}\cup\{{f}_{i,2}\}_{i=1}^{\lambda}
.
Denoting the ranking of
f_{i,1}
and
f_{i,2}
as
r_{i,1}
and
r_{i,2}
, respectively, the uncertainty level is computed using the rank change
\Delta_{i}=|r_{i,1}-r_{i,2}|-1
as

(13)

s=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{reev}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{reev}}}\biggl(2\Delta_{i}-\Delta^{\lim}_{\theta}\left({r_{i,2}}-\mathbb{I}\{f_{i,2}>f_{i,1}\}\right)\\
-\Delta^{\lim}_{\theta}\left({r_{i,1}}-\mathbb{I}\{f_{i,1}>f_{i,2}\}\right)\biggr)

where
I_{\mathrm{reev}}
denotes the set of the indices of the
\lambda_{\mathrm{reev}}
solutions. The function
\Delta^{\lim}_{\theta}(r)
denotes the
(\theta/2)
-quantile of the possible values of the rank change, that is,
(\theta/2)
-quantile in the set
\{|1-r|,|2-r|,\cdots,|2\lambda-1-r|\}
.

For noisy black-box optimization problem, the uncertainty level is often used to adapt the number of reevaluations
n_{\mathrm{eval}}
for each solution.
In the evaluation step of solutions, UH-CMA-ES evaluates each solution
n_{\mathrm{eval}}
times and uses the average evaluation value to compute the ranking of the solutions as

(14)

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i,j})\kern 5.0pt.

The number of reevaluations is increased by a factor
\alpha
when the uncertainty level is positive, and it is decreased by a factor
1/\alpha
otherwise.
See the detailed settings in
(
Heidrich-Meisner
and Igel, 2009
)
.

Population Size Adaptation CMA-ES

Population size adaptation CMA-ES (PSA-CMA-ES)
(
Nishida and
Akimoto, 2018
)
is a variant of CMA-ES with population size adaptation mechanism.
PSA-CMA-ES defines two update directions for the mean vector
\boldsymbol{m}
and the covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{C}
of the sampling distribution as

(15)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{m}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}}-\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)}

(16)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}\left((\sigma^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}})^{2}\boldsymbol{C}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}}-(\sigma^{(t)})^{2}\boldsymbol{C}^{(t)}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

where
\boldsymbol{m}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}}
,
\sigma^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{ori}}
are the updated distribution parameters in (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
).
1
1

1

The operation
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
transforms a given matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
to
d^{2}
-dimensional vector as
(\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{A}_{1,d},\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{A}_{2,d},\boldsymbol{A}_{3,1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{A}_{d,d})^{\mathrm{T}}
.

PSA-CMA-ES updates the population size so as to maintain the accuracy of the natural gradient estimation at a fixed target level.
PSA-CMA-ES introduces another evolution path
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}
for each update direction
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\mathrm{T}},\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}
and its normalization factor
\gamma_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
as

(17)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{(t)}+\sqrt{2(2-\beta)}\frac{\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t+1)}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t+1)}\right]}

(18)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\theta}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)^{2}\gamma_{\theta}^{(t)}+\beta(2-\beta)\kern 5.0pt,

(19)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{(t)}\exp\left(\beta\left(\gamma_{\theta}^{(t+1)}-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}}{\alpha}\right)\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\min\{\max\{\lambda^{(t+1)},\lambda_{\min}\},\lambda_{\max}\}\kern 5.0pt,

Learning Rate Adaptation CMA-ES

Learning rate adaptation CMA-ES (LRA-CMA-ES)
(
Nomura
et al., 2023
)
is a variant of CMA-ES that introduces a learning rate adaptation mechanism for the mean vector
\boldsymbol{m}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{C}
of the sampling distribution.
LRA-CMA-ES maintains the SNRs of those update directions in (
15
) and (
16
) by updating the learning rates.
To estimate the SNRs, LRA-CMA-ES computes the update directions
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
on the local coordinate system for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\}
, which are given by

(21)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}

(22)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{vec}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{vec}^{-1}\left(\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(t+1)}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

The LRA-CMA-ES also introduces two accumulations as

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathcal{E}^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathcal{V}^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\kern 5.0pt,

where
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the accumulation factor.
Assuming that the distribution does not change significantly for some iterations, the LRA-CMA-ES estimates the SNR as

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\|^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Cov}[\tilde{\Delta}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}])}\approx\frac{\|\mathcal{E}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{2-\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\|\mathcal{E}^{(t+1)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}}:=\widehat{\mathrm{SNR}}\kern 5.0pt.

LRA-CMA-ES updates the learning rate
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that the SNR is maintained at approximately
\alpha\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as

(26)

\displaystyle\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t+1)}=\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}\exp\left(\min\left\{\gamma\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\}\Pi_{[-1,1]}\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{SNR}}}{\alpha\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}}-1\right)\right)\kern 5.0pt,

where
\Pi_{[-1,1]}
is the projection onto
[-1,1]
, and
\alpha=1.4
and
\gamma=0.1
are hyperparameters. The accumulation factor is set
\beta_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0.1
in the update for
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=0.03
in the update for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
Subsequently, the distribution parameters are updated as

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{m}^{(t)}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}

(28)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t)}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(t+1)}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(t+1)}

(29)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)})^{\frac{1}{2d}}

(30)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}/(\sigma^{(t+1)})^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Like PSA-CMA-ES, LRA-CMA-ES corrects the step-size
\sigma^{(t+1)}
by multiplying
{\eta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}}/{\eta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t)}}
such that the step-size is proportional to
\eta_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(t+1)}
.

3.
Stochastic Relaxation in Noisy Optimization

Based on stochastic relaxation
(
Ollivier
et al., 2017
)
, we can reformulate the minimization problem in (
12
) as

(31)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\right]\right]\kern 5.0pt,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})}
. For CMA-ES, the distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the multivariate Gaussian distribution in (
1
).
Some update rules of the CMA-ES can be explained by the natural gradient decent
(
Akimoto
et al., 2010
)
as

(32)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\right]\right]\kern 5.0pt.

Under weak assumptions, the optimal distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}}
is given by the delta distribution around the optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
in (
12
).

In stochastic relaxation, the ranking-based weights are considered as the estimated utilities of the solutions, which are determined by the utility function using their quantile with respect to (w.r.t.) the current distribution.
Stochastic relaxation derives the update rules for the distribution parameters to maximize the expected utility function value.
For CMA-ES in noisy optimization, there are two possible choices for the distribution: the Gaussian distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})
and the joint distribution
\hat{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})=P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})P_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
of the Gaussian and noise distributions.
In this section, we explain their differences and discuss the preferable setting for noisy optimization.

3.1.
Possible Setting of Utility Functions

We discuss two possible settings for utility function: the noise-dependent utility
v(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and the noise-independent utility
u(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The noise-dependent utility computes the quantile w.r.t. the joint distribution
\hat{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
whereas the noise-independent utility
u(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
computes the quantile w.r.t. the Gaussian distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Noise-dependent utility:

The noise-dependent utility is applied to the CMA-ES with the population size and learning rate adaptations.
The noise-dependent utility has two definitions of the quantile on
f
w.r.t.
\hat{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
as

(33)

\displaystyle q_{v}^{<}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\sim\hat{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})<f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\right)

(34)

\displaystyle q_{v}^{\leq}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\sim\hat{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Introducing a non-increasing function
w:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, called the selection scheme, we obtain the utility function as

(35)

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{cases}w(q^{<}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}))&\text{if}\kern 5.0ptq^{<}_{v}=q^{\leq}_{v}\\
\frac{1}{q^{\leq}_{v}-q^{<}_{v}}\int_{q=q^{<}_{v}}^{{q=}q^{\leq}_{v}}w(q)\mathrm{d}q&\text{otherwise}\kern 5.0pt,\end{cases}

where we denote
q^{<}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q^{\leq}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})
as
q^{<}_{v}
and
q^{\leq}_{v}
for short, respectively.
We note that the quantile on the black-box function
f
cannot be computed analytically.
Practically, the utility values are estimated using the Monte Carlo approximation with
\lambda
pairs of solutions and random vectors
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\lambda}
using (
35
) replacing the quantiles
q^{<}_{v}
and
q^{\leq}_{v}
with the estimated quantiles

(36)

\displaystyle\bar{q}^{<}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum^{\lambda}_{k=1}\mathbb{I}_{\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{z}_{k})<f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\right\}}

(37)

\displaystyle\bar{q}^{\leq}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum^{\lambda}_{k=1}\mathbb{I}_{\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\right\}}\kern 5.0pt,

respectively.

This utility can recover the weights (
5
) used in the CMA-ES with corresponding selection scheme
w
.

Noise-independent utility:

The noise-independent utility is the formulation for CMA-ES with reevaluation mechanism such as UH-CMA-ES. The quantiles on
f
w.r.t.
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are defined as

(38)

\displaystyle q_{u}^{<}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z})]<\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})]\right)

(39)

\displaystyle q_{u}^{\leq}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})]\right)\kern 5.0pt.

The noise-independent utility function is then given by

(40)

\displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{cases}w(q^{<}_{u})&\text{if}\kern 5.0ptq^{<}_{u}=q^{\leq}_{u}\\
\frac{1}{q^{\leq}_{u}-q^{<}_{u}}\int_{q=q^{<}_{u}}^{{q=}q^{\leq}_{u}}w(q)\mathrm{d}q&\text{otherwise}\kern 5.0pt,\end{cases}

6.
Conclusion

In this study, we analyzed the existing noise-handling methods from the stochastic relaxation perspective.
We showed that the noise-dependent utility used in PSA-CMA-ES and LRA-CMA-ES is not suitable for some noisy optimization problems, particularly under multiplicative noise.
Subsequently, we focused on the reevaluation adaptation as a preferable approach for noisy optimization problems and proposed a novel reevaluation adaptation method for CMA-ES.
The proposed method, RA-CMA-ES, computes two update directions using half of the evaluations and adapts the number of reevaluations based on the estimated correlation of those two update directions.
RA-CMA-ES employs the learning rate adaptation to meet the assumption in the estimation mechanism of the correlation, thereby enhancing the correlation estimation accuracy.
We evaluated the performance of RA-CMA-ES on the benchmark functions under the additive noise and multiplicative noise.
Experimental results showed that the RA-CMA-ES outperformed the comparative method under multiplicative noise, while maintaining competitive performance under additive noise.

Given that our adaptation mechanism incorporates heuristic elements like target correlation adaptation, we must refine or justify these components in future work.
In addition, the performance evaluation of the RA-CMA-ES with other types of noise, including the input noise and solution-dependent noise
P_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
, is another topic for future work.

A.1.
Proof of Lemma 3.1

Proof.

Let us consider the objective function in the form
f(\boldsymbol{x},z)=zf_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x})+b
with a constant value
b\in\mathbb{R}
.
We consider the case that the noise
z
takes
1
or
-1
only, i.e.
z\in\{-1,1\}
, and given independently from
\boldsymbol{x}
.
We will construct a function
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}
satisfying

(65)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\notin\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{E}_{z\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\boldsymbol{\theta})]\kern 5.0pt.

for arbitrary optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
in (
12
) with convex and concave selection scheme
w
separately.

Convex case:

Consider the objective function is given with a unique optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
as

(66)

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\,0&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\\
\,1&\text{otherwise}\kern 5.0pt.\end{cases}

Considering
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast})=1
, the quantiles
q^{<}_{v}
and
q^{\leq}_{v}
are given by

(67)

\displaystyle q^{<}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x},z)=\begin{cases}\,p_{z}^{-}&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\\
\,0&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=-1\\
\,p_{z}^{-}&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=1\end{cases}

(68)

\displaystyle q^{\leq}_{v}(\boldsymbol{x},z)=\begin{cases}\,p_{z}^{-}&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\\
\,p_{z}^{-}&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=-1\\
\,1&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=1\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt.

where we denote
p_{z}^{-}=\Pr_{z^{\prime}\sim P_{n}}(z^{\prime}=-1)
for short.
The utility value is determined for
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
as

(69)

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast},z;\theta)

\displaystyle=w(p_{z}^{-})

(70)

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},-1;\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{z}^{-}}\int_{0}^{p_{z}^{-}}w(q)\mathrm{d}q

(71)

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},1;\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-p_{z}^{-}}\int_{p_{z}^{-}}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q

The expected utility is given by

(72)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]=w(\Pr(z=-1))

(73)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]\\
&=\int_{0}^{p_{z}^{-}}w(q)\mathrm{d}q+\int_{p_{z}^{-}}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q=\int_{0}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q\kern 5.0pt.\end{split}

Since
w
is strictly convex, Jensen’s inequality shows that there exists
\epsilon>0
that holds

(74)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q=w\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)+\epsilon\kern 5.0pt.

Moreover, we can consider arbitrary small
\delta\in(0,1/2)
which satisfies
p_{z}^{-}=\Pr(z=-1)=1/2-\delta
.
We have

(75)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]\\
&\qquad=\int_{0}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q-w(p_{z}^{-})\end{split}

(76)

\displaystyle\qquad=\epsilon-\left(w\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta\right)-w\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)

Since
w
is convex function on the open interval
(0,1)
, it is continuous on
(0,1)
. Therefore, for any
\epsilon
, there exists
\delta>0
which holds

(77)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]>\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]\kern 5.0pt.

Concave case:

Consider the objective function is given with a unique optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
as

(78)

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\,-1&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\\
\,0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt.

In this case, the quantiles
q^{<}
and
q^{\leq}
are give by

(79)

\displaystyle q^{<}(\boldsymbol{x},z)=\begin{cases}\,0&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=1\\
\,1&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=-1\\
\,1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(80)

\displaystyle q^{\leq}(\boldsymbol{x},z)=\begin{cases}\,0&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=1\\
\,1&\text{if}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad z=-1\\
\,0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt.

Then, the expected utility is given by

(81)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\Pr(z=1)w(0)+\Pr(z=-1)w(1)

(82)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q\kern 5.0pt.

For strictly concave function, we have

(83)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w(q)\mathrm{d}q

\displaystyle>\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-q)w(0)+qw(1)\right)\mathrm{d}q

(84)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}w(0)+\frac{1}{4}w(1)\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore, setting
\Pr(z=1)=3/4
satisfies

(85)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]>\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}[v(\boldsymbol{x},z;\theta)\mid\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}]\kern 5.0pt.

This is end of the proof.
∎
