On generic properties of nilpotent algebras
Yuri Bahturin, Alexander Olshanskii

Abstract
We study general nilpotent algebras. The results obtained are new even for the classical algebras, such as associative or Lie algebras. We single out certain generic properties of finite-dimensional algebras, mostly over infinite fields. The notion of being generic in the class of n n -generated algebras of an arbitrary primitive class of c c -nilpotent algebras appears naturally in the following way. On the set of the isomorphism classes of such algebras one can introduce the structure of an algebraic variety. As a result, the subsets are endowed with the dimensions as algebraic varieties. A subset 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} of a set 𝒳 \mathcal{X} of lesser dimension can be viewed as negligible in 𝒳 \mathcal{X} . For example, if n ≫ c n\gg c , we determine that an automorphism group of a generic algebra P P consists of the automorphisms, which are scalar modulo P 2 P^{2} . Generic ideals are in I ⁡ ( P ) I(P) , the annihilator of P P . In the case of classical nilpotent algebras as above, the generic algebras are graded by the degrees with respect to some generating sets.

Introduction

In this paper we are interested in the question: what is a “generic” group of automorphisms of a finite-dimensional nilpotent algebra? Or: what is a “generic” Lie algebras of derivations of a finite-dimensional nilpotent algebra? One says that a property of mathematical objects of a class
\mathcal{C}
is
generic
if “almost every” object of
\mathcal{C}
enjoys this property. The words “almost every” are understood differently, depending on the class
\mathcal{C}
.

At the same time, the answer does not depend on the particular class of algebras: associative, Lie, Jordan, etc. One of the early relevant papers on nilpotent algebras in such generality was the paper of A. I. Malcev
[
AIM
]
.

In particular, if several properties are generic then their (finite) intersection is also generic. Moreover, sometimes, it is easier to prove that some property is generic than to provide examples. For instance, in the papers
[
Be
,
Mil
,
Ols
]
, the estimate of the cardinality or the dimension of the set of counterexamples allows one to disprove certain conjectures without explicitly providing the counterexamples.

In the classes of finite groups (rings, graphs, etc.), it is natural to say that almost all, up to isomorphism, groups have certain property
\mathcal{P}
if
\frac{f_{\mathcal{P}}(n)}{f(n)}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
where
f_{\mathcal{P}}(n)
is the number of groups of order
n
with property
\mathcal{P}
and
f(n)
is the total number of groups of order
n
in the class of all groups in question. For example, a known conjecture is that a generic finite group is a 2-group.

If we consider the properties of pairs of elements in the symmetric groups
S_{n}
, then the classical Dixon’s
theorem
[
Dix
]

says that a pair of random permutations of a set of
n
elements
almost surely generates either the symmetric group or the
alternating group of degree
n
.

Infinite groups (rings, etc.) often have finite description, say, in terms of generators and defining relations. However here the number
n
restricting the order of the group above now restricts the length of defining relation when the number of relations is fixed. So the following statements make sense. “Almost any finitely presented group is hyperbolic”
[
OAY
]
and furthermore “almost every subgroup with lesser number of generators in such a group is free”
[
Gub
,
AO
]
.

One of the most common ways to make sense of the words “almost all” or “generic” is to introduce a probabilistic measure on the mathematical objects in question. An example : “almost every symmetric random walk on the set of integers is recurrent”.

But how can one handle the notion of being “generic” in the case of finite-dimensional algebras over an infinite field?
To reach our goals in this paper, we start with a “primitive” class of algebras
{\mathfrak{V}}
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
, defined by a set of identical relations, say the the class of associative, or Lie algebras, or commutative, etc. algebras.

Then we restrict the number of generators in algebras from
{\mathfrak{V}}
by some number
n
and obtain a subclass of
n
-generated algebras
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
. The class
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
is too large, unless we impose further restrictions on
{\mathfrak{V}}
. So we restrict ourselves to
nilpotent
primitive classes, that is, we assume that there is
c\geq 2
such that
P^{c+1}=\{0\}
for any algebra
P\in{\mathfrak{V}}
. Now
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
consists of finite-dimensional algebras and all algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
are homomorphic images of one algebra
F_{n}=F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}})
, called the
free algebra of rank
n
in
{\mathfrak{V}}
, or
{\mathfrak{V}}
-free algebra of rank
n
. As a matter of fact, if
{\mathbb{F}}
is infinite, as we assume throughout this paper except in Chapter
8
, then
F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}})
is
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded, hence finite-dimensional if and only if
{\mathfrak{V}}
is nilpotent.

Every algebra
P\in{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
has the form of
F_{n}/K
where
K
is an ideal of
F_{n}
. The set of all ideals is an algebraic variety, a subvariety in the abstract disjoint union of Grassmann varieties of subspaces of
F_{n}
. To study such sets, we can use the notion of the dimension of an algebraic variety. Then the statement that some set
\mathcal{A}
of ideals is “almost the same” as a bigger variety
{\mathcal{B}}
simply means that the complement
{\mathcal{B}}\setminus\mathcal{A}
has strictly smaller dimension than the dimension of
{\mathcal{B}}
. (According to this understanding, for instance, almost every point on the plane has its first coordinate nonzero.) Later, this comparison of the dimensions of the varieties of ideals is transferred to the factor-space of quotient-algebras, where we identify ideals with isomorphic quotient-algebras. For the rigorous definition of the notion “generic” see Definition
3
.

2
Primitive classes of algebras

In the study of automorphisms and derivations of nilpotent algebras, our basic objects are
relatively free nilpotent algebras
. To deal with them, in Sections
2
and
3
we quickly review some standard facts from the theory of primitive classes of general nonassociative algebras. Note that one of the first papers on the identical relations in general algebras was
[
AIM
]
.

For the reader’s convenience we give references to the respective theorems in the theory of primitive classes (varieties) of Lie algebras in the recent monograph
[
B
]
.

2.1
Absolutely free algebras

For any field
{\mathbb{F}}
and a nonempty set
X
, one defines an (absolutely) free algebra
\mathcal{F}(X)
as follows. We first define the
nonassociative monomials
in
X
by induction on their
degree
. The monomials of degree 1 are the elements of
X
. We write
\deg x=1
. Once the monomials of all degrees less than
n
have been defined, the monomials of degree
n
are all possible expressions of the form
(u)(v)
where
\deg u=k\geq 1
and
\deg v=n-k\geq 1
. The set of all nonassociative monomials in
X
is denoted by
\Gamma(X)
. One has
\Gamma(X)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}
where
\Gamma_{n}
is the set of monomials of degree
n
.

The set
\Gamma(X)
is endowed with an operation
\circ
, as follows. If
u\in\Gamma_{k}
and
v\in\Gamma_{\ell}
then
u\circ v=(u)(v)\in\Gamma_{k+\ell}
. With respect to this operation,
\Gamma(X)
is generated by
X
. In practice, while using this operation, one omits the symbol
\circ
and all unnecessary parentheses. For instance, one writes
(x_{1}x_{2})x_{2}
instead of

((x_{1})\circ(x_{2}))\circ(x_{2})
.

Finally, one defines the free algebra
\mathcal{F}(X)
as the linear span of
\Gamma(X)
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}
. The elements of
\mathcal{F}(X)
are often called the
nonassociative polynomials
. The product in
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the extension by linearity of the product in
\Gamma(X)
.

If
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is the linear span of
\Gamma_{n}
then
\mathcal{F}_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\subset\mathcal{F}_{k+\ell}
and so we have the canonical
{\mathbb{N}}
-grading of the free algebra, where
{\mathbb{N}}
is the additive semigroup of natural numbers, as follows

(1)

\mathcal{F}(X)=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n}

The elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are called
homogeneous
of degree
n
. The
ideals

\mathcal{F}^{n}=\bigoplus_{k=n}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}
satisfy
\mathcal{F}^{n}\mathcal{F}^{m}\subset\mathcal{F}^{m+n}
and form a descending filtration in
\mathcal{F}(X)
:

(2)

\mathcal{F}(X)=\mathcal{F}^{1}\supset\mathcal{F}^{2}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{F}^{n}\supset\cdots\mbox{ such that }\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}=\{0\}.

One also calls the terms of (
2
) the
powers of
\mathcal{F}(X)
and the filtration itself the
power
series of
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Now let
\Phi\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{X}
be the additive subsemigroup of the group of functions
\alpha:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}
consisiting of the functions with nonnegative values and finite positive norm
|\alpha|=\sum_{X}\alpha(x)
. By definition, a nonassociative monomial
u=u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is contained in
\Gamma_{\alpha}
if the degree
d_{i}
of
u
with respect to each
x_{i}
equals
\alpha(x_{i})
and
\deg u=|\alpha|
. One then can partition
\Gamma_{n}
as the union of subsets
\Gamma_{\alpha}
such that
|\alpha|=n
. One has
\Gamma=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi}\Gamma_{\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\beta}\subset\Gamma_{\alpha+\beta}
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
the linear span of
\Gamma_{\alpha}
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\beta}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\alpha+\beta}
and one then has a
\Phi
-grading of
\mathcal{F}(X)
:

(3)

\mathcal{F}(X)=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}.

If
|X|=n
then we can identify
\Phi
with a subsemigroup in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and so (
3
) becomes a
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
-grading, or more precisely a
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}
-grading where
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. The elements of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
are called
multihomogeneous
of
multidegree

\alpha
. If
\alpha(x)
is either
0
or
1
, for every
x\in X
, then the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
are called
multilinear
with respect to the set of the variables
x
for which
\alpha(x)=1
.

2.2
Primitive classes and relatively free algebras

Given a nonassociative monomial
u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and the elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of any algebra
P
over any field
{\mathbb{F}}
, one can use the induction on
\deg u
and the operation of
P
to define the
value

u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in P
. The value of a monomial
x_{i}
is just
a_{i}
, while
(uv)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})v(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. Now the value of a nonassociative polynomial
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
which is a linear combination of several monomials with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}
, is just the linear combination of the values of these monomials with the same coefficients.

If
\varphi:X\to P
is any mapping then the evaluation of nonassociative polynomials which replaces each
x\in X
by
\varphi(x)\in P
is a unique homomorphism of algebras
\bar{\varphi}:\mathcal{F}(X)\to P
, extending
\varphi
. This property is called the
universal property
of
\mathcal{F}(X)
. An immediate consequence of this property is that given any algebra
P
there exists
X
such that
P
is a homomorphic image of
\mathcal{F}(X)
under a homomorphism
\varepsilon
mapping
X
onto a generating set of
P
. Taking
K=\mathrm{Ker}\left(\varepsilon\right)
, we get
P\cong\mathcal{F}(X)/K
.

If
w(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
, for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in P
, we say that
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an
identical relation
or shortly an
identity
in
P
.

Now let us set
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\mathcal{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
. Given a nonempty subset
V\subset\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, the class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of all algebras (over fixed field
{\mathbb{F}}
) satisfying all identities
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
where
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in V
is called the
primitive class defined by
V
. By Birkhoff’s Theorem
[
B
, Theorem 4.3]
a nonempty class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras is primitive if and only if
{\mathfrak{V}}
is closed under subalgebras, quotient-algebras and Cartesian products of algebras.

Given a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
and algebra
P
, one considers the ideal
{\mathfrak{V}}(P)
(more often denoted by a matching notation
V(P)
) of all values
v(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in P
and
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an identity in
{\mathfrak{V}}
. By analogy with the group-theoretic notation, one calls
V(P)

the verbal ideal corresponding to the primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
.
V(P)
is an least ideal of
P
such that
P/V(P)\in{\mathfrak{V}}
. The ideal
V(P)
is closed under all endomorphisms of
P
. Such ideals are called
fully invariant
.

If
{\mathbb{F}}
is a fixed field, then there is one-one Galois-type correspondence between the primitive classes over
{\mathbb{F}}
and fully invariant ideals of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
:

Proposition 1
.

Given a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
, the corresponding verbal ideal
V(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})
is fully invariant. Conversely, every fully invariant ideal is a verbal ideal for an appropriate primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
.
\hfill\Box

If
U\subset\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and
P
is an algebra, one denotes by
U(P)
the ideal of
P
generated by all
u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in P
and
u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in U
. If
U=\{w\}
then it will be convenient to write
U(P)=w(P)
. This is the
verbal ideal of
P
defined by the set of nonassociative polynomials
U
.
If
U(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})={\mathfrak{V}}(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})
for some variety
{\mathfrak{V}}
then one says that the set of identities
\{u=0\,|\,u\in U\}
is a
basis of identities
of
{\mathfrak{V}}
. Any algebra
P
in which
u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
for any
u\in U
also satisfies all identities of
{\mathfrak{V}}
.

Given a nonempty set
X
, the algebra
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)=\mathcal{F}(X)/V(\mathcal{F}(X))
is called a
{\mathfrak{V}}
-free algebra with the free generating set
X
or a
free algebra in
{\mathfrak{V}}
with the free generating set
X
. If
{\mathfrak{V}}
is nonzero,
X
is injectively embedded in
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
. Each of these algebras possesses the
universal property within the primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
, namely, any map
\varphi:X\to P\in{\mathfrak{V}}
extends to a unique homomorphism
\overline{\varphi}:F({\mathfrak{V}},X)\to P
. It follows that every algebra
P\in{\mathfrak{V}}
is isomorphic to a quotient-algebra of an appropriate
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
. If we do not specify parameters
{\mathfrak{V}}
or
X
, then we simply say that
P
is a
relatively free algebra
.

Examples of relatively free algebras, that is free in some varieties, include free associative algebras, free Lie algebras, free commutative algebras, etc. In this paper we will be mostly interested in
relatively free nilpotent algebras
.

A useful characterization of relatively free algebras is the following.

Lemma 1
.

An algebra
P
generated by a set
X
is relatively free with
X
a free generating if and only if any map
\varphi:X\to P
extends to a homomorphism
\bar{\varphi}:P\to P
.

\hfill\Box

If
P
satisfies the extension property in the above Lemma, then
P
is free in the primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}

generated by

P
that is, the least primitive class of algebras over
{\mathbb{F}}
containing
P
. In this case, we write
{\mathfrak{V}}=\mathrm{var}\left(P\right)
. It follows by Birkhoff’s Theorem (see
[
B
, Theorem 4.3]
) that
{\mathfrak{V}}
consists of the quotient-algebras of the subalgebras of the Cartesian powers of
P
.

One more useful property of relatively free algebra, following from the universal property, is given below.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0\neq v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
be a nonassociative polynomial and
F=F({\mathfrak{V}},Y)
a free algebra in a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras, with a free generating set
Y=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}
. Suppose
v(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0
is a
relation
among
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
that holds in
F
. Then
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an
identical relation
in
{\mathfrak{V}}
.

\hfill\Box

Next we mention the following.

Any map of a set
X
to a set
Y
extends to a unique homomorphism
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)\to F({\mathfrak{V}},Y)
. In particular, the embedding
X\subset Y
extends to the embedding
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)\subset F({\mathfrak{V}},Y)
and
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
is a retract of
F({\mathfrak{V}},Y)
under the projection extending
x\mapsto x
for
x\in X
and
x\mapsto 0
for
x\in Y\setminus X
.

The cardinality of
X
is called the
rank
of
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
. Two relatively free algebras in a non-trivial primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
, that is,
{\mathfrak{V}}
contains a nonzero algebra, are isomorphic is and only if they have the same rank. So, with
{\mathfrak{V}}
fixed, we can simply write
F_{n}=F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}})
for the relatively free algebra of rank
n
in the variety
{\mathfrak{V}}
. In this case, we can choose
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.

We conclude this section by a rarely mentioned property of the homomorphisms of algebras in a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
. It is true not only for the primitive classes of algebras over a field but also for the primitive classes of universal
\Omega
-algebras, with arbitrary signature
\Omega
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
F=F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
be a free algebra in a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras. Let
P
and
Q
be two algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}
. Suppose we are given three homomorphisms
\alpha:F\to P,\,\beta:F\to Q,\,\gamma:P\to Q
such that
\beta
is surjective. Then there exists a homomorphism
\delta:F\to F
such that
\gamma\alpha=\beta\delta
.

Proof.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

For each
x\in X
we choose
a_{x}\in F
such that
\beta(a_{x})=\gamma\alpha(x)
. Since
X
is a free generating set for
F
, the map from
X
to
F
given by
x\mapsto a_{x}
extends to a homomorphism
\delta:F\to F
. Now two homomorphisms
\beta\delta
and
\gamma\alpha
from

Q
coincide on

. As a result,

2.3
Multihomogeneous primitive classes

In Section
2.1
we noticed that the free algebra
\mathcal{F}(X)
has natural gradings by degrees and multidegrees with respect to the free generating set
X
. This important property does not hold for general relatively free algebras. For instance, this is not true in the case of any primitive class
\mathrm{var}\left(P\right)
generated by a finite non-nilpotent algebra
P
. So one defines
multihomogeneous primitive classes
, as follows.

Definition 1
.

A primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
is called
multihomogeneous
if given an identity
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
holding in
{\mathfrak{V}}
, it is true that also
v_{\alpha}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an identity in
{\mathfrak{V}}
, for any multihomogeneous component
v_{\alpha}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. In other words,
V(\mathcal{F}(X))
is graded with respect to the multihomogeneous grading (
3
) of
\mathcal{F}(X)
, where
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n},\ldots\}
.

For any natural
n
or any
\alpha\in\Phi
one can define
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)_{n}
or
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)_{\alpha}
as the image of
\mathcal{F}(X)_{n}
or
\mathcal{F}(X)_{\alpha}
under the natural homomorphism
\varepsilon
extending the identity map
X\to X
. If
{\mathfrak{V}}
is multihomogneous, then the kernel
V(\mathcal{F}(X))
of
\varepsilon
is graded. This allows us to apply (
1
), (
3
) and (
2
). As a result, we have the following:

(4)

F({\mathfrak{V}},X)=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}F({\mathfrak{V}},X)_{n},\;F({\mathfrak{V}},X)=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}(F({\mathfrak{V}},X))_{\alpha},

and

(5)

\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}F({\mathfrak{V}},X)^{n}=\{0\}.

Now the following is true.

Lemma 3
.

If
{\mathbb{F}}
is an infinite field then any primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras over
{\mathbb{F}}
is multihomogeneous. For a
{\mathfrak{V}}
-free algebra
F({\mathfrak{V}},X)
the equations (
4
) and (
5
) hold valid.

\hfill\Box

See the proof in
[
AIM
, §5]
.

Of course, there are multihomogeneous varieties even in the case of finite fields. These are, for example, the primitive classes defined by the identities
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
, where
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is multilinear, as defined earlier, at the end of Section
2.1
. Such identities are called
multilinear
. If one deals only with the fields of characteristic 0, then the following is true.

Proposition 3
.

If
\mathrm{char}\left({\mathbb{F}}\right)=0
then any primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras over
{\mathbb{F}}
can be defined by a set of multilinear identities.

\hfill\Box

See the proof in
[
AIM
, §5]
.

2.4
Derivations of relatively free algebras

Recall that a linear map
d:P\to P
of an algebra
P
is called a
derivation
if for any
a,b\in P
one has
d(ab)=d(a)b+ad(b)
.

We noticed earlier, see Proposition
1
, that the verbal ideals of algebras are closed under their endomorphisms. This does not need to be true for their derivations, even in the case of
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Weare going to show now that this is still true in the case where

is multihomogeneous. We start with a simple fact about the absolutely free algebras

Lemma 4
.

Any map
\varphi:X\to\mathcal{F}(X)
extends to a derivation
d_{\varphi}:\mathcal{F}(X)\to\mathcal{F}(X)
.

Proof.

The desired extension of
\varphi
to a derivation

can be obtained, as follows. For

one has to set

and for

of degree greater than 1, using induction, set
d_{\varphi}(w)=d_{\varphi}(u)v+ud_{\varphi}(v)
. Using linearity, one expands
d_{\varphi}
to the whole of
\mathcal{F}(X)
. The defining property of the derivation easily follows.
∎

Now if

is a free algebra in the primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
, one gets the same result provided that

is invariant under

. In Lemma

we prove that the latter property holds valid in the case where
{\mathfrak{V}}
is a multihomogeneous primitive class.

Remark 1
.

Note that in the case of a finite field

the extendibility as in Lemma

does not take place. Indeed, choose

the primitive class of associative and commutative algebras with an additional identity

. Let

be

-free algebra with one free generator

does not extend to a derivation of

If

is an arbitrary nonempty set,
{\mathfrak{V}}
a multihomogeneous primitive class over a field

then

is invariant under all derivations of

Proof.

Let

{\mathfrak{V}}

{\mathfrak{V}}
. This reformulation allows one to restrict oneself to the case where

is finite set:

is multihomogeneous, if

3
Primitive classes of nilpotent algebras

If there is
m
such that the value of every nonassociative monomial of degree
m
in an algebra
P
is zero, then

P
is called
nilpotent
. The linear span of
the values of monomials of degree
i
in
P
is denoted by
P^{i}
. One says that
P
is
c
-step nilpotent if
P^{c+1}=\{0\}
, but
P^{c}\neq\{0\}
. For each
c\geq 0
, the class
\mathfrak{N}_{c}
of all nilpotent algebras
P
satisfying
P^{c+1}=\{0\}
is primitive. The relatively free algebras in
\mathfrak{N}_{c}
are called
free
c
-step nilpotent algebras
. One has
F(\mathfrak{N}_{c},X)\cong\mathcal{F}(X)/(\mathcal{F}(X))^{c+1}
. The basis of
F(\mathfrak{N}_{c},X)
is formed by the (images) of all nonassociative monomials of degree at most
c
.

In any algebra
P
we have two series of ideals: the descending power series

(7)

P=P^{1}\supset P^{2}\supset\cdots P^{n}\supset\cdots

and the ascending
annihilator series
, defined as follows. For any algebra
P
, we define the ideal
I(P)
called the
annihilator
of
P
by setting

I(P)=\{x\in P\,|\,(\forall a\in P)(ax=xa=0)\}.

Then we define
I_{n}(P)
by induction on
n
if we set
I_{1}=I(P)
and define
I_{n}(P)
as the full preimage of
I(P/I_{n-1}(P))
in
P
,
n>1
. As a result, we have an ascending annihilator series of ideals of
P
:

(8)

I_{1}(P)\subset I_{2}(P)\subset\cdots\subset I_{n}(P)\subset\cdots

In the case of a
c
-step nilpotent algebra
P
, both series have finite length and
P^{k}\subset I_{c-k+1}(P)
, for any
k=1,2,\ldots,c
.

Remark 2
.

In distinction with the lower and upper central series in groups or Lie algebras, the length of the annihilator series can be shorter than the length of the power series. A quick example is the quotient-algebra
P
of the free
4
-step nilpotent algebra
F(x,y,z,t)
by the ideal generated by all monomials of degree 4 except
(xy)(zt)
. One has
I_{1}(P)=P^{3}
,
I_{2}(P)=P^{2}
,
I_{3}(P)=P
. At the same time,
P^{4}\neq\{0\}
.

3.2
Some special automorphisms and derivations of nilpotent algebras

The group

Definition 2

Given

(b) If we take

Some linear algebra

In this section we gather some facts from Linear Algebra, which will be needed in the proofs of the main results in Section
7
. We quickly recall one of the versions of the Structure Theorem of modules over the principal ideal domain (see e.g.
[
Lan
, Theorem 4 in Chapter XV]
) in the case of the polynomial ring in one variable. Recall that a vector space
V
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
with the action of a linear operator
B:V\to V
becomes a module over the polynomial ring
R={\mathbb{F}}[t]
via
tv=B(v)
, for any
v\in V
. If
V
is finite-dimensional then
V
is a finitely generated
R
-module.

Proposition 6
.

Assume that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V<\infty
. Then, as an
R
-module,
V
is the direct sum of cyclic submodules. Moreover, there exists a sequence of monic polynomials
f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t),\ldots,f_{r}(t)
satisfying
f_{1}(t)|f_{2}(t)|\cdots|f_{r}(t)
such that

(15)

V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r},\mbox{ where }V_{i}\cong R/(f_{i}(t)).

\hfill\Box

In what follows, we will call
V
as above, a
B
-module. If
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V=m
and
B=\lambda\,\textrm{id\,}_{V}
is a scalar operator then any minimal generating set of
V
as a
B
-module is a basis of
V
so that the minimal number of generators needed to generate
V
as a
B
-module equals
m
. If
V
is a
B
-module as in (
15
), then the minimal number of generators equals
r
. Number
r
is an invariant of a
B
-module
V
; it is called the
rank
of
V
and we write
\mathrm{rank}\,_{B}(V)=r
. We always have
\mathrm{rank}\,_{B}(V)\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
B
be a linear operator on a vector space
V
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V=m
and
\mathrm{rank}\,_{B}(V)=r
. Then for any
\ell\in[\frac{2m+r}{3},m]
there is a pair of subspaces
U\subset W
in
V
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W=\ell
,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U=m-\ell
,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}B(U)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U
, and
V=W\oplus B(U)
.

Proof.

The case
m=1
being obvious, we assume
m\geq 2
.

Fist assume that
V
is cyclic as a
B
-module and
e
is a generator of
V
as a
B
-module. We want to show that in this case we can relax the interval for
\ell
to
[\frac{m+1}{2},m]
.

Set
e_{i}=B^{i}(e)
,
i=0,\ldots,m-1
. Then
\{e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{m-1}\}
is a basis of
V
.

If
m
is even, we set

W^{0}=U^{0}=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{0},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m-2}\},

B(U^{0})=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{1},e_{3},\ldots,e_{m-1}\}.

We have
V=W^{0}\oplus B(U^{0})
, also
\frac{m}{2}=\ell^{0}=m-\ell^{0}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U^{0}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W^{0}
.

If
m
is odd, we set

W^{0}=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{0},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m-1}\},

U^{0}=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{0},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m-3}\}.

Then

B(U^{0})=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{1},e_{3},\ldots,e_{m-2}\}.

Again we have
V=W^{0}\oplus B(U^{0})
, also
\frac{m+1}{2}=\ell=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W^{0}
,
\frac{m-1}{2}=m-\ell=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U^{0}
.

In both cases, if we want to have
U\subset W
with a greater value for
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W
, we simply remove, say,
e_{0},\ldots,e_{2k}
, from the basis of
U
and add
e_{1},\ldots,e_{2k+1}
to the basis of
W
. So in the case where
V
is a cyclc
B
-module, we can find the desired pair
U\subset W
with any
\ell\in[\frac{m+1}{2},m]
.

Now if
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}
,
r>1
, each
V_{i}
cyclic of dimension
m_{i}\geq 2
, we consider
W_{i}^{0}
and
U_{i}^{0}
in each
V_{i}
, built in the same way as in the case of a cyclic module, so that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W_{i}^{0}=\frac{m_{i}}{2}
if
m_{i}
is even and
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W_{i}^{0}=\frac{m_{i}+1}{2}
if
m_{i}
is odd. Set
W^{0}=W_{1}^{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{r}^{0}
,
U^{0}=U_{1}^{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{r}^{0}
. We then have
V=W^{0}\oplus B(U^{0})
. Set
\ell_{0}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W^{0}
. We have
\ell_{0}\leq\frac{m+r}{2}
. Now let
\ell
be any number,
\ell_{0}\leq\ell\leq m
. If
\ell\neq m
then one of
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W_{i}\leq m_{i}
. Using the same method as in the case of a cyclic module, we can modify
W_{i}^{0}
and
U_{i}^{0}
so that the value of
\ell
grows by 1.

In the general case,
V=V^{\prime}\oplus V^{\prime\prime}
where
V^{\prime}
is as just considered, with
m^{\prime}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V^{\prime}
,
r^{\prime}=\mathrm{rank}\,_{B}(V^{\prime})
and
V^{\prime\prime}
is the sum of one-dimensional
B
-invariant subspaces. We have
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V^{\prime\prime}=m-m^{\prime}
. The rank
r
of
V
as a
B
-module equals
r^{\prime}+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V^{\prime\prime}=r^{\prime}+m-m^{\prime}
. Since
\frac{m^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}{2}\leq\frac{2m^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}{3}
, using our previous argument, for any
\ell^{\prime}\in[\frac{2m^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}{3},m^{\prime}]
we find
U^{\prime}\subset W^{\prime}\subset V^{\prime}
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}
,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U^{\prime}=m^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}=W^{\prime}\oplus B(U^{\prime})
. We set
U=U^{\prime}
and
W=W^{\prime}\oplus V^{\prime\prime}
.

Now we can conclude that since for
\ell^{\prime}
we can choose an arbitrary value in the interval
\left[\frac{2m^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}{3},m^{\prime}\right]
, we can get for
\ell
an arbitrary integral value in
\left[\frac{2m^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}{3}+(m-m^{\prime}),m^{\prime}+(m-m^{\prime})\right]=\left[\frac{2m+r}{3},m\right]
, as claimed.

∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
m
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
and

a linear operator. If
\mathrm{rank}\,_{B}(V)>\frac{m}{2}
then there is

and a subspace

of dimension

such that

.

Proof.

Let us use (

where

and

. If all of

had dimension greater than 1 then we would have

. So one of

is 1-dimensional, hence spanned by an eigenvector with eigenvalue

and also each

is divisible by

. As a result, in each

there is an eigenvector

with eigenvalue

for the

.

∎

Lemma 12
.

Let
V
be a vector space over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
, written as

for some subspaces

, and

some subspaces in

at most

of the subspaces

have nonzero projection to

. Then

(16)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}+\cdots+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n}\leq c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n}).

Proof.

We start with few remarks. Set
U=U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n}
. Since for any

the projections of each
U_{i}
to

are in the projections of

to
V_{j}
, the conditions of the Lemma will be satisfied if we replace each

by the projection
\textrm{pr\>}_{V_{j}}U

is assumed, setting

, for any

, by the Homomorphism Theorem applied to the projection map of

onto

, we will have

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n})-\dim_{\mathbb{F}}((U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n})\cap\widetilde{V_{j}})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{j}.

Also note that since
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{i}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U
,
in the case

, our lemma easily follows because

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}+\cdots+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n}\leq n\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U\leq c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n}).

To proceed with the proof, we apply induction by

. If
t=1
then
V_{1}=V
and

is its own projection to

, for all

of

are nonzero. Removing zero subspaces from (

Now we need to consider the case

are zero.

By induction, for

and the subspaces

, we have

(18)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n}^{\prime}\leq c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+U_{n}^{\prime}).

Note that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}^{\prime}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1},\ldots,\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n-c}^{\prime}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n-c}
. Also, since

U_{i}/U_{i}^{\prime}\cong U_{i}+V^{\prime}/V^{\prime}\subset V/V^{\prime}\cong V_{t},

we have that

(19)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{i}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{i}^{\prime}+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{t}\mbox{ for all }i=n-c+1,\ldots,n.

Using (

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}+...+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U_{n}^{\prime}+c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{t}

\displaystyle\leq c(\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+U_{n}^{\prime})+c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{t}

\displaystyle=c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}((U_{1}\cap V^{\prime})+...+(U_{n}\cap V^{\prime}))+c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{t}

\displaystyle\leq c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}((U_{1}+...+U_{n})\cap V^{\prime})+c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V_{t}

\displaystyle=c\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{1}+...+U_{n}),

as claimed. Note that in the last equality we have used that
V_{t}=\mathrm{pr}_{V_{t}}U
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

For any
c\geq 1
there exist positive
\kappa_{1}
and

with the following property. Consider a primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}\subset{\mathfrak{N}}_{c}

6.1
Basic notions

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field
{\mathbb{F}}
. Given
0\neq v\in V
, one denotes by
{\mathbf{P}}(v)
the one-dimensional subspace of
V
containing
v
. The
projective space

{\mathbf{P}}(V)
built on
V
is the set of all
{\mathbf{P}}(v)
. One has
{\mathbf{P}}(u)={\mathbf{P}}(v)
iff there is
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that
v=\lambda u
. Thus, given a basis
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
in
V
, the point
{\mathbf{P}}(v)
is defined by its
projective coordinates
, which is the equivalence class of non-zero tuples
(\alpha_{0}:\ldots:\alpha_{n})
such that
\alpha_{0}e_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e_{n}\in{\mathbf{P}}(v)
. The tuple
(\beta_{0}:\ldots:\beta_{n})
defines the same point iff there exists
\lambda\neq 0
such that

(24)

\alpha_{0}=\lambda\beta_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}=\lambda\beta_{n}.

If
\alpha:U\to V
is an isomorphism of vector spaces then
\alpha
establishes a bijection between one-dimensional subspaces of
U
and
V
, which is denoted by
{\mathbf{P}}(\alpha)
. If
U=V
then the set of all
{\mathbf{P}}(\alpha)
with
\alpha\in\textrm{GL}(V)
is a group
\textrm{PGL}(V)
, which is isomorphic to the factor-group of
\textrm{GL}(V)
by its center.

The (standard) projective space
{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
is the set of equivalence classes of nonzero tuples
(\alpha_{1}:\ldots:\alpha_{n+1})
under the equivalence relation given by (
24
).

The projective space
{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
is a topological space under the
Zariski topology
. A closed subset
\mathcal{X}\subset{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
in this topology is defined as the set of solutions of a system of homogeneous polynomial equations with respect to the projective coordinates of the points in
\textbf{P}^{n}
. One calls
\mathcal{X}
a
projective variety
. The variety
\mathcal{X}
is called
irreducible
if
\mathcal{X}
is not the union of its proper projective varieties. An open subset
\mathcal{Y}
of a projective variety is called a
quasi-projective variety
. One calls
\mathcal{Y}

irreducible
if its closure is irreducible.

A finite union of quasi-projective varieties in a projective space
\textbf{P}^{n}
is called a
constructible
set, or simply a
variety
; it does not need to be a quasi-projective variety.

A map
f
of an irreducible quasi-projective variety
\mathcal{X}
to a variety
\mathcal{Y}\subset{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
is a function given by homogeneous monomials of the same degree on the coordinates of the points of an open subset of
\mathcal{X}
. The image
f(\mathcal{X})
of a quasi-projective variety
\mathcal{X}
under a map
f
does not need to be quasi-projective. By
[
HAG
, Exercises 3.18]
,
f(\mathcal{X})
is a constructible set.

According to
[
Sha
, 1.6.2]
, Corollary 3, the dimension
\dim\mathcal{X}
of a projective variety
\mathcal{X}
can be defined as the
maximal integer
n
for which there exists a strictly decreasing chain

\mathcal{Y}_{0}\supset\mathcal{Y}_{1}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{Y}_{n}\supset\varnothing

of length
n
of irreducible subvarieties
\mathcal{Y}_{i}\subset\mathcal{X}
. The dimension of any subset can be defined as the dimension of its closure in the Zariski topology. If a constructible variety
\mathcal{X}
is a finite union of quasi-projective varieties
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{X}_{n}
then
\dim\mathcal{X}=\max\{\dim\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\dim\mathcal{X}_{n}\}
.

Note.
We reserve the notation
\dim\mathcal{X}
for the dimension of a
variety

\mathcal{X}
; we keep using
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V
for the dimension of a
vector space

V
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.

Remark 6
.

The
Cartesian product

\textbf{P}^{n}\times\textbf{P}^{m}
becomes a quasi-projective variety via Segré embedding

where

(see
[

and

then explicit formula is

, for all

,

. Using Segré embedding, one gives the structure of a quasi-projective subvariety in
\textbf{P}^{N}
to
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
for any quasi-projective varieties
\mathcal{X}\subset\textbf{P}^{n}
and

If

then

while

and

Some properties of the dimension are listed in the following. They all can be found in

Let

6.2
Grassmann varieties

Our main type of varieties will be
Grassmann varieties
, their products and images under the maps of varieties. For the proofs of the facts given below see
[
Sha
, Chapter 1, §6, Example 5]
.

Given a vector space
V
with
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V=n
and a number
r
,
0\leq r\leq n
, one considers the projective space
\textbf{P}(\Lambda^{r}(V))
where
\Lambda^{r}(V)
is the homogeneous component of degree
r
in the Grassman algebra
\Lambda(V)
of the vector space
V
. The nonzero elements of the form
u_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge u_{r}\in\Lambda^{r}(V)
, where
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in V
, are called
decomposable
. The points
{\mathbf{P}}(u_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge u_{r})
form a Zariski closed subset
\mathrm{Grass}(r,V)
in
\textbf{P}(\Lambda^{r}(V))
, hence a projective variety. The variety
\textrm{Grass\,}(r,V)
is irreducible and

(25)

\dim\textrm{Grass\,}(r,V)=r(n-r),\mbox{ where }n=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V.

The points of the projective variety
\mathrm{Grass}(r,V)
are in one-one correspondence with
r
-dimensional subspaces of
V
. The correspondence is given by
\mathrm{Span}\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}\mapsto{\mathbf{P}}(u_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge u_{r})
. Thus the set of
r
-dimensional subspaces
U
in an
n
-dimensional space
V
acquires the structure of a projective algebraic variety. One keeps the notation
\textrm{Grass\,}(r,V)
for this variety and calls this the
Grassmann variety of
r
-dimensional subspaces in an
n
-dimensional space
.

If
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}
is a basis of
V
then the elements of the form
e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{r}}
with
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\leq n
form a basis of
\Lambda^{r}(V)
. The projective coordinates of
{\mathbf{P}}(u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{r})
with respects to this basis are thus the projective coordinates of
U=\mathrm{Span}\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}
in the Grassmann variety
\textrm{Grass\,}(r,V)
. They are called the
Plücker coordinates
of
U
.

In the proofs about the properties of the maps of various constructions (unions, Cartesian products, etc.) of Grassmann varieties we will be using the following property mentioned in
[
Sha
, Example 1 in Section 4.1]
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be a basis of
V
. Let the Plücker coordinates in
\textrm{Grass\,}(r,V)
be defined via
\mathcal{B}
, as above. Then there exist homogeneous rational functions of degree zero with the following property. In every
r
-dimensional subspace
U
, there is a basis
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}
such that the coordinates of the vectors of this basis with respect to
\mathcal{B}
are the values of the above functions in the Plücker coordinates of
U
.

\hfill\Box

Lemma 20
.

Let
W
be a vector space,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}W=n
. Consider the Grassmannians
\mathcal{X}=\textrm{Grass\,}(s,W)
and
\mathcal{Y}=\textrm{Grass\,}(t,W)
, where
s+t\leq n
. Then there is an open subset
\mathcal{O}
of
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
consisting of the pairs
(U,V)
with
U\cap V=\{0\}
on which the map
(U,V)\to U\oplus V
is a map of varieties from
\mathcal{O}
to
\textrm{Grass\,}(s+t,W)
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are embedded in projective spaces
\textbf{P}^{k}=\textbf{P}(\Lambda^{s}(W))
and
\textbf{P}^{\ell}=\textbf{P}(\Lambda^{t}(W))
, respectively. Then
\mathcal{Z}
is canonically embedded in
P^{N}
, where
N=(k+1)(\ell+1)-1
. By Remark
6
, the homogeneous coordinates
w_{ij}
of the point
(U,V)
are expressed as homogeneous polynomials of degree 2 in the (Plücker) coordinates of
U
and
V
. From these formulas one can see that, conversely, the coordinates of the points
U
and
V
can be obtained as ratios of the coordinates
w_{ij}
on an open subset, for example, the subset of the points where
w_{00}\neq 0
.

If
e_{1}\ldots,e_{n}
is a basis in
W
then by Lemma
19
the Cartesian coordinates of a certain basis of
U
(also
V
) can be rationally expressed in terms of the Plücker coordinates of
U
(also
V
).

If
U\cap V=\{0\}
, using the Cartesian bases for
U
and
V
, one can form the Cartesian basis

for
U+V
. The coordinates of the vectors of this basis are used to compute the coordinates of

in the basis
\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{s+t}}\,|\,i_{1}<\ldots<i_{s+t}\}
of
\Lambda^{s+t}(W)
. These coordinates are the homogeneous (Plücker) coordinates of
U+V
in the variety
\textrm{Grass\,}(s+t,W)
.

The composition of the above maps is also given by homogeneous polynomials. Now we have to exclude the pairs
(U,V)
where
U\cap V\neq\{0\}
. The intersection is nonzero if the span of the union of the bases for
U
and
V
has dimension
<s+t
. This condition is given by equating to zero all minors of size
s+t
of the matrix composed of the Cartesian coordinates of the spanning vectors, in terms of the basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. Using Lemma
19
once again, we obtain an equation in terms of Plücker coordinates which defines the open subset
\mathcal{O}
. Note that
\mathcal{O}
is not empty because there do exist pairs
(U,V)
with

.

In what follows we will be dealing with the ideals of algebras, rather than subspaces. We denote by

the set of

-dimensional ideals of a finite-dimensional algebra

Lemma 21
.

Given an algebra

with
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}P=n\leq\infty
and a natural number

, the set

is a closed subspace in

.

Proof.

If

is a basis of
P
and

a basis in an ideal

of

then the matrix of coordinates of the latter basis in terms of the former one has rank

. If we add to this matrix the rows of coordinates of

and

-dimensional ideals in

to

For each

Lemma 22
.

The map

, depending on the placement of brackets

, is given by polynomials in the homogeneous coordinates on an open subset of the Grassmann variety of
s_{i}
-dimensional subspaces of the space

Proof.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d_{i+1}}
be a basis of
I_{i+1}
and
U
be a subspace in
I_{i+1}
. As mentioned earlier,

is defined by an

-vector

, where

. This

-vector is a linear combination of
s_{i}
-vectors

,

, forming a basis in

in the Grassmann variety
\textrm{Grass\,}(s_{i},I_{i+1})
as a subvariety in the projective space

.

According to Lemma

, there is a basis

in

such that the coordinates of the vectors of this basis with respect to

are homogeneous rational functions of degree zero in the Plücker coordinates of

Let us first consider the case

say

Using the the Cartesian coordinates of

, one can find the coordinates of the vectors spanning

, where

is any monomial in the free generators of

(linear functions whose coordinates depend on the structure constants of

). Let

be a basis of

in

are homogeneous rational functions of degree zero in terms of the Plücker coordinates of

Now let us choose

for which the number

, that is, of a quasi-projective variety. This completes the proof in the case where

The proof in the case of any other of finitely many different placements of brackets

goes in the same way except that instead of considering vectors

spanning

.
∎

To proceed further, for all

The dimension of this variety is at most

s(d_{h}+\cdots+d_{2})+j(d_{1}-j)\mbox{ where }s=s_{1}+\cdots+s_{h}.

The following property of the points

with

is a property selected by an algebraic equation. In the same way, generic is the property of the points

, saying that the sum of

At this time, it is convenient to write

For

, and any placement of brackets

Since

Lemma 23

Each ideal

of

, which is not in

,

Indeed,

for some subspace

in

, such that

for some tuple

such that

, following because

. In this case, we choose

,

Clearly, this subspace is in the ideal

by a direct summand

∎

Note that the same image

under

, such that

The dimension of the quasi-projective variety of such subspaces

. This estimate from below of the dimension of the preimage of any point of

s(d_{h+1}+\cdots+d_{2})+j(d_{1}-j)-\gamma js\frac{n}{c}\leq s(d_{h+1}+\cdots+d_{2})+j(d_{1}-j-\gamma s\frac{n}{c}).

We need one more lemma before we proceed to the main result of the section (Theorem

Lemma 24

If

,

Note that with

∎

Theorem 1
.

For any
c\geq 2
there is a number
n_{0}=n_{0}(c)
with the the following property. Suppose that
F_{n}
is a relatively free
c
-step nilpotent algebra of rank
n
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{J}(F_{n})
be the set of ideals of
F_{n}
, which belong to
I(F_{n})
. If
n\geq n_{0}
then being in
\mathcal{S}
is a generic property for the ideals of
F_{n}
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{J}(F_{n})\setminus\mathcal{S}
then
\dim\mathcal{T}<\dim\mathcal{J}(F_{n})-n^{2}
.

Proof.

All
N
that are not in
I(F_{n})
are the points of the finite union of all images
\varphi(\textbf{s},\varpi,i,j)(\mathcal{Z}_{\textbf{s},j})
, for different values of
\textbf{s},\varpi,i,j
, in accordance with Lemma
23
. Using Lemma
24
and the fact that the dimension of the union is the maximum of the dimensions of the terms of the union, we can see that all these ideals are the points of a projective variety whose dimension is less than
\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{4}-n^{2}
. Incidentally, if
d_{1}=2k
is even,
\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{4}=\dim\textrm{Grass\,}(k,I(F_{n}))
, the dimension of the Grassmannn variety of
k
-dimensional subspaces, equivalently,
k
-dimensional ideals of
F_{n}
which are in
I(F_{n})
. If
d_{1}=2k+1
is odd,
\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{4}-n^{2}=k(k+1)+\frac{1}{4}-n^{2}<k(k+1)=(k+1)(2k+1-k)=\dim\textrm{Grass\,}(k+1,I(F_{n}))
, the dimension of the Grassmannn variety of
k+1
-dimensional subspaces, equivalently,
k+1
-dimensional ideals of
F_{n}
, which are in
I(F_{n})
. The proof is now complete.
∎

Remark 7
.

Using the restrictions of Theorem
1
, the following properties of the ideals of
F_{n}
are generic. If

is even then a generic ideal of
F_{n}
is contained in

and has dimension
\frac{d_{1}}{2}
. If

is odd then a generic ideal of
F_{n}
is contained in

and has of one of two dimensions
\frac{d_{1}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}
or

.

7.2
Ideals and automorphisms of relatively free algebras

Before we move with our proofs any further, we have to impose a restriction on the primitive classes in question, as described below.

7.2.1
Central primitive classes

Given a relatively free algebra
F_{n}
of a
c
-step nilpotent primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras, the action of
\textrm{GL}(n)
on
F_{n}^{c}
introduced in Proposition
5
does not need to be always extendible to the action on the annihilator
I(F_{n})
. One of the solutions to this problem is to restrict oneself to considering only the primitive classes
{\mathfrak{V}}
in which
F^{c}_{n}=I(F_{n})
for any
{\mathfrak{V}}
-free algebra of any rank
n
. In many important particular cases, such as the primitive classes of all associative algebras, all nonassociative algebras, all commutative, all anticommutative and all Lie algebras this condition is satisfied. Once we assume
F_{n}^{c}=I(F_{n})
, for all
n
, a generic ideal of
F_{n}
is contained in
F_{n}^{c}
(Theorem
1
).

Definition 4
.

Let
c\geq 2
. A
c
-step nilpotent primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of algebras over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
is called
central
if
I(F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}}))=F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}})^{c}
, for all
n\geq 1
.

Remark 8
.

Probably the easiest counterexample of non-central primitive class is the
5
-step nilpotent primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
of Lie algebras with identity
((x_{1}x_{2})(x_{3}x_{4}))x_{5}=0
(“centrally metabelian” Lie algebras). In the free algebra
F_{5}=F_{5}({\mathfrak{V}})
of rank
5
one has
(x_{1}x_{2})(x_{3}x_{4})\in I(F_{5})\setminus F_{5}^{5}
.

Remark 9
.

In the case where
{\mathfrak{V}}
is multihomogeneous, to check that
V
is central, we only need to check that
I(F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}}))=F_{n}({\mathfrak{V}})^{c}
for the ranks
n\leq c+1
. This is a direct consequence of Lemma
9
, Claim (a).

In the remainder of the paper we assume that
{\mathfrak{V}}
is a central primitive class,
F_{n}
the
{\mathfrak{V}}
-free algebra of rank
n>n_{0}
. We also recall the notation
\mathcal{J}
for the variety of ideals in
F_{n}^{c}
. Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{J}
the set of ideals
K
of
F_{n}
such that
\textrm{Stab}(K)\subset\textrm{Scal}(F_{n})
(see Definition
2
).

Theorem 2
.

For each
c\geq 2
there exists a natural number
n_{0}
such that being in
\mathcal{R}
is a generic property for the ideals of
F_{n}
. If
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{J}(F_{n})\setminus\mathcal{R}
then
\dim\mathcal{U}<\dim\mathcal{J}(F_{n})-n^{2}
.

Before we proceed to the proof, we make several remarks which will be useful also in the future.

We classify all the ideals of
F_{n}
as Class 1, Class 2 and Class 3. Class 1 is formed by the ideals
K
such that
K\not\subset F_{n}^{c}
.
By Theorem
1
, there is a variety of ideals of
F_{n}
of dimension strictly less than
\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{4}-n^{2}
which contains all the ideals of Class 1.

The ideals in
F_{n}^{c}
fall into classes 2 and 3. The dimension of the variety of ideals (=subspaces) of dimension
m
in
F_{n}^{c}
is given by the quadratic function

(29)

q(m)=m(d-m)=\dim\textrm{Grass\,}(m,d)=\dim\textrm{Grass\,}(m,F_{n}^{c}).

The maximum value of

is a rational number
\frac{d^{2}}{4}
. Now to split the ideals in
F_{n}^{c}
into two classes, we introduce an interval for the values of the dimension

:

(30)

\Omega=\left[\frac{d}{2}-n,\frac{d}{2}+n\right]

An easy exercise shows that for the values of

outside

we have

. We say that the ideals of dimension
m
in
F_{n}^{c}
such that

form Class 2. As a result,it is true that there is a variety of dimension strictly less than
\frac{d^{2}}{4}-n^{2}
which contains all the ideals of Class 1 and Class 2.

Now the ideals of Class 3 are those ideals of
F_{n}
which are not in Class 1 or Class 2.

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

By what we just mentioned, we only need to consider varieties of ideals of Class 3. We denote by
\mathcal{M}
be the Zarisski closure of the set of all Class 3 ideals of
F_{n}
,
with the following property. For each

there is an

-automorphism
\alpha
such that
\alpha(V)=V
. We need to prove that
\dim\mathcal{M}<\frac{d^{2}}{4}-n^{2}
. Note that according to Proposition
5
,
V
is invariant under the linear operator
\widetilde{A}
induced by
A\in GL(n)
, where

is induced by
\alpha
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{G}
be the set of linear maps
\widetilde{A}
on
F_{n}^{c}
, which are obtained from all
A\in\textrm{GL}(n)
,
as in Proposition
5
. The map

is a morphism of varieties

, so that

.

Using Lemma
13
, we choose an integer

such that

(31)

kd>5n^{2}\mbox{ (we remember that }c\geq 2).

If
n
is sufficiently great, we can choose

so that

.

We set
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\mathcal{Y}_{2}
where
\mathcal{Y}_{1}=\textrm{Grass\,}(k,F_{n}^{c})
and
\mathcal{Y}_{2}=\textrm{Grass\,}(m-2k,F_{n}^{c})

The dimension of

is given by

(32)

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(d-k)+(m-2k)(d-m+2k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(d-k)+\left(\frac{d}{2}-2k\right)\left(\frac{d}{2}+2k\right)+\Theta.

where

is a number such that

(for sufficiently great values of

).

Let us define the map
\varphi:\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\textrm{Grass\,}(m,I)
, given by

(33)

\varphi((\widetilde{A},(U,W)))=W*U*\widetilde{A}(U).

The right hand side is an

is a basis of

and

is a basis of

then the

-dimensional space

is defined by an

Note that this map is not always well-defined because the

are linearly dependent.

To handle this difficulty, we consider two cases.

Case 1
. In this case, we assume that
\mathrm{rank}\,_{\widetilde{A}}V\leq m-3k
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
V
be an
m
-dimensional
\widetilde{A}
-invariant subspace in

. Assume that
\mathrm{rank}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\,V\leq m-3k
. Then there exists a pair
(U,W)\in\mathcal{Y}
, such that
\varphi((\widetilde{A},(U,W)))=V
.

Proof.

Let us apply Lemma
10
. Since
r\leq m-3k
, we have that
\frac{2m+r}{3}\leq m-k
, so that
\ell=m-k
is within the limits for
\ell
in the statement of that lemma. As a result, there is a subspace
U
in
V
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}U=m-\ell=k
and

. Let
W
be any complement of dimension

to

in

. Then

.
∎

Using the same argument as in the proof of Lemma

, the Plücker coordinates of

given by (

) are homogeneous polynomials of the same degree in the projective coordinates of
\widetilde{A}
,
U
and

. Thus

, given by (

), is a map of an open subset of the irreducible projective variety
\mathcal{Z}
.

To proceed, we first estimate

.

Since

, it follows from (

\dim\mathcal{Z}=k(d-k)+\left(\frac{d}{2}-2k\right)\left(\frac{d}{2}+k\right)+n^{2}-1+\Theta.

In the statement of the next lemma the operators
\widetilde{A}
are induced by the operators

, as in Proposition

. So

is an
\widetilde{A}
-module, for each

.

Lemma 26
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of ideals
V
of dimension
m

, such that
\mathrm{rank}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\,V\leq m-3k

.

Proof.

To prove our claim, we first use Lemma
25
to choose

7.3

Remark 11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a connected algebraic group. One says that
\mathcal{G}
acts on an algebraic variety
\mathcal{Z}
if there is everywhere defined regular map
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
, satisfying
(gh)(z)=g(hz)
and
1z=z
, for all
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
and

. In this case, the set of orbits
\mathcal{Z}/\mathcal{G}
is an algebraic variety and there is a rational map
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}/\mathcal{G}
which takes different values on different orbits and constant on each particular orbit. The pre-image of any point is an orbit. Since the dimension of an orbit is at most the dimension of the acting group
\mathcal{G}
, using Lemma
18
, Claim (c), it follows that
\dim\mathcal{Z}/\mathcal{G}\geq\dim\mathcal{Z}-\dim\mathcal{G}
.

According Key observations, the dimension of varieties of ideals in Classes 1, 2 and 3a is always less than

. The same can then be said about the varieties of quotient-algebras by these ideals.

Let us consider Class 3b and compare the dimensions of varieties of algebras

on one hand and

is of Class 3b on the other. In both cases

, which amounts the action of
\textrm{PGL}(n)
are equal to the dimensions of varieties of ideals minus

.

From these estimates and (

∎

7.5
Width of verbal ideals in generic algebras

Given an algebra
P
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
and any nonassociative polynomial
f=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, one can consider a verbal ideal
f(P)
of
P
generated by
f
. If
{\mathfrak{V}}
is the primitive class defined by the identical relation
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})=0
then
f(P)
is the verbal ideal of
P
corresponding to
{\mathfrak{V}}
. Let
\overline{f(P)}
stands for the closure of the set of all values
f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}\in P
, under all possible multiplications by the elements of
P
. Then
f(P)
is the set of all linear combinations of the elements in
\overline{f(P)}
.

An element

is said to have

-width

if
a
is a linear combination of
m
elements from
\overline{f(P)}
and
m
is minimal with this property. We write

.

Finally, if exists, the number

w_{f}(f(P))=\max\{w_{f}(a)\,|\,a\in f(P)\}

is called the

of
f(P)
. If not, we write
w_{f}(f(P))=\infty
.

For example, for any algebra

the

-width of an element

with respect to

is equal to the least number
m
of nonzero summands in the expressions of the form

, where
b_{i},c_{i}\in P
. In the case of Lie algebras the width with respect to
xy
is called the
commutator width
. It is known to be finite for any finitely generated Lie algebra. For many finitely generated solvable Lie algebras the commutator width of the commutator subalgebra has been found by V. A. Romankov
[
Rom
]
.

The definition of the width of
f(P)
naturaly extends to the width with respect to arbitrary sets
{\mathfrak{f}}
of nonassociative polynomials and verbal ideals
{\mathfrak{f}}(P)
. We use the notation

and

Lemma 30
.

\mathrm{(a)}

If two finite sets of elements

(respectively, one and the same subclass

(respectively, any

).

\mathrm{(b)}

If the base field

is infinite and

contains a nonzero linear part then there exists a positive

w_{f}(f(P))\leq C
for any algebra

.

\mathrm{(c)}

If a primitive class

is nilpotent and

contains a nonzero linear part then for any field and any nonzero

we have
\overline{f(P)}=P
and

Proof.

(a)

Since the verbal ideals of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
(respectively,

) generated by

and

coincide, each polynomial in

bound from above the number of summands for all polynomials in

. Then replace in this expression each polynomial of
{\mathfrak{f}}
by its expression through the polynomials of

. Then the number of summands in the resulting expression of

through

will get increased by at most

(b)

By Lemma

,

defines the same primitive class

as the set of its multihomogeneous components, hence

Follows from

∎

8

In this section,
{\mathbb{F}}
is a finite field of

Definition 5

Let

be an infinite sequence of pairwise disjoint finite sets and

a sequence of subsets

. Let

\frac{|T_{n}|}{|S_{n}|}\to 1
(
\frac{|T_{n}|}{|S_{n}|}\to 1
faster than
1-q^{n}
for some

), as
n\to\infty
. One could also say that a
generic element
of

is in
T
.

In this section the role of

will be played by one of two objects. This could be the set
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
of the isomorphism classes of
n
-generated algebras in a
c
-step nilpotent primitive class
{\mathfrak{V}}
over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
. The other option is the set
\mathcal{J}_{n}
of ideals of a relatively free nilpotent algebra
F_{n}
.

All
n
-generated algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}
have the form of
F_{n}/K
, where

. The connection between the two cases is that by Proposition
5
and Theorem
8
, there is one-one correspondence between the algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
and the orbits of the natural action of
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})
on
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. In the particular case of quotient-algebras modulo the ideals in
F_{n}^{c}
, the isomorphism classes of such algebras are in one-one correspondence with the orbits of the natural action of the groups
\textrm{GL}(n)
on the set of subspaces of
F_{n}^{c}
(see Proposition
5
). Therefore, the results about the generic (properties of)
n
-generated algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}
are closely connected to the results about the generic (properties of) the ideals of the sequence of algebras
F_{n}
.

In Section
8.1
we give some estimates for the number of ideals in finite algebras. In Section
8.2
we examine the generic properties of the ideals of
F_{n}
. Finally, on the basis of this, in Section
8.3
, we turn our attention to the generic properties of

-generated algebras in
{\mathfrak{V}}
.

In the study of ideals of

, we denote by

the set of ideals inside the annihilator ideal

of

. We are going to prove that the sequence

.

Then we consider the set

of ideals each of which is invariant under an

-automorphism. We will show that the sequence of numbers

is exponentially negligible when compared with

Our results about the structure of generic algebras and their automorphisms in this section are quite similar to the results of Section

8.4
The automorphism group of a generic nilpotent algebra

Given a relatively free algebra
F_{n}=F_{\mathfrak{V}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
,
|{\mathbb{F}}|=q
, we call an element
f\in F_{n}

quasihomogeneous
if, when written as a polynomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, all its monomials have the same degree modulo
q-1
. For each
\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}
, we have a subspace
F_{n}^{\bar{k}}
spanned by the monomials of all degrees
\ell
such that
\ell\in\bar{k}
. We have
F_{n}=\sum_{\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}}F_{\bar{k}}
. We can write
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}}f_{\bar{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, where
f_{\bar{k}}\in F_{\bar{k}}
. Let us prove that this sum is direct. By contradiction, assume

(55)

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}}f_{\bar{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0

so that the number of nonzero summands is the least possible, different from zero. Suppose that
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
is the generator of the (cyclic) multiplicative group
{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
. If
\bar{k}\neq\bar{\ell}
then
\lambda^{k}\neq\lambda^{\ell}
.

Now (
55
) is an identity in
{\mathfrak{V}}
(see Lemma
2
). If we apply an automorphism
\psi
of
F_{n}
sending each
x_{i}
to
\lambda x_{i}
to (
55
), it remains to be identity. We will have

f(\lambda x_{1},\ldots,\lambda x_{n})=\sum_{\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}}\lambda^{k}f_{\bar{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0.

If for
k\leq\ell
we have
\bar{k}\neq\bar{\ell}
we have
f_{\bar{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\neq 0
and
f_{\bar{\ell}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\neq 0
subtracting from the latter relation (
55
) multiplied by
\lambda^{k}
, we will have a nontrivial relation with less number of summands. Thus
F_{n}=\oplus_{\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}}F_{\bar{k}}
is indeed the direct sum of quasi homogeneous components. Actually, this is a grading of
F_{n}
by the group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q-1}
.

It follows that the multiplication of the free generators by a scalar
\lambda\neq 0
extends to an automorphism of
F_{n}
such that each quasi homogeneous component
F_{\bar{k}}
is multiplied by
\lambda^{k}
. Even if the
c
-step nilpotent algebra
F_{n}
is not graded, every element of
F_{n}^{c}
is multiplied by
\lambda^{c}
which is true because
F_{n}^{c}
is contained in the graded component
F_{\bar{c}}
. It now follows that any ideal
N
inside
I=F_{n}^{c}
is invariant under such automorphism. Since by Theorem
6
a generic algebra
P
is isomorphic to a quotient-algebra
F_{n}/N
, where
N\subset I
, we conclude that a generic algebra admits a well defined faithful action by an automorphism of order
q-1
.

Finally, since the automorphism which are identity modulo
F_{n}^{2}
act trivially on
N
, it follows that in a generic algebra
P
the map adding to any of
n
generators an element of
P^{2}
extends to an automorphism of
P
. Since by Remark
3
,
I\subset F_{n}^{2}
, it follows that
|P/P^{2}|=|F_{n}/F_{n}^{2}|=q^{n}
, we obtain a formula for the order of the automorphism group of a generic algebra
P
in the following.

Proposition 10
.

Let
P
be a generic
n
-generated algebra in a central
c
-step nilpotent,
c\geq 2
, primitive class of algebras over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
with
q
elements. Then

|\mathrm{Aut}(P)|=(q-1)\left(\frac{|P|}{q^{n}}\right)^{n}.

The group
\mathrm{Aut}(P)
is a solvable group which is a semidirect product of a
(c-1)
-step nilpotent normal subgroup and the multiplicative group of
{\mathbb{F}}
.
\hfill\Box

3
, in the case of finite fields.
