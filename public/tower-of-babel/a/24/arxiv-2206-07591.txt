Gradient flows in asymmetric metric spaces and applications
Shin-ichi Ohta, Wei Zhao

Abstract
. This paper is devoted to the investigation of gradient flows in asymmetric metric spaces (for example, irreversible Finsler manifolds and Minkowski normed spaces) by means of discrete approximation. We study basic properties of curves and upper gradients in asymmetric metric spaces, and establish the existence of a curve of maximal slope, which is regarded as a gradient curve in the non-smooth setting. Introducing a natural convexity assumption on the potential function, which is called the ( p , λ ) (p,\lambda) -convexity, we also obtain some regularizing effects on the asymptotic behavior of curves of maximal slope. Applications include several existence results for gradient flows in Finsler manifolds, doubly nonlinear differential evolution equations on infinite-dimensional Funk spaces, and heat flow on compact Finsler manifolds.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this article is to develop the theory of gradient flows in
asymmetric
metric spaces
(i.e., the symmetry
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
is not assumed; see Definition
2.1
).
Typical and important examples are gradient flows of geodesically convex functions
on irreversible Finsler manifolds (or Minkowski normed spaces).
The theory of gradient flows has been successfully developed in “Riemannian-like” spaces
such as CAT
(0)
-spaces and RCD-spaces
(see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
11
,
20
,
24
,
30
,
31
,
38
,
43
]
);
nonetheless, the lack of the Riemannian-like structure causes a significant difference
and we know much less about gradient flows in “Finsler-like” spaces
(see
[
31
, Remark 3.2]
,
[
33
]
and Subsection
4.5
for more details).
In this article, based on the recent work
[
15
]
on the geometry of asymmetric metric spaces,
we investigate gradient flows in asymmetric metric spaces
by generalizing the minimizing movement scheme as in
[
1
]
.

Compared with the preceding studies
[
6
,
36
]
on asymmetric metric spaces,
we will be able to remove some conditions on the space
(X,d)
or the potential function
\phi

by a more careful analysis
(see Remarks
2.26
,
3.11
for details).
Moreover, the notion of
(p,\lambda)
-convexity (discussed in Section
4
)
seems new and of independent interest even in the symmetric case.

Asymmetric metrics often occur in nature and can be represented as Finsler metrics;
a prominent example is the Matsumoto metric
describing the law of walking on a mountain slope under the action of gravity
(see
[
19
]
).
Randers metrics appearing as solutions to the Zermelo navigation problem
(concerning a Riemannian manifold with “wind” blown on it)
provide another important class of irreversible metrics (see
[
4
]
).
A particular example of the latter metric is given as a “non-symmetrization”
of the Klein metric on the
n
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball

\mathbb{B}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\|x\|<1\}
(
n\geq 2
),
called the
Funk metric
(see, e.g.,
[
40
, Example 1.3.5]
),
defined as
F:\mathbb{B}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\lra[0,\infty)
by

(1.1)

F(x,v)=\frac{\sqrt{\|v\|^{2}-(\|x\|^{2}\|v\|^{2}-\langle x,v\rangle^{2})}+\langle x,v\rangle}{1-\|x\|^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{B}^{n},\ v\in T_{x}\mathbb{B}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\|\cdot\|
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote
the Euclidean norm and inner product, respectively.
The associated distance function
d_{F}
is written as (see
[
40
, Example 1.1.2]
)

(1.2)

d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})=\log\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}-(\|x_{1}\|^{2}\|x_{2}\|^{2}-\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle^{2})}-\langle x_{1},x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}-(\|x_{1}\|^{2}\|x_{2}\|^{2}-\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle^{2})}-\langle x_{2},x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle}\Bigg),\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}.

It is readily seen that
d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})\neq d_{F}(x_{2},x_{1})
and,
for
\mathbf{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
,

\lim_{\|x\|\to 1}d_{F}(\mathbf{0},x)=\infty,\qquad\lim_{\|x\|\to 1}d_{F}(x,\mathbf{0})=\log 2.

The Funk metric
(\mathbb{B}^{n},d_{F})

will be one of the model asymmetric structures we have in mind.
We remark that the symmetrization
d(x_{1},x_{2}):=\{d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{F}(x_{2},x_{1})\}/2

coincides with the Klein metric.

For functions on an asymmetric metric space
(X,d)
,
we shall study the associated
curves of maximal slope
in
X
(see Subsection
2.5
);
this conception generalizes gradient curves in the smooth setting.
In order to deal with the asymmetry, the
reversibility
of
(X,d)
, defined by

\lambda_{d}(X):=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{d(x,y)}{d(y,x)},

will be instrumental.
Clearly
\lambda_{d}(X)\geq 1
,
and
\lambda_{d}(X)=1
holds if and only if
d
is symmetric.
The reversibility may be
\infty
for noncompact asymmetric metric spaces.
Actually, for the model Funk metric (
1.1
),
a direct calculation yields
\lambda_{d_{F}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})=\infty
and

\lambda_{d_{F}}(B^{+}_{\mathbf{0}}(r))\leq 2{\ee}^{r}-1
,
where
B^{+}_{\mathbf{0}}(r)
is the forward open ball of radius
r
centered at
\mathbf{0}
, i.e.,

B^{+}_{\mathbf{0}}(r)=\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}|\,d_{F}(\mathbf{0},x)<r\}
.
The latter estimate suggests to consider a collection of pointed spaces

(X,\star,d)
whose reversibility satisfies
\lambda_{d}(B^{+}_{\star}(r))\leq\Theta(r)

for some non-decreasing function
\Theta:(0,\infty)\lra[1,\infty)
.
Such spaces are called
forward metric spaces

(see Subsection
2.1
) and intensively studied in
[
15
]
.
For instance, the Gromov–Hausdorff topology and the theory of curvature-dimension condition
developed by Lott, Sturm and Villani can be generalized to such spaces.
Every forward complete Finsler manifold is a forward metric space (see
[
15
]
for details).

In the present article, by generalizing the theory of
[
1
]
to forward metric spaces,
we are able to obtain some existence and regularity results of curves of maximal slope.
Among others, we establish the existence of curves of maximal slope
satisfying the energy identity (Theorem
3.30
),
and show some estimates on the behavior of the potential function and its upper gradient
along curves of maximal slope (Theorem
4.17
).

In the latter result, we make use of the
(p,\lambda)
-convexity
,
defined in Definition
4.1
by

\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)\leq(1-t)\phi\big(\gamma(0)\big)+t\phi\big(\gamma(1)\big)-\frac{\lambda}{p}t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}\big(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)\big)

(which slightly differs from the
(\lambda,p)
-convexity studied in
[
37
]
;
see Remark
4.2
for a further account), plays a role.
As an application, we have the following in the Finsler setting
(see Corollaries
3.33
,
4.19
for the precise statements).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,F)
be a forward complete Finsler manifold and
\phi\in C^{1}(M)
.
Then, for any
x_{0}\in M
,
there exists a
C^{1}
-curve
\xi:[0,T)\lra M
solving the gradient flow equation

\xi^{\prime}(t)=\nabla(-\phi)\big(\xi(t)\big),\qquad\xi(0)=x_{0},

where
\lim_{t\to T}d_{F}(x_{0},\xi(t))=\infty
if
T<\infty
.
If
\phi
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex for some
\lambda>0
,
then
T=\infty
,
\xi(t)
converges to a unique minimizer
\bar{x}
of
\phi

and
F(\nabla(-\phi)(\xi(t)))
decreases to
0
as
t\to\infty
.

Another application is concerned with “infinite dimensional Finsler spaces”
such as the unit ball in a Hilbert space endowed with the Funk metric (
1.2
)
and a reflexive Banach space
(\mathscr{B},\|\cdot\|)
equipped with
a Randers-type metric
d(x,y)=\|y-x\|+\omega(y-x)

for some
\omega\in\mathscr{B}^{*}
with
\|\omega\|_{*}<1
.
For such spaces, we prove that curves of maximal slope satisfy
a doubly nonlinear differential evolution equation or inclusion,
which generalizes some results in
[
36
]

(see Subsection
3.6
for details).

Last but not least, we also investigate gradient flows in the Wasserstein space
over a compact Finsler manifold.
We establish the equivalence between weak solutions to the heat equation
and trajectories of the gradient flow for the relative entropy (in the same spirit as
[
7
,
32
]
),
as well as the following existence result of weak solutions to the heat equation
as curves of maximal slope, which gives a direct construction rather than the extension
by the
L^{2}
-contraction as in
[
32
]
(see Subsection
4.4
for a further account).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M,F)
be a compact Finsler manifold endowed with a smooth positive measure
\m
.
For any function
u\in L^{2}(M)
bounded above,
there exists a weak solution
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}

to the heat equation
\partial_{t}u_{t}=\Delta_{\m}u_{t}
with
u_{0}=u
.

We stress that there remain important open problems for gradient flows
in Finsler-like spaces, even in (symmetric) normed spaces; see Subsection
4.5
.
We hope that our work motivates further investigations.

This article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce some necessary concepts to analyze
curves of maximal slope in forward metric spaces.
In Section
3
, we take advantage of the Moreau–Yosida approximation
to prove the existence of curves of maximal slope under mild topological assumptions.
Section
4
is devoted to the investigation of curves of maximal slope
for
(p,\lambda)
-convex functions, including some further existence and regularity results
and the study of heat flow.

2.
Curves and upper gradients in asymmetric metric spaces

2.1.
Forward metric spaces

First we discuss the basic properties of asymmetric metric spaces.
We refer to
[
6
,
15
,
21
,
22
,
36
,
39
]
for related studies (partly with different names).

Definition 2.1

(Asymmetric metric spaces)
.

Let
X
be a nonempty set and
d:X\times X\lra[0,\infty)
be a nonnegative function on
X\times X
.
We call
(X,d)
an
asymmetric metric space
if
d
satisfies

(1)

d(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in X
with equality if and only if
x=y
;

(2)

d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

The function
d
is called a
distance function
or a
metric
on
X
.

Since the function
d
could be asymmetric (i.e.,
d(x,y)\neq d(y,x)
),
there are two kinds of balls.
For a point
x\in X
and
r>0
, the
forward
and
backward balls

of radius
r
centered at
x
are defined as

B^{+}_{x}(r):=\{y\in X\,|\,d(x,y)<r\},\qquad B^{-}_{x}(r):=\{y\in X\,|\,d(y,x)<r\}.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{T}_{-}
) denote the topology
induced from forward balls (resp. backward balls).
In order to study the relation between
\mathcal{T}_{+}
and
\mathcal{T}_{-}
,
the following notion on the reversibility of
d
was introduced in
[
15
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Pointed forward
\Theta
-metric spaces)
.

Let
\Theta:(0,\infty)\lra[1,\infty)
be a (not necessarily continuous) non-decreasing function.
A triple
(X,\star,d)
is called a
pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space

if
(X,d)
is an asymmetric metric space and
\star
is a point in
X
such that

\lambda_{d}(B^{+}_{\star}(r))\leq\Theta(r)
for all
r>0
, where

\lambda_{d}\big(B^{+}_{\star}(r)\big):=\inf\big\{\lambda\geq 1\,\big|\,d(x,y)\leq\lambda d(y,x)\text{ for any }x,y\in B^{+}_{\star}(r)\big\}.

If we can take a constant function
\Theta\equiv\theta
(i.e.,
\lambda_{d}(X)\leq\theta
),
then we call
(X,d)
a
\theta
-metric space
.

Note that we have the bounded reversibility
\lambda_{d}<\infty
only on forward balls,
thereby the reversibility of backward balls may be infinite (like the Funk metric).
For pointed forward
\Theta
-metric spaces,
the backward topology is weaker than the forward topology as follows (see
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
).

Theorem 2.3

(Properties of
\mathcal{T}_{+}
)
.

Let
(X,\star,d)
be a pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space.
Then,

(i)

\mathcal{T}_{-}\subset\mathcal{T}_{+}
holds and,
in particular,
d
is continuous in
\mathcal{T}_{+}\times\mathcal{T}_{+}

and
(X,\mathcal{T}_{+})
is a Hausdorff space
;

(ii)

\mathcal{T}_{+}
coincides with the topology
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}

induced from the symmetrized distance function

\widehat{d}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\big\{d(x,y)+d(y,x)\big\}.

We remark that the topology
\tau
considered in
[
21
,
22
]

coincides with
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
.
Precisely,
\tau
is induced from both forward and backward balls
and associated with
\widetilde{d}(x,y):=\max\{d(x,y),d(y,x)\}
,
then
\widehat{d}\leq\widetilde{d}\leq 2\widehat{d}
yields
\tau=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
.
Some more remarks on pointed forward
\Theta
-metric spaces are in order.

Remark 2.4
.

(a)

A sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
X
converges to
x

with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{+}
if and only if

\lim_{i\to\infty}d(x,x_{i})=0,

which implies the convergence
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},x)=0
in
\mathcal{T}_{-}

(thanks to
\lambda_{d}(B^{+}_{\star}(r))\leq\Theta(r)<\infty
).
However, the converse does not necessarily hold true (when
d(\star,x_{i})\to\infty
).

(b)

If
(X,\star,d)
is a pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space, then, for every
x\in X
,
the triple
(X,x,d)
is a pointed forward
\overline{\Theta}
-metric space
for
\overline{\Theta}(r):=\Theta(d(\star,x)+r)
.
Moreover, if
\diam(X):=\sup_{x,y\in X}d(x,y)<\infty
,
then
(X,d)
is a
\theta
-metric space with
\theta:=\Theta(\diam(X))
.

(c)

One can similarly introduce a
pointed backward
\Theta
-metric space

(X,\star,d)
by
\lambda_{d}(B^{-}_{\star}(r))\leq\Theta(r)
for
r>0
.
Note that a pointed backward
\Theta
-metric space may not be
a pointed forward
\overline{\Theta}
-metric space for any
\overline{\Theta}
;
recall the Funk metric in the introduction.
Since
(X,\star,d)
is a pointed backward
\Theta
-metric space
if and only if
(X,\star,\overleftarrow{d})
is a pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space
for the
reverse metric

\overleftarrow{d}(x,y):=d(y,x)
,
we will focus only on pointed forward
\Theta
-metric spaces.

Notation 2.5

(Forward metric spaces)
.

In the sequel, every pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space
(X,\star,d)

is endowed with the forward topology
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.
Suppressing
\star
and
\Theta
for the sake of simplicity,
we will write
(X,d)
and call it a
forward metric space
.

Forward metric spaces possess many fine properties.
For example, one can define a generalized Gromov–Hausdorff topology
to study the convergence of forward metric spaces,
and Gromov’s precompactness theorem remains valid.
Besides, optimal transport theory can be developed and
the weak curvature-dimension condition in the sense of Lott–Sturm–Villani
[
18
,
41
,
42
]

is stable also in this setting.
Furthermore, various geometric and functional inequalities
(such as Brunn–Minkowski, Bishop–Gromov, log-Sobolev and Lichnerowicz inequalities)
can be established.
We refer to
[
15
]
for details and further results.

We next recall some concepts related to the completeness (cf.
[
21
,
22
,
15
,
39
]
).

Definition 2.6

(Completeness)
.

Let
(X,d)
be an asymmetric metric space.

(1)

A sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
X
is called
a
forward
(resp.
backward
)
Cauchy sequence
if, for each
\varepsilon>0
,
there is
N\geq 1
such that
d(x_{i},x_{j})<\varepsilon
(resp.
d(x_{j},x_{i})<\varepsilon
) holds
for all
j\geq i>N
.

(2)

(X,d)
is said to be
forward
(resp.
backward
)
complete

if every forward (resp. backward) Cauchy sequence in
X
is convergent
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.

(3)

We say that
(X,d)
is
forward
(resp.
backward
)
boundedly compact

if every closed set in any forward (resp. backward) bounded ball is compact.

If
\Theta
is bounded (i.e.,
\lambda_{d}(X)<\infty
),
then the forward and backward properties are mutually equivalent.
However, they are not equivalent when
\Theta
is unbounded;
e.g., the Funk metric is forward complete but backward incomplete.

2.2.
Forward absolutely continuous curves

Let
(X,\star,d)
be a forward complete pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space in this subsection.

Definition 2.7

(Forward absolutely continuous curves)
.

A curve
\gamma:I\lra X
on an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is said to be

p
-forward absolutely continuous
for
p\in[1,\infty]

(denoted by
\gamma\in\FAC^{p}(I;X)
) if there is a nonnegative function
f\in L^{p}(I)
such that

(2.1)

d\big(\gamma(s),\gamma(t)\big)\leq\int^{t}_{s}f(r)\,{\dd}r\quad\text{for all}\ s,t\in I\ \text{with}\ s\leq t.

We will denote
\FAC^{1}(I;X)
by
\FAC(I;X)

and call its element a
forward absolutely continuous
curve.

A standard argument combined with the forward completeness yields the following.

Lemma 2.8
.

Any curve
\gamma\in\FAC^{p}([a,b);X)
is forward uniformly continuous,
i.e., for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))<\varepsilon
holds for any
s,t\in[a,b)
with
0\leq t-s<\delta
.
In particular, if
b<\infty
, then the left limit
\gamma(b_{-}):=\lim_{t\to b^{-}}\gamma(t)
exists.
Moreover, if
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b);X)
, then
\gamma(b_{-})
exists even when
b=\infty
.

We remark that, for
\gamma\in\FAC^{p}((a,b);X)
, the right limit

\gamma(a_{+}):=\lim_{t\to a^{+}}\gamma(t)
may not exist.
This is the reason why we call
\gamma
a
forward
absolutely continuous curve.
For example, in the Funk space (
1.1
),
consider the unit speed minimal geodesic
\gamma:(-\log 2,0]\lra\mathbb{B}^{n}

such that
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{0}
and
\gamma(t)
converges to
(-1,0,\ldots,0)

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
t\to-\log 2
.
Clearly
\gamma\in\FAC((-\log 2,0];\mathbb{B}^{n})
with
f\equiv 1
in (
2.1
);
however,
\gamma
is not defined at
t=-\log 2
.
In fact,
\lim_{t\to-\log 2}d_{F}(\mathbf{0},\gamma(t))=\infty
.

Thanks to Lemma
2.8
, we always have

\FAC^{p}([a,b);X)=\FAC^{p}([a,b];X)
for
b<\infty

and
\FAC([a,\infty);X)=\FAC([a,\infty];X)
.
Hence, we will mainly consider
\FAC^{p}([a,b];X)
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Owing to
[
36
, Proposition 2.2]
, there always exists a minimal function
f

satisfying (
2.1
) as follows (we also refer to

[
1
, Theorem 1.1.2]
,
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
and
[
32
, Lemma 7.1]
).

Theorem 2.9

(Forward metric derivative)
.

Suppose that either
p=1
with
b\leq\infty
or
p\in(1,\infty]
with
b<\infty
.
Then, for any curve
\gamma\in\FAC^{p}([a,b];X)
, the limit

|\gamma_{+}^{\prime}|(t):=\lim_{s\to t}\frac{d(\gamma(\min\{s,t\}),\gamma(\max\{s,t\}))}{|t-s|}

exists for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
and we have

d\big(\gamma(s),\gamma(t)\big)\leq\int^{t}_{s}|\gamma_{+}^{\prime}|(r)\,{\dd}r\quad\text{ for any }a\leq s\leq t\leq b.

Moreover,
|\gamma_{+}^{\prime}|
belongs to
L^{p}([a,b])

and satisfies
|\gamma_{+}^{\prime}|(t)\leq f(t)
for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)

for any
f
satisfying (
2.1
).

We call
|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|
the
forward metric derivative
of
\gamma
,
and
\mathscr{L}^{1}
denotes the
1
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

One can also define the
p
-backward absolute continuity
by requiring (
2.1
)
for
s,t\in I
with
t\leq s
.
Then, due to the asymmetry, the resulting
backward metric derivative

|\gamma^{\prime}_{-}|

may not coincide with
|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|
.

Definition 2.10

(Length)
.

For
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
, its
length

L(\gamma)
is defined by

L(\gamma):=\int^{b}_{a}|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t.

Thanks to Theorem
2.9
and Lemma
2.8
,
it is not difficult to see that

L(\gamma)=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}d\big(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})\big)\,\middle|\,N\in\mathbb{N},\ a=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{N}=b\right\}.

Definition 2.11

(Lipschitz curves)
.

A curve
\gamma:[a,b]\lra X
is said to be
C
-Lipschitz
for
C>0

if it satisfies
d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))\leq C(t-s)
for all
a\leq s\leq t\leq b
.

Obviously, a
C
-Lipschitz curve belongs to
\FAC([a,b];X)
(provided
b<\infty
)
and
|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\leq C
for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
.
The next lemma (proved in the same manner as
[
1
, Lemma 1.1.4]
)
tells that every forward absolutely continuous curve can be viewed as a Lipschitz curve.

Lemma 2.12

(Reparametrization)
.

Given
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
with length
L:=L(\gamma)
, set

\mathfrak{s}(t):=\int^{t}_{a}|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(r)\,{\dd}r,\qquad\mathfrak{t}(s):=\min\{t\in[a,b]\,|\,\mathfrak{s}(t)=s\}.

Then
\mathfrak{s}:[a,b]\lra[0,L]
is a non-decreasing absolutely continuous function
and
\mathfrak{t}:[0,L]\lra[a,b]
is a left-continuous increasing function such that

\mathfrak{s}(a_{+})=0
,
\mathfrak{s}(b_{-})=L
and
\mathfrak{s}\circ\mathfrak{t}(s)=s
.
Moreover, the curve
\hat{\gamma}:=\gamma\circ\mathfrak{t}:[0,L]\lra X
is

1
-Lipschitz and satisfies

\gamma=\hat{\gamma}\circ\mathfrak{s}
and
|\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}_{+}|=1

\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e. in
[0,L]
.

2.3.
Asymmetric metrics on Finsler manifolds

In this subsection, we discuss the case of Finsler manifolds as a typical example of
asymmetric metric spaces.

2.3.1.
Finsler manifolds

We first recall the basics of Finsler geometry;
see
[
3
,
27
,
40
]
for further reading.
Let
M
be an
n
-dimensional connected
C^{\infty}
-manifold without boundary,
and
TM=\bigcup_{x\in M}T_{x}M
be its tangent bundle.
We call
(M,F)
a
Finsler manifold
if a nonnegative function
F:TM\lra[0,\infty)

satisfies

(1)

F\in C^{\infty}(TM\setminus\{0\})
;

(2)

F(cv)=cF(v)
for all
v\in TM
and
c\geq 0
;

(3)

For any
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
, the
n\times n
symmetric matrix

g_{ij}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}[F^{2}]}{\partial v^{i}\partial v^{j}}(v)

is positive-definite, where
v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{i}(\partial/\partial x^{i})|_{x}

in a chart
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
around
x
.

We remark that
g_{ij}(v)
cannot be defined at
v=0
unless
F
is Riemannian.
For
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
, we define a Riemannian metric
g_{v}
of
T_{x}M

by
g_{v}(w,\bar{w}):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{ij}(v)w^{i}\bar{w}^{j}
.
Euler’s homogeneous function theorem yields that

F^{2}(v)=g_{v}(v,v)
for any
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
.
The
reversibility
of
F
on
U\subset M
is defined as

\lambda_{F}(U):=\sup_{v\in TU\setminus\{0\}}\frac{F(-v)}{F(v)}.

If
\lambda_{F}(M)=1
(i.e.,
F(-v)=F(v)
for all
v\in TM
),
then we say that
F
is
reversible
.
Note that
\lambda_{F}(U)<\infty
for any compact set
U\subset M

thanks to the smoothness of
F
.

We define the
dual Finsler metric

F^{*}
of
F
by

F^{*}(\zeta):=\sup_{v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\zeta(v)}{F(v)},\quad\zeta\in T_{x}^{*}M.

By definition, we have

(2.2)

\zeta(v)\leq F(v)F^{*}(\zeta).

Then the
Legendre transformation

\mathfrak{L}:T_{x}M\lra T_{x}^{*}M

is defined by
\mathfrak{L}(v):=\zeta
, where
\zeta
is the unique element satisfying

F(v)=F^{*}(\zeta)
and
\zeta(v)=F^{2}(v)
.
Note that
\mathfrak{L}:TM\setminus\{0\}\lra T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}
is a diffeomorphism.
For
f\in C^{1}(M)
,
the
gradient vector field
of
f
is defined by
\nabla f:=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}({\dd}f)
.
We have
{\dd}f(v)=g_{\nabla f}(\nabla f,v)
provided
{\dd}f\neq 0
.
We remark that the grandient
\nabla
is nonlinear; indeed,

\nabla(f+h)\neq\nabla f+\nabla h
and
\nabla(-f)\neq-\nabla f
in general
(the latter is due to the irreversibility of
F
).
Note also that

(2.3)

\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{d_{F}(x,y)}=F^{*}\big({\dd}f(x)\big)=F\big(\nabla f(x)\big),

where
d_{F}
is the Finsler distance function defined below.

2.3.2.
Length structure and absolutely continuous curves

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}([0,1];M)
denote the class of piecewise smooth curves in
M

defined on
[0,1]
.
Given
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{\infty}([0,1];M)
, we define its
length
by

L_{F}(\gamma):=\int^{1}_{0}F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\,{\dd}t.

Then the associated
distance function

d_{F}:M\times M\lra[0,\infty)
is defined as

d_{F}(x,y):=\inf\{L_{F}(\gamma)\,|\,\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{\infty}([0,1];M),\,\gamma(0)=x,\,\gamma(1)=y\}.

Note that
d_{F}:M\times M\lra[0,\infty)
is a continuous function
and
(M,d_{F})
is an asymmetric metric space in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
Indeed,
d_{F}(x,y)
may not coincide with
d_{F}(y,x)
unless
F
is reversible.
Observe also that
\mathcal{T}_{+}=\mathcal{T}_{-}
is exactly the original topology of
M
.

A Finsler manifold is said to be
forward
(resp.
backward
)
complete

if
(M,d_{F})
is forward (resp. backward) complete in the sense of Definition
2.6

(see
[
3
, Theorem 6.6.1]
for a Finsler analogue of the Hopf–Rinow theorem).
As for an estimate of the reversibility, one has the following (see
[
15
, Theorem 2.23]
).

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
(M,F)
be a forward complete Finsler manifold.
Then, for any
\star\in M
, the triple
(M,\star,d_{F})
is a pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space for

\Theta(r):=\lambda_{F}\Big(B^{+}_{\star}\big(2r+\lambda_{F}\big(B^{+}_{\star}(r)\big)r\big)\Big).

Since
M
is smooth and the reversibility is finite on every compact set,
the study of absolutely continuous curves can be largely reduced
to the case of Riemannian manifolds or Euclidean spaces.
We only briefly explain for thoroughness.
According to
[
5
]
,
we say that a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\lra M
is
absolutely continuous

if, for any chart
\varphi:U\lra\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
M
, the composition

\varphi\circ\gamma:\gamma^{-1}(U)\lra\varphi(U)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is locally absolutely continuous, i.e., absolutely continuous on any closed subinterval of

\gamma^{-1}(U)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{\ac}([0,1];M)
denote the class of absolutely continuous curves defined on
[0,1]
.
For any
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{\ac}([0,1];M)
,
the derivative
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
exists for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in[0,1]
and we can define

L_{F}(\gamma):=\int^{1}_{0}F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\,{\dd}t.

Note that
L_{F}(\gamma)<\infty
and
F(\gamma^{\prime})\in L^{1}([0,1])
.
Moreover, we have

(2.4)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{d(\gamma(t),\gamma(t+\delta))}{\delta}=F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(0,1).

One can see
\mathcal{A}_{\ac}([0,1];M)=\FAC([0,1];M)
by an argument similar to that of
[
5
, Proposition 3.18]
.
Then, for
\gamma\in\FAC([0,1];M)
, we find from (
2.4
) that

(2.5)

|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)=F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(0,1).

2.4.
Upper gradients

In this subsection, we introduce upper gradients for functions on asymmetric metric spaces.
First, let us consider the case of Finsler manifolds.

Example 2.14
.

Let
(M,d_{F})
be a forward metric space induced by a forward complete Finsler manifold
(M,F)
.
Given
\phi\in C^{1}(M)
, for any
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];M)
,

\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous and (
2.2
) yields

(2.6)

(\phi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)={\dd}\phi\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\leq F^{*}\big({\dd}\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)\big)F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)=F\big(\nabla\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)\big)F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)

for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
.
Hence, a nonnegative function
\mathfrak{g}:M\lra[0,\infty)

satisfies
F(\nabla\phi)\leq\mathfrak{g}
if and only if

\phi\big(\gamma(t_{2})\big)-\phi\big(\gamma(t_{1})\big)\leq\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)F\big(\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\,{\dd}t

for all
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];M)
and
a\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq b
.

Now, let
(X,d)
be a forward complete forward metric space.
In what follows, let
\phi:X\lra(-\infty,\infty]
denote a
proper
function, i.e.,
its
proper effective domain

\mathfrak{D}(\phi):=\{x\in X\,|\,\phi(x)<\infty\}

is nonempty.

Definition 2.15

(Strong upper gradients)
.

A function
\mathfrak{g}:X\lra[0,\infty]
is called a
strong upper gradient
for
\phi

if, for every curve
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
,
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma
is Borel and satisfies

(2.7)

\phi\big(\gamma(t_{2})\big)-\phi\big(\gamma(t_{1})\big)\leq\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t\quad\text{for all}\ a\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq b.

Notice that, as is natural in view of Example
2.14

(see also Example
2.20
),
we did not take the absolute value in the left-hand side of (
2.7
).
Therefore, our definition of upper gradients is weaker than
[
6
, Definition 3.6]
.

If
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
,
then
\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous (see the remark below) and

(\phi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)\leq\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\quad\text{for }\mathscr{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.}\ t\in(a,b).

Remark 2.16
.

For
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
,
we deduce from (
2.7
) for
\gamma
and its reverse curve that

\big|\phi\big(\gamma(t_{1})\big)-\phi\big(\gamma(t_{2})\big)\big|\leq\Theta\big(d\big(\star,\gamma(0)\big)+L(\gamma)\big)\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t

for all
a\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq b
.
Hence, if
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
,
then
\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous and

|(\phi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)|\leq\Theta\big(d\big(\star,\gamma(0)\big)+L(\gamma)\big)\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\quad\text{for }\mathscr{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.}\ t\in(a,b).

Definition 2.17

(Weak upper gradients)
.

A function
\mathfrak{g}:X\lra[0,\infty]
is called a
weak upper gradient
for
\phi

if, for every curve
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
satisfying

(1)

\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
;

(2)

\phi\circ\gamma
is
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e. equal to a function
\varphi

with finite pointwise variation in
(a,b)
(see
[
1
, (1.1.2)]
),

we have

(2.8)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq\mathfrak{g}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma_{+}^{\prime}|(t)\quad\text{for }\mathscr{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.}\ t\in(a,b).

Note that a strong upper gradient is a weak upper gradient.
A sufficient condition for a weak upper gradient to be a strong upper gradient is as follows.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a weak upper gradient for
\phi
.
If
\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous for every
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)

with
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
,
then
\mathfrak{g}
is a strong upper gradient for
\phi
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
and
a\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq b
.
On the one hand, if
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(t_{1},t_{2})
,
then
\varphi=\phi\circ\gamma
satisfies (
2.8
) and hence (
2.7
) holds.
On the other hand,
(
2.7
) is trivial if
\mathfrak{g}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\not\in L^{1}(t_{1},t_{2})
.
∎

Definition 2.19

(Slopes)
.

The
local
and
global
(
descending
)
slopes
of
\phi
at
x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)

are defined by

|\partial\phi|(x):=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x,y)},\qquad\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x):=\sup_{y\neq x}\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x,y)},

respectively, where
[a]_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
.
For
x\in X\setminus\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
,
we set
|\partial\phi|(x)=\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x):=\infty
.

The local slope
|\partial\phi|
represents how fast the function
\phi
can decrease.
We remark that
|\partial(-\phi)|\neq|\partial\phi|
even in symmetric metric spaces.

Example 2.20
.

Let
(M,d_{F})
and
\phi
be as in Example
2.14
.
It follows from (
2.3
) that
F(\nabla(-\phi)(x))=|\partial\phi|(x)
.
We remark that
F(\nabla(-\phi))
may not coincide with either
F(-\nabla\phi)
or
F(\nabla\phi)
.
Note also that
|\partial\phi|=F(\nabla(-\phi))
is a strong upper gradient for
-\phi

by (
2.5
) and (
2.6
).

In general, we have the following (cf.
[
1
, Theorem 1.2.5]
).

Theorem 2.21

(Slopes are upper gradients)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a forward complete forward metric space
and
\phi:X\lra(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper function.

(i)

|\partial\phi|
is a weak upper gradient for
-\phi;

(ii)

If
\phi
is lower semicontinuous
(
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{+})
,
then
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
is lower semicontinuous and a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
.

Proof.

(
i
)
Let
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
and
\varphi
satisfy the assumptions (1), (2)
in Definition
2.17
for
-\phi
.
Set

A:=\big\{t\in(a,b)\,\big|\,{-}\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)=\varphi(t),\,\text{$\varphi$ is differentiable at }t,\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\text{ exists}\big\}.

Note that
(a,b)\setminus A
is
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-negligible (by Theorem
2.9
).
To see (
2.8
), it suffices to consider
t\in A
with
\varphi^{\prime}(t)>0
.
Since
\varphi^{\prime}(t)>0
,
we may assume that
\gamma(s)\neq\gamma(t)
for
s\,(\neq t)
close to
t
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to t^{+},\,s\in A}\frac{\phi(\gamma(t))-\phi(\gamma(s))}{s-t}\leq\limsup_{s\to t^{+},\,s\in A}\frac{\phi(\gamma(t))-\phi(\gamma(s))}{d(\gamma(t),\gamma(s))}\limsup_{s\to t^{+},\,s\in A}\frac{d(\gamma(t),\gamma(s))}{s-t}

\displaystyle\leq|\partial\phi|\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t).

Therefore,
|\partial\phi|
is a weak upper gradient for
-\phi
.

(
ii
)
We first prove the lower semicontinuity.
It follows from the assumption that
x\longmapsto[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}
is lower semicontinuous.
Hence, for any sequence
x_{i}\to x
and
y\neq x
,
we have
x_{i}\neq y
for large
i
and

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x_{i})\geq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{[\phi(x_{i})-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x_{i},y)}\geq\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x,y)}.

Then, taking the supremum in
y\neq x
furnishes the lower semicontinuity of
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
.

Next, we prove that
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
is a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
.
Since
|\partial\phi|\leq\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
by definition,

\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
is a weak upper gradient for
-\phi
.
Therefore, in view of Proposition
2.18
,
it is sufficient to show the following.

Claim 2.22
.

For any
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)

with
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
,

-\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous.

To this end, for
\mathfrak{t}:[0,L]\lra[a,b]
given by Lemma
2.12

with
L=L(\gamma)
, we set

\hat{\gamma}:=\gamma\circ\mathfrak{t},\quad\varphi:=-\phi\circ\hat{\gamma},\quad g:=\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\circ\hat{\gamma}.

Since
L<\infty
and
\hat{\gamma}([0,L])=\gamma([a,b])
,
the triangle inequality yields

\lambda:=\sup_{s\in[0,L]}\Theta\big(d\big(\star,\hat{\gamma}(s)\big)\big)\leq\Theta\big(d\big(\star,\gamma(a)\big)+L\big)<\infty.

Recalling that
\hat{\gamma}
is a
1
-Lipschitz curve, we have

d(\hat{\gamma}(s_{1}),\hat{\gamma}(s_{2}))\leq\lambda|s_{1}-s_{2}|
for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,L]

(regardless of the order of
s_{1},s_{2}
), thereby

(2.9)

[\varphi(s_{2})-\varphi(s_{1})]_{+}\leq\lambda g(s_{1})|s_{1}-s_{2}|\quad\text{for all}\ s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,L].

Thus, we have

(2.10)

|\varphi(s_{1})-\varphi(s_{2})|=[\varphi(s_{1})-\varphi(s_{2})]_{+}+[\varphi(s_{2})-\varphi(s_{1})]_{+}\leq\lambda\big(g(s_{1})+g(s_{2})\big)|s_{1}-s_{2}|.

Moreover, by the hypothesis
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)

and Lemma
2.12
, we find

\int^{L}_{0}g(s)\,{\dd}s=\int_{0}^{L}\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\big(\gamma\big(\mathfrak{t}(s)\big)\big)\,{\dd}s=\int^{b}_{a}\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\big(\gamma(t)\big)|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t<\infty.

Therefore, we obtain
g\in L^{1}(0,L)
, which together with (
2.10
)
and
[
1
, Lemma 1.2.6]
yields that
\varphi
belongs to
W^{1,1}(0,L)

with
|\varphi^{\prime}|\leq 2\lambda g
and has a continuous representative.

To see that
\varphi
itself is continuous, on the one hand,
note that
\varphi
is upper semicontinuous by the lower semicontinuity of
\phi
.
On the other hand, we infer from (
2.9
) and
g\in L^{1}(0,L)
that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int^{\varepsilon}_{-\varepsilon}\varphi(s+r)\,{\dd}r\geq\varphi(s)\quad\text{for all}\ s\in(0,L).

This implies that
\varphi
is continuous and, since it lives in
W^{1,1}(0,L)
,
absolutely continuous.
Then we observe from
-\phi(\gamma(t))=\varphi(\mathfrak{s}(t))

that
-\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous.
This completes the proof of Claim
2.22
.
∎

2.5.
Curves of maximal slope

Let
(X,d)
be a forward complete forward metric space.
For
p\in[1,\infty]
, denote by
\FAC^{p}_{\loc}((a,b);X)

the class of
locally
p
-forward absolutely continuous curves

\xi
defined on
(a,b)
,
i.e.,
\xi|_{[s,t]}\in\FAC^{p}([s,t];X)
for every
a<s<t<b
.

Definition 2.23

(Curves of maximal slope)
.

Let
\phi:X\lra(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper function,

\mathfrak{g}
be a weak upper gradient for
-\phi
, and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
We call
\xi\in\FAC^{1}_{\loc}((a,b);X)

a
p
-curve of maximal slope
for
\phi
with respect to
\mathfrak{g}

if
\phi\circ\xi
is
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e. equal to a non-increasing function
\varphi
satisfying

(2.11)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq-\frac{1}{p}|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|^{p}(t)-\frac{1}{q}\mathfrak{g}^{q}\big(\xi(t)\big)\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.\ $t\in(a,b)$},

where
1/p+1/q=1
.
In the case of
p=2
, we simply call
\xi
a
curve of maximal slope
.

In fact, equality holds in (
2.11
) for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
as follows.

Proposition 2.24

(Energy identity)
.

If
\xi:(a,b)\lra X
is a
p
-curve of maximal slope for
\phi
with respect to
\mathfrak{g}
,
then we have
\xi\in\FAC_{\lo}^{p}((a,b);X)
and
\mathfrak{g}\circ\xi\in L^{q}_{\lo}(a,b)
with

(2.12)

|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|^{p}(t)=\mathfrak{g}^{q}\big(\xi(t)\big)=-\varphi^{\prime}(t)\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(a,b).

Moreover, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
,
then
\varphi=\phi\circ\xi
is locally absolutely continuous in
(a,b)
and satisfies
the
energy identity

(2.13)

\frac{1}{p}\int^{t}_{s}|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|^{p}(r)\,{\dd}r+\frac{1}{q}\int^{t}_{s}\mathfrak{g}^{q}\big(\xi(r)\big)\,{\dd}r=\phi\big(\xi(s)\big)-\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)\quad\text{for all}\,\ a<s<t<b.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is non-increasing,
\varphi^{\prime}(t)
is locally integrable.
This together with (
2.11
) implies
\mathfrak{g}\circ\xi\in L^{q}_{\lo}(a,b)

and
|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{p}_{\lo}(a,b)
, and hence
\xi\in\FAC_{\lo}^{p}((a,b);X)
and

\mathfrak{g}\circ\xi\,|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}_{\loc}(a,b)
.
For
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
,
since
\mathfrak{g}
is a weak upper gradient for
-\phi
,
we have
-\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq\mathfrak{g}(\xi(t))|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|(t)
.
Combining this with the Young inequality yields the reverse inequality to
(
2.11
), thereby we obtain (
2.12
).

If
\mathfrak{g}
is additionally a strong upper gradient,
then
\phi\circ\xi
is locally absolutely continuous in
(a,b)
(recall Remark
2.16
)
and we have
\varphi=\phi\circ\xi
.
Then it follows from the Young inequality and (
2.11
) that

\displaystyle\phi\big(\xi(s)\big)-\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\int^{t}_{s}\mathfrak{g}\big(\xi(r)\big)|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|(r)\,{\dd}r\leq\frac{1}{p}\int^{t}_{s}|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|^{p}(r)\,{\dd}r+\frac{1}{q}\int^{t}_{s}\mathfrak{g}^{q}\big(\xi(r)\big)\,{\dd}r

\displaystyle\leq-\int^{t}_{s}\varphi^{\prime}(r)\,{\dd}r=\phi\big(\xi(s)\big)-\phi\big(\xi(t)\big).

This furnishes the energy identity (
2.13
).
∎

Example 2.25
.

Let
(M,d_{F})
and
\phi
be as in Example
2.14
.
According to (
2.5
) and Example
2.20
,
if
\xi:(a,b)\lra M
is a
p
-curve of maximal slope for
\phi

with respect to
F(\nabla(-\phi))=F^{*}(-{\dd}\phi)
, then we have

(2.14)

(\phi\circ\xi)^{\prime}(t)=-F^{p}\big(\xi^{\prime}(t)\big)=-F^{*}\big({-}{\dd}\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)\big)^{q}.

This implies
(\phi\circ\xi)^{\prime}(t)=-F(\xi^{\prime}(t))F^{*}(-{\dd}\phi(\xi(t)))
,
and hence
\xi^{\prime}(t)=\alpha(t)\nabla(-\phi)(\xi(t))
holds for some
\alpha(t)\geq 0
.
Actually, we deduce from (
2.14
) that

(2.15)

\xi^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}F^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\big(\nabla(-\phi)\big(\xi(t)\big)\big)\cdot\nabla(-\phi)\big(\xi(t)\big)&\text{ if }\nabla(-\phi)\big(\xi(t)\big)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ if }\nabla(-\phi)\big(\xi(t)\big)=0.\end{cases}

In particular,
\xi
is
C^{1}
since
\phi
is
C^{1}
,
thereby (
2.14
) holds for all
t\in(a,b)
.
We may rewrite (
2.15
) as
\mathfrak{j}_{p}(\xi^{\prime}(t))=\nabla(-\phi)(\xi(t))

by introducing an operator
\mathfrak{j}_{p}:TM\lra TM
defined by

\mathfrak{j}_{p}(v):=F^{p-2}(v)v
if
v\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{j}_{p}(0):=0
.
In the case of
p=2
, we obtain the usual gradient flow equation
\xi^{\prime}(t)=\nabla(-\phi)(\xi(t))
.
We stress that
\xi^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla\phi(\xi(t))
holds only when
F
is reversible.

We conclude the section with a comparison to the setting of
[
36
]
.

Remark 2.26
.

In
[
36
]
, they considered a convex function
\psi:[0,\infty)\lra[0,\infty]

satisfying some natural conditions (see
[

, (2.30)]
) and investigated curves
\xi
fulfilling

(2.16)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq-\psi\big(|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\big)-\psi^{*}\big(\mathfrak{g}(\xi(t))\big)

instead of (
2.11
),
where

.
Choosing
\psi(x)=x^{p}/p
recovers (

).
Nonetheless, to establish the corresponding existence theory,

[

),

3.
Generalized minimizing movements and curves of maximal slope

3.1.
Problem and strategy

Throughout this section, let
(X,d)
be a forward complete forward metric space,

\phi:X\lra(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper function, and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
The main objective of this section is to study the following problem.

Problem 3.1
.

Given an initial datum
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
,
does there exist a
p
-curve
\xi:(0,\infty)\lra X
of maximal slope for
\phi

such that
\lim_{t\to 0}\xi(t)=x_{0}
?

We shall solve this problem via a discrete approximation.
We begin with some definitions and notations.

Definition 3.2

(Resolvent operator)
.

We define the
p
-resolvent operator
by, for
\tau>0
and
x\in X
,

J_{\tau}[x]:=\argmin\Phi(\tau,x;\cdot),

where

\Phi(\tau,x;y):=\phi(y)+\frac{d^{p}(x,y)}{p\tau^{p-1}},\quad y\in X.

That is to say,
y\in J_{\tau}[x]
if and only if
\Phi(\tau,x;y)\leq\Phi(\tau,x;z)
for all
z\in X
.

Let
P_{\gtau}:=\{0=t^{0}_{\gtau}<t^{1}_{\gtau}<\cdots<t^{k}_{\gtau}<\cdots\}

be a partition of the time interval
[0,\infty)
corresponding to a sequence of
positive time steps
\gtau=(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in the sense that

\tau_{k}=t^{k}_{\gtau}-t^{k-1}_{\gtau},\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}t^{k}_{\gtau}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}=\infty.

Set
\|{\gtau}\|:=\sup_{k\geq 1}\tau_{k}
.
We will consider the following recursive scheme:

(3.1)

\text{Given }\Xi^{0}_{\gtau}\in X,\text{ whenever }\Xi^{1}_{\gtau},\ldots,\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}\text{ are known, take }\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}\in J_{\tau_{k}}\big[\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}\big].

This is a well-known scheme to construct (descending) gradient curves of
\phi
.
The following example in the case of Minkowski spaces may be helpful to understand
the choice of
J_{\tau}[x]
as above.

Example 3.3
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{n},F)
be a
Minkowski space
, i.e.,
each of its tangent spaces is canonically isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{n},F)
,
and
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
For any
C^{1}
-curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\lra\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
\gamma(0)=\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
, we have

\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}\bigg|_{t=0}d\big(\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau},\gamma(t)\big)=\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}\bigg|_{t=0}F\big(\gamma(t)-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}\big)=\frac{g_{\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}}({\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}},v)}{F(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau})},

provided
\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}\neq\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}
.
Combining this with
\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}|_{t=0}\Phi(\tau_{k},\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau};\gamma(t))=0

by the choice (
3.1
) of
\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}
, we find

-{\dd}\phi(v)=g_{\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}}(w,v)=[\mathfrak{L}(w)](v),\quad\text{where }\,w=\frac{F^{p-2}(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau})}{\tau^{p-1}_{k}}(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}).

Since
v
was arbitrary, we arrive at the equation

w=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}(-{\dd}\phi(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}))=\nabla(-\phi)(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})
.
By the choice of
w
, this is equivalent to

\frac{\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}-\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}}{\tau_{k}}=F^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\big(\nabla(-\phi)(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})\big)\cdot\nabla(-\phi)(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}),

which can be regarded as a discrete version of (
2.15
).

Definition 3.4

(Discrete solutions)
.

Given
{\gtau}
,
\Xi^{0}_{\gtau}\in X

and a sequence
(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})_{k\geq 1}
solving (
3.1
),
we define a piecewise constant curve
\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}:[0,\infty)\lra X
by

\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}(0):=\Xi^{0}_{\gtau},\qquad\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t):=\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}\quad\text{for }t\in(t^{k-1}_{\gtau},t^{k}_{\gtau}],\ k\geq 1.

We call
\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}
a
discrete solution
corresponding to the partition
P_{\gtau}
.

Under appropriate conditions on
(X,d)
and
\phi
,
we shall solve Problem
3.1
in the following steps:

•

Show that the minimization algorithm (
3.1
) starting from
x_{0}
is solvable;

•

Find a sequence
(P_{\gtau_{m}})_{m}
of admissible partitions with
\|{\gtau_{m}}\|\to 0

such that the discrete solutions
(\overline{\Xi}{}_{{\gtau}_{m}}^{k})_{k\geq 1}
converge to
a solution to Problem
3.1
with respect to a suitable topology
\sigma
on
X
.

3.2.
Topological assumptions

In the sequel, we always assume that
\sigma
is a Hausdorff topology on
X
,
possibly different from
\mathcal{T}_{\pm}
, compatible with
d
in the following sense:

(1)

\sigma
is weaker than the forward topology
\mathcal{T}_{+}
induced from
d

(i.e.,
x_{i}\to x
in
\mathcal{T}_{+}
implies
x_{i}\to x
in
\sigma
);

(2)

d
is
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous
(i.e., if
x_{i}\to x
and
y_{i}\to y
in
\sigma
, then
\liminf_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},y_{i})\geq d(x,y)
).

We will denote by
x_{i}\,\overset{\sigma}{\lra}\,x

the convergence with respect to the topology
\sigma
.

Remark 3.5

(Topology comparison)
.

(a)

Recall that
d
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-continuous by Theorem
2.3
,
thereby
\sigma=\mathcal{T}_{+}
always satisfies (1) and (2) above.

(b)

If
x_{i}\,\overset{\mathcal{T}_{-}}{\lra}\,x
,
then we deduce from the triangle inequality the following upper semicontinuity:

\limsup_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},y)\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}\big\{d(x_{i},x)+d(x,y)\big\}=d(x,y).

(c)

By the
\sigma
-sequential lower semicontinuity of
d
, the limit under
\sigma
is unique.
Indeed, if
x
and
x^{\prime}
are
\sigma
-limit points of
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
then
0=\liminf_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},x_{i})\geq d(x,x^{\prime})
necessarily holds,
thereby
x=x^{\prime}
.

We give an example where
\sigma
is different from
\mathcal{T}_{\pm}

(see
[
1
, Remark 2.3.9]
for another example).

Example 3.6

(Randers-like spaces)
.

Let
(X,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a Hilbert space
and
\|x\|:=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}
.
Choose
a\in X
with
\|a\|<1
and define a function
d:X\times X\lra[0,\infty)
by

d(x,y):=\|y-x\|+\langle a,y-x\rangle.

Then
(X,d)
is a
[(1+\|a\|)/(1-\|a\|)]
-metric space (recall Definition
2.2
), and

\mathcal{T}_{+}=\mathcal{T}_{-}
coincides with the (strong) topology of
(X,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.
Now, let
\sigma
be the weak topology of
X
.
Since
\|\cdot\|
is
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous, so is
d
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Every
\sigma
-sequentially compact set
K\subset X
is forward complete.

Proof.

Given a forward Cauchy sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
K
, on the one hand,
the
\sigma
-sequential compactness of
K
yields
a subsequence
(x_{i_{j}})_{j\geq 1}
of
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}

converging to a point
x^{\prime}\in K
in
\sigma
.
On the other hand,
the forward completeness of
X
furnishes a point
x\in X

such that
x_{i}
converges to
x
in
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.
Since
\sigma
is weaker than
\mathcal{T}_{+}
,
x_{i}
converges to
x
in
\sigma
as well.
Hence,
x_{i_{j}}
converges to both
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\sigma
,
and we find from Remark
3.5
(
c
) that
x=x^{\prime}\in K
.
Thus,
K
is forward complete.
∎

A set
A\subset X
is said to be
forward bounded

if
A\subset B^{+}_{\star}(r)
for some
r>0
.
We remark that, thanks to
\lambda_{d}(B^{+}_{\star}(r))\leq\Theta(r)

in Definition
2.2
,

A
is forward bounded if and only if
\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)<\infty
.

Now we introduce our main assumptions on
(X,d)
and
\phi
.

Assumption 3.8
.

(a)

Lower semicontinuity.

\phi
is
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous on forward bounded sets, i.e.,
if
\sup_{i,j}d(x_{i},x_{j})<\infty
and
x_{i}\,\overset{\sigma}{\lra}\,x
,
then we have
\liminf_{i\to\infty}\phi(x_{i})\geq\phi(x)
.
(In particular,
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous.)

(b)

Coercivity.

There exist
\tau_{*}>0
and
x_{*}\in X
such that

\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*}):=\inf_{y\in X}\Phi(\tau_{*},x_{*};y)=\inf_{y\in X}\Bigg\{\phi(y)+\frac{d^{p}(x_{*},y)}{p\tau^{p-1}_{*}}\Bigg\}>-\infty.

(c)

Compactness.

Every forward bounded set contained in a sublevel set of
\phi

is relatively
\sigma
-sequentially compact, i.e.,
if a sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
X
satisfies

\sup_{i}\phi(x_{i})<\infty
and
\sup_{i,j}d(x_{i},x_{j})<\infty
,
then it admits a
\sigma
-convergent subsequence.

Remark 3.9

(
\sigma=\mathcal{T}_{+}
case)
.

When
\sigma=\mathcal{T}_{+}
,
(
a
) and (
c
) above can be rewritten as follows, respectively:

(a’)

\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous;

(c’)

Every forward bounded set in a sublevel set of
\phi
is relatively compact in
X
.

The next proposition presents one of the simplest situations
where Assumption
3.8
holds (cf.
[
1
, Remark 2.1.1]
).
We remark that
\sigma
may be different from
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose that every sublevel set of
\phi
is compact in
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.
Then Assumption
3.8
holds.

Proof.

(
a
)
Assume that
x_{i}\,\overset{\sigma}{\lra}\,x

and the limit
\alpha:=\lim_{i\to\infty}\phi(x_{i})
exists.
To see
\phi(x)\leq\alpha
, suppose
\alpha<\infty
without loss of generality.
For any
\varepsilon>0
,

A_{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in X\,|\,\phi(y)\leq\alpha+\varepsilon\}

is compact by hypothesis, and hence a subsequence of
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}

converges to some
x^{\prime}\in A_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.
Then
x=x^{\prime}\in A_{\varepsilon}
by Remark
3.5
(
c
),
and the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
yields
\phi(x)\leq\alpha
as desired.

(
b
) is seen by noticing
\inf_{X}\phi>-\infty
, which follows from (
a
).
(
c
) is clear by hypothesis.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

In
[
36
]
, Rossi, Mielke and Savaré investigated the
doubly nonlinear evolution equation
(DNE),
which is more general than the gradient flow equation.
Their topological requirements are close to ours, however,
recall from Remark
2.26
that they assumed
\inf_{X}\phi>-\infty
,
which is stronger than the coercivity above and can simplify some arguments below.
We remark that Chenchiah–Rieger–Zimmer’s
[
6
]

is also concerned with the existence of (
2
-)curves of maximal slope
in the asymmetric setting.
On the one hand, they assumed the lower semicontinuity of
d
only in the second argument.
On the other hand, they assumed that the backward convergence implies the forward convergence
(see
[
6
, Assumption 4.3]
; compare it with
[
36
, Remark 2.9]
and
Remark
2.4
(
a
))
and used a stronger notion of upper gradient (recall Definition
2.15
).

3.3.
Moreau–Yosida approximation

In this subsection, we will present an existence result of solutions to (
3.1
).
For this purpose, we recall the definition of Moreau–Yosida approximation.

Definition 3.12

(Moreau–Yosida approximation)
.

For
\tau>0
and
x\in X
, the
Moreau–Yosida approximation

\Phi_{\tau}

is defined as

\Phi_{\tau}(x):=\inf_{y\in X}\Phi(\tau,x;y)=\inf_{y\in X}\ \Bigg\{\phi(y)+\frac{d^{p}(x,y)}{p\tau^{p-1}}\Bigg\}.

We also set

\tau_{*}(\phi):=\sup\{\tau>0\,|\,\Phi_{\tau}(x)>-\infty\text{ for some }x\in X\}.

Note that Assumption
3.8
(
b
) is equivalent to
\tau_{*}(\phi)>0
.
Moreover, we have the following (cf.
[
1
, Lemma 2.2.1]
).

Lemma 3.13
.

Suppose Assumption
3.8
(
b
).
For
0<\tau<\tau_{*}\leq\tau_{*}(\phi)
, set

\epsilon=\epsilon(p,\tau_{*},\tau):=\frac{\tau_{*}^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}}{2\tau^{p-1}}>0,\qquad C(p,\tau_{*},\tau):=\frac{\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)}{p\tau_{*}^{p-1}}>0,

where
\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)
is the constant introduced in Lemma
A.1
.
Then we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\tau}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)\,d^{p}(x_{*},x),

(3.3)

\displaystyle d^{p}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}{\tau^{p-1}_{*}-\tau^{p-1}}\big\{\Phi(\tau,x;y)-\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})+C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)d^{p}(x_{*},x)\big\}

for all
x,y\in X
.
In particular, sublevel sets of
\Phi(\tau,x;\cdot)
are forward bounded.

Proof.

We deduce from Lemma
A.1
(with
a=d(x,y)
and
b=d(x_{*},x)
)
and the triangle inequality that

\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{*}+\tau^{p-1}}{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y)+C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)\,d^{p}(x_{*},x)\geq\frac{d^{p}(x_{*},y)}{p\tau_{*}^{p-1}}

for any
x,y\in X
.
By the definition of
\Phi_{\tau_{*}}
, this implies

\phi(y)+\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{*}+\tau^{p-1}}{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y)+C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)d^{p}(x_{*},x)\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*}).

Then the first claim (
3.2
) follows since, for any
y\in X
,

\displaystyle\Phi(\tau,x;y)

\displaystyle=\phi(y)+\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{*}+\tau^{p-1}}{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y)+\frac{\tau_{*}^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}}{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)d^{p}(x_{*},x)+\frac{\tau_{*}^{p-1}-\tau^{p-1}}{2p\tau^{p-1}\tau_{*}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)d^{p}(x_{*},x).

Observe also that the first inequality corresponds to the second claim (
3.3
).
The forward boundedness of sublevel sets of
\Phi(\tau,x;\cdot)

readily follows from (
3.3
).
∎

Now we prove the existence of a solution to (
3.1
),
giving a discrete solution as in Definition
3.4
.

Theorem 3.14

(Existence of discrete solutions)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.8
(
a
)–(
c
).
Then, for every
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
and
x\in X
, we have
J_{\tau}[x]\neq\emptyset
.
In particular, for any
\Xi^{0}_{\gtau}\in X
and partition
P_{\gtau}
with
\|{\gtau}\|<\tau_{*}(\phi)
,
there exists a discrete solution
\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}
corresponding to
P_{\gtau}
.

Proof.

Given
c>\Phi_{\tau}(x)
, consider the sublevel set

A:=\{y\in X\,|\,\Phi(\tau,x;y)\leq c\}
.
Recall from Lemma
3.13
that
A
is forward bounded,
and hence
d
is bounded on
A\times A
.
Moreover, for any
y\in A
, we have

\Phi_{\tau}(x)-\frac{d^{p}(x,y)}{p\tau^{p-1}}\leq\phi(y)\leq\Phi(\tau,x;y)\leq c.

Thus,
\phi
is also bounded on
A
.

Next, we show that
A
is
\sigma
-sequentially compact.
For any sequence
(y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
A
,
since
\sup_{i,j}d(y_{i},y_{j})
and
\sup_{i}\phi(y_{i})
are bounded,
Assumption
3.8
(
c
) yields a subsequence
(y_{i_{k}})_{k\geq 1}

which is
\sigma
-convergent to some
y_{\infty}\in X
.
Then, since both
\phi
and
d
are
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous,
we find
\Phi(\tau,x;y_{\infty})\leq c
, thereby
y_{\infty}\in A
.
Hence,
A
is
\sigma
-sequentially compact.

By the
\sigma
-sequential compactness of
A
and
the
\sigma
-sequential lower semicontinuity of
\phi
and
d
,
we can take
y_{*}\in A
with
\Phi(\tau,x;y_{*})=\inf_{y\in A}\Phi(\tau,x;y)=\Phi_{\tau}(x)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

As in
[
36
, (3.2)]
,

J_{\tau}[x]:=\underset{y\in X}{\argmin}\left\{\phi(y)+\tau\psi\left(\frac{d(x,y)}{\tau}\right)\right\}

) in Remark

For
x\in X
and
\tau>0
with
J_{\tau}[x]\neq\emptyset
, we set

d_{\tau}^{+}(x):=\sup_{y\in J_{\tau}[x]}d(x,y),\qquad d_{\tau}^{-}(x):=\inf_{y\in J_{\tau}[x]}d(x,y).

We introduce the following assumption for convenience;
note that it is stronger than Assumption
3.8
(
b
).

Assumption 3.16
.

For any
x\in X
and
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
,
J_{\tau}[x]\neq\emptyset
holds.

Remark 3.17
.

By Theorem
3.14
,
if Assumption
3.8
(
a
)–(

) hold,
then Assumption
3.16
holds as well.
See also Remark
3.9
, Proposition
3.10
and Remark

for some situations where Assumption

holds.

We first discuss some continuity and monotonicity properties
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 3.1.2]
).

Lemma 3.18
.

Suppose Assumption
3.16
in (
ii
)–(
v
) below.

(i)

The function
(\tau,x)\longmapsto\Phi_{\tau}(x)
is continuous in
(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\times X
.

(ii)

For any
x\in X
,
0<\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}
and
y_{i}\in J_{\tau_{i}}[x]

(i=0,1)
, we have

(3.4)

\phi(x)\geq\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)\geq\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x),\quad d(x,y_{0})\leq d(x,y_{1}),\quad\phi(x)\geq\phi(y_{0})\geq\phi(y_{1}),\quad d^{+}_{\tau_{0}}(x)\leq d^{-}_{\tau_{1}}(x).

(iii)

If

, then
\lim_{\tau\to 0}d^{+}_{\tau}(x)=0
.

(iv)

For any
x\in X
,
there exists an at most countable set
\mathscr{N}_{x}\subset(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))

for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\setminus\mathscr{N}_{x}
.

(v)

If
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous, then we have,
for all
x\in\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}
,

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}(x)=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\inf_{y\in J_{\tau}[x]}\phi(y)=\phi(x).

Moreover,
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}(x)=\phi(x)
holds for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

(
i
)
Take
(\tau,x)\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\times X
and a sequence

((\tau_{i},x_{i}))_{i\geq 1}
in
(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\times X
converging to
(\tau,x)
.
On the one hand, for any
y\in X
, we have

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x_{i})\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Phi(\tau_{i},x_{i};y)=\Phi(\tau,x;y).

Taking the infimum in
y\in X
yields the upper semicontinuity

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x_{i})\leq\Phi_{\tau}(x)
.
On the other hand, to see the lower semicontinuity,
let
(y_{i})_{i\geq 1}\subset\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
be a sequence such that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\big\{\Phi(\tau_{i},x_{i};y_{i})-\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x_{i})\big\}=0.

Since
\sup_{i\geq 1}\Phi(\tau_{i},x_{i};y_{i})<\infty
,
we find from (
3.3
) that
D:=\sup_{i\geq 1}d(x_{i},y_{i})<\infty
.
Thus, the triangle inequality implies
\sup_{i\geq 1}d(x,y_{i})<\infty
.
It follows from Lemma
A.1
that

d^{p}(x,y_{i})\leq\{d(x,x_{i})+d(x_{i},y_{i})\}^{p}\leq(1+\epsilon)d^{p}(x_{i},y_{i})+\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)d^{p}(x,x_{i})

for any
\epsilon>0
.
Hence, we have

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x_{i})=\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Phi(\tau_{i},x_{i};y_{i})\geq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Bigg\{\phi(y_{i})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{i})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{i}}\Bigg\}-\frac{\epsilon D^{p}}{p\tau^{p-1}}\geq\Phi_{\tau}(x)-\frac{\epsilon D^{p}}{p\tau^{p-1}}.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
furnishes the lower semicontinuity

\Phi_{\tau}(x)\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x_{i})
,
which completes the proof.

(
ii
)
The first claim is clear by the definition of
\Phi_{\tau}(x)
,
and the second claim follows from

\displaystyle\phi(y_{0})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{0})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)\leq\Phi(\tau_{0},x;y_{1})=\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x)+\Bigg(\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{0}}-\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}\Bigg)d^{p}(x,y_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\phi(y_{0})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{0})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}+\Bigg(\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{0}}-\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}\Bigg)d^{p}(x,y_{1}).

Note also that the fourth claim is an immediate consequence of the second claim.
Finally, in the third claim, the first inequality is obvious and the second one
is a consequence of the second claim as

\phi(y_{1})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{1})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}\leq\phi(y_{0})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{0})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}\leq\phi(y_{0})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{1})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{1}}.

(
iii
)
For
x\in\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}
,
y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]
and any
y\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
,
we deduce from (
3.2
) that

(3.5)

{\infty>\Phi(\tau,x;y)\geq\Phi(\tau,x;y_{\tau})\geq}\phi(y_{\tau})\geq\Phi_{\tau}(y_{\tau})\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)d^{p}(x_{*},y_{\tau}).

Then, since
\tau<\tau_{*}
,
y_{*}\in J_{\tau_{*}}[x]
satisfies
d(x,y_{\tau})\leq d(x,y_{*})

by the second claim in (
ii
) and we find

{\Phi(\tau,x;y)}\geq\phi(y_{\tau})\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)\big(d(x_{*},x)+d(x,y_{*})\big)^{p}.

Combining this with

(3.6)

\lim_{\tau\to 0}C(p,\tau_{*},\tau)=\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{*}}<\infty

from Lemmas
3.13
and
A.1
, we find

(3.7)

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\tau^{p-1}\inf_{y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]}\phi(y_{\tau})=0.

Now, for any
y\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
, we have

d^{+}_{\tau}(x)^{p}=\sup_{y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]}p\tau^{p-1}\big(\Phi(\tau,x;y_{\tau})-\phi(y_{\tau})\big)\leq p\tau^{p-1}\phi(y)+d^{p}(x,y)-p\tau^{p-1}\inf_{y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]}\phi(y_{\tau}).

Then, (
3.7
) yields
\limsup_{\tau\to 0}d^{+}_{\tau}(x)^{p}\leq d^{p}(x,y)

for any
y\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
, and
\lim_{\tau\to 0}d^{+}_{\tau}(x)=0

since
x\in\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}
.

(
iv
)
Given
x\in X
, since the function
\tau\longmapsto d_{\tau}^{-}(x)
is non-decreasing,
it is continuous except for at most countably many points.
Then, at any continuous point
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
,
the last claim in (
ii
) implies

d_{\tau}^{+}(x)\leq\lim_{t\to\tau^{+}}d_{t}^{-}(x)=d_{\tau}^{-}(x)\leq d_{\tau}^{+}(x).

Hence,
d_{\tau}^{+}(x)=d_{\tau}^{-}(x)
.

(
v
)
Given
x\in X
, we deduce from (
iv
) that

\phi
is constant on
J_{\tau}[x]
for
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\setminus\mathscr{N}_{x}
.
Moreover, thanks to the monotonicity as in (
ii
),
it suffices to show the convergence within
(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\setminus\mathscr{N}_{x}
.
Thus, we choose
y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]
for each
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\setminus\mathscr{N}_{x}

and consider the convergence of
\phi(y_{\tau})
.

First, if
x\in\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}
,
then
\lim_{\tau\to 0}d^{+}_{\tau}(x)=0
by (
iii
)
and hence
y_{\tau}\to x
as
\tau\to 0
.
Thus, on the one hand, the
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuity of
\phi
yields

\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}(x)=\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\bigg\{\phi(y_{\tau})+\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p-1}}\bigg\}\geq\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\phi(y_{\tau})\geq\phi(x).

On the other hand, we have

\phi(x)\geq\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}(x)\geq\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\phi(y_{\tau})
.
Combining these shows the first assertion.

Next, let
x\not\in\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}
.
On the one hand, clearly
\liminf_{\tau\to 0}d(x,y_{\tau})>0
holds
since
y_{\tau}\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
.
On the other hand,
by (
3.5
), (
3.6
), the triangle inequality and

d(x,y_{\tau})\leq d(x,y_{\tau_{*}})
from (
ii
), we find

\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\phi(y_{\tau})\geq\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})-\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}_{*}}\big(d(x_{*},x)+d(x,y_{\tau_{*}})\big)^{p}>-\infty.

This is enough to obtain the claim, indeed,

\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}(x)\geq\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\phi(y_{\tau})+\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p-1}}=\infty=\phi(x).

∎

Next we investigate the derivative of the Moreau–Yosida approximation
(cf.
[
1
, Theorem 3.1.4]
,
[
36
, Lemma 4.5]
).

Theorem 3.19

(Derivative of
\Phi_{\tau}(x)
)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.16
.
Then, for any
x\in X
, the function
\tau\longmapsto\Phi_{\tau}(x)

is locally Lipschitz in
(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
and

(3.8)

\frac{\dd}{{\dd}\tau}[\Phi_{\tau}(x)]=-\frac{p-1}{p}\bigg(\frac{d^{+}_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}\bigg)^{p}=-\frac{p-1}{p}\bigg(\frac{d^{-}_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}\bigg)^{p}

holds for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
.
Moreover, if in addition
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous,
then we have

(3.9)

\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p-1}}+\int^{\tau}_{0}\frac{p-1}{p}\bigg(\frac{d^{\pm}_{r}(x)}{r}\bigg)^{p}\,{\dd}r=\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau})

for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
and
y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]
.

Proof.

For
0<\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\tau_{*}(\phi)
and any
y_{\tau_{1}}\in J_{\tau_{1}}[x]
,
we have

\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)-\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x)\leq\Phi(\tau_{0},x;y_{\tau_{1}})-\Phi(\tau_{1},x;y_{\tau_{1}})=\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{1}-\tau^{p-1}_{0}}{p\tau_{0}^{p-1}\tau_{1}^{p-1}}d^{p}(x,y_{\tau_{1}}).

Note that

\tau_{1}^{p-1}-\tau_{0}^{p-1}\leq(p-1)\tau_{i}^{p-2}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})\quad\text{with}\,\ i=\begin{cases}0&\text{for}\ 1<p\leq 2,\\
1&\text{for}\ 2\leq p<\infty\end{cases}

by the concavity (
1<p\leq 2
) or the convexity (
2\leq p<\infty
) of
t^{p-1}
in
t>0
.
This implies

\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)-\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x)\leq\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{1}-\tau^{p-1}_{0}}{p\tau_{0}^{p-1}\tau_{1}^{p-1}}\big(d^{-}_{\tau_{1}}(x)\big)^{p}\leq\frac{(p-1)(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})}{p\tau_{1-i}^{p-1}\tau_{i}}\big(d^{-}_{\tau_{1}}(x)\big)^{p}.

We similarly observe

\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)-\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x)\geq\frac{\tau^{p-1}_{1}-\tau^{p-1}_{0}}{p\tau^{p-1}_{0}\tau^{p-1}_{1}}\big(d^{+}_{\tau_{0}}(x)\big)^{p}\geq\frac{(p-1)(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})}{p\tau_{1-i}\tau^{p-1}_{i}}\big(d^{+}_{\tau_{0}}(x)\big)^{p}.

Combining them furnishes

\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{(d^{+}_{\tau_{0}}(x))^{p}}{\tau_{1-i}\tau^{p-1}_{i}}\leq\frac{\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x)-\Phi_{\tau_{1}}(x)}{\tau_{1}-\tau_{0}}\leq\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{(d^{-}_{\tau_{1}}(x))^{p}}{\tau^{p-1}_{1-i}\tau_{i}},

which shows that
\tau\longmapsto\Phi_{\tau}(x)
is locally Lipschitz in
(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
.

Take
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))\setminus\mathscr{N}_{x}

at where
\Phi_{\tau}(x)
is differentiable,
with
\mathscr{N}_{x}
from Lemma
3.18
(
iv
).
Then, by applying the above estimates to
\tau_{1}\to\tau^{+}
(
\tau_{0}=\tau
)
and
\tau_{0}\to\tau^{-}
(
\tau_{1}=\tau
),
we have

\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{(d^{+}_{\tau}(x))^{p}}{\tau^{p}}\leq-\frac{\dd}{{\dd}\tau}[\Phi_{\tau}(x)]\leq\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{(d^{-}_{\tau}(x))^{p}}{\tau^{p}}.

Therefore, (
3.8
) follows.
Integrating (
3.8
) on
[\tau_{0},\tau]\subset(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
yields

\Phi_{\tau}(x)+\int^{\tau}_{\tau_{0}}\frac{p-1}{p}\bigg(\frac{d^{\pm}_{r}(x)}{r}\bigg)^{p}\,{\dd}r=\Phi_{\tau_{0}}(x).

Letting
\tau_{0}\to 0
, we deduce (
3.9
) from
Lemma
3.18
(
v
) and
\Phi_{\tau}(x)=\Phi(\tau,x;y_{\tau})
.
∎

Now we estimate the local slope
|\partial\phi|

defined in Definition
2.19
(cf.
[
1
, Lemmas 3.1.3, 3.1.5]
).
Let
\mathfrak{D}(|\partial\phi|):=\{x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)\,|\,|\partial\phi|(x)<\infty\}

be the proper effective domain of
|\partial\phi|
.
Recall that
q=p/(p-1)
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Suppose Assumption
3.16
.
Then we have, for any
x\in X
,
\tau\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
and
y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]
,

|\partial\phi|^{q}(y_{\tau})\leq\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau^{p}}.

In particular,
y_{\tau}\in\mathfrak{D}(|\partial\phi|)
holds and

\mathfrak{D}(|\partial\phi|)
is dense in
\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}

with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{+}
.

Proof.

Assume
|\partial\phi|(y_{\tau})>0
without loss of generality
and take a sequence
(y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
converging to
y_{\tau}
with

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\phi(y_{\tau})-\phi(y_{i})}{d(y_{\tau},y_{i})}=|\partial\phi|(y_{\tau}).

Observe from the choice of
y_{\tau}
that

\phi(y_{\tau})-\phi(y_{i})\leq\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{i})}{p\tau^{p-1}}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p-1}}.

Dividing this inequality by
d(y_{\tau},y_{i})
and using the mean value theorem,
we obtain the first claim as

|\partial\phi|(y_{\tau})\leq\frac{d^{p-1}(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau^{p-1}}\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{d(x,y_{i})-d(x,y_{\tau})}{d(y_{\tau},y_{i})}\leq\frac{d^{p-1}(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau^{p-1}}.

Thus
y_{\tau}\in\mathfrak{D}(|\partial\phi|)
,
and the density of
\mathfrak{D}(|\partial\phi|)
in
\overline{\mathfrak{D}(\phi)}

follows from Lemma
3.18
(
iii
).
∎

Lemma 3.21
.

Suppose Assumption
3.16
.
Then we have

(3.10)

\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\phi(x)-\Phi_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}=\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}\quad\text{for all}\,\ x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi).

Moreover, there exists a sequence
\tau_{i}\to 0
such that, for
y_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}[x]
,

(3.11)

|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau_{i}})}{\tau_{i}}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau_{i}})}{\tau_{i}^{p}}\geq\liminf_{\tau\to 0}|\partial\phi|^{q}(y_{\tau}).

Proof.

We first show (
3.10
).
By the Young inequality, for any
s>0
, we have

(3.12)

\frac{s^{q}}{q}=\sup_{t>0}\bigg(st-\frac{t^{p}}{p}\bigg)=\sup_{t>a}\bigg(st-\frac{t^{p}}{p}\bigg)

for all
a\in(0,s^{1/(p-1)})
.
This implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\phi(x)-\Phi_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\bigg\{\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau})}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\bigg\{\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau})]_{+}}{d(x,y_{\tau})}\frac{d(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\bigg\{|\partial\phi|(x)\frac{d(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}\leq\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q},

where we used Lemma
3.18
(
iii
) in the second inequality.
If
|\partial\phi|(x)=0
,
then we are done with the help of the first claim in (
3.4
).
To show the reverse inequality under
|\partial\phi|(x)>0
,
we again use (
3.12
) to see

\displaystyle\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{1}{q}\bigg(\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{{d(x,y)}}\bigg)^{q}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{y\to x}\sup_{0<\tau<d(x,y)/a}\bigg\{\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x,y)}\frac{d(x,y)}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y)}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}

for any
a\in(0,|\partial\phi|^{1/(p-1)}(x))
.
Hence, for small
\varepsilon>0
,

\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}\leq\sup_{y\neq x}\sup_{0<\tau<\varepsilon}\bigg\{\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y)}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}=\sup_{0<\tau<\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(x)-\Phi_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
shows

\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}\leq\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\phi(x)-\Phi_{\tau}(x)}{\tau}

and completes the proof of (
3.10
).

We next prove (
3.11
).
First of all,
the last inequality in (
3.11
) immediately follows from Lemma
3.20
.
Now, on the one hand, (
3.10
) furnishes a sequence
\tau_{i}\to 0
such that

(3.13)

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)-\Phi_{\tau_{i}}(x)}{\tau_{i}}=\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}.

On the other hand, we find from the above argument that

\frac{|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)}{q}=\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\bigg\{|\partial\phi|(x)\frac{d(x,y_{\tau})}{\tau}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau})}{p\tau^{p}}\bigg\}.

Comparing this with the Young inequality (
3.12
),
taking a subsequence if necessary, we obtain

|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\bigg(\frac{d(x,y_{\tau_{i}})}{\tau_{i}}\bigg)^{p}.

Finally, substituting this into (
3.13
) yields

|\partial\phi|^{q}(x)=q\lim_{i\to\infty}\bigg\{\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau_{i}})}{\tau_{i}}-\frac{d^{p}(x,y_{\tau_{i}})}{p\tau_{i}^{p}}\bigg\}=q\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y_{\tau_{i}})}{\tau_{i}}-(q-1)|\partial\phi|^{q}(x).

This completes the proof of (
3.11
).
∎

3.4.
A priori estimates for discrete solutions and a compactness result

We introduce De Giorgi’s variational interpolation associated with
a solution to the recursive scheme (
3.1
)
(cf.
[
1
, Definition 3.2.1]
).

Definition 3.22

(Variational interpolation)
.

Given a solution
(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})_{k\geq 0}
to (
3.1
),
we denote by
\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}:[0,\infty)\lra X
its arbitrary interpolation satisfying

\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t^{k}_{\gtau})=\Xi^{k}_{\gtau},\qquad\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t^{k-1}_{\gtau}+\delta)\in J_{\delta}\big[\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau}\big]\quad\text{for}\ \delta\in(0,t^{k}_{\gtau}-t^{k-1}_{\gtau}).

Define a function
G_{\gtau}
on
(0,\infty)
associated with the discrete solution

(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})_{k\geq 0}
as, for
\delta\in(0,t^{k}_{\gtau}-t^{k-1}_{\gtau}]
,

G_{\gtau}(t^{k-1}_{\gtau}+\delta):=\frac{d^{+}_{\delta}(\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau})}{\delta}\geq\frac{d(\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau},\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t^{k-1}_{\gtau}+\delta))}{\delta}.

Observe that
G_{\gtau}
is a Borel function by (
3.4
).
Moreover, we find from Lemma
3.20
that

(3.14)

|\partial\phi|^{q}\big(\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t)\big)\leq G^{p}_{\gtau}(t)\quad\text{for}\ t>0.

We also define a piecewise constant function
|\Xi^{\prime}_{{\gtau}}|
on
(0,\infty)
by

(3.15)

|\Xi^{\prime}_{\gtau}|(t):=\frac{d(\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})}{t^{k}_{\gtau}-t^{k-1}_{\gtau}}=\frac{d(\Xi^{k-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})}{\tau_{k}}\quad\text{for}\ t\in(t^{k-1}_{\gtau},t^{k}_{\gtau}].

Then we have the following a priori estimates
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 3.2.2, Remark 3.2.5]
,
[
36
, Proposition 4.7]
).
Recall Definition
3.4
for the piecewise constant interpolation

\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t)=\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}
for
t\in(t^{k-1}_{\gtau},t^{k}_{\gtau}]
,
and Notation
2.5
that
(X,\star,d)
is always a pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space.

Lemma 3.23

(A priori estimates)
.

Suppose that
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous
and Assumption
3.16
holds,
and let
(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})_{k\geq 0}
be a solution to (
3.1
).

(i)

If
\|{\gtau}\|\in(0,\tau_{*}(\phi))
, then, for each couple of integers
0\leq k<l
, we have

\frac{1}{p}\int^{t^{l}_{\gtau}}_{t^{k}_{\gtau}}|\Xi^{\prime}_{\gtau}|^{p}(t)\,{\dd}t+\frac{1}{q}\int^{t^{l}_{\gtau}}_{t^{k}_{\gtau}}G^{p}_{\gtau}(t)\,{\dd}t+\phi(\Xi^{l}_{\gtau})=\phi(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}).

(ii)

For any
x_{*}\in X
and
S,T>0
, there exist positive constants

C_{1}=C_{1}(p,x_{*},\tau_{*}(\phi),S,T)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Theta,p,x_{*},\tau_{*}(\phi),S,T)
such that, if

\phi(\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})\leq S,\qquad d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})\leq S,\qquad t^{N-1}_{\gtau}\leq T,\qquad\|{\gtau}\|\leq\frac{\tau_{*}(\phi)}{2^{p/(p-1)}p^{2}},

then we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})\leq C_{1},\ \quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d^{p}(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{i}}\leq\phi(\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})-\phi(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})\leq C_{1}\quad\text{for all}\,\ 1\leq k\leq N,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\max\left\{d^{p}\big(\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t),\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t)\big),d^{p}\big(\widetilde{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t),\overline{\Xi}_{\gtau}(t)\big)\right\}\leq C_{2}\|{\gtau}\|^{p-1}\quad\text{for all}\,\ t\in[0,t_{\gtau}^{N}].

Proof.

(
i
)
It follows from (
3.9
) that, for each
k+1\leq i\leq l
,

\phi(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau})-\phi(\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})=\frac{d^{p}(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{i}}+\frac{1}{q}\int_{t_{\gtau}^{i-1}}^{t_{\gtau}^{i}}G_{\gtau}^{p}(t)\,{\dd}t=\frac{1}{p}\int_{t_{\gtau}^{i-1}}^{t_{\gtau}^{i}}|\Xi^{\prime}_{\gtau}|^{p}(t)\,{\dd}t+\frac{1}{q}\int_{t_{\gtau}^{i-1}}^{t_{\gtau}^{i}}G_{\gtau}^{p}(t)\,{\dd}t.

Summing up in
k+1\leq i\leq l
shows the claim.

(
ii
)
Observe from (
i
) that

(3.18)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d^{p}(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})}{p\tau_{i}^{p-1}}=\frac{1}{p}\int^{t^{k}_{\gtau}}_{0}|\Xi^{\prime}_{\gtau}|^{p}(t)\,{\dd}t\leq\phi(\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})-\phi(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau}).

Note also that

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})d^{p-1}(x_{*},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg\{\varepsilon^{p-1}\frac{d^{p}(\Xi^{i-1}_{\gtau},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})}{p\tau^{p-1}_{i}}+\frac{p-1}{p\varepsilon}\tau_{i}d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{p-1}\big(\phi(\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})-\phi(\Xi^{k}_{\gtau})\big)+\frac{p-1}{p\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})

\displaystyle\leq 2d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})+p\tau^{p-1}_{*}\big(\phi(\Xi^{0}_{\gtau})-\Phi_{\tau_{*}}(x_{*})\big)+\frac{2^{p/(p-1)}(p-1)}{\tau_{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}d^{p}(x_{*},\Xi^{i}_{\gtau})

3.6.
Doubly nonlinear evolution equations

This subsection is devoted to gradient flows in infinite dimensional Funk and
Randers-like spaces.
Let
(\mathscr{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a Hilbert space
and set
\|\cdot\|:=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}
.
On the unit ball
\mathbb{B}:=\{x\in\mathscr{H}\,|\,\|x\|<1\}
,
we define an asymmetric distance function
d:\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}\lra[0,\infty)

in the same way as (
1.2
).
Similarly to (
1.1
),
d
is associated with the (infinite dimensional) Finsler structure

F(x,v):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{d(x,x+\varepsilon v)}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\sqrt{(1-\|x\|^{2})\|v\|^{2}+\langle x,v\rangle^{2}}+\langle x,v\rangle}{1-\|x\|^{2}},\quad v\in T_{x}\mathbb{B}\cong\mathscr{H}.

We call
(\mathbb{B},d)
a
generalized Funk space
and observe the following
(see
[
1
, Remark 1.1.3]
for (
iii
)).

Proposition 3.35
.

Let
(\mathbb{B},d)
be a generalized Funk space.

(i)

(\mathbb{B},\mathbf{0},d)
is a forward complete pointed forward
\Theta
-metric space
with
\Theta(r)=2{\ee}^{r}-1
.

(ii)

Both the forward and backward topologies coincide with the original topology of

\mathbb{B}\subset\mathscr{H}
.

(iii)

A curve
\gamma
belongs to
{\FAC}^{p}((a,b);\mathbb{B})
if and only if
it is differentiable at
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
with the derivative
\gamma^{\prime}

satisfying
F(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\in L^{p}(a,b)
and

\gamma(t)-\gamma(s)=\int^{t}_{s}\gamma^{\prime}(r)\,{\dd}r\quad\text{for any }a<s\leq t<b.

Moreover, for
\gamma\in{\FAC}^{p}((a,b);\mathbb{B})
, we have

|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|(t)=F(\gamma(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t))
for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
.

Let
\mathscr{H}^{*}
denote the dual space of
\mathscr{H}
.
For
\zeta\in T^{*}_{x}\mathbb{B}\cong\mathscr{H}^{*}
(
\cong\mathscr{H}
),
the dual norm of
F
is defined as

F^{*}(x,\zeta):=\sup_{v\in T_{x}\mathbb{B}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\langle\zeta,v\rangle}{F(x,v)}.

For
p\in(1,\infty)
,
q=p/(p-1)
and
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{B}
,
define
\mathfrak{J}_{p}(x,v)\subset T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{B}
by

\mathfrak{J}_{p}(x,v):=\{\zeta\in T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{B}\,|\,\langle\zeta,v\rangle=F^{p}(x,v)=F^{*}(x,\zeta)^{q}=F(x,v)F^{*}(x,\zeta)\}.

Note that
\mathfrak{J}_{p}(x,y)
is at most a singleton by the differentiability of
\|\cdot\|

(see Proposition
B.1
).

Given
\phi:\mathbb{B}\lra(-\infty,\infty]
and
x\in\mathbb{B}
, we set

\displaystyle\partial\phi(x)

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{\zeta\in T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{B}\,\bigg|\,\liminf_{v\to\mathbf{0}}\frac{\phi(x+v)-\phi(x)-\langle\zeta,v\rangle}{d(x,x+v)}\geq 0\bigg\}\,\ \text{for}\ x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi),

\displaystyle\partial^{\circ}\phi(x)

\displaystyle:=\{\zeta\in\partial\phi(x)\,|\,F^{*}(x,-\zeta)\leq F^{*}(x,-\eta)\ \text{for all}\ \eta\in\partial\phi(x)\}\,\ \text{for}\ x\in\mathfrak{D}(\partial\phi),

\displaystyle F^{*}\big({-}\partial^{\circ}\phi(x)\big)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\inf_{\zeta\in\partial\phi(x)}F^{*}(x,-\zeta)&\text{ if }x\in\mathfrak{D}(\partial\phi),\\
+\infty&\text{ if }x\notin\mathfrak{D}(\partial\phi),\end{cases}

where
\partial\phi(x)
is the
Fréchet subdifferential
of
\phi
at
x

and
\mathfrak{D}(\partial\phi):=\{x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)\,|\,\partial\phi(x)\neq\emptyset\}
.
The function
x\longmapsto F^{*}(-\partial^{\circ}\phi(x))
is a weak upper gradient for
-\phi

(see Proposition
B.2
).

The main result of this subsection reads as follows
(cf.
[
1
, Proposition 1.4.1, Theorem 2.3.7]
).

Theorem 3.36
.

Let
(\mathbb{B},d)
be a generalized Funk space,
and let
\phi:\mathbb{B}\longmapsto(-\infty,\infty]
admit a decomposition
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}

where
\phi_{1}
is a proper, lower semicontinuous, convex function
and

(i)

The local slope

is a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
.

(ii)

If

is a
p

\phi
with respect to

,
then it satisfies the following

(3.28)

\mathfrak{J}_{p}\big(\xi(t),\xi^{\prime}(t)\big)=-\partial^{\circ}\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)\neq\emptyset\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.\ $t\in(a,b)$.}

(iii)

If Assumption
3.8
(
b
), (

) hold with

,
then, for every

,

is nonempty
and its element

satisfies (

) and the energy identity

\int^{T}_{0}|\xi^{\prime}_{+}(t)|^{p}\,{\dd}t+\phi\big(\xi(T)\big)=\phi(x_{0})\quad\text{for all}\,\ T>0.

Proof.

(
i
)
By Proposition

,
the same argument as in the proof of

yields that

\partial\phi=\partial\phi_{1}+{\dd}\phi_{2}
satisfies (

) and (

),
and

is a strong upper gradient for

with

.
Moreover,

follows from (the proof of)
[
1
, Lemma 2.3.6]

(with
\sigma=\mathcal{T}_{+}
).

(
ii
)
Since
\xi^{\prime}(t)
exists
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e. due to Proposition
3.35
(

),
Proposition
2.24
combined with (

\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)=-\frac{1}{p}|\xi^{\prime}_{+}|^{p}(t)-\frac{1}{q}F^{*}\big({-}\partial^{\circ}\phi(\xi(t))\big)^{q}\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(a,b).

On the other hand, for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(a,b)
and any

\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}\phi\big(\xi(t)\big)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\phi(\xi(t+\varepsilon))-\phi(\xi(t))}{\varepsilon}\geq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\langle\zeta,\xi(t+\varepsilon)-\xi(t)\rangle}{\varepsilon}=-\langle-\zeta,\xi^{\prime}(t)\rangle.

and Proposition

(

).

(

)
This follows from Corollary
3.29
, Theorem

,
and (

), (

) above.
∎

We remark that the above argument also applies to the unit balls in general Banach spaces
\mathscr{B}

as well as Minkowski normed spaces of infinite dimension.
For example, let

be a reflexive Banach space
and

Given

both

Theorem

)
with the

4.

In this section, we study the behavior of curves of maximal slope

where

Let

4.1.
Convexity assumption

A curve

Definition 4.1

(p,\lambda)
-convexity)
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
we say that
\phi
is
(p,\lambda)
-convex

(resp.
(p,\lambda)
-geodesically convex
) if, for any
x_{0},x_{1}\in X
,
there is a curve (resp. a minimal geodesic)
\gamma:[0,1]\lra X
from
x_{0}
to
x_{1}

such that

\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)\leq(1-t)\phi(x_{0})+t\phi(x_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{p}t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})\quad\text{for all}\,\ t\in[0,1].

In the particular case of
p=2
,
\phi
is also called a
\lambda
-convex

(resp.
\lambda
-geodesically convex
) function.

Remark 4.2
.

We remark that our definition of
(p,\lambda)
-convexity slightly differs from
the
(\lambda,p)
-convexity
in
[
37
, Definition 2.5]
(discussed on symmetric metric spaces),
which requires

\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)\leq(1-t)\phi(x_{0})+t\phi(x_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{p}t(1-t)d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})\quad\text{for all}\,\ t\in[0,1]

(see also
[
1
, Remark 2.4.7]
).
Since
t\in[0,1]
, this is stronger (resp. weaker) than our
(p,\lambda)
-convexity
for
\lambda>0
and
p\in(1,2)
or
\lambda<0
and
p\in(2,\infty)

(resp. for
\lambda>0
and
p\in(2,\infty)
or
\lambda<0
and
p\in(1,2)
).
Our
(p,\lambda)
-convexity is well-behaved in subdivisions in the sense that combining

\phi\bigg(\gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{4}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{2}\phi(x_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\phi\bigg(\gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)-\frac{\lambda}{p}\frac{1}{2}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{d(x_{0},x_{1})}{2}\bigg)^{p}

and

\phi\bigg(\gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{2}\phi(x_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\phi(x_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{p}\frac{1}{2}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\bigg)d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})

implies

\phi\bigg(\gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{4}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\frac{3}{4}\phi(x_{0})+\frac{1}{4}{\phi(x_{1})}-\frac{\lambda}{p}\frac{1}{4}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{4^{p-1}}\bigg)d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1}).

Moreover, our usage of
\Phi
below in the spirit of
[
1
]
seems also advantageous.
See Remarks
4.12
,
4.18
for further discussions.

Now we introduce an important convexity assumption
(in the spirit of
[
1
, Assumption 2.4.5]
).

Assumption 4.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and put
\lambda_{-}:=-\min\{\lambda,0\}
.
We assume that, for any
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
,
there exists a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\lra X
from
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
such that

(4.1)

\Phi\big(\tau,x_{0};\gamma(t)\big)\leq(1-t)\Phi(\tau,x_{0};x_{0})+t\Phi(\tau,x_{0};x_{1})-\frac{1}{p}\bigg(\lambda+\frac{1}{\tau^{p-1}}\bigg)t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})

for all
\tau\in(0,\lambda_{-}^{-1/(p-1)})
and
t\in[0,1]
,
where we set
\lambda_{-}^{-1/(p-1)}:=\infty
if
\lambda_{-}=0
.

In other words,
\Phi(\tau,x_{0};\cdot)
is
(p,\lambda+\tau^{1-p})
-convex
for a common curve
\gamma
for all
\tau\in(0,\lambda_{-}^{-1/(p-1)})
.
We remark that
\gamma
is always emanating from
x_{0}

(cf.
[
1
, Assumption 4.0.1]
, which is a stronger convexity condition
requiring the convexity of
\Phi(\tau,x_{0};\cdot)
between any pair of points).

Example 4.4
.

(a)

It is readily seen that a
(p,\lambda)
-geodesically convex function

\phi:X\lra(-\infty,\infty]
satisfies Assumption
4.3

(regardless of the convexity of the distance function).
Indeed, for a minimal geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\lra X
from
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
along which
the
(p,\lambda)
-convexity holds, we have

\displaystyle\Phi\big(\tau,x_{0};\gamma(t)\big)

\displaystyle=\phi\big(\gamma(t)\big)+\frac{d^{p}(x_{0},\gamma(t))}{p\tau^{p-1}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\phi(x_{0})+t\phi(x_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{p}t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})+\frac{t^{p}}{p\tau^{p-1}}d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})

\displaystyle=(1-t)\Phi(\tau,x_{0};x_{0})+t\Phi(\tau,x_{0};x_{1})-\frac{1}{p}\bigg(\lambda+\frac{1}{\tau^{p-1}}\bigg)t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1}).

(b)

The convexity of the distance function is intimately related to how the space is curved.
For a complete, simply-connected Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
,

d_{g}^{2}(x,\cdot)
is
2
-geodesically convex for every
x\in M

if and only if the sectional curvature is nonpositive.
This is a fact at the origin of the fruitful theory of CAT
(0)
-spaces
(see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).
In the Finsler case, only the flag curvature is not sufficient to control the convexity
of the distance function and we need some additional conditions.
For example, for forward complete, simply-connected Finsler manifold
(M,F)
of Berwald type
with nonpositive flag curvature,
d_{F}^{2}(x,\cdot)
is
0
-geodesically convex for every
x\in M
.
We refer to
[
40
, §15.1]
and
[
26
, §5]
for this and more general results.

We collect some immediate consequences of Assumption
4.3
in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
\phi
satisfies Assumption
4.3
for some
(p,\lambda)
.
Then we have the following.

(i)

\phi
satisfies Assumption
4.3
for all
(p,\lambda^{\prime})
with
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
.

(ii)

For any
\tau\in(0,\lambda_{-}^{-1/(p-1)})
and
t\in(0,1]
, we have

(4.2)

\frac{\phi(\gamma(t))-\phi(x_{0})}{t}\leq\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{0})+\frac{t^{p-1}-\lambda\tau^{p-1}(1-t^{p-1})}{p\tau^{p-1}}d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1}),

where
\gamma
is the curve satisfying (
4.1
).
In particular,

(4.3)

d\big(x_{0},\gamma(t)\big)\leq td(x_{0},x_{1})\quad\text{for all}\,\ t\in[0,1].

(iii)

If
\lambda\geq 0
, then we have

(4.4)

\frac{\phi(\gamma(t))-\phi(x_{0})}{t}\leq\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{0})-\frac{\lambda}{p}(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})\quad\text{for all}\,\ t\in(0,1].

In particular,
\phi
is
(p,\lambda)
-convex.

Proof.

(
i
) is trivial by definition.
In (
ii
), (
4.2
) follows from

\phi(\gamma(t))\leq\Phi(\tau,x_{0};\gamma(t))
and (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle\Phi\big(\tau,x_{0};\gamma(t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\phi(x_{0})+t\phi(x_{1})+t\frac{d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})}{p\tau^{p-1}}-\frac{1}{p}\bigg(\lambda+\frac{1}{\tau^{p-1}}\bigg)t(1-t^{p-1})d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})

(4.5)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\phi(x_{0})+t\phi(x_{1})+t\frac{t^{p-1}-\lambda\tau^{p-1}(1-t^{p-1})}{p\tau^{p-1}}d^{p}(x_{0},x_{1}).

We obtain (
4.3
) by letting
\tau\to 0
in the above inequality.
For (
iii
), the assumption
\lambda\geq 0
implies
\lambda_{-}=0
and hence
(
4.2
) holds for all
\tau\in(0,\infty)
,
which furnishes (
4.4
) as
\tau\to\infty
.
∎

Next we give an estimate of
\tau_{*}(\phi)
(see Definition
3.12
)
under Assumption
4.3
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 2.4.8]
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.3
holds for some
(p,\lambda)

and there are
x_{*}\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
and
r_{*}>0
such that

(4.6)

m_{*}:=\inf\{\phi(x)\,|\,d(x_{*},x)\leq r_{*}\}>-\infty.

(i)

We have
\tau_{*}(\phi)\geq\lambda^{-1/(p-1)}_{-}
.
In particular,
\tau_{*}(\phi)=\infty
if
\lambda\geq 0
.

(ii)

If
\lambda>0
, then
\phi
is bounded from below.
Moreover, if
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous,
then it has a unique minimizer
\bar{x}\in X
.

Proof.

(
i
)
Given
\tau=(\lambda_{-}+\varepsilon)^{-1/(p-1)}\in(0,\lambda^{-1/(p-1)}_{-})
, we claim that

(4.7)

\inf\{\Phi(\tau,x_{*};y)\,|\,d(x_{*},y)>r_{*}\}>-\infty.

Combining this with (
4.6
) implies

\tau_{*}(\phi)\geq(\lambda_{-}+\varepsilon)^{-1/(p-1)}
,
and then (
i
) follows by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In order to show (
4.7
),
take an arbitrary point
y\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
with
d(x_{*},y)>r_{*}
,
let
\gamma:[0,1]\lra X
be a curve from
x_{0}=x_{*}
to
x_{1}=y
satisfying (
4.1
),
and set
y_{*}:=\gamma(r_{*}/d(x_{*},y))
.
Then (
4.3
) implies
d(x_{*},y_{*})\leq r_{*}
and hence
\phi(y_{*})\geq m_{*}
.
We deduce from (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d(x_{*},y)}{r_{*}}\big(\phi(y_{*})-\phi(x_{*})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\phi(y)-\phi(x_{*})+\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}}\bigg\{\bigg(\frac{r_{*}}{d(x_{*},y)}\bigg)^{p-1}-\lambda\tau^{p-1}\bigg(1-\bigg(\frac{r_{*}}{d(x_{*},y)}\bigg)^{p-1}\bigg)\bigg\}d^{p}(x_{*},y).

This together with
\phi(y_{*})\geq m_{*}
yields

(4.8)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{*})+c_{*}d(x_{*},y)\leq\phi(y)-\frac{\lambda}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)\leq\phi(y)+\frac{\lambda_{-}}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y),

\displaystyle\text{where}\,\ c_{*}:=\frac{m_{*}-\phi(x_{*})-(\tau^{1-p}+\lambda)r^{p}_{*}/p}{r_{*}}.

Combining this with the Young inequality

-c_{*}d(x_{*},y)\leq|c_{*}|d(x_{*},y)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)+\frac{\varepsilon^{-q/p}}{q}|c_{*}|^{q}

and recalling
\tau=(\lambda_{-}+\varepsilon)^{-1/(p-1)}
, we find

\Phi(\tau,x_{*};y)=\phi(y)+\frac{\lambda_{-}+\varepsilon}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)\geq\phi(x_{*})+c_{*}d(x_{*},y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)\geq\phi(x_{*})-\frac{\varepsilon^{-q/p}}{q}|c_{*}|^{q}.

This implies (
4.7
) and completes the proof.

(
ii
)
If
\lambda>0
, then for any
\varepsilon>\lambda
, the above argument with
\tau=(\varepsilon-\lambda)^{-1/(p-1)}
shows

\phi(y)+\frac{\varepsilon-\lambda}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)\geq\phi(x_{*})+c_{*}d(x_{*},y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}d^{p}(x_{*},y)\geq\phi(x_{*})-\frac{\varepsilon^{-q/p}}{q}(-c_{*})^{q}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to\lambda
, we deduce from the above inequality and (
4.6
) that

\inf_{X}\phi\geq\min\Bigg\{m_{*},\phi(x_{*})-\frac{\lambda^{-q/p}}{q}\frac{(\phi(x_{*})-m_{*}+(\lambda r_{*}^{p})/p)^{q}}{r_{*}^{q}}\Bigg\}.

When
\phi
is lower semicontinuous,
take any minimizing sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
i.e.,
\phi(x_{i})\to\inf_{X}\phi
.
For any
0<i\leq j
, let
\gamma_{i,j}
be a curve from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
satisfying (
4.1
).
It follows from (
4.4
) (with
t=1/2
) that

2\bigg\{\phi\bigg(\gamma_{i,j}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)-\phi(x_{i})\bigg\}\leq\phi(x_{j})-\phi(x_{i})-\frac{\lambda}{p}(1-2^{1-p})d^{p}(x_{i},x_{j}),

which furnishes

\frac{\lambda}{p}(1-2^{1-p})d^{p}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\phi(x_{i})+\phi(x_{j})-2\phi\bigg(\gamma_{i,j}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\phi(x_{i})+\phi(x_{j})-2\inf_{X}\phi\to 0

as
i,j\to\infty
.
Thus,
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a forward Cauchy sequence and converges to some
\bar{x}\in X
,
which is a minimizer of
\phi
due to the lower semicontinuity of
\phi
.
The uniqueness also follows from the above argument, as any minimizing sequence is convergent.
∎

4.2.
Existence of curves of maximal slope

Under the convexity as in Assumption
4.3
,
we can show a global formula for the local slope
|\partial\phi|
(recall Definition
2.19
),
which plays an important role in proving the existence of curves of maximal slope
(cf.
[
1
, Theorem 2.4.9]
,
[
36
, Lemma 5.3]
,
[
37
, Proposition 2.7]
).

Theorem 4.8

(Global formula of
|\partial\phi|
)
.

If Assumption
4.3
holds for some
(p,\lambda)
, then

(4.9)

|\partial\phi|(x)=\sup_{y\neq x}\Bigg[\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{d(x,y)}+\frac{\lambda}{p}d^{p-1}(x,y)\Bigg]_{+}\quad\text{for all}\,\ x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi).

Moreover, when
\lambda\geq 0
, we have
|\partial\phi|(x)=\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x)

for all
x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi)
.
Clearly we have

|\partial\phi|(x)=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{[\phi(x)-\phi(y)]_{+}}{d(x,y)}\leq\sup_{y\neq x}\Bigg[\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{d(x,y)}+\frac{\lambda}{p}d^{p-1}(x,y)\Bigg]_{+}.

In order to show the reverse inequality, without loss of generality,
we assume that there is
y\neq x
such that

\phi(x)-\phi(y)+\frac{\lambda}{p}d^{p}(x,y)>0.

Let
\gamma
be a curve from
x
to
y
satisfying (
4.1
).
Then (
4.2
) yields that, for any
\tau\in(0,\lambda_{-}^{-1/(p-1)})
,

\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(\gamma(t))}{d(x,\gamma(t))}\geq\bigg\{\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{d(x,y)}+\frac{\lambda\tau^{p-1}(1-t^{p-1})-t^{p-1}}{p\tau^{p-1}}d^{p-1}(x,y)\bigg\}\frac{td(x,y)}{d(x,\gamma(t))}.

Combining this with
d(x,\gamma(t))\leq td(x,y)
from (
4.3
),
we find

|\partial\phi|(x)\geq\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(\gamma(t))}{d(x,\gamma(t))}\geq\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{d(x,y)}+\frac{\lambda}{p}d^{p-1}(x,y)>0.

Now (
4.9
) follows by taking the supremum in
y
.

When
\lambda\geq 0
, it is straightforward from (
4.9
) that

|\partial\phi|(x)\geq\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x)
.
Since the reverse inequality clearly holds by definition,
we have
|\partial\phi|(x)=\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x)
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.3
holds for some
(p,\lambda)

and
\phi
is
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous.
Then
|\partial\phi|
is a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.21
that

|\partial\phi|
is a weak upper gradient and
\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}
is a strong upper gradient.
Hence, if
\lambda\geq 0
, Theorem
4.8
implies that

|\partial\phi|
is a strong upper gradient.

Next, suppose that
\lambda<0
and
\diam(X)<\infty
.
Observe from (
4.9
) that

(4.10)

\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}(x)\leq|\partial\phi|(x)-\frac{\lambda}{p}\diam(X)^{p-1}\quad\text{for all}\,\ x\in\mathfrak{D}(\phi).

Hence, for any
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
,

|\partial\phi|\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
implies that

\mathfrak{l}_{\phi}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)

and
-\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous by Remark
2.16
.
Thus, Proposition
2.18
shows that

|\partial\phi|
is a strong upper gradient.

Finally, when
\lambda<0
and
\diam(X)=\infty
,
we shall again prove that
-\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous
for
\gamma\in\FAC([a,b];X)
with
|\partial\phi|\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)
.
Given such a curve
\gamma
,
we find from Lemma
2.8
that
\gamma([a,b])
is compact.
Let
X_{0}:=\gamma([a,b])
be the compact forward metric space
equipped with the restricted metric

,
and denote by
\mathfrak{l}^{0}_{\phi}
the corresponding global slope of
\phi
.
Then, the same argument as above together with
(
4.10
) yields
\mathfrak{l}^{0}_{\phi}\circ\gamma\,|\gamma^{\prime}_{+}|\in L^{1}(a,b)

and
-\phi\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous.
Therefore,
|\partial\phi|
is always a strong upper gradient for
-\phi
.

The lower semicontinuity of
|\partial\phi|

can be shown in the same way as Theorem
2.21
(
ii
)
by (
4.9
).
∎

Lemma 4.16

Proof.

(

(

Then, in view of Lemma

4.4.
Heat flow on compact Finsler manifolds

This subsection is devoted to a study of heat flow on compact Finsler manifolds.
It is well known that heat flow can be regarded as gradient flow in the Wasserstein space;
we refer to
[
7
,
24
,
32
,
44
]
for more details.
In what follows, let
(M,F,\m)
be a compact Finsler manifold
endowed with a smooth positive measure
\m
.
Along
[
7
]
, we shall present a slightly more general framework than
[
32
]
.

4.4.1.
Finsler structure of the Wasserstein space

Denote by
\mathcal{P}(M)
the collection of Borel probability measures on
M
.
For
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
is defined by

d_{W}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\inf_{\pi}\bigg(\int_{M\times M}d^{2}_{F}(x,y)\,\pi({\dd}x\,{\dd}y)\bigg)^{1/2},

where
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(M\times M)
runs over all couplings of
(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
.
The fundamental theory of optimal transport yields that
the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein space
(\mathcal{P}(M),d_{W})
is a compact
\lambda_{F}(M)
-metric space
and any
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
admit a minimal geodesic
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
(cf.
[
15
, Corollary 4.17]
).

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m)\subset\mathcal{P}(M)

be the set of measures absolutely continuous with respect to
\m
.
Then we have the following standard fact (cf.
[
25
, Theorem 4.10]
).

Proposition 4.20
.

For any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m)
and
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
,
there exists a
\mu_{0}
-a.e. unique vector field
\Psi_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu_{1}}
on
M
such that

(i)

\pi=(\id_{M},\exp\Psi_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu_{1}})_{\sharp}\mu_{0}
is a unique optimal coupling of
(\mu_{0},\mu_{1});

(ii)

\mu_{t}=[\exp(t\Psi^{\mu_{1}}_{\mu_{0}})]_{\sharp}\mu_{0}
,
t\in[0,1]
,
is a unique minimal geodesic from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1};

(iii)

d^{2}_{W}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{M}F^{2}(\Psi^{\mu_{1}}_{\mu_{0}})\,{\dd}\mu_{0};

(iv)

there exists a
(d_{F}^{2}/2)
-convex function
\varphi:M\lra\mathbb{R}

such that
\Psi_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu_{1}}=\nabla\varphi
,

where
f_{\sharp}\mu
denotes the push-forward measure of
\mu
by
f
.

The vector field
\Psi^{\mu_{1}}_{\mu_{0}}
will be referred to as the
optimal transport vector field
.
Observe that
\Psi^{\mu_{t}}_{\mu_{0}}=t\Psi_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu_{1}}
holds for all
t\in[0,1]
.
By
[
26
]
, the function
\varphi
in (
iv
) is Lipschitz and twice differentiable
\m
-a.e.

Now we recall the Finsler structure of
\mathcal{P}(M)
introduced in
[
32
]

(see
[
7
,
44
]
for the Riemannian case).
For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
,
let
L^{2}(\mu;TM)
denote the space of measurable vector fields
\w

with the asymmetric norm

\|{\w}\|_{\mu}:=\bigg(\int_{M}F^{2}(\w)\,{\dd}\mu\bigg)^{1/2}<\infty.

We similarly define
L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
as the space of measurable
1
-forms
\zeta
with

\|\zeta\|^{*}_{\mu}:=\bigg(\int_{M}F^{*}(\zeta)^{2}\,{\dd}\mu\bigg)^{1/2}<\infty.

We also define

\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}:=\int_{M}\zeta(\w)\,{\dd}\mu

for
\w\in L^{2}(\mu;TM)
and
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
,
and the
Legendre transformation

\LL_{\mu}:L^{2}(\mu;TM)\lra L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)

by applying the pointwise Legendre transformation induced by
F

(recall Subsection
2.3
).
The
tangent
and
cotangent spaces
of
\mathcal{P}(M)
at
\mu
are defined by

T_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M):=\overline{\{\nabla\varphi\,|\,\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)\}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mu}},\qquad T^{*}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M):=\overline{\{{\dd}\varphi\,|\,\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)\}}^{\|\cdot\|^{*}_{\mu}},

respectively.
We summarize some properties of these spaces (see Appendix
C
for the proof).

Proposition 4.21
.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
, we have the following.

(i)

The Legendre transformation
\LL_{\mu}:L^{2}(\mu;TM)\lra L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
is a homeomorphism.
In particular, its restriction
\LL_{\mu}:T_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)\lra T^{*}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)

is also a homeomorphism.

(ii)

For any
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
, we have

\|\zeta\|^{*}_{\mu}=\sup_{\w\in L^{2}(\mu;TM)\setminus\{0\}}\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}/\|{\w}\|_{\mu}

and
\w=\LL^{-1}_{\mu}(\zeta)
is a unique element in
L^{2}(\mu;TM)
satisfying

\|{\w}\|_{\mu}^{2}=(\|\zeta\|^{*}_{\mu})^{2}=\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}
.

(iii)

For every bounded linear functional
\mathcal{Q}
on
L^{2}(\mu;TM)
,
there exists a unique
1
-form
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
such that

\mathcal{Q}(\w)=\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}
for all
\w\in L^{2}(\mu;TM)
.

(iv)

We have

T^{*}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)=\{\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)\,|\,\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}=0\ \text{for all}\ \w\in\mathbf{Ker(div)}(\mu)\}
, where

\mathbf{Ker(div)}(\mu):=\big\{{\w}\in L^{2}(\mu;TM)\,\big|\,\langle{\dd}\varphi,\w\rangle_{\mu}=0\ \text{for all}\ \varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)\big\}.

(v)

If
\vv,\w\in T_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)
satisfy
\vv-\w\in\mathbf{Ker(div)}(\mu)
,
then we have
\vv=\w
.

The next theorem is seen in the same manner as
[
32
, Lemma 7.2, Theorem 7.3]

or
[
7
, Proposition 2.5]
.
(In fact, (
4.21
) does not depend on the metric
and can be reduced to any bi-Lipschitz equivalent Riemannian metric.)

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval
and
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in I}
be a continuous curve in
\mathcal{P}(M)
.

(i)

If
\mu\in\FAC^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{P}(M))
,
then there exists a Borel vector field
\vv:I\times M\lra TM

with
\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}\in L^{2}_{\loc}(I)

(\vv_{t}(x):=\vv(t,x)\in T_{x}M)
such that

(a)

\vv_{t}\in T_{\mu_{t}}\mathcal{P}(M)
for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I;

(b)

the
continuity equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}+\di(\vv_{t}\cdot{\mu_{t}})=0
holds
in the sense of distributions, i.e.,

(4.21)

\int_{I}\int_{M}\{\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+\langle{\dd}\varphi(t,x),\vv_{t}(x)\rangle\}\,\mu_{t}({\dd}x)\,{\dd}t=0\quad\text{for all}\,\ \varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I\times M),

where
C^{\infty}_{0}(I\times M)
denotes the set of
C^{\infty}
-functions on
I\times M

of compact support.

Moreover, such a vector field
\vv_{t}
is unique and satisfies
\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}=|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)

for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I
.

(ii)

If
\mu
satisfies (
4.21
) above for some Borel vector field
(\vv_{t})_{t\in I}

with
\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}\in L^{2}_{\loc}(I)
,
then
\mu
is locally forward absolutely continuous with
|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\leq\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}
.

The vector field
\vv=(\vv_{t})_{t\in I}
in (
i
) above is called
the
tangent vector field
of the curve
\mu
.

Lemma 4.23
.

(i)

For any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m)
and
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
,
we have
\Psi^{\mu_{1}}_{\mu_{0}}\in T_{\mu_{0}}\mathcal{P}(M)
.

(ii)

For any
(\mu_{t})_{t\in I}\in\FAC^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m))

and its tangent vector field
(\vv_{t})_{t\in I}
, we have, for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I
,

(4.22)

\frac{1}{h}\Psi_{\mu_{t}}^{\mu_{t+h}}\to\vv_{t}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mu_{t};TM)\text{ as }h\to 0^{+}.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
4.20
(
iv
),
one can show (
i
) via an approximation by smooth functions.
(
ii
) follows from a similar argument
to the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 2.7]
along with
Proposition
4.21
(
v
), Theorem
4.22

and (
i
) above.
∎

4.4.2.
Subdifferentials and gradient flows

Let
\phi:\mathcal{P}(M)\lra(-\infty,\infty]
be a
\mathcal{T}_{+}
-lower semicontinuous function
on the Wasserstein space
(\mathcal{P}(M),d_{W})

with
\mathfrak{D}({\phi})\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m)
.

Definition 4.24

(Subdifferentials)
.

For
\mu\in\mathfrak{D}({\phi})
, a
1
-form
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
is said to
belong to
the subdifferential

\partial{\phi}(\mu)
if

{\phi}(\nu)-{\phi}(\mu)\geq\langle\zeta,\Psi^{\nu}_{\mu}\rangle_{\mu}+o\big(d_{W}(\mu,\nu)\big)\quad\text{ for all}\,\ \nu\in\mathcal{P}(M).

We call
\zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu)
a
strong subdifferential
if it satisfies

\phi\big((\exp\w)_{\sharp}\mu\big)-\phi(\mu)\geq\langle\zeta,\w\rangle_{\mu}+o(\|{\w}\|_{\mu})\quad\text{ for all}\,\ {\w}\in L^{2}(\mu;TM).

Proposition 4.25
.

Given
\mu\in\mathfrak{D}({\phi})
, we have the following.

(i)

If
\partial{\phi}(\mu)\neq\emptyset
,
then
|\partial{\phi}|(\mu)\leq\inf_{\zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu)}\|{-}\zeta\|^{*}_{\mu}
.

(ii)

If
\zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu)\cap T^{*}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)
,
then
\zeta
is a strong subdifferential.

(iii)

If
\phi
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex,
then
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
belongs to
\partial{\phi}(\mu)
if and only if

(4.23)

\phi(\nu)-\phi(\mu)\geq\langle\zeta,\Psi^{\nu}_{\mu}\rangle_{\mu}+\frac{\lambda}{2}d^{2}_{W}(\mu,\nu)\quad\text{ for all}\,\ \nu\in\mathcal{P}(M).

Proof.

Definitions
2.19
and
4.24
directly yield (
i
) directly.
(
ii
) and (
iii
) follow from the same arguments as in

[
7
, Lemmas 3.2, 3.5]
, respectively.
∎

Proposition 4.26
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
(\mu_{t})_{t\in I}\in\FAC^{2}(I;\mathfrak{D}({\phi}))

with the tangent vector field
(\vv_{t})_{t\in I}
.
If
\phi
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex and
\int_{I}|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t<\infty
,
then
t\longmapsto{\phi}(\mu_{t})
is absolutely continuous in
I

and we have, for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I
,

\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}{\phi}(\mu_{t})=\langle\zeta,\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}\quad\text{for all}\,\ \zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu_{t}).

Proof.

The absolute continuity of
{\phi}(\mu_{t})
follows from Example
4.4
(
a
),
Corollary
4.9
and Remark
2.16
.
It follows from the assumption and Lemma
4.23
(
ii
)
that, for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I
,
|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})<\infty
,

s\longmapsto{\phi}(\mu_{s})
is differentiable at
t
, and (
4.22
) holds.
Then, for any
\zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu_{t})
, we deduce from (
4.23
) that

\displaystyle{\phi}(\mu_{t+h})-{\phi}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\zeta,\Psi^{\mu_{t+h}}_{\mu_{t}}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}d^{2}_{W}(\mu_{t},\mu_{t+h})

\displaystyle=h\bigg\langle\zeta,\frac{1}{h}\Psi^{\mu_{t+h}}_{\mu_{t}}-\vv_{t}\bigg\rangle_{\mu_{t}}+h\langle\zeta,\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}d^{2}_{W}(\mu_{t},\mu_{t+h})

\displaystyle=h\langle\zeta,\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}+o(h)

for
h>0
.
This implies

\frac{{\dd}}{{\dd}t_{+}}{\phi}(\mu_{t})\geq\langle\zeta,\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}.

Moreover, by considering the reverse curve
\bar{\mu}_{t}:=\mu_{-t}

with the tangent vector field
\bar{\vv}_{t}:=-{\vv}_{-t}

for the reverse Finsler structure
\overleftarrow{F}(v):=F(-v)
,
we also obtain

-\frac{{\dd}}{{\dd}t_{-}}{\phi}(\mu_{t})\geq-\langle\zeta,\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we introduce gradient flows in the Wasserstein space in the same spirit as

[
7
, Definition 3.7]
(slightly weaker than
[
32
, Definition 7.6]
),
which is compatible with the notion of (
2
-)curves of maximal slope
as we will see in Proposition
4.29
below.

Definition 4.27

(Gradient flows in the Wasserstein space)
.

A curve
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}\in\FAC^{2}_{\loc}((0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(M))

with the tangent vector field
(\vv_{t})_{t>0}
is called a
trajectory of the gradient flow

for
\phi
if

(4.24)

-\LL_{\mu_{t}}(\vv_{t})\in\partial{\phi}(\mu_{t})\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(0,\infty).

Remark 4.28
.

Owing to Theorem
4.22
(
i
) and
Proposition
4.25
(
i
), we have

|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})\leq\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}\|{\LL}_{\mu_{t}}(\vv_{t})\|^{*}_{\mu_{t}}=\|{\vv}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mu_{t}}\in L^{1}_{\loc}\big((0,\infty)\big).

Thus, it follows from Proposition
4.26

(provided that
\phi
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex)
that
t\longmapsto{\phi}(\mu_{t})
is locally absolutely continuous in
(0,\infty)
and

-\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}{\phi}(\mu_{t})=\|{\vv}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mu_{t}}\quad\text{for $\mathscr{L}^{1}$-a.e.}\ t\in(0,\infty),

which implies
{\phi}(\mu_{t})<\infty
for all
t>0
and the energy identity

\phi(\mu_{r})-\phi(\mu_{s})=\int^{s}_{r}\|{\vv}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mu_{t}}\,{\dd}t\quad\text{ for all}\,\ 0<r<s.

Proposition 4.29
.

Let
\phi
be a
\lambda
-geodesically convex function such that,
for every
\mu\in\mathfrak{D}(|\partial{\phi}|)
,
there is
\zeta\in\partial{\phi}(\mu)
with
\|{-}\zeta\|^{*}_{\mu}=|\partial{\phi}|(\mu)
.
Then,
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}\in\FAC^{2}_{\loc}((0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(M))

is a curve of maximal slope for
{\phi}
with respect to
|\partial{\phi}|

if and only if it is a trajectory of the gradient flow for
{\phi}
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}
is a trajectory of the gradient flow for
{\phi}
.
Since
|\partial{\phi}|
is a weak upper gradient for
-{\phi}

by Theorem
2.21
(
i
),
it follows from Definition
2.17
together with Remark
4.28
that

-\|{\vv}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mu_{t}}=\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}{\phi}(\mu_{t})\geq-|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\geq-\|{\vv}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mu_{t}}

for
\mathscr{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(0,\infty)
.
This implies

(4.25)

\frac{{\dd}}{{\dd}t}{\phi}(\mu_{t})=-\frac{1}{2}|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}|\partial{\phi}|^{2}(\mu_{t}),\qquad|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})=\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}=|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t).

Thus,
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}
is a curve of maximal slope for
{\phi}
with respect to
|\partial{\phi}|
.

Conversely, if
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}
is a curve of maximal slope with the tangent vector field
(\vv_{t})_{t>0}
,
then (
4.25
) holds (see (
2.12
)) and we have

\int_{r}^{s}|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)\,{\dd}t=\int_{r}^{s}|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|^{2}(t)\,{\dd}t<\infty\quad\text{for all}\,\ 0<r\leq s<\infty.

By hypothesis, there exists
\zeta_{t}\in\partial\phi(\mu_{t})

with
\|{-}\zeta_{t}\|^{*}_{\mu_{t}}=|\partial\phi|(\mu_{t})
.
Then Proposition
4.26
yields

|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t)=-\frac{\dd}{{\dd}t}{\phi}(\mu_{t})=-\langle\zeta_{t},\vv_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{t}}\leq\|{-}\zeta_{t}\|^{*}_{\mu_{t}}\|{\vv}_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}=|\partial{\phi}|(\mu_{t})|\mu^{\prime}_{+}|(t),

which implies
-\LL_{\mu_{t}}(\vv_{t})=\zeta_{t}\in\partial{\phi}(\mu_{t})
as desired.
∎

Remark 4.30
.

Although we need only the
p=2
case in the analysis of heat flow,
one can generalize the concepts and results in this subsection to the

-Wasserstein distance with

by suitable modifications.
For instance, it follows from
[

, Theorem 3.4]
that Proposition
4.20
remains valid
by replacing

with

,
where

is the conjugate exponent of

.
The

-Wasserstein distance can be used to analyze the gradient flow structure of the

-heat flow
as studied in the symmetric case by Kell
[

]
;
one could apply our method to generalize it to the asymmetric setting.

4.4.3.
Gradient flow for the relative entropy

The
relative entropy

H_{\m}:\mathcal{P}(M)\lra(-\infty,\infty]

with respect to
\m
is defined by

H_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mu):=\int_{M}\rho\log\rho\,{\dd}\mathfrak{m}

if
\mu=\rho\m
and
[\rho\log\rho]_{+}
is integrable;
otherwise we set
H_{\m}(\mu):=+\infty
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{D}(H_{\m})\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(M;\m)
.
We recall some basic properties (see
[
25
,
32
]
).

Proposition 4.31
.

(i)

H_{\m}(\mu)\geq-\log\m(M)
for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(M);

(ii)

H_{\m}

(iii)

The Sobolev space

is defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}(M)

with respect to the asymmetric norm

The proof of the following key proposition is postponed to Appendix
C
.

Proposition 4.32
.

For
\mu=\rho\m\in\mathfrak{D}(H_{\m})
, the following are equivalent
:

(I)

|\partial H_{\m}|(\mu)<\infty;

(II)

\rho\in W^{1,1}(M)
with
{\dd}\rho=\rho\zeta
for some
\zeta\in L^{2}(\mu;T^{*}M)
.

In this case,
\zeta\in\partial H_{\m}(\mu)\cap T^{*}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(M)

and is a unique strong subdifferential with

.

The following existence result follows from Proposition
4.10

with the help of Propositions
4.29
,
4.32
.

Theorem 4.33
.

For any
\mu\in\mathfrak{D}(H_{\m})
,
there exists a trajectory
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the gradient flow for
H_{\m}
with
\mu_{0}=\mu
.

For
u\in W^{1,1}(M)
, the
distributional Laplacian

\Delta_{\m}u
is defined as

\int_{M}\varphi\Delta_{\m}u\,{\dd}{\m}:=-\int_{M}\langle{\dd}\varphi,\nabla u\rangle\,{\dd}{\m}\quad\text{for all}\,\ \varphi\in C^{\infty}(M).

Note that the Laplacian
\Delta_{\m}
is a nonlinear operator
(unless
F
comes from a Riemannian metric).

Definition 4.34

(Weak solutions to the heat equation)
.

We say that
u\in L^{1}_{\loc}((0,\infty);W^{1,1}(M))
is a
weak solution
to the heat equation

\partial_{t}u_{t}=\Delta_{\m}u_{t}
if

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{M}\big\{u_{t}\cdot\partial_{t}\varphi-\langle{\dd}\varphi,\nabla u_{t}\rangle\big\}\,{\dd}{\m}\,{\dd}t=0\quad\text{for all}\,\ \varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}\big((0,\infty)\times M\big).

Remark 4.35
.

The
W^{1,1}
-regularity along with (
4.27
) below is the minimum regularity
to which our characterization result applies.
In the Riemannian case, it is well known that such a weak solution
has a smooth version (e.g., by using the heat kernel).
In the Finsler setting, however, there is no heat kernel due to the nonlinearity and
it seems unclear (to the authors) if a solution to the heat equation
given in Theorem
4.36
(
II
)
automatically enjoys a higher regularity (e.g.,
C^{1,\alpha}
as in
[
9
,
32
]
).

Theorem 4.36
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous curve in

.

(II)

(

\,\Rightarrow\,
(

Corollary 4.37
.

(i)

.

(ii)

with

,

to the heat equation

\overleftarrow{F}

(

Remark 4.38
.

In
[
32
, §3]
, we constructed a weak solution to the heat equation starting from
u\in H^{1}(M)

as a trajectory of the gradient flow for the energy in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(M)
.
Then, by the
L^{2}
-contraction property,
we can extend it to a contraction semigroup acting on
L^{2}(M)
.
In contrast, Corollary
4.37
provides a direct construction for (bounded)
u\in L^{2}(M)
.
Because of the lack of a higher regularity (Remark
4.35
)
and the Wasserstein contraction (see
[
33
]
and Subsection
4.5
below),
it seems unclear if these flows starting from
u\in L^{2}(M)\setminus H^{1}(M)
coincide.

4.5.
Further problems and related works

In “Riemannian-like” spaces,
one can proceed to the
contraction property
asserting that
two gradient curves emanating from different points
are getting closer with an exponential rate depending on the convexity of
\phi
:

d\big(\xi_{1}(t),\xi_{2}(t)\big)\leq{\ee}^{-\lambda t}d\big(\xi_{1}(0),\xi_{2}(0)\big)

(see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 4.0.4(iv)]
).
This property is, however, known to fail in Finsler-like spaces (see
[
33
]
).
It is an intriguing open problem if one can obtain
any weaker contraction estimate for convex functions on Finsler manifolds or normed spaces.
An important apparatus to study the contraction property is
the
evolution variational inequality
(see
[
1
,
23
]
),
which also forces the space to be Riemannian.

The contraction property plays a vital role in the study of heat flow.
In view of Section
4.4
and
[
32
]
,
heat flow can be regarded as the gradient flow of the relative entropy
in the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein space, and the convexity of the relative entropy
is equivalent to the associated lower Ricci curvature bound (see
[
25
,
35
,
44
]
).
Moreover, the contraction property of heat flow is
equivalent to the corresponding gradient estimate (see
[
16
]
).
Though we know some gradient estimates in the Finsler setting (see
[
28
,
27
,
34
]
),
the lack of the Riemannian-like structure prevents us to obtain a contraction property of heat flow
(precisely, the nonlinearity of the Finsler Laplacian causes an essential difference).
Therefore, generalizing the contraction property to the Finsler setting
will make a breakthrough also in the study of heat flow on Finsler manifolds.

We refer to
[
29
]
for a recent work on discrete-time gradient flows in Gromov hyperbolic spaces;
note that some non-Riemannian Finsler manifolds can be Gromov hyperbolic.
Let us also mention another related work
[
17
]
concerning
self-contracted curves
,
which is available for some Finsler manifolds.

Appendix A
An auxiliary lemma

Lemma A.1
.

Given
p\in[1,\infty)
and any
\epsilon>0
, we have

(1+\epsilon)a^{p}+\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)b^{p}\geq(a+b)^{p}\quad\text{for all}\,\ a,b\geq 0,

where

\mathfrak{C}(1,\epsilon)=1,\qquad\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)=\frac{1+\epsilon}{((1+\epsilon)^{1/(p-1)}-1)^{p-1}}\quad\text{for}\,\ p>1.

In particular,
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\mathfrak{C}(p,\epsilon)=1
for all
p\geq 1
.

Proof.

We assume
p>1
and
a,b>0
without loss of generality.
Moreover, by dividing both sides with
b^{p}
, it suffices to consider the case of
b=1
.
Put
f(a):=(a+1)^{p}-(1+\epsilon)a^{p}
for
a>0
.
Since

f^{\prime}(a)=p(a+1)^{p-1}-p(1+\epsilon)a^{p-1}

attains
0
only at
\bar{a}=\{(1+\epsilon)^{1/(p-1)}-1\}^{-1}
, we have

f(a)\leq f(\bar{a})=\frac{1+\epsilon}{((1+\epsilon)^{1/(p-1)}-1)^{p-1}}

for all
a>0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Complementary results for generalized Funk spaces

Let
(\mathbb{B},d)
be a generalized Funk space as in Subsection

Proposition B.1
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{B}
, we have the following.

(i)

F(x,\cdot)
is convex on
T_{x}\mathbb{B}\cong\mathscr{H}
,
and
F(x,v)\leq d(x,x+v)

with

(ii)

is convex on

and weakly*-lower semicontinuous, i.e.,
if
\zeta_{i}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\zeta

(
weakly* convergent
)

then we have
F^{*}(x,\zeta)\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}F^{*}(x,\zeta_{i})

(iii)

The set
\mathfrak{J}_{p}(x,v)
is at most a singleton
for every
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{B}\cong\mathscr{H}
.

Proof.

(
i
)
This is reduced to the finite-dimensional case.
The inequality
F(x,v)\leq d(x,x+v)
can be seen from

d(x,x+\varepsilon v)\leq d(x+tv,x+(t+\varepsilon)v)
for
0\leq t<t+\varepsilon\leq 1

(by the interpretation of

as in
[
40
, (1.1)]
).

(
ii
)
This is a direct consequence of the definition of
F^{*}
.

(
iii
)
Assume
v\neq\mathbf{0}
without loss of generality.
Given any
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\mathfrak{J}_{p}(x,v)
,
set
\bar{v}:=v/F(x,v)
and
\bar{\zeta}_{i}:=\zeta_{i}/F^{*}(x,\zeta_{i})
,

.
It suffices to show
\bar{\zeta}_{1}=\bar{\zeta}_{2}
.
Since

is differentiable at

, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists

such that

f(\bar{v}+w)+f(\bar{v}-w)\leq 2f(\bar{v})+\varepsilon\|w\|=2+\varepsilon\|w\|

with
\|w\|<\delta

(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Lemma 8.3]
).
Combining this with
F^{*}(x,\bar{\zeta}_{i})=\langle\bar{\zeta}_{i},\bar{v}\rangle=1
, we find

\langle\bar{\zeta}_{1},w\rangle-\langle\bar{\zeta}_{2},w\rangle=\langle\bar{\zeta}_{1},\bar{v}+w\rangle+\langle\bar{\zeta}_{2},\bar{v}-w\rangle-\langle\bar{\zeta}_{1},\bar{v}\rangle-\langle\bar{\zeta}_{2},\bar{v}\rangle\leq f(\bar{v}+w)+f(\bar{v}-w)-2\leq\varepsilon\|w\|

for any
w
with
\|w\|<\delta
.
This implies
\bar{\zeta}_{1}=\bar{\zeta}_{2}
.
∎

Proposition B.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{B}\lra(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper lower semicontinuous function.

(i)

We have
|\partial\phi|(x)\leq F^{*}(-\partial^{\circ}\phi(x))

.
In particular,

(iii)

is convex, then the graph of

in

Proof.

(
i
)
Assume

and
|\partial\phi|(x)>0

For any

(

(
