A frame approach for equations involving the fractional Laplacian
Ioannis P. A. Papadopoulos, Timon S. Gutleb, José A. Carrillo, Sheehan Olver

Abstract
Exceptionally elegant formulae exist for the fractional Laplacian operator applied to weighted classical orthogonal polynomials. We utilize these results to construct a solver, based on frame properties, for equations involving the fractional Laplacian of any power, s ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s\in(0,1) , on an unbounded domain in one or two dimensions. The numerical method represents solutions in an expansion of weighted classical orthogonal polynomials as well as their unweighted counterparts with a specific extension to ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ∈ { 1 , 2 } d\in\{1,2\} . We examine the frame properties of this family of functions for the solution expansion and, under standard frame conditions, derive an a priori estimate for the stationary equation. Moreover, we prove one achieves the expected order of convergence when considering an implicit Euler discretization in time for the fractional heat equation. We apply our solver to numerous examples including the fractional heat equation (utilizing up to a 6 th 6^{\text{th}} -order Runge–Kutta time discretization), a fractional heat equation with a time-dependent exponent s ⁡ ( t ) s(t) , and a two-dimensional problem, observing spectral convergence in the spatial dimension for sufficiently smooth data.

Definition 3.1

then

\displaystyle{\bf\Phi}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\phi_{1}(x)\ \ \phi_{2}(x)\ \ \cdots\right).

(3.1)

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider the equation
Eq.

1.1

H
be a Hilbert space, and fix a right-hand side

. Suppose that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)
. Consider a finite-dimensional set of functions
\Phi_{N}=\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}\}
and define
\Psi_{N}=\mathcal{L}\Phi_{N}=\{\mathcal{L}\phi_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}\phi_{N}\}
. Consider any expansion coefficient
{\bf f}
for

in the family of functions

. Then

\displaystyle\|u-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|f-{\bf\Psi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H^{*}}.

(3.2)

Proof.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|u-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H}&=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathcal{L}u-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H}\\
&=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}f-\mathcal{L}^{-1}{\bf\Psi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|f-{\bf\Psi}_{N}{\bf f}\|_{H^{*}}.\end{split}

(3.3)

∎

The generalized Zernike polynomials,
Z^{(b)}_{n,m,j}(x,y)
,
a>-1
, are multivariate orthogonal polynomials in Cartesian coordinates defined on the unit disk,
\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\}
. The subscript
n
denotes the polynomial degree and
(m,j)
denotes the Fourier mode and sign. If
n
is even, then
m\in\{0,2,\dots,n\}
and, if
n
is odd, then
m\in\{1,3,\dots,n\}
. If
m=0
, then
j=1
, otherwise
j\in\{0,1\}
. The generalized Zernike polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the weight
(1-r^{2})^{b}
, where
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
. They may be defined via the Jacobi polynomials as:

\displaystyle V_{m,j}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq r^{m}\sin(m\theta+j\pi/2)

(4.9)

\displaystyle Z^{(b)}_{n,m,j}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq V_{m,j}(x,y)P^{(b,m)}_{(n-m)/2}(2r^{2}-1).

(4.10)

We define the extended Zernike functions for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
r\neq 1
, (when they exist) as

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{(b,s)}_{n,m,j}(x,y)\coloneqq(-\Delta)^{s}[(1-r^{2})^{b}Z^{(b)}_{n,m,j}(x,y)].

(4.11)

The following theorem provides explicit expressions for

.

Theorem 4.3

(Row A
∗∗
, Tab. 7 in
[

]

Suppose that

,

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{Z}^{(s,s)}_{n,m,j}(x,y)=V_{m,j}(x,y)\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(1+s+n)}{\Gamma(n+1)}\\
&\times\resizebox{24155235}{}{$\begin{cases}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+m+n+s\right)P_{n}^{(s,m)}(2r^{2}-1)}{\Gamma\left(1+n+m\right)}&r<1,\\

(4.12)

5
Frames

As the family of approximating functions is not orthogonal, the expansion of a known function,
f(x)
, is nontrivial. In particular, the expansion need not be unique. For our purposes, we desire an expansion that approximates our known function to the required tolerance and the coefficients of the expansion are (relatively) small in magnitude
[
4
]
.

We turn to the techniques used in frame theory
[
2
,
3
]
. Essentially, we find the coefficients of the expansion that optimally interpolate the values of
f(x)
(or a linear operator applied to
f
if
f
is not defined pointwise) at a set of collocation points in a least squares sense. Consider the collocation points
\bm{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
. Let
{\bf\Phi}(x)
denote the quasimatrix for the expansion of the solution
u(x)
. Pick the number of functions in the truncation
N
. As depicted in line 3 of
Algorithm

1
, the least-squares matrix is given by

\displaystyle X_{ij}=[\ell_{j}(\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi})]_{i},\;\;i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1\,\dots,M.

(5.1)

Similarly we compute
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=\ell_{j}(f)
. As discussed, we choose
\ell_{j}(\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi})=(\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi})(x_{j})
, for the collocation points
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, provided
(\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi})(x_{j})
and
f(x_{j})
are well-defined. We then solve the following least-squares problem for the expansion coefficients
{\bf u}
:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\|X{\bf u}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}},

(5.2)

so that
f\approx\mathcal{L}{\bf\Phi}{\bf u}
and, therefore,
u\approx{\bf\Phi}{\bf u}
(as discussed in
Section

3
). In a frame setting, the least-squares matrix is often ill-conditioned for increasing
M
and
N
. However, in practice we recover suitable least squares solutions if we sufficiently oversample the collocation points and use a truncated SVD solver. The remainder of this section will focus on explaining this phenomenon.

Consider the SVD factorization
X=U\Sigma V^{\top}
with the convention where
\Sigma
is a square diagonal matrix with the singular values arranged in decreasing magnitude along the diagonal. Given a precision tolerance
\epsilon
, a truncated SVD solver finds the first singular value such that
\Sigma_{i+1,i+1}<\epsilon<\Sigma_{i,i}
. Then, the submatrix
\Sigma_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{i\times i}
,
[\Sigma_{\epsilon}]_{j,j}=\Sigma_{j,j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
is extracted. The
\epsilon
-truncated SVD projection of
Eq.

5.2
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{\epsilon}=V_{\epsilon}\Sigma^{-1}_{\epsilon}U_{\epsilon}^{\top}{\bf y},

(5.3)

where
V_{\epsilon}
and
U_{\epsilon}
are the first
i
columns of
V
and
U
, respectively.

Remark 5.1

(Truncated SVD cost)
.

With
M>N
the cost of computing the SVD of
X
scales as
\mathcal{O}(MN^{2})
. In the examples found in
Section

7
, we found that this cost is negligible but since our analytical results generalize to two and three dimensions, the cost of the factorization may eventually prove prohibitive. If required, then for suitable frames, the solve may be implemented in
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N)
operations via the
AZ
-algorithm
[
17
]
and randomized linear algebra solvers for least-squares
[
29
,
39
,
40
]
.

Remark 5.2

(Evaluation of the least-squares matrix)
.

Another potential computational bottleneck is the assembly of the least-squares matrix
X
as defined in
Eq.

5.1
, particularly if we are required to evaluate many hypergeometric functions at many collocation points. Although a direct evaluation of many
{{}_{2}}F_{1}
functions may be slow, we note that the extended Jacobi functions satisfy the same three-term recurrence as their Jacobi polynomial counterparts. Thus one may use the forward recurrence for efficient evaluation of the extended Jacobi functions on the interval
[-1,1]
and (F. W. J) Olver’s and Miller’s algorithm for evaluation off the interval
[-1,1]
, cf.
[
27
, Sec. 2.3]
,
[
42
, Sec. 3.6]
and
[
43
, App. B]
. Alternatively, off the interval one can use a continued fraction approach advocated by Gautschi
[
26
]
.

We now introduce the notion of a
frame
.

Definition 5.1

(Frame)
.

Consider a Hilbert space
(H,(\cdot,\cdot)_{H})
. An indexed family of functions
\{\phi_{n}\}\in H
is called a
frame
for
H
if there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that

\displaystyle c\|f\|^{2}_{H}\leq\sum_{n}|(f,\phi_{n})_{H}|^{2}\leq C\|f\|^{2}_{H}\;\;\text{for all}\;\;f\in H.

(5.4)

As the family of functions for the expansion of the right-hand side will vary according to the choice of
Eq.

1.1
, it is helpful to construct a framework whereby if one shows that the family of functions for the solution expansion is a frame (which remains fixed), then the induced family of functions for the right-hand is automatically a frame provided that the operator
\mathcal{L}
in
Eq.

1.1
operator is well-behaved.

Theorem 5.1

(Frames on the dual space)
.

Suppose that the family of functions
\Phi=\{\phi_{n}\}
is a frame on the Hilbert space
H
. Consider the bounded linear operator
\mathcal{L}:H\to H^{*}
where
H^{*}
is the dual space of
H
. Moreover, assume that the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}:H^{*}\to H
is bounded and positive-definite with constants
M_{b}
and
M_{p}
, respectively. Then
\mathcal{L}{\Phi}=\{\mathcal{L}\phi_{n}\}
is a frame on the Hilbert space
H^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{n}=\mathcal{L}\phi_{n}
, for any
n
. Now consider any
f^{*}\in H^{*}
. The lower bound is derived as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}cM_{p}^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{*}}\leq c\|\mathcal{L}^{*}f^{*}\|^{2}_{H}\leq\sum_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{*}f^{*},\phi_{n})^{2}_{H}=\sum_{n}(f^{*},\psi_{n})^{2}_{H^{*}},\end{split}

(5.5)

where
c
is the lower bound for the frame condition of
\Phi
. The first inequality follows from the positive-definiteness of the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and the second inequality follows from the frame condition of
\Phi
. The final equality follows by the definition of the adjoint operator and
\psi_{n}
.

Similarly, let
C
denote the upper bound for the frame condition of
\Phi
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{n}(f^{*},\psi_{n})^{2}_{H^{*}}=\sum_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{*}f^{*},\phi_{n})^{2}_{H}\\
&\indent\leq C\|\mathcal{L}^{*}f^{*}\|^{2}_{H}\leq C\|\mathcal{L}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{*}}=CM_{b}^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{*}}.\end{split}

(5.6)

∎

5.1

\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{I}+(-\Delta)^{s})

In this subsection, we show that a family of functions consisting of weighted Jacobi polynomials and their fractional Laplacian counterparts
{\bf\Phi}(x)
are a frame on a weighted Hilbert space. Then, by utilizing
Theorem

5.1
, we conclude that
(\mathcal{I}+(-\Delta)^{s}){\bf\Phi}(x)

Definition 5.2

(Weighted Lebesgue space)
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
denote a Muckenhoupt weight
[
41
]
. Then we denote the weighted Lebesgue space by
L^{p}_{w}(a,b)
when equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}_{w}(a,b)}\coloneqq(\int_{a}^{b}|f|^{p}w\,\mathrm{d}x)^{1/p}
. If
p=2
, then
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
(f,g)_{L^{2}_{w}(a,b)}\coloneqq\int_{a}^{b}fgw\,\mathrm{d}x
.

Definition 5.3

(Hilbert space
H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

Let
H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
denote the Hilbert space

\displaystyle H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\coloneqq\{u\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}):\mathrm{supp}(u)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(w),\;(-\Delta)^{s/2}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\},

(5.7)

equipped with the inner-product

(u,v)_{H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\coloneqq(u,v)_{L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}+((-\Delta)^{s/2}u,(-\Delta)^{s/2}v)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Here
\mathrm{supp}
denotes the support of a function.

Remark 5.3
.

When considering nonlocal operators defined on
\mathbb{R}
, such as
(-\Delta)^{s}
, we note that even if the support of a function
u
is compactly contained in
\mathbb{R}
, this does not mean
(-\Delta)^{s}u
is compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}
. This is the motivation behind considering the inner-product defined on all of
\mathbb{R}
in
Definition

5.3
.

The following lemma proves orthogonality of weighted and extended Jacobi polynomials. This is heavily utilized for the frame result in
Theorem

5.2
. A similar result may be found in
[
45
, Prop. 3.6]
.

Lemma 5.1

(Orthogonality)
.

Let
w(x)=(1-x^{2})_{+}^{-s}
,
s<1
. Then,
\{Q_{n}^{(s,s)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\{\tilde{P}_{n}^{(-s,-s)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfy

\displaystyle(\tilde{P}_{n}^{(-s,-s)},\tilde{P}_{m}^{(-s,-s)})_{H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}=\tilde{C}_{n,m}\delta_{n,m}\;\;\text{and}\;\;(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},Q_{m}^{(s,s)})_{H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}=C_{n,m}\delta_{n,m},

(5.8)

where
\delta_{nm}
denotes the Kronecker delta and
C_{n,m},\tilde{C}_{n,m}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
c_{s,n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, denote the constants such that, for all
x\in(-1,1)
,
\tilde{P}_{n}^{(s,s)}(x)=c_{s,n}P_{n}^{(s,s)}(x)
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},Q_{m}^{(s,s)})_{H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent=(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},Q_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}+((-\Delta)^{s/2}Q_{n}^{(s,s)},(-\Delta)^{s/2}Q_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent=(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},{P}_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}(-1,1)}+(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},(-\Delta)^{s}Q_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent=(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},{P}_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}(-1,1)}+(Q_{n}^{(s,s)},c_{s,m}P_{m}^{(s,s)})_{L^{2}(-1,1)}={C}_{n,m}\delta_{n,m}.\end{split}

(5.9)

The result for
\{\tilde{P}_{n}^{(-s,-s)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
follows similarly.
∎

Theorem 5.2

(Frame on
H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
,
s\in(1/2,1)

.

Let
w(x)=(1-x^{2})_{+}^{-s}
,
s\in(1/2,1)

\{\hat{Q}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}
and
\{\hat{P}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}

\{Q_{n}^{(s,s)}\}
and
\{\tilde{P}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}

\{\hat{Q}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\cup\{\hat{P}_{n}^{(-s,-s)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

.

Proof.

The union of an orthonormal system and an orthonormal basis results in a frame

2

. In

5.1

\{\hat{Q}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}

Consider any

,

and 1. By a Hölder regularity result
[

since

must be continuous. By definition

in

and, therefore,

\{\hat{Q}_{n}^{(s,s)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

.
∎

A basic building block for a frame suitable for expanding the solution of

Corollary 5.1

(Frame on
H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})^{*}
,

.

\Psi=\{\hat{P}^{(s,s)}_{n}+\hat{Q}^{(s,s)}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\cup\{\hat{Q}^{(-s,-s)}_{n}+\hat{P}^{(-s,-s)}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a frame on
(H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
where
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{-s}_{+}
,
s\in(1/2,1)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)^{s}+\mathcal{I}
. In
Theorem

5.2
it was shown that
\Phi=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Psi
is a frame on
H=H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
. By
Theorem

5.1
, it is sufficient to show that the adjoint
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is positive-definite and bounded on
H^{*}=(H^{s}_{w}(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
.

Bounded.
Consider any
u^{*},v^{*}\in H^{*}
. Since
H
is a Hilbert space, it is reflexive. Thus, by the Riesz Representation Theorem
[
23
, Sec. D.2, Th. 2]
, for any
u^{*}\in H^{*}
there exists a unique

such that
\|u^{*}\|_{H^{*}}=\|u\|_{H}
and
\langle u^{*},v\rangle_{H^{*},H}=(u,v)_{H}
for all

. Let
u,v\in H
denote the unique Riesz representations of
u^{*}
and
v^{*}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\langle\mathcal{L}^{*}v^{*},u^{*}\rangle|&=|(\mathcal{L}^{*}v^{*},u)_{H}|=|(\mathcal{L}u,v^{*})_{H^{*}}|=|\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle|=|(u,v)_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}|\\
&\leq\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}\|v\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|u\|_{H}\|v\|_{H}=\|u^{*}\|_{H^{*}}\|v^{*}\|_{H^{*}},\end{split}

(5.10)

where the first, third, and final equalities follow by the Riesz Representation Theorem, the second equality follows by definition of the adjoint operator, the fourth equality follows from an integration by parts, the first inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and the second inequality follows since
\mathrm{supp}(u),\mathrm{supp}(v)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(w)
and
w^{1/2}(x)\geq 1
for

.
Thus

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}^{*}u^{*}\|_{H}=\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{|\langle\mathcal{L}^{*}u^{*},v^{*}\rangle|}{\|v^{*}\|_{H^{*}}}\leq\|u^{*}\|_{H^{*}}.

(5.11)

Positive-definite.
Recall that

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{H}=(u,u)_{H}=((-\Delta)^{s/2}u,(-\Delta)^{s/2}u)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+(w^{1/2}u,w^{1/2}u)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(5.12)

Now

\displaystyle(w^{1/2}u,w^{1/2}u)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|w^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|w^{1/2}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})},

(5.13)

where

\displaystyle(w^{1/2}u,w^{1/2}u)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C^{2}\|w^{1/2}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}.

(5.14)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{*}}=\|u\|^{2}_{H}\leq(C^{2}\|w^{1/2}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}+1)(u,u)_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}=\tilde{C}\langle\mathcal{L}u,u\rangle\\
&\indent=\tilde{C}(\mathcal{L}u,u^{*})_{H^{*}}=\tilde{C}(u,\mathcal{L}^{*}u^{*})_{H}\leq\tilde{C}\|\mathcal{L}^{*}u^{*}\|_{H}\|u\|_{H}=\tilde{C}\|\mathcal{L}^{*}u^{*}\|_{H}\|u^{*}\|_{H^{*}}.\end{split}

6
Time-dependent problems

Implementing solvers for fractional-in-space time dependent problems is notoriously difficult due to the slow decay of the solutions and the accumulation of errors in the coefficients of the expansion. If the domain is truncated then the artificial boundary layers will eventually dominate the error in the solution. Similarly, although many time-stepping schemes are initially stable, the coefficients of the spatial expansion often become increasingly larger in magnitude leading to floating point cancellation error and a breakdown in the solution for large time
t
.

Our spectral method may be coupled with any Runge–Kutta (RK) method allowing for an arbitrary order method in time and spectral in space. Consider the fractional-in-space time-dependent equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+F(t,u)=0,\;u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),

(6.1)

where
u(\cdot,t)\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
for a.e.
t\geq 0
. For instance, in the fractional heat equation, we have

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider the Hilbert space
H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})
, where
\omega(x)=1
in
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, for an arbitrary open interval
\Omega
, and
\omega(x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\Omega
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{I}+(-\Delta)^{s}
. Fix any
u^{*}\in H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})
and also denote by
u^{*}\in H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}
the embedding of
u^{*}
into
H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\|u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}),H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}))}\|u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}.

(6.8)

Proof.

Consider any
u^{*}\in H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}
. There exists a unique
u\in H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})
such that, for all
v\in H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})

[
23
, Sec. D.2, Th. 2]
,

\displaystyle(u,v)_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}=\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*},H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}=\langle u^{*},v\rangle_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*},H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})},

(6.9)

and
\|u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}}=\|u\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}
. Hence, the Riesz map is
u=\mathcal{L}^{-1}u^{*}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})^{*}}=\|u\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}),H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}))}\|u^{*}\|_{H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})}.

(6.10)

∎

Theorem 6.1

(Implicit Euler convergence)
.

Consider the fractional heat equation
Eq.

6.2
and choose the uniform time discretization points
t_{0},\dots,t_{J}
. Define
\delta t=t_{1}-t_{0}
and let
{\bf\Phi}_{N}(x)
denote the truncated quasimatrix of a frame on the Hilbert space
H=H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})
with
H^{s}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R})
as defined in
Lemma

6.1
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\delta t}=(\mathcal{I}+\delta t(-\Delta)^{s})
which corresponds to an implicit Euler discretization in time. Fix
{\bf\Phi}_{N}^{*}(x)=\mathcal{L}_{\delta t}{\bf\Phi}_{N}(x)
. Suppose that
\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}}
and
\|\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\|_{{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}}
are bounded and there exists a Lipschitz constant
L>0
such that for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in H
,
\|(-\Delta)^{s}(w_{1}-w_{2})\|_{H^{*}}\leq L\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{H}
. Fix the number of collocation points
M
and the required SVD tolerance
\epsilon
. Define
h=\max_{j\in\{0,\dots,J\}}h_{j}
with
h_{j}
being defined as in
Eq.

6.7
. We denote the exact solution of
Eq.

6.2
by
u(x,t)
. Suppose that we have a
{\bf u}_{0}
and
0<h_{0}\leq h<1
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{0}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H}\leq h_{0}\|u_{0}\|_{H}.

(6.11)

Then, for some
C_{0}>0
, the following error bound holds:

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{j})-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H}\leq C_{0}\delta t+C_{1}(u_{0},j)C_{2}(h,j)h,

(6.12)

where

C_{2}(h,j)=\sum_{i=0}^{j}(1+h)^{i}
and
C_{1}(u_{0},j)=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|^{j}_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|^{j}_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|u_{0}\|_{H}.

Proof.

Let
u_{j}(x)
,
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
, denote the solutions of

\displaystyle\frac{u_{j+1}(x)-u_{j}(x)}{\delta t}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j+1}(x)=0,\;\;u_{0}(x)=u(x,t_{0}).

(6.13)

By assumption
(-\Delta)^{s}:H\to H^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous and thus there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
independent of
\delta t
such that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{j})-u_{j}\|_{H}\leq C_{0}\delta t.

(6.14)

Eq.

6.14
follows from classical results for implicit Euler discretizations, e.g.
[
24
, Sec. 5]
where the Eucledian norms have been replaced by
\|\cdot\|_{H}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{*}}
as appropriate. Hence,

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{j})-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H}\leq C_{0}\delta t+\|u_{j}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H}.

(6.15)

Now, by recalling the implicit Euler time step
Eq.

6.7
and utilizing
Lemma

6.1
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|u_{j}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H}=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}(\mathcal{L}_{\delta t}u_{j}-\mathcal{L}_{\delta t}{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j})\|_{H}\\
&\indent\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|u_{j-1}-{\bf\Phi}^{*}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H^{*}}\\
&\indent\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\left(\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j-1}-{\bf\Phi}^{*}_{N}{\bf u}^{*}_{j-1}\|_{H^{*}}+\|u_{j-1}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j-1}\|_{H^{*}}\right)\\
&\indent\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\left(h\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j-1}\|_{H^{*}}+\|\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|u_{j-1}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j-1}\|_{H}\right)\\
&\indent\leq h\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|^{j-i}_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|^{j-i-1}_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{i}\|_{H^{*}}\\
&\indent\indent\indent\indent+\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|^{j}_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|^{j}_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|u_{0}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H}.\end{split}

(6.16)

We note that, for any
i\in\{0,1,\dots,j-1\}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{i}\|_{H^{*}}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{i}\|_{H}=\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}^{*}_{i-1}\|_{H}\\
&\indent\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|{\bf\Phi}^{*}_{N}{\bf u}^{*}_{i-1}\|_{H^{*}}\\
&\indent\leq(1+h)\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H)}\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{i-1}\|_{H^{*}}\\
&\indent\leq(1+h)^{i}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}^{i}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|^{i}_{\mathcal{B}(H,H^{*})}\|{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H^{*}}\\
&\indent\leq(1+h)^{i+1}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H,H)}^{i+1}\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\delta t}\|^{i}_{\mathcal{B}(H,H^{*})}\|u_{0}\|_{H},\end{split}

(6.17)

By assumption
\|u_{0}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H}\leq h\|u_{0}\|_{H}
. Hence, by utilizing the bound in
Eq.

6.17
for
Eq.

6.16
and the subsequent bound of
\|u_{j}-{\bf\Phi}_{N}{\bf u}_{j}\|_{H}
into
Eq.

6.15
, we deduce the result.
∎

8
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a frame approach, summarized in
Algorithm

1
, for solving equations involving nonlocal and fractional terms such as the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
,
s\in(0,1)
. One fixes a family of functions for the expansion of the solution and expands the right-hand side of the equation in the space induced by applying the equation operator to this family of functions. This diagonalizes the equation and reduces the problem from a solve of a nonlocal problem to an interpolation problem.

Under suitable conditions, we showed that if the family of functions for the solution expansion is a frame in a suitable Hilbert space, then the induced family of functions for the right-hand side expansion is a frame in the dual of the Hilbert space. By considering solution expansions in weighted Jacobi and Zernike polynomials, we utilized recent results to deduce the family of functions for the right-hand side expansion. With this knowledge, one automatically recovers favourable properties of frames, in particular, the utilization of a truncated SVD projection to find a well-posed expansion. This culminated in deriving an a priori estimate in
Theorem

5.3
.

This spatial discretization was coupled with Runga–Kutta methods in time in order to develop a method for time-dependent problems including the fractional heat equation as well as a problem where the exponent of the fractional Laplacian evolved in time. We proved that one recovers the optimal convergence rates under an implicit Euler discretization and observed the expected convergence rates for our chosen Runge-Kutta methods in practice, up to a
6^{\text{th}}
order method. Moreover, the method was also competitive when applied to a two-dimensional problem.

This approach is general and is not limited to the operators considered in this work. Provided one has explicit expressions of the equation operator applied to a family of functions, one may use this frame approach for a considerable number of different problems. Future work will consider generalizing this technique to be competitive in higher dimensions. The bottleneck inevitably becomes the SVD factorization to interpolate the right-hand side. However, by using randomized SVD solvers
[
29
,
40
]
and the
AZ
-algorithm
[
17
]
, we hope to considerably reduce the computational complexity.
