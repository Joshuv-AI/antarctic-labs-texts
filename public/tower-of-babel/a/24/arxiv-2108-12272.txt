An Abstract Beurling’s Theorem for Several Complex Variables I Thanks: Dedicated to the memory of the late Lee Rubel. Lee, this one’s for you.
Charles W. Neville

Abstract
. How to extend Beurling’s theorem on the shift invariant subspaces of Hardy class H 2 H^{2} of the unit disk to several complex variables has been an open problem at least since 1964. In this paper, we prove a generalization of Beurling’s theorem to valuation Hilbert modules over valuation algebras. We shall apply our abstract Beurling’s theorem to obtain a complete description of the closed invariant subspaces of H 2 H^{2} of the polydisk. As we shall show in a subsequent paper, further consequences of our abstract Beurling’s theorem are complete extensions of Beurling’s theorem to many more Hilbert spaces of analytic functions in several complex variables.

1.
Introduction.

When confronted with an
R
module
M

R

R

M

closed

R

of polynomials in the complex variable

of the unit disk
[

]
. (See
[

of the unit disk.

As for extensions of Beurling’s theorem to several complex variables, the question was raised as far back as 1964 by Henry Helson (
[
10
]
, p. 25 ff). In 1986, Agrawal, Clark, and Douglas observed, “Almost everyone who has thought about this topic must have considered the corresponding problem for
H^{2}
of the polydisk” but that little progress had been made, except for counter-examples and the important theorem of Ahern and Clark
[
1
]
. (The theorem of Ahern and Clark characterizes the closed submodules of
H^{2}
of the polydisk of finite codimension as the closures of polynomial ideals of finite codimension
[
2
]
, cf. also the the exposition in
[
14
]
.) Since Agrawal, Clark, and Douglas wrote this, significant progress was made in related areas (cf. the papers
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
7
]
). In 2007, there was a characterization of an important class of closed submodules of
H^{2}
of the polydisk with deep ties to operator theory
[
16
]
. But a complete characterization of the closed submodules of
H^{2}
of the polydisk had eluded us.. In this paper, we shall prove an abstract generalization of Beurling’s classical theorem, which specializes to a concrete generalization of Beurling’s theorem to
H^{2}
of the polydisk. In a second paper, we shall apply our abstract generalization of Beurlings theorem to obtain.concrete generalizations of Beurling’s theorem to a number of Hilbert modules of analytic functions on domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and on multi-dimensional complex analytic manifolds.

3.
A Preview of Coming Attractions.

The special case where the Hilbert module
\mathbb{H}
is Hardy class
H^{2}
of the polydisk and the algebra
R
is
P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
provides an excellent guide to our abstract Beurling’s theorem. It also illustrates why our abstract Beurling’s theorem deserves to be considered a generalization of Beurling’s theorem.

We need yet more standing notation. Throughout this paper,
D^{n}
will be the open
n
-dimensional unit polydisk,

\{z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z_{j}|<1\text{ for }j=1,\dots,n\}

and
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
will be
E
-valued Hardy class
H^{2}
of the polydisk. Of course,
\mathbb{C}
itself is a complex vector space (the simplest one), so
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{n})
is the familiar scalar valued
H^{2}
space of the polydisk
[
14
]
.

Throughout this paper,
T^{n}
will be the
n
-dimensional unit torus,

\{z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z_{j}|=1\text{ for }j=1,\dots,n\}.

T^{n}
is the distinguished boundary of
D^{n}
and a subset of the topological boundary
\partial D^{n}
of
D^{n}
. The standard parametrization of
T^{n}
is
e^{\imath\theta}=(e^{\imath\theta_{1}},\dots,e^{\imath\theta_{n}})
,
0\leq\theta_{j}\leq 2\pi
, for
j=1,\dots,n
. When speaking of
T^{n}
,
d\sigma
will be normalized Haar measure on
T^{n}
with respect to the group of coordinate rotations,

z\mapsto e^{\imath\theta}z=(e^{\imath\theta_{1}}z_{1},\dots,e^{\imath\theta_{n}}z_{n}),\>\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

The measure
d\sigma
has the simple form,
d\sigma=d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}/(2\pi)^{n}
, so we shall often use the shorthand
d\sigma=d\theta/(2\pi)^{n}.

A function
h\colon D^{n}\mapsto E
is
n
-harmonic if it is continuous, and if at each point
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in D^{n}
, each slice function

(a_{1},\dots,a_{j}+z_{j},\dots,a_{n})\mapsto h((a_{1},\dots,a_{j}+z_{j},\dots,a_{n}))

is harmonic in
z_{j}
near
a_{j}
(c.f.
[
14
]
).

H^{2}(E,D^{n})
is the space of all
E
-valued functions
f
analytic on
D^{n}
, such that the
n
-subharmonic function
z\mapsto\|f(z)\|_{E}^{2}
has an
n
-harmonic majorant. The norm on
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
is given by

\|f\|^{2}=h_{f}(0)

where
h_{f}
is the least
n
-harmonic majorant of the function
z\to\|f(z)\|_{E}^{2}
.

If the Hilbert space
E
is of finite or countable dimension, there are no measurability issues, and the well known boundary value theory for the scalar valued case (cf
[
14
]
) carries over directly to the
E
-valued case. This allows us to identify
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
with the subspace of a.e.
d\sigma
defined functions in
L^{2}(E,d\sigma)
with Fourier coefficients supported in the “first quadrant,”
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
, exactly as in the scalar valued case.

However, if
E
is of uncountable dimension, there are significant measurability issues. Basically, these can be resolved by using the Dunford-Pettis integral and weak measurability. (
f
is weakly measurable if
\langle f,e\rangle_{E}
is measurable for each
e\in E
.)

We need to discuss how to extend an
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
function
f
, only defined a.e. on
T^{n}
, to the interior of
D^{n}
. The key is to use the
n
-harmonic Poisson integral formuula

(3.1)

\begin{split}f(a)&=\int_{T^{n}}K(a,z)f(z)\,d\sigma(z)\\
h_{f}(a)&=\int_{T^{n}}K(a,z)\|f(z)\|_{E}^{2}\,d\sigma(z)\end{split}

for
a\in D^{n}
. Here,
K(a,z)
is the
n
-harmonic Poisson kernel, equal to the product
K_{1}(a_{1},z_{1})\cdots K_{1}(a_{n},z_{n})
of ordinary Poisson kernels for the unit disk
D^{1}
, and
h_{f}
is, as before, the least
n
-harmonic majorant of
\|f\|_{E}^{2}
. Note that
K(0,0)=1
, so the two definitions of
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
are equivalent even in norm.

We can also profitably use the Szegő integral formula for
f\in H^{2}(E,D^{n})
:

(3.2)

f(a)=\int_{T^{n}}S(a,z)d\sigma(z)

for
a\in D^{n}
. Here,
S(a,z)
is the Szegő kernel for the polydisk, equal to the product
S_{1}(a,z_{1})\cdots S_{1}(a,z_{n})
of ordinary Szegő kernels for the unit disk.

Finally, to make this absolutely simple,
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
is the space of square summable
E
-valued power series,

(3.3)

\{f(z)=\sum a_{m}z^{m}:\sum\|a_{m}\|_{E}^{2}<\infty\}

Here, and throughout this paper, we freely use standard multi-index notation:

a_{m}=a_{m_{1},\dots,m_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad z^{m}=z_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{m_{n}}

for
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
and
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

In later papers, we shall consider
H^{2}(E,D^{n}(r))
, where
r
is a real number with
0<r<1
, or
r=r_{1},\dots r_{n})
is a poly-radius with
0<r_{j}<1
for
j=1,\dots n
. One simply has to divide
a_{j}
and
z_{j}
by
r_{j}
or
r
in the above formulas to reduce the study of
H^{2}(E,D^{n}(r))
to that of
H^{2}(E,D^{n}))
(
[
14
]
p. 3).

Whichever way, using formula (
3.1
), (
3.2
), or (
3.3
), we note several additional properties of
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(E,D^{n})
and
R=P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
:

(3.4.1)

\mathbb{H}
is a Hilbert module over the complex algebra
R
.

(3.4.2)

Both
\mathbb{H}
and
R
come equipped with a
\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mspace{0.5mu}\mathbin{\cup}\mspace{0.5mu}\{\infty\}
valued ord function which measures the order of the zero of
f\in\mathbb{H}
or
p\in R
at
0
.

(3.4.3)

For
p\in R
,
\ord(p)=0
if
p
is a unit of
R
.

(3.4.4)

For
f\in\mathbb{H}
or
f\in R
,
\ord(f)=\infty
if and only if
f
is identically
0
.

(3.4.5)

The
\ord
function almost respects multiplication:
\ord(pf)\geq\ord(p)

+\ord(f)
, and
\ord(pq)\geq\ord(p)+\ord(q)
, for
f\in\mathbb{H}
,
p\in R
, and
q\in R
. (Of course, in this case, inequality may be replaced by equality, but we want to state this in as general a form as possible.)

(3.4.6)

The
\ord
function respects scalar multiplication:
\ord(\lambda f)=\ord(f)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\lambda\neq 0
, and
f\in\mathbb{H}
or
f\in R
.

(3.4.7)

The
\ord
function (almost) respects addition:
\ord(f+g)\geq\\
\min(\ord(f),\ord(g))
for
f\text{ and }g\in\mathbb{H}
or
f\text{ and }g\in R
.

(3.4.8)

The
\ord
function is upper semi-continuous on
\mathbb{H}
. Thus the subspaces
\mathbb{H}_{m}=\{f\in\mathbb{H}:\ord(f)\geq m\}
are closed for
m=1,2,\dots
because
\mathbb{H}_{m}
is the inverse image of the set
R_{m}=\{r\in R:\ord(r)\geq m\}
.

There are, in fact, several possible
\ord
functions. The one we shall use is

Definition 3.5
.

\ord(f)=
the degree of the lowest degree term in the power series expansion of
f
for
f\in\mathbb{H}
, and similarly for
R
.

We still need to define the degree of a monomial: The

of
z^{m}=z_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{m_{n}}
is
|m|
, where here and throughout this paper,
|m|=|m_{1}|+\dots+|m_{n}|
. This, of course, is the standard definition.

Another equivalent definition of the
\ord
function is

Definition 3.6
.

\ord(f)=
the lowest degree term in the expansion of
f
in homogeneous polynomials for
f\in\mathbb{H}
, and similarly for
p\in R
.

Of course, the degree of a homogeneous polynomial is the common degree of its monomial terms. (See
[
15
]
for homogeneous expansions of analytic functions.)

In the next section, we shall call a left Hilbert module
\mathbb{H}
over a (possibly non-commutative) complex algebra
R
a
valuation Hilbert module
, and
R
a
valuation algebra
, if both are equipped with an
\ord
function satisfying properties (
3.4.1
) through (
3.4.8
). Our abstract Beurling’s theorem will characterize the closed left
R
submodules of of an arbitrary valuation Hilbert module. (Truth in advertising: Actually, the closed
R_{1}
submodules, where
R_{1}=\{r\in R:\ord(r)\geq 1\}
. But if
R
has a unit element
1_{R}
and
R=\mathbb{C}\cdot 1_{R}\,+\,R_{1}
, these are the same as the closed left
R
submodules. See definition
4.10
and proposition
4.11
.) As hinted above, homogeneous subspaces, similar to subspaces of of homogeneous polynomials all of the same degree or
\{0\}
, will play a prominent role.

To gain an inkling of what our actual abstract Beurling’s theorem will look like in the polydisk case, recall the wandering subspace approach to the classical
1
-variable Hilbert space valued Beurling’s theorem due to Halmos
[
8
]
. (See
[
11
]
for a complete exposition.) This theorem characterizes the closed
P[\mathbb{C},z]
submodules (classically called closed shift invariant subspaces) of
H^{2}(E,D^{1})
as the subspaces of the form

(3.7)

V=I\cdot H^{2}(W,D^{1})

where
W
is a complex Hilbert space and
I
is an operator valued inner function. This means

(3.8)

I(z)\colon W\mapsto E

is a (usually partial) isometry a.e.
d\theta
on
T^{1}
, the unit circle. Given a closed
P[\mathbb{C},z]
submodule
V
, the operator valued inner function is constructed from the
wandering subspace

(3.9)

W=V\ominus_{\perp}zV

Here and throughout this paper,
\ominus_{\perp}
will denote the orthogonal complement. Then an equivalent statement of the
1
-variable Beurling’s theorem is

(3.10)

V=W\oplus_{\perp}zW\oplus_{\perp}z^{2}W\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

where here and throughout this paper,
\oplus_{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal direct sum.

Our abstract Beurling’s theorem uses a similar idea. Specialized to the case of
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
, our theorem may be briefly stated as follows:

Let
V
be a closed subspace of
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
. In place of the single wandering subspace
W
in equations (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we consider the decreasing sequence of closed subspaces

(3.11)

\begin{split}V_{0}=V,\quad V_{1}&=\{f\in V:\ord(f)\geq 1\},\\
V_{2}&=\{f\in V:\ord(f)\geq 2\},\>\dots\end{split}

and the sequence of
near homogeneous
subspaces,

(3.12)

W_{0}=V_{0}\ominus_{\perp}V_{1},\quad W_{1}=V_{1}\ominus_{\perp}V_{2},\>\dots

(A subspace is
near homogeneous
if the
\ord
function is constant on all non-zero elements of the subspace.) Then (as we shall prove later although it is fairly obvious)

(3.13)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

We call this orthogonal decomposition the
near homogeneous decomposition
of
V
.

Any closed subspace has a near homogeneous decomposition, but closed left submodules also have a
near inner
decomposition. In the polydisk case, a closed subspace
W
of
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
is
near inner
if
W\perp z^{m}W
for all multi-indices
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
with
|m|=|m_{1}|+\cdots+|m_{n}|\geq 1
. We can define a
near inner decomposition
of a closed subspace
V
to be an orthogonal decomposition

(3.14)

V=W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}W^{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

of near inner subspaces such that

(3.15)

z^{k}W^{l}\perp W^{m}

whenever
|k|+l>m
. (The actual definition is slightly more complicated – see definition
6.3
.) We have superscripted the subspaces
W^{0},W^{1},W^{2},\ldots
to distinguish them from the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
. However, there is a close connection between near homogeneous decompositions and near inner decompositions, and this connection constitutes the core of our abstract Beurling’s theorem:

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
V
be a closed subspace of
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
. Then
V
is a closed
P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
submodule of
V
if and only if the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
is near inner and has the full projection property.

The full projection property, which we shall not go into until section
7
, is designed to eliminate situations such as

(3.17)

V=\LH\{I\}\oplus_{\perp}\LH\{z^{2}I\}\oplus_{\perp}\LH\{z^{4}I\}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

where
I
is an inner function in
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
. (Here and throughout this paper,
\LH
denotes the linear hull of a set.) In this case, the orthogonal decomposition (
3.17
) is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
, and it is near inner, but it does not have the full projection property. This is a good thing because
V
is not a
P[\mathbb{C},z]
submodule of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
.

In the classical
1
-variable case, our theorem
3.16
says the the closed
P[\mathbb{C},z]
submodules of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
are exactly the closed subspaces of the form (
3.10
), although this takes some proving. In fact, the decomposition (
3.10
) turns out to be the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
. Furthermore, it is near inner and has the full projection property, as we shall show in a second paper. Thus our abstract Beurling’s theorem really is a generalization of the classical
1
-variable Beurling’s theorem in the case where the Hilbert space of values equals
\mathbb{C}
.

4.
Valuation Algebras and Hilbert Modules.

The notion of a valuation on a field will probably be familiar to most readers. An extension of one form of this notion to algebras and Hilbert modules will provide the setting for our abstract Beurling’s theorem. As you may have already guessed from section
3
, the motivating example for a
valuation algebra
and
valuation Hilbert module
is the algebra
P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
and Hilbert module
H^{2}(E,D^{n})
, with the
\ord
function introduced there as the valuation.

There are several notions of a valuation in abstract algebra. The one we shall borrow (and amend slightly) is often called an
exponential valuation

[
12
]
. Perhaps we should call our notion of a valuation an‘
inverse exponential valuation
, but the phrase is way too cumbersome, so we shall simply use the term
analytic valuation
to avoid confusion and distinguish it from the usual notion of a valuation in abstract algebra. For concision, we shall usually drop the word analytic and smply call our notion a
valuation
.

We state here and now that ALL algebras and Hilbert spaces will be
complex
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
R
be a (possibly non-commutative) algebra. An
analytic valuation
on
R
is a function
\ord\colon\!R\mapsto\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mspace{0.5mu}\mathbin{\cup}\mspace{0.5mu}\{\infty\}
such that for all
r
and
s\in R
,

(4.1.1)

\ord(r)=0
if
r
is a left or right unit of
R
.

(4.1.2)

\ord(r)=\infty
if and only if
r=0
.

(4.1.3)

\ord(rs)\geq\ord(r)+\ord(s)
.

(4.1.4)

\ord(\lambda r)=\ord(r)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\lambda\neq 0

(4.1.5)

\ord(r+s)\geq\min(\ord(r),\ord(s))

Of course, condition (
4.1.1
) is satisfied vacuously if
R
does not have a two sided identity element.

Here and throughout, we follow the usual conventions with respect to
\infty
:
m<\infty
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and for such
m
,
m\cdot\infty=\infty\cdot m=\infty
if
m\neq 0
. Further,
\infty\cdot 0=0\cdot\infty=\infty
and
\infty\cdot\infty=\infty
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
valuation algebra
is an ordered pair
(R,\ord)
, where
R
is a (possibly non-commutative) algebra and
\ord
is an analytic valuation on
R
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
(R,\ord_{R})
be a valuation algebra, and let
\mathbb{H}
be a Hilbert space which is a left
R
Hilbert module. A
Hilbert module valuation
on
\mathbb{H}
(with respect to
(R,\ord_{R})
) is a function
\ord\colon\mathbb{H}\mapsto\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mspace{0.5mu}\mathbin{\cup}\mspace{0.5mu}\{\infty\}
such that for all
h
,
h_{1}
and
h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
, and for all
r\in R
,

(4.3.1)

\ord(h)=\infty
if and only if
h=0
.

(4.3.2)

\ord(rh)\geq\ord_{R}(r)+\ord(h)

(4.3.3)

\ord(\lambda h)=\ord(h)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\lambda\neq 0
.

(4.3.4)

\ord(h_{1}+h_{2})\geq\min(\ord(h_{1}),\ord(h_{2}))
.

(4.3.5)

The
\ord
function is upper semi-continuous on
\mathbb{H}
.

Note that each set
\mathbb{H}_{m}
is a subspace by properties (
4.3.3
) and (
4.3.4
). The subspaces
\mathbb{H}_{m}=\{h\in\mathbb{H}:\ord(h)\geq m\}
are closed for
m=0,1,2,\,\dots
because
\ord
is upper semicontinuous. The subspace
\mathbb{H}_{0}
is automatically a closed subspace because
\mathbb{H}_{0}=\mathbb{H}
. Fiinally, as part of the definition of an
R
Hilbert module, the multiplication map
m_{r}\colon h\mapsto rh
is continuous for each

.

We need to review some of the properties of upper semicontinuous functions (
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
): Here is the easiest way to see that the prototype of the

, but that we cannot define

to be a continuous function: Recall that the one of the conditions that
\ord
is upper semicontinuous at

is

\limsup\ord(h_{k})\leq\ord(h)\text{ if }h_{k}\text{ is a sequence in }\mathbb{H}\text{ converging to }h.

Now suppose that
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}
, and recall that for
h\in H^{2}
,
\ord(h)
is the order of the zero of
h
at
0
. The upper semicontinuity condition is clearly satisfied by
\ord
in
H^{2}
.

Now if
h(0)\neq 0
,
\ord(h_{k})
converges to
\ord(h)=0
, so
\ord
is continuous at
h
. But if
h
has a zero at
0
, then
\ord(h)>0
. Choose
h_{k}=h+1/k
and observe that
h_{k}
converges to
h
, but
\ord(h_{k})=0
does not converge to
\ord(h)
. Thus
\ord
is not continuous at
h
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
(R,\ord_{R})
be a valuation algebra. A
valuation Hilbert module
over
(R,\ord_{R})
is an ordered pair
(\mathbb{H},\ord)
, where
\mathbb{H}
is a left Hilbert module over
R
and
\ord
is a Hilbert module valuation on
\mathbb{H}
with respect ot
(R,\ord_{R})
.

Where it will cause no confusion, we shall denote both the algebra valuation on
R
and the Hilbert module valuation on
\mathbb{H}
by the same symbol,
\ord
. We shall also make the gloss, whenever convenient, of denoting the valuation algebra
(R,\ord)
by
R
alone, and the valuation Hilbert module
(\mathbb{H},\ord)
by
\mathbb{H}
alone. Finally, we note that the concepts of a valuation algebra and a valuation Hilbert module apply equally well to
real
algebras and
real
Hilbert modules, but we shall not consider these here. Our focus will be exclusively on
complex
valuation algebras and Hilbert modules.

The inequalities in properties (
4.1.3
) and (
4.3.2
) will doubtless raise the eyebrows of some readers, as will the fact that the algebra
R
might be non-commutative, might have divisors of zero, and might not have an identity element. Normally in abstract algebra, a valuation algebra must be an integral domain (ie. be commutative and have no divisors of zero) with an identity element, and there must be equality rather than inequality in property (
4.1.3
). There are examples of valuation Hilbert modules over valuation algebras of bounded operator valued functions which are non-commutative and have divisors of zero. In these examples, there will sometimes be inequality in properties (
4.1.3
) and (
4.3.2
). However, we shall not consider these in this or our subsequent paper.

In the most common examples of valuation algebras and valuation Hilbert modules, equality does hold. We need a name for this situation:

Definition 4.5
.

An algebra valuation
\ord
on an algebra
R
is
strict
if equality holds in property (
4.1.3
), that is if

(4.5.1)

\ord(rs)=\ord(r)+\ord(s)

for all
r
and
s\in R
. A
strict
valuation algebra is a valuation algebra for which the valuation
\ord_{R}
is strict.

A Hilbert module valuation
\ord
on a left Hilbert module
\mathbb{H}
is
strict
if equality holds in property (
4.3.2
), that is if

(4.5.2)

\ord(rh)=\ord(r)+\ord(h)

for all
r\in R
and
h\in\mathbb{H}
. A
strict
valuation Hilbert module is a valuation Hilbert module for which the valuation
\ord
is strict.

If an algebra
R
or a left Hilbert module over
R
admits a strict valuation, then the algebra must be quite well behaved.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
R
be a strict valuation algebra. Then
R
has no left divisors of zero and no right divisors of zero. If
R
is commutative, then it is an integral domain.

Proof.

Suppose
R
had divisors of zero. Let
r
be a left divisor of zero and
s
be a right divisor of zero, such that
r\neq 0,s\neq 0
, but
rs=0
. Then
\ord(rs)=\infty>\ord(r)+\ord(s)
, which contradicts the strictness of
R
.
∎

Note that the second assertion of the proposition, that
R
is an integral domain, follows immediately from the definition of an integral domain as a commutative algebra with no divisors of zero.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a strict valuation Hilbert module over a valuation algebra
R
. Then
R
is a strict valuation algebra and has no left divisors of zero, and no right divisors of zero. Further, if
R
is commutative, then
R
is an integral domain.

Proof.

That
R
has no left divisors of zero and no right divisors of zero follows immediately from proposition
4.6
once we prove that
R
is strict. To that end, let
r
and
s
be non-zero elements of
R
, and let
h
be a non-zero element of
\mathbb{H}
. Then

(4.8)

\begin{split}\ord((rs)h)&=ord(rs)+ord(h)\\
\ord((rs)h)&=\ord(r(sh))=ord(r)+ord(sh)\\
&=\ord(r)+\ord(s)+\ord(h)\end{split}

Thus
\ord((rs)h)\neq\infty
, and
\ord(h)\text{\ certainly}\neq\infty
, so we can set

ord(rs)+ord(h)=\ord(r)+\ord(s)+\ord(h)

and cancel out
\ord(h)
to obtain
\ord(rs)=\ord(r)+\ord(s)
. Hence
R
is strict.
∎

The subalgebra

will be particularly important in this paper. In fact, the natural form of our abstract Beurling’s theorem will concern

invariant subspaces.

Definition 4.9
.

Let

Let

.

Let

5.
Near Homogeneous Decompositions.

The additional structure provided by the valuation on a valuation Hilbert module allows us to decompose its elements into parts analogous to the decomposition of a power series into a sum of homogeneous polynomials. We shall call these decompositions
near homogeneous decompositions
.

It will be convenient to introduce some more standing notation. Unless otherwise stated,
R
will be a valuation algebra,
\mathbb{H}
will be a valuation Hilbert module over
R
, and
V
will be a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. To avoid trivial issues, unless otherwise stated,
V\neq\{0\}
.
Remember from our standing notation in section
2
and definition
4.4
that if
R
is non-commutative, then
\mathbb{H}
is a
left
(as opposed to a

) Hilbert module over

.

Definition 5.1
.

The valuation subspace series for
V
is the decreasing sequence of closed subspaces

(5.1.1)

V=V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\cdots

where

(5.1.2)

V_{k}=\{h\in V:\ord(h)\geq k\}

The valuation ideal series for
R
is the decreasing sequence of two sided ideals

(5.1.3)

R=R_{0}\supseteq R_{1}\supseteq R_{2}\supseteq\cdots

where

(5.1.4)

R_{k}=\{r\in R:\ord(r)\geq k\}

We have already met the valuation subspace series for
\mathbb{H}
; it is the decereasing sequence of closed subspaces
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{0}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{1}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{2}\supseteq\cdots
following property (
4.3.5
). Note that each of the sets
V_{k}
is a subspace by properties (
4.3.3
) and (
4.3.4
). Because
V_{k}=\mathbb{H}_{k}\mathbin{\cap}V
, each of the sets
V_{k}
in the valuation subspace series (
5.1.1
) for

is a closed subspace of
V
.

That each of the sets
R_{k}
in the valuation ideal series (

4.1.3
), (
4.1.4
), and (
4.1.5
). That

(5.2)

\bigcap R_{k}=\{0\},\quad\bigcap V_{k}=\{0\},\quad\bigcap\mathbb{H}_{k}=\{0\}

all follow from properties (
4.1.2
) and (
4.3.1
) To see this, suppose, that

. Then for each

,

, so

for each

. Thus

, so

by property (

).

The valuation ideal series for
R
is entirely analogous to the valuation subspace series for
V
, except that we can use the Hilbert space structure of

to form orthogonal complements:

Definition 5.3
.

The
near homogeneous decomposition
of
V
is the orthogonal direct sum

(5.3.1)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\,\cdots

where

(5.3.2)

W_{0}=V_{0}\ominus_{\perp}V_{1},\;W_{1}=V_{1}\ominus_{\perp}V_{2},\;W_{2}=V_{2}\ominus_{\perp}V_{3},\,\dots

and
V=V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\,\cdots
is the valuation subspace series for
V
. The orthogonal subspaces
W_{0}
,
W_{1}
,
W_{2},\,\dots
are called the
near homogeneous components
of

.

We need an all too obvious comment here. For algebraists, a direct sum like (
5.3.1
) is the subspace of all

sums of elements from the subspaces
W_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. But for analysts like us, the orthogonal direct sum (
5.3.1
) is the closed subspace of all norm convergent sums, whether
finite
or

, of elements from the subspaces
W_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

Note that the near homogeneous components
W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\,\dots
are closed because they are orthogonal complements of sets in closed subspaces. Further, they are orthogonal by construction. However, that the orthogonal direct sum (
5.3.1
) equals all of
V
requires proof:

Proposition 5.4
.

The orthogonal direct sum

W=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\,\cdots

equals all of
V
.

Proof.

By construction,
W\subseteq V
. We shall show that if
h\in V
and
h\perp W
, then
h=0
. This will prove
W=V
.

So let
h\in V
and
h\perp W
. Then

h\perp W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots\oplus_{\perp}W_{m}

for
m=0,1,2,\cdots
. By construction,

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots\,\oplus_{\perp}W_{m}\oplus_{\perp}V_{m+1}

so
h\in V_{m+1}
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. By the equations (
5.2
),
h=0
.
∎

It will be convenient at this point to introduce some additional standing notation: Unless otherwise stated,
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{0}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{1}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{2}\supseteq\cdots
will denote the valuation subspace series for
\mathbb{H}
, and
\mathbb{H}=H_{0}\oplus_{\perp}H_{1}\oplus_{\perp}H_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots
will denote the near homogeneous decomposition of
\mathbb{H}
.
V=V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\cdots
will denote the valuation subspace series for
V
, and

will denote the near homogeneous decomposition of

.

will denote the valuation ideal series for

,

,

To continue, unless otherwise stated,

(5.5)

\begin{split}P_{k}^{\mathbb{H}}\colon\mathbb{H}&\mapsto H_{k},\quad Q_{k+1}^{\mathbb{H}}\colon\mathbb{H}\mapsto\mathbb{H}_{k+1}\\
P_{k}^{V}\colon V&\mapsto W_{k},\quad Q_{k+1}^{V}\colon V\mapsto V_{k+1}\end{split}

will denote the orthogonal projections onto

,
\mathbb{H}_{k+1}

, and

(5.6)

\begin{split}P_{k}^{\mathbb{H}}+Q_{k+1}^{\mathbb{H}}&=I\text{ on }\mathbb{H}_{k}\\

where

(5.7)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}+Q_{k+1}^{\mathbb{H}}&=I\text{ on }\mathbb{H}\\
\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{j}^{V}+Q_{k+1}^{V}&=I\text{ on }V\end{split}

Now we are equipped to talk about the series of near homogeneous components of individual elements of
\mathbb{H}
and

.

Definition 5.8
.

Let

. The

of

is the convergent series of orthogonal terms

(5.8.1)

h=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(h)\oplus_{\perp}P_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}(h)\oplus_{\perp}P_{2}^{\mathbb{H}}(h)\oplus_{\perp}\,\cdots

The individual terms
P_{k}^{\mathbb{H}}
are called the
near homogeneous components
of

.

If

, the
near homogeneous decomposition
of

with respect
to

is the convergent series of orthogonal terms

(5.8.2)

h=P_{0}^{V}(h)\oplus_{\perp}P_{1}^{V}(h)\oplus_{\perp}P_{2}^{V}(h)\oplus_{\perp}\,\cdots

The individual terms

are called the
near homogeneous components
of

with respect
to
V

We shall postpone the proof that the series (

) and (

) are in fact convergent and converge to

until after we establish their connection to the
\ord
function:

Proposition 5.9
.

For
h\in V
,
\ord(h)
is the index (subscript) of the first non-vanishing term in the near homogeneous decomposition of
h
with respect to
V
if
h\neq 0
, or
\ord(h)=\infty
if all the terms vanish, that is if
h=0
.

Proof.

Suppose
h\in\mathbb{H}
and
h\neq 0
. Then
\ord(h)=m<\infty
, so
h\in V_{m}=W_{m}\oplus_{\perp}V_{m+1}
, and
h\notin V_{m+1}
. If
P_{m}^{V}(h)=0
, then

would belong to

which is not possible. Thus
P_{m}^{V}(h)\neq 0
.

On the other hand,

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots\oplus_{\perp}W_{m-1}\oplus_{\perp}V_{m}

so if one or more of the terms

for

, then

would not belong to

, which is also not possible. Thus
P_{j}^{V}=0
, for

, so the index of the first non-vanishing term in the near homogeneous decomposition of

with respect to

is

.

Finally, all the terms
P_{j}^{V}(h)
vanish if and only if

, which is true if and only if

by the equations (

). Thus all the terms vanish if and only if
\ord(h)=\infty
.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{H}
. Then
\ord(h)
is the index (subscript) of the first non-vanishing term in the near homogeneous decomposition of

if

, or
\ord(h)=\infty

Proof.

Consider the special case where
\mathbb{H}=V
.
∎

.

If

∎

.
∎

We used the phrase “

One way of interpreting proposition

Until now, we have been treating the near homogeneous decomposition of

as a formal series. We are now in a position to prove that it converges to

. At the considerable risk of boring the experts, we do so now:

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
h\in V
. Then the near homogeneous decomposition (

) of

with respect to

converges in norm to

.

Proof.

By equation (

h=\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{j}^{V}(h)\oplus_{\perp}Q_{k+1}^{V}(h)

By orthogonality, the partial sums

\|\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{j}^{k}(h)\|^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|P_{j}^{V}(h)\|^{2}\leq\|h\|^{2}

so the partial sums form a Cauchy sequence in

. Because

is closed, the partial sums converge to some element

. Now because
P_{k}^{V}
is a projection and a bounded linear operator on
\mathbb{H}
, and because
P_{k}^{V}(P_{j}^{V})=0
for

P_{k}^{V}(g)=P_{k}^{V}(\lim_{l\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{l}P_{j}^{V}(h))=(P_{k}^{V})^{2}(h)=P_{k}^{V}(h)

by orthogonality, so

by corollary

.
∎

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{H}
. Then the near homogeneous decomposition (

) of

converges in norm to
h
.

Proof.

Consider the special case where
\mathbb{H}=V
.
∎

We turn now to the study of the near homogeneous components

,

,

,

of

determines the element
h
itself. We shall now make this precise:

Definition 5.15
.

A closed linear subspace
W
of
\mathbb{H}
is

if the
\ord
function is constant on
W\mathbin{\sim}\{0\}
.

Proposition 5.16
.

The near homogeneous components of

are all near homogeneous subspaces. In fact,
\ord(h)=m
for all non-zero

.

Proof.

W_{m}
is a closed subspace because it is an orthogonal complement. That

follows immediately from proposition
5.9
, but here is another short proof: Let
h\in W_{m}
and suppose that
\ord(h)\neq m
. We shall show that
h
must equal
\{0\}
. Since
W_{m}=V_{m}\ominus_{\perp}V_{m+1}
,
h\in V_{m}
, and so
\ord(h)\geq m
. Since
\ord(h)\neq m
,
\ord(h)
must be
>m
, and so
h\in V_{m+1}
. But
W_{m}\perp V_{m+1}
, so

. Thus
h=0
.
∎

It can happen that one or more of the near homogeneous components of

may be
\{0\}
. For example, simply delete a non-zero homogeneous component of a larger subspace to obtain

.

We now turn to the crucial property that projection of an element

onto

determines the element

itself. In fact, this is true for any near homogeneous subspace

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
W
be a near homogeneous subspace of
\mathbb{H}
not equal to
\{0\}
, and let

be the common value of the
\ord

W\mathbin{\sim}\{0\}
. Let

be

L_{W}\colon W\mapsto P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W)\subseteq H_{m}

Then

is an invertible bounded linear transformation.

Proof.

Linearity and boundedness are trivial. In fact,
L_{W}={P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}|}_{W}
and
\|L_{W}\|\leq\|P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}\|=1
. To show that
L_{W}
is invertible, let

and

. Then

, and by corollary

is the first non-vanishing term in the near homogeneous decomposition (

) of

is invertible.

∎

In the exceptional case where

is the

.

An interesting question is, when does

have a

The near homogeneous components of

Proposition 5.18

The non-zero near homogeneous components of

W_{m}=W^{\prime}
.

Our first proof of our abstract Beurling’s theorem used this proposition, but, as it turns out, we can base a simpler proof on the projection lemmas in the next section. Hence we shall not need proposition
5.18
in this paper, and so we leave its proof to the reader.

6.
Near Inner Decompositions and the Projection Lemmas.

In the one variable case of Beurling’s theorem for the closed shift invariant subspaces of
H^{2}(E,D^{1})
, inner functions play a prominent role, and these arise from closed subspaces
W
with the property that
W\perp z^{m}W
for all
m>0
. We might call such subspaces
inner
subspaces.

We can easily extend this idea to closed subspaces of a valuation Hilbert module
\mathbb{H}
over a valuation algebra
R
:

Definition 6.1
.

A closed subspace
W
of
\mathbb{H}
is
near inner
if

(6.1.1)

W\perp R_{1}W

where
R=R_{0}\supseteq R_{1}\supseteq R_{2}\supseteq\cdots
is the valuation ideal series of
R
. An element
h
of
\mathbb{H}
is
near inner
if the
1
-dimensional subspace generated by it is near inner.

In the
1
-dimensional scalar valued case, a near inner function in
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
is simply a scalar multiple of an inner function, hence the name “near inner” in the abstract case.

Our abstract version of Beurling’s theorem will involve a sequence of near inner subspaces. The simple motivating example is

Example 6.2
.

Let
V
be the closed
P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},z_{2}]
submodule of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{2})
generated by the coordinate functions
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
:

(6.2.1)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

where, as usual, the orthogonal direct sum is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
:
W_{0}=\{0\}
,
W_{1}=\LH\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
,
W_{2}=\LH\{z_{1}^{2},z_{1}z_{2},z_{2}^{2}\}
, etc. Here, again as usual,
\LH
denotes the linear hull.

Thus we are led to

Definition 6.3
.

Let
V
be a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. A
near inner
decomposition of
V
is an orthogonal direct sum of subspaces

(6.3.1)

V=W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}W^{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

such that if
k
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
h\in W^{k}
,
g\in V
,
r\in R_{1}
, and
\ord(rh+g)>m
, then

(6.3.2)

rh+g\perp W^{m}

A few comments are in order. First, we have denoted the subspaces
W^{0}
,
W^{1}
,
W^{2},

\dots
using superscripts rather than subscripts because they are not necessarily equal to the near homogeneous components
W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\,\dots
of
V
. Second, each of the subspaces
W^{m}
in a near inner decomposition of
V
is closed because the decomposition is an orthogonal direct sum, and orthogonal complements in closed subspaces are closed. (We could equally easily have dropped the condition that
V
be closed, but instead insisted that each subspace
W^{m}
be closed. In other words, a subspace
V
given by an orthogonal direct sum of closed subspaces is closed.) Third, if
\ord(h)\geq m
, we may choose
g=0
to make
\ord(rh+g)>m
. Thus
rh\perp W^{m}
by equation (
6.3.2
). In particular,
R_{1}W^{m}\perp W^{m}
, so each of the closed subspaces
W^{m}
is itself near inner. For future reference, we shall state this last observation as a proposition:

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}W^{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdot
be a near inner decomposition of
V
. Then each of the subspaces
W^{m}
is closed and near inner, and if
h\in W^{k}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
r\in R_{1}
, and
\ord(rh)>m
, then
rh\perp W^{m}
.

Proof.

See the remarks above.
∎

It can be hard to verify in practice that a given orthogonal decomposition of
V
is near inner, but it is often easier to verify that the decomposition satisfies the conclusion of proposition
6.4
. Thus decompositions satisfying this conclusion deserve a name:

Definition 6.5
.

Let
V
be a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. A
weakly near inner
decomposition of
V
is an orthogonal direct sum of subspaces

(6.5.1)

V=W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}W^{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

such that if
k
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
h\in W^{k}
,
r\in R_{1}
, and
\ord(rh)>m
, then

(6.5.2)

rh\perp W^{m}

Note that a weakly near inner decomposition is simply one which satisfies the
g=0
case of the definition of a near inner decomposition. Note also that the subspaces in a weakly near inner decomposition are closed (by the remarks above) and are themselves near inner because the definition of a near inner subspace does not involve the auxiliary function
g
.

An interesting question is, when is a weakly near inner decomposition near inner? In particular, is this always true for near homogeneous decompositions which are weakly near inner?

Near inner decompositions are closely related to
R_{1}
invariant subspaces. Recall from definition
4.9
that
V
is
R_{1}
invariant if
R_{1}V\subseteq V
. Recall also, from the standing notation introduced in section
5
, that
V
is always a
closed
subspace of
\mathbb{H}
, that
V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots
is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
, and
V=V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\cdots
is the valuation subspace series for
V
.

The reader can quickly verify that the near homogeneous decomposition in our simple motivating example
6.2
is, in fact, a near inner decomposition. This immediately leads us to the following general proposition:

Proposition 6.6
.

If
V
is
R_{1}
invariant, then the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
is near inner.

Proof.

Suppose
V
is
R_{1}
invariant. Let
h\in W_{k}
,
g\in V
, and
r\in R_{1}
. Then
rh\in V
, so
rh+g\in V
. If
\ord(rh+g)>m
, then
rh+g\in V_{m+1}
, so
rh+g\perp W_{m}
.
∎

The reader might ask, where did we use the hypothesis that
V
was closed in the above? The answer is that only closed subspaces are guaranteed to have near homogeneous decompositions.

It would be strikingly convenient if the near inner property were also a sufficient condition for
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant, but the simplest examples show us this is not so:

Example 6.7
.

Let
R=P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},z_{2}]
, and let
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{2})
, scalar valued
H^{2}
of the polydisk
D^{2}
. Let the
\ord
function on
R
and
\mathbb{H}
be the usual one, the degree of the first non-vanishing term in the power series expansion for
f
. Let

(6.7.1)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

where

\begin{split}W_{0}=\{0\},\;W_{1}&=\LH\{z_{1},z_{2}\},\;W_{2}=\LH\{z_{1}^{2},z_{2}^{2}\},\\
W_{3}&=\LH\{z_{1}^{3},z_{1}^{2}z_{2},z_{1}z_{2}^{2},z_{2}^{3}\},\;\dots\end{split}

and, as usual,
\LH
denotes the linear hull. Then
V
is closed, the decomposition (
6.7.1
) is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
, and it is near inner. However,
V
is
not

R_{1}
invariant because
W_{2}
lacks the needed element
z_{1}z_{2}
. And, in fact, the closed
R_{1}
invariant subspace generated by
V
is the closed
P[\mathbb{C},z_{1},z_{2}]
submodule generated by
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
.

We need to add an additional property, which we call the
full projection property
, to obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for a closed subspace
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant. We shall discuss the full projection property in the next section.

To return to the topic of near inner decompositions, the next two
projection lemmas
will provide most of the remaining part of the proof of our abstract Beurling’s theorem in section
8
. To set the stage, let

(6.8)

V=W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}W^{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

be a near inner decomposition of
V
. As before, because the decomposition (
6.8
) is an arbitrary near inner decomposition, and does not necessarily coincide with the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
, we use superscripts rather than subscripts throughout.

The
first projection lemma
is:

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
r\in R_{1}
and
h\in W^{k}
. Suppose
f^{m}=g^{0}+g^{1}+\cdots g^{m}
, where
g^{0}\in W^{0}
,
g^{1}\in W^{1}
,
\dots

g^{m}\in W^{m}
. Suppose that
\ord(rh-f^{m})>m
. Then the projection of
rh
on the subspace
W=W^{0}\oplus_{\perp}W^{1}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots\oplus_{\perp}W^{m}
is
f^{m}
. Furthermore,

(6.9.1)

\|f^{m}\|^{2}=\|g^{0}\|^{2}+\|g^{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|g^{m}\|^{2}\leq\|rh\|^{2}

Proof.

For any element
w\in W
,
w=w^{0}+w^{1}+\cdots+w^{m}
, where
w^{0}\in W^{0}
,
w^{1}\in W^{1}
,
\dots
,
w^{m}\in W^{m}
. By the definition of a near inner decomposition, that is definition
6.3
,
rh-f^{m}\perp W^{j}
for
j=0,1,\dots,m
. Thus

\langle rh-f^{m},w\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\langle rh-f^{m},w^{j}\rangle=0

so
rh-f^{m}\perp W
. Hence
f^{m}
is the projection of
rh
on
W
.

As for the norm of
f^{m}
,

\|f^{m}\|^{2}=\|g^{0}\|^{2}+\|g^{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|g^{m}\|^{2}

because the near inner decomposition (
6.8
) is orthogonal. Furthermore,

\|rh\|^{2}=\|rh-f^{m}\|^{2}+\|f^{m}\|^{2}

because
rh-f^{m}\perp f^{m}
. Thus inequality (
6.9.1
) holds.
∎

The
second projection lemma
is:

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
r\in R_{1}
, and
h\in W_{k}
. Consider the formal series

(6.10.1)

g^{0}+g^{1}+g^{2}+\cdots

where
g^{0}\in W^{0}
,
g^{1}\in W^{1}
,
g^{2}\in W^{2}
,
\cdots
, and let
f^{m}=g^{0}+g^{1}+\cdots+g^{m}
. Suppose that
\ord(rh-f^{m})>m
for
m=0,1,2,\dots
. Then the formal series (
6.10.1
) converges in norm to an element
f
in
V
, and
rh=f
.

Proof.

We shall consider the formal series of positive terms

(6.11)

\|g^{0}\|^{2}+\|g^{1}\|^{2}+\|g^{2}\|^{2}+\cdots

By the first projection lemma, the partial sums are

(6.12)

\|f^{m}\|^{2}=\|g^{0}\|^{2}+\|g^{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|g^{m}\|^{2}\leq\|rh\|^{2}

Thus the sequence of partial sums
\|f^{m}\|^{2}
,
m=0,1,2,\cdots
is Cauchy. Furthermore, if
l<m
,
\|f^{m}-f^{l}\|^{2}=\|g^{l+1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|g^{m}\|^{2}
, so the sequence of partial sums of the formal series (
6.10.1
) is Cauchy in norm. Because each
f_{m}\in V
and
V
is complete, the formal series (
6.10.1
) converges to an element
f\in V
.

Now let
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. For all
m>l
,
\ord(rh-f^{m})>l
, so
rh-f^{m}\in\mathbb{H}_{l}
. By the comments following definition (
4.3
),
\mathbb{H}_{l}
is closed, so
rh-f\in\mathbb{H}_{l}
Thus

rh-f\in\bigcap_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{l}=\{0\}

where the right hand equality follows from the equations (
5.2
). Thus
f=rh
.
∎

Note that since
r\in R_{1}
,
\ord(rh)>0
. We have included
g^{0}
in the above even though
g^{0}
must equal
0
in order for
\ord(rh-f^{m})
to be
>m
. In fact,
g^{0}
through
g^{k}
must all be equal to
0
for the same reason.

7.
The Full Projection Property.

In example
6.7
, we saw that for a closed subspace
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant, it was not sufficient for the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
to be near inner because some of the components
W_{k}
might not have enough elements. The
full projection property
, which we shall define below, guarantees that this can’t happen.

Recall from our standing notation introduced in section
5
that
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{0}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{1}\supseteq\mathbb{H}_{2}\supseteq\cdots
is the valuation subspace series for
\mathbb{H}
,
\mathbb{H}=H_{0}\oplus_{\perp}H_{1}\oplus_{\perp}H_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots
is the near homogeneous decomposition of
\mathbb{H}
, and
P_{k}^{\mathbb{H}}\colon\mathbb{H}\mapsto H_{k}
is orthogonal projection onto
H_{k}
. Also, because we shall be speaking of near homogeneous decompositions, we shall return to using subscripts rather than superscripts.

Definition 7.1
.

The near homogeneous decomposition of
V
has the full projection property if for each
k
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(7.1.1)

P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh+g)\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m}),

whenever
r\in R_{1}
,
h\in W_{k}
,
g\in V
, and
\ord(rh+g)\geq m
.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
V
is
R_{1}
invariant, then the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
has the full projection property.

Proof.

Suppose
V
is
R_{1}
invariant. Let
k
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
r\in R_{1}
,
h\in W_{k}
, and
g\in V
. For simplicity, denote
rh+g
by
f
, and suppose
\ord(f)\geq m
.

Because
V
is
R_{1}
invariant,
rh\in V
so
f=rh+g\in V
. Now there are two cases: If
ord(f)>m
, then
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(f)=0
because
f\in\mathbb{H}_{m+1}
and
H_{m}\perp\mathbb{H}_{m+1}
. Thus
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m})
. On the other hand, if
\ord(f)=m
, then we can write
f=f_{m}+F_{m+1}
, where
f_{m}\in W_{m}
and
F_{m+1}\in V_{m+1}\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{m+1}
. Since
F_{m+1}\in\mathbb{H}_{m+1}
and
H_{m}\perp\mathbb{H}_{m+1}
,
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(F_{m+1})=0
. Thus
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(f)=P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(f_{m})\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m})
as required.
∎

When checking whether the near homogenous decomposition of
V
has the full projection property, certain simplifications can be made. First, we never used the fact that
f=rh+g\in V
in the
\ord(f)>m
case. Thus
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rf+g)\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m})
always, whenever
\ord(rh+g)>m
, even if
V
is not
R_{1}
invariant. Second,
P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh+g)\in P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{0})
always, even if
V
is not
R_{1}
invariant, because
r\in R_{1}
, and so
\ord(rh)>0
. Thus
P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh+g)=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g)=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{0})\in P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{0})
. Here we have written
g
as
g_{0}+G_{1}
, where
g_{0}\in W_{0}
and
G_{1}\in V_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{1}
. Thus
P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g)=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{0})+P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(G_{1})=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{0})+0=P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{0})
.

We can summarize these remarks in a corollary:

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
V
be an arbitrary closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
, possibly not
R_{1}
invariant. Let
k
,
m
,
h
,
r
, and
g
be as in the proof of the proposition. Then
P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh+g)
always
\in P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{0})
, and
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh+g)\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m})
whenever
\ord(rh+g)>m
.

Proof.

See the remarks above.
∎

As in the case of the near inner property, it would be strikingly convenient if the full projection property were also a sufficient condition for
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant. However, the following simple one-variable example shows that this is not so:

Example 7.4
.

Let
R=P[\mathbb{C},z]
, and let
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
, scalar valued
H^{2}
of the unit disk
D^{1}
. Let
a\in D^{1}
with
a\neq 0
, and consider the simplest possible non-constant inner function not vanishing at
0
, the simple Blaschke factor

(7.4.1)

B(z)=\frac{a-z}{1-\bar{a}z}

Let

(7.4.2)

V=\{a_{0}+a_{1}z+a_{2}z^{2}B(z)+a_{3}z^{3}B(z)+\cdots\>:\>\sum|a_{j}|^{2}<\infty\}

Then
V
is a closed subspace of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
, the one-dimensional subspaces

(7.4.3)

\begin{split}W_{0}=\LH\{1\},W_{1}&=\LH\{z\},\\
W_{2}&=\LH\{z^{2}B(z)\},W_{3}=\LH\{z^{2}B(z)\},\dots\end{split}

are closed, near homogeneous, and mutually orthogonal (because
B
is inner), and

(7.4.4)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}W_{3}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
. Clearly,
V
has the full projection property. However,
V
is not
R_{1}
invariant (ie. shift invariant). In fact, the closed shift invariant subspace generated by
V
is all of
H^{2}(\mathbb{C},D^{1})
.

Variations on this example, except for the part about (
7.4.4
) being the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
, are standard in the one-variable theory of
H^{2}
spaces.

So the full projection property is not sufficient for
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant. As our abstract Beurling’s theorem, theorem
8.1
below, shows, we must add the property that the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
is near inner to obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for
V
to be
R_{1}
invariant.

However, we do have a partial converse to proposition
7.2
:

Proposition 7.5
.

Suppose the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
has the full projection property. Suppose
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
r\in R_{1}
,
h\in W_{k}
, and
V^{\prime}=V+\LH\{rh\}
. As in definition
5.3
, let

(7.5.1)

V^{\prime}=W_{0}^{\prime}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}^{\prime}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}^{\prime}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

be the near homogeneous decomposition of
V^{\prime}
. Suppose
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
W_{m}\subseteq W_{m}^{\prime}
. Then
W_{m}=W_{m}^{\prime}
.

We can summarize the conclusion of proposition
7.5
as “containment implies equality.”

Our first proof of our abstract Beurling’s theorem used this proposition, but we shall not need it here because we now base our proof on the projection lemmas of section
6
. Thus we shall leave the proof of proposition
7.5
to the reader.

8.
The Abstract Beurling’s Theorem.

Now we can at last state and prove our abstract Beurling’s theorem. It fully characterizes the closed
R_{1}
invariant subspaces of valuation Hilbert modules. For clarity, we shall repeat some of our standing notation in the statement of the theorem and its proof:

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
V
be a closed subspace of a valuation Hilbert module over a valuation algebra
R
. Then
V
is
R_{1}
invariant if and only if the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
is near inner and has the full projection property.

Proof.

Necessity follows from proposition
6.6
, which states that the near homogeneous decomposition of a closed
R_{1}
invariant subspace is near inner, and proposition
7.2
, which states that a closed
R_{1}
invariant subspace has the full projection property.

As for sufficiency, suppose the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
is near inner and has the full projection property. Let
r\in R_{1}
and
h\in W_{k}
, where

(8.2)

V=W_{0}\oplus_{\perp}W_{1}\oplus_{\perp}W_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

is the near homogeneous decomposition of
V
. We shall show that
rh\in V
. First, we shall use the full projection property (definition
7.1
) to show that there are elements

(8.3)

g_{0}\in W_{0},\>g_{1}\in W_{1},\>g_{2}\in W_{2},\>\dots

such that for
m=0,1,2,\dots
,

\ord(rh-f_{m})>m

Here, we have denoted the sum
g_{0}+\cdots g_{m}
by
f_{m}
. Then we shall use the second projection lemma (
6.10
) to conclude that the formal sum

(8.4)

g_{0}+g_{1}+g_{2}+\cdots

converges to an element
f\in V
, and that
rh=f
. This will prove that
rh\in V
.

First, let us construct the sequence (
8.3
) by induction. Because
r\in R_{1}
,
\ord(rh)>0
. Thus
P_{0}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh)=0
, so we can (and must) choose
g_{0}=0
. Now suppose
g_{0},\dots,g_{m-1}
have been constructed inductively, so that
g_{j}\in W_{j}
and
\ord(rh-f_{j})>j
for
j=0,\dots,m-1
. We shall use the full projection property of the near homogeneous decomposition (
8.2
) of
V
to construct
g_{m}
. Note that
\ord(rh-f_{m-1})\geq m
. By the full projection property,
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m-1})\in P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(W_{m})
. If
W_{m}\neq\{0\}
, we may use proposition
5.17
to conclude that there is a unique element
g_{m}\in W_{m}
such that
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{m})=P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m-1})
. In the exceptional case where
W_{m}=\{0\}
, we can (and must) choose
g_{m}=0
. (See the remark following proposition
5.17
.) Let
f_{m}=f_{m-1}+g_{m}
. Then
f_{m}=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{m}
, and
g_{m}\in W_{m}
by construction.

But we still must prove that
\ord(rh-f_{m})>m
. To that end, recall from corollary
5.10
that
\ord(rh-f_{m})
is the index of the first non-vanishing term in the near homogeneous decomposition of
rh-f_{m}
, or
\infty
if
rh-f_{m}=0
. Since
\ord(rh-f_{m-1})>m-1
, all the terms in the near homogeneous decomposition of
rh-f_{m-1}
vanish up to and including the index
m-1
, that is
P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m-1})=0
for
j=0,\dots,m-1
. Also, because
g_{m}\in W_{m}
,
\ord(g_{m})=m
(or
\infty
if
g_{m}=0
). Thus
P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{m})=0
as well for
j=0,\dots m-1
. Furthermore,
P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m-1})=P_{m}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{m})
by construction. Thus

P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m})=P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}(rh-f_{m-1})-P_{j}^{\mathbb{H}}(g_{m})=0

for
j=0,\dots,m
, and so
\ord(rh-f_{m})>m
.

This concludes the first part of the proof that
rh\in V
. The second part is immediate. Because the near homogeneous decomposition (
8.2
) of
V
is near inner, we may apply the second projection lemma. By the second projection lemma, the formal series
8.4
converges in norm to an element
f\in V
, and
rh=f
. Thus
rh\in V
.

Now let
h\in V
, not necessarily an element of
W_{k}
. Let
r\in R_{1}
as before, and let

(8.5)

h=h_{0}\oplus_{\perp}h_{1}\oplus_{\perp}h_{2}\oplus_{\perp}\cdots

be the near homogeneous decomposition of
h
with respect to
V
. By definition
5.8
, each
h_{k}\in W_{k}
, and by proposition
5.13
, the series (
8.5
) converges in norm to
h
. By the definition of a Hilbert module, the linear operators
m_{r}\colon f\to rf
,
f\in\mathbb{H}
are bounded in operator norm, so the series

rh=rh_{0}+rh_{1}+rh_{2}+\cdots

converges in norm to
rh
. Further, each term
rh_{k}\in V
, so, since
V
is closed,
rh\in V
as required. Thus
V
is
R_{1}
invariant.
∎

The last part of this proof is useful enough to isolate as a separate corollary:

Corollary 8.6
.

A closed subspace
V
is
R_{1}
invariant if and only if, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
r\in R_{1}
,
rW_{k}\subseteq V
.

Proof.

See the above.
∎

A proper generalization of Beurling’s theorem should characterize, not just closed

invariant subspaces of

4.10

(8.7)

R=\{\lambda 1_{R}:\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}+R_{1}

From proposition

Corollary 8.8
.

Let

be a valuation algebra satisfying either of the following conditions:

(8.8.1)

R=R_{1}
, or

(8.8.2)

Then a closed subspace

Proof.

.
∎

We remind the reader that in the iconic case where

9.
Example –
H^{2}
Spaces.

The closed invariant subspaces of many familiar Hilbert spaces of analytic functions are completely described by our Abstract Beurling’s Theorem, Theorem
8.1
and its corollaries. In this section, we shall describe the closed invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(E,D^{n})
, where, as usual,
E
is a complex Hilbert space and
D^{n}
is the
n
-dimensional polydisk. Again, as usual,
R
will be the familiar algebra of complex valued polynomials in the variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
. And both
R
and
\mathbb{H}
will have the usual ord function,
\ord(f)=
the multiplicity of the zero of f at
0
.

Example 9.1
.

Theorem
8.1
and its corollaries completely characterize the closed invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(E,D^{n}
).

Proof.

All we have to show is that the
\ord
function on
\mathbb{H}
is upper semi-continuous. So let
f_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,\dots
be a norm convergent sequence in
\mathbb{H}
converging to
f
. For what follows, we need to briefly review one more thing about multi-index notation: Recall that
|m|=|m_{1}|+\cdots|m_{n}|
.

Now by definition
3.5
, the lowest degree terms in the power series expansion of
f
have degree
\ord(f)
. In multi-index notation, let
a_{m}z^{m}
be one of these terms (so
\ord(f)=|m|
), and let
a_{j,m}
be the corresponding term in the power series expansion of
f_{j}
. Then

a_{m}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{\imath\theta})e^{-\imath m\theta}d\theta/(2\pi^{n})

in multi-index notation, with a corresponding formula for
a_{j,m}
with
f
replaced by
f_{j}
. Here, the integral is repeated
n
times,
\theta=\theta_{1}\cdots\theta_{n}
, and
d\theta=d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}
.

The reader will note that
a_{m}
is simply the
m^{\text{th}}
Fourier coefficient of
f
. Since the sequence
f_{j}
converges to
f
in
H^{2}
norm (and
e^{-\imath m\theta}
is bounded)
a_{j,m}
converges to
a_{m}
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, for
j\geq\text{ some }J
,
|a_{j,m}|\geq|a_{m}|/2\neq 0
. Hence, for each
j\geq J
,
f_{j}
has terms in its power series expansion of degree
|m|=\ord(f)
, so for
j\geq J
,
\ord(f_{j})\leq\ord(f)
. Consequently,
\limsup\ord(f_{j})\leq\ord(f)
, so the
\ord
function is upper semi-continuous on
\mathbb{H}
.
∎
