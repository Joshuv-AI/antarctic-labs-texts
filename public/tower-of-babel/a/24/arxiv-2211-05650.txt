On power sum kernels on symmetric groups
Iskander Azangulov, Viacheslav Borovitskiy, Andrei Smolensky

Abstract
In this note, we introduce a family of “power sum” kernels and the corresponding Gaussian processes on symmetric groups S n \Sym_{n} . Such processes are bi-invariant: the action of S n \Sym_{n} on itself from both sides does not change their finite-dimensional distributions. We show that the values of power sum kernels can be efficiently calculated, and we also propose a method enabling approximate sampling of the corresponding Gaussian processes with polynomial computational complexity. By doing this we provide the tools that are required to use the introduced family of kernels and the respective processes for statistical modeling and machine learning.

Introduction

Gaussian processes on various non-Euclidean domains are of great interest both from the point of view of pure mathematics
[
18
,
12
,
20
,
4
,
3
]
, and in terms of various applications where they can be used as statistical models
[
13
,
11
,
6
]
.

Important are the cases when Gaussian processes and their kernels respect the geometric structure of their domain
X
.
Usually this means
stationarity
in a generalized sense, that is, the invariance of all finite-dimensional distributions to the symmetry group of the space
X
.
For example, if
X
is a Riemannian manifold, with respect to the isometry group, or, if
X
is a group, with respect to the action of
X
on itself.
In this note, we will be interested in the latter case: we will consider symmetric groups
X=\Sym_{n}
and
bi-invariant
Gaussian processes with their respective kernels, i.e. invariant under the action of
\Sym_{n}
on itself by left and right translations
[
20
]
.
This, under the assumption that the Gaussian process is centered, is expressed by the relation

k(\sigma g,\sigma h)=k(g\sigma,h\sigma)=k(g,h)

(1)

for its kernel
k:\Sym_{n}\times\Sym_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and elements
g,h,\sigma\in\Sym_{n}
.

We define the family of
power sum
kernels generating bi-invariant Gaussian processes on symmetric groups
\Sym_{n}
.
As far as the authors’ knowledge goes, these were not considered before in the literature.
These kernels are defined in terms of the cycle type of the quotient of two permutations, and due to classical relations in representation theory are automatically non-negative definite.
Moreover, it turns out that these kernels can be computed efficiently and it is possible to sample the corresponding Gaussian processes efficiently, which makes these objectives attractive for statistical modeling of functions on
\Sym_{n}
.

For comparison, the typical covariances considered in the literature are defined as decreasing functions of some distance
[
2
]
, with different possible definitions of distance (see
[
7
, Chapter 6B]
) yielding different covariances functions.
In all these cases, it is required to separately prove their non-negative definiteness.
The covariances we define are not, in general, functions of distance in any standard sense, but they remain monotonic along shortest paths in the Cayley graph of the symmetric group.

2
Power sum kernels

We call a finite sequence of natural numbers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{\ell}
and with
\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{\ell}=n
a
partition
of
n
of
height

\ell
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
p_{d}:\mathC^{m}\to\mathC
denote the Newton’s power sum, that is, a symmetric polynomial of the form

p_{d}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})=z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\ldots+z_{m}^{d}.

(2)

For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
define
p_{\lambda}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{\lambda_{j}}
.
Finally, for a permutation
g\in\Sym_{n}
define
\mu(g)=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{\ell})
, where
\ell
is the total number of cycles in permutation
g
, and
\mu_{j}
is the length of the
j
-th largest cycle.

We will define a new family of stationary kernels on
\Sym_{n}
, which we will call
power sum kernels
in what follows.

For a vector
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
z_{j}\geq 0
, set

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=p_{\mu(gh^{-1})}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(3)

Theorem 1
.

Function
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}:\Sym_{n}\times\Sym_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is positive definite and bi-invariant, i.e.
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\sigma g,\sigma h)=k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g\sigma,h\sigma)=k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)
for all
\sigma\in\Sym_{n}
.
Moreover, if the permutation
gh^{-1}
has cycle type
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
, i.e. the permutation
gh^{-1}
has
c_{j}
cycles of length
j
with
j=1,\ldots,n
, then

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(z)^{c_{j}}.

(4)

The proof of
Theorem

1
will be given in
Section

2.2
.
For now we will focus on simple properties and examples of kernels from this family.

2.1
Properties and examples of power sum kernels

First, note that
Equation

4
does obviously make it possible to compute the values of power kernels
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)
efficiently.

Since
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)
, as a function of
z
, is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
, we have
k_{r\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=r^{n}k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)
.
In case
\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}=1
one gets that
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,g)=(\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j})^{n}=1
, which implies that the variance
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,g)
equals
|\boldsymbol{z}|_{1}^{n}
.

In what follows, without loss of generality, we assume that
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,g)\equiv 1
.
With this normalization, it can be seen that the constructed covariance multiplicatively penalizes each cycle of length
j
, multiplying the result by
z_{1}^{j}+\ldots+z_{m}^{j}
.

Consider the Cayley graph of the group
\Sym_{n}
with respect to the set of all transpositions (permutations that swap only two elements).
This is a graph
G=(V,E)
whose vertex set
V
coincides with
\Sym_{n}
, and the set of edges
E
consists of those pairs
(g,h)
for which the permutation
gh^{-1}
is a transposition.

The distance between permutations
g
and
h
in this graph (commonly called the Cayley distance) can be computed as

d_{C}(g,h)=n-|\operatorname{cycles}(gh^{-1})|,

(5)

where
\operatorname{cycles}(g)
denotes the set of cycles of
g
, including cycles of length
1
.
Note that
|\operatorname{cycles}(gh^{-1})|=\ell
, where
\ell
is the total number of cycles of
gh^{-1}
from the definition of
\mu(gh^{-1})
.

Let us introduce the following order relation on
\Sym_{n}
: we say that
h\prec g
if there is a shortest path in the Cayley graph from vertex
e
(the identity permutation) to vertex
g
passing through vertex
h
.

Proposition 1
.

If
h\prec g
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
m>1
then
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(h,e)\geq k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,e)
.
If
z_{j}>0
for at least two distinct indices
j
, then
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(h,e)>k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,e)
.

Proof.

For each vertex of the Cayley graph along the shortest path from
g
to
e
, the number of cycles increases by
1
by formula (
5
).
Hence, it suffices to prove that
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,e)\leq k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g\sigma,e)
, where
\sigma
is a permutation that increases the number of cycles by
1
.
Let the permutation split some cycle of length
a
into two cycles of lengths
b
and
c
(so that
a=b+c
). Then

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,e)=k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g\sigma,e)\frac{(z_{1}^{a}+z_{2}^{a}+\ldots+z_{m}^{a})}{(z_{1}^{b}+z_{2}^{b}+\ldots+z_{m}^{b})(z_{1}^{c}+z_{2}^{c}+\ldots+z_{m}^{c})}\leq k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g\sigma,e),

(6)

since expanding the product in the denominator produces the sum of the numerator and terms of the form
z_{j}^{b}z_{l}^{c}\geq 0
.
If there are distinct indices
j,l
for which
z_{j}>0
and
z_{l}>0
, then
z_{j}^{b}z_{l}^{c}>0
and the strict inequality holds.
∎

Substituting different values of
z
, we can see that the family of power kernels includes the following Gaussian processes as “extreme” cases.

•

For
\boldsymbol{z}=(1)\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
one gets

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}1^{c_{j}}\equiv 1.

(7)

Therefore, if
f
is a centered Gaussian process with covariance
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, then
f(g)=f(e)
,
g\in\Sym_{n}
, where
f(e)\sim\operatorname{N}(0,1)
.

•

For
\boldsymbol{z}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
one gets

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{m}1^{\mu_{j}(gh^{-1})}\Big)=m^{\ell}

(8)

where, by the definition of
\mu
, the number
\ell
is the total number of cycles of the permutation
gh^{-1}
.
In particular, in this case, the covariance is a function of the Cayley distance.
Note that the frequently used
Cayley distance kernels
, given by
\exp(-\beta\cdot d_{C}(g,h))
, are positive semidefinite under the sufficient condition
\exp(\beta)\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\cup[n,\infty)

[
5
]
, and
Equation

8
corresponds precisely to the Cayley distance kernel with
\log
-integer “inverse temperature”
\beta
.

•

For
\boldsymbol{z}=(1/m,\ldots,1/m)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, using homogeneity, it is easy to see that for any permutation other than the identity the following inequality holds:

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,e)=m^{k-n}\leq m^{-1}.

(9)

This means that for
m\to\infty
the covariance
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}
converges to delta function, and the process itself converges to the Gaussian white noise on
\Sym_{n}
.

Figure

.

2.2
Proof of the theorem
1

Yaglom’s theory
[
20
]
implies that bi-invariant kernels on compact groups can be described in terms of representation theory.
In particular, any bi-invariant kernel
k:G\times G\to\mathbb{R}
on a compact group
G
, whose identity element we denote by
e_{G}
, has the form

k(g,h)=k(gh^{-1},e_{G})=k(e_{G},g^{-1}h)=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}\chi_{\lambda}(gh^{-1}),

(10)

where
a_{\lambda}\geq 0
and
\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}<\infty
, the summation is over all irreducible unitary representations
\pi_{\lambda}
of the group
G
,
\chi_{\lambda}\colon G\to\mathC
denotes the character of the representation
\pi_{\lambda}
, and
d_{\lambda}
denotes its dimension.
Moreover, any function of the form (
10
) is bi-invariant and positive semidefinite (thus a well-defined kernel).

The group
\Sym_{n}
is finite, and therefore compact.
The number of its irreducible unitary representations is finite.
It is known that irreducible representations of the group
\Sym_{n}
can be indexed by Young diagrams of size
n
.
A Young diagram is the representation of a partition
\lambda
in form of the table with rows of size
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}
.
In this case, the number
\ell
is called the length of the Young diagram, and
n=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}
is called its weight.
It known that the characters
\chi_{\lambda}
in the case of
\Sym_{n}
are real-valued and even integer
[
16
, Section 13.1, Corollary 1]
.

Young diagrams are also closely related to the unitary group
\operatorname{U}(m)
: diagrams of length at most
m
enumerate the irreducible representations of
\operatorname{U}(m)
.
Moreover, there is a deep connection between the representations of
\Sym_{n}
and
\operatorname{U}(m)
, expressed through the Schur–Weyl duality.
The character
s_{\lambda}
of the irreducible representation of the group
\operatorname{U}(m)
corresponding to the Young diagram
\lambda
can be expressed as the Schur polynomial in the eigenvalues
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
of the unitary matrix
\mathbf{Z}
.
Using Weyl’s formula
[
17
, §7.15]
it can be expressed by:

s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{Z})=s_{\lambda}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})=\frac{\det\left(z_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+m-j}\right)_{i,j=1}^{m}}{\det\left(z_{i}^{m-j}\right)_{i,j=1}^{m}},

(11)

where for
j
greater than the length of
\lambda
it is assumed that
\lambda_{j}=0
, and the denominator divides the numerator, implying that
s_{\lambda}
is indeed a polynomial in the variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
.
Note that representations of the group
\operatorname{U}(m)
can be extended to representations of the ambient general linear group
\operatorname{GL}(m,\mathC)
, whose character is also given by
Equation

11
.

There is another representation for
s_{\lambda}
, given in terms of semistandard Young tableaux, which we will now define.
Let
\lambda
be a partition.
A semistandard tableau of shape
\lambda
is a Young diagram corresponding to the partition
\lambda
, filled with natural numbers in such a way that values do not decrease in rows and increase in columns.
Polynomials
s_{\lambda}
can be described as follows
[
17
, §7.10]
:

s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{T}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}=\sum_{T}\prod_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{t_{j}},

(12)

where the summation is over all semistandard tables
T
of shape
\lambda
filled with numbers from
1
to
m
, and
t_{j}
denotes the number of cells in the table
T
containing the number
j
.

The set of polynomials
s_{\lambda}
, similarly to the set of polynomials
p_{\lambda}
, forms a basis of the space of symmetric polynomials, while the characters
\chi_{\lambda}
of the group
\Sym_{n}
define the elements of the basis change matrix.
More formally, the following holds.

Theorem

(Frobenius Formula,
[
10
, §4.1]
)
.

p_{\mu(g)}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m})=\sum\chi_{\lambda}(g)s_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m}),

(13)

where the sum is over all Young diagrams of weight
n
and length at most
m
, that is, partitions
\lambda
of
n
into at most
m
terms.

We are now ready to prove the theorem
1
.

Using (
13
), we write

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=p_{\mu(gh^{-1})}(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum\chi_{\lambda}(gh^{-1})s_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m}).

(14)

Since
z_{j}\geq 0
for
j=1,\ldots,n
by assumption, we see from the equality (
12
) that
s_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\geq 0
.
It follows that
k_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is of form (
10
) and is therefore a bi-invariant covariance on
\Sym_{n}
.

Finally, by definition of
\mu(gh^{-1})
we have
|\{\mu_{j}(gh^{-1})=r\}|=c_{r}
.
Taking this into account and grouping polynomials by their respective values of
\mu_{j}
, we get

k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(g,h)=p_{\mu(gh^{-1})}(\boldsymbol{z})=p_{\mu_{j}(gh^{-1})}(\boldsymbol{z})=\prod_{r=1}^{n}p_{r}^{c_{r}}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(15)

3
Sampling power sum Gaussian processes

Here we discuss ways of sampling Gaussian processes with power sum covariance functions, i.e. computational algorithms for obtaining their (approximate) trajectories.
Let
f
be a centered Gaussian process on the group
\Sym_{n}
with covariance
k\colon\Sym_{n}\times\Sym_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{f}=\left(f(g_{1}),\ldots,f(g_{n!})\right)^{\top}
, where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n!}
are arbitrarily enumerated elements of
\Sym_{n}
.
Then
\boldsymbol{f}\sim\operatorname{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{K})
, where
\mathbf{K}
is the covariance matrix of the random vector
\boldsymbol{f}
.
Its elements are
\mathbf{K}_{jr}=k(g_{j},g_{r})
.
Sampling the process
f
means sampling the vector
\boldsymbol{f}
.
Let us write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\operatorname{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{I}),

(16)

where
\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
is a root of the matrix
\mathbf{K}
in the sense that
\left(\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}=\mathbf{K}
, and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix of size
n!
.

The representation (
16
) makes it possible to algorithmically sample
f
by 1) calculating the root
\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
, 2) sampling the standard normal vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\operatorname{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{I})
, and 3) computing the matrix-vector product
\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
.
Step (2) has computational complexity
O(n!)
, step (3) has computational complexity
O((n!)^{2})
, and step (1) has computational complexity
O((n!)^{3})
.
1
1

1

The power
3
can be reduced by using specialized algorithms such as Strassen algorithm. However, this exponent will always be no less than
2
and such specialized algorithms are rarely used in practice because of the large constants hidden behind the
O
notation.

This ultimately makes this approach untenable for computing.
This is not surprising given its excessive generality and not even relying on bi-invariance in any way.
In what follows, we propose other techniques that leverage bi-invariance to allow, albeit approximately, sampling
f
in a much more computationally efficient way.

The first such technique is a special case
of the
generalized random phase Fourier features
method proposed by
[
1
]
for modeling bi-invariant Gaussian processes on general compact groups.

Let
k\colon\Sym_{n}\times\Sym_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bi-invariant covariance.
Then it is of form (
10
), i.e.
k(g,h)=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}\chi_{\lambda}(gh^{-1})
.
Thus
[
1
]
gives

\displaystyle f(g)\approx\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_{l}^{\lambda_{r}}\chi_{\lambda_{r}}(gu_{l}),

\displaystyle w_{l}^{\lambda_{r}}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\operatorname{N}\left(0,\frac{a_{\lambda_{r}}}{d_{\lambda_{r}}}\right),

(17)

where
u_{l}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\operatorname{U}(\Sym_{n})
with
\operatorname{U}(\Sym_{n})
denoting the uniform distribution (Haar probability measure) on
\Sym_{n}
,
d_{\lambda}
denotes the dimension of the representation
\pi_{\lambda}
, and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{R}
correspond to the
R
representations of the group
\Sym_{n}
with the highest value
a_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}
.
To apply this approach, one has to be able to find
R
representations with the largest value
a_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}
.
The number of representations of
\Sym_{n}
grows as
\left(4n\sqrt{3}\right)^{-1}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}

[
8
]
, thus it is not clear how to do this in a computationally tractable way.
Because of this we propose another technique for approximate modeling that does not require such a search.

It is similar to the technique from
[
1
]
described above, but it is intended for bi-invariant processes on
\Sym_{n}
specifically.
To do this, we need some preliminaries.

As before, by
s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{X})
, where
\mathbf{X}\in\operatorname{GL}(m,\mathC)
is a diagonalizable matrix, we denote
s_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})
, where
x_{j}
are eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{X}
.
By the symbol
\operatorname{N}_{\mathC^{m\times m}}
we denote the ensemble of random matrices of size
m\times m
with complex entries of the form
\operatorname{N}(0,1/2)+i\operatorname{N}(0,1/2)
, where all real and imaginary parts are jointly independent.
Then the following is true
[
15
,
9
]
:

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\operatorname{N}_{\mathC^{m\times m}}}s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})\overline{s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})}

\displaystyle=\pi^{-m^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})\overline{s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})}e^{-\tr(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{*})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Z}

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{n!\cdot s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{*})}{d_{\lambda}}.

Also for any character
\chi_{\lambda}
of an irreducible representation of
\Sym_{n}
we have
[
1
]

\frac{\chi_{\lambda}(gh^{-1})}{d_{\lambda}}=\int_{\Sym_{n}}\chi_{\lambda}(gu)\chi_{\lambda}(hu)=\E_{u\sim\operatorname{U}(\Sym_{n})}\chi_{\lambda}(gu)\chi_{\lambda}(hu).

(19)

Rewriting the right hand side of the formula (
14
) using (
18
) with

\mathbf{A}=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{z_{1}},\sqrt{z_{2}},\ldots,\sqrt{z_{m}})

(20)

and using

\displaystyle k_{\boldsymbol{z}}(gh^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\chi_{\lambda}(gh^{-1})s_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m})

(21)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{d_{\lambda}^{2}}{n!}\E_{u\sim\operatorname{U}(\Sym_{n})}\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\operatorname{N}_{\mathC^{m\times m}}}\chi_{\lambda}(gu)\chi_{\lambda}(hu)s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})\overline{s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})}.

Finally, we need some properties of the Robinson—Schensted algorithm
[
14
]
.
Given a permutation
g\in\Sym_{n}
, this algorithm constructs in a bijective way a pair of standard Young tableaux
(P(g),Q(g))
of the same shape

.
We omit all the detail details concerning this mapping, but note only that there is an implementation with computational complexity

For
\lambda(g)
from the Robinson—Schensted algorithm and for

Therefore, summing over

k_{x}(gh^{-1})=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,u\sim\operatorname{U}(\Sym_{n})\\
\mathbf{Z}\sim\operatorname{N}_{\mathC^{m\times m}}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\lambda(v)}(gu)\chi_{\lambda(v)}(hu)s_{\lambda(v)}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})\overline{s_{\lambda(v)}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})}.

(22)

As a result, we obtain the desired approximation of the following form:

f(g)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(w_{j,1}\operatorname{Re}s_{\lambda(v_{j})}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}_{j})+w_{j,2}\operatorname{Im}s_{\lambda(v_{j})}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}_{j})\right)\chi_{\lambda(v_{j})}(gu_{j}),

(23)

with random coefficients

w_{j,1}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\operatorname{N}(0,1)
,

w_{j,2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\operatorname{N}(0,1)
,
and indices

v_{j}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}U(\Sym_{n})
,

u_{j}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}U(\Sym_{n})
,

\mathbf{Z}_{j}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\operatorname{N}_{\mathC^{m\times m}}
.
