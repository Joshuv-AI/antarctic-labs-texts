Super-Resolution on the Two-Dimensional Unit Sphere
Frank Filbir, Kristof Schröder, Anna Veselovska

Abstract
We study the problem of recovering an atomic measure on the unit 2-sphere 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2} given finitely many moments with respect to spherical harmonics. The analysis relies on the formulation of this problem as an optimization problem on the space of bounded Borel measures on 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2} as it was considered by Y. de Castro & F. Gamboa and E. Candés & C. Fernandez-Granda in 2013. We construct a dual certificate using a kernel given in an explicit form and make a concrete analysis of the interpolation problem. Numerical examples are provided and analyzed.

Introduction

Signals on a spherical manifold arise in various applications, staring from medical imaging
[
9
,
11
,
26
]
,
computer graphics
[
14
,
18
]
and sound recording
[
10
,
13
]
to astrophysics
[
22
]
and topography
[
20
]
.
As mentioned in
[
28
]
, in many of these settings inverse problems arise, where an unknown signal need to be recovered from linear measurements acquired through a convolution process.
In this work, we consider the case, when a spatially highly resolved
signal is modelled as a weighted sum of Dirac measures
\mu^{\star}
on the two-dimensional Euclidean
\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
and the information one can access is only the convolved version of the signal
\mu^{\star}\ast D_{N}
with the Dirichlet kernel
D_{N}
on the sphere, for possibly low
N
. The problem of recovery of a spatially highly resolved
signal from its coarse scale information is called the
super-resolution problem
or, in other words, the
de-convolution problem
.

In general, this sort of problem
has been treated in different geometric settings and with respect to different
systems of functions. Exact measure recovery in the classical Fourier setting, i.e. when the underlying space is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, has a very long history starting with the initial work by G. R. de Prony in 1795
[
1
]
in the univariate case, and then moving on to different one-dimensional
[
7
,
6
,
21
]
and multi-dimensional Prony-based techniques
[
31
,
37
,
41
,
47
,
48
]
that have stabilized and generalized the method in various directions. Recently, the Prony’s method has also been extend to the
d
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Less than ten years ago, considering the one-dimensional de-convolution problem in the light of convex optimization, several authors
[
25
,
32
]
have proposed a variational recovery approach that is to minimize the
total variation
over the set of all finite complex measures supported on
\mathbb{T}
, given the convolved version of the measure. It has been
shown that if the support atoms of the measure are well separated then the target measure
\mu^{\star}

is the unique solution of the minimization problem, therewith a sufficient criteria for
\mu^{\star}
being the unique solution is the existence of so-called
dual certificate
, that is, a polynomial of degree
N
whose sup-norm is reached at the points of the measure support.

These two fundamental papers have ushered in new ways of treating the super-resolution problem.
Beside the fact that this variational recovery method does not need the number of unknowns points beforehand, it also has proved to enjoy stability in the case when the low frequency information of corrupted by noise
[
27
,
35
,
36
]
.
Hereupon, the generalization to higher dimensions on the torus has been considered in

[
39
,
44
,
43
]
.
Another big advantage of considering the super-resolution as the minimization problem is its adaptivity to different geometric settings, namely, semi-algebraic domains in higher dimensions
[
38
]
, or compact smooth Riemannian manifolds such es the rotation group
SO(3)

[
40
]
and two-dimensional sphere
[
33
,
34
]
.
Lately, this procedure has been generalized to
short-time Fourier measurements

[
42
]
.

In this work, we consider de-convolution problem on the two-dimensional Euclidean
\mathbb{S}^{2}
as a total variation minimization problem.
Although to prove uniqueness of an optimal solution, we follow a general idea from
[
32
,
40
]
.
the actual construction of a dual certificate requires localization
estimates for interpolation kernels and its derivatives on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with explicit constants, and heavily depends on special behaviour of the geodesic distance with respect to the boundedness of the derivatives and on the fact that there is no nowhere not vanishing vector field on the sphere, due to
the Hairy ball theorem
.
From numerical point of view, we consider two approaches. First, we use the dual formulation of the minimization problem solving it via a single semi-definite
program (SDP), going along the same line as
[
40
,
34
]
.
In the second approach we discretize the primal problem beforehand, then solve the corresponding finite-dimensional optimization problem.
To analyze the convergence of the discretization process we build on results stated in
[
29
]
.
We also would like to mention the for the nonnegative total variation minimization problem an alternative way of construction of a dual certificate that involves some algebraic techniques has been proposed in
[
48
]
, and
in pure compressed sensing setting
a recovery of sparse signal on the two-dimensional sphere has been considered in
[
23
]

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, briefly the
necessary analytical tools on the sphere including spherical harmonics are introduced, and the
problem of super-resolution is stated.
In Section 3 provides the localization results for the chosen interpolating kernel that are essential for construction of a dual certificate. The actual construction of a dual certificate as a solution of the Hermite-type interpolation problem is the content of Section 4. Finally, we finish by presenting the numerical solution and the discretization of the problem in Section 5.

2
Unit Sphere and Super-Resolution

In this section we briefly summarize analytical tools on the two dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and state the super-resolution problem.

2.1
Analysis on the Sphere

The unit sphere is an embedded sub-manifold of the three-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\|x\|_{2}=1\},

where the
l_{2}
-norm is
\|x\|_{2}=\sqrt{x^{\mathrm{T}}x}.
Such
embedding provides a very simple definition of the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
at a point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, that namely it is given as the orthogonal complement of the linear subspace
\mathrm{span}\{x\}
, i.e.

T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle{x,y}\rangle=0\},

where
\langle{x,y}\rangle=x^{\mathrm{T}}y
is the standard inner product.
Induced by the Riemannian metric of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Riemannian metric on the sphere
g_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}:T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}\times T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is given for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
by

g_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(v,w):=\langle{v,w}\rangle,\quad v,w\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}.

In this metric the geodesic distance between two points
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is given by the great-circle distance

d(x,y)=\mathrm{arccos}(\langle{x,y}\rangle),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Let us also shortly describe the differential structure on the sphere. We will use two different
local coordinates on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. First, for each starting point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and a direction
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
, there exists the unique geodesic
\gamma_{x,v}
such that
\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{x,v}^{\prime}(0)=v
, and the equation of such geodesic reads as

\gamma_{x,v}(t)=\cos{(\|v\|_{2}t)}x+\sin{(\|v\|_{2}t)}\frac{v}{\|v\|_{2}}.

Then at a point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
the exponential map
{\exp_{x}{v}:\ T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is given by

\exp_{x}{v}=\gamma_{x,v}(1)

(2.1)

Now, let us fix an orthonormal basis
\eta_{1}^{x},\eta_{1}^{x}\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that
\eta_{2}^{x}=\eta_{1}^{x}\times x
and
\eta_{1}^{x}=x\times\eta_{2}^{x}
.
It is always possible to do so, although we can not choose a local bases in a continuous way, as there is no continuous
nowhere not vanishing vector field on the sphere, due to
the Hairy ball theorem.
This is a special property of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is in contrast to some other manifolds, e.g. the rotation group
SO(3)
, where the tangent space is basically a translation of the tangent space at the
identity. It was a classical problem to determine which of the spheres
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\|x\|_{2}=1\}
are parallelizable, then it has been shown that along with
\mathbb{S}^{0}
and the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
parallelizable are only
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
\mathbb{S}^{7}

[
4
]
.

One way to obtain a local bases
\eta_{1}^{x},\eta_{1}^{x}\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
is to choose a point
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and an orthonormal basis
\eta_{1}^{z},\eta_{1}^{z}\in T_{z}\mathbb{S}^{2}
and then set

\eta_{i}^{x}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\mathrm{e}^{d(x,z)\cdot[\frac{z\times x}{\sin{(d(x,z))}}]\eta_{i}^{z}},&x\neq-z,\\
-\eta_{i}^{z},&x=-z,\end{matrix}\right.

(2.2)

where for a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

[v]=\begin{pmatrix}0&-v_{3}&v_{2}\\
v_{3}&0&-v_{1}\\
-v_{2}&v_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}

is the corresponding skew-symmetric matrix in the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3)
. In other words, we rotate the local
coordinate system at
z
, which is continuous for all points but the antipodal point
-z
.

Combination of the coordinates of a vector
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
in the basis
\eta_{1}^{x},\eta_{1}^{x}\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the exponential map (
2.1
) yields the normal coordinates
centered at
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, i.e. we parametrize a neighborhood of
x
by

\varphi(v_{1},v_{2})=(\cos{\|v\|_{2}t})x+\sin{(\|v\|_{2}t)}\frac{v_{1}\eta_{1}^{x}+v_{2}\eta_{2}^{x}}{\|v\|_{2}}.

(2.3)

and the inverse parametrization for
y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
in a neighborhood of
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is given by

v_{i}(y)=\frac{d(y,x)}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\langle{y,\eta_{i}^{x}}\rangle,\quad i=1,2.

(2.4)

Moreover, the vectors

\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{1}}\varphi(v_{1}(y),v_{2}(y)),\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{2}}\varphi(v_{1}(y),v_{2}(y))

(2.5)

form a basis of
T_{y}\mathbb{S}^{2}
. One can show that in the center of the normal coordinates, the derivatives of the basis vectors have the following properties

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}v_{i}}\varphi(v_{1}(x),v_{2}(x))=-x,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial v_{j}\partial v_{j}}\varphi(v_{1}(x),v_{2}(x))=0.

(2.6)

Since in a normal coordinate system centered at
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the Christoffel symbols vanish at the point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the gradient of a differentiable function
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
at the point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
has the
representation

\nabla f(x)=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}f(x)\\
X_{2}f(x)\end{pmatrix},

(2.7)

where the differential operators
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are defined by

X_{i}f(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{1}}(f\circ\varphi)(v_{1}(x),v_{2}(x))=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-1}(f(\gamma_{x,\eta_{i}^{x}}(t))-f(x)).

(2.8)

The Hessian matrix of a twice differentiable function
{f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}}
in the center
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
of the normal coordinates, has also a special
representation, namely

Hf(x)=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}X_{1}f(x)&X_{1}X_{2}f(x)\\
X_{2}X_{1}f(x)&X_{2}X_{2}f(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.9)

However, the representations (
2.7
) and (
2.9
) are only true in the center of the normal coordinates, since as mentioned before the Christoffel symbols vanish. For different
points, we would have to compute the Christoffel symbols with respect to the normal coordinates, which becomes quite complicated.
Alternatively, we introduce a second set of coordinates, such that the computation of the Christoffel
symbols is much more simpler. For a point
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we parametrize the set
B_{\pi}(0)\setminus\{0\}
by

v(r,\theta)=r(\cos(\theta)\eta_{1}^{z}+\sin(\theta)\eta_{2}^{z})

(2.10)

for
(r,\theta)\in(0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
. Combining (
2.10
) with the exponential map (
2.1
), i.e.

\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}(r,\theta)=\exp_{z}(v(r,\theta)),

(2.11)

yields the
polar coordinates
centered at
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which parametrize
{\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{z,-z\}}
. To give an example, for
z=(0,0,1)^{\mathrm{T}}
, these are the usual spherical coordinates on the sphere, given by

\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}(r,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\sin{r}\cos{\theta}\\
\sin{r}\sin{\theta}\\
\cos{r}\end{pmatrix}.

Following the line, for a vector
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{z,-z\}
, the inverse parametrization is given by

\begin{split}r(x)&=\mathrm{arccos}(\langle{x,z}\rangle)=d(x,z),\\
\theta(x)&=\mathrm{arctan}_{2}(\langle{x,\eta_{2}^{z}}\rangle,\langle{x,\eta_{1}^{z}}\rangle),\end{split}

where
\mathrm{arctan}_{2}(x,y)
denotes the
\mathrm{arctan}
of
\frac{y}{x}
with respect to the different branches of the tangent function,
which means

\cos{(\theta(x))}=\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{1}^{z}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,z))}},\quad\quad\sin{(\theta(x))}=\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{2}^{z}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,z))}}.

For each vector
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{z,-z\}
, the vectors

\gamma_{1}^{x}=\frac{\partial\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial r}(r(x),\theta(x)),\quad\gamma_{2}^{x}=\frac{1}{\sin{(r(x))}}\frac{\partial\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial\theta}(r(x),\theta(x)),

(2.12)

form an orthonormal basis of the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Notably, we have
\gamma_{2}^{x}=\gamma_{1}^{x}\times x
and
\gamma_{1}^{x}=x\times\gamma_{2}^{x}
. We remark, due to the singularities at the poles
z,-z
there is no basis in spherical coordinates of the corresponding tangent spaces.

In the polar coordinates (
2.11
), the Riemannian metric takes the form

g(r,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sin^{2}{(r)}\end{pmatrix},

(2.13)

and the Christoffel symbols in these coordinates are therefore given by

\Gamma^{r}(r,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\sin{r}\cos{r}\end{pmatrix},\quad\Gamma^{\theta}(r,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cot{r}\\
\cot{r}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.14)

For a twice differentiable function
{f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}}
and a vector
{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{z,-z\}}
,
the Hessian matrix with respect to the polar
coordinates centered at
z
, i.e. with respect to the basis (
2.12
), is represented by

Hf=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial r^{2}}&\frac{1}{\sin{r}}\frac{\partial^{2}f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial r\partial\theta}\\
\frac{1}{\sin{r}}\frac{\partial^{2}f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial\theta\partial r}&\frac{1}{\sin^{2}{r}}\frac{\partial^{2}f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sin^{2}{r}}\frac{\partial f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial r}\Gamma^{r}-\frac{1}{\sin{r}}\frac{\partial f\circ\varphi^{\mathrm{pol}}}{\partial\theta}\Gamma^{\theta}.

(2.15)

2.2
Spherical harmonics

Now, let us describe the involved basis functions, known as spherical harmonics. For a detailed overview see
[
24
]
.

Let consider the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
of all functions
{f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}}
such that

\|f\|_{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}|f(r,\theta)|^{2}sin{(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

where
\Omega
is the Riemannian volume form on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
for the metric
g
, and the second part of the equality represents
\|f\|_{2}
in the spherical coordinates.
It is well known, that the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
can be decomposed into an orthogonal sum

L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=\mathrm{cl}_{\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}}\bigoplus\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}H_{l}

where
H_{l}
is the eigenspace to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{l}=-l(l+1)
of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
\mathrm{dim}(H_{l})=2l+1
.
Looking for an orthonormal system of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the unit sphere leads to the spherical harmonics. Namely, the
spherical harmonics

Y_{m}^{l}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
m\in\mathbb{N}
and order
l
are functions

Y_{m}^{l}(x(r,\theta))=N_{\ell m}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos{(r)})\mathrm{e}^{im\theta},

where
-l\leq m\leq l
, the normalization constant
N_{\ell m}
is given by

N_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{2}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}},

P_{l}^{m}(t)
are
associated Legendre polynomials
,
r\in[0,\pi]
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
are the inclination and azimuth respectively.
With this, we have that the system

\{Y_{m}^{l}\colon l\in\mathbb{N},\;-l\leq m\leq l\}

constitutes an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
Moreover, the following addition theorem is valid

P_{l}(\langle{x,y}\rangle):=P_{l}^{0}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)=\frac{4\pi}{2l+1}\sum\limits_{m=-l}^{l}Y_{m}^{l}(x)\overline{Y_{m}^{l}(y)}.

(2.16)

The space of all finite linear combinations
of spherical harmonics with degree less or equal to
N
will be denoted as

\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2}):=\mathrm{span}\{Y_{m}^{l},-l\leq m\leq l,l\leq N\}

and will be called generalized polynomials of degree
N
.
The projection operator onto the set of generalized polynomials
\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, is given by

\mathcal{P}_{N}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\to C(\mathbb{S}^{2})\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{P}_{N}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(y)D_{N}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\Omega(y),

(2.17)

where the Dirichlet kernel on the sphere reads as

D_{N}(x,y)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}P_{l}(\langle{x,y}\rangle).

2.3
Super-Resolution on the Unit Sphere

In the following, we will introduce the problem of super-resolution, or in another words, so called deconvolution problem, on the unit sphere sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, i.e. exact recovery of Dirac measure from its moments with respect to the spherical harmonics up to a degree
N
.

To this aim, let us consider a weighted superposition of spikes

\mu^{\star}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta_{x_{i}}
is a Dirac measure centered at pairwise distinct
x_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are real valued amplitudes.
We assume that all parameters
M,c_{i},x_{i}
are unknown and we can only access

\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}D_{N}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(y)

(2.18)

for possibly low degree
N
.
The super-resolution problem is to
recover the unknown locations
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{supp}{(\mu^{\star})}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}

and the coefficients
c_{i}
from the low frequency information (
2.18
).
Due to consideration of the total variation norm as being the continuous analog of the
\ell_{1}
norm, in the light of convex optimization, see
[
25
,
27
,
32
,
33
,
40
]
, the super-resolution problem can be formulated as the following minimization problem

\min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R})}\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star},

(RP)

where the minimization is carried out over the set of all finite measures
\mu
supported on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and the
total variation
for a signed Borel measure is defined by

\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{2})=\sup\sum_{j}|\mu(B_{j})|,

and the supremum is taken over all partitions
B_{j}
of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

The main ingredient to ensure the fact
\mu^{\star}
is the unique minimizer of the convex program (
RP
) is the existence of a dual interpolating polynomial
{q\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}
, or so-called
dual certificate
, that for each sign sequences
{u_{i}\in\{-1,1\}}
satisfies

\begin{split}q(x_{i})&=u_{i},\quad x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\\
|q(x)|&<1,\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{X}.\end{split}

(2.19)

The connection between the uniqueness of optimal solution and the existence of a dual certificate
has been exploited in different settings, see e.g.
[
25
,
30
,
32
,
33
,
40
]
.
In order to fulfill the interpolating conditions (
2.19
) let us consider the following
Hemite-type
interpolation problem

\begin{split}q(x_{i})&=u_{i},\\
X_{1}q(x_{i})&=X_{2}q(x_{i})=0\end{split}

(2.20)

for
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
X_{k}
are the differential operators defined in (
2.8
). This means, we ask not only the interpolation, but also for local extrema at all the interpolation points. In order to approach the interpolation problem (
2.20
), we consider a kernel
J_{N}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that the kernel
J_{N}(\cdot,y)
itself, and its derivatives
X_{k}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)
, where the superscript indicates the action of the differential operators on the second variable, are generalized polynomials of order
N
in the first variable, i.e.
J_{N}(\cdot,y)
,
X_{k}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
for all vectors
y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The dual certificate we construct is of the form

q(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M}\alpha_{0,i}J_{N}(x,x_{i})+\alpha_{1,i}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{i})+\alpha_{2,i}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{i}).

(2.21)

Thus, is easily follows that due to the construction
q\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
Applying the interpolation conditions (
2.20
) leads to the linear system of equations

K\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}J_{N}\\
X_{1}^{y}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}\\
X_{2}^{y}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.22)

where the block
J_{N}
in the matrix
K
correspond to matrix of the form

J_{N}=(J_{N}(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{M}
, and the same we have for the derivatives. The entries in the vectors are given by
\alpha_{k}=(\alpha_{k,j})_{j=1}^{M}
,
k=0,1,2
, and
u=(u_{j})_{j=1}^{M}
. To find the vector of coefficients
\alpha
, we need to show that the matrix
K
is invertible. To this aim, we follow the general idea provided
[
32
,
40
]
. Namely, due to the block structure of
K
one can proof the invertibility of
K
using an
iterative block inversion
and the fact that a matrix
A
is invertible if
\|I-A\|_{\infty}<1,

where
\|A\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|a_{i,j}|
. In this case the norm of the inverse matrix is bounded by

\|A^{-1}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{1-\|I-A\|_{\infty}}.

Thus, to show the invertibility of
K
, we need to make use of
localization properties
of the entries of
K
, i.e. we need to bound the expressions
|J_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})|

|X_{k}^{y}J_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})|
and
|X_{n}^{x}X_{k}^{y}J_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})|
.
Namely, we are looking for the estimation for the kernel
J_{N}
of the form

|J_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})|<\frac{c}{((N+1)d(x_{i},x_{j}))^{s}},

with some constants
s
and
c
, and for equivalent bounds for the derivatives. Using these estimates we can find explicit bounds on the supremum norm of the coefficients
\alpha_{k}
. Once the coefficients are bounded, we need to show that
|q(x)|<1
for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{X}
. To this aim, we need to show convexity property of
q
, that can be done by estimating the entries of the
Hessian matrix
of
q
. Since by construction (
2.21
)
q
alredy includes first derivatives of
J_{N}
, it will put into consideration third mixed derivatives of
J_{N}
in the Hessian. Therefore, we also need some estimates of the derivatives of third order. All this together results in the topic of the next sections, where we choose a specific kernel and show needed locality estimations.

3
Localized kernels

The aim of this section is to show localization results for the constituents of dual certificate (
2.21
) for a particularly chosen interpolation kernel
J_{N}
.

Let us star discussing the choice of the interpolation kernel. First, the interpolation kernel need to have an expansion in terms of generalized polynomials. Therefore we choose a kernel that owns the representation

J_{N}(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\widetilde{w}_{l}\sum\limits_{m=-l}^{l}Y_{m}^{l}(x)\overline{Y_{m}^{l}(y)},

hence, due to the constriction we have
J_{N}(\cdot,y),

X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)\in{\Pi}_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
for all
y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Moreover, using the addition theorem (
2.16
) for spherical harmonics leads to

J_{N}(x,y)=\widetilde{J}_{N}(d(x,y))=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P_{l}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)

where
\widetilde{J}_{N}
is a trigonometric polynomial. This means that
J_{N}
is a zonal function, i.e. its value only depends on the
distance between
x
and
y
. Such a property of
J_{N}
lends us a hand in deriving estimates of the interpolation kernel
J_{N}
, since on condition localization estimates can derived from localization principles for the trigonometric polynomial
\widetilde{J}_{N}
.

Relying on the discussed above, as an interpolation kernel we choose the specific kernel, given by

J_{N}(x,y)=\widetilde{J}_{N}(d(x,y))=\frac{1}{(\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor+1)^{4}}\frac{\sin^{4}\big((\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor+1)d(x,y)/2\big)}{\sin^{4}(d(x,y)/2)},

(3.1)

i.e. the classical Jackson kernel evaluated at the distance between
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Lemma 3.0.1
.

The Jackson kernel
J_{N}(x,y)
has an expansion of the form

J_{N}(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P_{l}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)

(3.2)

with positive Legendre coefficients

\displaystyle{\widetilde{w}_{l}=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}P_{l}(t)\widetilde{J}_{N}(\mathrm{arccos}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,}

and thus
J_{N}(\cdot,y),X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
for all
y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

For
t\in[-1,1]
, let us consider the function
\widetilde{F}_{n}
defined as

\widetilde{F}_{n}(\mathrm{arccos}(t))=\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)^{2}\mathrm{arccos}(t)/2)}{\sin^{2}(\mathrm{arccos}(t)/2)}

i.e. Fejér kernel evaluated at
\mathrm{arccos}{(t)}
. Then for the trigonometric polynomial
\widetilde{J}_{N}
it holds

\widetilde{J}_{N}(\mathrm{arccos}{(t)})=\widetilde{F}_{n}^{2}(\mathrm{arccos}(t)),\quad n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor

As it was shown in
[
16
]
, the Fejér kernel can be represented in terms of Legendre polynomials as

\widetilde{F}_{n}(\mathrm{arccos}(t))=\sum_{l=0}^{M}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{v}_{l}P_{l}(t),

(3.3)

which shows that
\widetilde{F}_{n}(d(x,\cdot)),\widetilde{F}_{n}(d(\cdot,y))\in\Pi_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, and therefore
\widetilde{J}_{N}(d(x,\cdot))
,
\widetilde{J}_{N}(d(\cdot,y))\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. The positivity of
\widetilde{w}_{l}
follows from the positivity of the linearization coefficients of a product of two Legendre polynomials

P_{n}(t)P_{m}(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{\min(m,n)}\frac{2m+2n-4l+1}{2m+2n-2l+1}\frac{A(m-l)A(l)A(n-l)}{A(n+m-l)}P_{m+n-2}(t),

where
A(m)=\frac{1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot(2m-1)}{m!},
for details see
[
2
]
.
∎

Before we proceed with stating the necessary localization estimates, we would like to discuss the behaviour of the Jackson kernel. The geodesic distance of the sphere behaves in a special way with respect to the boundedness of the derivatives. Namely the derivatives of the geodesic distance
d(x,y)
have true poles at
x=y
and
x=-y
. Whereas the first case can be handled using the point-wise estimates, the second case can not be covered in the same way, since the sign of
\cos(kd(x,y))
and
\sin(kd(x,y))
alternates with
k
in a neighborhood of
x=-y
. Consequently, we need to deal with the singularity at
x=-y
induced by the derivatives of the geodesic distance in different way. Especially, we need to bound the following trigonometric expressions

\begin{split}G_{1}(\omega)&=\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin\omega},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad G_{2}(\omega)=\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos\omega}{\sin\omega},\\
G_{3}(\omega)&=\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\sin\omega-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos\omega}{\sin^{2}\omega},\end{split}

(3.4)

that appear in the spherical derivatives.
As we see, knowing the asymptotic estimates for
\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}
and
\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}
is not sufficient, so we have to consider the difference in the closed form, and to achieve this, we use the closed form expression (
3.1
) of the Jackson kernel.

Lemma 3.0.2
.

Let
\widetilde{J}_{N}
be a trigonometric polynomial defined in (
3.1
), then for some
\omega\neq 0
and for
n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor
, the following estimations holds true

\begin{split}|\widetilde{J}_{N}(\omega)|&\leq\frac{\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{4}|\omega|^{4}}\quad|\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\frac{3\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{3}|\omega|^{4}}\quad|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\frac{12.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}|\omega|^{4}}\\
|G_{1}(\omega)|&\leq\frac{2\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}|\omega|^{4}}\quad|G_{2}(\omega)|\leq\frac{14.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}|\omega|^{4}}\quad|G_{3}(\omega)|\leq\frac{8\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)|\omega|^{4}}\\
|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|&\leq\frac{50.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{4}|\omega|^{4}},\end{split}

where the functions
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
are defined in (
3.4
). Furthermore, for
\displaystyle|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}
we have

\begin{split}|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)-\cos(\omega)G_{1}(\omega)|&\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}|\omega|^{2},\;|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}|\omega|^{2}\\
|G_{2}(\omega)|&\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}|\omega|^{2},\;|G_{3}(\omega)|\leq 0.52\cdot\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|,\\
\left|\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)-\cos(\omega)\sin(\omega)\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|&\leq 0.52\cdot\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|,\quad|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(0)|\leq\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|,\end{split}

and for
\omega=0
we have

\begin{split}\widetilde{J}_{N}(0)&=1,\quad\quad\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(0)=\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(0)=0,\quad\quad\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)=-\frac{n(n+2)}{3},\\
\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)&=\frac{1}{30}n(n+1)(9n(n+2)-2).\end{split}

For the proof of Lemma
3.0.2
please see Appendix
A
.

Now, using these bounds we can formulate the localization property of the spherical derivatives of the Jackson kernel. More precisely, the bounds on the derivatives in normal coordinates are stated in the Theorem
3.0.1
and the those in polar coordinates are derived in Theorem
3.0.2
. In the proofs, we use several identities regarding the cross product, see Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3.0.1
.

The Jackson kernel (
3.1
) fulfills for
x\neq y
and
n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor

\displaystyle|J_{N}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{4}d(x,y)^{4}},\quad|X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)|\leq\frac{3\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{3}d(x,y)^{4}},

\displaystyle|X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}},\quad|X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,y)|\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}}

\displaystyle|X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{101\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}}

and for the case
x=y
we have

\displaystyle J_{N}(x,x)

\displaystyle=1,\quad X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=-X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,x)=-\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)

\displaystyle X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)

\displaystyle=X_{i}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{y}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=0.

Proof.

The first estimate follows directly from Lemma
3.0.2
. For the second estimate, we first calculate the derivative of
J_{N}
using its representation (
3.2
) to get

X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P_{l}^{\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle

(3.5)

which immediately yields
X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x)=0
. In case
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{y,-y\}
, we have for
X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)
the following representation

\displaystyle X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle}{\sin(d(x,y))}=\pm\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{i}^{y}\times y}\rangle}{\sin(d(x,y))}

\displaystyle=\mp\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\frac{\langle{\eta_{i}^{y},x\times y}\rangle}{\sin(d(x,y))}=\mp\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\langle{\eta_{i}^{y},n_{x,y}}\rangle,

where
n_{x,y}
denotes the unique unit vector perpendicular to
x
and
y
. This together with Lemma
3.0.2
yields the second estimate. For the next estimates, let us compute the second derivatives using the representation
X_{n}^{y}J_{N}
in terms of Legendre polynomials (
3.5
) to get

\displaystyle X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}\Big(P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{y,\eta_{i}^{x}}\rangle+P_{l}^{\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}\Big(P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{\eta_{n}^{x},y}\rangle\langle{y,\eta_{i}^{x}}\rangle-\delta_{in}P_{l}^{\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{x,y}\rangle\Big).

For
i\neq n
, it follows
X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x)=X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,x)=X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,-x)=0
. In case
i=n
, we have

\begin{split}-X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{x}J_{N}(x,x)&=X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P_{l}^{\prime}(1)\\
&=\lim\limits_{t\to 1}-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\mathrm{arccos}{(t)})}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}=\lim\limits_{\omega\to 0}-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin{(\omega)}}=-\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0).\end{split}

(3.6)

Following the same line, we obtain

\begin{split}-X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{x}J_{N}(x,-x)=X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x)&=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P_{l}^{\prime}(-1)\\
&=\lim\limits_{\omega\to\pi}\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin{(\omega)}}=-\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\pi).\end{split}

(3.7)

For
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{y,-y\}

, the second derivatives reads as

\begin{split}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)&=\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},y}\rangle}{\sin^{2}{d(x,y)}}\\
&-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\left(\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},y}\rangle\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin^{3}{(d(x,y))}}+\frac{\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\right)\\
&=\langle{\eta_{i}^{y},n_{x,y}}\rangle\langle{\eta_{n}^{x},n_{x,y}}\rangle G_{2}(d(x,y))-\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle G_{1}(d(x,y)),\end{split}

and analogically we get

X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,y)=\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},n_{x,y}\langle{\eta_{n}^{x},n_{x,y}}\rangle}\rangle G_{2}(d(x,y))+\delta_{in}\cos{(d(x,y))}G_{1}(d(x,y)),

where

are defined in (

denotes the unique unit vector perpendicular to both

and

\begin{split}|X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{x}J_{N}(x,y)|,|X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)|&\leq|G_{2}(d(x,y))|+|G_{1}(d(x,y))|\\
&\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}},\end{split}

and yields the estimates for the second derivatives. The third derivatives can be represented as

\begin{split}X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)&=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}\left(P_{l}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{\eta_{j}^{x},y}\rangle\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},y}\rangle\langle{\eta_{j}^{y},x}\rangle\right.\\
&+P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},y}\rangle\langle{\eta_{n}^{y},\eta_{j}^{x}}\rangle+P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{y,\eta_{j}^{x}}\rangle\\
&\left.-\delta_{ij}\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\big(P_{l}^{\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)+P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,y}\rangle)\langle{x,y}\rangle\big)\right)\end{split}

which immediately shows that

X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x)=X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x)=0.

Using the closed form of the second derivatives for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{y,-y\}
, the third derivatives can also be represented as

\begin{split}X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)&=\frac{\langle{\eta_{j}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle-\delta_{ij}\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{x,y}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y))\\

&-3\cos{(d(x,y)}G_{3}(d(x,y))\Big)\\
&+\frac{\langle{\eta_{i}^{x},\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle\langle{\eta_{j}^{x},y}\rangle}{\sin{d(x,y)}}G_{3}(d(x,y))+\delta_{ij}\cos{(d(x,y))}G_{1}(d(x,y)).\end{split}

Hence, using the estimates from Lemma
3.0.2
, we obtain

|X_{j}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)|\leq\frac{101\cdot\pi^{3}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}},

which finishes the proof.

Theorem 3.0.2
.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be pairwise different and
x\neq-z
,then with
{n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor}
the entries of the Hessian matrix
H
of
J_{N}(\cdot,y)

\begin{split}|(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{ii}|&\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}},\quad|(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{ij}|\leq\frac{14.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}}\\
|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{ii}|&\leq\frac{103\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}},\quad|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{ij}|\leq\frac{93.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}}.\end{split}

In case
y=z
and

with

\begin{split}|\widetilde{J}_{N}^{\prime\prime}(0)-(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{ii}|&\leq\frac{3}{20}\delta^{2}(n+1)^{2},\quad|(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{ij}|\leq\frac{3}{20}\delta^{2}(n+1)^{2}\\
|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,z))_{11}|&\leq\frac{3}{10}\delta(n+1)^{3},\quad|(HX_{y}^{n}J_{N}(x,z))_{22}|\leq\frac{1}{5}\delta(n+1)^{3}\\
|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,z))_{ij}|&\leq\frac{1}{5}\delta(n+1)^{3}.\end{split}

Proof.

As it has been discussed at the beginning, in polar coordinates centered at
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, there is the local parametrization

\phi^{\mathrm{pol}}(r,\theta)=\cos{(r)}z+\sin{(r)}\big(\cos{(\theta)}\eta_{1}^{z}+\sin{(\theta)}\eta_{2}^{z}\big),

where
\eta_{1}^{z}
,
\eta_{1}^{z}
form an orthonormal basis of

, such that
\eta_{2}^{z}=\eta_{1}^{z}\times z
, and the implicit inverse parametrization for

\begin{split}r(x)&=d(x,z),\\
\cos{(\theta(x))}&=\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{1}^{z}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,z))}}=-\langle{\eta_{2}^{z},n_{z,x}}\rangle,\\
\sin{(\theta(x))}&=\frac{\langle{x,\eta_{2}^{z}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,z))}}=\langle{\eta_{1}^{z},n_{z,z}}\rangle.\end{split}

First, let us compute the partial derivatives of the function
f_{\xi}(r,\theta)=\langle{\phi^{\mathrm{pol}}(r,\theta),\xi}\rangle
, for a vector
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Carrying out simple computations results in

\begin{split}\partial_{r}f_{\xi}(r,\theta)&=-\sin{(r)}\langle{z,\xi}\rangle+\cos{(r)}\big(\cos{(\theta)}\langle{\eta_{1}^{z},\xi}\rangle+\sin{(\theta)}\langle{\eta_{2}^{z},\xi}\rangle\big),\\

\partial^{2}_{r\theta}f_{\xi}(r,\theta)&=\partial^{2}_{\theta r}f_{\xi}(r,\theta)=\cos{(r)}\big(\cos{(\theta)}\langle{\eta_{2}^{z},\xi}\rangle-\sin{(\theta)}\langle{\eta_{1}^{z},\xi}\rangle\big).\end{split}

\begin{split}\partial_{r}f_{\xi}(r,\theta)&=\sin{(d(x,\xi))}\langle{n_{x,\xi},n_{z,x}}\rangle,\\
\partial_{\theta}f_{\xi}(r,\theta)&=\sin{(d(x,z))}\sin{(d(x,\xi))}\langle{x,n_{x,\xi}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle,\\

&\quad-\sin^{2}{(d(x,z))\cos{(d(x,\xi))}},\\

Now, let us proceed with calculation of the full derivatives. For abbreviation, we use further the following notation

First, we start with the kernel

(3.8)

where

and

are given in (
3.4
). Thus, using Lemma

|(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{11}|\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}}.

If

\begin{split}(HJ_{N}(x,-x))_{11}&=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}\Big(P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(\langle{x,-x}\rangle)(\partial_{r}f_{-x}(x))^{2}\\

&-P^{\prime}_{l}(-1)\cos(d(x,-x))\Big)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\widetilde{w}_{l}P^{\prime}_{l}(-1).\end{split}

Following the same argumentation as in (

(HJ_{N}(x,-x))_{11}=\widetilde{J}_{N}(\pi).

In case
y=z
and
d(x,z)\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}
, from the representation (
3.8
), we get

(HJ_{N}(x,-x))_{11}=\widetilde{J}_{N}(d(x,z)).

and combining this together with Lemma
3.0.2
results in

|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)-(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{11}|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}d(x,z)^{2}\leq\frac{3}{20}d(x,z)^{2}(n+1)^{4}\leq\frac{3}{20}\delta^{2}(n+1)^{2}.

For the second diagonal entry of the Hessian matrix, we have

\begin{split}(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{22}&=\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle^{2}\left(\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin{(d(x,y))})}\right)\\
&+\cos{(d(x,y))}\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\\
&=\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle^{2}G_{2}(d(x,y))+\cos{(d(x,y))}G_{1}(d(x,y))\end{split}

which provides the following estimate

|(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{22}|\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{4}d(x,y)^{4}}

and gives zero entry
(HJ_{N}(x,-x))_{22}=0
for
y=-x
.

In case
y=z
, we get

(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{22}=\cos{(d(x,z))}\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,z))}{\sin{(d(x,z))}},

that together with Lemma
3.0.2
provides the estimate

|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)-(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{22}|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}d(x,z)^{2}\leq\frac{3}{20}d(x,z)^{2}(n+1)^{4}\leq\frac{3}{20}\delta^{2}(n+1)^{2}.

For the off-diagonal entries of the Hessian matrix, we have

(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{ij}=\langle{n_{x,y},n_{z,x}}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle G_{3}(d(x,y)),

and analogous procedure to the one carried out above provides

\begin{split}|(HJ_{N}(x,y))_{ij}|&\leq\frac{14.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}d(x,y)^{4}},\quad|(HJ_{N}(x,z))_{ij}|\leq\frac{3}{20}\delta^{2}(n+1)^{2},\\
(HJ_{N}(x,-x))_{ij}&=0.\end{split}

For the derivative
X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)
of the kernel the estimates can be derived folowing the same lines. Namely, the derivative
X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(\cdot,y)
is defined as

X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)=-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle,

where
\eta_{n}^{y}\in T_{z}\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then, with
\xi=\eta_{n}^{y}
and the abbreviations in (
3.4
), we have

\begin{split}\partial_{r}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)&=\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\partial_{r}f_{y}(x)}{\sin(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y))-\partial_{r}f_{\xi}(x)G_{1}(d(x,y))\\
\partial_{\theta}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)&=\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\partial_{\theta}f_{y}(x)}{\sin(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y))-\partial_{\theta}f_{\xi}(x)G_{1}(d(x,y)).\end{split}

In polar coordinates centered at
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we have for
y\neq-x
and
\psi\in\{r,\theta\}

\begin{split}\partial_{\psi}G_{1}(d(x,y))&=-\frac{\partial_{\psi}f_{y}(x)}{\sin(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y)),\\
\partial_{\psi}\left(\frac{G_{3}(d(x,y))}{\sin(d(x,y))}\right)&=-\frac{\partial_{\psi}f_{y}(x)}{\sin^{3}(d(x,y))}\Big(\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))+\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\\
&\quad-3\cos{(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\Big).\end{split}

Therefore, for the first diagonal entry of the Hessian matrix one has

\begin{split}(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{11}&=\partial_{rr}X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y)\\
&=\frac{\partial_{r}f_{\xi}(x)\partial_{r}f_{y}(x)}{\sin(d(x,y))}G_{2}(d(x,y))+\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\partial_{rr}f_{y}(x)}{\sin(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\\
&+\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\partial_{r}f_{y}(x)\partial_{r}\left(\frac{G_{3}(d(x,y))}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\right)\\
&-\partial_{rr}f_{\xi}(x)G_{1}(d(x,y))-\partial_{r}f_{\xi}(x)\partial_{r}G_{1}(d(x,y)).\end{split}

Inserting the pre-computed derivatives results in

\begin{split}(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{11}&=2\langle{n_{x,\xi},n_{z,x}}\rangle\langle{n_{z,x},n_{x,y}}\rangle\sin{(d(x,\xi))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\\
&-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y))+\langle{x,\xi}\rangle G_{1}(d(x,y))\\
&-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{n_{z,x},n_{x,y}}\rangle^{2}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\left(\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))+\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\right)\\
&+\frac{3\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{n_{z,x},n_{x,y}}\rangle^{2}\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y)).\end{split}

Since the following equalities hold

\langle{x,\xi}\rangle=\langle{x,\eta_{n}^{y}}\rangle=\pm\langle{\eta_{i}^{y},x\times y}\rangle=\pm\langle{\eta_{i}^{y},n_{x,y}}\rangle\sin(d(x,y)),

we have that

\left|\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\right|\leq 1.

Then using Lemma
3.0.2
, the above derived representation of
(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{11}
we get

|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{11}|\leq\frac{103.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}}.

In case
y=-x
, we again use the polynomial representation of
J_{N}
, which directly provides

(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x))_{11}=0.

In case
y=z
and
d(x,z)\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}
, we have

(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x))_{11}=-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,z)),

and again making use of Lemma
3.0.2
, we get the estimate

|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x))_{11}|\leq\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)d(x,z)\leq\frac{3}{10}\delta(n+1)^{3}.

Using an analogous procedure as above, the second diagonal entry can be represented as

\begin{split}(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{22}&=2\langle{x,n_{x,\xi}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle\sin(d(x,\xi))G_{3}(d(x,y))\\
&-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y))+\langle{x,\xi}\rangle G_{1}(d(x,y))\\
&-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle^{2}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\left(\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))+\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\right),\\
&+\frac{3\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle^{2}\cos(d(x,y))}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y)),\end{split}

which shows that

|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{22}|\leq\frac{103.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}}.

Moreover, we also have
(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x))_{22}=0
, and using Lemma
3.0.2
gives

\begin{split}|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,z))_{22}|&=\left|\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle}{\sin^{2}(d(x,y))}\left(\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,z))\right.\right.\\
&\left.\left.-\cos(d(x,z))\sin{(d(x,z))}\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,z))\right)\right|\\
&\leq 0.52\cdot\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)d(x,z)\leq\frac{1}{5}\delta(n+1)^{3}.\end{split}

Lastly, similar computation for the off-diagonal entries shows

\begin{split}(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{ij}&=\langle{x,\langle{n_{x,y},n_{z,x}}\rangle(n_{x,\xi}\times n_{z,x})}\rangle\sin{(d(x,\xi))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\\
&+\langle{x,\langle{n_{x,\xi},n_{z,x}}\rangle(n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x})}\rangle\sin{(d(x,\xi))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\\
&-\frac{\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle\langle{n_{x,y},n_{z,x}}\rangle}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}\left(\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))+\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(d(x,y))\right)\\
&+\frac{3\langle{x,\xi}\rangle\langle{x,n_{x,y}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle\langle{n_{x,y},n_{z,x}}\rangle\cos{(d(x,y))}}{\sin{(d(x,y))}}G_{3}(d(x,y)),\end{split}

which results in

\begin{split}|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,y))_{ij}|&\leq\frac{93.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)d(x,y)^{4}},\quad(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,-x))_{ij}=0,\\
|(HX_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,z))_{ij}|&=\left|\langle{x,n_{x,\xi}\times n_{z,x}}\rangle\sin{(d(x,y))}G_{3}(d(x,y))\right|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{5}\delta(n+1)^{3}.\end{split}

∎

We finish this section bounding sums of point-wise expression from the previous theorems.

Let us consider a discrete set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that the minimal separation distance between the set elements is bounded from below in the following way

\rho(\mathcal{X})=\min\limits_{x_{i},x_{j}\in\mathcal{X},x_{i}\neq x_{j}}d(x_{i},x_{j})\geq\frac{\nu}{n+1}.

(3.9)

Involving classical ringing arguments on the sphere, the following result holds true.

Lemma 3.0.3
.

Let
x_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a discrete set that satisfies a separation condition (
3.9
). Let a vector
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be such that
d(x,x_{m})\leq\varepsilon\frac{\nu}{n+1}
for
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1/2
. Suppose that a function
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
fulfills

|f(x,y)|\leq\frac{c_{f}}{((n+1)d(x,y))^{s}}

(3.10)

for
x\neq y
and some
s\geq 3
, then

\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|f(x,x_{k})|\leq\frac{c_{f}a_{\varepsilon}}{\nu^{s}},

where
a_{\varepsilon}=\zeta(s-1)\cdot\min\{9\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{-s}+25,25\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{-s}\}
. Here
\zeta
denotes the Riemannian Zeta function.

Now, based on Lemma
3.0.3
we obtain the following bound for the point-wise summation of the Jackson kernel and its derivatives.

Lemma 3.0.4
.

Let
x_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a discrete set, which obeys a separation condition (
3.9
). Let
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that
d(x,x_{m})\leq\varepsilon\frac{\nu}{n+1}
for
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, then it follows

\begin{split}&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|J_{N}(x,x_{k})|\leq\frac{\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}}{\nu^{4}},\;\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|X_{n}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{k})|\leq\frac{3\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)}{\nu^{4}},\\
&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{x}J_{N}(x,x_{k})|,\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|(HJ_{N}(x,x_{k}))_{ii}|\leq\frac{16.5\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)^{2}}{\nu^{4}},\\
&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|X_{i}^{x}X_{i}^{x}X_{j}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{k})|\leq\frac{101\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)^{3}}{\nu^{4}},\\
&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|(HJ_{N}(x,x_{k}))_{ij}|\leq\frac{14.5\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)^{2}}{\nu^{4}},\\
&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|(HX_{i}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{k}))_{ii}|\leq\frac{103.5\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)^{3}}{\nu^{4}},\\
&\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{m}\}}|(HX_{i}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{k}))_{ij}|\leq\frac{93.5\cdot\pi^{4}\cdot\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}(n+1)^{3}}{\nu^{4}},\end{split}

where
\tilde{a}_{\varepsilon}=\zeta(3)\cdot\min\{9\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{-4}+25,25\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{-4}\}
and
\zeta
denotes the Riemannian Zeta function.

Proof.

The above given estimates follow immediately after combining the estimates from Theorems
3.0.1
and
3.0.2
and the results of Lemma
3.0.3
.
∎

Having established the necessary localization estimates for the Jackson kernel and its derivatives in this section, we are now able to construct and validate a dual certificate using the Hermite interpolation in the next section.

4
Dual Certificate on the Sphere

4.1
Solution of the Interpolation Problem

In this section, we construct a dual certificate as a solution of the Hermite type interpolation problem. Let us remind, we would like to solve the interpolation problem

\begin{split}q(x_{i})&=u_{i},\quad x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\\
|q(x)|&<1,\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{X},\end{split}

(4.1)

where
q
needs to be an element of
\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Following Section
2.3
, we consider the interpolant of the form

q(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M}\alpha_{0,i}J_{N}(x,x_{i})+\alpha_{1,i}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{i})+\alpha_{2,i}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}(x,x_{i}).

(4.2)

and would like to show the the linear system of equations

K\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}J_{N}\\
X_{1}^{y}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}\\
X_{2}^{y}J_{N}&X_{1}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(4.3)

has a solution for the Jackson kernel
J_{N}
chosen in Section
3
.

We assume that the interpolation points
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
obey a minimal separation distance of the form

\rho(\mathcal{X})=\min\limits_{x_{i},x_{j}\in\mathcal{X},x_{i}\neq x_{j}}d(x_{i},x_{j})\geq\frac{\nu}{n+1},

(4.4)

where
n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor
.
For abbreviation, we use the following notation

J_{ij}=X_{i}^{x}X_{j}^{y}J_{N}.

As have been discussed before, to show the existence of the interpolating polynomial
q
, we need to show the invertibility of the matrix
K
. Even more, we need to partially compute the inverse of
K
to derive bounds on the coefficients
\alpha
. The following Lemma provides bounds the entries of the interpolation matrix.

Lemma 4.1.1
.

Suppose the set of points
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
satisfies the separation condition (
4.4
). Then the entries of the interpolation matrix
K
are bounded in the following way

\begin{split}\|I-J_{00}\|&\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\nu^{4}},\quad\quad\quad\|-J^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)I-J_{ii}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{2}(n+1)^{2}}{\nu^{4}},\\
\|J_{0i}\|_{\infty},\;\|J_{i0}\|_{\infty}&\leq\frac{C_{1}(n+1)}{\nu},\quad\|J_{ij}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{2}(n+1)^{2}}{\nu^{4}},\quad\text{for }i\neq j,\;i,j\neq 0,\end{split}

where the constants are defined as

\begin{split}C_{0}=25\cdot\zeta(3)\cdot\pi^{4},\quad C_{1}=75\cdot\zeta(3)\cdot\pi^{4},\quad C_{2}=412.5\zeta(3)\cdot\pi^{4}.\end{split}

(4.5)

If
n\geq 9
and
\nu^{4}>\frac{3}{0.99}\cdot C_{2}
, then for the inverse of
J_{00}
and
J_{ii}
the following bounds are valid

\|J_{00}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{C_{0}}{\nu^{4}}},\quad\quad\|J_{ii}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{3}{0.99(n+1)^{2}(1-\frac{3C_{2}}{0.99\nu^{4}})}.

Proof.

The bounds follows directly from Theorem
3.0.1
and Lemma
3.0.2
, as well as from the invertibility of a matrix
A
, if

\|I-A\|_{\infty}<1,

and the bounds on the norm of the inverse given by

\|A^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\|I-A\|_{\infty}}.

For the bound on
J_{ii}^{-1}
, one uses additionally that for
n\geq 9
, so that

{|J^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)|=\frac{n(n+1)}{3}\geq\frac{0.99\cdot(n+1)^{2}}{3}}

.
∎

Relying on the above stated results, we move on to the main Theorem of this section, which provides a condition on the separation of the interpolation points to guarantee the invertibility of the interpolation matrix
K
and gives bounds on the coefficients
\alpha
.

Theorem 4.1.1
.

Suppose the separation condition (
4.4
) is satisfied by the set
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
for some
\nu\geq 0
and a constant
b\geq 3
such that

\nu^{4}\geq\frac{3}{0.99}\cdot b\cdot C_{2},

where the constant
C_{2}
is given in (
4.5
). Then the matrix
K
is invertible and the entries of the coefficient vector
\alpha
in (
4.3
) satisfy

\begin{split}\|\alpha_{0}\|_{\infty}&\leq 1+\frac{1}{45(b-2)-1},\quad\quad\quad\|\alpha_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{(n+1)^{-1}}{4.5(b-2)-0.1},\quad j=1,2.\end{split}

Furthermore, for elements of the vector
\alpha_{0}
we have the lower bound

|\alpha_{0,i}|\geq 1-\frac{1}{45(b-2)-1}.

Proof.

In the first place, we partition the matrix
K
into blocks as

K=\begin{pmatrix}K_{0}&\widetilde{K}_{1}\\
K_{1}&K_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with the blocks given by

\begin{split}K_{0}&=J_{00}=J_{N},\\
K_{1}&=\left[J_{01}\;J_{02}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}=\left[X_{1}^{y}J_{N}\;X_{2}^{y}J_{N}\right]^{\mathrm{T}},\\
\widetilde{K}_{1}&=\left[J_{10}\;J_{20}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}=\left[X_{1}^{x}J_{N}\;X_{2}^{x}J_{N}\right]^{\mathrm{T}},\\
K_{2}&=\begin{bmatrix}J_{11}&J_{21}\\
J_{12}&J_{22}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{1}^{y}J_{N}\\
X_{1}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}&X_{2}^{x}X_{2}^{y}J_{N}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

after that we use an iterative block inversion. For abbreviation, we set

a_{1}:=\frac{3\cdot C_{2}}{0.99\nu^{4}}

that represents the quotient of the off-diagonal upper bound and the on-diagonal lower bound.
Thus, the assumption of the theorem reads now as

a_{1}\leq\frac{1}{b},\quad\quad\text{where}\quad b\geq 3.

Using the bounds from Lemma
4.1.1
, one can shown that

\left\|I-\frac{K_{2}/J_{22}}{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{b-1}.

Due to this, the invertibility of the matrix
K_{2}
follows easily from

\|(K_{2}/J_{22})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{3(b-1)}{0.99(n+1)^{2}(b-2)}.

With the abbreviation
T=K_{2}/J_{22}
, the inverse matrix of
K_{2}
has the representation

K_{2}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}T^{-1}&-T^{-1}J_{21}(J_{22})^{-1}\\
-(J_{22})^{-1}J_{12}T^{-1}&(J_{22})^{-1}+(J_{22})^{-1}J_{12}T^{-1}J_{21}(J_{22})^{-1}.\end{pmatrix}

(4.6)

In the next step, again relying on Lemma
4.1.1
and on the bound for
(K_{2}/J_{22})^{-1}
, it can be shown that

\|I-K/K_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{45(b-2)},

which in turn results in

\|(K/K_{2})^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{45(b-2)}}

and in the invertibility of
K
. Now, using the representation (
4.6
) of the inverse of
K_{2}
and the abbreviation
S=K/K_{2}
, one comes to the solution of the interpolation problem is given by

\begin{split}\alpha_{0}&=S^{-1}u\\
\alpha_{1}&=-T^{-1}(J_{01}-J_{21}(J_{22})^{-1}J_{02})\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{2}&=-J_{22}^{-1}(J_{12}\alpha_{1}+J_{02}\alpha_{0}).\end{split}

This yields the bounds

\|\alpha_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq 1+\frac{1}{45(b-2)-1},\quad\quad\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\infty},\,\|\alpha_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{(n+1)^{-1}}{4.5(b-2)-0.1}.

Moreover, the absolute value of
\alpha_{0,i}
has the lower bound

\begin{split}|\alpha_{0,i}|&\geq\left|\left(\left(I-\left(I-(K/K_{2})^{-1}\right)\right)u\right)_{i}\right|\\
&\geq\left|1-\left|\left(\left(I-(K/K_{2})^{-1}\right)u\right)_{i}\right|\right|\\
&\geq\left|1-\left|\left(I-K/K_{2}\right)(K/K_{2})^{-1}\right|\right|.\end{split}

Since
b\geq 3
, the subtrahend in the last inequality can be bounded as

\left|(I-K/K_{2})(K/K_{2})^{-1}\right|\leq\|I-K/K_{2}\|_{\infty}\|(K/K_{2})^{-1}\|_{\infty},

which results in the corresponding bound for
\alpha_{0,i}
, namely

|\alpha_{0,i}|\geq 1-\frac{1}{45(b-2)-1}.

∎

Looking for explicit bounds for the coefficients
\alpha
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.1.1
.

Suppose the set of interpolation points
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{i}\}_{m=0}^{M}
satisfies a separation distance of

\rho(\mathcal{X})\geq\frac{19.2\pi}{N}

(4.7)

for
N\geq 20
. Then the interpolation problem (
4.3
) has a unique solution, such that

\|\alpha_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq 1+6.3\cdot 10^{-3},\quad\quad\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\infty},\,\|\alpha_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{6.3\cdot 10^{-2}}{(n+1)},

and for elements of the vector
\alpha_{0}
we have the lower bound

|\alpha_{0,i}|\geq 1-6.3\cdot 10^{-3}.

Proof.

For
n=\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor
, we have
(n+1)\geq N/2
and therefore

\rho(\mathcal{X})\geq\frac{9.6\pi}{(n+1)}.

It is easy to check that with
\nu=9.6\pi
and
b=5.6
, we have

\nu^{4}\geq\frac{3}{0.99}\cdot b\cdot C_{2}.

Thus, it follows from Theorem
4.1.1
that

5
Numerical Solution

5.1
Semidefinite Formulation of the Optimization Problem

At first glance, finding the solution of the total variation minimization problem (
RP
) might seem rather complicated since it is an infinite dimensional optimization problem over the whole measure space
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R})
, therefore numerically not feasible.
The aim of this section is to provide a formulation of the convex program problem (
RP
) such that it can be handled by the existing convex optimization engines. To do so, we follow the ideas in
[
32
,
34
,
40
]
.

First, let us switch to the convex pre-dual to (
RP
) that is given by

\max\limits_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}\mathrm{Re}\langle{\mathcal{P}_{N}f,\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\|{\mathcal{P}_{N}f}\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{N}
in the projection operator given in (
2.17
), immediately the equivalent formulation follows

\max\limits_{f\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}\mathrm{Re}\langle{f,\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\|{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,

(dRP)

where the constraint imposes that the modulus of the generalized polynomial

\begin{split}f(x)=f(x(r,\theta))&=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}f_{l,m}Y_{l}^{m}(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}f_{l,m}N_{lm}P_{l}^{m}(\cos(r))\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}\end{split}

is uniformly bounded by
1
over the whole
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, i.e. for
(r,\theta)\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi]
.
On the grounds that there is a deeply developed theory for multi-variate trigonometric polynomials providing numerous condition on their boundedness on a frequency domain, see e.g.
[
15
]
, it would be clearly better to represent
f
as a purely trigonometric expressions.
Since each associated Legendre polynomial
P_{l}^{m}(\cos(r))
can be uniquely represented in as
P_{l}^{m}(\cos(r))=\sum_{k=-l}^{l}p_{lmk}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kr}
, we easily obtain

\begin{split}f(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\sum_{k=-l}^{l}f_{l,m}N_{lm}p_{lmk}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kr}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}=\sum_{m=-N}^{N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\tilde{f}_{mk}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kr}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta},\end{split}

where the coefficients
{f}_{mk}
, see
[
34
]
, are defined as

\tilde{f}_{mk}=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\tilde{f}_{mkl},\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{f}_{lmk}=\left\{\begin{matrix}f_{l,m}N_{lm}{p}_{lmk},&m,k\in[-l,l],\\
0,&else.\end{matrix}\right.

Now, to replace the norm constraint in (
dRP
) by a finite dimensional conditions we use so-called
the Bounded Real Lemma
. To do this, let us first throw light on the notion of the half-space
\mathcal{H}
. S set
\mathcal{H}
is called a half-space of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
if
\mathcal{H}\cap(-\mathcal{H})=\{0\}
, and
\mathcal{H}\cup(-\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{H}
. A standard way to construct half-space is given iteratively. We start with
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathbb{N}
and we say that
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{H}
if either
k_{2}>0
or
k_{2}=0
and
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, of course, such iterative representation is very useful for numerical purposes. With this preparation, a particular version of the Bounded Real reads as following.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

[
15
]

Let
h(r,\theta)
be a positive orthant polynomial defined by

h(r,\theta)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h_{m,k}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta},

where
{(r,\theta)}\in\mathcal{D}=[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
. Then
providing that
|H(r,\theta)|\leq 1
on the frequency domain
\mathcal{D}
, there exist a positive semi-definite matrix
Q_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2N+1)^{2}\times(2N+1)^{2}}
and s half-space
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}Q&h\\
h^{\mathrm{H}}&1\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0,\quad\mathrm{tr}(\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}Q)=\delta_{\mathbf{k}},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{H},\quad-n\leq\mathbf{k}\leq n,

(5.1)

where
{h\in\mathbb{C}^{(2N+1)^{2}}}
is a column stacked vector of coefficients
h_{m,k}
.

Returning to (
dRP
), we see that the domain
(r,\theta)\in[0,\pi]\times[0,2\pi)
of the generalized polynomial
f
is slightly shifted version of the frequency domain
\mathcal{D}
in Lemma
5.1.1
, moreover
f
is not a positive orthant polynomial. So to reach the desired frequency domain and to have the means to apply the Bounded Real Lemma
5.1.1
we can consider, for example, a trigonometric polynomial

h(r,\theta)=\mathrm{e}^{Nr+N\theta}f(r+\pi,\theta+\pi)=\sum_{m=0}^{2N}\sum_{k=0}^{2N}(-1)^{(m+k)}\tilde{f}_{m-N,k-N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}.

The polynomial
h(r,\theta)
is
positive orthant, has the same magnitude as
f
does, and is defined on
(r,\theta)\in[-\pi,0]\times[-\pi,\pi)\subset\mathcal{D}
. Hence, the dual problem (
dRP
) is equivalent to

\max\limits_{f\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2}),\,Q}\mathrm{Re}\langle{f,\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\eqref{finite_dim_const},

(fdRP)

with
h_{m,k}=(-1)^{(m+k)}\tilde{f}_{m-N,k-N}
, for
m,k=0,\dots 2N.

By strong duality, if
\mu^{\star}
is a solution of the primal problem (
RP
) and
f^{\star}
is any solution of the pre-dual problem, then it follows

\mathrm{Re}\langle{f^{\star},\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle=\mathrm{Re}\langle{\mathcal{P}_{N}f^{\star},\mu^{\star}}\rangle=\|\mu^{\star}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

In the case
\mu^{\star}
is a discrete measure, this implies that the generalized polynomial is exactly equal to the sign of
\mu^{\star}
, when
\mu^{\star}
is not vanishing, namely

f^{\star}(x_{i})=\mathrm{sign}_{\mu^{\star}}(x_{i}),\quad x_{i}\in\mathrm{supp}{(\mu^{\star})}.

which in turn means that the supporting points form a subset of the zeros of the polynomial
1-|f^{\star}(x)|\in\Pi_{2N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

Summing up, we obtain the following computational algorithm.

Data:

low frequency information
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}
of a measure
{\mu^{\star}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{2})}

begin

Solve for
f^{\star}\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
,
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{(2N+1)^{2}\times(2N+1)^{2}}

\begin{split}\max\limits_{f\in\Pi_{N},Q}\mathrm{Re}\langle{c,\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle\quad\mbox{ s.t. }\quad&\begin{bmatrix}Q&h\\
h^{\mathrm{H}}&1\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0,\quad\mathrm{tr}(\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}Q)=\delta_{\mathbf{k}},\\

5.2
Discretization of the Optimization Problem

To avoid the hing complexity of the the semi-definite program for the higher order of moments we propose to use a discretization of the primal problem. In this section we discuss the convergence behavior of the solution of the discretized problem.

Let us choose a sequence of discrete sets
\mathcal{G}_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then the filling distance of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is defined as

\rho(\mathcal{G}_{n})=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}d(x,y).

For the chosen sets
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we consider the discretized version of the primal problem (
RP
), namely

\min\limits_{\mathrm{supp}{(\mu)}\subset\mathcal{G}_{n}}\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star},

(RP
n
)

To discuss the convergence behavior of the solution of the discretized problem (
RPn
), we follow ideas from
[
29
]
, where the convergence for continuously parameterized dictionaries has been discussed.

Theorem 5.2.1
.

Let assume that the measure
\mu^{\star}=\sum_{x_{i}\in X}c_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
is the unique solution of the primal problem (
RP
) and sequence
\mathcal{G}_{n}
of discretizations is chosen in such way that

\rho(\mathcal{G}_{n})\to 0,

when
n\to\infty
. Then each sequence of solutions
\mu_{n}
of (
RPn
) converges to
\mu^{\star}
in the weak
∗
-topology.
Moreover, there exists
\varepsilon>0
, such that

\mu_{n}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))\to c_{i},\quad|\mu_{n}|(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))\to c_{i},

and

|\mu_{n}|\Big((\cup_{i}B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))^{c}\Big)\to 0.

(5.2)

Proof.

Following the same line as in
[
29
]
, we show that each sequence of solutions is bounded and thus, due to the sequentially Banach-Alaoglu Theorem, admits a weak
∗
convergent subsequence that converges to as solution of the continuous problem (
RP
). For the boundedness, let us consider the discretized convex pre-dual problem to (
RPn
)

\max\limits_{f\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}\mathrm{Re}\langle{f,\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad\|{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\,x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}

(dRP
n
)

We show that the feasible sets of the dual problem, i.e. the sets of function
f\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
such that
|f(x)|\leq 1
for
x\in G_{n}
, are bounded and therefore compact. By assumption
\rho(\mathcal{G}_{n})\to 0
, therefore we have the for large enough
n
,

\rho(\mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{N}.

Applying the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality
[
16
,
17
]
, yields for all
f\in\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})

\|{f}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-N\rho(\mathcal{G}_{n}))^{-1}\max\limits_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}|f(x)|,

meaning that all feasible sets are uniformly bounded and, thus, compact. This shows that the discretization problem has a solution, and we denote these minimizers by
f_{n}
.

The rest of the proof is identical to the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
29
]
. Let us briefly sketch it. Due to the uniform boundedness, it can be shown that the sequence
f_{n}
of solutions of the discretized dual problem converges to the solution
f^{\star}
of the continuous dual problem. Since strong duality holds for both the discretized problems and the continuous one, we have

\mathrm{Re}\langle{f_{n},\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle=\|\mu_{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\quad\text{and }\quad\mathrm{Re}\langle{f^{\star},\mathcal{P}_{N}^{*}\mu^{\star}}\rangle=\|\mu^{\star}\|_{\mathrm{TV}},

which shows that the sequence
\mu_{n}
is bounded. Then due to the Banach-Alaoglu Theorem we get the weak
∗
convergence to the minimizer
\mu^{\star}
. The convergence of the measure of the epsilon balls follows analogically to Corollary 1 in
[
29
]
.
∎

Additionally, as it has been mentioned in
[
29
]
, for fine enough discretization the property (
5.2
) suggests that the support of the solutions
\mu_{n}
of the discrete minimization problems cluster around the support of
\mu^{\star}
.

Now let us proceed with the exact digitization procedure via considering the following grid
[
8
]
in the spherical coordinates for inclination
[0,\pi)
and azimuth
[0,2\pi)

r_{k}=\frac{\pi(2j+1)}{4n},\quad\quad\theta_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{2n},\quad\quad j,k=0,\dots,2n-1,

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. These points generate a grid
\mathcal{G}_{n}
of
4n^{2}
points.

Let the matrix
{\bf Y}_{N}
of spherical harmonics from
\Pi_{N}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
evaluated at the grid points,

{\bf Y_{N}}=\big(Y_{m}^{\ell}(x(r_{k},\theta_{k})\big)_{\ell=0,|m|\leq\ell}^{N}\quad\in\mathbb{C}^{\dim(\Pi_{N})\times 4n^{2}}.

(5.3)

Using such a matrix notation, the problem (
RPn
) can be transformed to the following finite dimensional basis pursuit problem

\min\limits_{c\in\mathbb{C}^{4n^{2}}}\|c\|_{1},\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad{\bf Y_{N}}\,c=y,

(dicRP
n
)

where
y=\big(\langle{Y_{m}^{\ell},\mu^{*}}\rangle\big)_{\ell=0,|m|\leq\ell}^{N}
is the given data, and
c\in\mathbb{C}^{4n^{2}}
represents the vector of coefficients of the spherical harmonics. Since we measure is supported only on a few points of the grid
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we need to impose sparsity of
c
, which can be done by minimizing the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
c
.

Due to the well-known basis mismatch phenomenon, a completely sparse solution of (
dicRPn
) can not be obtained and one needs a certain threshold in absolute value keeping only entries that are large enough, i.e. one keeps only points with solution
c^{\star}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
such that
|c^{\star}|>thresh
. After that one can see that recover grid points cluster around the support of
\mu^{\star}
. To find the centers of such cluster, and correspondingly the support of
\mu^{\star}
we use an algorithm known as the bivariate
kernel density estimator
, for more details see
[
12
]
, with the normal kernel
\displaystyle K_{N}({\bf x})=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf x}/h\|^{2}_{2}\right)}
and a scaling parameter
h>0
.
Combining all above described steps results in our second algorithm based on the discretized optimization problem (
RPn
).

For the asymptotic estimates, we will use the property

and component-wise estimates of the expressions
|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}

\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)=2\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega),

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)&=\frac{1}{2(n+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\left((n+1)\sin((n+1)\omega)\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{2\cos(\omega/2)\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}\right)\\

and

|\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)|\leq\frac{1.5}{(n+1)\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}.

Since the Fejér kernel can be written as

\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(n+1)}\left(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\right)\cos(k\omega),

\begin{split}\frac{\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)}{\sin\omega}&=\frac{1}{2(n+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\left((n+1)\frac{\sin((n+1)\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{2\cos{(\omega/2)}\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega)\sin(\omega/2)}\right),\\

Then the boundedness

\left|\frac{\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\lim\limits_{\omega\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}=\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}(0).

k^{2}\cos(k\omega)-\frac{k\sin(k\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\leq 0,

\displaystyle=k^{2}-\frac{k\sin(k\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin(d(x,z))}

\displaystyle\leq k^{2}(1-\cos(k\omega))\leq k^{4}\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}.

\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\left(-2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{2n}c_{k}k\sin(k\omega)\right)

c_{k}

\left|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)-\frac{\cos(\omega)\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}|\omega|^{2}

In the same way, one derives

|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)}{2}|\omega|^{2}.

\widetilde{F}_{n}

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)&=\frac{1}{2(n+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\Big((n+1)^{2}\Big((2+\cos(\omega))\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)+\cos((n+1)\omega)\Big)\\
&-2(n+1)(1+\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos(\omega))\Big),\end{split}

then we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\left((n+1)^{2}\Big(2(1+\cos(\omega))\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\right.

(A.2)

\displaystyle\left.+\cos((n+1)\omega)\Big)-(n+1)\Big(2+3\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos(\omega))\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega/2)}\left((n+1)^{2}\left(2(1+\cos(\omega)\right)\cdot\right.

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(\omega))}{n+1}\right)+(n+1)^{2}\cos((n+1)\omega)

\displaystyle\left.-(n+1)\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos(\omega))\right).

Using the above derived representations yields the following estimate

\left|G_{2}(\omega)\right|=\left|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{14.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}|\omega|^{2}}

and similar result for the second derivative

\left|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\right|\leq\frac{12.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}|\omega|^{2}}.

For

\left|G_{2}(\omega)\right|=\left|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)-\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}(0)}{2}w^{2}.

\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)=2\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(0)=-\frac{4}{(n+1)}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(k^{2}-\frac{k^{3}}{(n+1)}\right)=\frac{n(n+2)}{3}.

G_{3}(\omega)=\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}.

Observe, that for
\omega=\pi
this expression vanishes. However, for
\omega\neq\pi
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}&=2\left(\left(\frac{\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)}{\sin(\omega)}\right)\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\right.\\
&+\left.\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\left(\frac{\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right)\right),\end{split}

with

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)&\left(\frac{(\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\cos(\omega))}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right)=\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{2(n+1)^{4}\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\times\\
&\left(2(n+1)^{2}\frac{(1+\cos{(\omega)})}{\sin(\omega)}\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(w))}{n+1}\right)\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left.+(n+1)\frac{(n+1)\cos((n+1)\omega)-\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos(\omega))}{\sin(\omega)}\right).\end{split}

Here the first the right-hand side summand can be represented as

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{(n+1)^{2}\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\cdot\frac{(1+\cos{(\omega)})}{\sin(\omega)}\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(w))}{n+1}\right)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\left|\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{(n+1)}\frac{\cos(\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(w))}{n+1}\right)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\left|\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{(n+1)\sin(\omega/2)}\right|\times\\
&\left|\sin((n+1)\omega/2)\cos(\omega/2)\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(w))}{n+1}\right)\right|\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)}}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\left|\sin((n+1)\omega/2)\cos(\omega/2)\left(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)-\frac{U_{n}(\cos(w))}{n+1}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{2}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}.\end{split}

For the second summand, due to the derivative representation of Chebychev polynomials, we have

\frac{(n+1)\cos((n+1)\omega)-\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos{(\omega)})}{\sin(\omega)}=-\sin(\omega)U^{\prime}_{n}(\cos(\omega)).

Using the Bernstein inequality for algebraic polynomials, i.e.

|P_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{n}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\|P_{n}\|_{\infty},\quad-1<x<1,

for a polynomial of degree
n
, see e.g.
[
3
]
, we derive

\left|\frac{(n+1)\cos((n+1)\omega)-\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos{(\omega)})}{\sin(\omega)}\right|\leq n\|U_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq(n+1)^{2}.

Hence, the second summand can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sin^{2}((n+1)\omega/2)}{(n+1)^{2}\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\cdot(n+1)\cdot\frac{(n+1)\cos((n+1)\omega)-\cos(\omega)U_{n}(\cos(\omega))}{\sin(\omega)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{0.5}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}.

Together, this yields

\left|\widetilde{F}_{n}\left(\frac{(\omega)(\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\cos(\omega))}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right)\right|\leq\frac{2.5}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}

and consequently we get an estimate for
G_{3}
of the form

|G_{3}(\omega)|=\left|\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{8}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}.

Again, for
|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}
, we estimate the expression component-wise. Observe, that

\left|\frac{k^{2}\cos(k\omega)\sin(\omega)-k\sin(k\omega)\cos(\omega)}{/sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|\frac{1}{1+\cos(\omega)}k^{3}\sin(k\omega)\leq\frac{k^{4}|w|}{1+\cos(\omega)}.

For this reason, we have for
|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)\sin(\omega)-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)\cos(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|}{1+\cos(\omega)}\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|}{1+\cos({\pi}/{(4(n+1))})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|}{1+\cos(\pi/8)}\leq 0.52\cdot\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|

It can be also shown that the following inequality holds

\left|\frac{k^{2}\cos(k\omega)\cos(\omega)\sin(\omega)-k\sin(k\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{1+\cos(\omega)}k^{3}\sin(k\omega)\leq\frac{k^{4}|\omega|}{1+\cos(\omega)},

and thus

\left|\frac{\widetilde{J}^{\prime}_{N}(\omega)-\cos(\omega)\sin(\omega)\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)}{\sin^{2}(\omega)}\right|\leq 0.52\cdot\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|

Similarly, we can compute the third derivative of
\widetilde{J}_{N}
, that is

\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)=2\Big(3\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)+\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)\Big).

Writing explicitly the first and second summand, we get

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\left(-\frac{1}{2(n+1)}\cos(\omega/2)\sin((n+1)\omega/2)\cdot\right.\\
U_{n}(\cos(\omega/2))\big((5+\cos{(\omega)})\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)+3\cos((n+1)\omega)\big)+\frac{1}{4}\sin((n+1)\omega)\cdot\\
\left.\big(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)(6+3\cos(\omega))+\cos((n+1)\omega)-1\big)\right)\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{3(n+1)\sin^{4}(\omega/2)}\left(-\frac{3}{2(n+1)}\cos(\omega/2)\sin((n+1)\omega/2)\cdot\right.\\
U_{n}(\cos(\omega/2))\big((2+\cos(\omega))\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)+3\cos((n+1)\omega)+2\big)+\frac{3}{4}\sin((n+1)\omega)\cdot\\
\left.\big(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)(4+3\cos(x))+\cos((n+1)\omega)\big)\right).\end{split}

Then using the triangle inequality, we can obtain the estimate for
\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}
, that is

|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\frac{50.5\cdot\pi^{4}}{(n+1)|w|^{4}}.

With
\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(\pi)=0
, we get for
|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{4(n+1)}
,

|\widetilde{J}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{N}(\omega)|\leq\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)|\omega|.

The fourth derivative can be written as

\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(\omega)=2\Big(3(\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega))^{2}+4\widetilde{F}^{\prime}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n}(\omega)+\widetilde{F}_{n}(\omega)\widetilde{F}^{(4)}_{n}(\omega)\Big)

and therefore we obtain

\widetilde{J}^{(4)}_{N}(0)=2\Big(3(\widetilde{F}_{n}(0))^{2}+\widetilde{F}^{(4)}_{n}(0)\Big).

Now, since for
\omega=0
one has

\widetilde{F}^{(4)}_{n}(0)=\frac{2}{n+1}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(k^{4}-\frac{k^{5}}{n+1}\right)=\frac{(n(n+2))}{30}(2n(n+2)-1),

we easily get the value

\widetilde{F}^{(4)}_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{30}n(n+2)(9n(n+2)-2).

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma

Proof.

For

, with

for some

, we define the ring around

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. As it have been shows in
[
16
]
, we can estimate the number of elements in the intersection of
S_{m}
with the set
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}
for
m\geq 1
by

\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\cap}S_{m})\leq 25m.

Thus, it remains to estimate the number of elements in
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}S_{0}
. We are going to use the same argument as in
[
16
]
, namely, we see that for
x_{i},x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}\in\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}S_{0}
one has
{B_{\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}}(x_{i}){\displaystyle\cap}B_{\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}}(x_{n})=\emptyset}
and

\displaystyle\cup_{x_{i}\in\{x_{j}\}{\cap}S_{0}}B_{\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}}(x_{i})\subseteq B_{\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}}(x_{j}).

Since
\varepsilon\leq 1/2
and the Riemannian volume form is rotation invariant, we can bound the number of elements by

\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}S_{0})\leq 2\frac{\Omega(B_{\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}}(e_{3}))}{\Omega(B_{\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}}(e_{3}))}

where

is the north pole on the sphere. In polar coordinates around

, we consequently have the bound

\begin{split}\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}S_{0})&\leq\frac{\Omega(B_{\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}}(e_{3}))}{\Omega(B_{\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}}(e_{3}))}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}}\sin(r)\mathrm{d}r}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}}\sin(r)\mathrm{d}r}\\
&=\frac{1-\cos{\left(\frac{3\nu}{2(n+1)}\right)}}{1-\cos{\left(\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}\right)}}=\left(1+2\cos{\left(\frac{\nu}{2(n+1)}\right)}\right)^{2}\leq 9.\end{split}

Due to

for

), we can therefore estimate

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}}|f(x,x_{i})|&\leq\sum\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}S_{0}}\frac{c_{f}}{((n+1)d(x,x_{i}))^{s}}\\

where the last inequality follows by the definition of the Zeta function. On the other hand, we can define the ring around
x_{j}
again by

\widetilde{S}_{m}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\nu m}{n+1}\leq d(x_{j},y)\leq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\nu(m+1)}{n+1}\right\}.

d(x,x_{j})\leq\varepsilon\frac{\nu}{n+1}
, we have

for

3.10

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}}|f(x,x_{i})|&\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{j}\}{\displaystyle\cap}\widetilde{S}_{m}}\frac{c_{f}}{((n+1)d(x,x_{i}))^{s}}\\

Afterwards, we choose

Appendix C
Properties of the Cross Product

Let us consider two vectors
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then the cross product is given by the vector

a\times b=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}b_{3}-a_{3}b_{2}\\
a_{3}b_{1}-a_{1}b_{3}\\
a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have the following relations between the dot product ”
\cdot
” and the cross product ”
\times
”:

(i)

a\times b=-(b\times a)
,

(ii)

a\cdot(b\times c)=b\cdot(c\times a)=c\cdot(a\times b)
,

(iii)

a\times(b\times c)=b(a\cdot c)-c(a\cdot b)
,

(iv)

(a\times b)\times(a\times c)=(a\cdot(b\times c))a
,

(v)

(a\times b)\cdot(c\times d)=(a\cdot c)(b\times d)-(a\cdot d)(b\times c)
.
