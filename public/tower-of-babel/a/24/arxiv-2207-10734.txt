On exponential splitting methods for semilinear abstract Cauchy problems
Bálint Farkas, Birgit Jacob, Merlin Schmitz

Abstract
Due to the seminal works of Hochbruck and Ostermann [ 21 ] , [ 22 ] exponential splittings are well established numerical methods utilizing operator semigroup theory for the treatment of semilinear evolution equations whose principal linear part involves a sectorial operator with angle greater than π 2 \frac{\pi}{2} (meaning essentially the holomorphy of the underlying semigroup). The present paper contributes to this subject by relaxing on the sectoriality condition, but in turn requiring that the semigroup operators act consistently on an interpolation couple (or on a scale of Banach spaces). Our conditions (on the semigroup and on the semilinearity) are inspired by the approach of T. Kato [ 25 ] to the local solvability of the Navier-Stokes equation, where the L p \mathrm{L}^{p} - L r \mathrm{L}^{r} smoothing of the Stokes semigroup was fundamental. The present abstract operator theoretic result is applicable for this latter problem (as was already the result of Ostermann and Hochbruck), or more generally in the setting of [ 21 ] , but also allows the consideration of examples, such as non-analytic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups or the Navier-Stokes flow around rotating bodies.

Semilinear Cauchy problems, exponential splitting methods, convergence order,

-semigroups, scales of Banach spaces

Introduction

We investigate the semilinear Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)=Au(t)+g(t,u(t)),\quad u(0)=u_{0},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1)

where
[0,T]
is a given time interval,
A\colon D(A)\subset X\to X
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
X
,
u_{0}\in D(A)
and
g\colon[0,T]\times V\rightarrow X
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function, where
V
is a Banach space as well. The precise conditions on
A
and
g
are described below.
We assume that the linear problem
\dot{u}(t)=Au(t)
is well-posed, that is
A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup, and we suppose that the solutions can be calculated effectively with high precision (or are explicitly known). In this article we prove convergence estimates for the so-called
exponential splitting methods
. This is a particular
operator splitting method
, that is, a general procedure for finding (numerical) solutions of complicated evolution equations by reduction to subproblems, whose solutions are then to be combined in order to recover the (approximate) solution of the compound problem.
The literature both on the functional and the numerical analysis sides are extremely extensive, see, e.g., the surveys
[
32
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
. The decomposition of the compound problem can be based on various things, such as: on physical grounds (say, separating advection and diffusion phenomena, e.g.,
[
24
]
), by mathematical-structural reasons (separating linear and non-linear parts, see e.g.,
[
21
,
22
]
,
[
19
]
; separating the history and present in case of delay equations, see
[
2
]
), etc. The starting point for exponential splitting methods is the definition of the mild solution of problem (
1
), that is the variation-of-constants formula:

\displaystyle u(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tA}u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{(t-\tau)A}g(\tau,u(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2)

First, we describe the method introduced by Hochbruck and Ostermann in
[
21
,
22
]
in the case when
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
is an analytic semigroup, see also
[
20
]
.
An
exponential integrator
is a time stepping method and it approximates the convolution term on the right-side by a suitable quadrature rule in a given time step, where the effect of the linear propagator is not approximated but inserted precisely. Thus, for given time step
h>0
and
t_{n}\coloneqq nh
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
nh\leq T=Nh
, the solution
u(t_{n})
of the semilinear equation (
1
), given recursively by

\displaystyle u(t_{n+1})=\mathrm{e}^{hA}u(t_{n})+\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}g(t_{n}+\tau,u(t_{n}+\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(3)

is approximated by the
s
-stage Runge-Kutta approximation
u_{n}
, which is subject to the recursion

u_{n+1}=\mathrm{e}^{hA}u_{n}+\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(the initial value
u_{0}=u(0)
is known).
Here
s
is a positive integer,
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in[0,1]
are pairwise distinct and
(U_{n,i})_{i=1,\dots,s}
are defined as the solution of the integral equation

\displaystyle U_{n,i}=\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u_{n}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(4)

The values
U_{n,i}
provide an approximation of
u(t_{n}+c_{i}h)
for
n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
at internal steps and

\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s}
are Lagrange interpolation polynomials with nodes
c_{1}h,\dots,c_{s}h\in[0,h]
, thus
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})
yields an approximation of
g(t_{n}+\tau,u(t_{n}+\tau))
for
\tau\in[0,h]
. We require the following conditions on the semilinear Cauchy problem (
1
).

Assumption 1.1

(The linear setting)
.

1.

A\colon D(A)\subset X\to X
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
X
and
u_{0}\in X
.

2.

(X,V)
is an interpolation couple, that is, also
V
is a Banach space,
V
and
X
are (continuously) embedded in a topological vector space
\mathcal{X}
. We assume moreover that for each

the linear operator
\mathrm{e}^{tA}
leaves

invariant and extends to a linear operator
\mathrm{e}^{tA}\in L(V)\cap L(X,V)
with
M\coloneqq\max_{t\in[0,T]}\{\|\mathrm{e}^{tA}\|_{L(X)},\|\mathrm{e}^{tA}\|_{L(V)}\}<\infty
.

3.

W
satisfies the same condition as
V
above. (Interesting will be the case
W\in\{X,V\}
.)

4.

There is a continuous, non-increasing function
\rho_{X}\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\rho_{X}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T)
such that

\|\mathrm{e}^{tA}\|_{L(X,V)}\leq\rho_{X}(t)\quad\text{for every $t\in(0,T]$}.

And similarly, there is a continuous, non-increasing function
\rho_{W}\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\rho_{W}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T)
such that

\|\mathrm{e}^{tA}\|_{L(W,V)}\leq\rho_{W}(t)\quad\text{for every $t\in(0,T]$}.

This set of conditions, together with the ones about the non-linearity (see Assumption
1.3
below), is inspired by T. Kato’s iteration scheme in his operator theoretic approach to the Navier-Stokes equations, see
[
25
]
and Example
3.2
below. He used the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
\mathrm{L}^{r}
smoothing of the linear Stokes semigroup to “compensate the unboundedness” of the nonlinearity, and thus could apply Banach’s fixed point theorem, just as it is required by the exponential splitting in the internal steps.

In the setting of Assumption
1.1
we clearly have
M\geq 1
, and if we set

\varOmega_{X}(h)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{h}\rho_{X}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\quad\text{and}\quad\varOmega_{W}(h)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{h}\rho_{W}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

then
\varOmega_{X}
,
\varOmega_{W}
is a monotone increasing, continuous function from

to

with
\varOmega_{X}(0)=\varOmega_{W}(0)=0
. Thus
\varOmega_{X}
and
\varOmega_{W}
are so-called

-functions. Moreover, we abbreviate
\rho\coloneqq\rho_{X}
and
\varOmega\coloneqq\varOmega_{X}
, and set

\displaystyle C_{\varOmega}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\Bigl\{h\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|\mathrm{e}^{khA}\|_{L(X,V)}\>\Big|\>0<nh\leq T\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\Bigl\{h\sum_{k=1}^{n}\rho(kh)\>\Big|\>0<nh\leq T\Bigr\}\leq\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T)}<\infty.

The following example is motivated by the framework of the paper
[
21
]
by Hochbruck and Ostermann.

Example 1.2

(Bounded, analytic semigroups)
.

Let
A
generate a bounded, analytic
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
on
X
and suppose (without loss of generality) that
0\in\rho(A)
. For a fixed
\alpha\in[0,1)
we set

, the domain of the fractional power of
-A
, and equip it with the norm
\|v\|_{V}\coloneqq\|(-A)^{\alpha}v\|_{X}
. Since the semigroup operators commute with the powers of the generator we have
\|\mathrm{e}^{hA}\|_{L(V)}=\|\mathrm{e}^{hA}\|_{L(X)}
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{A}>0
such that for
h>0
we have

\|\mathrm{e}^{hA}\|_{L(X,V)}\leq C_{A}h^{-\alpha}.

(5)

(We refer to
[
14
, Ch. 3]
,
[
27
, Ch. 4]
, or
[
6
, Ch. 9]
for details concerning fractional powers of sectorial operators.)
Thus for this example
\rho(h)\leq C_{A}h^{-\alpha}
and
\rho\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,1)
. Further,
\varOmega(h)\leq\frac{C_{A}}{1-\alpha}h^{1-\alpha}
and

C_{\varOmega}\leq C_{A}\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}
.

We remark that the fractional powers for negative generators of not necessarily analytic
C_{0}
-semigroups can be also defined, see,
[
26
]
and e.g.,
[
8
, Sec. II.5.c]
, but the validity of an estimate as in (
5
) for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
implies analyticity of the semigroup, see
[
26
, Thm. 12.2]
.

More examples, also for non-analytic semigroups, are provided in Section
3
below.

Assumption 1.3

(Properties of the solution)
.

1.

The semilinear Cauchy problem (
1
) has a unique mild solution
u
, that is,
u\colon[0,T]\rightarrow X
and

are continuous and

satisfies the integral equation (

).

2.

Let

and

(6)

3.

The composition

satisfies

for a given natural number

. Note that

equals the set of absolutely continuous functions.

Remark 1.4
.

If

is uniformly Lipschitz and bounded in the second variable on bounded sets in

, i.e., for each
B\subseteq V
bounded there is

such that
for all

and

\displaystyle\|g(t,v)-g(t,w)\|_{X}\leq L_{B}\|v-w\|_{V},

and the solution

is bounded, then Assumption
1.3
.
2
is satisfied.

.

Suppose Assumption

) satisfy Assumption

. Then there exists a constant

Proof of Theorem

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

We remark that for

Lemma 2.1

Let

,

\displaystyle MC\mathfrak{h}^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n,\mathfrak{h})+\varOmega(\mathfrak{h})(sC_{\ell}+1)\max_{(t,y)\in S_{r}}\|g(t,y)\|_{X}\leq r\quad\text{and}\quad\varOmega(\mathfrak{h})C_{\ell}sL<1.

\displaystyle\|u_{n}-u(t_{n})\|_{V}\leq Ch^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n,h).

Then, the equation (

Proof.

The main idea is to show existence of

For

.
Further, let

\displaystyle(\Phi_{h}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{s}))_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u_{n}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

First, we show that

. Indeed this follows from the calculation

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{h}(x)_{i}-u(t_{n}+c_{i}h)\|_{V}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u_{n}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt-\left.\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u(t_{n})-\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}g(t_{n}+\tau,u(t_{n}+\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq MCh^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n,h)+\varOmega(h)(sC_{\ell}+1)\max_{(s,y)\in S_{r}}\|g(s,y)\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq r.

Next, we show that

Since

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{h}(x)-\Phi_{h}(\tilde{x})\|_{V^{s}}

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,s\}}\|(\Phi_{h}(x))_{i}-(\Phi_{h}(\tilde{x})_{i}\|_{V}

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,s\}}\left\|\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)\bigl(g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,x_{j})-g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,\tilde{x}_{j})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})-g(t_{n}+\tau,u(t_{n}+\tau))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)\Bigl(g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})-f(t_{n}+c_{j}h)+f(t_{n}+c_{j}h)-f(t_{n}+\tau)\Bigr)

Taylor expansion yields

\displaystyle f(t_{n}+c_{j}h)-f(t_{n}+\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(t_{n}+\tau)}{k!}(c_{j}h-\tau)^{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{t_{n}+\tau}^{t_{n}+c_{j}h}\frac{(t_{n}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Recall the following property of Lagrange interpolation polynomials, see (
29
) in Lemma

: For
k\leq s-1

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)(c_{j}h-\tau)^{k}=0.

(16)

Inserting this into the equation (
15
) above finishes Step
2
as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s}

\displaystyle\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})-g(t_{n}+\tau,u(t_{n}+\tau))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)(g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})-f(t_{n}+c_{j}h))

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(t_{n}+\tau)}{k!}(c_{j}h-\tau)^{k}\right.+\left.\int_{t_{n}+\tau}^{t_{n}+c_{j}h}\frac{(t_{n}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)(g(t_{n}+c_{j}h,U_{n,j})-f(t_{n}+c_{j}h))

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(t_{n}+\tau)}{k!}\underset{\hskip 16.38895pt=\,0\text{ by \eqref{eq:lagrangeid}}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)(c_{j}h-\tau)^{k}}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)\int_{t_{n}+\tau}^{t_{n}+c_{j}h}\frac{(t_{n}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Step 2.
Let
\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}
and
\delta_{k+1}
,
k=0,\dots,n
, be given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(g(t_{k}+c_{i}h,U_{k,i})-f(t_{k}+c_{i}h))\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(17)

\displaystyle\delta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)\int_{t_{k}+\tau}^{t_{k}+c_{i}h}\frac{(t_{k}+c_{i}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(18)

Then Step
2
implies

\displaystyle e_{n+1}

\displaystyle=u_{n+1}-u(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{hA}e_{n}+\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)\left(g(t_{n}+c_{i}h,U_{n,i})-f(t_{n}+\tau)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{hA}e_{n}+\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(g(t_{n}+c_{i}h,U_{n,i})-f(t_{n}+c_{i}h))\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt+\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-\tau)A}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)\int_{t_{n}+\tau}^{t_{n}+c_{i}h}\frac{(t_{n}+c_{i}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{hA}e_{n}+\tilde{\delta}_{n+1}+\delta_{n+1}.

Solving the recursion yields:

\displaystyle e_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{hA}e_{n}+\tilde{\delta}_{n+1}+\delta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{hA}\left(\mathrm{e}^{hA}e_{n-1}+\tilde{\delta}_{n}+\delta_{n}\right)+\tilde{\delta}_{n+1}+\delta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{khA}\left(\tilde{\delta}_{n+1-k}+\delta_{n+1-k}\right),

as
e_{0}=0
. Hence

\displaystyle\left\|e_{n+1}\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\|\mathrm{e}^{khA}\tilde{\delta}_{n+1-k}\|_{V}+\|\mathrm{e}^{khA}\delta_{n+1-k}\|_{V})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}\|_{V}+\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\delta_{k+1}\|_{V}).

(19)

We will estimate the norms on the right-hand in (
19
) side separately.

Step 3.
We start by bounding the Taylor-remainders for each fixed
i
.
For
k=0,\ldots,n
and
i=1,\ldots,s
we define

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,i}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)\int_{t_{k}+\tau}^{t_{k}+c_{j}h}\frac{(t_{k}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(20)

and estimate

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{k,i}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\|\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\|_{L(W,V)}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|\ell_{j}(\tau)\right|\left\|\int_{t_{k}+\tau}^{t_{k}+c_{j}h}\frac{(t_{k}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}f^{(s)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right\|_{W}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\sum_{j=1}^{s}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k}+h}\left|\frac{(t_{k}+c_{j}h-\xi)^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}\right|\|f^{(s)}(\xi)\|_{W}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqn:frac}}{\leq}C_{\ell}sC_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)}.

(21)

Step 4.

We now consider the norm of
\delta_{k+1}
(see (
18
)).
We estimate as in Step
2

\displaystyle\|\delta_{k+1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell}sC_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)},

and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\delta_{k+1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}MC_{\ell}sC_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{G,2}h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h).

(22)

Step 5.
We prove

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\|E_{k,i}\|_{V}\leq 2sM\|e_{k}\|_{V}+2C_{\ell}s^{2}C_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)}.

(23)

Thanks to Step
2
we can calculate

\displaystyle E_{k,i}=U_{k,i}-u(t_{k}+c_{i}h)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u_{k}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)g(t_{k}+c_{j}h,U_{k,j})\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}u(t_{k})+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}g(t_{k}+\tau,u(t_{k}+\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{c_{i}hA}e_{k}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}(\tau)(g(t_{k}+c_{j}h,U_{k,j})-f(t_{k}+c_{j}h))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\Delta_{k,i},

where
\Delta_{k,i}
is given by (
20
).
Using the Lipschitz-continuity of

on
S_{r}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|E_{k,i}\right\|_{V}\leq M\|e_{k}\|_{V}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}h}\left\|\mathrm{e}^{(c_{i}h-\tau)A}\right\|_{L(X,V)}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|\ell_{j}(\tau)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\underset{\leq\,L\cdot\|E_{k,j}\|_{V}\,\text{ by }\eqref{eqn:lipschitz}}{\underbrace{\|g(t_{k}+c_{j}h,U_{k,j})-g(t_{k}+c_{j}h,u(t_{k}+c_{j}h))\|_{X}}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\left\|\Delta_{k,i}\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq M\|e_{k}\|_{V}+\varOmega(h_{0})C_{\ell}L\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left\|E_{k,j}\right\|_{V}+\left\|\Delta_{k,i}\right\|_{V}.

Thus, using
\varOmega(h_{0})C_{\ell}sL\leq\frac{1}{2}
we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\|E_{k,i}\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq sM\|e_{k}\|_{V}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left\|E_{k,j}\right\|_{V}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\|\Delta_{k,i}\|_{V}.

This together with (
21
) implies (
23
).

Step 6.

For

, we now investigate the norm of
\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}
(see (

)).
Using (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\|_{V}\leq\varOmega(h)C_{\ell}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}\|E_{k,i}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\varOmega(h)C_{\ell}L\left(2sM\|e_{k}\|_{V}+2C_{\ell}s^{2}C_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)}\right).

and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\|_{X}\leq MhC_{\ell}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}\|E_{n,i}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq MhC_{\ell}L\left(2sM\|e_{k}\|_{V}+2C_{\ell}s^{2}C_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],W)}\right).

Thus, using
\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}h\leq C_{\varOmega}

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{\delta}_{n+1}\|_{V}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}\|\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\varOmega(h)C_{\ell}L\left(2sM\|e_{n}\|_{V}+2C_{\ell}s^{2}C_{F}h^{s-1}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([t_{n},t_{n+1}],W)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+MhC_{\ell}L2sM\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}\|e_{k}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\quad+2MhC_{\ell}LC_{\ell}s^{2}C_{F}h^{s-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{\tau A}\|_{L(W,V)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([0,t_{n}],W)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{G,1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}h\|e_{k}\|_{V}+C_{G,1}\|e_{n}\|_{V}+\frac{1}{2}C_{G,2}h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h).

(24)

Step 7.
In the final step we estimate
e_{n+1}
.
Using (

), (

) and (

)
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|e_{n+1}\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\tilde{\delta}_{k+1}\|_{V}+\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\delta_{k+1}\|_{V})

\displaystyle\leq C_{G,1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}h\|e_{k}\|_{V}+C_{G,1}\|e_{n}\|_{V}+C_{G,2}h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h),

and Gronwall’s Lemma (see Theorem
B.1
) implies

\displaystyle\|e_{n+1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq C_{G,2}(1+C_{G,1})\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+C_{G,1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}h)h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h)

\displaystyle\leq C_{G,2}\exp\Bigl(C_{G,1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\mathrm{e}^{(n-k)hA}\|_{L(X,V)}h+C_{G,1}\Bigr)h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h)

\displaystyle\leq C_{G,2}\exp\Bigl(C_{G,1}C_{\Omega}+C_{G,1}\Bigr)h^{s-1}C_{f,W}(n+1,h)

\displaystyle=C\varOmega_{W}(h)h^{s-1}\|f^{(s)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}([0,t_{n+1}],W)}.

By Lemma
2.1
applied to
n+1
, for
h\in(0,\mathfrak{h})
if
(n+1)h\leq T
equation (
4
) has a unique solution
U_{n+1,1},\ldots,U_{n+1,s}\in V
satisfying
(t_{n+1}+c_{j}h,U_{n+1,j})\in S_{r}
. ∎

3
Examples

We present here examples for the situation described in Assumption
1.1
and also for some admissible non-linearities satisfying Assumption
1.3
.

Example 3.1

(Gaussian heat semigroup)
.

Consider the Gaussian heat semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
t>0
given as

\mathrm{e}^{tA}f\coloneqq g_{t}*f,

where
g_{t}(x)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is the Gaussian kernel. Then, actually,
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
yields a consistent family of analytic
C_{0}
-semigroups on the whole
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-scale,
p\in[1,\infty)
. A short calculation using the Young convolution inequality yields for
1<p\leq r<\infty
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that

\|\mathrm{e}^{tA}f\|_{r}\leq c_{p,r}t^{-\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})}\|f\|_{p}

with an absolute constant
c_{p,r}
(whose optimal value can be determined, cf.
[
3
]
). As usual, we shall refer to this phenomenon as
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
\mathrm{L}^{r}
-smoothing. We conclude that the choices
X=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
V=\mathrm{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\varOmega(h)=c_{p,r}h^{1-\alpha}

with
\alpha=\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})
are admissible choices in Assumption
1.1
, provided
\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})<1
. For similar estimates in case of symmetric, Markov semigroups we refer, e.g., to
[
7
, Ch. 2]
.

Example 3.2

(Stokes semigroup)
.

Similarly to the foregoing examples,
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
\mathrm{L}^{r}
smoothing is valid for the Stokes semigroup on the divergence free space
\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, so
X=\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
,
V=\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
and the
\varOmega
from Example
3.1
are admissible in Assumption
1.1
, see
[
25
]
.

Example 3.3

(Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a positive semidefinite matrix and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Suppose that the positive semidefinite matrix

Q_{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{sB}Q\mathrm{e}^{sB^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}s

is invertible for some
t>0
(for this, a sufficient but not necessary, assumption is that
Q
itself is invertible). Then
Q_{t}
is invertible for all
t>0
, see
[
38
, Ch. 1]
. Consider the Kolmogorov kernel

k_{t}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}\langle Q_{t}^{-1}x,x\rangle},

and for
t>0
the operator
S(t)
defined by

S(t)f(x)\coloneqq(k_{t}*f)(\mathrm{e}^{tB}x)=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}\langle Q_{t}^{-1}y,y\rangle}f(\mathrm{e}^{tB}x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Then by Young’s convolution inequality, we see that
S(t)
acts indeed on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for each fixed
p\in[1,\infty)
, it is linear and bounded with

\|S(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{p})}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(B)}{p}t}.

Setting
S(0)=I
, we obtain a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, called the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup, see, e.g.,
[
31
]
for details. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is, in general, not analytic on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
[
33
]
and
[
10
]
).
Similarly to Example
3.1
for
r\geq p
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have for
t>0
that

\|S(t)f\|_{r}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(B)}{r}t}\|k_{t}*f\|_{r}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(B)}{r}t}\|k_{t}\|_{q}\|f\|_{p},

with
1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
. We can calculate

\displaystyle\|k_{t}\|^{q}_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{qd}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{q}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{q}{4}\langle Q_{t}^{-1}y,y\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{q^{\frac{d}{2}}(4\pi)^{\frac{qd}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{q}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}\langle Q_{t}^{-1}y,y\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}y

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{q^{\frac{d}{2}}(4\pi)^{\frac{qd}{2}}\det(Q_{t})^{\frac{q}{2}}}=c_{q}\det(Q_{t})^{-\frac{q}{2}(1-\frac{1}{q})}=c_{q}\det(Q_{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})}.

If
Q
is invertible, then we have
\|Q_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, see, e.g.,
[
28
]
(but also below).
Since
\det(Q_{t}^{-1})\leq C\|Q_{t}^{-1}\|^{d}
, we obtain
\det(Q_{t})\geq C^{\prime}t^{-d}
, which, when inserted into (
25
), yields

\|k_{t}\|_{q}\leq c_{p,r,Q}t^{-\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})},

for some constant
c_{p,r,Q}
depending on
p,r,Q
. This result, for invertible
Q
is essentially contained in
[
18
]
(or
[
15
]
in an even more general situation of evolution families, see also
[
13
]
). It follows that
X=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
V=\mathrm{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\varOmega(h)=c_{p,r,Q}h^{1-\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})},

are admissible choices in Assumption
1.1
provided
r\geq p
,
\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})<1
.

Now if
Q
is not necessarily invertible, but for some/all
t>0
the matrix
Q_{t}
is non-singular, then
there is a minimal integer
n>0
such that

[Q^{\frac{1}{2}},BQ^{\frac{1}{2}},B^{2}Q^{\frac{1}{2}},\dots,B^{n-1}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}]\quad\mbox{has rank $d$,}

see, for example,
[
38
, Ch. 1]
(if
Q
is invertible, then
n=1
).
One can show that in this case
\|Q_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq Ct^{\frac{1}{2}-n}
(for
t
near
0
), see,
[
29
, Lemma 3.1]
(and, e.g.,
[
36
,
9
]
), hence

\|k_{t}\|_{q}\leq ct^{-\frac{d(2n-1)}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})},

i.e.,
X=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
V=\mathrm{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\varOmega(h)=c_{p,r,Q}h^{1-\alpha}

with
\alpha=\frac{d(2n-1)}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})
are admissible choices in Assumption
1.1
provided
r\geq p
and
r
is near to
p
. Similar results hold for (strongly elliptic) Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operators on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
,
\Omega
an exterior domain with smooth boundary, see
[
12
]
.

Example 3.4

(Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups on Sobolev space)
.

Consider again the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
S
from the foregoing example, given by
S(t)f(x)\coloneqq(k_{t}*f)(\mathrm{e}^{tB}x)
for
t\geq 0
,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We have
\partial_{x}S(t)f(x)=(k_{t}*\partial_{x}f)(\mathrm{e}^{tB}x)\mathrm{e}^{tB}
, hence
S(t)
leaves
\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
invariant, and is locally uniformly bounded thereon. On the other hand
\partial_{x}S(t)f(x)=(\partial_{x}k_{t}*f)(\mathrm{e}^{tB}x)\mathrm{e}^{tB}
, and analogously to Example
3.3
one can prove that for
t>0
,
1\leq p\leq r
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\|\partial_{x}S(t)f\|_{r}\leq ct^{-\frac{d(2n-1)}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})+\frac{1}{2}-n}\|f\|_{p}.

So if
n=1
, i.e., in the elliptic case, we obtain that
V=\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\varOmega(h)=c_{p,r,Q}h^{1-\alpha}

with
\alpha=\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r})+\frac{1}{2}

are admissible choices in Assumption
1.1
provided
r\geq p
and
r
is near to
p
(if
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r}<\frac{1}{d}
).

Example 3.5

(Stokes operator with a drift)
.

Examples
3.2
and
3.3
can be combined. The operator
A
, defined by
Au(x)=\Delta u(x)+Mx\cdot\nabla u(x)-Mu(x)
(with appropriate domain) generates a (in general, non-analytic)
C_{0}
-semigroup on the divergence free spaces
\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{d}
,
1<p\leq r<\infty
subject to
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-
\mathrm{L}^{r}
-smoothing, with
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see
[
18
]

Example 3.9
.

Let

,

F\colon\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{\frac{p}{\alpha}}(U),\quad F(u)=\lvert u\rvert^{\alpha-1}u,

F^{\prime}(u)v=\lvert u\rvert^{\alpha-1}v+(\alpha-1)\lvert u\rvert^{\alpha-3}u\,\mathrm{Re}(u\bar{v})\qquad\text{for }u,v\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(U).

Example 3.10
.

The function
g\colon\mathrm{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\cap\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
,
g(u)=u\cdot\nabla u
is Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets if
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{s}
. Indeed, for
u,v\in\mathrm{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\cap\mathrm{W}^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
we can write by Hölder inequality that

\displaystyle\|u\cdot\nabla u-v\cdot\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\cdot\nabla u-u\cdot\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}+\|u\cdot\nabla v-v\cdot\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{s}}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}}+\|u-v\|_{s}\|v\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,r}}),

proving the asserted Lipschitz continuity.

Example 3.11

(Second order systems)
.

The result can be applied to second order problems via the following technique.

Consider the second order Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\ddot{w}(t)

\displaystyle=Aw(t)+g(t,w(t),\dot{w}(t))\quad,t\in[0,T]

(26)

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=w_{0}\in X,\quad\dot{w}(0)=w_{1}\in X

on a Banach space
X
, where
A\colon D(A)\subset X\to X
generates a Cosine function
(\mathrm{Cos}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.

The associated Sine function
\mathrm{Sin}\colon\mathbb{R}\to L(X)
is given by

\mathrm{Sin}(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{Cos}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

We assume that the system (
26
) has a classical solution
w\in C^{2}
. Sufficient conditions for the existence of solutions can be found in
[
37
]
.

Choosing
u=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}w\\
\dot{w}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
we can rewrite (
26
) as a first order problem

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)=\mathcal{A}u(t)+\tilde{g}(t,u(t)),\quad u(0)=u_{0},

where
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
A&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\tilde{g}(t,u(t))=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
g(t,w(t),\dot{w}(t))\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
u_{0}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}w_{0}\\
w_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

As stated in
[
1
, Thm. 3.14.11]
there exists a Banach space
V
such that
D(A)\hookrightarrow V\hookrightarrow X
and such that the part
\mathcal{A}
of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
A&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in
V\times X
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup given by

T(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Cos}(t)&\mathrm{Sin}(t)\\
A\mathrm{Sin}(t)&\mathrm{Cos}(t)\end{pmatrix}\quad,t\in\mathbb{R}.

We will illustrate this procedure with a short example which is presented in
[
23
, Ch. 9]
.

Consider the nonlinear wave equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions on a bounded open set
\emptyset\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
described by the system

\displaystyle\ddot{w}(t)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{D}w(t)-\alpha w(t)\lvert w(t)\rvert^{2},\quad t\in[0,T],

(27)

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=w_{0},\quad\dot{w}(0)=w_{1},

where
w_{0}\in D(\Delta_{D})\coloneqq\{u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(U)\mid\exists f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(U)\ \forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(U):\int_{U}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle f\mid v\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\}
,
w_{1}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(U)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
are given.

We can reformulate this as the semilinear system

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)=Au(t)+F(u(t)),\quad t\in[0,T],\quad u(0)=u_{0}

on the Hilbert space
X=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(U)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(U)
endowed with the norm given by
\|(u_{1},u_{2})\|^{2}=\|\lvert\nabla u_{1}\rvert\|_{2}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{2}^{2}
. Choose
u_{0}=(w_{0},w_{1})
and

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
\Delta_{D}&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{with }D(A)=D(\Delta_{D})\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(U),

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle=(0,-\alpha u_{1}\lvert u_{1}\rvert^{2})\eqqcolon(0,F_{0}(u_{1}))\;\text{for }u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in X.

As mentioned in Example
3.9
,
F_{0}\colon\mathrm{L}^{6}(U)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(U)
is real continuously differentiable and Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets. Since
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, Sobolev’s embedding yields
H_{0}^{1}(U)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{6}(U)
thus
F\colon X\to X
has the same properties.
Now Duhamel’s formula or the variation of constants in combination with the semigroup given above yield that the mild solution of (
27
) satisfies

w(t)=\mathrm{Cos}(t)w_{0}+\mathrm{Sin}(t)w_{1}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{Sin}(t-s)F_{0}(w(s))\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T],

on
H_{0}^{1}(U)
. Thus the main theorem is applicable with
s=1
.

Appendix A
Lagrange interpolation polynomials

In this section we summarize some useful facts on Lagrange interpolation polynomials.

For fixed
s\in\mathbb{N}
and different
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in[0,1]
denote by

\ell_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,s
the Lagrange basis polynomials with nodes
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}
, i.e.

\ell_{j}(\tau)=\prod_{j=1\atop m\neq j}^{s}\dfrac{\tau-c_{m}}{c_{j}-c_{m}}.

(If
s=1
, then
\ell_{1}\equiv 1
.)
Thus we have
\ell_{i}(c_{j})=1
if
i=j
, and
\ell_{i}(c_{j})=0
if
i\neq j
.

Lemma A.1
.

The Lagrange basis polynomials are of degree
s-1
and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)=1\quad\text{for all }\tau\in\mathbb{R},

(28)

and for every integer
k
with
1\leq k\leq s-1

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k}=\tau^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(c_{i}-\tau)^{k}=0\quad\text{for all }\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

(29)

(Note that for
s=1
the foregoing statement is vacuously true.)

Proof.

The polynomial
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)
is of degree at most
s-1
and equal
1
at
s
points
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}
. So equation (
28
) follows by the identity theorem.

We show equation (
29
) by induction over
k
. For
k=1
we can write by (
28
)

\displaystyle q(\tau)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(c_{i}-\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}-\tau\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}-\tau.

Since each
\ell_{i}
is of degree
s-1\geq 1
, the first expression on the right-hand side is of degree at most
s-1
. So
q
is polynomial of degree at most
s-1
with the
s
zeroes
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}
. By the identity theorem
q\equiv 0
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}=\tau.

Let
k<s-1
and suppose that

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(c_{i}-\tau)^{m}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{m}=\tau^{m}\quad\text{if }1\leq m\leq k.

We need to show that these hold also for
m=k+1
. By the binomial theorem

\displaystyle p(\tau)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)(c_{i}-\tau)^{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\binom{k+1}{j}c_{i}^{k+1-j}(-\tau)^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j}c_{i}^{k+1-j}(-\tau)^{j}+(-\tau)^{k+1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)

by (
28
) we can write

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j}(-\tau)^{j}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1-j}+(-\tau)^{k+1}

by the induction hypothesis we conclude

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j}(-\tau)^{j}\tau^{k+1-j}+(-\tau)^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1}+\tau^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j}(-1)^{j}+(-\tau)^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{i}(\tau)c_{i}^{k+1}-\tau^{k+1}.

Since
k<s-1
, the polynomial
p(\tau)
has degree at most
s-1
but
s
zeros
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}
. So that the identity theorem again yields that
p(\tau)\equiv 0
. By induction equality (
29
) holds for all
k\leq s-1
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
0\leq c_{1}<\cdots<c_{s}\leq 1
. There is a constant
C_{\ell}
such that for all
h>0
and for the Lagrange basis polynomials
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s}
with nodes
c_{1}h,\dots,c_{s}h
one has

|\ell_{i}(\tau)|\leq C_{\ell}\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,\dots,s\}\text{ and for all }\tau\in[0,h].

Note that the constant
C_{\ell}
depends only on the nodes
c_{1},\dots c_{s}
but not on
h
.

Proof.

The assertion follows directly from the definition, since
|\tau/h-c_{m}|\leq 1
for
\tau\in[0,h]
and
\min\{|c_{m}-c_{j}|:m\neq j\}>0
.
∎

Appendix B
Discrete Gronwall Inequality

The discrete Gronwall inequality will be crucial for the proof of our main result. We refer to
[
5
]
for a short proof, even in a more general case.

Theorem B.1

(Discrete Gronwall Inequality)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
a_{0},\dots,a_{N}\geq 0
,
b_{0},\dots,b_{N}\geq 0
and
z_{0},\dots,z_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
be given.
Suppose that for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}

z_{n}\leq a_{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}b_{j}z_{j}.

Then for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}

z_{n}\leq\left(\max_{j=0,\dots,n}a_{j}\right)\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+b_{j}).

Proof.

Here we recall the proof of the discrete Gronwall inequality from
[
5
]
: For
0\leq j\leq k\leq N
set

B_{k,j}\coloneqq\prod_{i=j}^{k-1}(1+b_{i})^{-1}

and notice that

B_{k,j+1}-B_{k,j}=\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}(1+b_{i})^{-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{1+b_{j}}\Bigr)=B_{k,j}b_{j}

and for
i\leq j\leq k

B_{k,j}B_{j,i}=B_{k,i}.

Let
m\in\{0,\dots,N\}
such that
z_{m}B_{m,0}=\max\{z_{j}B_{j,0}:j=0,\dots,N\}
.
By the assumption we have

\displaystyle z_{m}B_{m,0}

\displaystyle\leq aB_{m,0}+B_{m,0}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}b_{j}z_{j}=aB_{m,0}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}B_{m,j}B_{j,0}b_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle\leq aB_{m,0}+z_{m}B_{m,0}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}B_{m,j}b_{j}

\displaystyle=aB_{m,0}+z_{m}B_{m,0}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(B_{m,j+1}-B_{m,j})

\displaystyle=aB_{m,0}+z_{m}B_{m,0}(B_{m,m}-B_{m,0})=aB_{m,0}+z_{m}B_{m,0}-z_{m}B_{m,0}^{2}.

By rearranging we arrive at

\displaystyle z_{m}B_{m,0}

\displaystyle\leq a.

Since for each
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
one has
z_{n}B_{n,0}\leq z_{m}B_{m,0}\leq a
, the assertion is proved.
∎
