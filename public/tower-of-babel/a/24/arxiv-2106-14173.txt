Emergent non-Hermitian boundary contributions to charge pumping and electric polarization
K. Kyriakou, K. Moulopoulos

Abstract
The phenomenon of charge pumping and the modern theory of electric polarization are reconsidered by analytically taking into account emergent non-Hermitian contributions. These are accounted for through the use of an extended definition of the velocity operator and are determined by means of a dynamic Hellmann–Feynman theorem (DHFT) that we derive here for the first time. The DHFT introduces generalized Berry curvatures and it is valid for calculating observables non-perturbatively, hence with results valid to all orders of the external fields. By using the extended velocity operator we rigorously show how the charge pumping is linked up with the boundaries of the material (with the non-Hermiticity being essential for this connection), and by means of the DHFT we show that the well-known topological quantization of the pumped charge breaks down due to a non-integrable Aharonov-Anandan phase in driven non-equilibrium processes whenever the periodic gauge cannot be applied to the Floquet-Bloch states. Likewise, we show that the electronic polarization change has an additional non-Hermitian contribution, which is overlooked in the modern theory of electric polarization. The non-Hermitian contribution is by definition a bulk quantity that may equally be evaluated as a boundary quantity due to a symmetric structure that allows the bulk integration to be transformed into a boundary one. This non-Hermitian contribution is very sensitive to the realistic boundary conditions imposed on the wavefunctions and it is therefore expected to be significant in biased insulators where charge accumulation over their boundaries is present during the process that causes the polarization change. Finally, we show how a well-defined surface-charge theorem can be formulated in terms of the boundary non-Hermitian contribution.



I
Introduction

Boundary effects, combined with the involvement of dynamics and topological concepts have become the cornerstones of the quantum mechanical description of topological condensed matter systems
Roy and Harper 2017
;
Caio et al. 2015
;
Kitagawa et al. 2010
.
The interplay between bulk and boundary contributions within a quantum system
Qi et al. 2006
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Hatsugai and Fukui 2016
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
, seems to be far less clear than the counterpart in a classical system. A simple quantum theoretical framework that, at the same time takes into account the dynamics, the bulk and boundary contributions of an observable, together with the use of topological concepts, seems to be absent.
In the theoretical studies of charge pumping and electric polarization, dynamics and bulk contributions, as well as topological concepts, are always explicitly involved. It would therefore be a desirable theoretical advance, if a simple theoretical method could be given that takes into account all involved aspects, together with explicit boundary contributions, in a unified formulation. The present article proposes precisely such an advance in an analytically careful and rigorous manner.

The central quantity to be calculated in charge pumping studies is the electrons’ collective center of mass displacement

{\displaystyle\Delta\!\left\langle\mathbf{r}\right\rangle_{\text{coll}}/N}
at zero voltage-bias (absence of mean force) after a time period
T
that the Hamiltonian has gone a periodic change, where
N
is the number of electrons that have been displaced.
The adiabatic (equilibrium) charge pumping was first studied by Thouless in the seminal work of
Ref.
Thouless 1983
and serves as a simple yet fundamental example of topology in quantum systems. It shares the same topological origin as the integrally quantized Hall conductivity
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Kohmoto 1985
and may thus be regarded as a dynamical version of the Integer Quantum Hall Effect (IQHE).
This will actually show up in our derivation of the dynamic Hellmann–Feynman theorem (DHFT), where the IQHE and Thouless charge pumping will appear as parts of a single result; this connection appeared analytically in earlier unpublished work
Kyriakou and Moulopoulos 2015
but without the emergent non-Hermitian contribution that is the central focus of the present work.

The hallmark of this effect of (equilibrium) charge pumping is the transport of a precisely quantized amount of charge during an adiabatic cycle in the parameter space.
This remarkable phenomenon, has been demonstrated by experiments in a one-dimensional chain of ultra-cold atoms trapped in an optical lattice
Lu et al. 2016
;
Nakajima et al. 2016
;
Lohse et al. 2016
.
On the other hand, driven non-equilibrium (non-adiabatic) particle processes using the Floquet-Bloch bands
\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})
and the counterpart Floquet states, were first studied in
Ref.
Shih and Niu 1994
where it was shown that the quantization of the particle transport breaks down due to emergence of band gaps at the quasienergy bands.
Similar findings were presented in a recent study
Privitera et al. 2018
, where,
by a careful Floquet analysis
of a closed, clean, and non-interacting driven Rice-Mele model in the thermodynamic limit,
it was found that the pumped charge deviates from the topologically quantized value by an exponentially small amount, provided that the initial state coincides with the lowest-energy Floquet state.
Specifically, in
Refs
Shih and Niu 1994
;
Privitera et al. 2018

a Hellmann-Feynman theorem for time-periodic systems
Sambe 1973
has been used (derived from the eigenvalue equation of the so-called Floquet Hamiltonian) that, however, does not take into account
non-Hermitian, Berry curvature and Aharonov-Anandan phase contributions; in this respect, charge pumping has been studied solely in terms of the spectral properties of Floquet quasienergy alone.
Along these lines but in a different context (by studying the Floquet operator spectra)  Ref.
Kitagawa et al. 2010
has provided a topological characterization of periodically driven quantum systems in terms of the quasienergy winding alone.

In the development of the modern theory of electric polarization
Resta 1992
;
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Ortiz and Martin 1994
;
Resta 1994
;
Resta and Vanderbilt 2007
;
Resta 2010
;
Vanderbilt 2018
for periodic and extended systems, the charge pumping process has played a central role.
One of the differences between charge pumping and polarization change processes, is the voltage-bias (non-zero mean force) that is present in the latter process while is absent in the former.
Polarization changes
{\Delta\mathbf{P}}

have been theoretically realized to be more fundamental quantities
than the “absolute” bulk polarization
{\mathbf{P}}
itself, and they can be quantified by using the Berry phase of the electronic wavefunctions.
The modern theory avoids addressing the “absolute” polarization of a given equilibrium state, quite in agreement with the experiments, which invariably measure polarization differences.
The polarization difference is defined in Ref.
Resta and Vanderbilt 2007
as
the time-integrated transient macroscopic current that flows through the insulating sample during the switching process

\Delta\mathbf{P}=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{J}(t)dt,

(1)

provided that the time-dependent Hamiltonian remains insulating at all times. Although  Eq. (
1
)  is the fundamental equation of modern theory, its differential form

\frac{d\mathbf{P}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{J}(t),

(2)

has the same structure as the one that gives the classical polarization current with respect to bound charges
{\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{P_{b}}(t)}{dt}=\mathbf{J}_{b}(t)}
.

On the other hand, by having in mind that in a periodic band insulator the distinction between “free” (de-localized)
and
“bound” (localized)
charges is ambiguous, one can realize that the polarization difference

{\Delta\mathbf{P}}
given by  Eq. (
1
)  must generally have an extra contribution due to de-localized (bound) Bloch states that may be regarded as the
“free” charge states contribution. We show that such a contribution indeed exists and is captured by an emerging non-Hermitian term that accounts for such delocalized states.
Therefore, we propose that the collective induced electric polarization difference
\Delta\mathbf{P}
must be defined (assuming non-interacting particles and by deploying the many-body Slater determinant wavefunction) as

\Delta\mathbf{P}=\frac{1}{V}\sum\limits_{i}q_{i}\ \Delta\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle,

(3)

where
q_{i}
and
{\displaystyle\Delta\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle}

are the charge and the displacement of each particle respectively,
and
V
is the volume of the material.
In  Ref.
Kyriakou and Moulopoulos 2018
it was shown that, for a periodic and extended system in the thermodynamic limit, even though each electron’s position expectation value

{\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle}

is an undefined quantity, its displacement

{\displaystyle\Delta\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle}

is a well-defined quantity provided that one uses the extended velocity operator definition

\Delta\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{v}_{ext}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle dt,

(4)

where the expectation values are assumed to be evaluated over the entire volume
V
of the system.
The extended velocity operator expectation value

{\left\langle\psi_{i}(t)|\,\mathbf{v}_{ext}\,|\psi_{i}(t)\right\rangle}

has two separate contributions (originating from the additive structure of the extended operator itself, see below Eq. (
7
)); one is due to the standard velocity operator
{\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H(\mathbf{r},t),\mathbf{r}\right]}
,  and another due to an emerging non-Hermitian contribution that is attributed to the boundary velocity operator defined as
{\mathbf{v}_{b}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\!\left({H(\mathbf{r},t)}^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\!\mathbf{r}}
.
The boundary velocity expectation value has the physical meaning of a position weighted probability flux through the face (boundary) of the material.
For bulk localized states when the wavefunctions are zero over the material boundaries, the boundary velocity expectation value turns to zero; on the other hand, bulk delocalized Bloch states give
non-zero boundary velocity expectation value.
In this framework,  Eq. (
3
)  together with  Eq. (
4
)  may be regarded as an extended theoretical method within the modern theory of polarization, that simultaneously takes into account both the “bound” charge contribution (due to localized states) and the
“free” charge contribution (due to delocalized states) described by this emergent non-Hermitian effect, and as such it has not yet been considered in the modern theory. (We shall see later in
Eq. (
II
) that such a contribution is necessary for a complete study of polarization changes and the associated induced currents.)

In order to calculate exactly the boundary and the extended velocity operator expectation values (without using time-dependent perturbation theory), quantities that, as we shall see, are involved in the charge pumping and polarization change respectively, we have derived in the present work a dynamical Hellmann-Feynman theorem (DHFT). This theorem can be applied to arbitrary time-dependent states with arbitrary time-dependent parameters, and it is valid for calculating observables up to all orders of the external fields.
The DHFT explicitly involves generalized Berry curvatures as well as boundary contributions due to an emerging non-Hermitian effect term.
By using this DHFT, one is enabled to study simultaneously in the simplest manner possible the dynamics of the system, the emerging non-Hermitian contributions and the involvement of topology, in a unified formalism.
The DHFT can also be employed to non-equilibrium driven quantum processes,
i.e.
to Floquet states, where the quest for topological invariants is still in an ongoing research state
Kitagawa et al. 2010
;
Dehghani et al. 2015
;
Lindner et al. 2011
;
Carpentier et al. 2015
;
Rudner et al. 2013
;
Budich et al. 2017
;
Oka and Aoki 2009
;
Privitera et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Kolodrubetz et al. 2018
.

With respect to the new DHFT with the inclusion of the non-Hermitian boundary effect (that is here incorporated for the first time into the general foundations of the theory of pumping and polarization), it is useful to provide some further details on how this has been accomplished: we first re-examine the Thouless adiabatic particle transport (for an insulator with a fully occupied valence band) by using the extended velocity operator definition and the adiabatic limit of the DHFT without using (first-order) time-dependent perturbation theory.
Instead of assuming a sliding potential
{V(\mathbf{r}-\upsilon\,t\,\bm{e}_{x})}
like in the original problem
Thouless 1983
,
we assume a microscopic electric field
{\displaystyle E_{x}^{(micro)}(x,t)=E(t)cos(2\pi x/a)}
along
\bm{e}_{x}
direction. The microscopic electric field is periodic in space (with period equal to the lattice constant
a
) as well as over time (with period equal to
T
) that (i) originates from a scalar potential that crucially breaks the bulk mirror symmetry of the Hamiltonian along
\bm{e}_{x}
direction and (ii) gives zero macroscopic (spatially averaged) electric field, therefore no voltage-bias is present during the pumping.
By using the emerging non-Hermitian effect boundary velocity operator
{\mathbf{v}_{b}}
,  we show that, for delocalized Bloch states, each electron’s displacement over the material boundaries can be calculated by means of

{\displaystyle\Delta\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle dt}
.
Given now that each electron’s ground eigenstate evolves in time adiabatically, and by using the DHFT, we find that the collective non-Hermitian electrons’ displacement in the
x
direction and over the boundaries
{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\ /\ {N}}
is quantized in units of lattice constant due to topology. Hence, we demonstrate this way that, in the adiabatic limit, the non-Hermitian boundary term is needed in order to obtain the standard topological invariant (in fact, an omission of this would lead to a paradox in topological arguments - see discussion after Eq. (
58
)).
We then relax adiabaticity and study the non-equilibrium driven charge pumping. We assume delocalized Floquet-Bloch states
Gómez-León and Platero 2013
for the electrons’ motion, and use the extended velocity operator definition together with the non-adiabatic form of DHFT, in order to evaluate the non-Hermitian collective displacement of the centre of mass of the electrons
{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\ /\ {N}}
.
We find that, for a fully occupied Floquet-Bloch band
\varepsilon_{a}
,  the quantization of the pumped charge (per cycle)
breaks down due to a non-trivial (non-integrable) Aharonov-Anandan phase
Aharonov and Anandan 1987
. Particularly, in non-equilibrium driven processes the particle transport
deviates from the topologically quantized values as the difference of the Aharonov-Anandan phases at the edges of the Brillouin zone. This driven particle transport breaking, emerges whenever the Floquet-Bloch states

{\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

of the electrons cannot satisfy the requirements of a periodic gauge along the direction of the externally applied electric field, that is

{\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\neq\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}+G_{x}\bm{e}_{x})}
,
where the electric field is assumed to be in
\bm{e}_{x}
direction and

G_{x}
is any reciprocal lattice vector in
\bm{e}_{x}
.

Subsequently, we extend the standard outcomes of the modern theory of polarization by evaluating the electronic polarization change by means of  Eqs. (
3
) – (
4
)  and by using the adiabatic form of DHFT.
In this framework, we rigorously take into account both contributions, the ones due to localized states as well as the ones due to delocalized states.
By using the DHFT we calculate the collective electrons’ displacement

{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle\mathbf{r}_{i}\right\rangle}

as a continuous
\mathbf{k}
-space formula,
which in turn provides the induced electronic polarization change

\Delta\mathbf{P}
according to  Eq. (
3
).
Surprisingly, we find that the adiabatically induced electronic polarization change, has two extra, overlooked, contributions which are absent from the modern theory
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Ortiz and Martin 1994
;
Resta 1994
;
Resta and Vanderbilt 2007
;
Resta 2010
;
Vanderbilt 2018
. The one is attributed to a Berry curvature and is not zero only within ferromagnetic insulators where time-reversal symmetry is broken, and the other, is a non-Hermitian boundary contribution that captures the delocalization of the wavefunctions.
The non-Hermitian contribution is by definition a bulk quantity that, however, may equivalently be evaluated as a boundary quantity due to a symmetry that allows the bulk integration to be transformed into a boundary integration. When one takes into account only bulk localized states, that is, the wavefunctions are zero over the material boundaries, the overlooked non-Hermitian contribution always goes to zero.
This non-Hermitian contribution is very sensitive to the realistic boundary conditions of the wavefunctions during the time-periodic process that causes the polarization change. We therefore expect it to be significant in biased insulators with electron charge accumulation over their boundaries during the process that causes the polarization change (i.e. during the spontaneous polarization change experiments in a ferroelectric material, or during the induced polarization change experiments in a piezoelectric material).

By using the non-Hermitian contribution to the polarization change, we show how one can extend the surface-charge theorem of
Ref.
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
. Specifically, we argue that a macroscopic surface charge density change theorem can be formulated by means of

\Delta\sigma_{\text{surf}}=\Delta\mathbf{P_{b}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{a},

(5)

where,

{\Delta\mathbf{P_{b}}}

is the
polarization change that is attributed only to boundary properties of the electrons’ wavefunctions and

\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}
is the unit vector normal to the surface of the material.
When only bulk localized states are taken into account, the surface charge change
\Delta\sigma_{\text{surf}}
Eq. (
5
)
turns to zero by definition, as expected.

II
Extended velocity operator

The extended velocity operator
{\mathbf{v}_{ext}}
was first introduced in  Ref.
Kyriakou and Moulopoulos 2018
in order to take into account an anomaly of the position operator
\mathbf{r}
that creates a boundary, non-Hermitian effect term, in the Ehrenfest theorem. Namely, by defining the velocity operator as the operator that its expectation value is always equal with the rate of change of the position operator expectation value, it turns out that the velocity must be defined in an extended theoretical framework

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{ext}\right\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\!\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle,

(6)

where the extended velocity operator has two terms

\mathbf{v}_{ext}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{b},

(7)

and the expectation values in  Eq. (
6
)  are evaluated over the entire volume
V
of the system.

The first term

\mathbf{v}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H(\mathbf{r},t),\mathbf{r}\right]

(8)

is the standard velocity operator, while the second term

\mathbf{v}_{b}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\!\left({H(\mathbf{r},t)}^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\!\mathbf{r}

(9)

is a new object that we have termed the boundary velocity operator.

The introduction of this new operator
\mathbf{v}_{b}
is rather naturally motivated by  Refs.
Esteve 1986
;
Esteve 2002
and its expectation value is not zero only whenever the position operator becomes anomalous due to the non-Hermitian effect, in which case there are paradoxes first noted in  Ref.
Hill 1973
.
Specifically, the position operator becomes an anomalous operator whenever the state
\mathbf{r}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
does not lie within the given Hilbert space of the Hermitian Hamiltonian
{H(\mathbf{r},t)}
. As consequence, whenever periodic boundary conditions (PBCs) are adopted for the wavefunction

\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
the boundary velocity expectation value
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle}
is not zero.
Although the expectation value of the boundary velocity operator  Eq. (
9
)  given by

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle=\frac{i}{\hbar}(\left\langle H(\mathbf{r},t)\Psi(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\Psi(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle\Psi(t)|\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{r}\Psi(t)\right\rangle)

(10)

is by definition a bulk quantity, through a space-volume integration in position representation (assuming for the moment a 3D system), it can always and equivalently be evaluated as a boundary quantity due to the symmetric structure of the integrands that allows an integration by parts. (Everything however carries out in an obvious manner to other dimensionalities as well, with the results that follow still being valid but with appropriate adjustments, with boundaries being lines (edges) in a 2D and points (ends) in a 1D system).

In this respect, by working in position representation, for real scalar and vector potentials and after a straightforward integration by parts, the expectation value of  Eq. (
9
)  can be given in the form

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle=-\oiint_{S}\mathbf{r}\,(\mathbf{J}_{pr}(\mathbf{r},t)\!\cdot\!d\mathbf{S})+\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\oiint_{S}\left|\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)\right|^{2}\!d\mathbf{S}

(11)

with
S
being the terminated boundary surface of the system where the boundary conditions are imposed, and
{\mathbf{J}_{pr}(\mathbf{r},t)\!=\!\text{Re}[\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\,\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)]}
is the standard local probability current density.
It is to be noted that, apart from the imaginary part (which will always be cancelled out in the total velocity expectation value as we shall see below), the boundary velocity expectation value
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle}
has the meaning of a position weighted probability flux through the face of the material.

The general form of  Eq. (
11
)  can be further reduced for periodic systems. Specifically, by assuming a Bloch wavefunction
{\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k.r}}u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)}
and cell normalization convention

{\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle_{cell}=1}

(where
N
is the total number of the unit cells enclosed within the volume
V
of the system), then,
Eq. (
10
)  truncates into the form

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\left\langle H_{k}\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|\,\mathbf{r}\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle_{cell}-\left\langle u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|\,H_{k}\,\mathbf{r}\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle_{cell}\right)

(12)

where
{H_{k}}

is given by

{H_{k}=e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k.r}}H(\mathbf{r},t)e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k.r}}}
.
In deriving  Eq. (
12
)  we have used the fact
that
{u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)}
lies within the domain of definition of the Hamiltonian
{H_{k}}
, that is
{\left\langle H_{k}\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|\,H_{k}\,u_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\right\rangle=0}
.
By then exploiting the symmetry of the integrands and performing integration by parts,  Eq. (
12
)  takes the simplified form

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle=-\oiint_{cell}\!\!\!\mathbf{r}\,(\,\mathbf{J}_{pr}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\!\cdot\!d\mathbf{S}\,)

(13)

that is valid for periodic systems.

The first term of  Eq. (
11
)  can be seen as a position-weighted probability flux through the boundaries of the system, while the second and purely imaginary part, cancels a possible imaginary remnant part of the standard velocity operator expectation which is given by

\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle=\iiint_{V}\mathbf{J}_{pr}(\mathbf{r},t)dV-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\oiint_{S}\left|\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)\right|^{2}\!d\mathbf{S}

(14)

(by adding  Eq. (
11
)  and  Eq. (
14
), that is taking
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{ext}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle}
,
we see that
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{ext}\right\rangle}

is always a real quantity, as should be expected from Eq. (
6
)). Incidentally, these elementary facts (never been pointed out in the literature to the best of our knowledge) exemplify the general inadequacy (for certain boundary conditions) of the standard velocity operator in basic quantum mechanics; most importantly for the present work, they display the necessity of defining and using the augmented velocity operator
\mathbf{v}_{ext}
as a first example (with more demonstrations of this necessity to be discussed in the more focused solid state applications that follow). A point that we wish to make is that this need for the use of

\mathbf{v}_{ext}
is
required
for serious investigations, especially if one wants to have an analytical machinery, that can address issues with possible subtleties (such as the ones that we will confront with in the present work).

The extended velocity operator guarantees that, each Hamiltonian’s stationary eigenstate indexed by
n
, will always produce zero displacement (irrespectively of the static potentials) for the electron

\frac{d}{dt}\!\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle_{n}=\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{ext}\right\rangle_{n}=\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{n}+\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle_{n}=0,

(15)

as expected from the trivial fact that the position operator expectation value
\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle_{n}
is a static quantity with respect to any stationary state (since the phase factors containing time multiply to 1). However, for a stationary and extended plane wave state of a free electron of mass
m
with well-defined momentum
\hbar\mathbf{k}
in a finite volume
V
,  Eq. (
15
)  is violated (hence a paradox) whenever the boundary velocity expectation value
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle_{n}}
is not taken into account (as the standard velocity
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{n}\right\rangle_{n}}=\hbar\mathbf{k}/m
is generally nonzero).
This apparent paradox is bypassed within the extended velocity operator definition, as it turns out that the boundary velocity contributes a quantity of equal magnitude and opposite sign to the bulk electrons’ velocity
\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{n}
resulting in zero displacement
{\displaystyle\Delta{\left\langle\mathbf{r}\right\rangle}_{n}=0}
at every instant
t
for the assumed stationary state. In this framework, Eq. (
15
) can be used to form a bulk-boundary correspondence in a general sense for every stationary state, namely
{\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{n}=-\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle_{n}}
; this is an example, therefore, of a bulk formulation that properly takes into account boundary currents that are rigorously related to the bulk band structure.
We will see in what follows that similar line of reasoning applied to (and extending) the Hellmann-Feynman theorem will lead to the same conclusion about various systems of modern interests (see discussions after Eq. (
37
)).

By taking into account Eqs.
6
-
7
and
Eq.
11
, we can formally define a non-Hermitian displacement of the electron through the sample boundaries
{\Delta\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}}
by using the identification

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\!\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\!|_{bound},

(16)

which results into

\displaystyle\Delta\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}=\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}\right\rangle dt^{\prime}.

(17)

With the aid of  Eq. (
7
) – (
9
), the extended velocity operator can be recast in the form

\mathbf{v}_{ext}=\frac{i}{\hbar}({H(\mathbf{r},t)}^{+}\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}H(\mathbf{r},t))

(18)

which, by direct application of Eq. (
7
) with respect to the Hamiltonian’s instantaneous eigenstates labelled by
n
and
m
, gives the off-diagonal position matrix elements

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}(E_{m}(t)-E_{n}(t))\left\langle m(t)|\mathbf{r}|n(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle m(t)|\mathbf{v}|n(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\langle m(t)|\mathbf{v}_{b}|n(t)\right\rangle

(19)

that are proportional to the electron’s transition dipole moment. We see, therefore, that the emission and absorption of photons can be rigorously related with boundary properties owing to the off-diagonal boundary velocity matrix elements.
It is thus essential to point out that,  Eq. (
II
), without the boundary velocity matrix elements taken into account, is generally inadequate to be employed as a starting point formula - as often done in the modern theory (i.e. in Ref.
Vanderbilt 2018
) - in order to theoretically study the electronic polarization change and the associated induced current, especially if subtle issues need to be addressed for all types of realistic boundary conditions.

III
Derivation of the Dynamic Hellmann-Feynman theorem (DHFT)

We consider a real vector parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
that has an arbitrary time-dependence (without any adiabatic approximation involved), namely,
{\mathbf{R}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t,\mathbf{R_{o}})}
where
{\mathbf{R_{o}}}

is the initial value of the parameter satisfying
{\mathbf{R_{o}}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}(0,\mathbf{R_{o}})}
. Therefore, the parameter satisfies the general equation of motion
{\displaystyle\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R_{o}}+\int_{0}^{\displaystyle t}\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}}
,  and its time derivative is given by
{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t,\mathbf{R_{o}})}{\partial t}}
.
The theorem that we are about to prove is for a continuous vector parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
,  the initial value
{\mathbf{R_{o}}}
been therefore assumed to have continuous values as well.
The Hamiltonian of the system
{H(t,\mathbf{R})}
,  apart from the implicit time-dependence (via the parameters) may also have an arbitrary explicit time-dependence (something that is often not discussed). The derivation that is given below owes its existence to the Hamiltonian being the generator of time evolution of quantum states. We provide the derivation for a single-particle state while the generalization to a many-particle system is straightforward. A particle’s motion is generally encoded in its normalized time-dependent state
{\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
which evolves either by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for non-relativistic and spinless particle, or by the time-dependent Dirac equation for spinful particle. We assume for simplicity a one particle quantum system. The motion of the particle is described by a general state, not necessarily an eigenstate of the Hamiltonian nor a localized state (such as a narrow wave packet). The system is assumed to be closed

{\left\langle\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=1}
,
and the quantum state time evolution is determined by the time-dependent equation

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=H(t,\mathbf{R})\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle,

(20)

where the Hamiltonian is either of Schrödinger or Dirac type.
The time derivative in  Eq. (
20
)  is the total time derivative given by

\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}

(21)

where
{\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{e}_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial R_{i}}}
. The initial value of the parameter

{\mathbf{R_{o}}}
that implicitly enters  Eq. (
20
)  can be used to label the quantum states
{\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
.
The expectation value of the Hamiltonian

\left\langle\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right|H(t,\mathbf{R})\left.\!\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=E(t,\mathbf{R})

(22)

can be viewed as the instantaneous time-dependent
“energy” of the particle
{E(t,\mathbf{R})}
.
Differentiation with respect to the parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
of both sides of  Eq. (
22
)  gives

\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\left.\Psi\right\rangle=\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E-\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,|\,H\Psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\Psi|\,H\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle.

(23)

Taking now into account that the parameter gradient operator
{\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}}
can generally be an anomalous operator, that is, the states
{\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle}
may not fall within the domain of the Hermitian Hamiltonian, something that can be expressed by the non-trivial inequality
{\left\langle H\Psi\,|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi\,|\,H^{+}\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle\neq\left\langle\Psi\,|\,H\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle}
,
we recast  Eq. (
23
)  into the form

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\left.\Psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E-\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,|\,H\Psi\right\rangle-\left\langle H\Psi|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle

(24)

\displaystyle+\left\langle\Psi\,|\,\left(H^{+}-H\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle.

By then using  Eq. (
20
)  in  Eq. (
24
)  we find

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\left.\Psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E\,+\,\left\langle\Psi\,|\left(H^{+}-H\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\hbar\left(\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,|\,\frac{d\Psi}{dt}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{d\Psi}{dt}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle\right).

Applying then the total time derivative  Eq. (
21
)  on  Eq. (
III
)  we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\left.\Psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E\,+\,\left\langle\Psi\,|\left(H^{+}-H\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\hbar\left(\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,|\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-i\hbar\left(\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,|\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\right\rangle\right).

Using then the vector identity

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\times(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{A}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B}\right)-\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{A}\right)\mathbf{B}}
in the last term on the right hand side of
Eq. (
III
),  we finally obtain our new dynamic Hellmann-Feynman theorem (DHFT), namely

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E(t,\mathbf{R})\,+\,\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})

(27)

\displaystyle-\,\hbar\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t,\mathbf{R})-\hbar\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}(t,\mathbf{R}),

where

\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H(t,\mathbf{R})\right)\left.\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

(28)

is the observable in quest, whereas

\bm{\mathcal{B}}(t,\mathbf{R})=i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

(29)

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t,\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

are generalized Berry curvatures (of both ‘magnetic’ and ‘electric’ type) in the
{t\!\times\!\mathbf{R}}
space, each of them being a real quantity as manifest from their definition.

The
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}

term is an emergent non-Hermitian contribution to the above theorem which is by definition a bulk quantity. However, as shown in Appendix
A
, by working in position representation and assuming real scalar and vector potentials, the
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}

term is always transformed (due to symmetric form of the integrands) and evaluated by means of a boundary integral over the system’s boundaries and
is given (assuming a 3D system, although this can trivially be generalized) by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\,|\,\left(H(t,\mathbf{R})^{+}-H(t,\mathbf{R})\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left(\,(\mathbf{v}\,\Psi(t,\mathbf{R}))^{+}+\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})^{+}\,\mathbf{v}\,\right)\!\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\,dS,

where
{\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H(t,\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{r}\right]}

is the standard velocity operator and
{\mathbf{n}}
is the unit vector that is locally normal to the surface that encloses the system.
As shown in Appendix
A
, the boundary integral form of the non-Hermitian contribution

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}
given by Eq. (
III
), is valid for every energy scale of the particle, that is, either the time evolution is governed by a (i) spinless non-relativistic Hamiltonian, or (ii) a spinful Dirac Hamiltonian, or a (iii) a spinful Hamiltonian in the non-relativistic limit.
In all above cases, the non-Hermitian contribution

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}
is extremely sensitive to the realistic boundary conditions on the wavefunctions.
Moreover,
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}
is a real quantity whenever the operator
{\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H}
behaves as a Hermitian operator. This can be shown
by starting from a closed system assumption
{\left\langle\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right|H\left.\!\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle-\left\langle H\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right|\left.\!\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=0}
,  and then differentiate both sides with respect to the parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
giving

\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})=\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})^{\displaystyle*}+\left(\,\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\left.\Psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}H\right)\,\Psi\,\right|\left.\!\Psi\right\rangle\,\right).

(32)

Interestingly, whenever the assumed Hamiltonian does not have explicit parameter dependence, but the considered states do depend on the parameters (as i.e. it happens for the original
H
in a periodic system (before passing to the Bloch Hamiltonian), which does not depend on the Bloch wavevector
k
, although its eigenstates, the standard Bloch states, do), then, the non-Hermitian contribution is always a real quantity; in the example given above, the non-Hermitian contribution evaluated with respect to a Bloch state by identifying
{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{k}}
is always a real quantity.
Eq. (
32
) will be crucial in properties such as the surface-charge theorem discussed towards the end of the paper (see Eq. (
100
)).

Due to the structure of the DHFT, the last term on the right side of  Eq. (
27
)  gives a contribution that is transverse to
the direction of the parameter variation
{\partial\mathbf{R}}
,  namely,

{\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle_{tran}=-\hbar\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}(t,\mathbf{R})}

that satisfies
{\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle_{tran}\cdot\partial\mathbf{R}=0}
. This occurs in IQHE, but this term is also expected to contribute significantly to non-linear quantum processes whenever the assumed parameter varies with time,
i.e
within non-linear materials where longitudinal as well as transverse polarization changes occurs simultaneously.

III.1
Application of the DHFT to a state with ansatz form

\displaystyle\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})}\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle
.

When the considered quantum state, assumed to be a solution of  Eq.(
20
),  has the ansatz form

\left|\Psi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})}\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle,

(33)

then, as shown in Appendix
B
,
the DHFT  Eq. (
27
)  retains its structure form but all involved quantities are now evaluated with respect to the quantum state

\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle
,
namely

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E_{\Phi}(t,\mathbf{R})\,+\,\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\Phi}(t,\mathbf{R})

(34)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!-\hbar\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{\Phi}(t,\mathbf{R})\,-\,\hbar\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi}(t,\mathbf{R}).

In other words, the observables in quest

{\left\langle\bm{O}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

given by  Eq. (
28
)  are insensitive to phases

{e^{\displaystyle i\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})}}

that do not depend explicitly on the position coordinate

{\mathbf{r}}
. Such simple applications of the DHFT
are described below:

For an adiabatically evolved eigenstate of a time-dependent Hamiltonian, we replace in  Eq.(
33
),
{\displaystyle\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})\mapsto\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}

and

{\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle\mapsto\left|n(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
,
where

{\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}
is the total (sum of dynamic and geometric) phase and

{\left|n(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

is the instantaneous eigenstate of the Hamiltonian satisfying the eigenvalue equation

{H(t,\mathbf{R})\left|n(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\left|n(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle.}

For a stationary eigenstate of a static Hamiltonian where the assumed parameter is static

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}(t)}{\partial t}=0}
,  we replace in  Eq.(
33
),
{\displaystyle\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})\mapsto\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}

and

{\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle\mapsto\left|n(\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
,
where

{\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}
is the dynamic phase and

{\left|n(\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

is the instantaneous eigenstate of the Hamiltonian satisfying the eigenvalue equation

{H(\mathbf{R})\left|n(\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=E_{n}(\mathbf{R})\left|n(\mathbf{R})\right\rangle.}

The Berry curvature

{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\mathbf{R})}

is zero by definition according to
Eq. (
137
) owing to

{\left|n(\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

that does not have explicit time dependence,
whereas the Berry curvature

{\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(\mathbf{R})}

is not zero but it does not contribute into the DHFT  Eq. (
34
) due to zero velocity of the parameter.

For a non-equilibrium and nonadiabatic quantum state where
the assumed parameter may have arbitrary high magnitude of velocity
{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}(t)}{\partial t},}
that, however, evolves in time by a time-periodic Hamiltonian

{H(t+T,\mathbf{R}(t+T))=H(t,\mathbf{R}(t))}
,
where
{\mathbf{R}(t+T)=\mathbf{R}(t)}
and
{T}
is the period of driving,
we replace in  Eq.(
33
)

{\displaystyle\Theta(t,\mathbf{R})\mapsto-\frac{\varepsilon_{a}\,t}{\hbar}}

and

{\left|\Phi(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle\mapsto\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
,
where
{\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}
is the Floquet mode which is periodic in time
{\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=\left|\Phi_{a}(t+T,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

and satisfies the eigenvalue equation

{\displaystyle(H(t,\mathbf{R})-i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\,)\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\varepsilon_{a}\,\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),}

with
{\varepsilon_{a}}
being the static quasienergy.
In conclusion, we argue that the DHFT
Eq. (
34
)  can be applied to general topological band theory.

III.2
The need for the non-Hermitian term in the DHFT

The simplest example where one can ascertain the necessity of presence of the boundary non-Hermitian term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}
is a free electron motion in 1D.
In this respect, we assume the Hamiltonian
{\displaystyle H=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}}
which does not have any parameter dependence. We consider an electron’s motion described by the eigenstate

{\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{\displaystyle ikx}}

of the Hamiltonian
with well defined energy
{\displaystyle E(k)=\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}}
,
where
L
is the length of the system. We identify the static electron’s 1D wave vector
k
(assumed to take continuous values) as the parameter
{R\equiv k}
and examine the necessity of a nonzero
{S(k)}
.

Specifically, by employing the standard static HF theorem without the non-Hermitian term, that is, the 1D analog of  Eq. (
148
)  derived in Appendix
B.3
without the boundary term
{S(k)}
, will eventually lead to a paradox since
{\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{dH}{dk}\right\rangle=0}
while

{\displaystyle\frac{dE(k)}{dk}=\frac{d}{dk}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}\right)=\frac{\hbar^{2}k}{m}\neq 0}
.
The paradox is resolved whenever one takes into account the boundary non-Hermitian term
{S(k)}
. Specifically, the 1D analogue of  Eq. (
148
)  is given by

0=\frac{dE(k)}{dk}+S(k),

(35)

where
{S(k)}
is in 1D truncated to a two point formula (
cf
. Eq. (
138
)) given by

S(k)=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[\left(\,(\text{v}\,\Phi_{k}(x))^{\displaystyle*}+\Phi_{k}(x)^{\displaystyle*}\,\text{v}\,\right)\!\,\frac{d\Phi_{k}(x)}{dk}\right]_{x}^{x+L}.

(36)

We substitute in  Eq. (
36
)  the standard velocity operator
{\displaystyle\text{v}=-\frac{i\hbar}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}
,  as well as
\text{v}\,\Phi_{k}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\frac{\hbar k}{m}e^{\displaystyle ikx}=\frac{\hbar k}{m}\Phi_{k}(x)

and
{\displaystyle\frac{d\Phi_{k}(x)}{dk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}i\,x\,e^{\displaystyle ikx}=i\,x\,\Phi_{k}(x)}
.   Eq. (
36
)  then takes the value

\displaystyle S(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[\left(\,\frac{\hbar k}{m}\Phi_{k}(x)^{\displaystyle*}-\frac{i\hbar}{m}\Phi_{k}(x)^{\displaystyle*}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\right)\!\,i\,x\,\Phi_{k}(x)\right]_{x}^{x+L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[\frac{i\,\hbar\,k}{m}|\Phi_{k}(x)|^{2}x+\frac{\hbar}{m}|\Phi_{k}(x)|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+i\,x\,\Phi_{k}^{\displaystyle*}\,(\text{v}\,\Phi_{k}(x))\frac{}{}\right]_{x}^{x+L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[2\frac{i\,\hbar\,k}{m}|\Phi_{k}(x)|^{2}x+\frac{\hbar}{m}|\Phi_{k}(x)|^{2}\right]_{x}^{x+L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{2mL}\left[2\,i\,k\,x+1\frac{}{}\right]_{x}^{x+L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{2mL}(2\,i\,k\,L)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}k}{m},

which together with

{\displaystyle\frac{dE(k)}{dk}=\frac{\hbar^{2}k}{m}}
verifies  Eq. (
35
)  and guarantees the validity of the static HF theorem. Whenever, therefore, the momentum derivative operator
{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dk}}
becomes anomalous, the boundary non-Hermitian contribution cannot be excluded from the standard, static HF theorem;
a similar kind of anomaly as in
Eq. (
35
),  was noticed in
Ref.
Esteve et al. 2010
but, notably,
without indicating any usage into conventional band theory.
The above non-Hermitian contribution satisfies the antisymmetry

{S(k)=-S(-k)}
,  which,
as shown in Appendix
C
, is a general property of band theory (recall that the non-Hermitian contribution is always evaluated with respect to a Bloch state, Eq. (
III
)) when the Hamiltonian satisfies mirror symmetry along
x
.

III.3
The non-Hermitian contribution as a probe of the dispersion relation

If we identify the wave vector as a static parameter entering
Eq. (
34
),  that is
{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{k}}
, then, whenever the initial static Hamiltonian of a bulk periodic system does not depend explicitly on the wave vector

{\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}H(\mathbf{k})=0}
, since the wave vector is only involved only in the Bloch states

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})}
,  one can in principle use the non-Hermitian boundary term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k})}
(where now the label
n
refers to consideration of a particular band) as a probe for detecting information about the energy dispersion relation (through the boundary behavior of the Bloch functions), namely

\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k}).

(37)

Consequently, by defining the bulk localized eigenstates of the Hamiltonian as the ones that satisfy

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k})=0}
for every crystal momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
in the Brillouin zone
(which in the simplest scenario occurs when the Bloch wavefunction and all of its derivatives are zero over the boundaries of the material), then, it is evident from  Eq. (
37
)  that these kind of states will have flat band dispersion relations

{\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(\mathbf{k})=0}
,  indicating the existence of a large degree of degeneracy.
Notably, flat bands are known to be formed in conventional band theory of periodic (perfectly ordered) systems

Leykam et al. 2018
;
Röntgen et al. 2018
;
Maimaiti et al. 2017
;
Bodyfelt et al. 2014
;
Danieli et al. 2015
;
Ramachandran et al. 2017
and are attributed to compact localized states that are created due to destructive interference, but, their formation still lacks a comprehensive generating principle

Maimaiti et al. 2017
.
In this framework we argue that, whenever the non-Hermitian contribution of a given band is zero, it signals the creation of a flat band.

Moreover, by employing  Eq. (
37
),  one can theoretically explain the changing of the dispersion relation slope in relation to the localization of the involved states.
For example, in the Integer Quantum Hall Effect, the bulk localized states are described by flat bands (Landau levels)
E_{n}(\mathbf{k})\equiv E_{n}=\textit{constant}
, while the states near the boundaries have dispersion relations with non-zero slopes that are approximately linear

Halperin 1982
(this linearity being with respect to guiding center coordinates in real space, which however for the simple Landau problem in a Landau gauge is directly proportional to a wave vector value k, hence the linearity being again in k). This kind of behavior, can qute possibly be theoretically explained when the non-Hermitian boundary term is taken into account.
Moreover, in Dirac and Weyl semimetals
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Vafek and Vishwanath 2014
;
Yan and Felser 2017
;
Jia et al. 2016
;
Burkov 2018
,
linear dispersion relations in k are found near the vicinity of energy-degenerate points in the Brillouin zone which represent the bulk electrons’ physics near these degeneracy points in k-space.
Although the electrons’ motions are not fully relativistic (apart from correction due to spin-orbit coupling), these
are theoretically explained by employing various model Hamiltonians
{H(\mathbf{k})}
which are effectively (formally) relativistic.
We propose that,
these emerging relativistic linear dispersion relations that are accompanied by exotic boundary behaviors such as Fermi arcs, can alternatively be explained by employing the non-Hermitian boundary term.
Specifically, by taking a Taylor expansion of the non-Hermitian boundary term

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k})}
near a degeneracy point
{\mathbf{k}_{o}}
we find

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k}_{o})-\left((\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_{o})\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k})\arrowvert_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{o}}

(38)

\displaystyle+O(\mathbf{k}^{2})+\dots.

With the dispersion relation
{E_{n}(\mathbf{k})}
profile experimentally obtained,
one
can extract information about the boundary term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(\mathbf{k})}
by keeping only up to first order terms
in the Taylor expansion Eq. (
38
). By then also
employing the Bloch functions

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})}

in  Eq. (
138
),  further information about the behavior of the wavefunction at the boundary can be extracted.

IV
Thouless adiabatic charge pump

Let us now re-examine the adiabatic charge pumping of non-interacting spinless electrons in a slightly different context than the original method of Ref
Thouless 1983
. Rather than assuming a sliding potential
{V((\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{u}\,t)\,\bm{e}_{x})}
,  we assume an electric field along
\bm{e}_{x}
direction which is periodic in space (over
x
coordinate with period equal to the lattice constant) as well as over time
t
(with elementary time period equal to
T
). By then using the non-Hermitian boundary velocity operator and the adiabatic limit of DHFT, we here evaluate the collective, center of mass, electrons’ displacement in the
\bm{e}_{x}
direction and over the boundaries
{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\ /\ {N}}
without using (first-order) time-dependent perturbation theory.

By attributing the electric field to a scalar potential only, each spinless electron’s wavefunction evolves in time by the Hamiltonian

H(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{2m}{\mathbf{p}^{2}+e\phi(x,t)}+V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})

(39)

where
m
and
-e
are the mass and charge of the electron respectively,

\mathbf{p}
is the canonical momentum operator, and
V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})
is the crystal potential.
The applied scalar potential is given by

\phi(x,t)=-\frac{a}{2\pi}E(t)sin(2\pi x/a),

(40)

where
a
is the primitive cell length in
x
direction, and satisfies

{\phi(x+na,t)=\phi(x,t)}
with
n
an integer, as well as,

{\phi(x,T)=\phi(x,0)=0}
and

{\left.\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(x,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}=\left.\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(x,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=T}=0}
.
Therefore, the Hamiltonian  Eq. (
39
)  is periodic with respect to the elementary time period
T
satisfying
{H(\mathbf{r},0)=H(\mathbf{r},T)}

and

{\left.\displaystyle\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}=\left.\displaystyle\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=T}=0}
,
and it is also periodic over position coordinates

H(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R},t)=H(\mathbf{r},t)
, where
\mathbf{R}
is a Bravais lattice vector.

Provided that the crystal potential
V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})
is centromeric, the applied scalar potential crucially breaks the
space-inversion symmetry of the Hamiltonian as well as the mirror symmetry along the
\bm{e}_{x}
direction due to

{\phi(x,t)\neq\phi(-x,t)}
.
This results to the Berry curvature’s
(defined by Eq. (
30
) and assumed to be evaluated with respect to an instantaneous eigenstate of the Hamiltonian)
breaking of antisymmetry

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\neq-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}
,
which in turn,
gives non-zero collective charge pumping.
Analogous mirror symmetry breaking over
x
direction
is involved into the case of the Thouless sliding scalar potential

{V(x-\upsilon\,t,y,z)\neq V(-x-\upsilon\,t,y,z)}
.
Due to the applied scalar potential, each electron is subject to an applied microscopic electric field

{\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(micro)}(x,t)=-\bm{\nabla}\phi(x,t)=E(t)cos(2\pi x/a)\,\bm{e}_{x}}

along
\bm{e}_{x}
direction that, is periodic over position coordinate
x
(with period equal to the lattice constant
a
) and over time (
E(T)=E(0)=0
).
The applied microscopic electric field gives zero macroscopic (spatially averaged) electric field, therefore no voltage-bias is present during the pumping.
Crucially, as we will show later, if an intermediate (middle-) time period symmetry is present, that is,
E(T)=E(0)=E(T/2)
, the charge pumping turns into zero due to time reversal symmetry.

Each electron’s state evolves in time according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE)

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle=\left(\frac{1}{2m}{\mathbf{p}^{2}+e\phi(x,t)}+V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})\right)\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle.

(41)

We assume that each electron is initially in a non-degenerate ground state,
{\left|\Psi(t_{o})\right\rangle\equiv\left|\psi_{n}(t_{o})\right\rangle}
,  where
n
labels the band.
The ground state energy band is for all moments
{0\leq t\leq T}
separated by a gap from the upper bands.
Each electron’s ground state wavefunction satisfies periodic boundary conditions over the material’s boundaries

\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t_{o})=\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t_{o}),

where
\mathbf{L}
denotes the (vector) length of the material in each direction.
Assuming that the electric field
{\mathbf{E}(x,t)}
is slowly changing with time, meaning
{T\rightarrow\infty}
,  each ground state evolves in time adiabatically

\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t)=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{n}(t)}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t),

where
{\Theta_{n}(t)}
is the total phase of the wavefunction (the sum of the dynamic and the geometric adiabatic phase). Each wavefunction
{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t)}
satisfies the instantaneous eigenvalue equation

\left(\frac{1}{2m}{\mathbf{p}^{2}+e\phi(x,t)}+V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})\right)\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t)=E_{n}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t).

(42)

By following Ref. 9, we assume that the electron’s wavefunction has the form

\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

(43)

and the
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
satisfies periodic boundary conditions

u_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})
,  which constrains the allowed wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
-values
by the condition
{e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{L}}=1}
.
In order to apply the DHFT given by  Eq. (
27
)  we need to define a continuous parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
and its corresponding velocity
{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}}
.
The parameter definition is made by using the assumed form of the quantum state.
Namely, by having in mind that, in the presence of translational invariance of the Hamiltonian, the wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
is a conserved quantity of the dynamics,
we define the parameter
{\mathbf{R}}
as the static wave vector
{\mathbf{R}\equiv\mathbf{k}}
which has zero corresponding velocity

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}\equiv\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}=0}
.
In order for the application of the theorem to be valid, the parameter
{\mathbf{k}}
has to be a continuous one. Therefore, we assume that the length of the system
{\mathbf{L}}
is infinite, which results to infinitesimal minimum spacing between the allowed values of the wave vectors
{\Delta\mathbf{k}\rightarrow 0}
.
By then noting that the Hamiltonian
{H(\mathbf{r},t)}
does not depend on any parameter

\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}H(\mathbf{r},t)=0,

application of the adiabatic form of DHFT with respect to the state
{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,
namely, Eq. (
B.2
)
derived in Appendix
B.2

gives

0=\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\hbar\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k}),

(44)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(45)

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle

is the earlier defined electric field type of Berry curvature,
and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left(\,(\mathbf{v}\,\psi_{n})^{+}+\psi_{n}^{+}\,\mathbf{v}\,\right)\!\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}\,dS

is the non-Hermitian contribution,
where
\mathbf{n}
is the unit vector that is locally normal to the surface
S
.

By then using
Eq. (
43
)  into  Eq. (
46
),  that is, by substituting the identity

\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=i\,\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

we find an expression that relates the non-Hermitian contributions

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=\hbar\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\right\rangle_{n}\,+\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k}),

(47)

where
{\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\right\rangle_{n}}
is the non-Hermitian boundary velocity expectation value (that is non-zero when the position operator
\mathbf{r}
becomes anomalous) given by  Eq. (
10
).
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
and
{\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\right\rangle_{n}}

are evaluated with respect to the Bloch state

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,  while

{\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})}

is a non-Hermitian boundary contribution that is evaluated with respect to the instantaneous cell periodic states
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
(whose value is not zero when the momentum gradient operator
\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}
becomes anomalous with respect to the
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
)
and is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H_{k}(t,\mathbf{k})^{+}-H_{k}(t,\mathbf{k})\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left(\,(\mathbf{v}_{k}\,u_{n})^{+}+u_{n}^{+}\,\mathbf{v}_{k}\,\right)\!\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}\,dS,

where
{\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{r}\right]}

is the standard velocity operator,

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}H(\mathbf{r},t)e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}}
,
and
{u_{n}\equiv u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
.

By substituting  Eq. (
47
)  into  Eq. (
44
)  we find the non-Hermitian boundary velocity of the electron given by

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{n}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\frac{1}{\hbar}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k}).

(49)

Because we have assumed periodic boundary conditions,
{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

as well as

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,
the quantum state
{\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

is periodic with respect to space coordinates

{\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,
it therefore belongs within the domain of definition of the Hamiltonian

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
which results into

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})=0.

(50)

This can be shown by expanding the cell periodic functions in a Fourier series over all reciprocal lattice vectors
{\mathbf{G}}
, that is,
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}C_{n}(t,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G})\ e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{G}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}}
, which gives

{\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}C_{n}(t,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G})\ e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{G}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}}
. Substitution of
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
and
{\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

in the definition of the non-Hermitian term, namely
{\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})=\left\langle H_{k}\,u_{n}\,|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}\right\rangle-\left\langle u_{n}\,|\,H_{k}\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}\right\rangle}
,
results in
{\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})=0}

(for spinful electrons, although, when spin-Hall effect is present, this non-Hermitian term may not be zero).

On the other hand, the quantum state
{\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

lies outside of the domain of definition of periodic wavefunctions and leaves a residue in the DHFT resulting in

\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{n}\neq 0.

(51)

Eq. (
47
) together with Eq. (
50
) indicates that the non-Hermitian term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
,  evaluated with respect to the Bloch state

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,  is never zero when periodic boundary conditions are adopted for the wavefunctions.
To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that the particle transport is explicitly related with a
non-Hermitian boundary velocity

{\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{n}}
,  together with the electric field type of Berry curvature
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
that originates from the DHFT.

The electron’s non-Hermitian boundary velocity in
x
direction is therefore given by

\left\langle\text{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{n,\,x}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\,+\,\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})

(52)

where the electric curvature is given from

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\,|\,\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(53)

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\frac{\partial\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\right\rangle

By defining the collective electrons’ displacement (for a fully occupied band)
through the sample boundaries
in the
x
direction, as

{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle_{n}\!|_{bound}=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\!\left\langle\text{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{n,\,x}dt}
,  then, for a cubic crystal with lattice constant
a
and in the thermodynamic limit, this displacement is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\upsilon_{n,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k

\displaystyle+\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k.

where

{\displaystyle\upsilon_{n,x}(t,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}}

is the
x
component of the group velocity.

The group velocity contribution is always truncated into a boundary contribution over the Brillouin edges

\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\oiint\limits_{BZ}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\mathbf{n}_{k}\,d^{2}k,

where
{\mathbf{n}_{k}}
is the unit vector that is locally normal to the Brillouin zone boundaries,
without any gauge phase ambiguities involved.
On the contrary, the electric field type of Berry curvature term of Eq. (
IV
) is not generally amenable to further simplifications (unless some symmetries are operating, which is thoroughly discussed next).

IV.1
Symmetry Considerations

If the time-dependent Hamiltonian is space-inversion symmetric, then,

{E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})}
and the group velocity
{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
will satisfy
{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}E_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})}
,  resulting in
{\displaystyle\iiint_{BZ}\!\!\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=0}
,  which means that the first term on the right side of  Eq. (
IV
)  integrates to zero.
Similarly, if the Hamiltonian is mirror symmetric along
x
, then,

{E_{n}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=E_{n}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}
resulting in
{\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\frac{\partial E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\,dk_{x}=0}
,
which also indicates that the first term on the second side of  Eq. (
IV
)  integrates to zero.
On the other hand, due to the assumed scalar potential  Eq. (
40
),  space-inversion symmetry and mirror symmetry along
x
are broken, therefore, the first term on the second side of  Eq. (
IV
)  cannot be zero due to space inversion or mirror symmetry.
If the scalar potential satisfies
{\phi(x,-t)^{\displaystyle*}=\phi(x,t)}
, then, the Hamiltonian is time-reversal symmetric and the energy satisfies
{E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}
,  resulting in
{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}
for the group velocity.
When the eigenvalue of the  “energy”
E_{n}

in the instantaneous eigenvalue equation
Eq. (
42
)  does not have explicit time-dependence

{\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{n})\right|\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}\left|\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle=\frac{\partial E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}=0}
,  then, the system does not absorb energy from the environment. In this framework the group velocity does not have explicit time-dependence, thus, due to the assumed time-reversal symmetry it satisfies

{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(-\mathbf{k})}
,  and as a result,
the group velocity on the second side of  Eq. (
IV
)  integrates to zero. On the other hand, when the energy has explicit time-dependence
the group velocity on the second side of  Eq. (
IV
)  does not necessarily integrate to zero unless an extra, intermediate (middle) time period symmetry of the Hamiltonian is present, namely,

{H(\mathbf{r},t)=H(\mathbf{r},t+T)=H(\mathbf{r},t+T/2)}
.
The latter symmetry indicates that the wavefunctions differs by a phase

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},0)=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{1k}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},T)=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{2k}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},T/2)}
,
which guarantees that the integration
{\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=\int\limits_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t+T/2,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k}
turns into zero due to
(i)
{\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t+T/2,\mathbf{k})=\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
and
(ii)

{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}
.
Counter to a time-reversal symmetric Hamiltonian, when the assumed (real) scalar potential breaks time-reversal symmetry (
i.e.
owing to
{E(-t)\neq E(t)}
),  then, the group velocity of the time-reversal breaking state
in  Eq. (
IV
)  does not necessarily integrate to zero (unless we are assuming compact localized states with a flat bands).

On the other hand, when the translation invariant Hamiltonian has translation-inversion symmetry (see Appendix
C
), namely, it remains invariant under the inversions
{(i,\mathbf{k})\mapsto(-i,-\mathbf{k})}
,  this symmetry dictates that
{E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})}
and the group velocity integrates to zero, hence, for the assumed scalar potential  Eq. (
40
) the first term on the right side of  Eq. (
IV
)  is zero.

Interestingly, if an additional confinement potential
{V_{c}(y,z)}
that localize the electrons near to the
x
axis is assumed in the Hamiltonian  Eq. (
39
),  then, the translation symmetry is broken along
y
and
z
coordinates and the energy band of the 3D system becomes
{E_{n}\equiv E_{n}(t,k_{x})}
,  therefore,
{E_{n}(t,-k_{x})=E_{n}(t,k_{x})}
due to time-reversal symmetry. This is precisely the assumption that was made in Ref
Thouless 1983
where the adiabatic particle transport of a 3D system was found quantized.

As ofr the Berry curvature
{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})}
,  as shown in Appendix
C
, it does not satisfy the antisymmetry relation

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}
due to the mirror symmetry that is broken along
x
,
therefore,
the integration

{\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})\,dk_{x}}

does not turn into zero.
On the other hand, if time-reversal symmetry is present

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}

together with an assumed
intermediate time period symmetry of the Hamiltonian

{H(\mathbf{r},t)=H(\mathbf{r},t+T)=H(\mathbf{r},t+T/2)}
,  then,

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t+T/2,\mathbf{k})}

and the Berry curvature integrates to zero according to

\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=\int\limits_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t+T/2,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=\int\limits_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k=0
. (For consequences of all this see below.)

IV.2
Topological arguments

The integration of the Berry curvature

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})}

over the manifold spanned by
{dk_{x}\,dt}

is quantized due to topology and it is equal to

\left(\,\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})\,dt\,dk_{x}\right)=2\pi\,C_{1}^{(n)},\ \ \ \ \ \ C_{1}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{Z}

(55)

where
{C_{1}^{(n)}}
is the first Chern number
Nakahara 2003
.
The first Chern number defines the mapping from the parameter space
{(t,\mathbf{k})}
to the complex projective space of normalized Bloch states

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
. Non-zero Chern number indicates that the mapping is topologically non-trivial.
Specifically, due to the assumed periodic boundary conditions at every instant,

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

as well as

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t,\mathbf{k})=u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
, it implies that the Bloch states are generally periodic over the parameter
{\mathbf{k}}
up to a phase
\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{\!{}_{G}}}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\thinspace

where
{\mathbf{G}}
is a reciprocal lattice vector. Similarly, because we have assumed time-periodic Hamiltonian, the Bloch states are periodic over
{t}
up to a phase

{\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t+T,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{{}_{T}}}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}).}

The last two equations imply that the Bloch states
{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

are not in general single-valued quantities in
the parameter space
{t\times\mathbf{k}}
.  Non-zero first Chern number
{C_{1}^{(n)}}
indicates that the Bloch states
{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
cannot be everywhere single-valued in the parameter space domain
{0\leq t\leq T}
and

{\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{a}\leq k_{x}\leq\frac{\pi}{a}}
.

IV.3
Quantization of the non-Hermitian displacement

Assuming, either a time-reversal symmetric Hamiltonian and a system that does not absorb energy from the environment, or, bulk compact localized states with flat bands,
then, by employing  Eq. (
55
)  in  Eq. (
IV
),  it turns out that the collective electrons’ displacement through the system’s boundaries in the
x
direction is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{Boundary}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,C_{1}^{(n)}\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!\!\!dk_{y}\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!\!\!dk_{z}

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,C_{1}^{(n)}N\alpha,

where
N
is the total number of unit cells of the material.
Eq. (
56
)  implies that, after one period
T
of adiabatic driving, the displacement of the center of mass of the electrons
in the
x
direction and over the boundaries,
is quantized in units of lattice constant
a
and is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}=\,C_{1}^{(n)}\,\alpha,

(57)

meaning that the center of mass performs a quantized rigid displacement over the boundaries along the
x
direction, or equivalently, the electrons behaves as an incompressible fluid in
\mathbf{k}
-space.
Accordingly, the collective electrons’ displacement over
y
and
z
directions are zero

\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle y_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}=\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle z_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}=0,

(58)

due to the mirror inversion symmetries (assumed to be present) that implies
{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,y}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,y}(t,k_{x},-k_{y},k_{z})}

as well as

{\mathcal{E}_{n,\,z}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,z}(t,k_{x},k_{y},-k_{z})}

which in turn gives zero displacements.

We conclude that, if the non-Hermitian term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
is not taken into account in the adiabatic form of DHFT  Eq. (
44
),  then, this will eventually lead to an apparent paradox since the curvature flux must always give zero value,

On the other hand, by keeping track of the emerging non-Hermitian contribution

V
Non-adiabatic Charge Pump

We now employ the Floquet states and counterpart Floquet-Bloch bands
\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})
in order to theoretically study the non-equilibrium and driven charge pumping with the DHFT.
Each spinless electron’s wavefunction evolves in time by the Hamiltonian,

{\displaystyle H(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{2m}{\mathbf{p}^{2}+e\phi(x,t)}+V_{crys}(\mathbf{r}),}

where
m
and
e
are the mass and charge of the electron respectively,

\mathbf{p}
is the canonical momentum operator, and
V_{crys}(\mathbf{r})
is the crystal potential.
The applied scalar potential is given by

\phi(x,t)=\frac{a}{2\pi}E(t)\,cos(2\pi x/a+\theta),

(59)

where
a
is the primitive cell length in
x
direction,
{E(t)}
is the amplitude of the microscopic
electric field, and
\theta
is a dimensionless constant.
The scalar potential satisfies

{\phi(x+na,t)=\phi(x,t)}
where
n
is an integer, as well as

{\phi(x,T)=\phi(x,0)=0}
.
Whenever
\theta
is zero (or more generally
\theta=2\pi n
), the Hamiltonian has mirror symmetry along
\bm{e}_{x}
direction, otherwise the mirror symmetry is broken.
The macroscopic (spatially averaged) electric field is zero, thus, no voltage-bias is present during the pumping.
The time-dependent amplitude of the electric field

E(t)
is periodic
{E(t+T)=E(t)}
, but, has arbitrary large velocity
\displaystyle\frac{dE(t)}{dt}
, therefore, no adiabatic time-evolution can be implied. Instead, we use the non-equilibrium Floquet states and apply the DHFT of Eq. (
34

Each electron’s wavefunction evolves in time according to the TDSE

(60)

and the solution of  Eq. (

The Floquet modes

satisfy the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)-i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

where

{H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)}

is the (static) quasienergy that is restricted into the interval

{\displaystyle-\pi\hbar/T\leq\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})\leq\pi\hbar/T}
called
first Floquet-zone which contains all the physically non-equivalent quantum states.
Furthermore, we assume that each Floquet mode is expressed
with respect to the Floquet-Bloch state

{\mathbf{r}}
and

Whenever the Floquet Hamiltonian

is time-reversal invariant the quasienergy spectrum is symmetric with respect to crystal momentum inversion

. Moreover, when the Floquet Hamiltonian satisfies mirror symmetry, i.e. along

The parameter definition is made by using the assumed form of the quantum state.
Due to the presence of translational invariance of the Hamiltonian, the wave vector

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\left\langle\text{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{a,\,x}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\ -\ i\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\left\langle\!\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{d\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\!\right\rangle

In this respect, each electron’s displacement

where, interestingly, the quantity

(71)

For a spinless electron and time-reversal symmetric Floquet Hamiltonian,
the Floquet modes satisfy

{\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}

(up to a constant

{U(1)}

{e^{\displaystyle i\lambda}}

{\displaystyle i\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{d\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\right\rangle=i\left\langle\Phi_{a}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{d\Phi_{a}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}{d(-t)}\right\rangle,}

which results to an Aharonov-Anandan phase that is symmetric under the inversion of crystal momentum

{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})=\beta_{a}(-\mathbf{k})}
.
Analogously, for a space-inversion symmetric Floquet Hamiltonian
(
{\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Phi_{a}(-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})}

up to a constant

{U(1)}

{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})=\beta_{a}(-\mathbf{k})}
, while, for a mirror-symmetric Floquet Hamiltonian, i.e. along
x

{\beta_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\beta_{a}(-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}
.

Interestingly, whenever the Floquet Hamiltonian satisfies time-reversal symmetry but the mirror symmetry is broken along
x
, then,

\varepsilon_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})-\varepsilon_{a}(-k_{x},-k_{y},-k_{z})=0

(due to time-reversal symmetry)

{\varepsilon_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})-\varepsilon_{a}(-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\neq 0}
(due to breaking of the mirror symmetry), for every crystal momentum within the first Floquet-Brillouin zone. This implies that there is at least one discontinuity of the Floquet quasienergy band (which extends in the interval

{\displaystyle-\pi\hbar/T\leq\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})\leq\pi\hbar/T}
)  while traversing the
k_{x}
momenta of the first Brillouin zone, which results to an apparent winding (along the quasienergy axe) of the Floquet band.
Provided that there is no gap in the
first Floquet-Brillouin zone, each apparent winding (discontinuity)
contributes a quantized amount of

{\displaystyle|\Delta\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})|=2\pi\hbar/T}

resulting into
{\displaystyle\int_{BZ}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}dk_{x}=n\,2\pi\hbar/T}

where

is the integer number of windings.

Similarly, when the Floquet Hamiltonian satisfies time-reversal symmetry but the mirror symmetry is broken along
x
, then, the Aharonov-Anandan phase satisfies

\beta_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})-\beta_{a}(-k_{x},-k_{y},-k_{z})=0

(due to time-reversal symmetry),
but,
{\beta_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})-\beta_{a}(-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\neq 0}
(due to breaking of the mirror symmetry) for every crystal momentum within the first Floquet-Brillouin zone.
This implies that there is at least one discontinuity of the Aharonov-Anandan phase
{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})}
with unspecified jump while traversing the
k_{x}
momenta of the first Brillouin zone.
Specifically, although the Aharonov-Anandan phase values (modulo
2\pi
) lie in the interval
{0\leq\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})\leq 2\pi}
,
the jump is not necessarily equal to
2\pi

because the phase is not coming out as a product of an eigenvalue equation,
in contrast to the Floquet energy

{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}

1
1

1

Each Floquet energy gives the total phase of the quantum state after the time period of
T
, that is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})T
,  therefore, all different quasienergy eigenvalues define the projective Hilbert space of rays of the single valued Floquet modes

which is the eigenvalue of the Floquet Hamiltonian.
In this respect, for mirror-breaking (along
x
) Floquet Hamiltonians, we cannot naively assume that

{\displaystyle\int_{BZ}\frac{\partial\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}dk_{x}=0}
,
and the Aharonov-Anandan phase must be taken into account when
calculating the collective non-Hermitian displacement of the electrons.
We conclude that, for mirror-breaking Floquet Hamiltonians,
the Aharonov-Anandan phase behaves as a non-integrable quantity with respect to crystal momentum,
and as a consequence it contributes to charge pumping an amount proportional to its non quantized winding.

V.1.2
Mirror-breaking Floquet Hamiltonian

For a mirror-symmetric along

Floquet Hamiltonian we assume

{\theta\neq 2\pi n}

Due to mirror symmetry breaking,

{0\leq t\leq T}
and

{\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{a}\leq k_{x}\leq\frac{\pi}{a}}

{\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t+T,\mathbf{k})}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n\,a\ +\ C_{1}^{(n)}\alpha

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-\frac{a^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{y}\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{z}\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\frac{\partial\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!dk_{x},

{\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Phi_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}+G_{x}\mathbf{e}_{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k}+G_{x}\mathbf{e}_{x})-\beta_{a}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=i\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k}+G_{x}\mathbf{e}_{x})\,|\,\frac{d\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k}+G_{x}\mathbf{e}_{x})}{dt}\right\rangle dt

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-i\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{d\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\right\rangle dt

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=0.

In a slightly different context, presumably a more intuitive one, by employing  Eq. (
68
),
the pumped charge can be expressed in terms of the dynamical phase as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(n)}\,\alpha

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!+\frac{a^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{y}\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{z}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\frac{\partial D_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!dk_{x},

where the dynamical phase is given by
\displaystyle D_{a}(\mathbf{k})=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|H(t)\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle dt
.
If the Hamiltonian is mirror-inversion symmetric along

, then,

{C_{1}^{(n)}=0}
, and,
due to the dynamical phase that satisfies

\displaystyle D_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=D_{a}(-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

the pumped charge turns into zero. On the other hand, for the present mirror-reversal breaking case

\displaystyle D_{a}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\neq D_{a}(-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

In conclusion, for a mirror-breaking Hamiltonian, the quantization of the pumped charge breaks down due to a non-integrable Aharonov-Anandan phase even if no gaps exist in the Floquet-Brillouin zone, provided that the insulator is a topologically nontrivial one, where the periodic gauge cannot be taken (the well-known obstruction in satisfying it).

V.2
Trivialization of the non-adiabatic displacement

The boundary velocity Eq. (
69
) can be simplified by assuming that,

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle=0}
,  which is the integrability condition that guarantees that the Floquet modes
{\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle}

are analytic (single valued) functions of
t
and
k_{x}
in the
{t\!\times\!k_{x}}
space. Therefore,
either the material is a conventional band insulator if we take into account the entire
{t\!\times\!k_{x}}
space, or we are assuming a fiber bundle theory with patches.
In this framework, the Berry curvature takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{a,x}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\langle\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\right\rangle

and the boundary velocity
Eq. (
69
)  truncates into

\displaystyle\left\langle\text{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle_{a,\,x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\ -\ i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\langle\!\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle

(76)

\displaystyle\,-\,\frac{1}{\hbar}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{a,\,x}}(t,\mathbf{k}).

Therefore,
by assuming that
{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{a,x}}(t,\mathbf{k})=0}
,  the center of mass, non-adiabatic displacement over the boundaries Eq. (
V.1
) takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\,=-\frac{a^{3}\,T}{(2\pi)^{3}\,\hbar}\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{y}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{z}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!dk_{x}\ -\ \frac{a^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{y}\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!dk_{z}\!\int\limits_{0}^{T}\frac{\gamma_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\!dt

where
{\gamma_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}

is a first Brillouin zone accumulated phase given by

\gamma_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=i\!\int\limits_{-\pi/a}^{+\pi/a}\!\!\!A_{x}(t,\mathbf{k})\,dk_{x},

(77)

where

{\displaystyle A_{x}(t,\mathbf{k})=i\left\langle\!\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle}

is the Berry connection of the Floquet modes.
The Floquet modes
{\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle}
are periodic with respect to the time period
T
,
{\left|\Phi_{a}(t+T,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle=\left|\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle}
.  If we further assume that the momentum derivative of the Floquet modes

{\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle}

is also periodic with respect to the time period
T
,
that is,
{\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t+T,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle=\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle}
,
which is true whenever the Floquet modes are analytic functions of
{k_{x}}
in the
{t\!\times\!k_{x}}
space,  then, it is evident that
\displaystyle\left\langle\!\Phi_{a}(T,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(T,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\Phi_{a}(0,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{a}(0,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\!\right\rangle
which results into

{\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{T}\frac{\gamma_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\!dt=0}
,  thus, the pumped charge is then given only in terms of the winding of the Floquet band. This is the formal case that is encountered in the literature
Shih and Niu 1994
;
Privitera et al. 2018
, recovered here as a special instance of our more general theory.

VI
Electronic polarization

By using the DHFT and the extended velocity operator definition, we now calculate the adiabatic electronic polarization change of a fully occupied valence band
{\Delta\mathbf{P}_{n}}
. In this framework, we calculate the collective (adiabatic) electrons’ displacement
{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle\mathbf{r}_{i}\right\rangle}
over the boundaries
(while a crystal is modified or deformed) that gives the induced dipole moment change of the material, which in turn provides the quantum electronic induced polarization change when divided by the volume of the material.

One of the main differences between polarization change and charge pumping processes, is the voltage-bias (non-zero mean force) that is present in the polarization phenomena while is absent in pumping counterparts. In this manner, the presence of voltage-bias enforces a breaking of periodic Born-von Kármán boundary conditions between the edges of the material, hence

{\left|\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L},t)\right|^{2}\neq\left|\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t)\right|^{2}}
, although the wavefunction may retain its Bloch form in the bulk.
According to Ref.
Resta and Vanderbilt 2007
, similar breaking of the periodic Born-von Kármán boundary conditions emerges in piezoelectric phenomena due to strain, which is not merely a perturbing term in the Hamiltonian.

For an applied electric filed that is now spatially uniform, each spinless electron’s quantum state evolves in time according to the TDSE

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\left(\frac{1}{2m}\mathbf{p}^{2}-e\mathbf{r}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{E}(t)+V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\Psi(\mathbf{r},t),

(78)

where
{\mathbf{E}(t)}
is the applied homogenous electric field
that is also assumed to be time-periodic and to satisfy
{\mathbf{E}(0)=\mathbf{E}(T)=0}
.
The crystal potential
{V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},t)}
is assumed to have time-dependence, either as a result of the externally applied electric field, or due to applied strain, both deforming the crystal’s ions orderly arrangement.
For example, when computing the spontaneous polarization
\mathbf{P}_{s}
in a ferroelectric material such as
{\text{PbTiO}_{3}}
, one can let

{V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},0)}
refer to the centrosymmetric cubic crystal structure and

{V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},T)}
to the noncentrosymetric, ferroelectric crystal structure.

Due to the lack of translational invariance of the Hamiltonian, the wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
is not a conserved quantity of the dynamics.
We assume the ansatz

\psi(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}u(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

(79)

where the wave vector
{\mathbf{k}(t)}

is a continuous time-dependent vector parameter.
We define the time-dependent parameter
{\mathbf{R}(t)}
as equal to the time-dependent wave vector
{\mathbf{R}(t)\equiv\mathbf{k}(t)}
,  and then replace
Eq. (
79
)  into
Eq. (
78
).
The time-dependent wave-vector (parameter) is defined to satisfy the equation of motion

\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}(t)

(80)

which guarantees that, if the electron is initially in a Bloch state, it will continue to be in a Bloch state after the electric field is turned on.
Therefore, the parameter evolves in time according to

\mathbf{k}(t)=\mathbf{k}_{o}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\displaystyle t}\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

(81)

where
{\mathbf{k}_{o}}
is the initial value of the parameter.
Substituting  Eq. (
79
)  into
Eq. (
78
),  and by using
Eq. (
80
)  gives

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}u(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})u(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

(82)

where the quantum state

{u(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

evolves over time by the Hamiltonian

H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\mathbf{p}+\hbar\mathbf{k}(t)\right)^{2}\,+\,V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},t).

(83)

We assume that each electron is initially in a ground, Bloch state, and that the electric field turns on and changes very slowly over time
{T\rightarrow\infty}
,  resulting to adiabatic time-evolution, namely

u(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\equiv e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

where
{\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
is the total phase of the wavefunction (the sum of the dynamic and the geometric adiabatic phase) and

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
is the instantaneous eigenstate of the Hamiltonian

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
satisfying the eigenvalue equation

H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}).

(84)

We apply the adiabatic form of the
DHFT Eq. (
34
)
on  Eq. (
82
)  where the assumed Hamiltonian is

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
.
The gradient of the Hamiltonian with respect to the wave vector gives

{\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\hbar\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}[H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{r}]\right)=\hbar\mathbf{v}}
,
where
\mathbf{v}
is the standard velocity operator.
Therefore, we find

\displaystyle\hbar\left\langle\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})

(85)

\displaystyle\,-\,\hbar\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\hbar\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k}),

where all involved quantities are evaluated with respect to the time-dependent eigenstates
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
.  Specifically, the curvatures are given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(86)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

and

\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle,

and the non-Hermitian contribution is given from

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})=\left\langle u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H_{k}(t,\mathbf{k})^{+}-H_{k}(t,\mathbf{k})\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle.

(87)

We note that, when the instantaneous eigenstates
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
do not have explicit time-dependence the electric type of Berry curvature

{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
is by definition zero.

A crucial difference with respect to the theoretical approach that was made in the study of the adiabatic topological charge pumping (Thouless pump) is that the states
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
are assumed to be cell periodic in the bulk of the material at every instant, but need not satisfy periodic boundary conditions over the boundaries of the material, therefore

{\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k})}

is not zero, due to the presence of voltage-bias or strain.
We can then use  Eq. (
47
)  that relates the non-Hermitian contributions, that is,

{\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=\hbar\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\right\rangle_{n}\,+\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}(t,\mathbf{k}),}

where
{\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\right\rangle_{n}}
is the non-Hermitian boundary velocity expectation value (that is non-zero when the position operator
\mathbf{r}
becomes anomalous)
and
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}

is evaluated with respect to the instantaneous Bloch state, namely,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(88)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left(\,(\mathbf{v}\,\psi_{n})^{\dagger}+\psi_{n}^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{v}\,\right)\!\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\psi_{n}\,dS,

where
{\psi_{n}\equiv\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,
\mathbf{v}
is the standard velocity operator and
\mathbf{n}
is the unit vector that is locally normal to the surface
S
that encloses the material. The non-Hermitian contribution
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}

is always a real quantity according to  Eq. (
32
).
In this framework
Eq. (
85
)  takes the form

\displaystyle\hbar\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}\,\right\rangle_{n}+\hbar\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}\,\right\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

(89)

\displaystyle\,-\,\hbar\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\hbar\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k}).

By then using the extended velocity operator definition

{\displaystyle\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{ext}\,\right\rangle_{n}=\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}\,\right\rangle_{n}+\left\langle\,\mathbf{v}_{b}\,\right\rangle_{n}=\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\!\left\langle\,\mathbf{r}\,\right\rangle_{n},}

we obtain the electron’s mean position time derivative that is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\!\left\langle\,\mathbf{r}\,\right\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

(90)

\displaystyle\,-\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k}).

where
{\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
is the group velocity.

Therefore, each electron’s adiabatic displacement after the cyclic evolution of the Hamiltonian over a period of time
T
is given by

\Delta\left\langle u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})|\,\mathbf{r}\,|u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right)dt.

(91)

By then using  Eq.(
3
)  in the thermodynamic limit, the induced electronic polarization change for a single fully occupied valence band is given by

\Delta\mathbf{P}_{n}=\frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,+\,\frac{1}{\hbar}\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,-\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\!\times\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right)d^{3}k.

(92)

Some remarks are then in order regarding the way we have transformed the sum of  Eq. (
3
)  into an integration with respect to the time-dependent parameter
{\mathbf{k}(t)}
in
Eq. (
92
).
Mathematically speaking,
the equation of motion of the time-dependent parameter in the
DHFT
can always be defined arbitrarily.
Each elementary volume in parameter space (assumed to be occupied by a single spinless electron) expands or dilates according to the equation

{\displaystyle d^{3}k(t)=\text{Det}J(t,t_{o})\ d^{3}k_{o}}

where
{\text{Det}J(t,t_{o})}
denotes the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of the transformation that satisfies
{\text{Det}J(t_{o},t_{o})=1}
.
The Jacobian matrix, captures the transformation of the initial value of the parameter
to a subsequent one.
The time evolution of the Jacobian matrix
is solely determined by the way that one defines the velocity of the parameter and is given by
{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\text{Det}J(t,t_{o})=\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\right)\!\text{Det}J(t,t_{o}).}
In this framework, the equation of motion of the elementary volume of the parameter is determined by

{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(d^{3}k(t)\right)=\left(\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}\right)d^{3}k(t)}
.  Owing to our definition of the parameter  Eq. (
80
),
{\displaystyle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}}{\partial t}=0}
, which indicates that the elementary volume per particle in parameter space remains constant
{d^{3}k_{o}=d^{3}k(t)}
,  so, no modification is needed when we transform the sum into an integration over time-dependent wavevectors.

Comparing Eq. (
92
) to the polarization change formula
of the Modern’s Theory of Polarization
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
,
we have rigorously found three extra contributions: one due to the group velocity

{\bm{\upsilon}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
,  another one due to the non-Hermitian boundary contribution

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
,  and the last one due to the Berry curvature

{\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
.

A question now emerges: is each one of these extra contributions non-zero separately?
To answer the question based on simple criteria, we must rely on the localization of the assumed electrons’ wavefunctions (in general the realistic boundary conditions of the Bloch eigenstates) and the discrete symmetries of the assumed states.
Due to localization, for example, in the simplest model, when encountering only bulk localized states, in the sense that each electron completely avoids the boundaries of the material, the non-Hermitian contribution is zero
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=0}
according to  Eq. (
88
).
On the other hand, in order to take into account the consequences of the involved discrete symmetries, we present
in Table

the transformations of the quantities entering into Eq. (
92
) as a result of, time-reversal symmetry (T.R.S.), space-inversion symmetry (S.I.S.) and mirror symmetry along
x
(M.S.);
each symmetry is derived in Appendix
C
.

VI.0.1
Time-reversal symmetry

When the Hamiltonian

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
given by  Eq. (
83
)  is time-reversal symmetric, namely, is invariant under the inversions
{(i,\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}(t))\longmapsto(-i,\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k}(t))}
,
and provided that the Hamiltonian
{H(\mathbf{r},t)}
of  Eq. (
78
)  has an
intermediate time period symmetry
{H(\mathbf{r},0)=H(\mathbf{r},T)=H(\mathbf{r},T/2)}
,  (although

{\displaystyle\frac{dH(\mathbf{r},0)}{dt}\neq\frac{dH(\mathbf{r},T/2)}{dt}}
),  implying that the wavefunctions differ by a phase

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},0)=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{1k}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},T)=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{2k}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},T/2)}
, leads to zero polarization change

{\Delta\mathbf{P}_{n}=0}
for the time interval of
T
.
We point out that, the inversions actually assumed are
{(i,\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}_{o})\longmapsto(-i,\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k}_{o})}

where

{\mathbf{k}_{o}}

is the initial value of the wavevector. The time evolution of the wavevector
{\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(t)=\mathbf{k}_{o}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\displaystyle t}\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}
,  indicates that
{\mathbf{k}(t)\longmapsto-\mathbf{k}(t)}

when

{\mathbf{E}(-t)=\mathbf{E}(t)}
,
which is true due to the assumed time-reversal symmetry.
This, for example, gives zero polarization change for the whole hysteresis loop for an assumed time-reversal electric field

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c r}\mathbf{E}_{o}\,\left|sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right)\right|&\mbox{for}&0\leq t\leq\frac{T}{2}\\[8.0pt]
-\mathbf{E}_{o}\,\left|sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right)\right|&\mbox{for}&\frac{T}{2}\leq t\leq{T},\end{array}\right.

while for the half hysteresis loop

{0\leq t\leq T/2}
,
this gives non-zero polarization change along the direction of the electric field.
Time-reversal symmetry, can for example be broken when the material is magnetically ordered; in this framework, each now spinful electron’s Hamiltonian
Eq. (
83
)  may assume to have an extra term
{-J_{ex}\,\bm{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{M}}}
, where
{J_{ex}}
is the exchange coupling strength between the electron spin and the magnetization and
{\hat{\mathbf{M}}}
is the magnetization unit vector.

VI.0.2
Space-inversion and mirror symmetries

Space-inversion symmetry can for example be broken by an externally applied electric field or by a time-dependent strain that deforms the crystal.
The time-dependent electric field breaks the space-inversion symmetry of the Hamiltonian: under the inversions
{(i,\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}_{o})\longmapsto(i,-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k}_{o})}
,
the time-dependent wavevector

{\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(t)=\mathbf{k}_{o}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\displaystyle t}\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}
does not transform like

{\mathbf{k}(t)\longmapsto-\mathbf{k}(t)}

due to the electric field

{\mathbf{E}(t)}

that is not inverted, therefore, the Hamiltonian
{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
does not remain invariant.
Similarly, an assumed time-dependent strain that deforms the crystal breaks the space-inversion symmetry of the Hamiltonian for the time interval that

{V_{crys}(\mathbf{r},t)\neq V_{crys}(-\mathbf{r},t)}
.

Likewise, mirror inversion symmetry is broken in the direction of the externally applied field and in the directions that the crystal potential is modified either due to lower equilibrium symmetry of the crystal or as a result of deformation by an externally applied strain.

VI.1
Longitudinal and transverse polarization change

Assuming that the externally applied electric field
is in the
x
direction
{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{k}(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{e}{\hbar}E(t)\,\mathbf{e}_{x}}
,
the polarization change  Eq. (
92
)  can be separated into two normal contributions, the longitudinal one in the
x
direction

{\Delta{P}_{n,x}=\Delta\mathbf{P}_{n}\!\cdot\mathbf{e}_{x}}
and the transverse one normal to the electric field

{\Delta{P}_{n,y}=\Delta\mathbf{P}_{n}\!\cdot\mathbf{e}_{y}}
.

The longitudinal polarization change in the
x
direction, where the mirror symmetry is broken,
is given by

\Delta{P}_{n,x}=\frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\upsilon_{n,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k\ \ +\ \frac{e}{\hbar(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{S}_{n,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k\ -\ \frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k,

(95)

where the last term on the right side is the
Modern’s Theory of Polarization well-known contribution
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
.
On the other hand, the transverse polarization change
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta{P}_{n,y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\upsilon_{n,y}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k\ \ +\ \frac{e}{\hbar(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{S}_{n,y}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k\ -\ \frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{E}_{n,\,y}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k

(96)

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\,\left(E(t)\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{B}_{n,\,z}(t,\mathbf{k})d^{3}k\right)

and has an extra bulk contribution, that is
{\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}dt\,\left(E(t)\iiint\limits_{BZ}\mathcal{B}_{n,\,z}(t,\mathbf{k})\right),}

to be compared to the longitudinal counterpart  Eq. (
95
).
Assuming that, during the polarization change process, the mirror symmetry remains unbroken along the transverse
y
direction, the first three contributions on the right side of  Eq. (
96
)  integrate to zero according to Table
1
. On the other hand, the last contribution does not turn into zero due to mirror symmetry; instead, it integrates to zero due to time-reversal symmetry if it is present. Therefore, in magnetically ordered materials, where time-reversal symmetry is broken, the last contribution is not zero (although mirror symmetry may be present along
y
direction).
The above transverse polarization change formula, is plausible to be compared to the one found by Ref.
Goryo and Kohmoto 2002

where the electrons were subject to static and homogeneous electric and magnetic fields.

VI.2
Experimental setups and the non-Hermitian contribution

It is helpful to investigate the existence of the
non-Hermitian contributions in two important polarization change experiments. Namely, in the polarization change experiment performed with a ferroelectric material and in the induced polarization change experiment performed with a piezoelectric material.

VI.2.1
Spontaneous polarization change in a ferroelectric material

In an insulating ferroelectric material, the spontaneous polarization change is experimentally evaluated by applying a time-periodic bias
{V(\mathbf{r},t)}
at the opposite boundaries of the material (which is in a capacitor configuration) that results to a time-varying current
{I(t)}

In this framework (assuming non-magnetically ordered material), both integrands containing

VI.2.2
Induced polarization change in a piezoelectric material

In an insulating piezoelectric material, strains are externally applied to the material without any externally applied electric field

{\mathbf{E}=0}
. The material is inserted into a shorted capacitor and the surface charges are removed by the electrodes passing through a shorten ammeter. While the crystal is strained the transient current flowing trough the material is measured.
In this respect, we can employ a Hamiltonian like  Eq. (
83

{\phi(x,t)=0}
,  but, with time-dependent effective deformation energy term owing to time-dependent strains in order to describe the piezoelectric phenomena. The Hamiltonian

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t))}
will have implicit time-dependence due to the assumed time-dependent

{\varepsilon_{ij}(t)}
stain tensor coefficients.
In this fashion, the assumed instantaneous eigenvalue equation to be satisfied is

H_{k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t))\ u_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t))=E_{n}(\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t))\ u_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t)),
and because the instantaneous eigenfunctions

do not have explicit time-dependence, results to zero curvature

{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\mathbf{k},\varepsilon_{ij}(t))=0}

by definition Eq. (

Therefore, the polarization change due to the piezoelectric phenomena can be theoretically modelled solely by means of the non-Hermitian boundary term

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}

VII
Conclusions

In this work we have re-examined the charge pumping and polarization change processes.
By using the extended velocity operator definition and a dynamical extension of the Hellmann-Feynman theorem (DHFT) that we have derived, we have shown how the charge pumping can be linked to the boundaries of the system by means of a displacement originating from an emergent
non-Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian, that can be evaluated in terms of a Berry curvature owing to the DHFT.
Similarly, by using the extended velocity operator and the DHFT, we have extended the modern theory of polarization by finding two overlooked contributions. One is an emergent boundary non-Hermitian contribution, and the other depends on generalized Berry curvatures.

Boundary non-Hermitian contributions are involved in both extended velocity operator definition and DHFT.
A common characteristic of these non-Hermitian contributions is that they are both by definition bulk quantities, that, however, may equally be evaluated as boundary quantities due to a symmetry that allows the bulk integrations to be transformed into boundary integrations.
These non-Hermitian boundary contributions are always overlooked within von Neumann’s theory of self-adjoint operators. On the other hand, if one lays on the Schrödinger (or Dirac) dynamics, and insists on the unitary time evolution of closed systems, the non-Hermitian boundary contributions have to be taken into account a priori in order to have self-consistent coupling between time-evolution and observables’ operators. This route leads to an extended definition of operators which gives contributions to observables so far widely overlooked.

We have also studied the non-equilibrium, driven charge pumping processes by assuming delocalized Floquet-Bloch states for the electrons’ motion. By using the extended velocity operator definition together with the non-adiabatic form of DHFT, we have evaluated the collective displacement of the center of mass of the electrons
{\sum_{i}^{N}\Delta\left\langle x_{i}\right\rangle\!|_{bound}\ /\ {N}}
that has an emergent non-Hermitian origin.
We have found that, for a fully occupied Floquet-Bloch band
\varepsilon_{a}
,  the quantization of the pumped charge (per cycle)
breaks down due to a non-trivial (non-integrable) Aharonov-Anandan phase
Aharonov and Anandan 1987
, that emerges whenever the Floquet-Bloch states
cannot satisfy the periodic gauge along the direction of the externally applied electric field.

We then studied the general polarization change processes, and found that the adiabatic ground state polarization change
has two extra, overlooked, contributions which are absent from the modern theory
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Ortiz and Martin 1994
;
Resta 1994
;
Resta and Vanderbilt 2007
;
Resta 2010
;
Vanderbilt 2018
. The one is attributed to a Berry curvature and is not zero only within ferromagnetic insulators and the other is a non-Hermitian boundary contribution. When one takes into account only bulk localized states, that is, the wavefunctions are zero over the material boundaries, the overlooked non-Hermitian contribution always turns into zero.
This non-Hermitian contribution is very sensitive to the realistic boundary conditions of the wavefunctions during the time-periodic process that causes the polarization change. We therefore expect it to be significant in biased insulators with electron charge accumulation over their boundaries during the process that causes the polarization change (i.e. during the spontaneous polarization change experiments in a ferroelectric material, or during the induced polarization change experiments in a piezoelectric material).

Finally, we have demonstrated how one can extend the surface-charge theorem of
Ref.
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
.
We have shown how one can evaluate a well defined surface charge change in terms of a boundary, non-Hermitian contribution. When only bulk localized states are taken into account, the surface charge change  Eq. (
5
)
turns into zero by definition as expected. Overall, our work demonstrates that the widely overlooked emergent non-Hermiticities can be crucial to be included for the consistent and complete description of key properties and phenomena in the physics of condensed matter, especially in view of the fact that they are intertwined with geometric (of a generalized Berry type) and topological quantities, something that has carefully and rigorously been shown in the present article.

\displaystyle-i\hbar\nabla\!\cdot\!(\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}})\,-\,\frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!(\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}})\,+\,i\hbar(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{\mathcal{O}})\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}},

we find

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4,x}(t,\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{x}\iiint_{V}\left(\frac{}{}i\hbar\nabla\Psi^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}\,-\,\frac{e}{c}\Psi^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}\right)dV

(124)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{x}\iiint_{V}\left(-i\hbar\Psi^{\dagger}\,\nabla\!\cdot\!(\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}})\,-\,\frac{e}{c}\Psi^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!(\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}})\,+\,i\hbar\Psi^{\dagger}\,(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{\mathcal{O}})\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}\right)dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{x}(i\hbar)\iiint_{V}\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\,\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}\right)dV\,-\,\mathbf{e}_{x}(i\hbar)\iiint_{V}\Psi^{\dagger}\,(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{\mathcal{O}})\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{x}(i\hbar)\oiint_{S}\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\,\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial R_{x}}\right)\!\cdot\!d\mathbf{s}

where we have used
{\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{\mathcal{O}}=0}
, which is true either when taking into account the spin-orbit interaction

\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{O}}=\frac{\hbar}{4m^{2}c^{2}}\nabla\cdot\left(\bm{\sigma}\times\nabla V(\mathbf{r},t)\right)=\frac{\hbar}{4m^{2}c^{2}}\left(\,\nabla V(\mathbf{r},t)\!\cdot\!\left(\nabla\times\bm{\sigma}\right)-\bm{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\left(\nabla\times\nabla V(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\,\right)=0,

or when taking into account the relativistic kinetic energy

\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{O}}=c\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{\alpha}=0.

By adding all of the Cartesian components
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4}(t,\mathbf{R})=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4,x}(t,\mathbf{R})+\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4,y}(t,\mathbf{R})+\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4,z}(t,\mathbf{R})}
, we find the form of the

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4}(t,\mathbf{R})}

that is given from

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4}(t,\mathbf{R})=i\hbar\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!(\Psi^{\dagger}\,\bm{\mathcal{O}})\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi ds.

(125)

By then using
{(\bm{\mathcal{O}}\,\Psi)^{\dagger}=\Psi^{\dagger}\,\bm{\mathcal{O}}}
in Eq. (
125
) we restructure it in the symmetrical form

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{4}(t,\mathbf{R})=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left((\bm{\mathcal{O}}\Psi)^{\dagger}\,+\,\Psi^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{O}}\right)\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi ds.

(126)

A.3
Analytic form of
\mathbf{S}(t,\mathbf{R})

In this framework, the non-Hermitian boundary term contribution

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}

of the DHFT is evaluated by

\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})=\lambda_{1}\,\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left((\,\frac{\bm{\Pi}}{m}\Psi\,)^{\dagger}\,+\,\Psi^{\dagger}\frac{\bm{\Pi}}{m}\,\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,ds\right)\ +\ \lambda_{4}\,\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left((\bm{\mathcal{O}}\Psi)^{\dagger}\,+\,\Psi^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{O}}\right)\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi ds\right)

(127)

where the
\lambda_{i}
accounts for the different energy scales of the electron, thus for different types of Hamiltonians.
By noting that,
{\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}[\,H_{1}(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\,,\mathbf{r}\,]=\frac{\bm{\Pi}}{m}}
as well as
{\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}[\,H_{4}(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\,,\mathbf{r}\,]=\bm{\mathcal{O}}}
,
and the fact that

{[\,H_{2}(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\,,\mathbf{r}\,]=[\,H_{3}(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\,,\mathbf{r}\,]=[\,H_{5}(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\,,\mathbf{r}\,]=0}
,
and by also using the definition of the standard velocity operator

\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R}),\mathbf{r}\right]=\mathbf{v},

(128)

we find that the non-Hermitian boundary contribution

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})}
of the DHFT is in general evaluated by

\bm{\mathcal{S}}(t,\mathbf{R})=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{n}\!\cdot\!\left(\,(\mathbf{v}\,\Psi^{+}+\Psi^{+}\,\mathbf{v}\,\right)\!\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}}\Psi\,dS,

(129)

either for (i) a spinless non-relativistic motion, (ii) a fully relativistic (Dirac) spinful motion, or a (iii) a spin-full motion in the non-relativistic limit; with the only requirement that one needs to use the appropriate Hamiltonian
{H(t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})}
generating the time-evolution. The above final form is indeed the flux (across the 2D boundary) of a generalized current (see main text).

We consider a parameter with arbitrary high magnitude of velocity

Appendix C
Discrete symmetry considerations

It is useful to investigate the discrete symmetries that are satisfied by the curvatures and the non-Hermitian contribution, Eqs.(
136
) – (
138
), that enters into the DHFT  Eq.(
133
).
These symmetries gives valuable information for the collective transport quantities when they are calculated by means of the DHFT
Eq.(
133
).
Specifically, we are assuming solutions of
Eq.(
20
)  that have the ansatz form  Eq.(
130
)  and we are deriving discrete symmetries that come out from the eigenvalue equation  (
140
)  for a nonequilibrium Floquet state, and from
(
144
)  for an adiabatically evolved eigenstate of the Hamiltonian.

C.1
Instantaneous eigenstates of the Hamiltonian

From the symmetries of the instantaneous eigenvalue equation

H(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{R})\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{R})=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{R}),

(149)

the symmetries of the curvatures themselves can be deduced, which gives valuable information about the topology of the projective Hilbert space of the energy band
{E_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}
.
In the latter eigenvalue equation, the time
t
and the parameter momentum
{\mathbf{R}}
are assumed to be parameters.
We identify the parameter entering into the DHFT as the
wave vector

{\mathbf{R}\equiv\mathbf{k}}
,  and we assume that the instantaneous wavefunction has the ansatz form

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
.  For simplicity reasons we assume spinless motion,
that is, the Hamiltonian is a scalar operator and the
wavefunctions
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
are scalar quantities. We further assume that the initial Hamiltonian does not have explicit wave vector (parameter) dependence, rather than the parameter is involved only due to the assumed form of the considered state

{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}).}

Therefore, the wavefunction
{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
satisfy the eigenvalue equation

H(\mathbf{r},t)\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

(150)

and the wavefunction

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
the corresponding

e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

(151)

where
{e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}=H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
.

We further assume adiabatic time-evolution, that is,

{\left|\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=e^{\displaystyle i\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})}\left|\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle}

satisfy the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

{\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\left|\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle=H(\mathbf{r},t)\left|\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{R})\right\rangle,}

{\displaystyle-\hbar\frac{d\Theta_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}=E_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})-i\hbar\left\langle\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})|\,\frac{d\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{dt}\right\rangle}

C.1.1
Time-reversal symmetry

A time-reversal symmetric (spinless) Hamiltonian

{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,
under the action of the anti-unitary operator
{\mathcal{O}_{\text{T.R.}}}
that performs the inversions

(i,\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\longmapsto(-i,\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})

remains invariant,

\mathcal{O}_{\text{T.R.}}\left(e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,\right)\mathcal{O}_{\text{T.R.}}^{-1}=e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}},

that is,
{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=H(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}
,
or simply
{H(\mathbf{r},t)=H(\mathbf{r},-t)^{\displaystyle*}}
.
As a result, the spinless eigenfunctions of a time-reversal invariant Hamiltonian satisfy the symmetries

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}

and

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=u_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*},}

up to a

{U(1)}
phase
{e^{\displaystyle i\lambda}}
which is neglected for simplicity (the curvatures and the non-Hermitian boundary term are invariant with respect to such phases).
In this respect, the curvatures
evaluated with respect to

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\left\langle\mathbf{r}|n(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle}

transform like

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(152)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(153)

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!-i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!-i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}{\partial(-t)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}{\partial(-t)}|\,\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k}).

Similarly, the non-Hermitian term transform like

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(154)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\left\langle\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}|\ \left(\!H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\left\langle\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}|\left(\!{H(\mathbf{r},t)^{\displaystyle*}}^{+}\!\!\!-\!H(\mathbf{r},t)^{\displaystyle*}\right)\!\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\left\langle\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}\!\!-\!H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=-\left\langle\left(\!H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}\!\!-\!H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\!\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\Psi_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(-t,-\mathbf{k}).

In the last step of the above equation we have used that
the non-Hermitian contribution satisfies the relation

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}+\left(\left\langle\psi_{n}\right|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}H\left.\psi_{n}\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}H\,\psi_{n}\right|\left.\!\psi_{n}\right\rangle\right),

thus, the non-Hermitian contribution
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
evaluated with respect to an instantaneous Bloch state

\left|\psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle
is always a real quantity owing to
{\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}H(\mathbf{r},t)=0}
.

All above
time-reversal discrete symmetry transformation properties remain the same in spinful motions, provided that the Hamiltonian satisfies

{H(\mathbf{r},t)=TH(\mathbf{r},-t)T^{-1}}
,  where

{T=-iK\sigma_{y}=K\left(\!\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&0\end{array}\!\right)}

is the so-called time-reversal operator,
{K}
is the complex conjugated operator and
{\sigma_{y}}
the Pauli matrice.
For spinful motions
the cell periodic wavefunction becomes a two-component spinor

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\equiv\left(a_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),\ b_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\right)^{\text{T}}}
,  where
{a_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

and
{b_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
are cell periodic scalar wavefunctions.
For time-reversal Hamiltonians, the spinor wavefunctions
satisfy

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=T\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})}

and

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,\mathbf{k})=Tu_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})}
,
that is, the components of the spinor wavefunction transform like

{a_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=-b_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}

and

{b_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=a_{n}(\mathbf{r},-t,-\mathbf{k})^{\displaystyle*}}
.
By using the transformation of the spinors’ components in the definition of the Berry curvatures, it is straightforward to verify that
Eqs. (
152
) - (
153
) also hold for spinful electrons under time-reversal symmetry. Similarly, by using

{H(\mathbf{r},t)=TH(\mathbf{r},-t)T^{-1}}
as well as
{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=T\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})}

it is easy to show that  Eq. (
154
)  is also valid for spinful electrons.

C.1.2
Space-inversion symmetry

In a space-inversion symmetric spinless Hamiltonian
{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
, under the action of the unitary operator
{\mathcal{O}_{\text{S.I.}}}
that performs the inversions

(i,\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\longmapsto(i,-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})

remains invariant,

\mathcal{O}_{\text{S.I.}}\left(e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,\right)\mathcal{O}_{\text{S.I.}}^{-1}=e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}},

that is,
{H_{k}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=H(-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})}
,
or
{H(\mathbf{r},t)=H(-\mathbf{r},t)}
.
As a result, the eigenfunctions of this space-inversion symmetric Hamiltonian satisfy the symmetries

{\Psi_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Psi_{n}(-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})}

and

{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=u_{n}(-\mathbf{r},t,-\mathbf{k})}
, and the curvatures transform like

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(155)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right|\times\left|\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

(156)

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))}{\partial t}|\,\bm{\nabla}_{(-\mathbf{k})}u_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}).

Analogously, the non-Hermitian term transform like

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(157)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\left\langle\Psi_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}}\Psi_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=-\left\langle\Psi_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{k},t)\right)\bm{\nabla_{(-\mathbf{k})}}\Psi_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,-\mathbf{k}).

C.1.3
Mirror inversion symmetry

We assume that the Hamiltonian is invariant under the action of the unitary operator
{\mathcal{O}_{\text{M.I.}}}

\mathcal{O}_{\text{M.I.}}\left(e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,\right)\mathcal{O}_{\text{M.I.}}^{-1}=e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,H(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}

that performs the mirror inversions
over the plane
x=0

(i,x,y,z,t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\longmapsto(i,-x,y,z,t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}).

The eigenfunctions satisfy the symmetries

\psi_{n}(x,y,z,t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\psi_{n}(-x,y,z,t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

and

u_{n}(x,y,z,t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=u_{n}(-x,y,z,t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

respectively.
Therefore, the curvatures
transform like

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\cdot\bm{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

(158)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{y}}\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{z}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{z}}|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{y}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}),

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\cdot\bm{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

(159)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\,|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-i\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}|\,\frac{\partial u_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{E}_{n,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}),

and the non-Hermitian term
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{n}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\cdot\bm{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\left\langle\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\,|\,\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)^{+}-H(\mathbf{r},t)\right)|\,\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}(t,\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{S}_{n,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}).

C.2
Floquet eigenstates

Similar arguments can also be made for
the Floquet eigenstates. The Floquet eigenstates

{\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}

are periodic in time

{\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t+T,\mathbf{k})}

and
satisfy the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\left(H(\mathbf{r},t)-i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})

where
{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}
is the
quasienergy.
We assume that each Floquet state is expressed
with respect to the Floquet-Bloch state
Gómez-León and Platero 2013

{\Phi_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),}

where
{u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
is periodic both in
{\mathbf{r}}
and
t
.
Accordingly, the wavefunction

{u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
satisfies the instantaneous eigenvalue equation

e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}\,u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})=\varepsilon_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})\,u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k}),

(161)

which is the counterpart  Eq. (
151
).
Therefore, by examining the symmetries that are satisfied by the Hamiltonian
{e^{\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\,e^{\displaystyle i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}}}

we may infer the symmetries of the
Floquet-Bloch states, which in turn gives the symmetries of the curvatures themselves that are evaluated with respect to the Floquet-Bloch states.

We can easily find the symmetries by mapping  Eq. (
151
)  to  Eq. (
161
)  with the identification
{H(\mathbf{r},t)\mapsto H_{F}(\mathbf{r},t)}
and
{u_{n}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})\mapsto u_{a}(\mathbf{r},t,\mathbf{k})}
,  and this gives for:

C.2.1
Time-reversal symmetry

{\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{a}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}

and
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}
,  as well as

{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{a}(-t,-\mathbf{k})}
.

C.2.2
Space-inversion symmetry

{\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{a}(t,-\mathbf{k})}

and
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(t,-\mathbf{k}),}

as well as
{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{a}(t,\mathbf{k})=-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{a}(t,-\mathbf{k})}
.

C.2.3
Mirror inversion symmetry

\mathcal{B}_{a,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\mathcal{B}_{a,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

and

\mathcal{E}_{a,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=-\mathcal{E}_{a,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
,
as well as

\mathcal{S}_{a,\,x}(t,k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=-\mathcal{S}_{a,\,x}(t,-k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
.
