Note on disjoint faces in simple topological graphs
Ji Zeng

Abstract
We prove that every n n -vertex complete simple topological graph generates at least Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) pairwise disjoint 4 4 -faces. This improves upon a recent result by Hubard and Suk. As an immediate corollary, every n n -vertex complete simple topological graph drawn in the unit square generates a 4 4 -face with area at most O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) . This can be seen as a topological variant of Heilbronn’s problem for 4 4 -faces. We construct examples showing that our result is asymptotically tight. We also discuss the similar problem for k k -faces with arbitrary k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 .



1
Introduction

A
topological graph
is a graph drawn in the plane such that its vertices are represented by points, and its edges are represented by non-self-intersecting curves connecting the corresponding points. The curves are not allowed to pass through vertices different from their endpoints, and if two edges share an interior point, then they must properly cross at that point in common. A topological graph is
simple
if any two of its edges intersect at most once, either at a common endpoint or at a proper crossing point. A subgraph
H
of a simple topological graph
G
is said to be
plane
if its edges do not cross. A
k
-face
generated by
G
refers to the bounded open cell enclosed by a plane
k
-cycle in
G
. Here a
k
-cycle means a cycle of length
k
. We remark that the boundary cycles of two disjoint faces can share vertices or edges.

The main result of this paper is the following theorem. It improves the main result in a recent paper
[
7
]
by Hubard and Suk from a weaker bound of
\Omega(n^{1/3})
in the same context.

Theorem 1.1
.

Every
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph generates at least
\Omega(n)
pairwise disjoint
4
-faces.

This gives us the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Every
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph drawn in the unit square generates a
4
-face with area at most
O(1/n)
.

We also show that Corollary
1.2
, and hence Theorem
1.1
, are asymptotically tight.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
n\geq 1
, there is a
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph drawn in the unit square such that every face it generates has area at least
\Omega(1/n)
.

The famous Heilbronn’s problem
[
9
]
asks to determine the smallest
h(n)
such that any set of
n
points in the unit square form a triangle with area at most
h(n)
. This problem is still open despite many efforts through over 70 years. The current best known bounds (due to
[
8
,
3
]
) for
h(n)
are

\Omega\left(\frac{\log n}{n^{2}}\right)<h(n)<O\left(n^{-\frac{8}{7}-\frac{1}{2000}}\right).

We call a topological graph
geometric
if its edges are straight line segments. With this notion, Heilbronn’s problem can be reformulated as follows: What is the smallest
h(n)
such that any
n
-vertex complete geometric graph drawn in the unit square generates a
3
-face with area at most
h(n)
? Therefore, Corollary
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
can be seen as answers to a topological variant of Heilbronn’s problem for
4
-faces.

It’s natural to consider the topological Heilbronn’s problem for
3
-faces. However, Hubard and Suk
[
7
]
pointed out that we cannot expect a similar phenomenon for
k
-faces when
k
is odd.

Theorem 1.4

(Hubard–Suk)
.

For every
n\geq 1
and every
\epsilon>0
, there is a
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph drawn in the unit square such that every odd face it generates has area at least
1-\epsilon
.

In particular, Theorem
1.4
tells us that it’s possible for all odd faces to have a nonempty intersection. On the other hand, we can still prove the existence of many pairwise disjoint
k
-faces when
k
is even.

Theorem 1.5
.

For even
k\geq 4
, every
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph generates at least
(\log n)^{1/4-o(1)}
pairwise disjoint
k
-faces.

We conjecture that the bound of
(\log n)^{1/4-o(1)}
in Theorem
1.5
can be improved to a bound of
\Omega(n)
. This will (if true) asymptotically match the counterpart given by Theorem
1.3
.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Our proof is based on a phenomenon of simple topological graphs discovered by Fulek and Ruiz-Vargas
[
4
,
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Fulek–Ruiz-Vargas)
.

Suppose
G
is a complete simple topological graph,
H
is a plane connected subgraph, and
v
is a vertex of
G
not in
H
, then there exist at least two edges between
v
and the vertices of
H
that do not cross
H
.

The following lemma is the key to our proof. It has been essentially proven (using Lemma
2.1
) by Hubard and Suk
[
7
]
except for some small cases.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
G
is a complete simple topological graph and
C\subset G
is a plane
k
-cycle, if the face
F
enclosed by
C
contains at least
6k
vertices of
G
in its interior, then
G
generates a
4
-face that lies inside
F
.

Proof Sketch.

When
k=3
, Lemma
2.1
obviously guarantees a
4
-face inside
F
as long as
F
contains
1
vertex of
G
. When
k\geq 5
, Lemma 3.4 in
[
7
]
states that if
F
contains more than
6(k-4)
vertices, then
G
generates a
4
-face inside
F
. This implies what we want. When
k=4
, we apply Lemma
2.1
to any vertex
v
of
G
inside
F
, so we obtain two edges emanating from
v
to
C
that lie inside
F
. This divides
F
into two faces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
4
-faces, then we are done. Otherwise, we can assume without loss of generality that
F_{1}
is a
3
-face and
F_{2}
is a
5
-face. By our hypothesis and the Pigeonhole principle, either
F_{1}
contains
1
vertex or
F_{2}
contains
6k-1=23
vertices, hence we finish the proof by reduction to previous cases.
∎

We need a fact by García, Pilz, and Tejel
[
5
]
, whose proof also relies on Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.3

(García–Pilz–Tejel)
.

In a complete simple topological graph, every plane subgraph is contained in another plane subgraph that is spanning and biconnected.

Our proof is inspired by the argument of the main result in
[
1
]
by Aichholzer et al.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
G
be any
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph. A
4
-cell
refers to a (bounded)
4
-face generated by
G
or an unbounded cell enclosed by a plane
4
-cycle of
G
. We consider a partial order on collections of pairwise disjoint
4
-cells of
G
. For two collections
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of disjoint
4
-cells, we say
\mathcal{C}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{C}_{2}
if
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|<|\mathcal{C}_{2}|
, or if
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=|\mathcal{C}_{2}|
and each
4
-cell in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is contained in some
4
-cell in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a maximal element with respect to this partial order.

Let
H
be the subgraph of
G
that consists of all the vertices and edges on the boundaries of the
4
-cells in
\mathcal{C}
. Notice that
H
is plane. By Lemma
2.3
, we can augment
H
, by adding only edges of
G
, to another plane subgraph
H^{\prime}\subset G
such that
H^{\prime}
is biconnected and the vertex sets of
H
and
H^{\prime}
coincide. Since
H^{\prime}
is plane and biconnected, it cuts the plane into cells
f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
each of whose boundaries is a plane cycle. Here,
f_{0}
denotes the unbounded cell of this cutting.

Let
|f_{i}|
be the number of vertices on the boundary of
f_{i}
, and
v(f_{i})
be the number of vertices in the interior of
f_{i}
. Suppose that
v(f_{0})\geq 6|f_{0}|
, we consider
G
as drawn on a sphere, choose a new pole outside
f_{0}
, and apply Lemma
2.2
, then we conclude that
G
generates a
4
-cell inside
f_{0}
. However, this contradicts
\mathcal{C}
being maximal with respect to
\preceq
. Similarly, by the maximality of
\mathcal{C}
and Lemma
2.2
, we must have

v(f_{i})<6|f_{i}|.

On the other hand, we have
\sum_{i}(|f_{i}|+v(f_{i}))\geq n
, so
\sum_{i}|f_{i}|>n/7
.

Let
v(H^{\prime})
be the number of vertices of
H^{\prime}
, and
e(H^{\prime})
be the number of edges of
H^{\prime}
. We know that
2e(H^{\prime})=\sum_{i}|f_{i}|
by double-counting, so we have
e(H^{\prime})>n/14
. Since
H^{\prime}
is plane, we have the classical bound
3v(H^{\prime})-6\geq e(H^{\prime})
. Hence, we have
v(H)=v(H^{\prime})>n/42
. Since each vertex of
H
lies on the boundary of some cell in
\mathcal{C}
, and each cell in
\mathcal{C}
has
4
vertices on its boundary,

|\mathcal{C}|\geq v(H)/4>n/168.

Notice that
\mathcal{C}
contains at most one unbounded
4
-cell, and the others form a collection of pairwise disjoint
4
-faces. Therefore we conclude the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We describe a complete simple topological graph
D_{n}
drawn in the unit square
S=[0,1]\times[0,1]
.

First, for each
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we pick a point

v_{ij}=(\frac{i-1}{n}+\frac{j}{n(n+1)},0.5)\in S.

We also define a region
S_{i}=(\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n})\times(0.5,1)
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Notice that
v_{ij}
is located on the lower side of the boundary of
S_{i}
. Since each
S_{i}
has area
1/(2n)
, we can place a closed rectangle
R_{i}
inside
S_{i}
that has area
1/(3n)
.

Then, we draw curves in the upper half part of
S
. For each
i\neq j
, we draw a convex curve
c_{ij}
emanating from
v_{ii}
to
v_{ij}
such that the interior of
c_{ij}
is contained in
S_{i}
. These curves can be drawn without any crossings. Furthermore, we make sure that
R_{i}
is contained inside the convex hull of the curve
c_{ij}
whenever
j<i
. See Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the drawing in
S_{i}
.

Next, we draw curves in the lower half part of
S
. For each
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n
, we take
j_{1}=i_{1}+(n+1-i_{2})
and
j_{2}=i_{2}-i_{1}
. Then we draw a lower semicircle
s_{i_{1}i_{2}}
from
v_{i_{1}j_{1}}
to
v_{i_{2}j_{2}}
. We can check that two semicircles
s_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
s_{i_{3}i_{4}}
cross if and only if
i_{1}<i_{3}<i_{2}<i_{4}
or
i_{3}<i_{1}<i_{4}<i_{2}
. Note that two semicircles here can have at most one crossing.

Finally, we define the complete simple topological graph
D_{n}
. We take the point
v_{ii}
as the vertex
v_{i}
of
D_{n}
for each
i
. For each
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n
, we take
j_{1}=i_{1}+(n+1-i_{2})
and
j_{2}=i_{2}-i_{1}
and draw the edge
v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}
by concatenating the curve
c_{i_{1}j_{1}}
in
S_{i_{1}}
, the semicircle
s_{i_{1}i_{2}}
, and the curve
c_{i_{2}j_{2}}
in
S_{i_{2}}
. Because crossings of edges only happen between their semicircle part, we can check that
D_{n}
is indeed simple using the crossing pattern of our semicircles.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
C
be a plane
k
-cycle in
D_{n}
. Because two edges
v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}
(
i_{1}<i_{2}
) and
v_{i_{3}}v_{i_{4}}
(
i_{3}<i_{4}
) cross in
D_{n}
if and only if
i_{1}<i_{3}<i_{2}<i_{4}
or
i_{3}<i_{1}<i_{4}<i_{2}
, we can check that
C
can be written as
v_{i_{1}}\to v_{i_{2}}\to\dots\to v_{i_{k}}\to v_{i_{1}}
for some
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}
. Therefore, the face enclosed by
C
must contain the rectangles
R_{i_{2}},R_{i_{3}},\dots,R_{i_{k-1}}
each of whose areas is at least
1/(3n)
, concluding the proof.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In this proof, we shall consider two prototypes of simple topological graphs: convex geometric graphs and twisted graphs.

The complete
convex geometric graph

C_{m}
is a complete simple topological graph whose vertices are
m
points in convex position, and edges are straight line segments. We remark that any complete geometric graph must have its vertices in general position (i.e. no collinear triples). The vertices of
C_{m}
can be labelled as
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
, such that two edges
v_{i}v_{j}
(
i<j
) and
v_{k}v_{\ell}
(
k<\ell
) cross if and only if
i<k<j<\ell
or
k<i<\ell<j
.

The complete
twisted graph

T_{m}
is a complete simple topological graph whose vertices can be labelled as
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
, such that two edges
v_{i}v_{j}
(
i<j
) and
v_{k}v_{\ell}
(
k<\ell
) cross if and only if
i<k<\ell<j
or
k<i<j<\ell
. Specifically, for
j
ranging from
1
to
m
, we let
v_{j}
be the point
(j,0)
on the
x
-axis and draw the edges
v_{i}v_{j}
(
i<j
) as follows: for
i
ranging from
1
to
j-1
, we draw a convex curve
c_{ij}
from
v_{j}
to a point
u_{ij}
on the
y
-axis; we make sure the interior of the convex hull of
c_{ij}
contains the plane origin, the points
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{j-1}
, and all the previously drawn edges; we draw the edge
v_{i}v_{j}
by concatenating the curve
c_{ij}
and the line segment from
u_{ij}
to
v_{i}
. See Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Illustration of
T_{5}
. The cross represents the plane origin.

Our proof is based on a Ramsey-type result on the crossing patterns of simple topological graphs by Suk and Zeng
[
11
]
. Two simple topological graphs
G
and
H
are said to be
weakly isomorphic
if there is an incidence preserving bijection between
G
and
H
such that two edges of
G
cross if and only if the corresponding edges in
H
cross as well.

Theorem 4.1

(Suk–Zeng)
.

Every
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph has an induced subgraph on
m\geq(\log n)^{1/4-o(1)}
vertices that is weakly isomorphic to either
C_{m}
or
T_{m}
.

Another ingredient of our proof is the following theorem by Gioan
[
6
]
(see also
[
2
]
). A
triangle mutation
is a process of moving an edge over the crossing point of two other edges, assuming all three edges are pairwise crossing and the triangular region bounded by them doesn’t intersect other vertices or edges throughout this process. See Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of a triangle mutation. The triangular region is shaded.

Theorem 4.2

(Gioan)
.

If two complete simple topological graphs
G
and
H
are weakly isomorphic, then we can transform
G
to
H
by a sequence of triangle mutations and a homeomorphism of the sphere.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
G
be any
n
-vertex complete simple topological graph. By Theorem
4.1
, there are
m\geq(\log n)^{1/4-o(1)}
vertices of
G
inducing a subgraph
H
that is weakly isomorphic to either the convex geometric graph
C_{m}
or the twisted graph
T_{m}
. By Theorem
4.2
, we can transform
H
to either
C_{m}
or
T_{m}
by a sequence of triangle mutations and a homeomorphism of the sphere.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint
k
-faces, then after a triangle mutation, the cells corresponding to elements in
\mathcal{C}
still form a collection of pairwise disjoint
k
-faces. Indeed, we consider a triangle mutation moving an edge
e_{1}
over the crossing point of
e_{2}
and
e_{3}
. If
e_{1}
is not contained in the boundary of any face in
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{C}
remains unchanged after the triangle mutation. If
e_{1}
is contained in the boundary of some face in
\mathcal{C}
, then neither
e_{2}
nor
e_{3}
is the boundary of any face in
\mathcal{C}
as
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
are pairwise crossing. Because the triangular region bounded by
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
doesn’t intersect other vertices or edges, the triangle mutation only affects
\mathcal{C}
by a deformation of the one or two faces containing
e_{1}
. Moreover, a homeomorphism of the sphere may make at most one cell in
\mathcal{C}
become unbounded, the others still form a collection of pairwise disjoint
k
-faces.

It suffices for us to show that both
C_{m}
and
T_{m}
generate
\Omega(m)
many pairwise disjoint
k
-faces. For the convex geometric graph
C_{m}
, we can label its vertices as
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
in a non-decreasing order of
their
x
-coordinates. Then we divide the vertex set into
\Omega(m)
many
k
-tuples

\tau_{i}=\{v_{(i-1)k+1},\ v_{(i-1)k+2},\ \dots,\ v_{ik}\}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq\lfloor m/k\rfloor.

Because
C_{m}
is a convex geometric graph, the interior of the convex hull of each
\tau_{i}
is a
k
-face. Furthermore, the projections of these
k
-faces on the
x
-axis are pairwise disjoint open intervals, so
C_{m}
generates
\Omega(m)
many pairwise disjoint
k
-faces.

For the twisted graph
T_{m}
, we recall, from the beginning of this section, that the vertices of
T_{m}
are labelled as
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
. We divide the vertex set into
\Omega(m)
many
k
-tuples
\tau_{i}
as we did for
C_{m}
. For a particular
k
-tuple (for example)
\tau_{1}
, we define its corresponding cycle

C_{1}=v_{1}\to v_{\frac{k}{2}+1}\to v_{k}\to v_{\frac{k}{2}}\to v_{k-1}\to v_{\frac{k}{2}-1}\to v_{k-2}\to v_{\frac{k}{2}-2}\to\dots\to v_{\frac{k}{2}+2}\to v_{2}\to v_{1}.

Using the crossing pattern of
T_{m}
, we can check that
C_{1}
is plane and hence it encloses a
k
-face
F_{1}
. Similarly we can define the cycle
C_{i}
and the face
F_{i}
according to
\tau_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq\lfloor m/k\rfloor
. We claim that any two faces
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
(
i<j
) are disjoint. Indeed, we use
R
to denote the convex hull of the edge
v_{(i+1)k}v_{jk+1}
. By the process of drawing
T_{m}
, the induced subgraph on
\tau_{i}
is inside
R
, and the induced subgraph on
\tau_{j}
is outside
R
. As a consequence,
F_{i}
is contained in
R
, and
F_{j}
either contains
R
or doesn’t intersect
R
. Notice that the negative portion of the
x
-axis is a ray emanating from the plane origin that crosses
C_{j}
an even number of times. This means the plane origin is outside the face
F_{j}
enclosed by
C_{j}
. However, the plane origin is contained inside
R
. So
F_{j}
doesn’t intersect
R
and hence is disjoint from
F_{i}
. Therefore, we conclude that
T_{m}
generates
\Omega(m)
many pairwise disjoint
k
-faces.
∎
