A unified approach to higher order discrete and smooth isoperimetric inequalities
Kwok-Kun Kwong

Abstract
. We present a unified approach to derive sharp isoperimetric type inequalities of arbitrary high order. In particular, we obtain (i) sharp high order discrete polygonal isoperimetric type inequalities, (ii) sharp high order isoperimetric type inequalities for smooth curves with both upper and lower bounds for the isoperimetric deficit, and (iii) sharp higher order Chernoff type inequalities involving a generalized width function and higher order locus of curvature centers. Our approach involves obtaining higher order discrete or smooth Wirtinger inequalities via Fourier analysis, by examining a family of linear operators. The key to our approach is identifying the appropriate linear operator and translating the analytic inequalities into geometric ones.



1.
Introduction

This paper presents a unified approach to obtaining sharp higher order isoperimetric-type inequalities and their stability, which can be divided into two parts: discrete and smooth. The approach involves identifying the appropriate function space and family of linear operators, analyzing these operators using the corresponding Fourier analysis to derive sharp higher order Wirtinger type inequalities, and translating the analytic information into geometric ones. These results are novel, even in the smooth case. Notably, we are not aware of any prior work that establishes geometric inequalities involving the “derivatives” of the “curvature” of a polygon.

In the first part of this paper, we derive a series of sharp higher order discrete Wirtinger inequalities together with their stability results, and use them to obtain sharp bounds for the isoperimetric inequalities for polygons. There is already a large number of results on the higher order Wirtinger type inequalities (see for example
[
1
,
12
,
14
]
). However, up to now, the applications of the Wirtinger inequalities to isoperimetric type inequalities seem to be restricted only to the first order case, and these inequalities involve only the area, the length (or sum of the squares of the side lengths), and some special distances, but not the “curvature” or its higher “derivatives”, see for example
[
2
,
19
,
20
]
, or
[
15
,
16
,
21
]
for some analogous results in the smooth case. This is not surprising because for a polygon, the area and various lengths or distances involve only the zeroth and the first order differences. It is therefore desirable to see what kind of higher order discrete Wirtinger inequalities can be constructed to obtain information about the geometry of a polygon, especially those involving more delicate geometric quantities such as the curvature. To this end, we are going to define the curvature of a polygon to be the second order differences, and at the same time construct higher order Wirtinger inequalities which naturally contain the information about the curvature.

Indeed, we obtain a family of inequalities (
\displaystyle I_{m}
) indexed by
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
, which involves up to the
\displaystyle m
-th derivative.
The inequality

in a sense measures the regularity of the
\displaystyle k
-gon up to the
\displaystyle m
-th order.
There are two remarkable properties of these inequalities: 1) when
\displaystyle m
is odd, it provides a lower bound for the isoperimetric deficit, and when
\displaystyle m
is even, it provides an upper bound; 2) the inequality
\displaystyle I_{m+1}
measures the stability of
\displaystyle I_{m}
by giving an upper bound on its deficit.

Our approach also has the advantage that all the constants that appear are explicit and sharp. See e.g.

]

for another approach based on the spectral theory for circulant matrices, with a constant which is less explicit.

Finally, we will generalize the Chernoff area-width inequality. The classical Chernoff inequality states that for a closed convex curve
\displaystyle\gamma

F\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}w(\theta)w\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)d\theta.

2.
Basic idea to generate higher order inequalities

The idea to generate higher order Wirtinger or Poincare type inequalities is as follows. For the sake of discussion, we will discuss the case of a Riemannian manifold, although the case for a polygon is completely analogous, and let us ignore any convergence or regularity issue in this section, and more details will be given later. (Of course, in the discrete case, there is no such issue.)
Let
\displaystyle M
be a closed Riemannian manifold and
\displaystyle\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}:C^{\infty}(M)\to C^{\infty}(M)
be a family of self-adjoint differential operators which mutually commute with each other. Then there exists an orthonormal Schauder basis
\displaystyle\{e_{n}\}
which are simultaneous eigenfunctions of the
\displaystyle T_{j}
, with eigenvalues
\displaystyle\{\lambda_{n,j}\}
, i.e.
\displaystyle T_{j}[e_{n}]=\lambda_{n,j}e_{n}
.

Example 1
.

Let us give some examples of such operators. If
\displaystyle M=\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the standard unit circle, examples of such operators include differential operators of the form
\displaystyle\displaystyle T[h]=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}h^{(2j)}
. An important case is
\displaystyle T[h]=h+\ddot{h}
, which gives the reciprocal of the curvature of a convex curve
\displaystyle\gamma
if
\displaystyle h=h(\theta)
is the support function of a convex curve, parametrised by the normal angle
\displaystyle\theta
(
[
5
, p. 34]
).
Another class of such operators is the averaged translational operator such as

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{k}[h](\theta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}h\left(\theta+\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{k}\right)
for some
\displaystyle\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
. This operator is the generalization of the Chernoff operator
\displaystyle T_{2}
.
In 1969, Chernoff
[
4
]
obtained an area-width inequality for convex plane curves: Let
\displaystyle\gamma
be a closed convex curve in the plane
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
with area
\displaystyle F
and width function
\displaystyle w(\theta)
, then

F\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}w(\theta)w\left(\theta+\frac{1}{2}\pi\right)d\theta,

and the equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle\gamma
is a circle. Indeed, the Chernoff inequality can be regarded as a Wirtinger inequality for
\displaystyle T_{2}
. By obtaining a first order Wirtinger-type inequality for
\displaystyle T_{k}
, Ou and Pan
[
17
]
obtained a generalized version of the Chernoff inequality:

F\leq\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/k}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{1}{k}\pi\right)d\theta,

where
\displaystyle w_{k}(\theta)=h(\theta)+h\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)+\cdots+h\left(\theta+\left(\frac{2(k-1)\pi}{k}\right)\right)
and
\displaystyle h
is the support function. Note that Ou and Pan did not explicitly state the operator
\displaystyle T_{k}
in their paper, but their analysis essentially focused on the spectral properties of
\displaystyle T_{k}
.

If
\displaystyle M=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we can consider the family of operators
\displaystyle\{-\Delta-\lambda_{j}\mathrm{Id}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, where
\displaystyle\Delta
is the (negative definite) Laplacian and

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\rightarrow\infty
are the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle-\Delta
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. In
[
10
]
, the author considered this family of operators and obtained higher order Poincare inequalities on the sphere
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. As a result, new Minkowski-type inequalities involving higher order mean curvatures for convex hypersurfaces in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
were also obtained.

Let us go back to the recipe to obtain higher order Wirtinger type inequalities. Suppose we can construct a family of such
\displaystyle\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
such that
\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{n,j}\geq 0
for all
\displaystyle n
. Suppose
\displaystyle f\in C^{\infty}(M)
can be expressed in the orthonormal Schauder basis as
\displaystyle f=\sum_{n}a_{n}e_{n}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{M}f\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}T_{j}[f]=\sum_{n}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{n,j}\right)|a_{n}|^{2}\geq 0.

This is our higher order Wirtinger inequality. Although the idea seems simple, when we suitably choose
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle T_{j}
, and with some rearrangement, this gives us some geometric inequalities and stability results that are new.

3.
Background knowledge: discrete case

We will start from the discrete case first.
Let us first give the necessary definitions and notation. Let
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
, which we are going to fix.
A
\displaystyle k
-gon
\displaystyle P
in the Euclidean plane is an ordered
\displaystyle k
-tuple of points,
\displaystyle P=\left(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1}\right).
These points are called vertices, and the line segments joining
\displaystyle z_{0}
to
\displaystyle z_{1},z_{1}
to
\displaystyle z_{2},\ldots,z_{k-1}
to
\displaystyle z_{0}
are called sides.
We identify a point on the plane with a complex number. For convenience, we also identify
\displaystyle z_{k-l}
with
\displaystyle z_{-l}
(and later on
\displaystyle\zeta_{k-l}
with
\displaystyle\zeta_{-l}
), so for example
\displaystyle\{z_{\nu}:|\nu|\leq m\}=\{z_{\nu}:0\leq\nu\leq m\textrm{ or }k-m\leq\nu\leq k-1\}
.

We can then define scaling by complex numbers and linear combination of two polygons. So
\displaystyle\left(re^{i\theta}\right)P
is the image of
\displaystyle P
under a dilatation by the real factor
\displaystyle r
and a rotation through the angle
\displaystyle\theta
, both about the origin.
A linear combination of
\displaystyle k
-gons
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
is the
\displaystyle k
-gon identified with the linear combination of the corresponding vectors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

Let
\displaystyle\tau^{n}P
be the
\displaystyle k
-gon obtained from
\displaystyle P
by a cyclic shift of the entries of

\displaystyle\left(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{k-1}\right)n
places to the right. That is,

\begin{split}\tau P=\left(z_{k-1},z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k-2}\right),\;\tau^{2}P=\left(z_{k-2},z_{k-1},\ldots,z_{k-3}\right),\;\text{ etc. }\end{split}

We will denote by
\displaystyle R_{n}
the special
\displaystyle n
-regular
\displaystyle k
-gon centered at 0 with
\displaystyle z_{0}=1
, namely,

R_{n}=\left(1,\omega^{n},\omega^{2n},\ldots,\omega^{(k-1)n}\right)

where
\displaystyle\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{k}}
is a
\displaystyle k
-th root of unity.

Since our results will be proved using finite Fourier series, it is more convenient to work with various Hermitian forms on the vector space
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{k}
of all
\displaystyle k
-gons. In particular, we say a Hermitian form
\displaystyle I
which is invariant under
\displaystyle\tau
(i.e.
\displaystyle I(\tau P,\tau Q)=I(P,Q)
) a polygonal form (
[
6
, Section 3]
). For simplicity we denote a Hermitian form and the quadratic form associated to it by the same symbol.

The signed area of
\displaystyle P
is given by the polygonal form
[
6
, p. 30]

(3.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(P):=\frac{1}{4i}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(z_{j+1}\bar{z}_{j}-z_{j}\bar{z}_{j+1}\right).

Another polygonal form that we will consider here is
\displaystyle S=S(P)
, the sum of the squares of the side lengths of the polygon
\displaystyle P
.
Thus from the definition

(3.2)

S(P)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|z_{j+1}-z_{j}\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(z_{j+1}-z_{j}\right)\left(\bar{z}_{j+1}-\bar{z}_{j}\right).

It turns out that in the Fourier series approach,
\displaystyle S
is a better quantity to control than the length
\displaystyle L=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}|z_{\nu+1}-z_{\nu}|
of the polygon. This is because
\displaystyle S
is a polygonal form whereas the squared length
\displaystyle L^{2}
is not even a Hermitian form. This is analogous to the fact that Fourier series is harder to be used to control the
\displaystyle L^{p}
norm of a function or its derivatives for
\displaystyle p
other than
\displaystyle 2
. In many instances, the isoperimetric deficit
\displaystyle L^{2}-4\pi F
in the smooth case is replaced by the deficit
\displaystyle S-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F
in the discrete case, see for example Theorem
2
.

For the convenience of the reader, we now provide some basic knowledge on finite Fourier series here. For more details on finite Fourier series, please refer to
[
18
]
.

Let

\displaystyle z=\left(z_{0},\cdots z_{k-1}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
.
The finite Fourier series (FFS) of
\displaystyle z
is

z_{\nu}=\zeta_{0}+\zeta_{1}\omega_{\nu}+\zeta_{2}\omega_{\nu}^{2}+\cdots+\zeta_{k-1}\omega_{\nu}^{k-1}\quad(\nu=0,1,\cdots,k-1)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{\nu}=e^{\frac{2\pi i\nu}{k}}
. It is well-known that the Fourier coefficients
\displaystyle\zeta_{\nu}
are given by

\displaystyle\zeta_{\nu}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}z_{j}\bar{\omega}_{\nu}^{j}=\frac{1}{k}\langle z,R_{\nu}\rangle
,
so that
\displaystyle z=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}R_{\nu}
.

We define
\displaystyle\dot{z}
by

\dot{z}=\left(z_{1}-z_{0},z_{2}-z_{1},\cdots,z_{0}-z_{k-1}\right).

Up to a sign, this is called the first difference, nevertheless we prefer to call it the “first derivative” in order to compare with
[
11
]
.

Using this notation,
(
3.2
) and (
3.1
) become, respectively,

S(P)=\|\dot{P}\|^{2}=\langle\dot{P},\dot{P}\rangle\quad\textrm{and }\quad F(P)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\langle P,\dot{P}\rangle,

where the
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard Hermitian inner product given by
\displaystyle\langle z,w\rangle=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}\overline{w_{\nu}}
.
This is the discrete analogue of the area enclosed by a smooth curve
\displaystyle C
:

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Area}=\int_{C}xdy-ydx=-\int_{C}\mathrm{Im}(z\dot{\bar{z}})dt
.

In terms of FFS, we have

\displaystyle\dot{z}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}\dot{R}_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}(\omega_{\nu}-1)R_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}(\omega_{\nu}-1)R_{\nu}
.
So we have

\displaystyle\left\|\dot{z}\right\|^{2}=k\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}
.
Naturally we define the “higher derivatives”, by defining
\displaystyle\ddot{z}
to be the derivative of
\displaystyle\dot{z}
, etc. In the literature, these are called the differences of higher order. Denote the
\displaystyle j
-th derivative of
\displaystyle z
by either
\displaystyle z^{(j)}
or
\displaystyle D^{j}z
.
Inductively, we have the Parseval identity

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2j}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}4^{j}\sin^{2j}\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}.

We will also make frequent use of the fact that for a polygonal form
\displaystyle I
(
[
6
, Theorem 3.6]
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(z)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}|\zeta_{\nu}|^{2}I(R_{\nu}).

4.
Discrete Wirtinger inequalities

We now give a family of sharp discrete Wirtinger inequalities, which involves any arbitrary order of derivatives.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\displaystyle m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
be a natural number.
Define
\displaystyle m
-th degree polynomial
\displaystyle Q_{m}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(x-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{k}\right)\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{m,j}x^{j}
.
Suppose
\displaystyle z=(z_{0},\cdots,z_{k-1})
satisfies
\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}=0
, then

(4.1)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{m,j}\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\geq 0.\end{split}

The equality holds if and only if the Fourier coefficients satisfy
\displaystyle\zeta_{\nu}=0
for
\displaystyle m<\nu<k-m
.

Proof.

As
\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}=0
, we have
\displaystyle\zeta_{0}=0
, where
\displaystyle z=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}R_{\nu}
is its finite Fourier series.
By (
3.3
),

\begin{split}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{m,j}\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}&=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{m,j}\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2j}\right]\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}Q_{m}\left(\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}\right)\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{k}\right)\right)\right]\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}-\left|1-\omega_{j}\right|^{2}\right)\right]\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\nu=m+1}^{k-m-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}-\left|1-\omega_{j}\right|^{2}\right)\right]\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\\
&\geq 0.\end{split}

Here, we have used the fact that

\begin{split}\left|1-\omega_{\nu}\right|^{2}=\left(\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\nu}{k}\right)-1\right)^{2}+\sin^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi\nu}{k}\right)=2-2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\nu}{k}\right)=4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right).\end{split}

Hence for
\displaystyle m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
,

|1-\omega_{1}|^{2}=|1-\omega_{k-1}|^{2}<|1-\omega_{2}|^{2}=|1-\omega_{k-2}|^{2}<\cdots<|1-\omega_{m}|^{2}=|1-\omega_{k-m}|^{2}

and all
\displaystyle|1-\omega_{\nu}|^{2}>|1-\omega_{m}|^{2}
for
\displaystyle m<\nu<k-m
.
∎

To obtain geometric applications of the inequality (
4.1
), we rearrange it so that each term has its geometric meaning. We are going to do it in two steps. The form that we are looking for is the inequality (
4.2
) and we are going to give its geometric interpretation in Section
5
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\displaystyle m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
be a natural number.
Define
\displaystyle P_{1}=1
and the
\displaystyle(m-1)
-th degree polynomial
\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{m}(x):=\prod_{j=2}^{m}\left(x-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{k}\right)\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{m,k}x^{k}
for
\displaystyle m\geq 2
.
Suppose
\displaystyle\displaystyle z=\left(z_{0},\cdots,z_{k-1}\right)
satisfies
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}=0
, then

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{m,j}\left(\left\|z^{(j+1)}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\right)\geq 0.

Proof.

From the relation
\displaystyle Q_{m}(x)=\left(x-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)P_{m}(x)
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle c_{m,j}=\lambda_{m,j-1}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\lambda_{m,j}
, where we use the standard convention that
\displaystyle\lambda_{m,k}=0
for
\displaystyle k\notin\{0,\cdots,m-1\}
.
From this and Proposition
1
we have

\begin{split}0\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{m,j}\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}=&\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\lambda_{m,j-1}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\lambda_{m,j}\right)\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\\
=&\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{m,j}\left(\left\|z^{(j+1)}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\right).\end{split}

∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
\displaystyle m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
be a natural number.
Define the
\displaystyle(m-2)
-th degree polynomial
\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}(x)=\frac{P_{m}(x)-P_{m}\left(4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)}{x-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)}=\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}x^{l-1}
and
\displaystyle S_{m,0}=P_{m}\left(4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)
.
Suppose
\displaystyle z=\left(z_{0},\cdots,z_{k-1}\right)
satisfies
\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}=0
, then

(4.2)

\begin{split}0&\leq S_{m,0}\left(\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left\|\tau z^{(l+1)}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

The equality holds if and only if the Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle z
satisfy
\displaystyle\zeta_{\nu}=0
for
\displaystyle m<\nu<k-m
.

Proof.

By definition,

\begin{split}P_{m}(x)&=\left(x-4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\mathcal{S}_{m}(x)+P_{m}\left(4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\\
&=\left(x-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\mathcal{S}_{m}(x)+S_{m,0}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(S_{m,l}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)S_{m,l+1}\right)x^{l}.\end{split}

Therefore

(4.3)

\lambda_{m,l}=S_{m,l}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)S_{m,l+1}.

Define
\displaystyle I_{l}=\left\|\tau z^{(l+1)}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}
and
\displaystyle J_{l}=\left\|z^{(l+1)}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l)}\right\|^{2}
. We have

(4.4)

\begin{split}I_{l}&=\left\|\tau z^{(l+1)}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\left\|z^{(l+1)}\right\|^{2}+16\sin^{4}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}+8\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\langle\tau z^{(l+1)},z^{(l-1)}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\|z^{(l+1)}\right\|^{2}+16\sin^{4}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}-8\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l)}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\left(\left\|z^{(l+1)}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l)}\right\|^{2}\right)-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(\left\|z^{(l)}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}\right)\\
&=J_{l}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)J_{l-1}.\end{split}

Here we have used the summation by parts formula
\displaystyle\langle\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle=-\left\langle\ddot{w},\tau^{-1}w\right\rangle=-\langle\tau\ddot{w},w\rangle
.
So by Proposition
4.3
, (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we have

\begin{split}0\leq&\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{m,l}J_{l}=\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(S_{m,l}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)S_{m,l+1}\right)J_{l}=S_{m,0}J_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left(J_{l}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)J_{l-1}\right)\\
=&S_{m,0}\left(\left\|\dot{z}\right\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z\right\|^{2}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left\|\tau z^{(l+1)}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

∎

Using the recurrence relations satisfied by the polynomials
\displaystyle Q_{m}
and
\displaystyle S_{m}
, we can also derive stability results for the inequalities in Proposition

and Proposition

.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\displaystyle m\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor-1
be a natural number and

\displaystyle z=\left(z_{0},\cdots,z_{k-1}\right)
with
\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}z_{\nu}=0
.
With the notation of Proposition
1
and
3
, the following inequalities hold.

(1)

(4.5)

\sum_{l=0}^{m}C_{m,l}\left\|z^{(l)}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m+1)\pi}{k}\right)}\sum_{l=0}^{m}C_{m,l}\left\|z^{(l+1)}\right\|^{2}.

(2)

(4.6)

\begin{split}&S_{m,0}\left(\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left\|\tau z^{(l+1)}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z^{(l-1)}\right\|^{2}\\

.

By the identity

, we have the recurrence relation

Here, we use the standard convention that

for

. By replacing

with

5.
Geometric inequalities

Recall that
\displaystyle S=S(P)
and
\displaystyle F=F(P)
are the sum of squared sides and the signed area of the polygon
\displaystyle P
respectively.
If
\displaystyle P
is a
\displaystyle k
-gon represented by
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, then

F(P)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle z,\dot{z}\right\rangle\quad\textrm{and}\quad S(P)=\|\dot{z}\|^{2}.

For a polygon
\displaystyle z
, we define
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=\dot{z}
to be its set of tangent vectors (at the corresponding vertices
\displaystyle z_{\nu}
) and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\tau\ddot{z}
to be the discrete curvature vectors (again at the vertices
\displaystyle z_{\nu}
). Note that the translation
\displaystyle\tau
is necessary because

\displaystyle\tau\ddot{z}
at the
\displaystyle\nu
-th position corresponds to the change of the tangent vectors across the vertex
\displaystyle z_{\nu}
, i.e. it is the change from
\displaystyle\overrightarrow{z_{\nu-1}z_{\nu}}
to
\displaystyle\overrightarrow{z_{\nu}z_{\nu+1}}
. Also, it is not true that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is perpendicular to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}
in general, unlike the smooth case.

For a regular polygon with centroid
\displaystyle 0
which is positively oriented,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=\dot{z}=2ie^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z
. Therefore the quantity
\displaystyle\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}
measures how far a given polygon
\displaystyle P
deviates from a regular
\displaystyle k
-gon.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\displaystyle P
be a
\displaystyle k
-gon represented by
\displaystyle z
. Then

\displaystyle\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}=2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)-\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}.

Proof.

By writing
\displaystyle z=\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\zeta_{\nu}R_{\nu}
, we have

(5.1)

\begin{split}&\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}+\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left[-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z,\dot{z}\right\rangle\right]\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}R_{\nu},\dot{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle\right].\end{split}

By direct computation,
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}R_{\nu},\dot{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle=k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)
and
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle R_{\nu},\dot{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle F\left(R_{\nu}\right)=k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)
. Therefore

\begin{split}&\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}+\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\left(\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}F\left(R_{\nu}\right)-2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\nu=0}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}S\left(R_{\nu}\right)\\
=&2S(P)-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)-2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)S(P)\\
=&2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right),\end{split}

where we have used
\displaystyle S\left(R_{\nu}\right)=4k\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)
.
∎

In
[
3
]
, Chakerian proved the following sharpened isoperimetric inequality for a simple closed curve with centroid
\displaystyle 0
:

\frac{2\pi^{2}}{L}\int_{C}\left|z-\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}ds\leq L^{2}-4\pi F.

A discrete analogue of Chakerian’s result is given by the following result. Indeed, it is the
\displaystyle m=1
case of Theorem
4
. We single out this case here in order to compare with Theorem
3
.

Theorem 2

(Discrete Chakerian’s isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
\displaystyle k\geq 3
.
For any
\displaystyle k
-gon
\displaystyle P
with centroid
\displaystyle 0
,

S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\geq 2\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z+\frac{ie^{-i\frac{\pi}{k}}\boldsymbol{t}}{2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)}\right\|^{2}.

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle P
has the form
\displaystyle\zeta_{1}R_{1}+\zeta_{k-1}R_{k-1}
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
5
and the discrete Wirtinger inequality
\displaystyle\|\dot{z}\|^{2}\geq 4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}
given that
\displaystyle z
has centroid
\displaystyle 0
.

If the equality holds, by the equality case of Proposition
1
, we have
\displaystyle z=\zeta_{1}R_{1}+\zeta_{k-1}R_{k-1}
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

For an equilateral
\displaystyle k
-gon
\displaystyle P
with perimeter
\displaystyle L,L^{2}\geq 4k\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)|F(P)|
, and equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle P
is a regular
\displaystyle k
-gon.

If we are allowed to weaken the lower bound in Theorem
2
, we can obtain a similar result with a better rigidity case.

Theorem 3

(Discrete Chakerian’s isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
\displaystyle k\geq 3
. For any
\displaystyle k
-gon
\displaystyle P
with centroid 0,

S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\geq 2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left\|z+\frac{ie^{-i\frac{\pi}{k}}\boldsymbol{t}}{2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)}\right\|^{2}.

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle P
is a positively oriented regular
\displaystyle k
-gon.

Proof.

By (
5.1
) and the discrete Wirtinger inequality
\displaystyle\|\dot{z}\|^{2}\geq 4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}
,

\begin{split}\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{i}{k}}z\right\|^{2}\leq&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\mathrm{Im}\left\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}R_{\nu},\dot{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle\right]\\
=&2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left[\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}|\zeta_{\nu}|^{2}\cdot k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)\right].\end{split}

As
\displaystyle F(R_{\nu})=k\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)
, we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\left\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}R_{\nu},\dot{R_{\nu}}\right\rangle=\frac{\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)}{\cos\frac{\nu\pi}{k}}F\left(R_{\nu}\right)
unless
\displaystyle\cos\frac{\nu\pi}{k}=0
.
In the case where
\displaystyle\cos\frac{\nu\pi}{k}=0
,
\displaystyle k=2\nu
and so
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}\langle e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}R_{\nu},\dot{R_{\nu}}\rangle=k\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)>0
and
\displaystyle F(R_{\nu})=0
.
By combining with the
\displaystyle m=1
case of Proposition
1
, we arrive at

(5.2)

\begin{split}\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\frac{\pi}{k}e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}\leq 2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{0<\nu<k,\nu\neq\frac{k}{2}}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\frac{\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)}F\left(R_{\nu}\right).\end{split}

If
\displaystyle\nu\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
and
\displaystyle\nu\neq\frac{k}{2}
, then
\displaystyle F(R_{\nu})\geq 0
and

\frac{\cos\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}}{\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)+\tan\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\geq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)+\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)=\frac{1}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)}.

If
\displaystyle\nu>\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
, then
\displaystyle F(R_{\nu})<0
, and

(5.3)

\begin{split}-\frac{\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)}F\left(R_{\nu}\right)&=\frac{\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)}\left|F\left(R_{\nu}\right)\right|\\
&=\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)+\tan\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\left|F\left(R_{\nu}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)+\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\left|F\left(R_{\nu}\right)\right|\\
&=-\frac{1}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)}F\left(R_{\nu}\right).\end{split}

In view of (
5.2
), we have

\begin{split}\left\|\dot{z}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}&\leq 2\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\nu<k\\
\nu\neq\frac{k}{2}\end{subarray}}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}\frac{\cos\left(\frac{(\nu-1)\pi}{k}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{k}\right)}F\left(R_{\nu}\right)\\
&\leq 2\left\|\dot{z}\right\|^{2}-8\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\left|\zeta_{\nu}\right|^{2}F\left(R_{\nu}\right)\\
&=2\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right).\end{split}

If the equality holds, by the equality case of Proposition
1
, we have
\displaystyle z=\zeta_{1}R_{1}+\zeta_{k-1}R_{k-1}
.
When
\displaystyle\nu=k-1
, we have
\displaystyle\nu>\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
and the inequality in (
5.3
) is strict. Therefore (
5.2
) is strict unless
\displaystyle\zeta_{k-1}=0
.
Hence
\displaystyle z
represents a regular convex
\displaystyle k
-gon.
∎

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}-{\widetilde{S}_{m,0}}^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left\|D^{l-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z\right)\right\|^{2}.

Here
\displaystyle S_{m,l}
are defined in Proposition
3
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{m,0}=|S_{m,0}|=4^{m-1}\prod_{j=2}^{m}\left(\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{k}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\displaystyle m<\frac{k}{2}
be an even natural number. Suppose
\displaystyle P
is a
\displaystyle k
-gon represented by
\displaystyle z
whose centroid is
\displaystyle 0
. Then

\begin{split}&2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(L(P)^{2}-4k\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)\\
\leq&k\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}-k\widetilde{S}_{m,0}^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}S_{m,l}\left\|D^{l-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z\right)\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

Here
\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{m,0}=\left|S_{m,0}\right|=4^{m-1}\prod_{j=2}^{m}\left(\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{k}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)
.

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle P
is represented by
\displaystyle z=\zeta_{\nu}R_{\nu}
for some
\displaystyle 0<|\nu|\leq m
.

Proof.

The inequality follows from Theorem
4
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\displaystyle kS(P)\geq L^{2}(P)
. By the rigidity case of Theorem
4
, the Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle\dot{z}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k-1}\xi_{\nu}R_{\nu}
satisfy
\displaystyle\xi_{\nu}=0
for
\displaystyle m<\nu<k-m
.
By the equality case of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
\displaystyle\left|\dot{z}_{\nu}\right|^{2}
is a constant which is independent of
\displaystyle\nu
. Since

\displaystyle\left(R_{n}\right)_{\nu}=e^{\frac{2\pi in\nu}{k}}
, we have

\left|\dot{z}_{\nu}\right|^{2}=\sum_{n=-2m}^{2m}\left(\sum_{n_{1}-n_{2}=n}\xi_{n_{1}}\overline{\xi}_{n_{2}}\right)e^{\frac{2\pi in\nu}{k}}=\sum_{n=-2m}^{2m}c_{n}\omega_{\nu}^{n}=\text{ constant }

for all
\displaystyle\nu
, where we have identified
\displaystyle\xi_{k-l}
with
\displaystyle\xi_{-l}
. By the uniqueness of Fourier series, for
\displaystyle n\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle c_{n}=\sum_{n_{1}-n_{2}=n}\xi_{n_{1}}\bar{\xi}_{n_{2}}=0
. By Lemma
1
below, at most one of the coefficients, say
\displaystyle\xi_{\nu}
, can be non-zero. As
\displaystyle\dot{R}_{\nu}=(\omega_{\nu}-1)R_{\nu}
and
\displaystyle z
has centroid zero, we conclude that
\displaystyle z=\frac{\xi_{\nu}}{\omega_{\nu}-1}R_{\nu}
.
∎

Lemma 1
.

Let
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
\displaystyle a_{-m},a_{-m+1},\cdots,a_{-1},a_{1},\cdots,a_{m}
are complex numbers such that for all
\displaystyle n\neq 0
,

\displaystyle c_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p-q=n\\
0<|p|,|q|\leq m\end{subarray}}a_{p}\overline{a_{q}}=0
.
Then at most one of the
\displaystyle a_{p}
is non-zero.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
\displaystyle m
. The case where
\displaystyle m=1
is easy. Assuming the induction hypothesis, we now fix a natural number
\displaystyle m
.

As
\displaystyle c_{2m}=a_{m}\overline{a_{-m}}=0
, either
\displaystyle a_{m}=0
or
\displaystyle a_{-m}=0
. By interchanging
\displaystyle a_{l}
with
\displaystyle a_{-l}
if necessary, we may assume
\displaystyle a_{-m}=0
. We can assume
\displaystyle a_{m}\neq 0
, for otherwise it reduces to the
\displaystyle(m-1)
-case and we can apply the induction hypothesis, and then the results follows.

We claim that
\displaystyle a_{m}
is the only nonzero coefficient.

As
\displaystyle a_{-m}=0
, we have
\displaystyle c_{2m-1}=a_{m}\overline{a_{-m+1}}=0
. The assumption
\displaystyle a_{m}\neq 0
then implies
\displaystyle a_{-m+1}=0
. We can go on to consider
\displaystyle c_{2m-2},c_{2m-3},\cdots,c_{1}
one by one to deduce that the remaining coefficients
\displaystyle a_{-m+2}=a_{-m+3}=\cdots=a_{m-1}=0
. This proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2
.

Let us examine the inequalities in Theorem
4
for
\displaystyle m=1
and
\displaystyle 2
.

(1)

As explained before, when
\displaystyle m=1

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}.

(2)

When

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle 4\left(\sin^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z\right\|^{2}.

This is the discrete analogue of (
5.4
).
By Proposition

, it can also be rearranged so that it gives a measure of the deficit of the discrete Chakerian’s isoperimetric inequality:

\begin{split}&2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)-\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(\|\dot{z}\|^{2}-4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\|z\|^{2}\right)+\left\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z\right\|^{2}\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(2\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\left(S(P)-4\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)F(P)\right)-\left\|\boldsymbol{t}-2i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)e^{i\frac{\pi}{k}}z\right\|^{2}\right)\right.\\
&\left.+\left\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)z\right\|^{2}\right].\end{split}

(3)

Similar conclusion holds for
\displaystyle m=3
and beyond. Please refer to the arXiv version for details.

6.
The smooth case

We now give the result analogous to Theorem
4
for a smooth curve. In some sense, it is easier because the geometric quantities are more straightforward to compute and the higher order Wirtinger inequalities are somewhat simpler.

First we need the smooth version of Proposition
3
.

Proposition 6

(
[
11
]
Proposition 1)
.

Let
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle z:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a
\displaystyle 2\pi
-periodic function of class
\displaystyle C^{m}
with zero mean.Then

(6.1)

0\leq\prod_{j=2}^{m}\left(1-j^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(|\dot{z}|^{2}-|z|^{2}\right)dt+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}s_{m,l}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|z^{(l+1)}+z^{(l-1)}\right|^{2}dt.

Here, the constants
\displaystyle s_{m,1},\cdots,s_{m,m-1}
are the coefficients of the
\displaystyle(m-2)
-th degree polynomial
\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{m}(t):=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{m}(t)-\mathrm{P}_{m}(1)}{t-1}=\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}s_{m,l}t^{l-1}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{m}(t):=\prod_{j=2}^{m}\left(t-j^{2}\right)=\left(t-2^{2}\right)\cdots\left(t-m^{2}\right)
.

The equality holds if and only if the function
\displaystyle z
is of the form

z(t)=\sum_{1\leq|n|\leq m}a_{n}e^{int}

for some constants
\displaystyle a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\displaystyle C
be a simple closed
\displaystyle C^{m}
curve in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
with length
\displaystyle L
and a unit speed counter-clockwise parametrization
\displaystyle z(s)
with
\displaystyle s\in[0,L]
.
Assume that
\displaystyle C=\partial\Omega
is the boundary curve of a domain
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
with the area
\displaystyle F
. Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}
denotes the outward-pointing unit normal on
\displaystyle C
. Assume
\displaystyle C
has centroid
\displaystyle 0
, then

\begin{split}0\leq&\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}s_{m,l}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2l-3}\int_{C}\left|\left(\frac{d}{ds}\right)^{l-1}\left(z+\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right)\right|^{2}ds\\
&-\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2}(m-1)!(m+1)!\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\left(L^{2}-4\pi F\right)-\frac{2\pi}{L}\int_{C}\left|z-\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}ds\right).\end{split}

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle z(t)=be^{it}
for some
\displaystyle 0\neq b\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Using the change of variable
\displaystyle t=\frac{2\pi}{L}s
, we have

z(t)+\ddot{z}(t)=z+\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}.

Therefore

(6.2)

z^{(l+1)}(t)+z^{(l-1)}(t)=\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{l-1}\left(\frac{d}{ds}\right)^{l-1}\left(z+\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right).

It is easy to see that

\frac{L^{2}}{2\pi}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\dot{z}|^{2}dt

and

2F=\iint_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\left(z\right)\,dxdy=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{Im}(z\dot{\bar{z}})dt.

So we can write

\frac{1}{\pi}\left(L^{2}-4\pi F\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}2\left(|\dot{z}|^{2}+\mathrm{Im}(z\dot{\bar{z}})\right)dt.

We rewrite this integrand as

2\left(|\dot{z}|^{2}+\mathrm{Im}(z\dot{\bar{z}})\right)=|z+i\dot{z}|^{2}+|\dot{z}|^{2}-|z|^{2}.

The first term on the right has integral

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|z+i\dot{z}|^{2}dt=\frac{2\pi}{L}\int_{C}\left|z-\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}ds.

Therefore

(6.3)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(|\dot{z}|^{2}-|z|^{2}\right)dt=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(L^{2}-4\pi F\right)-\frac{2\pi}{L}\int_{C}\left|z-\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}ds.

Putting (
6.2
), (
6.3
) into (
6.1
), we have

\begin{split}0\leq&\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}s_{m,l}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|z^{(l+1)}+z^{(l-1)}\right|^{2}dt-\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2}(m-1)!(m+1)!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(|\dot{z}|^{2}-|z|^{2}\right)dt\\
=&\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}s_{m,l}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2l-3}\int_{C}\left|\left(\frac{d}{ds}\right)^{l-1}\left(z+\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right)\right|^{2}ds\\
&-\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2}(m-1)!(m+1)!\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\left(L^{2}-4\pi F\right)-\frac{2\pi}{L}\int_{C}\left|z-\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}ds\right).\end{split}

Suppose that the equality holds. By Proposition
6
, we have

\dot{z}(t)=\sum_{0<|n|\leq m}a_{n}e^{int}

for some constants
\displaystyle a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Also, ,
\displaystyle|\dot{z}(t)|^{2}=\mu
is a constant. Then

\mu=\dot{z}(t)\overline{\dot{z}(t)}=\sum_{n=-2m}^{2m}\left(\sum_{p+q=n}a_{p}\overline{a_{-q}}\right)e^{int}

The uniqueness of Fourier series shows that, for all
\displaystyle 0<|n|\leq 2m
, the coefficients vanish:

\sum_{p-q=n,1\leq|p|,|q|\leq 2}a_{p}\overline{a_{q}}=0

By Lemma
1
, at most one of the coefficients
\displaystyle a_{n}
is nonzero, i.e.
\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)=a_{n}e^{int}
for some
\displaystyle 0<|n|\leq m
.
As
\displaystyle z(t)
has centroid
\displaystyle 0
, we have
\displaystyle z(t)=\frac{a_{n}}{in}e^{int}
. Since
\displaystyle z(t)
is assumed to be simple and is positively oriented, we deduce that
\displaystyle z(t)=be^{it}
for some
\displaystyle 0\neq b\in\mathbb{C}
.
∎

7.
Higher order Chernoff inequality

We now prove a generalization of the Chernoff inequality
[
4
]
, which states that
for a closed convex curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
with area
\displaystyle F
and width function
\displaystyle w(\theta)
, we have

F\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}w(\theta)w\left(\theta+\frac{1}{2}\pi\right)d\theta.

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle\gamma
is a circle.
Ou and Pan
[
17
]
proved the following generalized version of the Chernoff inequality:

F\leq\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/k}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{1}{k}\pi\right)d\theta

where
\displaystyle w_{k}(\theta)=h(\theta)+h\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)+\cdots+h\left(\theta+\left(\frac{2(k-1)\pi}{k}\right)\right)
and
\displaystyle h
is the support function.

The definition of
\displaystyle w_{k}
suggests we consider the following operator: let
\displaystyle T_{k}
be the transform defined on the space of real-valued functions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
, given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{k}[h](\theta):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}h\left(\theta+\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{k}\right).

If
\displaystyle h
is the support function of a convex curve, then the convex curve with support
\displaystyle T_{k}[h]
is one whose support is the average of
\displaystyle h
in
\displaystyle k
directions.
Now, let
\displaystyle\gamma
be a simple closed convex curve parametrised by the normal angle
\displaystyle\theta
, and
\displaystyle h=h(\theta)
be the support function of
\displaystyle\gamma
.
Let
\displaystyle\displaystyle h=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{in\theta}
be the Fourier series of
\displaystyle\displaystyle h
. Then the Fourier series of
\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{k}[h]
becomes

\begin{split}T_{k}[h](\theta)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}e^{in\left(\theta+\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{k}\right)}=&\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}e^{\frac{in(2m-1)\pi}{k}}e^{in\theta}\\
=&\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\cos\frac{n(2m-1)\pi}{k}e^{in\theta}\\
=&\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\beta_{n}e^{in\theta},\end{split}

where
\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\cos\frac{n(2m-1)\pi}{k}
. It is easy to see that
\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{n}\in\{-1,0,1\}
,
\displaystyle\beta_{0}=1
and
\displaystyle\beta_{1}=0
if
\displaystyle k\geq 2
. Indeed,
\displaystyle\beta_{n}\neq 0
if and only if
\displaystyle n
is a multiple of
\displaystyle k
.

On the other hand, consider the operator

\displaystyle A[h]=h+\ddot{h}
.
The Fourier series of
\displaystyle A[h]
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle A[h](\theta)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}(1-n^{2})e^{in\theta}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\delta_{n}e^{in\theta}.

Obviously,
\displaystyle\beta_{n}\geq\delta_{n}
for all
\displaystyle n
.
As shown in Section
2
, this shows that for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
and
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)\cdot(T_{k}-A)^{m}[h](\theta)d\theta\geq 0.

Since
\displaystyle\beta_{n}>\delta_{n}
for
\displaystyle|n|\geq 2
, it follows that the equality case holds if and only if
\displaystyle h=\sum_{n=-1}^{1}a_{n}e^{in\theta}
, with
\displaystyle a_{-n}=\overline{a_{n}}
. This together with the formula

\displaystyle\gamma(\theta)=h(\theta)\boldsymbol{n}+\dot{h}(\theta)\boldsymbol{t}=h(\theta)e^{i\theta}+\dot{h}(\theta)ie^{i\theta}=2\overline{a}_{1}+a_{0}e^{i\theta}
, shows that
\displaystyle\gamma
is a circle.

It remains to give a geometric interpretation to the inequality (
7.1
).
Recall the definition
\displaystyle w_{k}(\theta)=h(\theta)+h\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)+\cdots+h\left(\theta+\left(\frac{2(k-1)\pi}{k}\right)\right)
.
When
\displaystyle m=1
, we have (cf.
[
17
, Eqn. 3-2]
)

(7.2)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{k}\right)d\theta=&\frac{1}{2k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)h\left(\theta+\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{k}\right)d\theta\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)T_{k}[h](\theta)d\theta.\end{split}

On the other hand, the area
\displaystyle F
enclosed by
\displaystyle\gamma
is given by (
[
7
, Eqn 2.4.27]
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(h(\theta)^{2}-\dot{h}(\theta)^{2}\right)d\theta=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)\cdot A[h](\theta)d\theta.

Therefore when
\displaystyle m=1
, we recover the Ou-Pan generalized Chernoff inequality

F\leq\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{k}\right)d\theta.

Now we assume
\displaystyle m\geq 2
.
We can parameterise
\displaystyle\gamma
by the normal angle
\displaystyle\theta
. In fact, it can be explicitly parametrised by
[
5
, p. 34]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma(\theta)=h(\theta)\boldsymbol{n}+\dot{h}(\theta)\boldsymbol{t},

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)
.
The locus of curvature centers
\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}
of
\displaystyle\gamma
is then defined to be
\displaystyle\gamma(\theta)-\rho(\theta)\boldsymbol{n}
, where
\displaystyle\rho
is the radius of curvature. Since
\displaystyle\rho=h+\ddot{h}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}(\theta)=\dot{h}\boldsymbol{t}-\ddot{h}\boldsymbol{n}.

Of course, this curve can fail to be simple and convex, but we can still regard it as a smooth parametrised curve and compute its algebraic area using the formula
\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}=\frac{1}{2}\int xdy-ydx
.
Differentiating
\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}
gives
\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{(1)}=-(\dot{h}+\dddot{h})\boldsymbol{n}
, and hence we can fix
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}
as a unit normal field of
\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}
, and w.r.t. this choice of normal, the support function of
\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}
is then
\displaystyle\dot{h}
.

Inductively, we can define the locus of curvature centers
\displaystyle\gamma_{(2)}
of
\displaystyle\gamma_{(1)}
, and so on, and deduce that
\displaystyle\gamma_{(j)}
has support function
\displaystyle h^{(j)}(\theta)
w.r.t. the normal
\displaystyle J^{j}\boldsymbol{n}
, where
\displaystyle J
is the anti-clockwise rotation by
\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}
. Therefore the algebraic area
\displaystyle F
of
\displaystyle\gamma_{(j)}
is given by

(7.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F[\gamma_{(j)}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}({h^{(j)}(\theta)}^{2}-{h^{(j+1)}(\theta)}^{2})d\theta.

The R.H.S. of (
7.1
) can be written as

(7.4)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}\left(T_{k}\right)^{j}[h]d\theta\\
=&(-1)^{m}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m}[h]d\theta+\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot\left(T_{k}\right)^{m}[h]d\theta+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}\left(T_{k}\right)^{j}[h]d\theta.\end{split}

Using (
7.3
), it is not hard to show by induction that

(7.5)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}hA^{m}[h]=&\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1}{r}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(h^{(r)}(\theta)^{2}-h^{(r+1)}(\theta)^{2}\right)d\theta\\
=&2\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)}\right].\end{split}

It is also easy to see that
\displaystyle\left(T_{k}\right)^{l+2}=\left(T_{k}\right)^{l}
if
\displaystyle l\geq 1
. So for
\displaystyle j\geq 1
, the term
\displaystyle\left(T_{k}\right)^{j}[h]
only depends on
\displaystyle j\pmod{2}
. By abuse of notation, we denote by
\displaystyle T_{k}\gamma
the convex curve whose support function is
\displaystyle T_{k}[h]
and by
\displaystyle T_{k}^{2}\gamma
the convex curve whose support function is
\displaystyle(T_{k})^{2}[h]
.

We now consider the term
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot\left(T_{k}\right)^{m}[h]d\theta
in (
7.4
). If
\displaystyle m
is odd, then (
7.2
) gives

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)(T_{k})^{m}[h](\theta)d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)T_{k}[h](\theta)d\theta=\frac{2}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{k}\right)d\theta.

When
\displaystyle m
is even,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)(T_{k})^{m}[h](\theta)d\theta=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)(T_{k})^{2}[h](\theta)d\theta.

Let us consider the Hermitian form defined on
\displaystyle C(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}(\phi,\psi)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\overline{\psi}\cdot(T_{k})^{2}[\psi]d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\overline{\psi}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\psi\left(\theta+\frac{2j\pi}{k}\right)d\theta.

We claim that
\displaystyle\{e^{in\theta}\}
forms an orthogonal (but not necessarily orthonormal) Schauder basis for
\displaystyle I_{1}
. To see this, note that
\displaystyle I_{1}(e^{in\theta},e^{im\theta})
is the integral of

\displaystyle\displaystyle e^{-im\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{in\left(\theta+\frac{2j\pi}{k}\right)}=e^{i(n-m)\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{\frac{2inj\pi}{k}}.

If
\displaystyle\displaystyle k\nmid n
, then
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{\frac{2inj\pi}{k}}=0
. Otherwise,
\displaystyle\displaystyle k\mid n
and the integrand is just
\displaystyle\displaystyle e^{i(n-m)\theta}
, which has integral
\displaystyle 0
over
\displaystyle[0,2\pi]
when
\displaystyle m\neq n
. So for
\displaystyle\displaystyle\phi=\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{in\theta}
,
\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}(\phi,\phi)=2\pi\sum_{n:k|n}|a_{n}|^{2}
.

Similar calculation shows that the Hermitian form
\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}(\phi,\psi)=\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{k}}\overline{\psi}\cdot(T_{k})^{2}[\phi]d\theta
satisfies the property that for
\displaystyle\displaystyle\phi=\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{in\theta}
,
\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}(\phi,\phi)=\frac{2\pi}{k}\sum_{n:k\mid n}|a_{n}|^{2}=\frac{1}{k}I_{1}(\phi,\phi)
.

Therefore, when
\displaystyle m
is even,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)\left(T_{k}\right)^{m}[h](\theta)d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)\left(T_{k}\right)^{2}[h](\theta)d\theta=k\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{k}}\left(T_{k}[h]\right)^{2}d\theta=\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)^{2}d\theta.

Now, consider the last term in (
7.4
)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}\left(T_{k}\right)^{j}[h]\\
=&(-1)^{m}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\mid j}\binom{m}{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}T_{k}^{2}[h]-(-1)^{m}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\nmid j}\binom{m}{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}T_{k}[h]\\
\end{split}

The term
\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}T_{k}[h]d\theta
has a similar geometric meaning as (
7.5
).
In fact, same computation gives

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h\cdot A^{m-j}T_{k}[h]d\theta=&\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}\int_{0}^{2n}\left(h^{(r)}\left(T_{k}[h]\right)^{(r)}-h^{(r+1)}\left(T_{k}[h]\right)^{(r+1)}\right)d\theta\\
=&2\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F[\gamma_{(r)},(T_{k}\gamma)_{(r)}].\end{split}

Here
\displaystyle F[\cdot,\cdot]
is the algebraic mixed area (cf.
[
7
, Eqn 2.4.28]
).

Combining the above, we have the following conclusion. When
\displaystyle m
is odd,

\begin{split}0\leq&-\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1}{r}F[\gamma_{(r)}]+\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{k}\right)d\theta\\
&-\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\mid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}^{2}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right]\\
&+\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\nmid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right].\end{split}

When
\displaystyle m
is even,

\begin{split}0\leq&\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{r}(-1)^{r}F[\gamma_{(r)}]+\frac{1}{2k}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)^{2}d\theta\\
&+\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\mid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}^{2}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right]\\
&-\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\nmid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right].\end{split}

So we have proved the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\displaystyle 2\leq k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\gamma
be a smooth closed convex curve on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
When
\displaystyle m
is odd,

\begin{split}0\leq&-\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1}{r}F[\gamma_{(r)}]+\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)w_{k}\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{k}\right)d\theta\\
&-\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\mid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}^{2}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right]\\
&+\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\nmid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right].\end{split}

When
\displaystyle m
is even,

\begin{split}0\leq&\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{r}(-1)^{r}F[\gamma_{(r)}]+\frac{1}{2k}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{k}}w_{k}(\theta)^{2}d\theta\\
&+\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\mid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}^{2}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right]\\
&-\sum_{1\leq j\leq m-1\atop 2\nmid j}\binom{m}{j}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{r}\binom{m-1-j}{r}F\left[\gamma_{(r)},\left(T_{k}\gamma\right)_{(r)}\right].\end{split}

Here
\displaystyle w_{k}(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}h\left(\theta+\frac{2j\pi}{k}\right)
is the generalized width,
\displaystyle h
is the support function of
\displaystyle\gamma
,
\displaystyle F
is the algebraic area or the algebraic mixed area,
\displaystyle(T_{k})^{j}\gamma
is the curve whose support function is
\displaystyle(T_{k})^{j}[h]
, and
\displaystyle\beta_{(j)}
is the
\displaystyle j
-th order locus of curvature centers of a curve
\displaystyle\beta
as defined above.

The equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle\gamma
is a circle.
