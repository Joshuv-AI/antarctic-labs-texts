Evolution of proto-neutron stars to pulsars, magnetars and central compact objects
İrem Bakır, Kazım Yavuz Ekşi

Abstract
Some of the young neutron stars, the magnetars, have ultra-strong magnetic fields. The models invoked for the origin of magnetar fields require very specific conditions with extreme parameters, which are incompatible with the inference that the birth rate of magnetars is comparable to the rate of core-collapse supernovae. We propose that these seemingly contradictory trends can be reconciled if both pulsars and magnetars pass through a dynamo process at the proto-neutron star (PNS) stage. We invoke a shear-driven dynamo model for the generation of PNS magnetic fields. We argue that the toroidal fields of central compact objects are shaped by the Ω \Omega -effect, even though they do not pass through a genuine dynamo process.



1
Introduction

Discussions about the origin of the strong magnetic fields of neutron stars have a long history
(
Spruit 2009
)
, rekindled by the identification of magnetars, i.e., neutron stars with super-strong magnetic fields
(
Kaspi &
Beloborodov 2017
)
. According to the “fossil field” hypothesis
(
Woltjer 1964
;
Ginzburg 1964
;
Ruderman 1972
)
, the conservation of magnetic flux during core-collapse would lead to the amplification of magnetic fields. This is commonly assumed to be the origin of the magnetic fields of conventional neutron stars. One line of argument also invokes the “fossil field” hypothesis for the origin of magnetic fields of magnetars
(
Ferrario &
Wickramasinghe 2006
;
Ferrario &
Wickramasinghe 2008
)
, suggesting that they descend from the strong-field tail of the progenitor distribution.
Makarenko et al. 2021
tested this hypothesis using population synthesis methods and found that the model can not explain the distribution of the inferred magnetic fields of the neutron star population because the number of high-field progenitors is not sufficiently large.

Another line of argument for the origin of neutron star magnetic fields invokes the dynamo action
(
Ruderman &
Sutherland 1973
)
, and this is widely accepted as the origin of magnetic fields in magnetars
(
Duncan &
Thompson 1992
;
Thompson &
Duncan 1993
)
. Numerical simulations
(
Bonanno
et al. 2003
;
Bonanno
et al. 2005
;
Bonanno
et al. 2006
;
Naso et al. 2008
;
Rheinhardt &
Geppert 2005
;
Geppert &
Rheinhardt 2006
;
Raynaud et al. 2020
;
Lander et al. 2021
)
suggest that the dynamo action could indeed produce the magnetar fields if the neutron star begins its life with a period of a few milliseconds
(see
Bonanno &
Urpin 2008
, for a review)
.
The dynamo process is active at the proto-neutron star
(
Prakash et al. 1997
;
Pons et al. 1999
;
Camelio et al. 2016
, PNS;)
stage with initial radius
R_{0}\sim 40\,{\rm km}
lasting about
t\sim 30-40\,{\rm s}

(
Raynaud et al. 2020
)
.

The rather extreme source parameters invoked by the magnetar formation scenarios (very massive and magnetized progenitors in the case of the fossil fields and very rapidly rotating neutron stars in the case of the dynamo models) seem to be at odds with the relatively high birth rates of magnetars. The core-collapse rate in the Galaxy is
1.9\pm 1.1\,{\rm century^{-1}}
, as implied by the high-spectral-resolution measurements of
26
Al emission at
1808.65\,{\rm keV}

(
Diehl
et al. 2006
)
. A more recent estimate is
1.63\pm 0.46\,{\rm century^{-1}}

(
Rozwadowska et al. 2021
)
. The birth rate of magnetars is estimated to be
0.3\,{\rm century^{-1}}

(
Keane &
Kramer 2008
)
and
0.23-2\,{\rm century^{-1}}

(
Beniamini et al. 2019
)
. This is only an order of magnitude smaller or comparable to the core collapse rate, suggesting that magnetar formation is not as rare as the formation scenarios suggest.

Further evidence for the ordinary magnetar formation parameters comes from X-ray observations of supernova remnants, which provide no evidence that the formation of magnetars involved a greater energy input than the formation of ordinary neutron stars
(
Vink &
Kuiper 2006
)
. Moreover, magnetars appear to have typical space velocities
(
Deller et al. 2012
;
Tendulkar et al. 2013
)
rather than being on the high end tail of the space velocity distribution, as some magnetar models claim
(
Duncan &
Thompson 1992
;
Thompson &
Duncan 1993
)
. Any model for magnetar formation should reconcile the extreme parameter requirements with the high formation rates, an issue highlighted by
Mereghetti et al. 2015
.

A characteristic feature of magnetars is their strong toroidal magnetic field component. The typical pulsars have
B_{\phi}\sim 10^{14}\,{\rm G}

(
Gourgouliatos et al. 2013
;
Gourgouliatos & Hollerbach 2018
)
, while the magnetars likely have order of magnitude stronger toroidal fields (
B_{\phi}\gtrsim 10^{15}\,{\rm G}
). This difference is generally attributed to the dynamo process the magnetars pass through. The stronger toroidal fields of magnetars are suggested by theoretical models for their persistent emission
(
Ferrario &
Wickramasinghe 2008
;
Perna et al. 2013
;
Gourgouliatos & Hollerbach 2018
;
Igoshev et al. 2021a
)
and the giant flares that could result from its reconfigurations
(
Thompson &
Duncan 2001
)
. The post-glitch relaxation time-scale of magnetars can be explained by the coupling of vortices to strong toroidal fields
(
Gügercinoğlu 2017
)
. Toroidal fields stronger than dipole fields are implied by the identification of low-magnetic field “magnetars”
(
Rea et al. 2010
)
. The dipole (poloidal) fields of the objects inferred from their spin-down are not extreme, yet they exhibit magnetar bursts which suggests the presence of supercritical fields. Their quiescent emission cannot be simply explained by rotational energy loss or thermal energy and is addressed by the presence of decaying toroidal fields. The surface fields inferred from phase-dependent absorption features
(
Güver et al. 2011
;
Tiengo
et al. 2013
)
imply the presence of strong toroidal fields and/or higher multipoles
(
Ertan &
Alpar 2003
;
Ekşi &
Alpar 2003
;
Alpar et al. 2011
)
.
Other observations implying the presence of strong toroidal fields in magnetars arise from their free precession, which suggests that they have prolate shapes, as would be caused by strong toroidal fields
(
Makishima
et al. 2014
)
. Three-dimensional magneto-thermal simulations of magnetars also favour the presence of strong toroidal fields for addressing the high pulsed fraction of these objects
(
Igoshev et al. 2021a
)
.

Neutron stars descend from O- and B-type stars which are massive (
M_{*}\sim 10M_{☉}
)
and large
R_{*}\sim 10^{11}~{\rm cm}
. Such stars have strong magnetic fields with maximum values of
B_{\max}\sim 10^{4}\,{\rm G}

(
Donati &
Landstreet 2009
)
.
Textbook arguments employ magnetic flux conservation as if the whole star collapses to
the radius of the neutron star,
B_{\rm NS}=B_{*}(R_{*}/R_{\rm NS})^{2}
, corresponding to a factor
10^{10}
amplification of the field which seems to provide even the magnetar-like
fields
B_{\rm NS}\sim 10^{14}\,{\rm G}
. The iron core, the collapsing component of the star immediately before the collapse, has a radius about
R_{\rm core}\simeq 3\,000\,{\rm km}

(
Sukhbold et al. 2016
)
. The magnetic field inferred from the seismic observations suggests that the field near the core is about
B_{\rm core}=5\times 10^{5}\,{\rm G}

(
Lecoanet et al. 2022
)
. Thus, as recently argued by
Lander et al. 2021
, this would result in
B_{\rm NS}=B_{\rm core}(R_{\rm core}/R_{\rm NS})^{2}
, which corresponds to a factor of
10^{5}
amplification, leading to
B_{\rm NS}\sim{\rm a~few}\times 10^{10}\,{\rm G}
. Obviously, this field is
1-2
orders of magnitude lower than typical pulsar fields (
B_{\rm p}\sim 10^{12}~{\rm G}
), implying a necessity for dynamo action for typical pulsars, though not as vigorous as that proceeding in magnetars. Interestingly, such fields of
B_{\rm NS}\sim 10^{10}\,{\rm G}
are of the order of the magnetic fields of central compact objects
(
Gotthelf &
Halpern 2007
;
Halpern et al. 2007
;
Gotthelf &
Halpern 2009
;
De Luca 2017
, CCOs;)
. These arguments suggest that the fossil field arguments can only address the dipole magnetic fields of CCOs. Note also that these objects could also have stronger small-scale magnetic fields
(
Gourgouliatos et al. 2020
;
Igoshev et al. 2021b
)
.

Since the core does not directly collapse into a neutron star but passes through the stage of a PNS with an initial radius of
R_{0}\sim 40\,{\rm km}
, the flux conservation implies that the PNS inherits a magnetic field about

B_{\rm PNS}=B_{\rm core}\left(\frac{R_{\rm core}}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}\simeq 3\times 10^{9}\,{\rm G}

(1)

which then will be enhanced, upon collapse to
R_{\infty}=12~{\rm km}
, by
R_{0}/R_{\infty}=(40~{\rm km}/12~{\rm km})^{2}\simeq 11
times reaching
B_{\rm p}\sim{\rm a~few}\times 10^{10}~{\rm G}
.

The results of
Raynaud et al. 2020
suggest that the dynamo-generated magnetic fields depend on the initial periods, slower initial periods resulting in less vigorous dynamo action and weaker magnetic fields. By analyzing the results of three-dimensional core-collapse simulations,
White et al. 2022
argued that the pulsar/magnetar dichotomy arises from the different rotation rates of the cores of their progenitors.
Barrère et al. 2022
brought into attention the role of fallback matter in spinning up the PNS to induce vigorous dynamo action to lead to magnetar field strengths.

In this work, we assume that both ordinary neutron stars and magnetars have dynamo-generated fields and study the parameter space to find what leads to the differences in the poloidal and toroidal magnetic fields. We use and suitably extend the one-dimensional dynamo model
(
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
)
originally proposed for magnetic field generation in white dwarf mergers. We seek what initial parameters will lead to the different toroidal field strengths at the end of the dynamo stage to determine whether the PNS becomes a magnetar (
B_{\phi}\gtrsim 10^{15}\,{\rm G}
) or a typical neutron star (
B_{\phi}\sim 10^{14}\,{\rm G}
).

The organization of the paper is as follows. In §
2
, we introduce the shear-driven dynamo model of
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
, adopt it to the PNS parameters, and add the effects of changing radius and gravitational mass. In §
3
, we present the implications of the model for PNSs, and finally in §
4
we discuss the implications of our results for young neutron star populations.

2
Model equations

There are now very sophisticated dynamo models that solve the PNS dynamo from the first principles i.e., the magnetohydrodynamic equations
(
Raynaud et al. 2020
;
Masada
et al. 2022
, see e.g., )
. These 2- and 3-dimensional simulations cannot be operated at realistic parameters (namely magnetic Reynolds numbers and magnetic Prandtl numbers)
(
Lander 2021
)
since this would require enormous computational power beyond the capability of the present resources
(
Guilet et al. 2022
, but see)
. Thus, there is some room for reduced dynamo models, as we consider here.

As a simple description of the
\alpha-\Omega
dynamo in PNS
(
Duncan &
Thompson 1992
;
Thompson &
Duncan 1993
)
, we adopt the model proposed by
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
for the generation of magnetic fields in white dwarf mergers. This is a shear-driven dynamo model that effectively describes the turbulent dynamo process driven by differential rotation and convection, both of which undoubtedly operate in PNS. The new ingredients we add to the model are the required modifications owing to the shrinking radius of the PNS and the changing gravitational mass, and inclusion of viscous processes.

2.1
The mean field dynamo

In magnetohydrodynamics, the evolution of magnetic fields is governed by the magnetic induction equation

\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}=\nabla\times(\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{B})+\lambda\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}

(2)

where
\mathbf{U}
is the velocity of the fluid and
\lambda
is the magnetic diffusivity due to the electrical conductivity of the fluid. This equation has been extensively employed for analyzing dynamos
(
Brandenburg
& Subramanian 2005
;
Rincon 2019
;
Moffatt 1978
)
.

In the mean-field dynamo model
(
Krause &
Raedler 1980
)
, large-scale
magnetic fields are generated by small-scale turbulent flow. The application of the mean-field method to the induction equation leads to

\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}_{0}}{\partial t}=\mathbf{\nabla}\times\left(\mathbf{U}_{0}\times\mathbf{B}_{0}+\alpha\mathbf{B}_{0}-\beta\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}_{0}\right)+\lambda\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}_{0}

(3)

where subscript
0
indicates the mean part of the fields and we will drop it in the following equations. Since the magnetic field can be written as the sum of the toroidal fields,
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{\rm p}+\mathbf{B}_{\phi}
, the equation governing each component can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{\phi}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=r\left(\mathbf{B}_{\rm p}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}\Omega\right)+\lambda_{\rm e}\nabla^{2}B_{\phi}~,

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{\rm p}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\rm t}\frac{\partial B_{\phi}}{\partial r}+\lambda_{\rm e}\nabla^{2}B_{\rm p}~,

(5)

where we ignored several terms on the basis of being small. Here,
\alpha_{\rm t}\sim 10^{5}~{\rm cm~s^{-1}}

(
Reboul-Salze et al. 2022
, e.g.)
is the turbulent transport coefficient. Its physical effect can be described as the twisting of the mean magnetic field lines by turbulence, resulting in the creation of magnetic field loops. These loops facilitate the conversion between the poloidal and toroidal magnetic fields. In the above equations,

\lambda_{\rm e}
denotes the effective magnetic diffusivity enhanced by turbulence. The value of
\lambda_{\rm e}
extends from
10^{10}~{\rm cm^{2}~s^{-1}}
to
10^{13}~{\rm cm^{2}~s^{-1}}
for the solar convection zone
(
Hotta
et al. 2012
)
. For the convectively unstable regions of the PNSs, the inferred effective magnetic diffusivity can well exceed this value.
Thompson &
Duncan 1993
approximates
\lambda_{\rm e}\sim h_{\rm p}v_{\rm c}
where
h_{\rm p}
is the pressure scale-height,
v_{\rm c}
is the convective velocity. Using
\Delta R=\alpha_{\rm MLT}h_{\rm p}
where
\Delta R
is the size of the convective zone and
\alpha_{\rm MLT}\approx 1

(
Kippenhahn
et al. 2012
)
. In the simulations of
Raynaud et al. 2020
the convective region extends from 12.5 km to 25 km for a
R=40~{\rm km}
PNS. Since the radius of the PNS changes in our setting, we keep the relative size (
\Delta R/R=0.3
) constant, simultaneously the effective diffusivity becomes
\lambda_{\rm e}=\Delta Rv_{\rm c}\simeq 10^{14}~{\rm cm^{2}~s^{-1}}
. Thus,
\lambda_{\rm e}\sim 10^{14}~{\rm cm^{2}~s^{-1}}
for
R_{0}=40~{\rm km}
PNSs.

These equations motivate the reduced dynamo equations that we discuss below.

2.2
Reduced dynamical equations for the fields

According to
Equation 4
, the toroidal field,
B_{\phi}
, is generated from the coupling of the poloidal field with the differential rotation within the star (the first term on the right), known as the
\Omega
-effect, and is also reduced by diffusion due to turbulent magnetic diffusivity.
At a representative radius where the dynamo is the most effective, the evolution of the field can thus be written as

\frac{dB_{\phi}}{dt}=q\Omega B_{\rm p}-\frac{B_{\phi}}{\tau_{\phi}}

(6)

where
q\equiv d\ln\Omega/d\ln r
is the differential rotation
(
Barrère et al. 2022
, see e.g.)
and
\tau_{\phi}=(\Delta R)^{2}/\lambda_{\rm e}\sim 10^{-2}~{\rm s}
is the diffusive time-scale, but as we will discuss in §
2.4
its value will be set by field instabilities. The correspondence with
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
can be found by noting
q\sim\Delta\Omega/\Omega
.

According to
Equation 5
, the poloidal field is generated from the toroidal field by the
\alpha
-effect and is reduced by diffusion owing to the magnetic diffusivity. The effect is significant in the presence of cyclonic convection while it also relaxes on a time-scale
\tau_{\rm p}
(see below) when the field configuration is unstable. Therefore, the evolution of the poloidal field is described by

\frac{dB_{\rm p}}{dt}=\alpha\frac{B_{\phi}}{\tau_{\phi}}-\frac{B_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{for $R$ constant}

(7)

(
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
)
where
\alpha=\tau_{\phi}\alpha_{\rm t}/\Delta R=\Delta R\alpha_{\rm t}/\lambda_{\rm e}
is a factor that originates from the
\alpha
-effect that characterizes the efficiency of the dynamo action generating the poloidal field from the toroidal field. Since the PNS collapses to a NS with radius
R=12~{\rm km}
and retains this size for most of its evolution
\Delta R/R=0.3
indicates
\Delta R=4~{\rm km}
, with
\alpha_{\rm t}\sim 10^{5}~{\rm cm~s^{-1}}

(
Reboul-Salze et al. 2022
)
, the associated value of
\alpha
lies roughly between
4\times 10^{-4}
and
4
. We choose
\alpha=1\times 10^{-3}
in most simulations, but also discuss its impact on the results by varying it.
The time-scale for the diffusion of the poloidal magnetic field is
\tau_{\rm p}\simeq(\Delta R)^{2}/\lambda_{\rm e}\sim 10^{-2}~{\rm s}
.

Considering that the radius of the PNS shrinks, the poloidal field would also increase by the conservation of magnetic flux (
\Phi
). If there was no dynamo action
\Phi=4\pi R^{2}B_{\rm p}
with
d\Phi/dt=0
would imply
\dot{B}_{\rm p}=-2B_{\rm p}\dot{R}/R
where the dot (
\cdot
) denotes the time derivative. We thus replace Eqn.(
7
) with

\frac{dB_{\rm p}}{dt}=\alpha\frac{B_{\phi}}{\tau_{\phi}}-\frac{B_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm p}}-2B_{\rm p}\frac{\dot{R}}{R}~.

(8)

where the last term stands for the field enhancement by flux conservation.

2.3
The stability of the fields

It is well known that purely toroidal or poloidal fields are unstable and the unstable modes will dominate the loss of the fields by diffusion in unstable cases.
Numerical simulations of
Braithwaite 2009
suggest that for the toroidal and poloidal magnetic fields to be stable, they should satisfy the conditions

a\eta^{2}<\eta_{\rm p}<0.8\eta

(9)

where
a
is the buoyancy factor,
\eta\equiv E/|U|
is the ratio of magnetic energy to gravitational potential energy, and
\eta_{\rm p}\equiv E_{\rm p}/|U|
is the magnetic energy in the poloidal field scaled in the same way.
The buoyancy factor,
a
, limits the growth of the toroidal component in the dynamo process and is about
10
for the main-sequence stars
(
Braithwaite 2009
;
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
)
.
It is not easy to obtain the exact value of
a

(
Rincon 2019
)
. In the case of neutron stars, with their much higher densities and stronger local gravitational fields compared to white dwarfs and combined with their superfluidity and superconductivity, the buoyancy factor is expected to be weaker
(
Braithwaite 2009
;
Goldreich &
Reisenegger 1992
;
Muslimov &
Tsygan 1985
)
. Accordingly, we choose the buoyancy factor as
a=2
in most simulations, but also reveal its impact on the results by varying it over a wide range.

The
\Omega
-process for any differentially rotating or shearing system leads to an effective dynamo process coupled with the complementary
\alpha
-effect. The field, if it does not satisfy the above constraint given in
Equation 9
, may also decay with a timescale
\tau_{\phi}
(see below).

According to
Equation 9
the toroidal field is subject to decay if
\eta_{\rm p}<a\eta^{2}

(
Braithwaite 2009
)
.
The total magnetic energy of the star is
E=B^{2}R^{3}/6
where we assumed the magnetic field to be uniform within the star. Similarly, the magnetic energy in the poloidal field is then
E_{\rm p}=B_{\rm p}^{2}R^{3}/6
.
We scale these quantities with the gravitational potential energy of a uniform Newtonian star,
|U|=3GM^{2}/5R
to determine
\eta
and
\eta_{\rm p}

(
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
)
.

The field instabilities, for a nonrotating star, operate on an Alfvén crossing-timescale
\tau_{\rm A}=R/v_{\rm A}
where
R
is stellar radius and
v_{\rm A}=B/\sqrt{4\pi\bar{\rho}}\simeq 10^{5}B_{12}~{\rm cm~s^{-1}}
is the Alfvén velocity and
\bar{\rho}=3M/4\pi R^{3}\simeq 10^{14}~{\rm g~cm^{-3}}
is the mean density of the star. In the presence of rotation, the growth rate of the instabilities is reduced by a factor of
\Omega\tau_{\rm A}/2\mathrm{\pi}

(
Pitts &
Tayler 1985
)
. Thus,
\tau_{\phi}
is given by

\tau_{\phi}=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{ if }\eta_{\rm p}>a\eta^{2}~,\\
\max\left(1,\frac{\Omega\tau_{\rm A}}{2\mathrm{\pi}}\right)\tau_{\mathrm{A}}&\text{ otherwise }~,\end{cases}

(10)

where
\Omega
is the stellar angular velocity.

The time-scale for the decay of the poloidal field is given by

\tau_{\rm P}=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{ if }0.8\eta>\eta_{\rm P}~,\\
\max\left(1,\frac{\Omega\tau_{\rm A}}{2\pi}\right)\tau_{\rm A}&\text{ otherwise}~.\end{cases}

(11)

Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
chooses
\alpha=1.52\times 10^{-4}
for their simulations of white dwarf mergers.

2.4
Angular momentum transport

A PNS is born with some shear rate which is expected to get damped in magnitude over time and terminate the dynamo process eventually. We define this change as

\frac{dq}{dt}=-\frac{q}{\tau}

(12)

where
\tau
denotes its damping time-scale. One contributing factor to this change is the shrinkage of the PNS which amplifies the shear rate with a time-scale
\tau_{\rm I}=I/\left|\dot{I}\right|
where
I=\gamma MR^{2}
is the moment of inertia and we have taken
\gamma=0.35
for a PNS of
1.4M_{☉}
.

The shear rate, which drives the dynamo process, will be damped by the viscous processes
(
Shapiro 2000
;
Margalit et al. 2022
)
with a viscous timescale
\tau_{\nu}=R^{2}/\nu
where
\nu
is the internal viscosity. In scenarios involving a rapidly rotating PNS exhibiting a rotation period on the order of several milliseconds, the energy stored in rotation serves as a significant reservoir which can be effectively utilized when magnetic fields are present. In these conditions, turbulence due to magnetorotational instability (MRI) plays a crucial role in the angular momentum transport within stellar interiors
(
Akiyama et al. 2003
)
corresponding to an effective viscosity.

\nu_{\rm MRI}=\frac{B_{\rm p}B_{\phi}}{4\pi\rho q\Omega}

(13)

(
Fuller
et al. 2019
;
Barrère et al. 2022
)
.

The PNS, initially is opaque for neutrinos, and is expected to be convectively unstable due to entropy and/or compositional (leptonic) gradients
(
Epstein 1979
;
Burrows &
Lattimer 1988
;
Burrows &
Fryxell 1992
;
Mezzacappa et al. 1998
;
Roberts et al. 2012
)
.
In fact, understanding the convective instability of the PNS gave rise to the original scenario invoked for the generation of magnetic fields of magnetars
(
Thompson &
Duncan 1993
)
.
Thus, convective dynamos has been studied extensively
(
Raynaud et al. 2020
;
Raynaud et al. 2022
;
Masada
et al. 2020
;
Masada
et al. 2022
;
White et al. 2022
)
.

Margalit et al. 2022
approximates the effective viscosity due to the convection as

\nu_{\rm conv}\simeq\tilde{\beta}v_{\rm c}\Delta R

(14)

in which
\tilde{\beta}
represents the effectiveness parameter,
v_{\rm c}
is the convective velocity and
\Delta R
is the thickness of the convective shell. Relating the surface neutrino luminosity,
L_{\nu}
, to the convective flux,
F_{\rm conv}
, by
L_{\nu}=10^{53}~{\rm erg/s}=F_{\rm conv}4\pi r^{2}
and referring
F_{\rm conv}\simeq\rho v_{\rm c}^{3}
, the convective velocity can be written as

v_{\rm c}=10^{8}~{\rm cm~s^{-1}}\left(\frac{L_{\nu}}{10^{53}~{\rm erg/s}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}\right)^{-1/3}

(15)

(
Margalit et al. 2022
)
. With
\Delta R/R=0.3
and
\tilde{\beta}=0.01
we write
\nu_{\rm conv}=0.003Rv_{\rm c}
where the radius changes from 40 km to 12 km. Since the radius changes exponentially (see
Equation 21
) we have equated the neutrino luminosity to the time derivative of the gravitational potential energy

L_{\nu}=-\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^{2}}{R^{2}}\dot{R}

(16)

and allowed
L_{\nu}
to change. A more sophisticated method can be found in
Suwa et al. 2021
. Consequently, during the initial phase, we have computed the internal viscosity through

\nu_{\rm early}=\nu_{\rm MRI}+\nu_{\rm conv}~.

(17)

To terminate the dynamo process (see §
2.8
), we assume an exponential increase in viscosity following
\sim 45\,{\rm s}
, defined as

\nu_{\rm late}=\nu_{\rm early}+(\nu_{\infty}-\nu_{\rm early})\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{t-45\,{\rm s}}{\tau_{\rm sat}}\right)\right]

(18)

where
\nu_{\infty}
represents the late-time saturation value of the viscosity and we approximate it to be in the vicinity of
10^{12}\,{\rm{cm}^{2}{s}^{-1}}
,
\tau_{\rm sat}=1\,{\rm s}
is the characteristic time-scale describing the transition of the viscosity from the early-time viscosity to
\nu_{\infty}
. Consequently, we have determined the internal viscosity employing

\nu=\begin{cases}\nu_{\rm early}&\text{ if }t<45\text{s}~,\\
\nu_{\rm late}&\text{otherwise}~,\end{cases}

(19)

with the damping time-scale

\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{1}{\tau_{\nu}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{I}}~.

(20)

\alpha=1\times 10^{-3}
.

During the PNS stage, the radius of the object shrinks from
R_{0}\sim 40\,{\rm km}
to
R_{\infty}\simeq 12\,{\rm km}
. The timescale for the collapse of the PNS depends on the mass of the PNS and the equation of state (EoS).

Camelio et al. 2017
modelled detailed evolution of the PNS for three different masses (
M=1.25,\,1.40,\,1.60\,M_{☉}
) and three different EoS.
The EoSs considered by
Camelio et al. 2017
are (i) a nuclear many-body EoS obtained by correlated basis function theory with effective interaction (CBF-EI;
Benhar &
Lovato 2017
), a mean field EoS by
Glendenning
& Moszkowski 1991
(GM3) and
Lattimer &
Swesty 1991
model (LS-bulk). We have seen that the evolution of the radius of the PNS, as obtained from the numerical results of
Camelio et al. 2017
, can be fitted with an exponential decay

R(t)=(R_{0}-R_{\infty})\exp\left(-\frac{t-t_{0}}{\tau_{R}}\right)+R_{\infty}

(21)

where
\tau_{R}=1.3-2.3
s depending on the EoS and the mass of the PNS as shown in
Figure 1
. The evolution starts after
t_{0}\sim 1
s and we assumed, for simplicity that, for
0<t<t_{0}
, the radius is constant,
R=R_{0}
, which is obviously not correct but has little effect on our results since this is a short time interval. These are consistent with the results of
Nagakura et al. 2020
.
We note that the above equation is the solution of the differential equation
\dot{R}=-(R-R_{\infty})/\tau_{R}

and we have used this form whenever it simplifies calculations.

2.6
Evolution of the gravitational mass of the PNS

Since PNS are relativistic objects there is a difference between the gravitational mass and the baryonic mass depending on the compactness,
GM/Rc^{2}
. Although the number of baryons and hence the baryonic mass remains constant, the gravitational mass decreases as the radius shrinks
(
Camelio et al. 2017
)
. We have seen that the evolution of the gravitational mass can also be fitted with an exponential function

M(t)=(M_{0}-M_{\infty})\exp\left(-\frac{t-t_{0}}{\tau_{M}}\right)+M_{\infty}

(22)

with
3.4~{\rm s}<\tau_{M}<4.5~{\rm s}
. We thus considered the mass of the PNS evolves according to
\dot{M}=-(M-M_{\infty})/\tau_{M}
, though it only leads to about
10\%
difference in mass. We again assumed that the mass is constant for
0<t<t_{0}
for the same reason as referred to in §
2.5
.

2.7
Evolution of the spin angular velocity

The angular velocity of the shrinking PNS needs to increase due to the conservation of angular momentum as

\frac{d\Omega}{dt}=-\frac{\dot{I}}{I}\Omega~.

(23)

Here, the moment of inertia will change both due to change in the radius and gravitational mass.
The external torques on the object due to electromagnetic emission, gravitational waves and neutrinos will try to spin it down. It is likely that the neutrino-driven PNS winds
(
Burrows
et al. 1995
;
Janka &
Mueller 1996
)
lead to even stronger external spin-down torques
(
Bucciantini et al. 2006
;
Metzger et al. 2007
)
. We first added these effects, but we have also seen that the change in the spin frequency due to the change in the moment of inertia dominates the change due to these external torques. We thus, for simplicity, do not include the external torques during the short time interval we consider in this work.

2.8
Termination of the dynamo action

Several processes contribute to the termination of the dynamo process, the main one being the loss of leptonic gradients reducing the convective processes.

Initially, the PNS is opaque and the neutrino cooling proceeds by diffusion until the temperature drops to
\sim 10^{10}\,{\rm K}
(0.86 MeV). This happens at around 40 s. Once the stellar matter becomes neutrino-transparent (
T\simeq 10^{10}\,{\rm K}
), any gradients sustaining the convection will flatten and this will quench the
\alpha
-process. The dynamo process, thus, proceeds for about
40\,{\rm s}

(
Raynaud et al. 2020
)
which is sufficient to achieve saturation of the poloidal component with regard to the
\alpha
-effect.

Rapid cooling of the neutron star as it becomes neutrino-transparent will lead to the solidification of a crust
(
Burrows &
Lattimer 1986
;
Glendenning 2000
)
. An effective viscosity approaching infinity damps the differential rotation to that of a rigid body, thus effectively suppressing the sources of turbulence
(
Talon &
Zahn 1997
;
Meynet &
Maeder 2000
)
.

Since cooling of the PNS is beyond the scope of the present paper, we terminate the dynamo process smoothly by increasing the viscosity after 45 s as given in Eqn. (
18
).

Results

We have numerically solved Equations (
6
)-(
12
) with the Runge-Kutta method.
We set the initial parameters as
M_{0}=1.55M_{☉}
and
R_{0}=40\,{\rm km}
as suitable for a PNS and
M_{\infty}=1.46M_{☉}
and
R_{\infty}=12
km which are appropriate for a cold neutron star. The initial angular velocity is derived from the conservation of angular momentum such that the PNS will have
P_{\infty}\simeq 3~{\rm ms}
when it settles. We assumed that the PNS inherits a small field (
B_{\rm p}(t=0)=B_{\phi}(t=0)=3\times 10^{9}\,{\rm G}
) from the progenitor by flux freezing as discussed in §
1
. We have also studied the implications of changing these parameters.

In
Figure 2
we show the evolution of the poloidal and toroidal magnetic fields and the shear rate of the star for a PNS initially rotating with
P_{0}=29.5\,{\rm ms}
. The PNS transforms into a magnetar approximately
50\,{\rm ms}
after the dynamo process, with a rotational period of
P_{\infty}=2.5\,{\rm ms}
. We note that the dynamo process can produce various types of NSs, like normal pulsars or low-B magnetars, for PNSs that initially have longer rotational periods as our following analysis, showing the dependence on initial parameters, implies. The initial evolution of the fields and differential rotation matches with that given in
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
if the radius is kept constant and viscosity is neglected. In this regime, the toroidal field grows in time from the seed field until attaining its saturation value,
B_{\phi,\infty}
. Simultaneously, the poloidal field grows monotonically and approaches the value at which it will saturate,
B_{\rm p,\infty}
, due to the non-linear feedback of
B
on the shear.

We note that the poloidal field is two orders of magnitude smaller than the toroidal field. The poloidal field will be enhanced, by flux conservation, upon the collapse of the PNS to the typical size of a neutron star. The enhancement will be
(40\,{\rm km}/12\,{\rm km})^{2}\simeq 11.1
times
B_{\rm p,\infty}
. The toroidal field will not be affected by the collapse. The gravitational potential energy
|3GM^{2}/5R|
will also be enhanced by a factor of
40\,{\rm km}/12\,{\rm km}\simeq 3.3
.

The shear rate attains a maximum value during the contraction phase of the PNS. Subsequently, it undergoes a gradual decline to zero, consistent with the theoretical expectations. This arises from the significant increase in the internal viscosity which suppresses the differential rotation, and consequently reduces the shear rate.

\sim 10^{15}\,{\rm G}
. This was recently confirmed by

P_{\infty}

B_{\rm p}\sim 10^{10}-10^{11}~{\rm G}

. Our results suggest that, although their poloidal fields are no different than what they inherited from their progenitor by flux conservation, their toroidal fields are shaped by the dynamo process.

4
Discussion

We studied the
\alpha-\Omega
dynamo process, which is largely assumed to operate during the PNS phase. This dynamo mechanism is recognized for its ability to produce strong magnetic fields through the combined effects of vigorous differential rotation and convective motions; hence, it is regarded as a potential source for the magnetic fields of the magnetars.

We used a simple one-dimensional model originally introduced by
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
for mergers of white dwarfs. We additionally included the effects of fundamental evolutionary processes like the shrinkage of the PNS and the resulting reduction in the gravitational mass. We also consider magnetorotational and convective instabilities which play an important role in the dynamo process contributing to the internal viscosity. The simplicity of the model allows us to study the full episode of the dynamo and make a parameter scan to see the dependencies.

Following a recent argument by
Lander 2021
that the magnetic fields of neutron stars obtained through flux conservation are small, we considered the possibility that normal pulsars also pass through the same dynamo process, albeit with larger initial periods.
We considered a specifically wide range of initial spin periods reaching up to 200 ms.

Our findings indicate that, under different initial conditions of convective flow and rotation rate, PNSs can not only have magnetic fields evolving into magnetar level strengths (
\gtrsim 10^{14}\,{\rm G}
), but also into the field strength of distinct sub-populations that may be observed as low-B magnetars, high-field pulsars and normal pulsars. This diversity arises from the sensitivity of the dynamo process to the initial conditions and to the internal structural parameters of the star. Our study confirms that PNSs with very rapid rotation rates are particularly prone to magnetar formation, whereas those with more moderate spin rates can evolve into high-magnetic field pulsars or even normal pulsars. In this picture, only central compact objects would not pass through a dynamo process, since their fields can easily be explained with flux conservation. This is also consistent with the initial periods estimated for these objects:
P_{0}\simeq 100\,{\rm ms}
. Such slow periods are unlikely to trigger dynamo action within the neutron star. We found that, although the poloidal fields of CCOs are set by flux conservation, their toroidal fields are shaped by the
\Omega
-effect (§
3.1.7
).

All neutron stars may have some small-scale fields. However, it appears that the relative strength of the small-scale fields of CCOs with their dipole fields is greatest among young pulsars, a result of their relatively small dipole fields. In general, the dynamo action organizes the fields by converting the small-scale fields into dipole and toroidal fields. As such, the relatively strong small-scale fields of CCOs
(
Shabaltas &
Lai 2012
;
Gourgouliatos et al. 2020
;
Alford et al. 2022
)
could be attributed to the fact that they have not gone through a genuine dynamo process.

Consequently, our examination of the
\alpha-\Omega
dynamo process suggests that this model, initially proposed for magnetars, has a reasonable parameter space that can address young neutron star families.
