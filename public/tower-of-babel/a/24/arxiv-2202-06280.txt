On the complexity of All ε \varepsilon -Best Arms Identification
Aymen Al Marjani, Tomas Kocak, Aurélien Garivier

Abstract
We consider the question introduced by [ MJTN20 ] of identifying all the ε \varepsilon -optimal arms in a finite stochastic multi-armed bandit with Gaussian rewards. We give two lower bounds on the sample complexity of any algorithm solving the problem with a confidence at least 1 − δ 1-\delta . The first, unimprovable in the asymptotic regime, motivates the design of a Track-and-Stop strategy whose average sample complexity is asymptotically optimal when the risk δ \delta goes to zero. Notably, we provide an efficient numerical method to solve the convex max-min program that appears in the lower bound. Our method is based on a complete characterization of the alternative bandit instances that the optimal sampling strategy needs to rule out, thus making our bound tighter than the one provided by [ MJTN20 ] . The second lower bound deals with the regime of high and moderate values of the risk δ \delta , and characterizes the behavior of any algorithm in the initial phase. It emphasizes the linear dependency of the sample complexity in the number of arms. Finally, we report on numerical simulations demonstrating our algorithm’s advantage over state-of-the-art methods, even for moderate risks.

Introduction

The problem of finding all the
\varepsilon
-good arms was recently introduced by
[
MJTN20
]
. For a finite family of distributions
({\bm{\nu}}_{a})_{a\in[K]}
with vector of mean rewards
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{a})_{a\in[K]}

G_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}})\triangleq\{a\in[K]:\mu_{a}\geq\max_{i}\mu_{i}-\varepsilon\}

G_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}})\triangleq\{a\in[K]:\mu_{a}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\max_{i}\mu_{i}\}

arms selection and the THRESHOLD bandits. The former aims to find the
k

[

]

\varepsilon
-good arms is a more robust objective than the TOP-K and THRESHOLD problems, which require some prior knowledge of the distributions in order to return a relevant set of solutions. Take for example drug discovery applications, where the goal is to perform an initial selection of potential drugs through
in vitro

k

k

\varepsilon

In this paper, we want to identify

in a PAC learning framework with fixed confidence: for a risk level

in a sequential manner to gather information about the distribution means

]

\delta\to 0

]
combined with an algorithmic reduction to Best Arm Identification and shows the dependency of the sample complexity on the number of arms
K
for moderate values of
\delta
. They also proposed FAREAST, an algorithm matching the first lower bound, up to some numerical constants and log factors, in the asymptotic regime

. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Usual lower bounds on the sample complexity write as

•

We derive a second lower bound that writes as

in Theorem
3
.
g(\nu)
shows an additional linear dependency on the number of arms which is negligible when

but can be dominant for moderate values of the risk. This result generalizes Theorem 4.1 in
[

•

We present two efficient methods to solve the minimization sub-problem (resp. the entire max-min program) that defines the characteristic time. These methods are used respectively in the stopping and sampling rule of our Track-and-Stop algorithm, whose sample complexity matches the lower bound when
\delta
tends to 0.

•

Finally, to corroborate our asymptotic results, we conduct numerical experiments for a wide range of the confidence parameters and number of arms. Empirical evaluation shows that Track-and-Stop is optimal either for a small number of arms

\delta
goes to 0, and excellent in practice for much larger values of

We start by proving a lower bound on the sample complexity of any

2.1
First lower bound

Let
\Delta_{K}
denote the
K

\operatorname{kl}(p,q)

p

Using change-of-measure arguments introduced by

Proposition 1
.

For any
\delta

\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\delta}]\geq T_{\varepsilon}^{*}({\bm{\mu}})\log(1/2.4\delta)

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}^{*}({\bm{\mu}})^{-1}\triangleq\sup_{{\bm{\omega}}\in\Delta_{K}}T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}\qquad\hbox{and}

(1)

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}\triangleq\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Alt}({\bm{\mu}})}\sum_{a\in[K]}\omega_{a}\frac{(\mu_{a}-\lambda_{a})^{2}}{2}\;.

(2)

The characteristic time
T_{\varepsilon}^{*}({\bm{\mu}})
above is an instance-specific quantity that determines the difficulty of our problem. The optimization problem in the definition of
T_{\varepsilon}^{*}({\bm{\mu}})
can be seen as a two-player game between an algorithm which samples each arm
a
proportionally to
\omega_{a}
and an adversary who chooses an alternative instance
\bm{\lambda}
that is difficult to distinguish from
{\bm{\mu}}
under the algorithm’s sampling scheme. This suggests that an optimal strategy should play the optimal allocation
{\bm{\omega}}^{*}
that maximizes the optimization problem (
1
) and, as a consequence, rules out all alternative instances as fast as possible. This motivates our algorithm, presented in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Algorithm

We propose a simple Track-and-Stop strategy similar to the one proposed by
[
GK16
]
for the problem of Best-Arm Identification. It starts by sampling once from every arm
a\in[K]
and constructs an initial estimate
\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{K}
of the vector of mean rewards
{\bm{\mu}}
. After this burn-in phase, the algorithm enters a loop where at every iteration it samples according to the estimated optimal sampling rule (
3
) and updates its estimate
\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}
of the arms’ expectations. Finally, the algorithm checks if the stopping rule (
4
) is satisfied, in which case it stops and returns the set of empirically
\varepsilon
-good arms.

Sampling rule:

our sampling rule performs so-called C-tracking: first, we compute
\widetilde{{\bm{\omega}}}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})
, an allocation vector which is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}
-optimal in the lower-problem (
1
) for the instance
\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}
. Then we project
\widetilde{{\bm{\omega}}}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})
on the set
\Delta_{K}^{\eta_{t}}=\Delta_{K}\cap[\eta_{t},1]^{K}
. Given the projected vector
\widetilde{{\bm{\omega}}}^{\eta_{t}}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})
, the next arm to sample from is defined by:

a_{t+1}=\argmin\limits_{a}N_{a}(t)-\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{{\bm{\omega}}}_{a}^{\eta_{t}}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{s})

(3)

where
N_{a}(t)
is the number of times arm
a
has been pulled up to time
t
. In other words, we sample the arm whose number of visits is farther behind its corresponding sum of empirical optimal allocations. In the long run, as our estimate
\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}
tends to the true value
{\bm{\mu}}
, the sampling frequency
N_{a}(t)/t
of every arm
a
will converge to the oracle optimal allocation
{\bm{\omega}}^{*}_{a}({\bm{\mu}})
. The projection on
\Delta_{K}^{\eta_{t}}
ensures exploration at minimal rate
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{(K^{2}+t)}}
so that no arm is left-behind because of bad initial estimates.

Stopping rule:

To be sample-efficient, the algorithm should should stop as soon as the collected samples are sufficiently informative to declare that
G_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})=G_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}})
with probability larger than
1-\delta
. For this purpose we use the Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) test
[
Che59
]
. We define the
Z
-statistic:

Z(t)=t\times T_{\varepsilon}\bigg(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t},\frac{N(t)}{t}\bigg)^{-1}

where
N(t)=\big(N_{a}(t)\big)_{a\in[K]}
. As shown in
[
GK16
,
GK21
]
, the Z-statistic is equal to the ratio of the likelihood of observations under the most likely model where
G_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})
is the correct answer, i.e.
\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}
, to the likelihood of observations under the most likely model where
G_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})
is not the set of
\varepsilon
-good arms. The algorithm rejects the hypothesis
G_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})\neq G_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}})
and stops as soon as this ratio of likelihoods becomes larger than a certain threshold
\beta(\delta,t)
, properly tuned to ensure that the algorithm is

-PAC. The stopping rule is defined as:

\tau_{\delta}=\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ Z(t)>\beta(t,\delta)\big\}

(4)

One can find many suitable thresholds from the bandit literature
[
Gar13
]
,
[
MCP14
]

[
KK18
]

\beta(\delta,t)\approx\log(1/\delta)+\frac{K}{2}\log(\log(t/\delta))
is enough to ensure that
\mathbb{P}\big(G_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\tau_{\delta}})\neq G_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}})\big)\leq\delta
, i.e. that the algorithm is
\delta
-correct.

Track and Stop

For a given vector

\bm{\lambda}^{*}_{\varepsilon,{\bm{\mu}}}({\bm{\omega}})\triangleq\argmin_{\bm{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Alt}({\bm{\mu}})}\sum_{a\in[K]}\omega_{a}\frac{(\mu_{a}-\lambda_{a})^{2}}{2}.

4.0.1
Computational complexity of our algorithm.

To simplify the presentation and analysis, we chose to focus on the vanilla version of Track and Stop. However, in practice this requires solving the optimization program that appears in the lower bound at every time step, which can result in large run times. Nonetheless, we note that there are many possible adaptations of Track and Stop that reduce the computational complexity, while retaining the guarantees of asymptotic optimality in terms of the sample complexity (and with a demonstrated small performance loss experimentally). A first solution is to use Franke-Wolfe style algorithms

6
Conclusion

We shed a new light on the sample complexity of finding all the
\varepsilon
-good arms in a multi-armed bandit with Gaussian rewards. We derived two lower bounds, identifying the characteristic time that reflects the true hardness of the problem in the asymptotic regime. Moreover, we proved a second bound highlighting an additional cost that is linear in the number of arms and can be arbitrarily larger than the first bound for moderate values of the risk. Then, capitalizing on an algorithm solving the optimization program that defines the characteristic time, we proposed an efficient Track-and-Stop strategy whose sample complexity matches the lower bound for small values of the risk level. Finally, we showed through numerical simulations that our algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art methods for bandits with small to moderate number of arms. Several directions are worth investigating in the future. Notably, we observe that while Track-and-Stop performs better in the fixed-
K
-small-
\delta
regime, the elimination based algorithms (ST)
2
and FAREAST become more efficient in the large-
K
-fixed-
\delta
regime. It would be interesting to understand the underlying tradeoff between the number of arms and confidence parameter. This will help design pure exploration strategies having best of both worlds guarantees.

\forall i\in[|0,2^{n}-1|],\ \mu^{i}\textrm{ and }\mu^{i+1}\textrm{ have the same arm ordering and }\left\lVert\mu^{i+1}-\mu^{i}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{2^{n-1}}.

We can construct such a sequence in the following way: Split
[\mu_{min},\mu_{max}]^{K}
into
K!
regions such that any two instances in the same region share the same arm ordering. The boundaries between these regions correspond to instances where two or more arms are equal. Starting from
\mu^{0}\triangleq{\bm{\mu}}
, span the segment
[{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}]
and define
\mu^{i+1}
to be the first instance where: either the
L^{\infty}
distance from
\mu^{i}
is equal to
\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{2^{n-1}}
, or we cross a boundary between two regions. Since there can be at most
K!-1
changes in the arm ordering, for
n
large enough such sequence always exists. Now we have:

\displaystyle|T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}}^{\prime},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}-T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\omega}},{\bm{\mu}}^{i+1})^{-1}-T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\omega}},{\bm{\mu}}^{i})^{-1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}2(\mu_{max}-\mu_{min}+\varepsilon)\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{2^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 4(\mu_{max}-\mu_{min}+\varepsilon)\left\lVert{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

where in the second inequality we use the first case and the fact that
\mu^{i}
and
\mu^{i+1}

\displaystyle|T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}}^{\prime},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}-T_{\varepsilon}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}|\leq 4(\mu_{max}-\mu_{min}+\varepsilon)\left\lVert{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}.
