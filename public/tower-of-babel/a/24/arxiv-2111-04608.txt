Variance asymptotics and central limit theory for geometric functionals of Poisson cylinder processes
Carina Betken, Matthias Schulte, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
This paper deals with the union set of a stationary Poisson process of cylinders in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} having an ( n − m ) (n-m) -dimensional base and an m m -dimensional direction space, where m ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , n − 1 } m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\} and n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . The concept simultaneously generalises those of a Boolean model and a Poisson hyperplane or m m -flat process. Under very general conditions on the typical cylinder base a Berry-Esseen bound for the volume of the union set within a sequence of growing test sets is derived. Assuming convexity of the cylinder bases and of the window a similar result is shown for a broad class of geometric functionals, including the intrinsic volumes. In this context the asymptotic variance constant is analysed in detail, which in contrast to the Boolean model leads to a new degeneracy phenomenon. A quantitative central limit theory is developed in a multivariate set-up as well. Keywords . Berry-Esseen bound, central limit theorem, geometric functional, intrinsic volume, multivariate central limit theorem, Poisson cylinder process, second-order Poincaré inequality, stochastic geometry, variance asymptotics MSC . Primary 60D05; Secondary 52A22, 53C65, 60F05.

Introduction

The development of quantitative central limit theorems for spatial random structures has been one of the driving forces in stochastic geometry over the last years. Most of the recent breakthroughs were made possible due to the development of new technical devices that were perfectly adapted to geometry-driven applications. Most notable in this context is the Malliavin-Stein method for normal approximation of functionals of Poisson processes. Originally introduced in
[
28
]
and further developed in many subsequent works it has turned out to be a versatile device with a vast of potential applications. As concrete examples we mention the works
[

,
18
,
19
,

,
21
,

,
30
,

]
on various models for geometric random graphs, the paper
[

]
dealing with geometric random simplicial complexes, the application to the classical Boolean model
[
16
]
, the works
[
8
,
15
,
23
,
29
]
dealing with Poisson hyperplane tessellations in Euclidean and non-Euclidean spaces, the applications in
[
20
,
34
]
to Poisson-Voronoi tessellations, the works on excursion sets of Poisson shot-noise processes
[
17
,
19
]
as well as the papers
[
4
,
5
,
20
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
considering different models for random polytopes. For an illustrative overview on the Malliavin-Stein method for functionals of Poisson processes we refer to the collection of surveys in
[
27
]
.

The present paper continues this line of research by developing a central limit theory for functionals of so-called Poisson cylinder processes. In this paper we understand by a cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
any set of the form
X+E
, where
E
is an
m
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
X\subset E^{\perp}
is a compact subset in the orthogonal complement of
E
and
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is a fixed dimension parameter. We refer to
E
as the direction space and to
X
as the cylinder base. A Poisson cylinder process in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(for fixed
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
) is a Poisson process on the space of cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as just described, and our focus is on the induced random set that arises as the union of all cylinders. We shall focus on the case where this union set is a stationary random closed set in the usual sense of stochastic geometry (see
[
33
, Chapter 2]
). In this situation a Poisson cylinder process is described by three parameters: the intensity parameter, the dimension parameter
m
and the joint distribution of the direction and the base of the so-called typical cylinder in the sense of Palm distributions. One motivation to study Poisson cylinder processes arises from the observation that they interpolate between two classical models in stochastic geometry, the Boolean model (for
m=0
) and the Poisson hyperplane process (for
m=n-1
and for degenerate cylinder bases) or, more generally, Poisson
m
-flat processes (again for degenerate cylinder bases). However, while the Boolean model is, in a sense, locally defined as all its grains are compact, Poisson hyperplane processes show long-range dependencies induced by the infinitely extended hyperplanes. A Poisson cylinder process inherits properties of both of these extreme cases and it is the purpose of this paper to contribute to a better understanding of the resulting geometric and probabilistic phenomena. Moreover, we would like to mention that Poisson cylinder processes have found concrete applications in material technology, for example, in mathematical models for gas diffusion layers, see
[
37
]
. In this context, the results we develop in this paper can be useful in statistical inference, for example, by developing parametric hypothesis tests. We remark that our results heavily depend on the fact that we have an underlying Poisson process. For some results on planar cylinder processes driven by other point processes we refer the reader to
[
9
]
.

To be more concrete, we briefly describe the main contributions of this paper. We start by considering the volume of general stationary Poisson cylinder processes. In this case a central limit theorem together with a bound for the speed of convergence was already obtained in
[
13
]
, but the Malliavin-Stein method allows us to significantly reduce the necessary moment assumptions. While in
[
13
]
it was assumed that the volume of the so-called typical cylinder base has finite exponential moments, we are able to deduce a rate of convergence of the same quality under a third or fourth moment condition (depending on the probability metric in which the speed is measured). This is also in line with the classical Berry-Esseen theorem for sums of independent random variables and answers a question raised in
[
13
,
14
]
. In a next step, we specialise our set-up by requiring the cylinder bases to be convex. This implies that the intrinsic volumes and many more general geometric functionals related to a stationary Poisson cylinder process become well-defined random variables. For such functionals we develop a comprehensive qualitative and quantitative central limit theory for the univariate and the multivariate case, which parallels and extends the results for the classical Boolean model in
[
16
]
. However, in our situation we will uncover new effects which are not present for the Boolean model. We will see that due to the long-range correlations that are immanent to Poisson cylinder processes the speed of convergence in the central limit theorem gets slowed down with increasing dimension parameter
m
. Rather notable in this context is the asymptotic analysis of second-order quantities. While the asymptotic variance constant is known to be strictly positive for a very broad class of functionals of the Boolean model (including the celebrated intrinsic volumes, for example) and also for the volume of a Poisson cylinder process, we will see that this is not necessarily true for other geometric functionals such as the surface area or, more generally, the intrinsic volumes. More precisely, using the general Fock space representation of Poisson functionals we shall see that asymptotically for
m\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, second-order quantities of Poisson cylinder processes behave like their (possibly vanishing) projections onto the first Wiener chaos whenever the distribution of the direction of the typical cylinder has no atoms. This unexpected and striking new effect is in sharp contrast to the results for the Boolean model, where the projections to all chaoses contribute to the asymptotic behaviour (see
[
16
,
22
]
). Even more, we shall explain that this phenomenon breaks down for discrete direction distributions. In this situation again the projections to all chaoses play a non-trivial role. We shall also develop a criterion that ensures strict positivity of asymptotic variance constants of the intrinsic volumes of order
m
to
n
.
As a concrete example, we provide fully explicit formulas for the asymptotic covariance structure of the intrinsic volumes of order
n
and
n-1
by means of a reduction to the classical Boolean model in
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
and some integral formulas from
[
16
]
.

be a centred Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the same covariance matrix as
\mathbf{F}
. Then

d_{3}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{F}})\leq d\,\alpha_{\mathbf{F},1}+{d\over 2}\,\alpha_{\mathbf{F},2}+{d^{2}\over 4}\,\alpha_{\mathbf{F},3}.

3
Main results

3.1
Central limit theorem for the volume

Figure 1:
Realisations of Poisson cylinder processes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
m=1
and circular (left) and rectangular (right) cylinder bases.

Fix a space dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
and another dimension parameter
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. As introduced before, we denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
the space of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
and for
X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
we define the
cylinder

Z(X):=X\times\mathbb{E}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mathbb{E}^{m}
stands for the
m
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
generated by the
m
last unit vectors of the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the product space

\mathbb{M}_{n,m}:=\textup{SO}_{n,m}\times\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})

and let
\mathbb{Q}
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
. By
\zeta
we denote a stationary Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
with intensity
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
and by
\eta
an independent
\mathbb{Q}
-marking of
\zeta
. By the well-known marking property of Poisson processes (see, e.g.,
[
22
, Theorem 5.6]
),
\eta
is itself a Poisson process on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
with intensity measure given by
\mu:=\gamma\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. We call
\eta
a
Poisson cylinder process
and denote by

Z=\bigcup_{(x,\theta,X)\in\eta}Z(x,\theta,X),

where
Z(x,\theta,X)=\theta((X+x)\times\mathbb{E}^{m})
, the associated
union set
, see Figure
1
. In what follows it will be convenient for us to denote by
(\Theta,\Xi)
a random element in
\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
with distribution
\mathbb{Q}
and to put

{m_{a}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi)^{a}]\qquad\text{for}\qquad a>0.}

Under a condition on the typical cylinder base the union set
Z
is a random closed set, see
[
13
, Lemma 4]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The union set
Z
is a random closed set, provided that

{\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi+\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n-m})]<\infty\quad\text{for some}\quad r>0,}

(3.1)

where
\Xi+\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n-m}
stands for the Minkowski sum of
\Xi
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n-m}
.

From now on we shall assume that (
3.1
) is satisfied. In particular, (
3.1
) ensures that the union set
Z
is
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely locally finite. We are interested in the volume of the union set
Z
that can be observed in a test set
W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\mathscr{L}^{n}(W)>0
. The expectation and the variance of this random variable are given as follows, see
[
13
]
. Also, we recall a lower variance bound from
[
13
, Lemma 1]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and let
W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then the following statements hold:

(i)

\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W)]=\mathscr{L}^{n}(W)\big(1-e^{-\gamma m_{1}}\big)
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W))=e^{-2\gamma m_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(W\cap(W-x))\big(e^{-\gamma\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi\cap(\Xi-\Pi(\Theta^{T}x))]}-1\big)\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(\textup{d}x)
,
where
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
stands for the orthogonal projection onto the first
n-m
coordinates.

(iii)

Suppose that
m_{2}\in(0,\infty)
and that
\mathbb{B}_{\delta}^{n}\subseteq W
for some
\delta>0
. Then, putting
W_{r}:=rW
for
r>0
, and
c_{v}:=\liminf\limits_{r\to\infty}r^{-(n+m)}\operatorname{var}(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r}))
, one has that
c_{v}\in(0,\infty)
and there exists a constant
\underline{c}_{v}\in(0,\infty)
only depending on
n,m,\gamma,\delta,m_{1}
and
m_{2}
such that
c_{v}\geq\underline{c}_{v}
.

The next quantitative central limit theorem is our main contribution for the volume of the union set of a Poisson cylinder process.

Theorem 3.3

(CLT for the volume)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Suppose that
m_{2}\in(0,\infty)
. Let
W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
\mathbb{B}_{\delta}^{n}\subseteq W
for some
\delta>0
, and put
W_{r}:=rW
for
r>0
. Also, let
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be a standard Gaussian random variable.

(i)

If
m_{3}<\infty
, then there exist constants
C,r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
r\geq{r_{0}}
,

d_{W}\bigg({\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r})-\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r})]\over\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r}))}},N\bigg)\leq C\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}.

(ii)

If
m_{4}<\infty
, then there exist constants
C,r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
r\geq{r_{0}}
,

d_{K}\bigg({\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r})-\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r})]\over\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r}))}},N\bigg)\leq C\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}.

Remark 3.4
.

The constants
C
in parts (i) and (ii) of Theorem
3.3
depend only on
n
,
m
,
\gamma
,
W
,
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
and
m_{3}
as well as - in case of (ii) -
m_{4}
.
The dependence on
W
is only via
\mathscr{L}^{n}(W)
,
\operatorname{diam}(W)
and
\delta
. In both parts, the constant
r_{0}
is chosen in such a way that
r^{-(n+m)}\operatorname{var}(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W_{r}))\geq\frac{1}{2}{\underline{c}_{v}}
for all
r\geq r_{0}
, where
\underline{c}_{v}
is the lower bound for the asymptotic variance constant from Proposition
3.2
(iii).

Let us discuss the relation of Theorem
3.3
to the existing literature. The bound for the Kolmogorov distance was previously found by Heinrich and Spiess
[
13
]
, but under much more restrictive moment conditions. In fact, they assume that the
(n-m)
-volume of the typical cylinder base
\Xi
has finite
exponential
moments, while our approach works under a third- or fourth-order moment assumption, respectively, depending on whether one seeks for a bound for the Wasserstein or the Kolmogorov distance. Against this light, Theorem
3.3
answers a question raised after Theorem 2 in
[
13
]
. Without a rate of convergence, a central limit theorem for the volume of the union set of a Poisson cylinder process was proved by Heinrich and Spiess
[
14
]
under the second moment assumption
m_{2}<\infty
. This is in line with the classical central limit theorem for sums of independent and identically distributed random variables, which holds under a second moment assumption as well. When we take
m=0
, the union set
Z
is the same as the classical Boolean model. In this case, central limit theorems for the volume were first obtained by Baddeley
[
2
]
and Mase
[
24
]
. A quantitative central limit theorem for the Kolmogorov distance was shown by Heinrich
[
10
]
under the assumption that the volume of the typical grain has some finite exponential moments.
A rate of convergence in terms of the Wasserstein distance is due to Hug, Last and Schulte
[
16
]
(see also Chapter 22 in
[
22
]
). A direct proof for the rate of convergence for the Kolmogorov distance under the assumption that the fourth moment exists is new even for the Boolean model, but the result can also be concluded as a special case of the quantitative multivariate central limit theorem
[
36
, Theorem 4.2 (d)]
for the convex distance.

3.2
Central limit theorems for geometric functionals

We adopt the notation from the previous section and let
\eta
be a Poisson cylinder process with stationary union set
Z
. However, from now on we assume that all cylinders have a
convex
base, that is, instead of
\textup{SO}_{n,m}\times\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
in the construction of
\eta
we consider the mark space

\mathbb{M}_{n,m}=\textup{SO}_{n,m}\times\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m}),

where
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
denotes the space of compact convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
. Assuming that the condition (
3.1
) on the typical cylinder base
\Xi
is satisfied, the set
Z\cap W
is
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely an element of the convex ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any test set
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where we recall that
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the space of finite unions of compact convex sets. In particular, this implies that the intrinsic volumes
V_{i}(Z\cap W)
for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
are well–defined random variables (here and in what follows, by the intrinsic volumes we understand the additive extension of the intrinsic volumes for convex sets to the convex ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
). Our goal in this section is to prove a central limit theorem for
V_{i}(Z\cap W_{r})
, as
r\to\infty
, where
W_{r}=rW
for some fixed
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. More generally, following
[
16
,
22
]
, we shall consider a rather broad class of additive functionals satisfying some further natural assumptions.

A function
\varphi:\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
is called
additive
, provided that
\varphi(\varnothing)=0
and

\varphi(K\cup L)=\varphi(K)+\varphi(L)-\varphi(K\cap L)

(3.2)

for all
K,L\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We call
\varphi

translation invariant
if

\varphi(K+x)=\varphi(K)

(3.3)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
K\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover, we say that
\varphi
is
locally bounded
if

M(\varphi):=\sup\{|\varphi(K)|:K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),K\subseteq[0,1]^{n}\}<\infty.

(3.4)

Finally, by a
geometric
functional
\varphi
we understand a measurable functional which is additive, translation invariant and locally bounded. The wide class of geometric functionals contains the following examples, which are of particular interest (see also the discussion in
[
16
, page 79]
):

-

the intrinsic volumes
\varphi(K)=V_{i}(K)
with
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
(see
[
31
, Chapter 4]
),

-

mixed volumes of the form
\varphi(K)=V(K[\ell],K_{1},\ldots,K_{n-\ell})
for
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, where the notation
K[\ell]
means that
K
is repeated
\ell
times and
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n-\ell}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are fixed (see
[
31
, Chapter 5]
),

-

integrals of surface area measures of the form
\varphi(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}g(u)\,S_{K}^{(\ell)}(\textup{d}u)
, where
S_{K}^{(\ell)}
,
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is the
\ell
th surface area measure of
K
and
g:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded measurable function (see
[
31
, Chapter 4]
),

-

the centred support function
\varphi(K)=h(K-s(K),u)
for fixed
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, where
s(K)
stands for the Steiner point of
K
(see
[
31
, page 262]
),

-

the total measures from translative integral geometry (see
[
33
, Chapter 6.4]
).

Our main result in this section is the following quantitative central limit theorem for geometric functionals. To state it, we introduce the following notation. For a geometric functional
\varphi
and
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we define the asymptotic (lower) variance constant
v(\varphi,W)\in[0,\infty]
by

v(\varphi,W):=\liminf_{r\to\infty}r^{-(n+m)}{\operatorname{var}(\varphi(Z\cap W_{r}))}.

(3.5)

We emphasise that for
\varphi=V_{n}
(where we write
V_{n}
instead of
\mathscr{L}^{n}
for sets from the convex ring) we have already encountered the variance constant in Proposition
3.2
(iii), where
v(V_{n},W)=c_{v}
.

Theorem 3.5

(CLT for geometric functionals)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and let
\varphi
be a geometric functional. Let
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
V_{n}(W)>0
, and put
W_{r}:=rW
for
r>0
. Also, let
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be a standard Gaussian random variable and assume that
v(\varphi,W)>0
.

(i)

If
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{2}]<\infty
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, then

{\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})]\over\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\varphi(Z\cap W_{r}))}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{3}]<\infty
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, then there exist constants
C,r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
r\geq\max\{1,r_{0}\}
,

d_{W}\bigg({\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})]\over\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\varphi(Z\cap W_{r}))}},N\bigg)\leq C\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}.

(iii)

If
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{4}]<\infty
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, then there exist constants
C,r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
r\geq\max\{1,r_{0}\}
,

d_{K}\bigg({\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})]\over\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\varphi(Z\cap W_{r}))}},N\bigg)\leq C\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}.

Remark 3.6
.

The constants
C
in parts (ii) and (iii) of Theorem
3.5
only depend on
n
,
m
,
\gamma
,
W
,
M(\varphi)
,
v(\varphi,W)
,
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{2}]
,
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{3}]
and - in case of (iii) -
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{4}]
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
. The dependence on the test set
W
is only via
v(\varphi,W)
,
V_{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})
and
\operatorname{diam}(W)
, where we recall that
W^{\sqrt{n}}
is the
\sqrt{n}
-parallel set of
W
. Note that according to Steiner’s formula (see
[
33
, Equation (14.5)]
),
V_{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})
can be expressed as a linear combination of the intrinsic volumes
{V_{0}(W)},\ldots,V_{n}(W)
with dimension-dependent coefficients. The constant
r_{0}
appearing in parts (ii) and (iii) is chosen in such a way that
r^{-(n+m)}\operatorname{var}(\varphi(Z\cap W_{r}))\geq\frac{1}{2}v(\varphi,W)
for all
r\geq r_{0}
, which is possible due to our assumption that
v(\varphi,W)>0
.

Choosing
\varphi=V_{n}
Theorem
3.5
(ii) and (iii) reduce to Theorem
3.3
but under more restrictive geometric assumptions on the typical cylinder base
\Xi
and the test set
W
. We would like to remark that for
\varphi=V_{n-1}
a central limit theorem for
\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})
under a second moment assumption was derived in
[
14
]
without a rate of convergence.
Moreover, choosing
m=0
, in which case the random set
Z
is just the Boolean model, the results of Theorem
3.5
(i) and (ii) are known from
[
16
,
22
]
, while the bound in Theorem
3.5
(iii) is again a special case of
[
36
, Theorem 4.2 (d)]
. Non-quantitative central limit theorems for the surface area and related quantities of Boolean models were derived in
[
12
,
25
]
.

After having developed a univariate central limit theory, we now turn to the multivariate case.
Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{d}
be geometric functionals and put

F_{r}^{(i)}:=\varphi_{i}(Z\cap W_{r}),\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

where
W_{r}=rW
for
r>0
. Further define the
d
-dimensional random vector

\mathbf{F}_{r}:=\bigg({F_{r}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}[F_{r}^{(1)}]\over r^{n+m\over 2}},\ldots,{F_{r}^{(d)}-\mathbb{E}[F_{r}^{(d)}]\over r^{n+m\over 2}}\bigg)

as well as

\sigma_{ij}(r):=\operatorname{Cov}\bigg({F_{r}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}[F_{r}^{(i)}]\over r^{n+m\over 2}},{F_{r}^{(j)}-\mathbb{E}[F_{r}^{(j)}]\over r^{n+m\over 2}}\bigg),\qquad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

The following result is the multivariate version of Theorem
3.5
, where in the quantitative part (ii) the speed of convergence is measured in the
d_{3}
-distance.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let the set-up just described prevail and put
\Sigma_{r}:=(\sigma_{ij}(r))_{i,j=1}^{d}
.

(i)

Assume that
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{2}]<\infty
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
and that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{r}
converges, as
r\to\infty
, to some covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Denote by
\mathbf{N}_{\Sigma}
a
d
-dimensional centred Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Then,

\mathbf{F}_{r}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{N}_{\Sigma},\qquad\text{as}\qquad r\to\infty.

(ii)

Assume that
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{3}]<\infty
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
and denote by
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{F}_{r}}
a
d
-dimensional centred Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{r}
. Then there exists a constant
C\in(0,\infty)
only depending on
n
,
m
,
d
,
\gamma
,
W
,
M(\varphi_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{3}]
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
such that, for all
r\geq 1
,

d_{3}(\mathbf{F}_{r},\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{F}_{r}})\leq C\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}.

As a special case the previous result contains a multivariate central limit theorem for the (centred and normalised) random vector of intrinsic volumes of
Z\cap W_{r}
, as
r\to\infty
, if we choose
d=n+1
and
\varphi_{i}=V_{i-1}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
. Theorem
3.7
generalises the multivariate central limit theorem from
[
16
]
for the Boolean model (corresponding to the choice
m=0
) to general Poisson cylinder processes.

Remark 3.8
.

As a multivariate counterpart to the one-dimensional Kolmogorov distance one usually considers the so called convex distance. For two
d
-dimensional random vectors
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
it is given by

d_{\rm convex}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}):=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{Y}\in A)|:A\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big\}.

The convex distance dominates the multivariate Kolmogorov distance, which is the supremum norm of the difference of the distribution functions of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, and is in contrast to the multivariate Kolmogorov distance invariant under linear transformations. In
[
36
]
second-order Poincaré inequalities for the multivariate normal approximation of Poisson functionals in the
d_{\rm convex}
-distance were derived. As an example, multivariate central limit theorems with rates of convergence for the convex distance were shown for intrinsic volumes of Boolean models in
[
36
, Theorem 4.2]
. By combining
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
with similar arguments as in the proofs of Theorem
3.5
, Theorem
3.7
and
[
36
, Theorem 4.2]
, one can extend Theorem
3.7
to the
d_{\rm convex}
-distance. Under the slightly stronger assumption that
\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{4}]<\infty
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
and that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{r}
is positive definite, one obtains

d_{\rm convex}(\mathbf{F}_{r},\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{F}_{r}})\leq C\max\{\|\Sigma_{r}^{-1/2}\|_{\rm op},\|\Sigma_{r}^{-1/2}\|_{\rm op}^{3}\}\,r^{-{n-m\over 2}}

(3.6)

for all
r\geq 1
. Here,
C\in(0,\infty)
is some constant depending on the model parameters,
\Sigma_{r}^{-1/2}
denotes the unique positive definite matrix such that
\Sigma_{r}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{r}^{-1/2}=\Sigma_{r}^{-1}
, and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\rm op}
stands for the operator norm of a matrix. For the Boolean model, one can show for many choices of additive functionals that
\Sigma_{r}
converges to a positive definite matrix as
r\to\infty
(see
[
16
, Section 4]
) so that the maximum in (
3.6
) can be bounded by a constant. For general Poisson cylinder processes the covariance structure can behave differently than for the Boolean model and it can happen that the limiting covariance matrix is singular (see Subsection
3.3
and, in particular, the discussion next to Remark
3.16
). In this situation it is not clear how the right-hand side of (
3.6
) behaves for
r\to\infty
as the maximum tends to infinity. For that reason we have restricted ourselves to the
d_{3}
-distance.

Remark 3.9
.

If
\Xi=\{0\}

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, the Poisson cylinder process reduces to a Poisson process of
m
-dimensional flats in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this case one has for any additive functional
\varphi
that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely

\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\ell!}\sum_{((x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{\ell},\theta_{\ell},X_{\ell}))\in\eta^{\ell}_{\neq}}\varphi(\theta_{1}(\{x_{1}\}\times\mathbb{E}^{m})\cap\ldots\cap\theta_{\ell}(\{x_{\ell}\}\times\mathbb{E}^{m})\cap W_{r}),

where
\eta^{\ell}_{\neq}
denotes the set of all
\ell
-tuples of distinct elements of
\eta
. The
\ell
-th summand on the right-hand side is a so-called Poisson
U
-statistic of order
\ell
. For such random variables multivariate central limit theorems were established in
[
23
]
. If the geometric functionals are the intrinsic volumes, the assertion of Theorem
3.7
can also be deduced from
[
23
, Theorem 3]
.

3.3
Variance asymptotics for geometric functionals

In this section we discuss the assumption that
v(\varphi,W)>0
in Theorem
3.5
. In contrast to previous findings, we assume that
m>0
throughout and, thus, exclude the case of a Boolean model, for which the asymptotic covariance structure is studied in
[
16
]
. We start by presenting an explicit formula for the asymptotic variance constant
v(\varphi,W)
. To this end, we introduce some notation, which follows
[
33
, Chapter 9.2]
. For a geometric functional
\varphi:\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
,
X\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
and
\theta\in\textup{SO}_{n,m}
we put

\displaystyle\varphi(\theta([0,1)^{m}+X)):=\varphi(\theta([0,1]^{m}+X))-\varphi(\theta(\partial^{+}[0,1)^{m}+X)),

(3.7)

where
\partial^{+}[0,1)^{m}:=[0,1]^{m}\setminus[0,1)^{m}
is the upper right boundary of the unit cube in
\mathbb{E}^{m}
. For
\theta\in\textup{SO}_{n,m}
we define

T(W,\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{L}^{m}(H(y,\theta)\cap W)^{2}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}y),

(3.8)

where
H(y,\theta)=\theta(\mathbb{E}^{m}+y)
. Finally, if
\mathbb{Q}

The following result gives a sufficient condition which ensures that the asymptotic variance constant is positive for intrinsic volumes of order greater or equal to

Proposition 3.19
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and consider a direction non-atomic Poisson cylinder process with rotation invariant base-direction distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose that the covariance matrix
C=\big(\operatorname{Cov}(V_{i}(\Xi),V_{j}(\Xi))\big)_{i,j={0}}^{{n-m}}
of the intrinsic volumes of the typical cylinder base
\Xi
exists and is positive definite. Then
v(V_{k},W)>0
for each
k\in\{m,\ldots,n\}
.

An example to which Proposition
3.19
applies arises if
\Xi
is a random dilatation of a fixed convex body, that is, if
\Xi=R\cdot K
for a non-constant random variable
R\geq 0
and a fixed convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
with
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(K)>0
. In fact, assuming that
R
has a density and that
\mathbb{E}[R^{2(n-m)}]<\infty
we obtain for
{0}\leq i,j\leq{n-m}
that

\operatorname{Cov}(V_{i}(\Xi),V_{j}(\Xi))=V_{i}(K)V_{j}(K)\,\operatorname{Cov}(R^{i},R^{j})=V_{i}(K)V_{j}(K)\,\big(\mathbb{E}[R^{i+j}]-\mathbb{E}[R^{i}]\,\mathbb{E}[R^{j}]\big).

For
u=(u_{0},\ldots,u_{n-m})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m+1}
, we obtain that

u^{T}Cu=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-m}u_{\ell}V_{\ell}(K)R^{\ell}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-m}u_{\ell}V_{\ell}(K)R^{\ell}\bigg]^{2}.

By Hölder’s inequality and the assumptions on the distribution of
R
the last expression is non-zero. This implies the positive definiteness of the matrix
C
and hence positivity of the asymptotic variance constant for the intrinsic volumes of order
m,\ldots,n
and convex windows
W
.

We prepare the proof of Theorem

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}((X+x)\cap W)\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)=\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(X)\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(W),\qquad X,W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m}),

(4.1)

stated in
[
33
, Theorem 5.2.1]
, which is included as special case
m=0
(and with
n
formally replaced by
n-m
). A version for cylinders with a convex base can be found in
[
32
, Theorem 2]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Let
X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})
and
W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for any
\theta\in\textup{SO}_{n,m}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x,\theta,X)\cap W)\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)=\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(X)\mathscr{L}^{n}(W).

Proof.

Using Fubini’s theorem we write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x,\theta,X)\cap W)\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bf 1}\{y\in W\}{\bf 1}\{y\in Z(x,\theta,X)\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(\textup{d}y)\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bf 1}\{y\in W\}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{y\in Z(x,\theta,X)\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(\textup{d}y).

Next we notice that
{\bf 1}\{y\in Z(x,\theta,X)\}=1
if and only if
y\in\theta((X+x)\times\mathbb{E}^{m})
, which in turn is equivalent to
\theta^{T}y\in(X+x)\times\mathbb{E}^{m}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{y\in Z(x,\theta,X)\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{\theta^{T}y\in(X+x)\times\mathbb{E}^{m}\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)=\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(X),

independently of
\theta
and
y
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x,\theta,X)\cap W)\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(X)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bf 1}\{y\in W\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(\textup{d}y)=\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(X)\mathscr{L}^{n}(W)

and the proof is complete.
∎

We shall next compute the difference operators of the Poisson functional
F:=\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W)
. A version of this result for the Boolean model (which is included as the special case

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
W\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
F:=\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W)
and fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely and for

-almost all

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}^{k}F=(-1)^{k}\bigg[\mathscr{L}^{n}\Big(Z\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})\cap W\Big)-\mathscr{L}^{n}\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})\cap W\Big)\bigg].

Proof.

We recall that for
(x,\theta,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
the first-order difference operator
{\sf D}_{(x,\theta,X)}F
is given by

{\sf D}_{(x,\theta,X)}F=\mathscr{L}^{n}((Z\cup Z(x,\theta,X))\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap W).

This representation and the additivity of
\mathscr{L}^{n}
imply that

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{(x,\theta,X)}F=\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x,\theta,X)\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap Z(x,\theta,W)\cap W)

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely and for
(\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
-almost all
(x,\theta,X)
. Similarly, one has that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely and for
(\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q})^{2}
-almost all
(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
,

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})}^{2}F={\sf D}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1})}{\sf D}_{(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})}F

\displaystyle=(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}((Z\cup Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}))\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W))

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}((Z\cup Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}))\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z\cap Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1})\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1})\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})\cap W).

Iterating this argument leads to the result for general
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

and where

is a constant which only depends on the parameters mentioned in Remark
3.4
.
Plugging now (

), (

), (

), (

), (

) and (

) into Proposition

Proofs II: Central limit theorems for geometric functionals

5.1
Preparations

For
A\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we denote by
\eta([A])
the number of cylinders of
\eta
that intersect
A
. Let us also recall that the number of
k
-dimensional faces of the
n
-dimensional cube is given by
2^{n-k}{n\choose k}
,
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Hence, the total number of faces of an
n
-dimensional cube is
3^{n}
, a constant which will repeatedly appear below. The following two lemmas generalise the ideas of
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
22
, Proposition 22.4]
from the case of the Boolean model (
m=0
) to general Poisson cylinder processes (with
m\geq 1
). Recall from (
3.4
) that

M(\varphi):=\sup\{|\varphi(K)|:K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),K\subseteq[0,1]^{n}\}

for a geometric functional
\varphi:\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For a geometric functional
\varphi:\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
,
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
(\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q})^{{k}}
-almost all
(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
,

\displaystyle\big|{\sf D}^{k}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}\varphi(Z\cap W)\big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 3^{n}\,M(\varphi)\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\mathbf{1}\Big\{(z+[0,1]^{n})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})\cap W\neq\varnothing\Big\}2^{\eta([z+[0,1]^{n}])}

holds
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
4.2
for additive functionals (recall Remark
4.3
) that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely and for
(\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q})^{k}
-almost all
(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
,

\displaystyle{\sf D}^{k}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}\varphi(Z\cap W)

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{k}\Big[\varphi\Big(Z\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})\cap W\Big)-\varphi\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})\cap W\Big)\Big].

For a set
K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is contained in a translate of
[0,1]^{n}
let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{\eta([K])}
denote the cylinders of
\eta
that hit
K
. The additivity (see (
3.2
)), the translation invariance (see (
3.3
)) and the local boundedness (see (
3.4
)) of
\varphi
yield

|\varphi(Z\cap K)|=\bigg|\sum_{\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\eta([K])\}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{I}|-1}\varphi\Big(\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{I}}Z_{i}\cap K\Big)\bigg|\leq(2^{\eta([K])}-1)M(\varphi).

(5.1)

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and define
\mathcal{Q}(A):=\{z+[0,1]^{n}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},(z+[0,1]^{n})\cap A\neq\varnothing\}
. By additivity of
\varphi
we obtain that

\varphi(Z\cap A)=\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|-1}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}\varphi\Big(Z\cap A\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big)

(5.2)

so that (
5.1
) leads to

|\varphi(Z\cap A)|\leq M(\varphi)\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}(2^{\eta([\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q])}-1).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle|\varphi(A)|\leq\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}\Big|\varphi\Big(A\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big)\Big|\leq M(\varphi)\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}.

Since each cube of
\mathcal{Q}(A)
is contained in at most
3^{n}
intersections (recall that
3^{n}
is the total number of faces of an
n
-dimensional cube), we obtain that

\displaystyle|\varphi(Z\cap A)|\leq 3^{n}\,M(\varphi)\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}(A)}(2^{\eta([Q])}-1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\varphi(A)|\leq 3^{n}\,M(\varphi)|\mathcal{Q}(A)|,

(5.3)

which proves the assertion.
∎

To proceed, we define

c_{k,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{E}[2^{k\eta([[0,1]^{n}])}]=e^{\mathbb{E}[\eta([[0,1]^{n}])](2^{k}-1)}\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}:=c_{4,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}=e^{15\mathbb{E}[\eta([[0,1]^{n}])]},

and note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta([[0,1]^{n}])]

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{M}_{n,m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{Z(x,\theta,X)\cap[0,1]^{n}\neq\varnothing\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)\mathbb{Q}(\textup{d}(\theta,X))

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{M}_{n,m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{(X+x)\times\mathbb{E}^{m}\cap\theta^{T}[0,1]^{n}\neq\varnothing\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)\mathbb{Q}(\textup{d}(\theta,X))

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{M}_{n,m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\bf 1}\{(X+x)\cap\Pi(\theta^{T}[0,1]^{n})\neq\varnothing\}\,\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\textup{d}x)\mathbb{Q}(\textup{d}(\theta,X))

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{M}_{n,m}}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Pi(\theta^{T}[0,1]^{n})+(-X))\,\mathbb{Q}(\textup{d}(\theta,X))

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Pi(\Theta^{T}[0,1]^{n})+(-\Xi))],

where we recall that
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
stands for the orthogonal projection onto the first
n-m
coordinates.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\varphi
be a geometric functional and
W\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for
(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}),

(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle(i)\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[({\sf D}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1})}\varphi(Z\cap W))^{2}({\sf D}_{(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})}\varphi(Z\cap W))^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 81^{n}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}\,M(\varphi)^{4}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{2}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{2},

\displaystyle(ii)\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[({\sf D}^{2}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3})}\varphi(Z\cap W))^{2}({\sf D}^{2}_{(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}),(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3})}\varphi(Z\cap W))^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 81^{n}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}\,M(\varphi)^{4}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap Z(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap Z(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{2},

\displaystyle(iii)\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[({\sf D}^{2}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2})}\varphi(Z\cap W))^{4}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 81^{n}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}\,M(\varphi)^{4}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{4},

\displaystyle(iv)\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\sf D}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1})}\varphi(Z\cap W)|^{k}]\leq 3^{kn}\,c_{k,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}\,M(\varphi)^{k}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}})^{k}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
the expressions in the expectations can be bounded by products of the form

\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\bigg[3^{n}M(\varphi)\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\mathbf{1}\{(z+[0,1]^{n})\cap W\cap Z_{j}\neq\varnothing\}2^{\eta([z+[0,1]^{n}])}\bigg],

where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
Z_{j}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, are intersections of subsets of
Z(x_{i},\theta_{i},X_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. For
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\mathbf{1}\{(z+[0,1]^{n})\cap A\neq\varnothing\}\leq\mathscr{L}^{n}(A^{\sqrt{n}})

(5.4)

by definition of

. Hence, the
j
-th factor in the above product has at most
\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}}\cap Z_{j}^{\sqrt{n}})
summands. Moreover, Hölder’s inequality yields that, for
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}2^{\eta([z_{j}+[0,1]^{n}])}\leq\mathbb{E}2^{\ell\eta([0,1]^{n})}.

Combining these estimates proves the desired inequalities (i)–(iv).
∎

The following bound for the expectation of the
k
-th difference operator will be used in Section

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a geometric functional,

and

\big|\mathbb{E}[{\sf D}^{k}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}\varphi(Z\cap W)]\big|\leq 3^{n}\,c_{1,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}\,M(\varphi)\,\mathscr{L}^{n}\big(\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}Z(x_{j},\theta_{j},X_{j}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W^{\sqrt{n}}\big)

.

We have that

\big|\mathbb{E}[{\sf D}^{k}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}\varphi(Z\cap W)]\big|\leq\mathbb{E}[|{\sf D}^{k}_{(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},\theta_{k},X_{k})}\varphi(Z\cap W)|].

The next auxiliary result will be applied in Section
6
. We denote by
A\triangle B
the symmetric difference of two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.
A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\varphi
be a geometric functional and
A,B\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(Z\cap A)-\varphi(Z\cap B)|]\leq 2\cdot 3^{n}M(\varphi)\big(c_{1,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}-1\big)\mathscr{L}^{n}((A\triangle B)^{\sqrt{n}})
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(A)-\varphi(B)|]\leq 2\cdot 3^{n}M(\varphi)\mathscr{L}^{n}((A\triangle B)^{\sqrt{n}})
.

Proof.

It follows from (
5.2
) that

\varphi(Z\cap A)=\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|-1}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}\varphi\Big(Z\cap A\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big)

and

\varphi(Z\cap B)=\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(B)}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|-1}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\neq\varnothing\Big\}\varphi\Big(Z\cap B\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big).

These identities imply that

\displaystyle|\varphi(Z\cap A)-\varphi(Z\cap B)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap A\neq\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap B\Big\}\Big|\varphi\Big(Z\cap A\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(B)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap A\neq\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap B\Big\}\Big|\varphi\Big(Z\cap B\cap\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\Big)\Big|.

It follows from the equivalence of
\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap A\neq\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap B
and
\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap(A\triangle B)\neq\varnothing
together with (
5.1
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(Z\cap A)-\varphi(Z\cap B)|]

\displaystyle\leq M(\varphi)(c_{1,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}-1)\bigg(\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(A)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap(A\triangle B)\neq\varnothing\Big\}+\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(B)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bigcap_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap(A\triangle B)\neq\varnothing\Big\}\bigg).

Combining the fact that each
Q
belongs to at most
3^{n}
intersections with

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}(M)}\mathbf{1}\Big\{Q\cap(A\triangle B)\neq\varnothing\Big\}\leq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\mathbf{1}\Big\{(z+[0,1]^{n})\cap(A\triangle B)\neq\varnothing\Big\}\leq\mathscr{L}^{n}((A\triangle B)^{\sqrt{n}})

for
M\in\{A,B\}
completes the proof of (i). The proof of (ii) goes analogously.
∎

5.2
Proof of Theorem

: Wasserstein bound and qualitative result

Using Proposition
2.1
we can now prove a central limit theorem for general additive functionals for the Wasserstein distance. In principle, the proof works in the same way as the proof of Theorem
3.3
, but some careful analysis is needed to handle the general additive functionals. Analogously to Section
4.2
we put

F_{\varphi,r}{:=}\varphi(Z\cap W_{r})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad G_{\varphi,r}:=\frac{F_{\varphi,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[F_{\varphi,r}\right]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})}}.

Starting with the bound for
\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},1}
, Fubini’s theorem and Lemma
5.2
yield

\begin{split}\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},1}^{2}\leq&\frac{4\cdot 81^{n}\,\gamma^{3}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}M(\varphi)^{4}}{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{M}_{n,m}^{3}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n-m})^{3}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})\\
&\quad\times\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{1},\theta_{1},X_{1}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap Z(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})\\
&\quad\times\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z(x_{2},\theta_{2},X_{2}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap Z(x_{3},\theta_{3},X_{3}^{\sqrt{n}})\cap W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})\\
&\quad\times(\mathscr{L}^{n-m})^{3}(\textup{d}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))\,{\mathbb{Q}^{3}}(\textup{d}((\theta_{1},X_{1}),(\theta_{2},X_{2}),(\theta_{3},X_{3}))).\end{split}

(5.5)

Using exactly the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
for the Wasserstein distance, we derive that

\displaystyle\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},1}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\cdot 81^{n}\,\gamma^{3}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}M(\varphi)^{4}\,{\operatorname{diam}(W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})^{3m}}}{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})^{2}}\mathscr{L}^{n}(W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})\,(\widetilde{m}_{2})^{3},

where for
a>0
we define
{\widetilde{m}_{a}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi^{\sqrt{n}})^{a}].}

Note that we use the convention
W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}}=(W_{r})^{\sqrt{n}}
. For

\mathscr{L}^{n}(W_{r}^{\sqrt{n}})=\mathscr{L}^{n}(W_{r}+\mathbb{B}^{n}_{\sqrt{n}})\leq\mathscr{L}^{n}(W_{r}+r\mathbb{B}^{n}_{\sqrt{n}})\leq r^{n}\mathscr{L}^{n}(W+\mathbb{B}^{n}_{\sqrt{n}})=r^{n}\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},1}\leq 2\cdot 9^{n}\,\gamma^{\frac{3}{2}}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,M(\varphi)^{2}\,\operatorname{diam}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{3}{2}m}\,\widetilde{m}_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}\,{\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}m+\frac{1}{2}n}}{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})}.

(5.6)

(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{i})^{k}\leq\ell^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{i}^{k}
, which is valid for
k,\ell\geq 1

a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}>0

\displaystyle\widetilde{m}_{k}\leq(n-m+1)^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-m}n^{k(n-m-j)\over 2}\kappa_{n-m-j}^{k}\mathbb{E}[V_{j}(\Xi)^{k}],

(5.7)

which implies that

5.2

\displaystyle\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},2}\leq 9^{n}\,\gamma^{\frac{3}{2}}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}M(\varphi)^{2}\,\operatorname{diam}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{3}{2}m}\,\widetilde{m}_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}\,{\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}m+\frac{1}{2}n}}{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})}.

\begin{split}T_{pq}:=&\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\times\mathbb{M}_{n,m}}\mathbb{E}[|{\sf D}_{(x,\theta,X)}G_{\varphi,r}|^{p}]^{q}\,(\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\otimes\mathbb{Q})(\textup{d}(x,\theta,X))\\

for

and

.

\displaystyle\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},3}\leq 27^{n}\,\gamma\,c_{3,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}M(\varphi)^{3}\,\operatorname{diam}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{2m}\,\widetilde{m}_{3}\,\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})\,\frac{r^{2m+n}}{\operatorname{var}(F_{\varphi,r})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(5.10)

5.7
) shows that
\widetilde{m}_{3}<\infty
by our assumptions on the moments of the intrinsic volumes of the typical cylinder base.

\displaystyle d_{W}(G_{\varphi,r},N)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},1}+\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},2}+\alpha_{G_{\varphi,r},3}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(6v(\varphi,W)^{-1}9^{n}\,\gamma^{\frac{3}{2}}\,c_{\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}M(\varphi)^{2}\,\operatorname{diam}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{3}{2}m}\,\widetilde{m}_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}\,{\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=:S_{1,u}+S_{2,u}.

By bounding

and
S_{2,u}
as
T_{pq}
above, we obtain that

, as

, and that

\limsup_{r\to\infty}S_{2,u}\leq\frac{{2}\gamma 3^{2n}c_{3,\gamma,\mathbb{Q}}^{2/3}M(\varphi)^{2}}{v(\varphi,W)}\operatorname{diam}(W^{\sqrt{n}})^{m}\mathscr{L}^{n}(W^{\sqrt{n}})\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi^{\sqrt{n}})>u\}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(\Xi^{\sqrt{n}})^{2}].

Letting now

completes the proof of Theorem

(i) since the expectation on the right-hand side converges to zero by the second moment assumptions on the intrinsic volumes of
\Xi
.

5.3
Proof of Theorem

: Kolmogorov bound

In this section we carry out the proof of Theorem

(iii). As for the volume, this requires to bound the three additional terms

and

In order to bound the fourth moment of

\displaystyle V_{i}([0,1)^{m}+\Xi)
