Singular solutions to 𝒌 \bm{k} -Hessian equations with fast-growing nonlinearities Thanks: ∗ Corresponding Author
João Marcos do Ó ∗, Evelina Shamarova, Esteban da Silva

Abstract
. We study a class of elliptic problems, involving a k k -Hessian and a very fast-growing nonlinearity, on a unit ball. We prove the existence of a radial singular solution and obtain its exact asymptotic behavior in a neighborhood of the origin. Furthermore, we study the multiplicity of regular solutions and bifurcation diagrams. An essential ingredient of this study is analyzing the number of intersection points between the singular and regular solutions for rescaled problems. In the particular case of the exponential nonlinearity, we obtain the convergence of regular solutions to the singular and analyze the intersection number depending on the parameter k k and the dimension d d .

1.  Introduction

In this paper, we are concerned with the following Dirichlet problem:

(1)

\begin{cases}S_{k}(D^{2}w)=\lambda\,F(-w)&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
w<0,&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
w=0,&\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded domain,
F
is a positive function increasing to infinity, whose exact properties
will be specified later, and
\lambda>0
is a parameter.
Above,
1\leqslant k\leqslant d
is an integer, where
d>2
is the dimension, and
S_{k}(D^{2}w)
is the
k
-Hessian,
i.e., the symmetric elementary function of the
k
-th order of the eigenvalues of

D^{2}w
.

We prove the existence of a specific radial singular solution to (
1
) in the case
when
\Omega
is a unit ball and obtain its exact asymptotic behavior in a neighborhood of the origin. We furthermore demonstrate that
this specific singular solution allows to study properties of radial regular solutions to (
1
) such as bifurcation diagrams and
multiplicity, where an important tool is the number of intersection points
between the singular and regular solutions for rescaled problems.

1.4  Methods

In this work, we use combinations of newly developped methods along with extensions, from the Laplacian to
the
k
-Hessian, of existing techniques.
New methods are used for construction
of a singular solution and obtaining its sharp asymptotic (Theorem
1.1
), for
proving the convergence of regular solutions to the singular (Theorem
1.7
), and
for showing that the regular and singular solutions to equation (
9
) do not intersect each other if

\alpha-\beta-1\geqslant 4\theta(\beta+1)
(Theorem
1.5
). To be specific, in the proof of Theorem

1.1
, we arrive at the equation

(11)

\displaystyle v^{\prime\prime}-(\hat{\alpha}-1)v^{\prime}=-\frac{e^{-\theta t+f(v)}}{|v^{\prime}|^{\beta}}

which is equivalent to the equation in (

P
λ

), but the variable
t
varies
in a neighbourhood of infinity. The difficulty arises due to the fact that the denominator
on the right-hand side contains
|v^{\prime}|^{\beta}
, the term which is not present in the Laplacian case
(
\beta=0
). This does not allow to apply methods known for semilinear equations with the Laplacian
(see, e.g.,
[
12
,
24
,
28
]
).
In particular, the singular solution
v^{*}
to (
11
) takes the form

(12)

\displaystyle v^{*}(t)=g(\theta t+\varphi_{1}(t)+\varphi_{2}(t))+\eta(t),

where

(13)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}=\ln\{\theta^{\beta}\big(g^{\prime}(\theta t)+(\beta+1)g^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)\ln g^{\prime}(\theta t)\big)^{\beta}\},

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}=\ln\Big\{\theta\Big(\frac{\alpha}{\beta+1}-1\Big)\Big\}+\ln\{g^{\prime}(\theta t)+(\beta+1)g^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)\ln g^{\prime}(\theta t)\}

and
\eta(t)=O(g^{\prime\prime}(\theta t))
.
Remark that the term
\varphi_{1}
does not appear in the Laplacian case;
it was for the first time introduced in this work.
Its role is to make certain cancelations with the denominator
|{v^{*}}^{\prime}|^{\beta}
to ensure
the possibility to reduce (
11
) to a pair of semilinear equations with respect to
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
. The order of the reminder
\eta
, in the general setting, was
also first established in this work. Additional technical difficulties arise due
to the fact that the function
f
(unlike
[
12
,
24
,
28
]
)
is arbitrary, so the asymptotic representation (
6
) is expressed via the derivatives of
f^{-1}
.

Furthermore, in the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we use the result of Section
3
on the relative compactness of regular solutions on each
compact interval of
(0,+\infty)
(along with the result of
[
18
]
).
To the best of our knowledge, the aforementioned technique is new. Also, it is worth to mention Theorem
1.6

whose proof does not appear to have analogs in the literature; while the result itself, together
with Theorem
1.5
, can be regarded as fundamental. Indeed, Theorems
1.5

and
1.6
study the number of intersection points between the regular and singular
solutions for the canonical equation (
9
), the result which did not appear in
the literature prior to this work.

Furthermore, many of our methods were previously unknown in the context of
k
-Hessian equations. They represent non-trivial extensions, from the Laplacian to the
k
-Hessian, of existing techniques.
For example, in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, dealing with the intersection number between the regular
and singular solutions, one obtains a certain equation via the following transformation of equation (
7
):

(14)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(s,\rho)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1}\varepsilon_{\rho}^{-\frac{\theta}{\beta+1}}\mathcal{F}(u(\varepsilon_{\rho}s,\rho)),\quad\varepsilon_{\rho}=\Big(\frac{\mathcal{F}(\rho)}{\mathcal{F}_{1}(1)}\Big)^{(\beta+1)/\theta},

where

\mathcal{F}(u)=\int_{u}^{+\infty}\exp\big\{\!\!-\!\frac{f(s)}{1+\beta}\big\}ds
and

\mathcal{F}_{1}(u)=(\beta+1)e^{-\frac{u}{\beta+1}}
.
We believe that in the
k
-Hessian case, this transformation, in its exact form (
14
),
was first used in this work.
A version of this transformation, slightly different from (
14
), was introduced in
[
13
]
.
The “Laplacian” analog of (
14
) can be found in, e.g.,
[
30
]
.
The important difference with the Laplacian case is the following: when we apply this tranformation,
the first two terms
L(\tilde{u})+e^{\tilde{u}}
of the transformed equation are exactly as in
the canonical equation (
9
); however, the additional
term
s^{\alpha-\gamma}|\tilde{u}^{\prime}|^{\beta+2}\big(\frac{I(u(\varepsilon_{\rho}s))}{\beta+1}-1\big)
,
where

,
has a more complicated structure and its
convergence to zero is rather difficult to show.
This convergence however is crucial to show the convergence
of
\tilde{u}
to the regular solution of the canonical (limit) equation (
9
).
Remark that even in the Laplacian case, considered in
[
30
]
,
the aforementioned convergence was announced without a proof.
It is important to mention that in order to show the convergence
of singular solutions, which is also crucial for proving Theorem
1.3

we employ our novel asymptotic (

1.1

Thus, we reinforce that the proof of Theorem

Furthermore, the proof of Theorem

1.3
discussed above. In addition,

on the oscillation of
\lambda(\rho)
arround
\lambda^{*}
compared to
[

]
and preceeding works.

Finally, we
mention Proposition

\displaystyle u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r)-B\geqslant\lambda^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}\int_{r}^{1}t^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta+1}}dt\,\Big(\int_{0}^{r}s^{\gamma}e^{f(u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r))}ds\Big)^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}.

\displaystyle\sup_{u\geqslant B}\big\{(u-B)\,e^{-\frac{f(u)}{\beta+1}}\big\}\geqslant(u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r)-B)e^{-\frac{f(u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r))}{\beta+1}}\geqslant K_{1}\lambda^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}(r^{\frac{\theta}{\beta+1}}-r^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\beta+1}}),

By (B2),

.
Hence, the right-hand side of (

, we obtain that
for all
B\geqslant 0

\rho\geqslant B

(52)

\displaystyle R(B,\rho)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\leqslant K_{2}\sup_{u\geqslant B}\big\{(u-B)\,e^{-\frac{f(u)}{\beta+1}}\big\}^{\frac{\beta+1}{\theta}}=\bar{R}(B),

where

is a constant. Now we prove the existence of a lower bound

by using identity (

By L’Hopital’s rule and (B2),

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to+\infty}\frac{u\,e^{f(u)}}{\int_{0}^{u}e^{f(t)}dt}=\lim_{u\to+\infty}\frac{e^{f(u)}+u\,e^{f(u)}f^{\prime}(u)}{e^{f(u)}}=1+\lim_{u\to+\infty}uf^{\prime}(u)=+\infty.

As we show below, it suffices to prove the existence of

\displaystyle(\gamma+1)\int_{0}^{u}e^{f(t)}dt<au\,e^{f(u)}\quad\text{for all}\;\;u\geqslant B,

is to be fixed later.
Indeed, suppose the existence of
\underline{R}(B)
is obtained for those

which make inequality (
53

u\geqslant B
.
If
B\geqslant 0
is arbitrary, we find a number
\tilde{B}>B
such that (

) holds for

.
Then,
\underline{R}(\tilde{B})\leqslant R(\tilde{B},\rho)\leqslant R(B,\rho)
for

and
\rho_{0}(B)=\rho_{0}(\tilde{B})
.
Thus, without loss of generality, we assume that

is

The number
a

By (

\displaystyle r\,|u^{\prime}(r)|<a\,\frac{\beta+1}{\beta+2}\,u(r).

\displaystyle r^{\alpha}|v^{\prime}|^{\beta}v^{\prime}=-\tilde{D}\int_{\tilde{R}}^{r}s^{\gamma}ds-\tilde{R}^{\alpha}|u^{\prime}(\tilde{R})|^{\beta+1},

It remains to show that on

\displaystyle(r^{\alpha}|v^{\prime}|^{\beta+1})^{\prime}=r^{\gamma}e^{f(D)}\geqslant r^{\gamma}e^{f(u)}=(r^{\alpha}|u^{\prime}|^{\beta+1})^{\prime}\text{ and}

\displaystyle r^{\alpha}|v^{\prime}|^{\beta+1}\geqslant r^{\alpha}|u^{\prime}|^{\beta+1}.

Proposition 3.2

\displaystyle u(r,\rho)=\rho-\int_{0}^{r}t^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta+1}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}s^{\gamma}e^{f(u(s,\rho))}ds\Big)^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}dt\geqslant-e^{\frac{f(B)}{\beta+1}}(\gamma+1)^{-\frac{1}{\beta+1}}\hat{\theta}^{-1}r_{2}^{\hat{\theta}}.

By the Arzelà-Ascolli theorem, the family

Let us prove now that each limit point of the family

\displaystyle u(r,\rho_{n})=u(r_{1},\rho_{n})-\int_{r_{1}}^{r}t^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta+1}}\Big(r_{1}^{\alpha}|\partial_{r}u(r_{1},\rho_{n})|^{\beta+1}+\int_{r_{1}}^{t}s^{\gamma}e^{f(u(s,\rho_{n}))}ds\Big)^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}dt.

\{u(r,\rho_{n})\}

.
Note that
\{u(r,\rho_{n})\}

on
(0,+\infty)

Let us show now the following fact. Suppose

converges to

pointwise on
(0,+\infty)

\rho_{n}
is unbounded,

is a singular solution, if

{\lambda^{*}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}
are zeros
of
u(\,\cdot\,,\rho)
and
u^{*}
, respectively. Since
u
and
u^{*}
are decreasing in
r
,
they do not have other zeros.
Define
r(\rho)=\min\{\lambda(\rho)^{\frac{1}{\theta}},{\lambda^{*}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\}
and

z(\rho)=\mathscr{L}_{(0,r(\rho))}\hat{v}(\,\cdot\,,\rho)
. Note that if
\rho
is sufficiently large, then,
by Proposition
3.1
, we can find two positive constants
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
such that

\tau_{1}<r(\rho)<\tau_{2}
. Furthermore, we note that
z(\rho)
is positive and finite.
Indeed,
z(\rho)>0
since, by Theorem
4.2
,

\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}\mathscr{L}_{(0,\tau_{1})}(\hat{v}(\,\cdot\,,\rho))=+\infty
.
Assuming that
z(\rho)
is infinite, we can find a sequence
\{r_{n}\}
of zeros of
\hat{v}
converging to
a point
\tilde{r}\in[0,r(\rho)]
. One has
\hat{v}(\tilde{r},\rho)=\partial_{r}\hat{v}(\tilde{r},\rho)=0
which
contradicts to (
77
).
Thus, we conclude that for each fixed
\rho
, the equation
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0
has a finite number of solutions on
(0,r(\rho))
.
By the same argument, for each fixed
\rho>0
,
the equation
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0
has a finite number of solutions on any finite interval
[0,R]
, and therefore,
there is a countable number of zeros of
\hat{v}(r,\rho)
on
(0,+\infty)
.

Fix
\rho_{0}>0
and
take an arbitrary zero
r_{0}
of
\hat{v}(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{0})
.
By (
77
),
\partial_{r}\hat{v}(r_{0},\rho_{0})\neq 0
. By the implicit function theorem,
there exists an interval
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
, containing
\rho_{0}
,
and a unique continuous function
\mathfrak{r}(\rho)
solving the equation
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0

on
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and such that
\mathfrak{r}(\rho_{0})=r_{0}
.
Without loss of generality,
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
can be regarded
as the maximal interval to which
\mathfrak{r}(\rho)
can be extended. This extension
will be unique by (
77
) and the implicit function theorem.
The curve
\mathfrak{r}(\rho)
may cross
r(\rho)
at some point from
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

or may not. When it happens a zero enters or exits the interval
(0,r(\rho))
.
Remark that
\rho_{1}
can be either zero or positive;

\rho_{2}
can be either finite or
+\infty
.
Let us show that if
\rho_{2}
is finite, then
\lim_{\rho\to\rho_{2}-}\mathfrak{r}(\rho)=+\infty
.
Suppose
\rho_{2}
is finite, but
\lim_{\rho\to\rho_{2}-}\mathfrak{r}(\rho)\neq+\infty
.
Then, we can find a sequence
\rho^{n}\to\rho_{2}
as
n\to+\infty

such that
\mathfrak{r}(\rho^{n})
converges to a number
r_{2}\in(0,+\infty)
. By continuity,
\hat{v}(r_{2},\rho_{2})=0
.
If
r_{2}
is the limit of
\mathfrak{r}(\rho)

as
\rho\to\rho_{2}
, then, by the implicit function theorem, we can find an extension of
\mathfrak{r}(\rho)

beyond
\rho_{2}
which is a contradiction. Therefore, the limit at
\rho_{2}
does not exist.
Then, we can find a neighborhood
U=U(r_{2},\rho_{2})=(r_{2}-\delta,r_{2}+\delta)\times(\rho_{2}-\delta,\rho_{2}+\delta)
,
where 1) the equation
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0
is uniquely solvable for each fixed
\rho\in(\rho_{2}-\delta,\rho_{2}+\delta)

and, furthermore, determines
r
as a continuous function of
\rho
living inside
U
; and 2)

\mathfrak{r}(\rho)
exits and enters
U
infinitely many times. This contradicts to
the implicit function theorem. Hence, if
\rho_{2}
is finite, we have that
\lim_{\rho\to\rho_{2}-}\mathfrak{r}(\rho)=+\infty
.
By the similar argument, one can show that if
\rho_{1}>0
, then

\lim_{\rho\to\rho_{1}+}\mathfrak{r}(\rho)=+\infty
.
Also remark that by the implicit function theorem and (
77
), two functions
\mathfrak{r}_{1}(\rho)
and
\mathfrak{r}_{2}(\rho)
representing
two different solutions of
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0
cannot exit or enter the interval
(0,r(\rho)]
through its right end at the same point
\rho
.
One can extend this observation stating that two functions
\mathfrak{r}_{1}(\rho)
and
\mathfrak{r}_{2}(\rho)
representing
two different solutions of
\hat{v}(r,\rho)=0
never intersect each other, which means that two zeros cannot merge.
The above arguments allow us to conclude that
z(\rho)
is positive,
finite, and changes only by
1
when a zero of
\hat{v}(\,\cdot\,,\rho)
enters or exists the
interval
(0,r(\rho))
through its right end.

Let us prove now that
\lambda(\rho)
oscillates infinitely around
\lambda^{*}
as
\rho\to+\infty
.
First, we note that
\hat{v}(0,\rho)=-\infty
for all
\rho>0
, i.e., does not change the sign as
\rho
varies.
Then,
z(\rho)
changes only if
\hat{v}(r(\rho),\rho)
changes the sign.
Suppose there exists
\sigma_{1}>0
such that
for all
\rho>\sigma_{1}
,
\lambda(\rho)\geqslant\lambda^{*}
. Then,
r(\rho)={\lambda^{*}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}
and we have that

\hat{v}(r(\rho))=u({\lambda^{*}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}},\rho)\geqslant 0
for all
\rho>\sigma_{1}
. Likewise, if we assume that there exists

\sigma_{2}>0
such that for all
\rho>\sigma_{2}
,
\lambda(\rho)\leqslant\lambda^{*}
, then
r(\rho)=\lambda(\rho)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}
and

\hat{v}(r(\rho))=-u^{*}({\lambda(\rho)}^{\frac{1}{\theta}},\rho)\leqslant 0
for all
\rho>\sigma_{2}
. In both situations,
i.e.,
\lambda(\rho)\geqslant\lambda^{*}
for all
\rho>\sigma_{1}
or
\lambda(\rho)\leqslant\lambda^{*}
for all
\rho>\sigma_{2}
,
a zero of
\hat{v}
cannot enter
the open interval
(0,r(\rho))
through its right end (however it can reach
r(\rho)
and then turn back) when
\rho>\max\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}
,
which contradicts to Theorem
4.2
stating that
\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}\mathscr{L}_{(0,\tau_{1})}(\hat{v}(\,\cdot\,,\rho))=+\infty
.
∎

5.  Liouville-Bratu-Gelfand problem

In this section, we consider the equation

(78)

\displaystyle L(u)+e^{u}=0.

As before, when we deal with regular solutions, we complete equation (
78
) with the initial conditions

u(0)=\rho
and
u^{\prime}(0)=0
; the regular solution will be also denoted by
u(r,\rho)
.
Furthermore, we let
u^{*}
be the singular solution to (
78
) given by (
69
).

In this section, we aim to complement the results of
[
18
]
,
where the authors studied bifurcation diagrams for problem (
42
),
with a study of
the number of intersection points between the regular and singular solutions to equation (
78
)
and the convergence of regular solutions to the singular. Recall that equations (
42
) and (
78
) are equivalent
by the change of variable (
8
).

5.1  Number of intersection points between the regular and singular solutions

We already studied the number of intersection points between the regular and singular solutions
u(\rho,\,\cdot\,)
and
u^{*}

to (
78
) in Subsection
4.2
. Namely, we obtained Theorem
4.1
whose statement we repeat here
for completeness of the presentation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume (A6).
Suppose
\alpha-\beta-1<4\hat{\theta}

(2k<d<2k+8)
. Then,
\mathscr{L}_{(0,\infty)}(u-u^{*})=+\infty
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume (A6).
Suppose
\alpha-\beta-1\geqslant 4\theta(\beta+1)
(
d\geqslant 2k+8k^{2}
). Then,
\mathscr{L}_{(0,+\infty)}(u-u^{*})=0
.

Proof.

First of all, we note that
\alpha-\beta-1\geqslant 4\theta(\beta+1)\geqslant 4\hat{\theta}
. We keep the same notation as in the proof
of Theorem
4.1
in Subsection
4.2
, except for the notation for the regular and singular solutions. Namely, we define the same objects
with
w=u
and
w^{*}=u^{*}
.
The unique steady state
(0,0)
of (
76
), and therefore of (
75
), is then an unstable node. We further note that
the eigenvectors for the linearized system (
76
) point to the first quadrant of the phase plane.
Therefore, in a small neighborhood of the origin, the integral curves are located at the first and the third quadrants.
As
t
increases, the motion along the curves occurs clockwise.
Note that the solution to (
75
) satisfies the following conditions:

(79)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}x(t)=-\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to+\infty}y(t)=-\theta^{\beta+1}.

From (
75
), we will evaluate the derivative
\frac{dy}{dx}
at the points of the curve

y=\mathfrak{c}(x)=(\ell x+\theta)^{\beta+1}-\theta^{\beta+1}
, which is the same as
(y+\theta^{\beta+1})^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}-\theta=\ell x
,
where the number
\ell
is to be fixed later. Introducing the notation
\delta=\alpha-\beta-1
, we obtain that
in the area
\{x<0
,
-\theta^{\beta+1}<y<0\}
, the following estimate holds:

(80)

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{\mathfrak{c}}=\delta\Big(\frac{y}{(y+\theta^{\beta+1})^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}-\theta}-\frac{\theta^{\beta+1}}{\ell}\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}\Big)>\delta\theta^{\beta}\Big(1-\frac{\theta}{\ell}\Big)\geqslant(\beta+1)\theta^{\beta}\ell,

where the number
\ell
is to be chosen in such a way that the last inequality is satisfied.
To see why the second inequality is true, we note that the function
y\mapsto\frac{y}{(y+\theta^{\beta+1})^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}-\theta}
is non-decreasing on

(-\theta^{\beta+1},0)
(a straightforward computation shows that it has a positive derivative if
\beta\geqslant 0
), and therefore,
on
(-\theta^{\beta+1},0)
, it is greater than
\theta^{\beta}
. Now solving the inequality

\displaystyle\delta\Big(1-\frac{\theta}{\ell}\Big)\geqslant(\beta+1)\ell

with respect to
\ell
, we obtain that under the assumption
\delta\geqslant 4\theta(\beta+1)
, it has at least one solution.
Next, we remark that
\mathfrak{c}(x)=(\ell x+\theta)^{\beta+1}-\theta^{\beta+1}
is a convex increasing function on
[-\frac{\theta}{\ell},0]

whose graph connects the points
(-\frac{\theta}{\ell},-\theta^{\beta+1})
and
(0,0)
.
We further note that the integral curves of (
75
) cannot intersect the curve
\mathfrak{c}

when approaching it from the right. Indeed,
\frac{d\mathfrak{c}}{dx}=(\beta+1)(\ell x+\theta)^{\beta}\ell<(\beta+1)\theta^{\beta}\ell
when
x<0
.
Assuming that such an intersection occurs, by (
80
), we obtain that

\frac{dy}{dx}\big|_{\mathfrak{c}}>\frac{d\mathfrak{c}}{dx}
.
Therefore, the only possibility for (
79
) to hold is when the integral curve, solving (
75
), never leaves the third quadrant.
This means that this curve never intersects the
y
-axis, and therefore
v(t)\neq v^{*}(t)
for all
t
. This implies that
u(\rho,r)\neq u^{*}(r)

for all
r>0
.
∎

5.2  Convergence of regular solutions

Here, we prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Fix an arbitrary interval
[r_{1},r_{2}]\subset(0,+\infty)

and find a sequence
u(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n})
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}=+\infty
,
converging uniformly on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
to a singular solution
u^{**}
to (
78
). Let us prove that
u^{**}=u^{*}
,
where
u^{*}
is the singular solution to (
78
) given by (
69
).

For any
B\geqslant 0
, we define
R^{*}(B)=(u^{*})^{-1}(B)
.
Recall that
R^{*}(0)=(\lambda^{*})^{\frac{1}{\theta}}
.
From
[
18
]
(Theorem 3.1), we know that
R(0,\rho_{n})\to R^{*}(0)
.

Note that
u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n})=u(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n})-B
solves the equation
L(u)+e^{B}e^{u}=0
.
This implies that
\tilde{u}_{B}(r,\rho_{n})=u_{B}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}r,\rho_{n})
satisfies equation (
78
) and
the initial conditions
\tilde{u}_{B}(0,\rho_{n})=\rho_{n}-B
,

\partial_{r}\tilde{u}_{B}(0,\rho_{n})=0
.
Therefore,
u_{B}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}r,\rho_{n})=u(r,\rho_{n}-B)
. Hence,

u_{B}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R(0,\rho_{n}-B),\rho_{n})=0
which is the same as

u(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R(0,\rho_{n}-B),\rho_{n})=B
.
Thus, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle R(B,\rho_{n})=e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R(0,\rho_{n}-B)\to e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R^{*}(0)=R^{*}(B),

where the convergence holds by Theorem 3.1 in
[
18
]
.
To see why
R^{*}(B)=e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R^{*}(0)
, we observe that

u^{*}_{B}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}r)=\lambda^{*}-\theta\ln r=u^{*}(r)
(a verification is straightforward).
This implies that

u^{*}_{B}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R^{*}(0))=0
, which is the same as
u^{*}(e^{-\frac{B}{\theta}}R^{*}(0))=B
.
Hence,

\displaystyle u^{-1}(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n})\to(u^{*})^{-1}\qquad\text{in}\;\;{\rm C}_{loc}(0,+\infty).

For simplicity, we write
u_{n}
for
u(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n})
.
Since
u_{n}
and
u^{**}
are decreasing in
r
and

u_{n}\rightrightarrows u^{**}
on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
, for a small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

[u_{n}(r_{2}),u_{n}(r_{1})]\subset[u^{**}(r_{2})-\varepsilon,u^{**}(r_{1})+\varepsilon]
for
n\geqslant N
. Since
u_{n}^{-1}\rightrightarrows(u^{*})^{-1}

on
[u^{**}(r_{2})-\varepsilon,u^{**}(r_{1})+\varepsilon]
, we obtain that uniformly on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
,

\displaystyle|r-(u^{*})^{-1}(u_{n}(r))|=|u_{n}^{-1}(u_{n}(r))-(u^{*})^{-1}(u_{n}(r))|\to 0\quad\text{as}\;\;n\to\infty.

By continuity of
u^{*}
,

\displaystyle|u^{*}(r)-u_{n}(r)|\to 0\quad\text{as}\;\;n\to\infty

uniformly on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
. Therefore,
u^{*}=u^{**}
.

Let us obtain the first limit in (
10
).
Suppose it is not true. Then, one can find an interval
[r_{1},r_{2}]\subset(0,+\infty)
,
a sequence
\rho_{n}\to+\infty
, and a number
\varepsilon>0
such that

|u(r,\rho_{n})-u^{*}(r)|>\varepsilon
on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
for all
n
. On the other hand, we can find a subsequence
u(\,\cdot\,,\rho_{n_{k}})

which converges to a solution of (
78
) uniformly on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
.
By Corollary
3.1
, this solution is singular. By what was proved, this singular solution is
u^{*}
.
This is a contradiction. Thus, the first limit in (
10
) is obtained.

To show the second equality in (
10
), we note that by Theorem 3.1 from
[
18
]
,

\lambda(\rho)\to\lambda^{*}
as
\rho\to+\infty
. Since
u_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda(\rho)}}(r)=u(\lambda(\rho)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r,\rho)
and
u^{*}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\!*}}(r)=u^{*}({\lambda^{*}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}r)
,
the result follows.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Let assumptions of Theorem
1.7
be fulfilled and let
\lambda^{\#}
be as in Theorem
1.4
.
Then, if
0<\alpha-\beta-1<4\hat{\theta}

(2k<d<2k+8)
,

(u_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\#}},\lambda^{\#})
is a regular solution;
if
\alpha-\beta-1\geqslant 4\hat{\theta}

(d\geqslant 2k+8)

\lambda^{\#}=\lambda^{*}
.

Proof.

If
0<\alpha-\beta-1<4\hat{\theta}
, the statement follows from Theorems
4.3

and
1.7
. If
\alpha-\beta-1\geqslant 4\hat{\theta}
, the statement follows from Theorem 3.1 (case III) in

[
18
]
.
∎
