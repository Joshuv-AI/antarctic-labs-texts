On special quadratic Lyapunov functions for linear dynamical systems with an invariant cone
Omri Dalin, Alexander Ovseevich, Michael Margaliot

Abstract
We consider a continuous-time linear time-invariant dynamical system that admits an invariant cone. For the case of a self-dual and homogeneous cone we show that if the system is asymptotically stable then it admits a quadratic Lyapunov function with a special structure. The complexity of this Lyapuonv function scales linearly with the dimension of the dynamical system. In the particular case when the cone is the nonnegative orthant this reduces to the well-known and important result that a positive system admits a diagonal Lyapunov function. We demonstrate our theoretical results by deriving a new special quadratic Lyapunov function for systems that admit the ice-cream cone as an invariant set.



I
Introduction

A set
C
is called a cone if
x\in C
implies that
\alpha x\in C
for all
\alpha>0
.
Given a
closed and convex cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
let
M(C)
denote the set of matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
C
is an invariant set
of the linear system

\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t).

(1)

In other words,

x(0)\in C\implies x(t)\in C\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

(2)

The set
M(C)
is sometimes referred to as the set of matrices that are exponentially nonnegative on
C
(see, e.g.,
[
20
]
).

For example, consider the special case where
C
is
the cone

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,x_{i}\geq 0,\ i=1,\dots,n\},

that is, the nonnegative orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
) that
M(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
is the set of Metzler matrices, that is, the set of matrices with nonnegative off-diagonal terms.
The system (
1
), with
A
Metzler, is called a positive system
[
8
]
and has found many applications, as in many real-world models the state-variables represent quantities that cannot take negative values (e.g., concentrations, probabilities, densities, sizes of populations), and thus the dynamics must map
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
to itself.

Recall that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called a
Z
-matrix if
-A
is Metzler, that is, if
-A\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
. Some authors refer to the property
-A\in M(C)
as the
Z
-property of
A
with respect to
C

[
10
]
.

The problem of characterizing
M(C)

is an important question in
linear algebra and dynamical systems theory.
Indeed, the existence of invariant sets has strong implications on the behaviour of dynamical systems and also for control synthesis
[
3
]
. Moreover, the results for linear systems play an important role also in the analysis of nonlinear systems.
For example, the variational equation associated with the nonlinear system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))
is

\dot{z}(t)=J(x(t))z(t),

(3)

where
J(x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)
is the Jacobian of the vector field
f
. Note that (
3
) is a linear system, and if this system admits an invariant set then this has strong implications on the behaviour of the nonlinear system. The most important case is
when the nonnegative orthant is an invariant set of (
3
), and then the nonlinear system is cooperative
[
19
]
. These systems, and their generalization to control systems
[
1
]
, have many special properties.

A matrix
A\in M(C)
has special properties. For example,
cone preserving
operators have special spectral properties
[
2
, Chapter 11]
. Here, we are interested in the case where
A\in M(C)
and
A
is Hurwitz. It is well-known that then
A
admits special stability properties (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). In particular, if
A\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
and
A
is Hurwitz then it a admits a
positive diagonal Lyapunov function, that is, there exists a positive diagonal matrix
D
such that
DA+A^{T}D
is negative-definite. This property and its extensions have found many applications in systems theory, see, e.g.,
[
23
,
14
]
. One reason for this is that the number of parameters in a diagonal matrix scales
linearly
with the dimension of the system.
Our goal here is to show that if
A\in M(C)
, where
C
is a symmetric
cone, but not necessarily
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, and
A
is Hurwitz then it admits a quadratic Lyapunov function that
scales linearly with the system dimension.

Since the solution of (
2
) is
x(t)=\exp(At)x(0)
,
it is natural to address the problem of characterizing special Lyapunov functions using Lie-groups and Lie-algebras. Here we apply such an approach to
derive an explicit special quadratic Lyapunov function for Hurwitz matrices
A\in M(C)
, where
C
is a symmetric cone. The complexity of this Lyapunov function scales linearly with the dimension of the system.
In the special case where
C=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, this reduces to a diagonal Lyapunov function.
We demonstrate our theoretical results by deriving a new quadratic Lyapunov function for Hurwitz matrices
A\in M(K_{n})
, where
K_{n}

is the ice-cream cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The remainder of this note is organized as follows. The next section reviews several known definitions and results that are used later on. Section
III
describes our theoretical results. These are demonstrated by deriving a new special quadratic Lyapunov function for a Hurwitz matrix that is exponentially nonnegative on the ice-cream cone.

We use standard notation.
Vectors [matrices] are denoted by small [capital] letters.
The canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
e^{1},\dots,e^{n}
.
The transpose of a matrix

is denoted by

. A matrix

) if it is symmetric and

) if

.
For a square matrix

, and

is the determinant of
A
.
For two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, let
\langle A,B\rangle:=\operatorname{{\mathrm{T}r}}(AB)
. The interior [closure] of a set

is denoted by
\operatorname{int}(S)

\operatorname{clos}(S)

Given a set of matrices

write

\cone(S)=\{\alpha_{1}A_{1}+\alpha_{2}A_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{k}A_{k}\,|\,k\geq 1,A_{i}\in S,\,\alpha_{i}\geq 0\}

The Minkowski sum

The ice-cream cone

1

(4)

II-B

Automorphisms and endomorphisms of a cone

Given a cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a linear map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(that can be identified with a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
), let

\phi(C):=\{\phi(x)\,|\,x\in C\}.

A cone
C
defines two algebraic objects.

Definition 1
.

The group of automorphisms of
C
, denoted

\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
, is the set of all linear maps (i.e., matrices)
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\phi(C)=C
. Let
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(C)
denote the connected component of the unit element of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
.

Definition 2
.

The semigroup of endomorphisms of
C
, denoted
\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(C)
, is the set of all linear maps
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\phi(C)\subseteq C
.

More generally, given two cones
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, let
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits(C_{1},C_{2})
denote the set of linear maps
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\phi(C_{1})\subseteq C_{2}
.

It is well-known that the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
is a closed subgroup in the group of invertible matrices, and therefore, by Cartan’s theorem
[
18
]
it is a Lie group.

The semigroup
\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(C)
is a manifold with corners.
Let
H
denote either
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
or
\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(C)
.
Then a tangent cone
T_{h}(H)
is defined at any point
h
of any of these two objects
H
. By definition,
T_{h}(H)
is the set of tangent vectors:
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\gamma(t)
to smooth curves
\gamma:[0,\varepsilon)\to H
, with
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma(0)=h
.

In the case of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
the tangent cone
T_{h}(\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C))
is just the tangent space at
h
,
and for the particular case
h=I_{n}
it is the Lie algebra:

\mathop{\rm Lie}\nolimits\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C):=T_{I_{n}}(\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C))
.

Example 1
.

For the case where
C
is the
nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, it is straightforward to verify that
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
consists of matrices of the form
\Sigma D
, where
\Sigma
is a permutation matrix, and
D\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, with

\mathcal{D}_{+}:=\{\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\,|\,d_{i}>0\}.

In particular, the connected component
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
of
I_{n}
in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
is
\mathcal{D}_{+}
.
Also,
\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
is the semigroup (under multiplication) of matrices with nonnegative entries.

Example 2
.

For the case where
C
is the ice-cream cone
K_{n}
,
it follows from
[
20
, Theorem 3.31]
that
if
H=\exp(\alpha I_{n}+B)
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&b\\
b^{T}&0\end{bmatrix},

B_{1}
an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
skew-symmetric matrix, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

then
H\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(K_{n})
. Moreover, any
H\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{0}(K_{n})
that is sufficiently close to
I_{n}

admits this exponential form. In particular, the Lie algebra
\mathop{\rm Lie}\nolimits\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(K_{n})
consists of matrices of the form
\alpha I_{n}+B
.

II-C

Duality theory

The dual cone of
C
(see, e.g.,
[
15
]
) is

C^{\vee}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,y^{T}x\geq 0\mbox{ for all }x\in C\}.

In particular,

(C^{\vee})^{\vee}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,z^{T}y\geq 0\mbox{ for all }y\in C^{\vee}\}.

(5)

The cone
C
is called self-dual if
C^{\vee}=C
.
For example,
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
K_{n}
are self-dual.

The duality theorem
[
15
]
asserts that if
C
is closed and convex then

(C^{\vee})^{\vee}=C.

(6)

Remark 1
.

Eq. (
6
) is useful in analyzing the sets
\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(C)
,
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
, and
M(C)
. To demonstrate this, assume that

and that
(Ax)^{T}\xi<0

\xi\in C^{\vee}
.
Then (
5
) and (

) imply that
Ax\not\in(C^{\vee})^{\vee}=C
,
so we conclude that
A\not\in\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(C)
.

Let

be a closed convex cone.
Recall that a matrix
A
is called

cross-positive on
C

[
17
]
if for any
x\in C
and
\xi\in C^{\vee}
satisfying

, we have that
(Ax)^{T}\xi\geq 0
. Note that if
A
is cross-positive on

then
A^{T}
is
cross-positive on

.

Theorem 1
.

(see, e.g.
[

, Thm. 3]
)
Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

Then

is the set of matrices that are cross-positive on

.

Remark 2
.

Note that this implies in particular that the set of matrices

is a closed convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

Remark 3

Let

Consider

Then the condition in Theorem

The next result provides a Lie-algebraic characterization of

For a set

,

Example 3

Consider the case

III
Main Results

This section includes our main results. These results generalise special stability properties of matrices that are Hurwitz and in
M(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
to matrices that are Hurwitz and in
M(C)
.

III-A

Invariance of stability under automorphisms of the cone

It is well-known that
if
A
is Metzler and Hurwitz and
D
is a positive diagonal matrix then both

AD
and
DA
are also Metzler and Hurwitz.
This D-stability property originated in the analysis of economic models, and has found many applications (see, e.g., the survey paper
[
12
]
).
The next result generalizes this property.

Proposition 2
.

Let
C
be a proper cone.
Suppose that
A\in M(C)
is Hurwitz, and

D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
.
If

D_{1}AD_{2}\in M(C)

(8)

then

D_{1}AD_{2}\text{ is Hurwitz}.

Proof.

Since
A
is Hurwitz, Proposition
1

implies that
-A^{-1}
maps
C
to
C
.
Since
D_{i}^{-1}
are automorphisms of
C
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle-(D_{1}AD_{2})^{-1}=D_{2}^{-1}(-A^{-1})D_{1}^{-1}

also maps
C
to
C
. Combining this with (
8
) and Proposition
1
implies that
D_{1}AD_{2}
is Hurwitz.
∎

Remark 5
.

In the special case where
D_{2}=D_{1}^{-1}
, it is immediate that

D_{1}AD_{1}^{-1}\in M(C)\text{ and }D_{1}AD_{1}^{-1}\text{ is Hurwitz},

as

\exp(D_{1}AD_{1}^{-1}t)=D_{1}\exp(At)D_{1}^{-1}
.

Remark 6
.

For the case where
C=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,
A\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
implies that
A
is Metzler, and
D_{i}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
implies that
D_{i}
is a positive diagonal matrix, and then
D_{1}AD_{2}
is also Metzler, so condition (
8
) automatically holds. However, for other cones it is not immediately clear when (
8
) indeed holds.

Example 4
.

Consider the case
C=K_{3}
.
The matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}-\epsilon_{1}&-1&0\\
1&-\epsilon_{1}&0\\
0&0&-\epsilon_{2}\end{bmatrix},\text{ with }0<\epsilon_{2}\leq\epsilon_{1},

satisfies

, because

(see

]
),
and

is Hurwitz as

is a negative diagonal matrix. The matrix

-b&0&0\\

Consider the matrix

This matrix belongs to

that is, iff there exists

III-B

Quadratic Lyapunov functions with a special structure

It is well-known that if a matrix
A
is Metzler and Hurwitz
then it admits a quadratic
diagonal
Lyapunov function, that is, there exists a positive diagonal matrix
D
such that
A^{T}D+DA\prec 0
.

To generalize this property to matrices
A\in M(C)
that are Hurwitz, we need to impose more structure on the cone
C
.

Definition 4
.

A cone
C
is called
homogeneous

if
\operatorname{int}(C)
is a homogeneous space under the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
. In other words, for any two points
x,y\in\operatorname{int}(C)
there exists
L\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
such that
Lx=y
.

Definition 5
.

A cone that is self-dual and homogeneous is called a symmetric cone
[
7
]
.

Symmetric cones are sometimes called domains of positivity
[
16
]
.

Example 5
.

The cone
C=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
is homogeneous since

\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,x_{i}>0\}
is homogeneous under the natural action of the group of positive diagonal matrices. Indeed, for any
x,y\in\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
we have
that
\operatorname{diag}(y_{1}/x_{1},\dots,y_{n}/x_{n})x=y
. Since
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
is also self-dual, it is a symmetric cone.
The ice-cream cone
K_{n}
is also homogeneous (see
[
7
, Chapter 1]
) and self-dual, so it is symmetric.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
C
is a proper, homogeneous, and self-dual cone. Let
A\in M(C)
be Hurwitz. Then there exists a symmetric matrix
P
such that
P:C\to C^{\vee}=C
is an automorphism of
C
, and the quadratic form
x^{T}Px
is a Lyapunov function for the dynamical system (
1
).

Proof.

The quadratic Lyapunov function
depends on some properties of the characteristic function associated with the cone
C
, denoted
\varphi:C\to[0,\infty]
. Recall that

\varphi(x):=\int_{C^{\vee}}\exp(-x^{T}y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

(11)

For any
G\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(C)
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(Gx)

\displaystyle=\int_{C^{\vee}}\exp(-x^{T}G^{T}y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=|\det(G^{-T})|\int_{C^{\vee}}\exp(-x^{T}z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=|\det(G^{-1})|\varphi(x),

(12)

where
G^{-T}:=(G^{-1})^{T}
.
In particular, for

The proof of Theorem

Claim 1

Let

Proof.

By definition, the dual cone of the closed and convex cone

(B+C)^{\vee}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,y^{T}(b+c)\geq 0\text{ for all }b\in B,\,c\in C\}.

It is straightforward to verify that this implies that

(19)

Now suppose that

(B+C)^{\vee}\subseteq A^{\vee}

To prove the converse implication, assume that
B^{\vee}\cap C^{\vee}\subseteq A^{\vee}
.
Then
(B+C)^{\vee}\subseteq A^{\vee}
, so

, and using duality gives

.
∎
