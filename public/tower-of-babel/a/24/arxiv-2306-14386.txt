On the embedding of Galois groups into wreath products
Adrian Barquero-Sanchez and Jimmy Calvo-Monge

Abstract
. In this paper we make explicit an application of the wreath product construction to the Galois groups of field extensions. More precisely, given a tower of fields F ⊆ K ⊆ L F\subseteq K\subseteq L with L / F L/F finite and separable, we explicitly construct an embedding of the Galois group Gal ⁡ ( L c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F) into the regular wreath product Gal ⁡ ( L c / K c ) ≀ r Gal ⁡ ( K c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F) . Here L c L^{c} (resp. K c K^{c} ) denotes the Galois closure of L / F L/F (resp. K / F K/F ). Similarly, we also construct an explicit embedding of the Galois group Gal ⁡ ( L c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F) into the smaller sized wreath product Gal ⁡ ( L c / K ) ≀ Ω Gal ⁡ ( K c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F) , where Ω = Hom F ⁡ ( K , K c ) \Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c}) is acted on by composition of automorphisms in Gal ⁡ ( K c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F) . Moreover, when L / K L/K is a Kummer extension we prove a sharper embedding, that is, that Gal ⁡ ( L c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F) embeds into the wreath product Gal ⁡ ( L / K ) ≀ Ω Gal ⁡ ( K c / F ) \operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F) . As corollaries we obtain embedding theorems when L / K L/K is cyclic and when it is quadratic with char ⁡ ( F ) ≠ 2 \operatorname{char}(F)\neq 2 . We also provide examples of these embeddings and as an illustration of the usefulness of these embedding theorems, we survey some recent applications of these types of results in field theory, arithmetic statistics, number theory and arithmetic geometry.

1.
Introduction

Wreath products of groups constitute an essential construction in group theory and in different applications outside of pure group theory, with recent applications even in areas as unexpected as music theory! (see e.g.
[
Hoo02
]
,
[
Pec09
]
and the very nice blog post
[
Yis20
]
). These groups appeared first in the context of permutation groups during the first half of the 20th century, where now they are of fundamental importance (see e.g.
[
DM96
]
). They also appear naturally for example in the study of the extension problem for groups, where, along with direct products, they are the most common examples of group extensions.

In Galois Theory, wreath products have also appeared in different contexts. One instance where they are used prominently is in the problem of realizing certain groups as Galois groups in connection with the inverse Galois problem (see e.g.
[
Völ96
, Chapter 8]
,
[
MM18
, Section IV.2]
and
[
Gow86
]
). They have also appeared recently in the study of certain problems in enumerative geometry (see e.g.
[
EL22
]
).

Nevertheless, as J. D. P. Meldrum wrote in the preface of his book
[
Mel95
]
,
“…in almost all cases where wreath products are used, in book or paper, the author has to develop the theory himself, using material from a variety of sources.”
. This was written in 1995, but it still remains partially valid almost thirty years later, for even though there are now several specialized books on group theory that treat wreath products to a certain extent, these groups are rarely even mentioned in the usual mathematics curriculum, even at the graduate level.

In particular, in this paper we prove several theorems giving different
explicit
embeddings of Galois groups of separable extensions of fields into wreath products of certain associated field extensions. This type of results have important applications in different areas of mathematics. For example, in arithmetic statistics they can be used to study the distribution of number fields in the context of the Malle conjectures (see e.g.
[
Mal02
]
,
[
Klü12
]
and
[
BSMT23
]
); in arithmetic geometry they have been used in defining the notion of
Weyl CM points
on the moduli space
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
of principally polarized abelian varieties (see e.g.
[
CO12
]
) and in studying the Colmez conjecture from a probabilistic perspective, which even yielded a conditional proof that the conjecture is true with probability 1 (see e.g.
[
BSM18
, §1.2]
and
[
BSMT23
]
); in field theory they have been used in characterizing the solvability by radicals of imprimitive polynomials (see e.g.
[
Cox12
, §14.2]
). These applications are surveyed in more detail in Section
2
of the paper.

Some of the results of this paper are known to some experts in certain areas, possibly in a somewhat different form than the one given here. Some have even appeared in special cases in papers, books or in expository notes, but unfortunately there hasn’t been a more complete and unified treatment of these embedding results as far as we know. Thus, we have strived to give general results under a unified approach, making clear use of the different versions of the wreath products involved and highlighting important similarities and differences between the results. We hope that this will help remedy the situation and contribute as a more standard reference, since, as was mentioned in the previous paragraph, this type of results have found important uses in different areas.

1.1.
Statement of main results

We now proceed to state the main theorems of the paper. The first two theorems (Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
) are of a general nature and apply to towers of fields
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
with
L/F
finite and separable. Then, in Theorem
1.11
and its corollaries (Corollaries
1.13
and
1.14
), we put some conditions on the top extension
L/K
in the tower, which allow us to get sharper embeddings, as will be explained below. The basic background and terminology on wreath products, as it is used in the statements of the theorems below, is given in Section
3
of the paper. Thus, our first theorem is the following. It gives an embedding of a Galois group into a regular wreath product, which, according to
[
Mel95
, p. 5]
, is the most commonly used wreath product.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
be a tower of field extensions with
L/F
finite and separable. Denote the Galois closures of
K/F
and
L/F
by
K^{c}
and
L^{c}
, respectively. Then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
embeds into the regular wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. More precisely, consider the short exact sequence from Galois theory

1\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\overset{\iota}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\overset{\varepsilon}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)\longrightarrow 1,

where
\iota
is the inclusion and
\varepsilon
is the restriction map, i.e., if
\rho\in\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
, then
\varepsilon(\rho)=\rho|_{K^{c}}
.
Let
s\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
be a right inverse of
\varepsilon
, i.e., suppose that
\varepsilon\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)}
. Consider the map

(1.1)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho))

with
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})
defined by

\sigma_{\rho}(\tau):=s(\tau)^{-1}\circ\rho\circ s\big(\varepsilon(\rho)^{-1}\circ\tau\big)

for every
\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. Then the map
\varphi
is an injective group homomorphism.

Remark 1.2
.

As will be explained in Section
5
, this is an application of a very important embedding theorem of Kaloujnine and Krasner
[
KK51b
]
from 1951.

Remark 1.3
.

Observe that the embedding (
1.1
) is essentially breaking up an automorphism
\rho\in\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
into two pieces of information with respect to the tower of fields
F\subseteq K^{c}\subseteq L^{c}
, as Figure
1
shows. More precisely, the restriction
\varepsilon(\rho)
of
\rho
to
K^{c}
gives an automorphism in the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
, and thus
\varepsilon(\rho)
retains all the information about
\rho
relative to the bottom extension
K^{c}/F
in Figure
1
. On the other hand, the function
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})
retains all the information about
\rho
relative to the top extension
L^{c}/K^{c}
.

A different embedding is given in the following theorem. In particular, this result gives an embedding into a wreath product which in general will have much smaller size than the regular wreath product appearing in Theorem
1.1
, as will be clarified in Remarks
1.6
and
1.7
below.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider a tower of fields
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
with
L/F
finite and separable. Let
L^{c}
be the Galois closure of
L/F
and
K^{c}
denote the Galois closure of
K/F
. Let
\Omega:=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
, and let
s:\Omega\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
be a map that extends every
F
-embedding
\omega\in\Omega
to an automorphism
s(\omega)\in\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
. Define the map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho)),

where
\sigma_{\rho}:\Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rho}(\omega)=s(\omega)^{-1}\circ\rho\circ s(\varepsilon(\rho)^{-1}\circ\omega)

and
\varepsilon
is the the restriction from
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
to
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. Then, the map
\varphi
is an injective group homomorphism, embedding the group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
into the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
.

Remark 1.5
.

We note that Theorem
1.4
gives an explicit version of a result that was sketched by B. de Smit in an unpublished note
[
dS07
]
, which also appears in the master’s thesis of his student M. Pintonello
[
Pin18
, Theorem 1.1.2]
. In both places, the result is obtained in the language of permutation groups and no explicit embedding is given. Here, we have avoided the more cumbersome language of permutation groups and moreover we give a direct and simpler proof of the result from the explicit form of the embedding that we obtained.

Remark 1.6
.

Observe that when
K/F
is a Galois extension we have
K^{c}=K
. In particular, this implies that
\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})=\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
and
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)=\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})
. Thus, this means that in this situation the two wreath products appearing in Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
are actually the same group and furthermore the embeddings coincide.

Remark 1.7
.

Let
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
be a tower of fields with
L/F
finite and separable, as in the statement of Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
. Both of those two theorems offer embeddings of
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
into wreath products of associated Galois groups. Nevertheless, there is an important difference in the corresponding sizes of the wreath products involved. To explain this, referring ahead to Remark
3.4
, we know that the size of a wreath product
K\wr_{\Omega}H
is given by
|K\wr_{\Omega}H|=|K|^{|\Omega|}\cdot|H|
. Hence, for the wreath product in Theorem
1.1
we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})|^{|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|}|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{[L^{c}:F]}{[K^{c}:F]}\right)^{[K^{c}:F]}[K^{c}:F]

and similarly for the wreath product appearing in Theorem
1.4
we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)|^{|\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})|}|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{[L^{c}:F]}{[K:F]}\right)^{[K:F]}[K^{c}:F].

As these two formulas show, the two wreath products have the same size (and in fact are actually the same group as was observed in Remark
1.6
) when
K^{c}=K
, i.e., when
K/F
is a Galois extension. Moreover, observe that by the tower law for field extensions we always have that
[K^{c}:F]
divides
[L^{c}:F]
.

Now, in Table
1
in Subsection
1.4
we use the identities (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) to list formulas for the corresponding sizes for the different possibilities for
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
when
2\leq[K:F]\leq 5
. The formulas are given as functions of
m:=|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)|=[L^{c}:F]
. Moreover, since
[K^{c}:F]
divides
[L^{c}:F]
, each fraction appearing in the table is actually an integer. In particular, the reader should note that the exponent of
m
is in general larger for the regular wreath product
|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|
than for the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. This implies that, as functions of
m
, as
m
gets bigger, the size of
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
will be a lot larger than the size of
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. Therefore, in this respect, Theorem
1.4
can be seen as giving a sharper embedding than Theorem
1.1
, since one will in general be embedding the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
into a wreath product of much smaller size in Theorem
1.4
.

Moreover, experimentally we have observed that when
K/F
is non-Galois and when
m=[L^{c}:F]\geq 3[K^{c}:F]
, the regular wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K^{c})\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
is actually strictly bigger than the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
, and when
m=2[K^{c}:F]
they have the same size if and only if
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)\simeq D_{n}
is dihedral. This can be seen in Figures
2
,
3
and
4
at the end of Subsection
1.4
.

1.2.
Sharper embedding theorems for Kummer, cyclic and quadratic extensions

It turns out that one can get a sharper embedding when in the tower of fields
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
we have
L/K
quadratic. For example, C-L. Chai and F. Oort proved the following (see
[
CO12
, Lemma 2.8]
).

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
and let
L
be a quadratic extension of
K
. Denote by
L^{c}
the Galois closure of
L/\mathbb{Q}
. Then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}S_{n}
, where
\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and the action of
S_{n}
on
C_{2}^{\Omega}=C_{2}^{n}
is by permuting the components as in Remark
3.6
.

Moreover, under the same hypotheses as in the previous theorem, one can get an even sharper embedding. This was done by the first author, R. Masri and F. Thorne in
[
BSMT23
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
and let
L
be a quadratic extension of
K
. Denote by
L^{c}
the Galois closure of
L/\mathbb{Q}
and let
G
be a transitive subgroup of
S_{n}
with
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq G
. Then
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}G
, where
\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and the action of
G
on
C_{2}^{\Omega}=C_{2}^{n}
is by permuting the components as in Remark
3.6
.

We will now give a very broad generalization of Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
. More precisely, we will prove an embedding theorem for towers of fields
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
with
L/K
a Kummer extension. We describe this now.

First, recall that if
K
is a field, an element
\omega\in K
with
\omega^{n}=1
is called an
n
-th root of unity
. Moreover, if the order of
\omega
in the multiplicative group
K^{\times}
is
n
, then we say that
\omega
is a
primitive
n
-th root of unity
. In particular, if
\omega\in K
is a primitive
n
-th root of unity, then
\operatorname{char}(K)
does not divide
n
(see e.g.
[
Mor96
, §7]
). An extension
L/K
is called an
n
-Kummer extension
if
L/K
is Galois and
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is abelian with exponent dividing
n
. We use the following theorem characterizing
n
-Kummer extensions with roots of unity. It is adapted from
[
Mor96
, Theorem 11.4]
and its proof.

Theorem 1.10

(Characterization of Kummer extensions)
.

Let
K
be a field containing a primitive
n
-th root of unity and let
L
be a finite extension of
K
. Then
L/K
is an
n
-Kummer extension if and only if
L=K(\sqrt[n_{1}]{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\sqrt[n_{r}]{\alpha_{r}})
for some
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in K
with
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}
positive integers dividing
n
. Moreover, if
L/K
is an
n
-Kummer extension, then by the Fundamental Theorem of Finite Abelian Groups, the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\langle\eta_{1}\rangle\cdots\langle\eta_{r}\rangle
is a product of cyclic groups and if the order of
\eta_{i}
is
n_{i}
, for each
i=1,\dots,r
, then the fixed field
L_{i}
of the subgroup
H_{i}=\langle\eta_{1}\rangle\cdots\langle\eta_{i-1}\rangle\langle\eta_{i+1}\rangle\cdots\langle\eta_{r}\rangle
is a cyclic extension of
K
with
\operatorname{Gal}(L_{i}/K)=\langle\eta_{i}\rangle
and there exists an element
\alpha_{i}\in K
such that
L_{i}=F(\sqrt[n_{i}]{\alpha_{i}})
. Then
L=K(\sqrt[n_{1}]{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\sqrt[n_{r}]{\alpha_{r}})
.

Then, we have the following embedding for towers
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
where
L/K
is an
n
-Kummer extension.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
be a tower of fields with
L/F
finite and separable. Moreover, suppose that
K
contains a primitive
n
-th root of unity
\omega
and that
L/K
is an
n
-Kummer extension. As in Theorem
1.10
, write
L=K(\sqrt[n_{1}]{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\sqrt[n_{r}]{\alpha_{r}})
for some
\alpha_{i}\in K
and some positive integers
n_{i}
dividing
n
, and put
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\langle\eta_{1}\rangle\cdots\langle\eta_{r}\rangle
, where the order of
\eta_{i}
equals
n_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,r\}
. Denote the Galois closures of
K/F
and
L/F
by
K^{c}
and
L^{c}
, respectively and let
\Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
be the set of
F
-homomorphisms
K\hookrightarrow K^{c}
. The group
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
acts on
\Omega
on the left by composition of maps. Then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
embeds into the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. More precisely, consider the map

(1.4)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho))

where
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is defined for every embedding
\tau\in\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
by

\sigma_{\rho}(\tau)=\eta_{1}^{\chi(\rho,\tau,\alpha_{1})}\circ\cdots\circ\eta_{r}^{\chi(\rho,\tau,\alpha_{r})},

with
\chi(\rho,\tau,\alpha_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}/n_{j}\mathbb{Z}
chosen to satisfy

\tau(\omega_{j})^{\chi(\rho,\tau,\alpha_{j})}=\frac{\rho\left(\sqrt[n_{j}]{\rho^{-1}(\tau(\alpha_{j}))}\right)}{\sqrt[n_{j}]{\tau(\alpha_{j})}},

where
\omega_{j}=\omega^{n/n_{j}}
for
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,r\}
. Then the map
\varphi
is an injective group homomorphism.

Remark 1.12
.

Theorem
1.11
indeed gives a sharper embedding than the one from Theorem
1.4
. In fact, as we showed in Remark
1.7
, the size of the wreath product in Theorem
1.4
is equal to
|\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)|^{[K:F]}|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|
, while the size of the wreath product in Theorem
1.11
is given by
|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|^{[K:F]}|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)|
, which will be much smaller whenever
L/F
is a non-Galois extension. In particular, in Example
8.2
we will give an instance of a 6-Kummer extension
L/K
of degree 72 for which the latter wreath product is about 40 million times smaller than the first one!

Now, recall that a field extension
L/K
is said to be
cyclic
if it is Galois and
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is cyclic. Then, since a cyclic extension of degree
n
is an
n
-Kummer extension, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
be a tower of fields with
L/F
finite and separable. Moreover, suppose that
K
contains a primitive
n
-th root of unity
\omega
, that
L/K
is a cyclic extension of degree
n
and write
L=K(\sqrt[n]{\alpha})
for some
\alpha\in K
. Denote the Galois closures of
K/F
and
L/F
by
K^{c}
and
L^{c}
, respectively, let
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\langle\eta\rangle=\{\operatorname{id}_{L},\eta,\dots,\eta^{n-1}\}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, and let
\Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
be the set of
F
-homomorphisms
K\hookrightarrow K^{c}
. The group
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
acts on
\Omega
on the left by composition of maps. Then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
embeds into the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. More precisely, consider the map

(1.5)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho))

where
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is defined for every embedding
\tau\in\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
by

\sigma_{\rho}(\tau)=\eta^{\chi(\rho,\tau)},

with
\chi(\rho,\tau)\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
chosen to satisfy

\tau(\omega)^{\chi(\rho,\tau)}=\frac{\rho\left(\sqrt[n]{\rho^{-1}(\tau(\alpha))}\right)}{\sqrt[n]{\tau(\alpha)}}.

Then the map
\varphi
is an injective group homomorphism.

In particular, an important special case of the previous corollary is the following.

Corollary 1.14
.

Let
F\subseteq K\subseteq L
be a tower of fields with
L/F
finite and separable and
\operatorname{char}(F)\neq 2
. Moreover, suppose that
L/K
is a quadratic extension and write
L=K(\sqrt{\alpha})
for some
\alpha\in K\smallsetminus K^{2}
. Denote the Galois closures of
K/F
and
L/F
by
K^{c}
and
L^{c}
, respectively, let
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\{\operatorname{id}_{L},\eta\}
, and let
\Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
be the set of
F
-homomorphisms
K\hookrightarrow K^{c}
. The group
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
acts on
\Omega
on the left by composition of maps. Then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)
embeds into the wreath product
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)
. More precisely, consider the map

(1.6)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho))

where
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is defined for every embedding
\tau\in\operatorname{Hom}_{F}(K,K^{c})
by

\sigma_{\rho}(\tau)=\eta^{\chi(\rho,\tau)},

with
\chi(\rho,\tau)\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
chosen to satisfy

(-1)^{\chi(\rho,\tau)}=\frac{\rho\left(\sqrt{\rho^{-1}(\tau(\alpha))}\right)}{\sqrt{\tau(\alpha)}}\in\{\pm 1\}.

Then the map
\varphi
is an injective group homomorphism.

Remark 1.15
.

We note that Corollary
1.14
gives a sharper version of
[
CO12
, Lemma 2.8]
(see Theorem
1.8
) and a more general version of
[
BSMT23
, Proposition 3.1]
(see Theorem
1.9
). In particular, as we will show in Section
4
, we can use Corollary
1.14
in combination with the technical results for identifying wreath products with isomorphic components from that section, to deduce these two theorems.

2.
Some applications of the embedding of Galois groups into wreath products

In this section we illustrate with some explicit examples, instances where embedding theorems for Galois groups into wreath products like the ones we prove in the paper, and also some instances for Galois groups that are equal to certain wreath products, have been used in recent applications in field theory, arithmetic statistics, number theory and arithmetic geometry.

The background required for each application increases as we move from one to the next. We have made an effort to give enough details for the reader to get a reasonable, if superficial, understanding of each application, and we have given several references so that the interested reader can read further on each one. Nevertheless, naturally we won’t be able to define every term, so the reader who doesn’t already know some of the concepts that are left undefined in the applications, will have to take them as black boxes and consult the references for the definitions and details.

2.1.
Application to the solvability by radicals of imprimitive polynomials

For the details of the material described in this subsection the reader is referred to
[
Cox12
, §6.4 and §14.2]
.

Let
p
be a prime and consider the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. For
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, let
\gamma_{a,b}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the affine linear transformation defined by
\gamma_{a,b}(t)=at+b
for every
t\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Such an affine linear transformation is bijective if and only if
a\neq 0
. Then, the
one dimensional affine linear group over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is the group defined by

\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p}):=\{\gamma_{a,b}\;|\;(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}\},

with group operation given by composition of functions. This group has order
p(p-1)
and moreover
\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\simeq\mathbb{F}_{p}\rtimes_{\theta}\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, where
\theta\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is given, for every
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, by
\theta_{c}(t)=ct
for every
t\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. An explicit isomorphism is given by
\psi\colon\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}\rtimes_{\theta}\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, where
\psi(\gamma_{a,b})=(b,a)
for every
\gamma_{a,b}\in\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

Now, let
G
be a subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{n}
. Then
G
is said to be
imprimitive
if there exists a partition

\{1,\dots,n\}=R_{1}\cup\cdots\cup R_{k}

with
k>1
and
|R_{i}|>1
for at least one
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, such that for every
\tau\in G
and every
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
we have
\tau(R_{i})=R_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Moreover, if no such partition exists, then
G
is said to be
primitive
.

In particular, this terminology can also be used for polynomials. More precisely, if
f\in F[x]
is a separable polynomial over a field
F
, then
f
is said to be
imprimitive
if its Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(f)
over
F
, thought as a permutation group on the roots of
f
, is imprimitive. Similarly, if its Galois group is not imprimitive, the polynomial
f
is said to be
primitive
.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 4
be composite and let
n=\ell k
be a nontrivial factorization. Then, the wreath product
S_{\ell}\wr_{\Omega}S_{k}
, where
\Omega:=\{1,\dots,k\}
and
S_{k}
acts on
\Omega
as in Remark
3.6
, is an example of an imprimitive subgroup of
S_{n}
. For this, see e.g.
[
Cox12
, §14.2 B]
.

Then, the solvability by radicals of an imprimitive polynomial of degree
p^{2}
is characterized as follows (see e.g.
[
Cox12
, Corollary 14.2.16]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic 0, let
f(x)\in F[x]
be an irreducible imprimitive polynomial of degree
p^{2}
, and let
L
be a splitting field for
f
over F. Then
f
is solvable by radicals over
F
if and only if the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
of
f
over
F
embeds into the wreath product
\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
, where
\Omega:=\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
acts on
\Omega
by evaluation, i.e., for every
t\in\Omega
and every
\gamma_{a,b}\in\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
we have
\gamma_{a,b}\cdot t:=\gamma_{a,b}(t)=at+b
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the polynomial
f(x)=x^{9}-3x^{8}+2x^{7}+2x^{4}-2x^{2}-1\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. This corresponds to the entry
[
LMF23
,
Number field 9.1.74839217.1
]
in the
LMFDB
database. Then
f
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
and its Galois group over
\mathbb{Q}
is
\operatorname{Gal}(f)\simeq S_{3}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}S_{3}
, where
\widehat{\Omega}:=\{1,2,3\}
is an
S_{3}
-set with the natural action by permutations. Hence, by Remark
2.1
, the polynomial
f
is imprimitive. Note that
|\operatorname{Gal}(f)|=|S_{3}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}S_{3}|=|S_{3}|^{\widehat{\Omega}}\cdot|S_{3}|=6^{3}\cdot 6=1296
. Moreover, we observe that
\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{3})
is a non-abelian group of order
6
, and hence
\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{3})\simeq S_{3}
. Therefore, by Proposition
4.1
, we have
\operatorname{Gal}(f)\simeq\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{3})\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{3})
with
\Omega:=\mathbb{F}_{3}
. Then, by Theorem
2.2
, we conclude that the polynomial
f
is solvable by radicals.

2.2.
Application to the distribution of number fields and the Malle Conjectures

In 2002 and 2004, G. Malle
[
Mal02
,
Mal04
]
made two very precise conjectures regarding the density of number fields with a given Galois group. Specifically, for a number field
K
, a finite transitive permutation group
G\leq S_{n}
and a positive real number
x
, he defined the counting function

N(K,G;x)=|\{L/K\;|\;\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\simeq G\text{ and }\left|\mathcal{N}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(d(L/K))\right|\leq x\}|,

which counts the number of field extensions
L
of
K
of degree
n
, whose Galois closure
L^{c}
has Galois group over
K
isomorphic to the given group
G
, and such that the absolute value of the norm of their relative discriminant is bounded by
x
.

For any permutation group
G\leq S_{n}
, Malle defines a constant
0\leq a(G)\leq 1
, which is a rational number that depends only on the group
G
itself and is explicitly defined in terms of the indices of the permutations in
G
(see e.g.
[
Mal02
, p. 316]
).

Then, Malle’s first conjecture describes the growth of the counting function
N(K,G;x)
by asserting that for all
\epsilon>0
there exist positive constants
c_{1}(K,G)
and
c_{2}(K,G;\epsilon)
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle c_{1}(K,G)x^{a(G)}\leq N(K,G;x)\leq c_{2}(K,G;\epsilon)x^{a(G)+\epsilon}.

Furthermore, in
[
Mal02
, Sections 4–5]
, Malle studies the
inductive consistency
of his conjecture (
2.1
) with the formation of direct products and wreath products of groups for which conjecture (
2.1
) holds. In particular, for wreath products, Malle proves the following result.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
Mal02
]
, Corollary 5.3)
.

Assume that the upper bound in (
2.1
) holds for a transitive permutation group
H\leq S_{k}
, and that there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for a fixed
\epsilon>0
we have
c_{2}(L,H,\epsilon)\leq c(H,\epsilon)d_{L}^{\delta_{0}}
for all
L/K
with
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\simeq G\leq S_{\ell}
and where
d_{L}=|\mathcal{N}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(d(L/K))|
. Assume moreover that there exists a constant
e_{G}
such that
e_{G}<ka(H)-\delta_{0}
and

N(K,G;x)\leq c(K,G;\epsilon)x^{e_{G}+\epsilon}.

Then, the upper bound in (
2.1
) holds for the wreath product
G\wr_{\Omega}H
, where
\Omega=\{1,\cdots,k\}
and
H
acts on
\Omega
in the natural way.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that this deals with the upper bound in (
2.1
). Obtaining a lower bound for
N(K,G\wr_{\Omega}H;x)
is, unfortunately, not that clear. Let
L/K
be an extension such that
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\simeq H
for some transitive subgroup
H\leq S_{k}
and
let
G\leq S_{\ell}
be a transitive subgroup.
Malle briefly mentions in the last paragraph of page 322 in
[
Mal02
]
that for any extension
M/L
with
\operatorname{Gal}(M^{c}/L)\simeq G
, the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(M^{c}/K)
can be embedded in the wreath product

\operatorname{Gal}(M^{c}/L)\wr_{\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(L,L^{c})}\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\simeq G\wr_{\Omega}H,

where
\Omega:=\{1,\dots,k\}
and
H
acts on
\Omega
in the natural way. This is a consequence, for example, of the result of Theorem
1.4
and Proposition
4.1
of this paper. Then, Malle argues in
[
Mal02
, pp. 322–323]
that the lower bound in (
2.1
) can be obtained for the counting function
N(K,G\wr_{\Omega}H;x)
if the density of extensions
M/L
for which
\operatorname{Gal}(M^{c}/L)
is maximal, is positive; that is, the extensions for which the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(M^{c}/K)
equals the full wreath product
G\wr_{\Omega}H
. This strategy was first carried out by J. Klüners for the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H
, as is explained in the two following propositions.

Malle refined his first conjecture (
2.1
) in
[
Mal04
]
, now providing an asymptotic estimation for the counting function. More explicitly, again for a transitive subgroup
G\leq S_{n}
and a number field
K
, Malle defined another constant
b(K,G)
that only depends on
G
and
K
and is defined in terms of conjugacy classes of
G
. Then, the stronger version of the Malle conjectures states that there exists a positive constant
c(K,G)
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle N(K,G;x)\sim c(K,G)x^{a(G)}(\log x)^{b(K,G)-1}.

Malle further supports this stronger form of the conjecture with heuristic arguments and experimental data.

Moreover, regarding the topic of this paper, it is important to mention that this stronger form of the conjecture was further explored by J. Klüners for wreath products in
[
Klü12
]
, particularly for wreath products of the form
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H
, with
H
a subgroup of
S_{k}
and
\Omega=\{1,\dots,k\}
. For example, Klüners shows that Proposition
2.4
becomes simpler for wreath products like this, as is stated in the following result.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
Klü12
]
, Proposition 1)
.

Let
H\leq S_{k}
be a transitive permutation group and suppose that for every
\delta>0
, there exist positive constants
c(K,H;\delta)
such that
N(K,H;x)\leq c(K,H;\delta)x^{1+\delta}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a constant
c(K,C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H;\epsilon)
such that

N(K,C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H;x)\leq c(K,C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H;\epsilon)x^{a(C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H)+\epsilon}.

Here,
\Omega=\{1,2,\cdots,k\}.

Furthermore, Klüners obtains conditions for conjecture (
2.2
) to hold for wreath products. This is stated in the following result.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
Klü12
]
, Corollary 5 and Theorem 6)
.

Assume
H\leq S_{k}
satisfies that

N(K,H;x)=O_{K,H,\epsilon}(X^{1+\epsilon}),

that is, that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists a constant
c(K,H;\epsilon)
such that
N(K,H;x)\leq c(K,H;\epsilon)x^{1+\epsilon}
for large enough
x
. Then conjecture (
2.2
) is true for all groups of the form
G=C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}H
, where
\Omega=\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
.

The two decades that have passed since the formulation of the Malle conjectures have seen a continued stream of work towards them. In particular, it is important to point out that Klüners
[
Klü05
]
showed that the predicted value of the constant
b(K,G)
in the strong form of the Malle conjectures (
2.2
) is not always correct. Later on S. Türkelli
[
Tür15
]
proposed a correction for the exponent of the logarithm in (
2.2
). The interested reader can consult for example the introduction of
[
Alb21
]
for a short survey of some of the more relevant advances in this area. Moreover, in that paper, Alberts proposes a refinement of the Malle conjectures and compares it to the correction predicted by Türkelli.

2.3.
Application to the distribution of CM fields and the Colmez conjecture

In this application, we briefly describe a conjecture of P. Colmez that relates a height function defined on abelian varieties to certain sums of logarithmic derivatives of Artin
L
-functions evaluated at 0. In particular, we describe some recent advances on the conjecture that make use of the embedding of Galois groups into wreath products. We will start by describing the conjecture in the following paragraphs.

A
CM field

E
of degree
2n
is a number field such that
E
is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real number field
F
of degree
n
. In particular, this means that there exists a totally negative element
\Delta\ll 0
in
F
such that
E=F(\sqrt{\Delta})
(see e.g.
[
Mil20
, Proposition 1.4]
). Basic examples of CM fields are given by the cyclotomic fields
E_{n}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
, where
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
is a primitive
n
-th root of unity. In this case, the maximal totally real subfield of
E_{n}
is
F_{n}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1})=\mathbb{Q}(\cos{(2\pi/n)})
. In the quadratic case, the CM fields of degree 2 are just the imaginary quadratic fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
, where
d>0
is a square-free integer, and the maximal totally real subfield is
\mathbb{Q}
. For a
CM
field
E
of degree
2n
, the complex embeddings
E\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
come in complex conjugate pairs. Thus, the embeddings can be numbered as
\sigma_{1},\overline{\sigma_{1}},\dots,\sigma_{n},\overline{\sigma_{n}}
. Then, a
CM type
for
E
is a set of
n
embeddings containing exactly one embedding from each complex conjugate pair. Since there are two choices from each pair, the set of CM types for
E
, denoted by
\Phi(E)
, has exactly
2^{n}
CM types.

These fields appear naturally in the theory of complex multiplication for abelian varieties and this is the reason for the terminology CM. The theory of complex multiplication was described by D. Hilbert as
“not only the most beautiful part of mathematics but also of the whole of science.”
(see e.g.
[
Tau43
]
). The interested reader can consult for example
[
ST15
, Chapter 6]
and
[
Cox22
]
for a very readable treatment of the basic theory of complex multiplication for elliptic curves, or
[
Sil94
, Chapter 2]
for a more advanced and complete treatment, and ultimately
[
Mil20
]
or
[
Lan83
]
for a more general treatment of the theory for abelian varieties.

An abelian variety
A
of dimension
n
, defined over a number field
K
, is said to have
complex multiplication
, or to be a
CM abelian variety
, if its endomorphism algebra
\operatorname{End}^{0}(A)=\operatorname{End}(A)\otimes\mathbb{Q}
contains a commutative subring of dimension
2n
over
\mathbb{Q}
(see e.g.
[
Mil20
, Proposition 3.3]
). Moreover, let
F
be a totally real number field of degree
n
and let
E
be a CM field with maximal totally real subfield
F
and let
\Phi
a CM type for
E
. Then, we say that an abelian variety
A
is of type
(\mathcal{O}_{E},\Phi)
if it admits complex multiplication by
\mathcal{O}_{E}
, in the sense that
[E:\mathbb{Q}]=2\dim{(A)}
and there is a ring homomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{E}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}(A)
.

Thus let
X_{\Phi}
is said to be a CM abelian variety defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
of type
(\mathcal{O}_{E},\Phi)
. Also, let
L\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be a number field over which
X_{\Phi}
has everywhere good reduction
and choose a differential
\omega\in H^{0}(X_{\Phi},\Omega^{n}_{X_{\Phi}})
. Then the
Faltings height
of
X_{\Phi}
is defined by

\displaystyle h_{\textrm{Fal}}(X_{\Phi}):=-\frac{1}{2[L:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\log\left|\int_{X_{\Phi}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{C})}\omega^{\sigma}\wedge\overline{\omega^{\sigma}}\right|.

It is known that the Faltings height
h_{\textrm{Fal}}(X_{\Phi})
does not depend on the choice of
L,\omega
, or
X_{\Phi}
. In particular, by a result of Colmez
[
Col93
, Théorème 0.3 (ii)]
, the Faltings height depends only on the choice of CM type
\Phi
and not on the choice of CM abelian variety
X_{\Phi}
. This means that if
X_{\Phi}
and
Y_{\Phi}
are CM abelian varieties of type
(\mathcal{O}_{E},\Phi)
, then
h_{\textrm{Fal}}(X_{\Phi})=h_{\textrm{Fal}}(Y_{\Phi})
. Hence it is often denoted just by
h_{\textrm{Fal}}(\Phi)
. We observe that in the literature there are different normalizations of the Faltings height and so some versions will differ by the addition of a constant.

Let
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{CM}}
be the compositum of all CM fields and
G^{\mathcal{CM}}:=\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{CM}}/\mathbb{Q})
. Given an irreducible Artin character
\chi_{\pi}
of
G^{\mathcal{CM}}
we let
L(\chi_{\pi},s)
be the (incomplete) Artin
L
–function of
\chi_{\pi}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{\chi_{\pi}}
be the analytic Artin conductor of
\chi_{\pi}
. Then, Colmez
[
Col93
, Conjecture 0.4]
made the following conjecture (see also
[
Yan10
]
).

Conjecture 2.8
.

Let
E
be a CM field. Then if
\Phi
is any CM type for
E
and

X_{\Phi}
any CM abelian variety of type
(\mathcal{O}_{E},\Phi)
, we have

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Fal}}(X_{\Phi})=\sum_{\chi_{\pi}}c_{\Phi,\chi_{\pi}}\left(-\frac{L^{\prime}(\chi_{\pi},0)}{L(\chi_{\pi},0)}-\frac{1}{2}\log{(\mathfrak{f}_{\chi_{\pi}})}\right),

where the sum is over all the irreducible Artin characters of
G^{\mathcal{CM}}
, the coefficients
c_{\Phi,\chi_{\pi}}
can be explicitly described and only a finite number of them are nonzero.

Originally, Colmez
[
Col93
, Théorème 0.5]
was able to prove that his conjecture is true for any
abelian
CM field
E
, up to an unknown rational multiple of
\log{2}
, which was removed in later work by A. Obus
[
Obu13
]
. Thus, the conjecture only remains open in general for
non-abelian
CM fields. Interestingly, in this direction, and certainly sparked by the very important work of T. Yang
[
Yan10
]
and
[
Yan13
]
, the last decade has seen a great amount of work on the
non-abelian
case of this conjecture. In particular, the biggest advance was a proof of an averaged version of the conjecture by S. Yuan and S. Zhang
[
YZ18
]
and independently by F. Andreatta, E. Goren, B. Howard and K. Madapusi-Pera
[
AGHMP18
]
.

Now, given a CM field
E
of degree
2n
with maximal totally real subfield
F
, we let
\Phi(E)
be the set of CM types of
E
. It turns out that the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(E^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
(or equivalently
G^{\mathcal{CM}}
) acts on
\Phi(E)
by composition of maps (see e.g
[
BSM18
, Definition 2.1]
). Then, using the proved averaged version of the Colmez conjecture as the basis, the first author and R. Masri showed that if this action is transitive, then Conjecture
2.8
is true for the CM field
E
(see
[
BSM18
, Proposition 5.1]
). In that same paper, using this result as a stepping stone, an approach to study the Colmez conjecture from an arithmetic statistics perspective was initiated (see
[
BSM18
, §1.2]
). Later on, that approach was carried out by the first author, R. Masri and F. Thorne in
[
BSMT23
]
.

More precisely, in the latter paper, the authors defined the notion of a CM field with maximal Galois group with regards to an embedding into a wreath product. Specifically, according to Theorem
1.9
, if
G\leq S_{n}
is a transitive subgroup with
\operatorname{Gal}(F^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq G
, then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(E^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}G
, where
\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and the action of
G
on
C_{2}^{\Omega}=C_{2}^{n}
is by permuting the components. Thus the CM field
E
is said to be a
G
-Weyl CM field
if
\operatorname{Gal}(E^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
is maximal, i.e., if
\operatorname{Gal}(E^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}G
. Using this notion, the authors proved in
[
BSMT23
, Theorem 1.14]
that if
E
is a
G
-Weyl CM field, then the Colmez conjecture
2.8
is true for
E
.

Now, for any
X>0
let
N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X,G)
count the number of
G
-Weyl CM fields
E
of degree
2n
with discriminant
|d_{E}|\leq X
and let

N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X):=\sum_{G\leq S_{n}}N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X,G).

Hence
N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X)
counts the total number of
G
-Weyl CM fields of degree
2n
with
|d_{E}|\leq X
for all the possible transitive subgroups
G
of
S_{n}
. Moreover, we let
N_{2n}^{\text{cm}}(X)
count the total number of CM fields
E
of degree
2n
with
|d_{E}|\leq X
.

Then, assuming a weak form of the upper bound in the Malle conjecture (
2.1
), which is known to be true in many cases (see e.g
[
BSMT23
, p. 5, Remark 1.8 and §4]
), it was proved in that paper
[
BSMT23
, Theorem 1.11]
that there exist positive constants
C(n,G)
,
\alpha(n,G)
and
\beta(n)
such that

\frac{N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X,G)}{N_{2n}^{\text{cm}}(X)}=C(n,G)+O(X^{-\alpha(n,G)})

and

\frac{N_{2n}^{\text{Weyl}}(X)}{N_{2n}^{\text{cm}}(X)}=1+O(X^{-\beta(n)}).

This last result shows that for a fixed
n\geq 1
, out of all the possible CM fields of degree
2n
, it happens that 100% of them are of Weyl type. Therefore, when combined with the aforementioned results about the Colmez conjecture, this asymptotics imply that, conditional on the weak version of the upper bound in the Malle conjecture (
2.1
), the Colmez conjecture holds true for 100% of CM fields of degree
2n
for any
n\geq 1
. For more details about this the reader is referred to
[
BSMT23
, §1.2]
.

2.4.
Application in arithmetic geometry

This subsection describes the application that is by its nature the more demanding of the reader in terms of background. Since we only want to provide an indication of the usefulness of the embedding theorems of the paper, we won’t go into the full details of the definitions of the concepts mentioned here and instead we refer the interested reader to the original paper of C-L. Chai and F. Oort
[
CO12
]
, and to the excellent notes of J. S. Milne on Complex Multiplication
[
Mil20
]
and on Shimura Varieties
[
Mil17
]
.

To begin with, in
[
CO12
]
, the authors study the question of
whether there exists an abelian variety
A
, defined over the field
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
of all algebraic numbers, that is not isogenous to the Jacobian of a stable algebraic curve over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Now, let
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
be the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties. A subset of
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
is called a
special subset
if it is a finite union of
Shimura varieties
. Also, for a point
x=[(A,\lambda)]\in\mathcal{A}_{g,1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
with
A
an abelian variety with a principal polarization
\lambda
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the
isogeny orbit

\mathcal{I}(x)
of
x
in
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
is defined to be the set of all points
y=[(B,\nu)]\in\mathcal{A}_{g,1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that the abelian variety
B
is isogenous to
A
.

In their paper, Chai and Oort reduce their problem to proving that
for any special subset
Y\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, there exists a CM-point
y\in\mathcal{A}_{g,1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that the isogeny orbit
\mathcal{I}(y)
of
y
and the special subset
Y
are disjoint
.

In order to do this, they define a notion of CM-points of sufficient generality, which, as they explain, are expected to have density one among all the CM-points in
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
under any reasonable counting procedure (see
[
CO12
, Footnote 2]
).

Then, for the sake of defining those special CM-points, they first prove Theorem
1.8
(see
[
CO12
, Lemma 2.8]
), that is, that if
K
is a number field of degree
g=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
and
L
is a quadratic extension of
K
, then the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
embeds as a subgroup of the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}S_{g}
, where
\Omega=\{1,\dots,g\}
. Using this, they define a
Weyl CM field
of degree
2g
as a CM field
L
with maximal totally real subfield
K
of degree
g
, such that the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)
is maximal, i.e., such that
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq C_{2}\wr_{\Omega}S_{g}
. Moreover, with this, Chai and Oort define a
Weyl CM point
to be a point
[(A,\lambda)]\in\mathcal{A}_{g,1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
satisfying that the endomorphism algebra
\operatorname{End}^{0}(A)=L
, where
L
is a Weyl CM field of degree
2g
(see
[
CO12
, Definition 2.11]
).

Finally, as Chai and Oort explain, they use the notion of a Weyl CM point to prove that an irreducible Shimura subvariety
S\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
of positive dimension that contains a Weyl CM point
[(A,\lambda)]
with endomorphism algebra
\operatorname{End}^{0}(A)=L
as above, is actually a Hilbert modular variety associated to the maximal totally real subfield of
L
(see
[
CO12
, p. 3 and Lemma 3.5]
).

3.
Wreath products of groups

In this section we describe the wreath product construction on groups. This is defined in terms of semidirect products so we begin by recalling the definition of a semidirect product.

3.1.
Semidirect products

Let
K
and
H
be groups and let
\theta\colon H\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(K)
be a group homomorphism, where for every
h\in H
we denote
\theta(h)
by
\theta_{h}
in order to simplify the notation. Then, then
(outer) semidirect product
of
K
and
H
with respect to
\theta
, denoted
K\rtimes_{\theta}H
, is the group whose underlying set is the cartesian product
H\times K
, with group operation given by

(k_{1},h_{1})(k_{2},h_{2}):=(k_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(k_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})

for arbitrary
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
and
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
. The identity element in
K\rtimes_{\theta}H
is
(e_{K},e_{H})
and the inverse of an element
(k,h)\in K\rtimes_{\theta}H
is given by
(k,h)^{-1}=(\theta_{h^{-1}}(k^{-1}),h^{-1})
.

3.2.
Wreath products

Let
\Omega
be an arbitrary set and let
K
and
H
be groups. The set
K^{\Omega}
of all functions
f\colon\Omega\to K
forms a group under the binary operation of pointwise multiplication of functions, i.e., if
f,g\in K^{\Omega}
, then their product is the function
fg\colon\Omega\to K
defined by
(fg)(\omega)=f(\omega)g(\omega)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Suppose that
H
acts on the left on
\Omega
, i.e., that there’s an action
\cdot\colon H\times\Omega\to\Omega
such that

(i)

h_{1}\cdot(h_{2}\cdot\omega)=(h_{1}h_{2})\cdot\omega
for every
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
and every
\omega\in\Omega
; and

(ii)

e_{H}\cdot\omega=\omega
for every
\omega\in\Omega
.

From this action we define a homomorphism in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

There is a homomorphism of groups

\displaystyle\theta\colon

\displaystyle H\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})

\displaystyle h\longmapsto\theta_{h}

where
\theta_{h}\colon K^{\Omega}\to K^{\Omega}
is an automorphism, defined by
\theta_{h}(f)(\omega):=f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)
for every
f\in K^{\Omega}
and every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\theta_{h}\in\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
. Let
f,g\in K^{\Omega}
. Then certainly
\theta_{h}(f)\in K^{\Omega}
by definition. Moreover,
\theta_{h}
is a group homomorphism because if
\omega\in\Omega
, then

\displaystyle\theta_{h}(fg)(\omega)=(fg)(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)=f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)g(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)=\big(\theta_{h}(f)(\omega)\big)\big(\theta_{h}(g)(\omega)\big).

Thus
\theta_{h}(fg)=\theta_{h}(f)\theta_{h}(g)
so
\theta_{h}
is a homomorphism. Now, we want to check that
\theta_{h}
is a bijective function.

Injectivity of
\theta_{h}
:
Suppose that
\theta_{h}(f)=\theta_{h}(g)
and let
\omega\in\Omega
. Then note that this implies that

\displaystyle\quad\theta_{h}(f)(h\cdot\omega)=\theta_{h}(g)(h\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f(h^{-1}\cdot(h\cdot\omega))=g(h^{-1}\cdot(h\cdot\omega))

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f((h^{-1}h)\cdot\omega)=g((h^{-1}h)\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f(e_{H}\cdot\omega)=g(e_{H}\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f(\omega)=g(\omega).

Thus we conclude that
f=g
, so that
\theta_{h}
is injective.

Surjectivity of
\theta_{h}
:
Let
g\in K^{\Omega}
. Define a function
f\colon\Omega\to K
by
f(\omega):=g(h\cdot\omega)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
. Then observe that
\theta_{h}(f)(\omega)=f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)=g(h\cdot(h^{-1}\cdot\omega))=g((h\cdot h^{-1})\cdot\omega)=g(e_{H}\cdot\omega)=g(\omega)
. In other words, this shows that
\theta_{h}(f)=g
, so
\theta_{h}
is surjective.

This completes the proof that
\theta_{h}\in\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
for every
h\in H
.

\theta
is a group homomorphism:
Now we must show that the map
\theta\colon H\to\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
is a group homomorphism. Thus let
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
. Then we want to prove that
\theta(h_{1}h_{2})=\theta(h_{1})\circ\theta(h_{2})
, that is, that
\theta_{h_{1}h_{2}}=\theta_{h_{1}}\circ\theta_{h_{2}}
, where
\circ
denotes the operation of composition of functions, which is the binary operation in
\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
. To see this, again let
f\in K^{\Omega}
and let
\omega\in\Omega
. Then

\displaystyle\theta_{h_{1}h_{2}}(f)(\omega)

\displaystyle=f((h_{1}h_{2})^{-1}\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle=f((h_{2}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1})\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle=f(h_{2}^{-1}\cdot(h_{1}^{-1}\cdot\omega))

\displaystyle=\theta_{h_{2}}(f)(h_{1}^{-1}\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle=\theta_{h_{1}}\big(\theta_{h_{2}}(f)\big)(\omega)

\displaystyle=\big(\theta_{h_{1}}\circ\theta_{h_{2}}\big)(f)(\omega).

This proves that
\theta_{h_{1}h_{2}}(f)=(\theta_{h_{1}}\circ\theta_{h_{2}})(f)
and hence this implies that
\theta(h_{1}h_{2})=\theta(h_{1})\circ\theta(h_{2})
, as we wanted to show. Thus
\theta
is a group homomorphism and this completes the proof of the proposition.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

The reader might wonder why in defining the map
\theta\colon H\to\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
in the previous proposition, we defined
\theta_{h}(f)(\omega)
as
f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)
instead of defining it as
f(h\cdot\omega)
? The reason is that if it had been defined in the latter way, then we wouldn’t have been able to prove that
\theta
is a group homomorphism, but instead an antihomomorphism. More explicitly, if we had defined
\theta_{h}(f)(\omega)=f(h\cdot\omega)
for
h\in H
,
f\in K^{\Omega}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, then for
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
we would have obtained

\displaystyle\theta_{h_{1}h_{2}}(f)(\omega)

\displaystyle=f((h_{1}h_{2})\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle=f(h_{1}\cdot(h_{2}\cdot\omega))

\displaystyle=\theta_{h_{1}}(f)(h_{2}\cdot\omega)

\displaystyle=\theta_{h_{2}}\big(\theta_{h_{1}}(f)\big)(\omega)

\displaystyle=\big(\theta_{h_{2}}\circ\theta_{h_{1}}\big)(f)(\omega).

In other words, with that definition
\theta
would have been an antihomomorphism, that is, a map satisfying the relation
\theta(h_{1}h_{2})=\theta_{h_{1}h_{2}}=\theta_{h_{2}}\circ\theta_{h_{1}}=\theta(h_{2})\circ\theta(h_{1})
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
K
and
H
be groups, let
\Omega
be an
H
-set, that is, a set equipped with a left action of
H
given by
\cdot\colon H\times\Omega\longrightarrow\Omega
, and let
\theta\colon H\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
be the homomorphism defined in Proposition
3.1
. Then the
(complete) wreath product
of the groups
K
and
H
with respect to the action of
H
on
\Omega
, denoted
K\wr_{\Omega}H
, is defined by the semidirect product

K\wr_{\Omega}H:=K^{\Omega}\rtimes_{\theta}H.

The group
K^{\Omega}
is called the
base
of the wreath product. Thus, the binary operation on the wreath product
K\wr_{\Omega}H
is given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle(f_{1},h_{1})(f_{2},h_{2})=(f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})

for
f_{1},f_{2}\in K^{\Omega}
and
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
. Note that the product
f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2})
is the product of functions in
K^{\Omega}
, which was defined pointwise.

In the particular case in which the set
\Omega=H
and the action of
H
on
\Omega
is the left regular action, that is, when
H
acts on the left on itself by left multiplication, then the corresponding wreath product is called the
regular wreath product
of
K
and
H
and it is denoted by
K\wr_{r}H
.

Remark 3.4
.

When
\Omega
is finite, the order of the wreath product is given by
|K\wr_{\Omega}H|=|K|^{|\Omega|}\cdot|H|
.

Remark 3.5
.

What we have called the
regular wreath product
is also called the
standard complete wreath product
by some authors (see e.g.
[
Rob96
, p. 326]
). The terminology
regular wreath product
is used for example by Rotman
[
Rot95
, p. 175]
.

Remark 3.6
.

If the functions in
K^{\Omega}
are written in “tuple notation”, that is, if we write a function
f\colon\Omega\to K
as a sequence
(a_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where
f(\omega)=a_{\omega}\in K
for every
\omega\in\Omega
, then the relation
\theta_{h}(f)(\omega)=f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)
defining the automorphism
\theta_{h}\in\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
is written as

\theta_{h}((a_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega})=(a_{h^{-1}\cdot\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}.

This means that the
\omega
-th component of
\theta_{h}((a_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega})
is equal to the
(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)
-th component of
(a_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
.

We now give a detailed example of a wreath product in order to consider these definitions in a concrete case. This example will be reconsidered later on as part of Example
8.1
.

Example 3.7
.

Let
C_{2}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{[0]_{2},[1]_{2}\}
. We will now consider the regular wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}=C_{2}^{C_{2}}\rtimes_{\theta}C_{2}
. This group has order
|C_{2}|^{|C_{2}|}\cdot|C_{2}|=2^{2}\cdot 2=8
. We note that here the operation on the group
C_{2}^{C_{2}}
is given by pointwise addition, i.e., if
f,g\in C_{2}^{C_{2}}
, then
(f+g)([n]_{2})=f([n]_{1})+g([n]_{2})
for every
[n]_{2}\in C_{2}
. Thus, in this example, certain group operations will use additive notation.

Now, the group
C_{2}^{C_{2}}=\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\}
consists of the four functions

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto[0]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto[0]_{2},

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto[0]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto[1]_{2},

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto[1]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto[0]_{2},

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto[1]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto[1]_{2}.

Here the function
f_{1}
is the identity elelement in
C_{2}^{C_{2}}
and moreover we have
C_{2}^{C_{2}}\simeq C_{2}\oplus C_{2}
. Thus, as a set, the regular wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
is given by

C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}=\{(f_{1},[0]_{2}),(f_{2},[0]_{2}),(f_{3},[0]_{2}),(f_{4},[0]_{2}),(f_{1},[1]_{2}),(f_{2},[1]_{2}),(f_{3},[1]_{2}),(f_{4},[1]_{2})\}.

As we showed, this is a group of order 8. Up to isomorphism, we know that there are five groups of order 8, namely, the three abelian ones
C_{8}
,
C_{4}\oplus C_{2}
and
C_{2}\oplus C_{2}\oplus C_{2}
, and the two non-abelian ones given by the dihedral group
D_{4}
and the quaternion group
Q_{8}
.

We will now show that
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}\simeq D_{4}
. To see this, we will prove that
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
satisfies the standard presentation for the dihedral group
D_{4}
, that is, the presentation
\langle x,y\mid x^{4}=y^{2}=e,\,yxy^{-1}=x^{-1}\rangle
(see e.g.
[
Hun80
, p. 67]
).

Now, the map
\theta\colon C_{2}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(C_{2}^{C_{2}})
is determined by the two automorphisms

\theta_{[0]_{2}}\colon C_{2}^{C_{2}}\longrightarrow C_{2}^{C_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{[1]_{2}}\colon C_{2}^{C_{2}}\longrightarrow C_{2}^{C_{2}}

that we compute below.

For
\theta_{[0]_{2}}
, note that given a function
f\colon C_{2}\longrightarrow C_{2}
, we have

\theta_{[0]_{2}}(f)([n]_{2})=f(-[0]_{2}+[n]_{2})=f([n]_{2})

and hence
\theta_{[0]_{2}}(f)=f
for every function
f\in C_{2}^{C_{2}}
.

Similarly, for
\theta_{[1]_{2}}
we have

\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f)([n]_{2})=f(-[1]_{2}+[n]_{2})=f([n+1]_{2}).

From this we see that

\displaystyle\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{1})\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto f_{1}([1]_{2})=[0]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto f_{1}([0]_{2})=[0]_{2},

\displaystyle\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{2})\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto f_{2}([1]_{2})=[1]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto f_{2}([0]_{2})=[0]_{2},

\displaystyle\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{3})\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto f_{3}([1]_{2})=[0]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto f_{3}([0]_{2})=[1]_{2},

\displaystyle\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{4})\colon[0]_{2}\mapsto f_{4}([1]_{2})=[1]_{2},\quad[1]_{2}\mapsto f_{4}([0]_{2})=[1]_{2}.

Thus
\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{1})=f_{1}
,
\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{2})=f_{3}
,
\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{3})=f_{2}
and
\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{4})=f_{4}
.

With this information we can compute the product of any two elements in the wreath product
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
. We note that the element
(f_{1},[0]_{2})
is the identity element in
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
.

Now let
x:=(f_{2},[1]_{2})\in C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
. We will check that
x
has order 4. Observe for instance that

\displaystyle x^{2}=(f_{2},[1]_{2})(f_{2},[1]_{2})=(f_{2}+\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{2}),[1]_{2}+[1]_{2}))=(f_{2}+f_{3},[0]_{2})=(f_{4},[0]_{2}).

By doing similar calculations one can check that
x^{3}=(f_{3},[1]_{2})
and
x^{4}=(f_{1},[0]_{2})
.

In a similar way, let
y:=(f_{1},[1]_{2})\in C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
. Then one can check that
y
has order 2 and hence we have
x^{4}=y^{2}=e
.

Now, since
x
has order 4, we have
x^{-1}=x^{3}
, that is
x^{-1}=(f_{3},[1]_{2})
. Similarly, since
y
has order 2, we have
y^{-1}=y
. Then we can check that

\displaystyle yxy^{-1}

\displaystyle=(f_{1},[1]_{2})(f_{2},[1]_{2})(f_{1},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{1}+\theta_{[1]_{2}}(f_{2}),[0]_{2})(f_{1},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{1}+f_{3},[0]_{2})(f_{1},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{3},[0]_{2})(f_{1},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{3}+\theta_{[0]_{2}}(f_{1}),[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{3}+f_{1},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=(f_{3},[1]_{2})

\displaystyle=x^{-1}.

Therefore, these calculations imply that
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}
has the presentation

C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}=\langle x,y\mid x^{4}=y^{2}=e,\,yxy^{-1}=x^{-1}\rangle

and from this we conclude that
C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}\simeq D_{4}
.

4.
Some technical results about isomorphisms and subgroups of wreath products

As the title indicates, in this section we prove some technical results about isomorphisms and subgroups of wreath products. These results will be useful in identifying the wreath products into which the Galois groups are being embedded in the main theorems of the paper, as will be illustrated in the examples from Section
8
.

The first result we prove allows for the identification of complete wreath products with isomorphic factors.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
K,\widehat{K},H,\widehat{H}
be arbitrary groups with
K\simeq\widehat{K}
and
H\simeq\widehat{H}
, and let
\varphi\colon H\longrightarrow\widehat{H}
and
\psi\colon K\longrightarrow\widehat{K}
be isomorphisms. Suppose that
\Omega
is an
H
-set, that
\widehat{\Omega}
is an
\widehat{H}
-set, and that there is an equivariant bijection
\xi:\Omega\longrightarrow\widehat{\Omega}
such that
\xi(h\cdot\omega)=\varphi(h)\cdot\xi(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
and
h\in H
. Then
K\wr_{\Omega}H\simeq\widehat{K}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{H}
and an isomorphism is given by

\displaystyle\vartheta\colon

\displaystyle K\wr_{\Omega}H\longrightarrow\widehat{K}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{H}

\displaystyle\ (f,h)\longmapsto\big(\psi\circ f\circ\xi^{-1},\varphi(h)\big).

Proof.

The direct proof that
\vartheta
is injective and surjective is straightforward, so we will just indicate that the inverse of
\vartheta
is given by

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}\colon

\displaystyle\widehat{K}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{H}\longrightarrow K\wr_{\Omega}H

\displaystyle\ (\hat{f},\hat{h})\longmapsto\big(\psi^{-1}\circ\hat{f}\circ\xi,\varphi^{-1}(\hat{h})\big).

Therefore, we must prove that
\vartheta
is a group homomorphism. First we let
\theta\colon H\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(K^{\Omega})
and
\widehat{\theta}\colon\widehat{H}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{K}^{\widehat{\Omega}})
be the homomorphisms from Proposition
3.1
, which for
h\in H
,
\hat{h}\in\widehat{H}
,
f\colon\Omega\longrightarrow K
and
\hat{f}\colon\widehat{\Omega}\longrightarrow\widehat{K}
are defined by

\theta_{h}(f)(\omega_{0})=f(h^{-1}\cdot\omega_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\theta}(\hat{f})(\hat{\omega}_{0})=\hat{f}(\hat{h}^{-1}\cdot\hat{\omega}_{0})

for every
\omega_{0}\in H
and every
\hat{\omega}_{0}\in\widehat{H}
.

Now let
(f_{1},h_{1}),(f_{2},h_{2})\in K\wr_{\Omega}H
. We will prove that

\vartheta\big((f_{1},h_{1})(f_{2},h_{2})\big)=\vartheta\big((f_{1},h_{1})\big)\vartheta\big((f_{2},h_{2})\big).

Observe first that

\displaystyle\vartheta\big((f_{1},h_{1})(f_{2},h_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\vartheta\big((f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\psi\circ(f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}))\circ\xi^{-1},\varphi(h_{1}h_{2})\big)

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\big(\psi\circ(f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}))\circ\xi^{-1},\varphi(h_{1})\varphi(h_{2})\big).

On the other hand

\displaystyle\vartheta\big((f_{1},h_{1})\big)\vartheta\big((f_{2},h_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\xi^{-1},\varphi(h_{1})\big)\big(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1},\varphi(h_{2})\big)

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\big((\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\xi^{-1})\widehat{\theta}_{\varphi(h_{1})}(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1}),\varphi(h_{1})\varphi(h_{2})\big).

Therefore, comparing equations (
4
) and (
4
), we must prove that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\psi\circ(f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}))\circ\xi^{-1}=(\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\xi^{-1})\widehat{\theta}_{\varphi(h_{1})}(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1}).

Note that both the function on the left hand side and the one on the right hand side of (
4.3
) are functions in
\widehat{K}^{\widehat{\Omega}}
. Hence, we will check that both sides give the same value when evaluated at an arbitrary element
\hat{\omega}\in\widehat{\Omega}
.

For the LHS of equation (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\big(\psi\circ(f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}))\circ\xi^{-1}\big)(\hat{\omega})

\displaystyle=\psi\big((f_{1}\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2}))(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\theta_{h_{1}}(f_{2})(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))f_{2}(h_{1}^{-1}\cdot\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega})\big)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)\psi\big(f_{2}(h_{1}^{-1}\cdot\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big).

On the other hand, for the RHS of equation (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\big((\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\xi^{-1})\widehat{\theta}_{\varphi(h_{1})}(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1})\big)(\hat{\omega})

\displaystyle=(\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\xi^{-1})(\hat{\omega})\,\widehat{\theta}_{\varphi(h_{1})}(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1})(\hat{\omega})

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)\big(\psi\circ f_{2}\circ\xi^{-1}\big)(\varphi(h_{1})^{-1}\cdot\hat{\omega})

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)\psi\big(f_{2}(\xi^{-1}(\varphi(h_{1})^{-1}\cdot\hat{\omega}))\big)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\psi\big(f_{1}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))\big)\psi\big(f_{2}(\xi^{-1}(\varphi(h_{1}^{-1})\cdot\hat{\omega}))\big).

Now, observe that as a consequence of the equivariance of the map
\xi
we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle h_{1}^{-1}\cdot\xi^{-1}(\hat{\omega})=\xi^{-1}(\varphi(h_{1}^{-1})\cdot\hat{\omega}).

Finally, comparing (
4
) with (
4
) and using (
4.6
), we see that (
4.3
) holds, and that implies that
\vartheta
is a homomorphism. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Now, taking
\Omega=H
,
\widehat{\Omega}=\widehat{H}
and
\xi=\varphi
in the previous proposition, we get the corresponding result for regular wreath products, which is stated in the following corollary. In this case the condition

is reduced to the fact that
\varphi
is a group homomorphism, because the action of

is the left regular action, i.e.
H
acts on itself on the left by left multiplication.

and

and an isomorphism is given by

\displaystyle\vartheta\colon

\displaystyle K\wr_{r}H\longrightarrow\widehat{K}\wr_{r}\widehat{H}

\displaystyle\ (f,h)\longmapsto\big(\psi\circ f\circ\varphi^{-1},\varphi(h)\big).

Finally, the following result gives conditions under which a wreath product formed from subgroups of the original components is actually a subgroup of the original wreath product.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\widehat{K},\widehat{H}
be arbitrary groups and let
K\leq\widehat{K}
and
H\leq\widehat{H}
be subgroups. Suppose that
\Omega
is an
H
-set, that
\widehat{\Omega}
is an
\widehat{H}
-set, and that there is an equivariant bijection
\xi:\Omega\longrightarrow\widehat{\Omega}
such that
\xi(h\cdot\omega)=h\cdot\xi(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
and all

\widehat{K}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{H}
under the injective group homomorphism given by

\displaystyle\vartheta\colon

\displaystyle K\wr_{\Omega}H\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\widehat{K}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{H}

\displaystyle\ (f,h)\longmapsto\big(\iota\circ f\circ\xi^{-1},h\big),

This follows from the proof of Proposition

\varphi:H\longrightarrow\widehat{H}

are both injective homomorphisms, then the map
\vartheta
from Proposition
4.1
is also an injective homomorphism, and using the natural inclusions
K\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\widehat{K}
and
H\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\widehat{H}
as injective homomorphisms.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

be a number field of degree

and let

be a quadratic extension of
K
. Let
\alpha
be a primitive element for
K/\mathbb{Q}
, that is, write
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
and let
\alpha_{1}:=\alpha,\dots,\alpha_{n}
be the distinct conjugates of
\alpha

we define a permutation

The Kaloujnine-Krasner Universal Embedding Theorem

In 1951 Léo Kaloujnine
1
1

1

He was Russian and this is the transliteration he used for his name at the time when he wrote the papers on wreath products with Marc Krasner. His Russian name is nowadays transliterated as Lev Kaluznin.
and Marc Krasner published two very influential papers on wreath products for permutation groups and the theory of group extensions, titled
“Produit complet des groupes de permutations et problème d’extension de groupes II and III”
(see
[
KK51a
]
and
[
KK51b
]
). In particular, in
[
KK51a
]
they prove a fundamental result now known as the Kaloujnine-Krasner Universal Embedding Theorem. A generalization of the Universal Embedding Theorem of Kaloujnine and Krasner was given by D. F. Holt in
[
Hol78
, Theorem 2]
.

This theorem puts wreath products at the heart of the study of what is now known as the extension problem for groups. More specifically, in the theory of group extensions, the main objective is to study how a group
G
can be constructed from knowledge of a normal subgroup
N\vartriangleleft G
and knowledge of the corresponding quotient
G/N
. The interested reader can consult for example
[
Rob96
, Chapter 11]
or
[
Rot95
, Chapter 7]
to learn more about the theory of group extensions.

Here we shall restrict ourselves to giving the definition of a group extension and a clear exposition of the theorem of Kaloujnine and Krasner. Let
N
and
Q
be arbitrary groups. Then, an
extension
of
N
by
Q
is a group
G
such that there is a normal subgroup
M\vartriangleleft G
satisfying

(i)

M\simeq N
, and

(ii)

G/M\simeq Q
.

Equivalently, we say that
G
is an extension of
N
by
Q
if there is a short exact sequence of groups

1\longrightarrow N\overset{\iota}{\longrightarrow}G\overset{\varepsilon}{\longrightarrow}Q\longrightarrow 1.

In this setting, we would have
M=\ker{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{Im}(\iota)
.

Then, the Kaloujnine-Krasner theorem is the following. The proof that we give is adapted from the one sketched in
[
Rot95
]
. Additionally, we exhibit the explicit embedding, which is then used at the end of the section to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.1

(Kaloujnine-Krasner)
.

Let
N
and
Q
be arbitrary groups. Then the regular wreath product
N\wr_{r}Q
contains an isomorphic copy of every extension of
N
by
Q
. More precisely, suppose that
G
is a group extension of
N
by
Q
given by the exact sequence

1\longrightarrow N\overset{\iota}{\longrightarrow}G\overset{\varepsilon}{\longrightarrow}Q\longrightarrow 1.

Then
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the regular wreath product
N\wr_{r}Q
. In particular, if we identify
N
with
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)=\ker(\varepsilon)\vartriangleleft G
and thus think of
N
as a normal subgroup of
G
, then an explicit embedding is given by the map

(5.1)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle G\longrightarrow N\wr_{r}Q

\displaystyle g\longmapsto(\sigma_{g},\varepsilon(g)),

where
\sigma_{g}\in N^{Q}
is the map
\sigma_{g}\colon Q\longrightarrow N
defined by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{g}(q):=s(q)^{-1}gs\big(\varepsilon(g)^{-1}q\big)

for every
q\in Q
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is an extension of
N
by
Q
given by the exact sequence

1\longrightarrow N\overset{\iota}{\longrightarrow}G\overset{\varepsilon}{\longrightarrow}Q\longrightarrow 1.

To make the notation simpler in the proof, we will identify
N
with
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)=\ker(\varepsilon)\vartriangleleft G
and thus we will assume without loss of generality that
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
.
Now, since
\varepsilon\colon G\to Q
is surjective, let
s\colon Q\to G
be a right inverse of
\varepsilon
. Thus
\varepsilon\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{Q}
.

With this notation, define a map

(5.3)

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle G\longrightarrow N\wr_{r}Q

\displaystyle g\longmapsto(\sigma_{g},\varepsilon(g)),

where
\sigma_{g}\in N^{Q}
is the map
\sigma_{g}\colon Q\longrightarrow N
defined by

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{g}(q):=s(q)^{-1}gs\big(\varepsilon(g)^{-1}q\big)

for every
q\in Q
.

We will now prove that the map
\varphi
is an injective homomorphism and that will finish the proof of the theorem.

First we observe that the map
\sigma_{g}
is well defined, that is, that for every
q\in Q
we have
\sigma_{g}(q)\in N
. To see this, note that
\sigma_{g}(q)\in\ker(\varepsilon)=N
. Indeed, using the fact that
\varepsilon
is a homomorphism and that
\varepsilon\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{Q}
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\sigma_{g}(q))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(s(q))^{-1}\varepsilon(g)\varepsilon\big(s(\varepsilon(g)^{-1}q)\big)

\displaystyle=q^{-1}\varepsilon(g)\varepsilon(g)^{-1}q

\displaystyle=e_{Q}.

Injectivity of
\varphi
:
Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and suppose that
\varphi(g_{1})=\varphi(g_{2})
. Then
(\sigma_{g_{1}},\varepsilon(g_{1}))=(\sigma_{g_{2}},\varepsilon(g_{2}))
and hence we have
\sigma_{g_{1}}=\sigma_{g_{2}}
and
\varepsilon(g_{1})=\varepsilon(g_{2})
. Then, evaluating the functions
\sigma_{g_{1}}
and
\sigma_{g_{2}}
at
e_{Q}
(to make things simpler) and using the fact that
\varepsilon(g_{1})=\varepsilon(g_{2})
, we see that

\displaystyle\sigma_{g_{1}}(e_{Q})=\sigma_{g_{2}}(e_{Q})

\displaystyle\implies s(e_{Q})^{-1}g_{1}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}e_{Q}\big)=s(e_{Q})^{-1}g_{2}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{2})^{-1}e_{Q}\big)

\displaystyle\implies g_{1}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}\big)=g_{2}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{2})^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\implies g_{1}=g_{2}.

In the previous calculation we have successively cancelled equal elements appearing on both sides of the equalities. Therefore the map
\varphi
is injective.

The map
\varphi
is a homomorphism:
Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. Then we want to prove that

\varphi(g_{1}g_{2})=\varphi(g_{1})\varphi(g_{2}).

Note first that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\varphi(g_{1}g_{2})=(\sigma_{g_{1}g_{2}},\varepsilon(g_{1}g_{2})).

On the other hand, using the definition (
3.1
) of the multiplication on the regular wreath product
N\wr_{r}Q
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(g_{1})\varphi(g_{2})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{g_{1}},\varepsilon(g_{1}))(\sigma_{g_{2}},\varepsilon(g_{2}))

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{g_{1}}\theta_{\varepsilon(g_{1})}(\sigma_{g_{2}}),\varepsilon(g_{1})\varepsilon(g_{2}))

(5.6)

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{g_{1}}\theta_{\varepsilon(g_{1})}(\sigma_{g_{2}}),\varepsilon(g_{1}g_{2})).

Thus, comparing the right hand side of (
5.5
) with the right hand side of (
5
), it remains to show that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{g_{1}}\theta_{\varepsilon(g_{1})}(\sigma_{g_{2}})=\sigma_{g_{1}g_{2}}.

Now, in order to prove this, recall that
N\wr_{r}Q=N^{Q}\rtimes_{\theta}Q
, where

\displaystyle\theta\colon

\displaystyle Q\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(N^{Q})

\displaystyle q\longmapsto\theta_{q}

and
\theta_{q}\colon N^{Q}\to N^{Q}
is an automorphism, defined by
\theta_{q}(\sigma)(x):=\sigma(q^{-1}x)
for every
\sigma\in N^{Q}
and every
x\in Q
.
Thus, to prove (
5.7
), we let
x\in Q
be arbitrary and evaluate to get

\displaystyle\big(\sigma_{g_{1}}\theta_{\varepsilon(g_{1})}(\sigma_{g_{2}})\big)(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{g_{1}}(x)\big(\theta_{\varepsilon(g_{1})}(\sigma_{g_{2}})\big)(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{g_{1}}(x)\sigma_{g_{2}}(\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}x)

\displaystyle=\Big(s(x)^{-1}g_{1}s(\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}x)\Big)\Big(s\big(\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}x\big)^{-1}g_{2}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{2})^{-1}\varepsilon(g_{1})^{-1}x\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=s(x)^{-1}g_{1}g_{2}s\big(\varepsilon(g_{1}g_{2})^{-1}x\big)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{g_{1}g_{2}}(x).

Therefore, this proves (
5.7
) and this completes the proof that
\varphi(g_{1}g_{2})=\varphi(g_{1})\varphi(g_{2})
. Hence
\varphi\colon G\longrightarrow N\wr_{r}Q
is an injective group homomorphism and this proves the theorem.
∎

Theorem 6.1

Consider a tower of fields

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/F)\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/F)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho)),

are given by

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\sqrt{5}\mapsto-\sqrt{5},\quad\sqrt{7}\mapsto\sqrt{7},

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

\displaystyle\colon\sqrt{5}\mapsto\sqrt{5},\quad-\sqrt{7}\mapsto\sqrt{7},

\displaystyle\rho_{3}

\displaystyle\colon\sqrt{5}\mapsto-\sqrt{5},\quad\sqrt{7}\mapsto-\sqrt{7}.

We note that
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and in particular we have
\rho_{1}^{2}=\rho_{2}^{2}=\rho_{3}^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{L}
and
\rho_{1}\circ\rho_{2}=\rho_{3}
,
\rho_{2}\circ\rho_{3}=\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{1}\circ\rho_{3}=\rho_{2}
. On the other hand, for the extension
L/K
the Galois group is
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\{\operatorname{id}_{L},\rho_{2}\}
.

With this, we can describe the elements of the regular wreath product

\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}\rtimes_{\theta}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}),

which is a group of order
|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|^{|\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})|}\cdot|\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})|=2^{2}\cdot 2=8
.

First, the group
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}
of functions
f\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
has four elements, which are given by

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto\operatorname{id}_{L},\quad\eta\mapsto\operatorname{id}_{L},

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto\operatorname{id}_{L},\quad\eta\mapsto\rho_{2},

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto\rho_{2},\quad\eta\mapsto\operatorname{id}_{L},

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto\rho_{2},\quad\eta\mapsto\rho_{2}.

We observe that
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}=\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
with
f_{2}^{2}=f_{3}^{2}=f_{4}^{2}=f_{1}
and
f_{2}f_{3}=f_{4}
,
f_{2}f_{4}=f_{3}
and
f_{3}f_{4}=f_{2}
.

Therefore, as a set, we have

\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})=\{(f_{1},\operatorname{id}_{K}),(f_{2},\operatorname{id}_{K}),(f_{3},\operatorname{id}_{K}),(f_{4},\operatorname{id}_{K}),(f_{1},\eta),(f_{2},\eta),(f_{3},\eta),(f_{4},\eta)\}.

Now, recall that the surjective map
\varepsilon\colon\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
is given by
\varepsilon(\rho)=\rho|_{K}
for every
\rho\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})
. Therefore, we have
\varepsilon(\operatorname{id}_{L})=\varepsilon(\rho_{2})=\operatorname{id}_{K}
and
\varepsilon(\rho_{1})=\varepsilon(\rho_{3})=\eta
. In order to define the embedding (
1.6
) from Theorem
1.1
we need a right inverse
s
of
\varepsilon
. Thus, consider the map
s\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})
given by
s(\operatorname{id}_{K})=\operatorname{id}_{L}
and
s(\eta)=\rho_{1}
. Then we have
\varepsilon\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}
. Recall that the embedding from Theorem
1.1
is given by the map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\rho\longmapsto(\sigma_{\rho},\varepsilon(\rho))

where
\sigma_{\rho}\colon\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
is defined by

\sigma_{\rho}(\tau):=s(\tau)^{-1}\circ\rho\circ s\big(\varepsilon(\rho)^{-1}\circ\tau\big)

for every
\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
. We now compute explicitly the maps
\sigma_{\rho}
for every
\rho\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})
. For example, for
\sigma_{\operatorname{id}_{L}}
we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\operatorname{id}_{L}}(\operatorname{id}_{K})=s(\operatorname{id}_{K})^{-1}\circ\operatorname{id_{L}}\circ s\big(\varepsilon(\operatorname{id}_{L})^{-1}\circ\operatorname{id}_{K}\big)=\operatorname{id}_{L}^{-1}\circ s\big(\operatorname{id}_{K}^{-1}\big)=\operatorname{id}_{L}\circ\operatorname{id}_{L}=\operatorname{id}_{L}

and similarly

\displaystyle\sigma_{\operatorname{id}_{L}}(\eta)=s(\eta)^{-1}\circ\operatorname{id_{L}}\circ s\big(\varepsilon(\operatorname{id}_{L})^{-1}\circ\eta\big)=\rho_{1}^{-1}\circ s\big(\operatorname{id}_{K}^{-1}\circ\eta\big)=\rho_{1}\circ s(\eta)=\rho_{1}\circ\rho_{1}=\rho_{1}^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{L}.

This shows that
\sigma_{\operatorname{id}_{L}}=f_{1}
. Analogous calculations show that
\sigma_{\rho_{1}}=f_{1}
,
\sigma_{\rho_{2}}=f_{4}
and
\sigma_{\rho_{3}}=f_{4}
. Therefore, the embedding
\varphi\colon\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(\operatorname{id}_{L})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{\operatorname{id}_{L}},\varepsilon(\operatorname{id}_{L}))=(f_{1},\operatorname{id}_{K}),

\displaystyle\varphi(\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{\rho_{1}},\varepsilon(\rho_{1}))=(f_{1},\eta),

\displaystyle\varphi(\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{\rho_{2}},\varepsilon(\rho_{2}))=(f_{4},\operatorname{id}_{K}),

\displaystyle\varphi(\rho_{3})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{\rho_{3}},\varepsilon(\rho_{3}))=(f_{4},\eta).

In order to look at the group structure of the wreath product we can use the isomorphisms
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\simeq C_{2}
and
\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\simeq C_{2}
, as well as Corollary
4.2
to conclude that

\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\simeq C_{2}\wr_{r}C_{2}.

Then, from the calculations carried out in Example
3.7
we conclude that this wreath product is isomorphic to
D_{4}
. However, we can determine this structure manually as well. First we recall from Proposition
3.1
that the homomorphism
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle\theta\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad h\longmapsto\theta_{h}

where
\theta_{h}\colon\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}
is the automorphism defined by

\theta_{h}(f)(\tau)=f(h^{-1}\circ\tau)

for every
f\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}
and every
\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
. From this we can compute
\theta_{\operatorname{id}_{K}}(f)(\tau)=f(\operatorname{id}_{K}^{-1}\circ\tau)=f(\tau)
for every
f\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})}
, so that
\theta_{\operatorname{id}_{K}}
is the identity automorphism in
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})})
. For the other automorphism we have

\theta_{\eta}(f)(\tau)=f(\eta^{-1}\circ\tau)=f(\eta\circ\tau).

Therefore, we see that

\displaystyle\theta_{\eta}(f_{1})\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto f_{1}(\eta\circ\operatorname{id}_{K})=f_{1}(\eta)=\operatorname{id}_{L}\qquad\eta\mapsto f_{1}(\eta\circ\eta)=f_{1}(\operatorname{id}_{K})=\operatorname{id}_{L},

\displaystyle\theta_{\eta}(f_{2})\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto f_{2}(\eta\circ\operatorname{id}_{K})=f_{2}(\eta)=\rho_{1}\qquad\eta\mapsto f_{2}(\eta\circ\eta)=f_{2}(\operatorname{id}_{K})=\operatorname{id}_{L},

\displaystyle\theta_{\eta}(f_{3})\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto f_{3}(\eta\circ\operatorname{id}_{K})=f_{3}(\eta)=\operatorname{id}_{L}\qquad\eta\mapsto f_{3}(\eta\circ\eta)=f_{3}(\operatorname{id}_{K})=\rho_{1},

\displaystyle\theta_{\eta}(f_{4})\colon\operatorname{id}_{K}\mapsto f_{4}(\eta\circ\operatorname{id}_{K})=f_{4}(\eta)=\rho_{1}\qquad\eta\mapsto f_{4}(\eta\circ\eta)=f_{4}(\operatorname{id}_{K})=\rho_{1}.

This shows that

\theta_{\eta}\colon f_{1}\mapsto f_{1},\quad f_{2}\mapsto f_{3},\quad f_{3}\mapsto f_{2},\quad f_{4}\mapsto f_{4}.

Thus
\theta_{\eta}
just permutes
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
and leaves
f_{1}
and
f_{4}
fixed. Using this fact, if we define the elements
r:=(f_{2},\eta)
and
s:=(f_{1},\eta)
of
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{r}\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
, it is a short calculation to verify that
r
and
s
are generators of this group, and moreover that
\text{ord}(r)=4,\text{ord}(s)=2
and that
sr=r^{-1}s
, thus proving that the wreath product is isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{4}
, as was already mentioned above as a consequence of Corollary
4.2
and Example
3.7
.

Example 8.2

(A Kummer extension)
.

Let
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha
is a root of the irreducible polynomial
p(x):=x^{6}-x^{5}-x^{2}-x+1\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Then

is a degree 6 extension with discriminant
d_{K}=-11691=-3^{3}\cdot 433
. Thus, the only rational primes that ramify in

are 3 and 433. This number field corresponds to the entry

in the

is the quadratic field

. In particular, this implies that

contains the 6-th roots of unity
\{\pm 1,\frac{1}{2}(\pm 1\pm\sqrt{-3})\}
. Moreover, the Galois closure
K^{c}
of

is a number field of degree
[K^{c}:\mathbb{Q}]=72
and
\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq S_{3}\wr_{\Omega_{K}}C_{2}=S_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{2}
, where
\Omega_{K}=\{1,2\}
and
C_{2}
acts on
\Omega_{K}
by multiplication and reduction modulo
2
by using the elements
1
and
2
as representatives of the residue classes.

Now, let
L:=K(\sqrt{2},\sqrt[3]{5})
. Then by Theorem
1.10
we know that
L/K
is a 6-Kummer extension. This means that
L/K
is an abelian extension with abelian Galois group of exponent 6. For example, in

This number field has discriminant

and moreover

. Therefore, by the tower law we know that

. Since the extension

is abelian (and thus Galois) of degree 6, and since there is only one abelian group of order 6, we know that
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\simeq C_{6}
.

Then, we tried computing directly the Galois closure
L^{c}
of

in

with the command

, but the computation was very heavy and it ended up exceeding the default stack space used in the algorithm. Also, the number field

L^{c}=K^{c}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt[3]{5}).

Now, as was mentioned above, the rational primes that ramify in
K
are 3 and 433 and it is known that a prime ramifies in
K
if and only if it ramifies in the Galois closure
K^{c}
; this follows from the fact that the Galois closure
K^{c}
is the compositum of all the Galois conjugate fields of

and that if a prime

(see e.g.

). Therefore the only ramified primes in

are 3 and 433. This implies that

and

because otherwise 2 or 5 would be ramified in

[L^{c}:\mathbb{Q}]=[K^{c}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt[3]{5}):\mathbb{Q}]=[K^{c}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt[3]{5}):K^{c}][K^{c}:\mathbb{Q}]=6\cdot 72=432.

Then, by Theorem

we know that
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
, which is a group of order

(8.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq C_{6}\wr_{\widehat{\Omega}}(S_{3}\wr_{\Omega_{K}}C_{2}),

where
\Omega:=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(K,K^{c})
is the set of embeddings, which has 6 elements, and similarly
\widehat{\Omega}=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
.
Note that this wreath product has order

|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q})|=|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|^{|\Omega|}\cdot|\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q})|=6^{6}\cdot 72=$3\,359\,232$=2^{9}\cdot 3^{8}.

Finally, observe that Theorem
1.4
gives an embedding of
\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/\mathbb{Q})
into the wreath product

\operatorname{Gal}(L^{c}/K)\wr_{\Omega}\operatorname{Gal}(K^{c}/\mathbb{Q}),

which has order

. To compare, this is a group that is about 40 million times bigger than the wreath product (
8.1
)!

Thus, this example shows, in a very striking fashion, how the embedding from Theorem
1.11
is significantly sharper than the one from Theorem
1.4
.
