Genealogical transition in the noisy N N -Branching Random Walk. How stronger selection may promote genetic diversity.
Emmanuel Schertzer, Alejandro H. Wences

Abstract
We consider an extension of the noisy N N -Branching Random Walk that models the evolution of a population subject to natural selection. We show the existence of a critical value for the noise which separates the limiting genealogical structure into two regimes, which we respectively call the semi-pulled and the fully-pulled regimes. In the fully-pulled regime, the genealogy converges to a discrete time Poisson-Dirichlet coalescent. In the semi-pulled regime, the genealogy converges to the Bolthausen-Sznitman coalescent. We discuss some interesting biological consequences of this result. In particular, our model predicts a non-monotone relation between the selection strength and the effective population size.

Population genetics, Coalescent theory, Noisy

1
Introduction.

Darwinian evolution emerges from the processes of mutation, reproduction, competition, and genetic drift.
To model these evolutionary forces, an
asexual
population can be naturally encoded as a
fitness wave
: a cloud of individuals moving in an abstract
1
-dimensional fitness space
[
9
,
41
,
40
,
34
]
.
Assuming discrete-time dynamics, each generation is generally subdivided into two sub-phases: a
reproduction phase
followed by a
“culling” phase
, which constraints the population size to remain at equilibrium. See Figure
1
.
In the reproduction phase, each individual gives birth to a random number of individuals whose fitness deviates from the parental fitness due to the accumulation of random mutations along the genome
[
34
]
. The reproduction phase generally increases the population size whereas the culling phase, by selecting individuals according to their fitness, maintains the population at a fixed size
N
.

Fitness wave models have attracted quite a lot of attention in the Physics and Mathematics literature in the last two decades, see e.g.,

[
9
,
41
,
40
,
34
,
14
,
15
,
20
,
51
,
31
,
38
,
46
,
47
]
.
These models are natural extensions of the exchangeable (or neutral) paradigm for population models
[
10
,
11
,
48
]
.
However, even in the simplest settings,
they are notoriously difficult to analyse and, to this date, a large gap remains between our understanding of neutral models and more realistic models with natural selection.

The aim of the present article is to uncover a new phase transition for fitness waves. We present an exactly solvable model where the parameter space is partitioned into two main regimes, that we call the
semi-pulled
and
fully-pulled
regimes,
each characterized by its own genealogical structure. As we shall see, this phase transition in the shape of the genealogy will have important implications for the following fundamental question in population genetics:

(Q)

How is genetic diversity related to selection strength? In other words, if we increase selection strength, does it lead to a decrease or increase in genetic diversity within the population, measured by the expected number of neutral genetic differences between two randomly sampled individuals?

Many results since the foundation of population genetics
[
21
,
25
,
26
,
49
]
, point to natural selection as one of the main causes of reduction of genetic diversity in a population. Here we present a directional selection model with the unexpected feature that stronger selection may induce a higher genetic diversity.

1.1
The model.

We consider a population evolution model of
N
particles on the real line which reproduce at discrete times.
Every generation is of size
N
and particles are given
a fitness value on the real line. For
j\leq N
, we denote by
X^{N}_{j}(t)
the position of the
j^{th}
particle at generation
t
, assuming that particles are ranked
in decreasing order.
Particles at generation
0
are placed according to any arbitrary configuration.
At a given time
t\geq 1
, the population is constructed from
the previous generation in three consecutive steps: one reproduction step, a truncation selection step, and a
natural selection step.

(Reproduction)
The children of individual
X^{N}_{k}(t-1)
are given by the random point process

\Delta_{k}(t-1)

(1)

where conditionally on
(X_{k}(t-1);k\in[N])
,
\Delta_{k}(t-1)

is an independent Poisson Point Process (PPP) of intensity measure

e^{-(s-X_{k}^{N}(t-1))}\hskip 3.0ptds
. After the reproduction step, all parents die out.

(Truncation Selection)
This is parametrized by
\rho\geq 1
.
In this step we only retain the
N^{\rho}
-rightmost individuals among those produced in the previous reproduction step. This is well defined since, almost surely, there are only finitely many particles on any right interval
[a,\infty)
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
.

(Natural Selection)
This is parameterized by

\beta>0
.
Conditionally on the positions of the surviving
N^{\rho}
particles, we sample without replacement

N
individuals with the probability of picking an individual at position

y
being proportional to
e^{\beta y}
.

When
\rho=1
, the natural selection step becomes innocuous, keeping always the
N
right-most newly produced particles.
After the reproduction and truncation selection steps, the effect of the natural selection step is to bring the total population size back to its equilibrium
N
by selecting fit individuals
through a noisy mechanism parameterized by
\beta
. The larger the
\beta
is, the less noisy this procedure is, so that fittest (rightmost) particles do indeed
make it to the next generation. When
\beta=\infty
the natural selection step is “perfect” in the sense that the
N
right-most individuals are always chosen (thus coinciding with truncation selection of parameter
\rho=1
); whereas if
\beta=0
then
individuals are chosen uniformly at random, which corresponds to neutral evolution.
Thorough biological interpretations will be given in Section
3.1
.

The above model
interpolates between several models in the literature. The cases
\rho=\infty
and
\beta=\infty
, and
\rho=1
(
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the natural selection step is innocuous), are equivalent to the original integrable exponential model of
Brunet and Derrida
[
8
,
9
,
7
]
.
The case
\rho=\infty
and
\beta\in(1,\infty)
coincides with the model introduced by Cortines and Mallein in
[
12
]
, who find similar
phenomenology as in the Brunet and Derrida model. In this setting, they also proved that the natural selection step is not well defined whenever
\rho=\infty
and
\beta\leq 1
.
Here we avoid this impasse by introducing the truncation selection step parameterized by
\rho\geq 1
, and focus on the regime
\beta\in(0,1)
for
\rho\in(1,\infty)
.

3
Biological interpretation

3.1
Biological assumptions

The reproduction phase in our model incorporates both a reproduction and a mutation mechanisms, where daughter particles have a mutated fitness (position on the real line) centered around the fitness of the parent. Our choice of the exponential point process in the reproduction phase may seem artificial at first sight. Our motivation is two-fold. First,
as in
[
8
,
9
,
7
]
, the model turns out to be exactly solvable. Second, extremal theory states that the extremes of a long sequence of Gaussian random variables converges (after scaling and centering)
to the same exponential point process (see e.g. Example 1.1.7
[
24
]
). Since fitness waves are usually driven by extremal particles
[
1
]
, our model should be a good approximation of a model where individuals produce a large number of offspring with a Gaussian perturbation of their parental fitness.
For the original model in
[
8
,
9
,
7
]
, more “realistic” models (which do not rely on the assumption of an exponential birth process) have been shown to exhibit an analog qualitative behavior, see e.g.
[
1
]
.

The main objective of the truncation selection step is to fix the total carrying capacity of the environment, keeping the number of initial
offspring to be finite and fixed before the natural selection phase. Alternatively, this constraint could be enforced by assuming that every individual in the parental population has a fixed number of offspring. For instance, for every individual one can only retain the first
N^{\rho-1}
rightmost individuals in the PPP encoding the position of its offspring. By doing so the total number of offspring before the natural selection step is
N^{\rho}=N^{\rho-1}\times N
as in the present model.
This model corresponds to the case where the number of offspring produced during the reproductive phase is large (
r
-elected species
[
32
]
). Thus, our
\rho
parameter can be re-interpreted as a measure of the number of offspring per individual.
While this alternative model is presumably more realistic, it turns out to be much more challenging to solve than our model. However, the numerical simulations in Figure
2
show
that both models have similar qualitative behavior.

As mentioned above, the natural selection step is noisy and
the level of noise is captured by the
\beta
parameter of our model:
\beta=\infty
consists in selecting the
N
fittest individuals (no noise); whereas
\beta=0
consists in selecting individuals uniformly at random.
This feature of our model reflects that the survival of individuals depends not only on their
relative fitness but also on random and/or confounding factors.

3.2
Non linear response of evolution to natural selection

Our model predicts a complex response of evolutionary characteristics (speed of evolution and genetic diversity)
to the selection parameters
\rho
and
\beta
. Let us fix the carrying capacity
N^{\rho}
of the environment, so that the value of
\beta
can be directly interpreted as a measure of the strength of natural selection.

Speed of evolution.

As expected, the speed of evolution is increasing with
\beta
(Theorem
2.1
); however, the relationship between speed and
\beta
is highly non-linear (see right panel in Figure
3
).
As a matter of fact, as
N\to\infty
and as
\beta
increases,
the gain on the speed of evolution becomes more and more negligible.

Genetic diversity.
In population genetics, the effective population is often defined as

N_{e}:={\mathbbm{E}[T_{2}^{N}]}.

and measures the “depth” of the genealogy for two sampled individuals.
Assuming that neutral mutations accumulate at constant rate along the genome (as in the infinite sites model
[
53
,
37
]
)
the expected number of neutral genetic differences (e.g. single nucleotide polymorphisms) between two individuals is proportional to the effective population size
N_{e}

[
18
]
. As a consequence,
N_{e}
provides a measure of the expected neutral genetic diversity in the population.

As mentioned above, many results
[
21
,
25
,
26
,
49
]
, point to natural selection as one of the main causes of reduction of genetic diversity in a population.

Our model predicts that increasing the strength of selection may in fact induce a higher genetic diversity.
We show that
N_{e}

decreases as a function of
\beta
on
(0,\beta_{c})
but, surprisingly, it
increases
on the interval
(\beta_{c},\infty)
(see the left panel of Figure
3
and
Theorem
2.5
). Indeed, even if we observe a
general reduction of
N_{e}
when compared to neutral models –for which
N_{e}=\mathcal{O}(N)
–, in our model it is on the order of
N_{e}=\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
in the “weak selection regime” (
\beta\in(0,\beta_{c})
), and on the order of
\mathcal{O}(1)

in the “strong selection regime” (
\beta\in(\beta_{c},1)
).

3.3
Semi and fully pulled waves.

Let us now describe the phenomenology of the phase transition observed in our model in terms of traveling wave dynamics.

t

In other words, at any given time, individuals always descend from a highly ranked parent, that is when compared to the average rank (
N/2
) of an individual chosen uniformly at random.
Such propagation fronts are referred to as
“pulled”
waves, because the wave is pulled along by the action of individuals located to the right of the front
[
52
]
. Theorem
2.2
then uncovers a phase transition in the pulled regime which is new to our knowledge:

i)

In the fully pulled regime, the wave is pulled by the very extreme individuals: the parental rank is
O(1)
so that the wave at generation
t+1
is generated by the individuals close to the right edge at generation
t
.

ii)

In contrast, in the semi pulled regime, the wave is pulled by high ranked individuals, but those individuals are still far from the right edge: the parental rank is
\approx N^{\chi U}
.

Semi- and fully- pulled fronts are characterized by very distinct genealogical structures.
For semi-pulled waves,
the genealogy converges to the celebrated Bolthausen-Sznitman coalescent
[
6
,
35
,
42
]
.
This is consistent with previous findings in
[
9
,
7
]
where the natural selection step consists in deterministically selecting the
N
-fittest individuals after the reproduction phase (truncation selection only).
The same universal behavior has been found in many population models (see
[
47
,
12
]
for rigorous results, and

[
34
,
14
,
15
]
for non-rigorous arguments).

One of the interesting features of the present model is that the Bolthausen-Sznitman universal behavior breaks down when the selection noise below the critical parameter
\beta_{c}
and the transition from the semi to the fully pulled regime occurs. In the fully pulled regime, the genealogy then converges to a discrete time coalescent (Poisson-Dirichlet coalescent).

Let us now comment on the difference of time scale between the two regimes.
In the fully pulled regime, ancestral lineages only visit sites at distance
O(1)
from the front so that coalescence may occur with positive probability at every generation.
This implies that coalescence occurs on a time scale of order
1
and the limiting genealogy is in discrete time.

In contrast,
the semi-pulled regime is driven by extreme events of the rightmost particle.
If we trace the ancestry of a sampled individual backwards in time, his lineage will typically be located far from the edge (Theorem
2.2
ii)) where a coalescence with other lineages is unlikely to happen. However, at some exceptional past generation, the right-most individual of the fitness wave happens to have a much higher relative fitness than the rest of the population. At that time, some of the ancestral lineages will jump to the rightmost point of the traveling wave (
R_{2}^{N}\Longrightarrow 1
according to Theorem
2.5
ii)) and then coalesce.
As indicated by Theorem
2.5
ii), the typical time scale to reach such generations is of order
\log(N)>>1
and the limiting genealogy is now in continuous time.

Intricate phase transitions in front propagation have been observed in previous works.

In turn, the pushed regime is itself partitioned into 2 sub-regimes: the semi-pushed regime (

In the fully-pushed regime, the fluctuations at the edge of the front obey the central limit theorem and the genealogy is conjectured
[

When

Analogously to our model in the strong selection regime, ancestral lines at coalescent times are typically placed away from the bulk population and the genealogy is conjectured
[

Let us now compare the pushed regime for the noisy F-KPP equation with Allee effect described above with our results for pulled fitness waves.
Our model is always pulled.
However, and analogously to (

Analogously to the pushed case, we observe that the genealogical depth also reduces when transitioning from the fully-pulled to the semi-pulled regime.

6
Heuristics for the genealogy

In this section, we derive the guiding heuristics for the phase transition between the semi- and fully-pulled regimes. Since the genealogy is given by an exchangeable coalescent directed by
\eta^{N}
(Proposition

), the phase transition will emerge from the asymptotics of the mass partition

introduced in Definition

6.1
Fully-pulled regime.

Let
\beta<\beta_{c}(\rho)
and recall that
\alpha\equiv\alpha(\beta,\rho)
in (
7

\alpha>0
in this case.
Set

\forall y>0,\ \ L^{N}(y)\ =\ \#\{i:N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{i}^{N}}\geq y\},

Let us first interpret this quantity. Recall that

and

.
As a consequence,

can be interpreted as the number of elements in

that are selected in the
\beta
-sampling (natural selection)
and whose rank is smaller than

The guiding heuristic is that

in Definition
2.3

becomes equivalent in the limit as

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left(L^{N}(y)=n\right)\ \approx\ {N\choose n}p^{n}(1-p)^{N-n},\ \ \mbox{where $p=\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}w_{i}\geq y}w_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rho}}w_{i}^{\beta}}$.}

Since
\beta<1
and
\alpha>0
, and
w_{i}\approx 1/i
for large

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sum_{i\leq N^{\alpha}/y}\frac{1}{i^{\beta}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rho}}\frac{1}{i^{\beta}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{1}{y^{1-\beta}}\right)^{n}

so that, as
N\to\infty
and for fixed

,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left(L^{N}(y)=n\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}e^{-\frac{1}{y^{1-\beta}}}(\frac{1}{y^{1-\beta}})^{n}.

Recall
Z_{1}>\cdots Z_{N}>\cdots
the atoms of a PPP(
(1-\beta)\frac{dx}{x^{\beta-2}}
) arranged in decreasing order.
The previous computations show that

A generalization of the previous computation conducted in Lemma

N^{\alpha}(w_{\hat{I}^{N}_{1}},w_{\hat{I}^{N}_{2}},\cdots)\ \Longrightarrow(Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots),

so that the random mass partition can be approximated by

\bm{\eta}^{N}\approx\bm{\eta}^{\infty}=\left(\frac{Z_{i}}{\sum_{j}Z_{j}}\right).

The r.h.s. above, by Proposition 6 in
[
36
]
, has
Poisson-Dirichlet
(1-\beta,0)

The above approximation will entail the convergence of the
\bm{\eta}^{N}
-directed coalescent process

N\to\infty
.

6.2
Semi-pulled regime.

Let us now consider
\beta_{c}(\rho)<\beta<1
and recall that
\chi
in (

\chi\in(0,1]

For the sake of simplicity, let us again assume for the time being that the sampling of the indices in Definition

\displaystyle\forall i\leq N^{\chi},\ \eta^{N}_{i}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{e^{-(z_{i}^{\beta-1}-z_{i-1}^{\beta-1})}\left(z_{i}^{\beta-1}-\left(z_{i-1}\right)^{\beta-1}\right)^{n_{i}}}{n_{i}!}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}\left(Z([z_{i},z_{i-1}))=n_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq\ell\right).

Thus we have the a.s. limit

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{P}\left(L^{N}_{i}\geq i\text{ for }1\leq i\leq\ell\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}\left(Z([z_{i},z_{i-1}))\geq i\text{ for }1\leq i\leq\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}\left(Z_{1}\geq z_{1},\dots,Z_{\ell}\geq z_{\ell}\right),

so that (

) holds by Lemma
A.1
.

It remains to prove (
13

(14)

Lemma 7.5
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathbbm{P}\bigg(\#\left\{k\in{\cal K}^{N}\colon w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha+\delta}}\right\}=0\bigg)\to 1\ \ \ \mbox{as $N\to\infty$}.

Proof.

By Markov’s inequality and dominated convergence it is sufficient to find
\delta>0
such that

\mathbbm{E}\left[\#\left\{k\in K^{N}\colon w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha+\delta}}\right\}\wedge 1\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right]\to 0\ \ \mbox{a.s. when $N\to\infty$.}

Recall that
J^{N}
denotes the indices sampled with replacement (with possible repetition), and that
\#J^{N}
is the number of distinct
indices in
J^{N}
. Given
J^{N}
, the probability that
k\notin J^{N}

is in
{\cal K}^{N}
is always bounded from above by

\frac{w^{\beta}_{k}}{\sum_{j=N}^{[N^{\rho}]}w_{j}^{\beta}}
.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\#\left\{k\in K^{N}\colon w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha+\delta}}\right\}\ \ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(N-\#J^{N})\sum_{k:w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\delta+\alpha}}}\frac{w^{\beta}_{k}}{{\sum_{j=N}^{N^{\rho}}w_{j}^{\beta}}}.

Integrating over
J^{N}
, and using the fact that
kw_{k}\to 1
a.s., we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\#\left\{k\in K^{N}\colon w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha+\delta}}\right\}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sum_{j=N}^{N^{\rho}}w_{j}^{\beta}}\mathbbm{E}\bigg[N-\#J^{N}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\bigg]\ \sum_{k:w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\delta+\alpha}}}w^{\beta}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\bigg[N-\#J^{N}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\bigg]\frac{N^{(\delta+\alpha)(1-\beta)}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}}.

In order to evaluate the expected value on the r.h.s., we will use the simple bound

N-\#J^{N}\ \leq\ \sum_{(i,j)\in[N]:i<j}1_{J_{i}^{N}=J_{j}^{N}}.

We use again the fact that the probability that
w_{k}

is sampled in
J^{N}
at any step of the procedure is bounded from above by

\frac{w^{\beta}_{k}}{\sum_{j=N}^{[N^{\rho}]}w_{j}^{\beta}}
.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigg[N-\#J^{N}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in[N]:i<j}\mathbbm{P}({J_{i}^{N}=J_{j}^{N}}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i})=\sum_{(i,j)\in[N]:i<j}\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\mathbbm{P}\left({J_{i}^{N}=J_{j}^{N}=k}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\frac{w_{k}^{2\beta}}{\left(\sum_{i=N}^{N^{\rho}}w_{i}^{\beta}\right)^{2}}.

Combining this with the previous estimates and using again the fact that
kw_{k}\to 1
, there exists a deterministic constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\#\left\{k\in K^{N}\colon w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha+\delta}}\right\}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\left(\frac{N^{\rho(1-2\beta)_{+}}\vee\log(N)}{N^{2\rho(1-\beta)}}\right)\left(\frac{N^{(\alpha+\delta)(1-\beta)}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\limsup_{N\to\infty}N\left(\frac{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}}{N^{2\rho(1-\beta)}}\right)N^{\delta(1-\beta)}=c\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{1-\rho(1-\beta)+\delta(1-\beta)}.

In the fully-pulled regime recall that
1-\rho(1-\beta)<0
.
Thus, we can choose
\delta>0
small enough such that the exponent on the r.h.s. remains negative. This completes the proof of the Lemma.

8
Proofs for the semi-pulled regime.

In this section we assume throughout that
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho)=1-\frac{1}{\rho}
, and recall that then

\chi\equiv\chi(\beta,\rho)=\frac{1-\rho(1-\beta)}{\beta}\in(0,1].

8.1
Selection of the fittest individuals.

In this section we prove that all the high fitness children positioned above level
\approx N^{-\chi}

are all chosen during the selection step with probability converging to 1 as
N\to\infty

(Proposition
8.1
); whereas the “aggregated” fitness
of individuals below level
\approx N^{-\chi}
becomes negligible as
N\to\infty

(Proposition
8.3
). We will use these two heuristics in Section
8.2

in order to “reduce” the dynamics of our model
to those of the original exponential model of Brunet, Derrida et al
[
8
,
9
,
7
]
with an effective population size equal to

N^{\chi}
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
0<\epsilon<\chi
. Define the event

A_{N,\epsilon}\equiv A_{N}\ :=\ \left\{\left\{i\ :\ w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}\right\}\subseteq I^{N}\right\},

then

\exists\eta>0\colon\hskip 9.24994pt\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{\eta}\mathbbm{P}\left(A_{N,\epsilon}^{c}\right)=0.

Proof.

For every
i\in[N^{\rho}]

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\bigg(i\notin I^{N}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\bigg)\ \leq\ \exp\left\{N\ln\left(1-\frac{w_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{j}^{\beta}}\right)\right\}\ \ \leq\ \exp\left\{-\frac{Nw_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{j}^{\beta}}\right\}.

Summing over
i
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left(A_{N,\epsilon}^{c}\ |\ (w_{i})_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\ :\ w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}\exp(-\frac{Nw_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{j}^{\beta}}).

(21)

Using the large deviation bound of Lemma
A.2
, for every
C>1-\beta

we have

{\forall\eta>0,\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbbm{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{j}^{\beta}\leq C^{-1}N^{\rho(1-\beta)}\right)=o\left(N^{-\eta}\right).}

Gathering the previous observations, we need to show that as
N\to\infty
, we have

{\exists\eta>0\colon\hskip 9.24994ptN^{\eta}\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}\exp\left(-C\frac{Nw_{i}^{\beta}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}}\right)\right]\ \to\ 0.}

Indeed, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}\exp\left(-C\frac{Nw_{i}^{\beta}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}^{\infty}\exp(-C\frac{Nx^{\beta}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}})\frac{dx}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}^{\infty}\exp(-CN^{\beta\chi}x^{\beta})\frac{dx}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=N^{\chi}\int_{N^{\epsilon}}^{\infty}\exp(-Cy^{\beta})\frac{dy}{y^{2}}{=\mathcal{O}(N^{\chi-\epsilon}e^{-CN^{\epsilon\beta}})}.

∎

Corollary 8.2
.

Let

\bar{A}_{N}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}>\frac{\chi}{2}\log(N)\right\}

then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\mathbbm{P}(\bar{A}_{N}^{c})=0.

Proof.

By Proposition
8.1
we may work on the set
A_{N}
on which we have

\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}\geq\sum_{w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}w_{i}
.
Let
a_{N}=\frac{\chi}{2}\log(N)
.
Then
by Campbell’s formula combined with Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\mathbbm{P}\left(\bar{A}_{N}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\mathbbm{P}\left(\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}w_{i}\leq a_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\exp(a_{N})\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\exp(-\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}w_{i})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\exp(a_{N})\exp\left(\int_{\frac{1}{N^{\chi-\epsilon}}}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{2}}(\exp(-x)-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\exp(a_{N})\exp\bigg(-\log(N^{\chi-\epsilon})+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)\bigg).

(22)

Take
\epsilon
small enough such that
\chi-\epsilon>\frac{\chi}{2}
, it is straightforward to check that the last term above converges to

as
N\to\infty
.
∎

.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)

B_{N,\epsilon}:=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{I^{N}_{k}>N^{(\chi+\epsilon)}}<N^{-\epsilon\beta/2}\right\}.

Lemma 8.5

Recall

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{N}\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{i}^{2-\epsilon}\right]}{\left(\chi\log(N)/2\right)^{2-\epsilon}}+o\left(\log(N)^{-1}\right).

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
a_{N}\to 0
a non-negative deterministic constant converging to

\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{w_{j}}{a_{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\right)^{b}\right]=\frac{1}{b-1}.

Proof.

First observe that

\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{w_{j}}{a_{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\right)^{b}\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\frac{a_{N}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}+1}\right)^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{w_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\right)^{b}\right].

Second, by Markov’s inequality followed by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left(\frac{a_{N}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}a_{N}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\right]=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{a_{N}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}k^{-1}}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}k^{-1}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}kk^{-1}}\right]

where
\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{k}^{-1}\right]=k

Thus, intersecting with the set
\left\{\frac{a_{N}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\leq\epsilon\right\}

and using (29) in
[
7

a_{N}=0

\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\log(N)\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\frac{a_{N}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}+1}\right)^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{w_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}}\right)^{b}\right]

We then obtain an inequality by taking

.

Let
a>0,b\geq 0
. Assume that
A=a+\beta-1<0
.
Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-\chi\beta+bA}\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}^{N}}\geq\frac{1}{N^{b}}}\right]\ \leq\ (1-\beta)\frac{1}{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}.

Proof.

This can be proved analogously to the previous result by Lemma
A.2
.
By the same argument as in the previous corollary, intersecting again with the set

\bar{E}_{N}=\left\{\sum_{i=N}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{i}^{\beta}\leq c^{-1}N^{\rho(1-\beta)}\right\}
, but now using the trivial bound

\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}^{N}}\geq N^{-b}}\leq Nw_{1}^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{1}\geq\frac{1}{N^{b}}}

instead of (
35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{N}w^{a}_{I^{N}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I^{N}_{k}}>\frac{1}{N^{b}}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq N\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\ w^{a}_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{k}>\frac{1}{N^{b}}}\ \frac{w_{k}^{\beta}}{\sum_{j=N}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}w_{j}^{\beta}},\bar{E}_{N}^{c}\right]\ +\ N\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{1}^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{1}>N^{-b}},\bar{E}_{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq cN^{1-\rho(1-\beta)}\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\ w^{a+\beta}_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{k}>\frac{1}{N^{b}}}\right]+N\left\lVert w_{1}^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{1}>N^{-b}}\right\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\left(\mathbbm{P}\left(\bar{E}_{N}\right)\right)^{1/2}.

Using Lemma
5.2
we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{1}^{2a}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{1}>N^{-b}}\right]=\int_{N^{-b}}^{\infty}x^{-2a}e^{-x}dx
which is of polynomial order on
N
for any choice of
a
.
Thus Lemma
A.2
acertains that the second term above is of order
o\left(N^{\chi\beta-bA}\right)
.

For the first term we compute, using Lemma
5.2
and the hypothesis
A<0
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\ w^{a+\beta}_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{k}>\frac{1}{N^{b}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{N^{b}}}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{2-\alpha+\beta}}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}N^{-bA}

and the Corollary follows.
∎

Remark 1
.

Corollaries
A.4
and
A.5
remain valid if we replace the sampled indices
I^{N}

(sampled without replacement) by a sample
J^{N}
with replacement from
[N^{\rho}]
with the same weights

\left(w_{i}^{\beta}\right)_{i=1}^{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}
as for
I^{N}
. Indeed, this follows by observing that
even in this case the
key bound in (
34
) remains valid.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
(E_{N})
be a sequence of events such that, for some
\eta^{\prime}>0
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{\eta^{\prime}}\mathbbm{P}(E_{N})\ =\ 0.

Then, for every
\eta>0

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\bigg[|\log(\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}})|^{\eta};E_{N}\bigg]\ =\ 0.

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
be fixed. Since
Nw_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\leq Nw_{1}

we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta};E_{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(Nw_{1});\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}>1,E_{N}\right]+\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(w^{-1}_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}/N);\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\leq 1,E_{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(Nw_{1});Nw_{1}>1,E_{N}\right]+\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(w_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}^{-1}/N);w^{-1}_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}/N\geq 1,E_{N}\right]

(36)

We first deal with the first term on the r.h.s. of the inequality.
Let
\epsilon>0
. There exists a constant
c_{\epsilon,\eta}
such that for every
x\geq 1
, we have
\log^{\eta}(x)\leq c_{\epsilon,\eta}w^{\epsilon}
.
By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(Nw_{1});Nw_{1}>1,E_{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{\epsilon,\eta}N^{\epsilon}\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{1}^{\epsilon};E_{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{\epsilon,\eta}N^{\epsilon}\mathbbm{E}\left[w_{1}^{2\epsilon}\right]^{1/2}\mathbbm{P}(E_{N})^{1/2}.

Recall that
w_{1}
is identical to the inverse of a standard exponential r.v. As a consequence, the latter expectation is finite for
\epsilon<1/2
. Further, picking
\epsilon
small enough such that
\epsilon<2\eta^{\prime}
,
the assumption of our lemma implies that
N^{\epsilon}\mathbbm{P}(E_{N})^{1/2}\to 0
so that the first term on the r.h.s. of (
36
) must go to
0
.

We turn to the second term.
By a similar argument as before, for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\log^{\eta}(w_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}^{-1}/N);w^{-1}_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}/N\geq 1,E_{N}\right]\leq\frac{c_{\epsilon}}{N^{\epsilon}}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{1}{{w_{N^{\rho}}^{2\epsilon}}}\right]^{1/2}\mathbbm{P}(E_{N})^{1/2}

Lemma
5.2
together with Stirling’s approximation yield

\left\lVert w_{\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil}^{-\epsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{\Gamma(\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil+2\epsilon)}{\Gamma(\lceil N^{\rho}\rceil)}\sim N^{\rho 2\epsilon}
. Thus, taking
\epsilon
such that
0<\epsilon(\rho-1)<2\eta^{\prime}
, the second term in (
36
) is also vanishing.
∎

Appendix B
Uniform integrability

Proposition B.1
.

The collection of r.vs.
\left\{\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}N^{\alpha}w_{I^{N}_{k}}\right)\right\}_{N}
is
uniformly integrable.

The following easy-to-derive technical lemma will be used in the upcoming proof of Proposition
B.1
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\eta>1
and
\delta>0
. Then there exists
c>0
such that

\forall b\geq 1,x\geq 0,\ \ \log^{\eta}(b+x)\leq cb^{\delta}+cx.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

This boils down to proving that for some
\eta>1
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta};N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}<1\right]<\infty

(37)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta};N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I^{N}_{k}}>1\right]<\infty.

(38)

(See e.g. (3.18) in
[
3
]
.)

Step 1.
We start by proving (
37
).
Let
E_{N}
be an arbitrary subset such that, for some
\eta^{\prime}>0
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ N^{\eta^{\prime}}\mathbbm{P}(E_{N})\ =\ 0.

(39)

From Lemma
A.6
, it is enough to show that for such
E_{N}
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\ \mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta},\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ N^{\alpha}w_{I_{k}}\leq 1,E_{N}^{c}\right]\ <\ \infty.

To ease the notation, we write
J^{N}\equiv J
and
I^{N}\equiv I
. We also use the same notation for the ordered set
\hat{I}
and
\hat{J}
. First,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta};\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ N^{\alpha}w_{I_{k}}\leq 1,E_{N}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\left(-\log\left(N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\right)\right)^{\eta};{N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\leq 1,E_{N}^{c}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{P}\bigg(-\log\left(N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\right)\geq u^{\frac{1}{\eta}},N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\leq 1,E_{N}^{c}\bigg)du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{P}\bigg(N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\leq\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}}),E_{N}^{c}\bigg)du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{P}\bigg(N^{\alpha}w_{\hat{J}_{1}}\leq\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}}),E_{N}^{c}\bigg)du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}w_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rho}}w_{i}^{\beta}}\right)^{N};E_{N}^{c}\right]du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\exp\left(-N\frac{\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}w_{i}^{\beta}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rho}}w_{i}^{\beta}}\right);E_{N}^{c}\right]du,

where for the third inequality we have used the stochastic inequality

\max_{1\leq k\leq N}N^{\alpha}w_{J_{k}}\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq N}N^{\alpha}w_{I_{k}}

(see our coupling
(I^{N},J^{N})\equiv(I,J)
described at the beginning of Section
7
.)

We now handle the expectation appearing in the r.h.s. of
(
B
)
. Let
2c\in(0,1-\beta)
and define

E_{N}\ =\ \left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rho}}w_{i}^{\beta}>c^{-1}N^{\rho(1-\beta)}\right\}.

By Lemma
A.3
, (
39
) is satisfied. Further, using (
B
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lvert\log\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\right)\right\rvert^{\eta};\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ N^{\alpha}w_{I_{k}}\leq 1,E_{N}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\exp(-cN\frac{\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}w_{i}^{\beta}}{N^{\rho(1-\beta)}})\right]du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\exp(-cN^{\chi\beta}\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}w_{i}^{\beta})\right]du.

Define
\bar{c}=\min_{(0,1]}\frac{1}{x^{\beta}}(1-\exp(-cx^{\beta}))>0
.
The latter, together with Campbell’s formula and Lemma
5.2
, and the inequality

N^{\chi-\alpha}\leq 1
in the last line, yield

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\exp(-cN^{\chi\beta}\sum_{i:w_{i}\geq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}w_{i}^{\beta})\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\int_{\frac{\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}{N^{\alpha}}}^{\infty}\left(e^{-c(N^{\chi}x)^{\beta}}-1\right)\frac{dx}{x^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(N^{\chi}\int_{\frac{N^{\chi}}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}^{\infty}\left(e^{-cx^{\beta}}-1\right)\frac{dx}{x^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(N^{\chi}\int_{\frac{N^{\chi}}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}^{1}\left(e^{-cx^{\beta}}-1\right)\frac{dx}{x^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(-\bar{c}N^{\chi}\int_{\frac{N^{\chi}}{N^{\alpha}}\exp(-u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^{2-\beta}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\frac{\bar{c}}{1-\beta}\exp((1-\beta)u^{\frac{1}{\eta}})).

Finally, (
37
) follows from the observation that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\bar{c}}{1-\beta}\exp\left((1-\beta)u^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\right)\bigg)du<\infty.

Step 2.
We now prove (
38
).
Let
\delta>0
. By Lemma

, we can pick
c>0
to ensure that

\forall b\geq 1,x\geq 0,\ \ \log^{\eta}(b+x)\leq cb^{\delta}+cx.

Then, on the set
\{N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}>1\}
, and using the fact that
\log(x)\leq x
for
x>0
and

\log^{\eta}(x)\leq\tilde{c}x

\displaystyle\log^{\eta}\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{w_{\hat{I}_{1}}>N^{-\alpha}}\log^{\eta}\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}>N^{-\alpha}}\ +\ N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}\leq N^{-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbbm{1}_{w_{\hat{I}_{1}}\leq N^{-\alpha}}\log^{\eta}\left(N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}\leq N^{-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq cN^{\delta\alpha}(\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}>N^{-\alpha}})^{\delta}+(c+\tilde{c})N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}\leq N^{-\alpha}}.

First, a direct application of Corollary

with

\displaystyle N^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}\big[w_{I_{k}};w_{I_{k}}\leq N^{-\alpha}\big]\ =\ \mathcal{O}(1).

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[N^{\delta\alpha}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{I_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{w_{I_{k}}>N^{-\alpha}}\big)^{\delta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(N^{\alpha\delta+1-\rho(1-\beta)+\alpha(1-\beta-\delta)}\right)\ =\ \mathcal{O}(1).
