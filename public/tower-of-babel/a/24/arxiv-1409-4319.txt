Topological actions of wreath products
Sergiy Maksymenko

Abstract
. Let G G and H H be two groups acting on path connected topological spaces X X and Y Y respectively. Assume that H H is finite of order m m and the quotient maps p : X → X / G p:X\to X/G and q : Y → Y / H q:Y\to Y/H are regular coverings. Then it is well-known that the wreath product G ≀ H G\wr H naturally acts on W = X m × Y W=X^{m}\times Y , so that the quotient map r : W → W / ( G ≀ H ) r:W\to W/(G\wr H) is also a regular covering. We give an explicit description of π 1 ​ ( W / ( G ≀ H ) ) \pi_{1}(W/(G\wr H)) as a certain wreath product π 1 ​ ( X / G ) ≀ ∂ Y π 1 ​ ( Y / H ) \pi_{1}(X/G)\,\wr_{\partial_{Y}}\pi_{1}(Y/H) corresponding to a non-effective action of π 1 ​ ( Y / H ) \pi_{1}(Y/H) on the set of maps H → π 1 ​ ( X / G ) H\to\pi_{1}(X/G) via the boundary homomorphism ∂ Y : π 1 ​ ( Y / H ) → H \partial_{Y}:\pi_{1}(Y/H)\to H of the covering map q q . Such a statement is known and usually exploited only when X X and Y Y are contractible, in which case W W is also contractible, and thus W / ( G ≀ H ) W/(G\wr H) is the classifying space of G ≀ H G\wr H . The applications are given to the computation of the homotopy types of orbits of typical smooth functions f f on orientable compact surfaces M M with respect to the natural right action of the groups 𝒟 ⁡ ( M ) \mathcal{D}(M) of diffeomorphisms of M M on 𝒞 ∞ ​ ( M , ℝ ) \mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}) .

2.1.
Homotopies in spaces of maps

Exponential law

Let
T,X
be topological spaces.
By
\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
we will denote the set of all continuous maps from
T
to
X
.
A
compact open topology
on
\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
is a topology generated by sets of the form
[K,V]=\{f\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)\mid f(K)\subset V\}
, where
K
runs over all compact subsets of
T
and
V
runs over all open subsets of
X
.

If
S
is one more topological space and
F\colon S\times T\to X
is a continuous map, then for each
s\in S
we define the map
F_{s}\colon T\to X
by
F_{s}(t)=F(s,t)
,
t\in T
.
The following well known statement is called the
exponential law
:

Lemma 2.1.1

(e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 3.4.8]
)
.

Let
S
,
T
,
X
be topological spaces such that
S
is Hausdorff and
T
is locally compact and Hausdorff.
Then we have a well-defined homeomorphism (called the
exponential map
):

E\colon\mathcal{C}\bigl(S\times T,X\bigr)\to\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)\bigr),\qquad E(F)(s)=F_{s},

where
F\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(S\times T,X\bigr)
,
s\in S
, and all the spaces of continuous maps are endowed with the corresponding compact open topologies.
∎

Given a subset
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
, define the following subset of
\mathcal{C}\bigl(S\times T,X\bigr)
:

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X):=\{F\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(S\times T,X\bigr)\mid F_{s}\in\mathcal{U}\ \text{for all $s\in S$}\}.

Corollary 2.1.2
.

Under assumptions of Lemma
2.1.1
, for every subset
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
, endowed with the induced compact open topology, the map
E
yields a homeomorphism

E\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)\to\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr),

where
\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
is regarded as a subset of
\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)\bigr)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
E\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)\bigr)=\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)
, so
F_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
for all
s\in S
.
Then by Lemma
2.1.1
the map
f=E(F)\colon S\to\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
,
f(s)=F_{s}
, is continuous.
Moreover,
f(S)\subset\mathcal{U}
, by assumption on
F
, i.e.
f\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
.
Thus,
E\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)\bigr)\subset\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
.

Conversely, let
f\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
.
Then by Lemma
2.1.1
,
f=E(F)
, where
F\in\mathcal{C}\bigl(S\times T,X\bigr)
is given by
F(s,t)=f(s)(t)
.
In particular,
F_{s}=f(s)\in\mathcal{U}
for all
s\in S
, so
F\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)
.
Thus,
E\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(S\times T,X)\bigr)=\mathcal{C}\bigl(S,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
.
∎

Paths and homotopies

Let
I=[0;1]
.
Every continuous map
{\alpha}:I\to X
is called a
path
in
X
.
In this case the points
{\alpha}(0),{\alpha}(1)\in X
are the

of
{\alpha}
, and one also says that
{\alpha}(0)
and
{\alpha}(1)
are
connected by the path
{\alpha}
.
If
{\alpha}(0)={\alpha}(1)
, then
{\alpha}
is a
loop
at the point
{\alpha}(0)
.
Also, given two subsets
A,B\subset X
, denote by

\mathcal{P}(X,A,B):=\mathcal{C}\bigl((I,0,1),(X,A,B)\bigr)

the set of all paths
{\alpha}\colon I\to X
such that
{\alpha}(0)\in A
and
{\alpha}(1)\in B
.

Notice that the relation on
X
to ‘‘
be connected by a path
’’ is an equivalence relation.
The corresponding equivalence classes are called

of
X
, and the set of all such classes is denoted by
\pi_{0}X
.

A

is merely a continuous map
F\colon I\times T\to X
of a topological product of

by the segment
I
.
In this case, it is also said that

is a
homotopy between the maps
F_{0},F_{1}\colon T\to X
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
be a subset.
Then, by definition, each element
F\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(I\times T,X)
is a homotopy
F\colon I\times T\to X
satisfying

for all

.
We will call such

a

\mathcal{U}
.
Also, say that two maps
f,g\in\mathcal{U}
are

\mathcal{U}
if there exists a homotopy
F
in
\mathcal{U}
such that

and

.
Evidently, the relation to ‘‘
be homotopic in
\mathcal{U}
’’ is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{U}
, and we will denote by
\pi(\mathcal{U})
the corresponding set of equivalence classes, called

.

Corollary 2.1.3
.

Suppose
T
is locally compact and Hausdorff.
Then for every subset
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{C}\bigl(T,X\bigr)
the exponential map
E
induces a bijection

(2.1)

\pi(\mathcal{U})\equiv\pi_{0}\mathcal{U}

between the set homotopy classes of maps in
\mathcal{U}
and the set of path components of
\mathcal{U}
endowed with the compact open topology.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.1.2
, we have a homeomorphism
E\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}(I\times T,X)\cong\mathcal{C}\bigl(I,\mathcal{U}\bigr)
of the
space of homotopies in
\mathcal{U}
onto
the space of paths in
\mathcal{U}
.
In particular, two maps

are homotopic in
\mathcal{U}
if and only if they are conected by a path in
\mathcal{U}
, i.e. belong to the same path component of

.
This gives the required bijection (

∎

Let

,

be two collections of subsets, and
\mathcal{U}:=\mathcal{C}\bigl((T,T_{k},\ldots,T_{1}),(X,X_{k},\ldots,X_{1})\bigr)
be the subspace of

consisting of maps

of

for all

Then the corresponding set of homotopy classes

is usually denoted by

[(T,T_{k},\ldots,T_{1}),(X,X_{k},\ldots,X_{1})]
.

Homotopy groups and sets

For the convenience of the reader not familiar with homotopy groups we will briefly recall their definition, see e.g.
[
15
, Chapter 4.1]
, and further discuss the cases when they are not actually groups.

For
n\geq 1
let
D^{n}
be the unit
n
-disk in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
centered at the origin,
S^{n-1}=\partial D^{n}
be the corresponding
n
-sphere, and
q=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in S^{n-1}
.
Identifying
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times 0\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
we can assume that
q
is a common point of all those spheres and disks, and therefore we will use the same point
q
for all

.

Now let
X
be a topological space,
A\subset X
a subset, and
x\in A
a point.
Then the following sets of homotopy classes:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(X,x)

\displaystyle:=[(S^{n},q),(X,x)],\ n\geq 0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(X,A,x)

\displaystyle:=[(D^{n},S^{n-1},q),(X,A,x)],\ n\geq 1,

are called the
n
-th homotopy set
of
X
and
(X,A)
respectively at the point
x
.
Notice that there is an infinite (to the left) sequence of maps called the
long exact sequence of
(X,A,x)
, e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
:

\cdots\to\pi_{n}(A,x)\xrightarrow{i_{n}}\pi_{n}(X,x)\xrightarrow{j_{n}}\pi_{n}(X,A,x)\xrightarrow{\partial_{n}}\pi_{n-1}(A,x)\to\cdots\\
\cdots\to\pi_{1}(A,x)\xrightarrow{i_{1}}\pi_{1}(X,x)\xrightarrow{j_{1}}\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\pi_{0}(A,x)\xrightarrow{i_{0}}\pi_{0}(X,x)

and defined as follows.
Let
i:A\subset X
be a natural inclusion.

•

Then
i_{n}
associates to the homotopy class of each map
\gamma\colon(S^{n},q)\to(A,x)
the homotopy class of the composition
i\circ\gamma\colon(S^{n},q)\xrightarrow{\gamma}(A,x)\xrightarrow{i}(X,x)
.

•

Also, note that there is a continuous map
\phi\colon D^{n}\to S^{n}
such that
\phi(\partial D^{n})=q
and
\phi
homemorphically maps
\mathrm{Int}{D^{n}}
onto
S^{n}\setminus q
.
In particular,
\phi
can be regarded as a map of triples
\phi\colon(D^{n},S^{n-1},q)\to(S^{n},q,q)
.
Then
j_{n}
associates to the homotopy class of each map of pairs
\gamma\colon(S^{n},q)\to(X,x)
the homotopy class of the following composition regarded as a map of triples:

\gamma\circ\phi\colon(D^{n},S^{n-1},q)\xrightarrow{\phi}(S^{n},q,q)\xrightarrow{\gamma}(X,A,x).

•

Finally,
\partial_{n}
associates to the homotopy class of the map of triples
\gamma\colon(D^{n},S^{n-1},q)\to(X,A,x)
the homotopy class of its restriction
\gamma|_{(S^{n-1},q)}\colon(S^{n-1},q)\to(A,x)
regarded as a map of pairs.

It is known that the above long sequence is
exact
in the ‘‘homotopy’’ sense, i.e. if
P\xrightarrow{a}Q\xrightarrow{b}R
is a part of that sequence and
\gamma\in Q
is any element, then
b(\gamma)
is homotopic in
R
to a constant map if and only if
\gamma=a(\beta)
for some
\beta\in P
.

Moreover, the sets
\pi_{n}(A,x)
,
\pi_{n}(X,x)
for
n\geq 1
and
\pi_{n}(X,A,x)
for

have natural group structures such that the maps
i_{n}
for
n\geq 1
and
j_{n},\partial_{n}
for
n\geq 2
are homomorphisms and the above infinite sequence is also
exact
in the ‘‘algebraic’’ sense up to the term
\pi_{1}(X,x)
, i.e. if
P\xrightarrow{a}Q\xrightarrow{b}R
is a part of that sequence, and
Q
stands before
\pi_{1}(X,x)
in that sequence, then
\mathrm{image}(a)=\ker(b)
.

The reader is referred to
[
15
, Chapter 4.1]
for the definition of multiplication in those homotopy sets, and we will recall now only the multiplication in
\pi_{1}(X,x)
and discuss other homotopy sets of dimension
1
and
0
.
It will be convenient to replace in the definitions
D^{1}=[-1;1]
with
I=[0;1]
.
Then
S^{0}:=\partial I=\{0,1\}
.

Fundamental group
\pi_{1}(X,x)

Define the following map
\phi\colon I\to S^{1}
,
\phi(t)=(\cos(2\pi t),\sin(2\pi t))
.
Evidently,
\phi(0)=\phi(1)=q
, and
\phi
homeomorphically maps
(0;1)
onto
S^{1}\setminus q
.
One easily checks that we have a bijection

\displaystyle\phi_{*}\colon\mathcal{C}\bigl((S^{1},q),(X,x)\bigr)\to\mathcal{C}\bigl((I,\partial I),(X,x)\bigr)\equiv\mathcal{P}(X,x,x),

\displaystyle\phi_{*}(\gamma)=\gamma\circ\phi\colon I\xrightarrow{\phi}S^{1}\xrightarrow{\gamma}X,

which also induces a bijection on the corresponding homotopy classes of maps.
Hence, we get another description of
\pi_{1}(X,x)
as the set of homotopy classes of
loops at
x
:

\pi_{1}(X,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:pinXx}}}{{:=}}[(S^{1},q),(X,x)]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi_{*}}}{{\equiv}}[(I,\partial I),(X,x)].

Say that two paths
{\alpha},{\beta}\colon I\to X
are
composable
, if
{\alpha}(1)={\beta}(0)
.
Then for a pair of composable paths
{\alpha},{\beta}\colon I\to X
one can define their

{\alpha}\,\sharp\,{\beta}\colon I\to X
by the following standard formula:

(2.4)

({\alpha}\,\sharp\,{\beta})(t)=\begin{cases}{\alpha}(2t),&t\in[0;\tfrac{1}{2}],\\
{\beta}(2t-1),&t\in[\tfrac{1}{2};1].\end{cases}

In particular, if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}(X,x,x)
are
loops
at
x
, then
{\alpha}\,\sharp\,{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}(X,x,x)
is also a loop at
x
.
It is well known and is easy to see that the homotopy class of
{\alpha}\,\sharp\,{\beta}
in
\mathcal{P}(X,x,x)
depends only on the homotopy classes of
\alpha
and
\beta
, and the operation of composition of loops induces a certain
group
operation on the set
\pi_{1}(X,x)
of their homotopy classes.
The group
\pi_{1}(X,x)
is called the

of
X
at
x
.

However, in general, neither of the latter three terms of the long exact sequence:

(2.5)

\cdots\xrightarrow{j_{1}}\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\pi_{0}(A,x)\xrightarrow{i_{0}}\pi_{0}(X,x)

has some natural group structure under which the corresponding arrows are homomorphisms.

The set
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)

Evidently, for
n=1
the general definition (
2.3
) reduces to the following one:
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)=[(I,1,0),(X,A,x)]
.
Note that, if

are two paths started at

and finished in

, then in general,
{\alpha}
and

are not composable.
This illustrates an absence of some ‘‘evident’’ group structure on

related with compositions of paths.

The set
\pi_{0}(X,x)

Due to definition (
2.2
),
\pi_{0}(X,x):=[(S^{0},q),(X,x)]
.
Since every continuous map
\gamma\colon(S^{0},0)\to(X,x)
sends

to

, it is uniquely determined by its value

.
Moreover, the correspondence
\mathcal{C}\bigl((S^{0},0),(X,x)\bigr)\to X

\gamma\mapsto\gamma(1)
, is evidently a bijection.
Also, if

is a homotopy in

, then

, and

is just a path between two points

and

.
This implies that

can be identified with the set of path components of

.

From that point of view the presence of a base point

in

might look artificial.
Therefore, sometimes it is omitted and this agrees with the notation
\pi_{0}X
from Section

.
However, for the ‘‘exactness’’ of the above long exact sequence of

the base point is essential.

Let us also describe the maps from (
2.5
).

•

The mapping

associates to each loop
\alpha\colon(I,\partial I)\to(X,q)
at

the homotopy class of
\alpha
regarded as a path
\alpha\colon(I,1,0)\to(X,A,x)
.

•

The map
\partial_{1}\colon\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\to\pi_{0}(A,x)
associates to each homotopy class of a path
\gamma:(I,1,0)\to(X,A,x)
the path component of
A
of the point
\gamma(1)\in A
.

•

Finally, the map
i_{0}\colon\pi_{0}(A,x)\to\pi_{0}(X,x)
associates to each path component

of

containing

.

Corollary 2.1.4
.

For a subset
A\subset X
and a point

there are the following identifications:

\displaystyle\pi_{0}\mathcal{P}(X,x,A)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}(X,A,x),

\displaystyle\pi_{0}\Omega(X,x)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}(X,x),

where

is the space of loops at

.

The proof is a direct consequence of definitions and (

\pi_{1}(X,A,x):=[(I,1,0),(X,A,x)]\equiv\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,x,A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:Pi0U_pi0U}}}{{=}}\pi_{0}\mathcal{P}(X,x,A).

\pi_{0}(X,x):=[(I,1,0),(X,x,x)]\equiv\pi(\Omega(X,x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:Pi0U_pi0U}}}{{=}}\pi_{0}\Omega(X,x).

∎

2.2.
Group structure on
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)

We will consider here several situations in which
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)
,
\pi_{0}(X,x)
,
\pi_{0}(A,x)
are groups and the corresponding arrows between them are homomorphisms.

Example 2.2.1
.

Let
X
be a topological group.
Then the path component
X_{e}
of the unit
e
of
X
is a normal subgroup of
X
, and one can naturally identify
\pi_{0}X
with the quotient group
X/X_{e}
.
One can also define a point-wise multiplication of loops
\alpha,\beta\colon(I,\partial I)\to(X,e)
, and it is well known and is easy to see that on the level of homotopy classes that multiplication coincides with the multiplication in
\pi_{1}(X,e)
.
More generally, if
A\subset X
is a subgroup, then one can also define a point-wise multiplication of paths
\alpha,\beta\colon(I,0,1)\to(X,e,A)
and on the level of homotopy classes this turns
\pi_{1}(X,A,e)=\pi_{0}\mathcal{P}(X,e,A)
into a group such that the corresponding exact sequence of
(X,A,e)
:

\cdots\to\pi_{1}(A,e)\xrightarrow{i_{1}}\pi_{1}(X,e)\xrightarrow{j_{1}}\pi_{1}(X,A,e)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\pi_{0}(A,e)\xrightarrow{i_{0}}\pi_{0}(X,e)

consists of homomorphisms.
It is well known and easy that
\pi_{1}(X,e)
and
\pi_{1}(A,e)
are abelian, and the image of
j_{1}
is contained in the center of
\pi_{1}(X,A,e)
.

Example 2.2.2
.

Let
p\colon X\to Y
be a fibration between path connected spaces, i.e.
p
satisfies homotopy lifting property.
Let also
x\in X
,
y=p(x)\in Y
, and
A=p^{-1}(y)
.
Then that homotopy lifting property implies that
p
induces a bijection
p_{k}\colon\pi_{k}(X,A,x)\cong\pi_{k}(Y,y)
being an isomorphism of groups for
k\geq 2
.
For
k=1
we have a bijection
p_{1}
between
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)
and the
group

\pi_{1}(Y,y)
.
This allows to endow
\pi_{1}(X,A,x)
with a groups structure from
\pi_{1}(Y,y)
via
p_{1}
.
However, the boundary map
\partial_{1}\colon\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\to\pi_{0}(A,x)
is still not a homomorphism, since
\pi_{0}(A,x)
is not a group.

Example 2.2.3
.

Suppose that in the previous example
p\colon X\to Y
is a regular covering map, which means that
A
is discrete, so
p_{1}\colon\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\pi_{1}(Y,y)
is injective, and the image of
p_{1}
is a normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(Y,y)
.
Then the quotient group
G=\pi_{1}(Y,y)/p_{1}\bigl(\pi_{1}(X,x)\bigr)
can be naturally identified with
A=\pi_{0}A
so that the boundary map
\partial_{1}\colon\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\to\pi_{0}(A,x)\equiv G
becomes a homomorphism.

In particular, the non-trivial part
\pi_{1}(X,x)\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\penalty\ j_{1}\penalty\ }\pi_{1}(X,A,x)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\!\!\!\!\!\to\pi_{0}(A,x)
of the long exact sequence of pair
(X,A,x)
turns into
\pi_{1}(X,x)\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\penalty\ j_{1}\penalty\ }\pi_{1}(Y,y)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\!\!\!\!\!\to G
.
In this case we have a natural action of
G
on
X
so that
X/G
can be identified with
Y
and
A
with the orbit
Gx
of
x
.

2.3.
Weakly discontinuous actions

Suppose now that a discrete group
G
acts from the left on a topological space
X
by homeomorphisms.
For a point
x\in X
its
G
-orbit will be denoted by
Gx
.
Our aim is to show that the standard arguments from the theory of covering spaces allow to prove that
\pi_{1}(X,Gx,x)
still has a group structure under more general settings.
This should probably be known for specialists, however the author did not find any such exposition in the literature.

For a point
a\in X
denote by
[a]
its path component in
X
.

Definition 2.3.1
.

Say that a
G
-action is
weakly discontinuous at a point
x\in X
(WD at
x
) if it satisfies either of the following equivalent properties:

(WD1)

the natural map
\sigma_{x}\colon G\xrightarrow{\penalty\ g\,\mapsto\,gx\penalty\ }Gx\xrightarrow{\penalty\ a\,\mapsto\,[a]\penalty\ }\pi_{0}(Gx,x)
is a bijection;

(WD2)

the path component of
x
in
Gx
consists of that point
x
only, and the stabilizer of
x
is trivial;

(WD3)

every continuous path
\alpha\colon I\to Gx
is constant, and if
gx=g^{\prime}x
for some
g,g^{\prime}\in G
, then
g=g^{\prime}
.

One also checks that every WD-action at
x
is effective (since distinct elements of
G
differently act on
Gx
) and is also WD at any other point
y
of the orbit
Gx
.

Definition 2.3.2
.

Say that
G
acts
properly discontinuous
(PD) if either of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(PD1)

the action is free and the quotient map
p\colon X\to X/G
is a covering, where
X/G
is endowed with the corresponding quotient topology;

(PD2)

each
x\in X
has an open neighborhood
V_{x}
such that
gV_{x}\cap hV_{x}=\varnothing
for
g\not=h
.

A neighborhood
V_{x}
in
(PD2)
is called
wandering (with respect to this action of
G
)
or simply
G
-wandering
.

Evidently, every PD action is WD at each point
x\in X
and the restriction of
p
to every
G
-wandering neighborhood is a homeomorphism onto.
Moreover, it is well known and is easy to see that if
X
is Hausdorff and
G
is a finite group freely acting on
X
, then this action is also PD, e.g.
[
4
, 11.1.3]
.

In fact, there are several definitions of
properly
discontinuous actions involving compact subspaces and adopted to the actions on locally compact Hausdorff spaces, e.g.
[
7
, I, page 4, Remark]
.
We will use only the one equivalent to
(PD1)
.

2.4.
Group structure on

Let
X
be a path connected topological space and

.
Suppose we are given an action of a group

on

.

1) Then there is a natural map

Let

Hence,

2.5.
Dynamical systems

Let
X
be a path connected topological space,
x\in X
be a point, and
\phi\colon X\to X
be a homeomorphism, so the iterations of
\phi
define an action of
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
X
.
If this action is WD at
x
, then one can define the group
\pi_{1}(X,{\mathbb{Z}}x,x)
.
We will consider below few computations of that group, but first let us discuss the situations when such points exist.

Remark 2.5.1
.

a) Clearly, the condition that the stabilizer of
x
is trivial means that
x
is
non-periodic
, i.e.
x\not=\phi^{n}(x)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus 0
.

Note that existence of non-periodic points is a very typical situation.
Even more, for ‘‘good’’ spaces like CW-complexes, homeomorphisms with only periodic points, and in particular, periodic homeomorphisms, are ‘‘rare’’ in the corresponding homeomorphism groups: having all points periodic is an unstable property.
One might also mention a result by D. Montgomery
[
35
]
claiming that every homeomorphism of a
connected
manifold with all periodic points is itself periodic.

b) Assume further that
x
is non-periodic.
Then a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action is WD at
x
if
\{x\}
is the path components of
x
in its orbit
{\mathbb{Z}}x
.

This condition is also typical for ‘‘good’’ spaces.
It may fail in some ‘‘pathological’’ cases, e.g. when the orbit
{\mathbb{Z}}x
or all
X
has anti-discrete topology (consisting only of two sets:
X
and
\varnothing
).

On the other hand,
if the one-point set
\{x\}
is closed in
{\mathbb{Z}}x
(which holds e.g. when
X
is a
T_{1}
-space), then
\{x\}
is the path components of
x
in
{\mathbb{Z}}x
.

Indeed, as noted above, since
\phi
is a homeomorphism, for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
the one-point set
\{\phi^{n}(x)\}
is also closed in
{\mathbb{Z}}x
.
Now let
\alpha\colon I\to{\mathbb{Z}}x
be a path in the orbit, with
\alpha(0)=x
.
Then for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
the set
A_{n}=\alpha^{-1}(\phi^{n}(x))
is closed in
I
, and so we get at most countable partition
I=\sqcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}A_{n}
of
I
into closed subsets.
By Sierpiński’s theorem, (see e.g.
[
9
]
for details), this is possible only when
all of these sets are empty except one of them
, which means that
A_{0}=I
, and so
\alpha
is a constant path.

For instance, it follows from the above discussion that a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action is WD at each
wandering
point
x
of
\phi
.

Example 2.5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\phi\colon S^{1}\to S^{1}
,
\phi(w)=we^{2\pi i\alpha}
, be the rotation of the circle.

1) Suppose
\alpha=a/m
is rational, with
a,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathrm{gcd}(a,m)=1
.
Then
\phi
generates a free action of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
on
S^{1}
.
One easily checks that for any
w\in S^{1}
the short exact sequence (
2.7
) for
(S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}w,w)
is isomorphic with
m{\mathbb{Z}}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
.
In particular,
\pi_{1}(S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}w,w)={\mathbb{Z}}
.

2) If
\alpha
is irrational, then
\phi
generates a free action of
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
One easily checks that now for any
w\in S^{1}
, the short exact sequence (
2.7
) for
(S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}}w,w)
is isomorphic with

{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus 0\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to 0\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}.

This will also follow from Lemma
2.5.4
below, since
\phi
is embeddable into a flow on
S^{1}
, i.e. an action of
{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Example 2.5.3
.

Let
\phi\colon S^{1}\to S^{1}
,
\phi(w)=\overline{w}
, be the complex conjugation.
Then
\phi
defines an action of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
on
S^{1}
with two fixed points
\pm 1
.
That action is WD at each
w\in S^{1}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
, and one checks that the short exact sequence (
2.7
) for
(S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}w,w)
is
{\mathbb{Z}}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\rtimes}{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
and corresponds to a unique non-trivial
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-extension
{\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\rtimes}{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Recall that, by definition,
{\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\rtimes}{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is the semidirect product of
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
corresponding to the canonical isomorphism
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
More precisely,
{\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\rtimes}{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is the Cartesian product of sets
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
with the following operation:
(a,\delta)(b,\varepsilon):=(a+\delta b,\delta\varepsilon)
, where
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\delta,\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
One can also regard
{\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\rtimes}{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
as the following group of integer
(2\times 2)
-matrices:

\bigl\{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
a&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\mid a\in{\mathbb{Z}},\delta\in\{\pm 1\}\bigr\}
.

Consider also one explicit computation of the sequence (
2.7
) for flows, i.e. actions of
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
\mathbf{F}\colon X\times{\mathbb{R}}\to X
be a flow on a topological space
X
, and
G\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a subgroup distinct from
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let also
x\in X
be a point and
Gx=\{\mathbf{F}(x,\tau)\mid\tau\in G\}
be its
G
-orbit.

Lemma 2.5.4
.

Suppose that the path component of
x
in
Gx
is
\{x\}
and either

(a)

x
is non-periodic, or

(b)

x
is periodic of some period
\theta>0
such that
\theta\not\in G
.

Then the action of
G
is WD at
x
, and the short exact sequence (
2.7
) splits, i.e. it is isomorphic with

\pi_{1}(X,x)\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha\ \mapsto\ (\alpha,0)\penalty\ }\pi_{1}(X,x)\times G\xrightarrow{\penalty\ (\alpha,\tau)\ \mapsto\ \tau\penalty\ }\!\!\!\!\!\to G.

Proof.

Since
G\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}
, the path component of
0\in G
must be
\{0\}
.
Then each of the conditions
(a)
and
(b)
imply that the stabilizer of
x
with respect to
G
is trivial.
As the path component of
x
in
Gx
is
\{x\}
, we see that the action of
G
is WD at
x
.

Let us compute the short exact sequence (
2.7
):

\pi_{1}(X,x)\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\penalty\ j\penalty\ }\pi_{1}(X,Gx,x)\xrightarrow{\delta_{{}_{X,x}}\!}\!\!\!\!\!\to G
.
For each
\tau\in G
define the following path
\gamma_{\tau}\colon(I,0,1)\to(X,x,Gx)
by
\gamma_{\tau}(t)=\mathbf{F}(x,t\tau)
, so it goes along the trajectory of
x
from
x
to
\mathbf{F}(x,\tau)
.

One easily check that
\delta_{{}_{X,x}}\!(\gamma_{\tau})=\tau
and
\gamma_{\tau}\,\sharp\,\gamma_{\tau^{\prime}}=\gamma_{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}
for all
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in G
.
Therefore,
K=\{[\gamma_{\tau}]\}_{\tau\in G}
is a subgroup of
\pi_{1}(X,Gx,x)
which is isomorphically mapped
K
onto
G
by
\delta_{{}_{X,x}}\!
.

It suffices to show that
K
commutes with
\pi_{1}(X,x)
.
This will imply that
\pi_{1}(X,Gx,x)
splits into the direct product of
\pi_{1}(X,x)
and
K
.

Let
\alpha\colon(I,\partial I)\to(X,x)
be a loop at
x
and
\tau\in G
.
Define the homotopy
F\colon I^{2}\to X
by
F(s,t)=\mathbf{F}(\alpha(s),t\tau)
.
Denote by
J=\overline{\partial I^{2}\setminus(I\times 0)}
the arc being the union of the left, top and right sides of
I^{2}
.
Then it is evident that
[F|_{I\times 0}]=[\alpha]
, while
[F|_{J}]=[\gamma_{\tau}\,\sharp\,\alpha\,\sharp\,\gamma_{\tau}^{-1}]
.
Thus,
\alpha
is homotopic to
\gamma_{\tau}\,\sharp\,\alpha\,\sharp\,\gamma_{\tau}^{-1}
in
\pi_{1}(X,x)
, whence
\pi_{1}(X,x)
commutes with
K
.
∎

3.
Wreath products

3.1.
Definitions

Let
\mu\colon X\times K\to X
be a
right
action of some group
K
on a set
X
.
To simplify notation we will also write
xk
instead of
\mu(x,k)
.
For each
k\in K
denote by
R_{k}\colon X\to X
,
R_{k}(x)=xk
, the
shift
of
X
by
k
.
Evidently,
R_{l}\circ R_{k}(x)=R_{l}(xk)=xkl=R_{kl}(x)
.

Let also
G
be another group.
Then the set
\mathrm{Map}(X,G)
of all maps
X\to G
is a group with respect to the point-wise multiplication, and we also have a natural
left
action

\kappa\colon K\times\mathrm{Map}(X,G)\to\mathrm{Map}(X,G),\qquad\kappa(k,\alpha)=\alpha\circ R_{k}\colon X\xrightarrow{R_{k}}X\xrightarrow{\alpha}G,

of
K
on
\mathrm{Map}(X,G)
.
It is indeed a
left
action, since

\kappa(k,\kappa(l,\alpha))=\kappa(k,\alpha\!\circ\!R_{l})=\alpha\!\circ\!R_{l}\!\circ\!R_{k}=\alpha\!\circ\!R_{kl}.

The semidirect product
\mathrm{Map}(X,G)\rtimes_{\mu}K
associated with this action is called the
(unrestricted) wreath product
of
G
and
K
corresponding to
\mu
and will be denoted by
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu}}K
or even
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu,X}}K
if we need to specify
X
.
More precisely, the multiplication in
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu,X}}K
is defined as follows: if
\alpha,\beta\colon X\to G
are two maps and
k,l\in K
, then

(\alpha,k)(\beta,l):=(\alpha\cdot(\beta\!\circ\!R_{k}),kl),

where
\cdot
means the point-wise multiplication of maps, i.e.
(\alpha\cdot(\beta\!\circ\!R_{k}))(x)=\alpha(x)\beta(xk)
for all
x\in X
.

Let
1_{G}
and
1_{K}
be the units of
G
and
K
respectively, and
\mathbf{e}_{G}\colon X\to G
be the constant map into the unit
1_{G}\in G
.
Then
(\mathbf{e}_{G},1_{K})
is the unit of
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu,X}}K
, and the inverse of
(\alpha,k)
is
((\alpha\!\circ\!R_{k^{-1}})^{-1},k^{-1})
, where
(\alpha\!\circ\!R_{k^{-1}})^{-1}\colon X\to G
is the point-wise inverse of
\alpha\!\circ\!R_{k^{-1}}\colon X\to G
, so
(\alpha\!\circ\!R_{k^{-1}})^{-1}(x)=(\alpha(xk^{-1}))^{-1}
for all
x\in X
.

Again, the
restricted wreath product
is defined similarly, but one should replace
\mathrm{Map}(X,G)
with its subset
\mathrm{FMap}(X,G)
of functions
\alpha\colon X\to G
whose
support
,
X\setminus\alpha^{-1}(1_{G})
, is finite.

We will be interested in the following two particular cases of this construction for the situation when
X
itself is a group and
K
acts on
X
by left shifts.

3.2.
Regular wreath products of groups corresponding to effective actions

Suppose
X=K
and the action
\mu\colon K\times K\to K
is just the multiplication in
K
.
In this case

G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu,K}}K:=\mathrm{Map}(K,G)\rtimes_{\mu}K

is denoted simply by
G\wr K
and usually called the
regular wreath product
of
G
and
K
.

Example 3.2.1
.

Let
K
be a finite group of some order
m\geq 1
.
Then
\mathrm{Map}(K,G)
can be identified with the
m
-th Cartesian power
G^{m}
of
G
, whose coordinates are enumerated by elements of
K
, and

G\wr K
is the product of sets
G^{m}\times K
with the following multiplication:

(3.1)

\bigl(\{a_{k}\}_{k\in K},g\bigr)\bigl(\{b_{k}\}_{k\in K},h\bigr)=\bigl(\{a_{k}b_{kg}\}_{k\in K},gh\bigr)

where
a_{i},b_{j}\in G
,
k,l\in K
.

Example 3.2.2
.

In particular, if
K={\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
is a finite cyclic group of some order
m\geq 1
, then
G\wr{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
can be regarded as the product of
sets

G^{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
with the following multiplication:

(3.2)

(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m-1};p)(b_{0},\ldots,b_{m-1};q)=(a_{0}b_{p},a_{1}b_{1+p},\ldots,a_{m-1}b_{p-1},p+q),

where
a_{i},b_{j}\in G
,
p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
, and all indices are taken modulo
m
.

Example 3.2.3
.

Similarly, let
K={\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
be the product of two finite cyclic groups of orders
m,n\geq 1
.
Then
\mathrm{Map}({\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n},G)
can be identified with
mn
-th Cartesian power
G^{mn}
of
G
, whose elements can be regarded as
(m\times n)
-matrices with entries in
G
.
Moreover,
G\wr({\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n})
is the product of sets
G^{mn}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
with the following multiplication:

(3.3)

\bigl(\{a_{i,\,j}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{m},\,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}};p,q\bigr)\,\bigl(\{b_{i,\,j}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{m},\,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}};r,s\bigr)\,=\\
=\,\bigl(\{a_{i,\,j}\,b_{i+p,\,j+q}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{m},\,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}};p+r,q+s)\bigr),

where
a_{i,\,j},b_{i,\,j}\in G
,
p,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
,
q,s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
, and all first and second indices are taken modulo
m
and
n
respectively.

3.3.
Wreath products corresponding to non-effective actions

There is an extensive literature on wreath products, see e.g.
[
39
,
34
,
3
]
and references therein.
Most of them correspond to effective actions, see also
[
16
]
.
However, it was recently proved by the author,
[
33
]
, that for typical smooth functions
f
on with isolated critical points of compact surfaces
M
, the fundamental groups of their orbits with respect to natural actions of the diffeomorphism groups
\mathcal{D}(M)
are ‘‘built’’ from wreath products corresponding to certain non-effective actions of
{\mathbb{Z}}
, see Sections
5
,
6
.
We will define below those ‘‘building blocks’’.

Assume now that
X
is a group and let
\delta\colon K\to X
be a homomorphism.
Then we have a natural right action
\mu\colon X\times K\to X
of
K
on
X
(as a set) by right shifts given by
\mu(x,k)=x\delta(k)
.
In this case the corresponding wreath product
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu,X}}K
will also be denoted by
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta\colon K\to X}}K
or simply by
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta}}K
.
Again, it is the set
\mathrm{Map}(X,G)\times K
with the following multiplication: if
\alpha,\beta\colon X\to G
are two maps and
k,l\in K
, then

(\alpha,k)(\beta,l):=(\alpha\cdot\beta\!\circ\!R_{\delta(k)},kl).

Notice that if
\delta
has a non-trivial kernel, then the corresponding action
\mu
is
non-effective
.

Evidently, the regular wreath product
G\wr K
is the same as
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mathrm{id}_{K}}}K
, i.e. it corresponds to the identity isomorphism of
K
.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Let
K,X
and
Y
be any groups.
Then every homomorphism
\phi\colon X\to Y
induces a homomorphism

\phi^{*}\colon\ G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\phi\circ\delta\colon K\to Y}}K\ \longrightarrow\ G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta\colon K\to X}}K,\qquad\phi^{*}(\alpha,k)=(\alpha\circ\phi,k),

for
\alpha\colon Y\to G
and
k\in K
.
Moreover,
(\mathrm{id}_{X})^{*}=\mathrm{id}_{(G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta\colon K\to X}}K)}
, and if
\psi\colon Y\to Z
is another homomorphism of groups, then
(\psi\circ\phi)^{*}=\phi^{*}\circ\psi^{*}
.
In particular, if
\phi
is an isomorphism, then so is
\phi^{*}
.

Proof.

Note that for every
k,k^{\prime}\in K
we have that

(3.4)

R_{\phi(\delta(k))}\circ\phi(k^{\prime})=\phi(k^{\prime})\,\phi(\delta(k))=\phi(k^{\prime}\delta(k))=\phi\circ R_{\delta(k)}(k^{\prime}).

Hence, if
(\alpha,k),(\beta,l)\in G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\phi\circ\delta\colon K\to Y}}K=\mathrm{Map}(Y,G)\rtimes K
, then

\displaystyle\phi^{*}\bigl((\alpha,k)(\beta,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl((\alpha\cdot\beta\!\circ\!R_{\phi(\delta(k))})\circ\phi,kl\bigr)=\bigl((\alpha\circ\phi)\cdot(\beta\!\circ\!R_{\phi(\delta(k))}\circ\phi),kl\bigr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:R__phi_commute}}}{{=}}\bigl((\alpha\circ\phi)\cdot(\beta\!\circ\!\phi\circ R_{\delta(k)}),kl\bigr)=\phi^{*}(\alpha,k)\phi^{*}(\beta,l).

Thus,
\phi^{*}
is a homomorphism.
All other statements are evident.
∎

Example 3.3.2
.

Let
K={\mathbb{Z}}
,
X={\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
and
\delta\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
,
\delta(k)=k\bmod m
, be the natural
\bmod\,m
epimorphism.
Then the group
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}}}{\mathbb{Z}}
will be denoted by
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m}}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
It can be regarded as the product of sets
G^{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
with the multiplication given precisely by the same formula (
3.2
) in which
p,q
now belong to
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Example 3.3.3
.

Similarly, let
\delta\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
,
\delta(k,l)=(k\bmod m,l\bmod n)
.
Then the group
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\delta}}{\mathbb{Z}}
will be denoted by
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m,n}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
It can be regarded as the product of sets
G^{mn}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with the multiplication given precisely by the same formula (
3.3
) in which
p,q,r,s
now belong to
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Example 3.3.4
.

More generally, let
K={\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{n}}
be the product of
n
finite cyclic groups of orders
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\geq 1
, and
m=m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{n}
be their product.
Define also the homomorphism
\delta\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to K
,
\delta(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})=(k_{1}\bmod m_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\bmod m_{n})
.
Again for any group
G
the set
\mathrm{Map}(K,G)
can be identified with
m
-th Cartesian power
G^{m}=G^{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times G^{m_{n}}
of
G
.
Then similarly to
3.2.3
and
3.3.3
one can define two wreath products:
G\wr K
and
G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
They correspond respectively to the effective action of
K
and the non-effective action of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
on
G^{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times G^{m_{n}}
by independent cyclic shifts of coordinates.
We will also denote

G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{\mu}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}:=G{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

Short exact sequences

\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{q}\colon A\times K\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\times L\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C\times M.

5.
Classes of short exact sequences

Iterated wreath products naturally act on trees, e.g.
[
26
]
.
That observation was made in 1869 by C. Jordan
[
19
]
who probably introduced wreath products, see
[
37
, p. 209]
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of isomorphism classes of groups of automorphisms of finite trees.
Jordan proved that every group from
\mathcal{T}
is obtained from the unit group by finitely many operations of direct product and wreath product of the form
\cdot{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{X_{n}}}S_{n}
, where
X_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
S_{n}
is the permutation group of
X_{n}
.
We will now define in a similar way two classes of groups generated by wreath products
\cdot\wr_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
and
\cdot\wr\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{i}}
and related by certain short exact sequences.

Definition 5.1
.

For
n\geq 1
let
\mathcal{B}_{n}
be the minimal set of isomorphism classes of short exact sequences having the following properties (see Section
3.4
for notations):

(a)

the sequence
\mathbf{c}_{0}:1\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow 1\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to 1
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
;

(b)

if
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{B}
, then
\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
;

(c)

if
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{B}
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1
is a finite collection of natural numbers with
k\leq n
, then
\mathbf{p}\wr\mathbf{c}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\ \in\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Due to
(a)
, the set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is non-empty, and its minimality means that every short exact sequence
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is obtained from
\mathbf{c}_{0}
by finitely many operations of direct product and wreath product of the form
\cdot\wr\mathbf{c}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}
with
k\leq n
.
For example,

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{1},(k\geq 1),\quad(\mathbf{c}_{2}\times\mathbf{c}_{9})\wr\mathbf{c}_{5,7}\in\mathcal{B}_{2},\quad\bigl(\mathbf{c}_{32}\times(\mathbf{c}_{2}\wr\mathbf{c}_{5,7,3})\bigr)\wr\mathbf{c}_{9,34,6,2}\in\mathcal{B}_{4}.

It is also evident that
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{l}
for
k<l
.
Put
\mathcal{B}:=\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{C}
be the sets of isomorphism classes of middle and right groups appearing in the short exact sequences
\mathbf{p}\colon A\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C\in\mathcal{B}
.
Then it follows from the definition that
\mathcal{B}_{B}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{C}
) is the minimal set of classes containing the unit group
1
and closed under direct product and wreath products
\cdot{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
(resp.
\cdot\wr\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{i}}
).
Thus,
\mathcal{B}_{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{C}
are defined similarly to
\mathcal{T}
.

Any short exact sequence isomorphic to a sequence of the form
\mathbf{p}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C
with finite
C
and some
n\geq 0
will be called
crystallographic
.
In this case the middle group
B
will also be called
crystallographic
.
We will also say that
\mathbf{p}
as well as
B
are
Bieberbach
, whenever
B
is torsion free.
In
[
33
, Lemmas 2.2 & 2.6]
the author proved that every sequence from
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is Bieberbach.

Remark 5.3
.

In
[
33
, Theorem 2.5]
the following statement is mentioned as
Bieberbach theorem
and referred to
[
7
, Corollary 5.1]
for its proof:

for every Bieberbach sequence
\mathbf{p}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C
there exists a free action of
C
on a torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that the exact sequence of that covering
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n}/C)\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{p}
relatively to the last term
C
.
In particular,
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n}/C)\cong B
.
However, Bieberbach theorem claims existence of such actions only under assumption that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is a
maximal
abelian subgroup.

Let us show that the above statement is true even for non-maximal free abelian subgroups, which will give lacking arguments.
This is essential, since
[
33
, Theorem 5.10]
and the orientable part of
[
33
, Corollary 1.3]
are based on the variant of Bieberbach theorem for non-maximal subgroups.
For example, in the sequence
\mathbf{c}_{m}\colon m{\mathbb{Z}}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
with
m\geq 2
, the abelian subgroup
m{\mathbb{Z}}
of finite index is non-maximal, however there is a free action of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
on
\mathbb{T}^{1}=S^{1}
(by rotations by
2\pi/m
) such that
\mathbf{c}_{m}
is isomorphic with the short exact sequence of the quotient map
p\colon\mathbb{T}^{1}\to\mathbb{T}^{1}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}=\mathbb{T}^{1}
.

Thus, let
A\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C
be a Bieberbach sequence, where
A
is a free abelian subgroup of
B
of rank
n
.
If
A
is non-maximal, take any maximal abelian subgroup
A^{\prime}\subset B
containing
A
.
In fact, see A. Vasquez
[
Vasquez:JDG:1970
, Theorem 3.1]
,
A^{\prime}
is the centralizer of the normal subgroup
A
in
B
, and therefore it is normal as well.
Since
A^{\prime}/A
is finite,
A^{\prime}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and we get another Bieberbach sequence
\mathbf{q}\colon A^{\prime}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow B\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C^{\prime}
.
Then by the ‘‘maximal subgoup’’ case of Bieberbach theorem, there exists an action of
C^{\prime}:=B/A^{\prime}
on
T^{\prime}:=\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that the exact sequence of the corresponding covering map
q\colon T^{\prime}\to T^{\prime}/C^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{q}
.
Let

be the covering map corresponding to the subgroup

, and

.
Then

, so

is a regular covering, its short exact sequence is isomorphic with
\mathbf{p}
, and we can identify

.
In particular, this gives the required action of

on

such that
\pi_{1}(T/C)\cong B
.

The following lemma provides an explicit description of the actions on tori for the short sequences from class

For each

(i)

is Bieberbach, so

for some

(ii)

\pi_{1}\mathbb{T}^{n}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n}/C)\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to C

.

Therefore, since
\mathbb{T}^{n}/C

and
(ii)
.
We need to prove that

(a)

Let
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{c}_{0}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{0}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow 1\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to 1

Then

trivially holds for
\mathbf{c}_{0}
.
Also, the latter group

so that the short exact sequence of the corresponding covering map

is
\mathbf{c}_{0}
.
This also proves

, so

(b)

Let

(c)

m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1
be any natural numbers, and
m=m_{1}\cdots m_{k}
.
In particular, we have a free action of the group
C
on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{p}
is short exact sequence of the covering map
p\colon\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}/C
.
Then
H:=C\wr\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{i}}
is finite and solvable as well as
C
.
Moreover, by Example
4.2.5
, there exists a free action of
H
on the torus
(\mathbb{T}^{n})^{m}\times\mathbb{T}^{k}=\mathbb{T}^{nm+k}
such that the short exact sequence of the covering map
q\colon\mathbb{T}^{nm+k}\to\mathbb{T}^{nm+k}/H
is isomorphic with

\mathbf{p}\wr\mathbf{c}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{nm+k}\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }B{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \penalty\ }\!\!\!\!\!\to C\wr\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}_{m_{i}}.

Note that
B{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
is also solvable as well as
B
.
Moreover, by the arguments similar to
[
33
, Lemma 2.2]
for
k=1
,
B{\mathop{\ \wr\ }\limits_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
is also torsion free.
Hence,
\mathbf{p}\wr\mathbf{c}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\in\mathcal{B}
.
∎

6.
Homotopy types of orbits of smooth functions on surfaces

Let
M
be a compact surface and
P
be either the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
or the circle
S^{1}
.
For a closed subset
X\subset M
denote by
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
the group of all smooth (
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
) diffeomorphisms of
M
fixed on
X
.
Then the group
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
acts on the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,P)
by the following rule: if
h\in\mathcal{D}(M,X)
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,P)
, then the result of the action of
h
on
f
is the composition map
f\circ h:M\to P
.
For
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,P)
let

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(f,X)

\displaystyle=\{h\in\mathcal{D}(M,X)\mid f\circ h=f\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(f,X)

\displaystyle=\{f\circ h\mid h\in\mathcal{D}(M,X)\}

be respectively the
stabilizer
and the
orbit
of
f
under that action.
It will be convenient to say that elements of
\mathcal{S}(f,X)

preserve

f
.
Endow the above spaces with the corresponding strong
C^{\infty}
topologies and denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X)
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{id}}(f,X)
the corresponding path components of
\mathrm{id}_{M}
in
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
and
\mathcal{S}(f,X)
, and by
\mathcal{O}_{f}(f,X)
the path component of
\mathcal{O}(f,X)
containing
f
.
If
X
is empty, then it will be omitted from notation.

Let also
\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,P)\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,P)
be the subset consisting of maps
f\colon M\to P
satisfying the following axiom:

(B)

f
takes a constant value at every connected component of
\partial M
and has no critical points on
\partial M
.

A map
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,P)
is called
Morse
if all its critical points are non-degenerate.
Then the set
\mathrm{Morse}(M,P)
of Morse maps is open and everywhere dense in
\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,P)
.
Further, let
\mathcal{F}(M,P)
be the subset of
\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,P)
consisting of maps
f
satisfying one more axiom:

(H)

for every critical point
z
of
f
, there are local coordinates in which
f
is a homogeneous polynomial
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of degree
\geq 2
without multiple factors.

By Morse Lemma every non-degenerate singularity is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
equivalent to a homogeneous polynomial without multiple factors
\pm x^{2}\pm y^{2}
and thus satisfies
(H)
.
This means that
\mathrm{Morse}(M,P)\subset\mathcal{F}(M,P)
.
Notice that
(H)
also implies that each critical point of
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,P)
is isolated, whence the set of critical points of
f
is finite.
By
[
8
,
11
,
38
]
, for every isolated critical point
z
of a
\mathcal{C}^{3}
function
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
the local
topological structure
of level-sets of
f
near
z
is realized by level sets of homogeneous polynomial without multiple factors.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}(M,P)
contains not only ‘‘all typical’’ (i.e. Morse) maps, but also maps with all possible topological types of critical points.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,P)
.
A connected component
K
of a level-set
f^{-1}(c)
,
c\in P
, will be called a
contour
(of
f
).
We also call
K

regular
if it contains no critical points, and
critical
otherwise.
Then a compact submanifold
X\subset M
whose connected components have dimensions
1
and
2
will be said
f
-saturated
if it is a union of contours of
f
.
In particular, every regular contour of
f
and
\partial M
are
f
-saturated.
Also, if
a,b\in P
are regular values of
f
, and
J\subset P
an interval with
\partial J=\{a,b\}
, then
f^{-1}(J)
is
f
-saturated.

The study of homotopy types of stabilizers and orbits of Morse maps
f\in\mathrm{Morse}(M,P)
was initiated in
[
29
]
.
In a series of papers
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
it was proved that for
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,P)
and an
f
-saturated submanifold
X
the following statements hold.

(1)

The map
p\colon\mathcal{D}(M,X)\to\mathcal{O}(f,X)
is a Serre fibration with fiber
\mathcal{S}(f)
,
[
29
, Th. 2.1]
,
[
31
, Th. 5.1]
.

(2)

The path component
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{id}}(f)
is contractible except for few types of maps for which it is homotopy equivalent to the circle,
[
29
, Th. 1.3]
,

[
31
, Th. 5.1]
,
[
32
, Th. 2.1]
.

(3)

There is a short exact sequence
\pi_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X)\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow\pi_{1}\mathcal{O}_{f}(f,X)\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to G
, where
G
is a finite group and
k\geq 0
both depending on
f
and
X
.

(4)

\mathcal{O}_{f}(f)
is aspherical iff either
M\not=S^{2},{\mathbb{R}}{P}^{2}
or
X\not=\varnothing
,
[
29
, Th. 1.5]
,
[
32
, Th. 2.3]
.
In that case

•

\pi_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X)
is a free abelian of rank
\leq 2
.
In fact,
\pi_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X)=0
iff either
X\not=\varnothing
or
\chi(M)<0
;

\pi_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})={\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
; and in all other cases
\pi_{1}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M)={\mathbb{Z}}
.

•

\pi_{1}\mathcal{O}_{f}(f,X)
is torsion free,
[
33
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Hence, the short exact sequence from
(3)
is Bieberbach.

(5)

If
f
is a
generic
Morse map (i.e. it takes distinct values at distinct critical points), then
G
is trivial and expect for few cases
\mathcal{O}_{f}(f)
has the homotopy type of
\mathbb{T}^{k}\times R
, where
k\geq 0
and
R=SO(3)
if
M=S^{2},{\mathbb{R}}{P}^{2}
and
R
is a point otherwise,
[
29
, Th. 1.5]
.

E. Kudryavtseva
[
25
,
22
,
21
,
23
,
24
]
studied the homotopy type of the space of Morse functions on compact surfaces and rediscovering ideas from
[

]
extended the above results, see
[

, footnote after Theorem 1.2]
.
She proved that if
M
is orientable, then under additional mild assumptions on
f\in\mathrm{Morse}(M,{\mathbb{R}})
there exists a free action of
G
on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{f}(f)
is homotopy equivalent to
(\mathbb{T}^{n}/G)\times R
, where
R
is the same as above, which generalizes
(5)
.

Further in
[
33
, Section 5]
the author described a precise algebraic structure of the Bieberbach sequence
(3)
, which allowed to explicitly compute all its groups.
Also, in a series of papers with B. Feshchenko that sequence
(3)
was also computed for

.
Those results can be formulated as follows:

Theorem 6.1

(
[

,
27
,
28
,
13
,
20
,
14
]
)
.

Let
M
be a compact orientable surface,

,
X\subset M
an
f
-saturated submanifold, and

(6.1)

\mathbf{p}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow\pi_{1}\mathcal{O}_{f}(f,X)\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to G

(f,X)

(a)

If

(b)

The following statement extends
[

to all orientable surfaces distinct from

Corollary 6.2

(c.f.
[

,

]

.

Under notation of Theorem

there is a free action of

\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{p}
coincides with the short exact sequence of the covering map
