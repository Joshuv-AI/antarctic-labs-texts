On the binary adder channel with complete feedback, with an application to quantitative group testing
Samuel H. Florin, Matthew H. Ho Zilin Jiang

Abstract
We determine the exact value of the optimal symmetric rate point ( r , r ) (r,r) in the Dueck zero-error capacity region of the binary adder channel with complete feedback. We proved that the average zero-error capacity r = h ⁡ ( 1 / 2 − δ ) ≈ 0.78974 r=h(1/2-\delta)\approx 0.78974 , where h ⁡ ( ⋅ ) h(\cdot) is the binary entropy function and δ = 1 / ( 2 ​ log 2 ⁡ ( 2 + 3 ) ) \delta=1/(2\log_{2}(2+\sqrt{3})) . Our motivation is a problem in quantitative group testing. Given a set of n n elements two of which are defective, the quantitative group testing problem asks for the identification of these two defectives through a series of tests. Each test gives the number of defectives contained in the tested subset, and the outcomes of previous tests are assumed known at the time of designing the current test. We establish that the minimum number of tests is asymptotic to ( log 2 ⁡ n ) / r (\log_{2}n)/r as n → ∞ n\to\infty .

Application to quantitative group testing

The connection between the quantitative group testing problem with two defectives and the binary adder channel is established through a variation of the group testing problem. Aigner
[
3
]
introduced this variation in the context of search problems on graphs. Consider two disjoint sets
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, each of which consists of
n

X_{1}\cup X_{2}
. Let
t(n,n)
be the minimum number of tests required in this situation. Hao
[
10
]
observed that
t(n)\sim t(n,n)
.

We establish a one-to-one correspondence between the above variation of the quantitative group testing problem, and the binary adder channel with complete feedback.

We also need the following elementary algebraic fact.

Proposition 16
.

For every
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
and
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4})\in\Delta^{3}
with
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}>0

and define

by
\rho(r):=\sqrt{r}(r^{\lambda}-1)/(r-r^{\lambda})
. If

\frac{(e_{1}e_{2})^{\lambda}}{e_{1}+e_{2}}=\frac{(e_{3}e_{4})^{\lambda}}{e_{3}+e_{4}},\quad\frac{(e_{1}e_{3})^{\lambda}}{e_{1}+e_{3}}=\frac{(e_{2}e_{4})^{\lambda}}{e_{2}+e_{4}},

then one of the following holds.

(a)

e_{1}=e_{4},e_{2}=e_{3}
;

(b)

e_{2}=e_{3}
and
y=\rho(r)
, where
r=e_{1}/e_{4}\neq 1
;

(c)

e_{1}=e_{4}
and
y=1/\rho(r)
, where
r=e_{2}/e_{3}\neq 1
.

Moreover the function
\rho(r)
is increasing on
(0,1)
and decreasing on
(1,\infty)
,
\rho(r)
takes values in
(0,\lambda/(1-\lambda))
, and if
\rho(r)=\rho(r^{\prime})
then
r=r^{\prime}
or
rr^{\prime}=1
.

Proof.

By setting
r_{1}:=e_{1}/e_{4}
and
r_{2}:=e_{2}/e_{3}
, we rewrite the equations as

\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}^{\lambda}-r_{2}\right)e_{3}+\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}^{\lambda}-r_{1}\right)e_{4}=0,\quad\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}-r_{2}^{\lambda}\right)e_{3}+\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\lambda}\right)e_{4}=0,

(15)

which implies that

\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}^{\lambda}-r_{2}\right)\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\lambda}\right)-\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}^{\lambda}-r_{1}\right)\left(r_{1}^{\lambda}r_{2}-r_{2}^{\lambda}\right)=0,

which is equivalent to

(r_{1}^{2\lambda}-r_{1})r_{2}^{\lambda}(1-r_{2})+r_{1}^{\lambda}(1-r_{1})(r_{2}^{2\lambda}-r_{2})=0.

Since
r_{i}^{2\lambda}-r_{i}
and
1-r_{i}
have the same sign for
i\in[2]
, for the above equation to hold, it must be the case that
r_{1}=1
or
r_{2}=1
. Case
(a)
corresponds to the case where
r_{1}=1
and
r_{2}=1
. Case
(b)
corresponds to the case where
r_{1}\neq 1
and
r_{2}=1
. In this case, (
15
) is equivalent to

(r_{1}^{\lambda}-1)e_{3}+(r_{1}^{\lambda}-r_{1})e_{4}=0,

which implies that

y=\frac{\sqrt{e_{1}e_{4}}}{\sqrt{e_{2}e_{3}}}=\frac{\sqrt{r_{1}}e_{4}}{e_{3}}=\frac{\sqrt{r_{1}}(r_{1}^{\lambda}-1)}{r_{1}-r_{1}^{\lambda}}=\rho(r_{1}).

Case
(c)
corresponds to the case where
r_{1}=1
and
r_{2}\neq 1
, which can be dealt similarly.

For the “moreover” part, one can compute

\rho^{\prime}(r)=\frac{r^{\lambda}((1-2\lambda)(1-r)-(r^{\lambda}-r^{1-\lambda}))}{2\sqrt{r}(r-r^{\lambda})^{2}}.

When
r\in(0,1)
, the mean value theorem states that there exists
s\in(r,1)
such that

\frac{r^{\lambda}-r^{1-\lambda}}{1-r}=\frac{(1^{1-\lambda}-1^{\lambda})-(r^{1-\lambda}-r^{\lambda})}{1-r}=(1-\lambda)s^{-\lambda}-\lambda s^{\lambda-1}=(1-2\lambda)s^{-\lambda}-\lambda s^{\lambda-1}(1-s^{1-2\lambda}),

which is clearly less than
1-2\lambda
for
s\in(0,1)
. Thus
\rho^{\prime}(r)>0
for
r\in(0,1)
, and so
\rho
is increasing on
(0,1)
. One can check that
\rho(r)=\rho(1/r)
, and so
\rho
is decreasing on
(1,\infty)
. Given
r^{\prime}>0
, the monotonicity of the function
\rho
shows that the equation
\rho(r)=\rho(r^{\prime})
has at most two solutions, and so it has exactly two solutions
r=r^{\prime}
and
r=1/r^{\prime}
. Finally applying l’Hôpital’s rule results in
\lim_{r\to 1}\rho(r)=\lambda/(1-\lambda)
, and so
\rho(r)
takes values in
(0,\lambda/(1-\lambda))
.
∎

An open problem

At the moment, the Dueck zero-error capacity region
\mathcal{O}_{f}
of the binary adder channel is far from fully understood.
Theorem

2
gives the exact value of

\sup\left\{\frac{1}{2}R_{1}+\frac{1}{2}R_{2}\colon(R_{1},R_{2})\in\mathcal{O}_{f}\right\}.

In general, the
weighted
average zero-error capacity of the binary adder channel with complete feedback is defined by

R(\mathcal{O}_{f};c_{1},c_{2}):=\sup\left\{c_{1}R_{1}+c_{2}R_{2}\colon(R_{1},R_{2})\in\mathcal{O}_{f}\right\},

for every
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\Delta^{1}
. One can derive from

1
that

is given by the optimum of an optimization problem with

as a variable. Our numerical experiments suggest that
n=1
readily gives the optimal value.

Conjecture A
.

For every
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\Delta^{1}
, the weighted average zero-error capacity
R(\mathcal{O}_{f};c_{1},c_{2})
of the binary adder channel with complete feedback equals the optimum of the following optimization problem.

Maximize:

\displaystyle c_{1}H_{2}(a,\bar{a})+c_{2}H_{2}(b,\bar{b}),

subject to:

\displaystyle S_{3}\left(a\bar{b}+\bar{a}b+2c\right)\geq S_{4}(a,b,c)\text{ for every }c\in[-\min(a\bar{b},\bar{a}b),\min(ab,\bar{a}\bar{b})],

\displaystyle a\in[0,1],b\in[0,1].

Once the conjecture is established, it would be interesting to compute the optimal value of the above optimization problem. The explicit formula of

u^{\prime}=\frac{-8x(1-x)}{(1+x)^{2}(1-3x)^{2}},\quad v^{\prime}=2uu^{\prime}-2su^{\prime}.

(26)

We can then rewrite the equation
\varphi(x)=x\varphi^{\prime}(x)
as

u+\sqrt{v}-x=x(u^{\prime}+(\sqrt{v})^{\prime}-1).

After multiplying both sides by
2\sqrt{v}
, using the fact that
2\sqrt{v}(\sqrt{v})^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
, we can rearrange the above equation to

2\left(u-xu^{\prime}\right)\sqrt{v}=-2v+xv^{\prime}.

After completely replacing
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}
according to (
25
) and (
26
) except for
\sqrt{v}
, we obtain after simplification that

\frac{8x^{2}(1+3x^{2})}{(1+x)^{2}(1-3x)^{2}}\sqrt{v}=\frac{32x^{4}(1+3x^{2})}{(1+x)^{3}(1-3x)^{3}}+\frac{16sx^{3}(1+3x)}{(1+x)^{2}(1-3x)^{2}}-2t^{2},

which after multiplying by
(1+x)^{2}(1-3x)^{2}/(8x^{2})
gives

\displaystyle\left(1+3x^{2}\right)\sqrt{v}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{eqn:u-v}}}}{{=}}\frac{4x^{2}\left(1+3x^{2}\right)}{(1+x)(1-3x)}+2sx(1+3x)-\frac{t^{2}(1+x)^{2}(1-3x)^{2}}{4x^{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:u-v}}}{{=}}-(1+3x^{2})u+2sx(1+3x)-\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}.

The following claim immediately shows that
\varphi(x)\neq x\varphi^{\prime}(x)
for
x\in(0,1/3)
.

Claim 2
.

For every
x\in(0,1/3)
and
s,t
such that
0\leq t\leq 1
,
t\leq s\leq(1+t^{2})/2
and
(s,t)\neq(0,0)
,

\left(1+3x^{2}\right)\sqrt{v}>-(1+3x^{2})u+2sx(1+3x).

Proof of
2
.

Since the left hand side of the inequality is non-negative, squaring both sides strengthens the inequality, which after substituting
v
according to (
25
) gives that

\left(1+3x^{2}\right)(u^{2}-2su+t^{2})>\left(-(1+3x^{2})u+2sx(1+3x)\right)^{2}.

The difference between the two sides of the last inequality, after substituting
u
according to (
25
), equals

q:=4sx^{2}\left(2+6x^{2}-s(1+3x)^{2}\right)+\left(1+3x^{2}\right)t^{2}.

When
t=0
,
q=2sx^{2}(3(1-x)^{2}+(1-2s)(1+3x)^{2})
which is clearly positive for
0<s\leq 1/2
and
x\in(0,1/3)
. Hereafter we deal with the case where
t\in(0,1]
. Since
q
is a quadratic polynomial of
s
with a negative leading coefficient, it suffices to check
q>0
for
s\in\left\{t,(1+t^{2})/2\right\}
. When
s=t
,

q=t\left(\left(8x^{2}+24x^{4}\right)(1-t)+\left(1+7x^{2}-24x^{3}-12x^{4}\right)t\right).

One can check that both
8x^{2}+24x^{4}
and
1+7x^{2}-24x^{3}-12x^{4}
are positive for
x\in(0,1/3)
. Thus
q>0
for
x\in(0,1/3)
. When
s=(1+t^{2})/2
,

q=(1+t^{2})x^{2}\left(4+12x^{2}-(1+t^{2})(1+3x)^{2}\right)+\left(1+3x^{2}\right)t^{2},

which can be seen as a quadratic polynomial of
t^{2}
with a negative leading coefficient. It suffices to check
q>0
for
t^{2}=0
and
t^{2}=1
. Note that

q=\begin{cases}3x^{2}(1-x)^{2}&\text{when }t=0;\\
1+6x^{2}-6x^{3}+3x^{4}&\text{when }t=1.\end{cases}

In either case, one can check that
q>0
for
x\in(0,1/3)
.
∎

Case 3:
x\in(1/3,1]
.

Recall from (
23
) that for
x\neq 1/3
,

c_{a,b}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(-(a\bar{b}+\bar{a}b)+u\pm\sqrt{v})=\tfrac{1}{2}(ab+\bar{a}\bar{b}-1+u\pm\sqrt{v}).

When
1/3<x\leq 1
, since
u>1
and
c_{a,b}(x)\leq\min(ab,\bar{a}\bar{b})\leq(ab+\bar{a}\bar{b})/2
, we choose the minus sign in the above formula. Therefore for
x\in(1/3,1]

\varphi(x)=u-\sqrt{v}-x.

We can then rewrite the equation
\varphi(x)=x\varphi^{\prime}(x)
as

u-\sqrt{v}-x=x(u^{\prime}-(\sqrt{v})^{\prime}-1).

After multiplying both sides by
2\sqrt{v}
, using the fact that
2\sqrt{v}(\sqrt{v})^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
, we can rearrange the above equation to

2\left(u-xu^{\prime}\right)\sqrt{v}=2v-xv^{\prime},

which, by comparing with the corresponding computation in Case 2, is equivalent to

\left(1+3x^{2}\right)\sqrt{v}=(1+3x^{2})u-2sx(1+3x)+\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}.

The following claim immediately implies that
\varphi(x)=x\varphi^{\prime}(x)
could possibly have a solution in
(1/3,1]
only when
t=1
or
(s,t)=(1/2,0)
, which corresponds to
(a,b)\in\left\{(1/2,1/2),(0,1),(1,0)\right\}
. However one can compute that
\varphi_{1/2,1/2}(x)=x(1-x)/(1+x)
and
\varphi_{0,1}(x)=\varphi_{1,0}(x)=1-x
, and one can check directly that in neither case
\varphi=x\varphi^{\prime}
has a solution in
(0,1]
.

Claim 3
.

For every
x\in(1/3,1]
,
s,t
such that
0\leq t<1
,
t\leq s\leq(1+t^{2})/2
and
(s,t)\not\in\left\{(0,0),(1/2,0)\right\}
,

\left(1+3x^{2}\right)^{2}v>\left((1+3x^{2})u-2sx(1+3x)+\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)^{2}.

Proof of
3
.

After substituting
v
according to (
25
), the difference between the two sides of the last inequality equals

q:=\left(1+3x^{2}\right)^{2}(u^{2}-2su+t^{2})-\left((1+3x^{2})u-2sx(1+3x)+\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)^{2}.

Since
q
is a quadratic polynomial of
s
with a negative leading coefficient, it suffices to check
q\geq 0
when
t=0
and
s\in\left\{0,1/2\right\}
, and
q>0
when
t\in(0,1)
and
s\in\left\{t,(1+t^{2})/2\right\}
.

When
s=t
, we have

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\left((1+3x^{2})(u-t)\right)^{2}-\left((1+3x^{2})u-2tx(1+3x)+\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(t(3x-1)(x+1)-\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)\left(2(1+3x^{2})u-t(9x^{2}+2x)+\frac{4t^{2}x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right),

which after substituting
u
according to (
25
) can be further factorized into

q=t\biggl(t(1-x)^{2}+4(1-t)x^{2}\biggr)^{2}\biggl(\left(8x^{2}+24x^{4}\right)(1-t)+(-1+4x+10x^{2}-12x^{3}+15x^{4})t\biggr)\bigg/(16x^{4}).

One can check that both
8x^{2}+24x^{4}
and
-1+4x+10x^{2}-12x^{3}+15x^{4}
are positive for
x\in(1/3,1]
. Thus
q=0
when
t=0
, and
q>0
when
t\in(0,1)
.

When
s=(1+t^{2})/2
, we have

q=\left(1+3x^{2}\right)^{2}(u^{2}-(1+t^{2})u+t^{2})-\left((1+3x^{2})u-(1+t^{2})x(1+3x)+t^{2}\frac{4x^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)^{2},

which can be seen as a quadratic polynomial of
t^{2}
. We compute
q
for
t=0
and
t=1
. Note that, after substituting
u
according to (
25
),

q=\begin{cases}48(1-x)^{2}x^{6}&\text{when }t=0;\\
(1-x)^{4}(-1+4x+10x^{2}-12x^{3}+15x^{4})&\text{when }t=1.\\

One can check that
-1+4x+10x^{2}-12x^{3}+15x^{4}>0

q\geq 0
