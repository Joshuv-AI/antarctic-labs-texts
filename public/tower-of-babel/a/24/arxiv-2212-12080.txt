Limit case of Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality for martingales Supported by the Russian Science Foundation grant N. 19-71-10023.
Dmitry Yarcev

Abstract
We provide a version of the Stein–Weiss inequality for arbitrary martingales.



1
Introduction

The classical Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev plays an important role in analysis. It was initially introduced in
[
4
]
as a tool to prove Sobolev’s embedding theorem. The inequality says that the Riesz potential (i.e. a Fourier multiplier with the symbol
|\cdot|^{\alpha}
) is a continuous mapping from
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\frac{\alpha}{d}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}
and
1<p<q<\infty
. The notion of Riesz potential was later transferred to probability theory. Watari provided such a counterpart in
[
7
]
and proved the corresponding version of the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality. This idea was generalised further in
[
3
]
, and the corresponding estimates were proved for regular martingales. Finally, in
[
6
]
the authors propose a version of the Riesz potential and prove the corresponding inequality for arbitrary martingales. There are two versions of the Riesz potential in
[
6
]
. The one that is studied here provides a more interesting version of the inequality; however, it is not a matringale transform and lacks the semigroup property.

We are concerned with the limit case of the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality for martingales when
q=\infty
. In this case the Riesz potenial does not necessarily map the martingale space
L_{p}
to
L_{\infty}
; however, it was asked by Prof. Adam Osekowski whether it maps
L_{p}
to BMO. This conjecture is proved here along with a few auxiliary inequalities, which might be of some interest in themselves. Notably, one of those inequalities can be seen as the martingale counterpart to the Stein–Weiss inequality (theorem H in
[
5
]
).

I am grateful to Prof. Adam Osekowski for asking the question underlying this work and to Prof. Dmitriy Stolyarov for supervising the development of this paper.

2
Notation and main result

Consider a probability space
\mathfrak{X}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and a sequence of sigma-algebrae
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subset\mathcal{F}_{1}\subset...\subset\mathcal{F}
. We assume that this sequence is a filtration, i.e. for each two distinct points
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
in
\Omega
there is a number
n
and an event
X\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
such that
\omega_{1}\in X
and
\omega_{2}\notin X
. We also assume that
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}
and each subalgebra
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is generated by a finite or countable set of atoms. In other words, for each step
n
there is a finite or countable partition
\Omega=\bigsqcup_{j}a_{j}
such that the algebra
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is generated by the events
a_{j}
.

For a real parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
, define the Riesz potential
I_{\alpha}
by the formula

I_{\alpha}[F]=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}M_{n}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})F,\penalty\ F\in L_{1}(\mathfrak{X})

(2.1)

There are two families of operators in this formula. The operators
E_{n}
perform averaging over algebrae
\mathcal{F}_{n}
:
E_{n}f=\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})
. The operators
M_{n}
multiply by special functions:
[M_{n}f](\omega)=b_{n}^{\alpha}(\omega)f(\omega)
, where
b_{n}(\omega)=\mathbb{P}(a_{j})
if the atoms
a_{1},a_{2},...
generate
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\omega\in a_{j}
. Note that
(E_{n}-E_{n-1})F
is the
n
-th martingale difference of
F
:
(E_{n}-E_{n-1})F=F_{n}-F_{n-1}
.

Our goal is to prove the limit case of Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality for martingales. The non-limit case was proved in
[
6
]
. We cite it here.

Theorem 2.1
.

For all numbers
p,q
such that
1<p<q<\infty,
the operator
I_{\alpha},\penalty\ \alpha=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q},
maps
L_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
to
L_{q}(\mathfrak{X})
continuously. In other words, there is a constant
C=C(p,q)
such that for all functions
F\in L_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
the series (
2.1
) converges in
L_{q}
and the inequality

\|I_{\alpha}[F]\|_{q}\leqslant C\|F\|_{p}

(2.2)

holds.

In this paper, we will be concerned with the limit case of this inequality as
q\to\infty
. That means that the domain of the Riesz potential is the set
L_{p}(\mathfrak{X}),p>1,
and the parameter
\alpha
is set to
\frac{1}{p}
. In this case, the Riesz potential is not a continuous mapping from
L_{p}
to
L_{\infty}
(see Section
9
). However, we will prove that it is a continuous mapping between the spaces
L_{p}
and BMO.

We say that a martingale
\{F_{n}\}
with the limit value
F
belongs to the class BMO =
\text{BMO}(\mathfrak{X},\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\})
if for each step
n
and each atom
a\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
the value
\mathbb{E}((F-\mathbb{E}(F_{n}))^{2}\mid a)
is uniformly bounded. Note that this space depends on the choice of the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. The space BMO is a Banach space with the conventional seminorm being

\|F\|_{\text{BMO}}=\sup_{n=0,1,...}\sup_{a\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\mathbb{E}((F-\mathbb{E}(F_{n}\mid a))^{2}\mid a))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Theorem 2.2
.

For all numbers
r,\penalty\ 1<r<\infty,
the operator
I_{\alpha},\alpha=\frac{1}{r},
maps
L_{r}(\mathfrak{X})
to
\text{BMO}(\mathfrak{X},\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\})
continuously. In other words, there is a constant
C=C(r)
such that for all functions
F\in L_{r}(\mathfrak{X})
the series (
2.1
) converges in BMO and the inequality

\|I_{\alpha}[F]\|_{\text{BMO}}\leqslant C\|F\|_{r}

(2.3)

holds.

Our proof will go as follows. We will be dealing with the formally conjugate Riesz potential
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
instead of the initial one. Its domain is the pre-dual of the BMO space, which is the space
H_{1}
of martingales whose maximal functions belong to class
L_{1}
. Its range is the pre-dual of the
L_{r}
space, which is the
L_{r^{\prime}}
space (
r^{\prime}
is the conjugate exponent of
r
). Thus, the continuity of the operator
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
is expressed in terms of operators
M_{n}
,
E_{n}
and the maximal function. We refer the reader to
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
for additional information about martingale BMO and
H_{1}
spaces.

Consider an arbitrary martingale
F
of class
H_{1}
. After a single step, it splits into
s
conditional martingales,
s\in\{1,2,...,\infty\}
, each defined on their own atoms
a_{j},j=1,...,s
. For each atom, we obtain four values, named
p_{j},x_{j},y_{j}
and
A_{j}
. The value
p_{j}
is simply the probability
\mathbb{P}(a_{j})
, whereas the numbers
x_{j},y_{j},A_{j}
have a more complicated definition in terms of the maximal function and the Riesz potential. We claim that the continuity of the operator
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
follows from a numerical inequality regarding the four sequences.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\{x_{j}\},\{p_{j}\},\{A_{j}\}
and
\{y_{j}\}
be four sequences of
s
non-negative numbers and
\mu>0,p>1
be real numbers. Suppose that the following conditions hold:

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}p_{j}=1;

(2.4)

y_{j}\geqslant x_{j},\penalty\ j=1,...,s;

(2.5)

(1-p_{j})x_{j}\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k},\penalty\ k=1,...,s;

(2.6)

A_{j}\leqslant x_{j}(1-p_{j})+p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}(1-p_{j})^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},\penalty\ j=1,...,s.

(2.7)

Then,

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu A_{j}^{p}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu C^{\frac{p-1}{p}}A_{j}y_{j}^{p-1}\leqslant C((\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p})

(2.8)

for sufficiently large numbers
C\geqslant C(p)
.

Theorem
2.2
may now be proved in the following way. Firstly, we switch from the Riesz potential to its formally conjugate. We also reduce the inequality to the case of simple martingales, i.e. martingales that stop at some moment, as we would like to use induction with respect that moment. Secondly, we introduce the single step model and the auxiliary notation including the four sequences. Then we show that those sequences satisfy the conditions of Theorem
2.3
and prove the numerical inequality itself. Finally, we demonstrate how the continuity of operator
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
follows from this inequality.

3
Conjugate Riesz potential

We will work with the formally conjugate Riesz potential
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
rather than with the operator
I_{\alpha}
itself (we use the prime symbol to denote the formally conjugate operator since the asterisk will be associated with the maximal function). When we say “formally conjugate operator”, we mean that
\mathbb{E}(I_{\alpha}[f]g)=\mathbb{E}(fI_{\alpha}^{\prime}[g])
for all simple functions
f
and
g
(i.e. linear combinations of characteristic functions of atoms). First, we are going to list a few basic properties of the operators
E_{n}
and
M_{n}
, which will help us construct the formally conjugate operator. We omit the proof of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The operators
E_{n}
and
M_{n}
have the following properties.

1.

E_{n}^{\prime}=E_{n},M_{n}^{\prime}=M_{n}
;

2.

E_{n}E_{k}=E_{\min(n,k)}
;

3.

E_{n}M_{k}=M_{k}E_{n}
if
k\leqslant n
.

Note that since the operators
E_{n}
and
M_{n}
do not commute,
I_{\alpha}\neq I_{\alpha}^{\prime}
in general.

Now it becomes clear that the formally conjugate Riesz potential can be written as

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F.

(3.1)

The domain and range of the formally conjugate operator would be pre-dual spaces to
\text{BMO}(\mathfrak{X},\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\})
and
L_{r}(\mathfrak{X})
, respectively. The former is
H_{1}(\mathfrak{X},\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\})
. It is the space of martingales whose maximal function belongs to
L_{1}(\mathfrak{X})
. The latter is
L_{r^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{X})
, where
r^{\prime}=\frac{r}{r-1}
is the conjugate exponent of
r
.

We need to clarify how the
H_{1}
-norm of a random variable is defined. To compute
\|F\|_{H_{1}}
, we construct the martingale
F_{n}=\mathbb{E}(F|\mathcal{F}_{n})
. This martingale has the maximal function
F^{*}
. If this function is summable, its
L_{1}
-norm
\|F^{*}\|_{1}
is the
H_{1}
-norm of function
F
.

The next theorem describes the continuity of the formally conjugate Riesz potential from
H_{1}
to
L_{p}
. We will apply it with
p=r^{\prime}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all numbers
p,\penalty\ 1<p<\infty,
the operator
I^{\prime}_{\alpha},\alpha=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}},
maps
H_{1}(\mathfrak{X})
to
L_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
continuously. In other words, there is a constant
C=C(p)
such that for all functions
F\in L_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
the series (
3.1
) converges in
L_{p}
and the inequality

\|I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]\|_{p}\leqslant C\|F^{*}\|_{1}

(3.2)

holds.

We would like to note that this result is a martingale counterpart to the classical Stein–Weiss inequality (see Theorem H in
[
5
]
).

4
Truncating the series

In this section, we will show that it suffices to prove (
3.2
) only for simple martingales, i.e. martingales that stop at some step. We confirm this claim using identities from Lemma
3.1
. Consider a positive integer
N
. Note that if
N\geqslant n
, then

E_{N}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F=(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F=(E_{n}-E_{n-1})E_{n}M_{n}F=\\
=(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}E_{n}F=(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F_{n}.

If
N<n
, then

E_{N}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F=(E_{N}-E_{N})M_{n}F=0.

When combined, the two identities become

E_{N}I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F_{n}.

In other words, the martingale generated by the random variable
I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]
is the martingale

\{(I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F])_{N}\}=\{\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F_{n}\}.

Suppose we have managed to prove (
3.2
) for simple martingales and are now trying to prove it for an arbitrary function
F
. Since
(I^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{N}
only depends on
F_{0},F_{1},...,F_{N}
, for each
N
we have the bound

\|(I^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{N}\|_{p}\leqslant C\|F_{N}^{*}\|_{1}\leqslant C\|F^{*}\|_{1}.

Thus, the martingale
\{(I^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{N}\}
belongs to
L_{p}
and has
L_{p}
-norm less than or equal to
C\|F^{*}\|_{1}
. Since
p>1
, by Doob’s convergence theorem the series (
3.1
) converges in
L_{p}
to a random variable and Theorem
3.1
holds.

From now on we will be concerned with the simple martingale version of (
3.2
), which states

\|(I^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{N}\|_{p}\leqslant C\|F_{N}^{*}\|_{1}.

5
Single step model

Truncating the series in the previous section allows us to use induction in
N
. The induction basis for
N=0
is trivial. To prove the induction step, we introduce new notation.

Consider the first step of the martingale
F
with mean value
\bar{f}=F_{0}
. The corresponding algebra
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is generated by the atoms
a_{j},j=1,...,s
(with, possibly,
s=\infty
). For each atom
a_{j}
of positive probability
p_{j}
there is a conditional subspace
\mathfrak{X}_{j}=(a_{j},\mathcal{F}\cap 2^{a_{j}},\frac{1}{p_{j}}\mathbb{P}\mid_{a_{j}})
. The restrictions of random variables
F_{1},F_{2},...
on the conditional subspaces form conditional martingales, which we label as
G^{j},j=1,..,s
. We call the mean values of those martingales (i.e. values of the variable
F_{1}
on different atoms)
f_{j}
.

Now recall the definition of the conjugate Riesz potential
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}[F]
as the sum (
3.1
). We define a non-negative random variable
A
so that the first summand is
\pm A
, and a non-negative random variable
B
such that the remaining sum is
\pm B
. The
L_{1}
-norm of the maximal function
F^{*}
is a sum of integrals over different atoms. We denote the integral over the atom
a_{j}
by
y_{j}
. Using the same concept for the single step maximal function, we obtain smaller values
x_{j}
. The Values
A_{j}
are obtained in a similar manner, but instead of identities
\|F^{*}\|_{1}=\sum y_{j}
and
\|F_{1}^{*}\|_{1}=\sum x_{j}
we have
\|A\|_{p}^{p}=\sum A_{j}^{p}
.

6
Verification of the conditions

Before proving Theorem
2.3
, we check that it is applicable to the four sequences
p_{j},x_{j},y_{j},a_{j}
constructed above. Conditions (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) are evident by construction. Now let us verify the condition (
2.6
), which we will refer to as “the non-singularity condition” from now on.

Lemma 6.1
.

The inequality
(1-p_{j})x_{j}\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}
holds for all j = 1,…,s.

Proof.

If
x_{j}\leqslant 2|\bar{f}|
, then

(1-p_{j})x_{j}\leqslant 2(1-p_{j})|\bar{f}|=2\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{k}|\bar{f}|\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}.

If
x_{j}>2|\bar{f}|
, then, obviously,
x_{j}=p_{j}|f_{j}|
, so

x_{j}=|\bar{f}-\sum\limits_{k\neq s}p_{k}f_{k}|\leqslant|\bar{f}|+\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{k}|f_{k}|\leqslant\frac{x_{j}}{2}+\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k},

\frac{x_{j}}{2}\leqslant\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k},\penalty\ (1-p_{j})x_{j}\leqslant x_{j}\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}.

∎

The last condition to verify is (
2.7
).

Lemma 6.2
.

The inequality
A_{j}\leqslant x_{j}(1-p_{j})+p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}(1-p_{j})^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}
holds for all j = 1, …, s (recall that
p^{\prime}
is the conjugate exponent
\frac{p}{p-1}
).

Proof.

Recall that

F_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}f_{j}\mathbb{I}_{a_{j}},\penalty\ p_{j}=\mathbb{P}(a_{j}),\penalty\ \bar{f}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{s}p_{k}f_{k},\penalty\ x_{j}=\|F_{1}^{*}\mathbb{I}_{a_{j}}\|_{1}=p_{j}\max(|f_{j}|,|\bar{f}|),

and so

M_{1}F_{1}=E_{1}M_{1}F_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}f_{j}p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\mathbb{I}_{a_{j}},\penalty\ E_{0}M_{1}F_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}f_{j}p_{j}^{1+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},

A|_{a_{j}}=|E_{0}M_{1}F_{1}-M_{1}F_{1}||_{a_{j}}=|f_{j}p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(1-p_{j})-\sum\limits_{k\neq j}f_{k}p_{k}^{1+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}|,

A_{j}=p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}A|_{a_{j}}=|f_{j}p_{j}(1-p_{j})-\sum\limits_{k\neq j}f_{k}p_{k}^{1+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}|\leqslant x_{j}(1-p_{j})+\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}p_{k}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant\\
\leqslant x_{j}(1-p_{j})+p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}(f_{k}p_{k})^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{k}^{\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}}})^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\leqslant x_{j}(1-p_{j})+p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}x_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}(1-p_{j})^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}.

∎

7
Proof of the numerical theorem

Having obtained the four conditions, we now fully detach ourselves from the martingale nature of the problem and prove the numerical inequality (
2.8
). A tool used to prove this theorem is the non-singularity lemma. It is a quantification of the following statement: if neither of the numbers
x_{j}
is significantly larger than the others, then the sum
\sum x_{j}^{p}
cannot constitute a large portion of
(\sum x_{j})^{p}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

If non-negative numbers
x_{j}
satisfy the non-singularity condition (
2.6
) with probabilities
p_{j}
and the number
p_{1}
is the greatest of them, then

\big(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}\big)^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}^{p}\gtrsim(1-p_{1})x_{1}^{p}+\big(\sum\limits_{j>1}x_{j}\big)^{p}.

Proof.

Since the inequality is homogenous, we may assume
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}=1
. Note that

1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}^{p}\geqslant 1-(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j})\max\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}^{p-1}=1-\max\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}^{p-1}.

If neither of the numbers
x_{i}
is greater than
\frac{4}{5}
, then the final difference is at least
1-(\frac{4}{5})^{p-1}\gtrsim 1\geqslant(1-p_{1})x_{1}^{p}+(\sum\limits_{j>1}x_{j})^{p}
. If there is a number
x_{j}>\frac{4}{5}
, then it must be
x_{1}
since from the non-singularity condition
x_{j}\leqslant\frac{2}{3-p_{j}}
and all probabilities
p_{j}
except
p_{1}
are at most
\frac{1}{2}
. If
x_{1}>\frac{4}{5}
, denote
\sum\limits_{j>1}x_{j}
by
x
. Then

(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}^{p}\geqslant(x_{1}+x)^{p}-x_{1}^{p}-x^{p}=\int\limits_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+x}pt^{p-1}dt-x^{p}\geqslant pxx_{1}^{p-1}-x^{p}\geqslant\\
\geqslant(p-1)xx_{1}^{p-1}\gtrsim 3xx_{1}^{p-1}\geqslant x^{p}+(1-p_{1})x_{1}^{p}

(the final step uses the non-singularity condition
(1-p_{1})x_{1}\leqslant 2x
and the inequality
x_{1}>x
).
∎

Our next goal is to reduce (
2.8
) to the non-singular case when
y_{k}(1-p_{k})\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j}
for each
k
. Consider the difference between the right hand and left hand parts of (
2.8
) as a function of
y_{k}
. Its derivative is equal to

Cp\big[(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p-1}-y_{k}^{p-1}\big]-\mu(p-1)C^{\frac{p-1}{p}}A_{k}y_{k}^{p-2}.

Denote
\frac{\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j}}{y_{k}}
by
\theta
. Then the derivative is equal to

Cpy_{k}^{p-1}\big[(1+\theta)^{p-1}-1-\frac{\mu(p-1)}{pC^{\frac{1}{p}}}\frac{A_{k}}{y_{k}}\big].

The bound (
2.7
) along with the non-singularity condition implies

A_{k}\leqslant 3\sum\limits_{j\neq k}x_{j}\leqslant 3\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j},

so the derivative is at least

Cpy_{k}^{p-1}((1+\theta)^{p-1}-1-\frac{3\mu(p-1)}{pC^{\frac{1}{p}}}\theta).

When
\theta<2
and the constant
C
is so large that
(p-1)\max(1,3^{p-2})\geqslant\frac{3\mu(p-1)}{pC^{\frac{1}{p}}}
, we can see that

(1+\theta)^{p-1}-1-\frac{3(p-1)}{C^{\frac{1}{p}}}\theta=\int\limits_{0}^{\theta}(p-1)(1+t)^{p-2}-\frac{3\mu(p-1)}{pC^{\frac{1}{p}}}dt\geqslant 0,

so the derivative is non-negative. That means we may reduce the number
y_{k}
until either
y_{k}=x_{k}
or
\theta=2
, and the resulting inequality will imply the initial one. In the former case
y_{k}(1-p_{k})=x_{k}(1-p_{k})\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{j\neq k}x_{j}\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j}
, and in the latter
y_{k}(1-p_{k})\leqslant y_{k}=2\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j}
. Note that if the number
y_{k}
was reduced this way, then
y_{k}\geqslant y_{j}
for all
j
both before and after the reduction. Since
y_{k}(1-p_{k})\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{j\neq k}y_{j}
by construction and
y_{k^{\prime}}(1-p_{k^{\prime}})\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{j\neq{k^{\prime}}}y_{j}
follows from
y_{k^{\prime}}\leqslant y_{k}
for all other
k^{\prime}
, the non-singularity condition holds for the new numbers
y_{j}
.

Now we may increase each number
x_{j}
to its respective
y_{j}
. The inequality (
2.8
) is not affected by this, and the upper bound (
2.7
) only increases. All that remains is to prove the inequality when
x_{j}=y_{j}
.

The inequality (
2.8
) is equivalent to two separate inequalities.

Lemma 7.2
.

The following inequalities hold:

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}A_{j}^{p}\lesssim(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p};

(7.1)

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}A_{j}y_{j}^{p-1}\lesssim(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}.

(7.2)

Proof.

If (
7.1
) holds with constant
C_{1}
and (
7.2
) holds with constant
C_{2}
, then (
2.8
) holds for constants
C
such that
\mu C_{1}+\mu C^{\frac{p-1}{p}}C_{2}\leqslant C
. The proof for each of the inequalities is rather straightforward.

We start with (
7.1
). Note that
(\frac{x+y}{2})^{p}\leqslant\frac{x^{p}+y^{p}}{2}
from Jensen’s inequality for the function
|x|^{p}
, so,
(x+y)^{p}\lesssim x^{p}+y^{p}
. Then, (
2.7
) implies

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}A_{j}^{p}\lesssim\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}(1-y_{j})^{p}+\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{j}y_{k}^{p}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}((1-p_{j})^{p}+(1-p_{j}))\lesssim\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})\lesssim(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}

(the final step uses Lemma
7.1
). Moving to (
7.2
), we immediately see that

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}A_{j}y_{j}^{p-1}\leqslant\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})+(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j}))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{j}y_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}).

Applying H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder’s inequality to the second summand yields

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j}))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{j}y_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\sum\limits_{k\neq j}p_{j}y_{k}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}=\\
=(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})))^{\frac{1}{p}}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j})).

Overall, the sum is at most
2\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}(y_{j}^{p}(1-p_{j}))\lesssim(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}
by Lemma
7.1
. This concludes the proof.∎

8
Obtaining the continuity of the conjugate Riesz potential

Recall that the functions
A
and
B
were chosen in such a way that
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}[F]=\pm A\pm B
, and, by definition of the values
y_{j}
,
\|F^{*}\|_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}
. Thus, (
3.2
) can be written as

\int\limits_{\Omega}|\pm A\pm B|^{p}d\mathbb{P}\leqslant C^{p}(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}

(8.1)

for some constant
C
, and we will prove its stronger form

\int\limits_{\Omega}(A+B)^{p}d\mathbb{P}\leqslant C(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}

(8.2)

for some other constant, which we also call
C
.

Our first step in proving this inequality is to obtain an upper bound for the
L_{p}
-norm of the function
B
using induction hypothesis. Note that

((E_{n+1}-E_{n})M_{n+1}F_{n+1})|_{a_{j}}=(E_{n}-E_{n-1})(p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}M_{n})G_{n}^{j}.

Here we slightly abuse the notation: on the left hand side, the operators
E_{n}
and
M_{n+1}
are operators of averaging and multiplication on
\mathfrak{X}
, whereas the operators on the right hand side act on
\mathfrak{X}_{j}
. Using the induction hypothesis, we see

B|_{a_{j}}=p_{j}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[G^{j}],

\int\limits_{a_{j}}|B|^{p}d\mathbb{P}=p_{j}\int\limits_{a_{j}}|B|^{p}d\mathbb{P}_{j}=p_{j}^{1+\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}}\int\limits_{a_{j}}|I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[G^{j}]|^{p}d\mathbb{P}_{j}\leqslant Cp_{j}^{p}\|F^{*}|_{a_{j}}\|^{p}_{1}=Cy_{j}^{p}.

(8.3)

To conclude the proof, we use the properties of the power functions to split the power
(A+B)^{p}
into three parts. Our proof will be slightly different depending on whether
1<p\leqslant 2
or
p>2
. If
1<p\leqslant 2
, then
(x+y)^{p-1}\leqslant x^{p-1}+y^{p-1}
for any positive numbers
x
and
y
. If
p>2
, this inequality does not hold, but the function
|\cdot|^{p-1}
is convex. Thus, using Jensen’s inequality, we note that
(\frac{x+y}{2})^{p-1}\leqslant\frac{x^{p-1}+y^{p-1}}{2}
, or
(x+y)^{p-1}\leqslant 2^{p-2}(x^{p-1}+y^{p-1})
. The two cases can be combined into

(x+y)^{p}\leqslant\max(1,2^{p-2})(x^{p-1}+y^{p-1}).

Using this inequality and the convexity of the function
|\cdot|^{p}
(which is true in both cases), we get the bound

(A+B)^{p}=B^{p}+\int\limits_{B}^{A+B}pt^{p-1}dt\leqslant B^{p}+Ap(A+B)^{p-1}\leqslant B^{p}+\mu(A^{p}+AB^{p-1}),

where
\mu=p\max(1,2^{p-2})
. For each atom
a_{j}
this means

\int\limits_{a_{j}}(A+B)^{p}d\mathbb{P}\leqslant\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p}d\mathbb{P}+\mu A_{j}^{p}+\mu A|_{a_{j}}\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p-1}d\mathbb{P}.

Applying H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder’s inequality to the last integral, we get

\int\limits_{a_{j}}(A+B)^{p}d\mathbb{P}\leqslant\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p}d\mathbb{P}+\mu A_{j}^{p}+\mu A|_{a_{j}}\|\mathbb{I}_{a_{j}}\|_{p}\|B^{p-1}\mathbb{I}_{a_{j}}\|_{p^{\prime}}=\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p}d\mathbb{P}+\mu A_{j}^{p}+\mu A_{j}(\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p}d\mathbb{P})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}.

Finally, substitution of
\int\limits_{a_{j}}B^{p}d\mathbb{P}
with the bound from (
8.3
) yields

\int\limits_{a_{j}}(A+B)^{p}d\mathbb{P}\leqslant Cy_{j}^{p}+\mu A_{j}^{p}+C^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mu A_{j}y_{j}^{p-1}.

(8.4)

Substituting the left-hand part in (
8.2
) with a sum of these estimates and moving
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}
to the other side, we come to the inequality

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu A_{j}^{p}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu C^{\frac{p-1}{p}}A_{j}y_{j}^{p-1}\leqslant C((\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j})^{p}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{p}).

Since the four sequences
p_{j},x_{j},y_{j},A_{j}
satisfy the four conditions of Theorem
2.3
, this inequality holds true, from which the continuity of the conjugate Riesz potential follows, completing the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

9
Lack of continuity to
L_{\infty}

Since the operator
I_{\alpha}
is a continuous mapping from
L_{p}
to
L_{q}
when
\alpha=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}
and
1<p<q<\infty
, it is somewhat natural to expect that it is also a continuous mapping from
L_{p^{\prime}}
to
L_{\infty}
when
\alpha=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. However, we demonstrate that this is not true for any filtration. To prove that, we fix the filtration and show that for such filtration the partial sums of the series (
3.1
) are not uniformly bounded as operators from
L_{1}
to
L_{p}
. Thus, their formal conjugates do not converge in norm to a bounded operator from
L_{p^{\prime}}
to
L_{\infty}
.

Consider a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n}
on the space
\mathfrak{X}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and construct a sequence of atoms
a_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
with the following properties:

1.

a_{0}=\Omega
;

2.

for each
n

a_{n+1}\subset a_{n}
and
\mathbb{P}(a_{n})>0
;

3.

Either
\mathbb{P}(a_{n+1})=\mathbb{P}(a_{n})
or
\mathbb{P}(a_{n+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(a_{n})
.

Given a fixed atom
a_{n}
, we select the atom
a_{n+1}
the following way. If
a_{n}
is partitioned into smaller sub-atoms during step
n+1
, then we choose
a_{n+1}
as the second greatest in probability among those sub-atoms. If
a_{n}
is not partitioned, then we have to choose
a_{n+1}=a_{n}
. Since the probability space
\mathfrak{X}
has no atoms and
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
is a filtration, we have
\mathbb{P}(a_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Denote
d_{n}=\mathbb{P}(a_{n})
.

Consider the martingale
F_{n}=d_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{I}_{a_{n}}
. Clearly, the martingale has the
L_{1}
-norm of 1. We also have
E_{n}M_{n}F_{n}=M_{n}F_{n}=d_{n}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathbb{I}_{a_{n}}
and
E_{n-1}M_{n}F_{n}=\frac{d_{n}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}}{d_{n-1}}\mathbb{I}_{a_{n-1}}
. Now we calculate the conjugate Riesz potential of the martingale
F
:

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(E_{n}-E_{n-1})M_{n}F_{n}=-F_{0}+\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}(M_{n}-M_{n+1})F_{n}=\\
=-F_{0}+\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}(d_{n}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}-d_{n+1}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}})F_{n}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(d_{n}^{-\frac{1}{p}}-\frac{d_{n+1}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}}{d_{n}})\mathbb{I}_{a_{n}}-1.

Note that if
d_{n}=d_{n+1}
, then the corresponding summand
(d_{n}^{-\frac{1}{p}}-\frac{d_{n+1}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}}{d_{n}})\mathbb{I}_{a_{n}}
is zero, and if
d_{n+1}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}d_{n}
, then

(d_{n}^{-\frac{1}{p}}-\frac{d_{n+1}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}}{d_{n}})\geqslant(1-2^{-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}})d_{n}^{-\frac{1}{p}}.

Remove duplicates from the sequence
\{a_{n}\}
to get an infinite subsequence
\{a_{n_{k}}\}
. Using the new notation, we get a pointwise estimate

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]\geqslant(1-2^{-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}})\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}d_{n_{k}}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathbb{I}_{a_{n_{k}}}-1,

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]|_{a_{n_{k}}\setminus a_{n_{k+1}}}\geqslant-1+(1-2^{-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}})\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}d_{n_{l}}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\gtrsim\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}d_{n_{l}}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\geqslant d_{n_{k}}^{-\frac{1}{p}}

for sufficiently large
k\geqslant K(\mathfrak{X},p)
(i.e. such that
(1-2^{-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}})d_{n_{k}}^{-\frac{1}{p}}>2
). Then

\|I^{\prime}_{\alpha}[F]\|_{p}^{p}\gtrsim\sum\limits_{k=K(\mathfrak{X},p)}^{\infty}\frac{d_{n_{k}}-d_{n_{k+1}}}{d_{n_{k}}}\geqslant\sum\limits_{k=K(\mathfrak{X},p)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}.

Thus, the partial sums cannot be uniformly bounded operators from
L_{1}
to
L_{p}
, and the formally conjugate operators cannot converge to a bounded operator from
L_{p^{\prime}}
to
L_{\infty}
.
