Minimax Optimality in Contextual Dynamic Pricing with General Valuation Models
Xueping Gong, Jiheng Zhang

Abstract
Dynamic pricing, the practice of adjusting prices based on contextual factors, has gained significant attention due to its impact on revenue maximization. In this paper, we address the contextual dynamic pricing problem, which involves pricing decisions based on observable product features and customer characteristics. We propose a novel algorithm that achieves improved regret bounds while minimizing assumptions about the problem. Our algorithm discretizes the unknown noise distribution and combines the upper confidence bounds with a layered data partitioning technique to effectively regulate regret in each episode. These techniques effectively control the regret associated with pricing decisions, leading to the minimax optimality. Specifically, our algorithm achieves a regret upper bound of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ρ 𝒱 1 3 ​ ( δ ) ​ T 2 3 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\rho_{\mathcal{V}}^{\frac{1}{3}}(\delta)T^{\frac{2}{3}}) , where ρ 𝒱 ​ ( δ ) \rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta) represents the estimation error of the valuation function. Importantly, this bound matches the lower bound up to logarithmic terms, demonstrating the minimax optimality of our approach. Furthermore, our method extends beyond linear valuation models commonly used in dynamic pricing by considering general function spaces. We simplify the estimation process by reducing it to general offline regression oracles, making implementation more straightforward.

Introduction

The contextual dynamic pricing problem involves setting real-time prices for products or services based on contextual factors like product details and customer characteristics.
It has received significant attention in the literature due to its practical applications and its impact on revenue maximization.
For a comprehensive review of the literature on dynamic pricing, we refer readers to
[
8
]
, and for specific applications,
Saharan et al. 2020
provide valuable insights.

Recent works have particularly focused on feature-based (or contextual) pricing models,
which consider observable product features to determine both the market value of a product and the pricing strategy
[
9
,
4
,
5
]
.
These models account for product heterogeneity and enable personalized pricing.
However, designing low-regret policies in this setting presents several challenges.
Firstly, the demand curve continuously shifts due to changes in covariates over time.
Secondly, the shape of the demand curve is often unknown due to the lack of information on the market noise distribution,
which may not follow a specific parametric form.
Consequently, solving the dynamic pricing problem requires precise estimation of the demand function across a wide range of prices,
diverging from non-contextual dynamic pricing and non-parametric bandit problems,
where estimation around a single maximum is sufficient.
Thus, the contextual dynamic pricing problem presents unique challenges and potentially leads to higher regret compared to its non-contextual counterpart.

Despite extensive research on the contextual dynamic pricing problem,
previous findings suffer from either strong assumptions that restrict their applicability or sub-optimal regret bounds.
In our work, we aim to address both limitations by achieving improved regret bounds while minimizing assumptions about the problem.

To tackle the problem, we employs an explore-then-UCB approach and propose an epoch-based learning framework.
Initially, we conduct a pure exploration phase to gather samples for learning the true valuation function.
Subsequently, an UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) phase is executed to set a suitable price based on the evaluation estimator.
During the UCB phase, we discretize the domain of the unknown distribution function into equal-length intervals to effectively handle the challenge posed by its inherent uncertainty.
Notably, this discretization process leads to a linear bandit structure, enabling the development of upper confidence bounds that robustly account for estimation errors and biases arising from valuation estimation.
To further leverage the distinctive structure of this linear bandit, we employ a layered data partitioning technique to effectively regulate regret in each episode.
The adaptive nature of this technique eliminates the need for a Lipschitz constant, enhancing the flexibility and practicality of our approach.
Lastly, we carefully select an appropriate number of discretization intervals to strike a balance between the regret arising from the discrete-part and continuous-part of the problem,
ultimately achieving the minimax optimality of this challenging problem.

Related work

Dynamic pricing.

Dynamic pricing has been an active area of research,
driven by advancements in data technology and the increasing availability of customer information.
Initial research focused on non-contextual dynamic pricing without covariates
[
3
,
4
]
.
For example,
Wang et al. 2021
employed the UCB approach with local-bin approximations, achieving an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{(m+1)/(2m+1)})
regret for
m
-th smooth demand functions and establishing a matching lower bound.
However, these approaches did not incorporate covariates into pricing policies, making our model fundamentally distinct.

In the domain of dynamic pricing with covariates, the linear customer valuation model has been widely adopted.

Javanmard and Nazerzadeh 2019
studied this model assuming known noise distribution, but their methods were limited when the noise distribution was unknown or not log-concave.
In contrast,
Golrezaei et al. 2019
considered an ambiguity set for the noise distribution, achieving a regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{2/3})
compared to a robust benchmark.
However, their approach faced challenges with ambiguity sets encompassing infinite distributions.
Our proposed model addresses the general case of an unknown noise distribution and establishes regret bounds by comparing against the true optimal policy.

Chen et al. 2024
explored nonparametric aspects of the unknown demand function, using adaptive binning of the covariate space to achieve a regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{d_{0}+2}{d_{0}+4}})
. Notably, our method outperforms theirs when
d_{0}\geq 2
.
Under the Cox proportional hazards (PH) model,

Choi et al. 2023
introduced the CoxCP algorithm,
which demonstrated a regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{2/3})
.
However, their approach relied on the separability of the unknown linear structure and noise distribution in the PH model, making it unsuitable for our pricing model where these components are entangled.
Recent work by
Xu and Wang 2022
proposed an adaptive pricing policy with
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
regret for adversarial contexts and bounded noise distributions.
Our method improves the regret to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
under Lipschitz noise distribution,
while maintaining computational efficiency compared to the exponential computations required by the EXP4-based policy in
Xu and Wang 2022
.

Other related studies
[
17
,
16
,
9
]
share similarities with our work in terms of settings,
but differ in their assumptions about the smoothness of the noise distribution.
For example,
Luo et al. 2022
proposed an epoch-based algorithm with regret bounds of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d_{0}^{2}T^{\frac{2}{3}})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d_{0}T^{\frac{3}{4}})
under different smoothness assumptions.

Fan et al. 2024
considered
m
-th differentiable distributions and achieved a regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((d_{0}T)^{\frac{2m+1}{4m-1}})
using the Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression estimator.
In comparison, our method achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}})
regrets when applied to linear valuation models,
demonstrating minimax optimality while relying on minimal assumptions.

Contextual bandit.

Our pricing policy shares connections with bandit algorithms
[
14
,
11
,
1
,
22
,
2
]
,
which aim to balance exploration and exploitation in decision-making.
Specifically, our approach relates to the concept of misspecified linear bandits
[
2
,
16
]
within the context of dynamic pricing.
Unlike traditional bandit algorithms, dynamic pricing requires consideration of both estimation variance and the bias resulting from linear parameter perturbation.
Notably, we have discovered an intriguing finding that by leveraging the unique structure of the dynamic pricing problem,
we can achieve a more precise and improved regret bound compared to directly applying complex existing algorithms designed for misspecified linear bandits.
This improvement is mainly attributed to the fact that the bias encountered in each round is consistent across all prices (actions).
While previous research has explored bandit algorithms and misspecified linear bandits,
our work extends these concepts to the dynamic pricing domain,
incorporating the specific characteristics and demands of pricing decisions.
This underscores the importance of leveraging the distinctive structure and attributes of the pricing context in order to achieve superior performance.

Basic Setting

In the context of contextual dynamic pricing, a potential customer arrives at the platform during each round
t\in[T]
,
and the seller observes a covariate
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
representing the product features and customer characteristics.
Upon observing
x_{t}
, the customer’s valuation of the product, denoted as
v_{t}=v^{*}(x_{t})+\epsilon_{t}
,
is determined by a linear function of
v^{*}(x_{t})
and a market noise term
\epsilon_{t}
.
The value function
v^{*}
belongs to the function space
\mathcal{V}
,
and the noise terms
\{\epsilon_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
are independently and identically drawn from an unknown zero-mean distribution
F
.

If the customer’s valuation
v_{t}
exceeds the seller’s set price
p_{t}
, a sale occurs,
resulting in the seller collecting a revenue of
p_{t}
.
However, if
v_{t}
is lower than
p_{t}
,
the customer leaves without making a purchase,
and the seller does not receive any revenue.
We denote the occurrence of a sale as
y_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{\{v_{t}\geq p_{t}\}}
,
which can be modeled as a Bernoulli random variable with a parameter of
1-F(p_{t}-v^{*}(x_{t}))
.
Consequently, the reward from the sale is given by
r_{t}=p_{t}y_{t}
.
The triplet
(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})
captures the information pertaining to the pricing procedure at time
t
.

Given the customer choice model and the covariate
x
,
the expected reward of setting a price
p
can be expressed as
p(1-F(p-v^{*}(x)))
.
The optimal price
p^{*}(x)
is defined as the price that maximizes
p(1-F(p-v^{*}(x)))
.
In the dynamic pricing process, the seller dynamically sets prices and observes binary feedbacks to collect instant revenues while gathering information to estimate
v^{*}
and
F
.
Apparently, learning
v^{*}
and
F
gives the seller much more information to estimate the market value of a new product given its feature covariates.
On the other hand, the seller also wants to always give optimized price so as to maximize the expected revenue.
It is necessary to have a good policy that strikes a balance between exploration (collecting additional information for better future revenues) and exploitation (utilizing current knowledge for immediate revenues based on the learned model).

The regret at time
t
is defined as the loss in reward resulting from setting the price
p_{t}
compared to the optimal price
p^{*}(x_{t})
.
The cumulative regret over the horizon of
T
periods, denoted as
Reg(T)
, is given by the expression:

Reg(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}p^{*}(x_{t})(1-F(p^{*}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t})))-p_{t}(1-F(p_{t}-v^{*}(x_{t}))).

To evaluate the performance, we consider the expected cumulative regret
\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]
, which takes into account the randomness of the covariates and the potential randomness of the pricing policy.
The goal of our contextual dynamic pricing is to decide the price
p_{t}
for covariate
x_{t}
at time
t
,
by utilizing all historical data
\{(x_{s},p_{s},y_{s}),s\in[t-1]\}
, in order to minimize the expected cumulative regret.

Assumption 2.1
(Bounded Valuation)

For each round
t
, the customers’ valuations
v^{*}(x_{t})\in[0,B]
for a known constant
B
.

In
2.1
, a known upper bound is considered for the customers’ valuations,
which is a reasonable assumption for real-life products.
It is worth noting that this assumption also implies the existence of a known upper bound
B
for the optimal prices.
Moving forward, we introduce the Lipschitz condition in
2.2
,
which is widely discussed in the literature
[
17
,
16
,
5
]
.

2.2
is satisfied by
F
with bounded derivative as considered in
[
5
,
9
,
13
]
,
but fits for a broader class of
F
.

Assumption 2.2
(Lipschitz Continuity)

The noise distribution
F
is Lipschitz continuous with a positive constant
L
, i.e.,

|F(x)-F(y)|\leq L|x-y|,\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

3
Algorithm Design

In this section, we present a distribution-free pricing policy designed to address the contextual dynamic pricing problem in the presence of unknown
v^{*}
and unknown
F
.
To tackle this challenge, we propose an episode-based learning framework, outlined in
algorithm

1
, consisting of three phases: a pure exploration phase, an estimation phase, and a UCB phase.
Given the lack of prior knowledge regarding the horizon length
T
, we employ the doubling trick to partition the horizon into episodes.
This technique eliminates the requirement of knowing
T
and allows us to dynamically adapt our pricing strategy.
Furthermore, our algorithmic construction ensures that the size of candidate prices remains bounded within each episode.
The boundedness property is essential for controlling regret and achieving minimax optimality, ensuring that our pricing policy performs well under uncertainty.
The episode-based learning framework allows us to adaptively refine our pricing strategy as additional data is gathered and improved estimations of
v^{*}
and
F
are obtained.
By partitioning the horizon into episodes, our approach enables iterative improvement of the pricing policy, leading to near-optimal performance.

Estimation.

To overcome the challenge of not directly observing the valuation
v_{t}
in the dynamic pricing problem,
we leverage a key property.
During each episode, we employ a uniform random policy
p_{t}\sim Unif(0,B)
to generate data for estimating the true value
v^{*}
.
This approach results in a linear regression structure that incorporates the signal of
v^{*}
.
Specifically, we have the following relationship:

\mathbb{E}[By_{t}|x_{t}]=B\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{I}_{\{p_{t}\leq v^{*}(x_{t})+\epsilon_{t}\}}|x_{t},\epsilon_{t}]|x_{t}]=B\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{v^{*}(x_{t})+\epsilon_{t}}{B}\Big|x_{t}\Big]=v^{*}(x_{t})

This property allows us to decouple the estimation of
v^{*}

F

v^{*}
independently.
To achieve the minimax optimality, we set the exploration phase length

This choice ensures minimax optimality in terms of
T

|\mathcal{V}|

To ensure that the exploration phase length does not exceed

By addressing these broader problem domains, we demonstrate the versatility and relevance of our proposed methodology in a wider range of applications.

Update
\Psi_{t+1}^{s}=\Psi_{t}^{s}\cup\{t\}

Discretization for F-Learning.

During the UCB phase in episode
k
, we utilize the regression estimator
\hat{v}_{k}
to drive a UCB procedure that strikes a balance between
F
-learning and revenue maximization.
To explore potential optimal prices
p\in(0,B)
efficiently,
we focus our attention on the
F
-values within the range of
p-\hat{v}_{k}(x)
.
Specifically, we restrict our
F
-learning to the interval
[-\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty},B+\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}]
by discretizing it into
d_{k}
sub-intervals and concentrating our learning on the
d_{k}
midpoints
\{m_{j}\}_{j\in[d_{k}]}
.
This approach, in contrast to
[
5
,
9
]
where the entire cumulative distribution function
F
is estimated,
simplifies the estimation task by focusing on a limited number of
F
values.
This discretization approach allows for a mutually reinforcing process between the discretized price selection and the accumulation of knowledge in
F
at these specific points, creating a closed loop of improvement.

After discretization,
Luo et al. 2024
formulate the inner UCB as a perturbed linear bandit problem,
with
\xi^{*}=(1-F(m_{1}),1-F(m_{2}),\cdots,1-F(m_{d_{k}}))
as the parameter for this perturbed linear bandit.
The action takes the form
(0,0,\cdots,p_{t},\cdots,0)
, with a single non-zero element
p_{t}=m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})
at the
j
-th position.
Although the
\xi^{*}
parameter is perturbed in each round, its magnitude can be controlled due to
2.2
and the estimation error of
\hat{v}_{k}
.
While
Luo et al. 2024
employ the elliptical potential lemma from
[
1
]
to address this problem,
the formulation fails to fully exploit the special structure of the dynamic pricing problem and can be optimized further to achieve a suboptimal regret order.

A crucial observation is that the number of actions in a given episode is bounded by
d_{k}
.
Existing literature, such as
[
15
,
2
]
, has demonstrated that in linear contextual bandit problems, the elliptical potential lemma is unnecessary.
They have shown that the regret bound can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d_{k}T_{k}\log^{3}(KT_{k})})
when dealing with time-varying action sets with bounded size
K
.

However, the methods proposed in
[
15
,
2
]
cannot be directly applied to our current setting.
One key challenge arises from the normalized parameters.
If we were to normalize
\xi^{*}
, an additional
\sqrt{d_{k}}
term would still arise.
However, we can take advantage of the fact that the action vector has only one non-zero component and satisfies
|(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j}))|\leq B
.
This observation allows us to focus on the UCB for each component individually, rather than considering the entire parameter vector.
By doing so, we can exploit the
\ell_{\infty}
estimation error for linear parameters,
which excludes the extra
\sqrt{d_{k}}
term compared to the
\ell_{2}
norm.
Therefore, we can apply the Azuma’s inequality rather than the elliptical potential lemma to obtain concentration bounds.
To obtain statistical dependence, we apply the layered data partitioning technique in our algorithmic construction.

The second challenge we face is related to the perturbation of
\xi^{*}
.
This perturbation introduces misspecification errors in the reformulated linear bandit,
resulting in a complex construction of the UCB.
When considering the price
p_{t}
and covariate
x_{t}
,
\mathbb{E}[y_{t}]
is equal to
1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t}))
,
which is only approximately equal to
\xi_{j}^{*}
rather than an exact equality.
It is evident, as indicated in
2.2
, that estimation errors of
\hat{v}_{k}
can propagate into misspecification errors.
This assumption implies that the perturbation is proportional to the estimation error.
If the estimator
\hat{v}_{k}
is accurate, then
\xi^{*}
remains unperturbed,
and the problem reduces to a classic linear bandit.
However, a poorer estimator implies a larger perturbation,
increasing the difficulty in solving the perturbed linear bandit problem and potentially leading to a larger regret as shown in
4.1
.

Layered Data Partitioning.

In each time period
t
, we begin by constructing the candidate action set
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{j\in[d_{k}]|m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\in(0,B)\}
.
Instead of utilizing all the data collected prior to time
t
for an ordinary least squares estimator for
\xi^{*}
,
we divide the preceding time periods into disjoint layers indexed by
s\in[S]
, such that
\cup_{s\in[S]}\Psi_{t}^{s}=[t-1]
.
At time
t
, we sequentially visit the layers
s\in[S]
and,
within each layer, calculate the upper confidence bound for each
\xi^{*}_{j}
using data collected exclusively during the periods in
\Psi_{t}^{s}
.
From OLS estimator for
\xi^{*}
, we know that the estimator for
\xi_{j}^{*}
is

w^{j}_{t,s}=\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}y_{\tau}}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}},

where
\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}
contains the rounds that select the index corresponding to
1-F(m_{j})
.
To determine the radius for
\xi^{*}_{j}
, we design the following expression:

r^{j}_{t,s}=\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}},1\right\}.

If
\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}
is empty, indicating no corresponding rounds were selected, the UCB for that index is set to infinity.

The UCB property described in
3.2
is highly intuitive.
The first term captures the variance resulting from the randomness of
y_{t}
given
x_{t}
and
p_{t}
,
while the second term represents the bias stemming from the estimation of
\hat{v}_{k}
.
As the UCB phase progresses, the variance gradually diminishes,
and the bias caused by estimation errors tends to approach zero with each subsequent episode.
With an increasing number of samples of
x_{t}
and
y_{t}
, our constructed
w_{t,s}^{j}
becomes increasingly accurate and converges to
\xi^{*}_{j}
.
It is important to note that our constructed UCB does not involve the bias term. This allows us to eliminate the need for knowledge of the Lipschitz constant.
The reason behind this is that we compare the UCB values on both sides,
and the bias term is the same for all indices.
Therefore, we can deduct the bias term at both sides in Step (a) of the algorithm and the Lipschitz term is implicitly absorbed in the adaptive term
B2^{1-s}
.
This advantage of our algorithm eliminates the need for explicit knowledge of the Lipschitz constant.

Lemma 3.2

Consider the episode
k
, any round
t
in the episode
k
and any layer
s
in the round
t
.
Then for each
j\in[d_{k}]
, we have

|\xi_{j}^{*}-w_{t,s}^{j}|\leq r_{t,s}^{j}+\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}},

with probability at least
1-\delta/(d_{k}T_{k})
.

Proof.

Due to statistical independence of samples indexed in
\Psi_{t}^{s}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[y_{\tau}|x_{\tau}]=1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau}))
for
{\tau}\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}^{s}
.
Then we apply the Azuma’s inequality to
\frac{p_{\tau}^{2}y_{\tau}}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}
.
We can obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathbb{E}[y_{\tau}|x_{\tau}]}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|\geq\frac{\sqrt{\ln(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{4}}}{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{d_{k}T_{k}}

Thanks to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{4}\leq\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\right)^{2}.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathbb{E}[y_{\tau}|x_{\tau}]}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|\geq r_{t,s}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathbb{E}[y_{\tau}|x_{\tau}]}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|\geq\frac{\sqrt{\ln(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{4}}}{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{d_{k}T_{k}}.

From
2.2
, we can obtain

\left|\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}(\xi^{*}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[y_{\tau}|x_{\tau}])}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}},

so by the triangular inequality, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\xi_{j}^{*}\Big|\geq r_{t,s}^{j}+\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathbb{E}[y_{t}|x_{t}]}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|+\Big|\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}(\xi^{*}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[y_{t}|x_{t}])}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|\geq r_{t,s}^{j}+\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|w_{t,s}^{j}-\frac{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathbb{E}[y_{t}|x_{t}]}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}\Big|\geq r_{t,s}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{d_{k}T_{k}}.

\square

The algorithm commences with
s=1
, corresponding to the widest confidence bands, and incrementally increases
s
, while simultaneously eliminating sub-optimal prices using OLS estimates from
\Psi_{t}^{s}
.
This procedure is meticulously designed to ensure that the “stopping layer”
s^{\prime}
determined at time
t
solely depends on
\{\Psi_{t}^{s}\}_{s\leq s^{\prime}}
,
effectively decoupling the statistical correlation in OLS estimates.
The validity of this property has been formally stated and proven in
[
15
,
2
]
.

Based on
3.2
, we construct a high-probability event

\Gamma_{k}=\left\{|\xi_{j}^{*}-w_{t,s}^{j}|\leq r_{t,s}^{j}+\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}},\forall t,\forall s,\forall j\right\}.

Then by the union bound, we have

\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Gamma_{k}})\leq\frac{\delta}{d_{k}T_{k}}\times T_{k}\times d_{k}\times S=\delta S.

For brevity, we denote
\eta_{t,s}^{j}=\frac{L\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}
.
Since
x_{t}
is i.i.d. sampled from a fixed distribution, we know that
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\eta_{t,s}^{j}]=L\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]
.

Recall that the index of the discrete best price is
j_{t}^{*}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{j\in[d_{k}]}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t})))
.
We define the discrete best price at the layer
s
as

j_{t,s}^{*}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{s}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t}))).

For simplicity, we introduce the revenue function

rev_{t}(p)=p(1-F(p-v^{*}(x_{t}))).

In
[
2
]
, their algorithm ensures
j^{*}_{t,s}=j^{*}_{t}
,
as the optimal action will not be eliminated when their constructed high-probability event holds.
In contrast, due to the misspecification error being proportional to
BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]
when transitioning to a new layer,
the revenue between prices indexed by
j_{t,s}^{*}
and
j_{t}^{*}
is naturally proportional to
s-1
.
Combining the above discussion, we proceed to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3

Given the event
\Gamma_{k}
,
then for each round
t
in the episode
k
and each layer
s
in the round

\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}}[rev_{t}(m_{j_{t}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))-rev_{t}(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))]\leq 4BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|](s-1).

We prove this lemma by induction on

For

.
Assume that the bound holds in the layer

Hence we assume that

Let

be the index with the highest UCB in

From Step (a) we know
\hat{j}_{t,s}\in\mathcal{A}_{s+1}.
Then we have

\displaystyle rev_{t}(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))-rev_{t}(m_{j_{t,s+1}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle rev_{t}(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))-rev_{t}(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}))-(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}))+2BL|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t})|.

From the definition to
\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}))-(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}))

\displaystyle(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(w_{t,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}+r_{t,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}})-(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(w_{t,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}-r_{t,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{BL\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}}p_{\tau}^{2}}+\frac{BL\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}p_{\tau}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m_{j_{t,s}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(w_{t,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}+r_{t,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}})-(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(w_{t,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+r_{t,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}})+2r_{t,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}(m_{\hat{j}_{t,s}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{BL\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t,s}^{*}}}p_{\tau}^{2}}+\frac{BL\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}p_{\tau}^{2}|\hat{v}_{k}(x_{\tau})-v^{*}(x_{\tau})|}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{\hat{j}_{t,s}}}p_{\tau}^{2}}

Since

4
Regret Analysis

In this section, we analyze the regret of our proposed
algorithm

1
.
We begin with analyzing a single episode in
algorithm

1
and then extend it to the entire horizon.

4.1
Upper bounds

From the initial pure exploration phase, we obtain an estimator
\hat{v}_{k}
.
As this estimator guides the UCB procedure,
it is important to understand how the estimation error affects the regret in the UCB phase.
A lower error rate in the estimation is expected to result in less regret.
However, achieving a lower error rate requires a longer exploration phase, which leads to more regret.
Therefore, in addition to the “inner” balance between revenue maximization and
F
-learning in the UCB phase,
there exists an “outer” balance between the regret in the exploration phase and the regret in the UCB phase,
both related to learning
v^{*}
.

For the round
t
at the episode
k
, define

{j}_{t}^{*}:=\mathop{\arg\max}_{j\in[d_{k}]}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t})))
.
By defining the discrete best prices as
\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}=m_{j_{t}^{*}}+\hat{v}(x_{t})
,
the time
t
regret can be decomposed as

\underbrace{\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F(\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))-{p}_{t}(1-F({p}_{t}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))}_{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{1}}+\underbrace{{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F({p}_{t}^{*}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))-\tilde{p}^{*}_{t}(1-F(\tilde{p}^{*}_{t}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))}_{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}}.

We refer
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{1}
and
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}
as the discrete-part and continuous-part regrets.
We first analyze the regret for the inner UCB algorithm.
To bound the discrete-part regret of the inner UCB algorithm,
we prove the following theorem.

Lemma 4.1

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, the expected regret of the discrete-part satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{1}]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil}\left(8B\sqrt{d_{k}T_{k}\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)\ln T_{k}}+12BL\ln^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|\log T/\delta)\ell_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}\log\ell_{k}\right).

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

We consider the episode
k
.
Let
\Psi_{0}
be the set of rounds for which an alternative is chosen when
r_{t,s}^{j}\leq 1/\sqrt{T_{k}}
.
Since
2^{-S}\leq 1/\sqrt{T_{k}}
, we have
\Psi_{0}\cup\cup_{s\in[S]}\Psi^{s}_{T_{k}+1}=[T_{k}]
.

Recall
{j}_{t}^{*}:=\mathop{\arg\max}_{j\in[d_{k}]}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))(1-F(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-v^{*}(x_{t})))
and we define the discrete empirical best price as
\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}=m_{{j}_{t}^{*}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})
.
We first bound the regret
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=2^{k-1}+T_{k}^{e}+1}^{2^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{1}]
at the episode
k
as following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=2^{k-1}+T_{k}^{e}+1}^{2^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{1}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2^{k-1}+T_{k}^{e}+1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F(\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))-{p}_{t}(1-F({p}_{t}-{v}^{*}(x_{t})))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2^{k-1}+T_{k}^{e}+1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big[rev_{t}(\tilde{p}^{*}_{t})-rev_{t}({p}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}\mathbb{E}\Big[rev_{t}(\tilde{p}^{*}_{t})-rev_{t}({p}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}4B\cdot 2^{-s}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|+4BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|](3s-2)|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|,

where the last inequality is due to
3.5
.

From
B.2
, we have

|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\leq 2^{s}\sqrt{2d_{k}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}.

Putting it back yields

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}4B\cdot 2^{-s}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|+4BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|](3s-2)|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}4B\sqrt{2d_{k}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}+4BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]\sum_{s=1}^{S}(3s-2)|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4B\sqrt{2d_{k}\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)S\sum_{s=1}^{S}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|}+4BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]\cdot 3T_{k}\log T_{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8B\sqrt{d_{k}T_{k}\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)\ln T_{k}}+12BL\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]T_{k}\log T_{k}.

The second inequality is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Notice that
T_{k}\leq\ell_{k}
and

\mathbb{E}_{x}[|\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x)|]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\hat{v}_{k}(x)-v^{*}(x))^{2}]}\leq\ln^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|\log T/\delta)\ell_{k}^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

due to
3.1
.
We obtain the desired result by summing over all episodes.

\square

In
4.1
, the first term saves an additional
\sqrt{d_{k}}
compared to the regret bounds presented in
[
16
]
.
This term captures the impact of uncertainty in estimating the values of points on
F
and its influence on the regret bounds.
It signifies the importance of accurately estimating the underlying distribution and how uncertainty in these estimates affects the regret.
The second term in
4.1
arises from the transformation of baselines in the linear bandit problems.
It highlights the connection between the estimation error of
\hat{v}_{k}
and the resulting regret bounds.
The presence of this component illustrates how inaccuracies in estimating the baselines can influence the regret in the UCB phase.
Essentially, it indicates that a higher level of estimation accuracy leads to a lower regret in the UCB phase.

We then delve into the analysis of the continuous-part of regret, denoted as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}
.
A denser discretization, achieved by increasing the number of price points, leads to a reduction in continuous-part regret.
This is because a finer grid of prices brings the discrete best price closer to the overall best price.
Due to algorithmic construction, the continuous-part regret can be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\ell_{k}}{d_{k}})
.
However, it is important to note that a larger value of
d_{k}
results in higher discrete-part regret, as indicated by
4.1
.
This is due to the increased number of candidate prices and the resulting complexity in the search process.
Therefore, it is necessary to find an appropriate value of
d_{k}
that optimally balances the discrete-part and continuous-part regrets.
One approach to achieving this balance is to scale
d_{k}
with
T_{k}^{\frac{1}{3}}
, which allows for effective regulation of the trade-off between the discrete-part and continuous-part regret.

Lemma 4.2

Under
2.1
, the expected regret of the continuous-part satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil}\frac{4BT_{k}}{d_{k}}.

Proof.

We consider the continuous-part regret at each round
t
in the episode
k
. It admits the form

p_{t}^{*}(1-F(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t})))-\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F(\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))),

where
p_{t}^{*}
is the overall best price and
\tilde{p}_{t}^{*}
is the discrete best price among the candidate set.

Denote
\dot{p}_{t}=\max\{0,\max_{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))\}
. Then we discuss case by case.

1.

\{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}\}=\emptyset
.
Then we have
\dot{p}_{t}=0
and

p_{t}^{*}\leq m_{1}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq-\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}+\frac{B+2\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}}{2d_{k}}+\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{B+2\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}}{2d_{k}}\leq\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}.

Therefore, we have
0\leq p_{t}^{*}-\dot{p}_{t}\leq\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}
.

2.

\{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}\}\neq\emptyset
and
\max_{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))<m_{d_{k}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})
.
Then we have
\max_{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))\leq\dot{p}_{t}\leq\max_{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))+\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}
.
Namely, we have
0\leq p_{t}^{*}-\dot{p}_{t}\leq\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}.

3.

\{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}\}\neq\emptyset
and
\max_{j\in[d_{k}],m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}}(m_{j}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t}))=m_{d_{k}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})
.
Since
m_{d_{k}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\geq B+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})-\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}-\|\hat{v}_{k}\|_{\infty}\geq B-\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}
,
then we have
B-\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}\leq m_{d_{k}}+\hat{v}_{k}(x_{t})\leq p_{t}^{*}\leq B
.
Therefore we have
p_{t}^{*}-\dot{p}_{t}\leq\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}
.

From above discussion, we conclude that
0\leq p_{t}^{*}-\dot{p}_{t}\leq\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}
.
Hence we have

\displaystyle p_{t}^{*}(1-F(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t})))-\dot{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F(\dot{p}_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p_{t}^{*}(1-F(\dot{p}_{t}-v^{*}(x_{t})))-\dot{p}_{t}^{*}(1-F(\dot{p}_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(p_{t}^{*}-\dot{p}_{t})(1-F(\dot{p}_{t}-v^{*}(x_{t})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3B}{2d_{k}}.

We first sum over all rounds in the episode
k
and then sum over all episodes.
We can obtain the desired results.

\square

Theorem 4.1

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, the expected regret satisfies

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]=\mathcal{O}(BT^{\frac{2}{3}}\log^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|/\delta)\log^{\frac{3}{2}}T)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Since the price
p_{t}
is bounded by
B
, then the regret caused by the exploration phase is

\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil}BT_{k}^{e}\leq B\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil}\ell_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}\ln^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|/\delta)\leq BT^{\frac{2}{3}}\ln^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|/\delta)\log T.

The warm-up phase cause at most
B2^{k^{*}}\leq B\ln^{3}|\mathcal{V}|
regret.

We then combine the results in
4.1
and
4.2
and obtain

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil}\left(\sqrt{d_{k}T_{k}\log(d_{k}\ell_{k}/\delta)\ln T_{k}}+BL\ln^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|\log T/\delta)\ell_{k}^{\frac{2}{3}}\log\ell_{k}+\frac{3BT_{k}}{2d_{k}}\right)

Since
d_{k}=\lceil T_{k}^{1/3}\rceil
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]\leq 3T^{\frac{2}{3}}\sqrt{\log(T^{\frac{4}{3}}/\delta)}(1+\log T)+B(L+1)T^{\frac{2}{3}}\log^{\frac{1}{3}}(|\mathcal{V}|\log T/\delta)\log T+B\ln^{3}|\mathcal{V}|.

\square

Lower bound.

Xu and Wang 2022
demonstrated that under the Lipschitz continuity condition,
any policy will result in a regret of at least
\Omega(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
.
This finding establishes the minimax optimality of our algorithm, up to logarithmic terms.
However, there remains a gap of order
|\mathcal{V}|
in terms of exploration, which is an area for future research.
The Lipschitz continuity assumption is crucial as it enables us to control the misspecification error in the regret analysis.

Without the Lipschitz condition,
Xu and Wang 2022
proposed an algorithm with a regret bound of
\mathcal{O}(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
.
In their work, they discretize the linear parameter space and distribution space to form the policy space,
reducing the original problem into an EXP4 agent.
Although they discretize the distribution in a similar way to us,
they still consider finding a good distribution within the given set,
resulting in a policy space of size
\mathcal{O}(2^{d_{k}})
.
In contrast, for setting a suitable price, we only need to evaluate at
d_{k}
points.
This observation effectively reduces the search space from
\mathcal{O}(2^{d_{k}})
to
\mathcal{O}(d_{k})
,
leading to exponential decrease in both regret and time complexity.

However, it is currently unknown whether their bound is tight in the absence of the Lipschitz continuity assumption.
Further investigation is required to explore the tightness of the regret bound without the Lipschitz assumption.

5
Discussion

In this section, we explore several extensions and improvements to the existing framework,
aiming to enhance our understanding of the theoretical results and their applications.

General offline regression oracle.

To simplify the discussion on the estimation process, we consider a natural approach of reducing it to the general offline regression oracle.
This approach involves an offline regression oracle associated with
\mathcal{V}
.
The offline regression oracle is a procedure that generates a predictor based on the input data.

Assumption 5.1

The general offline regression oracle takes
n
samples of the form
(x_{i},y_{i})
, independently and identically drawn according to a fixed distribution such that
\mathbb{E}[By_{i}|x_{i}]=v^{*}(x_{i})
for
v^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
.
It returns a predictor
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{V}
which satisfies that for any
\delta>0

\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\hat{v}(x)-v^{*}(x))^{2}]\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)}{n}.

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

This assumption describes the estimation accuracy in statistical learning tasks.
The quantity
\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)
in
5.1
reflects the properties of the function space
\mathcal{V}
and decreases as
\delta
decreases.
Moreover, the function
\frac{\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)}{n}
serves as an upper bound on the squared
L^{2}
distance between the true value
v^{*}
and the estimated value
\hat{v}
in expectation.
Under realizability assumption (i.e.,
v^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
), this squared distance corresponds to the estimation error of
\hat{v}
.
Characterizing sharp bounds on the estimation error and developing efficient algorithms to achieve such bounds are fundamental tasks in the field of statistical learning.

The choice of function class
\mathcal{V}
is flexible and can encompass various parametric or nonparametric models,
including Lipschitz function classes, reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces,
and neural networks.
To achieve optimal regret in the contextual dynamic pricing problem,
it is crucial to have a computationally-efficient and statistically-optimal offline regression oracle tailored to the chosen function class
\mathcal{V}
.

Furthermore, we can extend the results under a general estimation assumption through a simple corollary.
By replacing the quantity
\ln(|\mathcal{V}|/\delta)
with
\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)
in the algorithmic steps and proofs,
we obtain an extended bound on the expected regret.
For completeness, we present new
algorithm

4
in Appendix.
Specifically, under the assumptions of boundedness, Lipschitz continuity,
and the availability of an offline regression oracle (
2.1
,
2.2
, and
5.1
),
the expected regret satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]=\mathcal{O}(BT^{\frac{2}{3}}\rho_{\mathcal{V}}^{\frac{1}{3}}(\delta)\log^{\frac{3}{2}}T)
with a probability of at least
1-\delta
.

Corollary 5.1

Under
2.1
,
2.2
and
5.1
, the expected regret of
algorithm

4
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]=\mathcal{O}(BT^{\frac{2}{3}}\rho^{\frac{1}{3}}_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)\log^{\frac{3}{2}}T)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

To demonstrate the versatility of the corollary, we can consider specific cases of function spaces and examine their corresponding bounds on

•

Finite function space: From

\mathcal{V}

The tightness of the method is thus established and we have presented this special case in

Linear function space: Linear valuation models have been widely employed in various dynamic pricing works

,
24
]

Classical analysis techniques applied to linear regression yield guarantees of
\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left({d_{0}\log(\delta^{-1})}\right)

The tightness of this upper bound has been demonstrated through empirical minimax theorem

Corollary 5.2

Under
2.1
,
A.1
,

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]=\mathcal{O}(\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)\log T).

with probability at least

Proof.

Given the estimator
\hat{v}_{k}
using data collected in the previous episode,

p_{t}
is only determined by

.
Therefore, we know that the data collected in one episode is i.i.d. due to the i.i.d. nature of covariates.
Thanks to

to

rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})-rev_{t}(p_{t})\leq C(p_{t}^{*}-p_{t})^{2},

where
C=\max_{p}|rev_{t}^{\prime\prime}(p)|

Using first order conditions, for the optimal posted price
p_{t}^{*}

p_{t}^{*}=\frac{1-F(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))}{F^{\prime}(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))}.

Since
1-F
is log-concave,
the hazard function

is increasing,
which implies

Note that

\phi(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))=p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t})-\frac{1-F(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))}{F^{\prime}(p_{t}^{*}-v^{*}(x_{t}))}=-v^{*}(x_{t})

Define

and we know that

g(v^{*}(x_{t}))=v^{*}(x_{t})+\phi^{-1}(-v^{*}(x_{t}))=p_{t}^{*}.

Similarly, since
p_{t}

From lemma 15 in

Due to
5.1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})-rev_{t}(p_{t})]\leq\frac{C\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)}{2^{k-1}}.

Summing over all rounds in all episodes yields

\mathbb{E}[Reg(T)]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil\log T\rceil}\sum_{t=2^{k-1}}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[rev_{t}(p_{t}^{*})-rev_{t}(p_{t})]\leq 3C\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)\log T.

\square

By removing the exploration phase, we can straightforwardly modify

Classification oracle.

To estimate the unknown value function
v^{*}
, we introduce a method that utilizes all the data
(x_{t},p_{t},y_{t})
from the previous
(k-1)
-th episode.
This allows us to obtain an estimate
\hat{v}_{k}
that can be used for pricing in the
k
-th episode.
Since
y_{t}
is binary and determined by
x_{t}
and
p_{t}
through
\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=1)=1-F(p_{t}-v^{*}(x_{t}))
, we can establish the following relationships:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=1)>\frac{1}{2},\text{ if }F^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})+v^{*}(x_{t})-p_{t}>0;\\
\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=1)=\frac{1}{2},\text{ if }F^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})+v^{*}(x_{t})-p_{t}=0;\\
\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=1)<\frac{1}{2},\text{ if }F^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})+v^{*}(x_{t})-p_{t}<0.\\
\end{array}\right.

This allows us to transform the estimation procedure into a classification problem.
By applying a classification oracle, we can obtain a predictor
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{V}
that approximates
v^{*}
.
The classification oracle provides a decision boundary based on the unknown function
v^{*}
.

Assumption 5.3

Given
n
samples of the form
(x_{i},p_{i},y_{i})
,
the classification oracle returns a predictor
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{V}
.
For any
\delta>0

1-\delta

\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\hat{v}(x)-v^{*}(x))^{2}]\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathcal{V}}(\delta)}{n^{\alpha}}

for some positive

In the work of
Luo et al. 2024

They demonstrate that in the linear valuation model,

\alpha\geq 1

However, for a general function space, it remains unknown whether we can design a suitable classification oracle that meets the required conditions.
Typically, classification oracles are criticized due to the lack of theoretical guarantees for estimation accuracy.

Under Lipschitz condition only, the upper bound for the contextual dynamic problem remains

6
Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose a novel algorithm for contextual dynamic pricing that achieves improved regret bounds while minimizing assumptions.
Our algorithm combines an explore-then-UCB approach with layered data partitioning,
achieving a regret upper bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\rho_{\mathcal{V}}^{\frac{1}{3}}(\delta)T^{\frac{2}{3}})
.
It extends beyond linear valuation models, simplifies estimation through offline regression oracles,
and demonstrates improved performance compared to existing approaches.
Our algorithm provides a comprehensive solution to the contextual dynamic pricing problem with minimax optimality and practical applicability.

Future research directions for the contextual dynamic pricing problem can focus on the following aspects. Firstly, investigating the regret lower bound without assuming Lipschitz continuity of the noise distribution
F
would provide a deeper understanding of the problem’s difficulty. Although
Xu and Wang 2022
demonstrated a regret upper bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
, it is conjectured that the lower bound remains
\Omega(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
when the Lipschitz continuity assumption is relaxed.

Secondly, exploring the leading term in the regret bound in terms of function spaces is an important direction for future research. It is still unknown whether our achieved regret bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}})
is optimal with respect to the covariate dimension
d_{0}
. Further investigation in this area would provide insights into the relationship between regret and function space complexity.

Lastly, improving regret upper bounds for smoother distributions represents a promising future direction. By incorporating additional second-order smoothness conditions, it is possible to enhance the regret upper bound to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{3}{5}})
. Exploring this avenue further would contribute to a deeper understanding of the impact of distribution smoothness on regret performance.

Lemma B.1

Assuming
|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\geq 2
, we have

\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}p_{t}r_{t,s}^{j_{t}}\leq B\sqrt{2d_{k}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}.

Proof.

We have

\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}p_{t}r_{t,s}^{j_{t}}=\sqrt{\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}\frac{p_{t}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t}}p_{\tau}^{2}}}}.

It suffices to bound the term
\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}\frac{p_{t}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t}}p_{\tau}^{2}}}}
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}\frac{p_{t}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1,s}^{j_{t}}p_{\tau}^{2}}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{T_{k}}^{j}}\frac{p_{t}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{t-1}^{j}}p_{\tau}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\sqrt{2\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{T_{k}}^{j}}p_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2d_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{T_{k}}^{j}}p_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B\sqrt{2d_{k}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\sum_{t\in\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}}p_{t}r_{t,s}^{j_{t}}\leq B\sqrt{2d_{k}|\Psi_{T_{k}+1}^{s}|\log(T_{k}d_{k}/\delta)}.

\square
