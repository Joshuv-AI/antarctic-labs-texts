On the Use of L L -functionals in Regression Models Thanks: Department of Mathematics, Stockholm University, 106 91 Stockholm, Sweden
Ola Hössjer

Abstract
In this paper we survey and unify a large class or L L -functionals of the conditional distribution of the response variable in regression models. This includes robust measures of location, scale, skewness, and heavytailedness of the response, conditionally on covariates. We generalize the concepts of L L -moments (Sittinen, 1969), L L -skewness, and L L -kurtosis (Hosking, 1990) and introduce order numbers for a large class of L L -functionals through orthogonal series expansions of quantile functions. In particular, we motivate why location, scale, skewness, and heavytailedness have order numbers 1, 2, (3,2), and (4,2) respectively and describe how a family of L L -functionals, with different order numbers, is constructed from Legendre, Hermite, Laguerre or other types of polynomials. Our framework is applied to models where the relationship between quantiles of the response and the covariates follow a transformed linear model, with a link function that determines the appropriate class of L L -functionals. In this setting, the distribution of the response is treated parametrically or nonparametrically, and the response variable is either censored/truncated or not. We also provide a unified asymptotic theory of estimates of L L -functionals, and illustrate our approach by analyzing the arrival time distribution of migrating birds. In this context a novel version of the coefficient of determination is introduced, which makes use of the abovementioned orthogonal series expansion.



Keywords—
Bird phenology, Coefficient of determination, L-functionals, L-statistics, Order numbers, Orthogonal series expansion, Quantile function,

Quantile regression

1
Introduction

Linear combinations of order statistics represent a wide class of estimators of location and scale parameters for samples of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations. These estimators are well known to combine high efficiency and robustness (Bickel and Lehmann, 1975). Their asymptotic properties are conveniently represented in terms of
L
-functionals of the empirical distribution formed by the sample, as summarized in Chapter 8 of Serfling (1980).

Many authors have proposed extensions of
L
-statistics for regression models. In a pioneering article Koenker and Bassett (1978) introduced regression quantiles. These nonparametric estimators make few assumptions on the conditional distribution of the response variable. They naturally extend order statistics to linear regression models, and have been applied to many fields of science (Koenker and Hallock, 2001). Koenker and Portnoy (1989) introduced linear combinations of regression quantiles and showed that much of the computational and asymptotic theory of
L
-functionals of order statistics for i.i.d. models extend to the regression framework. The monograph of Koenker (2005) summarizes these and a number of other aspects of regression quantiles, including nonlinear regression. Other contributions include parametric versions of regression quantiles (Gilchrist, 2000, 2007, Frumento and Bottai, 2016), and the use of
L
-functionals in survival analysis (Frumento and Bottai, 2017).

In this paper we review, unify, and extend the use of
L
-functionals for regression models. We consider four classes of
L
-functionals or ratios of
L
-functionals that represent measures of location, scale, skewness, and heavytailedness (kurtosis). Then we introduce order numbers for a large class of
L
-functionals through orthogonal series expansions of quantile functions (Takemura, 1983, Okagbue et al., 2019), and motivate why location, scale, skewness, and heavytailedness functionals have order numbers 1, 2, (3,2), and (4,2) respectively. In this context we describe how a given reference distribution gives rise to a whole collection of
L
-functionals. If Legendre polynomials and a uniform reference distribution is used, the resulting class of
L
-functionals of order
1,2,\ldots
corresponds to
L
-moments (Sillito, 1969), whereas the ratios of
L
-functionals of order (2,1), (3,2), and (4,2) agree with the
L
-coefficient of variation, the
L
-skewness, and the
L
-kurtosis (Hosking, 1990, 1992, 2006) up to normalizing constants. This Legendre class of
L
-functionals is a natural choice for distributions with bounded support, but Hermite polynomials (with a Gaussian reference distribution) or Laguerre polynomials (with an exponential reference distribution) might be preferable for data whose support is on the real line and on the positive real line respectively, in particular if the distribution of the response is close to the reference distribution.

We will apply the framework of
L
-functionals to a wide range of transformed linear regression models, with linear models a special case. We argue that the transformation (or link function) will determine the appropriate type of
L
-functionals to use. We also demonstrate how parametric and nonparametric methods of estimating conditional
L
-functionals can be put into a unified framework, for response variables with or without censoring and truncation.

In more detail, the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce
L
-functionals for models without covariates, with particular emphasis on functionals that quantify location, scale, skewness or heavytailedness, and how their order numbers can be assessed. Then in Section
3
we generalize the framework of Section
2
to linear and transformed linear regression models, for models with our without censoring or truncation. Section
4
contains numerical examples, in Section
5
we analyze a data set with migration times of birds and introduce a novel version of the coefficient of determination, whereas Section
6
provides a summarizing discussion.

2
L
-functionals without covariates

2.1
Definition of
L
-functionals

Let
F_{Y}(y)=F(y)=P(Y\leq y)
be the unknown distribution function of a random variable
Y
. Suppose we want to infer a certain functional
\theta=T(F)
of
F
, using a data set
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
of independent and identically distributed random variables with
P(Y_{i}\leq y)=F(y)
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. In this paper we will focus on
L
-functionals, i.e. linear combinations

\theta=T(F)=\int_{0}^{1}Q(p)dG(p)

(1)

of quantiles

Q(p)=Q_{Y}(p)=F^{-1}(p)=\inf\{y;\,F(y)\geq p\}

(2)

of
F
, using some weight function
G=G^{+}-G^{-}
that corresponds to a signed measure. When
\theta
is a measure of location of
F
, the weight function is often a positive measure (
G=G^{+}
), but this is not the case for measures of scale, skewness and kurtosis (cf. Section 2.3). Following Serfling (1980) we consider measures

dG(p)=g(p)dp+\sum_{m=1}^{M}g_{m}\delta_{\pi_{m}}(p)

(3)

on
[0,1]
that split into one absolutely continuous part
g(p)dp
and another finite sum of point masses
g_{m}\delta_{\pi_{m}}
at
\pi_{m}
with weights
g_{m}
for
m=1,\ldots,M
, with
0\leq\pi_{1}<\ldots\pi_{M}\leq 1
. This allows us to work with all the common types of distributions, i.e. continuous, discrete and mixtures, within the same framework. The
L
-functional is robust if extreme quantiles of
F
are excluded, i.e. if the total variation measure
|G|=G^{+}+G^{-}
satisfies
|G|([0,\pi)\cup(1-\pi,1])=0
for some sufficiently small
0<\pi\leq 0.5
. Then the breakdown point (Rousseeuw and Leroy, 1987) of
T
is at least
\pi
.

A wide class of estimators

\hat{\theta}=T(\hat{F})=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{Q}(p)dG(p),

(4)

of
\theta
are obtained by plugging
\hat{F}
, an estimate of
F
, into (
1
), with
\hat{Q}=\hat{F}^{-1}
the corresponding estimate of
Q
. This estimate of
F
could be nonparametric, i.e. the empirical distribution function

\hat{F}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}\leq y)

(5)

formed by the sample. Then

\hat{\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}Y_{(i)}

(6)

is an
L
-statistic, i.e. a linear combination of the order statistics
Y_{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq Y_{(n)}
, with
w_{i}=\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}dG(p)
the weight assigned to the
i
th order statistic. It is also possible to insert a parametric estimator

\hat{F}(y)=F(y;\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}})

(7)

of
F
into (
4
). In this case
F(y)=F(y;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
is fully determined by a finite-dimensional parameter
\psi=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{r})
, of which
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}=(\hat{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\psi}_{r})
is an estimate. If the density function
F^{\prime}(y;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=f(y;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
is tractable, this is typically the maximum likelihood estimator of
\psi
. For some distributional families, such as the Generalized Lambda Distributions (Karian and Dudewicz, 2000) or mixtures of quantile functions (Karvanen, 2006, Karvanen and Nuutinen, 2008), the quantile function
Q(p;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=F^{-1}(p;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
has a more explicit form. It might then be more tractable to estimate
\psi
by fitting some of the order statistics
Y_{(i)}
to
Q
(Gilchrist, 2007) or some empirical
L
-functionals to the corresponding population-based
L
-functionals (Karvanen, 2006).

2.2
Asymptotics

In order to study the large sample behavior of
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, we introduce
S
, the space of real-valued and integrable functions on
(0,1)
, equipped with a seminorm

\|Q\|_{S}=\int_{0}^{1}|Q(p)||g(p)|dp+\sum_{m=1}^{M}|g_{m}||Q(\pi_{m})|.

(8)

where
g(p)
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{M}
refer to the absolutely continuous part and the point masses of the weight measure
G
in (
3
) respectively. We may also regard (
8
) as a norm of the restriction of
Q
to
\mbox{supp}(G)
. If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are two distributions with quantile functions
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, it follows that

\begin{split}|T(F_{2})-T(F_{1})|&\leq\int_{0}^{1}|Q_{2}(p)-Q_{1}(p)|d|G|(p)\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}|Q_{2}(p)-Q_{1}(p)||g(p)|dp+\sum_{m=1}^{M}|g_{m}||Q_{2}(\pi_{m})-Q_{1}(\pi_{m})|\\
&=\|Q_{2}-Q_{1}\|_{S}.\end{split}

(9)

This implies in particular that
T(F)
is a continuous functional with respect to the distance measure
d(F_{2},F_{1})=\|Q_{2}-Q_{1}\|_{S}
introduced by
\|\cdot\|_{S}
. In particular, when
M=0
and
g(p)\equiv 1
, this distance measure equals the Wasserstein metric of order 1 (Olkin and Pukelsheim, 1982).

The asymptotic properties of
\hat{\theta}_{n}
are determined by the weight function
G
and the large sample behavior of the rescaled quantile process

Z_{n}(p)=\sqrt{n}[\hat{Q}_{n}(p)-Q(p)],\quad 0<p<1,

(10)

which is a random element of
S
. We will assume weak convergence (Billingsley, 1999)

Z_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,

(11)

with respect to the topology introduced by the norm in
S
, i.e.
E[h(Z_{n})]\to E[h(Z)]
for all bounded and continuous functions
h:S\to{\mathbb{R}}
. It is further assumed that the limit in (
11
) is a Gaussian process with mean function
E[Z(p)]=0
and covariance function
\mbox{Cov}[Z(p),Z(s)]=R(p,s)
for
p,s\in\mbox{supp}(G)
. Notice in particular that for discrete weight measures (
g\equiv 0
), only the last term on the right hand side of (
8
) is present. Then (
11
) corresponds to weak convergence of finite-dimensional distributions of
Z_{n}
towards
Z
at
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m}
. This follows from the fact that
Z_{n}
and
Z
, restricted to
\mbox{supp}(G)=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{M}\}
, represent weak convergence of random vectors of dimension
M
when
g\equiv 0
. On the other hand, (
11
) represents functional weak convergence on an infinite index set
p\in\mbox{supp}(G)
when
g\neq 0
, due to the first term of (
8
).

It is also possible to define

\|Q\|_{S}=\|Q\|_{\infty}=\sup_{0<p<1}|Q(p)|

(12)

as the supremum of
Q
on
(0,1)
. It is easy to see that
T(F)
is continuous with this choice of norm, since
|T(F_{2})-T(F_{1})|\leq|G|(0,1)\|Q_{2}-Q_{1}\|_{S}
. Note that
\|Q\|_{S}
in (
12
) does not involve the weight measure
G
. This is advantageous when simultaneous weak convergence of several
L
-functionals, based on different weight functions, is of interest. On the other hand, the advantage of (8) is that this (semi)norm exists for a larger class of functions
Q
. It also gives rise to a weaker topology on
S
, so that (11) requires less. Indeed, when (8) is used, weak convergence of
Z_{n}
need only be established on the index set
\mbox{supp}(G)
.

Since (
9
) implies that
\tilde{T}:S\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by
\tilde{T}(Q)=\int_{0}^{1}Q(p)dG(p)
, is a continuous functional, it follows from the Continuous Mapping Theorem that

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)=\tilde{T}(Z_{n})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tilde{T}(Z)\sim N(0,\Sigma)

(13)

as
n\to\infty
, where the asymptotic covariance matrix of the limiting normal distribution satisfies

\Sigma=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}R(p,s)dG(p)dG(s).

(14)

The expression for the covariance function
R
depends on which estimator of
F
that is used in (
4
). The nonparametric estimate (
5
) corresponds to

R(p,s)=\frac{\min(p,s)-ps}{f(Q(p))f(Q(s))},

(15)

where
f(y)=F^{\prime}(y)
is the density function of
Y
. When (
15
) is inserted into (
14
), we get a well known expression for the asymptotic variance of
L
-statistics, cf. Mosteller (1946), Bennett (1952), Jung (1955), Chernoff et al. (1967), and Moore (1968). In the parametric case we have that

R(p,s)=\frac{dQ(p;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})}{d\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}\left(\frac{dQ(s;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})}{d\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}\right)^{T},

(16)

provided
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{n}
is asymptotically normal with covariance matrix
V
, i.e.

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$V$})

(17)

as
n\to\infty
. In particular,
V
equals the inverse of the Fisher information matrix of
\psi
, when
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{n}
is the ML-estimator of
\psi
.

2.3
Examples of
L
-functionals

The choice of weight function
G
in (
4
) will determine the type of
L
-functional. In the present paper we will mainly focus on four types of statistical functionals, presented in Oja (1981), namely for location, scale, skewness and kurtosis:

Example 1

(Measures of location)

A measure of location (
T=T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}
) is equivariant with respect to linear transformations of data, i.e.

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{aY+b})=aT_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{Y})+b

(18)

for any real valued
a
and
b
. Since
Q_{aY+b}=aQ_{Y}+b
, it follows that (
18
) holds for all
L
-functionals with a weight function satisfying

\int_{0}^{1}dG(p)=1.

(19)

For location functionals we will also require that
G
is a positive measure, so that

G^{-}=0.

(20)

The two regularity conditions (
19
)-(
20
) imply that
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}
preserves stochastic ordering of distribution functions. By this we mean that
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{1})\leq T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{2})
whenever
F_{2}
is stochastically larger than
F_{1}
, that is, when the quantile functions of the two distributions satisfy
Q_{1}(p)\leq Q_{2}(p)
for all
0<p<1
.

Examples of location functionals with a discrete weight function (
g\equiv 0
) include quantiles

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F)=Q(\pi)

(21)

for some fixed
0<\pi<1
and for distributions
F
with bounded support the midrange

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F)=\frac{1}{2}\left[Q(0)+Q(1)\right].

(22)

Examples of location functionals with an absolutely continuous weight density include smoothed quantiles

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{b}K\left(\frac{p-\pi}{b}\right)Q(p)dp,

(23)

where
K
is the smoothing probability density and
0<b<\min(\pi,1-\pi)
the bandwidth (Parzen, 1979, Sheather and Marron, 1990), and compound expectations (CEs)

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F)=\frac{1}{\pi_{1}-\pi_{0}}\int_{\pi_{0}}^{\pi_{1}}Q^{-1}(p)dp

(24)

that average quantiles of
F
between
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{1}
for some appropriately chosen
0\leq\pi_{0}<\pi_{1}\leq 1
. Notice in particular that (
24
) is a special case of (
23
) that corresponds to a rectangular kernel
K(x)=1(|x|\leq 1)/2
,
\pi=(\pi_{1}+\pi_{2})/2
and
b=(\pi_{1}-\pi_{0})/2
.

A centralized measure of location is one whose weight function is symmetric around
p=0.5
and satisfies (
19
). If
F_{Y}
has a symmetric distribution around
\mu
, a centralized measure of location will equal the center of symmetry (
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{Y})=\mu
). These types of weight functions include the median (
\pi=0.5
in (
21
)), the midrange (
22
), and the trimmed mean (
\pi_{0}+\pi_{1}=1
in (
24
), see for instance Tukey and McLaughlin (1963), Bickel (1965), and Stigler (1977)). When
\pi_{0}=0
and
\pi_{1}=1
, the trimmed mean simplifies to the expected value

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(F_{Y})=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{Y}(p)dp=E(Y).

(25)

A location-scale family

F(y)=F_{0}\left(\frac{y-\mu}{\sigma}\right)

(26)

corresponds to a parametric family (
7
) with
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}=(\mu,\sigma)
and
F_{0}
known. It is then of interest to find the
optimal weight function
G
of location for the
L
-statistic (
6
). This weight function minimizes the asymptotic variance (
14
)-(
15
) among all
L
-functionals
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}
that estimate
\mu
in (
26
). It is well known (Chernoff et al., 1967, Chapter 8 of Serfling, 1980) that if the density
f_{0}=F_{0}^{\prime}
is twice differentiable, the asymptotically optimal
L
-estimator of
\mu
(when
\sigma
is known or when
f_{0}
is symmetric so that
\sigma
is Fisher orthogonal to
\mu
) corresponds to an absolutely continuous weight measure with density

g(p)=-\frac{(\log f_{0})^{\prime\prime}(F_{0}^{-1}(p))}{I(f_{0})},

(27)

where
I(f_{0})=\int f_{0}^{\prime}(y)^{2}/f_{0}(y)dy
is the Fisher information. In particular, the expected value (
25
) with weight function
g(p)\equiv 1
is optimal when
F\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, whereas the midrange (
22
) is optimal for a uniform distribution, as can be seen by approximating this distribution by a smooth
F_{0}
.

Example 2

(Measures of scale)

A measure of scale (
T=T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}
) is non-negative and satisfies

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{aY+b})=|a|T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{Y})

(28)

for all real-valued
a
and
b
. In the context of
L
-functionals, (
28
) is satisfied for weight functions that take on positive as well as negative values, in such a way that the conditions

dG\mbox{ is skew-symmetric around }p=0.5,

(29)

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G^{+}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }(0.5,1],\\
G^{-}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }[0,0.5)\end{array}

(30)

are fulfilled. In order to see that (
29
)-(
30
) correspond to a measure of scale, it is instructive to insert these conditions into (
1
). This makes it possible to rewrite the scale functional as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)&=&\int_{0}^{1}(Q(p)-Q(0.5))dG(p)\\
&=&\int_{0}^{1}|Q(p)-Q(0.5)|d|G|(p)\\
&=&\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dG^{+}(p).\end{array}

(31)

Notice in particular that (
31
) has an intuitive interpretation as a linear combination of interquantile ranges
Q(p)-Q(1-p)
.
Bickel and Lehmann (1976) and Oja (1981) defined a spread-ordering among distributions, where
F_{2}
is said to be at least as spread out as
F_{1}
if the difference
Q_{2}(p)-Q_{1}(p)
between the quantile functions of
F_{2}
and
F_{1}
is non-decreasing. It follows from (
31
) that scale functionals preserve spread ordering, i.e.
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{1})\leq T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{2})
. Since the unit of
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}
is somewhat arbitrary, some reference distribution
F_{0}
is typically chosen to have scale 1, i.e.

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{0}(p)dG(p)=1.

(32)

If this reference distribution is a standard normal
N(0,1)
, it follows that
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{Y})=\sigma
for
Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
.
The simplest scale functional that satisfies (
29
)-(
30
) and (
32
), is the one for which
G^{+}
is a point measure at
\pi
for some
0.5<\pi\leq 1
. Then

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)=\frac{Q(\pi)-Q(1-\pi)}{Q_{0}(\pi)-Q_{0}(1-\pi)}

(33)

equals the standardized interquartile range of
F
when
\pi=0.75
and the standardized range when
\pi=1
and
F
has bounded support. The standardized Gini’s mean difference

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)=KE|Y_{1}-Y_{2}|=\frac{\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right](p-0.5)dp}{\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q_{0}(p)-Q_{0}(1-p)\right](p-0.5)dp}

(34)

corresponds to having
dG^{+}(p)=K(p-0.5)1(0.5\leq p\leq 1)dp
in (
31
), with
K
chosen so that (
32
) holds. A third measure of scale

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)=\frac{\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dp}{\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q_{0}(p)-Q_{0}(1-p)\right]dp}

(35)

has a constant density
dG^{+}(p)=K1(0.5\leq p\leq 1)dp
on
(0.5,1)
, with
K>0
a constant chosen so that (
32
) holds.

It is sometimes of interest to choose the weight function
G
so that the asymptotic variance
\Sigma
in (
14
) is minimized. It can be shown (Chernoff et al., 1967, Chapter 8 of Serfling, 1980) that for the location-scale family (
26
), an
L
-functional with absolutely continuous weight function

g(p)=K\left[(\log f_{0})^{\prime}\left(F_{0}^{-1}(p)\right)+F_{0}^{-1}(p)(\log f_{0})^{\prime\prime}\left(F_{0}^{-1}(p)\right)\right]

(36)

corresponds to an asymptotically optimal estimator of the scale parameter
\sigma
whenever the location parameter
\mu
is known or orthogonal to
\sigma
, and the density function
f_{0}
is twice differentiable, with
K
chosen so that (
32
) holds for some appropriately chosen reference distribution. In particular,
K=1
yields a consistent estimator of the scale parameter
\sigma
in (
26
). For instance,
g(p)=F_{0}^{-1}(p)
is optimal for the normal distribution (
F_{0}\sim N(0,1)
). A robustified, skew-symmetric and trimmed version of this weight function has been studied by Welsh and Morrison (1990). More generally, it follows from (
36
) that the optimal
g
is skew-symmetric around 0.5 whenever
f_{0}
is symmetric around 0, but not when symmetry of
f_{0}
fails. The advantage of having a skew-symmetry requirement (
29
) on the weight function is the intuitive interpretation (
31
) of
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)
in terms of a linear combination of interquantile ranges. Koenker and Zhou (1994) define scale functionals more generally by dropping the skew-symmetry condition and only requiring that the weight function satisfies
G((0,p])<0
for all
0<p<1
and
G([0,1])=0
. An example of such a scale functional is presented in Section
2.4
.

Example 3

(Measures of skewness)

The traditional measure

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize skew}}(F_{Y})=\frac{E\left[(Y-E(Y))^{3}\right]}{\left\{E\left[(Y-E(Y))^{2}\right]\right\}^{3/2}}

(37)

of skewness compares the left and right tails of
F_{Y}
. It is not robust, since the third moment of
Y
must be finite. Here we will analyze versions of skewness that are more robust than (
37
), and defined as the ratio

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize skew}}(F)=\frac{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uskew}}(F)}{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)}

(38)

of two
L
-functionals. The numerator of (
38
) corresponds to an unstandardized measure of skewness, which transforms as

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uskew}}(F_{aY+b})=aT_{\mbox{\scriptsize uskew}}(F_{Y})

(39)

under linear mappings, for all real-valued
a
and
b
. The denominator of (
38
) is another
L
-functional that measures scale. It follows from (
28
) and (
39
) that the standardized skewness satisfies

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize skew}}(F_{aY+b})=\mbox{sgn}(a)T_{\mbox{\scriptsize skew}}(F_{Y})

(40)

for all non-negative
a
, with
\mbox{sgn}(a)=a/|a|
. We will assume here that

is chosen so that

(41)

holds for some reference distribution
F_{0}
whose right tail is heavier than the left tail (e.g. an exponential distribution).

A class of weight functions whose unstandardized skewness functional transform linearly, as in (

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\int_{0}^{1}dG(p)&=&0,\\
dG\mbox{ symmetric around }p&=&0.5,\end{array}

(42)

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{supp}(G^{-})&\subseteq&[\pi,1-\pi],\\
\mbox{supp}(G^{+})&\subseteq&[0,\pi]\cup[1-\pi,1],\end{array}

(43)

for some
0\leq\pi\leq 0.5
. Indeed, inserting (

)-(
43

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uskew}}(F)&=&\int_{0.5}^{1}\left[Q(p)+Q(1-p)-2Q(0.5)\right]dG(p)\\
&=&\int_{1-\pi}^{1}\left[Q(p)+Q(1-p)-2Q(0.5)\right]dG^{+}(p)\\
&-&\int_{0.5}^{1-\pi}\left[Q(p)+Q(1-p)-2Q(0.5)\right]dG^{-}(p).\end{array}

(44)

In particular, if
F_{Y}
is symmetric around its center of symmetry
\mu

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uskew}}(F)=0
. Since

measures skewness for each quantile
0.5<p<1

44

(45)

If the positive part of the weight function is chosen as

for some

and

, and
if the interquantile range in (

of skewness. It was introduced by Galton (1883) and Bowley (1920) for

and

, and for arbitrary

by Hinkley (1975). Here we will rather define

so that (
41

F_{0}
. An alternative to (

g^{+}
for quantiles above 0.5, in the denominator of (

K
in order for (
41
) to hold. Notice that the skewness measure (
47
) puts higher weights on the tails of the distribution, compared to (
46
). See also Kim and White (1994) for an overview of different robust measures of skewness.

is a convex function. Groeneveld and Meeden verified that (

Example 4

(Measures of heavytailedness)

There is no universal agreement whether the (excess) kurtosis

T(F_{Y})=\frac{E[(Y-E(Y))^{4}]}{\left\{E\left[(Y-E(Y))^{2}\right]\right\}^{2}}-3

(48)

quantifies peakness versus tails of
F_{Y}
or modality versus bimodality. Following Chissom (1970) and Oja (1981), we will regard kurtosis as a measure of heavytailedness. In order to find more robust measures of heavytailedness, we will consider functionals

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F)=\frac{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F)}{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)}

(49)

that are defined as the ratio of two
L
-functionals. The functional
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}
in the numerator of (
49
) corresponds to an unstandardized measure of heavytailedness, and it transforms as

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F_{aY+b})=|a|T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F_{Y})

(50)

under linear mappings, for all real-valued
a
and
b
, whereas the functional in the denominator of (
49
) corresponds to a measure of scale. It follows from (
28
) and (
50
) that
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}
is invariant with respect to linear transformations, i.e.

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F_{aY+b})=T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F_{Y}).

(51)

Notice that (
50
) is identical to the corresponding relation (
28
) for scale functionals. But whereas
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}
is always non-negative, we want
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}
to be positive for heavy-tailed distributions and negative for light-tailed distributions. In order to accomplish this we choose the weight function in (
1
), for
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F)
, as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}dG\mbox{ skew-symmetric around }p=0.5,\end{array}

(52)

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G^{+}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }[\pi,0.5)\cup(1-\pi,1],\\
G^{-}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }[0,\pi)\cup(0.5,1-\pi]\end{array}

(53)

for some
0<\pi<0.5
. In addition, we require that the unstandardized and standardized measures of heavytailedness satisfy

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F_{0})&=&\int_{0}^{1}Q_{0}(p)dG(p)=0,\\
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F_{1})&=&\int_{0}^{1}Q_{1}(p)dG(p)/T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F_{1})=1\end{array}

(54)

for two distributions
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
with quantile functions
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
. When there is no restriction on the range of
Y
,
F_{0}
is typically a normal distribution, whereas
F_{1}
is a symmetric and moderately light tailed distribution, such as the Laplace distribution.

In order to motivate that (
52

1
) and notice that

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavy}}(F)=\int_{1-\pi}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dG^{+}(p)-\int_{0.5}^{1-\pi}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dG^{-}(p)

(55)

equals the difference between the weighted interquantile differences
Q(p)-Q(1-p)
in the tails and in the central part of the distribution, respectively.
Several robust measures of kurtosis fit into our framework. As a first example, Moors (1988) introduced

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F)=K\left[\frac{Q(0.875)-Q(0.625)+Q(0.375)-Q(0.125)}{Q(0.75)-Q(0.25)}-1.23\right],

(56)

with
K=1
, where the unstandardized kurtosis in the numerator has a weight function
G
such that the restrictions of
G^{+}
and
G^{-}
to
(0.5,1)
have a one point distribution at
0.875
and a two point distribution at
0.625
and
0.75
respectively. The scale measure in the denominator of (
56
), on the other hand, corresponds to an unstandardized interquartile range. Finally, the constant 1.23 is chosen so that the upper part of (
54
) holds when
F_{0}\sim N(0,1)
. Here we will additionally choose
K>0
in (
56
) so that the lower part of (
54
) is satisfied for some appropriately chosen reference distribution
F_{1}
. Second, the tail ratio of Gilchrist (2000) can be normalized as

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F)=K_{1}\left[\frac{Q(0.9)-Q(0.1)}{Q(0.75)-Q(0.25)}-K_{0}\right],

(57)

where
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
are chosen so that (
54
) holds. A third class of kurtosis measures

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavy}}(F)=K_{1}\left[\frac{\int_{1-\pi_{0}}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dp}{\int_{1-\pi_{1}}^{1}\left[Q(p)-Q(1-p)\right]dp}-K_{0}\right]

(58)

was introduced by Hogg (1972,1974) for some conveniently selected
0<\pi_{0}<\pi_{1}\leq 0.5
, and with
K_{0}
chosen so that the upper part of (
54
) holds when
F_{0}\sim N(0,1)
.
Hogg conducted simulations for which
\pi_{0}=0.05
,
\pi_{1}=0.5
, and

gave satisfactory results. Whereas Hogg used
K_{1}=1
, we will rather choose
K_{1}
so that the lower part of (
54
) is satisfied for some appropriate reference distribution
F_{1}
. See also Kim and White (1994) for an overview of different robust measures of kurtosis.

A partial kurtosis-ordering of symmetric distributions (van Zwet, 1964, Oja, 1981) states that
F_{2}
has more kurtosis than
F_{1}
if
x\to F_{2}^{-1}(F_{1}(x))
is a concave (convex) to the left (right) of the point of symmetry. It can be shown that both (
56
) and (
58
) preserve this kurtosis-ordering among symmetric distributions.

Sometimes it is only one tail of
F
that is of interest. It is possible then to split the weight function of an unstandardized heavytailedness functional as

dG(p)=\mathbbm{1}(p<0.5)dG(p)+\mathbbm{1}(p>0.5)dG(p)=:dG_{\mbox{\scriptsize left}}(p)+dG_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}(p),

where only the low quantiles are included in
G_{\mbox{\scriptsize left}}
in order to study the left tail of
F
, whereas only the high quantiles are used in
G_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}
to study the right tail of
F
. It follows from (
52
) that the weight measure for the right tail satisfies

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}^{+}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }(1-\pi,1],\\
G_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}^{-}&=&G\mbox{ restricted to }(0.5,1-\pi].\end{array}

(59)

The corresponding functional

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavyr}}(F)=\int_{1-\pi}^{1}Q(p)G^{+}(p)-\int_{0.5}^{1-\pi}Q(p)dG^{-}(p)

(60)

can be viewed as a restriction of (
55

(0.5,1)
of quantiles, and it is standardized as

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavyr}}(F)=\frac{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize uheavyr}}(F)}{T_{\mbox{\scriptsize scale}}(F)}.

Suppose, for instance, that

has a heavy tail to the right, in the sense that

P(e^{Y}\geq y)=y^{-\alpha}L(y)

for some tail parameter
\alpha>0
, and with a function

as

. Then Hill’s estimator of

(Hill 1975, Haeusler and Teugels, 1985) corresponds to an estimator

in (

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}^{+}(p)&=&\mathbbm{1}(1-\pi<p<1)/(1-\pi),\\
dG_{\mbox{\scriptsize right}}^{-}(p)&=&\delta_{1-\pi}(p).\end{array}

(61)

In order for

to be a consistent estimator of

is constant for large enough

2.4
The order of
L
-functionals

In this section we introduce order numbers for a certain subclass of
L
-functionals. Since each
L
-functional is determined by its weight measure
G
, this amounts to introducing order numbers for a subclass of weight measures. We will state two mandatory conditions on
G
and a third optional symmetry condition, in order for it to be of order
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
. First, there has to exist a disjoint decomposition of
[0,1]
into
m
intervals
I_{1}<I_{2}<\ldots<I_{m}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G^{-}(I_{k})=0\mbox{ and }G^{+}(I_{k})>0,&\mbox{ if $m-k$ is even},\\
G^{-}(I_{k})>0\mbox{ and }G^{+}(I_{k})=0,&\mbox{ if $m-k$ is odd}.\end{array}

(62)

Second,

\int_{0}^{1}dG(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}1,&\mbox{if $G$ has order $m=1$},\\
0,&\mbox{if $G$ has order $m>1$}.\end{array}\right.

(63)

Third,

dG\mbox{ is symmetric (skew-symmetric) around $p=0.5$ if $m$ is odd (even).}

(64)

When all three conditions (
62
)-(
64
) hold, we refer to
G
as a symmetric (skew-symmetric) weight measure of order
m
.

It may be verified from Section
2.3
that location functionals have order 1, scale functionals order 2, unstandardized skewness functionals order 3, and unstandardized heavytailedness functionals order 4. Write

T_{m}(F)=\int_{0}^{1}Q(p)dG_{m}(p)

(65)

for an
L
-functional whose weight function
G=G_{m}
is of order
m
, and

T_{ml}(F)=\frac{T_{m}(F)}{T_{l}(F)}

(66)

for a functional that is the ratio of two
L
-functionals of order
m
and
l
respectively. We will refer to
(m,l)
as the order number of
T_{ml}
. Consequently, skewness functionals have order
(3,2)
and heavytailedness functionals order
(4,2)
.

It is possible to obtain collections
\{T_{m}(F)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
L
-functionals from orthogonal series expansions of the quantile function
Q=F^{-1}
.
Each such collection makes use of a reference distribution
F_{0}
with density function
f_{0}(y)=F_{0}^{\prime}(y)
on its support
[a,b]
, where
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
. Introduce the scalar product
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}f(y)g(y)f_{0}(y)dy
for functions that are square integrable with respect to
f_{0}(y)dy
, and suppose there exists an orthonormal system of polynomials
\{P_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
of degrees
k=0,1,2,\ldots
such that

\int_{a}^{b}P_{k}(y)P_{l}(y)f_{0}(y)dy=1(k=l).

(67)

Then define the absolutely continuous weight densities (
dG_{m}(p)=g_{m}(p)dp
)

g_{m}(p)=P_{m-1}(Q_{0}(p))

(68)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
, where
Q_{0}=F_{0}^{-1}
is the quantile function of
F_{0}
. It follows from (
67
)-(
68
) that
\{g_{m}\}
forms an orthonormal system of basis functions on
[0,1]
, i.e.

\int_{0}^{1}g_{m}(p)g_{l}(p)dp=1(m=l).

(69)

Equations (
65
) and (
69
) imply that
\{T_{m}(F)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
are the coefficients of an orthonormal series expansion of
Q
. Indeed, from Theorem 2.3 of Takemura (1983) we find that if
F
has a finite second moment

Q(p)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}T_{m}(F)g_{m}(p),

(70)

for all continuity points
p
of
Q(\cdot)
, strictly between 0 and 1. This is to say that
\{T_{m}(F)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
will quantify all aspects of the quantile function
Q
of
F
. Takemura (1983) used (
70
) in order to define goodness-of-fit tests of a location-scale family (
26
), where
F_{0}
in equation (
26
) is also the reference distribution of the series expansion. If we restrict ourselves to functionals up to order 4, it is clear that
\{T_{m}(F)\}_{m=1}^{4}
carries the same information about
F
as the four types of functionals of Section
2.3
, i.e.
T_{1}(F)
,
T_{2}(F)
,
T_{32}(F)
, and
T_{42}(F)
.

In order to verify that (
65
) and (
68
) for
m=1,2,\ldots
define a valid collection of
L
-functionals, the first condition (
62
) is equivalent to each polynomial
P_{k}(y)
having
k
distinct zeros with a leading positive coefficient of
y^{k}
. The second condition (
63
) follows by choosing
l=1
in (
69
), since
g_{1}(p)=1
. The third symmetry condition (
64
) holds whenever
F_{0}
is symmetric, and if
P_{k}(y)
is an even (odd) function of
y
when
k
is even (odd).

2.4.1
Symmetric and asymmetric collections of
L
-functionals

A collection
\{T_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
L
-functionals is symmetric if (
64
) holds for all
T_{m}
, and otherwise it is asymmetric. For a symmetric collection of functionals
\{T_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
we introduce a symmetric reference distribution
F_{0}
with mean 0 and variance 1 that satisfies

T_{m}(F_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&m=2,\\
0,&m\neq 2.\end{array}\right.

(71)

For a symmetric collection of
L
-functionals, we also introduce one distribution
F_{1}
that is moderately skewed to the right, and another symmetric distribution
F_{2}
, which is more heavytailed than
F_{0}
, such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T_{32}(F_{1})&=&1,\\
T_{42}(F_{2})&=&1.\end{array}

(72)

Let us verify that a symmetric polynomial collection (
69
) of
L
-functionals satisfies (
71
) and (
72
). Starting with (
71
), recall that
F_{0}
is assumed to be symmetric with expected value 0 and variance 1. Applying (
69
) with
m,l\in\{1,2\}
we find that
g_{1}(p)=1
and
g_{2}(p)=Q_{0}(p)
. Then a second application of (
69
) with variable
m
and
l=2
implies (
71
). It is also possible to relax the orthonormality condition (
69
) and adjust
g_{3}(\cdot)
and
g_{4}(\cdot)
by multiplicative constants in such a way that (
72
) holds for some appropriately chosen distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
.

When
F
is a life length distribution we typically choose a non-symmetric reference distribution
F_{0}
, supported on
[a,b)=[0,\infty)
,
and use a non-symmetric collection of
L
-functionals. Then we replace condition (
71
) by the milder requirement

T_{2}(F_{0})=1.

(73)

Since skewness and kurtosis are somewhat less natural concepts for life lengths, we will not always impose condition (
72
) in this context. For instance, we are typically more interested in quantifying how thick the right tail of

is (as in (
60
)-(

2.4.2
Examples of polynomial collections of

In this subsection we give three collections

Example 5

(Legendre collection of
L
-functionals)

This collection of symmetric
L
-functionals has absolutely continuous weight functions

g_{m}(p)=\sqrt{2m-1}L_{m-1}(2p-1),

(74)

where
L_{m-1}
is the Legendre polynomial of order
m-1
on
[-1,1]

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g_{1}(p)&=&1,\\
g_{2}(p)&=&\sqrt{3}(2p-1),\\
g_{3}(p)&=&\sqrt{5}[3(2p-1)^{2}-1]/2,\\
g_{4}(p)&=&\sqrt{7}[5(2p-1)^{3}-3(2p-1)]/2,\end{array}

T_{1}(F)=E(Y)
equals the mean (
25

is proportional to Gini’s mean difference (
34

The fact that

gives rise to an

68
), with a uniform reference distribution
F_{0}\sim U(-\sqrt{3},

\sqrt{3})
. Notice in particular that this symmetric distribution has first two moments

and

71
). Moreover, since
Q_{0}(p)=\sqrt{3}(2p-1)=g_{2}(p)

65
) and (

Equation (

62

g_{3}
and

by multiplicative constants so that (

) holds for some appropriate reference distributions, for instance the beta distributions
F_{1}\sim B(2,1)
and
F_{2}\sim B(0.5,0.5)

It turns out that the Legendre system of

-functionals is equivalent to the

-moments
\gamma_{m}(F)=T_{m}(F)/\sqrt{2m-1}
of
F
or order

are equivalent to the

\tau_{3}=\gamma_{3}/\gamma_{2}

of Hosking (1990). Another closely related concept is the family of probability weighted moments

\mu_{qrs}(F)=E\left[Y^{q}F(Y)^{r}(1-F(Y))^{s}\right]

is the distribution function of

is a linear combination of Legendre functionals

of
F
up to order

.

We argue that the Legendre system (
74
) of
L
-functionals is appropriate for bounded random variables
Y\in[a,b]
, and Hosking (1990) proves that

with a finite mean. In spite of this, each

is non-robust with a breakdown point of 0. It is possible though to define a robustified collection

of

g_{m}^{\pi}(p)=\frac{\sqrt{2m-1}}{1-2\pi}L_{m-1}\left(\frac{2p-1}{1-2\pi}\right)1(\pi<p<1-\pi)

(75)

and breakdown point

equals the trimmed mean, i.e.
\pi_{0}=\pi
and
\pi_{1}=1-\pi
in (

provides information about the conditional distribution of

for all quantiles between

at all continuity point

of

. In order for

to serve as a reference (

and

. More generally, we argue that the robustified Legendre collection of

-functionals is appropriate whenever

Example 6

(Hermite collection of

In this example we introduce a collection of symmetric

F_{0}\sim N(0,1)
is a reference distribution. The weight densities

g_{m}(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(m-1)!}}H_{m-1}[Q_{0}(p)]

(76)

are defined in terms of the quantile function
Q_{0}=F_{0}^{-1}
and the probabilistic Hermite polynomials

H_{k}(y)=(-1)^{k}e^{y^{2}/2}\frac{d^{k}e^{-y^{2}/2}}{dy^{k}},\quad-\infty<y<\infty,

(77)

of order
k=0,1,2,\ldots
. Inserting (
77
) into (
76
), we find that the first four weight densities have the form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g_{1}(p)&=&1,\\
g_{2}(p)&=&Q_{0}(p),\\
g_{3}(p)&=&[Q_{0}(p)^{2}-1]/\sqrt{2},\\
g_{4}(p)&=&[Q_{0}(p)^{3}-3Q_{0}(p)]/\sqrt{6}.\end{array}

(78)

Recall from (
27
) and (
36
) that
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
correspond to optimal
L
-functionals of location and scale for
F_{0}
.
It follows from well known orthogonality properties of Hermite polynomials that the weight functions in (
76
) constitute an orthonormal system (
69
) on
[0,1]
, and the series expansion (
70
) of
Q
can be interpreted as a robust Cornish-Fisher expansion (Fisher and Cornish, 1960). In particular, since
g_{2}(p)=Q_{0}(p)
, it follows from (
65
) and (
71
) that
F_{0}
indeed is a reference distribution for the Hermite class of
L
-functionals. As in Example
5
, one may multiply
g_{3}
and
g_{4}
by constants so that (
72
) holds for some appropriate reference distributions, for instance a non-central
t
-distribution
F_{1}
and central
t
-distribution
F_{2}
, with appropriate parameters.

Robustified versions
T_{m}^{\pi}(F)
of the Hermite functionals are constructed in the same way as in Example
5
, with weight functions

g^{\pi}_{m}(p)=\frac{1}{(1-2\pi)\sqrt{(m-1)!}}H_{m-1}[Q_{0}(\frac{p}{1-2\pi})]1(\pi<p<1-\pi).

The reference (
71
) of this system is the improper mixture distribution

F_{0}^{\pi}\sim\pi\delta_{-\infty}+(1-2\pi)N(0,1)+\pi\delta_{\infty},

with probabilities
\pi
at minus and plus infinity.

We argue that the (robustified) Hermite system of
L
-functionals is appropriate whenever
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, that is, when there are no upper or lower bound restrictions on
Y
.

Example 7

(Laguerre collection of
L
-functionals)

In this example we consider an asymmetric collection of
L
-functionals, which is of interest when
Y\geq 0
, for instance a lifetime. This class of
L
-functionals will have
F_{0}\sim\mbox{Exp}(1)
as a reference distribution. Let
\mbox{La}_{k}(y)
be the Laguerre polynomial of degree
k=0,1,2,\ldots
. These polynomials form an orthonormal system on
[0,\infty)
with respect to the density measure
dF_{0}(y)=e^{-y}dy
, i.e.

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{La}_{k}(y)\mbox{La}_{l}(y)e^{-y}dy=1(k=l).

(79)

The weight function of
T_{m}
is defined as

g_{m}(p)=(-1)^{m-1}\mbox{La}_{m-1}(Q_{0}(p))=(-1)^{m-1}\mbox{La}_{m-1}\left(\log\left((1-p)^{-1}\right)\right),

(80)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
. The factor
(-1)^{m-1}
of (
80
) ensures that all weight functions
g_{m}(p)
will have a leading positive coefficient of
Q_{0}^{m-1}(p)
, as required by (
62
). It can be seen that the first four weight functions are

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g_{1}(p)&=&1,\\
g_{2}(p)&=&Q_{0}(p)-1,\\
g_{3}(p)&=&Q_{0}^{2}(p)/2-2Q_{0}(p)+1,\\
g_{4}(p)&=&[Q_{0}^{3}(p)-9Q_{0}^{2}(p)+18Q_{0}(p)-6]/6.\end{array}

It follows from (
67
) and (
80
) that
\{g_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthonormal system (
69
) of weight functions on
[0,1]
, with

T_{m}(F_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{0}(p)g_{m}(p)dp=\int_{0}^{1}(g_{1}(p)+g_{2}(p))g_{m}(p)dp=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&m=1,2,\\
0,&m\geq 3,\end{array}\right.

in agreement with (
73
). Hence, according to this definition
T_{32}(F_{0})=0
although
F_{0}
is skewed to the right. The rationale is that most lifetime distributions are skewed in this direction. Using
F_{0}
as a yardstick we may therefore interpret
T_{32}(F)>0
as
F
being
more
skewed to the right than
F_{0}
.

When
Y\geq 0
it is typically only the heaviness of the right tail of
F=F_{Y}
that is of interest. A functional for right-heavytailedness is

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavyr}}(F)=\frac{T_{4,\mbox{\scriptsize right}}(F)}{T_{2}(F)},

where
T_{4,\mbox{\scriptsize right}}(F)
has weight function

g_{4,\mbox{\scriptsize right}}(p)=g_{4}(p)1(1-\pi<p<1),

and
0<\pi<1
is the smallest positive integer satisfying
\int_{1-\pi}^{1}Q_{0}(p)g_{4}(p)dp=0
. This definitions guarantees that
T_{\mbox{\scriptsize heavyr}}(F_{0})=0
, so that
F_{0}
is a reference for right-heavytailedness.

3
L
-functionals for regression models

In this section we regard
Y
as the outcome variable of a regression model with a vector
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})^{T}
of covariates. Let

F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)=P(Y\leq y\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\quad-\infty<y<\infty,

(81)

be the conditional distribution function of
Y
given
x
. By inverting this function we obtain the
p
:th conditional quantile (CQ)

Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}^{-1}(p)=\inf\{y;\,F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)\geq p\}.

(82)

Each functional (
1
) gives rise to a linear combination

\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=T(F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}})=\int_{0}^{1}Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG(p)

(83)

of CQs. We will refer to (
83
) as a conditional
L
-functional. The weight functions of Section
2
give rise to different conditional
L
-functionals that correspond to location, scale, unstandardized skewness or unstandardized heavytailedness measures of
F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
. For each
0<p<1
, let
\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
be an estimator of the conditional quantile (
82
), based on a sample of size
n
. The corresponding estimator

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG(p)

(84)

of
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
is a conditional
L
-statistic that reduces to (
4
) for a model without any covariates.

In order to study standardized measures of skewness and heavytailedness of
F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
, we need to consider ratios of two conditional
L
-functionals
T^{1}(F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}})
and
T^{2}(F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}})
that involve two different weight functions
G^{1}
and
G^{2}
. We will therefore study quantities

\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG^{1}(p)}{\int_{0}^{1}Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG^{2}(p)},

(85)

and their estimators

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG^{1}(p)}{\int_{0}^{1}\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dG^{2}(p)}.

(86)

For a model with covariates, the definitions of (
84
) and (
86
) will depend on whether the response variable
Y
is censored/truncated or not, and on the type of regression model that is used. In the following subsections we will consider several such models.

3.1
Data without censoring/truncation

In this subsection we assume there is no censoring or truncation, so that the response variable
Y
is observed. In more detail, suppose that a sample of independent random vectors
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{n},Y_{n})
is available, with the same conditional distribution
F_{Y_{i}\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}=F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}
of the response variable as in (
81
). In order to estimate
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
in (
83
) or (
85
) we have to make some smoothness assumptions on
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\to\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, which in turn requires smoothness of the conditional quantiles
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\to Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. The most general approach is to estimate
Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
(and hence also
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
) by some nonparametric method, for instance local polynomial regression (Chauduri, 1991), smoothing splines (Koenker et al., 1994), regression splines (He and Shi, 1994), piecewise polynomial regression tree methods (Chauduri and Loh, 2002), or a semiparametric linear model with varying coefficients (Kim, 2007). In the following two subsections we will rather concentrate on two fully parametric models for the relation between
x
and
Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
; linear models and transformed linear models.

3.1.1
Linear models

3.1.1.1 Models and estimators

When the outcome variable
Y\in(-\infty,\infty)
is unbounded, it is natural to use a linear model

Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p),

(87)

so that each conditional quantile (
82
) is a linear function of the covariates, with
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=(\beta_{1}(p),\ldots,\beta_{q}(p))^{T}
a vector of regression parameters. The simplest special case of (
87
) is the homoscedastic linear model, with

F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)=F_{0}(y-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}),

(88)

for some vector
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q})^{T}
. If the first regression component is an intercept (
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(1,x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})^{T}
) and
F_{0}
has median 0, it follows that
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\left[Q_{0}(p)-Q_{0}(0.5)\right]\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
and
Q_{0}
is the inverse of
F_{0}
. In particular, the framework of Section
2
is a special case of (
88
) with
q=1
and
b_{1}
the median of
F_{Y}
. It is also possible to incorporate heteroscedastic regression models of type

F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)=F_{0}\left(\frac{y-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$c$}}\right)

(89)

into (
87
), with
\mbox{\boldmath$c$}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q})^{T}
, for all
x
such that
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$c$}>0
. Again, it follows that
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\left[Q_{0}(p)-Q_{0}(0.5)\right]\mbox{\boldmath$c$}
if
F_{0}
has median 0.

The first
L
-based inference methods of regression focused on estimating
b
for the linear and homoscedastic model (
88
), based on some preliminary estimate (Bickel, 1973, Ruppert and Carroll, 1980, Welsh, 1987). Gutenbrunner and Jurečková (1992) introduced another approach based on regression rankscores. Here we will focus on procedures that estimate the conditional quantile (
87
) by first finding an estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
. The most general such estimator is a solution of the minimization problem

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=(\hat{\beta}_{1}(p),\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{q}(p))^{T}=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(Y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}),

(90)

where
\rho_{p}(y)=[p-1(y<0)]y
is the so called check function. This estimator is nonparametric in the sense that it makes few assumptions about the conditional distribution of
Y|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
, apart from its linear dependency on
x
in (
87
). It is usually referred to as a regression quantile and for the model of Section
2
, with no explanatory variables, it simplifies to the sample quantile
Y_{([np])}
. Koenker and Bassett (1978) introduced (
90
) for the homoscedastic regression model (
88
), and later it was extended by Koenker and Bassett (1982) and Koenker and Zhao (1994) to the heteroscedastic model (
89
). Efron (1991) proposed a slightly different nonparametric estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, by first minimizing an asymmetric squared loss function of the residuals
Y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
.

Frumento and Bottai (2016) modeled

\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{k}\beta^{k}(p)

(91)

parametrically in terms of a
q\times r
matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{r})
, where
\beta^{1}(p),\ldots,

\beta^{r}(p)
are known functions of
p
. This includes, for instance, the homoscedastic and heteroscedastic regression models (
88
) and (
89
), when
F_{0}
is regarded as known. Both of these models have
\beta^{1}(p)\equiv 1
,
\beta^{2}(p)=Q_{0}(p)-Q_{0}(0.5)
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
, whereas
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}
for the homoscedastic and
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$c$}
for the heteroscedastic model. The model in (
91
) gives rise to a parametric regression quantile estimator, where

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p;\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{k}\beta^{k}(p),

(92)

and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{k}
is an estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{k}
. Frumento and Bottai (2016) used

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{p}(Y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}))dp.

(93)

The conditional
L
-functional (
83
) of a linear model has a very tractable form. Recall first of all from Example
6
that the Hermite system (
76
) of weight functions
G=G_{m}
is a natural choice of
L
-functionals of order
m=1,2,\ldots
for a linear model, whenever the range of the outcome variable is unbounded. Moreover, since the conditional quantile (
87
) is a linear function of
x
, this linearity is preserved for conditional
L
-functionals (
83
). Indeed, inserting (
87
) into (
83
), we find that

\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)dG(p)=:\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$B$},

(94)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{q})^{T}
and
B_{j}=\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{j}(p)dG(p)
.

In order to estimate the conditional
L
-functional
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, the chosen estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)
(for instance the nonparametric (
90
) or the parametric (
92
)) is first plugged into (
87
) and then into (
84
). This gives an estimated conditional quantile
\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)
and an estimate

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}

(95)

of
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, where
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}=(\hat{B}_{1},\ldots,\hat{B}_{q})^{T}=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)dG(p)
is a linear combination of all
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)
. In the context of regression quantiles, the estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}
was first proposed by Koenker and Portnoy (1987) for the homoscedastic model (
88
), and then extended to heteroscedastic models (
89
) by Koenker and Zhao (1994). García-Pareja and Bottai (2018) studied (
94
) for weight functions that correspond to a uniform distribution on
[\pi_{0},\pi_{1}]
for some
0\leq\pi_{0}<\pi_{1}\leq 1
. They referred to
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
as a conditional compound expectation, and it generalizes the compound expectation (
24
) of the location model.

3.1.1.2 Asymptotics

In order to study the large sample asymptotics of
\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, we follow García-Pareja and Bottai (2018) and view
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p);\ 0<p<1\}
as an element of the
q
-dimensional product space
S^{q}
. In our setting the norm

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|_{S^{q}}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\|\beta_{j}\|_{S}

of this space generalizes (
8
) from
q=1
to
q\geq 1
. For
q
-dimensional vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
we introduce the
L^{1}
-norm
|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}|=\sum_{j=1}^{q}|B_{j}|
. Moreover, if
W,W_{1},\ldots,W_{n},\ldots
are random variables of a metric space
{\cal W}
, equipped with norm
|\cdot|
, we say that the sequence
W_{n}
converges in probability towards
W
as
n\to\infty
, i.e.
W_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}W
, if
P(|W_{n}-W|>\varepsilon)\to 0
for each
\varepsilon>0
. Equipped with these preliminaries, we have the following:

Proposition 1

(Consistency of (
95
).)

Suppose
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}\in S^{q}
is a consistent estimator of
\beta
, i.e.
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}
as
n\to\infty
.
Then
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
and the estimated conditional
L
-functional in (
95
) is consistent, i.e.
\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.
In analogy with (
9
) we have the inequalities

\begin{split}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}|&\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\int_{0}^{1}|\hat{\beta}_{j}(p)-\beta_{j}(p)|d|G|(p)\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\hat{\beta}_{j}(p)-\beta_{j}(p)||g(p)|dp+\sum_{m=1}^{M}|g_{m}||\hat{\beta}_{j}(\pi_{m})-\beta_{j}(\pi_{m})|\right)\\
&=\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|_{S^{q}}\end{split}

(96)

By assumption,
P(\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\|_{S^{q}}>\varepsilon)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for each
\varepsilon>0
. In conjunction with (
96
) it follows that
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
. Finally, since
\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=h(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n})
and
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=h(\mbox{\boldmath$B$})
for the continuous function
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{q}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by
h(\mbox{\boldmath$B$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
, consistency
\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
follows by the Continuous Mapping Theorem.

\Box

As a next step, in order to establish asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
as
n\to\infty
we first generalize (
10
) from the location model (
q=1
,
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\equiv 1
) and introduce the rescaled process

\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}(p)=\sqrt{n}[\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}(p)-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)],\quad 0<p<1

(97)

of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}(p)
. Viewing
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}(p);\,0<p<1\}
as a random element of
S^{q}
, we will assume weak convergence

\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

(98)

with respect to the topology in
S^{q}
introduced by
\|\cdot\|_{S^{q}}
. The limit in (
98
) is assumed to be a Gaussian process
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}(p);\,0<p<1\}
with mean
E(\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}(p))=(0,\ldots,0)^{T}
for all
0<p<1
and covariance function
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)=\mbox{Cov}(\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}(p),\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}(s))
for all
0<p,s<1
.

In the context of regression quantiles (
90
), in order to find the covariance function
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)
, it is helpful to rewrite (
97
) as

\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{n}^{-1}(p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}^{\prime}(Y_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p))+o_{p}(1),

where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{n}(p)&=&\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(p)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}}{n}\\
f_{i}(p)&=&\frac{dF_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}(y)}{dy}\bigg|_{y=Q(p\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i})}\\
\rho_{p}^{\prime}(y)&=&p-1(y<0)\end{array}

(99)

and
o_{p}(1)
is an asymptotically negligible remainder term, i.e.
o_{p}(1)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly for
p\in\mbox{supp}(G)
. Then assume there exist positive definite matrices
A
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(p)
such that

\begin{array}[]{cl}(i)&\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}/n=\mbox{\boldmath$A$},\\
(ii)&\lim_{n\to\infty}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{n}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(p),\\
(iii)&\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}|/\sqrt{n}\to 0.\end{array}

(100)

The regularity conditions in (
100
) imply that the Gaussian limit process has a covariance function

\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)=[\min(p,s)-ps]\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(p)\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(s)

(101)

for regression quantiles, see for instance Chapter 4 of Koenker (2005). This covariance function simplifies to

\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)=\frac{\min(p,s)-ps}{f_{0}(Q_{0}(p))f_{0}(Q_{0}(s))}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{-1}

(102)

for the homoscedastic regression model (
88
), cf. Koenker and Portnoy (1987). We notice that (
102
) generalizes the asymptotic covariance function (
15
) of the normalized quantile process (
10
) for data without covariates (
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}=1
and
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}=1
).

For the parametric estimator (
92
), in order to find the asymptotic covariance function of
\{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p);\\
0<p<1\}
, we first need to generalize (
17
) and establish asymptotic normality of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{n}
. To this end, it is convenient to introduce
\mbox{vec}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}})=(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{r}^{T})^{T}
, the column vector of length
rq
in which the columns of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}
are stacked on top of each other. It is shown in Frumento and Bottai (2016), under regularity conditions similar to (
100
), that

\sqrt{n}(\mbox{vec}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{n})-\mbox{vec}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}))\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$V$})=N\left(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{11}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{1r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{r1}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{rr}\end{array}\right)\right),

(103)

where

Equipped with these preliminaries, the following result provides asymptotic normality of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
and
\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

Proposition 2

(Asymptotic normality of (
95
))

Suppose (
98

whose covariance function
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)
is the asymptotic covariance function of
\{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}(p);\\
0<p<1\}
. Suppose further that the function

in (

. Then the estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$B$})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}),

(105)

where the mean vector of the
q
-dimensional limiting normal distribution is
\mbox{\boldmath$0$}=(0,\ldots,0)^{T}
and the covariance matrix equals

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)dG(p)dG(s).

(106)

Moreover, the estimator
\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
of the conditional
L
-functional in (

\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})-\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})]\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal L}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

(107)

as

.

Proof.
Introduce the functional
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}:S^{q}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
by means of
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}(\mbox{\boldmath$z$})=\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\mbox{\boldmath$z$}(p)dG(p)
. From the proof of Proposition
1

(110)

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{kl}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}(p,s)dG^{k}(p)dG^{l}(s)

3.1.2
Transformed linear models

Suppose the response variable
Y\in[a,b]
is constrained to lie in an interval with end points
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
. If at least one of these two end points is finite, the conditional quantile (
87
) of the linear model may fall outside
[a,b]
for some covariate vectors
x
. In order to avoid this, it is common to introduce a known and strictly increasing link function
h:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}
and assume that regression data
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},h(Y))
for the transformed outcome variable follows the linear model of Section
3.1.1
. This is analogous to link functions of Generalized Linear Models (McCullagh and Nelder, 1989), although here we focus on transformations of quantiles rather than of expected values. Since quantiles are preserved by monotone transformations it follows from (
87
) that the conditional quantiles satisfy

Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=h^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)\right)

(113)

for all
0<p<1
. If our objective is to estimate a conditional location, conditional scale, conditional unstandardized skewness or conditional unstandardized kurtosis of
Y
, we use the conditional
L
-functional in (
83
). For the transformed linear model (
113
), this functional

\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\int_{0}^{1}h^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p))dG(p)

(114)

is no longer a linear function of
x
, as in (
94
). In order to estimate
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
from data, we proceed as in Section
3.1.1
. We first estimate the regression parameter
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
of the transformed data set by some method. One possibility is to estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
nonparametrically with regression quantiles

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(h(Y_{i})-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}),

(115)

in analogy with (
90
). When
p=0.5
, we notice that (
115
) is a special case of
L_{1}
-estimation for nonlinear regression models (Oberhofer, 1982). Alternatively we estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
parametrically as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(h(Y_{i})-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(s;\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}))ds\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{1}(p)\\
\vdots\\
\beta^{r}(p)\end{array}\right),

(116)

in analogy with (
92
)-(
93
). As we will see below, for some models it is more convenient to estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
parametrically by maximum likelihood rather than using (
116
).

By plugging a nonparametric or parametric estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
into (
114
), we finally obtain an estimator

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\int_{0}^{1}h^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p))dG(p)

(117)

of
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. For some of the examples below, the link function
h
is unknown, and then we have to estimate
h(\cdot)
(parametrically or nonparametrically) as well as
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
. This gives rise to an estimator

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{h}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p))dG(p)

(118)

of
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, where
\hat{h}(\cdot)
is a monotone estimate of
h(\cdot)
.

We will now give several examples of link functions for which the corresponding transformed response variables follow a linear model, as described in (
113
).

Example 8

(Logit transformations.)

Suppose the outcome variables is bounded, so that
-\infty<a<b<\infty
are both finite. Liu et al. (2009) and Bottai et al. (2010) used model (
113
) with a logit transformation

h(y)=\mbox{logit}\frac{y-a}{b-a}=\log\frac{y-a}{b-y}

for such data.

\Box

Since
Y
is bounded in Example
8
, it is possible to use
L
-functionals in (
117
)-(
118
) with a weight density
dG(p)=g_{m}(p)dp
chosen from the Legendre system (
74
) of Example
5
. The remaining examples of this section concern outcome variables such as life lengths, where
Y
is constrained to be positive (
a=0
,
b=\infty
). Recall from Example
7
that it is possible then choose the weight density
dG(p)=g_{m}(p)dp
in (
117
)-(
118
) from the asymmetric collection (
80
) of basis functions, with an exponential reference distribution. The construction in Example
7
can be modified though so that some other lifetime distribution is used as reference.

Example 9

(Logarithmic transformations.)

When
Y
is non-negative(
a=0
,
b=\infty
) it is common to use the logarithmic link

h(y)=\log(y).

(119)

The Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) model (Kalbfleich and Prentice, 2002) is often used when
Y>0
is a lifetime, and it corresponds to having a parametric location-scale regression model (
88
) for the transformed data
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\log(Y_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In more detail,

\log\left[Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right]=\mu+\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}+\sigma Q_{0}(p)=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p),

(120)

where
Q_{0}
is the inverse of some reference distribution
F_{0}
of the log lifetime. Here
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(1,x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})^{T}
is the covariate vector with an added intercept,
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=(\mu+\sigma Q_{0}(p))\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(0,\mbox{\boldmath$b$}^{T})^{T}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})^{T}
contains the effect parameters of the
q-1
covariates. When
F_{0}
is known, for instance a logistic distribution, equation (
120
) corresponds to a parametric model (
116
) with
r=2
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}=(\mu,b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})^{T}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{2}=(\sigma,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
,
\beta^{1}(p)\equiv 1
and
\beta^{2}(p)=Q_{0}(p)
.
When computing
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)
it is possible though to use the maximum likelihood estimates of the
q+1
nonzero model parameters
\mu,\sigma,b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1}
of
\psi
, rather than (
116
). García-Pareja et al. (2019) used this approach, with a piecewise constant weight function (
24
), to estimate the conditional compound expectation
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
of an AFT model. When
F_{0}
is left unspecified it is also possible to estimate
\mu
and
b
nonparametrically according to (
115
) (Ying et al., 1995).

\Box

Example 10

(Power transformations.)

Assume as in Example
9
that
Y
is positive, i.e.
a=0
and
b=\infty
. Mu and He (2007) estimated conditional quantiles (
114
) with class of power link functions (Box and Cox, 1964), i.e.

h_{\gamma}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(y^{\gamma}-1)/\gamma,&\gamma\neq 0,\\
\log(y),&\gamma=0.\end{array}\right.

(121)

It was assumed in Mu and He (2007) that not only the regression parameter vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, but also the parameter
\gamma=\gamma(p)
of the power transformation (
121
), were unknown. For this reason their estimate included a combination of a CUSUM procedure and regression quantiles (
115

\hat{h}(y;p)=h_{\hat{\gamma}(p)}(y)
is then inserted into (
118
) in order to estimate
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
.

\Box

Example 11

(Log cumulative baseline hazard transformations.)

Asin the previous two examples, consider a positive response variable
Y
, so that
a=0
and
b=\infty
. The Cox regression model (Cox, 1972) expresses the hazard function

\lambda(y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})=\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)\exp(\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1})

(122)

of the lifetime
Y
as a product of a baseline hazard
\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}
and a term that involves the covariates
x_{2},\ldots,x_{q}
and a regression vector
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})^{T}
. Equivalently, (
122
) can be rewritten in terms of the cumulative hazard function as

\Lambda(y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})=\int_{0}^{y}\lambda(z\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})dz=\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)\exp(\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}),

(123)

where
\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)=\int_{0}^{y}\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(z)dz
is the corresponding cumulative baseline hazard. It is well known (Doksum and Gasko, 1990, Koenker and Geling, 2001, Portnoy, 2003, García-Pareja et al., 2019) that (
123
) can be rewritten as a transformed linear model (
113
), with a link function

h(y)=\log\left[\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y=Q(p\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$})}}{{=}}\log\left[\log(1-p)^{-1}\right]-\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)

(124)

that is the logarithm of the cumulative baseline hazard,
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(1,x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})^{T}
is a covariate vector with added intercept, and the regression vector is
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=\log\left[\log(1-p)^{-1}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}-(0,\mbox{\boldmath$b$}^{T})^{T}
. This is an instance of a parametric model (
116
) for which
r=2
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}
is known,
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{2}=(0,\mbox{\boldmath$b$}^{T})^{T}
,
\beta^{1}(p)=\log\left[\log(1-p)^{-1}\right]
is a quantile of a Gumbel distribution and
\beta^{2}(p)\equiv-1
. García-Pareja et al. (2019) estimate the conditional compound expectation
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
of a Cox model based on the piecewise linear weight function (
24
).

For a Cox model it is traditional to estimate
b
directly by partial likelihood rather than using the nonparametric or parametric estimators (
115
) and (
116
). Typically the baseline hazard is estimated nonparametrically (see for instance Kalbfleish and Prentice, 2002). This corresponds to a nonparametric estimate

\hat{h}(y)=\log[\hat{\Lambda}_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)]

(125)

of the link function
h
, which is inserted into (
118
) in order to estimate
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. On the other hand, if the baseline distribution
F_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)=1-\exp(-\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y))=1-\exp(-ay^{b})
is Weibull, with
a,b>0
known, then (
124
) is equivalent to an AFT model with a known logarithmic link function (
119
), and with
Q_{0}(p)
a quantile of a Gumbel distribution in (
120
). Gelfand et al. (2000) proposed a larger parametric model for the baseline distribution
F_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}
; a mixture of Weibull distributions. In our context this corresponds to having a finite number of unknown parameters
\gamma
of the link function (
124
), and a parametric estimate (
125
) of this link function based on
\hat{\Lambda}_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)=\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y;\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}})
. Finally, Royston and Parmar (2002) assumed a version

\log[\Lambda(y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})]=s(\log(y);\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})+\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}

(126)

of (
123
) where
\log[\Lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)]
is replaced by a cubic spline function
s(\cdot;\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})
of
\log(y)
that is parametrized by
\gamma
. It can be seen that this corresponds to replacing the link function in (
124
) by
h(y;\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})=s(\log(y);\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$})
. This gives rise to an estimated link function

\hat{h}(y)=s(\log(y);\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}),

(127)

which is inserted into (
118
) in order to estimate
\theta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
.

Example 12

(Log baseline odds transformations.)

As in the previousexample, let
x_{2},

\ldots,x_{q}
represent
q-1
covariates. Bennett (1983) introduced a model for which the proportional odds of death
satisfies

\Gamma(y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})=\frac{F_{Y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q}}(y)}{1-F_{Y\mid x_{2},\ldots,x_{q}}(y)}=\Gamma_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)\exp(\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}),

(128)

for some effect parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})^{T}
and baseline odds function
\Gamma_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)
. Doksum and Gasko (1990) showed that this model can be rewritten as a transformed linear model (
113
), with link function

h(y)=\log\left[\Gamma_{\mbox{\scriptsize bl}}(y)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y=Q(p\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$})}}{{=}}\log[\frac{p}{1-p}]-\sum_{k=2}^{q}x_{k}b_{k-1}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p).

(129)

The vector
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(1,x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})^{T}
contains an intercept and covariates, whereas
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)=\log[p/(1-p)]\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}-(0,\mbox{\boldmath$b$}^{T})^{T}
includes the effect parameters
b
of the covariates and an intercept parameter
\log[p/(1-p)]
that is a quantile of a standard logistic distribution. This corresponds to a parametric model (
116
) with

Assume there exists a collection

). The objective is to estimate the

114

136
) of
L
-functionals, when some data is lost due to censoring or truncation. This boils down to finding an estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
for all
0<p<1
, and then plugging this estimator into (
117
), (
118
) or (

1
-

apply to censored and truncated data as well, if consistency and asymptotic normality is established for
\{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p);\ 0<p<1\}

3.2.1
Censoring

When a distorted version
\tilde{Y}
of the outcome variable
Y
is observed, it is often the case that a censoring variable
C
causes this distortion. The two most common types of censoring are

\tilde{Y}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max(Y,C),&\mbox{left-censoring},\\
\min(Y,C),&\mbox{right-censoring}.\end{array}\right.

Without loss of generality we restrict ourselves to right-censoring. To this end, assume that
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},Y_{i},C_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. copies of covariate vectors, response and censoring variables, such that
Y_{i}
and
C_{i}
are conditionally independent given
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
, and with
\tilde{Y}_{i}=\min(Y_{i},C_{i})
the right-censored version of
Y_{i}
.

Let us first assume that all censoring variables
C_{i}
are observed, whether
Y_{i}
is censored or not. This corresponds to having a data set consisting of the observations
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},C_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. In this case the conditional quantile

\tilde{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$},C)=F_{\tilde{Y}\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}^{-1}(p)=\min(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p),C)

(140)

of censored data is an explicit function of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
. This gives rise to the estimate

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}[h(\tilde{Y}_{i})-\min(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},C_{i})]

of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
due to Powell (1986).

For the general right-censoring problem only
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},\Delta_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is observed,where
\Delta_{i}=1(Y_{i}=\tilde{Y}_{i})
indicates whether observation
i
has been censored or not. The relation between the quantile functions of censored and uncensored response variables is then somewhat more complicated than (
140
). Because of conditional independence of
Y
and
C
given
x
, it follows that

F_{\tilde{Y}\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)=1-(1-F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y))(1-F_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(y)),

(141)

and consequently the quantile function
\tilde{Q}(\cdot\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
of the right censored response variable is related to the quantile function
Q(\cdot\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
of the uncensored response as

\tilde{Q}(\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},p)\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),

(142)

where
\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},p)=1-(1-p)(1-F_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(Q(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})))
tells which quantile of the censored observation an uncensored
p
-quantile relates to. If
F_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
would be known for all
x
, then based on (
142
) and the fact that quantiles are preserved under the monotone transformation
h
, the estimator

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\pi(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i},p)}(h(\tilde{Y}_{i})-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$})

(143)

of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
due to Lindgren (1997) could be used. When the censoring distribution is unknown, a nonparametric Kaplan-Meier estimator
\hat{F}_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
was employed by Lindgren (1997) in order to replace
\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},p)
in (
90
) by an estimator
\hat{\pi}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},p)
.

Another consequence of (
141
) is that the random variable

\frac{1(h(\tilde{Y})-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)\geq 0)}{1-F_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p))}-(1-p)

has zero expectation. This motivated Ying et al. (1995) and Leng and Tong (2013) to propose and study the score-based estimator

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\left[\frac{1-\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})}{1-\hat{F}_{C\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$})}-(1-p)\right]\right|

(144)

of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, with
\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})=1(h(\tilde{Y}_{i})\leq\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$})
. Leng and Tong (2013) proved that this estimator is asymptotically normal with a
\sqrt{n}
-rate of convergence. As a drawback, (
144
) does not account for which observations that have been censored (
\Delta_{i}=0
) or not. In order to use this information, Wang and Wang (2009) introduced

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\left[p-\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})+\frac{(1-\Delta_{i})\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})(1-p)}{1-\hat{F}_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}(\tilde{Y}_{i})}\right]\right|,

(145)

where
\hat{F}_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
is a local Kaplan-Meier estimate of
F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}}
. This estimator can be motivated by noticing that each term of (
145
) has zero expectation when
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, and
\hat{F}_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}
is replaced by the true but unknown
F_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}
. Other estimators of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, for censored observations, have been proposed by Yang (1999), Portnoy (2003), Neocleous et al. (2004), and Peng and Huang (2008).

3.2.2
Truncation

Truncation means that some observations
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},Y)
are lost, depending on the value of some other truncation random variable
L
. The two most common types of truncation are that

(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},Y)\mbox{ is lost when }\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y<L,&\mbox{left-truncation},\\
Y>L,&\mbox{right-truncation}.\end{array}\right.

We will assume that left-truncation occurs together with right-censoring, so that the observed data set is
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},\Delta_{i},L_{i});\,i=1,\ldots,n,Y_{i}>L_{i}\}
.
Frumento and Bottai (2017) generalized (
145
) and presented an estimator

\begin{split}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}(p)=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\left[\omega_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})-\frac{\omega_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})(1-p)}{1-\hat{F}_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}(L_{i})}\right.\right.\hskip 74.0pt\\
\hskip 74.0pt\left.\left.-\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})+\frac{(1-\Delta_{i})\tilde{\omega}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})(1-p)}{1-\hat{F}_{Y\mid\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$x$}_{i}}(\tilde{Y}_{i})}\right]\right|,\end{split}

(146)

of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
, where
\omega_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$b$})=1(h(L_{i})\leq\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$})
. Frumento and Bottai (2017) gave conditions under which this estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
is consistent and asymptotically normal.

4.1

In this subsection we will investigate the suitability of the three orthogonal polynomial series of Examples

[a,b]

[0,\infty)

Thus, the Legendre polynomials should suit distributions with bounded essential support, Laguerre polynomials should be appropriate for distributions with half infinite essential support, whereas Hermite polynomials are preferable for distributions with unbounded essential support, to the left and right.

The approximated quantile function is denoted

&=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}T_{m}(F)g_{m}(p)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}p,\\
&=\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}T_{m}^{2}(F),\end{split}

m_{0}

L

5
Bird migration timing analysis

As an application of the regression methodology of Section
3
, we consider bird migration timing analysis, often called phenological analysis. We will use
L
-functionals and quantile regression to analyze changes over time in location, scale, skewness and kurtosis of bird migration timing distributions. We will also present the varying effects of covariates across the quantiles, and highlight reflections on how the covariates influence the values of the
L
-functionals. Our approach enables analysis of a larger number of distributional aspects than available with canonical methods, such as the one in Lehikoinen et.al. (2019).

5.1
The data

The data set we will analyze is collected by the Falsterbo Bird Observatory and concerns the species Common Redstart (
Phoenicurus phoenicurus
). This species was selected since it is a bird where the plumages of juveniles, adults, females and males are all distinct. Thus, reliable information on sex and age is available for use as covariates in the analysis.
Birds were captured and ringed by the bird observatory under similar schemes each year during the period 1980-2019, although we will use data from the years 1982-2019, since the identification of juvenile females was not performed the first two years. We will only use data on newly ringed birds. Recaptures between years are rare, but ideally these data should be incorporated as well.
Due to the extent of the ringing effort each year, it is safe to assume that the whole migration period was covered by the annual sampling window. The covariates and response variable of the data set are summarized in Table
2
.

5.3
Quantile regression

Fitting quantile regression models to the Common Redstart data makes it possible to find out whether covariates have different magnitudes of effect for different quantiles. Moreover, we can estimate the first four conditional
L
-functionals from (
84
), and the standardized conditional
L
-functionals from (
86
). In particular, we will study how these estimated conditional
L
-functionals change with covariates.

5.3.1
Model setup

We fitted models using two approaches: an identity link and a logit link. As presented in Section 3.1.2, quantiles are preserved under monotone transformations. The response variable is
Julian day
and thus the response values will be located within an interval
[a,b]
, where the sampling window has end points
a=80
and
b=162
. As presented in Example 8 the
logit
-link function is a monotone transformation. In accordance with Section
3.1.2
, we fit the model

\text{logit}\left(\frac{Q\left(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\right)-a}{b-a}\right)=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}(p),

(153)

with
q=4
parameters in
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}(p)
(corresponding to an intercept and an effect parameter for each one of the three covariates of Table
2
). Then we find
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p)
nonparametrically through (
115
), i.e.

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p)=\arg\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$b$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}\left(\text{logit}\left(\frac{Y_{i}-a}{b-a}\right)-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right).

(154)

For comparison with (
153
)-(
154
), we will also fit a linear quantile regression model (
87
). All implementations were made in
R
(R Core Team, 2021). We chose to implement the objective function ourselves and used
optim
to find estimates, but it is also possible to use software packages readily available for many types of quantile regression, such as
qgam
(Fasiolo et al., 2017) and
quantreg
(Koenker, 2020).

5.3.2
Model selection

When fitting the quantile regression model we may choose any link function that is a monotone transformation. Moreover, when having fitted the model we may choose between the three sets of weight functions
g_{m}(p)
presented in Examples
5
-
7
and then compute the associated four conditional (standardized)
L
-functionals
\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. An ideal model selection method should yield the combination of link function and weight functions that gives the best overall approximation of
\hat{Q}(\cdot\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
for all
x
. It should also be useful for selecting which covariates to use in the model. To this end, we modify (
148
) to create

\displaystyle R^{2}_{m_{0}}=1-\frac{\int_{\cal X}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\hat{Q}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})-\hat{Q}_{\text{appr}}(p\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}\hat{F}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$X$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})}{\int_{0}^{1}(\hat{Q}(p)-E_{\hat{F}}(Y))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}p},

(155)

where
\hat{F}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$X$}}
gives equal weight to all covariates vectors that appear in the data set (regardless of the number of observations with this covariate vector),

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(\cdot\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\hat{F}^{-1}(\cdot\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

(156)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(p)=\hat{F}^{-1}(p)=\inf\{y;\hat{F}(y)\geq p\}

(157)

and

\displaystyle\hat{F}(y)=\int_{\cal X}\hat{F}(y\mid\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,\mathrm{d}\hat{F}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$X$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}).

(158)

Thus,
E_{\hat{F}}(Y)=T_{1}(\hat{F})=T_{1}(\hat{F})g_{1}(p)
, and note that this is not the sample mean, but rather a weighted sample mean, since we weight the unique covariate vectors
x
(not the observations) equally. For the particular analysis of the Common Redstart data, this has the effect of weighting data from each year equally, meaning we do not let years with a larger number of registered birds have a larger influence. Since the number of birds can vary a lot between years, this is deemed advantageous for the purpose of the analysis.

As with (
148

m_{0}
index specifies the degree of
\hat{Q}_{\text{appr}}
. With
m_{0}=1

we get a measure of how much variation in the response is captured by the first
m_{0}
conditional
L

with
m_{0}\geq 2
includes variation in the response not explained be the covariates. In particular, if
R^{2}_{m_{0}}
increases significantly when
m_{0}
gets larger, this indicates that the corresponding weight functions
g_{m_{0}}(p)
capture an essential part of the variation in the conditional response distributions.

\hat{\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
for all three polynomial weight function sets. When computing the integrals in (

). The standardized skewness (c) and standardized kurtosis (d) are measured relative to a uniform distribution, since we use weight functions based on Legendre polynomials. The important information obtained from the figure is qualitative; how the various
L
-functionals change over time, rather than their actual values. From (c) we notice that the arrival distribution shifts from right-skewed towards symmetric over the study period, whereas from (d) we observe that the kurtosis increases. The conclusion would be that the left tail slowly becomes as thick as the right tail, meaning that early arrivers become more frequent over time.

6
Discussion

In this paper we developed a general theory of
L
-functionals of the response variable distribution of regression models. Based on orthogonal series expansions of the quantile functions of these distributions we generalized the concept of
L
-moments and identified collections
\{T_{m}(F)\}_{m=1}^{4}
of
L
-functionals that correspond to measures of location, scale, unstandardized skewness and unstandardized heavytailedness of the response. Different collections of
L
-functionals were introduced, depending on whether the domain of the response variable is bounded, or unbounded in one or two directions.

A number of generalizations of our work is possible. The first extension is to study more systematically the standardized
L
-functionals of skewness, heavytailedness, …
T_{m2}(F)=T_{m}(F)/T_{2}(F)
when
m\geq 3
. In particular, it is of interest to know which types of stochastic orderings between response distributions (Oja, 1981) these functionals preserve. Second, Takemura (1983) defined orthogonal series expansions of quantile functions for arbitrary reference distributions
F_{0}
for which
T_{m}(F_{0})
vanish when
m\geq 3
. In Examples
5
-
7
we considered expansions for uniform, normal, and exponential distributions. It is also possible to define a system (
68
) of
L
-functionals for Weibull, log-logistic, and other reference distributions
F_{0}
that are of interest in survival analysis. Third, a general way of robustifying a collection
\{T_{m}(F)\}
of
L
-functionals is to introduce a weight function
w
that downweights contributions from the lower and upper tails of
F
. The weight density

g_{m}(p)=w(p)P_{m-1}(Q_{0}(p))

of order
m
is a generalization of (
68
), using a variable rather than a constant weight function
w(p)\equiv 1
. For instance, the trimmed
L
-moments of Elamir and Seheult (2003) correspond to a weight function
w(p)=(1-p)^{t}p^{t}
for some positive integer
t
. Notice however that in general the orthogonality property (
69
) is lost for such a system of weight functions. Fourth, recursive estimation of quantiles (Stephanou et al., 2017) could be extended to online estimation of
L
-functionals. Fifth, regression quantiles have been used for time series (Cai and Zu, 2008, White et al., 2008) in order to estimate quantiles and robust measures of skewness/kurtosis of predictive distributions. It is of interest to analyze (ratios of)
L
-functionals of such predictive distributions. Sixth, measures of location, scale, skewness and heavytailedness of multivariate distributions can be defined in terms of multivariate
L
-statistics (Liu, 1990, Liu et al. 1999, Zuo et al., 2004, and Dang et al., 2009). In this context it is of interest to study different polynomial systems of
L
-functionals and their order numbers.
