Efficiency of stochastic coordinate proximal gradient methods on nonseparable composite optimization
Ion Necoara, Flavia Chorobura

Abstract
This paper deals with composite optimization problems having the objective function formed as the sum of two terms, one has Lipschitz continuous gradient along random subspaces and may be nonconvex and the second term is simple and differentiable, but possibly nonconvex and nonseparable . Under these settings we design a stochastic coordinate proximal gradient method which takes into account the nonseparable composite form of the objective function. This algorithm achieves scalability by constructing at each iteration a local approximation model of the whole nonseparable objective function along a random subspace with user-determined dimension. We outline efficient techniques for selecting the random subspace, yielding an implementation that has low cost per-iteration while also achieving fast convergence rates. We present a probabilistic worst-case complexity analysis for our stochastic coordinate proximal gradient method in convex and nonconvex settings, in particular we prove high-probability bounds on the number of iterations before a given optimality is achieved. Extensive numerical results also confirm the efficiency of our algorithm.

3
A stochastic coordinate proximal gradient algorithm

For minimizing the composite optimization problem (
1
), where
f
and
\psi
are assumed nonseparable functions (both possibly nonconvex), we propose a pure stochastic coordinate descent algorithm that requires sketching the gradient
\nabla f(x)
on some stochastic subspace and computing the prox of
\psi
also along this subspace. Hence, our Stochastic Coordinate Proximal Gradient (SCPG) algorithm is as follows:

Algorithm 1 (SCPG)
:

Give
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
a family of matrices and starting point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
k\geq 0
do:

1.   Randomly sample
U_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}
. Choose
\eta_{U_{k}}>0
and update:

H_{f,U_{k}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{L_{U_{k}}+\eta_{U_{k}}}{2}\;\text{ if }\psi\;\text{convex along subspaces from }\mathcal{U},\\
L_{U_{k}}+\eta_{U_{k}}\;\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

2.   Solve the following subproblem:

\displaystyle\;\;d_{k}\in\arg\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}f(x_{k})+\langle U^{T}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}),d\rangle+\dfrac{H_{f,U_{k}}}{2}\|d\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d).

(9)

3.   Update
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+U_{k}d_{k}
.

Note that, when the matrices
U_{k}
are chosen as the scaled sampling matrices, SCPG coincides with the random block coordinate descent method, while for
U_{k}=I_{n}
, SCPG recovers the
full
proximal gradient method, see Algorithm (46) in
[
32
]
. Moreover, for
f\equiv 0
and
H_{f,U_{k}}=\eta_{U_{k}}>0
our algorithm yields the popular
alternating minimization
(Gauss-Seidel ) method with regularization
[
3
,
15
]

\displaystyle d_{k}\in\arg\min_{d}\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d)+\dfrac{H_{f,U_{k}}}{2}\|d\|^{2},\;\;x_{k+1}=x_{k}+U_{k}d_{k}.

(10)

However,
[
32
]
derives rates only in the convex settings. Moreover, there are very few results ensuring that the iterates of a Gauss-Seidel method converges to a global minimizer, even for strictly convex functions, e.g.,
[
15
]
presents only asymptotic convergence results. In this paper we derive convergence rates for the general algorithm SCPG in both convex and nonconvex settings, with and without
f
. An important fact concerning our approach is that the convergence of SCPG works for any
\eta_{U_{k}}

\mathcal{L}_{F}(x_{0})=\left\{x:\,F(x)\leq F(x_{0})\right\}.

The main challenge when analyzing convergence of SCPG consists in connecting
d_{k}
with the full gradient
\nabla F(x_{k})
. If this is possible, then one can use the cost as a Lyapunov function for the iterates of SCPG, see Lemma
4.2
below. Hence, our key concept in the analysis of algorithm SCPG is related to the quality of our subspace selection, i.e., we consider some stochastic embedding of the gradient. This stochastic embedding of the gradient allows us to establish the connection between
d_{k}
and
\nabla F(x_{k})
in Lemma
4.4
. Hence, next definition introduces the notion of well-aligned matrix.

Definition 3.1

The matrix
U_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is well-aligned if

\|U^{T}_{k}\nabla F(x_{k})\|\geq\alpha\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|,

(14)

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
independent of the iteration
k
.

The notation of well-aligned iterations has been also used in
[
7
]
for derivative-free methods in nonlinear least-squares optimization. In our convergence analysis we use the set of well-aligned updates in
K
iterations, with
K
fixed, denoted
\mathbb{A}_{K}
, i.e.:

\mathbb{A}_{K}=\{k\in[K]:\;U_{k}\;\text{is well-aligned}\}.

In the sequel we also consider the following assumption related to well-alignment (subspace quality):

{assumption}

At each iteration
k
our subspace selection
U_{k}
is well-aligned for some fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
with probability at least
1-\delta
, with
\delta\in(0,1)
, independently of the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{k-1}\}
.

Fortunately, the random matrices theory
[
23
]
provides us many examples of classes of matrices that are probabilistic well-aligned. Below we discuss some classes of such matrices: e.g., scaled sampling matrices, Johnson-Lindenstrauss matrices and others (see also
[
7
,
23
,
37
]
).

3.1
Scaled Sampling matrices

First, we consider the class of scaled sampling matrices
\mathcal{U}
, which have one non-zero entry per row in any random column. They are defined as follows.

Definition 3.2

Matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a scaled sampling matrix if, independently for each
i\in[p]
, we sample
j\in[n]
uniformly at random and let
U_{ij}=\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{p}}
and the rest of the entries are zero.

The scaling factor is included so that
\mathbb{E}[\|U^{T}x\|]=\|x\|
. Scaled sampling matrices are computationally inexpensive to apply to vectors so that embeddings based on them can be computed efficiently. We have the following probabilistic result for this class, see e.g., Lemma 4.4.13 in
[
37
]
.
Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be a scaled sampling matrix. Let
\nu
be the maximum nonuniformity of the gradient:

\nu=\max_{x}\left\{\dfrac{\|\nabla F(x)\|_{\infty}}{\|\nabla F(x)\|}\right\}.

Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta=e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}p}{2n\nu^{2}}}
, we have:

\mathbb{P}[\|U^{T}\nabla F(x)\|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\|\nabla F(x)\|^{2}]\geq 1-\delta.

Note that Assumption
3
holds if:

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon\geq\alpha^{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;1-\sqrt{\dfrac{2n\nu^{2}}{p}\log\dfrac{1}{\delta}}\geq\alpha^{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;p\geq\dfrac{2n\nu^{2}}{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)^{2}}\log\dfrac{1}{\delta}.

Hence,
p
depends on
\nu
. Note that we always have
1/n\leq\nu^{2}\leq 1
. For well-behaved objective functions we have
\nu^{2}\simeq 1/n
and then, in this case
p
is independent of the dimension of the problem
n
. Otherwise,
p
may depend on
n
.

3.2
Johnson-Lindenstrauss embeddings

We can also consider for
\mathcal{U}
the class of Johnson-Lindenstrauss embeddings, i.e.
U\in\mathcal{U}
is a Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform (JLT). For these matrices we can improve the requirement on
p
. Recall that a random matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is an
(1-\alpha,\delta)
JLT if for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left[\alpha\|v\|^{2}\leq\|U^{T}v\|^{2}\leq(2-\alpha)\|v\|^{2}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Some common examples are:

(JLT-1): If
U
is a random Gaussian matrix with independent entries normally distributed,
U_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/p)
, and
p=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\alpha)^{-2}|\log(\delta)|\right)
, then
U
is an
(1-\alpha,\delta)
JLT (see Theorem 2.13 in
[
6
]
).

(JLT-2): We say that
U
is an
s
hashing matrix if it has exactly
s
nonzero entries per row (indices sampled independently), which take values
\pm 1/\sqrt{s}
selected independently with probability
1/2
. Then, any
s
-hashing matrix
U
with
s=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\alpha)^{-1}|\log(\delta)|\right)
and
p=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\alpha)^{-2}|\log(\delta)|\right)
is
(1-\alpha,\delta)
JLT
[
18
]
.

Since
\alpha^{2}\leq\alpha
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have for any
U
which is
(1-\alpha,\delta)
JLT:

\mathbb{P}\left[\|U^{T}v\|\geq\alpha\|v\|\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Then, for
v=\nabla F(x)
, Assumption
3
holds if
U
is any
(1-\alpha,\delta)
JLT. Moreover, in the previous examples the dimension of the subspace
p
satisfies:

p\geq(1-\alpha)^{-2}|\log(\delta)|.

Inequality above does not give a simple criterion for choosin
p
in terms of
\alpha
and
\delta
. In Figure 1 in
[
19
]
we can see numerical evidence that
p
can be choosen independent of
n
.

4.1
Sublinear convergence

Before proving sublinear rate of convergence for algorithm SCPG in the nonconvex case, we need to define the following constants (recall that we assume

bounded, at least with high probability):

H_{F,U_{k}}\leq H_{F,\max}\;\;\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Furthermore, consider:

\eta_{\min}=\min_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\eta_{U_{k}}\;\;\text{ and}\;\;C=\dfrac{\alpha^{2}\eta_{\min}}{2H_{F,\max}^{2}}.

Now, we are ready to prove a high-probability sublinear bound for the minimum norm of subgradients generated by algorithm SCPG.

Theorem 4.8

If Assumptions

Proof 4.9

Proof

\displaystyle\dfrac{\eta_{\min}}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{K}\|d_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(x_{0})-F(x_{K+1})\leq F(x_{0})-F^{*}.

Remark 4.10

Usually, in the coordinate descent literature, where
U
’s are submatrices of the identity matrix
I_{n}
and
\psi
is separable, the complexity estimates are expressed in terms of the number of blocks (i.e., the ratio between dimensions of the original problem and of the subproblems). In our notations, this ratio can be defined as

. Moreover, the existing works for separable
\psi
have derived convergence rates that depend linearly on such

[

,

,
27
,

]
. Let us also analyze the dependence on

of the convergence rate (

) of Theorem

for algorithm SCPG in the optimality given by the norm of the gradient. First, note that we can consider

and chose

(the golden number). Now, let us analyze the rate in the norm of the gradient:

Scaled sampling matrices. Choosing

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\nu}N^{\frac{1}{4}}H_{F,\max}}{\sqrt{\eta_{\min}k}}\right).

JLT matrices. Choosing

, then

and we get that with high-probability the convergence rate for the norm of the gradient is of order:

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{H_{F,\max}}{\sqrt{\eta_{\min}k}}\right).

In conclusion, when

are scaled sampling matrices our convergence estimate depends on

, while there is no explicit dependendence on

for JLT matrices (although, in some particular cases, one can still have some hiden dependence on

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\alpha^{2}\eta_{\min}}{2H_{F,U_{k}}^{2}}\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{\eta_{\min}}{2}\|d_{k_{j}}\|^{2}\leq F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x_{k_{j}+1}).

This implies that

\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|^{2}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\dfrac{1}{C}\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x_{k_{j}+1})\right).

Combining the inequality above with the KL property (
36
), we get:

\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F_{*}\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\sigma_{q}^{\frac{2}{q}}\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|^{2}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{q}^{\frac{2}{q}}}{C}\right)\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x_{k_{j}+1})\right).

(37)

Since
(F(x_{k}))_{k\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence (see Lemma
4.2
), we have
F(x_{k_{j+1}})\leq F(x_{k_{j}+1})
and then from (
37
) we get the following recurrence valid for all
k_{j}\geq k_{j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}
:

\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F_{*}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{j+1}})-F_{*}\right)\geq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}C\sigma_{q}^{-\frac{2}{q}}\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F_{*}\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}.

(38)

First, let us consider that
q\in(1,2)
.
Multiplying both sides of the inequality (
38
) by
\left(C\sigma_{q}^{-\frac{2}{q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}
, denoting
\Delta_{k_{j}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\gamma_{1}^{\frac{q}{2-q}}\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F_{*}\right)
and considering
\gamma_{1}
defined in (
30
), we obtain:

\Delta_{k_{j}}-\Delta_{k_{j+1}}\geq(\Delta_{k_{j}})^{\frac{2}{q}}\quad\forall k_{j}\geq k_{j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}.

(39)

Considering the second inequality in (
27
) for
\zeta=\frac{2-q}{q}>0
, we get:

\displaystyle\Delta_{k_{J}}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\zeta(J-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma})\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}}\iff\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\gamma_{1}^{\frac{q}{2-q}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{(J-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma})(2-q)}{q}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{(J-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma})(2-q)}{q}\gamma_{1}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}

Using
|\mathbb{A}_{K}|=J+1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}\right)(2-q)}{q}\gamma_{1}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}.

(40)

From Lemma
4.6
, we have for all
\beta\in(0,1)
:

\mathbb{P}[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1\geq(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-1]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

(41)

Hence, if
K>\dfrac{1+j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)}-1
, we get:

\mathbb{P}\left[\dfrac{1}{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}\leq\dfrac{1}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-1-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

(42)

From (
40
) and the inequality above, we get

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\gamma_{1}C_{1}\left(C_{2}(K+1)-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)\right)\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

Using basic probabilistic arguments, we have that:

\mathbb{P}\left[F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\leq\dfrac{1}{\left(\gamma_{1}C_{1}\left(C_{2}(K+1)-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)\right)\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

Since the sequence
(F(x_{k}))_{k\geq 0}
is decreasing, the first statement of the theorem follows.

Second, let us consider
q=2
. From (
28
) and (
38
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(1-\dfrac{C}{\sigma_{2}}\right)^{J-j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}(F(x_{k_{j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}})-F_{*}).

Using
|\mathbb{A}_{K}|=J+1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\gamma_{2}^{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)}(F(x_{k_{\bar{j}_{\delta,\epsilon,\gamma}}})-F_{*}).

(43)

with
\gamma_{2}
defined in (
30
). If
K>\dfrac{1+j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)}-1
by
(
41
)
, we get:

\mathbb{P}\left[\!\gamma_{2}^{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)}\leq\gamma_{2}^{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)}\right]\!\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

Using this probability bound in (
43
),
we get

\mathbb{P}\left[\!\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\right)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bar{\delta}}}\gamma_{2}^{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)}(F(x_{k_{j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}}})-F_{*})\right]\!\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

Using that
F(x_{k})\leq F(x_{0})
for all
k\geq 0
, we finally get:

\mathbb{P}\left[\!F(x_{k_{J}})-F_{*}\leq\gamma_{2}^{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-(j_{\bar{\delta},\epsilon,\gamma}+1)}(F(x_{0})-F_{*})\right]\!\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

Finally, since
(F(x_{k}))_{k\geq 0}
is decreasing, the second statement of the theorem also follows.

Third, if
q>2
, using
F(x_{k_{j+1}})\leq F(x_{k_{j}})
in (
39
), we obtain:

\Delta_{k_{j}}-\Delta_{k_{j+1}}\geq(\Delta_{k_{j+1}})^{\frac{2}{q}}.

Using
\zeta=\frac{2}{q}-1\in(-1,0)
, then we get (
29
), which yields the third statement of the theorem.
\square

Remark 4.17

Note that if the function
F
satisfies the Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) inequality or it is uniformly convex of degree
q>1
, then the KL inequality (
7
) holds for
F
at the minimum value
F^{*}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
4
]
for the PL and (
2
) for the uniform convex functions). Hence, in these settings we have a global relation of the form (
7
) for
F
, not a local one as in the general KL property. Under these settings (i.e., PL or uniform convex functions) the results of the previous Theorem
4.15
are stronger as they hold for all
K>1/((1-\beta)(1-\delta))-1
and for
\bar{\delta}=0
. Moreover, similar conclusions as in Remark
4.10
can be derived regarding the dependence on
N
for the convergence rates obtained in Theorem
4.15
.

5
Convergence analysis: convex case

In this section we assume that the composite objective function
F=f+\psi
is convex. Note that we do not need to impose convexity on
f
and
\psi
separately. Denote the set of optimal solutions of (
1
) by
X^{*}
and let
x^{*}
be an element of this set. Define also:

R:=\max_{y}\min_{x^{*}\in X^{*}}\{\|y-x^{*}\|:F(y)\leq F(x_{0})\},

which is a measure of the size of the sublevel set of
F
in
x_{0}
. Recall that
k_{0}
denotes the first well-aligned iteration. In these settings we get the following convergence result.

Theorem 5.1

If Assumptions
2
and
3
hold, the sublevel set
\mathcal{L}_{f}(x_{0})
is bounded and additionally
F
is convex, then for any number of iterations
K
satisfying

K>\max\left\{\dfrac{1}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)}-1,k_{0}\right\}

the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by algorithm SCPG has with high-probability the following sublinear rate in function values:

\mathbb{P}\left[F(x_{K})-F(x^{*})\leq\dfrac{1}{C}\left(\dfrac{R^{2}}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-1}\right)\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

(44)

Proof 5.2

Proof
From Lemma
4.4
, for
k\in\mathbb{A}_{K}
, we have that:

\|d_{k}\|^{2}\geq\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{H_{F,U_{k}}^{2}}\|\nabla F({x_{k}})\|^{2}.

Since
\mathbb{A}_{K}=\{k_{0},...,k_{J}\}
. Combining the inequality above, Lemma
4.2
and relation (
21
), we get:

F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x_{k_{j}+1})\geq\dfrac{\eta_{\min}}{2}\|d_{k_{j}}\|^{2}\geq C\|\nabla F({x_{k_{j}}})\|^{2}.

(45)

On the other hand, since
F
is convex, we have:

\displaystyle F(x^{*})-F(x_{k_{j}})\geq\langle\nabla F(x_{k_{j}}),x^{*}-x_{k_{j}+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|\|x_{k_{j}+1}-x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|R.

Hence

\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|\geq\dfrac{F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})}{R}.

(46)

By (
45
) and (
46
), we obtain:

\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)-\left(F(x_{k_{j}+1})-F(x^{*})\right)\geq C\dfrac{\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)^{2}}{R^{2}}.

Since
(F(x_{k}))_{k\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence (see Lemma
4.2
), we have
F(x_{k_{j+1}})\leq F(x_{k_{j}+1})
and thus:

\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)-\left(F(x_{k_{j+1}})-F(x^{*})\right)\geq C\dfrac{\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)^{2}}{R^{2}}.

Multiplying both sides by
C/R^{2}
, we further get:

\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)}{R^{2}}-\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{k_{j+1}})-F(x^{*})\right)}{R^{2}}\geq\left[\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)}{R^{2}}\right]^{2}.

We denote:

\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{k_{j}})-F(x^{*})\right)}{R^{2}}=\Delta_{k_{j}}.

Then, we obtain the following recurrence:

\Delta_{k_{j}}-\Delta_{k_{j+1}}\geq\left(\Delta_{k_{j}}\right)^{2}.

From Lemma
4.13
, relation (
27
), we obtain
\Delta_{k_{J}}\leq 1/J
, or equivalently:

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{k_{J}})-F(x^{*})\right)}{R^{2}}\leq\dfrac{1}{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1}\iff F(x_{k_{J}})-F(x^{*})\leq\dfrac{1}{C}\left(\dfrac{R^{2}}{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1}\right).

Using again the fact that
(F(x_{k}))_{k\geq 0}
is decreasing, we get:

F(x_{K})-F(x^{*})\leq\dfrac{1}{C}\left(\dfrac{R^{2}}{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-1}\right).

(47)

On the other hand, from Lemma
4.6
we have:

\mathbb{P}\left[\dfrac{1}{|A_{K}|-1}\leq\dfrac{1}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-1}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(1-\delta)(K+1)}.

for all
\beta\in(0,1)
. If
K>\dfrac{1}{(1-\beta)(1-\delta)}-1
, then
(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)-1>0
and from (
47
) our convergence rate (
44
) follows.
\square

We notice that the dependence on
N
, where
N=n/p
, in the convergence rate in function values from Theorem
5.1
for algorithm SCPG is for scaled sampling matrices of order:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\nu\sqrt{N}H_{F,\max}^{2}}{\eta_{\min}k}\right),

while for Johnson-Lindenstrauss matrices of order:

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{H_{F,\max}^{2}}{\eta_{\min}k}\right).

Hence, similar conclusions as in Remark
4.10
can be also derived in this case. Finally, it is important to note that an immediate byproduct of our previous convergence analysis (Theorems 1, 2 and 3) leads to novel convergence results and rates for the popular alternating minimization (Gauss-Seidel) algorithm with a proper regularization (i.e.,
f=0
) in the convex and nonconvex settings with general sampling strategies.

6
Simulations

In the numerical experiments we consider several applications: the subproblem in the cubic Newton method
[
34
]
, the smallest eigenvalue of a matrix
[
8
]
and the logistic regression with quadratic or cubic regularization
[
25
]
. In the sequel, we describe these problems and present extensive numerical results. Note that our composite problem (
1
) permits to handle general coupling functions
\psi(x)
, with
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})
, e.g.: (i)
\psi(x)=\|Ax\|^{\ell}
, with
\ell\geq 2
and
A
linear operator (in particular,
\psi(x_{1},x_{2})=\|A_{1}x_{1}-A_{2}x_{2}\|^{\ell}
, see
[
21
]
); (ii) when solving the subproblem in higher order methods (including cubic Newton) recently popularized by Nesterov
[
32
]
(where
\psi(x)=\|x\|^{\ell}
); (iii) when minimizing an objective function that is relatively smooth w.r.t. some (possibly unknown) function
h
, see
[
21
]
. In all the simulations, we choose scaled sampling matrices
U_{k}
and for a random algorithm we report the average time/full iterations after 5 runs.

6.1
Cubic Newton subproblem

One of our main motivations for analyzing coordinate descent schemes for composite problems with nonseparable terms came from the need of having fast algorithms for solving the subproblem in the cubic Newton method
[
34
]
, which is supported by global efficiency estimates for general classes of optimization problems. Recall that in the cubic Newton method at each iteration one needs to minimize an objective function of the form:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x):=\frac{1}{2}\langle Ax,x\rangle+\langle b,x\rangle+\frac{M}{6}\|x\|^{3},

(48)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
M>0
are given. As discussed in Section 2, the function
\psi(x)=\frac{M}{6}\|x\|^{3}
is uniformly convex, but it is nonseparable. Moreover, in this case
f(x)=\langle b,x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle Ax,x\rangle
is smooth. Hence, this problem fits into our general model (
1
) and we can use algorithm SCPG to solve it.

Implementations details:

In the SCPG method, we need to solve the following subproblem at each iteration:

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\dfrac{M}{6}\|x+Ud\|^{3}+\langle U^{T}\nabla f(x),d\rangle+\dfrac{H_{f,U}}{2}\|d\|^{2}.

Hence, from the optimality condition of the subproblem above, we obtain:

U^{T}\nabla f(x)+H_{f,U}d^{*}+\dfrac{M}{2}\|x+Ud^{*}\|U^{T}(x+Ud^{*})=0.

(49)

From the equality above, we have:

\left(H_{f,U}I_{p}+\dfrac{M}{2}\|x+Ud^{*}\|U^{T}U\right)d^{*}=-\left(U^{T}\nabla f(x)+\dfrac{M}{2}\|x+Ud^{*}\|U^{T}x\right).

(50)

Note that, if we know
\mu=\|x+Ud^{*}\|
, we can solve the linear system of equations (
50
) very efficiently in order to obtain
d^{*}
, even in
\mathcal{O}(p)
operations. Indeed, if the matrix
U
is chosen as scaled sampling matrix, then
U^{T}U
, via some permutation, is a block diagonal matrix with blocks rank one matrices of the form
\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}
, with
\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
a vector of ones, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{p}}p_{j}=p
. Then, solving (
50
) is equivalent to finding solutions to linear systems of the form:

\displaystyle\left(H_{f,U}I_{p_{j}}+\dfrac{M\mu n}{2p}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}\right)d^{(j)}=g^{(j)}\quad\forall j=1:\bar{p}.

(51)

When
p_{j}=1
the solution of (
51
) is trivial to compute. When
p_{j}>1
the linear system (
51
) can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(p_{j})
operations. Indeed, (
51
) is equivalently to:

\displaystyle H_{f,U}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}d^{(j)}+\dfrac{M\mu n}{2p}(\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}})\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}d^{(j)}=\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}g^{(j)}\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}d^{(j)}=\dfrac{\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}g^{(j)}}{H_{f,U}+(\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}})M\mu n/2p}.

Knowing now the value of
\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}d^{(j)}
, we can compute easily
d^{(j)}
as:

\displaystyle d^{(j)}=\dfrac{1}{H_{f,U}}\left(g^{(j)}-\dfrac{M\mu n}{2p}(\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}^{T}d^{(j)})\mathbb{1}_{p_{j}}\right).

Note that the total cost of computing
d^{(j)}
is
\mathcal{O}(p_{j})
and thus the total cost of computing the solution of the system (
50
) is
\mathcal{O}(p)
. Further, let us see how we can compute
\mu
. Note that if
U
is the scaled sampling matrix, then
UU^{T}
is a diagonal matrix with
\bar{p}\leq p
nonzero entries on the diagonal. Then, from (
49
) we have:

x+Ud^{*}=\left(H_{f,U}I_{n}+\dfrac{M\mu}{2}UU^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(H_{f,U}x-UU^{T}\nabla f(x)\right),

and it is easy to compute the inverse of the above matrix, since it is just a diagonal matrix. Let us denote
J
the index set corresponding to the indices of the nonzeros entries in the diagonal of
UU^{T}
and let
u_{j}
be the corresponding values, with
j\in J
. Taking the norm square in the equality above and using the notation
\mu=\|x+Ud^{*}\|
, we obtain the following nonlinear equation in
\mu
:

\displaystyle\mu^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\dfrac{\left(H_{f,U}x^{(j)}-u^{(j)}\nabla_{j}f(x)\right)^{2}}{\left(H_{f,U}+M\mu u^{(j)}/2\right)^{2}}+\sum_{i=1,i\notin J}^{n}(x^{(i)})^{2}.

Then,
\mu
is the positive root of the above scalar nonlinear equation.
Note that there are very efficient methods for finding the root of a scalar nonlinear equation, e.g., Newton method. Hence, for these settings, the overall complexity in the SCPG algorithm at each iteration is usually proportional to
p
. Similarly, when
\psi(x)=|a^{T}x|^{\ell}
, with
\ell>2
, and
U
is chosen as scaled sampling matrix, we can also solve the corresponding subproblems efficiently in the SCPG algorithm combining the same reasoning as above with the Sherman-Morrison formula.

We compare algorithm SCPG with the methods proposed in
[
8
]
and
[
32
]
which were designed for solving subproblem (
48
). Note that, since SCPG recovers Algorithm 46 in
[
32
]
, we can also apply that method in the nonconvex case. Recall that the update rule in
[
8
]
is given by:

x_{k+1}=(I-\eta A-\dfrac{M}{2}\eta\|x_{k}\|I)x_{k}-\eta b,

with
0<\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{4\|A\|+2MR}
and
R=\dfrac{\|A\|}{M}+\sqrt{\dfrac{\|A\|^{2}}{M^{2}}+\dfrac{2\|b\|}{M}}
. Moreover, the update rule in
[
32
]
is:

x_{k+1}=\dfrac{2}{2H+M\mu}(Hx_{k}-Ax_{k}-b),

with
\|A\|\leq H
and
\mu\geq 0
given by the solution of the equation
M/2\mu^{2}+H\mu-\|Hx_{k}-Ax_{k}-b\|=0
. In our implementations we used the parameters
\eta=\dfrac{1}{4\|A\|+2MR}
and
H=\|A\|
. Moreover, in SCPG we considered
H_{f,U_{k}}=\|U^{T}_{k}AU_{k}\|
. Following
[
8
]
, the starting point is:

x_{0}=-r\dfrac{b}{\|b\|},\text{ with }r=-\dfrac{b^{T}Ab}{M\|b\|^{2}}+\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{b^{T}Ab}{M\|b\|^{2}}\right)^{2}+\dfrac{2\|b\|}{M}}.

In the experiments the vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is generated from a standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
was generated as
A=Q^{T}BQ
, where
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an orthogonal matrix and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix (with positive entries in the convex case and negative entries in the nonconvex case, respectively). Moreover, we consider different values for
n
ranging from
10^{4}
to
10^{6}

p

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have designed stochastic coordinate proximal gradient methods for composite optimization problems having the objective function formed as a sum of two terms, one smooth and the second possibly nonconvex and nonseparable. We have provided probabilistic worst-case complexity analysis for our stochastic coordinate descent method in convex and nonconvex settings, in particular we have proved high-probability bounds on the number of iterations before a given optimality is achieved. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first proposing a pure stochastic coordinate descent algorithm which is supported by global efficiency estimates for general classes of nonseparable composite optimization problems. The numerical results on random and real data have also confirmed the efficiency of our algorithm.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
. From mean value theorem we have:

f(x+Uh)-f(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+tUh),Uh\rangle dt.

If the function
f
satisfies (
2
), then it follows:

\displaystyle|f(x+Uh)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),Uh\rangle|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+tUh)-\nabla f(x),Uh\rangle\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\langle U^{T}(\nabla f(x+tUh)-\nabla f(x)),h\rangle|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|U^{T}(\nabla f(x+tUh)-\nabla f(x))\|\|h\|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}L_{U}\|th\|\|h\|dt=L_{U}\|h\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}t\,dt=\frac{L_{U}}{2}\|h\|^{2},

which proves the statement.
\square

Proof of Lemma
2.3
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have:

U^{T}\int_{0}^{1}y\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}U^{T}y\,dt\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Therefore, we have:

U^{T}(\nabla f(x+Uh)-\nabla f(x))=\int_{0}^{1}U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)Uh\,dt.

If (
4
) is satisfied, we get:

\displaystyle\|U^{T}(\nabla f(x+Uh)-\nabla f(x))\|=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)Uh\,dt\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)Uh\|\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)U\|\|h\|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}L_{U}\|h\|\,dt=L_{U}\|h\|.

On the other hand, if (
2
) is satisfied, then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\alpha>0
, we have:

\left\|\int_{0}^{\alpha}U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)Uh\,dt\right\|=\|U^{T}(\nabla f(x+\alpha Uh)-\nabla f(x))\|\leq\alpha L_{U}\|h\|.

This implies that:

\left\|\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\alpha}U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x+tUh)U\,dt\dfrac{h}{\|h\|}\right\|\leq L_{U}.

Taking
\alpha\to 0
, we get:

\left\|U^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)U\dfrac{h}{\|h\|}\right\|\leq L_{U}.

From this and the definition of matrix norm
\displaystyle\|A\|=\max_{\|h\|=1}\|Ah\|
, we get (
4
).
\square

Proof of Lemma
4.2
:

First consider that
\psi
is convex along the subspaces generated by matrices from
\mathcal{U}
. Using the optimality condition (
15
) for
d_{k}
with the functions:

\displaystyle\theta(d)=f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),U_{k}d\rangle+\frac{H_{f,U_{k}}}{2}\|d\|^{2}\;\text{and}\;\phi(d)=\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d),

we get:

\displaystyle\langle U^{T}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})+H_{f,U_{k}}d_{k},d-d_{k}\rangle+\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d)\geq\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d_{k})\quad\forall d\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

From the previous optimality condition for
d=0

\displaystyle-\langle U^{T}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})+H_{f,U_{k}}d_{k},d_{k}\rangle+\psi(x_{k})\geq\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d_{k})=\psi(x_{k+1}).

Using Assumption
2
and (
3
), we obtain:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})+\psi(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),U_{k}d_{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{U_{k}}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k+1}).

(52)

Then, combining both inequalities, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})=f(x_{k+1})+\psi(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\!f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),U_{k}d_{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{U_{k}}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}\!-\!\langle U^{T}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}),d_{k}\rangle\!-\!H_{f,U_{k}}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k})

\displaystyle=F(x_{k})-\left(H_{f,U_{k}}-\dfrac{L_{U_{k}}}{2}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}.

Similarly, if
\psi
is a general function, given that
d_{k}
is the global optimum of subproblem (
9
), we have that the objective function in (
9
) evaluated in
d_{k}
is smaller than in
0

\langle U^{T}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}),d_{k}\rangle+\dfrac{H_{f,U_{k}}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k}+U_{k}d_{k})\leq\psi(x_{k}).

Combining this with inequality (
52
), we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),U_{k}d_{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{U_{k}}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k+1}).

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F(x_{k})-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(H_{f,U_{k}}-L_{U_{k}}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}.

This proves our statement.
\square

Proof of Lemma
4.4
:

Since
f
and
\psi
are differentiable, then from the optimality conditions for
d_{k}
, i.e.,
d_{k}
is a stationary point of subproblem (
9
), we have that:

\displaystyle U^{T}_{k}(\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla\psi(x_{k+1}))+H_{f,U_{k}}d_{k}=0.

(53)

Moreover,
\nabla F(x_{k})=\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla\psi(x_{k})
. Using (
53
) and Assumptions
2
and
3
, we get for all
k\in\mathbb{A}_{K}
:

\displaystyle\alpha\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|U^{T}_{k}\nabla F(x_{k})\|=\|U^{T}_{k}(\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla\psi(x_{k}))\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|U^{T}_{k}(\nabla\psi(x_{k})-\nabla\psi(x_{k+1}))-H_{f,U_{k}}d_{k}\|.

Consider
u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the
j
th column of
U_{k}
and
u_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
the
i
th element in the
j
th column. From the last inequality, we get:

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}|u_{j}^{T}(\nabla\psi(x_{k})-\nabla\psi(x_{k+1}))-H_{f,U_{k}}d_{k}^{(j)}|^{2}

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ij}(\nabla_{i}\psi(x_{k})-\nabla_{i}\psi(x_{k+1}))-H_{f,U_{k}}e_{j}^{T}d_{k}\right|^{2},

with

the

such that:

\nabla_{i}\psi(x_{k+1})-\nabla_{i}\psi(x_{k})=\nabla_{i}^{2}\psi(z_{i})U_{k}d_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1:n.

Using the equality above in (
54
), we have:

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ij}\nabla_{i}^{2}\psi(z_{i})U_{k}d_{k}+H_{f,U_{k}}e_{j}^{T}d_{k}\right|^{2}.

(55)

Considering (

) and the Frobenius norm definition, inequality (

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\left(u_{j}^{T}\bar{\nabla}^{2}\Psi(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})U_{k}+H_{f,U_{k}}e_{j}^{T}\right)d_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|u_{j}^{T}\bar{\nabla}^{2}\Psi(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})U_{k}+H_{f,U_{k}}e_{j}^{T}\|^{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|U_{k}^{T}\bar{\nabla}^{2}\Psi(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})U_{k}+H_{f,U_{k}}I_{p\times p}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|d_{k}\|^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
:

Let
T_{i}
be the indicator function for the event:
U_{i}
is well-aligned
. Then,
|\mathbb{A}_{K}|=\sum_{i=0}^{K}T_{i}
. Since
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, denoting
p_{i}:=\mathbb{P}[T_{i}=1|x_{i}]
, then for any
t>0
and
i=0,1,\cdots,K
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t(T_{i}-p_{i})}|x_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{i}e^{-t(1-p_{i})}+(1-p_{i})e^{tp_{i}}=e^{tp_{i}}(1-p_{i}+p_{i}e^{-t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{tp_{i}}e^{\log(1-p_{i}+p_{i}e^{-t})}=e^{tp_{i}+\log(1-p_{i}+p_{i}e^{-t})}\leq e^{\frac{t^{2}p_{i}}{2}},

where the inequality follows from the relation:

\displaystyle py+\log(1-p+pe^{-y})\leq\frac{y^{2}p}{2}\quad\forall p\in[0,1],\;y\geq 0.

(56)

Indeed, let us denote
\zeta(x)=px+\log(1-p+pe^{-x})
and
\eta(x)=\dfrac{px^{2}}{2}
. We have
\zeta(0)=\eta(0)=0
and also the relation:

\zeta^{\prime}(x)=p-\dfrac{pe^{-x}}{1-p+pe^{-x}},\;\;\eta^{\prime}(x)=px.

Hence
\zeta^{\prime}(0)=\eta^{\prime}(0)=0
. Moreover,
\eta^{\prime\prime}(x)=p
and the relation:

\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)=\dfrac{(1-p)pe^{-x}}{(1-p+pe^{-x})^{2}}.

(57)

We further have:

\displaystyle 0\leq\left((1-p)-e^{-x}\right)^{2}\iff 0\leq(1-p)^{2}-2(1-p)e^{-x}+(e^{-x})^{2},

\displaystyle\iff 4(1-p)e^{-x}\leq(1-p)^{2}+2(1-p)e^{-x}+(e^{-x})^{2}=\left(1-p+e^{-x}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\iff 2\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}\leq 1-p+e^{-x},

\displaystyle\iff 2\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}-(1-p)e^{-x}\leq 1-p+pe^{-x}.

Using this in (
57
), we obtain:

\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq\dfrac{(1-p)pe^{-x}}{\left(2\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}-(1-p)e^{-x}\right)^{2}}.

Since
x\geq 0
and
0\leq p\leq 1
, we have
(1-p)e^{-x}\geq 0
and thus:

\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}\leq(1-p)e^{-x}\iff\dfrac{1}{(1-p)e^{-x}}\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}}.

Finally, we get:

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{p(1-p)e^{-x}}{\left(2\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}-\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{p(1-p)e^{-x}}{\left(\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-x}}\right)^{2}}=p=\eta^{\prime\prime}(x).

Since
\eta^{\prime\prime}(x)-\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
, it follows that the function
h(x)=\eta(x)-\zeta(x)
is convex in the set
\mathbb{B}=\{x:\;x\geq 0\}
. Moreover, since
h^{\prime}(0)=0
, then
0
is a minimizer of
h
over
\mathbb{B}
. Since
h(0)=0
, we have
0\leq h(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{B}
. Hence,
\zeta(x)\leq\eta(x)
, i.e., (
56
) holds.
Using basic properties of conditional expectations and the fact that
T_{i}
only depends on
x_{i}
and not on the previous iterations (see Assumptions
3
), we then get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=0}^{K}(T_{i}-p_{i})}\right]

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}(T_{i}-p_{i})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t(T_{K}-p_{K})}|x_{K}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\frac{{t^{2}p_{K}}}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}(T_{i}-p_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{{t^{2}p_{K}}}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=0}^{K-2}(T_{i}-p_{i})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t(T_{K-1}-p_{K-1})}|x_{K-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\frac{{t^{2}p_{K}}}{2}}e^{\frac{{t^{2}p_{K-1}}}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=0}^{K-2}(T_{i}-p_{i})}\right]\leq e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}.

By Markov inequality and (
58
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[e^{-t\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}\geq e^{t\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-t\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-t\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}\right]

(59)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-t\lambda}\cdot e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}=e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)-t\lambda}.

On the other hand, one can note that:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}-\lambda

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle t\lambda\leq-t|\mathbb{A}_{K}|+t\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\iff e^{-t\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}\geq e^{t\lambda}.

Hence, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}-\lambda\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[e^{-t\left(|\mathbb{A}_{K}|-\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}\geq e^{t\lambda}\right].

(60)

Combining (
59
) and (
60
), we further get:

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}-\lambda\right]\leq e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)-t\lambda}.

Taking
\displaystyle t=\frac{\lambda}{\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}-\lambda\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}}.

Finally, taking
\displaystyle\lambda=\beta\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\leq(1-\beta)\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}{2}}.

and consequently

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\geq(1-\beta)\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}{2}}.

(61)

From Assumption
3
,
p_{i}\geq(1-\delta)
for all
i=0,1,\cdots,K
. Therefore, we get:

(1-\beta)\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\geq(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1).

Since the event
\{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\geq(1-\beta)\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\}
is contained in the event
\{|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\geq(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)\}
, from (
61
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\geq(1-\beta)(1-\delta)(K+1)\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathbb{A}_{K}|\geq(1-\beta)\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right]\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\right)}{2}}.

Since we also have
\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{K}p_{i}\geq(1-\delta)(K+1)
, we get our statement (
20
).

\square

Proof of Lemma
4.11
:

By Lemma
4.2
, we have
F(x_{k})\leq F(x_{0})
for all
k\geq 0
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{f}(x_{0})
is assumed bounded, then the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is also bounded. This implies that the set
\mathcal{X}(x_{0})
is also bounded. Closeness of
\mathcal{X}(x_{0})
also follows observing that
\mathcal{X}(x_{0})
can be viewed as an intersection of closed sets, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}(x_{0})=\cap_{j\geq 0}\cup_{\ell\geq j}\{x_{k_{\ell}}\}
. Hence
\mathcal{X}(x_{0})
is a compact set. Further, using the boundness of
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and the continuity of
F
and
\nabla F
, we have that the sequences
\left(F(x_{k})\right)_{k\geq 0}
and
\left(\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\right)_{k\geq 0}
are also bounded. From Lemma
4.2
, we have:

\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|d_{k}\|^{2}<\infty\quad\text{a.s.},\;\;\text{hence}\;\;\|d_{k}\|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Hence, by Lemma
4.4
we have:

\|\nabla F(x_{k_{j}})\|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

(62)

Moreover from Lemma
4.2
we have that
F(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is monotonically decreasing and since
F
is assumed bounded from below by
F^{*}>-\infty
, it converges, let us say to
F_{*}>-\infty
, i.e.
F(x_{k})\to F_{*}
a.s., and
F_{*}\geq F^{*}
.
On the other hand, let
\bar{x}
be a limit point of
(x_{k_{j}})_{j\geq 0}
, i.e.
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}(x_{0})
. This means that there is a subsequence
(x_{\bar{k}})_{\bar{k}\geq 0}
of
(x_{k_{j}})_{j\geq 0}

such that
x_{\bar{k}}\to\bar{x}
a.s. Since
f
and
\psi
are continuous, it follows that
F(x_{\bar{k}})\overset{a.s.}{\to}F(\bar{x})
. This implies that
F(\bar{x})=F_{*}
. Finally, to prove that
\nabla F(\mathcal{X}(x_{0}))=0
, one can notice that since
x_{\bar{k}}\to\bar{x}
, then
\nabla F(x_{\bar{k}})\to\nabla F(\bar{x})
a.s. Using basic probabilistic arguments, by (
62
), we get
\|\nabla F(\bar{x})\|=0
a.s.

\square

Proof of Lemma
4.13
:
For the recurrence
\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\geq\Delta_{k+1}^{\zeta+1}
, with
\zeta\in(0,1]
, a sublinear bound was derived in
[
32
]
. We extend this result to more general
\zeta
and to a different recurrence in the form (
26
). Consider
c=1
and
\zeta>0
. Multiplying (
26
) by
\Delta_{k}^{-(\zeta+1)}
we get:

1\leq\left(\Delta_{k}^{-\zeta}-\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}^{1+\zeta}}\right)=\left(1-\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right)\Delta_{k}^{-\zeta}.

(63)

Let us show that the function
g(y)=y^{-\zeta}+\zeta y-(1+\zeta)
satisfies
g(y)\geq 0
for all
y\in(0,1]
and
\zeta>0
. Indeed, since:

g^{\prime}(y)=-\zeta y^{-(\zeta+1)}+\zeta\leq 0\quad\forall y\in(0,1],

it follows that
g(y)
is decreasing on
(0,1]
. Moreover,
g(1)=0
and also

\lim_{y\searrow 0}g(y)=\lim_{y\searrow 0}\left(\dfrac{1}{y^{\zeta}}+\zeta y-(1-\zeta)\right)=+\infty.

Therefore,
g(y)\geq 0
for all
y\in(0,1]
and
\zeta>0
. Since
\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\in(0,1]
, we get:

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right)^{-\zeta}+\zeta\left(\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right)-1-\zeta\geq 0,

or equivalently

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\zeta}\left[\left(\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right)^{-\zeta}-1\right]\geq\left(1-\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right).

Combining the last inequality with (
63
), we obtain:

1\leq\dfrac{\Delta_{k}^{-\zeta}}{\zeta}\left[\left(\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}\right)^{-\zeta}-1\right]=\dfrac{\Delta_{k+1}^{-\zeta}-\Delta_{k}^{-\zeta}}{\zeta}.

Summing this relation, we get:

k\zeta\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\Delta_{i+1}^{-\zeta}-\Delta_{i}^{-\zeta}\right)=\Delta_{k}^{-\zeta}-\Delta_{0}^{-\zeta},

and by arranging the terms we finally obtain:

\Delta_{k}^{\zeta}\leq\dfrac{\Delta_{0}^{\zeta}}{\zeta k\Delta_{0}^{\zeta}+1},

thus proving (
27
). If
\zeta=0
and
c\in(0,1)
, we get:

\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}\leq\left(1-c\right)\Delta_{k},

thus proving (
28
). Finally, for
c>0
and
-1<\zeta<0
, we have
\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}
and consequently (
26
) leads to the following recurrence:

\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\geq c\Delta_{k+1}^{\zeta+1}\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Rearranging the terms we get:

\Delta_{k+1}\left(1+c\Delta_{k+1}^{\zeta}\right)\leq\Delta_{k}\iff\Delta_{k+1}\leq\left(\dfrac{1}{1+c\Delta_{k+1}^{\zeta}}\right)\Delta_{k},

which yields (
29
). Note that
\Delta_{k+1}^{\zeta}\to\infty
, provided that
\zeta<0
. These prove our statements.
\square
