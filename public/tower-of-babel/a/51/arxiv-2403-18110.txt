RANDOMISATION IN THE JOSEPHUS PROBLEM
faustin adiceam, steven robertson, victor shirandami & ioannis tsokanos

Abstract
The Josephus problem is a well–studied elimination problem consisting in determining the position of the survivor after repeated applications of a deterministic rule removing one person at a time from a given group. A natural probabilistic variant of this process is introduced in this paper. More precisely, in this variant, the survivor is determined after performing a succession of Bernoulli trials with parameter p p designating at each time which person is removed. When the number of participants tends to infinity, the main result characterises the limit distribution of the position of the survivor with an increasing degree of precision as the parameter approaches the unbiased case p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 . Then, the convergence rate to the position of the survivor is obtained in the form of a Central Limit Theorem. A number of other variants of the suggested probabilistic elimination process are also considered. They each admit a specific limit behavior which, in most cases, is stated in the form of an open problem.

The Josephus problem originates from Flavius Josephus’ (c. AD 37 – c. 100) recollection of the siege of Yodfat (AD 67) in his

For it would be unfair if, after the others had killed themselves, there were someone who could change his feelings and would want to survive

Rule for the Probabilistic Elimination Process.

Let there be
N
participants enumerated from
0
to
N-1
, standing on a unit circle with a regular spacing between them and labelled counterclockwise. The
0^{\textrm{th}}
participant holds first the knife : with probability
p

1-p
Participant
N-1
(standing on his left) before passing the knife onto the next participant still alive in the direction of the stabbing (namely, 2 or N-2, respectively). The next participant holding the knife then stabs on the same direction as in the previous step with probability
p
and, with probability
1-p
, in the opposite direction.

The case
p=1
recovers the classical deterministic process whereas, when
0<p<1
, the above rule can be seen as an elimination process where the survivor is determined by a succession of Bernoulli trials with parameter
p
. To put it differently, the survivor is then determined after performing a succession coin–tossings biaised according to the value of
p
.

The problem is now to determine the limit distribution of the survivor as the number
N
of participants tends to infinity. To this end, define
g_{N}(n,p)
as the probability of survival of the person labelled
n\in\left\llbracket 0,N-1\right\rrbracket
in a round with
N
participants when the elimination process follows Bernoulli trials with parameter
p\in(0,1)
. Let then
X_{N}^{(p)}
denote a random variable on the space
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
(identified throughout with the unit circle) standing for the normalized position of the survivor; that is, the probability that
X_{N}^{(p)}=n/N
is given by
g_{N}(n,p)
.

.

Assume that
p\in(0,1)

\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}

1.

X_{N}^{(p)}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\underset{N\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}X^{(p)}
is verified, where
X^{(p)}
is a Bernoulli random variable taking values in the set
\{0,1/2\}
;

2.

in the case that

lies in the middle interval
(1/3,2/3)
, the convergence in probability

Recurrence Formulae for the Probabilities of Survival

To facilitate the expression of the probabilities of survival, the domain of the map
n\in\left\llbracket 0,N-1\right\rrbracket\;\mapsto\;g_{N}(n,p)
is extended to
\mathbb{Z}
by evaluating its argument modulo
N\geq 3
. Thus, for instance,
g_{N}(-1,p)=g_{N}(N-1,p)
. In what follows, it will always be understood that the integer
n
is taken modulo
N
before applying the formulae.

Proposition 2.1

(Recursion relations for the probabilities of survival.)
.

Let
N\geq 3
and
p\in(0,1)
. Whenever
N\geq 4
, the probability vector
\left(g_{N}(n,p)\right)_{0\leq n\leq N-1}
meets the recurrence relation

g_{N}(n,p)=\begin{cases}g_{N-1}(-1,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{N}\\
(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv 1\pmod{N}\\
p\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv-1\pmod{N}\\
p\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

with base case
\left(g_{3}(0,p),g_{3}(1,p),g_{3}(2,p)\right)=(0,1-p,p)
.

Proof.

The base case
N=3
is easily verified by hand. Assume therefore that
N\geq 4
. The recurrence relation is obtained from the analysis of the consequences of the first step of the elimination process (carried out by the
0^{\textrm{th}}
Participant) on the disposition of the remaining participants. To this end, consider the distinction of cases induced by the statement :

\bullet

Probability of survival
g_{N}(0,p)
of participant
n\equiv 0\pmod{N}
.
After the first stabbing, with probability
p
, participant 1 is out and participant 2 holds the knife. Consequently, the
0^{th}
person who initiated the process with
N
participants becomes participant
-1\pmod{N-1}
in a new round with
N-1
participants : his probability of survival is then
g_{N-1}(-1,p)
. Similarly, with probability
1-p
, participant
-1\pmod{N}
is out and participant
-2\pmod{N}
holds the knife. Since the direction of stabbing is reversed compared with the previous situation, the
0^{th}
person who initiated the process with
N
participants still becomes participant
-1\pmod{N-1}
in a new round with
N-1
participants : his probability of survival is thus again
g_{N-1}(-1,p)
. Putting these two situations together, one obtains that

g_{N}(0,p)\;=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(-1,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(-1,p)\;=\;g_{N-1}(-1,p).

\bullet

Probability of survival
g_{N}(1,p)
of participant
n\equiv 1\pmod{N}
.
After the first step of the elimination process, with probability
p
, participant 1 is out : his survival probability vanishes. With probability
1-p
, participant
-1\pmod{N}
is out and, since the direction of stabbing is reversed compared with the previous situation, participant 1 now carries label
-2\pmod{N-1}
in the new round with
N-1
participants. This yields that

g_{N}(1,p)\;=\;p\cdot 0+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)\;=\;(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p).

\bullet

Probability of survival
g_{N}(-1,p)
of participant
n\equiv-1\pmod{N}
.
This case is
mutatis mutandis
analogous to the previous one in such a way that

g_{N}(-1,p)\;=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot 0\;=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p).

\bullet

Probability of survival
g_{N}(n,p)
of participant
n\not\equiv 0,\pm 1\pmod{N}
.
In this case, after the first elimination, with probability
p
, Participant
n
carries the label
n-2
and his survival probability in the new round with
N-1
participants becomes
g_{N-1}(n-2,p)
. Similarly, with probability
1-p
, taking into account the fact that the direction of stabbing is reverse compared with the previous situation, Participant
n
carries the label
-n-1\equiv N-n-2\pmod{N-1}
in a new round with
N-1
participants in such a way that his probability of survival becomes
g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)
. Putting these two situations together, one obtains that

g_{N}(n,p)\;=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p).

This completes the proof of the proposition.

Skewness of the Elimination Process and Rate of Convergence to the Position of the Survivor

Throughout this section, unless specified otherwise, the parameter
p\in(0,1)
and the integer
N\geq 3
are fixed. The goal is to establish successively each of the three points in Theorem
1.1
, the notations of which statements are adopted throughout.

To this end, it is more convenient to work with the closed unit interval
[0,1]
rather than the space
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. The set of continuous functions
\varphi
over
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is then identified with the set of continuous functions over
[0,1]
, denoted by
C^{0}([0,1])
, with periodic boundary conditions. Given such a function
\varphi

as

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;:=\;\mathbb{E}^{(p)}\!\left[\varphi\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)\right]

in such a way that

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(n,p).

(2)

Clearly,

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|\;\leq\;\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\infty},

(3)

where
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\infty}
denotes the sup–norm of a given bounded map
\varphi
over its domain of definition.

3.1
Limiting Behavior of the Random Process when
p\in\left(0,1\right)

The first point in Theorem
1.1
amounts to claiming that the distributional limit
X^{(p)}
of the sequence
\left(X^{(p)}_{N}\right)_{N\geq 3}
exists and takes value in the set
\left\{0,1/2\right\}
. This is established in this section as a consequence of a succession of lemmata, each relying on the preceeding ones. The following quantity plays an important

\eta_{N}(p)\;=\;\max_{-2\leq n\leq 2}g_{N}(n,p).

(4)

Lemma 3.1

(Approximate recursion relation for the expectation under regularity assumptions)
.

Let
N\geq 4
be an integer and let
\varphi
be a twice continuously differentiable function over
[0,1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}g_{N-1}(n,p)\cdot\left[p\cdot\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)+\left(1-p\right)\cdot\varphi\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{n}{N(N-1)}\right)\cdot\left(p\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)-(1-p)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)+O\left(\eta_{N}(p)\cdot\max\left\{\left|\varphi(0)\right|,\left|\varphi(1)\right|,\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|}{N},\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(1)\right|}{N}\right\}\right).

The subscript in the notation
O_{\varphi}(\,\cdot\,)
means that the implicit constant in the big-O notation depends on
\varphi
(whereas it is absolute otherwise).

Proof.

Applying a Taylor expansion yields that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;

\displaystyle=\;\varphi(0)\cdot g_{N}(0,p)+\varphi\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(1,p)+\varphi\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(-1,p)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(n,p)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{defeta}}{=}\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(n,p)+O\left(\eta_{N}(p)\cdot\max\left\{\left|\varphi(0)\right|,\left|\varphi(1)\right|,\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|}{N},\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(1)\right|}{N}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

From the recursion relations established in Proposition
2.1
, this last sum can be expanded as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N}(n,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}\left(n-2,p\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p)\cdot\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-4}\varphi\left(\frac{n+2}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}\left(n,p\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p)\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-4}\varphi\left(\frac{N-n-2}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}(n,p)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{defeta}}{=}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\left(p\cdot\varphi\left(\frac{n+2}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}\left(n,p\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+(1-p)\cdot\varphi\left(1-\frac{n+2}{N}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}(n,p)\right)+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+O\left(\eta_{N}(p)\cdot\max\left\{\left|\varphi(0)\right|,\left|\varphi(1)\right|,\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|}{N},\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(1)\right|}{N}\right\}\right).

From the decomposition

\frac{n+2}{N}\;=\;\frac{n}{N-1}+\left(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{n}{N(N-1)}\right),

another application of a Taylor expansion yields the relations

\varphi\left(\frac{n+2}{N}\right)\;=\;\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)+\left(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{n}{N(N-1)}\right)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)

and

\varphi\left(1-\frac{n+2}{N}\right)\;=\;\varphi\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)-\left(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{n}{N(N-1)}\right)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

Collecting together all the components of the calculation then gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}g_{N-1}(n,p)\cdot\left[p\cdot\varphi\left(\frac{n}{N-1}+(1-p)\cdot\varphi\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\left(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{n}{N(N-1)}\right)\cdot\left(p\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)-(1-p)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)+O\left(\eta_{N}(p)\cdot\max\left\{\left|\varphi(0)\right|,\left|\varphi(1)\right|,\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|}{N},\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(1)\right|}{N}\right\}\right),

which concludes the proof.

Lemma 3.2

(Decay of the expectation under assumptions of periodicity, regularity and oddness)
.

Let
N\geq 4
be an integer and let
\varphi
be a twice continuously differentiable function over

. Assume that
\varphi
is odd about 1/2 and that it meets the boundary condition
\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)
. Then, there exists a constant

\mathcal{E}^{(p)}_{N}[\varphi]\;\leq\;\frac{C(\varphi,p)}{N}\cdotp

Proof.

Under the assumptions of the statement (which imply in particular that

), Lemma

guarantees the existence of a constant

such that for all integers

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|\;\leq\;\left|2p-1\right|\cdot\left|\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|+\frac{C^{\prime}(\varphi,p)}{N}\cdotp

(5)

By induction, this implies that for all

,

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|\;\leq\;\left|2p-1\right|^{k}\cdot\left|\mathcal{E}_{N-k}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|+C^{\prime}(\varphi,p)\cdot\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\left|2p-1\right|^{l}}{N-l}\cdotp

, the series

converges in such a way that for all

. Then,

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|\;\leq\;\left|2p-1\right|^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\cdot\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\infty}+\frac{C^{\prime}(\varphi,p)}{\left(N/2+1\right)\cdot\left(1-|2p-1|\right)},

where the right–hand side decays as a

Lemma 3.3

3.1
yields that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}[\varphi]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[2\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]+O_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+O\left(\eta_{N}(p)\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right|}{N},\frac{\left|\varphi^{\prime}(1)\right|}{N}\right\}\right),

(7)

where the last term vanishes by assumption. Furthermore, from Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[2\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]+\frac{3}{2N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\varphi^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]+O_{\varphi,p}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

This is enough to complete the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.4

(Convergence of the expectation under assumptions of periodicity of the derivative, regularity, monotonicity and evenness)
.

Keep the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
and assume furthermore that
\varphi
is monotonic increasing on
[0,1/2]
and monotonic decreasing on
[1/2,1]
. Then, the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.3
, given any integers
L>M>3
,

\sum_{N=M+1}^{L}\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdotp\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{L}{(p)}[\varphi]\;-\;\mathcal{E}_{M}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;+\;O_{\varphi,p}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right),

(8)

which implies in particular that the series

\sum_{N\geq 4}\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdotp\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]

is bounded. Since by assumption,

\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdotp\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right)(x)\;=\;\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\cdot\varphi^{\prime}(x)\geq 0

for any
x\in[0,1]
, its general term is positive. As a consequence, it converges, hence is Cauchy. Fix then
\varepsilon>0
and an integer
N(\varepsilon)
such that for all
L>M\geq N(\varepsilon)
,

0\;\leq\;\sum_{N=M+1}^{L}\frac{1}{N}\cdotp\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdotp\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]\;<\;\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdotp

In view of relation (
8
), even if it means increasing the value of
N(\varepsilon)
to absorb the error term, this implies that for all
L>M\geq N(\varepsilon)
,

\left|\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(p)}[\varphi]-\mathcal{E}_{M}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right|\;<\;\varepsilon.

The sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
, being Cauchy, is thus convergent.
∎

The same conclusion as in the previous corollary can be obtained without imposing boundary periodic conditions on the derivative :

Corollary 3.5

(Convergence of the expectation under assumptions of regularity, monotonicity and evenness)
.

Let
\varphi
be a three times continuously differentiable function which is even about
1/2
. Assume that
\varphi
is monotonic increasing on
[0,1/2]
and monotonic decreasing on
[1/2,1]
. Then, the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges.

Proof.

In view of the hypothesis of evenness, assume without loss of generality that
\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)=0
. By uniform continuity, there exists a function
\epsilon\penalty\ :\eta>0\mapsto\epsilon(\eta)>0
tending to zero at the origin such that for any
x,y\in[0,1]
, it holds that
\left|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right|<\epsilon(\eta)
whenever
\left|x-y\right|<\eta
.

Let then
\left(\zeta_{k}\right)_{k\geq 3}
be any sequence of three times continuously differentible maps over
[0,1]
such that for all
k\geq 3
,

\bullet

\zeta_{k}(0)=\zeta_{k}(1)=0
and
\zeta^{\prime}_{k}(0)=\zeta^{\prime}_{k}(1)=0
;

\bullet

\zeta_{k}
is monotonic increasing on
[0,1/2]
and monotonic decreasing on
[1/2,1]
;

\bullet

\zeta_{k}
is even about
1/2
and
\zeta_{k}(x)=1
for any
x\in\left[1/k,1-1/k\right]
.

From Corollary
3.4
, the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\zeta_{k}\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
is convergent, say to a real
\rho_{k}
, which is bounded in absolute value by
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\infty}
(since so is each term
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\zeta_{k}\varphi]
).

By the construction of the map
\zeta_{k}
for any given
k\geq 3
and by the definition of the expectation
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}
(see (
2
)), it then follows that

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]-\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\zeta_{k}\varphi]\right|\;\leq\;\left\|\varphi-\zeta_{k}\varphi\right\|_{\infty}\;<\;\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{k}\right),

thence the inequalities

\rho_{k}-\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\;\leq\;\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;\leq\;\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;\leq\;\rho_{k}+\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

In particular,

0\;\leq\;\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]-\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;\leq\;2\cdot\epsilon\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

Upon letting
k
tend to infinity, this shows that the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges and thus completes the proof.
∎

The convergence of the expectation (
2
) evaluated at odd (Lemma
3.2
) and even (Corollary
3.5
) functions about
1/2
under regularity and monoticity assumptions can be generalised to the case of any continuous function by a density argument. This is the content of the (proof of the) next statement.

Lemma 3.6

(Convergence in law of the random variables.)
.

In the notations of Theorem
1.1
, the sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges in law to a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for any function
\varphi\in C^{0}([0,1])
such that
\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)
(recall that
\varphi
is then identified with a continuous function on the unit circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
), the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges. Indeed, under this assumption, since the unit circle is compact, the Prohorov Theorem (see
[
2
, Theorem 5.1]
) implies that the sequence (of the distributions) of the random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
is relatively compact : any subsequence contains a convergent one (in law). If the limits of all such subsequences are identically distributed, then the lemma holds. Otherwise, let
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
be partial limits with nonequal laws. From
[
2
, Theorem 1.2]
for instance, there exists a continuous function
f
over
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathbb{E}^{(p)}\!\left[f\left(\Xi_{1}\right)\right]\neq\mathbb{E}^{(p)}\!\left[f\left(\Xi_{2}\right)\right]
. This nevertheless contradicts the convergence of the sequence
\left(\mathbb{E}^{(p)}\!\left[f\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)\right]\right)_{N\geq 3}
.

To establish the lemma, it thus remains to prove that the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
indeed converges. To this end, define the auxiliary functions

\varphi_{O}\penalty\ :x\mapsto\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(1-x)}{2}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\varphi_{E}\penalty\ :x\mapsto\frac{\varphi(x)+\varphi(1-x)}{2}\cdotp

They respectively represent the odd and even parts of the function
\varphi
in the sense that they are respectively odd and even about
1/2
and that
\varphi
decomposes as
\varphi=\varphi_{O}+\varphi_{E}
. From Lemma
3.3
, it holds that

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{O}]+\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]+O_{\varphi,p}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

in such a way that it suffices to prove the convergence of the sequence of even parts
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]\right)_{N\geq 3}
.

To see this, given an integer
k\geq 0
, consider the polynomial map

\varphi_{k}\penalty\ :x\in[0,1]\;\mapsto\;\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)^{k}.

(9)

From the Weierstrass approximation theorem, given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a function
\psi_{\varepsilon}
lying in the real span of the family
\left\{\varphi_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
such that

\left\|\varphi-\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\;<\;\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdotp

By the definition of
\varphi_{E}
, this implies that

\left\|\varphi_{E}-\psi_{\varepsilon,E}\right\|_{\infty}\;<\;\varepsilon,

(10)

where

\psi_{\varepsilon,E}\penalty\ :x\;\mapsto\;\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)+\psi_{\varepsilon}(1-x)}{2}

is the even part (about
1/2
) of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and lies as such in the real span of the family of even functions (about
1/2
)
\left\{\varphi_{2k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
. Corollary
3.5
is then easily seen to imply the existence of the limit

\sigma(\varepsilon)\;=\;\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\psi_{\varepsilon,E}].

Since the set of limit values
\left\{\sigma(\varepsilon)\right\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
is clearly bounded, upon setting
\varepsilon=1/k
and even if it means considering a subsequence, it may be assumed that the sequence
\left(\sigma(1/k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges to a limit
\sigma\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]-\sigma\right|\;\leq\;\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]-\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\psi_{1/k,E}]\right|+\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\psi_{1/k,E}]-\sigma(1/k)\right|+\left|\sigma(1/k)-\sigma\right|.

Since, from inequality (
3
), the expectation
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}
has a norm bounded by 1, it follows from (
10
) that the first term on the right-hand can be made arbitrarily small for
k
large enough. So can the other two terms by the definition of a limit. The right–hand side thus becomes less than any arbitrarily fixed quantity provided that
k
is large enough : this shows that the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{E}]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges to the real
\sigma
.
∎

In order to establish the first point in Theorem
1.1
, it remains to to show that the distributional limit
X^{(p)}
of the sequence
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
, the existence of which is guaranteed by the above Lemma
3.6
, takes values in the set
\left\{0,1/2\right\}
.

Completion of the proof of Claim (1) in Theorem
1.1
.

Upon setting

\varphi\penalty\ :x\in[0,1]\mapsto-\cos(2\pi x)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\psi\penalty\ :x\in[0,1]\mapsto 2\pi\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\cdot\sin(2\pi x),

Lemma
3.3
yields that for all integers
L>M\geq 3
,

\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(p)}[\varphi]-\mathcal{E}_{M}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;=\;\sum_{M<N<L}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}[\psi]\;+\;O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)\cdot

Since, from Lemma
3.6
, the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges, hence is Cauchy, the above identity is easily seen to imply that the sequence of partial sums of the series

\sum_{N\geq 3}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}[\psi]

is also Cauchy, hence converges. Given that, from Lemma
3.6
once again, the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\psi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
is also convergent, one thus deduces that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\psi]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\psi\left(X^{(p)}\right)\right]\;=\;0.

Since
\psi
in nonnegative, this implies that
\psi\left(X^{(p)}\right)=0
almost surely. As a consequence,
X^{(p)}
takes values in the null set of the map
\psi
, namely
\left\{0,1/2\right\}
. This concludes the proof of Claim (1) in Theorem
1.1
.
∎

3.2
Existence of the Limit Position in the Middle Range
p\in\left(1/3,2/3\right)

Throughout this section, the probability parameter
p
is assumed to lie in the open interval
\left(1/3,2/3\right)
. Under this assumption, the convergence in law of the sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
to a Bernoulli random variable
X^{(p)}
taking values in the set
\left\{0,1/2\right\}
can be made more precise : the random variable
X^{(p)}
takes the almost sure value
1/2
. This is the content of Point 2 in Theorem
1.1
which is established hereafter. The key new ingredient allowing one to gather more information on the limit distribution is the following result implying that an exponential decay of the probability of survival occurs near the starting position.

Proposition 3.7

(Exponential bounds for the probabilities of survival)
.

Assume that
p\in(1/3,2/3)
. Then, there exist constants
K>0
and
\beta,\gamma>1
, all depending only on
p
, such that for all integers
N\geq 3
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

g_{N}(n,p)\;\leq\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{\left\langle n\right\rangle_{N}}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdotp

Here,
\left\langle n\right\rangle_{N}=\textrm{dist}\left(n,N\mathbb{Z}\right)
.

Proof.

The claimed result is established by an induction making it possible to provide effective values for the various parameters.

First, choose
K>0
large enough so that the conclusion of the proposition holds for the finitely many probabilities
g_{N}(n,p)
determined by the finitely many values of
N\leq 7
and
n\pmod{N}
. Let then
N\geq 8
be such that this conclusion holds for all integers
L\in\left\llbracket 3,N-1\right\rrbracket
and
n\pmod{L}
. Consider the following distinction of cases relying on the recursion relations for the probabilities of survival stated in Proposition
2.1
:

\bullet

when
n\equiv 0\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(0,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;g_{N-1}(-1,p)\;=\;p\cdot g_{N-2}(-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}}{\gamma^{N-2}}\;\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
p(\gamma\beta)^{2}\leq 1
;

\bullet

when
n\equiv 1\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(1,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;(1-p)\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}\;=\;K\cdot\frac{\beta}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left((1-p)\gamma\beta\right)\;\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
(1-p)\gamma\beta\leq 1
;

\bullet

when
n\equiv-1\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(1,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;p\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}\;=\;K\cdot\frac{\beta}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\gamma\beta\right)\;\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
p\gamma\beta\leq 1
;

\bullet

when
n\in\left\llbracket 2,(N-3)/2\right\rrbracket\pmod{N}
(then,
\left\langle n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=n-2
and
\left\langle N-n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=n+1
),

\displaystyle g_{N}(n,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;p\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n-2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}+(1-p)\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n+1}}{\gamma^{N-1}}\;=\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}+(1-p)\cdot\gamma\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{\left\langle n\right\rangle_{N}}}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
\gamma\cdot(p/\beta^{2}+(1-p)\beta)\leq 1
, which can be realised under the assumption that
p>1/3
;

\bullet

when
n\in\left\llbracket(N+3)/2,N-2\right\rrbracket\pmod{N}
(then,
\left\langle n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=N-n+1
and
\left\langle N-n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=N-n-2
),

\displaystyle g_{N}(n,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;p\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n+1}}{\gamma^{N-1}}+(1-p)\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n-2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}

\displaystyle=\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\gamma\beta+(1-p)\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}\right)\;\leq\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{\left\langle n\right\rangle_{N}}}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
\gamma\cdot(p\beta+(1-p)/\beta^{2})\leq 1
, which can be realised under the assumption that
p<2/3
;

\bullet

when
n\in\left\llbracket(N-3)/2,(N+3)/2\right\rrbracket\pmod{N}
(then,
\left\langle n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=n-2
and
\left\langle N-n-2\right\rangle_{N-1}=N-n-2
),

\displaystyle g_{N}(n,p)\;

\displaystyle=\;p\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot g_{N-1}(N-n-2,p)

\displaystyle\leq\;p\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n-2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}+(1-p)\cdot K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n-2}}{\gamma^{N-1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}+(1-p)\cdot\beta^{N-2n}\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}\right)&\textrm{ if }(N-3)/2\leq n\leq N/2;\\
\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\cdot\beta^{2n-N}\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}+(1-p)\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}\right)&\textrm{ if }N/2\leq n\leq(N+3)/2;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}+(1-p)\cdot\beta\gamma\right)&\textrm{ if }(N-3)/2\leq n\leq N/2;\\
\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{N-n}}{\gamma^{N}}\cdot\left(p\cdot\beta\gamma+(1-p)\frac{\gamma}{\beta^{2}}\right)&\textrm{ if }N/2\leq n\leq(N+3)/2;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{\beta^{\left\langle n\right\rangle_{N}}}{\gamma^{N}},

where the last inequality is valid for any choice of
\beta,\gamma>1
such that
\gamma\cdot\max\left\{p\beta+(1-p)/\beta^{2},p/\beta^{2}+(1-p)\beta\right\}\leq 1
, which can be realised under the assumption that
1/3<p<2/3
.

Since, under the assumption that
1/3<p<2/3
, all four inequalities
p\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}\leq 1
,
(1-p)\beta\gamma\leq 1
,
\gamma\cdot(p\beta+(1-p)/\beta^{2})\leq 1
and
\gamma\cdot((1-p)\beta+p/\beta^{2})\leq 1
can be met simultaneously, the proof of the proposition is complete.
∎

The following two results are derived from the above statement. They should be seen as variants of Lemmata

(Decay of the expectation under assumptions of regularity, oddness and localisation of the parameter

.

Let

Lemma 3.9

(Telescopic asymptotic of the expectation under assumptions of regularity, evenness and localisation of the parameter

.

be an integer and let

Completion of the proof of Point 2 in Theorem
1.1
.

The goal is to show that the distributional limit
X^{(p)}
of the sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
takes the almost sure value
1/2
under the assumption that
p\in(1/3,2/3)
. To this end, let
\varphi
be a thrice continuously differentiable function even about
1/2
on
[0,1]
. Assume that
\varphi
is monotonic increasing on
[0,1/2]
and monotonic decreasing on
[1/2,1]
. From Lemma
3.9
, given any integers
L>M\geq 3
,

\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(p)}[\varphi]-\mathcal{E}_{M}^{(p)}[\varphi]\;=\;\sum_{M<N\leq L}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]+O_{\varphi,p}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right).

(11)

This implies that the partial sums of the series

are bounded. Since the conditions placed onto
\varphi
imply that the general term of this series is a nonnegative sequence, it converges, hence is Cauchy. From relation (
11
), this claim also holds for the sequence
\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi]\right)_{N\geq 3}
, which is therefore also convergent.

In the particular case that
\varphi=-\varphi_{2k}
for some integer

\sum_{N\geq 3}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}^{(p)}[\varphi_{2k}]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{2k}]\right)_{N\geq 3}.

As a conquence, for any

,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(p)}[\varphi_{2k}]\;=\;0.

(12)

Consider now the general case where
\varphi\in C^{0}([0,1])
meets the boundary condition

. As in the proof of Lemma

. As for the even part, as in the proof of Lemma

\varphi_{E}
in the real span of

3.3
A Central Limit Theorem in the unbiased Case
p=1/2

Assume throughout this section that
p=1/2
. For the sake of simplicity of notations, set then

\displaystyle\eta_{N}\;=\;

\displaystyle\eta_{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{N}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(1/2)},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{N}\left[\varphi\right]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{(1/2)}\left[\varphi\right]

\displaystyle X_{N}\;=\;X_{N}^{(1/2)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad g_{N}(n)\;=\;g_{N}\left(n,\frac{1}{2}\right).

In this setup, the probability for the participant in position
n
to survive in a round with
N
participants is the same as the probability for the participant in position
-n\pmod{N}
to survive in a round with
N
participants when the directions of elimination, which are chosen equiprobably, are switched. As a consequence, the symmetry property

g_{N}(n)=g_{N}(-n)

(13)

is met and the recurrence relations stated in Proposition
2.1
simplify to

g_{N}(n)=\begin{cases}g_{N-1}(-1)&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{N}\\
g_{N-1}(-2)/2&\text{ if }n\equiv\pm 1\pmod{N}\\
\left(g_{N-1}(n-2)+g_{N-1}(n+1)\right)/2&\text{ if }n\not\equiv\pm 1,0\pmod{N}.\end{cases}

(14)

The goal in this section is to establish, in three steps, the Central Limit Theorem stated in Point 3 of Theorem
1.1
.

3.3.1
Decay Rate of the Probabilities of Survival.

The key result allowing one to obtain the convergence rate to the constant
1/2
in the form of a Central Limit Theorem is the following one, which shows that participants away from the mid-position in the circle have a probability of survival decreasing exponentially fast. This statement refines that of Proposition
2.1
(which was concerned with the case where
1/3<p<2/3
) and heavily relies on various symmetries met by the elimination process in the unbiased case
p=1/2
.

Proposition 3.10

(Exponential decay of the probability of survival away from the mid-point position in the unbiased case)
.

There exist a constant
K>0
such that given any sufficiently small
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any real
\alpha\in(1,1+\varepsilon]
, it holds that for all integers
N\geq 1
and
n\in\left\llbracket 0,N-1\right\rrbracket
,

g_{N}(n)\;\leq\;K\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)n-N}.

(15)

Proof.

Fix first
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Elementary calculations then show that the inequality in the variable
\alpha\geq 0

\max\left\{\alpha^{2+4(1+\varepsilon)},\;\alpha^{1-4(1+\varepsilon)}+\alpha^{1+2(1+\varepsilon)}\right\}\;\leq\;2

(16)

is met as soon as

\alpha\;\leq\;\alpha(\varepsilon),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\alpha(\varepsilon)\;=\;\min\left\{2^{1/10},1+4^{2+\varepsilon}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\right\},

and in particular for any

1\;<\;\alpha\;\leq\;1+\varepsilon

provided that
\varepsilon
is small enough.

This observation enables one to develop a proof of the statement analogous to that of Proposition
2.1
. To see this, fix any constant
K\geq 1
such that the upper bound in (
15
) is verified for the finitely many values of the probabilities corresponding to the integers
N\leq 4
for the two limit values
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
, already when
\varepsilon=0
. Fix then
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that for any
\alpha\in(1,1+\varepsilon]
, inequality (
16
) is satisfied. Given an integer
N\geq 5
for which the bound (
15
) holds for all probabilities up to the range
N-1
, consider the following distinction of cases relying on the recursion relations (
14
). In view of the symmetry property (
13
), they imply inequality (
15
) for all admissible values of the integer
n\pmod{N}
:

\bullet

when
n\equiv 0\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(0)

\displaystyle\;=\;g_{N-1}(-1)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-2}(-2)\;\underset{\eqref{symprounbia}}{=}\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-2}(2)

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 2-(N-2)}\;=\;K\cdot\alpha^{-N}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{2+4(1+\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{ineqcalcubas}}{\leq}\;K\cdot\alpha^{-N}.

\bullet

when
n\equiv 1\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(1)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-1}(-2)\;\underset{\eqref{symprounbia}}{=}\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-1}(2)

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 2-(N-1)}\;=\;K\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)-N}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{1+2(1+\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{ineqcalcubas}}{\leq}\;K\cdot\alpha^{-N}.

\bullet

when
n\in\left\llbracket 2,N/2\right\rrbracket\pmod{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{N}(n)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-1}(n-2)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{N-1}(n+1)

\displaystyle\leq\;K\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)\cdot(n-2)-(N-1)}+K\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)\cdot(n+1)-(N-1)}

\displaystyle=\;K\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)n-N}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{1-4(1+\varepsilon)}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha^{1+2(1+\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{ineqcalcubas}}{\leq}\;K\cdot\alpha^{2(1+\varepsilon)n-N}.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.3.2
Some Moment Estimates.

The Central Limit Theorem relies on various moment estimates for the polynomial maps already introduced in the proof of Lemma
3.6
, namely, given an integer
k\geq 1
,

\varphi_{k}\;:\;x\in[0,1]\;\mapsto\;\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)^{k}.

Lemma 3.11

(Moment Estimates)
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Then, there exists a real constant
\theta_{k}>0
such that

\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{k}|]\;\leq\;\theta_{k}\cdot\left(\frac{\ln N}{N}\right)^{k/2}.

Proof.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
N\geq 1
, define

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(N)\;=\;\left\llbracket 0,N\right\rrbracket\backslash\left\llbracket\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\cdot N,\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\cdot N\right\rrbracket\quad\textrm{and}\quad\nu_{\varepsilon}(N)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(N)}g_{N}(n).

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{k}|]\;

\displaystyle=\;\left(\sum_{\underset{0\leq n\leq N-1}{n\not\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(N)}}+\sum_{\underset{0\leq n\leq N-1}{n\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(N)}}\right)\left|\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{N}\right|^{k}\cdot g_{N}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\;\varepsilon^{k}\cdot\left(1-\nu_{\varepsilon}(N)\right)+\nu_{\varepsilon}(N).

(17)

Furthemore, from Proposition
3.10
, there exists a constant
K>0
such that, provided that
\varepsilon>0
is chosen small enough,

\displaystyle\nu_{\varepsilon}(N)\;

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{symprounbia}}{=}\;2\cdot\left(\sum_{0\leq n\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)N}g_{N}(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;2\cdot K\cdot\left(\sum_{0\leq n\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)N}\left(1+\varepsilon\right)^{2(1+\varepsilon)n-N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;8\cdot K\cdot\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2(1+\varepsilon)(1/2-\varepsilon)N}}{\left((1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1\right)\cdot(1+\varepsilon)^{N}}

\displaystyle\leq\;8\cdot K\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon\cdot(1+\varepsilon)^{\varepsilon N}}

\displaystyle\leq\;8\cdot K\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon\cdot\exp\left(\varepsilon^{2}N/2\right)},

where the last inequality follows from an easily verified convexity inequality. The last quantity is minimised when

\varepsilon\;=\;\varepsilon_{k}\;=\;\sqrt{(k+1)\cdot\frac{\ln N}{N}},

(18)

in which case one infers the existence of a real
\theta_{k}>0
such that

\nu_{\varepsilon_{k}}(N)\;\leq\;\theta_{k}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k/2}\cdot\sqrt{\ln N}}\cdot

(19)

The right–hand side of inequality (
17
) specialised to the cases when relations (
18
) and (
19
) hold then yields the sought conclusion upon adjusting the value of the real
\theta_{k}
.
∎

Lemma
3.11
can be further refined when restricting to the first and the second moments :

Lemma 3.12

(Refined first and second moment estimates)
.

The sum of the second moments satisfies the estimate

\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]\;\asymp\;\ln L

for all
L
large enough. As for the first moments, they decay exponentially in the sense that there exists a parameter
A>1
and a constant
\theta>0
such that for all
N\geq 3
,

\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]\right|\;\leq\;\frac{\theta}{A^{N}}\cdotp

The proof of Lemma
3.12
relies on an auxiliary statement analogous to Lemma
3.1
. As the argument to establish the former auxiliary statement is very similar to one developed for the latter (upon calling, in this case, on the recursion relations (
14
)), it is left to the reader.

Lemma 3.13

(Telescopic decomposition of the expectation in the unbiased case)
.

Let
\varphi
be a three-times continuously differentiable function and let
N\geq 4
be an integer. Then,

\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi]-\mathcal{E}_{N-1}[\varphi]\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N-1}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{*}\right]+\frac{C_{N}}{N^{2}}+O_{\varphi}\left(\max\left\{\eta_{N},\frac{\left\|\varphi^{{}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}}{N^{3}}\right\}\right),

where the
∗
operator is here again defined by
\varphi^{*}\penalty\ :x\mapsto x\cdot\varphi^{\prime}(x)
and where

C_{N}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(2-\frac{n}{N-1}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(1+\frac{n}{N-1}\right)^{2}\right)\cdot\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{n}{N-1}\right)\cdot g_{N-1}(n).

Proof of Lemma
3.12
.

Applying Lemma
3.13
to the polynomial map
\varphi_{2}
, one obtains that

\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N-1}[\varphi_{2}]-\frac{2}{N}\mathcal{E}_{N-1}[\varphi_{2}]+\frac{C_{N}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\eta_{N}\right),

(20)

where, taking into account the definition of the quantity
C_{N}
and the fact that
\varphi_{2}^{\prime\prime}
is the constant function equal to 2,

C_{N}\;=\;\mathcal{E}_{N}[\sigma]\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\sigma(x)\;=\;\frac{(2-x)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1+x)^{2}}{2}\cdotp

From Point 2 in Theorem
1.1
, one thus infers that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}C_{N}\;=\;\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\;=\;\frac{9}{4}\cdotp

(21)

Also, by partial summation, given an integer
M\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{N=3}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]\;=

\displaystyle\;(M+1)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}[\varphi_{2}]-3\cdot\mathcal{E}_{3}[\varphi_{2}]-\sum_{N=4}^{M}N\cdot\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]-\mathcal{E}_{N-1}[\varphi_{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{telepolymom2}}{=}\;(M+1)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}[\varphi_{2}]-3\cdot\mathcal{E}_{3}[\varphi_{2}]+2\cdot\left(\sum_{N=4}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{N-1}[\varphi_{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{N=4}^{M}\left(\frac{C_{N}}{N}+O\left(N\cdot\eta_{N}\right)\right).

(22)

Since Proposition
3.10
guarantees the existence of constants
K>0
and
A>1
such that
\eta_{N}\leq K\cdot A^{-N}
for all
N\geq 1
, one deduces from the norm inequality (
3

\sum_{N=3}^{M-1}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]+(M-2)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}[\varphi_{2}]\;=\;\sum_{N=4}^{M}\frac{C_{N}}{N}+O(1)\;\underset{\eqref{limCN}}{\asymp}\;\ln M,

(23)

where the last relation holds for all
M\geq 4
large enough. Upon summing up this identity, it implies with the help of an elementary manipulation of equation (
22
) that for all
L\geq 4
large enough,

\displaystyle\left(L-2\right)\cdot\sum_{M=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{M}[\varphi_{2}]\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{M=3}^{L}\left(\sum_{N=3}^{M-1}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]+(M-2)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}[\varphi_{2}]\right)

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{M=3}^{L}\left(\sum_{N=4}^{M}\frac{C_{N}}{N}+O(1)\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\;L\cdot\ln L.

This is easily seen to imply the estimate for the second moment in the statement of the lemma.

As for the first moment, note that by the symmetry property (

), the random variables
X_{N}
and

(where

\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]\;=\;\frac{g_{N}(0)}{2}\cdotp

, there exist constants
\theta>0

3.3.3
Verifying the Lyapunov Condition.

In order to complete the proof of the Central Limit Theorem, consider a sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}\right)_{N\geq 3}
on the unit circle (identified with the interval
[0,1)
), each drawn according to the discrete law assigning the probability
g_{N}(n)
to the event
X_{N}=n/N
when
0\leq n\leq N-1
. Let then

\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{n}{N}\cdot g_{N}(n),\qquad\mathbb{V}_{N}(X_{N})\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{n}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right)^{2}\cdot g_{N}(n)

and

\mathbb{W}_{N}(X_{N})\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left|\frac{n}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right|^{3}\cdot g_{N}(n).

When
L\geq 3
is an integer, set furthermore

B_{L}\;=\;\sqrt{\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathbb{V}_{N}(X_{N})}.

A classical version of the Central Limit Theorem applicable to the case where random variables behave independently without being necessarily identically distributed is due to Lyapunov
[
3
, p.362]
. In the present case, it asserts that if the
X_{N}
’s are drawn independently from each other, then the convergence in law

\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\left(X_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right)\;\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\underset{L\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\;\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(24)

holds provided that the
Lyapunov condition

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{B_{L}^{2+\kappa}}\cdot\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left|X_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right|^{2+\kappa}\right]\right)\;=\;0

(25)

is met for some
\kappa>0
. The goal in this section is to show that this condition is indeed verified and then to derive Point 3 in Theorem
1.1
from (
24
).

Proof of Point 3 in Theorem
1.1
.

Note first that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\left(X_{N}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\left(X_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right)+\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\left(\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})-\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\left(X_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right)-\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\sum_{N=1}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}].

From this decomposition and from the classical Slutsky Theorem, the convergence in the statement of Point 3 in Theorem
1.1
follows upon establishing these two points :

(a)

the limit relation

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{1}{B_{L}}\cdot\left(\sum_{N=1}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]\right)\;=\;0

holds;

(b)

the Lyapunov condition (
25
) is verified when
\kappa=1
(so that the convergence in law (
24
) is valid).

To this end, note first that Lemma
3.12
implies that the series

\sum_{N\geq 3}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]

is absolutely convergent. This also holds for the series

\sum_{N\geq 3}\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left|X_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right|^{3}\right]\;=\;\sum_{N\geq 3}\mathbb{W}_{N}(X_{N}).

Indeed, this claim immediately follows from Lemma
3.11
upon noticing that

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{N}(X_{N})\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\left|\frac{n}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{2}-\mathbb{E}_{N}(X_{N})\right|\right)^{3}\cdot g_{N}(n)

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\left|\frac{n}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\right|+\left|\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]\right|\right)^{3}\cdot g_{N}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\;\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{3}|]+3\cdot\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{2}|]\right)^{2}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{1}|]+4\cdot\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}[|\varphi_{1}|]\right)^{3}.

To show that both Points (a) and (b) hold, it thus suffices that the sequence
\left(B_{L}\right)_{L\geq 1}
should tend to infinity. This can be established in the quantitative way required by the statement of Theorem
1.1
. To see this, note that when
L\geq 3
is an integer,

B_{L}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathbb{V}_{N}(X_{N})\;=\;\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]+3\cdot\left(\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]^{2}\right).

In this decomposition, from Lemma
3.12
,

\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{2}]\;\asymp\;\ln L\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\sup_{L\geq 3}\;\left(\sum_{N=3}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{N}[\varphi_{1}]^{2}\right)\;<\;\infty.

This concludes the proof of Point 3 in Theorem
1.1
.
∎

4
Variations on the Theme of Randomisation in the Josephus Problem

The main problem left open by Theorem
1.1
is to determine the parameter of the Bernoulli limit distribution when
p\in(0,1/3]\cup[2/3,1)
; namely, with the notations of Claim (1) in the statement, to determine the probability of the event
\left\{X^{(p)}=1/2\right\}
. Denoting this parameter by
c(p)
, numerical simulations displayed in the appendix indicate that
c(p)=1
; in other words, this is saying that the sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
converges in probability to the constant
1/2
regardless of the value of
p\in(0,1)
. Note that when
p\not\in(1/3,2/3)
, the proof of Proposition
3.7
, which plays a crucial
rôle
in establishing the second point in Theorem
1.1
, is not valid anymore. The difficulty to determine the limit in this range can be gauged from the Central Limit Theorem stated in Theorem
1.1
: even in the unbiased case
p=1/2
, the convergence in law towards the constant
1/2
is extremely slow (it is only of the order of the square root of a logarithm).

The following variant of the probabilistic elimination process provides more insight into the subtlety involved in the determination of the existence of a limit measure.

Alternative Rule for the Probabilistic Elimination Process.

Let there be
N
participants enumerated from
0
to
N-1
, standing on a unit circle with a regular spacing between them and labelled counterclockwise. The
0^{\textrm{th}}
participant holds first the knife : with probability
p
, he eliminates Participant 1 (standing on his right) and passes the knife onto the person to the right of the victim (namely, 2). Similarly, with probability
1-p
, he eliminates Participant
N-1
(standing on his left) and passes the knife onto the person to the left of the victim (namely, N-2). The next participant holding the knife then stabs the person still alive on his right-hand side with probability
p
and the person still alive on his left–hand side with probability
1-p
, then passing the knife to the right and to the left, respectively.

Compare with the first probabilistic elimination rule stated in the introduction : the difference is that at each step, the outcome of the probabilistic choice is not about changing the
direction
of the stabbing anymore but about choosing which of the persons standing on the left or on the right of the knife holder must be eliminated.

Denoting by
f_{N}(n,p)
the probability that the
n^{th}
participant should be the survivor in a process involving
N
participants and evaluating the first argument
n
modulo
N
, the recursion relation satisfied by the probability vector
\left(f_{N}(n,p)\right)_{0\leq n\leq N-1}
can be shown to read

f_{N}(n,p)=\begin{cases}p\cdot f_{N-1}(-1,p)+(1-p)\cdot f_{N}(1,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{N}\\
(1-p)\cdot f_{N-1}(2,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv 1\pmod{N}\\
p\cdot f_{N-1}(-2,p)&\text{ if }n\equiv-1\pmod{N}\\
p\cdot f_{N-1}(n-2,p)+(1-p)\cdot f_{N-1}(n+1,p)&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

The arguments developed in the previous sections enable one to show that the resulting sequence of random variables
\left(X_{N}^{(p)}\right)_{N\geq 3}
(the notation with the previous rule is left unchanged for the sake of simplicity) converges in this case in probability to the constant
3p-1
when
1/3<p<2/3
. Because the alternative rule of elimination under consideration coincides with the original one presented in the introduction in the unbiased case
p=1/2
, one also obtain a Central Limit Theorem in this situation. However, one does not obtain any conclusive statement about the limiting behavior of the sequence in the range
p\in(0,1/3]\cup[2/3,1)
. In fact, numerical simulations displayed in the appendix suggest that, in this range, the sequence does not admit any distributional limit anymore (in contrast with the first point in Theorem
1.1
).

This second version of the elimination process can be seen as a particular case of an even more general probabilistic process involving two parameters
p,q\in[0,1]
:

General Rule for the Probabilistic Elimination Process.

Let there be
N
participants enumerated from
0
to
N-1
, standing on a unit circle with a regular spacing between them and labelled counterclockwise. Starting from the
0^{\textrm{th}}
participant, each one eliminates the participant on his right with probability p and the participant on his left with probability
1-p
; the knife is then passed onto the person on the right of the knife-holder with probability
q
and onto the person onto the left of the knife-holder with probability
1-q
.

Determining the distributional limit of the corresponding sequence of random variables as a function of the two parameters
p,q
is an open problem. Some numerical simulations are provided in the appendix.

and
