Euler scheme for SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motions: integrability and convergence in law
Jorge A. León, Yanghui Liu ∗, Samy Tindel

Abstract
. We prove that the modified Euler scheme for stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motions (fBm) with Hurst parameter H > 1 / 3 H>1/3 , together with its Malliavin derivatives, are integrable uniformly with respect to the step size n n . Then we use the integrability results to derive the convergence rate in law n 1 − 4 ​ H + ε n^{1-4H+\varepsilon} for the Euler scheme. The proof for integrability is based on a nontrivial generalization of the greedy sequence argument in [ 8 ] to a quadratic functional of the fBm. The proof of weak convergence applies Malliavin calculus and some upper-bound estimates for weighted random sums.



1.
Introduction

This note is concerned with the following stochastic differential equation driven by a
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion (fBm in the sequel)
x
with Hurst parameter
\frac{1}{3}<H<\frac{1}{2}
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle dy_{t}=V_{0}(y_{t})dt+V(y_{t})dx_{t}\,,\qquad y_{0}=a,

where we assume that
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the collection of vector field
V_{0}=(V_{0}^{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
belongs to
C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
{V}=(V^{i}_{j})_{1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq d}
sits in
C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m},{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
. Under this setting the theory of rough paths gives a framework allowing to get existence and uniqueness results for equation
(
1.1
)
, and the unique solution
y
in the rough paths sense has
{\gamma}
-Hölder continuity for all
{\gamma}<H
; see e.g.
[
13
,
16
]
.

One of the basic questions about systems like
(
1.1
)
concerns the existence of a proper numerical scheme approximating the solution
y
. In case of a Hurst parameter
H\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2})
, the simplest possible solution to this problem is to use a Milstein type scheme. However Milstein type schemes involve second order expansions and iterated integrals of the fBm
x
, which should be morally thought of as objects of the form

x^{2}_{st}=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u}dx_{v}\otimes dx_{u},

and are notoriously uneasy to simulate. Therefore several contributions aimed in the recent past at avoiding iterated integrals while still producing convergent numerical schemes for rough differential equations. The first article tackling this issue is
[
12
]
, where the iterated integrals in
x^{2}
were replaced by products of increments of
x
. The rate of convergence obtained in
[
12
]
was then pushed to its optimal limit in
[
14
]
. Let us also mention the article
[
32
]
, which thoroughly explores Runge-Kutta methods based on the same idea of replacing iterated integrals by products of increments.

In this paper we will focus our attention on another numerical approximation, called first-order scheme in the sequel. The main idea behind this method is to simply replace the second order terms
x^{2}
by their expected values. This yields simpler schemes than the aforementioned methods based on product of increments, and at the same time produces optimal convergence rates. Specifically, if
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H
and one uses an approximating grid with mesh of order
1/n
, then the rate of convergence is of order
1/n^{2H-1/2}
. This method has first been introduced in
[
22
]
for a Hurst parameter
H>1/2
, and has been extended to the rough path case in
[
28
]
. We also refer to
[
23
,
24
]
for further extensions.

In order to describe our first-order numerical scheme, let us introduce some basic settings.
For simplicity, we are considering a finite time interval
[0,T]
and we take the uniform partition
\pi:0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
on
[0,T]
. Specifically, for
k=0,\ldots,n
we have
t_{k}=k\Delta
, where we denote
\Delta=\frac{T}{n}
. In the sequel, the quantity
\delta x_{st}
will stand for the vector
x_{t}-x_{s}
. Our generic approximation is called
y^{n}
, and it starts from the initial condition
y^{n}_{0}=y_{0}=a
.
With this notation in hand, we can now define our scheme recursively as follows (here and below we set
\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=x_{t_{k+1}}-x_{t_{k}}
):

(1.2)

y^{n}_{t_{k+1}}=y^{n}_{t_{k}}+V_{0}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\Delta+V(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial V_{j}V_{j}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\,\Delta^{2H},

where the notation
\partial V_{i}V_{j}
stands for a vector field of the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial V_{i}V_{j}=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{m}\partial_{l}V_{i}^{k}V_{j}^{l};\,k=1,\dots,m\,,\right)

and
\partial_{l}
stands for the partial derivative in the
y_{l}
direction:
\partial_{l}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{y_{i}}}
.
As mentioned above, the rate of convergence of
y^{n}
to
y
is of order
1/n^{2H-1/2}
. One of the key results in
[
28
]
is a functional central limit theorem of the form

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{2H-1/2}\left(y^{n}-y\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}U,

where
U
is solution to a rough differential equation driven by
x
plus an additional Brownian term.

In the current contribution, we are mostly interested in the convergence in distribution of the approximation
y^{n}
defined by (
1.2
). This endeavor is motivated by three main reasons which can be summarized as follows:

(i)

The weak convergence of a numerical scheme is directly related to the performance of simulation for stochastic models, which is a center issue in mathematical finance and engineering.

(ii)

For diffusions processes, that is stochastic differential equations driven by a Brownian motion, the convergence in distribution for numerical schemes is a classical problem. This is assessed e.g by the remarkable publications
[
2
,
3
]
. As mentioned in those two references, a good knowledge about the weak convergence is useful in order to evaluate probabilities that
y
reaches a certain level, or to get some information about the moments of
y
.

(iii)

In
[
4
,
7
,
18
]
we have started a long term program aiming at understanding the law of Gaussian rough differential systems. The current result might play an important role in this approach.

Let us now describe the main result contained in this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
V\in C^{4}_{b}
and
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
. Let
y
be the solution of the rough differential equation (
1.1
) and let
y^{n}

be the corresponding Euler scheme
(
1.2
).
Then
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
f\in C^{4}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
t\in[0,T]
there is a constant
C>0
independent of
n
such that

(1.4)

\left|\mathbb{E}f(y^{n}_{t})-\mathbb{E}f(y_{t})\right|\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1.1
is the first weak convergence result for numerical schemes of differential equations driven by a fBm with
H<1/2
. In order to get a broader perspective on weak convergence for stochastic differential systems, let us recall some of the rates obtained in previous contributions:

(a)

It is well-known that the weak convergence rate for an equation like (
1.1
) driven by a Brownian motion is
n^{-1}
, versus a rate
n^{-1/2}
for the strong rate; This has been established in the classical references
[
2
,
3
]
.

(b)

In
[
23
]
the authors consider differential equations driven by a fBm for the range of Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
. It is shown that the weak rate is
n^{-1}
like in the Brownian case, regardless of the value of
H
. This rate is optimal in the sense that the normalized error
n[\mathbb{E}f(y^{n}_{t})-\mathbb{E}f(y_{t})]
converges to a nonzero limit for any test function
f\in C^{4}
.

(c)

The recent articles
[
15
,
17
]
consider the weak convergence of Euler schemes for a mixed stochastic integral model

{\mathcal{I}}=\int_{0}^{T}\varphi\left(B_{t}^{H}\right)\,dW_{s}
, where
W
is a Wiener process and
B^{H}
is a Liouville type fractional Brownian motion driven by
W
(with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
). It is proved that for a general choice of functions
f,\varphi
, the weak rate is
1/n^{(3H+1/2)\wedge 1}
(to be contrasted with the strong rate of the Euler scheme for
{\mathcal{I}}
above, which is
1/n^{H}
). Our result shows that this surprising behavior is probably due to some specific cancellations for mixed quantities like
{\mathcal{I}}
(see further remarks about this fact in
[
15
]
).

Compared to this body of literature, our Theorem
1.1
shows that when
1/3<H<1/2
the weak rate for equation (
1.1
) is
n^{1-4H}
(note that we believe that our rate is optimal for a generic test function).
This generalizes in a very natural way the convergence rate
n^{-1}
obtained for
H\geq 1/2
, except for the slightly non optimal
\varepsilon
in relation (
1.4
). Notice that this small
\varepsilon
is due to the fact that our analysis of the scheme is mostly pathwise, in spite of dealing with a convergence in distribution.
It is interesting to mention that Theorem
1.4
agrees with the rule of thumb in the martingale framework (see e.g. Heston’s model
[
1
]
, Schrödinger’s equation
[
10
]
, reflected diffusions
[
5
]
or the stochastic heat equation
[
11
]
), namely that the weak rate
n^{1-4H}
is twice the strong rate
n^{1/2-2H}
(see
[
28
]
).

At the core of our methodology for the proof of Theorem
1.1
lies a combination of rough paths and Malliavin techniques, plus some specific tools for discrete rough paths that have been developed by two of the authors in
[
28
,
29
]
. Those elements are summarized in Section
2
and Sections
4.1
-
4.2
.
Our main additional ingredient in the current contribution is to show the integrability of the Malliavin derivatives of the Euler scheme (
1.2
), uniformly in our approximation parameter
n
. A key observation in this direction is that the Euler scheme (
1.2
) is a discrete-time equation driven by the mix of a rough path (that is the process
x
) and a quadratic
Young
path (that is, a path which is a quadratic functional of
x
and has a Hölder component greater than
1/2
; see (
2.20
) for the precise definition). This representation enables us to adapt the very fruitful idea of
greedy sequence
put forward in
[
8
]
, in order to achieve exponential integrability in a rough paths context.
A new situation for the Euler scheme is that now we have a greedy sequence corresponding not only to
x
but also to the quadratic path
q
introduced in (
2.20
). One of our main efforts will then consist in showing a tail estimate for the greedy sequence via Borell’s inequality.
Furthermore, due to the discrete feature of equation (
1.2
), a separate estimate will involve the
big
steps related to our partition of
[0,T]
(namely the steps for which the increments
\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}
are very large) separately. These delicate estimates will be developed in Section
3
.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
contains the preliminary results on rough paths, Malliavin calculus, and the Euler scheme. In Section
3
we show that the Malliavin derivatives of the Euler scheme has moments of any order.
After some preparations in Section
4.1
-
4.5
, we prove the weak convergence of the Euler scheme in Section
4.6
.

Notation 1.2
.

In what follows, we take
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Delta=T/n
, and consider the uniform partition:

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
on
[0,T]
, where
t_{k}=k\Delta
. We denote by
\llbracket s,t\rrbracket
the discrete interval:
\llbracket s,t\rrbracket=\{t_{k}\in[s,t]:k=0,\dots,n\}
. For
u\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, we denote
\eta(u)=t_{k}
.
For an interval
[s,t]\subset[0,T]
we define the continuous- and discrete-time simplexes
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t])=\{(u,v):s\leq u\leq v\leq t\}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)={\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t])\cap\llbracket s,t\rrbracket^{2}
. We use the letters
C
and
K
to denote generic constant which can change from line to line.

2.
Preliminary results

In this section we recall some basic notions of rough paths theory and their application to fractional Brownian motion, which allow a proper definition of equation (
1.1
). We also give the necessary elements of Malliavin calculus in order to
quantify the weak convergence rate. Eventually we recall the pathwise estimates obtained in
[
27
]
for the Malliavin derivatives of our Euler scheme. Notice that this basic presentation can be found in a very similar way in our companion paper
[
27
]
.

2.1.
Elements of rough paths theory

This subsection is devoted to introduce some basic concepts of rough paths theory. We are going to restrict our analysis to a generic
p
-variation regularity of the driving path of order
1\leq p<3
, in order to keep expansions to a reasonable size. We also fix a finite time horizon
T>0
. The following notation will prevail until the end of the paper: for a finite dimensional vector space
{\mathcal{V}}
and two functions
f\in C([0,T],{\mathcal{V}})
and
g\in C({\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]),{\mathcal{V}})
we set

(2.1)

\displaystyle\delta f_{st}=f_{t}-f_{s},\quad\text{and}\quad\delta g_{sut}=g_{st}-g_{su}-g_{ut}.

Let us introduce the analytic requirements in terms of
p
-variation regularity which will be used in the sequel. Namely consider two paths
x\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x^{2}\in C({\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]),(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
. Then we denote

(2.2)

\|x\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}:=\left(\sup_{{\mathcal{P}}}\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{P}}}{|\delta x_{uv}|^{p}}\right)^{1/p},\quad\|x^{2}\|_{p/2\text{-var},[s,t]}:=\left(\sup_{{\mathcal{P}}}\sum_{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{P}}}{|x^{2}_{uv}|^{p/2}}\right)^{2/p},

where the supremum is taken among all partitions of the time interval
[s,t]
, and for a partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of
[s,t]
we write
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{P}}
if
u
and
v
are two consecutive partition points of
{\mathcal{P}}
. When the semi-norms in (
2.2
) are finite we say that
x
and
x^{2}
are respectively in
C^{p\text{-var}}([s,t],\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
C^{p/2\text{-var}}({\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t]),(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
.
For convenience, we denote
\|x\|_{p\text{-var}}:=\|x\|_{p\text{-var},[0,T]}
and
\|x^{2}\|_{p/2\text{-var}}:=\|x^{2}\|_{p/2\text{-var},[0,T]}
.
With this preliminary notation in hand, we can now turn to the definition of rough path.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
x^{2}\in C({\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]),(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
, and
1\leq p<3
. Denote
x^{1}_{st}=\delta x_{st}
.
We call
{\bf x}:=S_{2}(x):=(x^{1},x^{2})
a (second-order)
p
-rough path if
\|x\|_{p\text{-var}}<\infty
and
\|x^{2}\|_{p/2\text{-var}}<\infty
, and if the following algebraic relation holds true:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\delta x^{2}_{sut}=x^{2}_{st}-x^{2}_{su}-x^{2}_{ut}=\delta x_{su}\otimes\delta x_{ut},

where we have invoked (
2.1
) for the definition of
\delta x
and
\delta x^{2}
. For a
p
-rough path
S_{2}(x)
, we define a
p
-variation semi-norm as follows:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(x)\|_{p\text{-var}}:=\|x\|_{p\text{-var}}+\|x^{2}\|_{p/2\text{-var}}^{1/2}\,.

An important subclass of rough paths are the so-called
geometric
p
-variation rough paths
. A geometric
p
-variation rough path is a
p
-rough path
(x,x^{2})
such that there exists a sequence of smooth
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued paths
(x^{n},x^{2,n})
verifying:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\|x-x^{n}\|_{p\text{-var}}+\|x^{2}-x^{2,n}\|_{p/2\text{-var}}\right)=0.

We will mainly consider geometric rough paths in the remainder of the article.

In relation to (
2.5
), notice that when
x
is a smooth
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued path, we can choose
x^{2}
defined as the following iterated Riemann type integral,

(2.6)

\displaystyle x^{2}_{st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u}dx_{v}\otimes dx_{u}.

It is then easily verified that
S_{2}(x)=(x^{1},x^{2})
, with
x^{2}
defined in
(
2.6
)
, is a
p
-rough path with
p=1
. In fact, this is also the unique way to lift a smooth path to a
p
-rough path for some
p\geq 1
.

Recall now that we interpret equation (
1.1
) in the rough paths sense. That is, we shall consider the following general rough differential equation (RDE):

(2.7)

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a+\int_{0}^{t}V_{0}(y_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}{V}(y_{s})dx_{s}\,,\quad t\in[0,T],

where
V_{0}
and
V
are smooth enough coefficients and
x
is a rough path as given in Definition
2.1
. We shall interpret equation (
2.7
) in a way introduced by Davie in
[
9
]
, which is conveniently compatible with numerical approximations.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(x,x^{2})
be a
p
-rough path with
p<3
. We say that
y
is a solution of
(
2.7
)
on
[0,T]
if
y_{0}=a
and there exists a control function
\omega
on
[0,T]
(that is,
\omega
is a two variable function on
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
which satisfies the super-additivity condition
\omega(s,t)\geq\omega(s,u)+\omega(u,t)
for
s,u,t\in[0,T]:s<u<t
), a constant
K>0
and
\mu>1
such that

(2.8)

\Big|\delta y_{st}-\int_{s}^{t}V_{0}(y_{u})\,du-V(y_{s})\delta x_{st}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial V_{i}V_{j}(y_{s})x^{2,ij}_{st}\Big|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{\mu}

for all
(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{2}([0,T])
, where we recall that
\delta y
is defined by
(
2.1
)
and
\partial V_{i}V_{j}
is defined as in (
1.3
).

Notice that if
y
solves (
2.7
) according to Definition
2.2
, then it is also a controlled process as defined in
[
13
,
20
]
. Namely, if
y
satisfies relation (
2.8
), then we also have:

\displaystyle\delta y_{st}=V(y_{s})\delta x_{st}+r_{st}^{y},

where
r^{y}\in C^{p/2\text{-var}}({\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]))
. We can thus define iterated integrals of
y
with respect to itself thanks to the sewing map; see Proposition 1 in
[
20
]
. This yields the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{s}^{t}y_{u}^{i}dy^{j}_{u}-y^{i}_{s}\delta y^{j}_{st}-\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}=1}^{d}V^{i}_{i^{\prime}}V^{j}_{j^{\prime}}(y_{s})x_{st}^{2,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\omega(s,t)^{3/p},

for all
(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{2}([0,T])
and
i,j=1,\ldots,m
. In other words, the signature type path
S_{2}({\bf y})=(y^{1},y^{2})
defines a rough path according to Definition
2.1
, where
y^{2}
denotes the iterated integral of
y
.

We can now state an existence and uniqueness result for rough differential equations. The reader is referred to e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 10.36]
for further details.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that
V=(V_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d}
is a collection of
C^{3}_{b}
-vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Then there exists a unique RDE solution to equation
(
2.7
)
, understood as in Definition
2.2
. In addition, there exists a constant
K>0
such that the unique solution
y
satisfies the following estimate:

\displaystyle|S_{2}(y)_{st}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\left(1\vee\|S_{2}(x)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}\right).

Whenever
V=(V_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d}
is a collection of linear vector fields,
existence and uniqueness still hold for equation (
2.7
). Furthermore, there exist constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that we have the estimate:

\displaystyle|S_{2}(y)_{st}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{1}\|S_{2}(x)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\,\exp\left(K_{2}\|S_{2}(x)\|_{p\text{-var}}^{p}\right)\,.

We close this section by recalling a sewing map lemma with respect to discrete control functions. It is an elaboration of
[
28
, Lemma 2.5]
and proves to be useful in the analysis of the numerical scheme. Let
\pi:0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n-1}<t_{n}=T
be a generic partition of the interval
[0,T]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\llbracket s,t\rrbracket
the discrete interval
\{t_{k}:s\leq t_{k}\leq t\}
for
0\leq s<t\leq T
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
\omega
is a control on
\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
. In other words,
\omega
is a two variable function on
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)
which satisfies a super-additivity condition:
\omega(s,t)\geq\omega(s,u)+\omega(u,t)
for
s,u,t\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket:s<u<t
.
Consider a Banach space
{\mathcal{B}}
with norm
|\cdot|
and
R:{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)\to{\mathcal{B}}
, and
denote
\delta R_{sut}=R_{st}-R_{su}-R_{ut}
.
Suppose that
|R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|\leq\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})^{\mu}
for all
t_{k}\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
, and that
|\delta R_{sut}|\leq\omega(s,t)^{\mu}
with the exponent
\mu>1
. Then the following relation holds:

(2.9)

\displaystyle|R_{st}|\leq K_{\mu}\omega(s,t)^{\mu}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad K_{\mu}=2^{\mu}\,\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l^{-\mu}.

The discrete sewing lemma allows to bound discrete sums which are crucial in our numerical scheme context. As a first application along those lines we present a probabilistic result below, which combines Proposition 4.1 and Remark 4.2 in
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider two processes
f
and
g
such that for all
s,t\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
we have

\displaystyle\|\delta f_{st}\|_{L^{2p}}\lesssim|t-s|^{{\alpha}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\delta g_{st}\|_{L^{2p}}\lesssim|t-s|^{{\beta}},

for a given
p\geq 1
and
{\alpha},{\beta}
such that
{\alpha}+{\beta}>1
. Let
J_{st}
be the discrete sum given by

(2.10)

\displaystyle J_{st}=\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}\delta f_{st_{k}}\delta g_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\|J_{st}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim(t-s)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}}.

2.2.
Rough path above fractional Brownian motion

We now specialize our setting to a path
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{d})
defined as a standard
d
-dimensional fBm on
[0,T]
with Hurst parameter
H\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2})
.
This fBm is defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P})
, and we assume that the
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}
is generated by
x
.
In this situation, recall that the covariance function of each coordinate of
x
is defined on
\mathcal{S}_{2}([0,T])
by:

(2.11)

\displaystyle R(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[s^{2H}+t^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right],

where recall that the simplex
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
is introduced in Notation
1.2
.
We start by reviewing some properties of the covariance function of
x
considered as a function on
(\mathcal{S}_{2}([0,T]))^{2}
. Namely, take
(u,v,s,t)
in
(\mathcal{S}_{2}([0,T]))^{2}
and set

(2.12)

\displaystyle R([u,v],[s,t])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta x^{j}_{uv}\,\delta x^{j}_{st}\right],\qquad j=1,\dots,d.

Then, whenever
H>1/4
, it can be shown that the integral
\int R\,dR
is well-defined as a Young integral in the plane (see e.g.
[

, Section 6.4]
). Furthermore, if the intervals
[u,v]
and
[s,t]
are disjoint, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle R([u,v],[s,t])=\int_{u}^{v}\int_{s}^{t}\mu(dr^{\prime}dr).

Here and in the following, the signed measure
\mu
is defined as

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mu(dr^{\prime}dr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-H(1-2H)|r-r^{\prime}|^{2H-2}dr^{\prime}dr.

Using the elementary properties above, it is shown in
[

, Chapter 15]
that for any piecewise linear or mollifier approximation

to

, the smooth rough path

defined by (

) to a

-geometric rough path

(given as in Definition

) for
3>p>1/H
. In addition, for
i\neq j
the covariance of

can be expressed in terms of a
2
-dimensional Young integral:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x^{2,ij}_{uv}x^{2,ij}_{st}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{v}\int_{s}^{t}R([u,r],[s,r^{\prime}])dR(r^{\prime},r).

It is also established in
[
16
, Chapter 15]
that
S_{2}(x)
enjoys the following integrability property.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
S_{2}(x):=(x^{1},x^{2})
be the geometric rough path above
x
as given in Definition
2.1
, and
p\in(1/H,3)
. Then there exists a random variable

such that

, where

is defined by
(

)
.

According to Theorem

, given that the vector fields

2.3.
Malliavin calculus for
\mathbf{x}

As mentioned in the introduction, we will analyze the convergence of distribution for our numerical approximations thanks to Malliavin calculus tools. We proceed to recall the main concepts which will be used later in the paper and refer to
[
30
]
for further details.
We start by labeling a definition for the Cameron-Martin type space
{\mathcal{H}}
related to our fractional Brownian motion
x
.

Definition 2.7
.

Denote by
\mathcal{E}_{[a,b]}
the set of step functions on an interval
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
. We call
\mathcal{H}_{[a,b]}
the Hilbert space defined as the closure of
\mathcal{E}_{[a,b]}

with respect to the scalar product

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]},\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{[a,b]}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R([u,v],[s,t]).

In order to alleviate notations, we will write
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{[a,b]}
when
[a,b]=[0,T]
.
Notice that the mapping
\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rightarrow\delta x_{st}
can be extended to an isometry between
\mathcal{H}_{[a,b]}
and the Gaussian space associated with
\{x_{t},t\in[a,b]\}
.
We denote this isometry by
h\rightarrow\int_{a}^{b}h\,\delta^{\diamond}x
. The random variable
\int_{a}^{b}h\,\delta^{\diamond}x
is called the (first-order) Wiener integral and is also denoted by
I_{1}(h)
.

The space
{\mathcal{H}}
is very useful in order to define Wiener integrals with respect to
x
. In this paper we also need to introduce another Cameron-Martin type space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
. The space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
allows to identify pathwise derivatives with respect to
x
and the Malliavin derivatives. In order to construct
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
, let
{\mathcal{R}}
be the linear operator such that
{\mathcal{R}}:h\in{\mathcal{H}}\to\langle h,\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. Then the space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
is defined as the Hilbert space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}={\mathcal{R}}({\mathcal{H}})
equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{R}}(g),{\mathcal{R}}(h)\rangle_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}=\langle g,h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

We refer to
[
19
,
31
]
for more details about the spaces
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
.

For the sake of conciseness, we refer to
[
30
]
for a proper definition of Malliavin derivatives and related Sobolev spaces in Gaussian analysis. Let us just mention that we will denote the Malliavin derivative by
{D}F
, the Sobolev spaces by
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
and the corresponding norms by
\|F\|_{k,p}
. We denote by
D^{k}F
the
k
th iteration of the Malliavin derivative
D
applied on
F
.
The
n
-th order chaos of
x
is denoted by
{\mathcal{K}}^{x}_{n}
. Also notice that we are considering a
d
-dimensional fBm
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{d})
. Therefore, we shall consider partial Malliavin derivatives with respect to each coordinate
x^{i}
in the sequel.
Those partial derivatives will be denoted by
D^{(i)}
. Then for
h=(h^{1},\dots,h^{d})\in{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
we write
D_{h}F=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle D^{(i)}F,h^{i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
.
For
L\geq 2
we also denote by
D^{L}_{h}
the iterated versions of
D_{h}
. Namely we set

(2.16)

\displaystyle D^{L}_{h}F=D_{h}\circ\cdots\circ D_{h}F.

The Sobolev spaces related to the Malliavin derivatives are denoted by
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
and the corresponding norms are written
\|\cdot\|_{k,p}
. The dual of the Malliavin derivative is the Skorohod integral, for which we use the notation
\delta^{\diamond}
. Its domain includes the space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}({\mathcal{H}}^{d})
, and the integration by parts formula can be read as

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F\delta^{\diamond}(u)]=\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,u\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{d}}],

valid for
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
u\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}({\mathcal{H}}^{d})
.

2.3.1.
Differentiability

As we will see below, under the condition that
V\in C_{b}^{\lfloor 1/{\gamma}\rfloor+1}
the solution
y
to (
2.7
) is infinitely differentiable in the Malliavin calculus sense. We shall express its Malliavin derivative in terms of the Jacobian
\Phi
of the equation, which is defined by the relation
\Phi_{t}^{ij}=\partial_{a_{j}}y_{t}^{(i)}
, where recall that
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
is the initial value of the system (
2.7
). Setting
\partial V_{j}
for the Jacobian of
V_{j}
seen as a function from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let us recall that
\Phi
is the unique solution to the linear equation

(2.18)

\Phi_{t}=\text{Id}_{m}+\int_{0}^{t}\partial V_{0}(y_{s})\,\Phi_{s}\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\partial V_{j}(y_{s})\,\Phi_{s}\,dx^{j}_{s}.

Moreover, the following results hold true:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
y
be the solution to equation (
2.7
) and suppose
(V_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{d})
is a collection of vector fields in
C_{b}^{3}
. Then
for every
i=1,\ldots,m
,
t>0
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have
y_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{D}^{2,p}({\mathcal{H}})
for
p\geq 1
and

{D}^{(j)}_{s}y_{t}=\Phi_{s,t}V_{j}(y_{s}),\quad j=1,\ldots,d,\quad 0\leq s\leq t,

where
{D}^{(j)}_{s}y^{(i)}_{t}
is the
j
-th component of

{D}_{s}y^{(i)}_{t}
,
\Phi_{t}=\partial_{a}y_{t}
solves equation (
2.18
) and
\Phi_{s,t}=\Phi_{t}\Phi_{s}^{-1}
.

Let us now quote the result
[
8
]
, which gives a useful estimate for moments of the Jacobian of rough differential equations driven by Gaussian processes. Note that this result is expressed in terms of
p
-variations, for which we refer to
[
16
]
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Consider a fractional Brownian motion
x
with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/4,1/2]
and
p>1/H
. Then for any
\eta\geq 1
, there exists a finite constant
c_{\eta}
such that the Jacobian
\Phi
defined by (
2.18
) satisfies:

(2.19)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Phi\|^{\eta}_{p\text{-}{\rm var};[0,1]}\right]=c_{\eta}.

2.4.
Path-wise estimate of Euler scheme and its derivatives

In the remaining of the section we state a path-wise upper-bound estimate of the Malliavin derivatives of
y^{n}
obtained in our companion paper
[
27
]
. We first introduce some notations.

Let
b
be a fBm independent of
x
. Recall that the rough paths above
x
and
b
are denoted by
(x^{1},x^{2})
and
(b^{1},b^{2})
, respectively (see Definition
2.1
). We introduce some second chaos processes which play a prominent role in the analysis of Euler schemes (see
[
28
]
). Namely for
[s,t]\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
we set

(2.20)

\displaystyle q_{st}^{ij}=\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}\left(x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{2,ij}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}\right)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad q^{b,ij}_{st}=\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}\left(b_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{2,ij}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}\right).

We recall a basic inequality taken from
[
28
, Lemma 3.4]
: for
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)
we have

(2.21)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[\|q_{st}\|^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\frac{(t-s)^{1/2}}{n^{2H-1/2}}\,.

We also introduce a Gaussian process
w
which encompasses the coordinates of both the driving noise
x
and the extra noise
b
. Specifically we define

(2.22)

\displaystyle\delta\text{w}_{st}:=(\delta\text{w}_{st}^{1},\dots,\delta\text{w}_{st}^{2d}):=(\delta x_{st}^{1},\dots,\delta x_{st}^{d},\delta b_{st}^{1},\dots,\delta b_{st}^{d}).

Furthermore, we define a control
\omega
by

(2.23)

\displaystyle\omega(s,t)=\|{\bf w}\|_{p\text{-var};\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p}+\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|q^{b}\|_{p/2\text{-var};\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2},\qquad(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket),

where
q
is defined in
(
2.20
)
,
\text{w}=(x,b)
according to (
2.22
) and
{\bf w}=S_{2}(\text{w})
is the
p
-rough path above
w
(see Definition
2.1
).
Let
{\alpha}>0
be a positive constant.
Denote
s_{0}=0
. Then
given
s_{j}
, we define
s_{j+1}
recursively as

(2.24)

s_{j+1}=\begin{cases}s_{j}+\Delta\,,&\textnormal{if }\omega(s_{j},s_{j}+\Delta)>{\alpha}\\
\max\{u\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket:\,u>s_{j}\text{ and }\omega(s_{j},u)\leq{\alpha}\}\,,&\textnormal{if }\omega(s_{j},s_{j}+\Delta)\leq{\alpha}\end{cases}

Next we split the set of
s_{j}
’s as

(2.25)

\displaystyle S_{0}=\{s_{j}:{\alpha}/2\leq\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})\leq{\alpha}\};\quad S_{1}=\{s_{j}:\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})<{\alpha}/2\};

(2.26)

\displaystyle S_{2}=\{s_{j}:\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})>{\alpha}\}.

We set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{0}=\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{0}}\left(K\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})^{1/p}+1\right),\qquad{\mathcal{M}}_{1}=\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{1}}\left(K\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})^{1/p}+1\right),

(2.27)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{2}}\left(K|\delta{\text{w}}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|+K\Delta^{2H}+1\right),

and
K
is a constant independent of
n
.

We now recall the following path-wise estimates for the Euler scheme in
[
27
, Theorem 4.13]
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Take
r,r^{\prime}\geq 0
and let
k_{0},k_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
r\in(t_{k_{0}},t_{k_{0}+1}]
and
r^{\prime}\in(t_{k_{0}^{\prime}},t_{k_{0}^{\prime}+1}]
. Suppose that
V\in C^{4}_{b}
and
p>1/H
. Define a Malliavin derivative vector
\xi^{n}
as

(2.28)

\xi^{n}_{t}=(y^{n}_{t},D_{r}y^{n}_{t},D_{r}D_{r^{\prime}}y^{n}_{t}):=(\xi_{t}^{n,0},\xi_{t}^{n,1},\xi_{t}^{n,2})

Then for
L=0,1,2
and all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
we have the estimate

(2.29)

\displaystyle\|\xi^{n,L}\|_{p\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}\leq K\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\cdot{\mathcal{G}}\,,

where the random variable
{\mathcal{G}}
is defined by

(2.30)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}=|S_{0}\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}|\cdot({\mathcal{M}}_{0}\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{2})^{L}\,,

and where the quantities
S_{i}
,

{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
are respectively defined for
i=0,1,2
in (
2.25
)-(
2.26
) and (
2.4
). Moreover, for

(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s_{j},s_{j+1}\rrbracket)
such that
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
we have

(2.31)

\displaystyle|\delta\xi^{n,L}_{st}-{\mathcal{L}}^{L}V(y^{n}_{s})\delta x_{st}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{2/p}\cdot{\mathcal{G}}^{2},\qquad L=0,1,2,

where we have set

{\mathcal{L}}^{0}V(y^{n}_{s})=V(y^{n}_{s}),\qquad{\mathcal{L}}^{1}V(y^{n}_{s})=\partial V(y^{n}_{s})D_{r}y^{n}_{s},

and where

is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{2}V(y^{n}_{s})=\partial^{2}V(y^{n}_{s})D_{r}y^{n}_{s}D_{r^{\prime}}y^{n}_{s}+\partial V(y^{n}_{s})D_{r^{\prime}}D_{r}y^{n}_{s}.

(The reader is referred to
[

for the general definition of the operator

In both estimates

,

,

and

.

3.
Uniform Integrability for Malliavin derivatives of the Euler scheme

In this section we tackle the integrability issue for the Malliavin derivatives of the Euler scheme.
Before proceeding to our main considerations, some remarks about our global strategy are in order. Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are defined in (
2.4
),
b
is an independent copy of
x
, and
q^{b}
are defined in (
2.20
).
Recall that the signature
{\bf x}=S_{2}(x)=(x^{1},x^{2})
of
x
is defined in Definition
2.1
.

(1)

Due to the bound (
2.29
), the integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is our main task towards a uniform bound for the Malliavin derivatives as a function of
n
. We mostly focus on this problem in the sequel.

(2)

Once the bounds in Theorem
2.10
are established, the fractional Brownian motion
b
does not play any particular role in our estimates. Hence for notational sake we perform our computations below in the case
b\equiv q^{b}\equiv 0
. Modifications to cover the
b\neq 0
case are trivial.

We now turn our attention to the integrability of the random variables
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
.

3.1.
Uniform Integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}

In this subsection, we consider the uniform integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in
(
2.4
)
. The proof is achieved thanks to a tail analysis of the cardinality of the large size steps, that is steps with size
>\!\!>{\alpha}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}(n)
be
the random variable defined by (
2.4
). Specifically, since we are assuming that
b=0
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{2}}\left(K|\delta x_{s_{i}s_{i+1}}|+K\Delta^{2H}+1\right),

where
S_{2}
is the subset of
\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
displayed in (
2.26
).
Recall that
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
.
Then we have
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}_{2}|^{\nu}]<\infty
for all
\nu\geq 1
.

Proof.

Recall that
\omega
is defined by (
2.23
). Since we assume
b=0
and thus
q^{b}=0
, the control
\omega
is reduced to

\displaystyle\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})=|{\bf x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p}+|q_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p/2},

so that definition (
2.26
) yields the following relation:

(3.2)

\displaystyle S_{2}=\{t_{k}:\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})>{\alpha}\}=

\displaystyle\{t_{k}:|{\bf x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p}+|q_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p/2}>{\alpha}\}.

Moreover, it is readily checked that

\displaystyle|{\bf x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p}+|q_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p/2}\lesssim|\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p}+|x^{2}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|+|\Delta|^{2Hp}.

If we choose
n
large enough so that
|\Delta|^{2Hp}<\!\!<{\alpha}
, from the expression (
3.2
) we get

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\subset\{t_{k}:|{\bf x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{2}>K_{p}{\alpha}^{2/p}\}=:S_{3}.

This inclusion implies that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=

\displaystyle\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{2}}\left(K|{\bf x}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|+1\right)\leq\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{3}}\left(K|{\bf x}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|+1\right)\leq C_{{\alpha}}\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{3}}K|{\bf x}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|,

where we have invoked the fact that
|{\bf x}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|>K_{p}^{1/2}{\alpha}^{1/p}
whenever
s_{j}\in S_{3}
for the last inequality.
We now divide the proof in several steps.

Step 1: Some pathwise bounds.
Recall again that
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
. Pick then
{\beta}<H
such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}\equiv\|{\bf x}\|_{[0,T],{\beta}}=\|\delta x\|_{[0,T],{\beta}}+\|x^{2}\|_{[0,T],2{\beta}}^{1/2}\,,

as defined in (
2.4
), is almost surely finite.
Then since
|{\bf x}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}|\leq{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\leq\prod_{t_{k}\in S_{3}}K\left({\mathcal{G}}n^{-{\beta}}\right)\leq(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-{\beta}})^{|S_{3}|}.

In addition, according to (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle S_{3}\subset\bigcup_{i,j}\left(S^{i}_{31}\cup S^{ij}_{32}\cup S^{ij}_{33}\right),

where the sets
S_{31}
,
S_{32}
,
S_{33}
are defined by

\displaystyle S_{31}^{i}=\{t_{k}:(\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{i})^{2}>{\alpha}_{p}\},\quad S_{32}^{ij}=\{t_{k}:x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{2,i,j}>{\alpha}_{p}\},\quad S_{33}^{ij}=\{t_{k}:-x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{2,i,j}>{\alpha}_{p}\},

where
{\alpha}_{p}
is some constant depending on
{\alpha}
.
Therefore recalling that
K
designates a generic constant,
we have obtained the following upper bound for the random variable
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{|S^{i}_{31}|}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{i,j=1}^{d}(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{|S^{ij}_{32}|}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{i,j=1}^{d}(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{|S^{ij}_{33}|}\right).

In the following we consider the integrability of the random variable
(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{|S^{i}_{31}|}
for all
i=1,\dots,d
which appear in the right-hand side of (
3.4
).
The other terms in (
3.4
) can be handled very similarly.

Step 2: Tail estimates for
|S_{31}|
.
For each subset
\mathbf{u}\equiv\{u_{j};j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}
,
n^{\prime}\leq n
of the set of discrete instants
\{t_{k},k=0,1,\dots,n\}
we denote

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{t_{k}\in\{u_{j};\,j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}}(|\delta x^{i}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{2}-\Delta^{2H}).

Then notice that if
|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime}
, there exists a set
\mathbf{u}=\{u_{j};\,j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}
such that for all
j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
we have
(\delta x^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}})^{2}>{\alpha}_{p}
. Hence if we take a constant
K_{{\alpha},p}
such that
K_{{\alpha},p}<{\alpha}_{p}
and take
n
large enough, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})>n^{\prime}K_{{\alpha},p}.

We have thus proved that

\displaystyle\{|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{\mathbf{u}\subset\{t_{k}\}}\left\{{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})>K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}\right\}.

As a consequence of the above relation, we trivially get

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime}\}\leq

\displaystyle\,\sum_{\mathbf{u}\subset\{t_{k}\}}\mathbb{P}\left\{{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})>K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}\right\}.

Next set
\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}^{2}=(\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})|^{2})
. Owing to a slight variation of (
2.21
) we have
\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}^{2}\lesssim 1/n^{4H-1}
. Therefore starting from the right-hand side of (
3.5
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S^{i}_{31}|=n^{\prime})\leq

\displaystyle\,\sum_{\{u_{j};\,j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}\subset\{t_{k}\}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}}>\frac{K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}}\right\}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\sum_{\{u_{j};\,j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}\subset\{t_{k}\}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}}>\frac{K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}}{1/n^{2H-1/2}}\right\}.

The right-hand side of (
3.1
) is handled in the following way: taking into account the fact that
{\mathcal{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})/\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}
is a normalized random variable in the second chaos of
x
, we apply Borell’s inequality (see e.g.
[
21
, Theorem 5.12]
). In addition the number of sets of the form
\mathbf{u}=\{u_{j};j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}
is
{n}\choose{n^{\prime}}
.
Hence we end up with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S^{i}_{31}|=n^{\prime})\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{n!}{n^{\prime}!(n-n^{\prime})!}\exp\left(-n^{2H-1/2}\cdot K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}\right)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,n^{n^{\prime}}\exp\left(-n^{2H-1/2}\cdot K_{p,{\alpha}}\cdot n^{\prime}\right).

Step 3: Computations involving
{\mathcal{G}}
.
Let us now turn our attention to the term
{\mathcal{G}}
in (
3.3
). Since our fBm
x
is a Gaussian process, Fernique’s lemma asserts that
\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{G}}>x)\leq e^{-Kx^{2}}
for a given constant
K
and
x\geq 1
. This sub-Gaussian bound is sufficient to claim that for all
n^{\prime}\geq 1
we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{G}}^{n^{\prime}}\leq K(n^{\prime})^{n^{\prime}}=Ke^{n^{\prime}\ln n^{\prime}}.

We are now ready to go back to the study of the random variable
(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-{\beta}})^{\nu|S_{31}^{i}|}
,
K>0
. Namely we apply Hölder’s inequality with two conjugates
p,q>1
, and we combine this with (
3.1
) and (
3.8
).
We get

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left((K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu n^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K^{\nu n^{\prime}}n^{-{\beta}Kn^{\prime}}(\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{G}}^{pKn^{\prime}}])^{1/p}\mathbb{P}(|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime})^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\nu n^{\prime}\ln n^{\prime}}n^{-\beta Kn^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(|S_{31}^{i}|=n^{\prime})^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(f(n^{\prime})\right),

where the function
f
is defined by

(3.10)

\displaystyle f(n^{\prime}):=K_{1}n^{\prime}\ln n^{\prime}-{\beta}Kn^{\prime}\ln n+K_{2}n^{\prime}\ln n-K_{3}n^{2H-1/2}n^{\prime},

for three positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
,
K_{3}
whose exact value is irrelevant.

We now compute the maximum of the function
f
thanks to elementary considerations. First we calculate

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(n^{\prime})=K/n^{\prime}\geq 0.

Therefore
f
is upward convex and

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{2\leq n^{\prime}\leq n}f(n^{\prime})\leq f(2)\vee f(n)\leq f(2)+f(n).

Moreover one can explicitly compute
f(2)
and
f(n)
thanks to the expression (
3.10
). We obtain

\displaystyle f(n)=(K_{1}+K_{2}-\beta K)n\ln n-Kn^{2H+1/2},\qquad f(2)=K-K\ln n-Kn^{2H-1/2}.

Reporting this expression into (
3.9
), we discover that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu|S_{31}^{i}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left((K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu n^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|S_{31}|=n^{\prime}\}}\right)\leq\sum_{n^{\prime}=2}^{n}\exp\left(f(n^{\prime})\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left(f(n)+f(2)\right)\leq n\exp(C_{1}n\ln n-C_{2}n^{2H+1/2}).

Step 4: Conclusion.
Since we have assumed
H>1/3
, it is readily checked that the right-hand side of (
3.1
) is dominated by a constant. Therefore, we end up with the inequality
\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}[(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-{\beta}})^{\nu|S_{31}^{i}|}]\equiv M<\infty
.
This concludes the uniform (in
n
) integrability of
(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu|S^{i}_{31}|}
, for all indices
i=1,\dots,d
. The integrability of the other two quantities
(K{\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu|S^{ij}_{32}|}
and
({\mathcal{G}}n^{-\beta})^{\nu|S^{ij}_{33}|}
can be shown in a similar way. Combining these integrability results with relation
(
3.4
)
and with Hölder’s inequality, we obtain the uniform integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{\nu}
. Our proof is complete.
∎

Once the bound of
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is established, we can link the expected value of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to that of
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
by the observation that there are less steps with a small size (
<\!\!<{\alpha}
) than with a large size (
>\!\!>{\alpha}
). This is the content of the following result.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
be defined in
(
2.4
)
and we are still working with a fBm
x
with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
.
Then
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}_{1}|^{\nu}]<\infty
for all
\nu\geq 1
.

Proof.

In order to consider the integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
we observe that by the definition of
S_{1}
, for
s_{j}\in S_{1}
we have
\omega(s_{j+1},s_{j+1}+\Delta)\geq{\alpha}/2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle S_{1}\subset\{t_{k}:\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})>{\alpha}/2\}=:S_{3}^{\prime},

and so

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{1}=\prod_{s_{j}\in S_{1}}(K_{1}\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})^{1/p}+1)\leq(K_{1}{\alpha}+1)^{|S_{1}|}\leq K^{|S_{3}^{\prime}|}.

Observe that
K^{|S_{3}^{\prime}|}
is in the form similar to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in (
2.4
). So in a similar way as in Theorem
3.1
, we can show that
K^{\nu|S_{3}^{\prime}|}
and thus
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{\nu}
is uniformly integrable.
∎

3.2.
Integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}

In this section, we will take care of the products in (
2.4
) involving small increments of
\omega
. Now recall that those increments, defined by (
2.23
), involve the Gaussian process
w
and the second chaos process
q
. The presence of
q
will require a specific translation procedure on the Wiener space, which is carried out in Section
3.2.1
. Then a weighted sum argument is invoked in Section
3.2.2
.

3.2.1.
Translation of the fBm and some functionals

Let us recall that
x
is a fBm with
H>1/3
and
q
is defined in
(
2.20
)
.
In this subsection, we consider an upper-bound estimate for the translation of the fBm
x
and the process
q
. Notice that in the sequel our generic random element in the space
\Omega
will be denoted by
\phi
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Take
3>p>1/H
and
p^{\prime}>1
such that
1/p+1/p^{\prime}>1
. Let
h
be a path in
C^{p^{\prime}\text{-var}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and let
T_{h}
denote the translation operator:
T_{h}\phi=\phi+h
on the Wiener space related to our fBm. Then the following translation inequality holds:

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|T_{h}q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|T_{h}{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p}\leq K_{p}\left(\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}+\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}\right),

where
K_{p}
is a constant depending only on
p
.

Proof.

The estimate of
\|T_{h}{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p}
is shown in Lemma 3.1
[
8
]
. In the following we consider the estimate of
\|T_{h}q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}
.
Specifically, consider an element
u,v\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)
.
By definition we can write

(3.14)

\displaystyle T_{h}q_{uv}=q_{uv}+A^{1}_{uv}+A^{2}_{uv}+A^{3}_{uv}\,,

where

(3.15)

\displaystyle A^{1}_{uv}=\sum_{u\leq t_{k}<v}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\delta h_{t_{k}r}\otimes dx_{r}\,,\quad A^{2}_{uv}=\sum_{u\leq t_{k}<v}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\delta x_{t_{k}r}\otimes dh_{r}\,,

(3.16)

\displaystyle A^{3}_{uv}=\sum_{u\leq t_{k}<v}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\delta h_{t_{k}r}\otimes dh_{r}\,.

Next we further decompose the term
A^{1}
into

(3.17)

\displaystyle A_{uv}^{1}=A_{uv}^{11}+A_{uv}^{12},

where
A^{11}
and
A^{12}
are respectively defined by

(3.18)

\displaystyle A^{11}_{uv}=\int_{u}^{v}\delta h_{ur}\otimes dx_{r}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad A^{12}_{uv}=-{\mathcal{J}}_{u}^{v}(h,x),

and where the term
{\mathcal{J}}
above is given as

(3.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{u}^{v}(h,x)=\sum_{u\leq t_{k}<v}\delta h_{ut_{k}}\otimes\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}\,.

In the following, we bound the terms on the right side of
(
3.14
)
.

First, by a direct computation for all
(u,r,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{3}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)
we have

(3.20)

\displaystyle\delta A^{12}_{urv}=\delta h_{ur}\otimes\delta x_{rv}.

In order to bound
\delta A^{12}
, we consider the function

(3.21)

\displaystyle\omega(u,v)=:\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u,v]}\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[u,v]}.

It is well known that since
1/p+1/p^{\prime}>1
,
\omega
is a control function. In fact, it is easy to show that
\omega_{1}=:\omega^{1/\mu}
is a control function for
\mu
such that
1/p+1/p^{\prime}>\mu>1
.
It follows from
(
3.20
)
and the definition of
\omega_{1}
that

\displaystyle|\delta A^{12}_{urv}|\leq\omega_{1}(u,v)^{\mu}.

In addition, it is readily checked from our definition (
3.19
) that
A^{12}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=0
for all
t_{k}\in\llbracket s,t\rrbracket
. Therefore a direct application of Lemma
2.4
yields:

(3.22)

\displaystyle|A^{12}_{uv}|\leq K_{\mu}\,\omega_{1}(u,v)^{\mu}=K_{\mu}\,\omega(u,v).

Let us turn to the estimate of
A^{11}
defined by (
3.18
). In that case, due to the fact that
A^{11}
can be interpreted as a Young integral, some elementary estimates (see e.g.
[
33
]
) reveal that

(3.23)

\displaystyle|A^{11}_{uv}|\leq\omega(u,v).

Hence reporting
(
3.22
)
and (
3.23
) into
(
3.17
)
we end up with

(3.24)

\displaystyle|A^{1}_{uv}|\leq(K_{\mu}+1)\omega(u,v)\leq(K_{\mu}+1)(\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}),

where we recall that the control
\omega
is given by (
3.21
).
The term
A^{2}
in (
3.15
) can be bounded in a similar way as for
A^{1}
, and
we obtain the same estimate as in (
3.24
). The details are thus omitted.

In order to bound
A^{3}
defined by (
3.16
), we apply Young’s inequality again and also the super-additivity of the control
\omega_{2}(u,v)=:\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}
(Notice that
\omega_{2}
is a control owing to the fact that
p^{\prime}<2
). We get

\displaystyle|A^{3}_{uv}|\leq\sum_{u\leq t_{k}<v}\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}^{2}\leq\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}.

Putting together the estimates of
A^{1}
,
A^{2}
and
A^{3}
and equation
(
3.14
)
, we obtain

(3.25)

\displaystyle|T_{h}q_{uv}|\leq|q_{uv}|+2(K_{\mu}+1)\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}+(2K_{\mu}+3)\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u,v]}^{2}.

Now consider a generic partition
\pi=\{u_{j}\}
of
\llbracket s,t\rrbracket
. Thanks to (
3.25
) and super-additivity properties we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\{u_{j}\}}|T_{h}q_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}|^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{p}\left(\sum_{\{u_{j}\}}|q_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}|^{p/2}+\sum_{\{u_{j}\}}\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[u_{j},u_{j+1}]}^{p}+\sum_{\{u_{j}\}}\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[u_{j},u_{j+1}]}^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{p}\left(\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p/2}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}+\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}\right).

Finally, taking the sup over all partitions of
[s,t]

on the left side we obtain the desired estimate (
3.13
).
∎

3.2.2.
Integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}

This section is devoted to a study of the intermediate sized increments of
\omega
. Otherwise stated,
we are ready to show the uniform integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
S_{0}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
be defined in
(
2.4
)
, for a fBm
x
with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
and a threshold
{\alpha}>0
.
Then for any given
{\gamma}<2H+1
there exists
K=K_{{\gamma}}
such that for all
a\geq 1
we have

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S_{0}|>a)\leq Ke^{-Ka^{{\gamma}}}.

In particular,
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{M}}_{0}^{\nu}]<\infty
for all
\nu\geq 1
.

Proof.

The proof will be done in several steps.

Step 1. Preparations.

Let us go back to inequality (
3.13
). Remember that
p>1/H
therein. Since
H>1/4
, it is easily checked that one can pick

p^{\prime}>(H+1/2)^{-1}
such that
p
,
p^{\prime}
still satisfy
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}>1
. This pair of
p
,
p^{\prime}
will be fixed for the remainder of the proof. Recalling the constant
K_{p}
featuring in (
3.13
) and our threshold
{\alpha}
, we also choose

{\beta}>0
small enough so that
{\alpha}/2-K_{p}{\beta}>0
. Since
p>1/H
, according to

[
28
, Remark 3.6]
there exists an almost surely finite random variable
G_{p}
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}\leq G_{p}
.
Related to those quantities, we define the following two sets:

\displaystyle A^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\phi\in\Omega:\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}<{\beta}\},

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\phi\in\Omega:G_{p}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}<{\beta}\},

where we recall that the typical element of
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P})
is denoted by
\phi
.
It is clear that
A\subset A^{n}
.

Step 2. Tail inclusion relations.

Let
a\geq 1
be our generic threshold. Having the notation of Step 1 in mind we define a constant
\kappa
as follows:

(3.27)

\displaystyle\kappa=\left(\frac{{\alpha}/2-K_{p}{\beta}}{K_{p}}\right)^{1/p}a^{1/p^{\prime}}.

Recall that the Cameron-Martin type space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
is defined in Section
2.3
.
Let us call
B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
the unit ball in
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
, namely:
B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}=\{h\in\bar{{\mathcal{H}}};\|h\|_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}\leq 1\}
.
Our first aim is to show that

(3.28)

\displaystyle A+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}\subset A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}\subset\{|S_{0}|\leq a\}.

Suppose that
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
. In the following, we show that
|S_{0}|\leq a
for such
\phi
, which then implies the relation
(
3.28
)
. First, for
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
we have
\phi-h\in A^{n}
for some
h\in\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\|q(\phi-h)\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|{\bf x}(\phi-h)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}<{\beta}.

Recall that
T_{h}{\bf x}(\phi)={\bf x}(\phi+h)
for any
h\in\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}
almost surely. Hence the above relation becomes

(3.29)

\displaystyle\|T_{-h}q(\phi)\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|T_{-h}{\bf x}(\phi)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}<{\beta}.

We now consider the control
\omega
defined by
\omega(s,t)=\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p}
. For a generic element
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
we have

\displaystyle\omega(s,t)(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|T_{h}T_{-h}q(\phi)\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|T_{h}T_{-h}{\bf x}(\phi)\|_{p\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p}.

Hence invoking Lemma
3.3
we get

\displaystyle\omega(s,t)(\phi)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{p}\left(\|T_{-h}q(\phi)\|_{p/2\text{-var},\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|T_{-h}{\bf x}(\phi)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}^{p}+\|h\|^{p}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s,t]}\right),

and owing to
(
3.29
)
one ends up with the following relation valid for all
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
:

\displaystyle\omega(s,t)(\phi)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{p}{\beta}+K_{p}\|h\|^{p}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s,t]}.

In particular, when
s=s_{j}
and
t=s_{j+1}
for
s_{j}\in S_{0}
we obtain

\displaystyle{\alpha}/2\leq\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})\leq K_{p}{\beta}+K_{p}\|h\|^{p}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s_{j},s_{j+1}]},

and thus

(3.30)

\displaystyle\|h\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s_{j},s_{j+1}]}\geq\left(\frac{{\alpha}/2-K_{p}{\beta}}{K_{p}}\right)^{p^{\prime}/p}.

Since
\omega_{1}(s,t)\equiv\|h\|_{p^{\prime},[s,t]}^{p^{\prime}}
is a control it follows from
(
3.30
)
that

(3.31)

\displaystyle\|h\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[0,T]}\geq\sum_{s_{j}\in S_{0}}\|h\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[s_{j},s_{j+1}]}\geq\left(\frac{{\alpha}/2-K_{p}{\beta}}{K_{p}}\right)^{p^{\prime}/p}|S_{0}|.

We now specify the left-hand side of (
3.31
). First since we have chosen
p^{\prime}>(H+1/2)^{-1}
, the reference
[
7
, Page 14]
asserts that
|h|_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}\geq\|h\|_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[0,T]}
. Moreover we have assumed that
h\in\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle\kappa^{p^{\prime}}\geq|h|_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}^{p^{\prime}}\geq\|h\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}\text{-var},[0,T]}.

Plugging this inequality into (
3.31
), we obtain that if
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
then

\displaystyle|S_{0}|\leq\kappa^{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{{\alpha}/2-K_{p}{\beta}}{K_{p}}\right)^{-p^{\prime}/p}=a,

where the last identity stems from the definition (
3.27
) of
\kappa
. We have thus proved that if
\phi\in A^{n}+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}}
, then
|S_{0}|\leq a
. This concludes the proof of
(
3.28
)
.

Step 3. Tail estimates.
Let us introduce some extra bits of notation. Namely we write
\Phi
for the standard Gaussian CDF. For a set
A\subset\Omega
we also define
a_{A}\in\mathbb{R}
as the number such that
\Phi(a_{A})=\mathbb{P}(A)
. Then the isoperimetric type inequality in
[
26
, Theorem 4.3]
,
together with
(
3.28
)
, yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S_{0}|>a)\leq\mathbb{P}((A+\kappa B_{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}})^{c})\leq e^{-K(a_{A}+\kappa)^{2}}=e^{K\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}-K(a_{A}+\kappa)^{2}}e^{-K\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}}.

Let
K_{A}>0
be an upper bound of the quadratic function
f(\kappa)=K\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}-K(a_{A}+\kappa)^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then considering a constant
K
which can change from line to line and recalling the definition (
3.27
) of
\kappa
, we get

(3.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S_{0}|>a)\leq K_{A}\,e^{-K\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}}=K_{A}e^{-Ka^{2/p^{\prime}}}.

Recall again
that
p^{\prime}
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
(H+1/2)^{-1}
. Hence
2/p^{\prime}
is of the form
2H+1-\varepsilon
for a small
\varepsilon>0
. This conclude the tail estimate
(
3.26
)
.
It follows immediately from
(
3.26
)
that
|S_{0}|^{\nu}
and thus
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}^{\nu}
is uniformly integrable for any
\nu\geq 1
.
∎

3.3.
Integrability of Malliavin derivatives

With the preliminary results of Sections
3.1
and
3.2
in hand, we can now turn to the integrability result for the Malliavin derivatives of the Euler scheme. Notice that we restrict our analysis here to the first 2 Malliavin derivatives of
y^{n}
. However, it is clear that our estimates could be extended to arbitrary Malliavin derivatives.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
y^{n}
be the Euler scheme defined by (
1.2
). The first and second Malliavin derivatives of
y^{n}
are contained in the vector
\xi^{n}
introduced in (
2.28
). We assume that the vector field
V
is
C^{4}_{b}
and that
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
. Then for all
\nu\geq 1
we have

(3.33)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi^{n}\|_{p\text{-var}}^{\nu}\right]<\infty.

In particular, the following sup-norm inequality holds true:

(3.34)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N},\,r,r^{\prime},t\in[0,T]}|\xi^{n}_{t}|^{\nu}\Big]<\infty.

Proof.

Inequality (
3.33
) follows by showing that all terms in the right-hand side of (
2.29
) have moments of all orders.
Applying
Theorem
3.4
,
Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.1
respectively we obtain the integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. The integrability of
|S_{0}|
follows from (
3.26
). The integrability of
|S_{1}|
and
|S_{2}|
are implied by the relation
|S_{i}|\lesssim{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, respectively. The upper bound (
3.34
) is an easy consequence of (
3.33
).
∎

4.
Weak convergence

With our Malliavin derivative and integrability estimates in hand,
in this section we consider the weak convergence of the Euler scheme. The first sections are preparations of the main result.

4.1.
Estimation of an inner product in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}

In this subsection, we derive a useful upper-bound estimate for an inner product of the form
\langle\varphi,\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}
, involving some indicator functions. We first need a positivity result for the rectangular increment function
R
of the fBm.

Lemma 4.1
.

Recall that the covariance
R
is defined in (
2.11
), with rectangular increments
R([u,v],[s,t])
introduced in (
2.12
). Then
for any
u,v,s,t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
s\leq u\leq v\leq t
we have

\displaystyle R([u,v],[s,t])\geq 0.

Proof.

We first write

\displaystyle R([u,v],[s,t])=R([u,v],[u,v])+R([u,v],[u,v]^{C}),

where we denoted
[u,v]^{C}=[s,t]\setminus[u,v]
. Since
R([u,v],[u,v])=(v-u)^{2H}
it suffices to show that
R([u,v],[u,v]^{C})\geq-(v-u)^{2H}
.

By definition (
2.11
)-(
2.12
) of
R
we can write

(4.1)

\displaystyle R([u,v],[u,v]^{C})=R([u,v],[s,u])+R([u,v],[v,t])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(|v-s|^{2H}-|u-s|^{2H}-|v-u|^{2H})+\frac{1}{2}(|t-u|^{2H}-|t-v|^{2H}-|v-u|^{2H}).

Note that
|v-s|^{2H}-|u-s|^{2H}
and
|t-u|^{2H}-|t-v|^{2H}
are nonnegative. We thus obtain

(4.2)

\displaystyle R([u,v],[u,v]^{C})\geq-|v-u|^{2H}.

The proof is complete.
∎

The above positivity result leads to a surprisingly easy bound on products in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}
. For each
s\in[0,T]
we assume that
\varphi(s,\cdot)\in C^{p\text{-var}}([0,T])
. Let
f_{s}=\|\varphi(s,\cdot)\|_{p\text{-var}}
for
s\in[0,T]
. We also assume that
f\in C^{p\text{-var}}([0,T])
. For
s,t,u,v\in[0,T]
:
s<t
,
u<v
we define

{\alpha}(\eta,\zeta)=\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}(\zeta)
for
\eta,\zeta\in[0,T]
.
Then the following relation holds

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,{\alpha}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|\leq 4(t-s)^{2H}(v-u)^{2H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Starting from Definition
2.7
and taking limits on indicator functions of rectangles (similarly to
[
16
, Lemma 15.39]
), one can prove that
the norm in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}
can be expressed as a double 2D Young integral of the form

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,{\alpha}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}=\langle\varphi,\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}=\int_{[0,T]^{4}}\varphi(\eta,\zeta)\,\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}(\eta^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}(\zeta^{\prime})\,dR(\eta,\eta^{\prime})dR(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}).

One can then integrate out the
\eta^{\prime}
and
\zeta^{\prime}
variables in order to get

(4.4)

\langle\varphi,{\alpha}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}=\int_{[0,T]^{2}}\varphi(\eta,\zeta)\,dR(\eta,[u,v])dR(\zeta,[s,t]).

We further decompose the inner product
\langle\varphi,{\alpha}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}
using the identity:

\displaystyle{\bf 1}={\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{2}+{\alpha}_{3}+{\alpha}_{4},

where the functions
{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{4}
are given by

\displaystyle{\alpha}_{1}(\eta,\zeta)=\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}(\zeta),\qquad{\alpha}_{2}(\eta,\zeta)=\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]^{C}}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}(\zeta),

\displaystyle{\alpha}_{3}(\eta,\zeta)=\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]^{C}}(\zeta),\qquad{\alpha}_{4}(\eta,\zeta)=\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]^{C}}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]^{C}}(\zeta),

and where similarly to what we wrote in Lemma
4.1
, we have set

[s,t]^{C}=[0,T]\setminus[s,t]
and
[u,v]^{C}=[0,T]\setminus[u,v]
.
Otherwise stated, we recast (
4.4
) as

(4.5)

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,{\alpha}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}=\int_{[0,T]^{2}}\varphi(\eta,\zeta)\,dR(\eta,[u,v])dR(\zeta,[s,t])=\sum_{i=1}^{4}J_{T}^{i},

where the terms
J_{T}^{i}
are respectively defined by

\displaystyle J_{T}^{i}=\int_{[0,T]^{2}}\varphi(\eta,\zeta){\alpha}_{i}(\eta,\zeta)\,dR(\eta,[u,v])dR(\zeta,[s,t]).

Those four terms will be handled with slightly different arguments.
That is for
J^{1}_{T}
, owing to Lemma
4.1
we have that both
dR(\zeta,[s,t])
and
dR(\eta,[u,v])
are positive when
\eta\in[u,v]
and
\zeta\in[s,t]
. Therefore, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle|J^{1}_{T}|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}R([s,t],[s,t])R([u,v],[u,v])\leq(t-s)^{2H}(v-u)^{2H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

For the second term
J_{T}^{2}
in (
4.5
)
we observe that
dR(\zeta,[s,t])
is positive and
dR(\eta,[u,v])
is negative. Therefore the product
dR(\eta,[s,t])\cdot dR(\eta,[u,v])
does not change sign and we get

\displaystyle|J^{2}_{T}|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}|R([u,v],[u,v]^{C})R([s,t],[s,t])|.

Hence thanks to an elementary computation similar to (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) we discover that

(4.7)

\displaystyle|J^{2}_{T}|\leq(t-s)^{2H}(v-u)^{2H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

In conclusion, gathering (
4.6
), (
4.7
) and similar bounds for
J^{3}_{T}
,
J^{4}_{T}
into (
4.5
), we get the desired estimate (
4.3
). This concludes the proof.
∎

We now extend the previous lemma to the indicator of a simplex in
[0,T]^{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}
be as in Lemma
4.2
. Let
{\beta}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}
be of the form

(4.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{st}(u,v)=\mathbf{1}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t])}(u,v),

where we recall that the simplex
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t])
is defined in Notation
1.2
.
Then
there exists a constant
C_{H}
such that
the following relation holds

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,\beta_{st}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|\leq C_{H}(t-s)^{4H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

We will use a dyadic partition of the function
{\beta}
. Namely for
n\geq 0
and
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}
we set

u_{i,n}=s+2^{-n}(t-s)i
. Next for
\ell\geq 1
we define

\displaystyle{\beta}_{st}^{\ell}=\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n-1}-1}\mathbf{1}_{[u_{2i,n},u_{2i+1,n}]\times[u_{2i+1,n},u_{2i+2,n}]}.

Then it can be shown that
\|{\beta}^{\ell}_{st}-{\beta}_{st}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\to 0
. In order to prove the lemma it thus suffices to show that for all
\ell\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,\beta_{st}^{\ell}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|\leq(2^{4H}-2)^{-1}(t-s)^{4H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

In the following we prove this relation with the help of Lemma
4.2
. We first observe that by the definition of
{\beta}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,\beta_{st}^{\ell}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n-1}-1}\Big|\langle\varphi,\mathbf{1}_{[u_{2i,n},u_{2i+1,n}]\times[u_{2i+1,n},u_{2i+2,n}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|.

Applying Lemma
4.2
with
(s,u,v,t)=(u_{2i,n},u_{2i+1,n},u_{2i+1,n},u_{2i+2,n})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,\beta_{st}^{\ell}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\Big|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n-1}-1}(2^{-n}(t-s))^{2H}(2^{-n}(t-s))^{2H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(t-s)^{4H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}(2^{n})^{1-4H}\leq\frac{1}{2^{4H}-2}(t-s)^{4H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}.

This completes the proof of our claim (
4.9
).
∎

In the sequel we will also need an inequality for products in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Its proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
4.3
and is omitted for sake of conciseness.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}
be a function in
C^{p\text{-var}}([0,T])
.
Then the following relation holds

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\varphi,\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\Big|\leq(t-s)^{2H}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}

for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(0,T)
.

4.2.
An extension of the sewing lemma

In this section we extend Lemma
2.4
to the integral of two controlled processes. Our findings are summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
S_{2}(x):=(x^{1},x^{2})
be the geometric rough path above
x
as given in Definition
2.1
, and
p<3
.
We consider two couples of paths

(z,z^{\prime})
and
(\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime})
with
z,\tilde{z}\in C([s,t],\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
z^{\prime},\tilde{z}^{\prime}\in C([s,t],\mathbb{R}^{m\times d})
. Let
\omega^{x}(u,v)=\|x\|_{p\text{-var},[u,v]}^{p}
for
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s,t])
.
We assume the existence of two controlled functions
\omega^{z}
,
\omega_{1}^{z}
and
\omega^{z^{\prime}}
on
\llbracket s,t\rrbracket
such that for all
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)
we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle|\delta z_{uv}-z_{u}^{\prime}x^{1}_{uv}|\leq\omega^{z}(u,v)^{2/p},\qquad|\delta z_{uv}|\leq\omega_{1}^{z}(u,v)^{1/p},\qquad|\delta z_{uv}^{\prime}|\leq\omega^{z^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}.

We also assume that the relations in (
4.10
) hold for
\tilde{z}
, with related increments
\tilde{z}^{\prime},\omega^{z^{\prime}}
.
Next we introduce some new control functions:

(4.11)

\displaystyle\omega^{x,z,z^{\prime}}=\omega^{x}+\omega^{z}+\omega^{z^{\prime}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega^{x,z,z^{\prime}}_{1}=\omega^{x,z,z^{\prime}}+\omega_{1}^{z}\,,

and similarly for
(\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime})
. We now define some remainder terms in the integrals of
z
with respect to
\tilde{z}
or
x
. Namely for
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)

we set

(4.12)

\displaystyle R_{uv}^{z\tilde{z}}=\int_{u}^{v}(\delta z_{ur}-z_{u}^{\prime}x^{1}_{ur})\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{uv}^{\tilde{z}x}=\int_{u}^{v}(\delta\tilde{z}_{ur}-\tilde{z}_{u}^{\prime}x^{1}_{ur})\otimes dx_{r},

where the above integrals are understood in the rough path sense.
We suppose that the increments
R
are such that for any point
t_{k}
in our generic partition of
[s,t]
we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle|R^{z\tilde{z}}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|\leq\omega^{x}(t_{k},t_{k+1})^{3/p}\,\qquad\text{and}\qquad|R^{\tilde{z}x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|\leq\omega^{x}(t_{k},t_{k+1})^{3/p}.

Then the following relation holds for all
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)
:

(4.14)

\displaystyle\Big|R^{z\tilde{z}}_{uv}\Big|\leq K_{p}[\omega^{R}(u,v)]^{\mu},

where
\mu>1
is a given constant and
where
K_{p}>0
is a constant depending on
p
. In (
4.14
), the control
\omega^{R}
is also defined by the relation

(4.15)

[\omega^{R}(u,v)]^{\mu}:=\left(\omega^{z}(u,v)^{2/p}+\omega^{z^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\omega^{x}(u,v)^{1/p}\right)\omega_{1}^{x,\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\\
\times\left(\omega^{x,\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}+\|\tilde{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,[u,v]}+1\right)\,.

Proof.

The proof of the lemma is an application of Lemma
2.4
.
Namely the existence of
R
as a rough integral is ensured by general rough paths considerations (see e.g.
[
20
]
). Then some elementary manipulations starting from the definition (
4.12
) of
R
show that for
(u,s,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{3}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)
we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\delta R^{z\tilde{z}}_{usv}=(\delta z_{us}-z_{u}^{\prime}x^{1}_{us})\otimes\delta\tilde{z}_{sv}+z_{us}^{\prime}\int_{s}^{v}x^{1}_{sr}\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}\,,

where we recall that
\delta
is defined as in (
2.3
).

We first consider the case when
(z,\tilde{z})=(\tilde{z},x)
, that is the remainder
R^{\tilde{z}x}
defined in (
4.12
). In this case one can recast (
4.16
) as

(4.17)

\displaystyle\delta R^{\tilde{z}x}_{usv}=(\delta\tilde{z}_{us}-\tilde{z}_{u}^{\prime}x^{1}_{us})\otimes x_{sv}^{1}+\tilde{z}_{us}^{\prime}\,x^{2}_{sv}.

Applying the conditions in (
4.10
) we thus get

(4.18)

\displaystyle|\delta R^{\tilde{z}x}_{usv}|\leq\omega^{\tilde{z}}(u,v)^{2/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{1/p}+\omega^{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}.

Moreover, we have assumed that (
4.13
) holds true for the increments
R^{\tilde{z}x}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}
.
Hence a direct application of Lemma
2.4
implies that (
4.18
) also holds when
\delta R^{\tilde{z}x}_{usv}
is replaced by
R^{\tilde{z}x}_{uv}
, that is relation (
4.14
) holds for
R^{\tilde{z}x}
defined by (
4.12
).

In order to prove (
4.14
) for a general
\tilde{z}
,
let us first bound the integral
\int_{s}^{v}x^{1}_{sr}\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}
in (
4.16
).
To this aim, we observe that a simple integration by parts (valid for rough integrals thanks to a limiting procedure on smooth approximations) yields the relation

(4.19)

\int_{s}^{v}x^{1}_{sr}\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}=x^{1}_{sv}\otimes\delta\tilde{z}_{sv}-\int_{s}^{v}\delta\tilde{z}_{sr}\otimes dx_{r}\,.

Next owing to our conditions (
4.10
) for
\tilde{z}
, the first term in the right hand side of (
4.19
) is bounded by

(4.20)

\displaystyle|x^{1}_{sv}\otimes\delta\tilde{z}_{sv}|\leq\omega^{x}(s,v)^{1/p}\omega_{1}^{\tilde{z}}(s,v)^{1/p}.

For the second term in the right-hand side of (
4.19
), let us write

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{v}\delta\tilde{z}_{sr}\otimes dx_{r}=\tilde{z}_{s}^{\prime}\otimes x^{2}_{sv}+R^{\tilde{z}x}_{sv}.

Since we have obtained that (
4.14
) holds for
R^{\tilde{z}x}
, for every
s\leq u<v\leq t
we end up with

(4.21)

\Big|\int_{u}^{v}\delta\tilde{z}_{ur}\otimes dx_{r}\Big|\leq|R^{\tilde{z}x}_{uv}|+|\tilde{z}_{u}^{\prime}|\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}\\
\leq\omega^{\tilde{z}}(u,v)^{2/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{1/p}+\omega^{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}+\|\tilde{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,[u,v]}\,\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}.

We can now safely plug (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) (with
u
replaced by
s
) into relation (
4.19
). This yields the estimate

(4.22)

\Big|\int_{s}^{v}x^{1}_{sr}\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}\Big|\leq\omega^{\tilde{z}}(u,v)^{2/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{1/p}+\omega^{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}\\
+\|\tilde{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,[u,v]}\cdot\omega^{x}(u,v)^{2/p}+\omega^{x}(s,v)^{1/p}\omega_{1}^{\tilde{z}}(s,v)^{1/p}.

Let us now return to relation (
4.16
). By a simple application of (
4.10
) one discovers that

\displaystyle|\delta R^{z\tilde{z}}_{usv}|\leq\omega^{z}(u,v)^{2/p}\cdot\omega_{1}^{\tilde{z}}(u,v)^{1/p}+\omega^{z^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\cdot\Big|\int_{s}^{v}x^{1}_{sr}\otimes d\tilde{z}_{r}\Big|.

Inserting (
4.22
) into this relation, we thus obtain

(4.23)

\Big|\delta R^{z\tilde{z}}_{usv}\Big|\leq K_{p}\left(\omega^{z}(u,v)^{2/p}+\omega^{z^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\omega^{x}(u,v)^{1/p}\right)\omega_{1}^{x,\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}\\
\times\left(\omega^{x,\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime}}(u,v)^{1/p}+\|\tilde{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,[u,v]}+1\right).

Note that the right-hand side of (
4.23
) is equal to

K_{p}\omega^{R}(u,v)
defined in (
4.15
).
Taking (
4.13
) into account,
another use of Lemma
2.4

(together with an application of
[
16
, Exercise 1.9 (iii)]
to show that
{\omega}^{R}
is a control),
proves our claim
(
4.14
).
∎

4.3.
Interpolation of the Euler method

In this section we shall extend our Euler scheme to a process in continuous time and obtain some uniform bounds. Specifically, recall that the Euler approximation
y^{n}
is defined on
\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
by (
1.2
) and for convenience we will take
V_{0}\equiv 0
. For
t
in the continuous interval
[0,T]
we shall use the following interpolation:

(4.24)

\displaystyle\delta y^{n}_{t_{k}t}=

\displaystyle V(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\delta x_{t_{k}t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial V_{j}V_{j}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})(t-t_{k})^{2H},\qquad t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}].

Recall that
{\bf x}=S_{2}(x)=(x^{1},x^{2})
denotes the rough path above
x
(as given in Definition
2.1
).

In the sequel we will also need some continuous interpolations of the processes
q
and
q^{b}
, which had been defined on the grid in (
2.20
). Namely
for
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
such that
t_{k_{1}}\leq s<t_{k_{1}+1}<\cdots<t_{k_{2}-1}\leq t<t_{k_{2}}
,
we define

(4.25)

\displaystyle q_{st}^{ij}=\sum_{k_{1}\leq k<k_{2}}\left(x_{t_{k}\vee s,t_{k+1}\wedge t}^{2,ij}-\frac{1}{2}(t_{k+1}\wedge t-t_{k}\vee s)^{2H}\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}\right)

(4.26)

\displaystyle q^{b,ij}_{st}=\sum_{k_{1}\leq k<k_{2}}\left(b_{t_{k}\vee s,t_{k+1}\wedge t}^{2,ij}-\frac{1}{2}(t_{k+1}\wedge t-t_{k}\vee s)^{2H}\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}\right).

With the above definition (
4.25
) in hand, we will also extend the definition of
\omega
from the grid to
[0,T]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Recall that
\omega
has been defined on
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)
by (
2.23
)
for a fixed partition length parameter
n
, and that we are considering
p<3
.
We now extend
\omega
to
{\mathcal{S}}([0,T])
by setting:

(4.27)

\displaystyle\omega(s,t)=\|{\bf w}\|_{p\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p}+\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p/2}+\|q^{b}\|_{p/2\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p/2}+|t-s|.

Then

\omega
is a control on
[0,T]
. In other words,
\omega
is super-additive, continuous and vanishes on the diagonal.

Proof.

We first note that

(4.28)

\displaystyle\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p/2}\leq\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};\llbracket s,t\rrbracket}^{p/2}+\|{\bf w}\|_{p\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p}+|t-s|,

and the same kind of inequality holds true for
q^{b}
.
Therefore
\omega(s,t)
is finite almost surely.
Thanks to the definition (
4.27
) of
\omega
it is also readily checked that the superadditivity and zero on the diagonal properties hold true.

It remains to show the continuity of
\omega
. To this aim, taking into account the definition (
4.27
) of
\omega
, it is easily seen that we only have to focus on the increments
q
and
q^{b}
in (
4.25
)-(
4.26
). Moreover
q
and
q^{b}
are handled exactly in the same way. Hence we will just focus our attention on
\tilde{\omega}
given by

(4.29)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(s,t):=\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};[s,t]}^{p/2}\,.

Take
s<u<t<\eta(u)+\Delta
, where we recall from Notation
1.2
that
\eta(u)
is the largest
t_{k}\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
such that
t_{k}\leq u
.
In the following we show that
\tilde{\omega}(s,t)-\tilde{\omega}(s,u)\to 0
as
t-u\to 0
, which is one of the main steps towards the continuity of
\tilde{\omega}
.

Owing to the definition (
4.29
) of
\tilde{\omega}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can find a partition of
[s,t]
, denoted by
s=v_{0}<\cdots<v_{N}=t
such that

(4.30)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(s,t)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|q_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}|^{p/2}+\varepsilon\,.

Suppose that
u\in[v_{i_{0}},v_{i_{0}+1}]:=[v,v^{\prime}]
.
Then we can bound the summation in (
4.30
) by the following

(4.31)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{i_{0}-1}|q_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}|^{p/2}+|q_{vv^{\prime}}|^{p/2}+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}|q_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}|^{p/2}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(s,v)+|q_{vv^{\prime}}|^{p/2}+\tilde{\omega}(v^{\prime},t)+\varepsilon.

Now go back to (
4.28
) for
v^{\prime}
and
t
and pick
u
close enough to
t
so that
v^{\prime}\in[u,t]
satisfies
\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};\llbracket v^{\prime},t\rrbracket}=0
(since our grid
\llbracket u^{\prime},t\rrbracket
has fixed mesh
T/n
, this is easily seen when
u\to t
, owing to our expression (
4.25
)). One can thus recast (
4.28
) as

(4.32)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(v^{\prime},t):=\|q\|_{p/2\text{-var};[v^{\prime},t]}^{p/2}\leq\|{\bf w}\|_{p\text{-var};[v^{\prime},t]}^{p}+|t-v^{\prime}|.

It is then easily seen from (
4.32
) that
\lim_{v^{\prime}\to t}\tilde{\omega}(v^{\prime},t)=0
. Since
u<v^{\prime}<t
, we will pick
u
close enough to
t
so that
\tilde{\omega}(v^{\prime},t)\leq\varepsilon
. Plugging this information into (
4.31
), we obtain

(4.33)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(s,t)\leq\tilde{\omega}(s,v)+|q_{vv^{\prime}}|^{p/2}+2\varepsilon.

In addition, if
u\to t
we also have
|v^{\prime}-u|\to 0
. Therefore, basic continuity properties of
q
ensure that
\big||q_{vv^{\prime}}|^{p/2}-|q_{vu}|^{p/2}\big|\leq\varepsilon
if
u
is close enough to
t
. Hence (
4.33
) becomes

\tilde{\omega}(s,t)\leq\tilde{\omega}(s,v)+|q_{vu}|^{p/2}+3\varepsilon\leq\tilde{\omega}(s,u)+3\varepsilon,

where we have used the super-additivity property of
\tilde{\omega}
for the second inequality. Since
\tilde{\omega}(s,t)\geq\tilde{\omega}(s,u)
by monotonicity properties, we have obtained

(4.34)

\displaystyle|\tilde{\omega}(s,t)-\tilde{\omega}(s,u)|\leq 3\varepsilon,

for all
(s,u,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{3}([0,T])
such that
|t-u|
is sufficiently small. Since
\varepsilon
in (
4.34
) can be arbitrarily small, this proves that

\lim_{u\to t}\tilde{\omega}(s,u)=\tilde{\omega}(s,t)
. The same kind of arguments also show that

\lim_{u\to s}\tilde{\omega}(u,t)=\tilde{\omega}(s,t)
, which completes our proof.
∎

We now go back to the interpolated version of our Euler scheme
y^{n}
. In the following we show that
(y^{n},x,b)
is a rough path, which is an important step in the convergence analysis.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider the interpolated Euler scheme introduced in (
4.24
). Recall that
x
is our driving fBm and
b
is another fBm with parameter
H>1/3
, independent of
x
. Also recall that the augmented process
\emph{w}=(x,b)
has been introduced in (
2.22
). We assume that the vector field
V
sits in
C^{4}_{b}
. Then the following holds true.

(i)

Denote by
Z
the couple
Z=(y^{n},\emph{w})
. Then
Z
admits a lift
S_{2}(Z)
according to Definition
2.1
. Moreover, recalling the sets
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
in (
2.25
), consider
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
. Then for all
(s,t)\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s_{j},s_{j+1}])
we have the following uniform bound in
n
:

(4.35)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(Z)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\leq K\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p},

where
p>1/H
,
K
is a constant depending on
V
and where the control
\omega
is defined in (
4.27
).

(ii)

Under the setting of Theorem
2.10
, we now consider the vector-valued stochastic process

\tilde{Z}=(y^{n},D_{r}y^{n},D_{r^{\prime}}D_{r}y^{n},\emph{w})
for
r,r^{\prime}\in[0,T]
.
We still assume that
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
with
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
. Then we have (still uniformly in
n
):

(4.36)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(\tilde{Z})\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\leq K\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\cdot{\mathcal{G}}^{2},

where the quantities
{\mathcal{G}}
have been introduced in (
2.30
).

Proof.

We first prove (i).
Take
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
such that
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
. Theorem
2.10
, applied for
L=0
, shows that for
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s_{j},s_{j+1}\rrbracket)
we have

(4.37)

\displaystyle|\delta y_{st}^{n}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{1/p}|S_{0}\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}|\quad\text{and}\quad|\delta y_{st}^{n}-V(y_{s}^{n})\delta x_{st}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{2/p}\,,

where
\omega
is the control given in (
4.27
). Using standard interpolation methods, it can be shown in a straightforward way that the relation for
|\delta y^{n}_{st}|
in (
4.37
) still holds if
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s_{j},s_{j+1}])
. So in order to prove (
4.35
)
it remains to show that
\int_{s}^{t}y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}
and
\int_{s}^{t}y^{n}_{su}\otimes dy^{n}_{u}
are bounded by
\omega(s,t)^{2/p}
.

Consider the following remainder process for
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
such that
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
:

(4.38)

\displaystyle R_{st}=\int_{s}^{t}\left(\delta y^{n}_{su}-V(y^{n}_{s})\delta x_{su}\right)\otimes d\text{w}_{u}.

Note that
R
is a remainder of the form
R^{y^{n}w}
, defined as in (
4.12
). In order to apply Lemma
4.5
to this remainder, we need to check that
R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}\leq\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})^{3/p}
as in (
4.13
). Now according to (
4.38
) we have

(4.39)

\displaystyle R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(\delta y^{n}_{t_{k}u}-V(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\delta x_{t_{k}u}\right)\otimes d\text{w}_{u}.

Furthermore, owing to our interpolation formula (
4.24
), for all
u\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
we have

\displaystyle\delta y^{n}_{t_{k}u}-V(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\delta x_{t_{k}u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial V_{j}V_{j}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})(u-t_{k})^{2H}.

Reporting this identity into (
4.39
), we end up with

(4.40)

\displaystyle R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial V_{j}V_{j}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\otimes\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}(u-t_{k})^{2H}d\text{w}_{u}.

The stochastic integral in the right-hand side of (
4.40
) can be interpreted in the Young sense, and it is easy to see that
|R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|\lesssim\omega(t_{k},t_{k+1})^{3/p}
,
where
\omega
is still the control introduced in (
4.27
). This proves (
4.13
) for the remainder
R
, and therefore one can safely apply Lemma
4.5
in order to get that, provided
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
,

(4.41)

\displaystyle|R_{st}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{3/p}\qquad\text{ for }(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s_{j},s_{j+1}\rrbracket).

Going back to (
4.38
), observe that we have

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}=V(y^{n}_{s})\int_{s}^{t}\delta x_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}+R_{st}.

Invoking (
4.41
) and since
x
is part of the rough path
w
, we easily get

(4.42)

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{s}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}\Big|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{2/p},\qquad\text{ for }(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket s_{j},s_{j+1}\rrbracket).

In the following we extend the above estimate for
\int_{s}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{su}d\text{w}_{u}
to any
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s_{j},s_{j+1}])
. That is we take
(s,t)
such that
s\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
and
t\in[t_{k^{\prime}},t_{k^{\prime}+1}]
. We write

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t_{k+1}}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}+\int_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k^{\prime}}}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}+\int_{t_{k^{\prime}}}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t_{k+1}}\delta y^{n}_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}+\delta y^{n}_{st_{k+1}}\otimes\delta\text{w}_{t_{k+1}t_{k^{\prime}}}+\int_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k^{\prime}}}\delta y^{n}_{t_{k+1}u}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}

\displaystyle\quad+\delta y^{n}_{st_{k^{\prime}}}\otimes\delta\text{w}_{t_{k^{\prime}}t}+\int_{t_{k^{\prime}}}^{t}\delta y^{n}_{t_{k^{\prime}}u}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}=:\sum_{i=1}^{5}I_{i}.

Let us bound the terms
I_{1},\ldots,I_{5}
above. First it follows from (
4.42
) that
|I_{3}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{2/p}
. It is also clear that
|I_{2}|
and
|I_{4}|
are bounded by the same estimate
\omega(s,t)^{2/p}
.
In order to bound
I_{1}
, observe that according to our interpolation formula (
4.24
) we have

(4.43)

I_{1}=V(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\int_{s}^{t_{k+1}}\delta x_{su}\otimes d\text{w}_{u}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial V_{j}V_{j}(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\otimes\int_{s}^{t_{k+1}}\left[(u-t_{k})^{2H}-(s-t_{k})^{2H}\right]d\text{w}_{u}.

It is clear that the first integral in (
4.43
) is bounded by
\omega(s,t_{k+1})^{2/p}
. Note also that
(u-t_{k})^{2H}-(s-t_{k})^{2H}\leq(u-s)^{2H}
. So applying Young’s inequality we obtain that the second integral in (
4.43
) is bounded by
(t_{k+1}-s)^{2H}\omega(s,t_{k+1})^{1/p}
. Combining these two estimates we obtain that
|I_{1}|\lesssim\omega(s,t_{k+1})^{2/p}
. The term
I_{5}
is bounded in the similar way. Putting together our upper bounds on
I_{1}
, …,
I_{5}
, we have thus obtained that (
4.42
) holds for any
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s_{j},s_{j+1}])
.
Summarizing our considerations so far, we have proved that

(4.44)

\displaystyle\|M^{1}\|_{p\text{-var};[s,t]}\leq K\omega(s,t)\qquad\text{with }M_{uv}^{1}:=\int_{u}^{v}\delta y^{n}_{ur}\otimes d\text{w}_{r}.

The proof is now finished along the same arguments. Namely the increment
M_{uv}^{2}:=\int_{u}^{v}\delta y^{n}_{ur}\otimes dy^{n}_{ur}
can be bounded similarly to
M^{1}
, leading to the same inequality as (
4.44
). This proves our claim (
4.35
).

Eventually (
4.36
) is also obtained along the same lines. We just apply Theorem
2.10
with
L=1,2

in the arguments, and also write
{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\omega(s,t)
instead of
\omega(s,t)
in all inequalities for the remainders. This completes our proof.
∎

4.4.
Integrability of some linear equations

Our convergence estimates are based on linearization procedures. In this section we bound some related linear differential equations. We start by defining the objects we wish to study.

Definition 4.8
.

Recall that every
V^{i}
has to be seen, for
i=1,\dots,m
, as a smooth vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
y^{n}
be the interpolated scheme (
4.24
). Then for
i=1,\dots,m
we define an averaged
\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
-valued process
\tilde{V}^{i}(t)=\{\tilde{V}_{ji^{\prime}}^{i}(t);\,j=1,\dots,d,\,i^{\prime}=1,\dots,m\}
. This process is indexed by
t\in[0,T]
and is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{ji^{\prime}}^{i}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{i^{\prime}}V^{i}_{j}(\theta y_{t}+(1-\theta)y^{n}_{t})d\theta.

We also define a
(\mathbb{R}^{d\times m})^{m}
-valued process as
\tilde{V}(t)=(\tilde{V}^{1}(t),\dots,\tilde{V}^{m}(t))
.

We are now ready to define the linear equation we wish to analyze.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\tilde{V}
be the
(\mathbb{R}^{d\times m})^{m}
-valued process introduced in Definition
4.8
. We will call
{\Gamma}
the
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
-valued solution to the following systems of equations on
[0,T]
:

(4.45)

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}^{ii^{\prime}}=\text{Id}_{ii^{\prime}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{V}^{i}_{ji^{\prime\prime}}(s){\Gamma}_{s}^{i^{\prime\prime}i^{\prime}}dx_{s}^{j},\quad\text{for}\quad i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

For conciseness we will simply write (
4.45
) as
{\Gamma}_{t}=\text{Id}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{V}(s){\Gamma}_{s}\,dx_{s}
.
We also denote by
{\Lambda}
the inverse of
{\Gamma}
, namely
{\Lambda}
is defined by the relation
{\Lambda}_{t}{\Gamma}_{t}\equiv\text{Id}
.

Our next lemma presents an important estimate for the processes
{\Gamma}
and
{\Lambda}
defined above.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let the assumption be as in Theorem
2.10
.
Let
p>\frac{1}{H}
and
q
be such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1
.
Let
{\Gamma}
and
{\Lambda}
be defined in (
4.45
). Then

(a)

For all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
we have

(4.46)

|\delta{\Gamma}_{st}|+\sup_{r\in[0,T]}|D_{r}(\delta{\Gamma}_{st})|+\sup_{r,r^{\prime}\in[0,T]}|D_{rr^{\prime}}^{2}(\delta{\Gamma}_{st})|\\
\leq K\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\cdot|S_{0}\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}|\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{0}\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cdot\exp({\mathcal{N}}),

where
\omega
is defined in (
4.27
) and
{\mathcal{N}}
is some random variable such that
{\mathcal{N}}^{1/q}
has a Gaussian tail. The relation still holds when
{\Gamma}
is replaced by
{\Lambda}
.

(b)
Both processes
{\Gamma}
and
{\Lambda}
and their Malliavin derivatives are uniformly integrable. Precisely, for all
p\geq 1
we have we have

(4.47)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N},r,r^{\prime},t\in[0,T]}\left(|{\Gamma}_{t}|^{p}+|D_{r}({\Gamma}_{t})|^{p}+|D_{rr^{\prime}}^{2}({\Gamma}_{t})|^{p}\right)\right]<\infty.

Proof.

Applying Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.4
to the right-hand side of (
4.46
) we conclude the integrability relation in (
4.47
). It thus remains to prove relation (
4.46
).

In the following we prove the estimate (
4.46
) for
{\Gamma}
. Note that due to the fact that the initial condition
Id
in (
4.45
) is nondegenerate, the process
{\Lambda}
is well-defined and satisfies a differential equation which is very similar to
{\Gamma}
(see e.g.
[
7
,
22
]
for more details). Therefore the estimate of
{\Lambda}
in (
4.46
) can be obtained by following the same steps as for
{\Gamma}
. The proof for
{\Lambda}
and its derivatives is thus omitted.

With our Definition
4.8
in mind,
let us first introduce an auxiliary process
\xi
given, for
i,i^{\prime\prime}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
t\in[0,T]
, by

\displaystyle\xi_{t}^{ii^{\prime\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{V}^{i}_{ji^{\prime\prime}}(s)dx_{s}^{j}.

We now separate the estimates for
\xi
into two different cases

(i)

Case
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
such that
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
.

In this situation, since
(y^{n},x,b)
is a rough path (see Lemma
4.7
(i)) and
y
is a process controlled by
x
, it is readily checked that for all
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
such that
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
we have:

(4.48)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(\xi)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\leq K\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p}.

Furthermore, note that one can recast equation (
4.45
) as a linear system of the form

(4.49)

\displaystyle d{\Gamma}_{t}^{ii^{\prime}}=\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}=1}^{m}{\Gamma}_{t}^{i^{\prime\prime}i^{\prime}}d\xi_{t}^{ii^{\prime\prime}}.

Observe that the path
\xi
is a functional of the process
Z
introduced in Lemma
4.7
. Now
we recall from
[
16
, Theorem 10.53]
that for a linear equation like (
4.49
), there exist two constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that

(4.50)

\displaystyle|S_{2}({\Gamma},Z)_{st}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}|{\Gamma}_{s}|\cdot\|S_{2}(\xi)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\cdot\exp(C_{2}\|S_{2}(\xi)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}|{\Gamma}_{s}|\cdot\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\exp(C_{2}\omega(s,t)^{1/p}),

where the second relation stems from (
4.48
). In addition, we have chosen
s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}
. Therefore, one can simplify (
4.50
) and obtain that for any
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([s_{j},s_{j+1}])
,

(4.51)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}({\Gamma},Z)\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\leq K\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\cdot|{\Gamma}_{s}|\,.

(ii)

Case
(s,t)\subset(s_{j},s_{j+1})
such that
s_{j}\in S_{2}
.

For
s_{j}\in S_{2}
equation (
4.45
) is a linear equation driven by
x
and so we can apply the integrability result in
[
8
]
to get

(4.52)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}({\Gamma})\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\leq K|{\Gamma}_{s}|\cdot\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}\cdot\exp({\mathcal{N}}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}),

where
{\mathcal{N}}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}
is a random variable such that, denoting
{\mathcal{N}}:=\sum_{s_{j}\in S_{2}}{\mathcal{N}}_{s_{j}s_{j+1}}
, the random variable
\exp(K\cdot{\mathcal{N}})
is integrable for any constant
K>0
.

Our estimate (
4.46
) for
\delta{\Gamma}_{st}
is now easily obtained. Namely we iterate
(
4.51
)-(
4.52
). This yields

\displaystyle|\delta{\Gamma}_{st}|\leq K\left(\sum_{s_{j}\in S_{0}\cup S_{1}}\omega(s_{j},s_{j+1})^{1/p}+\sum_{s_{j}\in S_{2}}\|{\bf x}\|_{p\text{-var};[s_{j},s_{j+1}]}\right)\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{0}\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cdot\exp({\mathcal{N}}).

Finally taking into account the definition of
S_{i}
,
i=0,1,2
it follows that

\displaystyle|\delta{\Gamma}_{st}|\leq K\omega(s,t)^{1/p}\cdot|S_{0}\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}|\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{0}\cdot{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cdot\exp({\mathcal{N}}).

Namely we have proved (
4.46
) for
{\Gamma}
. It remains to upper bound the Malliavin derivatives of
{\Gamma}
.

Recall that
{\Gamma}
satisfies equation (
4.45
), with
\tilde{V}
given in Definition
4.8
. For sake of clarity, the remainder of our computations will be done assuming that all our quantities are real-valued (we will therefore drop the indices from our next equations). Moreover according to our standing assumptions, the process
\tilde{V}
is Malliavin differentiable. Hence using standard arguments for the differentiation of rough differential equations (see
[
6
,
7
,
25
,
31
]
) we get that
D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}
satisfies the linear equation:

(4.53)

\displaystyle D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{V}(s){\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(r){\Gamma}_{r}+\int_{r}^{t}\partial\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r}y^{n}_{s}){\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}+\int_{r}^{t}\tilde{V}(s)D_{r}{\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}.

Therefore one can use the variation of constant method, similarly to
[
25
, equation (2.7)]
, in order to get the following representation for
D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}
:

(4.54)

\displaystyle D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}={\Gamma}_{t}^{r}\tilde{V}(r){\Gamma}_{r}+{\Gamma}_{t}^{r}\int_{r}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}^{r}\partial\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r}y^{n}_{s}){\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s},

where
\{{\Gamma}^{r}_{t};\,t\in[r,T]\}
is the solution of equation (
4.49
) such that
{\Gamma}_{r}^{r}=\text{Id}
and
{\Lambda}_{t}^{r}
is the inverse of
{\Gamma}^{r}_{t}
. Note that because
{\Gamma}
and
{\Gamma}^{r}
satisfy the same equation with different initials, the estimate of
{\Gamma}
in (
4.52
) also holds for
{\Gamma}^{r}
.
In order to estimate
D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}
, it thus remains to get the estimate (
4.46
) for the integral

(4.55)

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}^{r}\partial\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r}y^{n}_{s}){\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}

in (
4.54
). Recall that

{\Gamma}
is the solution of the linear system (
4.49
) driven by
Z
, where we recall that
Z=(y^{n},\text{w})
.
According to (
4.36
)
(D_{r}y,D_{r}y^{n},Z)
is a rough path.
So
{\Gamma}
can also be considered as the solution of a linear system driven by
(D_{r}y,D_{r}y^{n},Z)
.
Hence along the same line as for (
4.50
) we can estimate the quantity (
4.55
), and thus we obtain the bound (
4.46
) for
D_{r}{\Gamma}_{t}
.

We turn to the equation satisfied by
D^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}{\Gamma}
. Differentiating (
4.53
), we let the patient reader check that the second derivative verifies a linear equation of the form

(4.56)

\displaystyle D_{rr^{\prime}}^{2}{\Gamma}_{t}=D_{r^{\prime}}[\tilde{V}(r){\Gamma}_{r}]+D_{r}[\tilde{V}(r^{\prime}){\Gamma}_{r^{\prime}}]+{\mathcal{E}}_{rr^{\prime}}(t)+\int_{r\vee r^{\prime}}^{t}\tilde{V}(s)D^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}{\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s},

where the term
{\mathcal{E}}_{rr^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{rr^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r\vee r^{\prime}}^{t}\partial\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r}y^{n}_{s})D_{r^{\prime}}{\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{r\vee r^{\prime}}^{t}\partial\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r^{\prime}}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r^{\prime}}y^{n}_{s})D_{r}{\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{r\vee r^{\prime}}^{t}\partial^{2}\tilde{V}(s)(\theta D_{r^{\prime}}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r^{\prime}}y^{n}_{s})(\theta D_{r}y_{s}+(1-\theta)D_{r}y^{n}_{s}){\Gamma}_{s}dx_{s}.

It is clear that the process
D_{rr^{\prime}}^{2}{\Gamma}
satisfies a linear equation system analogous to
(
4.53
). The estimate can thus be obtained by following the same arguments as above, invoking again
[
25
]
. This completes the proof of (

).
∎

4.5.
A decomposition of the error process

In
[
28
, equations (6.14) and (7.6)]
, we have decomposed the error process
y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}
according to the Jacobian of the equation and some remainder terms. In the following proposition we get a similar decomposition, adapted to our needs for the weak convergence estimates. Notice that similarly to what we did in Section
4.4
, we will drop the indices from our formulae below for sake of readability.

Lemma 4.11
.

We work under the conditions of Lemma
4.7
.
Let
y
and
y^{n}
be the solutions of equation (
2.7
) and the Euler scheme (
4.24
), respectively.
Let
{\Gamma}
and
{\Lambda}
be respectively the solution of equation
(
4.45
) and its inverse
{\Lambda}={\Gamma}^{-1}
.
We set
\eta(s)=t_{k}
for
s\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
.
For
t\in[0,T]
we also define

(4.57)

\displaystyle I_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial V\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H}dx_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}\int_{\eta(s)}^{u}\partial^{2}V(y^{n}_{v})dy^{n}_{v}dy^{n}_{u}\right)dx_{s}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{t}^{1}+I_{t}^{2}.

Then the difference

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{5}J_{t}^{e},

where the processes

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}({\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{\eta(s)})\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s),s}\,dx_{s}

\displaystyle J_{t}^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}\,dI_{s}\,,

and where

\displaystyle J_{t}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-H\cdot{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}({\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{\eta(s)})\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}\,ds

\displaystyle J_{t}^{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-H\cdot{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{\eta(s)}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}\,ds.

Proof.

We first recall that the continuous time Euler scheme defined in (

\displaystyle\delta y^{n}_{\eta(s),s}=V(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s),s}+\frac{1}{2}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H},

-H\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}\,ds.

Plugging

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(V(y^{n}_{s})-V(y^{n}_{\eta(s)}))dx_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial V(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\left(V(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s),s}+\frac{1}{2}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H}\right)dx_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}\int_{\eta(s)}^{u}\partial^{2}V(y^{n}_{v})dy^{n}_{v}dy^{n}_{u}\right)dx_{s},

Recalling the definition of
I_{t}^{1}
,
I_{t}^{2}
in (
4.57

)

We now decompose

in (

\displaystyle V(y_{t})-V(y^{n}_{t})=\int_{0}^{1}\partial V(\theta y_{t}+(1-\theta)y^{n}_{t})d\theta\cdot(y_{t}-y^{n}_{t})=\tilde{V}(t)\cdot(y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}),

where we recall that the process

We are ready to plug (

4.65
) into (
4.61

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{V}(s)\cdot(y_{s}-y^{n}_{s})dx_{s}+K_{t},

where the process

(4.67)

\displaystyle K_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s)s}dx_{s}+I^{1}_{t}+I^{2}_{t}-H\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}ds.

{\Gamma}

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}={\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}\,dK_{s}.

Thanks to our expression (

), the above equation can be written more explicitly as

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{n}_{t}={\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s)s}dx_{s}+{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}d(I^{1}_{s}+I^{2}_{s})

(4.68)

\displaystyle-H\cdot{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{s}\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}ds.

With relation (
4.5

Also, in the same equation, by plugging the decomposition

4.6.
The weak convergence of the Euler scheme

We can now gather all the previous preliminary estimates in order to obtain our main result. This is summarized in the theorem below.

Theorem 4.12
.

Consider a vector field
V\in C^{4}_{b}
and a driving fBm
x
with Hurst parameter
H>1/3
. Let
y
be the solution of the rough differential equation (
2.7
). The corresponding interpolated Euler scheme is
y^{n}
, displayed in (
4.24
).
Then
for any
f\in C^{4}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in[0,T]
there is a constant
C>0
independent of
n
such that

(4.69)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}f(y^{n}_{t})-\mathbb{E}f(y_{t})\right|\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

For conciseness we will prove the theorem for the case
V_{0}\equiv 0
only. The general case can be considered in the similar way and is left to the patient reader.

Let
f
be a generic
C_{b}^{4}
function. For
t\in[0,T]
we define an interpolated process

(4.70)

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial f({\lambda}y_{t}+(1-{\lambda})y^{n}_{t})d{\lambda}.

Here note that in order to alleviate notations, we still drop indices and perform our computation as if our quantities were real-valued. Next a simple application of the fundamental theorem of calculus plus Lemma
4.11
reveal that

(4.71)

\displaystyle f(y_{t})-f(y^{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)\cdot(y_{t}-y^{n}_{t})=\sum_{e=1}^{5}f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{e}.

The remainder of the proof is dedicated to estimate the five terms in the right-hand side of (
4.71
). For sake of conciseness we prove (
4.69
) for
t\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
only. The proof for
t\in[0,T]
follows the same lines and is left to patient reader.

Step 1: Estimating
J_{t}^{1}
and
J_{t}^{5}
.

In this step, we consider the first and fifth term in (
4.71
).
Note that the integrals in the expressions for
J_{t}^{1}
and
J_{t}^{5}
are in fact discrete sums. We can thus combine those two terms in order to get:

(4.72)

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)(J_{t}^{1}+J_{t}^{5})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{k}<t}f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\left(x^{2}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}\right).

Let us say a few words about the term
\psi_{k}\equiv x^{2}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}
in the right-hand side of (
4.72
). First we highlight again the fact that we are performing 1-d type computations in order to simplify notation. In a
d
-dim setting we would consider random variables of the form

\displaystyle\psi^{ij}_{k}=x^{2,ij}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}},\quad\text{for}\quad i,j\in\{1,\dots,d\}.

Here we will just focus on the terms
\psi_{k}\equiv\psi^{ii}_{k}
, which are the most demanding ones. We leave the off-diagonal terms
\psi^{ij}_{k}
to the patient reader for sake of conciseness. Next we should also have in mind the fact that
\psi_{k}
can be written as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2H}H_{2}\left(\frac{(\delta x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{2}}{\Delta^{2H}}\right),

where
H_{2}
stands for the Hermite polynomial
H_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1
. Invoking
[
30
, Page 23]

we thus get

\displaystyle\psi_{k}=\delta^{\diamond,2}({\beta}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}),

where
{\beta}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}
is defined by (
4.8
) and
\delta^{\diamond,2}
stands for a double Skorohod integral (see Section
2.3
for Malliavin calculus notation). Hence applying twice the integration by parts (
2.17
), we end up with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[f_{1}(t)(J_{t}^{1}+J_{t}^{5})\Big]=\sum_{t_{k}<t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle D^{2}\big[f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\big],{\beta}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes 2}}\right]\,,

where recall that
{\beta}
is defined in (
4.8
).
Applying Lemma
4.3
with
\varphi
given by

\varphi=D^{2}\big[f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\big]\,,

and recalling that
f_{1}
is the process in (
4.70
),
we obtain

(4.73)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[f_{1}(t)(J_{t}^{1}+J_{t}^{5})\Big]\Big|\leq\sum_{t_{k}<t}n^{-4H}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|D^{2}\big[f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\big]\right\|_{\infty}\right]\,.

The integrability results Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
4.10
(b) guarantee the uniform integrability in
n
of the sup-norm in the inequality (
4.73
). Therefore, we have the estimate

(4.74)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[f_{1}(t)(J_{t}^{1}+J_{t}^{5})\Big]\Big|\leq C\sum_{t_{k}<t}n^{-4H}=Cn^{1-4H}.

Step 2: Estimating
J_{t}^{2}
.

We turn to the estimate of

\displaystyle J_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}\Lambda_{u}\tilde{V}(u)dx_{u}\Big)\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\delta x_{\eta(s)s}dx_{s}.

Now let us write

\displaystyle\Lambda_{u}\tilde{V}(u)=\big(\Lambda_{u}\tilde{V}(u)-\Lambda_{\eta(s)}\tilde{V}(\eta(s))\big)+\Lambda_{\eta(s)}\tilde{V}(\eta(s)).

Reporting this relation into our expression (

\displaystyle J_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{t_{k}<t}{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\tilde{V}(t_{k})\delta x_{t_{k}s}\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\delta x_{t_{k}s}dx_{s}=:J_{t}^{21}+J_{t}^{22}.

We now proceed to the analysis of

In order to bound the term

\displaystyle J_{t}^{22}=-\sum_{t_{k}<t}\underbrace{{\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda_{t_{k}}\tilde{V}(t_{k})\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})}_{=f_{t_{k}}}\cdot\underbrace{\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\delta x_{t_{k}s}\delta x_{t_{k}s}dx_{s}}_{=\delta g_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}}.

Moreover, according to (

In addition

It follows that gathering (

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{22}]|\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}\sum_{t_{k}<t}{\Lambda}_{t_{k}}\partial^{2}V(y_{t_{k}})\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}\int_{\eta(s)}^{u}dy^{n}_{v}dy^{n}_{u}dx_{s}.

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J^{32}_{t}]\Big|\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

In order to bound the term

with

\displaystyle J_{t}^{311}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Lambda}_{\eta(s)}\partial V\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H}dx_{s}

\displaystyle J_{t}^{312}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\Gamma}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}({\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{\eta(s)})\partial V\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H}dx_{s}\,.

Note that by applying Lemma
2.5
we can bound
\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{311}]
by
\frac{1}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}
. Indeed, we can write

(4.91)

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{311}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\sum_{0\leq t_{k}<t}{\Lambda}_{t_{k}}\partial V\partial VV(y^{n}_{t_{k}})\cdot\nu_{t_{k},t_{k+1}\wedge t},

where the increment
\nu

(4.92)

\displaystyle\nu_{uv}=\int_{u}^{v}(s-\eta(s))^{2H}dx_{s}.

Next recall the following result from Lemma 4.6 in
[
28
]

x
with Hurst parameter

and

\|f\|_{{\gamma}}\in L^{p}

{\gamma}<H
and

, we have

(4.93)

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\Big|\sum_{0\leq t_{k}<t}f_{t_{k}}\nu_{t_{k},t_{k+1}\wedge t}\Big|^{p}\right]\right\}^{1/p}\leq\frac{CT}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

One can apply directly this estimate to (
4.91
) in order to get

(4.94)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{311}]\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}}.

The term
J^{312}_{t}
has to be compared to
J^{21}_{t}
in (
4.76
). We can thus follow some computations which are very similar to (
4.82
)-(
4.6

x

(4.95)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{312}]\leq\frac{C}{n^{4H-1-\varepsilon}},

similarly to (
4.86
) and (

We can thus conclude this step by gathering (
4.94
) and (
4.95

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{3}]|\leq Cn^{1-4H+\varepsilon}.

Step 4: Estimating
J_{t}^{4}

We now turn to an estimate of the term
J^{4}_{t}
in Lemma
4.11
. According to the expression therein and equation (
4.45
) for
{\Lambda}
, observe that

(4.97)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(t)J_{t}^{4}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-H\cdot\int_{0}^{t}Q^{t}_{s}\cdot(s-\eta(s))^{2H-1}ds,

where the quantity

is given by

\displaystyle Q_{s}^{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(t)\left(\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}{\Gamma}_{t}{\Lambda}_{u}\tilde{V}(u)^{T}dx_{u}\right)\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\right].

As for
J_{t}^{31}

(4.98)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(t){\Gamma}_{t}\left(\int_{\eta(s)}^{s}{\Lambda}_{u}\tilde{V}(u)^{T}dx_{u}\right)\partial VV(y^{n}_{\eta(s)})\right]\right|\leq\frac{C}{n^{2H-\varepsilon}}.

Indeed, by writing and substituting
