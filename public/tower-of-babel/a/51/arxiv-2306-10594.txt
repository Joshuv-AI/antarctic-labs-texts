A nonparametric test for elliptical distribution based on kernel embedding of probabilities
Yin Tang, Bing Li

Abstract
Elliptical distribution is a basic assumption underlying many multivariate statistical methods. For example, in sufficient dimension reduction and statistical graphical models, this assumption is routinely imposed to simplify the data dependence structure. Before applying such methods, we need to decide whether the data are elliptically distributed. Currently existing tests either focus exclusively on spherical distributions, or rely on bootstrap to determine the null distribution, or require specific forms of the alternative distribution. In this paper, we introduce a general nonparametric test for elliptical distribution based on kernel embedding of the probability measure that embodies the two properties that characterize an elliptical distribution: namely, after centering and rescaling, (1) the direction and length of the random vector are independent, and (2) the directional vector is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere. We derive the asymptotic distributions of the test statistic via von-Mises expansion, develop the sample-level procedure to determine the rejection region, and establish the consistency and validity of the proposed test. We also develop the concentration bounds of the test statistic, allowing the dimension to grow with the sample size, and further establish the consistency in this high-dimension setting. We compare our method with several existing methods via simulation studies, and apply our test to a SENIC dataset with and without a transformation aimed to achieve ellipticity.

Introduction

Elliptical distribution is a widely-used assumption for many statistical and machine learning methods for multivariate data. For example, in sufficient dimension reduction, the elliptical distribution assumption for the predictor is needed for moment-based methods such as Sliced Inverse Regression

(
Li 1991
)
, Ordinary Least Squares
(
Li and Duan 1989
)
, and Iterative Hessian Transformation
(
Cook and Li 2002
)
. See also
Li 2018
. When this assumption is violated, one needs either to perform data transformation or to modify the inverse-regression methods as in
Li and Dong 2009
. Another example is the statistical graphical model, where a class of methods, such as glasso
(
Yuan and Lin 2006
)
and the transelliptical graphical model
(
Liu, Han and
Zhang 2012
)
, require either a Gaussian or an elliptical distribution. See also
Vogel and Fried 2011
, which introduces a class of elliptical graphical models as a robust alternative to the Gaussian graphical model.

There are some existing tests on spherical distributions. For example,

Baringhaus 1991
proposes a test based on the
L^{2}
-distance between the empirical distribution of the data and the distribution function partially specified by the
spherical assumption.
Liang, Fang and
Hickernell 2008
introduces a necessary test by applying the Rosenblatt transformation on each element. The hypothesis in this test is necessary in the sense that it is implied by spherical distribution but does not imply the spherical distribution.
Henze, Hlávka and
Meintanis 2014
introduces a test by checking whether the characteristic function is constant over surfaces of spheres centered at the origin.
Kariya and Eaton 1977
introduces a robust test of a spherical distribution centered at the origin against an elliptical or a noncentered spherical distribution.
Koltchinskii and
Li 1998
uses the multivariate distribution and quantile functions to test spherical distributions with unknown centers. All tests above are for the spherical distributions where the covariance matrix is exactly the identity matrix, and most of them focus on the case when we know the center is zero. These methods do not yield direct extensions for testing elliptical distributions where both the mean vector and covariance matrix are unknown.

There also exist some tests on elliptical distributions.
Huffer and Park 2007
gives a test for multivariate normal and elliptical distribution of the data based on a chi-square statistic after slicing the data. For the multivariate normal distribution, they derive the asymptotic null distribution of their test; for the elliptical distribution, they propose a bootstrap test without giving a proof of its consistency and validity.
Albisetti, Balabdaoui and
Holzmann 2020
introduces a test based on a Kolmogorov-Smirnov type statistic and uses bootstrap to construct the null distribution.
Manzotti, Pérez and
Quiroz 2002
proposes a test on whether the standardized directional vector is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere, which is, again, only a necessary condition for spherical distribution.
Schott 2002
proposes a Wald-type test based on whether the 4th moments are consistent with an elliptical distribution. So its null hypothesis is not exactly the elliptical distribution. Furthermore, since it requires estimation of the 8th moments, it may not be robust.
Cassart, Hallin and
Paindaveine 2008
proposes a locally and asymptotically optimal Pseudo-Gaussian test for Fechner-type symmetry, which is wider than the class of elliptical distributions.

Babić et al. 2021
develops optimal tests for elliptical distributions against some generalized skew-elliptical alternatives. Some of the existing methods are summarized in
Babić, Ley and
Palangetić 2021
.

In this paper, we introduce a nonparametric test for elliptical distributions based on Hilbert-space embedding of a product probability measure that characterizes an elliptical distribution. The basic idea is the following. It is well known that a random vector
X
follows a spherical distribution centered at 0 if and only if

1.

its Euclidean norm
\|X\|
and its direction
X/\|X\|
are statistically independent;

2.

the direction vector
X/\|X\|
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere.

See, for example,
Anderson 2003
;
Paindaveine 2012
. This converts testing of sphericity to testing of the two conditions. Since an elliptical distribution can always be transformed into a spherical distribution by a linear map, we can further develop tests of ellipticity by testing the two conditions for the linearly transformed data. However, since the mean vector and covariance matrix need to be estimated, we have to take into account the estimation error in this step when deriving the asymptotic distribution.

More specifically, let
U=\|X\|
and
V=X/\|X\|
. If
X
has a spherical distribution, then the distribution
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,V}}
can be expressed by the product measure
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{V}}
, where
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{V}}
is a
known
distribution. We embed this distribution into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space as a cross-covariance operator and compare it against the kernel embedding of the fully empirical distribution. The norm of the difference should be small if
X
has a spherical distribution, and large otherwise. This is the core idea of our method. One side-note is that we may replace
V
by its polar coordinate representation, which will significantly simplify the computation. This procedure has several appealing features: first, the hypothesis is both necessary and sufficient, that is,
X
has a spherical distribution if and only if the distance is small; second, it is rather straightforward to go from spherical distribution to elliptical distribution by replacing
X
with its centered and rescaled version; third, since the test only involves functions of sample moments, its asymptotic null distribution can be relatively easily derived from the infinite-dimensional
\delta
-method, or the von-Mises expansion.

Probability embedding
(
Sriperumbudur, Fukumizu and
Lanckriet 2010
;
Sriperumbudur, Fukumizu and
Lanckriet 2011
)
is a powerful method that has been used in a variety of settings in statistics and machine learning, such as test of independence and the two-sample problem. See, for example,
Gretton et al. 2005
,
Gretton et al. 2007
,
Gretton et al. 2008
,
Gretton et al. 2009
, and
Gretton et al. 2012
. There is another type of tests of independence based on the distance covariance; see
Székely, Rizzo and
Bakirov 2007
and
Székely and
Rizzo 2009
among others.
Sejdinovic
et al. 2013
establishes the relation between these two types of tests of independence. Our test of elliptical distribution goes beyond the test of independence between
U
and
V
, as it must also incorporate the fact that the distribution of
V
is known.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we lay out the two characterizing properties of an elliptical distribution and construct the probability embedding into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space that embodies the two characterizing properties.
In Sections
3
and
4
, we introduce the test statistic based on the probability embedding and implement it numerically through coordinate mapping.
In Section
5
, for the fixed dimension, we derive the asymptotic null and alternative distributions of the test statistic via von-Mises expansion, and in Section
6
, we implement the asymptotic null distribution at the sample level, and establish the validity and consistency of our test.
In Section
7
, we derive the uniform concentration bounds for our test statistic allowing the dimension to grow with the sample size, and further establish the consistency of our test in this high-dimensional setting.
In Section
8
, we conduct simulation studies to demonstrate the usage and effectiveness of the proposed test.
In Section
9
, we apply our test to a data example.
Section
10
is devoted to some discussions on the choice of kernel functions.

Due to space limit, some technical lemmas, most of the proofs, and discussions of some theoretical results are placed in Appendix A; additional simulation comparisons are placed in Appendices B and C; further discussions on the choice of kernel functions are placed in Appendix D; and the scatter plot matrices of the dataset in Section
9
are placed in Appendix E. All Appendices are in the online Supplementary Material (
Tang and Li 2024
).
Our proposed method is implemented in the R package
KEPTED
(Kernel-Embedding-of-Probability Test for Elliptical Distribution).

2
Elliptical distribution and its kernel embedding

In this section we introduce the definition of the elliptical distribution and develop two equivalent conditions, one at the level of probability measures and the other at the level of linear operators. The sufficient condition at the operator level is the theoretical basis of our test.

2.1
Spherical and elliptical distributions

Let
(\Omega,{\cal{F}},P)
be a probability space, and
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
be a measurable space, where
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
, and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
. Let
X:\Omega\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
be a Borel random vector, and let
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}=P\mbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}$}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
be the distribution of
X
. Denote
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
. We say that
X
has a spherical distribution centered at
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
if

1.

P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is dominated by the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
with density
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}=dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}/d\lambda
;

2.

f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(x)
is a function of
(x-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}(x-\mu)
, that is,

f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(x)=h\left((x-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}(x-\mu)\right),

(2.1)

for some nonnegative function
h
satisfying

\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{-\infty}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\ldots\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{-\infty}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}h(x^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}x)dx_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\ldots dx_{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}=1.

(2.2)

Here,
\mu
is necessarily
EX
if
X
is integrable. Throughout,
\|\cdot\|
will denote the Euclidean norm (or
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
norm). Let
U=\|X-\mu\|
and
V=(X-\mu)/U
. By Theorem 1 of
Cambanis, Huang and
Simons 1981
,
X
has a spherical distribution if and only if
U\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,V
and
V
has a uniform distribution on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
, where

refers to independent throughout this paper. See also
Eaton 1986
and
Schmidt 2002
.

More generally,
X
is said to have an elliptical distribution centered at
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
with a positive definite shape parameter
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d\times d}}
if

1.

P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is dominated by the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
with density
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}=dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}/d\lambda
;

2.

the density of
X
is a function of
(x-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(x-\mu)
, that is,

f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(x)=|\Lambda|^{\scriptscriptstyle{-\frac{1}{2}}}h\left((x-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(x-\mu)\right),

for some nonnegative function
h
satisfying (
2.2
), where
|\Lambda|
denotes the determinant of
\Lambda
.

A direct corollary is that
Y=\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{-\frac{1}{2}}}(X-\mu)
has a spherical distribution centered at 0. Consequently, if we let

U=\sqrt{(X-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(X-\mu)},\quad V=\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(X-\mu)/U,

(2.3)

then
X
has an elliptical distribution with center
\mu
and shape parameter
\Lambda
if and only if
U\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,V
, and
V
has a uniform distribution on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
.

According to Theorem 2.7.2 of
Anderson 2003
, if the components of
X
are square-integrable, then

EX=\mu,\quad\mathrm{var}(X)=\frac{EU^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}{d}\Lambda.

Let
\Sigma=\mathrm{var}(X)
. In this paper, we always assume that
X
has finite mean and variance. Obviously, neither the dependence between
U
and
V
nor the distribution of
V
will be affected if we replace
\Lambda
by
\Sigma
in their definitions in (
2.3
). See
Paindaveine 2012
for a detailed discussion. So, for convenience, we reset
U
and
V
to be

\displaystyle U=\sqrt{(X-\mu)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(X-\mu)},\quad V=\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(X-\mu)/U,

(2.4)

for the rest of the paper.
The sufficient and necessary condition for elliptical distribution still applies to the redefined
U
and
V
, which we record below formally for easy reference.

Proposition 1
.

A random vector
X
has an elliptical distribution if and only if, for
U
and
V
defined in (
2.4
),

1.

U\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,V
;

2.

V
is uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
.

2.2
Polar coordinate transformation and equivalent condition

Since the random vector
V
only takes values in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
, it is more convenient to transform it into a
d-1
dimensional vector representing the direction of the unit vector
V
via the polar coordinate system. Specifically, let
v=(v_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d}})^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\in\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
and let

\displaystyle\theta=(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}})^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\times\cdots\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\times(-\pi,\pi]\equiv\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}.

For clarity of the subsequent discussion, we first give a definition of the precise meaning of the arc tangent function in the range of
(-\pi,\pi]
. For a real number
r\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\arctan(r)
be the unique
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
such that
r=\tan(\theta)
.
Then, for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, let

\displaystyle\mathrm{Arctan}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\arctan(y/x),&\mbox{if $x>0$},\\
\arctan(y/x)+\pi,&\mbox{if $x<0,y\geq 0$ },\\
\arctan(y/x)-\pi,&\mbox{if $x<0,y<0$},\\
\pi/2,&\mbox{if $x=0$, $y>0$},\\
-\pi/2,&\mbox{if $x=0$, $y<0$}.\end{cases}

(2.5)

We have written this function as
\mathrm{Arctan}(x,y)
instead of
\mathrm{Arctan}(y/x)
because it is no longer the function of the ratio
y/x
. Some useful properties of the Arctan function (
2.5
) are summarized in the Supplementary Materials. The next lemma, whose proof is also placed in the Supplementary Materials, gives the explicit one-to-one correspondence between
v\in\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
and
\theta\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
. In the following, we use
S_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}
to denote the Euclidean norm of the vector
(v_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},\ldots,v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d}})^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}
.

Lemma 1
.

The following function from
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
to
\mathbb{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}

\begin{split}v_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}&=\sin\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\\
v_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}&=\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\sin\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\\
\vdots&\\
v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}&=\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\ldots\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}\sin\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}},\\
v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}&=\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\ldots\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}.\end{split}

(2.6)

is bijective with inverse

\displaystyle\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Arctan}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle{j+1}},v_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})&j=1,\ldots,d-2,\\
\mathrm{Arctan}(v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d}},v_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}})&j=d-1.\end{cases}

(2.7)

We will denote the first function (
2.6
) as
v=\rho(\theta)
and the second function (
2.7
) as
\theta=g(v)
.

Then

\displaystyle x=uv=u\rho(\theta)\equiv\tau(u,\theta),

Evidently
\tau
is an invertible function, and the joint distribution of
U,\Theta
can be written as

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(u,\theta)=f_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\tau(u,\theta))\left|\tfrac{\partial\tau(u,\theta)}{\partial(u,\theta^{\mbox{{\sf T}}})}\right|.

(2.8)

According to
Anderson 2003
, the Jacobian on the right-hand side above is

\displaystyle\left|\tfrac{\partial\tau(u,\theta)}{\partial(u,\theta^{\mbox{{\sf T}}})}\right|=u^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-3}}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\ldots\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}.

(2.9)

Hence, by (
2.1
), (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), the joint p.d.f. of
(U,\Theta)
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}(u,\theta)=&\,u^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-3}}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\ldots\cos\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}h(u^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}),\quad(u,\theta)\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}},\end{split}

(2.10)

where
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}=[0,\infty)
. This relation implies (i)
U
and
\Theta
are independent, and (ii)
\Theta
has a known distribution. We summarize this result as the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

If
(U,\Theta)=\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}(X)
, then
X
has a spherical distribution centered at 0 if and only if

1.

U\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,\Theta
, or equivalently
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}=P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
;

2.

P_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
has p.d.f.

f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta)=c\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-3}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})\cdots\cos(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}),

where
\theta\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
and

\displaystyle c=\left(\textstyle{\int}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}\,\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-3}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})\cdots\cos(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}})\,d\theta\right)^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}.

2.3
Kernel embedding of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}

Let
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\to\mathbb{R}
be positive definite kernels, and let
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
be the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) generated by
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
. Let
{\cal{B}}
be the collection of linear operators

\{f\otimes g:f\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}},g\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\}
. Thus, each member of
{\cal{B}}
is a linear operator mapping from
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
such that, for any
h\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
,
(f\otimes g)(h)=f\langle g,h\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}
. Let
{\cal{S}}
be the linear span of
{\cal{B}}
, consisting of finite linear combinations of members of
{\cal{B}}
with real coefficients. Define in
{\cal{S}}
the inner product

\displaystyle\,\langle\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes g_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})+\cdots+\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}\otimes g_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}),\tilde{\alpha}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(\tilde{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes\tilde{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})+\cdots+\tilde{\alpha}_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}(\tilde{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{s}}\otimes\tilde{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle{s}})\rangle

\displaystyle\,\hskip 72.26999pt={\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{s}}\alpha_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}\tilde{\alpha}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\langle f_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\tilde{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}\,\langle g_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},\tilde{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}.

Endowed with this inner product,
{\cal{S}}
is an inner product space; its completion as a Hilbert space is the tensor product space
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
.

Let
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
be the Borel
\sigma
-fields on
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
, and let
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
be the product
\sigma
-field. Abbreviate
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\times{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
by
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}
and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}
. Let
{\cal{M}}(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}})
denote the class of all probability measures on
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}})
. We want to find an injective mapping from
{\cal{M}}(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}})
to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
so that testing equality of two measures in
{\cal{M}}(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}})
is equivalent to testing the equality of two operators in
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
. Such a mapping is provided by the following theorem. Recall that a kernel
\kappa
is characteristic if the mapping
P\mapsto\int\kappa(\cdot,X)dP
is injective. Here, the dot notation
\int\kappa(\cdot,X)dP
simply means the function
x\mapsto\int\kappa(x,X)dP
. So
\kappa
being characteristic means if
\int\kappa(x,X)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\int\kappa(x,X)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
for all
x
then
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=P_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
.

Theorem 1
.

If
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
are characteristic, then so is tensor product kernel
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
; that is, the mapping

\displaystyle{\cal{M}}(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}})\to{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}},\quad P_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}\mapsto\textstyle{\int}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}

is injective.

The dot notation
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)
means the function
u\mapsto\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(u,U)
; the same applies to
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta)
. So the operator
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta)
maps a function
f\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
to
f(\Theta)\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)
, which is a member of
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
. Correspondingly, the operator
\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}
maps a function
f\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
to

\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U)f(\Theta)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{U,\Theta}}
, which is a member of
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
. This type of dot notations will be used throughout the rest of the paper.
The proof of Theorem
1
can be found in Theorem 4 of
Szabó and
Sriperumbudur 2018
. As a result, we only need to guarantee that both
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
are characteristic kernels.
In fact, the Gaussian radial basis kernels, which in our context are

\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(u_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},u_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})=\exp[-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(u_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}-u_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}],\quad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\|\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}-\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\|^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}),

(2.11)

for some
\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}},\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}>0
, indeed satisfy the condition in Theorem
1
: it is shown in Theorem 3.2 of
Guella 2022
that a Gaussian radial basis kernel is integrally strictly positive definite, and it is further shown that an integrally strictly positive definite kernel is characteristic (see, for example,
Sriperumbudur, Fukumizu and
Lanckriet 2011
;
Fukumizu et al. 2009
;
Sriperumbudur
et al. 2010
).

Theorem
1
implies the following equivalence which is the basis of our test of ellipticity.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose

1.

X
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
with mean
\mu
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
;

2.

(U,\Theta)=\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}(\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(X-\mu))
;

3.

\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
are characteristic kernels.

Then
X
has an elliptical distribution with parameters
\mu
and
\Sigma
if and only if

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}

and the “closest” elliptically symmetric distribution

As we will see in the next section,

\displaystyle n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-2}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i,j}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}),\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}.

\displaystyle\left(n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-2}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i,j}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\right)\left(n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-2}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i,j}}\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}),\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}\right).

\Theta\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,U

4
Computing the test statistic

4.1
Coordinate mapping

The implementation of the test at the sample level relies on coordinate representation of linear operators. Let
{\cal{H}}
be an
r
-dimensional space with a basis
{\cal{B}}=\{b_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,b_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}\}
. Then every member
f
of
{\cal{H}}
can be represented as
c_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}b_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+\cdots+c_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}b_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
. The mapping from
{\cal{H}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
defined by
C(f)=(c_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,c_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}})^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}
is called the coordinate mapping. Let
G
be the Gram matrix
\{\langle b_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},b_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}}\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i,j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
. Then, for any
f,g\in{\cal{H}}
,

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}}={\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}C(f)_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}C(g)_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\langle b_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},b_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}}=C(f)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}GC(g).

In other words, if we let
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}(G)
represent the Hilbert space consisting of the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
along with the inner product
\langle a,b\rangle=a^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}Gb
, then
C:{\cal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}(G)
is an isomorphism. Let
A:{\cal{H}}\to{\cal{H}}
be a self-adjoint operator. An eigenvalue of
A
is defined by the following relations

\displaystyle Af=\lambda f,\quad\langle f,f\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}}=1,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}Cf=\lambda Cf,\quad(Cf)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}GCf=1,

where,
C^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}
stands for the adjoint operator of
C
. Note, again, that
*
and
\star
denote different concepts.
Letting
v=G^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}Cf
, the above can be restated as

\displaystyle G^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}G^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}v=\lambda v,\quad v^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}v=1.

In other words, a number is an eigenvalue of the operator
A
if and only if it is an eigenvalue of the matrix
G^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}G^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}
, which can be shown to be a symmetric matrix. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tra}(A)=\mathrm{tra}(G^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}G^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}})=\mathrm{tra}(CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}),

where
\mathrm{tra}
on the left represents the trace of a linear operator, in the middle and on the right represent that of a matrix.
This identity allows us to express the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of an operator as the trace of a matrix.

Let
e_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}
represent the
i
th column of the identity matrix
I_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
. Since
e_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}=Cb_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}
,
CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}
is simply the
n\times n
matrix

\displaystyle CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}(e_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,e_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}})=CAC^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}(Cb_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,Cb_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}})=(CAb_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,CAb_{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}).

(4.1)

4.2
Test statistic

At the sample level,
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
are spaces spanned by the two bases

\mathcal{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}=\{\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}):i=1,\ldots,n\},\quad\mathcal{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}=\{\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}):i=1,\ldots,n\}

respectively. Let
K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\tilde{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
be the
n\times n
matrices whose

th entries are

({K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}})_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}),\quad(\tilde{{K}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=\langle\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}),\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}

(4.2)

respectively. Note that
K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
is simply the Gram matrix of

, and

\displaystyle{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})-\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\theta)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(\theta)-\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},\theta)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(\theta)+\textstyle{\int}\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})d(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

Let
C:{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
be the coordinate mapping. Our goal is to compute

\displaystyle T_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}=n\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=n\,\mathrm{tra}(\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}).

By the discussion in Section
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tra}(\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}})=\mathrm{tra}(C\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}C^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}).

The next proposition gives the coordinate of
\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}

Proposition 3
.

If
K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\tilde{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
are the matrices defined in (
4.2

\displaystyle C\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}C^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}=\frac{1}{n^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}\tilde{{K}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}.

Let
\odot
denote Hadamard product between matrices, and let
1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
be the
n

\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}

\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\frac{1}{n^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}({K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\odot\tilde{{K}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}){1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}.

The computation of
{K}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}

\displaystyle\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\theta)d{P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}(\theta),\quad\textstyle{\int}\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})d({P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}})(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

We propose to compute these by numerical integration. By Proposition
2

\Theta

\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}

\displaystyle f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})=\begin{cases}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1-j}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})/\textstyle{\int}_{\scriptscriptstyle{-\pi/2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\pi/2}}\cos^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1-j}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})d\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},&\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2],\ j=1,\ldots,d-2\\

\displaystyle\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}=\begin{cases}(-\pi/2,\pi/2],&j=1,\ldots,d-2,\\

(4.3)

of which a typical example is the Gaussian radial basis kernel as in (

\displaystyle\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}\,\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\theta)d{P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\,\prod_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}}}\,\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}},\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})d\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}\,\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})d({P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\times P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}})(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\,\prod_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-2}}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}}}\,\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\prime})f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\prime})d\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}d\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\prime},

5
Asymptotic distribution

In this section we derive the asymptotic distribution of
T_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
under the null hypothesis (
3.2
). We use the von-Mises expansion to achieve this purpose; see, for example,
van der Vaart 1998
;
Fernholz 1983
;
Li 2018
. We first outline the key steps and notations for the von-Mises expansion, tailored for our current application.

5.1
von-Mises expansion

Let
\mathfrak{F}
denote the class of all distributions on
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
. Let
{\cal{H}}
be a generic Hilbert space. Let
T:\mathfrak{F}\to{\cal{H}}
be a mapping — such mappings are known as statistical functionals. Endow
\mathfrak{F}
with the uniform metric, and
{\cal{H}}
with the metric induced by its inner product. Let
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
be a member of
\mathfrak{F}
. Then
T
is Frechet differentiable at
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
if there is a linear operator
A:\mathfrak{F}\to{\cal{H}}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\scriptscriptstyle{\|F-F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathfrak{F}}}\to 0}}\frac{\|T(F)-T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})-A(F-F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}}}{\|F-F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathfrak{F}}}}=0.

Under the Frechet differentiability, the linear operator
A
can be calculated using Gateaux derivative: for any
F\in\mathfrak{F}
,
A(F)
is simply

\displaystyle\lim_{\scriptscriptstyle{\epsilon\to 0}}\frac{T((1-\epsilon)F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+\epsilon F)-T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})}{\epsilon}.

Let
x
be a member of
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
, and
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
be the Dirac measure at
x
. Then the mapping
x\mapsto A(\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}})
from
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
to
{\cal{H}}
is called influence function of
T
. We write
A(\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}})
as
T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(x)
. Note that we use
\star
to indicate influence function and
*
to indicate the adjoint operator. Both notations will be used heavily in our exposition. The key result that we use is this: if
X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,X_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
are i.i.d. from
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
and if
T:\mathfrak{F}\to{\cal{H}}
is Frechet differentiable at
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})-T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\cal D$}}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\Gamma),

(5.1)

where
\Gamma\in{\cal{H}}\otimes{\cal{H}}
is the linear operator

\displaystyle E(T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X)\otimes T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X))-E(T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X))\otimes E(T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X))=E(T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X)\otimes T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X)).

This fact is known as the
\delta
-method for statistical functionals. In our case,
{\cal{H}}
will be the tensor product space
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
introduced earlier. From the above discussion we see that the key to computing the asymptotic normal distribution of
\sqrt{n}(T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})-T(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}))
is to compute the influence function
T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(x)
. Next, we define our statistical functional.

5.2
Statistical functional for testing elliptical distributions

Now let us consider the statistical functional
T(F)
in our setting. Since it is a simple function of
\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}
, let us first figure out the statistical functional corresponding to this linear operator. For any
F\in\mathfrak{F}
, let

\mu(F)=\textstyle{\int}xdF(x),\quad\Sigma(F)=\textstyle{\int}(x-\mu(F))(x-\mu(F))^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}dF(x).

Clearly,

\mu(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})=\mu
,
\mu(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})=\hat{\mu}
,

\Sigma(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})=\Sigma
, and
\Sigma(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})=\hat{\Sigma}
.
For each
x\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
, let

\displaystyle W(x,F)=

\displaystyle\,\Sigma(F)^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(x-\mu(F)),

\displaystyle U(x,F)=

\displaystyle\,\|W(x,F)\|,

\displaystyle V(x,F)=

\displaystyle\,W(x,F)/U(x,F),

\displaystyle\Theta(x,F)=

\displaystyle\,g(V(x,F)).

It is easy to see that, when evaluated at
F=F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
,
U(x,F)
,
V(x,F)
, and
\Theta(x,F)
reduce to
U(x)
,
V(x)
, and
\Theta(x)
, and when evaluated at
F=F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
, they reduce to
\hat{U}(x)
,
\hat{V}(x)
and
\hat{\Theta}(x)
.
Our statistical functional of interest is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)=&\,\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U(x,F))\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta(x,F))dF(x)\\
&\,\quad-\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U(x,F))dF(x)\otimes\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\theta)d{P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}(\theta).\end{split}

(5.2)

It is important to note that the last term on the right,
\int\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\theta)d{P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}(\theta)
, does not involve the unknown distribution
F
. This is the true expectation determined by the known distribution
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
in Proposition
2
.
The operator in (
5.2
) can be re-expressed via the centered kernel as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)=\textstyle{\int}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,U(x,F))\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\Theta(x,F))dF(x).

(5.3)

Clearly, when evaluated at
F=F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)
reduces to
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}
, and when evaluated at
F=F_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)
reduces to
\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}
.
Our statistical functional of interest is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}\to\mathbb{R},\quad F\mapsto\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}.

5.3
Derivations of influence functions

In this subsection we derive the influence functions of statistical functionals involved in
\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}(F)\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}

F\mapsto G(x,F)
, which already depends on
x
. To make a distinction with this
x
and the argument
x
in
T^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(x)=A(\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}})
, we denote the argument in any influence function by
z
. Thus, we denote the influence function of the statistical functional
F\mapsto G(x,F)
as
G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(x,z)
. That is,

\displaystyle G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(x,z)=[\partial G(x,(1-\epsilon)F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+\epsilon\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{z}})/\partial\epsilon]_{\scriptscriptstyle{\epsilon=0}}.

We will refer to the process of deriving
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)
from
A(F)
as the
\star
-operation. The basic rules for the
\star
-operation are given in Proposition 9.2 of
Li 2018
. We start with the influence functions about
\mu(F)

5.5
Local power analysis

In this subsection, we derive the local alternative distribution, which can be used to compute the local power. The idea of the proof is similar to Theorem 13 of
Gretton et al. 2012

Theorem 3
.

Suppose

1.

\Gamma
has spectral decomposition
{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}(v_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\otimes v_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})
, where
v_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},v_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\ldots
is an orthonormal basis in
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
;

2.

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
is a fixed linear operator in

{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}v_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}

.

Then, under the local alternative hypothesis

we have

\displaystyle n\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\tilde{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}

Using this theorem, we calculate the local power of our test as

\displaystyle P\left(n\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{HS}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}>s\right)\to P\left({\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\tilde{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}>s\right).

6.1

In the following, for an integer

to represent the set

One of the advantages of the RKHS is that we can use the representer theorem (see, for example,
Schölkopf, Herbrich and
Smola 2001
) to turn an infinite dimensional problem into a finite dimensional problem. Let
{\cal{H}}
be the RKHS generated by a kernel

{\cal{H}}
only through

for

\hat{{\cal{H}}}\subseteq{\cal{H}}
, where
\hat{{\cal{H}}}
is spanned by
\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}})
. This is because there is always a function
\hat{f}\in\hat{{\cal{H}}}
such that

for

. In fact, let

\displaystyle\hat{f}=t_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})+\cdots+t_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}})=t_{\scriptscriptstyle{[m]}}^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[m]}}).

Up on solving the equation

We replace

Similarly,

Combining the above results, we approximate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)=&\,\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)+\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)+\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)\\

(6.3)

To approximate
\Gamma
, we replace

by
\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)
and
\int\cdots dF_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(x)

Approximating the asymptotic distribution of

Our goal is to find the eigenvalues of
\hat{\Gamma}
so as to form the approximation of
{\textstyle{\sum}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
. Recall that

is a self adjoint linear operator from

to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
, where, as mentioned earlier,

and

can be regarded as the finite-dimensional RKHS’s spanned by

and
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
.
As mentioned at the end of Section
6.1
, we need to compute the matrix (
6.1
).

We need to review a coordinate mapping rule in a tensor product space.
Suppose
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
are finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces of dimension
m_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
m_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, with bases
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\{b_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(1)}}}},\ldots,b_{\scriptscriptstyle{m_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(1)}}}}\}
and
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\{b_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(2)}}}},\ldots,b_{\scriptscriptstyle{m_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(2)}}}}\}
, respectively. Let
C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}:{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
and
C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}:{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
be coordinate mappings with respect to
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, respectively. Let
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
be the Gram matrices for
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
. A basis of the tensor product space
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
is

\displaystyle{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{12}}=\{b_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=b_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(1)}}}}\otimes b_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{{(2)}}}}:i=1,\ldots,m_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},j=1,\ldots,m_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\}.

For
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, the tensor product
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
can be viewed in two ways: as a member of
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
or as an operator from
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
.
As a member of
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, the coordinate of
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
with respect to
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{12}}
is

\displaystyle(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})=(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})\otimes(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}),

where
\otimes
on the right is the Kronecker product between matrices. As a linear operator from
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, the coordinate of
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
with respect to the bases
\{{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\}
is

\displaystyle C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}.

By
Li and Solea 2018
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\otimes f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}=(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}.

(6.4)

Applying (
6.4
) to
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}={\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}={\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
, we have

\displaystyle\,(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})](C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}

\displaystyle\,\hskip 36.135pt=[(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})][(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}).

Therefore, we can reexpress (
6.1
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\,(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Gamma}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}\\
&\,=n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\,[(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})][(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}.\end{split}

(6.5)

Let

\displaystyle\hat{m}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=

\displaystyle\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}),\ \ \hat{M}=\left(\hat{m}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,\hat{m}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})\right).

Then the matrix in (

) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Gamma}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}=\hat{M}\hat{M}^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}.

It remains to calculate
\hat{m}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})
, where the key is to calculate
(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})
. By (

), this is

\displaystyle(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{i})+(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{i})+(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{i}).

Reading off
\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)
from (

), we have

\displaystyle(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=

\displaystyle\,(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})[\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{e})}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,[C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]\otimes[C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\tilde{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{e})}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})],

where

\displaystyle C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=e_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\quad C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\tilde{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{e})}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=e_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}-1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}/n.

Reading off
\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)

), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=&\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})[\hat{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(\cdot,X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})\otimes\tilde{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{e})}}(\cdot,\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})]\\

(6.6)

To calculate

, we now give an expression for

for any

.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
{\cal{H}}
is a finite-dimensional RKHS generated by a kernel
\kappa
, with basis
\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{a}}),\ldots,\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}})
,
f
is a member of
{\cal{H}}
, and
C
is the coordinate mapping. Then

\displaystyle C(f)=K^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}f(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[k]}}),

where
f(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[k]}})
is the vector
(f(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,f(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{k}}))^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}
, and
K
is the Gram matrix
\{\kappa(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},a_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i,j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
.

Since
f\in{\cal{H}}
, we have
f=C(f)^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[m]}})
where
\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[m]}})
represents the vector of functions
(\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,\kappa(\cdot,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}))^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}
. Evaluate this equation at
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
, we have

\displaystyle f(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{[k]}})=KC(f).

Solving this equation, we have the desired result.
\Box

Applying Lemma
6
to (
6.6
), we have

\displaystyle\,(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}[K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{[n]}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]\otimes(e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}-1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}/n)

\displaystyle\,=n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\{K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{[n]}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})[\hat{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\hat{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})+\hat{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\mathrm{vec}{(\hat{\Sigma}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}))}]\}\otimes(e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}-1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}/n).

Reading off
\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)
from (

), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=&\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}[C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\cdot,\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})]\otimes[C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}\hat{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(\cdot,X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]\\

(6.7)

again to (

), we have

\displaystyle\,(C_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes C_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta,3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\otimes[K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{[n]}},\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\hat{\Theta}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]

\displaystyle\,=n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\otimes\{K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{[n]}},\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})[\hat{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\hat{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})+\hat{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\mathrm{vec}{(\hat{\Sigma}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}))}]\}.

To summarize, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{m}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})=&\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}[e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\otimes(e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}-1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}/n)]\\
&\,+n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1/2}}(K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes K_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}\left\{n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}[K_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{[n]}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(X_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}},X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})]\otimes(e_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}-1_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}/n)\right\}\\

We need to calculate the eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
of the matrix
\hat{M}\hat{M}^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}
. Since this is

, its eigenvalues are expensive to compute if computed directly. However, it is equivalent to calculate the eigenvalues of
\hat{M}^{\mbox{{\sf T}}}\hat{M}
, which is an
n\times n
matrix. We then use
{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
to approximate the asymptotic null distribution in (
5.13
). We apply the function
imhof
in the R package
CompQuadForm
(
Duchesne and de
Micheaux 2010
) to compute the p-value of the distribution of
{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
. If the p-value is smaller than some prespecified significance level
\alpha
, then we reject
H_{0}
.

6.4
Validity and consistency

Since we have plugged the estimated eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
into the asymptotic distribution of
n\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{HS}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, we want to show that this step preserves the validity and consistency of the test.

Notice that
\hat{\Gamma}
is a consistent estimator of
\Gamma
. Suppose that
\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}}<\infty
, then, by Theorem 1 of
Gretton et al. 2009
, we have

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\hat{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}{\textstyle{\sum}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}Z_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},

which indicates the validity of the test. The consistency is straightforward since, under the alternative hypothesis,
\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\xrightarrow{P}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}
which is nonzero, indicating that
n\|\breve{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{HS}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\xrightarrow{P}\infty
(see, for example, Section 12.4 of
Kokoszka and
Reimherr 2017
).

and

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(n,d,u)=c_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\sqrt{\frac{d[\log(2d)+u]}{n}},\\

In this section we present some simulation results of using

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}={n\choose 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n-1}}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=i+1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}|\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}-\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}|,\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}}}={n\choose 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n-1}}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=i+1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\|\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}-\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\|.

9
Application

In this section we apply our test to a dataset concerning a Study on the Efficacy of Nosocomial Infection Control (SENIC Project), which is used in
Haley et al. 1980
. We download the dataset from
https://users.stat.ufl.edu/˜rrandles/sta4210/Rclassnotes/data/textdatasets/KutnerData/Appendix%20C%20Data%20Sets/APPENC01.txt
.
This is one of the datasets from the book
Kutner et al. 2005
.

According to
Kutner et al. 2005
, the data of 113 hospitals during the 1975-76 study period are sampled from the original 338 hospitals surveyed. For a single hospital, there are 11 variables:

length of stay, age, infection risk, routine culturing, routine chest x-ray, number of beds, medical school affiliation, region, average daily census, number of nurses, available facilities.

For detailed information about these variables, see
Kutner et al. 2005
.

We treat the variable “length of stay” as the response variable, and check whether other predictor variables are elliptically distributed. We remove the two categorical variables, “medical school affiliation” and “region”. We carry out our test on the dataset where
n=113
and
d=8
at a significance level
\alpha=0.05
, and obtained the p-value 0.0014, which leads to rejection of the elliptical distribution hypothesis.
The original data is as shown in Figure 1 in Appendix E of the online Supplementary Material (
Tang and Li 2024
).

We then perform the Box-Cox transformation on the dataset. Using the method presented in Chapter 7 of
Li 2018
, we find the optimal
\lambda
for the Box-Cox transformation as

1.158,0.737,-0.105,0.947,-0.316,-0.105,-0.316,0.947.

The transformed variables are shown in Figure 2 in Appendix E of the online Supplementary Material (
Tang and Li 2024
). We carry out our test on the transformed data, and obtain the p-value 0.2454. This is much larger than 0.05, leading us to accept the elliptical distribution hypothesis after the Box-Cox transformation.

10
Discussion

For our method, the choice of kernels are indeed important. In general, the kernel functions
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
should satisfy the following three conditions.

1.

Both
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
should be characteristic kernels. This is to guarantee that
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}=0
if and only if
X
follows an elliptical distribution. If either of them fails to be characteristic, then there will exist cases when
X
does not follow an elliptical distribution and yet
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}=0
is satisfied, which leads to non-consistency of the test.

2.

Both
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
should be
C^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
-smooth kernel functions, i.e., the conditions in (
5.11
) should be satisfied. This is to guarantee that
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U\Theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\star}}(z)
is a member of
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
. Also, we need the derivatives
\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{U}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})
and
\dot{\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}},\hat{\Theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}})
in the implementation, which requires differentiability in
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
.

3.

From a computational perspective, a product-type kernel for
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}
is preferred. This is because, otherwise, we will need to compute
n
numerical integrals of dimension
(d-1)
in equation (
4.4
) and one numerical integral of dimension
(2d-2)
in equation (
4.5
). The product-type kernels allow us to replace these high-dimension numerical integration by 1- or 2-dimensional integration.

Clearly, the Gaussian kernel satisfies all three conditions above, so it is preferred. It also works well in our simulation studies. However, many other kernels also satisfy the three conditions. We can construct a broad class of product kernels that are computationally feasible for our method.

Specifically, for any
d-1
kernel functions on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, say
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\ldots,\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}}}
, their product given by (
4.3
) is still a reproducing kernel. Furthermore,
by Theorem
1
, the product of characteristic kernels is still characteristic. For example, we can use the product-type inverse-quadratic (PIQ) kernels defined as follows:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(u,u^{\prime})=\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}(u-u^{\prime})^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}},\quad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\prod_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Theta}}(\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}-\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}^{\prime})^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}},

(10.1)

which also satisfy the above three requirements.
In principle, all such kernels can be used for our purpose. This gives us broad choices for kernels.Further discussions on the choice of kernels are given in the Supplementary Materials.
