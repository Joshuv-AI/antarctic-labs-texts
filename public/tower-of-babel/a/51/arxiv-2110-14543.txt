Solving the Yamabe Problem by an Iterative Method on a Small Riemannian Domain
Steven Rosenberg, Jie Xu

Abstract
. We introduce an iterative scheme to solve the Yamabe equation − a ​ Δ g ​ u + S ​ u = λ ​ u p − 1 -a\Delta_{g}u+Su=\lambda u^{p-1} on small domains ( Ω , g ) ⊂ ℝ n (\Omega,g)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} equipped with a Riemannian metric g g . Thus g g admits a conformal change to a constant scalar curvature metric. The proof does not use the traditional functional minimization. Applications to the Yamabe problem on closed manifolds, manifolds with boundary, and noncompact manifolds are given in forthcoming papers.

Introduction

In this paper, we solve the Yamabe equation on small domains
(\Omega,g)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
. We introduce an iterative method developed for hyperbolic operators
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and elliptic operators
[
18
]
, with a long history in PDE theory dating back to
[
15
,
16
]
. This local solution will be applied in forthcoming papers by the second author to global settings including closed manifolds, manifolds with boundary, and certain noncompact manifolds.

For a brief history, in 1960 Yamabe proposed the following generalization of the classical uniformization theorem for surfaces:

The Yamabe Conjecture.

Given a compact Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n\geqslant 3
, there exists a metric conformal to
g
with constant scalar curvature.

Let
S=S_{g}
be the scalar curvature of
g
, and let
\tilde{S}
be the scalar curvature
of the conformal metric
\tilde{g}=e^{2f}g
.
Set
e^{2f}=u^{p-2}
, where
p=\frac{2n}{n-2}
and
u>0.
Then

(1)

\tilde{S}=u^{1-p}\left(-4\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-2}\Delta_{g}u+Su\right),

where the Laplacian
\Delta_{g}=-d^{*}d
is negative definite.
Setting
a=4\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-2}>0
, we have that

\tilde{g}=u^{p-2}g
has constant scalar curvature
\lambda
if and only if
u
satisfies the Yamabe equation

(2)

-a\Delta_{g}u+Su=\lambda u^{p-1},

The solution of the Yamabe conjecture for closed manifolds involved three major steps
(see
[
11
]
for a thorough treatment):

1.

Yamabe, Trudinger and Aubin proved that if the minimum
of the Yamabe functional
Y(g)=\int_{M}S\ d\text{Vol}_{g}/({\rm Vol}(M,g))^{(n-2)/n}

on a conformal class of
metrics on a closed manifold
(M,g)
is smaller than the minimum
on the conformal class of the standard metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
then (
2
) has a solution;

2.

Aubin then used Step 1 to prove that
if
\dim M\geqslant 6
and

(M,g)
is not locally conformally flat, then (
2
) has a solution;

3.

Finally, Schoen used the
positive mass theorem to prove that (
2
) has a solution if
M
has dimension
3,4,5
or is locally conformally flat, and
M
is not conformal to the standard sphere.

There are also results for manifolds with boundary
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
14
]
and open manifolds
[
3
,
8
,
19
]
with certain restrictions.

In contrast, our methods treat small domains in all dimensions greater than two. (To be honest, there is one place in the proof of Theorem
2.8
where an easy estimate depends on the dimension.)
The main result is:

Theorem
.

Let
(\Omega,g)
be a connected domain with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
and let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
\bar{\Omega}
. If the
g
-volume and the Euclidean diameter of
\Omega
are sufficiently small, then there is a conformal change
\tilde{g}=u^{p-2}g
of
g
to a constant scalar curvature metric. On
\partial\Omega
, we can arrange that

\tilde{g}=g.

The proof that
u
exists, is smooth, and is positive is contained in Theorems
2.3
,
2.5
,
3.1
. The last statement of the Theorem is Remark
3.1
.

The proof has technical advantages over previous proofs:
(i) Yamabe obtained the Yamabe equation (
2
) as the Euler-Lagrange equation of
Y(g)
, while we
solve (
2
) directly, without discussing whether a minimum of
Y(g)
exists;
(ii) In contrast to Yamabe and Trudinger’s arguments, which treated the subcritical case
s<p
of (
2
)
before passing to the limit
s=p
, we work directly with (
2
); (iii) Because our argument is local and does not involve minimizing a functional, it has applications to closed manifolds, manifolds with boudary, and noncompact manifolds.
The main disadvantage is that because we work with (
2
) directly, we cannot assume that
u
is positive as in previous approaches; the proof of positivity requires a separate argument.

The paper is organized as follows. In §2, we apply the iterative method to solve
(
2
) on a small bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\not\equiv 2\ ({\rm mod}\ 8)
, with
constant Dirichlet boundary conditions (Theorem
2.3
). The size of
\Omega
is determined in the proof. The dimensional restriction ensures
that
u^{p-1}
is well defined, even if
u
is possibly negative. The main technical difficulty is that the nonlinearity in (
2
) involves the function
x^{p-1}
, which is not globally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
; the easier case of an elliptic equation with globally Lipschitz nonlinearity is treated in
[
18
]
. The added difficulty is handled by familiar techniques:
the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, the Poincaré inequality, Li-Yau estimates for the first eigenvalue of
\Delta_{g}
, and elliptic estimates. The solution obtained is a weak solution in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega,g).
In Theorem
2.8
, we prove that the solution is in fact smooth, using arguments adapted from Yamabe and Trudinger’s work in the subcritical case.

In §3, we remove the dimension restriction by proving that the iterative method leads to a positive solution to the Yamabe equation (Theorem
3.1
). Although we could have proven positivity in §2, the argument is fairly technical, so we have given it its own section.

Appendix A proves a technical result from §3, and Appendix B gives a table of the constants used in the article.

2.
The Yamabe problem on a Riemannian domain

In this section, we start with an open, bounded subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},n\not\equiv 2\ ({\rm mod}\ 8),
where we assume that
\bar{\Omega}
is a smooth manifold with boundary. We apply an iterative method to solve the Yamabe equation (
2
) on
\Omega
with constant Dirichlet boundary conditions,
where
\Omega
is equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
which extends smoothly to
\bar{\Omega}.
There are two steps: in Theorem

2.3
we prove that a weak solution exists, and in Theorem
2.8

we prove that the solution is in fact smooth.

We call
(\Omega,g)
a Riemannian domain.

Thus we consider the
boundary value problem:

(3)

-a\Delta_{g}u+Su=\lambda u^{p-1}\;{\rm in}\;\Omega;\ u=c>0\;{\rm on}\;\partial\Omega.

Here
a=\frac{4(n-1)}{n-2},p=\frac{2n}{n-2}
,
S
is the scalar curvature of
g
, and
c
is a fixed positive constant.
\lambda
is an unspecified nonzero constant. When
n\not\equiv 2\ ({\rm mod}\ 8)
,
\frac{n+2}{n-2}
in lowest terms has odd denominator, so for real valued functions
u
,

u^{p-1}=u^{\frac{n+2}{n-2}}
is well-defined. For the rest of this section, we assume this condition on
n
.

On
\Omega
, we have
g=g_{ij}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j}

in the standard coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with volume form

d\text{Vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}dx_{1}\dotso dx_{n}.

(v,w)_{g}
and
|v|_{g}=(v,v)_{g}^{1/2}
denote the inner product and norm with respect to
g
.

We define two equivalent versions of the
\mathcal{L}^{p}
norms and two equivalent versions of the Sobolev norms on
(\Omega,g)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\Omega,g)
be a Riemannian domain.
For real valued functions
u
, we set:

(i)
For
1\leqslant p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega)\

\displaystyle{\rm is\ the\ completion\ of}\ \left\{u\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega):\|u\|_{p}^{p}:=\int_{\Omega}\lvert u\rvert^{p}dx<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,g)\

\displaystyle{\rm is\ the\ completion\ of}\ \left\{u\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega):\|u\|_{p,g}^{p}:=\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{p}d\text{Vol}_{g}<\infty\right\}.

(ii) For
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connection of
g
, and for

u\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

(4)

\lvert\nabla^{k}u\rvert_{g}^{2}:=(\nabla^{\alpha_{1}}\dotso\nabla^{\alpha_{k}}u)(\nabla_{\alpha_{1}}\dotso\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}u).

In particular,
\lvert\nabla^{0}u\rvert^{2}_{g}=\lvert u\rvert^{2}_{g}
and
\lvert\nabla^{1}u\rvert^{2}_{g}=\lvert\nabla u\rvert_{g}^{2}.

(iii)
For
s\in\mathbb{N},1\leqslant p<\infty
,

(5)

\displaystyle W^{s,p}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{u\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega):\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}:=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=0}^{s}\left\lvert D^{j}u\right\rvert^{p}dx<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle W^{s,p}(\Omega,g)

\displaystyle=\left\{u\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,g):\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s,p}(\Omega,g)}^{p}=\sum_{j=0}^{s}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla^{j}u\right\rvert^{p}_{g}d\text{Vol}_{g}<\infty\right\}.

Here
\lvert D^{j}u\rvert^{p}:=\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=j}\lvert\partial^{\alpha}u\rvert^{p}

in the weak sense. Similarly,
W_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
is the completion of
\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the

W^{s,p}
-norm.
In particular,
H^{s}(\Omega):=W^{s,2}(\Omega)

and
H^{s}(\Omega,g):=W^{s,p}(\Omega,g)
are the usual Sobolev spaces,
and we similarly define
H_{0}^{s}(\Omega),H^{s}_{0}(\Omega,g)
.

Remark 2.1
.

It is clear that the two
\mathcal{L}^{p}
norms are equivalent, the two
H^{s}
norms are equivalent, and the two
W^{s,p}
norms are equivalent on
\Omega.
Thus there are constants
C_{2}>C_{1}>0
such that

(6)

\begin{split}C_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega)}&\leqslant\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant C_{2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\\
C_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}&\leqslant\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s,p}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant C_{2}\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}\\
C_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega)}&\leqslant\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant C_{2}\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

In Riemannian normal coordinates centered at
p\in\Omega
,
g
agrees with the Euclidean metric up to terms of order
O(r^{2})
, where
r
is the distance to
p
. Thus
there exists a neighborhood

U_{p}
of
p
on which we may assume
C_{1}\geq 1/2,C_{2}\leq 2
in (
6
) for
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U_{p}).

Since we will eventually assume that the diameter of
\Omega
is sufficiently small, and since
C_{2}/C_{1}
for
\Omega^{\prime}
is smaller than
C_{2}/C_{1}
for
\Omega
when
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
, we can assume that
C_{2}/C_{1}\in[1,4].

The main tools used to solve
(
3
)
are (i) the version of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg (GN)

1 warning
interpolation inequality for the zero trace case;(ii) a version of the extension theorem; (iii)
the Poincaré inequality with respect to Laplace-Beltrami operator.

Proposition 2.1
.

(GN trace zero case)

[
2
, Thm. 3.70]

Let
q,r,l
be real numbers with
1\leqslant q,r,l\leqslant\infty
, and let

be integers with
0\leqslant j<m
.
Define

by solving

(7)

\frac{1}{l}=\frac{j}{n}+\alpha\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{m}{n}\right)+\frac{1-\alpha}{q},

as long as
l>0.
If
\alpha\in\left[\frac{j}{m},1\right]
, then
there exists a constant
C_{m,j,q,r}
,
depending only on
n,m,j,q,r,\alpha

such that for all
u\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(8)

\lVert\nabla^{j}u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C_{m,j,q,r,\alpha}\lVert\nabla^{m}u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\alpha}\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{1-\alpha}.

(If
r=\frac{n}{m-j}\neq 1
, then (
8
) is not valid for
\alpha=1
.)

Remark 2.2
.

For fixed
n,m,j,q,r,\alpha
, we can leave
C_{m,j,q,r,\alpha}
unchanged in (
8
)
if we restrict the support of

to a domain.

Proposition 2.2
.

(Extension Operator)

[
1
, Thm. 5.22]

Let
\Omega
be a bounded, open, connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary. Then there exists a bounded linear operator
E:W^{k,p}(\Omega)\to W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the extension operator,
such that
Eu
has compact support,
Eu=u
a.e. on
\Omega
, and

(9)

\lVert Eu\rVert_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant K(k,p,\Omega)\lVert u\rVert_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}.

If
\Omega
is fixed, we write
K(k,p,\Omega)=K(k,p).
Note that
K(k,p)\geq 1.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
12
]

(Poincaré inequality)
Let
(\bar{M},g)
be a compact manifold with smooth boundary and with interior
M
. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the first non-zero eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{g}
on
u\in H_{0}^{1}(M,g)
.
We have

(10)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(M,g)}\leqslant\lambda_{1}^{-1/\penalty 2}\left\lVert\nabla_{g}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(M,g)}.

Moreover,
\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}
is the optimal constant for (
10
) holds.

To control
\lambda_{1}
here, we need the following theorem of Li and Yau.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
13
, Thm. 7]
Let
(\bar{M},g)
be a compact manifold with smooth boundary, let
r_{inj}
be the injectivity radius of
M
, and let
h_{g}
be the minimum of the mean curvature of
\partial M
. Choose
K\geq 0
such that
Ric_{g}\geqslant-(n-1)K
.
For
\lambda_{1}
as in Proposition
2.3
,

(11)

\lambda_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{4(n-1)r_{inj}^{2}}\left(\log\gamma\right)^{2}-(n-1)K\right),

where

(12)

\gamma=\max\left\{\exp[{1+\left(1-4(n-1)^{2}r_{inj}^{2}K\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}],\exp[{-2(n-1)h_{g}r_{inj}}]\right\}.

Remark 2.3
.

(i) We will apply Proposition
2.3
and Theorem
2.1
only in the case

\bar{M}=\bar{\Omega}.

(ii)
As in Remark
2.1
, in Riemannian normal coordinates centered at
p\in\Omega
,
g
agrees with the Euclidean metric up to terms of order
O(r^{2})
. Thus if
\Omega
is a
g
-geodesic ball of radius
r
, the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
is close
(n-1)/r
, the mean curvature of a Euclidean
r
-ball in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In (
12
), as
r\to 0
,
K
can be taken to be unchanged (since

is independent of

),
r_{inj}\to 0
, and
h\cdot r_{inj}\to n-1.
Thus
\gamma\to e^{2}
, the right hand side of
(
11
) goes to infinity as
r\to 0
, and
\lambda_{1}\to\infty.

If
\Omega
is a general Riemannian domain with a small enough injectivity radius, then
\Omega
sits inside
a
g
-geodesic ball
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
of small radius. By the Rayleigh quotient characterization of
\lambda_{1}
, we have
\lambda_{1}^{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\leq\lambda_{1}^{\Omega}
. Thus for all Riemannian domains
(\Omega,g),

\lambda_{1}^{-1}\rightarrow 0
as the radius of
\Omega
goes to zero.

We recall the basic elliptic estimate for the Dirichlet problem.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
17
, Ch. 5, Thm. 1.3]

Let
(\Omega,g)
be a Riemmannian domain, and let
L
be a second order elliptic operator of the form
Lu=-\Delta_{g}u+Xu
where
X
is a first order differential operator with smooth coefficients on
\bar{\Omega}
.
For
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)
, a solution
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,g)
to
Lu=f
in
\Omega

with
u\equiv 0
on
\partial\Omega
belongs to
H^{2}(\Omega,g)
, and

(13)

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant C^{*}\left(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}\right).

C^{*}=C^{*}(L,\Omega,g)
depends on
L
and
(\Omega,g)
.

Remark 2.4
.

If
u,f
have support in
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
, we can set
C^{*}(L,\Omega^{\prime},g|_{\Omega^{\prime}})=C^{*}(L,\Omega,g)
in (

), since for

we have
\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega^{\prime},g)}=\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}
, etc.

We are now ready to prove the main theorem of this section by an iteration scheme.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(\Omega,g)
be Riemannian domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\not\equiv 2\ ({\rm mod}\ 8)
, with
C^{\infty}
boundary, and with
{\rm Vol}_{g}(\Omega)
and the Euclidean diameter of
\Omega
sufficiently small.
Then the Yamabe equation (
3
) has a
real solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega,g)
in the weak sense.

To be more precise, we start with
(\Omega,g)
and as necessary pass to sub-Riemannian domains
(\Omega^{\prime},g|_{\Omega^{\prime}})\subset(\Omega,g)
,
with
C^{\infty}
boundary, such that
{\rm Vol}_{g}(\Omega^{\prime})
and
r_{inj}(\Omega^{\prime})

are sufficiently small. This “smallness” is discussed after the proof in Remark
2.5
. Throughout the proof, we discuss the weak form of of linear elliptic PDE, i.e. we discuss the form
B[u,v]=(f,v)_{g},\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
where
B[u,v]=(-a\Delta_{g}u,v)_{g}

and
(h,k)_{g}
is the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)
inner product.

Proof.

We first consider the linear elliptic PDE with constant boundary condition:

(14)

au_{0}-a\Delta_{g}u_{0}=f\;in\;\Omega;u_{0}=c\;on\;\partial\Omega.

By taking
\tilde{u}_{0}=u_{0}-c
, (
14
) is equivalent to

(15)

a\tilde{u}_{0}-a\Delta_{g}\tilde{u}_{0}=f-ac\;in\;\Omega;\tilde{u}_{0}\equiv 0\;on\;\partial\Omega.

For any
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)
, the Lax-Milgram Theorem implies
that (
15
) has a unique solution
\tilde{u}_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,g)
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is dense in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,g)
, we can assume either that
\tilde{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
or
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,g)
.

By the Poincaré inequality and (
13
), we observe that

\displaystyle\left(a\tilde{u}_{0}-a\Delta_{g}\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{0}\right)_{g}=\left(f-ac,\tilde{u}_{0}\right)_{g}\Rightarrow\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{a}\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{a}\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant\frac{1}{a}\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant\frac{1}{a}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}.

(The first implication uses
\|\tilde{u}_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}^{2}=(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{0})_{g}+(\nabla\tilde{u}_{0},\nabla\tilde{u}_{0})_{g}=(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{0})_{g}+(-\Delta_{g}\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{0})_{g}.
)

Applying Theorem
2.2
to (
15
),
we have

(16)

\begin{split}\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}&\leqslant C^{*}\left(\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega,g)}\right)\leqslant C^{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{a}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\\
&:=C\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\\
\Rightarrow&\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant C\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+\lVert c\rVert_{H_{0}^{2}(\Omega,g)}:=C\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+c\cdot\tilde{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

It follows that
\tilde{u}_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,g)\cap H^{2}(\Omega,g)
. In particular,

(17)

\tilde{C}:=\text{Vol}_{g}(\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}

decreases as Vol
{}_{g}(\Omega)

shrinks.
Furthermore,
C=C(-\Delta_{g},\Omega,g)

is nonincreasing as
\Omega
shrinks. Indeed, as
\Omega
shrinks,

is bounded above by Remarks 2.3(ii) and 2.4.

For fixed

, we can take
\Omega
of small enough

-volume and choose

so that

C\lVert f-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+c\cdot\tilde{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant 1
,
so by (
16
)

(18)

\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}\leqslant 1,\lVert\tilde{u}_{0}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}<1.

We apply the iteration scheme by defining

au_{k}-a\Delta_{g}u_{k}=au_{k-1}-Su_{k-1}+\lambda u_{k-1}^{p-1}\;{\rm in}\;(\Omega,g),\ u_{k}=c\;{\rm on}\;\partial\Omega,\ k=1,2,\dotso.

The first main step is to prove the boundedness of

For

\tilde{u}_{k}=u_{k}-c,

(

a\tilde{u}_{k}-a\Delta_{g}\tilde{u}_{k}=au_{k-1}-Su_{k-1}+\lambda u_{k-1}^{p-1}-ac\;{\rm in}\;(\Omega,g),\ \tilde{u}_{k}=0\;{\rm on}\;\partial\Omega,\ k=1,2,\dotso.

By Lax-Milgram, for

, (

) has a unique solution
\tilde{u}_{1}
.
As with

, for the same

\begin{split}\lVert\tilde{u}_{1}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,g)}&\leqslant C\lVert au_{0}-Su_{0}+\lambda u_{0}^{p-1}-ac\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}\\
&\leqslant aC\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+C\sup\lvert S\rvert\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+C\lvert\lambda\rvert\lVert u_{0}^{p-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}+acC\tilde{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

We now apply Proposition

to bound
\lVert u_{0}^{p-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,g)}
in (
