Applying the effective-source approach to frequency-domain self-force calculations for eccentric orbits
Benjamin Leather, Niels Warburton

Abstract
Extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs) are expected to have considerable eccentricity when emitting gravitational waves (GWs) in the LISA band. Developing GW templates that remain phase accurate over these long inspirals requires the use of second-order self-force theory and practical second-order self-force calculations are now emerging for quasi-circular EMRIs. These calculations rely on effective-source regularization techniques in the frequency domain that presently are specialized to circular orbits. Here we make a first step towards more generic second-order calculations by extending the frequency domain effective-source approach to eccentric orbits. In order to overcome the slow convergence of the Fourier sum over radial modes, we develop a new extended effective-sources approach which builds upon the method of extended particular solutions. To demonstrate our new computational technique we apply it a toy scalar-field problem which is conceptually similar to the gravitational case.



I
Introduction

Direct observations of gravitational waves from binary black hole mergers
Abbott et al. 2016a
;
Abbott et al. 2016b
;
Abbott et al. 2017a
;
Abbott et al. 2017b
;
Abbott et al. 2017c
and more recently binary neutron star mergers
Abbott et al. 2017d
;
Abbott et al. 2020a
;
Abbott et al. 2021
signified a turning point in astronomy.
Gravitational waves are now an observable science rather than interesting theoretical concepts.

The increasing maturity of space-based gravitational wave detector designs has motivated much recent work to calculate gravitational wave emission from extreme-mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs).
There is also considerable theoretical interest in the problem of motion of a point mass in a background geometry in general relativity, influenced by its own self-force.

An EMRI system consists of a compact object of mass
\mu\sim 1-100\,M_{\odot}
(e.g., a neutron star or black hole) moving on a decaying orbit about, and ultimately into, a massive black hole of mass
M\sim 10^{5}-10^{7}\,M_{\odot}
.
During their inspiral EMRIs evolve adiabatically due to the back-reaction from the gravitational perturbation sourced by the smaller body.
Each EMRI emits tens to hundreds of thousands of gravitational wave cycles in the millihertz frequency range of the LISA mission
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2015
;
Barack et al. 2019
as the smaller body spends months to years orbiting in the strong-field of the massive black hole.
During this time, the associated waveform will encode detailed information of the surrounding spacetime geometry.
Detection of EMRI waveforms will therefore enable high-precision measurements of the central object’s mass and spin, test the Kerr hypothesis, and allow tests of proposed alternate theories of gravity
Barack and Cutler 2007
.

The inherent problem with detecting and characterising EMRI waveforms is that the instantaneous signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) is very small for the vast majority of signals
Gair et al. 2004
.
Therefore if one is to extract EMRI signals through matched filtering techniques we require accurate theoretical waveform templates.

The natural treatment of the EMRI problem is through the
gravitational self-force
(GSF) approach.
Here the smaller, compact body sources a perturbation to the metric of the larger black hole.
The perturbation is expressed a power series expansion in the small mass ratio,
\epsilon:=\mu/M
, such that, at leading order, the small body moves along a geodesic of the background spacetime.
At subsequent sub-leading orders the back-reaction due to the metric perturbation from the smaller body accelerates the body away from geodesic motion in the background spacetime.

Since SF theory is based on a perturbative expansion the accuracy of the result depends upon the order to which the expansion is carried out.
Producing waveform models that accurately track to the waveform phase to within a fraction of radian requires carrying out this expansion through
\epsilon^{2}

Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
.
These calculations also have to be carried out over a large parameter space for generic binaries that are both highly eccentric, and precessing due to the spins on their component masses
Seoane et al. 2023
.

First-order calculations have reached this goal and the first-order GSF can be calculated for an object on a generic orbit around a spinning (Kerr) primary
van de Meent 2018
.
Recently, corrections for generic orbits due to the spin of secondary were also computed
Skoupy et al. 2023
.
A range of works have also calculated the associated inspirals around a Schwarzschild black hole
Warburton et al. 2012
;
Osburn et al. 2016
;
Warburton et al. 2017
;
Van De Meent and Warburton 2018
or Kerr black hole

Fujita and Shibata 2020
;
Lynch et al. 2022
;
Hughes et al. 2021
;
Lynch et al. 2023
.

Calculations at second-order in the mass ratio are now emerging for quantities such the binding energy
Pound et al. 2020
, the gravitational wave flux
Warburton et al. 2021
, and recently the waveform
Wardell et al. 2023
.
Despite being a perturbative expansion in the mass ratio, the latter two have shown remarkable agreement with numerical relativity simulations, even for near-comparable mass binaries with
\epsilon=1/10
.
This suggests that GSF results can be used to model intermediate-mass-ratio inspirals (IMRIs)
Warburton et al. 2021
.
This is notable in light of the recent GW observations of binaries with
\epsilon\sim 1/30

Abbott et al. 2020b
;
Abbott et al. 2020c
.
So far all second-order calculations have focused on the quasi-circular case.
For EMRIs there is a strong motivation to push these calculations to eccentric orbits
Hopman and Alexander 2005
and this requires the development of new calculational techniques.
The focus of this paper is to develop such a computational approach to second-order perturbations for a compact object moving on an eccentric orbit.

I.1
Regularization and the effective-source approach

The point-particle model of EMRIs within SF-theory leads to distributional sources and their associated singular fields.
Consequently, an essential component of SF computations is regularization of the retarded metric perturbation
Mino et al. 1997
;
Quinn and Wald 1997
;
Gralla and Wald 2008
;
Pound 2010
.

The first, and most commonly used technique for first-order calculations, is
mode-sum regularization
.
Introduced in
Barack and Ori 2000
, the technique is based on the observation that a spherical-harmonic decomposition of the first-order metric perturbation yields individual multipole modes that are finite on the worldline.
Thus it is possible to subtract the singular contribution to retarded field on a mode-by-mode basis.
This procedure then gives the regular contribution to the field from which one can compute the self-force which drives the inspiral.
To date, second-order calculations have been carried out in the Lorenz gauge
Miller and Pound 2021
;
Pound et al. 2020
and here the mode-sum prescription is not tractable since the individual multipole modes of the retarded metric perturbation are logarithmically divergent at the location of the particle
Pound and Miller 2014
.
There has been some development in formulating a
highly regular gauge
that would render the multipole modes of the retarded field finite and allow a mode-sum procedure to be implemented at second-order
Upton and Pound 2021
, but this has not yet been used for practical calculations.
In this work we shall thus focus an alternative, effective-source treatment.

The effective-source method was pioneered in Refs.
Barack and Golbourn 2007
and
Vega and Detweiler 2008
.
It was designed for situations wherein the modes of the retarded field would diverge at the worldline, meaning it is crucial to second-order formalism as well as 2+1D and 3+1D calculations.
In this method one splits the retarded field into its singular and regular contributions and reformulates the field equations to solve directly for the residual field using an (effective) regular source term.
At first order, effective-source schemes have been implemented in time domain (TD)
Vega and Detweiler 2008
;
Dolan et al. 2011
;
Dolan and Barack 2013
;
Diener et al. 2012
and frequency domain (FD) calculations for quasi-circular orbits
Warburton and Wardell 2014
;
Wardell and Warburton 2015
.
Moreover, the approach is the workhorse of current second-order calculations.

I.2
The challenge of eccentric orbits

Extending the second-order calculation to eccentric orbits presents numerous theoretical and computational challenges.
Second-order calculations are presently carried out within a two-timescale framework
Miller and Pound 2021
which allows for a frequency domain approach where one works with ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for the metric perturbation.
In the time-domain, the second-order effective-source will be finitely differentiable at the particle’s worldline.
In contrast to circular orbits, for a particle in an eccentric orbit, the derivatives of the effective-source will be discontinuous functions of
t
at a fixed value of
r
.
Thus, one will have to reconstruct (into the time domain) a discontinuous function of
r
and
t
from its Fourier components and this will be tormented by the well known Gibbs phenomenon.
The standard Fourier transform of the source is therefore very slowly convergent and worse yet, the resultant regular field and its derivatives may not converge at all.

These Gibbs phenomenon issues affect self-force calculations at both first- and second-order and a variety of techniques have been developed to over come the problem of slow convergence of the Fourier sum.
For a distributional source the method of extended homogeneous solutions (EHS) was developed
Barack et al. 2008
.
In this approach the frequency domain solutions that are valid on either side of the particle’s libration region are analytically extended through the source region.
It is then found that the sum over the extended solutions convergenes exponentially to the correct time-domain solution.
This approach relies crucially on the spacetime away from the particle’s worldline being a vacuum solution in the time domain.
The method was first developed and implemented for a single mode of a scalar field, but was later extended to full self-force calculations for scalar
Warburton and Barack 2011
;
Nasipak et al. 2019
and gravitational perturbations
Akcay et al. 2013
;
Osburn et al. 2014
;
van de Meent and Shah 2015
;
van de Meent 2018
.

In the second-order problem the effective-source is non-zero in a finite region around the worldline, and furthermore the full second-order source has unbounded support
Miller and Pound 2021
.
With no vacuum region around the particle, or indeed anywhere in the spacetime, the method of extended homogeneous solutions cannot be applied.
Fortunately, Hopper and Evens developed the method of extended particular solutions (EPS) to handle this case
Hopper and Evans 2013
.
In this approach a particular solution to the field equations is sought and then the retarded solution is constructed by comparison with the true solution outside the libration region where the Fourier sum convergences rapidly.

In this paper we develop a version of this method which applies to the effective-source problem where the particle’s worldline is an eccentric geodesic.
We call this modified approach the method of
extended effective-sources
(EES).
In lieu of the full second-order source we will consider a scalar self-force analogue to develop the method.
In our setup we consider a scalar point charge on an eccentric orbit of a non-rotating black hole.
The effective-source we will construct will have support within some finite worldtube and be zero outside.
Although we do not consider a source with unbounded support, as appears in second-order gravitational calculations, the same method can be applicable for such sources.

Inspired by Newtonian mechanics, we choose an alternative, more gemoetric,

III.2
Frequency domain decomposition

In this work we focus on the FD approach, where the field
\psi_{\ell m}(t,r)
and the charge density
\rho_{\ell m}(t,r)
are further decomposed into the Fourier frequency modes
\psi_{\ell mn}(r)
and
J_{\ell mn}(r)
respectively.
For a given multipole mode, the frequency spectrum is formed of discrete overtones of the two fundamental frequencies such that

\omega_{mn}:=m\Omega_{\varphi}+n\Omega_{r},\quad\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(24)

The FT of the multipole modes of the field,
\psi_{\ell m}(t,r)
, is therefore
given by

\psi_{\ell mn}(r)=\frac{1}{T_{r}}\int^{T_{r}}_{0}\psi_{\ell m}(t,r)e^{i\omega_{mn}t}dt,

(25)

where the inverse FT to reconstruct the TD solution reduces to a summation over

\psi_{\ell m}(t,r)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=-\infty}\psi_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}.

(26)

Similarly, the multipole modes of the charge density also admit a discrete Fourier decomposition of the form

\rho_{\ell m}(t,r)=-\frac{1}{4\pi r}\sum^{\infty}_{n=-\infty}J_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}.

(27)

We note that the multipole decomposition of the charge density,
\rho_{\ell m}(t,r)
, given in Eq. (
20

e^{-im\varphi_{p}(t)}

\Omega_{r}
.
However, following Eq. (
14

e^{-im\Omega_{\varphi}t}e^{-im\Delta\varphi_{p}(t)}

\rho_{\ell m}(t,r)\,e^{im\Omega_{\varphi}t}

Thus the source term and, by extension, the field
\psi_{\ell m}(t,r)
can be expressed as a Fourier series when multiplied by this appropriate phase factor.
This is equivalent to being in a frame with angular velocity
\Omega_{\varphi}
and to an asymptotic observer within this frame the radial and azimuthal motion would appear periodic

Taking the Fourier transform of Eq. (

III.4
Regularization

One of the main challenges when computing the SF is that the field of the
particle diverges at the location of the particle.
It is known though that a particular divergent contribution to the field near the particle does not contribute to the orbital evolution

;
Poisson et al. 2011

Instead the evolution of the inspiral is driven by a (self-)force that can be

IV
Construction of the standard effective-source in the frequency domain

We now wish to calculate the modes of the effective-source in the frequency domain.
One approach would be to use Eqs. (

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell mn}^{\mathcal{R}}(r)

after applying the

Furthermore, the result must then cancel the Fourier transform of the delta
function,

and from Eq. (
29
) we see this
diverges as
1/u^{r}
at the orbital turning points making numerical cancellation
very challenging.

We find the above approach of computing the Fourier transform of the puncture and then constructing the FD effective-source to be unworkable.
Instead, we can first compute the time-domain effective-source, as in Eq. (
III.4
), and then take the Fourier transform.
That is, we define

\displaystyle S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)=\frac{1}{T_{r}}\int_{0}^{T_{r}}S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}(t,r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}\,dt

(46)

where
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}(t,r)
is defined in Eq. (
III.4
).
This has the distinct advantage that before carrying out the Fourier
decomposition (i) the operator
\Box_{\ell m}
can be applied analytically during
the construction of
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}(t,r)
and (ii) the cancellation of the distributional
term in the source can thus be done analytically.

We still have to numerically evaluate the Fourier integral in Eq. (
46
) on a grid of radial values.
This is still challenging as the time-domain effective-source is piecewise continuous (
C^{0}
) at the instantaneous particle location
r_{p}(t)
.
This lack of smoothness results in the well-known Gibbs phenomenon and the Fourier sum

S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}(t,r)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t},

(47)

converges very slowly as
1/N
.
This slow convergence is shown with numerical results in Figs.
1
and
2
.
The non-smoothness of the effective-source also hampers the efficient calculation of
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)
as we cannot directly apply the efficient Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm.
One option is to directly numerically evaluate the integral in Eq. (
46
) for each frequency mode, though we find this to be quite inefficient.
Fortunately, we find an alternative convolution method that allows the FFT to be employed – see Sec.
VII.1
below for details.

V
Calculation of the residual field using a worldtube method

We now present the standard calculation of the residual field using a worldtube method.
As we shall see, the convergence rate of the partial sum over Fourier modes of the residual field is very slow when using the standard effective-source approach.

V.1
Frequency domain worldtube method

Our goal is compute the residual field inside a worldtube of finite size around the particle’s worldline.
To achieve this we need a scheme for working with a worldtube in the frequency domain.
A version of such a scheme was outlined in Ref.
Warburton and Wardell 2014
but here we present a simpler form based on notes originally made by Barack
Barack 2019
.

The frequency domain field equation we want to solve takes the form of Eq. (
45
) where the effective-source is non-zero inside a worldtube with boundaries coinciding with the edges of the libration region,
r_{\text{min}}
and
r_{\text{max}}
.
Outside of the worldtube we will solve
\Box_{\ell mn}\psi_{\ell mn}=0
.
Let
\psi^{\infty/h}_{\ell mn}
be two independent homogeneous solutions satisfying retarded boundary conditions in the respective domains
r\longrightarrow 2M
and
r\longrightarrow\infty
.

We seek a solution of the following form,

\psi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell mn}(r)=\begin{cases}a_{\ell mn}^{h}\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r),&r\leq r_{\text{min}},\\
b_{\ell mn}^{\infty}\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)+b_{\ell mn}^{h}\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)+\psi^{\text{inh}}_{\ell mn}(r),&r_{\text{min}}<r<r_{\text{max}},\\
a_{\ell mn}^{\infty}\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r),&r\geq r_{\text{max}}.\end{cases}

(48)

where
a_{\ell mn}^{\infty/h}
and
b_{\ell mn}^{\infty/h}
are constants.

Here
\psi^{\text{inh}}_{\ell mn}(r)
is the particular inhomogeneous solution found from the standard variations of parameters approach

\displaystyle\psi^{\text{inh}}_{\ell mn}(r)=C_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)+C_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)

(49)

with

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\text{min}}}^{r}\frac{\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r^{\prime})S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime}),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})]}dr^{\prime}

(50)

\displaystyle C^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)

\displaystyle=\int^{r_{\text{max}}}_{r}\frac{\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r^{\prime})S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime}),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})]}dr^{\prime}

(51)

where
W[\psi_{1},\psi_{2}]:=\psi_{1}\frac{d\psi_{2}}{dr}-\psi_{2}\frac{d\psi_{1}}{dr}
is the Wronskian.
The unknown coefficients
a_{\ell mn}^{\pm}
and
b_{\ell mn}^{\pm}
are determined from the conditions that
\psi_{\ell mn}=\psi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell mn}+\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}
and
d\psi_{\ell mn}/dr
are continuous at the worldtube boundaries,
r=r_{\text{min}}
and
r=r_{\text{max}}
.
This gives

\displaystyle a_{\ell mn}^{\infty}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})}\big\{\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})[b_{\ell mn}^{\infty}+C_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r_{\text{max}})]

\displaystyle+b_{\ell mn}^{h}\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})+\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})\big\},

(52)

\displaystyle a_{\ell mn}^{h}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})}\big\{b_{\ell mn}^{\infty}\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})

\displaystyle+\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})[b_{\ell mn}^{h}+C_{\ell mn}^{h}(r_{\text{min}})]+\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})\big\}.

(53)

Similarly, the coefficients
b^{h/\infty}_{\ell mn}
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}b^{\infty}&=\left.\frac{W[\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)]}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{min}}},\\
b^{h}&=\left.\frac{W[\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)]}{W[\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{max}}}.\end{split}

(54)

V.2
The residual field computed from the standard effective-source

Given an effective-source, the Fourier modes of the regular field can be calculated using the variation of parameters with a worldtube approach outlined above.
The
\ell m
-mode of the residual field are then constructed via

\displaystyle\psi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell m}(t,r)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\psi_{\ell mn}^{\mathcal{R}}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}

(55)

The smoothness of the resulting regular field,
\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{R}}(t,r)
, at the
location of the particle depends upon the order of the puncture used to
construct the effective-source.
This in turn effects the rate of convergence of the Fourier sum in
Eq. (
55
).
In Appendix
A
we present the puncture through

\mathcal{O}(\Delta r)
where
\Delta r=r-r_{p}(t)
.
This puncture gives a regular field that is
C^{1}
in the radial direction at
the location of the particle and is thus sufficient to calculate the
self-force.
We find the partial sum when including up to
n=\pm N
terms in Eq. (
55
) converges very slowly as
1/N
.
This rate of convergence can be improved by using a high-order puncture, though the convergence remains a power law.
Using a puncture through
\mathcal{O}(\Delta r^{4})
the convergence improves to
1/N^{3}
– see Fig.
3
.
The explicit form of the higher-order puncture can be found in the supplmental material accompanying this work
sup
.

In principle higher-order punctures could be derived to further accelerate the
convergence of the Fourier sum.
Deriving higher-order punctures becomes increasingly more challenging as the
order in
\Delta r
increases
Heffernan et al. 2012
;
Heffernan et al. 2018
and
even with higher-order punctures the convergence would still be algebraic.
Instead we now seek a method to restore exponential convergence to the Fourier
sum inside the worldtube.
Note in the worldtube method with a source that is zero outside the libration region means that the convergence outside the worldtube is exponential.

.

\sim N^{-3}
.

Barack et al. 2008
.

VI
Construction of the Extended Effective-Sources

The slow convergence of the partial sum over Fourier modes of the residual field in Eq. (
55
) is reminiscent of the slow convergence of the retarded field calculation when using the standard delta function source
Barack et al. 2008
.
In that case the method of
extended homogeneous solutions
was developed in order to restore exponential convergence
Barack et al. 2008
.
For a review of the EHS method, see Appendix
B
.
That approach relied crucially on the perturbation away from the particle’s worldline being a solution to the vacuum field equations.
As such this approach cannot be applied with an effective-source as it is non-zero in a finite region around the wordline.
Later, in the context of gauge transformations, Ref.
Hopper and Evans 2013
, devised the method of
extended particular solutions
as a way restoring exponential convergence when summing over inhomogeneous frequency modes from an extended source.
In this section we present an equivalent approach that also incorporates the puncture scheme through a worldtube method.
We call our approach the method of
extended effective-sources
(EES).

We begin by analytically extending the effective-source either side of the particle’s location to form two smooth functions across the libration region:
S^{\text{eff},+}_{\ell m}(t,r)
and
S^{\text{eff},-}_{\ell m}(t,r)
.
For any
t
and
r
, the true effective-source in the TD is given by

S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}(t,r)=S^{\text{eff},+}_{\ell m}(t,r)\Theta^{+}(t,r)+S^{\text{eff},-}_{\ell m}(t,r)\Theta^{-}(t,r).

(56)

where the Heaviside functions are given by
\Theta^{\pm}(t,r)=\Theta[\pm(r-r_{p}(t))]
.
The construction of the extended effective-sources is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

,

and

,
with

, for a particle at

with orbital parameters
p=10M
and

.
The position of the particle is indicated by
r_{p}(t)
.
The function
S^{\text{eff},-}_{\ell m}(r)
is an extension of the effective-source from
r_{\text{min}}\leq r<r_{p}(t)
to
r_{\text{max}}
whilst
S^{\text{eff},+}_{\ell m}(r)
is an extension of the effective
source from
r_{p}(t)<r\leq r_{\text{max}}
to
r_{\text{min}}
.

We plot the an example of the Fourier modes of the effective-source and the extended effective-sources in Fig.

.

for

.
The effective-source is highly oscillatory with the number of oscillations growing as

increases.
Note the extended effective-source
S^{\text{eff},+}_{\ell mn}(r)
coincides with the effective-source at
r=r_{\text{max}}(\chi=\pi)
and similarly for
S^{\text{eff},-}_{\ell mn}(r)
at

r=r_{\text{min}}(\chi=0)
.

In the FD these extended sources transform as

S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)=\frac{1}{T_{r}}\int^{T_{r}}_{0}S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell m}(t,r)e^{i\omega_{mn}t}dt,

(57)

and the respective series representations of these extended sources in the TD
is then

S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell m}(t,r)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=-\infty}S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}.

(58)

We will also need extended puncture fields which we defined in an analogous
way:

\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P}}(t,r)=\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P},+}(t,r)\Theta^{+}(t,r)+\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P},-}(t,r)\Theta^{-}(t,r)

(59)

The extended punctures can be be expanded as Fourier series via

\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P},\pm}(t,r)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{\mathcal{P},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t},

(60)

where

\psi^{\mathcal{P},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)=\frac{1}{T_{r}}\int_{0}^{T_{r}}\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P},\pm}(t,r)e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,dt.

(61)

It is useful to note that
\psi^{\mathcal{P},+}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})=\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})
and
\psi^{\mathcal{P},-}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})=\psi^{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})
.
Since
S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell m}(t,r)
and
\psi_{\ell m}^{\mathcal{P},\pm}(t,r)
are smooth functions the Fourier sums in Eqs. (
58
) and (
60
) converge exponentially – see Fig.
6
.
Furthermore, the FFT algorithm can be employed to evaluate the integrals in (
57
) and (
61
).

We now proceed using a modified version of the worldtube method presented in
Sec.
V.1
whereby we integrate over the
extended effective-sources and ensure the resulting solution smoothly attaches
to the retarded solution at the worldtube boundaries.

To calculate the residual field we begin by defining extended regular solutions

\psi^{\mathcal{R},\pm}_{\ell mn}
via

\psi^{\mathcal{R},-}_{\ell mn}(r)=\begin{cases}a_{\ell mn}^{h}\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r),&r\leq r_{\text{min}}\\
b_{\ell mn}^{\infty,-}\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)+b_{\ell mn}^{h,-}\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)+\psi_{\ell mn}^{{\rm inh},-}(r),&r_{\text{min}}<r<r_{\text{max}}.\\
\end{cases}

(62)

and

\psi^{\mathcal{R},+}_{\ell mn}(r)=\begin{cases}b_{\ell mn}^{\infty,+}\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)+b_{\ell mn}^{h,+}\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)+\psi^{\text{inh},+}_{\ell mn}(r),&r_{\text{min}}<r<r_{\text{max}},\\
a_{\ell mn}^{\infty}\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r),&r\geq r_{\text{max}}.\end{cases}

(63)

where
a_{\ell mn}^{h}
and
a_{\ell mn}^{\infty}
are the constants computed by integrating over the true effective-source as given by Eq. (
53
).

The extended inhomogeneous solutions are computed via the usual variation of parameter approach such that

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell mn}^{{\rm inh},\pm}(r)=C^{\infty,\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)+C^{h,\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)

(64)

with

\displaystyle C_{\ell mn}^{\infty,\pm}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\text{min}}}^{r}\frac{\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime}),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})]}\,dr^{\prime}

(65)

\displaystyle C_{\ell mn}^{h,\pm}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{r_{\text{max}}}\frac{\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime}),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r^{\prime})]}\,dr^{\prime}.

(66)

We find the values of
b^{h,\pm}_{\ell mn}
and
b^{\infty,\pm}_{\ell mn}
by
requiring that
\psi^{+}_{\ell mn}:=\psi^{\mathcal{R},+}_{\ell mn}+\psi^{\mathcal{P},+}_{\ell mn}=\psi_{\ell mn}
at
r=r_{\text{max}}
and
\psi^{-}_{\ell mn}:=\psi^{\mathcal{R},-}_{\ell mn}+\psi^{\mathcal{P},-}_{\ell mn}=\psi_{\ell mn}
at
r=r_{\text{min}}
. This gives

\displaystyle b^{\infty,+}_{\ell mn}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{W[\kappa^{\infty,+}_{\ell mn}(r)\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)-\psi^{\mathcal{P},+}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)]}{W[\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{max}}},

(67)

\displaystyle b^{\infty,-}_{\ell mn}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{W[\kappa^{h,-}_{\ell mn}(r)\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)-\psi^{\mathcal{P},-}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r)]}{W[\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{min}}},

(68)

where

\displaystyle\kappa^{\infty,+}_{\ell mn}(r)

\displaystyle:=a^{\infty}_{\ell mn}-C^{\infty,+}_{\ell mn}(r),

(69)

\displaystyle\kappa^{h,-}_{\ell mn}(r)

\displaystyle:=a^{h}_{\ell mn}-C^{h,-}_{\ell mn}(r).

(70)

and

\displaystyle b^{h,+}_{\ell mn}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{W[\psi^{\mathcal{P},+}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)]}{W[\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{max}}},

(71)

\displaystyle b^{h,-}_{\ell mn}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{W[\psi^{\mathcal{P},-}_{\ell mn}(r),\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r)]}{W[\psi_{\ell mn}^{h}(r),\psi_{\ell mn}^{\infty}(r)]}\right|_{r=r_{\text{min}}}.

(72)

The time domain residual solution is then given by

\displaystyle\psi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell m}(t,r)=\psi^{\mathcal{R},+}_{\ell m}(t,r)\Theta^{+}(t,r)+\psi^{\mathcal{R},-}_{\ell m}(t,r)\Theta^{-}(t,r),

(73)

where

\displaystyle\psi^{\mathcal{R},\pm}_{\ell m}(t,r)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{\mathcal{R},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)e^{-i\omega_{mn}t}.

(74)

The key result of our method is that Eq. (
74
) convergences exponentially to the true residual field.
We empirically demonstrate this in the results section
VII.4
below.

VII
Implementation and numerical results

In this section we describe our numerical scheme and present some sample
results.

VII.1
Fourier expansion of the effective-source by convolution

One challenge that arises with our method is the practical calculation of
Fourier transform of the effective-source, as defined in
Eq. (
46
).
For any given radius within the libration region the effective-source is
non-smooth at the time the particle crosses that radius.
Let us define this time to be
t_{p}(r)
with
0\leq t_{p}(r)\leq T_{r}/2
.
This non-smoothness hampers the efficient calculation of the Fourier modes of
the
effective-source as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) will converge very slowly.

An alternative approach that will allow us to employ the FFT is to make use of
the
form of the effective-source given in Eq. (
56
).
We proceed by noting that the Fourier transform of a product of functions is
given by the convolution of the Fourier transforms of the individual functions.
The functions
S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell m}(r)
are smooth and so the coefficients of their Fourier
expansions, given by Eq. (
58
), can be computed efficiently
using the FFT algorithm.
The Fourier series of the Heaviside step function is given analytically by:

\displaystyle\Theta^{\pm}(t,r)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}b^{\pm}_{n}(r)e^{-in\Omega_{r}t}

(75)

where

\displaystyle b^{+}_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2t_{p}(r)}{T_{r}},

(76)

\displaystyle b^{-}_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2t_{p}(r)}{T_{r}},

(77)

\displaystyle b^{\pm}_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{n\pi}\sin\left(\frac{2n\pi\,t_{p}(r)}{T_{r}}\right)

(78)

We can now calculate the Fourier transform of
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell m}
using its form in
Eq. (
56
), via convolution

\displaystyle S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[b^{+}_{n^{\prime}-n}(r)S^{\text{eff},+}_{\ell mn}(r)+b^{-}_{n^{\prime}-n}(r)S^{\text{eff},-}_{\ell mn}(r)\right]

(79)

We find this approach allows us to efficiently calculate
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)
.

VII.2
Interpolating the Fourier modes of the effective-source using Chebyshev polynomials

The above technique allows us to efficiently compute the Fourier transform of the modes of the effective-source at a given radius.
In order to compute the associated residual field we need to integrate this source across the radial libration region as in Eqs. (
50
) and Eqs. (
65
)-(
66
).
We achieve this using an efficient Chebyshev interpolation scheme which we
describe now.

One can express a smooth function
g(x)
in terms of Chebyshev series,

g(x)=\sum^{\cal N}_{k=1}c^{({\cal N},\,g)}_{k}T_{k}(x),

(80)

where
\cal N\in\mathbb{Z}
is the Chebyshev expansion order,
c^{(\cal N)}_{k}
are the (spectral) Chebyshev coefficients which implicitly depend on
the order
{\cal N}
, and
T_{k}(x)=\cos[k\arccos(x)]
are the Chebyshev
polynomials of the first kind. The Chebyshev polynomials form an orthonormal
basis on the interval
x\in[-1,1]
such that

\int^{1}_{-1}\frac{T_{n}(x)T_{m}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\delta_{nm}.

(81)

While it is not immediately obvious from their definition, the Chebyshev
functions
T_{k}(x)
, are in fact simple polynomials in
x
of degree
k
. As
such, these polynomials have
k
real and distinct zeros within the interval
x\in[-1,1]
:

x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi(2k+1)}{2j+2}\right),\quad k=0,1,\dots,j.

(82)

One can leverage this property to fix the Chebyshev coefficients
c^{(\cal N)}_{k}
in order to obtain a global polynomial interpolant for the function

g(x)
. We introduce a discrete grid that coinicide with the roots of the
Chebyshev polynomials and require at these points Eq. (
80
) is
exactly equal to the function
g(x)
. There are several possible choices one
can make for such a grid, and thereby different resultant expressions for the
Chebyshev coefficients. Here we choose a Chebyshev-Lobatto grid, whereby

x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{{\cal N}-1}\right),\quad k=0,1,\,\dots,{\cal N}-1.

(83)

The reason for this choice is that our worldtube method requires the FT of the puncture and, by virtue, the resultant effective-source to be evaluated at the boundaries of the worldtube.
Hence, we require an accurate interpolant at these extrema.
In this case, the Chebyshev coefficients are given by

\displaystyle c^{({\cal N},\,g)}_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\frac{2-\delta_{k,0}-\delta_{k,\,{\cal N}-1}}{{\cal N}-1}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{2}[g(x_{0})+(-1)^{k}g(x_{{\cal N}-1})]+\sum^{{\cal N}-2}_{j=1}g(x_{j})T_{j}(x_{k})\right).

(84)

Inserting Eq. (
83
) into
Eq. (
84
), one finds

c^{({\cal N},\,g)}_{k}=(-1)^{k}\sqrt{\frac{2-\delta_{k,0}-\delta_{k,\,{\cal N}-1}}{{\cal N}-1}}\,{\cal G}_{k},

(85)

where
{\cal G}_{k}
are the real Fourier coefficients given by the discrete
cosine transform of type I (DCT-1),

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{{\cal N}-1}}\times\Bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[g(x_{0})+(-1)^{k}g(x_{{\cal N}-1})]

\displaystyle+\sum^{{\cal N}-2}_{j=1}g(x_{j})\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{{\cal N}-1}j\right)\Bigg).

(86)

This is not a surprising result as a Chebyshev series is simply a Fourier
cosine series under a change of variable; but the implication allows one to use
FFT methods in order to compute the nodes for the radial interpolants.
Furthermore, as the extended effective-sources are
C^{\infty}
-differentiable

opposed to algebraically as in interpolation with splines.

Thus far, we have described Chebyshev expansion for a generic function defined
on the interval
x\in[-1,1]

interpolation of the effective-source. The effective-sources are defined only

of the particle, i.e.

x=\frac{2r-(r_{\text{max}}+r_{\text{min}})}{r_{\text{max}}-r_{\text{min}}}.

(87)

Inverting this relation yields the Chebyshev-Lobatto grid in terms of the
radial coordinate,

\displaystyle r_{k}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(r_{\text{min}}+r_{\text{max}})+\frac{1}{2}(r_{\text{max}}-r_{\text{min}})x_{k},

\displaystyle k=0,1,\dots,\,{\cal N}-1,

(88)

allowing us to compute the relevant Chebyshev nodes of our effective-source.
We can then expand the effective-source in Eq. (

in terms of Chebyshev polynomials in the form of Eq. (

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{{\cal N}-1}}\times\Bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{min}})+(-1)^{k}S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r_{\text{max}})]

\displaystyle+\sum^{{\cal N}-2}_{j=1}S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r_{j})\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{{\cal N}-1}j\right)\Bigg).

(90)

The same expressions are also applicable to the extended effective-sources

S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)

There is one additional subtlety that must be considered when considering the interpolation of the convolved source
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)
.
To achieve the desired spectral (exponential) convergence of the interpolant the sampled function needs to be analytic throughout the domain.
The extended sources,
S^{\text{eff},\pm}_{\ell mn}(r)
, are indeed
C^{\infty}
-differentiable and therefore one finds the exponential decay of the Chebyshev coefficients.
In the form written in Eq. (
79

is only finitely differentiable at
r_{\text{min}}
and
r_{\text{max}}
.
This is because we have introduced the function

If left in this form, the convergence of the interpolation of
S^{\text{eff}}_{\ell mn}(r)
would be merely algebraic.
One can avoid this problem by changing variables from
r\longrightarrow r_{p}(\chi)
, which leads to
t_{p}(r)\longrightarrow t_{p}(\chi)

in Sec.
VI
will remain in the same as previously, except with a change of variables from

become

\displaystyle C_{\ell mn}^{\infty,\pm}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\chi}\frac{\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime}))S^{\pm}_{\ell mn}(\chi^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime})),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime}))]}\frac{dr_{p}}{d\chi^{\prime}}\,d\chi^{\prime}

(91)

\displaystyle C_{\ell mn}^{h,\pm}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\chi}^{\pi}\frac{\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime}))S^{\pm}_{\ell mn}(\chi^{\prime})}{W[\psi^{h}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime})),\psi^{\infty}_{\ell mn}(r_{p}(\chi^{\prime}))]}\frac{dr_{p}}{d\chi^{\prime}}\,d\chi^{\prime}.

(92)

One can then transform the resultant functions back to radial functions by a simple inversion of Eq. (

\chi(r)=\arccos\left(\frac{p-r}{e\,r}\right).

(93)

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper we have formulated an effective-source approach for eccentric orbits in the frequency domain.
The method allows one to overcome the Gibbs phenomenon and associated slow convergence experienced with naive frequency domain computations that use an effective-source.
As an example we show how the method can be used to compute the residual scalar field for a compact source moving on an eccentric orbit in a Schwarzschild background.
Our results were validated against those obtained using an independent implementation of the method of extended homogeneous solutions.
Crucially, with our new method we find the Fourier modes of the residual scalar field converge exponentially.

We find our method to be reasonably computational efficient with the main bottleneck being the need to interpolate the highly oscillatory standard effective-source.
It would be interesting to explore if the oscillatory nature of the effective-source could be understood analytically and thus removed to leave behind a more slowly varying numerical residual to be interpolated.

The main motivation for the development of our extended effective-sources approach was for application to second-order gravitational self-force calculations.
Although in this paper we only considered a source with support in a finite region around the libration region our method should extend to sources with unbounded support such as appear in the second-order field equations
Miller and Pound 2021
.
With our new method there is now no remaining obstacle to computing Lorenz-gauge second-order results for eccentric orbits on a Schwarzschild background, although in practice this will be a very significant undertaking.

Looking further to the future, it will be important push second-order GSF calculations to Kerr spacetime.
This will likely first be attempted for circular, equatorial orbits in either the Lorenz gauge
Dolan et al. 2021
or a emerging second-order Teukolsky frameworks
Spiers et al. 2023
.
As the majority of EMRIs are expected to be quite eccentric whilst emitting gravitational waves in the LISA band it will be important to apply the results of this paper to second-order perturbations for a body on an eccentric orbit around a Kerr black hole.
