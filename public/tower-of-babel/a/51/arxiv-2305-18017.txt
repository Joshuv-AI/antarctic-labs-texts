Trace models of concurrent valuation algebras
Naso Evangelou-Oost, Larissa Meinicke, Callum Bannister, Ian J. Hayes

Abstract
This paper introduces Concurrent Valuation Algebras (CVAs), a novel extension of ordered valuation algebras (OVAs). CVAs include two combine operators representing parallel and sequential products, adhering to a weak exchange law. This development offers theoretical and practical benefits for the specification and modelling of concurrent and distributed systems. As a presheaf on a space of domains, CVAs enable localised specifications, supporting modularity, compositionality, and the ability to represent large and complex systems. Furthermore, CVAs align with lattice-based refinement reasoning and are compatible with established methodologies such as Hoare and Rely-Guarantee logics. The flexibility of CVAs is explored through three trace models, illustrating distinct paradigms of concurrent/distributed computing, interrelated by morphisms. The paper also highlights the potential to incorporate a powerful local computation framework from valuation algebras for model checking in concurrent and distributed systems. The foundational results presented have been verified with the proof assistant Isabelle/HOL.

Introduction

Valuation algebras are versatile algebraic structures that parameterise information across multiple domains, representing for example subsets of variables or events. These structures have been widely utilised across diverse disciplines such as database theory, logic, probability and statistics, and constraint satisfaction, among others. What sets valuation algebras apart is their robust computational theory, enabling the deployment of highly efficient distributed algorithms for addressing inference problems that involve information combination and querying

A prealgebra
\mathbf{\Phi}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{Pos}
comprises a family of posets
\left\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{T}}
parameterised by the domains of the space
\mathcal{T}
, and a family of monotone restriction maps
\left\{a\mapsto a^{\downarrow{B}}:\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\to\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}\right\}_{A,B\in\mathcal{T},B\subseteq A}
parameterised by the inclusions of the space.

The elements of the posets
\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
represent abstract units of information pertaining to their domain.
Their ordering signifies information refinement:
a\preceq b
means
a
is
more
deterministic than
b
, a convention that aligns with the intuitions of program refinement.

The prealgebra’s restriction maps
a\mapsto a^{\downarrow{B}}
serve to project or query information
a\in A
onto a subdomain
B\subseteq A
.
These mappings facilitate the extraction of specific details from a wider context.
Further, restriction maps are transitive and idempotent: for
C\subseteq B\subseteq A
and
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
, we have
(a^{\downarrow{B}})^{\downarrow{C}}=a^{\downarrow{C}}
, and
a^{\downarrow{A}}=a
.

This family of posets
\left\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{T}}
can be unified into a single poset
\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, through a canonical process known as the
Grothendieck construction
of
\mathbf{\Phi}
(for a detailed explanation within a broader context, refer to
[
18
]
).

Definition 1
(covariant Grothendieck construction for a prealgebra)

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{Pos}
be a prealgebra.
The
covariant Grothendieck construction
of
\mathbf{\Phi}
is the poset
(\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}},\preceq)
whose elements are pairs
(A,a)\in\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
where
A\in\mathcal{T}
and
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
, and whose ordering
\preceq
is defined

(A,a)\preceq(B,b)\text{ if and only if }B\subseteq A\text{ and }a^{\downarrow{B}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}}b

(1)

For the projection map
\mathrm{d}:\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\to{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{op}},(A,a)\mapsto A
, call
\mathrm{d}a
the
domain of
a
.

Notation.

As shorthand, we suppress the domain in the first component of elements
(A,a)
belonging to
\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, writing
a
instead of
(A,a)
.

In

Next, the concept of an
ordered valuation algebra (OVA)
is introduced, which incorporates a prealgebra
\mathbf{\Phi}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{Pos}
, a binary operator
\otimes:\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\times\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\to\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, and a global element
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}:\mathbf{1}\Rightarrow\mathbf{\Phi}
, satisfying a number of axioms.
Before delving into the formal definition, we illustrate the concept with an example.

Example 2

A familiar instance of an OVA models
relational databases
. Here, a set
X
of
attributes
is fixed (e.g.,
X=\{\text{{`name'}},\text{{`age'}},\text{{`height'}}\}
).
A
schema
is a subset
A
of
X
defining a table’s columns, while each row defines a
tuple
: an assignment of a value to each attribute. A
relation
on
X
is a set
a
of tuples sharing a common schema
\mathrm{d}a
, and a relational database is a set of such relations.

To frame this within an OVA, we define a prealgebra
\mathbf{\Phi}:\mathbf{P}(X)\to\mathcal{Pos}
, mapping a schema
A\in\mathcal{T}
to the poset
\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
of all relations with schema
A
, with ordering given by inclusion.
The restriction maps of
\mathbf{\Phi}
correspond to
querying
, by projecting the tuples of a relation
a
to a sub-schema
B
; the result is the relation
a^{\downarrow{B}}=\left\{t^{\downarrow{B}}\mid t\in a\right\}
, where
t^{\downarrow{B}}
is the tuple
t
restricted to the attributes in
B
.

The operator
\otimes
is taken to be the
natural join
,

\displaystyle\mathbin{\bowtie}

\displaystyle:\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\times\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\to\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}

(2)

\displaystyle a\mathbin{\bowtie}b

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\left\{t\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b}\mid t^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a}}\in a\text{ and }t^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}b}}\in b\right\}

This operation is associative and monotone (forming an
ordered semigroup
), and the schema of
a\mathbin{\bowtie}b
is
\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b
.
Moreover, the natural join satisfies the following
combination axiom
:

(a\mathbin{\bowtie}b)^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a}}=a\mathbin{\bowtie}b^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a\cap\mathrm{d}b}}

(3)

This identity is fundamental to query optimisation algorithms in relational databases, with its right-hand side referred to as a
semi-join
.

Lastly, the global element
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}
assigns to each schema
A
the universal relation
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}
on
A
, encompassing all possible tuples on
A
.
These universal relations serve as units for the natural join, i.e. for all
a\in\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, we have
a\mathbin{\bowtie}\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}=a=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}\mathbin{\bowtie}a
.

Please note that our definition of an OVA below deviates from standard ones (e.g.
[
16
,
1
,
8
]
) in several ways.
First, we do not mandate commutativity of the operator
\otimes
, as a sequential product of programs is noncommutative.
This requires a symmetric revision

7
of the
combination axiom
.
Second, constraints in the classical definition such as the existence of infima in the posets
\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
are not imposed.
Yet, we stipulate that
neutral
valuations correspond to a global element, which is tantamount to the
stability
property in
[
16
]
, though we do not require neutral valuations to combine to neutral valuations—we call an algebra in which this property holds

(
Definition

4
).
Last, while Grothendieck constructions have been applied to ordered valuation algebras (
[
5
]
), their conventional definition does not involve a Grothendieck ordering.

Definition 2
(ordered valuation algebra (OVA))

An
ordered valuation algebra (OVA)
is a triple
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})
, where
\mathbf{\Phi}
is a prealgebra
\mathbf{\Phi}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{Pos}
,
\otimes
is a binary operator

\otimes:\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\times\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\to\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
,
called the
combine operator
,
and
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}:\mathbf{1}\Rightarrow\mathbf{\Phi}
is a global element, called the
neutral element
, satisfying the below four axioms for all
valuations

a,b,c,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
:

Ordered semigroup.

The combine operator
\otimes
is associative, and monotone:

a\otimes(b\otimes c)=(a\otimes b)\otimes c\text{, and, }a\preceq a^{\prime}\text{ and }b\preceq b^{\prime}\implies a\otimes b\preceq a^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime}

(4)

Labelling.

\mathrm{d}(a\otimes b)=\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b

(5)

Neutrality.

\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}\otimes a=a=a\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}

(6)

Combination.

(a\otimes b)^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a}}=a\otimes b^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a\cap\mathrm{d}b}},\qquad(a\otimes b)^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}b}}=a^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a\cap\mathrm{d}b}}\otimes b

(7)

Remark 2

The functor laws for
\mathbf{\Phi}
imply that for all
C\subseteq B\subseteq A
and
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
, we have
(a^{\downarrow{B}})^{\downarrow{C}}=a^{\downarrow{C}}
, and also
a^{\downarrow{A}}=a
.
The requirement that
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}
is a global element says for all
B\subseteq A
in
\mathcal{T}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}^{\downarrow{B}}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}
.

Remark 3

Monotonicity of
\otimes
says for
a_{1}\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A_{1}}
,
a_{2}\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A_{2}}
,
b_{1}\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{B_{1}}
,
b_{2}\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{B_{2}}
,
if
a_{1}\preceq b_{1}
and
a_{2}\preceq b_{2}
,
then

a_{1}\otimes a_{2}\preceq b_{1}\otimes b_{2}
,
that is,

(a_{1}\otimes a_{2})^{\downarrow{B_{1}\cup B_{2}}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{B_{1}\cup B_{2}}}b_{1}\otimes b_{2}
.
Taking
A_{1}=A_{2}=B_{1}=B_{2}
, this implies
local monotonicity
, i.e. the combine operator
\otimes
restricted to each domain
A
,
\otimes_{A}:\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\times\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\to\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
, is monotone.

Definition 3
(commutative OVA)

We call an OVA
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})

commutative
if
\otimes
is commutative.

Definition 4
(strongly neutral)

We call an OVA
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})

strongly neutral
if for all inclusions
B\subseteq A
in
\mathcal{T}
, we have

\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}^{\uparrow{A}}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}

Theorem 2.1

Let
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})
be an OVA.
Then for each inclusion
B\subseteq A
in
\mathcal{T}
, the restriction map
a\mapsto a^{\downarrow{B}}
has a right adjoint given by
b\mapsto\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b
.
Moreover, these right adjoints assemble to a functor
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{Pos}
.
We adopt the notation
b^{\uparrow{A}}\vcentcolon=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b
, and call
b^{\uparrow{A}}
the
extension of
b
to
A
.

Proof

We must show that for all
B\subseteq A
and all
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
and
b\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}
,

a^{\downarrow{B}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}}b\iff a\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}}\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b

Assume
a^{\downarrow{B}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}}b
.
Using the fact that
a\preceq a^{\downarrow{B}}
and monotonicity,

a=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes a\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}}\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes a^{\downarrow{B}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}}\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b

Now assume
a\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}}\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b
.
Using monotonicity of restriction, the combination axiom, naturality of
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}
, and neutrality,

a^{\downarrow{B}}\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}}(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b)^{\downarrow{B}}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\otimes b=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}\otimes b=b

So the adjunction holds.
That extension is functorial (i.e. for
C\subseteq B\subseteq A
and
c\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{C}
, both
(c^{\uparrow{B}})^{\uparrow{A}}=c^{\uparrow{A}}
and
c^{\uparrow{C}}=c
) is due to the composability and uniqueness of adjoints.

Corollary 1

Let
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})
be a strongly neutral OVA.
Then for all
A,B
in
\mathcal{T}
,

\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}
.
Also,
\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
is an ordered monoid.

Proof

We have,

\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}=(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B})=(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B})=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A\cup B}
.
If
a\in\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
,

a\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\emptyset}=(a\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a})\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\emptyset}=a\otimes(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\emptyset})=a\otimes\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}a}=a
.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{\emptyset}
is a unit for
\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
.

Corollary 2

Let
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})
be an OVA, and let
B\subseteq A
in
\mathcal{T}
,
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
, and
b\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}
.
Then,

1.

Restriction after extension is the identity map, i.e.,

(b^{\uparrow{A}})^{\downarrow{B}}=b
.

2.

Extension after restriction is
extensive
, i.e.,

a\leq_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}}(a^{\downarrow{B}})^{\uparrow{A}}
.

Proof

For the first claim, by the neutrality and combination axioms and naturality, we have

(b^{\uparrow{A}})^{\downarrow{B}}=(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}\otimes b)^{\downarrow{B}}=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A}^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\otimes b=\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{B}\otimes b=b
.
The second is always true of the composition of a right adjoint after its left adjoint.

Corollary 3

If for each

,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
is a complete lattice, then so is

.

Proof

See

, (ii) completeness of each poset

Definition 5
(morphism of OVAs)

Let
(\mathbf{\Phi},\otimes,\boldsymbol{\upepsilon})
and
(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}^{\prime})
be OVAs.
A
lax morphism
f:\mathbf{\Phi}\to\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}
is a family of monotone maps
\left\{f_{A}:\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}\to\mathbf{\Phi}^{\prime}_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{T}}
so that the below hold for all
a\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{A}
,
b\in\mathbf{\Phi}_{B}
and
C\subseteq A
,

Monotonicity.

a\preceq b\implies f_{A}(a)\preceq f_{B}(b)

(8)

Lax naturality.

f_{A}(a)^{\downarrow{C}}\preceq f_{C}(a^{\downarrow{C}})

(9)

Lax multiplicativity.

f_{A}(a)\otimes^{\prime}f_{B}(b)\preceq f_{A\cup B}(a\otimes b)

(10)

Lax unitality.

\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}^{\prime}_{A}\preceq f_{A}(\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}_{A})

(11)

Reversing the inequality directions above defines a
colax morphism
. A morphism that is both lax and colax is termed a

.

We define the

to be the binary operator,

\displaystyle\odot

\displaystyle:\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\times\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}\to\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}

\displaystyle a\odot b

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=a^{\uparrow{\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b}}\odot_{\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b}b^{\uparrow{\mathrm{d}a\cup\mathrm{d}b}}

is the extension of the family

Concurrent valuation algebras

We now introduce a

, structured as two OVAs sharing the same underlying prealgebra, whose combine operators represent

These operators are interlinked via a

, and their neutral elements are related by a pair of inequalities.

Definition 7
(concurrent valuation algebra (CVA))

A
concurrent valuation algebra (CVA)
is a structure

(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbin{\fatsemi},\mathbf{\mathtt{skip}},\mathbin{\parallel},\mathbf{\mathtt{run}})
satisfying the four axioms:

Sequential OVA.

(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbin{\fatsemi},\mathbf{\mathtt{skip}})
is an OVA.

Parallel OVA.

(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbin{\parallel},\mathbf{\mathtt{run}})
is a commutative OVA.

Weak exchange.

For all
a,b,c,d\in\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
,

(a\mathbin{\parallel}b)\mathbin{\fatsemi}(c\mathbin{\parallel}d)\preceq(a\mathbin{\fatsemi}c)\mathbin{\parallel}(b\mathbin{\fatsemi}d)

Neutral laws.

For all
A\in\mathcal{T}
,

\mathbf{\mathtt{skip}}_{A}\preceq\mathbf{\mathtt{skip}}_{A}\mathbin{\parallel}\mathbf{\mathtt{skip}}_{A}\text{, and, }\mathbf{\mathtt{run}}_{A}\mathbin{\fatsemi}\mathbf{\mathtt{run}}_{A}\preceq\mathbf{\mathtt{run}}_{A}

This definition is motivated by the relationship between sequential and parallel products. Sequential product, signifying a temporal juxtaposition, is generally noncommutative. and

b\mathbin{\fatsemi}d
. Pictorially, this can be represented by a diagram (

2
) where, on the left,
a
and
b
must finish together, causing
c
and
d
to start simultaneously. On the right, no such constraint is applied.

Local computation

Valuation algebras provide a foundation for practical computation through a suite of distributed
local computation
algorithms. These algorithms are designed to resolve
inference problems
that arise in the context of valuation algebras. A comprehensive reference to this topic is
[
16
]
.

Definition 10

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an OVA.
A
knowledgebase
is a finite subset of valuations
K\subseteq\smallint{\mathbf{\Phi}}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}\vcentcolon=\left\{A_{i}\in\mathcal{T}\right\}_{i\in I}
be a finite family of domains, so that for each
i\in I
, we have
A_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in K}\mathrm{d}a
.
Then the task of computing

\left\lparen\bigotimes_{a\in K}a\right\rparen^{\downarrow{A_{i}}}

for each
i\in I
, is called the
inference problem for
(K,\mathcal{A})
.
In this context,
\bigotimes_{a\in K}a
is called the
joint valuation
, and the domains
A_{i}
are called
queries
.

In distributed systems, an inference problem corresponds to determining the local behaviours of a composite system of interacting components. For example, sequential consistency of a specification, as shown in
[
6
]
, can be framed as an inference problem. The key to local computation is the combination axiom
(a\otimes b)^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a}}=a\otimes b^{\downarrow{\mathrm{d}a\cap\mathrm{d}b}}
. However, traditional theory falls short in our setting as it presumes a single commutative combine operator. Though the generalised combination axiom of
Combination.
supports local computation for CVAs, further exploration in this area is called for.

Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced the
concurrent valuation algebra (CVA)
, a new algebraic structure that expands upon
ordered valuation algebras (OVAs)
by incorporating parallel and sequential products. This integration places the theory of concurrent and distributed systems within the expansive scope of valuation algebras.

Our CVAs draw inspiration from existing algebraic frameworks in concurrency theory such as
Communicating Sequential Processes (CSP)

[
11
]
,
Concurrent Kleene Algebra (CKA)

[
14
]
,
Concurrent Refinement Algebra (CRA)

[
9
]
, and
duoidal/
2
-monoidal categories

[
2
]
. They also facilitate key reasoning methodologies for program specification, like Hoare logic
[
10
]
, and rely-guarantee reasoning
[
13
]
.

Within the framework of CVAs, we explored three trace models, each representing distinct computational paradigms, and related them by morphisms.

This research marks a promising pathway to practical applications, particularly through the potent
local computation
framework described in
Section

8
. Looking ahead, our work will focus on several key areas. We aim to explore a wider range of CVA models, including the trace semantics of CSP, as well as examples founded on different structures, like trees or transition systems, instead of traces. Our study will further involve deepening the understanding of the general theory of CVAs, including the exploration of their categorical structure, and the ways CVAs on different spaces relate via the pull-back and push-forward mechanisms of their underlying presheaves. Of special interest is the examination of potential links between OVAs and
the monoidal Grothendieck construction

[
15
]
.

Lemma 12

The operator
\mathbin{\smallsmile}
satisfies the combination axiom.

Proof

Note that one direction of the combination law follows from monotonicity.
It then suffices to show
a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\subseteq(a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b)^{\downarrow{A}}
and
a^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\mathbin{\smallsmile}b\subseteq(a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b)^{\downarrow{B}}
.

Let
a\in\mathbf{\Sigma}_{A}
,
b\in\mathbf{\Sigma}_{B}
, and
t\in a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}
.
By definition of
\mathbin{\smallsmile}
, there exists
t_{a}\in a
and
t_{b}\in(b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}})^{\uparrow{A}}
so that
t=t_{a}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}t_{b}
and
t_{a}^{+}=t_{b}^{-}
.
Let
t_{a}^{\prime}\in a^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}
be a lifting of
t_{a}
.
Let

s\vcentcolon=t_{b}^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\in((b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}})^{\uparrow{A}})^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}=b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}

where the equality follows by
Corollary

2
, and let
s^{\prime}\in b
be another lifting of
s
.
By binary gluing, there then exists a common lifting
t_{b}^{\prime}\in b^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}
of
t_{b}
and
s^{\prime}
.
We can assume that
{(t_{a})}^{+}={(t_{b}^{\prime})}^{-}
; if not, simply replace
t_{a}^{\prime}
by

where
t_{a}^{\prime\prime}
is
t_{a}^{\prime}
with its final component replaced by the first component of
t_{b}^{\prime}
.

t_{a}^{\prime\prime}
is then still a lifting of
t_{a}
; for this we must only check its last component restricts onto the last component of
t_{a}
.
By
Lemma

10
, we have
{(t_{a}^{\prime\prime+})^{\downarrow{B}}}={(t_{b}^{\prime-})^{\downarrow{B}}}=t_{b}^{-}=t_{a}^{+}
.
Define
t^{\prime}\vcentcolon=t_{a}^{\prime}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A\cup B}t_{b}^{\prime}\in a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b

a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b^{\downarrow{A\cap B}}\subseteq(a\mathbin{\smallsmile}b)^{\downarrow{A}}

Similarly,

The result follows.

Lemma 13

{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}

{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}
has the strong neutrality property.

Proof

Let

\displaystyle a\mathbin{\smallsmile}{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A}

\displaystyle=a^{\uparrow{A}}\mathbin{\smallsmile_{A}}{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A}^{\uparrow{A}}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{a}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}\mid t_{a}\in a,t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}\in{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A},t_{a}^{+}=t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}^{-}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{a}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}\mid t_{a}\in a,t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}\in\mathbf{T}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A},\lambda(t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}})=1,t_{a}^{+}=t_{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}^{-}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{a}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}[x]\mid t_{a}\in a,x\in\mathbf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A},t_{a}^{+}=x\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{a}\mid t_{a}\in a\right\}

\displaystyle=a,

and similarly
{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A}\mathbin{\smallsmile}a=a
.
Thus,
{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}
is a neutral element for gluing product.
For
A,B\in\mathcal{T}
, with

, we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{B}^{\uparrow{A}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A}\mathbin{\smallsmile}{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{1}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}t_{2}\mid t_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{A},t_{2}\in{\boldsymbol{\uptau}}_{B}^{\uparrow{A}},t_{1}^{+}=t_{2}^{-}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t_{1}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}t_{2}\mid t_{1}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}_{A},t_{2}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}_{A},t_{1}^{+}=t_{2}^{-},\lambda(t_{1})=1=\lambda(t_{2}^{\downarrow{B}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{[x]\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}[y]\mid x\in\mathbf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A},y\in\mathbf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A},x=y\right\}

Lemma 17

Let
t,s\in\mathbf{T}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A}
so that
t^{+}=s^{-}
.
Then
q_{A}(t\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.53333pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.53333pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A}s)=q_{A}(t)\mathbin{\cdot_{A}}q_{A}(s)

{q_{A}(t)}^{+}

{q_{A}(s)}^{-}

Proof

The action of
q
is to eliminate duplicate adjacent components, so the first claim is immediate by observing that we cannot have
t_{\lambda(t)-1}=s^{-}
or
t^{+}=s_{2}
(
t^{+}=t_{\lambda(t)}=s^{-}
).
For the second, note that
q
cannot change the first or last components of a trace.

Proof
(of
Proposition

16
)

Let
a\in\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{rel}}_{A}
and
b\in\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{rel}}_{B}
for some
A,B\in\mathcal{T}
.

Colax naturality.

Naturality of
q
is clear, and this directly implies (strict) naturality of
f
.

Monotonicity.

For monotonicity, first note that each
f_{A}
is monotone as
f_{A}=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{U}(q_{\mathbf{\Omega}^{\mathsf{state}}_{A}}))
and
\mathbf{P}
is a functor valued in posets.
Now if
a\preceq b
then
B\subseteq A
and
a^{\downarrow{B}}\subseteq b
.
Note we have
\mathrm{d}(f(a))=A
and
\mathrm{d}(f(b))=B
.
Then by naturality and local monotonicity,
f_{A}(a)^{\downarrow{B}}=f_{B}(a^{\downarrow{B}})\subseteq f_{B}(b)
, thus by definition
f(a)\preceq f(b)
.

Colax unitality.

Clearly, we have in fact
f(\boldsymbol{\top})=\boldsymbol{\top}^{\mathsf{rel}}
and
f({\boldsymbol{\uptau}})={\boldsymbol{\uptau}}^{\mathsf{rel}}
.

Colax multiplicativity.

First we show colaxity with respect to
\mathbin{\wedge^{\mathsf{rel}}}
.
We have,

\displaystyle f(a\wedge b)

\displaystyle=f_{A\cup B}(a^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}\cap b^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})

(definition)

\displaystyle\subseteq f_{A\cup B}(a^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})\cap f_{A\cup B}(b^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})

(property of image)

\displaystyle\subseteq f_{A}(a)^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}\cap f_{B}(b)^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}

(
Lemma

16
, monotonicity of
\cap
)

\displaystyle=f(a)\mathbin{\wedge^{\mathsf{rel}}}f(b)

(definition)

Finally, we show colaxity with respect to
\mathbin{\smallsmile^{\mathsf{rel}}}
, i.e.

Let

Then there is

and

with
r^{+}=s^{-}
so that
t=q_{A\cup B}(r\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\smallsmile$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{1.5764pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 5.0pt}}_{A\cup B}s)
.
By
Lemma

17
,
t=q_{A\cup B}(r)\mathbin{\cdot_{A\cup B}}q_{A\cup B}(s)
, and
{q_{A\cup B}(r)}^{+}={q_{A\cup B}(s)}^{-}
.
Note
q_{A\cup B}(r)\in f_{A\cup B}(a^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})
and

so

By
Lemma

\displaystyle f_{A\cup B}(a^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})\mathbin{\smallsmile^{\mathsf{rel}}}_{A\cup B}f_{A\cup B}(b^{\uparrow{A\cup B}})

\displaystyle\subseteq f_{A}(a)^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}\mathbin{\smallsmile^{\mathsf{rel}}}_{A\cup B}f_{B}(b)^{\uparrow{A\cup B}}

\displaystyle=f(a)\mathbin{\smallsmile^{\mathsf{rel}}}f(b)
