Effective Bertini theorem and formulas for multiplicity and the local Łojasiewicz exponent
Tomasz Rodak, Adam Różycki, Stanisław Spodzieja

Abstract
. The classical Bertini theorem on generic intersection of an algebraic set with hyperplanes states the following: Let X be a nonsingular closed subvariety of ℙ k n \mathbb{P}^{n}_{k} , where k k is an algebraically closed field. Then there exists a hyperplane H ⊂ ℙ k n H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k} not containing X X and such that the scheme H ∩ X H\cap X is regular at every point. Furthermore, the set of hyperplanes with this property forms an open dense subset of the complete linear system | H | |H| considered as a projective space. We will show that one can effectively indicate a finite family of hyperplanes H H such that at least one of them satisfies the assertion of the Bertini theorem. As an application of the method used in the proof we will give effective formulas for the multiplicity and the Łojasiewicz exponent of polynomial mappings.

Introduction

The classical Bertini theorem on generic intersection of an algebraic set with hyperplanes is as follows (see
[
7
, Theorem II.8.18]
):

Bertini theorem
1
.

Let X be a nonsingular closed subvariety
of
\mathbb{P}^{m}_{k}
, where
k
is an algebraically closed field. Then there exists a hyperplane
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{m}_{k}
not containing
X
and such that the scheme
H\cap X
is regular at every point. (In fact, if
\dim X\geqslant 2
, then
H\cap X
is connected, hence irreducible, and so
H\cap X
is a nonsingular variety.) Furthermore, the set of hyperplanes with this property forms an
open dense subset of the complete linear system
|H|
considered as a projective space.

This theorem is one of the fundamental tools in algebraic geometry. For more details we refer to
[
8
]
.

We will show that one can effectively indicate a finite family of hyperplanes
H
such that at least one of them satisfies the assertion of the Bertini theorem (see Theorems
3.4
,
3.6
and
3.8
). Theorems
3.4
and
3.8
are formulated in terms of algebraic cones, i.e., algebraic sets defined by homogeneous polynomials. The most general is Theorem
3.8
which says (in the projective case) the following:

Let
d,m,q,s
be positive integers such that
m\geqslant q\geqslant s
, let

\ell=d^{m-q}[(m-q)(d-1)+q]+mq-1,

and let
N_{j}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{(m+1)q}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell
, be a system of independent linear functions, i.e., any
(m+1)q
of these functions are linearly independent. Then

\!\!\!K_{s,j_{1},\ldots,j_{(m+1)q-1}}=\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{m}:\exists_{a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in(\mathbb{P}^{m})^{q}}\;N_{j_{1}}(a)=\cdots=N_{j_{(m+1)q-1}}(a)=0,\\
a_{1}x^{T}=\cdots=a_{s}x^{T}=0\}\quad\hbox{for }1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{(m+1)q-1}\leqslant\ell

is a system of linear subspaces of dimension
m-s
such that for any irreducible and smooth algebraic set
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{m}
of dimension
q
and degree
d
, there are
1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{(m+1)q-1}\leqslant\ell
such that
the intersection
X=V\cap K_{s,j_{1},\ldots,j_{(m+1)q-1}}
is transversal at any point
x\in X
and the set
X
is smooth. If moreover
q\geqslant s+1
then
X
is irreducible.

As a consequence of the Bertini theorem we obtain Corollary
3.11
which says that

for any algebraic set
V=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
of pure dimension
q
, where
f_{j}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
,
\deg f_{j}\leqslant d
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, we have

\deg_{0}V=\max_{1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{m(q-1)-1}\leqslant\ell}\deg_{0}(V\cap K_{s,j_{1},\ldots,j_{m(q-1)-1}})\quad\hbox{for any $1\leqslant s\leqslant q$.}

A crucial role in the proof of the Bertini theorem is played by the following observation (see Lemma
2.3
):
Let
m,q,d
be positive integers with
m\geqslant 2
,
m\geqslant q
, let
\ell=d(m-q)+q
, and let
N_{j}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell
, be a system of independent linear functions. Then for any algebraic cone
C_{0}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
with
\dim C_{0}\leqslant q
and with total degree
\delta(C_{0})\leqslant d
, there are
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leqslant\ell
such that

C_{0}\cap V(N_{i_{1}},\ldots,N_{i_{q}})=\{0\}.

The number
\ell
in the above observation is optimal in terms of
d
,
m
and
q
(see Remark
2.4
).

There are a lot of numerical invariants that could be associated with a polynomial map
f
.
In this note we are interested in two of them, namely the multiplicity and the Łojasiewicz exponent.
The multiplicity is a basic biholomorphic invariant characterizing the singularity of
f
at zero (see
[
1
,
4
,
15
,
16
,
20
]
). As a corollary from the above observation we will prove the following effective formula for the multiplicity
i_{0}(f)
of a finite polynomial mapping
f
(see Theorem
4.1
):

Let
\ell=d^{n}(m-n)+n
.
For any independent system
L_{j}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell
,
and any polynomial mapping
f\colon\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
of degree
d
, finite at
0
, we have

i_{0}(f)=\min_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leqslant\ell}\dim_{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}/(L_{i_{1}}\circ f,\ldots,L_{i_{n}}\circ f).

Note that in the case
m>n
, we only have
i_{0}(f)\geqslant\dim_{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
.

For the Łojasiewicz exponent (see
[
13
,
14
,
10
,
11
]
, the definition is given in Section
5
), we obtain the following formulas (see Corollaries
5.3
and
5.6
):

Let
m\geqslant n
be positive integers, let
\ell=d^{n}(m-n)+n
, and let
L_{j}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell
, be a system of independent linear functions. Then for any polynomial mapping
f:(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n},0)\to(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m},0)
finite at
0
with
\deg f\leqslant d
we have

{\cal{L}}_{0}(f)=\min_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leqslant\ell}{\cal{L}}_{0}((L_{i_{1}},\ldots,L_{i_{n}})\circ f)=\min_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leqslant\ell}{\cal{L}}_{0}(H_{f,(L_{i_{1}},\ldots,L_{i_{n}})}),

where
H_{f,L}(z)=L\circ f(z)+(z_{1}^{{d^{n}}+1},\ldots,z_{n}^{{d^{n}}+1})
,
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

for a linear mapping
L:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Then by using Płoski’s formula for the Łojasiewicz exponent of mappings
\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
(see
[
18
,
19
]
) we obtain an effective formula for the exponent in terms of orders of polynomials
P_{f,L,N}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},t]
defining the sets of values of the mappings

\Phi_{f,L,N}(z)=(H_{f,L}(z),N(z)),

where
N:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
is a linear function.
More precisely, we obtain the following formula (see Theorem
5.7
):
Let
\ell_{L}=d^{n}(m-n)+n
,
\ell_{N}=n+[(d^{n}+1)^{n}-1]n(n-1)
, and let

\mathbb{I}_{L}=\{{\bf s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbf{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:1\leqslant s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}\leqslant\ell_{L}\}
. Then for any sequences
N_{i}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}
,
L_{j}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell_{L}
, of independent linear functions we have

{\cal{L}}_{0}(f)=\min_{{\bf s}\in\mathbb{I}_{L}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}}\frac{1}{\Delta(P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}})},

where

\Delta(P)=\min_{j=0}^{r}\frac{\operatorname{ord}_{y}P_{j}}{r+1-j}

if
P(y,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(y)t^{j}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}\{y,t\}
is a power
series regular of order
r+1
in
t
.

In
[
19
, Theorem 7]
we obtained a similar result to Theorem
5.7
. There, however, we used the linear mappings
L
and
N
with generic coefficients. An important limitation in using
[
19
, Theorem 7]
is a quite large number,
n(m+1)
, of additional variables (i.e., coefficients of
L
and
N
) needed to determine the polynomial
P_{f,L,N}
. This results in an exponential extension of the time needed for calculations. Theorem
5.7
provides a formula for the Łojasiewicz exponent without using additional variables. However, we need to repeat the calculations
\ell_{N}\binom{\ell_{L}}{n}
times. From the point of view of the formula’s effectiveness and algorithmic implementation, this is a significant simplification and reduces the time of computer calculations considerably.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Basic notations

By
f\colon(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m},0)\to(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n},0)
we denote a mapping defined in a neighbourhood of
0\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
with values in
\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
f(0)=0
.
A holomorphic mapping
f\colon(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m},0)\to(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n},0)
is called
finite at
0
if
0
is an isolated point of
f^{-1}(0)
.

We denote by
\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
the ring of complex polynomials in
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
. The degree of a polynomial
f\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
is denoted by
\deg f
. We put
\deg 0=-\infty
. Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}):\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
be a polynomial mapping, i.e.,
f_{j}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
. By the degree of
f
, denoted by
\deg f
, we mean
\max\{\deg f_{j}:1\leqslant j\leqslant r\}
.

For any set
\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
, we denote by
V(\operatorname{\mathcal{I}})\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
the set of common zeros of all polynomials
f\in\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}
;
any set of the form
V(\operatorname{\mathcal{I}})
is called
algebraic
. We put
V(\emptyset)=\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
. By the Hilbert basis theorem
V(\operatorname{\mathcal{I}})=V(\operatorname{\mathcal{J}})=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
for some polynomials
f_{j}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, belonging to the ideal
\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x]
generated by
\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}
. If the polynomials
f_{j}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, are homogeneous, we call the set
V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
an
algebraic cone
.

We denote by
\dim V
the dimension of an algebraic (or locally analytic) set
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. We set
\dim\emptyset=-1
. Let
\dim_{a}V
denote the dimension of
V
at
a\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, i.e., the dimension of the germ of the set
V
at
a
. The set
V
is called
pure dimensional
if all irreducible components of
V
have the same dimension.

1.2.
Tangent cone

Let
C_{0}(V)
be the
tangent cone
to a set
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
at
0
in the sense of Whitney
[
25
]
, i.e., the set of vectors
w\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
for which there are sequences
p_{\nu}\in V
with
p_{\nu}\to 0
and
\alpha_{\nu}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\alpha_{\nu}p_{\nu}\to w
as
\nu\to\infty
. We have the following fact (cf.
[
25
, Theorem 8.10 and Lemma 8.11]
).

Fact
1.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
be an analytic set in a neighbourhood of the origin
with
0\in V
. Then
C_{0}(V)
is an algebraic cone and
\dim C_{0}(V)=\dim_{0}V
. If
0
is a simple point of
V
then
C_{0}(V)
is the tangent space
T_{0}(V)
to
V
at
0
.

1.3.
Degree of algebraic sets

The
degree
of a pure dimensional algebraic set
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
, denoted by
\deg V
,
is defined as the number of common points of
V
and a generic affine subspace
X\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
of dimension
m-\dim V
;
and the
degree of
V
at a point

a\in V
, denoted by
\deg_{a}V
, is the multiplicity
i_{a}(V,X)
of proper intersection of
V
with
X
as above such that
a\in X
(see
[
3
]
). Obviously,
\deg_{a}V\leqslant\deg V
.

Let
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{s}
be the decomposition of an algebraic set
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
into irreducible components. The number

\delta(V):=\deg V_{1}+\cdots+\deg V_{s}

is called the
total degree
of
V
(see
[
15
]
).

We have the following useful properties of the total degree (see
[
15
]
).

Fact
1.2
.

If
V,W\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
are algebraic sets, then

(1)

\displaystyle\delta(V\cap W)\leqslant\delta(V)\delta(W),

(2)

\displaystyle\delta(V)\leqslant\deg f_{1}\cdots\deg f_{r},\quad\hbox{where }V=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}),

(3)

\displaystyle\delta(\overline{L(V)})\leqslant\delta(V)\quad\hbox{for any linear mapping }L:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{k}.

Fact
1.3
.

If
V\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
is an algebraic set, then
\delta(C_{0}(V))\leqslant\delta(V)
. If
f:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
is a polynomial mapping of degree
d
, finite at
0
, then
\delta\big(C_{0}\big(\overline{f(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n})}\big)\big)\leqslant d^{n}
.

Proof.

The first assertion immediately follows from
[
3
, Theorem 6.4]
. We will prove the second assertion. Let
W=f(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
,
k=\dim\overline{W}
and
D=\deg\overline{W}
. Then
k\leqslant n
and by the definition of the degree of an algebraic set and the fact that
\dim\overline{\overline{W}\setminus W}<k
, for a generic linear mapping
L:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
and a generic
z\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
,
\#L^{-1}(z)\cap W=D
,
where
\#
stands for cardinality.
Hence
D\leqslant\delta((L\circ f)^{-1}(z))
. Since for
y\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
such that
L(y)=z
we have
(L\circ f)^{-1}(z)=(L\circ(f-y))^{-1}(0)
, (
2
) gives
D\leqslant d^{k}\leqslant d^{n}
. Consequently, the first assertion implies the second.
∎

We denote by

\mathbb{L}(m,r)
the set of linear mappings
\mathbb{C}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{r}
, where
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
\mathbb{L}(m,r)=\{0\}
if
r=0
.

We will need the following lemma (cf.
[
9
, Lemma 3.20]
and
[
21
, Lemma 1.1]
).

Lemma
1.4
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}):\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
be a polynomial mapping such that
\dim V(f)=q
. Let
W
be the union of the irreducible components of dimension
q
of the set
V(f)
. Then for a generic linear mapping
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n-q})\in\mathbb{L}(r,n-q)
the set
V_{1}=V(L_{1}\circ f,\ldots,L_{n-q}\circ f)
has dimension
q
,
W
is the union of some irreducible components of
V_{1}
, and

\deg W\leqslant\delta(V_{1})\leqslant\deg(L_{1}\circ f)\cdots\deg(L_{n-q}\circ f).

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
q\geqslant 0
.
Let
Y=\overline{f(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n})}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
. By Chevalley’s theorem (see
[
15
, p. 395]
) the set
Y
is algebraic and obviously irreducible. Moreover,
[
16
, Theorem 3.13]
easily implies that
r\geqslant\dim_{0}Y=\dim Y\geqslant n-q
, and there exists a Zariski open and dense subset
U
of
Y
such that
\dim f^{-1}(y)\leqslant q
for
y\in U
and obviously
\dim(Y\setminus U)\leqslant\dim Y-1
.

Let
k=\dim C_{0}(Y)
. By Fact
1.1
,
k=\dim Y
and so
r\geqslant k\geqslant n-q
.

If
k<r
, by definition of the degree of an algebraic set, for a generic linear mapping
L:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
the set
Y\cap L^{-1}(0)
is finite,
(Y\setminus U)\cap L^{-1}(0)\subset\{0\}
and obviously
0\in Y\cap L^{-1}(0)
. So,
V(L\circ f)
has dimension
q
and contains
W
. Consequently,
W
is the union of some irreducible components of
V(L\circ f)
. Hence, it suffices to consider the case
k=r
, and then obviously
Y=\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
and
r\leqslant n
.

Let

Z=\{(x,L)\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times\mathbb{L}(r,n-q):L\circ f(x)=0\},

and let

\pi:Z\ni(x,L)\mapsto L\in\mathbb{L}(r,n-q).

Obviously
\dim\mathbb{L}(r,n-q)=r(n-q)
. Since
Y=\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
and
r\leqslant n
, we have
\overline{\pi(Z)}=\mathbb{L}(r,n-q)
,
\dim Z=r(n-q)+q
and for a generic
L\in\mathbb{L}(r,n-q)
the set
\pi^{-1}(L)
has dimension
q
. This gives the first assertion. The second follows immediately from the first and Fact
1.2
.
∎

From Lemma
1.4
we immediately obtain

Corollary
1.5
.

If
V=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is an algebraic cone of pure dimension
q
, where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
are nonzero homogeneous polynomials with
\deg f_{j}\leqslant d
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, then

\delta(V)\leqslant d^{n-q}.

1.4.
Projective algebraic sets

Let
\mathbb{P}^{m}
be the
m
-dimensional complex projective space.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{m}
be an algebraic set, i.e., the set of common zeros of some system
S\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]
of homogeneous polynomials. Let
\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]
be the radical of the ideal generated by
S
. Then
\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}
is the ideal of all polynomials vanishing on
V
and

V^{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}}:=\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m+1}:[x_{0}:\ldots:x_{m}]\in V\}\cup\{0\}=V(\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}),

where
V(\operatorname{\mathcal{I}})=\{x\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m+1}:f(x)=0\hbox{ for any }f\in\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}\}
, is an algebraic cone. We have
\dim V=\dim V^{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}}-1
. The set
V
is called
pure dimensional
if all irreducible components of
V
have the same dimension.

Since any hyperplane
H\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m+1}
of dimension
k
can be written in the form
V(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m-k},L_{m-k+1}-a)
for some
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m-k+1}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
and
a\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
, we can define the

of

by
\deg V=\deg V^{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}}
(if

by
\delta(V)=\delta(V^{\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}})
.

2.
Auxiliary results

2.1.
Main lemma

A system of functions
N_{1},\ldots,N_{s}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
,
s\geqslant m
, will be called
independent
if for any sequence
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant s
the system
N_{i_{1}},\ldots,N_{i_{m}}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark
2.1
.

Take a system
N_{1},\ldots,N_{s}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
with
s\geqslant n
, and let
N_{i}(x)=a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,m}x_{m}
. Then the system
N_{1},\ldots,N_{s}
is independent if and only if
\prod_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant s}\det[a_{i_{j},l}]_{1\leqslant j,l\leqslant m}\neq 0
. So, the set of independent systems is a dense subset of
\mathbb{L}(m,s)
with algebraic complement.

Example
2.2
.

For any injective sequence
a_{i}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
,
s\geqslant m
, the system

N_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=x_{1}+a_{i}x_{2}+\cdots+a_{i}^{m-1}x_{m},\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant s,

is independent. Indeed, this follows from the properties of the Vandermonde determinant.

A crucial role in the discussions below will be played by the following lemma.

Lemma
2.3
.

Let
m,q,d
be positive integers with
m\geqslant 2
,
m\geqslant q
, let
\ell=d(m-q)+q
, and let
N_{j}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell
, be a system of independent linear functions. Then for any algebraic cone
C_{0}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
with
\dim C_{0}\leqslant q
and
\delta(C_{0})\leqslant d
, there exist
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leqslant\ell
such that

C_{0}\cap V(N_{i_{1}},\ldots,N_{i_{q}})=\{0\}.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
q\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Take any algebraic cone
C_{0}\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
with
\dim C_{0}=1
and
\delta(C_{0})\leqslant d
. Then there are
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
C_{0}=w_{1}\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}\cup\cdots\cup w_{d}\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
. Suppose that
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
there exists
\varphi(i)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
N_{i}(w_{\varphi(i)})=0
. Since
w_{\varphi(i)}\neq 0
, the choice of
N_{1},\ldots,N_{\ell}
yields
\#\varphi^{-1}(j)\leqslant m-1
, so

d(m-1)+1=\ell=\#\varphi^{-1}(\{1,\ldots,d\})=\#\varphi^{-1}(1)+\cdots+\#\varphi^{-1}(d)\leqslant d(m-1),

which is impossible.
This gives the assertion for
q=1
.

Assume that the assertion holds for
1\leqslant q-1<m
. Take any algebraic cone
C_{0}
with
\dim C_{0}\leqslant q
and
\delta(C_{0})\leqslant d
. If
q=m
then the assertion is obvious. Assume that
q<m
. Let
C_{0}=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{s}
be the decomposition into irreducible components. Since
\delta(C_{0})\leqslant d
, we have
s\leqslant d
. Observe that there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, such that

3.
Bertini’s theorem

3.1.
Bertini’s weak theorem

For any
a\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
, denote by
N_{a}
the linear function
x_{1}+ax_{2}+\cdots+a^{m-1}x_{m}
. We have

Corollary
3.1

(Bertini’s weak theorem)
.

Let
C\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
be an algebraic cone of pure dimension
q\geqslant 1
. Then the set
A\subset\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
of points
a\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
such that
C\cap V(N_{a})
is an improper intersection is finite. Moreover,
\#A\leqslant d(m-q)
, where
\delta(C)\leqslant d
.

Proof.

Let
k=d(m-q)
. Suppose to the contrary that
\#A>k
. Then there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1}\in A
such that
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant k+1
. Consequently,
\dim[C\cap V(N_{a_{j}})]=q
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1
. Then for any
a_{k+2},\ldots,a_{k+q}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
and any
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leqslant d(m-q)+q
we have
\dim[C\cap V(N_{a_{1}},\ldots,N_{a_{q}})]>0
. This contradicts Lemma
2.3
and Example
2.2
.∎

The properness of the intersection
C\cap V(N_{a})
in Corollary
3.1
cannot be replaced by transversality, as shown by the following example:

Example
3.2
.

Let

C=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{3}:y^{2}-4xz=0\}.

Obviously
C\setminus\{0\}
is a smooth cone of dimension
2
. Let
N_{a}(x,y,z)=x+ay+a^{2}z
,

. Then
C\cap V(N_{a})\neq\emptyset
for any
a\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover, if
(0,0,0)\neq(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})\in C\cap V(N_{a})
then
z_{0}\neq 0
,
a=\frac{-y_{0}}{2z_{0}}
and
x_{0}=\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{4z_{0}}
. So,

N_{a}(x,y,z)=x+\frac{-y_{0}}{2z_{0}}y+\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{4z_{0}^{2}}z=\frac{-1}{4z_{0}}\left(-4z_{0}x+2y_{0}y-4x_{0}z\right)

and

. This shows that the intersection

is not transversal at
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.

5.
Łojasiewicz exponent

The
local Łojasiewicz exponent
of a mapping
f\colon(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n},0)\to(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m},0)
, denoted by
\cal{L}_{0}(f)
, is defined to be the infimum of the set of all exponents
\nu\geqslant 1
such that

|f(z)|\geqslant C|z|^{\nu}

for some constant
C>0
in a neighbourhood of the origin in
\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
(see
[
24
]
).
In
[
12
]
it was proved that

is a rational number and the infimum in the definition
is in fact a minimum, provided
f
is finite at
0
.

A. Płoski
[
18
]
gave a formula for
\cal{L}_{0}(f)
for a finite mapping
f
in terms of the characteristic polynomial, provided
n=m
. In fact, the characteristic polynomial is a polynomial with holomorphic coefficients. In the case
n=m=2
such a formula was obtained by J. Chądzyński and T. Krasiński
[
2
]
in terms of resultants. In Proposition
5.1
below, we quote a version of Płoski’s formula obtained by T. Rodak and S. Spodzieja
[
19
, Proposition 3]
.

Let
P(y,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(y)t^{j}\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}\{y,t\}
be a power
series regular of order
r+1
in
t
. Write

\Delta(P)=\min_{j=0}^{r}\frac{\operatorname{ord}P_{j}}{r+1-j}.

and

and

\{(y,t)\in W_{0}:P_{k}(y,t)=0\}=\{(f(z),z_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}:z\in U_{0}\}.

Proposition

.

Under the above assumptions and notation,

N\in\mathbb{L}(n,1)
. Namely, let

\ell_{N}=n+[(d^{n}+1)^{n}-1]n(n-1),\qquad\ell_{L}=d^{n}(m-n)+n.

Take any families of independent linear functions

N_{i}\in\mathbb{L}(n,1),\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N},\qquad L_{s}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1),\quad 1\leqslant s\leqslant\ell_{L}.

Let

\mathbb{I}_{L}=\{{\bf s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbf{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:1\leqslant s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}\leqslant\ell_{L}\}.

Obviously
\#\mathbb{I}_{L}=\binom{\ell_{L}}{n}
.
Set

L_{\bf s}=(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}})\in\mathbb{L}(m,n)\quad\hbox{for }{\bf s}\in\mathbb{I}_{L}.

For any
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}
and
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{I}_{L}
we define a mapping
\Phi_{f,(i,{\bf s})}:\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}
by

\Phi_{f,(i,{\bf s})}(z)=\Phi_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}},

i.e.,
\Phi_{f,(i,{\bf{s}})}=\left(H_{f,{\bf s}}(z),N_{i}(z)\right)
, where
H_{f,{\bf s}}(z)=L_{\bf s}(f(z)(z))+(z_{1}^{d^{n}+1},\ldots,z_{n}^{d^{n}+1})
.

The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem
5.7
.

For any polynomial mapping
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)
finite at
0
of degree
d
the Łojasiewicz exponent
{\cal{L}}_{0}(f)
is given by

{\cal{L}}_{0}(f)=\min_{{\bf s}\in\mathbb{I}_{L}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}}\frac{1}{\Delta(P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}})}.

We will precede the proof of the above theorem with a remark and an example.

Remark
5.8
.

Since
P_{f,L,N}
vanishes exactly on the image of
\Phi_{f,L,N}
, it can be effectively computed, for instance by using Gröbner bases (see
[
6
]
). One can also compute it as a multipolynomial resultant of the coordinates of
\Phi_{f,L,N}
(see
[
5
]
).
An important point in this construction is properness of the mappings
H_{f,L,N}
and
\Phi_{f,L,N}
, which we owe to the component
(z_{1}^{{d^{n}}+1},\ldots,z_{n}^{{d^{n}}+1})
. Therefore, the polynomials
P_{f,L,N}
in Theorem
5.7
are regular in
t
and we can compute the numbers
\Delta(P_{f,L,N})
.
If we omit this component, with fixed
L,M
and
N=z_{k}
the polynomials
P_{f,L,N}
may not satisfy (
12
) (see Example
5.9
below).

Example
5.9
.

The polynomial mapping
f=(f_{1},f_{2}):\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
defined by

f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})=z_{1}(1-z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2}),\quad f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})=z_{2}(1-z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})

is finite at
(0,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and it is not proper. For the irreducible polynomial

P(y_{1},y_{2},t)=t^{3}(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})-ty_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{3}

we have
P(f(z_{1},z_{2}),z_{1})=0
for
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Moreover, the polynomial
P
is not regular in
t
and we cannot compute the number
\Delta(P)
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
.

By an analogous argument to the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 1]
we obtain

Lemma
5.10
.

If
P,Q,R\in\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}\{y,t\}
are series such that

P(y,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(y)t^{j},\quad Q(y,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q_{j}(y)t^{j},

and
Q=PR
and for some
r\geqslant 0
we have
\operatorname{ord}P_{j},\,\operatorname{ord}Q_{j}>0
,
j=0,\ldots,r
, then

(15)

\min_{0\leqslant j\leqslant r}\frac{\operatorname{ord}P_{j}}{r+1-j}\leqslant\min_{0\leqslant j\leqslant r}\frac{\operatorname{ord}Q_{j}}{r+1-j}.

If additionally
\operatorname{ord}R=0
, then equality holds in (
15
).

Fix
{\bf s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
with
1\leqslant s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}\leqslant\ell_{L}
and let
L_{{\bf s}}=(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}})\in\mathbb{L}(m,n)
.

Lemma
5.11
.

There are
1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leqslant\ell_{N}
such that

(16)

V(H_{f,L_{\bf s}})\cap V(N_{i_{j}})=\{0\}\quad\hbox{for }1\leqslant j\leqslant n.

Proof.

Since
H_{L,M}
has no zeros at infinity, it is proper. Consequently, by Bezout’s theorem it has at most
(d^{n}+1)^{n}-1
zeros in
\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus by Corollary
2.10
we obtain (
16
).
∎

Lemma
5.12
.

If
N\in\mathbb{L}(n,1)
satisfies (
16
), then for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
the germs of the sets

A_{\epsilon}=\{(H_{f,L_{\bf s}}(z),N(z)):|z|<\epsilon\}\;\;\hbox{ and }\;\;B=\{(y,t):P_{f,L_{\bf s},N}(y,t)=0\}

at
0\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
are equal.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a neighbourhood
U_{0}
of
0\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
such that
A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}=B\cap U_{0}
. Indeed, suppose there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for any neighbourhood
U_{0}
of zero we have
A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}\not=B\cap U_{0}
. Obviously
A_{\epsilon}\subset B
, hence
A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}\subset B\cap U_{0}
. So, the inclusion
A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}\supset B\cap U_{0}
fails, i.e., there exists a sequence
(y_{\nu},t_{\nu})\in B\cap U_{0}
such that
(y_{\nu},t_{\nu})\rightarrow(0,0)
and
(y_{\nu},t_{\nu})\not\in A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}
.
By (
14
) there exists a sequence
z_{\nu}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
y_{\nu}=H_{L_{\bf s}}(z_{\nu})
and
t_{\nu}=N(z_{\nu})
. Thus

(H_{f,L_{\bf s}}(z_{\nu}),N(z_{\nu}))\rightarrow(0,0)

as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. The sequence
(z_{\nu})
is bounded (by properness of
H_{f,L_{\bf s}}
); we can assume that
z_{\nu}\rightarrow z_{0}
as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. Then

(H_{f,L_{\bf s}}(z_{0}),N(z_{0}))=(0,0),

so, by (
16
),
z_{0}=0
and
z_{\nu}\rightarrow 0
as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
.
On the other hand,
(y_{\nu},t_{\nu})\not\in A_{\epsilon}\cap U_{0}
implies
|z_{\nu}|\geqslant\epsilon
, a contradiction.
∎

By Lemmas
5.11
and
5.12
, for any fixed
{\bf s}
we can find
1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant\ell_{N}
such that
P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i_{j}}}
satisfy the assumption (
12
) of Proposition
5.1
. Note that if
P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}}
does not satisfy (
12
), the characteristic polynomial for
\Phi_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}}
is a divisor of
P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}}
. Hence, in view of Lemma
5.10
, we obtain

{\cal{L}}_{0}(H_{f,L_{\bf s}})=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}}\frac{1}{\Delta(P_{f,L_{\bf s},N_{i}})}.

This together with Corollary
5.6
gives the assertion of Theorem
5.7
.
∎

Theorem
5.7
simplifies in the case of proper polynomial mappings finite at
0
. By an analogous argument to that in the proof of Theorem
5.7
we obtain

Corollary
5.13
.

Let
d,n,m
,
m\geqslant n
, be positive integers, let

\ell_{N}=(d^{n}-1)n(n-1)+n,\quad\ell_{L}=d^{n}(m-n)+n,

and let
N_{i}\in\mathbb{L}(n,1)
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}
,
L_{j}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell_{L}
, be sequences of independent functions.
Then for any proper polynomial mapping
f:(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
finite at
0
of degree
d
there exist polynomials

P_{f,(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}}),N_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}[y,t],\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N},\quad 1\leqslant s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}\leqslant\ell_{L},

regular in
t
, such that the set
V(P_{f,(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}}),N_{i}})
is equal to the image of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
under the mapping
z\mapsto((L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}})\circ f(z),N_{i}(z))
and

\mathcal{L}_{0}(f)=\min_{1\leqslant s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}\leqslant\ell_{L}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell_{N}}\frac{1}{\Delta(P_{f,(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}}),N_{i}})}.

Theorem
5.7
and Corollary
5.13
give effective algorithms for computing the multiplicity and the local Łojasiewicz exponent of a polynomial mapping
\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
finite at
0
. Moreover, they allow one to decide whether a polynomial mapping
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
is finite at
0
. Namely, by calculating the Łojasiewicz exponents of mappings
(f,M):\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m+q}
for
M\in\mathbb{L}(n,q)
instead of
f
, we obtain

Proposition
5.14
.

Let
d,n,m
,
m\geqslant n
, be positive integers, let
0\leqslant q\leqslant n
, and let

\ell_{L}=d^{n}(m-n)+n,\quad\ell_{M}=d^{n}(n-q)+q.

Let
L_{j}\in\mathbb{L}(m,1)
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell_{L}
,
M_{k}\in\mathbb{L}(n,1)
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant\ell_{M}
, be sequences of independent functions. Let
f:(\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{n},0)\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}}^{m},0)
be a polynomial mapping of degree
d
. Set

H_{f,L_{\bf s},M_{\bf k}}(z)=L_{\bf s}\circ(f(z),M_{\bf k}(z))+\left(z_{1}^{d^{n}+1},\ldots,z_{n}^{d^{n}+1}\right)

for
L_{\bf s}=(L_{s_{1}},\ldots,L_{s_{n}})
,
{\bf s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{I}_{L}
and
M_{\bf k}=(M_{k_{1}},\ldots,M_{k_{q}})
,
{\bf k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{q})
,
1\leqslant k_{1}<\cdots<k_{q}\leqslant\ell_{M}
.

1. If
\dim_{0}V(f)\geqslant q+1
then

\min_{{\bf s},\,{\bf k}}\mathcal{L}_{0}(H_{f,L_{\bf s},M_{\bf k}})\geqslant d^{n}+1.

2. If
\dim_{0}V(f)\leqslant q
then

\min_{{\bf s},\,{\bf k}}\mathcal{L}_{0}(H_{f,L_{\bf s},M_{\bf k}})\leqslant d^{n}.
