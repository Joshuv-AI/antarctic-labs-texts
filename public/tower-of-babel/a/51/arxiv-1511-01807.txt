Abstract
. The height of a piecewise-testable language L L is the maximum length of the words needed to define L L by excluding and requiring given subwords. The height of L L is an important descriptive complexity measure that has not yet been investigated in a systematic way. This article develops a series of new techniques for bounding the height of finite languages and of languages obtained by taking closures by subwords, superwords and related operations. As an application of these results, we show that 𝖥𝖮 2 ​ ( A ∗ , ⊑ ) {\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq) , the two-variable fragment of the first-order logic of sequences with the subword ordering, can only express piecewise-testable properties and has elementary complexity.

Our contribution.

We provide upper and lower bounds on the PT height of finite languages and on
PT languages obtained by downward-closure (collecting all subwords of
all words from some

(collecting words in

etc.) We also show that the incomparability relation preserves

languages. Crucially, we show that these bounds are

\displaystyle{\uparrow}{u}

{\uparrow}\emptyset=\emptyset\>,\quad L\subseteq{\uparrow}L={\uparrow}{\uparrow}L\>,\quad{\uparrow}\bigl(\bigcup_{i}L_{i}\bigr)=\bigcup_{i}{\uparrow}L_{i}\>,\quad{\uparrow}\bigl(\bigcap_{i}{\uparrow}L_{i}\bigr)=\bigcap_{i}{\uparrow}L_{i}\>,

2.5.
Piecewise-testable relations

Following the generic pattern laid out in

]

that a relation

if it is a finite boolean combination of principal

Since

\displaystyle(A^{*}\times A^{*})\smallsetminus{\uparrow}(u,v)

\displaystyle\;=\;(A^{*}\smallsetminus{\uparrow}u)\times A^{*}\>\cup\>A^{*}\times(A^{*}\smallsetminus{\uparrow}v)

\displaystyle\;=\;(\neg{\uparrow}u)\times{\uparrow}\varepsilon\>\cup\>{\uparrow}\varepsilon\times(\neg{\uparrow}v)

and de Morgan’s laws, we further see that a finite boolean combination
of filters

can be written as some
\bigcup_{i}\bigcap_{j}(\pm{\uparrow}u_{i,j})\times(\pm{\uparrow}v_{i,j})
, i.e.,
\bigcup_{i}\bigl((\bigcap_{j}\pm{\uparrow}u_{i,j})\times(\bigcap_{j}\pm{\uparrow}v_{i,j})\bigr)
. Finally, any
n
-PT
relation can be written under the form
R=L_{1}\times L^{\prime}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{m}\times L^{\prime}_{m}
where all
L_{i}
’s and
L^{\prime}_{i}
’s are
n
-PT
languages. Hence PT relations form a subclass, denoted
{\mathsf{PT}}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
of
{\mathsf{Rec}}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
. We shall not use PT relations until

Section

7
and, for the moment, keep focused on PT languages.

2.6.
Closure properties of PT languages.

By definition (see
2.2
), any class
{\mathsf{PT}}_{n}
is
closed under boolean operations. Furthermore,
{\mathsf{PT}}_{n}
is also closed
under (left and right) quotients and under inverse
morphisms
[
Thé81
]
. In terms of PT height, the
above statements can be written as

\displaystyle h\Bigl(\bigcup_{i}L_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\max\>\{h(L_{i})\}_{i}\>,

\displaystyle h\Bigl(\bigcap_{i}L_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\max\>\{h(L_{i})\}_{i}\>,

\displaystyle h(\neg L)

\displaystyle=h(L)\>,

(6)

\displaystyle h(\rho^{-1}(L))

\displaystyle\leq h(L)\makebox[0.0pt][l]{ for $\rho:A^{*}\to B^{*}$ a morphism,}

\displaystyle h(u^{-1}L\,v^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq h(L)\>.

(7)

Note that we can allow arbitrary unions and intersections in
Eq. (
6
) since, for fixed
A
, there are only finitely many
languages in
{\mathsf{PT}}_{n}
.

Let us also mention that
{\mathsf{PT}}_{n}
is closed under taking mirror images:
writing
\overleftarrow{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{\ell}}
for the mirror word

a_{\ell}\cdots a_{2}a_{1}
, and letting

\overleftarrow{L}=\{\overleftarrow{u}~|~u\in L\}
for a language

L\subseteq A^{*}
, one has
h\bigl(\overleftarrow{L}\bigr)=h(L)
.

Beyond that,
{\mathsf{PT}}
is not closed under any of the usual
language-theoretic operations as we now illustrate.

Concatenation and prefixing:

a(a+b)^{*}
is not PT: it is not
closed under any
\sim_{k}
since it contains

(ab)^{k}
but not
b(ab)^{k}
while
(ab)^{k}\sim_{k}b(ab)^{k}
.
4
4

4

An
alternative proof is by observing that the minimal automaton for

(a+b)^{*}a
—the mirror language— is not acyclic.

Since
(a+b)^{*}
is PT, we see that the class
{\mathsf{PT}}
is not closed under concatenation, even
in the special case of prefixing with
a
.

Kleene star:

{\mathsf{PT}}
is not closed under Kleene star (recall that

{\mathsf{PT}}
is a subvariety of the star-free languages):
aa
is finite
hence PT but
(aa)^{*}
is not PT since its minimal automaton is not acyclic.

Shuffle product:

ab^{*}
and
a^{*}
are PT but their shuffle
product
ab^{*}\shuffle a^{*}=a(a+b)^{*}
is not as just shown, see
[
HS19
]
for PT shufflings.

Conjugacy:

Recall that the conjugates of
u
are

\widetilde{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\{u_{2}u_{1}~|~u=u_{1}u_{2}\}
, and we extend with

\widetilde{L}=\bigcup_{u\in L}\widetilde{u}
. Now
L=ac(a+b)^{*}
is
PT but
\widetilde{L}=(a+b)^{*}ac(a+b)^{*}+c(a+b)^{*}a
is not.

Renaming:

c(a+b)^{*}
is PT but applying the renaming
c\mapsto a
yield
a(a+b)^{*}
which is not.

Erasing one letter:

This operation can be seen as the inverse
of
L\mapsto L\shuffle A
where an arbitrary letter is inserted at
an arbitrary position. Now
ac(a+b)^{*}
is PT but erasing one letter
yields
(a+c+ac)(a+b)^{*}
which is not PT.

Finally, we are only aware of one more positive instance of a closure
property, “
I(L)
is PT when
L
is”, but proving this is the topic
of
Section

6
.

2.7.
Relating PT height and state complexity.

For regular languages, a standard measure of descriptive complexity is

state complexity
, denoted
\textit{sc}(L)
, and defined as the number of
states of the minimal DFA for
L

[
Yu05
]
.

The bounds given in Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
)
let us contrast the height of a PT language with its state complexity.
If
L
is a PT language, one has
h(L)\leq\textit{sc}(L)

(equality occurs e.g. when
L=\{a^{\ell}\}
) since
\textit{sc}(L)
bounds the
depth of the minimal automaton for
L
, i.e., the maximum length of a simple path from
the initial to some final state, which in turns bounds

h(L)

[
KP13
,
MT15
]
.

In the other direction, we can prove

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
A
be an alphabet of size
k
with
k>1
. Suppose
L\subseteq A^{*}
is
n
-PT. Then the minimal DFA for
L
has at most
m

states,
5
5

5

It is shown in
[
MT15
,
MT17
]

that the depth (not the size) of the minimal DFA is bounded by

\binom{n+k}{n}-1
.
where

\log m=k\left(\frac{n+2k-3}{k-1}\right)^{k-1}\log n\log k\>.

Here
\log
means
\log
to the base
2
. Thus, for fixed
k
,
\textit{sc}(L)
is
2^{O(n^{k-1}\log n)}
, where
n=h(L)
.

Proof.

Write
L
under the form
L=[u_{1}]_{n}\cup\cdots\cup[u_{m}]_{n}
and
consider the
\sim_{n}
-
canonical DFA
, i.e., the DFA

\mathcal{A}=(Q,A,i,.,F)
whose states are all classes
[w]_{n}
for
w\in A^{*}
and with transitions given by
[w]_{n}.a=[wa]_{n}
. With initial
state
i=[\varepsilon]_{n}
, this automaton reaches
[w]_{n}
upon reading

w
. With accepting states
F=\{[u_{1}]_{n},\ldots,[u_{m}]_{n}\}
, it
recognises exactly
L
. In
[
KKS15
]
we showed that the number of
equivalence classes of
\sim_{n}
, i.e.,
|Q|
, is bounded by
m
. This
in turns bound
\textit{sc}(L)
.
∎

The general situation is that
h(L)
can be much smaller than

\textit{sc}(L)
as we shall see in the following sections. More importantly,
PT height is a more robust measure than state complexity. For
example, state complexity can increase exponentially when building
boolean combinations of regular languages (while PT height does not
increase). This classic phenomenon occurs even when we restrict to PT
languages, and even if we use nondeterministic state complexity, see
the examples in
[
KNS16
]
. This is not limited to boolean
operations: for example, we saw that

h\bigl(\overleftarrow{L}\bigr)=h(L)
but

\textit{sc}\bigl(\overleftarrow{L}\bigr)
cannot be bounded by a polynomial
of
\textit{sc}(L)
, even in the case of finite, hence PT,
languages
[
SWY04
]
.

3.
PT height of words and the small-subword theorem

Our starting point is an analysis of the PT height of single words.
It is clear that any singleton language
\{u\}
is PT since
\{u\}={\uparrow}u\smallsetminus\bigcup_{v\in\{u\}\shuffle A}{\uparrow}v
, which entails

h(\{u\})\leq|u|+1
. Here we used a
shuffle
product
notation,

\{u\}\shuffle A
, to denote
\{v~:~u\sqsubseteq v\land|v|=|u|+1\}
, i.e., the set of all words obtained from
u
by
inserting, at some position, one letter from
A
.
Below we often omit set-theoretical parentheses when
denoting singletons, writing e.g. “
h(u)
” or “
u\shuffle A
”.

The
|u|+1
upper bound for
h(u)
is tight. For example,

\displaystyle h(a^{\ell})=\ell+1\>.

(8)

(To see
that
h(a^{\ell})>\ell
, one notes that

a^{\ell}\sim_{\ell}a^{\ell+1}
.)
However, words on more than one letter can generally be described within some
PT height lower than their length. For example

\{aabb\}=({\uparrow}aa\cap{\uparrow}bb)\smallsetminus({\uparrow}ba\cup{\uparrow}aaa\cup{\uparrow}bbb)\>,

showing
h(aabb)\leq 3
. (Note that
h(aabb)>2
since
aabbb\sim_{2}aabb
, thus
h(aabb)=3
.)

It turns out that the PT height of words can be much lower than their
length as we shall see in
section

3.1
, but before considering lower
bounds on
h(u)
, let us make some easier observations.

Proposition 3.1
.

The PT-height of a word can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof.

Following
[
SS83
]
, we let

\delta(v,v^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\max\{n~|~v\sim_{n}v^{\prime}\}
for any two words
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{*}
.
We now claim that

\displaystyle h(u)=1+\max\{\delta(u,v)~|~{v\in u\shuffle A}\}\>.

(9)

To prove the claim, we note that
u\neq v
entails
\delta(u,v)<h(u)

since, by definition of
h(u)
,
u\sim_{h(u)}v
entails
u=v
.
In the other direction, let

n=h(u)-1
so that
[u]_{n}
is not a singleton. Then
[u]_{n}
is
infinite (Lemma
2.1
(
7
)) and in particular
contains some word
w
with
u\sqsubset w

(Lemma
2.1
(
4
)). In fact
[u]_{n}
contains
all words between
u
and
w
(Lemma
2.1
(
1
))
hence some
w^{\prime}\in u\shuffle A
. Thus the right-hand side of
(
9
) is at least
1+\delta(u,w^{\prime})
, i.e., at least

n+1=h(u)
.

We have thus reduced the computation of
h(u)
to polynomially many

\delta(u,v)
computations and now rely on the fact that
\delta
can
be computed in polynomial time
[
Sim03
]
.
6
6

6

It is also
possible to directly compute
h(u)
in time and space
O(|h|\cdot|A|)
by adapting the techniques used in
[
FK18
]
, where
the goal is to compute a canonical
\sim_{n}
-equivalent for
u
.

∎

Proposition

3.1

can be used to compute the PT height of finite languages in polynomial
time: for such
languages, the inequality in Eq. (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle h(\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\})=\max\{h(u_{1}),\ldots,h(u_{m})\}\>.

(10)

Indeed,
h(\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\})=n
implies
[u_{i}]_{n}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
for any

. Thus
[u_{i}]_{n}
is a singleton in
view of Lemma

(

). Hence
[u_{i}]_{n}=\{u_{i}\}
and
h(u_{i})\leq n
.

3.1.
Words with low PT height

We introduce a family of words with “low PT height” that will

Let
A_{k}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}

\displaystyle|U_{k}|

\displaystyle=(\lambda+1)^{k}-1\>,

\displaystyle h(U_{k})

\displaystyle=k\lambda+1\>,

\displaystyle h({\downarrow}U_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda(\lambda+1)^{k-1}+1\>.

The first equality,

To show

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\{\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle N_{0}

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}a_{k}^{\lambda+1}+\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}a_{k}^{i}\cdot N_{k-1}\cdot a_{k}^{\lambda-i}.

The words in

Claim 3.2
.

For any

\displaystyle\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\in P_{k}}v\sqsubseteq u\bigr)\>\land\>\bigl(\bigwedge_{w\in N_{k}}w\not\sqsubseteq u\bigr)\iff u=U_{k}.

By induction on
k

k=0

A_{0}
is empty and there is only one

A_{0}^{*}

) and there are no negative constraints in

Assume now that

is not empty, the

a_{k}^{i}va_{k}^{\lambda-i}\sqsubseteq u

|u|_{a_{k}}\geq\lambda
.
However the

and can be written

Consider some fixed

∎

3.2
entails

have length

and the words in

It remains to show that

\{U_{k}\}

not closed under
\sim_{k\lambda}

\displaystyle U_{k}=(U_{k-1}a_{k})^{\lambda}(U_{k-2}a_{k-1})^{\lambda}(U_{k-3}a_{k-2})^{\lambda}\ldots(U_{0}a_{1})^{\lambda}\>.

(15)

Claim 3.3

For any

By induction on

We then derive a PT-characterisation of

3.2.
Rich words and rich factorizations

Assume a fixed
k

A
.
We say that a word

is

if
\alpha(u)=A
, i.e., the
k

letters of
A

all occur in
u
, and that it is
poor
otherwise. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we
further say that
u
is
\ell
-rich if it can be written as a
concatenation
u=r_{1}\cdots r_{\ell}u^{\prime}
where the
\ell
factors

r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}
are rich.

The
richness
of
u
is the largest

\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u
is
\ell
-rich. Note that having

|u|_{a}\geq\ell
for all letters
a\in A
does not imply that
u

is
\ell
-rich.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are respectively
\ell_{1}
-rich and
\ell_{2}
-rich,
then
v\sim_{n}v^{\prime}
implies
u_{1}vu_{2}\sim_{\ell_{1}+n+\ell_{2}}u_{1}v^{\prime}u_{2}
.

Proof.

A subword
x
of
u_{1}vu_{2}
can be decomposed as
x=x_{1}yx_{2}
where

x_{1}
is the longest prefix of
x
that is a subword of
u_{1}
and
x_{2}

is the longest suffix of the remaining
x_{1}^{-1}x
that is a subword
of
u_{2}
. Thus
y\sqsubseteq v
since
x\sqsubseteq u_{1}vu_{2}
. Now, since

u_{1}
is
\ell_{1}
-rich, we have
|x_{1}|\geq\min(\ell_{1},|x|)
, and similarly
|x_{2}|\geq\min(\ell_{2},|x_{1}^{-1}x|)
. Finally

|y|\leq n
when
|x|\leq\ell_{1}+n+\ell_{2}
, and then

y\sqsubseteq v^{\prime}
since
v\sim_{n}v^{\prime}
, entailing
x\sqsubseteq u_{1}v^{\prime}u_{2}
. A
symmetrical reasoning shows that subwords of
u_{1}v^{\prime}u_{2}
of length
\leq\ell_{1}+n+\ell_{2}
are subwords of
u_{1}vu_{2}
and we are done.
∎

The
rich factorization
of
u\in A^{*}
is the decomposition

u=u_{1}a_{1}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}v

defined by induction in the following way: if
u
is poor, we
let
m=0
and
v=u
; otherwise
u
is rich, we let
u_{1}a_{1}
(with

a_{1}\in A
) be the
shortest prefix of
u
that is rich and let

u_{2}a_{2}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}v
be the rich factorization of the remaining suffix
(u_{1}a_{1})^{-1}u
.
By construction

is the richness of
u
.
E.g.,
assuming
k=3
and
A=\{a,b,c\}
, the following is a rich factorization with
m=2
:

{\overset{u}{\overbrace{bbaaabbccccaabbbaa}}}={\overset{u_{1}}{\overbrace{bbaaabb}}}\cdot c\cdot{\overset{u_{2}}{\overbrace{cccaa}}}\cdot b\cdot{\overset{v}{\overbrace{bbaa}}}

Note that, by construction,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
and

are poor.

.

Consider two words
u,u^{\prime}
of richness
m
and with rich factorizations

and

Proof.

Since each factor

is rich, one gets

\displaystyle u_{1}a_{1}u_{2}a_{2}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}v

\displaystyle\;\sim_{n+m}\;u^{\prime}_{1}a_{1}u_{2}a_{2}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}v\;\sim_{n+m}\;u^{\prime}_{1}a_{1}u^{\prime}_{2}a_{2}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}v

\displaystyle\;\sim_{n+m}\;\cdots

\displaystyle\;\sim_{n+m}\;u^{\prime}_{1}a_{1}u^{\prime}_{2}a_{2}\cdots u^{\prime}_{m}a_{m}v\;\sim_{n+m}\;u^{\prime}_{1}a_{1}u^{\prime}_{2}a_{2}\cdots u^{\prime}_{m}a_{m}v^{\prime}\>,

by repeated uses of

.

3.3.
The small-subword theorem

Our next result is used to prove lower bounds on the PT height of long
words. It will be used repeatedly in the course
of this article.

For
k=1,2,\ldots
define
f_{k}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
by induction on
k

\displaystyle f_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=n\>,

Theorem 3.7

(Small-subword Theorem)
.

Let
k=|A|
. For all
u\in A^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists some

v\in A^{*}
with
v\lesssim_{n}u
and such that
|v|\leq f_{k}(n)
.

Proof.

By induction on
k
, the size of the alphabet.

With the base case,
k=1
, we consider a unary alphabet
A=\{a\}
and
u
is

a^{|u|}
. Now
a^{\ell}\sim_{n}u
iff
\ell=|u|<n
or
n\leq\min(\ell,|u|)
. So
taking
v=a^{\ell}
for
\ell=\min(n,|u|)
proves the claim.

When
k>1
we consider the rich factorization

u=u_{1}a_{1}u_{2}a_{2}\cdots u_{m}a_{m}u^{\prime}
of
u
.
Let
n^{\prime}=\max(n+1-m,1)
. Every
u_{i}
is a word on the subalphabet

A\smallsetminus\{a_{i}\}
. Hence by induction hypothesis there exists

v_{i}\sqsubseteq u_{i}
with
|v_{i}|\leq f_{k-1}(n^{\prime})
and
v_{i}\sim_{n^{\prime}}u_{i}
,
entailing
u_{i}a_{i}\sim_{n^{\prime}}v_{i}a_{i}
. Similarly, the induction hypothesis
entails the existence of some
v^{\prime}\sqsubseteq u^{\prime}
with
v^{\prime}\sim_{n^{\prime}-1}u^{\prime}

and
|v^{\prime}|\leq f_{k-1}(n^{\prime}-1)
. Note that in these inductive steps we use a length
bound obtained with
f_{k-1}
by using the fact that

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
and
u^{\prime}
, being poor, use at most
k-1
letters from

A
.

We now consider two cases. If
m\leq n-1
, we let
v=v_{1}a_{1}\cdots v_{m}a_{m}v^{\prime}
, so that
v\sqsubseteq u
and
|v|\leq mf_{k-1}(n^{\prime})+m+f_{k-1}(n^{\prime}-1)
. We deduce
|v|\leq f_{k}(n)
using
Eq. (
18
) and since
n^{\prime}=n+1-m
. That
v\sim_{n}u
, hence

v\lesssim_{n}u
, is an application of Lemma
3.6
:

v_{1}a_{1}\cdots v_{m}a_{m}v^{\prime}
is indeed the rich decomposition of

since

,
v^{\prime}\sim_{n^{\prime}-1}u^{\prime}
, and
v_{i}\sim_{n^{\prime}}u_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

If
m\geq n
, then
u
is
n
-rich and its subwords include all words
of length at most
n
. It is easy to extract some
n
-rich subword
v
of

u
that only uses
kn
letters. Now
v\sim_{n}u
since both
u
and

are
n
-rich, entailing

v\lesssim_{n}u
. One also checks that
|v|=kn\leq f_{k}(n)
.
∎

Note that the bound
f_{k}(n)
in
Theorem
3.7
does not depend on
u
.

We can already apply the small-subword theorem to the case of finite

(Finite languages)

Suppose

is finite and nonempty with

. Let

be the length of the longest word in

Thanks to Eq. (

is a singleton. So assume

5.
Downward closures

We now move to downward closures. It is known that, for any

L\subseteq A^{*}
,
{\downarrow}L
and
{\downarrow}_{<}L
are PT since they are the
complement of upward-closed languages. Our strategy for bounding

h({\downarrow}L)
is to approximate
L
by finitely many D-products.

Definition 5.1
.

A
D-product over
A
is a regular expression
P
of the form
E_{1}\cdot E_{2}\cdots E_{\ell}
where every
E_{i}
is either of the form
B^{*}
for a
subalphabet
B\subseteq A
(
B^{*}
is called a
star factor
of

P
), or a single letter
a\in A
(a
letter factor
). We say
that
\ell
is the length of
P
.

As is common, we abuse notation and let
P
denote both a regular
expression and the associated language.

We note that our D-products are slightly more general than the
monomials of the form
B_{0}^{*}a_{1}B_{1}^{*}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}B_{n}^{*}
considered
in
[
DGK08
]
, where a strict alternation is imposed between
star factors and letter factors. However, any D-product is easily
translated as a polynomial (a finite sum of monomials) by replacing
any two consecutive letter factors
a\cdot a^{\prime}
by the equivalent

a\cdot\emptyset^{*}\cdot a^{\prime}
, and any two consecutive star factors
B^{*}\cdot B^{\prime*}
by
B^{*}+\sum_{a\in B^{\prime}}\bigl(B^{*}\cdot a\cdot B^{\prime*}\bigr)
and then
distributing concatenations over unions. Thus the languages described
by finite unions of D-products are exactly the languages described by
polynomials (see
[
DGK08
,
PW97
]
for algebraic and logical
characterisations).

D-products generalise words, and they share with words their nice
upper bound on the PT height of downward closures:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
P
be a D-product of length
\ell
. Then
h({\downarrow}P)\leq\ell+1

and
h({\downarrow}_{<}P)\leq\ell+1
.

Proof.

Let
P^{\prime}
be the regular expression obtained from
P
by replacing any
letter factor
a
by
(a+\varepsilon)
so that
P^{\prime}={\downarrow}P
. We claim
that any residual
w^{-1}P^{\prime\prime}
of a suffix
P^{\prime\prime}
of
P^{\prime}
is either the
empty language
\emptyset
, or is itself a suffix of
P^{\prime}
. The claim
is proven by induction on the length of
P^{\prime\prime}
, then on the length of

w
, recalling that residuals can be computed inductively via

\varepsilon^{-1}L=L
and
(wb)^{-1}L=b^{-1}(w^{-1}L)
. When
considering suffixes of
P^{\prime}
(or
\emptyset
), the following
equalities can be used:

\displaystyle b^{-1}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle b^{-1}\emptyset

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle b^{-1}\bigl[(a+\varepsilon)P^{\prime\prime}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P^{\prime\prime}&\text{if }b=a,\\
b^{-1}P^{\prime\prime}&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle b^{-1}\bigl[B^{*}P^{\prime\prime}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B^{*}P^{\prime\prime}&\text{ if }b\in B,\\
b^{-1}P^{\prime\prime}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that the correctness of the third equality when
b=a
, and of the
fourth equality when
b\in B
, rely on
b^{-1}P^{\prime\prime}\subseteq P^{\prime\prime}
:
this holds because
P^{\prime}
, and then each suffix
P^{\prime\prime}
, is
downward-closed.

Finally,
P^{\prime}
has at most
\ell+1
distinct non-empty residuals since

\ell+1
suffixes. Thus the minimal DFA for

has at most

\ell+1
productive states, hence has depth at most
\ell+1
. We now
apply Theorems 1 and 2 from
[
KP13
]
and conclude that

h({\downarrow}P)\leq\ell+1
.

For bounding

very little is changed. If

contains at

{\downarrow}_{<}P
and

coincide. If

denotes a singleton

with

and
{\downarrow}_{<}P
is a finite set of words of length at most
\ell-1
, entailing

.
∎

P^{\prime}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell}a_{i}\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{p_{i}}B_{i,j}^{\prime*}\bigr)
with the
B^{\prime}_{i,j}
’s
given by

B^{\prime}_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\bigl(B_{i,1}\cup B_{i,2}\cup\cdots\cup B_{i,j}\bigr)\cap\bigl(B_{i,j}\cup B_{i,j+1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{i,p_{i}}\bigr)\>.

(24)

That is, any star product
B_{i,j}
is enlarged with letters that
occur both on its left (inside the block of star products) and on its
right. For example, with

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=d^{*}\,a_{1}\,b^{*}\,(c+c^{\prime})^{*}\,d^{*}\,(b+e)^{*}\,c^{*}\,a_{2}\,e^{*}\>,

we associate

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{0}

\displaystyle=d^{*}\,a_{1}\,b^{*}\,(b+c+c^{\prime})^{*}\,(b+c+d)^{*}\,(b+e+c)^{*}\,c^{*}\,a_{2}\,e^{*}

(
\dagger
)

\displaystyle\equiv d^{*}\,a_{1}\,(b+c+c^{\prime})^{*}\,(b+c+d)^{*}\,(b+e+c)^{*}\,a_{2}\,e^{*}\>.

(
\ddagger
)

Since
B_{i,j}\subseteq B^{\prime}_{i,j}
for all
i,j
, one has
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. However
P^{\prime}
only enlarges
P
in the following safe way:

Lemma 5.9
.

For any
u\in A^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
P\subseteq[u]_{n}
implies

P^{\prime}\subseteq[u]_{n}
.

Proof.

A word in
P^{\prime}
may use letters from some
B^{\prime}_{i,j}
’s that are not in
the corresponding
B_{i,j}
and, with any
w\in P^{\prime}
, we associate

\#_{w}
, the smallest number of letters that must be removed from
w

before the resulting subword belongs to
P
.

We now assume
P\subseteq[u]_{n}
and prove
w\in[u]_{n}
for all
w\in P^{\prime}
by induction on
\#_{w}
. The base case where
\#_{w}=0
is trivial
because then
w\in P
. So assume that
\#_{w}>0
, i.e.,
w\not\in P
. Then there must exist in
w
an occurrence of some letter
b
,
from some
B^{\prime}_{i,j}
, that does not appear in the corresponding

B_{i,j}
. (Note that, by Eq. (
24
), there must exist
some
r<j
and some
s>j
such that
b\in B_{i,r}\cap B_{i,s}
.)
Accordingly, we factor
w
under the form

w=w_{1}\,\underline{a_{i}}\,\beta_{1}\,\underline{b}\,\beta_{2}\,\underline{a_{i+1}}\,w_{2}\>,

highlighting the selected
b
, the occurrences of
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}

that surround it, and where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
belong to

B_{i,1}^{\prime*}\cdots B_{i,j}^{\prime*}
and
B_{i,j}^{\prime*}\cdots B_{i,p_{i}}^{\prime*}
, respectively. Let

w^{\prime}=w_{1}\,a_{i}\,\beta_{1}\,\beta_{2}\,a_{i+1}\,w_{2}
, i.e.,
w^{\prime}
is
w

without the
b\in B^{\prime}_{i,j}
that we singled out, so that

\#_{w^{\prime}}<\#_{w}
and the induction hypothesis yields
w^{\prime}\in[u]_{n}
.
We now claim that
w\sim_{n}w^{\prime}
. To prove this, and since
w^{\prime}\sqsubseteq w
, it is enough to show that any subword
t\in A^{\leq n}
of
w
is
also a subword of
w^{\prime}
. So consider one such

. From
t\sqsubseteq w

we extract a factorization
t=t_{1}\,t_{2}\,t_{3}
such that

\displaystyle t_{1}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq w_{1}\,a_{i}\,\beta_{1}\>,

\displaystyle t_{2}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq b\>,

\displaystyle t_{3}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\beta_{2}\,a_{i+1}\,w_{2}\>.

(25)

Thus
t_{1}t_{3}\sqsubseteq w^{\prime}
and, since
w^{\prime}\in[u]_{n}
and
|t_{1}t_{3}|\leq n
,

t_{1}t_{3}\sqsubseteq x
for any
x\in[u]_{n}
. In particular,

t_{1}t_{3}\sqsubseteq a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{\ell}\in P
, which requires

t_{1}\sqsubseteq a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{i}
or
t_{3}\sqsubseteq a_{i+1}a_{i+2}\cdots a_{\ell}
(or both). Let us assume
t_{1}\sqsubseteq a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{i}
,
the other case being similar. Combining with Eq. (
25
),
we obtain

t=t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}\sqsubseteq(w^{\prime\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}})\>a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{i}\cdot b\cdot\beta_{2}a_{i+1}w_{2}\>.

Note that
w^{\prime\prime}\in P^{\prime}
and that
\#_{w^{\prime\prime}}<\#_{w}
since the
b
that
follows
a_{i}
in
w^{\prime\prime}
can be accounted for by
B_{i,r}^{*}
. Thus

w^{\prime\prime}\in[u]_{n}
by induction hypothesis, i.e.
w^{\prime\prime}\sim_{n}w^{\prime}
, from
which we deduce
t\sqsubseteq w^{\prime}
.
∎

We can now bound the number of star factors in the D-product
P^{\prime}

associated with
P
. For this, we first simplify

P^{\prime}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell}\bigl(a_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{p_{i}}B_{i,j}^{\prime*}\bigr)
by removing any
B_{i,j}^{\prime*}
star factor that is
subsumed by its immediate neighbour, i.e., such that

B^{\prime}_{i,j}\subseteq B^{\prime}_{i,j-1}
or
B^{\prime}_{i,j}\subseteq B^{\prime}_{i,j+1}
. This
is exactly how we moved from (

†

‣
5
) to (

‡

‣
5
) in
our earlier example, and it shortens

without changing the denoted
language. Once no more simplifications are possible, we can bound the
length of the resulting D-product with the following combinatorial
observation:

Lemma 5.10
.

Assume that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}\subseteq A
are
p
subalphabets such
that

— for all
1\leq j<p
,
A_{j}\not\subseteq A_{j+1}
and
A_{j+1}\not\subseteq A_{j}
;

— for all
b\in A
and
1\leq j<k<j^{\prime}\leq p
, if
b\in A_{j}\cap A_{j^{\prime}}
, then
b\in A_{k}
.

Then
p\leq|A|
.

Proof.

Note that by the first condition, each
A_{j}
is nonempty. Extend
the sequence by defining
A_{0}=A_{p+1}=\emptyset
. For
0\leq j\leq p
, define
\Delta_{j}=A_{j}\bigtriangleup A_{j+1}
, where
\bigtriangleup
denotes symmetric difference. Now

\Delta_{0}
and
\Delta_{p}
have size at least
1
, and by the first
condition, every other
\Delta_{j}
has size at least
2
. Thus

\sum_{j=0}^{p}|\Delta_{j}|\geq 2p
. By the second condition, any

b\in A
occurs in at most two
\Delta_{j}
’s, thus
\sum_{j=0}^{p}|\Delta_{j}|\leq 2|A|
. So we conclude
2p\leq 2|A|
.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

For every
u\in A^{*}
there is a D-product

of length at most

, and such that
u\in P^{\prime}_{u}\subseteq[u]_{n}
.

Proof.

P^{\prime}_{u}
as constructed above (and after simplifications) has at most

Consequently, any star factor block in

has length at most

, leading to a

.
Finally, that

is a consequence of

since

.
∎

We may now conclude the proof of

with

is closed under

6.
Piecewise-testability and PT height for
I(L)

Recall that
I(L)
is the set of words which are incomparable (via

\sqsubseteq
or
\sqsupseteq
) with
some word
in
L
. I.e., it is
the image of
L
by the incomparability relation
\perp
, or
equivalently its pre-image since
\perp
is symmetric.

In this section we prove the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose
L\subseteq A^{*}
is PT and let
k=|A|
and

m=f_{k}(h(L))
. Then
I(L)
is PT and

h(I(L))\leq m+1\>.

We saw that
\perp_{A^{*}}
is a rational
relation, so that
I(L)
is regular
when
L
is regular, see Proposition
2.2
.
Showing that
I
also preserves piecewise-testability requires more
work. For such questions,
I
does not behave as simply as the
pre-images we considered in earlier sections. In particular it does
not necessarily yield languages that are PT, unlike
{\uparrow}L
or
{\downarrow}L
.

At this point it is useful to examine some examples and make some
general observations. Let
A=\{a,b,c\}
and define the language
L_{1}

of all finite prefixes of
(abc)^{\omega}
via

L_{1}=(abc)^{*}(\varepsilon+a+ab)=\{\varepsilon,a,ab,abc,abca,abcab,\ldots\}\>.

Note that
L_{1}
is totally ordered by
\sqsubseteq
hence no word of

L_{1}
is in
I(L_{1})
, i.e.,
I(L_{1})\subseteq A^{*}\smallsetminus L_{1}
.

To prove the reverse inclusion, we rely on the fact that a word is
incomparable with any other word having same length. I.e.,

I(u)\supseteq A^{=|u|}\smallsetminus\{u\}
for any
u
, and thus, for any
language
L
,

I(L)=\bigcup_{u\in L}I(u)\supseteq\bigcup_{u\in L}\bigl(A^{=|u|}\smallsetminus\{u\}\bigr)\>.

(26)

Since
L_{1}
above contains at least one word of any given length,
Eq. (
26
) entails
I(L_{1})\supseteq A^{*}\smallsetminus L_{1}
. Finally we have proved that
I(L_{1})=A^{*}\smallsetminus L_{1}
. Thus

I(L_{1})
is not PT since
L_{1}
is not.

A similar example shows that
I(L)
is not necessarily regular when
L
is not.
For example, take
A=\{a,b\}
and let

L_{2}=\{a^{\ell}b^{\ell}(\varepsilon+b)~|~\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}=\{\varepsilon,b,ab,abb,aabb,a^{2}b^{3},a^{3}b^{3},\ldots\}\>.

Here too
L_{2}
is totally ordered by
\sqsubseteq
and contains one word
of each length. Hence
I(L_{2})=A^{*}\smallsetminus L_{2}
, which is not regular.

Let us now consider some PT languages. In the case of a singleton
language
L=\{w\}
, we know from Eq. (
20
)
that
h(I(w))=|w|
when
|A|\geq 2
, and
h(I(w))=0
when

|A|<2
. This can be used to bound
h(I(F))
for a finite language

F
, using
I(F)=\bigcup_{w\in F}I(w)
.

Consider now
L_{3}=[aab]_{2}
, i.e.,
L_{3}=aaa^{*}b
. This language is
infinite but it is totally ordered by
\sqsubseteq
and has one word of
each length
\ell\geq 3
. Hence
I(L_{3})=A^{*}\smallsetminus L_{3}\smallsetminus{\downarrow}aab
and we can easily bound
h(I(L_{3}))
using results
from the previous sections.

Another infinite PT language is
L_{4}=[aabb]_{2}
, i.e.,

L_{4}=aaa^{*}bbb^{*}
. A different strategy applies here:
L_{4}
contains
no words of length
\ell<4
and exactly
\ell-3

words of each length
\ell\geq 4
. We may invoke a consequence of
Eq. (
26
): if a language
L
contains at least two words
having same length
\ell
then
I(L)
contains all words of length

\ell
. Applied to
L_{4}
, this entails
I(L_{4})\supseteq A^{\geq 5}
,
which is enough to conclude that
I(L_{4})
is
A^{\geq 5}\cup F
for
some finite
F\subseteq A^{\leq 4}
, entailing
h(I(L_{4}))\leq 5
.

As the above examples suggest, it is useful to think of the “layers”
L\cap A^{=\ell}=\{w\in L:|w|=\ell\}
of
L
, and classify them into

empty
,
singular
, or
populous
layers, depending on
whether they contain 0, 1, or more words. Observe that if
L\cap A^{=\ell}
is populous then
I(L)\cap A^{=\ell}
equals
A^{=\ell}
.

It is also useful to decompose PT-languages into the equivalence
classes that make them up. Therefore, in the rest of this section, we
focus on some equivalence class
[w]_{n}\subseteq A^{*}
where
n=h(L)
.

A first observation is that the populous layers of
[w]_{n}
propagate
upwards:

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. If
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p}
is populous, then
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p+1}
is populous too.

Proof.

Assume that
[w]_{n}
contains two different words
u_{1},u_{2}
of length

p
. Then
p>0
and these words can be written under the form

u_{1}=u_{0}av_{1}
and
u_{2}=u_{0}bv_{2}
where
u_{0}
is their longest common
prefix and
a,b
are two distinct letters occurring at the first
position where
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
differ. Applying
Lemma
2.1
(
3
) we deduce that
[w]_{n}

contains either
u_{0}abv_{2}
or
u_{0}bav_{1}
. Let us assume, w.l.o.g.,
that
u_{0}bav_{1}\sim_{n}w
since the other case is similar. We now claim
that
u_{0}bbv_{2}\sim_{n}w
. Since
w\sim_{n}u_{2}\sqsubseteq u_{0}bbv_{2}
, it is
enough to show that every subword
s
of
u_{0}bbv_{2}
of length at most

n
is also a subword of
u_{2}
. So let us pick any such
s
and factor
it as
s=s_{0}s_{b}s_{2}
with
s_{0}\sqsubseteq u_{0}
,
s_{b}\sqsubseteq bb
, and

s_{2}\sqsubseteq v_{2}
, and with furthermore
s_{0}
chosen longest possible.
If
s_{b}\leq b
then
s\sqsubseteq u_{0}bv_{2}=u_{2}
and we are done, so we
assume
s_{b}=bb
. Let
s^{\prime}=s_{0}bs_{2}
and note that
s^{\prime}\sqsubseteq u_{2}
,
hence
s^{\prime}\sqsubseteq u_{1}
since
u_{1}\sim_{n}u_{2}
and
|s^{\prime}|<|s|\leq n
.
Now
s^{\prime}=s_{0}bs_{2}\sqsubseteq u_{0}av_{1}=u_{1}
requires
bs_{2}\sqsubseteq av_{1}

since our choice of
s_{0}
longest entails
s_{0}b\not\sqsubseteq u_{0}
. This
gives
s=s_{0}bbs_{2}\sqsubseteq u_{0}bav_{1}
, hence
s\sqsubseteq u_{2}
since

u_{0}bav_{1}\sim_{n}u_{2}
.

We have shown that
[w]_{n}
contains
u_{0}bav_{1}
and
u_{0}bbv_{2}
, both
words having length
p+1
.
∎

Populous layers also propagate downwards in the following sense:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
p\geq 2
. If
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p}
is populous then
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p-1}
is populous or
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p-2}
is empty (or both).

Proof.

Assume that layer
p
is populous and that layer
p-2
is non
empty. If layer
p-2
is populous, then layer
p-1
is populous by

Lemma

6.2
and we are done. So assume that
[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p-2}=\{x\}
is singular. Then
Lemma
2.1
(
5
) entails that
x\sqsubseteq w^{\prime}

for all
w^{\prime}\in[w]_{n}\cap A^{\geq p-1}
, and since layer
p
is not
empty, Lemma
2.1
(
1
) entails that layer
p-1

too is not empty. Pick
y\in[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p-1}
and factor it as

such that

. This entails

by Lemma
2.1
(
6
). By assumption,

[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p}
is populous, hence contains another word

and there is a word
y^{\prime}\in[w]_{n}\cap A^{=p-1}
with

. If

we have proved that layer

is
populous and we are done. Otherwise

and we may consider
the different possibilities for

, knowing

|z^{\prime}|=|y|+1
. If

is either some

or

with

then we let

. From

by Lemma

(

).
If

is

and again

.) If

is

with

, the same reasoning applies to

. In all

∎

With Lemmas
6.2
and

, we
see that almost all layers of

are populous as soon as one

We already saw an example of the second situation with

, and an example of the first situation is

, where the lowest nonempty layer is

.

We now consider the general case:

Lemma 6.4
.

h(I([w]_{n}))\leq m+1
.

Proof.

Recall that
[w]_{n}
is a singleton or is infinite
(Lemma
2.1
(
7
)). We consider two cases.

(1)

Assume that
[w]_{n}=\{w\}
is a singleton. Then
h(I(w))\leq|w|

by Eq. (
20
) and
|w|\leq m
by the
small-subword theorem. Hence
h(I([w]_{n}))\leq m
.

(2)

Assume that
[w]_{n}
is infinite. Let
u
be a shortest word in
[w]_{n}
and write
p
for
its length
|u|
. By the small-subword theorem,
p\leq m
. Since

[w]_{n}
is infinite, and by Lemma
2.1
(
1
),
it contains at least one word of each length

, hence

two subcases.

(a)

If

is a total order under

and

, we obtain

.

(b)

If
[w]_{n}
is not a total order under

, all layers

are populous by Lemmas

and

. If

is the unique shortest word in

, we have

, entailing

. Otherwise layer

is populous
too, entailing

, i.e.,

. We

by Eq. (

).
∎

We may now conclude:

Proof of

.

Being

-PT,

is a finite union

of equivalence classes of

, so that

is

∎

7.
Deciding the two-variable logic of subwords

In this section we use our results on PT heights to establish
complexity bounds on a decidable fragment of
{\mathsf{FO}}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq)
, the
first-order logic of subwords.

We assume familiarity with basic notions of first-order logic as
exposed in, e.g.,
[
Har09
]
: bound and free occurrences of
variables, quantifier depth of formulae, and fragments
{\mathsf{FO}}^{n}
where
at most
n
different variables (free or bound) are used. In
particular, if
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
has
n
free variables, we write

R_{\phi}
for the
n
-ary relation defined by
\phi
on the underlying
structure.

The signature of the
{\mathsf{FO}}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq)
logic only contains one
predicate symbol, “
\sqsubseteq
”, denoting the subword relation.
Terms are variables taken from a countable set
X=\{x,y,z,\ldots\}

and all words
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots\in A^{*}
as constant symbols (denoting themselves).
For example, with
A=\{a,b,c,\ldots\}
,
\exists x\bigl(ab\sqsubseteq x\land bc\sqsubseteq x\land\neg(abc\sqsubseteq x)\bigr)
is a true sentence
as witnessed by
x\mapsto bcab
.

The logic of the subword relation is a logic of substructure ordering
like those considered by Ježek and McKenzie
(see
[
JM09
]
and subsequent papers). It is one of the
simplest and most natural substructure ordering occurring in computer
science
[
Kus06
]
. In its full generality, this logic is
computably isomorphic with
{\mathsf{FO}}(\mathbb{N},+,\times)
, hence
undecidable
[
KSY10
]
. We showed that already the
\Sigma_{2}

fragment is undecidable
[
KS15
]
and recently Halfon
et al.
showed that even the
\Sigma_{1}
fragment is
undecidable
[
HSZ17
]
. This was very surprising: by comparison,
“words equations”, i.e., the
\Sigma_{1}
fragment of

{\mathsf{FO}}(A^{*},\cdot,=)
in which the prefix relation can be defined, are
decidable in
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}

[
Die02

Pla04

Jeż16
]

We have previously shown that
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq)
, the 2-variable fragment, is decidable by a

[

we extend our earlier analysis of the expressive power and complexity
of the
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}

When performing quantifier elimination, it is
convenient to enrich the basic logic by allowing all regular languages

as monadic predicates with the expected

rather than

and assume that

expression or a finite automaton — For example, we can state that

is the downward closure of

\forall x\bigl[x\in(a+b)^{*}\iff\exists y(y\in(ab)^{*}\land x\sqsubseteq y)\bigr]

7.1.
Subword-recognizable relations

In order to characterise the
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
-definable relations, we need some
definitions. A relation
R\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
is

subword-recognizable
, if it belongs to the boolean closure of

{\mathsf{Rec}}(A^{*}\times A^{*})\cup\{{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}},{\sqsupseteq_{A^{*}}}\}
. It is
furthermore
subword-piecewise-testable
, if it belongs to the
boolean closure of
{\mathsf{PT}}(A^{*}\times A^{*})\cup\{{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}},{\sqsupseteq_{A^{*}}}\}
. We write

{\mathsf{Rec}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
and
{\mathsf{PT}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})

for the corresponding classes.

Proposition 7.1

(Normal form for
{\mathsf{Rec}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
and
{\mathsf{PT}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
)
.

A relation
R\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
is subword-recognizable if, and
only if, it can be written under the form

R\;=\;({\sqsubset_{A^{*}}}\cap R_{1})\cup({=_{A^{*}}}\cap R_{2})\cup({\sqsupset_{A^{*}}}\cap R_{3})\cup({\perp_{A^{*}}}\cap R_{4})

(NF)

for some recognizable relations
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
.

Furthermore,
R
is subword-piecewise-testable if, and only if,
the relations
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
can be chosen among the
piecewise-testable relations.

Proof.

That the normal forms are subword-recognizable is clear since

{=_{A^{*}}}
and
{\sqsubset_{A^{*}}}
belong to the boolean closure of

\{{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}},{\sqsupseteq_{A^{*}}}\}
: they are

{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}}\cap{\sqsupseteq_{A^{*}}}
and

{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}}\smallsetminus{=_{A^{*}}}
.

Showing the other direction, i.e., that a subword-recognizable or
subword-piecewise-testable relation
R
can be put in normal forms,
is done by induction on the boolean combination realising
R
from the
generators of the boolean closure. Here it suffices to show that normal forms are closed under
intersections and complementations. Let us write

\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4}\}
for
\{{\sqsubset_{A^{*}}},{=_{A^{*}}},{\sqsupset_{A^{*}}},{\perp_{A^{*}}}\}

and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap R_{i}
for normal forms. Since the

\xi_{i}
’s are pairwise disjoint, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap R_{i}\Bigr)\cap\Bigl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap R^{\prime}_{i}\Bigr)\;=\;\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap(R_{i}\cap R^{\prime}_{i})\>.

(27)

Since the
\xi_{i}
’s form a partition of
A^{*}\times A^{*}
, we
further have

\displaystyle(A^{*}\times A^{*})\smallsetminus(\xi_{i}\cap R)\;=\;\Bigl(\xi_{i}\cap\bigl[(A^{*}\times A^{*})\smallsetminus R\bigr]\Bigr)\cup\bigcup_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}\cap(A^{*}\times A^{*})\>.

(28)

Thus we see that normal forms are closed under
boolean operations since
{\mathsf{Rec}}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
is.

Finally, the same proof applies to
{\mathsf{PT}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
.
∎

7.2.
Quantifier elimination for
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)

We may now characterise the
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
-definable relations.

Theorem 7.2
.

(i) A relation
R\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
is definable in the extended
logic
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},{\sqsubseteq,}
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)
iff it is subword-recognizable.

(ii) It is definable in the basic logic
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots)

iff it is subword-piecewise-testable.

(iii) Furthermore, a normal form for
R_{\phi}
can be computed from the

{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
formula
\phi(x,y)
,

Proof.

The
(\Leftarrow)
direction of
(i)
is obvious: for example

R={\sqsubset_{A^{*}}}\cap L\times L^{\prime}
is definable via
x\sqsubseteq y\land x\not=y\land x\in L\land y\in L^{\prime}
, a
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
formula. When proving the
same
(\Leftarrow)
direction for
(ii)
, we cannot use
regular predicates to express
x\in L
or
y\in L
. But since we
assume that
L
is PT, it is a boolean combination of filters
{\uparrow}u
,

{\uparrow}u^{\prime}
, etc., so
x\in L
can be expressed as a boolean
combination of atomic formulas
u\sqsubseteq x
,
u^{\prime}\sqsubseteq x
, etc.

We prove the
(\Rightarrow)
direction for
(i-ii)
by structural induction on the

{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
-formula
\phi(x,y)
that defines
R_{\phi}
. We consider all
cases:

If
\phi
is an atomic formula of the form
x\in L
or
y\in L^{\prime}
, then

R_{\phi}
is
L\times A^{*}
or
A^{*}\times L^{\prime}
. If
\phi
is some
u\in L
, then
R_{\phi}
is one of the trivial
\emptyset
or
A^{*}\times A^{*}
.

If
\phi
is an atomic formula of the form
x\sqsubseteq u
or
v\sqsubseteq y
for some constant word
u
or
v
then
R_{\phi}
is
({\downarrow}u)\times A^{*}
or
A^{*}\times({\uparrow}v)
respectively (a piecewise-testable
relation in each case). If
\phi
is
x\sqsubseteq y
then
R_{\phi}
is

{\sqsubseteq_{A^{*}}}
, and
R_{\phi}
is trivial if
\phi
is some
u\sqsubseteq v
.

If
\phi
is a conjunction
\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}
or a negation

\neg\phi_{1}
, we rely on the induction hypothesis and the closure
properties of
{\mathsf{Rec}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})

and
{\mathsf{PT}}_{\sqsubseteq}(A^{*}\times A^{*})
.

The remaining case is when
\phi(x,y)
is some
\exists x.\psi(x,y)
,
the case
\exists y.\psi(x,y)
being identical. Here
R_{\phi}=A^{*}\times\pi_{2}(R_{\psi})
where
\pi_{2}:A^{*}\times A^{*}\to A^{*}
is the
projection
(u,v)\mapsto v
lifted from pairs to sets of pairs, i.e.,
relations. To compute
\pi_{2}(R_{\psi})
we write
R_{\psi}
in normal
form —thanks to the induction hypothesis— and use
\pi_{2}\bigl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap R_{i}\bigr)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\pi_{2}\bigl(\xi_{i}\cap R_{i}\bigr)
and the following equalities (proofs
omitted):

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(=_{A*}\cap L\times L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=L\cap L^{\prime}\>,

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(\sqsubset_{A*}\cap L\times L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=({\uparrow}_{<}L)\cap L^{\prime}\>,

(29)

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(\perp_{A*}\cap L\times L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=I(L)\cap L^{\prime}\>,

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(\sqsupset_{A*}\cap L\times L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=({\downarrow}_{<}L)\cap L^{\prime}\>.

(30)

Finally, in the case where
\phi
does not use regular predicates, the
above inductive construction only produces subword-piecewise-testable
relations.

We now see why
(iii)
holds: the operations used above can all
computed effectively on relations in normal form, e.g., using a
quadruplet of automata for the
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
of (
NF
).
The required operations on automata are classic constructions: boolean
combinations and images of regular languages by
\sqsupset_{A}
,

\sqsubset_{A}
and
\perp_{A}
(all rational relations).
∎

We note that if
\phi(x)
is a
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
formula with a single free
variable,
R_{\phi}
can be put under the form
L\times A^{*}
, i.e.,

\phi(x)
defines a regular property of words, and a
piecewise-testable one if
\phi(x)
is in the basic logic.

Theorem

7.2
has several corollaries.
Firstly, and since the
normal forms can be effectively computed, we have

Corollary 7.3

(Decidability
[
KS15
]
)
.

Validity and satisfiability are decidable for

{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,\linebreak L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)
.

By contrast, note that the
{\mathsf{FO}}^{3}\cap\Sigma_{2}
and the
\Sigma_{1}

fragments of the basic logic are
undecidable
[
KS15
,
HSZ17
]
.

Secondly, the computations inside the proof of
Theorem

7.2
can
be seen as a quantifier-elimination procedure.

Corollary 7.4
.

Any
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)

quantifier-free
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}
formula. The same holds for the basic logic

{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots)
.

7.3.
Complexity for
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq)

The algorithm underlying the proof of
Theorem

7.2

(iii)
can be
implemented using finite-state automata to handle and compute
subword-recognizable relations via their normal forms.
The steps described in
Eqs. (
29
–
30
) involve computing
images of regular languages by (fixed) rational relations and may
induce an exponential complexity blow-up. In particular the
pre-images
{\uparrow}_{<}L
and
{\downarrow}_{<}L
can have exponential size if one
uses deterministic or alternating automata, while if one uses
nondeterministic
(or unambiguous) automata,
the dual pre-images
\neg({\uparrow}_{<}(\neg L))
and

\neg({\downarrow}_{<}(\neg L))
—used in eliminating a universal quantifier— can have doubly exponential size
[
KNS16
]
. Therefore
the best known upper bound for the decidability of

{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)
is a tower of exponentials with
height bounded by the nesting depth of the formula at hand, hence
a nonelementary complexity. Regarding lower bounds, only

{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hardness has been established
[
KS15
]
.

We now turn to the basic logic,
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots)
where
regular predicates are not allowed. As stated in
Theorem

7.2
,
the quantifier-elimination procedure will only produce
subword-piecewise-testable relations and languages. Furthermore, it
is possible to bound the PT height of the defined languages and deduce
an elementary complexity upper bound.

Theorem 7.5

(
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots)
has elementary complexity)
.

Assume that
\phi
is a
{\mathsf{FO}}^{2}(A^{*},\sqsubseteq,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots)
formula.
Then
h(R_{\phi})
is in
2^{2^{O(|\phi|)}}
.

Furthermore, computing DFAs for the normal form of
R_{\phi}
(hence deciding the
satisfiability or the validity of
\phi
) can be
done in
{\mathsf{3-}}{\mathsf{EXPTIME}}
.

Proof.

The quantifier-elimination procedure that proves

Theorem

7.2

(ii)
builds, for any subformula
\psi
of
\phi
, a
relation of the form
\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}\cap R_{i}

represented by a quadruplet
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
of
PT relations. The PT height of these relations can be bounded. For
example, the PT height is given by
|u|
for
\psi
an atomic formula
of the form
u\sqsubseteq x
. Boolean combinations do not increase
PT height even in the case of subword-piecewise-testable relations,
see Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
).
Quantifier-elimination can increase PT height when we
compute
{\uparrow}_{<}L
,
{\downarrow}_{<}L
and
I(L)
as prescribed by
Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
). But
Theorems
4.4
,
5.5
and

6.1
apply and show that the increase is
polynomially bounded. Such increases combined at most
|\phi|
times
give a PT height bounded in
2^{2^{O(|\phi|)}}
.

Finally, when the PT height of
R_{\phi}
(and of all intermediary

R_{\psi}
) have been bounded in
2^{2^{O(|\phi|)}}
, we obtain a bound
on the size of the minimal DFAs and the time and space needed to
compute them using
Theorem

2.3
.
∎

8.
Concluding remarks

We developed several new techniques for proving upper and lower bounds
on the PT height of languages constructed by closing w.r.t. the
subword ordering or its inverse. We also considered related
constructions like taking minimal elements, or taking the image by the
incomparability relation.
In general, the PT height of upward closures is bounded with the length of
minimal words.
For downward closures, we developed techniques for expressing them
with D-products and bounding their lengths.
We illustrated these techniques with regular and context-free
languages but more classes can be considered
[
Zet15
]
.
More importantly, the closures of PT languages have PT height bounded
polynomially in terms of the PT height of the argument.
Our main tool here is the small-subword theorem
that provides tight lower bounds on the PT height of finite
languages, with ad hoc developments for
I(L)
.

These results are used to bound the complexity of the two-variable
logic of subwords but we believe that the PT hierarchy can be used
more generally as an effective measure of descriptive complexity. (The
same can be said of the hierarchies of locally-testable languages, or
of dot-depth-one languages).

This research program raises many interesting
questions, such as connecting PT height and other measures, narrowing
the gaps remaining in our Theorems
4.4
,

5.5
, and
6.1
, and enriching the known
collection of PT preserving operations.
