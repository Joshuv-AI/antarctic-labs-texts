Variations on Keeler’s Theorem
A. Bartas, D. Lara, B. Leavitt, B. Vessely

Abstract
. The 2010 Futurama episode The Prisoner of Benda features a mind swapping machine that swaps the minds of two people at a time with the restriction that the same pair of people cannot use the machine more than once. We show that if a machine swaps n n people cyclically with the condition that the same group of people cannot use the machine again, we can find a way to get everyone back. We prove our solution is optimal for when n = 3 n=3 . We also introduce an infinite variant of the mind swapping machine.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context and Motivation

In
Futurama’s
acclaimed episode,
The Prisoner of Benda
, a machine swaps the minds of two people at once. The episodes features a restriction in which a pair can only use the machine to swap once. A single person, however, can use the machine multiple times with different partners. After a mind switching fiasco, an algorithm is found in the show that gets everyone back into their original body without knowing the sequence of switches that occurred. The solution was written up by one of the show’s writers, mathematician Ken Keeler. This algorithm is often referred to as Keeler’s Theorem.

Keeler reframed the problem in terms of permutations in the following way. Each brain swap can be described as a transposition in
S_{n}
. The mind switching fiasco is then a product of transpositions that yields a permutation that is contained in
S_{n}
. The problem to get everyone back into their original bodies reduces to finding a way to write the inverse of a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
as a product of distinct transpositions. Keeler’s solution uses two external individuals that have not used the machine prior to the mind switching frenzy. It is important to emphasize that the inverse of any permutation in
S_{n}
exists; the significance of Keeler’s result is that the inverse can be written as a product of distinct transpositions. For example, suppose that Person
1
and Person
2
use the machine. If there were no restriction, the inverse of
(1\;2)
is itself. That is, Person
1
and Person
2
would use the machine again to get themselves back into their original bodies. With the constraint, the inverse of the permutation can be written as a product of distinct transpositions as

(1.1)

(1\;2)^{-1}=(x\;y)(2\;x)(1\;y)(2\;y)(1\;x).

In 2016, Elder
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
considered a machine that permutes the minds of
k
people cyclically. That is, if people
1,2,\cdots,k
use the machine, then their minds get permuted as follows:

1\to 2\to\cdots\to k\to 1.

This can be expressed as the
k
-cycle
(1\;2\;3\cdots\;k)
. The restriction that Elder introduced is that once a cycle
\tau
has occurred, no cycle in the cyclic subgroup
\langle\tau\rangle
can occur. Elder showed that there is a way to return everyone’s mind back to their own body when
k
is either even or prime. In fact, Elder’s proof works for all natural numbers
k\geq 2
.

Evans, Huang, and Nguyen
[
5
]
explored Keeler’s Theorem from a different perspective. They refined Keeler’s result by providing a more efficient algorithm that uses the smallest possible number of switches, with the restriction that at least one external must be used in each step. In addition, they also proved that the identity permutation in
S_{n}
can can be written as a product of
m
distinct transpositions for when
m
is even and when
6\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
.

Our goals are to build upon these results. In our first variation of the problem, the machine permutes
k
people cyclically, as in Elder’s variation. Our work varies from Elder’s in that once a set of
k
people have used the machine, that same set cannot use the machine again. However, each individual can use the machine multiple times. Given any permutation that is obtained from permuting
n
people cyclically, we provide an algorithm that gets everyone back into their original body. To build on the work of Evans, Huang, and Nguyen, we explore the optimally of our solution for when the machine permutes
3
people. Finally, we introduce an infinite variation of the machine that swaps the minds of an infinite amount of people. Our main theorems are as follows.

1.2.
Main Results

We employ a variation similar to that of Elder
[
4
]
. A machine swaps
k
people cyclically. That is if
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}
use the machine, then the machine permutes their minds as

a_{1}\to a_{2}\to\cdots\to a_{k}\to a_{1}.

Our restriction is that no set of
k
people can use the machine more than once. Additionally, we follow Keeler and Elder’s assumption in that we don’t know which steps were taken to achieve the original mind scrambling, meaning we need to introduce external users when try to revert everyone back into their original bodies. We will refer to these external people as
outsiders
throughout the paper. Two of our main theorems under these assumptions are as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 3
,
n\geq m
. Let
H_{m}\subseteq S_{n}
denote the subgroup generated by all

, then there exist

such that

(1)

\mu_{i}\in S_{n+d}\setminus S_{n}
, where

3(\frac{m}{2}-1)&\text{if }m\text{ is even}\end{cases}

(2)

\sigma^{-1}=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}
, where
\mu_{i}
is an
m
-cycle permuting a distinct set of
m

.

That is, if a collection of
n
people have used a mind swap machine of size

, we can revert everyone back with the addition of
m-2
outsiders if
m
is odd and
3\left(m/2-1)\right)
outsiders if
m
is even.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be written as a product of
r
disjoint cycles as

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where
\tau_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-cycle with

and

. Consider outsider

which

acts upon. We can write

{(n+r)\over 2}
3-cycles, each of which contains

must have at least

If
n

, we can group them up into a

\left(\frac{n+r}{2}\right)
. We later consider a variation of the problem where the machine ”cycles” a countably infinite amount of people. The main result shown in this variation is,

.

Let

This was quite surprising as

could have been created using an arbitrarily large number of swaps and yet its inverse can always be written in 2 steps.

1.3.
Symmetric Group

In this section, we will present fundamental results about the symmetric group
S_{n}
. For a more detailed recollection, we refer the interested reader to standard references such as Dummit and Foote
[
2
]
.

We denote the group of permutations of
n
objects by
S_{n}
. A
cycle
of length
k
is a permutation that permutes
k
objects cyclically.

S_{n}
is defined to be the set of bijections on the set
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, and it forms a group under function composition. The objects of
S_{n}
are called
permutations
. For an example, consider the permutation
\sigma\in S_{4}
defined as

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
4&3&1&2\end{pmatrix}

where
\sigma
sends
1
to
4
,
2
to
3
, and so on. A convenient alternative notation for permutations is called cycle notation. We see that
\sigma
permutes the objects as

1\to 4\to 2\to 3\to 1.

We can then write
\sigma
in cycle notation as
\sigma=(1\;4\;2\;3)
. Note that if
\sigma=(1\;4\;2\;3)\in S_{10}
, it still permutes
1\to 4\to 2\to 3\to 1
cyclically, but all other numbers stay fixed.
A cycle
\tau\in S_{n}
of length
k\leq n
is a function that permutes
k
objects cyclically. Two cycles are
disjoint
if the numbers moved by one cycle are left fixed by the other. For example,
(1\;3)(2\;5)
is a product of two disjoint cycles.

Example 1.5
.

Let’s compute the product

(1.6)

(1\;5)(1\;2\;3)(1\;4).

When multiplying permutations, the permutation on the right is evaluated first. The right most permutation sends
1\to 4
. The second leaves
4
fixed, and so does the third. Thus, the permutation sends
1\to 4
. Next, we evaluate the permutation at
4
. The right most cycle sends
4\to 1
, the second sends
1\to 2
, and the third leaves
2
fixed. Continuing this process, we obtain

(1.7)

(1\;5)(1\;2\;3)(1\;4)=(1\;4\;2\;3\;5).

∎

The example illustrates that when we take a product of non-disjoint cycles, we obtain a product of disjoint cycles (a product of one cycle, in this example). This is true for all permutations.

Theorem 1.8
.

Every permutation of
S_{n}
can be written uniquely, up to order, as a product of disjoint cycles.

A
transposition
is a cycle of length
2
, such as
(1\;2)
. Every permutation can be written as a product of transpositions. A permutation is said to be
even
if it can be written as a product of of an even number of transpositions and
odd
if it can be written as a product of an odd number of transpositions. All permutations are either even or odd but not both.

Example 1.9
.

(123)=(13)(12)

so the cycle
(123)
is even.

(1234)=(14)(13)(12)

so the cycle
(1234)
is odd. Let
I
be the identity permutation. Observe

I=(12)(12)

so
I
is even.
∎

From these examples we can observe that a cycle of odd length is an even permutation and a cycle of even length is an odd permutation. The collection of all even permutation of
S_{n}
forms a subgroup called the alternating group
A_{n}.
We present several key results about the alternating group.

Theorem 1.10
.

A_{n}
is a subgroup of index
2
and for
n\geq 3
,
A_{n}
is generated by
3
-cycles.

That is, every permutation in
A_{n}
can be written as a product of three cycles. For example, consider
(3\;4)(1\;2)\in A_{n}
, then

(3\;4)(1\;2)=(3\;1\;4)(1\;2\;3).

Another interesting result is that
A_{n}
is a
normal
subgroup of
S_{n}
, meaning that we can mod out the objects of
S_{n}
by
A_{n}
. The resulting equivalence classes forms a subgroup. This is written as
S_{n}/A_{n}
. It turns out that for
n\geq 5
, we have the following interesting result.

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
n\geq 5
. Then if
H
is a non-trivial normal subgroup of
S_{n}
, then
H=A_{n}
.

1.4.
Mind Swapping Machines

The mind swapping machines can be completely described in terms of permutations. Suppose people
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}
use the machine.
The machine is ordered in such a way that person
a_{1}
sits on seat
1
and so on. The machine then performs the permutation
(a_{1}\;\cdots\;a_{k})
.

Given a collection of people whose minds have been permuted by some sequence of uses of the
k
-machine, we can always write that permutation of minds as a product of

k
-cycles. From fundamental results about the symmetric group, the resulting permutation is a product of distinct cycles.

The problem of getting everyone’s mind back to their own body is reduced to finding the inverse of
\tau\in S_{n}
. Every permutation can be written as a product of disjoint cycles. For example, we can write the permutation
\tau\in S_{n}
as a product of disjoint cycles where

\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k}

with
\tau_{i}\in S_{n}
for all
i
. Observe that in order to find the inverse of
\tau
, we just need to find the inverse of each
\tau_{i}
:

\tau^{-1}=\tau_{k}^{-1}\cdots\tau_{1}^{-1}.

Under our restrictions, we will not know which sets of people were cycled in the machine to create the original permutation. Thus, we again require that the inverse of each
\tau_{i}
must contain an outsider in each step.

Example 1.12
.

Suppose Amy, Bender, and Fry have used the
3
-machine. Regardless of what order they used the machine, we label people as follows. We start with Amy’s body. Her body gets the label
a_{1}
. Suppose Amy’s mind got moved to Fry’s body. Fry’s body is then labeled
a_{2}
. We then look for Fry’s mind and in this case, the only possibility is that it must be in Bender’s body. We label Bender as
a_{3}
, thus meaning Bender’s mind is in Amy’s body. The permutation is then written as

\tau=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;a_{3})

Of course, if the machine allowed the same group of people to use the machine more than once, we could just the machine two more times to get everyone back. Since this is not allowed under our restriction, the goal is then to find outsiders that have not used the machine yet to help revert their minds. That is, we want to write the inverse of
\tau
as a product of distinct
3
-cycles.
∎

2.
Keeler’s Result

In the original problem, two people switch minds and realize they cannot switch back. By the end of the episode in which this mind-switching occurs, the characters’ minds are all scrambled. Keeler’s original solution uses two outsiders to get everyone back into their original bodies. We will reprove this solution in a novel manner. We first note that our solution as proposed in Theorem
2.1
is not exactly the same as Keeler’s solution
[
3
]
. Nevertheless, our algorithm works similarly to Keeler’s, while allowing for a clearer proof.

We call our two outsiders
x
and
y
, letting
x=n+1
and
y=n+2
. We define
S_{n+2}\setminus S_{n}
to be the collection of permutations that permute
x
or
y
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Given any cycle
\tau_{k}=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})\in S_{n}
, its inverse can be expressed as a product of distinct transpositions in
S_{n+2}\setminus S_{n}
in the following form:

(2.2)

\tau_{k}^{-1}=(x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{\ell=2}^{k}(x\;a_{\ell})\bigg](y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1}).

Proof.

Let
\tau=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})\in S_{n}
be a
k
-cycle. We then define

(2.3)

\overline{\tau}_{k}=\prod_{\ell=2}^{k}(x\;a_{\ell}).

We claim that

(2.4)

\tau_{k}^{-1}=(x\;y)\overline{\tau}_{k}(y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1}),

or equivalently,

(2.5)

\overline{\tau}_{k}=(x\;y)\tau_{k}^{-1}(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1}).

We will prove this using induction on
\overline{\tau}_{k}
. For our base case, in which two people’s minds are cycled, we have

(2.6)

\tau_{2}=(a_{1}\;a_{2}).

By inspection, we see that

(2.7)

\tau^{-1}_{2}=(x\;y)(x\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1}).

We verify that
\overline{\tau}_{2}
has the desired form by rewriting
\tau^{-1}_{2}
as follows:

(2.8)

\tau^{-1}_{2}=(x\;y)\overline{\tau}_{2}(y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1}),

thus obtaining our desired form,

(2.9)

\overline{\tau}_{2}=(x\;y)\tau^{-1}_{2}(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1}).

We assume inductively that
\overline{\tau}_{k}=(x\;y)\tau^{-1}_{k}(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1})
holds for some
k\geq 2
. Let
\tau_{k+1}=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
be a cycle of length
k+1
.

(2.10)

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{\ell=2}^{k+1}(x\;a_{\ell})\bigg]

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{\ell=2}^{k}(x\;a_{\ell})\bigg](x\;a_{k+1})

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\overline{\tau}_{k}(x\;a_{k+1}).

By our inductive hypothesis,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\tau_{k}^{-1}(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1})(x\;a_{k+1})

(2.14)

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\tau_{k}^{-1}(x\;a_{1})(x\;a_{k+1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1})

(2.15)

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\tau_{k}^{-1}(a_{1}\;a_{k+1})(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1}).

We know that
\tau_{k}^{-1}=(a_{k}\;a_{k-1}\cdots a_{1})
, so
\tau_{k}^{-1}(a_{1}\;a_{k+1})=\tau_{k+1}^{-1}
. Thus, we have

(2.16)

\overline{\tau}_{k+1}=(x\;y)\tau_{k+1}^{-1}(x\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(y\;a_{1}),

giving us our desired result.
∎

Remark 2.17
.

The permutation
\tau^{-1}
can be expressed as a product of
k+3
distinct transpositions.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\tau=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
be an arbitrary
k
-cycle in
S_{n}
. Then we define

(2.19)

\widehat{\tau}:=\overline{\tau}(y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1})=\bigg[\prod_{\ell=2}^{k}(x\;a_{\ell})\bigg](y\;a_{1})(y\;a_{2})(x\;a_{1}).

Remark 2.20
.

We note that can use
\widehat{\tau}
to rewrite the result from Theorem
2.1
more concisely, which will make subsequent abstraction of the problem clearer:
given a
k
-cycle
\tau=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})\in S_{n}
, its inverse can be written in

as

\tau^{-1}=(x\;y)\widehat{\tau}.

We can rewrite this as
\widehat{\tau}=(x\;y)\tau^{-1}
, allowing us to derive the following identity:

(2.21)

\widehat{\tau}\tau=(x\;y)\tau^{-1}\tau=(x\;y).

We now look at the case where we have a general permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
which is not necessarily a single cycle.

Theorem 2.22
.

Given
\sigma\in S_{n}
,
\sigma^{-1}
can be written as a product of distinct transpositions in
S_{n+2}\setminus S_{n}
. Specifically, if

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{j}
for disjoint cycles
\tau_{i}\in S_{n}
with
1\leq i\leq j
, then
\sigma^{-1}
can be written in the form

\sigma^{-1}=\begin{cases}(xy)\widehat{\tau}_{j}\cdots\widehat{\tau}_{1}&\text{if $j$ is odd}\\
\widehat{\tau}_{j}\cdots\widehat{\tau}_{1}&\text{if $j$ is even}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Theorem
1.8
tells us that there exists
j\geq 1
such that
\sigma
can be written as a product of
j
disjoint cycles:

(2.23)

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\tau_{i}.

We first have the case when
j
is odd.
Consider

(2.24)

\gamma=(x\;y)\widehat{\tau}_{j}\cdots\widehat{\tau}_{1}=(x\;y)\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}.

We then have

(2.25)

\displaystyle\gamma\sigma

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\;\prod_{i=1}^{j}\tau_{i}

(2.26)

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg]\widehat{\tau}_{1}\tau_{1}\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg]

(2.27)

\displaystyle=(x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg](x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg].

Because
\tau_{i}\in S_{n}
for all
i\geq 1
,
\tau_{i}
does not contain the elements
x\text{ or }y
at any point. This means that
\tau_{i}\text{ and }(x\;y)
are disjoint, so we can commute them:

(2.28)

\gamma\sigma=(x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg]\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg](x\;y).

We then repeat this process, cancelling each
\widehat{\tau}_{i}
with
\tau_{i}
to get
(x\;y)
, then commuting
(x\;y)
to the far right of the equation. We will do this
j
times, once for each
\tau_{i}
, leaving us with

(2.29)

\displaystyle\gamma\sigma

\displaystyle=(x\;y)(x\;y)^{j}

(2.30)

\displaystyle=(x\;y)^{j+1}

(2.31)

\displaystyle=\left((x\;y)^{2}\right)^{(j+1)/2}

(2.32)

\displaystyle=(e)^{(j+1)/2}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=e

So, for all
j\geq 1
with
j

\gamma=(x\;y)\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}=\sigma^{-1}.

We now look at the case where
j
is even. We still have
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\tau_{i}
. Consider the product

(2.34)

\theta=\widehat{\tau}_{1}\cdots\widehat{\tau}_{j}=\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}.

We then have

(2.35)

\displaystyle\theta\sigma

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\tau_{i}

(2.36)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg]\widehat{\tau}_{1}\tau_{1}\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg]

(2.37)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg](x\;y)\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg]

(2.38)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{i=0}^{j-2}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}\bigg]\bigg[\prod_{i=2}^{j}\tau_{i}\bigg](x\;y),

where we’ve used the same logic as in the odd case to commute
(x\;y)
. We repeat this process
j-1
more times, once for each
\tau_{i}
remaining in the right product. This gives us x

(2.39)

\displaystyle\theta\sigma

\displaystyle=(x\;y)^{j}

(2.40)

\displaystyle=\left((x\;y)^{2}\right)^{j/2}

(2.41)

\displaystyle=\left(e\right)^{j/2}

(2.42)

Thus, we have

\theta=\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\widehat{\tau}_{j-i}=\widehat{\tau}_{j}\cdots\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\sigma^{-1}
,
as desired.

3.
Machines of Larger Sizes

Now suppose the machine swaps
m
people at at time, cyclically. That is,

1\to 2\to 3\to\cdots m\to 1

To extend the rules from Keeler’s original problem, we will assume that the same group of people cannot use the machine more than once but that individually, each person can use the machine multiple times. Reframed in terms of permutations, we have our main result for this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 3
,
n\geq m
. Let
H_{m}\subseteq S_{n}
denote the subgroup generated by all
m
-cycles. If
\sigma\in H_{m}
, then there exist
\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r}
such that

(1)

\mu_{i}\in S_{n+d}\setminus S_{n}
, where

d=\begin{cases}(m-2)&\text{if }m\text{ is odd}\\
3(\frac{m}{2}-1)&\text{if }m\text{ is even}\end{cases}

(2)

\sigma^{-1}=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}
, where
\mu_{i}
is an
m
-cycle permuting a distinct set of
m
elements for all
1\leq i\leq r
.

We will refer to
S_{n}
as the group that permutes
A=\{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\}
and
S_{n+d}
as the group that permutes
A\cup\{x_{1}\cdots x_{d}\}
. So an
m
-cycle
\mu\in S_{n+d}\setminus S_{n}
is a permutation that permutes at least one element in
\{x_{1}\cdots x_{d}\}
.
The part of this theorem that distinguishes our problem from Elder’s is (2). As an example:

(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;a_{3})^{-1}=(a_{1}\;a_{3}\;a_{2})=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;a_{3}\;x_{2}\;x_{1})(a_{3}\;a_{1}\;a_{2}\;x_{1}\;x_{2})

is a perfectly acceptable solution under Elder’s variation as

\langle(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;a_{3}\;x_{2}\;x_{1})\rangle\neq\langle(a_{3}\;a_{1}\;a_{2}\;x_{1}\;x_{2})\rangle.

But for our purposes we cannot use both of these cycles, as each cycle permutes the same 5 elements. It is worthwhile to note that any solution under our restriction can be used under Elder’s restriction (perhaps not as optimally). The case in which
\sigma
is a
3
-cycle is of particular interest to us. We will prove this case in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 3
,
n\geq m
. If
\tau\in S_{n}
is a
3
-cycle, then there exist
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
where
\mu_{i}\in S_{n+(m-2)}\setminus S_{n}

4.
Optimality for 3-machines

In our context, an optimal solution is defined to have the least possible number of machine uses. Evans et al. previously explored the problem of optimizing the solution to Keeler’s Theorem using the 2-machine
[
5
]
. Keeler’s original 2-machine solution has been shown to be optimal only when the number of disjoint cycles
r
that make up the original permutation is equal to 1 or 2. Evans et al. found an optimal solution for the case in which
r\geq 3

[
5
]
. We first expand this question by looking at the optimal solution for 3-machines.

Definition 4.1
.

For the rest of this section, we define

\varphi=\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\}
to be the set of elements that
S_{n}
acts on. Specifically, if
S
denotes the symmetric group, we have
S_{n}=S(\varphi)
.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

We denote the set of
d
outsiders as
X=\{x_{1},\cdots x_{d}\}
. We then define
\varphi\cup X
to be the set of elements that
A_{n+d}
acts on
(in other words
A_{n+d}=A(\varphi\cup X)
).
∎

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
be written as a product of disjoint cycles

\alpha=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where each
\tau_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq r
has length
k_{i}\geq 2
. Then,

(4.4)

C(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{j}\in\varphi\\
\tau_{i}(a_{j})\neq a_{j}\end{subarray}}1.

In words,
C(\alpha)
is the number of times that any element from
\varphi
shows up in a
\tau_{i}
cycle.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\beta
be a product of 3-cycles in
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
such that each 3-cycle contains
x
. Then the number of 3-cycles composing
\beta
is
\frac{C(\beta)}{2}
.

Proof.

Every 3-cycle is in
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
and contains
x
. This means that exactly 2 elements of any given cycle in
\beta
must be in
\varphi
. Thus,
C(\beta)
is two times the number of cycles, giving the number of cycles as
C(\beta)/2
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Let
\sigma\in A_{n}
be written as a product of
r
disjoint cycles,

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where
\tau_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-cycle with
k_{i}\geq 2
. We will assume for all of this section that
k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}=n
. This assumption does not present an isuse, because if
k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}=\ell<n
, there are
n-\ell
objects that remain unpermuted by
\sigma
and thus don’t need fixing, while the
\ell
elements that are permuted can be fixed by mimicking the following arguments with
n
replaced by
\ell

[
5
]
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\sigma\in A_{n}
be written as a product of
r
disjoint cycles,

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where
\tau_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-cycle with
k_{i}\geq 2
and

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}=n
. Consider outsider
x
which
S_{n+1}
acts on. We can write
\sigma^{-1}\in S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
as a product of
{(n+r)\over 2}
3-cycles, each of which contains
x
. Further, this solution is optimal in that any construction of
\sigma^{-1}
must have at least
{(n+r)\over 2}
steps.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, with each
\tau_{i}
a disjoint cycle.

We rewrite the equation as
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{q}\tau_{q+1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, with
\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{q}
representing cycles of odd length and
\tau_{q+1}\cdots\tau_{r}
representing cycles of even length. Even-length cycles have odd parity, and thus must occur in pairs. Corresponding to an arbitrary pair of even-length cycles
\tau_{v}=(b_{1}\cdots b_{k_{1}})
and
\tau_{w}=(c_{1}\cdots c_{k_{2}})
,
we define

G(\tau_{v},\tau_{w})=(c_{1}\;c_{k_{2}}\;x)\bigg[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{k_{2}-2}(c_{\ell+1}\;c_{\ell}\;x)\bigg](b_{1}\;b_{k_{1}}\;x)\bigg[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{k_{1}-2}(b_{\ell+1}\;b_{\ell}\;x)\bigg](c_{k_{2}}\;b_{k_{1}}\;x).

Corresponding to an arbitrary odd-length cycle
\tau_{u}=(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
, we define

F(\tau_{u})=\bigg[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{k-1}(a_{\ell+1}\;a_{\ell}\;x)\bigg](a_{2}\;a_{1}\;x).

Then we set

\sigma^{-1}=F(\tau_{1})\cdots F(\tau_{q})\cdot G(\tau_{q+1},\tau_{q+2})\cdots G(\tau_{r-1},\tau_{r}).

It can be verified that
\sigma^{-1}
is indeed the inverse of
\sigma
and that it is made up of
\frac{(n+r)}{2}
steps.

We will now prove that if
\sigma^{-1}\in S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
is written as a product of 3-cycles, it must have at least
\frac{(n+r)}{2}
elements of
\varphi
. By Lemma
4.5
, we just need to show that
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
.

We first note that every element of
\varphi
has had its mind permuted away from its body. This is true because we know that the lengths of the
\tau_{i}
cycles sum to
n
and that all
\tau_{i}
cycles are disjoint. In order to return each element’s mind to its body, the element must therefore be permuted again in the inverse, giving
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n
.

For the following argument, we also specify that every
\tau_{i}
cycle is written with
x
as the rightmost entry.

We will now show that every cycle
\tau_{i}
must have one element from
S_{n}
that shows up at least twice in
\sigma^{-1}
. Since there are
r
cycles, this will mean that
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
.

Observe cycle
\tau_{i}\in\sigma
for some
1\leq i\leq r
, and let
\gamma
be the set of elements of
\varphi
that are permuted by
\tau_{i}
. Next, find the right most element of
\gamma
in
\sigma^{-1}
, and call the cycle that this right-most element lies in
T
.

There are three cases for how
T
could look, which correspond to the (sum of binomial coefficients)
\binom{2}{1}+\binom{2}{2}
ways to have
x
and at least one
\gamma
element in
T
. First, it could look like

(a_{i,1}\;a_{i,2}\;x)
for some elements
a_{i,1},a_{i,2}\in\gamma
. It could also look like
(a_{i,3}\;u\;x)
for some
u
in a different
\tau
cycle and
a_{i,3}\in\gamma
. Finally, it could look like

(v\;a_{i,4}\;x)
for some
v
in a different
\tau
cycle and
a_{i,4}\in\gamma
.

For our first case where
T=(a_{i,1}\;a_{i,2}\;x)
, we note that
a_{i,1}
gets whatever mind was in
x
’s body. Because
T
is the farthest right (and thus chronologically first) cycle in
\sigma^{-1}
containing elements of
\gamma
,
x
must have its own mind or the mind of an element in
\varphi\setminus\gamma
. So,
a_{i,1}
gets a mind that is not from
\gamma
. This means that
a_{i,1}
definitely does not have its own mind at this point, so it must be used again in another 3-cycle in
\sigma^{-1}
to get its mind back.

We then look at the case that
T=(a_{i,3}\;u\;x)
. Notice that
a_{i,3}
gets the mind that was in
x
’s body. For the same reasoning as before,
a_{i,3}
must get used again in another
3
-cycle in
\sigma^{-1}
to get its mind back.

If
T=(v\;a_{i,4}\;x)
, then
a_{i,4}
gets the mind that
v
had. Since
T
is the farthest right 3-cycle in
\sigma^{-1}
containing elements of
\gamma
,
v
’s body must have had
x
’s mind or a mind from
\varphi\setminus\gamma
. So,
a_{i,4}
must be used at least twice in
\sigma^{-1}
.

We have shown that for an arbitrary cycle
\tau_{i}\in\sigma
, at least one element of
\tau_{i}
must show up twice in
\sigma^{-1}
. This is true for all
r
cycles in
\sigma
, so
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
. Lemma
4.5
tells us that
\sigma^{-1}
must have at least
\frac{(n+r)}{2}
steps, completing our proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be written as a product of
r
disjoint cycles as

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where
\tau_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-cycle with
k_{i}\geq 2
and
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}=n
. For

,
\sigma^{-1}\in S_{n+d}\setminus S_{n}
satisfies
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
.

Proof.

This is a generalization of the argument made in Theorem
4.7
, but with any number of outsiders.
For an arbitrary cycle
\tau_{i}\in\sigma
, define
T\text{ and }\gamma
as in Theorem
4.7
. There are two general cases: there is an element of
\gamma
in the far left position of
T
, or not. Assume that all 3-cycles in
\sigma^{-1}
are written with any outsiders as far right as possible.

If there is an element of
\gamma
in the far left position of
T
, then
T
looks like
(a_{i,1}\;b\;x_{j,1})
for
a_{i,1}\in\gamma
,
b
arbitrary, and
x_{j,1}\in\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}\}
. By construction,
x_{j,1}
does not contain a mind from
\gamma
. So,
a_{i,1}
gets a mind from outside of
\gamma
, which cannot be its own mind. This means
a_{i,1}
must be used at least twice in
\sigma^{-1}
, and since this was true for all cycles,
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
.

In the case that there is not an element of
\gamma
in the far left position of
T
, then
T
looks like
(c\;a_{i,2}\;x_{j,2})
for
c\notin\gamma\cup\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}\}
,
a_{i,2}\in\gamma
, and
x_{j,2}\in\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}\}
. By construction,
c
cannot contain a mind from
\gamma
. So,
a_{i,2}
gets this mind from outside of
\gamma
, again giving
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be written as a product of
r
disjoint cycles as

\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}
, where
\tau_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-cycle with
k_{i}\geq 2
and

. Then,
\sigma^{-1}\in S_{n+d}\setminus S_{n}
will take at least
\frac{(n+r)}{2}
steps for all

.

Proof.

Lemma
4.8
says that
\sigma^{-1}
must satisfy
C(\sigma^{-1})\geq n+r
. But, having at least one outsider per 3-cycle (regardless of which outsider it is) means that there can be at most 2 elements of
\varphi
in each 3-cycle. This means
\sigma^{-1}
must have at least
\frac{(n+r)}{2}
steps, as desired.
∎

5.
An Infinitely Large Machine

Here we present an infinite analogy of the machine. Consider a machine that takes a set of people enumerated by the natural numbers and that permutes their minds as follows.

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\to 2

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\to 3

This can be denoted as a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
f(n)=n+1
.

\displaystyle f=1\to 2\to 3\cdots

We extend the rules to this infinite case as follows.

(1)

Each seat must be occupied in order for the machine to work.

(2)

Once a collection of people have used the machine, that same collection cannot use the machine again.

This case differs since we are not working with bijective functions so there may be bodies that remain with no machine. Swaps that do this will be called
forgetful
. In the example above, no one will occupy body
1
. If the machine is only able to do forgetful swap, we would have no hope of fixing everyone as in every swap as someone will always remain mindless. Therefore, we give the machine another capability, to change direction in which minds are swapped:

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\to 1

\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\to 2

This can be denoted as a function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
g(n)=n-1
. Written in one line,

\displaystyle g=\cdots 3\to 2\to 1

it is more clear to see why we say the machine can ”change direction”. We will only use this kind of swapping when the first person (in this case body 1) has no mind occupying it (else body 1 would end up with two minds occupying it). We will call this type of swapping
retentive
, as in no bodies remain mindless. While the swapping above would solve our original problem, it is not
allowed under our rules as it swaps the same set of people. To summarize, here is an outline of our rules.

(1)

Each seat must be occupied in order for the machine to work.

(2)

Mindless bodies will remain on the machine.

(3)

Once a collection of people have used the machine, that same collection cannot use the machine again. Individuals can use the machine many times.

(4)

The machine can do
forgetful
swaps.

(5)

The machine can do
retentive
swaps.

We show the following results:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f_{0}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
f_{0}(n)=n+1
. Then there exist
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
, and
f_{3}
where
f_{1}
,
f_{3}
are retentive and
f_{2}
is forgetful, such that
dom(f_{i})\neq dom(f_{j})
for all
i\neq j
, and
f^{-1}_{0}=f_{3}\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Given a countably infinite set
X
, any permutation
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
and its inverse can be written as a composition of forgetful and retentive swaps each with unique domain.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set,
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
. Then there exist
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, which are forgetful and retentive respectively, such that
dom(f_{1})\neq dom(f_{2})
and
\sigma^{-1}=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

5.1.
Finite Disjoint Uses

In this case, we will suppose a countable set of people enumerated by
\mathbb{N}
have used the machine. The machine does the swap
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as
f(n)=n+1
. As in the finite case, we will introduce an outsider who has not used the machine. Let
z
denote this outsider. We can undo
f
in the following steps.

Step 1:

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\to 1

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to 2

\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle\to 3

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle\to 4

Step 2:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to 2

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\to 3

\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\to 4

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle\to 5

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle\to 6

Step 3:

\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle\to 3

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle\to 4

\displaystyle 6

\displaystyle\to 5

\displaystyle 7

\displaystyle\to 6

The original swap and map in step 1 can be defined as

(5.4)

\displaystyle f(x)=x+1\qquad f_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if $x=z$}\\
z&\text{ if $x=3$}\\
x-1&\text{ else}.\end{cases}

We can analogously define step 2 and step 3. The piecewise functions can be written more concisely using an extension of cycle notation. For example,

(5.5)

f=(1\;2\;3\;4\cdots)\qquad f_{1}=(\cdots 4\;3\;x\;2\;1).

In the cycle notation, each person has their mind sent to the person to their right. Notice that forgetful swaps trail off on the right while retentive ones trail off on the left. This notation makes the proof of the following lemma much easier to write:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f_{0}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
f_{0}(n)=n+1
. Then there exist
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
, and
f_{3}
where
f_{1}
,
f_{3}
are retentive and
f_{2}
is forgetful, such that
dom(f_{i})\neq dom(f_{j})
for all
i\neq j
, and
f^{-1}_{0}=f_{3}\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=(\cdots 4\;3\;x\;2\;1)

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=(x\;2\;3\;4\cdots)

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle=(\cdots 5\;4\;3\;x).

Then
dom(f_{0})=\mathbb{N}
,
dom(f_{1})=\mathbb{N}\cup\{z\}
,
dom(f_{2})=dom(f_{1})\setminus\{1\}
, and
dom(f_{3})=dom(f_{1})\setminus\{1,2\}
. By tracing elements, we see that
f^{-1}_{0}=f_{3}\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.
∎

We can generalize the swap as follows. Let
X
denote a countably infinite set of people, defined as
X=\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots\}
, that uses the machine. The machine does the swap defined by the function
f:X\to X
,
f(a_{n})=a_{n+1}
. Define

\widehat{f}=(\cdots a_{5}\;a_{4}\;a_{3}\;x)(x\;a_{2}\;a_{3}\;a_{4}\cdots)(\cdots a_{4}\;a_{3}\;x\;a_{2}\;a_{1})

and from the lemma, it is immediate to show
f^{-1}=\widehat{f}
.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be countably infinite, pairwise disjoint subsets of
X
where
X_{i}=\{x_{i,j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. Define
f:X\to X
by
f(x)=g_{1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{n}
where

g_{i}(x)=(x_{i,1}\;x_{i,2}\;x_{i,3}\cdots)=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if $x\notin X_{i}$}\\
x_{i,j+1}&\text{ if $x\in X_{i}$}\end{cases}

Then
f^{-1}=\widehat{g}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\widehat{g}_{1}
.

5.2.
Finitary Permutations

Let
X
be a countably infinite set, and
S(X)
the permutation group on
X
. Let
S_{fin}(X)
be the subgroup of
S(X)
consisting of permutations of
X
which fix all but finitely many elements of
X
. We call
\sigma\in S(X)
a
finitary
permutation if
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
. As it turns out, we can obtain any finitary permutation from swaps using the machine under our restrictions. We start with cycles.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set,
a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\in X
. Then there exists
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, forgetful and retentive respectfully, such that
dom(f_{1})\neq dom(f_{2})
and
(a_{1}\;\cdots\;a_{n})=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
f=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\cdots a_{n})
, then

f=(\cdots a_{n+2}\;a_{n+1}\;a_{n})(a_{1}\;a_{2}\cdots a_{n}\;a_{n+1}\cdots)

∎

Example 5.9
.

Let
f=(a_{1}\;a_{2})
and
z\notin X
. Then the transposition is solved by:

Step 1:

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle\to a_{2}

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to a_{3}

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle\to a_{4}

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle\to a_{5}

Step 2:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to a_{1}

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle\to a_{3}

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle\to a_{4}

\displaystyle a_{6}

\displaystyle\to a_{5}

In cycle notation,

(a_{1}\;a_{2})^{-1}=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{3}\;a_{4}\cdots)(\cdots a_{5}\;a_{4}\;a_{3}\ z\ a_{1})

∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set,
a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\in X
, and
z\notin X
. Then there exists
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, forgetful and retentive respectfully, such that
z\in dom(f_{1}),dom(f_{2})
,
dom(f_{1})\neq dom(f_{2})
, and
(a_{1}\;\cdots\;a_{n})^{-1}=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Proof.

(a_{1}\;a_{2}\cdots a_{k})^{-1}=(\cdots a_{k+2}\ a_{k+1}\ z\ a_{1})(a_{1}\;a_{k}\ a_{k-1}\cdots a_{3}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{k+1}\ a_{k+2}\cdots)

∎

Let
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
be the cycle
\sigma=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\cdots a_{k})
and define

\sigma^{*}=(\cdots a_{k+2}\ a_{k+1}\ z\ a_{1})(a_{1}\;a_{k}\;a_{k-1}\cdots a_{3}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{k+1}\;a_{k+2}\cdots)

Then from the above lemma,
\sigma^{*}=\sigma^{-1}
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Given a countably infinite set
X
, any permutation
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
and its inverse can be written as a composition of forgetful and retentive swaps each with unique domain.

Proof.

Since
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
, there exist disjoint cycles
\tau_{1},\cdots\tau_{m}\in S_{fin}(X)
, such that
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
. Then
\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{*}_{m}\cdots\tau^{*}_{1}
. Then we are done since the fact that each has a unique domain follows from the cycles being disjoint.
∎

Since each
\tau^{*}_{i}
uses two swaps on the machine, the total number of swaps needed to write
\sigma^{-1}
is
2m
. We can do better. Consider
\sigma=(a_{1}\;a_{2})(a_{3}\;a_{4}\;a_{5})
. Applying our solution from before we know
(a_{1}\;a_{2})^{-1}=(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{3}\;a_{4}\cdots)(\cdots a_{5}\;a_{4}\;a_{3}\;z\;a_{1})
. Examine the left permutation:
(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{3}\;a_{4}\;a_{5}\cdots).
For fixing
(a_{1}\;a_{2})
, the only part of this permutation that is important is
a_{1}\to a_{2}\to z
. So we can change it to
(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{5}\;a_{4}\;a_{3}\cdots)
. Notice this fixes
a_{4}
and
a_{3}
along with our intention to fix
a_{2}
. We can generalize this idea to obtain a much more efficient solution.

Example 5.12
.

\sigma=(a_{1}\;a_{2})(a_{3}\;a_{4}\;a_{5})

Let
z\notin X
. Then the problem is solved by:

Step 1:

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle\to a_{2}

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to a_{5}

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle\to a_{4}

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle\to a_{3}

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle\to a_{6}

\displaystyle a_{6}

\displaystyle\to a_{7}

Step 2:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to a_{1}

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle a_{6}

\displaystyle\to a_{5}

\displaystyle a_{7}

\displaystyle\to a_{6}

In cycle notation:

((a_{1}\;a_{2})(a_{3}\;a_{4}\;a_{5}))^{-1}=(\cdots a_{7}\;a_{6}\;a_{5}\;z\;a_{1})(a_{1}\;a_{2}\;z\;a_{5}\;a_{4}\;a_{3}\;a_{6}\;a_{7}\cdots)
.

∎

Generalizing this solution allows us to fix any finitary permutation in just 2 steps.
We create some notation for an arbitrary cycle:

Let
[a]_{k}:=a_{1}\ \cdots a_{k}
and
[a]^{-1}_{k}:=a_{k}\ \cdots a_{1}
we write

\displaystyle(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})=([a]_{k})

\displaystyle(a_{k}\cdots a_{1})=([a]^{-1}_{k})

Note that
([a]_{k})^{-1}=([a]^{-1}_{k})
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
X
be a countable infinite set,
\sigma\in S_{fin}(X)
. Then there exist
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, which are forgetful and retentive respectively, such that
dom(f_{1})\neq dom(f_{2})
and
\sigma^{-1}=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
be the cycle decomposition. Let
\tau_{i}=([a_{i}]_{k_{i}})
.
Then

\sigma=([a_{1}]_{k_{1}})([a_{2}]_{k_{2}})\cdots([a_{m}]_{k_{m}})

. And thus by tracing elements one can see:

\sigma^{-1}=(b_{3}\ b_{2}\ b_{1}\ a_{m,k_{m}}\cdots a_{2,k_{2}}\ x\ a_{1,k_{1}})([a_{1}]^{-1}_{k_{1}}\ x\ [a_{2}]^{-1}_{k_{2}}[a_{3}]^{-1}_{k_{3}}\cdots[a_{m}]^{-1}_{k_{m}}\ b_{1}\ b_{2}\ b_{3}\cdots)

where the
b_{j}\in X
not permuted by any
\tau_{i}
for any
i
.
∎

6.
Future Directions

In our finite case, we only proved optimally for when
n=3
so a natural extension is to prove optimally for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A noteworthy observation in the
3
cycle case, each product in the solution is a distinct
3
cycle. As an example, consider the solution for when the machine permutes
3
people.

(6.1)

(1\;2\;3)=(3\;1\;x_{1})(2\;3\;x_{1})(1\;2\;x_{1})

Since each
3
cycle is distinct, then they correspond to a unique triangle on the graph with vertices
\{1,2,3,x_{1}\}
. If we count all possible distinct triangles that have at least
x_{1}
in the graph, this places an lower bound for the the total number of moves required.
It would be interesting to see if these ideas carry over to more general cases.

In the infinite case we lost a group structure when we considered a machine that does
n+1
and
n-1
on
\mathbb{N}
. It would be interesting to explore the scenario with a group structure by considering a machine that does
n+1
and
n-1
on
\mathbb{Z}
. One could also look at a machine that preforms a different permutation on a countable set. For a countably infinite set
X
, one could consider a finite machine that only cycles
m
-people.
