On the Duflo-Serganova functor for the queer Lie superalgebra
M. Gorelik, A. Sherman

Abstract
. We study the Duflo-Serganova functor DS x \operatorname{DS}_{x} for the queer Lie superalgebra 𝔮 n \mathfrak{q}_{n} and for all odd x x with [ x , x ] [x,x] semisimple. For the case when the rank of x x is 1 1 we give a formula for multiplicities in terms of the arch diagram attached to λ \lambda . Further, we prove that DS x ⁡ ( L ) \operatorname{DS}_{x}(L) is semisimple if L L is a simple finite-dimensional module and x x is of rank 1 1 satisfying x 2 = 0 x^{2}=0 .

1.2.
Extension of the Duflo-Serganova functor

In this paper we study
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L)
for the case when
L=L_{\mathfrak{q}_{n}}(\lambda)
is a simple finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
-module. For
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
it is important to expand our use of the
\operatorname{DS}
-functor to odd elements
x
for which
[x,x]
is a semisimple element of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
; this is especially true because of the Cartan subalgebra admitting a nontrivial odd part.

Write
x^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}[x,x]\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. Recall that if
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
has that
x^{2}
acts semisimply on a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
, we may define the Lie superalgebra

M_{x}:=\frac{\ker(x:M^{x^{2}})}{\operatorname{Im}(x:M^{x^{2}})},

where
M^{x^{2}}
is the subspace of
M
killed by
x^{2}
. In particular if
\operatorname{ad}(x^{2})
acts semisimply, we obtain a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, and
M_{x}
will naturally be a module over
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
. Thus we will be interested primarily in the space:

\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}|\ \operatorname{ad}[x,x]\text{ is semisimple}\}.

1.2.1.
Remark

When
\mathfrak{g}
is a Kac-Moody Lie superalgebra, the usual definition of rank naturally extends to all elements in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
, and for these superalgebras the description of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)
remains independent of which element is chosen of a given rank, if
L(\lambda)
is finite-dimensional. However their actions on the larger category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules differs significantly.

1.3.
The case of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}_{n}
and arc diagrams

On the other hand, for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}_{n}
the behavior of the functor
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
on simple modules does not reduce to the case when
x^{2}=0
; in fact an element
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
has rank valued in
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, whereas if
x^{2}=0
we must have
\operatorname{rank}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
. Further, there are infinitely many
G_{0}
-orbits on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
in contrast to the self-commuting cone which has only finitely many orbits. We as yet do not understand
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
on simple modules for an arbitrary
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
; however we know what the possible simple constituents are, and in certain cases we know exactly how the functor behaves.

In order to study the simple constituents of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)
, we introduce arc diagrams that have similarities to those used for
\mathfrak{gl},\mathfrak{osp}
, and
\mathfrak{p}
. In these arc diagrams there are
full arcs
, illustrated with solid lines, and
half-arcs
, illustrated with dotted lines. The half arcs are exactly those emanating from 0. Each dominant weight
\lambda
has an associated arc diagram; for instance if
\lambda=7\epsilon_{1}+4\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}-\epsilon_{6}-7\epsilon_{7}
then
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
is given by:

\wedge
\wedge
\times
>
\times

In the above picture, the symbols
\wedge
lie at 0, and all other symbols lie at positions of
\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

Write
P^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
for the dominant weights of a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. The main theorem is as follows:

1.4.

Theorem.

Take

(ii)

The following are equivalent

\operatorname{smult}(\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda),L(\nu))\not=0

1.7.
Grading

Given an element
x\in(\mathfrak{q}_{n})_{\overline{1}}
with
x^{2}=0
, we may find a semisimple element

satisfying
[h,x]=x
. In this way we obtain an action of
h
on
DS_{x}
which commutes with
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, and thus induces a grading on the
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module

for a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
-module

(details explained in Sections
3.5
and

). Thus

will act by a scalar on each composition factor of

. Further it allows us to view
DS_{x}
as a functor from
\mathfrak{g}
-modules to
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-modules.

If
x
is of rank 1 and
L
is a finite-dimensional simple module, we have computed explicitly the eigenvalues of
h
on the composition factors of
DS_{x}L
, and we obtain the following:

1.8.

Theorem.

If
x
is of rank
1
and
L
is a simple finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
-module, then
DS_{x}L
is a semisimple, multiplicity-free
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-module.

We note that for a finite-dimensional Kac-Moody Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
we also always have such an element

which will acts on

and commutes with
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, giving a grading. We expect that in these cases we also have that if
L
is a simple finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}

will be multiplicity-free as a module over

1.9.
Projectivity

In Corollary

we show that if

1.11.
Outlook for higher rank elements

One of the central unanswered questions that we leave to future work is the computation of composition factors and multiplicities of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))
for
x
of rank bigger than
1
. Unlike in the Kac-Moody cases, where one only has to look at those
x
with
[x,x]=0
(see Remark
1.2.1
), for
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
(as previously mentioned) we may obtain very different results for elements in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
lying in distinct
G_{0}=GL(n)
-orbits (see Section
3.1
for a description of these orbits).

The
G_{0}
-orbits are parametrized by two pieces of data: first is the rank, a half-integer
r
. Once we fix a rank
r
,
the orbits become parametrized by
2r
unordered complex numbers, where an even number of them are 0 (they are given by the eigenvalues of the matrix
B
in
1.13
). This space is an open subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{2r}/S_{2r}
. It follows that the behavior of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
for
x
of rank
r
will be determined by values of symmetric functions on the
2r
complex numbers; or more precisely, due to the canonical equality
\operatorname{DS}_{cx}=\operatorname{DS}_{x}
for all
c\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the behavior of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
should be determined by the
zero sets
of homogeneous symmetric functions on the
2r
complex numbers; for an example see the computation of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
below.

\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
consisting of the matrices with the block form

T_{A,B}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix}

One has
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
. The group
GL_{n}
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
by the inner
automorphisms;
all triangular decompositions of
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
are
GL_{n}
-conjugated.
We denote by
\mathfrak{t}
the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}

spanned by the elements
h_{i}=T_{E_{ii},0}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Let
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}

be the basis dual to
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The algebra
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{\mathfrak{t}}
is
a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
; one has
\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{0}}=\mathfrak{t}
.
The elements
H_{i}:=T_{0,E_{ii}}
form a basis of
\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}
; one has

[H_{i},H_{j}]=2\delta_{ij}h_{i}
.

We fix a usual triangular decomposition:

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
,
where
\Delta^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
.

We write
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}

as
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and set

\operatorname{zero}(\lambda):=\#\{i|\ \lambda_{i}=0\},\ \ \ \ \operatorname{nonzero}(\lambda):=\#\{i|\ \lambda_{i}\not=0\}.

2.2.
Modules
C_{\lambda}
and
L(\lambda)

The algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})
can be naturally viewed as a Clifford algebra over the polynomial algebra
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{t})

with the symmetric bilinear form

B:\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}\otimes\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{t})\ \ \text{ with }\ \ \ B(H,H^{\prime})=[H,H^{\prime}].

For each
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}

the evaluation of
B
gives the symmetric form

B_{\lambda}:(H,H^{\prime})\mapsto\lambda([H,H^{\prime}])
which defines the Clifford algebra

\mathcal{C}\ell(\lambda):=\mathcal{C}\ell(\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}},B_{\lambda})=\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})/\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})I(\lambda)

where

I(\lambda)
stands for the kernel
of the algebra homomorphism
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{t})\to\mathbb{C}
induced by
\lambda
.
Since
(H_{i},H_{j})=2\delta_{ij}h_{i}
one has

\operatorname{rank}B_{\lambda}=\operatorname{nonzero}(\lambda).

If
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)=0
, then each finite-dimensional

\mathcal{C}\ell(\lambda)
-module is projective.

The algebra
\mathcal{C}\ell(\lambda)
admits a unique simple module
C_{\lambda}
(up to isomorphism
and parity change). One has

\dim C_{\lambda}=2^{[\frac{\operatorname{rank}B_{\lambda}+1}{2}]}

(and
\operatorname{sdim}C_{\lambda}=0
for
\lambda\not=0
).

We denote by
L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
a simple
\mathfrak{g}
-module of the highest weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
;
this module is a unique simple quotient of
\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{b}}C_{\lambda}
, where

C_{\lambda}
is viewed as a
\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{n}^{+}
-module with the zero action of
\mathfrak{n}^{+}
.
One has
L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)_{\lambda}=C_{\lambda}
. We will often omit the index
\mathfrak{g}

(resp.,
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
) in the notation
L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
(resp.,
L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\nu)
.

2.3.
Core

The weight
\lambda
is typical if
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}\not=0

for all
i,j
; in particular we require that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. We define
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda
to be
the number of nonzero disjoint pairs
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}=0

for
i\not=j
, plus
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)/2
. For example, if
\lambda=(2,1,0,0,0,-2,-3)
, then
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda=5/2
.

Let
\operatorname{core}\lambda
be
the set obtained from
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
by erasing all zeroes from
\lambda
and all pairs

\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}
with
i\not=j,\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}=0
.
For instance
\operatorname{core}(3,2,0,0,0,-2,-2)=\{3,-2\}
and this weight has atypicality

5/2
. We say that
\lambda
is
core-free
if
\operatorname{core}(\lambda)=\emptyset
.

We denote by
\chi_{\lambda}
the central character of
L(\lambda)
.
By
[
28
]
,

\chi_{\lambda}=\chi_{\nu}

if and only if
\operatorname{core}\lambda=\operatorname{core}\nu
. In particular, the core-free weights are
the weights with the central character equal to
\chi_{0}
.
We set

\operatorname{atyp}\chi_{\lambda}:=\operatorname{atyp}\lambda
.

2.4.
Subalgebras
\mathfrak{q}(J)

Set
I_{n}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For each
J\subset I_{n}
we denote by
\mathfrak{h}(J)
(resp.,
\mathfrak{t}(J)
) the span
of
H_{i},h_{i}
(resp., of
h_{i}
) with
i\in J
and set

\Delta(J):=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}|\ i,j\in J,\ i\not=j\}.

We denote by
\mathfrak{q}(J)
the subalgebra spanned by

\mathfrak{h}(J)
and
\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta(J)}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{q}(J)\cong\mathfrak{q}_{k}
, where
k
is the cardinality of

J
; we set

\mathfrak{h}_{x}:=\mathfrak{h}(I_{n}\setminus J)

and
\mathfrak{t}_{x}:=(\mathfrak{h}_{x})_{\overline{0}}
(
\mathfrak{t}_{x}
is spanned by

h_{i}
with
i\in I_{n}\setminus J
). The triangular decomposition of

\mathfrak{g}
induces a triangular
decomposition of
\mathfrak{q}(J)
with
\Delta(\mathfrak{q}(J))^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}|\ i<j,\ i,j\in J\}
. We will use analogous notation
\mathfrak{sq}(J),\mathfrak{pq}(J),\mathfrak{psq}(J)
.

\operatorname{DS}
-functor in

The

[

]

[
15

A study of the

3.1.
Representatives of
G_{0}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}

Retain notation of
2.4
.
Recall that the action of
G_{0}=GL(n)
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
is given by the adjoint action on
n\times n
-matrices. Therefore, finding the
G_{0}
-orbits on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
is equivalent to finding the
GL(n)
orbits on square-semisimple
n\times n
matrices. We give below (non-unique) representatives of these orbits which are easy to work with for our purposes.
Choose a subset
J:=\{i_{p}\}_{p=1}^{r}\subset I_{n}
. Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\leq r/2
, and for
p=1,\ldots,s
fix
a non-zero odd element
x_{p}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{i_{2p-1}}-\varepsilon_{i_{2p}}}
. Further, let
c_{2s+1},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and set

(1)

x=x_{nil}+x_{ss}:=\sum_{p=1}^{s}x_{p}+\sum\limits_{j=2s+1}^{r}c_{j}H_{i_{j}}

Then
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
and
x
has rank
r/2
.

We will always choose
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
of the above form and we will use
the above identification of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
with
\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)
. Further we will say in this case that
x
corresponds to the set
J
. In Section
3.4
we will show in what sense
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
is independent of the subset
J
.

The following lemma is an immediate consequence of Lemma
7.2.1
.

3.1.1.

Lemma.

Let
x=x_{ss}+x_{nil}
, and write
\overline{x}_{ss}
(resp.,
\overline{x}_{nil}
) for the image of
x_{ss}
(resp.
x_{nil}
) in
\mathfrak{g}_{x_{nil}}
(resp.,
\mathfrak{g}_{x_{ss}}
). Let
N
a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module with semisimple action of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. Then

\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\leq\min\bigg(\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{ss}}\circ\operatorname{DS}_{x_{nil}}(N),\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{nil}}\circ\operatorname{DS}_{x_{ss}}(N)\bigg).

Further, if
x_{nil}
is of rank
s
and we write
x_{nil}=x_{1}+\dots+x_{s}
, where each
x_{i}
is a rank
1
nilpotent operator with
[x_{i},x_{j}]=[x_{i},x_{ss}]=0
, then for each

we have

\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\leq\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{1}}\cdots\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{r}}\circ\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{ss}}\circ\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{r+1}}\circ\cdots\circ\operatorname{DS}_{x_{s}}(N),

where
\overline{x_{i}}
denotes the projection to the appropriate subquotient.

3.2.

Proposition.

Take
x
of rank
r/2
corresponding to
J\subseteq I_{n}
as in
3.1
.

(i)

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
can be identified with

\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)\cong\mathfrak{q}_{n-r}
;

(ii)

\operatorname{DS}_{x}
maps the standard
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
-module,
L(\varepsilon_{1})
, to the
standard
\mathfrak{q}_{n-r}
-module;

(iii)

if
r<n
, then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{sq}_{n})
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{pq}_{n})
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
can be identified with

\mathfrak{sq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
,
\mathfrak{pq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
,
\mathfrak{psq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
respectively;

(iv)

if
r=n
, then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{sq}_{n})\cong\mathbb{C}
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{pq}_{n})\cong\Pi\mathbb{C}
;

(v)

if
r=n>1
, then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
is a commutative
(1|1)
-dimensional Lie superalgebra
if
x=x_{nil}
or if
\sum c_{i}^{-1}=\sum c_{i}^{-3}=0
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})\cong\mathfrak{q}_{1}

if
\sum c_{i}^{-1}=0\not=\sum c_{i}^{-3}
and

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})=0
if
\sum c_{i}^{-1}\not=0
.

Proof.

All formulae can be easily checked in the following cases:

x=x_{ss}=\sum_{p=1}^{r}c_{i}H_{i}
or

x^{2}=0
and
\operatorname{rank}x=1
(in this case
J=\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
).
Now consider the general case. It is easy to see that

[x,\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)]=0
and that
[x,\mathfrak{q}_{n}]\cap\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)=0
. Therefore

\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)
with a subalgebra of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
.
Similarly, the standard
\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)
-module is a submodule
of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\varepsilon_{1}))
and, if
r<n
, then
\mathfrak{sq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
,
\mathfrak{pq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
,
\mathfrak{psq}(I_{n}\setminus J)
are subalgebras of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{sq}_{n})
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{pq}_{n})
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
respectively.

Using Lemma
3.1.1
we obtain

(2)

\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\leq\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{1}}\circ\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}_{p}}\bigl(\operatorname{DS}_{x_{ss}}(N)\bigr)

for any finite-dimensional module
N
. Using the cases
x=x_{ss}
and
\operatorname{rank}x=1

we get
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{q}_{n}))\leq\dim\mathfrak{q}(I_{n}\setminus J)
and the similar inequalities
for other cases. This establishes (i), (ii), (iii).

Consider the remaining case
\operatorname{rank}x=n/2
.
By (i),
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})=0
.
Using Hinich’s lemma we get (iv).

For (v) we have
J=\{1,\ldots,n\}
; thus
x
takes the form

x:=\sum_{p=1}^{s}x_{p}+\sum_{j=2s+1}^{n}c_{j}H_{j},

with
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
x_{p}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{2p-1}-\varepsilon_{2p}}
. We set

\ \ y:=\sum_{p=1}^{s}y_{p}+\sum_{p=2s+1}^{n}c_{i}^{-1}H_{i_{p}},

where
y_{p}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{2p}-\varepsilon_{2p-1}}
is an odd element satisfying
[x_{p},y_{p}]=2h_{2p-1}+2h_{2p}
. In particular, we have
[x,y]=2T_{\operatorname{id},0}
.

Applying Hinich’s Lemma to the short exact sequence

0\to\mathbb{C}T_{\operatorname{Id},0}\to\mathfrak{sq}_{n}\to\mathfrak{psq}_{n}\to 0

and using
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{sq}_{n})=\mathbb{C}

we obtain a long exact sequence

(3)

0\to E\to\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})\to\Pi E\to 0

where
E:=\mathbb{C}T_{\operatorname{Id},0}\cap[x,\mathfrak{sq}_{n}]
. It is easy to see that

E=\mathbb{C}T_{\operatorname{Id},0}
if
y\in\mathfrak{sq}_{n}

and
E=0
otherwise. This gives
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})=(1|1)

if
x=x_{nil}
or if
\sum c_{i}^{-1}=0
, and

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})=0
if
\sum c_{i}^{-1}\not=0
.

Consider the case when
x=x_{nil}
or
\sum c_{j}^{-1}=0
, so that
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})=(1|1)
.
By (
3
),

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})_{\overline{0}}
is an ideal in
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
, so

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
is either commutative or isomorphic to
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
.
It is easy to see that

y
has a non-zero image in
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
; we denote this image by
\overline{y}
.
Then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\mathfrak{psq}_{n})
is commutative if and only if
\overline{y}^{2}=0
that is

y^{2}\in\mathbb{C}T_{\operatorname{Id},0}+[\mathfrak{sq}_{n},x]
.

If
x=x_{nil}
, then
y^{2}=0
.
In the remaining case
\sum c_{j}^{-1}=0
and
y^{2}=\sum_{j=2s+1}^{n}c_{j}^{-2}h_{j}
. The elements in
[\mathfrak{sq}_{n},x]\cap\mathfrak{t}

are of the form
\sum d_{i}h_{i}
, where

d_{2p-1}=d_{2p}
for
p=1,\ldots,s
and
\sum_{j=2s+1}^{n}c_{j}^{-1}d_{j}=0
.
Therefore
y^{2}\in\mathbb{C}T_{\operatorname{Id},0}+[\mathfrak{sq}_{n},x]
is equivalent to
the existence of
d\in\mathbb{C}
and
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
for
j=2s+1,\ldots,n
such that

c_{j}z_{j}=c_{j}^{-2}+d
for all
j
and
\sum z_{j}=0
.
Since
\sum c_{j}^{-1}=0
, the last formula is equivalent to
\sum c_{j}^{-3}=0

This completes the proof.
∎

.

4.
Dominant weights and arc diagrams

In this paper we study the action of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
on finite-dimensional simple modules.
We denote by
P^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
the set of dominant weights, i.e.

P^{+}(\mathfrak{g}):=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}|\ \dim L(\lambda)<\infty\}.

By
[
22
]
,
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
if and only if

\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
and
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}

implies
\lambda_{i}=0
. We call weight
\lambda

integral
(resp.,
half-integral
)
if
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
(resp.,
\lambda_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
) for all
i
. If
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
is atypical, then
\lambda
is either integral or half-integral. By
3.3.2
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)=0
for each typical module
N

and
x\not=0
. Without loss of generality we assume

x\not=0
and

\lambda
is integral or half-integral
.

By Proposition
3.4.1
,
for half-integral weights it is only interesting to consider those
x
with
x^{2}=0
.

4.1.
Weight diagrams

Weight diagrams
were first defined in
[
4
]
for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. The conventions on how to draw these weight diagrams differ; we follow essentially
[
18
]
.

For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
we
construct the weight diagram
\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)
as follows:

—

for
s\not=0

we put
>
(resp.,
<
) at the position
s
if
there exists
j
with
\lambda_{j}=s
(resp.,
\lambda_{j}=-s
);

—

we write
\times
if
the position
s\neq 0
contains
>
and
<
;

—

if
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)\not=0
,
we put
\wedge^{r}
, where
r=\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)
;

—

if
\lambda
is integral (resp., half-integral)
we put the empty
sign
\circ
at each non-occupied position with the coordinate
in
\mathbb{N}
(resp., in
\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{N}
).

If
\lambda
is integral,
we draw the diagram from the zero position, and for
\lambda
half-integral we draw from the
\frac{1}{2}
position. For instance,

\begin{array}[]{lll}(4,1,-1,-3,-4)&&\ \circ\times\circ<\times\circ\circ\ldots\\
(5,2,0,0,0,0,0,-2-,-3)&&\wedge^{5}\ \circ\times<\circ>\circ\ldots\\
(\frac{5}{2},\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{5}{2})&&\times\circ\times\circ\ldots\end{array}

where
\ldots
stands for the infinite sequence of the empty symbols
\circ
.

We obtain a one-to-one correspondence between integral (resp., half-integral)
dominant weights
and the diagrams containing
n
symbols
>,<,\wedge
(where
\times
considered as
the union of
<
and
>
), where each non-zero position contains
exactly one of the symbols
>,<,\times
or
\circ
and the zero position
contains
\wedge^{r}
or
\circ
. Note that
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda
is equal to the number of
\times
s in
\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)
plus half the number of symbols
\wedge
at 0. We roughly think of the symbol
\wedge
as half of a symbol
\times
.

For a weight diagram
f
denote by
f(i)
the symbols at the
i
th position.

4.1.1.
Core diagrams

The symbols
>,<
are called
core symbols
.

A
core diagram
is a weight diagram which does not contain symbols
\times
and
\wedge
.
The core diagram
of
\lambda
is obtained from the weight diagram of
\lambda

by erasing all
\times
and
\wedge
symbols.

We say that a
\mathfrak{g}
-central character
\chi
is dominant if
there exists a finite-dimensional module with this central character.
By above, the central characters are parametrized by
\operatorname{core}(\lambda)
, so
the dominant central characters of atypicality
k>0

are parametrized by the core diagrams with

n-2k
non-empty symbols. For instance, the central character of the maximal atypicality corresponds to the
empty core diagram; for
\mathfrak{q}_{2}
the diagrams of the weights with such central character are
\wedge^{2}
,
\circ\times
,
\circ\circ\times
and so on; for
\mathfrak{q}_{3}
these diagrams are
\wedge^{3}\circ
,
\wedge\times
,
\wedge\circ\times
and so on.

For a core diagram
f
we denote by
\chi(f)
the corresponding central character.

4.1.2.
Core-free diagrams

Note that
\lambda
is core-free if and only if
the weight diagram of
\lambda
does not have core symbols. We assign to each diagram
f
a core-free diagram
\operatorname{howl}(f)

which is obtained from
f
by erasing all core symbols. For instance,

\begin{array}[]{lllll}f=\wedge^{4}>\circ\times<\times\circ\ldots&&\operatorname{core}(f)=\circ>\circ\circ<\circ\ldots&&\operatorname{howl}(f)=\wedge^{4}\circ\times\times\circ\ldots\\
g=\wedge^{3}\circ\times<\times\circ\ldots&&\operatorname{core}(g)=\circ\circ\circ<\circ\ldots&&\operatorname{howl}(g)=\wedge^{3}\circ\times\times\ldots\end{array}

For an atypical dominant weight
\lambda
we denote by
\operatorname{howl}(\lambda)
the weight corresponding to the diagram
\operatorname{howl}(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda))
.

4.2.
Partial order

We will consider the standard partial order
on
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
:

\lambda>\nu\ \text{ if }\lambda-\nu\in\mathbb{N}\Delta^{+}.

4.2.1.

Lemma.

For atypical weights
\eta,\mu\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
one has

\operatorname{core}(\eta)=\operatorname{core}(\mu)\ \ \&\ \ \eta>\mu\ \ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \ \operatorname{howl}(\eta)\not\leq\operatorname{howl}(\mu).

Proof.

Let
f,g
be weight diagrams of atypicality
p>0

with
\operatorname{core}f=\operatorname{core}g
,
and let

a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\ldots\geq a_{p}

(resp.,
b_{1}\geq b_{2}\geq\ldots\geq b_{p}
) be
the coordinates of the symbols

\times
in
f
(resp., in
g
).
We write
g\succ f
if
b_{j}>a_{j}
for some
j
and

a_{i}=b_{i}
for each
i<j
. For example

\wedge>\circ\times\times\ \ \succ\ \ \wedge^{3}>\circ\circ\times.

It is easy to see that for atypical weights
\mu,\eta\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
with

\operatorname{core}(\mu)=\operatorname{core}(\eta)
one has

\begin{array}[]{l}\eta>\mu\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \operatorname{diag}(\eta)\succ\operatorname{diag}(\mu)\\
\operatorname{diag}(\eta)\succ\operatorname{diag}(\mu)\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{howl}(\eta))\succ\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{howl}(\mu)).\end{array}

If
\eta>\mu
and
\operatorname{howl}(\eta)\leq\operatorname{howl}(\mu)
, then

\operatorname{diag}(\eta)\succ\operatorname{diag}(\mu)
and
\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{howl}(\mu))\succ\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{howl}(\eta))
,
a contradiction.
∎

4.2.2.
Remark

Note that

\operatorname{core}(\eta)=\operatorname{core}(\mu)\ \ \&\ \ \operatorname{howl}(\eta)>\operatorname{howl}(\mu)\ \ \ \not\Longrightarrow\ \ \ \eta>\mu.

For example, for
\lambda:=(4,1,0,0,-4)
,
\nu:=(3,2,1,-2,-3)
one has

\operatorname{howl}(\lambda)=(3,0,0,-3)
and
\operatorname{howl}(\nu)=(2,1,-1,2)
. In this case

\lambda\not>\nu
and

\operatorname{howl}(\lambda)>\operatorname{howl}(\nu)
.

4.3.
Arc diagrams

A
generalized arc diagram
is the following data:

\bullet

a weight diagram
f
, where
the symbols
\wedge
at the zero position are drawn vertically;

\bullet

a collection of non-intersecting arcs of two types:

–

a
full arc

\operatorname{arc}(a;b)
connects
the symbol
\times
at the position
a\not=0
with the empty symbol
at the position
b>a
; full arcs are depicted by solid arcs;

–

a
half arc

\operatorname{arc}(0;b)
connects
the symbol
\wedge
at the zero position with an empty symbol
at the position
b
; half arcs are depicted by dashed arcs.

An empty position is called
free
if it is not an end of an arc.

For
a\neq 0
, we call
\operatorname{arc}(a;b)
a
full arc supported at

a
, and

\operatorname{arc}(0;b)
a
half arc supported at

0
. In this sense, when later on we talk about a number of arcs, two half arcs will make one arc. For example, a quantity of 3/2 arcs consists either of a full arc with a half arc, or three half arcs.

A generalized arc diagram is called an
arc diagram
if each symbol
\times
and
\wedge
is the left end of exactly one arc and there are no free positions under the arcs.
Each weight diagram
f
admits a unique arc diagram
which we denote by
\operatorname{Arc}(f)
(as in
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{osp}(m|n)
case we
construct the arcs successively starting from the rightmost symbol

\times
). We write
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)

for
\operatorname{Arc}(\operatorname{diag}(\nu))
.
We write
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)\subset\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
if each arc in
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)

appears in
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
.

4.3.1.
Partial order

We consider a partial order on the set of arcs by saying that one arc is smaller than
another one if the first one is ”below” the second one, that is

arc(a;b)>arc(a^{\prime};b^{\prime})\ \text{ if and only if }\ a<a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<b.

Since the arcs do not intersect, one has

arc(a;b)>arc(a^{\prime};b^{\prime})\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ a<a^{\prime}<b,\\

and
any two distinct arcs of the form
arc(0,b_{1}),arc(0,b_{2})
are comparable: either

arc(0,b_{1})>arc(0,b_{2})
or

arc(0,b_{1})<arc(0,b_{2})
.

4.3.2.
Remarks

Notice that a maximal arc can be “removed”: if we erase the symbol
\times
(or
\wedge
) on the left end of the arc and the arc itself, we obtain another arc diagram (this does not hold if the arc is not maximal);
if
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
is obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
in this way, then

\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)\subset\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
.

Observe there is the natural one-to-one correspondence
between
\operatorname{Arc}(f)
and
\operatorname{Arc}(\operatorname{howl}(f))
; this correspondence preserves
the partial order on arcs and the type of arc (full or half).

4.3.3.
Examples

The arc diagram for the weight
\lambda=7\epsilon_{1}+4\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}-\epsilon_{6}-7\epsilon_{7}
looks as follows:

\wedge
\wedge
\times
>
\times

There are two maximal arcs:
\operatorname{arc}(0,5)
and
\operatorname{arc}(7,8)
.

The arc diagram for
\lambda=\frac{5}{2}\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{3}-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon_{4}-\frac{5}{2}\epsilon_{5}
is given by:

\times
<
\times

In this case there is only one maximal arc:
\operatorname{arc}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{9}{2})
.

5.
Main results

In this section we formulate the main results, illustrate them
by examples and give outlines of the proofs. In the following, for
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
of rank
s
,
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
, and
\nu\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n-2s})
, we write
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu):=[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L_{\mathfrak{q}_{n}}(\lambda)):L_{\mathfrak{q}_{n-2s}}(\nu)]
.

5.1.

Theorem.

Take
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
and
\nu\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n-2s})
where
s\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, and let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
be of rank
s
.

(i)

If
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)\not=0
, then
\operatorname{core}(\lambda)=\operatorname{core}(\nu)
and

\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
can be obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by successively removing
s
maximal arcs.

(ii)

We have
m_{x}(\lambda,\nu)=1
if and only if
\operatorname{zero}(\nu)=\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)-2s
. If
\operatorname{zero}(\nu)\neq\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)-2s
then

\operatorname{smult}(DS_{x}L(\lambda),L(\nu))=0.

(See Section
5.8
for the definition of
\operatorname{smult}
).

(iii)

Let
x=T_{0,E_{11}+\dots+E_{rr}}
. Then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))=0
if
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)<r
, and if
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)\geq r
then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))=L(\lambda^{\prime})
, where
\lambda^{\prime}
is obtained from
\lambda
by removing
r
zeroes; in other words
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda^{\prime})
is obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing
r
half arcs.

When
x
is of rank
1/2
its
G_{0}
-orbit intersects
\mathbb{C}\langle T_{0,E_{11}}\rangle
, and thus part (ii) of Theorem
5.1
covers this case. For the case when the rank of
x
is 1, we have the following more precise statements.

5.2.

Theorem.

Let
x=T_{0,B}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
be of rank 1 and
\lambda
be a dominant integral weight.

(i)

If
\lambda
is integral and

\operatorname{tr}B=0
then
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=2
if
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
can be obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing
one maximal full arc,
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=1
if
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
can be obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing two successive
maximal half arcs and
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=0
in other cases.

(ii)

If
\lambda
is integral and

\operatorname{tr}B\not=0
then
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=1
if
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
can be obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing two successive
maximal half arcs and
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=0
in other cases.

Recall that
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))=0
if
\lambda
is a dominant half-integral weight
and
x^{2}\not=0
, see Proposition
3.4.1
.

5.3.

Theorem.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
be of rank 1 with
x^{2}=0
.
If
\lambda
is half-integral, then
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=2
if
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
can be obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing
one maximal full arc and
m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=0
in other cases.

5.3.1.
Remark

If
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
is obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removing
r
maximal arcs and
s
maximal half arcs, then
\operatorname{zero}(\nu)=\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)-s
.

5.3.2.

Corollary.

For an atypical
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))=0
with
x^{2}=0
,
\operatorname{rank}x=1
;

(ii)

\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
has a unique maximal arc and
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)=1
;

(iii)

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))=0
for any
x\not=0
with
\operatorname{rank}x\in\mathbb{Z}
.

5.3.3.

Corollary.

For every simple module
L(\lambda)
with
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda>0
, there exists a nonzero
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
of rank
\leq 1
such that
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)=0
for all nonzero
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
of rank 1; then by Corollary
5.3.2
,
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)=1
. Therefore by Theorem
5.2
,
\operatorname{DS}_{C_{1}}L(\lambda)\neq 0
.
∎

5.3.4.
Remark

It is not hard to see that if the coordinates of
\nu
are obtained
from the coordinates of
\lambda
by removal
2r
zero coordinates, then

m_{x}(\lambda;\nu)=1
for
x
of rank
r
(see
[
11
]
, Prop. 5.7.2
for slightly more general statement); in this case

\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
is obtained from
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
by removal of
2r
half arcs.

5.4.

Corollary.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
.

(i)

All indecomposable components of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L_{\mathfrak{q}_{n}}(\lambda))

are isotypical.

(ii)

If
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)=0
, then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))

is completely reducible.

Proof.

In order to prove that each indecomposable component
of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))
is isotypical, it is enough to verify that

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\nu),L(\mu))=0
if
\mu\not=\nu
and

L(\mu),L(\nu)
are subquotients of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))
.
Take
\mu,\nu
as above.
By Theorem
5.1

all arcs in
\operatorname{Arc}(\mu)
,
\operatorname{Arc}(\nu)
are also arcs in
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
;
in particular, if
\operatorname{diag}\lambda
has
\circ
at some position, then both diagrams

\operatorname{diag}(\nu),\operatorname{diag}(\mu)
also have
\circ
at this position.
Assume that
\mu\not\geq\nu
and that

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\nu),L(\mu))\not=0
or
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\mu),L(\nu))\not=0
.
By
[
12
]
, Theorem A, this implies that
\operatorname{diag}(\nu)
can be obtained
from
\operatorname{diag}(\mu)
by moving one symbol
\times
in
\operatorname{diag}(\mu)

along the arc in
\operatorname{Arc}(\mu)
or changing two symbols
\wedge

to a symbol
\times
which occupies a position connected to one of the symbols

\wedge
in
\operatorname{Arc}(\mu)
. This means that
there are two positions
a<b
which
are connected by an arc in
\operatorname{Arc}(\mu)
(
\operatorname{diag}(\mu)
has
\circ
at the position
b
)
and
\operatorname{diag}(\nu)
has
\times
at the position
b
.
By above, the positions
a,b

are connected by an arc in
\operatorname{Arc}(\lambda)
, so

\operatorname{diag}\lambda
has
\circ
at the position
b
and thus

\operatorname{diag}(\nu)
has
\circ
at the position
b
, a contradiction.
This establishes (i).

If
\operatorname{zero}(\lambda)=0
and

L(\nu)
is a subquotient of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))
, then

\operatorname{zero}(\nu)=0
; by
Theorem 3.1 in
[
17
]
this gives

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\nu),L(\nu))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\nu),\Pi L(\nu))=0
.
Combining with (i) we deduce complete reducibility of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda))
.
∎

5.5.
A result on grading and semisimplicity

Take
x
with
x^{2}=0
to be of rank
1
.

5.5.1.
Notation

Let
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
be integral or half-integral. For
k\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, we let

g(\lambda,k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ell(\lambda,k)+1&\text{ if }\lambda\text{ integral}\\
\ell(\lambda,k)+1/2&\text{ if }\lambda\text{ half-integral}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Here
\ell(\lambda,k)
denotes the number of nonzero free positions strictly to the left of
k
on the arc diagram of
\lambda
(see
4.3
for definition of a free
position). For example, if
\lambda
is half-integral and has weight diagram
\times\circ\circ\times
then
g(\lambda,7/2)=3/2
and
g(\lambda,1/2)=1/2
, while if
\lambda
is integral with weight diagram
\wedge^{2}\times\circ\times
, then
g(\lambda,3)=g(\lambda,1)=1
.

5.5.2.

Theorem.

Let
x
be of rank 1 with
x^{2}=0
, and let
h
be as in Section
3.5
. Let
\lambda\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
.

(i)

If
\mu\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n-2})
is obtained from
\lambda
removal of 2 zeros, then
h
acts trivially on
L(\mu)
as a submodule of
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)
.

(ii)

If
\mu\in P^{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n-2})
is obtained from
\lambda
by removal of a symbol
\times
at position
k>0
in its arc diagram, then
h
acts with opposite eigenvalues
\pm g(\lambda,k)
on the two copies of
L(\mu)
in
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)
.

5.5.3.
Remark

Theorems
5.2
and
5.5.2
imply that
DS_{x}L(\lambda)
is multiplicity-free as a module over
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
. Thus we obtain as a simple corollary:

5.6.

Corollary.

For
x
of rank
1
with
x^{2}=0
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\lambda)
is semisimple.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Corollary
5.4
and Theorem
5.5.2
.
∎

5.6.1.
Example

If
\lambda=7\epsilon_{1}+4\epsilon_{2}+2\epsilon_{3}-2\epsilon_{5}-4\epsilon_{6}-7\epsilon_{7}
, then the corresponding arc diagram looks as follows:

\wedge
\times
\times
\times

As a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-module we have that

DS_{x}L(\lambda)\cong L(\lambda_{1})_{1}\oplus\Pi L(\lambda_{1})_{-1}\oplus L(\lambda_{2})_{1}\oplus\Pi L(\lambda_{2})_{-1}\oplus L(\lambda_{3})_{2}\oplus\Pi L(\lambda_{3})_{-2},

where
\lambda_{1}=7\epsilon_{1}+4\epsilon_{2}-4\epsilon_{4}-7\epsilon_{5}
,
\lambda_{2}=7\epsilon_{1}+2\epsilon_{2}-2\epsilon_{4}-7\epsilon_{5}
, and
\lambda_{3}=4\epsilon_{2}+2\epsilon_{3}-2\epsilon_{5}-4\epsilon_{5}
. Here for a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module
V
and
t\in\mathbb{C}
we write
V_{t}
for the
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-module on which
h
acts by
t
.

5.7.
Depth

We set
X(\mathfrak{g})_{r}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}|\ \operatorname{rank}x=r\}
.
For a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
N
we set

\breve{X}(N):=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}\setminus\{0\}|\ \operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\not=0\}

and introduce
0pt(N)\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
recursively by

0pt(N):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if }\breve{X}(N)=\emptyset\\
\displaystyle\max_{x\in\breve{X}(N)}\bigl(0pt(\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N))+\operatorname{rank}x\bigr)&\text{ if }\breve{X}(N)\not=\emptyset.\end{array}\right.

By Corollary
3.3.2
one has
0pt(N)\leq r
if
N
has a central character of atypicality
r
.

5.7.1.

Corollary.

For a finite-dimensional simple module
L=L(\lambda)
one has

0pt(L)=\operatorname{atyp}\lambda.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3.2
one has
0pt(L)\leq\operatorname{atyp}\lambda
. Assume that
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda>0
.
Combining Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
5.6
we deduce
the existence of
x\not=0
(with
\operatorname{rank}x\leq 1
) such that

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L)
has a simple direct summand
L(\nu)
.
By Corollary
3.3.2
,
\operatorname{atyp}\nu=\operatorname{atyp}\lambda-\operatorname{rank}x

Applying (

\frac{f_{-}\times\circ f_{+}}{g_{-}\times\circ g_{+}}=\frac{f_{-}<>f_{+}}{g_{-}<>g_{+}}&&\frac{\wedge^{r+1}\circ f_{+}}{\wedge^{r^{\prime}+1}\circ g_{+}}=\frac{\wedge^{r}>f_{+}}{\wedge^{r^{\prime}}>g_{+}}.\end{array}

6.5.5.

By

6.7.
Keeping track of the action of
h

We now explain how to prove the isomorphism (
5
) in the case when
x^{2}=0
and the rank of
x
is 1. In this case, we view
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
as a functor from the category of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules to the category of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-modules. If
V
is semisimple over
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
, which is the only case we consider, then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
will admit a semisimple action of
h
. Given a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module
V
and
t\in\mathbb{C}
, we write
V_{t}
for the
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-module with
h
acting by
t
.

For the standard module
L(\epsilon_{1})
, the
h
action on
\operatorname{DS}_{x}L(\epsilon_{1})
is trivial, and thus for any pair of cores
c,c^{\prime}
we have a natural isomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
-modules

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(T^{c^{\prime}}_{c}(N))\cong T^{c^{\prime}}_{c}(\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)).

Notice that the central characters of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
are parametrized by pairs
(c,t)
, where
c
is a core and
t\in\mathbb{C}
. On the RHS of the above formula,
T_{c^{\prime}}^{c}
denotes the translation functor between modules with central characters of the form

to modules with central characters of the form
(c,t)
. In particular,
T_{c^{\prime}}^{c}
takes modules with central character indexed by
(c^{\prime},t)
to modules with central character indexed by

In particular we obtain the following: for any

and

we have

(14)

\begin{array}[]{ll}[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(T^{c}_{c^{\prime}}(N)):L^{\prime}_{t}]&=[T^{c}_{c^{\prime}}(\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)):L^{\prime}_{t}]\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{L_{1}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})^{c}}[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N):(L_{1})_{t}][T^{c}_{c^{\prime}}(L_{1}):L^{\prime}].\end{array}

Using Equation (

The other check that needs to be made is that for stable weights

,

, and for

, we have

\displaystyle[DS_{x}L(\lambda):L(\mu)_{t}]=[DS_{x}L(\operatorname{howl}(\lambda)):L(\operatorname{howl}(\mu))_{t}].

However following the argument of Lemma

. Thus Lemma

becomes, in our case,

[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L(\lambda)):L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\nu)_{t}]=[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(L_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\times\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}}(\lambda)):L_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{x}\times\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}}(\nu)_{t}].

Now using the statement of Lemma

From this, we may use the same algorithm implicitly described in Section

7.
Appendix

The
\operatorname{DS}
-functor was introduced in
[
7
]
; see also
[
15
]
for an expanded exposition.
We will be using a slight extension of
\operatorname{DS}
-functor which we define below.

7.1.
Construction

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra. Define

\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}|\ \operatorname{ad}[x,x]\text{ is semisimple}\}.

For a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}
we set
M^{x}:=\operatorname{Ker}_{M}x
. Now let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
and write
x^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}[x,x]
. For a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
on which
x^{2}
acts semisimply we set

M_{x}=\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M):=\frac{\ker(x:M^{x^{2}})}{\operatorname{Im}(x:M^{x^{2}})}.

Then
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
is a tensor functor; in particular
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
will be a Lie superalgebra, and
M_{x}
will have the natural structure of a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module. Further there are canonical isomorphisms

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(\Pi(N))\cong\Pi(\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N))\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{DS}_{x}(M)\otimes\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\cong\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M\otimes N).

In addition we have a canonical isomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N^{*})\cong(\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N))^{*}
.

Thus
\operatorname{DS}_{x}:M\mapsto\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M)
is a tensor functor from the category of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules on which
x^{2}
acts semisimply to
the category of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules.

7.1.1.

We say that
\mathfrak{g}_{x}

can be idenitified with
a certain subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}

if

\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes([x,\mathfrak{g}]\cap\mathfrak{g}^{x})

7.1.2.
Grading on
\operatorname{DS}_{x}

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{ss}
with
x^{2}=0
, and write
\mathfrak{n}(x)
for the normalizer of
x
in
\mathfrak{g}
. Then if
M
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-module,
M_{x}
will have the natural structure of a
\mathfrak{n}(x)
-module. The action map gives an exact sequence

0\to\mathfrak{g}^{x}\to\mathfrak{n}(x)\to\mathbb{C}\langle x\rangle,

which defines a short exact sequence
whenever there exists
h\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
[h,x]=1
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a Kac-Moody superalgebra or
\mathfrak{q}_{n}
,
\mathfrak{sq}_{n}
, such an element
h
always exists, and further we may choose it such that under the embedding
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{x}
,
h
commutes with
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
. In this way we obtain naturally an action of

\mathfrak{g}_{x}\times\mathbb{C}\langle h\rangle
on
M_{x}
.

The action of
h
gives rise to a grading on
M_{x}
as a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module according to the eigenvalues of

:

M_{x}=\bigoplus\limits_{t\in\mathbb{C}}(M_{x})_{t}.

7.1.3.
Hinich Lemma

Each short exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules with semisimple action of
x^{2}

0\to M_{1}\to N\to M_{2}\to 0

induces a long exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules

0\to Y\to\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M_{1})\to\operatorname{DS}_{x}(N)\to\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M_{2})\to\Pi(Y)\to 0,

where
Y
is a some
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module; identifying
M_{1}
with its image
in
N
we have

Y=(M_{1}^{x}\cap[x,N])/(M_{1}^{x}\cap[x,M_{1}]).

If
[x,x]=0
and we have an element
h
as in Section
7.1.2
, the morphism
\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M_{2})\to\Pi Y
has weight
1
for the action of
h
, while all other maps commute with
h
.

7.1.4.
\operatorname{DS}
and restriction map

Let
\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{g})
denote the Grothendieck ring of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules and let
\mathscr{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
denote the
the quotient of

stands for the parity change functor.
For a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}

its image in

\mathscr{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Although
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
is not an exact functor, by the Hinich lemma
\operatorname{DS}_{x}
defines ring homomorphisms
on reduced Grothendieck rings

\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}))\to\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}^{x}))
which coincides with the restriction map

[M]\mapsto[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}^{x}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M]
, see
[

]
.
The ring
\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}))
is a subring
of
\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}^{x}))
and the image
of
\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}))\to\mathscr{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}^{x}))
lies in this subring, see
[
15
]
.

If
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
is idenitified with a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
we have

[\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M)]=[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M].

7.1.5.
Remark

The morphism
[M]\mapsto[DS_{x}M]=[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M]
is often denoted by
ds_{x}
in many papers, including
[
15
]
. We chose to avoid this notation to emphasize the simplicity and universality of restriction, in favor of the more limited setting of the
\operatorname{DS}
functor.

7.2.
Case of commuting
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}^{ss}_{\overline{1}}

Fix
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}^{ss}_{\overline{1}}
and
h\in\mathfrak{g}
such that

[x,y]=0,\ \ [h,x]=c_{x}x,\ \ \ [h,y]=c_{y}y\ \text{ with }\ c_{x},c_{y}\in\mathbb{C},\ c_{x}\not=c_{y}.

Note that

x\in\mathfrak{g}^{y}
; we denote by

\overline{x}
the image of
x
in
\mathfrak{g}_{y}
.

7.2.1.

Lemma.

Let
N
be a finite-dimensional

\mathfrak{g}
-module with a diagonal action of
h,x^{2},
and
y^{2}
. Assume that
(p|q):=\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}}\operatorname{DS}_{y}(N)
. Then

\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(N)=(p-j|q-j)

for some
j\geq 0
. Moreover,
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}=(p|q)
if
xN\cap yN=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma 3.1 of
[
29
]
,
\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}N\cong DS_{x+y}N^{x^{2},y^{2}}
, so we may assume that
x^{2}=y^{2}=0
. This reduces the statement to the case when
\mathfrak{g}
is
(0|2)
-dimensional commutative Lie superalgebra and
N
is a
\mathbb{C}
-graded
\mathfrak{g}
module
where
x,y
have different degrees. In particular, for
v\in N

the equality
xv=yv
implies
xv=0
.

The indecomposable finite-dimensional modules
over the ring
\mathbb{F}[u,v]/(u^{2},v^{2})
were classified in
[
25
]
.
From this classification it follows the indecomposable summands of
N
are, up to a parity change, from the following list:
a
4
-dimensional projective modules
M_{4}
satisfying

\operatorname{DS}_{x}(M_{4})=\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(M_{4})=0
and the “zigzag” modules
V_{s}^{\pm}
.
Each zigzag modules has a basis
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}
with
p(v_{i+1})=\overline{i}
;
we depict each module by the diagram, where
xv_{i}=v_{i+1}
is depicted as

v_{i}{\longleftarrow}v_{i+1}
and
yv_{i}=v_{i-1}
is depicted as

v_{i}{\longrightarrow}v_{i-1}
.
We have

\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{2n}^{+}:&v_{1}{\longrightarrow}v_{2}\longleftarrow v_{3}\longrightarrow v_{4}\longleftarrow\ldots\longrightarrow v_{2n}\\
V_{2n}^{-}:&v_{1}{\longleftarrow}v_{2}\longrightarrow v_{3}\longleftarrow v_{4}\longrightarrow\ldots\longleftarrow v_{2n}\\
V_{2n-1}^{+}:&v_{1}{\longrightarrow}v_{2}\longleftarrow v_{3}\longrightarrow v_{4}\longleftarrow\ldots\longleftarrow v_{2n-1}\\
V_{2n-1}^{-}:&v_{1}{\longleftarrow}v_{2}\longrightarrow v_{3}\longleftarrow v_{4}\longrightarrow\ldots\longrightarrow v_{2n-1}\end{array}

(The modules
V_{1}^{+}\cong V_{1}^{-}
are trivial). One sees that

\begin{array}[]{l}\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}}\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{\pm}_{2n-1})=\dim\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{\pm}_{2n+1})=\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(V^{\pm}_{2n+1})=(1|0),\\
\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{+}_{2n})=\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{-}_{2})=0,\ \ \ \operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(V^{\pm}_{2n})=0\end{array}

and
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}}\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{-}_{2n}))=\dim\operatorname{DS}_{y}(V^{-}_{2n})=(1|1)
for
n>1
.
This gives
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(N)=(p-j|q-j)
for some
j\geq 0
.
If
xN\cap yN=0
, then
N
is a direct sum
and the modules of the form
V_{1}^{\pm}
,
V^{\pm}_{2}
,
V^{-}_{3}
.
By above, this gives
\dim\operatorname{DS}_{x+y}(N)=\operatorname{DS}_{\overline{x}}\operatorname{DS}_{y}(N)
.
∎
