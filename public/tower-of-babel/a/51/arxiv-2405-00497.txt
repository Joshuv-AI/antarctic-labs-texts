Abstract
. We study the ϱ \varrho -th order variation seminorm of a general Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup ( ℋ t ) t > 0 \left(\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)_{t>0} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , taken with respect to t t . We prove that this seminorm defines an operator of weak type ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) with respect to the invariant measure when ϱ > 2 \varrho>2 . For large t t , one has an enhanced version of the standard weak-type ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) bound. For small t t , the proof hinges on vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund techniques in the local region, and on the fact that the t t derivative of the integral kernel of ℋ t \mathcal{H}_{t} in the global region has a bounded number of zeros in ( 0 , 1 ] (0,1] . A counterexample is given for ϱ = 2 \varrho=2 ; in fact, we prove that the second order variation seminorm of ( ℋ t ) t > 0 \left(\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)_{t>0} , and therefore also the ϱ \varrho -th order variation seminorm for any ϱ ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) \varrho\in[1,2) , is not of strong nor weak type ( p , p ) (p,p) for any p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) with respect to the invariant measure.

The proof relies on extensive application of properties of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup and its integral kernel

Let
\big(T_{t}\big)_{t>0}

(X,\mu)

\varrho

\big(T_{t}\big)_{t>0}
on an interval

for

(1.1)

\|T_{t}\,f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),I}=\sup\,\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|T_{t_{i}}f(x)-T_{t_{i-1}}f(x)|^{\varrho}\right)^{1/\varrho},\qquad x\in X,

where the supremum is taken over all finite, increasing

of points in
I

In the last fifty years,
variational inequalities, stating that the

[

have been generalized in various directions.
We refer to

with a focus on harmonic analysis; see
[

,

,

,

the study of oscillatory and jump inequalities,

,

,

.
For an overview of the connections

In this paper
the family
\big(T_{t}\big)_{t>0}
in (
1.1
) will always be the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\big)_{t>0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This is the semigroup generated by the elliptic operator

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big(Q\nabla^{2}\big)+\langle Bx,\nabla\rangle,

called the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator.
Here
\nabla
is the gradient and
\nabla^{2}
the Hessian matrix. Moreover,

Q
is a real, symmetric and positive definite
n\times n

matrix, called the covariance of
\mathcal{L}
, and

B
is a real
n\times n
matrix whose eigenvalues have negative real parts;

B
indicates the drift of
\mathcal{L}
.
In Section
2

we will provide explicit expressions for
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, seen as an integral operator with a kernel
K_{t}(x,u)
.
It is well known that there exists an invariant measure under the action of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, unique up to a positive factor. This measure, denoted by
\gamma_{\infty}
, is basic in Ornstein-Uhlenbeck theory; it is described explicitly in Section
2
.

In 2001 Jones and Reinhold
[
24
]
proved that for
\varrho>2
the variation operator
of any symmetric
diffusion semigroup is
L^{p}
bounded for
1<p<\infty
. Ten years later Le Merdy and Xu
[
30
]

extended this result to a nonsymmetric context. In fact, Corollary 4.5 in
[
30
]

applies to

\mathcal{H}_{t}
(see
[
2
, p. 31]
for a discussion). It says that the operator given by

f\mapsto\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),\mathbb{R}_{+}}
,
is bounded on
L^{p}(\gamma_{\infty})
for
1<p<\infty
and
\varrho>2
.
Our result gives the corresponding weak type
(1,1)
, as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each
\varrho>2
the operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),\mathbb{R}_{+}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the
v(\varrho)
seminorm is taken in the variable
t
, is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.

In other words, the inequality

(1.3)

\gamma_{\infty}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),\mathbb{R}_{+}}>\alpha\}\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})},\qquad\alpha>0,

holds for some
C>0
and all functions
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
.

The pointwise estimates of the Mehler kernel and its derivative needed to prove Theorem
1.1

are quite different for small and large
t
. Thus we are led to
distinguish between the variation in the interval
0<t\leq 1
and that in
1\leq t<\infty
.

The variation in
[1,\infty)
is treated following a geometric approach
recently developed by the authors in
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
, which relies on a system of polar-like coordinates. Proposition
4.3
is actually an enhanced version of (
1.3
) with
\mathbb{R}_{+}
replaced by

[1,\infty)
.

The study of the variation in
(0,1]
is more delicate and
requires a further distinction between local and global parts of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup operator.
In the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck setting,
the local part is usually defined by the relation
|x-u|\lesssim 1/(1+|x|)
between the two variables of the Mehler kernel.
This splitting was first introduced by
Muckenhoupt
[
40
]
in one dimension, and by the third author
[
44
]
in higher dimension. It has since been widely used in the literature, in particular in
[
41
]
. In this paper, Pérez shows that the local parts of many operators related to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup behave precisely like the corresponding classical operators in Euclidean space.

The reason for this definition of the local part is that it makes the density of the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
have the same order of magnitude at the two points
x
and
u
. But this remains true if
x
and
u
belong to a suitable elliptic ring of width roughly
1/(1+|x|)
. In this paper, we will define the local part by splitting
\mathbb{R}^{n}

into rings of this type. This makes the arguments more explicit; for details see Section
5
. The expression “local part” is not quite adequate here, since

x
and
u
may be far apart, but we prefer to keep it, since this part plays the same role in the proofs as it does in earlier work.

To prove (
1.3
) for the global part and
0<t\leq 1
, we estimate its kernel using a method from

[
14
]
. It is
based on the observation that the
number of zeros in
(0,1]
of the function
t\mapsto\partial_{t}K_{t}(x,u)

is bounded, uniformly in
(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The argument for
the local part and
0<t\leq 1
is based on vector–valued Calderón–Zygmund techniques for singular integrals
from
[
22
,
21
,
17
]
. This requires a transition to Lebesgue measure.

The parameter
\varrho
deserves more attention.
Some of our results hold for
\varrho\geq 1
as well. This is the case of the variational bounds in
[1,\infty)

and also in
(0,1]
for
the global part; we refer to Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
6.1
, respectively.
What forces the restriction
\varrho>2
in Theorem

1.1
is the variational bound
for
the local part of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
when
t\in(0,1]
;
see Theorem
7.2
, where we apply results from
[
30
]
holding only for
\varrho>2
.
In the last section, we provide a counterexample showing that the condition
\varrho>2
is necessary in Theorem
1.1
.

2.
The Mehler kernel

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup may be formally written as

\mathcal{H}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
,

{t>0}
, with
\mathcal{L}
given by (
1.2
). In order to give more explicit expressions for this semigroup,
we introduce the
positive definite, symmetric matrices

(2.1)

Q_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{*}}ds,\qquad\text{ $0<t\leq+\infty$},

and the
normalized Gaussian measures
\gamma_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
t\in(0,+\infty]
, whose densities
with respect to Lebesgue measure
dx
are given by

x\mapsto(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}(\text{det}\,Q_{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\langle Q_{t}^{-1}x,x\rangle}\right).

Here the case
t=+\infty
gives the invariant measure
\gamma_{\infty}
that appears
already in the introduction.

Kolmogorov’s formula states that
for all bounded and continuous functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has

(2.2)

\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)=\int f(e^{tB}x-y)\,d\gamma_{t}(y)\,,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\;t>0.

Starting from (
2.2
), one may write
\mathcal{H}_{t}
as an integral operator
with a (Mehler) kernel
K_{t}(x,u)
.
We point out that the term kernel in this paper refers to integration with respect to our basic measure
\gamma_{\infty}
, with an exception in Section
7
.
In fact, we saw in
[
11
]
that for each
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
and all
t>0
one has

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int K_{t}(x,u)\,f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u)\,,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where for
x,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
the Mehler kernel
K_{t}
is given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle K_{t}(x,u)\!=\!\Big(\frac{\det\,Q_{\infty}}{\det\,Q_{t}}\Big)^{{1}/{2}}e^{R(x)}\,\exp\Big[{-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(Q_{t}^{-1}-Q_{\infty}^{-1}\right)(u-D_{t}\,x)\,,u-D_{t}\,x\right\rangle}\Big]\!.\;\;\;\;

Here

R(x)
is
the quadratic form

R(x)={\frac{1}{2}\left\langle Q_{\infty}^{-1}x,x\right\rangle},\qquad\text{$x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

and

(2.5)

D_{t}=Q_{\infty}\,e^{-tB^{*}}Q_{\infty}^{-1},

which is a one-parameter group, defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

It will be convenient to introduce another norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

|x|_{Q}:=|Q_{\infty}^{-1/2}\,x|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then

|x|_{Q}\simeq|x|
,
and
R(x)=|x|_{Q}^{2}/2,\;\,\text{$x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}
.
Observe that the density of
\gamma_{\infty}
is proportional to
e^{-R(x)}
.

2.1.
Pointwise estimates for the Mehler kernel

We shall repeatedly use the following pointwise estimate of the Mehler kernel. For
0<t\leq 1
and
all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has

(2.6)

K_{t}(x,u)\,\lesssim\,\frac{e^{R(x)}}{t^{n/2}}\exp\left(-c\,\frac{|u-D_{t}\,x|^{2}}{t}\right).

This is proved in
[
11
, (3.4)]
.

We shall need estimates for the time derivative of the Mehler kernel as well.
For an explicit expression of
\dot{K}_{t}
, the reader is referred to
[
14
, Lemma 4.2]
; we only recall here the following pointwise bounds for
\dot{K}_{t}

(see
[
14
, (5.5) and (5.4)]
):

(2.7)

|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|\lesssim\,e^{R(x)}\,t^{-n/2}\,\exp{\left(-c\,\frac{|u-D_{t}\,x|^{2}}{t}\right)}\,\left(t^{-1}+|x|t^{-1/2}\right),

for
0<t\leq 1
, and

(2.8)

|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|\lesssim\,e^{R(x)}\,\exp{\left(-c\,|D_{-t}\,u-x|^{2}\right)}\,\left(|D_{-t}\,u|+e^{-ct}\right)

for
t\geq 1
.
Both formulae hold
for all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

3.
The variation seminorm

To state some basic properties of the variation seminorm, we define it for a generic continuous
real-valued function
\phi
defined in an interval
I
, by setting for any
1\leq\varrho<\infty

(3.1)

\|\phi\|_{v(\varrho),I}=\sup\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\phi(t_{i})-\phi(t_{i-1})|^{\varrho}\right)^{1/\varrho}.

As in (
1.1
), the supremum is taken over all finite, increasing sequences
\left(t_{i}\right)_{0}^{N}
of points in
I
.
This seminorm vanishes only for constant functions.

Observe that the seminorm

\|.\|_{v(\varrho),I}
is decreasing in
\varrho
for
1\leq\varrho<\infty
.
It is also subadditive in
I
, in the following sense. Take an inner point
\tau
of
I
and set
I_{+}=I\cap[\tau,+\infty)
and
I_{-}=I\cap(-\infty,\tau]
. Then for
1\leq\varrho<\infty
and any
\phi

\|\phi\|_{v(\varrho),I}\leq\|\phi\|_{v(\varrho),I_{+}}+\|\phi\|_{v(\varrho),I_{-}}.

If
\phi\in C^{1}(I)
and
\phi^{\prime}\in L^{1}(I)
, then for
1\leq\varrho<\infty

(3.2)

\|\phi\|_{v(\varrho),I}\leq\int_{I}|\phi^{\prime}(t)|\,dt,

see
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
.

In the last section, we will consider a discrete version of the variation, obtained by replacing
I
in the definition (
3.1
)
by a set
\mathcal{I}
which is the intersection of
\mathbb{N}_{+}
and an interval, and where
\phi
is defined.
For
\varrho=2
one has the simple estimate

(3.3)

\|\phi\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}}\lesssim\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}}\phi(\ell)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For future convenience, we note that
a combination of
(
3.2
)
and
[
14
, Lemma 5.3]
yields that for any
\varrho\in[1,\infty)
, any interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),I}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int K_{t}(x,u)f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u)\right|\,dt=\int_{I}\left|\int\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u)\right|\,dt

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq\int\int_{I}\big|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)\big|\,dt\,|f(u)|\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u),\qquad f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty}).

3.1.
Simplifications

By means of a few
observations, it is possible to simplify the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

First of all, when proving the inequality (
1.3
) one may take

f
such that

\|f\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})}=1
. As a consequence,
\alpha
in the same estimate may be assumed large, for instance
\alpha>2
, since
\gamma_{\infty}
is finite.

Moreover, as already observed in the study of the weak-type
(1,1)
of operators related to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup
(see
[
11
, Section 5]
),
when proving (
1.3
)
we mostly need to take into account only points
x
belonging to
the ellipsoidal annulus

{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\frac{1}{2}\log\alpha\leq R(x)\leq 2\log\alpha\right\}.

Indeed, the unbounded component of the complement of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
can always be neglected, since

\gamma_{\infty}\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,R(x)>2\log\alpha\right\}\right)\lesssim 1/\alpha
.

For the variation of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
in
[1,+\infty)
,
the bounded component of the complement of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}

is negligible as well. Indeed, we see from
[
14
, (5.4) and Lemma 5.1]

that

(3.5)

\int_{1}^{\infty}|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|\,dt\lesssim e^{R(x)}.

Combined with (
3.4
), where
I=[1,\infty)
and
f
is normalized in
L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
,
this leads to

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),[1,+\infty)}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{R(x)}\lesssim\sqrt{\alpha}

for
R(x)<\frac{1}{2}\log\alpha
.
Taking
\alpha
suitable large, we will have

\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),[1,+\infty)}<\alpha
in the bounded component of the complement of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

4.
The variation of
\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)
in
[1,+\infty)

We first introduce the adapted polar coordinates from
[
11
]
.
Fix
\beta>0
and consider the ellipsoid

E_{\beta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,R(x)=\beta\}\,.

As a consequence of
[

the map

s\mapsto R(D_{s}z)
is strictly increasing for each
0\neq z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus we may write any

, uniquely as

x=D_{s}\tilde{x}\,,

for some
\tilde{x}\in E_{\beta}

and

, and

and

We recall two results, previously proved by the authors, which will be essential in our arguments.
The following lemma is

[

Let

\int_{1}^{+\infty}\exp\Big({-\delta\left|D_{-t}\,u-x\right|^{2}}\Big)\big|D_{-t}\,u\big|\,dt\lesssim 1,

[

, and assume

Here the polar coordinates are defined with

, the implicit constant is as in Lemma

, and

We can now deduce the bound for the variation operator in

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
1\leq\varrho<\infty
. The operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),[1,+\infty)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.
In fact, the following stronger result holds: if
\|f\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})}=1
, then

(4.1)

\gamma_{\infty}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|H_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),[1,\infty)}>\alpha\right\}\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha\sqrt{\log\alpha}},\qquad\alpha>2.

Proof.

This proof is similar to that of Proposition 6.1 in
[
14
]
; we sketch it for the sake of completeness.
Let
f
be normalized in
L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
.
In the light of the considerations made in Subsection

,
it suffices to consider
\alpha
large and
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.
Using polar coordinates with
\beta=\log\alpha

for
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
and
u\neq 0
,
we write
x=D_{s}\,\tilde{x}
and

u=D_{\sigma}\,\tilde{u}
, with
s,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
R(x)\geq\beta/2
,
[
11
, Lemma 4.3
(i)
]

yields

\displaystyle|D_{-t}\,u-x|

\displaystyle=|D_{\sigma-t}\,\tilde{u}-D_{s}\,\tilde{x}|\gtrsim|\tilde{x}-\tilde{u}|,\qquad x\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}.

By reducing the value of
c
in the exponential factor in (
2.8
),
we arrive at

|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|\lesssim e^{R(x)}\,\exp\Big(-c\,|\tilde{x}-\tilde{u}|^{2}\Big)\,\exp{\left(-c\,|D_{-t}\,u-x|^{2}\right)}\,(|D_{-t}\,u|+e^{-ct}),\qquad t>1.

An application of Lemma
4.1
leads to

\int_{1}^{\infty}|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|dt\lesssim e^{R(x)}\,\exp\big(-c\,\big|\tilde{x}-\tilde{u}\big|^{2}\big),\qquad x\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha},

and then (
3.4
) yields

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),[1,\infty)}\lesssim e^{R(x)}\,\int\exp\big(-c\,\big|\tilde{x}-\tilde{u}\big|^{2}\big)\,|f(u)|\,d\gamma_{\infty}.

Finally, Lemma
4.2
implies (
4.1
), and the weak type
(1,1)
also follows.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Theorem
4.3
says that for the variation in
[1,\infty)

the standard weak type
(1,1)
estimate is enhanced by a logarithmic factor.
This phenomenon was observed in the one-dimensional case in
[
15
]
.

5.
Local versus global region

From now on, we shall focus on the variation of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
in the interval
(0,1]
.
This case requires a further distinction between the local and the global parts of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
We start describing the localization procedure.

5.1.
Splitting of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into rings

Let

(5.1)

\displaystyle R_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:j\leq R(x)\leq j+1\},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots.

These rings cover
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and are pairwise disjoint except for boundaries.
We also set
R_{j}=\emptyset
if
j<0
.

The width of
R_{j}
, defined as the
|\cdot|_{Q}
distance between the two components of its boundary, is for

j\geq 1

(5.2)

\sqrt{2}\left(\sqrt{j+1}-\sqrt{j}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{j+1}+\sqrt{j}}\in\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{j}},\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{j}}\right),

as easily verified.

We take a sequence of smooth non-negative functions
(r_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfying
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}(x)=1
for all
x
, with
\text{supp}\,r_{j}\subseteq R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
.
Further, we may choose them such that

(5.3)

\big|\nabla r_{j}(x)\big|\lesssim{1+|x|}.

We also introduce slightly larger functions
\widetilde{r}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

taking values in
[0,1]
such that

\widetilde{r_{j}}=1
in
\cup_{\nu=j-1}^{j+2}R_{\nu}
and

\text{supp}\,\widetilde{r_{j}}\subset\cup_{\nu=j-2}^{j+3}R_{\nu}
.
They also satisfy

(5.4)

\big|\nabla\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\big|\lesssim{1+|x|}.

Then the supports of these
\widetilde{r_{j}}
have bounded overlap,
and

(5.5)

e^{-R(x)}\simeq e^{-j}\qquad\text{for}\qquad x\in\text{supp}\,\widetilde{r_{j}}.

5.2.
The splitting of
\mathcal{H}_{t}

We can now split the operator
\mathcal{H}_{t}f
in a local and a global part, in a way adapted to the rings. The local part is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{loc}}f(x):=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\mathcal{H}_{t}\big(fr_{j}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{t}(x,u)\,f(u)\,r_{j}(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u).

This sum is locally finite and thus well defined for any
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
,
because of the bounded overlap of the sets
\text{supp}\>\widetilde{r_{j}}
.

Setting

\eta(x,u)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r_{j}}(x)\,r_{j}(u),

we get

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{loc}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{t}(x,u)\,\eta(x,u)\,f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u),

and
0\leq\eta(x,u)\leq 1
for all
x,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, if
\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
, then the support of
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{loc}}f
is contained in
\cup_{\nu=j-2}^{j+4}R_{\nu}
.

The global part of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is defined by
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{glob}}=\mathcal{H}_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{loc}}
, or equivalently

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{glob}}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{t}(x,u)\,(1-\eta(x,u))\,f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The next two lemmas give sufficient conditions for the kernel
K_{t}(x,u)\,(1-\eta(x,u))
of the global part to vanish.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
j\in\{0,1,\dots\}
.
If
x,\,u\in R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
, then
\eta(x,u)=1
.

Proof.

When
u\in R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
the function
r_{i}(u)
can be nonzero only for
i\in\{j-1,j,j+1\}
, and then

r_{j-1}(u)+r_{j}(u)+r_{j+1}(u)=1
.

Also, for
x\in R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
one has
{\widetilde{r}}_{j-1}(x)={\widetilde{r}}_{j}(x)={\widetilde{r}}_{j+1}(x)=1
.
This implies that
\eta(x,u)=\sum_{\nu=j-1}^{j+1}\widetilde{r_{\nu}}(x)\,r_{\nu}(u)=1
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

If

|x-u|_{Q}\leq\frac{1}{2(1+|x|_{Q})},

then
\eta(x,u)=1
.

Proof.

Suppose
x\in R_{j}
. Then
|x|_{Q}\geq\sqrt{2j}
and we can write

|x-u|_{Q}\leq\frac{1}{2(1+|x|_{Q})}\leq\frac{1}{2(1+\sqrt{j})}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{j+1}}.

From this and (
5.2
), we see that
|x-u|_{Q}
is less than the widths of
R_{j-1}
and
R_{j+1}
(only that of
R_{j+1}
if
j=0
or
j=1
).
Since
x\in R_{j}
, this means that both
x
and
u
must be in
R_{j-1}\cup R_{j}

or
R_{j}\cup R_{j+1}
. From Lemma
5.1
we then get the assertion.
∎

We will need an estimate for the gradient of
\eta(x,u)
. If a point
(x,u)
is in the support of
\eta
, then
x
and
u
are both in the support of some

\widetilde{r}_{j}
. It follows that
1+|u|\simeq 1+|x|
, and (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) then
imply that

(5.7)

|\nabla_{x}\,\eta(x,u)|+|\nabla_{u}\,\eta(x,u)|\lesssim 1+|x|.

6.
The variation of the global part

The result of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

For each
\varrho\geq 1
the operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

\|{\mathcal{H}}_{t}^{\mathrm{glob}}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.

Proof.

This proof follows the pattern from that of
[
14
, Proposition 10.2]
. In particular, we will
need the following result concerning a maximal operator.

Theorem 6.2
.

The maximal operator defined by

\displaystyle S_{0}^{{\rm{glob}}}f(x)=\int\sup_{0<t\leq 1}K_{t}(x,u)\,\big(1-\eta(x,u)\big)\,|f(u)|\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u)

is
of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the invariant measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.

This statement seems to coincide with that of
[
14
, Theorem 10.1]
, but
our
\eta(x,u)
is not the same as that in
[
14
]
. However, by tracing the proof in
[
14
]
,
one sees that what matters is only the implication

\eta(x,u)<1\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad|x-u|_{Q}>\frac{1}{2(1+|x|_{Q})},

which is a consequence of our Lemma
5.2
. In this way, Theorem
6.2
follows.

We will also need

[
14
, Proposition 9.1]
. It says
that for any
(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the number of zeros
N(x,u)
in
(0,1]
of the map
t\mapsto\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)
is bounded
by a positive integer
\bar{N}
that depends only on
n
and
B
.

As in
[
14
, Proposition 10.2]
,
we denote these zeros by
t_{1}(x,u)<\cdots<t_{N(x,u)}(x,u)
,
and set
t_{0}(x,u)=0
,
t_{N(x,u)+1}(x,u)=1
.
Since
K_{t}(x,u)
vanishes at
t=0
if
x\neq u
,
it follows from the fundamental theorem of calculus that

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)\right|dt=\sum_{i=0}^{N(x,u)}\left|\int_{t_{i}(x,u)}^{t_{i+1}(x,u)}\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)dt\right|\\
=\sum_{i=0}^{N(x,u)}\left|K_{t_{i+1}(x,u)}(x,u)-K_{t_{i}(x,u)}(x,u)\right|\\
\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{N(x,u)+1}K_{t_{i}(x,u)}(x,u)\;\lesssim\;\bar{N}\sup_{0<t\leq 1}K_{t}(x,u).

By (
3.4
), which remains valid with the extra factor
1-\eta(x,u)
, we obtain

\|\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{glob}}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}\lesssim\int\sup_{0<t\leq 1}K_{t}(x,u)\,(1-\eta(x,u)\,|f(u)|\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u).

Now Theorem
6.2
implies the assertion of Theorem
6.1
.
∎

7.
The local part for small
t

In this section, we prove the following result.

Theorem 7.1
.

For each
\varrho>2
the operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

\|\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{loc}}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.

The proof
relies on vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund theory.

7.1.
Preparations

Part of our notation in this section follows that of
[
17
]
.
Let
\Theta
be the set of all finite, increasing sequences in
(0,1]
written

{\underline{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{i})_{0}^{N}

for some
N=N({\underline{\varepsilon}})\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

Then we let
F
be the vector space of all functions

g:\{(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta:1\leq i\leq N({\underline{\varepsilon}})\}\to\mathbb{R}.

For
\varrho\in[1,\infty)
we define
F_{\varrho}
as that subspace of
F
consisting of those
g
for which the mixed norm

(7.1)

\|g\|_{F_{\varrho}}:=\sup_{{\underline{\varepsilon}}\in\Theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N({\underline{\varepsilon}})}|g(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})|^{\varrho}\right)^{1/\varrho}

is finite. Then
F_{\varrho}

is a normed space and a Banach space.

We shall work with the operator
V
that maps real-valued functions
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to
F
-valued functions defined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and is given by

\displaystyle Vf(x)(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})=\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon_{i}}f(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon_{i-1}}f(x),\qquad i=1,\dots,N({\underline{\varepsilon}}),\;\;{\underline{\varepsilon}}\in\Theta.

Similarly, we set

(7.2)

\displaystyle V^{\text{loc}}f(x)(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})=\mathcal{H}^{\text{loc}}_{\varepsilon_{i}}f(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\text{loc}}_{\varepsilon_{i-1}}f(x),\qquad i=1,\dots,N({\underline{\varepsilon}}),

and analogously for
V^{\text{glob}}f
.

Then for
\varrho\in[1,\infty)

(7.3)

\|Vf(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}=\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]},

and similar equalities hold with superscripts
loc
or
glob
.

Theorem
7.1
can now be rewritten in the following equivalent way.

Theorem 7.2
.

For each
\varrho>2
the operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

x\mapsto\|V^{\rm{loc}}f(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to the measure
\gamma_{\infty}
.

The advantage of Theorem

7.2
is that the proof can be based on vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund theory.
To apply this machinery, we will pass to Lebesgue measure,
and set

(7.4)

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,g(x)=e^{-R(x)}\,V^{\text{loc}}\big(g(\cdot)\,e^{R(\cdot)}\,\big)(x).

Lebesgue measure will be written either
dx
or
du
.

The following proposition clarifies the connection between

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
and
V^{\text{loc}}
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\varrho>2
. If
the operator that maps
g\in L^{1}(du)
to the function

x\mapsto\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\rm{loc}}\,g(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to Lebesgue measure,
then the operator that maps
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})
to the function

x\mapsto\|V^{\rm{loc}}f(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is of weak type
(1,1)
with respect to
\gamma_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\|V^{\text{loc}}f(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}=

\displaystyle\,\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r_{j}}(x)\,V(fr_{j})(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,V^{\text{glob}}(fr_{j})(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}+\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,V^{\text{loc}}(fr_{j})(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}=I_{\text{glob}}+I_{\text{loc}}.

Since the
\widetilde{r_{j}}
have supports with bounded overlap,
the sums here are uniformly locally finite. It follows that

\displaystyle\|I_{\text{glob}}\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,\|V^{\text{glob}}(fr_{j})(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}.

The
F_{\varrho}
norm here equals
\|\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\text{glob}}(fr_{j})(x)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}
. After estimating the factor
\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)
by 1, we can apply Theorem

6.1
and get

\displaystyle\|I_{\text{glob}}\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|fr_{j}\right\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})}\simeq\|f\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})}.

We estimate the
L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})
quasinorm of
II
similarly, but then apply the definition of

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
. This gives

\displaystyle\|I_{\text{loc}}\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,\|V^{\text{loc}}(fr_{j})(x)\|_{F_{\varrho}}\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,e^{R(x)}\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}(fr_{j}e^{-R(\cdot)})(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}(fr_{j}e^{-R(\cdot)})(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}(dx)};

in the last step here, we switched
from
\gamma_{\infty}
to Lebesgue measure. This was possible because of (
5.5
). Next, we estimate
\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)
by 1 and use the hypothesis of the proposition, getting

\displaystyle\|I_{\text{loc}}\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|fr_{j}e^{-R(\cdot)}\right\|_{L^{1}(du)}\simeq\|f\|_{L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})}.

The proposition is proved.
∎

7.2.
Vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund operators

This theory will be applied to the operator
\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
. The following proposition gives the strong
(p,p)
bound needed.

Proposition 7.4
.

For any
\varrho>2
and any
1<p<\infty
, the operator that maps
g\in L^{p}(du)
to the function

x\mapsto\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,g(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is bounded from
L^{p}(du)
to
L^{p}(dx)
.

Proof.

As a consequence of the definitions of
\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
and
V^{\text{loc}}
and the bounded overlap of the
\widetilde{r_{j}}
,
one has

\displaystyle\left\|\,\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,g(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,\right\|_{L^{p}(dx)}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,e^{-R(x)}\,V\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int\Big\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)e^{-R(x)}\,V\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\Big\|_{F_{\varrho}}^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-jp}\int\Big\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,V\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\Big\|_{F_{\varrho}}^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{j(1-p)}\int\Big\|\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)\,V\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\Big\|_{F_{\varrho}}^{p}\,d\gamma_{\infty}(x).

Notice that we also used (
5.5
) here.
In the last expression, we delete the factor
\widetilde{r}_{j}(x)
and observe that the resulting
F_{\varrho}
seminorm equals

\left\|\mathcal{H}_{t}\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\right\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the variation operator for
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\gamma_{\infty})
with
1<p<\infty
, for
\varrho>2

[
30
]
. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\,\left\|\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,g(x)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,\right\|_{L^{p}(dx)}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{j(1-p)}\int\left\|\mathcal{H}_{t}\left(g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right)(x)\right\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}^{p}d\gamma_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{j(1-p)}\left\|g\,r_{j}\,e^{R(\cdot)}\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{\infty})}^{p}\simeq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|g\,r_{j}\,\|_{L^{p}(du)}^{p}\leq\big\|g\big\|^{p}_{L^{p}(du)},

proving the assertion.
∎

We shall now prove the appropriate standard estimates for the vector-valued kernel of

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
.

From (
7.2
) and (
5.6
), we see that

V^{\text{loc}}
is an integral operator with an
F
-valued
kernel
M^{\text{loc}}(x,u)
given by

M^{\text{loc}}(x,u)(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})=\big(K_{\varepsilon_{i}}(x,u)-K_{\varepsilon_{i-1}}(x,u)\big)\,\eta(x,u),\qquad i=1,\dots,N({\underline{\varepsilon}}).

This means that for
f\in L^{1}(\gamma_{\infty})

(7.5)

V^{\text{loc}}f(x)=\int M^{\text{loc}}(x,u)f(u)\,d\gamma_{\infty}(u).

We will need the
kernel
\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)
of
\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}
, for integration against Lebesgue measure in the sense that

(7.6)

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,f(x)=\int\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)\,du

for suitable
f
.

Remark 7.5
.

Notice that formulae (
7.5
) and (
7.6
) make sense in
F
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since the integrals may be computed
coordinatewise, that is, at one
(i,\epsilon)
at a time.
In the Appendix (see Section
9
) it will be shown that
for
x\notin{\text{supp}}\,f

the integral in (
7.6
) is an
F_{\varrho}
-Bochner integral, and thus

\widetilde{V}^{\text{loc}}\,f(x)\in F_{\varrho}
.

From (
7.4
) we get

(7.7)

\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})=e^{-R(x)}\,M^{{\rm{loc}}}(x,u)(i,{\underline{\varepsilon}})=e^{-R(x)}\,\big(K_{\varepsilon_{i+1}}(x,u)-K_{\varepsilon_{i}}(x,u)\big)\,\eta(x,u),\\
\qquad\qquad i=1,\dots,N({\underline{\varepsilon}}).

In analogy with (
7.3
), this implies

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,y)\|_{F_{\varrho}}

\displaystyle=\,e^{-R(x)}\,\|K_{t}(x,u)\,\eta(x,u)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}

(7.8)

\displaystyle=e^{-R(x)}\,\eta(x,u)\,\|K_{t}(x,u)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]},

where the variation is meant with respect to
t
.

We will need an auxiliary result.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
p,\>r\geq 0
with
p+r/2>1
. Assume that
\eta(x,u)\neq 0
and
x\neq u
. Then for
\delta>0

\int_{0}^{1}t^{-p}\exp\left(-\delta\,\frac{|u-D_{t}\,x|^{2}}{t}\right)|x|^{r}\,dt\lesssim C\,{|u-x|^{-2p-r+2}}.

Here the constant
C
may depend on
\delta,\>p
and
r
, in addition to
n
,
Q
and
B
.

Proof.

The statement of this proposition is similar to that of
[
14
, Proposition 8.3]
, which is based on

[
12
, Lemma 8.1]
. But our function
\eta
is not the same as there, and we must verify that
for
\eta(x,u)\neq 0
and
0<t<1

(7.9)

\frac{|D_{t}\,x-u|^{2}}{t}>c\,\frac{|x-u|^{2}}{t}+ct|x|^{2}-C.

Once this is established, we can follow the argument for
[
12
, Lemma 8.1]
and finish the proof.

To verify (
7.9
), assume first that

t<c_{0}|x-u|/|x|
for a small
c_{0}>0
to be chosen. Then
[
12
, Lemma 2.3]
implies

|D_{t}\,x-x|\leq Ct|x|<Cc_{0}|x-u|
and thus

|D_{t}\,x-u|\geq|x-u|-|D_{t}\,x-x|>|x-u|-Cc_{0}|x-u|.

So with
c_{0}>0
small enough we get

|D_{t}\,x-u|\,\geq\frac{1}{2}\,|x-u|\,\gtrsim\,|x-u|+t|x|.

To obtain (
7.9
) in this case, take squares and divide by
t
.

In the opposite case
c_{0}|x-u|/|x|\leq t<1
, we use
[
11
, Lemma 4.1]
to conclude that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,|D_{t}\,x|_{Q}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\sqrt{2R(D_{t}\,x)}\simeq|D_{t}\,x|_{Q}\simeq|x|_{Q}.

Integration yields

|D_{t}\,x|_{Q}-|x|_{Q}\simeq t|x|_{Q}\,\gtrsim\,t|x|_{Q}+|x-u|.

Thus

\displaystyle|D_{t}\,x-u|_{Q}

\displaystyle\geq|D_{t}\,x|_{Q}-|u|_{Q}=|D_{t}\,x|_{Q}-|x|_{Q}+|x|_{Q}-|u|_{Q}

(7.10)

\displaystyle\geq ct|x|_{Q}+c|x-u|-\big||x|_{Q}-|u|_{Q}\big|.

Since
\eta(x,u)>0
, there exists a

8.
A counterexample

Theorem 8.1
.

The variation operator

f\mapsto\|\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)\|_{v(2),\mathbb{R}_{+}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is not of strong nor weak type
(p,p)
with respect to
\gamma_{\infty}
, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
.

This theorem shows that the condition
\varrho>2
is necessary in Theorem
7.1
and therefore also in Theorem
1.1
. The proof of Theorem
8.1

falls naturally into several parts and will occupy the entire section.

We first describe the plan of the proof.
It is based on a probabilistic result for the one-dimensional torus due to Qian
[
42
]
.
To relate our setting to that of Qian, we first approximate
\mathcal{H}_{t}
by a convolution operator
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{c}
. Staying in a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider only
t\in(0,1]
and verify that this approximation is admissible. The kernel
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{c}
is then diagonalized, which gives a tensor product of one-dimensional convolution operators.
Our focus will be on the first coordinate, and there we apply the convolution operator to a function defined as a sum of Rademacher functions. We also move from a compact interval to the one-dimensional torus, where we will have a convolution operator acting on a similar Rademacher sum. The parameter
t
is now restricted to a dyadic sequence.
Next, we verify that we can replace the operator by a simpler mean value operator on the torus, which is then replaced by a dyadic conditional expectation operator, to which Qian’s result applies. This gives the counterexample that proves Theorem
8.1
.

8.1.
Approximation of the kernel

We consider only
t\in(0,1]
, and
f
will be supported in a compact set. Moreover,
\mathcal{H}_{t}f
will only be considered at points
x
in
another compact set.
For the
L^{p}
norms of
f
and those of the variation of
\mathcal{H}_{t}f
, we can therefore use Lebesgue measure instead of
\gamma_{\infty}
. In the integral (
2.3
) defining
\mathcal{H}_{t}f(x)
, we can write
du
instead of
d\gamma_{\infty}(u)
.
This also allows us to delete the factors
{(\det Q_{\infty})}^{{1}/{2}}
and
e^{R(x)}
in the expression
(
2.4
) for the Mehler kernel. What remains is the kernel

(8.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{t}(x,u)={(\det\,Q_{t})}^{-{1}/{2}}\,\exp\Big[{-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(Q_{t}^{-1}-Q_{\infty}^{-1}\right)(u-D_{t}\,x)\,,u-D_{t}\,x\right\rangle}\Big]\!,

and we will approximate this kernel.
The
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
symbol will be used for
scalars, vectors and matrices and is for
t\to 0
. The implicit constants involved may depend on the compact sets mentioned, as well as on
Q
and
B
.

Since
t\leq 1
, the definition (
2.1
) implies
Q_{t}=Qt+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
, and then

Q_{t}^{-1}-Q_{\infty}^{-1}=Q^{-1}t^{-1}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(t)\big).

Further,
D_{t}\,x=x+\mathcal{O}(t)
because of (
2.5
). Since
x
and
u
stay bounded, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(Q_{t}^{-1}-Q_{\infty}^{-1}\right)(u-D_{t}\,x)\,,u-D_{t}\,x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle Q^{-1}t^{-1}(u-x+\mathcal{O}(t)),u-x+\mathcal{O}(t)\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}\big(|x-u|+t\big)

(8.2)

\displaystyle=\left\langle Q^{-1}t^{-1}(u-x),u-x\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}\big(|x-u|+t\big).

We also observe that
\det\,Q_{t}=t^{n}\,\det Q\,(1+\mathcal{O}(t))
, so that

(8.3)

{(\det\,Q_{t})}^{-{1}/{2}}={(\det\,Q)}^{-{1}/{2}}\,t^{-{n}/{2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(t)).

This makes it natural to approximate
\widetilde{K}_{t}(x,u)
by the simple convolution kernel

K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)
, where

(8.4)

K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(y)={(\det\,Q)}^{-{1}/{2}}\,t^{-{n}/{2}}\,\exp\Big({-\frac{1}{2}\,t^{-1}\left|Q^{-1/2}y\right|^{2}}\Big),

and we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}f(x)=K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}*f(x).

8.2.
The difference operator

Let
\Delta_{t}
be the operator defined by the kernel
\widetilde{K}_{t}(x,u)-K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)
.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let

and

be compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If

has support contained in

\left\|\left\|\Delta_{2^{-2\ell}}f\right\|_{v(2),\mathbb{N}_{+}}\right\|_{L^{2}(C_{2})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(C_{1})}.

Here the implicit constants may depend on
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, in addition to

and

Proof.

By means of (

) and (

), we get for

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{t}(x,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(\det\,Q)^{-{1}/{2}}\,t^{-{n}/{2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(t))\,\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}t^{-1}\left\langle Q^{-1}(u-x),u-x\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}\big(|x-u|+t\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)\,(1+\mathcal{O}(t))\,\exp\Big(\mathcal{O}\big(|x-u|+t\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)+K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)\,\mathcal{O}\big(|x-u|+t\big).

In the last term here, we may replace
|x-u|
by

, if we reduce the factor

in the exponential factor in the expression (

) for

\displaystyle|\widetilde{K}_{t}(x,u)-K^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}(x-u)|\lesssim t^{1/2}\,t^{-n/2}\,\exp\Big(-ct^{-1}|Q^{-1/2}(x-u)|^{2}\Big).

With

and

|\Delta_{t}f(x)|\lesssim t^{1/2}\,\int f(u)\,t^{-n/2}\,\exp\Big(-ct^{-1}|Q^{-1/2}(x-u)|^{2}\Big)\,du.

The integral here is the convolution of

\|\Delta_{t}f\|_{L^{2}(C_{2})}\lesssim t^{1/2}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(C_{1})}.

Letting

, squaring and summing, we obtain

\left\|\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(\Delta_{2^{-2\ell}}f)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{2}(C_{2})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(C_{1})}.

Because of (

), this completes the proof.
∎

8.3.
A counterexample for

For convenience we normalize

by multiplying by

.
Then we make an orthogonal change of variables in

By also scaling the coordinates, we can then replace

by the identity matrix. The new variables will be called

and

, and we replace

, with equivalent

norms. In the new coordinates, the relevant kernel is a tensor product

K_{t}^{*}(y^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{t}(y^{\prime}_{i})

where

J_{t}(y^{\prime}_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,\exp\Big({-\frac{1}{2}\,t^{-1}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}}\Big)

and the operator is given by
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{*}\,g=K_{t}^{*}*g

We will choose

g(u^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(u^{\prime}_{i})
, so that

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{*}\,g(x^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{t}*g_{j}(x^{\prime}_{i}),

with the convolutions taken in
\mathbb{R}
. In the sequel, we will consider the values of
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{*}g

in the cube
[0,1]^{n}
, and each

will have support in
[-1,2]
.
This determines the compact sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, via the change of variables.
For

, we simply choose

, and observe that for these

It follows that

(8.5)

1-\prod_{i=2}^{n}J_{t}*g_{i}=\mathcal{O}(t)\qquad\mathrm{in}\quad[0,1]^{n-1}.

The construction of

requires more care. In the rest of this subsection, we will write simply

in one dimension. As already mentioned, we restrict

to the set
\{2^{-2\ell},\>\ell=1,2,\dots\}
, and define operators

Notice that
J_{2^{-2\ell}}(y)=({2\pi})^{-1/2}\,2^{\ell}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\,\left|2^{\ell}y\right|^{2}}\right)

We will consider the variation in

To choose our function

We identify

In

Notice that the notation

The following is the main result of the present subsection.

Proposition 8.3
.

For some constant

(8.9)

\left\{x\in(0,1):\>\|A_{\ell}\,g_{N}(x)\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}>c\,\sqrt{N\,\mathrm{\log\log}N}\right\}

Starting with

Step 1
will take us from

to the

This definition works equally well in
\mathbb{R}
and in
\mathbb{T}
, and we will write

and

Jones and Wang
[

have introduced an equivalence relation

between sequences of operators acting on functions defined on the torus.
We define a similar relation for operators
P_{\ell}
and
Q_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, acting on functions in
\mathbb{R}
, with

.
By
(P_{\ell})\sim_{2}(Q_{\ell})
we mean
that for any sequence
\big(\nu_{\ell}\big)_{1}^{\infty}
, with
|\nu_{\ell}|\leq 1
, and any

\|\sum_{\ell\geq 1}(P_{\ell}f-Q_{\ell}f)\,\nu_{\ell}\|_{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{2}.

Here the norm is that in

.
As shown in
[

, this implies that

\left\|\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}|P_{\ell}f-Q_{\ell}f|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{2},

which by (

(8.10)

\left\|\left\|P_{\ell}f-Q_{\ell}f\right\|_{v(2),\mathbb{N}_{+}}\right\|_{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{2}.

Lemma 8.4
.

We have

(A_{\ell})\sim_{2}(D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}).

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[

to
\mathbb{R}

Observe first that
A_{\ell}
and

are convolution operators with kernels

J_{2^{-2\ell}}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\,2^{\ell}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,\left(2^{\ell}\,y\right)^{2}\right)
and

\chi_{\ell}(y)=2^{\ell-1}\,\chi\left(2^{\ell}\,y\right)
, respectively,
\chi
denoting the characteristic function of the interval
(-1,1)

We use Fourier transforms, defined by

\mathcal{F}\,f(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)=\int f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\,\xi}\,dx
,
and the Plancherel theorem, to deduce

\displaystyle\|\sum_{\ell\geq 1}(A_{\ell}f-D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}f)\,\nu_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big|\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\mathcal{F}(A_{\ell}f-D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}f)(\xi)\,\nu_{\ell}\Big|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\Big(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\big|{\widehat{J_{2^{-2\ell}}}(\xi)-\widehat{\chi_{\ell}}(\xi)\big|}\Big)^{2}d\xi.

It is thus enough to prove that

(8.11)

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\big|{\widehat{J_{2^{-2\ell}}}(\xi)-\widehat{\chi_{\ell}}(\xi)\big|}\lesssim 1.

The Fourier transform of the kernel

\widehat{J_{2^{-2\ell}}}(\xi)=\exp\left(-2\pi^{2}({2^{-\ell}\xi})^{2}\right),

so that

for

|\widehat{J_{2^{-\ell}}}(\xi)|\lesssim|2^{-\ell}\xi|^{-2}
for
|2^{-\ell}\xi|\geq 1
.
The Fourier transform

\widehat{\chi_{\ell}}(\xi)=\frac{\sin(2\pi 2^{-\ell}\xi)}{2\pi 2^{-\ell}\xi}

similarly satisfies

for

but only

. From this (

∎

To

for the variation seminorm and write for

\displaystyle\left\|A_{\ell}(g_{N})(x)\right\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}\geq\left\|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}(g_{N})(x)\right\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}-h_{1}(x),

where

From Lemma

\|h_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\sqrt{N}
.

Step 2
consists in passing from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{T}
. Observe that for any point
x\in(0,1)
, also considered as a point in
\mathbb{T}
, one has

D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}(q_{k})(x)=D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(r_{k})(x)
.
Summing in
k
, we get for these
x

D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}(g_{N})(x)=D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(T_{N})(x),

where the function

T_{N}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{N}}r_{k}

is defined in
\mathbb{T}
.
From (
8.7
) we conclude

(8.13)

\|T_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\simeq\sqrt{N}.

For
Step 3
, we
let
E_{\ell}
for
\ell=1,2,\dots
denote the conditional expectation operator
which replaces an integrable function defined in
\mathbb{T}
by its mean value in each dyadic subinterval
((j-1)2^{-\ell},\;j2^{-\ell})\subset[0,1]
. Then

(8.14)

E_{\ell}(T_{N})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{N},\>k\leq\ell}r_{k}

for
\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{N}
, so that the
E_{\ell}(T_{N})

form a finite martingale for the sequence of
\sigma
-algebras generated by the dyadic intervals of length
2^{-\ell}
,

\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{N}
, contained in
[0,1]
.
Step 3 aims at
replacing
D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}
by
E_{\ell}
.

Jones and Rosenblatt
prove in
[
25
, Remark 4]
that for functions
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})

\left\|\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}f-E_{\ell}f|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{2}.

Here we refer to the norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. These authors use a one-sided, right mean value operator (see
[
25
, p.528]
),
but since our
D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}
is the mean of the right and left operators, this is no problem.

Letting here
f=T_{N}
, we can apply (
3.3
) and then (
8.13
), to get

\left\|\left\|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(T_{N})-E_{\ell}(T_{N})\right\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{N}}|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(T_{N})-E_{\ell}(T_{N})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}\lesssim\|T_{N}\|_{2}\simeq\sqrt{N}.

The triangle inequality for the seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}
now implies that for
x\in\mathbb{T}

(8.15)

\left\|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(T_{N})(x)\right\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}\geq\left\|E_{\ell}(T_{N})(x)\right\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}-h_{2}(x),

with

(8.16)

\|h_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\sqrt{N}.

In
Step 4
we observe that the
r_{k}
with
k\in\mathcal{I}_{N}
are
N
independent random variables fulfilling the hypotheses of
Qian
[
42
, Theorem 2.2]
. Because of (
8.14
), this result
tells us that the probability, i.e., the Lebesgue measure, of the set

\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:\>\|E_{\ell}(T_{N})(x)\|_{v(2),\mathcal{J}_{N}}>c\,\sqrt{N\,\mathrm{\log\log}N}\right\}

tends to 1 as
N\to\infty
, for some
c>0
.

Finally,
Step 5
combines the result of Step 4 with the preceding steps, in reverse order. Considering Step 3, we see that (
8.16
) and Chebyshev’s inequality imply that

|h_{2}(x)|
is much smaller than
\sqrt{N\,\mathrm{log\log}N}
except on a subset of
\mathbb{T}
whose measure tends to 0 as
N\to\infty
.
Then (
8.15
) implies that the measure of the set

\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:\>\|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{T}}(T_{N})(x)\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}>c\,\sqrt{N\,\mathrm{\log\log}N}\right\}

tends to 1 as
N\to\infty
, for some
c>0
.

Now Step 2 says that this set is the same as

\left\{x\in[0,1):\>\|D_{\ell}^{\mathbb{R}}(g_{N})(x)\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}>c\,\sqrt{N\,\mathrm{\log\log}N}\right\}.

As for Step 1, we can apply (
8.12
) with
x\in[0,1)
and argue as we did for Step 3. Thus the set

(8.17)

\left\{x\in[0,1):\>\|A_{\ell}(g_{N})(x)\|_{v(2),\mathcal{I}_{N}}>c\,\sqrt{N\mathrm{\log\log}N}\right\}.

also has measure tending to 1, for some
c>0
.

The proof of Proposition
8.3
is complete.
∎

8.1

The function
g=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}
, where
g_{1}=g_{N}
,
satisfies
for
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{*}\,g(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=J_{t}*g_{N}(x^{\prime}_{1})\,\prod_{i=2}^{n}J_{t}*g_{i}(x^{\prime}_{1})

\displaystyle=J_{t}*g_{N}(x^{\prime}_{1})-J_{t}*g_{1}(x^{\prime}_{1})\,\left(1-\prod_{i=2}^{n}J_{t}*g_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right)

To estimate the second term here, we apply (

) to its first factor and (

, which is dominated by

2^{-N}
if

with

.
Then we apply Proposition

to
the first term, and conclude that the statement of this proposition also holds for

.

The same is then true for

9.
Appendix

We consider the integral in (
7.6
).

Lemma 9.1
.

If
f\in L^{1}(du)
and
x
is a point such that
x\notin\mathrm{supp}\,f
, then

(9.1)

\int\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)\,du

is a Bochner integral with respect to the Banach space
F_{\varrho}
.

Proof.

The function
f
and the point
x\notin\mathrm{supp}\,f
will be fixed.
Proposition
7.7
(
a
) shows that the integrand in (
9.1
) is in
F_{\varrho}
for each
u
.

We will construct
a family
S_{r}(u)
, with
0<r<r_{0}
for some
r_{0}>0
, of
F_{\varrho}
-valued simple functions such that for a.a.
u

(9.2)

\left\|\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)-S_{r}(u)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\to 0\qquad\mathrm{as}\quad r\to 0

(the integrand is then called strongly measurable), and also

(9.3)

\int\left\|\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)-S_{r}(u)\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,du\to 0\qquad\mathrm{as}\quad r\to 0.

Then (
9.1
) fulfills the definition of an
F_{\varrho}
-Bochner integral, see
[
18
, Appendix C]
.

To find these simple functions, we start by covering
\mathrm{supp}\,f
with
small cubes
Q_{\nu}\,,
pairwise disjoint and all of side
r
. Here
r>0
is so small that the distance from
x
to the union of these cubes is larger than some
\delta_{0}>0
independent of
r
. The center of
Q_{\nu}
is denoted
d_{\nu}
.

In this proof, we allow all implicit constants to depend on
x,\;\delta_{0}
and
f
.

The integrand
\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)
will be approximated by

S_{r}(u)=\sum_{\nu}\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\,\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}f(y)\,dy\>\chi_{Q_{\nu}}(u).

We then have

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)

\displaystyle\,f(u)-S_{r}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\left(\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)\,f(u)-\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\,f(y)\right)\,dy\>\chi_{Q_{\nu}}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\,\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}(f(u)-f(y))\,dy\,\chi_{Q_{\nu}}(u)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\nu}\left(\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)-\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\right)\,\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\,dy\,f(u)\>\chi_{Q_{\nu}}(u)=:\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{J}.

Starting with
\mathcal{J}
, we claim that

\|\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)-\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\|_{F_{\varrho}}=\mathcal{O}(r)
as
r\to 0
.
As in the proof of Proposition
7.7
(
a
) we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,u)-\widetilde{M}^{\rm{loc}}(x,d_{\nu})\|_{F_{\varrho}}

\displaystyle=e^{-R(x)}\,\|\eta(x,u)\,K_{t}(x,u)-\eta(x,d_{\nu})\,K_{t}(x,d_{\nu})\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-R(x)}\,|\eta(x,u)-\eta(x,d_{\nu})|\,\|K_{t}(x,u)\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}\,

\displaystyle+\,e^{-R(x)}\,\eta(x,d_{\nu})\,\|K_{t}(x,u)-K_{t}(x,d_{\nu})\|_{v(\varrho),(0,1]}\,.

In the sum here, the first term is no larger than constant times
e^{-R(x)}\,r\,\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{K}_{t}(x,u)|\,dt\lesssim r\,|x-u|^{-n}=\mathcal{O}(r)
, again because of
Proposition
7.7
(
a
) and its proof. The second term does not exceed

e^{-R(x)}\,\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{t}(K_{t}(x,u)-K_{t}(x,d_{\nu}))|\,dt\,.

If
w
denotes the vector
u-d_{\nu}
and
\nabla_{2}

the gradient in the second variable, we can write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(K_{t}(x,u)-K_{t}(x,d_{\nu}))

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\,\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla_{2}\,K_{t}(x,d_{\nu}+sw),w\right\rangle\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla_{2}\,\dot{K}_{t}(x,d_{\nu}+sw),w\right\rangle\,ds.

The estimate (
7.16
) shows that

\displaystyle e^{-R(x)}\,\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla_{2}\,\dot{K}_{t}(x,d_{\nu}+sw)|\,ds\lesssim 1.

Since
|w|\lesssim r
, the claim follows, and we see that

\left\|\mathcal{J}\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\lesssim Cr\,|f(u)|\to 0\qquad\mathrm{a.e.}

As for the term
\mathcal{I}
, Proposition
7.7
(
a
) implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}\|_{F_{\varrho}}\lesssim\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\,|f(u)-f(y)|\,dy\>\chi_{Q_{\nu}}(u),

whence

\|\mathcal{I}\|_{F_{\varrho}}\to 0
as
r\to 0
at any Lebesgue point.

Thus the measurability condition (
9.2
) is satisfied.

It remains to verify (
9.3
).
Reasoning as above, one has since
|w|\lesssim r

\int\left\|\mathcal{J}\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,du=\mathcal{O}\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}}\,r\right).

For the term
\mathcal{I}
, we write

\displaystyle\int\left\|\mathcal{I}\right\|_{F_{\varrho}}\,du

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\nu}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\,|f(u)-f(y)|\,dy\,du

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\nu}\int_{Q_{\nu}}\frac{1}{|Q_{\nu}|}\int_{|v|<r\sqrt{n}}\,|f(u)-f(u+v)|\,dv\,du

\displaystyle=r^{-n}\,\int_{|v|<r\sqrt{n}}\,dv\,\sum_{\nu}\,\int_{Q_{\nu}}|f(u)-f(u+v)|\,du,

since
|Q_{\nu}|=r^{n}
. Denoting by
\tau_{v}
the translation operator, we see that the last expression in this formula equals

\displaystyle r^{-n}\,\int_{|v|<r\sqrt{n}}\,dw\,\|f-\tau_{v}\,f\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\sup_{|v|<r\sqrt{n}}\|f-\tau_{v}\,f\|_{L^{1}}.

Since translation is continuous in the
L^{1}
norm, this tends to 0 with
r
.

Altogether, we have proved (
9.3
),
and so the integral (
9.1
) is
{F_{\varrho}}
-Bochner.
∎
