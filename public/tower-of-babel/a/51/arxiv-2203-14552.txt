Poisson–Lie Group structures on semidirect products Thanks: The authors were partially supported by the NFR funded project 300837 “Quantum Symmetry”.
Floris Elzinga, Makoto Yamashita

Abstract
. We look at the Poisson structure on the total space of the dual bundle to the Lie algebroid arising from a matched pair of Lie groups. This dual bundle, with the natural semidirect product group structure, becomes a Poisson–Lie group as suggested by a recent work of Stachura. Moreover, when we start from matched pairs given by the Iwasawa decomposition of simple Lie groups, the associated Lie bialgebra is coboundary.

1.
Introduction

Matched pairs of subgroups have been used to produce interesting examples of Hopf algebras
[
19
]
in the 80’s, and were further elaborated upon by Majid
[
11
]
and collaborators in the theory of quantum groups, where the primary motivating example is the Drinfeld double.

More recently in
[
17
]
[
18
]
, Stachura gave a groupoid quantization of the
\kappa
-Poincaré group
[
24
]
.
His model is based on a Lie groupoid arising from a matched pair of subgroups in
\mathrm{SO}(N+1,1)
, and its associated Lie algebroid.
The total space of the dual bundle of a Lie algebroid can be given the structure of a Poisson manifold
[
2
]
, and the main result of
[
17
]
amounts to identifying this Poisson structure with that of the
\kappa
-Poincaré group.

Motivated by this work, we look at the general case in the framework of matched pairs of Lie groups, and a particular case arising from the Iwasawa decomposition for real simple Lie groups.

In the general setting, suppose that
B,C\subset G
is a matched pair of Lie groups, with their Lie algebras
\mathfrak{b}
,
\mathfrak{c}
, and
\mathfrak{g}
.
The induced action of
C
on
B
defines a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{B}
with base
B
(and vice versa).
The dual bundle
E
of the associated Lie algebroid is trivializable, and the fibres can be identified with the orthogonal complement
\mathfrak{b}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
of
\mathfrak{b}
.

The starting observation is that
B
naturally acts on
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
, whence
E
has a semidirect group structure by combining this action with the linear group structure on
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
Our main result (Theorem
3.2
) is that the Poisson structure on
E
is multiplicative with respect to this group structure.
Hence
E
becomes a Poisson–Lie group.

This can be further motivated by the fact that, when
BC\subset G
is dense, the operator algebra associated with
\mathcal{G}_{B}
represents a locally compact quantum group, namely the bicrossed product of
B
and
C

[
1
]
.
In this scheme, the crossed product of
C
with the function algebra of
B
corresponds to the quantized algebra of functions on the Poisson–Lie group
E
.
However, as the action of
B
on
C
is not by group automorphisms, we ‘break’ the group structure on
C
and look at the semidirect product of
\mathfrak{b}^{0}\simeq\mathfrak{c}^{*}
by
B
.

As an example, when we start from the matched pair of
\mathrm{U}(1)
and the
(ax+b)
-group in
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
, we get a double cover of the
\mathrm{E}(2)
-group as
E
.
The corresponding Poisson structure is essentially the one studied by Maślanka in
[
12
]
.

One interesting feature of this Poisson–Lie group is that the associated cobracket on its Lie algebra is coboundary.
Motivated by this, we show that the cobracket is always coboundary for the matched pairs arising from the Iwasawa decomposition of real simple Lie groups (Theorem
4.1
).

The paper is organized as follows:
in Section
2
we collect some preliminary material and fix our conventions.
In Section
3
, we prove our first main result.
We also include a small discussion on the deformation quantization picture.
In Section
4
, we turn to matched pairs in real simple Lie groups and prove our second main result.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Conventions

Given a (real) vector space
V
, we denote its linear dual by
V^{*}
, and the complement of a subspace
W\subset V
by

W^{0}=\{\phi\in V^{*}\mid\forall w\in W\colon\phi(w)=0\}.

We identify the exterior power
{\textstyle\bigwedge}^{*}V
with a subspace of the tensor power
\mathcal{T}^{*}V
in such a way that the duality pairing satisfies

\langle x\wedge y,\phi\otimes\psi\rangle=\phi(x)\psi(y)-\psi(x)\phi(y)

for
x,y\in V
and
\phi,\psi\in V^{*}
.

For (real) Lie groups
G
,
A
, etc., we denote their Lie algebras by
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{a}
, etc.
The adjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}
is denoted by
\mathrm{Ad}
, and the coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is by
\mathrm{Ad}^{*}
.
The corresponding Lie algebra actions of
\mathfrak{g}
are denoted by
\mathrm{ad}
and
\mathrm{ad}^{*}
, so we have
\mathrm{ad}(x)(y)=[x,y]
and

\langle\mathrm{ad}^{*}(x)(\phi),y\rangle=-\langle\phi,\mathrm{ad}(x)(y)\rangle=\phi([y,x]).

When
B,C
are subgroups of
G
,
BC
denotes the set of elements of the form
bc\in G
for
b\in B
and
c\in C
.

2.2.
Matched pairs and Associated Structures

Let
G
be a second countable locally compact Hausdorff topological group.
By a
matched pair of subgroups
of
G
, we mean a pair of closed subgroups
B,C
such that
B\cap C=\{e\}
and that
BC
is open in
G
.
Thus, any element
g
in the open set
BC\cap CB
has unique factorizations
g=bc=c^{\prime}b^{\prime}
for
b,b^{\prime}\in B
and
c,c^{\prime}\in C
.

Given such a matched pair, we have a groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,B,C}
(denoted by
\Gamma_{B}
in
[
17
]
)
defined as follows:

•

base space:
\mathcal{G}_{G,B,C}^{(0)}=B

•

arrow space:
\mathcal{G}_{G,B,C}^{(1)}=BC\cap CB

•

range and source maps:
r(g)=b
,
s(g)=b^{\prime}
for
g=bc=c^{\prime}b^{\prime}
as above

•

composition:
g\circ g^{\prime}=bcc^{\prime\prime}
(product in
G
) when
g=bc=c^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}=b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}

The composition is well-defined as we have

bcc^{\prime\prime}=gb^{\prime-1}g^{\prime}=c^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}

when
g^{\prime}=b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}=c^{\prime\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}
.
We will write
\mathcal{G}_{B}=\mathcal{G}_{G,B,C}
.

If in addition
G
is a Lie group,
\mathcal{G}_{B}
becomes a Lie groupoid.
The Lie algebras form a matched pair
[
11
]
*Section 4:
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{b}\oplus\mathfrak{c}
as vector space, with
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
sitting inside as subalgebras.
There is a Haar system on the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{B}

[
9
]
, hence its groupoid C
∗
-algebras make sense.
If
G
is a
double Lie group
, i.e.,
G=BC
, these algebras are nothing but the crossed products for the corresponding action of
C
on
C_{0}(B)
.

In general, when
BC
is dense in
G
the partial action of
C
on
B
, whose graph is
\mathcal{G}_{B}
, is densely defined so that
C
acts on
L^{\infty}(B)
, and the associated crossed product von Neumann algebra
M=C\ltimes L^{\infty}(B)
admits the structure of a semi-regular locally compact quantum group, which is regular when
G=BC

[
1
]
.
Its dual algebra is given by
\hat{M}=L^{\infty}(C)\rtimes B
.
In particular, the associated reduced C
∗
-algebras are given by the reduced groupoid C
∗
-algebras
A=C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
and
\hat{A}=C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}_{G,C,B})
.

2.3.
Lie Groupoids and Lie Algebroids

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Lie groupoid with base
M=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.
Then we get a Lie algebroid
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
on
M
in the standard way, as follows.
As a vector bundle, it is given by
\mathcal{L}=\ker(\differential r\colon\iota^{*}T\mathcal{G}\to TM)
, where
\iota\colon M\to\mathcal{G}
is the embedding as identity morphisms in
\mathcal{G}
.
The bracket on
\Gamma(\mathcal{L})
is given by identifying it with the space of left invariant vector fields on
\mathcal{G}
, and restricting the usual bracket on
\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{G})=\Gamma(T\mathcal{G})
.
The anchor map
\mathsf{a}\colon\mathcal{L}\to TM
is the restriction of
\differential s
.

If
G
is a Lie group and its subgroups
B,C
form a matched pair in
G
, then the Lie algebroid
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
can be modeled on
B\times\mathfrak{c}
.
Namely, at
b\in B
, elements of the fiber
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})_{b}
correspond to the tangent vectors at
b
with integral curve
b\exp(t y)
for
y\in\mathfrak{c}
.
Equivalently, we use the left translation map
L_{b}\colon g\mapsto bg
to identify
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})_{b}
with
\mathfrak{c}
.
This gives the trivialization

(1)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})\cong B\times\mathfrak{c}.

3.
Poisson–Lie Groups from Matched Pairs

3.1.
Poisson Structures from Lie Algebroids

Let
G
be a Lie group, and
B
,
C
be its subgroups forming a matched pair.
Let us look in detail at the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{B}
over
B
and the associated Lie algebroid
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
.

Consider
E=(TB)^{0}
, the (total space of the) subbundle of
T^{*}G|_{B}=\iota^{*}T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}
orthogonal to
TB\subset\iota^{*}T\mathcal{G}_{B}
.
We identify
E
with
\mathfrak{b}^{0}\times B
by
right
translations.
Using this presentation, we interpret it as the semidirect product for the natural action of
B
on
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
That is, given
g=(v,a)
and
h=(w,b)
in
\mathfrak{b}^{0}\times B
, we put

\displaystyle gh

\displaystyle=(v+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}w,ab),

\displaystyle g^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a^{-1}}w,a^{-1}\right).

This is consistent with viewing
E
as a subgroup of
T^{*}G
, which has a semidirect product structure
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rtimes G
coming from
\mathrm{Ad}^{*}
.
Let us write the Lie algebra of
E
as
\mathfrak{e}=\mathfrak{b}^{0}\oplus\mathfrak{b}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The action of
g=(v,a)
for
\mathrm{Ad}^{E}
is as follows,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi

\displaystyle(\psi\in\mathfrak{b}^{0}),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y-\mathrm{ad}^{*}(\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y)(v)

\displaystyle(y\in\mathfrak{c}).

Proof.

First let us consider
\psi
.
The vector
\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}(\psi)
is the differential at
t=0
of the integral curve
g(t\psi,e)g^{-1}
.
By the commutativity of
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
, this is equal to

\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}t\psi,e\right)=\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi.

As for
y
, the vector
\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}(y_{i})
is the differential of the integral curve
g(v,\exp(t y))g^{-1}
.
Computing the adjoint by
g
, we get

\left(v-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a\exp(t y)a^{-1}}v,a\exp(t y)a^{-1}\right).

Its differential is indeed
\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y-\mathrm{ad}^{*}(\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y)(v)
.
∎

Note that
E
is isomorphic to the dual vector bundle of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
by the duality between
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
Let us describe the induced Poisson structure on
E

[
2
]
.
The bracket is defined on fiberwise linear functions
\tilde{X}
on
E
coming from the sections
X
of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
, and pullbacks
\pi^{*}(f)
of the smooth functions on
B
, as

(4)

\displaystyle\left\{\widetilde{X_{1}},\widetilde{X_{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\left[X_{1},X_{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\left\{\widetilde{X},\pi^{*}(f_{1})\right\}

\displaystyle=\pi^{*}\left(\mathsf{a}(X)f\right),

\displaystyle\left\{\pi^{*}(f_{1}),\pi^{*}(f_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle=0.

To obtain a more concrete formula for the second relation, let us denote the projections from
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{b}\oplus\mathfrak{c}
to
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
by
P_{\mathfrak{b}}
and
P_{\mathfrak{c}}
respectively.
Then, given
y\in\mathfrak{c}
and
a\in B
, the corresponding section (up to the trivialization (
1
))
X^{L}_{y}
of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{B})
satisfies

(5)

\mathsf{a}(X^{L}_{y})(a)=(\differential R_{a})_{e}P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y

with respect to the right translation map
R_{a}\colon b\to ba
.

Our first goal is to prove the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

The Poisson bracket on
E
characterized by (
4
), together with the semidirect product group structure, defines a Poisson–Lie group structure.

Let us first start with an observation: there is a nondegenerate linear duality pairing between
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
given as the restriction of the canonical paring between
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, while
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
is invariant under the transformations
\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}
for
a\in B
.
We then obtain an action of
B
on
\mathfrak{c}
by duality.
If
a\in B
and
y\in\mathfrak{c}
, the vector
P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y\in\mathfrak{c}
satisfies

\langle P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y,\phi\rangle=\langle y,\mathrm{Ad}_{a^{-1}}^{*}\phi\rangle

for
\phi\in\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
Thus, the operators
(P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a})_{a\in B}
give this action of
B
on
\mathfrak{c}
.

Let us fix a basis
(y_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathfrak{c}
, and take its dual basis
(\psi^{i})_{i\in I}
in
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
with respect to the duality pairing.
Then the element
t=\sum_{i\in I}\psi^{i}\otimes y_{i}
is invariantly defined, and we have

(6)

\sum_{i}\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\otimes P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i}=\sum_{i}\psi^{i}\otimes y_{i}.

The candidate group
1
-cocycle
E\to\bigwedge^{2}\mathfrak{e}
for our bracket is the function
\eta=\eta_{0}+\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}
, with the factors

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{a}[y_{i},y_{j}]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j},

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},

where we write
g=(v,a)
as above.

Lemma 3.3
.

The Poisson bivector
\Pi\in\Gamma(E,\bigwedge^{2}TE)
for (
4
) is given by
\Pi_{g}=(\differential R_{g})_{e}^{\otimes 2}\eta(g)
.

Proof.

We have to check

\langle\Pi_{g},\differential f\otimes\differential f^{\prime}\rangle=\{f,f^{\prime}\}(g)

for functions
f,f^{\prime}
of the form either
\tilde{X}
or
\pi^{*}(f^{\prime\prime})
.
The third relation in (
4
) is obviously satisfied as
\eta(g)
does not have components in
\bigwedge^{2}\mathfrak{b}
.

Note that we have

(7)

\begin{split}\left\langle(\differential\widetilde{y_{i}})_{g},(\differential R_{g})_{e}\mathopen{}\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}\right)\right\rangle&=\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}\widetilde{y_{i}}\mathopen{}\left(v+t\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j},a\right)\\
&=\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}\left\langle v+t\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j},\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i}\right\rangle\\
&=\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}t\left\langle\psi^{j},y_{i}\right\rangle=\delta_{i,j}.\end{split}

Here, the adjoint action of
a
on
y_{i}
comes from the fact that we are comparing left translates from the Lie algebroid with right translates in
E
.
This, combined with (
5
), implies the second relation in (
4
) for
\Pi
.

Finally, we have

\begin{split}\left\langle(\differential\widetilde{y_{i}})_{g},(\differential R_{g})_{e}\left(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right)\right\rangle&=\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}\left\langle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{\exp(t P_{\fr{b}}\Ad_a y_i)}(v),\mathrm{Ad}_{\exp(t P_{\fr{b}}\Ad_a y_i)a}(y_{j})\right\rangle\\
&=\left.\derivative{t}\right|_{t=0}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right\rangle\\
&=0.\end{split}

Combining this with (
7
), we obtain the first relation in (
4
) for
\Pi
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

With
g\in E
presented by
(v,a)\in\mathfrak{b}^{0}\times B
, we have

(8)

\eta_{0}(g)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j}-\mathrm{Ad}_{a}^{*}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v).

Proof.

The coefficient of
\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}
in
\eta_{0}(g)
can be written as

\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{a}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle=\left\langle v,P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle=\left\langle v,P_{\mathfrak{c}}\left[\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\right\rangle

using the assumption
v\in\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
The second term on the right hand side can be expanded as

P_{\mathfrak{c}}\left[\left(P_{\mathfrak{b}}+P_{\mathfrak{c}}\right)\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},\left(P_{\mathfrak{b}}+P_{\mathfrak{c}}\right)\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\\
=\left[P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]+P_{\mathfrak{c}}\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]+P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathopen{}\left[P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]

which we pair with
v
.

Then, using the invariance (
6
), we can write

\displaystyle\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}

\displaystyle=\left\langle v,\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j},

\displaystyle\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}

\displaystyle=\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j},

\displaystyle\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}

\displaystyle=\left\langle v,\left[y_{i},P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}.

Notice that the last two terms are the same after swapping the order of the bracket and the wedge product and renaming dummy indices.

Finally, by definition

\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle=-\left\langle\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v),y_{j}\right\rangle,

so that we can now complete one of the summations to get that

\left\langle v,\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j}=-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v).

Combining these we obtain the claim.
∎

Now we are ready for the proof of our first main result.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

By Lemma
3.3
, it is enough to show that
\eta
satisfies the cocycle condition

\eta(gh)=\eta(g)+\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta(h),

or equivalently,

\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta(h)=\eta(gh)-\eta(g).

We proceed by computing the left-hand side for the two pieces
\eta_{0}
and
\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}
separately, and then compare.
In the following we write
g=(v,a)
and
h=(w,b)
.

First we expand
\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta_{0}(h)
as

\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,\mathrm{Ad}_{b}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}w,\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}\\
=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}w,\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}.

We recognize the first term as
\eta_{0}(gh)
, so we focus on the second term.
If we apply our formula (
8
) to it, we find

\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j}-\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}^{*}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v).

The relation (
8
) also implies that

\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\psi^{i}\wedge\psi^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{a}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v).

Combining the two yields

\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{j}=\\
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}_{a}\mathopen{}\left[y_{i},y_{j}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{j}+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v)\\
-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v).

The first term on the right-hand side is precisely
\eta_{0}(g)
, so we now have

(9)

\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta_{0}(h)=\\
\eta_{0}(gh)-\eta_{0}(g)-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v)+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v).

\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(h)
is equal to

\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\left[\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i}-\mathrm{ad}^{*}(\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i})(v)\right]

by (
2
) and (
3
).
To obtain projections in the right places, write

\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i}=P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i}=P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}\mathopen{}\left(1-P_{\mathfrak{c}}\right)\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i}.

We proceed by exploiting the duality again,

\begin{split}\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\left[P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i}-P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i}\right]&=\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i}-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i}\\
&=\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(gh)-\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(g).\end{split}

This implies

\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(h)=\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(gh)-\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(g)-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i}-P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}P_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathrm{Ad}_{b}y_{i})(v),

and by invariance (

) we have

(10)

\begin{split}\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(h)&=\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(gh)-\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(g)\\
&\quad-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v)+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v).\end{split}

Combining (
9
) and (
10
), we find

\begin{split}\left(\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\otimes\mathrm{Ad}^{E}_{g}\right)\left(\eta_{0}(h)+\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(h)\right)&=\eta_{0}(gh)-\eta_{0}(g)+\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(gh)-\eta_{\mathfrak{b}}(g)\\
&\quad+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v)-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{ab}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{ab}y_{i})(v)\\
&\quad-\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v)+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{a}\psi^{i}\wedge\mathrm{ad}^{*}(P_{\mathfrak{b}}\mathrm{Ad}_{a}y_{i})(v)\\
&=\eta(gh)-\eta(g),\end{split}

as desired.

3.2.
Alternative Proof of Theorem
3.2
for Double Lie Groups

It was pointed out to us by P. Stachura that Theorem
3.2
can also be derived from work of Zakrzewski
[
23
]
when the matched pair decomposition is global.
This situation is referred to as a double Lie group.
We will freely use the terminology and notation of
[
16
]
.
Now we give a sketch of the argument.

Let
G=BC
be a double Lie group and consider the groupoids
\mathcal{G}_{B}
and
\mathcal{G}_{C}=\mathcal{G}_{G,C,B}
.
Write
m_{C}
for the multiplication relation of
\mathcal{G}_{C}
.
We begin by taking its transpose, which gives the relation

\displaystyle m_{C}^{T}(g)=\left\{\left.(b_{1}c,cb_{2})\right|b_{1},b_{2}\in B,c\in C,b_{1}cb_{2}=g\right\}.

This can be viewed as a relation
m_{C}^{T}\colon\mathcal{G}_{B}\rightarrowtriangle\mathcal{G}_{B}\times\mathcal{G}_{B}
and turns out to be a (Zakrzewski) morphism of Lie groupoids.

We now apply the phase lift functor to
\mathcal{G}_{B}
and the morphism
m_{C}^{T}
.
The phase lift of a differentiable relation
r\colon X\rightarrowtriangle Y
is a new relation
Pr\colon T^{*}X\rightarrowtriangle T^{*}Y
with graph

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)\in Gr(Pr)~\Leftrightarrow~\forall(u,v)\in T_{(\pi_{Y}(\alpha),\pi_{X}(\beta))}Gr(r):\langle\alpha,u\rangle=\langle\beta,v\rangle

The resulting groupoid
P\mathcal{G}_{B}
has as arrow space the total space of the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}
.
However, the base space is not
T^{*}B
, as one might expect.
The unit relation
e\colon\{1\}\rightarrowtriangle\mathcal{G}_{B}
becomes a relation
Pe\colon\{1\}\times\{0\}\rightarrowtriangle T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}
.
Because the original base space was
B
, it follows that the conditions for
(g,X^{*})\in T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}
to lie in
Gr(Pe)
are that
g\in B
and that
X^{*}\in\mathfrak{b}^{0}
, as the fibre of the tangent and cotangent bundle of
\{1\}
is the zero vector space.

We now claim that the base map of the phase lift
Pm_{C}^{T}
is precisely the group operation of
(TB)^{0}
.
The graph of the base map is the transpose of the intersection

\displaystyle Gr(Pm_{C}^{T})\cap(TB)^{0}\times(TB)^{0}\times(TB)^{0}\subset\bigl(T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}\times T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}\bigr)\times T^{*}\mathcal{G}_{B}.

Let
\bigl((b_{1},\psi_{1}),(b_{2},\psi_{2}),(b,\psi)\bigr)\in(TB)^{0}\times(TB)^{0}\times(TB)^{0}
.
Note that
(b_{1},b_{2},b)
lies in the graph of
m_{C}^{T}
if and only if
b=b_{1}b_{2}
.
We describe the tangent space to
Gr(m_{C}^{T})
at
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{1}b_{2})
.
Any tangent vector in the direction of
B
is not important, because
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi
are from
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
.
Take
X\in\mathfrak{c}
instead, then we get a curve

\displaystyle\left(b_{1}e^{tX},e^{tX}b_{2},b_{1}e^{tX}b_{2}\right)=\left(\left(b_{1}e^{tX}b_{1}^{-1}\right)b_{1},e^{tX}b_{2},\left(b_{1}e^{tX}b_{1}^{-1}\right)b_{1}b_{2}\right).

Hence we get the tangent vectors
(\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{1}}(X),X,\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{1}}(X))
, or equivalently
(Y,\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{1}^{-1}}(Y),Y)
.
Plugging this into the equation defining the graph of
Pm_{C}^{T}
, we find the condition that

\displaystyle\psi(Y)

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}(Y)+\psi_{2}(\mathrm{Ad}_{b_{1}^{-1}}(Y))

\displaystyle=\left(\psi_{1}+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{b_{1}}\psi_{2}\right)(Y).

Therefore the base map of
Pm_{C}^{T}
is

\displaystyle\left((b_{1},\psi_{1}),(b_{2},\psi_{2})\right)\mapsto\left(b_{1}b_{2},\psi_{1}+\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{b_{1}}\psi_{2}\right),

as claimed.

Then the phase lift of a Zakrzewski morphism produces a morphism of symplectic groupoids and that the base map of such a morphism is always a Poisson map, see
[
23
]
*Section 5.

3.4.
Deformation Quantization

Let us explain an analogue of strict deformation quantization, in the framework of unbounded multipliers.
The bounded picture, that is, a C
∗
-algebraic strict deformation quantization in the sense of Rieffel
[
14
]
, is already provided in
[
10
]
.

Let
U(\mathfrak{c})
be the complexified universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{c}
, i.e., the universal associative unital
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by a copy of
\mathfrak{c}
as its real subspace, with relations
xy-yx=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{c}}
for
x,y\in\mathfrak{c}
.
The Hopf algebra
U(\mathfrak{c})
acts on
C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
, and the elements of the crossed product
U(\mathfrak{c})\ltimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
can be regarded as unbounded multipliers of

, see Appendix
A
.

Since
\Sym(\mathfrak{c})
can be identified with the space of complex polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{b}^{0}
, the elements of
\Sym(\mathfrak{c})\otimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
can be regarded as unbounded multipliers of
C_{0}(E)
.
Let us consider the corresponding bracket on
\Sym(\mathfrak{c})\otimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
.

By choosing an ordered basis of
\mathfrak{c}
, by the same argument as the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt Theorem, we get a linear isomorphism

Q\colon\Sym(\mathfrak{c})\otimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)\to U(\mathfrak{c})\ltimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)

which is compatible with the filtrations by degree in
\mathfrak{c}
.
For an auxiliary parameter
h
, define
Q_{h}\colon\Sym(\mathfrak{c})\otimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)\to U(\mathfrak{c})\ltimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
by

Q_{h}(y_{1}\dots y_{k}\otimes f)=h^{k}Q(y_{1}\dots y_{k}\otimes f).

Proposition 3.5
.

We have

\frac{[Q_{h}(T\otimes f),Q_{h}(T^{\prime}\otimes f^{\prime})]}{h}=Q_{h}(\{T\otimes f,T^{\prime}\otimes f^{\prime}\})+O(h)

for
T,T^{\prime}\in\Sym(\mathfrak{c})
and
f,f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
.

Proof.

For linear polynomials, we have

\frac{[Q_{h}(y\otimes 1),Q_{h}(y^{\prime}\otimes 1)]}{h}=Q_{h}(\{y\otimes 1,y^{\prime}\otimes 1\})\quad(y,y^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{c})

with exact equality, from the structure of
U(\mathfrak{c})
.
(
y\otimes 1
corresponds to the function
\widetilde{X^{L}_{y}}
.)
For general elements
T,T^{\prime}\in\Sym(\mathfrak{c})
, induction on degree gives

\frac{[Q_{h}(T\otimes 1),Q_{h}(T^{\prime}\otimes 1)]}{h}=Q_{h}(\{T\otimes 1,T^{\prime}\otimes 1\})+O(h^{2}).

Between
\mathfrak{c}
and
C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
, we again have

\frac{[Q_{h}(y\otimes 1),Q_{h}(1\otimes f)]}{h}=Q_{h}(\{y\otimes 1,1\otimes f\})\quad(y\in\mathfrak{c},f\in C^{\infty}_{b}(B))

with exact equality, from the way the anchor map is defined.
(
1\otimes f
corresponds to the function
\pi^{*}f
.)
For general
T\in\Sym(\mathfrak{c})
, again by induction on degree we get

\frac{[Q_{h}(T\otimes 1),Q_{h}(1\otimes f)]}{h}=Q_{h}(\{T\otimes 1,1\otimes f\})+O(h).

Finally, we also have
[Q_{h}(1\otimes f),Q_{h}(1\otimes f^{\prime})]=0
.
∎

There is a structure of a Hopf algebra (up to completion) on

corresponding to the bicrossed product structure, as follows.
On

we consider the one coming from the group structure of

, implemented as

(We can also take

We mix this with the usual cocommutative coproduct

, using the action of

\mathfrak{c}
, as follows.

Note that

is identified with the space of smooth functions

with finite dimensional images.
For
T\otimes f
with

and

, denote by

the function

, where

is the

\mathfrak{c}
.
Since

We then put

\Delta(T\otimes f)=(T_{(1)}\otimes\theta(T_{(2)}\otimes\Delta(f)))_{1324}\quad(T\in U(\mathfrak{c}),f\in C^{\infty}_{b}(B)),

\displaystyle\left[y_{i},\psi^{j}\right]

Lemma 4.2

For

\displaystyle y^{(2)}

4.3.
Another Deformation Scheme

Beyond the case of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{e}^{*}
could be non-isomorphic from
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, which can be directly seen for the above examples
\mathfrak{su}(p,1)
.

Nonetheless, let us mention another similar construction that does relate
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{e}
.
Take the Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
.
Then we have
\mathfrak{e}\simeq\mathfrak{p}\rtimes\mathfrak{k}
, where we treat
\mathfrak{p}
as a vector space with an action of
\mathfrak{k}
.
To recover
\mathfrak{g}
from
\mathfrak{e}
, we just need to use the restriction of the bracket map
\bigwedge^{2}\mathfrak{p}\to\mathfrak{k}
as a cocycle (say
c
), and deform the bracket of
\mathfrak{e}
.
Moreover, as the standard maximal compact form
\mathfrak{g}_{c}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is
\mathfrak{k}\oplus i\mathfrak{p}
, the deformation of
\mathfrak{e}
by the inverted cocycle
-c
gives
\mathfrak{g}_{c}
.

On the other hand,
\sigma(\mathfrak{k}^{0})
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
which is stable under
\mathrm{ad}(\mathfrak{k})
and is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{p}
as a
\mathfrak{k}
-module.
Thus, the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathfrak{k}^{0})\rtimes\mathfrak{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\simeq\mathfrak{e}
.
Moreover,
(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is a Manin triple.

Thus, we have three Lie bialgebra structures on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
corresponding to
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{c}
, and
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
.
By the above discussion, the corresponding cobrackets
\delta_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
\delta_{\mathfrak{g}_{c}}
, and
\delta_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}
are different by one map
c^{\prime}\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\bigwedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
dualizing

.

.

Appendix A
Groupoid C
∗
-Algebras of Matched Pairs of Lie Groups

Let
(B,C)
be a matched pair of subgroups of a Lie group
G
.
Let us be precise about our convention of the groupoid C
∗
-algebra of the groupoid
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{B}
.
We are going to make sense of unbounded multipliers coming from elements of
\mathfrak{c}
.

First, let us fix our convention of Haar system on
\mathcal{G}
.
We mostly follow the convention of
[
13
]
.

Let
\lambda
be a left Haar measure on the Lie group
C
, and
\Delta
be the modular function of
C
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\int_{C}f(cc^{\prime})d\lambda(c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{C}f(c^{\prime})d\lambda(c^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\int_{C}f(c^{\prime}c)d\lambda(c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Delta(c^{-1})\int_{C}f(c^{\prime})d\lambda(c^{\prime})

for all
c\in C
and
f\in C_{c}(C)
.
Let us also write

\Delta(y)=\left.\frac{d\Delta(e^{ty})}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\quad(y\in\mathfrak{c}).

On the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, to avoid the confusion with the group structure of
G
, we write
g\cdot g^{\prime}
for the product of composable arrows and
\tilde{g}
for the groupoid inverse.
Thus, given
g=r(g)c=c^{\prime\prime}s(g)
and
g^{\prime}=s(g)c^{\prime}
with

, we have
g\cdot g^{\prime}=r(g)cc^{\prime}
and
\tilde{g}=c^{{}^{\prime\prime}-1}r(g)=s(g)c^{-1}
.

For a fixed
b\in B
, the set
\mathcal{G}^{b}
can be identified with

C^{(b)}=\{c\in C\mid bc\in CB\},

which is an open subset of
C
by our assumption.
We define the measure
\lambda^{b}
on
\mathcal{G}^{b}
to be the restriction of
\lambda
on
C^{(b)}
up to this identification.
Then the invariance condition

\int f(g\cdot g^{\prime})d\lambda^{s(g)}(g^{\prime})=\int f(g^{\prime})d\lambda^{r(g)}(g^{\prime})\quad(g\in\mathcal{G}_{B},f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}^{r(g)})

follows from the left invariance of
\lambda
.
By
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
and
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
, we mean the full and reduced groupoid C
∗
-algebras associated with this Haar system.

Next let us make sense of the associated the left derivation in direction of

as an unbounded multiplier on
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
(or on
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
).
We follow the approach of
[
8
]
*Chapters 9 and 10.
We consider the
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
-valued inner product on
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, defined as

\langle f,f^{\prime}\rangle(g)=f^{*}*f^{\prime}(g)=\int\bar{f}(\tilde{g}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{g})f^{\prime}(\tilde{g}^{\prime})d\lambda^{s(g)}(g^{\prime}).

Let us fix

, and formally write

(u^{s}*f)(g)=\Delta(e^{sy})^{1/2}f(e^{-sy}c^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime})\quad(g=c^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime}).

When
f
is compactly supported, this is well-defined for small

by our openness assumption of
\mathcal{G}\subset G
.
Moreover, we have

(15)

(u^{s}*f)*f^{\prime}=u^{s}*(f*f^{\prime})

from left invariance of

, and

(16)

(u^{s}*f)^{*}*f^{\prime}=f^{*}*(u^{-s}*f^{\prime})

from the defining property of

, whenever both sides are well-defined.
In particular the transform
f\mapsto u^{s}*f
preserves the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Now, we define the operator
t^{0}_{y}
on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{G})

t^{0}_{y}f=i\left.\frac{d}{ds}u^{s}*f\right|_{s=0}=\frac{i}{2}\Delta(y)f+iX_{y}f,

where

is the smooth vector field on

whose integral curve is

.
By (

), this is a map of right

-modules.
By (

), we also have

\langle t^{0}_{y}f,f^{\prime}\rangle-\langle f,t^{0}_{y}f^{\prime}\rangle=0.

Polynomials of

make sense as operators on

Looking at the commutators we have

(note that

Next, let

For

f\in C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
and
f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{G})
, we have
f*f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{G})
.
Given
y\in\mathfrak{c}
, the differentiation of the action of
e^{sy}
on
B
defines a vector field
X^{\prime}_{y}
on
B
.
We then have the commutation relation

t^{0}_{y}(f*f^{\prime})-f*(t^{0}_{y}f^{\prime})=iX^{\prime}_{y}(f)*f^{\prime}\quad(f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{G}),

giving a realization of the algebra
U(\mathfrak{c})\ltimes C^{\infty}_{b}(B)
inside the space of unbounded multipliers of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
.

In the case of double Lie group,
G=BC
, we can interpret
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
as the full crossed product
C_{0}(B)\rtimes C
for the induced action of
C
on
B
.
In this case there is a unital
*
-homomorphism
\mathcal{M}(C^{*}(C))\to\mathcal{M}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}))
, and
t^{0}_{y}
has an extension to an unbounded selfadjoint element affiliated to
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, see
[
20
]
.
