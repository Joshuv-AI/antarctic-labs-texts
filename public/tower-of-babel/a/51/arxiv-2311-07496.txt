Introduction and Statement of Results

At first glance, one might underestimate the value of the trivial observation that the number of partitions of an integer
n
into identical parts is also the number of divisors of
n
. This fact is a glimpse of a rich theory that relates integer partitions and divisor functions.
Indeed, MacMahon’s important paper
[
7
]
is based on the idea of connecting partitions to divisor sums: partition
of
n
using
k_{1}
repeated
s_{1}
times, and
k_{2}
repeated
s_{2}
times, and so on through
k_{a}
repeated
s_{a}
times. Using this convention, he considered the sum of products of the
multiplicities

MO(a;n):=\sum s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{a}
of size
n

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{a}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}MO(a;n)\,q^{n}=\sum_{0<k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{a}}\frac{q^{k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{a}}}{(1-q^{k_{1}})^{2}(1-q^{k_{2}})^{2}\cdots(1-q^{k_{a}})^{2}}.

His work
[
7
]
is populated with beautiful divisor function identities, where

such as:

(1.2)

\mathcal{U}_{1}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{1}(n)q^{n}\ \ \ \ {\text{\rm and}}\ \ \ \ \mathcal{U}_{2}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(n)}{8}-\frac{n\sigma_{1}(n)}{4}+\frac{\sigma_{3}(n)}{8}\right)q^{n}.

To entice the reader, we offer the first few terms of
\mathcal{U}_{1}(q),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{4}(q)

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}_{1}(q)&=q+3q^{2}+4q^{3}+7q^{4}+6q^{5}+12q^{6}+8q^{7}+\dots,\\

\mathcal{U}_{4}(q)&=q^{10}+3q^{11}+9q^{12}+22q^{13}+51q^{14}+97q^{15}+188q^{16}+\dots.\\
\end{split}

The inequalities in definition (

\lambda=(1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,t^{m_{t}}),
where

Theorem 1.1
.

The following are true:

(i) If
a

q^{-\frac{a(a+1)}{2}}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{a}(q)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{3}}+O(q^{a+1}).

(ii) If

, then we have that

MO(a;n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n-a}\,\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}\left(\frac{2}{h^{2}}+1\right)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n-a}\prod_{s=1}^{n-a}\binom{2+m_{s}}{2}.

Inspired by the
\mathcal{U}_{a}(q),
Amdeberhan-Andrews-Tauraso
[

initiated the study of the
q
-series

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{a}^{\star}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}M(a;n)\,q^{n}=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq\cdots\leq k_{a}}\frac{q^{k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{a}}}{(1-q^{k_{1}})^{2}(1-q^{k_{2}})^{2}\cdots(1-q^{k_{a}})^{2}},

where the strict inequalities in (

\mathcal{U}_{a}^{*}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}M(a;n)q^{n}=q^{a}+(2a+1)q^{a+1}+\dots.

To entice the reader, we offer the first few terms of
\mathcal{U}_{1}^{*}(q),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{4}^{*}(q)

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\star}(q)&=q+3q^{2}+4q^{3}+7q^{4}+6q^{5}+12q^{6}+\cdots,\\

\mathcal{U}_{3}^{\star}(q)&=q^{3}+7q^{4}+27q^{5}+77q^{6}+181q^{7}+378q^{8}+\cdots,\\
\mathcal{U}_{4}^{\star}(q)&=q^{4}+9q^{5}+44q^{6}+156q^{7}+450q^{8}+1121q^{9}+\cdots.\end{split}

In analogy with Theorem
1.1
, we consider the limiting behavior of these series. These series converge to specializations of the generating function for the polynomials

p_{0}(x):=1,p_{1}(x):=2x+1,p_{2}(x):=2x^{2}+3x,p_{3}(x):=\frac{4}{3}x^{3}+4x^{2}+\frac{5}{3}x,\dots.

For

these polynomials are defined by

(1.4)

p_{n}(x):=\binom{2x+n-1}{n}+\binom{2x+n-2}{n-1}.

As a companion to Theorem

Theorem 1.2
.

The following are true:

(i) If
a

q^{-a}\cdot\mathcal{U}^{*}_{a}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{a}p_{n}(a)q^{n}+O(q^{a+1}).

(ii) If

, then we have that

M(a;n)=p_{n-a}(a).

Remark
.

The
\mathcal{U}_{a}(q)
and
\mathcal{U}_{a}^{\star}(q)
are

we recall the
q

[k]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}
and the multiple

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(m_{1},\dots,m_{a}):

\displaystyle=\sum_{0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{a}}\frac{q^{(m_{1}-1)k_{1}+\cdots+(m_{a}-1)k_{a}}}{[k_{1}]_{q}^{m_{1}}\cdots[k_{a}]_{q}^{m_{a}}},

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}^{\star}(m_{1},\dots,m_{a}):

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\cdots\leq k_{a}}\frac{q^{(m_{1}-1)k_{1}+\cdots+(m_{a}-1)k_{a}}}{[k_{1}]_{q}^{m_{1}}\cdots[k_{a}]_{q}^{m_{a}}}.

We have that

and
(1-q)^{2a}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{a}^{\star}(q)=\zeta_{q}^{\star}({2,\ldots,2}).

As divisor functions arise as the coefficients of Eisenstein series, identities such as (

) suggest a strong relationship between the

and quasimodular forms. This speculation was confirmed by Andrews-Rose. Indeed, they proved
(see
[

is a linear combination of quasimodular forms on

with weights
\leq 2a
.
Similarly, Amdeberhan-Andrews-Tauraso
[

proved that each
\mathcal{U}_{a}^{\star}(q)
is a linear combination of quasimodular forms on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with weights
\leq 2a
.

Here we make this quasimodularity explicit. In the case of
\mathcal{U}_{a}(q),
we employ the standard generators of the graded ring of quasimodular forms: the quasimodular weight 2 Eisenstein series

(1.5)

E_{2}(q):=1-24\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}(n)q^{n},

and the weight 4 and 6 modular Eisenstein series

(1.6)

E_{4}(q):=1+240\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{3}(n)q^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{6}(q):=1-504\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{5}(n)q^{n}.

It is well known
[
6
]
that the ring of quasimodular forms is
\mathbb{C}[E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}],
and so our goal is to
obtain formulas in terms of the monomials
E_{2}^{\alpha}(q)E_{4}^{\beta}(q)E_{6}^{\gamma}(q)
, where
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
are non-negative integers.

Our formulas for
\mathcal{U}_{a}(q)
use of the triple index sequence of rational numbers defined by the recursion

\displaystyle c(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{3}(2\alpha+8\beta+12\gamma+1)\cdot c(\alpha-1,\beta,\gamma)+\frac{2}{3}(\alpha+1)\cdot c(\alpha+1,\beta-1,\gamma)

(1.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \ \ +\frac{8}{3}(\beta+1)\cdot c(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma-1)+4(\gamma+1)\cdot c(\alpha,\beta-2,\gamma+1),

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 0.
To seed the recursion, we let
c(0,0,0):=1,
and we let

c(\alpha,\beta,\gamma):=0
if any of the arguments are negative.
Here we list the “first few” values:

\displaystyle c(1,0,0)=-1,\ c(0,1,0)=-\frac{2}{3},\ c(0,0,1)=-\frac{16}{9},\ c(1,1,0)=\frac{14}{3},\ c(1,0,1)=\frac{64}{3},\dots.

We also require constants for the quasimodular summands sorted by weight.
For
0\leq t\leq a
, define

(1.8)

\displaystyle w_{t}(a):=\frac{\binom{2a}{a}}{16^{a}(2a+1)}\sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{t}<a}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\frac{1}{(2\ell_{j}+1)^{2}}.

In terms of

and the numbers

\mathcal{U}_{a}(q).

.

If
a
is a non-negative integer, then we have that

\mathcal{U}_{a}(q)=\sum_{t=0}^{a}w_{t}(a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 0\\
\alpha+2\beta+3\gamma=t\end{subarray}}c(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)E_{2}(q)^{\alpha}E_{4}(q)^{\beta}E_{6}(q)^{\gamma}.

We turn to the

Instead of using

For every non-negative integer

The second proof is a special case of one of three new infinite families of congruences.

Theorem 1.6
.

The following are true:

(i) For every pair of non-negative integers

and

(ii) For every pair of non-negative integers

(iii) For every pair of non-negative integers

However, it turns out that congruences are both rare and ubiquitous.

Theorem 1.7
.

For positive integers
a
and
m
, the following are true:

(i) There are infinitely many non-nested arithmetic progressions
tn+r

(
resp.

)
for which

\begin{split}M(a;tn+r)&\equiv 0\pmod{m},\\
MO(a;t^{*}n+r^{*})&\equiv 0\pmod{m}.\end{split}

(ii) There are infinitely many non-nested arithmetic progressions
tn+r
for which

M(a;tn+r)\equiv MO(a;tn+r)\equiv 0\pmod{m}.

(iii) There exists a positive real number
\alpha(a,m)>0
for which

\begin{split}\#\{n\leq X\ :\ M(a;n)\not\equiv 0\pmod{m}\}&=O\left(X/\log^{\alpha(a,m)}X\right)\\
\#\{n\leq X\ :\ MO(a;n)\not\equiv 0\pmod{m}\}&=O\left(X/\log^{\alpha(a,m)X}\right).\end{split}

In other words, the values
M(a;n)
and

are almost always multiples of any integer

Furthermore, Gordon
[
4
]
proved that

We further claim that

. To prove this congruence, we employ Ramanujan’s weight 12 cusp form

through the following observation:

\Delta^{2}\ |\ T_{17}\equiv 0\pmod{17},
which means that every seventeenth coefficient of

that

\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{17n})^{3}\in(\mathbb{Z}/17\mathbb{Z})[q^{17}].

Now suppose that

and

1\leq a\equiv\ell-1\pmod{\ell}.

\ell\nmid n(n+1)/2,
then we have

\frac{(2n+1)(n+a)!}{(2a+1)!(n-a)!}\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}.

Therefore, the identity in Lemma
2.1
collapses modulo

\mathcal{U}_{a}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}MO(a;n)q^{n}\equiv P_{3}(q)\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}A(a,\ell;\ell n)q^{\ell n}\pmod{\ell}.

and so the

inherit the
