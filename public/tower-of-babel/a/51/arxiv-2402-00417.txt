Presentation of monoids generated by a projection and an involution
Pascal Caron, Jean-Gabriel Luque, Bruno Patrou

Abstract
Monoids generated by elements of order two appear in numerous places in the literature. For example, Coxeter reflection groups in geometry, Kuratowski monoids in topology, various monoids generated by regular operations in language theory and so on. In order to initiate a classification of these monoids, we are interested in the subproblem of monoids, called strict 2-PIMs, generated by an involution and an idempotent. In this case we show, when the monoid is finite, that it is generated by a single equation (in addition to the two defining the involution and the idempotent). We then describe the exact possible forms of this equation and classify them. We recover Kuratowski’s theorem as a special case of our study.



1
Introduction

Kuratowski’s theorem
[
9
]
is a topology result which states that in a topological space, the monoid generated by a closure operation and the complement has at most 14 elements.
Moreover, there exists a topological space and a set for which this bound of 14 is reached.
For more on Kuratowski-type theorems for topological spaces, see Gardner and Jackson
[
7
]
.
Hammer
[
8
]
noticed that such a statement is valid in a more general setting; it is not necessary to consider topological spaces and topological closure. The theorem is also valid if, instead of topological closure, we use a closure operator on a set X.
Since Kleene’s star is a closure operation on formal languages, Peleg
[
10
]
was the first to extend Kuratowski’s result to operations in this context.

An orbit is a set of values corresponding to the different numbers of sets that can be obtained from operations. Dassow refines Kuratowski’s work by taking (formal) languages as a set and involution and closure as operations
[
4
,
5
]
, then extends his work to Boolean functions
[
6
]
. In the same framework and also for languages, Charlier
et al.

[
1
]
show that for a large number of operations, they also obtain finite orbits.
D. Sherman
[
11
]
generalizes Kuratowski’s theorem by considering, in addition to closures and complement, interior union and intersection operations.

Idempotency is a mathematical property of a function which states that the successive application of this function to its parameter is equal to the application once. This property arises both in the theory of projectors and in that of closure operations.

An involution, involutory function, or self-inverse function is a function that is its own inverse. This notion applies equally to projective geometry, where the square is the identity transformation, and to algebra, where an involution of an algebraic variety is an automorphism of the variety of order two.

An operation that generates a two-element monoid is either an involution or an idempotent operation. While the groups generated by only involutions have been extensively studied (see
e.g.
Coxeter
[
2
,
3
]
), adding idempotent operations in the generator sets gives rise to more complicated structures that have not been classified yet.

In the same vein, we propose to include Kuratowski’s theorem in a more general study: monoids generated by exactly one involution, not necessarily the complement, and one idempotent operation, not necessarily a closure one.

2
Preliminaries

A
monoid
is a set closed under an associative binary product having a neutral element denoted by
\mathrm{\emph{Id}}
. The juxtaposition of two elements represents their product, the operation symbol being omitted. A presentation of a monoid is a couple
\langle\cal{G}\mid\cal{R}\rangle
where
\mathcal{G}
is a set of generators and
\mathcal{R}
a set of equations inducing an equivalence relation between elements of the monoid.
The sets
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{R}
should be infinite but for this paper we restrict ourselves to finite sets and if
\mathcal{R}=\emptyset
we recover the free monoid
\mathcal{G}^{*}
. The presented monoid
\mathrm{M}=\langle\mathcal{G},\mathcal{R}\rangle
is isomorphic to the quotient of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
by the congruence induced by the equations
\mathcal{R}
, i.e.
\mathrm{M}=\mathcal{G}^{*}/\equiv_{\mathcal{R}}
. For example, the presentation
\langle a,b\mid ab=ba\rangle
designates the commutative monoid over the alphabet
\{a,b\}
where each element is equivalent to one of the form
a^{p}b^{q}
(
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
). To lighten the notations, the braces of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{R}
are omitted. A
word

w
is an element of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
. For any
a\in\mathcal{G}
, we denote by
|w|_{a}
the number of
a
in
w
. The
order
of a monoid is its number of elements.

A monoid generated by only one element,
\boxempty
, is said
monogenic
. All infinite monogenic monoids are isomorphic to the semigroup of natural integers equipped with the addition. The presentation of such a monoid is reduced to
\langle\boxempty\mid\rangle
and its elements are
\mathrm{Id},\boxempty,\boxempty^{2},\boxempty^{3},\cdots
. For any integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists, up to an isomorphism, exactly
n
monogenic monoids of order
n
. Their presentations are of the form
\langle\boxempty\mid\boxempty^{n}=\boxempty^{k}\rangle
, with
k\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}
and their extensive description is
\{\mathrm{Id},\boxempty,\boxempty^{2},\cdots,\boxempty^{n-1}\}
. The
order
of an element
\boxempty
is the order of the monoid generated by
\boxempty
.

The structure of monoids generated by two elements (
digenic
monoids) is much more complicated. In this paper, we focus on digenic monoids generated by a set of elements of order two. Such generators are either
projections
(idempotent operators
\boxempty
such that
\boxempty^{2}=\boxempty
) or
involutions
(operators
\Diamond
such that
\Diamond^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
). We, then, call
PIM
(for Projection-Involution-Monoid) any monoid generated by only generators of order
2
and
2-PIM
any monoid generated by exactly two generators of order
2
.

If both generators,
\boxempty
and
\Diamond
, are involutive then their roles are symmetrical and two situations can occur.

1.

There exists a smallest integer
p
such that
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}=\mathrm{Id}
. In fact, if it is not the case, we may have

(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}
then by multiplying both sides by
\Diamond
, we have
\Diamond(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}\boxempty\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}
. Then multiplying these two equalities, we have
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{2p+2}=\mathrm{Id}
. We have also
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}
implies
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}=\boxempty
and thus
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{2p}=\mathrm{Id}
. Then from the previous equations, we have
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
. Thus if
p
is even then
\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}
and if
p
is odd then
\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}
and so this case is degenerated.

In the non-degenerated case, the monoid

\langle\boxempty,\Diamond\mid\boxempty^{2}=\varepsilon,\Diamond^{2}=\varepsilon,(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}=\varepsilon\rangle,

is the
Coxeter
group
\mathrm{I}_{2}(p)
(see
[
2
]
)
which contains exactly
2p
elements.

2.

For all integer
p
we have
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{p}\neq\mathrm{Id}
.

Then the generated monoid is infinite and its presentation is:

\langle\boxempty,\Diamond\mid\boxempty^{2}=\varepsilon,\Diamond^{2}=\varepsilon\rangle.

When the generators of a 2-PIM are exactly one projection and one involution, we call the monoid a
strict 2-PIM
. This is the structure we study in this paper.

3.2
Degenerated cases

The details given in the third column avoid considering degenerate cases where monoids are in fact monogenic, as shown by Lemma

Lemma 1
.

For every
k>0
,

\begin{array}[]{llllll}\textrm{if}&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\mathrm{Id}&(i)&\textrm{or}&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}&(ii)\end{array}

then
\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}
.

Proof.

\begin{array}[]{llll}&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\mathrm{Id}&(i)&\\
\Rightarrow&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\boxempty&\\
\Rightarrow&\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}&&\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{llll}&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}&(ii)&\\
\Rightarrow&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\Diamond&\\
\Rightarrow&(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\Diamond\boxempty&\\
\Rightarrow&\Diamond\boxempty=\Diamond&&\\
\Rightarrow&\boxempty=\mathrm{Id}&&\end{array}

∎

Lemma 2
.

A strict 2-PIM is monogenic if one of the following equations appears in its presentation:

1.

\mathrm{Id}=(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}
for

;

2.

(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}=\Diamond
or
(\Diamond\Box)^{k}\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}
for
k\geq 0
;

3.

\mathrm{Id}=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}
for
k\geq 0
;

4.

\mathrm{Id}=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond
with
k\geq 0
;

5.

\Diamond=(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond
with
k>0
;

6.

\Diamond=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}
with
k\geq 0
;

7.

\Diamond=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond
with
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

1.

Lemma
1
(i) leads to an immediate conclusion.

2.

If
k>0
then both equalities are equivalent to Lemma

(ii). If
k=0
then the statement reads
\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}
.

3.

We have
\Box=\Box\mathrm{Id}=\Box\Box(\Diamond\Box)^{k}=\Box(\Diamond\Box)^{k}=\mathrm{Id}
.

4.

We have
\mathrm{Id}=\Diamond\Diamond=\Diamond\Box(\Diamond\Box)^{k}\Diamond\Diamond=(\Diamond\Box)^{k+1}
. Hence, the result is recovered from Item 1.

5.

From

, we have
\mathrm{Id}=(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}
and we conclude using Item 1.

6.

From
\Diamond=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}
, we have
\mathrm{Id}=(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k+1}
and we conclude using Item 1.

7.

From
\Diamond=\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k}\Diamond
, we have
\mathrm{Id}=(\Diamond\boxempty)^{k+1}\Diamond

∎

Size of strict 2-PIMs

To prove that our result gives a classification, we first need to count the elements of each monoid. Let us start with the case of monoids characterized by an equation
\mathrm{eq}_{00}(k,k+r)
(
(\Diamond\Box)^{k}=(\Diamond\Box)^{k+r}
).
There are
4
possibilities for the minimal writing of an element:

•

(\Diamond\Box)^{i}
for
i\in[0,k+r-1]
.

•

(\Diamond\Box)^{i}\Diamond
for
i\in[0,k+r-1]
.

•

(\Box\Diamond)^{i}
for
i\in[1,k+r-1]
. Be careful not to count twice the empty word (
i=0
) already counted in case 1.

•

(\Box\Diamond)^{i}\Box
for
i\in[0,k+r-2]
. Indeed,

(\Box\Diamond)^{k+r-1}\Box=\Diamond\Diamond(\Box\Diamond)^{k+r-1}\Box=\Diamond(\Diamond\Box)^{k+r}=\Diamond(\Diamond\Box)^{k}=(\Box\Diamond)^{k-1}\Box
.

This implies that the cardinality of the monoid is
2(k+r)+2(k+r-1)=2k+2r-2
elements.

For instance, we recover the case of the Kuratowski monoid, which is given by the equation
(\Diamond\Box)^{4}=(\Diamond\Box)^{2}
(
eq_{00}(2,2)
). We find its cardinal
4*4-2=14
.

We can refine this enumeration, looking at the length of the smallest word
lw(e)
representing element
e
and counting the number of elements
e
for a fixed
lw(e)
.
This information is summarized in the Hilbert series

\mathcal{H}_{M}(t)=\sum_{e\in M}t^{lw(e)}.

For example, for
M_{00}(k,r)
the Hilbert series is easily deduced from the enumeration above. We obtain

\mathcal{H}_{M_{00}(k,r)}(t)=1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2(k+r-1)}2t^{i}\right)+t^{2(k+r-1)+1}.

For the Kuratowski monoid, we obtain

\mathcal{H}_{M_{00}(2,2)}(t)=1+2(t+t^{2}+t^{3}+t^{4}+t^{5}+t^{6})+t^{7}.

This corresponds to the following graduation on the elements

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\
\hline\cr\varepsilon&\Diamond&\Diamond\Box&\Diamond\Box\Diamond&(\Diamond\Box)^{2}&(\Diamond\Box)^{2}\Diamond&(\Diamond\Box)^{3}&(\Diamond\Box)^{3}\Diamond\\
&\Box&\Box\Diamond&\Box\Diamond\Box&(\Box\Diamond)^{2}&(\Box\Diamond)^{2}\Box&(\Box\Diamond)^{3}&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

It is possible to systematize the calculation. First, let us consider the simplest case of the monoid
M_{\emptyset}
.
When a relation holds, it is the highest degree member that contains the element to be removed from the series. Applying inclusion-exclusion principle, we obtain the series

\mathcal{H}_{M_{\emptyset}}(t)=\frac{1}{1-2t+2t^{2}-2t^{3}+\cdots}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{2t}{1+t}}=\frac{1+t}{1-t}=1+2t+2t^{2}+\cdots.

(17)

When a relation between the generators is added, we need to consider the equivalent one that has the smallest degree (the highest degree of the two words in the equation) and remove the Hilbert series
of the ideal generated by the larger of the two words. For instance in
M_{00}(k,r)

(\Diamond\Box)^{k}=(\Diamond\Box)^{k+r}

\Diamond

\Box(\Diamond\Box)^{k-1}=\Box(\Diamond\Box)^{k+r-1}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{H}_{M_{00}(k,r)}(t)&=&\mathcal{H}_{M_{\emptyset}}(t)-t^{2(k+r)-1}\mathcal{H}_{M_{\emptyset}}(t)=\frac{(1-t^{2(k+r)-1})(1+t)}{1-t}\\
&=&(1+t+\cdots+t^{2(k+r)-2})(1+t)=1+2(t+\cdots+t^{2(k+r-1)})+t^{2(k+r-1)+1},\end{array}

as expected.

Now we examine the remaining cases by applying the same method

•

For
M^{\circ}_{01}(k)

(\Box\Diamond)^{k}=(\Box\Diamond)^{k}\Box
obtained by
conjugating

Classification of strict 2-PIMs

This section is dedicated to the classification of strict 2-PIMs. We will check that two monoids are defined by two distinct equations of
\mathbb{E}
if and only if they are non-isomorphic.

First of all, the results of the following lemma are a direct consequence of the enumeration calculations in the previous section. For two monoids
\mathrm{M}
and
\mathrm{N}
, we write
\mathrm{M}\sim\mathrm{N}
if they are isomorphic.

Proposition 4
.

1.

For every
i\in\{01,10,11\}
,
s\in\{\bullet,\circ\}
and every couple
k,k^{\prime}
of positive integers, we have

\mathrm{M}_{i}^{s}(k)\sim\mathrm{M}_{i}^{s}(k^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow k=k^{\prime}.

2.

For every
i\in\{01,10,11\}
and every couple
k,k^{\prime}
of positive integers, we have

\mathrm{M}_{i}^{\bullet}(k)\not\sim\mathrm{M}_{i}^{\circ}(k^{\prime}).

3.

For every
i,j\in\{01,10\}
and every couple
k,k^{\prime}
of positive integers, we have

\mathrm{M}_{i}^{\bullet}(k)\not\sim\mathrm{M}_{j}^{\circ}(k^{\prime}).

4.

For every
i\in\{01,10\}
,
s\in\{\bullet,\circ\}
and every couple
k,k^{\prime}
of positive integers, we have

\mathrm{M}_{i}^{s}(k)\not\sim\mathrm{M}_{11}^{s}(k^{\prime}).

Proof.

Item (
1
) comes from the fact that for the two monoids of
\mathrm{M}_{i}^{s}(k)
and
\mathrm{M}_{i}^{s}(k^{\prime})
to have the same number of elements, it is necessary and sufficient that
k=k^{\prime}
. Item (
2
) arises from the fact that the two monoids
\mathrm{M}_{i}^{\bullet}(k)
and
\mathrm{M}_{i}^{\circ}(k^{\prime})
can never have the same number of elements. The same argument can be used to prove item (
3
) and (
4
).
∎

In the following, we will show that a strict 2-PIM contains a unique involution.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\mathrm{M}=\langle\Diamond,\boxempty\mid\{\Diamond^{2}=\mathrm{Id},\boxempty^{2}=\boxempty,\mathrm{eq}\}\rangle
be a strict 2-PIM. Then
\Diamond
is the only involution (apart from
\mathrm{Id}
) of
\mathrm{M}
.

Proof.

Let us assume there is another involution
\Diamond^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{M}
. Necessarily, we have
\Diamond^{\prime}=\boxempty^{\alpha_{1}}(\Diamond\boxempty)^{p}\Diamond^{\alpha_{2}}
with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\{0,1\}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
As
\Diamond^{\prime 2}=\mathrm{Id}
, we have

\Diamond^{\prime}\Diamond^{\prime}=\boxempty^{\alpha_{1}}(\Diamond\boxempty)^{p}\Diamond^{\alpha_{2}}\boxempty^{\alpha_{1}}(\Diamond\boxempty)^{p}\Diamond^{\alpha_{2}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\Diamond\boxempty)^{2p}=\mathrm{Id}&\mbox{ if }\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0\\
\boxempty(\Diamond\boxempty)^{2p}=\mathrm{Id}&\text{ if }\alpha_{1}=1\text{ and }\alpha_{2}=0\\
(\Diamond\boxempty)^{2p-1}\Diamond=\mathrm{Id}&\text{ if }\alpha_{1}=0\text{,}\alpha_{2}=1\text{ and }p>0\\
\Diamond^{2}=\mathrm{Id}&\mbox{ if }\alpha_{1}=0\text{,}\alpha_{2}=1\text{ and }p=0\\
(\boxempty\Diamond)^{2p+2}=\mathrm{Id}&\text{ if }\alpha_{1}=1\text{ and }\alpha_{2}=1.\end{array}\right.

In each of the above cases, the generated monoid is degenerate and monogenic (see Lemma
2
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5

∎

The notion of closure is defined at the level of sets and seems not to be encapsulated in the formalism we developed above.
