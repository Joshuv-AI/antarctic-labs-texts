Infinite Probabilistic Databases \rsuper *
Martin Grohe, Peter Lindner

Abstract
. Probabilistic databases (PDBs) model uncertainty in data in a quantitative way. In the established formal framework, probabilistic (relational) databases are finite probability spaces over relational database instances. This finiteness can clash with intuitive query behavior (Ceylan et al., KR 2016), and with application scenarios that are better modeled by continuous probability distributions (Dalvi et al., CACM 2009). We formally introduced infinite PDBs in (Grohe and Lindner, PODS 2019) with a primary focus on countably infinite spaces. However, an extension beyond countable probability spaces raises nontrivial foundational issues concerned with the measurability of events and queries and ultimately with the question whether queries have a well-defined semantics. We argue that finite point processes are an appropriate model from probability theory for dealing with general probabilistic databases. This allows us to construct suitable (uncountable) probability spaces of database instances in a systematic way. Our main technical results are measurability statements for relational algebra queries as well as aggregate queries and Datalog queries.

1.
Introduction

Probabilistic databases (PDBs) are used to model uncertainty in data. Such
uncertainty can have various reasons like, for example, noisy sensor data, the
presence of incomplete or inconsistent information, or information gathered
from unreliable sources
[
Agg09
,
SORK11
]
. In the standard formal
framework, probabilistic databases are finite probability spaces whose sample
spaces consist of database instances in the usual sense, referred to as

“possible worlds”
. However, this framework has various shortcomings
due to its inherent
closed-world assumption

[
CDVdB16
,
CDVdB21
]
, and the
restriction to finite domains. In particular, any event outside of the finite
scope of such probabilistic databases is treated as an impossible event. Yet,
statistical models of uncertain data, say, for example, for temperature
measurements as in
Section

2
, usually feature the use of continuous
probability distributions in appropriate error models. This (continuous
attribute-level uncertainty) is not expressible in the traditional PDB model.
Finite PDBs also only have limited support for tuple-level uncertainty: in
finite PDBs, all possible worlds have a fixed maximum number of tuples.
Instead, in particular with respect to an open-world assumption, we would like
to be able to model probabilistic databases without an a priori bound on the
number of tuples per instance. It is worth noting that there have been a number
of approaches to PDB systems that are supporting continuous probability
distributions, and hence going beyond finite probability spaces (see the
related works section). These models, however, lack a general (unifying) formal
basis in terms of a possible worlds semantics
[
DRS09
]
. While both
open-world PDBs and continuous probability distributions in PDBs have received
some attention in the literature, there is no systematic joint treatment of
these issues with a sound theoretical foundation. In
[
GL19
]
,
we introduced an extended model of PDBs as arbitrary (possibly infinite)
probability spaces over finite database instances. However, the focus there was
on countably infinite PDBs. An extension to continuous PDBs, which is necessary
to model probability distributions appearing in many applications that involve
real-valued measurement data, raises new fundamental questions concerning the
measurability of events and queries.

In this paper, we lay the foundations of a systematic and sound treatment of
infinite, even uncountable, probabilistic databases. In particular, we prove
that queries expressed in standard query languages have a well-defined
semantics that is compatible with the existing theoretical point of view.
Our model is based on the mathematical theory of finite point
processes
[
Moy62
,
Mac75
,
DVJ03
]
. Adopting this theory to the
context of relational databases, we give a suitable construction of measurable
spaces over which our probabilistic databases can then be defined. The only
(and mild) assumption we need is that the domains of all attributes are Polish
spaces. Intuitively, this requires them to have nice topological properties.
All typical domains one might encounter in database theory, for example
integers, strings, and reals, satisfy this assumption.

For queries and views to have a well-defined open-world semantics, we need them
to be measurable mappings between probabilistic databases. Our main technical
result states that indeed all queries and views that can be expressed in the
relational algebra, even equipped with arbitrary aggregate operators
(satisfying some mild measurability conditions) are measurable mappings. The
result holds for both a bag-based and set-based relational algebra and entails
the measurability of Datalog queries.

Measurability of queries may seem like an obvious minimum requirement,
but one needs to be very careful. We give an example of a simple, innocent
looking “query” that is not measurable (see Example
3.2
).
The proofs of the measurability results are not trivial, and to our knowledge
not immediately covered by standard results from point process theory. At their
core, the proofs are based on finding suitable “countable approximations” of
the queries. That such approximations can be obtained is guaranteed by our
topological requirements.

In the last section of this paper, we briefly discuss queries for probabilistic
databases that go beyond those that are just set-based versions of traditional
database queries. This also casts other natural PDB queries into our framework.
Examples of such a queries are probabilistic threshold queries and rank
queries. Note that these examples refer not only to the facts in a database,
but also to their probabilities, and hence are inherently probabilistic.

Infinite Probabilistic Databases
that was presented at the 23rd
International Conference on Database Theory (ICDT 2020)
[

.
The presentation of various proofs and arguments has

Our classification of views towards the end of this paper is similar to

[

,

[

]

suggests a distinction between
“traditional”
and
“out-of-world

similar to the one we present.

Measurable Spaces and Functions

In the following we recap the relevant background and foundations from
probability and measure theory that are needed throughout this paper. For a
general introduction to measure theory we point the reader to
[

1]

[

Let
\mathbb{X}\neq\emptyset
. A
\sigma
-algebra
on
\mathbb{X}
is a family
\mathfrak{X}

of subsets of
\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathbb{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
and

is closed under
complements and countable unions (an equivalent definition is obtained by
replacing
“countable unions”
with
“countable intersections”
).

{nota}

Throughout the paper, we use double struck letters (
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\dots
) to denote underlying spaces of interest, and fraktur letters (
\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y},\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\dots
) to denote set families and
\sigma
-algebras
over such spaces, in particular. We usually denote subsets of the underlying
spaces with bold italics letters
(\bm{X},\bm{Y},\bm{Z},\bm{A},\bm{B},\dots)
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
(fraktur
“
G
”
) be a set of subsets of
\mathbb{X}
(that is,
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
). The
\sigma
-algebra generated by

\mathfrak{G}
is the coarsest (i. e. smallest with respect to set inclusion)

\sigma
-algebra
\mathbb{X}
containing
\mathfrak{G}
. For any

, the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{G}
is unique. A
measurable space
is a pair
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
where
\mathbb{X}
is an arbitrary set and
\mathfrak{X}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{X}
. The sets in
\mathfrak{X}
are called
\mathfrak{X}
-measurable
(or

\mathfrak{X}
is clear from the context). If
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{Y})
is another measurable space, then a function

is called

(cf.
[

)
.

(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
,
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{Y})
,
(\mathbb{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

(1)

probability measure
on a measurable space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
is a
countably additive function
P\colon\mathfrak{X}\to[0,1]
with
P(\mathbb{X})=1
.
Then
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X},P)
is called a
probability space
. Measurable
sets in probability spaces are also called
events
. A measurable
function
\varphi
from a probability space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X},P)
into a
measurable space
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{Y})
introduces a new probability measure
P^{\prime}

on
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{Y})
by

P^{\prime}(\bm{Y})=P\big(\varphi^{-1}(\bm{Y})\big)=P\big(\{X\in\mathbb{X}\colon\varphi(X)\in\bm{Y}\}\big)

for all
\bm{Y}\in\mathfrak{Y}
. Then
P^{\prime}=P\circ\varphi^{-1}
is called the

image
or
push-forward probability measure
of
P
under (or
along)
\varphi
. The measurability of
\varphi
ensures that
P^{\prime}
is
well-defined.

Standard Constructions

Throughout this paper, we will encounter a variety of measurable spaces. The
most basic of these are either well-known measurable spaces like countable
spaces with the powerset
\sigma
-algebra or uncountable spaces like
\mathbb{R}
with
its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. From these spaces, we then construct more
complicated measurable spaces with the use of the following standard
constructions.

(1)

Product spaces.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, let
(\mathbb{X}_{i},\mathfrak{X}_{i})
be a measurable space. The

product
\sigma
-algebra

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{X}_{i}=\mathfrak{X}_{1}\otimes\dotsc\otimes\mathfrak{X}_{n}

is the
\sigma
-algebra on
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}
that is generated by
the sets
\{\proj_{j}^{-1}(\bm{X})\colon\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}_{j}\}
with
j=1,\dots,n
where
\proj_{j}
is the canonical projection
\proj_{j}\colon\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}\to\mathbb{X}_{j}
. If
(\mathbb{X}_{i},\mathfrak{X}_{i})=(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
for all

i=1,\dots,n
, we also write
\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes n}
instead of
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{X}
.

(2)

Disjoint unions.

If the spaces
\mathbb{X}_{i}
are pairwise disjoint, then the
disjoint union

\sigma
-algebra
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}
is given by

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{X}_{i}\coloneqq\big\{\bm{X}\subseteq{\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}}\mathbb{X}_{i}\colon\bm{X}\cap\mathbb{X}_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}_{i}\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n\big\}\text{.}

It may be easily verified that this is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}
.

(3)

Subspaces.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
be a measurable space and
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
. Then

\mathfrak{X}\rvert_{\bm{X}}\coloneqq\{\bm{X}^{\prime}\cap\bm{X}\colon\bm{X}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{X}\}

is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\bm{X}
.

Polish Topological Spaces

There are deep connections between measure theory and general topology. In
fact, virtually everything we discuss and show in this paper relies on the
presence of certain topological properties. The central notion from topology we
need is that of
Polish spaces

[
Kec95
, Chapter 3]
. In the
following, we assume familiarity with the basic topological concepts. A small
introduction to the basic terms can be found in
Appendix

A
. For a thorough
introduction, we refer to
[
Wil04
]
.

A
Polish space
is a topological space that is separable (i. e. there
exists a countable dense set), and completely metrizable (i. e. there is a
complete metric on the space generating its topology). Polish and (the later
introduced) standard Borel spaces are introduced and treated in detail
in
[
Kec95
, Chapter 1 & 2]
and
[
Sri98
]
. In this paper,
we heavily exploit the properties of Polish spaces. The existence of a
countable dense set, together with the existence of a complete metric
generating the topology allows us to approximate any point in the space by
countable collections of open sets. More specifically, for every point in the
space, there exists a sequence over a fixed
countable
set that converges
to the point.

The following fact lists a few natural classes of spaces that are
Polish.

Fact 2
.

All finite and countably infinite spaces (with the discrete topology) are
Polish
[
Kec95
, p. 13]
. The Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Polish for
all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}

[
Kec95
, ibid.]
.

Countable sums and products of sequences of Polish spaces are Polish
(subject to the sum and product topologies)

[
Kec95
, Proposition 3.3]
. Moreover, subspaces of a Polish space
that are countable intersections of open sets are Polish. This also implies
that closed subspaces of Polish spaces are Polish, cf.
[
Kec95
, Proposition 3.7
and Theorem 3.11]
.

The above properties arguably capture all typical spaces of interest for
database theory. When we later work with Polish spaces, we always assume that
we have a fixed Polish metric at hand, that is, a complete metric generating
the Polish topology. With respect to said
compatible
metric, we write

B_{\varepsilon}(X)
to denote the open ball of radius
\varepsilon>0
around the
point
X\in\mathbb{X}
.

Standard Borel Spaces

If
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
is a topological space, the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra

\mathfrak{B\mkern-1.0muo\mkern-0.2mur}(\mathbb{X})
on
\mathbb{X}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the open sets

\mathfrak{O}
(the topology in use is usually clear from context; in our case,
provided there is one, we always use a Polish topology on
\mathbb{X}
). Sets in the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra are also called
Borel sets
. In the following, we
state some nice basic properties of standard Borel spaces.

Fact 3

(see
[
Kal02
, Lemmas 1.5 & 1.10]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O}_{\mathbb{X}})
and
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{O}_{\mathbb{Y}})
be topological spaces.

(1)

If
\varphi\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is continuous, then
\varphi
is
\big(\mathfrak{B\mkern-1.0muo\mkern-0.2mur}(\mathbb{X}),\mathfrak{B\mkern-1.0muo\mkern-0.2mur}(\mathbb{Y})\big)
-measurable.

(2)

If
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{O}_{\mathbb{Y}})
is a metric
topological space,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of
\big(\mathfrak{B\mkern-1.0muo\mkern-0.2mur}(\mathbb{X}),\mathfrak{B\mkern-1.0muo\mkern-0.2mur}(\mathbb{Y})\big)
-measurable functions
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}=\varphi
, then
\varphi
is
measurable as well.
∎

A measurable space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
is called a
standard Borel space
if
there exists a Polish topology
\mathfrak{O}
on
\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathfrak{X}
is the (Borel)

\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{O}
.

Fact 4

(Lusin-Souslin, see
[
Kec95
, Theorem 15.1]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
and
(\mathbb{Y},\mathfrak{Y})
be standard Borel spaces, let
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
, and let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
be a continuous function such
that
\varphi\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{\bm{X}}
(the restriction of
\varphi
to the set
\bm{X}
)
is injective. Then
\varphi(\bm{X})\in\mathfrak{Y}
.∎

In other words, the above fact states that between standard Borel spaces, the
image of a measurable set
\bm{X}
under a function is measurable itself,
provided that the function is continous, and injective on
\bm{X}
.

The following fact intuitively states that the property of measurable spaces
being standard Borel is closed under using the constructions introduced before.

Fact 5

(see
[
Kec95
, p. 75]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{X}_{i},\mathfrak{X}_{i})
be a sequence of standard Borel spaces.

(1)

The product space
\big(\prod_{i}\mathbb{X}_{i},\bigotimes_{i}\mathfrak{X}_{i}\big)
is standard Borel.

(2)

If the spaces
\mathbb{X}_{i}
are pairwise disjoint, then the disjoint sum

\big(\sum_{i}\mathbb{X}_{i},\bigoplus_{i}\mathfrak{X}_{i}\big)
is standard
Borel.

Moreover, if
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
is standard Borel, and
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
, then

(\bm{X},\mathfrak{X}\rvert_{\bm{X}})
is standard Borel.
∎

(Finite) Point Processes

A
point process

[
DVJ03
,
DVJ08
]
is a
probability space over countable sets of points in some abstract
“state
space”
such as the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Point processes are a well-studied
subject in probability theory and they appear in a variety of applications such
as particle physics, ecology, geostatistics
(cf.
[
DVJ03
,
Bad07
]
and target tracking
[
Deg17
]
.
In computer science, they have applications in queuing
theory
[
Fra82
]
and machine learning
[
KT12
]
. The
individual outcomes (that is,
point configurations) drawn from a point process are called a
realization
of the process
. A point process is called
finite
if all of its
realizations are finite. In the following, we recall the construction of a
finite point process over a Polish state space, following the classic
constructions of
[
Moy62
,
Mac75
]
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
be a standard Borel space. Recall that
(\mathbb{X}^{k},\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes k})
is also standard Borel for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. For all

(X_{1},\dots,X_{k}),(X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{X}^{k}
, we define

(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\sim_{k}(X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime})

if and only if there exists a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
such
that

(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=\big(X_{\pi(1)}^{\prime},\dots,X_{\pi(k)}^{\prime}\big)\text{.}

Then
\sim_{k}
is an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{X}^{k}
. Tuples in
\mathbb{X}^{k}
are
equivalent under
\sim_{k}
if and only if they contain every individual element
the exact same number of times. Thus, the quotient space
\mathbb{X}^{k}\mkern-2.0mu/\mkern-1.0mu{\sim_{k}}
can be identified with the set
\mathcal{B}_{k}(\mathbb{X})
of bags of
cardinality exactly
k
over
\mathbb{X}
. Note that in the case
k=0
,
\mathbb{X}^{0}\mkern-2.0mu/\mkern-1.0mu{\sim_{0}}
(by convention) consists of a single distinguished point
representing the empty realization. The space

\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{X}^{k}\mkern-2.0mu/\mkern-1.0mu{\sim_{k}}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{k}(\mathbb{X})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})

is one of the canonical and equivalent choices for the sample space for a
finite point process on
\mathbb{X}

[
Mac75
]
. The original construction
of
[
Moy62
]
considers the
symmetrization

\sym\colon\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{X}^{k}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})
where for all
(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\in\mathbb{X}^{k}
it holds that

\sym(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=[(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})]_{\sim_{k}}=\left\{\hskip-39.12204pt\middle\{\mathopen{}X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\mathclose{}\middle\}\hskip-39.12204pt\right\}\text{.}

Then
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes k}
is the natural

\sigma
-algebra on
\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{X}^{k}
. The
\sigma
-algebra on

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})
is then defined as

\big\{\bm{B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})\colon\sym^{-1}(\bm{B})\in{\textstyle\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}}\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes k}\big\}\text{,}

(3)

that is, as the
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})
induced by
\sym\colon\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{X}^{k}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})
. Note
that this is indeed a
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})
,
cf.
[
Kal02
, Lemma 1.3]
.

Remark 6

(cf.
[
Moy62
, Section 2]
)
.

A set
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes k}
is called
symmetric
, if for
all
(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\in\mathbb{X}^{k}
it holds that

(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\in\bm{X}
implies
\big(X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(k)}\big)\in\bm{X}
for all permutations
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
. The
function
\bm{X}\mapsto\sym(\bm{X})
is a bijection between the
symmetric sets
\bm{X}
in
\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{X}^{\otimes k}
and the sets of
3
.

An equivalent, but technically more convenient construction is motivated by
interpreting point processes as

random counting measures

[
DVJ08
]
.
For
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\#(\bm{X},n)\coloneqq\big\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})\colon\lvert B\rvert_{\bm{X}}={\textstyle\sum_{X\in\bm{X}}}\lvert B\rvert_{X}=n\big\}\text{,}

that is,
\#(\bm{X},n)
is the set of finite bags over
\mathbb{X}

containing exactly
n
elements from
\bm{X}
, counting multiplicities. The sets

\#(\bm{X},n)
with
\bm{X}\in\mathfrak{X}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
are called

counting events
. The
counting
\sigma
-algebra

\mathfrak{C\mkern-0.2muo\mkern-0.5muu\mkern-0.4mun\mkern-0.5mut}(\mathbb{X})

on
\mathbb{X}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all counting events.

Fact 7

(
[
Moy62
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

The
\sigma
-algebra from
3
coincides with

\mathfrak{C\mkern-0.2muo\mkern-0.5muu\mkern-0.4mun\mkern-0.5mut}(\mathbb{X})
.
∎

{defi}

A
finite point process
with standard Borel state space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{X})
is a probability space
\big(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X}),\mathfrak{C\mkern-0.2muo\mkern-0.5muu\mkern-0.4mun\mkern-0.5mut}(\mathbb{X}),P\big)
. A finite point process is
simple
if
P\big(\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{fin}}(\mathbb{X})\big)=1
, that is, if its realizations are almost surely
sets.

3.
Probabilistic Databases

In this section, we introduce our framework for infinite probabilistic
databases and their query semantics. To begin with, we recall the conventional
formal definition of probabilistic databases as it is found in textbooks on
the subject
[
SORK11
,
VdBS17
]
:

{defi}

[Finite Probabilistic Databases; adapted from
[
SORK11
, Section 2.2]
]
Let
\tau
be a database schema and let
\mathbb{U}
be a universe. A

probabilistic database (PDB)

\mathcal{D}
over
\tau
and
\mathbb{U}
is a
probability space
\mathcal{D}=\big(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}},\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}),P\big)
where
\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
is a
finite set of database instances over
\tau
and
\mathbb{U}
.

From a probability theoretic point of view,
Section

3
is a severe
limitation to the vast expressive power of stochastic models, only allowing
probability distributions over finitely many alternative database instances. As
an example, in a setting such as that of
Section

2
(the database of
temperature measurements), we would typically model noise or uncertainty in the
sensor measurements by continuous distributions. This directly leads to (even
uncountably) infinite probability spaces that are not covered by the typical
textbook definition of probabilistic databases (
Section

3
).
In
[
GL19
]
, we introduced the following general notion of
probabilistic databases as probability spaces of database instances:

{defi}

[see
[
GL19
, Definition 3.1]
]
Let
\tau
be database schema and let
\mathbb{U}
be a universe. Suppose that

\mathfrak{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on the space
\mathbb{F}_{[\tau,\mathbb{U}]}
of all

(\tau,\mathbb{U})
-facts. A
probabilistic database (PDB)

\mathcal{D}
over
\tau

and
\mathbb{U}
is a probability space
\mathcal{D}=(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}},P)
where
\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}\subseteq\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{[\tau,\mathbb{U}]}
and
\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}

satisfying

\big\{D\in\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}\colon\lvert D\rvert_{\bm{F}}>0\big\}\in\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}

for all
\bm{F}\in\mathfrak{F}
.

Therein, the sample space
\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
may be infinite, even uncountable. While we
only discussed set PDBs in
[
GL19
]
, we broaden the definition
to support bag instances here. In any case, it was left open
in
[
GL19
]
, how to construct such probability spaces, let alone
how to obtain suitable measurable spaces
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}})
. This is
no longer a trivial task once
\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
is uncountable. In this section, we
provide a general construction for such measurable spaces.

Remark 8
.

At this point, we want to stress again the meaning of our terminology. In
general probabilistic databases there are lots of components, on different
levels of abstraction that could, in principle, be infinite spaces. The term

infinite
probabilistic databases is derived from the notion of an

infinite
probability space, meaning that the sample space is of
infinite size. Still, in the PDBs we consider in this paper, database
instances themselves (the concrete outcomes, or realizations of a PDB) are
always finite collections of facts. The framework we discribe here is not
suitable for discussing probability spaces over infinite database instances
in the sense of
[
AHV95
, Section 5.6]
, such as
constraint
databases

[
KLP00
]
.

3.1.
Probabilistic Databases are Finite Point
Processes

Our counstruction of measurable spaces of database instances stems from

{conv}

From now on, we only consider database schemas
\tau

3.2.
The Possible Worlds Semantics of Queries and Views

Views
are mappings between database instances. That is, a view
V

V\colon\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{[\tau,\mathbb{U}]}\to\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{[\tau^{\prime},\mathbb{U}^{\prime}]}
for some database
schemas
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
and universes
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{U}^{\prime}
. We call
\tau
the

input
, and
\tau^{\prime}
the
output schema
of the view

. If
\tau^{\prime}

consists of a single relation symbol only, we call

a
query
. Queries
are typically denoted by
Q
. Usually, queries and views are given as syntactic
expressions in some query language. As usual, we blur the distinction between a
query or view, and its syntactic representation. In the following, we let
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}})=\big(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{[\tau,\mathbb{U}]},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{[\tau,\mathbb{U}]}\big)
, and
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V})=\big(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{[\tau^{\prime},\mathbb{U}^{\prime}]},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{[\tau^{\prime},\mathbb{U}^{\prime}]}\big)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}},P)
be a PDB and let
V\colon\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}\to\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V}
be a view.
For
\bm{D}\subseteq\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
, the image of
\bm{D}
under
V
is

V(\bm{D})\coloneqq\{V(D)\colon D\in\bm{D}\}\subseteq\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V}\text{.}

If the function
V
is
(\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V})
-measurable, then
V
introduces a
push-forward measure
P_{V}
on
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V})
via

P_{V}(\bm{D})\coloneqq P\big(V^{-1}(\bm{D})\big)=P\big(\{D\in\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}\colon V(D)\in\bm{D}\}\big)

(5)

for all
\bm{D}\in\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V}
. In this case

V(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\big(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V},P_{V}\big)

is a PDB.

Remark 11
.

The kind of semantics we introduce here is the natural generalization of a
standard choice for semantics on probabilistic databases. Conceptually, we
consider queries and views that have well-defined semantics on traditional
database instances. That is, they get as input a database instance, and as
output, produce a new database instance. Such a semantics is lifted to
probabilistic databases by applying the query or view on every possible
world, and weighting it according to the probability measure of the input
PDB. This notion of semantics is commonly called the
possible answer
sets semantics
of probabilistic databases
[
SORK11
, Section 2.3.1]
.
It (or, to be more precise, its discrete version) has previously been also
called
possible worlds semantics (of queries)

[
DS07
]
,
which is the term we prefer to use in this work, as we deem it the natural
choice on how to define query (or view) semantics on PDBs that are modelled
as a collection of possible worlds with a probability distribution, matching
the standard definition of output probabilities of a (measurable) function
on a probability space (cf.
[
Gre09
]
). Note that strictly speaking,
this overloads the term
“possible worlds semantics”
: in reference to
PDBs,
“possible worlds semantics”
means the definition of PDBs as
probability spaces over database instances, whereas in reference to
queries or views, it means the definition of the output of a query with
respect to an application per possible world, as in
(
5
).

For queries, another semantics has been discussed in literature, wich was
later dubbed the
possible answers semantics

[
SORK11
, Section
2.3.2]
. Under this semantics, the output of a query is the
collection of tuples that may appear as an answer to the query (i. e. the
tuples that appear in the output possible worlds under the possible worlds
semantics), together with their marginal probability. For finite PDBs, this
notion makes sense, because the result will be much smaller than a
description of the whole output probability under possible worlds semantics.
For uncountable infinite PDBs, however, this is not of much use. As soon as
continuous probability distributions are involved, we naturally encounter
PDBs where the marginal probability of every particular fact (or tuple in
the output) may be zero.

We note that to the best of our knowledge, there has been no formal
description of these semantics when duplicates are allowed. Note that for

Boolean queries
with set semantics, i. e., queries whose output is
either
\{()\}
(true) or
\emptyset
(false), both of the above semantics
are essentially equivalent: the only possible answer tuple is the empty
tuple
()
, and the only possible worlds of the answer are
\emptyset
and

\{()\}
.

Note that if
V
fails to be measurable, then

5
is not well-defined. In this case,
V
has no
meaningful semantics on probabilistic databases! Thus, discussing the
measurability of views and queries is an issue that requires attention. The
following example shows that there are inconspicuous, seemingly simple queries
that are not measurable.

{exa}

Consider
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{U}^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}
, together with the database schemas

consisting of the single, unary relation symbol

(equipped with the Borel

3.3.
Assembling Views from Queries

A view
V
with output relations
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
can be identified with a set
of queries
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}
, one per output relation symbol, such that for all

D\in\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
it holds that

V(D)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}Q_{i}(D)\text{.}

Let
(\mathbb{F}_{V},\mathfrak{F}_{V})
be the measurable space of facts belonging to
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{V},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{V})
and let
(\mathbb{F}_{Q_{i}},\mathfrak{F}_{Q_{i}})
be the measurable space of facts
belonging to
(\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}_{Q_{i}},\mathord{\mathfrak{D}\mkern-2.5mu\mathfrak{B}}_{Q_{i}})
. From the above equality, we get that
for all
D\in\mathord{\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}}
, all
\bm{F}\in\mathfrak{F}_{V}
, and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\lvert V(D)\rvert_{\bm{F}}=n
if and only if there exist non-negative integers

n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
with
n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n
and the property that

\lvert Q_{i}(D)\rvert_{\bm{F}\cap\mathbb{F}_{Q_{i}}}=n_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.
Note that this condition corresponds to an event given by a countable union of
counting events. Thus, we obtain the following.

Lemma 12
.

The view
V
is measurable if and only if
Q_{i}
is measurable for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

By the merit of
12
, we only need to discuss the
measurability of queries.

The following is a partial restatement of what is known as the

In general however, it can still be mathematically more conventient to use

set semantics setting. The point processes defining PDBs should then just be

Note that we proceeded similar to

In

6

the shape of

(2)

9.
Conclusions

We introduce the notion of standard PDBs, for which we rigorously describe how
to construct suitable measurable spaces for infinite probabilistic databases,
completing the picture of
[
GL19
]
. The viability of this model
as a general and unifying framework for finite
and infinite
databases is
supported by the well-definedness and compositionality of (typical) query
semantics. Other kinds of PDB queries embed into the framework as well.

It is currently open, whether, and if so, how more in-depth results on point
processes can be used for probabilistic databases, for example, to perform
open-world query answering. Also, while we focused on relational algebra and
aggregation, the queries of
Section

8
deserve a systematic treatment
in their own right in infinite PDBs.

Topologies and Metric Spaces

The brief account of topological notions is based upon
[
Wil04
]
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be some set. A
topology
on
\mathbb{X}
is a family
\mathfrak{O}
of subsets
of
\mathbb{X}
that contains both
\emptyset
and
\mathbb{X}
, and is closed under
finite intersections and (arbitrary, i. e. possibly uncountable) unions. The
pair
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
is called a
topological space
. The sets in
\mathfrak{O}
are
called
open
sets, and their complements (with respect to
\mathbb{X}
) are
called
closed
sets.

For
\bm{X}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
, the
closure
of
\bm{X}
is the intersection of
all closed sets that contain
\bm{X}
. A set
\bm{X}
is called
dense
in

(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
if its closure is
\mathbb{X}
. The topological space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
is
called
separable
if there exists a countable subset of
\mathbb{X}
that is
dense in
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a non-empty set and let
d\colon\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be
a function satisfying

•

d(X,Y)\geq 0
with
d(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X=Y
;

•

d(X,Y)=d(Y,X)
; and

•

d(X,Z)\leq d(X,Y)+d(Y,Z)
(the
triangle inequality
)

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{X}
. Then
d
is called a
metric
on
\mathbb{X}
and the
pair
(\mathbb{X},d)
is called a
metric space
. For
\varepsilon>0
, the set

B_{\varepsilon}(X)\coloneqq\big\{Y\in\mathbb{X}\colon d(X,Y)<\varepsilon\big\}

is the
open ball of radius
\varepsilon
around
X
. The
metric
topology
on
\mathbb{X}
with respect to
d
is the topology
\mathfrak{O}
on
\mathbb{X}
that is
defined by

\mathfrak{O}\coloneqq\big\{\bm{X}\subseteq\mathbb{X}\colon\text{ for all }X\in\bm{X}\text{ there exists some }\varepsilon>0\text{ such that }B_{\varepsilon}(X)\subseteq\bm{X}\big\}\text{.}

A sequence
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
of elements in a metric space
(\mathbb{X},d)
is
called a
Cauchy sequence
if
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(X_{i},X_{i+1})=0
.
A sequence
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
in
(\mathbb{X},d)

converges
to an element

X\in\mathbb{X}
in
(\mathbb{X},d)
if for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
there exists
some
i
, large enough, such that
d(X,X_{i})<\varepsilon
. A metric
d
on

\mathbb{X}
is called
complete
, if all Cauchy sequences over
(\mathbb{X},d)

converge to elements of
\mathbb{X}
. A metric space is called
complete
, if
its metric is complete. In a metric space, a set
\bm{X}
is dense (with respect
to the metric topology) if and only if for all
X\in\mathbb{X}
and all
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
Y\in\mathbb{X}
such that
d(X,Y)<\varepsilon
.

A topological space
(\mathbb{X},\mathfrak{O})
is called
metrizable
if there exists a
metric
d
on
\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathfrak{O}
is the corresponding metric topology. If
such
d
exists that is additionally complete, the space is called

completely metrizable
. A
Polish space
is a completely metrizable,
separable topological space.
