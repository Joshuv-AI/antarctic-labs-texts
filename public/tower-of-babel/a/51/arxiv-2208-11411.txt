Spectrum of non-Hermitian deep-Hebbian neural networks
Zijian Jiang 1, Ziming Chen 2, Tianqi Hou 3, Haiping Huang 1

Abstract
Neural networks with recurrent asymmetric couplings are important to understand how episodic memories are encoded in the brain. Here, we integrate the experimental observation of wide synaptic integration window into our model of sequence retrieval in the continuous time dynamics. The model with non-normal neuron-interactions is theoretically studied by deriving a random matrix theory of the Jacobian matrix in neural dynamics. The spectra bears several distinct features, such as breaking rotational symmetry about the origin, and the emergence of nested voids within the spectrum boundary. The spectral density is thus highly non-uniformly distributed in the complex plane. The random matrix theory also predicts a transition to chaos. In particular, the edge of chaos provides computational benefits for the sequential retrieval of memories. Our work provides a systematic study of time-lagged correlations with arbitrary time delays, and thus can inspire future studies of a broad class of memory models, and even big data analysis of biological time series.



I
Introduction

Neural sequence specifies a temporally structured dynamics of neuronal population activity, which is ubiquitous in functions of many brain regions,
such as hippocampal replay
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
and choice-selective neural dynamics
[
4
]
. Generation of neural sequence
requires general circuit-level mechanisms, which can be simulated by artificial neural networks composed of recurrent units
[
5
]
.
In essence, the synaptic coupling between each pair of neurons can be constructed by a temporally asymmetric Hebbian (TAH) rule, which has been extensively studied
in the physics community
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
and recently the computational neuroscience community
[
9
,
10
]
. This rule reflects
the asymmetric connectivity ubiquitous in many brain regions
[
11
]
, bringing the network activity out of equilibrium and the patterns in each sequence can be reactivated
according to their intrinsic temporal order. Therefore, the TAH rule plays an important role in understanding how neural sequences are stored and retrieved, even episodic memory (memory traces of past experiences)
[
12
]
.

As a limitation, the standard TAH rule incorporates only nearest-neighbor pairs of patterns in a sequence of uncorrelated patterns, whereas, the synaptic integration leading to the plasticity
could occur in a wide range of temporal windows (e.g., synaptic modification in neocortex)
[
13
,
14
]
. This observation motivates the inclusion of an arbitrary Hebbian interaction range
in the TAH rule, which is the main focus of this work. We remark that a symmetric version of synaptic integration considering
the long range Hebbian interaction has been recently studied, which revealed a significantly different phase diagram of the network collective behavior from the learning rule considering only the nearest-neighbor pair
[
15
,
16
]
.
In contrast, the extended TAH rule brings the network out of equilibrium, yielding rich modes of network dynamics. In this work, we characterize thoroughly the spectral density of the non-normal synaptic coupling matrix
and the associated Jacobian matrix of linearized dynamics, which can reveal different collective modes supporting the network function.
Moreover, transition to chaos and sequence memory are also explored and discussed.

The spectral density of this new type of non-normal matrix adds an
important category of asymptotic spectrum into the non-Hermitian random matrix theory, displaying an anisotropic distribution of eigenvalues in the complex plane. In this sense, our study could open a new avenue to study
the neural sequence generation and processing in more biological plausible setting with the random matrix theory. In addition, the synaptic integration can be decomposed into contributions of time-lagged correlations among
neural patterns, which shares the common features observed in complex systems like price fluctuations in financial markets and
physiological time series in biology
[
17
,
18
]
. Therefore, our analysis may also provide insights toward understanding a broad range of
collective phenomena.

II
Model

We study a recurrent neural network composed of
N
interacting neurons. The neural dynamics obeys the following rule,

\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-r_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\Phi(r_{j})

(1)

where
r_{i}
is the synaptic current reflecting the neural activity of the neuron
i
,

\tau
indicates the time constant of the dynamics (
\tau=0.01s
here, the time is in seconds in this paper), and
\Phi
is the non-linear activation function transferring the synaptic current into the firing activity (
\Phi=\tanh
here).
For simulations, one can add an external input
I(t)
(e.g., an instantaneous stimulus in our simulations)
to the right-hand-side of the dynamics equation.
The synaptic coupling between any two neurons (say
i
and
j
) is given by

J_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\left(c\xi^{\mu}_{i}\xi^{\mu}_{j}+\gamma\sum_{r=1}^{d}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\xi^{\mu+r}_{j}\right),

(2)

which contains the concurrent Hebbian term and pattern-separated (with a most distant separation of
d
patterns) Hebbian term. The strength of the concurrent Hebbian term is specified by

c
, and the strength of the pattern-separated term is specified by
\gamma
. As
d
takes an arbitrary number and thus represents the depth of Hebbian interaction, we call this model deep-Hebbian network.

In this model, we have
P
patterns, and each pattern follows the Rademacher distribution
P(\xi_{i}^{\mu})=\frac{1}{2}\delta(\xi_{i}^{\mu}-1)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\xi_{i}^{\mu}+1)
,
in which the superscript
\mu
denotes the index of pattern and the subscript
i
denotes the index of neuron.
We are interested in the situation of large
P
and
N
, with fixed memory load
\alpha=\frac{P}{N}
.
When the pattern entries follow a standard Gaussian distribution, the statistics, e.g., the first and second moments, does not change (see the following rotated patterns as well), and thus does not affect the asymptotic spectrum.
Note that these patterns form a cyclic sequence with periodic boundary, corresponding to an ordered stimulus sequence in animal experiments
[
19
]
.

The synaptic coupling in our model is clearly asymmetric and there thus exists an out-of-equilibrium collective behavior.
The coupling asymmetry is ubiquitous in neocortex
[
11
]
, e.g., the mechanism of spike-timing-dependent plasticity and other higher-order variants support
this asymmetry
[
13
,
14
]
. The presence of the asymmetry in the connectivity can generate a steady flow of neural activity, related to the
sequence storage and retrieval
[
6
,
7
,
10
]
. As the synaptic integration can occur in a broad range of temporal windows, we incorporate
an arbitrary window size (indicated by
d
), yet violating the coupling symmetry. Setting
(c,\gamma)=(1,0)
, we recovers the standard Hopfield model
[
20
,
21
]
where the coupling is symmetric and
an equilibrium distribution of neural activity is guaranteed (or a Lyapunov function exists). Setting
(c,\gamma,d)=(0,1,1)
, we recovers the standard TAH model
[
6
]
.
Therefore, our model setting is more general than any previous models of associative memory, in either equilibrium or non-equilibrium context.

The coupling matrix in Eq. (
2
) can be recast in a compact form as

\mathbf{J}=\frac{1}{N}\bm{\xi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\xi}

(3)

where
\bm{\xi}
is the
P\times N
pattern matrix, and
\mathbf{X}
is a
P\times P
circulant matrix with entries

X_{\mu\nu}=c\delta_{\mu\nu}+\gamma\sum_{r=1}^{d}\delta_{\mu,[(\nu+r)\mod P]}.

(4)

The circulant property is due to our cyclic-sequence setting, i.e.
\bm{\xi}^{P+1}=\bm{\xi}^{1}
.
For example, if
P=5,d=2
,
\mathbf{X}
reads

\left[\begin{matrix}c&0&0&\gamma&\gamma\\
\gamma&c&0&0&\gamma\\
\gamma&\gamma&c&0&0\\
0&\gamma&\gamma&c&0\\
0&0&\gamma&\gamma&c\end{matrix}\right],

(5)

where the number of
\gamma
in each row of the matrix is conserved, equal to
d
.
We emphasize that our method in the following analysis does
not rely on the explicit form of
\mathbf{X}
. Therefore, despite our interest in deep-Hebbian network,
other models of collective phenomena expressed through correlation matrices can also be analogously treated by the same approach.

The function of the network is to store and retrieve
P

N
-dimensional patterns
\{\bm{\xi}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\xi}^{P}\}
.
When the network
is stimulated by a state correlated with one of the stored patterns, the hint will trigger the retrieval,
which is marked by a significant overlap between neuron activations and stored patterns.
The overlap between
the
\mu
-th pattern and the neuron activity at time
t
is defined as

m_{\mu}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\xi_{{j}}^{\mu}\Phi(r_{j}(t)).

(6)

t
, and overlaps with different patterns are

N=4000
and
\alpha=0.01
,

. The overlap with the first pattern

\mathbf{r}(0)=0.2\bm{\xi}^{1}+0.2\bm{\xi}^{30}+0.6\mathbf{z}
. In this case,
the phase of the dynamics displays two types of cyclic behavior.

The retrieval behavior differs in different parameter regimes. In this paper, the memory overlap is averaged over thirty realizations of the pattern distribution (unless stated otherwise).
The behavior falls within two types—static and dynamical recall. In a static recall, the network activity gets
stuck in a stable fixed point correlated with some pattern (but not others), and thus
m_{\mu}(t)
becomes stationary over time.
But in a dynamical recall, the activity leads to sequential memories of stored patterns (Fig.
1
). We remark that due to the nature of our model
(see also previous works in the equilibrium counterpart
[
15
]
or the out-of-equilibrium case
[
10
]
),
the peak overlap magnitude can not achieve one (but a non-zero value), which we call sequence memory (see also the theoretical work
[
8
]
which used
the condition of vanishing overlap to determine the storage capacity in a simpler scenario). In Fig.
1
, we also observe that
the key model parameter
d
tunes the sequence-replay period, which is very interesting for future neurophysiology experiment tests.
We also study how robust the recall behavior is when the network state initialization varies, which is shown in Fig.
2
.
We conclude that as long as the memory load is small, the recall behavior is quite robust from different noisy initializations, and the large deep-Hebbian strength is able
to maintain a relatively high magnitude of peak overlap (see also Fig.
5
for detailed analysis). We will analyze
the relationship between network structure and function in the remaining sections.

Compared to other architectures (e.g., feedforward or symmetric networks), our rate model of recurrent networks shows richer properties of network dynamics only via simple deep-Hebbian construction (see next sections),
thereby being able to show sequence replay characteristics of cortical and hippocampus circuits, and moreover,
the introduced Hebbian depth
d
can tune the replay period, which was not reported before, and in mathematics, higher values of
d
(e.g.,
d>1
) lead to an anisotropic spectrum of the connectivity matrix, which can be captured by our theory.
In addition, due to the pattern-separated Hebbian term, the network activity during replay can smoothly evolve in time and becomes transiently correlated in order with each of the patterns in the sequence.
This is a nice property in our recurrent rate network that helps the network activity to explore a broad dynamics regime.
The theoretical study in this paper would thus hopefully inspire future algorithmic designs in temporal sequence processing with our proposed recurrent rate networks, which we will demonstrate in forthcoming future works.
We next analyze the relationship between the recall dynamics and intrinsic property of the network structure.

III
Linearized recurrent dynamics

In this section, we first study the Jacobian spectrum of fixed points and transient states of low speed. The spectrum is then connected to the spectrum of the
original non-normal coupling matrix ensemble.

To explore the stability of an arbitrary point in the phase space, including fixed points or transient states, we linearize the dynamics equation [Eq. (
1
)]
as follows,

\tau\frac{d\delta r_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{ij}\delta r_{j},

(7)

where
\delta r_{i}\equiv r_{i}-r^{*}_{i}
denotes the displacement from the operating
point
\mathbf{r}^{*}=\left(r^{*}_{1},\cdots,r^{*}_{N}\right)
, and
D_{ij}=-\delta_{ij}+J_{ij}\Phi^{\prime}(r^{*}_{j})
specifies
the Jacobian matrix, in which
\Phi^{\prime}(r^{*}_{j})

denotes the derivative
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(a)}{\mathrm{d}a}\right|_{a=r^{*}_{j}}
. Considering that the
operating point is a fixed point or a low-speed point, the linearized dynamics [Eq. (
7
)] can be solved as follows,

\delta\mathbf{r}(t)=\exp(\mathbf{D}t/\tau)\delta\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{R}\exp(\bm{\Lambda}t/\tau)\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\delta\mathbf{r}(0),

(8)

where we use the spectral decomposition
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{R}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
, where
\dagger
denotes the conjugate
transpose operation. The instantaneous Jacobian matrix captures the behavior of the linearized dynamics around the operating point. The real-part eigenvalues of
the matrix determines the timescales of growth or decay of perturbation, and thus determines the local stability of the dynamics, while the imaginary parts control the oscillation frequency of the neural dynamics.
Through studying the spectrum of this
matrix, we can identify the key network parameters underlying the macroscopic behavior of the deep-Hebbian neural networks.

III.1
Spectrum of Jacobian matrix

We first consider the shifted Jacobian
\tilde{D}_{ij}\equiv D_{ij}+\delta_{ij}=J_{ij}\Phi^{\prime}(r^{*}_{j})
, for which the shifted Jacobian shares the same eigenvectors with the original Jacobian, but
the spectrum has a translation along the real axis.
By inserting Eq. (
3
) into the shifted Jacobian and performing
the diagonalization
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}
, where
\dagger
means the Hermitian conjugate operation
(the circulant matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be diagonalized through a unitary transformation), we arrive at a simplified formula

\tilde{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{N}\bm{\hat{\xi}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\hat{\xi}}\bm{\Phi^{\prime}},

(9)

where
\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}=\mbox{diag}\left[\Phi^{\prime}(r^{*}_{1}),\cdots,\Phi^{\prime}(r^{*}_{N})\right]
,
and
\bm{\hat{\xi}}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\xi}
denotes the rotated pattern. It is easy to check
the first two moments in statistics of the rotated patterns are respectively

\langle\hat{\xi}_{i}^{\mu}\rangle=0
,
\langle\overline{\hat{\xi}_{i}^{\mu}}\hat{\xi}_{j}^{\nu}\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\mu\nu}
,
which can be fully described by a standard complex Gaussian distribution in the large
N
limit.
Here
\bar{\cdot}
denotes the complex conjugate operation, and
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the disorder average over random instances of i.i.d. patterns,
or random instances of the shifted matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
(given the operating point).

The spectral density is defined as the probability density
\tilde{\rho}(w)
of finding an eigenvalue which coincides with a point
w

on the complex plane. In the large
N
limit, we assume that the self-averaging property holds for
the density, i.e., the density of any single instance matches the asymptotic density after the matrix ensemble average is performed.
The spectral density is intuitively defined as follows,

\tilde{\rho}(w)=\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(2)}(w-\tilde{\lambda}_{i})\right\rangle,

(10)

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
, and
\delta^{(2)}(\cdot)
defines the two
dimensional Dirac delta function. In fact, the coupling matrix
\mathbf{J}
or the shifted Jacobian is a non-Hermitian matrix, and thus the associated eigenvalues are distributed
on the complex plane (i.e., 2D space with two axes, one for real part and the other for imaginary part).
The spectral density of the Jacobian is recovered by the horizontal translation in the complex plane as

{\rho}(w)=\tilde{\rho}(w+1)
.
The standard procedure to calculate the density requires the computation of the eigenvalue potential
\phi
as follows,

\tilde{\rho}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\nabla^{2}\phi,

(11)

which is exactly the Poisson equation in a two-dimensional electrostatic problem
[
22
]
. Therefore, the spectral density is equivalent to a two-dimensional charge distribution, and

\phi
becomes an electrostatic potential, which is also called the eigenvalue potential in the random matrix theory.
A brief introduction of this mapping is given in Appendix
A
.

The real-valued potential can be written in a free-energy form as
[
22
]

\phi(w)=-\frac{1}{N}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left\langle\ln Z(w,\epsilon)\right\rangle,

(12)

where the partition function
Z(w,\epsilon)=\operatorname{det}\left[\left|w{\mathds{1}}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\right|^{2}+|\epsilon|^{2}{\mathds{1}}\right]
in which
{\mathds{1}}
is an
N\times N
identity matrix.
The additional infinitesimal variable
\epsilon
ensures the matrix inside the determinant
to be positive definite even when
w
hits an eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
. The regularization for avoiding the singularity can also be understood from
the mathematical definition of the Dirac delta function
\pi\delta^{(2)}\left(w-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\right)=\lim_{|\epsilon|^{2}\rightarrow 0}\frac{|\epsilon|^{2}}{\left(\left|w-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\right|^{2}+|\epsilon|^{2}\right)^{2}}
.
In the electrostatic mapping, it is also convenient to introduce the electric field that is the very Green’s function:

G(w)=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial w}=\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\mbox{Tr}\left[\left(w{\mathds{1}}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\right)^{-1}\right]\right\rangle,

(13)

which is also called the resolvent in the random matrix theory. Therefore, the density can be estimated from
G
as follows (see Appendix
A
),

\tilde{\rho}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\bar{w}},

(14)

where the complex-valued
G
is a function of
w
and
\bar{w}
.

A standard route to treat the disorder average in Eq. (
12
) is the replica method, i.e.,

\left\langle\ln Z\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{\left\langle Z^{n}\right\rangle}{n}
where
n
copies of the original system are introduced,
and finally an analytical continuation
n\to 0
is performed. However, the replica analysis for the current setting becomes complicated.
Instead, we adopt an annealed approximation, i.e., we assume that
\left\langle\ln Z^{-1}(w,\epsilon)\right\rangle=\ln\left\langle Z^{-1}(w,\epsilon)\right\rangle
, which nevertheless leads to accurate results
confirmed by an alternative Feynman diagram method (see Appendix
H
) and numerical simulations.
A rigorous proof would be very interesting in future studies.

Next, we compute
\ln\left\langle Z^{-1}(w,\epsilon)\right\rangle
. By using a multivariate complex Gaussian integral representation of
the determinant and the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation, we can write

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\ln\operatorname{det}\left[(\bar{z}{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J}^{\dagger})(z{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J})+|\epsilon|^{2}{\mathds{1}}\right]\right\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{N}\ln\left[\lim_{|\epsilon|\to 0}\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{y}\exp\left(-|\epsilon|^{2}\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}-\bm{y}^{\dagger}\bm{y}-\mathrm{i}\bar{w}\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{y}-\mathrm{i}w\bm{y}^{\dagger}\bm{z}\right)\left\langle\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\bm{z}^{\dagger}{\tilde{\mathbf{D}}}^{\dagger}\bm{y}+\mathrm{i}\bm{y}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\bm{z}\right)\right\rangle\right],

where
\propto
means up to an irrelevant factor that does not contribute to the leading order of
\phi
in the large
N
limit.
To carry out the disorder average in Eq. (
15
), the local chaos hypothesis is used
[
23
]
, which states that
large random neural networks is able to reach a steady-state where the network state is independent of the random coupling matrices.
In fact, the operating point is fixed in the current context, and thus
the disorder average can be even done without the local chaos hypothesis, unless the steady-state distribution of network activity are taken into account.
To proceed, we have to introduce the following order parameters together with their hatted conjugate parameters, introduced through
applying the Fourier representation of the delta function,

\displaystyle u\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z},

\displaystyle u^{\prime}\equiv\frac{1}{N}(\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}\bm{z})^{\dagger}(\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}\bm{z}),

(16)

\displaystyle t\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{y}^{\dagger}\bm{z},

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{y}^{\dagger}(\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}\bm{z}),

\displaystyle\bar{t}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{y},

\displaystyle\bar{t^{\prime}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}(\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}\bm{z})^{\dagger}\bm{y},

\displaystyle v\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{y}^{\dagger}\bm{y},

in which
u,v,u^{\prime}
are real positive parameters, and
t,t^{\prime}
are complex parameters. These order parameters have their own physics interpretations,
e.g.,
\mathrm{i}t
is exactly the Green’s function, while
uv
gives the eigenvector overlap function (see Appendix
D
).

After a lengthy calculation (see Appendix
C
), the potential can be recast into the following concise form,

\phi\propto\frac{1}{N}\ln\left[\lim_{|\epsilon|^{2}\to 0}\int\mathcal{D}[\cdots]\exp\left[N\left(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}+\phi_{3}\right)\right]\right],

(17)

where
\int\mathcal{D}[\cdots]
denotes the integration over all relevant order parameters and their hatted conjugated variables, and the sum of
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
and
\phi_{3}

is called the action in physics. More precisely,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=

\displaystyle-|\epsilon|^{2}u-v+\mathrm{i}\hat{u}u+\mathrm{i}\hat{v}v+\mathrm{i}\bar{\hat{t}}t+\mathrm{i}\bar{t}\hat{t}+\mathrm{i}\hat{u}^{\prime}u^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}t^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\bar{t^{\prime}}\hat{t}^{\prime},

(18)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left[\bar{\hat{t}}\hat{t}-\hat{u}\hat{v}+(\Phi^{\prime}_{ii})^{2}(\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}\hat{t}^{\prime}-\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v})+\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}(\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}\hat{t}+\bar{\hat{t}}\hat{t}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\phi_{3}=

\displaystyle-\alpha\ln(u^{\prime}v-\bar{t^{\prime}}t^{\prime})-\mathrm{i}\bar{w}\bar{t}-\mathrm{i}wt

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\ln\left(k-ik\Lambda_{\mu}t^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}k\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\bar{t^{\prime}}+\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}\right),

where
k=\left[u^{\prime}v-\bar{t^{\prime}}t^{\prime}\right]^{-1}
. By applying the Laplace method in the large
N
limit,
\phi
is just the sum of the above three terms.
All relevant order parameters obey saddle-point equations whose solutions maximizing the action. Technical details for deriving the saddle point equations are given in Appendix
D
.

The non-normal matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
can be decomposed by using its right and left eigenvectors. The eigenvector overlap function captured by
\frac{uv}{\pi}
determines the spectrum boundary (see Appendix
D
), i.e.,

uv=0
outside the spectrum, and
uv>0
within the spectrum. Therefore, the value of
uv
is related to whether the Green’s function is analytic (the Cauchy-Riemann condition is satisfied) or not.

Taking the limit
|\epsilon|\to 0
, the spectral density can be estimated by solving the following closed-form equations:

\displaystyle C\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}T-1|^{2}+|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}u^{\prime}v}=1,

(19a)

\displaystyle C\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\Lambda_{\mu}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}T-1|^{2}+|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}u^{\prime}v}=Bw,

(19b)

where
T\equiv\mathrm{i}t^{\prime}
and
\Lambda_{\mu}
is the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}
, which relies on the model parameters as follows,

\Lambda_{\mu}=c+\gamma\sum_{r=1}^{d}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}r\mu/P).

(20)

Details of the derivation are given in Appendix
D
.
The neural-state dependent auxiliary quantities are specified as follows,

\displaystyle A(w,\hat{t}^{\prime},\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{ii}^{\prime}\hat{t}^{\prime}+\bar{w}|^{2}-(\Phi_{ii}^{\prime})^{2}\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}},

(21a)

\displaystyle B(w,\hat{t}^{\prime},\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}}{|\Phi_{ii}^{\prime}\hat{t}^{\prime}+\bar{w}|^{2}-(\Phi_{ii}^{\prime})^{2}\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}},

(21b)

\displaystyle C(w,\hat{t}^{\prime},\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\Phi^{\prime}_{ii})^{2}}{|(\Phi_{ii}^{\prime}\hat{t}^{\prime}+\bar{w})|^{2}-(\Phi_{ii}^{\prime})^{2}\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}},

(21c)

where
\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}
is determined by the choice of activation function and the operating point.
Although
A
does not appear in Eq. (

Note that the order parameters

can be expressed by

\displaystyle T=B\bar{w}+C\hat{t}^{\prime},

(22a)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}v=-C^{2}\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}.

(22b)

Considering Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
21

immediately find the Eq. (

of

, and
\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}

By setting
uv\to 0
,
we have the following boundary equations (see details in Appendix
D
)

\displaystyle C\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}\left(B\bar{w}+C\hat{t}^{\prime}\right)-1|^{2}}=1,

(23a)

\displaystyle C\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\Lambda_{\mu}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}\left(B\bar{w}+C\hat{t}^{\prime}\right)-1|^{2}}=Bw.

(23b)

After solving Eq. (
19
) in terms of
\hat{t}^{\prime}
,
\hat{u}^{\prime}
and
\hat{v}
, one can recover the Green’s
function
G
and
uv
through the saddle-point condition, i.e.,

\displaystyle G=A\bar{w}+B\hat{t}^{\prime},

(24a)

\displaystyle uv=-AC\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v}.

(24b)

We conclude that
G
leads to the density by using Eq. (
14
),
and
uv
reports the eigenvector overlap function by
O(w,\bar{w})=uv/\pi
(see the derivation in Appendix

III.2
Spectrum of synaptic coupling matrix

The Jacobian spectrum can be greatly simplified by assuming
\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}
is an identity matrix. In this case,
the spectrum of the Jacobian reduces to the spectrum of the synaptic coupling matrix.
In this simplification, we have
A=B=C
. Hence, Eq. (
19
) reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mu}G|^{2}+|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}uv}{|\Lambda_{\mu}G-1|+|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}uv}=1,

(25a)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\Lambda_{\mu}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}G-1|+|\Lambda_{\mu}|^{2}uv}=w.

(25b)

This result coincides with that derived by a diagrammatic method (see Appendix
H
), which confirms our annealed computation in turn.
The spectrum boundary obeys the following equations,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mu}G|^{2}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}G-1|}=1,

(26a)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\Lambda_{\mu}}{|\Lambda_{\mu}G-1|}=w.

(26b)

We remark that only the case of
c=0,d=1
can have an analytic closed-form expression of the spectrum.
The spectra of both the connectivity and Jacobian matrices bear disk-like or annulus-like shapes
[
24
,
25
]
. Technical details to obtain the
closed form results for the connectivity are given in Appendix
E
.
Our work
extends this single ring law to the Jacobian matrix (see details in Appendix
E
), as the outer boundary of the
spectrum is given by

R_{\rm{out}}=\gamma\sqrt{\alpha\left\langle(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}\right\rangle_{r}+\left\langle\Phi^{\prime}\right\rangle_{r}^{2}},

(27)

where
\left\langle\Phi^{\prime}\right\rangle_{r}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}
and
\left\langle(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}\right\rangle_{r}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}_{ii})^{2}

denote the empirical moments of the diagonal elements of
\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}
. The subscript
r
means that the moments can be computed by using the distribution of synaptic currents.

Spectrum, phase diagram, chaos and sequence retrieval

In this section, we investigate the effects of model parameters (i.e.,
\alpha
,
c
,

and

and moreover the Jacobian matrix, which determines the stability and time scales of the linearized dynamics.

The loading rate
\alpha
constrains the rank
of the connectivity matrix, i.e.,
\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{J})=\min(N,\alpha N)
, whose derivation uses elementary (e.g., Sylvester) rank inequality. Therefore,
if

(1-\alpha)N
eigenvalues localized at the origin
[Fig.

(a)]. In addition, the spectrum is affected in the other two manners.
(i) When

, there emerge voids within the spectrum. The number of the voids depends
on the model parameters, especially the Hebbian length

. As
\alpha
decreases, the voids are enlarged,
and all non-zero eigenvalues finally collapse onto the outer spectrum-boundary
as
\alpha
gets close (but not equal) to zero. (ii) The spectrum becomes significantly extended in both
horizontal and longitudinal directions when
\alpha
gets larger, roughly grows as
\sqrt{\alpha}
when
\alpha
is large.

, i.e., the spectrum is invariant under

.
Because

, it is easy to see any function

V
Conclusion

In this work, we develop a random matrix theory of asymmetric associative memory networks of arbitrary Hebbian length. The network function such as sequence retrieval is related to
the spectral properties of the connectivity matrix and the Jacobian matrix. First, we calculate the spectrum of the Jacobian
\mathbf{\tilde{D}}=\bm{\xi}^{T}\mathbf{X}\bm{\xi}\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}
, which
consists of the structured matrix
\mathbf{X}
and an arbitrary diagonal (activity-dependent) matrix
\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}
. By this analysis, we extend the well-known single ring theorem
[
24
,
33
]
.
We recover the spectrum of the connectivity matrix by setting
\bm{\Phi^{\prime}}={\mathds{1}}
, which reveals several novel types of spectrum in non-Hermitian random matrix ensembles. In particular,
some types of spectra break the rotational symmetry about the origin, being of nested
d
-hole shapes as well (
\alpha<1
), or break the gauge invariance (
d>1
).
We then study how the model parameters affect the geometric shape of the connectivity spectrum and analyze
the linear stability of the null fixed point, which is determined by the right edge of the spectrum. Approaching the critical boundary from the left-hand side, the network operates with a transient memory, and the time scales are related to
the distance towards the right edge of the spectrum, while the chaos state emerges after the critical line is crossed.
Even if the null fixed point becomes unstable, a small-
\alpha
regime limits the number of unstable directions, allowing for sequence replay as well.
We thus conclude that in a finite-size system approaching the edge of chaos from below has also the computational benefits for sequence memory.

Our theoretical analysis not only applies to a broad class of associative memory models of sequence retrieval, but also applies to mathematical modeling of time-lagged correlations (of arbitrary time delays) in financial or biological time series data
[
18
,
17
]
.
Our study also inspires several promising directions. First, a complete understanding of the edge of chaos in our model requires developing dynamical mean-field theory based on the functional path integral, such as recent theoretical works of random recurrent neural networks
[
34
,
35
]
.
This theory may fully characterize the time scales of the network dynamics beyond the linear stability line, and furthermore clarify whether chaotic attractors emerge continuously. In particular,
the topological complexity, counting the number of fixed points, is thus an important physics quantity to be explored in future works, e.g., via the Kac-Rice formula
[
36
]
.
Taking a step further, considering both the sequence length and the number of sequences is also very interesting, and
the theoretical capacity of the sequence storage may be also calculated, such as in the previous works
[
8
,
10
]
in simpler settings.
Second, the neural circuits in a biological brain are always not fully connected. It is thus interesting to combine the network sparseness in the connectivity with the sequence retrieval function of the network.
Lastly, the spectrum of our non-Hermitian matrix ensemble belongs to an intriguing category with isotropic or anisotropic properties depending on the specific model parameters. Moreover, the density within the spectrum is highly non-uniform with the nested

d
-hole structure. The non-normal property also causes the transient amplification, especially when the network setting falls within the critical regime. Thus, it is also very interesting in future works to show
how these characteristics are modified when more biological constrains are considered, such as cell types, sparseness, and excitation-inhibition balance.

Appendix A
Introduction of spectral density for non-Hermitian random matrices

A.1
Spectral density and the Green’s function

The spectral density of an
N\times N
non-Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{J}
at
z
on the complex plane is defined as

\rho(z)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(2)}(z-\lambda_{i}),

(30)

where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{J}
. To proceed, we introduce the Green’s function
G
or the so-called resolvent in random matrix theory as follows,

G(z)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\frac{1}{{\mathds{1}}_{N}z-\mathbf{J}}\right),

(31)

where
{\mathds{1}}_{N}
is an
N\times N
identity matrix. Transforming the summation over eigenvalues to the integral with the spectral density, we have

G(z)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{z-\lambda},

(32)

where the integral is done over the complex plane. Eigenvalues are not real-valued any more for a non-Hermitian matrix.
Then, we carry out a contour integral of
G(z)
along an arbitrary closed path
\partial\mathcal{C}
, assuming that no eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}

lie on the path, and we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}{z}G(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}{z}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{C}}1=2\pi\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}z\rho(z),

where we have used the residue theorem, and
\mathcal{C}
denotes the region bounded by
\partial\mathcal{C}
.
We then apply the complex version of Gauss’s law as follows,

\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}z\ G(z)=\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}z\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial y}\right)=2\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}z\ \frac{\partial G}{\partial\bar{z}},

(34)

where
x
and
y
is respectively the real part and imaginary part of
w
, and the Wirtinger derivatives are defined as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right),

(35a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right).

(35b)

Hereafter,
\bar{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of the complex number
z
.
Because
\mathcal{C}
is arbitrarily chosen, by comparing Eq. (
33
) and Eq. (
34
), we have

\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial y}\right),

(36)

which indicates that if
G(w)
is an analytic function of
w
, the density vanishes and vice versa. Equation (
36
) thus establishes the
relationship between the spectral density
\rho
and the resolvent
G
.

Next, we give a concrete example to show how to use Eq. (
36
) to calculate the spectral density from the Green’s function.
In the classic circular law for the fully asymmetric random matrix
[
22
]
, where
J_{ij}
and
J_{ji}
follows independently a Gaussian distribution,
the spectral density is bounded by
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
on the complex plane,
and the Green’s function is given by

G(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{z},&\mbox{$z$ outside the circle}\\
\bar{z},&\mbox{$z$ inside the circle}\end{cases}

(37)

where the Green’s function can be derived by using the following replica method (see Appendix
C
) or Feynman diagrammatic techniques (see Appendix
H
).
We immediately have

\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial G(z,\bar{z})}{\partial\bar{z}}=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{$z$ outside the circle}\\
1/\pi.&\mbox{$z$ inside the circle}\end{cases}

(38)

A.2
Poisson equation and eigenvalue potential

From the Gauss’s law, one can define an electrostatic potential to turn the eigenvalue spectrum problem into a two-dimension classical electrostatic problem.
The eigenvalue potential is constructed via the following way

\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial z}=-G.

(39)

Using the Wirtinger derivatives, we arrive at

4\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}\equiv\nabla^{2}\phi=-4\pi\rho(z),

(40)

which suggests that
\rho
is the two-dimensional charge distribution, and the spectral density is now related to the computation of the potential.
Moreover, the Green’s function
G
plays a role like that of an electric field in two dimensional space. The explicit expression of the potential consistent with
the Green’s function reads as follows
[
22
,
37
]
,

\phi=-\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Tr}\ln(z{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J})-\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Tr}\ln(\bar{z}{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J}^{\dagger})

(41)

After using the mathematical identities
\operatorname{Tr}\ln\mathbf{A}=\ln\operatorname{det}\mathbf{A}
,
\operatorname{det}\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}=\operatorname{det}\mathbf{A}
, and
\operatorname{det}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})=\operatorname{det}\mathbf{A}\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}
,
one can rewrite Eq. (
41
) into a compact form as follows

\phi(z)=-\frac{1}{N}\left(\ln\operatorname{det}\left[(\bar{z}{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J}^{\dagger})(z{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J})\right]\right).

(42)

Finally, the procedure to compute the spectral density can be summarized below,

1.

Calculate the potential
\phi(z,\bar{z})
,

2.

Get the Green’s function through
G(z,\bar{z})=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}
,

3.

Calculate the spectral density via
\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\bar{z}}
.

Appendix B
Useful formulas of complex Gaussian integral

In this section, we introduce three types of complex Gaussian integral useful for our calculation of the spectral density of non-Hermitian random matrices.

B.1
Standard form

The first type is given below,

\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}\right),

(43)

where
\bm{z}
is a complex vector of length
N
, and
\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}
denotes the integral over the whole complex plane.

\bm{B}
is a positive definite Hermitian matrix. By a unitary diagonalization of
\mathbf{B}
, we get

\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}=\operatorname{diag}\left(b_{1},\cdots,b_{N}\right).

(44)

Hence, we can introduce the new integral variable
\bm{z}^{\prime}\equiv\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\bm{z}
.
We then rewrite Eq. (
43
) as

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}^{\prime}\exp\left(-\sum_{i}b_{i}\left|z^{\prime}_{i}\right|^{2}\right)=\prod_{i}\frac{\pi}{b_{i}}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}}.

Equation (
45
) suggests that one can turn a determinant to a complex Gaussian integral, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\pi^{N}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}\right).

(46)

B.2
With Hermitian linear term

Now we consider a bit more complex case, where an Hermitian linear term is added to the exponential term in Eq. (
43
):

\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}\right).

(47)

The linear term is Hermitian because
(\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j})^{\dagger}=\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}
.
We first rewrite the exponential term in Eq. (
47
) as

-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}=-(\bm{z}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j})^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}(\bm{z}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j})+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j}.

(48)

Then, with the change of the integral variable
\bm{z}\leftarrow\bm{z}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j}
,
Eq. (
47
) can be transformed into the form of Eq. (
43
), leading to the integral result given below,

\frac{\pi^{N}}{\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}}\exp(\bm{j}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j}),

(49)

which implies the complex Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation as follows,

\exp(\bm{j}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j})=\frac{\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}}{\pi^{N}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}\right).

(50)

B.3
With non-Hermitian linear term

We next consider the added term is a non-Hermitian term as follows,

\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}^{\prime}\right).

(51)

In this case, we can treat
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\dagger}
as independent integral variables and shift them separately as

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{\dagger}\to\bm{z}^{\dagger}+\bm{j}^{\dagger},

(52)

\displaystyle\bm{z}\to\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\prime}.

Then the linear term becomes Hermitian, and we can repeat the manipulation in Eq. (
48
). Finally, the integral is worked out as follows,

\int\mathrm{d}^{2N}\bm{z}\exp\left(-\bm{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\bm{z}+\bm{j}^{\dagger}\bm{z}+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{j}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}}\exp(\bm{j}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{j}^{\prime}).

(53)

Appendix C

Appendix D
Saddle point equations

The Laplace method to estimate the integral
\int\mathcal{D}[\cdots]
requires that

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\left[\cdots\right]}=0,

(75)

i.e., the partial derivatives with respect to all order parameters vanish, yielding the so-called saddle point equations.

\lim_{|\epsilon|^{2}\to 0}
is carried out after the saddle point equations are derived.

D.1
Derivatives with respect to hatted variables

The derivatives with respect to hatted variables lead to the following results:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\hat{u}}=\mathrm{i}u+A\hat{v}=0,

(76a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\hat{v}}=\mathrm{i}v+A\hat{u}+C\hat{u}^{\prime}=0,

(76b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\hat{u}^{\prime}}=\mathrm{i}u^{\prime}+C\hat{v}=0,

(76c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\hat{t}}=\mathrm{i}\bar{t}-A\bar{\hat{t}}-B\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}=0,

(76d)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\hat{t}}}=\mathrm{i}t-A\hat{t}-B\hat{t}^{\prime}=0,

(76e)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\hat{t}^{\prime}}=\mathrm{i}\bar{t^{\prime}}-B\bar{\hat{t}}-C\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}=0,

(76f)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}}=\mathrm{i}t^{\prime}-B\hat{t}-C\hat{t}^{\prime}=0.

(76g)

where the neural-state dependent coefficients are given by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{q_{i}},

(77a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}}{q_{i}},

(77b)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{(\Phi^{\prime}_{ii})^{2}}{q_{i}},

(77c)

where

q_{i}\equiv\bar{\hat{t}}\hat{t}-\hat{u}\hat{v}+(\Phi^{\prime}_{ii})^{2}(\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}\hat{t}^{\prime}-\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{v})+\Phi^{\prime}_{ii}(\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}\hat{t}+\bar{\hat{t}}\hat{t}^{\prime}).

(78)

D.2
Derivatives with respect to non-hatted variables

The derivatives with respect to non-hatted variables proceed as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial u}

\displaystyle=-|\epsilon|^{2}+\mathrm{i}\hat{u}=0,

(79a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial u^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\hat{u}^{\prime}-\alpha kv\mathcal{I}=0,

(79b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial v}

\displaystyle=-1+\mathrm{i}\hat{v}-\alpha ku^{\prime}\mathcal{I}=0,

(79c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}w+\mathrm{i}\bar{\hat{t}}=0,

(79d)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\bar{w}+\mathrm{i}\hat{t}=0,

(79e)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\overline{\hat{t}^{\prime}}+\alpha k\mathcal{I}\bar{t^{\prime}}+\mathrm{i}\alpha k\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=0,

(79f)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\hat{t}^{\prime}+\alpha k\mathcal{I}t^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\alpha k\overline{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}=0,

(79g)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\equiv\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}}{\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}},

(80a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\equiv\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\Lambda_{\mu}}{\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}},

(80b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}\equiv k-\mathrm{i}k\Lambda_{\mu}t^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}k\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\bar{t^{\prime}}+\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}.

(80c)

D.3
Interpretation of order parameters

According to the electrostatic mapping in Sec.
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial w}=-\frac{\partial\psi_{0}}{\partial w}=\mathrm{i}t,

(81)

\displaystyle\bar{G}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\bar{w}}=-\frac{\partial\psi_{0}}{\partial\bar{w}}=\mathrm{i}\bar{t}.

where
\psi_{0}
is the value of
\psi
at the maximum. Hence, the physical meaning of
\mathrm{i}t

is exactly the Green’s function. Equations (
76d
) and (
76e
) suggests that
\mathrm{i}\bar{t}=\overline{\mathrm{i}t}
. Note that
t
and
\bar{t}
are no longer complex conjugates with each other when evaluated at the saddle point,
because the integral is deformed to be over contours in the complex plane
[
38
]
.
Equations (
76f
) and (
76g
) imply that
\bar{t^{\prime}}
is not a complex conjugate of
t^{\prime}
, and
\mathrm{i}\bar{t^{\prime}}=\overline{\mathrm{i}t^{\prime}}
.

By considering Eqs. (
76a
), (
76c
) and (
79c
), we have

A=u(1-C\alpha k\mathcal{I}).

(82)

Similarly, considering Eqs. (
76b
), (
79a
) and (
79b
), we have

A|\epsilon|^{2}=v(1-C\alpha k\mathcal{I}).

(83)

It is immediately found that

\frac{v}{u}=|\epsilon|^{2}.

(84)

If we assume that
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{R}
are the left and right eigenvectors of the non-Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{J}
, respectively,
we then have the spectral decomposition
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{R}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
, which has the biorthogonal property
\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}={\mathds{1}}
.
Note that
\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{L}\neq{\mathds{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}\neq{\mathds{1}}
.
We then define the eigenvector overlap matrix
O_{\alpha\beta}=(\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{L})_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{\dagger}\mathbf{R})_{\beta\alpha}
, which describes the correlation between left and right
eigenvectors. Then,
the one-point correlation function of eigenvectors is defined as
[
39
]

O\left({w,\bar{w}}\right)=\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}O_{\alpha\alpha}\delta^{(2)}(w-\lambda_{\alpha})\right\rangle.

(85)

The correlator is related to the eigenvalue potential through the relationship

O\left({w,\bar{w}}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{|\epsilon|\to 0}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\bar{\epsilon}}

[
33
]
.
Then we can calculate the eigenvector overlap function as follows,

\displaystyle O(w,\bar{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\bar{\epsilon}}

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}|\epsilon|^{2}u^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}uv,

where
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
is implied. Hence, the product
uv
is exactly the eigenvector overlap function.

Considering Eq. (
79d
) and Eq. (
79e
), we also have

\displaystyle\bar{\hat{t}}=w,

(87)

\displaystyle\hat{t}=\bar{w}.

Appendix E
Closed-form solutions of spectrum

In general, Eq. (
96
) can only be solved numerically,
but closed-form solutions can be found when the spectrum is rotationally symmetric.
One simplest case is
c=0,d=1
, where the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}
form a circle
on the complex plane as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mu/P)

(104)

\displaystyle=\gamma\cos(2\pi\mu/P)-\mathrm{i}\gamma\sin(2\pi\mu/P),

which results in the rotational symmetry of the spectrum, as we shall show later.

E.1
Single ring law

We first consider the spectrum of the connectivity.
Equation (
103
) can be further calculated as follows,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}\overline{G}G}{\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}\overline{G}G-\Lambda_{\mu}G-\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\overline{G}+1},

(105)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2a\gamma\cos(2\pi\mu/P)-2b\gamma\sin(2\pi\mu/P)+1},

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2\gamma|G|\sin(2\pi\mu/P+\arctan(b/a))+1},

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2\gamma|G|\sin(x+\arctan(b/a))+1},

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2\gamma|G|\sin x+1},

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{|\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-1|},

where we have decomposed
G=a+b\mathrm{i}
into its real and imaginary parts.
The reasonable values of
|G|^{2}
are given by

|G|^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(1\pm\alpha)}&\alpha<1,\\
\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(1+\alpha)}&\alpha>1.\\
\end{cases}

(106)

Then by substituting
z(\theta)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}
can
be re-parameterized as
\Lambda(\theta)=\gamma z(\theta)
, and for any function

\mathcal{F}
, we have

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{\mu})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\mathcal{F}(\Lambda(\theta))
.
Thus the second equation of Eq. (
103
) can be further simplified by

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\gamma z}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-\gamma Gz-\gamma\overline{G}\overline{z}+1}

(107)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|z|=1}\mathrm{d}z\frac{1}{z}\frac{\gamma z}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-\gamma Gz-\gamma\overline{G}/z+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|z|=1}\mathrm{d}z\frac{\gamma z}{(\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}+1)z-\gamma Gz^{2}-\gamma\overline{G}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|z|=1}\mathrm{d}z\frac{\frac{1}{G}z}{-(z-1/(\gamma G))(z-\gamma\overline{G})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-1}\frac{1}{G}&\gamma|G|>1,\\
\frac{\alpha\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}{1-\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}}\frac{1}{G}&\gamma|G|<1.\end{cases}

In the above Eq. (
107
), we have used
\overline{z}=\frac{1}{z}
and carried out the contour integral to obtain the last equality.
Therefore the inner and outer boundaries of the spectrum are given below.

•

When
\alpha<1
, the radii of the outer and inner boundaries are respectively given below

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}}=\gamma\sqrt{1+\alpha},

(108)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\alpha}{\frac{1}{(\sqrt{1-\alpha})^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}=\gamma\left(\sqrt{1-\alpha}\right)^{3}.

•

When
\alpha>1
, the inner boundary vanishes and the radius of the outer boundary is given by

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}}=\gamma\sqrt{1+\alpha}.

(109)

We next derive the analytic spectral density. The first equation of Eq. (
102
) is simplified as

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}\overline{G}G+\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}uv}{\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}\overline{G}G-\Lambda_{\mu}G-\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\overline{G}+1+\overline{\Lambda_{\mu}}\Lambda_{\mu}uv}

(110)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}+\gamma^{2}uv}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2a\gamma\cos(2\pi\mu/P)-2b\gamma\sin(2\pi\mu/P)+1+\gamma^{2}uv}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\frac{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}+\gamma^{2}uv}{\gamma^{2}|G|^{2}-2\gamma|G|\sin(2\pi\mu/P+\arctan(b/a))+1+\gamma^{2}uv}

\displaystyle w_{0}

F.1
Calculating the boundary curve

We first give a detailed introduction of how to calculate the boundary curve from Eq. (
96

One can simplify Eq. (

96

and then write the remaining two equations as follows

\displaystyle F_{1}(\bm{x})=0,

(129)

\displaystyle F_{2}(\bm{x})=0,

\displaystyle F_{3}(\bm{x})=0,

where
F_{1}
is the first equation of Eq. (

(resp.

part of the second equation in Eq. (

corresponds

\{{\rm Re}(w),{\rm Im}(w),{\rm Re}(\hat{t}^{\prime}),{\rm Im}(\hat{t}^{\prime})\}

Now we have three real equations and four real variables to be determined,
and thus finding the solution amounts to finding the intersection curve of three surfaces in four-dimensional space.
We use the marching method
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
to carry out this numerical analysis.
This method makes use of local gradient information, consisting of two steps—Newton and tangent steps.

F.1.1
Newton step

Given an initial point in the vicinity of the intersection curve, the newton step bring the starting point closer
to the intersection curve. The update rule is given by

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta_{t}\bm{x},

(130)

where the Newton increment is set to be the linear combination of all gradients

\Delta_{t}\bm{x}=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\mathcal{D}_{t}^{a}\nabla F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t}).

(131)

The linear coefficient can be found from the following constraint

\Delta_{t}\bm{x}\cdot\nabla F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})=-F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t}),

(132)

which finds the direction which approaches three surfaces at the same time.
By writing Eq. (
132
) into the component form:

\sum_{b,i}\mathcal{D}_{t}^{b}\left(\nabla F_{b}(\bm{x})\right)_{i}\left(\nabla F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})\right)_{i}=-F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t}),

(133)

we find it is convenient to define the following gradient matrix

\mathbf{g}_{t}\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}&\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial x_{3}}&\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial x_{4}}\\
\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}&\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x_{3}}&\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x_{4}}\\
\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial x_{2}}&\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial x_{3}}&\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial x_{4}}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(134)

Defining
\bm{F}_{t}=\{F_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})\}
, we can then rewrite Eq. (
133
) into a compact form as

\mathbf{g}_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{{\cal D}}_{t}=-\bm{F}_{t}.

(135)

Hence the linear coefficient is solved as

\bm{{\cal D}}_{t}=-\frac{\bm{F}_{t}}{\mathbf{g}_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}}.

(136)

F.1.2

Given an initial point on the intersection curve, the tangent step will make the point move along
the direction of the local tangent of the intersection curve.
The tangent direction should be orthogonal to all three gradients, specified by

\bm{T}=\left|\begin{matrix}\bm{e}_{1}&\bm{e}_{2}&\bm{e}_{3}&\bm{e}_{4}\\
g_{11}&g_{12}&g_{13}&g_{14}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&g_{23}&g_{24}\\
g_{31}&g_{32}&g_{33}&g_{34}\\
\end{matrix}\right|

(137)

where
\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3}
, and
\bm{e}_{4}
are standard bases in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. It is easy to
check

is indeed orthogonal to the three gradients.
Therefore the tangent step is given by

Appendix G
Numerical method for calculating Lyapunov exponents

The evolution of the perturbation
\delta\mathbf{r}
is given by

\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\delta\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{D}(t)\delta\mathbf{r},

(140)

where
\mathbf{D}(t)
is the Jacobian of the system at time
t
.
Hence the long time perturbation can be expressed into the exponential form as

\delta\mathbf{r}(T)=\delta\mathbf{r}(0)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{D}(t)/\tau\mathrm{d}t\right).

(141)

The perturbation will grow exponentially along the directions with positive exponents,
and shrink along the directions with negative exponents.
Lyapunov exponents (LEs) are a set of exponents
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{N}
in the descending order.
The collection of LEs is called the Lyapunov spectrum. They
describe the growth rate of the volume of the perturbation spanning the tangent space.

The maximum Lyapunov exponent (MLE)
\ell_{1}
is commonly
used as the criterion for determining whether a system is chaotic or not.
The dynamics is chaotic if
\ell_{1}>0
, indicating that nearby trajectories diverge exponentially fast.
The MLE can be calculated as follows,

\ell_{1}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\lim_{||\delta\mathbf{r}(0)||\to 0}\ln\frac{||\delta\mathbf{r}(T)||}{||\delta\mathbf{r}(0)||},

(142)

where the two limits can not be exchanged.
The full Lyapunov spectrum provides additional insights into the long time behavior of the dynamics.
The LEs are the logarithms of the eigenvalues of the following Oseledets matrix
[
29
,
30
,
44
]

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[\left(\exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{D}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{D}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2t}}
.
By this definition, inserting Eq. (
141
) into Eq. (
142
), one can find the maximal value of LEs is the very MLE.
However, the long-term Jacobian would become ill-conditioned, i.e., the ratio between the largest and smallest singular values diverges with time.

In practice, we directly estimate the shrinkage or expansion of the volume of the tangent space. More precisely, we first initialize a matrix
\mathbf{Q}
by an identity, and then run the discretized dynamics
of the original continuous one
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{J}\phi(\mathbf{r})
as

\mathbf{r}_{a+1}=F(\mathbf{r}_{a}),

(143)

where
a=1,2,\ldots,T
indicates the discrete time step with a fixed small step size
\epsilon
,
F(\cdot)
defines the discrete mapping,
and the associated Jacobian
\mathbf{D}_{a}={\mathds{1}}+\frac{\epsilon}{\tau}\mathbf{D}(a\epsilon)
. The Jacobian at the state
\mathbf{r}_{a}
can then be used to update the
\mathbf{Q}
matrix.
After performing the QR decomposition of the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, we can relate the
i
-th diagonal element of
\mathbf{R}
in the QR decomposition to the
i
-th Lyapunov exponent by a time average as follows
[
29
,
30
]
,

\ell_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{a=1}^{T}\ln\mathbf{R}_{ii}(a),

(144)

where
\mathbf{R}_{ii}
describes the expansion or contraction of
\mathbf{Q}
. An initial relaxation of the original dynamics is required before the calculation of the Lyapunov spectrum,
as the dynamics has to converge to an attractor state. The pseudocode for the estimation of LEs is given in the algorithm
3
.

) for a sufficiently long time, and record the final state as

do

9:

\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}\leftarrow\operatorname{QR\ decomposition}
of
\mathbf{Q}

11:

a\leftarrow a+1

12:

end

13:

\bm{\ell}
in descending order

Appendix H
Feynman diagrammatic techniques for calculating the eigen-spectrum of non-Hermitian synaptic coupling matrices

The eigenvalue spectrum of non-Hermitian coupling matrix can also be derived by adopting Feynman diagrammatic techniques. We show the technical details to reproduce
the results obtained by applying the annealed approximation, as a cross-checking of the spectrum formula.

The synaptic coupling between any two neurons
i
and
j
in our model is given by

{J_{ij}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}{\left[{c\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}+\gamma\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}{\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu+r}}}\right]},

(145)

which contains the concurrent Hebbian term and pattern-separated Hebbian term. The strength of the concurrent Hebbian term is specified by

c
, and the strength of the pattern-separated term is specified by
\gamma
.

In this model, we have
P
patterns, and each pattern follows an independent Rademacher distribution,
i.e.,
p\left({\xi_{i}^{\mu}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\delta\left({\xi_{i}^{\mu}-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\delta\left({\xi_{i}^{\mu}+1}\right)
,
in which the superscript
\mu
denotes the index of pattern and the subscript
i
denotes the index of neuron. The pattern entries can be Gaussian i.i.d. random variables, which does not
change the distribution of the following rotated patterns.
We are interested in the situation of large
P
and
N
, with the fixed memory load

\alpha=\frac{P}{N}.

(146)

Note that these patterns form a cyclic sequence with periodic boundary, corresponding to an ordered stimulus sequence in animal experiments
[
19
]
.

The asymmetric couplings in Eq. (
145
) can be recast into the matrix form:

\mathbf{J}=\frac{1}{N}{{\bm{\xi}}^{T}}{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\xi},

(147)

in which the matrix
\mathbf{X}
is a
P\times P
circulant matrix defined by

{X_{\mu\nu}}=c{\delta_{\mu\nu}}+\gamma\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}{{\delta_{\mu,[({\nu+r})\bmod P]}}}.

(148)

For a circulant matrix, the
m
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{X}
is given by
[
45
]

{\Lambda_{m}}=c+\gamma\sum_{r=1}^{d}\cos\Bigl(2\pi\frac{mr}{P}\Bigr).

(149)

Note that the non-Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be diagonalized using a unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}
as
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}
. Hence, we
introduce a rotated memory pattern
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
(
\forall\mu
) defined as

\hat{\bm{\xi}}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\xi}.

(150)

Considering the case of
P\to\infty
, and using the central limit theorem, we have

\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{i}^{\mu}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{\nu=1}^{P}{U_{\mu\nu}\xi_{i}^{\nu}}\right\rangle=0,

(151)

and

\left\langle\bar{{{\hat{\xi}}}}_{i}^{\mu}\hat{\xi}_{i}^{\mu}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\sum_{\nu=1}^{P}{\bar{U}_{\mu\nu}\xi_{i}^{\nu}}\sum_{\rho=1}^{P}{U_{\mu\rho}\xi_{i}^{\rho}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{\nu=1}^{P}{{{\left({U_{\mu\nu}\bar{U}_{\mu\nu}}\right)}^{2}}{{\left({\xi_{i}^{\nu}}\right)}^{2}}}=1,

(152)

where the average is done over the distribution of the original pattern. We conclude that the
distribution of the rotated memory patterns is an i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian distribution, which greatly helps our analytical computation.
The coupling matrix can thus be rewritten as

\mathbf{J}=\frac{1}{N}\hat{\bm{\xi}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Lambda}\hat{\bm{\xi}}.

(153)

In the following derivation, we denote an
N\times P
matrix
\bm{\Xi}=\hat{\bm{\xi}}^{\dagger}
. Then the coupling matrix is recast as

\mathbf{J}=\frac{1}{N}\bm{\Xi}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Xi}^{\dagger},

(154)

and the only non-vanishing second-order moments are given as follows,

\left\langle{{\Xi_{i\mu}}\bar{\Xi}_{i\mu}}\right\rangle=1.

(155)

The density of eigenvalues for the random matrix
\mathbf{J}
is defined as follows,

\rho(z)=\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta^{(2)}\left(z-\lambda_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbf{J}}.

(156)

The density is related to the Green’s function
[
37
]
:

G\left({z,\bar{z}}\right)=\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{J}}=\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}\frac{1}{z{\mathds{1}}_{N}-\mathbf{J}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{J}},

(157)

and we can get the density from the Green’s function via

\rho\left({x,y}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{{\partial\bar{z}}}\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{J}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{{\partial\bar{z}}}G\left({z,\bar{z}}\right),

(158)

which actually expresses the Gauss law in two dimension. The spectral density is clearly related to the nonholomorphic behavior of the Green’s function
[
37
]
.

In order to calculate the density of eigenvalues of this non-Hermitian coupling matrix,
we first define an auxiliary matrix
{\cal J}
as

{{\cal J}}=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}\mathbf{J}&0\\
0&{{\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}}}\end{array}}\right),

(159)

and the quaternionic Green’s function (resolvent) can be defined as

{{\cal G}}\left(Q\right)=\left\langle{\frac{1}{{Q\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{N}-{{\cal J}}}}}\right\rangle,

(160)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,
{\mathds{1}}_{N}
denotes an
N\times N
identity matrix, and

Q=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}z&{\mathrm{i}\bar{w}}\\
{\mathrm{i}w}&{\bar{z}}\end{array}}\right),

(161)

is a quaternion in the
2\times 2
matrix form. For an
i\times j
matrix
A
and an
m\times n
matrix
B
, their
Kronecker product
\otimes
is
defined as

A\otimes B=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}A_{11}B&A_{12}B&\dots&A_{1j}B\\
A_{21}B&A_{22}B&\dots&A_{2j}B\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
A_{i1}B&A_{i2}B&\dots&A_{ij}B\\
\end{array}\right),

(162)

which forms a new matrix with dimension
(im)\times(jn)
.

Using this quaternionic Green’s function, we can define a Green’s function of
2\times 2
matrix form:

G\left(Q\right)=\frac{1}{N}{\rm{bTr_{2}}}{{\cal G}}\left(Q\right),

(163)

where
\rm{bTr}_{2}
denotes a block trace operation taking the
trace separately for four
N\times N
block matrices in a
2N\times 2N
matrix, yielding
a
2\times 2
matrix.
Using the block inverse formula:

{\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}}\right)^{-1}}=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{{A^{-1}}+{A^{-1}}B{{\left({D-CA^{-1}B}\right)}^{-1}}C{A^{-1}}}&{-{A^{-1}}B{{\left({D-{C^{-1}}AB}\right)}^{-1}}}\\
{-{{\left({D-CA^{-1}B}\right)}^{-1}}C{A^{-1}}}&{{{\left({D-CA^{-1}B}\right)}^{-1}}}\end{array}}\right),

(164)

we obtain the explicit form of
G(Q)
as follows,

G\left(Q\right)=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\frac{1}{N}\left\langle{{\rm{Tr}}\frac{{\bar{z}{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}-{\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}}}}{D}}\right\rangle}&{\frac{{-\mathrm{i}\bar{w}}}{N}\left\langle{{\rm{Tr}}\frac{1}{D}}\right\rangle}\\
{\frac{{-\mathrm{i}w}}{N}\left\langle{{\rm{Tr}}\frac{1}{D}}\right\rangle}&{\frac{1}{N}\left\langle{{\rm{Tr}}\frac{{z{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}-\mathbf{J}}}{D}}\right\rangle}\end{array}}\right),

(165)

where
D=\left({z{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}-\mathbf{J}}\right)\left({\bar{z}{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}-{\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}}}\right)+{\left|w\right|^{2}}{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}
.
G(Q)

can be simplified in the following form as

G\left(Q\right)=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}g&{\mathrm{i}\bar{v}}\\
{\mathrm{i}v}&{\bar{g}}\end{array}}\right),

(166)

g={\frac{1}{N}{\rm{Tr}}\left\langle{\frac{{\bar{z}{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}-{\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}}}}{D}}\right\rangle},\quad v=-\frac{{\bar{w}}}{N}{\rm{Tr}}\left\langle\frac{1}{D}\right\rangle,

(167)

where we find that the Green’s function is given by

G\left({z,\bar{z}}\right)=\mathop{\lim}\limits_{\left|w\right|\to 0}g.

(168)

The eigenvector correlator is given by
[
46
]

O\left({z,\bar{z}}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathop{\lim}\limits_{\left|w\right|\to 0}{\left|v\right|^{2}}.

(169)

This shows that the eigenvector correlation is actually the product of the off-diagonal elements of the quaternionic Green’s function.

O\left({z,\bar{z}}\right)=0
provides the condition that determines the boundary in the two-dimension complex space separating holomorphic and nonholomorphic solutions of
the spectral problem
[
39
,
46
]
.

Provided that
\|{\cal J}{\cal Q}^{-1}\|<\|{\mathds{1}}_{2N}\|
, we can expand the quaternionic Green’s function into a geometric series

{{\cal G}}{\rm{=}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}+\left\langle{{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal J}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal J}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal J}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}}\right\rangle+...,

(170)

where
{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{\rm{=}}{Q^{-1}}\otimes{{\mathds{1}}_{N}}
.
According to the definition of
\cal J
in Eq. (
159
), we can decompose
\cal J
as follows

{{\cal J}}{\rm{=}}\frac{1}{N}{{\cal X}{\cal L}}{{{\cal X}}^{\dagger}},\quad{{\cal X}}{\rm{=}}\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}\bm{\Xi}&0\\
0&\bm{\Xi}\end{array}}\right),\quad{{\cal L}}=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}\bm{\Lambda}&0\\
0&{{\bm{\Lambda}^{\dagger}}}\end{array}}\right).

(171)

As a result, we can precisely separate the random parts and the deterministic parts in the series in Eq. (

{{\cal G}}{\rm{=}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}+\left\langle{{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal X}}\frac{{{\cal L}}}{N}{{{\cal X}}^{\dagger}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal X}}\frac{{{\cal L}}}{N}{{{\cal X}}^{\dagger}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}{{\cal X}}\frac{{{\cal L}}}{N}{{{\cal X}}^{\dagger}}{{{\cal Q}}^{-1}}}\right\rangle+\ldots.

(172)
