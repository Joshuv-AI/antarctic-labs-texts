Abstract
. We present a new approach for computing planar hexagonal meshes that approximate a given surface, represented as a triangle mesh. Our method is based on two novel technical contributions. First, we introduce Coordinate Power Fields , which are a pair of tangent vector fields on the surface that fulfill a certain continuity constraint. We prove that the fulfillment of this constraint guarantees the existence of a seamless parameterization with quantized rotational jumps, which we then use to regularly remesh the surface. We additionally propose an optimization framework for finding Coordinate Power Fields, which also fulfill additional constraints, such as alignment, sizing and bijectivity. Second, we build upon this framework to address a challenging meshing problem: planar hexagonal meshing. To this end, we suggest a combination of conjugacy, scaling and alignment constraints, which together lead to planarizable hexagons. We demonstrate our approach on a variety of surfaces, automatically generating planar hexagonal meshes on complicated meshes, which were not achievable with existing methods.

1.
Introduction

Trianglular meshes are the most prevalent surface representation in geometry processing. However, for many applications, such as architectural design, animation and numerical simulation, a more structured representation is required, such as a quadrangular or hexagonal mesh. Specifically in architectural design, meshes with planar faces are amenable to realization using wood or glass panels, and are thus highly sought after
(
Li and Knippers 2015
;
Sonntag
et al. 2019
)
. While planar quadrangular meshes have been intensively researched in recent years
(
Liu
et al. 2006
;
Pottmann et al. 2015
)
,
planar hexagonal meshes remain an open challenge. Due to considerable technical difficulties, such as a strict restriction on allowable element orientations, and the necessary existence of non-convex faces, existing approaches are either limited to specific types of surfaces, or are not fully automatic, or both
(
Pottmann et al. 2015
)
.

From the perspective of the geometry processing framework available for meshing, much progress has been made in recent years
(
Bommes
et al. 2009
;
Bommes et al. 2013a
;
Campen
et al. 2015
)
.
Still, the “standard” approach relies on computing vector fields on the surface, which are the
candidate gradients
of the parameterization functions
(
Bommes et al. 2013b
)
. However, with the exception of a few recent works, there is no guarantee that these candidates are in fact
integrable
, i.e. that there exist parameterization functions whose gradients are these vector fields. Furthermore,
while using the candidate gradients as the primary variables is convenient in some applications, in many cases, such as the planar hexagonal meshing application, and especially when the gradients are not expected to be orthogonal, this is not the most natural choice.

We propose a novel optimization framework for parameterization-based meshing, whose primary variables are a pair of tangent vector fields, which are candidate
Coordinate Power Fields
(CPF). CPFs are a generalization of
coordinate vector fields
, which are the pushforward of the
2D
coordinate grid under a given local patch parameterization. Unlike coordinate vector fields, coordinate power fields allow for rotational jumps in the parameterization, and thus (up to global holonomy constraints) lead to a seamless global paramterization. Specifically, at the heart of our framework lies a new
continuity
constraint, which two tangent vector fields should fulfill in order to be CPFs, and which provably guarantees the local existence of a seamless parameterization with quantized rotational jumps.

We leverage our framework to compute planar hexagonal meshes. Specifically, we define additional constraints on the CPFs, using a novel
Dupin metric
approach, such that a pushed forward hexagonal grid would be planarizable. We demonstrate that this holds if the CPFs are
conjugate
, and are additionally aligned and sized according to the
Dupin Indicatrix

(
Do Carmo 2016
)
. By combining these constraints with our optimization framework we automatically generate planar hexagonal meshes that approximate a wide variety of shapes, including complex shapes which, to the best of our knowledge, are not achievable by existing automatic methods. Furthermore, on meshes where existing approaches are applicable, we achieve a comparable or better planarity error as existing approaches, while using a considerably smaller number of elements. Finally, we demonstrate that our framework is additionally applicable to planar quad meshing.

1.1.
Related Work

Global seamless quantized parameterization.

Global parameterization, meshing and directional field design are vast topics in geometry processing, with ample amount of existing work. For a general overview of these topics we refer the reader to the excellent surveys on parameterization
(
Sheffer
et al. 2006
;
Hormann
et al. 2007
)
, quad meshing
(
Bommes et al. 2013b
)
and directional field design
(
Vaxman et al. 2016
)
.

Our meshing is parameterization-guided, namely, we compute an
integer grid-map

(
Bommes et al. 2013a
;
Campen
et al. 2015
)
and push forward a regular grid through this map to the surface to generate the new mesh elements. Within this framework, our main contributions is the computation of the gradients of a
seamless
global parameterization with quantized rotational continuity across the cuts. The remaining parts of the algorithm, namely integrating the parameterization, quantizing the translations, generating the mesh
(
Vaxman
et al. 2019
)
, and planarization
(
Tang
et al. 2014
)
are based on existing work.

One challenge in this framework is achieving smooth gradient vector fields while allowing for discontinuities which lead to quantized singularities (or
cone points
). The most prevalent approach to designing such fields is working with rotationally symmetric vector fields (RoSy-fields), which are computed by solving a mixed-integer
(
Bommes
et al. 2009
)
or integer-valued
(
Farchi and
Ben-Chen 2018
)
optimization problem, or with a reformulation to a complex-valued problem
(
Knöppel et al. 2013
;
Viertel and
Osting 2019
;
Azencot et al. 2017
)
(which led to the notion of
power fields
). More versatility is achieved with complex polynomials (PolyVectors)
(
Diamanti et al. 2014
)
, as they relax the requirement of rotational symmetry. Additionally, generalization beyond cross fields has been suggested by using a non-Euclidean metric, either extrinsically
(
Panozzo et al. 2014
)
or intrinsically
(
Jiang et al. 2015a
)
.

These rotational fields are then integrated into an
integer grid-map

(
Bommes et al. 2013a
)
, where care is taken to guarantee that some translational components are also quantizable
(
Lyon
et al. 2019
)
. However, with the exception of a few methods, most approaches that compute direction fields do not guarantee their
integrability
. Thus, the resulting parameterization gradients often deviate from the computed direction fields. Notable exceptions are PGP
(
Ray
et al. 2006
)
, that attempt to reduce the curl from the computed field before integrating, and the work of Myles et al.
(
Myles
et al. 2014
)
, that explicitly optimizes for a parameterization that agrees with an input cross field, yet it may introduce additional cone points. Furthermore, both approaches use a cross-field, which cannot, by definition, define the scale of a non-uniform parameterization, or the orientation of a non-conformal one (since cross-fields are unit-length and orthogonal). Harmonic cone-based seamless parameterization methods (e.g., HGP
(
Bright
et al. 2017
)
, and the orbifold methods
(
Aigerman and
Lipman 2016
)
) guarantee local injectivity for given cone values, yet do not consider the field, hence they also cannot generate non-uniform parameterizations. The simplex assembly method
(
Fu and Liu 2016
)
uses affine transformations as the main variables and guarantees injectivity, yet it does not allow for quantized rotational discontinuities, nor does it consider the alignment. The only method that allows for non-orthogonal fields, considers alignment, and also optimizes integrability is the work by
Diamanti et al. 2015
on integrable PolyVector fields. However, PolyVector fields allow for
any
set of consistently oriented vector fields, and thus form a much bigger space than the space of coordinate vector fields of parameterizations.

Our method, on the other hand, uses the vectors tangent to the meshing elements as the primary variables, thus considerably simplifying the formulation of constraints. In addition, we allow for non-orthogonal gradients, by posing the rotationally-symmetric constraint in the
parametric domain
. Thus, our variables are exactly as general as required to allow for any seamless parameterization with quantized rotations, without spurious degrees of freedom. The methods closest to our approach, are the recent algorithms for computing Chebyshev nets
(
Sageman-Furnas et al. 2019
;
Liu
et al. 2020
)
. The second also used the tangent vector fields as primary variables and introduced an integrability constraint that is formulated using the
Lie Bracket operator
. Notably, this operator is
discretized
, and not
discrete
, hence it will not be exactly zero even if a seamless parameterization exists. We, on the other hand, present a novel
discrete
integrability constraint, which is provably exactly zero if and only if a seamless parameterization exists locally (without considering global homology obstructions). Moreover, our formulation is a generalization of the classic integrability of curl-free piecewise constant vector fields
(
Polthier and
Preuß 2003
)
, which is obtained for the special case
N=1
. The second method used both the tangent vector fields and the candidate gradients as variables, defining the smoothness on the first representation with PolyVectors, and the integrability on the second, and used alternating descent for the optimization. Our approach simplifies this formulation by removing the need for PolyVectors, while keeping the discrete integrability guarantee. Finally, both approaches were tailored for Chebyshev quad meshes, and a generalization to planar hexagonal meshes (or any other general formulation) was not presented.

We further elaborate on the relations between our formulation and the most similar approaches in section
3.4
.

Planar hexagonal meshing.

Planar hexagonal (PH) and planar quad (PQ) meshes are of paramount importance in architecture
(
Pottmann 2007
;
Pottmann et al. 2015
)
. PH meshes in particular are highly sought after for grid-shell constructions with wood panels
(
Li and Knippers 2015
;
Sonntag
et al. 2019
;
Wagner et al. 2020
)
. This is due to their valence-
3
vertices which lead to (conical) meshes with a natural face offset that can be realized with panels of constant thickness. They are further known to have beneficial mechanical properties
(
La Magna
et al. 2012
;
Li 2017
)
.
A few attempts have been made in the architecture literature to generate such meshes given an input smooth surface
(
Li 2017
;
Contestabile and
Iuorio 2019
;
Mesnil
et al. 2017
;
Henriksson and
Hult 2015
)
, yet the resulting algorithms often require manual work and are mostly limited to simple surfaces. It is worth noting that finding the PH mesh, or the flat segments that will form the panels, is only the first step in the realization of the surface. Further structural analysis is required to validate mechanical stability (see e.g. the work done on the Landesgartenschau exhibition hall
(
Li and Knippers 2015
)
), and thus a fully automatic approach that can generate a variety of meshes based on a small number of parameters (such as element size) is critical.

In the graphics and geometry processing literature there has also been a limited amount of work on this topic, unlike PQ meshes which were extensively researched
(
Liu
et al. 2006
;
Zadravec
et al. 2010
;
Liu
et al. 2011
)
. PH meshes are in general more constrained than PQ meshes, as they, for example, require non-convex faces to create a watertight cover of a surface with negative Gaussian curvature
(
Pottmann et al. 2015
)
. While purely convex hexagonal faces have been used for architectural structures
(
De Leon 2012
;
Romero and Ramos 2013
)
, the resulting facade is not watertight. One approach for hexagonal meshing is using the duality with triangular meshes, either by triangulating the Gauss map
(
Almegaard et al. 2007
)
, or by tangent plane intersections
(
Troche 2008
;
Troche 2009
;
Zimmer
et al. 2012
)
. These approaches often do not lead to regular planar hexagonal shapes.

Better hexagonal shapes can be achieved if the primal triangular mesh is aligned with the principal directions, and sized according to the
Dupin indicatrix

(
Wang
et al. 2008
;
Li et al. 2014
)
. To achieve such an “ideal triangulation” the authors first construct an anisotropic quadrangular mesh, which is then decomposed into a triangular mesh, whose dual is the sought after planar hexagonal mesh. These multiple transitions require special care for singularities regions. Furthermore, to obtain aesthetic hexagon shapes in transition regions between positive and negative Gaussian curvature, these transition regions must be specified manually. A related approach
(
Wang and Liu 2010
)
also starts from a quad mesh obtained from a conjugate curve network, and then shifts the quads to obtain a “brick-wall” pattern which is later optimized for planarization, which will again require special care for singularities. Our method, on the other hand, directly computes a triangulation that is aligned and sized according to the Dupin indicatrix, whose barycetric dual is a hexagonal mesh which is planarizable. Our approach does not require any special treatment of singularities, or manual adjustments in curvature transition regions, and generates planar hexagonal meshes on surfaces more complex than previously possible.

Other approaches aim at generating different variations of hexagonal meshes. For example, Nieser et al.
(
Nieser
et al. 2011
)
generate curvature-aligned triangular meshes (without considering the planarity of the dual meshes), and we follow a similar approach for choosing alignment directions. Other variants include conformal planar hexagonal meshes
(
Müller 2011
)
, which are only applicable to a restricted set of surfaces, honeycomb structures
(
Jiang
et al. 2014
)
and caravel meshes
(
Tellier et al. 2020a
;
Tellier
et al. 2020b
)
, and polyhedral patterns
(
Jiang et al. 2015b
)
. The latter work focuses on the post-processing of a polygonal mesh (PH meshes as a special case), to generate an aesthetic planar polygonal pattern. We note that our approach generates the initial polygonal mesh which this approach receives as input. We use a similar method for post-processing, yet with some modifications as we do not have the regular stripe structure used there.

1.2.
Contributions:

Our main contributions are:

•

Coordinate Power Fields (CPFs) - a novel discrete formulation of integrability for seamless global parameterizations with quantized rotations.

•

A corresponding general optimization framework that is applicable to different meshing applications.

•

A new parameterization-based method for generating planar hexagonal meshes, using CPFs and a Dupin-indicatrix based metric, that exceeds the state of the art in both the quality of the resulting meshes, and the complexity of the surfaces that can be approximated.

2.1.2.
Meshing by grid-pushforward

A common approach for regular meshing

)

2.1.3.
Triangular meshing

Our PH-meshing algorithm is based on tangent duality with a triangular mesh, which we compute using our parameterization framework. Here, the grid
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is replaced by the triangular grid
\mathbb{A}^{2}

\mathcal{U}

There is a linear relationship between the Cartesian and triangular grids, thus we can alternate between the two representations using matrix multiplication. Specifically, we have
\begin{pmatrix}\hat{h}_{1}&\hat{h}_{2}&\hat{h}_{3}\end{pmatrix}_{2\times 3}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{u}&\hat{v}\end{pmatrix}_{2\times 2}\,A

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&-1/2\\

\mathtt{h}_{1},\mathtt{h}_{2},\mathtt{h}_{3}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}

\begin{pmatrix}\mathtt{h}_{1}&\mathtt{h}_{2}&\mathtt{h}_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathtt{u}&\mathtt{v}\end{pmatrix}\,A
. Finally,
H_{i}
are given by the pushforward of

Coordinate Power Fields

3.1.
Rotational Continuity across patches

Consider two parameterizations
r_{i}\!:\!\mathcal{U}_{i}\to\Omega_{i}\!\subset\!\mathcal{M},\,i\!\in\![1,2]
. In general, if
r_{i}
are part of a valid atlas of
\mathcal{M}
, then for any
p\!\in\!\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
and any

r_{1}^{-1}(p)=r_{2}^{-1}(p)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
dr_{1}^{-1}(X)=dr_{2}^{-1}(X)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In the context of meshing, however, we are only interested in the
pushforward of a grid
through these parameterizations. Hence, the pullback maps are part of a valid
grid atlas
, if they agree up to a
grid automorphism
(see e.g.
(
Nieser
et al. 2011
)
). These consist of quantized rotations of integer multiples of
2\pi/N

(5)

(6)

\bigl(dr_{1}^{-1}(X)\bigr)^{N}=\bigl(dr_{2}^{-1}(X)\bigr)^{N}.

Using Equations (

(7)

\bigl((D_{X}u_{1})_{p},(D_{X}v_{1})_{p})\bigr)^{N}=\bigl((D_{X}u_{2})_{p},(D_{X}v_{2})_{p})\bigr)^{N},

u_{i}(p)=\mathtt{u}(r_{i}^{-1}(p)),\hskip 10.00002ptv_{i}(p)=\mathtt{v}(r_{i}^{-1}(p)),\hskip 10.00002pti=[1,2],

3.3.
Discrete Rotational Continuity and CPFs

Given an oriented manifold triangle mesh

Definition 3.3.

Two PC vector fields

Following previous work

3.4.
Relation to other approaches

The existence conditions of an Integer Grid Map, i.e. a parameterization s.t. the pushforward of an integer grid leads to a valid mesh, have been given previously
(
Bommes et al. 2013a
;
Kälberer et al. 2007
)
.
The full problem leads to a difficult mixed integer optimization problem, which, to the best of our knowledge, is not solved directly in any approach.
Methods vary in the
decomposition
of the problem, and in the method that is used to solve each part.

QuadCover, MIQ, Globally Optimal (GO), and IGM

The most common decomposition is to first compute a
cross-field
either by smoothing the principal curvature directions (QuadCover
(
Kälberer et al. 2007
)
), by solving a mixed integer problem (MIQ
(
Bommes
et al. 2009
)
), or by solving a convex complex-valued problem (GO
(
Knöppel et al. 2013
)
). In the second step, a parameterization is computed which aligns with the computed cross field and adheres to the integer transition constraints, either by a mixed integer formulation (MIQ
(
Bommes
et al. 2009
)
), or using a branch-and-cut algorithm (IGM
(
Bommes et al. 2013a
)
).

This decomposition has several disadvantages. First, the sizing of the elements is only addressed in the second step, and thus cannot influence the topology (the singularity locations) of the resulting mesh. Furthermore, integrability is only enforced in the second step (by explicitly computing the parameterization functions), and thus the resulting parameterization can be arbitrarily far from the input cross-field.
Finally, using a cross field implies that the resulting gradient directions are orthogonal, which is not always preferable.

Our approach does a
different decomposition
. The only part which is not addressed in our optimization are the integer transitions between charts. All the other components: integrability, sizing and alignment are all addressed in the vector field optimization stage. This allows, for example, the singularities to move to modify the sizing of the elements and not only the directions.

FrameFields, Metric Customization, Simplex Assembly and PolyVectors

A different first step is to allow for non-orthogonal non-unit length fields, and then proceed with the second parameterization step as described above. This is done by either computing a new 3D geometry that is prescribed by a given metric (FrameFields
(
Panozzo et al. 2014
)
), by enforcing a different intrinsic metric using a different connection on the input mesh (Metric Customization
(
Jiang et al. 2015a
)
), or by generalizing the complex-valued formulation to a complex-polynomial one (PolyVectors
(
Diamanti et al. 2014
)
). None of these approaches guarantees integrability, and thus when computing the parameterization in the second step, the gradients of the parameterization may differ greatly from the computed fields.
The FrameFields and Metric Customization approaches optimize for a
2x2
matrix per face, as do we. However, they consider only the SPD component (without the planar rotation), whereas we compute the full linear map (without the translation). Some notion of integrability of the computed metric exists for both approaches, the first since they construct a new mesh embedding, and the second since the metric of two neighboring triangles should agree on the length of the edge. Note, however, that this constraint is weaker than ours, as we enforce a seamless quantized rotation. The Simplex Assembly
(
Fu and Liu 2016
)
method computes the full affine map per triangle (including the translation part), yet they do not take into account field alignment, or allow for quantized rotational jumps.

Integrable PolyVectors, Commuting PolyVectors and Discrete Integrable Frame Fields

The only three approaches that allow for non-orthogonal fields and optimize integrability are Integrable PolyVectors (IPV)
(
Diamanti et al. 2015
)
, Commuting PolyVectors (CPV)
(
Sageman-Furnas et al. 2019
)
and Discrete Integrable Frame Fields (DIFF)
(
Liu
et al. 2020
)
. Integrability is defined for IPVs through the
PolyCurl
, which is
0
if there exists a matching between neighboring faces which leads to a zero edge-based curl for all the vectors of the PolyVector. However, IPVs describe a larger space than CPFs, and it is not clear how to restrict them to represent only vector fields that are the coordinate vector fields for some parameterization. In CPVs, integrability is defined through the
discretization
of the Lie bracket, which in general does not guarantee discrete integrability. Finally, in DIFFs integrability is defined on the pulled-back edges yet with a known matching, unlike our approach where we allow for rotational jumps in the pulled back edges.

4.
CPFs Optimization Framework

Given an input mesh
M
, we setup an optimization framework for computing CPFs
U,V
. The CPFs are then used to compute a parameterization and a regular mesh, as explained in Section
4.6
.

4.1.
Variables

Our main variables are
U,V\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, the candidate CPFs, represented using a local basis
B
.

4.2.
Hard Constraints

We have two hard constraints. The LICO constraint:

(C1)

\langle U_{i},-JV_{i}\rangle>0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F},

and the CPF constraint:

(C2)

\bigl(dr_{i}^{-1}(e_{ij})\bigr)^{N}=\bigl(dr_{j}^{-1}(e_{ij})\bigr)^{N},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall e_{ij}\!\in\!\mathcal{E}^{I}.

4.3.
Soft Constraints

Conditions on alignment, sizing and orthogonality are required in general for parameterization.

4.3.1.
Alignment

Let
d_{i}\neq 0
be a direction in the face
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
, and
{n_{i}}
be an even divisor of
N
. Our alignment constraint is:

(C3)

\imaginary((dr_{i}^{-1}(d_{i})\big)^{{n_{i}}/2})=0.

Lemma 4.1.

If
U,V
are LICO and the constraint (
C3
) is fulfilled, then one of the pushed forward grid directions in the face
t_{i}
will be parallel to
d_{i}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

4.3.2.
Sizing

The element sizing is determined by the scale of the CPFs
U,V
. We add a constraint on the size of the elements under a user-defined pointwise metric
g
. The metric is the identity for uniform scale, a diagonal matrix for isotropic scale, or any symmetric positive definite matrix for anisotropic scale.
We have:

(C4)

\|U_{i}\|^{2}_{g_{i}}=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\|V_{i}\|^{2}_{g_{i}}=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F},

where
g_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a positive definite matrix defined in the local basis of
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
, and
\|x\|^{2}_{g}=x^{T}gx
.

U,V
s shown. (right) the resulting quad meshes, and the energy graph for the train station model.

4.3.3.
Orthogonality

The elements’ angles are determined by the pointwise inner product between
U,V
. We add an orthogonality constraint under a symmetric inner product
s
chosen by the user. The inner product can be the identity for orthogonality, or the shape operator for conjugacy, for example.
We have:

(C5)

\langle U_{i},V_{i}\rangle_{s_{i}}=0,

where
s_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a symmetric matrix, defined using the local basis of the face
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
, and
\langle x,y\rangle_{g}=x^{T}gy
.

4.4.
Relaxed Problem and Optimization Approach

The constraints we require lead to a non-linear non-convex optimization problem. As a design choice, we opted for posing the problem as an unconstrained non-linear least squares problem, and solving it with the Levenberg-Marquardt
(
Gavin 2020
)
algorithm.

For the LICO constraint (
C1
), we follow previous work on preserving local injectivity
(
Schüller et al. 2013
)
, and formulate it using a spline barrier function.
This approach requires a valid initial guess, and therefore is not applicable to the CPF constraint (
C2
). Hence, we opt for a penalty approach
(
Leyffer and
Mahajan 2010
, Sec 4.2)
.
Specifically, we reformulate the constraint as a quadratic objective, and use an increasing sequence of penalty parameters.
For simplicity, we introduce auxiliary variables
z\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{E}^{I}|}
, which represent the “consensus” pulled back edge through the local transformations at its neighboring triangles.
The soft constraints (
C3
), (
C4
), (
C5
) are reformulated as (potentially weighted) quadratic objectives.

Our relaxed optimization problem is given by:

(11)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle U,V\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}, z\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{E}^{I}|}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle U,V\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m},z\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{E}^{I}|}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\hskip 10.00002ptE^{s}(U,V,z)+\beta E^{\Psi_{c}}(U,V,z)+E^{\Psi}(U,V)\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

where
E^{s}
is a smoothness objective,
E^{\Psi_{c}}
is the penalty objective for the CPF constraint (
C2
), and
\beta
is the penalty parameter. Finally,
E^{\Psi}
is the objective for the rest of the constraints:

(12)

E^{\Psi}(U,V)=E^{\Psi_{lico}}(U,V)+E^{\Psi_{l}}(U,V)+E^{\Psi_{s}}(U)+E^{\Psi_{s}}(V)+E^{\Psi_{o}}(U,V),

where
E^{\Psi_{lico}}
,
E^{\Psi_{l}}
,
E^{\Psi_{s}}
,
E^{\Psi_{o}}
are penalty objectives for the LICO (
C1
), alignment (
C3
), sizing (
C4
) and orthogonality (
C5
) constraints.

The objectives are defined either per interior edge (smoothness and CPF penalty), or per face (all the others). For each such objective we specify the formulation for a single element. The total energy is computed by averaging all the elements’ objectives values.
All the gradients are provided in the supplemental material.

4.4.1.
LICO penalty

We use the barrier function designed for Locally Injective Mappings (LIM)
(
Schüller et al. 2013
)
, where the barrier term is applied to
s_{i}
:

(13)

E^{\Psi_{lico}}_{i}(U,V)=\phi_{i}^{2}\bigl(s_{i}(U,V)-\epsilon^{\Psi_{lico}}\bigr),\hskip 10.00002pts_{i}(U,V)=\langle U_{i},-JV_{i}\rangle,

where
\phi_{i}
is
(
Schüller et al. 2013
, Eq.(5),(6))
:

(14)

\phi_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\ \infty,&\quad x\leq 0\\
\ \frac{1}{g_{i}(x)}-1,&\quad 0<x<\bar{s}_{i}\\
\ 0,&\quad x\geq\bar{s}_{i}\\
\end{cases},\hskip 10.00002ptg_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\bar{s}_{i}^{3}}x^{3}-\frac{3}{\bar{s}_{i}^{2}}x^{2}+\frac{3}{\bar{s}_{i}}x.

Here
\bar{s}_{i}
is the value of
s_{i}
in the previous iteration times a constant
s=0.1
, and we take
\epsilon^{\Psi_{lico}}=10^{-3}
. We take the barrier with respect to the previous iteration instead of a fixed constant to be more resilient to different scalings.

4.4.2.
CPF penalty

The
z
variables conciliate the pullback of
t_{i}
and the pullback of
t_{j}
:

(15)

E^{\Psi_{c}}_{ij}(U,V,z)=\bigl|\bigl(dr_{i}^{-1}(e_{ij})\bigr)^{N}-z_{ij}\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|\bigl(dr_{j}^{-1}(e_{ij})\bigr)^{N}-z_{ij}\bigr|^{2},

where we measure absolute difference between complex numbers.

4.4.3.
Soft constraints

Let
\Psi(U,V)=0
be one of the constraints (
C3
), (
C4
) or (
C5
). Then the corresponding objective
E^{\Psi}
is given by
|\Psi(U,V)|^{2}
.

4.4.4.
Smoothness

The regularizer that is often used in
N
-RoSy field generation is the vector Laplacian, modified to accommodate the rotational symmetry
(
Knöppel et al. 2013
)
.
This formulation favors vector fields which are similar in neighboring faces when raising them to the
N
-th power in the appropriate basis.
The integrability constraint requires that the
N
-th power of the pulled back edge agrees across neighboring triangles, yet the orthogonal direction is not constrained.
Hence, for the regularizer we use:

(16)

E^{S}_{ij}(U,V,z)=\bigl|\bigl(dr_{i}^{-1}(Je_{ij})\bigr)^{N}-z_{ij}\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|\bigl(dr_{j}^{-1}(Je_{ij})\bigr)^{N}-z_{ij}\bigr|^{2}.

Note that, similarly to the CPF constraint, for
N=1
Equation (
16
) is equivalent to requiring that the discrete
edge-based
divergence
(
Polthier and
Preuß 2003
, Def. 4)
is
0
for the vector fields given by the rows of
dr_{i}^{-1}
.

4.5.
Implementation Details

Initialization

The only constraint on our initialization is that the initial
U,V
are LICO. The initial
z
is always computed as the average of the
N
-th power of the pulled-back edges, namely:
z_{ij}=\big(dr_{i}^{-1}(e_{ij}))^{N}+dr_{j}^{-1}(e_{ij}))^{N}\big)/2
.

The best initialization is highly dependent on the application. For simple surfaces,
we can obtain a good solution from random (valid) initialization. For example, taking
U
to be a random unit length vector field,
V_{i}=JU_{i}
, and rescaling both according to
g_{i}
if a sizing constraint given. Figure
4
(left) shows examples of optimized CPFs
U,V
(bottom) computed from a random initialization (top), with curvature based sizing (Section
5.6
) and the conjugacy and orthogonality constraints. Figure
4
(right) shows the resulting quad meshes and the convergence graph of the energy for the train station model (the other graphs are similar). Note that for quad meshes, conjugacy and orthogonality combined imply alignment to curvature directions, although we did not enforce this explicitly.

Optimization

We use the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm
(
Gavin 2020
)
with Jacobian scaling for optimization, as implemented in Matlab, with
\beta_{0}=1
. If required, we repeat the optimization, using the optimized variables as the initial solution, and increasing
\beta
by a multiplicative factor of
2
, until mesh integration with integer seams succeeds, or a maximum of
10
iterations is reached. In most of the results we show, the first iteration sufficed.

Balancing the energy terms

Different energy terms in Equations (
11
), (
12
) have different units, which are mesh-dependent. Without balancing, different weighting parameters are required for different meshes. Specifically,
E^{s}
,
E^{\Psi_{c}},E^{\Psi_{l}}
are defined on the
N
-th power of a pulled back vector, whereas the rest of the objectives are defined on the input mesh. Hence, we rescale these energies, taking
1/\lambda^{2N}E
, where
\lambda
is the average expected
2D
edge length, given by
\lambda=\frac{1}{3m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{e_{ij}\in t_{i}}\|e_{ij}^{T}\,g_{i}\,e_{ij}\|
.

4.6.
Meshing with CPFs

Given the computed
U,V
vector fields, the seamless parameterization can be theoretically constructed using the definitions of the parameterization functions
u,v
that are given in the proof. However, even if the CPF constraint is accurate to machine precision, such an implementation would require computing integer translations per edge, which is unnecessarily expensive.
Thus, we follow the standard procedure for extracting a mesh
(
Vaxman
et al. 2019
)
. Specifically, integrating the parameterization with the prescribed quantized rotations on the edges using a Poisson solve, computing a full integer grid map by quantizing the translations
(
Vaxman 2021
)
, and extracting the mesh by overlaying a grid on the integer grid map. We provide the details in Section
B
.

5.
Planar Hex Meshing

Our main goal is generating planar hexagonal meshes which approximate a given surface, and we use the CPF framework for the optimization. In the following we describe the constraints that we apply, which are unique for hexagonal meshes.

5.1.
Background: Curvature and the Dupin Indicatrix

Notations and polar curvature representation.

The maximal and minimal curvatures at
p\!\in\!\mathcal{M}
are denoted by
\kappa_{1}(p)
,
\kappa_{2}(p)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}
, respectively, with corresponding maximal and minimal curvature directions denoted by
d_{1}(p),d_{2}(p)\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
. The Gaussian and mean curvatures are denoted by
K(p),H(p)
, respectively, and the shape operator by
S_{p}
. The normal curvature in the direction
v\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\kappa_{n}(v)
. Two directions
x,y\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
are conjugate if they are orthogonal with respect to the shape operator, namely
\langle x,y\rangle_{S_{p}}=x^{T}S_{p}y=0
. We drop
p
when the meaning is clear.

Curvature on triangle meshes is notoriously difficult to compute robustly, requiring special treatment in the context of curvature aligned meshing
(
Campen et al. 2016
)
.
Thus, similarly to Niesser et al.
(
Nieser
et al. 2011
)
, we use a polar representation of the curvatures:

\varphi=\left|\operatorname{atan}\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)\right|,\hskip 10.00002pt\rho=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}},

where
\varphi
is a smooth representation of the curvature type of the point (elliptic
K>0
, parabolic
K=0
or hyperbolic
K<0
), and
\rho
represents distance from planarity (we normalize it between
0
and
1
). Thus,
\varphi
is well defined only for
\rho>0
(see Figure
6
).

The Dupin Indicatrix

An important notion for computing PH meshes, as has been shown by Wang et al.
(
[
)
Thm. 1]wang2009note, is the concept of the
Dupin Indicatrix

(
Eisenhart 1960
, Chap. IV)
.

The Dupin indicatrix at an elliptic point
p\!\in\!\mathcal{M}
is a curve on the tangent plane
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
, given by the points
p+|\kappa_{n}(v)|^{-1/2}\hat{v}
for all unit length vectors
\hat{v}\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
. In local curvature coordinates, i.e. taking
p
as the origin and
d_{1},d_{2}
as the local
x,y
axes, the Dupin indicatrix is the ellipse
|\kappa_{1}|x^{2}+|\kappa_{2}|y^{2}=1
. At a hyperbolic point it is given by the conjugate hyperbolas
\kappa_{1}x^{2}+\kappa_{2}y^{2}=\pm 1
, and at parabolic points by two parallel lines, either
|\kappa_{1}|x^{2}=1
or
|\kappa_{2}|y^{2}=1
(see Fig.
7
(top)).

The relation between the Dupin indicatrix and planar meshing is clearly visualized when considering the local geometry at
p
(see Figure
8
, inspired by
(
Lisle 2003
, Fig.2)
). Consider a normal plane in the direction
\hat{v}\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
at
p
, and its intersection curve with the surface. Further, take a plane parallel to
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
and at distance
t
from it, such that it intersects the surface, and let
2d
be the distance between the two intersection points of the surface and these two planes.
If
r
is the radius of curvature at
p
, then for small enough
t
we have
d^{2}=r^{2}-(r-t)^{2}=2t|\kappa_{n}(\hat{v})|^{-1}+O(t^{2})
.
Hence, up to first order in
t
, the intersection curve between the cutting plane and the surface is a uniform scale of the dupin indicatrix (see Figure

5.2.
Dual Meshing

Givan a parameterization
r:\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\Omega\subset\mathcal{M}
, and a point on the triangular grid

, let
\mathtt{p}_{i},i\in[1,..,6]
be its one-ring grid neighbors, and
\bar{\mathtt{p}}_{i}=(\mathtt{p}+\mathtt{p}_{i}+\mathtt{p}_{i+1})/3
be the barycentric dual vertices (see Figure

and the corresponding hexagon
{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}
generated by linearly connecting the pushed forward points to yield a closed polyline. This hexagon represents one element in the hexagonal remeshing of the input surface.
Our general goals are:

•

Planarity.
The vertices of
{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}
are co-planar, and
{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}
is a simple polygon.

•

Approximation.
The Hausdorff distance between the surface and
{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}
is minimal among all hexagons with the same area approximating the surface at
p=r(\mathtt{p})
.

•

Fairness.

{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}
has horizontal and vertical reflection symmetry.

5.3.
Obstruction to convexity.

Parameterization-based dual meshing generates
convex
hexagonal faces. However, the faces of a PH mesh approximating a given surface are inscribed in a quadric which is a uniform scale of the Dupin indicatrix
(
Wang and Liu 2009
, Thm. 1)
(see also Fig.
8
(left)). Hence, for elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic regions the expected face shapes are
convex
hex,
brick
hex, and
bow-tie
hex, (see Fig
9
).

Thus, regardless of the parameterization chosen, we
cannot

There exist methods for

(
Tang

)
. These approaches generate a valid PH mesh, which fulfills our stated goals,
given a good initialization
. We denote a mesh that can serve as the initial solution for a planarization approach as
planarizable
. We compute planarizable meshes using the CPF framework, by designing constraints on the CPFs
U,V
. In the following we describe our pipeline, and the required CPF constraints.

5.5.
Planarity by Conjugacy and Alignment

5.5.1.
Conjugacy and developable strips

Following Wang et al.
(
Wang and Liu 2009
)
we consider strips of hexagons which are developable (see Figure
11
(left)).

The relation between conjugate direction fields and planar meshing is well established
(
Liu
et al. 2006
;
Liu
et al. 2011
;
Wang and Liu 2009
)
. Specifically, consider a hexagon
{\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{p}}}=\{\bar{p}_{i}\,|\,i\!\in\![1,..,6]\}
at the tangent plane at
p\!\in\!\mathcal{M}
. Using the argument that the discrete ruling directions are conjugate to the central curve of a developable strip, Wang et al.
(
Wang and Liu 2009
)
have shown that for a convex hexagon, every pair of consecutive edge vectors
e_{i}=\bar{p}_{i}-p
fulfills
\langle e_{i}+e_{i+1},e_{i}-e_{i+1}\rangle_{S_{p}}=0
.

Plugging in the expressions for the edge vectors as a function of
U_{p},V_{p}
it is straightforward to show (see the supplemental material) that in elliptic regions, these conditions are equivalent to:

(17)

\displaystyle\langle U_{p},V_{p}\rangle_{S_{p}}

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle\langle U_{p},U_{p}\rangle_{S_{p}}

\displaystyle=\langle V_{p},V_{p}\rangle_{S_{p}}.

Therefore, in the CPF framework, we use the orthogonality constraint
\langle U_{i},V_{i}\rangle_{s_{i}}=0
, taking
s_{i}
to be the shape operator at face
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
. Note that Equation (
18
) implies
\langle U_{p},U_{p}\rangle_{S_{p}}=\kappa_{n}(U_{p})\|U_{p}\|^{2}=\kappa_{n}(V_{p})\|V_{p}\|^{2}=\langle V_{p},V_{p}\rangle_{S_{p}}
. In elliptic regions, where
S_{p}
is positive definite and the normal curvatures have the same sign, this is equivalent to
|\kappa_{n}(U_{p})|\|U_{p}\|^{2}=|\kappa_{n}(V_{p})|\|V_{p}\|^{2}
, and hence
U_{p},V_{p}
trace a uniform scale of the Dupin indicatrix. Thus, this constraint will be covered by the scaling constraint as we explain in Section
5.6
.

5.5.2.
Alignment

A strip of convex hexes is planarizable if there are degrees of freedom allowing the hex vertices to move to the required hex shapes (see Figure
11
(right)).
Hence, in parabolic regions the
U
direction
must
be aligned with the ruling direction, i.e. the direction of
minimal absolute curvature
, which we denote by
\bar{d}
.
In hyperbolic regions, the
U
direction should be aligned with either
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
, and in elliptic regions alignment is flexible, yet hexes would be easier to planarize and better shaped if
U
is aligned either with
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
.

The minimal absolute curvature direction
d
is defined by:

(19)

\bar{d}=\begin{cases}d_{1}&|\kappa_{1}|<|\kappa_{2}|\\
d_{2}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

and it is not necessarily smooth on the surface. For example, in parabolic regions of positive mean curvature, we have
\kappa_{1}=0,\kappa_{2}>0
, and thus
\bar{d}=d_{1}
. On the other hand, in parabolic regions of negative mean curvature, we have
\kappa_{1}<0,\kappa_{2}=0
and thus
\bar{d}=d_{2}
. Furthermore, when the discrete curvature is noisy
\bar{d}
is not stable in umbilical regions where
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}
, nor in regions where
\kappa_{1}=-\kappa_{2}
.

To consolidate the different alignment constraints in different regions, we define
curvature regions
in terms of
\rho,\varphi
and then compute a smooth
2
-RoSy
guiding field
which will provide alignment constraints for the CPFs computation.

5.5.3.
Curvature regions.

Following Nieser et al.
(
Nieser
et al. 2011
)
, we use
\rho,\varphi
to robustly define curvature regions that contribute to the alignment constraints as previously discussed. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{e},{\mathcal{F}}_{p},{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\subset\mathcal{F}
denote the faces sets of elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic curvature regions, respectively. They are defined as follows:

(20)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{e}

\displaystyle=\{\,t_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{npl}\setminus{\mathcal{F}}_{u}\hskip 10.00002pt|\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi-\pi/4\geq\varphi_{p}\,\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{p}

\displaystyle=\{\,t_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{npl}\hskip 10.00002pt|\hskip 10.00002pt|\varphi-\pi/4|<\varphi_{p}\,\}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\,t_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{npl}\hskip 10.00002pt|\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi-\pi/4\leq-\varphi_{p}\,\},

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{npl}=\{t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}\,|\,\rho>\rho_{m}\}
are the non planar regions,
{\mathcal{F}}_{u}=\{t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}\,|\,\varphi\geq\pi/2-\varphi_{e}\}
are the umbilical regions, and we take
\rho_{m}=0.01,\varphi_{p}=0.05,\varphi_{e}=0.2
. Figure
12
shows a few examples of color coded curvature regions.

5.5.4.
Guiding field.

We define an optimization problem for a
2
-RoSy
guiding field

y
, that is aligned with either
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
according to the curvature regions. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}={\mathcal{F}}_{p},{\mathcal{F}}_{4}={\mathcal{F}}_{e}\cup{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Then, for all faces in
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
, the guiding field should be aligned with
\pm\bar{d}
, where as for faces in
{\mathcal{F}}_{4}
it should be aligned either with
\pm\bar{d}
or
\pm\mathtt{i}\bar{d}
. Hence, we obtain the following optimization problem.

(21)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\hskip 10.00002pty^{T}\Delta_{2}y\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle y_{i}-\bar{d}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle t_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{2},

\displaystyle y_{i}^{2}-\bar{d}_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle t_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{4},

where
\Delta_{2}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
denotes the
2
-RoSy Laplacian
(
Knöppel et al. 2013
)
, expressed in the local face basis.

This problem has a convex quadratic objective and linear and quadratic constraints. We reformulate it as an unconstrained non-linear least squares problem, as follows.

(22)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2m}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{\lambda}y^{T}\Delta_{2}y+\beta(E^{\Psi_{2}}+E^{\Psi_{4}}),\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

where
E^{\Psi_{2}},E^{\Psi_{4}}
are the average
L2
errors of the first and second constraints, respectively,
\lambda
is the first non-zero eigenvalue of
\Delta_{2}
, and we take the penalty weight
\beta=10^{5}
.

The problem (
21
) without the second constraint is convex, and we take its solution as the initial solution for the problem (
22
), for which we use the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm. Figure
13
(left) shows an example of
2
- and
4
-RoSy constraints and the resulting guiding field

(middle).

5.5.5.
CPF Alignment constraints.

Finally, we use the guiding field to setup the alignment constraints for the CPF framework. As discussed, in elliptic regions there are three developable strips, thus the guiding field can be aligned with any of the pushed forward grid directions. On the other hand, in parabolic and hyperbolic regions there is a single strip, which should be aligned with the pushed forward
\pm\hat{\mathtt{u}}

(23)

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{y_{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt{n_{i}}=6,\hskip 10.00002ptt_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{e},

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{y_{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt{n_{i}}=2,\hskip 10.00002ptt_{i}\!\in\!{\mathcal{F}}_{p}\cup{\mathcal{F}}_{h}.

5.6.
Approximation by Scaling

5.6.1.
Regularized Dupin Indicatrix.

Our conjugacy and alignment constraints set the
orientation
of the pushed forward hexagon. For the resulting hexagons to be circumscribed in a uniform scale of the Dupin indicatrix, as required, we additionally constrain the
scaling
of a pushed forward vector
w\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
. In general, in an elliptic region,
w
should fulfill
\|w\|^{2}|\kappa_{n}(w)|=\eta,\forall w\,\text{ s.t.}\,\kappa_{n}(w)\neq 0
, where
\eta>0
controls the area of the element.

There are two issues to note. First, the vector size
\|w\|
becomes unbounded as
\kappa_{n}\to 0
. Second, our hexagons are regular hexagons which are pushed forward through a parameterization, and thus are
always
convex. Thus,
w
should always trace an ellipse, even in hyperbolic regions, and it should have the aspect ratio of the Dupin indicatrix. We address both issues with the following definition.

Definition 5.1.

Let
p\!\in\!\mathcal{M}
, with curvature directions
d_{1},d_{2}
, and consider the local curvature coordinates in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
where
p
is the origin, and
d_{1},d_{2}
are the
x,y
axes, respectively. Given
\epsilon>0
, the
\epsilon
-regularized Dupin indicatrix

D_{\epsilon}(p)
is the ellipse
x^{2}\tilde{\kappa}_{1,\epsilon}+y^{2}\tilde{\kappa}_{2,\epsilon}=1
, where
\tilde{\kappa}_{1,\epsilon}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\kappa}_{2,\epsilon}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
. The
\epsilon
-regularized normal curvature
in direction
w\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
is given by
\tilde{\kappa}_{n,\epsilon}(w)=\tilde{\kappa}_{1,\epsilon}\cos^{2}\theta+\tilde{\kappa}_{2,\epsilon}\sin^{2}\theta
, where
\theta
is the angle of
w
w.r.t
d_{1}
.

The function
x\to\sqrt{x^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}
is a well known smoothing of the function
|x|
that is known to be convex, differentiable, positive, and within an
\epsilon
-dependent bound from
|x|

(
Argaez
et al. 2011
)
. Geometrically, we replace
|x|
with the hypotenuse of a right angle triangle with leg lengths
|x|
and
\epsilon\!>\!0
, and thus we are guaranteed that the result is positive, and has the desired approximation effect. Furthermore, the regularized Dupin indicatrix is always well defined, and provides a smooth, non-degenerate transition between elliptic and hyperbolic regions. We further have the following.

Lemma 5.2.

Let
p\!\in\!\mathcal{M}
, with curvatures
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
, and let
D_{\epsilon}(p)
be its regularized Dupin indicatrix. Then, the area
\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)
of
D_{\epsilon}(p)
fulfills:

(24)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\kappa_{1}|\gg 0\\
|\kappa_{2}|\gg 0\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{|\kappa_{1}|}\sqrt{|\kappa_{2}|}},\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa_{1}\to 0\\
\kappa_{2}\to 0\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa_{1}\to 0\\
|\kappa_{2}|\gg 0\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\sqrt{|\kappa_{2}|}},\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa_{2}\to 0\\
|\kappa_{1}|\gg 0\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\sqrt{|\kappa_{1}|}}.

The proof follows directly from the fact
\lim_{x\to 0}\sqrt{x^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}=\epsilon
.

Hence, by choosing
\epsilon,\gamma
, and requiring
\|w\|^{2}|\tilde{\kappa}_{n,\epsilon}(w)|=\gamma^{2},\forall w=dr(\mathtt{w})\!\in\!T_{p}\mathcal{M}
, we constrain the pushforward unit circle to be a
uniform
\gamma
-scale of the
\epsilon
-regularized Dupin indicatrix
. This allows us to control the areas of the pushed forward hexagons, and provide a smooth transition between elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic regions.

5.6.2.
CPF scaling constraints.

We set up the scaling constraints:

(25)

\displaystyle\|U_{i}\|_{g_{i}(\epsilon,\gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\|V_{i}\|_{g_{i}(\epsilon,\gamma)}^{2}=1,

\displaystyle g_{i}(\epsilon,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&d_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\kappa}_{1,\epsilon}&0\\
0&\tilde{\kappa}_{2,\epsilon}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&d_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{T}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{S}_{\epsilon}.

We discuss the effect of the combined constraints in different regions in Section
5.8
.

5.6.3.
Choosing
\epsilon
and
\gamma
.

The choice of the scaling parameters
\epsilon,\gamma
affects the aspect ratio of the hexagonal elements in parabolic regions, as well as the maximal element size in planar regions.
The area of the
\gamma
-scaled
\epsilon
-regularized Dupin indicatrix is
\gamma^{2}\alpha_{\epsilon}(p)
, which is equal to
\gamma^{2}\pi/\epsilon
in planar regions (Equation (
24
)).

Let
\delta
be the desired elements’ distance to the surface, and
\eta
the maximal element size.
Thus, we have
\pi\gamma^{2}/\epsilon=\eta
, where
\gamma^{2}=2\delta+O(\delta^{2})
(see Figure

(left)). Hence, we take
\epsilon_{\delta,\eta}=\frac{2\pi\delta}{\eta},\gamma_{\delta,\eta}=\sqrt{2\delta}
.

5.7.
Fairness by Orthogonality

Our last objective is
fairness
, formalized as horizontal and vertical symmetry of the hexagonal elements. We achieve this by requiring the orthogonality of
U_{i},V_{i}
, namely by adding the CPF constraints:

(26)

\langle U_{i},V_{i}\rangle=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}.

In parabolic and hyperbolic regions the planarity optimization modifies the shapes of the pushed-forward hexes away from convexity. If the symmetry axes of the pushed forward hexagon are not aligned with the curvature directions, this modification can lead to skewed hexes, for example parallelograms instead of rectangular bricks in parabolic regions.
By enforcing orthogonality as well as conjugacy, we effectively require that
U_{i},V_{i}
are aligned with the curvature directions, and thus that the planarization preserves the horizontal and vertical reflectional symmetry of the hexes. Note that, unlike for PQ meshes, these combined constraints do not replace alignment, since for PH meshing the
U
direction must be aligned with the minimal absolute curvature direction, whereas for PQ meshes
U,V
can be aligned with either minimal or maximal directions.

Figure

demonstrates the effect of adding orthogonality constraints. On the left we show the result with orthogonality, and on the right without it. We show the dual mesh before planarization
M_{D}
(top) and the final PH mesh
M_{H}
(bottom). Note that without orthogonality the hexes of
M_{H}
are still planar, but lose horizontal and vertical symmetry with respect to the ruling direction.

5.8.
Constraint analysis

5.8.1.
Elliptic regions

When
\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\!\gg\!0
we have
\tilde{S}_{\epsilon}\!\approx\!S
, and thus the Dupin Equation (
18
) holds approximately.
Furthermore, in these regions, for any
\mathtt{w}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2},\,|\mathtt{w}|=1
we have that
\|dr(\mathtt{w})\|^{2}_{g_{i}(\epsilon,\gamma)}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\mathtt{w}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}U_{i}&V_{i}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\tilde{S}_{\epsilon}\begin{pmatrix}U_{i}&V_{i}\end{pmatrix}\mathtt{w}\approx\mathtt{w}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathtt{w}=1
. Hence, in elliptic regions the scaling and conjugacy constraints imply that pushing forward the unit circle yields (approximately) a uniform
\gamma
-scale of the
\epsilon
-regularized Dupin indicatrix. Indeed, in elliptic regions (when
\gamma
is small enough) the initial planarity error is considerably lower compared to the rest of the surface (see Figure
18
).

6.
Implementation Details

We provide the details on mesh generation and planarization in the Appendices.

6.1.
Constraints computation

6.1.1.
Shape operator

For the shape operator we take the tangential gradient of the normals at the vertices, symmetrized and projected to the tangent plane
(
Azencot et al. 2015
)
. If normals at the vertices are available (e.g. if the surface was sampled from a parametric surface) we use those, otherwise we take the area-weighted average of the normals of the neighboring faces.
For the large majority of meshes, the normals are computed.

6.1.2.
Boundary faces

Boundary faces are defined as faces with at least one vertex on the boundary. On these faces, the computation of vertex normals (and the shape operator) is not reliable, and therefore we do not constrain these faces for alignment, nor conjugacy.

6.1.3.
Guiding field, special cases

If there are no constraints, the guiding field is given by the lowest eigenvector of the
2
-RoSy Laplacian. If there are only
2
-RoSy constraints, the optimization problem (
21
) is convex quadratic and is solved directly using constrained linear least squares. If there are only
4
-RoSy constraints, then the curvature is uniform, either hyperbolic or elliptic, and we take the minimal curvature direction
d_{1}
in constrained regions as the
2
-RoSy constraints for the guiding field optimization.

6.1.4.
Alignment weighting

As is common in meshing, we add diagonal weights to the alignment constraint, to reduce the weight in nearly planar regions. Specifically, for
N=4
we weigh the alignment constraint on the face
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
with
\rho_{i}^{2}
, and for
N=6
with
\big(\rho_{i}^{3}-3\rho_{i}^{2}+3\rho_{i}\big)^{2}
, a polynomial which resembles a Sigmoid function and has vanishing first and second derivatives at
1
.

6.2.
Boundaries

Unlike quadrangular meshes, hexagonal elements cannot naturally conform to the surface boundaries, as in convex regions the hexagons will form “jagged” boundaries.
Thus, a boundary-conforming mesh will have many non-hexagonal faces. Furthermore, if the boundaries are parallel to the maximal absolute curvature direction, arbitrarily shaped low quality faces may be obtained when the curvature-aligned hexagons intersect the non-aligned boundary. Thus, many recent works on hexagonal remeshing
(
Jiang et al. 2015b
;
Wang and Liu 2009
;
Müller 2011
;
Wang
et al. 2008
)
have chosen to generate meshes whose boundaries do not conform to the input shape. Following the large body of existing work, we have chosen a similar path.
Note, however, that methods that preserve boundaries, such as
Li et al. 2014
, are advantageous in many scenarios, especially in the architectural context.

When there is a single planar boundary curve, one possible solution is
doubling
the input surface by reflecting it with respect to the boundary plane.
This leads to a closed surface, which is then be intersected with the boundary plane after hex-meshing. See Figure
19
for such an example.
Finding a robust, general solution for handling the boundary is an interesting avenue for future work.

6.3.
Limitations

Our method relies on the existence of a smooth
2
-RoSy guiding field, which is aligned to the maximal absolute curvature direction in parabolic regions.
In some cases, when there are adjacent parabolic regions with a maximal absolute curvature direction which changes abruptly (see Fig.
20
(a)), there exists no such smooth field. In these cases, multiple singularities are introduced along the discontinuity line, leading to badly shaped hexagons (Fig.
20
(b)).
This is a limitation that can potentially be addressed by allowing both quads and hexagons, a direction we leave for future work.

We solve a non-linear non-convex optimization problem, and the solution that we obtain depends on the initialization. While our algorithm is quite stable, as we have demonstrated using convergence from a random initialization, we cannot guarantee that the global optimum would be reached.
Furthermore, we rely heavily on curvature information, for all our constraints except orthogonality. While we have used a standard off-the-shelf algorithm for computing curvatures, using more elaborate curvature computation schemes can potentially improve the accuracy of our results.

8.
Conclusions

We proposed a novel framework for parameterization-driven meshing, at the heart of which lies a novel integrability constraint, which is provably zero when a quantized rotationally-seamless parameterization exists. We have leveraged this framework for designing planar hexagonal meshes, succeeding to mesh a variety of complex models, which have not been demonstrated to be PH-meshable previously. We have additionally showed that our method is general, and is easily applicable to planar quad meshing as well.

We believe that both our general framework, as well as the PH meshing application would be highly useful to upstream geometry processing applications and to practitioners.
Due to the flexibility of our framework, it is interesting to investigate how additional constraints, e.g. physical constraints, can be added. Furthermore, using our approach for quick validation during form-finding is a fruitful direction for future work. Finally, generalizing the approach to work directly on a free-form parameterized surface instead of a surface mesh is both theoretically interesting and practically useful.

Appendix B
Integration and mesh generation

For integration and meshing we use standard practice and existing software that we do not claim as a contribution; however we discuss here specific consequences to our configuration choices.

B.1.
Integration with seams in
\mathbb{Z}

From the CPFs
U_{i},V_{i}
we first compute the gradients
(\nabla u)_{i},(\nabla v)_{i}
for every face
t_{i}\!\in\!\mathcal{F}
using Equation (
2
). Let
h_{1,2,3}
denote the pullback of the triangular grid coordinates to
M
. The linear relation between the Cartesian and triangular grid carries over to the gradients, and we use it to compute
F_{1,2,3}
, the candidate gradient fields of the triangular grid functions.

We generate the mesh by integrating the field
F_{1,2,3}
into the seamless multivariate function
h_{1,2,3}
defined on the vertices on the mesh
M
. We use the new seamless integration package in Directional
(
Vaxman
et al. 2019
;
Vaxman 2021
)
. This guarantees that the solution is order-preserving and faithful to our original field as much as possible, as our field is near-integrable (with quantized rotational jumps). The algorithm first “combs”
F_{1,2,3}
according to our prescribed matching (the integer jumps on interior edges), cutting the mesh into a topological disc with the singularities on the boundaries, and creating an order-preserving function on the vertices of the cut mesh. The function is seamless across these cuts, which means there exist permutations and integer translational jumps, where the permutations correspond to the matching of the field and the singularities indices. We further prescribe that
h_{1}-h_{2}+h_{3}=0
to get a consistent triangle mesh, which matches the symmetry of the field
F
.

We choose a configuration where we round the seam translational jumps to integers, rather than the singularity coordinates, to relax the constraints on our otherwise very-constrained field design; that means that a singularity coordinate can fall on any point in
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}^{2}

(
Nieser
et al. 2011
)
. The practical implication is that the resulting primal mesh
M_{R}
can be only triangle-dominant rather than fully triangular. To mitigate that we triangulate any resulting quadrilateral faces by adding the Delaunay diagonal.

B.2.
Mesh extraction

To get
M_{R}
from the parameterization functions
h_{1,2,3}
, we trace their isolines on the original mesh and enumerate their intersections and faces; this is effectively and exactly done using the CGAL arrangement package
(
Wein et al. 2020
)
also as a part of the seamless integration package.

Hex-dominant dualization

We dualize the mesh
M_{R}
into the barycentric hex-dominant mesh
M_{D}
by inserting the barycenteric dual vertex in each interior face. Special measures are taken for the dualization of boundary faces, to ensure that the output mesh is consistent. We first remove one strip of boundary faces from the primal mesh and then another strip of faces from the dual mesh. In doing so, we avoid boundary faces with very small area, which may lead to instabilities during planarization.

Appendix C
Planarization

The dual meshes we obtain are only approximately planar, with the planarity error concentrated in non-elliptic regions.
However, the meshes are
planarizable
using a standard approach inspired by
Jiang et al. 2015b
, and planarization
is necessary to generate brick and bowtie concave hexagonal shapes.
We optimize for face planarity (
29
) under regularization with several objectives.

Surface distance.
To prevent the vertices from drifting from the original
surface
M
, we minimize the distance
\|\hat{v}_{i}-v_{i}\|
between the vertices
v_{i}
and their closest
projections
\hat{v}_{i}
on
M
.

Symmetry.
To preserve the symmetry of the even-degree faces, we consider
the vectors connecting the barycenter
c_{f}
with every
v_{i}\in f
. We then require each pair of
opposite vectors to be as equal as possible. In detail, we consider the vectors
p_{i}=c_{f}-v_{i}
and

q_{i}=c_{f}-v_{(i+d/2)},i\in[1,..,d/2]
, where
d
is the face
degree. Finally,
f
is symmetric when
p_{i}=-q_{i},\forall i\in[1,..,d/2]
.

Tangential drift.
To prevent normal drift, we favor vertex
movement in the tangent plane, computed at vertex locations in the previous iteration. We minimize

\langle n_{\tilde{v}_{i}},(v_{i}-\tilde{v}_{i})\rangle^{2}
, where
\tilde{v}_{i}
is the previous value of
v_{i}
, and

n_{\tilde{v}_{i}}
is the average face normal around
\tilde{v}_{i}
.

Edge and diagonal length.
We prevent the degenration of edges and diagonals using the geometric barrier in Equation (
14
) applied to the edges’ and diagonals’ length.

Quad fairness.
For quad meshes we add an extra fairness term, the squared-norm of the difference between a vertex and its averaged neighborhood
\|v_{i}-(w_{1}+\dots+w_{n})/n\|^{2}
, where
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
are the
n
nearest neighbors of
v_{i}
. For more details see
Tang
et al. 2014
.

We present a new approach for computing planar hexagonal meshes that approximate a given surface, represented as a triangle mesh. Our method is based on two novel technical contributions. First, we introduce
