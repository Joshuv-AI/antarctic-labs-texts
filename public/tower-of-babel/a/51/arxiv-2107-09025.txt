The spum and sum-diameter of graphs: labelings of sum graphs
Rupert Li

Abstract
. A sum graph is a finite simple graph whose vertex set is labeled with distinct positive integers such that two vertices are adjacent if and only if the sum of their labels is itself another label. The spum of a graph G G is the minimum difference between the largest and smallest labels in a sum graph consisting of G G and the minimum number of additional isolated vertices necessary so that a sum graph labeling exists. We investigate the spum of various families of graphs, namely cycles, paths, and matchings. We introduce the sum-diameter , a modification of the definition of spum that omits the requirement that the number of additional isolated vertices in the sum graph is minimal, which we believe is a more natural quantity to study. We then provide asymptotically tight general bounds on both sides for the sum-diameter, and study its behavior under numerous binary graph operations as well as vertex and edge operations. Finally, we generalize the sum-diameter to hypergraphs.

Introduction

In 1990, Harary
[
10
]
defined a
sum graph
to be a graph whose vertices can be labeled with distinct positive integers such that two vertices are adjacent if and only if the sum of their labels is itself another label in the graph.
Not every graph is a sum graph: the vertex with highest label must be isolated, so any graph without isolated vertices cannot be a sum graph.
However, if one adds enough isolated vertices to a graph it will become possible to represent it as a sum graph, and Harary analyzed the minimum number of isolated vertices one must add to an (unlabeled) graph so that it can be represented as a sum graph, which he called the
sum number

\sigma(G)

The sum number of various special families of graphs have been identified, notably including complete graphs
K_{n}
, cycles
C_{n}
, and trees.
See Gallian’s survey
[
7

for a comprehensive list of known results on the sum number.
Four years later, Harary
[
11

extended his notion of a sum graph to allow distinct integer labels, rather than simply positive integers; the corresponding graph is called an

, and the corresponding

is denoted

For precise definitions of all of these concepts, see
Section

Goodell, Beveridge, Gallagher, Goodwin, Gyori, and Joseph

Let

Finally, let

We start with the definition of a sum graph and an integral sum graph, introduced by
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
, respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

The
induced sum graph
of a set
L\subset\mathbb{Z}
is the simple graph whose vertex set is
L
and
(u,v)\in E
if and only if
u+v\in L
.

A simple graph
G=(V,E)
is called a
sum graph
if it is isomorphic to the induced sum graph of some set
L\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
We call
L
a set of labels for the sum graph
G
.
We will often not distinguish between the vertices and their respective labels.

Similarly, a graph
G
is called an
integral sum graph
if such a bijection exists to a set of integers
L\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

Conversely, any set of positive integers
L
induces a sum graph with vertex set
V=L
, and any set of integers
L
induces an integral sum graph.
Notice that the vertex with maximum label in a sum graph must be isolated, so a graph without isolated vertices cannot be a sum graph.
In particular, a connected graph on at least two vertices cannot be a sum graph.
This leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

The
sum number
of a graph
G
, denoted
\sigma(G)
, is the minimum number of isolated vertices that must be added to
G
in order to yield a sum graph.

Similarly, the
integral sum number
of a graph
G
, denoted
\zeta(G)
, is the minimum number of isolated vertices that must be added to
G
in order to yield an integral sum graph.

It was shown in
[
10
]
that
\sigma(G)\leq|E|
, and as we clearly have
\zeta(G)\leq\sigma(G)
for all
G
, this implies both
\sigma(G)
and
\zeta(G)
are finite.

The sum number and integral sum numbers of various classes of graphs are known; for a collection of such results, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
.

We now define the spum of a graph
G
.

Definition 2.3
.

The
spum
of a graph
G
, denoted
\operatorname{spum}(G)
, is the minimum value of
\operatorname{range}(L)
among all sets of positive integers that induce the sum graph consisting of
G
with
\sigma(G)
isolated vertices.

Similarly, the
integral spum
of
G
, denoted
\operatorname{ispum}(G)
, is the minimum value of
\operatorname{range}(L)
among all sets of integers
L
that induce the integral sum graph consisting of
G
with
\zeta(G)
isolated vertices.

According to
[
7
]
, the notion of spum was first introduced in an unpublished paper
[
9
]
, which supposedly also proved that
\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
.
This result has been confirmed in
[
16
]
, which also calculated the spum and integral spum for several other classes of graphs, namely including
K_{1,n}
and
K_{n,n}
, along with bounds for path graphs
P_{n}
and cycles
C_{n}
.

Remark 2.4
.

For any graph
G
without isolated vertices and a corresponding sum graph labeling
L
that achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{spum}(G)
, let
S\subset L
be the set of labels assigned to the vertices of
G
.
Notice that
\min S=\min L
, as otherwise removing the label
\min L
does not change the fact that this labeling induces
G

\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{spum}(G)
.

In
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
, it was shown that for all connected graphs
G
with
n
vertices and maximum and minimum vertex degrees
\Delta
and
\delta
, respectively,
\operatorname{spum}(G)\geq 2n-(\Delta-\delta)-2
.
The result can be extended to all graphs
G
without isolated vertices without changing the proof.
We provide the following refinement for the equality case.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
be a graph without isolated vertices, whose order is
n
and whose maximum and minimum vertex degrees are
\Delta
and
\delta
, respectively.
If
\operatorname{spum}(G)=2n-(\Delta-\delta)-2
, then let
L
be any labeling that achieves this spum value.
Let
S\subset L
be the set of labels assigned to the vertices of
G
and let
a_{1}=\min S
.
Then
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\subseteq S
.

Proof.

The proof uses the same structure as the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
, with some additional arguments.

We use the same notation as in the statement of the lemma.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\not\subseteq S
.
Sort
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
in increasing order
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
.
Define
S_{1}=S\cap[a_{1},2a_{1}]
,
S_{2}=S\setminus S_{1}
,
S_{3}=S_{2}-a_{1}
, and
T=[a_{1},a_{n}]\setminus S
.
As
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\not\subseteq S
, we have
|S_{1}|\leq a_{1}
.
Notice that every element of
S_{3}\cap S
is adjacent to
a_{1}
: for each
x\in S_{3}
, we have
a_{1}+x\in S_{2}\subseteq S
and thus if
x\in S
then
x
is adjacent to
a_{1}
.
Hence,
|S\cap S_{3}|\leq\Delta
.
As
S_{3}\subseteq[a_{1}+1,a_{n}-a_{1}]
, we find

|S_{3}\cap S|+|S_{3}\cap T|=|S_{3}\cap[a_{1},a_{n}]|=|S_{3}|=|S_{2}|=|S|-|S_{1}|\geq n-a_{1}.

If
|S\cap S_{3}|=\Delta
, then
|S_{3}\cap T|\geq n-\Delta-a_{1}
, and
a_{1}
is already adjacent to
\Delta
labels in
[a_{1}+1,a_{n}-a_{1}]
, so it cannot be adjacent to
a_{n}
.
We have

(a_{n}-a_{1}+1)-n=|T|\geq|S_{3}\cap T|\geq n-\Delta-a_{1},

which implies

.
As
a_{n}
is adjacent to at least
\delta\geq 1

\operatorname{range}(L)=2n-(\Delta-\delta)-2

Otherwise, we have
|S\cap S_{3}|\leq\Delta-1

As
a_{n}
is adjacent to at least

\operatorname{range}(L)\geq a_{n}+a_{\delta}-a_{1}\geq 2n-\Delta+(\delta-1)=2n-(\Delta-\delta)-1,

again contradicting the assumption that
\operatorname{range}(L)=2n-(\Delta-\delta)-2

Hence,
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\subseteq S
.
∎

P_{n}
as assumed.

Case 2:
a_{n}=2n-3
.
This yields
S=[n-3,2n-3]\setminus\{k\}
for some
n-3<k<2n-3
.
In other words, it is only missing one integer in the interior of
[n-3,2n-3]
.
We notice that
a_{1}+a_{n}=b
so
a_{1}
is adjacent to
a_{n}
.
We also notice that
a_{1}+n=a_{n}
,
a_{1}+(n-1)=2n-4
, and
a_{1}+(n-2)=2n-5
.
For
n\geq 7
, we have
n-2,n-1,n,2n-5
, and
2n-4
are all distinct labels in the interior of
[n-3,2n-3]
, and at most one of them is missing from
S
, which means that for at least two of the three given equations, all terms are in
S
and thus
a_{1}
is adjacent to at least two vertices with labels in
[n-2,n]
.
As
a_{1}
is also adjacent to
a_{n}
, this yields
a_{1}
has degree at least 3, which is forbidden.

We may now assume
a_{1}\leq n-4
.
As
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\subset S
and
a_{1}+1\leq n-3
, we have at least 3 elements in
S
strictly larger than
2a_{1}
.
Any element
x\in S
contained in
[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]
would yield
x-a_{1}
being adjacent to
a_{1}
, so as
\operatorname{deg}(v_{1})\leq 2
, we can have at most 2 elements of
S
in
[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]
.
If there are
x\leq 2
such elements, there are at least
3-x\geq 1
elements of
S
strictly greater than
3a_{1}
, and the largest such element is at least
3a_{1}+3-x
.
If
x<2
, this element is at least
3a_{1}+2
, and as its vertex has degree at least 1, we have
\max L\geq 3a_{1}+2+a_{1}=4a_{1}+2
.
Otherwise
x=2
, which implies
v_{1}
is already adjacent to two vertices whose labels are in
[a_{1}+1,2a_{1}]
, so
\max S\geq 3a_{1}+1
and its vertex has degree at least 1, but is not adjacent to
v_{1}
, so we still have
\max L\geq 3a_{1}+1+a_{1}+1=4a_{1}+2
.
This means
\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})=2n-3\geq 3a_{1}+2
, so
a_{1}\leq\frac{2n-5}{3}
.

Similarly, the same reasoning as in the proof of
[
16
, Claim 2]
with only some very minor modifications implies
a_{1}\geq n-7
.
The inequalities

yield no integer solution for
a_{1}
when

, so for

we have
\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})\neq 2n-3
.

Table

gives that
\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})\geq 2n-2
for

.
The final remaining case of

can be easily verified via computer search.
We must have
a_{1}=9
and
[9,18]\subset S
with

, which leaves 6 more labels for

that must be within
[19,37]
, so only
\binom{19}{6}=27132
labelings must be checked.
∎

Together, these results prove
Theorem

3.1
.

4.
Spum of cycles

In this section we show that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-1
for
n\geq 4
, where
C_{n}
is the cycle graph on
n
vertices.
This corrects for a logical flaw in the proof by Singla, Tiwari, and Tripathi
[
16
]
that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-1
for
n\geq 13
, as well as extending it to include
4\leq n\leq 12
.

The spum and integral spum were determined exactly for complete graphs in
[
16
]
, so as the cycle graphs are complete for
n\leq 3
, we only consider cycle graphs
C_{n}
for
n\geq 4
.

We know from
[
10
]
that the sum number
\sigma(C_{n})=2
, except for
n=4
where
\sigma(C_{4})=3
.
Likewise, from Sharary
[
14
]
the integral sum number
\zeta(C_{n})=0
, except for
n=4
where
\zeta(C_{4})=3
, from Xu
[
17
]
.

Remark 4.1
.

The authors of
[
16
, Theorem 6.1]
show that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1
for all
n\geq 4
; however, as
\sigma(C_{n})
is different for
n=4
, their construction does not hold for
n=4
.
We provide a valid construction in
Theorem

4.2
for this case, and thus the result still holds, despite the original proof not accounting for
n=4
.

A lower bound of
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})\geq 2n-2
for all
n\geq 4
follows from
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
, so
2n-2\leq\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1
.

Then in
[
16
, Theorem 6.2]
, it is claimed that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-1
for all
n\geq 13
.
However, in this proof, in particular the proof of Claim 1, they argue that a vertex with label
2a_{1}
, where
a_{1}=\min L
is the minimum label, is adjacent to a vertex with label greater than
a_{1}
.
They then claim that this implies the maximum label
a_{n}
associated to one of the
n
vertices of
C_{n}
is greater than
3a_{1}
.
Unfortunately, while it can be concluded that a vertex with label greater than
3a_{1}
exists in
L
, this vertex does not necessarily belong to
C_{n}
, as it can be one of the two isolated vertices.
As a simple example of this, consider the labeling
L=[3,6]\cup[8,10]
, which induces
C_{4}
with the four labels in
[3,6]
constituting
C_{4}
, while the three labels in
[8,10]
are additional isolated vertices.
In this case
a_{1}=3
, and
2a_{1}=6
is indeed adjacent to a label greater than
a_{1}=3
, namely 4.
Hence
\max L=10>9=3a_{1}
, but their statement that
a_{n}>3a_{1}
is false, as
a_{n}=6
while
3a_{1}=9
.

In
Theorem

4.2
, we prove this result, and extend it to also address the
4\leq n\leq 12
cases, thus including all
n\geq 4
.
So while the original proof may not be correct, the result of
[
16
, Theorem 6.2]
is still true.
Many of the techniques used in this proof are inspired by the original argument from
[
16
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=\begin{cases}6&n=3\\
2n-1&n\geq 4.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
n=3
, we recover
C_{3}=K_{3}
, whose spum was found in
[
16
]
to be 6.

We first separately address the case
n=4
.
As
\sigma(C_{4})=3
, we have 7 total vertices.
Notice that
L=[3,6]\cup[8,10]
induces a sum graph consisting of
C_{4}
and three isolated vertices, so
\operatorname{spum}(C_{4})\leq\operatorname{range}(L)=7
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{4})\neq 7
, so we have
\operatorname{spum}(C_{4})=6
, meaning the labels have to be a consecutive block of 7 positive integers.
Let the smallest of these seven integers be
a
.
For
a\leq 3
, we have that
a
has degree strictly larger than 2, so this cannot yield
C_{4}
.
For
a\geq 4
, the only edges in the induced sum graph are incident to
a
, because for any two distinct vertices
b
and
c
that are both not
a
, we have
b+c\geq 2a+3\geq a+7
, while
\max L=a+6
.
This cannot yield
C_{4}
.
Hence we have
\operatorname{spum}(C_{4})=7
, as desired.

From
[
16
, Remark 6.1]
, we know
2n-2\leq\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1
for all
n\geq 5
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-2
.
Suppose the vertices of
C_{n}
are labeled
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
, and the two isolated vertices are labeled
b<c
in a labeling
L
that achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=2n-2
.
Notice that in order for
a_{n}
to be adjacent to two other labels
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
for
i<j<n
, we must have
b=a_{i}+a_{n}
and
c=a_{j}+a_{n}
, so the total ordering of our
n+2
labels is
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}<b<c
.
Let
S=\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.
From
Lemma

2.5
, we know
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-2
implies
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\subset S
.
Notice that
a_{1}\leq n-2
, as
a_{n}\geq a_{1}+n-1
, so

\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})\geq(a_{1}+n-1+a_{1}+1)-a_{1}=a_{1}+n,

and thus
a_{1}+n\leq 2n-2
yields
a_{1}\leq n-2
.

We first eliminate the possibility that
a_{1}=n-2
.
If this were so, then
\max L=c=3n-4
.
We have
a_{n}\geq 2n-3
, and as
a_{n}
is adjacent to two labels in
S
, this yields

\max L\geq(2n-3)+(n-1)=3n-4.

As we need this inequality to be sharp, we require
a_{n}=2n-3
and
a_{n}
being adjacent to
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.
With
a_{n}=2n-3
, this means
S=[n-2,2n-3]
.
We must have
a_{n}+a_{1}=3n-5\in L\setminus S
, so
b=3n-5
.
However, we find that
a_{1}+a_{2}=2n-3=a_{n}
, so
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are adjacent.
Thus
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{n}
form a triangle, so this does not yield
C_{n}
for
n\geq 5
.

Now we eliminate the possibility that
a_{1}=n-3
.
If this were so, then
\max L=c=3n-5
.
We have
a_{n}\geq 2n-4
, so
\max L\geq(2n-4)+(n-2)=3n-6
.
As
c=3n-5
, we have a slackness of 1, which affords only a small number of cases:

1)

a_{n}=2n-4
and
a_{n}
is adjacent to
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.

As
a_{n}=2n-4
, this requires
S=[n-3,2n-4]
.
We have
a_{1}+a_{n}=3n-7\in L\setminus S
, so
b=3n-7
.
We also have
a_{2}+a_{n}=(n-2)+(2n-4)=3n-6\in L\setminus S
, so
c=3n-6
, contradicting the fact that
c=3n-5
.

2)

a_{n}=2n-4
and
a_{n}
is adjacent to
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
.

Notice that
a_{1}+a_{3}=(n-3)+(n-1)=2n-4=a_{n}
, so we have a triangle between
a_{1}
,
a_{3}
, and
a_{n}
, contradicting the fact that our induced sumgraph is
C_{n}
for
n\geq 5
.

3)

a_{n}=2n-4
and
a_{n}
is adjacent to
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
.

We have
a_{2}+a_{n}=3n-6=b
, while
a_{3}+a_{n}=3n-5=c
.
Then
a_{n-1}=2n-5
is adjacent to
a_{3}=n-1
as
a_{3}+a_{n-1}=b
.
So
a_{3}
is adjacent to
a_{n}
and
a_{n-1}
.
However, notice that
a_{1}+a_{3}=(n-3)+(n-1)=2n-4=a_{n}
, so
a_{3}
is also adjacent to
a_{1}
, giving
a_{3}
degree 3, contradicting the fact that each vertex in
C_{n}
has degree 2.

4)

a_{n}=2n-3
and
a_{n}
is adjacent to
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.

We have
a_{2}\geq n-2
, but
a_{2}+a_{n}\geq 3n-5=c
, so we need equality and thus
a_{2}=n-2
.
We find
a_{1}+a_{n}=3n-6\in L\setminus S
, so
b=3n-6
.
With
a_{n}=2n-3
, we have
S=[n-3,2n-3]\setminus\{k\}
for some integer
k
where
n-1\leq k\leq 2n-4
.
We cannot have an edge between
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, or else
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{n}
form a triangle.
So
a_{1}+a_{2}=2n-5\not\in S
, which means
k=2n-5
.

Then
a_{1}+a_{3}=2n-4\in S
, so
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
are adjacent.
If
n\geq 6
, then
2n-5\geq n+1
, so
a_{4}=n
and
a_{1}+a_{4}=2n-3=a_{n}
, so
a_{1}
is adjacent to
a_{3}
,
a_{4}
, and
a_{n}
, contradicting the fact that it must have degree 2.

Otherwise
n=5
, and we have
S=\{2,3,4,6,7\}
with
b=9
and
c=10
.
We observe 3 is adjacent to 4, 6, and 7, contradicting the fact that it must have degree 2.

So we can now assume
a_{1}\leq n-4
.
As
[a_{1},2a_{1}]\subset S
, which accounts for
a_{1}+1\leq n-3
of the elements of
S
, we have at least 3 elements in
S
that are strictly larger than
2a_{1}
.
Any such element
x
contained in
[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]
would cause
x-a_{1}
to be adjacent to
a_{1}
, so as
\operatorname{deg}(v_{1})=2
, we can have at most 2 elements of
S
in
[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]
.
If there are
x\leq 2
such elements, there are at least
3-x\geq 1
elements of
S
greater than
3a_{1}
.
The largest such element is at least
3a_{1}+3-x
, and if
x<2
then this element is at least
3a_{1}+2
, and its vertex has degree 2, so
\max L\geq 3a_{1}+2+a_{1}+1=4a_{1}+3
.
Otherwise
x=2
, which implies
v_{1}
is already adjacent to two vertices whose labels are in
[a_{1}+1,2a_{1}]
, so while the element is at least
3a_{1}+1
, its vertex has degree 2 and is not adjacent to
v_{1}
, so we still have
\max L\geq 3a_{1}+1+a_{1}+2=4a_{1}+3
.
This means
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-2\geq 3a_{1}+3
, so
a_{1}\leq\frac{2n-5}{3}
.

On the other hand, we have
a_{1}\geq n-7
from
[
16
, Claim 2]
.

We now analyze what occurs if
a_{1}\leq n-6
.
As
a_{1}
is adjacent to all elements of

\left(S\cap[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]\right)-a_{1}\subseteq[a_{1}+1,2a_{1}],

we have
|S\cap[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]|\leq 2
, and similarly, as
2a_{1}
is adjacent to 2 labels, we have
|S\cap[3a_{1},4a_{1}-1]|\leq 2
.
So

|S\cap[a_{1},4a_{1}-1]|\leq a_{1}+5\leq n-1,

and thus we have
a_{n}\geq 4a_{1}+n-a_{1}-6=3a_{1}+n-6
.
Let
x=|S\cap[2a_{1}+1,3a_{1}]|
; if
x=2
, then
a_{1}
cannot be adjacent to
a_{n}
, meaning
\max L\geq 4a_{1}+n-4
.
Otherwise
x<2
, so we improve our original bound
|S\cap[a_{1},4a_{1}-1]|\leq a_{1}+4
, yielding
a_{n}\geq 4a_{1}+n-a_{1}-5
, and so
\max L\geq 4a_{1}+n-4
.
In either case, we have
a_{1}+2n-2=\max L\geq 4a_{1}+n-4
, which yields
a_{1}\leq\frac{n+2}{3}
.

Notice that for
n\geq 11
, we have
a_{1}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n-5}{3}\right\rfloor\leq n-6
, and thus
n-7\leq a_{1}\leq\frac{n+2}{3}
, which admits no integer solutions for

when
n\leq 11
.
So we have proven the result for all

.

The only remaining cases are

, where we know
\max\{1,n-7\}\leq a_{1}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n-5}{3}\right\rfloor
.

We now show that
a_{1}\geq 2
by showing that if
a_{1}=1
, the degree of
a_{1}
is at least 3.
If
a_{1}=1
, then
c=2n-1
and
a_{1}
could be adjacent to
x
, for all integers

.
There are
2n-3
such integers
x
.
The only way for

to not be adjacent to

is if
x\not\in L
or
x+1\not\in L
.
As
b
and the
n-1
other labels in
S
are contained in
[2,2n-2]
, there are only
2n-3-n=n-3
values in this interval not contained in

.
Each such missing value

can prevent

from being adjacent to
y
and

, so these

potential edges out of the

has at least 3 incident edges.

For
n=5
, our original

bound gives

, but we also need

, which yields no solution.
For
n=11
, we have

which requires

, so we can eliminate the possibility of

.
Hence, we have reduced our problem to

, with the following bounds on

•

If
n=6
, then
2\leq a_{1}\leq 2
.

•

If
n=7
, then
2\leq a_{1}\leq 3
.

•

If
n=8
, then
2\leq a_{1}\leq 3
.

•

If
n=9
, then

.

•

If
n=10
, then

.

•

If
n=11
, then

.

These remaining cases can be easily ruled out by computer search.
We have bounded
a_{1}=\min L
, and as we assume
\operatorname{range}(L)=2n-2
, we have

.
Between these two values we must pick

.

If one wanted to do these remaining cases by hand, the following is an example for the case

.

For
n=6
, we have

, and

.
Among 5, 6, and 7, we can have at most 2 elements; otherwise, 2 is adjacent to 3, 4, and 5.
This yields

, but also notice that

, so

.
If

, then 8 and 4 are adjacent, and 8 is also adjacent to either 2 or 3.

, but then

and

So 8 is adjacent to 3 and 4; additionally, 7 cannot be in

Otherwise, we have

, which must be adjacent to 2 and 3, so

.
As 2 is adjacent to some element in

for each element in

.

would create an edges between itself and both 2 and 4 is 7, which would cause an additional edge between 3 and 4, thus not creating

.
∎

7.
The sum-diameter of a graph

As integral spum is allowed to use the integers
\mathbb{Z}
instead of just the positive integers
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we expect
\operatorname{ispum}(G)
to be lower than
\operatorname{spum}(G)
.
For example, we have

\operatorname{spum}(K_{3})=6>2=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{3})

as shown in
[
16
]
.
However, there is no obvious relationship between
\operatorname{ispum}(G)
and
\operatorname{spum}(G)
, due to
\zeta(G)
possibly being strictly smaller than
\sigma(G)
, i.e., when the inequality
\zeta(G)\leq\sigma(G)
is strict.
For example,
Theorem

4.2
implies
\operatorname{spum}(C_{12})=23
, yet
\operatorname{ispum}(C_{12})=25
as calculated in
[
16
]
, which we independently verified by exhaustive computer search.
In such cases,
\operatorname{ispum}(G)
is restricted to work with fewer additional isolated vertices, which can make it more difficult to have a lower
\operatorname{range}(L)
.
It would be convenient to remove the restriction that we are simultaneously minimizing the number of additional vertices as well as the range of the labeling, and instead solely minimize the range.
Motivated by this, we introduce the following modification of spum, which we call the sum-diameter.

Definition 7.1
.

The
sum-diameter
of a graph
G
, denoted
\operatorname{sd}(G)
, is the minimum possible value of
\operatorname{range}(L)
for a set
L
of positive integer labels, such that the induced sum graph of
L
consists of the disjoint union of
G
with any number of isolated vertices.

The difference between the definition of sum-diameter and spum is that in spum, one is required to use the minimum number of additional isolated vertices possible.

Notice that the sum-diameter is not related to the distance along edges of a graph, though the terminology is consistent with the diameter of a set in a metric space, if one considers the set of labels
L
to be inside a metric space.

We similarly define the
integral sum-diameter
of a graph
G
, denoted
\operatorname{isd}(G)
, by allowing the labels to be arbitrary distinct integers.

Clearly
\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq\operatorname{spum}(G)
, as any set of labels
L
from the definition of
\operatorname{spum}(G)
, i.e., on
n+\sigma(G)
vertices, is a valid labeling for the definition of
\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
Similarly,
\operatorname{isd}(G)\leq\operatorname{ispum}(G)
.
Finally, as desired, any labeling
L
from the definition of
\operatorname{sd}(G)
is a valid labeling for the definition of
\operatorname{isd}(G)
, which yields the following proposition.

Proposition 7.2
.

For all graphs
G
,
\operatorname{isd}(G)\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)
.

A set

We use Sidon sets to prove the following result.

Theorem 7.11
.

Let

Then
\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq 64n^{2}-64n+9

Proof.

For

\operatorname{sd}(G)=0
so the bound holds.

For

, we provide a general construction for a set of labels

that induces

along with
|E|

|E|
is the number of edges in
G
.
As

, we have by the well-known Bertrand’s postulate, first proven by Chebyshev, that there exists an odd prime

such that

(see, for example, Derbyshire
[
4

).
Erdős and Turán
[

showed that there exists a Sidon set with

Pick an arbitrary
n

through

Clearly
S=\{4s_{i}+1\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
is also a Sidon set itself.
Enumerating the

, we label each vertex

with
4s_{i}+1
.
For each edge
(i,j)\in E

4s_{i}+4s_{j}+2
to
L
.
We claim

, which has size
|L|=|V|+|E|
, induces
G
along with
|E|
isolated vertices.

Our labeling
L
has “vertex-type” labels that are all congruent to 1 mod 4, and “edge-type” labels that are all congruent to 2 mod 4.
Thus, by simply considering their sums modulo 4, the only edges that
L
can induce are between vertex-type labels.
So it suffices to show that
L
induces an edge between labels
4s_{i}+1
and
4s_{j}+1
if and only if
(i,j)\in E
.
By construction, if
(i,j)\in E
, then

, so there is an edge between labels
4s_{i}+1
and
4s_{j}+1
.
For the other direction, suppose

4s_{i}+1
and
4s_{j}+1
for

, or in other words
4s_{i}+4_{j}+2\in L
.
Then as
4s_{i}+4s_{j}+2\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, this is an edge-type label, and was constructed for some edge
(u,v)\in E
for

.
So

(4s_{i}+1)+(4s_{j}+1)=(4s_{u}+1)+(4s_{v}+1),

but as
S
is a Sidon set this implies
\{i,j\}=\{u,v\}
.
Hence
(i,j)\in E
.

Thus
L
correctly induces
G
along with
|E|
isolated vertices.
We observe that

\max L\leq 4s_{n-1}+4s_{n}+2\leq 8\cdot 2p^{2}-2\leq 16(2n-1)^{2}-2

and
\min L=4s_{1}+1\geq 5
, so

\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq\operatorname{range}(L)\leq 64n^{2}-64n+9,

as desired.
∎

Theorem 7.13
.

Let
f(n)
be defined as the maximum value of
\operatorname{sd}(G)
over all graphs
G
with
n
vertices.
Then
f(n)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}-O(n\log n)=\Omega(n^{2})
.

Proof.

Consider the set of unlabeled connected graphs
G
with
n
vertices.
The cardinality of this set is easily seen to be at least
(1-o(1))2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!
.
For each unlabeled connected graph
G
, consider a labeling
L
that induces
G
while achieving
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
The labelings for all of the unlabeled connected graphs must be pairwise distinct.
All of these labelings satisfy
\operatorname{range}(L)\leq f(n)
, and induce a connected graph
G
with
n
vertices, along with some additional isolated vertices.
We claim there are at most
2^{2f(n)}
such labelings.
Consider the set of labels
S\subset L
of the
n
vertices of
G
, and enumerate them
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.
Notice that
a_{1}=\min S=\min L
, as otherwise labels below
a_{1}
can be removed while still inducing
G
, and strictly decreasing
\operatorname{range}(L)
, contradicting the minimality of
\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
Then
a_{1}
must be adjacent to some label, meaning
a_{1}+a_{i}\in L
for some
i
, and thus

a_{1}+\operatorname{sd}(G)=\max L\geq a_{1}+a_{i}\geq a_{1}+a_{2}\geq 2a_{1}+1,

so
a_{1}\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
Thus

\max L=a_{1}+\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1\leq 2f(n).

There are only
2^{2f(n)}
sets of positive integers such that the maximum element is at most
2f(n)
, and as each distinct connected graph with
n
vertices must have a distinct such labeling, we find
2^{2f(n)}\geq(1-o(1))2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!
, so

2f(n)\geq\binom{n}{2}-\log_{2}(n!)=\frac{n^{2}}{2}-O(n\log n)

using Stirling’s approximation, and thus we have
f(n)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}-O(n\log n)
, as desired.
∎

Remark 7.14
.

In fact, the proof of
Theorem

7.13
implies that not only does some graph have
\Omega(n^{2})
sum-diameter, but for sufficiently large
n
, nearly all, i.e.,
1-o(1)
proportion, of the graphs on
n
vertices have
\Omega(n^{2})
diameter.
This follows from the observation that only
o(1)
proportion of the graphs can have
o(n^{2})
sum-diameter.
This sharply contrasts the current knowledge of bounds on the sum-diameter, upper bounded by the spum, of special families of graphs, e.g.
K_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
K_{1,n}
,
K_{n,n}
, and
nK_{2}
, all of which have been upper bounded linearly.

8.
Sum-diameter of complete graphs
K_{n}

We find that the (integral) sum-diameters have the same values as the (integral) spum for complete graphs, i.e.,
\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})
for all
n\geq 2
and
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})
for all
n\geq 2
.

Proposition 8.1
.

For
n\geq 5
, we have

\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6.

Proof.

From
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
we know
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
.
Hence

\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})\leq\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})\leq\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6.

It suffices to show that
\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})\geq 4n-6
.

Our proof is similar to the original proof by Singla, Tiwari, and Tripathi
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
that
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=4n-6
.
Let
L
be a labeling of an integral sum graph
G
consisting of
K_{n}
and some isolated vertices.
Let the labels of
K_{n}
be
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
, sorted in increasing order
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
.
As any two
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
must be adjacent, we have
a_{i}+a_{j}\in L
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
Furthermore, observe that if
x,x+a_{i}\in L
for some
i
such that
x\neq a_{i}
, then
x\in S
, because
a_{i}
and
x
are adjacent and thus
x
must be part of
K_{n}
.
We will make use of this fact frequently.

Define
A=a_{1}+\left(S\setminus\{a_{1}\}\right)
and
B=a_{n}+\left(S\setminus\{a_{1},a_{n}\}\right)
.
From Chen
[
3
, Theorem 1]
we know that for
n\geq 5
, the sets
A
,
B
, and
S
are pairwise disjoint.
Hence,
A\cup B\subseteq L\setminus S
.
We now show that
a_{i}+a_{j}\in L\setminus S
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
Using the prior results about
A
and
B
, it suffices to show this for the remaining case
1<i<j<n
.
Suppose otherwise, that
a_{i}+a_{j}\in S
for some
1<i<j<n
, so that
a_{i}+a_{j}=a_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq n
.
Then
a_{n}+a_{j}\in L
and
(a_{n}+a_{j})+a_{i}=a_{n}+a_{k}\in L
.
As
\zeta(K_{n})=2n-3>0
, we find
0\not\in L
; we can assume
a_{n}>0
, as otherwise all
a_{i}<0
and we can use
-L
instead of
L
.
Moreover,
a_{n}+a_{j}>0+a_{i}
, so
a_{n}+a_{j}\neq a_{i}
, so by our prior observation this implies
a_{n}+a_{j}\in S
, yet
a_{n}+a_{j}\in B
which is disjoint from
S
, a contradiction.

We now consider
B-|a_{1}|
, and we split into two cases depending on the sign of
a_{1}
.

If
a_{1}>0
, notice that
B-a_{1}
is disjoint from
S
, as
\max S=a_{n}<a_{n}+(a_{2}-a_{1})=\min(B-a_{1})
.
We now claim
B-a_{1}
is disjoint from
L
.
Suppose otherwise: then there exists an
i
such that
a_{n}+a_{i}-a_{1}\in L\setminus S
.
Suppose
a_{n}+a_{i}-a_{1}=x
, for
x\in L
.
Then
x+a_{1}=a_{n}+a_{i}
, where
x=a_{n}+a_{i}-a_{1}>a_{i}>a_{1}
, so
x
and
a_{1}
are adjacent, but
a_{1}
cannot be adjacent to an element in
L\setminus S
.
Note that
B-a_{1}\subset[\min L,\max L]
, as

\min L\leq\max S<\min(B-a_{1})<\max(B-a_{1})<\max B\leq\max L.

If
a_{1}<0
, then
B+a_{1}
is disjoint from
L
, as otherwise
a_{n}+a_{i}+a_{1}\in L
for some
i
.
But then
a_{n}+a_{i}\in L\setminus S
is adjacent to
a_{1}\in S
, which is forbidden.
Note that
B+a_{1}\subset[\min L,\max L]
, as

\displaystyle\min L

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}+a_{2}<a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{n}=\min(B+a_{1})<\max(B+a_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{1}+a_{n-1}+a_{n}<a_{n-1}+a_{n}\leq\max L.

In either case, as
A\cup B\subseteq L\setminus S
, we have

|L|\geq|S|+|A|+|B|=n+(n-1)+(n-2)=3n-3,

and as
\left|B-|a_{1}|\right|=n-2
, we have

\max L-\min L\geq|L|+\left|B-|a_{1}|\right|-1\geq(n+(2n-3))+(n-2)-1=4n-6.

Hence,
\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})\geq 4n-6
.
∎

It is known and easy to confirm that
\sigma(K_{2})=1
and
\sigma(K_{3})=2
(see for example
[
7
]
), whereas
\zeta(K_{2})=\zeta(K_{3})=0
, which can be seen by the labelings
\{0,1\}
and
\{0,\pm 1\}
.
From
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have
\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
even for
n=2,3
, but
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{2})=1
and
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{3})=2
.

Lemma 8.2
.

We have
\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
for
n=2,3
, as well as
\operatorname{isd}(K_{2})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{2})=1
and
\operatorname{isd}(K_{3})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{3})=2
.

Proof.

The spum and ispum values are already known.
To show that
\operatorname{sd}(K_{2})=2
, notice that as
K_{2}
has no isolated vertices, at least one additional vertex is needed, yielding at least 3 vertices and thus
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 2
for all valid labelings
L
.
A range of 2 can be achieved by
\{1,2,3\}
, and thus
\operatorname{sd}(K_{2})=2
.

To show that
\operatorname{sd}(K_{3})=6
, since we have
\sigma(K_{3})=2
we need at least two more vertices.
If we use exactly two additional vertices, then the smallest value of
\operatorname{range}(L)
is simply
\operatorname{spum}(K_{3})=6
.
If we use at least four additional vertices, for a total of at least seven, then
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 6
.
The only remaining case is using three additional vertices.
With six vertices, we have
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 5
.
In order to rule out
\operatorname{sd}(K_{3})=5
, we simply need to check the cases when
L=\{a_{1},a_{1}+1,\dots,a_{1}+5\}
.
If
a_{1}\leq 2
, then the degree of
a_{1}
exceeds two, while the maximum degree in
K_{3}
is two.
If
a_{1}\geq 3
, then all edges are incident to
a_{1}
, as any other edge, say between
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
, would require
a_{i}+a_{j}\in L
, but

a_{i}+a_{j}\geq a_{2}+a_{3}=2a_{1}+3\geq a_{1}+6\geq\max L,

so this is impossible.
This cannot yield
K_{3}
, so
\operatorname{sd}(K_{3})=6
.

To show that
\operatorname{isd}(K_{2})=1
, notice that
K_{2}
already has two vertices so
\operatorname{isd}(K_{2})\geq 1
, but
\operatorname{isd}(K_{2})\leq\operatorname{ispum}(K_{2})=1
, so
\operatorname{isd}(K_{2})=1
.

Similarly, to show that
\operatorname{isd}(K_{3})=2
, as
K_{3}
itself has three vertices, we have
\operatorname{isd}(K_{3})\geq 2
, but
\operatorname{isd}(K_{3})\leq\operatorname{ispum}(K_{3})=2
, so
\operatorname{isd}(K_{3})=2
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

For
n=4
, we have
\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
.

Proof.

From
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
we know
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})\leq\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})\leq 4n-6
.
Hence it suffices to show that
\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})\geq 4n-6
.

The proof is similar to the original proof that
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=4n-6
, except
n=4
requires some additional consideration due to the result from
[
3
]
assuming
n\geq 5
.
In particular,
[
3
]
requires that at least three labels in
S
are of the same sign.
So if at least three of the four labels in
S
are of the same sign, the same argument as in
Proposition

8.1
applies, yielding
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 4n-6=10
.

Otherwise, we have
a_{1}<a_{2}<0<a_{3}<a_{4}
.
Note that 0 cannot be in
L
as 0 would be adjacent to every other label, yet we must have at least
\zeta(K_{4})=\sigma(K_{4})=2n-3=5
isolated vertices.
We need at least 9 total vertices.
If there are exactly 9 vertices in our graph, and the interval
[\min L,\max L]
must contain
0\not\in L
, we find
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 9
with equality if and only if there are no other gaps, i.e.,
L=[x,x+9]\setminus\{0\}
for some
x<0
.
Replacing
L
with
-L
if necessary, we can assume at least 5 of the terms are positive, whereas at least 2 of the terms are negative as
a_{1}<a_{2}<0<a_{3}<a_{4}
.
So
L
contains
[1,5]\cup[-2,-1]
, meaning 1 is adjacent to 2, 3, 4, and
-2
, yielding degree 4 which is too high.
Thus
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 10
in this case.

If we have at least 10 vertices, where
a_{1}<a_{2}<0<a_{3}<a_{4}
, then
[\min L,\max L]
contains
0\not\in L
, so
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 10
.

In all cases,
\operatorname{range}(L)\geq 10
, so
\operatorname{isd}(K_{4})\geq 10=4n-6
.
∎

Together, these three results imply the following theorem.

Theorem 8.4
.

We have
\operatorname{sd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=4n-6
for all
n\geq 2
.
In addition, for all
n\geq 2
we have
\operatorname{isd}(K_{n})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=\begin{cases}n-1&n\leq 3\\
4n-6&n\geq 4.\end{cases}

Remark 8.5
.

In
[
16
]
, Theorem 3.2 states that for
n\geq 4
, we have
\operatorname{spum}(K_{n})=\operatorname{ispum}(K_{n})=4n-6
.
Their proof cites results from
[
3
]
claiming that
S
is sum-free, i.e.,
a_{i}+a_{j}\in L\setminus S
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
However,
[
3
]
only demonstrates that
A\cup B\subseteq L\setminus S
, i.e., they only address sums
a_{i}+a_{j}
where either
i=1
or
j=n
.
Thankfully, as shown in the proof of
Proposition

8.1
, it is not hard to extend this observation to include all
a_{i}+a_{j}
; this has also been addressed in a recent paper by Elizeche and Tripathi
[
5
]
.

More pressingly,
[
3
]
assumes
n\geq 5
in order for at least three labels to be of the same sign, which allows them to conclude that
A\cup B\subseteq L\setminus S
.
Thus, the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
does not hold for
n=4
.
We separately address this case in
Lemma

8.3
, taking care to only assume
\operatorname{ispum}(K_{4})\leq\operatorname{spum}(K_{4})\leq 10
using the explicit construction from
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
, rather than using the stronger assertion of
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Lemma

8.3
resolves the case
n=4
, and thus while the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
may not be entirely correct, the result itself is demonstrably correct.

9.
Integral sum-diameter of cycles
C_{n}
and paths
P_{n}

The current knowledge regarding
\operatorname{ispum}(C_{n})
and
\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})
is relatively lacking compared to other common families of graphs.
The integral spum of complete graphs
K_{n}
, star graphs
K_{1,n}
and complete symmetric bipartite graphs
K_{n,n}
are all exactly known
[
16
]
.
The spum of cycles was previously bounded within
[2n-2,2n-1]
by
[
16
]
; however, the current best bounds for the integral spum of cycles are

\displaystyle 2n-5\leq\operatorname{ispum}(C_{n})\leq\begin{cases}8(n-9)&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
\frac{3}{2}(3n-14)&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\end{cases}

where the upper bound comes from
Theorem

5.1
and holds for
n\geq 12
.
The lower bound is due to
[
16
]
and holds for all
n\geq 4
.

Intuitively, the reason it is harder to close this gap for the integral spum of cycles is that
\zeta(C_{n})=0
for all
n\neq 4
, as shown by
[
14
]
, while
\sigma(C_{n})=2
for all
n\neq 4
.
This restriction to not being able to use additional isolated vertices makes constructing an efficient, i.e., low range, labeling
L
quite difficult.

This is a second example in which the sum-diameter is arguably a better property to study, in addition to there being simple upper bounds on the sum-diameter of arbitrary graphs as provided in
Section

7
.
First, we bound
\operatorname{sd}(C_{n})
by the same bounds as for
\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})
.
Notice that we assume
n\geq 4
, as when
n=3
we recover
K_{3}
, which we have completely determined in
Section

8
.

Proposition 9.1
.

For all
n\geq 4
, we have

\displaystyle 2n-2\leq\operatorname{sd}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from the fact that
\operatorname{sd}(C_{n})\leq\operatorname{spum}(C_{n})=2n-1
, from
Theorem

4.2
.
The lower bound follows from
Theorem

7.7
.
∎

This allows us to bound
\operatorname{isd}(C_{n})
far more effectively than the current bounds for
\operatorname{ispum}(C_{n})
.

Proposition 9.2
.

For all
n\geq 4
, we have

\displaystyle 2n-5\leq\operatorname{isd}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
Theorem

7.9
, and the upper bound follows from
\operatorname{isd}(C_{n})\leq\operatorname{sd}(C_{n})\leq 2n-1
, where the first inequality comes from
Proposition

7.2
and the second comes from
Proposition

9.1
.
∎

Similarly, for the path graphs
P_{n}
, the current best bounds for
\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})
are

\displaystyle 2n-2\leq\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})\leq\begin{cases}2n+1&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
2n-1&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\end{cases}

where the lower bound is due to
Theorem

3.5
and holds for
n\geq 7
, and the upper bound is from
Theorem

3.2
.
However, the current best bounds for
\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})
are

(6)

\displaystyle 2n-5\leq\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})\leq\begin{cases}\frac{5}{2}(n-3)&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
2n-3&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\end{cases}

again due to
[
16
]
, where the upper bounds were proven for
n\geq 7
.

Despite the integral spum being able to work with a larger set of possible labels, due to
\zeta(P_{n})=0

[
11
]
being strictly less than
\sigma(P_{n})=1

[
10
]
, it is not necessarily true that
\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})\leq\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})
, and without any additional isolated vertices to work with, constructing an efficient labeling
L
is quite difficult.

However, by using the sum-diameter, we find a much tighter result for integral sum-diameter.
First, we translate
Eq.

1
to
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})
.

Proposition 9.3
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle 2n-3\leq\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})\leq\begin{cases}2n+1&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
2n-1&\text{if }n\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The upper bound follows from
Theorem

3.2
and the fact that
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})\leq\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})
for all
n
.
The lower bound follows from
Theorem

7.7
.
∎

In fact, we can improve the upper bound using a construction with two isolated vertices.

Proposition 9.4
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle 2n-3\leq\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})\leq 2n-2.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
Proposition

9.3
.
The upper bound follows from the labeling
L=[n-1,2n-2]\cup\{3n-4,3n-3\}
, which induces the path
2n-2,n-1,2n-3,n,\dots
, alternating between decreasing from
2n-2
and increasing from
n-1
, where the exact formulation of the other end of the path depends on the parity of
n
.
It is straightforward to check that no other edges exist, as all edges must have their sum of the two labels being either
3n-4
or
3n-3
.
∎

An exhaustive computer search yields
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})=2n-3
for
3\leq n\leq 6
, but
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})=2n-2
for
7\leq n\leq 13
.
This yields the following conjecture.

Conjecture 9.5
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})=\begin{cases}2n-3&3\leq n\leq 6,\\
2n-2&n\geq 7.\end{cases}

In order to address the general case of
n\geq 7
, it suffices to assume
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})=2n-3
, which allows one to use
Remark

7.8
, and reach a contradiction.

This yields a far more efficient bound for
\operatorname{isd}(P_{n})
than the bounds for
\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})
.

Proposition 9.6
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle 2n-5\leq\operatorname{isd}(P_{n})\leq\begin{cases}2n-2&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
2n-3&\text{if }n\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
Theorem

7.9
.
The upper bound for the odd case follows by applying
Proposition

7.2
to
Proposition

9.4
, and the upper bound for the even case when
n\geq 8
follows from
Eq.

6
and the fact that
\operatorname{isd}(P_{n})\leq\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})
for all
n
.
For
n\in\{4,6\}
, we have
\operatorname{isd}(P_{n})\leq\operatorname{ispum}(P_{n})\leq 2n-4<2n-3
by using
L=\{-1,1,2,3\}
and
L=\{-1,1,3,4,6,7\}
.
∎

10.
Sum-diameter under various binary graph operations

In this section we discuss the behavior of the sum-diameter under various binary graph operations.

First, we prove the following lemma, which will be frequently used.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices.
Then for a labeling
L
that induces
G
along with some additional isolated vertices and achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
, we have
\max L\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.

Proof.

Suppose the labels of
G
in
L
are
S
, so that
L\setminus S
are the labels of the additional isolated vertices.
Let
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
where we sort
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
.
As
G
has no isolated vertices,
a_{1}
must be adjacent to some other vertex
a_{i}
for some
i\geq 2
, which means
a_{1}+a_{i}\in L
, and thus
a_{1}+a_{i}\leq\max L=a_{1}+\operatorname{sd}(G)
, so
a_{i}\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
As
a_{1}\leq a_{i}-(i-1)\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
, we find
\max L=a_{1}+\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
∎

10.1.
Disjoint union

Let
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
denote the disjoint union of graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
The following result provides an upper bound on
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})
given
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})
and
\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})
.

Theorem 10.2
.

Suppose
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two graphs with no isolated vertices.
Then

(7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})\leq 2(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1)(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1)-1.

Proof.

Consider a labeling
L_{1}
that induces
G_{1}
(along with some isolated vertices) while achieving
\operatorname{range}(L_{1})=\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})
and likewise a labeling
L_{2}
that induces
G_{2}
while achieving
\operatorname{range}(L_{2})=\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})
.
From
Lemma

10.1
, we know
\max L_{1}\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1
and
\max L_{2}\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1
.

Now consider the labeling
L_{2}^{\prime}=(4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2)L_{2}
, i.e., every label in
L_{2}
is multiplied by
4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2
.
We claim
L_{1}\cup L_{2}^{\prime}
induces a labeling consisting of
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
along with some isolated vertices.
Notice that scaling a labeling does not change its induced sum graph, so
L_{2}^{\prime}
induces
G_{2}
and
L_{1}
induces
G_{1}
.
Hence it suffices to show that no additional edges exist between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
, and no additional edges exist between the vertices in
L_{1}
due to the presence of
L_{2}^{\prime}
or vice versa.

As
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})\geq 1
, we have
4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2\geq 2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})>2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1
.
So,

\max L_{1}\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1<4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2\leq\min L_{2}^{\prime}.

If an edge existed between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
, suppose this edge is between
x_{1}\in L_{1}
and
y_{1}\in L_{2}^{\prime}
.
Then
x_{1}+y_{1}\in L_{2}^{\prime}
, so suppose
x_{1}+y_{1}=y_{2}
for
y_{2}\in L_{2}^{\prime}
.
As
x_{1}>0
we have
y_{2}>y_{1}
, and in particular
x_{1}=y_{2}-y_{1}\geq 4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2>2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1
, but
x_{1}\leq\max L_{1}\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1
, a contradiction.
Thus no edge exists between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
.

Clearly no additional edge between two vertices in
L_{2}^{\prime}
exists due to the presence of
L_{1}
, as
\min L_{2}^{\prime}>\max L_{1}>0
.
It remains to show that no additional edge between two vertices in
L_{1}
exists due to the presence of
L_{2}^{\prime}
.
Suppose otherwise, that there exist distinct
x_{1},x_{2}\in L_{1}
such that
x_{1}+x_{2}\in L_{2}^{\prime}
.
However, we find

x_{1}+x_{2}\leq 2\max L_{1}-1\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-3<4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2\leq\min L_{2}^{\prime},

so
x_{1}+x_{2}\not\in L_{2}^{\prime}
, and thus no such edge exists.

We now evaluate
\operatorname{range}(L_{1}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})
.
As
\max L_{1}<\min L_{2}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{range}(L_{1}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2)\max L_{2}-\min L_{1}\leq(4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-2)(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1)-1

\displaystyle=2(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1)(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1)-1.

Hence,
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})\leq 2(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-1)(2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1)-1
.
∎

Remark 10.3
.

The proof of
Theorem

10.2
cannot be modified to work for spum, as while the proof shows
\sigma(G_{1}\cup G_{2})\leq\sigma(G_{1})+\sigma(G_{2})
, without knowing the precise value of
\sigma(G_{1}\cup G_{2})
, this construction may not be a valid labeling for
\operatorname{spum}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})
if it does not use the minimum number of additional vertices necessary, i.e., if
\sigma(G_{1}\cup G_{2})<\sigma(G_{1})+\sigma(G_{2})
.

If we had
\sigma(G_{1}\cup G_{2})=\sigma(G_{1})+\sigma(G_{2})
, then one could replace all of the sum-diameters in
Eq.

7
with spums, as the same proof would hold.

This is a third reason, in addition to the ones provided in Section
7
and
9
, as to why sum-diameter is arguably a better property to study than spum.

The previous result relies on the fact that the sum graph structure is invariant under scaling of the labelings.
While the sum graph structure is not invariant under translation, the following lemma demonstrates translation can be a useful construction method to enforce additional structure on a labeling.

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
L
be a labeling that induces
G
along with some additional isolated vertices that achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
Let
S\subset L
be the set of labels associated with
G
itself, and let
T
be the set of labels
c\in L
such that there exist distinct
a,b\in L
where
a+b=c
.
Then for any
x\geq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1-\min L
, let
L^{\prime}=(S+x)\cup(T+2x)
.
Then
L^{\prime}
induces
G
along with some additional isolated vertices, so that if for any
c\in L^{\prime}
there exist distinct
a,b\in L^{\prime}
with
a+b=c
, then
c
is an isolated vertex.

Proof.

If we originally had an edge relation of the form
a+b=c
in
L
, as
a,b\in S
and
c\in T
, then we still have this edge relation in
L^{\prime}
, for
(a+x)+(b+x)=c+2x
.
From
Lemma

10.1
we know
L\subseteq[\min L,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)]
, and as
\max L
must be isolated, we have
\max L\not\in S
, so
S\subseteq[\min L,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)-1]
.
Hence
S+x\subseteq[\min L+x,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)+x-1]
.
Similarly,
T\subseteq[2\min L+1,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)]
yields
T+2x\subseteq[2\min L+1+2x,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)+2x]
.
Notice that
\max(S+x)<\min(T+2x)
as
x\geq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1-\min L
.
We have

\operatorname{range}(T+2x)\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)-\min L-1<\operatorname{sd}(G),

so there are no edges between
S+x
and
T+2x
in the induced sum graph of
L^{\prime}
.
There are no edges between two elements of
T+2x
as the sum of any two such elements strictly exceeds
\max L^{\prime}=\max T+2x
, again using the assumption that
x\geq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1-\min L
.
Two elements of
S+x
are adjacent in the induced sum graph of
L^{\prime}
if and only if their corresponding elements in
S
are adjacent in the induced sum graph of
L
.
The sum of two adjacent elements in
S+x
must be in
T+2x
, by construction.
Hence, we have the desired result.
∎

Notice that this translation construction separates
S
and
T
, for
\max(S+x)<\min(T+2x)
.
As
S+x
corresponds to the vertices of
G
while
T+2x
consists of isolated vertices that induce the desired edges in
G
, we will often refer to
S+x
as the set of “vertex labels” and
T+2x
as the set of “edge labels.”

The translation method of
Lemma

10.4
implies the following result.

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices.
The minimum range of a labeling
L^{\prime}
of
G
that only has edges of the form
(u,v)
where
u+v
is an isolated vertex is at most
2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
.

Proof.

Let
L
be a labeling that induces
G
along with some additional isolated vertices that achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
We use the construction of
Lemma

10.4
with
x=\operatorname{sd}(G)-1-\min L
to yield
L^{\prime}
.
Notice that
L^{\prime}
satisfies the required condition, and we observe

\displaystyle\operatorname{range}(L)

\displaystyle=\max L-\min L=\max T-\min S+x\leq\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)-\min L+x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sd}(G)+x=2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1-\min L\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2,

as desired.
∎

\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})

Theorem 10.6

Finally, it remains to show that there are no edges between

The maximum element of

Thus,

Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{range}(L_{1}^{\prime}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})\leq(12\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-\min L_{2}+\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})+4)-(\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+1)

Remark 10.7
.

The upper bound from

To see this, consider the infinite family of graphs

10.2.
Adding isolated vertices

Theorem

10.6
assumes
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
do not have isolated vertices.
For completeness, if one wishes to add isolated vertices to a graph
G
, the following upper bound holds.

Theorem 10.8
.

Suppose
G
is a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
N_{k}
be the empty graph on
k
vertices, so that
G\cup N_{k}
is the graph consisting of
G
with
k
additional isolated vertices.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G\cup N_{k})\leq\max\{k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k-5.

Proof.

Consider a labeling
L
that induces
G
, along with at least one additional isolated vertex, that achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
From
Lemma

10.1
, we know
\max L\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
, and thus adding additional vertices with labels at least
4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
does not add any edges incident to any vertex in
L
.
We now separate into two cases.

Case 1:
k\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
If we add isolated vertices with labels in
[4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2,8\operatorname{sd}(G)-4]
, these vertices cannot be adjacent to each other, as their smallest sum is
8\operatorname{sd}(G)-3
, which is larger than any label.
This is a total of
4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
possible labels, so if
k-1\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
we can simply add the
k-1
labels in
[4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2,4\operatorname{sd}(G)+k-4]
to
L
, which will induce
G
along with at least
k
isolated vertices, namely the
k-1
isolated vertices we added along with the at least one additional isolated vertex that
L
must have originally induced, due to
G
not having any isolated vertices.
As
4\operatorname{sd}(G)+k-4
is the new largest label and
\min L\geq 1
, the range of our labeling is at most
4\operatorname{sd}(G)+k-5=\max\{k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k-5
.

Case 2:
k>4\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
We can add in
k-1
isolated vertices with labels being
[k-2,2k-4]
, where
k-2\geq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1>4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
, so these vertices are truly isolated and do not add any edges incident to any vertex in
L
.
As
2k-4
is the new largest label and
\min L\geq 1
, the range of our labeling is at most
2k-5=\max\{k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k-5
.
∎

Remark 10.9
.

The upper bound from
Theorem

10.8
is tight up to a constant factor.

To see this, notice that
\operatorname{sd}(G\cup N_{k})\geq\max\{\operatorname{sd}(G),k+1\}
, so if
\operatorname{sd}(G)\geq k+1
, then

5\operatorname{sd}(G\cup N_{k})\geq 5\operatorname{sd}(G)\geq\max\{k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k-5,

and if
\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq k
, then

5\operatorname{sd}(G\cup N_{k})\geq 5k+5\geq\max\{4k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k+5\geq\max\{k,4\operatorname{sd}(G)\}+k-5,

so the upper bound is within a factor of 5 of the true sum-diameter value.

10.3.
Graph join

Let
G_{1}+G_{2}
denote the join of graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, which is the disjoint union
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
together with all the edges joining
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, i.e., edges where one vertex is from the first vertex set and the other from the second.

Before we address the graph join, however, we first address adding a vertex with arbitrary edges incident to it.

Theorem 10.10
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
G^{\prime}
be a graph obtained by adding a vertex to
G
along with any desired edges incident to this new vertex.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G^{\prime})\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1.

Proof.

Let
L
be an optimal labeling that induces
G
along with some isolated vertices.

Lemma

10.1
implies that
\min L\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
We use the translation construction in
Lemma

10.4
with
x=\operatorname{sd}(G)-\min L
to yield
L^{\prime}
, which induces
G
.
The vertex labels of
L^{\prime}
are within
[\operatorname{sd}(G),2\operatorname{sd}(G)-1]
as the vertex labels of
L
are within
[\min L,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)-1]
, and the edge labels of
L
are within
[2\min L+1,\min L+\operatorname{sd}(G)]
, so the edge labels of
L^{\prime}
are within
[2\operatorname{sd}(G)+1,3\operatorname{sd}(G)-\min L]
.

Now multiply all labels of
L^{\prime}
by 2 to yield
L^{\prime\prime}
, which still induces
G
.
Thus the vertex labels are within
[2\operatorname{sd}(G),4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2]
and the edge labels are within
[4\operatorname{sd}(G)+2,6\operatorname{sd}(G)-2]
.
Now we add our new vertex with label
b=2\operatorname{sd}(G)+1
.
For every vertex label
a_{i}\in L^{\prime\prime}
that our new vertex is adjacent to in
G^{\prime}
, we add an edge label
b+a_{i}
, so that our new vertex is adjacent to the desired vertices in
G
.
As all the labels of
L^{\prime\prime}
are even and the new labels are all odd, these new labels do not create new edges between any pair of labels in
L^{\prime\prime}
.
As all edge labels are distinct from the vertex labels in
L^{\prime\prime}
, no edge label of
L^{\prime\prime}
is adjacent to
b
.
Now it remains to show that all the new edge labels
b+a_{i}
are isolated.
Clearly
b
cannot be adjacent to such an edge label, as
b+b+a_{i}\geq 6\operatorname{sd}(G)+2
, which is larger than any label.
And any label
\ell\in L^{\prime\prime}
cannot be adjacent to
b+a_{i}
, as this would require
\ell+b+a_{i}=b+a_{j}
, which implies
\ell=a_{j}-a_{i}\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
, but
\ell\geq\min L^{\prime\prime}=2\operatorname{sd}(G)
, so no such
\ell
exists.
Finally,
b+a_{i}
and
b+a_{j}
cannot be adjacent, as their sum is larger than any label.

This labeling induces
G^{\prime}
, so we find
\operatorname{sd}(G^{\prime})\leq(b+4\operatorname{sd}(G)-2)-2\operatorname{sd}(G)=4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
∎

Remark 10.11
.

In particular, having our new vertex be adjacent to all original vertices yields
G^{\prime}=G+K_{1}
, and thus
\operatorname{sd}(G+K_{1})\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
The graph
G+K_{1}
is obtained from
G
by adding a vertex that is connected to all vertices in
G
, which visually adds a “cone” over
G
.
Repeatedly joining
K_{1}
to a graph
n
times, which is equivalent to
G+K_{n}
, has been called the
n
th cone over
G
, and its chip-firing dynamics have been studied by Brown, Morrow, and Zureick-Brown
[
2
]
and then by Goel and Perkinson
[
8
]
.

In the other extreme case, if we do not wish for this new vertex in
G^{\prime}
to have any edges incident to it, we can use
Theorem

10.8
instead for a better bound, namely
\operatorname{sd}(G\cup K_{1})\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-4
.

Theorem

10.6
had four intervals containing labels, which in increasing order were the vertex labels for
G_{1}
, the edge labels for
G_{1}
, the vertex labels for
G_{2}
, and the edge labels for
G_{2}
.
Similarly,

10.10
also uses four intervals in its translation-based argument, though these intervals were slightly different as they were able to overlap by having different parities: the vertex labels for

, and the edge labels for the join.

, though this would have yielded a worse bound.
It stands to reason that the join between two arbitrary graphs can be constructed using five intervals containing labels, in the following increasing order: the vertex labels for
G_{1}

G_{1}

G_{2}
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}+G_{2})\leq 8\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})+11\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})-5.

We use the same notation as in

Similar reasoning as before yields

From our previous argument in

\min L_{2}^{\prime}\geq 2\max L_{1}^{\prime}

L_{2}^{\prime}
would not induce additional edges between labels in

.
Thus we apply the translation construction in

10.4

x=6\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+4\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-2-\min L_{2}

L_{2}^{\prime}
that in isolation induces

.
Similar to

and

, define
V_{2}
and

.
We find

V_{2}\subseteq[6\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+4\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-2,6\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+5\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-3]

and

E_{2}\subseteq[12\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+8\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-3,12\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+9\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-5].

By the same reasoning as in
Theorem

10.6
, we find this labeling
L_{1}^{\prime}\cup L_{2}^{\prime}
induces
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
.

We now add in all the edges between a vertex of
G_{1}
and a vertex in
G_{2}
, i.e., all
a_{i}+b_{j}
where
a_{i}\in V_{1}
and
b_{j}\in V_{2}
.
Let the set of these labels be denoted
E_{12}
.
It suffices to simply add the entire interval

[\min V_{1}+\min V_{2},\max V_{1}+\max V_{2}]=[7\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+5\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-2,8\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+6\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-4]\supseteq E_{12}.

We find that these sets of labels, in increasing order, are
V_{1},E_{1},V_{2},E_{12}
, and
E_{2}
.

We now confirm this does not add any unwanted edges, i.e., edges not between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.
Suppose
E_{12}
caused an additional edge not incident to a label in
E_{12}
.
As
\min E_{12}\geq 2\max E_{1}
, the two labels cannot both be from
V_{1}\cup E_{1}=L_{1}^{\prime}
.
Thus at least one of the labels must be from
V_{2}
.
We easily see that the labels cannot both be from
V_{2}
as
2\min V_{2}+1>\max E_{12}
, and any edge between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
was already wanted, so it remains to check sums between
E_{1}
and
V_{2}
.
But

\min E_{1}+\min V_{2}\geq 8\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+6\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-1>8\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+6\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-4\geq\max E_{12},

so no such unwanted edges are induced.
Finally, suppose
E_{12}
caused an additional edge incident to a label in
E_{12}
.
The labels cannot both be from
E_{12}
as
2\min E_{12}+1>\max E_{2}
.
If the sum of the labels of this edge was in
E_{12}
, as the range of the interval containing
E_{12}
is
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-2
, the other label incident to this edge must have value at most
\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-2<\min V_{1}
, but no such label exists.
Otherwise, the sum of the labels of this edge must be in
E_{2}
, but the minimum distance between an element of
E_{2}
and an element of
E_{12}
is at least
4\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+2\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})+1
, so the label must be at least this value, which excludes
V_{1}
and
E_{1}
from consideration.
The only remaining possibility is a label in
V_{2}
plus a label in
E_{12}
equals a label in
E_{2}
.
But

\min V_{2}+\min E_{12}\geq 13\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+9\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-4>12\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+9\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-5\geq\max E_{2},

so this is not possible.

Hence, the addition of
E_{12}
only induces edges between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, and it induces all such edges, as desired.
Our labeling
L
thus induces
G_{1}+G_{2}
, so

\operatorname{sd}(G_{1}+G_{2})\leq\operatorname{range}(L)\leq 11\operatorname{sd}(G_{1})+8\operatorname{sd}(G_{2})-5,

proving the result.
∎

Remark 10.13
.

The upper bound from

K_{n}+K_{m}=K_{n+m}

11.
Sum-diameter under vertex and edge operations

In
Section

10
we looked at the sum-diameter under various binary graph operations, including the disjoint union and graph join.
In this section we study the sum-diameter under removing or adding a vertex or edge, as well as contracting an edge.

In
Theorem

10.8
and
Theorem

10.10
we already bounded the sum-diameter under adding a vertex with any arbitrary set of incident edges.
We now study the reverse direction, removing a vertex
v
along with its incident edges from
G
, which we denote
G-v
.

Proposition 11.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
v
be a vertex of
G
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G-v)\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2.

Proof.

From
Lemma

10.5
, we find that the optimal range of a labeling of
G
for which no label is simultaneously both a vertex label and an edge label is at most
2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
.
For such a labeling, removing the vertex label associated with
v
thus removes
v
along with its incident edges from
G
, but as this label is not an edge label, does not change the induced sum graph otherwise.
Hence, a labeling
L
with
\operatorname{range}(L)\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
exists which induces
G-v
, so
\operatorname{sd}(G-v)\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2
, as desired.
∎

In fact, by using the same argument but removing multiple vertices, we have the following result on induced subgraphs of
G
.
Recall that for a subset
U\subseteq V
of the vertex set
V
of a graph
G
, the induced subgraph
G[U]
is the subgraph of
G
consisting of
U
and all of the edges connecting pairs of vertices in
U
.

Proposition 11.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
U\subseteq V
be a subset of the vertex set
V
of
G
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G[U])\leq 2\operatorname{sd}(G)-2.

We present the following stronger statement as an open question.

Question 11.3
.

For any induced subgraph
G[U]
of
G
, is
\operatorname{sd}(G[U])\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)
?

Intuitively, the answer to this question seems to be yes: deleting vertices should make the graph simpler, so a labeling with smaller range would be expected.
However, difficulty arises due to the possibility that some of the deleted vertex labels could simultaneously be edge labels, so that removing them would also remove an edge of the induced subgraph.
We observe that
11.3
holds for complete graphs, and would hold for cycles assuming cycles
\operatorname{sd}(C_{n})=2n-1
, i.e., being sharp on the upper bound of
Proposition

9.1
, due to the upper bound on
\operatorname{sd}(P_{n})
from
Proposition

9.3
.

Now we look at deleting or contracting an edge.
For some edge
e=(u,v)
in
G
, let
G\setminus e
denote edge deletion, i.e., the graph
G
with
e
removed, and let
G/e
denote edge contraction, the graph obtained from
G
by removing
e
and then identifying
u
and
v
together.

Proposition 11.4
.

Suppose
G
is a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
e=(u,v)
be an edge of
G
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G\setminus e)\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G/e)\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1.

Proof.

We first address the
\operatorname{sd}(G\setminus e)
bound.
Intuitively, our approach will be remove
v
and then re-insert it with
e
missing.
Suppose
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
are the vertices other than
u
to which
v
is adjacent, and let their corresponding vertex labels be
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
.
We start with the construction as in
Theorem

10.10
, where we add a new vertex
v^{\prime}
with label
b=2\operatorname{sd}(G)+1
that is adjacent to vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
by adding edge labels
b+a_{1},\dots,b+a_{k}
.
This gives a new labeling that has range at most
4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.
This construction initially translated the labels so that the vertex labels and edge labels are separated, and thus by the same reasoning as
Proposition

11.1
, removing the label associated with vertex
v
simply removes
v
from the induced sum graph, as this label is not an edge label so it does not remove any edges not incident to
v
.
Removing this label cannot increase the range, and we find the net effect on the induced sum graph is that we replaced
v
with
v^{\prime}
, where
v^{\prime}
is adjacent to the same vertices except
u
, so our induced sum graph is
G\setminus e
, and thus
\operatorname{sd}(G\setminus e)\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1
.

For the
\operatorname{sd}(G/e)
bound, the proof is essentially identical: we remove both vertices
u
and
v
, replacing them by a vertex with the desired edges resulting from the edge contraction.
∎

Similarly, we look at adding an edge.
For two vertices
u,v
in
G
that are not adjacent, the graph obtained from
G
by adding edge
e=(u,v)
will be denoted
G+e
.

Proposition 11.5
.

Suppose
G
is a graph with no isolated vertices, and let
e=(u,v)
be an edge between two vertices of
G
that is not present in
G
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G+e)\leq 4\operatorname{sd}(G)-1.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of
Proposition

11.4
, where we instead reinsert
v
with the extra edge
e
rather than deleting it.
∎

12.
The sum-diameter of hypergraphs

In this section we generalize the sum-diameter to be defined for
k
-uniform hypergraphs and study some of its basic properties.
Recall that a hypergraph is a generalization of a graph where edges can be incident to an arbitrary number of vertices, and a
k
-uniform hypergraph is a hypergraph where all edges are incident to
k
(distinct) vertices.
When
k=2
, we recover the definition of a graph, so we will study
k
-uniform hypergraphs for
k>2
.
As loops, i.e., edges that are incident to only one vertex, cannot occur in such a graph, a simple
k
-uniform hypergraph is simply a
k
-uniform hypergraph where no edges are repeated.

We first generalize the notion of a sum graph to a
k
-sum hypergraph.

Definition 12.1
.

A simple
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
is called a
k
-sum hypergraph
if there is a bijection
\ell
from the vertex set
V
to a set of positive integers
L\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that edge
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in E
exists if and only if
\ell(v_{1})+\cdots+\ell(v_{k})\in L
.
We call
L
a set of labels for the
k
-sum hypergraph
G
.
We will often not distinguish between the vertices and their respective labels.

Similarly, a simple
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
is called an
integral
k
-sum hypergraph
if such a bijection exists to a set of integers
L\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

Conversely, any set of positive integers
L
induces a
k
-sum hypergraph with vertex set
V=L
, and any set of integers
L
induces an integral
k
-sum hypergraph.
As before, the vertex with maximum label in a
k
-sum hypergraph must be isolated, so any hypergraph without isolated vertices cannot be a
k
-sum hypergraph.
This leads to the following generalization of the sum number of a graph
G
.

Definition 12.2
.

The
sum number
of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
, denoted
\sigma(G)
, is the minimum number of isolated vertices that must be added to
G
in order to yield a
k
-sum hypergraph.

Similarly, the
integral sum number
of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
, denoted
\zeta(G)
, is the minimum number of isolated vertices that must be added to
G
in order to yield an integral
k
-sum hypergraph.

Finally, we generalize the sum-diameter of a graph
G
.

Definition 12.3
.

The
sum-diameter
of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
, denoted
\operatorname{sd}(G)
, is the minimum possible value of
\operatorname{range}(L)
for a set
L
of positive integer labels, such that the induced
k
-sum hypergraph of
L
consists of the disjoint union of
G
with a nonnegative number of isolated vertices, namely at least
\sigma(G)
additional isolated vertices.

We similarly define the
integral sum-diameter
of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
G
, denoted
\operatorname{isd}(G)
.

As before, it is clear that
\zeta(G)\leq\sigma(G)
for all
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
, and thus we have the following proposition.

Proposition 12.4
.

For all
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
, we have
\operatorname{isd}(G)\leq\operatorname{sd}(G)
.

We now provide a lower bound on
\operatorname{sd}(G)
for
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
.

Theorem 12.5
.

For
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
of order
n
without any isolated vertices, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G)\geq n+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}-1.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary labeling
L
that induces
G
along with some isolated vertices and achieves
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
.
Then let
S\subset L
be the set of vertex labels of
L
, i.e., the set of labels that correspond to vertices of
G
in the induced
k
-sum hypergraph.
Sort
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
in increasing order
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
.
Notice that
a_{1}=\min L
as any labels smaller than
a_{1}
could be removed while still inducing
G
, and this would decrease
\operatorname{range}(L)
, contradicting the assumption that
\operatorname{range}(L)=\operatorname{sd}(G)
is minimized.
As no vertices of
G
are isolated,
a_{n}
must have an edge incident to it, and the existence of this edge implies an edge label of value at least

a_{n}+a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k-1}\geq ka_{1}+\frac{(k-2)(k-1)}{2}+n-1,

where this inequality uses the observation that
a_{i}\geq a_{1}+(i-1)
for all
i
.
Hence,

\operatorname{range}(L)\geq(k-1)a_{1}+\frac{(k-2)(k-1)}{2}+n-1\geq n+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}-1,

as desired.
∎

Similar to
Theorem

7.11
, we provide an upper bound on
\operatorname{sd}(G)
for
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
using the analogous generalization of Sidon sets to
k
-Sidon sets.
However, first we introduce the necessary definitions for
k
-Sidon sets.
We use the notation of O’Bryant
[
13
]
, who provides a comprehensive survey of Sidon sets.

Definition 12.6
.

A set of integers
\mathcal{A}
is a
B_{k}
set if the coefficients of
\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}z^{a}\right)^{k}
are bounded by
k!
.

Notice that a
B_{2}
set is equivalent to a Sidon set: if
a+b\neq c+d
when
\{a,b\}\neq\{c,d\}
, then each coefficient is at most 2, as we can have
a+b
and
b+a
for a given sum.
So a
B_{k}
set is a generalization of a Sidon set, i.e., a
k
-Sidon set as it is otherwise referred to.

Definition 12.7
.

Let
R_{k}(k!,n)
denote the largest cardinality of a
B_{k}
set contained in
[1,n]
.
Then
\sigma_{k}(k!)
is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(k!)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{k}(k!,n)}{\sqrt[k]{n}}.

The construction of Bose and Chowla
[
1
]
shows that
\sigma_{k}\geq 1
, so conversely there exists a
B_{k}
set of cardinality
n
contained in
[1,f(n)]
where
f(n)=O(n^{k})
.

Theorem 12.8
.

For
k
-uniform hypergraphs
G
of order
n
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sd}(G)=O(k^{3}n^{k}).

Proof.

We provide a general construction for a set of labels
L
that induces
G=(V,E)
along with
|E|
isolated vertices in a similar manner as
Theorem

7.11
.
We use the construction of a
B_{k}
set of cardinality
n
contained in
[1,O(n^{k})]
, and enumerate the elements of this set
s_{1}
through
s_{n}
in increasing order.
Clearly
S=\{k^{2}s_{i}+1\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
is also a
B_{k}
set, so we label the vertices of
G
using
S
.
For each edge
(v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{k}})\in E
, where each
v_{i_{j}}
is labeled with
k^{2}s_{i_{j}}+1
, add the label
k^{2}(s_{i_{1}}+\cdots+s_{i_{k}})+k
to
L
.
We claim
L
, which has size
|L|=|V|+|E|
, induces
G
along with
|E|
isolated vertices.

The reasoning is essentially the same as
Theorem

7.11
.
Our vertex labels are all congruent to 1 mod
k^{2}
, and the edge labels are all congruent to
k
mod
k^{2}
.
By simply considering their sums modulo
k^{2}
, the only edges that
L
can induce are between
k
vertex labels, and thus it suffices to show that
L
induces an edge between
k
distinct vertex labels if and only if these
k
vertices form an edge in
G
.
By construction, if
k
vertices form an edge in
G
, then these
k
vertex labels form an edge in the induced
k
-sum hypergraph.
For the other direction, suppose
L
induces an edge between
k
distinct vertex labels.
The sum of these
k
vertex labels is thus an edge label, and was constructed for some edge
e\in E
.
Noting that
e
is a set of
k
elements, and as all
k!
orderings of these
k
elements yield a sum that equals this edge label, by the definition of a
B_{k}
set we know no other sums can equal the value of this edge label, so
e
must correspond to our
k
vertex labels.
Essentially, the
B_{k}
set condition ensures that no two sums of
k
distinct vertex labels are the same, so adding the necessary edge label for a given edge does not induce other edges.

Thus
L
correctly induces
G
along with
|E|
isolated vertices.
We observe that
\max L=O(k^{3}n^{k})
as each edge label is the sum of
k
vertex labels, each of which are at most
O(k^{2}n^{k})
.
Hence,
\operatorname{sd}(G)\leq\operatorname{range}(L)\leq\max L=O(k^{3}n^{k})
.
∎

13.
Conclusion and open questions

There are many avenues for further research regarding sum-diameter.
One could determine or bound the sum-diameter or integral sum-diameter for other special families of graphs.
In particular, the sum-diameter for path graphs has been bounded to be either
2n-3
and
2n-2
, and supported by computer data we conjecture it is the latter for all
n\geq 7
, as stated in
9.5
.
The spum of path graphs is similarly bounded within a constant number of options, and a computer search supports our conjecture,
3.4
, that the current upper bound is tight for
\operatorname{spum}(P_{n})
.

In
11.3
, we also leave as an open question whether the sum-diameter is monotonic with respect to taking induced subgraphs.

While we naturally introduced the integral sum-diameter in addition to the sum-diameter, many of our bounding techniques for sum-diameter cannot immediately generalize to integral sum-diameter.
Thus, the basic properties of the integral sum-diameter are still open to be studied.

Lastly, we generalized and bounded the sum-diameter of
k
-uniform hypergraphs.
The generalization of a Sidon set, or a
B_{2}
set, to a
B_{k}
set allows us to upper bound the sum-diameter, but requires the
k
-uniform assumption.
It is thus an open question as to how the study of sum-diameter can be extended to hypergraphs that are not
k
-uniform.
