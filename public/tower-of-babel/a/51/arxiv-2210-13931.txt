Introduction

We study the decentralized optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf{x}})\triangleq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}({\bf{x}}),

(1)

over a connected network with
m
agents, where
f_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the local function on the
i
-th agent that has the finite-sum structure with
n
individual functions as follows

\displaystyle f_{i}({\bf{x}})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{i,j}({\bf{x}}).

(2)

We suppose each individual function
f_{i,j}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is smooth but possibly nonconvex.
This formulation includes a lot of applications in statistics
[

,
91
]
, signal processing
[

[

.
In decentralized scenario, all of agents target to collaboratively solve problem (
1
) and each of them can only communicate with its neighbors.
We focus on the complexity for achieving the approximate stationary point of the global objective at every agent.

For decentralized optimization, the limitation on the communication protocol leads to the local agents cannot access the exact global information at each round, which leads to the requirement of communication rounds to reduce the consensus error.
The gradient tracking
[
55
,
63
,
65
,

is a useful technique to approximate the average of local gradients and make the local first-order estimator accurate.
Directly extending (stochastic) gradient descent methods to decentralized setting

It is well-known that the algorithms with stochastic recursive gradient estimator

,

,

can achieve the optimal incremental first-order oracle (IFO) complexity for finding the approximate stationary point of the finite-sum nonconvex function under the mean-squared smooth assumption.

Sun et al. 2020
first combined stochastic recursive gradient estimator with gradient tracking to solve decentralized nonconvex finite-sum optimization problem by proposing Decentralized Gradient Estimation and Tracking (
D-GET

Later,
Xin et al. 2022
and

proposed

Li et al. 2022a
proposed DEcentralized STochastic REcurSive gradient methodS (

), which introduces Chebyshev acceleration
[
7
]
to achieve the tighter dependency on the spectral gap of the mixing matrix associated with the network.

Metelev et al. 2024
further considered the nonconvex problem over the time-varying network.
Additionally,
Lu and De Sa 2021
,

It is worth noting that existing works

for decentralized nonconvex optimization only consider the local smoothness parameters, which may be arbitrary larger than the global ones.
Furthermore, their analysis for the computation complexity focuses on one of the overall local incremental first-order oracle (LIFO) calls and the number of computation rounds.
Noticing that in each computation round, a distributed algorithm can make partial agents access their LIFO and allow other agents skip their local computation steps
[

,
52
]
.
Therefore, the LIFO calls and the computation rounds should be addressed separately.
Recently,
Ye et al. 2023

studied the distributed optimization by considering the global smoothness dependency and the partial participated protocol, while their results only address the convex problem.

In this paper, we refine the setting of decentralized smooth
nonconvex finite-sum optimization (

We proposed a novel stochastic algorithm called DEcentralized probAbilistic Recursive gradiEnt deScenT (

Notice that the smoothness parameters

For single-machine scenario (i.e.,

We consider the black-box optimization procedure based on the local incremental first-order oracle and decentralized communication protocol as follows.

Definition 1

(
[
29
,
46
]
)
.

A local incremental first-order oracle (LIFO) algorithm over a network of
m
agents satisfies the following constraints:

•

Local memory:
Each agent
i
can store past values in a local memory
\mathcal{M}_{i,s}
at time
s>0
.
These values can be accessed and used at time
s
by running the algorithm on agent
i
. Additionally, for all
i\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s}\subset\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}\bigcup\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s},

where
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}
and
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s}
are the values come from the computation and communication respectively.

•

Local computation:
Each agent
i
can access its local first-order oracle
\{f_{i,j^{s}}({\bf{x}}),\nabla f_{i,j^{s}}({\bf{x}})\}
for given
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}
and index
j^{s}\in[n]
at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}={\rm span}\big(\{{\bf{x}},\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}):{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s-1}\}\big),

where
{\rm span}(\cdot)
is the linear span.

•

Local communication:
Each agent
i
can share its value to all or part of its neighbors at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s}={\rm span}\Bigg(\bigcup_{j\in{\rm nbr}_{i}}\mathcal{M}_{j}^{s-\tau}\Bigg),

where
{\rm nbr}_{i}
is the set consists of the indices for the neighbors of agent
i
and
\tau<s
.

•

Output value:
Each agent
i
can specify one vector in its memory as local output of the algorithm at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[m]
, we have

{\bf{x}}^{s}_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s}
.

For the general nonconvex setting, we desire to achieve an
\epsilon
-stationary point for every agent in expectation, i.e., output the local variables
{\bf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bf{x}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla f({\bf{x}}_{i})\right\|\leq\epsilon
for all
i\in[m]
, where
{\bf{x}}_{i}
comes from the memory of the
i
-th agent.
For the PL condition, we desire to achieve an
\epsilon
-suboptimal solution for every agent in expectation, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}[f({\bf{x}}_{i})-f^{*}]\leq\epsilon
for all
i\in[m]
.

4.4
The Proofs of Corollary

We prove Corollary
1

4.4.1
The Upper Bound on Communication Rounds

We upper bound the communication rounds as follows

\displaystyle\hat{K}+KT={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\ln(mn{{\bar{L}}}/(L\epsilon))}{\sqrt{\gamma}}+\frac{\ln(mn{{\bar{L}}}/L)}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\cdot\frac{L}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{L}{\sqrt{\gamma}\epsilon^{2}}\right),

where we use the settings of
{{\hat{K}}}
,
K
, and
T
in Theorem
1
.

4.4.2
The Upper Bound on LIFO Calls

In the case of
{{\bar{L}}}\leq\sqrt{mn}L
, we upper bound the number of LIFO calls as follows

\displaystyle mn+(pn+(1-p)b)T

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle mn+(pmn+b)T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(mn+\left(\frac{{{\bar{L}}}}{\sqrt{mn}L}\cdot mn+\left\lceil\frac{{{\bar{L}}}\sqrt{mn}}{L}\,\right\rceil\right)\frac{L}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(mn+\frac{\sqrt{mn}{{\bar{L}}}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),

where we use the settings of
p
,
n
, and
T
in Theorem
1
.
In the case of
{{\bar{L}}}>\sqrt{mn}L
, the number of LIFO calls can be upper bounded by
mnT={\mathcal{O}}(mn+mnL\epsilon^{-2})
.

Combining above two cases, the overall LIFO complexity of Algorithm
1
under the settings of Theorem
1
is no more than

{\mathcal{O}}\big(mn+\min\{mnL,\,\sqrt{mn}{{\bar{L}}}\}\epsilon^{-2}\big)
.

4.4.3
The Upper Bound on Computation Rounds

In the case of
{{\bar{L}}}>\sqrt{mn}L
, the agents always perform the full gradient at each iteration, which means the number of computation round is no more than

\displaystyle n+Tn={\mathcal{O}}\left(n+\frac{nL}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(72)

The remains in this subsection considers the case of
{{\bar{L}}}\leq\sqrt{mn}L
.
The main issue is the case of
\zeta^{t}=0
, leading to

\displaystyle{\bf{g}}^{t+1}_{i}={\bf{g}}^{t}_{i}+\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\xi^{t}_{i,j}}{bq}\big(\nabla f_{i,j}({\bf{x}}^{t+1}_{i})-\nabla f_{i,j}({\bf{x}}^{t}_{i})\big).

(73)

The setting

\displaystyle[\xi^{t}_{1,1},\dots,\xi^{t}_{m,n}]^{\top}\sim{\rm Multinomial}\left(b,q{\bf{1}}\right)

with

means the update (
73

b

(i_{1}^{t},j_{1}^{t}),\dots,(i_{b}^{t},j_{b}^{t})
from the set

\displaystyle\{(i,j):i\in[m],j\in[n]\}

independently and uniformly with replacement and performing the computation

\displaystyle{\bf{g}}^{t+1}_{i}={\bf{g}}^{t}_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{b}\frac{\tau_{i}^{t}(k)}{bq}\big(\nabla f_{i_{k}^{t},j_{k}^{t}}({\bf{x}}^{t+1}_{i_{k}^{t}})-\nabla f_{i_{k}^{t},j_{k}^{t}}({\bf{x}}^{t}_{i_{k}^{t}})\big)

on each node, where we define

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{t}(k)\triangleq{\mathbb{I}}(i_{k}=i)

(74)

for
i\in[m]
and
k\in[b]
to present whether the
k
-th index pair
(i_{k}^{t},j_{k}^{t})
in iteration
t
corresponds to the component function on node
i
.

Since there are
mn
individual functions in total and each node has
n
individual functions, the uniform distribution of
(i_{k}^{t},j_{k}^{t})
and their independence imply

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{t}(k)\sim\text{Bernoulli}(nq)

(75)

and
\tau_{i}^{t}(1),\dots,\tau_{i}^{t}(b)
are mutually independent.
Therefore, the procedure of Algorithm
1
means the number of LIFO calls on node
i
at the
t
-th iteration is no more than

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{b}\tau_{i}^{t}(k).

We denote

\displaystyle Y_{i}^{t}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{b}\tau_{i}^{t}(k),

(76)

which holds that
Y_{i}^{t}\sim\text{Binomial}(b,nq)
because of the distribution (
75
).

To analyze the expected computation rounds in the
t
-th iteration, we only need to upper bound the quantity

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[2\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right].

(77)

In a recent work,
Liu et al. 2024
analyzed the computation rounds of Katyusha-type methods
[
3
,
64
,
34
,
71
]
in decentralized convex optimization by establishing the upper bound of the quantity in the form of (
77
) with the mutually independent binomial variables
Y_{1}^{t},\dots,Y_{m}^{t}
.
Although the random variables
Y_{1}^{t},\dots,Y_{m}^{t}
are not mutually independent in our setting, we can also follow the analysis of
Liu et al. 2024
to bound the quantity (
77
).
For the completeness, we present the details as follows.

Lemma 8

(Theorem 4.1 of
Motwani and Raghavan 1995
)
.

Suppose that the random variables
X_{1},...,X_{b}
are independent and each
X_{k}
is distributed to
{\rm Bernoulli}(p_{k})
for some
p_{k}\in[0,1]
.
We let
Z=\sum_{k=1}^{b}X_{k}
and
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[Z]
.
Then for any
\delta>0
, it holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq(1+\delta)\nu\right)\leq\left(\frac{\exp(\delta)}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\nu}.

(78)

Based on Lemma
8
, we further achieve the following upper bound for the sum of Bernoulli variables with high probability.

Lemma 9
.

Under the settings and notations of Lemma
8
,
For any constant
a\geq\exp(2)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq 2{\rm e}\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[Z],(\ln a)^{2}\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{a^{2}}.

(79)

Proof.

We denote
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[Z]=\sum_{k=1}^{b}p_{k}
.
We consider the upper bounds for different cases of
\nu
as follows:

(a)

In the case of
\nu\geq\ln a
, we apply Lemma
8
with
\delta=2{\rm e}-1
to achieve

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Z\geq 2{\rm e}\nu)\overset{(\ref {eq:lem_chernoff_bound})}{\leq}\left(\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}-1)}{(2{\rm e})^{2{\rm e}}}\right)^{\nu}=\left(\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}-1)}{2^{2{\rm e}}\exp(2{\rm e})}\right)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2^{2{\rm e}}\rm e}\right)^{\nu}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2^{2{\rm e}}\rm e}\right)^{\ln a}\leq\frac{1}{a^{2}},

where the last step can be verified by taking logarithm on both sides and the fact

\displaystyle 2\leq\ln\left(2^{2{\rm e}}\rm e\right)=1+2{\rm e}\ln 2.

(b)

If
1\leq\nu\leq\ln a
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq 2{\rm e}(\ln a)^{2}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq(2{\rm e}\ln a)\nu\right).

According to Lemma
8
with
\delta=2{\rm e}\ln a-1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq(2{\rm e}\ln a)\nu\right)\overset{(\ref {eq:lem_chernoff_bound})}{\leq}\left(\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a-1)}{(2{\rm e}\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}}\right)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a-1)}{(2\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a)}\right)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{(2\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}{\rm e}}\right)^{\nu}\leq\left(\frac{1}{4^{2{\rm e}\ln a}{\rm e}}\right)^{\nu}\leq\frac{1}{a^{2}},

where the second inequality is because
a\geq{\rm e}^{2}
and the last inequality holds as
\nu\geq 1
.

(c)

If
\nu<1
, we let
X_{1}^{\prime}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(p_{1}^{\prime})
with
p_{1}^{\prime}=1-\nu+{\mathbb{E}}[X_{1}]
which is independent with
X_{1},\dots,X_{b}
, and denote
Z^{\prime}=\sum_{j=2}^{b}X_{j}+X_{1}^{\prime}
. It is clear that for any
c\geq 0
, it holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Z\geq c)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Z^{\prime}\geq c).

(80)

We also can verify that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{\prime}]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{b}X_{j}+X_{1}^{\prime}\right]=\sum_{j=2}^{b}p_{j}+1-\nu+p_{1}=1.

(81)

Therefore, applying Lemma
8
on random variable
Z^{\prime}
with
\delta=2{\rm e}\ln a-1
leads to that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq(2{\rm e}\ln a)\right)

\displaystyle\,\,=\,\,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq(2{\rm e}\ln a)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z^{\prime}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref {eq:Z-Z'})}{\leq}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z^{\prime}\geq(2{\rm e}\ln a)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z^{\prime}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref {eq:lem_chernoff_bound})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a-1)}{(2{\rm e}\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}}\right)^{{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{\prime}]}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref {eq:Z'-1})}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a-1)}{(2{\rm e}\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}}

\displaystyle\,\,=\,\,

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a-1)}{(2\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}\exp(2{\rm e}\ln a)}

\displaystyle\,\,=\,\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\ln a)^{2{\rm e}\ln a}{\rm e}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{2{\rm e}\ln a}{\rm e}}\leq\frac{1}{a^{2}},

where the second last inequality is because
a\geq{\rm e}^{2}
.

Combining all three cases, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq 2{\rm e}\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[Z],(\ln a)^{2}\}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(Z\geq\max\{2{\rm e}\nu,2{\rm e}(\ln a)^{2}\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{a^{2}}

for all
a\geq{\rm e}^{2}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Recall that it always holds
Y_{i}^{t}\leq b
for all
i\in[m]
, then we can apply Lemma
9
to upper bound the expectation of
\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}
.

Lemma 10
.

Following the definitions in equations (
74
) and (
76
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right]\leq 4{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}+2.

(82)

Proof.

Notice that the definition of
Y_{i}^{t}
in equation (
76
) means
it is a summation of Bernoulli random variables.
Thus, we can apply Lemma
9
with
a=mb\exp(2)
and
Z=Y_{i}^{t}
for all
i\in[m]
to achieve

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{i}^{t}],(\ln a)^{2}\right\}\right)\\
=&{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)\overset{(\ref {eq:lem_binomial_bound})}{\leq}\frac{1}{m^{2}b^{2}\exp(4)},\end{split}

(83)

where the first step is based on the fact
{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{i}^{t}]=bnq
.

Then we apply Boole’s inequality to achieve

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)\\
\,\,=\,\,&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\text{exists}~i\in[m]~\text{such that}~Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)\\
\,\,\leq\,\,&\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)\\
\overset{(\ref {eq:prob-Y})}{\leq}&\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m^{2}b^{2}\exp(4)}=\frac{1}{mb^{2}\exp(4)}.\end{split}

(84)

Since we have

, the conditional expectation formula implies

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right]

\displaystyle\,\,=\,\,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}<2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\,\Big|\,\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}<2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\,\Big|\,\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\geq 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref {eq:proof_lem_computation_per_iter})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{mb^{2}\exp(4)}\right)\cdot 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}+\frac{1}{mb^{2}\exp(4)}\cdot b

\displaystyle\,\,\leq\,\,

\displaystyle 2{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}+1

Hence, we have

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right]\leq 4{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}+2={\mathcal{O}}\left(bnq+(\ln mb)^{2}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

In the case of
\zeta^{t}=0
, Lemma
10
means the expected number of computation rounds is no more than

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right]\leq 4{\rm e}\max\left\{bnq,(2+\ln mb)^{2}\right\}+2={\mathcal{O}}\left(bnq+(\ln mb)^{2}\right)

In the case of
\zeta^{t}=1
, it is obviously that the number of computation rounds is
n
.
Therefore, the overall expected number of computation rounds for Algorithm
1
in the case of
{{\bar{L}}}\leq\sqrt{mn}L
is no more than

\displaystyle n+\left((1-p){\mathbb{E}}\left[2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{t}\right]+pn\right)T

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+(pn+(1-p)(bnq+(\ln mb)^{2}))T\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+(pn+bnq+(\ln mb)^{2})T\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+\left(\frac{n{{\bar{L}}}}{\sqrt{mn}L}+\frac{{{\bar{L}}}\sqrt{mn}\,n}{mnL}+\left(\ln\frac{m{{\bar{L}}}\sqrt{mn}}{L}\right)^{2}\right)L\epsilon^{-2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\,{{\bar{L}}}+L\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right),

where we use the parameter settings of
b
,
p
, and
T
in Theorem
1
.
Combining the upper bound (
72
) in the case of
{{\bar{L}}}\leq\sqrt{mn}L

\displaystyle\min\left\{{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+nL\epsilon^{-2}\right),\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\,{{\bar{L}}}+L\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n+\left(L+\min\left\{nL,\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\,\bar{L}\right\}\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right).

Hence, we finish the proof of Corollary

5.1
The Lower Bound on Communication Rounds

The communication complexity of the decentralized optimization depends on the topology of the network.
We let
{\mathcal{G}}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
be the graph associated with the network in the problem, where the node set
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,\dots,m\}
corresponds to the
m
agents and the edge set
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,j):\text{agents $i$ and $j$ are connected}\}
describes the connectivity of the agents.
We use use the notation
{\rm dia}({\mathcal{G}})
to present the diameter of graph
{\mathcal{G}}
.

We introduce the ring-lattice graph and its properties as follows.

Definition 2

(Ring-Lattice Graph
[
79
]
)
.

Given any positive integers
m,k
such that
k
is even and
2\leq k<m-1
, the
k
-regular ring-lattice graph over nodes
\{1,\cdots,m\}
, denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{m,k}
, is an undirected graph in which each node
i\in[m]
is connected with the set of nodes
\{(i+\ell)\;\mathrm{mod}\;m:\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}},1\leq|\ell|\leq k/2\}
where
(i+\ell)\;\mathrm{mod}\;m
is defined as

\displaystyle(i+\ell)\;\mathrm{mod}\;m=\begin{cases}i+\ell&\text{if $1\leq i+\ell\leq m$};\\
i+\ell+m&\text{if $i+\ell<1$};\\
i+\ell-m&\text{if $i+\ell>m$}.\\
\end{cases}

Additionally, we use notation
{\bf L}_{m,k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
to present the Laplacian matrix of the ring-lattice graph
\mathcal{R}_{m,k}
and
\lambda_{m-1}({\bf L}_{m,k})
to present its second smallest eigenvalue.

Lemma 16

(
[
85
, Lemma 1]
)
.

For any two nodes
i,j\in[m]
in the ring-lattice graph
{\mathcal{R}}_{m,k}
, the distance between nodes
i
and
j
is

\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{2\min\{j-i,i+m-j\}}{k}\right\rceil.

In particularly, the diameter of
\mathcal{R}_{m,k}
is
\lceil{2\lfloor m/2\rfloor}/{k}\rceil=\Theta({m}/{k})
.

Recently,
Yuan et al. 2022a
showed that for all fixed
m\geq 2
and
\gamma\in[1-\cos(\pi/m),1]
, we can always establish a mixing matrix associated with some ring-lattice graph that has the spectral gap
\gamma
.

Lemma 17

(
[
85
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For any
m\geq 2
and
\gamma\in[1-\cos({\pi}/{m}),1]
, there exists a ring-lattice graph
\mathcal{G}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
with an associated mixing matrix
{\bf W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
such that
\lvert{\mathcal{V}}\rvert=m
,
1-\lambda_{2}({\bf W})=\gamma
, and
{\rm dia({\mathcal{G}})}=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\gamma})
.

Based on above results, we can construct the graph whose nodes contains two disjoint subsets with the specific distance.

Lemma 18
.

For any
m\geq 2
and
\gamma\in[1-\cos(\pi/m),1]
, there exists a ring-lattice graph
\mathcal{G}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
with associated mixing matrix
{\bf W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{1},{\mathcal{V}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}

such that
\lvert{\mathcal{V}}\rvert=m
,
\lvert{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\rvert\geq m/3
,
\lvert{\mathcal{V}}_{2}\rvert\geq m/3
,

{\mathcal{V}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2}
satisfies

.
Specifically, such graph
{\mathcal{G}}
and matrix

can be constructed by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}_{m,m-1},&\gamma\in[\max\{1-\cos({\pi}/{m}),1-\cos({\pi}/{9})\},1],\\
\mathcal{R}_{m,k},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle{\bf W}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\gamma}{m}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}+(1-\gamma){\bf I},&\gamma\in[\max\{1-\cos({\pi}/{m}),1-\cos({\pi}/{9})\},1],\\[2.84544pt]
{\bf I}-\dfrac{\gamma}{\lambda_{m-1}({\bf L}_{m,k})}{\bf L}_{m,k},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle k=2\left\lceil m\sqrt{\frac{3\gamma}{\pi^{2}(1-\pi^{2}/12)}}\,\right\rceil.

Proof.

Based on Lemma
17
, there exists a ring-lattice graph
\mathcal{G}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
associated with the mixing matrix
{\bf W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}

\lvert{\mathcal{V}}\rvert=m

\lvert{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\rvert\geq m/3
and
\lvert{\mathcal{V}}_{2}\rvert\geq m/3

and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2}

\displaystyle{\rm dist}({\mathcal{V}}_{1},{\mathcal{V}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{2\min\limits_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{1},j\in{\mathcal{V}}_{2}}\{j-i,i+m-j\}}{k}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{2\min\{\lfloor m/2\rfloor+1-\lceil m/3\rceil,1+m-\lfloor m/2\rfloor-\lceil m/3\rceil\}}{k}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{2\min\{m/2-1/2+1-m/3-2/3,1+m-m/2-m/3-2/3\}}{k}\right\rceil

∎

(142)

Now show the lower bound
\Omega(\kappa\ln(1/\epsilon))
on the computation rounds. It is worth noting that it is necessary since the case of
\bar{L}/L<\sqrt{m/n}
leads to

\displaystyle\kappa>\min\{\kappa n,\bar{\kappa}\sqrt{n/m}\}.

We consider the PL function
g_{T,t}
defined in equation (

\displaystyle T=\left\lfloor\frac{\kappa}{37a}\right\rfloor,\quad t=2\left\lfloor\log_{\frac{8}{7}}\frac{2\Delta}{3\epsilon}\right\rfloor,\quad\text{where}~~\epsilon<0.01\Delta~~\text{and}~~a=19708.

(143)

According to Lemma
14
with

\displaystyle g({\bf{x}})=g_{T,t}({\bf{x}}),\qquad\alpha=\frac{\Delta}{3T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=\sqrt{\frac{3LT}{37\Delta}},

(144)

we can conclude the function

is
37\alpha\beta^{2}
-smooth and
\alpha\beta^{2}/(aT)
-PL,
\hat{g}({\bf{x}})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}\hat{g}({\bf{y}})=\alpha\left(g_{T,t}({\bf{x}})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}g_{T,t}({\bf{y}})\right)
, and it holds

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\mathbf{0})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}\hat{g}({\bf{y}})=\alpha\left(g_{T,t}(\mathbf{0})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}g_{T,t}({\bf{y}})\right)\leq 3\alpha T,

(145)

where the inequality is based on Lemma

(d).
We construct the hard instance as

\displaystyle f_{i,j}({\bf{x}})=\hat{g}({\bf{x}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f({\bf{x}})=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{i,j}({\bf{x}})

such that

37\alpha\beta^{2}
-mean-squared smooth and

37\alpha\beta^{2}
-smooth with

.

, we have

∎

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the complexity of decentralized smooth nonconvex finite-sum optimization by considering both the smoothness of the global objective and the mean-squared smoothness of all individual functions.

10
The Finite-Sum Optimization with Convex Individual Functions

Recall that Remark
6
says that the IFO complexity of vanilla gradient descent may be sharper than the stochastic recursive gradient method since the ratio
{{\bar{L}}}/L
can be arbitrary large.
However, the similar result does not hold in the convex case.
We consider the finite-sum optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{\rm c}({\bf{x}})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{\rm c}_{i}({\bf{x}}),

(156)

where
f^{\rm c}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, and each
f^{\rm c}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
L_{\max}
-smooth and convex.
It is well-known that accelerated gradient descent (AGD)
[
58
]
can find an
\epsilon
-suboptimal solution of problem (
156
) with
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{L/\mu}\ln(1/\epsilon))
exact gradient calls, corresponding to the IFO complexity of

{\mathcal{O}}(n\sqrt{L/\mu}\ln(1/\epsilon))
.
On the other hand, the stochastic variance reduced gradient methods (e.g., Katyusha
[
3
]
) can achieve the IFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}((n+\sqrt{nL_{\max}/\mu})\ln(1/\epsilon))
, which is always better or equal to the result of AGD since the ratio
L_{\max}/L
(for the tight
L_{\max}
and
L
) is no larger than
n
in the convex setting.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose the differentiable functions
f_{1}^{\rm c},\dots,f_{n}^{\rm c}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are convex, and the function
f^{\rm c}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}^{\rm c}
is
L
-smooth, then each
f_{i}^{\rm c}
is
nL
-smooth.

Proof.

For all
{\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the convexity of the individual function means

\displaystyle f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{y}})-f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{y}})-\langle\nabla f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}}-{\bf{x}}\rangle\geq 0

(157)

for all
j\in[n]
. Combining the smoothness of
f^{\rm c}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}^{\rm c}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq f^{\rm c}({\bf{y}})-f^{\rm c}({\bf{y}})-\langle\nabla f^{\rm c}({\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}}-{\bf{x}}\rangle\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\|^{2}.

(158)

Then we have

\displaystyle f^{\rm c}_{i}({\bf{y}})-f^{\rm c}_{i}({\bf{y}})-\langle\nabla f^{\rm c}_{i}({\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}}-{\bf{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle\,\,\overset{(\ref {eq:cvx-1})}{\leq}\,\,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{y}})-f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{y}})-\langle\nabla f^{\rm c}_{j}({\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}}-{\bf{x}}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,=\,\,\,\,

\displaystyle n\big(f^{\rm c}({\bf{y}})-f^{\rm c}({\bf{y}})-\langle\nabla f^{\rm c}({\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}}-{\bf{x}}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref {eq:cvx-2})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\frac{nL}{2}\left\|{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\|^{2},

for all
i\in[n]
and
{\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

The IFO Algorithm

We consider solving the finite-sum optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf{x}})\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}({\bf{x}})

where each
f_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is differentiable.
We formally present the definition of the incremental first-order oracle (IFO) algorithm as follows

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
.

Definition 3
.

An IFO algorithm for given initial point
{\bf{x}}^{0}
is defined as a measurable mapping from functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to an infinite sequence of point and index
pairs
\{({\bf{x}}^{t},i_{t})\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
with random variable
i_{t}\in[N]
, which satisfies

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}^{t}\in\mathrm{span}\big(\{{\bf{x}}^{0},\ldots,{\bf{x}}^{t-1},\nabla f_{i_{0}}({\bf{x}}^{0}),\ldots,\nabla f_{i_{t-1}}({\bf{x}}^{t-1})\}\big),

where
\mathrm{span}(\cdot)
denotes the linear span and
i_{t}
denotes the index of individual function chosen at the
t
-th step.
