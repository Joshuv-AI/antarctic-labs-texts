Solving the linear transport equation by a deep neural network approach Journal: Journal of Discrete and Continuous Dynamical System – S
Zheng Chen, Liu Liu, Lin Mu

Abstract
In this paper, we study the linear transport model by adopting the deep learning method , in particular the deep neural network (DNN) approach. While the interest of using DNN to study partial differential equations is arising, here we adapt it to study kinetic models, in particular the linear transport model. Moreover, theoretical analysis on the convergence of the neural network and its approximated solution towards the analytic solution is shown. We demonstrate the accuracy and effectiveness of the proposed DNN method in the numerical experiments.

Introduction

The deep learning method is a new approach for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) that can resolve some difficulties appeared in traditional numerical methods
[
50
]
,
such as the expensive computational cost for high-dimensional problems, the challenges dealing with complex boundary conditions, truncation of the velocity domain (in some models such as the Boltzmann equation
[
7
]
, the velocity lies in the whole space thus a truncation in numerical discretization is necessary and one needs to study decaying properties of the solution). One of the deep learning approaches that worth mentioning is the physical informed neural networks (PINN)
[
51
,
54
,
56
]
. This method incorporates the easy-to-use auto-differentiation in the current software and the physics information of the PDEs under study into the network, by constructing total loss functions that involves the PDE residuals and other constraints such as the initial and boundary conditions.

Besides, the deep learning algorithm, as a mesh-free method, has the advantage of being intuitive and easy to be executed. For example, instead of designing mass (or momentum and energy) conservative schemes for kinetic models, which can be challenging for traditional numerical methods and may need extra efforts
[
23
]
, one can simply involve the derivative in time of conserved quantities of interests in the total loss function, as was done in
[
31
]
for the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation.
However, we mention that there exist indeed some weaknesses of the deep learning approach.
First, there is no guarantee that the deep learning algorithms will converge and it is theoretically difficult to show their convergence in practice. It is also hard to evaluate the accuracy of the deep neural network (DNN) approach in contrast with conventional numerical methods such as finite volume or finite element methods.

We mention some other advantages of using the DNN approach to solve our kinetic problems, in particular the linear transport model:
a) to obtain the distribution function at any given time
t
, position
x
, velocity
\Omega
, instead of only the discrete values at uniform mesh in traditional finite difference numerical methods; as a mesh-free method, works efficiently for high-dimensional physical space problems;
b) to avoid high computational cost on the simulation of kinetic equations due to the spacial and velocity variables, and the integral-based, nonlocal collision operators.

2
The transport equation

In this paper, we consider a single-group, linear transport equation of the form

\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+\sigma_{t}\psi=\sigma_{s}\mathcal{S}\psi+\mathcal{Q},

(2.1)

where
x\in\mathrm{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is position,
\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is direction of flight,
t>0
is time. The particle speed
c
is a fixed scalar and
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}(x)
is a source. The material is characterised by the absorption, scattering, and total cross-sections, denoted as
\sigma_{a}
,
\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{a}+\sigma_{s}
, respectively.

For each fixed
x
, the scattering operator
\mathcal{S}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a bounded linear operator, and for any
f(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
,

\left(\mathcal{S}f\right)(x,\Omega)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\,g(x,\Omega\cdot\Omega^{\prime})f(x,\Omega^{\prime})\,d\Omega^{\prime},

(2.2)

where
g
is the scattering angular redistribution function and is normalized to one, i.e. for any
x\in\mathrm{X}
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\,g(x,\Omega\cdot\Omega^{\prime})\,d\Omega^{\prime}=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\,g(x,\mu)\,d\mu=1.

(2.3)

3
The Neural Network approach

We review the deep neural network (DNN) structure and approach for general PDEs. Please see Figure
1
below.
Denote the approximated solution to the linear transport model (
2.1
) by

\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)
and suppose the neutral network has
L
layers; the input layer takes
(t,x,\Omega)
and the final layer gives
\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)
as the output. The relation between the
l
-th and
(l+1)
-th layer (
l=1,2,\cdots L-1)
is
given by

\theta_{j}^{(l+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{l}}w_{ji}^{(l+1)}\bar{\sigma}_{l}(\theta_{i}^{l})+b_{j}^{(l+1)},

(3.1)

where
m=\left(m_{0},m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{L-1}\right)
,
w=\left\{w_{ji}^{(k)}\right\}_{i,j,k=1}^{m_{k-1},m_{k},L}
and
b=\left\{b_{j}^{(k)}\right\}_{j=1,k=1}^{m_{k},L}
. More specifically,

1.

\theta_{i}^{l}
: the
i
-th neuron in the
l
-th layer

2.

\bar{\sigma}_{l}
: the activation function in the
l
-th layer

3.

w_{ji}^{(l+1)}
: the weight between the
i
-th neuron in the
l
-th layer and the
j
-th neuron in the
(l+1)
-th layer

4.

b_{j}^{(l+1)}
: the bias of the
j
-th neuron in the
(l+1)
-th layer

5.

m_{l}
: the number of neurons in the
l
-th layer.

Structure of the deep neural network.

3.1
Definition of loss functions

Recall the linear transport equation (
2.1
), for simplicity, we let
c=1
,
\mathcal{Q}=0
and denote the operator

\mathcal{L}(\psi)=\sigma_{s}\mathcal{S}\psi-\sigma_{t}\psi
.
By (
2.1
), we consider the linear transport equation given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi=\mathcal{L}(\psi),\\[4.0pt]
&\psi(t=0,x,\Omega)=\psi_{0}(x,\Omega).\end{cases}

(3.2)

The loss function for the governing equation (
3.2
) is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\displaystyle Loss_{\text{GE}}&\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathrm{X}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Big|\partial_{t}\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)\\[4.0pt]
&\displaystyle\quad-\mathcal{L}(\psi^{nn})(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)\Big|^{2}\,d\Omega\,dx\,dt\\[4.0pt]
&\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i,j,k}\Big|\partial_{t}\psi^{nn}(t_{i},x_{j},\Omega_{k};m,w,b)+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi^{nn}(t_{i},x_{j},\Omega_{k};m,w,b)\\[4.0pt]
&\displaystyle\quad-\mathcal{L}(\psi^{nn})(t_{i},x_{j},\Omega_{k};m,w,b)\Big|^{2}\omega_{i,j,k},\end{split}

(3.3)

where
\{(t_{i},x_{j},\Omega_{k})\}
are the discretization points used to approximate the integral over the whole domain, with
\omega_{i,j,k}
the associated weight for point
(t_{i},x_{j},\Omega_{k})
. For uniform points, it is simply given by

\omega_{i,j,k}=\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}N_{k}},

(3.4)

where
N_{i},N_{j},N_{k}
are the numbers of discretization points used in
(t,x,\Omega)
direction respectively.
We shall introduce the discretized form

\mathcal{L}(\psi^{nn})(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)
in Section
5
later.

The loss term for the initial condition is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\displaystyle Loss_{\text{IC}}&:=\int_{\mathrm{X}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Big|\psi^{nn}(0,x,\Omega)-\psi_{0}(x,\Omega)\Big|^{2}\,d\Omega\,dx\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle&\approx\frac{1}{N_{\text{IC}}}\sum_{(x,\Omega)\in\mathbb{X}_{\text{IC}}}\Big|\psi^{nn}(0,x,\Omega)-\psi_{0}(x,\Omega)\Big|^{2},\end{split}

(3.5)

where
\mathbb{X}_{\text{IC}}
denotes the set of sampling points in
\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
used to approximate the initial data with total number
N_{\text{IC}}
.

Define
n_{x}
the
unit
outward normal vector on the boundary
\partial\mathrm{X}
, let
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ def }}}{{=}}\partial\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
This phase boundary can be split into an outgoing boundary
\gamma_{+}
, incoming boundary
\gamma_{-}
and a singular boundary
\gamma_{0}
, defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}:=\left\{(x,\Omega)\in\partial\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}:n_{x}\cdot\Omega>0\right\},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}:=\left\{(x,\Omega)\in\partial\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}:n_{x}\cdot\Omega<0\right\},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}:=\left\{(x,\Omega)\in\partial\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}:n_{x}\cdot\Omega=0\right\}.

(3.8)

We assume the inflow boundary condition

\psi(t,x,\Omega)|_{\gamma^{-}}=h(t,x,\Omega)\quad\text{ for }x\in\partial\mathrm{X},

(3.9)

with the loss term defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\displaystyle Loss_{\text{BC}}&:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\Big|\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)-h(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)\Big|^{2}\,ds\,dt\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle&\approx\frac{1}{N_{\text{BC}}}\sum_{(t,x,\Omega)\in\mathbb{X}_{\text{BC}}}\Big|\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)-h(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)\Big|^{2},\end{split}

(3.10)

where
\mathbb{X}_{\text{BC}}
denotes the set of sampling points in
[0,T]\times\gamma_{-}
used to approximate the inflow boundary data, with total number
N_{\text{BC}}
.
Similarly, for the specular reflection boundary condition given by

\psi(t,x,\Omega)=\psi(t,x,\mathcal{R}\Omega)\quad\text{ for }(t,x,\Omega)\in\gamma,

(3.11)

where
\mathcal{R}\Omega=\Omega-2(n_{x}\cdot\Omega)n_{x}
, one can define the corresponding loss term

\displaystyle\begin{split}\displaystyle\widetilde{Loss}_{\text{BC}}&:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}\Big|\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)-\psi^{nn}(t,x,\mathcal{R}\Omega;m,w,b)\Big|^{2}\,ds\,dt\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle&\approx\frac{1}{\tilde{N}_{\text{BC}}}\sum_{(t,x,\Omega)\in\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{\text{BC}}}\Big|\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m,w,b)-\psi^{nn}(t,x,\mathcal{R}\Omega;m,w,b)\Big|^{2},\end{split}

(3.12)

where
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{\text{BC}}
denotes the set of sampling points in
[0,T]\times\gamma
, with total number
\tilde{N}_{\text{BC}}
.

Adding up (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and (
3.10
) defines the total loss function as follows

{Loss}_{\text{Total}}:=Loss_{\text{GE}}+Loss_{\text{IC}}+Loss_{\text{BC}}.

(3.13)

4
Analysis main results

In this section, we show two main theoretical results. We first prove that there exists a sequence of parameters (weights, biases and number of nodes) such that the total loss function, defined earlier in (
3.13
), converges to
0
.
Consequentially, we then show that the sequence of these neural networks equipped with such parameters converge to the analytic solution of the linear transport model (
2.1
). We first review some preliminary results about the existence of the approximated neural network solution in section
4.1
.

4.1
Preliminaries

The neural network architecture was first introduced in
[
44
]
. Later in
[
14
]
, Cybenko established sufficient conditions where a continuous function can be approximated by finite linear combinations of single hidden layer neural networks, followed by
the work in
[
37
]
that extends the theory to the multi-layer network case.

In the following, we will review an important theorem on the existence of the approximated neural network solution, that will prepare us for the analysis shown later in this section.
First, we recall the Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT) in
[
14
]
which uses the two-level neural network. We adapt to discuss our model equation and use the notations introduced in Section
3
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose the solution to (
2.1
) satisfies
\psi\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathrm{X})
. Also, let the activation function
\bar{\sigma}
be any non-polynomial function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a two-layer neural network

\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}w_{1i}^{(2)}\bar{\sigma}\left(\left(w_{i1}^{(1)},w_{i2}^{(1)},w_{i3}^{(1)}\right)\cdot(t,x,\Omega)+b_{i}^{(1)}\right)+b_{1}^{(2)},

such that

\left\|\psi-\psi^{nn}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\epsilon,\quad\left\|\partial_{t}(\psi-\psi^{nn})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\epsilon,\quad\left\|\nabla_{x}(\psi-\psi^{nn})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\epsilon,

(4.1)

where the domain
K
denotes
[0,T]\times\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that the above result can be generalized to the neural network with several hidden layers
[
37
]
. For simplicity, we review the above UAT in the two-layer neural network case.

4.2
Convergence of the loss function

We first show that a sequence of neural network solutions to (
3.2
)
exists such that the total loss function converges to zero, if the analytic solution
\psi\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathrm{X})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume the solution
\psi
to (
2.1
) is sufficiently smooth in its physical domain
K=[0,T]\times\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, in the sense that
\psi\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathrm{X})
. Then there exists a sequence of neural network parameters
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that the sequence of DNN solutions with
m_{[j]}
nodes, denoted by
\{\psi_{j}(t,x,\Omega)=\psi^{nn}(t,x,\Omega;m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies

Loss_{\text{Total}}(\psi_{j})\to 0,\qquad\text{as }j\to\infty.

Proof.
Define a sequence of small numbers
\epsilon_{j}=\frac{1}{j}
.
By the UAT above, for any
\epsilon_{j}
, there exists a DNN solution

\psi_{j}(t,x,\Omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{[j],1}}w_{[j],1i}^{(2)}\bar{\sigma}\left(\left(w_{[j],i1}^{(1)},w_{[j],i2}^{(1)},w_{[j],i3}^{(1)}\right)\cdot(t,x,\Omega)+b_{[j],i}^{(1)}\right)+b_{[j],1}^{(2)}

(4.2)

such that

||\psi_{j}-\psi||_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\epsilon_{j}
and similarly for their first-order derivatives in
t
and
x
as in (
4.1
). Here the neural network parameters
m
,
w
and
b
satisfies the definition in (
3.1
), except that there is an extra subscript
[j]
, which describes the index of the sequence
of DNN solutions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
and their parameters.
Define

d_{\text{ge},j}(t,x,\Omega):=-\left[\partial_{t}+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\right]\psi_{j}+\mathcal{L}(\psi_{j}).

(4.3)

Integrate
|d_{\text{ge},j}|^{2}
over
K
, which is equivalent to

||\partial_{t}(\psi-\psi_{j})+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}(\psi-\psi_{j})-\mathcal{L}(\psi)+\mathcal{L}(\psi_{j})||^{2}_{L^{2}(K)},

(4.4)

in which the first two terms are bounded by

||\partial_{t}(\psi-\psi_{j})+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}(\psi-\psi_{j})||^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}\lesssim\epsilon_{j}^{2}\,T\,4\pi\,|\mathrm{X}|\,(1+16\pi^{2}),

(4.5)

where we used the fact that
L^{2}
norm is bounded by
L^{\infty}
norm, due to the boundedness of physical domain
K
.
The last two terms in (
4.4
), thanks to the operator
\mathcal{L}
being bounded, are bounded as follows:

||\mathcal{L}(\psi)-\mathcal{L}(\psi_{j})||^{2}_{{L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}}\lesssim||\psi-\psi_{j}||^{2}_{{L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}}\leq||\psi-\psi_{j}||^{2}_{{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}}\,4\pi\,|\mathrm{X}|<C\,\epsilon_{j}^{2}.

(4.6)

Thus the loss term
Loss_{\text{GE}}
is bounded by
O\left(\epsilon_{j}^{2}\right)
.

For the inflow boundary data,
Loss_{\text{BC}}
is bounded by

||\psi_{j}-\psi||_{L^{2}(\gamma_{T}^{-})}^{2}\leq T\,4\pi\,|\partial\mathrm{X}|\,||\psi_{j}-\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\gamma_{T}^{-})}^{2}\leq T\,4\pi\,|\partial\mathrm{X}|\,||\psi_{j}-\psi||_{L^{\infty}(K)}^{2}\leq O\left(\epsilon_{j}^{2}\right),

(4.7)

where
\gamma_{T}^{\pm}:=[0,T]\times\gamma_{\pm}
. Note that a similar result holds for the specular reflection boundary condition (
3.11
).

For the initial data, we denote the DNN approximation by
\psi_{j}(0,x,\Omega)
, then

Loss_{\text{IC}}=||\psi_{j}(0,x,\Omega)-\psi_{0}(x,\Omega)||_{L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}\leq||\psi_{j}(0,x,\Omega)-\psi_{0}(x,\Omega)||_{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}\,4\pi\,|\mathrm{X}|\leq O(\epsilon_{j}^{2}).

(4.8)

Combining all these upper bounds for loss terms in (
4.5
),(
4.6
),(
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we conclude that

Loss_{\text{Total}}(\psi_{j})\leq O\left(\frac{1}{j^{2}}\right).

(4.9)

Therefore,
Loss_{\text{Total}}(\psi_{j})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

4.3
Convergence of the DNN solution

Now we prove that with the parameters
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
equipped, the neural network in Theorem
4.3
indeed converges to the analytic solution of the linear transport model, as one desires.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence defined in Theorem
4.3
, and
\psi
is the solution to the linear transport model (
2.1
). Then,
Loss_{\text{Total}}(\psi_{j})\to 0
implies that

||\psi_{j}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})-\psi||_{L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})\right)}\to 0.

(4.10)

Recall the definition (
4.3
), since
\psi
solves the linear transport model (
3.2
), thus

\left[\partial_{t}+\Omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\right]\left(\psi-\psi_{j}\right)=d_{\text{ge},j}(t,x,\Omega)+\mathcal{L}(\psi)-\mathcal{L}(\psi_{j}).

(4.11)

Define

d_{\text{ic},j}(x,\Omega):=\psi_{0}(x,\Omega)-\psi_{j}(0,x,\Omega),\quad\text{ for }(x,\Omega)\in\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},

(4.12)

in addition to

d_{\text{bc},j}(t,x,\Omega):=h(t,x,\Omega)-\psi_{j}(t,x,\Omega)\quad\text{ for }\,(t,x,\Omega)\in\gamma^{-}_{T},

(4.13)

for inflow boundary condition (
3.9
).

For simplicity of notations, the
L^{2}
norms and the corresponding inner products below stand for in the physical space

(x,\Omega)\in\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Multiplying
(\psi-\psi_{j})
onto (
4.11
) and integrating over
\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, one gets

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\displaystyle\quad\int_{\mathrm{X}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\partial_{t}(\psi-\psi_{j})^{2}\,d\Omega dx+2\int_{\gamma^{+}}(\psi-\psi_{j})^{2}\,\Omega\cdot n_{x}\,ds-2\int_{\gamma^{-}}d_{\text{bc},j}^{2}\,|\Omega\cdot n_{x}|\,ds\\[4.0pt]
&\displaystyle=2\big\langle\mathcal{L}(\psi-\psi_{j}),\psi-\psi_{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}}+2\big\langle d_{\text{ge},j},\psi-\psi_{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}}.\end{split}

(4.14)

The first term on the RHS above is bounded by

2\big\langle\mathcal{L}(\psi-\psi_{j}),\psi-\psi_{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\|\mathcal{L}(\psi-\psi_{j})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi-\psi_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|\psi-\psi_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.15)

The fact that
\int_{\gamma^{+}}(\psi-\psi_{j})^{2}\,\Omega\cdot n_{x}\,ds\geq 0
yields

\frac{d}{dt}||\psi-\psi_{j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\underbrace{2\int_{\gamma^{-}}d_{\text{bc},j}^{2}\,|\Omega\cdot n_{x}|\,ds+||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}}_{:=H(t)}+C_{1}||\psi-\psi_{j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.16)

Due to
\int_{\gamma^{-}}d_{\text{bc},j}^{2}\,|\Omega\cdot n_{x}|\,ds\leq\int_{\gamma^{-}}d_{\text{bc},j}^{2}\,ds
and the definitions

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\gamma^{-}}\left(d_{\text{bc},j}(\tau,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\right)^{2}\,dsd\tau=Loss_{\text{BC}},\quad\int_{0}^{t}||d_{\text{ge},j}(\tau,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\tau=Loss_{\text{GE}},

(4.17)

we have, by Grönwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\displaystyle||\psi(t,\cdot,\cdot)-\psi_{j}(t,\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{1}t}Loss_{\text{IC}}+e^{C_{1}t}\int_{0}^{t}H(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim e^{C_{1}t}\left(Loss_{\text{IC}}+2Loss_{\text{BC}}+Loss_{\text{GE}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2e^{C_{1}t}\,Loss_{\text{Total}}.

(4.18)

It’s already known in (
4.9
) from Theorem
4.3
that
Loss_{\text{Total}}(\psi_{j})\leq O(\frac{1}{j^{2}})
, thus

||\psi(t,\cdot,\cdot)-\psi_{j}(t,\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{2}(\mathrm{X}\times\mathbb{S}^{2})}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\,e^{C_{1}T}\,\frac{1}{j^{2}},\quad\text{ for }t\in[0,T].

(4.19)

After taking
L^{\infty}
norm in
t\in[0,T]
, we conclude that

(4.20)

6
Conclusions and future work

This paper studies the DNN approach in solving linear transport equations. Theoretical analysis is conducted, and we show two main results:
1) the loss function will go to zero;
2) the neural network solution will converge point-wisely to the analytic solution.
Effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed method are validated in the numerical simulations.

It is noticed that in Figure
4
(a), around the left shift from void to the peak, the value of density function is negative, although the magnitude is very small, which is also commonly observed in traditional numerical methods using polynomials as approximations for near-void area. In the future, we are interested in enforcing positivity of the solution in the DNN approach to improve or fix this issue.
For future work, we shall extend this DNN approach to study high-dimensional kinetic problems with random parameters and multiple scales.
Another important and interesting topic in the rarefied gas dynamics is to study the asymptotic behaviors of particles, such as convergence towards the equilibrium state of the distribution and behaviors of macroscopic quantities. We will explore answers to these questions when using the deep learning methods.
