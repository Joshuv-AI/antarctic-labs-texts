Bounded Solutions in Incompressible Hydrodynamics
Dimitri Cobb

Abstract




Abstract

In this article, we study bounded solutions of Euler-type equations on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which have no integrability at
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
. As has been previously noted, such solutions fail to achieve uniqueness in an initial value problem, even under strong smoothness conditions. This contrasts with well-posedness results that have been obtained by using the Leray projection operator in these equations. This apparent paradox is solved by noting that using the Leray projector requires an extra condition the solutions must fulfill at
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
. Our goal is to find one such condition which is sharp. We then apply the methods we develop to prove a full uniqueness result for Besov-Lipschitz solutions, as to the theory of Serfati solutions. In the last Section, we see how these techniques also apply to the Elsässer variables used in ideal MHD.

Keywords:

Leray projection, incompressible fluids, bounded solutions, Euler equations, far field condition.

Introduction

In this article, we study some specific features of incompressible fluids evolving in unbounded domains. In order to illustrate the methods we develop, we will focus mainly on the Euler equations, which concentrates the difficulties of the problem. However, we will also show how the principles we present can also be applied to a broader class of equations by applying them to the study of Elsässer variables in ideal magnetohydrodynamics.

We are concerned with
bounded
solutions to systems of PDEs describing incompressible fluid mechanics. The simplest of these equations is the incompressible (homogeneous) Euler system, which reads

(1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla\pi=0\\
\D(u)=0.\end{cases}

This system is set on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its unknowns are the velocity field
u(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the pressure field
\pi(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
. As we will see, the issues raised in this article are very specific to the non-compact nature of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so we will not be interested by flows defined on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Our concern is the resolution of the Cauchy problem for (
1
). Most PDEs require a form of boundary condition to be well-posed, and the Euler system is no different. But when working on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which has no boundary, one must rather require conditions at infinity in the form of a far field condition on the velocity: for example, we might demand the fluid to be at rest at infinity in some inertial reference frame given by a fixed vector
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

u(t,x)\tend V\qquad\text{as }|x|\rightarrow+\infty.

The absolute necessity of such a condition is made obvious by a simple example: consider
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define a flow by

(2)

u(t,x)=f(t)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\pi(t,x)=-f^{\prime}(t)\cdot x.

Then the couple
(u,\pi)
is a solution of (
1
). But taking
f
to be any function that is compactly supported away from
t=0
, we construct an infinity of solutions that correspond to the initial data
u(0)=0
. And this lack of uniqueness cannot be dismissed as an artifact of the Galilean invariance of the equations: there is no inertial reference frame in which the fluid (
2
) is always at rest at infinity.

Note that this issue is of a different nature than the
C^{m}
or
C^{1}\cap L^{2}
ill-posedness displayed in
[
5
]
and
[
11
]
. For instance as the authors of
[
11
]
rely on singular integral operators not mapping
C^{0}\cap L^{2}
to
L^{\infty}
, while our example (
2
) hinges on a lack of far field-conditions.

The questions we ask in this chapter are the following:
What type of far field condition should a (smooth) solution of
(
1
)
satisfy to be uniquely determined by the initial data? Can such a condition be “optimal”, in the sense that any other condition has to be stronger?

Before diving into the bulk of this article, let us note that bounded solutions of infinite energy, such as (
2
), are of specific interest in fluid mechanics: in particular, the space
W^{1,\infty}
of Lipschitz functions is essentially

1.1
Role of the Pressure and Leray Projection

The analysis of this problem hinges on our understanding of the pressure force
-\nabla\pi
. An intuitive reason
\pi
has this key role can be seen from our counter-example. In (
2
), the flow is subject to a forcing: a pressure differential coming from
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
induces the motion of the fluid. And of course, any fluid that is under the influence of an arbitrary forcing cannot have its dynamics solely determined by initial data.

Now, although the pressure is formally one of the unknowns of the system, it is entirely determined (up to a constant) by the velocity: ultimately, only the velocity field really matters when solving the system. In fact, we may formally compute
\nabla\pi
as a function of
u
by taking the divergence of the first equation in (
1
) and solving the elliptic equation thus produced: we obtain

(3)

\nabla\pi=\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k}).

From the previous equation, we introduce the Leray projection operator

\mathbb{P}={\rm Id}+\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\D

which may be seen as the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector on the subspace of divergence-free functions. In other words, we may see the pressure term in
\nabla\pi
in (
1
) as an orthogonal projection enforcing the divergence-free condition
\D(u)=0
at all times. Applying
\mathbb{P}
to (
1
), we get a new equation

(4)

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)=0,

which is (up to a commutator term) a transport equation.

The importance of this new equation lies in that, unlike the original Euler system (
1
), the projected equation (
4
) is
well-posed
in a space of (regular enough) bounded functions: for example, Pak and Park prove in 2004
[
28
]
that for any Besov-Lipschitz initial datum
u_{0}\in B^{1}_{\infty,1}
, there is a unique (local) solution
u\in C^{0}([0,T[;B^{1}_{\infty,1})
for some
T>0
.

However, we see that systems (
1
) and (
4
) are not equivalent. For instance, the uniform flow
u(t,x)=f(t)
of (
2
) is a solution of (
1
) but not of (
4
). While this may seem a bit surprising, general unpleasantness must be expected when dealing simultaneously with constant functions, as in (
2
), and Leray projection: constant functions are gradients and divergence-free functions at the same time.

The difference between (
1
) and (
4
) is that the projected equations contain an implicit far field condition: the pressure force can only be expressed by Leray projection (
3
) under a (to be determined) far field condition. The obvious reason for this is the presence of the inverted Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}
, which has its range in a space of functions with no harmonic component. Because the pressure is given by a Poisson equation

Regarding our question of whether a solution

Remark 1
.

It should be noted that our problem is due to the fact that the solutions

are defined on the
non-compact

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the Euler problem set on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}

\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\{0\},\qquad\widehat{\pi}(k)=-\sum_{j,l}\frac{k_{j}k_{l}}{|k|^{2}}\widehat{u_{j}u_{l}}(k)

and a constant function (for

(in other words, the mean value of the solution is preserved). Therefore, the velocity field does indeed solve the projected equation (

\mathbb{T}^{d}
and periodic flows on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

-\nabla\pi
is periodic, although the pressure itself is not.

1.2
Previous Results

This question has been abundantly studied in the past twenty years, but mainly for the Navier-Stokes equations, although all existing results can be applied to the Euler system with suitable adaptations. We mention some of the notable advances on the topic. Throughout this paragraph,
(u,\pi)
is a solution of the Euler system (
1

The first kind of result is a condition on the pressure
\pi
for it to be given by (
3

\pi

\pi=\pi_{0}+\sum_{i,j}R_{i}R_{j}\pi_{ij},

[
15

[

extended this result: in order for (

\pi(t)\in{\rm BMO}
. Other results include those of Kukavica and Vicol
[
22
]
, who require that
|\pi(t,x)|=o(|x|)
as
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
, or Maremonti
[
25
]
with a finite moment condition
(1+|x|)^{d+1}\pi(t,x)\in L^{1}
. Finally, Nakai and Yoneda
[
27
]
introduce a condition based on Campanato-type spaces.

A second type of theorem shows that any solution
u
of the Euler system (
1
) must be given by transformation of a solution
v
of (
4
) through a “generalized Galilean transform”:

(5)

u(t,x)=v\big(t,x-G(t)\big)+g(t)\qquad\text{where }G(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(s){\rm d}s.

See for example the articles of Kukavica
[

and Kukavica and Vicol
[

\nabla(g^{\prime}(t)\cdot x)
, and interpreted as a forcing term.

Finally, a last type of result refers to the velocity field only: a far field condition based on

is sufficient to constrain the form of the pressure. For instance, in
[
9
]
, we have proven that a loose integrability condition at

is enough for (
3

[
13

(6)

\forall\,T>0,\qquad\int_{0}^{T}\int\frac{|u(t,x)|^{2}}{(1+|x|)^{d}}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t<+\infty.

Finally, we point out a last result, which is given by Lemarié-Rieusset in
[
24
]
(point
ii
of Theorem 11.1, pp. 109–111), where the flow is required to satisfy the Morrey-type condition

(7)

\forall t_{1}<t_{2},\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda}\big|u(t,y)\big|^{2}{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}t\tend_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}0.

These last conditions of
[

]

[

]
and
[

6

) or the one in
[
9
]
. For instance, these conditions fail for any smooth nonzero periodic solution of (
4

e.g.
the constant flow

Finally, we refer to

1.3
Main Result

We now present and discuss the main theorem of this chapter. In order to introduce the formal ideas behind our main result, Theorem
3
below, consider a smooth bounded solution
u
of the Euler system which has bounded derivatives. As we have explained above, the pressure field is a solution of a Poisson equation

-\Delta\pi=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k})

and the issue lies in the fact that the solutions of this elliptic problem are not unique, as they are given, in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, up to the addition of a harmonic polynomial. Therefore, in order to recover the pressure force
-\nabla\pi
, we must add to (
3
) the gradient of a harmonic polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{R}[X]
, so as to obtain

(8)

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla Q=0.

Therefore,
u
will be a solution of the projected system (
4
) if and only if
\nabla Q\equiv 0
.

A crucial element of our argument is that polynomial functions are spectrally supported at
\xi=0
. This means that to determine whether
\nabla Q
is nonzero or not, it is enough to study the
low frequency
behavior of (
8
). Consider a nonnegative and compacty supported cut-off function
\chi
with
\chi(\xi)=1
around
\xi=0
. Then, by applying the Fourier multiplication operator
\chi(\lambda D)
to (
8
) for
\lambda>0
, we get
3
3

3

We adopt the notation
D=-i\nabla
. More generally, for any function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\tend\mathbb{R}^{d}

\phi(D)

\mathcal{F}[\phi(D)f](\xi):=\phi(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)

\partial_{t}\chi(\lambda D)u+\chi(\lambda D)\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla Q=0,

and by letting

\chi(\lambda D)\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)

is a homogeneous function of order

(9)

\big\|\chi(\lambda D)\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)\big\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

Unfortunately, the Bernstein inequalities do not apply to the symbol of

(10)

\chi(\lambda D)\partial_{t}u\tend 0\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}

Definition 2
.

Let
\chi\in\mathcal{D}
be the cut-off function defined above. The space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
is the set of all tempered distributions

such that we have

\chi(\lambda\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\tend_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}0\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}.

In particular, there is no nontrivial polynomial function in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
, that is
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}\cap\mathbb{R}[X]=\{0\}
.

The convergence (
10

\partial_{t}u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}

Theorem 3
.

Let
T>0
,
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
and
u\in C^{0}([0,T[;L^{\infty})

1

\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(i)

the flow

that satisfies
u(0)-u_{0}={\rm Cst}\in\mathbb{R}

(ii)

for all times

u(t)-u(0)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
,

(iii)

for all times
t\in[0,T[

u(t)-u(0)\in{\rm BMO}^{-1}
,

(iv)

the pressure satisfies
\pi\in C^{0}(]0,T[;{\rm BMO})

(v)

\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda}|f(y)|^{2}{\rm d}y=\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda}\left(\frac{1+|y|}{\lambda}\right)^{d}\frac{|f(y)|^{2}}{(1+|y|)^{d}}\,{\rm d}y.

Set

Therefore, by separating in our integral the

&\leq\left(\frac{1+|x|+R}{\lambda}\right)^{d}\|g\|_{L^{1}}+\epsilon\left(1+\frac{1+r}{\lambda}\right)^{d}.\end{split}

L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
convergence. Finally, we only have to show that the second condition implies
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
. Consider
\psi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}
as in the proof of Lemma
7
above and let
\epsilon>0
. We fix a
R>0
such that
\|\mathds{1}_{|y|\geq R}\psi(y)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\epsilon
. We have

|\chi(\lambda D)f(x)|\leq\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)f(y)|\,{\rm d}y\;+\int_{|x-y|\geq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)f(y)|\,{\rm d}y.

Because
\psi\in\mathcal{S}
is a Schwartz function and
f\in L^{\infty}
, the second integral is bounded by

\begin{split}\int_{|x-y|\geq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)f(y)|\,{\rm d}y&\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{|x-y|\geq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)|\,{\rm d}y\\
&=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathds{1}_{|y|\geq R}\psi(y)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\epsilon\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{split}

On the other hand, we may apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the first integral, so as to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)f(y)|\,{\rm d}y&\leq\left(\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|f(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|\psi_{\lambda}(x-y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|f(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}\left(\int\left|\psi\left(\frac{x-y}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}y}{\lambda^{d}}\right)^{1/2}\\
&=\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\int_{|x-y|\leq\lambda R}|f(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Since, by assumption, this quantity converges uniformly locally to zero, it also does so in the
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
topology.
∎

Remark 9
.

In fact, the results of Lemma
8
hold when
f
is in the Morrey space of uniformly locally
L^{2}
functions (see Definition 11.4 p. 108 in
[
24
]
) with pretty much the same proofs. These are the
f\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}
such that the integrals

\int_{B}|f(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leq C

are bounded for all balls
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a constant that depends only on the volume of the balls
C=C(|B|)
. But we will not need such a level of generality.

Remark 10
.

There are many functions in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
which do not fulfill the conditions of Lemma
8
. For instance, any periodic function with average value zero is in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
without any of the properties of Lemma
8
being true. Similarly, as shown in
[
7
]
, the sign function
\sigma=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}-\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}
is
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
, while it is obvious that none of the properties of Lemma
8
are fulfilled.

2
Preliminary Definitions

In this paragraph, we define some notation and function spaces, as well as provide some elements of harmonic analysis that are used throughout the paper.

2.1
The Homogeneous Littlewood-Paley Decomposition

First of all, it will be convenient to define the
homogeneous Littlewood-Paley decomposition
. For this, we fix a smooth radial and compactly supported function
\chi
that is supported in the ball
B(0,2)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, equal to
\chi(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 1
and such that the function
r\mapsto\chi(re)
is non-increasing over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for all
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set
\varphi(\xi)=\chi(\xi)-\chi(2\xi)
and
\varphi_{m}(\xi)=\varphi(2^{-m}\xi)
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the functions
(\varphi_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
define a partition of unity over the frequency space

(11)

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi_{m}(\xi)=1\qquad\text{for }\xi\neq 0.

The sum above should be understood in the sense of pointwise convergence. This partition of unity allows us to define the operators
\Delta_{m}
by Fourier multiplication

\forall m\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\dot{\Delta}_{m}=\varphi_{m}(D),

which are called the
Littlewood-Paley
operators. They are bounded in the
L^{p}\tend L^{p}
for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
, and their norm depends solely on
\chi
. By virtue of the fact that the
\varphi_{m}
form a partition of unity, it formally is possible to reconstruct any function
f
by means of the
\dot{\Delta}_{m}
. This is the
homogeneous Littlewood-Paley decomposition
, or the formal identity

(12)

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\dot{\Delta}_{m}f=f.

However, it must be noted that because the partition of unity (
11
) does not hold for
\xi=0
, the homogeneous Littlewood-Paley decomposition cannot be true for all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. In particular, it fails for polynomial functions (but not only! See
[
7
]
), as their Fourier transform is supported at
\xi=0
. The space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
which we have mentioned in the Introduction above is, broadly speaking, the space of distributions
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for which the Littlewood-Paley decomposition (
12
) converges in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
topology.

Definition 11
.

The space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
is the set of all tempered distributions
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that we have

(13)

\chi(\lambda D)f\tend_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}0\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}.

Remark 12
.

There seems to be no consensus on the way the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
should be defined: for example, the convergence (
13
) is sometimes required to hold in the norm topology of
L^{\infty}
, as in
[
2
]
. However this has no real bearing on this article: our results would remain true regardless. Let us mention the discussion in
[
7
]
concerning
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
and the different definitions that have been proposed.

The following topological property of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
will be used several times in the sequel.

Proposition 13

(see Proposition 19 in
[
7
]
)
.

The space
L^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
is closed in
L^{\infty}
for the norm topology of
L^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a converging sequence of functions in
L^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
whose limit is
f\in L^{\infty}
. We have, for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\chi(\lambda D)f,\phi\right\rangle_{L^{\infty}\times L^{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\langle\chi(\lambda D)(f-f_{n}),\phi\right\rangle_{L^{\infty}\times L^{1}}\right|+\left|\left\langle\chi(\lambda D)f_{n},\phi\right\rangle_{L^{\infty}\times L^{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\chi(\lambda D)(f-f_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}}+\left|\left\langle\chi(\lambda D)f_{n},\phi\right\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}}\right|.

The fact that the
\chi(\lambda D)f_{n}
converge to
0
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
shows that we have, for all
n\geq 0
,

\limss_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\left\langle\chi(\lambda D)f,\phi\right\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}}\right|\leq C\left\|(f-f_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}}.

This term has limit
0
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
so that
f
indeed lies in
L^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
∎

On of the main properties of the Littlewood-Paley decomposition is the way the functions
\dot{\Delta}_{m}f
transform under the action of derivatives: because
\dot{\Delta}_{m}f
is spectrally localized in an annulus of radius roughly
2^{m}
, applying
\nabla
essentially amounts to a multiplication by
2^{m}
. This idea is the essence of the next Lemma, the Bernstein inequalities.

Lemma 14

(Bernstein inequalities)
.

Let
0<r<R
. A constant
C
exists so that, for any nonnegative integer
k
, any couple
(p,q)

in
[1,+\infty]^{2}
, with
p\leq q
, and any function
u\in L^{p}
, we have, for all
\lambda>0
,

\hfil\displaystyle{\rm supp}\,(\widehat{u})\subset B(0,\lambda R)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\nabla^{k}u\|_{L^{q}}\,\leq\,C^{k+1}\,\lambda^{k+d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\,\|u\|_{L^{p}}\;;

\hfil\displaystyle{\rm supp}\,(\widehat{u})\subset\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,r\lambda\leq|\xi|\leq R\lambda\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C^{-k-1}\,\lambda^{k}\|u\|_{L^{p}}\,\leq\,\|\nabla^{k}u\|_{L^{p}}\,\leq\,C^{k+1}\,\lambda^{k}\|u\|_{L^{p}}\,.

More generally, Fourier multipliers whose symbol are homogeneous functions of degree
s
act on the functions
\dot{\Delta}_{m}f
roughly as a multiplication by
2^{ms}
.

Lemma 15

(see Lemma 2.2 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\sigma(\xi)
be a
C^{k}
function away from
\xi=0
with
k=2\lfloor 1+d/2\rfloor
and assume there is a degree
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|\nabla^{l}\sigma(\xi)|\leq C|\xi|^{s-l}
for all
\xi\neq 0
and
0\leq l\leq k
. Then there exists a constant depending only on
\sigma
and on the the dyadic decomposition function
\chi
such that, for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
,

\forall m\in\mathbb{Z},\forall f\in L^{p},\qquad\|\dot{\Delta}_{m}\sigma(D)f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C2^{ms}\|\dot{\Delta}_{m}f\|_{L^{p}}.

2.2
Besov Spaces

In this paragraph, we define the class of Besov spaces (both homogeneous and non-homogeneous). These are based on the Littlewood-paley decomposition.

Definition 16
.

We define the
homogeneous Besov space

\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}
as the set of those classes of distributions modulo polynomials
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/\,\mathbb{R}[X]
such that
5
5

5

In the sequel, we will note
[f]=\{f+Q,\;Q\in\mathbb{R}[X]\}
the class of
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
modulo polynomials. However, for the sake of conciseness, we will drop the brackets and note
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/\,\mathbb{R}[X]
when there is no ambiguity.

\|f\|_{\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}}:=\left\|\left(2^{ms}\|\dot{\Delta}_{m}f\|_{L^{p}}\right)_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\right\|_{\ell^{r}(\mathbb{Z})}<+\infty.

The space
\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}
is Banach for all values of
(s,p,r)
. Let us point out that
\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}
does not depend on the Littlewood-Paley decomposition function
\chi
up to an isomorphism of Banach spaces (see Theorem 3.3 in
[
30
]
).

Of course, it is highly unconvenient to work with classes of distributions modulo polynomial when dealing with non-linear PDEs, as is the case here. We therefore define realizations of some homogeneous Besov spaces as subspaces of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
(see
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
for more on that topic).

Definition 17
.

Consider
(s,p,r)\in\mathbb{R}\times[1,+\infty]^{2}
such that we have

(14)

s<\frac{d}{p}\qquad\text{or}\qquad s=\frac{d}{p}\text{ and }r=1.

Then we define the space
\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{s}_{p,r}
as the set of all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
such that
\|f\|_{\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}}<+\infty
. The space
\mathfrak{B}^{s}_{p,r}
is Banach for all values of
(s,p,r)
such that (
14
) holds. Moreover, the linear map

f\in\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}\longmapsto\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\dot{\Delta}_{m}f\in\mathfrak{B}^{s}_{p,r}

defines an isometric isomorphism
\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{s}_{p,r}\approx\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}
. In particular, every
f\in\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}
is the image of an element of
\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{s}_{p,r}
by the natural projection map
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\tend\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/\,\mathbb{R}[X]
.

The main interest of homogeneous Besov spaces is that Fourier multipliers whose symbol are homogeneous functions act very naturally on them. For example, if
\mathbb{P}
is the Leray projector defined in the Introduction above, then Lemma
15
shows that it is a bounded operator

\mathbb{P}:\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}\tend\dot{B}^{s}_{p,r}

for all values of
(s,p,r)\in\mathbb{R}\times[1,+\infty]^{2}
. This fact is particularly useful for dealing with enpoint exponents
p\in\{1,+\infty\}
, where usual Calderón-Zygmund theory of Singular Integral Operators fails.

We now focus on
non-homogeneous
Besov spaces. They are defined so as to avoid the question of (non)convergence of the Littlewood-Paley decomposition: define the operator
\Delta_{-1}=\chi(D)
, so that we have the identity

\Delta_{-1}f+\sum_{m\geq 0}\dot{\Delta}_{m}f=f

for
every

f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. This is the
non-homogeneous Littlewood-Paley decomposition
, which is always convergent in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. To simplify notation, we note
\Delta_{m}:=\dot{\Delta}_{m}
for
m\geq 0
.

Definition 18
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
1\leq p,r\leq+\infty
. The
non-homogeneous Besov space

B^{s}_{p,r}\,=\,B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as the set of tempered distributions
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for which

\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\,:=\,\left\|\left(2^{ms}\,\|\Delta_{m}f\|_{L^{p}}\right)_{m\geq-1}\right\|_{\ell^{r}}\,<\,+\infty\,.

The non-homogeneous space
B^{s}_{p,r}
is Banach for all values of
(s,p,r)\in\mathbb{R}\times[1,+\infty]^{2}
.

2.3
Functions of Bounded Mean Oscillations

Finally, we will need a few properties of the space
{\rm BMO}
of functions of Bounded Mean Oscillations.

Definition 19
.

The space
{\rm BMO}
is the set of classes of locally integrable functions modulo constant functions
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}/\,\mathbb{R}
such that
6
6

6

We will adopt the same notation as above for the class
[f]=\{f+C,\;C\in\mathbb{R}\}
of
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}
, or simply
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}/\,\mathbb{R}
when there is no ambiguity.

\|f\|_{\rm BMO}:=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}\big|f(x)-(f)_{B}\big|\,{\rm d}x<+\infty,

where the supremum ranges over all balls
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(f)_{B}=|B|^{-1}\int_{B}f
is the average value of
f
on
B
. The space
{\rm BMO}
is Banach.

One of the main features of
{\rm BMO}
is that Singular Integral Operators act continuously on it. In particular, the Leray projector is a bounded operator

\mathbb{P}:{\rm BMO}\tend{\rm BMO},

whose precise definition involves the Fefferman-Stein duality
(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}={\rm BMO}
with the Hardy space
\mathcal{H}^{1}
. We refer to
[
34
]
, in particular Theorem 1 pp. 142–144, for more on this topic.

A useful result is the way
{\rm BMO}
interacts with Besov spaces. The following embedding Lemma consists of the dual embeddings for the Hardy space
\dot{B}^{0}_{1,1}\subset\mathcal{H}^{1}\subset\dot{B}^{0}_{1,2}
, which can be found in paragraph 2.2.2 p. 105 of
[
16
]
.

Lemma 20
.

The following embeddings hold:
\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,2}\subset{\rm BMO}\subset\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}
.

Remark 21
.

The embeddings of Lemma
20
require an explanation. On the one hand, the inclusion map
{\rm BMO}\subset\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}
can only make sense if any
[f]\in{\rm BMO}
has a representative
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. This follows for example from the following integral inequality:

(15)

\int\frac{\big|f(x)-(f)_{B_{1}}\big|}{(1+|x|)^{d+1}}\,{\rm d}x\leq C(d)\|f\|_{\rm BMO}

where
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unit ball. A proof can be found in
[
34
]
, see equation (2) in paragraph 1.1.4, pp. 141.
7
7

7

See also Remark 1.27 in
[
8
]
for a logarithmic version of the inequality.
Note in particular that
{\rm BMO}
cannot contain any non-constant polynomial function.

For the inclusion
\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,2}\subset{\rm BMO}
, its meaning is that any
[f]\in\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,2}
has a representative
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that
\|f\|_{\rm BMO}<+\infty
.

We now define the space
{\rm BMO}^{-1}
of derivatives of
{\rm BMO}
functions, after the work
[
20
]
of Koch and Tataru, who introduced it as a set of possible initial daat for the Navier-Stokes equations.

Definition 22
.

Let
{\rm BMO}^{-1}
be the set of
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that there are functions
g_{1},...,g_{d}\in{\rm BMO}
with
f=\sum_{k}\partial_{k}g_{k}
.

From Lemma
20
, we gather that there is an embedding
{\rm BMO}^{-1}\subset\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
. One of the advantages of
{\rm BMO}^{-1}
is that its elements are distributions, and not classes of distributions modulo constants as in
{\rm BMO}
, as the expression
\sum_{k}\partial_{k}g_{k}
above does not depend on the representatives of the
g_{k}
modulo constants. In fact, we can go one step further.

Proposition 23
.

We have the inclusion
{\rm BMO}^{-1}\subset\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
. In particular, any function
f\in{\rm BMO}^{-1}
is the sum of its homogeneous Littlewood-Paley decomposition, or in other words
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
.

Proof.

We start by recalling that
{\rm BMO}^{-1}
contains no nontrivial polynomial function. This follows from the fact that no polynomial of degree
1
or higher can be in
{\rm BMO}
(see inequality (
15
) in Remark
21
).

Now, let
f\in{\rm BMO}^{-1}
. Because of the embedding
{\rm BMO}^{-1}\subset\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
, and since
\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
can be realized as
\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
, we see that
f
can be written as a sum

f=h+Q\in\dot{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}\oplus\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}.

As
Q=f-h\in{\rm BMO}^{-1}+\,\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
belongs in a space which contains no nontrivial polynomial function, we deduce that
Q=0
, and so
f=h
. Therefore
f\in\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
.
∎

We also will need to evaluate the rough growth of
{\rm BMO}
. The John-Nirenberg inequality shows that it is essentially logarithmic, which is what the following Proposition endeavours to prove.

Proposition 24
.

Let
f\in{\rm BMO}
. Then we have

\Delta_{-1}f(x)-\Delta_{-1}f(0)=O\big(\log(1+|x|)\big),\qquad\text{as }|x|\rightarrow+\infty.

Proof.

A key element of the proof will be the use of the embedding
{\rm BMO}\subset\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}
. For notational convenience, set
g=\Delta_{-1}f
. We wish to make use of the (homogeneous) Littlewood-Paley decomposition of
g
, but we must be careful as it may not converge. We therefore define the partial sum operator

T_{N}=\sum_{-N}^{0}\dot{\Delta}_{m}

for
N\geq 0
. We then have

|g(x)-g(0)|\leq|T_{N}g(x)-T_{N}g(0)|+\big|({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(x)-({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(0)\big|.

In order to bound the first term of the righthand side, we note that

(16)

\begin{split}|T_{N}g(x)-T_{N}g(0)|&\leq\sum_{-N}^{0}\left|\dot{\Delta}_{m}g(x)-\dot{\Delta}_{m}g(0)\right|\\
&\leq 2(N+1)\|g\|_{\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}}.\end{split}

For the second term, we bound the difference by the mean value theorem:

\big|({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(x)-({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(0)\big|\leq|x|\,\big\|\nabla({\rm Id}-T_{N})g\big\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Let us write
\nabla({\rm Id}-T_{N})g
as the sum of its Littlewood-Paley decomposition, by Proposition
23
. We deduce from the previous equation that

\begin{split}\big|({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(x)-({\rm Id}-T_{N})g(0)\big|&\leq|x|\sum_{m<-N}\big\|\nabla\dot{\Delta}_{m}g\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq|x|\,\|g\|_{\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}}\sum_{m<-N}2^{m}\\
&\leq C|x|\,\|g\|_{\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}}2^{-N}.\end{split}

By taking
N
such that
N=\log(1+|x|)
, we may put the previous inequality together with (
16
) in order to obtain the desired result:

(17)

|g(x)-g(0)|\leq C\|g\|_{\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}}\log\big(1+|x|\big).

∎

Remark 25
.

In fact, we have proved a slightly more general result: if
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is a representative of
[f]\in\dot{B}^{0}_{\infty,\infty}
such that
f(x)=o(|x|)
as
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
, then it follows that inequality (
17
) also holds.

3
Bounded Weak Solutions of the Euler System

In this paragraph, we clarify the precise meaning of a bounded weak solution of the Euler system (
1
) and the projected problem (
4
). This will require exploring further the properties of the operator
\mathbb{P}\D
.

3.1
Weak Solutions of the Euler Equations

We define and comment the notion of bounded weak solutions of the Euler equations: these read

(18)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla\pi=0\\
\D(u)=0.\end{cases}

Definition 26
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
be a divergence-free function and
T>0
. A function
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
is said to be a
bounded weak solution
of the Euler problem (
18
) associated to the initial datum
u_{0}
if there is a pressure
\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(19)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla\pi=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes u_{0}(x)\\
\D(u)=0\end{cases}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

In the equation above, the tensor product
\delta_{0}(t)\otimes u_{0}(x)
is defined by the relation

\forall\phi\in\mathcal{D}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\big\langle\delta_{0}(t)\otimes u_{0}(x),\phi(t,x)\big\rangle:=\int u_{0}(x)\cdot\phi(0,x)\,{\rm d}x.

Remark 27
.

Several remarks are in order concerning this definition. Firstly, by testing (
19
) against any divergence-free
\phi\in\mathcal{D}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we obtain the usual integral formulation

(20)

\int_{0}^{T}\int\Big\{\partial_{t}\phi\cdot u+\nabla\phi:u\otimes u\Big\}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t+\int u_{0}\cdot\phi(0)\,{\rm d}x=0

of the momentum equation. Strictly speaking, Definition
26
is a bit stronger than the previous integral formulation because an extra assumption is made on the pressure: it has to define a distribution with respect to both
time and space
.

Remark 28
.

Next, we note that, contrary to most evolution equations, there is no need for a weak solution
u
to be continuous with respect to time, even in the
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
topology: the presence of the pressure may compensate time singularities in the flow, as would be the case if, for example,
u(t,x)=\mathds{1}_{[1,+\infty[}(t)V
where
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a constant vector. As we will see, this problem arises only in the
L^{\infty}
framework:
L^{p}
regularity of the solutions (with
p<+\infty
) implies continuity with respect to time in some weak topology, since, in that case, we may indeed apply the Leray projection to obtain (
21
) below.

Remark 29
.

Finally, we must comment on the notion of initial datum in Definition
26
. Because the weak form of the Euler equations is taken in the sense of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is with
t=0
included in the time interval, any translation
u_{0}+V
(with
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) of the initial datum is also a valid initial datum
for the same solution
:

\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla\big(\pi+\delta_{0}(t)\otimes V\big)=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes\big(u_{0}(x)+V\big).

Simply, a different choice of pressure allows for
u
to be associated with infinitely many initial data, regardless of the time regularity of
u
(it could be
C^{1}_{T}(L^{\infty})
). Seen from the perspective of the integral form (
20
) of the momentum equation, the ambiguity of the initial data is a direct consequence of testing with
divergence-free
functions only: for any fixed
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and divergence-free
\phi\in\mathcal{D}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int(u_{0}+V)\cdot\phi(0)\,{\rm d}x=\int u_{0}\cdot\phi(0)\,{\rm d}x.

These test functions are orthogonal, in the sense of the
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}
duality, to all gradients. Therefore, any (regular) flow
u
which solves the Euler problem is also a weak solution (according to Definition
26
) related to the initial data
u(0)+\nabla g
(for any
g\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
).

In the
L^{p}
framework (with
p<+\infty
), this ambiguity totally disappears, as long as we require the initial data to be
L^{p}
. Indeed, any divergence-free
f\in L^{p}
which is also the gradient of some tempered distribution
f=\nabla g
must be a harmonic polynomial, since we would then have
\Delta g=0
. Since there is no nonzero polynomial in
L^{p}
, this implies
f=0
.

Our problem lies in the fact that the space
L^{\infty}
intersects non-trivially with
\mathbb{R}[X]
, so that there are nonzero divergence-free functions which are also gradients: constant functions.

As we said in the introduction, bounded solutions of the Euler equations (
18
) are not unique, even under
C^{\infty}
smoothness assumptions.

3.2
Weak Solutions of the Projected Problem

In this section, we focus on the projected problem: formally, one may apply the Leray projection operator to kill the pressure term and obtain an equation on
u
only, namely

(21)

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)=0.

However, as we saw in Chapter 1, Fourier multiplication operators with bounded homogeneous symbols of degree zero, such as the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}
, are not properly defined on
L^{\infty}
. We must therefore clarify the meaning of equation (
21
) when the solutions are solely bounded, say
u\in L^{2}_{T}(L^{\infty})
.

The general idea of this paragraph is to study the order one operator
\mathbb{P}\D
instead of
\mathbb{P}
, which is a combination of operators the form
T_{j,k,l}:=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}(-\Delta)^{-1}
. By introducing the fundamental solution
E\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}
of the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

E(x)=\frac{C(d)}{|x|^{d-2}}\text{ if }d\geq 3\qquad\text{ and }\qquad E(x)=C(2)\log|x|\text{ if }d=2,

where the constant
C(d)>0
is such that
-\Delta E=\delta_{0}
, we may see
T_{j,k,l}
as a convolution operator whose kernel is
\Gamma_{j,k,l}:=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E
(seen as a distribution). We prove the following Proposition, which is a decomposition of Lemarié-Rieusset
[
24
]
(see Lemma 11.1 p. 106) also used in the simpler setting of bounded solutions by Pak and Park
[
28
]
(see equations (20) and (21) p. 1161).

Proposition 30
.

Consider
j,k,l\in\{1,...,d\}
and let
\Gamma_{j,k,l}=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Then
\Gamma_{j,k,l}
is the sum of a compactly supported distribution and an
L^{1}
function. In addition, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,r\in[1,+\infty]
, convolution by
\Gamma
defines a bounded operator on the non-homogeneous Besov spaces

T_{j,k,l}:B^{s}_{p,r}\tend B^{s-1}_{p,r}.

Remark 31
.

The Besov boundedness of
T_{j,k,l}:B^{s}_{p,r}\tend B^{s-1}_{p,r}
is falsely obvious in the case
p=+\infty
. Although Lemma
15
makes it clear that the operator

\tilde{T}_{j,k,l}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\dot{\Delta}_{m}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}(-\Delta)^{-1}

is bounded in the
B^{s}_{\infty,r}\tend B^{s-1}_{\infty,r}
topology, as the sum converges normally in
L^{\infty}
when restricted to ranks
j\leq-1
, the two operators
\tilde{T}_{j,k,l}
and
T_{j,k,l}
might not coincide: they could differ by a polynomial. This is why we need two extra steps: firstly to define
T_{j,k,l}
, this is the purpose of Proposition
30
, and secondly to show that
\tilde{T}_{j,k,l}
and
T_{j,k,l}
are actually equal, this being the scope of Proposition
39
below.

Proof.

By fixing a compactly supported function
\chi\in\mathcal{D}
with
\chi(x)\equiv 1
around
x=0
, we write
\Gamma_{j,k,l}=\chi\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E+(1-\chi)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E
. Because of the form of the fundamental solution
E
, its third derivatives are integrable at infinity:

(22)

\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{d+1}}\right)\qquad\text{as }|x|\rightarrow+\infty.

Therefore, the function
(1-\chi)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E
is
L^{1}
and we have proved that
\Gamma_{j,k,l}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}+L^{1}
, where
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is the space of compactly supported distributions. Concerning boundedness in Besov spaces, we note that the operator
\mathbb{P}\D
is entirely defined for frequencies
\xi\neq 0
by the Fourier transform

\forall\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\};\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\mathcal{F}\big[\mathbb{P}\D\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\phi]\big](\xi)=\sigma(\xi)\phi(\xi),

where
\sigma
is the symbol of
\mathbb{P}\D
. Consequently, Lemma
15
makes it clear that for any
m\geq 0
and
f\in B^{s}_{p,r}
we have

\|\Delta_{m}\mathbb{P}\D(f)\|_{L^{p}}\leq C2^{m}\|\Delta_{m}f\|_{L^{p}}.

It only remains to estimate the low frequency block
\Delta_{-1}\mathbb{P}\D(f)
. Let
\psi\in\mathcal{S}
such that the operator
\Delta_{-1}
is the convolution by
\psi
, that is
\Delta_{-1}f=\psi*f
. We use the cut-off function
\theta
as above and split the kernel into two parts

\Delta_{-1}\mathbb{P}\D(f)=\psi*\Gamma_{j,k,l}*f=\psi*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}(\theta E)+\psi*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta)E\big).

Integrating by parts in the convolution product yields

\psi*\Gamma_{j,k,l}*f=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi*(\theta E)*f+\psi*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta)E\big)*f.

Now, because
E
is locally integrable and thanks to (
22
), both kernels

\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi*(\theta E)\in L^{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta)E\big)\in L^{1}

are integrable functions. The Hausdorff convolution inequality guarantees that
\Delta_{-1}\mathbb{P}\D
is a bounded
L^{p}\tend L^{p}
operator for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
.
∎

Proposition
30
makes it possible to define the notion of bounded weak solution of the projected Euler system (
21
).

Definition 32
.

Let
T>0
and
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
be a bounded divergence free function. We say that
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
is a
bounded weak solution
of the projected Euler system (
21
) associated to the initial datum
u_{0}
if

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes u_{0}(x)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Bounded weak solutions of the projected system (
21
) behave in many ways better than weak solutions of the Euler system. For example, solutions are necessarily continuous with respect to time in the
B^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
topology. In addition, the pressure, which is given by

(23)

\pi=(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k})\qquad(\text{modulo constants})

has minimal regularity properties: being the image of the bounded function
u_{j}u_{k}\in C^{0}_{T}(L^{\infty})
by the singular integral operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}
, it defines a
{\rm BMO}
function

\pi\in C^{0}([0,T[;{\rm BMO}).

However, it must be noted that because
{\rm BMO}
is a space of functions defined up to a constant (and hence
{\rm BMO}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/\,\mathbb{R}[X]
), the pressure does not define a distribution on
[0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as the constant summand in (
23
) may be extremely singular in the time variable. On the other hand, because the pressure force is the derivative of (
23
), it unambiguously defines a
{\rm BMO}^{-1}\subset\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
function

\nabla\pi\in C^{0}([0,T[;{\rm BMO}^{-1})\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

4
Proof the Main Result

We are now ready to prove our main result, Theorem
3
. We will start by recalling the statement of Theorem
3
and making a few additional comments. Then, the proof will start with the simpler case where the solutions are
C^{1}
with respect to the time variable, before dealing with the full statement of Theorem
3
.

4.1
Statement of the Theorem and Comments

For the readers convenience, we recall the statement of our main result.

Theorem 33
.

Let
T>0
,
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
and
u\in C^{0}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
be a weak solution of the Euler equations (
18
) associated to the initial datum
u_{0}
and to a pressure
\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

the flow
u
solves the projected problem with initial datum
u(0)
that satisfies
u(0)-u_{0}\in{\rm Cst}
,

(ii)

for all times
t\in[0,T[
, we have
u(t)-u(0)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
,

(iii)

for all times
t\in[0,T[
, we have
u(t)-u(0)\in{\rm BMO}^{-1}
,

(iv)

the pressure satisfies
\pi\in C^{0}(]0,T[;{\rm BMO})
,

(v)

the pressure force is continuous with respect to time
\nabla\pi\in C^{0}(]0,T[;\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
and
\nabla\pi(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
for all
0<t<T
.

(vi)

the pressure defines a continuous function in the topology of locally integrable functions modulo constants
\pi\in C^{0}(]0,T[;L^{1}_{\rm loc}/\,\mathbb{R})
, and we have
\chi(D)\pi(t,x)=O\big(\log|x|\big)
as
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
for all
0<t<T
.

Remark 34
.

In this Theorem, the velocity field is assumed to be continuous with respect to time in the
L^{\infty}
topology. This may seem odd when compared to known solutions of the Euler equations. For example, the solutions of Pak and Park
[
28
]
are continuous in
B^{1}_{\infty,1}
, and therefore, by Proposition
30
,

(24)

\partial_{t}u=-\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)\in C^{0}(B^{0}_{\infty,1}),

so that a
C^{1}(L^{\infty})
assumption might seem more natural, in addition to (as we will see) simplifying the proof. Unfortunately, exchanging space regularity for time regularity as we just did, requires having a solution of the projected problem. If
u\in C^{0}_{T}(B^{1}_{\infty,1})
is only a solution of the Euler equations (
18
), one cannot repeat operation (
24
), as the pressure force
-\nabla\pi
may well not be regular with respect to time. Recall that the uniform flow (
2
) solves the Euler problem even if
f(t)
is not
C^{1}
.

In that regard, working with
C^{1}_{T}(L^{\infty})
solutions would be somewhat artificial. In contrast, continuity in the time variable is a very reasonable assumption, in the light of the existence of paradoxical solutions (that dissipate kinetic energy) in the class
C^{0}(L^{2})
, see
[
10
]
. In other words, there is no point in going below time-continuous regularity.

Remark 35
.

We note that condition
(ii)
of the theorem is in fact a Galilean condition: its validity is independent of the inertial reference frame in which the velocity is computed. This shows that the equivalence issue has something to do with a fundamental property of the solution, and is not a mere artifact of the Galilean nature of the equations.

4.2
Equivalence of the Two Formulations: Smooth in Time Solutions

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
33
under a comfortable assumption that the solutions are
C^{1}(L^{\infty})
. This simplifies the proof very much, as it will allow the derivative
\partial_{t}u
to be handled as a locally integrable function.

Proposition 36
.

Consider
T>0
. Let
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
be a divergence-free initial datum and
u\in C^{1}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
be a weak solution of the Euler problem (according to Definition
26
) for some pressure field
\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and related to the initial datum
u_{0}
. Then all conditions of Theorem
33
are equivalent.

The proof of this proposition is split in several steps. We start by reformulating the problem in a way that lets the Leray projector appear in the equations. This is the purpose of the following lemma.

Lemma 37
.

Let
u
be as in Proposition
36
. For all times
t\in]0,T[
, there exists a polynomial
Q(t)\in\mathbb{R}[X]
such that

(25)

\partial_{t}u+\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla Q(t)=0.

Moreover,
Q(t)
must be at most one linear:
\deg\big(Q(t)\big)\leq 1
.

Remark 38
.

A consequence of Lemma
37
is that any solution of the Euler system (
18
) is obtained from a solution of the projected system (
21
) from a “generalized Galilean transformation”, or in other words writing the projected equations (
21
) in an accelerated reference frame. It is mainly a matter of taking
g(t)=\nabla Q(t)
in (
5
). We refer to
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
for precise arguments.

Proof of Lemma
37
.

Since
u\in C^{1}_{T}(L^{\infty})
is a solution of the Euler equations for the pressure
\pi
, we may write

\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla\pi=0\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(]0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Now we fix once and for all a time
t\in]0,T[
. By taking the divergence of this equation, we see that the pressure solves the elliptic equation

-\Delta\pi=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k}),

in which there is an implicit sum on the repeated indices. We wish to invert this equation in order to recover
\pi
. In order to avoid the singularity of the inverse Laplacian operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}
at the frequency
\xi=0
, we localize away from low frequencies. For any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\widehat{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
, we have

\big\langle\nabla\pi,\phi\big\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}}=\big\langle\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k}),\phi\big\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}},

where the quantity
\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}u_{k})=T_{j,k}(u_{j},u_{k})
it to be understood in the sense of Proposition
30
. Therefore, the difference between the two distributions
\nabla\pi
and
T_{j,k}(u_{j}u_{k})
is spectrally supported at
\xi=0
, and must be a polynomial: there exists a
\nabla Q(t)\in\mathbb{R}[X]
such that

\nabla\pi=-({\rm Id}-\mathbb{P})\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla Q,

and the polynomial
\nabla Q
is indeed a gradient function because all other terms in this equation are gradients. We have shown that (
25
) holds. The assertion on the degree of
Q(t)
is obtained by applying the low frequency block
\Delta_{-1}
to (
25
) and noting that

-\nabla Q=\partial_{t}\Delta_{-1}u+\Delta_{-1}\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)\in C^{0}_{T}(L^{\infty})

is at all times bounded in view Proposition
30
, thus ending the proof of the lemma.
∎

Before moving onwards, we study the Leray projection more closely. The following proposition shows that, for any
f\in L^{\infty}
, the function
({\rm Id}-\mathbb{P})\D(f)
is in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
.

Proposition 39
.

Let
\Gamma
be as defined in Proposition
30
. Then we have, for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
and all
f\in L^{p}
, the estimate

\left\|\chi(\lambda D)\big(\Gamma*f\big)\right\|_{L^{p}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(\lambda)\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

The factor
\log(\lambda)
may be dispensed with whenever
d\geq 3
.

Remark 40
.

As mentioned above, the
O\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)
estimate (neglecting the logarithmic term when
d=2
) seems rather natural, considering that convolution by
\Gamma
is formally the Fourier multiplication by a homogeneous symbol of order
1
. However, the first Bernstein inequality is insufficient to obtain this result, as, though it may be generalized to Fourier multipliers, it requires their symbol to be smooth.

Remark 41
.

In fact, Proposition
39
could be replaced by a much shorter but much more advanced argument: assume that
f\in L^{p}
with
p>1
. If
1<p<+\infty
then Calderón-Zygmund theory implies that
\Gamma*f
is the derivative of a
L^{p}
function, so we may legitimately use the first Bernstein inequality to get

\left\|\chi(\lambda D)\big(\Gamma*f\big)\right\|_{L^{p}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{1+d/p}}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty,

but this argument fails for the endpoint exponents, and in particular for
p=+\infty
which corresponds to our framework for solutions. When
p=+\infty
, we may use the other properties of Singular Integral Operators to see that the map
(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}:L^{\infty}\tend{\rm BMO}^{-1}
is bounded. Since the space
{\rm BMO}^{-1}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
by Proposition
23
, we may conclude that
\Gamma*f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
. However, we prefer giving an elementary proof based on an integral decomposition of the kernel
\Gamma
rather than resorting to Fefferman-Stein duality.

Proof of Proposition
39
.

We recall that the Fourier multiplier
\Delta_{-1}=\chi(D)
is equal to the convolution operator
f\mapsto\psi*f
(see Proposition
30
). Therefore, we have, for any
\lambda>0
,

\chi(\lambda D)=\psi_{\lambda}*,\qquad\text{ where }\psi_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\psi\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right).

Fix an exponent
\alpha>0
and define, for all
\lambda>0
, the cut-off function
\theta_{\lambda}(x)=\chi(\lambda^{-\alpha}x)
. By proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
30
, we have, for
\lambda
large enough that
\chi(\lambda D)\Delta_{-1}=\chi(\lambda D)
,

\chi(\lambda D)\Gamma=\psi_{\lambda}*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta_{\lambda})E\big)+\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta_{\lambda}E).

To prove the proposition, the Hausdorff-Young convolution inequality asserts that it is enough to show that the
L^{1}
norm of both these convolution products is
O(\lambda^{-1}\log(\lambda))
. We start by studying the second one:

\left\|\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta_{\lambda}E)\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{1}}\|\theta_{\lambda}E\|_{L^{1}}.

On the one hand, the three derivatives
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}
provide a
\lambda^{-3}
decay, as

\left\|\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{1}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\int\left|\left(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi\right)\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)\right|\frac{\,{\rm d}x}{\lambda^{d}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\right).

On the other hand, the fundamental solution
E(x)
is locally integrable, because it has a
O(|x|^{d-2})
singularity at
x=0
when
d\geq 3
, and a
O(\log|x|)
one if
d=2
. Since the function
\theta_{\lambda}
is supported in the ball
B(0,2\lambda^{\alpha})
, we have another inequality: in the case where
d\geq 3
, we get

\|\theta_{\lambda}E\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\int_{|x|\leq\lambda^{\alpha}}\frac{\,{\rm d}x}{|x|^{d-2}}=O(\lambda^{2\alpha}),

and in the case where
d=2
, we instead have

\begin{split}\|\theta_{\lambda}E\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\int_{|x|\leq\lambda^{\alpha}}\log|x|\,{\rm d}x&\leq C\int_{0}^{\lambda^{\alpha}}r\log(r){\rm d}r\\
&=\frac{C}{2}r^{2}\Big(\log(r)-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\bigg|_{r=0}^{\lambda^{\alpha}}=O\left(\lambda^{2\alpha}\log(\lambda)\right).\end{split}

We conclude that, in all dimensions
d\geq 2
, the convolution product
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta_{\lambda}E)
has a
L^{1}
norm that tends to zero as long as
\alpha<3/2
, at the speed
O(\lambda^{2\alpha-3}\log(\lambda))
.

We next look at the other convolution product
\psi_{\lambda}*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta_{\lambda})E\big)
. Here, we will take advantage of the integrability the third derivatives of
E(x)
possess at
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
. However, this in itself is not enough, as we aim at showing decay as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
. We will also have to use the fact that the support of the cutoff
1-\theta_{\lambda}
shrinks as
\lambda
becomes large. More precisely, we have the estimate, which holds in any dimension
d\geq 2
,

\left|\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta_{\lambda})E\big)(x)\right|\leq C\frac{1-\mathds{1}_{B(0,2\lambda^{\alpha})}(x)}{|x|^{d+1}}:=M_{\lambda}(x).

By using this on the convolution product, we find that

\left\|\psi_{\lambda}*\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\big((1-\theta_{\lambda})E\big)\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}\|M_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|M_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}.

Finally, we may bound this last integral by

\|M_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\int_{|x|\geq\lambda^{\alpha}}\frac{\,{\rm d}x}{|x|^{d+1}}=C\int_{\lambda^{\alpha}}^{+\infty}\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\right).

Putting both estimates together, we have a low frequency inequality for the kernel
\Gamma
. In the limit
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
,

\|\chi(\lambda D)\Gamma\|_{L^{1}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{3-2\alpha}}\log(\lambda)\right),

and this gives convergence to
0
as long as
0<\alpha<3/2
. Taking
\alpha=1
(the optimal value) ends proving our statement.
∎

Proposition
39
has the following consequence.

Corollary 42
.

Let
f\in B^{0}_{\infty,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a field of matrices. Then we have

\mathbb{P}\D(f)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\dot{\Delta}_{m}\mathbb{P}\D(f)\qquad\text{ with convergence in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}.

We therefore define a bounded operator
\mathbb{P}\D:B^{0}_{\infty,\infty}\tend\mathfrak{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}\subset\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
. Here,
\mathfrak{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
refers to the realization of
\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
as a subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
, see Definition
17
. The same statement holds for the operator
\mathbb{Q}\D=({\rm Id}-\mathbb{P})\D
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the operator
A=\mathbb{P}\D
or
A=\mathbb{Q}\D
. Because the symbol of
A
is a homogeneous function of degree one, Lemma
15
makes it clear that

\forall f\in B^{0}_{\infty,\infty},\qquad\|Af\|_{\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}}\leq C\|f\|_{B^{0}_{\infty,\infty}}.

In addition, Proposition
39
shows that for any
f\in B^{0}_{\infty,\infty}
, the function
T_{j,k,l}f=(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}f
lies in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
, and the first Bernstein inequality (Lemma
14
) that

\|\chi(\lambda D)\D(f)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|\chi(\lambda D)\Delta_{-1}\D(f)\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

Therefore
Af
lies in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
.
∎

We now have all the necessary elements to prove Proposition
36
.

Proof of Proposition
36
.

With Lemma
37
and Corollary
42
at our disposal, we are ready to complete the proof of Proposition
36
, as it is equivalent for
u
to solve the projected problem (
21
) and for the polynomial
\nabla Q
in Lemma
37
to be zero. Now, by Corollary
42
, we see that
\partial_{t}u
decomposes as

\partial_{t}u=-\mathbb{P}\D(u\otimes u)-\nabla Q\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}\oplus\mathbb{R}[X],

and so

, we have a

5.2

In this paragraph, we work exclusively on the 2D plane

Serfati solutions of the Euler equations

9

are a class of 2D bounded solutions introduced by Philippe Serfati in
[

(see also

for an english presentation). These solutions have a threefold description: a solution

if

(i)

both

and the vorticity
\omega=\partial_{1}u_{2}-\partial_{2}u_{1}
are uniformly bounded

(29)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\Big(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)<+\infty,

(ii)

the vorticity solves the usual pure transport equation
\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0

(iii)

the velocity

Serfati identity
: for any radial cut-off function
\theta\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\theta(x)=1
around

(30)

u(t)-u(0)=(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*(u\otimes u){\rm d}s,

where in the equation above

is the Biot-Savart kernel and the convolution product contains an implicit contraction between both

\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)
and
u\otimes u
.

The Serfati identity follows naturally from the Euler equations in their vorticity form. Formally, the velocity flow satisfies
u=K*\omega
and hence

\partial_{t}u=(\theta K)*\partial_{t}\omega+\big((1-\theta)K\big)*\partial_{t}\omega.

On the one hand, the first convolution product
(\theta K)*\omega
makes sense if
\omega\in L^{\infty}
because the Biot-Savart kernel is locally integrable. Integration with respect to time gives the first term in the righthandside of (
30
). On the other hand, by substituting
\partial_{t}\omega=-u\cdot\nabla\omega={\rm curl}\,\D(u\otimes u)
and integrating by parts, we obtain the second term:

\int_{0}^{t}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*\partial_{t}\omega{\rm d}s=-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*(u\otimes u){\rm d}s.

A notable feature of Serfati solutions is that they lead to
global a priori estimates
for the quantity
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}
and to a well-posedness result: for any
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
with
\omega_{0}={\rm curl}\,(u_{0})\in L^{\infty}

u_{0}
.

As noted in Section 7 of
[
1
]

2

18

(i)

a “generalized” Serfati identity holds, where the uniform flow

is added to (

(ii)

the velocity satisfies a renormalized Biot-Savart law: if

\theta_{R}(x)=\theta(x/R)

(\theta_{R}K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)\tend_{R\rightarrow+\infty}U_{\infty}(t)+u(t)-u(0)

(iii)

\pi(t,x)-U_{\infty}^{\prime}(t)\cdot x\;\in L^{\infty}_{T}({\rm BMO}).

Here, we show the relation between Serfati solutions and our own result: more precisely, we prove that a Serfati solution fulfills the equivalent conditions of Theorem

Theorem 46

Let

Remark 47

Serfati solutions are more general than the

33
holds for very rough solutions, whereas the Serfati identity requires
\omega\in L^{\infty}
to be written.

Proof.

Let, as usual,
\psi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\widehat{\psi_{\lambda}}(\xi)=\chi(\lambda\xi)
and consider the convolution product of the Serfati identity (
30
)

\psi_{\lambda}*\big(u(t)-u(0)\big)=\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)-\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{\lambda}*\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*(u\otimes u){\rm d}s.

This equation is true for all nonnegative radial
\theta\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\theta(x)=1
near
x=0
. As in the proof of Proposition
39
, our aim is to estimate the convolution products with the Hausdorff convolution inequality to obtain our result. We focus on the first term. Integration by parts gives

(31)

\begin{split}\Big\|\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}&=\left\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(u(t)-u(0)\big)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq\|\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}\|\theta K\|_{L^{1}}\|u(t)-u(0)\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{1}}\|\theta K\|_{L^{1}}\|u(t)-u(0)\|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{split}

We leave here, for now, this first term and come to the second one. Similarly, the Hausdorff convolution inequality gives

\begin{split}\left\|\psi_{\lambda}*\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*(u\otimes u)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}&\leq\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{1}}\big\|\nabla^{2}\big((1-\theta)K\big)\big\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\\
&\leq\big\|\nabla^{2}\big((1-\theta)K\big)\big\|_{L^{1}}\|\psi\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2},\end{split}

but this is not sufficient: the upper bound we have found does not depend on
\lambda
, so it will not decay as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
. To force this upper bound to be small, we take advantage of the fact that the Serfati identity holds for all radial cut-off functions
\theta\in\mathcal{D}
. Fix a such
\theta\in\mathcal{D}
that is supported in the ball
|x|\leq 1
and define
\theta_{\lambda}(x)=\theta(x/\lambda^{\alpha})
for some
\alpha>0
to be fixed later. Then we have

\big\|\nabla^{2}\big((1-\theta)K\big)\big\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\int_{\lambda^{\alpha}}^{+\infty}\frac{{\rm d}r}{r^{2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty,

and therefore

(32)

\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{\lambda}*\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big((1-\theta)K\big)*(u\otimes u){\rm d}s\,\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

On the other hand, our choice of cut-off function comes at a cost: because the support of
\theta_{\lambda}
grows as
\lambda
does, we lose decay in (
31
):

\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\theta_{\lambda}K\|_{L^{1}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{1-\alpha}}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

By putting this together with (
32
) and choosing the optimal
\alpha=1/2
, we see that
\chi(\lambda D)(u(t)-u(0))
does indeed tend to zero in
L^{\infty}
at the speed
O(\lambda^{-1/2})
. With Theorem
33
, this is enough to prove that
u
is a solution of the projected problem.

However, we are not entirely sated, because the
O(\lambda^{-1/2})
does not seem optimal: by Proposition
39
, we know that the decay is truly of order roughly
O(\lambda^{-1})
. By revisiting the computations above, we see that the estimates in (
31
) are not really optimal: because convolution by
\psi_{\lambda}
is a low frequency cut-off operator
\chi(\lambda D)
, we have

\begin{split}\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)&=\psi_{2\lambda}*\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)\\
&=\nabla\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta_{\lambda}K)*\Big(\psi_{2\lambda}*(u(t)-u(0))\Big).\end{split}

Now, thanks to the estimates we have just found, we know that
\psi_{2\lambda}*(u(t)-u(0))
also decays in
L^{\infty}
at the speed
O(\lambda^{-1/2})
. By taking this into account, we obtain

\Big\|\psi_{\lambda}*(\theta K)*\big(\omega(t)-\omega(0)\big)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{3/2-\alpha}}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty

and by setting
\alpha=3/4
we obtain a
O(\lambda^{-3/4})
estimate instead of a
O(\lambda^{-1/2})
one, and we can once again use this improved bound in (
31
)… By iterating, we find that the bound may be brought to
O(\lambda^{-\alpha})
for any
\alpha<1
, but at the price of a constant that depends on
\alpha
.
∎

6
Application to Ideal MHD and Elsässer Variables

In this Section, we study the ideal magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations. These describe ideal conducting fluids which are subject to the magnetic field generated through their own motion. The ideal MHD equations read:

(33)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla\pi=(b\cdot\nabla)b\\
\partial_{t}b+(u\cdot\nabla)b=(b\cdot\nabla)u\\
\D(u)=0.\end{cases}

In the equations above,
\pi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\tend\mathbb{R}
is the MHD pressure, which is the sum of the usual hydrodynamic pressure and the magnetic pressure
\frac{1}{2}|b|^{2}
. Formally, the MHD equations are a generalization of the Euler system, as the latter can be recovered by setting
b=0
. However, this obscures the fact that MHD possesses its own interesting and intriguing properties, such as non-trivial static solutions (with
u=0
), additional conserved quantities, peculiar stability issues, a much more intricate theory of solutions,
etc
. We refer to the excellent book
[
3
]
for an overview of some of these issues.

Formally, system (
33
) is overdetermined, as it is a system of
2d+2
equations whereas there are
2d+1
scalar unknowns
(u,b,\pi)
. However, the divergence condition
\D(b)=0
is in fact redundant, because it automatically follows from the magnetic field equation, provided that the initial datum
\D(b_{0})=0
is also divergence-free:

\partial_{t}\D(b)=\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\big(u_{k}b_{j}-b_{k}u_{j}\big)=0,

where, once again, there is an implicit summation on both repeated indices
j,k=1,...,d
.

By using a suitable change of variables, introduced by the German-American Physicist W. M. Elsässer
[
12
]
, we may see system (
33
) as a system of transport equations: define the
Elsässer variables
by

(34)

\alpha=u+b\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=u-b.

By adding and substracting the momentum and magnetic field equations, we see that these variables solve the new problem, which we will refer to as the
Elsässer system
,

(35)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\alpha+\D(\beta\otimes\alpha)+\nabla\pi_{1}=0\\
\partial_{t}\beta+\D(\alpha\otimes\beta)+\nabla\pi_{2}=0\\
\D(\alpha)=\D(\beta)=0.\end{cases}

In the previous system,
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are two (
a priori
distinct) scalar functions which enforce the independent divergence-free conditions
\D(\alpha)=\D(\beta)=0
. In contrast to (
33
), the Elsässer system (
35
) has the same number
2d+2
of equations as of unknowns, which are
(\alpha,\beta,\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
. This difference is explained by noting that the structure of system (
35
) implies that we must have, say under mild integrability assumptions (see
[
9
]
and Theorem
53
below), the equality of the “pressure” functions
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}
. This is consistent with the fact that, whenever we sum and substract the first two equations of (
33
), we obtain (
35
) with
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=\pi+\frac{1}{2}|b|^{2}
.

As a set of transport equations, the Elsässer system is much easier to deal with than the “classical” ideal MHD system, since (
33
) only is (up to the non-local pressure term) a quasi-linear symmetric hyperbolic system
10
10

10

In contrast with transport equations, the basic theory of such systems only exists in
L^{2}
based spaces. We refer to
[
6
]
for more insight on the this: a symmetric hyperbolic system with constant coefficients is well-posed in
L^{p}
,
p\neq 2
, if and only if the matrix coefficients all commute.
. As a matter of fact, all well-posedness results obtained for the ideal MHD equations have used, in one way or another, this change of variables (see
[
31
]
,
[
32
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
9
]
).

The catch is that, although systems (
33
) and (
35
) are closely related,
they are not, strictly speaking, equivalent
. In
[
9
]
(see Section 4), we had shown that equivalence between the two systems holds in the framework of
L^{p}
solutions (with
p<+\infty
), and that it does not in for
L^{\infty}
solutions.

Let us explain further how this works: if
(\alpha,\beta)
is a solution of (
35
) with pressures
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, then by setting
(u,b)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta)
we construct a solution
u
of the momentum equation with MHD pressure
\pi=\frac{1}{2}(\pi_{1}+\pi_{2})
and a solution
b
of

(36)

\partial_{t}b+\D(u\otimes b-b\otimes u)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})

so that
b
solves the magnetic field equation if and only if
\nabla\pi_{1}=\nabla\pi_{2}
. Of course, there is not much possibility for this equality not holding: by taking the divergence of (
36
), we see that the difference
Q=\pi_{2}-\pi_{1}
is a harmonic polynomial

\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(u_{j}b_{k}-u_{k}b_{j})=0,

but this does not imply that
Q=0
. Let us give an example inspired by the uniform flow (
2
) presented above: consider

(37)

\alpha(t,x)=f(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta(t,x)=-f(t),

where
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
a smooth function. Then
(\alpha,\beta)
solves the Elsässer system with pressure functions
\pi_{1}(t,x)=-f^{\prime}(t)\cdot x=-\pi_{2}(t,x)
but we have
\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})=f^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
. We see that we have the same kind of issue here we had with the Euler system above: in other words, though a solution of the ideal MHD equations (
33
) always define a solution of the Elsässer system (
35
) through (
34
), the converse is false and a solution of (
35
) does not necessarily provide a solution of the ideal MHD equations (
33
) through the reverse transformations
(u,b)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta)
.

Our question is the following:
on what condition does a bounded solution of (
35
) also define a solution of the ideal MHD system (
33
)?

Remark 48
.

The solution (
37
) displayed above offers an interesting discussion as to whether it is physically relevant. Written in physical variables, it reads

(38)

u(t,x)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad b(t,x)=2f(t).

On the one hand, it would seem that this solution has an obvious physical interpretation: a uniform magnetic field may be created in the interior of an infinite ideal cylindrical solenoid, so that (
38
) could be seen as generated by solenoidal-type currents “at infinity”.

However, it turns out that this is an acceptable solution of the Maxwell equations (even in the non-relativistic regime suitable for MHD, see
[
23
]
) only for non-variable fields
f^{\prime}(t)=0
. Because time variations of the magnetic field induce a nonzero electromotive force (by Faraday’s law), the presence of an electric field
e
only is possible if the fluid is in motion, as
e=-u\times b
.

6.1
Weak Solutions of the MHD equations

In this paragraph, we define the appropriate notions of weak solutions of systems (
33
) and (
35
). We start by the usual MHD system (
33
) before doing so for the Elsässer system.

Definition 49
.

Let
T>0
and
u_{0},b_{0}\in L^{\infty}
be two divergence-free functions. A couple
(u,b)\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
is said to be a
bounded weak solution
of (
33
), related to the initial data
(u_{0},b_{0})
if there exists a (MHD) pressure
\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(i)

the momentum equation is satisfied in the sense of distributions:

\partial_{t}u+\D(u\otimes u-b\otimes b)+\nabla\pi=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes u_{0}(x)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\,;

we refer to Definition
26
for the meaning of the tensor product
\delta_{0}\otimes u_{0}\;
;

(ii)

the magnetic field equation is satisfied in the sense of distributions:

\partial_{t}b+\D(u\otimes b-b\otimes u)=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes b_{0}(x)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(iii)

the divergence-free condition
\D(u)=0
holds in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(]0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 50
.

Let
T>0
and
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\in L^{\infty}
be two divergence-free functions. A couple
(\alpha,\beta)\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}([0,T[;L^{\infty})
is said to be a
bounded weak solution
of (
35
) related to the initial data
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
if there exist two pressure functions
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(i)

the equation for
\alpha
is satisfied in the sense of distributions:

\partial_{t}\alpha+\D(\beta\otimes\alpha)+\nabla\pi_{1}=\delta_{0}(t)\otimes\alpha_{0}(x)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and likewise for
\beta
;

(ii)

both divergence-free conditions
\D(\alpha)=\D(\beta)=0
hold in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(]0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 51
.

As above with the Euler system, we have the same ambiguity concerning initial data. If
(u,b)
is as in Definition
49
, then there is no need for
u_{0}
and
u(0)
to be the same, even if the solution lies in
C^{1}_{T}(L^{\infty})
. Likewise, there is no reason for
u
to be continuous with respect to time. However, the magnetic field has a much more pleasant behavior: it automatically lies in
W^{1,1}_{T}(W^{-1,\infty})
, and we have
b_{0}=b(0)
.

As for the Elsässer variables
(\alpha,\beta)
in Definition
50
, they share the same problems the Euler solutions may have: both initial values
\alpha(0)
and
\beta(0)
may differ from the initial data
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}
, and both
\alpha
and
\beta
may be discontinuous with respect to time.

Remark 52
.

As we have noted in
[
9
]
(Section 4.1), to define weak solutions, it is only necessary, strictly speaking, that
u\otimes u-b\otimes b
and
u\otimes b-b\otimes u
be in
L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Interestingly, this is absolutely equivalent to
\alpha\otimes\beta
being locally integrable. But we will not need such a level of generality.

6.2
Equivalence of the Systems

The following statement is a sharp result for the equivalence of systems (
33
) and (
35
) in the framework of bounded solutions.

Theorem 53
.

Let
T>0
,
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\in L^{\infty}
and
(\alpha,\beta)\in C^{0}\big([0,T[;L^{\infty}\big)
. Define
(u,b)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta)
, and
(u_{0},b_{0})
accordingly. The assertions below are true.

1.

Assume that
(u,b)
is a weak solution of (
33
) that is related to the initial data
(u_{0},b_{0})
, in the sense of Definition
49
. Then
(\alpha,\beta)
solves the Elsässer system (
35
) with initial data
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
and
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=\pi
.

2.

Assume that
(\alpha,\beta)
is a weak solution of (
35
) for the initial data
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the functions
(u,b)
solve the “classical” MHD system (
33
) with initial data
(u_{0},b(0))
, and the difference
b(0)-b_{0}
is a constant function;

(ii)

we have, for all times
t\in[0,T[
,
b(t)-b(0)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}
.

In addition, if one of these equivalent conditions is fulfilled, then the pressure gradients
\nabla\pi_{1}
and
\nabla\pi_{2}
are equal.

Proof.

Because the proof of this theorem bears strong similarities with that of Theorem
33
, we only give an outline of the arguments.

If
(u,b)
is a weak solution of (
33
), then adding and subtracting the weak forms of the momentum and magnetic field equations makes it clear that
(\alpha,\beta)
solves the Elsässer system with initial data
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
with
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}
.

On the other hand, assume that
(\alpha,\beta)
is a solution of the Elsässer system (
35
), according to Definition
50
, and with the initial data
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
. As in the proof of Theorem
33
, extend the solution
(\alpha,\beta,\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
to
]-\infty,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and continue to note
(\alpha,\beta,\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
the extension. By subtracting both equations for
\alpha
and
\beta
, we see that the magnetic field satisfies

\partial_{t}b+\D(u\otimes b-b\otimes u)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})+\delta_{0}(t)\otimes b_{0}(x).

As with
C^{0}_{T}(L^{\infty})
solutions of the Euler system, we apply the same mollification sequence
\big(K_{\epsilon}\big)_{\epsilon>0}
in order to deal with low regularity in the time variable. We have, by noting
(u_{\epsilon},b_{\epsilon})=K_{\epsilon}*(u,b)
the convolution with respect to time,

\partial_{t}b_{\epsilon}+K_{\epsilon}*\D(u\otimes b-b\otimes u)=\frac{1}{2}K_{\epsilon}*\nabla(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})+K_{\epsilon}(t)b_{0}(x)\qquad\text{in }]-\infty,T-\epsilon[\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By taking the divergence of this equation, we see that
K_{\epsilon}*\Delta(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})=0
, hence the functions
Q_{\epsilon}(t)=K_{\epsilon}*(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})(t)\in\mathbb{R}[X]

\nabla Q_{\epsilon}=K_{\epsilon}*\nabla Q
for some distribution
\nabla Q\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\nabla Q=0
in

We now notice that the quadratic term in the magnetic field equation lies in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}

\left\|\chi(\lambda D)\D(u\otimes b-b\otimes u)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

Therefore, by arguing with

exactly as with
u
at the end of the proof of Theorem

(i)
and
(ii)
.

As with the Euler equations, well-posedness of a projected version of the ideal MHD system has been studied in the critical Besov space

\big(\alpha(0),\beta(0)\big)=(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(\alpha(t)-\alpha(0),\beta(t)-\beta(0)\big)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{h}\text{ for }t\in[0,T[,

Remark 54
.

In
[

and
[

Firstly, ideal MHD, just as the Euler equations, is invariant under the operation of the Galileo group, provided the unknowns transform as

u^{\prime}(t,x)=u(t,x-Vt)+V,\quad b^{\prime}(t,x)=b(t,x-Vt),\quad\pi^{\prime}(t,x)=\pi(t,x-Vt).

This set of transformations is consistent with the non-relativistic nature of the MHD equations: they are deduced from the Euler system (which follows from Newtonian mechanics) and the

of the Maxwell equations, that is the non-relativistic limit of the Maxwell equations in which the magnetic field dominates over the electric field

23

As a consequence, it is true that every solution
