Spinor representation in isotropic 3-space via Laguerre geometry
Joseph Cho, Dami Lee, Wonjoo Lee, Seong-Deog Yang

Abstract
. We give a detailed description of the geometry of isotropic space, in parallel to those of Euclidean space within the realm of Laguerre geometry. After developing basic surface theory in isotropic space, we define spin transformations, directly leading to the spinor representation of conformal surfaces in isotropic space. As an application, we obtain the Weierstrass-type representation for zero mean curvature surfaces, and the Kenmotsu-type representation for constant mean curvature surfaces, allowing us to construct many explicit examples.

Primary 53A10; Secondary 53B30.

1.
Introduction

The Erlanger program
[
17
]
of Klein was epochal to the field of differential geometry for obtaining space form geometries via
transformation groups
, also investigated in his two papers
[
16
,
18
]
.
The central idea was to present
projective geometry
as the commonground for various known space form geometries.
In particular, applying the ideas of Cayley
[
6
]
, Klein showed a way to define metrics in the realm of projective geometry by restricting to projective transformations that leave a certain quadric invariant.
This was referred to as the
absolute
by Cayley.
By doing so, Klein unified Euclidean geometry with the various non-Euclidean geometries including hyperbolic geometry and elliptical geometry as subgeometries of projective geometry.

Researchers found other ways to choose the absolute; the geometry of isotropic plane is one of such
Cayley-Klein geometries
, first studied in works such as
[
3
,
35
,
4
]
.
The study of isotropic
3
-space followed soon, most notably by Strubecker, covering the basics of isotropic geometry
[
29
]
, space curve theory
[
30
]
, and surface theory
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
Many modern expositions on the topic of isotropic plane and isotropic space including
[
38
,
27
]
also follow such approach of Klein.
Recently, the geometry of isotropic space has gained interest, most notably for their applicability to architectural geometry
[
25
]
(see also, for example,
[
37
,
15
,
21
,
36
]
).

On the other hand,
spin transformations
for conformal surfaces in Euclidean space were defined in
[
13
]
by relating two conformally equivalent surfaces via homotheties and rotations of the corresponding tangent planes.
Spin transformations were used to characterize
Bonnet pairs
via isothermic surfaces, a class of surfaces that admit conformal curvature coordinates.
The
quaternionic description
of rotation in Euclidean space proved to be central to obtaining the Dirac-type equation as the compatibility condition for spin transformations
[
13
]
, and deriving
spinor representations
of conformal surfaces
[
19
]
via spin transformations
[
9
]
.

In this paper, we propose
Laguerre geometry
as a basis for understanding isotropic space and surface theory within, which in itself is closely related to the approach of Klein.
Laguerre geometry
[
20
]
is concerned with the set of transformations in Euclidean space that maps points (viewed as zero radius spheres) and spheres to points and spheres, and planes to planes. (For details, see
[
5
,
7
]
, for example.)
The corresponding Laguerre geometry of isotropic geometry was given in
[
10
,
26
,
24
]
; our primary motivation for using Laguerre geometry is to unify the approaches to Euclidean geometry and isotropic geometry.
Thus in Section
2
, we show that the Laguerre geometric description of Euclidean space carries over to isotropic space by viewing both space forms as hyperplanes in the Minkowski
4
-space (see also
[
23
,
22
]
).
In particular, we carefully review the notions of Laguerre geometry in Euclidean space in Section
2.1
, where the Euclidean notions will provide valuable intuition for the definitions of points, spheres, planes, and normals in isotropic space, which we introduce in Section
2.2
.
In doing so, we also check that our definitions are in harmony with the classical definitions given in
[
29
]
or
[
25
]
.
Such approach allows for the geometry of isotropic space to be understood in parallel to that of Euclidean space, without any prior knowledge of projective geometry.

Laguerre geometric approach to isotropic space turns out to be highly suitable for surface theoretic considerations, which is the center of our attention in Section
3
.
By viewing surfaces as codimension two immersions in Minkowski
4
-space, we use the notion of lightcone Gauss maps of
[
12
]
, which we call

after
[
23
]

This allows us to recover the structure equations of Gauss and Weingarten, given by Strubecker in
[

]
(see Section

).

Furthermore, the well-known isomorphism between Minkowski
4
-space and Hermitian matrices allows us to use the matrix group

to obtain a quaternion-like description of rotations in isotropic
3
-space.
Thus, after converting the structure equations in terms of Hermitian matrices in Section

, we define

of conformal surfaces in isotropic
3
-space in Definition

as the compatibility condition in Theorem

We note here that since surfaces in isotropic

[

(see also
[

]

Using spin transformations, we then obtain the spinor representation of conformal surfaces in isotropic

[

]
(see Remark

As an application, we recover the Weierstrass-type representation for minimal surfaces

(see also

We also obtain the Kenmotsu-type representation for non-zero constant mean curvature (cmc) surfaces in isotropic

A standout feature of the Kenmotsu-type representation for cmc surfaces in isotropic

2.
Geometry of isotropic
3
-space

The parallels between the geometry of isotropic
3
-space and Euclidean
3
-space cannot be understood using the approach involving inner products, since the inner product of isotropic
3
-space is degenerate.
Thus to see the parallelism between the two spaces, we need to understand both geometries without any dependence on metrics.
We propose
Laguerre geometry

[
20
]
as the commonground for understanding the geometry of both Euclidean and isotropic
3
-spaces without any use of inner products (see also
[
22
, Example 3.3]
).

In this section, we first give a detailed Laguerre geometric description of basic geometric objects such as planes, spheres, and normals in Euclidean
3
-space without dependence on the standard Euclidean metric, where Euclidean geometric intuition will serve as justifications for the definitions.
We then use the intuition gained from the Euclidean case to define the analogous geometric objects in isotropic
3
-space using Laguerre geometry, making connections to previously known descriptions of isotropic
3
-space.

2.1.
Laguerre geometry of Euclidean
3
-space

First, we give an account of the Laguerre geometric description of Euclidean
3
-space (see also
[
7
, §3.4]
or
[
5
, 4. Kapitel]
).
Let
\mathbb{L}^{4}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
denote the Minkowski
4
-space with the given metric

\langle x,y\rangle=\langle(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{t},(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})^{t}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+x_{3}y_{3}.

Choosing a unit timelike vector
\mathfrak{p}

\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}\rangle=-1

\mathbb{L}^{4}=\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}\oplus\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle\cong\mathbb{E}^{3}\oplus\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle

We note here that any vector
x\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
can be expressed as

x=x_{E}+\alpha\mathfrak{p}

for some
x_{E}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\pi_{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{L}^{4}\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
be the orthoprojection, that is,
\pi_{E}x=x_{E}

Explicitly, one can normalize
\mathfrak{p}=(1,0,0,0)^{t}
with a coordinate chart
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\}\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
so that

\mathbb{E}^{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})^{t}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}\}.

2.1.2.
Contact elements and normals

A sphere is in
oriented contact
with a plane if and only if the affine lightcone
\mathcal{L}_{s}
defining the sphere is tangent to the isotropic hyperplane
I_{g,q}
defining the plane.
Thus a sphere
S
defined by
s\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
and plane
P
defined by
I_{g,q}
are in oriented contact if and only if
s\in I_{g,q}
(see Figure
3(a)
).

Now suppose that
s\in I_{g,q}
, and let
L
be the affine line along which
I_{g,q}
is tangent to
\mathcal{L}_{s}
, i.e.
L=I_{g,q}\cap\mathcal{L}_{s}
.
For any
\tilde{s}\in L
, we have that

\langle\tilde{s},g\rangle=q\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\tilde{s}-s,\tilde{s}-s\rangle=0.

Since
s\in I_{g,q}
implies that
\langle s,g\rangle=q
, we note that

\langle\tilde{s}-s,g\rangle=0,

implying that
\tilde{s}-s
and
g
are both null vectors that are orthogonal to each other.
Thus,
\tilde{s}-s=ag
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
, that is,
L
is an affine null line in the direction of
g
.

Defining
x=L\cap\mathbb{E}^{3}
, we see that
S
is in oriented contact with
P
at the point
x
(see Figure
3(b)

Furthermore, any other
\tilde{s}\in L

in oriented contact with

at the point

Thus, an affine null line

\mathcal{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\text{affine null lines in $\mathbb{L}^{4}$}\}.

2.1.3.
Surfaces and their contact elements

Let
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
be an immersion defined on some simply-connected domain
\Sigma
.
For every point
p\in\Sigma
, the tangent plane to
x
at
x(p)
, denoted by
T_{x(p)}x
, gives rise to a contact element of
T_{x(p)}x
at
x(p)
, represented by an affine null line
L(p)\in\mathcal{Z}
.
The contact element
L(p)
also gives rise to the unit normal
\nu(p)
to
T_{x(p)}x
at
x(p)
, so that
\nu\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to S^{2}\subset\mathbb{E}^{3}
is the Gauss map of
x
.
Thus a surface, together with its contact elements, can be thought of as a map into the set of contact elements
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{Z}
, which we call the
contact lift
of
x
.
In fact, we have

(2.5)

L=x+\langle\nu+\mathfrak{p}\rangle.

Now, we aim to understand when a surface can be recovered from a map into the set of contact elements
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{Z}
, where we view
L
as a null line bundle.
For this, we first define the following notions:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{Z}
be a map into the set of contact elements, and let
G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
such that
G(p)\parallel L(p)
for every
p\in\Sigma
.

L
is called a
Legendre map
or a
frontal
if
L
satisfies the contact condition on
\Sigma
:

(2.6)

\langle\dif{\ell},g\rangle=0\text{ for any sections $\ell\in\Gamma L$ and $g\in\Gamma G$.}

A Legendre map
L
further satisfying the immersion condition on
\Sigma
:

(2.7)

|{\dif{\ell}}|^{2}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle\dif{\ell}(X),\dif{\ell}(X)\rangle=0\text{ for any $\ell\in\Gamma L$ implies $X=0$}

is called a
Legendre immersion
, or a
\emph{front}
.

From a Legendre map
L
, one can recover the surface by taking the intersection with
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, i.e.
x=L\cap\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
.
Under the assumption that

G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
be as in Definition

, and consider the affine hyperplane
\Pi

\mathbb{E}^{3}

\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{x\in\mathbb{L}^{4}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\langle x,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=-1\}.

Let

be defined via

, that is,

with
\langle g,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=-1
.
Then we have

for some

.
Therefore,

To justify our Definition
2.2

Now, let
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{Z}
with

, and define

.
Again, let

\langle g,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=-1
, and define

To consider the contact condition (
2.6

, and let

Then there is a function

satisfying

Therefore,

\langle\dif{\ell},g\rangle=\langle\dif{x}+\dif{\alpha}\,g+\alpha\dif{\nu},g\rangle=\langle\dif{x},\nu\rangle.

Hence we have that

For the immersion condition (

Equivalently, we have for any

(2.8)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\dif{x}(X)+\dif{\alpha}(X)\,g+\alpha\dif{\nu}(X),\dif{x}(X)+\dif{\alpha}\,g+\alpha\dif{\nu}(X)\rangle

\displaystyle=|{\dif{x}}|^{2}(X)+2\alpha\langle\dif{x}(X),\dif{\nu}(X)\rangle+\alpha^{2}|{\dif{\nu}}|^{2}(X)

2.1.4.

Finally, denoting by

, the isometries of

2.1.5.
Summary

We conclude this section by providing a summary of the Laguerre representatives of Euclidean objects:

•

The space form
\mathbb{E}^{3}
is obtained via a timelike point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
through the straightforward isomorphism
\mathbb{E}^{3}\cong\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}
.

•

An oriented sphere with center
c\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
and radius
r\in\mathbb{R}
is represented by
s=c+r\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
, and recovered by taking the intersection of
\mathbb{E}^{3}
with the affine lightcone centered at
s
.

•

An oriented plane
P
is represented by an isotropic hyperplane
I_{g,q}
determined by a null vector
g
and a constant
q
.

•

A contact element (elliptic sphere pencil) is represented by an affine null line
L
.

•

The normal line
N
to the plane
P
is given by the orthoprojection of an affine null line
L\subset I_{g,q}
.

•

A surface together with its tangent bundle is given by the map into the set of contact elements
\mathcal{Z}
.

2.2.
Laguerre geometry of isotropic
3
-space

Now we turn our attention to isotropic
3
-space and define its basic geometric objects analogously to those of the Euclidean case using Laguerre geometry summarized in Subsection
2.1.5
.

Choosing a point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
so that
\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}\rangle=0
, the isotropic
3
-space
\mathbb{I}^{3}
can be defined via

\mathbb{I}^{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{x\in\mathbb{L}^{4}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\langle x,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=0\}.

Now we let
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathcal{L}
with
\langle\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle=1
, so that

\mathbb{L}^{4}=\mathbb{I}^{3}\oplus\langle\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle.

Then any
x\in\mathbb{L}^{4}
can now be written as

x=x_{I}+\alpha\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

for
x_{I}\in\mathbb{I}^{3}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.2.5.
Isometries in isotropic space – Hermitian matrix model

Finally, the isometries of isotropic
3
-space fixing the origin are given analogously by all
A\in\mathrm{SO}(3,1)
such that
A\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}
.
Recalling that a spacelike plane
P
through the origin is defined by
I_{g,0}
for lightlike
g\not\in\mathbb{I}^{3}
, it is straightforward to observe that:

Lemma 2.10
.

In isotropic
3
-space, there is only one spacelike plane through the origin up to origin-fixing isometries.

On the other hand, such isometries can be described explicitly when using the Hermitian matrix model of isotropic
3
-space, which we explain now.
The set of
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices denoted by
\mathrm{Herm}(2)
is given by

\mathrm{Herm}(2)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}+x_{3}&x_{1}+ix_{2}\\
x_{1}-ix_{2}&x_{0}-x_{3}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

and we identify
\mathbb{L}^{4}\cong\mathrm{Herm}(2)
via

x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{t}\sim\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}+x_{3}&x_{1}+ix_{2}\\
x_{1}-ix_{2}&x_{0}-x_{3}\end{pmatrix}=X.

Under this identification, we have

\langle x,x\rangle=-\det X.

Thus the action of
F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
on
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)
via

X\mapsto FXF^{*},

amounts to an origin-fixing isometry of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
, where
F^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose of
F
.
Since
F
and
-F
are the same action, we have that
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is a double cover of
\mathrm{SO}(3,1)
.

The Hermitian matrix model of isotropic
3
-space is then given by

\mathbb{I}^{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}2x_{0}&x_{1}+ix_{2}\\
x_{1}-ix_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Noting that

\mathfrak{p}=(1,0,0,1)^{t}\sim\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\mathfrak{P},

we have that for
F=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
,

F\mathfrak{P}F^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}2a\bar{a}&2a\bar{c}\\
2\bar{a}c&2c\bar{c}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, if
F
is an isometry of isotropic
3
-space fixing the origin, that is,
F\mathfrak{P}F^{*}=\mathfrak{P}
, then we have
c=0
and
a=e^{i\theta}
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Furthermore, since
F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, we must have that

1=ad-bc=e^{i\theta}d

so that
d=e^{-i\theta}
.
Summarizing:

Lemma 2.11
.

The action of
F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
on
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)
is an isometry of isotropic
3
-space fixing the origin if and only if
F\in\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
where

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{F\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}F\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&\alpha+i\beta\\
0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\alpha,\beta,\theta\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

With such explicit parametrization of
\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
, we can also parametrize the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(1,0,1)
:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{su}(1,0,1)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\trace{A}=0,A\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}A^{*}=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}ia&z\\
0&-ia\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a\in\mathbb{R},z\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

We state the following corollary which will be useful later on:

Corollary 2.12
.

If
F\in\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
, then

F^{*}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The result follows directly using calculation with explicit parametrization given in (
2.14
).
∎

3.
Spin transformations and spinor representation

In this section, we consider the spin transformations of conformally immersed spacelike surfaces in isotropic
3
-space, analogously to those of Euclidean
3
-space defined in
[
13
, Definition 2.1]
.
After recovering the structure equations using the Minkowski model of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
, we utilize the Hermitian matrix model to define spin transformations since the group of origin-fixing isometries play an important role in the definition.
Using spin transformations, we recover the spinor representation of conformal immersions in isotropic
3
-space, and obtain the Weierstrass-type representation for minimal surfaces and the Kenmotsu-type representation for (non-zero) constant mean curvature (cmc) surfaces as an application.

3.1.
Basic surface theory of spacelike surfaces

Suppose that
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
is a spacelike immersion, i.e. the induced metric from the ambient space
\mathbb{L}^{4}
is Riemannian.
Thus, we may assume that
(u,v)\in\Sigma
are conformal coordinates, allowing us to introduce a complex structure via
z=u+iv
, and let

\partial_{z}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{u}-i\partial_{v}),\quad\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{u}+i\partial_{v})

denote the Wirtinger derivatives.
Then the metric of the surface can be written as

\dif{s}^{2}=e^{2\sigma}(\dif{u}^{2}+\dif{v}^{2})=e^{2\sigma}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}=2\langle x_{z},x_{\bar{z}}\rangle\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}

for some function
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
.

The key difference from the Euclidean case arises in the consideration of the second fundamental form: the Gauss map
\nu
of the surface in isotropic
3
-space is defined via contact, not metric, and thus,
\langle\dif{x},\nu\rangle\neq 0
in general.
To overcome this issue, we take advantage of viewing isotropic
3
-space within Minkowski
4
-space: we treat spacelike surfaces in isotropic
3
-space as spacelike surfaces (of codimension two) in Minkowski
4
-space with a flat normal bundle.
Every fiber of the normal bundle is spanned by a constant vector
\mathfrak{p}
and the lightlike Gauss map
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}
of
x
, i.e.
\langle g,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=1
, since Lemma
2.9
tells us that the fact that
x
is an immersion implies
\langle\dif{x},g\rangle=0
.
In other words,
\mathfrak{p}
and
g
constitute a null basis of the fibers of the normal bundle.

Furthermore, since
\mathfrak{p}
is a constant section of the normal bundle, we only need to consider the lightlike Gauss map
g
for the second fundamental form; thus, we define the coefficients of the second fundamental form as

L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle x_{uu},g\rangle,\quad M\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle x_{uv},g\rangle,\quad N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle x_{vv},g\rangle.

Remark 3.1
.

Since
g=\nu+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}
for the Gauss map
\nu
, we have

\dif{g}=\dif{\nu},

so that the coefficients of the second fundamental form can also be calculated from the Gauss map
\nu
via

L=-\langle x_{u},\nu_{u}\rangle,\quad M=-\langle x_{u},\nu_{v}\rangle=-\langle x_{v},\nu_{u}\rangle,\quad N=-\langle x_{v},\nu_{v}\rangle.

The shape operator
S
satisfies

S=e^{-2\sigma}\begin{pmatrix}L&M\\
M&N\end{pmatrix},

so that the mean curvature
H
is

(3.1)

H\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}\trace S=\frac{1}{2e^{2\sigma}}(L+N)=2e^{-2\sigma}\langle x_{z\bar{z}},g\rangle,

while the Hopf differential can be defined by

Q\dif{z}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{4}(L-N-2iM)\dif{z}^{2}=\langle x_{zz},g\rangle\dif{z}^{2}.

Now we use
\{x_{z},x_{\bar{z}},\mathfrak{p},g\}
as a basis to calculate the Gauss-Weingarten equations:

(3.2)

\begin{cases}x_{zz}=2\sigma_{z}x_{z}+Q\mathfrak{p}\\
x_{z\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{2\sigma}H\mathfrak{p}\\
g_{z}=-Hx_{z}-2e^{-2\sigma}Qx_{\bar{z}}.\end{cases}

Note that the coefficient of
g
always vanishes; therefore, we can treat the Gauss-Weingarten equations (
3.2
) purely within the scope of isotropic
3
-space
[
32
, § 72]
.
The Gauss equation and the Codazzi equation follow:

\sigma_{z\bar{z}}=0,\quad H_{z}=2e^{-2\sigma}Q_{\bar{z}}.

Remark 3.2
.

Noting that the (extrinsic) Gaussian curvature is given by

K\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\det S=H^{2}-4e^{-4\sigma}Q\bar{Q},

we see that the Gauss equation implies that the Gaussian curvature is extrinsic.
This is a fact noted by Strubecker in
[
31
, § 27]
, where
K
is called the
relative curvature
.

Remark 3.3
.

The calculation of the structure equations for surfaces in Euclidean space can be carried out similarly: Namely, viewing the surface in Minkowski
4
-space (having codimension two), one can note that the fibers of the normal bundle are spanned by the Euclidean Gauss map
\nu
and the timelike point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
.
Since
\mathfrak{p}
is again a constant section of the normal bundle, the second fundamental form is calculated using
\nu
.
The Gauss-Weingarten equations can also be calculated using the basis
\{x_{z},x_{\bar{z}},\nu,\mathfrak{p}\}
; however, in the Euclidean case, the coefficient of
\mathfrak{p}
vanishes, resulting in the usual Gauss-Weingarten equations for surfaces in Euclidean space expressed in terms of
\{x_{z},x_{\bar{z}},\nu\}
.

For spacelike surfaces in isotropic
3
-space, the coordinate function for the vertical direction is directly related to the mean curvature:

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that a spacelike surface
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
is parametrized via

x(u,v)=(\mathbf{l}(u,v),\mathbf{x}(u,v),\mathbf{y}(u,v),\mathbf{l}(u,v))^{t}.

Then we have

\mathbf{l}_{z\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{2\sigma}H.

Proof.

Note that since
\langle x,\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle=\mathbf{l}
, we have that
\mathbf{l}_{z\bar{z}}=\langle x_{z\bar{z}},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle
.
Thus,

\mathbf{l}_{z\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{4}\langle x_{uu}+x_{vv},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle=\frac{1}{4}(L+N),

giving us the desired conclusion.
∎

Isometries fixing the origin are central to the definition of spin transformations
[
13
, Definition 2.1]
; thus, we will use the Hermitian matrix model of
\mathbb{I}^{3}
as the switch allows us to write the isometries explicitly as in Lemma
2.11
.
For this, we first rewrite the Gauss-Weingarten equations (
3.2
) within the context of Hermitian matrix model.

Choosing the basis of
\mathbb{L}^{4}
as
\{e_{1},e_{2},\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\}
for

e_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(0,1,0,0)^{t},\quad e_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(0,0,1,0)^{t},

we see that they correspond to

E_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathfrak{P}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

in the Hermitian matrix model, respectively.

Now, let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
such that

(3.3)

X_{u}=e^{\sigma}FE_{1}F^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{v}=e^{\sigma}FE_{2}F^{*}.

Since
F\subset\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
implies that

\mathfrak{P}=F\mathfrak{P}F^{*},

we then have

G=F\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}F^{*},

where
G
is the lightlike Gauss map in the Hermitian matrix model.

Thus, using
X_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{u}-iX_{v})=e^{\sigma}F\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}F^{*}
, we may rewrite the structure equations (
3.2
) as

(3.4)

\begin{cases}X_{zz}=F\left(2\sigma_{z}e^{\sigma}\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+Q\mathfrak{P}\right)F^{*}=F\begin{pmatrix}2Q&2\sigma_{z}e^{\sigma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{*}\\
X_{z\bar{z}}=F\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{2\sigma}H\mathfrak{P}\right)F^{*}=F\begin{pmatrix}e^{2\sigma}H&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{*}\\
G_{z}=F\left(-e^{\sigma}H\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}-2e^{-\sigma}Q\frac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}\right)F^{*}=F\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{\sigma}H\\
-2e^{-\sigma}Q&0\end{pmatrix}F^{*}.\end{cases}

On the other hand, differentiating
X_{z}
and
G
, we also obtain that

(3.5)

\begin{cases}X_{zz}=e^{\sigma}F\left(\sigma_{z}\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+F^{-1}F_{z}\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}(F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}})^{*}\right)F^{*}\\
X_{z\bar{z}}=e^{\sigma}F\left(\sigma_{\bar{z}}\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}}\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+\frac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}(F^{-1}F_{z})^{*}\right)F^{*}\\
G_{z}=F(F^{-1}F_{z}\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}+\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}(F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}})^{*})F^{*}.\end{cases}

Since
F^{-1}\dif{F}
is an
\mathfrak{su}(1,0,1)
-valued
1
–form, we use (
2.15
) to write

F^{-1}F_{z}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&-A\end{pmatrix},\quad F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&-a\end{pmatrix}

for some
A,B,a,b\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
.
Now comparing the different expressions for
X_{zz},X_{z\bar{z}},G_{z}
in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we obtain the relations

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2e^{-\sigma}Q&2\sigma_{z}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{b}&\sigma_{z}+A-\bar{a}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}e^{\sigma}H&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{B}&\sigma_{\bar{z}}+a-\bar{A}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{\sigma}H\\
-2e^{-\sigma}Q&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-B\\
-\bar{b}&A+\bar{a}\end{pmatrix},

allowing us to solve for
A,B,a,b
, and conclude that

(3.6)

F^{-1}F_{z}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}&e^{\sigma}H\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}\end{pmatrix},\quad F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\bar{z}}&2e^{-\sigma}\bar{Q}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}.

3.2.
Spin transformation and Dirac–type operator

Spin transformations relate two conformally equivalent surfaces via homotheties and rotations of the corresponding tangent planes.
Since rotations fixing the origin are given by
\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
, we let

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbb{R}^{+}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)

where
\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes the set of positive real numbers.
We define spin transformations of a conformal immersion as follows:

Definition 3.5

(cf.
[
13
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

Let
X,\tilde{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be conformal immersions.
Then
\tilde{X}
is called a
spin transformation
of
X
if there exists some
B\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{G}
such that

(3.7)

\dif{\tilde{X}}=B\dif{X}B^{*}.

Suppose that we wish to find spin transformations
\tilde{X}
of
X
using (
3.7
).
For such
\tilde{X}
to exist, we need
\dif{(\dif{\tilde{X}})}=0
, or equivalently using coordinates,

\tilde{X}_{z\bar{z}}=\tilde{X}_{\bar{z}z}.

To see when
\dif{\tilde{X}}
is closed, note that for
\hat{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=BF
, (
3.7
) implies

\tilde{X}_{z}=BX_{z}B^{*}=e^{\sigma}\hat{F}\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}\hat{F}^{*},\quad\tilde{X}_{\bar{z}}=BX_{\bar{z}}B^{*}=e^{\sigma}\hat{F}\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}\hat{F}^{*},

allowing us to calculate

(3.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{z\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=e^{\sigma}\hat{F}\left(\sigma_{\bar{z}}\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{\bar{z}}\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}+\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}(\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{z})^{*}\right)\hat{F}^{*}

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{\bar{z}z}

\displaystyle=e^{\sigma}\hat{F}\left(\sigma_{z}\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}+\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{z}\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}+\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}(\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{\bar{z}})^{*}\right)\hat{F}^{*}.

Defining

\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{11}&\Omega_{12}\\
\Omega_{21}&\Omega_{22}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{z}F,\quad\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda_{11}&\Lambda_{12}\\
\Lambda_{21}&\Lambda_{22}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{\bar{z}}F,

we can verify that

\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{z}=\Omega+F^{-1}F_{z},\quad\hat{F}^{-1}\hat{F}_{\bar{z}}=\Lambda+F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}},

so that (
3.8
) implies that
\dif{\tilde{X}}
is closed if and only if

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Omega_{12}}+e^{\sigma}H&\Lambda_{11}+\overline{\Omega_{22}}\\
0&\Lambda_{21}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{12}+e^{\sigma}H&0\\
\overline{\Lambda_{11}}+\Omega_{22}&\overline{\Lambda_{21}}\end{pmatrix},

or equivalently,

(3.9)

\Omega_{12}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\Lambda_{21}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\overline{\Lambda_{11}}+\Omega_{22}=0.

Now suppose that
B\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{G}
such that the compatibility condition (
3.9
) is satisfied.
To see how the metric changes, we note that

(3.10)

e^{2\tilde{\sigma}}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\langle\dif{\tilde{x}},\dif{\tilde{x}}\rangle=-\det{\dif{\tilde{X}}}=-\det{(B\dif{X}B^{*})}=(\det{B})^{2}e^{2\sigma}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}.

Now, for some
\tilde{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
, we can write

\tilde{X}_{z}=e^{\tilde{\sigma}}\tilde{F}\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}\tilde{F}^{*},

so that
\tilde{X}
satisfies the analogous Gauss-Weingarten equations (
3.6
):

(3.11)

\tilde{F}^{-1}\tilde{F}_{z}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}&e^{\tilde{\sigma}}\tilde{H}\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{F}^{-1}\tilde{F}_{\bar{z}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}&2e^{-\tilde{\sigma}}\bar{\tilde{Q}}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}

where
\tilde{H}
and
\tilde{Q}
are the mean curvature and Hopf differential factor of
\tilde{X}
, respectively.
On the other hand, if we let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=fB\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
for some
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then we have

\tilde{F}=AF=fBF.

Thus we also have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{-1}\tilde{F}_{z}

\displaystyle=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{z}F+f^{-1}f_{z}I+F^{-1}F_{z},

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{-1}\tilde{F}_{\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{\bar{z}}F+f^{-1}f_{\bar{z}}I+F^{-1}F_{\bar{z}}.

Noting that since
\det{A}=1
, the change in metric (
3.10
) implies

f^{2}=\frac{1}{\det{B}}=e^{\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}},

so that

f^{-1}f_{z}=\frac{(f^{2})_{z}}{2f^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{z}-\tilde{\sigma}_{z}).

Therefore, comparing the two expressions for the logarithmic derivative of
\tilde{F}
(
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}&e^{\tilde{\sigma}}\tilde{H}\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{11}+\sigma_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}&\Omega_{12}+e^{\sigma}H\\
\Omega_{21}&\Omega_{22}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{z}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}&2e^{-\tilde{\sigma}}\bar{\tilde{Q}}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda_{11}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}&\Lambda_{12}+2e^{-\sigma}\bar{Q}\\
\Lambda_{21}&\Lambda_{22}+\sigma_{\bar{z}}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix},

so that

(3.13)

\Omega_{21}=\Omega_{22}=\Lambda_{11}=\Lambda_{21}=0,\quad\Omega_{11}=\overline{\Lambda_{22}},

while

\tilde{H}=\frac{\Omega_{12}+e^{\sigma}H}{e^{\sigma}\det{B}}=\frac{H+\rho}{\det{B}}

for

(3.14)

\rho=e^{-\sigma}\Omega_{12}.

Finally, we also note the change in the Hopf differential:

\tilde{Q}=\frac{1}{2}\det B(e^{\sigma}\overline{\Lambda_{12}}+2Q).

The compatibility condition on
\Omega
and
\Lambda
(
3.9
), and the subsequent equations (
3.13
), (
3.14
) can be expressed in terms of a single Dirac–type equation for isotropic
3
–space:

Theorem 3.6

(cf.
[
13
, Lemmata 2.3, 2.4]
)
.

Let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a conformal immersion with mean curvature
H
.
A spin transformation
\tilde{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
of
X
is well-defined if and only if
B\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{R}^{+}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
satisfies the Dirac–type equation for isotropic
3
–space, that is, for some
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
,

(3.15)

B^{-1}\dif{B}\wedge\dif{X}=-\rho\dif{X}\wedge\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}\dif{X},

where
\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
.
Furthermore, the mean curvature
\tilde{H}
of
\tilde{X}
can be obtained via

(3.16)

\tilde{H}=\frac{H+\rho}{\det{B}}.

Proof.

We only need to see that the Dirac–type equation (
3.15
) is equivalent to the compatibility condition for
\tilde{X}
(
3.9
).
To do this, first we calculate using coordinates that

\displaystyle B^{-1}\dif{B}\wedge\dif{X}

\displaystyle=(B^{-1}B_{z}X_{\bar{z}}-B^{-1}B_{\bar{z}}X_{z})(\dif{z}\wedge\dif{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=e^{\sigma}F(\Omega\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}-\Lambda\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2})F^{*}(\dif{z}\wedge\dif{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=e^{\sigma}F\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{12}&-\Lambda_{11}\\
\Omega_{22}&-\Lambda_{21}\end{pmatrix}F^{*}(\dif{z}\wedge\dif{\bar{z}}).

On the other hand, we also have using Corollary
2.12
,

\displaystyle-\rho\dif{X}\wedge\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}\dif{X}

\displaystyle=-\rho(X_{z}\dif{z}+X_{\bar{z}}\dif{\bar{z}})\wedge(\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}X_{z}\dif{z}+\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}X_{\bar{z}}\dif{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=-\rho e^{2\sigma}F(\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}-\tfrac{E_{1}+iE_{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}\tfrac{E_{1}-iE_{2}}{2})F^{*}(\dif{z}\wedge\dif{\bar{z}})

\displaystyle=-\rho e^{2\sigma}F\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F^{*}(\dif{z}\wedge\dif{\bar{z}}).

Thus the Dirac–type equation is satisfied if and only if

(3.17)

\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{12}&-\Lambda_{11}\\
\Omega_{22}&-\Lambda_{21}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho e^{\sigma}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Now, for one direction of the proof, suppose the compatibility condition (
3.9
) is satisfied so that
\tilde{X}
is well-defined.
Thus, we have the relations (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), which imply that (
3.17
) holds, that is,
B
satisfies the Dirac–type equation (
3.15
).

For the other direction, if
B
satisfies the Dirac–type equation (
3.15
), then (
3.17
) holds, which implies that the compatibility condition (
3.9
) is satisfied.
∎

3.3.
Spinor representation

We have now gathered the necessary ingredients to obtain the spinor representation of conformal spacelike surfaces in isotropic
3
-space.
By Lemma
2.10
, we can map any spacelike plane through the origin to another one via isometries fixing the origin.
Thus, any conformal spacelike immersion can be obtained as a spin transformation of the map
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
where

X(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&u+iv\\
u-iv&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
\bar{z}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have that
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
as in (
3.3
) is the identity map.

Now to consider spin transfomations of
X
, write
B\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{G}
explicitly as

B=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\bar{\beta}\\
0&\bar{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},

for some complex functions
\alpha,\beta
defined on
\Sigma
, and note that
\alpha
never vanishes since
\det B\neq 0
everywhere.
Then we calculate

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{z}F=B^{-1}B_{z}=\frac{1}{|\alpha|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\alpha}\alpha_{z}&\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}_{z}-\bar{\beta}\bar{\alpha}_{z}\\
0&\alpha\bar{\alpha}_{z}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=F^{-1}B^{-1}B_{\bar{z}}F=B^{-1}B_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{|\alpha|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\alpha}\alpha_{\bar{z}}&\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}_{\bar{z}}-\bar{\beta}\bar{\alpha}_{\bar{z}}\\
0&\alpha\bar{\alpha}_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}.

The map
B
must satisfy the Dirac–type equation; using equivalent conditions in (
3.17
), we find that since
\alpha
never vanishes

\alpha_{\bar{z}}=0,\quad\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}}\in\mathbb{R}.

For such
B
, we can then calculate

\tilde{X}_{z}=BX_{z}B^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\bar{\beta}\\
0&\bar{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\alpha}&0\\
\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\beta&\alpha^{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

or in terms of coordinates,

(3.18)

\tilde{x}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha},1,-i\right)\alpha^{2}.

Summarizing:

Theorem 3.7

(Spinor representation of conformal immersions)
.

Any conformal immersion
\tilde{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
can locally be represented as

(3.19)

\tilde{x}=\frac{1}{2}\Re\int\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha},1,-i\right)\alpha^{2}\dif{z},

for some non-vanishing holomorphic function
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
, and a complex valued function
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

(3.20)

\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}}-\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}_{z}=0.

The metric of
\tilde{x}
then is

\dif{s}^{2}=|\alpha|^{4}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}},

while the mean curvature
\tilde{H}
of
\tilde{x}
is given by

(3.21)

\tilde{H}=\frac{\beta_{\bar{z}}}{\bar{\alpha}|\alpha|^{2}}.

Moreover, the lightlike Gauss map
\tilde{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}
is given by

(3.22)

\tilde{g}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+|\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}|^{2},2\Re\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},-2\Im\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},-1+|\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}|^{2}\right)^{t}.

Proof.

Since the representation by the integral formula (
3.19
) is already proven, we proceed to prove the rest of the claims.
For the metric, we use the relation on metric (
3.10
) to see that

\dif{\tilde{s}}^{2}=\det B^{2}\dif{s}^{2}=|\alpha|^{4}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}},

while for the mean curvature
\tilde{H}
, we use (
3.16
) with the value of
\rho
given by (
3.14
):

\tilde{H}=\frac{\rho}{\det B}=\frac{\beta_{\bar{z}}}{\bar{\alpha}|\alpha|^{2}}.

For the lightlike Gauss map, first note that if
A=fB\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathrm{SU}(1,0,1)
for some
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then

A=\tfrac{1}{|\alpha|}B.

Thus the new lightlike Gauss map is given by

\tilde{G}=A\tilde{\mathfrak{P}}A^{*}=\frac{1}{|\alpha|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\bar{\beta}\\
0&\bar{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\alpha}&0\\
\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{1}{|\alpha|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}|\beta|^{2}&\alpha\bar{\beta}\\
\bar{\alpha}\beta&|\alpha|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\tilde{G}\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)
, we can let
\tilde{g}
be the corresponding vector in
\mathbb{L}^{4}
to obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

We remark here that one can also prescribe the mean curvature function
H\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
first, and find holomorphic function
\alpha
and a complex function satisfying (
3.21
).
Such
\alpha
and
\beta
will satisfy the compatibility condition (
3.20
), thus giving us the
Kenmotsu representation
of conformal surfaces with prescribed mean curvature
[
14
, Theorem 2]
.

To check the robustness of the representation, we can verify all the claims of Theorem
3.7
directly as follows.
Let
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be defined via (
3.19
).
To calculate the metric, first note that with
x_{z}
as in (
3.18
), we have

\langle x_{z},x_{z}\rangle=\tfrac{\alpha^{4}}{4}\Big\langle\left(\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},1,-i,\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{t},\left(\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},1,-i,\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{t}\Big\rangle=0

while using
x_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\bar{\alpha}},1,i\right)\bar{\alpha}^{2}
, we have

2\langle x_{z},x_{\bar{z}}\rangle=\tfrac{|\alpha|^{4}}{2}\Big\langle\left(\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},1,-i,\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right),\left(\tfrac{\bar{\beta}}{\bar{\alpha}},1,i,\tfrac{\bar{\beta}}{\bar{\alpha}}\right)\Big\rangle=|\alpha|^{4}.

Thus the metric of the given surface
x
is

\dif{s}^{2}=2\langle x_{z},x_{\bar{z}}\rangle\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}=|\alpha|^{4}\dif{z}\dif{\bar{z}}.

On the other hand, with
g
given as in (
3.22
), we can check that

\langle g,g\rangle=0,\quad\langle g,\mathfrak{p}\rangle=1,\quad\langle x_{z},g\rangle=0,

so that
g
is the lightlike Gauss map of
x
.

Finally, since
\alpha
is holomorphic, we have

x_{z\bar{z}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha\beta,\alpha^{2},-i\alpha^{2},\alpha\beta)^{t}_{\bar{z}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}},0,0,\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}})^{t},

and using the formula for the mean curvature (
3.1
), we calculate

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{|\alpha|^{4}}\langle x_{z\bar{z}},g\rangle

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2|\alpha|^{4}}\langle\left(\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}},0,0,\alpha\beta_{\bar{z}}\right)^{t},\left(1+|\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}|^{2},2\Re\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},-2\Im\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha},-1+|\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}|^{2}\right)^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{\bar{z}}}{|\alpha|^{2}\bar{\alpha}}.

3.4.
Weierstrass-type representation and Kenmotsu-type representation for cmc surfaces

Now suppose that the conformal immersion is minimal, i.e.
H=0
, so that (
3.21
) implies that
\beta_{\bar{z}}=0
.
Since
\alpha
and
\beta
are now both holomorphic, define a meromorphic function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
and a holomorphic
1
–form
\omega
via

h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\quad\omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\alpha^{2}\dif{z}.

Then the Weierstrass-type representation for minimal surfaces in isotropic
3
-space
[
32
, Equation 8.31]
is obtained as an application of the spinor representation:

Theorem 3.9

(Weierstrass-type representation of minimal immersions)
.

Any minimal immersion
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
can locally be represented as

x=\frac{1}{2}\Re\int\left(h,1,-i\right)\omega,

for some meromorphic
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
and holomorphic
1
–form
\omega
such that
h^{2}\omega
is holomorphic.

The metric of
x
is then given by

\dif{s}^{2}=|\omega|^{2},

the Hopf differential

Q=\frac{1}{2}\omega\dif{h},

and the lightlike Gauss map
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}

g=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+|h|^{2},2\Re h,-2\Im h,-1+|h|^{2}\right).

On the other hand, suppose that the conformal immersion has non-zero cmc
H\neq 0
.
Then by (
3.21
) we have

\beta_{\bar{z}}=H\bar{\alpha}^{2}\alpha

for some holomorphic
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
.
Thus,

\beta=H\alpha\int\bar{\alpha}^{2}\dif{\bar{z}}+\gamma

for some holomorphic
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
.
Therefore, if we define
h_{1},h_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
via

h_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H\int\alpha^{2}\dif{z},\quad h_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha},

then we have that
h_{1}
is holomorphic, while
h_{2}
is meromorphic, satisfying

\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=\bar{h}_{1}+h_{2}.

Thus the Kenmotsu-type representation for non-zero constant mean curvature
H
surfaces can also be obtained as an application of the spinor representation:

Theorem 3.10

(Kenmotsu-type representation of cmc immersions)
.

Any non-zero cmc
H
immersion
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
can locally be represented as

x=\frac{1}{2}\Re\int\left(\bar{h}_{1}+h_{2},1,-i\right)\omega,

for some holomorphic
h_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
, meromorphic
h_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
and holomorphic
1
–form
\omega
such that
h_{2}^{2}\omega
is holomorphic while

(3.23)

\dif{h}_{1}=H\omega.

The metric of
x
is then given by

\dif{s}^{2}=|\omega|^{2},

the Hopf differential

Q=\frac{1}{2}\omega\dif{h_{2}},

and the lightlike Gauss map
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}

g=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+|h|^{2},2\Re h,-2\Im h,-1+|h|^{2}\right)

for
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bar{h}_{1}+h_{2}
.
We call
(h_{2},\omega)
the Kenmotsu data.

Remark 3.11
.

We note here that the Weierstrass-type representation for minimal surfaces in Theorem
3.9
is a special case of the Kenmotsu-type representation for cmc surfaces in Theorem
3.10
, as (
3.23
) says that if
H=0
, then
h_{1}\equiv\text{constant}
, and
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bar{h}_{1}+h_{2}
is meromorphic with holomorphic
h^{2}\omega
.

3.5.
Examples of cmc surfaces

In this section, we compute some cmc surfaces explicitly using the Kenmotsu-type representation in Theorem
3.10
.

Spheres:

For some constant
H
, let
\omega=e^{z}\dif{z}
and
h_{2}=0
.
Then

h_{1}=H\int e^{z}\dif{z}=He^{z}.

Then by the Kenmotsu-type representation, we have

x_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(He^{2u},e^{z},-ie^{z}),\quad x_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(He^{2u},e^{\bar{z}},ie^{\bar{z}}),

so that

\displaystyle x_{u}

\displaystyle=x_{z}+x_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(2He^{2u},e^{z}+e^{\bar{z}},-i(e^{z}-e^{\bar{z}}))=(He^{2u},e^{u}\cos v,e^{u}\sin v),

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle=i(x_{z}-x_{\bar{z}})=\frac{1}{2}(0,i(e^{z}-e^{\bar{z}}),e^{z}+e^{\bar{z}})=(0,-e^{u}\sin v,e^{u}\cos v).

Integrating, we see that

x=(\tfrac{1}{2}He^{2u},e^{u}\cos v,e^{u}\sin v),

giving us a sphere with radius
\frac{1}{H}
by (
2.10
) (see Figure
7(a)
).

In fact, if
h_{2}=0
, then the resulting surface is a sphere: Kenmotsu-type representation tells us that

x_{z}\dif{z}=(\bar{h}_{1},1,-i)\omega,\quad x_{\bar{z}}\dif{\bar{z}}=(h_{1},1,i)\bar{\omega}

Since we have that
h_{1}
is holomorphic,

\dif{h}_{1}=h_{1,z}\dif{z}=H\omega,

so that

x=\frac{1}{2H}\left(h_{1}\bar{h}_{1},2\Re h_{1},2\Im h_{1}\right)

which is a sphere with radius
\frac{1}{H}
by (
2.10
).

Cylinders:

Fixing some constant
H
, let
\omega=\dif{z}
and
h_{2}=Hz

Thus we have

h_{1}=H\int\dif{z}=Hz.

Using the Kenmotsu-type representation, we find

x_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(H(\bar{z}+z),1,-i)=\frac{1}{2}(2Hu,1,-i),\quad x_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(2Hu,1,i).

Calculating that

x_{u}=x_{z}+x_{\bar{z}}=(2Hu,1,0),\quad x_{v}=i(x_{z}-x_{\bar{z}})=(0,0,1),

we can integrate and obtain

x=(Hu^{2},u,v).

Delaunay-type surfaces:

For this example, let
\omega=e^{z}\dif{z}
so that
h_{1}=He^{z}
, and set
h_{2}=ae^{-z}
for some constant
a
.
Using the Kenmotsu-type representation, we find

\displaystyle x_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(He^{\bar{z}}+ae^{-z},1,-i\right)e^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(He^{2u}+a,e^{z},-ie^{z}\right),

\displaystyle x_{\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(He^{2u}+a,e^{\bar{z}},ie^{\bar{z}}\right).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle x_{u}

\displaystyle=x_{z}+x_{\bar{z}}=(He^{2u}+a,e^{u}\cos v,e^{u}\sin v),

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle=i(x_{z}-x_{\bar{z}})=(0,-e^{u}\sin v,e^{u}\cos v),

and integrating tells us that

x=\left(\frac{He^{2u}+2au}{2},e^{u}\cos v,e^{u}\sin v\right).

These are the rotationally invariant cmc
H

3

Singly periodic cmc surfaces:

To obtain examples of singly periodic cmc surfaces, let
\omega=e^{az}\dif{z}
so that
h_{1}=\frac{H}{a}e^{az}
, and set
h_{2}=e^{(b-a)z}
for some constants
a,b
.
Then the Kenmotsu-type representation tells us that

\displaystyle x_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{H}{a}e^{a\bar{z}}+e^{(b-a)z},1,-i\right)e^{az}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{H}{a}e^{2au}+e^{bz},e^{az},-ie^{az}\right),

\displaystyle x_{\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{H}{a}e^{2au}+e^{b\bar{z}},e^{a\bar{z}},ie^{a\bar{z}}\right).

Thus we calculate that

\displaystyle x_{u}

\displaystyle=x_{z}+x_{\bar{z}}=\left(\tfrac{H}{a}e^{2au}+e^{bu}\cos bv,e^{au}\cos av,e^{au}\sin av\right),

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle=i(x_{z}-x_{\bar{z}})=\left(-e^{bu}\sin bv,-e^{au}\sin av,e^{au}\cos av\right).

Integrating, we obtain

x=\left(\frac{He^{2au}}{2a^{2}}+\frac{e^{bu}\cos bv}{b},\frac{e^{au}\cos av}{a},\frac{e^{au}\sin av}{a}\right).

Let
a,b
be rational numbers so that there are relatively prime pair
(p_{1},q_{1}),(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with

a=\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}},\quad\text{and}\quad b=\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}.

For such
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
, the surface is
2\pi L
-periodic over
v
, i.e.
x(u,v)=x(u,v+2\pi L)
for

L=\frac{\lcm(q_{1},q_{2})}{\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})},

where
\gcd
and
\lcm
stands for the greatest common divisor and the least common multiple, respectively.
In particular, the cmc surface has dihedral symmetry
D_{L|b|}
(see Figure
9
).
