2D discrete Hodge-Dirac operator on the torus
Volodymyr Sushch

Abstract
We discuss a discretisation of the de Rham-Hodge theory in the two-dimensional case based on a discrete exterior calculus framework. We present discrete analogues of the Hodge-Dirac and Laplace operators in which key geometric aspects of the continuum counterpart are captured. We provide and prove a discrete version of the Hodge decomposition theorem. Special attention has been paid to discrete models on a combinatorial torus. In this particular case, we also define and calculate the cohomology groups.



Key words and phrases:

discrete exterior calculus, discrete operators, Hodge-Dirac operator, discrete Laplacian, Hodge decomposition, combinatorial torus, cohomology groups

Let
\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\oplus\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
denote the graded vector space of smooth differential forms on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
denotes the subspace of
r
-forms,
r=0,1,2
. Let

d:\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\Lambda^{r+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the exterior derivative. The codifferential

\delta:\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\Lambda^{r-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined by
\delta=-\ast d\ast
, where
\ast
is the Hodge star operator such that
\ast:\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\Lambda^{2-r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\ast^{2}=(-1)^{r}
.
The operator

d+\delta:\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(1.1)

is called the Hodge-Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Laplacian
\Delta:\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined by

\Delta=(d+\delta)^{2}=d\delta+\delta d.

(1.2)

Our purpose is to develop a satisfactory discrete model of de Rham-Hodge theory on manifolds which are homeomorphic to the torus.
We consider a chain complex as a combinatorial model of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. When we add to this discrete analogues of the exterior derivative, the Hodge star operator, and the exterior product acting on cochains, we have all of the basic ingredients for the calculus of discrete counterparts of differential forms. We show that discrete analogues of the operators (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) have properties like those in the continual case. We formulate and prove a discrete version of the Hodge decomposition theorem.
We give an example illustrating how cohomology groups are calculated for our discrete model.
Note that our construction of discrete versions of the Hodge-Dirac and Laplace operators is very close to the construction in Section 5 of
[
6
]
. Matrix forms of these discrete operators on the torus are the same in both of case. The difference between our approach and one in
[
6
]
is in the definitions of discrete Hodge-Dirac and Laplace operators. In
[
6
]
, the definitions are given in terms of the exterior derivative and boundary operators. Meanwhile, as in the continual theory, we define these operators in terms of the exterior derivative and its adjoint.

Discrete model

In this section, we briefly review the construction of a discrete exterior calculus framework, which was initiated in
[
7
]
and developed in e.g.,
[
14
,
15
]
.

The starting point for consideration is a two-dimensional chain complex (a combinatorial model of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
).
Let the sets
\{x_{k}\}
and
\{e_{k}\}
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, be the generators of free abelian groups of
zero-dimensional and one-dimensional
chains of the one-dimensional complex
C=C_{0}\oplus C_{1}
.
The free abelian group is understood as the direct sum of infinity cyclic groups generated by
\{x_{k}\}
,
\{e_{k}\}
.
The boundary operator
\partial:C_{1}\rightarrow C_{0}
is the homomorphism defined by

\partial e_{k}=x_{k+1}-x_{k}

and the boundary of every zero chain is defined to be zero.
Geometrically we can interpret the zero-dimensional basis elements
x_{k}
as points of the real line and the one-dimensional basis elements
e_{k}
as open intervals between points. We call the complex
C
a combinatorial real line.
Let the tensor product

C(2)=C\otimes C

be a combinatorial model of the two-dimensional Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The two-dimensional complex
C(2)=C_{0}(2)\oplus C_{1}(2)\oplus C_{2}(2)
contains the free abelian groups of
r
-chains,
r=0,1,2
, generated by the basic elements

\displaystyle x_{k,s}=x_{k}\otimes x_{s},\qquad e_{k,s}^{1}=e_{k}\otimes x_{s},\qquad e_{k,s}^{2}=x_{k}\otimes e_{s},\qquad V_{k,s}=e_{k}\otimes e_{s},

where
k,s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
It is convenient to
introduce the shift operators
\tau,\sigma
in the set of indices by

\tau k=k+1\qquad\sigma k=k-1.

(2.1)

The boundary operator
\partial:C_{r}(2)\rightarrow C_{r-1}(2)

is given by

\displaystyle\partial x_{k,s}=0,\qquad\partial e_{k,s}^{1}=x_{\tau k,s}-x_{k,s}\qquad\partial e_{k,s}^{2}=x_{k,\tau s}-x_{k,s},

\displaystyle\partial V_{k,s}=e_{k,s}^{1}+e_{\tau k,s}^{2}-e_{k,\tau s}^{1}-e_{k,s}^{2}.

(2.2)

The definition (
2
) is extended to arbitrary chains by linearity.

Let
K(2)
be a complex of cochains with real coefficients. The cochain complex
K(2)
with a coboundary operator defined in it is the dual object to the chain complex
C(2)
. It has a similar structure to
C(2)
and consists of cochains of dimension 0, 1 and 2.
Then
K(2)
can be expressed by

K(2)=K^{0}(2)\oplus K^{1}(2)\oplus K^{2}(2),

where
K^{r}(2)
is the set of all
r
-cochains. We will call cochains forms (or discrete forms) emphasizing their relationship with differential forms. Then the complex
K(2)
is a discrete analogue of the grade algebra of differential forms
\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Denote by
\{x^{k,s}\}
,
\{e^{k,s}_{1},\ e^{k,s}_{2}\}
and
\{V^{k,s}\}
the basis elements of
K^{0}(2)
,
K^{1}(2)
and
K^{2}(2)
respectively.
The pairing is defined with the basis elements of
C(2)
by the rule

\langle x_{k,s},\ x^{i,j}\rangle=\langle e_{k,s}^{1},\ e^{i,j}_{1}\rangle=\langle e_{k,s}^{2},\ e^{i,j}_{2}\rangle=\langle V_{k,s},\ V^{i,j}\rangle=\delta_{k}^{i}\delta_{s}^{j},

(2.3)

where
\delta_{k}^{i}
is the Kronecker delta.
The operation (
2.3
) is linearly extended to arbitrary chains and cochains.
Let
\overset{r}{\omega}\in K^{r}(2)
, then we have

\overset{0}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}\overset{0}{\omega}_{k,s}x^{k,s},\quad\overset{1}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(\omega^{1}_{k,s}e_{1}^{k,s}+\omega^{2}_{k,s}e_{2}^{k,s}),\quad\overset{2}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}\overset{2}{\omega}_{k,s}V^{k,s},

(2.4)

where
\overset{0}{\omega}_{k,s}
,
\omega^{1}_{k,s}
,
\omega^{2}_{k,s}
and
\overset{2}{\omega}_{k,s}
are real numbers
for any
k,s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The coboundary operator
d^{c}:K^{r}(2)\rightarrow K^{r+1}(2)
is defined by

\langle\partial a_{r+1},\ \overset{r}{\omega}\rangle=\langle a_{r+1},\ d^{c}\overset{r}{\omega}\rangle,

(2.5)

where
a_{r+1}\in C_{r+1}(2)
. The operator
d^{c}
is an analog of the exterior differential.
From the above it follows that

d^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad d^{c}d^{c}\overset{r}{\omega}=0\quad\mbox{for any}\quad r=0,1.

By (
2
) and (
2.3
) we can calculate

d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(\Delta_{k}\overset{0}{\omega}_{k,s})e_{1}^{k,s}+(\Delta_{s}\overset{0}{\omega}_{k,s})e_{2}^{k,s},

(2.6)

d^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(\Delta_{k}\omega_{k,s}^{2}-\Delta_{s}\omega_{k,s}^{1})V^{k,s},

(2.7)

where
\Delta_{k}
and
\Delta_{s}
are the difference operators defined by

\Delta_{k}\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{\tau k,s}-\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s},\qquad\Delta_{s}\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,\tau s}-\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s}.

(2.8)

Here
\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s}
is a component of
\overset{r}{\omega}\in K^{r}(2)
and
\tau
is given by (
2.1
).
Note that
\overset{1}{\omega}_{k,s}=\{\omega^{1}_{k,s},\ \omega^{2}_{k,s}\}
.

We now consider a multiplication of discrete forms which is an analogue of the
exterior multiplication for differential forms. Denote by
\cup
this multiplication.
For the basis elements of
K(2)
the
\cup
-multiplication is defined as follows

x^{k,s}\cup x^{k,s}=x^{k,s},\qquad x^{k,s}\cup e^{k,s}_{1}=e^{k,s}_{1},\qquad x^{k,s}\cup e^{k,s}_{2}=e^{k,s}_{2},

x^{k,s}\cup V^{k,s}=V^{k,s},\qquad e^{k,s}_{1}\cup x^{\tau k,s}=e^{k,s}_{1},\qquad e^{k,s}_{2}\cup x^{k,\tau s}=e^{k,s}_{2},

V^{k,s}\cup x^{\tau k,\tau s}=V^{k,s},\qquad e^{k,s}_{1}\cup e^{\tau k,s}_{2}=V^{k,s},\qquad e^{k,s}_{2}\cup e^{k,\tau s}_{1}=-V^{k,s},

supposing the product to be zero in all other cases. The operation is extended to arbitrary forms by linearity.
It is important to note that this definition leads to the following discrete counterpart of the Leibniz rule for differential forms.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\overset{r}{\omega}\in K^{r}(2)
and
\overset{p}{\varphi}\in K^{p}(2)
.
Then

d^{c}(\overset{r}{\omega}\cup\overset{p}{\varphi})=d^{c}\overset{r}{\omega}\cup\overset{p}{\varphi}+(-1)^{r}\overset{r}{\omega}\cup d^{c}\overset{p}{\varphi}.

(2.9)

This was proved by Dezin
[
7
]
.

Define the operation
\ast:K^{r}(2)\rightarrow K^{2-r}(2)
by the rule

\ast x^{k,s}=V^{k,s},\quad\ast e^{k,s}_{1}=e^{\tau k,s}_{2},\quad\ast e^{k,s}_{2}=-e^{k,\tau s}_{1},\quad\ast V^{k,s}=x^{\tau k,\tau s}.

(2.10)

Again, the operation is extended to arbitrary forms by linearity. This operation is a discrete analogue of the Hodge star operator.
It is true that for any
\overset{r}{\omega}\in K^{r}(2)
we have

\overset{r}{\omega}\cup\ast\overset{r}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s})^{2}V^{k,s}.

(2.11)

Consider the two-dimensional finite chain
V\in C_{2}(2)
with unit coefficients of the form

V=\sum_{k,s}V_{k,s},\qquad k=1,2,...,N,\quad s=1,2,..,M.

(2.12)

This chain imitates a rectangle.
Using (
2
) we have

\partial V=\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{1}_{k,1}+\sum_{s=1}^{M}e^{2}_{N,s}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{1}_{k,M}-\sum_{s=1}^{M}e^{2}_{1,s}.

(2.13)

Then for forms

\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r}{\omega}\in K^{r}(2)
of the same degree
r
the inner
product over the set
V
is defined by the rule

(\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r}{\omega})_{V}=\langle V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r}{\omega}\rangle.

(2.14)

For forms of different degrees the product (
2.14
) is set equal to zero.
From (
2.11
) and (
2.3
) we have

(\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r}{\omega})_{V}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\overset{r}{\varphi}_{k,s}\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,s}.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\overset{r}{\varphi}\in K^{r}(2)
and
\overset{r+1}{\omega}\in K^{r+1}(2)
,
r=0,1
. Then we have

(d^{c}\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r+1}{\omega})_{V}=\langle\partial V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle+(\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \delta^{c}\overset{r+1}{\omega})_{V},

(2.15)

where

\delta^{c}\overset{r+1}{\omega}=(-1)^{r+1}\ast^{-1}d^{c}\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}

(2.16)

is the
operator formally adjoint of
d^{c}
.

Here
\ast^{-1}
is the inverse of
\ast
, i.e.,
\ast\ast^{-1}=1
. By (
2.10
) for the basic elements we have

\ast^{-1}x^{k,s}=V^{\sigma k,\sigma s},\quad\ast^{-1}e^{k,s}_{1}=-e^{k,\sigma s}_{2},\quad\ast^{-1}e^{k,s}_{2}=e^{\sigma k,s}_{1},\quad\ast^{-1}V^{k,s}=x^{k,s},

where
\sigma
is given by (
2.1
).

Proof.

By Definitions (
2.5
), (
2.14
) and Formula (
2.9
) we obtain

\displaystyle(d^{c}\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r+1}{\omega})_{V}=\langle V,\ d^{c}\overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle=\langle V,\ d^{c}(\overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega})-(-1)^{r}\overset{r}{\varphi}\cup d^{c}\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\partial V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle+(-1)^{r+1}\langle V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\ast^{-1}d^{c}\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\partial V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{r+1}{\omega}\rangle+\langle V,\ \overset{r}{\varphi}\cup\ast(\delta^{c}\overset{r+1}{\omega})\rangle.

∎

The operator
\delta^{c}:K^{r+1}(2)\rightarrow K^{r}(2)
given by (
2.16
) is a discrete analogue of the codifferential
\delta
. For the 0-form
\overset{0}{\omega}\in K^{0}(2)
we have
\delta^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}=0
.
It is obvious from (
2.16
) that

\delta^{c}\delta^{c}\overset{r}{\omega}=0
for any
r=1,2.

Using (
2.6
)–(
2.8
), (
2.10
) and (
2.16
) we can calculate

\delta^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(-\Delta_{k}\omega_{\sigma k,s}^{1}-\Delta_{s}\omega_{k,\sigma s}^{2})x^{k,s},

(2.17)

\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}=\sum_{k,s}(\Delta_{s}\overset{2}{\omega}_{k,\sigma s})e_{1}^{k,s}-(\Delta_{k}\overset{2}{\omega}_{\sigma k,s})e_{2}^{k,s}.

(2.18)

In the particular case
r=0
, the equality (
2.15
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{M}((\Delta_{k}\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,s}){\omega}_{k,s}^{1}+(\Delta_{s}\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,s}){\omega}_{k,s}^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,\tau M}\omega^{2}_{k,M}-\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,1}\omega^{2}_{k,0})+\sum_{s=1}^{M}(\overset{0}{\varphi}_{\tau N,s}\omega^{1}_{N,s}-\overset{0}{\varphi}_{1,s}\omega^{1}_{0,s})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,s}(-\Delta_{k}\omega_{\sigma k,s}^{1}-\Delta_{s}\omega_{k,\sigma s}^{2}),

where
\overset{1}{\omega}_{k,s}=\{\omega^{1}_{k,s},\ \omega^{2}_{k,s}\}
.
The similar equality holds in the case
r=1
.

It should be noted that the relation (
2.15
) includes not only the forms with the components
\overset{r}{\varphi}_{k,s}
and
\overset{r+1}{\omega}_{k,s}
, where the subscripts
k,s
would run only over the values from (
2.12
), but also the components

\overset{r}{\varphi}_{0,s}
,
\overset{r}{\varphi}_{\tau N,s}
,
\overset{r+1}{\omega}_{0,s}
,
\overset{r+1}{\omega}_{\tau N,s}
,
\overset{r}{\varphi}_{k,0}
,
\overset{r}{\varphi}_{k,\tau M}
,
\overset{r+1}{\omega}_{k,0}
and
\overset{r+1}{\omega}_{k,\tau M}
.

Let us set

\displaystyle\overset{r}{\omega}_{0,s}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{N,s},\qquad\overset{r}{\omega}_{\tau N,s}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{1,s},\qquad s=1,2,..,M,

\displaystyle\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,0}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,M},\qquad\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,\tau M}=\overset{r}{\omega}_{k,1},\qquad k=1,2,...,N.

(2.19)

For
r
-forms satisfying conditions (
2
) the inner product (
2.14
) generates the finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
H^{r}(V)
.
Now we consider the operators

d^{c}:H^{r}(V)\rightarrow H^{r+1}(V),\qquad\delta^{c}:H^{r+1}(V)\rightarrow H^{r}(V).

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\overset{r}{\varphi}\in H^{r}(V)
and
\overset{r+1}{\omega}\in H^{r+1}(V)
,
r=0,1
. Then we have

(d^{c}\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \overset{r+1}{\omega})_{V}=(\overset{r}{\varphi},\ \delta^{c}\overset{r+1}{\omega})_{V}.

(2.20)

Proof.

In fact, by use of conditions (
2
) one has

\langle\partial V,\ \overset{0}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{1}{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,\tau M}\omega^{2}_{k,M}-\overset{0}{\varphi}_{k,1}\omega^{2}_{k,0})+\sum_{s=1}^{M}(\overset{0}{\varphi}_{\tau N,s}\omega^{1}_{N,s}-\overset{0}{\varphi}_{1,s}\omega^{1}_{0,s})=0,

\langle\partial V,\ \overset{1}{\varphi}\cup\ast\overset{2}{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\varphi^{1}_{k,1}\overset{2}{\omega}_{k,0}-\varphi^{1}_{k,\tau M}\overset{2}{\omega}_{k,M})+\sum_{s=1}^{M}(\varphi^{2}_{\tau N,s}\overset{2}{\omega}_{N,s}-\varphi^{2}_{1,s}\overset{2}{\omega}_{0,s})=0,

where
\overset{1}{\omega}_{k,s}=\{\omega^{1}_{k,s},\ \omega^{2}_{k,s}\}
and
\overset{1}{\varphi}_{k,s}=\{\varphi^{1}_{k,s},\ \varphi^{2}_{k,s}\}
.
Hence by Proposition 2.2 it follows (
2.20
).
∎

3
Discrete Hodge decomposition

In this section, we discuss the properties of discrete analogues of the Laplacian and Hodge-Dirac operators using the concepts of the previous section. We also present a discrete version of the Hodge decomposition theorem, emphasizing that it provides an exact counterpart to the continuum theory.

Let us consider the operator

\Delta^{c}=d^{c}\delta^{c}+\delta^{c}d^{c}:H^{r}(V)\rightarrow H^{r}(V).

This is a discrete analogue of the Laplacian (
1.2
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
r
-form
\varphi\in H^{r}(V)
we have

(\Delta^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}\geq 0

and
(\Delta^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}=0
if and only if
\Delta^{c}\varphi=0
.

Proof.

By Proposition 2.3 one has

\displaystyle(\Delta^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}=(d^{c}\delta^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}+(\delta^{c}d^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}

\displaystyle=(\delta^{c}\varphi,\ \delta^{c}\varphi)_{V}+(d^{c}\varphi,\ d^{c}\varphi)_{V}=\|\delta^{c}\varphi\|^{2}+\|d^{c}\varphi\|^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the norm and

\|\varphi\|^{2}=(\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{M}({\varphi}_{k,s})^{2}.

From this if
(\Delta^{c}\varphi,\ \varphi)_{V}=0
then
\|\delta^{c}\varphi\|^{2}=0
and
\|d^{c}\varphi\|^{2}=0
. It gives
\delta^{c}\varphi=0
and
d^{c}\varphi=0
. Hence

\Delta^{c}\varphi=d^{c}\delta^{c}\varphi+\delta^{c}d^{c}\varphi=0.

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

\Delta^{c}\varphi=0
if and only if
\delta^{c}\varphi=0
and
d^{c}\varphi=0
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The operator
\Delta^{c}:H^{r}(V)\rightarrow H^{r}(V)
is self-adjoint, i.e.

(\Delta^{c}\varphi,\ \omega)_{V}=(\varphi,\ \Delta^{c}\omega)_{V}.

Proof.

By (
2.20
) it is obvious.
∎

Consider the spaces

R_{d^{c}}^{r}=\{d^{c}\varphi\in H^{r}(V):\ \varphi\in H^{r-1}(V)\},

R_{\delta^{c}}^{r}=\{\delta^{c}\omega\in H^{r}(V):\ \omega\in H^{r+1}(V)\},

and

N_{\Delta^{c}}^{r}=\{\psi\in H^{r}(V):\ \Delta^{c}\psi=0\}.

By analogy with the continuum case the discrete
r
-form
\omega
is called closed if
d^{c}\omega=0
and exact if
\omega\in R_{d^{c}}^{r}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For each
r=0,1,2
we have the direct sum decomposition

H^{r}(V)=R_{d^{c}}^{r}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}^{r}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}}^{r}.

Proof.

The space
H^{1}(V)
decomposes into

H^{1}(V)=R_{d^{c}}^{1}\oplus N_{\delta^{c}}^{1},\qquad H^{1}(V)=R_{\delta^{c}}^{1}\oplus N_{d^{c}}^{1},

(3.1)

where
N_{\delta^{c}}^{1}
and
N_{d^{c}}^{1}
are the orthogonal complements of the corresponding spaces.
For any
\omega\in R_{\delta^{c}}^{1}
we have
\omega=\delta^{c}\psi
and

(d^{c}\varphi,\ \omega)_{V}=(d^{c}\varphi,\ \delta^{c}\psi)_{V}=(d^{c}d^{c}\varphi,\ \psi)_{V}=0

for any
\varphi\in H^{0}(V)
. Therefore
\omega
is orthogonal to
R_{d^{c}}^{1}
. It follows that

R_{\delta^{c}}^{1}\subset N_{\delta^{c}}^{1}.

Similarly we find

R_{d^{c}}^{1}\subset N_{\delta^{c}}^{1}
.
Hence (
3.1
) becomes

H^{1}(V)=R_{d^{c}}^{1}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}^{1}\oplus N^{1},

where

N^{1}=N_{\delta^{c}}^{1}\cap N_{d^{c}}^{1}=\{\varphi\in H^{1}(V):\ d^{c}\varphi=0,\ \delta^{c}\varphi=0\}.

By Corollary 3.2 we have
N^{1}=N_{\Delta^{c}}^{1}
.

Similar reasonings apply to the spaces
H^{0}(V)
and
H^{2}(V)
. Thus we have

H^{0}(V)=R_{\delta^{c}}^{0}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}}^{0},\qquad H^{2}(V)=R_{d^{c}}^{2}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}}^{2},

since
R_{d^{c}}^{0}=\{0\}
and
R_{\delta^{c}}^{2}=\{0\}
.
∎

The Proposition 3.4 is a discrete version of the well-known Hodge decomposition theorem (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).

Let
\Omega
be an inhomogeneous discrete form, i.e.
\Omega=\overset{0}{\omega}+\overset{1}{\omega}+\overset{2}{\omega}
, where
\overset{r}{\omega}\in H^{r}(V)
. The inner product (
2.14
) can be extend to an inner product of inhomogeneous discrete forms by the rule

(\Omega,\ \Phi)_{V}=\sum_{r=0}^{2}(\overset{r}{\omega},\ \overset{r}{\varphi})_{V},

(3.2)

where
\Phi=\overset{0}{\varphi}+\overset{1}{\varphi}+\overset{2}{\varphi}
.
The inner product (
3.2
) generates the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
H(V)
. It is true that

H(V)=H^{0}(V)\oplus H^{1}(V)\oplus H^{2}(V).

By Proposition 3.4 the following holds

H(V)=R_{d^{c}}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}},

(3.3)

where

R_{d^{c}}=R_{d^{c}}^{1}\oplus R_{d^{c}}^{2},\qquad R_{\delta^{c}}=R_{\delta^{c}}^{0}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}^{1},

and

N_{\Delta^{c}}=N_{\Delta^{c}}^{0}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}}^{1}\oplus N_{\Delta^{c}}^{2}.

The discrete Hodge-Dirac operator is defined as

d^{c}+\delta^{c}:H(V)\rightarrow H(V).

(3.4)

Proposition 3.5
.

Operator (
3.4
) is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product (
3.2
), i.e.

((d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Phi,\ \Omega)_{V}=(\Phi,\ (d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Omega)_{V}.

Proof.

By (
2.20
) it is obvious.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

(d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Omega=0\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad\Omega\in N_{\Delta^{c}}.

Proof.

The equation
(d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Omega=0
can be written as

\displaystyle\delta^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=0,\quad d^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=0,

\displaystyle d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}=0.

Let
\Omega\in N_{\Delta^{c}}
. This means that
\overset{0}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{0}
,
\overset{1}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{1}
and
\overset{2}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{2}

By Corollary 3.2,
\overset{1}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{1}
if and only if
\delta^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=0
and
d^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}=0
.
It is easy to show that

\|d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}\|^{2}=(d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega},\ d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega})_{V}=\|d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}\|^{2}+\|\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}\|^{2}

(3.5)

for any
\overset{0}{\omega}\in H^{0}(V)
and
\overset{2}{\omega}\in H^{2}(V)
.
For
\overset{0}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{0}
and
\overset{2}{\omega}\in N_{\Delta^{c}}^{2}
, by (
2.20
) and (
3.5
), we have

0=(\delta^{c}d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega},\overset{0}{\omega})_{V}+(d^{c}\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega},\overset{2}{\omega})_{V}=(d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega},\ d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega})_{V}+(\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega},\ \delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega})_{V}=\|d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}\|^{2}

and thus
d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}=0
. The converse is trivially true.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

For any inhomogeneous form
F\in R_{d^{c}}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}
, there exists a unique form
\Omega\in R_{d^{c}}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}
which is a solution to the equation

(d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Omega=F.

(3.6)

Proof.

Since the operator (
3.4
) is self-adjoint and
H(V)
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, the existence of the solution is a consequence of the uniqueness of the solution and vice versa.
By Proposition 3.6,
(d^{c}+\delta^{c})\Omega=0
implies
\Omega\in N_{\Delta^{c}}
. From this and by (
3.3
), the uniqueness of the solution
\Omega\in R_{d^{c}}\oplus R_{\delta^{c}}
of Eq. (
3.6
) follows immediately.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

If
\Omega\in H(V)
is a solution of Eq. (
3.6
) then the following holds

\|\Omega\|^{2}\leq c(\|d^{c}\Omega\|^{2}+\|\delta^{c}\Omega\|^{2})+\|\Omega_{\Delta^{c}}\|^{2},

where
c
is a constant,
d^{c}\Omega=d^{c}\overset{0}{\omega}+d^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}
,
\delta^{c}\Omega=\delta^{c}\overset{1}{\omega}+\delta^{c}\overset{2}{\omega}

and
\Omega_{\Delta^{c}}
is the projection of
\Omega
onto
N_{\Delta^{c}}
.

Let us define analogues of the cohomology groups
\mathcal{H}^{r}(T)
of the combinatorial torus
C(T)
.
The quotient space of the linear space of closed
r
-forms

N_{d^{c}}^{r}(T)=\{\omega\in K^{r}(T):\ d^{c}\omega=0\}

modulo the subspace of exact
r
-forms

R_{d^{c}}^{r}(T)=\{\omega\in K^{r}(T):\ \exists\varphi\in K^{r-1}(T)\quad\omega=d^{c}\varphi\}

is called the
r
-th cohomology group of
C(T)
, that is,

\mathcal{H}^{r}(T)=N_{d^{c}}^{r}(T)/R_{d^{c}}^{r}(T).

Two closed
r
-forms
\omega^{1}
and
\omega^{2}
are cohomologous,
\omega^{1}\sim\omega^{2}
, if and only if they differ by an exact form, i.e.,

\omega^{1}\sim\omega^{2}\Leftrightarrow\omega^{1}-\omega^{2}\in R_{d^{c}}^{r}(T).

An element of
\mathcal{H}^{r}(T)
is thus an equivalence class
[\omega]
of closed
r
-forms
\omega+d^{c}\varphi
, defined by the equivalence relation
\sim
. These equivalence classes endow
\mathcal{H}^{r}(T)
with a group structure.

Calculation of
\mathcal{H}^{0}(T)
. Since there are no
-1
-forms, a
0
-form
\varphi
can never be exact, i.e.
R_{d^{c}}^{0}(T)=\{0\}
.
If
\varphi\in N_{d^{c}}^{0}(T)
then
d^{c}\varphi=0
. By (
4
) it follows immediately that
\varphi_{1,1}=\varphi_{2,1}=\varphi_{1,2}=\varphi_{2,2}
. Hence

\varphi=c(x^{1,1}+x^{2,1}+x^{1,2}+x^{2,2}),

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant. Thus
\mathcal{H}^{0}(T)
is isomorphic to the group generated by one independent generator which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
and we write

\mathcal{H}^{0}(T)\cong\mathbb{R}
.

Calculation of
\mathcal{H}^{1}(T)
. Let
\omega^{1}=\{u^{1},v^{1}\}\in R_{d^{c}}^{1}(T)
. Then
\omega^{1}=d^{c}\varphi
for some
\varphi\in K^{0}(T)
. From (

\displaystyle u^{1}_{1,1}=\varphi_{2,1}-\varphi_{1,1},\qquad u^{1}_{1,2}=\varphi_{2,2}-\varphi_{1,2},

\displaystyle u^{1}_{2,1}=\varphi_{1,1}-\varphi_{2,1},\qquad u^{1}_{2,2}=\varphi_{1,2}-\varphi_{2,2},

\displaystyle v^{1}_{1,1}=\varphi_{1,2}-\varphi_{1,1},\qquad v^{1}_{2,1}=\varphi_{2,2}-\varphi_{2,1},

\displaystyle v^{1}_{1,2}=\varphi_{1,1}-\varphi_{1,2},\qquad v^{1}_{2,2}=\varphi_{2,1}-\varphi_{2,2}.

It follows that

u^{1}_{2,1}=-u^{1}_{1,1},\qquad u^{1}_{1,2}=-u^{1}_{2,2},\qquad v^{1}_{1,2}=-v^{1}_{1,1},\qquad v^{1}_{2,1}=-v^{1}_{2,2}.

Hence any form
\omega^{1}\in R_{d^{c}}^{1}(T)
can be written as

\displaystyle\omega^{1}=u^{1}_{1,1}e_{1}^{1,1}-u^{1}_{1,1}e_{1}^{2,1}-u^{1}_{2,2}e_{1}^{1,2}+u^{1}_{2,2}e_{1}^{2,2}

\displaystyle+v^{1}_{1,1}e_{2}^{1,1}-v^{1}_{1,1}e_{2}^{1,2}-v^{1}_{2,2}e_{2}^{2,1}+v^{1}_{2,2}e_{2}^{2,2}

\displaystyle=u^{1}_{1,1}(e_{1}^{1,1}-e_{1}^{2,1})+u^{1}_{2,2}(e_{1}^{2,2}-e_{1}^{1,2})+v^{1}_{1,1}(e_{2}^{1,1}-e_{2}^{1,2})+v^{1}_{2,2}(e_{2}^{2,2}-e_{2}^{2,1}).

Let now
\omega=\{u,v\}
be a closed 1-form, i. e.,
\omega\in N_{d^{c}}^{1}(T)
and
d^{c}\omega=0
.
By (
4
) the requirement

means that

\displaystyle u_{1,1}-u_{1,2}+v_{2,1}-v_{1,1}=0,

\displaystyle u_{2,1}-u_{2,2}-v_{2,1}+v_{1,1}=0,

\displaystyle u_{1,2}-u_{1,1}+v_{2,2}-v_{1,2}=0,

\displaystyle u_{2,2}-u_{2,1}+v_{1,2}-v_{2,2}=0.

From this we obtain

\displaystyle u_{1,1}-u_{1,2}+u_{2,1}-u_{2,2}=0,

\displaystyle v_{1,1}-v_{2,1}+v_{1,2}-v_{2,2}=0.

Hence any form
\omega\in N_{d^{c}}^{1}(T)
can be written as

\displaystyle\omega=u_{1,1}e_{1}^{1,1}+(u_{1,2}-u_{1,1}+u_{2,2})e_{1}^{2,1}+u_{1,2}e_{1}^{1,2}+u_{2,2}e_{1}^{2,2}

\displaystyle+v_{1,1}e_{2}^{1,1}+(v_{1,1}+v_{1,2}-v_{2,2})e_{2}^{2,1}+v_{1,2}e_{2}^{1,2}+v_{2,2}e_{2}^{2,2}.

\displaystyle\omega=u_{1,1}(e_{1}^{1,1}-e_{1}^{2,1})+u_{1,2}(e_{1}^{2,1}+e_{1}^{1,2})+u_{2,2}(e_{1}^{2,2}+e_{1}^{2,1})

\displaystyle+v_{1,1}(e_{2}^{1,1}+e_{2}^{2,1})+v_{1,2}(e_{2}^{2,1}+e_{2}^{1,2})+v_{2,2}(e_{2}^{2,2}-e_{2}^{2,1})

\displaystyle=(u_{1,2}+u_{2,2})(e_{1}^{2,1}+e_{1}^{1,2})+(v_{1,2}+v_{1,1})(e_{2}^{2,1}+e_{2}^{1,2})+\omega^{0},

\mathcal{H}^{1}(T)
has two independent generators. Thus
\mathcal{H}^{1}(T)\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}

Calculation of
\mathcal{H}^{2}(T)
. A 2-form

\psi^{1}=\psi^{1}_{1,1}V^{1,1}+\psi^{1}_{2,1}V^{2,1}+\psi^{1}_{1,2}V^{1,2}+\psi^{1}_{2,2}V^{2,2}

\displaystyle\psi^{1}_{1,1}=u_{1,1}-u_{1,2}+v_{2,1}-v_{1,1},\quad\psi^{1}_{2,1}=u_{2,1}-u_{2,2}-v_{2,1}+v_{1,1},

\displaystyle\psi^{1}_{1,2}=u_{1,2}-u_{1,1}+v_{2,2}-v_{1,2},\quad\psi^{1}_{2,2}=u_{2,2}-u_{2,1}+v_{1,2}-v_{2,2}.

\psi^{1}_{1,1}+\psi^{1}_{2,1}+\psi^{1}_{1,2}+\psi^{1}_{2,2}=0.

Hence any element
\psi^{1}\in R_{d^{c}}^{2}(T)

\psi^{1}=\psi^{1}_{1,1}V^{1,1}+\psi^{1}_{2,1}V^{2,1}+\psi^{1}_{1,2}V^{1,2}+(-\psi^{1}_{1,1}-\psi^{1}_{2,1}-\psi^{1}_{1,2})V^{2,2}.

Thus

\psi\sim(\psi_{1,1}+\psi_{2,1}+\psi_{1,2}+\psi_{2,2})V^{2,2},

since
\psi^{0}\in R_{d^{c}}^{2}(T)
. This means that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(T)
has only one independent generator. So that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(T)\cong\mathbb{R}

Thus the cohomology groups are exactly the same as in the continuum case.
