For

2.
Notational Conventions

Taking a page from measure theory, we present the following decomposition of integers. If
a\in\mathbb{Z},
we define
a^{+}=\max(a,0)
and
a^{-}=\max(-a,0).
Much like other such decompositions,
a=a^{+}-a^{-}
and
|a|=a^{+}+a^{-}.
Also, for any two integers
a
and
b,
there is exactly one integer
c
such that
a-c^{+}=b-c^{-},
namely
c=a-b.
This follows easily from the fact that
a-b=(a-b)^{+}-(a-b)^{-}.

We shall also avail ourselves of the neatness and brevity of multi-index notation. If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},
then we set

\alpha^{+}=(\alpha_{1}^{+},\ldots,\alpha_{d}^{+}),\hskip 25.00003pt\alpha^{-}=(\alpha_{1}^{-},\ldots,\alpha_{d}^{-}),\hskip 25.00003pt\text{abs}(\alpha)=(|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{d}|),

and if
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
(where
\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the set of nonnegative integers),

|\alpha|=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\alpha_{k},\hskip 25.00003pt\alpha!=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\alpha_{k}!,\hskip 25.00003pt\binom{|\alpha|}{\alpha}=\frac{|\alpha|!}{\alpha!}.

It will also be advantageous to have a notion of divisibility for integer vectors. Given an integer
a,
we say that
a\mid\alpha
if
a\mid\alpha_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d.
We will use
\|\,{\scriptscriptstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{{}}}}\,\|_{p}
to symbolize the standard
p
-norm.

Let
[a:b]
denote the set of integers between
a
and
b,
inclusive. Because the precise lettering of an alphabet is immaterial for our purposes, we take
\Sigma_{d}=[1:d]
throughout the rest of this manuscript. The length of a string
w
is the number of characters in the string and is denoted by
|w|.
The
Parikh vector
of a string
w\in\Sigma_{d}^{*}
is given by
\rho(w)=\left(\rho_{1}(w),\ldots,\rho_{d}(w)\right),
where
\rho_{j}(w)
denotes the number of occurrences of
j
within
w.
Clearly
|w|=\rho_{1}(w)+\cdots+\rho_{d}(w).
(Rohit Parikh, the namesake of this string signature, first implemented these vectors in his work on context-free languages in
[
23
]
.)

Lastly,
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix,
\mathbf{0}_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional zero vector, and we set
\mathbf{1}_{d}
to be the vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with each component equal to 1.

3.
Fourier Analysis of the Spectral Density Functions of Stabilized Power Sum Symmetric Polynomials

Definition 3.1
.

The
r^{\text{th}}

stabilized power sum symmetric polynomial
in
d
variables is

p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=1-xp_{r,d}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}),

where
x
is an indeterminate.

These polynomials are strongly
\mathbb{D}^{d}
-stable precisely when
|x|<1/d.
Corollary 5.7 of
[
17
]
indicates that
p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
admits the determinantal representation

(3.1)

p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=\det\!\left(I_{d}-xJ_{d}\text{diag}(z_{1}^{r},\ldots,z_{d}^{r})\right)

where
J_{d}
is the
d\times d
all-ones matrix. (Grinshpan et al.
[
17
]
proved more generally that every irreducible polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
that is zero-free on the closure of a matrix unit polyball with
P(0)=1
admits some determinantal representation
P(\mathbf{z})=\det(I_{d}-K\text{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})),
where
K
is a strictly contractive matrix.) Under the additional assumptions that
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},
we can write

(3.2)

\overline{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})}=\det\!\left(I_{d}-xJ_{d}\text{diag}(\overline{z_{1}}^{r},\ldots,\overline{z_{d}}^{r})\right),

and so the restriction of the spectral density function
\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}
to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left|p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\det\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{d}-xJ_{d}\text{diag}(z_{1}^{r},\ldots,z_{d}^{r})&0\\
0&I_{d}-xJ_{d}\text{diag}(\overline{z_{1}^{r}},\ldots,\overline{z_{d}^{r}})\end{array}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\det\!\left(I_{2d}-x\!\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{d}\text{diag}(z_{1}^{r},\ldots,z_{d}^{r})&0\\
0&J_{d}\text{diag}(\overline{z_{1}}^{r},\ldots,\overline{z_{d}}^{r})\end{array}\right]\right)^{-1}.

If we let
A_{d}(\mathbf{z})=(a_{i,j})_{2d\times 2d}
denote the block diagonal matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{d}\text{diag}(z_{1}^{r},\ldots,z_{d}^{r})&0\\
0&J_{d}\text{diag}(\overline{z_{1}}^{r},\ldots,\overline{z_{d}}^{r})\end{array}\right],

then the MacMahon Master Theorem gives us that

(3.7)

\frac{1}{\left|p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\right|^{2}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{2d}=n\end{subarray}}G(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2d})\right]x^{n}

where, because of the way
A_{d}(\mathbf{z})
is partitioned,

\displaystyle G(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2d})

\displaystyle=[y_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots y_{2d}^{n_{2d}}]\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(a_{i,1}y_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,2d}y_{2d})^{n_{i}}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\binom{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}}{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}\binom{n_{d+1}+\cdots+n_{2d}}{n_{d+1},\ldots,n_{2d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{rn_{i}}\overline{z_{i}}^{rn_{i+d}}.

For a given
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},
series (
3.7
) converges absolutely for
|x|<1/\rho(A_{d}(\mathbf{z})),
where
\rho(A)
is the spectral radius of
A.

Now if we were to calculate an individual Fourier coefficient of (
3.7
), a subset of the
G(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2d})
terms in the resulting formal power series will vanish. In this chapter, we impart enumerative meaning to those terms in the summation that survive. More specifically, the Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}
are all OGFs for combinatorial classes of constrained finite strings.

3.1.
Offset Words

Let us first forge the combinatorial classes enumerated by
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)
for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Definition 3.2
.

For
(n,\xi)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d},
an
n^{\text{th}}

order word pair offset by

\xi
is an ordered pair of words
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{d}^{*}
such that the length of
ww^{\prime}
is
2n+\|\xi\|_{1}
and
\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime})=\xi.
We denote the set of
n^{\text{th}}
order word pairs offset by
\xi
by
\mathcal{W}_{(n,\xi)},
the set of all word pairs offset by
\xi
(regardless of order) by
\mathcal{W}_{\xi},
and let
w_{(n,\xi)}=\left|\mathcal{W}_{(n,\xi)}\right|.

Intuitively
\xi
signals how removed a particular concatenation
ww^{\prime}
is from producing an abelian square while the order
n
tells how many characters
w
and
w^{\prime}
have in common. (See Definition
3.5
and Theorem
3.7
below.) Evidently an
n^{\text{th}}
order word pair offset by
\mathbf{0}_{d}
yields an abelian square of length
2n
since a string
ww^{\prime}
is an abelian square if and only if
\rho(w)=\rho(w^{\prime}).

Example 3.3
.

Consider the alphabet
\Sigma_{3}=\{1,2,3\}.
Let
w=13
and
w^{\prime}=12.
Then
(w,w^{\prime})
is a first order word pair offset by
(0,-1,1).
The string 1312 is also the concatenation of

•

a zeroth order word pair offset by
(-2,-1,-1)
with
w=\varepsilon,
the empty string, and
w^{\prime}=1312,

•

a first order word pair offset by
(0,-1,-1)
with
w=1
and
w^{\prime}=312,

•

a zeroth order word pair offset by
(2,-1,1)
with
w=131
and
w^{\prime}=2,

•

a zeroth order word pair offset by
(2,1,1)
with
w=1312
and
w^{\prime}=\varepsilon.

Proposition 3.4
.

Every string
w\in\Sigma_{d}^{*}
emanates from
|w|+1
of the sets
\mathcal{W}_{(n,\xi)}.

Proof.

Suppose
|w|=N
and fix
k\in[0:N].
There is only one way to write
w
as the concatenation of two strings
w_{k}
and
w_{N-k}^{\prime}
such that
|w_{k}|=k
and
|w_{N-k}^{\prime}|=N-k.
In setting
\xi_{k}=\rho(w_{k})-\rho(w_{N-k}^{\prime}),
we note that
N-\|\xi_{k}\|_{1}
is even because

\displaystyle N-\|\xi_{k}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=N-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\rho_{j}(w_{k})-\rho_{j}(w_{N-k}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\equiv N-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\rho_{j}(w_{k})-\rho_{j}(w_{N-k}^{\prime})\right)(\text{mod}\,2)=2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\rho_{j}(w_{N-k}^{\prime})\,(\text{mod}\,2).

Furthermore

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\rho_{j}(w_{k})-\rho_{j}(w_{N-k}^{\prime})\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\left|\rho_{j}(w_{k})\right|+\left|\rho_{j}(w_{N-k}^{\prime})\right|\right)=|w|,

and so
\frac{1}{2}(N-\|\xi\|_{k})
is a nonnegative integer. Therefore
w\in\mathcal{W}_{(n_{k},\xi_{k})}
with
n_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(N-\|\xi_{k}\|_{1}).
It is clear that different
k
yield different
\xi_{k}
and that there are only
N+1
ways to factor
w
into two consecutive substring blocks.
∎

Except for the empty string, which is solely an abelian square, every string in
\Sigma_{d}^{*}
can be associated with multiple offset word pairs. This may seem to undermine the utility of Definition 3.2, but offset word pairs are a natural generalization of abelian squares in the following sense: the OGFs for
w_{(n,\xi)}
are the Fourier coefficients of one, and essentially only one, operator from
\mathbb{R}
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
We will demonstrate this later in the chapter.

We will now count the number of
n^{\text{th}}
order word pairs offset by
\xi.
To elucidate the counting argument, let us first introduce the concept of mutuality.

Definition 3.5
.

The
mutuality

\nu(w,w^{\prime})
of two strings
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{d}^{*}
is the component-wise minimum of
\rho(w)
and
\rho(w^{\prime}),

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{d}^{*},

(3.9)

\nu(w,w^{\prime})=\rho(w)-(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime}))^{+}=\rho(w^{\prime})-(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime})^{-}.

Proof.

The rightmost equality follows immediately from the integer decomposition defined in Chapter 2. From the fact that for any two numbers
x
and
y,

\min\{x,y\}=(x+y-|x-y|)/2,
we see that

\displaystyle\nu(w,w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\rho(w)+\rho(w^{\prime})-\text{abs}(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\rho(w)+\rho(w^{\prime})-((\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime}))^{+}+(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime}))^{-}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\rho(w)-(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime}))^{+}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\rho(w^{\prime})-(\rho(w)-\rho(w^{\prime})^{-}\right).

The result now follows.
∎

So the concatenation
ww^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathcal{W}_{\xi}
if and only if
\rho(w)=\nu(w,w^{\prime})+\xi^{+}
and
\rho(w^{\prime})=\nu(w,w^{\prime})+\xi^{-}.
Thus, for each
j\in\Sigma_{d},
the words
w
and
w^{\prime}
contain, respectively, at least
\xi_{j}^{+}
and
\xi_{j}^{-}
instances of
j.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
(w,w^{\prime})\in\mathcal{W}_{(n,\xi)},
then
\nu(w,w^{\prime})
is a weak composition of
n.

Proof.

Since
|ww^{\prime}|=2n+\|\xi\|_{1},
it follows from Proposition
3.4
and Lemma
3.6
that

|\nu(w,w^{\prime})|=\frac{1}{2}\!\left|\rho(w)+\rho(w^{\prime})-\text{abs}(\xi)\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}[\rho_{j}(ww^{\prime})-|\xi_{j}|]=n.

∎

Theorem 3.8
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(3.10)

w_{(n,\xi)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
|\nu|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{|\nu+\xi^{+}|}{\nu+\xi^{+}}\binom{|\nu+\xi^{-}|}{\nu+\xi^{-}}.

Proof.

Given a weak composition
\nu
of
n
into
d
parts, the number of ways to build two strings
w
and
w^{\prime}
so that
\rho(w)=\nu+\xi^{+}
and
\rho(w^{\prime})=\nu+\xi^{-}
is
\binom{|\nu+\xi^{+}|}{\nu+\xi^{+}}\binom{|\nu+\xi^{-}|}{\nu+\xi^{-}}.
Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
indicate that summing this across all weak compositions of
n
into
d
parts gives
w_{(n,\xi)}.

∎

3.2.
The Fourier Coefficients as Generating Functions

We now return our attention to the polynomial
p_{1,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
with
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.
Since
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}|_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
can be presented as a power series in each variable
z_{j},
it converges uniformly on every compact subset of
(-1/d,1/d)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}.
Of course, expressing
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}|_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
as a power series in the
z_{j}
requires rearrangement of the terms. This is justified since (
3.7
) holds on all of
(-1/d,1/d)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},
rendering the series absolutely convergent.

Let us now compute the Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}:

(3.11)

\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Here
\xi^{\textsf{T}}
is the transpose of
\xi,

\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}),
and the integral is relative to the completion of the
d
-fold product of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}.
By (
3.7
),
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)
can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\!\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\!\left[\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\!e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{2d}=n\end{subarray}}\!\binom{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}}{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}\!\binom{n_{d+1}+\cdots+n_{2d}}{n_{d+1},\ldots,n_{2d}}\!\prod_{i=1}^{d}\!e^{i(n_{i}-n_{i+d})\theta_{i}}\mathrm{d}\theta\!\right]\!x^{n}

(3.12)

=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
|\kappa|=k,\\
|\kappa^{\prime}|=n-k\end{subarray}}\binom{|\kappa|}{\kappa}\binom{|\kappa^{\prime}|}{\kappa^{\prime}}e^{i(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}-\xi)^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\!x^{n}.

where the uniform convergence of
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}
allows us to interchange the summation and the multiple integral with impunity. Fubini’s theorem also grants us treatment of these Fourier coefficients as iterated integrals. So since
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{ia\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
is
2\pi
when
a=0
and
0
if
a\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\},
the only terms in the integrand of (
3.12
) that remain are those for which
\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}-\xi=\mathbf{0}_{d},
which implies that
\kappa-\xi^{+}=\kappa^{\prime}-\xi^{-}.
Hence, in setting
\nu=\kappa-\xi^{+},
we can simplify the power series for
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p[x]_{1,d}^{*}}}(\xi)
to

(3.13)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
|\nu|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{|\nu+\xi^{+}|}{\nu+\xi^{+}}\binom{|\nu+\xi^{-}|}{\nu+\xi^{-}}\right]\!x^{2n+\|\xi\|_{1}}.

Replace
x
with
\sqrt{x}
in (
3.13
) and then normalize to conclude the following.

Theorem 3.9
.

The OGF for
\mathcal{W}_{\xi},
with
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},
counted according to the order
n,
is

(3.14)

W_{\xi}(x)=\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{2}\|\xi\|_{1}}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[\sqrt{x}]}}(\xi).

The OGF for
\mathcal{W}_{\xi},
counted according to the length
2n+\|\xi\|_{1},
is just
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi).

The Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}
can be calculated in the same manner by noticing that
p_{r,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=p_{1,d}^{\star}[x](z_{1}^{r},\ldots,z_{d}^{r}).
Hence

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}(e^{ir\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{ir\theta_{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
|\kappa|=k,\\
|\kappa^{\prime}|=n-k\end{subarray}}\binom{|\kappa|}{\kappa}\binom{|\kappa^{\prime}|}{\kappa^{\prime}}e^{i(r\kappa-r\kappa^{\prime}-\xi)^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\!x^{n}

for
|x|<1/d.
Like before, the only terms in the integrand of (
3.2
) that contribute to the sum are those for which
r\kappa-r\kappa^{\prime}-\xi=\mathbf{0}_{d}.
So
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)=0
unless
r\mid\xi,
in which case we equivalently require
\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}-r^{-1}\xi=\mathbf{0}_{d}.
The ensuing computation and analytic technicalities involved now match those of
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(r^{-1}\xi).

Corollary 3.10
.

For
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(3.16)

\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)=\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(r^{-1}\xi)\mathbbold{1}_{r\,\mid\,\xi},

where
\mathbbold{1}_{r\,\mid\,\xi}=1
if
r\mid\xi
and
0
otherwise.

Consequently,
x^{-\frac{1}{2}\|r^{-1}\xi\|_{1}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[\sqrt{x}]}}(\xi)=W_{r^{-1}\xi}(x)
is the OGF for
w_{(n,r^{-1}\xi)},
provided that
r\mid\xi.
For this reason, we will henceforth analyze
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}
exclusively; the combinatorics behind
\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{r,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)
for
r>1
is redundant.

4.
The Integer Sequence
w_{(n,\xi)}

4.1.
Combinatorial Meaning of the Fourier Inversion

We begin this section by deriving a multiple integral representation of
w_{(n,\xi)}.

Corollary 4.1
.

If
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},
then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle w_{(n,\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\left[e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right)^{|\xi^{+}|}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-i\theta_{k}}\right)^{|\xi^{-}|}\!\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right|^{2n}\right]\mathrm{d}\theta.

Proof.

According to Theorem
3.9
,

(4.2)

w_{(n,\xi)}=[x^{2n+\|\xi\|_{1}}]\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi).

Now work backwards from (
3.12
). Bearing in mind that the
k=n+|\xi^{+}|
term of the sum on the right-hand side of (
3.12
) is the only one that contributes to the integral, we see that

\displaystyle w_{(n,\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\\
|\kappa|=n+\xi^{+},\\
|\kappa^{\prime}|=n+\xi^{-}\end{subarray}}\binom{|\kappa|}{\kappa}\binom{|\kappa^{\prime}|}{\kappa^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}e^{i(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}-\xi)^{\mathsf{T}}\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\!\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\!e^{-i\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\!\left(e^{i\theta_{1}}+\cdots+e^{i\theta_{d}}\right)^{n+|\xi^{+}|}\!\left(e^{-i\theta_{1}}+\cdots+e^{-i\theta_{d}}\right)^{n+|\xi^{-}|}\mathrm{d}\theta.

Formula (
4.1
) now follows by virtue of the fact that

e^{-i\theta_{1}}+\cdots+e^{-i\theta_{d}}=\overline{e^{i\theta_{1}}+\cdots+e^{i\theta_{d}}}.

∎

In particular, if we set
\xi=\mathbf{0}_{d}
and perform a change of variables, then we recover the identity

\sum_{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=k}\binom{k}{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}}^{2}=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{n}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{2\pi ix_{k}}\right|^{2k}\mathrm{d}\theta

that was derived in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For all
x\in(-1/d,1/d),

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(1-dx)^{2}}.

Due to the Fourier inversion theorem,

(4.3)

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)=\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}(\mathbf{1}_{d})=\frac{1}{(1-dx)^{2}}.

Here, though, is a different, substantially more informative explanation.

Proof.

By construction,
\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]}}(\xi)
is an OGF for the class of ordered pairs of words from
\Sigma_{d}^{*}.
In the vein of Section I.4 of
[
10
]
, this class has combinatorial specification
\textsc{Seq}(\Sigma_{d})\times\textsc{Seq}(\Sigma_{d})
and thus an OGF of
\frac{1}{(1-dx)^{2}}.

∎

Equating the coefficients of
x^{\ell}
for both of the formal power series in Theorem
4.2
leads us to the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\\
2n+\|\xi\|_{1}=\ell\end{subarray}}w_{(n,\xi)}=(\ell+1)d^{\ell}.

We can certainly use Corollary
4.1
to prove this, but, as always, a bijective proof is more enlightening. The left-hand sum is the number of all ordered pairs of words from
\Sigma_{d}^{*}
whose concatenation has length
\ell.
On the other hand, there are
d^{\ell}
words in
\Sigma_{d}^{*}
of length
\ell,
each of which admits
\ell+1
bisections.

4.2.
Generalization of Andrews’ Heuristic.

Naturally, we would like to find a closed-form expression for
w_{(n,\xi)}.
Unfortunately, the sum in (
3.10
) and the integral in (
4.1
) look wholly intractable. The summation is over multi-indices which encumbers customary computational procedures such as the snake oil method and Gosper’s algorithm. At the time of writing,
w_{(n,\mathbf{0}_{d})}
alone has defied evaluation for slightly over three decades. Richards and Cambanis
[
5
]
proposed the problem of calculating what they call
S(n,k)=\sum[\frac{k!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots k_{n}!}]^{2},
where the sum is over all nonnegative integers
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n},
such that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k.
Ciaobă
[
6
]
once commented that “obtaining a closed formula…seems to be an interesting and difficult combinatorial problem in itself.” Andrews
[
1
]
remarked that the presence of large prime factors in
S(n,k)
makes obtaining a closed form finite product representation of
S(n,k)
implausible. As we shall see in Theorem 4.2, Lemma 4.3, and Theorem 4.4, these same heuristic barriers persist in enumerating the
n^{\text{th}}
order word pairs offset by
\xi.
We can, however, glean a noteworthy recursive trait of the
w_{(n,\xi)}.

Theorem 4.4
.

The number of
n^{\text{th}}
order word pairs offset by
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies the recurrence

(4.4)

\displaystyle w_{(n,\xi)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n+|\xi^{+}|}{j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{+}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{+}}\binom{n+|\xi^{-}|}{j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{-}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{-}}

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt\times w_{(j,(\xi_{s_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{s_{t}}))}w_{(n-j,(\xi_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{1}}},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{t}}},\ldots,\xi_{d}))},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ d\geq 2

where
t\in[1:d-1],

s_{1}<\cdots<s_{t}
are
t
natural numbers selected from
\Sigma_{d},
and
\widehat{\xi_{s_{1}}},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{t}}}
means that the indices
\xi_{s_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{s_{t}}
are deleted from
\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}\}.

Proof.

Partition
\Sigma_{d}
into two subsets
S
and
T
of
t
and
d-t
characters, respectively. Let
s_{1}<\cdots<s_{t}
be the elements of
S.
We now count the number of
n^{\text{th}}
order word pairs
(w,w^{\prime})
offset by
\xi
by conditioning on the value of
\rho_{s_{1}}(w)+\cdots+\rho_{s_{t}}(w).
Recall that
w
must have at least
\xi_{s_{k}}^{+}
occurrences of
s_{k}
for each
s_{k}\in S
and at least
\xi_{m}^{+}
occurrences of
m
for each
m\in T.
So
\rho_{s_{1}}(w)+\cdots+\rho_{s_{t}}(w)
can range from
\xi_{s_{1}}^{+}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{+}
to
n+|\xi^{+}|-\sum_{m\in T}\xi_{m}^{+}=n+\xi_{s_{1}}^{+}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{+},
inclusive.

Now for a given
j\in[1:n],
assume that
j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{+}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{+}
of the characters in
w
inhabit
S.
To preserve the assigned offsetness,
j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{-}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{-}
of the characters in
w^{\prime}
must inhabit
S
as well. Once the placements in
ww^{\prime}
for characters from
S
have been selected, they can be filled in
w_{(j,(\xi_{s_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{s_{t}}))}
ways. The remaining spots,which are comprised of characters from
T,
can be filled in
w_{(n-j,(\xi_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{1}}},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{t}}},\ldots,\xi_{d}))}
ways. Summing
\binom{n+|\xi^{+}|}{j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{+}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{+}}\binom{n+|\xi^{-}|}{j+\xi_{s_{1}}^{-}+\cdots+\xi_{s_{t}}^{-}}w_{(j,(\xi_{s_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{s_{t}}))}w_{(n-j,(\xi_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{1}}},\ldots,\widehat{\xi_{s_{t}}},\ldots,\xi_{d}))}
over all possible
j
completes the argument.
∎

We now prove a divisibility property of
w_{(n,\xi)}
that generalizes a fact about
w_{(n,\mathbf{0}_{d})}
conjectured by Andrews and later proven by Kolitsch in
[
1
]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}\equiv 0\ (\textup{mod}\ d),\hskip 25.00003pt\text{for all integers}\ n\ \text{and}\ m\ \text{not both}\ 0.

Proof.

Let the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
act on the set
C
of weak compositions of
n
into
d
parts by cyclically permuting the parts of a composition. Pick a representative
\pi^{\mathcal{O}}
from each orbit
\mathcal{O}\in C/\mathbb{Z}_{d}.
Then

(4.5)

\displaystyle w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in C/\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\mathcal{O}|\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+(m\mathbf{1}_{d})^{+}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+(m\mathbf{1}_{d})^{+}}\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+(m\mathbf{1}_{d})^{-}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+(m\mathbf{1}_{d})^{-}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in C/\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\mathcal{O}|\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}}\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+|m|\mathbf{1}_{d}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+|m|\mathbf{1}_{d}}

since one of
m^{+}
and
m^{-}
is 0 while the other is
|m|.

Now let
\mathcal{O}\in C/\mathbb{Z}_{d}
be arbitrary. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, together with Lagrange’s theorem, the sizes of the orbits divide
d,
and so

\pi^{\mathcal{O}}=(\underbrace{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1},\ldots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}}_{\text{written}\ d/|\mathcal{O}|\ \text{times}}).

Since
\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+|m|,\ldots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|m|
cannot all be 0, their greatest common divisor is nonzero. As a result,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}}

\displaystyle=\binom{(d/|\mathcal{O}|)(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+\cdots+\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|\mathcal{O}||m|)}{\underbrace{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+|m|,\ldots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|m|}_{\text{written}\ d/|\mathcal{O}|\ \text{times}}}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\ \left(\text{mod}\ \frac{(d/|\mathcal{O}|)(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+\cdots+\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|\mathcal{O}||m|)}{\gcd(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+|m|,\ldots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|m|)}\right),

in which we have applied Theorem 1 in
[
15
]
. Yet
\gcd(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+|m|,\ldots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|m|)
divides
\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+\cdots+\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|\mathcal{O}||m|
so that

(4.7)

\frac{(d/|\mathcal{O}|)(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+\cdots+\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}}+|\mathcal{O}||m|)}{\gcd(\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{1}+|m|,\cdots,\pi^{\mathcal{O}}_{|\mathcal{O}|}+|m|)}\equiv 0\ (\text{mod}\ d/|\mathcal{O}|).

Therefore

(4.8)

\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}}\equiv 0\ (\text{mod}\ d/|\mathcal{O}|),

and so

(4.9)

|\mathcal{O}|\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}}\binom{|\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}|}{\pi^{\mathcal{O}}+m\mathbf{1}_{d}}\equiv 0\ (\text{mod}\ d),

which shows that
w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}\equiv 0\ (\text{mod}\ d).

∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{\mathbf{0}\},
and for each
a\in\mathbb{Z},
let
o_{a}(\xi)
be the number of occurrences of
a
in
\xi.
Then

(4.10)

w_{(n,\xi)}\equiv 0\ \left(\textup{mod}\ \textup{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,\max_{a\neq 0}o_{a}(\xi))\right).

Proof.

Note that
\max_{a\neq 0}o_{a}(\xi)\geq 1.
By letting
a_{0}
be a nonzero integer with the maximal number of occurrences in
\xi
, we can apply Theorem
4.4
to get

(4.11)

w_{(n,\xi)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n+|\xi^{+}|}{j+ta_{0}^{+}}\binom{n+|\xi^{-}|}{j+ta_{0}^{-}}w_{(j,a_{0}\mathbf{1}_{t})}w_{(n-j,(\xi_{1},\ldots,\widehat{a_{0}\mathbf{1}_{t}},\ldots,\xi_{d}))}

for each
t\in[1:\max_{a\neq 0}o_{a}(\xi)].
It follows from Lemma
4.5
that every summand is divisible by
t.

∎

5.
Asymptotics

In this chapter, we extract the asymptotic behavior of
w_{(n,\xi)},
first as
n\to\infty
with
\xi
fixed, then as
\|\xi\|_{1}\to\infty
in a fixed direction in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
n
fixed, and then as the dimension
d\to\infty
with
n
fixed and the components of
\xi
fixed and all the same.

5.1.
Coefficient Asymptotics of
W_{\xi}

Below is our main asymptotic result.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},
then

(5.1)

w_{(n,\xi)}\sim d^{2n+d/2+\|\xi\|_{1}}(4\pi n)^{(1-d)/2}

as
n\to\infty.

This agrees with the already established leading-term asymptotic of
w_{(n,\mathbf{0}_{d})}
ascertained by Richmond and Rousseau in
[
25
]
. We shall derive the coefficient asymptotics of
W_{\xi}
by applying Laplace’s method on (
3.10
). The following version of Laplace’s method for sums over lattice point translations, which is explained in
[
16
]
, is effective for approximating sums over multi-indices.

Theorem 5.2

(Greenhill, Janson, Ruciński)
.

Suppose the following:

(i)

\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a lattice with rank
r\leq N.

(ii)

V\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
r
-dimensional subspace spanned by
\mathcal{L}.

(iii)

W=V+w
is an affine subspace parallel to
V
for some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(iv)

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a compact convex set with nonempty interior
K^{\circ}.

(v)

\phi:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and the restriction of
\phi
to
K\cap W
has a unique maximum at some point
x_{0}\in K^{\circ}\cap W.

(vi)

\phi
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
and
H_{\phi}(x_{0})
is its Hessian at
x_{0}.

(vii)

\psi:K_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function on some neighborhood
K_{1}\subset K
of
x_{0}
with
\psi(x_{0})>0.

(viii)

For each positive integer
n
there is a vector
\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\ell_{n}/n\in W.

(ix)

For each positive integer
n
there is a positive real number
b_{n}
and a function
a_{n}:(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n})\cap nK\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that, as
n\to\infty,

a_{n}(\ell)=O\!\left(b_{n}e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+o(n)}\right),\hskip 25.00003pt\ell\in(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n})\cap nK,

and

a_{n}(\ell)=b_{n}\!\left(\psi(\ell/n)+o(1)\right)e^{n\phi(\ell/n)},\hskip 25.00003pt\ell\in(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n})\cap nK_{1},

uniformly for
\ell
in the indicated sets.

Then, regarding
-H_{\phi}
as a bilinear form on
V
and provided
\left.\det\!\left(-H_{\phi}\right|_{V}\right)\neq 0,
as
n\to\infty,

\sum_{\ell\in(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n})\cap nK}a_{n}(\ell)\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{r/2}\psi(x_{0})}{\det(\mathcal{L})\sqrt{\left.\det\left(-H_{\phi}\right|_{V}\!(x_{0})\right)}}b_{n}n^{r/2}e^{n\phi(x_{0})},

where
\det(\mathcal{L})
is the square root of the discriminant of
\mathcal{L}.

Proof of Theorem 5.1.

The asymptotic trivially holds for
d=1
since
w_{(n,\xi)}=1
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{Z},
so we let
d\geq 2.
To start, write

(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})=(n_{1}-n,n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})+n(1,0,\ldots,0)

so that if
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}=n,
then
(n_{1}-n,n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})
is an integer vector whose components sum to 0. This suggests that we let
\mathcal{L}
be the root lattice
A_{d-1}=\{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\omega_{1}+\cdots+\omega_{d}=0\},
let
V
be the real linear span of
A_{d-1},
let
w=(1,0,\ldots,0),
let
W=V+w,
and let
\ell_{n}=nw.
Letting
K
be the unit hypercube
[0,1]^{d},
we have

(5.2)

w_{(n,\xi)}=(n+|\xi^{+}|)!(n+|\xi^{-}|)!\sum_{\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK}a_{n}(\ell)

where

(5.3)

a_{n}(\ell)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\ell_{j}!(\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|)!}.

Let
x_{j}=\ell_{j}/n
for all
j\in[1:d].
Applying Stirling’s approximation in the form

(5.4)

\log(n!)=n\log n-n+\frac{1}{2}\log(\max\{n,1\})+\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ n\geq 0

we obtain, uniformly for
\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK
with
n
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\log(a_{n}(\ell))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\log(\ell_{j}!)+\log((\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|)!)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left((2\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|+1)\log n-(2\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|)+\log 2\pi\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-n\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(x_{j}\log x_{j}+(x_{j}+|\xi_{j}|/n)\log(x_{j}+|\xi_{j}|/n)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\log(\max\{x_{j},1/n\})+\log(\max\{x_{j}+|\xi_{j}|/n,1/n\})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+1}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-(2n+\|\xi\|_{1}+d)\log n+2n+\|\xi\|_{1}-d\log 2\pi

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-n\sum_{j\ \text{with}\ x_{j}\neq 0}\left(x_{j}\log x_{j}+(x_{j}+|\xi_{j}|/n)(\log x_{j}+|\xi_{j}|/\ell_{j}-O(\xi_{j}^{2}/\ell_{j}^{2}))\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-n\sum_{j\ \text{with}\ x_{j}=0}\left(x_{j}\log x_{j}+(|\xi_{j}|/n)\log(|\xi_{j}|/n)\right)-\sum_{j\ \text{with}\ x_{j}=\xi_{j}=0}\log(1/n)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,\,x_{j}=0,\xi_{j}\neq 0}\left(2\log(1/n)+\log|\xi_{j}|\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,\,x_{j}\neq 0}\left(2\log x_{j}+O(|\xi_{j}|/\ell_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+1}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-(2n+\|\xi\|_{1}+d)\log n+2n-d\log 2\pi

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt+\sum_{j,\,x_{j}=0}\left(|\xi_{j}|-|\xi_{j}|\log|\xi_{j}|-\frac{1}{2}(\log|\xi_{j}|)\mathbbold{1}_{\xi_{j}\neq 0}\right)-n\sum_{j=1}^{d}2x_{j}\log x_{j}

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\log(\max\{x_{j},1/n\})+|\xi_{j}|\log(\max\{x_{j},1/n\})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\!\left(\!O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+1}\right)\!+\!O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}+|\xi_{j}|+1}\right)\!\right)\!+\!\sum_{j,\,x_{j}\neq 0}\!\left(\!O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}}\right)\!+\!O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{j}^{2}}\right)\!\right).

where
\mathbbold{1}_{\xi_{j}\neq 0}=1
if
\xi_{j}\neq 0
and 0 otherwise. We can therefore write

a_{n}(\ell)=b_{n}\psi(\ell/n)e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+\sum\limits_{j,\,x_{j}=0}\left(|\xi_{j}|-\left(|\xi_{j}|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbbold{1}_{\xi_{j}\neq 0}\right)\log|\xi_{j}|\right)}\!\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\min_{j}\ell_{j}+1}\right)\right),

where, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

b_{n}=\frac{e^{2n}}{(2\pi)^{d}n^{2n+\|\xi\|_{1}+d}},\hskip 25.00003pt\psi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{x_{j}^{|\xi_{j}|+1}},\hskip 25.00003pt\phi(x)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}2x_{j}\log x_{j},

unless if some
x_{j}
is 0, in which case we replace it with
1/n
in the formula for
\psi.
This means that

a_{n}(\ell)=O\!\left(b_{n}e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+O(1)}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(1/n)^{|\xi_{j}|+1}}\right)=O\!\left(b_{n}e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+O(\log n)}\right)

for
\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK.
On
K^{\circ},

\sum_{j,\,x_{j}=0}\left(|\xi_{j}|-\left(|\xi_{j}|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{\xi_{j}\neq 0}\right)\log|\xi_{j}|\right)

is an empty sum. Lastly,
\psi
is continuous and positive on
K^{\circ}.
All that remains is to prove that
\phi|_{K\cap W}
has a unique maximum at some point located inside
K^{\circ}\cap W
and that there is a neighborhood
K_{1}\subset K
of that point that will satisfy conditions (vii) and (ix) of Theorem
5.2
.

By Jensen’s inequality,

(5.5)

\phi(x)=2\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{x_{j}}\right)\leq 2\log d,

for all
x\in K\cap W=\{x\in K:x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}=1\}.
Since equality holds if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{d},
we see that
\phi|_{K\cap W}
attains its maximum value of
2\log d
at the point
x_{0}=(1/d)\mathbf{1}_{d}
only. Finally, the Hessian
H_{\phi}(x)
is diagonal with entries
-2/x_{j}.
Hence
H_{\phi}(x_{0})=-2dI_{d}.

Now set
K_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{2d},\frac{3}{2d}\right)^{d}.
As a continuous function,
\psi
is bounded on the compact set
\left[\frac{1}{2d},\frac{3}{2d}\right]^{d}.
Furthermore, if
\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK_{1},
then

0\leq\frac{1}{\min_{j}\ell_{j}+1}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{n}{2d}+1}\to 0

as
n\to\infty.
Hence
a_{n}(\ell)=b_{n}(\psi(\ell/n)+o(1))e^{n\phi(\ell/n)}
for
\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK_{1}.
Every assumption of Theorem
5.2
has now been verified.

It is well known that
A_{d-1}
has rank
d-1
and discriminant
d,
so we are left to calculate
\left.\det\left(-H_{\phi}\right|_{V}\!(x_{0})\right).
Let
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
be a basis for
V.
Then

(5.6)

\left.\det\left(-H_{\phi}\right|_{V}\!(x_{0})\right)=\frac{\det([\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}(2dI_{d})\mathbf{e}_{j}]_{i,j=1}^{d-1})}{\det([\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{e}_{j}]_{i,j=1}^{d-1})}=(2d)^{d-1}.

Therefore

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in(A_{d-1}+nw)\cap nK}a_{n}(\ell)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{(d-1)/2}d^{d+\|\xi\|_{1}}}{d^{1/2}(2d)^{(d-1)/2}}\frac{e^{2n}}{(2\pi)^{d}n^{2n+\|\xi\|_{1}+d}}n^{(d-1)/2}e^{2n\log d}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2n+d/2+\|\xi\|_{1}}e^{2n}}{2^{d}\pi^{(d+1)/2}n^{2n+\|\xi\|_{1}+(d+1)/2}}.

Using (
5.7
) and Stirling’s formula on (
5.2
) yields

(5.8)

\displaystyle w_{(n,\xi)}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi(n+|\xi^{+}|)}\left(\frac{n+|\xi^{+}|}{e}\right)^{n+|\xi^{+}|}\sqrt{2\pi(n+|\xi^{-}|)}\left(\frac{n+|\xi^{-}|}{e}\right)^{n+|\xi^{-}|}

\displaystyle\hskip 25.00003pt\times\frac{d^{2n+d/2+||\xi||_{1}}e^{2n}}{2^{d}\pi^{(d+1)/2}n^{2n+||\xi||_{1}+(d+1)/2}}.

Simplifying (
5.8
) via the asymptotic expressions
\sqrt{n+|\xi^{+}|}\sim\sqrt{n+|\xi^{-}|}\sim\sqrt{n},

(n+|\xi^{+}|)^{n+|\xi^{+}|}\sim n^{n+|\xi^{+}|}e^{|\xi^{+}|},
and
(n+|\xi^{-}|)^{n+|\xi^{-}|}\sim n^{n+|\xi^{-}|}e^{|\xi^{-}|}
(each as

) begets (
5.1
).
∎

5.2.
Stationary Phase Approximation of

We now derive the following leading order estimate of

as

in a fixed direction in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with

fixed.

Theorem 5.3
.

If
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{\mathbf{0}_{d}\},
then

(5.9)

w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}=\frac{(2\pi)^{1-3d/2}}{\sqrt{\|\xi\|_{1}(d+1)}}d^{\lambda\|\xi\|_{1}+2n+d/2+1}\lambda^{-d/2}(1+o(1))

as
\lambda\to\infty
with
n
fixed.

To prove this, we will employ the integral representation

w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\left[e^{-i\lambda\xi^{\textsf{T}}\theta}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{+}|}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-i\theta_{k}}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{-}|}\!\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right|^{2n}\right]\mathrm{d}\theta.

and allow the positive parameter
\lambda
to tend to
+\infty.
This oscillatory integral is mostly amenable to the following variation of stationary phase method
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Theorem 5.4

(Pemantle, Wilson)
.

Let
A
and
\varphi
be complex-valued analytic functions on a compact neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and suppose that the real part of
\varphi
is nonnegative, vanishing only at the origin. Suppose that the Hessian matrix
H_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
at the origin is nonsingular. Denoting
\mathcal{I}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathcal{N}}A(\mathbf{x})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},
there is an asymptotic series

\mathcal{I}(\lambda)\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}c_{\ell}\lambda^{-d/2-\ell}

where

c_{0}=A(\mathbf{0}_{d})\frac{(2\pi)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}

and the choice of sign of the square root is defined by taking the product of the principal square roots of the eigenvalues of
H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}).

We say “mostly” because we actually need the following slight modification of Theorem
5.4
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
A
be a complex-valued continuous function on a compact neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Let
\varphi
be a complex-valued analytic function satisfying the conditions of Theorem
5.4
. Denoting
\mathcal{I}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathcal{N}}A(\mathbf{x})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},
then

\mathcal{I}(\lambda)=A(\mathbf{0}_{d})\frac{(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}(1+o(1))

as
\lambda\to\infty.
The choice of sign of the square root is defined by taking the product of the principal square roots of the eigenvalues of
H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}).

Proof.

By the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem, there exists a sequence
\{P_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
that converges uniformly to
A
over
\mathcal{N}.
(Technically, it is the complex unital
∗
-algebra generated by
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
that is dense in
C(\mathcal{N},\mathbb{C}),
the algebra of complex-valued continuous functions on
\mathcal{N},
endowed with the infinity norm. This
∗
-algebra, however, is
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
itself since the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
are real, and so the set of complex-coefficient polynomials on
\mathcal{N}
is closed under complex conjugation.) Now for each
n\in\mathbb{N},
define

\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda):=\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}P_{n}(\mathbf{x})e^{-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Then for every
\lambda\in[0,\infty),

\left|\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda)-\mathcal{I}(\lambda)\right|\leq\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}\left|(P_{n}(\mathbf{x})-A(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\lambda\varphi(\mathbf{x})}\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{N}}|P_{n}(\mathbf{x})-A(\mathbf{x})|\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\to 0

as
n\to\infty.
Therefore
\{\mathcal{I}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges uniformly to
\mathcal{I}.
Since each
P_{n}
is analytic, Theorem
5.4
tells us that

\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda)=P_{n}(\mathbf{0}_{d})\frac{(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}(1+o(1))

as
\lambda\to\infty.
If
A(\mathbf{0}_{d})\neq 0,
then we can use the Moore-Osgood Theorem
[
28
, Theorem 7.11]
to conclude that

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{I}(\lambda)}{\frac{A(\mathbf{0}_{d})(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}}=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda)}{\frac{P_{n}(\mathbf{0}_{d})(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda)}{\frac{P_{n}(\mathbf{0}_{d})(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))}}}=1.

If
A(\mathbf{0}_{d})=0,
then the cruder limit

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\lambda)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{n}(\lambda)=0

is adequate.
∎

Proof of Theorem 5.3.

First we invoke the change of variables given by the map
\mathbf{f}_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
with components

\theta_{j}=-\tau_{j-1}+\tau_{j}+\delta,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq d

where we have introduced the placeholders

in order to avoid messy casework later on. (One may envision this as a transformation from the Cartesian coordinate system to a sort-of “signed distance from
A_{d-1}

\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

-\delta
gives the value of
t
for which the hyperplane
\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d}=0
intersects with the normal line
\ell(t)=\theta_{0}+t\mathbf{1}_{d}
and
(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})
are the coordinates of this intersection point relative to the standard basis of
A_{d-1}.
) The Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{f}_{d}
is

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{d}}(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1},\delta)=\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{\partial\theta_{1}}{\partial\tau_{1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{1}}{\partial\tau_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\theta_{1}}{\partial\tau_{d-1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{1}}{\partial\delta}\\
\frac{\partial\theta_{2}}{\partial\tau_{1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{2}}{\partial\tau_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\theta_{2}}{\partial\tau_{d-1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{2}}{\partial\delta}\\
\frac{\partial\theta_{3}}{\partial\tau_{1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{3}}{\partial\tau_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\theta_{3}}{\partial\tau_{d-1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{3}}{\partial\delta}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\theta_{d}}{\partial\tau_{1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{d}}{\partial\tau_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\theta_{d}}{\partial\tau_{d-1}}&\frac{\partial\theta_{d}}{\partial\delta}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0&1\\
-1&1&\ddots&0&1\\
0&-1&\ddots&0&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&-1&1\end{matrix}\right],

a
d\times d
matrix with 1s on the main diagonal, 1s in the last column,
-1

We use induction to show that the Jacobian determinant is equal to
d.
For the base case
d=1,

\mathbf{f}_{1}(\theta_{1})=\delta
which trivially has a Jacobian determinant of
1.
Now assume the induction hypothesis holds for some integer
k,
that is,
\det(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{k}})=k.
Laplace expansion along the first row of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{k+1}}
yields

\det(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{k+1}})=\det(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{k}})+(-1)^{k+2}\det(J_{-1,k})

where

is the
k\times k
Jordan block with eigenvalue
-1.
We thus have

\det(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}_{k+1}})\underbrace{=}_{\text{ind. hyp.}}k+(-1)^{k+2}(-1)^{k}=k+1.

Since the Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant,
\mathbf{f}_{d}
is invertible and
\mathbf{f}_{d}^{-1}
is differentiable. The transformation produces the following integral representation:

(5.10)

\displaystyle w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{(2\pi)^{d}}\!\int\limits_{\mathbf{f}_{d}^{-1}([-\pi,\pi]^{d})}\!\left[e^{-i\lambda\xi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}_{d}^{-1}(\theta)}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}+\delta)}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{+}|}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}+\delta)}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{-}|}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\left.\times\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}+\delta)}\right|^{2n}\right]\!\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}\tau_{d-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\delta.

Note

and
e^{-i\delta}
can be factored out of

and

respectively, to get

\displaystyle w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{(2\pi)^{d}}\!\int\limits_{\mathbf{f}_{d}^{-1}([-\pi,\pi]^{d})}\!\left[e^{-i\lambda\xi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}_{d}^{-1}(\theta)}e^{i\delta\lambda|\xi^{+}|}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{+}|}\!e^{-i\delta\lambda|\xi^{-}|}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{-}|}\right.

so that every instance of

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\left.\times\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{-}|}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\right|^{2n}\right]\!\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}\tau_{d-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\delta.

Integrate with respect to
\delta
first to get

(5.11)

\displaystyle w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}\left[\left(2\pi+m(\tau)-M(\tau)\right)e^{-i\lambda\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}\xi_{k}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{i(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})}\right)^{\lambda|\xi^{+}|}\right.

where

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}),

]

\displaystyle\det(H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d}))

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\|\xi\|_{1}}{d}\right)^{d}\lim_{D\to-2^{-}}\frac{(-1)^{d}\sinh\!\left((d+1)\cosh^{-1}(-D/2)\right)}{\sinh\!\left(\cosh^{-1}(-D/2)\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\|\xi\|_{1}}{d}\right)^{d}(d+1)

By Proposition 2.1 of
[
19
]
, the eigenvalues of
H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d})
are

-\frac{\|\xi\|_{1}}{d}\left(-2-2\cos\frac{k\pi}{d+1}\right)=\frac{4\|\xi\|_{1}}{d}\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{k\pi}{2(d+1)}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ k=1,2,\ldots,d,

and so the product of the principal square roots of the eigenvalues of
H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d})
is a nonnegative real number. We thus take the positive square root of
H_{\varphi}(\mathbf{0}_{d})
and apply Corollary
5.5
to get that

(5.14)

w_{(n,\lambda\xi)}=\frac{d^{\lambda\|\xi\|_{1}+1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\cdot 2\pi d^{2n}\frac{(2\pi\lambda)^{-d/2}}{\sqrt{(\|\xi\|_{1}/d)^{d}(d+1)}}(1+o(1))

as
\lambda\to\infty.

∎

5.3.
The Association Between
w_{(n,\xi)}
and Modified Bessel Functions of the First Kind

The cardinalities
w_{(n,\xi)}
can be linked to products of modified Bessel functions of the first kind, which are functions given by the power series

(5.15)

I_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ \nu\in\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{Z}_{<0},

where
\Gamma
is the eponymous gamma function. It is customary to keep to the principal branch of
(z/2)^{\nu}
so that
I_{\nu}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}-(-\infty,0]
and two-valued and discontinuous on the cut
\text{Arg}\,z=\pm\pi
(if
\nu\not\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). To facilitate the asymptotic analysis of
w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})},
we will instead work with the normalized Bessel function

(5.16)

\tilde{I}_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ \nu\in\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{Z}_{<0},

which is entire on
\mathbb{C}.
Next, observe that

(5.17)

w_{(n,\xi)}=(n+|\xi^{+}|)!(n+|\xi^{-}|)![x^{n}]\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\tilde{I}_{|\xi_{j}|}\!\left(2\sqrt{x}\right)}{|\xi_{j}|!}\right).

In particular,

(5.18)

w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}=\frac{n!(n+d|m|)!}{(|m|!)^{d}}[x^{n}]\left(\tilde{I}_{|m|}(2\sqrt{x})\right)^{d}.

Bender, Brody, and Meister
[
3
]
proved that

(5.19)

\left(\tilde{I}_{\nu}(z)\right)^{d}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n},

where
B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)
is a polynomial defined recursively by

(5.20)

B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)=d\frac{\nu+n}{\nu+1}B^{(\nu)}_{n-1}(d)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{b_{j}(\nu)}{n}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+2)}{\Gamma(j+\nu+2)}\binom{\nu+n}{j}B^{(\nu)}_{n-1}(d).

with initial condition
b^{(\nu)}_{0}(d)=1.
The sequence
\{b_{n}(\nu)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is identified by the generating function

(5.21)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n}(\nu)}{(n-1)!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}x^{n}=\frac{x}{\Gamma(\nu+2)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\nu+1}\frac{I_{\nu}(2\sqrt{x})}{I_{\nu+1}(2\sqrt{x})}-2\right).

However, Moll and Vignat
[
21
]
found an alternative characterization of
B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)
in terms of the exponent
d.
To start, they exploit the Hadamard factorization

(5.22)

\tilde{I}_{\nu}(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{j_{\nu,k}^{2}}\right),

where
\{j_{\nu,k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an enumeration of the zeros of
\tilde{I}_{\nu}(iz),
from which it follows that

(5.23)

\log\tilde{I}_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\zeta_{n}(2n)z^{2n},

where
\zeta_{\nu}
is the Bessel zeta function

(5.24)

\zeta_{\nu}(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j_{\nu,k}^{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ p>1.

They then cite the fact that the exponential of a power series is computed via

(5.25)

\exp\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

where
\mathbf{B}_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
is the
n^{\text{th}}
complete Bell polynomial. (Read
[
27
]
, section 5.2, for details.) Multiplying (
5.23
) by
d
and then plugging it into (
5.25
) leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 5.6

(Moll, Vignat)
.

Define

(5.26)

a_{n}=a^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\zeta_{\nu}(2n)d.

Then
B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)
is given by

(5.27)

B^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)=4^{n}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\mathbf{B}_{n}(a^{(\nu)}_{1}(d),\ldots,a^{(\nu)}_{n}(d)).

Substitute (
5.27
) into (
5.19
) to get

(5.28)

w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}=\frac{4^{n}(n+d|m|)!}{(|m|!)^{d}}\mathbf{B}_{n}(a^{|m|}_{1}(d),\ldots,a^{|m|}_{n}(d)).

We are now poised to derive the following asymptotic formula.

Theorem 5.7
.

(5.29)

w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}(|m|d)^{|m|d+1/2}}{(|m|!e^{|m|})^{d}}\left(\frac{|m|d^{2}}{|m|+1}\right)^{n}+O(d^{|m|d+2n-1/2})

as
d\to\infty
with
n
and
m\neq 0
fixed. If
m=0,
then

(5.30)

w_{(n,\mathbf{0}_{d})}=n!d^{n}+O(d^{n-1}).

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{B}_{n}(a^{|m|}_{1}(d),\ldots,a^{|m|}_{n}(d))
is a polynomial in
d,
it suffices to find its degree and leading coefficient. This is achieved by invoking the Bell polynomial’s determinantal representation

(5.31)

\mathbf{B}_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\text{det}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}&-1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{3}&\binom{2}{1}a_{2}&a_{1}&-1&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n-1}&\binom{n-2}{1}a_{n-2}&\binom{n-2}{2}a_{n-3}&\binom{n-2}{3}a_{n-4}&\ddots&-1\\
a_{n}&\binom{n-1}{1}a_{n-1}&\binom{n-1}{2}a_{n-2}&\binom{n-1}{3}a_{n-3}&\cdots&a_{1}\end{matrix}\right).

(This determinantal representation is explained in
[
7
]
, albeit with a typographical error.) Each
a_{i}
includes exactly one factor of
d,
and so, because the above matrix is lower triangular save for the superdiagonal comprised entirely of
-1
s,
\mathbf{B}_{n}(a^{|m|}_{1}(d),\ldots,a^{|m|}_{n}(d))
is an
n^{\text{th}}
degree polynomial in
d
with leading coefficient given by the product of the diagonal entries of (
5.31
), which is

(5.32)

\left(a^{|m|}_{1}(d)\right)^{n}=(d\zeta_{|m|}(2))^{n}=\left(\frac{d}{4(|m|+1)}\right)^{n}.

(Here we have used the fact that
\zeta_{\nu}(2)=\frac{1}{4(\nu+1)}
which is elaborated on page 502 of
[
30
]
.) Hence

(5.33)

w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}=\frac{4^{n}(n+d|m|)!}{(|m|!)^{d}}\left(\left(\frac{d}{4(|m|+1)}\right)^{n}+O(d^{n-1})\right).

Applying Stirling’s approximation on
(n+d|m|)!
completes the proof.
∎

Formula (
5.33
) suggests that
w_{(n,m\mathbf{1}_{d})}
is roughly equal to

\binom{n+d|m|}{|m|,\ldots,|m|,n}\left(\frac{d}{|m|+1}\right)^{n}n!

when
d
is substantially large. In the case that
m=0,
we get
d^{n}n!,
which is the number of ways to construct a length
n
string
w
and a permutation
w^{\prime}
of
w,
provided that the characters of
w
are always mutually distinct. This, of course, is untrue, and so
d^{n}n!
is a bit of an overestimate for the number of abelian squares of length
2n.
However, if one chooses a character from
\Sigma_{d}
uniform randomly and independently for each letter of
w,
then the probability that
w
has no repeated characters is

\frac{d(d-1)\cdots(d-n+1)}{d^{n}}=1-O\!\left(\frac{1}{d^{n-1}}\right).

The characters of
w
are therefore asymptotically almost surely different, and so
d^{n}n!
is a satisfactory estimate for
w_{(n,\mathbf{0}_{d})}
when
d
is very large relative to
n.

6.
Discussion and Future Work

In this chapter we briefly discuss three possible avenues of future investigation.

6.1.
Fourier Analysis of Generating Functions

We begin by applying the Cauchy-Hadamard formula to
W_{\xi},
for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},
to see that
W_{\xi}
has a radius of convergence of

(6.1)

\frac{1}{\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}|w_{(n,\xi)}|^{1/n}}=\frac{1}{\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\,(d^{2n+d/2+\|\xi\|_{1}}(4\pi n)^{(1-d)/2})^{1/n}}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

This coupled with Plancharel’s theorem from harmonic analysis leads us to conclude the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

The spectral density function
\mathcal{S}_{p_{1,d}^{\star}[x]},
with
|x|<1/d^{2},
is, up to sets of measure zero, the only operator
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
that satisfies

x^{\frac{1}{2}\|\xi\|_{1}}W_{\xi}(x)=\widehat{f(x)}(\xi)

for all
x\in(-1/d^{2},1/d^{2}).

Theorems
4.2
and
6.1
hint towards “generatingfunctionological” analogues of several classical results from harmonic analysis. We plan to explore these ideas further in a sequel paper.

6.2.
Stabilized Symmetric Polynomials

Because the ring of symmetric polynomials with rational coefficients equals the rational polynomial ring generated by the power sum symmetric polynomials, one can then “stabilize” any rational symmetric polynomial. How, if at all, does this translate into operations on free objects over a finite set in general?

6.3.
A Unified Framework for “Offsetness”

At heart, the extent of a word pair’s “offsetness” is the magnitude of the difference between the component words’ “frequencies,” which are given by their Parikh vectors. Fundamentally, this is what suggests Fourier analytic treatment. Unitarity of the Fourier transform assures a one-to-one correspondence between the component Parikh vectors of a word pair and the offsetness of their concatenation.

Similar constructs should therefore be permissible on any Cartesian product of an unlabelled combinatorial class with itself. Candidates for analogous notions of offsetness include concave and convex compositions, rooted trees where the root has two children, and directed multigraphs. A more thorough, unified development of this concept may therefore be worthwhile.
