Exact calculation of quantizer constants for arbitrary lattices
Daniel Pook-Kolb, Bruce Allen, Erik Agrell

Abstract
We present an algorithm for the exact computer-aided construction of the Voronoi cells of lattices with known symmetry group. Our algorithm scales better than linearly with the total number of faces and is applicable to dimensions beyond 12 12 , which previous methods could not achieve. The new algorithm is applied to the Coxeter–Todd lattice K 12 K_{12} as well as to a family of lattices obtained from laminating K 12 K_{12} . By optimizing this family, we obtain a new best 13 13 -dimensional lattice quantizer (among the lattices with published exact quantizer constants).



Keywords—
lattice quantizer, quantizer constant, normalized second moment,
Coxeter–Todd lattice,
laminated lattice, Voronoi cell

11H06, 11H56, 52B20, 52C07

The Voronoi cells of each lattice point are translated copies of each other,

In fact,

\Omega=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:2\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{n}}\|^{2},\ \ \forall{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega)\right\}\;.

A generalization of the second moment

The

Zamir and Feder show in

This result has been generalized

The geometric interpretation of the second moment tensor being

When viewed as a discrete subset of

2.3
Finding the vertices

Given the description of
\Omega
by the relevant vectors
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
,
determining the vertices
\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
is a

representation conversion
problem.
Standard approaches to this problem are described in

[
32
]
.
Other methods, which make use of symmetries, are given in

[
9
,
12
,
18
,
19
]

and publicly available code
[
20
]
.

We take a different, more pragmatic approach. Instead of computing
the full set of vertices
\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
given
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
, we
find a subset of vertices using a random search and use
\mathcal{G}
as well
as the translational symmetries of the lattice to determine additional
vertices. As discussed below, this alone does not ensure that we find
the full set of vertices. However, the
subsequent steps of our analysis provide a proof
of completeness. In practice, this has turned out to be an efficient
method to determine the full set
\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
with reasonable
computational cost.

To find a subset of
\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
, we do not form intersections of facets
as in step 2 of Sec.
2.1
. Instead, it is more efficient
to search
within
\Omega

for local maxima of
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}
.
This can be done using linear programming, with
\Omega
characterized by
(
9
), and yields exact expressions of the vertices.
As in
[
5
, Section 2]
, we solve

\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega}({\boldsymbol{c}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}})

multiple times, but rather than choosing
{\boldsymbol{c}}
uniformly, we find it more
efficient to set it to a relevant vector
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
with a
small random perturbation.

Further vertices are obtained by utilizing the translational symmetries of

\Lambda
as follows:
Let
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be a vertex and let
\mathcal{M}({\boldsymbol{v}})
be the set of nearest
lattice points of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
.
These can be found using the algorithms presented in
[
3
]
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a vertex of the Voronoi cell, the origin is one of those lattice
points.
More generally,
{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a vertex of all the Voronoi cells

\Omega({\boldsymbol{n}})
for any

{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{M}({\boldsymbol{v}})
.
However, since all the Voronoi cells are congruent translated copies of
each other, we have

{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)\implies{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega),\ \ \forall{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{M}({\boldsymbol{v}})\;.

(15)

Vertices found via (
15
) need not be equivalent
under
\mathcal{G}
, as we have used the translational symmetries here.
These are, by definition, not included in the automorphism group
\mathcal{G}

since that fixes the origin.
Any new vertex thus leads to further vertices by constructing its
orbit under
\mathcal{G}
.

Whenever we construct the orbit of a vertex, we first define that vertex as
the fixed representative of its class and assign it a new
Upon applying the group to this representative, we store the representative
and the group element in the respective fields of our “Vector” class
(see Fig.
1
).
We use the sets of vertices in the orbits found thus far
in order to quickly check if any newly found vertex represents a new or a
known class.
Vertices in known classes are then discarded.

The search for vertices is continued with random initial conditions
until no new classes of vertices appear.
We do not have a strict criterion for stopping this search.
However, when many iterations produce only known classes, one may proceed with
the analysis under the assumption that all vertices have been found.
At a later point, a side product of our calculations is the volume of
the convex hull of the vertices. Comparing this volume with the known
volume
\Vol(\Lambda)=\lvert\det{\boldsymbol{B}}\rvert
provides an
unambiguous consistency check, since the convex hull of every proper
subset of the full set of vertices also has a strictly smaller volume
than the full Voronoi cell. This technique of testing for
completeness has been used previously in
[
37
]
.
However, the test can not be carried out during or immediately
after the vertex search, because the volume is only available near
the end of the second moment computations. So although the overall
computation tests that the vertex classes are complete, that test is
far more expensive than the vertex search.

Our random search for vertices makes the task of determining all vertices feasible even for
higher dimensions.
For example, the symmetry group of laminated
K_{12}
(see Sec.
4.2
)
partitions the

52\,351\,632

vertices of the Voronoi cell into
482
different classes.
By using (
15

classes from just
74
vertices.
This means that a random search needs to find at least one of each
of the
74
subsets of vertices, which in practice is accomplished on a single core
within less than a day.

A different method for finding vertices is given in

[
21
, Section 3.2]
.
After finding a single initial vertex using a cutting-plane algorithm

[
21
, Section 3.3]
, it is deterministic and finds all
vertices of
\Omega
up to automorphisms and translations by lattice vectors.

The method
[
21
, Section 3.3]
of finding the initial vertex
shares certain aspects with our random search for vertices.
However, the two methods scale differently with the number of vertices found.
While our method is faster when finding many vertices, as needed here,
the algorithm in
[
21
, Section 3.3]
is faster when only a
very small number of vertices is required.

In both vertex-finding algorithms, the complexity is dominated by solving

linear programs
(LP) and finding
all closest points
(ACP) in a lattice.
Let
C_{\text{LP}}(n,k)
denote the complexity of solving an LP in
n
dimensions with

k
constraints.
Let
C_{\text{ACP}}(n)
denote complexity of solving an ACP problem in
n
dimensions.
These functions depend not only on
n
and
k
but also on the lattice,
implementation, and hardware.

For our algorithm, the relevant vectors are determined in a preprocessing step
using
[
3
]
.
This requires solving
2^{n}-1
ACP problems.
To find one vertex when the
r
relevant vectors are known, an LP with
r/2

constraints is solved.
For a single vertex, including the preprocessing, the complexity is thus

(2^{n}-1)C_{\text{ACP}}(n)+C_{\text{LP}}(n,r/2)

(16)

and
per vertex
it is

C_{\text{PAA}}=\frac{2^{n}-1}{M}C_{\text{ACP}}(n)+C_{\text{LP}}(n,r/2)

(17)

when
M

In contrast, the algorithm in
[
21
, Section 3.3]
requires no
preprocessing.
To find one vertex, an iterative process is applied, where each step
consists of solving one LP and one ACP problem.
The number of constraints increases in every iteration from
2n
in the first LP.
The number of iterations needed varies with different vertices for the same
lattice, and so does the increasing sequence of the number of constraints.
If
L
iterations are needed to find a certain vertex, and the numbers of
constraints are
c_{1},\ldots,c_{L}
, where
c_{1}=n/2
, then the complexity
of the algorithm in
[
21
, Section 3.3]

to find that vertex is

L\,C_{\text{ACP}}(n)+\sum^{L}_{i=1}C_{\text{LP}}(n,c_{i})\;.

(18)

The average complexity per vertex is therefore

C_{\text{DSV}}=\mathbb{E}[L]\,C_{\text{ACP}}(n)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{L}_{i=1}C_{\text{LP}}(n,c_{i})\right]\;,

(19)

where the expectations are over different sequences of
L
and

c_{1},\ldots,c_{L}
, depending on the starting points.

We illustrate the difference with
K_{12}
in
n=12

dimensions.
It has
r=4\,788
relevant vectors.
We found
M=1\,000
vertices using the algorithm in

[
21
, Section 3.3]

and obtained sequences of
L
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{L}
for each vertex.
The number of iterations
L
was on average
40.9
, ranging from
29
to
52
.
The
40\,900
LPs used on average
70.5
constraints, ranging from
24
to
330
.

K_{12}
.

While the numerical results will vary for different dimensions, lattices,

for our application, our method is more than an order of magnitude faster than

[

We will describe our solution to this problem in two parts.
The first part is discussed in this section

2.4.1

Our method is similar to that of

Our basic construction is visualized in Fig.

We point out a few important properties of this structure:

(

(

(

(

none of its children are constructed.

Points

and

are essential for
the correctness of our results.
After describing our algorithm in detail, we will therefore give proofs that
the resulting structure possesses these properties.

One might be worried that the arbitrary choice of a representative in each

For example, consider two inequivalent representatives

2.4.4
Iterated classification

The classification step in Alg.

For a given set of faces, one may perform the classification via

The choice of subgroups may have a large influence on the resulting

In practice, it turned out to be sufficient to use a subset of stabilizers of

Calculating the second moment

With the face hierarchy built as discussed in the previous section, it is
possible to calculate the second moment tensor (
7
) of the
Voronoi cell
\Omega
using explicit recursion relations.
The strategy for our calculations closely follows the method described in
Section 3 of
[
5
]
, which corrects and extends the
original relations derived in Section IV-C of
[
37
]
.

In our case, however, we can greatly reduce the computational cost of
evaluating these relations by making use of the symmetries of the faces.
When a quantity has been computed for a representative in

\mathcal{F}^{\text{rep}}_{d}
, we immediately obtain this quantity for all equivalent faces.
Scalar quantities are equal for all faces within a class, while
vector or tensor quantities can be transformed
using the known group element.
This significantly reduces the number of steps in our calculations.

Let
F_{d}\in\mathcal{F}^{\text{rep}}_{d}
be the selected representative of one class of

d
-faces.
Its centroid
{\boldsymbol{c}}(F_{d})
is defined as the mean of its vertices
\mathcal{V}(F_{d})
,

{\boldsymbol{c}}(F_{d})\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}(F_{d})|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{V}(F_{d})}{\boldsymbol{v}}\;,

(39)

and, due to the convexity of
F_{d}
, the centroid is guaranteed to lie in
F_{d}
.
The volume of
F_{d}
is defined by

\Vol(F_{d})\triangleq\int_{F_{d}}d^{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\;.

(40)

A recursion relation for the volume is

\Vol(F_{d})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{C}(F_{d})}h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)\Vol(F)\;,

(41)

where
\mathcal{C}(F_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{d-1}
is the set of all child faces of
F_{d}

and
h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)
is the height of the parent’s centroid,
{\boldsymbol{c}}(F_{d})
, above the plane containing
its child face
F
.

The height
h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)
is calculated in either of
two ways described in
[
5
]
.
The first is to project the difference

\Delta{\boldsymbol{c}}_{F}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{c}}(F)-{\boldsymbol{c}}(F_{d})

onto the space orthogonal to the child face
F

and then calculating the norm.
This requires the orthogonalization and normalization of a
linearly independent set of, e.g.,
relevant vectors
\mathcal{N}(F)
orthogonal to
F
.

The second method of computing
h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)
is to construct any set of vectors

{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{d-1}
,
spanning the
(d-1)
-dimensional plane the child
F
lies in.
12
12

12

This set of spanning vectors can be obtained recursively, starting with the

1
-faces and taking the vector connecting its two vertices.
Then, in dimensions
k>1
, we take the spanning vectors of an arbitrary child face
and append the vector connecting the centroid of the
k
-face to the centroid
of the child.

Then we have

h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)^{2}=\frac{\det\Gram({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{d-1},\Delta{\boldsymbol{c}}_{F})}{\det\Gram({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{d-1})}\;,

(42)

where
\Gram(\,\cdot\,)
is the Gram matrix of the vectors in its argument.

As in
[
5

, the decision on which method to use is based on the
dimension

For low dimensions, calculating the spanning vectors and Gram determinants
turned out to be faster while in higher dimensions, the projections are less
expensive.
We switch the method usually close to

seemed not to become a major factor up to

Being a scalar quantity that is invariant under
\mathcal{G}
, we calculate the volume

The recursion starts with the

0

Similarly, the barycenter of

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{b}}(F_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}\Bigg({\boldsymbol{c}}(F_{d})

\displaystyle\qquad+(\Delta{\boldsymbol{b}}_{F})^{T}\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{b}}_{F}\,\Vol(F)\Big]\;.

\Delta{\boldsymbol{b}}_{F}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{b}}(F_{d})-{\boldsymbol{b}}(F)
.
The heights
h_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}(F)
are calculated in the same way as
h_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(F)
.

The second moment tensors
{\boldsymbol{U}}(F)
of child faces are obtained from those of
their representative faces as

{\boldsymbol{U}}(F)={\boldsymbol{M}}_{g}^{T}{\boldsymbol{U}}({F^{\text{rep}}}){\boldsymbol{M}}_{g}\;,

(47)

where, as in (
12
),

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{g}^{T}
is
the matrix representing
the transformation
g\in\mathcal{G}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Finally, taking the trace of (
46
) provides a recursion
for the scalar second moment

\displaystyle U(F_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+2}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{C}(F_{d})}\!h_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}(F)\Big[U(F)

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\Delta{\boldsymbol{b}}_{F}\|^{2}\,\Vol(F)\Big]\;.

(48)

]
)
or numerical

[

)
library.
This makes the implementations of the second moment, volume, and the other

For certain dimensions, there are specializations of

See the main text for details.

shows the classes responsible for implementing

Recall from Fig.

that each

This class contains the high-level implementations of the formulas presented
in the present section.
The low-level operations are
performed by the “Calculator” object.
This latter object
is a thin wrapper around a symbolic or numerical library.

Any time a property is requested from a face that is not the selected
representative of its class, the value is retrieved (and suitably
transformed) from the representative.
Such a request may happen in the recursive formulas where quantities of
child faces are needed.
Since we are guaranteed to have all children of the representative faces, the
algorithms in the “FaceProperties” class always have all the information
they need.

Additionally, the “FaceProperties” instances hold a cache of results
calculated for representative faces.

other faces in its class.

Certain properties have specialized algorithms for faces of dimension

(vertices),
1
(edges),
n-1
(facets), and/or

(the top face, i.e., the Voronoi cell).
For example, a vertex has a fixed volume of
1
and a

Similarly, the centroid of the Voronoi cell is always the origin.
The specialized methods are overridden in subclasses of the
“FaceProperties” class.

4
Analyzing families of lattices

In this section, we apply our algorithm to calculate the second moment
G

of an individual lattice and
to minimize
G
in a one-parameter family of lattices.
In
9
dimensions, such an optimization problem has led to the conjectured
optimal lattice quantizer
\text{AE}_{9}

[
5
]
, which is obtained by

laminating
the root lattice
D_{8}
.
We will first review the closely related construction of

product lattices
and then the
lamination procedure.
This is then applied to optimize a family of
13
-dimensional lattices.

Product lattices are analyzed in detail in
[
1
]
.
If
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
are lattices of dimension
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
,
respectively, then the one-parameter family of product lattices of
dimension
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
is defined via

\Lambda(a)\triangleq\Lambda_{1}\times a\Lambda_{2}\;,

(49)

where
a>0
is a real parameter.
Let
G(a)
be the quantizer constant of
\Lambda(a)
and
let further
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}
,
V_{i}
, and
G_{i}
be, respectively, a generator matrix, the volume
and the quantizer constant of
\Lambda_{i}
.
Then a generator matrix for
\Lambda(a)
is

{\boldsymbol{B}}(a)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&a{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(50)

The quantizer constant of
\Lambda(a)
is minimal when

[
1
, Corollary 5]

a=a_{\text{opt}}\triangleq\frac{V_{1}^{1/n_{1}}}{V_{2}^{1/n_{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{G_{1}}{G_{2}}}\;,

(51)

for which
G(a)
satisfies

G^{n}(a_{\text{opt}})=G_{1}^{n_{1}}G_{2}^{n_{2}}\;.

(52)

From a collection of different lattices with known values for
G
,
Agrell and Allen form more than
30
product lattices whose
quantizer constants are smaller than any previously published ones
in their respective dimension.
Additional improved product lattices are constructed in
[
25
]

in dimensions
15
,
19
,
21
, and
23
.
Starting at low dimensions
n
, the first one
found in
[
1
]
is
a product of the Coxeter–Todd lattice
K_{12}
and the one-dimensional lattice

{\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e.,
\Lambda=K_{12}\times a{\mathbb{Z}}
, which has

G(a_{\text{opt}})\approx 0.071034583\;.

(53)

The following key result motivates investigating
K_{12}\times a{\mathbb{Z}}

further.
If
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
are two lattices with generator matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}

and
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}
as before,
then we define
\Lambda_{{\boldsymbol{H}}}(a)
as the lattice generated by

{\boldsymbol{B}}_{{\boldsymbol{H}}}(a)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{H}}&a{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(54)

where
{\boldsymbol{H}}
is an
n_{2}\times n_{1}
matrix.
Let
G_{{\boldsymbol{H}}}(a)
be the quantizer constant of
\Lambda_{{\boldsymbol{H}}}(a)
.
Then it is shown in
[
1
, Theorem 7]
that

G_{{\boldsymbol{H}}}(a)\leq G(a),\ \ \forall{\boldsymbol{H}}\;.

(55)

This means that in general, any product lattice (
49
)
can be further optimized by taking
{\boldsymbol{H}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
.
In particular, it should be possible to get better than (
53
) in

13
dimensions.

The special case of the construction (
54
)
with
n_{2}=1
(i.e.,
\Lambda_{2}={\mathbb{Z}}
)
is called a
lamination
of
\Lambda_{1}
.
For that case, we will write

{\boldsymbol{B}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}(a)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{h}}&a\end{bmatrix},

(56)

where
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
is the
offset vector
.
We will henceforth assume that
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is fixed and drop
the subscript of the quantities
{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
G
of the laminated lattice

\Lambda(a)
.

Note that for
{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, we no longer have a general closed form
expression for the optimal value
a_{\text{opt}}
that minimizes
G(a)
.
For example,
\text{AE}_{9}
is obtained by laminating
D_{8}
with
{\boldsymbol{h}}
chosen as
a vertex of the Voronoi cell of
D_{8}
that is most distant from
{\boldsymbol{0}}
.
Such a vertex is called a
deep hole
.
It is shown in
[
5
]
that the optimal value of
a=0.573\ldots
is
an algebraic number whose square is a root of a
9
th order polynomial.

In the following, we will try a similar strategy in
13
dimensions and
laminate
K_{12}
in the direction of a deep hole.
This will require calculating
G
as a function of
a
and then finding the
minimum of
G(a)
.
Our method for doing so is discussed in Sec.
4.2
.
However, in order to verify our code and to explore the possible symmetries of
laminated
K_{12}
, we shall first apply our algorithm to
K_{12}
itself.

In this section, we apply our algorithm to a lamination

{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}

Since we are interested in finding the minimum of

via Monte Carlo integration for several values
of

A resulting rough estimate

In the present case of laminated

We next carry out the full analysis at

and determine

of all faces

as

This can be done via symbolic calculations of the equations in

We remark that instead of symbolically calculating with an unknown

Following the discussion in
[

, we used

to initially determine

and
later verified our results with full symbolic calculation with

At this point, we do not yet know for which values of

As

resulting in changes to the hierarchy of faces.
At these

values of

, not only may the functional dependence
of

on

the whole data structure we build for one value of

We perform the following steps to determine the domain of

where our
results apply.
This is done after the data structure has been calculated at

and the volumes of all
d

.
First,
we find an interval

\Vol(P(a))

,

does not need to agree with
\Omega(a)
.

\Omega(a)
can split in this interval and new faces may appear,
so that the vertices of

need not coincide with those of

\Omega(a)
for all

.
Therefore, we additionally check the following

)

) The

.

) and

.

.

5
Conclusions

For a lattice whose symmetry group is known,
the algorithm presented in this work provides a way to explicitly construct a
part of the full face hierarchy of its Voronoi cell.
This partial structure contains sufficient information for exactly
calculating, among other properties, its quantizer constant
G
.
In addition, one obtains a full classification of all faces of the cell,
despite most faces never being constructed in this process.

A key property of our approach is the possibility to parallelize the
algorithm,
which enables the work to be distributed over many
processor cores.
By carefully planning the steps needed to evaluate the recursion formulas
for
G
, parallelization can be employed not only during
construction of the face hierarchy, but also in the subsequent
computations.

We have applied our algorithm to reproduce the results known for
\text{AE}_{9}
and

K_{12}
and then analyzed a new
13
-dimensional family of lattices obtained
by laminating
K_{12}
.
This led to a new currently best known lattice quantizer in
13
dimensions.
With
430\,051
different classes of faces, analyzing this family with our
method proved to still be computationally feasible.

The most expensive part is determining
whether two faces are equivalent under the lattice’s symmetry group and
obtaining a group element transforming one into the other.
If a more efficient method can be found for this step, then most of the
strategy discussed in this paper is still applicable
and may enable the analysis of lattices in significantly higher dimensions.

Although the algorithm presented here builds upon that developed for the analysis of

\text{AE}_{9}

[
5
]
, it contains major improvements in several key aspects.
Most significantly, it exploits the symmetries of a lattice during
construction of the face hierarchy, which is not done in
[
5
]
.
This has been crucial for the analysis in higher dimensions.
For example, for
K_{12}
, only about
15\,000
of the in total

11\,971\,901\,593
faces are constructed in the new method.
It also improves upon
[
5
]
by utilizing the translational symmetries
of the lattice in the search for vertices.

Some of our methods have similarities with those of
Dutour Sikirić, Schürmann, and Vallentin
[
21
]
.
Both use the automorphisms combined with the translational symmetries of a
lattice to find representatives of all vertices of the Voronoi cell.
Furthermore, both methods calculate the scalar second moment and
the second moment tensor (which in
[
21
, p. 1725]
is called
I_{2}(P)
).

There are also a number of differences.
One example is the already-discussed different method to test for the
equivalence of faces.
Another example is our search for vertices.
We find all representatives of vertices via a random search
using a carefully chosen distribution of initial vectors.
This is optimized to run many times by pre-computing all relevant vectors and
using these to find a vertex in a single linear program.
In contrast,
[
21
]
finds one random vertex using multiple linear
programs and then deterministically constructs representatives of all other
classes of vertices. This is done by constructing the Delaunay cell of the
vertex and then finding and classifying all adjacent Delaunay cells
iteratively until no new classes appear.
This graph traversal algorithm completes when all classes have been found.
A further difference is that we unconditionally construct the full
face hierarchy (up to symmetry), whereas
[
21
]
uses a heuristic for
deciding to construct only a part of it, using a simplicial decomposition
of some faces instead.
Another difference from
[
21
]
is that we use floating point operations
to speed up certain steps, although our method does not depend upon
floating point operations and can be implemented without them.
On an implementation level, a major difference is that our method can be
used with families of lattices parametrized by one or more parameters.

In principle, our algorithm could be improved by adopting some of the
methods of
[
21
]
.
By selectively performing a simplicial decomposition as well as incorporating
the translational symmetries in the classification of faces,
the number of face classes to construct can be reduced.
This may lead to a lower
computational cost for a given lattice, at the cost of
potentially more expensive equivalence tests.
We leave this possible extension of our method to future work.
