Fast convergence rates for estimating the stationary density in SDEs driven by a fractional Brownian motion with semi-contractive drift
Chiara Amorino, Eulalia Nualart, Fabien Panloup, Julian Sieber

Abstract
We study the estimation of the invariant density of additive fractional stochastic differential equations with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) H\in(0,1) . We first focus on continuous observations and develop a kernel-based estimator achieving faster convergence rates than previously available. This result stems from a martingale decomposition combined with new bounds on the (conditional) convergence in total variation to equilibrium of fractional SDEs. For H < 1 / 2 H<1/2 , we further refine the rates based on recent bounds on the marginal density. We then extend the methodology to discrete observations, showing that the same convergence rates can be attained. Moreover, we establish concentration inequalities for the estimator and introduce a data-driven bandwidth selection procedure that adapts to unknown smoothness. Numerical experiments for the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process illustrate the estimator’s practical performance. Finally, our results weaken the usual convexity assumptions on the drift component, allowing us to consider settings where strong convexity only holds outside a compact set. MSC class: primary 60G22, 60H10; secondary 62M09.



Keywords:
Convergence rate, non-parametric statistics, fractional Brownian motion, stationary density, density estimation

Introduction

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) is a stochastic process that has attracted considerable attention in statistics due to its ability to capture long-range dependence and self-similarity. Its flexibility makes it a valuable modeling tool across a wide range of disciplines.
In geophysics and hydrology, fBm has been used to model phenomena such as earthquake occurrences, river flows, and precipitation patterns (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
31
]
), where long-range dependence provides a realistic description of these processes. In economics and finance, it has been applied to model and forecast stock prices, exchange rates, and commodity prices, with implications for risk management, option pricing, and the study of long memory and rough volatility (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
18
]
for long-memory volatility and
[
13
,
24
,
50
]
for rough volatility). Further applications include modeling traffic patterns in high-speed data networks (
[
11
]
), medical processes (
[
35
]
), and turbulence phenomena (
[
19
]
).
The breadth of these applications continues to motivate research on statistical inference for fBm, which remains of both theoretical and practical importance.

In this paper, we consider a time horizon
T>0
and the stochastic differential equation (SDE) with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

dX_{t}=\sigma\,dB_{t}+b(X_{t})\,dt,\qquad t\in[0,T],

(1)

where
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given (random) initial condition,
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a measurable function, and
\sigma
is a nondegenerate
d\times d
matrix throughout the paper.

The process
B=(B_{t}^{1},\ldots,B_{t}^{d})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional fBm with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Its law is characterized by the covariance function

\mathbb{E}\big[B_{t}^{i}B_{s}^{j}\big]=\frac{1}{2}\left(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right)\delta_{i,j},\qquad s,t\geq 0.

Even in this non-Markovian setting, where the Hurst exponent
H\neq 1/2
, the process
X
can be embedded into an infinite-dimensional Markov structure, as shown in
[
27
]
and further developed in
Section

2
. This embedding ensures the existence of a unique invariant distribution for
X
with density
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, under standard regularity conditions on
b
and
\sigma
(see
Proposition

1
and references therein).

We study the nonparametric estimation of the invariant density
\pi
from the continuous observation of
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
as
T\to\infty
. Throughout,
X
is assumed to evolve in its stationary regime—a standard assumption in the analysis of ergodic systems, reflecting that many processes reach equilibrium before observation.

The estimation of invariant densities and drift coefficients in stochastic differential equations is a classical topic in statistics, with substantial theoretical and applied interest. Recent work has focused on models driven by fBm, which introduce additional challenges beyond the Markovian framework. Over the past decade, considerable progress has been made on parameter estimation for such processes; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
32
,
33
]
. Advances include drift estimation in additive fBm models from continuous
[
49
]
and discrete data
[
43
]
, estimation of diffusion coefficients
[
9
]
and the Hurst parameter
[
25
]
, and joint estimation of all parameters
[
28
]
. In contrast, the nonparametric estimation of the drift or invariant density has received comparatively little attention.

In the diffusion framework with standard Brownian noise, invariant density estimation has received substantial attention for its numerical and theoretical significance. On the computational side, efficient algorithms for approximating invariant densities, such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods, are well established (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
). Theoretically, the analysis of invariant distributions provides key insights into the stability of stochastic differential systems (see
[
5
]
) and serves as a fundamental tool for nonparametric drift estimation (see, e.g.,
[
46
]
).

In the classical one-dimensional diffusion setting, invariant density estimators can achieve parametric convergence rates under standard nonparametric assumptions (see Chapter 4.2 in
[
34
]
). This result relies on properties of the diffusion local time, which restrict the method to
d=1
. In this context,
[
45
]
investigates the discrete-time case and constructs penalized least squares estimators for successive derivatives of the stationary density, obtaining for
j=0
the same convergence rate as in
[
15
,
16
]
.

In higher dimensions, the asymptotic statistical equivalence for drift estimation in multidimensional diffusions is established in
[
20
]
. Their results imply fast pointwise convergence rates for invariant density estimators: if the density belongs to an isotropic Hölder class
\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
, the rate is
T^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d-2}}
. The proof relies on variance bounds for additive diffusion functionals, derived from spectral gap inequalities and transition density estimates. Extensions to anisotropic settings were later obtained in
[
47
]
, and the optimality of this rate was established in
[
3
]
.

As discussed above, only a few works address the nonparametric estimation of the drift coefficient and invariant density for fractional SDEs. In
[
41
,
44
]
, the authors study consistency and convergence rates of nonparametric drift estimators, while
[
14
]
analyzes Nadaraya–Watson-type estimators for the case
H>1/2
.

More closely related to our objectives,
[
8
]
investigates the anisotropic estimation of the invariant density associated with (
1
) from discrete observations. Under a strong convexity assumption, the authors propose a data-driven method achieving, in the isotropic setting, the rate
T^{-(2-\max(2H,1))\frac{\beta}{2(1+\beta)+2d}}
. Their analysis relies on novel concentration inequalities for the stationary process, controlling deviations between a functional and its mean through a decomposition of conditioned paths—an approach revisited in the first part of this paper.

1.1
Our contribution

Our approach estimates the invariant density
\pi
using the kernel estimator
\hat{\pi}_{h,T}(x)
, whose mean squared error decomposes into bias and variance components. We first derive an upper bound for the stochastic term via a martingale decomposition (see
Proposition

2
), yielding a faster convergence rate than in
[
8
]
, as shown in
Theorem

1
and discussed in Remark
4
.
This improvement stems from the observation that, in our framework, the concentration inequality must be applied directly to the kernel estimator, whose Lipschitz norm is substantially larger than its supremum norm. Our main result is obtained by establishing new bounds for the martingale decomposition, replacing the Lipschitz norm in
[
8
]
with the supremum norm, better suited to our setting.
With this adjustment, we show in Theorem
1
that for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exist constants
c
and
c_{\varepsilon}
and a suitable bandwidth
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})}
for the kernel estimator, depending on
T
,
d
, and the smoothness parameters such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

{}\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\pi}_{h,T}(x)-\pi(x)|^{2}]\leq\begin{cases}c\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}+d}}&\text{if $H<1/2$},\\[5.69054pt]
c_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\frac{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}(1-H)}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}+d}-\varepsilon}&\text{if $H>1/2$},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\bar{\beta}
denotes the harmonic mean of the smoothness parameters, i.e.,

\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\beta_{l}}
.

Then, we focus on improving the convergence rate in the case
H<\frac{1}{2}
. This refinement is based on novel bounds for the density of the semigroup, specifically developed for the non-Markovian setting (
see
[
38
]
). These bounds exploit the fact that the
L^{1}
norm of the kernel density estimator is uniformly bounded, independently of the bandwidth
h
.
As a result, we obtain in Theorem
2
convergence rates that improve upon those in (
2
), expressed as

\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\pi}_{h,T}(x)-\pi(x)|^{2}]\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\min\left(\frac{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}}{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}+\alpha_{d,H}},\frac{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}(1-H)}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\beta}}+d}-\varepsilon\right)}},

where
\alpha_{d,H}:=\max\left(2d-\frac{1}{H},\frac{4d}{5-2H}\right)
.

Although these rates surpass those in existing literature, they remain slower than the convergence observed in classical SDEs with
H=\frac{1}{2}
.
Further discussion of this discrepancy is provided in
Remark

7
.

After that, we turn to a more practically relevant framework in which only discrete observations of the process are available. We show in Theorem
3
that the discrete version of our estimator attains the same convergence rate as in the continuous case, with
T=n\Delta_{n}
, where
n
denotes the number of observations and
\Delta_{n}
the discretization step. Moreover, achieving this rate requires choosing the bandwidth in a rate-optimal way, which in turn depends on the (unknown) smoothness of the invariant density. This motivates the introduction of a data-driven procedure for bandwidth selection. In particular, we establish a concentration inequality (see Proposition
4
for the discrete case and Theorem
5
for the continuous one), which ensures that the estimator obtained via this adaptive procedure achieves the same rate as in the previous setting, as proved in Theorem
4
and Corollary
1
. The good performance of the estimator is further illustrated in Section
3
through numerical experiments for the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model in dimension 3.

Furthermore,
another key contribution of this paper is that all results are stated under relatively relaxed convexity assumptions
. Specifically, we do not require global strong convexity; instead, we assume that the drift is
contractive outside a compact set
. In the case where
b
derives from a potential, i.e.,
b=-\nabla V
for some
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, this condition implies that
V
is uniformly and strictly convex outside a compact set. Within the compact set, however,
V
may contain multiple wells, allowing the drift to exhibit local repulsion.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce the assumptions on the drift, present the framework, and state the main results.
Section

3
presents the numerical results and
Section

4
establishes several preliminary estimates that are essential for bounding the stochastic term via a martingale decomposition and for controlling the semigroup density, as developed in
Section

5
. The proofs of the main statistical results are given in
Section

6
, while technical arguments are deferred to the Appendix. In particular,
Appendix

A
contains the proof of the variance bound based on the martingale decomposition,
Appendix

B
provides the proof of the concentration inequality, which is then used in
Appendix

to establish the adaptive procedure. Further technical proofs are collected in
Appendix

.

2
Model assumptions and main results

In the context of the non-Markovian dynamics described by (
1
), a proper definition of the invariant distribution for the process
X
requires embedding the dynamics into an infinite-dimensional Markovian framework. Specifically, the resulting Markov process, referred to as the Stochastic Dynamical System, can be represented as a mapping on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{W}

\mathcal{W}
denotes a suitable space of Hölder functions from
(-\infty,0]

\mathbb{R}^{d}

Before giving the full embedding, we state the assumption on the drift coefficient:

\mathbf{(S)}_{\kappa,R,\lambda}
:

The function
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz continuous, with

[b]_{\text{Lip}}:=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|b(x)-b(y)|}{|x-y|},

and satisfies

\langle b(x)-b(y),x-y\rangle\leq\begin{cases}-\kappa|x-y|^{2},&|x|,|y|\geq R,\\
\lambda|x-y|^{2},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

for some constants
\lambda,R\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
.

This hypothesis is relatively weak compared with assumptions in previous works (e.g.,
[
8
]
for the fBm case or
[
20
,
47
]
for classical SDEs), where global contractivity is assumed,
i.e., (

When

It is well-known that

Remark 1
.

The exponential convergence holds if

To achieve

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x)dx=1,\quad\left\|K\right\|_{\infty}<\infty,\quad\mbox{supp}(K)\subset[-1,1],\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x)x^{i}dx=0,

\mathbb{K}_{h}(z)=\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l}}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}K\left(\frac{z_{i}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{i}}}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Remark 7

Even in the refinement stated above, the convergence rate we obtained is slower compared to that of classical SDEs. This is mainly due to the decay of the covariance, which is exponential for classical SDEs but only fractional in this setting (on this topic, see also

It is clear from the results above that the variance, and therefore the rate-optimal choice of bandwidth, depend on the (unknown) smoothness of the invariant density. This motivates the introduction of a data-driven bandwidth selection procedure. With this goal in mind, we define a quantity that heuristically represents the bias term, together with a penalty term whose order matches that of the variance. Following the Goldenshluger–Lepski approach, the selected bandwidth is the one that minimizes the sum of these two quantities.

To formalize this reasoning, let us introduce the set of candidate bandwidths given by

\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset\left\{h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})\in(0,1]^{d}:\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\log(n\Delta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}+a},\,a>0\right\}.

We assume that the growth of
|\mathcal{H}_{n}|
is at most polynomial in
T=n\Delta
, i.e., there exists a constant
c>0
such that

{}|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\leq c(n\Delta)^{c}.

(11)

Such an assumption is classical in the analysis of adaptive procedures; see, for instance, Section 3.2 in
[
3
]
.
An example of a set satisfying this condition is

\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\left\{h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})\in(0,1]^{d}:\forall i\in\{1,\dots,d\},\,h_{i}=\frac{1}{z_{i}},\,z_{i}\in\mathbb{N}:\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{z_{l}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\log(n\Delta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}+a}\right\}.

(12)

Corresponding to the candidate bandwidths, we define the set of candidate estimators as

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{n}):=\left\{\check{\pi}_{h,n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{K}_{h}(X_{i\Delta}-x):\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}\right\}.

Our goal is to select an estimator from the family
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
in a fully data-driven way, based solely on the discrete observations of the process
X
.
To this end, for any
h,\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and any

, we set

\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\mathbb{K}_{\eta}(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(K_{h_{j}}\star K_{\eta_{j}})(x_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{h_{j}}(u-x_{j})K_{\eta_{j}}(u)\,du.

We then introduce the associated kernel density estimator defined by

\check{\pi}_{(h,\eta),n}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\mathbb{K}_{\eta})(X_{i\Delta}-x).

Since the convolution is commutative, we have
\check{\pi}_{(h,\eta),n}=\check{\pi}_{(\eta,h),n}
. Next, we define the penalty function
V_{n}(h)
as an estimate of the order of the squared variance:

V_{n}(h):=\frac{1}{n\Delta\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l}^{2}}\min\big(1,(n\Delta)^{2H-1+\epsilon}\big).

We then compare the differences between
\check{\pi}_{(h,\eta),n}
and
\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}
, which heuristically correspond to an estimate of the squared bias:

A_{n}(h,x):=\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\big(|\check{\pi}_{(h,\eta),n}(x)-\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)|^{2}-V_{n}(\eta)\big)_{+},

(13)

where
(y)_{+}:=\max(0,y)
denotes the positive part of

. The selected bandwidth is then defined as

\tilde{h}(x):=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\big(A_{n}(h,x)+V_{n}(h)\big),

and the final plugin estimator is the one in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
associated with
\tilde{h}(x)
, namely
\check{\pi}_{\tilde{h}(x),n}(x).

Before presenting the results on
\check{\pi}_{\tilde{h}(x),n}(x)

In what follows, it will be useful to consider

\pi_{h}:=\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\pi,

that is, the smoothed version of
\pi

\check{\pi}_{h,n}

\mathbb{E}[\check{\pi}_{h,n}(x)]=\pi_{h}(x)

Similarly, we define

\pi_{(h,\eta)}:=\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\star\pi.

Moreover, we introduce the (pointwise) bias as

B_{n}(h,x):=\big|\mathbb{E}[\check{\pi}_{h,n}(x)]-\pi(x)\big|=\big|\pi_{h}(x)-\pi(x)\big|,

and its uniform version as

B_{n}(h):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}B_{n}(h,x).

With this notation in mind, we are now ready to state our first result on the adaptive procedure.

Remark 9
.

An example of a drift satisfying the weaker contractivity condition
(
4
)
but not the global one can be obtained by replacing the linear drift
in the three-dimensional fOU equation (
16
) with a state-dependent radial drift
-\phi(\|X_{t}\|)\,X_{t}
, where
\phi
is a smooth function of the distance from the origin. The drift is weaker near the origin and increases with distance, ensuring contractivity outside a compact set while failing globally. The invariant density is not explicit, and its smoothness depends on
\phi
: if
\phi
is only once differentiable, the density inherits limited regularity and the bias of kernel estimators matters; if
\phi
is twice differentiable or smoother, the density is sufficiently regular, the bias is negligible, and the estimator operates in a variance-dominated regime as in the fOU case. Thus, the choice of smoothness in the bandwidth schedule reflects the regularity of
\phi
, and while our theoretical results apply directly to this model, performing numerical simulations would require approximating the invariant density, which is not explicit and would be computationally challenging.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|}\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T}]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|}\lesssim\|F\|_{\infty}\left(\int|X_{0}-y|\pi(dy)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int\mathfrak{C}(W^{-}-{w^{-}},\epsilon)\mathbb{P}_{W^{-}}(dw^{-})T^{H+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right).

It is classical that
\sup_{t\geq 0}(1\wedge t)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}|B_{t}|
has moments
of any order
. It easily follows that
\mathfrak{C}
defined in (
21
) satisfies:

\int\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\tilde{w}^{-}-{w^{-}},\epsilon)\mathbb{P}_{W^{-}}^{\otimes 2}(d\tilde{w}^{-},dw^{-})<+\infty.

(36)

Hence, for every
T\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T}])^{2}\right]\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(\int|x-y|^{2}\pi^{\otimes 2}(dx,dy)+T^{2H+\varepsilon}\right)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}T^{2H+\varepsilon}\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}.

On the other hand, for all
k\in\left\{1,...,T\right\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle=\int_{k-1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{k-1}]ds.

To simplify the notations, let us detail the case
k=1
(the generalization easily follows from a Markov argument). We have for
s\geq 1

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{0}]|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\|F\|_{\infty}\int\|{\cal L}(X_{s}|{\cal F}_{1})-{\cal L}(X_{s}|{\cal F}_{1},(W_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}=(w_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})\|_{TV}\mathbb{P}_{W}(dw).

At the price of a translation from
[0,1]
to
[-1,0]
, we are exactly in the setting of the second statement of
Proposition

6
. More precisely, the conditioning of the Wiener process on
[0,1]
implies that we are considering two solutions for which the past before time
1
differs only on
[0,1]
. We thus deduce from
Proposition

6
that for
s\geq 1

\displaystyle\|{\cal L}(X_{s}|{\cal F}_{1})

\displaystyle-{\cal L}(X_{s}|{\cal F}_{1},(W_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}=(w_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})\|_{TV}

\displaystyle\leq\left(e^{-c(s-1)}|X_{1}-X_{1}^{w}|+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|w-W\|_{\infty,[0,1]}}((s-1)\vee 1)^{H-\frac{5}{2}}\right),

where
X_{1}^{w}
denotes the solution at time
1
starting from
X_{0}
but with Wiener path
w
on
[0,1]
. Since
X_{1}
and
X_{1}^{w}
have the same starting point
X_{0}
, a Gronwall argument implies that

|X_{1}-X_{1}^{w}|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}\int_{0}^{1}e^{[b]_{\rm Lip}(s-t)}|\tilde{B}_{s}-\tilde{B}_{s}^{w}|ds\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}{\cal T}(1,W,w),

where
\tilde{B}
(or
\tilde{B}^{W}
) denotes the Liouville process (defined by (
18
)),
\tilde{B}_{s}^{w}
the Liouville path driven by
w
and

{\cal T}(k,W,w)=\sup_{s\in[k-1,k]}|\tilde{B}_{s}^{k-1}-\tilde{B}_{s}^{k-1,w}|\textnormal{ with}\quad\tilde{B}_{s}^{t}=\tilde{B}_{s}-\tilde{B}_{t}.

From what precedes and a Markov argument for extending to any
k
, we get for
s\geq k

\begin{split}&|\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{s})|{\cal F}_{k-1}]|\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lesssim}\|F\|_{\infty}\int\left(e^{-c(s-k)}{\cal T}(k,W,w)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|w^{k-1}-W^{k-1}\|_{\infty,[k-1,k]}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(s-k)\vee 1})^{H-\frac{5}{2}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{W}(dw),\end{split}

where
w^{t}_{s}=w_{s}-w_{t}
and
W^{t}_{s}=W_{s}-W_{t}
. The random variables involved in the above inequality do not depend on
s
and have moments of any order. This implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k-1}]\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(\int_{k-1}^{T}((t-k)\vee 1)^{H-\frac{5}{2}}dt\right)^{2}\lesssim\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Thus,

\sum_{k=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T}|{\cal F}_{k-1}]\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim T{\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}}.

Plugging into (
34
), this yields

{\rm Var}\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(X_{s})ds\right)\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}(cT+c_{\epsilon}T^{2H+\epsilon}).

The result follows (taking, for example,
\varepsilon=\frac{1-2H}{2}
when
H<1/2
).

\square

Appendix B
Concentration inequalities and discrete observations

In this section, we focus on the proof of
Proposition

4
, addressing the case of discrete observations and concentration inequalities. To this end, we provide below a general concentration inequality that can be applied in both the discrete and continuous settings.

We focus on

F_{T,\mu}=\int_{0}^{T}F(X_{s})\,\mu(ds),

(37)

where
\mu
is a positive measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. When

(F(X_{s}))_{0\leq s\leq T}

We set

\displaystyle a_{0,\mu}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-c\tau}\mu(d\tau),\quad b_{0,\mu,\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{T}(1+\tau)^{H-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\mu(d\tau)\quad\textnormal{and for $k\in\llbracket 1,\lfloor T\rfloor\rrbracket$,}

\displaystyle a_{k,\mu}=\int_{0}^{T-k}e^{-c\tau}\mu(d\tau),\quad b_{k,\mu}=\int_{0}^{T-k}(1+\tau)^{H-\frac{5}{2}}\mu(d\tau).

We finally set
a_{\lceil T\rceil,\mu}=b_{\lceil T\rceil,\mu}=1

In the sequel, we usually write
b_{0,\mu}

b_{0,\mu,\varepsilon}

Theorem 5
.

There exists
\Lambda>0
such that if

(
S
)

κ
,
R

In this case, for all

Proof.

Set
M_{k}=\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}|{\cal F}_{k}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda|F_{T,\mu}-\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}]|}\right]

The bound (

Lemma 3
.

Let

•

There exists

•

For all

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda|\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}|{\cal F}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}]|}]\leq e^{C_{\varepsilon}\lambda^{2}\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}(a_{0,\mu}^{2}+b_{0,\mu,\varepsilon}^{2})}.

Proof.

By

4
below, it is enough to prove that there exists
\alpha>0
such that
for every
\eta

\eta\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}(a_{k,\mu}^{2}+b_{k,\mu}^{2})\leq\alpha

\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta(\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}|{\cal F}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}])^{2}}]<+\infty.

(40)

For (

|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lesssim}\|F\|_{\infty}\int\left(a_{k,\mu}{\cal T}(k,W,w)+b_{k,\mu}{\|w^{k-1}-W^{k-1}\|_{\infty,[k-1,k]}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{W}(dw).

Thus,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta(M_{k}-M_{k-1})^{2}}|{\cal F}_{k-1}]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[e^{2\eta\|F\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(a_{k,\mu}^{2}U_{1}^{2}+b_{k,\mu}^{2}U_{2}^{2}\right)}]

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it remains to prove that

are subgaussian. For

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}dW_{s}|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,1]}t^{H-\frac{1}{2}}|W_{t}|+C_{H,\varepsilon}\sup_{0\leq s<t<1}\frac{|W_{s}-W_{t}|}{(t-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{H,\varepsilon})\underbrace{\sup_{0\leq s<t<1}\frac{|W_{s}-W_{t}|}{(t-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}}_{\|W\|_{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon,[0,1]}},

where in the second line we used that

[
23

.
For (
40

35

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|}\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}|{\cal F}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[F_{T,\mu}]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|}\lesssim\|F\|_{\infty}\left(a_{0,\mu}\int|X_{0}-y|\pi(dy)+b_{0,\mu,\varepsilon}U_{3}\right)

Once again, it is now enough to prove that

Lemma 4
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable and
\eta>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta X^{2}}]<+\infty
. Then, for every
\lambda\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\leq 1+\frac{\lambda\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\eta}}e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\eta}}\leq Ce^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\eta}}

where
C
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We have:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]=1+\lambda\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{\lambda u}\mathbb{P}(X>u)du.

Since
\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta X^{2}}]<+\infty
, the Markov inequality implies that

\mathbb{P}(X>u)\leq e^{-\eta u^{2}}.

Then, standard computations imply that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{\lambda u}\mathbb{P}(X>u)du\leq\sqrt{\pi}{\eta}e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\eta}}.

This yields the first bound. The second follows thanks to the elementary inequality
xe^{\frac{x^{2}}{4}}\leq e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
for every
x\geq 0
.

Appendix C
Proof adaptive procedure

This section is devoted to the proofs of the results stated in Section
2.1
, concerning the adaptive procedure. In particular, we provide the proofs of Theorem
4
and Corollary

.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

The adaptive procedure crucially relies on the following bound on the expectation of
A_{n}(h,x)
, defined in (
13
), which we state here and prove below, valid for any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
:

\mathbb{E}[A_{n}(h,x)]\lesssim B_{n}(h)+(n\Delta)^{c_{1}}e^{-c_{2}(\log(n\Delta))^{c_{3}}},

(41)

for some positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, and
c_{3}>1
.

The fact that the bound (
41
) implies the result stated in
Theorem

4
follows from standard algebraic arguments and is therefore omitted here. A detailed proof of this implication can be found in Section 6 of
[
1
]
, where the bound above appears as Proposition 5, and its connection to the adaptive procedure is established in the proof of Theorem 1 therein. This reasoning is now classical: even in the proof of the adaptive procedure in
[
3
]
(see Theorems 3 and 4), the verification that (
41
) yields the desired convergence rate for the data-driven estimator is omitted for brevity.

Let us now turn to the proof of (
41
).
By adding and subtracting
\pi_{(h,\eta),n}
and
\pi_{\eta,n}
, and using the triangle inequality, we obtain, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
,

\displaystyle A_{n}(h,x)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\Big[(|\check{\pi}_{(h,\eta),n}(x)-\pi_{(h,\eta),n}(x)|^{2}-\tfrac{V_{n}(\eta)}{2})_{+}+|\pi_{(h,\eta),n}(x)-\pi_{\eta,n}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt+(|\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)-\pi_{\eta,n}(x)|^{2}-\tfrac{V_{n}(\eta)}{2})_{+}\Big]

\displaystyle=:\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\big[I_{1}^{h,\eta}(x)+I_{2}^{h,\eta}(x)+I_{3}^{\eta}(x)\big].

We now analyze the three terms above separately.
We begin with
I_{2}^{h,\eta}(x)
, which is the simplest one.
By definition,

|\pi_{(h,\eta),n}(x)-\pi_{\eta,n}(x)|=|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\star(\pi_{h}-\pi)(x)|\leq\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{L^{1}}\,\|\pi_{h}-\pi\|_{\infty}\lesssim B_{n}(h),

where we used the boundedness of
\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{L^{1}}
and the definition of
B_{n}(h)
.

To control both
I_{1}^{h,\eta}(x)
and
I_{3}^{\eta}(x)
, the main tool will be the concentration inequality stated in Proposition
4
.
Let us start with
I_{3}^{\eta}(x)
. We have

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\big((|\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)-\pi_{\eta,n}(x)|^{2}-\tfrac{V_{n}(\eta)}{2})_{+}\geq t\big)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(|\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)-\pi_{\eta,n}(x)|\geq(\tfrac{V_{n}(\eta)}{2}+t)^{1/2}\big)\,dt.

Recall that

\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{K}_{\eta}(x-X_{i\Delta}),\qquad\pi_{\eta}=\mathbb{E}[\check{\pi}_{\eta,n}(x)],

so that the concentration inequality in Proposition
4
applies, yielding

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(t+\tfrac{V_{n}(\eta)}{2})(n\Delta)^{1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon)}}{C_{\epsilon}\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2C_{\epsilon}(\prod_{l=1}^{d}\eta_{l})^{4}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-\frac{t(n\Delta)^{1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon)}}{C_{\epsilon}\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right)dt,

where we used the definition of
V_{n}(\eta)
and the fact that

\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\prod_{l=1}^{d}\eta_{l}
. Computing the integral explicitly gives

\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)\leq c\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\frac{\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{(n\Delta)^{1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon)}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2C_{\epsilon}(\prod_{l=1}^{d}\eta_{l})^{4}}\right).

Since for all
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
we have

\prod_{l=1}^{d}\eta_{l}\leq(1/\log(n\Delta))^{1/4+a}
, it follows that

\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2C_{\epsilon}(\prod_{l=1}^{d}\eta_{l})^{4}}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log(n\Delta))^{1+a/4}}{2C_{\epsilon}}\right).

Moreover, according to (
11
),
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is assumed to be polynomial in
n\Delta
, which implies

\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)\lesssim(n\Delta)^{c_{1}}e^{-c_{2}(\log(n\Delta))^{c_{3}}},

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
c_{3}>1
, as desired.

The bound on
\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{1}^{h,\eta}(x)
follows the same reasoning as that for
\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)
.
The only difference lies in
\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}
being replaced by
\|\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}
, which satisfies however

\|\mathbb{K}_{h}\star\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\leq c\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbb{K}_{h}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\|\mathbb{K}_{\eta}\|_{\infty},

since the
L^{1}
-norm of the kernel function is uniformly bounded by a constant.
It then follows immediately that the same bound established for
\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{3}^{\eta}(x)
also holds for
\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}I_{1}^{h,\eta}(x)
.
This completes the proof of (
41
) and, consequently, the proof of Theorem
4
.

∎

),

\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l}(n\Delta)=\left(\frac{1}{n\Delta}\right)^{-\frac{\bar{\beta}}{2(\bar{\beta}+d)}(1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon))\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\beta_{l}}}=\left(\frac{1}{n\Delta}\right)^{-\frac{d}{2(\bar{\beta}+d)}(1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon))},

where we have used the definition of
\bar{\beta}
.
It follows that, for
T=n\Delta
sufficiently large, this product is indeed smaller than

\left(\frac{1}{\log(n\Delta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}+a}
for any
a>0
.

However, to guarantee that the bandwidths defined in (
42
) actually belong to the discrete set
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we require them to be of the form

h_{l}=\frac{1}{z_{l}}
with
z_{l}\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor n\Delta\rfloor\}
, which does not necessarily hold in general.
To address this, we define instead the discretized bandwidths

\tilde{h}_{l}(n\Delta)=\frac{1}{\lfloor(n\Delta)^{-\frac{\bar{\beta}}{2\beta_{l}(\bar{\beta}+d)}(1\wedge(2-2H+\epsilon))}\rfloor},\qquad\forall l\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

which are asymptotically equivalent to
h_{l}(n\Delta)
(and hence yield the same rate), but satisfy

\tilde{h}(n\Delta):=(\tilde{h}_{1}(n\Delta),\ldots,\tilde{h}_{d}(n\Delta))\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
by construction.
From Theorem
3
we then deduce that

\arg\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\big(B_{n}(h)+V_{n}(h)\big)=\tilde{h},

and substituting this into Theorem
4
completes the proof of the corollary.

∎
