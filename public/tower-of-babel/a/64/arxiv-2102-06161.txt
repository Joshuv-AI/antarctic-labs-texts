Equidistant quenches in few-level quantum systems
Sreekanth K Manikandan

Abstract
A recent work [Phys. Rev. Lett. 125, 110602] showed that among a pair of thermodynamically equidistant quenches from a colder and a hotter initial state at a fixed ambient temperature, the relaxation from the colder initial state ( uphill relaxation) is always faster, for dynamics close to stable minima. Here we show that this is not generically the case for open quantum systems with two or three energy levels. We find that both faster uphill and faster downhill relaxation and symmetric thermal relaxation can be observed in equidistant quenches, depending on the transition rates and the choice of the distance measure used. Furthermore, we obtain a phase diagram in the parameter space for the three-level system corresponding to different thermalization behaviours.



I
Introduction

Thermal relaxation is one of the simplest scenarios leading to non-equilibrium dynamics
Dattagupta 2012
. In macroscopic systems, thermal relaxation is often directly monitored by measuring the temperature of the system
Childs et al. 2000
, or by measuring the time taken for a phase transition to occur (e.g., cooling of water). On the contrary, if we consider
small
systems
Seifert 2012
such as a colloidal particle in one dimension
Martínez et al. 2016
, or a single atom heat engine
Roßnagel et al. 2016
, unless the system fully equilibrates to the environment, the notion of the temperature of the system is ill-defined. It is not common to have phase transitions either. Hence, an alternate method suggested involves estimating the
distance

D(\rho(t)||\rho_{Eq})
between the instantaneous probability density
\rho(t)
and the equilibrium density
\rho_{Eq}
of the system
Lu and Raz 2017
;
Kumar and Bechhoefer 2020
;
Carollo et al. 2021
;
Lapolla and Godec 2020
.

The main requirements on such a distance function are that it has to be continuous, convex, and a non-increasing function in time as
\rho(t)
approaches
\rho_{Eq}

Lu and Raz 2017
.
However, they do not necessarily obey some of the standard axioms of distance functions such as symmetry under the change of arguments
Johnson and Sinanovic 2001
or the triangle inequality
Kailath 1967
.

One of the well-used distance functions is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL divergence)
Cover 1991
defined as,

\displaystyle D_{KL}\left(\rho(t)||\rho_{Eq}\right)=\text{Tr}\left[\;\rho(t)\left(\log\rho(t)-\log\rho_{Eq}\right)\;\right].

(1)

Thermodynamically,
D_{KL}\left(\rho(t)||\rho_{Eq}\right)
is related to the excess free energy of the state
\rho(t)

which vanishes as the system equilibrates (see Refs.
Parrondo et al. 2015
;
Lapolla and Godec 2020
for a simple derivation)
.
For quantum systems, another choice of the distance measure is the trace distance
Nielsen and Chuang 2002
, defined as,

\displaystyle D_{\text{Tr}}(\rho(t)||\rho_{Eq})=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}|\rho(t)-\rho_{Eq}|,

(2)

where
|A|=\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
.

In recent times, several anomalous thermal relaxation properties have been studied in microscopic systems using an appropriate choice of
distance measure. One of the well-known anomalies is the
Mpemba
effect
Bechhoefer et al. 2021
, where certain systems cool down exponentially faster to an ambient temperature
T_{a}
from an initially hot temperature
T_{hot}
than a rather warm temperature
T_{warm}
(
T_{a}<T_{warm}<T_{hot}
). Mpemba effect is observed in systems ranging from water
Jeng 2006
to Granular gases
Lasanta et al. 2017
. In many of these cases, the effect has been associated with the presence of energy barriers and metastable states in the potential energy landscape
Lu and Raz 2017
;
Klich et al. 2019
;
Kumar and Bechhoefer 2020
. On the other hand, systems with simple energy landscapes with no metastable states were not expected to show non-trivial thermal relaxation properties.

However, the latter class of systems were studied in great detail recently in Ref.
Lapolla and Godec 2020
, by considering the issue of thermal relaxation from a pair of
thermodynamically equidistant
initial states. For initial equilibrium states at temperatures
T_{c}
and
T_{h}
with
T_{c}<T_{a}<T_{h}
, satisfying the equidistant constraint
D_{KL}^{(T_{c},T_{a})}=D_{KL}^{(T_{h},T_{a})}
, it was shown that the thermal relaxation at the ambient temperature is asymmetric in general (
D_{KL}^{(T_{c},T_{a})}(t)\neq D_{KL}^{(T_{h},T_{a})}(t)
), and the relaxation from a colder temperature to the ambient temperature (uphill relaxation) is always
faster
(
D_{KL}^{(T_{c},T_{a})}(t)<D_{KL}^{(T_{h},T_{a})}(t)
) for dynamics close to a stable minima.
The results in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
are proved for overdamped diffusive systems.

One of the interesting aspects of the result in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
is that it is a non-trivial thermal relaxation property in an energy landscape that does not have any metastable states. However, it is not clear whether the results can be extended to dynamical systems other than those described using overdamped Langevin equations and whether the choice of the distance measure is crucial.

In this paper, we investigate the generality of the findings in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
. We check if the asymmetry in equidistant quenches and faster uphill relaxation can be seen in two and three-level quantum systems in contact with a thermal reservoir, whose dynamics is described using Lindblad Quantum Master Equations
Davies 1974
;
Davies 1976
;
Davies 1975
;
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
. We also consider both KL divergence and Trace distance measures. For two-level systems,
we consider generic initial states with coherences as well. We find that both faster uphill and faster downhill relaxation, as well as symmetric thermal relaxation, can be observed in equidistant quenches, depending on both the transition rates as well as the distance measure used. Furthermore, we obtain a phase diagram in the parameter space for the three-level system corresponding to different thermalization behaviours. These findings demonstrate that faster uphill relaxation is not a universal phenomenon, even in systems with only a few energy levels.

The paper is organized as follows. In section II, we present the general framework and define equidistant quenches. In section III, we first analyze two-level systems using both KL divergence and trace distance measures. We then extend the analysis to three-level systems. Finally, in section IV, we present the conclusions.

General framework

Quantum Master Equations:

We begin by considering the Markovian dynamics of open quantum systems, described in an N-dimensional Hilbert space,
Davies 1974
;
Davies 1976
;
Davies 1975

(3)

If the transition rates obey the detailed balance condition

\displaystyle\rho_{Eq}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H}\right).

Strictly speaking, the thermal state is only reached in the asymptotic,

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{L}t}[\rho_{0}]=V^{R}_{1}+\sum_{n=2}^{N^{2}}\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{0}V_{n}^{L}\right)V^{R}_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}t}.

At large enough times, the approach to equilibrium of the system will be governed by the slowest decaying mode of the Lindblad operator. Then we can approximate,

\displaystyle\rho(t)\sim\rho_{Eq}+\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{0}V^{L}\right)V^{R}e^{\lambda t},

(6)

where
\lambda
is the eigenvalue with minimum
|
Re
(\lambda)|

using Eq. (
5

III
Results

Two-level system
We first consider a two-level system that is in contact with a thermal reservoir at inverse temperature
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}
. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{0}\sigma_{z},

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)

The eigenvectors as well as the eigenvalues of this system are,

\displaystyle|u_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle|u_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}

We assume that the reservoir consists of an electromagnetic field in thermal equilibrium at an inverse temperature
\beta

;
Szczygielski et al. 2013

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{01}&=\mathcal{A}|\omega_{0}|^{3}\frac{e^{\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}}{e^{\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}-1},\\
\Gamma_{10}&=e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}=\frac{\mathcal{A}|\omega_{0}|^{3}}{e^{\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}-1}.\end{split}

(13)

Here
\mathcal{A}

The corresponding Lindblad operators are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{01}&=|u_{-}\rangle\langle u_{+}|=\sigma_{-},\\
L_{10}&=|u_{+}\rangle\langle u_{-}|=\sigma_{+}.\end{split}

(14)

Including an explicit dephasing channel of strength
\Delta

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma_{z},\rho\right]+\Gamma_{01}\left[\sigma_{-}\rho\sigma_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-},\rho\right\}\right]\\
&+\Gamma_{10}\left[\sigma_{+}\rho\sigma_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{-}\sigma_{+},\rho\right\}\right]-\frac{\Delta}{4}\left[\sigma_{z}\left[\sigma_{z},\rho\right]\right].\end{split}

(15)

Given an initial density matrix
\rho_{0}
, we can straightforwardly obtain the solution

using Eq. (
5
) (see Appendix I for details).

Now we look at equidistant quenches in this setup. We consider the following generic initial state,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\rho}(0)&=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}}{2\cosh\left(\frac{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}\right)}&re^{i\phi}\\
re^{-i\phi}&\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}}{2\cosh\left(\frac{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}\right)}\end{array}\right),\\

(16)

The non-diagonal part of the density matrix

2

2

&=S[\rho]-S[\hat{\rho}]+\\

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the properties of equidistant quenches in two and three-level systems in contact with a thermal reservoir. We find that both faster uphill and faster downhill relaxation and symmetric thermal relaxation can be observed in equidistant quenches, depending on the transition rates and the choice of the distance measure used. We obtain a phase diagram in the parameter space for the three-level system corresponding to different thermalization behaviours. These results demonstrate that equidistant quenches do not show a single universal trend as recently suggested in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
, even in simple systems with a few energy levels.

Our results for the two-level system show that allowing coherence in the initial state leads to a broader range of behaviours in equidistant quenches. The case (
\Delta>\Delta_{c}
) in which the equidistant initial state with
\beta_{0}=\beta
and
r\neq 0
thermalizes the fastest is one interesting possibility not found in the classical case. It will be interesting to see if these findings can be used for designing optimal protocols for extracting work from quantum coherences
Korzekwa et al. 2016
.

In all cases, we also find that the asymmetry of equidistant quenches, whenever it exists, is robust to minor-parameter changes, as the trend typically spans over a region in the parameter space. Hence, it will be interesting to see if this framework could be used for practical applications, such as the designing of optimal microscopic heat engine cycles (as suggested in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
), or the charging of quantum batteries by thermalization
Hovhannisyan et al. 2020
. It will also be interesting to extend the study presented here to see if there is a continuum limit of discrete space systems, where the results in
Lapolla and Godec 2020
are recovered.

Solution of the Lindblad equation
We begin with the Lindblad equation for the two level system,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma_{z},\rho\right]+\Gamma_{01}\left[\sigma_{-}\rho\sigma_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-},\rho\right\}\right]\\
&+\Gamma_{10}\left[\sigma_{+}\rho\sigma_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{-}\sigma_{+},\rho\right\}\right]-\frac{\Delta}{4}\left[\sigma_{z}\left[\sigma_{z},\rho\right]\right],\end{split}

(24)

where,

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{11}(t)&\rho_{10}(t)\\
\rho_{01}(t)&\rho_{00}(t)\end{array}\right).

For simplicity of the analysis, we rewrite the above equation as a Matrix equation for the vector
\vec{\rho}(t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\rho_{11}(t)&\rho_{10}(t)&\rho_{01}(t)&\rho_{00}(t)\end{array}\right]^{T}
, as
\frac{\partial\vec{\rho}(t)}{\partial t}=L\vec{\rho}(t)
, where

\displaystyle L=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\Gamma_{01}&0&0&e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta+2i\omega_{0})&0&0\\
0&0&-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta-2i\omega_{0})&0\\
\Gamma_{01}&0&0&-e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}\\
\end{array}\right)

The Right eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this operator are given by,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda_{1}^{R}&=0,\\
\vec{V}_{1}^{R}&=\left[e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{0}},0,0,1\right]^{T}\\
\lambda_{2}^{R}&=-\Gamma_{01}\left(e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1\right),\\
\vec{V}_{2}^{R}&=\left[-1,0,0,1\right]^{T}\\
\lambda_{3}^{R}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta-2i\omega_{0}),\\
\vec{V}_{3}^{R}&=\left[0,1,0,0\right]\\
\lambda_{4}^{R}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta+2i\omega_{0}),\\
\vec{V}_{4}^{R}&=\left[0,0,1,0\right]\end{split}

(31)

Similarly, the Left eigenvalues and eigenvectors are given by,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda_{1}^{L}&=0,\\
\vec{V}_{1}^{L}&=\left[1,0,0,1\right]^{T}\\
\lambda_{2}^{L}&=-\Gamma_{01}\left(e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1\right),\\
\vec{V}_{2}^{L}&=\left[-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{0}},0,0,1\right]^{T}\\
\lambda_{3}^{L}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta+2i\omega_{0}),\\
\vec{V}_{3}^{L}&=\left[0,1,0,0\right]^{T}\\
\lambda_{4}^{L}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta-2i\omega_{0}),\\
\vec{V}_{4}^{L}&=\left[0,0,1,0\right]^{T}\end{split}

(32)

If we now right the column vectors
\vec{V}_{i}
in the Matrix form, we get the left and right eigenmatrices
V_{i}
of the Linblad operator. Now we can use this together with Eq. (
5
), we obtain,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\vec{\rho}(t)&=\frac{V_{1}^{R}}{\text{Tr}(V_{1}^{R}V_{1}^{L})}+\text{Tr}(\rho(0)V_{2}^{L})\frac{V_{2}^{R}e^{\lambda_{2}t}}{\text{Tr}(V_{2}^{R}V_{2}^{L})}\\
&+\text{Tr}(\rho(0)V_{3}^{L})\frac{V_{3}^{R}e^{\lambda_{3}t}}{\text{Tr}(V_{3}^{R}V_{3}^{L})}+\text{Tr}(\rho(0)V_{4}^{L})\frac{V_{4}^{R}e^{\lambda_{4}t}}{\text{Tr}(V_{4}^{R}V_{4}^{L})}\end{split}

(33)

The division with the trace factor is for taking care of the normalization of the left and right eigenvectors as
\text{Tr}(V^{L}V^{R})=1
, Explicitly computing the sum, we obtain,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{11}(t)&=\frac{e^{-t\Gamma_{01}\left(e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1\right)}\left(\rho_{11}(0)e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}-\rho_{00}(0)\right)+1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1},\\
\rho_{10}(t)&=\rho_{10}(0)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta+2i\omega_{0})\right),\\
\rho_{01}(t)&=\rho_{01}(0)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{01}+e^{-\beta\hbar|\omega_{0}|}\Gamma_{01}+2\Delta-2i\omega_{0})\right),\\
\rho_{00}(t)&=\frac{e^{-t\Gamma_{01}\left(e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1\right)}\left(\rho_{00}(0)-\rho_{11}(0)e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}\right)}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}+\frac{1}{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}.\end{split}

(34)

It is possible to verify that,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\rho(t)=\rho_{Eq}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}&0\\
0&\frac{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\\
\end{array}\right).

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the calculation of the KL divergence distance function with the initial state given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\rho(0)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}&0\\

\end{array}\right).

Since
\rho(0)
is a diagonal matrix,
\rho(t)

&-\rho_{11}(t)\log\rho_{Eq,11}-\rho_{00}(t)\log\rho_{Eq,00}.\end{split}

(39)

To find equidistant temperatures, we first obtain

\displaystyle D_{KL}\left(\rho(0)||\rho_{Eq}\right)=\frac{-e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}\log\left(\frac{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right)+\log\left(\frac{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}{e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right)+e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}\log\left(\frac{e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}}{e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right)}{e^{\beta_{0}\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}

(40)

It is interesting to look at the limiting behaviour of this function as a function of
\beta_{0}
. In the
\beta_{0}\rightarrow 0
limit (Initial temperature
T_{0}\rightarrow\infty
), we get,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta_{0}\rightarrow 0}D_{KL}\left(\rho(0)||\rho_{Eq}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\log\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right)-\log\left(\frac{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right)-2\log(2)\right),

(41)

and in the
\beta_{0}\rightarrow\infty
limit (Initial temperature
T_{0}\rightarrow 0
), we get,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta_{0}\rightarrow\infty}D_{KL}\left(\rho(0)||\rho_{Eq}\right)=-\log\left(\frac{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}+1}\right).

(42)

For diffusive classical systems, both the limits in Eq. (
41
) and Eq. (
42
) do not exist as the function diverges to positive
\infty

Lapolla and Godec 2020

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta_{0}\rightarrow\infty}D_{KL}\left(\rho(0)||\rho_{Eq}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\beta_{0}\rightarrow 0}D_{KL}\left(\rho(0)||\rho_{Eq}\right)

\displaystyle\text{ if }\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\log 2.

(43)

We are guaranteed a pair of equidistant temperatures only when the equality holds. In all the other cases, there can be a hot/cold initial states without an equidistant pair. This is also a consequence of the fact that
