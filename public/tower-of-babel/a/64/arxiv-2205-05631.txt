Abstract
We consider a binary statistical hypothesis testing problem, where n n independent and identically distributed random variables Z n Z^{n} are either distributed according to the null hypothesis P P or the alternate hypothesis Q Q , and only P P is known. For this problem, a well-known test is the Hoeffding test, which accepts P P if the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the empirical distribution of Z n Z^{n} and P P is below some threshold. In this paper, we consider Hoeffding-like tests, where the KL divergence is replaced by other divergences, and characterize, for a large class of divergences, the first and second-order terms of the type-II error for a fixed type-I error. Since the considered class includes the KL divergence, we obtain the second-order term of the Hoeffiding test as a special case.



I
Introduction

Statistical hypothesis testing is known to have applications in areas such as information theory, signal processing, and machine learning. The most simple form of hypothesis testing is binary hypothesis testing, where the goal is to determine the distribution of a random variable
Z
between a null hypothesis
P
and an alternate hypothesis
Q
. There can be two types of errors in a binary hypothesis testing: The type-I error is the probability of declaring the hypothesis as
Q
when the true distribution is
P
. The type-II error is the probability of declaring the hypothesis as
P
when the true distribution is
Q
. In general, we are interested in analyzing the trade-off between these two types of errors for a given test. This test may have full or only partial access to the distributions
P
and
Q
.

When both
P
and
Q
are known, the likelihood ratio test (also known as Neyman-Pearson test
[
1
]
) achieves the optimal error trade-off between type-I and type-II errors. The Neyman-Pearson test is also investigated in an asymptotic setting, where one observes
n
independent copies of
Z
and the errors are analyzed asymptotically as
n
tends to infinity. In this case, we are often interested in the behaviour of the type-II error
\beta_{n}
for a fixed type-I error
\alpha_{n}
. It is known that for
\alpha_{n}\leq\epsilon,\;\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the corresponding type-II error satisfies
[
2
, Prop. 2.3]

\displaystyle-\ln\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nD(P\|Q)-\sqrt{nV(P\|Q)}Q^{-1}(\epsilon)+o(\sqrt{n}),\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty

(1)

where
D(P\|Q)
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
P
and
Q
, defined as

\displaystyle D(P\|Q)\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\ln\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}},

(2)

Q^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse of the tail probability of the standard Normal distribution,

\displaystyle V(P\|Q)\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\left[\left(\ln\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}-D(P\|Q)\right)^{2}\right],

and
o(\sqrt{n})
denotes a term that grows more slowly than
\sqrt{n}
as
n\to\infty
. Thus the first-order term of
-\ln\beta_{n}
, defined as
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}}{n}
and sometimes referred to as the
error exponent
, is given by
D(P\|Q)
. The second-order term, defined as
\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}-nD(P\|Q)}{\sqrt{n}}
, is given by
-\sqrt{V(P\|Q)}Q^{-1}(\epsilon)
.

The case where the test has only partial access to the distributions
P
and
Q
is generally studied under the name of
composite hypothesis testing
. Here, the assumption is that
P
and
Q
belong to certain classes of distributions denoted by
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, respectively. Several special cases of composite hypothesis testing have been investigated in the literature. Two such cases are: 1)
P
is known and
Q
can be any distributions but
P
; 2)
P
is known and
Q
belongs to a special class
\mathcal{Q}
. A test proposed by Hoeffding
[
3
]
, known as the Hoeffding test, is suitable for these two cases. In the Hoeffding test, the null hypothesis
P
is accepted if the KL divergence between the type
t_{Z^{n}}
(the empirical distribution) of the observations
Z^{n}=(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n})
and
P
is below some threshold
c
. Otherwise, the alternative hypothesis is accepted. Mathematically,

\displaystyle\text{if}\;D(t_{Z^{n}}\|P)<c,\text{ then accept }H_{0};\text{ otherwise accept }H_{1}.

In
[
4
, Th. III.2]
, it was shown that the Hoeffding test achieves the same first-order term of
-\ln\beta_{n}
as the Neyman-Pearson test. Consequently, not having access to the distribution of the alternate hypothesis does not affect the first-order term. To analyze the second-order term of
-\ln\beta_{n}
in the above two cases of composite hypothesis testing, Watanabe proposed a test that is second-order optimal in some sense
[
5
]
.

The Hoeffding test does not require side information about
the special class
\mathcal{Q}
to which
Q
belongs. It is therefore perhaps not surprising that tests that have knowledge of
Q
(case 2 of composite hypothesis testing) may outperform the Hoeffding test. Such tests include the generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT)
[
6
]
and test via mismatched divergence
[
7
]
. For example, it has been observed through simulations that the test via mismatched divergence performs better than the Hoeffding test
[
7
]
. However, an analytical comparison between the Hoeffding test and other tests is missing since refined asymptotics of the Hoeffding are not available.

In this paper, we wish to understand the behavior of the second-order term of the Hoeffding test as well as of Hoeffding-like tests where the KL divergence is replaced by other divergences, in the following referred to as
divergence test
. For a large class of divergences, we demonstrate that the second-order term is given by
-\sqrt{V(P\|Q)Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\epsilon)}
, where
Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse of the tail probability of the chi-squared distribution with
k-1
degrees of freedom. This class of divergences includes the KL divergence, so we obtain the second-order term of the Hoeffding test, as a special case.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents notations and the problem formulation. Section
III
discusses our main results. Section
IV
presents the proof of our results. Section
V
concludes the paper with a summary and discussion of our results.

II-A

Notations

Let
f(x)
and
g(x)
be two real-valued functions.
For
a\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, we write
f(x)=O(g(x))
as
x\rightarrow a
if
\limsup_{x\to a}\frac{|f(x)|}{|g(x)|}<\infty
. Similarly, we write
f(x)=o(g(x))
as
x\rightarrow a
if
\lim_{x\to a}\frac{|f(x)|}{|g(x)|}=0
. Finally, we write
f(x)=\Theta(g(x))
as
x\to a
if
f(x)=O(g(x))
and
\liminf_{x\to a}\frac{|f(x)|}{|g(x)|}>0
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}
be a sequence of positive real numbers. We say that a sequence of random variables is
X_{n}=o_{p}(a_{n})
if for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\left|\frac{X_{n}}{a_{n}}\right|>\epsilon\right)=0.

Similarly, we say that
X_{n}=O_{p}(a_{n})
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exist
M_{\epsilon}>0
and

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{X_{n}}{a_{n}}\right|>M_{\epsilon}\right)\leq\epsilon,\quad n\geq N_{\epsilon}.

II-B

Divergence and Divergence Test

We consider a divergence test on a random variable
Z
that takes value in a discrete set
\mathcal{Z}=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 2
. We denote the probability distribution function of
Z
by a
k
-length vector
P=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})^{T}
, where

\displaystyle P_{i}\triangleq\text{Pr}\{Z=a_{i}\},\quad i=1,\cdots,k.

Let

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Z})\triangleq\left\{P=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})^{T}\;|\;P_{i}\geq 0,\;i=1,\cdots,k\mbox{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}=1\right\}

be the set of all such probability distributions. The set of all positive probability distributions with
P_{i}>0,\;i=1,\cdots,k
on
\mathcal{Z}
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
. Let
Z^{n}=(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n})
denote an
n
-length i.i.d. sequence of
Z
.
By denoting the realization of
Z^{n}
by
z^{n}
, we define the type distribution
t_{z^{n}}=(t_{z^{n}}(a_{1}),\cdots,t_{z^{n}}(a_{k}))^{T}
of the sequence
z^{n}
as

\displaystyle(t_{z^{n}}(a_{1}),\cdots,t_{z^{n}}(a_{k}))^{T}\triangleq\left(\frac{N_{a_{1}|z^{n}}}{n},\cdots,\frac{N_{a_{k}|z^{n}}}{n}\right)^{T}

where
N_{a_{i}|z^{n}},\;i=1,\cdots,k
denotes the number of times
a_{i}
occurs in the sequence
z^{n}
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}=1
, a probability vector
P=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})^{T}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
can be represented by any
(k-1)
components of
P
. With this representation, the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
can be identified as a
(k-1)
-dimensional manifold; see
[
8
]
. In this paper, we choose the coordinate system of the probability distribution
P=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})^{T}
as the first
(k-1)
components denoted by
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k-1})^{T}
.

Given any two probability distributions
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, one can define a non-negative binary function
\tilde{D}(P\|Q)
called a
divergence
which represents a measure of discrepancy between them. A divergence is not necessarily symmetric in its arguments and also need not satisfy the triangle inequality; see
[
8
,
9
]
for more details. Mathematically, a divergence is defined as follows.

Definition 1

Consider two points
P
and
Q
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
whose coordinates are
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k-1})^{T}
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{k-1})^{T}
. A divergence
\tilde{D}(P\|Q)
between
P
and
Q
is a smooth function of
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
(we may write
\tilde{D}(P\|Q)=\tilde{D}(\mathbf{P}\|\mathbf{Q})
) satisfying the following conditions:

1.

\tilde{D}(P\|Q)\geq 0
for any
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
.

2.

\tilde{D}(P\|Q)=0
if, and only if,
P=Q
.

3.

The Taylor expansion of
\tilde{D}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(\mathbf{P}+\varepsilon\|\mathbf{P})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k-1}g_{ij}(\mathbf{P})\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}+O(\|\varepsilon\|_{2}^{3}),\quad\text{as }\|\varepsilon\|_{2}\rightarrow 0,

for some
(k-1)\times(k-1)
-dimensional positive-definite matrix
G=(g_{ij})
that depends on
\mathbf{P}
and
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{k-1})^{T}
, and where
\|\varepsilon\|_{2}\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{i})^{2}}
is the Euclidean norm of
\varepsilon
.

For any

\tilde{D}(P\|Q)=\tilde{D}(\mathbf{P}\|\mathbf{Q})
, the partial derivatives of
\tilde{D}(P\|Q)
with respect to the first variable
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k-1})^{T}
are given by
\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{i}}\tilde{D}(P\|Q)=\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{i}}\tilde{D}(\mathbf{P}\|\mathbf{Q}),\;i=1,\cdots,k-1
. Similarly, the partial derivatives of
\tilde{D}(P\|Q)
with respect to the second variable
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{k-1})^{T}
are given by
\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{i}}\tilde{D}(P\|Q)=\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{i}}\tilde{D}(\mathbf{P}\|\mathbf{Q}),\;i=1,\cdots,k-1
. Since, by definition, every divergence
\tilde{D}
attains a minimum at
P=Q
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{i}}\tilde{D}(P\|Q)\right|_{P=Q}=0,\quad i=1,\cdots,k-1

(3)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{i}}\tilde{D}(P\|Q)\right|_{P=Q}=0,\quad i=1,\cdots,k-1.

See
[
9
, (3.1)–(3.4)]
for more details.

There are many classes of divergences that are widely used in various fields of science and engineering; see, e.g.,
[
10
]
for more details. A well-known example of a divergence is the
f
-divergence defined as follows:
Let
f:(0,\in\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
f(1)=0
. Then, the
f
-divergence
between
P
and
Q
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{f}(P\|Q)\triangleq\sum Q_{i}f\left(\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}\right).

(4)

When
f(u)=u\log u
,
D_{f}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
For

\displaystyle f(u)=\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}(1-u^{(1+\alpha)/2}),\;\text{where}\;\alpha\neq\pm 1

(5)

the
f
-divergence is the
\alpha
-divergence
given by

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)\triangleq\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left[1-\sum P_{i}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}Q_{i}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right],\quad\alpha\neq\pm 1.

(6)

We next define a divergence test for case 1 of composite hypothesis testing (see Section
I
), That is, it is known that under hypothesis
H_{0}
the distribution is
P
, while under hypothesis
H_{1}
the distribution is anything but
P
. A Hoeffding-like test or divergence test
T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r)
for testing
H_{0}:\bar{P}=P
against the alternative
H_{1}:\bar{P}=Q
is defined as follows:

Upon observing
z^{n}
, if
\tilde{D}(t_{z^{n}}\|P)<r
for some
r>0
, then the null hypothesis
P
is accepted; else
P
is rejected.

For
r>0
, define the acceptance region for
P
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\tilde{D}}_{n}(r)\triangleq\left\{z^{n}\;\left|\right.\;\tilde{D}(t_{z^{n}}\|P)<r\right\}.

Then, the type-I and the type-II errors are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r))

\displaystyle\triangleq P^{n}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\tilde{D}}_{n}(r)^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r))

\displaystyle\triangleq Q^{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\tilde{D}}_{n}(r)).

Our goal is to analyze the asymptotics of the type-II error when the type-I error satisfies
\alpha_{n}\leq\epsilon,\;0<\epsilon<1
. Following the discussion in Section
I
, we define the first-order term
\beta^{\prime}
and the second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}
for the divergence test as follows:

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\beta_{n}

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}-n\beta^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}

if the limits exist. For the Hoeffding test, it is known that
\beta^{\prime}=D(P\|Q)
. In this paper, we generalize this result to the divergence test for a class of divergences defined in Definition
2
. We further obtain
\beta^{\prime\prime}
for the divergence test.

III
Second-order Asymptotics of the Divergence Test

We shall consider the following class of divergences.

Definition 2

Let
\varXi
denote the class of divergences satisfying the following conditions:

1.

For any
P,T\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, the second-order Taylor approximation of
\tilde{D}(T\|P)
around
T=P
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(T\|P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta d_{\chi^{2}}(T,P)+O(\|\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{P}\|_{2}^{3}),\quad\text{as}\quad\|\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{P}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0

(7)

for some constant
\eta>0
(that possibly depends on
P
). In (
7
),
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\cdots,T_{k-1})^{T}
,
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k-1})^{T}
, and
d_{\chi^{2}}
is the
\chi^{2}
-divergence defined as

\displaystyle d_{\chi^{2}}(T,P)\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(T_{i}-P_{i})^{2}}{P_{i}}.

(8)

2.

For any
P,T\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, the divergence
\tilde{D}
satisfies the Pinsker-type inequality

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\|T-P\|^{2}_{1}\leq\tilde{D}(T\|P),\quad\text{for some }\tilde{C}>0

(9)

where
\|T-P\|_{1}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|T_{i}-P_{i}|
denotes the
L^{1}
norm of

T-P
.

3.

The CDF of the statistic
\eta^{-1}n\tilde{D}(t_{z^{n}}\|P)
can be approximated as

\displaystyle P^{n}(\eta^{-1}n\tilde{D}(t_{Z^{n}}\|P)<c)=F_{\chi^{2},k-1}(c)+O(\delta_{n}),\quad c>0

(10)

for some positive sequence
\delta_{n}
satisfying
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{n}=0
, where
F_{\chi^{2},k-1}
is the CDF of the chi-squared random variable
\chi^{2}_{k-1}
with
k-1
degrees of freedom.

The following theorem characterizes
\beta^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime\prime}
of the divergence test for any divergence
\tilde{D}
in
\varXi
.

Theorem 1

Let
\tilde{D}\in\varXi
and
0<\epsilon<1
. Consider the divergence test
T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r)
for testing
H_{0}:P
against the alternative
H_{1}:Q
, where
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
P\neq Q
. Then, the type-II error
satisfies the following:

Part 1: There exists a threshold value
r_{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r_{n}))\leq\epsilon

(11)

such that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle-\ln\beta_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle\geq nD(P\|Q)-\sqrt{nV(P\|Q)Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\epsilon)}+O(\max\{\delta_{n}\sqrt{n},\ln n\}).

(12)

Part 2: For all
r_{n}>0
satisfying (
11
), we have as
n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle-\ln\beta_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq nD(P\|Q)-\sqrt{nV(P\|Q)Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\epsilon)}+O(\max\{\delta_{n}\sqrt{n},\ln n\})

(13)

where
Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse of
c\mapsto 1-F_{\chi^{2},k-1}(c)
.

Proof.

See Section
IV
.
∎

Since the sequence
\delta_{n}
in (
12
) and (
13
) tends to zero as
n\to\infty
, we have that
O(\max\{\delta_{n}\sqrt{n},\ln n\})=o(\sqrt{n})
. Consequently, (
12
) and (

) imply that, for the optimal threshold value
r_{n}
,

\displaystyle-\ln\beta_{n}(T_{n}^{\tilde{D}}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle=nD(P\|Q)-\sqrt{nV(P\|Q)Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\epsilon)}+o(\sqrt{n}).

(14)

Thus, Theorem
1
characterizes the first and second-order terms of the divergence test for any
\tilde{D}\in\varXi
. It can be verified that
\sqrt{Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2},k-1}(\epsilon)}>Q^{-1}(\epsilon)
for all
0<\epsilon<1
. Consequently, the second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}
of the divergence test is strictly smaller than the second-order term of the Neyman-Pearson test.

For certain divergences in
\varXi

Condition 1
:
When
\tilde{D}
is the
f
-divergence

satisfies (

V
Conclusions

For the divergence test and for a large class of divergences, we established the first-order and the second-order terms of the type-II error given that the type-I error is upper-bounded by a given value. The divergence test does not require the knowledge of the distribution of the alternative hypothesis, hence, it is suitable for problems where we only have access to the distribution of the null hypothesis. The class of divergences considered in this paper includes well-known divergences such as the KL divergence and the
\alpha
-divergence, and our divergence test specializes to the Hoeffding test if the chosen divergence is the KL divergence. It is well-known that the Hoeffding test is first-order optimal in the sense that its first-order term is equal to the first-order term of the Neyman-Pearson test. However, our results demonstrate that the second-order term of the divergence test for the class of divergences considered in this paper, and therefore also of the Hoeffding test, is strictly smaller than the second-order term of the optimal Neyman-Pearson test. The question whether there exists a test that only requires the knowledge of the distribution of the null hypothesis and that achieves the second-order term of the Hoeffding test for the some
Q
’s while it achieves a higher second-order term for the remaining
Q
’s is yet to be explored.
