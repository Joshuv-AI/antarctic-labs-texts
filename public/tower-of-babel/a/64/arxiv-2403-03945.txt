SPEAR : Exact Gradient Inversion of Batches in Federated Learning
Dimitar I. Dimitrov, Maximilian Baader, Mark Niklas Müller, Martin Vechev, {dimitar.iliev.dimitrov}@insait.ai, {mbaader, mark.mueller, martin.vechev}@inf.ethz.ch

Abstract
Federated learning is a framework for collaborative machine learning where clients only share gradient updates and not their private data with a server. However, it was recently shown that gradient inversion attacks can reconstruct this data from the shared gradients. In the important honest-but-curious setting, existing attacks enable exact reconstruction only for batch size of b = 1 b=1 , with larger batches permitting only approximate reconstruction. In this work, we propose SPEAR , the first algorithm reconstructing whole batches with b > 1 b>1 exactly . SPEAR combines insights into the explicit low-rank structure of gradients with a sampling-based algorithm. Crucially, we leverage ReLU-induced gradient sparsity to precisely filter out large numbers of incorrect samples, making a final reconstruction step tractable. We provide an efficient GPU implementation for fully connected networks and show that it recovers high-dimensional ImageNet inputs in batches of up to b ≲ 25 b\lesssim 25 exactly while scaling to large networks. Finally, we show theoretically that much larger batches can be reconstructed with high probability given exponential time.

Low-Rank Decomposition

We first show that the weight gradient

Next, we leverages the sparsity of

We show that ReLU layers induce sparse activation gradients

Figure

We now have to select the correct

3
Gradient Inversion via Sparsity and Low-Rankness

In this section, we will demonstrate that both low rankness and sparsity arise naturally for gradients of fully connected ReLU networks and explain theoretically how we recover
{\bm{X}}
.
Specifically, in
Section

3.1
, we first argue that
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}{\bm{X}}^{T}
follows direclty from the chain rule. We then show that for every decomposition
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{R}}
, there exists an unknown disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
allowing us to reconstruct
{\bm{X}}^{\top}={\bm{Q}}^{-1}{\bm{R}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{Q}}
.
The remainder of the section then focuses on recovering
{\bm{Q}}
.
To this end, we show in
Section

3.2
that ReLU layers induce sparsity in
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
, which we then leveraged in
Section

3.3
to reconstruct the columns of
{\bm{Q}}
up to scale. Finally, in
Section

3.4
, we show how the scale of
{\bm{Q}}
’s columns can be recovered from
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}}
.
Unless otherwise noted, we defer all proofs to
App.

B
.

3.1
Explicit Low-Rank Representation of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}

We first show that the weight gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}
can be written as follows:

Theorem 3.1
.

The network’s gradient w.r.t. the weights
{\bm{W}}
can be represented as the matrix product:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}{\bm{X}}^{T}.

(1)

For batch sizes
b\leq n,m
, the dimensionalities of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times b}
and
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
in
Equation

1
directly yield that the rank of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}
is at most
b
. This confirms the observations of
Kariyappa et al. [9]
and shows that
{\bm{X}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
correspond to a specific low-rank decomposition of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}
.

To actually find this decomposition and thus recover
{\bm{X}}
, we first consider an arbitrary decomposition of the form
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{R}}
, where
{\bm{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times b}
and
{\bm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n}
are of maximal rank. We chose the decomposition obtained via the reduced SVD decomposition of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{S}}{\bm{V}}
by setting
{\bm{L}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{S}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
{\bm{R}}={\bm{S}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{V}}
, where
{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
,
{\bm{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
and
{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n}
. We now show that there exists an unique disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
recovering
{\bm{X}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
from
{\bm{L}}
and
{\bm{R}}
:

Theorem 3.2
.

If the gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
and the input matrix
{\bm{X}}
are of full-rank and
b\leq n,m
, then there exists an unique matrix
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
of full-rank s.t.
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{Q}}
and
{\bm{X}}^{T}={\bm{Q}}^{-1}{\bm{R}}
.

Theorem

3.2
is a direct application of
Lemma

B.1
shown in
App.

B
, a general linear algebra result stating that under most circumstances different low-rank matrix decompositions can be transformed into each other via an unique invertible matrix. Crucially, this implies that recovering the input
{\bm{X}}
and the gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
matrices is equivalent to obtaining the unique disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
. Next, we show how the ReLU-induced sparsity patterns in
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
or
{\bm{X}}
can be leveraged to recover
{\bm{Q}}
exactly.

3.2
ReLU-Induced Sparsity

ReLU activation layers can induce sparsity both in the gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
(if the ReLU activation succeeds the considered linear layer) or in the input (if the ReLU activation precedes the linear layer).

Gradien Sparsity

If a ReLU activation succeeds the linear layer, i.e.,
{\bm{Y}}=\relu({\bm{Z}})
, we have
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Y}}}\odot\mathds{1}_{[{\bm{Z}}>0]}
, where
\odot
is the elementwise multiplication and
\mathds{1}_{[{\bm{Z}}>0]}
is a matrix of
0
s and
1
s with each entry indicating if the corresponding entry in
{\bm{Z}}
is positive. At initialization, roughly half of the entries in
{\bm{Z}}
are positive, making
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
sparse with
\sim 0.5
of the entries
=0
.

Input Sparsity

ReLUs also introduce sparsity if the linear layer in question is preceded by a ReLU activation. Here,
{\bm{X}}=\relu(\tilde{{\bm{Z}}})
will again be sparse with
\sim 0.5
of the entries
=0
at initialization.

Note that for all but the first and the last layer of a fully connected network, we have sparsity in both,
{\bm{X}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
.
Due to the symmetry of their formulas in
Theorem

3.2
, our method can be applied in all three arising sparsity settings.
In the remainder of this work, we assume w.l.o.g. that only
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
is sparse, corresponding to the first layer of a fully connected network. We now describe how to leverage this sparsity to compute the disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
and thus recover the input batch
{\bm{X}}
.

3.3
Breaking Aggregation through Sparsity

Our exact recovery algorithm for the disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
is based on the following insight:

If we can construct two submatrices
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b-1\times b}
and
{\bm{L}}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{b-1\times b}
by choosing
b-1
rows with the same indices from
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
and
{\bm{L}}
, respectively, such that
{\bm{A}}
has full rank and an all-zero
i^{\text{th}}
column, then the kernel
\ker({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}})
of
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
contains a column
{\bm{q}}_{i}
of
{\bm{Q}}
up to scale. We formalize this as follows:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b-1\times b}
be a submatrix of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
s.t. its
i^{\text{th}}
column is
\mathbf{0}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,b\}
.
Further, let
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
,
{\bm{X}}
, and
{\bm{A}}
be of full rank and
{\bm{Q}}
be as in
Theorem

3.2
.
Then, there exists a full-rank submatrix
{\bm{L}}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{b-1\times b}
of
{\bm{L}}
s.t.
\Span({\bm{q}}_{i})=\ker({\bm{L}}_{A})
for the
i^{\text{th}}
column
{\bm{q}}_{i}
of
{\bm{Q}}=({\bm{q}}_{1}|\cdots|{\bm{q}}_{b})
.

Proof.

Pick an
i\in\{1,\dots,b\}
.
By assumption, there exists a submatrix
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b-1\times b}
of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
of rank
b-1
whose
i^{\text{th}}
column is
\mathbf{0}
.
To construct
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
, we take rows from
{\bm{L}}
with indices corresponding to
{\bm{A}}
’s row indices in
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
. As
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
and
{\bm{X}}
have full rank, by
Theorem

3.2
, we know that
\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{Q}}
, and hence
{\bm{A}}={\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}
. Multiplying from the right with
{\bm{e}}_{i}
yields
0={\bm{A}}{\bm{e}}_{i}={\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{e}}_{i}={\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{q}}_{i}
,
and hence
\ker({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}})\supseteq\Span({\bm{q}}_{i})
.
Further, as
\rank({\bm{A}})=b-1
and
\rank({\bm{Q}})=b
, we have that
\rank({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}})=b-1
. By the rank-nullity theorem
\dim(\ker({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}))=1
and hence
\ker({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}})=\Span({\bm{q}}_{i})
.
∎

As
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
is not known a priori, we can not simply search for such a set of rows. Instead, we have to sample submatrices
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
of
{\bm{L}}
at random and then filter them using the approach discussed in
Section

4
. However, we will show in
Section

5.2
that we will find suitable submatrices with high probability for
b<m
due to the sparsity of
\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
and the large number
\binom{m}{b-1}
of possible submatrices. We will now discuss how to recover the scale of the columns
{\bm{q}}_{i}
given their unscaled directions
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}
forming
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}
.

3.4
Obtaining
\boldsymbol{Q}
: Recovering the Scale of columns in
\overline{\boldsymbol{Q}}

Given a set of
b
correct directions
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}=(\overline{{\bm{q}}_{1}}|\cdots|\overline{{\bm{q}}_{b}})
, we can recover their scale, enabling us to reconstruct
{\bm{X}}
, as follows. We first represent the correctly scaled columns as
{\bm{q}}_{i}=s_{i}\cdot\overline{{\bm{q}}_{i}}
with the unknown scale parameters
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now, recovering the scale is equivalent to computing all
s_{i}
. To this end, we leverage the gradient w.r.t. the bias
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}}
:

Theorem 3.4
.

The gradient w.r.t. the bias
{\bm{b}}
can be written in the form

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vphantom{\int\limits^{x}}\smash{\vdots}\\
1\end{bmatrix}
.

Thus, the coefficients
s_{i}
can be calculated as:

Theorem 3.5
.

For any left inverse
{\bm{L}}^{-L}
of
{\bm{L}}
, we have

\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}\\
\vphantom{\int\limits^{x}}\smash{\vdots}\\
s_{b}\end{bmatrix}=\overline{{\bm{Q}}}\,^{-1}{\bm{L}}^{-L}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}}

Theorem

3.5
allows us to directly obtain the true matrix
{\bm{Q}}=\overline{{\bm{Q}}}\diag(s_{1},\ldots,s_{b})
from the unscaled matrix
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}
. We now discuss how to recover
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}
via sampling and filtering candidate directions
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}
.

4
Efficient Filtering and Validation of Candidates

In the previous section, we saw that given the correct selection of submatrices
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
, we can recover
{\bm{Q}}
directly. However, we do not know how to pick
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
a priori. To solve this, we rely on a sampling approach: We first randomly sample submatrices
{\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}}
of
{\bm{L}}
and corresponding direction candidates
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
spanning
\ker({\bm{L}}_{\bm{A}})
.
However, checking whether
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
is a valid direction is not straightforward as we do not know
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
and hence can not observe
{\bm{A}}
directly as reconstructing
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{Q}}
requires the full
{\bm{Q}}
.

To address this, we filter the majority of wrong proposals
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
using deduplication and a sparsity-based criterion (
Section

4.1
), leaving us with a set of candidate directions
\mathcal{C}=\{\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j\in\{1,\dots,c\}}
. We then select the correct directions in
\mathcal{C}
greedily based on a novel sparsity matching score (
Section

4.2
).

4.1
Efficient Filtering of Directions
\overline{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}

Filtering Mixtures via Sparsity

It is highly likely (
p=(1-\tfrac{1}{2^{b-1}})^{b}
) that a random submatrix of
{\bm{L}}
will not correspond to an
{\bm{A}}
with any
\mathbf{0}
column. We filter these directions by leveraging the following insight. The kernel of such submatrices is spanned by a linear combination
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}
. Thus
{\bm{L}}\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
will be a linear combination of sparse columns of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{{\bm{L}}}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
. As this sparsity structure is random, linear combinations will have much lower sparsity with high probability.
We thus discard all candidates
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
with sparsity of
{\bm{L}}\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}
below a threshold
\tau
, chosen to make the probability of falsely rejecting a correct direction
p_{fr}(\tau,m)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor m\cdot\tau\rfloor}\binom{m}{i}
, obtained from the cumulative distribution function of the binomial distribution, small. For example for
m=400
and
p_{fr}(\tau,m)<10^{-5}
, we have
\tau=0.395
.
We obtain the candidate pool
\mathcal{C}=\{\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j\in\{1,\dots,c\}}
from all samples that were not filered this way.

Filtering Duplicates

As it is highly likely to have multiple full-rank submatrices
{\bm{A}}
, whose
i^{\text{th}}
column is
\mathbf{0}
, we expect to sample the same proposal
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}^{\prime}
multiple times. We remove these duplicates to substantially reduce our search space.

4.2
Greedy Optimization

While filtering duplicates and linear combinations significantly reduces the number
c
of candidates, we usually still have to select a subset of
b<c
.
Thus, we have
\binom{c}{b}
possible
b
sized subsets, each inducing a candidate
{\bm{Q}}^{\prime}
and thus
{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
.
A naive approach is to compute the gradients for all
{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
and compare them to the ground truth. However, this is computationally infeasible even for moderate
c
.

To address this, we propose a greedy two-stage procedure optimizing a novel sparsity matching score
\lambda
, which resolves the computational complexity issue above while also accurately selecting the correct batch elements and relying solely on
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}
. As both can be computed directly via
{\bm{Q}}^{\prime}
, the procedure is local and does not need to backpropagate gradients.
Next, we explain the first stage.

Dictionary Learning
[
10
]

As a first stage, we leverage a component of the algorithm proposed by
Spielman et al. [10]
for sparsely-used dictionary learning. This approach is based on the insight that the subset of column vectors
\mathcal{B}=\{\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{b}
, yielding the sparsest full-rank gradient matrix
\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
is often correct. As the scaling of
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}^{\prime}
does not change the sparsity of the resulting
\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
, we can construct the subset
\mathcal{B}
by greedily collecting the
b
directions
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}^{\prime}
with the highest corresponding sparsity that still increase the rank of
\mathcal{B}
. While this method typically recovers most directions
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}
, it often misses directions whose gradients
\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{z}}_{i}}
are less sparse by chance.

Sparsity Matching

We alleviate this issue by introducing a second stage to the algorithm where we greedily optimize a novel correctness measure based solely on the gradients of the linear layer, which we call the sparsity matching coefficient
\lambda
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\lambda_{-}
be the number of non-positive entries in
{\bm{Z}}
whose corresponding entries in
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
are
0
. Similarly, let
\lambda_{+}
be the number of positive entries in
{\bm{Z}}
whose corresponding entries in
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
are not
0
. We call their normalized sum the
sparsity matching coefficient

\lambda
:

\lambda=\frac{\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+}}{m\cdot b}.

Intuitively, this describes how well the pre-activation values
{\bm{Z}}
match the sparsity pattern of the gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
induced by the ReLU layer (See
Section

3.2
). While this sparsity matching coefficient
\lambda
can take values between
0
and
1
, it is exactly
\lambda=1
for the correct
{\bm{X}}
, if the gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Y}}}
w.r.t. the ReLU output is dense, which is usually the case. We note that
\lambda
can be computed efficiently for arbitrary full rank matrix
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}^{\prime}
by computing
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}^{\prime}={\bm{L}}{\bm{Q}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}={\bm{W}}{\bm{X}}^{\prime}+({\bm{b}}|\dots|{\bm{b}})
for
{\bm{X}}^{\prime\top}={\bm{Q}}^{\prime-1}{\bm{R}}
.

To optimize
\lambda
, we initialize
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}^{\prime}
with the result of the greedy algorithm in
Spielman et al. [10]
, and then greedily swap the pair of vectors
\overline{{\bm{q}}}^{\prime}_{i}
improving
\lambda
the most, while keeping the rank, until convergence.

We can lower bound the probability of a submatrix

Lemma 5.1

Let

obtained by sampling
b-1

\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}

Related Work

In this section, we discuss how we relate to prior work.

Gradient Inversion Attacks

Since gradient inversion attacks have been introduced
[
3
]
, two settings have emerged:
In the
malicious setting
, the server does not adhere to the training protocol and can adversarially engineer network weights that maximize leaked information
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
In the strictly harder
honest-but-curious setting
, the server follows the training protocol but still aims to reconstruct client data.
We target the honest-but-curious setting, where prior work has either recovered the input exactly for batch sizes of
b=1

[
5
,
6
]
, or approximately for
b>1

[
1
,
23
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. In this setting, we are the
first to reconstruct inputs exactly for batch sizes
b>1
.

Most closely related to our work is
Kariyappa et al. [9]
which leverage the low-rank structure of the gradients to frame gradient inversion as a blind source separation problem, improving their approximate reconstructions. In contrast, we derive an
explicit
low-rank representation and additionally leverage gradient sparsity reconstruct inputs exactly.

Unlike a long line of prior work, we rely neither on any priors on the data distribution
[
8
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
nor on a reconstructed classification label
[
1
,
27
,
7
,
23
,
8
]
. This allows our approach to be employed in a much wider range of settings where neither is available.

Defenses Against Gradient Inversion

Defenses based on Differential Privacy
[
28
]
add noise to the computed gradients on the client side, providing provable privacy guarantees at the cost of significantly reduced utility.
Another line of work increases the empirical difficulty of inversion by increasing the effective batch size, by securely aggregating gradients from multiple clients
[
29
]
or doing multiple gradient update steps locally before sharing an aggregated weight update
[
4
]
.
Finally, different heuristic defenses such as gradient pruning
[
3
]
have been proposed, although their effectiveness has been questioned
[
30
]
.

Sparsely-used Dictionary learning

Recovering the disaggregation matrix
{\bm{Q}}
is related to the well-studied problem of sparsely-used dictionary learning. However, there the aim is to find the sparsest coefficient matrix (corresponding to our
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
) and dense dictionary (
{\bm{Q}}^{-1}
) approximately encoding a signal (
{\bm{L}}
). In contrast, we do not search for the sparsest solution yielding an approximate reconstruction but a solution that exactly induces consistent
{\bm{X}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
, which happens to be sparse. Sparsely-used dictionary learning is known to be NP-hard
[
31
]
and typically solved approximately
[
32
,
10
,
33
]
. However, under sufficient sparsity, it can be solved exactly in polynomial time
[
10
]
. While our
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
are not sparse enough, we still draw inspiration from

in

.

8
Limitations

We focus on recovering the inputs to fully connected layers with ReLU activations such as they occur at the beginning of fully connected networks or as aggregation layers of many other architectures. Extending our approach to other layers is an interesting direction for future work.

Further, our approach scales exponentially with batch size
b
. While
SPEAR
’s massive parallelizability and its ability to be combined with optimization-based attacks, as shown in
Section

6.3
, can partially mitigate the computational complexity, future research is still required to make reconstruction of batches of size
b>100
practical.

9
Conclusion

We propose
SPEAR
, the first algorithm permitting batches of
b>1
elements to be recovered exactly in the honest-but-curious setting. We demonstrate theoretically and empirically that
SPEAR
succeeds with high probability and that our highly parallelized GPU implementation is effective across a wide range of settings, including batches of up to
25
elements and large networks and inputs.

We thereby demonstrate that contrary to prior belief, an exact reconstruction of batches is possible in the honest-but-curious setting, suggesting that federated learning on ReLU networks might be inherently more susceptible than previously thought. To still protect client privacy, large effective batch sizes, obtained, e.g., via secure aggregation across a large number of clients, might prove instrumental by making reconstruction computationally intractable.

Appendix A
Broader Impact

In this work, we demonstrate that contrary to prior belief, an exact reconstruction of batches is possible in the honest-but-curious setting for federated learning.
As our work demonstrates the susceptibility of federated learning systems using ReLU networks, this work inevitably advances the capabilities of an adversary.
Nonetheless, we believe this to be an important step in accurately assessing the risks and utilities of federated learning systems.

To still protect client privacy, large effective batch sizes, obtained, e.g., via secure aggregation across a large number of clients, might prove instrumental by making reconstruction computationally intractable.
As gradient information and network states can be stored practically indefinitely, our work highlights the importance of proactively protecting client privacy in federated learning not only against current but future attacks.
This underlines the importance of related work on provable privacy guarantees obtained via differential privacy.

∎

We now compute the number of samples

required to recover all

1-p

Lemma B.2
.

In the same setting as

on the number of submatrices we need to sample until we have recovered all
b
correct direction vectors by solving the following quadratic inequality for
n^{p}_{\text{total}}

\frac{p}{2}\leq\Phi\left(\frac{b\log(2b/p^{*})-n^{p}_{\text{total}}q}{\sqrt{n^{p}_{\text{total}}q(1-q)}}\right),

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution and

.

Proof.

At a high level, bound the number of valid directions
c^{p}
we need to discover until we recover all
b
distinct ones and then the number of submatrices
n^{p}_{\text{total}}
we need to sample to obtain these
c^{p}
directions, each with probability
1-\tfrac{p}{2}
, before applying the union bound.

However, we first note that with probability
1-(1-p_{fr})^{b}
we will (repeatedly) reject a correct direction due to a lack of induced sparsity and thus fail irrespective of the number of samples we draw. We thus correct our failure probability budget from
p
to
p^{*}=p-1+(1-p_{fr})^{b}
, using the union bound.

We now show how to compute the upper bound on the number of correct directions
c^{p}
we need to find until we have found all
b
distinct directions. To this end, we bound the probability of not sampling the
i^{\text{th}}
direction
\overline{{\bm{q}}}_{i}
after finding
c

p_{\lnot i}=(1-\frac{1}{b})^{c}\leq e^{-\frac{c}{b}}
. We can then bound the probability of missing any of the
b

p_{\lnot\text{all}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{b}p_{\lnot i}=be^{-\frac{c^{p}}{b}}
. We thus obtain the minimum number
c^{p}
of correct directions to find all
b
distinct ones with probability at least
\tfrac{p^{*}}{2}

c^{p}\geq b\log(2b/p^{*})
.

We can now compute the number

of samples required to find
c^{p}
submatrices satisfying the condition of

]

required to find
c^{p}
valid directions with high probability
1-\tfrac{p^{*}}{2}

\tfrac{p^{*}}{2}=\Phi(\frac{c^{p}-n^{p}_{\text{total}}q}{\sqrt{n^{p}_{\text{total}}q(1-q)}})

By the union bound, we have that the total failure probability of not finding all

Adapting

In the experiments presented in

6

Invariance to Gradient Clipping

In
Table

9

C
. This is not a coincidence, but rather a mathematical fact. To see this, note that the observed gradients w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
under clipping are given by:

\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{b}c_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{b}c_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}_{i}}{\bm{X}}_{i},

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are the unknown to the attacker factors applied by the clipping procedure to each individual input gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}_{i}}
. One can adapt the proof to
Theorem

3.1
, to show that
\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}}=\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}}{\bm{X}}^{T}
, where we define
\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}_{i}}}
to be the clipped gradient w.r.t
{\bm{Z}}
, consisting of the columns
\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}_{i}}}=c_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}_{i}}
. We also observe that one can adapt
Theorem

3.4
to work directly on the clipped gradients as well, resulting in the formula
\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}}}=\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vphantom{\int\limits^{x}}\smash{\vdots}\\
1\end{bmatrix}
for the clipped gradient w.r.t.
{\bm{b}}
. The formula follows from the observation that in our setting the same clipping factor
c_{i}
is applied to the gradients of each layer, including
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{b}}^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[2.66785pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[2.66785pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.2058pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[0.86128pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{{i}}}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}_{i}}
. By applying the rest of the theoretical results of the paper without change but on clipped gradients
\dot{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}}
, instead of the original unclipped gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}}
, we conclude that
SPEAR
is directly applicable on the clipped client gradient and that applying it on those still recover the true input matrix
{\bm{X}}
without the need of knowing the clipping constants
c_{i}
.

Appendix F
SPEAR
under FedAvg Updates

In this section, we first demonstrate theoretically that
SPEAR
can be generalized to attack FedAvg
[
4
]
client updates, and then present empirical results confirming that
SPEAR
is indeed very effective under FedAvg protocols with different number of epochs
\mathcal{E}
, local client learning rates
\eta
, and, even works, when mini-batches of size
b_{\text{mini}}
are used.

Generalizing
SPEAR
to FedAvg Updates

Assuming that a client uses all of its data points,
{\bm{X}}
, in each local gradient step of the FedAvg protocol, i.e.
b_{\text{mini}}=b
, the client computes and subsequently shares with the server the following updated linear layer weights:

{\bm{W}}^{\mathcal{E}}={\bm{W}}^{0}-\eta\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{e}}={\bm{W}}^{0}-\eta\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}\cdot{\bm{X}}^{T}={\bm{W}}^{0}-\eta\left(\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}\right)\cdot{\bm{X}}^{T},

where
{\bm{W}}^{0}
is the global model sent by the server,
{\bm{W}}^{e}
represent the local client weights after
e
client epochs, and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{e}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
are the weight and output gradients at epoch
e
.

We empirically observe that sparsity patterns of the different local gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
are usually similar. This is expected as these patterns correspond to the ReLU activation patterns for the layer outputs
{\bm{Z}}^{e}
(see
Section

3.2
) at different local steps which are computed on the same data
{\bm{X}}
and with similar weights
{\bm{W}}^{e}
. As the sparsity patterns for the individual gradients are similar, their sum
\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
also shares this sparsity pattern and is, thus, also sparse. As the server knows
{\bm{W}}^{0}
and it can subtract it from the client’s shared weights
{\bm{W}}^{\mathcal{E}}
and apply
Theorem

3.3
, as before, on the sparse matrix
\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
to obtain the corresponding matrix
{\bm{Q}}
and client data
{\bm{X}}
. We note that while our sparsity matching coefficient
\sigma
will typically not reach 1 for the final reconstruction in this setting, as there is some mismatch between the sparsity patterns of the different output gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
, we have found that
SPEAR
remains practically effective regardless.

We note that
SPEAR
can be even be generalized to FedAvg protocols that use random mini-batches
{\bm{X}}^{e}
of size
b_{\text{mini}}<b
sampled from
{\bm{X}}
at each local step. This is the case, as each local client gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{e}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}({\bm{X}}^{e})^{T}
, can be represented as
\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}{\bm{X}}^{T}
, where
\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}
is derived from
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
by adding
0
columns at batch positions corresponding to batch elements not in
{\bm{X}}^{e}
. Importantly, as
\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}
only adds
0
columns to
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
, the sparsity of
\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}
can only increase, allowing to conclude that
\sum_{e=1}^{\mathcal{E}}\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}
remains sparse, and, thus,
Theorem

3.3
can still be applied to it.

Experiments with FedAvg Updates

Next, we show empirically the effectiveness of
SPEAR
for FedAvg updates. In
Table

10
, we show the results of attacking clients with
b=20
datapoints from the
TinyImageNet
dataset for different number of local client epochs
\mathcal{E}
. We observe that even for
\mathcal{E}=50
gradient steps we recover data from most batches, with quality similar to the quality achieved when attacking individual gradients. This is expected as
Theorem

3.3
still holds, as described in the previous paragraph. The slight dip in the fraction of reconstructed batches for larger number of steps
\mathcal{E}
can be attributed to some client batches inducing larger discrepancy between the sparsity patterns of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
compared to others, resulting in their sum being much less sparse. Further,
Table

10
also shows that
SPEAR
can attack client updates that take
b/b_{\text{mini}}=4
local steps per epoch for
\mathcal{E}=20
epochs. Interestingly, while a total of
80
gradient steps are taken in this scenario the results are closer to the
b_{\text{mini}}=20,\mathcal{E}=20
setting, instead of the
b_{\text{mini}}=20,\mathcal{E}=50
setting. This can be explained by the increased sparsity of the individual expanded gradients
\overline{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}}
.

Finally, we experiment with different local client learning rates
\eta
and show the results in
Table

11
. We observe that even for large learning rates
SPEAR
still recovers its inputs well, showing that while the individual weights
{\bm{W}}^{e}
can change a lot, their induced sparsity on
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{Z}}^{e}}
remains consistent.
