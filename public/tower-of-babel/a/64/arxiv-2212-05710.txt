For a continuously differentiable complex-valued mapping
f(z)=u(z)+iv(z)
,
z=x+iy
, we use the common notions for its formal derivatives:

\displaystyle f_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{x}-if_{y}\right)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;f_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{x}+if_{y}\right).

We say that
f
is a harmonic mapping in a simply connected domain
\Omega
if
f
is twice continuously differentiable and satisfies the Laplacian equation
\Delta f=4f_{z\bar{z}}=0
in
\Omega
, where
\Delta
is the complex Laplacian operator defined by

\Delta={\partial^{2}}/{\partial x^{2}}+{\partial^{2}}/{\partial y^{2}}.

Methods of harmonic mappings have been applied to study and solve the fluid flow problems (see
[

32

[
8

established a connection between harmonic mappings and ideal fluid flows. In fact, Aleman and Constantin have developed ingenious technique to solve the incompressible two dimensional Euler equations in terms of univalent harmonic mappings (see
[
32

for details).

Let
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
be the class of complex-valued functions harmonic in
\Omega
. It is well-known that functions
f
in the class
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
has the following representation
f=h+\overline{g}
, where
h
and
g
both are analytic functions in
\Omega
. The famous Lewy’s theorem
[
53
]
in
1936
states that a harmonic mapping
f=h+\overline{g}
is locally univalent on
\Omega
if, and only if, the determinant
|J_{f}(z)|
of its Jacobian matrix
J_{f}(z)
does not vanish on
\Omega
, where

|J_{f}(z)|:=|f_{z}(z)|^{2}-|f_{\bar{z}}(z)|^{2}=|h^{\prime}(z)|^{2}-|g^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\neq 0.

In view of this result, a locally univalent harmonic mapping is sense-preserving if
|J_{f}(z)|>0
and sense-reversing if
|J_{f}(z)|<0
in
\Omega
. For detailed information about the harmonic mappings, we refer the reader to
[
31
,
36
]
. In
[
49
]
, Kayumov
et al.
first established the harmonic extension of the classical Bohr theorem, since then investigating on the Bohr-type inequalities for certain class of harmonic mappings becomes an interesting topic of research in geometric function theory.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of all analytic functions of the form
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
defined on
\mathbb{D}
and we define the class
\mathcal{B}=\{f\in\mathcal{A}:|f(z)|\leq 1\;\mbox{in}\;\mathbb{D}\}
. Let us first recall the theorem of Bohr
[
26
]
in
1914
, which inspired a lot in the recent years.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
26
]

If
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathcal{B}
, then

(1.1)

M_{f}(r):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq 1\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;|z|=r\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Initially, Bohr showed the inequality (
1.1
) for
|z|\leq 1/6
, but later M. Riesz, I. Schur and F. Wiener subsequently improved the inequality (
1.1
) for
|z|\leq 1/3
and showed that the constant
1/3
is best possible. It is quite natural that the constant
1/3
and the inequality (
1.1
) are called respectively, the Bohr radius and Bohr inequality for the class
\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, for:

f_{a}(z)=\frac{a-z}{1-az},\;\;a\in[0,1)

it follows easily that
M_{f_{a}}(r)>1
if, and only if,
r>1/(1+2a)
, which shows that
1/3
is best possible as the limiting case
a\rightarrow 1
suggests.

Bohr phenomenon can be studied in view of Euclidean distance and in this paper, we study the same for certain classes of harmonic mappings. Before we go into details, we recall here the following concepts. Let
f
and
g
be two analytic functions in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. We say that
g
is subordinate to
f
if there is a function
\varphi
, analytic in
\mathbb{D}
,
\varphi(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
and
\varphi(0)=0
so that
g=f\circ\varphi
. In particular, when the function
f
is univalent,
g
is subordinate to
f
when
g(\mathbb{D}\subset f(\mathbb{D}))
and
g(0)=f(0)
(see
[
35
, p. 190]
). Consequently, when
g
is subordinate to
f
,
|g^{\prime}(0)|\leq|f^{\prime}(0)|
. The class of all function
g
subordinate to a fixed function
f
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(f)
and
f(\mathbb{D})=\Omega
.

Definition 1.1
.

[
1
]

We say that
\mathcal{S}(f)
has Bohr phenomenon if for any
g(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}\in\mathcal{S}(f)
and
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
there is a
\rho^{*}_{0}
,
0<\rho^{*}_{0}\leq 1
so that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|b_{n}z^{n}|\leq d(f(0),\partial\Omega)
, for
|z|<\rho^{*}_{0}
. Notice that
d(f(0),\partial\Omega)
denote the Euclidean distance between
f(0)
and the boundary of a domain
\Omega
,
\partial\Omega
. In particular, when
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
,
d(f(0),\partial\Omega)=1-|f(0)|
and in this case
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq d(f(0),\partial\Omega)
reduces to
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq 1
.

Equation (
1.1
) can be written as

(1.2)

d\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|,|a_{0}|\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq 1-|f(0)|=d(f(0),\partial(\mathbb{D})),

where
d
is the Euclidean distance. More generally, a class
\mathcal{F}
of analytic functions
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
mapping
\mathbb{D}
into a domain
\Omega
is said to satisfy a Bohr phenomenon if an inequality of type (
1.2
) holds uniformly in
|z|\leq\rho_{0}
, where
0<\rho_{0}\leq 1
for functions in the class
\mathcal{F}
. Similar definition makes sense for harmonic functions (see
[
49
]
).

Abu-Muhanna
[
1
]
have established the following result for subordination
S(f)
when
f
is univalent.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
1
]

If
g(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}\in S(f)
and
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
is univalent, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|b_{n}z^{n}|^{n}\leq d(f(0),\partial\Omega)

for
|z|=\rho_{0}^{*}\leq 3-\sqrt{8}\approx 0.17157,
where
\rho_{0}^{*}
is sharp for Koebe function
f(z)=z/(1-z)^{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the class of all complex-valued harmonic functions
f=h+\bar{g}
defined on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, where
h
and

are analytic in
\mathbb{D}
with the normalization

and

. Let

be defined by
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{f=h+\bar{g}\in\mathcal{H}:g^{\prime}(0)=0\}.
Therefore, each

has the following representation

(1.3)

f(z)=h(z)+\overline{g(z)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}+\overline{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}}=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}+\overline{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}},

where

and

, since

and

Let us recall the Bohr radius for the class of harmonic mappings.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
be given by (
1.3
). Then the Bohr phenomenon is to find a constant
R^{*}\in(0,1]
such that the inequality
r+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(|a_{n}|+|b_{n}|\right)r^{n}\leq d\left(f(0),\partial\Omega\right)
holds for
|z|=r\leq R^{*}
, where

is the Euclidean distance between
f(0)
and the boundary of
\Omega:=f(\mathbb{D})
. The largest such radius
R^{*}
is called the Bohr radius for the class
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Based on the notion of Rogosinski’s inequality and Rogosinski’s radius investigated in

Theorem 1.3

[

Suppose that

with

(1.4)

where

is the positive root of the equation

R_{N}
is best possible. Moreover,

(1.5)

where
R^{\prime}_{N}
is the positive root of the equation
(1+r)r^{N}-(1-r)^{2}=0
. The radius
R^{\prime}_{N}
is best possible.

2.
Main results

Before stating the main results, we recall here the definition of dilogarithm
{\rm Li}_{2}(z)
which is defined by the power series

{\rm Li}_{2}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n^{2}}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;|z|<1.

The following equality of dilogarithm holds

{\rm Li}_{2}(r)+{\rm Li}_{2}(1-r)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\log r\log(1-r).

Moreover,

\displaystyle{\rm Li}_{2}(1-r^{2})\rightarrow 0\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\frac{\log(1/r)}{1-r}\rightarrow 1\;\;\mbox{as}\;\;r\rightarrow 1.

In particular, the analytic continuation of the dilogarithm is given by

{\rm Li}_{2}(z)=-\int_{0}^{z}\log(1-u)\frac{du}{u}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty).

The Euclidean distance between
f(0)
and the boundary of
f(\mathbb{D})
is given by

(2.1)

d(f(0),\partial f(\mathbb{D}))=\liminf\limits_{|z|=r\rightarrow 1}|f(z)-f(0)|.

2.1.
Refined Bohr-type inequality for the class
\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)

Motivated by the class
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}^{0}

The Bohr phenomenon has been studied recently for the class

(2.17)

\displaystyle 2M\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)+\left(1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)\ln\left(1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)-\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}\frac{\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)^{n}}{n(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle+\mu\Phi^{N}_{M,t}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)+4M^{2}\lambda\left(1+\frac{R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)}{1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)}\right)G_{2,t}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)

\displaystyle+\left(G_{M}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)\right)^{m}=1+2M\left(1-2\ln 2\right).

Since
f(0)=0
, then in view of Lemma
2.4
and (
2.1
), a simple computation shows that

(2.18)

d(f(0),\partial f(\mathbb{D}))\geq 1+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}2M\dfrac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n(n-1)}=1+2M\left(1-2\ln 2\right).

Therefore, in view of (
2.16
) and (
2.18
), we see that
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)
holds. Now it remains to show that the constant
R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)
is best possible. Henceforth, we consider the function
f=f_{M}
defined by

f_{M}(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2Mz^{n}}{n(n-1)}.

It is easy to see that
f_{M}\in\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(M)
and for
f=f_{M}
, in view of (
2.1
), by a routine computation, we can show that

(2.19)

d(f_{M}(0),\partial f_{M}(\mathbb{D}))=1+2M\left(1-2\ln 2\right).

For
f=f_{M}
and
z=r>R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)
, by the similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, using (
2.16
)
to (
2.19
), it can be shown that

\displaystyle S^{f_{M}}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)

\displaystyle>2M\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)+\left(1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)\ln\left(1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)-\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}\frac{\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)^{n}}{n(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\Phi^{N}_{M,t}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)+4M^{2}\lambda\left(1+\frac{R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)}{1-R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)}\right)G_{2,t}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(G_{M}\left(R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)\right)\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=d(f_{M}(0),\partial f_{M}(\mathbb{D})).

Therefore,
R^{m,N,t}_{2,\mu,\lambda}(M)
is best possible. This completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Refined Bohr-type inequality for the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)

In
1977
, Chichra
[
27
]
introduced the class
\mathcal{W}(\alpha)
consisting of normalized analytic functions
h
, satisfying the condition
{\rm Re}\left(h^{\prime}(z)+\alpha zh^{\prime\prime}(z)\right)>0
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
and
\alpha\geq 0
. Moreover, Chichra
[
27
]
has shown that functions in the class
\mathcal{W}(\alpha)
constitute a subclass of close-to-convex functions in
\mathbb{D}
. In
2014
, Nagpal and Ravichandran
[
62
]
studied the following class

\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}=\{f=h+\bar{g}\in\mathcal{H}:{\rm Re}\left(h^{\prime}(z)+zh^{\prime\prime}(z)\right)>|g^{\prime}(z)+zg^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\;\mbox{for}\;z\in\mathbb{D}\}

and obtained the coefficient bounds for the functions in the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}
. In
2019
, Ghosh and Vasudevarao studied the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
, where

\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)=\{f=h+\bar{g}\in\mathcal{H}:{\rm Re}\left(h^{\prime}(z)+\alpha zh^{\prime\prime}(z)\right)>|g^{\prime}(z)+\alpha zg^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\;\mbox{for}\;z\in\mathbb{D}\}.

From the following results, it is easy to see that functions in the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
are univalent for
\alpha\geq 0
, and they are closely related to functions in
\mathcal{W}(\alpha)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
39
]

The harmonic mapping
f=h+\bar{g}
belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
if, and only if, the analytic function
F=h+\epsilon g
belongs to
\mathcal{W}(\alpha)
for each
|\epsilon|=1.

The coefficient bounds and the sharp growth estimates for functions in the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
have been studied in
[
39
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
39
]

Let
f\in\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
for
\alpha\geq 0
and be of the form (
1.3
). Then for any
n\geq 2
,

(i)

|a_{n}|+|b_{n}|\leq\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}
;

(ii)

||a_{n}|-|b_{n}||\leq\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}
;

(iii)

|a_{n}|\leq\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}
.

All these inequalities are sharp for the function
f=f^{*}_{\alpha}
given by

(2.20)

f^{*}_{\alpha}(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2z^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

Lemma 2.7
.

[
39
]

Let
f\in\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
and be of the form (
1.3
) with
0\leq\alpha<1
. Then

(2.21)

|z|+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}|z|^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\leq|f(z)|\leq|z|+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2|z|^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

Both the inequalities are sharp for the function
f=f^{*}_{\alpha}
given by (
2.20
).

We prove the following sharp refinement of the Bohr-Rogosinski inequality for functions in the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
be given by (
1.3
) and
0\leq\alpha<1
. Then for
\mu,\;\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
N\geq 5
, we have
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)

for
|z|=r\leq R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
, where
R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation
\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r)=0
, where

\displaystyle\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r):=

\displaystyle\left(r+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\right)^{m}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}+\frac{r^{N}}{1-r}\sum_{n=1}^{t}\frac{4\mu\;sgn(t)}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}-1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

The constant
R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is best possible.

Remark 2.3
.

For the triplets
(0,0,1)
,
(0,0,2)
,
(1,1,1)
,
(1,1,2)
corresponding to
(\lambda,\mu,m)
, the inequality
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)
is harmonic analog of (
1.4
), (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
), respectively, for the class
\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
.

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m,N}(r):

\displaystyle=\left(r+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\right)^{m}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}

\displaystyle\quad-1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

As a corollary of Theorem
2.3
, we obtain the following result exploring the situation when
N=1,2,3,4
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
be given by (
1.3
) and
0\leq\alpha<1
and
\mu,\;\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

(i)

If
N=1
, then
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,1}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)

for
|z|=r\leq R^{m,1,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
, where
R^{m,1,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m,1}(r)+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}=0.

(ii)

If
N=2
, then
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,2}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)

for
|z|=r\leq R^{m,2,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
, where
R^{m,2,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m,2}(r)+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}=0.

(iii)

If
N=3
, then
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,3}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)

for
|z|=r\leq R^{m,3,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
, where
R^{m,3,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m,3}(r)+\frac{r^{3}}{1-r}+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}=0.

(iv)

If
N=4
, then
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,4}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)

for
|z|=r\leq R^{m,4,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
, where
R^{m,4,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m,4}(r)+\frac{r^{4}}{1-r}+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}=0.

The constants
R^{m,1,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
,
R^{m,2,0}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
,
R^{m,3,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
and
R^{m,4,1}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
are best possible.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Since
f\in\mathcal{W}^{0}_{\mathcal{H}}(\alpha)
, in view of Lemma
2.7
and (
2.1
), it is easy to see that

(2.22)

\displaystyle d(f(0),\partial f(\mathbb{D}))\geq 1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

An easy computation using Lemma
2.6
shows that

(2.23)

\displaystyle S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\left(r+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\right)^{m}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{r^{N}}{1-r}\sum_{n=1}^{t}\frac{4\mu\;sgn(t)}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}

if
|z|=r\leq R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is the smallest root of the equation

\displaystyle\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r)=

\displaystyle\left(r+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\right)^{m}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2r^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}+\frac{r^{N}}{1-r}\sum_{n=1}^{t}\frac{4\mu\;sgn(t)}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\left(1+\frac{r}{1-r}\right)\sum_{n=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{4r^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}-1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}=0

in
(0,1)
. It is easy to see that
\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r)
is a real valued differentiable function satisfying

\displaystyle\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(0)=-1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}<0\;\mbox{and}\;\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow 1}\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r)=+\infty

and by a simple computation it can be shown that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(\Phi^{N,m,\alpha}_{3,\mu,\lambda,t}(r)\right)>0\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;r\in(0,1).

By adopting the similar arguments as applied in the proof of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, we can prove the existence and uniqueness of the root
R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
.

Thus, we must have

(2.24)

\displaystyle\left(R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2\left(R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)\right)^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}\right)^{m}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2\left(R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)\right)^{n}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\left(R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)\right)^{N}}{1-R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)}\sum_{n=1}^{t}\frac{4\mu\;sgn(t)}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}+\lambda\left(1+\frac{R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)}{1-R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)}\right)\sum_{n=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{4\left(R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)\right)^{2n}}{\left(\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

In view of (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we see that
S^{f}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)\leq{d}\left(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}\right)
holds. To show that the constant
R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is best possible, and henceforth, we consider the function
f=f^{*}_{\alpha}
. A simple computation shows that

\displaystyle d(f^{*}_{\alpha}(0),\partial f^{*}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D}))=1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n-1}}{\alpha n^{2}+(1-\alpha)n}.

By the similar arguments as used in the proof of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, using (
2.24
), it can be easily shown for
z=r>R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
that
S^{f^{*}_{\alpha}}_{\mu,\lambda,m,N}(r)>d(f^{*}_{\alpha}(0),\partial f^{*}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})).
Therefore,
R^{m,N,t}_{3,\mu,\lambda}(\alpha)
is best possible. This completes the proof.
∎
