A Mathematical Model of Package Management Systems
Gershom Bazerman 1, Emilio Minichiello 2, Raymond Puzio 3

Abstract
. This paper brings mathematical tools to bear on the study of package dependencies in software systems. We introduce structures known as Dependency Structures with Choice (DSC) that provide a mathematical account of such dependencies, inspired by the definition of general event structures in the study of concurrency. We equip DSCs with a particular notion of morphism and show that the category of DSCs is isomorphic to the category of antimatroids. We perform a similar analysis with Winskel’s general event structures [ Win99 ] , showing that that the category of DSCs is isomorphic to a certain subcategory of event structures without conflict. We study the exactness properties of these isomorphic categories and show that they are finitely complete with finite coproducts but do not have all coequalizers. Further, we construct a contravariant functor from a category of DSCs equipped with a certain subclass of morphisms to the category of finite distributive lattices, making use of a simple finite characterization of the Bruns-Lakser completion. Finally, we introduce a formal account of versions of packages and introduce a mathematical account of package version-bound policies.



1.
Introduction

Package repositories and package management systems are pervasive in modern software. Such repositories can consist of binary packages in Linux distributions (such as Debian or Arch), or of source packages for use in the course of developing software and managing the libraries on which it depends (such as the npm package repository for the JavaScript language or the Hackage package repository for the Haskell language).

This paper works towards the development of a basic toolkit for considering package dependencies in software systems. Specifically we develop a mathematical model of package management systems which we call
Dependency Structures with Choice
or
DSC
s for short. A preDSC consists of a finite set
E
, whose elements we think of as packages, and a function
\text{dep}:E\to P(P(E))
, where
P(E)
denotes the power set of
E
. We think of this function as a dependency structure on
E
. To every package
e
we use
dep
to associate a collection of “possible dependency sets”
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
. This models how packages can be run using various different sets of other packages. The inspiration for this mathematical model comes from general event structures, which were introduced for the purpose of studying concurrent computation
[
Win80
]
. DSCs are then a subclass preDSCs satisfying four natural axioms that any package management system should satisfy (Definition
2.19
). To every preDSC
(E,\text{dep})
we can associate a poset
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
, known as its
reachable dependency poset
, whose elements are those subsets of
E
that are reachable (Definition
2.4
). One can think of a subset
A\subseteq E
of packages as being reachable if the elements of
A
can be ordered into a sequence of package installations such that each new package installed depends only on already installed packages. It turns out that reachable dependency posets of preDSCs inherit more structure, namely they are finite join-semilattices, which implies that they are finite lattices. This structure gives us an order-theoretic way of describing and analyzing the dependency structure inherent to a preDSC.

We define a notion of morphism for preDSCs in such a way that
\mathsf{rdp}
becomes a functor from the category of preDSCs to the category of finite join-semilattices. If a preDSC
(E,\text{dep})
is furthermore a DSC, then
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is in particular a finite diamond-free semimodular lattice (Definition
3.23
). These diamond-free semimodular lattices are intimately related to antimatroids, and this led us to discover a correspondence between DSCs and antimatroids.

Antimatroids have been discovered and rediscovered for many purposes in lattice theory, graph theory, and combinatorics since the 1940s
[
Mon85
]
. An antimatroid consists of a set
E
and a collection of subsets
\mathcal{F}
, whose elements are called feasible sets, satisfying the axioms of Definition
3.1
. Antimatroids have several applications in computer science, such as in
[
GY94
]
and
[
MR16
]
. We provide another application by showing that if
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC, then the pair
(E,\mathsf{rdp}(E))
is an antimatroid. This is the content of Proposition
3.13
. Since the literature on antimatroids does not provide a standard definition of what a morphism of antimatroids should be, we take morphisms of antimatroids to be those functions that pull feasible sets back to feasible sets. This natural choice defines a category of antimatroids. One of the main results of this paper is Theorem
3.18
, which gives an isomorphism between the category of DSCs and the category of antimatroids. Combining this theorem with a result of Czedli
[
Czé14
]
then shows that any finite diamond-free semimodular lattice is isomorphic to the reachable dependency poset of a DSC.

While every DSC
(E,\text{dep})
gives a finite diamond-free semimodular lattice
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
, it is not always the case that
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
will be a distributive lattice. Distributive lattices are well studied objects of order theory, and we precisely characterize the subclass of DSCs whose reachable dependency poset is distributive. These turn out to be what we call Dependency Structures with No Choice, or those DSCs
(E,\text{dep})
where
\text{dep}(e)
is a singleton for every
e\in E
.

We establish an isomorphism of categories between irredundant preDSCs (Definition
2.12
) and those general event structures without conflict, which we call
conflict-free event structures
or
CFES
s. When we restrict this isomorphism to DSCs, we obtain an appropriate subcategory of CFESs in Theorem
5.12
, which we call
dependency event structures
. This leads also to Proposition
5.14
, which says that every family of configurations of a CFES can be produced by a dependency event structure. As a corollary it shows that given a preDSC
(E,\text{dep})
there exists a DSC
(\widetilde{E},\widetilde{\text{dep}})
and an isomorphism
\mathsf{rdp}(E)\cong\mathsf{rdp}(\widetilde{E})
.

We also prove exactness properties for the category of DSCs, equivalently the category of antimatroids and dependency event structures. In particular we prove that this category has all finite limits, but not all finite colimits. In particular, while the category of DSCs has finite coproducts, not all coequalizers exist. This can be seen in contrast to
[
HP18
]
, which characterizes exactness properties of the category of matroids.

Given a DSC
(E,\text{dep})
, its reachable dependency poset
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
will in general not be a distributive lattice. One can complete a lattice using the Bruns-Lakser completion (or injective envelope)
[
BL70
]
. When applied to the reachable dependency poset of a DSC, the Bruns-Lakser completion defines a Merkle tree, which gives a complete description of the execution traces of the DSC. We give an easily computed description of the Bruns-Lakser completion for finite lattices in Proposition
7.23
(implicit in the work of
[
GG13
]
) which is of independent interest.

Lastly, we axiomatize what it means for a package to be an updated version of another package in a DSC, characterizing it as an idempotent monad on
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
. We extend this to an idempotent monad on the Bruns-Lakser completion of
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
as well.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we define DSCs, the reachable dependency poset construction, morphisms of DSCs, and show that with these definitions,
\mathsf{rdp}
defines a contravariant functor from the category of DSCs to the category of finite join-semilattices. We also show that by restricting to a smaller class of morphisms,
\mathsf{rdp}
extends to a functor to the category of finite lattices. In Section
3
we prove that the category of DSCs and the category of antimatroids are isomorphic. From this we prove that a lattice is diamond-free semimodular if and only if it can be written as the reachable dependency poset of a DSC. In Section
4
, we study a subclass of DSCs where every element has a unique possible dependency set and show that it is isomorphic to the opposite of the category of finite posets. In section
5
we relate DSCs to event structures, which are a widely studied model of concurrent computation. While this section is independent of the rest of the paper, the connection was an initial starting point for this work, and suggests several avenues for future research. In Section
6
we study the exactness properties of the category of DSCs. In Section
7
, we study the Bruns-Lakser completion of reachable dependency posets, showing that they produce Merkle trees. In Section
8
we define version parametrization for DSCs. Finally in Section
9
we discuss future avenues of research.

Definition 2.4

Given a preDSC

for

. Thus

for every depset

. Since

is complete by assumption, there exists a depset

. But then

, since

. Continuing this argument,

implies there exists a

such that

, so

. Thus

, which is a contradiction.
∎

Now we will prove that reachability and completeness are equivalent conditions on a subset of a DSC. Let
E
be a DSC and

. If

, then we write

if for every depset

such that

,

. We say that

must depend on

within

, then

if and only if

.

Lemma 2.28
.

For any nonempty subset
X\subseteq E
,
\leftarrow_{X,m}
is a transitive relation. If
X
is a nonempty complete subset, then
\leftarrow_{X,m}
is also irreflexive.

Proof.

Suppose that
e\leftarrow_{X,m}e^{\prime}
and
e^{\prime}\leftarrow_{X,m}e^{\prime\prime}
. Now suppose that
D^{e^{\prime\prime}}
is a depset and
D^{e^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq X
. Then
e^{\prime}\in D^{e^{\prime\prime}}
, since
e^{\prime}\leftarrow_{X,m}e^{\prime\prime}
. But
D^{e^{\prime\prime}}
is complete, since
E
is a DSC, so there exists a depset
D^{e^{\prime}}
such that
D^{e^{\prime}}\subseteq D^{e^{\prime\prime}}
. But in that case
D^{e^{\prime}}\subseteq X
, so
e\in D^{e^{\prime}}
since
e\leftarrow_{X,m}e^{\prime}
, which implies that
e\in D^{e^{\prime\prime}}
. Thus
e\leftarrow_{X,m}e^{\prime\prime}
.

Now if
X
is a nonempty complete subset of
E
, and
e\leftarrow_{X,m}e
, then since
e\in X
, there exists a depset
D^{e}_{0}
such that
D^{e}_{0}\subseteq X
. But then
e\in D^{e}_{0}
, which is not possible since
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC. Thus
\leftarrow_{X,m}
is irreflexive.
∎

Lemma 2.29
.

Suppose
E
is a DSC, and
X\subseteq E
is a nonempty complete subset. Then there exists an
e\in X
such that
(X\setminus e)
is complete.

Proof.

Suppose not, then
(X\setminus e)
is not complete for any
e\in X
. Since
X
is nonempty, choose
e_{0}\in X
. Then
(X\setminus e_{0})
is not complete. Thus there exists some
e_{1}\in(X\setminus e_{0})
such that none of its depsets are contained in
(X\setminus e_{0})
. However since
X
is complete, and
e_{1}\in X
, there exists a depset
D^{e_{1}}\subseteq X
. Since
D^{e_{1}}\nsubseteq(X\setminus e_{0})
, this implies that
e_{0}\in D^{e_{1}}
. Furthermore, this is true for any depset of
e_{1}
that is a subset of
X
. Therefore
e_{1}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{0}
.

Now
(X\setminus e_{1})
is also not complete, so by the same argument there exists some
e_{2}
such that
e_{2}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{1}
. If
|X|=n
, and we repeat this process
n
times, we end up with a sequence

e_{n}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{n-1}\leftarrow_{X,m}\dots\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{1}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{0}

such that either there is a cycle, i.e.
e_{i}=e_{j}
for some
0\leq i<j\leq n
, or the above sequence enumerates all of
X
.

By Lemma
2.28
,
\leftarrow_{X,m}
is transitive, so if there is a cycle in the above sequence, we have
e_{k}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{k}
for some
0\leq k\leq n
. However, also by Lemma
2.28
,
\leftarrow_{X,m}
is irreflexive, so we have a contradiction.

Now suppose the above sequence enumerates all of the elements of
X
. Since
(X\setminus e_{n})
is not complete by assumption, by the same argument as above, there exists some
e_{k}
such that
e_{k}\leftarrow_{X,m}e_{n}
. Therefore one obtains a cycle. However, by the previous paragraph, this cannot happen, so we have a contradiction. Therefore, there must exist some
e\in X
such that
(X\setminus e)
is complete.
∎

Proposition 2.30
.

Let
E
be a DSC, then a subset
X\subseteq E
is reachable if and only if it is complete.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
This is Lemma
2.17
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose that
X
is a complete subset. If
X
is empty, then it is reachable, so assume that it is nonempty. By Lemma
2.29
, there exists an
e_{0}\in X
such that
(X\setminus e_{0})
is complete. Thus if
e\in(X\setminus e_{0})
, then there exists a depset
D^{e}\subseteq(X\setminus e_{0})
. Since
X
is complete and
e_{0}\in X
, there exists a depset
D^{e_{0}}\subseteq X
. Since
E
is a DSC,
e_{0}\notin D^{e_{0}}
, thus
D^{e_{0}}\subseteq(X\setminus e_{0})
, thus
(X\setminus e_{0})\,\mathsf{rch}\,X
. Since
X
is finite, say with cardinality
|X|=n
, we can repeat the above argument until we have exhausted all of
X
, obtaining a sequence

\varnothing\,\mathsf{rch}\,(X\setminus\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}\})\,\mathsf{rch}\,\dots\,\mathsf{rch}\,(X\setminus e_{0})\,\mathsf{rch}\,X.

Thus
X
is reachable.
∎

Remark 2.31
.

For the rest of the paper, we will not distinguish between reachable and complete subsets of a DSC, and will mostly refer to them as complete subsets.

Let us now analyze the structure of
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
for a DSC

more deeply.

Definition 2.32
.

Let
(E,\text{dep})
be a DSC,
e\in E
, and
X\subseteq E
. Say

is an
e
-minimal complete

is complete,

and if for every complete subset

. Let

Lemma 2.33
.

Suppose
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC,
e\in E
and
X\subseteq E
is a complete subset. If
e\in X
, then there exists an
e
-minimal complete subset
M_{e}\subseteq E
such that

.

Proposition 2.34
.

Given a DSC
(E,\text{dep})
and
e\in E
, a subset
X\subseteq E
is an
e
-minimal complete subset if and only if it is of the form
D^{e}\cup e
, with
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
First note that if
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
, then
D^{e}\cup e
is a complete subset. Now let us show that
D^{e}\cup e
is an
e
-minimal complete subset. Suppose that
X
is complete,
X\subseteq(D^{e}\cup e)
and
e\in X
. Then there exists a depset
D^{e}_{0}
of
e
such that
D^{e}_{0}\subseteq(X\setminus e)
, because
X
is complete, and depsets of
e
can’t contain
e
. This implies that
D^{e}_{0}\subseteq D^{e}
, but
(E,\text{dep})
is irredundant, which implies
D^{e}_{0}=D^{e}
, in which case
X=(D^{e}\cup e)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Now suppose that
X
is an
e
-minimal complete subset. Then
e\in X
, and since
X
is complete, there exists some depset
D^{e}\subseteq X
. Clearly
(D^{e}\cup e)\subseteq X
, and thus since
X
is an
e
-minimal complete subset,
(D^{e}\cup e)=X
.
∎

Definition 2.35
.

Given a join-semilattice
L
, an element
x\in L
is
join-irreducible
if
x\neq\bot
and whenever
a\vee b=x
then
a=x
or
b=x
. Let
\mathcal{J}(L)
denote the subposet of join irreducible elements.

Given a DSC
(E,\text{dep})
, we can characterize the join-irreducible elements of
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
.

Proposition 2.36
.

Given a DSC
(E,\text{dep})
, a nonempty and complete subset
X\subseteq E
is join-irreducible in
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
if and only if
X
is an
e
-minimal complete subset for some
e\in E
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose that
X
is an
e
-minimal complete subset and
X=A\cup B
, where
A,B
are complete. Since
e\in X
, then
e\in A
or
e\in B
or both. Without loss of generality suppose that
e\in A
. Then by definition of
e
-minimal complete subsets,
X=A
. Thus
X
is join-irreducible.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that
X
is a complete subset of
E
but not an
e
-minimal complete subset for any
e\in E
. We wish to show that
X
is not join-irreducible. If
X
is not
e
-minimal complete for any
e\in E
, then it is not
x
-minimal complete for any
x\in X
. Therefore, for every
x\in X
, by Lemma
2.33
there exists an
x
-minimal complete subset
M_{x}\subseteq X
. Therefore
X=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}M_{x}
, so
X
is a join of finitely many complete subsets. Thus
X
is not join-irreducible.
∎

Proposition 2.37
.

Let
(E,\text{dep})
be a DSC and suppose that
X\subseteq E
is join-irreducible in
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
. Then there exists a unique
e\in E
and
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
such that
E=(D^{e}\cup e)
.

Proof.

By Propositions
2.34
and
2.36
we know that
X=(D^{e}\cup e)
for some
e\in E
and
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
. Now suppose that
X=(D^{e^{\prime}}\cup e^{\prime})
for
e^{\prime}\in E
and
D^{e^{\prime}}\in\text{dep}(e^{\prime})
, where
e\neq e^{\prime}
. Since
E
is a DSC, both
D^{e}
and
D^{e^{\prime}}
are complete subsets of
E
, and furthermore
D^{e}\subseteq X
and
D^{e^{\prime}}\subseteq X
, so
D^{e}\cup D^{e^{\prime}}\subseteq X
. Since
(D^{e}\cup e)=(D^{e^{\prime}}\cup e^{\prime})
, it must be the case that
e\in D^{e^{\prime}}
and
e^{\prime}\in D^{e}
, so
D^{e}\cup D^{e^{\prime}}=X
. But
X
is join-irreducible, and union is the join in
\mathsf{rdp}
so
X=D^{e}
or
X=D^{e^{\prime}}
. But
X
contains both
e
and
e^{\prime}
, and since
(E,\text{dep})
is acyclic, there is a contradiction. Thus

. If
X=(D^{e}_{0}\cup e)=(D^{e}_{1}\cup e)
, then clearly
D^{e}_{0}=X\setminus e=D^{e}_{1}
.
∎

Now let us show that
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is a lattice. This is a general fact about finite join-semilattices. Indeed, if
P
is a finite join-semilattice with a bottom element and
X,Y\in P
, then their meet always exists and is given by

(6)

X\wedge Y=\bigveeop\displaylimits\left\{Z\in P\,:\,Z\leq X,Z\leq Y\right\}.

In the case of
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
, this means that the meet of two reachable subsets
X
and
Y
will be the union of all those reachable subsets that are a subset of
X\cap Y
.

Lemma 2.38
.

Let
(E,\text{dep})
be a DSC, and
A,B\subseteq E
complete subsets. Then

A\wedge B=\{x\in A\cap B\,:\,\exists D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)\,\text{ such that }D^{e}\subseteq A\cap B\}.

Proof.

Let
C=\{x\in A\cap B\,:\,\exists D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)\,\text{ such that }D^{e}\subseteq A\cap B\}.
Now
C\subseteq A\cap B
. We wish to show that
C
is complete. So suppose
x\in C
. Then there exists
D^{x}\in\text{dep}(x)
such that
D^{x}\subseteq A\cap B
. If
y\in D^{x}
, then since
D^{x}
is complete, there exists a
D^{y}\subseteq D^{x}
with
D^{y}\in\text{dep}(y)
. Thus
D^{y}\subseteq A\cap B
, so
y\in C
. Since
y
was arbitrary,
D^{x}\subseteq C
, so
C
is complete. Thus
C\subseteq A\wedge B
. We wish to prove the opposite inclusion. By (
6
) if
x\in A\wedge B
then
x
belongs to some set
X\subseteq A\cap B
that is complete, so there exists a
D^{x}\subseteq X
, thus
x\in C
. Thus
A\wedge B=C
.
∎

Definition 2.39
.

We say that a lattice
L
is
distributive
if for every
x,y,z\in L
, the following identity holds:

(7)

x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z).

Distributive lattices enjoy a key characterization theorem due to Birkhoff
[
Bir40
]
. Consider the following lattice, known as
M_{3}
or the
diamond lattice
, which has five elements,
\bot,a,b,c,\top
, and order structure presented by the Hasse diagram:

(8)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\top}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}

Note that this lattice is not distributive because
a\wedge(b\vee c)=a\wedge\top=a
, but
(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)=\bot\vee\bot=\bot
.

Now let
N_{5}
denote the following lattice, also known as the
pentagon lattice
, which also has five elements,
\bot,d,e,f,\top
with the following order structure:

(9)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\top}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}

Note that this lattice is also not distributive.

Definition 2.40
.

Given a lattice
L
, a subset
S\subseteq L
is called a
sublattice
if for all
x,y\in S
,
x\wedge y\in S
and
x\vee y\in S
.

Theorem 2.41

(
[
DP02
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

A lattice
L
is distributive if and only if there exists no sublattice of
L
that is isomorphic to
M_{3}
or
N_{5}
.

Many examples of lattices are distributive, for example if
S
is a set, then its lattice of subsets
P(S)
is distributive. However it is typically not the case that if
E
is a DSC, then
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
will be a distributive lattice.

Example 2.42
.

Notice that Example
2.24
is not a distributive lattice, as

b\vee(ab\wedge ac)=b\vee\varnothing=b

whereas

(b\vee ab)\wedge(b\vee ac)=ab\wedge abc=ab.

In other words
\{\varnothing,b,ab,ac,abc\}
is a sublattice of
a.b\vee c
that is isomorphic to
N_{5}
. In fact it contains two such sublattices, the other being
\{\varnothing,c,ac,ab,abc\}
.

We will see in Section
3
that while the lattices in the image of
\mathsf{rdp}
are not all distributive, they satisfy a weaker condition called diamond-free semimodularity, which means that they will not contain a copy of the diamond lattice
M_{3}
, but they could possibly contain the pentagon lattice
N_{5}
. Further, in Section
4
, we will show that there is a subclass of DSCs whose image under
\mathsf{rdp}
is precisely the set of distributive lattices.

Lemma 2.43
.

Given DSCs
(E,\text{dep})
and
(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
and a set function
f:E\to E^{\prime}
, then

is a morphism of DSCs if and only if for all

, and all

, there exists a

such that
D^{e}\subseteq f^{*}\left(D^{f(e)}\cup f(e)\right)
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Since we are only considering DSCs, by Proposition
2.30
a subset is reachable if and only if it is complete. Suppose that
A\subseteq E^{\prime}
is complete. We wish to show that
f^{*}(A)
is complete. Suppose
e\in f^{*}(A)
. Then
f(e)\in A
, but
A
is complete so there exists a depset
D^{f(e)}\subseteq A
. Since
f
is a morphism of DSCs, there exists a depset
D^{e}\subseteq f^{*}(D^{f(e)}\cup f(e))
. Since
(D^{f(e)}\cup f(e))\subseteq A
, this implies that
D^{e}\subseteq f^{*}(A)
, so
f^{*}(A)
is complete.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that
f^{*}
preserves complete subsets,
e\in E
and
D^{f(e)}\in\text{dep}(f(e))
. Then
D^{f(e)}\cup f(e)
is complete, so
f^{*}\left(D^{f(e)}\cup f(e)\right)
is complete and contains

, thus there exists some

such that

. Thus

is a morphism of DSCs.
∎

Remark 2.44

.

Lemma 2.46

(\Leftarrow)

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that

is complete and contains
f(e)
, thus there exists a depset
D^{f(e)}\subseteq f_{*}\left(D^{e}\cup e\right)
.
∎

Corollary 2.47
.

If
f
is a comorphism of preDSCs, then
f_{*}
descends to an order preserving morphism of posets
f_{*}:\mathsf{rdp}(E)\to\mathsf{rdp}(E^{\prime})
. Further, as joins are given by unions, this is a morphism of join-semilattices.

Now we wish to define a category
\mathsf{DSC}_{\text{bi}}
whose objects are DSCs, and with morphisms such that
\mathsf{rdp}
is a functor of type
\mathsf{rdp}:\mathsf{DSC}^{\text{op}}_{\text{bi}}\to\mathsf{FinLatt}
, where
\mathsf{FinLatt}
is the category whose objects are finite lattices and whose morphisms are order, join and meet-preserving functions. In other words, we wish to find a class of set functions
f
such that
\mathsf{rdp}(f)=f^{*}
preserves reachable subsets and is a lattice morphism, i.e. preserves joins and meets. We have already established join preservation, and now turn our attention to meets.

Lemma 2.48
.

If
f:E\to E^{\prime}
is a morphism and comorphism of DSCs, then
f^{*}:\mathsf{rdp}(E^{\prime})\to\mathsf{rdp}(E)
preserves meets.

Proof.

We want to show that
f^{*}(A\wedge B)=f^{*}(A)\wedge f^{*}(B)
.
Since
A\wedge B\subseteq A\cap B
, we have
f^{*}(A\wedge B)\subseteq f^{*}(A\cap B)=f^{*}(A)\cap f^{*}(B)
. But
f^{*}(A\wedge B)
is complete since
f
is a morphism of DSCs, and
f^{*}(A)\wedge f^{*}(B)
is the largest complete subset of
f^{*}(A)\cap f^{*}(B)
, so
f^{*}(A\wedge B)\subseteq f^{*}(A)\wedge f^{*}(B)
.

Let us show that
f^{*}(A\wedge B)
is the largest complete subset of
f^{*}(A\cap B)
, as this will imply the result. Suppose that
X\subseteq f^{*}(A\cap B)
is complete. Then
f_{*}(X)\subseteq f_{*}f^{*}(A\cap B)\subseteq A\cap B
. Since
f
is a comorphism,
f_{*}(X)
is complete, so
f_{*}(X)\subseteq A\wedge B
. Thus
X\subseteq f^{*}f_{*}(X)\subseteq f^{*}(A\wedge B)
. Thus
f^{*}(A\wedge B)
is the largest complete subset of
f^{*}(A\cap B)
.
∎

Definition 2.49
.

We say that
f:(E,\text{dep})\to(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
is a
bimorphism
of DSCs if it is both a morphism and comorphism of DSCs. Let
\mathsf{PreDSC}_{\text{bi}}
and
\mathsf{DSC}_{\text{bi}}
denote the categories of preDSCs and DSCs with bimorphisms respectively.

Corollary 2.50
.

Given a morphism of DSCs
f:(E,\text{dep})\to(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
, if
f
is further a bimorphism, then
\mathsf{rdp}(f):\mathsf{rdp}(E^{\prime})\to\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is a morphism of finite lattices. Furthermore, the rdp construction extends to a functor
\mathsf{rdp}:\mathsf{DSC}_{\text{bi}}^{\text{op}}\to\mathsf{FinLatt}
.

Remark 2.51
.

We have chosen morphisms of DSCs to be compatible with
f^{*}
rather than
f_{*}
for several reasons. One reason is that Lemma
2.48
demonstrates that
f^{*}
preserves meets when
f
is a bimorphism, while the same does not hold for
f_{*}
(this is used later in Section
7
). Further, this choice establishes a connection to notions of morphisms of greedoids and similar structures (including continuous functions on topological spaces), which are used in connection with antimatroids (see Remark
3.5
).

3.
Antimatroids and DSCs

In this section we will establish an isomorphism between the category of antimatroids and the category of DSCs. Further, we will use this to characterize the essential image of the functor
\mathsf{rdp}:\mathsf{DSC}^{\text{op}}\to\mathsf{FinJSLatt}
as finite diamond-free semimodular lattices.

3.1.
Antimatroids

Antimatroids are a mathematical construction notable for their frequent rediscovery, as chronicled in
[
Mon85
]
. Our work here is another instance of that rediscovery. First studied by Dilworth in 1940
[
Dil40
]
, these arise in characterizing greedy algorithms
[
BF90
]
, combinatorics of convex spaces
[
Czé14
]
, and artificial intelligence planning
[
Par03
]
, among other applications.

Definition 3.1
.

An
antimatroid
is a pair
(E,\mathcal{F})
, where
E
is a finite set and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq P(E)
is a nonempty family of subsets, called the
feasible subsets
of
E
, such that:

(A1)

if
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
, then
A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}
,

(A2)

if
S\neq\emptyset
and
S\in\mathcal{F}
, then there exists an element
x\in S
such that
(S\setminus x)\in\mathcal{F}
, and

(A3)

E=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A
.

Remark 3.2
.

Condition (A3) in Definition
3.1
is sometimes omitted in the literature, and antimatroids that satisfy (A3) are called shelling structures in
[
KL85
]
. In
[
Czé14
]
however they are just referred to as antimatroids. It makes little difference in the theory as one could always just consider the resulting antimatroid
(\bigcupop\displaylimits_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A,\mathcal{F})
.

Example 3.3
.

If
E
is a finite set, then the pair
(E,P(E))
is an antimatroid, which we call the
maximal antimatroid
of
E
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{F})
and
(E^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
be antimatroids and
f:E\to E^{\prime}
a set function such that if
A\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, then
f^{*}(A)\in\mathcal{F}
. We call
f
a
morphism of antimatroids
and denote it by
f:(E,\mathcal{F})\to(E^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. Let
\mathsf{AntiMat}
denote the category of antimatroids.

Remark 3.5
.

There are several notions of morphisms of antimatroids in the literature, with most sources not defining morphisms at all. The above definition agrees with a restriction of the definition of morphism of greedoids to antimatroids as given in
[
XS15
, Definition 4]
. Theorem
will justify this choice of morphism of antimatroids.

Definition 3.6
.

Given a poset
P
, a subset
A\subseteq P
is called a
downset
if for every
x,y\in P
such that
y\leq x
and
x\in A
, we have that
y\in A
. In particular, let
(\downarrow x)=\{y\in P\,:\,y\leq x\}
. Let
\mathcal{O}(P)
denote the set of downsets of
P
, equipped with the partial order of inclusion of subsets.

Remark 3.7
.

If
P
is a finite poset, then
\mathcal{O}(P)
is a finite distributive lattice
[
Sta11
, Section 3.4]
. In fact, every finite distributive lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of downsets of some (essentially unique) finite poset
[
Sta11
, Theorem 3.4.1]
.

Remark 3.8
.

The downsets construction extends to a functor
\mathcal{O}:\mathsf{FinPos}^{\text{op}}\to\mathsf{FinDLatt}
, by defining
\mathcal{O}(f):\mathcal{O}(P^{\prime})\to\mathcal{O}(P)
by
X\mapsto f^{*}(X)
, for a morphism
f:P\to P^{\prime}
of posets. Furthermore for every finite poset
P
, there exists a morphism
\downarrow_{P}:P\to\mathcal{O}(P)
that sends
x
to the downset
(\downarrow x)
.

Example 3.9
.

If
P
is a finite poset, then the pair
(P,\mathcal{O}(P))
is an antimatroid. We call antimatroids of this form
poset antimatroids
. These are a well-studied class of antimatroids, and are the unique such class whose feasible sets are closed under intersections
[
KL13
]
. We will consider the connection between certain kinds of DSCs and this class of antimatroids in Section
4
.

Lemma 3.10
.

The construction of poset antimatroids forms a functor
D:\mathsf{FinPos}\to\mathsf{AntiMat}
.

Proof.

If
f:P\to P^{\prime}
is an order preserving morphism of finite posets, then let us show that
D(f):(P,\mathcal{O}(P))\to(P^{\prime},\mathcal{O}(P^{\prime}))
is a morphism of antimatroids. If
V
is a downset of
P^{\prime}
, we wish to show that
f^{*}(V)
is a downset of
P
. Suppose that
x\in f^{*}(V)
and
y\in P
such that
y\leq x
. Then since
f
is order-preserving,
f(y)\leq f(x)
, and since
V
is a downset,
f(y)\in V
, thus
y\in f^{*}(V)
. Thus
f^{*}(V)
is a downset of
P
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

The functor
D:\mathsf{FinPos}\to\mathsf{AntiMat}
is fully faithful.

Proof.

It is easy to see that if
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathsf{FinPos}
, then the canonical function
D:\mathsf{FinPos}(P,P^{\prime})\to\mathsf{AntiMat}((P,\mathcal{O}(P)),(P^{\prime},\mathcal{O}(P^{\prime})))
is injective, since
D(f)
is just
f
on the underlying sets. We wish to show that it is also surjective. So suppose that
f:P\to P^{\prime}
is a function such that it pulls downsets of
P^{\prime}
back to downsets of
P
. We wish to show that it is also order preserving. Suppose that
y\leq x
in
P
. Then
y\in(\downarrow x)
, and
f^{*}(\downarrow f(x))
is a downset containing
x
, therefore
(\downarrow x)\subseteq f^{*}(\downarrow f(x))
. Thus
f(y)\in(\downarrow f(x))
, so
f(y)\leq f(x)
. Thus
D
is fully faithful.
∎

3.2.
Correspondence of Antimatroids and DSCs

In this section we prove one of the main results of this paper, Theorem
3.18
, which shows that the category of DSCs and the category of antimatroids are isomorphic.

Definition 3.12
.

Given a preDSC
(E,\text{dep})
, let
\Phi(E,\text{dep})=(E,\mathsf{rdp}(E))
denote the set
E
equipped with the family of reachable subsets
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
.

Proposition 3.13
.

If
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC, then
\Phi(E,\text{dep})=(E,\mathsf{rdp}(E))
is an antimatroid. Furthermore a subset
X\subseteq E
is complete in
(E,\text{dep})
if and only if
X
is feasible in
(E,\mathsf{rdp}(E))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.9
, complete subsets are closed under unions, proving condition (A1) of Definition
3.1
. Proposition
2.30
and Lemma
2.29
prove (A2). Since
E
is complete, this proves (A3).
∎

The construction of Proposition
3.13
extends to a functor
\Phi:\mathsf{DSC}\to\mathsf{AntiMat}
, acting as the identity on morphisms. Let us find an inverse to this functor.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{F})
be an antimatroid, and
e\in E
. We say that a feasible subset
X\subseteq E
is an
e
-minimal feasible
subset if
e\in X
and for all feasible subsets
Y
such that
e\in Y
and
Y\subseteq X
, it follows that
X=Y
. Let
\text{Min}_{\mathcal{F}}(e)
denote the set of
e
-minimal feasible sets.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
(E,\mathcal{F})
is an antimatroid and
X\in\mathcal{F}
is a feasible set. If
X
contains an element
e
, then there exists an
e
-minimal feasible set
M_{e}\subseteq X
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose that
(E,\mathcal{F})
is an antimatroid,
e\in E
, and
M_{e}
is an
e
-minimal feasible set. If
x\in(M_{e}\setminus e)
, then there exists an
x
-minimal feasible set
M_{x}\subseteq(M_{e}\setminus e)
.

Proof.

Since
x\in M_{e}
, by Lemma
3.15
there exists an
x
-minimal feasible set
M_{x}\subseteq M_{e}
. Suppose that
M_{e}
contains no feasible strict subsets that contain
x
, so that
M_{x}=M_{e}
. By (A2) there exists a
y\in M_{e}
such that
M_{e}\setminus y
is feasible. If
y\neq x
, then
(M_{e}\setminus y)\subset M_{x}
, so
(M_{e}\setminus y)
is a feasible set containing
x
, which is a contradiction. If
y=x
, then
(M_{e}\setminus x)
is feasible, but
M_{e}
is an
e
-minimal feasible set, so
(M_{e}\setminus x)=M_{e}
, which is also a contradiction. Thus
M_{x}\subset M_{e}
. If
M_{x}
contains
e
, then since
M_{e}
is an
e
-minimal feasible set,
M_{x}=M_{e}
, which is a contradiction. Thus
M_{x}\subseteq(M_{e}\setminus e)
.
∎

Now suppose
(E,\mathcal{F})
is an antimatroid. Consider the preDSC
(E,\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}})
, where
\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}}:E\to P(P(E))
is defined by
\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}}(e)=\{(M_{e}\setminus e)\,:\,M_{e}\in\text{Min}_{\mathcal{F}}(e)\}
. If
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC, then using Proposition
2.34
it is not hard to see that
(E,\text{dep})=\Psi\Phi(E,\text{dep})
.

Lemma 3.17
.

Given an antimatroid
(E,\mathcal{F})
, the corresponding preDSC
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})=(E,\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}})
is a DSC. Furthermore, a subset
X\subseteq E
is complete in
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})
if and only if
X
is a feasible set of
(E,\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

We check the axioms of Definition
2.19
. If
X,Y\in\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}}(e)
, and
X\subseteq Y
, then since
X\cup e
and
Y\cup e
are
e
-minimal feasible sets, we have that
X=Y
, so
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})
is irredundant. If
X\in\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}}(e)
, then
X
is of the form
(M_{e}\setminus e)
, therefore
e\notin X
, so
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})
is acyclic. By (A3), we know that every
e\in E
must be contained in some feasible set, and therefore must be contained in some
e
-minimal feasible set by Lemma
3.15
. Thus
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})
is nonsingular. If
x\in(M_{e}\setminus e)
, then by Lemma
3.16
, there exists an
x
-minimal feasible set
M_{x}\subseteq(M_{e}\setminus e)
. Thus
(M_{x}\setminus x)\subseteq(M_{e}\setminus e)
. So
(M_{e}\setminus e)
contains a depset of
x
. Thus
(M_{e}\setminus e)
is complete, which implies that
\Psi(E,\mathcal{F})
has complete depsets and therefore is a DSC.

Now if
X\in\mathcal{F}
is feasible, then by Lemma
3.15
,
X
is a complete subset in
(E,\text{dep}_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Conversely suppose that
X\subseteq E
is complete. Then for each
x\in X
, there exists a depset
(M_{x}\setminus x)\subseteq X
. Of course this means
M_{x}\subseteq X
. Thus
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}M_{x}\subseteq X
and
X\subseteq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}M_{x}
. Since unions of feasible sets are feasible,
X
is feasible.
∎

Thus by Lemma
3.17
,
\Psi
extends to a functor
\Psi:\mathsf{AntiMat}\to\mathsf{DSC}
, acting as the identity on morphisms. Furthermore if
(E,\mathcal{F})
is an antimatroid, then
(E,\mathcal{F})=\Psi\Phi(E,\mathcal{F})
. Thus we have proven the following result.

Theorem 3.18
.

The functors
\Phi
and
\Psi
above are inverse to each other, defining an isomorphism of categories

(10)

\mathsf{DSC}\cong\mathsf{AntiMat}.

3.3.
Semimodular Lattices

Here we review the definitions of modular and semimodular lattices and relate them to DSCs.

Given a lattice
L
and elements
a,b\in L
, consider the sublattice
[a,b]=\{x\in L\,:\,a\leq x\leq b\}
. Then there is an adjoint pair

a\vee(-):[a\wedge b,b]\rightleftarrows[a,a\vee b]:(-)\wedge b,

which is equivalent to saying that whenever
a\wedge b\leq x\leq b
, then
x\leq(a\vee x)\wedge b
and whenever
a\leq y\leq a\vee b
, then
a\vee(y\wedge b)\leq y
.

Definition 3.19
.

We say that a lattice
L
is
modular
if the above is an adjoint equivalence for all
a,b\in L
, i.e. if whenever
a\wedge b\leq x\leq b
,
x=(a\vee x)\wedge b
and whenever
a\leq y\leq a\vee b
, it follows that
a\vee(y\wedge b)=y
.

Now recall the pentagon lattice
N_{5}
from (
9
).

Theorem 3.20

(
[
DP02
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

A lattice
L
is modular if and only if it does not contain
N_{5}
as a sublattice.

Definition 3.21
.

A lattice
L
is said to be
upper semimodular
if whenever
a\wedge b\prec a
, then
b\prec a\vee b
, where
\prec
is the cover relation (Definition
2.22
. We say it is
lower semimodular
if whenever
b\prec a\vee b
then
a\wedge b\prec a
.

Remark 3.22
.

If we refer to a lattice
L
as
semimodular
, that means
L
is upper semimodular. Note that a lattice is modular if and only if it is upper semimodular and lower semimodular.

Definition 3.23
.

A lattice
L
is a
diamond-free semimodular lattice
(also known as a
join-distributive lattice
) if it is semimodular and does not contain the diamond lattice
M_{3}
as a sublattice.

Let
(E,\mathcal{F})
denote an antimatroid as in Definition
3.1
. Since
\mathcal{F}\subseteq P(E)
, it inherits a poset structure. Since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under unions,
\mathcal{F}
is actually a join-semilattice. Since it is finite and has a bottom element, one can also define meets as in (
6
). In fact we have the following characterization:

Proposition 3.24

(
[
Czé14
, Corollary 7.5.i]
, originally
[
Ede80
, 3.3]
)
.

If
(E,\mathcal{F})
is an antimatroid, then
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
is a diamond-free semimodular lattice.

In fact
[
Czé14
, Corollary 7.5]
proves that every diamond-free semimodular lattice is of the form
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
for some antimatroid
(E,\mathcal{F})
.

Corollary 3.25
.

If
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC, then
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is a finite diamond-free semimodular lattice. Conversely, every finite diamond-free semimodular lattice is of the form
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
for some DSC
(E,\text{dep})
.

In Section
4
we will pinpoint those DSCs
E
where
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is not only diamond-free semimodular but is also distributive.

4.
Dependency Structures with No Choice

In this section we turn to a special class of DSCs, namely those with a unique dependency set per event, i.e. those with no choice. We show that the essential image of
\mathsf{rdp}
when restricted to DSNCs is equivalent to the opposite of the category of finite distributive lattices.

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSC. We say that
(E,\text{dep})
is a
dependency structure with no choice
or DSNC if
\text{dep}(e)
is a singleton for every
e\in E
. We will abuse notation and write
\text{dep}(e)
to mean the unique dependency set it contains. Let
\mathsf{DSNC}
denote the full subcategory of
\mathsf{DSC}
whose objects are DSNCs.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(E,\text{dep})
be a DSNC, with
e,e^{\prime}\in E
. Then the following are equivalent

(1)

e^{\prime}\in\text{dep}(e)
,

(2)

e\leftarrow_{m}e^{\prime}
,

(3)

e\leftarrow e^{\prime}
.

Furthermore if
e\leftarrow e^{\prime}
and
e^{\prime}\leftarrow e^{\prime\prime}
, then
e\leftarrow e^{\prime\prime}
, i.e. the relation
\leftarrow
is transitive.

Given a DSNC
(E,\text{dep})
, let
\leftarrow^{r}
denote the reflexive closure of the relation
\leftarrow
. Then
\leftarrow^{r}
is still transitive, and it is also antisymmetric. Thus
\leftarrow^{r}
defines a partial order on
(E,\text{dep})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given a DSNC
(E,\text{dep})
, let
R(E)
denote the underlying set
E
equipped with the partial order
\leftarrow^{r}
. Then
R
extends to a functor
R:\mathsf{DSNC}\to\mathsf{FinPos}
.

Proof.

If
f:(E,\text{dep})\to(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
is a morphism of DSNCs, then let
f:R(E)\to R(E^{\prime})
denote the same underlying function as
f
. We wish to show that if
a,b\in R(E^{\prime})
and
a\leftarrow^{r}b
then
f(a)\leftarrow^{r}f(b)
. Since
f
is a morphism of DSNCS, this implies that
b\in f^{*}(\text{dep}(f(a))\cup f(a))
. If
b\in f^{*}(\text{dep}(f(a)))
, then
f(b)\in\text{dep}(f(a))
, so
f(b)\leftarrow^{r}f(a)
. If
b\in f^{*}(f(a))
, then
f(b)=f(a)
, so
f(b)\leftarrow^{r}f(a)
.
∎

In what follows, if
(E,\text{dep})
is a DSNC, then we will often denote the relation
\leftarrow^{r}
on
E
by
\leq
.

Example 4.4
.

A simple example of a DSNC is
a.b
, the DSC with
E=\{a,b\}
and
\text{dep}(a)=\{\{b\}\}
. Its corresponding poset is
R(E)=\{b\leq a\}
.

The functor
R
has a (strict) inverse
R^{-1}:\mathsf{FinPos}\to\mathsf{DSNC}
defined as follows. If
P
is a finite poset, then let
R^{-1}(P)
denote the DSNC with same underlying set as
P
and where
b\in\text{dep}(a)
if
b<a
. Morphisms of posets pullback downsets to downsets, thus by Lemma
4.6
, letting
R^{-1}
act on morphisms by the identity defines a morphism of DSNCs, thus
R^{-1}
defines a functor which is a two-sided inverse to
R
.

Proposition 4.5
.

The functors
R
and
R^{-1}
defined above form an isomorphism of categories

(11)

\mathsf{DSNC}\cong\mathsf{FinPos}.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
E
be a DSNC, then a subset
X\subseteq E
is complete if and only if
X
is a downset in
R(E)
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
x\in X
, and
y\in\text{dep}(x)
. Then
y\leq x
in
R(E)
by definition. But
X
is a downset, so
y\in X
. Thus
\text{dep}(x)\subseteq X
, so
X
is a complete subset.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that
X\subseteq E
is complete,
x\in X
and
y\leq x
in
R(E)
. Then either
y=x
or
y\in\text{dep}(x)
, in either case
y\in X
since
X
is complete. Thus
X
is a downset in
R(E)
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

The following diagram (strictly) commutes

(12)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{DSNC}^{\text{op}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{DSC}^{\text{op}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{FinPos}^{\text{op}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{FinDLatt}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{FinJSLatt}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{rdp}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{\text{op}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}}

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
, we know that objectwise the two functors are equal. Similarly if
f:(E,\text{dep})\to(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
is a morphism of DSNCs, then

6.
The Category of DSCs

In this section we study exactness properties of the category of DSCs. Let
U:\mathsf{DSC}\to\mathsf{FinSet}
denote the forgetful functor
U(E,\text{dep})=E
. Let us show that this functor has a left adjoint. Define the free functor
F:\mathsf{FinSet}\to\mathsf{DSC}
by

, where
\text{dep}_{S}:S\to P(P(S))
is defined by
\text{dep}_{S}(s)=\{\varnothing\}
. We call

the

is a set function, then
f
will be a morphism of DSCs

\varnothing\subseteq f^{*}(D^{f(s)}\cup s)
for all

. This proves the following result.

Lemma 6.1
.

The above functors define an adjunction
F:\mathsf{FinSet}\rightleftarrows\mathsf{DSC}:U
. Further, the forgetful functor
U:\mathsf{DSC}\to\mathsf{FinSet}
is faithful, making
\mathsf{DSC}
into a concrete category.

This result implies that

preserves whatever limits exist in
\mathsf{DSC}
. Thus if a limit of a diagram of DSCs exists, it must be a DSC whose underlying set is a limit of the underlying sets in the diagram.

for any DSC. Similarly there is a unique morphism
(E,\text{dep})\to F(*)=(*,\text{dep}_{*})
, where

denotes the singleton set.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

A morphism

of DSCs is an epimorphism if and only if

is a surjection of sets.

Consider two DSCs
(E,\text{dep}),(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
. We can take the coproduct of their underlying sets

, and consider the function

\text{dep}^{\prime}(e)&\text{if }e\in E^{\prime}.\end{cases}

It is not hard to show that

Lemma 6.7
.

Given two DSCs
(E,\text{dep}),(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
, the DSC

Proof.

It is obvious that the inclusion morphisms

and

. We wish to show that the induced set function

is a morphism of DSCs. Namely we need to show that for every

, and every

, there exists a

for some

This also proves that the forgetful functor

Let
(E,\text{dep})
and

Proposition 6.8
.

Given two DSCs
(E,\text{dep}),(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
, the DSC

and

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle^{-1}\left(D^{(e,e^{\prime})}\cup(e,e^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=[f^{-1}(D^{e})\cap g^{-1}(D^{e^{\prime}})]\cup[f^{-1}(D^{e})\cap g^{-1}(e^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\cup[f^{-1}(e)\cap g^{-1}(D^{e^{\prime}})]\cup[f^{-1}(e)\cap g^{-1}(e^{\prime})]

7.
The Bruns-Lakser Completion

In this section we will consider a construction known in the order theory literature as the
Bruns-Lakser Completion
or
Bruns-Lakser Injective Envelope
. It was first defined in
[
BL70
]
, and it defines an idempotent completion of meet-semilattices to distributive lattices in a way that preserves certain kinds of joins. Here we will describe this construction, apply it to the image of lattices under
\mathsf{rdp}
and show how the composition of Bruns-Lakser after
\mathsf{rdp}
provides an interesting interpretation of DSCs as a kind of data structure known in the computer science literature as a Merkle tree.

First we will recall the classical Bruns-Lakser completion. Later we will recast this construction as the Yoneda embedding of a meet-semilattice into its category of “thin sheaves,” as described in
[
Stu05
]
.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
L
be a meet-semilattice. We say that a subset
S\subseteq L
is a
distributive subset
if for every
x\in L
, the following joins exist and the following identity holds:

x\wedge\bigveeop\displaylimits S=\bigveeop\displaylimits(x\wedge S),

where
\bigveeop\displaylimits(x\wedge S)
is the join of the subset
\{x\wedge s\,:\,s\in S\}
. We call elements of the form
\bigveeop\displaylimits S
for a distributive subset a
distributive join
. We say a morphism
f:L\to L^{\prime}
of meet-semilattices preserves distributive joins if whenever
S
is a distributive subset of
L
, then
f_{*}(S)
is a distributive subset of
L^{\prime}
, and
f(\bigveeop\displaylimits S)=\bigveeop\displaylimits f_{*}(S)
. We call such a morphism
f
a
distributive morphism
of meet-semilattices.

Remark 7.2
.

In some papers such as
[
GG13
]
and
[
BL70
]
they refer to distributive subsets as admissible subsets. A distributive morphism of meet-semilattices is called a
\langle\wedge,a\vee\rangle
-morphism in
[
GG13
]
.

For our purposes, the condition requiring joins existing is vacuous, since
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
is a lattice for any DSC
E
. However, not all subsets of
\mathsf{rdp}(E)
will be distributive, and thus even though the Bruns-Lakser construction was originally defined for meet-semilattices, it will apply to our case as well.

A

Let

of

If

Proof.

If

For a meet-semilattice

8.
Version Parametrization

One motivation for the study of package dependency systems has been to address package version solving and constraint resolution. In fact, different versions of the same package are the prototypical example of dependency sets. Given a desired set of packages to install, picking a uniform set of versions for all their dependencies to enable their simultaneous installation is a nontrivial problem – in fact, it is NP-complete
[
Man+06
]
.

The difficulty of this problem is often not apparent to end-users of software package systems, because in many cases that work has already been done for them. The repository itself does not consist of all version of packages – but a single fixed version of each package, such that all are known to be mutually consistent and compatible. The issue presents itself to maintainers of such distribution repositories, but also, more broadly to developers who make use of software library repositories, and contribute their own packages to them as well.

Semi-formal versioning policies have been introduced as a means to aid package maintainers in constructing and managing correct version-bounds. The most prominent and notable of these is Semantic Versioning, (or semver)
[
Pre13
]
. Under semver, the first (major) component of a package version is taken to be for incompatible api changes, and the second (minor) for additions of functionality that do not break backwards compatibility. As semver is a cross-language specification, it does not provide detailed per-language rules on which changes specifically are taken to break backwards-compatibility. The Haskell language also produced its own roughly contemporaneous package versioning policy
[
Has16
]
. Because this policy is specific to a single language, it does attempt to give specific rules on what constitute “breaking” or“non-breaking” changes.

Here we put forward a possible mathematical formalization of update policies, by formalizing the semver notion of a minor or non-breaking update using the following notion of higher version relation on a DSC.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
(E,\text{dep})
be a DSC. We introduce a relation on
E
, written
e\blacktriangleleft e^{\prime}
if

(1)

for all
D^{e}\in\text{dep}(e)
, there exists a depset
D^{e^{\prime}}\subseteq D^{e}
, and

(2)

for all
x\in E
, if
D^{x}
is a depset such that
e\in D^{x}
, then
[(D^{x}\setminus e)\cup e^{\prime}]
is also a depset of
x
.

We call
\blacktriangleleft
the

. We say that
e^{\prime}
is a higher version of

.

Remark 8.2
.

One might interpret the above definition as follows. Condition (1) says loosely that higher versions of packages cannot “gain” new dependencies, and condition (2) says that if a package

can depend on a package

and
e\blacktriangleleft e^{\prime}
, then
x
can depend on
e^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It is clear that

is reflexive. Now suppose that

and

for some

, then

. Thus

.
∎

Now if

.

Note that this is precisely the same thing as an
idempotent monad
on
P
thought of as a category.

Corollary 8.6
.

Given a DSC
(E,\text{dep})
,
V
descends to a function
V:\mathsf{rdp}(E)\to\mathsf{rdp}(E)
, and is a closure operator.

Now we wish to describe how
V
descends to a closure operator on the Bruns-Lakser completion. If
V:L\to L
is a closure operator on a finite lattice, we want to show that it induces a closure operator
\mathbb{V}:\mathsf{BL}(L)\to\mathsf{BL}(L)
. If
X\in\mathsf{BL}(L)=\mathcal{OJ}(L)
, then by the proof of Proposition
7.23
,
X=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}\widehat{x}
. Thus define

\mathbb{V}(X)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}\widehat{V(x)}.

Proposition 8.7
.

The function
\mathbb{V}:\mathsf{BL}(L)\to\mathsf{BL}(L)
as defined above is a closure operator.

Proof.

Suppose that
X\subseteq Y
. Then clearly
\mathbb{V}(X)\subseteq\mathbb{V}(Y)
. Thus
\mathbb{V}
is order preserving.

We wish to show that
X\subseteq\mathbb{V}(X)
. Suppose that
x\in S
. Then
x\leq V(x)
, since
V
is a closure operator on
L
. Thus
\widehat{x}\subseteq\widehat{V(x)}
. Therefore
X\subseteq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{x\in X}\widehat{V(x)}=\mathbb{V}(X)
.

Now we wish to show that
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{V}(X))\subseteq\mathbb{V}(X)
. Suppose that
z\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{V}(X))
. Then
z\in\widehat{V(y)}
for some
y\in\mathbb{V}(X)
. But then
y\in\widehat{V(x)}
for some
x\in X
. This implies
y\leq V(x)
, so
V(y)\leq V(V(x))\leq V(x)
, since
V
is a closure operator. Thus
\widehat{V(y)}\subseteq\widehat{V(x)}
, and
z\in\widehat{V(x)}\subseteq\mathbb{V}(X)
, therefore
\mathbb{V}
defines a closure operator.
∎

Thanks to Proposition
8.7
, the closure operator

\mathbb{V}:\mathsf{BL}(\mathsf{rdp}(E))\to\mathsf{BL}(\mathsf{rdp}(E))
is now defined for any DSC
(E,\text{dep})
. We call this the
version monad
on
\mathsf{BL}(\mathsf{rdp}(E))
.

Frames correspond to complete Heyting algebras, which give in turn models of propositional intuitionistic logic
[
Vic89
,
MM92
]
. Furthermore, closure operators yield Kripke frames on these corresponding logics
[
Pal09
]
. Consequently, we can associate to any DSC with a choice of versioning relation a corresponding constructive logic with a “possibility”-like modal operator, and with terms in this logic corresponding to specifications of sets of packages. Our hope is that this “internal logic of event dependencies” (or related logics) can be a setting which is suited to formal reasoning about and manipulation of propositions regarding event dependencies.

9.
Conclusion

From a mathematical standpoint, this paper has made the following contributions. By introducing DSCs and Theorems
3.18
and
5.12
, we have provided a connection between the theory of general event structures and that of antimatroids, and shown that both can be used as models of package management systems. We have also proposed natural definitions of morphisms of antimatroids and dependency event structures, and explored properties of the resulting equivalent categories. Furthermore, we have given and shown applications of a simple characterization of the finite Bruns-Lakser completion.

From an applied standpoint, we have put this mathematics to work in providing a formal characterization of the dependency data of a package repository, as well as a formal characterization of “minor” versions of packages, and of package versioning policies. One place we hope this is of use is the field of “empirical software engineering” and study of “repository mining”. Work there has put significant effort into understanding the structure of software repositories, but at the cost of simplifying assumptions. For example,
[
Kik+17
]
creates dependency graphs with different versions entirely independent, and with each package pinned to a specific single choice of version for each of its dependencies. The work in this paper provides a mathematical structure that can accurately represent both multiple versions of packages with choices of dependencies and also the versioning relation between them.

We have also given a mathematical account of the logical and order-theoretic interpretation of Merkle trees, which sheds light on the reasons for their widespread utility. It is worth noting that the construction and characterization of Bruns-Lakser given in Proposition
7.23
in fact extends beyond finite lattices to all finite posets, as explored in
[
BP20
]
.

There remain some interesting outstanding questions regarding morphisms of DSCs. For instance, if
f:(E,\text{dep})\to(E^{\prime},\text{dep}^{\prime})
is a bimorphism of DSCs, then by Corollary
2.50
,
f^{*}
is a map of lattices. Can we characterize exactly the class of maps of DSCs such that
f^{*}
is a map of lattices? Given the connection between DSCs and event structures, it is worth exploring what a ”comorphism-first” view of the categorical structure of DSCs would look like. In other words, how do
\mathsf{DSC}
and
\mathsf{DSC}_{\text{co}}
differ, and how does using a covariant version of
\mathsf{rdp}
, namely having
f_{*}:\mathsf{rdp}(E)\to\mathsf{rdp}(E^{\prime})
compare with its contravariant version? Are there certain applications to package management systems where comorphisms are more appropriate?

There are a number of possible directions for future work. One is further development of a category whose internal logic can encode package dependency problems. Another is modeling updates to package repositories by means of morphisms in the category of DSCs. Still another is extending DSCs with a notion of conflict, so that they would more fully correspond to general event structures. Beyond that is development of insights into efficient dependency solvers – a topic of great practical interest. Finally, there is much we hope that can be extracted from the surprising connection between dependency systems and antimatroids. There are many high-powered tools available for the study of the latter, as well as their cousins, matroids, and the more general class of greedoids to which both belong, such as characteristic polynomials. It would be of interest to see what insights these tools can bring when transported to the world of dependency structures.
