Reflections of convex bodies and their sections
Jorge L. Arocha, Javier Bracho and Luis Montejano

Abstract
. The purpose of this paper is to study the reflections of a convex body. In particular, we are interested in orthogonal reflections of its sections that can be extended to reflections of the whole body. For this reason, we need to study the case of a non-spherical ellipsoid, where a surprising structure arises (Section 2). These results allow us to give a new characterization of ellipsoids in terms of their reflections and, on the other hand, to prove a result deeply related to a conjecture due to K. Bezdek.



1.
Introduction

Every nonempty planar section of a 3-dimensional ellipsoid is an ellipse. Hence, it admits at least two orthogonal reflections because the axes of the ellipse are reflection lines.
Likewise, every nonempty planar section of a 3-dimensional body of revolution admits a reflection line (which is coplanar with the axis of revolution). Karoly Bezdek conjectured in
[
2
]
that if every nonempty planar section of a
3
-dimensional convex body admits an orthogonal reflection then it must be either an ellipsoid or a body of revolution.
Some work has been done in the direction of, or inspired by, this conjecture:

Given a convex body
K
, let us define a
Bezdek
plane (for
K
, it is understood) to be a plane whose section with
K
admits an orthogonal reflection; observe that non-intersecting planes are Bezdek planes.
We know that if
K
is a convex body for which every plane is a Bezdek plane (the hypothesis of the conjecture) then,
through every interior point of
K
there is a section which is a disk,
[
5
]
. Moreover, if every plane is a Bezdek plane with a reflection line coplanar to a fixed line, then

K
is a body of revolution,
[
3
]
. For more related problems see
[
6
]
.

Here we prove a weaker form of the conjecture. It leads to the proof of a similar statement in higher dimensions. First, we need to establish some terminology.

A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
ellipsoid
if it is the image of a ball under an affine automorphism. We say that a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-
rotational body
if there is a
(n-2)
-dimensional flat
L
, called the
axis of rotation
of
K
, with the property that for every plane
P
orthogonal to
L
:
P\cap K
is either empty, a single point or a disk centered at
L\cap P
. Clearly, in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-rotational body is a body of revolution.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\Gamma
be a hyperplane. Generalizing directly, we say that
\Gamma
is a
Bezdek
hyperplane if
\Gamma\cap K
admits an orthogonal reflection. If, in addition, this orthogonal reflection in
\Gamma
extends to a reflection

\bf{r}
of
K
we say that
\Gamma
is
strong-Bezdek
and we call
\bf{r}
the
strong reflection
associated to
\Gamma
. Observe that in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, every plane is strong-Bezdek for an ellipsoid or a body of revolution.

Our main purpose is to prove the following theorem which, in dimension
3
, yields a weaker version of Bezdek’s Conjecture. The other variation is on the set of planes considered.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K
be a
n
-dimensional convex body.
If there exists an open subset of the Grasmannian space whose elements are strong-Bezdek hyperplanes, then
K
is either an ellipsoid or a
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-rotational body.

Given a reflection
\mathbf{r}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we must distinguish the following associated elements: the
mirror
is the hyperplane which is pointwise fixed, and the
direction
which is a line through the origin all of whose parallel lines are rigidly inverted at their intersection with the mirror.
The direction line is complementary to the mirror hyperplane. And if the direction is orthogonal to the mirror, the reflection is said to be
orthogonal
.

We say that
\Gamma
is a
ground
hyperplane for the reflection
\mathbf{r}
if
\Gamma
contains the direction of
\mathbf{r}
(which implies that
\Gamma
is invariant under
\mathbf{r}
) and
\mathbf{r}
restricted to
\Gamma
is an orthogonal reflection in
\Gamma
.
Observe that if
\Gamma
is a ground hyperplane of a reflection, so is any parallel hyperplane, so that a ground hyperplane may be assumed to pass through the origin.

Let
\mathbf{r}
be a reflection with direction
\ell
and mirror
M
. If
\ell
is not orthogonal to
M
then the hyperplane
\Gamma
generated by
M\cap\ell^{\perp}
and
\ell
is the unique ground plane for
\mathbf{r}
. On the other hand, if
\mathbf{r}
is an orthogonal reflection then all the hyperplanes through
\ell
are ground hyperplanes for
\mathbf{r}
.

Now, we can rephrase the definition of strong-Bezdek hyperplanes. First, a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has to be explicit in the context. Then, a hyperplane
\Gamma
is strong-Bezdek if it is a ground hyperplane of a
reflection of

K
(i.e., that leaves
K
invariant). If this is the case, all the parallel hyperplanes to
\Gamma
are also strong-Bezdek because they are ground hyperplanes of the same strong reflection associated to
\Gamma
. So the notion of strong-Bezdek is defined up to parallelism. However, observe that even if all the parallel hyperplanes to
\Gamma
are Bezdek, for
\Gamma
to be strong-Bezdek one still needs that they have mirrors that make them Bezdek and align inside a hyperplane.

For the proof of Theorem
1
, we need to understand the question of how many reflections of a
n
-dimensional body
K
are enough to prove that
K
is an ellipsoid? A possible answer to this question is given in Theorem 2 of the next section.

2.
Characterizing ellipsoids by means of reflections

The main result of this section is the following characterization of ellipsoids, which is critical for our proof of Theorem 1. To state it simply, let us define a subset of a topological space to be
thick
if it contains a nonempty open set; that is, if it has non-empty interior. Let
G_{1,n}
and
G_{n-1,n}
be the Grassmannian of lines and hyperplanes, respectively, through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 2
.

A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an ellipsoid if one of the following holds:

i)

the lines
\ell
whose midpoints of all chords of
K
parallel to
\ell
lie in a hyperplane, are thick in
G_{1,n}
.

ii)

the lines
\ell
such that there is a reflection of
K
with direction
\ell
, are thick in
G_{1,n}
.

iii)

the ground hyperplanes of non orthogonal reflections of
K
, are thick in
G_{n-1,n}
.

First we need to study ellipsoids with special attention to the ground planes of their non orthogonal reflections. Then we use that knowledge to prove Theorem
2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an ellipsoid; we may assume it is centered at the origin.
Recall that
G_{1,n}=G_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the Grassmannian of lines through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Any line
\ell\in G_{1,n}
is the direction of a unique reflection
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
of
\mathcal{E}
. Indeed, the midpoints of all non-trivial chords in
\mathcal{E}
parallel to
\ell
generate its
mirror

M(\ell)=M_{\mathcal{E}}(\ell)
, which is also the parallel at the origin of the tangent hyperplanes to
\mathcal{E}
at the endpoints of the
chord

\ell\cap\mathcal{E}
. So that
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
is an orthogonal reflection if and only if
\ell
is a
binormal
line to
\mathcal{E}
, that is, a line
\ell
whose tangent hyperplanes at the endpoints of
\ell\cap\mathcal{E}
are orthogonal to
\ell
; and this happens if and only if
M(\ell)
is the orthogonal hyperplane to
\ell
. These lines are also called
axes
of the ellipsoid.

We will use that the bijective assignment of mirror hyperplanes to lines given by the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
reverses inclusion; that is, given lines
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
in
G_{1,n}
, then

(1)

\ell^{\prime}\subset M(\ell)\quad\iff\quad\ell\subset M(\ell^{\prime})\,.

To see this, consider a quadratic form that defines
\mathcal{E}
. It has an associated symmetric bilinear form. What determines if a vector lies in the mirror of the line generated by another vector, is that the symmetric bilinear form vanishes on that pair. Then, the assertion (
1
) easily follows.

Let
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}\subset G_{1,n}
be the set of binormal lines of
\mathcal{E}
, and denote its complement by

(2)

\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}=G_{1,n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}\,;

it is the set of directions of non orthogonal reflections of
\mathcal{E}
; we refer to the elements of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
as
diagonal
lines of
\mathcal{E}
. Assume henceforth that
\mathcal{E}
is not a sphere so that
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
is an open dense subset of
G_{1,n}
, instead of being empty.

Denote by
G_{n-1,n}
, the Grasmannian space of hyperplanes through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Our main goal is to understand the
ground map

\gamma:\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}\to G_{n-1,n}

which sends
\ell\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
to the unique ground hyperplane
\gamma(\ell)=\Gamma_{\ell}\in G_{n-1,n}
of the reflection
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
of
\mathcal{E}
in the direction
\ell
. The binormals
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
had to be removed from
G_{1,n}
to have the ground map well defined as a function.

Note that
\gamma(\ell)
varies continuously with
\ell
because we can write

\gamma(\ell)=(M(\ell)\cap\ell^{\perp})\oplus\ell\,,

where “
\,{}^{\perp}
” denotes the orthogonal complement and “
\oplus
” the sum of complementary subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
And note also that
\ell\subset\gamma(\ell)
is an axis of the ellipsoid

\gamma(\ell)\cap\mathcal{E}
, because the reflection
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
restricted to the hyperplane
\gamma(\ell)
is an orthogonal reflection.

Indeed, given a diagonal line
\ell\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
,
\gamma(\ell)
is the unique hyperplane
\Gamma
containing
\ell
for which
\ell
is a binormal of
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
. To see this, observe that for any
\ell\in G_{1,n}
and
\Gamma\in G_{n-1,n}
such that
\ell\subset\Gamma
, the mirror of
\ell
with respect to the ellipsoid
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
is the intersection of
\Gamma
with its mirror with respect to
\mathcal{E}
, which we can write

M_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}}(\ell)=\Gamma\cap M(\ell)\,.

So that a line which is an axis of two distinct hyperplane sections of
\mathcal{E}
has to be a binormal line of
\mathcal{E}
, and by definition it is not a diagonal.

We can summarize these last remarks with the following:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Gamma\in G_{n-1,n}
, then
\gamma^{-1}(\Gamma)
consists of the axes of the ellipsoid
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
which are not axes of
\mathcal{E}
. ∎

Theorem 3
.

There are open dense sets
\mathbb{G}\subset\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\mathbb{T}\subset G_{n-1,n}

such that the ground map of
\mathcal{E}
restricts to a covering map
\gamma:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Given a line
\ell\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let us denote by
\lambda(\ell)
the
length
of
\ell
, that is,
the length of the segment
\ell\cap\mathcal{E}
. Let

\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbb{B}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathbb{B}_{k}

be the partition of the binormal lines of
\mathcal{E}
given by their
lengths
; that is, two binormals of
\mathcal{E}
, belong to the same class
\mathbb{B}_{i}
if and only if they have the same length
\lambda_{i}
.
Denote by
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the subspace generated by the lines in
\mathbb{B}_{i}\subset G_{1,n}
; so that
\mathbb{B}_{i}=G_{1}(V_{i})
. We then have that

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{k}

is the orthogonal partition of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the eigenspaces of the symmetric matrix associated to a quadratic form that defines
\mathcal{E}
; so, we may refer to the
V_{i}
as the
eigenspaces
of
\mathcal{E}
. Observe that the partitions are non-trivial (
k\geq 2
) because we are assuming that
\mathcal{E}
is not a sphere.

Let us define the
generic
diagonals

(3)

\mathbb{G}=\{\,\ell\in G_{1,n}\,\mid\,\ell\not\subset V_{i}^{\perp}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\lambda(\ell)\neq\lambda_{i}\,,i=1,\dots,k\,\}\,.

Observe that
\mathbb{G}\subset\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
because if
\ell\not\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
then
\ell\subset V_{j}
for some
j
and
V_{j}\subset V_{i}^{\perp}
for all
i\neq j
. So that the ground map
\gamma
is well defined on
\mathbb{G}
.

Define the
generic
hyperplanes to be

(4)

\mathbb{T}=\{\,\Gamma\in G_{n-1,n}\,\mid\,V_{i}\not\subset\Gamma,i=1,\dots,k\,\}\,.

First note that
\mathbb{G}\subset G_{1,n}
and
\mathbb{T}\subset G_{n-1,n}
are open dense subsets. We will prove that the ground map restricted to
\mathbb{G}
is a covering map of
k-1
sheets over
\mathbb{T}
, and in particular that

\gamma(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{T}.

To see that
\gamma(\mathbb{G})\subset\mathbb{T}

consider
\ell\in\mathbb{G}
, and suppose
\Gamma=\gamma(\ell)\notin\mathbb{T}
. This implies that
V_{i}\subset\Gamma
for some
i
.
Since any two binormals of an ellipsoid having different lengths are orthogonal and both
\ell
and the lines of
V_{i}
are binormals of the ellipsoid
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
, then
\lambda(\ell)\neq\lambda_{i}
implies that
\ell\subset V_{i}^{\perp}\cap\Gamma\subset V_{i}^{\perp}
, which contradicts
\ell\in\mathbb{G}
.

Now consider a fixed hyperplane
\Gamma\in\mathbb{T}
. For each
i=1,\dots,k
, since
V_{i}\not\subset\Gamma
, we can choose a line
e_{i}\subset V_{i}
which is not in
\Gamma
: if
V_{i}
is a line, then
e_{i}=V_{i}
and if
V_{i}
is more than a line we choose
e_{i}\subset V_{i}
to be the orthogonal line to
\Gamma\cap V_{i}
. Let us assume that
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
correspond to the first coordinate axes of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that we can refer to the subspace they generate as
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, but observe, for later on, that it really depends continuously on
\Gamma
. By construction, the binormal lines of
\mathcal{E}
inside
\mathbb{R}^{k}
are exactly the coordinate axis
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
and they all have different lengths. We will now prove that in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
there are exactly
k-1
generic lines of different lengths which are the axes of
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
. This implies, by Lemma
1
, that

\#\gamma^{-1}(\Gamma)=k-1
because generic lines are not binormal to
\mathcal{E}
.

Let
\ell\subset\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a binormal line to
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
, that is, such that
M_{\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}}(\ell)=M(\ell)\cap\Gamma
is orthogonal to
\ell
. To prove that
\ell
is generic, we first see that
\ell\not\subset V_{i}^{\perp}
. For this, it suffices to prove that
\ell\not\subset e_{i}^{\perp}
because
V_{i}^{\perp}\subset e_{i}^{\perp}
. So suppose
\ell\subset e_{i}^{\perp}
. Since
e_{i}^{\perp}=M(e_{i})
(
e_{i}
is binormal to
\mathcal{E}
) then, (
1
) implies that
e_{i}\subset M(\ell)
. On the other hand,
e_{i}\not\subset\Gamma
yields by simple dimension arguments that

M(\ell)=(M(\ell)\cap\Gamma)\oplus e_{i}\,.

So that
M(\ell)
is orthogonal to
\ell
because both summands are orthogonal to
\ell
.
Then
\ell
is binormal to
\mathcal{E}
, but being inside
e_{i}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\ell
has to be
e_{j}
for some
j\neq i
, which contradicts that
e_{j}\not\subset\Gamma
. So, we have that
\ell\not\subset V_{i}^{\perp}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.

We are left to prove
\lambda(\ell)\neq\lambda_{i}
to conclude that
\ell\in\mathbb{G}
. Suppose not; that is, assume
\lambda(\ell)=\lambda_{i}
. Consider the plane
P=\ell\oplus e_{i}
which intersects
\Gamma
in
\ell
. The ellipse
P\cap\mathcal{E}
has
e_{i}
as an axis (because it is an axis of
\mathcal{E}
). Then if another chord through its center has the same length, it has to be a circle; because the length function of an ellipse reaches its unique extremes at the axes unless it is constant. This implies that the orthogonal line to
\ell
in the plane
P
,
\ell^{\prime}=P\cap\ell^{\perp}
, is contained in
M(\ell)
but it is not in
\Gamma
, so that
M(\ell)=(M(\ell)\cap\Gamma)\oplus\ell^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
\ell
. Then,
\ell
is a binormal of
\mathcal{E}
, which implies, because of its length, that
\ell\subset V_{i}
; but this contradicts that
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

So, we have proved that any axis of
(\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E})\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a generic line.

Now consider two different lines
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
in
\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
which are binormal to
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
. Suppose they have the same length
\lambda
. Let
P=\ell\oplus\ell^{\prime}\subset\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the plane generated by them. All of the lines in
G_{1}(P)
are binormal to
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
and have length
\lambda
. This implies, in particular, that
\ell^{\prime\prime}=P\cap e_{1}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a binormal line of
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
. But
\ell^{\prime\prime}
is not generic (it is contained in
V_{1}^{\perp}
), contradicting the conclusion of the previous paragraph. And therefore,
\lambda(\ell)\neq\lambda(\ell^{\prime})
.

Summarizing, given
\Gamma\in\mathbb{T}
, since
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}
is an ellipsoid of dimension
k-1
, it has
k-1
mutually orthogonal axes. We have proved that they are generic lines with different lengths, so that, by Lemma
1
,
\gamma^{-1}(\Gamma)
consists of exactly
k-1
isolated points in
\mathbb{G}
because all the other binormal lines to
\Gamma\cap\mathcal{E}
are also binormal lines to
\mathcal{E}
.

Finally note that in a small neighborhood of
\Gamma
the order of the lengths of the axes of the corresponding sections of
\mathcal{E}
is well determined, which implies not only that
\#\gamma^{-1}(\Gamma)=k-1
but also that
\gamma:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{T}
is a
(k-1)
-covering space, as we wished.
∎

Now, we can prove Theorem
2
. It will ease reading to restate it:

Theorem 2
.

A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an ellipsoid if one of the following holds:

i)

the lines
\ell
whose midpoints of all chords of
K
parallel to
\ell
lie in a hyperplane, are thick in
G_{1,n}
.

ii)

the lines
\ell
such that there is a reflection of
K
with direction
\ell
, are thick in
G_{1,n}
.

iii)

the ground hyperplanes of non orthogonal reflections of
K
, are thick in
G_{n-1,n}
.

Proof.

With hypothesis (i) this theorem is a generalization of Brunn’s characterization of ellipsoids (see Theorem 2.12.1 of
[
4
]
and
[
1
]
), in the sense that only thickness of such lines is asked instead of being all of them.

First observe that hypothesis (i) and (ii) are equivalent because there is a reflection of
K
in the direction
\ell
if and only if the midpoints of all the chords of
K
parallel to
\ell
lie in a hyperplane (the mirror). So, it is enough to consider (ii).

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the unique ellipsoid of minimal volume containing
K
, see e.g.
[
1
]
. Observe that any affine symmetry of
K
is also an affine symmetry of
\mathcal{E}
. Indeed, if
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an affine map such that
K=g(K)
, then
g(\mathcal{E})
is an ellipsoid that contains
K
, but since
g
preserves volume (
K
is a body), the uniqueness of
\mathcal{E}
implies that
\mathcal{E}=g(\mathcal{E})
.

Let
\Lambda_{K}\subset G_{1,n}
be the set of lines which are the direction of a reflection of
K
. As in the beginning of this section, let
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
be the reflection of
\mathcal{E}
with direction
\ell
, so that
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
is well defined for all
\ell\in G_{1,n}
. But observe that only for
\ell\in\Lambda_{K}
we have that
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
is also a reflection of
K
(that is,
\ell\in\Lambda_{k}\iff\mathbf{r}_{\ell}(K)=K
).

Suppose (ii) is true, that is, assume
\Lambda_{K}
is thick. We will prove
K=\mathcal{E}
.

To see that
K=\mathcal{E}
, it is enough to prove that
K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
is open in
\partial\mathcal{E}
. Indeed, since
K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}=\partial K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
is non-empty and closed in
\partial\mathcal{E}
, if it is also open it has to be all of
\partial\mathcal{E}
, which implies that
K=\mathcal{E}
because both sets are convex.

Consider a point
p\in K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
, and let
T_{p}
be the tangent plane to
\mathcal{E}
at
p
(which is also a support plane to
K
at
p
). Denote by
W_{p}\subset G_{1,n}
the set of lines not parallel to the hyperplane
T_{p}
; it is an open dense set of
G_{1,n}
homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, also known as an
affine chart
of
G_{1,n}
.
For any line
\ell\in W_{p}
, we have that
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}(p)\neq p
because the parallel to
\ell
through
p
, which we will denote
\ell(p)
, intersects
\partial\mathcal{E}
in another point which is
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}(p)
; that is,
\ell(p)\cap\partial\mathcal{E}=\{p,\mathbf{r}_{\ell}(p)\}
. Since
\Lambda_{K}
is thick in
G_{1,n}
, there clearly exists an open set
V\subset\Lambda_{K}\cap W_{p}
. Consider the set

V_{p}=\{\,\mathbf{r}_{\ell}(p)\,|\,\ell\in V\,\}\,.

It is contained in
K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
because
p\in K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
and
V\subset\Lambda_{K}
. And it is homeomorphic to
V
because it can be identified with its image under the
stereographic projection from
p
that sends a line
\ell\in W_{p}
to the point different from
p
in
\ell(p)\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
, and which is a homeomorphism between
W_{p}
and
\partial\mathcal{E}\setminus\{p\}
. So that
V_{p}
is an open set in
\partial\mathcal{E}
. Finally, fix any line
\ell_{0}\in V\subset\Lambda_{K}
. Since
\mathbf{r}_{\ell_{0}}
restricts to a homeomorphism of
K
and of
\partial\mathcal{E}
, we have that
\mathbf{r}_{\ell_{0}}(V_{p})\subset K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
is an open neighborhood of
p
in
\partial\mathcal{E}
and therefore
K\cap\partial\mathcal{E}
is open in
\partial\mathcal{E}
: which proves
K=\mathcal{E}
.

Next, assume (iii) and we will prove
K=\mathcal{E}
showing that (ii) holds. Let
\Omega_{K}\subset G_{n-1,n}
be the set of hyperplanes that are ground hyperplanes of non orthogonal reflections of
K
(hypothesis (iii) is that
\Omega_{K}
is thick). That it is non-empty, implies that
\mathcal{E}
is not a sphere and so Theorem
3
with its notation applies. Since
\Omega_{K}
is thick, there exists a set
U\subset\Omega_{K}\cap\mathbb{T}
which is open in
G_{n-1,n}
and evenly covered by the ground map
\gamma
, so that

\gamma^{-1}(U)=U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k-1}

with each
U_{i}
homeomorphic to
U
via
\gamma
. Consider
\Gamma\in U
, the
k-1
reflexions
\mathbf{r}_{\ell}
with
\ell\in\gamma^{-1}(\Gamma)
are all the non orthogonal reflexions of
\mathcal{E}
with ground hyperplane
\Gamma
. At least one of them is a reflexion of
K
because
\Gamma\in\Omega_{K}
so that at least one of the lines in

3.
Proof of the main theorem

For the proof of Theorem 1, we need first the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a convex body which is not
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-rotational. Then the set of orthogonal reflections of
K
is finite

Proof.

Let
G
be the group of isometries of
K
. We may assume that
G\subset O_{n}
. Note that
K
is an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-rotational body if and only if the group
G
contains a copy of the group
O_{2}
of isometries of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore,
G
does not contain a copy of the group
SO_{2}\subset O_{2}
and, since it is a compact subgroup of
O_{n}
, it is a finite group. This implies that the set of orthogonal reflections of
K
is finite.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1
.
Recall that a hyperplane
\Gamma\in G_{n-1,n}
is strong-Bezdek if it is the ground plane of a reflection of
K
, and by assumption there is an open subset
U\subset G_{n-1,n}
of such hyperplanes.

Suppose
K
is not a
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-rotational body. By Lemma
2
, the set of orthogonal reflections of
K
is finite. For each of them, the set of ground hyperplanes is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n-2}\cong G_{n-2,n-1}\subset G_{n-1,n}
(the hyperplanes that contain the direction of the reflection). So that the set of ground hyperplanes of orthogonal reflections of
K
is, at most, a finite collection of flats
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n-2}
contained in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\cong G_{n-1,n}
. In its complement, there clearly is an open set
U^{\prime}\subset U
of ground hyperplanes of non orthogonal reflections of
K
. Therefore, by item (iii) of Theorem
2
,
K
is an ellipsoid. ∎
