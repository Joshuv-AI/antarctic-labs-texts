Automated Robot Recovery from Assumption Violations of High-Level Specifications
Qian Meng, Hadas Kress-Gazit

Abstract
This paper presents a framework that enables robots to automatically recover from assumption violations of high-level specifications during task execution. In contrast to previous methods relying on user intervention to impose additional assumptions for failure recovery, our approach leverages synthesis-based repair to suggest new robot skills that, when implemented, repair the task. Our approach detects violations of environment safety assumptions during the task execution, relaxes the assumptions to admit observed environment behaviors, and acquires new robot skills for task completion. We demonstrate our approach with a Hello Robot Stretch in a factory-like scenario.

Introduction

Formal synthesis – the process of transforming a high-level specification into a correct-by-construction system – is increasingly being used to create provably-correct robot behaviors that fulfill complex, high-level tasks
[
1
]
.
A common approach to synthesis first creates a discrete abstraction of the environment and the robot skills (or actions)
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
,
then generates a temporal logic
[
6
]
specification from the abstraction and the user-provided tasks.
Synthesis algorithms (e.g.
[
7
]
) transform the specification into a symbolic strategy that guides the composition of the robot skills to satisfy the high-level task.
If the synthesis fails, meaning the task cannot be guaranteed, researchers have leveraged the feedback from synthesis to modify the abstraction and the specification to make the specification realizable
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Synthesis for
Generalized Reactivity(1) (GR(1))
specifications has been used for synthesizing robot control from high-level specifications due to its relatively-low quadratic time complexity in the size of its state space
[
7
]
.

GR(1)
specifications are a fragment of
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)

[
6
]
and are structured as an implication from

assumptions
on the environment behaviors to
guarantees
on the system behaviors, as described in
Section
II-C
.
The assumptions and guarantees contain
safety constraints
, which specify requirements that should always hold, and
liveness constraints
, which specify requirements that should hold repeatedly.
Because of the implication, the synthesized strategy is only required to satisfy the system guarantees if the environment behaviors satisfy the assumptions.

While
GR(1)
synthesis can produce provably-correct robot strategies, the high-level specification includes assumptions about the behavior of the environment,
such as the location of obstacles or the state of objects;
if, at runtime, these assumptions are violated, the behavior of the robot is no longer guaranteed to satisfy the task.
In this work we address the problem of
autonomously repairing such violations
, if possible, to ensure the robot satisfies its intended task.

Contributions.

To our knowledge, this is the first framework to allow a robot to autonomously recover from assumption violations of temporal logic specifications during task execution.
This work closes the loop of executing correct-by-construction robot strategies in uncertain environments, for which the initial environment assumptions may be inaccurate, by gradually adjusting the assumptions to meet the actual environment behaviors
and acquiring the required robot skills to satisfy the tasks.
This work will enable formal synthesis to create autonomous robotic systems that are more applicable to complex, real-world environments.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Linear Temporal Logic

We use a fragment of
LTL
to capture specifications.
Let
\mathcal{V}
be a set of atomic propositions.
We define the syntax of
LTL
recursively using the following grammar:

\varphi\coloneqq\pi~|~\neg\varphi~|~\varphi\vee\varphi~|~\bigcirc\varphi~|~\varphi\ \mathcal{U}\ \varphi

where
\pi\in\mathcal{V}
is an atomic proposition,

\varphi
is an
LTL
formula,

\neg
is negation,

\vee
is disjunction,

\bigcirc
is the next operator,
and
\mathcal{U}
is the strong until operator.
Conjunction (
\land
), implication (
\to
), equivalence (
\leftrightarrow
), eventually (
\lozenge
), and always (
\square
) can be derived from the grammar.

The semantics of
LTL
are defined over infinite sequences
\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
where
\sigma_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is the set of atomic propositions that are
True
at step
i
.
We denote that a sequence
\sigma
satisfies a formula
\varphi
at step
i
by
\sigma^{i}\models\varphi
.
A sequence
\sigma
satisfies a formula
\varphi
, denoted
\sigma\models\varphi
, iff
\sigma^{0}\models\varphi
.
Formally,

\sigma^{i}\models\pi
iff
\pi\in\sigma_{i}
,

\sigma^{i}\models\bigcirc\varphi
iff
\sigma^{i+1}\models\varphi
,

\sigma^{i}\models\lozenge\varphi
iff
\exists j\geq i
such that
\sigma^{j}\models\varphi
, and

\sigma^{i}\models\square\varphi
iff
\forall j\geq i
,
\sigma^{j}\models\varphi
.
The full semantics of
LTL
can be found in
[
6
]
.

II-B

Abstractions

We partition the set of atomic propositions into sets of inputs
{\mathcal{X}}
and outputs
{\mathcal{Y}}
(
\mathcal{V}={\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}
).
The inputs describe the environment states, such as the position of an object.
An input state is a subset of the inputs (
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
).
We partition the set of inputs into sets of controllable inputs
{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}
and uncontrollable inputs
{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
(
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
).
Controllable inputs describe the part of the physical world that the robot can control, for example its position. A controllable input state is a subset of the controllable inputs (
\sigma_{\textrm{c}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}
).
Uncontrollable inputs describe the part of the physical world that the robot does not have control over, such as the position of an obstacle, or a user input.
The input abstraction can be obtained automatically
[
2
,
3
]
.
In this paper, we obtain
{\mathcal{X}}
by taking a Cartesian product of a set of regions
{\mathcal{R}}
that partitions the workspace of the robot and a set of objects
{\mathcal{O}}
that includes the robot,
along with a set of user-controlled inputs
{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{user}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
. The set of inputs is

{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\{\pi_{{o}}^{{r}}\mid o\in{\mathcal{O}},r\in{\mathcal{R}}\}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{user}}
where
\pi_{{o}}^{{r}}
is
True
when object
o
is in region
r
.
In Example
I
,

{\mathcal{O}}=\{\textrm{robot},\textrm{obs}\}
,

{\mathcal{R}}=\{\textrm{assm},\textrm{aisle},\textrm{wlkwy},\textrm{load}\}
,

{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{user}}=\emptyset
,

{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}=\{\pi_{{robot}}^{{r}}\mid r\in{\mathcal{R}}\}
,

{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}=\{\pi_{{obs}}^{{r}}\mid r\in{\mathcal{R}}\}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{user}}
,
and
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
.

A grounding function
G
maps an input proposition to a set of states in the physical world
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.
\textrm{G}:{\mathcal{X}}\to 2^{X}
.
We lift the grounding function to map an input state
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}
to a set of physical states by taking the intersection of the groundings of each input in
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}
,
i.e.
\textrm{G}(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}})=\bigcap_{\pi\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\textrm{G}(\pi)\bigcap_{\pi\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}(X\setminus\textrm{G}(\pi))
.
An inverse grounding function
\textrm{G}^{-1}:X\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}
maps a physical state
x\in X
to an input state
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
such that

x\in\textrm{G}(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}})

and
\forall\pi\not\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},x\not\in\textrm{G}(\pi)
.
In Example
I
, if initially the robot is at
x_{\textrm{init}}^{\textrm{robot}}=(5,1)
, and the obstacle is at
x_{\textrm{init}}^{\textrm{obs}}=(3.8,0.5)
, then the initial abstract input state is
\sigma_{\textrm{init}}=\textrm{G}^{-1}((x_{\textrm{init}}^{\textrm{robot}},x_{\textrm{init}}^{\textrm{obs}}))=\{\pi_{{robot}}^{{assm}},\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}}\}
.

The set of outputs
{\mathcal{Y}}
represents the set of skills the robot can execute. A skill can only be executed at a set of controllable input states (initial preconditions),
results in a set of controllable input states (final postconditions),
and may pass through a set of unique controllable input states (intermediate states).
Each state except for the final postconditions has a set of postconditions.
Each state except for the initial preconditions has a set of preconditions.
In Example
I
, the initial precondition of the skill
move_{1}
is

\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{1}}=\{\pi_{{robot}}^{{assm}}\}
,
the postcondition of
move_{1}
on
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{1}}
is

\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{2}}=\{\pi_{{robot}}^{{aisle}}\}
, and
the postcondition of
move_{1}
on
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{2}}
is

\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{3}}=\{\pi_{{robot}}^{{load}}\}
.
The state
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}},\textrm{3}}
is also the final postcondition of
move_{1}
.
The skills are implemented by the robot’s low-level controllers.
The skill abstraction can be obtained automatically
[
4
]
.
In this paper, we assume that the user provides the skill abstraction.
We assume that skill execution does not terminate until it reaches the final postcondition, unless an assumption violation occurs, in which case we terminate the skill prematurely and start the recovery process.

II-C

Synthesis and Specification Violation

In this paper, we consider the
GR(1)
fragment of
LTL
to leverage its synthesis algorithm
[
7
]
.
The specifications are of the form
\varphi=\varphi_{\textrm{e}}\to\varphi_{\textrm{s}}

where
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}=\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{i}}\land\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}\land\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{g}}
is the environment assumption and

\varphi_{\textrm{s}}=\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{i}}\land\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{t}}\land\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{g}}
is the system guarantee:

\bullet

\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{i}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{i}}
are Boolean formulas over
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}

\bullet

\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{t}}
are the environment safety assumptions and the system safety guarantees, respectively.
They are of the form
\bigwedge_{i}\square\psi_{i}
where
\psi_{i}
is a Boolean formula over
v
and
\bigcirc u
.
For
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}
,
v\in{\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}
and
u\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
For
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{t}}
,
v,u\in{\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}
.
We divide
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}
into the skill assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,skill}}
and the the hard assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
(
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}=\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,skill}}\land\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
).
The skill assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,skill}}
describe the postconditions of the skills and can be modified by the repair process (see Section
IV-C
).
The assumptions in
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
are the hard assumptions that cannot be modified by repair.

\bullet

\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{g}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{g}}
are the environment liveness assumptions and the system liveness goals, respectively.
They are of the form
\bigwedge_{i}\square\lozenge J_{i}
where
J_{i}
is a Boolean formula over
{\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}
.

The synthesis algorithm in
[
7
]
formulates a two-player game between the environment and the system.
The initial conditions of the specification define the initial positions of the players,
the safety formulas define the transition relations of the players,
and the liveness formulas induce the winning condition for the system.
The synthesis algorithm solves a
\mu
-calculus formula with three nesting fixpoints
on the game structure to compute a
winning strategy for the system.

Definition 1.

A
strategy
is a deterministic finite state machine
{\mathcal{A}}=(\Sigma,\Sigma_{0},{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}},\delta,\gamma_{\mathcal{X}},\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}})
where

\bullet

\Sigma
is a set of states and
\Sigma_{0}\subseteq\Sigma
is the set of initial states.

\bullet

\delta:\Sigma\times 2^{\mathcal{X}}\rightharpoonup\Sigma
is the transition function that maps a state and an input state to the next state.

\bullet

\gamma_{\mathcal{X}}:\Sigma\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}
maps a state to its input state.

\bullet

\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}}:\Sigma\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
maps a state to its output state.

For subset
V\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, let
\bigcirc V\coloneqq\{\bigcirc\pi\mid\pi\in V\}
. We define the violation of safety assumptions during strategy execution:

Definition 2.

Given the safety formula
\square\psi

where
\psi
is a Boolean formula over
{\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}\cup\bigcirc{\mathcal{X}}
,
and
a triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
where
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
and
\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
,
the triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})

violates

\square\psi
iff

\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}\cup\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\cup\bigcirc\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}\not\models\psi

IV-A

Violation Detection

The violation detection procedure takes in a specification
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}

and
a triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
where
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
and
\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
.
We use a monitor, compiled from
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}
, to detect violations of environment safety assumptions.

We use a compiler to translate the environment safety assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}=\bigwedge_{i}\square\psi_{i}
in
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}
into a monitor
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The compiler first uses a tokenizer and a parser to translate
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}
into a set of Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs)
[
28
]
.
Each AST represents a Boolean formula
\psi_{i}
in a tree structure according to the syntax of the specifications
[
27
]
.
Then the compiler translates the ASTs to a set of Boolean constraints for the SAT solver Z3
[
29
]
,
which is then used to check whether any safety assumptions are violated.
In Example
I
, the compiler translates
\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}}\to\bigcirc\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}}

to the AST in Fig.
3
and creates the Boolean expression
\tt{Implies}
(\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}},\bigcirc\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}})
in the Z3 format.

Fig. 3
:
Abstract Syntax Tree for
\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}}\to\bigcirc\pi_{{obs}}^{{wlkwy}}
.

The monitor
{\mathcal{M}}
uses the triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
to evaluate the environment safety assumption
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}
.
For each safety assumption
\square\psi_{i}
,
the monitor evaluates the truth value of the Boolean formula
\psi_{i}

given the literal values indicated by the triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
.
In practice, the monitor creates the following Boolean formula for each assumption
\square\psi_{i}
:

\begin{split}\psi_{i}^{\textrm{check}}\coloneqq\psi_{i}\land\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\neg x\land\bigwedge_{y\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}y\land\bigwedge_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}\neg y\land\\
\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\bigcirc x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\neg\bigcirc x\end{split}

(1)

to compute the satisfiability of Formula

, which indicates whether the triplet violates the assumption

or not.
The monitor returns a set of violated assumptions

.

IV-B

Assumption Relaxation

The assumption relaxation procedure takes in
a specification
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}
,
a set of violated environment safety assumptions
{\mathcal{I}}
,
and a triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})

that violates the assumptions in
{\mathcal{I}}
.
The procedure relaxes the violated assumptions in
{\mathcal{I}}
to admit the violated transition indicated by the triplet.
The procedure follows
Environment Characterization (EC)
in
[
21
,
22
]
, which relaxes the assumptions solely using input states.
In contrast to
EC
, our procedure utilizes
the complete observation triplet
(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
to rewrite violated assumptions.
Consequently, our procedure can handle the discovery of previously unknown nondeterminism in robot skills by
incorporating the nondeterminism into the skill abstraction.
In addition, our procedure allows the robot to resume the execution in the current input state and continue to attempt the latest liveness goal.

Recall that we split the environment safety assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t}}=\bigwedge_{i}\square\psi_{i}
into the skill assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,skill}}
,
which describe the postconditions of the skills,
and the hard assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
, which cannot be modified by the repair procedure in Section
IV-C
.
For each violated assumption
\square\psi_{i}
in
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
,
the procedure replaces
\square\psi_{i}
with
\square(\psi_{i}\lor\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{hard}})

where
\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{hard}}
is the following formula:

\begin{split}\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{hard}}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\neg x\land\bigwedge_{y\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}y\land\bigwedge_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}\neg y\land\\
\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\bigcirc x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\neg\bigcirc x\end{split}

(2)

For each violated assumption
\square\psi_{i}
in
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,skill}}
,
the procedure replaces the assumption
\square\psi_{i}
with

\square(\psi_{i}\lor\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{skill}})

where
\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{skill}}
is shown in Formula
3
.
Intuitively, both formulas add the observed behavior, using disjunction, to the assumptions, thereby allowing for more environment behaviors.
The difference between Formula
3
and
2
is that the former only adds the controllable inputs, and the latter adds all inputs.
The reason for this distinction is that skill abstractions are defined solely over controllable inputs,
so we can only modify them with controllable inputs,
whereas the hard assumptions
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{t,hard}}
are defined over all inputs.

\begin{split}\psi_{\textrm{r}}^{\textrm{skill}}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}}x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\neg x\land\bigwedge_{y\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}y\land\bigwedge_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}\neg y\land\\
\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}}\bigcirc x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\neg\bigcirc x\end{split}

(3)

Fig.

depicts the workflow of the relaxation procedure in practice.
We relax the violated assumptions by modifying the ASTs in the monitor compiler (Section
IV-A
) according to Formulas
2
and
3
.
The compiler then generates the new specification by recursively visiting every node in the modified ASTs and generating the corresponding
LTL
formula.

Before generating the new specification, we set the initial input condition
\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{i}}
to be the current input state
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}
, as shown in Formula
4
.
Doing so allows the robot to resume the task execution in the current input state.

\varphi_{\textrm{e}}^{\textrm{i}}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{x\in\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}x\land\bigwedge_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime}}\neg x

(4)

Moreover, we reorder the liveness goals to
allow the robot to attempt
the latest goal first.
Recall the liveness goal formula
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{g}}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}\square\lozenge J_{i}
.
Suppose the strategy attempts to satisfy the
j^{\textrm{th}}
liveness goal
J_{j}
before the violation happens.
Since the synthesized strategy loops through the liveness goals in a cyclic manner
[
7
]
,
we reorder
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{g}}
by moving
J_{j}\dots J_{m}
to the front of
\varphi_{\textrm{s}}^{\textrm{g}}
.

IV-C

Repair

The repair procedure takes in
an unrealizable specification
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}
and
the abstraction
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. We find a set of new skills
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
and modify the specification
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}
based on the new abstraction
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}})
such that the new specification includes the new skills
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
and is realizable.

We leverage the synthesis-based repair approach proposed in
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
to find a set of new skills.
The algorithm suggests new skills by alternating two procedures:

1.

Modify-Preconditions.

Randomly select an existing skill transition such that the robot can satisfy the task from its postcondition.
Modify the precondition of the transition to a random new state from which the robot cannot satisfy the task before the modification.

2.

Modify-Postconditions.

Randomly select an existing skill transition
such that the robot cannot satisfy the task from either the pre- or postcondition of the transition.
Modify the postcondition to a random new state from which the robot can satisfy the task.

In this work we change the Modify-Preconditions algorithm from
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
to improve the efficiency of repair.
Repair can be inefficient
since both modifying the pre- and postconditions involves randomly selecting a transition and a new state.
In Modify-Precondition,
suppose the original precondition is an intermediate state of a skill. Repair ensures that the new precondition is reachable from the initial precondition of the skill by adding a transition from the precondition of the original precondition (pre-precondition) to the new precondition.
However, even if the robot can satisfy the task from the new precondition after the modification, the robot still cannot satisfy the task from the pre-precondition because it has a nondeterministic transition to the old precondition.
We remove the transition from the pre-precondition to the old precondition, allowing the robot to satisfy the task from the pre-precondition.
In Example
I
, the original Modify-Precondition algorithm returns a symbolic skill
move^{\prime}
which goes from
\pi_{{robot}}^{{assm}}
nondeterministically to either
\pi_{{robot}}^{{wlkwy}}
or
\pi_{{robot}}^{{aisle}}
, and from
\pi_{{robot}}^{{wlkwy}}
to
\pi_{{robot}}^{{load}}
.
Because of the nondeterminism, the robot cannot guarantee it will reach
Loading
and requires further refinement of the skills.
Our approach returns the skill
move_{2}
,
which goes from

\pi_{{robot}}^{{assm}}

to
\pi_{{robot}}^{{wlkwy}}

to
\pi_{{robot}}^{{load}}
,
in one iteration of the repair.

Given a set of suggested skills
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
from the repair algorithm, in order to decrease the size of the abstraction,
we attempt to remove unnecessary skills in
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
.
For each skill
y\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
, we remove
y
from
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}

and check whether the specification with the updated
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
is realizable.
If the result is realizable, then we remove the skill
y

permanently. Otherwise, we add the skill
y
back to the set of new skills.

We physically implement the new symbolic skills using a motion planner
[
30
]
.
If we cannot physically implement a symbolic skill,
we incorporate the skill as a repair constraint,
as done in
[
26
]
, and continue the repair process.

VI
Conclusion

We provide a framework for automatically recovering from assumption violations of temporal logic specifications.
To do so we detect the assumption violations during the robot execution, relax the violated assumptions to admit the observed environment behavior, and add new robot skills for the robot to complete the tasks.
We demonstrate our approach
with a physical robot in a factory-like scenario.

There are assumption violations, however, from which our approach cannot recover.
If assumptions about the postconditions of skills are violated due to faulty low-level robot controllers, then repair at the symbolic level cannot solve the problem.
In Example
I
, our framework cannot recover from the violation if the low-level controller cannot reliably implement the new skill
move_{2}
due to severe localization error and uncertainty in
Walkway
.
Moreover, if an assumption violation results in the robot’s current state violating its hard safety constraints,
then no new skills can repair the task from that violating state.
In Example
I
, if the obstacle moves to
Aisle
when the robot is already in
Aisle
,
our approach cannot repair because the current state violates the safety constraint which prohibits the robot and the obstacle from being in the same region.
In future work, we plan to
tackle the violations that our approach cannot repair,
develop local repair to improve the computational efficiency of our approach,
and
extend our framework to multi-agent and human-robot interaction settings where assumptions about human behaviors may be violated.
