Hybrid Quantum-Classical Machine Learning with String Diagrams
Alexander Koziell-Pipe, Aleks Kissinger

Abstract
Central to near-term quantum machine learning is the use of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms. This paper develops a formal framework for describing these algorithms in terms of string diagrams: a key step towards integrating these hybrid algorithms into existing work using string diagrams for machine learning and differentiable programming. A notable feature of our string diagrams is the use of functor boxes, which correspond to a quantum-classical interfaces. The functor used is a lax monoidal functor embedding the quantum systems into classical, and the lax monoidality imposes restrictions on the string diagrams when extracting classical data from quantum systems via measurement. In this way, our framework provides initial steps toward a denotational semantics for hybrid quantum machine learning algorithms that captures important features of quantum-classical interactions.



1
Introduction

At time of writing, we are still in the Noisy Intermediate Scale (NISQ) era of quantum computing
[
30
]
, where techniques to correct errors are still under development and the size of quantum circuits are limited. Quantum algorithms requiring deep circuits and larger numbers of qubits, notably Shor’s
[
35
,
34
]
and Grover’s
[
20
]
algorithms, are still beyond our reach for practical use.

Hybrid quantum-classical algorithms, where quantum computations interoperate with classical,
offer a pragmatic solution to these limitations. These hybrid algorithms extract as much performance as possible out of limited quantum resources by delegating a large part of their computation to classical computers, then using quantum processors for small subroutines for which they are particularly well-suited.

The archetypal example of hybrid algorithms in the context of quantum machine learning is the Variational Quantum Eigensolver
[
28
]
and more generally, Variational Quantum Algorithms
[
8
]
. In these algorithms a parameterized quantum circuit is used to compute a cost function, which is provided to a classical optimizer that computes updated parameters to minimize the cost. Computing updated parameters may involve further quantum processing in order to evaluate gradients of the cost function with respect to the parameters
[
26
,
31
]
. This process is repeated iteratively until a desired performance or termination condition is met. These algorithms are inherently hybrid in the sense of
[
7
]
, as they require non-trivial amounts of both quantum and classical computational resources to run. Furthermore, they involve the repeated transfer of information between quantum and classical systems.

In this work, we provide a diagrammatic formalism of hybrid algorithms using category theory. Originally developed to study algebraic topology
[
14
]
, category theory has since found applications across a range of scientific disciplines, including quantum computing
[
4
,
5
,
11
]
and machine learning
[
16
,
13
,
19
]
1

1

[
18
]
.
.

Category theory provides a unifying language for different branches of mathematics. Furthermore, it offers an abstract framework for understanding and formalizing different mathematical structures and the relationships between them. In the context of this paper, category theory bridges the gap between machine learning and quantum computing. The mathematics of machine learning is characterised by smooth, differentiable functions and the ability to freely copy and delete information without restriction. This sits in stark contrast with quantum computation, where calculating gradients of arbitrary quantum circuits is highly non-trivial, and copying and deleting information is physically prohibited by the no-cloning and no-deleting theorems of quantum information.

This separation between the mathematics of machine learning and quantum computing is reflected by the distinct properties of the categories in which each are described. While categorical quantum mechanics is formulated in terms of dagger compact closed categories, the categorical machine learning typically uses Cartesian categories in which there is a suitable notion of a reverse derivative. Our work is a step towards combining these two theories of systems and processes into a single framework, with the intention of facilitating seamless interoperability in practical implementations of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms.

Our contributions are as follows. First, following
[
13
]
, we establish the Cartesian category
\mathsf{Smooth}
as the category with which to describe the classical part of our algorithms. Next, we choose the compact closed category
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
of completely positive maps
[
33
]
for the quantum part. We then show that there exists a lax monoidal functor
\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Smooth}
, which we define as the composition of two functors
\mathsf{G}:\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathsf{H}:\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Smooth}
. We give a concrete description of this functor by fixing a basis in terms of Pauli operators for each object of
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
. The lax monoidal functor bridges our quantum and classical systems, and allows the description of hybrid classical-quantum algorithms using string diagrams
[
32
,
29
]
in which functorial boxes
[
9
,
25
]
represent classical-quantum interfaces. The lax condition on our functor allows multiple wires to enter the box, representing the encoding of classical data into the quantum circuit, but only one wire to exit, representing a measurement probability. Furthermore, we are able to represent the quantum system as a ZX-diagram
[
10
,
37
,
3
]
, making the quantum part of our algorithm amenable to quantum circuit optimization methods. Finally, we show a concrete application of our framework to the quantum machine learning algorithm of
[
15
]
, in which parameterized quantum circuits are trained on MNIST classification
[
23
]
. Our framework gives us a denotational semantics of this quantum machine learning algorithm that could form the basis of a programming language for quantum machine learning, or hybrid quantum-classical algorithms more generally.

Our work is intended as a step toward the extension of categorical gradient based learning framework to allow ‘quantum nodes’, and future work will aim to formalise parameterization and backpropagation in these hybrid algorithms through the use of the parametric lens construction of
[
13
]
and diagrams for ‘gradient recipes’
[
26
,
31
]
, an established method for computing the gradient of a parameterized quantum circuit for use in quantum machine learning.

2.1
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
and ZX-diagrams

Pure state quantum mechanics can be described mathematically in terms of complex Hilbert spaces. Furthermore, in quantum computing we are usually interested in finite-dimensional systems. As a consquence, much of the work applying category theory to quantum mechanics involves the use of the category
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
:

Definition 1

(
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
)
.

The symmetric monoidal category
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
is defined by:

Objects

finite-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces.

Morphisms

H\rightarrow K
linear maps from
H
to
K
.

Monoidal product

the tensor product of Hilbert spaces.

Monoidal unit

the 0-dimensional Hilbert space.

When working with quantum computation, in particular qubits, we can simplify the objects we have to work with by moving to the subcategory of
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
consisting of products of 2-dimensional complex spaces
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. We call this category
\mathsf{Qubit}
and define it as a PROP:

Definition 2

(
\mathsf{Qubit}
)
.

The category
\mathsf{Qubit}
is the PROP whose morphisms
n\rightarrow m
are given by
2^{m}\times 2^{n}
complex matrices,
\mathsf{Qubit}(n,m):=\mathbb{M}^{2^{m}\times 2^{n}}_{\mathbb{C}}
. The monoidal product on morphisms is given by the Kronecker product of matrices.

We can think of the objects
n\in\mathsf{Qubit}
as corresponding to Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
containing the complex amplitudes for an
n
-qubit system. When working in
\mathsf{Qubit}
, we can use ZX-diagrams
[
10
,
37
,
3
]
to describe our morphisms. ZX-diagrams are generated by green (light) Z spiders and red (dark) X spiders, corresponding to the quantum states
\lvert 00...0\rangle\langle 00..0\rvert+e^{i\alpha}\lvert 11...1\rangle\langle 11...1\rvert
and
\lvert++...+\rangle\langle++...+\rvert+e^{i\alpha}\lvert--...-\rangle\langle--...-\rvert
, respectively:

\alpha
\vdots
\vdots

\alpha
\vdots
\vdots

Any morphism in
\mathsf{Qubit}
can be expressed as (possibly multiple) ZX-diagram(s). Furthermore, the diagrams can be equipped with a set of rewrite rules allowing one diagram to be transformed into another. There are known sets of rewrite rules that are sound and complete, meaning that two diagrams can be transformed into one another if and only if they correspond to the same quantum process. ZX-diagrams together with a canonical set of rewrite rules are referred to as the ZX-calculus, and has useful applications to quantum circuit simplification and quantum error correction. For a more in-depth introduction, see
[
37
]
.

2.2
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
and Doubled ZX

When bridging from quantum to classical systems, we will choose to work with the density operator formalism of quantum mechanics. This allows us to work with mixed-state quantum mechanics, as well as express our diagrams using a basis of Pauli operators with real coefficients. This requires deriving a different category from
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
, using the CPM-construction of
[
33
]
. When applied to
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
, this gives us the following category, where we denote the space of linear operators on a Hilbert space
H
by
\mathcal{L}(H)
:

Definition 3
.

The category
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
is defined by

Objects

complex Hilbert spaces.

Morphisms

H\rightarrow K
completely positive linear superoperators
\mathcal{L}(H)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(K)
.

Like
\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}}
,
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
is symmetric monoidal, with monoidal product given by the tensor product. If we once again restrict to 2-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces, we can similarly define
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
:

Definition 4
.

The category
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
is the PROP whose morphisms
n\rightarrow m
are the completely positive linear superoperators
\mathcal{L}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes m})
.

Contrasting with
\mathsf{Qubit}
, objects
n
of
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
can be thought of as corresponding to spaces
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}:=\ \mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
. States (morphisms
0\rightarrow n
for some
n\in\mathsf{Qubit}
) correspond to density matrices, while processes
n\rightarrow m
correspond to quantum channels.

We can extend the ZX-calculus to
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
through a process known as ‘doubling’
[
11
, Chapter 8]

.

n\rightarrow m

n

g

m^{\prime}
-tuple) on the last

Now that we have a functor to go from our abstract quantum circuit representations in

\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}

Definition 8
.

The functor
\mathsf{H}:\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Smooth}
is defined by the following:

Objects

\mathsf{H}
is identity on objects
n\mapsto n
.

Morphisms

m\times n
real matrices are mapped to the corresponding smooth map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

The composition of these two functors
\mathsf{F}:=\mathsf{G}\mathbin{\raise 2.58334pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{o}$\hfil\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{9}$\hfil\crcr}}}}\mathsf{H}

We now prove a theorem about our functor

We now prove that
\mathsf{F}

Theorem 1
.

The functor
\mathsf{F}
is lax monoidal.

Proof.

We begin by defining the coherence maps:

•

The morphism
\epsilon:0\longrightarrow\mathsf{F}(0)=1
is the map
\mathbb{R}^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
taking the single element
*\mapsto 1
.

•

We define the natural transformation
\mu_{n,m}:\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m)\longrightarrow\mathsf{F}(n+m)
by noting that in
\mathsf{Smooth}
, the object
\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m)=4^{n}+4^{m}
corresponds to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{4^{n}+4^{m}}
. Elements of this space can be characterized by pairs of
4^{n}
and
4^{m}
-dimensional real vectors
(\vec{v},\ \vec{w})
. We map these to the space
\mathbb{R}^{4^{n+m}}
corresponding to
\mathsf{F}(n+m)=4^{n+m}
as follows:

(\vec{v},\ \vec{w})\longmapsto\vec{v}\otimes\vec{w}

(10)

Where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

We now prove that these maps satisfy the coherence conditions. For clarity, we denote the monoidal product on morphisms as
\otimes
in
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
and as
\times
in
\mathsf{Smooth}
, reflecting their operational behaviour.

•

For associativity, we must show that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m))+\mathsf{F}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+(\mathsf{F}(m)+\mathsf{F}(k))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+m)+\mathsf{F}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m+k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}((n+m)+k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+(m+k))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha^{\mathsf{Smooth}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m}\times id_{\mathsf{F}(k)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id_{\mathsf{F}(n)}\times\mu_{m,k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n+m,k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m+k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(\alpha^{\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}})}

(11)

Where
\alpha
denotes the associators in
\mathsf{Smooth}
and
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
. Given that the associators in
\mathsf{Smooth}
and
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}
are trivial, we draw the simpler diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m)+\mathsf{F}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m+k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+m)+\mathsf{F}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+m+k)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id_{\mathsf{F}(n)}\times\mu_{m,k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m}\times id_{\mathsf{F}(k)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m+k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n+m,k}}

(12)

We prove this diagram commutes as follows. Assume any element
(\vec{u},\ \vec{v},\ \vec{w})
from the top left of the square. The top corner of the square maps this element as follows:

(\vec{u},\ \vec{v},\ \vec{w})\mapsto(\vec{u},\ \vec{v}\otimes\vec{w})\mapsto(\vec{u}\otimes(\vec{v}\otimes\vec{w}))

(13)

Conversely, the bottom corner of the square maps this element as follows:

(\vec{u},\ \vec{v},\ \vec{w})\mapsto(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v},\ \vec{w})\mapsto((\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v})\otimes\vec{w})

(14)

These two mappings are equal by the associativity of the Kronecker product. Hence the square commutes.

•

For unitality, we must show that the following diagrams commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0+\mathsf{F}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(0)+\mathsf{F}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(0+n)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon\times id_{\mathsf{F}(n)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces l^{\mathsf{Smooth}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{0,n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(l^{\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}})}

(15)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(0)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+0)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id_{\mathsf{F}(n)}\times\epsilon}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r^{\mathsf{Smooth}}_{\mathsf{F}(n)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(r^{\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{Qubit})}}_{n})}

(16)

Where
l
and
r
denote left and right unitors, respectively.
Take an element from the top left corner of the first square. Tracing the long route to the right, down, then left, we have:

(*,\ \vec{u})\mapsto(1,\ \vec{u})\mapsto 1\otimes\vec{u}\mapsto\vec{u}

(17)

The route straight down maps the element as:

(*,\ \vec{u})\mapsto\vec{u}

(18)

Thus the diagram commutes. The second diagram is similar. Along the right, down, left route:

(\vec{u},\ *)\mapsto(\vec{u},\ 1)\mapsto\vec{u}\otimes 1\mapsto\vec{u}

(19)

Along the route straight down:

(\vec{u},\ *)\mapsto\vec{u}

(20)

•

It remains to show that
\mu
is a natural transformation. For this we must prove that the following diagrams commute for all
n,m,k
and completely positive superoperators
T,T^{\prime}
on the appropriate Hilbert spaces:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+k)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id_{\mathsf{F}(n)}\times\mathsf{F}(T)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(id_{n}\otimes T)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,k}}

(21)

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n)+\mathsf{F}(m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(n+m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(k)+\mathsf{F}(m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(k+m)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(T^{\prime})\times id_{\mathsf{F}(m)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{F}(T^{\prime}\otimes id_{m})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{n,k}}

(22)

We prove only that the first square commutes: the second is similar by the symmetry of the tensor and Cartesian products.

First notice that since
\mathsf{G}
represents linear maps as matrices with respect to orthonormal bases, it preserves the tensor product:
\mathsf{G}\left(T_{1}\otimes T_{2}\right)=\mathsf{G}\left(T_{1}\right)\otimes\mathsf{G}\left(T_{2}\right)
3
3

3

\mathsf{G}
is in fact strict monoidal.
. Thus
\mathsf{F}(id_{n}+T)=\mathsf{H}\mathbin{\raise 2.58334pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{o}$\hfil\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{9}$\hfil\crcr}}}}\mathsf{G}\left(id_{n}\otimes T\right)=\mathsf{H}(I_{4^{n}}\otimes M)
, where
I_{4^{n}}
is the
4^{n}\times 4^{n}
identity matrix and
M
is the matrix for
T
with respect to the tensored Pauli bases.

Next note that since by definition of the tensor product,
(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v})=M_{1}\vec{u}\otimes M_{2}\vec{v}
. Thus in order for the map
\mathsf{H}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})
in
\mathsf{Smooth}
to correspond to
M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
, it must act on
\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}
, viewed as an element in Euclidean space, as
\mathsf{H}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v})=\mathsf{H}(M_{1})(\vec{u})\otimes\mathsf{H}(M_{2})(\vec{v})
.

With this in mind, when taking any element of from the top left corner of the first square and taking the upper route to the bottom right corner, we see that:

(\vec{u},\ \vec{v})\mapsto\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}\mapsto\vec{u}\otimes\vec{w}

(23)

Along the bottom route, we have

(\vec{u},\ \vec{v})\mapsto(\vec{u},\ \vec{w})\mapsto\vec{u}\otimes\vec{w}

(24)

Thus the square commutes.

•

Hence have shown that there exist two coherence maps
\epsilon
and
\mu
satisfying the appropriate conditions to make
\mathsf{F}
lax monoidal.

.

In the context of quantum information, the fact that our functor is lax monoidal but not oplax is not a bug, but a feature. Classical information can be encoded into a quantum system accurately, reflected by the preservation of wires entering the quantum system in our string diagrams. On the other hand, extracting information from the quantum system involves measurement, collapsing the superposition of the quantum state. This yields a probabilistic outcome and is reflected by the bunching up of wires leaving the quantum system into scalar probabilities.

4
Conclusion and Future Work

We have taken initial steps towards a category-theoretic formalism of hybrid quantum-classical machine learning. This was done by embedding a the dagger compact closed category
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}
, in which quantum computation takes place, into a Cartesian category
\mathsf{Smooth}
for the classical computation. This was achieved using a lax monoidal functor, which we defined concretely as the composition of two functors: one from
\mathsf{CPM(\mathsf{FHilb_{\mathbb{C}}})}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}
, and another from
\mathsf{Mat}_{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Smooth}
. Furthermore, through the use of functor boxes we were able to capture both the classical and quantum part of hybrid algorithms in a single string diagram where functor boxes delineate the quantum-classical interface. A notable consequence of the lax monoidality of our functor was that multiple wires could enter the quantum system when encoding classical data on the quantum computer whereas only one wire, representing a measurement probability, could exit the quantum system. The quantum part of the algorithm could be described as a ZX-diagram, making amenable to quantum circuit optimization methods. Finally, we applied our framework to the MNIST classification task in
[
15
]
, giving us a denotational semantics of a concrete quantum machine learning algorithm that could form the basis of a programming language.

In future work, we aim to formalise parameterization and backpropagation in these hybrid algorithms using the parametric lens construction of
[
13
]
. This will allow for a full specification of a hybrid quantum classical machine learning algorithm with string diagrams capturing both the forward and backward pass. In such a framework, we would need to specify the gradient of quantum parts of the algorithm. This can be done using gradient recipes: quantum circuits that compute the gradients of others. We note the use of the ZXW calculus for computing gradient recipes
[
22
]
[
36
]
and are interested in employing them in our framework.
