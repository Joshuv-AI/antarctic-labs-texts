Actuators À La Mode: Modal Actuations for Soft Body Locomotion Journal: TOG
Otman Benchekroun, Kaixiang Xie, Hsueh-Ti Derek Liu, Eitan Grinspun, Sheldon Andrews, Victor Zordan

Abstract
. Traditional character animation specializes in characters with a rigidly articulated skeleton and a bipedal/quadripedal morphology. This assumption simplifies many aspects for designing physically based animations, like locomotion, but comes with the price of excluding characters of arbitrary deformable geometries. To remedy this, our framework makes use of a spatio-temporal actuation subspace built off of the natural vibration modes of the character geometry. The resulting actuation is coupled to a reduced fast soft body simulation, allowing us to formulate a locomotion optimization problem that is tractable for a wide variety of high resolution deformable characters.

1.
Introduction

The world is comprised of a rich diversity of deformable organisms.
These organisms leverage their unique geometry and deformable nature for complex interactions with their environment in order to locomote, manipulate objects, or take flight.
They also serve as inspiration for the many fantastical creatures and virtual characters that populate video games, films, and virtual environments.
Having simulation and control methods that are able to faithfully capture the essence of these organisms and their behaviors is key to filling virtual worlds with these creative characters
However, most work on developing controllers for virtual characters is predicated on an extremely narrow set of morphologies that assumes an underlying rigid skeletal structure, and further imposes that they be bipedal or quadrupedal.

These assumptions preclude a wide range of characters, which are either bipedal or quadrupedal, from being animated.
For instance, the motion of an octopus with tentacles as shown in Fig.
2
could not be accurately modeled using rigid segments, an issue that applies to a wide variety of deformable organisms.
These limitations motivate the need to develop locomotion controllers for soft-body characters with a wide range of highly detailed deformable mesh geometry, yet without the requirement of a piecewise rigid skeleton.

Unfortunately, such controllers are difficult to generate for three reasons.
First, there exists
significantly
less motion data for arbitrary geometries than there is for bipedal or quadripedal characters.
This data is key for creating natural-looking motions and its absence results in behaviors that exhibit unrealistic jittery motion
(
Heess et al. 2017
)
.
Second, deformable characters make use of a complex organization of various organic tissues, all interacting in harmony to carry out even the simplest of tasks
(
Keller et al. 2023
)
.
Designing this musculo-skelature for many characters becomes a tedious, unintuitive ordeal.

Figure 2.
Traditional animation techniques an underlying skeleton structure, which is actuated via joint torques. The resulting actuation exhibits kinky, piecewise rigid deformation. Our actuation properly models the octopus tentacle as a deformable body, allowing for a naturally smooth deformation.

Finally, high resolution deformable characters usually come with the price of an expensive simulation, which scales in complexity with the resolution of the character geometry.

We propose a very simple, scalable pipeline for generating locomotion controllers for deformable characters of arbitrary user-defined geometry.

The key to our method lies in defining a small data-free spatio-temporal actuation subspace based off the geometry of the character alone.
An actuation signal in this subspace corresponds to a shape that the deformable character naturally wants to take on.
We then define a plasticity-based actuation energy, that guides our simulated character towards this actuated target shape.
This actuation is coupled to a
reduced
deformable simulation, which allows us to perform the entire soft-body controller optimization within the reduced spaces of actuation and simulation, decoupled from the mesh resolution.
This allows us to achieve a variety of crawling, running and hopping-like locomotion behaviors with an off the shelf CMAES optimizer.

We evaluate the effectiveness of our actuation subspace on a wide variety of character geometries, obtaining varied locomotions for highly detailed characters within minutes, while also providing avenues for intuitive motion control and design.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Soft Body Controllers

Soft body characters bring with them many technical challenges in the design of locomotion controllers.
In particular, the physical model used for simulation of soft bodies is often characterized by a large number of degrees of freedom, bottle-necking the controller optimization.

A limited amount of previous work addresses constructing soft body controllers, with the significant majority modeling the full space of the character geometry. Due to the increased complexity from using high resolution models, most only animate simple 2D or coarse 3D characters
(
Coros et al. 2012
;
Jain and Liu 2011
;
Tan et al. 2012
)
.
Even with simpler physical models and character geometries,

Bhatia et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Rojas et al. 2021
;
Min et al. 2019
all report that training controllers for soft-body creatures with fewer than several thousand degrees of freedom can require hours, or even days, of training time, with simulations running much slower than real-time.

The large number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) required of soft-body simulations has motivated the development of reduced order models in order to improve simulation performance
(
Brandt et al. 2018
;
Benchekroun et al. 2023
;
Trusty et al. 2023
)
, with most of the prior work focusing on accelerating
passive
simulation.
ROMs have been leveraged to accelerate high resolution trajectory optimization tasks

(
Pan and Manocha 2018
)
, but their use remains limited for more general controllers.
While
Liang et al. 2023
accelerate a closed loop Model Predictive Control (MPC) controller, their approach still remains limited to simple and coarse character geometries, while exhibiting artifacts upon actuation Fig.
3
.

Figure 3
.
Our plasticity based actuation energy conserves angular momentum, while taking on the expected target shape. The force-based actuation from
Liang et al. 2023
does not conserve angular momentum, creating supernatural rotational motion upon actuation .

2.2.
Designing Character Musculature

Beyond fast simulation, an important step in designing controllers for a character lies in defining a viable musculoskelature for activating the physical system, and for arbitrary soft body characters, this is far from trivial.
By far the most common method of constructing this structure for rigid articulated characters is through the use of joint torques applied at the intersection of adjacent bones
(
Yin et al. 2007
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
. These joint torques are either actuated directly, or indirectly through desired joint angles via PD controllers
(
Reda et al. 2020
;
Tan et al. 2011b
)
.
Similar joint torques have been used to model soft-body characters whose motion is
skeleton
driven
(
Liu et al. 2013
;
Kim and Pollard 2011a
;
Kim and Pollard 2011b
)
, but this does not generalize to arbitrary soft body characters where a skeleton is either not obvious or non-existent.

Instead of joint torques,
Geijtenbeek et al. 2013
use biomechanically inspired muscles, resembling springs with adjustable rest lengths, which ease the creation of contractible spring-like structures in deformable bodies
(
Rojas et al. 2021
)
.

Of course, the configuration and placement of muscles play an important contribution to the type of control that can be achieved.

Lee et al. 2009
;
Lee et al. 2014
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Saito et al. 2015
;
Keller et al. 2023
all propose highly detailed musculoskeletal models based on the real-world human anatomy.
However, as we explore different character morphologies, realistic anatomical models become ill-defined, and many methods rely on a user to manually design the placement of muscle fibers.
In the absence of an anatomical model, designers are left to their own devices, often opting to draw muscles directly onto the character
(
Tan et al. 2012
;
Min et al. 2019
)
.

Alternatively, others shift the burden to an optimization procedure to design the control actuation structure
(
Lin et al. 2020
;
Bhatia et al. 2021
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Liang et al. 2023
)
.
The resulting optimization has a large search space, and has again been limited to coarse character geometries.

Yet another set of approaches from
Coros et al. 2012
;
Ichim et al. 2017
;
Pan and Manocha 2018
propose a musculature defined by a plasticity-like deformation.
The character is encouraged to take on a changing target shape, as prescribed by a coarse cage, face scan data, or user-prescribed constraints and objectives.
Our approach similarly encourages the shape towards a new rest shape, but in our case, this rest shape is described by the natural vibrations of the character geometry.

2.3.
Motion Priors

State-of-the-art character controllers often crucially make use of motion priors in order to guide the character to more natural behaviors.
This is done by specifying the expected characteristics of the desired motion.

The copious amounts of motion data available for articulated characters lends itself well to this task, with many modern controllers making use of this data as reference motion that characters are encouraged to imitate
(
Peng et al. 2018
;
Peng et al. 2021
;
Xu and Karamouzas 2021
)
.
Aside from imitation, another effective method of leveraging this motion data is by constructing a motion subspace (or motion manifold
(
Starke et al. 2022
)
), either with PCA
(
Chai and Hodgins 2005
)
or more modern Deep Learning-based autoencoders
(
Holden et al. 2015
;
Holden et al. 2020
;
Bergamin et al. 2019
)
.

Unfortunately, for soft body control of arbitrary geometries, motion data is not readily available.
Thus, we must turn to alternative principles for guiding controllers, without the use of motion data.

For example,
Ranganath et al. 2021
generate their own synthetic motion data for articulated characters, and derive an actuation subspace using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) applied to their synthetic data.
They show their deep reinforcement learning framework can animate articulated character with widely varying morphologies.

Heess et al. 2017
instead turn to a more rigorous training routine to fill the hole, observing that training a controller on a diverse set of environments leads to more robust rigid body controllers that better satisfy user defined objectives, albeit with some undesired jittery motion.

Periodicity has shown itself to be a particularly powerful prior, with

Yu et al. 2018
generating locomotion controllers that reward periodic and symmetric behaviors for articulated-bodies.
This can be taken one step further by imbuing periodicity into our controller actuation space itself;
Central Pattern Generators (CPG)
(
Guertin 2009
)
model character motions with simple periodic control signals that are propagated across the character according to a low-dimensional set of parameters.
These kinds of temporal motion subspaces have been effective for generating
swimming and crawling motions
(
Min et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Tu and Terzopoulos 1994
;
Tan et al. 2011a
)
and have been injected into modern deep reinforcement learning architectures to create rich encodings of human motions

(
Holden et al. 2017
;
Starke et al. 2022
)

On top of periodicity, others also turn to energy efficiency to guide their animations. For example,
Kry et al. 2009
and
Nunes et al. 2012
show that the natural vibrations of articulated characters can be used to generate kinematic locomotion animations for rigid characters of varying morphology.

Our approach extends these energy efficient, periodic subspaces demonstrated in prior works for soft body locomotion.
Where we derive an actuation subspace through the periodic actuation of the natural vibration modes of the deformable character.

3.
Method Overview

Our goal is to derive an actuation for deformable characters that generates motion satisfying certain objectives, while remaining physically plausible.
We cast this as a controller optimization problem,

(1)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{d}(t)}\quad J(\boldsymbol{x}(t)),

(2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{x}|_{t}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad E(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d}|_{t})\quad\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],

where we are solving for a time-varying actuation
\boldsymbol{d}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
that corresponds to the target shape our character should take.

J(\boldsymbol{x}(t))
is a task-specific objective on the vertex positions
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
that rewards desired motions (e.g for a locomotion task, it can reward motion of the center of mass along a target direction).

The constraint Eq. (
2
) ensures the resulting motion abides the laws of soft-body physics with contact; The vertex positions at any point in time
\boldsymbol{x}|_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
must be the minimizers of the total energy
E(\boldsymbol{x}|_{t},\boldsymbol{d}|_{t})
of the physical system.
This physical energy can be split into two components,

(3)

\displaystyle E(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})=E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})+E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d}),

a passive component
E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
that captures motion according to inertia, external forces, and elastic forces, and an active component
E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})
that encourages the elastic body to take on the shape of
\boldsymbol{d}
.

On top of being non-linear in both
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{d}(t)
, Eq. (
1
) becomes especially difficult to solve for high resolution characters because the problem scales in complexity with the increased dimensionality of
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{d}(t)
.
The sour combination of high non-linearity and high dimensionality makes solving this problem an extremely slow process prone to large null spaces.

Our solution to these problems is to introduce an actuation subspace for
\boldsymbol{d}
based on the natural elastic vibration modes of the character
(
Pentland and Williams 1989
)
.
We combine this reduction with a separate simulation subspace for
\boldsymbol{x}
, built off Skinning Eigenmodes
(
Benchekroun et al. 2023
)
.
The separation of these two subspaces allows us to independently tailor each one to their own specific tasks, actuation and simulation, which have different desiderata.

We then show how to carry out simulation in these reduced spaces while remaining independent from the resolution of the mesh.
Our reduced-space framework allows us to quickly evaluate simulations of arbitrarily high resolution characters.

This speedup in simulation evaluation lends itself well to the use of a CMAES
(
Hansen 2006
)
optimizer in order to solve our control optimization Eq. (
1
).
This derivative-free genetic algorithm iteratively samples populations of
\boldsymbol{d}(t)
, which are used to drive multiple simulations.
The resulting objective
J
is measured for each simulation, and is used by CMAES to inform the collection of a next, more optimal generation of
\boldsymbol{d}(t)
.
The process is repeated until convergence, or until a maximum number of iterations are achieved.

3.1.
Modal Actuation Subspace

Figure 4
.
The first 5 non-rigid elastic vibration modes of a seal, corresponding to reasonable low-energy motions one could expect to see from a seal.

Without access to motion data for arbitrary meshed characters, the guiding principles for defining our spatial-temporal actuation subspace become energy efficiency and periodicity.

Drawing inspiration from modal analysis in computer animation
(
Pentland and Williams 1989
)
, we build our subspace starting with the vibration modes of the character.
These are deformations of the character that induce the
least
elastic energy, describing motions that the character
prefers
to take as visualized on a seal in Fig.
4
.
While these vibration modes have been used by many prior works for accelerating passive simulations
(
Barbič and James 2005
;
Trusty et al. 2023
)
, we show that they form an effective subspace for soft body character actuation.

Vibration modes are the solutions to the generalized eigenvalue problem of the elastic energy Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times 3n}
,

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\ ,

where the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times m}
form an
m
-dimensional basis of
spatial actuation modes
.
Above,
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times 3n}
is the diagonal vector-mass matrix and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

Deformed target positions
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
in this basis of actuation modes can be compactly represented by a low dimensional time-varying actuation vector
\boldsymbol{a}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
as well as the rest geometry
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
,

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{D}\quad\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{a}(t)\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\bar{\boldsymbol{D}}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}(t).

Where the
\bar{\mathrm{bar}}
denotes a homogeneous expression of this actuation space
\bar{\boldsymbol{D}}=[\boldsymbol{D}\;\boldsymbol{x}_{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times(m+1)}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}=[\boldsymbol{a}(t)\;1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
, which we employ to simplify notation on future expressions.

With the intuition that most organisms make use of periodic motion patterns for locomotion, we imbue our actuation subspace with periodicity.
Our temporal actuation subspace is defined via a sum of
k
sinusoids,

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j}^{k}\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\mathrm{sin}\left(2\pi\Big(\frac{t}{\boldsymbol{T}_{ij}}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ij}\Big)\right).

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{ij},\boldsymbol{T}_{ij},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ij}
are the amplitude, period and phase shift for each actuation mode
i
and sinusoidal function
j
, which are parameters that are either learned by Eq. (
1
), or set by a user.

3.2.
Reduced Simulation

With a low-dimensinoal actuation subspace in hand, we can actuate a soft body independently of its mesh resolution.
However, this resolution Independence does not transfer over to the simulation of the deformable, impeding forward simulation of high resolution characters.
Worse yet, optimizing the parameters of the actuation subspace by solving Eq. (
1
) requires many such forward simulations, becoming intractable as resolution increases.

For this reason, we also introduce a separate reduction of the soft body,
starting a linear Skinning Eigenmode Subspace
(
Benchekroun et al. 2023
)
for our vertex positions,

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{z}

where
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are the reduced space vertex positions and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times r}
being our Skinning Eigenmode subspace matrix.
We refer the reader to App.
A
for a refresher on the construction of this subspace.

With our two subspaces for simulation and actuation in hand, we must now rewrite the simulation optimization problem described in Eq. (
2
) entirely in our reduced spaces.
We restate the full-space optimization problem in question,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}}\;

\displaystyle E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})+E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})

with the goal of finding a method for solving this optimization problem, without ever touching the full resolution of the mesh.
Prior work
(
Barbič and James 2005
;
Jacobson et al. 2012
;
Brandt et al. 2018
;
Benchekroun et al. 2023
)
discusses extensively how to reduce passive soft body simulation and we refer the reader to App.
D
for details, summarizing that we largely follow the method of
Benchekroun et al. 2023
to arrive at a reduced passive approximation,

(8)

\displaystyle E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\tilde{E}_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}).

which can be evaluated entirely in a reduced space of
r
skinning eigenmodes and a set of
|\mathcal{C}_{p}|
passive rotation clusters, as visualized in the appendix on a tardigrade Fig.
12
.

3.2.1.
Reducing the Actuation Energy
E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})

We focus the remainder of our discussion on the actuation energy
E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})
.
This energy aims to make our simulated character
\boldsymbol{x}
take on a target shape
\boldsymbol{d}
, and has the following form
(
Ichim et al. 2017
)
,

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})=&\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\min_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}}\gamma_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}(\boldsymbol{d})||^{2}_{F}\\
&s.t.\;\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}\in\mathcal{SO}(3).\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})
corresponds to the deformation Jacobian of the simulated character
\boldsymbol{x}
, while
\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{d})
is the target deformation Jacobian of the actuation shape
\boldsymbol{d}
.

\gamma_{e}
and
V_{e}
are the per-element actuation stiffness and volume respectively, the former of which can be tuned to achieve a stronger/weaker character.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}
is a locally defined best fit rotation matrix, essential for filtering out rotations from our actuation;
an actuation signal
\boldsymbol{d}
should lead to the same motion regardless of how the simulated element shape
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and the target element shape
\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}(\boldsymbol{d})
are oriented.

This also imposes that our actuator conserves angular momentum, ensuring the character cannot supernaturally create an external torque.
This is not the case for a simple force-based actuation
(
Liang et al. 2023
)
which can introduce spurious linear and angular forces as shown in Fig.
3
.

Plugging our subspaces for simulated positions
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{z}
and actuation target
\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{a}
into this energy,

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{a}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{1}{2}&\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\min_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}}\gamma_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}(\boldsymbol{a})||^{2}_{F},\\
&s.t.\;\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}\in\mathcal{SO}(3),\end{split}

leads us to a disappointing result;
evaluating this energy requires the computation of the per-element best fit rotation matrix
\Omega_{e}
, requiring computation that scales with the number of tetrahedra in the character mesh.

3.2.2.
Clustering
\;\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{e}
for Mesh Independence of
E_{a}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a})

To avoid recomputing
\Omega_{e}
for each element in the mesh every simulation step, we derive a clustering scheme for our actuation energy, providing a reduction from the full number of elements
|\mathcal{T}|
.
Specifically, we allow for multiple elements to share the same rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c(e)}
, where
c(e)\in|\mathcal{C}_{a}|
identifies the actuation cluster
c
, in the set of actuation clusters
|\mathcal{C}_{a}|
to which an element
e
belongs.
Our approximation for the actuation energy becomes

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{d})\approx\tilde{E}_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c(e)}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}||^{2}_{F}\\
s.t.\;\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c(e)}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)\;\forall{c}\in|\mathcal{C}_{a}|.\end{split}

We prove in App.
G
that the optimal clustered rotation
\Omega_{c}
minimizing the objective above can be found via a polar decomposition of the
\gamma V
-weighed sum of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}
,

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}=\mathrm{polar}\left(\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}\right)=\mathrm{polar}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{c}\right),

where
\ \mathrm{polar}(\boldsymbol{H}_{c})
is a function performing polar decomposition on
\boldsymbol{H}_{c}
in order to achieve its rotational component
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c(e)}
.

While evaluating this term once again involves a per-element sum, we notice that this sum is a bilinear form in our two low-dimensional time-varying quantities,
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
With this intuition, we show in App.
F
that by precomputing the appropriate tensor we can efficiently evaluate this sum at run-time in a manner scaling only in complexity with our small
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
,

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{c}=\sum_{u}^{r}\sum_{v}^{m+1}\mathcal{H}_{cuv}z_{u}\bar{a}_{v}

Above, we have introduced the tensor
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|\times 3\times 3\times r\times(m+1)}
, which we derive in App.
F
, that when multiplied against
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
, computes our per-cluster covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{a}|\times 3\times 3}
.
Note that evaluating
\boldsymbol{H}_{c}
every stimulation step via this tensor product only requires operations that scale with the number of clusters
|\mathcal{C}_{a}|
, the dimension of our reduced positional DOFs
\boldsymbol{z}
, and the size of our actuation
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

As shown in Fig.
5
, a low number of actuation clusters allows for a more globally defined rotation invariance, with every character tetrahedron sharing the same rest frame.
This results in a simulated character that responds quickly to the actuation signal prescribed.
Increasing the number of clusters actuatues each tetrahedron locally, independently of its neighbors, resulting in a slower response to actuation.

Finally, with our newfound ability to quickly evaluate our rotations, we arrive at the final form of our reduced and clustered simulation optimization problem.

(14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{z}}\;

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{p}(\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{E}_{a}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a})

Where we have followed prior work in reducing per-element non-linearities that appear in the passive term
(
Jacobson et al. 2012
)
.

Figure 5.
Decreasing the number of actuation clusters results in a more globally actuated shape, accelerating the speed at which the simulation matches the target actuation signal.

3.3.
Local Global Solver

We make use of a local global solver to solve our reduced simulation optimization problem.
This kind of solver comes with the advantage of maintaining a constant energy Hessian, allowing us to compute a factorization for this matrix once, then reuse it throughout the entire rest of the optimization pipeline.
This is comprised of two main stages, a local step and a global step, that are repeated until a convergence criterion is met.

The
local
step first holds
\boldsymbol{z}
fixed and optimizes for
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}
using Eq. (
13
) and Eq. (
12
).
Then, the
global
step holds
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{t}
fixed, optimizing for
\boldsymbol{z}
. This is resolved using a linear solve we derive in App.
D
,

(15)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{f},

with
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
given by,

(16)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{p}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}^{p}+\boldsymbol{f}^{a}+\boldsymbol{f}^{c}.

The constant system matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}
is a sum of the passive energy Hessian
\boldsymbol{Q}^{p}
and our actuation energy Hessian
\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}
.
Details on the derivation of
\boldsymbol{Q}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}
can be found in App.
D
.

The right hand side to the system in Eq. (
15
)
\boldsymbol{f}
is a sum of the passive forces
f^{p}
, actuation forces
f^{a}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, and contact forces
\boldsymbol{f}^{c}(\boldsymbol{z})
.
These contact forces are found in a projective fashion such that the resulting simulation after the global step Eq. (
15
) satisfies our no interpenetration, damping friction contact conditions.
Collision detection and handling is performed on a subset of the mesh vertices to maintain reduced complexity, as visualized on a tardigrade in the appendix Fig.
12
.
More details on the computation of this contact force can be found in App.
E
.

Controller Optimization Speed

Our controller optimization is carried out entirely in a reduced space.
This allows us to obtain locomotions for characters of arbitrarily high resolutions, with the slowest mesh we collect statistics on in Table.
1
, the octopus, taking just under an hour to locomote.
In contrast, using simulation statistics from a state of the art full space simulator of
Trusty et al. 2022
on the smaller sized gecko mesh (a mesh smaller than all our examples), and using the fastest locomotion optimization parameters we’ve tested (200 simulation steps, 200 CMAES iterations, population size of 16), would take an expected optimization time of at least 17 hours.
Using this same analysis, all the examples in Table.
1
are generously at least 17x faster than if we had used a full space simulation to optimize for their locomotion.

Independence on Mesh Resolution

Building further on our reduced space complexity, Fig.
6
shows the optimized locomotions of two different
Arc de Triomphe
meshes: a visibly coarse one, and another that is finer by a factor of 10.
Despite large differences in resolutions the optimization time, as averaged over 5 different trials, is negligible between the two meshes.
In contrast, traditional full space local-global simulators
(
Bouaziz et al. 2014
)
have an
O(n^{2})
complexity to the resolution of the mesh, and an increase in resolution by a factor of 10 would result in a corresponding optimization time hit by a factor of 100.
Moreover, because our actuation subspace is defined via the elastic energy Hessian, which is convergent to the continuum of the geometry, we also get qualitatively similar locomotions across mesh resolutions.

Comparison to Different Actuation Subspaces

Fig.
7
compares our actuation subspace with one defined by a coarse embedding cage, as proposed by
Coros et al. 2012
.
A bat making use of these embedding cages can indeed find locomotions, however with a cage that is too low-dimensional, the locomotions make use of unnatural warping actuations, scaling/shearing the bat excessively.
Increasing the resolution of the cage to something that better conforms to the bat’s geometry quickly increase dimension of the actuation subspace (for 32 cage vertices, the actuation subspace has 96 DOFs).
This increased dimensionality makes the bat prone to finding locomotions with small, shuffling steps, an observation echoed by
(
Pan and Manocha 2018
)
.
In contrast, our modal actuations allow the bat to take more semantically meaningful deformations, such as flapping its wings, with only 3 actuation degrees of freedom.
Another factor that sets us apart from
Coros et al. 2012
is our use of a reduced order physics model, which allows us to find locomotions for 3D examples of much higher resolution than theirs.

Motion Style Control

Aside from allowing a user to select local bounds to each actuation mode, our actuation subspace provides other intuitive avenues for control.

By selecting which vibration modes to construct the actuation subspace, a user can generate different locomotion gaits.
For example, Fig.
8
shows a user picking different sets of actuation modes, one set that have the gorilla’s limbs moving asynchronously, and another with limbs moving in synchrony.
The resulting synchronicity of the limbs in the actuation subspace is intuitively reflected in the locomotion generated.

Yet another way to imbue user guidance into our framework is by having users constrain different parts of the temporal actuation parameters.
Fig.

shows a user specifying target periods our temporal sinusoids take on in order to generate locomotions varying in style.
Larger periods create more of a sluggish crawl for the octopus, while the smaller period makes the octopus skitter across the terrain with smaller steps.

Co-Optimizing for Actuations Modes

While we have found selecting the first 10 actuation modes as a default to usually generate viable locomotions, we can also ask our CMAES optimization to select
which
subset of the top
m
actuation modes to use.
This requires a simple tweak in our optimization, where we add to our optimization degrees of freedom an actuation mode participation vector
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, whose highest valued entries chose the selected actuation modes used in the optimization.
Note that these can rapidly be queried and updated throughout the optimization without requiring any full-space matrix recomputations.
Fig.
10
shows the resulting locomotion for the bat, changing the number of desired queried control modes from 2 to 3.
While both provide locomotion, querying for three modes unexpectedly converges to a less optimal solution than querying only for two.
This happens due to the non-linear nature of our optimization problem with respect to the actuation parameters, making it susceptible to local minima.

Note on Realism

The locomotions we obtain for many biologically inspired characters like the octopus are not
realistic
, in the sense that a real living octopus wouldn’t move forward by pushing against its back tentacles as shown in Fig.
9
.
This isn’t surprising, our actuation structure by design has no concept of the real-world anatomy of an octopus.
Instead, our actuation forms a fictional
virutal
musculature, generating motions that are

geometrically
plausible, requiring little manual effort from the user to achieve a viable locomotion.

Robustness Across Geometries

Finally, and most importantly, our method provides a framework for designing locomotions for characters of arbitrary geometry, without burdening the user with meticulous design of muscle fibers and bone connectivity
(
Tan et al. 2012
;
Geijtenbeek et al. 2013
;
Min et al. 2019
)
.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

We have shown that the pairing of a periodic energy efficient actuation subspace with a reduced space soft body simulation provides a simple but powerful framework for designing locomotion for highly detailed characters with arbitrary deformable geometries.

We have many exciting avenues for future work.
First, our actuation subspace is built entirely off the elastic vibration modes of the character in isolation.
However, we believe a stronger actuation subspace could be built if we additionally made us of prior information regarding expected contact points and forces.

While our local-global solver is fast for softer characters, these solvers are known to converge slowly for highly stiff elastic material.
If one wanted to model locomotions for characters composed of extremely stiff materials, like steel and bones,
we would recommend switching to other reduced solvers that are better equipped with dealing with such stiffnesses
(
Trusty et al. 2023
)
, which would come at the cost of abandoning our fast constant system factorization.

While we have evaluated the effectiveness of our method on open loop controllers, many of the roadblocks we have cleared along the way are also bottlenecks in training deep reinforcement learning-based closed loop controllers.
We are especially excited about the possibility combining our framework to train such state of the art controllers, for more dynamics and controllable tasks and making soft-body characters replace articulated rigid ones as the default in the field of character animation.

Appendix A
Skinning Eigenmode Positional Subspace

We reduce our positional degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{x}
with a linear Skinning Eigenmode subspace
(
Benchekroun et al. 2023
)
,

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{W})\boldsymbol{z}

where
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are the reduced space vertex positions.

\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{W})\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times r}
is a subspace matrix, parameterized by a set of Linear Blend Skinning weights
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{w}
, with
r=12w
.
We obtain these weights from a modal analysis on the elastic energy Laplacian,
\boldsymbol{L}=\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

(19)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}.

\boldsymbol{B}
is then constructed from
\boldsymbol{W}
via the equation:

(20)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{I}_{3}\otimes(\boldsymbol{W}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{4}^{T})\odot(\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{T}\otimes[\boldsymbol{X}\;\boldsymbol{1}_{n}]),

Where
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
is a matrix of stacked vertex positions at rest.

Appendix B
Full Space Passive Energy

The passive term is standardly comprised of a kinetic energy
E_{k}
, an external potential energy for gravity
E_{f}
, and an elastic potential energy which we choose to be ARAP
(
Sorkine and Alexa 2007
;
Kim and Eberle 2022
)
for all our examples
E_{v}
,

(21)

\displaystyle E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=E_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+E_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})+E_{v}(\boldsymbol{x})

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\frac{1}{2h^{2}}||\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}},\\
E_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{g},\\
E_{v}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\min_{\boldsymbol{R}_{e}}\mu_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{R}_{e}||^{2}_{F}\quad s.t.\;\boldsymbol{R}_{e}\in\mathcal{SO}(3).\end{split}

Above,
h
is the simulation timestep size,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
are inertial positions,
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
is the acceleration due to gravity acting on each vertex,
|\mathcal{T}|
is the set of all mesh elements,
\mu_{e}
and
V_{e}
are the per-element elastic stiffness and volume respectively.
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the per-element deformation Jacobian of the simulated character, while
\boldsymbol{R}_{e}
is its corresponding best fit rotation matrix.

Appendix C
Reducing the Passive Energy

We seek to detach the evaluation of the passive energy
E_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
, defined in App.
B
, with the resolution of the mesh.
We follow an approach similar to
(
Benchekroun et al. 2023
)
, where we approximate our full space positions
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{z}
, with the Skinning Eigenmode subspace from App.
A
.
To mitigate the evaluation of per-element rotations
\boldsymbol{R}_{e}
in the elastic potential energy, we make use of the clustering scheme from
(
Jacobson et al. 2012
)
, where multiple elements may share the same rotation.
We call these clusters
passive
, as they group together rotations from our passive elastic energy term. These are distinct from our
active
clusters, which also allow for elements in a cluster to share the same rotation matrix.

Our active term finds the rotation matrix that aligns
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})
to
\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}(\boldsymbol{a})
, whereas the passive term finds the rotation matrix that aligns
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})
to the identity.
A distinction that can incur significant implementational differences in reduction.

Plugging in our active positional subspace and our rotation clustering scheme brings us to the reduced form of the passive elastic energy, which can be evaluated entirely in a reduced space.

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{E}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\frac{1}{2h^{2}}||\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{q}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{N}}\\
\tilde{E}_{f}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{g},\\
\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{R}_{c}}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)}||^{2}_{F}\\
&\quad s.t.\;\boldsymbol{R}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)\forall c\in|\mathcal{C}_{p}|.\\
\end{split}

Appendix D
Global Step Derivation for Local-Global Solver

To solve for the character vertex locations in the reduced space
\boldsymbol{z}
, we make use of a local-global solver.
The local step optimize for a set of rotations both from our passive energy, and from our actuation energy, holding
\boldsymbol{z}
fixed. These optimal rotations are derived in App.
G
.
The global step requires us to optimize for
\boldsymbol{z}
, while holding our rotation variables fixed.

We state our reduced space optimization problem

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{z}}\;\tilde{E}_{p}(\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{E}_{a}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a})

(24)

\displaystyle=\tilde{E}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{E}_{f}(\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})+\tilde{E}_{a}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a})

with all of our reduced space energies defined as,

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{E}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\frac{1}{2h^{2}}||\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{q}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{N}}\\
\tilde{E}_{f}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{g},\\
\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})&=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{R}_{c}}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)}||^{2}_{F}\\
&\quad s.t.\;\boldsymbol{R}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)\forall c\in|\mathcal{C}_{p}|.\\
\tilde{E}_{a}(\,\boldsymbol{a})&=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c(e)}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}(\boldsymbol{a})||^{2}_{F}\\
&\quad s.t.\;\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)\forall c\in|\mathcal{C}_{a}|\end{split}

The global step assumes we’ve already found the minimizing rotations
\boldsymbol{R}_{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}
for the current iterate, and so we can treat these as constants and drop the two nested minimization problems for these two quantities, as well as the constraints that had appeared in the full energy Eq. (
11
).

Our strategy will be to expand all energies in Eq. (
25
) and drop any terms that explicitly do not depend on
\boldsymbol{z}
, as they have no bearing on our optimization task.

D.1.
Expanding the Elastic Energy
\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})

Starting with the passive energy in Eq. (
25
), we expand out the Frobenius norm and obtain,

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)}||^{2}_{F},

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\frac{\mu_{e}V_{e}}{2}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{e})-\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})+\mathrm{const}.

We deal with the first term
\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{e})
, and rewrite the integrand inside the sum in index notation, reintroducing our definition for
\boldsymbol{F}_{eij}={\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}z_{k}
from App.
F
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu_{e}V_{e}tr({\boldsymbol{F}_{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{e})&=\mu_{e}V_{e}({\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}z_{k})({\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijl})z_{l},\\
&=\mu_{e}V_{e}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijl}z_{k}z_{l},\\
&=Q^{v}_{ekl}z_{k}z_{l},\end{split}

with
Q^{v}_{ekl}=\mu_{e}V_{e}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijl}
.
Plugging this back into the sum gives us

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}Q^{v}_{ekl}z_{k}z_{l}

\displaystyle=z_{k}(\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}Q^{v}_{ekl})z_{l}=\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}\boldsymbol{z},

where we’ve exposed the Laplacian matrix for our actuation energy
\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}(\boldsymbol{V}^{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{9})\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and
\boldsymbol{V}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}|\times|\mathcal{T}|}
being the diagonal
\mu

V^{v}_{ee}=\mu_{e}V_{e}

We turn our attention to the second term in this energy,
\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})
.
With a similar treatment, we rewrite it in index notation,

(28)

\displaystyle\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})

\displaystyle=\mu_{e}V_{e}(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})_{ii},

\displaystyle=\mu_{e}V_{e}F_{ij}R_{c(e)ij},

\displaystyle=\mu_{e}V_{e}({\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}z_{k})(P_{ec}R_{cij}),

(29)

\displaystyle=(\mu_{e}V_{e}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}P_{ec})R_{cij}z_{k},

where we have made use of a
\mathcal{|\mathcal{T}|\times|\mathcal{C}|}
cluster selection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
to select out the tetrahedron inside each cluster.

(30)

\displaystyle P_{ec}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{if}\;e\in\mathcal{T}(c),\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}.

With this selection matrix in hand, we can rewrite the second term going through the full summation,

\displaystyle-\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})

\displaystyle=-z_{k}(\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}{\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{eijk}P_{ec})R_{cij},

\displaystyle=z_{k}K^{v}_{ijkc}R_{cij},

(31)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{z}^{T}(\mathcal{K}^{v}:\boldsymbol{R}),

Where we’ve revealed the tensor
\mathcal{K}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times 3\times 3\times|\mathcal{C}|}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{v}=\mathrm{reshape}(-\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}((\boldsymbol{V}^{v}\boldsymbol{P})\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{9}),\{r,3,3,|\mathcal{C}|\}).

Abstracting this away further we arrive at

(33)

\displaystyle-\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}|}\mu_{e}V_{e}tr(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}_{c(e)})=\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{v},

Finally, we can rewrite the reduced clustered passive muscle energy as a quadratic,

(34)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{v}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{v}+\mathrm{const},

D.5.
Putting it All Together

Assembling all the terms of our energy that depend on

(44)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{k})\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{z}^{T}(\boldsymbol{f}^{a}+\boldsymbol{f}^{v}+\boldsymbol{f}^{k}+\boldsymbol{f}^{f})+\mathrm{const}.

Taking the minimizer of this quadratic energy finally reveals the system (without contact) we need to solve every timestep:

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{k})\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{f}^{a}+\boldsymbol{f}^{v}+\boldsymbol{f}^{k})

(45)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{f}

Note that
\boldsymbol{Q}^{v}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{a}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{k}
are constant matrices, and so the system factorization can be solved once and reused throughout the course of the entire optimization problem.

shows our iteration step cost breakdown on the Arc de Triomphe character.

Appendix E
Computing Contact Forces
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}

To account for contact, we add an extra force term to the right hand side of Eq. (
45
),
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,

(46)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{f}_{c}.

This extra force term
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}
is computed every local-global iteration, before the global step to ensure
\boldsymbol{z}
upholds the zero interpenetration, damping friction conditions,

(47)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{N}\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=u\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}_{prev},

where
u
is a velocity damping coefficient,
\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|\times r}
is a contact Jacobian that maps from our reduced velocity
\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}
to per-contact point normal velocities.
Likewise
\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2|\mathcal{I}_{c}|\times r}
maps to tangential velocities.
The above two equations can be summarized by the constraint,

(48)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=\boldsymbol{v},

where
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{c}=[\boldsymbol{J}^{c}_{N}\;\boldsymbol{J}^{c}_{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3|\mathcal{I}|\times r}
is the full contact Jacobian and
\boldsymbol{v}=[\boldsymbol{0}\;u\boldsymbol{J}^{c}|_{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}_{prev}]^{T}
.

Through finite differences, we rewrite our constraint on the velocity to act on positions DOFs
\boldsymbol{z}
,

(49)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\frac{\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{curr}}{h}=\boldsymbol{v},\\
\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{z}=h\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{z}_{curr}.\end{split}

To compute an
\boldsymbol{f}
that ensures
\boldsymbol{z}
satisfies this condition, we plug in our global step solve
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{f}_{c})
Eq. (
46
) into this constraint and obtain,

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{f}_{c})&=h\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{z}_{curr},\\
\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}&=h\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{z}_{curr}-\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f},\\
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}&=\boldsymbol{b},\end{split}

With
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{b}=h\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{z}_{curr}-\boldsymbol{J}^{c}\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}
.
The matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a dense matrix, but it’s also small and constant, and so the product
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|\times r}
can be precomputed once and reused throughout the entire optimization procedure.
The system described by Eq. (
50
) can either be over or underdetermined, depending on whether the number of contacting degrees of freedom
3|\mathcal{I}|
is greater or less than the dimensionalilty of our simulation subspace
r
.

If it’s overdetermined, we solve for
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}
in a least squares sense,

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{f}_{c}}&||\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}-\boldsymbol{b}||^{2},\\
&\Downarrow\\
\boldsymbol{L}^{T}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}&=\boldsymbol{L}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.\end{split}

If it’s underdetermined, we solve for the smallest
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}
satisfying our constraint,

(52)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{f}_{c}}||\boldsymbol{f}_{c}||^{2}\quad&\mathrm{s.t.}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{c}=\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\Downarrow\\
\boldsymbol{f}_{c}&=\boldsymbol{L}^{T}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^{T})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}.\end{split}

Appendix F
Tensor Precomputation for Clustering Rotations

We start with the assumption that we have access to the simulation Skinning Eigenmode Subspace
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times r}
Eq. (
20
),
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times(m_{s}+1)}
obtained from Eq. (
4
),
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{9|T|\times 3n}
the standard deformation Jacobian mapping matrix
(
Kim and Eberle 2022
)
.

We construct the subspace deformation Jacobian mapping matrix
\boldsymbol{J}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{9|T|\times r}
and
\boldsymbol{J}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{9|T|\times(m_{s}+1)}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}^{B}=\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}^{D}=\boldsymbol{J}\bar{\boldsymbol{D}}

We reorder each of the entries in the form of a new 4-th order tensor
\mathcal{J}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|\times 3\times 3\times r}
and

\mathcal{J}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|\times 3\times 3\times(m_{s}+1)}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{B}=\mathrm{reshape}(\boldsymbol{J}^{B},\{|T|,3,3,r\}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\boldsymbol{D}}=\mathrm{reshape}(\boldsymbol{J}^{D},\{|T|,3,3,m_{s}+1\})

Which we can use to express the deformation Jacobian and the control target’s deformation Jacobian matrices,
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}|\times 3\times 3}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}|\times 3\times 3}
, using index notation,

(53)

\displaystyle F_{eij}=\mathcal{J}^{B}_{eijk}z_{k},

\displaystyle\quad Y_{eij}=\mathcal{J}^{D}_{eijl}\bar{a}_{l},

where a repeated index implies a component wise product along that dimension if the index is also present on the other side of the equation, or a standard dot product along that dimension if the index is not repeated on the other side of the equation.

We re-express
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}
, the matrix that appears inside the sum of Eq. (
70
),

\displaystyle C_{eij}

\displaystyle=F_{eik}Y_{ejk},

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{J}^{B}_{eiku}z_{u})(\mathcal{J}^{D}_{ejkv}\bar{a}_{v}),

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{J}^{B}_{eiku}\mathcal{J}^{D}_{ejkv})z_{u}\bar{a}_{v},

(54)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{eijuv}z_{u}\bar{a}_{v},

revealing the constant tensor
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}|\times 3\times 3\times r\times(m_{s}+1)}
, which, when multiplied against the changing
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
, provides us with the per-tet matrix product of
\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}
every timestep.

We then introduce a grouping matrix
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|\times|\mathcal{T}|}
, a matrix that assembles for each cluster a
\gamma
-weighed sum of its member tetrahedra’s scalar quantities.

(55)

\displaystyle G_{ce}=\begin{cases}\gamma_{e}&\mathrm{if}\;e\in\mathcal{T}(c)\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

This matrix allows us now to fully express the averaged covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|\times 3\times 3}
in index notation,

(56)

\displaystyle H_{cij}=G_{ce}C_{eij}

Finally, plugging in our expression for
C_{eij}
, we have

\displaystyle H_{cij}

\displaystyle=G_{ce}\mathcal{K}_{eijuv}z_{u}\bar{a}_{v}

(57)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{cijuv}z_{u}\bar{a}_{v}

Where we finally expose the constant tensor
\mathcal{H}_{cijuv}
, which multiplies
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
and provides the covariance matrices for all clusters
\boldsymbol{H}
.

Appendix G
Optimal Clustered Rotations

We want to derive the optimal rotation
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}
for each cluster
c
.
Starting from Eq. (
11
), computing the optimal rotation
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}
for each
c
amounts to the following optimization problem

(58)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}\sum_{e}^{|T(c)|}\gamma_{t}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}||_{F}^{2}

As presented in Eq. (
12
), we will show that the optimal solution is given by the polar decomposition:

(59)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}=\mathrm{polar}\left(\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}\right).

The same derivation for the active clustered rotation
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}
can be extended to the optimal rotation
\boldsymbol{R}
for the passive term
E_{v}
by setting
\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}=\boldsymbol{I}_{3\times 3}
for every tetrahedral element
e
.

Proof

We start by expanding each term in the summation of Eq. (
59
) as

(60)

\displaystyle\gamma_{e}V_{e}||\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}||_{F}^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{e}V_{e}tr\left((\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e})^{T}(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}-{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e})\right)

(62)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{e}V_{e}tr\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}+\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}-\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}^{T}F_{e}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{e}V_{e}tr\left(2\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\right)+\mathrm{const}

(64)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{e}V_{e}tr\left(2{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\right)+\mathrm{const}

Above, in the second equality we made use of the identity
||\boldsymbol{A}||^{2}_{F}=tr(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})
.
In the third equality we expanded the multiplication, and used the orthogonality of rotation matrices
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}=\boldsymbol{I}
.
In the fourth equality we dropped terms that do not depend on the variable we are optimizing over and placed them inside the constant
c
.
In the fifth and final equality we have made use of the trace’s invariance under permutation of its input.
We’ve also used the identity that the trace is invariant under transposes
tr(A+A^{T})=tr(2A)
.

With the above result, we return to our full optimization problem (Eq. (
59
)) and rewrite it as:

(65)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}\sum_{e}^{|T(c)|}-\gamma_{e}V_{e}2tr\left({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\right)+\mathrm{const}

(66)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}\sum_{e}^{|T(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}tr\left({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}tr\left(\sum_{e}^{|T(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}tr\left({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\sum_{e}^{|T(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\in\mathcal{SO}(3)}tr\left({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}\boldsymbol{H}_{c}\right)

In the second equality we have dropped the
\mathrm{const}
and switched to a maximization instead of a minimization, while also dropping the factor of 2 from the optimization.
In the third equality we have made use of the linearity of the trace operator and the sum operator, allowing them to commute.
In the fourth equality, we finally pull out the per-cluster rotation
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}
from the sum, as all tets in
|T(c)|
share the same rotation matrix by definition.
This final form is an instance of the
Orthogonal Procrustes
problem, where the target matrix we are trying to match is
\boldsymbol{H}_{c}
.
Based on the proof in
Schoenemann 1964
;
Wikipedia contributors 2023
, the optimal solution to the Orthogonal Procrustes problem can be obtained from the polar decomposition of
\boldsymbol{H}_{c}
.

(70)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{c}=\mathrm{polar}(\boldsymbol{H}_{c})=\mathrm{polar}\left(\sum_{e}^{|\mathcal{T}(c)|}\gamma_{e}V_{e}\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}_{e}^{T}\right)

∎
