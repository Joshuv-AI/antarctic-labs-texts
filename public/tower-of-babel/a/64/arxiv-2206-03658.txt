Reviving the Vortex Particle Method: A Stable Formulation for Meshless Large Eddy Simulation
Eduardo J. Alvarez, Andrew Ning

Abstract
The vortex particle method (VPM) is a mesh-free approach to computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solving the Navier-Stokes equations in their velocity-vorticity form. The VPM uses a Lagrangian scheme, which not only avoids the hurdles of mesh generation, but it also conserves vortical structures over long distances with minimal numerical dissipation while being orders of magnitude faster than conventional mesh-based CFD. However, VPM is known to be numerically unstable when vortical structures break down close to the turbulent regime. In this study, we reformulate the VPM as a large eddy simulation (LES) in a scheme that is numerically stable, without increasing its computational cost. A new set of VPM governing equations are derived from the LES-filtered Navier-Stokes equations. The new equations reinforce conservation of mass and angular momentum by reshaping the vortex elements subject to vortex stretching. In addition to the VPM reformulation, a new anisotropic dynamic model of subfilter-scale (SFS) vortex stretching is developed. This SFS model is well suited for turbulent flows with coherent vortical structures where the predominant cascade mechanism is vortex stretching. Extensive validation is presented, asserting the scheme comprised of the reformulated VPM and SFS model as a meshless LES that accurately resolves large-scale features of turbulent flow. Advection, viscous diffusion, and vortex stretching are validated through simulation of isolated and leapfrogging vortex rings. Mean and fluctuating components of turbulent flow are validated through simulation of a turbulent round jet, where Reynolds stresses are resolved directly and compared to experimental measurements. Finally, the computational efficiency of the scheme is showcased in the simulation of an aircraft rotor in hover, showing our meshless LES to be 100x faster than a mesh-based LES with similar fidelity, while being 10x faster than a low-fidelity unsteady Reynolds-average Navier-Stokes simulation and 1000x faster than a high-fidelity detached-eddy simulation. The implementation of our meshless LES scheme is hereby released as an open-source software, called FLOWVPM .

I
Introduction

Large eddy simulation (LES) is a class of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) that filters the Navier-Stokes equations to decompose small-scale fluctuations of the flow from large ones.
The large scales are then resolved directly, while the effects of the smaller scales are modeled.
The most common LES methods use a mesh or grid to discretize the space and calculate fluxes and derivatives, classified as finite volume/element/difference methods.
Significant user effort is spent in the generation and manipulation of the mesh, with studies showing that about 67% of engineering time in mesh-based CFD is spent in these efforts.
Hardwick et al. 2005

Vortex methods
Leonard 1980
;
Cottet and Koumoutsakos 2000a
;
Winckelmans 2004
;
Mimeau and Mortazavi 2021
are a class of meshless CFD solving the Navier-Stokes equations in their velocity-vorticity form.
This form is solved in a Lagrangian scheme, which not only avoids the hurdles of mesh generation, but also conserves vortical structures over long distances with minimal numerical dissipation.

The vortex particle method
Winckelmans and Leonard 1993
(VPM) is a vortex method that uses particles to discretize the Navier-Stokes equations, with the particles representing radial basis functions that construct a continuous vorticity field
Barba 2004
.
This meshless CFD has several advantages over conventional mesh-based CFD.
In the absence of a mesh, the VPM (1) does not suffer from the conventional Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, (2) does not suffer from the numerical dissipation introduced by a mesh, (3) derivatives are calculated exactly rather than approximated through a stencil, and (4) it has been shown to be 100x to 1000x faster than mesh-based CFD with comparable fidelity
Alvarez et al. 2020
.
Furthermore, the VPM is spatially second-order accurate
Yokota and Barba 2013
, it is highly efficient since elements are placed only where vorticity exists, the spatial discretization can be automatically adapted
Barba, Leonard, and Allen 2005a
;
Lakkis and Ghoniem 2009
;
Stock and Gharakhani 2021
in the fashion of an adaptive mesh refinement, and simulations are highly-parallelizable in heterogeneous CPU and GPU architectures
Yokota et al. 2013
;
Hu et al. 2014
.

, distributed electric propulsion

.

In spite of its growing popularity, VPM is known to be numerically unstable when vortical structures break down close to the turbulent regime.
This has limited its range of applications in the aforementioned studies to mostly benign cases with well-behaved numerics (
e.g.,
coarse simulations of rotors with axial inflow).
The instability is triggered when vortex stretching gives raise to a rapid increase of local vorticity, which the low numerical dissipation of the method fails to damp out.
Such numerical instability is believed to be caused by a combination of Lagrangian distortion, a vorticity field that is not divergence-free, and the absence of subfilter-scale (SFS) turbulent diffusion, among other reasons.

Multiple meshless schemes have been developed over the years to address Lagrangian distortion
Barba, Leonard, and Allen 2005b
;
Lakkis and Ghoniem 2009
;
Kirchhart and Rieger 2021
, divergence of the vorticity field
Pedrizzetti 1992
;
Gharakhani 1997
, and SFS effects
Gharakhani 2002
;
Gharakhani 2005
.
While some of these schemes are effective in two-dimensional cases, they have not succeeded at making the three-dimensional VPM numerically stable.
The need to circumvent this challenge motivated further development of the vortex-in-cell (VIC) method
Christiansen 1997
;
Cottet and Poncet 2004
;
Koumoutsakos 2005
, also known as vortex particle-mesh method.
In this method, the particles are projected onto a background mesh at every time step, and vortex stretching, viscous diffusion, and the Biot-Savart law are computed in a mesh-based scheme.
This approach has shown to be numerically stable, recently enabling the study of wake dynamics with unprecedented fidelity.
Chatelain et al. 2008
;
Chatelain et al. 2017
;
Caprace, Chatelain, and Winckelmans 2020
;
Nguyen 2019
;
Nguyen and Duong 2021
;
Colognesi, Ronsse, and Chatelain 2021
;
Ramos-García et al. 2021

However, the introduction of this mesh also forfeits a few of the aforementioned benefits of a purely Lagrangian (meshless) scheme.

In this study, we propose an LES formulation of the VPM that is both numerically stable and meshless.
A new set of VPM governing equations are derived from the LES-filtered Navier-Stokes equations in
Section

II
.
In
Section

III
, the classic VPM is shown to be only one out of multiple possible formulations of these governing equations.
Such formulation is shown to locally violate conservation of mass and angular momentum under vortex stretching, explaining why the classic VPM tends to be numerically unstable.
In
Section

IV
, we consider multiple possible formulations using different element shapes and conservation laws.
One formulation is selected, which uses the particle size to reinforce local conservation of both mass and angular momentum.
This formulation, referred to as the
reformulated VPM
, is implemented and hereby released as the open-source software
FLOWVPM
.
*
*

*

The code is available at
https://github.com/byuflowlab/FLOWVPM.jl

In addition to the reformulated VPM, a novel anisotropic structural model of subfilter-scale (SFS) vortex stretching is developed in
Section

V
.
The means for backscatter control are also provided, along with a dynamic procedure for the automatic computation of the model coefficient.
This SFS model is apt for both meshless and mesh-based CFD, and is well suited for turbulent flows with coherent vortical structures where the predominant cascade mechanism is vortex stretching.

The scheme comprised of the reformulated VPM and SFS model is extensively tested in
Section

VI
, validating the scheme as an LES method.
Advection, viscous diffusion, and vortex stretching are validated through simulation of isolated and leapfrogging vortex rings.
Large-scale turbulent dynamics are validated through simulation of a turbulent round jet, where predicted fluctuations and Reynolds stress are compared to experimental measurements.
Finally, the computational efficiency of our meshless LES is showcased in an engineering application through the simulation of an aircraft rotor in hover.

II
Fundamentals of

the Vortex Particle Method

A new set of VPM governing equations are hereby derived from the LES-filtered Navier-Stokes equations in their vorticity form.
Along the way, the derivation here presented introduces all the fundamental concepts necessary to bring the reader up to speed with LES and vortex methods.

II.1
Vorticity Form of the Navier-Stokes Equations

In a Newtonian, incompressible fluid with constant viscosity, the linear momentum of a differential fluid element is governed by the Navier-Stokes equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\textbf{u}}}{\partial t}+({\textbf{u}}\cdot\nabla){\textbf{u}}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}{\textbf{u}},

(1)

where
{\textbf{u}}({\textbf{x}},t)
is the velocity field,
p({\textbf{x}},t)
is the pressure field, and
\rho
and
\nu
are the density and kinematic viscosity of the fluid, respectively.

Taking the curl over
Eq.

1
, the pressure dependence disappears obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{\text{D}}{\text{D}t}\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla){\textbf{u}}+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega},

(2)

where
\frac{\text{D}}{\text{D}t}
denotes the material derivative operator,
\frac{\text{D}}{\text{D}t}()\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}()+{({\textbf{u}}\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ \nabla)()}
, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}({\textbf{x}},t)=\nabla\times{\textbf{u}}({\textbf{x}},t)
is the vorticity field.

Eq.

2
is the vorticity form of the Navier-Stokes linear-momentum equation.
This equation depends on
\boldsymbol{\omega}
alone since
u
can be calculated from
\boldsymbol{\omega}
through the Biot-Savart law.
While
Eq.

1
stems from conservation of linear momentum,
Eq.

2
can be interpreted as some form of conservation of angular momentum
Chatwin 1973
, which is further discussed in
Section

IV.1
.
From the right-hand side, we see that the evolution of the vorticity field is governed by vortex stretching (first term) and viscous diffusion (second term).

II.2
Large Eddy Simulation Equation

We now derive the filtered vorticity Navier-Stokes equation—here referred to as
large-eddy simulation (LES) equation
—and define the stress tensor that transfers energy between resolved scales and subfilter scales.

Let
\phi
be a field and
\zeta_{\sigma}
a filter kernel, the filtered field is denoted by a bar and defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\mathbf{y})\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

where the filter
\zeta_{\sigma}
is associated to a certain cutoff length scale
\sigma
.
In this study,
\zeta_{\sigma}
is defined as
\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}}\zeta(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|}{\sigma})
where
\zeta
is a radial basis function, and is required to have a volume integral of unity,
\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=1
.

In order to derive the LES version of the vorticity equation,
Eq.

2
is written in tensor notation and filtered as

\displaystyle\overline{\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial t}}+\overline{u_{j}\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}=\overline{\omega_{j}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}+\nu\overline{\nabla^{2}\omega_{i}}.

(3)

In this equation,
\overline{u_{j}\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}
and
\overline{\omega_{j}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}
are non-linear terms that cannot be calculated from resolved quantities, but are rather approximated through a tensor
T_{ij}
that encapsulates the error between
\overline{u_{i}\omega_{j}}
and
\overline{u_{i}}\,\overline{\omega_{j}}
as

\displaystyle\overline{u_{i}\omega_{j}}=\overline{u_{i}}\,\overline{\omega_{j}}+T_{ij}.

The gradient of
T_{ij}
and its transpose result in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}=\overline{u_{j}\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}-\overline{u_{j}}\frac{\partial\overline{\omega_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}=\overline{\omega_{j}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}-\overline{\omega_{j}}\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}

Replacing this into
Eq.

3
and using the derivative-filter commutation property, we obtain the LES vorticity equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{\omega_{i}}}{\partial t}+\overline{u_{j}}\frac{\partial\overline{\omega_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=

(
4
)

\displaystyle\overline{\omega_{j}}\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle+\nu\nabla^{2}\overline{\omega_{i}}-\frac{\partial T^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}.

The term
\frac{\partial T^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
represents the subfilter-scale (SFS) contributions arising from the advective term (vorticity advection), while
\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
represents the contributions arising from vortex stretching.

T_{ij}
is associated to the SFS vorticity stress that encapsulates the interactions between large scale dynamics and SFS dynamics and has to be modeled in terms of resolved quantities.
The accuracy of LES hinges on the modeling of this tensor.
Its divergence represents the rate at which enstrophy—a measure of rotational kinetic energy—is transferred from resolved scales to subfilter scales (diffusion) and from subfilter scales to resolved scales (backscatter).
In
Section

V
we will develop a model that approximates the vortex-stretching component of this tensor, and provide the means for backscatter control.

For convenience, we write
Eq.

4
in vector notation as

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\nu\nabla^{2}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}},

(5)

where
\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}\right)_{i}\equiv\frac{\partial T^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
is the SFS vorticity advection,
\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}\right)_{i}\equiv-\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
is the SFS vortex stretching, and the
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}
operator is a linearized version of the filtered material derivative,
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}()\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}()+{(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot\nabla)()}
.

II.3
Viscous Diffusion

One common practice for solving non-linear partial differential equations (PDE), like
Eq.

5
, is that of splitting the PDE operator into a linear sum of its non-linear pieces.
This permits discretizing and solving each non-linear piece in a separate numerical scheme.
In this study we will split the PDE in
Eq.

5
into inviscid and viscous pieces as

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{inviscid}}+\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{viscous}},

where

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{inviscid}}

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{viscous}}

\displaystyle=\nu\nabla^{2}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}.

Over the years, multiple Lagrangian schemes have been developed that accurately resolve the viscous component, like the vortex redistribution method
Shankar and Dommelen 1996
;
Gharakhani 2001
, particle strength exchange
Degond and Mas-Gallic 1989
, and core spreading
Rossi 1996
, to name a few.
In this study, viscous diffusion will be solved through the core spreading method coupled with the radial basis function interpolation approach for spatial adaptation developed by Barba
Barba, Leonard, and Allen 2005b
;
Barba 2004
;
Torres and Barba 2009
.
This viscous scheme diffuses the vorticity by thickening each particle’s core size
\sigma
over time, while using an RBF interpolation to reset core sizes when they have overgrown.
As shown by Rossi
Rossi 1996
, the core spreading method has second-order spatial convergence, while showing linear convergence when coupled with spatial adaptation.

In the following sections we will focus on the inviscid part of the PDE, developing a scheme for its numerical solution.

II.4
Lagrangian Discretization: The Vortex Particle

:
Vortex particle with unit size and strength: (left) core size
\sigma

\Gamma

The material derivative in
Eq.

2
and the material-conservative nature of the vorticity makes the
\boldsymbol{\omega}
field especially well fit for a Lagrangian description.
We now discretize the vorticity equation with Lagrangian elements, termed
vortex particles
.
Each particle represents a volume of fluid that is convected by the velocity field carrying an integral quantity of vorticity.

The unfiltered
\boldsymbol{\omega}
field is discretized with singular vortex particles of positions
{\textbf{x}}_{p}
and coefficients
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
, approximating
\boldsymbol{\omega}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}({\textbf{x}},t)\approx\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}(t)\delta({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{x}}_{p}(t)),

(6)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta.
Each particle travels with the local velocity as

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\textbf{x}}_{p}={\textbf{u}}({\textbf{x}}_{p}),

where
{\textbf{x}}_{p}
is the position of the
p
-th particle.
Thus, each coefficient
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
(termed
vortex strength
) represents the average vorticity that is carried in the volume of each particle since

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}({\textbf{x}},t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\approx\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}(t).

Using the singular particle approximation in the filtered vorticity
\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\delta({\textbf{y}}-{\textbf{x}}_{p})\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

we obtain an approximation of the filtered vorticity field,

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\approx\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{p}).

(7)

As seen in Eq. (
7
), the filter operator has the effect of spreading the vortex strength
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
in space, regularizing the singularity originally introduced by the Dirac delta in Eq. (
6
).
Thus, the filter kernel takes the role of a basis function that is used to discretize and approximate the filtered vorticity field through particles.
We let the filter width
\sigma
change in time and space according to the evolution of each individual particle.
Here on, the filter width is referred to as
smoothing radius
or
core size
, denoted
\sigma_{p}
, and defined as
\sigma_{p}(t)\equiv\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{p},t)
.
We approximate the filtered vorticity as
\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\approx\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\sigma}
, with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}(t)\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{p}(t))

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{p}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{p}^{3}(t)}\zeta\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{p}(t)\|}{\sigma_{p}(t)}\right).

Replacing the original filter
\zeta_{\sigma}
with the variable-width filter
\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}
introduces an error from commuting filter and differentiation operators in the LES equations.
However, this error is second order in the filter width
Ghosal and Moin 1995
, hence it is assumed to be negligible or part of the discretization error of the VPM.
Also,
Eq.

7
introduces numerical issues by approximating
\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
through a field that is in general not divergence-free.
Cottet
Cottet and Koumoutsakos 2000a
suggested that the divergence of
\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
arises from unphysical small scales produced by the computation, which could be properly addressed with subfilter-scale diffusion.
The divergence of
\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
will be treated using the relaxation proposed by Pedrizzetti
Pedrizzetti 1992
, which, as shown in
Section

VI
, will be sufficient to attain both numerical stability and physical accuracy with the LES formulation and subfilter-scale model developed in this study.

The rotational part of the velocity field is calculated from the particles using the Helmholtz decomposition
\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi}
where
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is some vector potential.
This is done by analytically solving the Poisson equation
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}=-\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, which solution is a regularized Biot-Savart law.

2
shows a vortex particle with unit size and strength, the spreading of the vortex strength by the filter kernel, and the resulting velocity field.

II.5
General VPM Governing Equations

We will now use vortex particles to discretize the LES vorticity equation,
Eq.

5
, and derive the equations governing the evolution of the Lagrangian elements.
For ease of notation, here on we denote the filtered velocity field
\overline{\mathbf{u}}
simply
\mathbf{u}
and use
\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{D}t}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
interchangeably.
Also, time dependence is no longer explicitly indicated, but
\mathbf{x}_{p}
,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
, and
\sigma_{p}
are time-dependent variables.

Starting from the inviscid part of the LES-filtered vorticity equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}},

we write the filter operator explicitly,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left[\left(\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right).

Using the singular particle approximation,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}({\textbf{y}})\approx\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\delta({\textbf{y}}-{\textbf{x}}_{q}),

both integrals collapse resulting in

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)=

(
8
)

\displaystyle\left[\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\right]{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}({\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}({\mathbf{x}}).

With the help of
Appendix

A
, the left-hand side unfolds as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{q}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{q}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}}{\partial t}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\left[\left(\mathbf{u}({\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{u}({\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right\}

while vortex stretching is rearranged as

\displaystyle\left[\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\right]{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum\limits_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right){\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}}).

Evaluating at the position of the

-th particle,
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{p}
, and pulling the

-th term out of each sum,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}({\mathbf{0}})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}}{\partial t}({\mathbf{0}})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum\limits_{q\neq p}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}}{\partial t}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\left[\left(\mathbf{u}({\mathbf{x}}_{p})-\mathbf{u}({\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right\}

and

\displaystyle\left[\left(\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\right]{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}({\mathbf{0}})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right){\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p})+\sum\limits_{q\neq p}\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{x}}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right){\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}).

Thus,
Eq.

8

II.6
Numerical Schemes

In

Yokota and Barba 2011
;
Wang, Yokota, and Barba 2021

Bezanson et al. 2017

III
Classic Vortex Particle Method

Before further exploring the general equation governing vortex stretching,
Eq.

14
, we pause to analyze the equation that has been used extensively in the literature throughout the years:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p}).

(16)

We denote this equation as the
Classic VPM
.

Multiple variations of
Eq.

16
have been used over the years.
For instance, Gharakhani
Gharakhani 1997
introduced a new term accounting for the divergence of the approximated vorticity field, Winckelmans and Leonard
Winckelmans and Leonard 1993
replaced the differential operator in vortex stretching with its transpose, and Mansfield et al.
Mansfield, Knio, and Meneveau 1998
;
Mansfield, Knio, and Meneveau 1999
and Cottet
Cottet 1996
reintroduced the SFS contributions.
However, all these variations and the classic equation have in common that they all neglect
\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}}{\partial t}
and are free of the interdependence posed by the
\mathbf{M}
-terms in
Eq.

14
.

We now delineate the assumption underlying the classic VPM that will justify neglecting the
\mathbf{M}
-terms, termed
localized-vorticity assumption
, as follows.

Given a vorticity field
\boldsymbol{\omega}
that is compact in a small volume
\mathrm{Vol}
, the average vorticity
\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>
is calculated as

\displaystyle\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>=\frac{\int\limits_{\mathrm{Vol}}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}}{\mathrm{Vol}}.

The field can be approximated through a radially-symmetric field
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>\mathrm{Vol}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}),

where
\zeta_{\sigma}
is a radial basis function of spread
\sigma
and center
\mathbf{x}_{0}
approximating the vorticity distribution of the original field
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
If
\zeta_{\sigma}
is normalized such as to have a volume integral of unity,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

approximates
\boldsymbol{\omega}
in an average sense since

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathrm{Vol}}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Defining
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>\mathrm{Vol}
, the localized-vorticity field
\boldsymbol{\omega}
can be approximated with a single particle as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\approx\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}).

Replacing this in the inviscid part of the vorticity equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla){\mathbf{u}},

evaluating at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
, and following similar steps as in
Section

II.5
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla\right){\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})+3\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}.

(17)

This is the evolution equation of the vortex strength approximating the field of localized vorticity, discretized with only one particle.
In contrast, in
Section

II.5
we showed that a more general vorticity field that is discretized using multiple particles leads to a governing equation that is slightly different:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})

\displaystyle+3\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\frac{1}{\sigma_{p}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}}{\partial t}

(
18
)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}({\mathbf{0}})}(-\mathbf{M}_{p}^{0}+\mathbf{M}_{p}^{1}+\mathbf{M}_{p}^{2}),

after ignoring the SFS contributions.
Here, the
\mathbf{M}
-terms, as defined in
Eqs.

10
,
11
and
12
, pose a dependence on neighboring particles that arises from having filtered the vorticity equation.

Discarding these
\mathbf{M}
-terms is equivalent to assuming that the vorticity field can be approximated by the superposition of blobs of fluid with compact vorticity that evolve somewhat independently from each other.
‡
‡

‡

The interactions neglected here are only the ones pertaining to the evolution of vortex strength, while other interactions are still accounted for in the convection and viscous diffusion of the particles.

This assumption, which we call
localized-vorticity assumption
, reduces

Eq.

17

In both derivations of the classic VPM by Winckelmans and Leonard

and Cottet and Koumutsakos

, these
\mathbf{M}
-terms are not present due to the construction of the method:
In the classic derivation, the unfiltered vorticity equation (
Eq.

2

Eq.

5

In order to bring both approaches into agreement, in
Section

IV

\mathbf{M}
-terms.

Interestingly, discarding the
\mathbf{M}
-terms resembles the LES decomposition approach of truncated basis functions
Meneveau and Katz 2000

In such an approach, the flow field is expanded using orthonormal basis functions.
The summation of bases is then truncated to define the large-scale field, and the discarded modes represent the range of subfilter scales.
In the localized-vorticity assumption, the sum over all the particles is truncated after the leading term,
p
, while neglecting the contributions of neighboring particles.
Hence, the localized-vorticity assumption can be regarded as a secondary LES filter with the neglected
\mathbf{M}
-terms becoming part of the subfilter-scale contributions.

While it is justifiable to neglect the
\mathbf{M}
-terms through the localized-vorticity assumption, it is unclear to us what the basis is for the classic VPM to assume
\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}}{\partial t}=0
.
In fact, we hypothesize that this last assumption is the cause of the numerical instabilities that pervade the classic VPM.

The classic VPM simply regards
\sigma
as a numerical parameter with no physical significance.
However, Leonard

\sigma

Ojima and Kamemoto 2000

Kornev, Denev, and Samarbakhsh 2020

Even though these authors let

\frac{\partial\sigma_{p}}{\partial t}=0

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}

In the following chapter we propose a formulation that uses

in the governing equation of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}

IV.1
Physical Implications of the Vorticity Equation

In
Section

II.1
we showed that the linear-momentum Navier-Stokes equation can be transformed into an expression that only depends on vorticity, namely

2

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}.

(
2

We now point our attention to two laws that follow from

The discussion that follows is inspired by the writing of P. A. Davidson

Davidson 2019

IV.1.1
Conservation of Angular Momentum

Consider a spherical differential fluid element carrying a mean vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with moment of inertia
I
.
Due to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, the element is then rotating at an angular velocity of
\boldsymbol{\omega}/2
and its angular momentum
\mathbf{L}
is calculated as
(
Batchelor 1967
, p. 82)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\frac{I\boldsymbol{\omega}}{2}.

(19)

Given that the element is spherical (and before strain distorts the element), the pressure field exerts no torque on the element and the only torque-producing forces are due to viscous effects, namely
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{viscous}}
.
The change of angular momentum is then calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{L}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{viscous}}.

(20)

Replacing
Eq.

19
in
Eq.

20
, we arrive to the following expression of the total derivative of vorticity
§
§

§

More generally, this equation should be expressed with an inertia tensor, but for a spherical body this tensor reduces to a scalar due to the symmetry of the body.
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\omega}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{I}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}I+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{viscous}}^{*},

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{viscous}}^{*}=2\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{viscous}}/I
.
When viscous effects are ignored,
Eq.

21
implies that, in order to conserve angular momentum, the angular velocity (or vorticity) must decrease whenever the moment of inertia increases, and vice versa.

Eqs.

2
and
21
suggest that the vorticity Navier-Stokes equation is simply an expression of the conservation of angular momentum in a spherical fluid element
Chatwin 1973
.
Furthermore, the first term in the right hand side of
Eq.

2
accounts for the change of moment of inertia as

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{I}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}I,

(22)

which leads to an increase/decrease of vorticity to conserve momentum as the moment of inertia decreases/increases.
Hence,
(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}
is referred to as vortex stretching as it accounts for the deformation exerted by the velocity field on the fluid element, intensifying the vorticity in the direction that the element is stretched, as illustrated in

IV.1.2
Conservation of Mass

Another physical implication of
Eq.

2
is the existence of vortex lines and vortex tubes, described as follows.
We define a short material line
\boldsymbol{\ell}
that moves with the fluid, with start and end points
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\ell}
, respectively.
If
\boldsymbol{\ell}
is infinitesimally small, the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\ell}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\ell}=\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)-\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right).

(23)

Defining
\boldsymbol{\ell}
as being tangent to the local vorticity, and comparing
Eq.

23
to the inviscid part of
Eq.

2
, we see that vorticity evolves as the material line, being identically stretched and reoriented by the velocity field.
This led Helmholtz to conclude the existence of lines of vorticity, termed
vortex lines
, that move with the fluid.
We then define a
vortex tube
as the surface formed by all the vortex lines passing through a closed curve, depicted in
Fig.

4
.

The fact that vortex lines move with the fluid implies that the volume of a vortex tube segment must be conserved in incompressible flow.
For instance, let us discretize and approximate a vortex tube through Lagrangian cylindrical elements of length
\ell
and cross section
r
.
As the length of the element is stretched, the cross section
r
must shrink to conserve the same volume
\mathrm{Vol}=\pi r^{2}\ell
. This is,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Vol}=0,

leading to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}r=-\frac{r}{2\ell}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ell.

(24)

IV.1.3
Implications for the VPM

The aforementioned laws of conservation point our attention to two major pitfalls of the classic VPM, if particles are considered as material elements.
First,
Eq.

22
establishes that vortex stretching must lead to a change in the shape of the fluid element.
Otherwise, if the vorticity (angular velocity) has changed due to vortex stretching while the shape of the element (moment of inertia) is kept constant, the conservation of angular momentum has been locally violated.
This is exactly the case when the classic VPM assumes a constant core size,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma=0
.

Second, in an incompressible tube of vorticity,
Eqs.

23
and
2
establish that an increase in vorticity due to vortex stretching is associated to a lengthening of the tube, which must cause the cross section of the tube to shrink according to
Eq.

24
.
Otherwise, the tube has kept a constant cross section while being stretched, thus locally violating conservation of mass.
Again, this is exactly the case when the classic VPM assumes
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma=0
.

In summary, we believe that the classic VPM violates conservation of both angular momentum and mass when it assumes
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma=0
.
We also hypothesize that this is the reason for its poor numerical stability.
In the following sections we will consider some possible formulations that use
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma
as a degree of freedom to locally reinforce these conservation laws.

IV.2.3
Other Formulations

Combining the two conservations laws with the two element shapes, one can devise up to six different formulations: Each permutation between sphere/tube elements, momentum/mass conservation, and the assumption that mass conservation has or has not been already ensured by the convection of the elements.
Two of them have already been discussed and turn out to be the same.
For completeness, we now derive and briefly discuss the four remaining formulations.

Mass-Conserving Sphere

The mass of a spherical fluid element is conserved in incompressible flow as long as its volume remains constant.
This translates into vortex particles of constant radii,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma_{p}=0,

(31)

which reduces the vortex strength equation to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p}).

Notice that these are the equations of the classic VPM.
Even though this formulation preserves mass of spherical chunks of the flow, it is deemed unphysical as it violates local conservation of mass when the vorticity field encompasses a tube of vorticity that is being stretched, as discussed in
Section

IV.1
.

Momentum-Conserving Tube

Consider a cylindrical element that rotates with angular velocity
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}/2}
.
Let
\mathbb{I}
denote its inertia tensor and
I_{z}
the moment of inertia about the centerline axis.
If we assume that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is always aligned with its centerline axis, the inertia tensor product
\mathbb{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
becomes
\mathbb{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=I_{z}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
since
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbb{I}
, with
I_{z}
the corresponding eigenvalue.
In order to conserve angular momentum, neglecting pressure torque and following the discussion in
Section

IV.1.1
, the moment of inertia must change according to

\displaystyle-\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{I_{z}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}I_{z}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}.

(32)

Using
I_{z}=\frac{1}{2}mr^{2}
and without assuming that the mass
m=\rho\pi r^{2}\ell
is automatically conserved, the dot product of
Eq.

\boldsymbol{\omega}
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}r=-\frac{r}{4\ell}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ell-\frac{r}{4\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}}\left[(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}.

In the vortex particle scheme this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma_{p}=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\sigma_{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\|}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\sigma_{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\|}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p},

(33)

which in conjunction to the vortex strength equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{7}\frac{\sigma_{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\|}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})-\frac{6}{7}\left\{\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}\right\}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}.

Assumed Mass Conservation

Vortex methods typically assume that the continuity equation is implicitly solved by convecting the elements with the velocity field, automatically satisfying mass conservation.
Repeating the momentum-conserving tube derivation, but this time assuming that the convection of the particles automatically ensures
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m=0
, we get the following governing equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sigma_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma_{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\|}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}

(
34
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})-\frac{3}{2}\left\{\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}\right\}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}.

Taking the dot product of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}
over the vortex strength equation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{p}.

In this last equation, notice that the derivative of the strength projected on its own direction is negative when vortex stretching is positive.
This means that the magnitude of vortex strength (and associated vorticity) decreases when the particle is stretched, which is an unphysical result.

Along the same lines, we can repeat the momentum-conserving sphere derivation from
Section

IV.2.1
, but this time assuming automatic mass conservation,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m=0

This turns out to lead to the same governing equations in
Eq.

34

Hence, we conclude that it is not possible to impose momentum conservation with either tube or sphere elements while assuming automatic mass conservation.

and

we develop a dynamic procedure for computing the model coefficient while also providing the means for backscatter control.
Finally, in
Section

V.5
we show how our model can be implemented in conventional mesh-based CFD with a pressure-velocity solver.

V.1
Existing Models

Over the years, only a few models have been proposed that are suitable for meshless vortex methods.
The most popular one is a variant of the Smagorinsky eddy-viscosity model formulated for the vorticity stress
Winckelmans 1995
;
Mansfield, Knio, and Meneveau 1998
;
Mansfield, Knio, and Meneveau 1996
;
Mansfield, Knio, and Meneveau 1999
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}=-\nabla\times\left(\nu_{\mathrm{SFS}}\nabla\times\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right),

where
\mathbf{E}\equiv\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}
,
\nu_{\mathrm{SFS}}=C_{d}^{2}\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2S_{mn}S_{mn}}
,
S_{mn}
is the strain-rate tensor,
\sigma
is the filter width, and
C_{d}
is a model coefficient which is either prescribed or computed dynamically.
This functional model and others alike were developed on the basis of homogeneous isotropic turbulence, which makes them overly diffusive in simulations with coherent vortical structures.
In the latest developments of the vortex particle-mesh scheme
Caprace, Winckelmans, and Chatelain 2020
;
Caprace, Winckelmans, and Chatelain 2021
, this drawback has been avoided with the variational multiscale method
Hughes, Mazzei, and Jansen 2000
;
Vreman 2003
;
Jeanmart and Winckelmans 2007
;
Cocle, Bricteux, and Winckelmans 2009
, however its applicability to a meshless scheme is not clear.

In a different approach, Cottet
Cottet 1996
;
Cottet 1998
;
Cottet, Jiroveanu, and Michaux 2003
developed an anisotropic structural model of the advective SFS term
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\mathbf{x}_{p})=

\displaystyle\quad C_{d}\sum\limits_{q}\mathrm{Vol}_{q}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{q}\right)\left[\left(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\right)\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\sigma_{q}}(\mathbf{x}_{p}-\mathbf{x}_{q})\right],

where the model coefficient
C_{d}
is usually prescribed with
ad hoc
calibration
Mimeau, Cottet, and Mortazavi 2019
.
This model is reportedly
Stock, Gharakhani, and Stone 2010
;
Mimeau, Cottet, and Mortazavi 2018
less dissipative than Smagorinsky-type models, however, it was developed on the basis of 2D flow which is absent of vortex stretching.
Thus, this model neglects
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}
even though vortex stretching is known to be one of the main mechanisms for enstrophy production in the energy cascade in three dimensions.

To address the need for a low-dissipation SFS model that captures vortex stretching as the physical mechanism for turbulence, in the following sections we develop an anisotropic structural model of SFS vortex stretching with a dynamic model coefficient.

V.2
SFS Vortex Stretching Model

Starting from the definition of SFS vortex stretching,

\displaystyle E_{i}^{\mathrm{str}}=-\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}\equiv-\left(\overline{\omega_{j}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}-\overline{\omega_{j}}\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}\right),

(44)

we group the filter operator as

\displaystyle E_{i}^{\mathrm{str}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=-

\displaystyle\int\limits\omega_{j}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}{\small\left(\mathbf{y}\right)}-\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Using the singular particle approximation,
\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{x})\approx\sum\limits_{q}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q})
, the integral collapses to

\displaystyle E_{i}^{\mathrm{str}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\approx-\sum\limits_{q}\Gamma_{j}^{q}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)-\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q}).

Assuming
\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\approx\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}
and writing in vector notation, the model then becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\approx\sum\limits_{q}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right)\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\overline{\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)\right).

Interestingly, both Cottet’s model and our model are strikingly similar to the non-localized vorticity terms
\mathbf{M}_{p}^{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{p}^{2}
, respectively (defined in
Eqs.

11
and
12
), which are neglected (or filtered out) by the localized-vorticity assumption.
Thus, these models can be thought of as soft deconvolutions of the localized-vorticity assumption.
Furthermore, introducing a model coefficient,
C_{d}
, they can approximate full deconvolutions encompassing the entire spectrum of subfilter scales.
This model coefficient is computed dynamically as follows.

V.3
Dynamic Procedure

V.3.1
Derivative Balance

Since SFS vortex stretching needs to be modeled from resolved quantities, the initial equality given in
Eq.

44
, written here in vector notation

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}=-\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right],

becomes only an approximation,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}\approx-\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right]

once the
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}
model is introduced.
To recover the equality (or at least improve the approximation), we introduce a dynamic model coefficient
C_{d}(\mathbf{x},\,t)
satisfying

\displaystyle C_{d}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right].

(45)

However, this equation is not useful
per se
since, in order to determine
C_{d}
,
Eq.

45
requires knowing the SFS quantity
{\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}}
, which is exactly what we are trying to model with
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}
.
However, assuming scale similarity (meaning that
C_{d}
is independent of the filter width), we differentiate this equation with respect to the filter width
\sigma
to obtain a more useful relation:

\displaystyle C_{d}\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right].

(46)

Using the singular particle approximation, the right-hand side of
Eq.

46
before differentiation becomes

\displaystyle\left.\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right]\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\approx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\sum\limits_{q}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right)\left(\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)-\overline{\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right),

and after differentiation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left.\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right]\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\approx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\sum\limits_{q}\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right)\left(\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)-\overline{\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum\limits_{q}\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q})\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\cdot\nabla\right)\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}\right).

Recalling that
\zeta_{\sigma}
is defined as
\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}}\zeta\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|}{\sigma}\right)
, its width-derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{\sigma}\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|}{\sigma^{5}}\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial r}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|}{\sigma}\right),

and assuming that
\zeta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})
reaches a maximum at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
(meaning,
{\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial r}(0)=0}
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{0})=-\frac{3}{\sigma}\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{0}\right).

Then, evaluating at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{p}
, assuming
\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{0}\right)\neq 0
, and using the localized-vorticity assumption to neglect all terms
q\neq p
,
Eq.

46
becomes

\displaystyle C_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{p})\frac{1}{\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{0}\right)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{p})=

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\frac{3}{\sigma}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\left(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})-\overline{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{p})\right)+\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{p}),

or

\displaystyle C_{d}\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{L},

(47)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}\equiv\frac{\sigma^{3}}{\zeta(0)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{p})

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\equiv\frac{3}{\sigma}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\left(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{p})-\overline{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{p})\right)+\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\nabla\right)\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{p}).

The relation in
Eq.

47
will be the basis for our dynamic procedure.
This procedure differs from the classic dynamic procedure developed by Germano
et al.
Germano et al. 1991
;
Germano 1992
in that the former is motivated by the balance of derivatives between true and modeled SFS contributions given in
Eq.

46
, while the latter is based on the Germano identity.

V.3.2
Enstrophy-Production Balance

The procedure aims at calculating
C_{d}
such as to impose the relation given in
Eq.

47
, however, this is an overdetermined system as there are three equations (one for each spatial dimension) and only one unknown,
C_{d}
.
Thus, the relation is now contracted by also imposing a balance of enstrophy production between true and modeled SFS contributions as follows.

Enstrophy, a measure of the rotational kinetic energy of the flow, is defined locally as
\xi\equiv\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}
. The rate of local enstrophy production is then calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi=\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right),

which can be decomposed between resolved and unresolved domains as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi=\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)+\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)\right],

The local enstrophy production in the resolved domain is then defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi_{r}\equiv\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right),

and the global enstrophy production in the resolved domain is then calculated by integration,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

The SFS contribution to the rate of enstrophy production is isolated through operator splitting as

\displaystyle\left(\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)_{\mathrm{SFS}}\equiv\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{SFS}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Now, we constrain the SFS model to match the enstrophy production of the true SFS contribution,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{true\,SFS}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Using the singular particle approximation,
\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{x})\approx\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{p})
, the integrals collapse into

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}=\sum\limits_{p}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{true\,SFS}}.

One instance that this equality is satisfied is when each term in the sum satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{true\,SFS}}.

Differentiating with respect to the filter width, we arrive to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{true\,SFS}}.

(48)

When SFS advection is neglected, the SFS model and the true SFS become

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)^{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}=-C_{d}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)^{\mathrm{true\,SFS}}={\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}},

thus
Eq.

48
is simply the dot-product of
Eq.

46
with
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
,

\displaystyle C_{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}}{\partial\sigma}=-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left[\overline{(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}}-(\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right].

Finally, following the same steps that led from
Eq.

46
to
Eq.

47
, the enstrophy balance in
Eq.

48
becomes

\displaystyle C_{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{m}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{L},

and
C_{d}
is then calculated dynamically as

\displaystyle C_{d}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{L}}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{m}}.

(49)

Thus, this
C_{d}
calculated at the position of every particle is the coefficient that approximates the derivative balance while satisfying a local balance of enstrophy production between the model and the true SFS contribution.

V.3.3
Lagrangian Average

Similar to the classic procedure based on the Germano identity
Germano et al. 1991
,
Eq.

49
poses numerical issues when the denominator is close to zero, leading to large fluctuations.
In the classic procedure, Meneveau
et al.

Meneveau, Lund, and Cabot 1996
addressed this issue by integrating both numerator and denominator along Lagrangian trajectories (pathlines), effectively building ensemble averages.
Applying this technique, our dynamic procedure becomes

\displaystyle C_{d}=\frac{\left<\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{L}\right>}{\left<\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{m}\right>},

(50)

where
\left<\cdot\right>
denotes the Lagrangian integration.
As suggested by Meneveau
et al.
, the integration is performed as a relaxation process at every time step of the form

\displaystyle\left<\phi\right>_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-\alpha)\left<\phi\right>_{\mathrm{old}}+\alpha\phi,

where
\alpha
is calculated as
\alpha=\Delta t/T\leq 1
,
\Delta t
is the time step of the simulation, and
T
is the time length of the ensemble average.

V.4
Backscatter Control

For stability reasons, it is common in LES to control the amount of energy being backscattered from the unresolved scales into the resolved scales.
The SFS term is purely-dissipative if its contributions to the enstrophy budget decrease the total enstrophy.
This is,

\displaystyle\left(\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)_{\mathrm{SFS}}\leq 0.

Following the derivation in
Section

V.3.2
, this condition is satisfied if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)_{(\mathbf{x}_{p})}^{\mathrm{SFS\,model}}\leq 0

for each particle, or equivalently,

\displaystyle C_{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{str}}(\mathbf{x}_{p})\geq 0

Therefore, enstrophy backscatter can be filtered out at each particle by clipping the model coefficient as
C_{d}=0
whenever the condition shown above is not satisfied.

V.5
Usage in Conventional Mesh-Based CFD

Even though our SFS model is tailored for vortex methods as it only uses the primitive variables of the VPM, the model can also be readily applied to conventional mesh-based CFD as follows.
The vortex strength is expressed from mesh quantities as
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}=\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>_{q}\mathrm{Vol}_{q}
, where
\left<\boldsymbol{\omega}\right>_{q}
and
\mathrm{Vol}_{q}
are the average vorticity and volume associated to each element in the grid (
i.e.
, cells in a finite volume method or nodes in a finite difference method).
The model in the vorticity transport equation then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle C_{d}\Delta^{-3}\sum\limits_{q}\left<\omega\right>_{j}^{q}\mathrm{Vol}_{q}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{q}\right)-\frac{\partial\overline{u_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\zeta\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{q}\|}{\Delta}\right),

where the index
q
iterates over each element in the grid,
\Delta
is the width of the grid filter, and
\zeta
is the filter kernel.
Furthermore, the model can be used in the pressure-velocity form of the momentum equation noticing that the SFS terms in the vorticity transport equation are simply the curl of the SFS term in the pressure-velocity equation,
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
, as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\tau_{kl}}{\partial x_{l}}=\frac{\partial T^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}},

where
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita tensor.
Hence, the SFS models of the vorticity transport equation can be used to model
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}
by “un-curling” the equation above.

VI
Results

In the preceding sections we have developed a scheme for numerically solving the LES-filtered Navier-Stokes equations in their vorticity form.
The proposed scheme uses a reformulation of the VPM and a novel model of SFS vortex stretching to achieve a meshless large eddy simulation.

We now proceed to test and validate both the VPM reformulation and the vortex-stretching SFS model that comprise our meshless LES.
Simulations are compared to results reported in the literature from experimental work, direct numerical simulation (DNS), the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM), and unsteady Reynolds-average Navier-Stokes (URANS) simulation.
These validation cases also serve as examples on how to impose initial and boundary conditions in our meshless scheme.

In
Section

VI.1
, advection and viscous diffusion is validated on an isolated vortex ring, which is also used to test convergence of the reformulated VPM.
In
Section

VI.2
, vortex stretching is validated through the simulation of two leapfrogging vortex rings.
Results with classic and reformulated VPM are first compared without SFS effects.
The SFS model is then introduced to capture the subfilter energy cascade that leads the rings into turbulent breakdown.
In
Section

VI.3
, a turbulent round jet is simulated to test the accuracy and numerical stability of the LES scheme.
The evolution of the jet and predicted Reynolds stress are compared to experimental measurements, validating the scheme as an LES method accurately resolving large-scale features of turbulent flow.
Finally, in
Section

VI.4
, our meshless LES is compared to conventional mesh-based CFD in a real-world engineering problem: an aircraft rotor and its wake.

VI.4
Aircraft Rotor in Hover

The rotation of blades in static air drives a strong axial flow caused by the shedding of tip vortices.
This is a challenging case to simulate since, in the absence of a freestream, the wake quickly becomes fully turbulent as tip vortices leapfrog and mix close to the rotor.
Thus, a rotor in hover is a good engineering application to showcase the accuracy, numerical stability, and computational efficiency of the reformulated VPM.

In this test case, we simulated the experiment by Zawodny
et al.

Zawodny, Boyd, Jr., and Burley 2016
consisting of a DJI 9443 rotor in hover at 5400 RPM.
This two-bladed rotor is 9.4 inches in diameter, resulting in a tip Mach number of 0.20 and chord and diameter-based Reynolds numbers at 70% of the blade span of
6\times 10^{4}
and
7\times 10^{5}
, respectively.
Our simulations were also compared to unsteady Reynolds-average Navier-Stokes (URANS) results reported by Schenk,
Schenk 2020
obtained with the commercial software
STAR-CCM+
.
The URANS is an unsteady compressible solver with an SST
k
–
\omega
turbulence model, resolving the blades with an all-
y^{+}
wall treatment on a rotating mesh surrounding the rotor.
It used an unstructured mesh with 14 million cells, mesh refinement down to a
y^{+}
of only 30, and time steps equivalent to
3^{\circ}
of rotation on a first-order time integration scheme, which is a rather coarse simulation, but requiring very low computational resources.

The rotating blades are computed in our VPM through an actuator line model (ALM), which is a common practice in LES
Churchfield et al. 2017
.
Our ALM discretizes the geometry into blade elements, using a two-dimensional viscous panel method to compute forces and circulation along each blade cross section as the blades move, as described in previous work
Alvarez and Ning 2020
.
The vorticity of each blade is introduced in the fluid domain by embedding static particles along the surface that capture the blade’s circulation distribution, while shedding free particles at the trailing edge associated with unsteady loading and trailing circulation, as shown in
Fig.

23
.
The frequency of particle shedding per revolution determines the initial spacing
\Delta x
in between particles, which, along with the core size
\sigma
, determines the spatial resolution at which the wake is being resolved.
The initial particle size
\sigma
was set as to provide a particle overlap
\lambda=\frac{\sigma}{\Delta x}
of 2.125 at the blade tip.
The VPM simulations used a time step equivalent to a rotation of
1^{\circ}
while shedding particles every
0.5^{\circ}
with 50 blade elements.

First, the simulation was attempted on the classic VPM without the SFS model, which quickly ended in numerical blow up after one revolution, shown in
Fig.

24
.
Introducing the SFS model made the classic VPM noticeably more stable, however, the simulation still blew up before the wake became fully developed.
Introducing the reformulated VPM made the simulation completely stable, using up to
1\times 10^{6}
particles after 16 revolutions.
As shown in
Fig.

24
, the rate of enstrophy produced by the rotor eventually balances out with the forward scatter of the SFS model.
This further asserts the numerical stability gained with the reformulated VPM and SFS model.

VII
Conclusions

In this study we have developed a meshless scheme for large eddy simulation that is accurate, stable, and computationally efficient.
We started off by deriving a general set of VPM governing equations that solve the LES-filtered Navier-Stokes equations.
The classic VPM turns out to be one of the formulations arising from these general equations, which seems to locally violate both conservation of mass and angular momentum, thus explaining its tendency to be numerically unstable.
We then derived a new VPM formulation that uses the particle shape to reinforce local conservation of mass and angular momentum.

In addition to the reformulated VPM, we developed a low-dissipation anisotropic SFS model that uses vortex stretching as the physical mechanism for turbulence.
We proposed a novel dynamic procedure for calculating the model coefficient and a strategy for backscatter control.
The dynamic procedure is based on a simultaneous balance of enstrophy-production and derivatives between true and modeled SFS contributions.
This SFS model is apt for both meshless and conventional mesh-based CFD, and is well suited for flows with coherent vortical structures where the predominant cascade mechanism is vortex stretching.

Extensive validation was presented, asserting the scheme comprised of the reformulated VPM and SFS model as a meshless LES accurately resolving large-scale turbulent dynamics.
Advection, viscous diffusion, and vortex stretching were validated through the simulation of vortex rings, showing good agreement with DNS and LBM.
It was shown that the reformulated VPM runs as fast as the classic VPM, adding no significant overhead, while the SFS model adds a computational overhead of 8% when a constant model coefficient is prescribed and 43% with the dynamic procedure.
In the simulation of a turbulent round jet, a favorable agreement with experiments showed that our scheme is able to resolve the mean and fluctuating components of turbulent flow, while also resolving Reynolds stress directly.

Finally, computational efficiency was demonstrated in an engineering application through the simulation of an aircraft rotor in hover.
Our meshless LES showed to be two orders of magnitude faster than a mesh-based LES with similar fidelity, while being one order of magnitude faster than a low-fidelity URANS simulation and three orders of magnitude than a high-fidelity DES.

This study validates the reformulated VPM as an LES that is both numerically stable and meshless, while able to accurately resolve mean and fluctuating large-scale features of turbulent flow with minimal computational effort.
In future work, a wider variety of boundary conditions can be explored, especially aiming at introducing solid boundaries without a mesh.
This study limited its computation to conventional CPU paradigms, but previous work
Yokota et al. 2013
;
Hu et al. 2014
shows a strong potential for massive parallelization and speed up in heterogenous GPU architectures.

Derivation of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\zeta_{\sigma_{p}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{p})\right)

Let us define two auxiliary functions
G
and
g
such that

\displaystyle G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)=g\left(\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y}(t),\,\sigma(t)\right)

and

\displaystyle g\left(\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y}(t),\,\sigma(t)\right)=\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right),

resulting in the following properties.

Property 1

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)+\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\right)G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right),

by definition.

Property 2

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(g\left(\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y}(t),\,\sigma(t)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial g}{\partial\sigma}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}+\sum\limits_{i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial g}{\partial\sigma}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}+\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)g

Property 3

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)g\left(\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y},\,\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\right)\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right),

since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\left(\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)\right)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)\right)

Now, using
\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial\sigma}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}
and replacing Property 3 in Property 2 we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)-\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\right)\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right),

and using this in Property 1,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)-\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\right)\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\right)\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)+\left[\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right).

Defining
\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial t}
and assuming
\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial t}=\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left[\left(\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right).

Finally, given that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}G\left(\mathbf{x},\,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

by definition, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\zeta_{\sigma}}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left[\left(\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right)\cdot\nabla\right]\zeta_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right).

Appendix B
Derivation of
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

Decomposing the vortex strength
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
into its magnitude
\Gamma\equiv\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\|
and unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\equiv\frac{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\|}
as
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\Gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
, we calculate the following

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)-\left\{\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\right]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right\}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\mathrm{d}t}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}+\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\mathrm{d}t}\cancelto{1}{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}+\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\left(\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\cancelto{1}{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}\right)\right]\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{1}{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}.

Appendix C
Analytical Dynamics of Vortex Rings

Given a system of
N
collinear vortex rings with circulation
\Gamma_{i}
, centroid
Z_{i}
, and radius
R_{i}
for
i=1,...,N
, let us assume a singular vorticity along each ring’s centerline,

\displaystyle\omega(r,z,t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\delta\left(z-Z_{i}(t)\right)\delta\left(r-R_{i}(t)\right)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta, and
r
and
z
are the radial and axial coordinates of a cylindrical system.
As described by Borisov
et al.

Borisov, Kilin, and Mamaev 2013
, the interactions between singular rings lead to dynamics dictated by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{1}{R_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_{i}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\Gamma_{j}G\left(R_{i},Z_{i},R_{j},Z_{j}\right)

(51)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=V_{i}+\frac{1}{R_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial R_{i}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\Gamma_{j}G\left(R_{i},Z_{i},R_{j},Z_{j}\right)

(52)

where
V_{i}
is the self-induced velocity of the ring and
G
is the streamfunction of an infinitely thin circular vortex filament of unit intensity.

G
is given by

\displaystyle G(r,z,R,Z)=\frac{\sqrt{rR}}{2\pi}\left[\left(\frac{2}{k}-k\right)K(k^{2})-\frac{2}{k}E(k^{2})\right],

where

\displaystyle k=\sqrt{\frac{4rR}{\left(z-Z\right)^{2}+\left(r+R\right)^{2}}},

and
K(m)
and
E(m)
are the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind, respectively:

\displaystyle K(m)=\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}\theta}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

and

\displaystyle E(m)=\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Since the velocity induced by a singular filament on itself is infinite, a regularized vorticity distribution must be assumed when calculating
V_{i}
.
Assuming a thin toroidal vortex ring, Saffman
Saffman 1970
;
Saffman 1985
obtained

\displaystyle V=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi R}\left(\ln\frac{8}{a/R}+C\left(a/R\right)\right),

(53)

where the function
C
is determined from the vorticity distribution inside the ring core.
For thin Gaussian rings with
a\ll R
,
C(\epsilon)
becomes
C=-0.558
.
For thick Gaussian rings, Archer
et al.

Archer, Thomas, and Coleman 2008
observed through direct numerical simulation that the initial Gaussian distribution quickly becomes skewed and leads to

\displaystyle C(\epsilon)\approx-0.558-1.12\epsilon^{2}-5.0\epsilon^{4},

(54)

where
\epsilon\equiv a/R
.
Thus, the self-induced velocity depends on the current core size
a
of the ring.
At the same time, viscous diffusion constantly spreads the vorticity distribution according to
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
In a vortex filament with a Gaussian vorticity distribution,
{\omega(r)=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi a^{2}}e^{-r^{2}/a^{2}}}
, the viscous diffusion leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}a^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}=4\nu.

(55)

Finally, when viscous effects are ignored through operator splitting, the ring must conserve mass due to Helmholtz’ circulation theorem in incompressible flow.
This is expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(Ra^{2}\right)=0,

which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{a}{2R}\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t},

(56)

meaning that the core size must shrink as the ring is stretched by the interaction with neighboring rings.
We have observed that incorporating this last equation introduces dynamics that are incongruent with the numerical results from DNS, LBM, and VPM discussed in
Sections

VI.1
and
VI.2
.
This could be due to the fact that
Eqs.

51
and
52
ignore the effects of core size in the mutual interactions between rings, thus
Eq.

56
is introducing an unphysical one-way coupling between
\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}t}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t}
.
Hence,
Eq.

56
was not used in the analytical solutions shown in
Sections

VI.1
and
VI.2
.

In summary,
Eqs.

51
,
52
,
53
,
54
and
55
form a system of analytical ordinary differential equations that describe the dynamics of an arbitrary number of coaxial, viscous, incompressible vortex rings. In this derivation we have assumed that the core thickness only affects the self-induced velocity of each ring, while its effects are ignored in the interactions between the rings and the core size is not affected by vortex stretching.
