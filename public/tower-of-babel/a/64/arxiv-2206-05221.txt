Combinatorial proof of Maslov index formula in Heegaard Floer Theory
Roman Krutowski

Abstract
. We prove Lipshitz’s Maslov index formula in Heegaard Floer homology via the combinatorics of Heegaard diagrams.



1.
Introduction

In
[
7
,
8
]
Peter Ozsváth and Zoltán Szabó introduced Heegaard Floer homology, a collection of invariants for closed oriented 3-manifolds. Since then Heegaard Floer homology has emerged as an extremely powerful invariant, producing strong results in low-dimensional topology (see
[
7
,
8
,
10
,
9
,
4
,
1
,
5
]
).
All of the numerous versions of Heegaard Floer homology involve counting the number of points in the
unparametrized moduli space of
J
-holomorphic disks of a certain Maslov index joining some
intersection points of two half-dimensional tori in a certain symmetric product of a surface.

The most notable advantage of Heegaard Floer homology when compared to other types of Floer Homology is its combinatorial nature which allows computation of these invariants in various cases. One of the ingredients is the combinatorial formula for Maslov index
\mu(\varphi)
of a Whitney disk
\varphi
, which is the Fredholm index of some differential operator, or alternatively, a homology class of a certain loop in the Lagrangian Grassmanian. In
[
11
]
, Jacob Rasmussen gave a formula that relates the intersection
number of the disk with the fat diagonal in the symmetric product, with its Maslov index. Later on, Robert Lipshitz in the paper devoted to the cylindrical reformulation of the whole theory
[
2
]
gave a purely combinatorial formula for the Maslov index of these disks.

The proof of this formula in
[
2
,
3
]
is based on an elegant geometric approach. In this paper, we provide a combinatorial proof of this formula which is inspired by the proof of the index formula for Maslov
n
-gons due to Sucharit Sarkar
[
12
]
.

Let
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
be a Heegaard diagram. Any Whitney disk
\varphi\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
connecting
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in Sym^{g}(\Sigma)
has a
shadow

D(\varphi)
(see Definition
2.1
), a certain
2
-chain in
\Sigma
with boundary satisfying
\partial(\partial D(\varphi)\cap\bm{\alpha})=\bm{y}-\bm{x}
and
\partial(\partial D(\varphi)\cap\bm{\beta})=\bm{x}-\bm{y}
. We denote the set of such
2
-chains by
\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and call them
domains
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}
the set of all domains in all Heegaard diagrams (see Section
2
for details).

In this paper, we assume that
\bm{\alpha}
curves intersect
\bm{\beta}
curves perpendicularly with respect to some metric on
\Sigma
. Among the domains, there are two special types that serve us as building blocks.
Bigons
and
rectangles
are
2
-sided and
4
-sided domains, respectively, which are homeomorphic to disks with all angles equal to
90^{\circ}
and which do not contain any
\bm{x}
- and
\bm{y}
-coordinates in the interior.

Given two domains
D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
the composition of these domains
D*D^{\prime}
is a domain
D+D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{z})
.

In this paper any map
\overline{\mu}\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
will be called an
index
.
An index
\overline{\mu}
is said to be
additive
if for any Heegaard diagram and any two of its domains
D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
the following holds

\overline{\mu}(D*D^{\prime})=\overline{\mu}(D)+\overline{\mu}(D^{\prime}).

In Section
2
we introduce two types of transformations of any Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
which assign to each of its domain
D
a new domain
D^{\prime}
in the new Heegaard diagram. These transformations are called
finger moves
and
empty stabilizations
.
An index
\overline{\mu}
is said to be
stable
if for any such transformation
\overline{\mu}(D)=\overline{\mu}(D^{\prime})
.

Define the
combinatorial index

\widetilde{\mu}
of a domain
D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
via the formula due to Lipshitz
[
2
]

(1)

\widetilde{\mu}(D)\coloneqq\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D)+\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D)+e(D).

where
\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D)
is a point measure of
D
at
\bm{x}
and
e(D)
is the Euler measure of
D
(see Subsection
2.1
).

We are now ready to formulate our main results.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a unique index
\overline{\mu}\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

\overline{\mu}
is additive;

(2)

\overline{\mu}
is stable;

(3)

\overline{\mu}(B)=1
for any bigon
B\in\mathcal{D}
;

(4)

\overline{\mu}(R)=1
for any rectangle
R\in\mathcal{D}
.

Moreover, this index agree with the combinatorial index
\widetilde{\mu}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
be a Heegaard diagram and
\varphi
be a Whitney disk connecting two generators of the corresponding Heegaard Floer chain complex. Then

\mu(\varphi)=\widetilde{\mu}(D(\varphi)),

where
\mu(\varphi)
is the Maslov index of
\varphi
.

2.1.
Heegaard Floer Theory preliminaries

To start, recall the definition of a Heegaard diagram. Let
\Sigma
be an oriented Riemannian surface (i.e., there is a fixed Riemannian metric) and
\bm{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{g}\}

\bm{\beta}=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{g}\}

oriented

\bm{\alpha}

\bm{\beta}
generate half-dimensional subspaces of

\bm{\alpha}

The Heegaard Floer homology chain complex of a diagram

is generated by

\bm{x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{g}\}

Let as consider the unit disk

Definition 2.1
.

Given a Whitney disk
\varphi\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

Let us recall the definition of the Maslov index of a Whitney disk

\bm{x}

\bm{y}

The map from

We also introduce

Definition 2.4
.

Let

(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
be a domain in Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
and let
\varphi\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
be a Whitney disk. Let
D^{\prime}
be the image of
D
under a finger move or an empty stabilization with respect to
D
. Let
\varphi^{\prime}
be the image of
\varphi
under a finger move or an empty stabilization with respect to
\varphi
. Then
\mu(\varphi)=\mu(\varphi^{\prime})
and
\widetilde{\mu}(D)=\widetilde{\mu}(D^{\prime})
.

Proof.

First, consider the case when this transformation is an empty stabilization with respect to
D
(or
\varphi)
. Let
\alpha_{g+1}
and
\beta_{g+1}
be two new curves of
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime})
and
z
be their point of intersection. We have
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}\cup\{z\}
,
\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{y}\cup\{z\}
and
D^{\prime}=D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime})
(or
\varphi^{\prime}\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
. First,
\widetilde{\mu}(D)=\widetilde{\mu}(D^{\prime})
, because
\widetilde{\mu}_{z}(D)=0
.
As for the Maslov index, notice that paths
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
in

\gamma_{0}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
in

corresponding to

and
T_{z}\beta_{g+1}
respectively. Hence
\mu(\varphi)=\mu(\varphi^{\prime})
.

If the transformation is a finger move then the Maslov indices are equal because finger move keeps
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
unchanged near
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

coming from the boundary

2.3.
Additivity of indices

Our strategy for proving Lipshitz’s formula is based on decomposing any domain into the composition of trivial pieces: bigons and rectangles.
To apply this decomposition we need the additivity of the Maslov index
\mu
and of the combinatorial index
\widetilde{\mu}
under the composition of domains.
We know that the Maslov index
\mu
is additive. It was shown in more generality in
[
12
]
that
\widetilde{\mu}
is additive and here we repeat the proof in our terms.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be oriented
1
-chains in
\Sigma
intersecting transversely. Denote by
\gamma_{1}\cdot\gamma_{2}
intersection number of these
1
-chains, which is equal to the signed count of intersection points where the sign is defined by comparing the orientation of
\Sigma
and orientation coming from
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. A contribution to intersection number at an endpoint is given by a fraction
\pm\frac{1}{2}
or
\pm\frac{1}{4}
as in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
D\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
D^{\prime}
be any other
2
-chain in the Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
. Then

\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D^{\prime})=\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\partial D|_{\bm{\beta}}=\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}\cdot\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}}
.

Proof.

Given orientations on
\bm{\alpha}
at each intersection point
p
between
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
we give numbers to regions from
\mathit{I}
to
\mathit{IV}
. Namely,
\mathit{I}
and
\mathit{II}
quadrants are in the upper half of
\bm{\beta}
(the half in the positive direction of
\bm{\alpha}
) and
\mathit{I}
st quadrant is the one for which the orientation induced from
\Sigma
is opposite to the orientation of
\bm{\alpha}
at
p
. Quadrants
\mathit{III}
and
\mathit{IV}
are defined follow
\mathit{II}
in the counterclockwise order on
\Sigma
.

Let us pick a point
p
in the first quadrant near the point
x_{i}\in\bm{x}
lying on some
\alpha_{k}
and travel parallely to
\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}_{k}}
until we reach a first quadrant near
y_{j}\in\bm{y}
where
\partial(\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}_{k}})=y_{j}-x_{i}
. Then let us track the coefficient of
D^{\prime}
at each point along this path. Each time we change the region passing through
\bm{\beta}
this coefficient changes by the intersection number of this small portion of our path with
\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}
. Hence, the difference between the coefficients is equal to
\partial D|_{\alpha_{k}}\cdot\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}
.

Now, by taking such paths for all points in
\bm{x}
and all
4
quadrants for each of these points and then averaging we get

\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D^{\prime})=\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}

∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
D\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
D^{\prime}\in\pi_{2}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
. Then
\widetilde{\mu}(D*D^{\prime})=\widetilde{\mu}(D)+\widetilde{\mu}(D^{\prime})
.

Proof.

First, we easily see that

\widetilde{\mu}(D*D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}(D)-\widetilde{\mu}(D^{\prime})=\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D^{\prime})+\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{z}}(D)-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D).

Second, applying Lemma
2.7
we get
\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{x}}(D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D^{\prime})=\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{y}}(D)-\widetilde{\mu}_{\bm{z}}(D)=\partial D|_{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\partial D^{\prime}|_{\bm{\beta}}
.

Hence

\widetilde{\mu}(D*D^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mu}(D)-\widetilde{\mu}(D^{\prime})=0.

∎

3.
Main theorem

Theorem 3.1
.

For a given domain
D
in a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
there is a sequence of finger moves and empty stabilizations such that in the new Heegaard diagram the image of
D
can be represented as a composition of bigons, rectangles and their negatives.

Before proving this theorem we show how Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
follow from Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. Apply Theorem
3.1
to obtain the image
D^{\prime}
of
D
and the decomposition
D^{\prime}=D_{1}*\ldots*D_{k}
of
D^{\prime}
in the new Heegaard diagram. Here each
D_{i}
is either a bigon, a rectangle, or the negative of a bigon or a rectangle. Since
\overline{\mu}
is additive we may compute
\overline{\mu}(D^{\prime})=\overline{\mu}(D_{1})+\ldots+\overline{\mu}(D_{k})
. For each
D_{i}
the value
\overline{\mu}(D_{i})
can be easily inferred from the
4
axioms.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We are given a Whitney disk
\varphi\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and we may assume that
g(\Sigma)>1
by applying an empty stabilization if necessary. By Theorem
3.1
there is a transformation of the given Heegaard diagram such that there is a decomposition
D(\varphi^{\prime})=D_{1}*\ldots*D_{k}
, where
\varphi^{\prime}
is an image of
\varphi
under this transformation; equivalently
0=D(\varphi^{\prime})*(-D_{1})*\ldots*(-D_{k})
. Since each
D_{i}
is either a bigon or a rectangle (possibly negative) there exists a corresponding Whitney disk
\varphi_{i}
such that
D(\varphi_{i})=-D_{i}
. Then
\varphi^{\prime}*\varphi_{1}*\ldots*\varphi_{k}\in\pi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{x})

and, moreover,
D(\varphi^{\prime}*\varphi_{1}*\ldots*\varphi_{k})=0
. From
[
7
, Proposition 2.15]
it now follows that

It now suffices to show that
