Influence Through Mixing: Hotspots as Benchmarks for Basic Black-Hole Behaviour
G. Kaplanek, C.P. Burgess, and R. Holman

Abstract
Effective theories are being developed for fields outside black holes, often with an unusual open-system feel due to the influence of large number of degrees of freedom that lie out of reach beyond the horizon. What is often difficult when interpreting such theories is the absence of comparisons to simpler systems that share these features. We propose here such a simple model, involving a single external scalar field that mixes in a limited region of space with a ‘hotspot’ containing a large number of hot internal degrees of freedom. Since the model is at heart gaussian it can be solved explicitly, and we do so for the mode functions and correlation functions for the external field once the hotspot fields are traced out. We compare with calculations that work perturbatively in the mixing parameter, and by doing so can precisely identify its domain of validity. We also show how renormalization-group EFT methods can allow some perturbative contributions to be resummed beyond leading order, verifying the result using the exact expression.

What usually helps when developing EFT tools are concrete systems for which both UV and IR sectors are well-understood and within which the EFT description can be assessed by comparing to other methods. These kinds of comparisons are not yet available for black holes, and the search for effective descriptions of black-hole physics are the poorer for it. The purpose of this paper is to help fix this situation by providing a simple black-hole proxy that can help fill this void. On one hand the model should be simple enough to solve, but on the other hand share enough black hole properties to be informative about some of their putative EFT descriptions.

Broadly speaking there are two types of black-hole EFTs that are usually pursued, and both can have counterparts in our hotspot model. The main variant is one that is appropriate to gravitational wave emission, and applies on length scales

The response of the black hole to applied ‘bulk’ fields (and the back-reaction of the black hole back onto these fields) is described by an action defined as a functional of the bulk fields integrated along the black hole’s one-dimensional world line. This type of EFT is obtained in the hotspot example by taking the radius
\xi

\xi\ll\epsilon\ll\lambda

The puzzle for this EFT is how it should capture the enormous number of degrees of freedom that are internal to the black hole, its perfect absorber properties and the Hawking radiation that comes with it.
In

Although the fluctuation-dissipation theorem implies that predictions in linear response do not depend on these details, it remains open the extent to which other predictions do, and if so whether the same might be true for low-energy black hole properties. Although the extra degrees of freedom can again be integrated out, they are not the traditional massive states of the usual Wilsonian treatments, and so can lead to actions with unusual properties including some forms of nonlocality. In companion papers

2
Modelling the hotspot

This section sets up the benchmark model whose properties we study. We do so using the language of open systems, with degrees of freedom divided up into an observable system and an ‘environment’ — a proxy for the black hole interior — whose properties are never measured.

2.1
Hotspot definition

For the observable sector we choose a single real scalar field,
\phi(x)
, and take it to live in a spatial region,
{\cal R}_{+}
, of infinite extent. The environment is given by
N
real scalar fields,
\chi^{a}
with
a=1,\cdots,N
, that reside in a different spatial region
{\cal R}_{-}
. While one or both of
{\cal R}_{+},{\cal R}_{-}
could in principle be curved, we take them to be flat for simplicity. We also take all of these fields to be massless.

We suppose that the fields interact with one another locally and only do so on a relatively small codimension-1 2-sphere,
{\cal S}_{\xi}
, of radius
\xi
which is the only place where
{\cal R}_{+}
and
{\cal R}_{-}
actually touch one another (see Fig.
1
). In practice this means that both
{\cal R}_{+}
and
{\cal R}_{-}
have a small sphere excised from the origin (for all time) and the surface of this sphere is identified in the two spaces.

Our interest for much of this paper is in scales much larger than
\xi
and so consider the idealization of taking
\xi\to 0
, in which case
{\cal S}_{\xi}
reduces to a single point of contact between
{\cal R}_{+}
and
{\cal R}_{-}
, which we take to be the origin
{{\bf x}}=\mathbf{0}
of both
{\cal R}_{\pm}
. In this limit the couplings of
\phi
to
\chi^{a}
are captured by an effective action localized at
{{\bf x}}=0
.

2.1.1
Action and Hamiltonian

The action that defines the model is therefore taken to be
S=S_{+}+S_{-}+S_{\rm int}
where the kinematics of
\phi
and
\chi^{a}
are described by

S_{+}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\cal R}_{+}}{\hbox{d}}^{4}x\;\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial^{\mu}\phi\quad\hbox{and}\quad S_{-}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\cal R}_{-}}{\hbox{d}}^{4}x\;\delta_{ab}\,\partial_{\mu}\chi^{a}\partial^{\mu}\chi^{b}\,.

(2.1)

Our later interest is usually in the case where the
\chi^{a}
couplings do not break the
O(N)
symmetry of their kinetic terms.

The lowest-dimension interaction (mixing, really) that involves
\phi
on the interaction surface is given by

S_{\mathrm{int}}=-\int_{{\cal S}_{\xi}^{t}}{\hbox{d}}^{4}x\;\left[G_{a}\,\chi^{a}\phi+\frac{G_{\phi}}{2}\,\phi^{2}\right]\,,

(2.2)

in which the integration is over the world-tube,
{\cal S}_{\xi}^{t}
swept out by the surface
{\cal S}_{\xi}
over time. The Einstein summation convention applies, so there is an implied sum over
a
. The couplings
G_{a}
and
G_{\phi}
here have dimension mass:
[G_{a}]=[G_{\phi}]=+1
.

In the limit
\xi\to 0
the 2-sphere
{\cal S}_{\xi}
degenerates to a point and this interaction becomes

S_{\rm int}\simeq-\int{\hbox{d}}t\;\left[g_{a}\,\chi^{a}(t,\mathbf{0})\,\phi(t,\mathbf{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})\right]\,,

(2.3)

where the integration is over the proper time of the interaction point
{{\bf x}}=0
in both
{\cal R}_{+}
and
{\cal R}_{-}
. The couplings appearing here are
g_{a}=4\pi\xi^{2}G_{a}
and
\lambda=4\pi\xi^{2}G_{\phi}
and have dimensions
[g_{a}]=[\lambda]=-1
. Although the coupling
\lambda
might seem unnecessary, in later sections we see how it can be generated by the presence of the couplings
g_{a}
.

In what follows we allow the couplings
g_{a}
and
\lambda
to depend on time, and in particular will use this time dependence to turn on suddenly the interaction between the fields at
t=0
. Doing so allows us both to study transient effects associated with the couplings turning on as well as late-time effects after the transients have passed.

The quantization of this model follows closely the treatment of a field coupled to a central qubit given in
Lin:2005uk
. The canonical momenta for this problem are

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}:=\partial_{t}{\phi}\quad\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\quad\Pi_{a}:=\delta_{ab}\,\partial_{t}{\chi}^{b}\ ,

(2.4)

and quantization proceeds by demanding these satisfy the equal-time commutation relations

\Bigl[{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{{\mathfrak{p}}}(t,{{\bf y}})\Bigr]=i\delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})\quad\hbox{and}\quad\Bigl[{\chi}^{a}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\Pi}_{b}(t,{{\bf y}})\Bigr]=i\delta^{a}_{b}\delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})\,.

(2.5)

The free Hamiltonian is
{H}_{0}:={{\cal H}}_{+}\otimes{\cal I}_{-}+{\cal I}_{+}\otimes{{\cal H}}_{-}
, where
{\cal H}_{\pm}
and
{\cal I}_{\pm}
are the Hamiltonian and identity operators acting separately within the
\phi
- and
\chi
-sectors of the Hilbert space. Explicitly

{{\cal H}}_{+}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\cal R}_{+}}{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\;\Bigl[{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{2}+\big(\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\phi}\big)^{2}\Bigr]\quad\hbox{and}\quad{{\cal H}}_{-}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\cal R}_{-}}{\hbox{d}}^{3}x\;\Bigl[\delta^{ab}{\Pi}_{a}{\Pi}_{b}+\delta_{ab}\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\chi}^{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\chi}^{b}\Bigr]\ .

(2.6)

The interaction Hamiltonian (in the limit of a point-like interaction surface) is similarly

{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=g_{a}\,{\phi}(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes{\chi}^{a}(t,\mathbf{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes{\cal I}_{-}\ .

(2.7)

2.1.2
Initial conditions and the sudden approximation

For later calculations we assume the state of the total system at
t=0
to be of the form

\rho(0)=\rho_{+}\otimes\rho_{-}\,,

(2.8)

for separate density matrices
\rho_{\pm}
in the two sectors. In general, interactions introduce correlations and so do not preserve this factorized form, and it is for this reason that we imagine the couplings between
\phi
and
\chi^{a}
to be initially absent, being turned on suddenly with

g_{a}(t)=\Theta(t)\,g_{a}\,,

(2.9)

where
\Theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function. This allows us to prepare initially uncorrelated states and then observe how the joint system reacts to the onset of coupling.

In practice we choose the
\phi
sector initially to be in its vacuum,

\rho_{+}=\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}

(2.10)

where
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
is the standard Minkowski vacuum defined by
{\mathfrak{a}}_{{\bf p}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=0
. With eventual comparison to black holes in mind we take the
\chi^{a}
sector to be in a thermal state,

\rho_{-}=\varrho_{\beta}:=\frac{e^{-\beta{\cal H}_{-}}}{\underset{}{\mathrm{Tr^{\,{}^{\prime}}}}[e^{-\beta{\cal H}_{-}}]}\,,

(2.11)

with inverse temperature
\beta>0
. The prime on the trace indicates that it is only taken over the
\chi
sector.

2.2
Time evolution in different pictures

Our goal is to solve for the time-evolution of the
\phi
-sector of the system and because
S_{\rm int}
is bilinear in
\phi
and
\chi^{a}
the system’s evolution can be evaluated in quite some generality. An exact solution is in particular given in §
5
, after first detouring in §
3
to describe an approximate solution that is evaluated perturbatively in the following combination of hotspot couplings

\tilde{g}^{2}:=\delta^{ab}g_{a}g_{b}=Ng^{2}\,,

(2.12)

where the second equality specializes to the case where all couplings are equal.

Although not required when solving the model, a large-
N

\tilde{g}
is held fixed (and need not itself be particularly small) as
N\to\infty
. This limit is briefly discussed in §

\chi^{a}

2.2.1
Interaction picture

For perturbative evaluation we first diagonalize the free Hamiltonian. This is done in the usual way, by writing (with time-dependence as appropriate for the interaction picture)

{\phi}(x)=\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}p}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{p}}}\big[e^{ip\cdot x}{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}+e^{-ip\cdot x}{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}\big]\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\chi}^{a}(x)=\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}p}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2}2E_{p}}}\big[e^{ip\cdot x}{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{a}_{{{\bf p}}}+e^{-ip\cdot x}{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{a\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}\big]

(2.13)

where
p\cdot x:=p_{\mu}x^{\mu}=-E_{p}t+{{\bf p}}\cdot{{\bf x}}
with
E_{p}\ =\ |\mathbf{p}|
, and the canonical commutation relations imply the usual creation- and annihilation-operator algebra:
[{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}},{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf q}}}]=[{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{a},{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf q}}}^{b}]=0
(together with their adjoints) and
[{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}},{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf q}}}^{\ast}]=\delta^{3}({{\bf p}}-{{\bf q}})

[{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{a},{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf q}}}^{b\ast}]=\delta^{ab}\delta^{3}({{\bf p}}-{{\bf q}})
. This diagonalizes
{{\cal H}}_{\pm}

\displaystyle{{\cal H}}_{+}=\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}p\;\frac{E_{p}}{2}\bigg[{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{\ast}+{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{\ast}{{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}\bigg]\quad\hbox{and}\quad{{\cal H}}_{-}=\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}p\;\frac{E_{p}}{2}\delta_{ab}\bigg[{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{a}_{{{\bf p}}}{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{b\ast}+{{\mathfrak{b}}}_{{{\bf p}}}^{a\ast}{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{b}_{{{\bf p}}}\bigg]\ .

(2.14)

The interaction-picture interaction Hamiltonian in the pointlike limit (
\xi\to 0
) similarly becomes

\displaystyle{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{a}{\phi}(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes{\chi}^{a}(t,\mathbf{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes{\cal I}_{-}

(2.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}p\;{\hbox{d}}^{3}q}{2(2\pi)^{3}\sqrt{E_{p}E_{q}}}\;\Bigl[g_{a}\big({{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}e^{-iE_{p}t}+{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}e^{+iE_{p}t}\big)\otimes\big({{\mathfrak{b}}}^{a}_{{{\bf q}}}e^{-iE_{q}t}+{{\mathfrak{b}}}^{a\ast}_{{{\bf q}}}e^{+iE_{q}t}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\lambda}{2}\big({{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf p}}}e^{-iE_{p}t}+{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}e^{+iE_{p}t}\big)\big({{\mathfrak{a}}}_{{{\bf q}}}e^{-iE_{q}t}+{{\mathfrak{a}}}^{\ast}_{{{\bf q}}}e^{+iE_{q}t}\big)\otimes{\cal I}_{-}\Bigr]\,.

Matrix elements of this can be used in standard fashion to compute the evolution of the system’s state.

2.2.2
Heisenberg picture

Later sections solve explicitly for time evolution, and do so by solving how the fields evolve in Heisenberg picture, including the effects of the couplings in
H_{\rm int}
. To this end it is worth briefly setting up the Heisenberg picture quantities and in particular exposing differences from the interaction-picture description given above.

Keeping in mind that we later entertain time-dependent couplings,
g_{a}(t)
, the full time-evolution operator
{U}(t,t^{\prime})
can be defined as the solution to
\partial_{t}{U}(t,t_{0})=-i{H}(t){U}(t,t_{0})
that satisfies
U(t=t_{0})={\cal I}
. This leads to the usual time-ordered form

{U}(t,t_{0})={\cal T}\exp\left(-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ {H}(s)\right)\ .

(2.16)

4
RG Improvement and resumming the
\lambda
expansion

This section studies the dependence of hotspot physics on the self-coupling
\lambda
, in particular exploring how limiting it is to treat its implications perturbatively. Although the validity of expansions in
\lambda
might seem to be a tangential issue if one’s focus is on the thermal coupling
\tilde{g}
, it really is not. As discussed earlier, the response of a field like
\phi
to the hotspot typically diverges at the hotspot position —
c.f.
for example equations (
2.39
) and (
2.40
) — and these divergences are ultimately handled by being renormalized into couplings like
\lambda
, as in (
2.41
). As a consequence of this renormalization couplings like
\lambda
run in the renormalization-group sense, and (as we show here, following
PPEFT
) this can make it inconsistent to set them to zero at all scales.

This section derives precisely how the coupling
\lambda
runs, and along the way shows that the dimensionless expansion parameter that justifies treating
\lambda
perturbatively turns out to be
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is the very small regularization length scale used to regulate the divergences (as in eqs. (
2.39
) and (
2.40
)). Physically, both
\lambda
and
\epsilon
might reasonably be expected to be of order the size
\xi
of the compact hotspot; a length scale that has been assumed to be much smaller than the other scales of physical interest. If perturbative calculations actually require
\lambda\ll 4\pi\epsilon
then they may not be that useful, since
\lambda
would have to be much smaller even than this already very microscopic scale. The renormalization-group arguments presented here show how perturbative predictions can be extended to the regime
\lambda\mathrel{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$>$\kern-8.50006pt\lower 4.30554pt\hbox{$\sim$}}}4\pi\epsilon
, providing results that can be compared to the exact calculations to follow in §
5
.

4.1
Effective interactions and boundary condition

To better understand the effects of
\lambda
beyond perturbation theory this section temporarily turns off the coupling
\tilde{g}
in order to eliminate unnecessary distractions. Non-perturbative information is then extracted by leaving
\lambda
nonzero for all time and exploring more systematically how it modifies the dynamics of the
\phi
field. A natural framework for this is the language of point-particle (or world-line) EFTs, since these systematically incorporate the effects of small objects on their surroundings, organized in powers of
ka
(where
a
is the object’s size and
k
is the momentum of a typical probe). In practice we therefore work completely in the
{\cal R}_{+}
sector, following closely the logic of
PPEFT
;
EFTBook
, with the bulk field interacting only with the contact interaction

H_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}x\;\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{x})\,\delta^{3}({{\bf x}})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\;\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})\ ,

(4.1)

with
\lambda
independent of time.

The implications of
\lambda
are incorporated by identifying the mode functions that are appropriate in the presence of this interaction. Since the Heisenberg equation of motion in this case –
c.f.
equation (
2.22
) – is

(-\partial_{t}^{2}+\nabla^{2})\phi(t,{{\bf x}})=\lambda\,\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,\delta^{3}({{\bf x}})\ ,

(4.2)

this is also the equation satisfied by each mode function,
u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}})
, in an expansion (
c.f.
equation (
2.13
)) like

\phi(t,{{\bf x}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{+\ell}\int{\hbox{d}}\omega\;\bigg[u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}}){\mathfrak{a}}_{\omega\ell m}+u^{\ast}_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}}){\mathfrak{a}}^{\ast}_{\omega\ell m}\bigg]\ .

(4.3)

Once the
\lambda
-dependence of these mode functions is identified by solving (
4.2

\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(s,{{\bf y}})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{+\ell}\int{\hbox{d}}\omega\;u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}})u^{\ast}_{\omega\ell m}(s,{{\bf y}})\,.

Here

satisfies

{\mathfrak{a}}_{\omega\ell m}
satisfies the standard commutation relations
[{\mathfrak{a}}_{\omega\ell m},{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}_{\tilde{\omega}\tilde{\ell}\tilde{m}}]=\delta(\omega-\tilde{\omega})\delta_{\ell\tilde{\ell}}\delta_{m\tilde{m}}

The main observation is that the dependence of

\lambda

4.2
Renormalization group and the interpretation of
\epsilon

There are two ways to read the above boundary conditions. The naive way is as given in (
4.13
) or (
4.15
): they give
D_{\ell}(\omega)/C_{\ell}(\omega)
as an explicit function of
\ell
and the two dimensionless variables
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
and
\omega\epsilon
. What is bothersome about this interpretation is that it makes
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
depend not only on the coupling
\lambda
, but also on the arbitrary regularization scale
\epsilon
.

But if
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
depends on
\epsilon
then so also will the physical observables that are built from it. Normally regularization dependence in a calculation drops out of physical quantities because it gets renormalized into a redefinition of the couplings. Or, equivalently, it is cancelled by an implicit regularization dependence that is hidden within couplings like
\lambda
.

4.2.1
Running of
\lambda

This observation suggests a different way to interpret the above boundary condition
PPEFT
. This alternative reading insists physical quantities cannot depend on arbitrary regularization scales, and because of this neither can
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
. In this case expressions like (
4.12
) or (
4.17
) should be reinterpreted as making explicit how
\lambda=\lambda(\epsilon)
must depend on
\epsilon
in order to ensure that
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
remains
\epsilon
-independent. That is to say, in this interpretation (
4.17
) is an RG equation for the coupling
\lambda(\epsilon)
.

To see what the evolution implied by (
4.17
) means more explicitly, it is worth expressing it in differential form. As explored in detail in Appendix
B
, this can be put into a universal form by defining the new variable

(4.20)

Of course there is nothing wrong with simply regarding the boundary condition as specifying

once a coupling
\lambda_{0}
is specified using a specific choice of regularization scale
\epsilon_{0}
. What the RG interpretation tells us is that once this choice is made, we are completely free to use any other regularization scale,
\epsilon_{1}
instead,
provided that we also change the value of the coupling to
\lambda_{1}
where both pairs
(\epsilon_{0},\lambda_{0})
and
(\epsilon_{1},\lambda_{1})
lie on the same RG trajectory
\lambda(\epsilon)
defined by (
4.17
) (or, equivalently by (
4.19
) and (
4.21
)). It is only when the coupling and regulator are changed in this correlated way that physical quantities remain unchanged.

4.2.2
RG-invariant characterization of coupling strength

Because physical observables depend only on the coupling
trajectories
it is more informative to specify the strength of the coupling by labelling the coupling trajectories using a more convenient RG-invariant parameterization, rather than simply by specifying its value
\lambda_{0}=\lambda(\epsilon_{0})
for a specific (but arbitrary) regularizations scale
\epsilon_{0}
. This section follows
PPEFT
and identifies a particular choice of RG-invariant parameterization that is convenient because (unlike the value
\lambda_{0}
, say) the parameters are simply related to physical quantities.

To this end the first observation is that the evolution equation (
4.20
) has two fixed points,
v=\pm 1
, along which
v
is
\epsilon
-independent. Trajectories that
do
evolve therefore cannot cross
v=\pm 1
and so fall into two distinct categories, distinguished by

\eta_{\star}:=\hbox{sign}(v^{2}-1)\,.

(4.22)

\eta_{\star}
is an RG-invariant quantity inasmuch as the sign of
v^{2}-1
does not depend on
\epsilon
for any
v(\epsilon)
satisfying (
4.20
).

Any specific curve can be uniquely characterized in an RG-invariant way by specifying both
\eta_{\star}
and the new variable
\epsilon_{\star}
, defined as the place where the curve passes through zero (if
\eta_{\star}=-1
) or where it diverges (if
\eta_{\star}=+1
). Using this definition the general solution (
4.21
) simplifies to

v(\epsilon)=\frac{(\epsilon/\epsilon_{\star})^{2\ell+1}+\eta_{\star}}{(\epsilon/\epsilon_{\star})^{2\ell+1}-\eta_{\star}}

(4.23)

and this shows that the pair
(\eta_{\star},\epsilon_{\star})
are related to any specific choice of initial condition
(\epsilon_{0},v_{0})
by
\eta_{\star}=\hbox{sign}(v_{0}^{2}-1)
and

\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\star}}{\epsilon_{0}}\right)^{2\ell+1}=\eta_{\star}\left(\frac{v_{0}-1}{v_{0}+1}\right)=\left|\frac{v_{0}-1}{v_{0}+1}\right|\,.

(4.24)

What makes these variables convenient is that
\epsilon_{\star}
provides an invariant length scale that is shared by all representatives
(\epsilon,v)
or
(\epsilon,\lambda)
along a particular RG trajectory. It is consequently this length scale — and
not

\epsilon_{0}
or
\lambda_{0}
, say — that is physical and so whose size characterizes the values of physical observables. This is shown in detail in
PPEFT
;
PPEFT2
;
PPEFT3
;
PPEFTDis
;
PPEFTH1
;
PPEFTH2
, where cross sections and energy shifts in many examples are evaluated and found to be simply related to
\epsilon_{\star}
.

To see explicitly why this is so, we write
\lambda(\epsilon)
in terms of
\epsilon_{\star}
by combining (
4.19
) and (
4.23
) to get

\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}+1=(2\ell+1)v(\epsilon)=(2\ell+1)\,\frac{\left({\epsilon}/{\epsilon_{\star}}\right)^{2\ell+1}+\eta_{\star}}{\left({\epsilon}/{\epsilon_{\star}}\right)^{2\ell+1}-\eta_{\star}}\ ,

(4.25)

and for later purposes also record its inverse (
c.f.
eqs. (
4.19
) and (
4.24
))

\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\star}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\ell+1}=\left|\frac{v-1}{v+1}\right|=\left|\frac{[\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon)]-\ell}{[\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon)]+\ell+1}\right|\,.

(4.26)

Eq. (
4.25
) can be used in (
4.15
) to determine the integration constant ratio
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
from which physical quantities are ultimately determined. This exercise gives

\frac{D_{\ell}(\omega)}{C_{\ell}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})\,\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}\,,

(4.27)

verifying that the explicit dependence on
\epsilon
and
\lambda
combines into the invariant combinations
\eta_{\star}
and
\epsilon_{\star}
. In particular, it is the dimensionless quantity
\omega\epsilon_{\star}
that controls the size of any physical response, and (
4.27
) shows quantitatively in this language how small angular momenta
\ell
are preferred when
\omega\epsilon_{\star}\ll 1
.

In practical examples
\epsilon_{\star}
is set by the size of the underlying object (in this case the hotspot) times the appropriate coupling that controls the interactions through which it is probed. For example, when a similar analysis is applied to describing the effects of finite nuclear size on the energy levels in pionic atoms, one finds
\epsilon_{\star}\sim R_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
is of order the nuclear radius
PPEFT2
. But the same analysis when describing nuclear-size effects on Hydrogen energy levels finds
\epsilon_{\star}\sim\alpha R_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, with
\alpha
the fine-structure constant
PPEFT
.

By comparison, if boundary conditions must be imposed for an effective theory outside the nucleus then
\epsilon>R_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
. Concrete examples like these show that a small source probed by a weakly coupled field tends to produce
\epsilon_{\star}\ll\epsilon
, if
\epsilon
is regarded to be typical linear size of the compact object.

4.2.3
Resumming all orders in
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon

It is instructive to explore the connection between
\lambda(\epsilon)
and
\epsilon_{\star}
explicitly in the weak-coupling limit, by expanding (
4.25
) in powers of
\epsilon_{\star}/\epsilon
. In this limit (
4.23
) simplifies to
v(\epsilon)\simeq 1+2\eta_{\star}\left({\epsilon_{\star}}/{\epsilon}\right)^{2\ell+1}+\cdots
, and so

\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}\simeq 2\ell+2\eta_{\star}(2\ell+1)\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\star}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\ell+1}\,.

(4.28)

For the
\ell=0
mode in particular
\lambda
becomes
\epsilon
-independent in the perturbative limit, with

\lambda\simeq 4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star}\qquad(\hbox{for}\;\ell=0\;\hbox{and}\;\epsilon_{\star}\ll\epsilon)\ .

(4.29)

This shows that for
s
-wave processes, expressions for physical quantities as functions of
\omega\epsilon_{\star}
(for mode frequency
\omega
) can be turned into corresponding expressions as functions of
\lambda\omega
, by using (
4.29
). Provided powers of
\epsilon_{\star}/\epsilon
are negligible these expressions need have no dependence on regulators like
\epsilon
, making any discussion of RG evolution completely unnecessary.

But what happens if
\epsilon
is now decreased and
\lambda(\epsilon)
adjusted along a particular RG flow to a point where
\epsilon_{\star}/\epsilon
is no longer negligible and
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
is no longer small? The answer for the physical observable as a function of
\omega\epsilon_{\star}
does not change at all, because physics depends only on which RG trajectory one lives, and not on the particular point one sits along this trajectory. All that changes as
\epsilon
and
\lambda
are varied is that expression (
4.29
) can no longer be used to trade
\epsilon_{\star}
for
\lambda
; instead one must go back to the full result (
4.25
) when doing so.

This observation provides a way to resum all orders in
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
while holding quantities like
\omega\epsilon_{\star}
fixed. Suppose one computes an observable as a function of two dimensionless quantities
O=O[\omega\epsilon,\lambda/4\pi\epsilon]
, and does so perturbatively in
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
. The result can be turned into an expression
O=O[\omega\epsilon,\epsilon_{\star}/\epsilon]
by trading
\lambda
for
\epsilon_{\star}
using (
4.29
). But we know that
\epsilon
is just a calculational artefact that is not actually in the physical result, which must therefore really only be a function of the one variable
\omega\epsilon_{\star}
.

The result for the same observable elsewhere on the RG trajectory, where
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
and
\epsilon_{\star}/\epsilon
are
not
small, is given by the same expression
O=O[\omega\epsilon_{\star}]
since it does not depend at all on
\epsilon
. Expressing this result in terms of
O=O[\omega\epsilon,\lambda/4\pi\epsilon]
using (
4.25
) then gives an explicit resummation of the observable to all orders in
\lambda(\epsilon)/4\pi\epsilon
.

4.3
Resummation of the two-point function

The above reasoning can be applied to the perturbative calculation of
\phi
-field response given above, allowing results that are derived to lowest nontrivial order in
\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
to be promoted into expressions that work to all orders in this variable.

4.3.1
s
-wave resummation

We derive the resummed results here, and then check (in this section) that they capture a full mode sum using the boundary condition (
4.6
). In later sections we also verify that the results found for large

The starting point is the perturbative expression (

W_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}-(|{{\bf x}}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}\bigg]\,.

(4.30)

Anticipating the dominance of the
\ell=0

say, see next section), we may trade
\lambda
in this expression for

W_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}+\frac{\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star}}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}-(|{{\bf x}}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}\bigg]\,.

(4.31)

But an expression with broader validity than (

) can be obtained from (

) by using in this result the more general
\ell=0
relation giving
\epsilon_{\star}
in terms of

), leading to

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{-(t-t^{\prime}-i\eta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\right]

(4.32)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\left|\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda/4\pi\epsilon)+1}\right|\bigg[\frac{|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}-(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}\bigg]\,.

This clearly agrees with (
4.30

4.3.2
Mode-sum calculation

We next recompute (
4.32
) by evaluating the
\phi
-field correlator as an exact function of
\lambda
, by calculating the sum over mode-functions whose
\lambda
-dependence is acquired through the boundary condition (
4.6
). As described above, this boundary condition fixes the ratio of integration constants
D_{\ell}/C_{\ell}
to be given in terms of
\epsilon_{\star}
as in (

\frac{D_{\ell}(\omega)}{C_{\ell}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})\,\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}\,.

(4.33)

Mode normalization

The integration constants
C_{\ell}
and
D_{\ell}
are determined separately by combining (
4.33
) with mode-function normalization, which requires

\langle u_{\omega\ell m},u_{\tilde{\omega}\tilde{\ell}\tilde{m}}\rangle=\delta(\omega-\tilde{\omega})\delta_{\ell\tilde{\ell}}\delta_{m\tilde{m}}\,,\quad\langle u_{\omega\ell m},u^{\ast}_{\tilde{\omega}\tilde{\ell}\tilde{m}}\rangle=0\,,\quad\langle u^{\ast}_{\omega\ell m},u^{\ast}_{\tilde{\omega}\tilde{\ell}\tilde{m}}\rangle=-\delta(\omega-\tilde{\omega})\delta_{\ell\tilde{\ell}}\delta_{m\tilde{m}}

(4.34)

where the angle brackets denote the Klein-Gordon inner product

\langle F,G\rangle:=i\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}x\;\bigg(F(t,{{\bf x}})\partial_{t}{G}^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}})-\partial_{t}{F}(t,{{\bf x}})G^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}})\bigg)\,.

(4.35)

As is easily verified, this inner product is time-independent when evaluated for any solutions
F,G
to the Klein-Gordon equation, and this remains true even in the presence of the modified boundary condition (
4.6
), whenever the effective coupling
\lambda
is real. To see why notice that this boundary condition implies the radial flux density of Klein-Gordon probability at
r=\epsilon
is

J_{r}(\epsilon)\propto\Bigl[F(t,{{\bf x}})\partial_{r}{G}^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}})-\partial_{r}{F}(t,{{\bf x}})G^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]_{r=\epsilon}=(\lambda^{*}-\lambda)\Bigl[F(t,{{\bf x}}){G}^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]_{r=\epsilon}

(4.36)

and so vanishes for real
\lambda
.

The normalization integrals are computed in Appendix
C.1
, leading to the following
\epsilon_{\star}
-dependent results for

and
D_{\ell}
separately

C_{\ell m}(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\pi}}\left\{1+\left[\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}\bigg(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\bigg)^{2\ell+1}\right]^{2}\right\}^{-1/2}\,,

(4.37)

and

\displaystyle D_{\ell m}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\pi}}\left\{1+\left[\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}\bigg(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\bigg)^{2\ell+1}\right]^{2}\right\}^{-1/2}\,.

(4.38)

With these choices the mode functions and field operators satisfy the required boundary condition at
r=\epsilon
, and this completely determines their dependence on
\epsilon_{\star}
.

The main approximation made in deriving (
4.37
) and (
4.38
) is to assume that
\omega\epsilon
is small enough to allow the replacement of

and

with their leading asymptotic forms; that is by using (
4.15

4.13

4.33
) using a more accurate representation of the Bessel functions.

Mode Sum

We are now in a position to compute the Wightman function in terms of a mode sum, using the above
\epsilon_{\star}
-dependent form for the modes,

u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}})=C_{\ell m}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\left[j_{\ell}(\omega r)+\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}y_{\ell}(\omega r)\right]Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\ ,

(4.39)

that properly matching at the boundary condition at
r=\epsilon
(dropping subdominant terms in
\omega\epsilon
). As argued above, all explicit dependence on
\epsilon
drops out of this exression once evaluated at
|{{\bf x}}|=\epsilon
, cancelling between any explicit dependence and the
\epsilon
-dependence implicit without the coupling
\lambda
, leaving a dependence only on the RG-invariant quantity
\epsilon_{\star}
. In particular, eqs. (
4.37
) and (
4.39
) do
not
assume validity of the weak-coupling limit

(or equivalently

need not be much smaller than unity).

The Wightman function is given in terms of these modes by

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{+\ell}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}})u^{\ast}_{\omega\ell m}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

(4.40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{+\ell}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}|C_{\ell m}(\omega)|^{2}\left[j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}y_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}y_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\right]Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)Y^{\ast}_{\ell m}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})\ .

Performing the sum over
m
(see Appendix
C.2
for details) leads to the intermediate expression

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}|C_{\ell 0}(\omega)|^{2}\left[j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}y_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\right]\qquad\qquad

(4.41)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left[j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}y_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\right]P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\ .

The terms in this sum involving two factors of

Leading order in

To make further progress we assume

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\frac{(4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star})}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\bigg[\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\bigg]+{\cal O}(\omega^{2}\epsilon_{\star}^{2})\right\}\ .

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

6
Conclusions

Black hole physics is a puzzle wrapped in an enigma hidden by a horizon, and ongoing studies of information loss show that Hawking radiation is the discovery that keeps on giving. Although Hawking radiation in principle occurs in the weak-field regime where calculation control should be good, reliable and explicit calculations of corrections to Hawking radiation are relatively rare (partly due to the extremely late times involved). Yet resolving issues such as the existence (or not) of firewalls, possible loss of locality and the like are crucial towards gaining an understanding of what a theory of quantum gravity should ultimately look like.

In this paper we construct a Caldeira-Leggett type
CaldeiraLeggett
toy model of a hot compact relativistic object that captures some of the features of black holes, and so can be used as a benchmark against which real calculations can be compared. The model is simple enough to be solved explicitly, but complicated enough to capture some of the open-system effects believed to be important for black holes. Although the toy model cannot in itself resolve the thorniest puzzles associated with horizons, it can show which features are shared with more mundane systems that are hot and relatively small.

There are several directions in which this model might fruitfully be explored. In a companion work
Companion
, we apply to it several approximate Open-EFT techniques designed to probe late-time evolution, to better identify their domains of validity and whether they can illuminate the extent to which the open nature of the hotspot causes a breakdown of local descriptions of the physics of the field
\phi
living in
{\cal R}_{+}
. A second companion
Kaplanek:2021xxx
explores the behaviour of an Unruh detector (or qubit) that couples to the field
\phi
in the vicinity of the hotspot, to determine the extent to which it thermalizes as a function of its couplings and its distance from the hotspot.

Other useful directions might explore the regime where the radius
\xi
of the interaction sphere
{\cal S}_{\xi}
is not small, and so parallels the EFT discussion of
Burgess:2018pmm
. By tuning the physics at the interface between the spaces
{\cal R}_{\pm}
one might hope to mock up the entanglement between modes inside and outside the horizon, and provide a simple analog of the matching calculations often used to extract black hole phenomenology from scattering amplitudes. The model can be further developed to include redshifting and the geometrical effects of gravitational fields in regions
{\cal R}_{\pm}
. We offer up the hotspot model in the hopes that such comparisons and extensions will prove instructive.

Appendix B
RG evolution

This Appendix summarizes some parts of the renormalization evolution not made explicit in the main text, closely following the discussion in Appendix F of
PPEFTH2
.

B.1
Universal evolution

The boundary conditions of the main text provide examples where the effective couplings are found to satisfy equations of the form

g(\epsilon)=\frac{A\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}+B}{C\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}+D}\,,

(B.1)

where
g
is a representative coupling and
\epsilon
appears on the right-hand side through the variable
\rho_{\epsilon}
. In the example of (
4.17
), for instance, we have
g=\lambda/4\pi\epsilon
while
\rho_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega\epsilon
, the power is
\zeta=\ell+\frac{1}{2}
and the parameters
A,B,C
and
D
are given explicitly by

A=\ell\,,\quad B=(\ell+1)\,X_{\ell}(\omega)\,,\quad C=1\quad\hbox{and}\quad D=-X_{\ell}(\omega)\,,

(B.2)

where

X_{\ell}(\omega):=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\ell+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{3}{2}$}}\right)\,\Gamma\left(\ell+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\right)\,\frac{D_{\ell}(\omega)}{C_{\ell}(\omega)}\,.

(B.3)

For later use, eq. (
B.1
) also inverts to give

\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}=\frac{B-Dg}{Cg-A}\,.

(B.4)

The goal is to derive a universal differential version of this evolution (see, for example
PPEFT
;
PPEFT2
;
PPEFT3
;
PPEFTDis
for more details). To start this off directly differentiate (
B.1
) holding
A,B,C,D
fixed, leading to

\epsilon\,\frac{{\hbox{d}}g}{{\hbox{d}}\epsilon}=2\zeta\left[\frac{AD-BC}{(C\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}+D)^{2}}\right]\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}=2\zeta\left[\frac{(Cg-A)(B-Dg)}{AD-BC}\right]\,,

(B.5)

where the second equality uses (
B.4
) to trade
\rho_{\epsilon}^{2\zeta}
for
g
. This evolution equation has fixed points at
g=g_{*}
, where

g_{*}=\frac{A}{C}\quad\hbox{or}\quad g_{*}=\frac{B}{D}\,,

(B.6)

which can also be seen as the
\rho_{\epsilon}\to 0
and
\rho_{\epsilon}\to\infty
limits of (
B.1
).

Equation (
B.5
) can be put into a standard form by redefining
g
to ensure that
g_{*}=\pm 1
. To this end write

g(\epsilon)=u(\epsilon)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A}{C}+\frac{B}{D}\right)\,,

(B.7)

in terms of which the fixed points are

u_{*}=\pm\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A}{C}-\frac{B}{D}\right)=\pm\left(\frac{AD-BC}{2CD}\right)\,,

(B.8)

and (
B.5
) becomes

\epsilon\,\frac{{\hbox{d}}u}{{\hbox{d}}\epsilon}=-\frac{2\zeta CD}{AD-BC}\left[u-\left(\frac{AD-BC}{2CD}\right)\right]\left[u+\left(\frac{AD-BC}{2CD}\right)\right]\,.

(B.9)

Finally rescale

u=\left[\frac{AD-BC}{2CD}\right]v

(B.10)

to see that

\epsilon\,\frac{{\hbox{d}}v}{{\hbox{d}}\epsilon}=\zeta(1-v^{2})\,

(B.11)

is an automatic consequence of (
B.1
) once one defines

g=u+\frac{AD+BC}{2CD}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A}{C}-\frac{B}{D}\right)v+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A}{C}+\frac{B}{D}\right)\,.

(B.12)

These expressions emphasize that although the positions of the fixed points for
g
depend on the ratios
A/C
and
B/D
, the speed of evolution along the RG flow depends only on
\zeta
. Indeed the general solution to (
B.11
) is

v(\epsilon)=\frac{(v_{0}+1)(\epsilon/\epsilon_{0})^{2\zeta}+(v_{0}-1)}{(v_{0}+1)(\epsilon/\epsilon_{0})^{2\zeta}-(v_{0}-1)}

(B.13)

where the integration constant is chosen to ensure
v(\epsilon_{0})=v_{0}
. For
\zeta>0
this describes a universal flow that runs from
v=-1
to
v=+1
as
\epsilon
flows from 0 to
\infty
.

Since the trajectories given in (
B.13
) cannot cross the lines
v=\pm 1
for any finite nonzero
\epsilon
there are two categories of flow, distinguished by the flow-invariant sign of
|v|-1
(see Figure
6
). That is, if
|v_{0}|-1
is negative (positive) for any
0<\epsilon_{0}<\infty
, then
|v(\epsilon)|-1
is negative (positive) for all
0<\epsilon<\infty
. Every trajectory is therefore uniquely characterized by a pair of numbers. These can equally well be chosen to be the pair
(\epsilon_{0},v_{0})
that specifies an initial condition
v_{0}=v(\epsilon_{0})
, or it can be taken to be the pair
(\epsilon_{\star},y_{\star})
where
y_{\star}=\hbox{sign}(|v|-1)=\pm 1
distinguishes the two classes of trajectories, and
\epsilon_{\star}
is defined as the value of
\epsilon
for which
v(\epsilon_{\star})=0
(if
y_{\star}=-1
) or the value for which
v(\epsilon_{\star})=\infty
(if
y_{\star}=+1
). The parameterization using
(\epsilon_{\star},y_{\star})
is useful because physical observables turn out to have particularly transparent expressions in terms of these variables.

For the specific cases given in (
B.2
) the fixed points are

\frac{A}{C}=\ell\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{B}{D}=-\ell-1

(B.14)

and
\lambda
is related to the universal scaling variable
v
by (
B.12
), which becomes

\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\left(2\ell+1\right)v-1\Bigr]\quad\hbox{and so}\quad v=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}+1\right)\,,

(B.15)

as used in the main text.

This Appendix computes the

Our mode functions have the form

\displaystyle u_{\omega\ell m}(t,{{\bf x}})

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega t}\big[C_{\ell}(\omega)j_{\ell}(\omega r)+D_{\ell}(\omega)y_{\ell}(\omega r)\big]Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi),

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{\ast}(\omega)C_{\tilde{\ell}}(\tilde{\omega})(\omega+\tilde{\omega})e^{+i(\omega-\tilde{\omega})t}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}x\;j_{\ell}(\omega r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)j_{\tilde{\ell}}(\tilde{\omega}r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+D_{\ell}^{\ast}(\omega)D_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega})(\omega+\tilde{\omega})e^{+i(\omega-\tilde{\omega})t}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}r\;r^{2}y_{\ell}(\omega r)y_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega}r)\bigg]\ .

The

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}r\;r^{2}j_{\ell}(\omega r)j_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega}r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}r\;r^{2}y_{\ell}(\omega r)y_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega}r)=\frac{\pi}{2\omega^{2}}\delta(\omega-\tilde{\omega})\ ,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell\tilde{\ell}}\delta_{m\tilde{m}}\bigg[C_{\ell}^{\ast}(\omega)C_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega})(\omega+\tilde{\omega})e^{+i(\omega-\tilde{\omega})t}\frac{\pi}{2\omega^{2}}\delta(\omega-\tilde{\omega})\qquad\qquad

(C.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+C_{\ell}^{\ast}(\omega)D_{\ell}(\tilde{\omega})(\omega+\tilde{\omega})e^{+i(\omega-\tilde{\omega})t}\frac{\left({\omega}/{\tilde{\omega}}\right)^{\ell}}{\tilde{\omega}(\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2})}

. Proper normalization then implies

and seek only the leading
\epsilon_{\star}
-dependent contribution. Because of the factor
(\omega\epsilon_{\star})^{2\ell+1}
this allows restricting to
\ell=0
in the
\epsilon_{\star}
-dependent term. In this regime we can approximate the mode sum

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)

(C.24)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)j_{0}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)y_{0}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)P_{0}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi\eta_{\star}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})}$}}\left(\frac{\omega\epsilon_{\star}}{2}\right)y_{0}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)j_{0}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)P_{0}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+{\cal O}(\omega^{2}\epsilon_{\star}^{2})\,.

where the
s
-wave normalization simplifies to
|C_{\ell m}(\omega)|^{2}\simeq\frac{\omega}{\pi}[1+{\cal O}(\omega^{2}\epsilon_{\star}^{2})]
. Next use the explicit form for the low-order spherical Bessel functions,

j_{0}(x)=\frac{\sin(x)}{x}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad y_{0}(x)=-\frac{\cos(x)}{x}\ ,

(C.25)

and along with
\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})=\frac{\pi}{2}
and
P_{0}(x)=1
to get

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\left\{\omega\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)j_{\ell}(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\right.

(C.26)

\displaystyle\;\left.-\frac{(4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star})}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\bigg[\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\bigg]\right\}\,

which drops
(\omega\epsilon_{\star})^{2}
terms. The
\ell
sum is performed using (10.60.2) from
NIST
which says

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)j_{\ell}(u)j_{\ell}(v)P_{\ell}(\cos\alpha)\ =\ \frac{\sin\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}-2uv\cos(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}-2uv\cos(\alpha)}}

(C.27)

and so

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\omega\cdot\frac{\sin\left(\omega\sqrt{|{{\bf x}}|^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}-2|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\cos(\theta)}\right)}{\omega\;\sqrt{|{{\bf x}}|^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}-2|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\cos(\theta)}}

(C.28)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\frac{(4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star})}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\bigg[\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\cos(\omega|{{\bf x}}|)\sin(\omega|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\sin\left(\omega|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{(4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star})}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\sin\Bigl[\omega\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)\Bigr]\ .

The above result uses the alignment of the coordinates so that
{{\bf x}}^{\prime}
points along the 3-axis to write

|{{\bf x}}|^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}-2|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\cos(\theta)=|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\,.

(C.29)

The frequency integral finally is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;e^{-iT\omega}\sin(X\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\omega\;\Theta(\omega)\bigg[e^{-i(T+X)\omega}+e^{-i(T-X)\omega}\bigg]

(C.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{T+X-i\delta}-\frac{1}{T-X-i\delta}\bigg)

where
\delta
is, as usual, the positive infinitesimal that arises in the Fourier transform of the Heaviside step function. In this way the mode sum evaluates to the result quoted in the main text:

\displaystyle\braket{\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}

(C.31)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{(4\pi\eta_{\star}\epsilon_{\star})}{32\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}-\frac{1}{t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}\bigg]\ .

The temperature-independent contribution,

(D.8)

It is more convenient to arrange the integrals as

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{1}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\bigg(e^{-ip(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}-e^{-ip(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}\bigg)\frac{1}{p+ic}

(D.10)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{i\epsilon}{8\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\bigg(e^{-ip(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}-e^{-ip(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;e^{-ip(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}\bigg[p-\frac{\big[c^{2}-({4\pi\lambda}/{\tilde{g}^{2}})^{2}\big]p}{c^{2}+p^{2}}\bigg]

D.1.1
Perturbative Limit of
\mathscr{S}

From here we wish to consider the perturbative limit of the above, which is taken by assuming that

c\tau\ =\ \left(\frac{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}{\tilde{g}^{2}}+\frac{4\pi\lambda}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\right)\tau\gg 1\ .

(D.19)

Note that the function
E_{1}(z)
has the following asymptotic series

E_{1}(z)\ \simeq\ e^{-z}\bigg[\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}+{\cal O}(z^{-3})\bigg]\qquad\qquad\mathrm{for}\ |z|\gg 1

(D.20)

which implies that the functions
I_{\mp}(\tau,c)
have the following asymptotic series for
|c\tau|\gg 1

I_{\mp}(\tau,c)\ \simeq\ \pm\frac{1}{c(\tau-i\delta)}-\frac{1}{c^{2}(\tau-i\delta)^{2}}+{\cal O}(|c\tau|^{-3})\qquad\qquad\mathrm{for}\ |c\tau|\gg 1\ .

(D.21)

We also note in passing that for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
(with
|\mathrm{Arg}(z)|<\pi
so not directly on the branch cut) has the series expansion

E_{1}(z)\simeq-\gamma-\log(z)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{k}}{k\cdot k!}

(D.22)

which is a convergent sum for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
but is particularly useful when
|z|\ll 1
. This means that for
|c\tau|\ll 1
we have

I_{\mp}(\tau,c)\ \simeq\ -\gamma-\log\big(c(\tau-i\delta)\big)+{\cal O}(c\tau)\qquad\qquad|c\tau|\ll 1\ ,

(D.23)

where this limit will clearly suffer from secular growth problems once
c\tau
is no longer small.

Taking the
|c\tau|\gg 1
limit of the expression
\mathscr{S}
here (dropping
{\cal O}(|c\tau|^{-3})
contributions), and simplifying after using
c=(16\pi^{2}\epsilon+4\pi\lambda)/\tilde{g}^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\left[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\right]}

(D.24)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}}\cdot\frac{|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}-(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{4}\big(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\big)^{2}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}|}\frac{t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|}{\big[(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-i\delta)^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]^{2}}+\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\frac{t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{\big[(t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)^{2}-|{{\bf x}}|^{2}\big]^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\big(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\big)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)^{2}}\ .

For perturbatively small
\lambda
(meaning
\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon)\ll 1
) the above turns into

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\left[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\right]}

(D.25)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\cdot\frac{|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}-(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{4}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}|}\frac{t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|}{\big[(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-i\delta)^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]^{2}}+\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\frac{t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{\big[(t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)^{2}-|{{\bf x}}|^{2}\big]^{2}}\bigg]

where we neglect
{\cal O}(\lambda^{2})
contributions. Notice that this exactly matches the temperature-independent contribution to the correlator in the perturbative limit (see (
3.10
)).

D.1.2
Evaluating the integrals
I_{\mp}(\tau,c)

Here we evaluate the integrals
I_{\mp}(\tau,c)
defined in (
D.13
). Since
I_{-}(\tau,c)=I_{+}^{\ast}(-\tau,c)
, it suffices to compute
I_{-}(\tau,c)
here. Splitting apart
I_{-}(\tau,c)
into real and imaginary parts gives

I_{-}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p\cos(p\tau)+c\sin(p\tau)}{p^{2}+c^{2}}+i\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{c\cos(p\tau)+p\sin(p\tau)}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\ .

(D.26)

Assuming that
c>0
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and using formulas (3.723.1)-(3.723.4) from
grad
the above can be easily evaluated to give

I_{-}(\tau,c)=-e^{c\tau}\mathrm{Ei}(-c\tau)+i\pi e^{c\tau}\Theta(-\tau)

(D.27)

where
\mathrm{Ei}(x):=-\int_{-x}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}u\;\frac{e^{-u}}{u}
is the exponential integral function. Since
I_{-}(\tau,c)=I_{+}^{\ast}(-\tau,c)
this immediately implies that

I_{+}(\tau,c)=-e^{-c\tau}\mathrm{Ei}(c\tau)-i\pi e^{-c\tau}\Theta(\tau)

(D.28)

The above formulae can be simplified into a more useful form by relating it to the function
E_{1}(z):=\int_{z}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}u\;\frac{e^{-u}}{u}
(the so-called exponential
E_{n}
-function with
n=1
, related to the exponential integral function for
x>0
by
E_{1}(x)=-\mathrm{Ei}(-x)
). Obviously
E_{1}
closely related to
\mathrm{Ei}
— however for complex arguments, the definition of
\mathrm{Ei}
becomes somewhat ambiguous due to branch points at
0
and
\infty
, and so
E_{1}
is better defined for this reason.

Noting the behaviour of the function
E_{1}(z)
nearby its branch cut (along the negative real axis) where

\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}E_{1}(-x\pm i\delta)\ =\ -\mathrm{Ei}(x)\mp i\pi\qquad\qquad\mathrm{for}\ x>0\ ,

(D.29)

the functions
I_{\mp}(\tau,c)
can be written in the more useful form

\displaystyle I_{-}(\tau,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{c\tau}E_{1}\big(c(\tau-i\delta)\big)

(D.30)

\displaystyle I_{+}(\tau,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-c\tau}E_{1}\big(-c(\tau-i\delta)\big)

where the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}
is understood as usual (note that we have
E_{1}^{\ast}(z)=E_{1}(z^{\ast})
is satisfied, and so
I_{-}(\tau)=I_{+}(-\tau)
remains true).

D.2
The temperature-dependent contribution,
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

In order to evaluate the trace, we put the system in a box (as done in Appendix
A.1
for the free thermal correlation function). Performing the trace, and then reverting back to the continuum limit, yields the mode sum

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{N}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{p}}\bigg[s^{a}_{{{\bf p}}}(t,{{\bf x}})s^{a\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})+\frac{2\mathrm{Re}\left[s^{a}_{{{\bf p}}}(t,{{\bf x}})s^{a\ast}_{{{\bf p}}}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\right]}{e^{\beta E_{p}}-1}\bigg]\ .

(D.31)

Using the explicit form of the mode function
s^{a}_{{{\bf p}}}
given in (
5.11
) the function
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})
turns into the integral

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}$}}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{p}}\bigg[\;\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{e^{-iE_{p}(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}E_{p}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\right)^{2}}$}}+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\cos\big[E_{p}(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\big]}{\left[\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}E_{p}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\right)^{2}\right]\left[e^{\beta E_{p}}-1\right]}$}}\;\bigg]\ .

(D.32)

Integrating the angles away and simplifying the above yields

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\bigg[\;e^{-ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}+\frac{2\cos\big[p(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\big]}{e^{\beta p}-1}\;\bigg]\qquad

(D.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}I\big(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|,c,\beta\big)

where
c
is the constant (
5.18
) consisting of the couplings and
\epsilon
defined by

c\ :=\ \frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}}{\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}}\ =\ \frac{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}{\tilde{g}^{2}}+\frac{4\pi\lambda}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\ ,

(D.34)

and we define the integral

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\bigg[\;e^{-i\tau p}+\frac{2\cos(\tau p)}{e^{\beta p}-1}\;\bigg]\ ,

(D.35)

which we evaluate here for
c,\beta>0
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. To compute
I
, use
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\sin(pq)=\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}
giving

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;e^{-cq}\sin(pq)\bigg[\;e^{-i\tau p}+\frac{2\cos(\tau p)}{e^{\beta p}-1}\;\bigg]\ .

(D.36)

Next we rearrange the above into the form

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{i\big(e^{-i(\tau+q)p}-e^{-i(\tau-q)p}\big)}{2}$}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\sin\big((\tau+q)p\big)-\sin\big((\tau-q)p\big)}{e^{\beta p}-1}$}}\bigg]\ .

(D.37)

We note the elementary result (with the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}
understood)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Theta(x)e^{-iyx}\ =\ \int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}x\;e^{-iyx}\ =\ \frac{-i}{y-i\delta}

(D.38)

as well as formula (3.911.2) from
grad
(valid for
a\neq 0
and
\mathrm{Re}[\beta]>0
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}x\;\frac{\sin(ax)}{e^{\beta x}-1}\ =\ \frac{\pi}{2\beta}\coth\left(\frac{\pi a}{\beta}\right)-\frac{1}{2a}\ .

(D.39)

With these formulae the integral (
D.37
) becomes

\displaystyle I(\tau,c,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\bigg[\frac{1}{\tau+q-i\delta}-\frac{1}{\tau-q-i\delta}

(D.40)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\pi}{\beta}\coth\left(\frac{\pi(\tau+q)}{\beta}\right)-\frac{1}{\tau+q}-\frac{\pi}{\beta}\coth\left(\frac{\pi(\tau-q)}{\beta}\right)+\frac{1}{\tau-q}\bigg]\ .

Using the result of the Sochocki-Plemelj theorem

\frac{1}{x\pm i\delta}\ =\ \frac{1}{x}\mp i\pi\delta(x)\ ,

(D.41)

the integral (
D.40
) simplifies to

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\bigg[i\pi\delta(\tau+q)-i\pi\delta(\tau-q)+\frac{\pi}{\beta}\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(\tau+q)}{\beta}$}}\right)-\frac{\pi}{\beta}\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(\tau-q)}{\beta}$}}\right)\bigg]\ .

(D.42)

Applying the Sochocki-Plemelj theorem yet again to the above yields

\displaystyle I(\tau,c,\beta)=\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\bigg[\coth\left(\frac{\pi(q+[\tau-i\delta])}{\beta}\right)+\coth\left(\frac{\pi(q-[\tau-i\delta])}{\beta}\right)\bigg]

(D.43)

where the behaviour
\coth(z)\simeq{1}/{z}
near
z\simeq 0
allows use of formula (
D.41
) (and so justifying the
i\delta
-prescription in the arguments of the
\coth(\cdot)
functions). From here we note the identity

\coth\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)=\frac{2}{1-e^{-z}}-1\ ,

(D.44)

and make the change of integration variable to
Q={2\pi q}/{\beta}
giving

\displaystyle I(\tau,c,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}Q\;e^{-\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}Q}\bigg[\coth\left(\frac{Q+{2\pi[\tau-i\delta]}/{\beta})}{2}\right)+\coth\left(\frac{Q-{2\pi[\tau-i\delta]}/{\beta})}{2}\right)\bigg]\qquad\qquad

(D.45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}Q\;e^{-\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}Q}\left[\frac{1}{1-e^{-{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}/{\beta}}e^{-Q}}+\frac{1}{1-e^{+{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}/{\beta}}e^{-Q}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Phi\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}{\beta}},1,\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\Phi\bigg(e^{+\tfrac{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}{\beta}},1,\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}\bigg)-\frac{\pi}{c\beta}\ ,

where we use the integral representation (see formula (25.14.5) in
NIST
)

\Phi(z,s,a)\ =\ \frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}x\;\frac{x^{s-1}e^{-ax}}{1-ze^{-x}}\qquad\mathrm{valid\ for\ }\mathrm{Re}[s]>0,\ \mathrm{Re}[a]>0\ \&\ z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty)

(D.46)

where
\Phi(z,s,a)
is the Lerch Transcendent, usually defined by the series (see formula (25.14.1) in
NIST
)

\Phi(z,s,a)\ =\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{(a+n)^{s}}\qquad\mathrm{valid\ for\ }|z|<1\ .

(D.47)

For other values of
z\in\mathbb{C}
(
ie.
not inside the unit disc) the function
\Phi
is defined via analytic continuation in the complex plane. At the end of the day, using the above formula (
D.45
) in (
D.33
) yields

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\Phi\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)}{\beta}},1,\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}\bigg)+\Phi\bigg(e^{+\tfrac{2\pi(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)}{\beta}},1,\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}\bigg)-\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}\bigg]

(D.48)

which is the result quoted in (
5.20
) in the main text.

D.2.1
Perturbative Limit of
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}

Here we take the perturbative limit of
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
which turns out to be the limit in which

\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}\gg 1\ .

(D.49)

We first note the asymptotic series of the Lerch transcendent for any large
a

\Phi(z,s,a)\simeq\frac{1}{1-z}\frac{1}{a^{s}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^{n}\Gamma(s+n)}{n!\;\Gamma(s)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{-n}(z)}{a^{s+n}}+{\cal O}(a^{-s-N})\qquad\quad\mathrm{for\ }a\gg 1

(D.50)

for fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}
and fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty)
, where
\mathrm{Li}_{-n}(z)=\left(z\partial_{z}\right)^{n}\frac{z}{1-z}
are polylogarithm functions of negative integer order. Using
\mathrm{Li}_{-1}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}
and
\mathrm{Li}_{-2}(z)=\frac{z+z^{2}}{(1-z)^{3}}
as well as
\mathrm{Li}_{-3}(z)=\frac{z+4z^{2}+z^{3}}{(1-z)^{4}}
we find that for
c\beta/(2\pi)\gg 1
we have

\displaystyle\Phi\left(z,1,\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{1-z}$}}\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}$}}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}\right)^{2}+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{z+z^{2}}{(1-z)^{3}}$}}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}\right)^{3}+{\cal O}\big((c\beta)^{-4}\big)

(D.51)

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{z}$}},1,\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\left[1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{1-z}$}}\right]\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}$}}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}\right)^{2}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{z+z^{2}}{(1-z)^{3}}$}}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}$}}\right)^{3}+{\cal O}\big((c\beta)^{-4}\big)

Which means that (half of) the sum of these two functions has the asymptotics

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(z,1,\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{z}$}},1,\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{c\beta}-\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}\bigg)^{2}+{\cal O}\big((c\beta)^{-4}\big)\ ,\qquad\qquad

(D.52)

which when used for the function
I(\tau,c,\beta)
given in (
D.45
) yields

\displaystyle I(\tau,c,\beta)\simeq-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{e^{-\tfrac{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}{\beta}}}{\bigg[1-e^{-\tfrac{2\pi(\tau-i\delta)}{\beta}}\bigg]^{2}}$}}\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{c\beta}\bigg)^{2}+{\cal O}\big((c\beta)^{-4}\big)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{c^{2}\beta^{2}}\mathrm{csch}^{2}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi[\tau-i\delta]}{\beta}$}}\right)+{\cal O}\big((c\beta)^{-4}\big)\qquad\qquad

(D.53)

Using this and
c=\frac{16\pi^{2}\epsilon}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\right)
, at the end of the day we find that
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
in the perturbative limit is

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{csch}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi[t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta]}{\beta}\right)+\ldots

which (at leading-order) is exactly the expected temperature-dependent contribution to the perturbative result when
\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon)\ll 1
(see formula (
3.10
), which neglects
{\cal O}(\lambda^{2})
contributions).

D.2.2
I(\tau,c,\beta)
in the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}

Because
\Phi(z,s,a)
has a branch cut along
z\in[1,\infty)
, the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}
of the expression (
D.45
) is somewhat tricky to take. For completeness we take this limit here: to this end, revert back to the integral form (
D.42
) and integrate the
\delta
-functions explicitly to get

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\bigg[\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(q+\tau)}{\beta}$}}\right)+\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(q-\tau)}{\beta}$}}\right)\bigg]+\frac{i\pi}{2}\bigg[e^{+c\tau}\Theta(-\tau)-e^{-c\tau}\Theta(\tau)\bigg]

(D.54)

The remaining integrals over
q
are now ill-defined for general
\tau
(the reason for this being that for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
one of the two
\coth(\cdot)
functions gets integrated over a singularity at
q=\tau
). Since the remaining integral is clearly symmetric under
\tau\to-\tau
, we assume for now that
\tau>0
. For
\tau>0
the first integral is easier to compute, where we change the integration variable to
u=\exp\big(-2\pi(q+\tau)/\beta\big)
where

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\coth\left(\frac{\pi(q+\tau)}{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}e^{c\tau}\int_{0}^{\exp\left(-2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}{\hbox{d}}u\;u^{\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}-1}\bigg[\frac{2}{1-u}-1\bigg]

(D.55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e^{c\tau}\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\tau}{\beta}};\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)-\frac{\pi}{2c\beta}

where
\mathrm{B}(z;a,b)=\int_{0}^{z}{\hbox{d}}u\;u^{a-1}(1-u)^{b-1}
is the incomplete Beta function. For the second integral, the procedure is similar with the added complication that the integrand gets integrated over the root at
q=-\tau
(since we assume here that
\tau>0
). We find in much the same way that

\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(q-\tau)}{\beta}$}}\right)=\frac{e^{-c\tau}}{2}\int_{0}^{\exp\left(+2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}{\hbox{d}}u\;u^{\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}-1}(1-u)^{-1}-\frac{\pi}{2c\beta}\ ,

(D.56)

however since the upper limit on the integral is greater than 1 (since
\tau>0
is assumed), the above integrand gets integrated over a singularity at
u=1
. Interpreting the above integral as a Cauchy Principal value turns the above into

\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi}{2\beta}$}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(q-\tau)}{\beta}$}}\right)=\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{e^{-c\tau}}{2}$}}\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1-\eta}+\int_{1+\eta}^{\exp\left(+2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}\bigg]{\hbox{d}}u\;u^{\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}-1}(1-u)^{-1}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi}{2c\beta}$}}\ .

(D.57)

The first integral is easily seen to evaluate to
\mathrm{B}\big(1-\delta;\frac{c\beta}{2\pi},0\big)
, while the second integral requires a variable change
u=1/v
giving

\displaystyle\int_{1+\eta}^{\exp\left(+2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}{\hbox{d}}u\;u^{\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}-1}(1-u)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1/(1+\eta)}^{\exp\left(-2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}{\hbox{d}}v\;v^{-\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}}(1-v)^{-1}

(D.58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg[\int_{0}^{\exp\left(-2\pi\tau/\beta\right)}-\int_{0}^{1/(1+\eta)}\bigg]{\hbox{d}}v\;v^{-\tfrac{c\beta}{2\pi}}(1-v)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\tau}{\beta}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)-\mathrm{B}\bigg(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{1+\eta}$}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)

which then implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\coth\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\pi(q-\tau)}{\beta}$}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-c\tau}}{2}\bigg[\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\tau}{\beta}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)

(D.59)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\bigg\{\mathrm{B}\bigg(1-\eta;\frac{c\beta}{2\pi},0\bigg)-\mathrm{B}\bigg(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{1+\eta}$}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)\bigg\}\bigg]-\frac{\pi}{2c\beta}\ .

The limit can be taken noting
\mathrm{B}(z;a,0)\simeq-\log(1-z)-\psi^{(0)}(a)-\gamma+{\cal O}(z)
for
z\to 1^{-}
(with
\psi^{(0)}(z):=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
the digamma function) giving

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-cq}\coth\left(\frac{\pi(q-\tau)}{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-c\tau}}{2}\bigg[\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\tau}{\beta}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)+\pi\cot\left(\frac{\beta c}{2}\right)\bigg]-\frac{\pi}{2c\beta}\ .

where the identity
\psi^{(0)}(1-a)-\psi^{(0)}(a)=\pi\cot(\pi a)
has been used. Putting the above all together (extending the domain to
\tau>0
for the above integrals) leaves

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\frac{e^{c|\tau|}}{2}\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi|\tau|}{\beta}};\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)+\frac{e^{-c|\tau|}}{2}\bigg[\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi|\tau|}{\beta}};1-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)+\pi\cot\left(\frac{c\beta}{2}\right)\bigg]-\frac{\pi}{c\beta}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\mathrm{sign}(\tau)e^{-c|\tau|}\ .

(D.60)

To write the above formula in a slightly more convenient manner, we note formula (8.17.20) from
NIST
which implies that

B(z;1-a,0)=B(z;-a,0)+\frac{z^{-a}}{a}\ ,

(D.61)

and so allows us to write the above formula as

I(\tau,c,\beta)=\frac{e^{c|\tau|}}{2}\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi|\tau|}{\beta}};\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)+\frac{e^{-c|\tau|}}{2}\bigg[\mathrm{B}\bigg(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi|\tau|}{\beta}};-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{c\beta}{2\pi}$}},0\bigg)+\pi\cot\left(\frac{c\beta}{2}\right)\bigg]-\frac{i\pi}{2}\mathrm{sign}(\tau)e^{-c|\tau|}\ .

(D.62)

The beta function can only be related to the Lerch transcendent for arguments
|z|<1
, which explains why the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}
is not straightforward from the representation (
D.45
).

D.2.3
KMS-like Condition for
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}

Here we show that the function
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
is thermal, in the sense that it obeys a KMS-like condition (as does the free thermal correlator of Appendix
A.1.1
) where

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t-i\beta,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t,{{\bf x}})

(D.63)

c.f.
equation (
A.15
). The proof for this follows almost identically as the proof given in Appendix
A.1.1
, save for the fact that
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
enjoys a time-translation invariance only when
t>|{{\bf x}}|
and
t^{\prime}>|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
— in this limit, we have the representation (
D.33
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\bigg[\;\frac{e^{-ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{1-e^{-\beta p}}+\frac{e^{+ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{e^{\beta p}-1}\;\bigg]\ ,

(D.64)

after using the identity
1+(e^{\beta p}-1)^{-1}=(1-e^{-\beta p})^{-1}
. The above function is a complex analytic function of time for in the strip where
-\beta<\mathrm{Im}[t]<0
— we then clearly have

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t-i\beta,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\bigg[\;\frac{e^{-\beta p}e^{-ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{1-e^{-\beta p}}+\frac{e^{+\beta p}e^{+ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{e^{\beta p}-1}\;\bigg]

(D.65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{g}^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\frac{p}{p^{2}+c^{2}}\bigg[\;\frac{e^{-ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{e^{\beta p}-1}+\frac{e^{+ip(t-|{{\bf x}}|-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}}{1-e^{-\beta p}}\;\bigg]

(D.66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{\beta}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t,{{\bf x}})

(D.67)

which shows that (
D.63
) holds true. Note however that the full correlation function
W_{\beta}=\mathscr{S}+\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
is not explicitly thermal since the temperature-independent contribution
\mathscr{S}
obviously fails to satisfy a KMS-like condition analogous to (
D.63
). However, in a limit where
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
dominates over
\mathscr{S}
(provided
t>|{{\bf x}}|
and
t^{\prime}>|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
) one should expect to see thermality manifest itself more directly (this is explored using an Unruh-Dewitt detector model in
Companion
, where it is shown that a stationary qubit outside the hotspot thermalizes whilst only interacting with
\phi
).
