The Weak differentiability of norm and a generalized Bhatia-Šemrl Theorem Thanks: The research of the author is sponsored by the Institute Post Doctoral Fellowship at Indian Institute of Technology Bombay.
Saikat Roy

Abstract
. We completely characterize the weak differentiability (or, in other words Gateaux differentiability) of the norm in the spaces of bounded multilinear maps. Also, we obtain a multilinear generalization of the well-known Bhatia-Šemrl theorem on Birkhoff-James orthogonality.

Primary 46G25, 47A30 Secondary 46A32

1.
Introduction

The aim of this article is to characterize the weak differentiability of norm in the spaces of bounded multilinear maps that works equally well in the contexts of (real and complex) normed linear spaces and Hilbert spaces, regardless of the dimension (finite or infinite).
Let
(\mathbb{X},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed linear space over a field
\mathbb{F}
(
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
). The norm
\|\cdot\|
is said to be
weakly differentiable
or
Gateaux differentiable
at a non-zero point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
, if

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|x_{0}+ty\|-\|x_{0}\|}{t}

exists finitely for all
y\in\mathbb{X}
. The study of differentiability of norm in the spaces of operators has a rich literature background
[
1
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
12
]
. The initiation of such a study can be traced back to the seminal works due to Heinrich, followed by Abatzoglou and Hennefeld. More recently, the weak differentiability of norm in the spaces of bounded linear (bilinear) operators have been studied in
[
15
,
20
,
26
]
, in the setting of real normed linear spaces. It is worth mentioning that the current work presents a tractable characterization of Birkhoff-James orthogonality in the spaces of multilinear maps which facilitates a significant improvement of the previously obtained results on the weak differentiability of the norm in the spaces of linear (bilinear) operators.

Notations and terminologies
. Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
denote normed linear spaces (
1\leq i\leq k\penalty\ ;

k\in\mathbb{N}
), over the field
\mathbb{F}
of real or complex numbers (
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
). The symbol
\mathbb{H}
is reserved for a Hilbert space equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle.
A
k
-linear map

\mathsf{T}:\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}\to\mathbb{Y}
is a function that is separately linear in each variable. For simplicity, we denote the norm in each
\mathbb{X}_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) and
\mathbb{Y}
by the same symbol
\|\cdot\|.
The map
\mathsf{T}
is said to be
bounded
, if there exists a positive constant
C
such that
\|\mathsf{T}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\|\leq C\|x_{1}\|\dots\|x_{k}\|
for all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}.
In that case the norm of
\mathsf{T}
is defined by

\|\mathsf{T}\|=\sup\{\|\mathsf{T}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\|:\penalty\ \|x_{1}\|=\dots=\|x_{k}\|=1\}.

The product space
\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}
is always equipped with the product topology. Note that a bounded
k
-linear map is continuous and vice versa. We use the symbol
\mathbf{x}
to denote a member
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
in
\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}
while the members of a normed space
\mathbb{X}
are simply written as
x.
The zero vector in any space other than the scalar field is written as
\theta
. The closed unit ball and the unit sphere of a normed linear space
\mathbb{X}
are denoted by
B_{\mathbb{X}}
and
S_{\mathbb{X}}
, respectively. The symbol
\mathbb{X}^{*}
stands for the topological dual of
\mathbb{X}.
We write
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y})
to denote the space of all bounded
k
-linear maps from
\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}
to
\mathbb{Y}
, endowed with the usual supremum norm on
S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}.
In particular when
k=1
, the space
\mathcal{B}^{1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
(or
\mathcal{B}^{1}(\mathbb{X})
when
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{Y}
) is the space of all bounded linear operators from
\mathbb{X}
to
\mathbb{Y}
, endowed with the usual operator norm. The identity operator on
\mathbb{X}
is denoted by
\mathbf{I}.
Let
M_{\mathsf{T}}
stand for the norm attainment set of the
k
-linear map
\mathsf{T}
, i.e.,

M_{\mathsf{T}}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}:\penalty\ \|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathsf{T}\|\right\}.

A standard compactness argument ensures that
M_{\mathsf{T}}\neq\emptyset
, when
\mathbb{X}_{i}
is finite-dimensional for each
i=1,\dots,k
. We cannot ensure the same if any
\mathbb{X}_{i}
is infinite-dimensional. However, in that case we can always ensure the existence of a norming sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}
such that
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|
. By Hahn-Banach Theorem, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find
y_{n}^{*}\in S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}
such that
y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})=\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|.
Thus, the following collection of sequences

\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}:=\left\{((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\subseteq\left(S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}\right)\times S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}:\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})\to\|\mathsf{T}\|\right\},

is always non-empty, regardless
\mathbb{X}_{i},\mathbb{Y}
are finite or infinite-dimensional for
1\leq i\leq k.

Following Birkhoff
[
2
]
, we say that an element
x\in\mathbb{X}
is Birkhoff-James orthogonal to another element
y\in\mathbb{X}
, written as
x\perp_{B}y
, if
\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|
for all scalars
\lambda.
James
[
11
]
showed that
x\perp_{B}y
if and only if there exists
x^{*}\in S_{\mathbb{X}^{*}}
such that
x^{*}(x)=\|x\|
and
x^{*}(y)=0.
Given any non-zero
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the non-empty collection
J(x):=\{x^{*}\in S_{\mathbb{X}^{*}}:\penalty\ x^{*}(x)=\|x\|\}
is known as the collection of all support functionals at
x
. The point
x
is called smooth if
J(x)
is a singleton set. Weak differentiability of norm can also be characterized in terms of Birkhoff-James orthogonality and support functionals. Let us recall that famous result due to James which will play the central role in this paper.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
11
]
, Theorem 4.2)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a normed linear space and let
x\in\mathbb{X}
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

Birkhoff-James orthogonality is right-additive at
x
, i.e.,
x\perp_{B}y
and
x\perp_{B}z
imply that
x\perp_{B}(y+z)
for all
y,z\in\mathbb{X}
.

(ii)

x
is smooth.

(iii)

The norm in
\mathbb{X}
is weakly differentiable at
x
.

Including the introductory part, the present work is demarcated into three sections. In the second section, we completely characterize the Birkhoff-James orthogonality of multilinear maps, in the setting of normed linear spaces, which is a new addition to the existing literature. The said characterization, whenever restricted to the setting of Hilbert space, leads to a multilinear generalization of the well-known Bhatia-Šemrl Theorem in both finite and infinite-dimensional cases. Finally, in the third section, we employ the results of the second section to characterize the weak differentiability of the norm in the spaces of multilinear maps. The cumulative results of this section extend and strengthen some earlier results related to the weak differentiability of the norm in the spaces of linear operators in both finite and infinite-dimensional contexts
[
15
,
20
,
25
]
.

2.
Orthogonality of Multilinear Maps

We begin with a lemma which will be used in the sequel. Given any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \lambda
and
\overline{\lambda}
denote the real part of
\lambda
and conjugate of
\lambda
, respectively.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be normed linear spaces;
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
. Then the collection

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}):=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}:\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})\to\lambda,\penalty\ ((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}\right\},

is a compact subset of
\mathbb{F}.

Proof.

Evidently
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is bounded. Consider a sequence
(\lambda_{j})\subseteq\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
such that
\lambda_{j}\to\lambda_{0}
, for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
. We show that
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).
Clearly, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a sequence
\left(\mathbf{x}_{n,j},y^{*}_{n,j}\right)\subseteq\left(S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}\right)\times S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}
such that

{y^{*}_{n,j}\left(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n,j}\right)}\to\lambda_{j}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad y^{*}_{n,j}\left(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n,j}\right)\to\|\mathsf{T}\|,\penalty\ \mathrm{as}\penalty\ n\to\infty.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, choose
m_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
in such a way that the following hold:

(i)\penalty\ \left|{y^{*}_{m_{j},j}\left(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{m_{j},j}\right)}-\lambda_{j}\right|<\frac{1}{j},\quad(ii)\penalty\ \left|y^{*}_{m_{j},j}\left(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{m_{j},j}\right)-\|\mathsf{T}\|\right|<\frac{1}{j}.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, put

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\mathbf{x}_{m_{j},j},\penalty\ \widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}=y^{*}_{m_{j},j}

Note that

\displaystyle\left|{\widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{A}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})}-\lambda_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{A}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})}-\lambda_{j}\right|+|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{0}|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{1}{j}+|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{0}|.

Letting
j\to\infty,
we have
{\widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{A}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})}\to\lambda_{0}.
Also,
\widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\to\|\mathsf{T}\|
, as
j\to\infty
. Therefore,
((\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\widetilde{y}^{*}_{j}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
and
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
.
∎

For a non-empty subset
D
of
\mathbb{F}
, let
\mathbf{Co}(D)
denote the convex hull of
D
. Note that
\mathbf{Co}(D)
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{F}
, whenever
D
is compact. Therefore, the preceding lemma ensures that
\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{F}
, for any
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
. We use this fact in the following result to completely characterize Birkhoff-James orthogonality in
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right).
This is one of the main results of the paper.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be normed linear spaces;
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
.

(ii)

0\in\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})),
where
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.1
).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that
\|\mathsf{A}\|=\|\mathsf{T}\|=1
.

(i)
\implies
(ii): Suppose if possible
0\notin\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})).
We now proceed in three steps to obtain a contradiction.

Step I:
Since
\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))\subseteq\mathbb{F}
is compact, rotating
\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
suitably, we may assume that
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \lambda>r
for some
0<r<\dfrac{1}{2}
and for all
\lambda\in\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
. In particular,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \lambda>r\quad\forall\penalty\ \lambda\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).

Step II:
Define

G:=\left\{(\mathbf{x},y^{*})\in\left(S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}\right)\times S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}:\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x})\overline{y^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})}\leq r\right\}.

We claim that
\sup\{|y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x})|:\penalty\ (\mathbf{x},y^{*})\in G\}<1-2\varepsilon
for some
0<\varepsilon<\dfrac{1}{2}.
Suppose on contrary that there exists a sequence
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\subseteq G
so that
|y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})|\to 1.
Then we can find a subsequence
(n_{j})
of natural numbers such that

(i)\penalty\ y^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\to\sigma,\penalty\ |\sigma|=1;\penalty\ (ii)\penalty\ {y^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})}\to\alpha.

Put
\widetilde{y}_{n_{j}}^{*}=\overline{\sigma}\penalty\ y^{*}_{n_{j}}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}.
We now have
\widetilde{y}_{n_{j}}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\to\overline{\sigma}\alpha\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}),
since
((\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}},\widetilde{y}^{*}_{n_{j}}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \overline{\sigma}\alpha>r
, by (
2.2
). Observe that

\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ {y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\overline{{y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})}=\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \widetilde{y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\overline{\widetilde{y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})}\to\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \sigma\overline{\alpha}>r.

However, it follows from the definition of
G
that

\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ {y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\overline{{y}^{*}_{n_{j}}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})}\leq r,\qquad\mathrm{for\penalty\ all}\penalty\ j\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, we arrive at a contradiction and the claim is proved.

Step III:
Choose
0<\mu<\min\left\{\varepsilon,r\right\}.
Then for any
(\mathbf{x},y^{*})\in G
, we have

|y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}-\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})|<1-2\varepsilon+\mu<1-\varepsilon.

On the other hand, given any
(\mathbf{x},y^{*})\in\left(\left(S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}\right)\times S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}\right)\setminus G

|y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}-\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})|^{2}\leq 1+\mu^{2}-2\mu\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x})\overline{y^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})}\leq 1+\mu^{2}-2\mu r.

In other words,

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}-\mu\mathsf{A}\|=\sup\left\{|y^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}-\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})|:\penalty\ (\mathbf{x},y^{*})\in\left(S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}\right)\times S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}\right\}<1=\|\mathsf{T}\|.

This is a contradiction as
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}.

(ii)
\implies
(i): We first show that for any non-zero scalar
\kappa
, there exists
\lambda\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
such that
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \kappa\lambda\geq 0.
Indeed, since
0\in\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
, by Carathéodory Theorem there exist
\lambda_{i}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}),t_{i}\in[0,1]
,
1\leq i\leq 3;
such that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}t_{i}\lambda_{i}=0,\penalty\ \sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}t_{i}=1.

Thus,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}t_{i}\kappa\lambda_{i}=0
and at least for one
\lambda_{i},
we must have
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \kappa\lambda_{i}\geq 0.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}
be non-zero. By the forgoing discussion, we can find
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
such that
\mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\lambda_{0}\geq 0
. More precisely, we can find
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
such that

{y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})}\to\lambda_{0}\penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\penalty\ \lambda_{0}\geq 0.

So, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}+\gamma\mathsf{A}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\gamma\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})|^{2}+|\gamma y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})\overline{y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})}

\displaystyle\geq|y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})\overline{y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})}.

Letting
n\to\infty,
we have

|y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})\overline{y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})}\to 1+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \gamma\penalty\ \lambda_{0}\geq 1.

Consequently,
\|\mathsf{T}+\gamma\mathsf{A}\|\geq\|\mathsf{T}\|.
Since
\gamma
was chosen arbitrarily, we get
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
. The proof is now complete.
∎

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}

(ii)

Let
\mathbb{H}
be an

-dimensional Hilbert space. We can think of
\mathcal{B}^{1}(\mathbb{H})
as the space of all

matrices, identified as operators acting on
\mathbb{H}

reduces to the well-known Bhatia-Šemrl Theorem, once we apply the Toeplitz-Hausdorff Theorem

. We sketch the outline of the proof in the following remark.

Remark 2.3.1
.

Let
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}
be two non-zero
n\times n
matrices acting on
\mathbb{H}
. It follows from Riesz representation Theorem that given any non-zero

, the unique support functional

at

is of the form
y^{*}(z)=\left\langle z,\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right\rangle
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}.
Now, it is not difficult to see that

\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T})=\left\{\left\langle\mathsf{A}x,\frac{\mathsf{T}x}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}\right\rangle:\penalty\ x\in M_{\mathsf{T}}\right\}.

On the other hand, it is well-known
[
23
]
that
M_{\mathsf{T}}=S_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}
, where
\mathbb{H}_{0}
is the eigen space of
\mathsf{T}^{*}\mathsf{T}
corresponding to the highest eigen value
\|\mathsf{T}\|^{2}
. Therefore, we have

\left\{\left\langle\mathsf{A}x,\frac{\mathsf{T}x}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}\right\rangle:\penalty\ x\in M_{\mathsf{T}}\right\}=\left\{\left\langle\mathsf{A}x,\frac{\mathsf{T}x}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}\right\rangle:\penalty\ x\in S_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}\right\},

which is convex by the Toeplitz-Hausdorff Theorem
[
6
]
. Consequently, it follows from Corollary
2.3
that

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}\penalty\ \iff\penalty\ \exists\penalty\ x_{0}\in S_{\mathbb{H}}\penalty\ \mathrm{such\penalty\ that}\penalty\ \|\mathsf{T}x_{0}\|=\|\mathsf{T}\|\penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}x_{0},\mathsf{T}x_{0}\rangle=0.

One can expect more sophisticated characterization of orthogonality in
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{H}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{H}_{k},\mathbb{H}\right)
due to the inner product structure of a Hilbert space. For this reason, given any
\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{H}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{H}_{k},\mathbb{H}\right),
below we consider another (non-empty) collection of scalars:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}):=\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}:\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle\to\beta,\penalty\ (\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{H}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{H}_{k}},\penalty\ \|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|\right\}.

The following result completely characterizes Birkhoff-James orthogonality of multilinear maps in the setting of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{H}
be Hilbert spaces,
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{H}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{H}_{k},\mathbb{H}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
.

(ii)

0\in\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
, where
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.1
).

(iii)

0\in\mathbf{Co}(\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}))
, where
\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.4
).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\|\mathsf{T}\|=\|\mathsf{A}\|=1.
Note that the equivalence of (i) and (ii) is already established in Theorem
2.2
. So we only prove (ii)
\implies
(iii) and (iii)
\implies
(i).

(ii)
\implies
(iii): It is enough to show that
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).
Let
\lambda\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).
Then there exists a sequence
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
so that

(i)\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})\to 1,\penalty\ (ii)\penalty\ {y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})}\to\lambda.

Evidently,
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to 1.
By Riesz representation Theorem, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique
y_{n}\in S_{\mathbb{H}}
such that

y_{n}^{*}(z)=\langle z,y_{n}\rangle,\qquad\forall\penalty\ z\in\mathbb{H}.

Now,

\displaystyle\left\|y_{n}-{\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle y_{n},y_{n}\rangle-\langle y_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle-\langle\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}\rangle+\langle\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=1-2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})+\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0,\penalty\ \mathrm{as}\penalty\ n\to\infty.

Thus,

\displaystyle|\langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle-y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})|

\displaystyle=|\langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle-\langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}-y_{n}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}-y_{n}\|\to 0,\penalty\ \mathrm{as}\penalty\ n\to\infty.

Therefore,
\langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle\to\lambda\in\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
, and
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{W}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).

(iii)
\implies
(i): Let
\mu\in\mathbb{F}
be non-zero. By the same arguments as in the proof ((ii)
\implies
(i)) of Theorem
2.2
, we can find
(\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{H}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{H}_{k}}
such that

(i)\penalty\ \|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|,\penalty\ (ii)\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle\to\beta_{0},\penalty\ (iii)\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \mu\penalty\ \beta_{0}\geq 0.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}+\mu\mathsf{A}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\langle\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}+\|\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \mu\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \mu\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle.

Letting

we have

\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|^{2}+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \mu\penalty\ \langle\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rangle\to 1+2\penalty\ \mathfrak{Re}\penalty\ \mu\penalty\ \beta_{0}\geq 1.

Consequently,
\|\mathsf{T}+\mu\mathsf{A}\|\geq\|\mathsf{T}\|.
Since
\mu
was chosen arbitrarily, we have
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
and the proof is complete.
∎

3.
Weak differentiability of the norm

We devote this section to characterize the weak differentiability of the norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}).
Most of the results of this section are dependent on the results of the previous section. Let
\mathbb{S}
denote the unit sphere of the scalar field
\mathbb{F}
. The symbol
\mathbb{S}^{k}
denotes the product
\underbrace{\mathbb{S}\times\dots\times\mathbb{S}}_{k-times}
. The following theorem completely characterizes the weak differentiable points in
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y})
and is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be normed linear spaces, for
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}
.

(ii)

\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is a singleton set for any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
, where
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined in (
2.1
).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\|\mathsf{T}\|=\|\mathsf{A}\|=1.

(i)
\implies
(ii): Suppose on contrary that
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is not a singleton set for some
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
. Let
\alpha
,
\beta
be two distinct members of
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}).
Then there exist
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
and
((\mathbf{z}_{n},w_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
such that the following hold:

(i)\penalty\ {y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})}\to\alpha,\penalty\ (ii)\penalty\ {w_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{z}_{n})}\to\beta.

Now, there are two possibilities.

Case I: Suppose that
\alpha,\beta\neq 0.
Let
\mathsf{U}_{1},\mathsf{U}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be defined by

\mathsf{U}_{1}:=\mathsf{T}-\frac{1}{{\alpha}}\mathsf{A},\penalty\ \mathsf{U}_{2}:=\mathsf{T}-\frac{1}{{\beta}}\mathsf{A}.

Then we get

\displaystyle\lim y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{U}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n})=\lim y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n})-\frac{1}{\alpha}\lim y_{n}^{*}\left(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n}\right)=0.

Similarly,
w_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{U}_{2}\mathbf{z}_{n})\to 0.
Thus,
0\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{U}_{1})\cap\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{U}_{2}).
It now follows from Theorem
2.2
that
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{U}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{U}_{2}.
By the homogeneity of Birkhoff-James orthogonality, we get

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\dfrac{-{\alpha}}{({\beta}-{\alpha})}\mathsf{U}_{1}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\dfrac{{\beta}}{({\beta}-{\alpha})}\mathsf{U}_{2}.

Since the norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
T
, it follows from Theorem
1.1
that Birkhoff-James orthogonality is right-additive at
\mathsf{T}
. Thus, we have

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\left(\dfrac{-{\alpha}}{({\beta}-{\alpha})}\mathsf{U}_{1}+\dfrac{{\beta}}{({\beta}-{\alpha})}\mathsf{U}_{2}\right).

This proves that
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{T}
, which is a contradiction.

Case II: At least one of
\alpha,\beta
is zero. Without loss of generality, let
\alpha=0.
Then
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
. Also, by Case I,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\left(\mathsf{T}-\dfrac{1}{{\beta}}\mathsf{A}\right).
Therefore, we have

\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\left(\frac{1}{{\beta}}\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{T}-\frac{1}{{\beta}}\mathsf{A}\right).

Thus,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{T}
which is again a contradiction.

(ii)
\implies
(i): Consider
\mathsf{A}_{1},\mathsf{A}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
with
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{1}
,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{2}
. Therefore,
0\in\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{1}))\cap\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{2}))
, by Theorem
2.2
. On the other hand,
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{1})
and
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{2})
are singleton sets. This implies
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{1})=\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{2})=\{0\}.

Thus, given any
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
, we can find a common subsequence
(n_{j})
of natural numbers so that
y_{n_{j}}^{*}(\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\to 0,\penalty\ y_{n_{j}}^{*}(\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}})\to 0,
as
j\to\infty
. Since
((\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}},y_{n_{j}}^{*}))
is also a member of
\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
, and
y_{n_{j}}^{*}((\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})\mathbf{x}_{n_{j}}))\to 0
, we have
0\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})\subseteq\mathbf{Co}(\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2}))
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.2
,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}(\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})
and consequently, the proof follows from Theorem
1.1
.
∎

As a corollary of Theorem
3.1
, we have the following conclusive result regarding the weak differentiability of the norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be normed linear spaces;
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}
.

(ii)

For any two members
((\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n}^{*}))
and
((\mathbf{z}_{n},w_{n}^{*}))
of
\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}
and for any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)

\lim y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})
and
\lim w_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{z}_{n})
exist, and are equal.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii): We first note that

•

\lim y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n})
and
\lim w_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{z}_{n})

\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})

•

and
(w_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{z}_{n}))

\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})

•

Since the norm is weakly differentiable at

for some

, by Theorem
3.1
.

Clearly,

converges to

the sequence
(y_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n}))
itself converges to

Similarly,

.

(ii)
\implies
(i): The stated condition indicates that

is a singleton set for any

.
∎

Theorem
3.1
can also be stated in terms of semi-inner-products. We illustrate this in our next result. Let us recall the definition of semi-inner-product
[
7
,
13
,
21
]
in this connection.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a vector space over
\mathbb{F}.
A semi-inner-product (SIP) on
\mathbb{X}
is a function
[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{F}
that satisfies the following conditions:

•

[x,x]>0
for all non-zero
x\in\mathbb{X}.

•

[\alpha y+\beta z,x]=\alpha[y,x]+\beta[z,x]
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{X},
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}.

•

[y,\lambda x]=\overline{\lambda}[y,x]
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{X}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}.

•

|[y,x]|^{2}\leq\|y\|^{2}\|x\|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{X}.

The vector space
\mathbb{X}
, equipped with an SIP, is called an SIP space. Every SIP space is a normed linear space with the norm
\|x\|=[x,x]^{\frac{1}{2}}.
On the other hand, every normed linear space can be made into an SIP space, in general, in infinitely many different ways.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be normed linear spaces for
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the collection of all SIPs on
\mathbb{Y}.
Let
\Lambda(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
be the (non-empty) collection of scalars defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}):=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}:[\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}\to\lambda,([\cdot,\cdot]_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{L},\penalty\ (\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}},\penalty\ \|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|\right\}.

Then for any
\lambda\in\Lambda(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
,
\dfrac{\lambda}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}
is a member of
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}),
where
\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.1
).

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
. Then, there exist a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}
with
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|
and a sequence of SIPs
([\cdot,\cdot]_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{L}
such that
[\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}\to\lambda.
Choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|>0
for all
n>N.
Let
\mathbf{z}_{m}=\mathbf{x}_{{}_{N+m}}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}.
For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define
y_{m}^{*}:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{F}
by

y_{m}^{*}(y)=\frac{1}{\left\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{z}_{m}\right\|}\left[y,\mathsf{T}\mathbf{z}_{m}\right]_{N+m},\qquad\mathrm{for\penalty\ all}\penalty\ y\in\mathbb{Y}.

Then
y_{m}^{*}\in S_{\mathbb{Y}^{*}}
and
y_{m}^{*}(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{z}_{m})\to\|\mathsf{T}\|
. Consequently,
(\mathbf{z}_{m},y_{m}^{*})\in\mathscr{M}_{\mathsf{T}}.
It is now obvious that
\lim y_{m}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{z}_{m})=\dfrac{\lambda}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}.
Thus,
\dfrac{\lambda}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
and the proof is complete.
∎

be any sequence of SIPs. Consider any convergent subsequence
\left(\left[\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}}\right]_{n_{p}}\right)
of the bounded sequence
([\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n})
, and let
\left[\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}}\right]_{n_{p}}\to\lambda.
Now, by Lemma
3.4
, we have
\dfrac{\lambda}{\|\mathsf{T}\|}\in\Omega(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
. Thus, we have
\lambda=0.
Since any convergent subsequence of
([\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n})
converges to
0
, the sequence
([\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n})
converges to
0.

(iii)
\implies
(i): Let
\mathsf{A}_{1},\mathsf{A}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
with
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{1}
,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{2}.
Let
(\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}
with
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|\to\|\mathsf{T}\|
and let
([\cdot,\cdot]_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{L}
be any sequence of SIPs. Note that
[\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}=[\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}+[\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.
It now follows from the hypothesis that
[\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}\to 0.
Moreover, for any scalar
\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}+\lambda(\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})\|\|\mathsf{T}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\lambda(\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n})\|\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|[\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\lambda\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\lambda\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|[\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}+\lambda[\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{n}]_{n}\right|

\displaystyle\to\|\mathsf{T}\|^{2},\penalty\ \mathrm{as}\penalty\ n\to\infty.

In other words,
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}(\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})
. Consequently, the proof follows from Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We now turn our attention towards the finite-dimensional case. An additional advantage in this case is that
M_{\mathsf{T}}\neq\emptyset
here. Before that let us state a result which is an obvious corollary of Theorem
3.1
and a proof of this follows from Remark
2.2.2
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be finite-dimensional Banach spaces for
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}
.

(ii)

\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is a singleton set for any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
, where
\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.3
).

It turns out that the norm attainment set of a
k
-linear map
\mathsf{T}
plays a decisive role in determining the smoothness of
\mathsf{T}.
This is illustrated in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be finite-dimensional Banach spaces;
1\leq i\leq k,
and let
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be non-zero. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}
.

(ii)

M_{\mathsf{T}}:=\left\{\left(\mu^{(1)}x_{1},\dots,\mu^{(k)}x_{k}\right)\in S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}:\penalty\ \left(\mu^{(1)},\dots,\mu^{(k)}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{k}\right\}
and the norm of
\mathbb{Y}
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}).

(iii)

\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is a singleton set for any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
, where
\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is the subset of scalars defined by (
2.3
). To be more precise,
\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})=\{y_{0}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0})\}
, where
y_{0}^{*}\in J(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

Proof.

We only prove (i)
\implies
(ii) and (ii)
\implies
(iii), as (iii)
\iff
(i) follows from Corollary
3.6
.

(i)
\implies
(ii): Suppose on the contrary that
M_{\mathsf{T}}
is not of the stated form. Then there exists
\mathbf{z}_{0}\in M_{\mathsf{T}}
such that
\mathbf{z}_{0}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\neq\left(\mu^{(1)}x_{1},\dots,\mu^{(k)}x_{k}\right)
for any
\left(\mu^{(1)},\dots,\mu^{(k)}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{k}.
Therefore, there exists some
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
such that
z_{j}\neq\mu x_{j}
for any unimodular scalar
\mu.
In particular,
\{x_{j},z_{j}\}
is a linearly independent subset of
\mathbb{X}_{j}
. For all such indices
j\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
such that
\{x_{j},z_{j}\}
is linearly independent, we extend
\{x_{j},z_{j}\}
to a basis of
\mathbb{X}_{j}
. Also, for all such indices
m\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
such that
\{x_{m},z_{m}\}
is linearly dependent, we extend
\{x_{m}\}
to a basis of
\mathbb{X}_{m}
. In this way, we get a basis, say
B_{i}
, of
\mathbb{X}_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Let

\mathbf{v}_{0}:=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\penalty\ \mathrm{where}\penalty\ v_{j}=\begin{cases}z_{j},\penalty\ &\mathrm{if}\penalty\ \{x_{j},z_{j}\}\penalty\ \mathrm{\penalty\ is\penalty\ linearly\penalty\ independent},\\
x_{j},\penalty\ &\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It can be shown with a little computation that
\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0}\in M_{\mathsf{T}}\cap\left(B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{k}\right).
Note that any (bounded)
k
-linear map
\mathsf{A}:\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k}\to\mathbb{Y}
is determined by its action on
B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{k}.
Now, we construct two
k
-linear maps
\mathsf{A}_{1}
and
\mathsf{A}_{2}
in the following way.

For any
\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{k}
,

\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathbf{x}=\begin{cases}\theta,&\penalty\ \mathrm{if}\penalty\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0},\\
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{v}_{0},&\penalty\ \mathrm{if}\penalty\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{0},\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x},&\penalty\ \mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

\mathsf{A}_{2}\mathbf{x}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0},&\penalty\ \mathrm{if}\penalty\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0},\\
\theta,&\penalty\ \mathrm{if}\penalty\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{0},\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x},&\penalty\ \mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Evidently
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}_{2}.
Also, it follows from Theorem
1.1
that Birkhoff-James orthogonality is right-additive at
\mathsf{T}
. Therefore, we have
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}(\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})
and this is a contradiction as
(\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2})=\mathsf{T}.

Next, suppose that the norm of
\mathbb{Y}
is not weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
is not a smooth point. Then, we can find two distinct support functionals
y_{1}^{*},\penalty\ y_{2}^{*}
at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}.
Let
\phi,\psi:\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)\to\mathbb{F}
be two linear functionals defined by

\phi(\mathsf{U})=y_{1}^{*}(\mathsf{U}\mathbf{x}_{0}),\penalty\ \psi(\mathsf{U})=y_{2}^{*}(\mathsf{U}\mathbf{x}_{0}),\qquad\mathrm{for\penalty\ all}\penalty\ \mathsf{U}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right).

Since
\ker y_{1}^{*}\neq\ker y_{2}^{*}
, it is straightforward that
\phi
and
\psi
are distinct support functionals at
\mathsf{T}
. Thus,
\mathsf{T}
is not smooth and this is a contradiction to Theorem
1.1
.

(ii)
\implies
(iii): Let
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be arbitrary. Consider any
\mathbf{x}\in M_{\mathsf{T}}
. Then
\mathbf{x}=(\mu_{1}x_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}x_{k})
for some
(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})\in\mathbb{S}^{k}
. Since
\mathbb{S}
is a group under multiplication and
\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}
are
k
-linear, we get

\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}=\mu\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0},\penalty\ \mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0},\qquad\mathrm{for\penalty\ some\penalty\ unimodular\penalty\ scalar}\penalty\ \mu.

Also, since norm of
\mathbb{Y}
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
is smooth. Consequently,
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}
is smooth. Therefore, the unique support functional
y^{*}
at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}
is of the form
\overline{\mu}y_{0}^{*}
, where
y_{0}^{*}
is the unique support functional at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Consequently, we have

{y^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x})}={\overline{\mu}y_{0}^{*}(\mu\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0})}=y_{0}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}).

To be more precise, we have

\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})=\{y_{0}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0})\},

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in M_{\mathsf{T}}
and
y_{0}^{*}\in J(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0})
. In other words,
\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})
is a singleton set.
∎

Remark 3.7.1
.

For
k=2
, the above theorem reduces to the characterization of weak differentiability of the norm on the spaces of bounded bilinear operators. In particular, it simplifies and extends Theorem 2.5 in
[

.

A bounded linear operator
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is said to have the
Bhatia-Šemrl property

[

,

]
, if given any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
with

such that

and
\mathsf{T}x_{0}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}x_{0}.
We conclude the article with the following corollary which shows that a smooth multilinear map also satisfies the Bhatia-Šemrl property.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{i},\penalty\ \mathbb{Y}
be finite-dimensional Banach spaces;
1\leq i\leq k,
and let the norm of
\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}
. Then given any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
with
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
implies that there exists
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in M_{\mathsf{T}}
(i.e.,
\|\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}\|=\|\mathsf{T}\|
) such that
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{X}_{k},\mathbb{Y}\right)
be arbitrary with
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}.
It follows from (i)
\implies
(ii) of Theorem
3.7
that

M_{\mathsf{T}}:=\left\{\left(\mu^{(1)}x_{1},\dots,\mu^{(k)}x_{k}\right)\in S_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mathbb{X}_{k}}:\penalty\ \left(\mu^{(1)},\dots,\mu^{(k)}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{k}\right\},

and the norm of
\mathbb{Y}
is weakly differentiable at
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
. Then again from (ii)
\implies
(iii) of Theorem
3.7
, we have

\Omega^{\prime}(\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A})=\{y_{0}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0})\},

where
y_{0}^{*}\in J(\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Since
\mathsf{T}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}
, applying Corollary
2.3
, we have
y_{0}^{*}(\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0})=0
. Hence,
\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}\perp_{B}\mathsf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}
and the proof is complete.
∎
