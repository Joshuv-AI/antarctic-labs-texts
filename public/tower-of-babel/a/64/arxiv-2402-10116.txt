A geometric approach toconjugation-invariant random permutations
Victor Dubach

Abstract
We propose a new approach to conjugation-invariant random permutations. Namely, we explain how to construct uniform permutations in given conjugacy classes from certain point processes in the plane. This enables the use of geometric tools to study various statistics of such permutations. For their longest decreasing subsequences, we prove universality of the 2 ​ n 2\sqrt{n} asymptotic. For Robinson–Schensted shapes, we prove universality of the Vershik–Kerov–Logan–Shepp limit shape, thus solving a conjecture of Kammoun. For the number of records, we establish a phase transition phenomenon as the number of fixed points grows. For pattern counts, we obtain an asymptotic normality result, partially answering a conjecture of Hamaker and Rhoades.



1
Introduction

1.1
Conjugation-invariant random permutations

We say that a random permutation
\tau
of
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
is
conjugation-invariant
when for any fixed permutation
\rho
, the conjugate
\rho\circ\tau\circ\rho^{-1}
follows the same law as
\tau
.
Standard examples of conjugation-invariant permutations are uniformly random permutations, random involutions, Ewens random permutations
[
Ewe72
]
, their generalizations
[
Kin78
,
Tsi00
,
BUV11
]
, and many more.

An equivalent description of conjugation-invariance makes use of the cycle decomposition.
Define the
cycle type
of
\tau
as the sequence
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n})
where
t_{p}
is the number of
p
-cycles in
\tau
.
We also say that
t
has size
\sum_{p=1}^{n}pt_{p}=n
and that
\tau
is
t
-cyclic.
Since the cycle type is a total invariant for conjugation in the symmetric group, a random permutation
\tau
is conjugation-invariant if and only if, when conditioned on its cycle type
t
,
\tau
is a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation.
Thus, studying conjugation-invariant random permutations often amounts to studying uniform permutations with given cycle types.

There are reasons to believe in universality phenomena for several statistics of conjugation-invariant permutations, with a dependence on the number of fixed points and sometimes
2
-cycles.
Recent advances motivate this idea by generalizing asymptotic properties of uniformly random permutations to conjugation-invariant random permutations under certain hypotheses
[
Fér13
,
Kam18
,
FKL22
,
HR22
]
.
The aim of this paper is to make further progress in this direction by introducing a “geometric” construction for random permutations in given conjugacy classes, see
Section

1.2
.
This new point of view allows us to establish asymptotic results on the longest monotone subsequences, Robinson–Schensted shape, number of records, and pattern counts.

Longest monotone subsequences.

The maximum length of a monotone subsequence in a uniform permutation famously behaves as
2\sqrt{n}

[
VK77
]
.
Baik and Rains
[
BR01
]
showed similar asymptotics for random involutions, with a dependence on the number of fixed points, and Kammoun
[
Kam18
]
proved that the
2\sqrt{n}
asymptotic holds for conjugation-invariant permutations with few cycles.
In
Section

2.1
we extend these results to general conjugation-invariant random permutations for their longest
decreasing
subsequences.
Longest increasing subsequences are harder to handle, and we only provide such asymptotics up to a multiplicative constant.

Robinson–Schensted shape.

The Robinson–Schensted shape of a uniform permutation converges, after suitable rescaling, to a limit curve
[
LS77
,
VK77
]
.
Kammoun
[
Kam18
]
extended this result to conjugation-invariant permutations with few cycles, and conjectured that this assumption could be lifted.
In
Section

2.2
we provide a positive answer to this conjecture.

Records.

The numbers of records in uniform permutations are known to satisfy asymptotic normality.
In
Section

2.3
we prove a general limit theorem for the number of high records (or left-to-right maxima) in conjugation-invariant permutations, showcasing a phase transition as the number of fixed points grows.
More precisely, the number of high records interpolates from logarithmic with Gaussian fluctuations to asymptotically Gamma, when the number of non-fixed points is of order
n/\sqrt{\log n}
.
We also find the first order asymptotics of low records (or left-to-right minima), and their second order asymptotics in some cases.

Pattern counts.

Janson et al.
[
JNZ15
]
established asymptotic normality for all pattern counts in uniform permutations.
Kammoun
[
Kam22
]
and Hamaker and Rhoades
[
HR22
]
extended this to conjugation-invariant permutations, under certain assumptions on their cycles.
The authors of
[
HR22
]
conjectured that the (non-degenerate) asymptotic normality of pattern counts holds for all conjugation-invariant permutations, where the asymptotic variance depends on the numbers of fixed points and
2
-cycles.
In
Section

2.4
we partially answer this conjecture, by proving asymptotic normality for all conjugation-invariant permutations, and non-degeneracy in some cases.

1.2
A geometric construction

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\}
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with no redundant x- or y-coordinates.
Then we can define a permutation
\tau
of
[n]
by letting
\tau(i)=j
when the
i
-th point from the left in
\mathcal{P}
is
j
-th from the bottom.
We write
\tau={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
for this permutation;
see
Figure

1
for an example.

Figure 1:
Representation of the geometric construction of a uniform
(2,2,2,1)
-cyclic permutation as in
1.1
, written in one-line notation and in cycle product notation.

By considering adequate planar point processes
\mathcal{P}
, we may obtain random permutations
\tau
with various laws:
for instance if
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)
variables then
\tau
is uniformly random, and if we symmetrize this family with respect to the diagonal of
[0,1]^{2}
then
\tau
is a uniform involution of size
2n
with no fixed point
[
BR01
]
.
An asset of this point of view is that some properties of
\tau
may be easier to derive directly from
\mathcal{P}
: see e.g.
[
AD95
]
for a study of increasing subsequences in uniform permutations, and
[
Kiw06
]
for the case of involutions.

1.1
generalizes the geometric construction of Baik and Rains for random involutions
[
BR01
]
to uniform permutations in any conjugacy class.
Although simple, this lemma is fundamental to our analysis:
general conjugation–invariant permutations do not benefit from the same valuable algebraic properties as uniform permutations or involutions, and our geometric construction bypasses this problem.

If
t
is a cycle type of size
n
, define
\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\left\{\big.(p,k,l)\,:\,p\in[n],k\in[t_{p}],l\in[p]\right\}
.
Using a family of i.i.d. uniform variables indexed by this set, we can construct a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation as follows.
This is illustrated on
Figure

1
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
.
Let
\left(U_{p,k}^{l}\right)_{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
be a family of i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]\right)
random variables.
For each
(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
, write
Z_{p,k}^{l}:=\left(U_{p,k}^{l},U_{p,k}^{l+1}\right)
where
l\!+\!1
is taken modulo
p
, and set
\mathcal{P}:=\left\{Z_{p,k}^{l}:(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\right\}
.
Then
\tau:={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
is a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation.

Proof.

The list of x-coordinates of
\mathcal{P}
is the same as its list of y-coordinates, namely
\mathcal{U}=\left\{U_{p,k}^{l}\,:\,(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\right\}
.
Write
i_{p,k}^{l}\in[n]
for the relative order of
U_{p,k}^{l}
in
\cal U
, i.e.
U_{p,k}^{l}
is the
i_{p,k}^{l}
-th lowest value in
\cal U
.
Then the presence of
\left(U_{p,k}^{l},U_{p,k}^{l\!+\!1}\right)
in
\mathcal{P}
means that
\tau\left(i_{p,k}^{l}\right)=i_{p,k}^{l\!+\!1}
.
Therefore
\tau
is the product of cycles
\left(i_{p,k}^{1},\dots,i_{p,k}^{p}\right)
for
p\in[n]
,
k\in[t_{p}]
.
In other words,
\tau
is obtained by writing the sequence
\left(i_{p,k}^{l}\right)_{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
with the lexicographic order on
(p,k,l)
, parenthesizing it according to the cycle type
t
, and interpreting this as a product of cycles.
By standardization of the i.i.d. sequence
\left(U_{p,k}^{l}\right)_{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
, the sequence
\left(i_{p,k}^{l}\right)_{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
is the one-line representation of a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
.
From there, it is classical that
\tau
is a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation.
∎

As we explain in
Sections

2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
, monotone subsequences and records of
\tau={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
can be read directly on
\mathcal{P}
.
This allows us to study these statistics of the permutation by investigating the behavior of the point process in certain regions of the plane.
To this aim, a list of useful properties of the geometric construction may be found in
Section

3
.
Regarding pattern counts, we rather use the “weak dependency” of points in
\mathcal{P}
(see
Section

2.4
for more details).

1.3
Notation

We denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
the set of permutations of
[n]
and by
\Delta
the diagonal of
[0,1]^{2}
.
Throughout this paper we consider the standard partial order on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
(x,y)\leq(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
when
x\leq x^{\prime}
and
y\leq y^{\prime}
.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
.
We say that
\mathcal{P}
is a geometric construction of
t
when
\mathcal{P}
is constructed as in
1.1
.
We can decompose it into its cycles
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{p\in[n],k\in[t_{p}]}\mathcal{P}_{p,k}
where
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}:=\left\{Z_{p,k}^{l}:l\in[p]\right\}
.
We can also decompose it into
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}:=\mathcal{P}\cap\Delta
and
\check{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\Delta
, and we write
\check{t}:=(0,t_{2},t_{3},\dots,t_{n})
for the cycle type of size
\check{n}:=n-t_{1}
with no fixed point.
It is straightforward to check that
\check{\mathcal{P}}
is a geometric construction of
\check{t}
.

We define a “successor” operator
\mathfrak{s}:(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\mapsto(p,k,l\!+\!1)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
where
l\!+\!1
is taken modulo
p
, and a graph
\mathcal{L}_{t}:=(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t})
with vertice
\mathcal{V}_{t}
and edges between
i
and
\mathfrak{s}(i)
for each
i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
(forgetting self-loops).
We refer to
\mathcal{L}_{t}
as the “dependency graph” associated with
t
.
See
Figure

2
for an example, and
Section

7.1
for an explanation of this name.

Figure 2:
The dependency graph
\mathcal{L}_{t}
associated with the cycle type
t=(2,1,2,0,1)
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive numbers.
We say that a sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(a_{n})
as
n\to\infty
if

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|X_{n}\right|>a_{n}M\right)\underset{M\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Also,
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
o_{\mathbf{P}}(a_{n})
as
n\to\infty
if
X_{n}/a_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
in probability.
Finally, a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of events happens with high probability (w.h.p.) as
n\to\infty
if
\mathbf{P}\left(A_{n}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1
.

2
Results: universality and beyond

2.1
Longest monotone subsequences

Let
\tau
be a permutation of
[n]
.
An increasing subsequence of
\tau
is a sequence of indices
i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}
such that
\tau(i_{1})<\dots<\tau(i_{\ell})
, and the maximum length of an increasing subsequence of
\tau
is denoted by
\mathrm{LIS}(\tau)
.
We may define analogously
\mathrm{LDS}(\tau)
as the maximum length of a decreasing subsequence of
\tau
.

The study of monotone subsequences in permutations has been an active and captivating field of research; see
[
Rom15
]
for an introduction.
In particular when
\tau_{n}
is uniformly random, the law of
\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})
(which is the same as that of
\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})
in this case) is now well understood: it behaves asymptotically as
2\sqrt{n}

[
VK77
]
, satisfies a large deviation principle
[
DZ99
]
, and admits Tracy–Widom fluctuations of order
n^{1/6}

[
BDJ99
]
.

One might expect these results to mostly hold when the law of
\tau_{n}
is invariant by conjugation.
The first advance on this question was obtained by Baik and Rains, who studied random involutions
[
BR01
]
.
More recently, Kammoun
[
Kam18
,
Kam23
]
and Guionnet
[
GK23
]
could settle the case of conjugation-invariant random permutations with few cycles.
Here we start by stating first order asymptotic results and concentration inequalities for the longest decreasing subsequences of uniform permutations with given cycle types and no fixed points.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that the following holds.
For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
such that
t_{1}^{(n)}=0
and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Then:

\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})<2(1-\delta)\right)\leq\exp\left(\big.-c_{\delta}n\right)\quad\text{;}\quad\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})>2(1+\delta)\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

In particular
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}2
in probability and in
L^{q}
for all
q\geq 1
.

It is not difficult to add fixed points in the previous theorem, and to consider random cycle types instead of deterministic ones.
This yields the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

For any
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that the following holds.
Let
(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, where
\tau_{n}
has size
n
and (random) cycle type
t^{(n)}
.
Then for any
n
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})<2(1-\delta)\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\big.-c_{\delta}\left(n-t_{1}^{(n)}\right)\right)\right];\\
\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})>2(1+\delta)\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}\right)\right].\end{array}\right.

In particular if
n-t_{1}^{(n)}\to\infty
in probability then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}}\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})\to 2
in probability and in
L^{q}
for all
q\geq 1
.

Remark 1
.

In comparison with
[
Kam18
, Theorem 1.2]
and its variants in
[
Kam23
]
,
2.2
does not require any hypothesis on the number of cycles.
Our results can in particular be applied to random permutations with cycle weights, some of which have a lot of cycles
[
BUV11
,
EU14
]
, and to central virtual permutations, some of which have a macroscopic number of fixed points
[
Tsi00
]
.
A similar observation can be made for all of the results presented in this paper.

Although increasing and decreasing subsequences share the same distribution in uniformly random permutations, this fact does not hold for conjugation-invariant random permutations.
The study of increasing subsequences in this context is trickier, and we were only able to derive their first order asymptotics up to a multiplicative constant.
As before, we start by stating our result for uniform permutations with given cycle types and no fixed points, and then we generalize it to all conjugation-invariant permutations.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that the following holds.
For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
such that
t_{1}^{(n)}=0
and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Then:

\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})<2(1-\delta)\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right)\quad\text{ ; }\quad\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})>2(1+\delta)\sqrt{3}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that the following holds.
Let
(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, where
\tau_{n}
has size
n
and (random) cycle type
t^{(n)}
.
Then for any
n
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{P}\left(\big.\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})<2(1-\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right);\\
\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})>t_{1}^{(n)}+2(1+\delta)\sqrt{3\left(n-t_{1}^{(n)}\right)}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}\right)\right].\end{array}\right.

In particular if
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}t_{1}^{(n)}\to\alpha\geq 0
in probability then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})
is asymptotically bounded between
2
and
\alpha+2\sqrt{3}
in probability.

Let us briefly explain the techniques used to prove the results of this section.
Deuschel and Zeitouni
[
DZ95
]
studied the first order asymptotics of longest monotone subsequences in
{\rm Perm}\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)
, where
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are i.i.d. under certain probability densities on
[0,1]^{2}
(such random permutations are called
locally uniform
in
[
Sjö23
]
).
This is made possible by the following key observation:
increasing and decreasing subsequences of
\tau={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
correspond to up-right and down-right paths of points in
\mathcal{P}
.
The authors of
[
DZ95
]
then slice the unit square into small rectangles in which the points are almost uniformly distributed, so that they may locally apply the result of
[
VK77
]
, and finally stitch these local paths of points into one global path.
When studying uniform permutations in given conjugacy classes, the same method can be adapted to the geometric construction of
1.1
.

Finally, we state a reasonable conjecture in the direction of
2.3
and
2.4
.
It was already established for random involutions in
[
BR01
]
, and for conjugation-invariant random permutations satisfying certain cycle constraints in
[
Kam18
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
Kam23
, Theorem 3]
.

Conjecture 2.5
.

Let
(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, where
\tau_{n}
has size
n
and (random) cycle type
t^{(n)}
.
Suppose that
t_{1}^{(n)}/\sqrt{n}\rightarrow 0
in probability.
Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}2

in probability.

Remark 2
.

Baik and Rains actually proved
[
BR01
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.4]
that if
\tau_{n}
is a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation where
t^{(n)}~\hskip-3.00003pt=~\hskip-3.00003pt\left(t_{1}^{(n)},t_{2}^{(n)},0,\dots\right)
satisfies
t_{1}^{(n)}/\sqrt{n}\to\alpha\geq 0
, i.e. if
\tau_{n}
is a uniform involution with
\alpha\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n})
fixed points, then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2&\text{if }\alpha\leq 1\\
\alpha+\frac{1}{\alpha}&\text{if }\alpha\geq 1\end{array}\right.

in probability.
We could expect the same first order asymptotics for any sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations such that
t_{1}^{(n)}/\sqrt{n}\to\alpha
in probability.
However we chose not to include this refinement of
2.5
in its statement, as it might be more speculative.

The results of this section are proved in
Section

4
.

2.2
Robinson–Schensted shape

Let
\tau
be a permutation of
[n]
.
If
x\geq 0
, we call
x
-decrasing subsequence
any union of
\lfloor x\rfloor
individually decreasing subsequences, and we define
\mathrm{LDS}_{x}(\tau)
as the maximal size of an
x
-decreasing subsequence of
\tau
.

The study of
x
-decreasing subsequences in permutations is partly motivated by their fundamental link with the
Robinson–Schensted correspondence
.
This is a one-to-one correspondence between permutations and pairs of standard Young tableaux with the same shape.
This shape, which is a Young diagram, is called the
RS shape
of the permutation:
it appears in several domains, such as integrable probability or representation theory.
A well-known theorem of Greene
[
Gre74
]
states that for any integer
k
, the number of boxes in the first
k
columns of the RS shape of
\tau
equals
\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(\tau)
.
Equivalently, the length of the
k
-th column equals
\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(\tau)-\mathrm{LDS}_{k\!-\!1}(\tau)
.
A similar connection can be made between increasing subsequences of
\tau
and the row lengths of its RS shape.

Historically, studying the asymptotics of
\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})
and
\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})
when
\tau_{n}
is uniformly random actually required finding the asymptotics of its entire RS shape.
The law of this shape is known as the Plancherel measure, for which Vershik and Kerov and simultaneously Logan and Shepp established the following.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
LS77
,
VK77
]
)
.

For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
.
Then there exists an explicit nondecreasing, concave function
F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for each
r\geq 0
:

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LDS}_{r\sqrt{n}}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}(r)

in probability.

According to Greene’s theorem,
F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}(r)
thus describes the asymptotic proportion of boxes in the first
\lfloor r\sqrt{n}\rfloor
columns of the RS shape of
\tau_{n}
, and the derivative of
F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}
describes the limit curve of this shape.

A classical property of the Robinson–Schensted correspondence is that it induces a bijection between involutions and (single) standard Young tableaux.
If
(\tau_{n})
is a sequence of uniformly random involutions, the proofs of
[
LS77
]
and
[
VK77
]
can then be adapted to show that
Theorem

2.6
still holds (see e.g. Equation (24) in
[
Mel11
]
and the discussion below).
More generally if
(\tau_{n})
is a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, Kammoun proved that
Theorem

2.6
still holds under the assumption that the number of cycles in
\tau_{n}
is sublinear
[
Kam18
, Theorem 1.8]
.
He later conjectured that this assumption could be lifted, provided we take into account the proportion of fixed points
[
Kam23
, Conjectures 6 and 7]
.
The main difficulty is that none of the previous approaches (via hook-length formula
[
LS77
]
, representation theory
[
IO02
,
Mel11
]
, coupling
[
Kam18
]
…) work in this wider setting.
Here, using our geometric construction, we solve this conjecture.

Theorem 2.7
.

For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
with no fixed point and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Then for each
r\geq 0
:

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LDS}_{r\sqrt{n}}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}(r)

in probability.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, where
\tau_{n}
has size
n
and (random) cycle type
t^{(n)}
.
Suppose that
n-t_{1}^{(n)}\to\infty
in probability.
Then for each
r\geq 0
:

\frac{1}{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}\mathrm{LDS}_{r\sqrt{n-t_{1}^{(n)}}}(\tau_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}F_{\mathrm{LSKV}}(r)

in probability.

Let us briefly discuss the strategy of proof.
As in
Section

2.1
,
k
-decreasing subsequences of
\tau={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
have a nice visual interpretation:
they correspond to unions of
k
down-right paths in
\mathcal{P}
.
In the continuity of
[
DZ95
]
, where the longest monotone subsequences of locally uniform permutations were studied with geometric tools, Sjöstrand
[
Sjö23
]
recently studied the limit RS shape of locally uniform permutations using more advanced analysis.
His approach broadly consisted in computing the “score” associated with a bundle of decreasing curves, by decomposing the unit square into small regions where the sampling density is almost constant and the curves are almost parallel.
In
3.3
we show that the points of our geometric construction, when restricted to any rectangle outside the diagonal of the unit square, are i.i.d. uniform.
This simple fact is at the basis of how we apply and adapt the results and methods of
[
Sjö23
]
.

The results of this section are proved in
Section

5
.

2.3
Records

Let
\tau
be a permutation of
[n]
.
We say that a position
i\in[n]
is a
high record
(or left-to-right maximum) for
\tau
if for any
j<i
, we have
\tau(j)<\tau(i)
.
We say that it is a
low record
(or left-to-right minimum) for
\tau
if for any
j<i
, we have
\tau(j)>\tau(i)
.
We denote by
\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\tau\right)
and
\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau\right)
the numbers of high and low recods in
\tau
, respectively.
Records and their many variants are standard statistics in enumerative combinatorics.

The numbers of low and high records in uniformly random permutations are well understood: thanks to a classical bijection they follow the same law as the number of cycles (see e.g.
[
ABNP16
, Section 1.2]
), and this yields the following asymptotic normality (found e.g. in
[
ABT03
, Equation (1.31)]
).

Theorem 2.9
.

For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

The same holds for
\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)
.

Records can be interpreted nicely when the permutation is obtained from a family of points in the plane.
If
\tau={\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
, a point
Z=(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}
corresponds to a high record in
\tau
when no other point lies in its up-left corner
[0,X]\times[Y,1]
.
Similarly,
Z
corresponds to a low record in
\tau
when no other point lies in its down-left corner
[0,X]\times[0,Y]
.
We write
\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
and
\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
as shortcuts for
\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\right)
and
\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\right)
.

There is no more link between the numbers of cycles and records in conjugation-invariant permutations, but the geometric construction enables a new approach.
We prove a limit theorem for the number of high records, showcasing a phase transition as the number of fixed points grows.
First recall that, for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\lambda>0
, the
\mathrm{Gamma}\left(n,\lambda\right)
distribution is defined as the
n
-th convolution power of the
{\rm Exp}\left(\lambda\right)
distribution.
By convention,
\mathrm{Gamma}\left(n,+\infty\right)
is the a.s. null distribution.

Theorem 2.10
.

For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Write
\check{n}:=n-t_{1}^{(n)}
for the number of non-fixed points.
Suppose that
\check{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\infty
and that

\frac{\check{n}}{n/\sqrt{\log n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\alpha

for some
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\log\left(\check{n}\right)}{t_{1}^{(n)}/\check{n}+\sqrt{\log\left(\check{n}\right)}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}Y+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\Gamma_{2}

where
Y
and
\Gamma_{2}
are independent
{\cal N}\left(0,1\right)
and
\mathrm{Gamma}\left(2,1\right)
random variables.
In particular if
\check{n}=\omega\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)
as
n\to\infty
then:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

Similar to the case of monotone subsequences, the study of low records in conjugation-invariant random permutations differs from that of high records.
This asymmetry is already seen in some regimes for the first order asymptotics, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.11
.

For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.

1.

If
t_{1}^{(n)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
then we have the following convergence in probability:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)}{\log n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1.

2.

If
\sqrt{n}\ll t_{1}^{(n)}\ll n
, i.e.
\sqrt{n}=o\left(t_{1}^{(n)}\right)
and
t_{1}^{(n)}=o(n)
, then we have the following convergence in probability:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)}{\log n-\log t_{1}^{(n)}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}2.

3.

If
t_{1}^{(n)}=\Theta\left(n\right)
then
\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(1)
.

We could not compute fluctuations for the number of low records in general.
To simplify the analysis, we restrict ourselves to products of
2
-cycles and products of
3
-cycles, for which exhibit different fluctuations.

Proposition 2.12
.

For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
such that
t_{1}^{(n)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
, and let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.

1.

Suppose that
t_{1}^{(n)}+2t_{2}^{(n)}=n
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,2\right).

2.

Suppose that
t_{1}^{(n)}+3t_{3}^{(n)}=n
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\downarrow}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

It would be interesting to find the interpolation between the two items of
2.12
.
However this would notably require understanding the interaction between
2
-cycles and other cycles, and its effect on the law of low records, which seems difficult to grasp.

2.4
Pattern counts

Let
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\}
be a subset of
[n]
.
We may define a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
by the following rule:
for any
j,k\in[r]
,
\pi(j)>\pi(k)
if and only if
\tau(i_{j})>\tau(i_{k})
.
We write
\mathrm{pat}(I,\tau)
for this permutation, and say that this is the
pattern
induced by
\tau
on
I
.

Permutation patterns are a natural notion of substructures for permutations, and as such they have attracted a lot of interest.
A central question is that of pattern counts: given a permutation
\pi
of
[r]
, how many subsets of
\tau
induce
\pi
as a pattern?
In other words, we are interested in the statistic

X_{\pi}(\tau):=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{r}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{pat}(I,\tau)=\pi}

where
\binom{[n]}{r}
denotes the subsets of
[n]
of size
r
.
For example,
X_{2\,1}(\tau)
counts the number of inversions in
\tau
,
and
X_{1\,2\,\dots\,r}(\tau)
counts the number of increasing subsequences of length
r
in
\tau
.

Pattern counts in uniformly random permutations are known to be asymptotically normal since the works of Bóna for monotone patterns
[
Bón10
]
, and later Janson et al. for all patterns:

Theorem 2.13

(
[
JNZ15
]
)
.

For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of
[n]
.
Then for any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\left(\frac{X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})-\binom{n}{r}\frac{1}{r!}}{n^{r-1/2}}\right)_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\Sigma\right)

for some matrix
\Sigma
, of rank
(r\!-\!1)^{2}
and such that
\Sigma_{\pi,\pi}>0
for all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
if
r\geq 2
.

The convergence result in this theorem can be established via the method of
dependency graphs
.
To prove non-degeneracy, i.e. that
\Sigma_{\pi,\pi}
is positive, Bóna was able to find a lower bound for the variance of
X_{\pi}
by expressing it in terms of covariances, while the authors of
[
JNZ15
]
used the method of
U-statistics
.

In
[
Fér13
]
, Féray established a central limit theorem for pattern counts in Ewens random permutations, and conjectured its non-degeneracy.
Kammoun
[
Kam22
, Proposition 31]
and Hamaker and Rhoades
[
HR22
, Theorem 8.8]
could generalize the non-degenerate asymptotic normality of
Theorem

2.13
to conjugation-invariant permutations, under certain conditions on their cycles.
It was conjectured in
[
HR22
, Problem 9.9]
that those conditions could be lifted, and that the asymptotic variance would only depend on the proportions of fixed points and
2
-cycles.

Here, using the geometric construction of
1.1
, we extend the convergence of
Theorem

2.13
to most conjugation-invariant permutations.
If
\tau_{n}
is a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation then we can write, for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
:

X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})=\sum_{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}}\mathbf{1}_{{\rm Perm}\left(Z_{i},i\in I\right)=\pi}

(1)

with the notation of
Section

1.2
.
Thanks to the dependency graph
\mathcal{L}_{t}
defined in
Section

1.3
, we derive a limit theorem for
X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})
.
More precisely, Stein’s method yields explicit bounds on the speed of convergence.
In the following, we denote by
d_{K}
the Kolmogorov distance between two probability distributions.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
Y\sim{\cal N}\left(0,1\right)
.
For each
n
, let
t^{(n)}
be a cycle type of size
n
and
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
Then for any
n
:

d_{K}\left(\frac{X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]}}\,,\,Y\right)\leq 42\frac{r}{(r\!-\!1)!^{2}}\frac{n^{2r-3/2}}{\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]}+72\frac{r}{(r\!-\!1)!^{3}}\frac{n^{3r-2}}{\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]^{3/2}}.

Furthermore if
\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]=\sigma^{2}n^{2r-1}+o(n^{2r-1})
for some
\sigma\geq 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

\frac{X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]}{n^{r-1/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)

in distribution, and if
\sigma>0
then the previous distance is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.

Thanks to
[
HR22
, Proposition 7.2 and Theorem 8.14]
, the condition
\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]=\sigma^{2}n^{2r-1}+o(n^{2r-1})
holds under the simple assumption that
t_{1}^{(n)}/n
and
t_{2}^{(n)}/n
converge.
We may still want to find an explicit formula for
\sigma
, and to understand in which cases
\sigma>0
.
For this we use the method of
U
-statistics developed by Hoeffding
[
Hoe48
]
, as was done in
[
JNZ15
]
.
Before stating our results, we need a few definitions.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
p_{1}\in[0,1]
.
Then let
\hat{Z}_{1},\dots,\hat{Z}_{r}
be i.i.d. variables distributed under
p_{1}\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta}+(1\!-\!p_{1})\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}
, where
\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on the diagonal
\Delta
of
[0,1]^{2}
.
Define, for any
z\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(z):=\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\hat{Z}_{1},\dots,\hat{Z}_{r-1},z\right)=\pi\right)

and

\mu_{\pi}^{p_{1}}:=\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\hat{Z}_{1},\dots,\hat{Z}_{r}\right)=\pi\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}\left(\hat{Z}_{r}\right)\right].

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
(t^{(n)})_{n}
be a sequence of cycle types of size
n
such that
t_{1}^{(n)}=np_{1}+o(\sqrt{n})
and
2t_{2}^{(n)}=np_{2}+o(n)
as
n\to\infty
, for some
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
.
For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}
be a uniform
t^{(n)}
-cyclic permutation.
Then for any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have the following convergence in distribution:

\left(\frac{X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})-\binom{n}{r}\mu_{\pi}^{p_{1}}}{n^{r-1/2}}\right)_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}{\cal N}\left(0,\Sigma^{p_{1},p_{2}}\right)

where for any
\pi,\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, if
U,V,W
are i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]\right)
variables:

(r\!-\!1)!^{2}\,\Sigma_{\pi,\rho}^{p_{1},p_{2}}=p_{1}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(U,U),\psi_{\rho}^{p_{1}}(U,U)\right]+(1\!-\!p_{1})\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}^{p_{1}}(U,V)\right]\\
+p_{2}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}^{p_{1}}(V,U)\right]+2(1\!-\!p_{1}\!-\!p_{2})\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}^{p_{1}}(V,W)\right].

Corollary 2.16
.

Let
(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of conjugation-invariant random permutations, where
\tau_{n}
has size
n
and (random) cycle type
t^{(n)}
.
Suppose that
t_{1}^{(n)}=np_{1}+o_{\mathbf{P}}(\sqrt{n})
and
2t_{2}^{(n)}=np_{2}+o_{\mathbf{P}}(n)
as
n\to\infty
, for some
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
.
Then the convergence of
Theorem

2.15

Note that
Theorem

2.15
states a joint convergence, rather than the marginal convergence stated in
Theorem

2.14
.
We stress that the true novelty of
Theorem

2.15

Indeed,
Theorem

2.14

The matrix

.
Without further hypotheses, we could only prove that

satisfies non-degenerate asymptotic normality if

.

If
p_{1}<1
and

When fixed points vanish, i.e. when

We use the notation of

2.15

.

Let

3
Properties of the geometric construction

In this section, we present several interesting facts about the geometric construction of
1.1
.
These will be essential to prove the results of this paper, generally by reducing the study of conjugation-invariant permutations to uniformly random permutations.
We use the notation of
Sections

1.2
and
1.3
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
such that
t_{1}=0
, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a geometric construction.
Then it can be decomposed into an a.s. disjoint union

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{P}^{(2)}\cup\mathcal{P}^{(3)}

where each
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
is a family of i.i.d. uniform points in
[0,1]^{2}
, of (deterministic) sizes
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
bounded between
n/3\!-\!1
and
n/3\!+\!1
.

Proof.

The idea is to define the three subsets
\mathcal{P}^{(1)},\mathcal{P}^{(2)},\mathcal{P}^{(3)}
by assigning them the points of each cycle in an alternating order.
The rule for ensuring the i.i.d. uniformity property is that whenever some point
Z_{p,k}^{l}
lies in
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
, then the adjacent points
Z_{p,k}^{l\pm 1}
shall not belong to
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
.
To construct the
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
’s in a balanced way while satisfying this rule, we do the following:

•

For each
p
such that
p=0\!\mod 3
and
1\leq k\leq t_{p}
, the points of
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}
can be put in the three subsets by simply alternating:

Z_{p,k}^{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{(1)},Z_{p,k}^{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{(2)},Z_{p,k}^{3}\in\mathcal{P}^{(3)},Z_{p,k}^{4}\in\mathcal{P}^{(1)},\dots,Z_{p,k}^{p}\in\mathcal{P}^{(3)}.

Then each
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\cap\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
has size
p/3
and contains i.i.d. uniform points.

•

For each
p
such that
p=1\!\mod 3
and
1\leq k\leq t_{p}
, the points of
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}
can be put in the three subsets by simply alternating, but then one subset will get a surplus.
Thus two of the subsets
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\cap\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
have size
(p\!-\!1)/3
, the other one has size
1+(p\!-\!1)/3
, and they each contain i.i.d. uniform points.
Namely if
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
is a permutation of
(1,2,3)
then the choice

Z_{p,k}^{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{1})},Z_{p,k}^{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{2})},Z_{p,k}^{3}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{3})},Z_{p,k}^{4}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{1})},\dots,Z_{p,k}^{p-1}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{3})},Z_{p,k}^{p}\in\mathcal{P}^{(i_{2})}

yields a surplus for
\mathcal{P}^{(i_{2})}
.
For each such
(p,k)
we can choose which subset gets a surplus in order to balance out those surpluses over the
(p,k)
’s.
Consequently the sets

\bigcup_{p=1\hskip-5.7361pt\mod 3,1\leq k\leq t_{p}}\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\cap\mathcal{P}^{(i)}

have sizes of the form
m+\epsilon_{i}
for some integer
m
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
i=1,2,3
.

•

For each
p=2\!\mod 3
and
1\leq k\leq t_{p}
a similar reasoning can be made, except this time one subset will get a deficit.
By balancing out the deficits as before, we can construct the sets

\bigcup_{p=2\hskip-5.7361pt\mod 3,1\leq k\leq t_{p}}\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\cap\mathcal{P}^{(i)}

in such a way that they each contain i.i.d. uniform points and have sizes of the form
m^{\prime}-\delta_{i}
for some integer
m^{\prime}
and
\delta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
i=1,2,3
.

In the end, by balancing out the surpluses with the deficits, the subsets
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
have sizes
m^{\prime\prime}+\eta_{i}
for some integer
m^{\prime\prime}
and
\eta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
i=1,2,3
, and each contain i.i.d. uniform points.
The lemma follows.
∎

Thanks to
3.1
, we can apply Chernoff-type bounds to control the number of points in any zone of the plane.
Recall that
\check{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\Delta
is a geometric construction for
\check{t}:=(0,t_{2},\dots)
of size
\check{n}:=n-t_{1}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
and
\mathcal{P}
be a geometric construction.
If
C
is a measurable subset of
[0,1]^{2}
then
\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right|
is distributed like a (correlated) sum of
{\rm Binom}\left(n_{i},\mathrm{Leb}(C)\right)
variables, where
\left|n_{i}-\check{n}/3\right|\leq 1
for
i=1,2,3
.
In particular for any
\delta>0
there exists a constant
c>0
, which depends only on
\delta
and
\mathrm{Leb}(C)
, such that:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{\check{n}}\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right|-\mathrm{Leb}(C)\right|>\delta\right)\leq\exp\left({-c\check{n}}\right).

The following two lemmas informally state that conjugation-invariant random permutations are, in some sense, “locally uniform”.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
t
be a cycle type and
\mathcal{P}
be a geometric construction.
If
I,J
are two essentially disjoint intervals of
[0,1]
then the set
\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)
is a (random-sized) family of i.i.d. uniform points in
I\times J
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the lemma for each set
\mathcal{P}_{p,k}
.
Fix
(p,k)
.
Note that if some point
\big(U_{p,k}^{l},U_{p,k}^{l+1}\big)
is in
I\times J
then a.s. neither
\big(U_{p,k}^{l-1},U_{p,k}^{l}\big)
nor
\big(U_{p,k}^{l+1},U_{p,k}^{l+2}\big)
are in
I\times J
, where all exponents are taken modulo
p
in
[p]
.
Moreover
\big(U_{p,k}^{l},U_{p,k}^{l+1}\big)
is independent from

\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\setminus\left\{\big(U_{p,k}^{l-1},U_{p,k}^{l}\big),\big(U_{p,k}^{l},U_{p,k}^{l+1}\big),\big(U_{p,k}^{l+1},U_{p,k}^{l+2}\big)\right\}.

Thus for any
l_{1},\dots,l_{j}
, the event

\left\{\Bigg.\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\cap(I\times J)=\left\{\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{1}},U_{p,k}^{l_{1}+1}\big),\dots,\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{j}},U_{p,k}^{l_{j}+1}\big)\right\}\right\}

is either negligible or essentially rewrites as

\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{1}},U_{p,k}^{l_{1}+1}\big)\in I\!\times\!J\,,\dots,\,\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{j}},U_{p,k}^{l_{j}+1}\big)\in I\!\times\!J\;\;,\;\;(I\!\times\!J)\cap\mathcal{P}_{p,k}\setminus\left\{\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{1}-1},U_{p,k}^{l_{1}}\big),\dots,\big(U_{p,k}^{l_{j}+1},U_{p,k}^{l_{j}+2}\big)\right\}=\emptyset.

Conditionally on this event when it is non-negligible, these points are i.i.d. uniform in
I\times J
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
and
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\big.(U_{i},U_{\mathfrak{s}(i)})\,:\,i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\right\}
be a geometric construction.
Then conditionally given the unordered set
\{U_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\}
, the following holds a.s.:

1.

{\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
is a uniform
t
-cyclic permutation.

2.

If
I,J
are two essentially disjoint intervals of
[0,1]
then, conditionally given
\left|\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)\right|
, the permutation
{\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)\right)
is uniformly random of size
\left|\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)\right|
.

Proof.

The conditional law of
\mathcal{P}
given
\{U_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\}
can be a.s. re-described as follows.
Sort
\{U_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\}
as
U_{(1)}<\dots<U_{(n)}
, let
\rho
be a uniformly random bijection from
[n]
to
\mathcal{V}_{t}
, then set
V_{i}:=U_{(\rho^{-1}(i))}
for any
i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
, and finally
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\big.(V_{i},V_{\mathfrak{s}(i)})\,:\,i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\right\}
.
The first claim then follows from the same argument as in the proof of
1.1
.

For the second claim, notice that
\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)
is a random subset of the finite grid
\left\{\left(U_{(i)},U_{(j)}\right):i,j\in[n]\right\}\cap(I\times J)
.
Consider
0\leq m\leq n
and indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\in[n]
such that for each
r\in[m]
,
U_{(i_{r})}\in I
and
U_{(j_{r})}\in J
.
The sets of indices
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}
and
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\}
are then a.s. disjoint.
Furthermore the probability

\mathbf{P}\left(\forall r\in[m],\;\left(U_{(i_{r})},U_{(j_{r})}\right)\in\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\forall r\in[m],\;\left(V_{\rho(i_{r})},V_{\rho(j_{r})}\right)\in\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\big.\forall r\in[m],\;\rho(j_{r})=\mathfrak{s}(\rho(i_{r}))\right)

only depends on
i_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{1},\dots,j_{m}
through
m
.
This readily implies that conditionally given
\{U_{i},i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\}
and
\left|\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)\right|
, the permutation
{\rm Perm}\left(\mathcal{P}\cap(I\times J)\right)
is uniformly random.
∎

Proofs of the results on monotone subsequences

4.1
Decreasing subsequences

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

Throughout the proof we fix
\delta>0
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a geometric construction of
\tau_{n}
.

Upper tail bound:

Suppose, without loss of generality, that
\delta=1/\beta
for some integer
\beta
.
Fix an arbitrary integer
K>16\beta^{2}
and set
\Delta x:=1/K
.
Slice
[0,1]^{2}
into a regular grid
\left(C_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq K}
made of square cells

C_{i,j}:=[(i-1)\Delta x,i\Delta x]\times[(j-1)\Delta x,j\Delta x].

Notice that any down-right path of points can only visit at most one diagonal square cell
C_{i,i}
.
Therefore

\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})=\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\big)+\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\mathrm{LDS}\left(\mathcal{P}\cap C_{i,i}\right)

(2)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{P}
to the cells
C_{i,j}
with
i\neq j
.
For each
i
,
3.1
asserts that
\mathcal{P}\cap C_{i,i}
is a superposition of three (correlated) random-sized sets of i.i.d. uniform points in
C_{i,i}
.
Writing
L_{m}
for the
\mathrm{LDS}
of a uniform permutation of size
m
, we obtain:

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C_{i,i})>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq 3\max_{1\leq j\leq 3}\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\mathcal{P}^{(j)}\cap C_{i,i}\big)>\frac{2\delta}{3}\sqrt{n}\right)\leq 3\max_{1\leq j\leq 3}\mathbf{P}\left(L_{\left|\mathcal{P}^{(j)}\cap C_{i,i}\right|}>\frac{2\delta}{3}\sqrt{n}\right).

Since each size
\left|\mathcal{P}^{(j)}\cap C_{i,i}\right|
is stochastically dominated by
{\rm Binom}\left(\big.n,K^{-2}\right)
and the variables
L_{m}
are stochastically nondecreasing in
m
, this yields:

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C_{i,i})>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq 3K\mathbf{P}\left(L_{{\rm Binom}\left(n,K^{-2}\right)}>\frac{2\delta}{3}\sqrt{n}\right).

(3)

First off, Chernoff bounds imply

\mathbf{P}\left(\big.{\rm Binom}\left(n,K^{-2}\right)>2nK^{-2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right)

(4)

for some constant
c_{\delta}>0
which depends only on
\delta
and whose value may change throughout the proof.
Then, since
2\sqrt{2}K^{-1}<\sqrt{2}\delta^{2}/8<2\delta/3
,
[
DZ99
, Theorem 2]
implies

\mathbf{P}\left(L_{2nK^{-2}}>\frac{2\delta}{3}\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right)

(5)

for some other
c_{\delta}>0
.
From
Equations

3
,
4
and
5
we deduce

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C_{i,i})>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right)

for some constant
c_{\delta}>0
.
Then by (
2
):

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})>2(1+3\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\big.\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\big)>2(1+2\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)+\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

(6)

To study the remaining term, we can broadly rewrite the proof of
[
DZ95
, Lemma 9]
.
Set
\Delta y:=\Delta x/\beta=1/(K\beta)
and slice
[0,1]^{2}
into a thinner grid
\left(\widetilde{C}_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i\leq K,1\leq j\leq K\beta}
made of rectangular cells

\widetilde{C}_{i,j}:=[(i-1)\Delta x,i\Delta x]\times[(j-1)\Delta y,j\Delta y].

Figure 3:
Representation of the down-right sequence of cells (highlighted) encoded by the admissible sequence
\big((10,8),(8,7),(5,4),(4,4),(4,2)\big)
, with
K=5
and
\beta=2
.

Say a sequence of indices
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=(a_{i},b_{i})_{1\leq i\leq K}
is
admissible
if it satisfies
1\leq b_{i+1}\leq a_{i+1}\leq b_{i}\leq a_{i}\leq K\beta
for all
i\in[K-1]
.
Informally, it encodes a down-right sequence of cells where
a_{i}
, resp.
b_{i}
, is the highest, resp. lowest, cell in the
i
-th column.
See
Figure

3
for a representation.
Fix such
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
and write
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
for the restriction of
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
to the cells associated with
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
.
For each
1\leq i\leq K
, write

R_{i}:=[(i-1)\Delta x,i\Delta x]\times[(b_{i}-1)\Delta y,a_{i}\Delta y]

for the
i
-th column of
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
, and set
h_{i}:=a_{i}-b_{i}+1
.
Since
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
encodes a down-right path, we can write

\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}\big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathrm{LDS}(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\cap R_{i}).

(7)

Furthermore, at most one column
R_{i}
can intersect the diagonal square cells
C_{i,i}
.
When this happens, write
i_{0}
for the corresponding index, and arbitrarily set
i_{0}:=1
otherwise.
Now notice that, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{h_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}\leq\sqrt{K\sum_{i=1}^{K}h_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}\leq\sqrt{K\Delta x\Delta y(K+K\beta)}=\sqrt{1+\delta}

where the middle inequality is obtained by noticing that a down-right path has at most
K+K\beta
cells.
This yields

1+2\delta\geq\delta+\sqrt{1+\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{h_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}.

(8)

Using
Equations

7
and
8
, we deduce:

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}\big)>2(1+2\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\\
\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\exists i\neq i_{0},\;\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap R_{i})>2\sqrt{1+\delta}\sqrt{nh_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\cap R_{i_{0}}\big)>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right).

(9)

We can decompose the family
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\cap R_{i_{0}}=(\mathcal{P}\cap A)\sqcup(\mathcal{P}\cap B)
into its points above and below
C_{i_{0},i_{0}}
.
Since we have
\mathrm{Leb}(A)\leq\Delta x<\delta^{2}/16
,
3.2
yields:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{P}\cap A\right|>\frac{\delta^{2}}{16}n\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right),

and likewise for
B
.

3.3
asserts that the sets
\mathcal{P}\cap A
and
\mathcal{P}\cap B
consist of i.i.d. uniform points, hence:

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\cap R_{i_{0}}\big)>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\mathbf{P}\left(L_{\left|\mathcal{P}\cap A\right|}>\delta\sqrt{n}\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(L_{\left|\mathcal{P}\cap B\right|}>\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq 2\mathbf{P}\left(L_{\frac{\delta^{2}}{16}n}>\delta\sqrt{n}\right)+\exp(-c_{\delta}n).

Therefore, by
[
DZ99
, Theorem 2]
:

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\cap R_{i_{0}}\big)>2\delta\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

(10)

The first term of (
9
) is handled in a similar way.
If
i\neq i_{0}
, by
3.2
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\left|\mathcal{P}\cap R_{i}\right|>(1+\tfrac{\delta}{2})nh_{i}\Delta x\Delta y\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right).

We can then use
3.3
and
[
DZ99
, Theorem 2]
as before:

\mathbf{P}\left(\exists i\neq i_{0},\;\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap R_{i})>2\sqrt{1+\delta}\sqrt{nh_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}\right)\\
\leq K\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right)+K\max_{i\neq i_{0}}\mathbf{P}\left(L_{(1+\frac{\delta}{2})nh_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}>2\sqrt{1+\delta}\sqrt{nh_{i}\Delta x\Delta y}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

(11)

Finally, putting together
Equations

9
,
10
and
11
:

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LDS}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}\big)>2(1+2\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right).

Since any down-right path of points defines an admissible sequence and the number of admissible sequences is bounded by

, we deduce:

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\check{\mathcal{P}})>2(1+2\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}\sqrt{n}\right)

for some constant
c_{\delta}>0
which depends only on
\delta
.
Using (
6
), this concludes the proof of the upper tail bound.

Lower tail bound:

Define
C:=[0,1/2]\times[1/2,1]
and
C^{*}:=[1/2,1]\times[0,1/2]
.
Then:

\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P})\geq\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C)+\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C^{*}).

We can apply
3.2
to obtain

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\left|\mathcal{P}\cap C\right|<(1-\delta/2)^{2}n/4\right)\leq\exp\left(-2c_{\delta}n\right)

for some

, therefore by
3.3
and
[

:

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C)<(1-\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-2c_{\delta}n\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(L_{(1-\delta/2)^{2}n/4}<2(1-\delta)\sqrt{n/4}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right)

and likewise for

.
Finally:

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\tau_{n})<2(1\!-\!\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C)<(1\!-\!\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(\Big.\mathrm{LDS}(\mathcal{P}\cap C^{*})<(1\!-\!\delta)\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right)

for some

, as desired.

2.1
.
∎

Proofs of the results on Robinson–Schensted shapes

Our proof of
Theorem

2.7
relies heavily on the substantial work of Sjöstrand
[
Sjö23
]
.
Let us recall a few definitions from the latter.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
If
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is Lebesgue-measurable, we write
\lVert u\rVert_{1,\Omega}:=\int_{\Omega}\left|u\right|d\mathrm{Leb}
for its
L^{1}
norm.
Say that
u
is
doubly increasing
if
u(x,y)\leq u(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
whenever
(x,y)\leq(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Denote by
\mathcal{U}(\Omega)
the set of doubly increasing functions on
\Omega
, and by
\mathcal{U}_{h,r}(\Omega)
its subset consisting of functions with values in
[h,h+r]
.
For any
u\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega)
, let

D(u):=\left\{\Big.(x,y)\in\Omega\,:\,u(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }u(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<u(x,y)\text{ for any }(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Omega\setminus\{(x,y)\}\text{ s.t. }(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq(x,y)\right\}.

Informally,
D(u)
is the set of “south-west corners” in the integer level lines of
u
.
If
u\in\mathcal{U}_{0,r}(\Omega)
then
D(u)
is an
\lfloor r\rfloor
-decreasing subset of
\Omega

[
Sjö23
, Lemma 6.2]
.
Reciprocally, for any finite
k
-decreasing subset
P
of
\Omega
, there exists a

Invertly, all points of
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}
in
[0,U_{\text{left}}]^{2}\cup[V_{\text{up}},1]^{2}
, as well as the ones which are high records in
\mathcal{P}\cap C
, are high records in
\mathcal{P}
.
From the previous two observations we deduce the following inequality w.h.p. as
n\to\infty
:

\left|\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)-\left(\Big.\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)+S_{n}\right)\right|\\
\leq\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap\left([U_{\text{left}},U_{\text{left}}^{C}]\times[V_{\text{left}},V_{\text{left}}^{C}]\right)\right|+\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap\left([U_{\text{up}}^{C},U_{\text{up}}]\times[V_{\text{up}}^{C},V_{\text{up}}]\right)\right|

where
S_{n}:=\left|\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}\cap[0,U_{\text{left}}]^{2}\right|+\left|\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}\cap[V_{\text{up}},1]^{2}\right|
.
According to
6.1
,
U_{\text{left}}^{C}
is a
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}\left(1/\check{n}\right)
.
For any
M>0
, we may apply
3.2
to the rectangle
[0,M/\check{n}]\times[0,1]
to obtain
\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap\left([0,M/\check{n}]\times[0,1]\right)\right|\leq 2M
w.h.p. as
n\to\infty
.
Since
U_{\text{left}}^{C}\leq M/\check{n}
with probability tending to
1
as
M\to\infty
, uniformly in
n
, this implies that the variable
\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap\left([U_{\text{left}},U_{\text{left}}^{C}]\times[V_{\text{left}},V_{\text{left}}^{C}]\right)\right|
is a
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(1)
.
With the same reasoning applied to
1-V_{\text{up}}^{C}
and the corresponding rectangle, we deduce:

\left|\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)-\left(\Big.\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)+S_{n}\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(1).

(21)

Now it remains to analyse both terms in the sum.
According to
3.3
and
3.2
,
\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C
is a family of i.i.d. uniform points in
C
, with size
\check{n}/{4}+o_{\mathbf{P}}\left(\check{n}\right)
.
We can thus apply
Theorem

2.9
to deduce

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)-\log\check{n}}{\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right)

(22)

in distribution.
Let us turn to the fixed points.

\check{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}
are independent, so
(U_{\text{left}},V_{\text{up}})
and
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}
are as well.
Therefore, conditionally given
(U_{\text{left}},V_{\text{up}})
,
S_{n}
follows a
{\rm Binom}\left(t_{1}^{(n)},U_{\text{left}}\!+\!1\!-\!V_{\text{up}}\right)
law.
Recall from
6.1
that

\check{n}\left(U_{\text{left}}+1-V_{\text{up}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Gamma}\left(2,1\right):={\rm Exp}\left(1\right)*{\rm Exp}\left(1\right)

in distribution.
If
{t_{1}^{(n)}}/{\check{n}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
n\to\infty
then
S_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}(1)
(use e.g. the Bienaymé–-Chebyshev inequality), whereas if
{t_{1}^{(n)}}/{\check{n}}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
then

\frac{\check{n}}{t_{1}^{(n)}}S_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Gamma}\left(2,1\right)

(23)

in distribution.
Now we can use
Equations

21
,
22
and
23
to identify three regimes in the asymptotics of
\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
.

“Mostly uniform” regime:

Assume
\frac{\check{n}}{n/\sqrt{\log n}}\to\infty
.
Then
\log n\sim\log\check{n}
as
n\to\infty
, and since
t_{1}^{(n)}\leq n
, we have
\frac{t_{1}^{(n)}}{\check{n}\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}\to 0
.
Consequently the fluctuations of
\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)
dominate, and

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)-\log\check{n}}{\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

“Mostly fixed points” regime:

Assume
\frac{\check{n}}{n/\sqrt{\log n}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
\log\check{n}\leq\log n
and
t_{1}^{(n)}=n-\check{n}
, we have
\frac{t_{1}^{(n)}}{\check{n}\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}\to\infty
.
Consequently the fluctuations of
S_{n}
dominate, and

\frac{\check{n}}{t_{1}^{(n)}}\left(\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)-\log\check{n}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Gamma}\left(2,1\right).

“Intermediate” regime:

Assume
\frac{\check{n}}{n/\sqrt{\log n}}\to\alpha\in(0,\infty)
.
Then
\log\check{n}\sim\log n
as
n\to\infty
, and since
t_{1}^{(n)}=n-\check{n}
, we deduce
\frac{t_{1}^{(n)}}{\check{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\check{n}\right)}}\to 1/\alpha
.
Thus we need to understand the interplay between the limit laws of
Equations

22
and
23
.
To do this, notice that conditionally given
\left(U_{\text{left}},V_{\text{up}}\right)
, the variables
S_{n}
and
\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C
are independent.
Recall that under this conditioning,
S_{n}
follows a
{\rm Binom}\left(t_{1}^{(n)},U_{\text{left}}\!+\!1\!-\!V_{\text{up}}\right)
law.
According to
3.4
, the conditional law of
{\rm Perm}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)
given
\left(U_{\text{left}},V_{\text{up}}\right)=\left(U_{(1)},U_{(n)}\right)
and
\left|\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right|
is uniform.
From this property we deduce independence of the limit laws in
Equations

23
and
22
, whence:

\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)-\log\check{n}}{\frac{t_{1}^{(n)}}{\check{n}}+\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}=\frac{\mathrm{rec}^{\uparrow}\left(\check{\mathcal{P}}\cap C\right)-\log\check{n}}{(1+1/\alpha+o(1))\sqrt{\log\check{n}}}+\frac{S_{n}}{(1+\alpha+o(1))\frac{t_{1}^{(n)}}{\check{n}}}+o_{\mathbf{P}}(1)\\
\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right)*\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathrm{Gamma}\left(2,1\right)

in distribution, as announced.
∎

Lemma 6.2

For each

, let

be a cycle type of size

Proof.

By
3.2
,
\left|\mathcal{P}\cap B_{n}\right|
is distributed like a sum of three
{\rm Binom}\left(\lfloor n/3\rfloor\pm 1,\left(1-\epsilon/\sqrt{n}\right)\big.\epsilon/\sqrt{n}\right)
, and likewise for
B_{n}^{*}
.
The first claim readily follows from standard concentration inequalities.
Then w.h.p. as
n\to\infty
, the sets
\mathcal{P}\cap B_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}\cap B_{n}^{*}
are non-empty and
Z_{\text{down}}^{B}
,
Z_{\text{left}}^{B^{*}}
are well defined.
According to
3.3
, conditionally given

, the set

consists of i.i.d. uniform points in
B_{n}
.
We can thus write, for any

and large enough

\mathbf{P}\left(\epsilon\sqrt{n}V_{\text{down}}^{B}>s+\epsilon^{2}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(V_{\text{down}}^{B}>\frac{s}{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}+\epsilon/\sqrt{n}\right)=\left(1-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon/\sqrt{n}}\frac{s}{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}\right)^{\left|\mathcal{P}\cap B_{n}\right|}.

Hence
\mathbf{P}\left(\epsilon\sqrt{n}V_{\text{down}}^{B}>s+\epsilon^{2}\right)\to e^{-s}
as
n\to\infty
.
The same holds for
U_{\text{left}}^{B^{*}}

∎

7.1
Dependency graphs and asymptotic normality

We recall the notion of dependency graph, as introduced in
[
PL82
]
.
Let
(Z_{i})_{i\in V}
be a family of random variables indexed by some set
V
.
We say that a graph
(V,E)
is a
dependency graph
for
(Z_{i})_{i\in V}
if it has the following property:
for any disjoint subsets
V_{1},V_{2}
of
V
which are not linked by any edge in
E
, the two families
(Z_{i})_{i\in V_{1}}
and
(Z_{i})_{i\in V_{2}}
are independent.

Let
t
be a cycle type of size
n
, and
\mathcal{L}_{t}
be the graph defined in
Section

1.3
.
The following lemma is a direct consequence of the definitions.

Lemma 7.1
.

In the geometric construction of
1.1
, the graph
\mathcal{L}_{t}=(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t})
is a dependency graph for the variables
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
.

Now we can use
\mathcal{L}_{t}
to construct another dependency graph.
Let
\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
denote the set of subsets of size
r
in
\mathcal{V}_{t}
.
Define a graph
\binom{\mathcal{L}_{t}}{r}
with vertices
\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
as follows:

I,J\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
share an edge if and only if there exist
i\in I
and
j\in J
such that
i=j
or
i\sim j
in
\mathcal{L}_{t}
.
Using
7.1
, it is straightforward to check that
\binom{\mathcal{L}_{t}}{r}
is a dependency graph for the family of random variables
\left(\big.(Z_{i})_{i\in I}\right)_{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}}
.

The idea that the variables
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
are “partly dependent” is motivated by the fact that these graphs have low degrees:
the degrees of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
are bounded by
2
, and a rough upper bound for the degrees of
\binom{\mathcal{L}_{t}}{r}
is
3r\binom{n}{r-1}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{r-1}\right)
.
We could then apply
[
Jan88
, Theorem 2]
to deduce asymptotic normality for the pattern count
X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})
, but we rather use Stein’s method to obtain a stronger result.

Proof of
Theorem

2.14
.

We aim to apply
[
Hof18
, Theorem 3.5]
, which is based on the results of
[
CR10
]
and
[
Ros11
]
, to the decomposition (
1
) of
X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})
.
According to
7.1
and the discussion following it,
\binom{\mathcal{L}_{t}}{r}
is a dependency graph for the family of random variables
\left(\mathbf{1}_{{\rm Perm}\left(Z_{i},i\in I\right)=\pi}\right)_{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}}
.
Thus we can use
[
Hof18
, Theorem 3.5]
with
B=1
,
D\leq 3r\binom{n}{r-1}
, and
N=\binom{n}{r}
.
This yields the announced bound on the Kolmogorov distance.

Now suppose
\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]=\sigma^{2}n^{2r-1}+o(n^{2r-1})
as
n\to\infty
, for some
\sigma\geq 0
.
If
\sigma=0
then

\frac{X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\pi}(\tau_{n})\right]}{n^{r-1/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

in probability, by Bienaymé–Chebychev’s inequality.
If
\sigma>0
then the previous bound on the Kolmogorov distance is a
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.
In particular it goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
, which implies convergence in distribution of the random variables.
This concludes the proof.
∎

7.2
Computation of the variance

Proof of
Theorem

2.15
.

We only prove marginal convergence, for convenience.
Joint convergence is proved by the same method, using the Cramér–Wold theorem.

Fix a pattern
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
Throughout the proof we may omit the indices
\pi,p_{1},p_{2},n
.
Thanks to
Theorem

2.14
, it suffices to compute the mean and variance of
X:=X_{\pi}(\tau)
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}
be a geometric construction of
\tau_{n}
.
The first idea is to approximate
X
with a new variable

Y:=\sum_{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}\text{ indep.}}\mathbf{1}_{{\rm Perm}\left(Z_{i},i\in I\right)=\pi},

where the sum ranges over all subsets
I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
which induce an independent (i.e. empty) subgraph of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
.
Since the number of non-independent
r
-sized subgraphs of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is bounded by
2(r-1)\binom{n}{r-1}
, almost surely it holds that
\left|X-Y\right|\leq 2(r-1)\binom{n}{r-1}
.
In particular
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{r-1}\right)
and
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[Y\right]}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{r-1}\right)
, so it suffices to prove the desired result for
Y
.
To do this, we shall rewrite
Y
while keeping track of how many fixed points are picked in the sum.

Decompose
\mathcal{V}_{t}
as
\mathcal{V}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{V}_{2}\sqcup\mathcal{V}_{3}
where
\mathcal{V}_{m}:=\{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}:p=m\}
for
m\in\{1,2\}
correspond to fixed points and points in
2
-cycles, and
\mathcal{V}_{3}:=\{(p,k,l)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}:p\geq 3\}
corresponds to all other points.
Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{r},V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
be i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]\right)
random variables.
For any
q\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}
, define:

\mu_{q}:=\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\big.(U_{1},U_{1}),\dots,(U_{q},U_{q}),(U_{q+1},V_{q+1}),\dots,(U_{r},V_{r})\right)=\pi\right),

so that

\mu:=\mu_{\pi}^{p_{1}}=\sum_{q=0}^{r}\binom{r}{q}p_{1}^{q}(1-p_{1})^{r-q}\mu_{q}.

Define
\mathcal{I}_{q}:=\left\{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}\text{ indep., }\left|I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}\right|=q\right\}
and
c_{q}:=\left|\mathcal{I}_{q}\right|
.
For any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}
, the variables
Z_{i},i\in I
are independent by
7.1
, and
\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(Z_{i},i\in I\right)=\pi\right)=\mu_{q}
.
Therefore we can write
\mathbb{E}Y=\sum_{q=0}^{r}c_{q}\mu_{q}
.
Since the number of non-independent
I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{r-1})
and the total number of
I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}
with
|I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}|=q
is

\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{1}|}{q}\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}|}{r-q}=\frac{(np_{1})^{q}+o(n^{q\!-\!1/2})}{q!}\frac{(n(1\!\!-\!\!p_{1}))^{r\!-\!q}+o(n^{r\!-\!q\!-\!1/2})}{(r\!-\!q)!}=\binom{n}{r}\binom{r}{q}p_{1}^{q}(1\!\!-\!\!p_{1})^{r\!-\!q}+o(n^{r\!-\!1/2}),

we deduce
\mathbb{E}Y=\binom{n}{r}\mu+o(n^{r-1/2})
.
It remains to study the variance of
Y
, and for this we rely on the method of
U
-statistics developed in
[
Hoe48
]
.
The idea is to decompose
Y
using its univariate projections.
First define:

\bar{Y}:=Y-\mathbb{E}Y=\sum_{q=0}^{r}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}}\bar{\phi}_{q}(Z_{i},i\in I)\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{\phi}_{q}(z_{1},\dots,z_{r}):=\mathbf{1}_{{\rm Perm}\left(z_{1},\dots,z_{r}\right)=\pi}-\mu_{q}.

Then set for any
q\in\{0,1,\dots,r\!-\!1\}
and
z\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\varphi_{q}(z):=\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\big.(U_{1},U_{1}),\dots,(U_{q},U_{q}),(U_{q+1},V_{q+1}),\dots,(U_{r-1},V_{r-1}),z\right)=\pi\right),

so that for any
z\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\psi(z):=\psi_{\pi}^{p_{1}}(z)=\sum_{q=0}^{r-1}\binom{r-1}{q}p_{1}^{q}(1-p_{1})^{r-q-1}\varphi_{q}(z).

(30)

It follows from definition that for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\phi}_{q}(Z_{i},i\in I)\;\big|Z_{j}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi_{q}(Z_{j})-\mu_{q}&\text{ if }j\in I\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1};\\
\varphi_{q-1}(Z_{j})-\mu_{q}&\text{ if }j\in I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}.\end{array}\right.

Now for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}
and
(z_{i})_{i\in I}\in\left([0,1]^{2}\right)^{I}
, define the “residual function”:

\phi_{I}^{*}(z_{i},i\in I):=\bar{\phi}_{q}(z_{i},i\in I)-\sum_{i\in I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}}\left(\varphi_{q\!-\!1}(z_{i})-\mu_{q}\right)-\sum_{i\in I\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}}\left(\varphi_{q}(z_{i})-\mu_{q}\right).

Notice that if the sum over
i\in I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}
is non-empty then
q\geq 1
i.e.
q\!-\!1\geq 0
.
A key property of
\phi_{I}^{*}
is that its univariate projections vanish:
for any
j\in I
, a.s.
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)\;\big|Z_{j}\right]=0
.
This is checked directly by distinguishing between
j\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
j\notin\mathcal{V}_{1}
to use (
7.2
), observing that most terms have zero mean and that two of them cancel out.
With this notation, we can decompose
\bar{Y}
as:

\bar{Y}=\sum_{I\in\binom{\mathcal{V}_{t}}{r}\text{ indep.}}\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)+\sum_{q=0}^{r}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}}\left(\sum_{i\in I\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}}\varphi_{q\!-\!1}(Z_{i})+\sum_{i\in I\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}}\varphi_{q}(Z_{i})-r\mu_{q}\right)=Y_{*}+\hat{Y}.

When expanding
Y_{*}^{2}
, one can distinguish the pairs
(I,J)
of
r
-sized independent subgraphs of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
into three categories: the ones sharing no common vertex and connected by no edge; the ones sharing exactly one vertex or connected by exactly one edge; and the others.
If
(I,J)
is in the first category then
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)\phi_{J}^{*}(Z_{j},j\in J)\right]=0
by independence (see
7.1
).
If
(I,J)
is in the second category, write
(\alpha,\beta)\in I\times J
for the unique pair of connected or equal vertices.
Then
I\setminus\{\alpha\}
is independent from
J\setminus\{\beta\}
and we can write a.s.:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\big.\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)\phi_{J}^{*}(Z_{j},j\in J)\;\right|Z_{\alpha},Z_{\beta}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\big.\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)\;\right|Z_{\alpha}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\big.\phi_{J}^{*}(Z_{j},j\in J)\;\right|Z_{\beta}\right]=0.

In particular
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{I}^{*}(Z_{i},i\in I)\phi_{J}^{*}(Z_{j},j\in J)\right]=0
.
Since the number of pairs in the third category is bounded by
9r^{2}\binom{n}{r}\binom{n}{r-2}
, we deduce
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{*}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2r-2}\right)
as
n\to\infty
.
Thus
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\bar{Y}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\hat{Y}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{r-1}\right)
, and it suffices to study
\hat{Y}
.
Since
\sum_{q=0}^{r}c_{q}\mu_{q}=\binom{n}{r}\mu+o\left(n^{r-1/2}\right)
, we can rewrite it as:

\hat{Y}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\sum_{q=1}^{r}c_{i,q}\varphi_{q-1}(Z_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}}\sum_{q=0}^{r-1}c_{i,q}\varphi_{q}(Z_{i})-r\binom{n}{r}\mu+o\left(n^{r-1/2}\right),

where the
o
is deterministic, and for any
q\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}
and
i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
,
c_{i,q}
is the number of
I\in\mathcal{I}_{q}
containing
i
.
These numbers satisfy, as
n\to\infty
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{i,q}=\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{1}|-1}{q-1}\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}|}{r-q}+\mathcal{O}(n^{r-2})=\binom{n}{r-1}\binom{r-1}{q-1}p_{1}^{q-1}(1\!-\!p_{1})^{r-q}+o(n^{r-3/2})\text{ uniformly in }i\in\mathcal{V}_{1}\vskip 5.0pt;\\
c_{i,q}=\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{1}|}{q}\binom{|\mathcal{V}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}|-1}{r-q-1}+\mathcal{O}(n^{r-2})=\binom{n}{r-1}\binom{r-1}{q}p_{1}^{q}(1\!-\!p_{1})^{r-q-1}+o(n^{r-3/2})\text{ uniformly in }i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}.\end{array}\right.

Then define

\widetilde{Y}:=\binom{n}{r\!-\!1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}\left(\big.\psi(Z_{i})-\mu\right).

The previous calculation and (
30
) imply that
\left|\hat{Y}-\widetilde{Y}\right|=o(n^{r-1/2})
a.s. for some deterministic
o
, hence as before we just need to compute the variance of
\widetilde{Y}
.
Using
7.1
we can write:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\binom{n}{r\!-\!1}^{-1}\widetilde{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(Z_{i}),\psi(Z_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi(Z_{i})\right]+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(Z_{i}),\psi(Z_{\mathfrak{s}(i)})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(Z_{i}),\psi(Z_{\mathfrak{s}(i)})\right]+\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(Z_{i}),\psi(Z_{\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(i)})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=np_{1}\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi(U,U)\right]+n(1\!-\!p_{1})\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi(U,V)\right]+np_{2}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(U,V),\psi(V,U)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2n(1\!-\!p_{1}\!-\!p_{2})\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi(U,V),\psi(V,W)\right]+o(n)

where
U,V,W
are i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]\right)
variables.
Thus
\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]=\sigma^{2}n^{2r-1}+o(n^{2r-1})
with
\sigma^{2}:=\Sigma_{\pi,\pi}^{p_{1},p_{2}}
as defined in the statement of
Theorem

2.15
.
Thanks to
Theorem

2.14
, this concludes the proof.
∎

\hat{Z}_{i}^{\prime}s

\Delta

(33)

;

2.15
rewrites as:

(r\!-\!1)!^{2}\Sigma_{\pi,\rho}^{p_{2}}=\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(U,V)\right]+p_{2}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(V,U)\right]+2(1\!-\!p_{2})\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(V,W)\right]

where
U,V,W
are i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]\right)
variables.
To establish
Theorem

2.18
, we need to study the function
\psi
a bit more.

Lemma 7.3
.

If
p_{1}=0
then for any
\pi,\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
:

\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(V,W)\right]=0.

Proof.

For any distinct
u_{1},v_{1},\dots,u_{r},v_{r}\in[0,1]
, we can write
{\rm Perm}\left((u_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(u_{r},v_{r})\right)=\sigma_{2}^{-1}\circ\sigma_{1}

where
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
are caracterized by
u_{\sigma_{1}(1)}<\dots<u_{\sigma_{1}(r)}
and
v_{\sigma_{2}(1)}<\dots<v_{\sigma_{2}(r)}
.
If
(U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{r},V_{r})
are i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)
variables then
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are i.i.d. uniform permutations of size
r
.
Conditionally given
(V_{1},\dots,V_{r})
,
\sigma_{2}
is determined and
\sigma_{1}
is still uniformly random in
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
Subsequently

\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left((U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{r},V_{r})\right)=\pi\;\big|V_{1},\dots,V_{r}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\circ\pi\;\big|\sigma_{2}\right)=1/r!

almost surely, and similarly

\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left((U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{r},V_{r})\right)=\rho\;\big|U_{1},\dots,U_{r}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{1}\circ\rho^{-1}\;\big|\sigma_{1}\right)=1/r!

almost surely.
We can thus write
\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V)\psi_{\rho}(V,W)\;\big|V\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V)\,\big|V\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\rho}(V,W)\,\big|V\right]=(1/r!)^{2}
a.s., and the lemma readily follows.
∎

This yields the announced formula for
\Sigma_{\pi,\rho}^{p_{2}}
.
It remains to compute the rank and show non-degeneracy.
For this we shall give an explicit polynomial formula for
\psi_{\pi}
; it can already be found in
[
JNZ15
]
(see Equation (5) therein for its rescaled expression) but we provide a proof below for completeness.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
We can write, for any
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\psi_{\pi}(u,v)=\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\sum_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}(u)g_{\pi(i)}(v)

where for any
j\in[r]
and
w\in\mathbb{R}
,
g_{j}(w):=\binom{r-1}{j-1}w^{j-1}(1-w)^{r-j}
.
Moreover, if
r\geq 2
then the symmetrized function
(u,v)\mapsto\psi^{\rm sym}_{\pi}(u,v):=\psi_{\pi}(u,v)+\psi_{\pi}(v,u)
is not constant.

Proof.

We omit the index
\pi
in this proof.
Write
\psi=\psi_{1}+\dots+\psi_{r}
where for any
i\in[r]
and
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
, if
\hat{Z}_{1},\dots,\hat{Z}_{r-1}
denote i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)
random variables:

\psi_{i}(u,v):=\mathbf{P}\left({\rm Perm}\left(\hat{Z}_{1},\dots,\hat{Z}_{r-1},(u,v)\right)=\pi\;,\;(u,v)\text{ is $i$-th from the left}\right).

For this event, say
A_{i}(u,v)
, to be realized, there needs to be exactly
i\!-\!1
points left of
(u,v)
and
\pi(i)\!-\!1
below
(u,v)
.
More precisely there needs to be exactly
j_{\mathrm{SW}},j_{\mathrm{SE}},j_{\mathrm{NE}},j_{\mathrm{NW}}
points in each quadrant defined by
(u,v)
, where these quantities depend only on
\pi
and
i
.
Let
B_{i}(u,v)
be this last event; its probability can be written as
\binom{r-1}{j_{\mathrm{SW}},\,j_{\mathrm{SE}},\,j_{\mathrm{NE}},\,j_{\mathrm{NW}}}u^{i-1}(1-u)^{r-i}v^{\pi(i)-1}(1-v)^{r-\pi(i)}
.
Then conditionally on
B_{i}(u,v)
, the probability of
A_{i}(u,v)
does not depend on
(u,v)
anymore.
Indeed by a coupling argument, one can e.g. see that
\mathbf{P}\left(A_{i}(u,v)\big|B_{i}(u,v)\right)
is the probability that
j_{\mathrm{SW}}
uniform points in
[0,1/2]^{2}
,
j_{\mathrm{SE}}
uniform points in
[1/2,1]\!\times\![0,1/2]
,
j_{\mathrm{NE}}
uniform points in
[1/2,1]^{2}
, and
j_{\mathrm{NW}}
uniform points in
[0,1/2]\!\times\![1/2,1]
, all independent, form the permutation induced by
\pi
on
[r]\setminus\{i\}
.
Hence
\mathbf{P}\left(A_{i}(u,v)\big|B_{i}(u,v)\right)
equals a positive constant, independent of
(u,v)
, and we deduce that

\psi_{i}(u,v)=\mathbf{P}\left(A_{i}(u,v)\big|B_{i}(u,v)\right)\mathbf{P}\left(B_{i}(u,v)\right)=c_{i}u^{i-1}(1-u)^{r-i}v^{\pi(i)-1}(1-v)^{r-\pi(i)}.

To find the value of
c_{i}
, first notice that
\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{i}(U,V)\right]=\frac{1}{r}\frac{1}{r!}
.
Then in the previous formula:

\frac{1}{r}\frac{1}{r!}=c_{i}B\big(i,r\!-\!i\!+\!1\big)B\big(\pi(i),r\!-\!\pi(i)\!+\!1\big)

where
B
denotes the Beta function, and thus

c_{i}=\frac{(r-1)!}{(i\!-\!1)!(r\!-\!i)!(\pi(i)\!-\!1)!(r\!-\!\pi(i))!}=\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\binom{r\!-\!1}{i\!-\!1}\binom{r\!-\!1}{\pi(i)\!-\!1}.

This yields the announced formula for
\psi=\sum_{i}\psi_{i}
.
Now let us turn to the last claim of the lemma.
By reindexing we can write
\psi^{\rm sym}(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}u^{i-1}(1-u)^{r-i}Q_{i}(v)
where

Q_{i}(v):=c_{i}v^{\pi(i)-1}(1-v)^{r-\pi(i)}+c_{\pi^{-1}(i)}v^{\pi^{-1}(i)-1}(1-v)^{r-\pi^{-1}(i)}.

Suppose that
\psi^{\rm sym}
is constant on
[0,1]^{2}
.
Then by setting
u=0
we see that the polynomial
Q_{1}(v)
is constant in
v
.
If
\pi(1)>1
then
\pi^{-1}(1)>1
and the lowest degree term of
Q_{1}
has positive degree and coefficient, which contradicts the fact that
Q_{1}
is constant.
Therefore
\pi(1)=1
.
Likewise by setting
u=1
we see that
Q_{r}
is constant, which implies
\pi(r)=1
or
\pi^{-1}(r)=1
.
Hence
r=1
and this concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.18
.

First, let us show non-degeneracy.
Fix
p_{2}\in[0,1]
,
r\geq 2
,
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
Since
\psi_{\pi}
is continuous and not constant on
[0,1]^{2}
according to
7.4
, it holds that
\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V)\right]>0
.

By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
\left|\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\pi}(V,U)\right]\right|\leq\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V)\right]
.
Suppose by contradiction that
\Sigma_{\pi,\pi}^{p_{2}}=0
.
Then necessarily
p_{2}=1
and
\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\pi}(V,U)\right]=-\mathrm{Var}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V)\right]
.
This equality case can only happen if
\psi_{\pi}(U,V)
and
\psi_{\pi}(V,U)
are linearly dependent, and here we would have
\psi_{\pi}(U,V)=-\psi_{\pi}(V,U)
almost surely.
However it follows from
7.4
that
\psi_{\pi}(U,V)+\psi_{\pi}(V,U)
is not a.s. constant, and this proves by contradiction that
\Sigma_{\pi,\pi}^{p_{2}}>0
.

Now, Let us study the rank of
\Sigma^{p_{2}}
.
Fix
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
such that
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1
and
2\alpha\beta=p_{2}
(this is possible since
p_{2}\leq 1
), and define for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
and
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\chi_{\pi}(u,v):=(r-1)!\left(\alpha\bar{\psi}_{\pi}(u,v)+\beta\bar{\psi}_{\pi}(v,u)\right)

where
\bar{\psi}_{\pi}:=\psi_{\pi}-\frac{1}{r!}
.
Then:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(r-1)!^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\chi_{\pi}(U,V),\chi_{\rho}(U,V)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}\left[\alpha\psi_{\pi}(U,V)+\beta\psi_{\pi}(V,U),\alpha\psi_{\rho}(U,V)+\beta\psi_{\rho}(V,U)\right]

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(U,V)\right]+2\alpha\beta\mathrm{Cov}\left[\psi_{\pi}(U,V),\psi_{\rho}(V,U)\right]

\displaystyle=(r-1)!^{2}\Sigma^{p_{2}}_{\pi,\rho}.

Hence, up to a multiplicative constant,
\Sigma^{p_{2}}
is the Gram matrix associated to the family
(\chi_{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.
Therefore the rank of
\Sigma^{p_{2}}
equals the rank of this family.
Recall the notation of
7.4
.
For any
(u,v)
we have:

1=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\psi_{\pi}(u,v)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\sum_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}(u)g_{\pi(i)}(v)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq r}g_{i}(u)g_{j}(v).

Thus as in
[
JNZ15
]
we can write:

\bar{\psi}_{\pi}(u,v)=\frac{-1}{r!}+\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\sum_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}(u)g_{\pi(i)}(v)=\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\left(\delta_{j,\pi(i)}-\frac{1}{r}\right)g_{i}(u)g_{j}(v)

where
\delta
is the Kronecker symbol.
Define
A^{\pi}
as the matrix
\left(\delta_{j,\pi(i)}-\frac{1}{r}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
.
Then

\chi_{\pi}(u,v)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\left(\alpha A^{\pi}_{i,j}+\beta A^{(\pi^{-1})}_{i,j}\right)g_{i}(u)g_{j}(v).

Since the family
\left(\big.(u,v)\mapsto g_{i}(u)g_{j}(v)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
is linearly independent in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
, the rank of
(\chi_{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
is equal to the rank of the family
\left(\alpha A^{\pi}+\beta A^{(\pi^{-1})}\right)_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
in
\mathcal{M}_{r}(\mathbb{R})
.
As explained in
[
JNZ15
]
(see the proof of Theorem 2.5 therein), the family
(A^{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
spans the space of matrices with rows summing to 0 and columns summing to 0, and thus has rank
(r-1)^{2}
.

First suppose
p_{2}<1
, which corresponds to
\alpha\neq\beta
.
This implies that each matrix in
(A^{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
is a linear combination of matrices in
\left(\alpha A^{\pi}+\beta A^{(\pi^{-1})}\right)_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
and vice versa, hence the latter family also has rank
(r-1)^{2}
.

Now suppose
p_{2}=1
i.e.
\alpha=\beta
.
It is easily checked that the family
\left(A^{\pi}+A^{(\pi^{-1})}\right)_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
spans the space of
symmetric
matrices with rows summing to 0 and columns summing to 0.
Such a matrix
A
is exactly characterized by its coefficients
A_{i,j}
with
i\leq j<r
, hence this space has dimension
r(r-1)/2
.
This concludes the proof.
∎
