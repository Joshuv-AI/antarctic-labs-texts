On the Pauli Spectrum of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Shivam Nadimpalli, Natalie Parham, Francisca Vasconcelos, Henry Yuen

Abstract
The circuit class 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} was introduced by Moore (1999) as a model for constant depth quantum circuits where the gate set includes many-qubit Toffoli gates. Proving lower bounds against such circuits is a longstanding challenge in quantum circuit complexity; in particular, showing that polynomial-size 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} cannot compute the parity function has remained an open question for over 20 years. In this work, we identify a notion of the Pauli spectrum of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits, which can be viewed as the quantum analogue of the Fourier spectrum of classical 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} circuits. We conjecture that the Pauli spectrum of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits satisfies low-degree concentration , in analogy to the famous Linial, Mansour, Nisan (LMN) theorem on the low-degree Fourier concentration of 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} circuits. If true, this conjecture immediately implies that polynomial-size 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits cannot compute parity. We prove this conjecture for the class of depth- d d , polynomial-size 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits with at most n O ⁡ ( 1 / d ) n^{O(1/d)} auxiliary qubits. We obtain new circuit lower bounds and learning results as applications: this class of circuits cannot correctly compute • the n n -bit parity function on more than ( 1 2 + 2 − Ω ⁡ ( n 1 / d ) ) (\frac{1}{2}+2^{-\Omega(n^{1/d})}) -fraction of inputs, and • the n n -bit majority function on more than ( 1 − Ω ( n − 1 / 2 ) ) (1-\Omega(n^{-1/2})) -fraction of inputs. Additionally we show that this class of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits with limited auxiliary qubits can be learned with quasipolynomial sample complexity, giving the first learning result for 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits. More broadly, our results add evidence that “Pauli-analytic” techniques can be a powerful tool in studying quantum circuits.

Introduction

The Fourier spectrum of a Boolean function provides highly useful information about its complexity. For example, the celebrated result of Linial, Mansour, and Nisan
[
LMN93
]
shows that polynomial-size
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits give rise to functions whose Fourier spectra obey
low-degree concentration
; in other words, they are approximately low-degree polynomials. Since then, Fourier analytic techniques have played an essential role in breakthroughs in
learning theory
[
KM93
,
KKMS05
,
EIS22
]
, pseudorandomness
[
Bra09
,
CHLT18
,
CHHL19
]
, property testing
[
FLN+02
,
Bla09
,
KMS18
]
, classical-quantum separations
[
RT22
]
, and more.

Are there analogous analytic techniques for studying models of quantum computation? A natural quantum generalization of the Fourier spectrum of a Boolean function is the
Pauli spectrum
of a many-qubit operator. Recall that the set of
n
-qubit Pauli operators,
\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left\{I,X,Y,Z\right\}}^{\otimes n}
, forms an orthonormal basis for the space of
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
complex matrices, with respect to the (normalized) Hilbert–Schmidt inner product.
Consequently, any
n
-qubit operator
A
can be decomposed as
A=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\widehat{A}(P)\,P
, analogous to the Fourier expansion of a Boolean function.
Our paper is motivated by the following question:

When does the Pauli spectrum reveal useful information

about the complexity of a quantum computation?

Analyzing the Pauli spectrum of quantum operations has been a fruitful endeavor in recent years, leading to structural results about so-called quantum Boolean functions
[
MO08
,
RWZ22
]
, learning algorithms for quantum dynamics
[
HCP22
,
VZ23
,
SVZ23
]
, property testing of quantum operations
[
Wan11
,
CNY23
,
BY23
]
, and classical simulations of noisy quantum circuits
[
AGL+23
]
. Although each result uses a slightly different notion of Pauli decomposition, most of them focus on the Pauli spectrum of unitary operators. However, it is unclear how this notion connects with the complexity of the unitary operator.

A New Notion of Pauli Spectrum.

We introduce a different notion of Pauli decomposition: The Pauli spectrum of a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}

Our Results, In a Nutshell.

Our main technical result, at a high level, is that polynomial-size, single-qubit-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits that use few auxiliary qubits must have Pauli spectrum that is highly concentrated on low-degree coefficients. This is a new structural result about
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits (with limited auxiliary qubits) that immediately yields average-case circuit lower bounds: we show that such circuits cannot compute the parity or majority functions, even approximately (see
Section

1.2
for detailed theorem statements).

This raises the intriguing question of whether low-degree concentration holds for
all
polynomial-size
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits, not just ones with few auxiliary qubits. We conjecture that this is indeed true (see
1
for a formal statement). This would be directly analogous to the Linial-Mansour-Nisan theorem about the low-degree concentration of
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits
[
LMN93
]
, and would immediately show that parity is not computable by polynomial-size
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits, resolving the central open question posed in Moore’s 1999 paper introducing the
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit model in the first place
[
Moo99
]
.

Finally, we show that low-degree concentration of the Pauli spectrum of quantum channels yields sample-efficient
learning algorithms
for those channels (see
Theorem

3
for a more detailed theorem statement). This also directly corresponds to the learning result of
[
LMN93
]
who show that low-degree concentrated Boolean functions can be learned with quasipolynomial complexity. Our results directly imply that
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with few auxiliary qubits can be learned using quasipolynomial sample complexity, and if the conjectured low-degree concentration of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
holds, then
all
polynomial-size
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits are sample-efficiently learnable.

Although we weren’t able to prove “quantum LMN,” we believe that the conjecture provides tantalizing motivation for studying the analytic structure of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits, and for further investigating this notion of Pauli spectrum more broadly. The analogy with classical Fourier analysis of Boolean functions appears quite strong; it will be interesting to discover how far the analogy goes.

Before explaining our results in more detail, we give a brief overview of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits.

1.1
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits

The
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit model consists of constant-depth quantum circuits with arbitrary single-qubit gates and arbitrary-width Toffoli gates, which implement the unitary transformation

\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},b}\mapsto\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},b\oplus\bigwedge_{i}x_{i}}.

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
and related models were first introduced by Moore
[
Moo99
]
to explore natural quantum analogues of classical circuit classes such as
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
,
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
, and
\mathsf{AC}^{0}[q]
, which are well-studied models of shallow circuits in classical complexity theory.

Aside from being a natural generalization of a classical circuit model,
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
also gives a clean theoretical model with which to study the power of many-qubit operations in quantum computations. Recently there has been increasing motivation for understanding the power of short-depth quantum computations with many-qubit or non-local operations. Some experimental platforms are beginning to realize controllable operations that can affect many qubits at once; examples include analog simulators
[
BLS+22
]
, ion traps
[
GKH+21
,
GDC+22
]
, and superconducting qubit platforms that have mid-circuit measurements
[
RRG+22
]
.

Parity versus
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
.

Already in his 1999 paper
[
Moo99
]
, Moore posed the question of whether the
n
-bit parity function can be computed in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. Recall that computing parity is equivalent to computing fanout (i.e. the unitary operation

\ket{b,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\mapsto\ket{b,x_{1}\oplus b,\ldots,x_{n}\oplus b}
) up to conjugation by Hadamard gates
[
Moo99
]
. Consequently if
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits could compute parity, then this would imply that
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
would be remarkably powerful: among other things, they would be capable of generating GHZ states, computing the
\mathsf{MOD}_{q}
function for all
q
, computing the parity function, and performing phase estimation and approximate quantum Fourier transforms
[
Moo99
,
BGH07
]
.

Despite being open for more than twenty years, this question has seen limited progress. Fang et al.
[
FFG+03
]
showed that
\mathsf{QAC}
circuits with sublinear auxiliary qubits cannot compute parity, and more recently, Padé et al.
[
PFGT20
]
showed that depth-
2

\mathsf{QAC}
circuits with arbitrary auxiliary qubits cannot compute parity. Rosenthal
[
Ros21
]
proved that parity can be approximated exponentially well by
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits that have exponentially many auxiliary qubits (it was not known before whether
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits of
any
size could approximate parity). Rosenthal also proved that certain classes of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits require exponential size to approximate parity; however, extending these lower bounds to general
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits seems challenging. (See
Section

1.4
for more details about these prior works.)

Although lower bounds against classical
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
are considered one of the great successes of complexity theory
[
Yao85
,
Hås86
,
Raz87
,
Smo87
]
, it is far from clear how to extend those techniques (such as switching lemmas) to the setting of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. Furthermore, it is unclear whether
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
lower bounds imply
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
lower bounds: we do not know if
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits can even simulate classical
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits. Even though
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits appear quite weak (especially for classical computation), lower bounds have been difficult to obtain.

Going Beyond Lightcones.

On the other hand, if we restrict ourselves to constant-depth quantum circuits with only two-qubit gates (known as
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
circuits), it is comparatively much easier to prove limitations. For example, such circuits cannot prepare states with long-range entanglement (like the many-qubit GHZ state or states with topological order)
[
EH17
,
VTV21
,
ABN23
]
. At the heart of all
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
lower bound techniques, is the “lightcone argument”— the observation that each output qubit can only be affected by a small number of input qubits since there are only a few layers of small gates (see
Figure

2
). This argument immediately breaks when either the circuit has logarithmic depth, or large many-qubit gates. Thus, any effort to prove lower bounds against more general circuits calls for novel techniques
beyond lightcones
.
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits are at the frontier of this boundary and thus an attractive point of attack for developing new circuit lower bound techniques.

1.3.1
The Work of Linial, Mansour, and Nisan (LMN)

Suppose
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a Boolean function computed by a depth-
d

\mathsf{AC}^{0}

which can be viewed as a Fourier expansion of

. The main technical result of
[
LMN93
]

\sum_{|S|>k}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\leq{s\cdot 2^{-\Theta{\left(k^{1/d}\right)}}}\qquad\text{for all}~k\in[n],

(2)

which, intuitively, states that most “Fourier mass” of

lies on its low-degree part. Although this bound has been subsequently sharpened
[
Bop97
,
Hås01
,
IMP12
,
Hås14
,
Tal17
]

[
LMN93
]

Equation

2
.

The primary technical ingredient used by
[
LMN93
]
to prove the above bound is Håstad’s celebrated switching lemma
[
Hås86
]

As an immediate consequence of

Correlation Bounds Against Parity.

Suppose

is computed by a depth-

{\left|\widehat{f}{\left([n]\right)}\right|}\leq s\cdot 2^{-\Theta{\left(n^{1/d}\right)}}.

1.3.2
Spectral Concentration of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Circuits

Inspired by the classical importance of low-degree Fourier concentration, i.e.

U=X^{\otimes n}
, unitaries implemented by
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

not
have Pauli weight that is low-degree concentrated. Instead, we turn to analyzing the Pauli spectrum of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

.

The Pauli Decomposition of Channels.

We define the
Pauli spectrum of channel
\mathcal{E}

Our main learning result is an algorithm for learning channels with Pauli weight that is low-degree concentrated.
Combining this with our low-degree concentration bound for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

1

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit and

5

5

By “learning a channel” we mean learning an approximation (in Frobenius distance) of its Choi representation.
using quasipolynomial, i.e.

A Quantum Low-Degree Algorithm.

Our algorithm is inspired by—and closely follows the structure of—the classical low-degree algorithm introduced by
[
LMN93
]
.

The first step of the learning algorithm is to estimate all low-degree Pauli coefficients (i.e. all
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for
|P|\leq\mathrm{poly}\log(n)
) to sufficiently high accuracy. We consider two different learning models:

•

Choi state samples:
In the first model we are given copies of the Choi state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}{\tr(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}})}
. We apply Classical Shadow Tomography
[
HKP20
]
to learn the Pauli coefficients.

•

Measurement queries:
In this model, the learner is allowed to query an input state
\rho
and observable
O
and is given the measurement outcome of
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
with respect to
O
. For this setting, we perform direct tomography on the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
for specially chosen input states
\rho
and measurement observables
O
. This model was concurrently explored in the context of arbitary quantum processes, under the name “Quantum Process Statistical Queries (QPSQ)” model
[
WD24
]
.

While our approaches for both settings achieve similar sample complexity, the latter has the benefit of being more feasible (from an experimental implementation standpoint) than the former. Furthermore, the first approach can be thought of as analogous to learning from random labeled examples, and the second can be thought of as learning from query access to the function.

“Rounding” to CPTP maps.

After obtaining estimates
\widetilde{\Phi}(P)
for each of the Pauli coefficients
\widehat{\Phi}(P)
, the final step is to round

\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{P}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathcal{E}}(P)P

to a Choi representation of a valid quantum channel— that is, a completely-positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map.
Since the set of all CPTP Choi representations is a convex set, we show that there exists a projection onto this set that will only reduce the distance of our learned state to the true Choi representation. However, it remains open whether there exists an algorithm to implement an exact rounding procedure in quasipolynomial time.
Note that this final step is analogous to the classical low-degree algorithm’s use of the sign function, as in
Equation

3
.

Previous work on Pauli analysis.

The original proposal for “quantum analysis of Boolean functions” came from
[
MO08
]
, who proposed the study of Pauli decompositions (i.e. “Pauli analysis”) of
Hermitian unitaries
. The key intuition is that these unitaries possess
\pm 1
eigenvalues, which can be interpreted as the outputs of a classical Boolean function.
Recently,
[
RWZ22
]
extended seminal results from classical analysis of Boolean functions to this quantum setting. Furthermore,
[
CNY23
]
extended these Pauli analysis techniques to the setting of quantum
non-Hermitian unitaries
for applications to quantum junta testing and learning.
Unfortunately, however, as illustrated earlier in this section, the Pauli spectrum of a unitary
U
does not cleanly connect with the complexity of implementing
U
. In this work, we instead look to the Pauli decomposition of
channels
.

Recently,
[
BY23
]
also look to Pauli analysis of quantum channels for junta property testing, extending the work of
[
CNY23
]
to unitary channels. They, too, propose Fourier analysis of superoperators in the Choi representation, though they utilize a different Fourier basis than is used in this work. Bao and Yao decompose channels into their
Kraus representation
with Pauli Kraus operators. While this is a natural choice of basis for junta channels, it is not compatible with analyzing more general channels, or capturing circuit complexity. Notably, their analysis is limited to
n
to
n
-qubit channels. In this work, we also consider Pauli analysis of superoperators. However, we instead focus solely on the
Choi representation
, defining our Fourier spectrum in terms of the linear expansion of the Choi matrix itself into Pauli operators. By defining our Fourier basis in this way, we are able to analyze channels with an arbitrary number of output qubits. As discussed earlier in the section, this provides a definition of Pauli spectrum that connects more closely with computational complexity and allows us to prove spectral concentration, average-case lower bounds, and learning results for single-output-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits.

Previous work on
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

Since Moore’s paper
[
Moo99
]
that originally defined the model, there has only been a smattering of lower bound results on
\mathsf{QAC}
. We summarize known lower bounds against
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

Fang, et al.
[
FFG+03
]
established the first lower bounds on the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
model; in particular they proved that a depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}
circuit cleanly computes the
n
-bit parity function with
a
auxiliary qubits, then
d\geq 2\log(n/(a+1))
. Here, “cleanly” means that the auxiliary qubits have to start and end in the zero state.

The key to their lower bound proof is a beautiful lemma (Lemma 4.2 of
[
FFG+03
]
): for all depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}
circuits, there exists a subset
S
of
(a+1)2^{d/2}
input qubits and a state
\ket{\psi_{S}}
for that subset
S
, such that no matter what the other input qubits are set to, the output and auxiliary qubits result in the zero state. This immediately implies a lower bound for
\mathsf{QAC}
circuits that cleanly compute the parity function: First, the clean computation property implies that without loss of generality the subset
S
is supported on non-auxiliary qubits. Second, if
d<2\log(n/(a+1))
, then there exists a non-auxiliary input qubit
i
that is not fixed by
\ket{\psi_{S}}
, but the output qubit should depend on the state of the
i
’th qubit – except the output is already fixed to zero, a contradiction.

This lower bound is nontrivial as long as the number of auxiliary qubits is sublinear (i.e.
a=o(n)
), whereas our lower bound on the parity function can only handle up to
\sim n^{1/d}
auxiliary qubits. On the other hand, the lower bound of
[
FFG+03
]
appears to be tailored to the setting where the circuit has to compute parity both exactly and cleanly. For a circuit that computes parity exactly (i.e. on all input strings), the clean computation property is without loss of generality because one can always save the output and then uncompute. When the circuit only computes parity approximately (e.g. on
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
fraction of inputs), the clean computation property becomes an additional assumption.

Furthermore, the technique of
[
FFG+03
]
does not obviously extend to obtain average-case lower bounds: although there may be a fixing
\ket{\psi_{S}}
of
(a+1)2^{d/2}
input qubits that force the output qubit to be zero, such a fixing occurs with probability at most
2^{-(a+1)2^{d/2}}
under the uniform distribution on the
n
input qubits – note that this is
exponentially
small in
a
and
doubly-exponentially
small in
d
. This directly implies that a depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}
circuit with
a

1-2^{-(a+1)2^{d/2}}
fraction of inputs. When
a=\omega(\log n)
this fraction is extremely close to
1
. By comparison our average case lower bound shows that
\mathsf{QAC}
circuits with limited auxiliary qubits cannot compute parity on more than
\frac{1}{2}+2^{-\Omega(n^{1/d})}
fraction of inputs.

We note

However, for the same reasons as mentioned in the previous paragraph, it is unclear whether this argument can be extended to prove an average-case lower bound.

Related work on quantum learning.

Efficient learning of quantum dynamics is a long-standing challenge in the field.
Techniques such as quantum process tomography
[
MRL08
]
, which aim to fully characterize arbitrary quantum channels, require exponentially many data samples to guarantee a small error in the learned channel, for all possible channels.

One way of achieving sample-efficient quantum channel learning algorithms is performing full tomography on
specific classes
of quantum channels. By focusing on a specific class, rather than all possible quantum channels, there often exists nice structure which can be leveraged to reduce the number of data samples required to fully characterize channels in the class.
For example,
[
BY23
]
showed that
n
-qubit to
n
-qubit quantum
k
-junta channels (acting non-trivially on at most
k
out of
n
qubits) can be learned to error
\epsilon
, with high probability, via
O(4^{k}/\epsilon^{2})
samples. In our learning result, we focus on quantum channels with an arbitrary number of output qubits, and with ‘‘low-degree” Choi representations
7
7

7

We formally define the notion of a “low-degree” Choi representation in
Section

4
.
. We show that a
k
-degree channel, involving
m=n+\ell
total input and output qubits, can be learned to error
\epsilon
, with high probability, via
\widetilde{O}((3m)^{k}/(4^{\ell}\epsilon))
samples. Our result is incomparable to
[
BY23
]
since an
n
-qubit to
n
-qubit
k
-junta channel does not satisfy our notion of low-degree concentration. To our knowledge, this is the first work to analyze and offer a learning algorithm specific to channels with low-degree Choi representations. Furthermore, through our concentration result, we establish that
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits mapping to a single-qubit output (
\ell=1
) lie in this circuit class, resulting in the first quasipolynomial learning algorithm for single-qubit-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
.

An alternative approach for achieving sample-efficient learning algorithms is performing
partial
tomography on arbitrary quantum channels. For example, rather than full process tomography of a channel
\mathcal{E}
,
[
HCP22
]
consider the task of learning the function
f(O_{i},\rho)=\mbox{\rm Tr}(O_{i}\mathcal{E}(\rho))
for a class of
M
observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and input state
\rho
. For an arbitrary quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
and bounded-degree observables of spectral norm
\|O_{i}\|\leq 1
, they prove that
2^{O(\log(1/\epsilon)\log(n))}
samples are sufficient to learn the function for all observables to error
\epsilon
with high probability.
At a high level, our result and that of
[
HCP22
]
both establish and leverage Fourier concentration (i.e. low-degree approximation) to obtain efficient learning algorithms for quantum channels.
However, our results operate in different settings. Namely, our work learns a low-degree approximation of the channel’s Choi representation, whereas theirs learns low-degree approximations of the channel’s Heisenberg-evolved observables
O_{i}^{*}=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(O_{i})
, where
f(O_{i},\rho)=\mbox{\rm Tr}(O_{i}^{*}\rho)
.
[
HCP22
]
show that under a locally-flat input distribution, the Heisenberg-evolved observables of general channels are well approximated by low-degree observables. While this enables efficient learning of
any
quantum channel, restriction to locally-flat input distributions implies that, for quantum channels encoding classical Boolean functions, measurement expectations will be biased towards inputs which are not in the computational basis and, thus, uninformative. Our work instead obtains a sample-efficient learning result for the specific class of Choi representations of single-output-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits, with average-case guarantees according to the uniform distribution over computational basis states.
To obtain this result, we prove low-degree concentration of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Choi representations. This concentration result can further be shown to imply concentration of the channel’s Heisenberg-evolved observables
and, thus, could potentially be leveraged by the
[
HCP22
]
procedure to offer a learning guarantee for single-qubit-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channels without restriction to locally-flat input distributions. It is an interesting direction of future research to formally relate the two works.

Finally, sample-efficient quantum channel
learning algorithms can also be achieved by leveraging
quantum-enhanced
experiments.
[
CCHL22
]
proved exponential separations between learning algorithms with external quantum memory and those without. Building upon this,
[
Car23
]
recently demonstrated that full characterization of an unknown quantum channel’s Pauli transfer matrix requires exponentially many channel queries in the case of classical processing and memories, but only polynomial samples in the case of quantum processing and memory.
In this work, however, we do not consider quantum-enhanced experiments. Instead, we demonstrate that there exists a non-quantum-enhanced quasipolynomial learning algorithm for approximate characterization of the full Choi representation of single-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channels.

1.5
Discussion

We believe that much remains to be discovered about the analytic properties of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits. We list some natural concrete (and not-so-concrete) questions for future work below:

Improved Spectral Concentration?

Arguably the most natural open question is to improve the dependence on the number of auxiliary qubits in
Theorem

1
so as to get a lower bound against
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with polynomially many auxiliary qubits. In fact, we conjecture the following improved spectral bound:

Conjecture 1

(Spectral concentration for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}
is an
n
to
1
-qubit quantum channel that is implemented by a depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit on
n
input qubits and
\mathrm{poly}(n)
auxiliary qubits with
s
Toffoli gates. Then for all
k\in[n+1]
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\end{subarray}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\
|P|>k}{\left|\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(P)\right|}^{2}\leq\mathrm{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega{\left(k^{1/d}\right)}}

In particular, we expect no dependence on the number of auxiliary qubits in our spectral bound.
Note that
1
would immediately imply an average-case lower bound for parity as well as a lower bound for majority against
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with polynomially many auxiliary qubits, and extend the guarantees of our learning algorithm to this broader class of circuits. This would also match the classical bound on the Fourier spectrum of
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits obtained by
[
LMN93
]
.

Improved Learning Algorithms?

Another natural direction is to improve the runtime of our learning algorithm (see
Theorem

3
): while we obtain quasipolynomial sample complexity, we do not provide an explicit algorithm for the final Choi representation rounding step. We conjecture that there exists a quasipolynomial time algorithm implementing an exact rounding procedure, which would also achieve a quasipolynomial runtime for the procedure. Recall that the runtime of the
[
LMN93
]
learning algorithm is (under a strong enough cryptographic assumption) known to be optimal
[
Kha93
]
.

Connections to State Synthesis Problems?

Recent work of Rosenthal
[
Ros21
]
relates the problem of computing parity to various state synthesis problems. Could analytic methods as employed in this paper be used to prove state (or unitary) synthesis lower bounds?

Connections to Pseudorandomness?

Classically, circuit lower bounds have led to unconditional constructions of pseudorandom generators
[
NW94
]
. One could ambitiously hope for unconditional constructions of pseudorandom states against classes of shallow quantum circuits via circuit lower bounds.

An Emerging Analogy?

This work adds to an emerging analogy between Fourier analysis in the classical setting of Boolean functions and “Pauli analysis” in the quantum setting of unitary operators or more generally quantum channels
[
MO08
,
CNY23
,
RWZ22
,
BY23
,
VZ23
]
. Given the tremendous success of Fourier analysis in classical complexity theory, we suspect that much remains to be discovered about the Pauli spectrum of quantum operations.

{\left\langle A,B\right\rangle}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mbox{\rm Tr}(A^{\dagger}B).

Recall that the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product induces the Hilbert–Schmidt or Frobenius norm, which is given by

\|A\|_{F}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left\langle A,A\right\rangle}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|A_{i,j}|^{2}.

We will frequently make use of the fact that the Frobenius norm is invariant under multiplication by unitaries, i.e.

\|A\|_{F}=\|UA\|_{F}
for all unitary operators
U
.

It is a standard fact that the set of
2\times 2
Pauli operators, given by

\displaystyle I\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}_{N}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. By taking (normalized)
n
-fold tensors of the Pauli operators one obtains an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}_{N}
where
N=2^{n}
. In particular, we have that

\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left\{I,X,Y,Z\right\}}^{\otimes n}\quad

(6)

forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}_{N}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. It follows that every
M\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
has a unique representation as

A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\widehat{A}(P)P\qquad\text{where}\qquad\widehat{A}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{N}{\left\langle A,P\right\rangle}.

We will call
\widehat{A}(P)
the
Pauli coefficient
of
A
on
P
and will refer to the collection of Pauli coefficients
\{\widehat{A}(P)\}_{P}
as the
Pauli spectrum
of
A
.

Notation 5
.

Given a matrix
M\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
, we will write

\|\widehat{M}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\widehat{M}(P)|^{2}.

It is easy to see that
Parseval’s formula
holds for the Pauli decomposition: Given
A\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\|A\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}{\left\langle A,A\right\rangle}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\widehat{A}(P)|^{2}=\|\widehat{A}\|_{2}^{2}.

(7)

In the case that
U\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
is a unitary, then
\frac{1}{N}\|U\|_{F}^{2}=\|\widehat{U}\|_{2}^{2}=1
. More generally, we have
Plancherel’s formula
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}{\left\langle A,B\right\rangle}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\widehat{A}(P)^{\ast}\cdot\widehat{B}(P).

(8)

For a single-qubit Pauli operator
Q\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
, we write

Q_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=I^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes Q\otimes I^{\otimes(n-i)},

and more generally for a subset
S\subseteq[n]
and a single-qubit Pauli operator
Q\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
, we define

Q_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Q^{\mathbbm{1}{\left\{i\in S\right\}}}

(9)

with the convention that
Q^{0}\equiv I
.

For an
n
-qubit Pauli operator
P=Q_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes Q_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
as in
Equation

6
, we define

\mathrm{supp}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Q_{i}\neq I\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|P|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|\mathrm{supp}(P)|.

We will call
|P|
the
degree
of
P
; note that it is the number of qubits that
P
acts non-trivially on.
Finally, we borrow the classical notion of
spectral weight
, a well-studied quantity from Fourier analysis of Boolean functions:

Definition 6
.

For
M\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
, we define the
Pauli weight of
M
at level
k
as

\mathbf{W}^{=k}[M]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|P|=k\end{subarray}}|\widehat{M}(P)|^{2}.

We similarly define
\mathbf{W}^{\leq k}[M]
,
\mathbf{W}^{>k}[M]
, and the like.

The Fourier spectrum and spectral weight distribution of a Boolean function are intimately connected to various combinatorial as well as complexity-theoretic aspects thereof (e.g. edge boundaries, learnability, and noise stability); we refer the interested reader to the monographs
[
O’D14
,
GS14
]
for further background.

Note that
\sum_{P}|\widehat{M}(P)|^{2}
is not in general equal to
1
, as it is in the Boolean function analogue. For the special case where
U
is unitary, the sum is indeed equal to
1
.

2.2
Quantum Channels and the Choi Representation

In this paper, we use the Pauli decomposition to analyze quantum
channels
. Recall that a
quantum channel

\mathcal{E}
from
n
to
\ell
qubits is a completely-positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map from
\mathcal{M}_{N}
to
\mathcal{M}_{L}
— throughout this paper we maintain the convention that
n
denotes the number of input qubits,
\ell
is the number of output qubits,
m=n+\ell
and
N=2^{n},L=2^{\ell},M=2^{m}
. As clearly delineated in
[
Wat18
]
, there are various representations of linear maps — this paper will make use of the
Choi representation
.

Definition 7

(Choi representation, Choi state)
.

Given a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
mapping
n
qubits to
\ell
qubits, we define its
Choi representation
, written
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{M}_{M}
, as follows:

\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={(I^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{E})(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}})},

where
\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\otimes\ket{x}
is the
un-normalized

n
-qubit Bell state.
Furthermore, the
Choi state
is defined as

\rho_{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}{\tr(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}})}=\frac{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}{N}.

We designate the first
n
qubits as the register “
\mathrm{in}
”, and the remaining
\ell
qubits as “
\mathrm{out}
”.

For a particular input state
\rho
to the channel
\mathcal{E}
, we can determine
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
from the Choi representation
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}
using the following
Section
which can be easily verified.

Fact 8
.

For each
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
,
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\tr_{\mathrm{in}}{\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}{\left(\rho^{\top}\otimes I^{\otimes n}\right)}\right)}

We often use the
normalized Frobenius norm

\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert}_{F}
of a Choi representation
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{M}_{M}
. The following
Section
and
Proposition
highlight this convenience.

Fact 9
.

For each channel
\mathcal{E}
from
n
to
\ell
qubits,

\frac{1}{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq 1

Proof.

In
[
VDH02
]
, van Dam and Hayden show that Rényi entropy is weakly subadditive. For Rényi entropy of order 2 this means that for quantum state
\rho
on two subsystems
A,B
the following is true

\displaystyle S_{2}(\rho_{A})-S_{0}(\rho_{B})\leq S_{2}(\rho_{AB})\leq S_{2}(\rho_{A})+S_{0}(\rho_{B}).

Where
S_{2}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\log{\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\right)}
is Rényi entropy of order 2 and
S_{0}
is
max-entropy
:
S_{0}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\log\mathrm{rank}(\rho)
. Applying a logarithm this immediately gives us

\displaystyle\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{A}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}}{\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{B})}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{AB}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{A}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\cdot\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{B}).

(10)

Now let
\rho=\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
, and let
A
be the “
\mathrm{in}
” register (the first
n
qubits), and let
B
be the “
\mathrm{out}
” register (remaining
\ell
qubits). Since
\rho_{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{L}
we have that
\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{B})\leq L
.
Furthermore, since
\mathcal{E}
is trace preserving, we have that
\rho_{A}=\tr_{\mathrm{out}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{N}I_{N}
, and so
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{A}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}
. By
Equation

10
we have that

\frac{1}{NL}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{L}{N}
.
Since
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}=N\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq 1

Applying Parseval’s equation (
Equation

7
) this proves the
Section
.
∎

Indeed when
\ell\leq n
these bounds on the squared normalized Frobenius norm
\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}
are tight — it can be easily verified that the lower bound in
11
is achieved by
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\frac{1}{L}I_{L}
and the upper bound is achieved by the channel
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\tr_{\{\ell+1,\dots,n\}}(\rho)
.

The normalized Frobenius distance between Choi representations
\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right\rVert}_{F}
provides a notion of average-case distance between channels in the following sense.

Proposition 10
.

For channels
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
mapping
n
to
\ell
qubits, and for orthonormal Basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we have that

\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(\psi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq L\cdot\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}

with equality when
\mathcal{E}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=\mathcal{F}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=0
for all
\ket{\psi}\neq\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}
.

In words, the condition that
\mathcal{E}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=\mathcal{F}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=0
for all
\ket{\psi}\neq\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}
can be stated as that the channel
\mathcal{E}
is equivalent to the channel where we first measure in basis
\mathcal{B}
and then apply
\mathcal{E}
(likewise for
\mathcal{F}
).

Proof.

Using the definition of the Frobenius norm, and the fact that
\mathcal{B}
forms an orthonormal basis, it is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(\psi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\psi^{*}\otimes(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}.

(11)

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the channel that measures an
n
-qubit state in basis
\mathcal{B}^{*}=\{\ket{\psi}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\psi^{*}\otimes(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(\psi)=\sum_{\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{N}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|^{*})\otimes(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)

(12)

Recall that for any orthonormal Basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we can write the
2n
qubit (unnormalized) Bell state as
\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}=\sum_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\ket{\psi}^{*}\otimes\ket{\psi}

So we can rewrite the Choi representation as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}=\sum_{\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|^{*}\otimes(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)

(13)

Combining
Equations

11
,
12
and
13
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(\psi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(\mathcal{N}\otimes I_{L}){\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right)}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}.

Note that
\mathcal{N}
does not increase the Frobenius norm.

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{N}(A)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\sum_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}{\left|\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}\right|}^{2}\leq\sum_{\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}{\left|\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\right|}^{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}.

Therefore

\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(\psi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=L\cdot\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2},

completing the proof of the inequality. Note that when
\mathcal{E}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=\mathcal{F}(|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi^{\prime}}|)=0
for all
\ket{\psi}\neq\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}
, applying the channel
\mathcal{N}
to the first register of
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}
has no affect, so we get an equality.
∎

2.3
Pauli Spectrum of Quantum Channels

As shown in
Section

2.1
, we can write the Choi representation for channel
\mathcal{E}
in terms of its Pauli decomposition.

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\widehat{\Phi}(P)P.

We refer to the collection of coefficients
\{\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(P)\}
as the
Pauli spectrum of
\mathcal{E}
. Furthermore, the level (or degree)
k
Pauli weight of the channel
\mathcal{E}
refers to the level
k
weight of the Choi representation
\mathbf{W}^{=k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}]=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}|\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(P)|^{2}
. We say that the channel
\mathcal{E}
is
\epsilon
-concentrated up to degree
k
if
\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}]\leq\epsilon
.

An important dissemblance between Fourier weight of Boolean functions, and Pauli weight of channels, is that for any Boolean function
f
, the total weight is
always
1:
\sum_{k}\mathbf{W}^{=k}[f]=1
. Whereas this is not generally true for quantum channels. As we see by applying Parsevals (
Equation

7
) to
9
, the total weight is still at most
1
but can be as small as
\frac{1}{L^{2}}
.

Fact 11

(Corollary of
9
)
.

For each channel
\mathcal{E}
from
n
to
\ell
qubits,

\frac{1}{L^{2}}\leq\sum_{P}|\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(P)|^{2}\leq 1
.

In
Section

3
, we will see that channels computed by certain circuit classes have vanishing weight at higher degrees. Throughout the paper we focus on interesting properties of the Pauli weight
\mathbf{W}^{k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}]
at level
k
—
11
shows that this is related to the fraction of overall weight by a multiplicative factor between
1
and
L^{2}
.

2.4
Single-Output Channels.

We will primarily be interested in
n
to
1
-qubit quantum channels that are implemented by applying an
(n+a)
-qubit unitary
U
to the input
n
-qubit quantum state—together with
a
-qubit auxiliary state
\psi
—and outputting a single “target” qubit by tracing out the rest of the qubits as shown in
Figure

3
. To denote such channels, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 12

(
\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}
)
.

Let
U\in\mathcal{M}_{NA}
be a unitary acting on
n+a
qubits, and let
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{A}
be an
a
-qubit quantum state. We define
\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}
to be the following channel mapping
n
qubits to
1
qubit:

\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tr_{{\mathrm{out}}^{c}}{\left(U(\rho\otimes\psi)\,U^{\dagger}\right)}.

Where
\mathrm{out}=\{n+a\}
is the final qubit output by the circuit as in
Figure

3
. Its corresponding Choi representation is denoted

\Phi_{U,\psi}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left(I^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}\right)}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\right)}
, with registers labeled as shown in
Figure

4
.
In the setting without auxiliary states, when
a=0
, we denote
\mathcal{E}_{U}(\rho)
and
\Phi_{U}
for the corresponding channel and its Choi representation.

Figures

3
and
4

Definition 14

The
controlled phase-flip unitary
on

\textsf{CZ}_{k}\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}=(-1)^{x_{1}\cdots x_{k}}\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}

\textsf{CZ}_{S}\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=I\ket{x_{i}}_{i\notin S}\otimes\textsf{CZ}_{k}\ket{x_{i}}_{i\in S}.

Remark 17

as well as the number of auxiliary qubits. The proof proceeds via a straightforward lightcone argument (cf.

Proof.

Let
C
be the circuit as described in

22

as a directed graph where each edge is oriented along the direction of computation. Let
L
be the sub-circuit of
C
induced by all directed paths connecting the input wires of
C
with the target qubit (which, by convention, is the last qubit).

Let

be the unitary that
L
implements and let
V
be the unitary corresponding to the rest of the circuit. By definition, we have

C=VU_{L}.

Since

acts trivially on the target qubit, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C,\psi}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{in}}{\left({\left(VU_{L}\right)}\,(\rho\otimes\psi)\,{\left(VU_{L}\right)}^{\dagger}\right)}=\tr_{\mathrm{in}}{\left(U_{L}(\rho\otimes\psi)\,U_{L}^{\dagger}\right)}=\mathcal{E}_{U_{L},\psi}(\rho).

Since
C

\mathcal{E}_{C,\psi}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{k}{\left(\tr_{\{1,\dots,n-k\}}(\rho)\right)}
for some

. And

\displaystyle\Phi_{C,\psi}=I^{\otimes(n-k)}\otimes\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{k}}.

Since
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{k}}\in\mathcal{M}_{2^{k+1}}
, it follows that
\Phi_{C,\psi}
has Pauli degree at most
k+1=2^{d}+1
.
∎

Lemma

Lemma 23

Suppose

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\widehat{\Phi_{C}}-\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{C}}}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{m^{2}}{2^{\ell}}\right)

(16)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{>k}{\left[\Phi_{C}\right]}\leq\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{W}^{>k}{\left[\Phi_{\widetilde{C}}\right]}}+O\left(\sqrt{m^{2}/2^{\ell}}\right)\right)^{2}

Before proving

22

Proof of

Let

. Let

be the circuit resulting from removing all

\mathbf{W}^{>k}{\left[\Phi_{\widetilde{C}}\right]}\leq\mathbf{W}^{>(k-1)^{1/d}}{\left[\Phi_{\widetilde{C}}\right]}=0.

We now turn to the proof of

Proof of

Let

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}

Claim 24
.

We have
\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq 4m^{2}/2^{\ell}
.

Proof.

Let
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\subseteq[n]
denote the subsets of qubits that each of the
m

U

U=U_{0}\,\textsf{CZ}_{S_{1}}\,U_{1}\,\textsf{CZ}_{S_{2}}\dots\textsf{CZ}_{S_{m}}\,U_{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{U}=U_{0}\,U_{1}\dots U_{m},

where
U_{0},\dots,U_{m}
are
n
-qubit unitaries.
Let
\widetilde{U}^{(a)}
be the unitary resulting from removing the first

\widetilde{U}^{(a)}=U_{0}\,U_{1}\,\dots U_{a}\,\textsf{CZ}_{S_{a+1}}\,U_{a+1}\,\dots\textsf{CZ}_{S_{m}}\,U_{m}.

Note that
\widetilde{U}^{(0)}=U
and
\widetilde{U}^{(m)}=\widetilde{U}
. By the triangle inequality, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum_{a=1}^{m}\widetilde{U}^{(a)}-\widetilde{U}^{(a-1)}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq\sum_{a=1}^{m}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\widetilde{U}^{(a)}-\widetilde{U}^{(a-1)}\right\rVert}_{F}.

(18)

Note, however, that due to the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\widetilde{U}^{(a)}-\widetilde{U}^{(a-1)}\right\rVert}_{F}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert I-\textsf{CZ}_{S_{a}}\right\rVert}_{F}.

Since

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\widetilde{U}^{(a)}-\widetilde{U}^{(a-1)}\right\rVert}_{F}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert 2\cdot I_{\overline{S}_{a}}\otimes\ket{1^{|S_{a}|}}\!\!\bra{1^{|S_{a}|}}_{S_{a}}\right\rVert}_{F}=2\cdot 2^{(n-|S_{a}|)/2}.

Combining this with

and recalling that

, we get that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a=1}^{m}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\widetilde{U}^{(a)}-\widetilde{U}^{(a-1)}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq 2m2^{(n-\ell)/2},

from which the bound follows.
∎

We next obtain an upper bound on

in terms of

.

Claim 25
.

We have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{U}-\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq{4\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}.

Recall from

that we can express

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{U}=(I\otimes U^{\top})\rho_{0}(I\otimes U^{*})

\displaystyle\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}=(I\otimes\widetilde{U}^{\top})\rho_{0}(I\otimes\widetilde{U}^{*})

\rho_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tr_{\mathrm{junk}}\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{U}-\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}\right\rVert}_{F}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(I\otimes U^{\top})\rho_{0}(I\otimes U^{*})-(I\otimes\widetilde{U}^{\top})\rho_{0}(I\otimes\widetilde{U}^{*})\right\rVert}_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(I\otimes U^{\top})\rho_{0}(I\otimes(U^{*}-\widetilde{U}^{*}))+(I\otimes(U^{\top}-\widetilde{U}^{\top}))\rho_{0}(I\otimes\widetilde{U}^{*})\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{0}\left(I\otimes(U^{*}-\widetilde{U}^{*})\right)\right\rVert}_{F}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{0}\left(I\otimes(U^{\top}-\widetilde{U}^{\top})\right)\right\rVert}_{F}

thanks to the triangle inequality, and so

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho_{0}\left(I\otimes(U-\widetilde{U})\right)\right\rVert}_{F}.

Note that

can be written—up to a permutation of qubits—as

I^{\otimes n-1}\otimes\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{1}}.

Furthermore, note that
\frac{I^{\otimes n-1}}{2}\otimes\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{1}}
is a projector,
9
9

9

The

\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{1}}=\ket{00}+\ket{11}
is un-normalized.
and since projectors do not increase Frobenius norm,
10
10

10

To see that projectors do not increase Frobenius norm, write

in orthonormal basis

with eigenvalues

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert AP\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\sum_{v}\bra{v}P^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}AP\ket{v}=\sum_{v}\lambda_{v}\bra{v}A^{\dagger}A\ket{v}\leq\tr(A^{\dagger}A)

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{U}-\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}\right\rVert}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathinner{\!\left\lVert I\otimes(U-\widetilde{U})\right\rVert}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}

where the last inequality used the sub-multiplicativity of the Frobenius norm and the fact that

Note that the first item in

(i.e.

Claim 26
.

Suppose
\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert U-\widetilde{U}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\delta^{2}

Using the triangle inequality, we have that for all

Consequently, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{>k}\left[\Phi_{U}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}^{>k}\left[\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}+\left(\Phi_{U}-\Phi_{\widetilde{U}}\right)\right]

3.2
Postselecting for Auxiliary States

In this section, we investigate how the spectral concentration of channels is affected when we allow them to make use of an
auxiliary state
(independent of the input). That is, channels of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}(\rho\otimes\psi)\qquad\text{for~}\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{N}.

(19)

where
\mathcal{E}
is an
(n+a)
to
1
-qubit channel and
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{2^{a}}
is an
auxiliary
state. It is helpful to consider auxiliary states as
restricting
the input of the underlying circuit. From this perspective, a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channel using an auxiliary state is equivalent to an auxiliary-free
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channel with restricted input.

In this section, we will first walk through a straightforward analysis of how the spectral concentration of the Choi representation is affected by either a clean or a dirty auxiliary state. We will then formalize these statements to general auxiliary states.

3.2.1
“Clean” Auxiliary States

Suppose
U
is an
(n+a)
-qubit unitary. We use the shorthand
\mathcal{E}_{U,0^{a}}
to denote
\mathcal{E}_{U,\ket{0^{a}}\!\!\bra{0^{a}}}
, or in other words

\mathcal{E}_{U,0^{a}}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathcal{E}_{U}(\rho\otimes\ket{0^{a}}\!\!\bra{0^{a}}).

We emphasize that now
\mathcal{E}_{U}
is a channel mapping
n+a
qubits to
1
qubit, and so its Choi representation
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{U}}
is an
(n+a+1)
-qubit (un-normalized) state. We label the input registers of
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{U}}
as “
\mathrm{in}
” (
n
qubits), and “
\mathrm{aux}
” (
a
qubits), and the output registers as
\mathrm{junk}
(
n+a-1
qubits) and “
\mathrm{out}
” (1 qubit), i.e.

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{U}}={\left((I^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes I^{\otimes a}_{\mathrm{aux}})\otimes(\mathcal{E}_{U})_{\mathrm{out}}\right)}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n+a}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n+a}}_{(\mathrm{in},\mathrm{aux}),\mathrm{out}}\right)}

3.2.2
“Dirty” Auxiliary States

In contrast with a
clean
auxiliary state that is always initialized to the
\ket{0^{a}}
state, we consider the case where the auxiliary state given to the circuit is
dirty
, that is there is no guarantee what the auxiliary state will be, yet the circuit still has the desired behavior. This corresponds to an auxiliary state that is maximally mixed (
\psi=\frac{1}{2^{a}}I^{\otimes a}
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}{\left(\rho\otimes\tfrac{1}{2^{a}}I^{\otimes a}\right)}.

Following a similar argument to that used for “clean” auxiliary states, one can show that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2^{a}}\cdot\tr_{\mathrm{aux}}{\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]\leq\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}],\quad\text{for each }k\geq 1.

(22)

As opposed to clean auxiliary states, dirty auxiliary states cannot blow up high-degree Pauli weight, and can be considered “free.”
Below we prove analogous statements for general auxiliary states of which both dirty and clean auxiliary states are a special case.

3.2.3
Arbitrary Auxiliary States

More generally, we consider channels that have access to arbitrary auxiliary states, that are not necessarily pure states. As we will see in the following
Proposition
, the Choi representation of such channels is also conveniently related to the auxiliary-free channel.

Proposition 28
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a quantum channel mapping
n+a
qubit states to single qubit states, and let
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{2^{a}}
be a quantum state. Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be the
n

\mathcal{E}
to
\psi

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}(\rho\otimes\psi)\qquad\text{for}~\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{N}.

We label the registers of
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}
, as
\mathrm{in}
(
n

\mathrm{out}
(1 qubit),
\mathrm{aux}
(

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}={\left(I^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes I^{\otimes a}_{\mathrm{aux}}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n+a}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n+a}}_{(\mathrm{in},\mathrm{aux}),\mathrm{out}}\right)}.

Then
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\tr_{\mathrm{aux}}{\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}(I^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes(\psi^{\top})_{\mathrm{aux}}\otimes I_{\mathrm{out}})\right)}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathrm{aux}}{\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}{\left(I^{\otimes n+1}_{A^{c}}\otimes\psi^{\top}_{\mathrm{aux}}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
y,y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{a}\end{subarray}}\tr_{\mathrm{aux}}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\otimes{\left(|{y}\rangle\langle{y^{\prime}}|\psi^{\top}\right)}_{\mathrm{aux}}\otimes\mathcal{E}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\otimes|{y}\rangle\langle{y^{\prime}}|\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
y,y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{a}\end{subarray}}\mbox{\rm Tr}{\left(|{y}\rangle\langle{y^{\prime}}|\psi^{\top}\right)}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\otimes\mathcal{E}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\otimes|{y}\rangle\langle{y^{\prime}}|\right)}\right)}

\mathcal{E}

∎

Next, we generalize

27
for arbitrary auxiliary quantum states. Upon restricting
a

Proposition 29
.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\psi

\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]\leq 2^{a}\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}]\text{ for each }k\in\{0,1,\dots n+1\}.

Proof.

As shown in
Proposition

28
, we have
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\tr_{\mathrm{aux}}{\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}(I^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes(\psi^{\top})_{\mathrm{aux}}\otimes I_{\mathrm{out}})\right)}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}}(P)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{a}}\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(P\otimes Q)\tr(Q\psi^{\top})

\displaystyle\text{for each }P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}.

And so,

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\leq{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\

3.3
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Lower Bounds for Parity and Majority

In this section, we consider Boolean functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and show that when viewing them as a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{f}
, their Pauli spectrum is essentially the same as their Fourier spectrum. Using this observation, we connect our low-degree concentration results from the previous section to prove correlation bounds against
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with limited auxiliary qubits for computing high-degree Boolean functions. In particular, we provide explicit bounds for
\mathrm{Parity}_{n}
and
\mathrm{Majority}_{n}

The following proposition relates the Fourier spectrum of a Boolean function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
to the Pauli spectrum of this function, viewed as a quantum channel as discussed above. We refer the reader to
[
O’D14
]
for further background.

Proposition 31
.

Suppose
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a Boolean function and
\rho
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{f}
be the channel mapping
n
-qubit input state
\rho
to a single-qubit state as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\bra{x}\rho\ket{x}\ket{f(x)}\!\!\bra{f(x)},

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\otimes\ket{f(x)}\!\!\bra{f(x)}.

(26)

Then we have

\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}=\frac{1}{2}{\left(I^{\otimes(n+1)}+\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)Z_{S}\otimes Z\right)}

where
Z_{S}
is defined in
Equation

9
and
\widehat{f}(S)=\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[(-1)^{f(x)}\chi_{S}(x)]

11
11

11

Recall from

that
\chi_{S}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}

Proof.

For convenience, we will write
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathcal{E}_{f}
and
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}
throughout the proof. Note that

\displaystyle I^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{E}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\otimes{\left(\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\braket{y|x}\!\!\braket{x|y}\ket{f(y)}\!\!\bra{f(y)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\otimes\ket{f(x)}\!\!\bra{f(x)}.

(27)

It is straightforward to check that the Pauli decomposition of
\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}
is given by

\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{x\cdot y}Z_{y}

\displaystyle I^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{E}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{x\cdot y}Z_{y}\right)}\otimes{\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{f(x)\cdot b}Z_{b}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
b\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}{{\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{x\cdot y+f(x)\cdot b}\right)}}Z_{y}\otimes Z_{b}.

{{\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{x\cdot y+f(x)\cdot b}}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}{\left\{y=0^{n}\right\}}&\text{if}~b=0\\
\widehat{f}(y)&\text{if}~b=1\end{cases}

where we once again identify

\displaystyle I^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal{E}{\left(\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{EPR}_{n}}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}{\left(I^{\otimes(n+1)}+\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)Z_{S}\otimes Z\right)}.

(28)

Now that we have shown the correspondence between the Pauli spectrum and the Fourier spectrum of
f
, we next bound the correlation between a quantum channel and a Boolean function entirely in terms of their Pauli and Fourier spectra respectively.

Proposition 32
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}
is an
n

For each

Then for each

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\left[Q_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=f(x)\right]}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}^{>k}[f]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{W}^{>k+1}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}]}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{E},f
be as in
Proposition

32
, and let
\mathcal{E}_{f}
be as defined in
Proposition

31
. We refer to the probability that

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}[Q_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=f(x)]
, as the probability of success. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
be the

\mathcal{E}
. That is,
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is the channel resulting from applying a computational basis measurement before and after applying
\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}{\left(\mathcal{E}{\left(\mathcal{M}_{n}(\rho)\right)}\right)}

\mathcal{M}_{k}

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[Q_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=f(x)\right]}=\tr{\left(\ket{f(x)}\!\!\bra{f(x)}\mathcal{E}{\left(\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right)}\right)}=\tr{\left(\mathcal{E}_{f}{\left(\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right)}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}{\left(\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right)}\right)}.

We now show that the overall probability of success can be written in terms of
{\left\langle\Phi_{f},\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[Q_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=f(x)\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\tr{\left(\mathcal{E}_{f}{\left(\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right)}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}{\left(\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\tr{\left(\mathcal{E}_{f}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\right)}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}{\left(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|\right)}\right)}

\mathcal{E}_{f}
is a classical channel — for each

\mathcal{E}_{f}(|{x}\rangle\langle{x^{\prime}}|)=0

By Plancharel’s (

\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}{\left\langle\Phi_{f},\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle\widehat{\Phi_{f}},\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}\right\rangle}

\Phi_{f},\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{x}{\left[Q_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=f(x)\right]}=2\cdot{\left\langle\widehat{\Phi_{f}},\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}\right\rangle}

(29)

Recall from

\widehat{\Phi_{f}}(Z_{S}\otimes Z)=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{f}(S)

\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}}(I)=\widehat{\Phi_{f}}(I)=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle{\left\langle\widehat{\Phi_{f}},\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}\right\rangle}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\widehat{\Phi_{f}}(P)^{*}\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}(P)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}(Z_{S}\otimes Z)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}-{\left|\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}}(Z_{S}\otimes Z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\
|S|\leq k\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\right)}^{1/2}+{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}^{\leq k}[f]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{W}^{>k+1}[\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}]}

For our primary discussion of the learning procedure, we will consider a learning model in which we are given access to many copies of Choi state

Indeed, the Choi state is a valid quantum state as it has

\mathcal{E}

With this background, we can now state the main result of the section:

Theorem 35

(Quantum Low-Degree Learning)
.

Let

\mathcal{E}
be an
n
- to
\ell
-qubit quantum channel that is
\epsilon/2
-concentrated up to Pauli degree
k

O{\left((3m)^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{4^{\ell}\epsilon}\cdot\log(m^{k}/\delta)\right)}
copies of the Choi state,
outputs a description of quantum channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
such that
\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

We remark that as stated, the sample complexity improves as the output dimension of the channel grows for a fixed approximation error
\epsilon
. Though, it is not quite true that our algorithm is “better” for larger
\ell
. Rather, the notion of approximation used gets weaker as the number of outputs
\ell
grows. If we instead consider the expected trace distance between the channels output for a random input chosen from orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}

\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(\psi)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{tr}}\leq\sqrt{L}\cdot E_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})(\psi)\right\rVert}_{F}

Proposition

10

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{E}(\psi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{tr}}^{2}\leq\frac{L^{2}}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{F}}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}.

(34)

So if our goal is to learn the expected trace distance squared up to error
\eta

\epsilon=\eta/L^{2}
giving sample complexity
\mathrm{polylog}(n^{k})\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log(1/\delta)

In the special case where

Corollary 36
.

For each

2\epsilon

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{x}[f(x)\neq h(x)]\leq 4\epsilon

Proof.

First note that

\rho_{f}

31

\epsilon/2

\frac{1}{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{f}-\widetilde{\Phi}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I+f(x)Z)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)Z(S)\otimes Z

4.3
Learning with Measurement Queries

Finally, in our proof of
Lemma

37
, we proposed an efficient procedure for approximately learning Pauli coefficients of the Choi representation of quantum channel. Despite the promising sample complexity of this approach, there is a notable physical implementation challenge. Namely, physical implementation would require producing a Choi state, necessitating the preparation and manipulation of a physical Bell state over
2m
-qubits. This, however, is very difficult in practice for large
m

In light of these challenges, we will now offer a second approach for learning the Choi representation coefficients which is more implementation-friendly. Rather than performing tomography on the Choi state, the physical implementation will amount to preparing
n
-qubit input states, applying the channel
\mathcal{E}
, and performing measurements corresponding to
\ell

n
-qubit Choi state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
. The relevant query model consists of

[
WD24
]
formalize this query model as the “Quantum Process Statistical Query (QPSQ)” model and study its power for learning arbitrary quantum processes.

Convex CPTP Projection

Leveraging the fact that

We begin by decomposing the error between

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\Phi}-\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\Phi}-\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}},\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}-\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rangle\geq 0,

Fact 42

\displaystyle\forall\>\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,\ell}\>\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\>\langle\nabla f(\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}),\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}\rangle=2\langle\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}-\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathcal{E}},\Phi_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}\rangle\geq 0,
