Abstract

This paper investigates the potential of non-terrestrial and terrestrial
signals of opportunity (SOOP)
for navigation applications. Non-terrestrial
SOOP
analysis employs
modified Cramér-Rao lower bound (MCRLB)
to establish a relationship between
SOOP
characteristics and the accuracy of ranging information. This approach evaluates hybrid navigation module performance without direct signal simulation. The
MCRLB
is computed for ranging accuracy, considering factors like propagation delay, frequency offset, phase offset, and
angle-of-arrival (AOA)
, across diverse non-terrestrial
SOOP
candidates. Additionally, Geometric Dilution of Precision (GDOP) and
low earth orbit (LEO)
SOOP availability are assessed. Validation involves comparing
MCRLB
predictions with actual ranging measurements obtained in a realistic simulated scenario. Furthermore, a qualitative evaluation examines terrestrial
SOOP
, considering signal availability, accuracy attainability, and infrastructure demands.

1
Introduction

Signals of opportunity (SOOP)
are signals that, despite being designed for communications, are opportunistically used by the receiver for navigation purposes as well.

SOOP
signals encompass both satellite and terrestrial sources. Among terrestrial sources are cellular, i.e., LTE and 5G/6G networks, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth, while satellite
SOOP
include
low earth orbit (LEO)
satellite constellations like Starlink and Orbcomm.
Indeed,
SOOP
may be exploited to extract additional ranging estimations when
global navigation satellite systems (GNSS)
signals are compromised or unavailable. In particular, terrestrial
SOOP
offer dense deployment and bandwidth advantages, particularly beneficial in indoor settings and urban areas, where the number of visible
GNSS
satellites is limited.
Moreover,
LEO
satellite constellations are of interest concerning the satellite
SOOP
as they provide a good decorrelation of measurement errors, potentially assisting
GNSS
when a hybridized
position, velocity, and time (PVT)
solution is computed. Also due to their limited cost with respect to
medium Earth orbit (MEO)

GNSS
satellites, thousands of
LEO
satellites orbit around the Earth, and each of these may represent a source of ranging.

Still, employing
SOOP
for positioning presents challenges, as these signals are not designed for navigation but serve various purposes. For instance, retrieving pseudoranges requires information such as exact transmission time and transmitter location, which may not be trivial to obtain. For instance, while knowledge of the location of stationary transmitters is typically easy to retrieve and often already known to the receiver (e.g., the base station location in 5G/6G) when transmitters are moving, such information is often inaccurate and hard to retrieve.
Moreover, most communication systems used as
SOOP
are not time-synchronized to a nanosecond accuracy (as
GNSS
are) and a time correction may not be available to the receiver. Other relevant issues are
non-line-of-sight (NLOS)
and multipath effects.

Various measurements can be drawn from
SOOP
, such as carrier phase, time-of-arrival,
angle-of-arrival (AOA)
, or Doppler frequency. These measurements are later processed to extract ranging information such as the pseudorange or pseudorange rate, and may be integrated with
GNSS
-ranging information in a hybrid navigation module.
Indeed mixing signals from diverse systems/networks, even if not originally intended for positioning, may represent a huge opportunity for navigation.

For instance, the use of terrestrial
SOOP
from 5G/6G cellular or Wi-Fi networks may aid navigation in urban canyon environments. Moreover,
SOOP
represent a valid resource in contexts where the signal navigation cannot be trusted, e.g., after the detection of jamming or spoofing attacks.
Thus, approaches relying on hybrid navigation solutions enhance
PVT
resilience, availability, and accuracy.

This paper explores the usage of non-terrestrial and terrestrial
SOOP
for navigation purposes. For non-terrestrial
SOOP
, we employ the
modified Cramér-Rao lower bound (MCRLB)
analysis to establish a connection between the characteristics of
SOOP
and the accuracy of the ranging information estimated from each signal. This approach allows us to assess the performance potential of a hybrid navigation module without direct signal simulation.
Specifically, we calculate the
MCRLB
for ranging accuracy, considering various measurements such as propagation delay, frequency offset, phase offset, and
AOA
, across different non-terrestrial
SOOP
candidates.
We then evaluate
geometric dilution of precision (GDOP)
and the availability of various
LEO

SOOP
.
Finally, to validate the derived bounds rigorously, we compare the
MCRLB
with actual ranging measurements obtained in a realistic simulated scenario.

Lastly, we provide a qualitative assessment of the positioning performance achievable by terrestrial
SOOP
, taking into account signal availability, achievable accuracy, and infrastructure requirements.

2
Methodology

To identify the best
SOOP
, either terrestrial or non-terrestrial, we consider two criteria: the positioning accuracy and the
SOOP
availability.

We analyze the achievable positioning accuracy of the
PVT
when using different systems. This can be characterized by considering

\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}^{2}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{p}-\hat{\bm{p}}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(1)

where
\bm{p}
and
\hat{\bm{p}}
are respectively the true and measured
PVT
results.

Moreover, as in
Reid et al., (2016)
, positioning accuracy can be approximated as

\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}\,\mathrm{GDOP}\,,

(2)

thus it is related to both the ranging accuracy, expressed by
\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}
, that is the
user equivalent ranging error (UERE)
variance, and the
GDOP
.
The former term pertains to the estimation accuracy of the distance between the transmitter and receiver, considering both signal processing aspects and signal characteristics. Conversely, the latter term,
\mathrm{GDOP}
, addresses the accuracy influenced by the spatial arrangement of satellites relative to the receiver, e.g., the number of visible satellites and their distribution.

Thus, given (
2
), the best
SOOP
will be ones minimizing
\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}
and
\mathrm{GDOP}
.

3
Limits on the Accuracy of the Estimation of Non/Terrestrial SOOP Observables

We consider a receiver acquiring signals from different
LEO
systems, and from each of them, it estimates the propagation delay
\tau
, the phase offset
\theta
, and the frequency offset
\upsilon
.
The objective of this analysis is to determine the best non-terrestrial
LEO
candidate by considering the ranging and ranging rate accuracy via
MCRLBs
, considering various measurements. In other terms,
MCRLBs
will provide the minimum variance of the estimator associated with each measurement and
LEO
system under consideration.

In detail, we will consider Iridium NEXT, OrbComm, OneWeb, and Starlink as
LEO
candidates.

3.1
Channel and Signal Model

The signal at the receiver in baseband can be decomposed into useful signal,
s(t)
, and
additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)
noise
w(t)
at the receiver, having zero mean and variance
N_{0}
, i.e.,

r(t)=s(t)+w(t)\>,

(3)

where
s(t)
is defined as

s(t)=e^{j(2\pi\upsilon t+\theta)}\sum_{i=1}{N}c_{i}g_{i}(t-iT-\tau)\>.

(4)

In detail,
g_{i}
describes the waveform of the
i
th pulse,
T
is the symbol duration, and
c_{i}
denotes the message symbols. In particular, we consider the information to be modulated in (eventually complex) amplitude (e.g.,
QAM
) or in phase (
PSK
).

We remark that the phase and frequency offsets are defined with respect to the receiver local oscillator, having instantaneous phase
\Phi_{\rm L}
and frequency
f_{\rm cL}
. Thus the frequency offset is

\upsilon=f_{\mathrm{c}}-f_{\rm cL}\,,

(5)

modeling, for instance, also the shift induced by the Doppler effect, while the phase shift term
\theta
is instead

\theta=\Phi_{\rm L}-2\pi f_{\mathrm{c}}\tau\>.

(6)

3.2
Modified Cramer Rao Lower Bounds for LEO Observables

The traditional
Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB)

Mengali and D’Andrea, (1997)
provides the estimator variance when all the parameters are known.
However, we aim at deriving estimates from
SOOP
data signals, where the modulated data is not known by the receiver.
Thus, we resort to the
MCRLB
of
D’Andrea et al., (1994)
, where for a measurement
\lambda
and a signal in baseband

\mathrm{MCRLB}(\lambda)\triangleq\frac{N_{0}/2}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}\left[\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\left|\frac{\partial s(t,\lambda,\textit{{u}})}{\partial\lambda}\right|^{2}\,dt\right]}\>,

(7)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}
represents the expectation taken with respect to the random components of the signal (e.g., data) and
N_{0}
the noise
power spectral density (PSD)
.
Indeed,

\mathrm{CRLB}(\lambda)\geq\mathrm{MCRLB}(\lambda)\,,

(8)

so the
CRLB
represents a more conservative version of the lower bound on variance, and the two values coincide if the unwanted parameters are known at the receiver or no unwanted parameters exist at all.

Considering the signal pattern in (
4
), we compute the
MCRLB
for the variance of
\tau
,
\upsilon
and
\theta
estimators

\mathrm{MCRLB}(\tau)=\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0}\xi\frac{C}{N_{0}}}{8\pi^{2}}\,,

(9)

\mathrm{MCRLB(\theta)}=\frac{1}{{T_{0}}\frac{C}{N_{0}}}2

(10)

\mathrm{MCRLB}(\upsilon)=\frac{3}{{T_{0}}^{3}\frac{C}{N_{0}}}{2\pi^{2}}

(11)

which include the
carrier to noise ratio (
C/N_{0}
)
(in dB-Hz), the observation time
T_{0}
.
\xi
denotes the
normalized mean square bandwith (NMSB)
, defined, for a signal with
PSD

G(f)
, as

\xi=T^{2}\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f^{2}\left|G(f)\right|^{2}df}{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|G(f)\right|^{2}df}\,.

(12)

A fourth method could involve the estimation of the
AOA
,
\beta
, of one of the tones using an array of
M
(synchronized) equally spaced antennas, with a spacing
d
. The maximum accuracy with which the angle of arrival can be estimated as
Kay, (1997)

\mathrm{MCRLB}(\beta)\geq\frac{12}{(2\pi)^{2}M\frac{C}{N_{0}}T_{0}\frac{M+1}{M-1}\left(\frac{Lf_{\mathrm{c}}}{c}\right)^{2}\mathrm{sin}^{2}\beta}\,,

(13)

which, with respect to the previously described
MCRLBs
also depends on
M
, the overall array length
L=d(M-1)
, and the
AOA
itself.

All the
MCRLBs
show that as the
C/N_{0}
and signal observation time increase, the estimator accuracy increases as well. Specifically, concerning the
\tau
estimation of (
9
), the lower bound also depends on the actual signal bandwidth. Thus, to compute the bounds, we need the exact signal parameters.

An important issue to be tacked is the estimation of the
C/N_{0}
. In
Ferre and Lohan, (2021)
, the authors report for systems
LEO
between 20 dB-Hz and 50 dB-Hz of
C/N_{0}
.
The maximum
C/N_{0}
can be estimated via the link budget formula reported in
Dey et al., (2023)
here written in its linear form

C/N_{0}=\mathrm{EIRP}\frac{G}{T_{\rm temp}}L_{P}\frac{1}{k}\,,

(14)

where
\mathrm{EIRP}
stands for the effective isotropic radiated power, which takes into account the amount of power radiated by the transmitter antenna and its radiation pattern,
G
is the gain of the receiver antenna,
T_{\mathrm{temp}}
represents the system temperature,
L_{\rm P}P
is the free-space path loss, which in turn is function of the altitude of the satellite and the carrier frequency of the signal, and
k
is the Boltzmann constant.

In particular for the path loss we considered the minimum distance over the ground receiver position, i.e., the altitude of the satellite (e.g., for Starlink
550\text{\,}\mathrm{km}
).
In the following, we describe each
LEO
signal parameter.

Starlink

Concerning Starlink, the structure of the signal has been reverse-engineered in
Humphreys et al., (2023)
. Starlink uses an
orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM)
of which we know the overall symbol duration, cyclic prefix duration, number of subcarriers as well as the number and amplitude of each channel. Analyses on the
C/N_{0}
have been provided in
Osoro and Oughton, (2021)
;
Aguilar et al., (2019)
;
del Portillo Barrios et al., (2019)
;
Xia et al., (2019)
;
Ferre et al., (2022)
provides an estimate of
42.6
dB-Hz for outdoor. On the other hand, this quantity turns out to be much lower than the one we derived through (
14
) and
Osoro and Oughton, (2021)
;
Aguilar et al., (2019)
;
del Portillo Barrios et al., (2019)
;
Xia et al., (2019)
.
We remark that according to
Humphreys et al., (2023)
, consecutive frames from the same satellite and the same beam may be transmitted with different (not specified) powers.
The duration of a frame is about 1.33 ms of which the last 5.33
\mu
s of silence, however, frames are not transmitted with perfect regularity (the frequency depends on the demand for data traffic) but at least one frame is always detected about every 40 ms (1 in 30)
Humphreys et al., (2023)
.

The calculation of
\xi
for the Starlink system was performed by considering the entire channel and assuming a trapezoidal impulse shape, so (
12
) becomes

\begin{split}\xi=T^{2}\frac{\sum_{i=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f^{2}|G_{i}(f)|^{2}\,df}{\sum_{i=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|G_{i}(f)|^{2}\,df}&=T^{2}\frac{\sum_{i=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f^{2}|G_{0}(f-iF)|^{2}\,df}{\sum_{i=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|G_{0}(f-iF)|^{2}\,df}\\
&=T^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{2}{T_{C}}\frac{1}{T_{\rm sym}-\frac{1}{3}T_{C}}+\frac{{FN}^{2}}{12}+\frac{F^{2}}{6}\right)\end{split}

(15)

where
F
is the width of a single sub-band and
N
is the number of subcarriers,
T_{\rm sym}
the OFDM symbol period,
T_{\rm C}
the OFDM chip period.

del Portillo Barrios et al., (2019)
,
Xia et al., (2019)
and

Iridium

According to
ICAO, (2007)
, the signal transmitted by the Iridium satellites consists of
240
channels of
31.5\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
each. These use a QPSK modulation whose symbols last for
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
and their waveforms have a
40\%
roll-off factor. The link budget was computed by using the data reported in
Dey, (2014)
.

Orbcomm

The Orbcomm signal is the one using the least amount of bandwidth: the spectrum is only
4.8\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
wide. The modulation is a symmetric differential PSK whose pulse shape has a
40\%
roll-off. The data for the link budget were obtained from

.

4
Geometry and Availability Considerations

In this Section, we investigate the performance limits of the
SOOP
-aided navigation due to availability and geometry, i.e., the placement of the satellites with respect to the receiver position.

To do so we consider two metrics. First, we consider the
complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF)
of the number of satellites in view: indeed, while we are interested in having at least 4 satellites in view for most of the time, having a high number of satellites typically leads to a more accurate
PVT
solution.
Next, we consider the
cumulative distribution function (CDF)
of the
GDOP
, as the
GDOP
also reports information about how spread out the satellites are with respect to the receiver, with sparser constellation having a lower
GDOP
. Then, the derivation of the
GDOP
can be used to derive the overall positioning accuracy by using (
2
).

We consider two scenarios, which we call Scenario 1 and 2, and run a Montecarlo simulation for each, where the satellites’ positions are obtained via an orbital propagator with used real-world broadcast ephemeris.
In both scenarios, we considered as representative locations Padova, the Svalbard islands, La Reunion island, and ESTEC.
In Scenario 1 we neglected the visibility effect induced by the beamwidth and masking angle, thus considering
\varphi=$90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
and
\theta=$10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
, as typically chosen for
MEO
-GNSS navigation. On the other hand, in Scenario 2, we consider a
\varphi=[$30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$,\ldots,$80.92\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$]
and
\theta=$40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
.

Discussion on Terrestrial SOOP

Suitable candidates as terrestrial
SOOP
are i) Bluetooth 4.0 and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), ii) Wi-Fi, and iii) 5G cellular network.

Bluetooth

Bluetooth 4.0 and BLE have several drawbacks when used for positioning. First, their range is typically limited to around
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, restricting their availability and effectiveness in larger spaces. Additionally, the achievable position accuracy is limited since positioning algorithms mainly rely on
received signal strength indicator (RSSI)
measurements. Lastly, a significant limitation of using Bluetooth 4.0 and BLE as a
SOOP
is the lack of infrastructure as the positions of Bluetooth beacons are typically not known at the receiver.

Wi-Fi

Similar drawbacks can be associated with Wi-Fi positioning, which can guarantee good performance in terms of accuracy only in an indoor environment. Wi-Fi networks are typically designed for indoor use, resulting in limited coverage and signal strength in outdoor environments.
In an outdoor scenario, a solution adopted for positioning through Wi-Fi is to use fingerprinting
Bisio et al., (2019)
, thus creating a database of Wi-Fi signal characteristics at various locations. Each location’s unique fingerprint is recorded, capturing signal strength and other parameters from nearby Wi-Fi access points. When positioning is needed, the current WiFi signals are compared to the database to estimate the device’s location. This method can provide improved accuracy compared to simple
RSSI
-based methods but faces challenges such as environmental variability, signal interference, and the need for extensive initial data collection and maintenance of the fingerprint database.
Finally, effective Wi-Fi positioning requires a dense network of access points with known locations, which may not be available or feasible to deploy in many outdoor areas.

Cellular Networks

On the other hand, 5G measurements can offer improved reliability, and accuracy in urban scenarios, especially considering the soon-to-be massive deployment of
base stations (BSs)
, and thus anchors for positioning.
For instance, they may serve as a backup for GNSS when unavailable and enhance overall performance by integrating measurements from both systems
Gonzalez-Garrido et al., (2022)
. Differently from both Bluetooth and Wi-Fi, the information about the 5G infrastructure, such as the positions of 5G
BSs
, is publicly available.
Moreover, a new positioning method relying on 5G has been proposed, using the 5G
positioning reference signals (PRSs)
, defined in TS 38.211
3GPP, (2020)
.
The
PRSs
are transmitted to the
user equipment (UE)
by multiple
BSs
. Next, the
UE
performs measurements for positioning that are then reported back to the network, which calculate the
UE
’s position by using trilateration or triangulation methods.

The allocation of time-frequency resources for
PRSs
is adaptable
Wei et al., (2023)
, enabling the multiplexing of different downlink PRS signals from multiple
BSs
across different subcarriers in a comb-like pattern. Moreover, the comb structure of
PRS
manages the interference caused by
PRS
signals transmitted by multiple
BSs
.
Thus, depending on the application, the requirements may vary and the 5G flexibility will help optimize the resources.
Moreover, apart from
PRS
, other 5G synchronization signals (SS), such as the primary SS (PSS), the secondary SS (SSS), and the demodulation reference signals (DMRS), can be used for positioning
Tedeschini et al., (2023)
, achieving good performance.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have investigated the potential of non-terrestrial and terrestrial
SOOP
for navigation applications. Our analysis of non-terrestrial
SOOP
has employed
MCRLB
to establish a relationship between
SOOP
characteristics and the accuracy of ranging information. We have computed a lower bound on the ranging accuracy using
MCRLB
, considering the estimation of propagation delay, frequency offset, phase offset, and
AOA
across various non-terrestrial
SOOP
candidates.
Additionally, we assessed
GDOP
and the availability of
LEO
SOOP. Our validation process involved comparing
MCRLB
predictions with actual ranging measurements obtained in realistic simulated scenarios.
Furthermore, we qualitatively evaluated various terrestrial
SOOP
, considering aspects such as signal availability, achievable accuracy, and infrastructure requirements.

Based on the comprehensive investigation into non-terrestrial and terrestrial
SOOP
for navigation applications, this study concludes that Starlink and the 5G cellular network emerge as the optimal choices. The Starlink
LEO
constellation demonstrates significant advantages in accuracy of ranging information.
The terrestrial
SOOP
evaluation highlights the suitability of the 5G cellular network, emphasizing its widespread signal availability, achievable accuracy, and existing infrastructure support. These qualities position 5G as a strong contender for terrestrial navigation applications.
