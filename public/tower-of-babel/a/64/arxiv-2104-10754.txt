Wolstenholme Type Congruences and Framing of Rational 2 2 -Functions
L. Felipe Müller

Abstract
. We show that the framing of 2 2 -sequences whose generating functions are rational integrate to 3 3 -sequences. To do so, we give a generalization of Wolstenholme’s Theorem.



1.
Introduction

Sequences of integers
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

(1.1)

\displaystyle a_{mp^{r}}\equiv a_{mp^{r-1}}\mod p^{sr},

for all
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
and a fixed
s\in\mathbb{N}
, are referred to as
s
-realizable sequences
in
[
2
]
.
For instance, the sequence of coefficients of the Maclaurin expansion of the Yukawa coupling is expected to be
3
-realizable (here,
s=3
).
Taking the Lambert expansion of the generating power series of an
s
-realizable sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}n^{s}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}}

gives integral coefficients
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(and vice versa, given integers
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
eq.

1.2
, one obtains an
s
-realizable sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
).
In the case of the Yukawa coupling when the moduli space of complex structures is one-dimensional, the coefficients
\{b_{n}\}_{n}
are realized by instanton numbers,
[
14
]
.
Indeed, according to the Mirror Symmetry Conjecture (see
[
7
]
,
[
17
]
) the number
b_{n}
in the case of the Yukawa coupling is the number of rational curves of degree
n
on a generic quintic hypersurface in projective space
\mathbb{P}^{4}
.
In particular, Mirror Symmetry predicts the numbers
b_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, to be integers, which is a highly non-trivial fact and which is equivalent to
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
being
3
-realizable.

Let
K
be an algebraic number field and
\mathcal{O}
its ring of algebraic integers and
D
its discriminant.
Following the conventions given in
[
18
]
, we consider a generalization of
s
-realizable sequences to sequences of algebraic integers in
K
, called
s
-sequences.
For
s\in\mathbb{N}
, an
s
-sequence is a sequence
(a_{n})\in K^{\mathbb{N}}
, such that for any unramified prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{O}
lying above the prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and for all
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frob_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a_{p^{r-1}m}\right)-a_{p^{r}m}\equiv 0\mod\mathfrak{p}^{sr}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the ring of
\mathfrak{p}
-adic integers and
\frob_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the canonical lift of the standard Frobenius element of
\mathfrak{p}
in the Galois group of the local field extension
(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p})|(\mathbb{Z}/p)
.

One of the most interesting observations concerning
2
-sequences in particular is that the generating functions of these sequences permit a certain algebraic transformation (of formal power series) called
framing
.
Formally, framing to the parameter
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
can be characterized by a functional equation.
Let
V\in zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
be a power series then the
\nu
-framing
V^{(-,\nu)}\in zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
(the minus sign in the index referes to a sign convention explained later) of
V
gives a power series satisfying the functional equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle\smallint V^{(-,\nu)}\left(z\left(-\exp(-\smallint V(z))^{\nu}\right)\right)=\smallint V(z),

where
\smallint
is defined on power series by
\smallint\colon z^{n}\mapsto\frac{z^{n}}{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

These framing transformations appear in the context of open topological string theory, see
[
1
]
, where the name has been coined.
In
[
19
]
, it was expected that for an appropriate choice of parametrization, the coefficients of the Lambert expansion
eq.

1.2
, the coefficients
(b_{n})
are counting dimensions of spaces of appropriate BPS states, hence
(b_{n})_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
In this setting, the superpotential (and its BPS invariants) depend on the integer parameter
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
, called “
the framing
”.
Framing therefore results from an ambiguity in the identification of the open string modulus.
The main result in
[
22
]
, due to Schwarz, Vologodsky and Walcher, is the
Integrality of Framing Theorem
that states that the framing operator preserves
2
-functions (also for more general algebraic coefficients) and defines a group action of
\mathbb{Z}
on the set of
2
-functions.

There seems to be a subclass of
2
-functions whose framings integrate to
3
-functions.
For instance, this behavior has been observed in
[
10
]
by the (extremal) BPS invariants of twist knots and has been referred therein as an “improved integrality”.
These
3
-functions appear as solutions of so-called extremal A-polynomials of these knots, and all their framings are expected (
[
10
, Conj. 1.3]
) to be also
3
-functions, or at least, can be lifted to
3
-functions by multiplying with an appropriate constant.
This improved integrality does not hold in general.
However, it would be interesting to give a physical interpretation of this property.

In the present paper, the author tries to identify the subclass of those
2
-sequences, such that all framings of the corresponding generating functions integrate to
3
-functions (i.e. the sequences of coefficients of their Maclaurin expansion are
3
-sequences).
The result is given below by
Theorem

1.1
.
Let
V^{(+,\nu)}(z):=V^{(-,\nu)}((-1)^{\nu}z)\in zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
.
This sign convention for
V^{(+,\nu)}
is for simplifying some notations and calculations, since it does not effect the congruence condition
eq.

1.3
for the coefficients of
V^{(+,\nu)}
, except for
p=2
.
Recall from
[
18
]
, that
\mathcal{S}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})
denotes the set of generating functions of
s
-sequences, while an element in
\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}}
is a powers series, for which the underlying sequence is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear multiple of a sequence that satisfies
eq.

1.3
for almost all unramified prime ideals in
K
.
Also,
\Phi^{\pm}\colon\mathbb{Q}\times zK\llbracket z\rrbracket\rightarrow zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
is given by
\Phi^{\pm}(\nu,V)=V^{(\pm,\nu)}(z)
.
We have

Theorem 1.1
.

Then

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}\left(\mathbb{Z}\left[D^{-1}\right]\times\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\mathrm{rat}}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right)

\displaystyle\subset\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{3}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}\left(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\mathrm{rat}}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right)

\displaystyle\subset\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{3}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}}.

More precisely, let
V\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rat}}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})
be a rational
2
-function of periodicity
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\left[D^{-1}\right]
and let
a_{n}^{+}=\left[V^{(+,\nu)}(z)\right]_{n}
denote the
n
-th coefficient of
V^{(+,\nu)}(z)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for all primes
p
which are unramified in
K|\mathbb{Q}
such that
p\nmid N
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
– except for the case where
p=2
and
\ord_{2}(n)=0
– we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}(a_{n}^{+})-a_{pn}^{+}\equiv 0\mod p^{2(\ord_{p}(n)+1)-\delta_{2,p}+\max\{0,\ord_{p}(n)+1-\gamma_{p}\}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where
\gamma_{p}
is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}=\begin{cases}1+\ord_{2}(N+1),&\text{if $p=2$ and $2\nmid N$},\\
1,&\text{if $p=3$},\\
0,&\text{if $p\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

In particular, for all primes
p\geq 5
, which are unramified in
K|\mathbb{Q}
and does not divide
N
, we find for all
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}(a_{mp^{r-1}}^{+})-a_{mp^{r}}^{+}\equiv 0\mod p^{3r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

In
[
18
]
, the author gave a description of the elements in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rat}}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})
, which contributes to the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.
The main result in
[
18
]
states that the coefficients of an element
V\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rat}}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})
are periodic and lie in a cyclotomic field.
Let us give a summary of the proof for
Theorem

1.1
for
\nu=1
.
From the Integrality of Framing Theorem we directly obtain that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p^{2}n^{2}}\cdot\left(\frob_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a^{+}_{n}\right)-a^{+}_{pn}\right),

is an
p
-adic integer, that is a
\mathfrak{p}
-adic integer for all
\mathfrak{p}\mid(p)
in
\mathcal{O}
.
Assuming rationality of
V
we then find for all but finitely many
p

(1.6)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p^{2}n^{2}}\cdot\left(\frob_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a^{+}_{n}\right)-a^{+}_{pn}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{n,m,p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p(n-m)}\frac{a_{p(n-m)-\ell}a_{\ell}}{\ell^{2}}\right)\mod p^{\ord_{p}(pn)-\delta_{3,p}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where
\tilde{y}_{n,m,p}
are certain
p
-adic integers in
\mathcal{O}
.
The sum appearing in the big bracket of
eq.

1.6
, namely

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p(n-m)}\frac{a_{p(n-m)-\ell}a_{\ell}}{\ell^{2}},

should be interpreted as a weighted harmonic sum, weighted by a convolution of the
2
-sequence
(a_{n})_{n}
with itself.
At the same time, many supercongruences are known among the binomial coefficients, for instance the Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis, which are typically proven by using sharp
p
-adic estimations of harmonic sums.
This kind of
p
-adic estimations are often referred to as Wolstenholme type congruences, see also Wolstenholme’s Theorem (for instance
[
16
]
).
This connection led to the following generalization of Wolstenholme’s Theorem, handling the sum (
1.7
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p
be an unramified prime in
K|\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}
be a prime ideal dividing
(p)
.
Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a periodic sequence of periodicity
N
, i.e.
N\in\mathbb{N}
is given by

\displaystyle N=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,a_{k+i}=a_{k}\text{ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\}.

Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod p^{\max\{0,\ord_{p}(n)-\varepsilon_{p,N}\}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p,N}=\begin{cases}\max\{\ord_{2}(N),\ord_{2}(N+2)\},&\text{if $p=2$ and $2\mid N$},\\
1+\ord_{2}(N+1),&\text{if $p=2$ and $2\nmid N$},\\
1+\ord_{3}(N),&\text{if $p=3$,}\\
\ord_{p}(N),&\text{if $p\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

Finally,
\tilde{y}_{n,m,p}
sharply contributes the remaining
p
-divisibility to obtain
Theorem

1.1
.
This contribution can be considered as an auxiliary to Dwork’s Integrality Lemma, see
[
8
, Lem. 1]
for the original result.
In the setting of
s
-functions it is given by the following statement.
Let
V\in zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
and let
Y\in 1+zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
be related by
Y(z)=\exp(\smallint V(z))
.
Then
V
is the generating series of an
1
-sequence if and only if
Y
has integral coefficients at all unramified prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}
.
Dwork himself used his lemma (stated for
K=\mathbb{Q}
) as a key step to prove his theorem that for an affine hypersurface
H
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
the zeta-function
Z(H;X)
of
H
in the variable
X
is a rational function and its logarithmic derivative
\frac{Z^{\prime}(H;X)}{Z(H;X)}
is the generating function of the non-negative numbers
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
-points of
H
, i.e.
N_{n}=|H(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})|
.
We need the following statement

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
V\in\mathcal{S}^{1}(K|\mathbb{Q})
be the generating series of an
1
-sequence and let
p
be an unramified prime in
K|\mathbb{Q}
.
Then for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
\ord_{p}(n)\geq\ord_{p}(m)
, we find the following
\mathfrak{p}
-adic estimation for the
m
-th coefficient
\tilde{y}_{m}
of the function
\exp\left(n\smallint V(z)\right)

(1.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{m}\equiv 0\mod p^{\ord_{p}(n)-\ord_{p}(m)}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

Theorem

1.1
can be considered as a generalization of the Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis congruence.
Also the proof of the latter served as a source of inspiration to the author in the process of finding the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.
However, the Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis congruence does not follow from
Theorem

1.1
, yet!
For this we give an extension of
Theorem

1.1
and also of the Integrality of Framing Theorem, where we allow
\nu
to be a rational number in the following manner.
For a power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}\in K\llbracket z\rrbracket
and an number
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
the
Cartier operator

\mathscr{C}_{\ell}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\ell}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{\ell n}z^{n}.

Then fractional framing referes to power series obtained by
\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}V^{(\pm,\nu)}
, where
V\in zK\llbracket z\rrbracket
,
\nu\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

Integrality of Fractional Framing:
Then,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{-}\right)\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{Z}\right)\times\mathcal{S}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right)\subset\mathcal{S}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{+}\right)\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{Z}\right)\times\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\mathrm{rat}}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right)\subset\left(\mathcal{S}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\{2\}}\cap\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right).

(2)

Then

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{+/-}\right)\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{Z}\right)\times\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\mathrm{rat}}(K|\mathbb{Q})\right)

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{S}^{3}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}}.

More precisely, for a rational
2
-function
V\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rat}}^{2}(K|\mathbb{Q})
of periodicity
N
and
\nu\in\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathbb{Z}
and
S=\{p\text{ prim with }p\mid N\}\cup\{2,3\}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}(z):=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}\left(\Phi^{+}(\nu,V)\right)\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{3}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{S}.

For
\tilde{a}^{+}_{n}=\left[\widetilde{V}(z)\right]_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\tilde{a}_{n}^{+}\right)-\tilde{a}_{pn}^{+}\equiv 0\mod p^{2\ord_{p}(pn)-\delta_{2,p}+\max\{0,\ord_{p}\left(pn\right)-\gamma_{p}\}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where
\gamma_{p}
is equal to
1+\ord_{2}(N+1)
,
1
and
0
if
p
is equal to
2
,
3
and greater than
3
, respectively.
In particular, for unramified
p\geq 5
in
K|\mathbb{Q}
with
p\nmid N
, and all
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\frob_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\tilde{a}_{mp^{r-1}}^{+}\right)-\tilde{a}_{mp^{r}}^{+}\equiv 0\mod p^{3r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

The proofs of these statements go analogously to the non-fractional case.
The Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis congruence then is a special case of
Theorem

1.4

(2)
by taking
a_{n}=1
for all

, that is, taking

, and appropriately varying

and

.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the definitions and notational conventions that will
be used throughout the paper.
We mainly follow the conventions given in
[
18
]
.

Let
K
be a fixed algebraic number field and we assume
K
to be normal over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{O}
the ring of integers of
K
.
Let
D
be the discriminant of
K|\mathbb{Q}
.
We say that a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
is unramified in
K|\mathbb{Q}
if all prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}\mid p\mathcal{O}
are unramified.
Note that an unramified prime
p
is characterized by the property that
p\nmid D
.
For any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
denotes the ring of
\mathfrak{p}
-adic integers.
Then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is an integral domain and its field of fractions

is the
\mathfrak{p}
-adic completion of
K
.

For
\mathfrak{p}\mid(p)
, the
Frobenius element

\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathfrak{p}}
at
\mathfrak{p}
is the unique element satisfying the following two conditions:

\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is an element in the decomposition group
D(\mathfrak{p})\subset\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
of
\mathfrak{p}
and for all
x\in\mathcal{O}
,
\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\equiv x^{p}\mod\mathfrak{p}
.
By Hensel’s Lemma,
\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathfrak{p}}
can be lifted to
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
and then extended to an automorphism
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}\colon K_{\mathfrak{p}}\rightarrow K_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
By declaring
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)=z
,
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}
can be (linearly) extended to an endomorphism
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}\colon K_{\mathfrak{p}}\llbracket z\rrbracket\rightarrow K_{\mathfrak{p}}\llbracket z\rrbracket
.

In the following, let
R
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra.
The
Euler operator

\delta_{R}\colon R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)\rightarrow R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)
is given by
z\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\delta_{R}\left[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r_{n}z^{n}\right]=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}nr_{n}z^{n}.

Its (partial) inverse of
\delta_{R}
is the
logarithmic integration

\smallint_{R}\colon zR\llbracket z\rrbracket\oplus z^{-1}R\left\llbracket z^{-1}\right\rrbracket\rightarrow zR\llbracket z\rrbracket\oplus z^{-1}R\left\llbracket z^{-1}\right\rrbracket
given by

\displaystyle\smallint\hskip-3.50006pt\,_{R}\left[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r_{n}z^{n}\right]=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{r_{n}}{n}z^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\smallint\hskip-3.50006pt\,_{R}(0)=0.

For a number
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathscr{C}_{R,k}
be the operator
\mathscr{C}_{R,k}\colon R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)\rightarrow R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)
, called the
Cartier operator
, given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{R,k}\left[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r_{n}z^{n}\right]=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r_{kn}z^{n}.

For a number
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varepsilon_{R,\ell}\colon R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)\rightarrow R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)
be the
R
-algebra homomorphism uniquely determined by setting

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{R,\ell}(z)=z^{\ell}.

Hereafter, we will omit
R
from the notation of
\delta_{R}
,
\smallint_{R}
,
\mathscr{C}_{R,k}
and
\varepsilon_{R,\ell}
.

In
[

]
, an
s
-function with coefficients in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{s}}\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}\widetilde{V}\left(z^{p}\right)-\widetilde{V}(z)\in z\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}\llbracket z\rrbracket.

[
18
]
, the author introduced

-sequences

-function, listed in a sequence.
This notion generalizes the concept of

-realizable sequences
(a term introduced by Almkvist and Zudilin in
[

]

A sequence

-function property for

, and

.

is called an

-sequence
if it satisfies the local

in
K|\mathbb{Q}
.

.

.
Also,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}}=\bigcup_{S}\mathcal{S}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{S},

where
S
runs through all finite subsets of rational primes.
Analogously, the sets
\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{S}
and
\overline{\mathcal{S}}^{s}(K|\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{fin}}
are defined.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be an integer.
Let
[-]_{n}
denote the
R
-functional
[-]_{n}\colon R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)\rightarrow R
, uniquely determined by

\displaystyle\left[z^{k}\right]_{n}=\delta_{n,k},

where
\delta_{n,k}
denotes the
Kronecker symbol
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be the operator
\mathcal{G}\colon\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket
, sending the sequence
x\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
to its generating formal power series,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}.

Trivially, the inverse of
\mathcal{G}
is given by
\left([-]_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\colon zR\llbracket z\rrbracket\rightarrow R^{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.
\mathcal{G}\circ\left([-]_{n}\right)_{n}=\mathrm{id}_{zR\llbracket z\rrbracket}
and
\left([-]_{k}\right)_{k}\circ\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{id}_{R^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

In other words,
[-]_{n}
extracts the
n
-th coefficient of a Laurent series.
Obviously, for any
V\in R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left[\delta V(z)\right]_{n}=n\cdot\left[V(z)\right]_{n}.

In particular, for
n=0
we obtain a formula for
integrating by parts
:
Let
F,G\in R(\hskip-1.99997pt(z)\hskip-1.99997pt)
, then

\displaystyle 0=\left[\delta(F(z)\cdot G(z))\right]_{0}=\left[G(z)\cdot\delta F(z)+F(z)\cdot\delta G(z)\right]_{0}

and therefore

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left[G(z)\cdot\delta F(z)\right]_{0}=-\left[F(z)\cdot\delta G(z)\right]_{0}.

Analogously, if
[F(z)]_{0}=0
and
n\neq 0
, then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left[\smallint(F(z))\right]_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\left[F(z)\right]_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\left[\smallint F(z)\right]_{0}=0.

3.
Partial Bell Polynomials and Bell Transformations

Let
\mathbb{Q}[\mathfrak{X}]
be the ring of polynomials in a countable number of indeterminates
\mathfrak{X}=\{X_{1},X_{2},...\}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
The complete exponential Bell polynomials
\{B_{n}|n\in\mathbb{N}\}
(named in honor of the mathematician and science fiction writer Eric Temple Bell) are defined by the generating coefficients of
\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{X_{i}}{i!}z^{i}\right)
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{X_{i}}{i!}z^{i}\right)=:\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}B_{n}(\mathfrak{X})\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

For
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\leq n
, the
(n,k)
-th
partial Bell polynomial

B_{n,k}
is implicitly defined as the homogeneous part of degree
k
of the
n
-th complete exponential Bell polynomial
B_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathfrak{X}]
.
It is also given by the series expansion

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}\frac{z^{j}}{j!}\right)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}B_{n,k}(\mathfrak{X})\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

The polynomial
B_{n,k}
is then explicitly given by

\displaystyle B_{n,k}(\mathfrak{X})=n!\sum_{\alpha\in\pi(n,k)}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-k+1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}!}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{i!}\right)^{\alpha_{i}}\right),

where
\pi(n,k)
denotes the set of multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-k+1}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-k+1}\alpha_{i}=k\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n-k+1}i\alpha_{i}=n.

Note that
B_{n,k}(\mathfrak{X})
is in fact a polynomial in the variables
X_{1},...,X_{n-k+1}
for all
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\leq n
.
It follows immediately from the definition that the
(n,k)
-th partial Bell polynomial is homogeneous of degree
k
and of weight
n
, i.e. for a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle B_{n,k}\left(\left(\lambda^{i}X_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\right)=\lambda^{n}B_{n,k}(\mathfrak{X}).

For a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
(
r\in\mathbb{N}
), the
absolute value of
\alpha
is defined by the sum of components of
\alpha
, i.e.
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}
.
For a sequence
x=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
we will use the convention

\displaystyle!x=(n!x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

In
[
5
]
,
Bell transformations of sequences
were introduced to tackle a wide variety of problems in enumerative combinatorics.
These transformations come along with functional equations satisfied by the corresponding generating power series.
Let
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
be fixed.
Then the Bell transformation associated to
(a,b,c,d)
is a map
\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}\colon\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
, mapping a sequence
x=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
to its image
y=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}(x)
given by

\displaystyle y_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(an+bk+cj+d)\right]B_{n,k}(!x)\quad\text{for all $n\geq 1$}.

The main result in
[
5
]
is the following convolution formula (Thm. 1 in
[
5
]
):
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
y=\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}(x)
.
Assume
c\neq 0
.
Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(\lambda-dj+d)\right]B_{n,k}(!y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(an+bk+cj+d+\lambda)\right]B_{n,k}(!x).

The following statement is a direct consequence of
eq.

3.1
.
We give a proof for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 3.1

(Consequence of
eq.

3.1
)
.

Let
a,b,c,d,e,f\in\mathbb{C}
such that either
c\neq 0
or
b=c=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{e,0,-d,f}\circ\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}=\mathscr{Y}_{a+e,b,c,f}.

Proof.

Let
c\neq 0
and let
x,y,\hat{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
be sequences related by

\displaystyle y=\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{y}=\mathscr{Y}_{e,0,-d,f}(y).

In particular, we have

\displaystyle n!\hat{y}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(en-dj+f)\right]B_{n,k}(!y).

By
eq.

3.1
, we therefore find for
\lambda=en-d+f

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}((a+e)n+bk+cj+f)\right]B_{n,k}(!x).

This is the desired formula
\mathscr{Y}_{e,0,-d,f}\circ\mathscr{Y}_{a,b,c,d}=\mathscr{Y}_{a+e,b,c,f}
.

If
b=c=0
, then
\mathscr{Y}_{a,0,0,d}^{-1}=\mathscr{Y}_{-a,0,-d,0}
by using
[
5
, Cor. 2
(ii)
]
.
Hence, by the previous case, we may compute

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{a+e,0,0,f}\circ\mathscr{Y}^{-1}_{a,0,0,d}=\mathscr{Y}_{a+e,0,0,f}\circ\mathscr{Y}_{-a,0,-d,0}=\mathscr{Y}_{e,0,-d,f}.

Equivalently,
\mathscr{Y}_{e,0,-d,f}\circ\mathscr{Y}_{a,0,0,d}=\mathscr{Y}_{a+e,0,0,f}
.
∎

To us, Bell transformations come in handy to define the framing operators
\Phi^{+/-}
and use the corresponding functional equations.

\displaystyle\left[Y(z)^{n}\right]_{m}\equiv 0\mod p^{\max\{0,\ord_{p}(n)-\ord_{p}(m)\}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

5.
Bell Transformations and Framing

In this section we will define the framing operators
\Phi^{+/-}
as operators on formal power series via Bell transformations.

Proposition

5.2
ensures that the framing operators fulfill the functional equation
eq.

1.4
, which is a consequence of the theory of Bell transformations, and gives a formula for the coefficients.

Definition 5.1

(framing operators
\Phi^{+/-}
)
.

Define the framing operator
\Phi^{+}\colon\mathbb{C}\times z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket
,
(\nu,V)\mapsto V^{(+,\nu)}(z)
by the following composition

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}(\nu,-)\colon z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket\xrightarrow{\smallint}z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket\xrightarrow{([-]_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}}\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{Y}_{\nu,0,0,0}}\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket\xrightarrow{\delta}z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket.

Also, define
\Phi^{-}\colon\mathbb{C}\times z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket\rightarrow z\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket
,
(\nu,V)\mapsto\Phi^{-}(\nu,V)
by twisting sign convolution
z\mapsto(-1)^{\nu}z
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}(\nu,V)=V^{(+,\nu)}\left((-1)^{\nu}z\right).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let

.

and

.
Then

(1)

\Phi^{+}
and
\Phi^{-}
define group actions of the additive group
(\mathbb{C},+)
on the set

of formal power series with vanishing zeroth coefficient.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{+/-}(0,-)=\mathrm{id}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{+/-}(\nu,-)\circ\Phi^{+}(\mu,-)=\Phi^{+/-}(\nu+\mu,-).

(2)

The following functional equations are satisfied

(5.1)

\displaystyle\smallint V^{(+,\nu)}\left(z\exp(-\nu\smallint V(z))\right)=\smallint V(z),

and

(5.2)

\displaystyle\smallint V^{(-,\nu)}\left(z\left(-\exp(-\smallint V(z))^{\nu}\right)\right)=\smallint V(z).

(3)

For the coefficients
a_{n}^{+}
and
a_{n}^{-}
we have for all

,

(5.3)

\displaystyle a_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{\nu}\left[\frac{\exp(\nu n\smallint V(z))}{z^{n}}\right]_{0},

and consequently by definition,

(5.4)

\displaystyle a_{n}^{-}=(-1)^{\nu n}a_{n}^{+}=\frac{(-1)^{\nu n}}{\nu}\left[\frac{\exp(\nu n\smallint V(z))}{z^{n}}\right]_{0}.

\Phi^{+}
follows immediately from

by setting

,

.

passes its group action property on to

.

(i)
.

is given by
[

(iv)
]

6.
Wolstenholme’s Theorem: Harmonic Sums, Binomials and a new Generalization

The goal of the present section is to prove a generalization of Wolstenholme’s Theorem given by
Theorem

6.2
.
For a survey on Wolstenholme’s Theorem see
[
16
]
.

In 1862, J. Wolstenholme proved that for all primes
p\geq 5
we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv 1\mod p^{3}.

This result is originally known as Wolstenholme’s theorem, see
[
25
]
for the original work.
As pointed out by Rosen in
[
21
]
, the related congruence on harmonic numbers
H_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}
, stating that for all primes
p\geq 5
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle H_{p-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{k}\equiv 0\mod p^{2}

(which was discovered 80 years earlier by E. Waring in 1782 (see
[
24
]
) and later by C. Babbage in 1819 (see
[
3
]
)), is in fact equivalent to Wolstenholme’s original result.
In modern literature,
eq.

6.2
is referred to as
Wolstenholme’s Theorem
.
More generally, we have

Theorem 6.1

(“Wolstenholme’s Theorem”, Waring-Babbage, see for instance
[
11
]
)
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
\epsilon_{p}
be
2
,
1
, or
0
according to whether
p
is
2
,
3
or
\geq 5
, respectively. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\equiv 0\mod p^{\max\{0,2\ord_{p}(n)-\epsilon_{p}\}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

Proof.

First check the identity, for
1\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1}{n-k}=-\frac{n}{k^{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}(n-k)}.

Note that the sum given in
eq.

6.3
is trivially a
p
-adic integer.
Therefore, w. l. o. g., we assume
2\ord_{p}(n)-\epsilon_{p}\geq 0
.
Then,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1}{n-k}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\left(-\frac{n}{k^{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}(n-k)}\right)

\displaystyle=-n\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}+n^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}(n-k)}\equiv-n\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\mod p^{2\ord_{p}(n)}\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

Now, we immediately observe that the assertion
eq.

6.3
is equivalent to the validity of the following congruence,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod p^{\ord_{p}(n)-\epsilon_{p}+\delta_{p,2}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

A proof of

There are a number of generalizations and extensions of Wolstenholme’s Theorem in terms of multiple harmonic sums and congruences among binomial coefficients.
The next theorem gives a generalization in yet another direction.
We will allow the nominator each summand be the folding of an periodic sequence with algebraic coefficients.
The motivation for this has its origin in the proof of

.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
p
be an unramified prime in
K|\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a periodic sequence of periodicity
N
, i.e.
N\in\mathbb{N}
is given by

\displaystyle N=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,a_{k+i}=a_{k}\text{ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\}.

Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod p^{\max\{0,\ord_{p}(n)-\epsilon_{p,N}\}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{p,N}=\begin{cases}\max\{\ord_{2}(N),\ord_{2}(N+2)\},&\text{if $p=2$ and $2\mid N$},\\
1+\ord_{2}(N+1),&\text{if $p=2$ and $2\nmid N$},\\
1+\ord_{3}(N),&\text{if $p=3$,}\\
\ord_{p}(N),&\text{if $p\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Write
n=mp^{r}
for
r=\ord_{p}(n)
and suitable
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\gcd(m,p)=1
.
Then, by using the geometric series
(1-xp)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(xp)^{k}
for
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{k\mapsto\mu p^{r}+\ell}{=}\sum_{\mu=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p^{r}}\frac{a_{(m-\mu)p^{r}-\ell}a_{\mu p^{r}+\ell}}{(\mu p^{r}+\ell)^{2}}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p^{r}}\frac{a_{(m-\mu)p^{r}-\ell}a_{\mu p^{r}+\ell}}{\ell^{2}(1+\frac{\mu}{\ell}p^{r})^{2}}

(6.5)

\displaystyle\hskip 12.50002pt\equiv\sum_{\mu=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p^{r}}\frac{a_{(m-\mu)p^{r}-\ell}a_{\mu p^{r}+\ell}}{\ell^{2}}\mod p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

Note, that the sum in
section

is trivially an element in
\mathcal{O}_{p}
.
First, find
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p\nmid q
,
N\mid q-1
, and – whenever possible –
p\nmid q+1
.
We have

Case 1:

, choose

.
Trivially,

.
In that case,

, by definition, and

\displaystyle q^{2}-1=(q-1)(q+1)=N(N+2),

and therefore,

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(q^{2}-1)=\ord_{p}(N).

Case 2:

Let
p>2
. If
p\mid N+1
and
p\nmid N+2
, then choose
q=3N+1
.
Note that
p\mid N+1
implies
p\nmid N
.
Indeed,
N\mid q-1
and

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=3N+1\equiv 2N\not\equiv 0\mod p,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle q+1

\displaystyle=3N+2\equiv N\not\equiv 0\mod p,\quad\text{since $p\nmid N$ and $p\neq 2$}.

Also,
p\nmid q
, since
3N+1=N-1+2(N+1)
and
p\neq 2
.
Finally,
p\nmid q+1
, since
3N+2=N+2(N+1)
and
p\nmid N
.
In this case,

\displaystyle q^{2}-1=3N(3N+2).

Hence,

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(q^{2}-1)=\ord_{p}(N)+\delta_{p,3}.

Case 3:

Let
p=2
and
p\mid N+1
,
p\nmid N+2
.
Then choose
q=2N+1
.
Observe, that
N\mid q-1
and

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=2N+1\equiv N\neq 0\mod 2,\quad\text{since $p\nmid N$}.

At the same time,

\displaystyle\ord_{2}(q^{2}-1)=\ord_{2}((q-1)(q+1))=\ord_{2}(4N(N+1))=2+\ord_{2}(N+1).

Case 4:

Let
p\not\in\{2,3\}
and
p\nmid N+1
and
p\mid N+2
, then choose
q=2N+1
.
Trivially,
N|q-1
and we have

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=2N+1\equiv-3\not\equiv 0\mod p,\quad\text{since $p\neq 3$ and,}

\displaystyle q+1

\displaystyle=2N+2\equiv N\not\equiv 0\mod p,\quad\text{since $p\neq 2$}.

In that case,

\displaystyle q^{2}-1=4N(N+1).

Hence,

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(q^{2}-1)=\ord_{p}(N).

Case 5:

Let
p=3
,
3\nmid N+1
and
3\mid N+2
, then choose
q=3N+1
.
Note that
p=3
implies
3\nmid N
.
Hence,
N\mid q-1
and we have

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=3N+1\equiv N-3\equiv N\not\equiv 0\mod 3.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle q^{2}-1=3N(3N+2)

and therefore,

\displaystyle\ord_{3}(q^{2}-1)=1.

Case 6:

Let
p=2
,
2\nmid N+1
and
2\mid N+2
(i.e.
2\mid N
), then choose
q=N+1
.
We have

\displaystyle q=N+1\not\equiv 0\mod 2.

Then

\displaystyle q^{2}-1=N^{2}+2N=N(N+2)

\displaystyle\ord_{2}(q^{2}-1)=\ord_{2}(N)+\ord_{2}(N+2)=1+\max\{\ord_{2}(N),\ord_{2}(N+2)\}.

Since we may find
q
such that
q\equiv 1\mod N
in every case, we have
a_{m+q\ell}=a_{m+\ell}
for all

and

.
Since

, we see that multiplication by
q
mod
p^{r}
gives a bijection on

and hence, we may also permute the sumands in

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p^{r}}\frac{a_{(m-\mu)p^{r}-\ell}a_{\mu p^{r}+\ell}}{\ell^{2}}\mod p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mu=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
p\nmid\ell\end{subarray}}^{p^{r}}\frac{a_{(m-\mu)p^{r}-q\ell}a_{\mu p^{r}+q\ell}}{(q\ell)^{2}}\mod p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}-1}{q^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{k}a_{n-k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

By the above choice of

, we therefore conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{k}a_{n-k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod p^{r-\epsilon_{p,N}-\delta_{2,p}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

For
p>2

For
p=2
we may in particular assume
\ord_{2}(n)=r\geq 1
.
By using the symmetry (i.e. the invariance of

) of the coefficients

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 2\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\mod 2^{r}\mathcal{O}_{2}.

Then by the same calculation as for general

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\mod 2^{r}\mathcal{O}_{2}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}-1}{q^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}a_{k}}{k^{2}}\equiv 0\mod 2^{r-\epsilon_{p,N}}\mathcal{O}_{2},

∎

Remark 6.3

(

In the special case of

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\

and

has four square roots modulo

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\

.

Theorem 6.4

(Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be non-negative integers,
r\in\mathbb{N}
a positive integer, and let
p
be a prime.
Then we have

\displaystyle\binom{ap^{r}}{bp^{r}}\equiv\binom{ap^{r-1}}{bp^{r-1}}\mod p^{3r-\epsilon_{p}},

where
\epsilon_{p}
is (as in
Theorem

6.1
)
2
,
1
, or
0
, whether
p
is
2
,
3
, or greater than
3
, respectively.

Proof.

We begin with

\displaystyle\binom{ap^{r}}{bp^{r}}\hskip-2.5pt\left/\binom{ap^{r-1}}{bp^{r-1}}\right.

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{bp^{r}}\frac{(a-b)p^{r}+k}{k}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{bp^{r-1}}\frac{k}{(a-b)p^{r-1}+k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{bp^{r}}\left(1+p^{r}\frac{a-b}{k}\right)

(6.9)

\displaystyle\equiv 1+p^{r}(a-b)F_{1}+p^{2r}(a-b)^{2}F_{2}\mod p^{3r},

where
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are given by the harmonic sums

\displaystyle F_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
p\nmid p\end{subarray}}^{bp^{r}}\frac{1}{k}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0,i<j\\
p\nmid ij\end{subarray}}^{bp^{r}}\frac{1}{ij}.

We have

\displaystyle 2F_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j\\
p\nmid ij\end{subarray}}^{bp^{r}}\frac{1}{ij}=\left[\sum_{i=1,\,p\nmid i}^{bp^{r}}\frac{1}{i}\right]^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
p\nmid i\end{subarray}}^{bp^{r}}\frac{1}{i^{2}}.

By
Theorem

6.1
,
Theorem

6.2
and
Remark

6.3
, this implies

\displaystyle F_{2}\equiv 0\mod p^{r-\varepsilon_{p}},

and finally,

\displaystyle\binom{ap^{r}}{bp^{r}}\hskip-2.5pt\left/\binom{ap^{r-1}}{bp^{r-1}}\right.\equiv 1\mod p^{3r-\epsilon_{p}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Example 8.3

(Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis)
.

Let
V(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\mathrm{rat}}(\mathbb{Q})
and
\nu=\frac{\rho}{\sigma}
with
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gcd(\rho,\sigma)=1
.

V
has periodicity
N=1
and

\displaystyle\smallint V(z)=\smallint\left(\frac{z}{1-z}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}=-\log(1-z).

As always,
a_{n}^{+}:=\left[\Phi^{+}(\nu,V)\right]_{n}

Recall from
eq.

5.3

\displaystyle a_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{\nu}\left[\frac{\exp(\nu n\smallint V(z))}{z^{n}}\right]_{0},\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Then

\displaystyle a_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{\nu}\left[\frac{1}{z^{n}}(1-z)^{-\nu n}\right]_{0}.

By the
generalized Binomial Theorem
we have

\displaystyle(1-z)^{-\nu n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{-\nu n}{k}(-1)^{k}z^{k}.

Note that in this case the binomial coefficient is defined by

(8.9)

\displaystyle\binom{-\nu n}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(-\nu n-j).

Rewriting the binomial coefficient, we obtain

\displaystyle\binom{-\nu n}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(\nu n+k-1-j)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\nu n+k-j)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{\nu n}{\nu n+k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(\nu n+k-j)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\nu n}{\nu n+k}\binom{\nu n+k}{k}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle a_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{\nu}\left[\frac{1}{z^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\nu n}{\nu n+k}\binom{\nu n+k}{k}z^{k}\right]_{0}=\frac{1}{\nu+1}\binom{(\nu+1)n}{n}.

In particular, for
\widetilde{V}(z)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathscr{C}_{\sigma}\left(\Phi^{+}(\nu,V)\right)
,

\displaystyle a^{+}_{\sigma n}=\left[\widetilde{V}(z)\right]_{n}=\frac{1}{\rho+\sigma}\binom{(\rho+\sigma)n}{\sigma n}.

Applying
Theorem

8.2
to
\widetilde{V}(z)
gives for all primes
p\geq 3

\displaystyle a_{\sigma n}^{+}-a_{\sigma pn}^{+}\equiv 0\mod p^{3(\ord_{p}(n)+1)-\delta_{p,3}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

On the other hand for
p\geq 3

\displaystyle a_{\sigma n}^{+}-a_{\sigma pn}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho+\sigma}\left[\binom{(\rho+\sigma)n}{\sigma n}-\binom{(\rho+\sigma)pn}{\sigma pn}\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\binom{(\rho+\sigma)n}{\sigma n}-\binom{(\rho+\sigma)pn}{\sigma pn}

\displaystyle=(\rho+\sigma)\left(a_{\sigma n}^{+}-a_{\sigma pn}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\mod p^{3(\ord_{p}(n)+1)-\delta_{p,3}+\ord_{p}(\rho+\sigma)}.

Equivalently,

(8.10)

\displaystyle\binom{(\rho+\sigma)pn}{\sigma pn}\equiv\binom{(\rho+\sigma)n}{\sigma n}\mod p^{3(\ord_{p}(n)+1)-\delta_{p,3}+\ord_{p}(\rho+\sigma)}.

Now we will prove the Theorem of Jacobsthal-Kazandzidis (see
Theorem

6.4
) for
p\geq 3
as a consequence of
Theorem

8.2
.
Fix a prime
p\geq 3
, let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be non-negative integers and let
r\in\mathbb{N}
be an integer.
W. l. o. g., let
b\leq a
and
\gamma=\min\{\ord_{p}(a),\ord_{p}(b)\}
.
Then either
bp^{-\gamma}
or
(a-b)p^{-\gamma}
is a
p
-adic unit.
Since the binomial coefficient is symmetric (that is,
\binom{a}{b}
invariant under the exchange
b\leftrightarrow a-b
), we may assume that
bp^{-\gamma}
is a
p
-adic integer.
Then
Theorem

6.4
follows from
eq.

8.10
by setting
\sigma=p^{-\gamma}b
,
\rho=p^{-\gamma}(a-b)
and
n=p^{\gamma+r-1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\binom{ap^{r}}{bp^{r}}

\displaystyle\equiv\binom{ap^{r-1}}{bp^{r-1}}\mod p^{3(r+\gamma)-\delta_{p,3}}.
