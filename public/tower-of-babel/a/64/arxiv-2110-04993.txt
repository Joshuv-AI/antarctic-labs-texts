On the energy landscape of symmetric quantum signal processing
Jiasu Wang, Yulong Dong, Lin Lin

Abstract
Symmetric quantum signal processing provides a parameterized representation of a real polynomial, which can be translated into an efficient quantum circuit for performing a wide range of computational tasks on quantum computers. For a given polynomial f f , the parameters (called phase factors) can be obtained by solving an optimization problem. However, the cost function is non-convex, and has a very complex energy landscape with numerous global and local minima. It is therefore surprising that the solution can be robustly obtained in practice, starting from a fixed initial guess Φ 0 \Phi^{0} that contains no information of the input polynomial. To investigate this phenomenon, we first explicitly characterize all the global minima of the cost function. We then prove that one particular global minimum (called the maximal solution) belongs to a neighborhood of Φ 0 \Phi^{0} , on which the cost function is strongly convex under the condition ‖ f ‖ ∞ = 𝒪 ⁡ ( d − 1 ) \left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}) with d = deg ⁡ ( f ) d=\deg(f) . Our result provides a partial explanation of the aforementioned success of optimization algorithms. Keywords— Quantum signal processing, quantum algorithm, nonlinear optimization.

Introduction

Given a target polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]
satisfying (1)
\deg(f)=d
, (2) the parity of
f

d\bmod 2
, (3)
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}:=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert<1

[
14

is to find a set of parameters (called phase factors)
\Phi:=(\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{d})\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d+1}

so that

f(x)=g(x,\Phi):=\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U(x,\Phi)|0}],\quad x\in[-1,1],

(1)

with

U(x,\Phi):=e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}\cdots e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{d-1}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{d}Z}.

(2)

Here

X:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad Z:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)

(3)

are Pauli
X
and
Z

\braket{0|U(x,\Phi)|0}
stands for the upper-left entry of the matrix
U(x,\Phi)\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
, and
g(x,\Phi)

defines a mapping from
[-1,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
for fixed
\Phi

i.e.
,

\Phi=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{2},\phi_{1},\phi_{0})\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d+1},

(4)

this is referred to as the symmetric quantum signal processing. The simplest example is
\Phi=(0,\ldots,0)
. This gives
U(x,\Phi)=e^{\mathrm{i}d\arccos(x)X}
and
g(x,\Phi)=\cos(d\arccos(x))=T_{d}(x)
, where
T_{d}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree
d
.

Due to the parity constraint, the number of degrees of freedom in the target polynomial
f(x)
is
\widetilde{d}:=\lceil\frac{d+1}{2}\rceil
. Hence
f(x)
is entirely determined by its values on
\widetilde{d}
distinct points.
Throughout the paper, we choose these points to be
x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{4\widetilde{d}}\pi\right)
,
k=1,...,\widetilde{d}
,
i.e.
, positive nodes of the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{2\widetilde{d}}(x)
.
For any target polynomial
f(x)
defined above, the solution to
Eq.

1
exists
[
14
,
6
]
, and Ref.
[
5
]
suggests that the solution can be restricted to be symmetric.
The existence of the solution implies that the problem of symmetric quantum signal processing can be equivalently solved via the following optimization problem

\Phi^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Phi\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d+1},\\
\text{symmetric.}\end{subarray}}F(\Phi),\ F(\Phi):=\frac{1}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left\lvert g(x_{k},\Phi)-f(x_{k})\right\rvert^{2},

(5)

i.e.
, any solution
\Phi^{*}
to
Eq.

1
achieves the global minimum of the cost function with
F(\Phi^{*})=0
, and vice versa.

However, the energy landscape of the cost function
F(\Phi)
is very complex, and has numerous global as well as local minima (see
Section

7
).
This is already the case with the symmetry constraint.
(Without the symmetry constraint, the number of variables is larger than the number of equations and there should be an infinite number of global minima.)
Starting from a random initial guess, an optimization algorithm can easily be trapped at a local minima already when
d
is small

1

An early attempt (not using optimization-based approach) showed that even finding
\Phi
with
d>30
can be very costly
[
4
, Apendix H.3]
.
.
It is therefore surprising that starting from a special symmetric initial guess

\Phi^{0}=(\pi/4,0,0,\ldots,0,0,\pi/4),

(6)

which is independent of the target function, at least one global minimum can be robustly identified using standard unconstrained optimization algorithms even when
d
is as large as
10,000

[
5
]
, and the optimization method is free from being trapped by any local minima.
Direct calculation shows that
g(x,\Phi^{0})=0
, and therefore
\Phi^{0}
does not contain any
a priori
information of the target polynomial
f(x)
!

1.1
Main results

In this work, we provide a theory to characterize the energy landscape of the cost function
F(\Phi)
, and to explain this unusual behavior of numerical optimization. Under the assumptions on the maxnorm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
as stated in
Corollary

7
, this leads to the first provable algorithm for solving the QSP problem without referring to extended precision arithmetic operations
[
8
]
.

Let the domain of the symmetric phase factors be

D_{d}=\begin{cases}[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}\times[-\pi,\pi)\times[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})^{\frac{d}{2}},&d\mbox{ is even,}\\
[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})^{d+1},&d\mbox{ is odd.}\\
\end{cases}

(7)

Our first main result is the existence and uniqueness of symmetric phase factors for a class of polynomial matrices in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
.

Theorem 1

(Existence and uniqueness of symmetric phase factors)
.

Consider any
P\in\mathbb{C}[x]
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}[x]
satisfying the following conditions

(1)

\deg(P)=d
and
\deg(Q)=d-1
.

(2)

P
has parity
(d\bmod 2)
and
Q
has parity
(d-1\bmod 2)
.

(3)

(Normalization condition)
\forall x\in[-1,1]:|P(x)|^{2}+(1-x^{2})|Q(x)|^{2}=1
.

(4)

If
d
is odd, then the leading coefficient of
Q
is positive.

There exists a unique set of symmetric phase factors
\Phi:=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{1},\phi_{0})\in D_{d}
such that

U(x,\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}P(x)&\mathrm{i}Q(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
\mathrm{i}Q(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&P^{*}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

This result is a refinement of
[
6
, Theorem 4]
, which only shows the existence of the phase factors without symmetry constraints.

Theorem

1
states that in the presence of the symmetric constraint, the phase factors are also unique. Besides, under some constraint, the converse of
Theorem

1
also holds (see
Remark

12
). It follows that there is a bijection between the global minimizers of
Eq.

5
and all possible pairs of
(P(x),Q(x))
satisfying the assumption in
Theorem

1
and
\mathrm{Re}[P](x)=f(x)
.
The conditions (1), (2) for the target polynomial
f
are compatible with the first two requirements in
Theorem

1
.
The condition (3) on the maxnorm,
i.e.
,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}<1
is compatible with the normalization condition, which is itself a natural condition due to the unitarity of
U(x,\Phi)
.

To simplify the discussion, we introduce the following definition of admissible pair of polynomials associated with a target polynomial. Here
P_{\mathrm{Re}}:=\mathrm{Re}[P],P_{\mathrm{Im}}:=\mathrm{Im}[P]
.

Definition 2

(Admissible pair of polynomials)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]
be a target polynomial satisfying (1)
\deg(f)=d
, (2) the parity of
f
is
(d\bmod 2)
, (3)
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}<1
. Then
(P,Q)
is an admissible pair of polynomials associated with
f
if the conditions (1)-(3) in
Theorem

1
are satisfied, together with

(4)

The leading coefficient of
Q
is positive,

(5)

P_{\mathrm{Re}}(x)=f(x)
.

The polynomials
P_{\mathrm{Im}},Q\in\mathbb{R}[x]
are also called complementary polynomials to
f
.

Comparing
Theorem

1
and
Definition

2
, the main modification is that the leading coefficient of the complementary polynomial
Q
is always restricted to be positive.
This allows us to unify the discussion of odd and even values of
d
.
The bijection relation can then be concisely formulated as follows.

Corollary 3

(Bijection between global minima and admissible pairs)
.

If
d
is odd, there is a bijection between the global minima of
Eq.

5
and all admissible pairs
(P,Q)
.

If
d
is even, there is a bijection between the global minima of
Eq.

5
and all pairs of polynomials
(P,\pm Q)
, where
(P,Q)
is an admissible pair.

The proof of
Theorem

1
is constructive.
So given an admissible pair, we have an algorithm to evaluate the symmetric phase factor.
Theorem

4
generalizes the result of
[
8
, Lemma 4]
by explicitly constructing
all
admissible pairs.
Together with
Theorem

1
, we have a complete description of all global minima of the cost function in
Eq.

5
.

Theorem 4

(Construction of admissible pairs)
.

Given a target polynomial
f(x)
, all admissible pairs
(P,Q)
must take the following form,

\begin{split}P_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2},\\
\quad Q\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)-e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.\end{split}

(9)

Here,
e(z):=z^{-d}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})
, where each
r_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,2d)
is a root of the function

\mathfrak{F}(z):=1-\left[f\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)\right]^{2},

(10)

and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies
\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha e(z)e(z^{-1})
.

Note that the set
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2d}
does not include all roots of the
Laurent polynomial

\mathfrak{F}(z)
, which has
4d
roots in total.
By properly choosing the roots (see a more detailed description in
Remark

17
), we can construct all the admissible pairs.
The number of global minima is finite, which is a consequence of the compactness of the domain
D_{d}
.
Theorem

4
shows that the number of global minima can grow combinatorially with respect to
d
.

Unfortunately, the procedure described above for finding phase factors is numerically unstable. It requires extended precision arithmetic operations and is therefore very expensive when
d
is large (see detailed discussions in
Section

1.2
).
This is noticeably different from solving the optimization problem in
Eq.

5
, which is numerically stable and can be readily performed using standard double precision arithmetic operations.

Among the myriad of global minima,
Theorem

4
allows us to identify a special global minimum, which is obtained by choosing
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2d}
to be the roots of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
within the unit disc.
The unique symmetric phase factor associated with this admissible pair is referred to as the
maximal solution
(the reason for the naming is technical and is explained in
Section

4.2
).

The maximal solution enjoys many desirable properties. For any target polynomial
f
with
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the maximal solution lies in the neighborhood of
\Phi^{0}
, which is defined in
Eq.

6
. To characterize this property, we first introduce the definition of reduced phase factors. Given any symmetric phase factors
\Phi
of length
d+1
, reduced phase factors is referred to the left half part of
\Phi
, namely
\left(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)
, and is denoted as
\widetilde{\Phi}
. Recall that
\widetilde{d}:=\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rceil
. Due to symmetry,
\Phi
is fully determined by
\widetilde{\Phi}
. Hence, we may adopt Euclidean distance between
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
instead of that between
\Phi^{*}
and
\Phi^{0}
. Furthermore, throughout this paper, we choose
\widetilde{\Phi}
as the free variables. With some abuse of notation, we identify the cost function
F(\Phi)
with
F(\widetilde{\Phi})
.

Theorem 5

(Distance between the maximal solution and
\Phi^{0}
)
.

Let
\Phi^{*}
be the maximal solution for the target function
f(x)
. Denote
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
as the corresponding reduced phase factors of
\Phi^{*}
and
\Phi^{0}
respectively. If
\left\lVert f(x)\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\left\lVert f(x)\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(11)

In particular, if the target polynomial is
f=0
, then the maximal solution is the initial guess
\Phi^{0}
. We then prove that when
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
is sufficiently small, the maximal solution belongs to a neighborhood of
\Phi^{0}
, on which the cost function
F(\widetilde{\Phi})
is strongly convex,
i.e.
, the Hessian matrix denoted by
\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi})
is positive definite.

Theorem 6

(Local strong convexity)
.

If the target polynomial satisfies
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}
, for any symmetric phase factors
\Phi
of length
d+1
satisfying
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}
, the following estimate holds:

\frac{1}{4}\leq\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi})\right)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi})\right)\leq\frac{25}{4}.

(12)

The local strong convexity result in
Theorem

6
immediately implies that when
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
is sufficiently small, standard optimization algorithms, such as the projected gradient method
[
1
,
11
]
, can converge in the neighborhood of
\Phi^{0}
, without being trapped by any local minima.

Corollary 7

(Convergence of projected gradient method)
.

If the target polynomial satisfies
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}
, starting from
\Phi^{0}
, the projected gradient method with step size
t=\frac{1}{L}
converges exponentially to the maximal solution
\Phi^{*}
, i.e., at the
\ell
-th iteration

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq e^{-\frac{\sigma}{L}\ell}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(13)

Here
\sigma=\frac{1}{4}
and
L=\frac{25}{4}
.

Therefore when
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}
, in order to reach precision
\epsilon
, the projected gradient method can be terminated after
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon))
steps independent of the details of
f
. Since each iteration is numerically stable, the maximal solution can be readily obtained using standard double precision arithmetic operations in practice.

1.2
Background and related works

The mapping in
Eqs.

1
and
2
may seem a very peculiar way for encoding a polynomial (and it is).
However, such an SU(2) representation can be directly translated into a quantum circuit, which is so far the most concise way for performing eigenvalue transformation
f(A)
(when
A
is an Hermitian matrix)
[
14
]
, and singular value transformations
f^{\mathrm{SV}}(A)
(when
A
is a general matrix) on quantum computers
[
6
]
.
Many tasks in quantum computation can be formulated using such transformations.
When
f
is not a polynomial, it can be approximated by a polynomial with bounded precision.
This strategy can be used to unify a large class of quantum algorithms including Grover’s search and quantum phase estimation
[
17
,
16
]
, and to perform a wide range of computational tasks, such as solving linear system of equations
[
17
,
13
]
, eigenvalue problems
[
12
]
, Hamiltonian simulation
[
14
,
6
]
etc.

After the initial efforts
[
14
,
4
]
, significant progress has been made by using direct methods to obtain phase factors
[
6
,
8
]
. These methods are based on finding roots of high degree polynomials to high precision and are not numerically stable. Specifically, these algorithms require
\mathcal{O}(d\log(d/\epsilon))
bits of precision, where
d
is the degree of
f(x)
and
\epsilon
is the target accuracy
[
8
]
.
It is worth mentioning that the extended precision needed in these algorithms is not an artifact of the proof technique. For instance, for
d\approx 500
, the number of bits needed to represent each floating point number can be as large as
1000\sim 2000

[
5
]
, which is much larger than the
64
bits provided by standard double precision floating point format.

There have been two recent improvements of the factorization based method, based on the capitalization method
[
3
]
, and the Prony method
[
19
]
, respectively.
Although the two methods differ significantly, empirical results indicate that both methods are numerically stable, and are applicable to polynomials of large degrees. The algorithm in
[
3
]
is a variation of the algorithm in
[
6
]
, which introduces a small perturbation to the high order Chebyshev coefficients to enhance the numerical stability. The Prony method in
[
19
]
avoids the root finding of high degree polynomials by directly constructing the admissible pairs for a given target polynomial, and uses randomness to enhance the numerical stability.
Ref.
[
5
]
proposes a very different optimization based algorithm and suggests the use of the symmetric phase factors. This work provides a detailed discussion of the analytic structure of quantum signal processing with symmetric phase factors, and demonstrates the effectiveness of the optimization based approach under the condition that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
is sufficiently small.
This provides a partial solution to the open problem of finding phase factors using numerically stable algorithms, and justifies the usage of standard double precision arithmetic operations in practice.

1.3
Discussion and open questions

Without the symmetry constraint, the optimization problem in
Eq.

5
has
d
degrees of freedom.
This is larger than the number of degrees of freedom of target function
f
, which is only
\widetilde{d}
due to the parity constraint.
As a result, the Hessian matrix is always singular, preventing us from proving the exponential convergence of optimization methods
[
18
]
.

Our analytical result is the first result trying to explain the unexpected success of optimization algorithms in the problem of finding phase factors. In
Theorem

6
, we prove the local strong convexity of the cost function when
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1})
.
Therefore for a given target polynomial, in order to apply
Corollary

7
, we need to first multiply the target polynomial by a scaling factor
c=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1})
and find the phase factors for
cf(x)
instead. Such a scaling factor can be undesirable and may lead to suboptimal query complexities when implementing certain quantum algorithms. On the other hand, numerical results suggest that optimization algorithms can robustly converge to a global minimum when
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
is smaller than a constant (less than
1
) that is independent of
d
. Note that the estimate in
Theorem

5
is already independent of
d
. Hence the gap is mainly due to
Theorem

6
, where the eigenvalue estimate of the Hessian matrix is obtained via the estimate of each matrix entry, which introduces the
d
-dependence. It is a natural open question to prove the strong convexity of the cost function near
\Phi^{0}
where
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
can be independent of
d
.

The proof of
Corollary

7
requires the projection of
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}
to the domain
\{\widetilde{\Phi}:\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}\}
at each iteration. This is because the steepest descent method may potentially overshoot and deviate away from the locally strong convex region.
Numerical observation shows that unconstrained optimizers, such as quasi-Newton methods can robustly achieve the global minimum, which is beyond our current theoretical analysis.

Finally, since
\Phi^{0}
is only one of the solutions when
f=0
, we may ask what if we run the projected gradient method starting from other solutions when
f=0
, and to obtain optimal phase factors for more general target polynomials. These solutions correspond to non-maximal solutions. Numerical observations show that the convergence rate towards such non-maximal solutions can be significantly slower than that towards the maximal solution (see
Figs.

5
and
6
).
The analysis of such behavior requires the generalization of estimates of in
Theorems

5
and
6
to non-maximal solutions. These will be our future works.

Preliminaries

2.1
Chebyshev polynomials

We first collect some basic facts of Chebyshev polynomials, which is used to formulate the optimization problem in
Eq.

5
and will be extensively used in
Sections

5
and
6
.
Given
x\in[-1,1]
, the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind is
T_{n}(x)=\cos\left(n\arccos{(x)}\right)
, and that of the second kind is given by
U_{n}(x)\sin(\arccos{(x)})=\sin\left((n+1)\arccos{(x)}\right)
.

A useful fact is that both
T_{n}
and
U_{n}
form a sequence of orthogonal polynomials. The Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind
T_{n}
are orthogonal with respect to the weight
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
over
[-1,1]
, and that of the second kind
U_{n}
are orthogonal with respect to the weight
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
over
[-1,1]
,

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}T_{n}(x)T_{m}(x)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{nm}(1+\delta_{n0}),\\
&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}U_{n}(x)U_{m}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{nm}.\end{split}

The induced norms

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{T}^{2}:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}f^{2}(x)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x,\quad\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U}^{2}:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}f^{2}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

are referred to as the Chebyshev norm of the first kind and the second kind respectively. Furthermore, any function
h
with
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{T}<\infty
can be uniquely expressed as a series of Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind,

h(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}T_{n}(x),\qquad c_{n}=\frac{2}{\pi(1+\delta_{n0})}\int_{-1}^{1}h(x)T_{n}(x)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x.

(14)

Similarly, any function
h
with
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{U}<\infty
can be uniquely expressed as a series of Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind,

h(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}{U_{n}(x)},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}h(x){U_{n}(x)}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

(15)

Apart from the weighted orthogonality, Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
T_{n}
satisfies the discrete orthogonality condition:

\sum_{j=1}^{2\widetilde{d}}T_{n}(x_{j})T_{m}(x_{j})=\begin{cases}0&n\neq m\\
2\widetilde{d}&n=m=0\\
\widetilde{d}&n=m\neq 0\end{cases},

(16)

where
x_{j}=\cos\left(\pi\frac{2j-1}{4\widetilde{d}}\right)
are the nodes of
T_{2\widetilde{d}}
and
\max(n,m)\leq 2\widetilde{d}
. This set of points is also referred to as Chebyshev nodes. Furthermore, if
n
and
m
have the same parity which implies
T_{n}(-x)T_{m}(-x)=T_{n}(x)T_{m}(x)
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\widetilde{d}}T_{n}(x_{j})T_{m}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\widetilde{d}}T_{n}(x_{j})T_{m}(x_{j})=\begin{cases}0&n\neq m\\
\widetilde{d}&n=m=0\\
\frac{\widetilde{d}}{2}&n=m\neq 0\end{cases}.

(17)

2.2
Notation

Given a positive integer
n
,
\mathbb{K}_{n}[x]
denotes the set of all polynomials of degree at most
n
with variable
x
, and coefficients taken in the field
\mathbb{K}
. The Laurent polynomial is a linear combination of positive as well as negative powers of the variable
x
with coefficients in the field
\mathbb{K}
, and
\mathbb{K}[x,x^{-1}]
denotes the set of all Laurent polynomials. Specifically, we are only interested in the cases where
\mathbb{K}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Unless otherwise noted, the term “polynomial” refers to real polynomial or complex polynomial, which only have nonnegative power of the variable
x
.

Throughout the paper, we will interchangeably expand a polynomial using the monomial basis and the Chebyshev basis,

Q(x)&=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mathbf{q}_{j}x^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}q_{j}U_{j}(x)\quad\mathbf{q}_{j},q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}.\end{split}

For convenience, we set the values of

The coefficients in the monomial and Chebyshev basis satisfy the relation

\quad\mathbf{f}_{j}=2^{j-1}f_{j},\quad\mathbf{p}_{j}=2^{j-1}p_{j},\quad\mathbf{s}_{d}=2^{j-1}s_{j},\quad\mathbf{q}_{j}=2^{j}q_{j},\quad\forall j\geq 0.

(20)

The set

Quantum signal processing

In the absence of the symmetry constraint, the existence of phase factors for a unitary representation of a pair of polynomials

Quantum signal processing

[

2.4
Quantum signal processing as an optimization problem

The existence result in
Corollary

9
enables the following optimization based strategy to find phase factors.
Given the target polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
, we may minimize the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
distance between

and target function
f(x)
,
i.e.
,

L(\Phi)=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\left[g(x,\Phi)-f(x)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(24)

The desired phase factors
\Phi^{*}
satisfy
L(\Phi^{*})=0
.

Since both
g(x,\Phi)
and
f(\Phi)
are polynomials of degree
d
with definite parity, the number of degrees of freedom is
\widetilde{d}:=\lceil\frac{d+1}{2}\rceil
. These polynomials are determined by their values at
x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{4\widetilde{d}}\pi\right)
,
k=1,...,\widetilde{d}
, which are positive Chebyshev nodes of
T_{2\widetilde{d}}(x)
. This leads to the following optimization problem,

\Phi^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\Phi\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d+1}}F(\Phi),\ F(\Phi):=\frac{1}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left\lvert g(x_{k},\Phi)-f(x_{k})\right\rvert^{2}.

(25)

3.4
Optimization based method for finding symmetric phase factors

Ref.
[
5
]
suggests that the optimization problem
Eq.

5
should be solved by imposing the symmetry constraint on the phase factors. This allows us to use the reduced phase factors
\widetilde{\Phi}=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1})
, as optimization variables. For any scalar valued function of the set of reduced phase factors, we use subscripts to represent the partial derivative with respect to the corresponding phase factor, for example,
F_{i}(\widetilde{\Phi}):=\partial_{\phi_{i}}F(\widetilde{\Phi})
. We also use subscripts to represent the corresponding entry of any matrix. These notations and conventions can simplify the discussion in
Sections

5
and
6
.

The Hessian matrix of the problem in
Eq.

5
can be written element-wise as

\begin{split}F_{ij}(\widetilde{\Phi})&=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g_{i}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})g_{j}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})+\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}[g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x_{k})]g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})\\
&=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}(A^{\top}A)_{ij}+R_{ij},\end{split}

(38)

where
A(\widetilde{\Phi}):=[g_{j}(x_{i},\widetilde{\Phi})]\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d}\times\widetilde{d}}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\widetilde{\Phi}\mapsto\left(g(x_{1},\widetilde{\Phi}),\dots,g(x_{\widetilde{d}},\widetilde{\Phi})\right)^{\top}
, and
R
is a real
\widetilde{d}\times\widetilde{d}
matrix closely related to the residual with entry (note that
R_{ij}
does not mean the partial derivative here)

R_{ij}(\widetilde{\Phi}):=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}[g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x_{k})]g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}).

Due to the existence of phase factors stated in
Theorem

1
, at the optimal point
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}
, the second term vanishes, namely
R(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})=0
, and
\text{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}(A^{*})^{\top}A^{*}
, where
A^{*}:=A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})
and
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}
are the reduced phase factors corresponding to symmetric phase factors
\Phi^{*}
.

4.1
Characterizing complementary polynomials

Before constructing all possible pairs of
(P(x),Q(x))
satisfying
Definition

2
, we first introduce the following lemma, which views the problem from the perspective of Laurent polynomials.

Lemma 15
.

Given any real polynomial
f(x)
of degree
d
and with
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}<1
,
\mathfrak{F}(z)=1-\left(f\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)\right)^{2}
has a decomposition

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i}),

(39)

such that
\alpha,r_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

The decomposition is unique in the following sense: if
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\widetilde{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
such that

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\widetilde{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-\widetilde{r}_{i})(z^{-1}-\widetilde{r}_{i}),

(40)

then

n=2d,\qquad\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}=\widetilde{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}\widetilde{r}_{i},\qquad\frac{r_{i}+r_{i}^{-1}}{2}=\frac{\widetilde{r}_{i}+\widetilde{r}_{i}^{-1}}{2},

(41)

up to permutation of the roots.

Proof.

According to the fundamental theorem of algebra,
1-f(x)^{2}
has a unique decomposition

1-f(x)^{2}=c\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(s_{i}-x),\quad s_{i}\in\mathbb{C}.

Since
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}<1
,
s_{i}\neq 0
. The equation
s=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}
always has 2 nonzero solutions, say
z_{1},z_{2}
. In particular,
z_{1}=z_{2}^{-1}
. For any
s_{i}
, pick one solution
r_{i}
, use equality

r_{i}(s_{i}-x)=\frac{1}{2}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i}),

and one gets

\mathfrak{F}(z)=c\prod_{i=1}^{2d}\frac{1}{2r_{i}}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i})=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i}),

where
\alpha=c\prod_{i=1}^{2d}\frac{1}{2r_{i}}
. It provides a decomposition of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
satisfying the requirement.

On the other hand, given any decomposition

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\widetilde{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-\widetilde{r}_{i})(z^{-1}-\widetilde{r}_{i}),

then one has

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\widetilde{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2\widetilde{r}_{i})\left(\frac{\widetilde{r}_{i}+\widetilde{r}_{i}^{-1}}{2}-\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right).

It provides a decomposition of
1-f(x)^{2}
,
i.e.
,

1-f(x)^{2}=\widetilde{\alpha}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}2\widetilde{r}_{i}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\widetilde{r}_{i}+\widetilde{r}_{i}^{-1}}{2}-x\right).

By the uniqueness decomposition of
1-f(x)^{2}
in
\mathbb{C}[x]
, we obtain
Eq.

41
up to permutation of the roots.
∎

In particular, we are interested in the following decomposition of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
, which is referred to as an
admissible decomposition
,

•

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i})
,
\alpha>0,r_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

•

Multiset
\mathcal{D}:=\{r_{i}:1\leq i\leq 2d\}
is closed under complex conjugation and additive inverse.

Firstly,
\mathcal{D}
contains half of the roots of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
with multiplicity (hence
\mathcal{D}
is a multiset rather than set). Secondly, note that
\alpha>0
naturally comes from that
\alpha=\frac{1-f(1)^{2}}{\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(1-r)^{2}}>0
for any
\mathcal{D}
closed under complex conjugation. Another observation is that the roots of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
cannot be any number of absolute value 1. Otherwise,
1-f\left(\frac{r_{i}+r_{i}^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}=0
,
i.e.
,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}=1
, which contradicts the constraint on the maxnorm of
f
.

To be more precise, we rephrase
Theorem

4
as follows and present its proof.

Theorem 16
.

The pair of polynomials
\left(P(x),Q(x)\right)
satisfy
Definition

2
if only if there exists an admissible decomposition of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
,

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i}),

(42)

such that

\begin{split}P_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2},\\
\quad Q\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)-e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},\end{split}

(43)

where
e(z):=z^{-d}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})
.

Proof.

\Longrightarrow
: Denote
p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z):=P_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)
and
q(z):=Q\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)
. Then the normalization condition gives

\begin{split}\mathfrak{F}(z)&=p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)^{2}-q(z)^{2}\left(\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}\\
&=\left(p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)+\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}q(z)\right)\left(p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)-\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}q(z)\right).\end{split}

Define
H(z):=p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)+\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}q(z)
. Since
p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)=p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z^{-1})
and
q(z)=q(z^{-1})
, one gets

\mathfrak{F}(z)=H(z)H(z^{-1}).

Notice that
z^{d}H(z)
is a real polynomial of degree no more than
2d
. Thus
z^{d}H(z)
can be represented as
c\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)
, where
c\in\mathbb{C}
and the multiset
\mathcal{D}
contains all the roots of
z^{d}H(z)
with multiplicity. Since
H(z)
is real,
\mathcal{D}
is closed under complex conjugation and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

With
H(z)=cz^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)
, one gets

\mathfrak{F}(z)=H(z)H(z^{-1})=c^{2}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)(z^{-1}-r).

By
Lemma

15
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}
is
2d
and thus
z^{d}H(z)
is of degree
2d
. Moreover,
r\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
,
\forall r\in\mathcal{D}
.

Besides, we can check that the parity of
z^{d}H(z)
is even by examining the parity of
p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)
and
q(z)
. Thus
\mathcal{D}
is closed under additive inverse and
Eq.

43
holds as well.

\Longleftarrow
:
Denote
p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z):=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)+e(z^{-1})}{2}
and
q(z):=\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)-e(z^{-1})}{z-z^{-1}}
. Then

\begin{split}\mathfrak{F}(z)&=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i})=\alpha e(z)e(z^{-1})\\
&=\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)+e(z^{-1})}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)-e(z^{-1})}{2}\right)^{2}\\
&=\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)+e(z^{-1})}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\frac{e(z)-e(z^{-1})}{z-z^{-1}}\right)^{2}\\
&=p_{\mathrm{Im}}(z)^{2}+\left(1-\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)q(z)^{2}.\end{split}

Since
\mathcal{D}
is closed under complex conjugation,
\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})
is a real polynomial and
e(z)=z^{d}+\sum_{i=1-d}^{d-1}e_{i}z^{i}+\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)z^{-d}
for some
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that for
k>0

\begin{split}&\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{k}+\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{-k}=2T_{k}(x),\\
&\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{k}-\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{-k}=2\mathrm{i}U_{k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.\\
\end{split}

We can verify that
p_{\mathrm{Im}}(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}})
and
q(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}})
are real polynomials of
x
. Hence

P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x):=p_{\mathrm{Im}}(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}),\quad\quad Q(x):=q(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}).

are well defined and
\left(P(x)=f(x)+\mathrm{i}P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x),Q(x)\right)
satisfies the normalization condition,

1-f(x)^{2}=P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)^{2}+(1-x^{2})Q(x)^{2}.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is closed under additive inverse,

e(-z)=(-z)^{-d}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(-z-r_{i})=(-1)^{d}z^{-d}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})=(-1)^{d}e(z).

It leads to that
P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)
has parity
(d\bmod 2)
and
Q(x)
has parity
(d-1\bmod 2)
.

Another important observation is that
\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r<0
. It comes from

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z^{-1}-r_{i})=\alpha\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)z^{-2d}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(z-r_{i})(z-r_{i}^{-1})

(44)

and that the coefficient of
z^{2d}
in
\mathfrak{F}(z)
is negative. According to
Eq.

44
, one has that the coefficient of
z^{2d}
in
\mathfrak{F}(z)
is
\alpha\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)
. Therefore,

\alpha\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)<0\Longrightarrow\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)<0.

(45)

Since

\begin{split}\frac{e(z)-e(z^{-1})}{z-z^{-1}}&=\frac{1}{z-z^{-1}}\left[\left(1-\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)(z^{d}-z^{-d})+\sum_{i=1-d}^{d-1}e_{i}(z^{i}-z^{-i})\right]\\
&=\left(1-\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)\left(z^{d-1}+z^{-d+1}\right)+l.o..\end{split}

Here, we use
l.o.
to denote any linear combination of
z^{j}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
and
\left\lvert j\right\rvert<d-1
. By comparing with the coefficient of
x^{d-1}
in the monomial expansion for
Q(x)
,
\mathbf{q}_{d-1}
, one gets

\sqrt{\alpha}\left(1-\prod_{i=1}^{2d}r_{i}\right)=2^{1-d}\mathbf{q}_{d-1}.

(46)

In this way,
\text{deg}(Q)=d-1
,
\text{deg}(P)=d
and the leading coefficient of
Q(x)
is positive.
∎

4.2
Maximal solution

Among all possible choices of
\mathcal{D}
, we are interested in choosing
\mathcal{D}
to contain all roots of

within the unit disc and outside the unit disc.
As will be shown below, the associated complementary polynomials satisfy many desirapble properties. Let

,
\left(\bar{P}_{\mathrm{Im}},\bar{Q}\right)
denote the corresponding pair of complementary polynomials respectively. Moreover, throughout the paper, unless otherwise noted, we use

Lemma 18

(Maximal magnitude of leading coefficients)
.

The leading coefficients of the expansion of
Q
and
\bar{Q}
in the monomial basis have the largest magnitude among all possible complementary polynomials.

Proof.

Given any multiset
\mathcal{D}
satisfying the condition mentioned in

, note that
\mathfrak{F}(z)
can be represented as

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\beta z^{-2d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)(z-r^{-1})

for some
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. By checking the leading coefficient,
\beta=-\frac{\mathbf{f}^{2}_{d}}{4^{d}}
is a quantity fully determined by
f
.

Recall the proof of
Theorem

16
, we know that
\alpha\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r=\beta
by
Eq.

44
. Together with
Eq.

46
, we obtain

\mathbf{q}_{d-1}^{2}=4^{d-1}\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\left(K+\frac{1}{K}+2\right),

(47)

where
K:=-\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r>0
by

.

When
\mathcal{D}
contains all the roots within the unit disc or outside the unit disc,
\frac{1}{K}+K
is the largest among all the possibilities, that is, the leading coefficient of

and

has the largest magnitude.

The following lemma shows that
\left(P_{\mathrm{Im}},Q\right)
and

Lemma 19
.

The polynomials
\left(P_{\mathrm{Im}},Q\right)
and
\left(\bar{P}_{\mathrm{Im}},\bar{Q}\right)
are related to each other:

P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)=-\bar{P}_{\mathrm{Im}}(x),\quad\quad Q(x)=\bar{Q}(x).

(48)

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{D}:=\left\{r\text{ is a root of }\mathfrak{F}(z):\left\lvert r\right\rvert<1\right\}
, and

e(z):=z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r),\quad\bar{e}(z):=z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r^{-1}).

Then for some
\alpha,\bar{\alpha}>0
,

\mathfrak{F}(z)=\alpha e(z)e(z^{-1})=\bar{\alpha}\bar{e}(z)\bar{e}(z^{-1}).

(49)

Notice that

\begin{split}\bar{e}(z)&=z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r^{-1})=z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}\left(r^{-1}(rz-1)\right)=z^{d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}\left(r^{-1}(r-z^{-1})\right)\\
&=\left(\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r^{-1}\right)z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z^{-1}-r)=-\frac{1}{K}e(z^{-1}).\end{split}

(50)

where
K=-\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r
. Similarly,
\bar{e}(z^{-1})=-\frac{1}{K}e(z)
. Thus,
\bar{\alpha}=K^{2}\alpha
. By
Theorem

16
, we have

\begin{split}\bar{P}_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}}\frac{\bar{e}\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+\bar{e}\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2},\\
&=-\sqrt{\alpha K^{2}}\frac{1}{K}\frac{e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2}=-P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x),\end{split}

(51)

and

\begin{split}\bar{Q}\left(x\right)&=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}}\frac{\bar{e}\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)-\bar{e}\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\\
&=-\sqrt{\alpha K^{2}}\frac{1}{K}\frac{e\left(x-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)-e\left(x+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=Q(x).\end{split}

(52)

This proves the lemma.
∎

Due to this relation, we may only focus on the admissible pair

to contain all the roots of
\mathfrak{F}(z)

4.3
Relation to other constructive methods

There are two other methods for constructing complementary polynomials, which will be referred to as the GSLW method
[
6
]
and the Haah method
[
8
]
, respectively.
The two methods are equivalent. The GSLW method solves the problem without using Laurent polynomials. The Haah method first points out that the decomposition can be viewed more naturally in terms of Laurent polynomials.

The Haah method considers

1-P_{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}Q_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}

(55)

and the corresponding decomposition:

1-P_{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}Q_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\right)^{2}=\alpha\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)(z^{-1}-r)

(56)

where
\mathcal{D}
contains all the roots within the unit disc.

In our construction, we may omit the term
Q_{\mathrm{Im}}
due to the symmetry constraint. However, one can check that our method is also suitable for the case that
Q_{\mathrm{Im}}\neq 0
by simply replacing
\mathfrak{F}(z)
by
Eq.

55
. By the same method as that in
Theorem

16
, it can shown that any admissible complementary polynomial
(P_{\mathrm{Re}},Q_{\mathrm{Re}})
must take the form of
Eq.

43
, where
Q
is replaced by
Q_{\mathrm{Re}}
. Hence our method can be generalized to find all admissible complementary polynomials, by going through all possible
e(z)=z^{-d}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)
. In particular, the Haah method considers the special case that
\mathcal{D}
contains all the root of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
within the unit disc, and therefore does not generate all admissible complementary polynomials.

We would like to clarify that Ref
[
8
]
states that we can construct
P_{\mathrm{Im}}
and
Q
as long as the joint union of a conjugate-closed multiset
\mathcal{D}
and its reciprocal
\{\frac{1}{r}:r\in\mathcal{D}\}
is the list of all the roots of
Eq.

55
, if there is no parity requirement for
P_{\mathrm{Im}}
and
Q
. After imposing the parity condition, according to
Theorem

16
, another necessary condition for suitable complementary polynomials is that
\mathcal{D}
is closed under additive inverse. Without it, the parity condition may not hold. This can be verified by the proof of
Theorem

16
.

5
Local convergence

We prove
Theorem

5
in this section. Throughout this section, polynomials are expanded in the Chebyshev basis without otherwise noted. This is more convenient than the expansion in the monomial basis.

According to the definition of the Chebyshev norms in
Section

2.1
and the convention of the coefficients in
Section

2.2
, we specifically list the following relations which exploit the weighted orthogonality and will be frequently used in the proof,

\begin{split}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{T}^{2}=f^{2}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}f^{2}_{k},\quad\quad\left\lVert P_{\mathrm{Im}}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}s_{k}^{2},\quad\quad\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{U}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}{q_{k}}^{2}.\end{split}

(57)

By integrating the point-wise normalization condition
f(x)^{2}+P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)^{2}+(1-x^{2})Q(x)^{2}=1
with the weight function
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
, we obtain the following Parseval’s equality:

f^{2}_{0}+s_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(f^{2}_{k}+s_{k}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}{q_{k}}^{2}=1.

(58)

5.1
Sketch of the proof

We first give a sketch of the proof as a road map. For simplicity, we assume
d
is odd in the sketch, while the proof works for any
d
.

1.

We first show that each element of
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
is bounded by
\frac{\pi}{4}
by properly restricting
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

2.

We further upper bound the distance
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}
by
\left\lvert\sin\left(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right\rvert^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}
. It follows the inequality
\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\left\lvert\sin(x)\right\rvert
which holds for any
\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. The bound in the first step ensures that the inequality can be invoked.

3.

We upper bound the phase factors by the leading Chebyshev coefficients in the recursion sequence of polynomials. The upper bound turns out to be

\begin{split}&\left\lvert\sin\left(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{8}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2},\\
&\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{8}{3}\left(\left({q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}}\right)^{2}-\left({q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}}\right)^{2}\right),\quad 1\leq\ell\leq\widetilde{d}-1.\end{split}

4.

Combining the previous results, we finally get
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}+1-q_{d-1}^{2}\right)
. The upper bound can be further simplified to be
\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}
by exploiting the estimate to
q_{d-1}
(see
Lemma

23
). Then the statement in
Theorem

5
follows.

5.2
Recursion relation in terms of Chebyshev coefficients

In this subsection, we will convert the recursion relation in terms of monomial coefficients to that in terms of Chebyshev coefficients. Furthermore, we will derive some useful properties which will be used as intermediate steps in the proof of
Theorem

5
.

Let
(P,Q)\in\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]\times\mathbb{R}_{d-1}[x]
be a pair of polynomials satisfying the conditions in
Theorem

1
. The symmetric reduction procedures yield a sequence of polynomials
(P^{(\ell)},Q^{(\ell)})\in\mathbb{C}_{d-2\ell}[x]\times\mathbb{R}_{d-2\ell-1}[x]
satisfying the conditions and an associated sequence of phase factors
\phi_{\ell}
so that
e^{2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell}}=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(\ell)}_{d-2\ell}}{\mathbf{q}^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}}
. In particular,
(P^{(0)},Q^{(0)})=(P,Q)
. An important recursion relation generating the coefficients of new polynomials in the monomial basis is given in
Eq.

32
. For convenience, we restate it as follows: if
d\geq 3
, then

\begin{split}&\mathbf{p}_{d-2}^{(1)}=\frac{\mathbf{q}_{d-1}}{4}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}_{d-1}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[\mathbf{p}_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]\right\rvert^{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Im}[\mathbf{p}_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}],\\
&\mathbf{q}_{d-3}^{(1)}=\frac{\mathbf{q}_{d-1}}{4}+\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}_{d-1}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[\mathbf{p}_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]\right\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

Here, recall that the boldface symbols refer to the coefficients in the monomial basis. We will first rewrite this relation using the Chebyshev basis. As a remark, it suffices to consider the case when
\ell=0
, and the general case can be recovered by substituting
(P,Q,P^{(1)},Q^{(1)},\phi_{0})\leftarrow(P^{(\ell)},Q^{(\ell)},P^{(\ell+1)},Q^{(\ell+1)},\phi_{\ell})
in the arguments as long as
\deg\left(P^{(\ell)}\right)\geq 3
.

For simplicity, let us denote the Chebyshev polynomial in the monomial basis as
T_{d}(x)=2^{d-1}x^{d}+t_{d}x^{d-2}+l.o.
where we use
l.o.
to denote any polynomial of degree no more than
d-3
and
t_{d}
is a real number whose exact value is irrelevant in the proof.

Expanding the polynomials in mononial basis and Chebyshev basis, we have

\begin{split}&P(x)e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\mathbf{p}_{k}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}x^{k}=e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}\mathbf{p}_{d}x^{d}+e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}\mathbf{p}_{d-2}x^{d-2}+l.o.\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}T_{k}(x)=e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}2^{d-1}p_{d}x^{d}+\left(e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d}t_{d}+e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d-2}2^{d-3}\right)x^{d-2}+l.o..\end{split}

Then, by comparing the coefficients of terms
x^{d}
and
x^{d-2}
, we have
\mathbf{p}_{d}=2^{d-1}p_{d}
and

\begin{split}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}\mathbf{p}_{d-2}=e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d}t_{d}+e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d-2}2^{d-3}=q_{d-1}t_{d}+e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d-2}2^{d-3}.\end{split}

In the last equality, we use

e^{2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{d}}{\mathbf{q}_{d-1}}=\frac{2^{d-1}p_{d}}{2^{d-1}q_{d-1}}=\frac{p_{d}}{q_{d-1}}\Rightarrow e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}p_{d}=q_{d-1}\in\mathbb{R}.

An important observation is

\mathrm{Im}[\mathbf{p}_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]=2^{d-3}\mathrm{Im}[p_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}],

(59)

which follows
q_{d-1}t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Plugging the previous equations in terms of Chebyshev coefficients in
Eq.

32
, we have the following recursion relation

\begin{split}&p_{d-2}^{(1)}=q_{d-1}-\frac{1}{4q_{d-1}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]\right\rvert^{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Im}[p_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}],\\
&q_{d-3}^{(1)}=q_{d-1}+\frac{1}{4q_{d-1}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]\right\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

(60)

The second equality implies the following sign preserving property and the monotonicity

\text{sgn}(q_{d-1})=\text{sgn}(q_{d-3}^{(1)})\text{, and }\left\lvert q_{d-1}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert q_{d-3}^{(1)}\right\rvert.

Regarding the next phase factor
\phi_{1}
, its defined equation is converted similarly to Chebyshev basis

e^{2\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}}=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(1)}_{d-2}}{\mathbf{q}^{(1)}_{d-3}}=\frac{p^{(1)}_{d-2}}{q^{(1)}_{d-3}}.

Furthermore, we can use the derived recursion relation in
Eq.

60
to obtain

\sin\left(2\phi_{1}\right)=\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{p^{(1)}_{d-2}}{q^{(1)}_{d-3}}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{Im}[p_{d-2}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}]}{q_{d-3}^{(1)}}.

The derived results can be generalized to
\left(P^{(\ell)},Q^{(\ell)}\right)
where
1\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
(
\hat{d}
is defined by
Eq.

35
). We summarize these results as the following theorem.

Theorem 22
.

Given
\left(P(x),Q(x)\right)
satisfying the assumption in
Theorem

1
and
\Phi=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{1},\phi_{0})
being a set of symmetric phase factors in
\mathcal{D}_{d}
such that
Eq.

8
is satisfied, the following statements hold.

1.

For any
1\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
, the leading coefficients of
P^{(\ell)}
and
Q^{(\ell)}
satisfy the recursion relation

\begin{split}&p^{(\ell)}_{d-2\ell}=q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}-\frac{1}{4q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}],\\
&q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}=q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}+\frac{1}{4q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

(61)

2.

For any
0\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
,

e^{2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell}}=\frac{p^{(\ell)}_{d-2\ell}}{q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}},

(62)

and if
1\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
,

\sin\left(2\phi_{\ell}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]}{q_{d-1-2\ell}^{(\ell)}}.

(63)

3.

If
d
is even,
e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}}=p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-1)}
and

\cos(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1})=q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)},\ \sin\left(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)=-\mathrm{i}p^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{0}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}.

(64)

4.

(The monotonicity of the leading coefficient of
Q^{(\ell)}
in the Chebyshev basis)

If
q^{(0)}_{d-1}>0
, then

0<q^{(0)}_{d-1}\leq q^{(1)}_{d-3}\leq\cdots\leq q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}\leq\cdots\leq q^{(\hat{d})}_{d-1-2\hat{d}}\leq 1.

(65)

Proof.

The statements 1, 2 and 4 follow the previous discussion.
Hence, we only need to verify
Eq.

64
.

Note that when
\ell=\widetilde{d}-2
, we have
\deg\left(P^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right)=2
and

\begin{split}&\braket{0|e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}Z}W(x)e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}}W(x)e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}Z}|0}=e^{2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}\left(2\cos\left(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)x^{2}-e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}}\right)\\
&=P^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}(x)=p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}T_{2}(x)+p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}T_{0}(x)=2p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}x^{2}-p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}+p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}.\end{split}

The first equality is obtained by direct computation. Comparing the constant terms, we have

\begin{split}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}}&=\left(p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}\right)^{*}-\left(p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}\right)^{*}\\
&=q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}-\left(p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}\right)^{*}.\end{split}

Taking the modulus on both sides, we get

\begin{split}1&=\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}-\mathrm{Re}[p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}]\right)^{2}+\left(\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{0}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}]\right)^{2}\\
&=\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right)^{2}+\left\lvert p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert^{2}-2q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\mathrm{Re}[p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}].\end{split}

(66)

Applying Parseval’s equality derived from
\left\lvert P^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}(x)\right\rvert^{2}+(1-x^{2})\left\lvert Q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}(x)\right\rvert^{2}=1
, we get

\left\lvert p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right)^{2}=1.

Note that
\left\lvert p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert=\left\lvert p_{2}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert
, we have

\left\lvert p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right\rvert^{2}+\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\right)^{2}=1.

(67)

Plugging the last equality into
Eq.

66
, we obtain
\mathrm{Re}[p_{0}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}]=0
since
q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\neq 0
. Therefore,
p^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{0}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}}
is pure imaginary which yields
Eq.

64
.
∎

5.3
Bounding Chebyshev coefficients of the maximal solution

In this subsection, we assume that
(P,Q)
is the admissible pair corresponding to the maximal solution. We will derive bounds on the leading coefficients of
Q
and
P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)
in the Chebyshev basis,
i.e.
,
q_{d-1}
and
s_{d}
respectively.

Lemma 23
.

The leading coefficient of
Q
in the Chebyshev basis satisfies the following estimate:
1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}\leq q_{d-1}^{2}\leq 1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{T}+\frac{1}{2}f_{d}^{2}
.

Proof.

Expanding the polynomials in the monomial basis, the normalization condition reduces to

f(x)^{2}+P_{\mathrm{Im}}(x)^{2}+(1-x^{2})Q(x)^{2}=\left(\mathbf{f}_{d}^{2}+\mathbf{s}_{d}^{2}-\mathbf{q}_{d-1}^{2}\right)x^{2d}+l.o.=1,

where
l.o.
denotes any polynomial of degree less than
2d
.
It implies that
\mathbf{f}_{d}^{2}+{\mathbf{s}_{d}}^{2}=\mathbf{q}_{d-1}^{2}
which is further
f_{d}^{2}+{s_{d}}^{2}=q_{d-1}^{2}
. Hence, we have

\begin{split}q_{d-1}^{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(f^{2}_{d}+s_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{2}\right)\\
&=1-\left(f_{0}^{2}+s_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(f^{2}_{k}+s_{k}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}q_{k}^{2}\right)\\
&\leq 1-\left(f_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}f^{2}_{k}\right)=1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{T}+\frac{1}{2}f_{d}^{2}.\\
\end{split}

In the second line, we exploit
Eq.

58
, the Parseval’s equality derived from the normalization condition.

To lower bound
q_{d-1}
, we first remark that
Eq.

47
can be rewritten as

q_{d-1}=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\left(K+\frac{1}{K}+2\right),

(68)

where
K:=-\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}r>0
, and
\mathcal{D}
contains all roots of
\mathfrak{F}(z)
within the unit disc for the maximal solution.

Given a polynomial fully factored into
g(z)=\gamma\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-a_{i})\in\mathbb{C}[z]
, its
Mahler measure

[
15
]
is defined as

M(g):=\left\lvert\gamma\right\rvert\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max\{\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert,1\}.

This quantity is equal to the geometric mean of
\left\lvert g(z)\right\rvert
on the unit circle by using Jensen’s formula in
[
10
]

M(g)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ln{\left\lvert g(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right\rvert}\mathrm{d}\theta\right).

(69)

Remarkably, note that
\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\frac{1}{K}
is the Mahler measure of a polynomial
h(z)
defined as

h(z):=\beta\prod_{r\in\mathcal{D}}(z-r)(z-r^{-1})=z^{2d}\mathfrak{F}(z).

(70)

Therefore, one has

\begin{split}\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\frac{1}{K}&=M(h)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ln{\left\lvert h(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right\rvert}\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ln{\left(1-f(\cos{\theta})^{2}\right)}\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\\
&\geq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ln{\left(1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right)}\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\\
&=1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.\end{split}

(71)

To conclude,

q_{d-1}^{2}=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\left(K+\frac{1}{K}+2\right)\geq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\frac{1}{K}\geq 1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2},

(72)

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 24

(Convergence of the maximal solution towards
\Phi^{0}
)
.

The derived bounds on
q_{d-1}
consequentially yields the bounds on
s_{d}
. Using the equality
f_{d}^{2}+s_{d}^{2}=q_{d-1}^{2}
, we get the following estimate

1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}-f_{d}^{2}\leq s_{d}^{2}\leq 1-\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{T}-\frac{1}{2}f_{d}^{2}.

(73)

Combining the derived results, the remaining Chebyshev coefficients of
P_{\mathrm{Im}}
and
Q
can be upper bounded as follows

\begin{split}&s_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}s_{k}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}q_{k}^{2}\\
(\text{Parseval's equality})\ &=1-f_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}s_{d}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}q_{d-1}^{2}\\
(f_{d}^{2}+s_{d}^{2}=q_{d-1}^{2})\ &=1-q_{d-1}^{2}-f_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}f_{k}^{2}\\
(\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lma:coef_q_bound}})\ &\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}-f_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}f_{k}^{2}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.\end{split}

(74)

As a consequence of the upper bound, we have
s_{i},q_{j}\to 0\ \forall i\in[d],j\in[d-1]
as
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
.
Remark

21
implies that
s_{d}>0
. Together with
f_{d}^{2}+s_{d}^{2}=q_{d-1}^{2}
, it follows that
s_{d}=q_{d-1}
in that limit since
f_{d}\to 0
, and the normalization condition yields
s_{d}=q_{d-1}=1
when the leading coefficient of
Q
is fixed to be positive.

To conclude, as
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
, the maximal solution exhibits a limit
(P,Q)\to(\mathrm{i}T_{d},U_{d-1})
which agrees with the polynomials generated from the set of phase factors
\Phi^{0}=(\frac{\pi}{4},0,\cdots,0,\frac{\pi}{4})
.

5.4
Proof of

In this subsection, we will prove

Lemma 25
.

When

Proof.

The first statement directly follows

To show the second statement, we first notice that according to

When
1\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
, according to

\cos\left(2\phi_{\ell}\right)=\frac{1}{q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}}\left(q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}-\frac{1}{4q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Using Parseval’s equality, the normalization condition

\begin{split}1&=\left\lvert p^{(\ell-1)}_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d-2\ell+2}\left\lvert p_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{d-2\ell+1}\left(q_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right)^{2}\\

Furthermore, the cancellation of the leading order in the normalization condition implies

Using the first statement frequently, we have

\begin{split}&\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}\leq\left\lvert p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}\right\rvert^{2}\leq 2\left(1-\left(q_{d-2\ell+1}^{(\ell-1)}\right)^{2}\right)\\

(77)

Plugging

77

76

. Hence,
\left\lvert\phi_{\ell}\right\rvert<\frac{\pi}{4}

If

\cos(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1})=q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}=q_{1}^{(\hat{d})}\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.

Here,

when

.
∎

The second statement of the previous lemma ensures that the inequality

.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

We first estimate
\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}

\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Re}[p_{d}]}{q_{d-1}}\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{f_{d}^{2}}{q_{d-1}^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{3}f_{d}^{2}\leq\frac{8}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2},

where we use the inequality
f_{d}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}

For any

\begin{split}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}&=\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]}{q_{d-1-2\ell}^{(\ell)}}\right\rvert^{2}\\
\text{(\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lma:supplement} 1.})&\leq\frac{4}{3}\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}\\

Here, the monotonicity in

If

Therefore, we have

\begin{split}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&=\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\phi_{\ell}^{2}\\

If

is even, we have to treat

separately. Using

(79)

Similarly,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{8}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left(\left(q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}\right)^{2}-\left(q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{8}{3}\left(q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{1}\right)^{2}-\frac{8}{3}\left(q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right)^{2}.

Furthermore, combining the derived results, one has

\begin{split}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&=\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\phi_{\ell}^{2}+\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\left\lvert\sin\left(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\left(q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{1}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\left(q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{1}\right)^{2}\right)\\
(\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{thm:cheby_version} 4})&\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}-\frac{2}{3}\left(q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right)^{2}\right)\\
(\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lma:supplement} 1})&\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{3}\pi^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.\end{split}

In conclusion, we have

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}.

∎

6.1
Hessian matrix at the maximal solution is positive definite

We first show that the Hessian matrix at the initial point
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
is positive definite.

Theorem 26
.

At the initial point
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}:=\left(\frac{\pi}{4},0,\cdots,0\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d}}
, the Hessian matrix of the optimization problem
Eq.

5
is

\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})^{\top}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}4I&,\text{if }d\text{ is odd},\\
\mathrm{diag}\{4,\cdots,4,2\}&,\text{if }d\text{ is even}.\end{array}\right.

(80)

Proof.

According to
[
5
, Lemma 3]
,

\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle 0|U(x,\widetilde{\Phi})|0\rangle\right]=-\mathrm{Im}\left[\langle 0|e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}Z}U(x,\widetilde{\Phi})e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}Z}|0\rangle\right],

which suggests us to extract
\frac{\pi}{4}
from the phase factors at the edges and consider
e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}Z}U(x,\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}Z}=W(x)^{d}
. The integer power of the matrix
W(x):=e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}
exactly generates the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind as its upper-left matrix element. Furthermore, its upper-left is real. The claim follows the property of the exponential map of Pauli matrices
W(x)^{d}=e^{\mathrm{i}d\arccos(x)X}=\cos(d\arccos(x))+\mathrm{i}\sin(d\arccos(x))X
. Then, noting that
\braket{0|X|0}=0
, it yields
\braket{0|W(x)^{d}|0}=\cos(d\arccos(x))=T_{d}(x)
.

To proceed, let us consider the monomial
M^{d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}}(x):=W(x)^{d_{1}}(\mathrm{i}Z)W(x)^{d_{2}}(\mathrm{i}Z)W(x)^{d_{3}}
for any
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}\geq 0
. We will show that the upper-left element
\braket{0|M^{d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}}(x)|0}
is real. Following the anticommutation relation
ZXZ=-X
, a useful equality can be derived
ZW(x)Z=e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)ZXZ}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}=W(x)^{-1}
. Consequentially, by moving
Z
gates to the same site for cancellation, it can be shown that
\braket{0|M^{d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}}(x)|0}=-T_{\left\lvert d_{1}+d_{3}-d_{2}\right\rvert}(x)
is real.

Note that taking second-order derivative on the unitary defined in
Equation

5
at
\Phi^{0}
results in an integer-coefficient superposition of monomials of the defined form. To illustrate, given an odd integer
d=2\widetilde{d}-1
and
0\leq i<j<\widetilde{d}
, one can verify that

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi_{i}\partial\phi_{j}}U(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\bigg|_{\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}}=M^{i,j-i,d-j}(x)+M^{i,d-j-i,j}(x)+M^{d-j,j-i,i}(x)+M^{j,d-j-i,i}(x).

Then, by taking the imaginary component, the second-order derivative
g_{ij}
is vanishing at
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
. Thus, the second term in the right-hand side of
Equation

38
vanishes since
g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=0
.

When
d=2(\widetilde{d}-1)
is even,
g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=-2\mathrm{Im}\left[\braket{0|\mathrm{i}W(x)^{i}ZW(x)^{d-i}|0}\right]=-2T_{d-2i}(x)
for
i=0,\cdots,\widetilde{d}-2
and
g_{\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=-1
. According to the discrete orthogonality of Chebyshev nodes, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g_{i}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})g_{j}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=\widetilde{d}\left(2\delta_{ij}-\delta_{i,\widetilde{d}-1}\delta_{j,\widetilde{d}-1}\right).

When
d=2\widetilde{d}-1
is odd, we have similarly
\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g_{i}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})g_{j}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=2\widetilde{d}\delta_{ij}
.

Following
Eq.

38
, the Hessian matrix at
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
takes the form in the theorem which completes the proof.
∎

To show that the Hessian matrix is bounded from below, we need the following lemma regarding the perturbation analysis to the singular values of a matrix.

Lemma 27

(
[
7
, Corollary 8.6.2]
)
.

Given A, A+E
\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\geq n
, we have

\left\lvert\sigma_{k}(A+E)-\sigma_{k}(A)\right\rvert\leq\sigma_{0}(E)=\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2},\quad\forall k\in[n].

Then we bound the perturbation to the Jacobian matrix via that of the phase factors.

Lemma 28
.

Given any symmetric phase factors
\Phi
of length
d+1
, let
E:=A(\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-A(\widetilde{\Phi})
. Then
\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2}\leq 4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}
.

Proof.

Recall that in the proof of
Theorem

26
, we have shown that taking the derivative
w.r.t.

\phi_{i}
yields terms by adding an
\mathrm{i}Z
multiplication left to
e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}Z}
. Note that
\mathrm{i}Z=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}Z}
, it shifts the
i
-th site by
\frac{\pi}{2}
which essentially breaks the symmetry constraint. That means we have to use asymmetric set of phase factors to characterize the derivative. To capture this, we define the evaluation map of asymmetric phase factors as follows. Given an asymmetric set of phase factors
\Phi_{\text{as}}:=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{d})
, we define

U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}):=e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}\cdots e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{d-1}Z}e^{\mathrm{i}\arccos(x)X}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{d}Z}.

We use a superscript
(as)
to distinguish it with
U(x,\widetilde{\Phi})
which takes the reduced phase factors
\widetilde{\Phi}
of a symmetric set as argument.

When
d
is odd, we define an asymmetric set of phase factors as

\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)}:=\left(\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{i-1},\phi_{i}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{i+1},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\cdots,\phi_{i+1},\phi_{i},\phi_{i-1},\cdots,\phi_{0}\right)

which unrolls the full set of phase factors and add a
\frac{\pi}{2}
shift on the
i
-th site. As a remark, the shifted site
i
can be greater than
\widetilde{d}
which adds a phase shift on the right half.

Taking derivative yields that

\begin{split}g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})&=\mathrm{Re}\left[\braket{0|\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{i}}U(x,\widetilde{\Phi})|0}\right]=\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})|0}]+\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(d-i)})|0}]\\
&=\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})|0}]+\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})^{\top}|0}]=2\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})|0}].\end{split}

Due to the unitarity, we have

\left\lvert g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rvert\leq 2,\quad\forall i\in[\widetilde{d}].

To further take the second derivative, let us first introduce a double-shifted asymmetric set of phase factors. When
0\leq i<j<\widetilde{d}
,

\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i,j)}:=\left(\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{i-1},\phi_{i}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{i+1},\cdots,\phi_{j-1},\phi_{j}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{j+1},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\cdots,\phi_{0}\right).

When
0\leq i=j<\widetilde{d}
,

\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i,i)}:=\left(\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{i-1},\phi_{i}+\pi,\phi_{i+1},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\cdots,\phi_{i+1},\phi_{i},\phi_{i-1},\cdots,\phi_{0}\right).

Similarly, when
i\text{ or }j\geq\widetilde{d}
, the phase shift is added on the right half accordingly.

With the defined notation, we are able to compute the second derivative as

\begin{split}g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})&:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{j}}g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})=2\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{j}}U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})|0}]\\
&=2\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i,j)})|0}]+2\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Phi_{\text{as}}^{(i,d-j)})|0}].\end{split}

Due to the unitarity, we have

\left\lvert g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rvert\leq 4,\quad\forall i,j\in[\widetilde{d}].

Therefore, for any given odd integer
d
and symmetric
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we have

\left\lVert\nabla g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{j\in[\widetilde{d}]}\left\lvert g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rvert^{2}}\leq 4\sqrt{\widetilde{d}},\quad\forall i\in[\widetilde{d}].

(81)

When
d
is even, the full set of phase factors is
(\phi_{0},...\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2},...,\phi_{0})
. The only difference in the unrolled structure comparing to that in the odd case is that there is a single unpaired phase factor
\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}
at the middle. Therefore, when the derivative only involves
\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2}
, the analysis is exactly the same as that when
d
is odd.

When
i=\widetilde{d}-1
, taking derivative
w.r.t.

\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}
is equivalently shifting this site by
\frac{\pi}{2}
, namely,

g_{\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})=\mathrm{Re}[\braket{0|U(x,\widetilde{\Psi})|0}]=g(x,\widetilde{\Psi}),

where

\Psi:=\Phi+\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_{\widetilde{d}-1}=(\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{\widetilde{d}-2},\cdots,\phi_{0}).

Note that
\Psi
is a set of symmetric phase factors (and therefore we use a different notation). Then, taking the second derivative on
g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})
is equivalent to taking the first derivative on
g(x,\widetilde{\Psi})
whose analysis is the same as that in the previous case. Therefore,

g_{i,\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})=g_{i}(x,\widetilde{\Psi})=2\mathrm{Re}\left[\braket{0|U^{\text{(as)}}(x,\Psi_{\text{as}}^{(i)})|0}\right],\ \forall i\in[\widetilde{d}-1],

and

g_{\widetilde{d}-1,\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})=g_{\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Psi})=-g(x,\widetilde{\Phi}).

These results lead to the inequality

\left\lvert g_{i,\widetilde{d}-1}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rvert\leq 2,\quad\forall i\in[\widetilde{d}].

Thus, the inequality in
Eq.

81
also holds when
d
is even.

To conclude, for any given integer
d
and symmetric
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we obtain a uniform upper bound of the gradient of
g_{j}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})

\left\lVert\nabla g_{j}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{\widetilde{d}},\ \forall j\in[\widetilde{d}],\ \forall x\in[-1,1].

(82)

Following the mean value theorem, there exists
t\in(0,1)
so that for any
j\in[\widetilde{d}]
and
x\in[-1,1]

\left\lvert g_{j}(x,\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-g_{j}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\nabla g_{j}(x,\widetilde{\Phi}+t\bm{\epsilon})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}.

Furthermore

\left\lVert A(\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-A(\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left(g_{j}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-g_{j}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})\right)^{2}}\leq 4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}.

Then, the theorem follows

\left\lVert A(\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-A(\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert A(\widetilde{\Phi}+\bm{\epsilon})-A(\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{F}\leq 4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}.

∎

Then the perturbation analysis to the singular value of the Jacobian matrix is obtained by directly applying
Lemma

27
.

Corollary 29
.

If
\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}
, then

\frac{4}{5}\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}\leq\sigma_{\min}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}+\bm{\epsilon}))\leq\sigma_{\max}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}+\bm{\epsilon}))\leq\left(\frac{1}{5}+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}.

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

27
, we have

\left\lvert\sigma_{k}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}+\bm{\epsilon}))-\sigma_{k}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}))\right\rvert\leq 4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}.

(83)

As a consequence of
Theorem

26
,
\sigma_{\max}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}))=\sqrt{2\widetilde{d}}
and

\sigma_{\text{min}}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}&,\text{ when }d\text{ is even},\\
\sqrt{2\widetilde{d}}&,\text{ when }d\text{ is odd}.\end{array}\right.

When
\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}
, we have

\sigma_{\min}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}+\bm{\epsilon}))\geq\sigma_{\min}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}))-\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2}\geq\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}-4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{4}{5}\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}.

(84)

and

\sigma_{\max}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}+\bm{\epsilon}))\leq\sigma_{\max}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}))+\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{2\widetilde{d}}+4\widetilde{d}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{5}+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}.

(85)

∎

Recall that the Hessian matrix at the maximal solution satisfies
\text{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{\top}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})
. The positive definiteness of this matrix follows by combining the previous results with
Theorem

5
.

Theorem 30
.

Let
\Phi^{*}
be the optimal phase factors corresponding to the maximal solution. If
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}
, then
\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})
is positive definite and

\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})\right)\geq\frac{32}{25}.

(86)

Proof.

Theorem

5
implies that
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}
. As a consequence of
Corollary

29
, we have
\sigma_{\text{min}}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}))\geq\frac{4}{5}\sqrt{\widetilde{d}}
. Therefore

\lambda_{\text{min}}(\text{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}))=\lambda_{\text{min}}\left(\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{\top}A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})\right)=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sigma_{\text{min}}^{2}(A(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}))\geq\frac{32}{25},

which completes the proof.
∎

6.2
Lower bounding the distance to the initial point

In the last subsection, we show that the Hessian matrix is positive definite at the maximal solution. To show the positive definiteness of the Hessian matrix in a neighborhood of the optima, we need the following lower bound as an intermediate step.

Theorem 31
.

Given any symmetric phase factor
\widetilde{\Phi}
satisfying
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, the following inequality holds

\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}\leq\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}.

(87)

Proof.

We denote the expansion of the polynomial
g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})
in the Chebyshev basis as

g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})=\sum_{k=0}^{d}g_{k}(\widetilde{\Phi})T_{k}(x).

For simplicity, we drop the
\widetilde{\Phi}
-dependence in the Chebyshev coefficient
g_{k}
(in particular,
g_{k}
is not the derivative of
g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})
with respect to
\phi_{k}
as in the previous section).

Then we apply
Eq.

26
to estimate
q_{d-1}
, the coefficient of
Q(x)
. Notice that
\cos(\phi_{\ell})
is positive for any
1\leq\ell\leq\widetilde{d}-1
, which follows
\left\lvert\phi_{\ell}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}<\frac{\pi}{4}
. We use
Eq.

26
and the inequality
\cos(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1})\geq\cos(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1})^{2}
when
d
is even, and obtain

q_{d-1}^{(0)}\geq\prod_{\ell=1}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\cos^{2}(\phi_{\ell})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\left(1-\sin^{2}(\phi_{\ell})\right)\geq 1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\phi_{\ell}^{2}\geq 1-\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(88)

Hence
\deg(Q)=d-1
and the leading coefficient of
Q
is positive. Now we are able to apply
Theorems

22
and
23
and have

\sqrt{1-\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}\leq q^{(0)}_{d-1}\leq q^{(1)}_{d-3}\leq\cdots\leq q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}\leq\cdots\leq q^{(\hat{d})}_{d-1-2\hat{d}}\leq 1.

According to the reduction procedure, the leading Chebyshev coefficients and the phase factor are connected as follows

\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Re}[p_{d}]}{q_{d-1}}\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{g_{d}^{2}}{q_{d-1}^{2}}\geq g_{d}^{2},

(89)

where the last inequality follows
\left\lvert q_{d-1}\right\rvert\leq 1
.

Following
Eq.

36
, for any
1\leq\ell\leq\hat{d}
, we have

\begin{split}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}&=\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]}{q_{d-1-2\ell}^{(\ell)}}\right\rvert^{2}\geq\left\lvert\mathrm{Im}[p^{(\ell-1)}_{d-2\ell}e^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{\ell-1}}]\right\rvert^{2}\\
&=4q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\left(q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}-q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\right)\geq 4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}-q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\right),\\
\end{split}

where the last inequality follows the monotonicity in
Theorem

22
.

Now we are going to show

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right),

(90)

where
g_{d}
denotes the leading Chebyshev coefficient of
g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})
.

When
d
is odd, we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}\geq 4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left(q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}-q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\right)=4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(q_{0}^{(\hat{d})}-q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right)=4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right).

Here, we have used the fact that
q_{0}^{(\hat{d})}=1
obtained from the proof of
Lemma

14
.
Hence

\begin{split}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&=\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\phi_{\ell}^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right).\end{split}

When
d
is even, the dangling unpaired phase factor
\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}
has to be analyzed individually

\left\lvert\sin\left(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)\right\rvert^{2}=1-\left({q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{1}}\right)^{2}.

Furthermore, we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}\geq 4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left(q^{(\ell)}_{d-1-2\ell}-q^{(\ell-1)}_{d+1-2\ell}\right)=4q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}-q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right).

Unlike the odd case, the coefficient
q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}
is not necessary equal to
1
when
d
is even. Then

\begin{split}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&=\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\phi_{\ell}^{2}+\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\hat{d}}\left\lvert\sin(2\phi_{\ell})\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert\sin\left(\phi_{\widetilde{d}-1}\right)\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}-q_{d-1}^{(0)}\right)+1-\left(q^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}_{1}\right)^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right)+\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right)\left(1-{q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}}\right)+q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}\left(1-{q_{1}^{(\widetilde{d}-2)}}\right)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+q_{d-1}^{(0)}\left(1-{q_{d-1}^{(0)}}\right).\end{split}

According to
Eqs.

88
and
23
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\leq q_{d-1}^{(0)}\leq\sqrt{1-\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g_{d}^{2}}
. It implies that

\begin{split}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}&\geq\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}+\sqrt{1-\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g_{d}^{2}}-1+\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g_{d}^{2}\\
&\geq\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}-\frac{\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g_{d}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g_{d}^{2}}+1}\\
&\geq\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}g_{d}^{2}-\frac{2\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-g_{d}^{2}}{3}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{3}\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}.\end{split}

∎

6.3
Local strong convexity of the optimization problem

Now we are going to present the proof of
Theorem

6
, where we use
Corollary

29
and the following lemma regarding the perturbative analysis to the eigenvalues of a matrix.

Lemma 32

(
[
7
, Theorem 8.1.4]

Wielandt–Hoffman
)
.

If A, A+E
\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are symmetric matrices, then

\sum_{k\in[n]}\left(\lambda_{k}(A+E)-\lambda_{k}(A)\right)^{2}\leq\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Although Weyl’s inequality may give a sharper estimate about the perturbation of eigenvalues, we adopt
Lemma

32
in our analysis. That is because the former exploits the operator norm of the perturbation of matrix, which is hard to estimate in our problem.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Recall that

F(\widetilde{\Phi})=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\left(A^{\top}(\widetilde{\Phi})A(\widetilde{\Phi})\right){+}R(\widetilde{\Phi}),

(91)

where
R_{ij}:=\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left(g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x_{k})\right)g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})
. The residual term is estimated as follows

\begin{split}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}&=\sum_{i=0}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\left(\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left(g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x_{k})\right)g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{4}{\widetilde{d}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\widetilde{d}-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}\left(g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x_{k})\right)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})^{2}\right)\\
&\leq 8\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}f(x_{k})^{2}\right)\max_{i,j}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}}g_{ij}(x_{k},\widetilde{\Phi})^{2}\right)\\
&=8\widetilde{d}^{2}\left(\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}+\left\lVert f(x)\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)\max_{i,j}\left\lVert g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}^{2}.\end{split}

(92)

Here, the last line uses the discrete orthogonality of Chebyshev polynomial.

Then, we have the following inequality

\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 128\widetilde{d}^{2}\left(3\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{24}{25}\left(1+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\right),

(93)

where the first inequality uses
Theorem

31
, the fact
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{T}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
, and its consequence
\left\lVert g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{T}\leq\left\lVert g_{ij}(x,\widetilde{\Phi})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4
derived in the proof of
Lemma

28
.

Then, applying
Lemma

32
, one has

\begin{split}&\lambda_{\min}(\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}))\geq\lambda_{\min}(\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}A(\widetilde{\Phi})^{\top}A(\widetilde{\Phi}))-\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}\geq\frac{32}{25}-\sqrt{\frac{24}{25}\left(1+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\right)}\geq\frac{1}{4},\\
&\lambda_{\max}(\mathrm{Hess}(\widetilde{\Phi}))\leq\lambda_{\max}(\frac{2}{\widetilde{d}}A(\widetilde{\Phi})^{\top}A(\widetilde{\Phi}))+\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{5}+\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{24}{25}\left(1+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\right)}\leq\frac{25}{4}.\end{split}

(94)

∎

Remark 33
.

According to
Theorem

6
, we know that if
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{\widetilde{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d}}:\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}\right\}.

(95)

6.4
Projected gradient descend algorithm for symmetric phase-factor evaluation

Algorithm 1

Projected gradient descend algorithm for optimizing symmetric phase factors (with performance guarantee)

Input:
A target real polynomial
f
of degree
d
so that it has definite parity and
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{20\pi\widetilde{d}}
, an initial guess

and the error tolerance
\epsilon
.

Output:
A set of reduced phase factors
\widetilde{\Phi}
so that
F(\widetilde{\Phi})\leq\epsilon
implying
\left\lVert g(x,\widetilde{\Phi})-f(x)\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq d\sqrt{\epsilon}

[
5
, Theorem 4]
.

Evaluate the objective function
F(\widetilde{\Phi})
at
\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}
according to
Eq.

5
.

while

F(\widetilde{\Phi})>\epsilon

do

Evaluate the gradient
\nabla F(\widetilde{\Phi})
of the objective.

Update
\widetilde{\Phi}_{i}
by gradient descent
\widetilde{\Phi}\leftarrow\widetilde{\Phi}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla F(\widetilde{\Phi})
.

if

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}>\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}

then

Project the point onto the ball
\widetilde{\Phi}\leftarrow\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}-\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}}{20\widetilde{d}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert_{2}}
.

end

end

We list a practical algorithm for symmetric phase-factor evaluation in
Algorithm

1
which is based on the projected gradient descent method
[
2
]
. In
Corollary

7
, we give the convergence analysis of this algorithm and here is the proof.

Proof of
Corollary

7
.

According to
Theorem

6
, the cost function
F(\widetilde{\Phi})
is
\sigma
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth on ball
\mathcal{S}:=\{\widetilde{\Phi}:\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}\}
, where
\sigma=\frac{25}{4}
and
L=\frac{1}{4}
. Using
Theorem

5
, we know that
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
, since
\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{20\widetilde{d}}
. Applying
[
2
, Theorem 3.10]
, the projected gradient method with step size
\frac{1}{L}
converges exponentially, that is,

\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}\leq e^{-\frac{\sigma}{L}\ell}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2},

where
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}
is the set of reduced phase factors in the
\ell
-th iteration step. In particular,
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}\in\mathcal{S}
for all
\ell
follows the construction of the algorithm.
∎

Let
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}
be the set of reduced phase factors in the
\ell
-th iteration step. Note that
F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})=0,\ \nabla F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})=0
and
\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
. Following Taylor’s theorem, there exists
s\in(0,1)
so that

\begin{split}F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell})&=F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})+\nabla F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{\top}\left(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)^{\top}\text{Hess}\left(s\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}+(1-s)\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)\left(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)^{\top}\text{Hess}\left(s\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}+(1-s)\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)\left(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right).\end{split}

Because
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell},\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}
is convex, the intermediate point also lies in the convex set
s\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}+(1-s)\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
. Then, the Hessian is upper bounded at the intermediate point by
Theorem

6
. Thus

F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell})\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\text{Hess}\left(s\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}+(1-s)\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right)\right)\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Furthermore, using
Corollary

7
, we have

F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell})\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{L}{2}e^{-\frac{\sigma}{L}\ell}\left\lVert\widetilde{\Phi}^{0}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

To obtain
F(\widetilde{\Phi}^{\ell})\leq\epsilon
, it suffices to take
\ell=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right)
iterations.

Remarkably,
Algorithm

1
does not require a large number of bits. To see the claim, we consider the model of finite precision arithmetic (see standard axioms in
[
9
]
). The parameter
u
is referred to the unit roundoff. In each iteration step, the rounding error when computing the function
F
and its gradient is
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}u)
. Furthermore, the projection is needed to be executed when the proposed new
\Phi
lies outside
\mathcal{S}
. Under this circumstance, the possible error amplification by taking square roots and division does not contribute significantly and the total rounding error accumulated in each iteration step remains
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}u)
. To conclude, the numerical error of the algorithm is upper bounded by invoking triangle inequality. Then, there exists some constant
C
so that

Cd^{2}u\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\leq\epsilon\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{1}{u}:=2^{n_{\text{bits}}}\geq\frac{1}{C}\frac{d^{2}\log(1/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\ \Rightarrow\ n_{\text{bits}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{d\log(1/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right)\right).

Therefore, the number of bits required by
Algorithm

1
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{d\log(1/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right)\right)
.
Therefore the algorithm is numerically stable, and it is sufficient to use the standard double precision arithmetic operations.
