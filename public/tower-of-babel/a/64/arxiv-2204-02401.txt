Numerical calculation of Green’s function and momentum distribution for spin-polarized fermions by path integral molecular dynamics
Yunuo Xiong, Hongwei Xiong

Abstract
Most recently, path integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) has been successfully applied to perform simulations of identical bosons and fermions by B. Hirshberg et al.. In this work, we demonstrate that PIMD can be developed to calculate Green’s function and extract momentum distribution for spin-polarized fermions. In particular, we show that the momentum distribution calculated by PIMD has potential application to numerous quantum systems, such as cold atom simulation of Mott insulator in Fermi-Hubbard model.

The exchange symmetry for bosons and exchange antisymmetry for fermions, however, make the simulation of identical particles a quite challenging work. In 2019, PIMD was successfully developed by Hirshberg et al.,

Of course, it would be eager to know whether PIMD can be also used to simulate Green’s function for identical fermions, because it is well known that Green’s function plays a central role in revealing novel quantum phenomena of fermions

;

In this paper we extend traditional PIMD to study spin-polarized fermions. We show how one can extract Green’s function from PIMD simulations, taking the anti-symmetrical wave function for spin-polarized fermions into account. Therefore our method has direct applications to cold atom physics. We consider applications of our method to several systems of spin-polarized fermions. In particular, we consider the Mott insulator in Fermi-Hubbard model, which proved crucial to cold atom physics, condensed matter physics and semiconductors.

II
Partition function and path integral for spin-polarized fermions

In a recent work
HirshbergFermi
by Hirshberg et al., the recursion formula is given to calculate the partition function of fermions, based on their previous work on bosons
Hirshberg
. Here we give a brief introduction and derivation of the recursion formula, to help us understand how to calculate Green’s function for fermions with modified recursion formula.

For the following Hamiltonian operator of
N
identical spin-polarized fermions

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\hat{\textbf{p}}_{l}^{2}+\hat{V}(\textbf{r}_{1},\cdots,\textbf{r}_{N}),

(1)

the partition function for spin-polarized fermions is given by

Z_{F}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{p\in S_{N}}(-1)^{p}\int d\textbf{r}_{1}d\textbf{r}_{2}\cdots d\textbf{r}_{N}\left<p\{\textbf{r}\}|e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|\{\textbf{r}\}\right>.

(2)

Here
\beta=1/k_{B}T
, with
k_{B}
being the Boltzmann constant and
T
being the system temperature.
S_{N}
represents the set of
N!
permutation operations. The factor
(-1)^{p}
is due to the exchange effect of fermions. In addition,
\{\textbf{r}\}
denotes
\{\textbf{r}_{1},\cdots,\textbf{r}_{N}\}
.
Defining normalized position eigenstates for fermions as

\left|N_{F}\right>=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{p\in S_{N}}(-1)^{p}\left|p\{\textbf{r}\}\right>,

(3)

we have the unit operator

\hat{I}_{F}=\frac{1}{N!}\int d\textbf{r}_{1}d\textbf{r}_{2}\cdots d\textbf{r}_{N}\left|N_{F}\right>\left<N_{F}\right|.

(4)

We also have another unit operator

\hat{I}=\int d\textbf{r}_{1}d\textbf{r}_{2}\cdots d\textbf{r}_{N}\left|\{\textbf{r}\}\right>\left<\{\textbf{r}\}\right|.

(5)

By dividing
\beta
into
P
segments, with
\Delta\beta=\beta/P
, and using the identity

e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{I}_{F}=e^{-\Delta\beta\hat{H}}\hat{I}e^{-\Delta\beta\hat{H}}\hat{I}\cdots\hat{I}e^{-\Delta\beta\hat{H}}\hat{I}_{F},

(6)

we can obtain the discrete form for the partition function by inserting the definition for the identity operators from the above equations. After that is done, the partition function is a function of a set of ring polymer coordinates
(\textbf{R}_{1},\cdots,\textbf{R}_{N})
, with
\textbf{R}_{i}=(\textbf{r}_{i}^{1},\cdots,\textbf{r}_{i}^{P})
corresponding to
P
ring polymer coordinates for the
i
th particle. Explicitly, after considering all the permutation terms in Eq. (
2
), we have
HirshbergFermi

Z_{F}\sim\int d\textbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\textbf{R}_{N}e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}},

(7)

where
U_{F}^{(N)}
is

U_{F}^{(N)}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln W_{F}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{P}\sum_{j=1}^{P}V\left(\textbf{r}_{1}^{j},\cdots,\textbf{r}_{N}^{j}\right),

(8)

and
W_{F}^{(N)}
is

W_{F}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{k}e^{-\beta E_{N}^{(k)}}W_{F}^{(N-k)}.

(9)

Here the factor
(-1)^{k}
accounts for the exchange effect of fermions. For bosons, this factor is always 1.

E_{N}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{P}^{2}\sum_{l=N-k+1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{P}\left(\textbf{r}_{l}^{j+1}-\textbf{r}_{l}^{j}\right)^{2}.

(10)

Here
\textbf{r}_{l}^{P+1}=\textbf{r}_{l+1}^{1}
, except for
l=N
for which
\textbf{r}_{N}^{P+1}=\textbf{r}_{N-k+1}^{1}
.
In addition,
\omega_{P}=\sqrt{P}/\beta\hbar
.

Unlike the corresponding expression for bosons,
W_{F}^{(N)}
can be negative and the potential
U_{F}^{(N)}
is not real. Therefore we cannot sample the partition function directly through PIMD.
In order to see how we can obtain expectation values for a system of fermions, we denote the estimator for any observable
\hat{O}
by
\epsilon_{O}
and the expectation value for
\hat{O}
is

\left<O\right>_{F}=\frac{1}{Z_{F}}\int d\textbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\textbf{R}_{N}\epsilon_{O}e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}}.

(11)

Here
\left<\cdots\right>_{F}
represents the average based on the distribution given by
e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}}
.We can rewrite
\left<O\right>_{F}
as

\left<O\right>_{F}=\frac{1}{Z_{F}}\int d\textbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\textbf{R}_{N}\epsilon_{O}e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}\frac{e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}}}{e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}},

(12)

with

Z_{F}=\int d\textbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\textbf{R}_{N}e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}\frac{e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}}}{e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}},

(13)

where
U_{B}^{(N)}
is the potential function for bosons
Hirshberg
;
Xiong
, which can be obtained from Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) without considering the factor
(-1)^{k}
in Eq. (
9
).
U_{B}^{(N)}
is a real function so we can perform PIMD for it. Using the fact that

\frac{e^{-\beta U_{F}^{(N)}}}{e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}}=\frac{W_{F}^{(N)}}{W_{B}^{(N)}},

(14)

we can obtain the expectation value of any observable quantity for fermionic system as

\left<O\right>_{F}=\frac{\left<\epsilon_{O}s\right>_{B}}{\left<s\right>_{B}},

(15)

where
s=W_{F}^{(N)}/W_{B}^{(N)}
. Here
\left<\cdots\right>_{B}
represents the average based on the distribution given by
e^{-\beta U_{B}^{(N)}}
.

For example, the density estimator is given by

\rho(\textbf{x})=\left<\frac{1}{P}\sum_{j=1}^{P}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta(\mathbf{r}_{k}^{j}-\textbf{x})\right>_{F}.

(16)

As the number of particles increases or the temperature decreases, the magnitude of
\left<s\right>_{B}
decreases exponentially, leading to the so called fermion sign problem
ceperley
;
loh
;
troyer
;
lyubartsev
;
vozn
;
Yao1
;
Yao2
;
Yao3
. It is not the purpose of this work to solve the fermion sign problem. Therefore we consider here only a few fermions so that
\left<s\right>_{B}
is not too small to assure the accuracy of our numerical calculations.

III
PIMD for Green’s function and momentum distribution

The thermal Green’s function in this work is defined as
Mahan
;
Fetter

G(\textbf{x},\tau_{1};\textbf{y},\tau_{2})=\pm\left<\mathcal{T}\left\{\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{y},\tau_{2})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x},\tau_{1})\right\}\right>,

(17)

where
\left<\cdots\right>
denotes thermal average,
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator for field operator. Here,
+
is for
\tau_{2}>\tau_{1}
, while
-
for
\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}
. In addition,

\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=e^{\hat{H}\tau}\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x})e^{-\hat{H}\tau},~~~\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=e^{\hat{H}\tau}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})e^{-\hat{H}\tau}.

(18)

From the above Green’s function, we may get the momentum distribution of the system. Assuming that
\hat{a}({\textbf{p}})
and

\hat{a}^{\dagger}({\textbf{p}})
are the annihilation and creation operators for a particle with momentum
p
, we have

\hat{\psi}(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d/2}}\int d\textbf{p}\hat{a}({\textbf{p}})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x}},\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d/2}}\int d\textbf{p}\hat{a}^{\dagger}({\textbf{p}})e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x}}.

(19)

Here
d
is the spatial dimension of the system. From the above expression, we may also get

\hat{a}({\textbf{p}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d/2}}\int d\textbf{x}\hat{\psi}(\textbf{x})e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x}},\hat{a}^{\dagger}({\textbf{p}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d/2}}\int d\textbf{x}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{x})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x}}.

(20)

The momentum density distribution is

\rho(\textbf{p})=\frac{Tr(e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}({\textbf{p}})\hat{a}({\textbf{p}}))}{Tr(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}.

(21)

After simple derivations, we get

\rho(\textbf{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d}}\int d{\textbf{x}}d{\textbf{y}}G({\textbf{x}},\tau_{1};{\textbf{y}},\tau_{2})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}})}.

(22)

Here
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}+0^{+}
.

Hence, in our numerical simulation to get the momentum distribution, we should calculate

G(\textbf{x},\tau_{2}+\Delta\beta;\textbf{y},\tau_{2})=\left<\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x},\tau_{2}+\Delta\beta)\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{y},\tau_{2})\right>.

(23)

Here
\Delta\beta
is the imaginary time interval of the beads.

Figure 1:
Illustration of all the modified ring polymer configurations for 3 fermions for the case
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}+\Delta\beta
, to calculate Green’s function. Different colors have been used to distinguish different ring polymers, green stands for the third ring polymer. The gap indicates that a segment of the third ring polymer is missing. The sign
"+1"
and
"-1"
gives the exchange effect of fermions for different situations, respectively.

Similarly to Green’s function for bosons in our previous work
Xiong
, we may consider the following partition function for Green’s function

Z_{F}^{G}=\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{y}Tr\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x},\tau_{1})\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{y},\tau_{2})\right)

(24)

for
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}+0^{+}
.

Repeating the method of the derivation in previous section, we can get the recursion formula for the expression of
Z_{F}^{G}
. It is interesting to notice that the method for bosons in Ref.
Xiong
can be generalized to fermions, by including correctly the sign for the permutation of fermions.
Compared to the boson situation
Xiong
, the main change for the recursion formula to calculate Green’s function is the inclusion of the factor
(-1)^{k}
.
In Fig.
1
for
N=3
fermions, we illustrate the idea of the recursion rule and sign rule for the calculation of Green’s function.

The partition function of Green’s function now takes the form

Z_{F}^{G}\sim\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{y}\int d\mathbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\mathbf{R}_{N-1}d\mathbf{R}_{N}e^{-\beta(V_{G}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{P}\sum_{l=1}^{P}V(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{l},...,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l}))}.

(25)

Here
\mathbf{R}_{i}(1\leq i\leq N-1)
represents the collection of ring polymer coordinates (
\mathbf{r}_{i}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{r}_{i}^{P}
) corresponding to the
i
th ring polymer.
\mathbf{R}_{N}
represents
(\mathbf{r}_{N}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l+3},\cdots,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{P})
. The gap is between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
for the
N
th ring polymer.

In order to modify our PIMD program to accommodate this change, it suffices to modify
E_{N}^{(k)}
and its gradient. For the gap considered in this situation, the interaction potential
U_{G}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{l=1}^{P}V(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{l},...,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l}))
between particles will not be changed.
The recursion relation for
V_{G}^{(\alpha)}
is

e^{-\beta V_{G}^{(\alpha)}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{k}e^{-\beta(E_{\alpha}^{(k)}+V_{G}^{(\alpha-k)})},

(26)

where
V_{G}^{(0)}=0
. From the above recursion relation, we may get
V_{G}^{(N)}
needed in Eq. (
25
).
The factor
(-1)^{k}
is due to the exchange antisymmetry of fermions.

Let us denote positions of the beads corresponding to the
N
th particle at imaginary times
\tau_{1}(=\tau_{2}+\Delta\beta)
and
\tau_{2}
as
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
respectively, we see that for the
N
th particle,
E_{N}^{(N)}
should be modified as follows

\displaystyle E_{N}^{(N)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{P}^{2}\left\{\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{P}(\mathbf{r}_{k}^{j}-\mathbf{r}_{k}^{j+1})^{2}\right.\right]

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{r}_{N}^{1}-\mathbf{r}_{N}^{2})^{2}+...+(\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l}-\mathbf{y})^{2}

\displaystyle\left.+(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l+3})^{2}+...+(\mathbf{r}_{N}^{P}-\mathbf{r}_{N}^{P+1})^{2}\right\},

where the boundary conditions remain unaltered.
The formula for general
E_{\alpha}^{(k)}
is the same as before when
\alpha<N
; when
\alpha=N
, it includes all the usual spring energies between beads but without the
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}
term.

The gradient of
E_{N}^{(k)}
should be modified accordingly. For example, the gradient of
E_{N}^{(N)}
with respect to
\mathbf{x}
is

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}E_{N}^{(N)}=m\omega_{P}^{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l+3}).

(27)

Of course, similarly to the case of
Z_{F}
to calculate the energy and density distribution etc., we should consider the exchange sign in calculating Green’s function.
Defining

e^{-\beta\tilde{V}_{G}^{(\alpha)}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha}e^{-\beta(E_{\alpha}^{(k)}+\tilde{V}_{G}^{(\alpha-k)})}

(28)

and

\tilde{Z}_{G}\sim\int d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{y}\int d\mathbf{R}_{1}\cdots d\mathbf{R}_{N-1}d\mathbf{R}_{N}e^{-\beta(\tilde{V}_{G}^{(N)}+\frac{1}{P}\sum_{l=1}^{P}V(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{l},...,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{l}))},

(29)

Green’s function is

G(\textbf{x}^{\prime},\tau_{2}+\Delta\beta;\textbf{y}^{\prime},\tau_{2})=\frac{\left<\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\prime})\delta(\textbf{y}-\textbf{y}^{\prime})e^{\beta(\tilde{V}_{G}^{(N)}-{V}_{G}^{(N)})}\right>_{{\tilde{Z}}_{G}}}{\left<e^{\beta(\tilde{V}_{G}^{(N)}-{V}_{G}^{(N)})}\right>_{{\tilde{Z}}_{G}}}.

(30)

Here
x
and
y
denote the positions of two beads at the end of the gap.
\left<\cdots\right>_{{\tilde{Z}}_{G}}
denotes the average for the distribution generated by Eq. (
29
). The denominator in the above equation can be used to monitor the validity of our simulation.

IV
Examples to implement our algorithm

In order to test our method and show how to apply our method to realistic quantum system, we apply our algorithm to study various systems of identical fermions. In all of the following simulations, we will use massive Nosé-Hoover chain
Nose1
;
Nose2
;
Hoover
;
Martyna
;
Jang
to establish constant temperature for the system, where each degree of freedom of the system has been coupled to a separate Nosé-Hoover thermostat.
The number of beads used decreases as temperature increases to ensure numerical stability and assure convergence.
In all of the following we checked convergence with respect to the number of beads and MD steps performed. For details of how to assure the convergence, one may refer to the supplementary material in Ref.
Hirshberg

IV.1
Ideal fermions in a harmonic trap

We first consider two noninteracting fermions in a two-dimensional harmonic trap. The Hamiltonian operator of the system is

\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}({\bf{r}}_{1}^{2}+{\bf{r}}_{2}^{2}).

(31)

Here we have used the units with
\hbar=1,m=1,\omega=1
.

To test our numerical simulations, we first give the result by other method for comparison. In grand canonical ensemble, the average occupation number
N_{n_{x}n_{y}}
is

N_{n_{x}n_{y}}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}-\mu)}+1},~~~n_{x},n_{y}=0,1,\cdots

(32)

Here the single-particle energy spectrum
\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}=n_{x}+n_{y}+1
.

For a given
\beta
(in unit of
(\hbar\omega)^{-1}
), we may use the following equation to determine the chemical potential
\mu
.

N=\sum_{n_{x}n_{y}}\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}-\mu)}+1},

(33)

with
N
the total number of particles.

The average density distribution in grand canonical ensemble is then

\rho(\textbf{r})=\sum_{n_{x}n_{y}}\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}-\mu)}+1}|\psi_{n_{x}n_{y}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}.

(34)

Here
\psi_{n_{x}n_{y}}(\textbf{r})=\psi_{n_{x}}(x)\psi_{n_{y}}(y)

is the eigen wavefunction for the energy eigenvalue
\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}
, and

\psi_{n}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}H_{n}(x)e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}},

(35)

with
H_{n}(x)
the Hermite polynomials.

The average density distribution along
x
direction is

\rho(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\rho(\textbf{r})=\sum_{n_{x}n_{y}}\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n_{x}n_{y}}-\mu)}+1}|\psi_{n_{y}}(x)|^{2}.

(36)

In Fig.
2
, the solid line shows
\rho(x)
for
x>0
with the above equation for
\beta=1
. The solid circle shows our numerical result of the density distribution with PIMD, which shows good agreement.

IV.2
Green’s function and momentum distribution for interacting fermions

Now we turn to consider spin-polarized electrons with Coulomb interaction without external trap. The Hamiltonian operator is

\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Delta_{j}+\sum_{k>l}^{N}\frac{1}{\left|\textbf{r}_{k}-\textbf{r}_{l}\right|}.

(38)

Figure 4:
Green’s function for 4 fermions without external trap. Green corresponds to Green’s function for
\beta=0.5
(8 beads), while orange for
\beta=1.0
(12 beads), blue for
\beta=1.5
(24 beads). The inset shows the momentum distribution for corresponding temperatures.

We adopt periodic boundary condition with
L=10
and
4
particles in our simulation.
In Fig.
4
, we give the numerical result of Green’s function by PIMD for different temperatures.
2\times 10^{7}
MD steps are used to assure convergence.
We see that with increasing temperature, Green’s function becomes narrower, and we expect that the momentum width would be wider for higher temperature.

For a uniform system, we have

G(\textbf{x},\tau_{1};\textbf{y},\tau_{2})=G(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y},\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}).

(39)

In this case, the momentum distribution becomes

\rho(\textbf{p})=\frac{L^{d}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d}}\int d{\textbf{x}}G({\textbf{x}},\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\textbf{p}\cdot{\textbf{x}}},~\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}+0^{+}.

IV.3
Momentum distribution for ultracold fermionic atoms in a harmonic trap

We consider here an example of the following Hamiltonian operator which may have potential applications in ultracold fermionic atoms.

\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Delta_{j}+\sum_{k>l}^{N}\frac{\lambda}{\left|\textbf{r}_{k}-\textbf{r}_{l}\right|^{3}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\textbf{r}_{k}^{2}.

(41)

In the above
\hat{H}
, we consider dipole-dipole interaction
Stuhler
;
Filinov
between atoms, which is a typical and interesting interaction in cold atom physics.

Because of the presence of the harmonic trap, Green’s function can not be written as a function of
\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}
. In the unit
\hbar=1
, the momentum distribution in two dimensions is

\rho(p_{x},p_{y})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int d{\mathbf{r}_{1}}d{\mathbf{r}_{2}}G({\mathbf{r}_{1}},\tau_{2}+0^{+};{\mathbf{r}_{2}},\tau_{2})e^{i[p_{x}(x_{1}-x_{2})]+p_{y}(y_{1}-y_{2})]}.

(42)

The momentum distribution in
x
direction is then

\rho(p_{x})=\int dp_{y}\rho(p_{x},p_{y}).

(43)

We have

\rho(p_{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dy_{1}dx_{1}dx_{2}G(x_{1},y_{1},\tau_{2}+0^{+};x_{2},y_{1},\tau_{2})e^{ip_{x}(x_{1}-x_{2})}.

(44)

It is easy to verify that
\int\rho(p_{x})dp_{x}=N
. From the above equation, we may calculate
\rho(p_{x})
from the numerical simulation of Green’s function
G(x_{1},y_{1},\tau_{2}+0^{+};x_{2},y_{1},\tau_{2})
by PIMD.

In this case, to get the momentum distribution for nonuniform system, we had to calculate Green’s function with multiple variables. In Fig.

In cold atom experiments, usually thousands of ultracold fermionic atoms are studied. However, in many remarkable experiments, periodic optical lattice is used to simulate the Fermi-Hubbard model
Esslinger
. In this case, in the Mott insulator state, for each lattice site, there are only a few fermions confined in an equivalently harmonic trap. After switching off the optical lattice and the confining trap, what the experiment measures directly is the momentum distribution of the fermionic atoms in each lattice site, after sufficient time of free expansion
Dalfovo
;
Anderson
;
Davis
. The usual Fermi-Hubbard model can not calculate accurately the momentum distribution of the fermionic atoms in each lattice site because the wave function is usually approximated by noninteracting gas, while the method developed here can provide accurate calculation of the momentum distribution. Hence, our method may have direct application in the cold atom simulation of the Fermi-Hubbard model. Of course, this means that the Bose version of our method will have potential application for cold atom simulation of Bose-Hubbard model

V
Conclusions

As a summary, we extended PIMD method to calculate Green function and momentum distribution for spin-polarized fermions. We applied our method to study several systems of spin-polarized fermions. In particular, we studied the realistic momentum distribution for a few fermions in Mott insulator, which are of interests to experimental cold atoms physics for Fermi-Hubbard model. Our method can easily be adapted to study numerous realistic quantum systems.
