Generalized Orthogonal Procrustes Problem under Arbitrary Adversaries
Shuyang Ling

Abstract
The generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem (GOPP) plays a fundamental role in several scientific disciplines including statistics, imaging science and computer vision. Despite its tremendous practical importance, it is generally an NP-hard problem to find the least squares estimator. We study the semidefinite relaxation (SDR) and an iterative method named generalized power method (GPM) to find the least squares estimator, and investigate the performance under a signal-plus-noise model. We show that the SDR recovers the least squares estimator exactly and moreover the generalized power method with a proper initialization converges linearly to the global minimizer to the SDR, provided that the signal-to-noise ratio is large. The main technique follows from showing the nonlinear mapping involved in the GPM is essentially a local contraction mapping and then applying the well-known Banach fixed-point theorem finishes the proof. In addition, we analyze the low-rank factorization algorithm and show the corresponding optimization landscape is free of spurious local minimizers under nearly identical conditions that enables the success of SDR approach. The highlight of our work is that the theoretical guarantees are purely algebraic and do not assume any statistical priors of the additive adversaries, and thus it applies to various interesting settings.



Keywords
: Generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem, semidefinite relaxation, generalized power method, Banach fixed-point theorem

This problem, known as rigid point clouds registration and also generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem (GOPP), has found numerous applications in computer vision

In this work, we consider each point cloud
\bm{A}_{i}
is a noisy copy of a common point cloud
\bm{A}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{d\times m}
transformed by an unknown
d\times d
orthogonal matrix
\bm{O}_{i}
:

\bm{A}_{i}=\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}_{i},~1\leq i\leq n,

(1.1)

where
\{\bm{\Delta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is any arbitrary additive noise. Our goal is to recover
(\bm{A},\bm{O}_{i})
from
\{\bm{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In matrix form, we define

\bm{D}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{A}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{O}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{O}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{O}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{O}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n},\quad\bm{\Delta}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\Delta}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times m},

(1.2)

where

1.1
Least squares estimation

One approach is to find the least squares estimator by minimizing
f(\bm{S},\bm{A}):

\displaystyle f(\bm{S},\bm{A})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{S}_{i}\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}={n\|\bm{A}\|^{2}_{F}}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\bm{A},\bm{S}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{A}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}

where
\bm{S}^{\top}=[\bm{S}_{1}^{\top},\cdots,\bm{S}^{\top}_{n}]
is an element in
\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
.
For any fixed
\bm{S}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
, the global minimizer for
\bm{A}
is

\widehat{\bm{A}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{S}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}_{i}
.
Therefore, the least squares estimation is equivalent to find the global minimizer to the following program:

\min_{\bm{S}_{i}\in\Od(d)}~-\sum_{i,j}\left\langle\bm{A}_{i}\bm{A}_{j}^{\top},\bm{S}_{i}\bm{S}_{j}^{\top}\right\rangle=\min_{\bm{S}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n}}~-\left\langle\bm{C},\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\right\rangle

(P)

where
\bm{C}_{ij}=\bm{A}_{i}\bm{A}_{j}^{\top}
and
\bm{C}=\bm{D}\bm{D}^{\top}
is the data matrix:

\bm{C}=(\bm{O}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta})(\bm{O}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta})^{\top}=\bm{O}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{O}^{\top}+\bm{\Delta}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{O}^{\top}+\bm{O}\bm{A}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}.

(1.3)

For
n=2
, (
P
) can be easily solved by performing singular value decomposition
[
48
]
.
However, it becomes much more challenging for large
n
to find the global minimizer to (
P
)
[
27
,
44
,
51
]
. In fact, (
P
) is a well-known NP-hard problem even if
d=1
. Therefore, one would seek for alternative methods to approximate the global solution to this nonconvex program.

1.2
Convex relaxation and its tightness

Among many existing approaches, semidefinite relaxation (SDR) is one of the most powerful methods.
One common SDR uses the fact that
\bm{Z}=\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}
is positive semidefinite and also its diagonal block is
\bm{I}_{d}
. Using these two observations gives a convex program:

\min_{\bm{Z}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times nd}}~-\langle\bm{C},\bm{Z}\rangle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{Z}\succeq 0,~\bm{Z}_{ii}=\bm{I}_{d}.

(SDR)

Despite that this SDR is solvable with a polynomial-time algorithm, the main question is whether this SDR produces a solution close to the least squares estimator.
In
[
7
]
, Bandeira, Khoo, and Singer conjectured that a threshold exists such that if the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is above this threshold, the SDR succeeds in recovering the least squares estimator. Roughly speaking, if the noise strength
\|\bm{\Delta}\|
is weaker than the signal
\|\bm{A}\|
, the SDR can find the global minimizer to a highly nonconvex problem. In this work, we will try to investigate the following question

When does the SDR recover the least squares estimator?

This is known as the
tightness
of the SDR: the global minimizer to (
SDR
) is rank-
d
.

Convex relaxation has proven to be one powerful approach to solve the generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem
[

,

,

,

,
44
,

,

]
. The (

[

]

,

]
. On the other hand, as observed in

[

[

in which the optimal solution to the SDR is exactly the ground truth solution. However, unlike these aforementioned examples, the least squares estimator usually does not match the ground truth signal in the generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem in presence of noise. The similar issue also arises in angular synchronization

1.3
Generalized power method (GPM)

As the SDR is expensive to solve, we also consider an iterative method named generalized power method to efficiently find the least squares estimator. The algorithm follows a two-step procedure:
(a) start with an initialization
\bm{O}^{0}
via spectral method (spectral initialization), which is shown in the first two steps in Algorithm 1; (b) update the iterate
\bm{O}^{t}
by

\bm{O}^{t+1}=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{C}\bm{O}^{t})

(1.4)

where
\bm{C}\bm{O}^{t}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times d}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\cdot):\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times d}\rightarrow\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
maps each
d\times d
block to a
d\times d
orthogonal matrix by

\mathcal{P}(\bm{R}):=\argmin_{\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)}\|\bm{Q}-\bm{R}\|_{F}^{2}

(1.5)

whose global minimizer is
\bm{Q}=\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}
where
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
are the left and right singular vectors of
\bm{R}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{d\times d}
respectively.

Algorithm 1

Spectral initialization and generalized power methods for the GOPP

1:

Compute the top
d
left singular vectors
\bm{U}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times d}
of
\bm{D}
in (
1.2
) with
\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{U}=\bm{I}_{d}.

2:

Compute
\mathcal{P}(\bm{U}_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
where
\bm{U}_{i}
is the
i
th
d\times d
block of
\bm{U}
.

3:

Initialize
\bm{O}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{U})\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times d}.

4:

\bm{O}^{t+1}=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{C}\bm{O}^{t})
,
t=0,1,\cdots

5:

Stop when the iteration stabilizes.

Generalized power method is much more efficient than the convex relaxation: Every iteration of the GPM is essentially a matrix-vector multiplication followed by a projection step for each block. As shown in
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
65
]
, the GPM is significantly faster than the SDR in synchronization problem. However, this two-step approach is essentially nonconvex and has the risk of getting stuck at stationary points or local minimizer.
On the other hand, the output of the GPM is usually quite satisfactory and is even equal to the solution to (
SDR
). Therefore, we would like to theoretically justify why this efficient nonconvex approach is successful, especially in the regime of high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
In particular, we are interested in answering this question:

When does the generalized power method to recover the least squares estimator exactly?

In practice, the first-order optimization methods
[
2
,
9
,
21
,

,

]
are quite popular in solving medium/large-scale convex programs. The main idea is first to find a proper initialization and show that global convergence of the algorithm is guaranteed with this carefully-chosen initialization

For the generalized orthogonal Procrustes problem, we will focus on the generalized power method (GPM) since it has been successfully applied to the group synchronization problem. The GPM was first proposed and analyzed in joint alignment problem
[

]
, angular synchronization
[

1.4
Optimization landscape of low-rank factorization

The generalized power method works well empirically to solve the generalized Procrustes problem if the noise is small compared with the signal. More surprisingly, even without spectral initialization, the power method still recovers a tight solution. To partially explain this phenomenon, we investigate the performance of the Burer-Monteiro approach.
The Burer-Monteiro approach
[
12
,
13
,
9
]
replaces the orthogonality constraints of each
\bm{O}_{i}
in (
P
) by an element on the Stiefel manifold
\St(d,p):=\{\bm{S}_{i}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{d\times p}:\bm{S}_{i}\bm{S}_{i}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{d}\}

[
2
,
21
]
,

\min_{\bm{S}_{i}\in\St(d,p)}~-\sum_{i,j}\langle\bm{C}_{ij},\bm{S}_{i}\bm{S}_{j}^{\top}\rangle

(BM)

This family of optimization programs (
BM
) with different
p
is an “interpolation” between (
P
) and (
SDR
):
if
p=d
, (
BM
) equals (
P
) and if
p=nd
, (
BM
) is exactly the (
SDR
).
Despite the nonconvexity of (
BM
), it has been observed that even if the rank is quite small, the algorithm still does not often get stuck at spurious local minima even with random initialization. Therefore, we are interested in the following question:

Are there no other local minima in (
BM
) besides the global minima?

It has been shown that no spurious local minima exist for a class of the SDPs, i.e., the optimization landscape is
benign
, if the rank of the factorization
p
is sufficiently large, namely greater than the square root of the number of the constraints
[
11
,
58
]
.
Many works have shown that the landscape of the nonconvex objective function is benign under nearly identical conditions that enable the success of the SDR. Examples include phase retrieval
[
54
]
, phase synchronization and community detection
[
35
]
, orthogonal group synchronization
[
37
]
, matrix completion
[
25
]
and dictionary learning
[
53
]
. Therefore, we aim to provide a bound on the rank of the factorization that theoretically guarantees a benign optimization landscape for the GOPP under arbitrary noise.

2.1
Main theorems

Before we present the main theorems, we provide some additional assumption on the data matrix
\bm{A}
. Given a matrix
\bm{A}
, we let
\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A}):=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})}
and
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}):=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})}
, and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
are the largest and smallest eigenvalues respectively. In particular, we assume
\bm{A}
is full row-rank, i.e.,
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})>0
. Let
\kappa
be the condition number of
\bm{A}
, i.e.,
\kappa=\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})/\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
.
Define the SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) as

\SNR:=\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})}{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|}.

(2.1)

Now we present our first main result on the tightness of the SDR.

Theorem 2.1

(
Tightness of the SDR).

Suppose

\SNR\geq 30\kappa^{3}\sqrt{d},

the (
SDR
) is tight and recovers the unique global minimizer to (
P
), i.e., the global minimizer to the SDR is unique and exactly rank-
d
.

Theorem
2.1
does not assume any statistical models on
\bm{\Delta}
. The sufficient condition for the tightness is completely
algebraic
and works for any type of additive adversaries. In other words, solving (
SDR
) yields the least squares estimator to the generalized Procrustes problem in the high SNR regime.

Before we proceed to present the convergence of the GPM, we introduce one more notation.
Note that the value of (
P
) remains the same if we multiply
\bm{X}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
by another orthogonal matrix from the right side. We resolve this ambiguity by using the following distance for two elements
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times d}
by

d_{F}(\bm{X},\bm{Y}):=\min_{\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\bm{Q}\|_{F}=\min_{\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{Y}_{i}\bm{Q}\|_{F}^{2}}

(2.2)

where the minimizer
\bm{Q}=\mathcal{P}(\bm{Y}^{\top}\bm{X})
is given in (
1.5
). One can verify
d_{F}(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
in (
2.2
) is indeed a distance and satisfies triangle inequality.

Theorem 2.2

(
Convergence of the GPM).

Suppose

\SNR\geq 35\kappa^{4}\sqrt{d},

the generalized power method with spectral initialization recovers the globally optimal solution to the SDR with linear convergence

d_{F}(\bm{O}^{t},\widehat{\bm{O}})\leq\rho^{t}d_{F}(\bm{O}^{0},\widehat{\bm{O}}),~~\rho<1,

where
\widehat{\bm{O}}
and
\widehat{\bm{O}}\widehat{\bm{O}}^{\top}
are the unique globally optimal solution to (
P
) and (
SDR
) respectively. Moreover, it holds that

\min_{\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)}\|\widehat{\bm{O}}-\bm{O}\bm{Q}\|_{F}\leq 7\kappa^{2}\SNR^{-1}\sqrt{nd}.

Theorem
2.2
gives an efficient algorithm to produce the globally optimal solution to (
P
) and (
SDR
). Moreover, the sufficient conditions in Theorem
2.1
and
2.2
are almost the same. Both theorems are general and can be applied to different settings, e.g.,
\bm{\Delta}_{i}
is a random subgaussian matrix in which case
\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|
is straightforward to upper bound, see
[
56
]
. We will provide two concrete examples in the next sections.
Finally, we will discuss the optimization landscape of (
BM
), whose proof is characterized via Theorem 2.8 in
[
35
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
Optimization landscape of the Burer-Monteiro factorization).

Suppose that

\SNR\geq\frac{7\kappa^{2}\sqrt{d}(p+2\kappa^{2}d+d-2)}{p-2\kappa^{2}d+d-2}~~\text{ and }~~\SNR\geq 35\kappa^{4}\sqrt{d},

then any second order critical point
\bm{S}\in\St(d,p)^{\otimes n}
is global and
\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}
is equal to the unique global minimizer to (
SDR
). In particular,
p\geq 2\kappa^{2}d+2
along with
\SNR\geq 35\kappa^{4}\sqrt{d}
suffices to ensure a benign optimization landscape.

Theorem
2.3
justifies why gradient-based approach do not suffer from getting stuck at any local minimizers in (
BM
) as every local minimizer in (

) is a global minimizer to (

) provided that

is slightly larger than
d
in the high SNR regime.

Here we provide a brief discussion on how optimal our result is.
Given
\bm{D}=\bm{O}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}
, we define another notion of the signal-to-noise ratio as:

\SNR_{S}:=\frac{\|\bm{O}\bm{A}\|}{\|\bm{\Delta}\|}=\frac{\sqrt{n}\|\bm{A}\|}{\|\bm{\Delta}\|}

(2.8)

where
\|\bm{O}\bm{A}\|=\sqrt{n}\|\bm{A}\|.

This notion of the SNR arises naturally in bounding the estimator of
\bm{O}
via spectral method, i.e., extracting the top
d
left singular values
\bm{U}
of
\bm{D}
with
\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{U}=n\bm{I}_{d}
, and then it holds from Davis-Kahan theorem that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)}\|\bm{U}-\bm{O}\bm{Q}\|\leq\frac{2\|\bm{\Delta}\|}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})-\|\bm{\Delta}\|}=\frac{2}{\kappa^{-1}\SNR_{S}-1}.

This implies that the spectral method provides a non-trivial error bound if
\SNR_{S}
is at least greater than a constant. In our numerical experiments indicate that if
\SNR_{S}\geq 2
for both two models, then the global convergence of the GPM holds. Now we proceed to compare
\SNR
and
\SNR_{S}
whose difference is mainly on the measure of noise.
Let

\xi:=\frac{\sqrt{n}\max\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|}{\|\bm{\Delta}\|}\geq 1~~\text{ and then }~~\SNR_{S}=\frac{\sqrt{n}\|\bm{A}\|}{\sqrt{n}\xi^{-1}\max\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|}=\kappa\xi\SNR.

The reason why
\xi
is defined in this form is: if
\xi
is of order 1, then
\SNR_{S}
and
\SNR
are of the same order. In particular, if
\{\bm{\Delta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. Gaussian random matrices and
m/d
is not too large, then
\xi
is close to 1. The factor
\xi
determines how much
\SNR
is different from
\SNR_{S}.

Based on
\SNR_{S}\geq 2
from our numerical experiments, we have a bound in terms of the
\SNR
:

\SNR=\frac{\SNR_{S}}{\kappa\xi}\geq\frac{2}{\kappa\xi}

which differs from our bound in Theorem
2.1
and
2.2
by a factor that involves
\xi,\kappa
and
d
. The main difference comes from
\xi
as it may vary significantly in specific cases. In particular, if
\xi
is large, then our bound is far away from the phase transition boundary in the simulations. Now we roughly approximate a lower bound of
\xi
in the two applications. Suppose
\xi_{-}
is a lower bound, then as long as
\SNR\geq\SNR_{S}/(\kappa\xi_{-})\geq 2/(\kappa\xi_{-})
, the tightness of SDR holds which follows from

\SNR_{S}=\kappa\xi\SNR\geq\frac{2\kappa\xi}{\kappa\xi_{-}}\geq 2.

In both models, if
m\gg d
and
n
are large, then
\xi_{-}
is large and our bound is sub-optimal.

Gaussian noise model

For
\bm{\Delta}=\sigma\bm{W}
, then we have
\|\bm{W}\|\leq\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{nd}+2\sqrt{n\log n}
, and
\max\|\bm{W}_{i}\|\geq\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{d}-2\sqrt{\log n}.

Then

\xi\geq\frac{\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{d}-2\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{m/n}+\sqrt{d}+2\sqrt{\log n}}\gtrsim\min\left\{\sqrt{n},\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}\right\}

for some constant
C>0
where
m\geq d+1
.
In other words, if
m/d
and
n
are large, then our bound is not tight.

Uniform corruption model

For the uniform corruption model (
2.6
), we note that

\E\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}=n(1-\theta^{2})\bm{I}_{m},~~~\E\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}^{\top}=(1-\theta)\left(\theta\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}+\frac{m}{d}\bm{I}_{d}\right).

For
m
and
n
sufficiently large,
\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}^{\top}
will not deviate from their respective expectations much. As a result, we can argue that
\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|
has a lower bound of order at least
(1-\theta)\sqrt{m/d}
and
\|\bm{\Delta}\|
is of order at most
\sqrt{(1-\theta^{2})n}.

Therefore, we have

\xi=\frac{\sqrt{n}\max\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|}{\|\bm{\Delta}\|}\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}.

This implies that if
m/d
and
n
are large, then
\xi\gg 1
and our theorem is sub-optimal.

The distance

in (

d_{F}^{2}(\bm{X},\bm{Y})=\|\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}-2\|\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}\|_{*}

denotes the matrix nuclear norm, i.e., the sum of singular values.
In particular, if
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are both in

d_{F}^{2}(\bm{X},\bm{Y})=2nd-2\|\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}\|_{*}

(3.1)

To measure how close a given element
\bm{S}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
is to the ground truth state, i.e.,
\bm{O}\bm{Q}\in\Od(d)^{\otimes n}
for some
\bm{Q}\in\Od(d,p)

\epsilon

For any
\bm{S}\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}

\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}d}{2}\right)n\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{O})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{O})\leq n

which follows from

and

\frac{\epsilon^{2}nd}{2}\geq nd-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{O})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(n-\sigma_{i}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{O}))\geq n-\sigma_{\min}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{O})

Then we have a lower bound on the smallest singular value of

for any

(

\bm{V}_{\bm{B}}
and

d

Now we present the proof of Proposition

Proof of Proposition
3.6

Consider the data matrix
\bm{D}:=\bm{O}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}\in\hbox{\msbm{R}}^{nd\times m}

Denote

Let

\bm{A}.

\bm{U}_{i}

Using Weyl’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{D}-\bm{O}\bm{A}\|\leq\|\bm{\Delta}\|\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad|\sigma_{i}(\bm{D})-\sqrt{n}\sigma_{i}(\bm{A})|\leq\|\bm{\Delta}\|,~~1\leq i\leq d.

Applying the Davis-Kahan theorem to

\displaystyle\leq\|(\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}_{i})(\bm{V}-\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q})\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|

)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{A}\|(1+\SNR^{-1})\cdot\frac{2}{\SNR-1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})(1-\SNR^{-1})}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\bm{U}_{i}-\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{U}_{i}-(\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}+\bm{\Delta}_{i})\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|+\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\kappa(\SNR+1)}{\sqrt{n}(\SNR-1)^{2}}+\frac{\max\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\|}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})(1-\SNR^{-1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\kappa(\SNR+1)}{\sqrt{n}(\SNR-1)^{2}}+\frac{\SNR-1}{\sqrt{n}(\SNR-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{3\kappa(\SNR+1)}{\sqrt{n}(\SNR-1)^{2}}.

Now applying Lemma
3.9
gives:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}(\bm{U}_{i})-\mathcal{P}(\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sigma_{\min}({\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}})}\cdot\|\bm{U}_{i}-\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{n}(1+\SNR^{-1})\cdot\frac{3\kappa(\SNR+1)}{\sqrt{n}(\SNR-1)^{2}}=\frac{6\kappa^{2}(\SNR+1)^{2}}{(\SNR-1)^{2}}\SNR^{-1}

where
\sigma_{\min}(\bar{\bm{\Sigma}})=\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\min}(\bar{\bm{U}}\bar{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})\geq\sigma_{\min}(\bar{\bm{\Sigma}})\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})

Use

(
3.19
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})}{\sqrt{n}\|\bm{A}\|(1+\SNR^{-1})}=\frac{1}{\kappa\sqrt{n}(1+\SNR^{-1})}.

Finally we arrive at

\displaystyle d_{F}(\bm{O}^{0},\bm{O})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathcal{P}(\bm{U}_{i})-\mathcal{P}(\bm{O}_{i}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{Q}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})\|_{F}^{2}}\leq\frac{6\kappa^{2}(\SNR+1)^{2}}{(\SNR-1)^{2}}\SNR^{-1}\sqrt{nd}.

∎

, as we assume

\bm{A}
. Note that

via the Bernstein inequality (
[
55

\bm{A}
