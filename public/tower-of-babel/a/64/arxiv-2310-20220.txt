A Spectral Analysis of The Correlated Random Walk
Yusuke Ide, Akihiro Narimatsu

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a spectral analysis of the Correlated Random Walk (CRW) on the path. We apply an analytical method for the Quantum Walk to CRW. For the isospectral coin cases, we obtain all of the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of the time evolution operator of CRW, and also obtain the limiting distribution.



Keywords:
Correlated Random Walk; Quantum Walk; Birth and death chain; Spectral analysis

Introduction

The classical Random Walk (RW) is one of the most important models in the study of probability theory and related fields. Furthermore, a more general model called the Correlated Random Walk (CRW) or the Persistent Random Walk has been studied by various authors.

The motion of CRW depends on the movement of the previous step. This feature is regarded as the random walker to have multiple states at each vertex. The similar feature is seen in the Quantum Walk (QW), which is known as the quantum counterpart model of RW. For that reason, analytical methods for QW are applied to CRW in recent days
[

]
. In the past two decades, QW has been intensively studied from several points of view

because of its useful applications
[

]
. In the previous study, QW on the path and the cycle graphs are analyzed and the spectra and the eigen functions of the time evolution operators are obtained
[
2
,
8
]
.

In this paper, we focus on CRW on the path graph. At first, we consider the spectrum of a Jacobi matrix given by the probability transition matrix (or the time evolution operator) of CRW. This Jacobi matrix is something like the probability transition matrix of RW on the path.

Next, we discuss a spectral decomposition of the time evolution operator of CRWs with isospectral coins. We should note that under assumption
4.1
, all the coins are isospectral but need not be the same because it allows different eigenvectors. We can construct most of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the time evolution operator of the CRWs from that of the Jacobi matrix. Although this method is applicable to QWs
[
2
,
8
]
, it is not straightforward to apply it to CRW. On the other hand, we always have the eigenvalue
1
and the corresponding eigenvector of the time evolution operator of CRW, which do not come from that of the Jacobi matrix. This eigenvector corresponds to the birth and death chains and the correspondence means that CRW on the path is supported by RW on the path(birth and death chains).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the definition of CRW on the path graph and the limit distribution of it. Section
3

2
Definition of CRW on the Path

In this paper, we consider CRW on the path
P_{n+1}
with the vertex set
V_{n+1}=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
and the Edge set
E_{n+1}=\{(x,x+1):x=0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. Next we put the state space
{\mathcal{S}}_{n+1}={\rm Span}\{|x\rangle\otimes|L\rangle,|x\rangle\otimes|R\rangle:x\in V_{n+1}\}
, where each elements of the tensor product
\{|x\rangle:x\in V_{n+1}\}
and
\{|L\rangle=\ ^{T}[1\ 0],|R\rangle=\ ^{T}[0\ 1]\}
are orthonormal bases, with
{}^{T}A
represents the transpose of a matrix
A
. Then we define the time evolution operator
U=SC
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n+1}
with the coin operator
C
and the shift operator
S
as follows:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{n}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes C_{x},

\displaystyle S|x,J\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}|x+1,L\rangle\quad{\rm if}\ x\neq n,\ J=R,\\
|n,R\rangle\quad{\rm if}\ x=n,\ J=R,\\
|x-1,R\rangle\quad{\rm if}\ x\neq 0,\ J=L,\\
|0,L\rangle\quad{\rm if}\ x=0,\ J=L,\end{cases}

where
C_{x}(x=0,\dots,n)
are
2\times 2
probability transition matrix and
|x,J\rangle=|x\rangle\otimes|J\rangle
. We should note that the operator
U=SC
is multiplied from the left.
C_{x}
is defined by

\displaystyle C_{x}

\displaystyle=p_{x,L}|L\rangle\langle L|+(1-p_{x,L})|R\rangle\langle L|+p_{x,R}|L\rangle\langle R|+(1-p_{x,R})|R\rangle\langle R|

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,L}&p_{x,R}\\
1-p_{x,L}&1-p_{x,R}\end{bmatrix},

where
0\leq p_{x,J}\leq 1\ (J=L,R)
.
p_{x,L}
denotes the probability that the random walker, having moved from
x-1
to
x
, moves from
x
to
x-1
. On the other hand,
p_{x,R}
denotes the probability that the random walker, having moved from
x+1
to
x
, moves from
x
to
x-1
. To prevent this model from becoming equivalent to a random walk or becoming a trivial model, we set
0<|p_{x,L}-p_{x,R}|<1
. Next we put
|\Psi_{t}\rangle\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n+1}
be the states of the whole system at time
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. In particular, the initial state is described as
|\varphi\rangle=|\Psi_{0}\rangle
. Let
X_{t}\in V_{n+1}
be the position of the random walker at time
t
. Then the probability
{\mathbb{P}}_{|\varphi\rangle}(X_{t}=x)
that the walker with the initial state
|\varphi\rangle
is located at the vertex
x
at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{|\varphi\rangle}(X_{t}=x)=\Bigl\{\langle x|\otimes\bigl(\langle L|+\langle R|\bigr)\Bigr\}|\Psi_{t}\rangle.

We define the limiting distribution
p_{\infty,n}
of CRW on the path
P_{n+1}
is described as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\infty,n}(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{|\varphi\rangle}(X_{t}=x),

where
x=0,\dots,n
.

3
Jacobi matrix

In this section, we consider the Jacobi matrix, which is helpful for analyzing CRW. Let
\nu_{1,x},\nu_{2,x}
and
|w_{1,x}\rangle,\langle q_{1,x}|,|w_{2,x}\rangle,\langle q_{2,x}|
be the eigenvalues and the corresponding right and left eigenvectors of
C_{x}
. By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{1,x}=1,\quad|w_{1,x}\rangle=\frac{1}{1-(p_{x,L}-p_{x,R})}\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,R}\\
1-p_{x,L}\end{bmatrix},\ \langle q_{1,x}|=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\nu_{2,x}=p_{x,L}-p_{x,R},\quad|w_{2,x}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\langle q_{2,x}|=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{1-(p_{x,L}-p_{x,R})}\begin{bmatrix}1-p_{x,L}&-p_{x,R}\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

Then we consider the spectral decomposition of each matrix
C_{x}
as follows:

\displaystyle C_{x}

\displaystyle=1\cdot|w_{1,x}\rangle\langle q_{1,x}|+\nu_{2,x}|w_{2,x}\rangle\langle q_{2,x}|

\displaystyle=(\nu_{2,x}-1)|w_{2,x}\rangle\langle q_{2,x}|+I_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\nu_{2,x})|w_{2,x}\rangle\langle-q_{2,x}|+I_{2},

where
I_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix. Here we use the relation
I_{2}=|w_{1,x}\rangle\langle q_{1,x}|+|w_{2,x}\rangle\langle q_{2,x}|
coming from the diagonalizability of
C_{x}
. This shows that we can represent
C_{x}
without
|w_{1,x}\rangle
.

We define the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
Jacobi matrix
B
as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{(B)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{q_{i}(L)}{\sqrt{2}}\quad&{\rm if}\ j=i-1,\\
\frac{-q_{i}(R)}{\sqrt{2}}\quad&{\rm if}\ j=i+1,\\
\frac{-q_{0}(L)}{\sqrt{2}}\quad&{\rm if}\ i=j=0,\\
\frac{q_{n}(R)}{\sqrt{2}}\quad&{\rm if}\ i=j=n,\\
0\quad&{\rm otherwise},\end{cases}}

where
\langle q_{2,x}|=\begin{bmatrix}q_{x}(L)&q_{x}(R)\end{bmatrix}
. We should note that
q_{x}(L)-q_{x}(R)=\sqrt{2}
. In this setting, the corresponding Jacobi matrix is the following:

\displaystyle B=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}-q_{0}(L)&-q_{0}(R)&&&&&&\\
q_{1}(L)&0&-q_{1}(R)\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&q_{x-1}(L)&0&-q_{x-1}(R)&&&\\
&&&q_{x}(L)&0&-q_{x}(R)&&\\
&&&&q_{x+1}(L)&0&-q_{x+1}(R)&\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&&&q_{n-1}(L)&0&-q_{n-1}(R)\\
&&&&&&q_{n}(L)&q_{n}(R)\end{bmatrix}.

This Jacobi matrix represents the following inner products:

\displaystyle\bigl(\langle q_{2,x-1}|\otimes\langle x-1|\bigr)U\bigl(|x\rangle\otimes|w_{2,x}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2,x}(B)_{x-1,x},

\displaystyle\bigl(\langle q_{2,x+1}|\otimes\langle x+1|\bigr)U\bigl(|x\rangle\otimes|w_{2,x}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2,x}(B)_{x+1,x},

\displaystyle\bigl(\langle q_{2,0}|\otimes\langle 0|\bigr)U\bigl(|0\rangle\otimes|w_{2,0}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2,0}(B)_{0,0},

\displaystyle\bigl(\langle q_{2,n}|\otimes\langle n|\bigr)U\bigl(|n\rangle\otimes|w_{2,n}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2,n}(B)_{n,n}.

This properties help our spectral analysis in Sect.
4
.

B
is something like the probability transition matrix of a random walk with a right-hand side operation, as the sum of numbers in the each row (not column) is
1
, except for the
0
th and the
n
th rows. In the
0
th and the
n
th rows, where
-q_{0}(L)<0
and
q_{n}(R)<0
, it can be interpreted as random walkers being absorbed at the end points of the path.

Next we set an unit vector
\pi=\begin{bmatrix}\pi(0)&\dots&\pi(n)\end{bmatrix}
such that

\displaystyle\pi(0)

\displaystyle=C_{\pi},

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{y=1}^{x}q_{y}(L)}{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(-q_{y}(R))}C_{\pi},

where
C_{\pi}=\bigl(1+\sum_{x=1}^{n}\frac{\prod_{y=1}^{x}q_{y}(L)}{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(-q_{y}(R))}\bigr)^{-1}
. We put another Jacobi matrix
J
as follows:

\displaystyle J=D_{\pi^{1/2}}^{-1}BD_{\pi^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}-q_{0}(L)&\sqrt{-q_{0}(R)q_{1}(L)}\\
\sqrt{-q_{0}(R)q_{1}(L)}&0&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&0&\sqrt{-q_{n-1}(R)q_{n}(L)}\\
&&&\sqrt{-q_{n-1}(R)q_{n}(L)}&q_{n}(R)\end{bmatrix},

where
D_{\pi^{1/2}}={\rm diag}\Bigl(\sqrt{\pi(0)},\dots,\sqrt{\pi(n)}\Bigr)
and
A^{-1}
is the inverse matrix of matrix
A
. Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

B
has
n+1
simple real eigenvalues.

To prove this proposition, we consider the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

B
and
J
are isospectral.

proof of Lemma
3.2
.
Let
\lambda_{m}(m=1,\dots,n+1)
be an eigenvalue of
B
and
{\bf v}_{m}
be the corresponding eigenvector. Then
\lambda
and
v_{m}
satisfy
B{\bf v}_{m}=\lambda_{m}{\bf v}_{m}
. Noting that
J=D_{\pi^{1/2}}^{-1}BD_{\pi^{1/2}}
, we have

\displaystyle D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}B{\bf v}_{m}

\displaystyle=D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}\lambda_{m}{\bf v}_{m},

\displaystyle D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}BD_{\pi^{1/2}}D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}{\bf v}_{m}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}{\bf v}_{m},

\displaystyle JD^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}{\bf v}_{m}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}{\bf v}_{m}.

Thus
\lambda_{m}
is an eigenvalue of
J
and
D^{-1}_{\pi^{1/2}}{\bf v}_{m}
is the corresponding eigenvector. Since this correspondence holds for any eigenvalue of
B
,
{\rm Spec}(B)\subset{\rm Spec}(J)
. The similar argument holds for the inverse as well.
\square

By general argument about the Jacobi matrix, all of the eigenvalues of
J
are simple. Combining this with Lemma
3.2
, we obtain Proposition
3.1
.

4
A spectral analysis of CRW on the Path

In this section, we give a framework of spectral analysis for CRW on
P_{n+1}
. In order to do so, we restrict the coin operator as follows:

Assumption 4.1
.

We assume that the coin operator consists of isospectral probability transition matrices, i.e., we use

\displaystyle C=\sum_{x=0}^{n}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\Bigl\{(1-\nu_{2})|w_{2,x}\rangle\langle-q_{2,x}|+I_{2}\Bigr\},

as the coin operator, where
\nu_{2}=p_{x,L}-p_{x,R}
for all
x=0,\dots,n
.

4.1
The spectrum of
U
from
B

This subsection deals with the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of
U
related to those of
B
.

Let
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{n+1}
be the eigenvalues and
{\bf v}_{m}=\ ^{T}\begin{bmatrix}v_{m}(0)&\dots&v_{m}(n)\end{bmatrix}

(m=1,\dots,n+1)
be the corresponding normalized eigenvectors of
B
. For each
\lambda_{m}
and
{\bf v}_{m}
, we define two vectors

\displaystyle{\bf a}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{m}(x)\bigl(|x\rangle\otimes|w_{2}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\Bigl(v_{m}(x)|x\rangle\otimes\bigl(|L\rangle-|R\rangle\bigr)\Bigr),

\displaystyle{\bf b}_{m}

\displaystyle=S{\bf a}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x=1}^{n-1}|x\rangle\otimes\Bigl(v_{m}(x+1)|R\rangle-v_{m}(x-1)|L\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{|0\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\Bigl(v_{m}(1)|R\rangle+v_{m}(0)|L\rangle\Bigr)+\frac{|n\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\Bigl(-v_{m}(n)|R\rangle-v_{m}(n-1)|L\rangle\Bigr).

By using
S^{2}=I_{n+1}\otimes I_{2}
, it is easy to see that
C{\bf a}_{m}=\nu_{2}{\bf a}_{m}
and then
U{\bf a}_{m}=\nu_{2}{\bf b}_{m}
. Also we have
C{\bf b}_{m}=(1-\nu_{2})\lambda_{m}{\bf a}_{m}+{\bf b}_{m}
and
U{\bf b}_{m}={\bf a}_{m}+(1-\nu_{2})\lambda_{m}{\bf b}_{m}
. So we have the following relationship:

\displaystyle U\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}_{m}\\
{\bf b}_{m}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\nu_{2}\\
1&(1-\nu_{2})\lambda_{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}_{m}\\
{\bf b}_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

We also obtain
\|{\bf a}_{m}\|=\|{\bf b}_{m}\|=1
. Let
({\bf a}_{m},{\bf b}_{m})
be the inner product of vectors
{\bf a}_{m}
and
{\bf b}_{m}
. When
|({\bf a}_{m},{\bf b}_{m})|<1
,
{\bf a}_{m}
and
{\bf b}_{m}
are linearly independent. About the linearly independence, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

The vectors
{\bf a}_{m}
and
{\bf b}_{m}=S{\bf a}_{m}
which are related to
\lambda_{m}
are linearly dependent if and only if
\lambda_{n+1}=-1
.

proof of Lemma
4.2
.
By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle({\bf a}_{m},{\bf b}_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x=1}^{n-1}\bigl(-v_{m}(x-1)v_{m}(x)-v_{m}(x)v_{m}(x+1)\bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|v_{m}(0)|^{2}-v_{m}(0)v_{m}(1)\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(-v_{m}(n-1)v_{m}(n)+|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x=1}^{n}\Bigl(v_{m}(x-1)v_{m}(x)\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|v_{m}(0)|^{2}+|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr).

By triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle|({\bf a}_{m},{\bf b}_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x=1}^{n}|v_{m}(x-1)v_{m}(x)|+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|v_{m}(0)|^{2}+|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\Biggl(\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}|v_{m}(x)|^{2}\Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{x=1}^{n}|v_{m}(x)|^{2}\Biggr)}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|v_{m}(0)|^{2}+|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\bigl(1-|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr)\bigl(1-|v_{m}(0)|^{2}\bigr)}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|v_{m}(0)|^{2}+|v_{m}(n)|^{2}\bigr).

(3)

Since the rightmost side of inequality (
3
) takes the maximum value
1
when
|v_{m}(0)|=|v_{m}(n)|
, we have

\displaystyle|({\bf a}_{m},{\bf b}_{m})|\leq 1.

(4)

Although inequality (
4
) is trivial, the conditions for equality in inequalities (
3
) and (
4
) are important. By the equality condition of triangle inequality in (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle v_{m}(x-1)v_{m}(x)\leq 0\quad(x=1,\dots,n).

(5)

The equality condition of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle v_{m}(x-1)v_{m}(y)=v_{m}(x)v_{m}(y-1),

(6)

where
x\neq y
and
x,y=1,\dots,n
. Combining
|v_{m}(0)|=|v_{m}(n)|
with conditions (

) and (

\displaystyle v_{m}(x)=-v_{m}(x-1)\quad(x=1,\dots,n).

Owing to
\|{\bf v}_{m}\|=1

\displaystyle v_{m}(0)=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}},v_{m}(1)=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}},\dots,

(7)

where the double-sign corresponds. Because both of the case in (

B
corresponding to
\lambda_{m}=-1

\square

Another spectrum of
U

In this subsection, we consider the eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenvector of
U
which is not related to
B
. We should note that we already have
2n+1
eigenvalues and eigenvectors from
B
, and
U
is a
2(n+1)\times 2(n+1)
probability transition matrix. Thus, we still need one eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenvector of
U
.

We consider the following vector:

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{0}(x)|x\rangle\otimes\frac{1}{1-\nu_{2}}\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,R}\\
1-p_{x,L}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{0}(x)|x\rangle\otimes|w_{1,x}\rangle,

where
\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{0}(x)=1
. Noting that all of the eigenvectors of
U
obtained from
B
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{n}\Bigl\{\langle x|\otimes\bigl(\langle L|+\langle R|\bigr)\Bigr\}{\bf u}_{m}=0,

(11)

where
m\in\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm n,n+1\}
, we have
{\bf u}_{0}\notin{\rm Span}({\bf u}_{\pm 1},\dots,{\bf u}_{\pm n},{\bf u}_{n+1})
. Eq. (
11
) means that we can not obtain any probability distribution from any states in Span
({\bf u}_{\pm 1},\dots,{\bf u}_{\pm n},{\bf u}_{n+1})
. Since Eq. (
1
) shows
C_{x}|w_{1,x}\rangle=|w_{1,x}\rangle
for all
x=0,\dots,n
,
{\bf u}_{0}
is the eigenvector of the coin operator
C
, corresponding to the eigenvalue
\nu_{1}=1
. Therefore when
{\bf u}_{0}
also satisfies
S{\bf u}_{0}={\bf u}_{0}
,
{\bf u}_{0}
is the eigenvector of the time evolution operator
U
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{0}=1
. Thus we have the following conditions:

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)(1-p_{x,L})

\displaystyle=v_{0}(x+1)p_{x+1,R},

\displaystyle v_{0}(x+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p_{x,L}}{p_{x+1,R}}v_{0}(x),

(12)

where
x=0,\dots,n-1
. By Eq. (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)=\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}v_{0}(0),

where
x=1,\dots,n
. Owing to
\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{0}(x)=1
, we have

\displaystyle v_{0}(0)=\Biggl(1+\sum_{x=1}^{n}\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}\Biggr)^{-1},

and
v_{0}
can be regarded as the probability distribution. We should note that

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{n}\Bigl\{\langle x|\otimes\bigl(\langle L|+\langle R|\bigr)\Bigr\}{\bf u}_{0}=\sum_{x=0}^{n}v_{0}(x)=1.

(13)

Let
Q
be a probability transition matrix described as follows:

\displaystyle Q=\frac{1}{1-\nu_{2}}\begin{bmatrix}p_{0,R}&p_{1,R}\\
1-p_{0,L}&0&p_{2,R}\\
&1-p_{1,L}&0&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&\ddots&0&p_{n-1,R}\\
&&&&1-p_{n-2,L}&0&p_{n,R}\\
&&&&&1-p_{n-1,L}&1-p_{n,L}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, we can regard
v_{0}
as the probability distribution of RW on the path whose probability transition matrix is

. We see that
v_{0}
satisfies the detailed balance conditions by Eq. (

). Thus,
v_{0}
is the stationary distribution of birth and death chains. Combining this with Eq. (

Moreover, by the irreducibility of
U
and the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the largest eigenvalue
\mu_{0}=1
of the probability transition matrix

are not

4.3
Main results

This subsection is devoted to our main results.

As a consequence of our discussion so far, we obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 4.4
.

If
\nu_{2}>0
or
\nu_{2}<0
and every
\lambda_{m}(m=1,\dots,n+1)
satisfies
\frac{\sqrt{-4\nu_{2}}}{1-\nu_{2}}<|\lambda_{m}|\leq 1
, every element in
{\rm Spec}(U)
is simple. The eigenvalues
\mu_{0},\mu_{\pm m},\mu_{n+1}
and the corresponding eigenvectors
{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{\pm m},{\bf u}_{n+1}
are following:

(1)\ \mu_{0}=1
and

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{0}=\sum_{x=0}^{n}\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}v_{0}(0)|x\rangle\otimes\frac{1}{1-\nu_{2}}\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,R}\\
1-p_{x,L}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\rm where}\ v_{0}(0)=\Biggl(1+\sum_{x=1}^{n}\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}\Biggr)^{-1}.

(2)
For
m=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\pm m}=\frac{(1-\nu_{2})\lambda_{m}\pm\sqrt{(1-\nu_{2})^{2}\lambda_{m}^{2}+4\nu_{2}}}{2},

and

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{\pm m}

\displaystyle={\bf a}_{m}+\mu_{\pm m}{\bf b}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x=1}^{n-1}|x\rangle\otimes\Bigl\{\bigl(v_{m}(x)-\mu_{\pm m}v_{m}(x-1)\bigr)|L\rangle+\bigl(\mu_{\pm m}v_{m}(x+1)-v_{m}(x)\bigr)|R\rangle\bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle+\frac{|0\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\Bigl\{\bigl(1+\mu_{\pm m}\bigr)v_{m}(0)|L\rangle+\bigl(\mu_{\pm m}v_{m}(1)-v_{m}(0)\bigr)|R\rangle\Bigr\}

\displaystyle+\frac{|n\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\Bigl\{\bigl(v_{m}(n)-\mu_{\pm m}v_{m}(n-1)\bigr)|L\rangle-\bigl(1+\mu_{\pm m}\bigr)v_{m}(n)|R\rangle\Bigr\}.

(3)\ \mu_{n+1}=\nu_{2}
and

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\bf a}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\Bigl\{\frac{(-1)^{x}}{\sqrt{n+1}}|x\rangle\otimes\bigl(|L\rangle-|R\rangle\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Let
\langle{\bf q}_{m}|\ (m=0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm n,n+1)
be the projection onto
{\bf u}_{m}=|{\bf u}_{m}\rangle
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n+1}
. By Theorem
4.4
and the linear independence of the eigenvectors, we obtain the following description about the time evolution operator
U
.

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm n,n+1\}}\mu_{m}|{\bf u}_{m}\rangle\langle{\bf q}_{m}|,

\displaystyle U^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm n,n+1\}}\mu_{m}^{t}|{\bf u}_{m}\rangle\langle{\bf q}_{m}|.

We have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

If
\nu_{2}>0
or
\nu_{2}<0
and every
\lambda_{m}(m=1,\dots,n+1)
satisfies
\frac{\sqrt{-4\nu_{2}}}{1-\nu_{2}}<|\lambda_{m}|\leq 1
, there exists the following limiting distribution
p_{\infty,n}
of CRW on the path
P_{n+1}
,

\displaystyle p_{\infty,n}(0)

\displaystyle=\Biggl(1+\sum_{x=1}^{n}\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}\Biggr)^{-1},

(14)

\displaystyle p_{\infty,n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}(1-p_{y,L})}{\prod_{y=1}^{x}p_{y,R}}p_{\infty,n}(0)\quad(x=1,\dots,n).

(15)

proof of Corollary
4.5
.
Since the eigenvectors of
U
are linearly independent, the initial state of CRW
|\varphi\rangle
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle|\varphi\rangle=\sum_{m\in\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm n,n+1\}}\alpha_{m}|{\bf u}_{m}\rangle,

where

\displaystyle|\Psi_{t}\rangle

5
Summary

In this paper, we considered CRW on the path graph. We obtained the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of the time evolution operator of CRWs with isospectral but need not the same coins. Most of those eigenvalues and eigenvectors were constructed by those of the Jacobi matrix. We also had the rest eigenvalue
1
and the corresponding eigenvector coming from the reversible distribution, hence the limit distribution was given by the eigenvector. One of the interesting future problems is considering the CRW on more general graphs.
