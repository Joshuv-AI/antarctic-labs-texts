Physics-guided weak-form discovery of reduced-order models for trapped ultracold hydrodynamics
Reuben R. W. Wang, Daniel Messenger

Abstract
We study the relaxation of a highly collisional, ultracold but nondegenerate gas of polar molecules. Confined within a harmonic trap, the gas is subject to fluid-gaseous coupled dynamics that lead to a breakdown of first-order hydrodynamics. An attempt to treat these higher-order hydrodynamic effects was previously made with a Gaussian ansatz and coarse-graining model parameter [R. R. W. Wang & J. L. Bohn, Phys. Rev. A 108, 013322 (2023) ], leading to an approximate set of equations for a few collective observables accessible to experiments. Here we present substantially improved reduced-order models for these same observables, admissible beyond previous parameter regimes, discovered directly from particle simulations using the WSINDy algorithm (Weak-form Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics). The interpretable nature of the learning algorithm enables estimation of previously unknown physical quantities and discovery of model terms with candidate physical mechanisms, revealing new physics in mixed collisional regimes. Our approach constitutes a general framework for data-driven model identification leveraging known physics.

Trapped Hydrodynamics.

We focus on cross-dimensional rethermalization experiments
[
35
,
36
]
, designed to probe the re-equilibration rate of a gas and, consequently, the rate at which forced evaporation of molecules should occur.
These experiments are performed at temperatures above quantum degeneracy where evaporation typically commences, and consists of a near-instantaneous initial excitation of the gas along one direction before allowing it to relax.
When nondegenerate, molecular motion is well described by classical trajectories so that a collisionally-thick molecular gas, which rapidly thermalizes the local momentum distribution, is likened to a classical thermoviscous fluid
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.
Gas dynamics is then best described by the equations of fluid mechanics
[
19
]
(see SI Sec. 1), incorporating the transport tensors of thermal conductivity
\kappa_{ij}
and viscosity
\mu_{ijk\ell}

[
40
,
41
,
42
]
arising from quantum dipolar collisions
[
43
]
. The tensor subscripts denote coordinate indices.

Unfortunately, the fluid description can get muddied in optical dipole traps
[
44
]
that confine these molecular samples, as they give rise to soft boundary conditions. In particular, deep optical dipole traps that confine molecules of mass
m
are well approximated by a cylindrically symmetric harmonic potential

V(\bm{r})=m\omega_{\perp}^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2}+\lambda z^{2})/2
, where
\omega_{\perp}
is the radial trapping frequency and
\lambda=(\omega_{z}/\omega_{\perp})^{2}
is the trap aspect ratio,
indeed causing the gas density to decay away from the trap center. Sufficiently far from the trap center, we expect that the gas is mostly dilute, leading to the presence of a fluid-gaseous interstitial region.
Ref.
[
17
]
attempts a reductionist approach to this issue, by employing an empirically determined stationary coarse-graining parameter, the hydrodynamic volume
{\cal V}_{\rm hy}
, to subsume all multiphase fluid dynamics
2
2

2

A similar treatment has been attempted by Ref.
[
39
]
for isotropic scatterers.
. This approximation allowed the derivation of a reduced-order model for cross-dimensional rethermalization dynamics with a Gaussian ansatz

\rho(\bm{r},t)=mN\prod_{i=1}^{3}\exp(-\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}(t)})/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}(t)}
to describe the density profile.
As a result, the trapped gas was found to have its anisotropic Gaussian widths
\sigma_{i}(t)
governed approximately by the ordinary differential equations (ODEs)

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{i}(\bm{\sigma},\dot{\bm{\sigma}},\ddot{\bm{\sigma}})-\frac{2k_{B}T_{0}}{m\sigma_{i}}=-\frac{2}{5}\frac{{\cal V}_{\rm hy}}{Nm}\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{iijj}(T)}{\sigma_{i}}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{j}}{\sigma_{j}},

(1)

where
{\rm E}_{i}(\bm{\sigma},\dot{\bm{\sigma}},\ddot{\bm{\sigma}}):=\ddot{\sigma}_{i}+\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}+\frac{1}{3\sigma_{i}}\sum_{j}(\omega_{j}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}+\dot{\sigma}_{j}^{2})

Above,

the number of molecules and

The Supplementary Information (SI) provides further details on deriving the model above, which asserts the ideal gas law equation of state, an approximately spatially uniform viscosity over the sample, and a particular flow velocity field given by
U_{i}(\bm{r},t)=[\dot{\sigma}_{i}(t)/\sigma_{i}(t)]r_{i}

While Eq. (
1

1

We expect the newly learned model terms to arise predominantly from second-order hydrodynamics
[

47

[
22

Our machine-assisted, physics-informed approach is able to overcome these complications, enabling discovery of effective ROMs for the trapped gas dynamics.

Weak-form equation learning
.
We perform model discovery with
weak-form equation learning (WFEL), which attempts to learn a representation of a differential equation in a suitable
weak formulation
using discretely sampled solution data. For an ODE
\dot{{\bm{x}}}(t)={\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}(t))
in
d
dimensions with initial data
{\bm{x}}(0)={\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its weak formulation is obtained by integrating against a smooth, compactly-supported
test function

\boldsymbol{\phi}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and using integration by parts to get

-\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\cdot{\bm{x}}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)\cdot{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}(t))\,dt.

(2)

When equation (
2
) holds for all continuously differentiable
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, we say that
{\bm{x}}
is a
weak solution
to the ODE. The objective of WFEL is to identify the vector field
{\bm{f}}
governing the dynamics of
{\bm{x}}
by leveraging Eq. (
2
).

WSINDy extends the SINDy framework (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics)
[
32
]
to accomplish this task, whereby
{\bm{f}}
is assumed to satisfy
{\bm{f}}_{i}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{w}^{\star}_{ji}f_{j}({\bm{x}}):=\mathbb{L}({\bm{x}})\mathbf{w}^{\star}_{i}
for a sparse
weight matrix

\mathbf{w}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
and a library of
trial functions

\mathbb{L}=\{f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{j=1}^{J}
. We use the notation
\mathbb{L}({\bm{x}})
to refer to the
quasi-matrix
with columns given by the functions
f_{j}({\bm{x}}(\cdot)):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. A weak formulation for inference is defined by specifying a finite set of test functions
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{K}\}
and computing a linear system
(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{b})
according to (
2
), with

\mathbf{G}_{kj}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{k}(t)f_{j}({\bm{x}}(t))\,dt,\>\>\>\mathbf{b}_{ki}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{\phi}_{k}(t){\bm{x}}_{i}(t)\,dt.

(3)

As defined above,
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{w}^{\star}=\mathbf{b}
holds exactly on weak solutions
{\bm{x}}
. In practice,
(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{b})
is computed from corrupted data
\mathbf{y}={\bm{x}}(\mathbf{t})+{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
sampled on a timegrid
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{M})
, where
{\bm{x}}
is a (weak) solution to
\dot{{\bm{x}}}(t)={\bm{F}}({\bm{x}}(t))
. The corruptions
{\bm{e}}
may encapsulate measurement noise or model error. In the current setting,
{\bm{e}}
is associated with Monte Carlo sampling error of physical quantities with numerical methods discussed later.
The integrals in (
3
) are computed from observations
\mathbf{y}
using the trapezoidal rule, a highly accurate scheme when
{\bm{e}}=0
and
\phi_{k}
decays smoothly to the edge of its support
[
33
]
. An approximate solution
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}\approx\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is then found by solving
\mathbf{G}{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}\approx\mathbf{b}
using sparse regression
[
32
]
, as outlined in SI Sec. 2.

Learning with physical knowledge.

We systematically incorporated knowledge of the underlying physics into the optimization problem for
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}
, constituting three learning strategies:

naïve discovery
(ND),
ansatz verification
(AV), and
physics-informed
(PI) learning. These strategies enforce different degrees of physical knowledge to a) inform the library
\mathbb{L}
and b) constrain some coefficients in
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}
using the ansatz (
1
). See Table
1
for choices of
\mathbb{L}

Resulting WSINDy models will be referred to as WSINDy-ND, WSINDy-AV, and WSINDy-PI, respectively.

In the ND approach, a standard monomial library is used for
\mathbb{L}
and
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}
is left unconstrained. For the AV and PI approaches,
\mathbb{L}
is motivated from known approximations used to derive (
1

are removed from the learning process, with their respective coefficients

restricted to values derived in (
1
). Specifically, let

be the weight matrix of coefficients from the ansatz model (
1
), and let

be the index set for terms removed from the learning process in the
i
th equation. We then let
\mathbf{G}^{(i)}=\mathbf{G}_{(S_{i}^{0})^{c}}
,
\mathbf{b}^{(i)}=\mathbf{b}_{i}-\mathbf{G}_{S_{i}^{0}}\mathbf{w}^{\text{Ans}}_{i,S_{i}^{0}}

where
\mathbf{G}_{(S_{i}^{0})^{c}}
is the restriction of
\mathbf{G}
to the column indices
(S_{i}^{0})^{c}=[J]\setminus S_{i}^{0}
, and
\mathbf{w}^{\text{Ans}}_{i,S_{i}^{0}}
is the restriction of the

Monte Carlo experiments.
As a proxy for real experimental data, we perform simulations of the gas dynamics by numerical integration of the Boltzmann equation
[
48
]
.
Treating the particles as discrete points in classical phase-space, they are first sampled from the initial out-of-equilibrium distribution, then evolved in discrete time via the Störmer-Verlet symplectic integrator
[
49
]
.
Two-body collisions between particles are sampled using the direct simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method
[
50
,
51
,
17
]
, which exploits the locality of interactions for computational efficiency.
In our implementation, the simulation volume is first adaptively partitioned into discrete grid cells of local density-dependent volumes, into which the simulated molecules are binned based on their positions. Collisions are then sampled within each grid cell based on the scattering cross section between ultracold dipoles
[
52
]
, leading to updates in the molecular-pair momenta. For all our simulations, we take the molecular dipoles to be polarized along
\hat{\bm{z}}
, enforcing cylindrical symmetry of the system in tandem with the trap
V(\bm{r})
.
We leverage previous findings from Ref.
[
17
]
and restrict learning to be done on
\sigma_{i}^{2}(t)=\sum_{k}(r_{i}^{(k)})^{2}/N
from the simulation particle ensemble, summed over simulated particles indexed by
k
.
These observables mimic experimental data that would be extracted from in-situ absorption imaging of the molecular gas cloud
[
53
]
, permitting a natural extension to learning on real experimental data.

Physics-Informed Discovery (PI).

\lambda

I
The Ansatz Model

While detailed in Ref.
[
17
]
, we briefly outline the derivation of
the ansatz model from the fluid equations for completeness of this document.
The hydrodynamic equations of motion for a nondegenerate gas are
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\left({\rho U_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho U_{i}\right)+\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\left(\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{i}\left(nk_{B}T\right)-n\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})+\sum_{j,k,\ell}\partial_{j}\left(\mu_{ijk\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right),

(1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho T)+\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\left(\rho TU_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}\rho T\sum_{i}\partial_{i}U_{i}+\frac{2m}{3k_{B}}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}(\partial_{j}U_{i})\mu_{ijk\ell}(\partial_{\ell}U_{k})+\frac{2m}{3k_{B}}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\left(\kappa_{ij}\partial_{j}T\right),

(1c)

written in terms of the molecular velocity averaged field variables of mass density, flow velocity and temperature:

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=mn(\bm{r},t)=\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)m,

(2a)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(\bm{r},t)}\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)\bm{v},

(2b)

\displaystyle T(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3n(\bm{r},t)k_{B}}\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)\frac{1}{2}m\bm{u}^{2},

(2c)

respectively.
Above,
f(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)
denotes the molecular phase space distribution and
\bm{u}(\bm{r})=\bm{v}-\bm{U}(\bm{r})
is the comoving molecular velocity, relative to the frame of fluid flow.
Of concern to this study are molecular gases confined within a deep optical dipole trap
[
44
]
, well approximated by a cylindrically symmetric harmonic potential
V(\bm{r})
. Such external confinement leads to soft boundary conditions, conceivably complicating the fluid dynamics via the fluid-gaseous couplings.
Appropriate treatment of transport on the molecular level is thus paramount to an accurate description of the continuum dynamics, most readily included via the transport tensors of thermal conductivity
\kappa_{ij}
, and viscosity
\mu_{ijk\ell}
.

The interplay of dilute and hydrodynamic regions complicates reduced-order modeling of the dynamics, though Ref.
[
17
]
has attempted a first pass by employing a fluctuating Gaussian density to derive effective equations of motion. Although requiring several levels of approximation, the model of Ref.
[
17
]
showed favorable agreement to data generated from full Monte Carlo simulations, making it a useful benchmark for the machine-assisted learning methods applied here.
That effective model treats the effects of thermal transport as negligible, so we omit any further explicit references to the thermal conductivity.
At nondegenerate temperatures, the thermal energy is expected to greatly exceed the average dipolar mean-field energy per particle, leading to transport tensors that predominantly arise from two-body collisions.
A collision-induced viscosity is approximated to first-order in the Chapman-Enskog expansion by
[
58
]

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\beta}\left(\frac{n}{m\beta}\right)^{2}\left(\int d^{3}u\bm{W}(\bm{u})\otimes C[f_{0}\bm{W}]\right)^{-1},

(3)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
is the usual inverse temperature and
\bm{W}=\bm{u}\bm{u}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{u}^{2}\bm{I}
is a rank-2 comoving velocity tensor with the identity matrix
\bm{I}
.
The collision integral

\displaystyle C[f_{0}\bm{W}]

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}u_{1}|{\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{1}}|f_{0}(\bm{u})f_{0}(\bm{u}_{1})\int d\Omega^{\prime}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta\bm{W},

(4)

with
\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{W}^{\prime}+\bm{W}^{\prime}_{1}-\bm{W}-\bm{W}_{1}
and primes denoting post-collision quantities,
are evaluated with the Maxwell-Boltzmann equilibrium phase space distribution function
f_{0}(\bm{u})

[
48
]
.
The symbol
\otimes
denotes a dyadic product which takes two tensors of rank
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, and forms a tensor of rank
N_{1}+N_{2}
(e.g.
A_{ij}\otimes B_{k\ell}=C_{ijk\ell}
). Notably, the differential cross section
d\sigma/d\Omega
, resultant from ultracold field-polarized molecular scattering is one necessarily described by quantum mechanics
[
43
]
, and has an analytic form in the low energy regime
[
52
]
.
Refs.
[
40
,
41
]
have used this cross section to derive analytic expressions for
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\kappa}
.

In general, direct solutions to Eq. (
1
) require involved numerical solvers to obtain them. We therefore aim to construct a reduced order model by limiting the initial excitations to ones induced by increasing
\omega_{z}
instantaneously, restricting the dynamics to be effectively encompassed by a small set of collective observables.
One way of obtaining such a reduced order model is with a suitably chosen ansatz and ensemble averaging, a task we briefly describe below.

I.1
Fluctuating Gaussian ansatz: background from [Wang & Bohn, PRA 108, 013322 (2023)]

At equilibrium, the density distribution enforced by a harmonic trap follows

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{mN}{Z}\exp\left(-\frac{V(\bm{r})}{k_{B}T_{0}}\right),

(5)

where
Z=\int d^{3}r{\rm e}^{-\frac{V(\bm{r})}{k_{B}T_{0}}}
gives the appropriate normalization and
N
is the number of molecules.
The out-of-equilibrium excitations primarily induced by a trap quench of
\omega_{z}
, are oscillatory modes in the Gaussian density distribution widths, motivating an ansatz of the form

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=mN\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}(t)}\right),

(6)

where
\sigma_{i}(t)
is the distribution widths along each axis
i
that we allow to vary in time.
These widths will be taken as the observables of interest to the gas dynamics, obtainable in ultracold experiments with in-situ imaging of the sample. Enforcing the continuity equation (
1a
) on (
6
) yields a solution for the flow field

U_{i}({\bm{r}})=\left(\frac{\dot{\sigma_{i}}(t)}{\sigma_{i}(t)}\right)r_{i}.

(7)

Under this approximation, Ref.
[
17
]
derives a set of coupled differential equations that govern the time evolution of each
\sigma_{i}
from the momentum balance (
1b
) and average temperature (
2c
):

\displaystyle\ddot{\sigma}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle+\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{3\sigma_{i}(t)}\sum_{j}\left[\omega_{j}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}(t)+\dot{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(t)\right]-\frac{2k_{B}T_{0}}{m\sigma_{i}(t)}\approx-\frac{2}{5}\frac{{\cal V}_{\rm hy}}{Nm}\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{iijj}(T(t))}{\sigma_{i}(t)}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{j}(t)}{\sigma_{j}(t)},

(8)

where the relevant viscosity matrix elements are written in terms of a unit-free matrix

\displaystyle M_{ij}(\Theta)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mu_{iijj}(T;\Theta)}{\mu_{0}(T)}

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{512}\begin{pmatrix}117\cos(4\Theta)+84\cos(2\Theta)+415&-28(3\cos(2\Theta)+11)&-(117\cos(4\Theta)+107)\\[2.5pt]
-28(3\cos(2\Theta)+11)&616&28(3\cos(2\Theta)-11)\\[2.5pt]
-(117\cos(4\Theta)+107)&28(3\cos(2\Theta)-11)&117\cos(4\Theta)-84\cos(2\Theta)+415\end{pmatrix},

with the isotropic viscosity coefficient given by

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{16a_{d}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{mk_{B}T}{\pi}}.

(10)

See SI.
I.2
for more further details on deriving Eq. (
8
).
Above,
\Theta=\cos^{-1}\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}}\cdot\hat{\bm{z}}
parameterizes the dipole orientation axis
\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}}
, which we restrict to lie within the
x,z
-plane.
The hydrodynamic volume
{\cal V}_{\rm hy}
is a coarse-graining parameter necessary to formulate the ansatz model, identified as the effective volume over which viscosity serves as a good description of dissipative processes.
In
[
17
]
,
{\cal V}_{\rm hy}
was empirically determined to have the approximate functional form

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\rm hy}(\lambda,N,\Theta)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{6k_{B}T_{0}}{m\omega_{\perp}^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\left[2.21+0.67\left(1+0.26\frac{\sigma_{\rm coll}(\Theta)}{\overline{\sigma}_{\rm coll}}\right)\frac{N}{10^{5}}\right],

(11)

for cigar geometries (
\lambda<1
), where
\lambda=(\omega_{z}/\omega_{\perp})^{2}
is the aspect ratio of the cylindrically symmetric trap and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm coll}(\Theta)=a_{d}^{2}\frac{\pi}{3}\big[3+18\cos^{2}\Theta-13\cos^{4}\Theta\big],

(12)

is the elastic total collision cross section with its angular average being
\overline{\sigma}_{\rm coll}={32\pi a_{d}^{2}/15}

[
52
]
.

We consider the particular case of
\Theta=0
(i.e. aligning the dipoles along the trap
z
axis) in this work, so the relevant viscosity tensor and hydrodynamic volume greatly simplify to

\displaystyle\mu_{iijj}(T;0)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}(T)M_{ij}(0)=\frac{\mu_{0}(T)}{64}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}77&-49&-28\\
-49&77&-28\\
-28&-28&56\\
\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\rm hy}(\lambda,N,0)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{6k_{B}T_{0}}{m\omega_{\perp}^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\left[2.21+0.89\frac{N}{10^{5}}\right],

enforcing a strict cylindrical symmetry about
z
in the system.
The equations of motion are therefore fully encapsulated by the dynamics of just
\sigma_{\perp}(t)
and
\sigma_{z}(t)
as stated in the main text.

I.2
Fluctuating Gaussian ansatz: extended derivation with flow field corrections

Identification of physical mechanisms behind terms identified in the WSINDy-PI models leads to consideration of extensions to the ansatz model introduced above. In particular, one can consider perturbations
W_{i}
of the flow field (
7
). Letting the flow field take the form
U_{i}=V_{i}+W_{i}
with
V_{i}
given by (
7
), the continuity equation (
1a
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\left(\partial_{i}-\frac{r_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\cdot W_{i}=0,

(14)

which in turn implies
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{-2}\left\langle r_{i}W_{i}\right\rangle=0
using integration by parts and the decay of
\rho(\bm{r},t)
away from the trap center, where
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle=(1/N)\int d^{3}r(\cdot)n({\bm{r}},t)
denotes the spatial average. If we restrict each
W_{i}
to satisfy
\left\langle r_{i}W_{i}\right\rangle=0
individually, then we have
\left\langle V_{i}W_{i}\right\rangle=0
, so that the perturbation
W_{i}
is uncorrelated with
V_{i}
. We will assume this in the simplified derivation below, which leads to equations of motion for the widths
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
that naturally incorporate such flow field corrections. Additionally, we present a specific flow correction
\bm{W}
that satisfies these constraints and leads to terms identified in the WSINDy-PI models.

First, we filter the Navier-Stokes equations (
1b
) by multiplying each equation by
r_{i}
and integrating over space. Using integration by parts together with the rapidly decaying density assumption, the following integral quantities transform according to

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\partial_{t}(\rho U_{i})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\rho U_{i}

(15a)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\left(\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}r\,\sum_{j}\left[\partial_{j}\left(r_{i}\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right)-\delta_{ij}\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right]=-\int d^{3}r\,\rho(U_{i})^{2}

(15b)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\partial_{i}\left(nk_{B}T\right)

\displaystyle=-\int d^{3}r\,nk_{B}T

(15c)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}n\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}^{2}\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}^{2}\rho=mN\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}

(15d)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\sum_{j,k,\ell}\partial_{j}\left(\mu_{ijk\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int d^{3}r\,\sum_{j,k,\ell}\delta_{ij}\left(\mu_{ijk\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right)=-\int d^{3}r\,\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\mu_{iik\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right)

(15e)

With this in mind, we need only calculate the quantities on the right-hand sides in terms of
\bm{V}
and
\bm{W}
, assumed to be uncorrelated:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\rho U_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int d^{3}r\,r_{i}\rho V_{i}=mN\sigma_{i}\ddot{\sigma}_{i}+mN(\dot{\sigma}_{i})^{2},

(16a)

\displaystyle-\int d^{3}r\,\rho(U_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int d^{3}r\,\rho(V_{i})^{2}-\int d^{3}r\,\rho(W_{i})^{2}=-mN(\dot{\sigma}_{i})^{2}-mN\left\langle W_{i}^{2}\right\rangle,

(16b)

\displaystyle-\int d^{3}r\,nk_{B}T

\displaystyle=-Nk_{B}\left\langle T\right\rangle,

(16c)

\displaystyle-\int d^{3}r\,\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\mu_{iik\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=-N\left\langle\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\frac{\mu_{iik\ell}}{n}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}\right)\right\rangle=-N\sum_{k}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iikk}}{n}\right\rangle\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}-N\left\langle\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\frac{\mu_{iik\ell}}{n}\partial_{\ell}W_{k}\right)\right\rangle.

(16d)

Putting this all together, the NS equations become, after dividing through by
mN\sigma_{i}
,

\displaystyle\ddot{\sigma}_{i}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\left\langle W_{i}^{2}\right\rangle+\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}-\frac{k_{B}}{m\sigma_{i}}\left\langle T\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iikk}}{n}\right\rangle\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{k}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k}}-\frac{1}{m\sigma_{i}}\left\langle\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\frac{\mu_{iik\ell}}{n}\partial_{\ell}W_{k}\right)\right\rangle.

(17)

For the average temperature, we have by definition that

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}nk_{B}T

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\int d^{3}vf(r,v,t)v_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho U_{i}^{2}\right),

(18)

and

\displaystyle E_{\rm total}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\omega_{i}^{2}\left\langle r_{i}^{2}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{N}\int d^{3}rd^{3}vf(r,v,t)v_{i}^{2}\right)=3k_{B}T_{0},

(19)

with
T_{0}
the equilibrium temperature (assumed constant in space), so that

\displaystyle 3k_{B}T_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int d^{3}r\rho(U_{i})^{2}+\frac{3}{2}k_{B}\left\langle T\right\rangle,

(20)

or

\displaystyle\frac{k_{B}}{m}\left\langle T\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2k_{B}T_{0}}{m}-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left((\dot{\sigma}_{i})^{2}+\left\langle W_{i}^{2}\right\rangle\right).

(21)

Substituting this in for
\left\langle T\right\rangle
above, we get

\displaystyle\ddot{\sigma}_{i}(t)+\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{3\sigma_{i}(t)}\sum_{j}

\displaystyle\left[\omega_{j}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}(t)+\dot{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(t)\right]-\frac{2k_{B}T_{0}}{m\sigma_{i}(t)}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iikk}}{n}\right\rangle\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{k}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k}}

(22a)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\left\langle W_{i}^{2}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\left\langle W_{j}^{2}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{m\sigma_{i}}\sum_{k,\ell,p}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iik\ell}}{n}\partial_{\ell}W_{k}\right\rangle

(22b)

where we see that (
22a
) contains the original ansatz (resulting from only
V_{i}
) while (
22b
) contains the effects of velocity corrections
W_{i}
. Using the particular form

W_{i}=\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}r_{k}

(23)

for
\alpha_{j}
undetermined (and possibly time-dependent), which is consistent with the continuity equation and additional satisfies
\left\langle V_{i}W_{i}\right\rangle=0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\ddot{\sigma}_{i}(t)+\omega_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{3\sigma_{i}(t)}\sum_{j}

\displaystyle\left[\omega_{j}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}(t)+\dot{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(t)\right]-\frac{2k_{B}T_{0}}{m\sigma_{i}(t)}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iikk}}{\nu}\right\rangle\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{k}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k}}

(24a)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,k}|\varepsilon_{ijk}|\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j,l,m}|\varepsilon_{j\ell m}|\frac{\alpha_{\ell}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{m}^{2}}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k,\ell,p}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{iik\ell}}{n}\right\rangle\varepsilon_{kp\ell}\frac{\alpha_{p}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}

(24b)

which provides one possible explanation for the inverse cubic terms appearing in the WSINDy-PI models.

II
WSINDy algorithmic details

The quasi-matrix libraries in Tab. 1 of the main text are given a matrix representation when evaluated on the training data. That is, given data
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
,
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{X})
takes the matrix form

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{X})&\cdots&f_{J}(\mathbf{X})\\
|&&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times J}.

(25)

For library terms that are nonlinear in the first time derivative, we approximate
\dot{\sigma}_{\perp}
and
\dot{\sigma}_{z}
using 2nd-order centered finite differences. All other appearances of time derivatives are computed in weak form by placing the time derivative onto the test function via integrating by parts.

We enforce the weak form on a basis of test functions

\phi_{k}(t)=\varphi(t-t_{k};m_{t}\Delta t,\eta)

where
\varphi
is the
C^{\infty}_{c}
bump function

\varphi(t;a,\eta)=\exp\left(-\frac{\eta}{1-(t/a)^{2}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(-a,a)}(t)

and
\Delta t
is the sampling rate of the data, which is uniformly distributed in time. The parameters
\eta,m_{t}
are solved for in each
\sigma_{i}
equation using the method outlined in
[
34
, Appendix A]
, by finding a cornerpoint
k^{*}_{i}
in the cumulative sum of the power spectrum of
\sigma_{i}
, and assigning
k^{*}_{i}
to be two standard deviations into the tail of the power spectrum of
\varphi(t;a,\eta)
, the latter viewed as a probability distribution over frequency space. From
m_{t}
, the query points
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are chosen equally spaced such that
t_{k}-t_{k-1}=\lfloor m_{t}/4\rfloor
.

II.1
Rescaling

In dimensional variables with SI-units, fluctuations in the widths
(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{3}
are
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-5})
, while coefficients in the ansatz model can range from
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-10})
to
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{6})
, necessitating rescaling of the data, in the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}/\gamma_{\sigma},\quad\widetilde{t}=t/\gamma_{t},\quad\widetilde{m}=m/\gamma_{m},

(26)

for some characteristic scales
\gamma_{\sigma},\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma_{m}
in order to identify dominant terms using sparse regression. In the naïve library approach,
\gamma_{\sigma}
, the lengthscale, is chosen to enforce that the aggregate widths data
(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{3}
evaluated at the time grid
\mathbf{t}
has unit absolute mean. The timescale
\gamma_{t}
is chosen to be the period of the dominant slow mode of the dynamics, computed as the average difference between local maxima in the
\sigma_{z}
-coordinate using
findpeaks
in MATLAB.
The mass scale is simply the molecular mass
\gamma_{m}=m
.

For the ansatz verification (AV) and physics-informed (PI) library approaches, the length scale
\gamma_{\sigma}
is chosen such that the equipartitioned thermal energy matches the potential energy of the harmonic trap. Meanwhile,
\gamma_{t}
is chosen to enforce that the geometric mean of the natural trap oscillation frequencies is unity. Similarly,
\gamma_{m}=m
once more. Under this rescaling, terms significant to the dynamics yield coefficients that remain similar in magnitude, while superfluous terms have much smaller coefficients that are easily thresholded.

II.2
Sparse regression

To arrive at a sparse weight matrix
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}\approx\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, an approximate solution is sought for the
\ell_{0}
-regularized least-squares problem

\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}}\left\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\nu^{2}\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{0}

(27)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm and
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{0}
is the number of nonzero elements of
\mathbf{w}
, referred to as the
\ell_{0}
-pseudonorm. The WSINDy algorithm involves solving (
27
) using the Modified Sequential Thresholding Least-Squares algorithm (MSTLS)
[
34
]
, which employs alternating hard thresholding steps and least-squares solves, together with a grid search for the sparsity threshold
\nu
. In all cases we use the MSTLS algorithm with a grid of 300 equally log-spaced values
\boldsymbol{\nu}
for the sparsity threshold
\nu
, ranging from
10^{-8}
to
10^{-1}
.
