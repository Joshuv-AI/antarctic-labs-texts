CAT : Caution Aware Transfer in Reinforcement Learning via Distributional Risk
Mohamad Fares El Hajj Chehade, Amrit Singh Bedi, Amy Zhang, Hao Zhu

Abstract
Transfer learning in reinforcement learning (RL) has become a pivotal strategy for improving data efficiency in new, unseen tasks by utilizing knowledge from previously learned tasks. This approach is especially beneficial in real-world deployment scenarios where computational resources are constrained and agents must adapt rapidly to novel environments. However, current state-of-the-art methods often fall short in ensuring safety during the transfer process, particularly when unforeseen risks emerge in the deployment phase. In this work, we address these limitations by introducing a novel Caution-Aware Transfer Learning (CAT) framework. Unlike traditional approaches that limit risk considerations to mean-variance, we define “caution” as a more generalized and comprehensive notion of risk. Our core innovation lies in optimizing a weighted sum of reward return and caution—based on state-action occupancy measures—during the transfer process, allowing for a rich representation of diverse risk factors. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to explore the optimization of such a generalized risk notion within the context of transfer RL. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We propose a Caution-Aware Transfer (CAT) framework that evaluates source policies within the test environment and constructs a new policy that balances reward maximization and caution. (2) We derive theoretical sub-optimality bounds for our method, providing rigorous guarantees of its efficacy. (3) We empirically validate CAT, demonstrating that it consistently outperforms existing methods by delivering safer policies under varying risk conditions in the test tasks.



Introduction

The primary goal of reinforcement learning (RL) is to discover policies that maximize the expected return—the cumulative rewards an agent gathers throughout its trajectory
(
Bellemare, Dabney, and Munos 2017
)
. While RL is capable of developing highly optimal policies without explicit environment models, it demands extensive agent-environment interactions
(
Kamthe and Deisenroth 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, which can be impractical due to constraints on data acquisition, computational resources, or time. To mitigate these challenges, transfer RL has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance data efficiency by leveraging knowledge from prior tasks
(
Taylor and Stone 2009
)
.

Although transfer RL can help deal with the data efficiency issue, it often fails to eliminate risky policies during the transfer process. Data efficiency and safety are crucial in
deployment
tasks, where agents must operate safely and effectively with limited computational resources. Reusing optimized policies from previous training phases can be advantageous, particularly in robotics. For instance, a robot trained to navigate a dry corridor in a simulated environment may unexpectedly encounter rain during real-world deployment. Such scenarios highlight the importance of preparing for unforeseen risks that were not present during training.

To address the safety issues in traditional RL, several approaches have been proposed to account for risk
(
Jain et al. 2021a
;
Mannor and Tsitsiklis 2013a
;
Kwon et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. The majority, restricted in the classic
primal
domain, have focused on minimizing the variance of the return
(
Jain et al. 2021a
;
Mannor and Tsitsiklis 2013a
;
Kwon et al. 2022
)
. Meanwhile, the dual formulation of the RL problem in
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Sikchi et al. 2023
)
has facilitated the representation of more general forms of risk, such as occupying dangerous sets and deviating from expert behavior. However, most of the safety-focused research in RL is limited to traditional settings and has not been considered in the transfer RL setup, which is the focus of this work.

While some studies have developed approaches for risk-aware transfer, these typically focus on specific types of risk objectives, such as mean-variance
(
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
, and do not extend to broader risk concepts, which we refer to as
caution
in this work. Moreover, existing risk-aware transfer methods require source policies to be trained with awareness of potential risks. This presents a significant challenge in realistic settings where information about risks at test time may not be available during training. Even if some risk knowledge exists during source tasks, source policies must be adaptable to varied test conditions—some of which may present no risk, while others could introduce new and different types of risks. This variability necessitates that source policies maintain a risk-neutral stance, which is not necessarily a limitation. In fact, risk-aware training often leads to
conservative
policies, whereas risk-neutral training promotes the development of more
diverse
policies, better equipped to manage a wider array of test scenarios. To the best of our knowledge, this work pioneers the introduction of caution-aware transfer at test time, applying risk-neutral source policies across diverse reward functions. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

A Novel Caution-Aware Transfer Approach:
We introduce caution aware transfer, where the set of source policies are first evaluated in the test task. Then, a policy is designed for that task by maximizing a weighted sum of return and caution factor (which can be any risk function). We explicitly define the caution factor using state-action occupancy measures due to their ability to express different types of risk, called distributional risk.

2.

Theoretical Bounds on Sub-Optimality:
We derive theoretical bounds for the sub-optimality of our method, even after one iteration (due to test-time constraints). The loss of optimality depends on the difference in rewards between tasks and the caution weight constant.

3.

Empirical Validation of Caution-Aware Behavior:
We demonstrate, through experiments, caution aware behavior in a range of test tasks. We specifically show that our proposed solution works better than the existing state-of-the-art approaches.

(

;

;

Our problem is fundamentally different, as we do not assume any knowledge of risk at the training level; therefore, the original policies can be trained to be risk-agnostic. Moreover, we make the realistic assumption of limited resources at test-time, so no further optimization or iterative process can be performed on the policy obtained from the transfer. Preliminaries

Markov Decision Process Modeling

The interaction between the RL agent and the environment is assumed to follow a Markov decision process (MDP)
(
Puterman 1990
)
. An MDP is defined as the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,R,\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are finite sets of states and actions, respectively. For an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
that an agent takes in a given state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the distribution
p(\cdot|s,a)
models the transition to the next state.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the transfer problem. A set of risk-neutral optimal policies are first evaluated in a new task
i
. Then, the policy
\pi_{i}
for that task is designed on a state-by-state basis, by applying
f
to the resulting action values.

With the transition
s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}
,
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, the agent receives a scalar reward, represented by the random variable
R(s,a,s^{\prime})
. In the general RL setting, we are interested in the expected value of this reward variable, denoted by
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
. We represent the reward function in matrix form as
r\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
, where
r(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim p(\cdot|s,a)}[r(s,a,s^{\prime})]
. Finally, the discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
gives smaller weights for future rewards.
The action
a
taken by the agent given a state
s
is determined by policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
.
\pi(a|s)
gives the probability of taking the action
a
when in the state
s
. The performance of the policy at a given time
t
is evaluated through the return
G_{t}
, defined as the discounted sum of rewards received by the agent over its trajectory:
G_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}R_{t+i+1}
, where
R_{t}
is the reward received at time
t
. Most of the work in RL focuses on the expected value of this return,
Q^{\pi}(s,a)
, given by:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)\equiv\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[G_{t}\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}
is the expected value following
\pi
, and
S_{t}
and
A_{t}
are, respectively, the state and action in which the agent is at time
t
.
Q^{\pi}(s,a)
is
action-value function
of the policy
\pi
evaluated at
(s,a)
, and represents the expected return received by taking an action
a
at a certain state
s
, and following the policy
\pi
thereafter.
The action values can be written recursively in terms of the instantaneous reward
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
and the action value of the next state
Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
:

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s^{\prime}\sim p(\cdot|s,a),a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\left[r(s,a,s^{\prime})+\gamma\cdot Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right].

(2)

The
optimal
policy
\pi^{*}
results in the maximum possible expected return across all policies, i.e.
Q^{\pi^{*}}(s,a)=\max_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(s,a)\>\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\>\forall a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Problem Formulation: Risk-aware Transfer

The Transfer Learning Problem.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a set of MDPs sharing the same transition function
p
and having different (but bounded) reward functions
r
. As shown in Figure
1
, a set of sources, or
training
, tasks
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\{M_{1},M_{2},...,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{M}
, with optimal policies
\prod=\{\pi_{1}^{*},\pi_{2}^{*},...,\pi_{n}^{*}\}
, is instantiated. These could be tasks that the agent has been extensively trained on and an optimal policy exists for each of them.
We then consider a
test
task
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
. This could be a task that the agent faces during the
deployment
stage, and therefore, will not have the time to train an optimal policy
\pi_{i}^{*}
, but must use prior knowledge from
\prod
to find a good-enough policy
\pi_{i}
with limited time and computational resources.

Given a training task
M_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
,
Q^{\pi_{j}^{*}}_{i}(s,a)
is the action value function of the policy
\pi_{j}^{*}
for the state action tuple
(s,a)
in the test task
M_{i}
. We note that despite
\pi_{j}^{*}
being the optimal policy in the training task
M_{j}
, it is probably not optimal in the test task
M_{i}
. Therefore,
\pi_{i}
can be a composition of the different source policies, where the agent chooses the best policy on a state-by-state basis (cf. Figure
1
). Since we have defined the transfer learning problem, we next introduce the risk-aware transfer, which is the main focus of this work.

Remark

A lot of problems common in transfer reinforcement learning involve a stationary transition function. A famous example is goal-conditioned RL, where the position of the goal changes between tasks
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. The reward is often constructed to be a function of the distance to the goal and consequently changes across tasks. On the other hand, the transition dynamics remains the same
(
Barreto et al. 2017
;
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
.
Another famous example is the controlled decoding problem in large language models (LLMs)
(
Mudgal et al. 2023
)
, where information from one LLM (one task) is used in the construction of another LLM with a different reward function. In such settings, the transition function is deterministic and therefore is the same across different tasks
(
Chakraborty et al. 2024
)
.
We also kindly note that a stationary transition is a standard assumption in transfer reinforcement learning via successor features
(
Barreto et al. 2017
;
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)

The Risk Aware Transfer Learning Problem

The transfer is said to be risk-aware if the resultant policy from transfer has a risk-sensitive behavior in the test task. Mathematically, the function
f
in Figure
1
accounts for risk in the test task. For example, rather than maximizing the expected return only, i.e., where
f
is a
\max
operator,
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
balances the expectation with the variance of the return. However, crucial to the approach in
(
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
is that the source policies also have to be risk-aware, themselves being obtained through maximizing a mean-variance objective in their source tasks. This leads us to ask two very important questions related to risk-aware transfer.

(Q1) Does risk-aware transfer require training policies to be risk-aware, or can such a restriction negatively impact the transfer process?

(Q2) Can the mean-variance formulation, alone, mitigate any type of risk in the test task?

To answer the above-mentioned questions, let us consider a motivating example of navigation in a grid world environment as follows.

(a), (b) show the risk-aware optimal policies for each of the training tasks. (c) shows the results of risk-aware and risk-neutral transfer. Risk-aware transfer fails to find a path to the goal, whereas risk-neutral transfer finds a risky path, both showing significant failure rates in the new task, as indicated in (d).

A Motivating Example.
Let us consider the gridworld example in Figure
2
. The objective is to move the agent from the start cell (in yellow) to the goal cell (in green) while avoiding the danger block (in gray). The first two figures ((a) and (b)) show the source tasks, where the agent finds an optimal risk-aware policy (based on a mean-variance objective as in
(
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
) and reaches the goal while avoiding any contact with the block. Now, the transfer task is given in Figure
2
(c) where we note the presence of another gray area (danger block) in the middle top area. The goal here is to utilize the source policies in Figure
2
(a) and (b) and find a risk-aware policy for the test task of Figure
2
(c). Interestingly, applying the existing state-of-the-art risk-aware transfer to the risk-aware source policies, as in
(
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
, causes the agent to start in the opposite direction, failing in the task 80% of the time. On the other hand, risk-neutral transfer, i.e., without considering any risk factor in the test task
A
, finds a risky path to the goal since hugging the danger block will cause the agent to enter into it danger region due to environment stochasticity and consequently fails about 15% of the time.

Remark.
Therefore, we realize that having risk-aware source policies does not necessarily guarantee risk-aware behavior in the test task (answering Q1). This is prominent in tasks where the risk structure varies between the training and test tasks. In such situations, a mean-variance objective may not be the best way to express environmental risk (answering Q2). To further investigate, we did experiments in deterministic settings and derived similar conclusions (see Appendix).

Proposed Approach: CAT in RL

Risk-neutral training and Caution-aware transfer.
Motivated by the remarks in the previous section, we propose to keep the source policies to be trained in a risk-neutral manner and only apply the caution-aware transfer for the test tasks. We specifically use ‘caution’ instead of ‘risk’ to highlight that caution covers a general range of risk functions, while risk is usually referred to as mean-variance risk in the existing literature
(
Gimelfarb et al. 2021
)
. Training the source policies to be risk-neutral is necessary for multiple reasons. First, the same set of source policies may be used for test tasks with different types of risk, or even without risk, as shown in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
The set of source tasks are transferred to different test tasks, grouped by their objectives. The test tasks might wish to include a risk factor in their objective, which could be minimizing variance, aligning with expert behavior, or constraining the occupancy of states. Some tasks may not even include any type of risk, solely focusing on maximizing expected return.

Since the test policies are to be determined during test time, and the type of risk may not be known beforehand, we cannot determine the optimal risk-aware policies for the source tasks, given the limited resources during the deployment phase. Even with enough time, risk-aware source policies may lead to worse performance, as shown in Figure
2
. This may be because risk-aware training yields
conservative
policies, while risk-neutral training results in more
diverse
policies, allowing it to handle a wider spectrum of reward functions. Next, we mathematically characterize the proposed transfer process of this work.

Caution Aware Transfer

Given a test task
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
, and a set of source tasks
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\{M_{1},M_{2},...,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{M}

\prod=\{\pi_{1}^{*},\pi_{2}^{*},...,\pi_{n}^{*}\}

\{Q^{\pi_{1}^{*}}_{i},Q^{\pi_{2}^{*}}_{i},...,Q^{\pi_{n}^{*}}_{i}\}

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(a|s)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a=\underset{b}{\arg\max}\,\underset{j=1,2,\ldots,n}{\max}\ W_{i}^{j}(s,b)\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3)

where
W_{i}^{j}(s,b):=\left(Q^{\pi_{j}}_{i}(s,b)-c\cdot\rho_{i}(d^{\pi_{j}})\right)
.
In (

We emphasize that in (

Theoretical Results

In this section, we theoretically analyze the performance of the proposed approach in Algorithm
1
. We derive a suboptimality upper bound in Theorem
1
which compares the performance of proposed transfer policy with the best possible caution aware policy.

Theorem 1
.

Let
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
and let

be the action-value function of an optimal (caution-aware or caution-neutral) policy

\displaystyle\>\pi_{i}(a|s)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a=\underset{b}{\arg\max}\,\underset{j=1,2,\ldots,n}{\max}\,\tilde{Q}_{i}^{\pi_{j}^{*}}(s,a)\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\big|

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{i}^{\pi_{i}^{*}}(s,a)-\tilde{Q}_{i}^{\pi_{i}}(s,a)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\min_{j}\left(\frac{2}{1-\gamma}\|r_{i}-r_{j}\|_{\infty}+(4\cdot L+K)\cdot c\right).

In this section, we empirically analyze the performance of the proposed transfer process. Through our empirical evaluations, we aim to answer the following questions:

(Q1) Can CAT demonstrate risk-sensitive behavior in the test task?

(Q2) Can the dual formulation of risk in CAT outperform the primal formulation, i.e. variance of the return across trajectories?

For that purpose, and since we are the first to propose risk-aware transfer of risk-neutral source policies, we consider as baseline the same approach as in Algorithm

Experiment Setup.
We again consider the gridworld environment, with the start (yellow) and goal (green) cells, and the danger (gray) block. The per-step reward the agent receives depends on the state it enters, with the white cells having a reward of +0.3, the gray -0.8, and the goal +10. Three source tasks have been defined, with their optimal risk-neutral policies shown in Figure

4

, is used as the caution factor for CAT. For SFs, the feature vector

is a one-hot encoding over the states.

Discussions.
The performance of CAT and the baseline on 10 different test tasks is shown in Figure
5
. On one hand, CAT results in risk-aware behavior in almost all cases. On the other hand, in several tasks, the baseline fails in outputting risk-aware policies. This is probably because the primal form of variance has limited effect when the structure of risk changes, as in (a), (b), (c), (f), (g) and (i). Therefore, in such situations, the more powerful dual formulation of risk, such as the barrier risk, is needed.

(Q2) Can CAT be used in conjunction with function approximation?

MDP modeling
The experiment involves a 4-dimensional continuous state-space and 9 possible values for the action (corresponding to maximum, minimum and zero value of torque for each of the 3 dimensions). The reward is a function of the distance to the goal, and the dynamics are governed by the simulator.

Tasks Description
Four source tasks have been considered, and instead of training Deep Q-Networks (DQNs) to choose the optimal action, to allow for instantaneous evaluation in the test tasks, we train Successor Feature Deep Q-Networks (SFDQNs). Therefore, each of the 4 source tasks has an associated SFDQN that carries its optimal policy. The SFDQN returns the successor feature vector for a given state and action, and if we wish to evaluate this policy in a new task, we simply perform the dot product of this SF vector with the weight vector of that task.

Training and Testing details
Since the source policies are risk-neutral and have no knowledge of caution, we use the same parameters for training as in the original SF paper

The bar graph of Figure

(

Conclusions

In this work, we develop a method for risk-aware transfer (at test-time) of risk-agnostic optimal policies, where tasks differ in the reward function. The policy for that test task is designed on a state-by-state basis, by maximizing a weighted objective of expected return and risk. Risk is defined in terms of the state-action occupancy measure due to its ability to represent various types of risk. We show a theoretical bound on the optimality of our result. We demonstrate, by experiments, the effectiveness of our method on discrete and continuous tasks. Future work includes extending this method to accommodate transfer between tasks that vary as well in the transition function.

\displaystyle U_{\beta}\left[G_{t}\right]
