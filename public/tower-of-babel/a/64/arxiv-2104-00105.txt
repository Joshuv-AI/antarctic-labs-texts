Hilbert transforms and the equidistribution of zeros of polynomials
Emanuel Carneiro, Mithun Kumar Das, Alexandra Florea, Angel V. Kumchev, Amita Malik, Micah B. Milinovich, Caroline Turnage-Butterbaugh, and Jiuya Wang

Abstract
. We improve the current bounds for an inequality of Erdős and Turán from 1950 related to the discrepancy of angular equidistribution of the zeros of a given polynomial. Building upon a recent work of Soundararajan, we establish a novel connection between this inequality and an extremal problem in Fourier analysis involving the maxima of Hilbert transforms, for which we provide a complete solution. Prior to Soundararajan (2019), refinements of the discrepancy inequality of Erdős and Turán had been obtained by Ganelius (1954) and Mignotte (1992).



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
42A05, 42A50

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Following the elegant treatment of Soundararajan
[
18
]
, we revisit the classical work of Erdős and Turán
[
9
]
on the distribution of zeros of polynomials in the complex plane. In particular, we establish a connection between the upper bound for the discrepancy of the angles of the zeros of a given polynomial and an extremal problem in Fourier analysis involving the maxima of Hilbert transforms. Before describing this extremal problem, which is solved completely in this paper, we first describe our application in number theory.

Let

P(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(z-\alpha_{j}\big)=z^{N}+a_{N-1}z^{N-1}+\cdots+a_{0}

be a monic polynomial of degree
N
, with
a_{0}\neq 0
and roots
\alpha_{j}=\rho_{j}\,e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}
. Roughly speaking,
Erdős and Turán proved that if the
size
of
P(z)
on the unit circle is small,
and
a_{0}
is not too small, then its roots cluster around the unit circle and
the angles
2\pi\theta_{j}
become equidistributed as
N\to\infty
. Two notions of size, or
height
, of a polynomial that have been considered in this problem are

H(P)=\max_{|z|=1}\,\frac{|P(z)|}{\sqrt{|a_{0}|}}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ h(P)=\int_{0}^{1}\log^{+}\!\left(\frac{\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|}{\sqrt{|a_{0}|}}\right)\,\text{\rm d}\theta,

where
\log^{+}\!x=\max\{\log x,0\}
. By Parseval’s identity, we have

\int_{0}^{1}|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\theta=1+|a_{N-1}|^{2}+\ldots+|a_{0}|^{2},

from which it follows easily that
H(P)\geq 1
and therefore
h(P)\leq\log H(P)
. Hence, the assumption that
h(P)
is small is weaker than the assumption that
H(P)
is small. Let us also define the quantity

\mathcal{M}(P)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\max\left\{\rho_{j},\frac{1}{\rho_{j}}\right\}.

The observation that the zeros cluster around the unit circle is given by the inequality
[
18
, Theorem 1]

\log\mathcal{M}(P)\leq 2\,h(P),

that follows by an interesting application of Jensen’s formula in complex analysis.

We focus on the study of the equidistribution of the angles
2\pi\theta_{j}
. Given an interval
I
on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, we let
N(I;P)
denote the number of zeros
\alpha_{j}=\rho_{j}\,e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}
for which
\theta_{j}\in I
. A convenient way to measure the distribution of the sequence
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
is by means of its
discrepancy
, defined by

\mathcal{D}(P):=\sup_{I}\Big|N(I;P)-|I|N\Big|,

where
|I|
denotes the length of the interval
I
. We list a few notable results in estimating the discrepancy
\mathcal{D}(P)
. Erdős and Turán, in their original paper
[
9
]
of 1950, proved that

(1.1)

\mathcal{D}(P)\leq C\sqrt{N\log H(P)},

with
C=16
. In 1954, Ganelius
[
10
]
established (
1.1
) with the constant
C=\sqrt{2\pi/k}=2.5619\ldots
, where
k=1/1^{2}-1/3^{2}+1/5^{2}-\ldots=0.9159\ldots
denotes Catalan’s constant. Amoroso and Mignotte
[
2
]
have produced examples that show that the constant
C
in (
1.1
) must be at least
\sqrt{2}
. In 1992, Mignotte
[
14
]
refined Ganelius’s result by establishing the stronger inequality

(1.2)

\mathcal{D}(P)\leq C\sqrt{N\,h(P)},

with the same constant
C=\sqrt{2\pi/k}=2.5619\ldots
. Only recently, in 2019, Soundararajan
[
18
]
improved this result by establishing (
1.2
) with the constant

C=\frac{8}{\pi}=2.5464\ldots.

Our goal is to provide an improvement of the admissible value of
C
in (
1.2
). We follow the general outline of proof of Soundararajan in
[
18
]
up to a certain point, then we diverge and introduce a novel ingredient: the connection to a certain extremal problem in Fourier analysis involving the maxima of Hilbert transforms. As a direct consequence of Theorems
2
and
3
below, we prove that the constant

C=\frac{\ \ 4}{\sqrt{\pi}}=2.2567\ldots

is admissible in (
1.2
) and show that this constant is the best possible with our particular strategy.

Theorem 1
.

If
P
is a monic polynomial of degree
N
with
P(0)\neq 0
, then

\mathcal{D}(P)\leq\frac{\ \ 4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\sqrt{N\,h(P)}.

Remark
. In their original paper
[
9
]
, Erdős and Turán were also interested in estimating the number
\mathcal{R}(P)
of real roots of a polynomial
P
. In particular, the notion of discrepancy can be used towards this goal. From the definition, letting
I
denote either the point
0
or
\frac{1}{2}
, it plainly follows that
\mathcal{R}(P)\leq 2\,\mathcal{D}(P)
.

We also note that, for the example
P(z)=(z-1)^{N}
, one has
\mathcal{D}(P)=N
and

(1.3)

h(P)=N\int_{0}^{1}\log^{+}\!\big|e^{2\pi i\theta}-1\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta=N\,\frac{3\sqrt{3}\,L(2,\chi_{3})}{4\pi},

where
\chi_{3}
denotes the quadratic character modulo
3
. This last identity was observed by C. J. Smyth in a slightly different context, see
[
5
, Appendix 1]
. Hence, the constant
C
in (
1.2
) cannot be smaller than

(1.4)

\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}\,L(2,\chi_{3})}}=1.75936\ldots.

1.2.
Fourier optimization

Throughout this paper we consider functions in two different environments: the ones defined on
\mathbb{R}
(usually denoted here with capital letters) and the ones defined on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
(usually denoted here with lower case letters).

For
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we define its Fourier transform
\widehat{F}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\widehat{F}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi itx}\,F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

By Plancherel’s theorem one can extend the Fourier transform to an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
is another classical operator in harmonic analysis that has a few (equivalent) interpretations. As a singular integral it is defined by

(1.5)

\mathcal{H}(F)(x)={\rm p.v.}\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x-t)\,\frac{1}{t}\,\text{\rm d}t\,,

where the notation p.v. here means that such integral should be understood as a Cauchy principal value. The classical theory of singular integrals guarantees that the Hilbert transform is a well-defined operator on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for
1\leq p<\infty
, being a bounded operator if
1<p<\infty
, and satisfying a weak-type-
(1,1)
estimate when
p=1
. See, for instance
[
19
, Chapters V and VI]
or
[
13
, Chapter 4]
for proofs of these facts and the connections with the theory of conjugate harmonic functions. In particular, the appropriate limiting process in (
1.5
) converges a.e. for
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
1\leq p<\infty
. The operator
\mathcal{H}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is an isometry that can be alternatively defined on the Fourier space by the relation
1
1

1

Recall that
{\rm sgn}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
{\rm sgn}(t)=1
, if
t>0
;
{\rm sgn}(0)=0
; and
{\rm sgn}(t)=-1
, if
t<0
.

(1.6)

\widehat{\mathcal{H}(F)}(t)=-i\,{\rm sgn}(t)\,\widehat{F}(t).

Similarly, in the periodic setting, if
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
we define its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\widehat{f}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi ik\theta}\,f(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta.

The periodic Hilbert transform is the singular integral operator defined by

(1.7)

\mathcal{H}(f)(\theta)={\rm p.v.}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(\theta-\alpha)\,\cot(\pi\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Again, the appropriate limiting process in (
1.7
) converges a.e. if
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
for
1\leq p<\infty
, defining a bounded operator on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
if
1<p<\infty
, and verifying a weak-type-
(1,1)
estimate when
p=1
. In particular,
\mathcal{H}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
can be alternatively defined via the Fourier coefficients

(1.8)

\widehat{\mathcal{H}(f)}(k)=-i\,{\rm sgn}(k)\,\widehat{f}(k).

Although we use the same notation for the Fourier transforms and Hilbert transforms on
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, it will be clear from the context which one we are referring to. We consider below some sharp inequalities for the Hilbert transform. Classical works in this theme include the ones of Pichorides
[
15
]
, in which he finds the operator norm
\|\mathcal{H}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}
for
1<p<\infty
(see also
[
12
]
for a simplified proof), and of Davis
[
8
]
, in which he finds the weak-type-
(1,1)
operator norm (such works consider both the situation in the real line and in the periodic setting).

Throughout the paper we let
\mathcal{A}
be the following class of real-valued functions:

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\ \ {\rm even,continuous\ and\ non\!-\!negative};\\
{\rm supp}(F)\subseteq[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}];\\
\widehat{F}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).\end{array}\right.

For each
F\in\mathcal{A}
we define its periodization
f_{F}:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f_{F}(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}F(\theta+k).

One can verify that
f_{F}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
and that
\widehat{f_{F}}(k)=\widehat{F}(k)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, in this situation, by a classical result of Plancherel and Pólya
(see
[
16
]
or
[
21
, eq. (3.1)]
), for any
\delta>0
we have

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\big|\widehat{F}(\delta k)\big|\ll_{\delta}\big\|\widehat{F}\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

In particular, for
F\in\mathcal{A}
, both
\mathcal{H}(F)
defined by (
1.6
) and
\mathcal{H}(f_{F})
defined by (
1.8
) via Fourier inversion are bounded and continuous functions. We consider the following optimization problem involving the
L^{\infty}
-norms of these Hilbert transforms.

Extremal Problem 1
(EP1)
.

With notations as above, find the infimum:

(1.9)

\displaystyle{\bf C}:=\inf_{0\neq F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\max\big\{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,,\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\big\}}{\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}}.

This problem is the main theme of study in this paper. Without necessarily knowing the precise value of the constant
{\bf C}
, our first main result gives a non-obvious theoretical connection between this optimization problem, purely in analysis, and the angular discrepancy
\mathcal{D}(P)
of a polynomial
P
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\bf C}
be given by (
1.9
). If
P
is a monic polynomial of degree
N
with
P(0)\neq 0
, then

\mathcal{D}(P)\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\bf C}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\sqrt{N\,h(P)}.

We prove this result in Sections
2
and
3
. From the observations leading to (
1.4
) and Theorem
2
we automatically have a lower bound coming from the number theory side:

{\bf C}\geq\frac{\pi}{16}\left(\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}\,L(2,\chi_{3})}\right)=0.6077\ldots.

In Theorem 2, we go much further in our understanding of this problem. Before stating this result, we set up a second optimization problem, somewhat related to the first one. Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}
be the following class of real-valued functions (slightly larger than
\mathcal{A}
):

\mathcal{A^{*}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\ \ F\geq 0;\\
{\rm supp}(F)\subseteq[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}].\end{array}\right.

Consider the following problem:

Extremal Problem 2
(EP2)
.

With notations as above, find the infimum:

(1.10)

\displaystyle{\bf C^{*}}:=\inf_{0\neq F\in\mathcal{A^{*}}}\frac{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}}.

Since
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A^{*}}
and
\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\max\big\{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,,\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\big\}
, it is clear from the definitions of (EP1) and (EP2) that

{\bf C^{*}}\leq{\bf C}.

Our second main result establishes a complete solution for both of these extremal problems at once.

Theorem 3
.

For
{\bf C}
given by (
1.9
) and
{\bf C^{*}}
given by (
1.10
), we have

{\bf C^{*}}={\bf C}=1.

Moreover, there are no extremal functions
F\in\mathcal{A}
for the problem
{\rm(EP1)}
, and the unique
(
modulo multiplication by a positive constant
)
extremal function for the problem
{\rm(EP2)}
is

(1.11)

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \qquad\qquad\mathfrak{F}(x):=\frac{2}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4x^{2}}}{2|x|}\right)\ \ \ \ \ \big({\rm for}\ -\tfrac{1}{2}\leq x\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\big).

The proof of this theorem is given in Section
5
. The proof relies on the serendipitous existence of two
magic functions
: the even function given in (
1.11
) and an odd function given in (
5.2
). For a different perspective on lower bounds for the Hilbert transform over intervals (mostly in
L^{2}
), see
[
1
]
.

Theorem
1
now follows directly from Theorems
2
and
3
.

2.
Soundararajan’s proof revisited

We now prepare for the proof of Theorem
2
. At first, we closely follow Soundararajan’s strategy of proof for the inequality (
1.2
) in
[
18
]
, which we briefly review for the convenience of the reader. At a certain stage of the argument (discussed in §
2.4
below), we make a crucial change of direction that leads to our optimization problem in analysis. This is discussed in full detail in the next section, where we complete the proof of Theorem
2
.

2.1.
Schur’s observation

First note that we can assume without loss of generality that the zeros of the polynomial are all in the unit circle, an observation due to Schur
[
17
]
. In fact, letting
P(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(z-\rho_{j}\,e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\big)
as above, we may define
Q(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(z-e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\big)
and observe, for
|z|=1
, that

\left|\frac{z}{\sqrt{\rho_{j}}}-\sqrt{\rho_{j}}\,e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\right|^{2}\geq\big|z-e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\big|^{2}.

By multiplying over
j
, we find that
|P(z)|/\sqrt{|a_{0}|}\geq|Q(z)|
for
|z|=1
, and therefore
h(P)\geq h(Q)
. Hence, from now on we assume that
\rho_{j}=1
for
j=1,2,\ldots,N
.

2.2.
Smoothed sums and
h(P)

If we define
\psi(\theta)=\log|2\sin(\pi\theta)|
, then its Fourier coefficients are given by
\widehat{\psi}(0)=0
and
\widehat{\psi}(k)=-\tfrac{1}{2|k|}
for
k\neq 0
(e.g.
[
11
, §1.441, eq. 2]
). Hence, for
P(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(z-e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\big)
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi ik\theta}&\log\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi ik\theta}\log\big|e^{2\pi i\theta}-e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}}\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ik\theta_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi ik\alpha}\log\big|e^{2\pi i\alpha}-1\big|\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ik\theta_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\psi(\alpha)\,e^{2\pi ik\alpha}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha=-\frac{1}{2|k|}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ik\theta_{j}}.\end{split}

Identity (
2.1
) is essentially contained in
[
18
, Lemma 2]
. Let
g:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous and integrable function such that
\{k\,\widehat{g}(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is absolutely summable, and set

\mathcal{G}:=\max_{\theta}\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}2|k|\,\widehat{g}(k)\,e^{2\pi ik\theta}\right|.

By expanding
g
into its Fourier series, and using (
2.1
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}g(\theta_{j})-N\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}g(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right|=\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}\widehat{g}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ik\theta_{j}}\right|=\left|-\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\log\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\left(\sum_{k\neq 0}2|k|\widehat{g}(k)e^{2\pi ik\theta}\right)\text{\rm d}\theta\right|

(2.2)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \leq\mathcal{G}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\log\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\Big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta=\mathcal{G}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}2\log^{+}\!\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta-\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\log\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ =2\,\mathcal{G}\,h(P).

In the last passage above, note the use of Jensen’s formula in the identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\log\big|P\big(e^{2\pi i\theta}\big)\big|\,\text{\rm d}\theta=0.

Inequality (
2.2
) is the content of
[
18
, Proposition 1]
.

2.3.
Majorizing the characteristic function of an interval

Having established the preliminaries in §
2.1
and §
2.2
above, we now move on to the proof itself. First observe that if we can prove the upper bound

(2.3)

\displaystyle N(I;P)-|I|\,N\leq C\sqrt{N\,h(P)}\,,

for a certain universal constant
C
and all intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, we may use the identity

N(I;P)-|I|\,N=|I^{c}|\,N-N(I^{c};P),

where
I^{c}
denotes the complementary interval to
I
, to obtain the corresponding lower bound. Therefore, it suffices to obtain the upper bound (
2.3
).

Let
0\neq F\in\mathcal{A}
, normalized so that
\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
. For each
0<\delta\leq 1
, let

F_{\delta}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\delta}F\big(\tfrac{x}{\delta}\big)

so that
{\rm supp}(F_{\delta})\subset\big[-\tfrac{\delta}{2},\tfrac{\delta}{2}\big]
. We let

f_{\delta}(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{\delta}(\theta+k)

be the periodization of
F_{\delta}
. Note that
\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f_{\delta}(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta=1
and, more generally, that

\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)=\widehat{F_{\delta}}(k)=\widehat{F}(\delta k)

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, let
I_{\delta}
be the interval obtained by widening
I
on either side by
\delta/2
; if
|I|+\delta\geq 1
, then we just consider
I_{\delta}
to be all of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\chi_{I_{\delta}}
be the characteristic function of the interval
I_{\delta}
and let
g_{\delta}
be the convolution of
\chi_{I_{\delta}}
and
f_{\delta}
, that is

(2.4)

\displaystyle g_{\delta}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{I_{\delta}}(\alpha)\,f_{\delta}(\theta-\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Note that
g_{\delta}
is a continuous and non-negative function that majorizes the characteristic function of the original interval
I
. We then write

(2.5)

\displaystyle N(I;P)-|I|\,N\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{\delta}(\theta_{j})-|I|\,N=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{\delta}(\theta_{j})-N\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}g_{\delta}(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\!\right)\!+N\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}g_{\delta}(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta-|I|\!\right).

Our goal now is to bound the two terms appearing on the right-hand side of (
2.5
). For the second term, we use the definition (
2.4
) and Fubini’s theorem to get

(2.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq N\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}g_{\delta}(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta-|I|\right)=N\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{I_{\delta}}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha-|I|\right)\leq N\Big((|I|+\delta)-|I|\Big)=N\delta.

Now, if
I_{\delta}=[\alpha,\beta]
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
we have

(2.7)

\widehat{\chi_{I_{\delta}}}(k)=\frac{e^{-2\pi ik\alpha}-e^{-2\pi ik\beta}}{2\pi ik}.

Recall that
\widehat{g_{\delta}}(k)=\widehat{\chi_{I_{\delta}}}(k)\,\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, hence the sequence
\{k\widehat{g_{\delta}}(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is absolutely summable. Letting

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}:=\max_{\theta}\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}2|k|\widehat{g_{\delta}}(k)e^{2\pi ik\theta}\right|\,,

we have seen in (
2.2
) that the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.5
) satisfies

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{\delta}(\theta_{j})-N\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}g_{\delta}(\theta)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right|\leq 2\,\mathcal{G}_{\delta}\,h(P).

2.4.
Understanding the cancellation

We now need to bound the quantity
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
and this is where we diverge from Soundararajan’s original proof
[
18
]
. From (
2.7
) we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq 0}2|k|\widehat{g_{\delta}}(k)\,e^{2\pi ik\theta}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\neq 0}-i\,{\rm sgn}(k)\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)\left(e^{2\pi ik(\theta-\alpha)}-e^{2\pi ik(\theta-\beta)}\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}2|k|\widehat{g_{\delta}}(k)\,e^{2\pi ik\theta}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}-i\,{\rm sgn}(k)\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)\,e^{2\pi ik(\theta-\alpha)}\right|+\frac{1}{\pi}\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}-i\,{\rm sgn}(k)\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)\,e^{2\pi ik(\theta-\beta)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\big|\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta-\alpha)\big|+\frac{1}{\pi}\big|\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta-\beta)\big|.

This plainly yields

(2.10)

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\|\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}.

Equality is actually attained if one considers the maximum over all intervals
[\alpha,\beta]
, so there is no loss in this use of the triangle inequality.

Remark.
In the corresponding step in
[
18
]
, Soundararajan is working in the restricted subclass of
\mathcal{A}
for which
\widehat{F}\geq 0
, and at the end he chooses
F
to be a triangular graph. He couples the terms
k
and
-k
in (
2.9
) and uses the triangle inequality, further moving the absolute values inside the sum, to get

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\max_{\theta}\sum_{k\geq 1}\widehat{f_{\delta}}(k)\,|\sin(2\pi k\theta)|.

This particular extra step of moving the absolute values inside disregards some cancellation in the sum. This is precisely the point where our analysis diverges from
[
18
]
.

We now state a relation that is fundamental for our purposes, which essentially says that the supremum over this one-parameter family (for
0<\delta\leq 1
) of
L^{\infty}
-norms of Hilbert transforms in (
2.10
), when properly normalized, occurs at one of the endpoints
\delta=0^{+}
or
\delta=1
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{A}
and
0<\delta\leq 1
. With notations as above, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\delta\leq 1}\delta\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}=\max\!\left\{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,,\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\right\}.

We postpone the proof of this result until the next section.

2.5.
Conclusion

Assume for a moment that we have established Proposition
4
. Let us simplify the notation by writing

\mathcal{C}(F):=\max\!\left\{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,,\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\right\}.

It then follows from (
2.10
) and Proposition
4
that

(2.11)

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}\leq\frac{2}{\pi\delta}\,\mathcal{C}(F),

and from (
2.5
), (
2.6
), (
2.8
), and (
2.11
) we get

N(I;P)-|I|\,N\leq N\delta+\frac{4}{\pi\delta}\,\mathcal{C}(F)\,h(P).

The choice of

(2.12)

\delta=\sqrt{\frac{4\,\mathcal{C}(F)\,h(P)}{\pi N}}

minimizes the right-hand side of the expression above and leads to the bound

N(I;P)-|I|\,N\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\mathcal{C}(F)}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\sqrt{N\,h(P)}.

Note that this is independent of the interval
I
. Minimizing over
F\in\mathcal{A}
we arrive at the desired conclusion

\mathcal{D}(P)\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\bf C}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{N\,h(P)}.

Therefore, Theorem
2
follows from Proposition
4
.

Remark.

\log^{+}\!xy\leq\log^{+}\!x\,+\,\log^{+}\!y

in (

) indeed falls in the interval

if

\mathcal{C}(F)\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\sqrt{3}\,L(2,\chi_{3})}=2.43107\ldots.

In Section

3.
Maxima of Hilbert transforms

The purpose of this section is to prove the key Proposition
4
, hence concluding the proof of Theorem
2
. Recall that we have been using the definition of the Hilbert transforms via the multipliers (
1.6
) and (
1.8
) and Fourier inversion (hence all Hilbert transforms here are bounded and continuous functions). In this section, the alternative representations of the Hilbert transforms as singular integrals will be particularly useful. Throughout this section we continue to assume that
0\neq F\in\mathcal{A}
is normalized so that
\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
, and for each
0<\delta\leq 1
we let
F_{\delta}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\delta}F\big(\tfrac{x}{\delta}\big)
and
f_{\delta}(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{\delta}(\theta+k)

3.1.
Hilbert transforms as singular integrals

For each
0<\delta\leq 1
, since
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})
is an odd and continuous function in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(0)=\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\pm\tfrac{1}{2})=0
. We start by establishing the following useful relation between the periodic Hilbert transforms
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})
and the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}(F)
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
0<\delta\leq 1
and
-\frac{1}{2}<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
. Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)=\mathcal{H}(F)\left(\tfrac{\theta}{\delta}\right)+\frac{\delta}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\frac{4\theta(\theta^{2}-\alpha^{2}-k^{2})}{\big((\theta-\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}\big)\big((\theta+\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}\big)}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}
be the set of full measure (i.e.
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Gamma
has measure zero) such that for every
x\in\Gamma
the limit

(3.2)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\varepsilon\leq|t|}F(x-t)\,\frac{1}{t}\,\text{\rm d}t

exists and is equal to
\mathcal{H}(F)(x)
. Similarly, for a fixed
0<\delta\leq 1
, let
\Gamma_{\delta}\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be the set of full measure such that for every
\theta\in\Gamma_{\delta}
the limit

(3.3)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\varepsilon\leq|\alpha|\leq\frac{1}{2}}f_{\delta}(\theta-\alpha)\,\cot(\pi\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

exists and is equal to
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)
.

Recall that, for
\|\alpha\|\geq\varepsilon>0
, we have the absolutely convergent expansion (e.g.
[
11
, §1.421 eq. 3]
)

(3.4)

\cot(\pi\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha^{2}-k^{2}}\right).

Assume that
\theta\in\Gamma_{\delta}
and
\frac{\theta}{\delta}\in\Gamma
. Let
\varepsilon
be small and write
X_{\varepsilon}=\big\{\alpha\in\big[-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2}]\,:\,\|\theta-\alpha\|\geq\varepsilon\big\}
and
Y_{\varepsilon}=\big\{\beta\in\big[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\,:\,\|\theta-\delta\beta\|\geq\varepsilon\big\}
. Using (
3.4
), and with a change of variables
\alpha=\delta\beta
, we note that

\displaystyle\delta\int_{\varepsilon\leq|\alpha|\leq\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle f_{\delta}(\theta-\alpha)\,\cot(\pi\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha=\delta\int_{X_{\varepsilon}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\,\cot(\pi(\theta-\alpha))\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\pi}\int_{X_{\varepsilon}}\frac{f_{\delta}(\alpha)}{(\theta-\alpha)}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\ +\frac{\delta}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{X_{\varepsilon}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\frac{2(\theta-\alpha)}{(\theta-\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{Y_{\varepsilon}}\frac{F(\beta)}{\big(\frac{\theta}{\delta}-\beta\big)}\,\text{\rm d}\beta+\frac{\delta}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{X_{\varepsilon}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\frac{2(\theta-\alpha)}{(\theta-\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and using the fact that
f_{\delta}
is even (to combine
\alpha
and
-\alpha
in the integral below), we get

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)&=\mathcal{H}(F)\left(\tfrac{\theta}{\delta}\right)+\frac{\delta}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{-\frac{\delta}{2}}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\frac{2(\theta-\alpha)}{(\theta-\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\\
&=\mathcal{H}(F)\left(\tfrac{\theta}{\delta}\right)+\frac{\delta}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\frac{4\theta(\theta^{2}-\alpha^{2}-k^{2})}{\big((\theta-\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}\big)\big((\theta+\alpha)^{2}-k^{2}\big)}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.\end{split}

In principle, (
3.5
) holds for
\theta
in the set of full measure
(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\cap\Gamma_{\delta}\cap\delta\Gamma
. Since the functions in (
3.5
) are continuous functions of
\theta\in(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
we conclude that the identity is valid for all
\theta
in this range.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
4

We start by observing that, for
0<\delta\leq 1
and
-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\theta\leq-\tfrac{\delta}{2}
, we have

(3.6)

\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)\leq 0.

In fact, if
\delta<1
and
\theta\in(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{\delta}{2})\cap\Gamma_{\delta}
, using that
f_{\delta}
is even, non-negative and supported in
[-\tfrac{\delta}{2},\tfrac{\delta}{2}]
along with the singular integral representation (
3.3
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{X_{\varepsilon}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\,\cot(\pi(\theta-\alpha))\,\text{\rm d}\alpha=\int_{-\frac{\delta}{2}}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\,\cot(\pi(\theta-\alpha))\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}f_{\delta}(\alpha)\,\big(\cot(\pi(\theta-\alpha))+\cot(\pi(\theta+\alpha))\big)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\leq 0.

A similar argument shows that if
x\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}
then
\mathcal{H}(F)(x)\leq 0.

Since
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})
is an odd and continuous function in
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
(not identically zero), its maximum in absolute value coincides with the positive maximum, and we investigate the latter. Recall that
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(0)=\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\pm\tfrac{1}{2})=0
. We split our analysis into two cases.

3.2.1.
Case 1

Assume that
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
is such that
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)>0
. In this case, the sum on the right-side of (
3.1
) is clearly non-positive and it plainly follows by Lemma
5
that

(3.7)

\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)\leq\mathcal{H}(F)\left(\tfrac{\theta}{\delta}\right).

3.2.2.
Case 2

Assume that
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\theta<0
is such that
\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)>0
. As observed in (
3.6
), we must have
-\frac{\delta}{2}<\theta<0
in this situation. Using Lemma
5
, letting
\theta^{\prime}=\frac{\theta}{\delta}
(hence
-\frac{1}{2}<\theta^{\prime}<0
) and changing variables
\alpha=\delta\beta
in the integral, we rewrite (
3.1
) as

(3.8)

\displaystyle\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}(F)(\theta^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}F(\beta)\frac{4\theta^{\prime}\left(\theta^{\prime 2}-\beta^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)}{\left((\theta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)\left((\theta^{\prime}+\beta)^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)}\,\text{\rm d}\beta.

The important observation now is that, for each
k\geq 1
, the term

\frac{4\theta^{\prime}\left(\theta^{\prime 2}-\beta^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)}{\left((\theta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)\left((\theta^{\prime}+\beta)^{2}-\left(\tfrac{k}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)}

is positive and, for fixed
-\frac{1}{2}<\theta^{\prime}<0
and
0\leq\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the function

h(x):=\frac{4\theta^{\prime}\big(\theta^{\prime 2}-\beta^{2}-x^{2}\big)}{\big((\theta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}-x^{2}\big)\big((\theta^{\prime}+\beta)^{2}-x^{2}\big)}

verifies
h^{\prime}(x)<0
for
x\geq 1
. This is a routine calculation.
The conclusion is that we could replace
\delta
on each summand on the right-hand side of (
3.8
) by its maximum value
\delta=1
and do better, i.e.

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\theta)&\leq\mathcal{H}(F)(\theta^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}F(\beta)\frac{4\theta^{\prime}\left(\theta^{\prime 2}-\beta^{2}-k^{2}\right)}{\big((\theta^{\prime}-\beta)^{2}-k^{2}\big)\big((\theta^{\prime}+\beta)^{2}-k^{2}\big)}\,\text{\rm d}\beta\\
&=\mathcal{H}(f_{1})(\theta^{\prime})\,,\end{split}

where the last identity follows from another application of Lemma
5
.

3.2.3.
Conclusion

From (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), we plainly arrive at the conclusion that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\delta\leq 1}\delta\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\leq\max\big\{\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,,\,\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}\big\}=:\mathcal{C}(F).

If
\mathcal{C}(F)=\|\mathcal{H}(f_{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}
, then (
3.10
) is obviously an equality (recall that
f_{F}=f_{1}
in this notation). On the other hand, if
\mathcal{C}(F)=\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that

\mathcal{C}(F)=\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\mathcal{H}(F)(x_{0})

(note that
\mathcal{H}(F)
goes to zero at infinity, hence such
x_{0}
indeed exists). Let
\theta(\delta)=\delta x_{0}
, for
\delta
sufficiently small so that
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\theta(\delta)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. We apply Lemma
5
once more, by changing variables
\alpha=\delta\beta
in the integral and rewriting (
3.1
) in the form

(3.11)

\displaystyle\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\delta x_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}(F)(x_{0})+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}F(\beta)\frac{4\delta^{2}x_{0}\big(\delta^{2}x_{0}^{2}-\delta^{2}\beta^{2}-k^{2}\big)}{\big(\delta^{2}(x_{0}-\beta)^{2}-k^{2}\big)\big(\delta^{2}(x_{0}+\beta)^{2}-k^{2}\big)}\,\text{\rm d}\beta.

An application of the dominated convergence theorem on the right-hand side of (
3.11
) guarantees that

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\delta\,\mathcal{H}(f_{\delta})(\delta x_{0})=\mathcal{H}(F)(x_{0})\,,

and we have equality in (
3.10
) as desired. This concludes the proof of Proposition
4
.

4.1.
Dichotomy

In the definition of

-norms. One may wonder if one of these is always dominated by the other. Our first observation is that this is not always the case. In principle, there are examples of functions for which either

-norm can be maximal.

If the maximum value of

occurs at a certain

\mathcal{C}(F)=\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}.

This follows directly from (

is

Since
\mathcal{H}(f)
is continuous, this inequality is valid for all
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\theta<0
. Note that above we used the fact that
\|\theta+\alpha\|\leq\|\theta-\alpha\|
in our range to argue that

.

On the other hand, if the maximum value of
\mathcal{H}(F)(x)
occurs at a certain

(recall that we have seen that

\mathcal{C}(F)=\|\mathcal{H}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}.

This follows from (
3.1
) with

. There are indeed functions
F
with such behaviour, for instance the piecewise linear function, normalized so that

(4.3)

\displaystyle F(x)=\begin{cases}0,\ {\rm if}\ 0\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{4};\\

\frac{1}{3}(32-64|x|),\ {\rm if}\ \frac{5}{16}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

See Figure

-norms. We prove in the next section that functions

Consider the triangle function

and that its Hilbert transform is constant (equal to
-1
) in the interval
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
is precisely what makes it magical. The graphs of
\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{G})
are plotted in Figure
2
.

Take any
0\neq F\in\mathcal{A}
, normalized so that
\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
. Note that
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Using (
5.3
) with
F_{1}=F
and
F_{2}=\mathfrak{G}
, together with (
5.4
), (
5.5
), and the fact that
{\rm supp}(F)\subset[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
, we get the following relation

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}&=\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathfrak{G}(x)|\,\text{\rm d}x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}(F)(x)\,\overline{\mathfrak{G}(x)}\,\text{\rm d}x\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\overline{\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{G})(x)}\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1.\end{split}

Since (
5.6
) holds for any such normalized
F\in\mathcal{A}
, we plainly get the lower bound

{\bf C}\geq 1.

In addition, once we establish in the next subsection that
{\bf C}
is actually equal to
1
, relation (
5.6
) also tells us that there are no extremizers for the problem (EP1) in the class
\mathcal{A}
. In fact, equality in (
5.6
) could only be attained if

\mathcal{H}(F)(x)={\rm sgn}(x)\,\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}={\rm sgn}(x)

for a.e.
-\tfrac{1}{2}<x<\tfrac{1}{2}
, which cannot occur since
\mathcal{H}(F)
is odd and continuous when
F\in\mathcal{A}
.

5.2.
A rogue extremal function

We now turn our attention to the function
\mathfrak{F}
defined in (
5.1
). Observe first that
\mathfrak{F}\geq 0
in
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
,
\mathfrak{F}
is continuous and radial decreasing on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
(with a logarithmic singularity at the origin), and
\mathfrak{F}
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0,\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{F}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for
1\leq p<\infty
, and we note that

(5.7)

\|\mathfrak{F}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{F}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1.

5.2.1.
The Hilbert transform of
\mathfrak{F}

The Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})
is an odd function. For almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
it is given by its singular integral representation. We may carefully apply integration by parts (excluding the singularities and then passing to the limit) to get, for a.e.
x>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})(x)

\displaystyle={\rm p.v.}\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{F}(x-t)\,\frac{1}{t}\,\text{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(x-t)\,\log|t|\,\text{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(t)\log|x-t|\,\text{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(t)\log\left(\frac{|x+t|}{|x-t|}\right)\,\text{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{t\,\sqrt{1-4t^{2}}}\log\left(\frac{|x+t|}{|x-t|}\right)\,\text{\rm d}t.

This last integral can be evaluated explicitly, see
[
11
, §4.297 eqs. 8 and 10]
, yielding

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})(x)=\begin{cases}{\rm sgn}(x),\ {\rm if}\ |x|\leq\tfrac{1}{2};\\
\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\frac{1}{2x}\right),\ {\rm if}\ |x|>\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

From (
5.8
) we see that
\|\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=1
, and given that
\mathfrak{F}
has the correct normalization (
5.7
), it is essentially an extremizer for our problem. We say ‘essentially’ because
\mathfrak{F}
does not exactly belong to our class
\mathcal{A}
, but it is almost there (hence the
rogue
in the title of this subsection). The graphs of
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})
are plotted in Figure
3
.

5.2.2.
Approximating the rogue extremal function

We need to make a small correction to
\mathfrak{F}
via a standard approximation argument. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a non-negative radial decreasing function supported in
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
, with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
and Fourier transform
\widehat{\varphi}
also non-negative. To construct such a function, we can just take
\varphi=\psi*\psi
, where
\psi
is a smooth, radial decreasing, non-negative function supported in
[-\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{4}]
. Recall that the convolution of two radial decreasing functions is still radial decreasing (for a beautiful proof of this fact we refer the reader to
[
4
, p. 171]
). For
\varepsilon>0
small, let

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\!\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathfrak{F}_{1-\varepsilon}(x):=\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\,\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{1-\varepsilon}\right),

and define

F^{\varepsilon}:=\mathfrak{F}_{1-\varepsilon}*\varphi_{\varepsilon}.

Observe that
F^{\varepsilon}
is a smooth, radial decreasing and non-negative function, supported in
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
. Moreover,
\widehat{F^{\varepsilon}}(t)=\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}\big((1-\varepsilon)t\big)\,\widehat{\varphi}(\varepsilon t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
(recall that
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}
is bounded since
\mathfrak{F}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
). Hence
F^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}
.
At the level of the Hilbert transform, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(F^{\varepsilon})(x)=\big(\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F}_{1-\varepsilon})*\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big)(x)=\left(\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F})\left(\tfrac{{}^{\centerdot}}{1-\varepsilon}\right)*\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right)\!(x),

and we see from (
5.8
) that

\|\mathcal{H}(F^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}.

As we have argued in §
4.1
, since
F^{\varepsilon}
is radial decreasing we do have
\mathcal{C}(F^{\varepsilon})=\|\mathcal{H}(F^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Sending
\varepsilon\to 0
we conclude that

{\bf C}=1.

5.3.
The extremal problem (EP2)

Note that
\mathfrak{F}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
and that
{\bf C}^{*}\leq{\bf C}=1
. We now verify the lower bound. Let
0\neq F\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
be a given function, normalized so that
\|F\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=1
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
. Since
\mathcal{H}(F)(x)=1/(\pi x)+O\big(1/|x|^{2}\big)
for
|x|
large, we find that
\mathcal{H}(F)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for any
1<p\leq\infty
. This implies that
F
must have been in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
1\leq p<\infty
, from the start.

This last claim deserves a brief justification. An argument of Calderón and Capri
[
6
, Lemma 4]

\mathcal{H}(F*\Psi)(x)=\big(\mathcal{H}(F)*\Psi\big)(x).

Letting

(5.10)

, and hence
{\bf C}^{*}=1

\mathcal{H}(F)(x)={\rm sgn}(x)\,\|\mathcal{H}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}={\rm sgn}(x)

for a.e.

\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{F}-F)(x)=0

for a.e.
-\tfrac{1}{2}<x<\tfrac{1}{2}
. We are now in position to invoke a suitable uniqueness result, first established in a classical paper by Tricomi

Lemma 6

\mathfrak{F}(x)-F(x)=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-4x^{2}}}

, we conclude that
c=0
and

, as proposed.
