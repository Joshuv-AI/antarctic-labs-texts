Abstract
We consider a system of evolutionary equations that is capable of describing certain viscoelastic effects in linearized yet nonlinear models of solid mechanics. The essence of the paper is that the constitutive relation, involving the Cauchy stress, the small strain tensor and the symmetric velocity gradient, is given in an implicit form. For a large class of implicit constitutive relations we establish the existence and uniqueness of a global-in-time large-data weak solution. We then focus on the class of so-called limiting strain models, i.e., models for which the magnitude of the strain tensor is known to remain small a priori, regardless of the magnitude of the Cauchy stress tensor. For this class of models, a new technical difficulty arises, which is that the Cauchy stress is only an integrable function over its domain of definition, resulting in the underlying function spaces being nonreflexive and thus the weak compactness of bounded sequences of elements of these spaces is lost. Nevertheless, even for problems of this type we are able to provide a satisfactory existence theory, as long as the initial data have finite elastic energy and the boundary data fulfill natural compatibility conditions.

1.2
Relevance to the modelling of viscoelastic solids

With these results in mind, we will now discuss the importance of such problems.
We often encounter materials exhibiting viscoelastic response. By definition, viscoelasticity involves the material response of both elastic solids and viscous fluids, which can be modelled linearly or nonlinearly (see
[
8
]
for an extensive overview). On the other hand, it is well-known that implicit constitutive theories allow for a more general structure in modelling than explicit ones (cf.
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
), where the strain could be given as a function of the stress. Indeed, this is the case in our constitutive relation (
1.1b
) in system (
1.1
). Rajagopal’s main contribution
[
14
]
to the theory was to show that a nonlinear relationship between the stress and the strain can be obtained after linearizing the strain. The relation (
1.1b
) was first obtained by Erbay and Şengül in
[
9
]
as a result of the linearization of the relation between the stress and the strain tensors under the assumption that the magnitude of the strain is small. For models of this type it is possible that once the magnitude of the strain has reached a certain limiting value (as is the case in Theorem
1.2
), any further increase of the magnitude of the stress will cause no changes in the strain. These models are called
strain-limiting (strain-locking) models
and such behaviour has been observed in numerous experiments (see
[
7
]

Now we introduce some basic kinematics in order to discuss these limiting strain models from a mathematical perspective.
We denote by
\boldsymbol{u}(\mathbf{X},t):=\boldsymbol{x}(\mathbf{X},t)-\mathbf{X}
the displacement of a given body at a space-time point
(\mathbf{X},t)
, where
\mathbf{X}
is the position vector in the reference configuration and

\boldsymbol{x}(\mathbf{X},t)
is the position vector in the current configuration. We denote the deformation of the body, which is assumed to be stress-free initially, by
\boldsymbol{\chi}(\mathbf{X},t)
. The deformation gradient
is defined as
\mathbf{F}=\partial\boldsymbol{\chi}/\partial\mathbf{X}
. By the polar decomposition theorem, we can ensure the existence of positive definite, symmetric tensors
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}
, and a rotation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}
such that

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}
are the
right
and
left Cauchy–Green stretch tensor
, respectively.
Moreover, we know that each of these decompositions is unique and

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{\rm T},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}
are called the
right
and
left Cauchy–Green deformation tensor
, respectively. We define the velocity as
\boldsymbol{v}=\partial\boldsymbol{\chi}/\partial t
and denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
the symmetric part of the gradient of the velocity field
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}=\partial\boldsymbol{v}/\partial\boldsymbol{x}
.
Under the assumption that

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})}=O(\delta),\qquad 0<\delta\ll 1,

(1.25)

one can obtain the linearized strain, mentioned previously, as

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\rm T}\right].

(1.26)

As is explained in
[
7
]
, in the purely elastic setting, starting from the following constitutive relation between the stress and the strain

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})=\mathbf{0},

(1.27)

for frame-indifferent and isotropic bodies, one can obtain the representation

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})&=\chi_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}+\chi_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}+\chi_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}+\chi_{3}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}^{2}+\chi_{4}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}+\chi_{5}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\\
&\quad+\chi_{6}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2})+\chi_{7}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2})+\chi_{8}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}),\end{split}

(1.28)

where the functions
\chi_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,8
, depend only on the scalar invariants of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
, which can be expressed in terms of

\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{3}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{3}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}},\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{2}}.

Under the smallness assumption (
1.25
), we have that
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}-(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})|=O(\delta^{2})
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})
. Thus, at the end of the linearization process, (
1.28
) gives a nonlinear relationship between
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}
.
In many studies a simpler subclass of constitutive relations than (
1.28
) is considered, namely

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}=\tilde{\chi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}+\tilde{\chi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}+\tilde{\chi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2}.

(1.29)

Under the assumption (
1.25
), the equality (
1.29
) becomes

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}=\bar{\chi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}+\bar{\chi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}+\bar{\chi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{2},

(1.30)

with some invariant-dependent coefficients
\bar{\chi}_{i}
,
i=0,1,2
. The analysis of a limiting strain problem with a constitutive relation of the form
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})
, which is a more general version of (
1.30

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
, as those considered here, was also studied in
[
5
]
,
[
1
]
, where the authors highlight the analytical difficulties associated with such models, most notably the lack of weak compactness of approximations to the stress tensor in

. We rely on methods developed in

in order to show that (

We close this section with a thermodynamical justification of the model (
1.1
).
We will show in particular that the total energy of the system is constant and the sum of the kinetic energy and the elastic energy is a decreasing function of time.
We suppose that the constitutive relation can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})=:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})

where
\varphi
is a function from
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by
\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})=\phi(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})
.
We shall suppose that
\phi({0})=\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
and assume that
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}_{+})
is strictly convex.
Clearly this is the case if (
A4
) holds.
Under these assumptions,
\varphi
is also strictly convex, noting that
\phi
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
. Furthermore
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is monotone. Next, we define the convex conjugate
\varphi^{*}
by

\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{sym}}\big(\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\big).

We note that
\varphi^{*}
is also convex and, for any
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{sym}
, the following identity holds:

\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))+\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}.

Thus, the function
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}=\frac{\partial\varphi^{*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}
is also monotone. With these facts in mind, formally testing (
1.1a
) against
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}
and assuming the absence of all body forces, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(1.36)

However, the integrand in the second term on the right-hand side can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\varphi^{*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}+\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\frac{\partial\varphi^{*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})\right)\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}))+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\frac{\partial\varphi^{*}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}))+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}).

Substituting this back into (
1.36
) and defining
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}:=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})
, we see that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(1.37)

Recalling that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is monotone, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}|^{2}+\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{I}))\,\mathrm{d}x.

In particular the sum of the kinetic energy and elastic energy is decreasing. The extra term that appears in (
1.37
) corresponds to the dissipation; thus energy is conserved in accordance with the laws of thermodynamics.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

To prove the existence of a weak solution, we use a compactness argument based on a sequence of Galerkin approximations. However, since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is not invertible in general, we introduce the following regularization:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}):=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{p-2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}.

Note that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the regularized mapping still satisfies (
A1
)–(
A3
) (with
C_{2}
replaced by
(C_{2}+1)
) and in addition the inequality (
A1
) is strict whenever
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\neq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}
. Therefore, it directly follows from the theory of monotone operators that there exists a continuous inverse
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}
.

2.1
Galerkin approximation

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a basis of
W_{0}^{2d,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
2
2

2

Such a basis can be found by looking for eigenfunctions
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\in W_{0}^{2d,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the problem

-\Delta^{2d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\qquad\mbox{on $\Omega$.}

We denote by
P^{n}
the projection of
W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto the linear hull of
\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, which is continuous.
We look for
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}
of the form

\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t,x)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(t,x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}^{n}(t)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}(x),

by solving, for all
j=1,2,\ldots,n
and almost all
t\in(0,T)
, the following problem:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial^{2}_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\cdot\omega_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}^{-1}\left(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\right)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\rangle,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0),

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0).

(2.1c)

We note that (
2.1b
) and (
2.1c
) are equivalent to
\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(0)=\mathbf{0}
and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, respectively.
Since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}^{-1}
is continuous and the basis functions
\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the equation (
2.1a
) reduces to

\partial_{tt}C^{n}_{i}(t)=F_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(t),\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(t)),

where
F_{i}
are Carathéodory mappings. Hence, using standard Carathéodory theory for a system of ordinary differential equations, we deduce that there exists a solution on some maximal time interval
(0,T^{*})
. Furthermore, either we must have
|\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(t)|+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{n}(t)|\to\infty
as
t\to T^{*}_{-}
or we can extend the solution to the whole interval
(0,T)
. We shall next show that the latter is true by establishing uniform bounds on the sequence of Galerkin approximations.

2.2
Uniform bounds

First, let us define

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}:=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}^{-1}\left(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\right),

which is clearly equivalent to

\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}.

(2.2)

Then, we multiply (
2.1a
) by
C^{n}_{j}
and also by
\partial_{t}C^{n}_{j}
and sum the resulting identities with respect to
j=1,\ldots,n
to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x&=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle,\\
\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x&=\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle.\end{split}

(2.3)

Next, it follows from (
2.2
) that

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p}-\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\right).

Also, we can write

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using these two identities in (
2.3
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x+\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\cdot\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

(2.4)

and

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle.\end{split}

(2.5)

By summing these equalities we find that one term cancels and we deduce that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\cdot\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x+\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle.\end{split}

(2.6)

Next, we define on
[0,T]
the function

Y^{n}:=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|^{2}+\left|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using this, we can rewrite the first term on the left-hand side of (
2.6
) as

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x=Y^{n}-\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Consequently, using this identity in (
2.6
), applying (
A2
) to the second term on the left-hand side, and the Hölder inequality to the terms on the right-hand side together with the Poincaré and Korn inequalities, it follows that

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Y^{n}+\frac{C_{1}}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\left(1+Y^{n}\right)+C(\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{p^{\prime}}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{p^{\prime}})\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}\\
&+C(\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\|_{p^{\prime}}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\|_{p^{\prime}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{p^{\prime}}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{p^{\prime}})(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0})^{*}}+\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0})^{*}}),\end{split}

(2.7)

where
C
is a generic constant that is independent of
n
.
To bound the right-hand side, we use (
2.2
) to observe that

\partial_{t}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\right)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}t}}{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}),

which, after integration with respect to time, gives

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t))=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0))+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\tau}}{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(\tau)+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(\tau)|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Using properties of the Bochner integral, it follows that

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t))\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}&\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}+1\right),\end{split}

(2.8)

where for the second inequality we have used (
A3
). Consequently, using (
2.8
) and (
2.2
), we have also the following bound on the time derivative:

\begin{split}\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t))\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}&\leq C\left(1+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\|_{p}^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right).\end{split}

(2.9)

Hence, using (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) for the terms appearing on the right-hand side of (
2.7
), and applying Young’s inequality to the resulting right-hand side, we see that

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(Y^{n}+\frac{C_{1}}{4\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)+\frac{C_{1}}{4\beta}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\leq C\left(Y^{n}+\frac{C_{1}}{4\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\\
&\qquad+C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(t)\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}+C\left(\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{p}_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0})^{*}}+\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|^{p}_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0})^{*}}\right).\end{split}

(2.10)

Thus, using Grönwall’s lemma and the assumptions on the data, we have that

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in(0,T)}Y^{n}(t)+\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f})+Y^{n}(0)=C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}).\end{split}

(2.11)

Finally, from the definition of
Y^{n}
, the bounds (
2.8
), (
2.9
), and Korn’s inequality, we deduce that

\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left(\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|_{1,p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}).

(2.12)

It remains to provide a bound on
\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}
. We define the set
\mathcal{V}:=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1\}
. Using the orthonormality of the basis and the continuity of
P^{n}
, we deduce from (
2.1a
) that

\begin{split}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t)\|_{(W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}&=\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{V}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{V}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t)\cdot P^{n}\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\cdot\nabla(P^{n}\boldsymbol{w})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\\
&\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{V}}(\|\boldsymbol{f}(t)\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\|_{p})\|P^{n}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,p^{\prime}}\\
&\leq C\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{V}}(\|\boldsymbol{f}(t)\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\|_{p})\|P^{n}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2d,2}\\
&\leq C(\|\boldsymbol{f}(t)\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\|_{p}),\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
W^{2d,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuously embedded into
W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Therefore, it follows from (
2.12
) that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|^{p}_{(W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{p}_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}).

(2.13)

2.3
Limit
n\to\infty

Using the bounds from Section
2.2
in conjunction with the reflexivity and separability of the underlying spaces, we can find a subsequence, that we do not relabel, such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\bar{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}})),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{n}

\displaystyle\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{weakly${}^{*}$ in }W^{1,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }W^{1,p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{p}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}})),

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{p}(0,T;(W_{0}^{2d,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}).

Hence, we see that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
fulfills (
1.4
) and
\boldsymbol{u}
belongs to the first two spaces indicated in (
1.3
). In addition, thanks to the fact that
W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is compactly embedded into
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, using the Aubin–Lions lemma (for a further subsequence, not indicated,) we even have that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{n}

\displaystyle\to\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }\mathcal{C}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(2.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}

\displaystyle\to\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap\mathcal{C}([0,T];(W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}).

Thus, it follows directly from the fact that
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)
and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)
and the above convergence result (
2.15
) that

\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0).

Next, we let
n\to\infty
in (
2.1a
). Let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T])
be arbitrary. We multiply (
2.1a
) by
\phi
and integrate the result over
(0,T)
to get

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\phi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\nabla(\phi\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,

for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Thus, for a fixed
j
, we can let
n\to\infty
and using the weak convergence result (
2.14
) we deduce that

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\phi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\nabla(\phi\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
j
and
\phi
were arbitrary and recalling that
\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
forms a basis of
W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it follows that

\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}\in W_{0}^{2d,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\text{for a.e. }\,t\in(0,T).

(2.16)

Consequently, thanks to the density of
W^{2d,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
in
W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we see that for almost all
t\in(0,T)
we have
\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\in(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}
. Furthermore, we have

\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(t)\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d});\,\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1}\left[-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}(t)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathrm{d}x+\langle\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\right].

Thus, using (
2.12
) and (
2.14
), it follows that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\|_{p}^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{(W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}).

(2.17)

Hence, (
2.16
) can be strengthened so that (
1.5
) holds. In addition, by standard parabolic interpolation and the fact that
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we see that
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies (
1.3
).

Finally, letting
n\to\infty
in (
2.2
) and using (
2.14
), we see that

\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\quad\textrm{a.e. in }Q.

(2.18)

Hence, in order to show (
1.6
) and deduce the existence of a weak solution, it remains to show that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})
a.e. in
Q
.

2.4
Identification of the nonlinearity

In order to identify the nonlinearity, we will use monotone operator theory. Let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{0}([0,T])
be an arbitrary nonnegative function. We multiply both identities in (
2.3
) by
\phi
and integrate the result over
(0,T)
. With the help of integration by parts and the fact that
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)
and
\phi(T)=0
, we observe that

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi^{\prime}}{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(2.19)

and

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(2.20)

Next, we use the weak convergence results (
2.14
) and the strong convergence results (
2.15
) to identify the limits on the right-hand sides of (
2.19
) and (
2.20
). In particular, we obtain

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi^{\prime}}{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(2.21)

and

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(2.22)

Next, we use (
1.5
) to evaluate the terms on the right-hand sides of (
2.21
), (
2.22
).
We note that, thanks to the regularity of
\boldsymbol{u}
, both

\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and
\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})

are admissible test functions in (
1.5
). Using these two choices as the test function
\boldsymbol{w}
, multiplying each of the resulting equalities by
\phi
and integrating over
(0,T)
, we may apply integration by parts in order to obtain the following identities:

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi^{\prime}}{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(2.23)

and

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{f},(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi-\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\rangle\phi\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(2.24)

Comparing (
2.21
) with (
2.23
) and (
2.22
) with (
2.24
), we see that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2.25)

Therefore, using the nonnegativity of
\phi
, we observe that

\begin{split}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})+n^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\overset{\eqref{Tn}}{=}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\overset{\eqref{jk}}{\leq}\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\overset{\eqref{T-constw}}{=}\int_{Q}\phi\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(2.26)

The inequality (
2.26
) is the key to identifying the nonlinearity.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\in L^{p}(Q,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}})
be arbitrary. Using the monotonicity assumption (
A1
), the weak convergence results (
2.14
), the bound (
2.26
) and the nonnegativity of
\phi
, we obtain

0\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{Q}\phi\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Setting
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\kappa\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\in L^{p^{\prime}}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}})
and
\kappa>0
, we divide through by
\kappa
to deduce that

0\leq\int_{Q}\phi\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\kappa\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is continuous, we may let
\kappa\to 0_{+}
to deduce that

0\leq\int_{Q}\phi\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

As
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
and
\phi
are arbitrary, we conclude that

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\quad\text{a.e. in }Q.

Thus we have proved the existence of a weak solution.

2.5
Uniqueness of solution

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
it remains to show uniqueness of the weak solution.
To this end, let
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})
be two weak solutions of (
1.1
) emanating from the same data. We denote
\boldsymbol{u}:=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
. Then, using (
1.5
), we see that

\langle\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{w})\,\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}\in W_{0}^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\textrm{ and a.e. }t\in(0,T).

Since
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}
belong to
W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
for almost all
t\in(0,T)
, we can set
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}
in the above to deduce that, for almost all
t
, the following holds:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Therefore,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\cdot\left(\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the same procedure as in the a priori estimates and also the constitutive relation (
1.6
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\beta|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\beta|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\beta\left|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\boldsymbol{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\left(\beta+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right)|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq C(\alpha,\beta)\int_{\Omega}\beta|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\beta|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\beta\left|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\boldsymbol{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

The second term on the left-hand side is nonnegative thanks to (
A1
) so we may apply Grönwall’s inequality. Since
\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, we deduce that
\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{0}
a.e. in
Q
. In addition, by monotonicity, we also obtain that
\big(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\big)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})=0
a.e. in
Q
. This proves that
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
a.e. in
Q
, and if
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is strictly monotone then also
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}
.

3
Regularity estimates

In this section we prove the higher regularity estimates for the solution from Theorem
1.1
. We note that this is an essential part in the proof of the existence of a solution for the limiting strain model, i.e., the case
p=1
, as it
involves passing to the limit
p\rightarrow 1_{+}
.

As the focus turns to the limiting strain model, in this part we will now assume that there exists a strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for all
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}
,

\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}).

(3.1)

In this case,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is strictly monotone. To simplify the subsequent notation, for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}
, we denote

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}):=\frac{\partial^{2}F(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}},\qquad\mathcal{A}^{ij}_{kl}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}):=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{kl}}.

We also define a new scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}
by

(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})_{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}=\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{kl}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}_{ij}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{kl}.

(3.2)

The fact that (
3.2
) does indeed define a scalar product follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
has a potential
F
. In particular, we know that for all
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\textrm{sym}}
there holds
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{kl}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{kl}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}}
, i.e., symmetry, and also
\mathcal{A}
is positive definite as a result of the convexity assumption.

In what follows, we will split the regularity estimates. First, we focus on time regularity and then we consider regularity with respect to the spatial variable. Here, we provide only a formal proof. Nevertheless, the time regularity proof is in fact fully rigorous since it can be deduced already at the level of Galerkin approximations. The spatial regularity proof is only formal, but can be justified by using a standard difference quotient technique. We emphasise that we do not impose any coercivity and growth assumptions on
\mathcal{A}
here because, in the case
p=1
, we lose such information.

We note that when
p\in(1,\infty)
one can usually assume that

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})_{\mathcal{A}}|\leq C_{3}(1+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)^{p-2}\,|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}|\,|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}|,\qquad(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})_{\mathcal{A}}\geq C_{4}(1+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)^{p-2}\,|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}|^{2}.

(3.3)

Under assumption (
3.3
), the regularity estimates can be deduced in an easier way. However, they are not included here as the more challenging case of
p=1
is our primary interest.
Also, it is worth observing that our prototype models (
1.22
) do not satisfy (
3.3
)
2
and in general, the assumption (
3.3
)
2
cannot be satisfied when
p=1
.

Defining the convex conjugate
F^{*}
of
F
as in Section
1.2
, we recall that, from the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
, we have that

F(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})+F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}.

(3.4)

3.1
Time regularity

Here, we improve the bound on the time derivative. This bound will be used for the limiting strain model in order to pass to the limit in the term
\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}
in the weak formulation. We formulate the following lemma locally in time in order to keep the initial data as general as possible.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and suppose that (
3.1
) holds with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
fulfilling (
A1
)–(
A3
). Assume that
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{2,p^{\prime}}_{loc}(0,T;W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then for any weak solution to (
1.1
) and for every
\delta>0
, the following bound holds:

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in(\delta,T)}\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\delta}^{T}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C(\alpha,\beta)\left(\int_{\frac{\delta}{2}}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{f}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|_{2}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}(\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{C(\alpha,\beta)}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tau))+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)))+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

(3.5)

If additionally
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{2,p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, then we have the following global-in-time bound:

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C(\alpha,\beta)\left(\int_{Q}|\boldsymbol{f}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|_{2}^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|_{2}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}(\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\\
&\quad+C(\alpha,\beta)\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0))+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)))+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.6)

Proof.

Recalling that
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we set
\boldsymbol{w}:=\partial_{tt}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})
in (
1.5
) to observe that, for almost all
t\in(0,T)
,

\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\partial_{tt}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x.

This identity can be rewritten as

\begin{split}&\beta\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot(\beta\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=\beta\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\partial_{tt}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.7)

First, we evaluate the last term on the right-hand side. Setting
\boldsymbol{w}:=\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})
in (
1.5
), we see that

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x.

From the second term on the left-hand side of (
3.7
), using (
1.1b
), we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot(\beta\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-F(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus, using these two identities in (
3.7
) and applying Young’s inequality, we obtain the following:

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))+\frac{\alpha|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}}{2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&\quad\leq C(\alpha,\beta)(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}(\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})).\end{split}

(3.8)

Integrating (
3.8
) over
(0,T)
and using the fact that

F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}(0)))=F^{*}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})),

we deduce (
3.6
). Similarly, integrating (
3.8
) over
(\tau,t)
where
\delta/2\leq\tau\leq\delta\leq t\leq T
are arbitrary, we deduce that

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in(\delta,T)}\left(\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))+\frac{\alpha|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}}{2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+\int_{\delta}^{T}\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq C(\alpha,\beta){\int_{\frac{\delta}{2}}^{T}\int_{\Omega}}|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\partial_{t}(\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+C(\alpha,\beta)\int_{\Omega}F^{*}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tau))+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)))+|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.9)

Integrating with respect to
\tau\in(\delta/2,\delta)
and dividing by
\delta
, we directly obtain (
3.5
).
∎

3.2
Spatial regularity

Here, we will improve the spatial regularity of a weak solution. In particular,
we prove a weighted bound on
\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
, which is a key tool for obtaining the existence of a weak solution for the limiting strain model, i.e., in the case
p=1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let all of the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
be satisfied. In addition, assume that
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{2}+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Then, for an arbitrary open set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\subset\Omega
and any
\delta>0
, we have the following bound:

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in(\delta,T)}\|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\delta}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})_{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq C(\Omega^{\prime},\delta)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})|+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(3.10)

If, additionally,
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, then we also have

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})_{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq C(\Omega^{\prime})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})|+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+C\|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(3.11)

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary nonnegative smooth compactly supported
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then, we can choose
\boldsymbol{w}:=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}\nabla\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})
in (
1.5
) and integrate by parts to deduce the following identity:

\begin{split}\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x=-\beta\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}\nabla\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.12)

Similarly, setting
\boldsymbol{w}:=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{u})
in (
1.5
) leads to

\begin{split}&\alpha\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}\alpha\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad=-\alpha\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}x+\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.13)

Summing these two identities, we deduce that

\begin{split}\frac{\beta}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}+\left|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}(\alpha\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}x\\
=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}(\beta\nabla\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\alpha\nabla\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.14)

Now we show that the second integral on the left-hand side is the key source of information. We use (
1.1b
), integration by parts and the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
in order to observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}(\alpha\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}(\varphi^{2}(\alpha\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}))+2\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi(\alpha\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi^{2}\partial_{k}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u}))+4\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi(\alpha\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi^{2}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-4\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{k}(\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-4\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{kj}(\varphi^{2})\partial_{i}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x+2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}(\alpha\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\varphi,\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\varphi)_{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})}-4\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{k}(\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-4\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{k}(\varphi^{2})\partial_{i}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{k}(\varphi^{2})\partial_{ij}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{kk}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=:\sum_{m=1}^{6}I_{m}.

We need to determine what bounds can be deduced from (
3.15
). In particular, we show that the terms
I_{2},\ldots,I_{6}
can be bounded in terms of
I_{1}
and the data. The simplest bound is for
I_{2}
. In particular, it directly follows that

|I_{2}|\leq C(\varphi)\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|\,|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})|\,\mathrm{d}x.

Letting
\delta_{nk}
denote the Kronecker delta, in order to bound
I_{3}
we first rewrite it in the following way:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l,m,n=1}^{d}\delta_{nk}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\mathcal{A}^{ik}_{lm}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,n=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i,k,l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}^{ik}_{lm}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{lm}\delta_{nk}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\right).

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\mathcal{A}
generates a scalar product, by applying Young’s inequality we find that

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left|\sum_{j,n=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i,k,l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}^{ik}_{lm}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{lm}\delta_{nk}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left|\sum_{j,n=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i,k,l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}^{ik}_{lm}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{lm}\varphi\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ik}\varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{i,k,l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}^{ik}_{lm}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\delta_{nm}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{lj}\partial_{j}\varphi\delta_{nk}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}\partial_{j}\varphi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{I_{1}}{8}+C(\varphi)\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The term

For

, we use the equation (

) and Young’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle|I_{4}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{f}_{i}-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\partial_{k}(\varphi^{2})\partial_{i}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(\varphi)\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}+|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, to evaluate

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{u}))+\partial_{j}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ik}(\boldsymbol{u}))-\partial_{k}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u})).

Then, we can rewrite

with the help of (

) to find that

and we can deduce that

|I_{5}|\leq\frac{I_{1}}{8}+C(\varphi)\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus we have suitable bounds on the left-hand side of (
3.14

Next we rewrite the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.14

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\varphi^{2}(\alpha\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\beta\partial_{t}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}))\,\mathrm{d}x\\

4.22
) we must have

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u})\qquad\textrm{a.e. in }Q.

(4.27)

Next, we want to show that there exists a
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}}
such that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}})
. To do so, we appeal to Chacon’s biting lemma to deduce from (
4.10
) that there exists a
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in L^{1}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and a nondecreasing sequence of sets
Q_{1}\subset Q_{2}\subset\cdots
, with
|Q\setminus Q_{i}|\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
there holds

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\qquad\textrm{weakly in }L^{1}(Q_{i};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}).

(4.28)

However, thanks to (
4.25
), (
4.27
) and Egoroff’s theorem, we also know that for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
i\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
Q_{i,\varepsilon}\subset Q_{i}
, with
|Q_{i}\setminus Q_{i,\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon
, such that

\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})\to\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\qquad\textrm{strongly in }L^{\infty}(Q_{i,\varepsilon};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}).

Therefore, using the monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
and the above convergence result, we deduce that, for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\in L^{1}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}))\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
is a monotone mapping and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}
is arbitrary, we can use Minty’s method to deduce that

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\qquad\textrm{ a.e. in }Q_{i,\varepsilon}.

Recalling that
\varepsilon>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
are arbitrary, we see that (
1.20
) follows from (
4.27
) and the above identity. Additionally, setting
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
in the above and using the fact that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})
, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}|(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i,\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Consequently, we must have that

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\qquad\textrm{ a.e. in }Q_{i,\varepsilon},

as a result of the strict monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
. However, as before, since
\varepsilon>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
are arbitrary, we deduce that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\qquad\textrm{ a.e. in }Q.

(4.29)

Using (
4.10
), (
4.29
) and Fatou’s lemma, it follows that

\int_{Q}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}).

(4.30)

Next, we focus on the boundary and initial conditions for
\boldsymbol{u}
. It is evident from the convergence result (
4.20
), combined with the fact that
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
on
\Gamma
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
on
\Omega
, that we must have
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
on
\Gamma
as well. Furthermore, it follows that

\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{1,2}\to 0\qquad\textrm{ as }t\to 0_{+}.

Concerning the attainment of the initial condition for
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(0)
we need to proceed slightly differently since we have control on
\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}
locally in
(0,T)
. We integrate (
4.6
) over a time interval
(0,t)
, where
0<t<T
, and since we know that for each
n
the initial datum is attained we deduce that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\beta|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)|^{2}+\beta\left|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot((\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))+\alpha|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{f}-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\alpha(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))+\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

(4.31)

Our goal is to let
n\to\infty
. Since
t>0
, we can use the “local" convergence result (
4.23
) to let
n\to\infty
in the left-hand side of (
4.31
). To bound also the right-hand side, we first use the safety strain condition (
1.13
), which implies that there exists a
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\in L^{1}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
such that

\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0})\qquad\textrm{ a.e. in }Q.

Thus, using the monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
, we see that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot((\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))\leq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})).

Using the convergence results (
4.20
)–(
4.27
) applied to all terms in (
4.31
) with the above inequality yields the following:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\beta|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)|^{2}+\beta\left|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})(t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\cdot((\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}))+\alpha|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{f}-\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\alpha(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\beta\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))+\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\|_{1}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}+\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2}+1\,\mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

(4.32)

Letting
t\to 0_{+}
, we see that

\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(0)\|^{2}_{2})=0.

In addition, it also follows from (
4.23
) that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{loc}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, which combined with the above result gives that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

4.4
Validity of the equation in the limit

To summarize the results so far, we have found a couple
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}})
that satisfies (
1.3
)–(
1.18
) and (
1.20
), (
1.21
). It remains to show (
1.19
). To do so, we use the method developed in
[
1
]
. Let
g
be a smooth nonnegative nonincreasing function satisfying

g(s)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,&&\textrm{for }s\in[0,1],\\
&0,&&\textrm{for }s>2.\end{aligned}\right.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define

g_{k}(s):=g(s/k).

It is clear that
g_{k}\nearrow 1
. Next let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d})
be arbitrary but fixed. Thanks to (
4.23
) and (
4.29
), all terms in (
1.19
) are well-defined for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and we just need to check that the equality holds.

Using the properties of
g_{k}
, we have

\begin{split}I&:=\int_{Q}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{Q}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(4.33)

Using (
4.23
), (
4.28
), the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\in L^{2}_{loc}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}))
, which follows from (
4.17
), and the fact that
g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)
is supported only in the set where
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|\leq k
, we can rewrite the right-hand side of (
4.33
) in the following way:

\begin{split}I&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|))-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad-\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\nabla g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\nabla g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(4.34)

where for the last equality we have used (
4.4
) with
\boldsymbol{w}:=\boldsymbol{v}g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)
. It remains to show that the right-hand side of (
4.34
) vanishes. We define

M_{k,n}(s):=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{g^{\prime}_{k}(t)}{\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{t}+n^{-1}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{s}\frac{tg^{\prime}_{k}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t=:M_{k}(s).

Then, using that
|g_{k}^{\prime}(s)|\leq Cs^{-1}\chi_{\{s\in(k,2k)\}}
, we see that

M_{k}(s)\left\{\begin{aligned} &\leq C\min\{s,k\}&&\textrm{for all }s\geq 0,\\
&=0&&\textrm{for }s\leq k.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.35)

Next we can use the structural assumption (
A4
) to rewrite the term under the limit in (
4.34
) as

\displaystyle-\int_{Q}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\cdot(\nabla g_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

(4.36)

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot(\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|\otimes\boldsymbol{v})\frac{g^{\prime}_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)}{\frac{\phi^{\prime}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|}+n^{-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot(\nabla M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.36
), we use the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
alongside the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)|=\left|\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{d}(\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))^{ij}_{ab}\partial_{j}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ab}^{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{d}(\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))^{ij}_{ab}\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{ab}^{n}\delta_{mj}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}M_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n},\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})}\left(\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{d}(\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}))^{ij}_{ab}\delta_{mj}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\delta_{ma}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}M^{2}_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n},\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})}\left((n^{-1}+\frac{C}{1+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|})|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}M^{2}_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|.

Using this bound in (
4.36
) and then in (
4.34
), recalling the fact that
\boldsymbol{v}
is compactly supported, we deduce with the help of Hölder’s inequality and the uniform bound (
4.16
) that

\begin{split}|I|&\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n},\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n})}\left(\left(n^{-1}+\frac{C}{1+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|}\right)|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}M^{2}_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C(\boldsymbol{v})\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\int_{Q}\left(n^{-1}+\frac{C}{1+|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|}\right)M^{2}_{k,n}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&=C(\boldsymbol{v})\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\int_{Q}\frac{M^{2}_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

(4.37)

where for the last equality we used (
4.29
) and the boundedness of
M_{k}
. Consequently, using that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in L^{1}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and the structure of
M_{k}
(
4.35
), we deduce that

\begin{split}|I|&\leq C(\boldsymbol{v})\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\int_{Q}\frac{M^{2}_{k}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|)}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(\boldsymbol{v})\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\int_{Q\cap\{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|>k\}}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=0.\end{split}

Since
\boldsymbol{v}
is arbitrary, we see that (
1.19
) holds for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and all smooth compactly supported
\boldsymbol{w}
. Finally, using a weak
∗
density argument based on
[
1
, Lemma A.3]
we deduce that (
1.19
) holds for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
fulfilling
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{w})\in L^{\infty}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
. This concludes the proof of the existence of a solution as asserted in Theorem
1.2
.

4.5
Uniqueness of the solution

It remains to prove the uniqueness of such weak solutions.
Let
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})
be two solutions emanating from the same data and denote
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
. Then it follows from (
1.19
) that, for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and for every
\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{1,\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}+(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{w})\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(4.38)

Since
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{v})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{v})
belong to
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
for almost all
t\in(0,T)
, we can again use the weak
∗
density argument as in the previous section to deduce that (
4.38
) holds with
\boldsymbol{w}:=\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}
. Consequently, since we have

\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),

we can use the monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
and integration over
(t_{0},t)
, with
0<t_{0}<t<T
, to deduce from (
4.38
) that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot(\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\beta\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)|^{2}-|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t_{0})|^{2}+2\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(t)-2\alpha\boldsymbol{v}(t_{0})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(t_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x-2\alpha\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

We note that this procedure is rigorous for every such
t_{0}>0
thanks to the regularity of
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
asserted in (
1.15
).
Since
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
as a result of (
1.15
), we can use (
1.21
) and let
t_{0}\to 0_{+}
in the above inequality in order to deduce that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\beta\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)|^{2}+2\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\beta\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)|^{2}+2\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\,\mathrm{d}x-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-C(\alpha,\beta,T)\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(\tau)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-tC(\alpha,\beta,T)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-tC(\alpha,\beta,T)}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}(\tau)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right).

Simple integration with respect to
t
then gives that
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
almost everywhere in
Q
and consequently
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
. By strict monotonicity, we necessarily also have that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}
almost everywhere in
Q
and, hence, uniqueness follows.
