Introduction

With the increase in complexity of application domains such as automotive, robotics, avionics, and space, modern edge computing systems demand the integration of heterogeneous functionalities with different criticality levels.
These
mixed-criticality systems (MCSs)
require competitive peak performance for non-critical workloads while guaranteeing safety, reliability, and real-time properties for safety- and time-critical tasks.

Isolation
is the state-of-the-art technique to achieve freedom from interference on heterogenous
MCSs

[
1
]
.
In particular,
physical
isolation relies on federated hardware for each software component: non-critical, computationally-intensive workloads — e.g., image processing in advanced driver-assistance systems — are executed on application-class processors running general-purpose OSes, enhanced with targeted accelerators; safety- and time-critical control tasks — e.g., brake control in cars or attitude control in satellites — are executed by dependable co-processor architectures with
real-time operating system (RTOS)
support.

Despite their compact size, akin to commodity microcontroller units, the design of these co-processors is challenging since they require both dependability and predictability features.
Latency must be minimized to ensure the execution time of critical applications is tightly bounded.
For the reliability of the co-processor, no exotic technology choices, such as radiation-hardened nodes, process design kits, or standard cells, can be made for the critical system, as these would hamper the performance of the full
MCS
. Therefore, architectural mechanisms such as triple modular redundancy and
error-correcting codes (ECCs)
are required to protect against soft errors.
Closed-source solutions are available
[
2
,
3
]
; however, given the momentum of RISC-V, a vendor-neutral design based on the open
instruction set architecture (ISA)
is not yet available and could find significant market traction.
To fill this gap, we present SentryCore, a dependable 32-bit RISC-V-based mega-IP for safety-critical, real-time subsystems of MCSs. In particular:

•

We design a 32-bit system around RISC-V processing cores organized in
triple-core lockstep (TCLS)
architecture, with
ECC
-protected memory for reliability. The system can be plugged into any large
MCS
with commodity AXI4 interfaces.

•

We enhance SentryCore with CV32RT, tailored for faster context switching, and a specialized
direct memory access (DMA)
to automate sensor data acquisition in periodic control loops.

•

We develop a programmer-friendly software stack with support for common open-source
RTOSes
and provide a preliminary evaluation of its interrupt capabilities.

Architecture

The open-source SentryCore co-processor mega-IP
1
1

1

https://github.com/pulp-platform/safety_island
is a reliable, real-time platform built around the CV32RT
[
4
]
core, equipped with a floating-point unit critical for control tasks.
SentryCore offers significantly improved interrupt latency compared to vanilla RISC-V systems: it implements the RISC-V
core-local interrupt controller (CLIC)
, coupled with a custom
fastirq
extension providing register file banking and automatic context save/restore.

For reliability, three of these cores are grouped in a
TCLS

[
5
]
configuration, guaranteeing fault-tolerant operations.
Each core processes the same inputs, and their outputs undergo majority voting, ensuring system dependability even if a transient fault occurs.
To correct a mismatch within the cores’ state, they start a software-based recovery mechanism, saving the correct state to the stack through the majority voter. The cores are then reset to clear any remaining errors, reloading the correct saved state to resume program execution within just 600 cycles of starting this resynchronization.

As shown in
Fig.

1
, the
TCLS
CV32RT cores are connected to an
open bus interface (OBI)

[
6
]
interconnect crossbar, with independent access to two
ECC
-protected memory banks for instruction and data, with scrubbers to avoid latent errors. A further port connects to internal peripherals, namely the
CLIC
, platform control registers, a general-purpose timer, and a local boot ROM. A RISC-V debug module ensures debug capabilities. Access into and out of SentryCore is mediated by a manager and a subordinate
Advanced eXtensible Interface 4 (AXI4)
port.

Finally, the iDMA engine
[
7
]
, attached to the peripheral bus, features a
real-time
extension to launch three-dimensional transfers on a timed schedule, thus fetching or distributing data from or to any mapped device in the
MCS
in full autonomy. Previous work has proven this to be beneficial when periodically gathering sensor data for system management purposes
[
7
]
.

3
Software Support and Evaluation

In safety- and time-critical domains, software components such as
RTOSes
must ensure deterministic performance and robust isolation and transition the system to a safe state in case of unrecoverable errors
[
1
]
.
For these reasons, SentryCore supports several
RTOSes
, from the popular
FreeRTOS
and the open-source ASIL-D
ThreadX
to innovative frameworks such as the Rust-based real-time interrupt-driven concurrency (RTIC), rapidly gaining significant traction.

The streamlined CV32RT design allows SentryCore to achieve interrupt latencies as low as
6
clock cycles and context switching times below
110
clock cycles, comparing favorably to vendor-specific technologies such as the Arm Cortex-M4
[
4
]
.

Implementation

SentryCore was implemented in Intel16 technology as a
0.42\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}

20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}

).
The design is implemented at
500\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}

128\text{\,}\mathrm{KiB}
of
ECC
-protected memory. A preliminary gate-level power estimation suggests a power envelope of just
50-70

\mathrm{mW}
.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we presented SentryCore, an open-source, 32-bit RISC-V mega-IP designed for seamless integration in physically isolated
MCSs
.
With a streamlined interrupt architecture providing less than 110 clock cycles of context switching time,
ECC
memory protection, and lockstep execution, it can bring the benefits of
ISA
and hardware openness to safety-critical application domains such as automotive, robotics, avionics, and space, thus enabling license-free exploration of next-generation
MCSs
.

Extending reliability to the remaining components, such as the interconnect, will be handled in future work and currently relies on an external watchdog.
