At the Edge of a Cloud of Brownian Particles
Dom Brockington, Jon Warren

Abstract
We study a simple model for the trajectory of a particle in a turbulent fluid, where a Brownian motion travels through a random Gaussian velocity field. We study the quenched law of the process and prove that in a weak environment setting, the fluctuations at the transition from weak to strong disorder are described by the KPZ equation. We conjecture the same is true without the assumption of a weak environment.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider a model of turbulent advection describing particle trajectories in one dimensional turbulent fluids. The particles each have their own, independent molecular diffusive motion, and are also transported through a Gaussian random drift field which represents the effect of the fluid. The field is Brownian in time and smooth, but rapidly decorrelating, in space. Associated to this model is a stochastic flow of kernels,
[
LJR04
]
, which can be thought of as the density of a cloud of particles in the fluid, or as the random transition density of a single particle running through a realisation of the drift field. Using this interpretation, it is possible to show the flow of kernels solve a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) similar to a Fokker-Planck equation for a Brownian motion with drift.

The model is an example of the compressible Kraichnan model for turbulence, see the review
[
FGmcV01
]
, which models the trajectory of a particle in a turbulent fluid, where the fluid is represented by a random evolving velocity field that is white in time. In our case the spatial correlations are taken to be of short length and smooth in space, similar to the case considered in
[
GH04
]
, where the authors showed that removing the molecular diffusivity, at the same time as reducing the correlation length of the velocity field, with a certain relation holding between the two, led to sticky interactions between pairs of particles in the limiting process. This result was extended for the model we consider,
[
War15
]
, to a full description of the interactions between any number of particles in the limiting process.

In
[
DG21
]
Dunlap and Gu show that the fluctuations of the density of the flow of kernels are well approximated, at large times, by the product of the heat kernel and the stationary solution to the SPDE solved by the flow of kernels. We are instead interested in the fluctuations of the density in the tail. Barraquand and Le Doussal,
[
BD20
]
conjecture that, at a cetain distance from the origin, these fluctuations are governed by the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation (KPZ equation). We will show that this is indeed the case in a regime in which the strength of the environment is tending to zero. Further we conjecture that the KPZ equation also appears when the diffusivity of the molecular motion of the particles is taken to be small instead of the environment strength.

We now briefly recall the stochastic heat equation, its connection to the KPZ equation and describe some previous results on the universality of the KPZ equation. The stochastic heat equation (SHE) is the stochastic partial differential equation, driven by a space-time white noise
\dot{W}
, given below

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z=\frac{\nu}{2}\Delta z+\kappa z\dot{W}.

(1.1)

The logarithm of the stochastic heat equation,
h=\frac{\nu}{2\delta}\log z
, is the Cole-Hopf solution to the KPZ equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=\frac{\nu}{2}\Delta h+\delta(\partial_{x}h)^{2}+\kappa\dot{W}.

(1.2)

The KPZ equation describes the interface for surface growth model and is the canonical model in the KPZ universality class
[
KPZ86
]
. In fact, the KPZ equation is also a universal object itself; this is known as weak KPZ universality and it has been shown that a large class of continuous surface growth models lie in this class
[
HQ18
]
. In addition, it has been shown that certain observables of some discrete models converge to the KPZ equation, via convergence to the stochastic heat equation. The earliest such result was for the height function of the weakly asymmetric exclusion process
[
BG97
]
. More recently convergence to the KPZ equation has been shown for the free energy of directed random polymers in the intermediate disorder limit
[
AKQ14
]
. Following this result weak KPZ universality has been shown for a generalisation of ASEP
[
CST18
]
, for a class of weakly asymmetric non-simple exclusion processes
[
DT16
]
, and for Higher-Spin Exclusion process
[
CT17
]
and the related Stochastic 6-vertex model
[
CGST20
]
. Most relevant for us, in
[
CG16
]
Corwin and Gu studied a discrete version of our model, where the random drift field appears instead as random transition probabilities for a nearest neighbour random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. They showed that the transition probabilities evaluated in the large deviation regime, after rescaling, converge to the solution to the stochastic heat equation.

Barraquand and Corwin,
[
BC17
]
, showed that when the transition probabilities in the random walk model are chosen to be Beta distributed the model becomes exactly solvable. They then used their formulae to show that the tail probabilities of the Beta Random walk in a random environment have Tracy-Widom GUE fluctuations of size
N^{1/3}
, placing the model in the universality class of the KPZ fixed point, called strong KPZ universality. The same result is expected to hold for the density of the transition probabilities evaluated at point in the tail, not just for the cumulative tail probabilities
[
TLD16
]
.

Barraquand and Rychnovsky,
[
BR20
]
, found similar results exist for sticky Brownian motions and the associated Howitt-Warren flows; once again taking advantage of exact solvability in a special case. Further they conjectured the Howitt-Warren flows (
[
HW09
]
) converge, under rescaling, to the stochastic heat equation, based on the convergence of the moments. Note that by
[
War15
]
this special case of the sticky flow is not the same one that arises in the limit of our model, but it is reasonable to expect the results to hold more generally.

In this paper we show that the flow of kernels associated with our model converges to the solution to the stochastic heat equation, when the strength of the random environment and its correlation length are both taken to zero, with a certain relationship holding between the two. This result is similar to the result of Corwin and Gu,
[
CG16
]
, for the discrete random walk model. We will also consider the behaviour of the second moment of the kernel in more general regimes; Figure
1
shows the behavior we find to hold when the distance from the origin and the ratio of the strength of environment to molecular diffusivity vary. The stochastic heat equation occurs (or is conjectured to occur) at a transition between weak and strong disorder regimes. In the former the kernels converge to the deterministic heat equation. In the latter the kernels converge to zero. This is analogous to the behaviour of the partition function of a random polymer,
[
Com17
]
.

1.1
The Model

Suppose that
W_{C}
is a centred Gaussian field with covariance
\mathbb{E}[W_{C}(s,x)W_{C}(t,y)]=(s\wedge t)C(x-y)
, where
C\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
is positive-definite. Let
\sigma>0
, we are interested in the solutions to the SDE

\displaystyle dX(t)=W_{C}(dt,X(t))+\sigma dB(t).

(1.3)

In the above SDE,
B
is a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
independent of
W_{C}
and both stochastic integrals are understood in the Itô sense. As mentioned in the introduction, this is a simplified model for a particle trajectory in a turbulent fluid. The fluid is represented by the noise,
W_{C}
, and
\sigma
is the molecular diffusivity of the particle itself. Existence and uniqueness of solutions is proved in
[
Kun94b
, Theorem 3.4.1]
. In fact, by
[
Kun94b
, Theorem 4.5.1]
the solution exists as a stochastic flow,
X_{s,t}(x)
, such that for any
s\geq 0
, the process
(X_{s,t}(x))_{t>s}
solves the SDE started from
x
at time
s
.
The solution is distributed as a Brownian motion with diffusivity
\nu:=C(0)+\sigma^{2}
, which can be checked directly by calculating the quadratic variation and recalling Lévy’s characterisation of Brownian motion. Alternatively, the solution can be thought of as a Brownian motion with diffusivity
\sigma^{2}
, running through the time dependent Gaussian random field
W_{C}
, which we can think of as a random velocity field. We then consider the transition probabilities
(U_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
of the process
(X_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
conditional on
W_{C}
:

U_{s,t}(x,A):=\mathbb{P}(X_{s,t}(x)\in A|\ W),

(1.4)

Note that the family of kernels
(U_{s,t})_{s<t}
depend only on the field
W
, and form a stochastic flow of kernels as introduced by Le Jan and Raimond in
[
LJR04
]
. Further, because
X
is itself a Brownian motion, if we average
U_{s,t}
over the law of
W
we get the heat kernel,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{s,t}(x,A)]=P_{t-s}(x,A).

(1.5)

Here,
P
denotes the heat kernel with diffusivity
\nu
, the density of which we will denote by
p_{t-s}(x-y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu(t-s)}}e^{-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2\nu(t-s)}}
.

It is known from
[
DG21
]
that the family of probability kernels
(U(s,t,x,dy))_{s<t}
have continuous densities,
\mathcal{U}(s,t,x,\cdot)
, with respect to the Lebesgue measure which solve the stochastic partial differential equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}\mathcal{U}-\partial_{y}\left(\mathcal{U}\dot{W}_{C}\right),

(1.6)

together with the initial condition
\mathcal{U}(s,s,x,y)=\delta(x-y)
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta. In the above equation,
\dot{W}_{C}
is the (formal) time derivative of
W_{C}
, so that it is white in time and smoothly correlated in space. By solution, we mean it is a generalised solution in the sense of
[
Kun94a
]
, which we describe now by briefly recalling
[
DG21
, Proposition 2.1]
. The process
\mathcal{U}(0,t,x,\cdot)
, considered as a time-indexed family of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}
, is the unique solution to (
1.6
). That is, for every
s,x\in\mathbb{R}
and every Schwartz function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the following equality holds almost surely for every
t>s

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}(s,t,x,y)f(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)+\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}(s,r,x,y)f^{\prime\prime}(y)dydr+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}(s,r,x,y)f^{\prime}(y)W_{C}(dr,y)dy.

(1.7)

Here, the stochastic integral is interpreted in the Itô sense.

The same SPDE, in a slightly different formulation, was derived for the flow of kernels (
1.4
) in
[
LJR04
, Section 5]
. The solution to the SPDE can be constructed directly in terms of a Wiener chaos expansion,
[
LJR02
, Theorem 3.2]
. Note that (
1.6
) is similar to the Fokker-Planck equation for a Brownian motion with diffusivity
\sigma^{2}
moving through a velocity field
\dot{W}_{C}
; however, the coefficient of the Laplacian part is
\nu
instead of
\sigma^{2}
because of the Itô correction coming from the stochastic integral.

1.2
The Stochastic Heat Equation as a Limit

We are interested in the fluctuations of
\mathcal{U}
at large times far from the origin. Hence, we look first at the effect of diffusive rescaling on the kernel. Define for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}(s,t,x,y)=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{U}(\varepsilon^{-2}s,\varepsilon^{-2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}x,\varepsilon^{-1}y).

Then it is a straightforward calculation to show that
\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}
is a solution to the transport SPDE (
1.1
) in which
W_{C}
is replaced by its rescaled version
W^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=\varepsilon W_{C}(\varepsilon^{-2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}y)
, which has spatial covariance function
C^{\varepsilon}(x-y)=C(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-y))
. If we take
\varepsilon\to 0
then
\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}
converges to the solution to the Heat equation, as we will see in Proposition
1.3
. This convergence is only in a weak sense in the space variable; Dunlap and Gu,
[
DG21
]
, show that there is a non-trivial random fluctuation when the pointwise behaviour is considered.

Rather than just considering in the diffusive regime, we are also interested in the moderate deviation regime, where
y\approx t^{3/4}
and Barraquand and Le Doussal,
[
BD20
]
, conjectured the SHE would appear. Thus, we introduce the rescaled quantity,
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}
, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(s,t,x,y):=e^{\frac{\nu}{2}\lambda^{2}(t-s)+\lambda(y-x)}\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}(s,t,x+\lambda\nu s,y+\lambda\nu t)

(1.8)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter which we will vary with
\varepsilon
to control the distance from the origin. The prefactor is motivated by fixing the expectation of
\mathcal{V}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{V}(s,t,x,y)\right]=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\nu}{2}\lambda^{2}t+\lambda(y-x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}(s,t,x,y+\lambda\nu t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\nu}{2}\lambda^{2}t-\lambda(y-x)}p_{t-s}(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{t-s}(x-y).

In the most part, we will set
s,x=0
and consider
\mathcal{V}(t,y):=\mathcal{V}(0,t,0,y)
. According to Lemma
3.1
below,
\mathcal{V}
is the solution to the SPDE,

\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}\mathcal{V}+\lambda\mathcal{V}\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}-\partial_{y}\left(\mathcal{V}\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\mathcal{V}(0,y)=\delta_{0}(y),

(1.9)

where, here,
\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}
is a shifted version of the noise,
W^{\varepsilon}(t,y):=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{-2}t}W_{C}(ds,\varepsilon^{-1}y+\lambda\varepsilon^{-1}\nu s)
. The covariance of the shifted noise is given by
\mathbb{E}\left[W^{\varepsilon}(t,x)W^{\varepsilon}(s,y)\right]=(s\wedge t)C^{\varepsilon}(x-y)
, so that
W^{\varepsilon}
is equal in distribution to the
W^{\varepsilon}
discussed at the start of the section.

If
\lambda
is chosen to depend on
\varepsilon
in an appropriate manner, namely
\lambda=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then
\lambda^{2}C^{(\varepsilon)}
approaches a multiple of the Dirac delta distribution, and thus
\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}
approaches a space-time white noise as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Le Doussal and Barraquand,
[
BD20
]
, argue that taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
1.9
) will lead to the final term vanishing, suggesting the limit could be the stochastic heat equation.

One can also consider the moments of
\mathcal{V}
. First consider the moments of
\mathcal{U}
, which have a representation in terms of solutions to (
1.3
). More precisely, if
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
are two solutions to
(\ref*{system of SDEs})
, each driven by independent Brownian motions
B^{1}
and
B^{2}
but the same Gaussian velocity field, then for any
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{U}(0,t,0,y_{1})\mathcal{U}(0,t,0,y_{2})f(y_{1},y_{2})dy\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X^{1}(t),X^{2}(t))\right].

Notice that
(X^{1}(t),X^{2}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a diffusion with generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}f=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(\sigma^{2}\delta_{i,j}+C^{\varepsilon}(y_{i}-y_{j}))\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}.

(1.10)

To see that this representation is useful, we can note that it can be used to show the convergence towards the heat equation, in the weak spatial sense, simply by showing that
(\varepsilon X^{1}(\varepsilon^{-2}t),\varepsilon X^{2}(\varepsilon^{-2}t))_{t\geq 0}
converges towards a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with diffusivity
\nu
as
\varepsilon\to 0
; we will do this later in propositions
1.3
and
2.2
. There is a similar representation for the moments of
\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{V}(t,y_{1})\mathcal{V}(t,y_{2})f(y_{1},y_{2})dy\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds}f(Y^{1}(t),Y^{2}(t))\right],

(1.11)

where
Y=(Y^{1},Y^{2})
is a diffusion with generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}f=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(\sigma^{2}\delta_{i,j}+C^{\varepsilon}(y_{i}-y_{j}))\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda C^{\varepsilon}(y_{1}-y_{2})\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_{i}},\quad\text{for }f\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

\delta_{i,j}
being the Kronecker delta.

By taking
\varepsilon\to 0
,
Y^{1}
and
Y^{2}
once again converge to independent Brownian motions, and if we simultaneously take
\lambda=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then the right hand side of (
1.11
) approaches

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\nu}\mathcal{L}_{t}^{0}(B^{1}-B^{2})\right)f(B^{1}(t),B^{2}(t))\right],

(1.12)

where
B^{1}
and
B^{2}
are independent Brownian motions with diffusivity
\nu
,
\mathcal{L}^{0}_{t}(B^{1}-B^{2})
is the local time at
0
of the process
B^{1}-B^{2}
and
\kappa^{2}=\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C(y)}{\sigma^{2}+C(0)-C(y)}dy
. This is exactly the form of the second moment for the stochastic heat equation, (
1.1
). Note that, importantly, this prediction for the coefficient,
\kappa^{2}
, of the noise is different from that which one would obtain simply by neglecting the transport noise in (
1.9
). The latter would be
\int_{\mathbb{R}}C(y)dy
, which is strictly smaller. We can extend this argument by showing the convergence of the higher moments as well. However, the distribution of the stochastic heat equation is not determined by its moments, and unfortunately, we do not have any proof of the convergence of
\mathcal{V}
to a solution to the stochastic heat equation.

We consider a more general setup where we change the balance between the effects of the environment and the molecular diffusivity as we vary
\varepsilon
. More precisely, we consider
\mathcal{V}
solving (
1.9
) with noise
\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}
having covariance
C^{\varepsilon}(\cdot)=\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}C(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot)
, where
C
is a fixed positive definite function, as before. We can also let the molecular diffusivity,
\sigma(\varepsilon)
, depend on
\varepsilon
and then
\nu=\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}+\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}C(0)
. Two regimes are of interest, in addition to case considered previously, where
\mu(\varepsilon)\equiv 1
and
\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}=\sigma^{2}
. The weak environment setting, where
\mu(\varepsilon)
tends to
0
and
\sigma(\varepsilon)
tends to some
\sigma>0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
; and the weak diffusivity, where
\mu(\varepsilon)
tends to
1
and
\sigma(\varepsilon)
tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In the weak environment setting, we will prove the convergence of
\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda(\varepsilon)}
towards the solution to the stochastic heat equation when
\lambda(\varepsilon)
is chosen such that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
converges to a (non-zero) constant. In fact, we can couple the sequence of solutions,
\mathcal{V}
, to (
1.9
) to the limiting solution of the stochastic heat equation. This is achieved by constructing the driving noises from a common space-time white noise, through appropriate mollifications.

Let
W
be a cylindrical Brownian motion on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and suppose that
C
can be written as
C=\rho*\rho
for a symmetric mollifier
\rho
. Denote
C^{\varepsilon}(x-y):=\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}C(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-y))
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}(y):=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mu(\varepsilon)\rho(\varepsilon^{-1}y)
, so that
C^{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
. Define
W^{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}*W
, so that
W^{\varepsilon}
is a centred Gaussian field that is Brownian in time with spatial covariance function
C^{\varepsilon}
. In the following theorem, we will consider solutions to (
1.9
),
\mathcal{V}
, in the weak environment setting where
\mu(\varepsilon)
is taken to be converging to
0
as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
. Note that whilst we have coupled the solutions,
\mathcal{V}
, they are no longer connected by the diffusive scalings and shifts that we discussed earlier.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\mu(\varepsilon)=o\left(\log(\varepsilon^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}(\int_{\mathbb{R}}C(y)dy)^{1/2}\to\kappa
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Let
\mathcal{V}
be the solution to the SPDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}\mathcal{V}+\lambda(\varepsilon)\mathcal{V}\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}-\partial_{y}\left(\mathcal{V}\dot{W}^{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\mathcal{V}(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0},

(1.13)

where
\nu>0
is constant. Then, for any
t>0
, as
\varepsilon
tends to zero, the following convergence holds in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}(t),

where
\mathcal{Z}\in C((0,T);C(\mathbb{R}))
denotes the solution to the stochastic heat equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{Z}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}\mathcal{Z}+\kappa\mathcal{Z}\dot{W},\quad\mathcal{Z}(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0}.

(1.14)

The convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{R})
, established in Theorem
1.1
, implies convergence in distribution of
\mathcal{V}(t)
as random elements of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. If we adjust the definition of
{\mathcal{U}}
to account for the weak environment ( noting this makes
\mathcal{U}
depend on
\varepsilon
) then (
1.8
) holds as an equality in distribution, and we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{U}}
solves

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}\mathcal{U}-\mu(\varepsilon)\partial_{y}\left(\mathcal{U}\dot{W}_{C}\right),\quad\mathcal{U}(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0},

where
\mu(\varepsilon)=o\left(\log(\varepsilon^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. Let
\lambda(\varepsilon)
be chosen so that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\to\kappa\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}C(y)dy\right)^{-1/2}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then,

y\mapsto\varepsilon^{-1}\exp\bigl({\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\nu t/2+\lambda(\varepsilon)y}\bigr)\mathcal{U}\bigl(\varepsilon^{-2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}(y+\lambda(\varepsilon)\nu t)\bigr)

converges in distribution as a random element of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
to
\mathcal{Z}(t)
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}
solves the SHE with parameters
\nu
and
\kappa
.

This extends simply to multiple times, and it is natural to expect that in fact
\bigl(\mathcal{V}(t)\bigr)_{t>0}
is converging as a process. We have not established the tightness needed to prove this however.

Turning to the weak diffusivity regime, we can once again argue at the level of the moments and show that if we set
\mu(\varepsilon)=1
and suppose that
\sigma(\varepsilon)
tends to
0
slowly enough that
\sigma(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{-1}
tends to infinity as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, and we choose
\lambda(\varepsilon)
such that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
is converging to a positive constant,
c
, then the second moment of
\mathcal{V}
converges to (
1.12
), this time with
\kappa^{2}=\sqrt{2}c\nu\frac{C(0)}{|C^{\prime\prime}(0)|^{1/2}}
. We will justify this prediction for
\kappa
at the end of Section
2.1
. The nature of this convergence is expected to be quite different from in the weak environment setting. Firstly, some smoothing in the spatial variable will be needed to control oscillations in
\mathcal{V}
. Secondly, if the pair
\bigl(\mathcal{V},W^{\varepsilon}\bigr)
converges in distribution (possibly down some subsequence) then the limit of the noises
W^{\varepsilon}
is not the noise driving the limiting SHE.

We summarize our predictions for general choices of
\mu(\varepsilon),\lambda(\varepsilon)
and
\sigma(\varepsilon)
in Figure
1
. Let

\displaystyle\beta=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log(\lambda(\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)},

\displaystyle\alpha=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log(\mu(\varepsilon)\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1})}{\log(\varepsilon)},

where we suppose that the above limits exist and that
\beta
is positive.

Figure 1:
The limiting behaviour of the kernel
\mathcal{V}
for varying values of the exponents
\alpha
and
\beta
. The convergence to the SHE proven in Theorem
1.1
corresponds the line segment
\beta=\frac{1}{2}-\alpha
in the left quadrant; convergence to the SHE is conjectured for the line segment
\beta=\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)
in the right quadrant, including at the point
(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
which corresponds to diffusive scaling of
{\mathcal{U}}
. Below these line segments is the weak disorder regime in which the kernels converge to the deterministic heat equation (Propositions
1.3
and
1.4
). The limit of the kernels is a sticky flow at
(1,0)
; for
\alpha>1
and
\beta=0
the limit is the Arratia flow of coalescing Brownian motions. Above the line segments lies the strong disorder regime in which the limit of the kernels is conjectured to be zero; partially proven in Proposition
1.5
.

The next two results concern the behaviour of
\mathcal{V}
in the weak disorder regime, which in Figure
1
corresponds to the region below the line segments.

Proposition 1.3
.

Suppose we are in the setting of the preamble to Theorem
1.1
. Suppose further that
\mu(\varepsilon)
is bounded and
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}
tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For any
t>0
and
f\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{V}(t,y)f(y)dy\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(y)f(y)dy\ \text{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{P}),\ \text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

As previously,
p_{t}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu t}}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2\nu t}}
denotes the density of the heat kernel.

Notice that the above result involves spatial averaging; we expect that there are random fluctuations of the type described by Dunlap and Gu,
[
DG21
]
, which mean that pointwise convergence fails. However, in the weak environment setting these fluctuations vanish in the limit, and we obtain the following result.

Proposition 1.4
.

Suppose we are in the setting of the preamble to Theorem
1.1
. Suppose further that
\mu(\varepsilon)=o((\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))^{-\frac{1}{2}})
and
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then, for each
t>0
, the following convergence holds in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)\to p_{t},\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

In the strong disorder regime, corresponding to the region above the line segments in Figure
1
, the asymptotic behaviour of the kernels is expected to be quite different. We give a first result in this direction, showing convergence to zero; however the argument is only valid when
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
, so that there is a region in Figure
1
on the right which is not covered. The proof is an adaptation of an argument from Liggett
[
Lig04
]
, see also
[
CSY03
]
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Suppose we are in the setting of the preamble to Theorem
1.1
. Suppose further that
\mu(\varepsilon)
is bounded and
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
tends to infnity as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then, for each
t>0
, the following convergence holds in probability

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{V}(t,y)dy\to 0,\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

2
The 2-Point Motion

2.1
The general case

Before we move on to the main part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we will show that the second moments of
\mathcal{V}
can be written in terms of a two-dimensional diffusion, which we will call the two-point motion associated to
\mathcal{V}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}
is the solution to (
1.9
). Let
Y=(Y^{1},Y^{2})
be the diffusion in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}f=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(\sigma^{2}\delta_{i,j}+C^{\varepsilon}(y_{i}-y_{j}))\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda C^{\varepsilon}(y_{1}-y_{2})\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_{i}},

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta. Then we have the equality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}(t,y_{1})\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}(t,y_{2})f(y_{1},y_{2})dy\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds}f(Y^{1}(t),Y^{2}(t))\right].

(2.1)

Proof.

As a consequence of (
1.8
) and the definition of the underlying flow of kernels, (
1.4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}(t,y_{1})\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon,\lambda}(t,y_{2})f(y_{1},y_{2})dy\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{\nu}{2}\lambda^{2}(t-s)+\lambda y}\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}(0,t,0,y)f(y-\lambda\nu t)dy\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\frac{\nu}{2}\lambda^{2}(t-s)+\lambda(X^{1}(t)+X^{2}(t))}f(X^{1}(t)-\lambda\nu t)f(X^{2}(t)-\lambda\nu t)\right],

where the process
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
has the generator given in line (
1.10
). The stated equality follows by applying Girsanov’s theorem with change of measure given by the Doléans-Dade exponential of the process
(X^{1}+X^{2})
, and then defining the process
(Y(t))_{t>0}
as
Y^{i}(t)=X^{i}(t)-\lambda\nu t
, for
i=1,2
.
∎

Note that both
Y^{1}
and
Y^{2}
are distributed as Brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}
with diffusivity
\nu=\sigma^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)
plus an additional drift, which is the same for both processes. In fact, as
\varepsilon\to 0
the process
Y
converges in distribution to two-dimensional Brownian motion, which we will now prove. We will then use this result to show that
\mathcal{V}
converges in a weak sense towards the heat kernel.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that
Y
is the diffusion with the generator given in Line (
2.1
) for parameters depending on
\varepsilon
, such that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is bounded in
\varepsilon
(i.e. we are on or below the line in Figure
1
) and
\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)=\nu
for all
\varepsilon>0
. Then,
Y
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with diffusivity
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Proof.

We apply
[
EK09
, Theorem 8.2]
; this proves the convergence of finite dimensional distributions by considering the behaviour of the generator. We will use the following estimate, which we derive below. There is a constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
t>0
and
\nu
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds^{k}\right]^{1/k}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{-2}.

(2.2)

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the occupation times formula gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds^{k}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C^{\varepsilon}(y)}{\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y)}\mathcal{L}^{y}_{t}(Y^{1}-Y^{2})dy\right)^{k}\right],

where
\mathcal{L}
is the local time. If we apply Jensen’s inequality to the right hand side, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds^{k}\right]\leq m_{\varepsilon}^{k-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C^{\varepsilon}(y)}{\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}^{y}_{t}(Y^{1}-Y^{2})^{k}\right]dy,

where
m_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C^{\varepsilon}(y)}{\sigma(\varepsilon)^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y)}dy
.
One can easily show that for each
t>0
, there is a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\nu
, such that, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}^{y}_{t}(Y^{1}-Y^{2})^{k}]^{1/k}\leq C
. Thus, we have that for all
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds^{k}\right]^{\frac{1}{k}}

\displaystyle\leq Cm_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\varepsilon\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}}{\sigma(\varepsilon)}.

In the above inequality,
C^{\prime}>0
is a new constant that depends only on
t
and
\nu
; the second inequality is a consequence of the assumption that
C^{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
, where
\rho_{\varepsilon}=\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot)
for
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, this means that the main contribution to the integral comes from
y
small enough that
C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y)
looks quadratic. Our assumption that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mu(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
implies (
2.2
).

Estimate (
2.2
) allows us to apply
[
EK09
, Theorem 8.2]
by controlling the difference between the action of the generator (
2.1
) and the Laplacian. The Theorem gives us convergence of the finite dimensional distributions. The proof is concluded by noting that since, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
Y^{1}
and
Y^{2}
are both marginally Brownian motions with diffusivity
\nu
the sequence of processes
(Y)_{\varepsilon>0}
is tight.
∎

Proposition
1.3
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}(t)
converges to the heat kernel in a weak sense, is an immediate consequence of the above proposition.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

First note that Proposition
2.1
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{V}(t,y)-p_{t}(y)\right)f(y)dy^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds}f(Y^{1}(t))f(Y^{2}(t))\right]-\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(y)f(y)dy^{2}.

Note that (
2.2
) implies that the exponential term is converging to
1
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. Hence, Proposition
2.2
shows the above expression vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and thus proves the statement.
∎

Moving the discussion to the weak diffusivity regime, we will explain the predictions we made in the introduction for the coefficients of the limiting stochastic heat equation. Recall that in the weak diffusivity regime, we assume that
\sigma(\varepsilon)
tends to
0
more slowly than
\varepsilon
, as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
. We also assume that
\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\varepsilon}{\sigma(\varepsilon)}
converges to a positive constant
c
. The occupations times formula gives

\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds=\\
\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\varepsilon}{2\sigma(\varepsilon)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C(\sigma(\varepsilon)y)}{1+\underbrace{\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-2}(C(0)-C(\sigma(\varepsilon)y))}_{=-\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\varepsilon}(y))y^{2}}}\mathcal{L}^{\sigma(\varepsilon)\varepsilon y}_{t}(Y^{1}-Y^{2})dy,

(2.3)

where the equality under the brace is due to Taylor’s expansion and
|\xi_{\varepsilon}(y)|\leq\sigma(\varepsilon)|y|
. Proposition
2.2
shows that the process
(Y^{1},Y^{2})
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion
(B^{1},B^{2})
; if we assume that the local times,
\mathcal{L}^{y}_{t}(Y^{1}-Y^{2})
, are converging jointly with
(Y^{1},Y^{2})
to
\mathcal{L}^{y}_{t}(B^{1}-B^{2})
, then (
2.3
) suggests the following convergence should hold as
\varepsilon\to 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(Y^{1}(s)-Y^{2}(s))ds}f(Y^{1}(t))f(Y^{2}(t))\right]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(c\sqrt{2}|C^{\prime\prime}(0)|^{-\frac{1}{2}}C(0)\mathcal{L}^{0}_{t}(B^{1}-B^{2})\right)f(B^{1}(t))f(B^{2}(t))].

This is the form of the second moment for the stochastic heat equation, with the coefficients as predicted in the Introduction. This convergence can be made rigorous, see the thesis
[
Bro22
]
for details.

2.2
In a Weak Environment

In the weak environment setting, we can make stronger statements about the limiting behaviour of the
\mathcal{V}
by looking at the behaviour of the density of the law of the two-point motion. In fact, we will only need to look at the difference between the two-point motions, which is a one-dimensional diffusion. One explanation for the better behaviour in this setting is that the diffusion coefficient in the generator of this difference is converging to a constant function pointwise, rather than only pointwise almost everywhere, as happens in the other two regimes. As a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, we can make the following statement about the process
D:=Y^{1}-Y^{2}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The process
D=Y^{1}-Y^{2}
is a diffusion with generator

\displaystyle(\sigma^{2}+(C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(\cdot)))\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}.

(2.4)

Let
q
be the transition density of
D
, that is let
\mathbb{P}(D_{t}\in dy|\ D_{s}=x)=q(t-s;x,y)
. The existence of this density is standard, see for example
[
Bor17
]
. In the following, we collect some necessary estimates on
q
, which hold uniformly in
\varepsilon>0
. In the rest of this section, we’ll fix a constant
T>0
and deal with a finite time window
(0,T)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
\mu(\varepsilon)
is a bounded sequence, there are constants
c,C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
t\in(0,T)
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle q(t;x,y)\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-c\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2t}}.

Proof.

We aim to apply the result
[
Aro67
, Theorem 1]
, which provides a Gaussian estimate for fundamental solutions to elliptic PDEs. To begin, we will show that
q
satisfies a PDE. Denote
a_{\varepsilon}(y):=(\sigma^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y))
, and define
F_{\varepsilon}(y):=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}(x)}dx
. Note that for each
\varepsilon>0
,
F_{\varepsilon}
is a continuous and strictly increasing function, and so has a well defined inverse. Let
\Delta
be the process defined by
\Delta(t):=F_{\varepsilon}(D(t))
, then it is a diffusion with generator
\frac{d}{dx}\left((a_{\varepsilon}\circ F_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{d}{dx}\right)
. If we denote the transition density of
\Delta
by
\tilde{q}
, then since
a
is bounded above and below uniformly over all
\varepsilon>0
, we can apply the result in
[
Aro67
, Theorem 1]
to get that there are constants
c,C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,
t\in(0,T)
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(t;x,y)\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-c\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2t}}.

However, it is easy to see that
q(t;x,y)=\tilde{q}(t;F_{\varepsilon}(x),F_{\varepsilon}(y))/a_{\varepsilon}(y)
, so that we in fact have the estimate

\displaystyle q(t;x,y)\leq Ca(y)^{-1}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-c\frac{(F_{\varepsilon}(x)-F_{\varepsilon}(y))^{2}}{2t}}.

(2.5)

The claimed estimate then follows from the fact that
0<\sigma^{2}\leq a_{\varepsilon}(y)\leq\nu
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the claim follows after noting

\displaystyle|F_{\varepsilon}(y)-F_{\varepsilon}(x)|\geq\nu^{-1}|x-y|\quad\text{and }|a_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{-1}\leq\sigma^{-2}.

∎

The above estimate can be used to prove that
\lambda(\varepsilon)\mathcal{V}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
converges to a multiple of the stochastic heat equation in the weak environment setting. In order to study
\mathcal{V}
itself, we must look at
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
, which we define through the following equality, which we impose for any
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{V}(t,y_{1})\mathcal{V}(t,y_{2})f(y_{1}-y_{2})dy\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)f(y)dy.

(2.6)

We can also rewrite the expectation on the left-hand side in terms of the difference of the two-point motion,
D=Y^{1}-Y^{2}
. This yields the equality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}C^{\varepsilon}(D(s))ds}f(D(t))\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)f(y)dy.

From which it is easily deduced that
q^{0}(t,y)=q(t;0,y)
. Suppose that
f\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, we can apply Itô’s formula, which shows us that
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
satisfies the following PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}=\partial_{y}^{2}\left(a_{\varepsilon}\cdot q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}\right)+\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}C^{\varepsilon}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)},\ q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(0,y)=\delta_{0}(y).

(2.7)

As before, the function
a_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(y)=\sigma^{2}+C^{\varepsilon}(0)-C^{\varepsilon}(y).

To simplify some notation we will introduce notation for the mass of
\lambda(\varepsilon)\rho_{\varepsilon}
, which in most of the cases we consider we will assume to be convergent as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
.

\displaystyle\kappa_{\varepsilon}:=\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy.

We can estimate the difference between
\mathcal{V}
and its smoothing
\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\lambda(\varepsilon)\mathcal{V}*\rho_{\varepsilon}
in terms of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\|\mathcal{V}(t)-\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\lambda(\varepsilon)\mathcal{V}(t)*\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,0)-2\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy+\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)\rho_{\varepsilon}*\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}(q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,0)-q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,\varepsilon y))\rho(y)dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,\varepsilon y)-q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,0))\rho*\rho(y)dy.

(2.8)

Thus, we just need equicontinuity of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,\cdot)
, as a sequence of functions depending on
\varepsilon
, to get the desired upgrade for the convergence. We will begin proving this by considering the
{\lambda(\varepsilon)}=0
case.

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that
\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}
converges to
0
as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, then for any
\delta>0
the sequence of functions formed by
q
is equicontinuous on
[\delta^{2},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

From inequality (II.1.10) proved in
[
Str88
, Theorem II.3.1]
and the fact that
\sigma^{2}\leq a_{\varepsilon}(y)\leq\nu
, we get that
\tilde{q}
forms an equicontinuous sequence of functions on
[\delta^{2},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
.
(F_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is equicontinuous because of the upper and lower bounds on
a_{\varepsilon}
, whereas
(a_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is equicontinous because of our assumption that
\mu(\varepsilon)
is null. The lower bounds on
a_{\varepsilon}
then imply that
(a_{\varepsilon}^{-1})_{\varepsilon>0}
is equicontinuous. Thus, the equality
q(t;x,y)=\tilde{q}(t;x,F_{\varepsilon}(y))/a_{\varepsilon}(y)
shows that
q
is also equicontinuous.
∎

We will use this result to prove the equicontinuity for
{\lambda(\varepsilon)}\neq 0
, but first we need a pointwise estimate on
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose that
\mu(\varepsilon)
and
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
are bounded for
\varepsilon>0
, then there is a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\in(0,T)
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{Ct}.

Proof.

In order to get the desired estimate, we use Duhamel’s principle to get an integral equation for
q^{\kappa}
in terms of
q
. In particular, Duhamel’s principle tells us that

\displaystyle q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)=q(t;0,y)+\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(t-s;x,y)C^{\varepsilon}(x)q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s,x)dxds.

(2.9)

One can easily check, assuming all the involved functions are sufficiently nice, that the solution to the above integral equation is also a solution to (
2.7
). One can also verify the above equation by applying Itô’s formula in the definition of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
with a suitable test function, and then taking a limit. We will use this equality to derive an inequality to which we can apply Gronwall’s inequality.

Applying (
2.4
), we get that

\displaystyle|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,y)|\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-c\frac{y^{2}}{2t}}+\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}C(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-c\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2(t-s)}}C^{\varepsilon}(x)q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s,x)dxds.

(2.10)

We can rewrite this in terms of the normalised version of
\rho_{\varepsilon}
to get

\displaystyle\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s)\|_{\infty}ds.

The inequality follows by estimating
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
by its supremum and noting that
\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}C^{\varepsilon}(x)dx=\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, which is bounded in
\varepsilon
. Iterating this inequality, and allowing the constants to change between lines, we get

\displaystyle\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(s-u)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(u)\|_{\infty}dsdu

\displaystyle=Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C+C\int_{0}^{t}\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(u)\|_{\infty}du.

The proof is concluded by applying Gronwall’s inequality and making some additional simplifications. To check we can apply the inequality we just need to show that the integral on the right hand side is always finite. Note that an
L^{1}
bound on
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
provides an
L^{\infty}
estimate through Equation (
2.10
), but an
L^{1}
bound is easily provided by setting
f(y)=1
in Equation (
2.6
).
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that
\mu(\varepsilon)^{2}
converges to
0
as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, and that
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, then, for each
t\in(0,T)
, the sequence
(q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous.

Proof.

We will use Equation (
2.9
) once more. Fix
t>0
and let
\delta<t
be arbitrary, then for any
\varepsilon>0
, Proposition
2.5
tells us that there exists a
\gamma_{\delta}>0
such that for all
|x-y|<\gamma_{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle|q^{\kappa}(t,x)-q^{\kappa}(t,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{t-\delta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varepsilon C^{\varepsilon}(z)q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s,z)dzds

(2.11)

\displaystyle\ +\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{t-\delta^{2}}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(q(t-s;z,x)-q(t-s;z,y))C^{\varepsilon}(z)q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s,z)dzds.

(2.12)

If we apply Propositions
2.6
and
2.4
, then we see that this is bounded above by

\displaystyle\varepsilon+\left(\varepsilon Ct^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{Ct}+C^{2}e^{2Ct}\int_{t-\delta^{2}}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}s^{-\frac{1}{2}}ds\right)\underbrace{\lambda(\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}C^{\varepsilon}(z)dz}_{=\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

The statement follows by noting that we are allowed to choose
\delta
to be sufficiently small.
∎

We conclude this section with a proof of Proposition
1.4
, which follows directly from the convergence of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
.

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

First note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{V}(t)-p_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t,0)-p_{2t}(0).

Hence, we only need to prove the convergence of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)(0)
towards
p_{2t}(0)
. We already have equicontinuity and boundedness of
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)
from propositions
2.7
and
2.10
; thus, the Arzela-Ascoli theorem implies that the sequence
q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}
is relatively compact in
L^{\infty}([-K,K])
, where
K>0
is arbitrarily chosen. From Equation (
2.9
), we get the inequality

\displaystyle\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(t)-q(t;0,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}|q(t-s;x,y)|\|q^{\lambda(\varepsilon)}(s)\|_{\infty}ds.

Applying Propositions
2.4
and
2.10
, and using that
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is a null sequence, we see that the right hand side vanishes. Finally, we note that propositions
2.4
and
2.5
prove that, for each
T>\delta>0
,
q
is relatively compact as a sequence in
\varepsilon
on
L^{\infty}([\delta^{2},T]\times[-K,K]\times[-K,K])
. Due to Proposition
2.2
, the process
D
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with diffusivity
2\nu
, combining this with the above relative compactness proves uniform convergence on compact sets of
q(t)
towards
p_{2t}
.
∎

3
The Mild Equation

In the following, we will use the notation for
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
