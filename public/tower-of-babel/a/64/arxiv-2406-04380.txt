Physics-Informed Neural Networks for the Numerical Modeling of Steady-State and Transient Electromagnetic Problems with Discontinuous Media
Michel Nohra, Steven Dufour

Abstract
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have emerged as a promising numerical method based on deep learning for modeling boundary value problems, showcasing promising results in various fields. In this work, we use PINNs to discretize three-dimensional electromagnetic, parametric problems, with material discontinuities, covering both static and transient regimes. By replacing the discontinuous material properties with a continuous approximation, we eliminate the need to directly enforce interface conditions. Using the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis, we show that using the first-order formulation of Maxwell’s equations is more suitable for interface problems. We introduce a PINN-based decomposition on overlapping domains to enhance the convergence rate of the PINN. Keywords: PINN, Electromagnetism, Maxwell, Parametric PINN

In this study, we use a PINN for modeling electromagnetic three-dimensional transient problems with discontinuous media.

A numerical study is conducted to identify the optimal choice of functions to be used for the boundary conditions. The verification is performed on 3D, parametric, static and transient problems.

We first give a brief introduction to Maxwell’s equations. We then describe the PINN method. We explain the choice of the formulation of Maxwell’s equations using the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). We then propose a neural network’s architecture that is suitable for the interface problem, and we finally verify and validate the proposed methodology on multiple

2
Maxwell’s equations

Maxwell’s equations are a set of four partial differential equations that describe the dynamics of electromagnetic and electric fields,
and how they interact with each other, and with matter. They form the basis for many technologies we use everyday, such as computer chips and antennas.
For linear materials, the relations between the magnetic field
\bm{H}
, the magnetic flux
\bm{B}
, the electric field
\bm{E}
, and the electric displacement
\bm{D}
, are given by:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu\bm{H}+\nabla\times\bm{E}=0;

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\epsilon\bm{E}-\nabla\times\bm{H}+\bm{J}=0;

(1b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\epsilon\bm{E}=\rho_{c};

(1c)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mu\bm{H}=0,

(1d)

with
\bm{B}=\mu\bm{H}
and
\bm{D}=\epsilon\bm{E}
, where the permeability
\mu
and permittivity
\epsilon
are constants, where
\rho_{c}
is the charge density, and
\bm{J}
is the current density given by
\bm{J}=\sigma\bm{E}+\bm{u}\times\mu\bm{H},
where
\sigma
is the conductivity, and
\bm{u}
is the velocity of the electric charge. In this work, we will consider this velocity to be zero.

2.1
Maxwell’s equations formulations

Various formulations exist for Maxwell’s equations, based on different assumptions, and for various applications. We start with the steady-state assumption, where we consider that the dependent variables do not vary in time, i.e.
\frac{\partial\cdot}{\partial t}=0
. System (
1
) then becomes:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{E}=0;

(2a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{H}=\sigma\bm{E};

(2b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\epsilon\bm{E}=\rho_{c};

(2c)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mu\bm{H}=0.

(2d)

From equation (
2a
), using the Helmholtz decomposition, we have that
\bm{E}=\nabla V
, where
V
is a scalar field, usually called the electric potential. For conductive media, where
\bm{J}\neq 0
, we obtain

\nabla\cdot\bm{J}=\nabla\cdot\sigma\bm{E}=\nabla\cdot\sigma\nabla V=0,

which is known as the electric potential field formulation for conductive media.
As for the case of perfectly insulating materials, where
\bm{J}=0
, we obtain from equation (
2c
),

\nabla\cdot\epsilon\nabla V=\rho_{c}.

For the case of perfectly insulated materials, and when the field of interest is
\bm{H}
, equation (
2b
) gives

\nabla\times\bm{H}=0.

Hence, using the Helmholtz decomposition, we obtain that
\bm{H}=\nabla A
, where
A
is a scalar field called the magnetic scalar potential. The property that
\mu\bm{H}

is divergence-free gives us an equation for
A
,

\nabla\cdot\mu\nabla A=0.

In conductive media, and when
\bm{H}
is the field of interest, equation (
2c
) implies that

\mu\bm{H}=\nabla\times\bm{A},
and we obtain, from equation (
2b
),

\nabla\times\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\bm{A}=\bm{J}.

As for the low-frequency regime assumption, where
\partial_{t}\epsilon\bm{E}<<\bm{J}
, the following quasi-static equations are obtained from system (
1
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu\bm{H}+\nabla\times\bm{E}=0;

(3a)

\displaystyle-\nabla\times\bm{H}+\sigma\bm{E}=0;

(3b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mu\bm{H}=0;

(3c)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\epsilon\bm{E}=\rho_{c}.

(3d)

A common formulation for the quasi-static equations is obtained by replacing
\bm{E}
from equation (
3b
) in equation (
3a
), which gives

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mu\bm{H}+\nabla\times\frac{1}{\sigma}\nabla\times\bm{H}=\bm{F}_{\rm ext};\\
\nabla\cdot\mu\bm{H}=0,\end{split}

(4)

and is usually referred to as the
H
-formulation.
A similar approach for
\bm{E}
yields

\mu\partial_{t}(\sigma\bm{E})+\nabla\times\nabla\times\bm{E}=0.

2.2
Interface conditions

In the presence of a material interface,
\mu
,
\epsilon
, and
\sigma
are discontinuous. This leads to the following interface conditions:

•

From the divergence-free property
\nabla\cdot\mu\bm{H}=0,
we obtain that

\mu_{1}\bm{H}_{1n}=\mu_{2}\bm{H}_{2n},

where
\bm{H}_{1n}
denotes the normal component of
\bm{H}
on the first side of the interface, and
\bm{H}_{2n}
is the normal component on the second side of the interface;

•

By applying the divergence operator on equation (
3b
), we obtain

\nabla\cdot(-\nabla\times\bm{H}+\sigma\bm{E})=\nabla\cdot\sigma\bm{E}=0,

leading to the interface condition

\sigma_{1}\bm{E}_{1n}=\sigma_{2}\bm{E}_{2n};

•

From equation (
3b
), we obtain that
\bm{H}_{1t}-\bm{H}_{2t}=\bm{K}_{f}
, where
\bm{K}_{f}
is the surface current density on the interface, and
\bm{H}_{1t}
and
\bm{H}_{2t}
are the tangential components of
\bm{H}
on both sides of the interface;

•

From equation (
3a
), we obtain
\bm{E}_{1t}=\bm{E}_{2t}.

3
Numerical methodology

3.1
Physics-informed neural networks

To better understand neural networks, let us look at a simple example of a one-layer neural network. If we consider the input vector
\bm{x}
and the output vector
\bm{x}_{1}
to have dimensions
n
and
m
respectively, then the output is given by

\bm{x}_{1}=\psi(W\bm{x}+\bm{b}),

where
W
is an
m\times n
weight matrix and
\bm{b}
is a bias vector of dimension
m
. The activation function
\psi(\bm{z})
is user-defined, which could be, for example, a cosine or a hyperbolic tangent function, and is applied to each component of a vector
\bm{z}
.
By stacking layers, we create what is known as a feedforward neural network, which involves
passing the output of one layer to the input of the next layer, as illustrated in figure
1
. In this setup, the final output of the network is described by

N(\bm{x})=\psi_{\ell}\big(W_{\ell}\psi_{\ell-1}(W_{\ell-1}\psi_{\ell-2}(\cdots(\psi_{1}(W_{1}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{1}))\cdots)+\bm{b}_{\ell-1})+\bm{b}_{\ell}\big).

Figure 1
:
Feedforward neural network

One can build a more complex neural network by changing the connections (the architecture), between the layers.
After the neural network is set up with its specific architecture and activation functions, the next step is to calibrate its parameters
\bm{\zeta},
a vector that contains all the
weights and biases, so that it produces the desired output. This calibration process, commonly referred to as training, is treated as an optimization problem, where we minimize a user-defined loss function
L
,

\min_{{\bm{\zeta}}}\ L(N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}),D(\bm{x})),

where
N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x})
denotes the output of the neural network for a given
\bm{\zeta}
,
D(\bm{x})
represents the desired output, and
L
is the loss function.
Examples of loss functions are the Mean Squared Error (MSE), often used for regression problems, and the Cross-Entropy, for classification problems.

While various optimization methods could address this minimization problem, in the context of this work, we will only consider gradient-based optimization methods.
Gradient-based methods leverage the property that the gradient of a function points in the direction of the function’s steepest ascent. Therefore, to minimize a function, one would move in the direction opposite to the gradient, and the general evolution of the parameters
{\bm{\zeta}}
during training is expressed as

{\bm{\zeta}}_{s}={\bm{\zeta}}_{s-1}-d(\alpha)\nabla L,

where
d
is the step size, and
{\alpha}
is a user-defined parameter, usually called the learning rate.

The two gradient-based methods that we will use are the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) method
[
17
]
and the Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (L-BFGS) method
[
5
]
.
The gradient of
N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x})
with respect to
{\bm{\zeta}}
and
\bm{x}
, which is needed for these minimization methods, is calculated using the back-propagation algorithm with automatic differentiation.

Let us consider a domain
\Omega
with the boundary
\mathrm{\Gamma}=\partial\Omega.
We want to find an approximation of the solution
u(\bm{x})
of a boundary-value problem:

\displaystyle R(\bm{x},u)=0\ ,\ \forall\bm{x}\in\Omega;

\displaystyle B(\bm{x},u)=0\ ,\ \forall\bm{x}\in\mathrm{\Gamma},

where
R
is the residual of a PDE, and
B
is the boundary condition on
u(\bm{x})
we want to impose on
{\rm\Gamma}.

With the original PINN
[
27
]
, we approximate
u(\bm{x})
using a feedforward neural network
N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x})
, and we tune the parameters
{\bm{\zeta}}
by minimizing the loss function

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{\Omega}}R(\bm{x}_{\Omega}^{i},N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\Omega}^{i}))^{2}+\alpha_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}}B(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{j},N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{j}))^{2}+\alpha_{3}\sum_{k=1}^{{n}_{\rm D}}(N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\rm D}^{k})-u(\bm{x}_{\rm D}^{k}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\|R(\bm{x}_{\Omega},N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\Omega}))\|+\alpha_{2}\|B(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}},N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}))\|+\alpha_{3}\|N_{\bm{\zeta}}(\bm{x}_{\rm D})-u(\bm{x}_{\rm D})\|,

where
\{\bm{x}_{\Omega}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{{n}_{\Omega}}
is a set of
{n}_{\Omega}
points that belong to
\Omega
,

\{\bm{x}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{{n}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}}
is a set of
{n}_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
points that belong to
\mathrm{\Gamma}
,

\{\bm{x}_{\rm D}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{{n}_{\rm D}}
is a set of

{n}_{\rm D}
points where the solution is known (experimental measurements),
\alpha_{i}
are weighting parameters that are user-defined, and
\|.\|
is the discrete L2 norm.

The original PINN exhibits multiple limitations leading to prolonged training times and poor convergence properties. These include the weak imposition of boundary conditions, the high number of constraints in the loss function which complicates the optimization landscape, and the inherent frequency bias of neural networks

•

PINN with order reduction
[
12
]
: In some cases, the presence of high-order derivatives slows the convergence during training, and leads to a stiff problem. High-order derivatives can be avoided using the order-reduction technique, where we obtain a set of first-order equations by introducing complementary variables for order reduction.
For example, let us consider the equation
\partial_{x}^{2}u=f(x)
. We then introduce a second variable
v
, and a complementary equation
v=\partial_{x}u
to obtain a set of first-order differential equations of the form:

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}u;

\displaystyle\partial_{x}v

\displaystyle=f(x).

Order-reduction also reduces the computational cost per training iteration, because the cost of training the extra parameters introduced by the complementary variable is less expensive than calculating the second-order derivative using back-propagation.

•

Conservative PINN
[
15
]
: Domain decomposition and a separate PINN for each subdomain is used. The continuity of the dependent variables and flux between each subdomain are treated as boundary conditions and are included in the loss function of the associated PINN.

•

Finite-Basis PINN
[
23
]
: Another approach for domain decomposition is to use multiple PINNs defined on overlapping domains
[
23
]
. A bump function defines each subdomain, and a neural network is assigned to each of them. The final result is obtained by taking the sum of the outputs of the neural networks, each multiplied by its corresponding bump function. Doing so removes the need to impose continuity conditions across subdomains.

•

Gradient-enhanced PINN (G-PINN)
[
40
]
: In this work, the authors added the gradient of the residual to the loss function. This could be seen as a regularization technique, where the neural network is trained to give smoother high-order derivatives. The authors found that the GPINN results in higher accuracy at the expense of computational resources. It was also noted that including the gradient in the loss function could result in lower accuracy if not correctly weighted, and in some cases, prevent the network from converging.

•

Fourier-feature PINN (FF-PINN)
[
32
]
: The input vector
\bm{x}
is mapped to a higher dimensional vector
\bm{x}_{\rm f}=[\cos{(\bm{x}B)},\sin{(\bm{x}B)}]
, where
B
is a random matrix with values between
\omega_{\rm min}
and
\omega_{\rm max}
. Mapping the input to the desired frequency makes it easier for the network to converge to a solution within that frequency range, effectively surpassing the frequency bias of neural networks.

•

Multi-level PINN
[
2
]
: In this work, the authors train multiple Fourier-feature PINNs in sequence, where each PINN aims to minimize the residual of the previous sequence. We train the first neural network
{\rm PINN}_{1}(\omega_{1},\bm{x})
for the large-scale, low-frequency component of the solution using the loss function
L({\rm PINN}_{1}(\omega_{1},\bm{x}))
, then we train the second neural network
{\rm PINN}_{2}(\omega_{2},\bm{x}))
for a higher frequency using the loss function

L({\rm PINN}_{1}(\omega_{1},\bm{x})+k_{1}{\rm PINN}_{2}(\omega_{2},\bm{x})),

where
k_{1}
is the approximate norm of the error
k_{1}=\|Y_{\rm exact}-{\rm PINN}_{1}(\omega_{1},\bm{x})\|
. The authors achieved machine precision for certain problems by using only four levels.

4
The choice of Maxwell’s equations formulation

As seen in section
2.1
, multiple formulations exist for Maxwell’s equations. In this section, we investigate which formulation, the first-order system of equations (
3
) or the second-order system of equations (
4
), are better suited to train a PINN in the presence of a material interface.
We will develop the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) for the first-order and second-order formulations of a simple 1D test-case, and compare the eigenvalues and eigenvectors obtained with two problem sets.

4.1
Neural tangent kernel

The frequency bias of neural networks, supported by empirical and theoretical evidence
[
26
,
8
,
14
]
, indicates that a neural network tends to learn the low-frequency components of a function before the high-frequency components during training. In practical terms, the frequency bias allows neural networks to be efficient approximators with inherent filtering capabilities. However, in scenarios where high-frequency components are crucial, the neural network may struggle to converge during training.

Recently, the frequency bias of neural networks has been explained using the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
[
14
]
. For a regression problem, given a desired output
y(x)
, a set of input points
x_{i}
, and the neural network output
N({\bm{\zeta}},x)
, the authors in
[
14
]
defined the operator

K_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle,

where
\big\langle\cdot,\cdot\big\rangle
is the inner product.
The authors then showed that, by using a gradient descent method, and as the network’s width tends to infinity, the operator
K
remains constant during training, and the evolution of the neural network
N({\bm{\zeta}},x)
follows the equation

\partial_{t}(N({\bm{\zeta}},x)-y(x))=-K(N({\bm{\zeta}},x)-y(x)).

Since
K
is symmetric and semi-definite, we can use the eigendecomposition
K=QDQ^{T}
, where
D
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements are the corresponding eigenvalues, and
Q
is a square matrix with the eigenvectors in its columns. This eventually yields

Q^{T}E_{\bm{\zeta}}=e^{-Dt}Q^{T}E_{\bm{\zeta}_{0}},

where
E_{\bm{\zeta}}=N({\bm{\zeta}},x)-Y(x).

This shows that the component of
Q^{T}Y
associated with the largest eigenvalue decays faster than those associated with smaller eigenvalues.

In a similar approach, the authors
[
35
]
derived the Neural Tangent Kernel for PINNs, which is expressed as

K=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}K_{uu}&K_{ur}\\
K_{ru}&K_{rr}\end{array}\right],

for:

\displaystyle(K_{uu})_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{b_{i}}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{b_{j}})\big\rangle;

\displaystyle(K_{rr})_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle;

\displaystyle(K_{ur})_{ij}=(K_{ru})_{ji}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{b_{i}}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle,

where
R
is the residual of the PDE, and
N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{b})
is the value of
N
on the boundary.

4.2
NTK for the second and first-order formulations

Using a similar approach
[
14
,
35
]
, we develop the NTK for the second-order equation of the form

\nabla\cdot\nabla\mu u=0,

and for the equivalent first-order formulation:

\displaystyle\nabla\mu u=v;

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot v=0.

The loss function for a PINN that is trained with the second-order formulation, with strongly imposed boundary conditions, is

L=\|R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)\|=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})^{2},

where
R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)=\nabla\cdot\nabla\mu N({\bm{\zeta}},x)
and
N({\bm{\zeta}},x)
is the PINN approximation of
u
.
For a gradient descent algorithm with a small step size
dt
, the network parameter
{\bm{\zeta}}
varies according to

\frac{d{\bm{\zeta}}}{dt}=-\nabla_{\bm{\zeta}}L({\bm{\zeta}},x)=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}).

Next, we express the rate of change of
R_{2}
during the gradient descent using the chain rule, to obtain

\frac{dR_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})}{dt}=\frac{dR_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})}{d\bm{\zeta}}\frac{d{\bm{\zeta}}}{dt}=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}).

We then define the NTK for the second-order formulation as

(K_{2})_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})\big\rangle.

For the first-order formulation, with a strong imposition of boundary conditions, the loss function becomes

L=\|R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x)\|+\|R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x)\|=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{1a}{\bm{\zeta}}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})^{2}+R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})^{2},

where
R_{1a}=\nabla(\mu N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x))-N_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)
,
R_{1b}=\nabla\cdot N_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)
,
N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x)
is the PINN approximation of
u
, and
N_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)
is the PINN approximation of
v
.

The rate of change of the parameter
{\bm{\zeta}}
during the gradient descent is given by

\frac{d{\bm{\zeta}}}{dt}=-\nabla_{\bm{\zeta}}L({\bm{\zeta}},x)=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})+\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}).

The rate of change of
R_{1a}
and
R_{1b}
during the gradient descent is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})}{dt}=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\,\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})\,R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})

\displaystyle+\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\,\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})\,R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i});

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})}{dt}=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\,\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})\,R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})

\displaystyle+\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\,\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})\,R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}).

Hence, we define the NTK associated with the first-order formulation as

K_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}K_{aa}&K_{ab}\\
K_{ba}&K_{bb}\end{array}\right],

where:

\displaystyle(K_{aa})_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle;

\displaystyle(K_{bb})_{ij}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle;

\displaystyle(K_{ab})_{ij}=(K_{ba})_{ji}=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1a}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i}),\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}R_{1b}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})\big\rangle.

In both cases, the evolution of the residual is expressed as

\frac{dR}{dt}=-KR,

where
R=R_{2}
for the second-order formulation, and
R=[R_{1a},R_{1b}]
for the first-order formulation.

Since
K
is a symmetric semi-definite matrix, it can again be expressed as
K=QDQ^{T}
, leading to a solution of the form

Q^{T}R=e^{-Dt}Q^{T}R_{(t=0)}.

If we consider the example of a second-order formulation with
\mu=S(100(0.5-x)),
where
S
is the sigmoid function, the kernel
K_{2}
becomes

\displaystyle(K_{2})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(\mu^{\prime\prime}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})+2\mu^{\prime}N^{\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})+\mu N^{\prime\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})),

\displaystyle\qquad\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(\mu^{\prime\prime}N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})+2\mu^{\prime}N^{\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j})+\mu N^{\prime\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j}))\big\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\big\langle\mu^{\prime\prime}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{i})),\mu^{\prime\prime}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N({\bm{\zeta}},x_{j}))\big\rangle,

because the magnitude of
\mu^{\prime\prime}
is dominant. Since
\mu^{\prime\prime}
is large around
x=0.5
, and zero elsewhere, we can expect the eigenvectors of
K_{2}
to be dominant around
x=0.5.

On the other hand, the loss function for the first-order formulation,

L=\|\partial_{x}N_{2}\|+\|\partial_{x}(\mu N1)-N_{2}\|,

still with
\mu=S(100(0.5-x))
, is dominated by the term

\|N_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x))-\partial_{x}(\mu N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x))\|\approx\|\partial_{x}(\mu N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x))\|,

so we can approximate

K_{aa}\approx\big\langle\mu^{\prime}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x))+\mu\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N_{1}^{\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x)),\mu^{\prime}\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x))+\mu\partial_{\bm{\zeta}}(N_{1}^{\prime}({\bm{\zeta}},x))\big\rangle,

and since
\max(\mu^{\prime})\approx 25
and
\max(\mu^{\prime\prime})\approx 1000
, we can expect the first-order formulation to be less restrictive than the second-order formulation at
x=0.5.

Numerical study on the effect of the hyperparameters on the PINN with strongly imposed boundary conditions

With the vanilla PINN
[
27
]
, the network is trained by minimizing a loss function that mainly contains the residuals of the governing PDEs, the boundary conditions, and known values of the solution. The large number of objectives in the loss function can result in a stiff convergence of the PINN. The usual remedy is to add weights to each term in the loss function. Multiple studies were conducted to determine the optimal values for these weights
[
35
,
4
,
21
,
10
]
. However, in this paper, we will use a PINN with strong imposition of boundary and initial conditions.
We do so to avoid boundary errors, and the arbitrary choice of weights in the loss function.
The architecture of a PINN with the strong imposition of boundary conditions is illustrated in figure
2
, where
d(\bm{x})
is a function that is zero on the boundary, such as the distance function to the interface, and
V(\bm{x})
is a lift function that satisfies the boundary conditions.

To determine the effect of the functions
d(\bm{x})
and
V(\bm{x})
on the convergence and accuracy of the approximated solution, we will conduct a test on the system of first-order differential equations:

\displaystyle u^{\prime}+v=30\cos{30x};

\displaystyle u-v^{\prime}=0;

\displaystyle u(0)=1,\ v(0)=0.

The analytical solution of this problem is given by

v=\sin(x)+\dfrac{30}{899}\cos(x)-\dfrac{30}{899}\cos(30x),

and

u=\cos(x)-\dfrac{30}{899}\sin(x)+\dfrac{900}{899}\sin(30x).

The solutions
u
and
v
are approximated with a PINN, with a strong imposition of the boundary condition of the form:

\displaystyle u=N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x)\,d(\bm{x})+1;

\displaystyle v=N_{2}({\bm{\zeta}},x)\,d(\bm{x}),

where
N_{1}({\bm{\zeta}},x)
and

are the outputs of the neural networks.
The test is performed for various functions

as illustrated in figure

.

From these results, we can see that the function
d(\bm{x})
plays an important role in the convergence of PINNs, and that the functions
d(\bm{x})
that showed promising results all have a derivative at the boundary with the same order of magnitude as the derivative of the exact solution. We explain this behavior by denoting that the approximate solution at the boundary is

u^{\prime}(0)=N_{1}^{\prime}(0)d(0)+d^{\prime}(0)N_{1}(0)=d^{\prime}(0)N_{1}(0).

Hence, the value of

, and for faster convergence, the output of the neural network needs to be normalized, with an order of magnitude of 1.
Thus, by taking the function

with a derivative on the boundary with the same order of magnitude as the derivative of the analytical solution, the function

would have a magnitude close to 1.

We did not find any clear relation between the choice of the function
V(x)
and the convergence speed. The only constraint on
V(x)
is for the function to be smooth.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we tackled three-dimensional, transient, and parametric electromagnetic problems with a material interface. We replaced the discontinuity at the interface with a smoothed Heaviside function that we built using a level-set function.
Among all the existing formulations of Maxwell’s equations, we showed, using the NTK, that the first-order formulations are better suited for discontinuous problems, or problems with sharp gradients. Doing so alleviates the need to include the interface jump condition, which would naturally be satisfied if Maxwell’s equations were satisfied. We proposed a neural network architecture that strongly imposes boundary and initial conditions, and incorporates sharp gradients at the interface. A numerical study was conducted to analyze the impact of the function
d(\bm{x})
used for imposing the boundary condition. We discovered that to have optimal convergence, the derivative of
d(\bm{x})
at the boundary must have the same order of magnitude as the derivatives of the exact solution, to ensure that the network’s output is normalized. The proposed methodology was tested on numerous 3D problems, including parametric static problems and parametric time-dependent problems, and was found to give results comparable to the FEM method. The proposed architecture, however, does not scale well for problems with multiple interfaces, because the network size will grow proportionally with the number of interfaces. Another drawback of the proposed architecture is that it does not directly address the frequency bias of neural networks, so increasing the sharpness of the interface increases the training difficulties of the neural network.
