Participatory Budgeting With Multiple Degrees of Projects And Ranged Approval Votes
Gogulapati Sreedurga

Abstract
In an indivisible participatory budgeting (PB) framework, we have a limited budget that is to be distributed among a set of projects, by aggregating the preferences of voters for the projects. All the prior work on indivisible PB assumes that each project has only one possible cost. In this work, we let each project have a set of permissible costs, each reflecting a possible degree of sophistication of the project. Each voter approves a range of costs for each project, by giving an upper and lower bound on the cost that she thinks the project deserves. The outcome of a PB rule selects a subset of projects and also specifies their corresponding costs. We study different utility notions and prove that the existing positive results when every project has exactly one permissible cost can also be extended to our framework where a project has several permissible costs. We also analyze the fixed parameter tractability of the problem. Finally, we propose some important and intuitive axioms and analyze their satisfiability by different PB rules. We conclude by making some crucial remarks.



1
Introduction

Participatory budgeting (PB) is a democratic voting paradigm that aggregates the opinions of citizens while deciding on how to fund the public projects
[
7
,
15
,
17
,
20
,
14
]
.

Divisible PB assumes that the costs of the projects are totally flexible and any amount can be allocated to each of them. The existing work on indivisible PB, on the other hand, assumes that every project has a fixed cost that is to be allocated to the project if it is selected. However, many times in real-world, each project can be implemented upto different levels of sophistication. For example, a building can be built with wood or stone, depending on the amount allocated to it. That is, each project has a set of possible and permissible costs. Each cost in this set corresponds to a degree of sophistication of the project. In addition to choosing the set of projects that are to be funded, the mechanism designer must also choose a permissible cost for each of the selected projects. This idea of having multiple permissible costs for each project was first proposed in a survey by Aziz and Shah
[
4
]
, in which the authors called each of these costs a
degree of completion
. However, the idea still remained to be studied and our paper bridges this gap.

Preference elicitation methods typically studied in any voting framework include approval votes, ordinal votes, and cardinal votes. These methods also continue to be the most studied preference elicitation approaches in PB
[
16
,
3
,
19
,
13
,
12
,
6
,
2
,
8
,
18
]
. However, PB is a setting with several attributes like costs attached to the projects. The preferences and utilities of the voters are thus much more complex. This propels the need to devise preference models specific to PB, as pointed out by Aziz and Shah
[
4
]
. One step in this direction is the introduction of a special case of approval votes, called knapsack votes, by Goel et al.
[
10
]
, where each voter reports her most favorite budget division. This idea however is criticised for its assumption that any project not in this division yields no utility to the voter.

Our paper is another step in the direction of preference modeling for PB. We introduce the approach of
ranged approval votes
, which strictly generalizes the approval votes. Each voter reports a lower bound and an upper bound on the cost that she thinks each project deserves. All the bounds are initially set to
0
by default. Voting proceeds in two steps. In the first step, the voter starts by approving the projects she likes. For these approved projects, only the upper bounds will automatically change to the highest permissible cost. In the second step, the voter may
optionally
change bounds for some of these approved projects, if she wishes to have a say on the amount they deserve. Note that this is cognitively not much more demanding than the approval votes since we do not force the voters to report the bounds.
Notably, all our computational results can also be extended to more general utility functions with minor tweaks. Nevertheless, we present the results for ranged approval votes due to their cognitive simplicity and natural relevance in the model.

It needs to be mentioned that a work by Goel et al.
[
10
]
views divisible PB as a model of indivisible PB where every unit of money in the cost corresponds to one possible degree of sophistication, and proposes a greedy algorithm. Their work however imposes knapsack constraint on each vote as well as assumes all lower bounds to be zero. Their model hence forms a very restricted special case of ours.

2
Model

In our model, a budget
b
is given. We denote the set of
n
voters by
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and the set of
m
projects by
P
. Each project
j\in P
has

t_{j}

possible degrees of sophistication captured by the set
\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)=\{P_{j}^{0},P_{j}^{1},\ldots,P_{j}^{t_{j}}\}
. The cost of each degree
P_{j}^{t}

is indicated by
c_{j}^{t}

. We assume that
c_{j}^{0}

is zero for all
j\in P
and it corresponds to not funding the project
j
.

Each voter
i\in N
reports for every project
j
, a lower bound

l_{i}(j)

and an upper bound

h_{i}(j)

such that
l_{i}(j),h_{i}(j)\in\{c_{j}^{0},\ldots,c_{j}^{t_{j}}\}
and
l_{i}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)
. Let
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{H}
respectively denote the collection all the lower bounds and upper bounds reported by all the voters.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of all the possible degrees of all projects, or in other words,
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{j\in P}{\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)}
. We denote the cost of a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{D}
,
\sum_{P_{j}^{t}\in S}{c_{j}^{t}}
, by

\operatorname{c}\!\left(S\right)

. Given a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{D}
, we use

S(j)

to denote the chosen degree(s) of project
j
in
S
. In other words,
S(j)=S\cap\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)
. We use the shorthand notation

c^{S}(j)

to denote

\operatorname{c}\!\left(S(j)\right)

. We say a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{D}
is
valid
if
\operatorname{c}\!\left(S\right)\leq b
and
|S(j)|=1
for every
j\in P
. Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the collection of all the valid subsets.

A PB instance with multiple degrees of sophistication is denoted by
I=\langle N,\mathcal{D},\operatorname{c},b,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H}\rangle
. Given an instance, the objective of a PB rule
R
is to output a valid subset
S\in\mathcal{V}
.

2.1
The PB Rules

The PB rules we study are utilitarian rules. This implies that, given a function
u
that measures utility a voter derives from a subset of projects, the corresponding PB rule outputs a valid set of projects that maximizes the sum of utilities of all the voters, i.e., it outputs some
S\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S\right)}
is maximized.
We say a subset
S
of projects is selected under a PB rule
R
if it maximizes the total utility of the voters. We say a project
P_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}

wins
under a PB rule
R
if it belongs to some set that can be selected under
R
. Let
R\left(I\right)
be the collection of all the projects that win under the PB rule
R
, given an instance
I
.

Now, we only need to define different utility functions. Given the lower and upper bounds reported by the voters, we define the utility of a voter from a valid set
S\in\mathcal{V}
in four ways. For each of these utility functions, we also give a shorthand notation for the utilitarian PB rule associated to it.

1.

Cardinal utility (
\boldsymbol{R_{|S|}}
rule)
: Each voter
i
derives a utility of
1
from a project
j
if the cost of the chosen degree falls within the bounds specified by the voter. Thus,
u_{i}\left(S\right)=|j\in P:l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)|,c^{S}(j)\neq 0
.

2.

Cost utility (
\boldsymbol{R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}}
rule)
: A voter
i
derives a utility of

c^{S}(j)

from a project
j
if its value falls within the bounds specified by the voter.

u_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum\limits_{j:l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)}{c^{S}(j)}
.

3.

Cost capped utility (
\boldsymbol{R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}}
rule)
: Each voter
i
derives a utility of

c^{S}(j)

from a project
j
if the value falls within the bounds reported by her, a utility of

h_{i}(j)

if
c^{S}(j)>h_{i}(j)
, and a utility of
0
if
c^{S}(j)<l_{i}(j)
. That is, with slight abuse of notation,
u_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum_{j\in P}{u_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
, where

u_{i}\left(S,j\right)

is defined as:

\displaystyle u_{i}\left(S,j\right)\!=\!\begin{cases}0&c^{S}(j)<l_{i}(j)\\
c^{S}(j)&l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)\\
h_{i}(j)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

4.

Distance disutility (
\boldsymbol{R_{\|\;\|}}
rule)
: From every project
j
, each voter derives a disutility of
0
if the value falls within the bounds reported by her, a disutility of
c^{S}(j)-h_{i}(j)
if
c^{S}(j)>h_{i}(j)
, and a disutility of
l_{i}(j)-c^{S}(j)
if
c^{S}(j)<l_{i}(j)
. That is, with slight abuse of notation,
d_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum_{j\in P}{d_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
, where

d_{i}\left(S,j\right)

is defined as follows:

\displaystyle d_{i}\left(S,j\right)\!=\!\begin{cases}l_{i}(j)-c^{S}(j)&c^{S}(j)<l_{i}(j)\\
0&l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)\\
c^{S}(j)-h_{i}(j)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The corresponding PB rule
\boldsymbol{R_{\|\;\|}}
minimizes the total disutility.

The first two notions of utilities are the natural extensions of the notions considered in
[
19
]
. The former reflects that the voter is happy as long as the cost allocated to the project is acceptable to her, whereas the latter reflects that as the project gets more money, the voter gets happier if the cost is acceptable to her.

The second utility notion clearly assumes that if the project gets more money than what the voter thinks it deserves, voter derives zero utility. However, this need not always be the case in real-world. For example, say a voter will be happy to have an entertainment park in the neighborhood but feels that the park deserves any amount between 1000 and 5000 units. Now, if the park project is allocated 7000 units, the voter could still be happy that there is a park in the neighborhood but derive no more utility than 5000 (since it is the maximum value she thinks the park deserves). The third utility notion tackles such scenarios by modifying the second utility notion to cap the utility at

h_{i}(j)

instead of dropping it to
0
.

The first three utility notions assume that allocating any cost outside the range reported by the voter yields the same utility to her. However, in many situations, closer the cost of the project is to the acceptable range of the voter, higher is the satisfaction voter derives from it. For example, say a voter feels that a certain project is worth at least 1000 units. An outcome that allocates 900 units to it is likely to be more preferred by the voter over something that allocates 50 units to it. The fourth rule handles such situations by considering the distance between the cost allocated and the closest acceptable cost as the disutility. Farther the cost is from the acceptable range, higher is the amount misspent.

3
Computational Complexity

Here, we analyze the computational complexity of our PB rules. We strengthen the existing positive results in the literature
[
19
]
and also present a few new results on fixed parameter tractability. All the exact algorithms we present are based on dynamic programming. The approximation schemes we present depend on both dynamic programming as well as a clever rounding scheme.

3.1
The Rule
{R_{|S|}}

Recall that
R_{|S|}
outputs a valid set that maximizes the sum of the utilities of voters, where the utility of a voter is defined as
u_{i}\left(S\right)=|j\in P:l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)|
. Talmon and Faliszewski
[
19
]
prove that for the case with only one permissible cost for each project, a subset maximizing the utility can be computed in polynomial time. We strengthen this result and prove that even when there are multiple permissible costs for each project, a subset maximizing the total utility can be computed in polynomial time.

Theorem 1
.

For any instance
I
, a subset
S\in\mathcal{V}
that is selected under
R_{|S|}
can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof.

We present an algorithm that uses dynamic programming. Let
\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)
denote the number of voters
i
such that
l_{i}(j)\leq c_{j}^{t}\leq h_{i}(j)
. Construct a dynamic programming table such that
A(x,y)
corresponds to the cost of cheapest valid subset of
\bigcup_{j=1}^{x}{\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)}
such that the total score of projects in the set is exactly
y
.

Let
F(y)\subseteq\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{1}\right)
such that
\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{1}^{t}\right)=y
for every
P_{1}^{t}\in\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{1}\right)
. We compute the first row as:
A(1,y)=\min(c_{j}^{t}:P_{j}^{t}\in F(y))
. All the remaining rows are computed recursively as follows:
A(x,y)=\min\Big(A\big(x-1,y\big)\;,\;\min_{t=1}^{t_{x}}{\big(A\big(x-1,y-\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{x}^{t}\right)\big)+c_{x}^{t}\big)}\Big)
.
In addition to computing this table, we store the sets corresponding to each entry in
A
in a separate table
B
as follows: if
A(x,y)
achieves the minimum value at
A(x-1,y)
, we copy
B(x-1,y)
to
B(x,y)
and append
P_{j}^{0}
. If
A(x,y)
achieves the minimum value at
A(x-1,y-\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{x}^{t}\right))+c_{x}^{t}
for some
t\in[1,t_{x}]
, we set
B(x,y)=B(x-1,y)\cup\{P_{x}^{t}\}
. Finally, we output the set at
B(x,y)
such that
A(x,y)\leq b
and
y
is maximized.

Correctness

Recurrence ensures that for any entry in
B
, we select at most one project from

\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)

for every
j
. While selecting the outcome from

B
, we ensured that its cost is within the budget. Hence, the output of the algorithm will be a valid subset. Optimality follows from the way
A
is defined.

Running Time

Each row in
A
corresponds to making a decision about one project from
P
, and hence the number of rows is
m
. Each column corresponds to a possible total score. Since we select only one project from each

\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)

and maximum score of any degree of a project is
n
by definition, maximum total score is
mn
. Thus, we have
mn
columns. Computing each entry in row
x
takes
O(t_{x})
time. Thus, the running time is
\boldsymbol{O(m^{2}nt^{*})}
, where
t^{*}=\max_{j\in P}{t_{j}}
.
∎

3.2
The Rule
{R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}}

Recall that
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
outputs a valid set that maximizes the sum of the utilities of voters, where the utility of a voter is:

u_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum\limits_{j:l_{i}(j)\leq c^{S}(j)\leq h_{i}(j)}{c^{S}(j)}.

Talmon and Faliszewski
[
19
]
prove that for the case where each project has only one permissible cost, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to determine if there exists a feasible subset that guarantees a total utility of at least a given value. Since their model can be modeled as a special case of ours, the hardness easily follows.

Proposition 1
.

For an instance
I
and a value
s
, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to check if
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
outputs a set with at least a total utility
s
.

Proof.

Given a PB instance with approval votes (each project
j
has some cost
c(j)
and every voter reports a subset
A_{i}
of projects that she likes), we construct an instance for
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
as follows: For every project
j\in P
, create exactly two degrees such that
c_{j}^{0}=0
and
c_{j}^{1}=c(j)
. For each voter
i
, set
l_{i}(j)=0
for every project
j
and set
h_{i}(j)=c_{j}^{1}
if and only if
j\in A_{i}
. Clearly, both the instances are equivalent and we skip the proof of correctness.
∎

To cope up with the intractability, Talmon and Faliszewski
[
19
]
prove that if every project has only one permissible cost, the problem has a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm and FPTAS. We extend both these results to our model with multiple permissible costs.

Proposition 2
.

For any instance
I
, a subset
S\in\mathcal{V}
selected under
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
can be computed in pseudo-polynomial time.

Proof.

We multiply every
\operatorname{s}(P_{j}^{t})
in the proof of Thm.
1
with
c_{j}^{t}

. DP tables are also constructed as explained in Thm.
1
. The maximum score achievable by each
P_{j}^{t}

is
nc_{j}^{t}
. The total score is upper bounded by
n\!\operatorname{c}\!\left(S\right)
since at most a single project from each
\operatorname{D}
is chosen into each
B(x,y)
. This is bounded by
nb
since
S\in\mathcal{V}
. Thus, the table size is
O(mnb)
and the time is
\boldsymbol{O(mnt^{*}b)}
, where
t^{*}=\max_{j\in P}{t_{j}}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

There is an FPTAS for
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
.

Proof.

The idea is inspired from one of the existing FPTAS algorithms of the knapsack problem
[
11
]
. We round the scores of all the projects and use the DP table explained in Thm.
1
on the modified instance.

Given an instance
I
, let
M
be the maximum score of a degree of project, i.e.,
M=\max_{j\in P,t\in[1,t_{j}]}{\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)}
. We can easily ensure that
M\leq OPT
by eliminating all the project degrees that cannot be part of any set with cost within
b
. Take any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Now, create new scores of all the projects as follows:
\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)=\left\lfloor\frac{s(P^{t}_{j})m}{\epsilon M}\right\rfloor
. We construct the DP tables similar to those in Thm.
1
, considering new scores.
By the definition of

\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)

, for every
j\in P
and
t\in[1,t_{j}]
:

\displaystyle\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)m}{\epsilon M}

(1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon M(\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right)+1)}{m}

(2)

Let
S
be the outcome of this DP algorithm. Say
O
denotes the optimal solution for
I
under
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
. Please note that the DP ensures that
S
is a valid set. Since
S
is the optimal solution for the modified scores,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{O(j)}\right)}\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{S(j)}\right)}

(3)

Now, we prove that the approximation factor of
(1-\epsilon)
holds.

\displaystyle OPT=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{O(j)}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\frac{\epsilon M(\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{O(j)}\right)+1)}{m}}

(From (
2
))

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\frac{\epsilon M\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{S(j)}\right)}{m}}+\epsilon M

(From (
3
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon M}{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\frac{\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{S(j)}\right)m}{\epsilon M}}+\epsilon M

(From (
1
))

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\operatorname{s}\!\left(P_{j}^{S(j)}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\epsilon)OPT

(
\because M\leq OPT
)

Running Time:
The table has
m
rows. The modified score of each degree of project is upper bounded by
\frac{s(P^{t}_{j})m}{\epsilon M}
, which is further bounded by
\frac{m}{\epsilon}
due to the definition of
M
. Since the output is a valid set, it has at most
m
projects and the maximum possible total score is upper bounded by
\frac{m^{2}}{\epsilon}
, which is the number of columns. Computing each entry in row
x
takes
O(t_{x})
time. Thus, the running time is
\boldsymbol{O(\frac{m^{3}t^{*}}{\epsilon})}
, where
t^{*}=\max_{j\in P}{t_{j}}
.
∎

3.2.1
Fixed Parameter Tractability

Recently, Sreedurga et al.
[
18
]
introduced a new parameter called scalable limit for PB. The authors observed that this value is often small in PB elections in real life (e.g., datasets at https://pbstanford.org/ where budget and all the costs are multiples of some really high value). Motivated by this, we prove that
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
is in FPT w.r.t. scalable limit. Before that, we define the scalable limit in our model as follows:

Definition 1
.

For any instance
I
, we refer to
\frac{\max_{P_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}}{c_{j}^{t}}}{\tiny{GCD}(c_{1}^{1},\ldots,c_{m}^{t_{m}},b)}
as the
scalable limit
(
\delta
) of the instance.

Intuitively, we scale down all the costs and budget as much as possible, while ensuring that all these values continue to be integers. The scalable limit then refers to the cost of the costliest degree in
\mathcal{D}
in this scaled down instance.

Proposition 3
.

For any instance
I
, computing a subset
S\in\mathcal{V}
that is selected under
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
is in
\mathsf{FPT}
w.r.t. scalable limit
\delta
.

Proof.

We again prove this by constructing a DP table. Let
k=\frac{1}{\tiny{GCD}(c_{1}^{1},\ldots,c_{m}^{t_{m}},b)}
. First, we get a new instance
I^{\prime}
by scaling down all the costs and budget as follows:
c_{j}^{{}^{\prime}t}=kc_{j}^{t}
and
b^{\prime}=kb
. Then, construct the DP table similar to
Theorem

1
.

Running Time:
The table has
m
rows. The modified cost of each degree of project is upper bounded by
\delta
and hence the maximum score possible from each degree of a project is upperbounded by
n\delta
. Since the output is a valid set, there can be at most
m
projects in it and hence the maximum possible total score is upper bounded by
mn\delta
, which is the number of columns. Computing each entry in row
x
takes
O(t_{x})
time and the running time is
\boldsymbol{O(m^{2}n\delta t^{*})}
.∎

Proposition 4
.

For any instance
I
, computing a subset
S\in\mathcal{V}
that is selected under
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
is in
\mathsf{FPT}
w.r.t.
m
.

The above proposition follows from the fact the total number of valid subsets is exponential in
m
(upper bounded by
(t^{*})^{m}
, where
t^{*}=\max_{j\in P}{t_{j}}
) and computing the total utility of any set under
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
can be done in polynomial time.

3.3
The Rule
{R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}}

This rule is similar to the previous rule
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
, with the only difference being the way a voter is assumed to view the projects allocated higher cost than the approved limit. The utility is defined as
u_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum_{j\in P}{u_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
, where

u_{i}\left(S,j\right)

is taken as defined in
Section

2.1
. The proof of the following result is deferred to the appendix.

Proposition 5
.

Given an instance
I
, the following statements hold:

1.

For any value
s
, it is
\mathsf{NP}

The Rule
{R_{\|\;\|}}

Recall that
R_{\|\;\|}
outputs the feasible set that minimizes

, where

is defined as in

Proposition 6
.

For an instance
I
and a value

, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to determine if

outputs a set that has a total disutility of at most

.

Proof.

For a PB instance with approval votes (each project
j
has some cost
c(j)
and every voter reports a subset
A_{i}
of projects that she likes) and a positive value
x
, we construct an instance for
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
as follows: For every project
j\in P
, create exactly two degrees such that
c_{j}^{0}=0
and
c_{j}^{1}=c(j)
. For each voter

, set
h_{i}(j)=c(j)
for every project

and set

if and only if

. Let

. Set

.

Correctness

To prove the forward direction, say the approval-based PB instance is a
Yes

S
of projects such that
\sum_{i\in N}{\operatorname{c}\!\left(S\cap A_{i}\right)}\geq x
. Now calculate the disutility of

under

. Then,

for every

. Then, total utility from

∎

3.4.1
Parameterized Approximation Algorithm (FPTAS)

The very high disutilities that are hard to be bound motivate us to propose a
parameterized FPTAS
, which has been of a great interest recently
[
9
]
. We consider a parameter that we call
variance coefficient
,
\gamma
, which, on being given an instance
I
, intuitively shows how divergent the disutilities of different degrees of projects in the instance are. It is captured by measuring the highest disutility a single project can have, relative to sum of the least possible disutilities from all the projects. We explain this formally below.

Given an instance
I
, for each degree of a project
P_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}
, we use
q_{j}^{t}
to denote the disutility the project will contribute to any set that contains it. We call this
disutility contribution
of
P_{j}^{t}

. Suppose for example,
P_{j}^{t}\in S
. That implies,
c^{S}(j)=c_{j}^{t}
(
\because S
is valid). Therefore,

q_{j}^{t}=\sum_{i:c_{j}^{t}<l_{i}(j)}{(l_{i}(j)-c_{j}^{t})}+\sum_{i:h_{i}(j)<c_{j}^{t}}{(c_{j}^{t}-h_{i}(j))}.

It can be observed that,
\sum_{i\in N}d_{i}\left(S\right)=\sum_{P_{j}^{t}\in S}{q_{j}^{t}}
.

Let
q_{m}=\max_{P_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}}{q_{j}^{t}}
. That is,
q_{m}
is the maximum of disutility contributions of all the degrees of all the projects. We use
q_{\sigma}
to denote the sum of minimum disutility contributions from each

\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)

, i.e.,
q_{\sigma}=\sum_{j\in P}{\min_{t\in\operatorname{D}\!\left(P_{j}\right)}{q_{j}^{t}}}
. Our parameter
\gamma
is the ratio
q_{m}/q_{\sigma}
. Intuitively, this parameter means that we want the disutility contribution from a single project not to be extremely higher than the sum of the least disutility contributions from all the
m
projects.

Theorem 3
.

For any instance
I
and
\epsilon\in[0,1]

such that

can be computed in

time, where

is the optimal possible total disutility under
R_{\|\;\|}
,

is the variance coefficient, and

Proof.

The idea is similar to that in Thm.

(4)

By the definition of

\displaystyle q_{j}^{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon q_{\sigma}\alpha\!\left(P_{j}^{t}\right){}}{m}

Now, we prove that the approximation factor of

\displaystyle OPT=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{q_{j}^{O(j)}}

The first axiom implies that if the voters are narrowing down their interval towards a degree that was winning, then the degree still continues to win.

(Shrink-resistant)

A PB rule

Given an instance

(Range-abiding)

A PB rule

, a project

.

and

and

Proof.

First, we prove that

and

are not range-abiding. Recall that

5
Summary

Many times, there are multiple ways of executing a public project and hence several, but limited number of, choices for the amount to be allocated to this project. Unfortunately, the existing preference elicitation methods and aggregation rules for participatory budgeting do not take this factor into account. Our work is an attempt to bridge this gap. We generalized two utility notions defined for PB with approval votes to our model. We also proposed two other utility notions unique to our model. We analyzed all the corresponding utilitarian PB rules computationally and axiomatically.

Our computational part strengthens all the existing positive results, and also introduces several new parameterized tractability results (FPT, parameterized FPTAS) taking into account the parameters recently introduced in the PB literature. It is worth highlighting that all our computational results in
Section

3
can be generalized by replacing the utilities with cardinal utility for every degree of each project. However, we present all the results for ranged approval votes due to their practical relevance, simplicity, and deep association with axiomatic analysis.

Followed by this, we introduce several axioms for our model with ranged approval votes and investigate which of these are satisfied by our PB rules. Note that, though none of the proposed PB rules satisfies all the axioms, every rule satisfies some axioms. Axiomatic analysis reflects the properties of each rule, using which the PB organizer can pick a rule based on the context. Also, it is worth bearing in mind that the novel disutility notion and PB rule
R_{\|\;\|}
we proposed for our model satisfies as many axioms as any simple approval-based PB rule satisfies. One of the key takeaways of this paper is hence a conclusion that
R_{\|\;\|}
is a very good choice when each voter approves a range of costs.

c^{S}(j)<l_{i}(j)
or
h_{i}(j)<c^{S}(j)
, then shifting

l_{i}(j)

and

h_{i}(j)

closer to

c^{S}(j)

may cause

u_{i}\left(S\right)

remain the same or increase by
1
. Utilities of sets without

S(j)

remain unchanged thus proving the claim. This logic can also be extended to
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
and
R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}
. For both these rules, shifting will make

u_{i}\left(S\right)

remain the same or increased by

c^{S}(j)

. Finally, look at
R_{\|\;\|}
. By the definition of

d_{i}\left(S\right)

, shifting

l_{i}(j)

and

h_{i}(j)

closer to

c^{S}(j)

will cause a strict decrease in

d_{i}\left(S\right)

if it was a non-zero value. Else,

d_{i}\left(S\right)

remains the same.
∎

Proposition

11
.

All the four rules
R_{|S|}
,
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
,
R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}
, and
R_{\|\;\|}
are range-converging.

Proof.

First, consider the first three rules. Let
R
be
R_{|S|}
,
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
, or
R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}
. Note that for these rules, the value
\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
decreases as
c^{S}(j)
moves farther from all the costs in
\tau_{j}
(the farther

c^{S}(j)

moves, more is the number of voters for whom

c^{S}(j)

falls out of their acceptable range). We prove the claim by contradiction. Assume that for any project
j\in P
such that
c^{S}(j)\notin\tau_{j}
and
c^{S}(j)\neq c^{S^{\prime}}(j)
,
c^{S}(j)
is closer to
\tau_{j}
than

c^{S^{\prime}}(j)

is. This implies that
\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S^{\prime},j\right)}<\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
. By adding these inequalities for all such
j
, we have
\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S^{\prime}\right)}<\sum_{i\in N}{u_{i}\left(S\right)}
. Thus,
S^{\prime}
cannot be selected by
R
since
S
continues to be feasible under the increased budget. The proof for rule
R_{\|\;\|}
follows similar idea. This is because,
\sum_{i\in N}{d_{i}\left(S,j\right)}
increases as
c^{S}(j)
moves farther from all the costs in
\tau_{j}
(as

c^{S}(j)

falls out of the acceptable range for more voters).
∎

Proposition

.

R_{\widehat{\operatorname{c}(S)}}
is degree-efficient, whereas

,
R_{\operatorname{c}(S)}
, and
R_{\|\;\|}

S

. Consider the set

Proposition

.
