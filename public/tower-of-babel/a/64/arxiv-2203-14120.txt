Abstract
. We give a constructive proof of a global controllability result for an autonomous system of ODEs guided by bounded locally Lipschitz and divergence free (i.e. incompressible) vector field, when the phase space is the whole Euclidean space and the vector field satisfies so-called vanishing mean drift condition. For the case when the ODE is defined over some smooth compact connected Riemannian manifold, we significantly strengthen the assertion of the known controllability theorem in absence of nonholonomic constraints by proving that one can find a control steering the state vector from one given point to another by using the observations of only the state vector, i.e., in other words, by changing slightly the vector field, and such a change can be made small not only in uniform, but also in Lipschitz (i.e. C 1 C^{1} ) topology.



1.
Introduction

Consider the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

(1.1)

\dot{x}=V(x)

where
V
is a globally bounded smooth (or at least locally Lipschitz) vector field in a Euclidean phase space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

divergence free
(or else also called
incompressibility
) condition

\mathrm{div}\,V=0,

where for locally Lipschitz vector fields the divergence operator is defined almost everywhere due to Rademacher’s theorem.
The classical point-to-point controllability problem is that of finding, given two points
p
and
q
in the phase space, the
control function
u=u(t)
steering the state vector
x(\cdot)
from
p
to
q
, i.e.  formally, such that
the solution (trajectory) of the ODE

(1.2)

\dot{x}=V(x)+u(t),

starting at
x(0)=p
satisfies
x(T)=q
for some
T>0
.
Usually the control is required to be small.
The problem has been interpreted in
[
1
]
in the following way:
a fish in an unbounded turbulent ocean (modelled by the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) with the flow velocity field given by
V
is
able to move with its own velocity
u
not exceeding in modulus the given value
\varepsilon>0
.
The assumptions of boundedness and incopressibility of
V
are quite natural in this setting. The control problem termed in these words is that of asking whether the fish can reach any point starting from an arbitrary one. If the answer to this question is positive, the ODE (
1.1
) is called
globally controllable
.

If the phase space of (
1.2
) is a smooth compact manifold instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the answer to the posed question is positive and provided by the known global controllability result (theorem 4.2.7 in
[
2
]
where it is formulated for analytic vector fields
on compact Riemannian manifolds). Nevertheless in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}

the incompressibility condition of
V
is not enough for global controllability to hold as can be seen just taking
V
to be constant vector field with sufficiently large norm.
However, it has been proven in
[
1
]
that if
V
is incompressible and has
vanishing mean drift

(called small mean drift in
[
1
]
) in the sense

(1.3)

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{\ell^{d}}\int_{[0,\ell]^{d}}V(x+y)\,dy\right|=0,

then (
1.1
) is globally controllable (this result has been further extended in
[
3
]
to nonautonomous ODEs).
Roughly speaking, the assumption of vanishing mean drift means that the average value of the flow velocity over big boxes vanishes with the corresponding limit uniform with respect to the selection of those big boxes.

In
[
4
]
we took a completely different approach to the proof of this global controllability result obtaining, as a side product, a version of C. Pugh’s closing lemma for divergence free vector fields on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying vanishing mean drift condition (
1.3
). However, both our proof of the controllability result and the original proof from
[
1
]
are inherently nonconstructive: in other words they assure the fish that it can reach any desired destination but do not give any clue of how to do it. The goal of this paper is to provide a constructive proof of the global controllability result for (
1.1
), namely, providing not only the result itself but an explicit construction of
the steering control
u(\cdot)
. Our basic instruments will be the main result of
[
4
]
which gives a constructive way of changing slightly the vector field so that all the points of the phase space become nonwandering for the corrected vector field.

We also compare the proven controllability result over the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the original classical setting of the global controllability theorem, i.e. with the case when the ODE (
1.1
) is defined over some smooth compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case we significantly strengthen its assertion, by proving that one can find a control
u(\cdot)
in the form
u(t)=W(x(t))
, where
x(\cdot)
is the trajectory of the controlled system (i.e. we may control the system by using the observations of only its state vector), and the vector field
W
is small not only in uniform, but also in Lipschitz (i.e.
C^{1}
) norm.
In other words, we give a constructive proof for the Connecting Lemma for orbits in the case of divergence-free vector fields.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

The Euclidean norm in the finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
will be denoted by
|\cdot|
,

B_{r}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
stands for the open Euclidean ball of radius

r
centered at
x
, and
x\cdot y
stands
for the usual scalar product of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{L}^{d}
stands for tor the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
For any set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\bar{D}
be its closure.
By

\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
(resp.
\mathrm{Lip}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
we denote
the set of
Lipschitz (resp. locally Lipschitz, continuously differentiable)
functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The standard uniform norm of functions and vector functions will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
For a
V\in\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote by
\mathrm{Lip}\,V
its least Lipschitz constant, and set

\|V\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}:=\|V\|_{\infty}+\mathrm{Lip}\,V
. The notation
C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
stands for the class of infinitely differentiable real-valued functions with compact support
in an open
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For a diffeomorphism
f\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to f(U)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open, and a vector field
V
over
U

we denote the push-forward
f_{*}V
of
V
by
f
by the formula

(f_{*}V)(y):=(Df)(f^{-1}(y))V(f^{-1}(y))=(Df^{-1})^{-1}(y)V(f^{-1}(y)),

so that if
x(\cdot)
is a trajectory of the ODE
\dot{x}=V(x)
in
U
, then
y(\cdot):=f(x(\cdot))
is a trajectory of
the ODE
\dot{y}=(f_{*}V)(x)
in
f(U)
. Of course an identical (up to notation) definition can be done for vector fields on smooth manifolds and diffeomorphisms of open subsets of smooth manifolds. For a distribution
u
(in particular, a measure) over
U
we define its pushforward
f_{\#}u
by
f
over
f(U)

as

\langle\varphi,f_{\#}u\rangle:=\langle\varphi\circ f,u\rangle

for every test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\langle\varphi,v\rangle
stands for the action of a distribution

v
on a test function
\varphi
, and
\varphi\circ f
stands for the composition of
\varphi
with
f
, once
f
is sufficiently smooth (e.g. when
u
is a finite Borel measure, then this definition can be extended to
\varphi
just bounded and continuous and
f
just Borel).

3.
Global controllability

The following theorem is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a bounded incompressible vector field
with uniformly continuous first derivatives, and satisfy vanishing mean drift condition (
1.3
). Then for every couple of points
\{p,q\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\varepsilon>0
there is a piecewise continuous function
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
(“control”) with
\|u\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
such that
the trajectory of the ODE (
1.2
)
satisfying
x(0)=p
passes through
q
, i.e.
x(T)=q
for some
T>0
.

The rest of this section will be dedicated to the proof of the above Theorem
3.1
.

The following statement uses a construction from proposition 3.3 of
[
4
]
and is of some independent interest.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x\colon[a,s]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a trajectory of
the ODE

\dot{x}(t)=F(t,x)

over some time interval
[a,s]
, where
F\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded and continuous.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\tau\in(0,s-a)
and a
\rho>0
depending on
\varepsilon
and
\tau
such that
whenever
|x(s)-y|<\rho
, there is a piecewise continuous control
u_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
with
|u_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)|<\varepsilon
different from zero only on
(s-\tau,s]
, for which
there is a solution

x_{\varepsilon}\colon[a,s]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
the ODE

(3.1)

\dot{x}_{\varepsilon}(t)=F(t,x_{\varepsilon}(t))+u_{\varepsilon}(t)

coinciding with
x(\cdot)
over

[a,s-\tau]
and having

x_{\varepsilon}(s)=y
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that in Lemma
3.2
one can choose
\tau
arbitrarily small, but the smaller is
\tau
, the smaller becomes also
\rho
.
In particular, it is easily deduced from the proof that when

|F(t,\bar{z})-F(t,z)|\leq L|\bar{z}-z|

for some
L>0
, then one can take

(3.2)

\tau<\min\left(s-a,\varepsilon/4L,\varepsilon/8L\|F\|_{\infty}\right),\quad\rho=\tau\varepsilon/4.

Proof.

Denote
z:=x(s)
. Given a
\varepsilon>0
, choose a
\delta>0
depending on
\varepsilon
so small that

(3.3)

|F(t,\bar{z})-F(t,z)|<\varepsilon/4,

for all
\bar{z}\in B_{\delta}(z)
and all
t\in[a,s]
, and a
\tau\in(0,s-a)
(depending on
\delta
, hence on
\varepsilon
) so small that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\infty}\tau<\delta/2,

so that in particular

(3.5)

\displaystyle|x(s-\tau)-z|\leq\|F\|_{\infty}\tau<\delta/2.

Denote
\bar{x}(\cdot)
over
[s-\tau,s]
the trajectory
of the ODE

\dot{\bar{x}}(t)=F(t,z).

satisfying

\bar{x}(s-\tau)=x(s-\tau)
.
We get that both

\bar{x}(t)\in B_{\delta}(z)
and
x(t)\in B_{\delta}(z)
for all

t\in[s-\tau,s]
due to (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).
Moreover, one has

(3.6)

|z-\bar{x}(s)|=|x(s)-\bar{x}(s)|<\tau\varepsilon/4

in view of (
3.3
).

Fixed an arbitrary
y\in B_{\rho}(z)
with a
\rho>0
to be chosen later,
we set

\alpha:=(y-\bar{x}(s))/\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

One has

\displaystyle|y-\bar{x}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|y-z|+|z-\bar{x}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|y-z|+\tau\varepsilon/4\quad\mbox{by\penalty\ \eqref{eq_SFcorr1a}}

\displaystyle<\rho+\tau\varepsilon/4,

so that once we choose

\rho:=\tau\varepsilon/4,

we get

|y-\bar{x}(s)|<\tau\varepsilon/2,

and hence

|\alpha|<\varepsilon/2.

Define

x_{\varepsilon}(t)

by

x_{\varepsilon}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}x(t),&t\in[a,s-\tau],\\
\bar{x}(t)+\alpha(t-s+\tau),&t\in(s-\tau,s].\end{array}\right..

Note that
\dot{x}_{\varepsilon}(t)=F(t,z)+\alpha
for
t\in(\tau-s,s)
, and hence
x_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
3.1
) with

u_{\varepsilon}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}0,&t\in[a,s-\tau],\\
F(t,z)-F(t,x_{\varepsilon}(t))+\alpha,&t\in(s-\tau,s].\end{array}\right.

Further, by the choice of
\alpha
one has
x(s)=y
.
Finally,

\displaystyle|u_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha|+|F(t,z)-F(t,x_{\varepsilon}(t))|

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\sup_{\bar{z}\in B_{\delta}(z)}|F(t,\bar{z})-F(t,z)|

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\quad\mbox{by\penalty\ \eqref{eq_SFcorr1}}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon,

which proves the claim.
∎

Then, given an

4.
Case of a compact manifold

It is worth comparing the Theorem
3.1
with the case when the vector field
V
and hence the differential
equation (
1.1
) are defined not on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but rather over some compact smooth connected Riemannian manifold
M
.
In this case we can say more, namely, that one can find a control
u(\cdot)
in the form
u(t)=W(x(t))
, where
x(\cdot)
is the trajectory of the controlled system (i.e. in more control theoretic terminology we may control the system by observing only its state vector), and the vector field
W
is small not only in uniform, but also in Lipschitz (i.e.
C^{1}
) norm.
Here for
C^{1}
vector field
V
on
M
we denote

\|V\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}:=\sup_{x\in M}|V(x))|_{x}+\sup_{\{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{M}}\times M,x\neq y\}}\frac{|P_{\gamma,x,y}V(x)-V(y)|_{y}}{d_{M}(x,y)},

where
d_{M}
stands for the Riemannian distance,
P_{\gamma,x,y}
stands for the canonical parallel transport operator of a vector
in the tangent space
T_{x}M
to the tangent space
T_{y}M
along the geodesics
\gamma
,
|\cdot|_{x}
stands for the norm over
T_{x}M
provided by the metric tensor. Clearly the convergence of vector fields with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
is equivalent to convergence in Whitney
C^{1}
topology once
M
is compact (this can be easily seen e.g. once one uses the rather simple and general equivalent definition of Whitney topologies from
[
6
]
, see also
[
7
, theorem 5.7]
).

.

Let
M
be a
C^{\infty}
smooth compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary,
the vector field
V
over

be
C^{1}
, i.e. be a continuously differentiable section of the tangent bundle

of

satisfying
\mathrm{div}\,V=0
, the divergence being intended with respect to the volume measure of

.
Then for every couple of points
\{p,q\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
C^{1}
vector field

\tilde{V}
on

(4.1)

\|\tilde{V}-V\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}<\varepsilon

and

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for

.
We use first theorem 1.1 from
[

]
to get a

\hat{V}
on

is topologically mixing, i.e. for every couple of open sets

in

starting at a point

and arriving at

.
We use then Lemma
4.2
below with
\hat{V}
instead of
V
and
\varepsilon/2
instead of
\varepsilon

to get a
\tilde{V}
such that the trajectory of (

) satisfying

passes through

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Consider a function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathrm{supp}\,\varphi\in B_{2}(0)
satisfying

\displaystyle 0\leq\varphi(x)\leq 1

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=1

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\bar{B}_{1}(0)$},

and set

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle:=\varphi_{\delta}(x)(x-(y_{0}-x(0)))+(1-\varphi_{\delta}(x))x,\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\delta}(x):=\varphi\left(\frac{x-x(0)}{\delta}\right).

By plugging
x=\Phi(y)
into (
1.1
), we get

\dot{y}=(D\Phi)^{-1}(y(t))V(\Phi(y(t))),

which is (
4.13
) with

\tilde{V}(y):=(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)V(\Phi(y)).

The rest of the proof will be divided in several steps.

Step 1
(preparatory observations).
We first note that

\Phi(x)=x-\varphi_{\delta}(x)(y_{0}-x(0)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle D\Phi

\displaystyle=\mbox{Id}-\nabla\varphi_{\delta}\otimes(y_{0}-x(0)),

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{j}}D\Phi

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\partial_{y_{j}}\varphi_{\delta})\otimes(y_{0}-x(0)),

so that, recalling

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi_{\delta}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}}{\delta},

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\partial_{y_{j}}\varphi_{\delta})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{\infty}}{\delta^{2}},

we get

(4.14)

\displaystyle|y-\Phi(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{3},

(4.15)

\displaystyle|D\Phi(y)|

\displaystyle\geq 1-\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\delta^{2},\quad\mbox{for all $y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\|D\Phi-\mbox{Id}|\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\delta^{2},

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y_{j}}D\Phi\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{\infty}\delta.

In particular, from (
4.15
) we get that when

(4.18)

0\leq\delta<\frac{1}{\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}^{1/2}},

then
|D\Phi(y)|>0
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
|\Phi(y)|\geq|y|-\delta^{3}
, we have that
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a proper map (i.e. preimage of a compact set is precompact), and hence under the condition (
4.18
) it is globally invertible.

Step 2
. Since
\Phi(x)=x
for
x\in B_{2\delta}(x(0))^{c}
, we get that

y(t)\in\theta
when
y(t)\not\in B_{2\delta}(x(0))
. Further, under condition (
4.18
) since
\Phi
is globally invertible,
then for every

one has that
z=y(t)
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e. in other words

y(\cdot)
coincides with
\theta
outside of
B_{2\delta}(x(0))^{c}
.

Step 3
. We claim that
y(0)=y_{0}
, if
\delta<1
. In fact,
x(0)=\Phi(y(0))
. This means that

y(0)\in B_{\delta}(x(0))
, since

y(0)-x(0)=\varphi_{\delta}(y(0))(y_{0}-x(0)),

and hence

|y(0)-x(0)|\leq|y_{0}-x(0)|\leq\delta^{3}<\delta.

But
\Phi(x)=x-(y_{0}-x(0))
for
x\in\bar{B}_{\delta}(x(0))
, and hence

x(0)=\Phi(y(0))=y(0)-(y_{0}-x(0)),

implying the claim.

Step 4
. It remains to prove (
4.12
) for a suitable choice of
\delta<1
.
We prove first

(4.19)

\|V-\tilde{V}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon/2.

To this aim we note

\displaystyle|V(y)-\tilde{V}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|V(y)-V(\Phi(y))|+|V(\Phi(y))-(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)V(\Phi(y))|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}V|y-\Phi(y)|+\|V\|_{\infty}|\mbox{Id}-(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}V|y-\Phi(y)|+\|V\|_{\infty}|(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)||(D\Phi)(y)-\mbox{Id}|.

Plugging (
4.14
), (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) into the above estimate, we get

(4.20)

|V(y)-\tilde{V}(y)|\leq\mathrm{Lip}V\delta^{3}+\|V\|_{\infty}\frac{\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\delta^{2}}{1-\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\delta^{2}},

so that to get (
4.19

so that the right-hand side of (

.

Step 5
. To conclude the proof of (
4.12

\|DV-D\tilde{V}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon/2.

Suppose

We estimate

(4.22)

\displaystyle|DV(y)-D\tilde{V}(y)|\leq

\displaystyle|DV(y)-(D\tilde{V})(\Phi(y))|

\displaystyle+|D((D\Phi)^{-1})(y)\cdot V(\Phi(y))|.

Clearly, when
\delta<1
, we have
x(0)\in B_{1}(y_{0})

Denoting by
\omega(\cdot)
the (nondecreasing) modulus of continuity of

\bar{B}_{1}(y_{0})
, we have

(4.23)

|DV(y)-(D\tilde{V})(\Phi(y))|\leq\omega(|y-\Phi(y)|)\leq\omega(\delta^{3}).

Further, from

\partial_{y_{j}}((D\Phi)^{-1})(y)=-(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)\partial_{y_{j}}(D\Phi)(y)(D\Phi)^{-1}(y)

and (

Plugging (
4.23
) and (
4.24

which implies (
4.21
) and hence, toghether with (

), also (
4.12
), thus concluding the proof.
