Difference Equation for Quintic 3-Fold
Yaoxinog WEN

Abstract
In this paper, we use the Mellin–Barnes–Watson method to relate solutions of a certain type of q q -difference equations at Q = 0 Q=0 and Q = ∞ Q=\infty . We consider two special cases; the first is the q q -difference equation of K K -theoretic I I -function of the quintic, which is degree 25; we use Adams’ method to find the extra 20 solutions at Q = 0 Q=0 . The second special case is a fuchsian case, which is confluent to the differential equation of the cohomological I I -function of the quintic. We compute the connection matrix and study the confluence of the q q -difference structure.

Introduction

Since the 1990s, the development of mirror symmetry has changed how people work on enumerative geometry and has made some surprising predictions in algebraic geometry. Calabi–Yau manifolds are essential in mirror symmetry. Among them, the quintic threefold was the first example for which mirror symmetry was used to make enumerative predictions
[
3
]
.

There are several ways to state mirror symmetry. In Givental’s approach to mirror symmetry, two cohomology valued formal functions play a crucial role, i.e., the so-called
J
-function and
I
-function. The
J
-function, by definition, encodes all the genus zero Gromov–Witten invariants, so it is essential. However, it is pretty hard to obtain an explicit formula. On the other hand, the
I
-function given by the oscillatory integral is computable. In
[
7
]
Givental proved that
I
-function lies on the range of big
J
-function, and up to a change of coordinate, we can obtain
J
-function from
I
-function.

Let
X
be the Fermat quintic, considered as a degree 5 hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, the cohomological
I
-function of
X
is as follows

\displaystyle I^{\rm coh}_{X}(\hbar,{\rm e}^{t})=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{5d}(5H+k\hbar)}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(H+k\hbar)^{5}}{\rm e}^{t\left(\frac{H}{\hbar}+d\right)},

where
H
is the hyperplane class of
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, and
\hbar
is the equivariant parameter, since
H^{4}=0
in the cohomology of
X
, the
I
-function of quintic satisfies the following degree 4 differential equation which is called the Picard–Fuchs equation

\displaystyle\bigg[\bigg(\hbar\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}\bigg)^{4}\!\!-5^{5}{\rm e}^{t}\bigg(\hbar\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}+\frac{1}{5}\hbar\bigg)\bigg(\hbar\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}+\frac{2}{5}\hbar\bigg)\bigg(\hbar\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}+\frac{3}{5}\hbar\bigg)\bigg(\hbar\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}+\frac{4}{5}\hbar\bigg)\bigg]I^{\rm coh}_{X}\big(\hbar,{\rm e}^{t}\big)=0.

Let
Q=5^{5}{\rm e}^{t}
, the above differential equation becomes the following form

\displaystyle\bigg[\bigg(Q\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}Q}\bigg)^{4}\!\!-Q\bigg(Q\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}Q}+\frac{1}{5}\bigg)\bigg(Q\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}Q}+\frac{2}{5}\bigg)\bigg(Q\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}Q}+\frac{3}{5}\bigg)\bigg(Q\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}Q}+\frac{4}{5}\bigg)\bigg]I^{\rm coh}_{X}(\hbar,Q)=0.

(1.1)

The fundamental solutions at
Q=0
are given by the expansion of
I^{\rm coh}_{X}(\hbar,Q)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle I^{\rm coh}_{X}(\hbar,Q)=I_{0}\cdot 1+I_{1}\cdot H+I_{2}\cdot H^{2}+I_{3}\cdot H^{3}\mod\big(H^{4}\big).

More preciously, the coefficients of the
I
-function relative to the cohomology basis give the fundamental solutions of (
1.1
). Moreover, the fundamental solutions at
Q=\infty
are related to FJRW theory
[
4
]
and could be constructed explicitly via the Frobenius method.

Around 2000, Givental
[
8
]
and Lee
[
11
]
introduced the
K
-theoretic Gromov–Witten (GW) invariants, these invariants are defined by replacing cohomological definitions by their
K
-theoretical analogs. The
K
-theoretic
J
-function and
I
-function are also defined and studied; unlike cohomological GW theory, the
K
-theoretic
I
-function satisfies a difference equation instead of a differential equation. For example, let us still consider quintic
X
, denote by
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}
the difference operator shifting
Q^{k}
by
q^{k}Q^{k}
, the
K
-theoretic
I
-function of
X
is as follows

\displaystyle I^{K}_{X}(q,Q)=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{5d}\big(1-P^{5}q^{k}\big)}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-Pq^{k}\big)^{5}}Q^{d},

(1.2)

where
P=\mathcal{O}(-1)
on
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, and

\displaystyle l_{q}(Q)=-Q\frac{\theta_{q}^{\prime}(Q)}{\theta_{q}(Q)}

is the
q
-logarithm function. Here
\theta_{q}(Q)
is the Jacobi’s theta function and the
q
-logarithm function satisfies

\displaystyle q^{Q\partial_{Q}}(l_{q}(Q))=l_{q}(Q)+1.

Since
(1-P)^{5}=0
in
K\big(\mathbb{P}^{4}\big)
, the
K
-theoretic
I
-function of
X
satisfies the following difference equation

\displaystyle\Bigg[\big(1-q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)^{5}-Q\prod_{k=1}^{5}\big(1-q^{5Q\partial_{Q}+k}\big)\Bigg]I^{K}_{X}(q,Q)=0.

(1.3)

It is a degree 25 difference equation but not fuchsian (Definition
2.15
).

The characteristic equation (see (
3.1
) for definition) at
Q=\infty
is

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{-1}x^{5}\big)\big(1-q^{-2}x^{5}\big)\big(1-q^{-3}x^{5}\big)\big(1-q^{-4}x^{5}\big)\big(1-q^{-5}x^{5}\big)=0,

with 25 distinct roots. Using Frobenius method, we obtain 25 solutions (at
Q=\infty
) of (
1.3
) given by

\displaystyle W_{l,m}(1/Q)=e_{q,q^{\frac{l}{5}}\xi^{m}}(1/Q)\sum_{d\geq 0}\frac{\prod_{k=0}^{d-1}\big(1-\xi^{-m}q^{-k-\frac{l}{5}}\big)^{5}}{\prod_{k=0}^{5d-1}\big(1-q^{-k-l}\big)}Q^{-d},

where

\displaystyle\xi^{5}=1,\qquad l=1,\dots,5,\qquad m=0,\dots,4,

and

\displaystyle e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)=\frac{\theta_{q}(Q)}{\theta_{q}(\lambda_{q}Q)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*}).

The function
e_{q,\lambda_{q}}
satisfies the
q
-difference equation
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)=\lambda_{q}e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)
.

The characteristic equation at
Q=0
is

\displaystyle(1-x)^{5}=0,

with 5 multiple roots. One may obtain explicit formulas for solutions via the Frobenius method. However, we could obtain 5 solutions at
Q=0
from
I^{K}_{X}(q,Q)
as mentioned above. We use Adams’ method to obtain the rest solutions.

Proposition 1.1
.

The exceptional
20
solutions of (
1.3
) are given as follows: for each
20
th root of unity
\xi
, we have a solution of form

\displaystyle e_{p,\xi p^{-1/2}}(z)F(z)=e_{p,\xi p^{-1/2}}(z)\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n}z^{n},

where
z=Q^{\frac{1}{20}}
,
p=q^{\frac{1}{20}}
. Let
\sigma_{p}=p^{z\partial_{z}}
, then
F(z)
satisfies

\displaystyle\Big[\big(z-\xi p^{-\frac{9}{2}}\sigma_{p}\big)\big(z-\xi p^{-\frac{7}{2}}\sigma_{p}\big)\big(z-\xi p^{-\frac{5}{2}}\sigma_{p}\big)\big(z-\xi p^{-\frac{3}{2}}\sigma_{p}\big)\big(z-\xi p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{p}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\!\big(z^{5}\!-\!\xi^{5}p^{-\frac{25}{2}}\sigma_{p}^{5}\big)\big(z^{5}\!-\!\xi^{5}p^{-\frac{15}{2}}\sigma_{p}^{5}\big)\big(z^{5}\!-\!\xi^{5}p^{-\frac{5}{2}}\sigma_{p}^{5}\big)\!\big(z^{5}\!-\!\xi^{5}p^{\frac{5}{2}}\sigma_{p}^{5}\big)\big(z^{5}\!-\!\xi^{5}p^{\frac{15}{2}}\sigma_{p}^{5}\big)\Big]F(z)\!=\!0.\!

To the best of the author’s knowledge, the connection matrix is critical in classifying fuchsian difference equations, and very little is known about the non-fuchsian case. Even in the fuchsian case, the connection matrix is hard to obtain if the characteristic equation has multiple roots.

We construct the following
K\big(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\big)
valued
q
-series motived by
[
4
]
to obtain connection matrix

\displaystyle F_{m,n}(Q)=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P{\alpha_{i}};q)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{n}}Q^{d}.

(1.4)

Here we use
q
-Pochhammer symbol notation:

\displaystyle(a;q)_{d}:=(1-a)(1-qa)\cdots\big(1-q^{d-1}a\big)\qquad\text{for}\quad d>0.

Since
(1-P)^{n}=0
in
K\big(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\big)
, then (
1.4
) satisfies the following difference equation

\displaystyle\Bigg[\big(1-q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)^{n}-Q\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-{\alpha_{i}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\Bigg]F_{m,n}(Q)=0\mod\big((1-P)^{n}\big).

(1.5)

Suppose
\alpha_{i}\notin\alpha_{j}q^{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}
then we could find the explicit formula for
m
solutions at
Q=\infty
denoted by
\{W_{k}(1/Q)\}_{k=1}^{m}
and we use Mellin–Barnes–Watson method to related solutions at
Q=0
and
Q=\infty
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
m\geq n
, the
K\big(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\big)
valued
q
-series has the following analytic continuation:

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P{\alpha_{i}};q)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{n}}Q^{d}=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i};q)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)_{\infty}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(q,q,P\alpha_{j}Q,q/(P\alpha_{j}Q);q)_{\infty}}{(P\alpha_{j},q/(P\alpha_{j}),Q,q/Q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P^{l_q(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty} \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P{\alpha_{i}};q)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{n}} Q^d=}{}\times\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q;q\big)^{n}_{\infty}{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,\alpha_{j}}(1/Q)}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}W_{j}(1/Q).

As for applications, if we take
n=5
,
m=25
and
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{25}=\big\{\xi^{l}q^{\frac{k}{5}}\mid k,l=1,2,3,4,5\big\}
, then (
1.4
) becomes (
1.2
). And if we take
n=4
,
m=4
and
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{4}=\big\{q^{\frac{i}{5}}\big\}_{i=1}^{4}
, then (
1.5
) becomes

\displaystyle\Bigg[\big(1-q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)^{4}-Q\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(1-q^{\frac{i}{5}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\Bigg]F(Q)=0.

(1.6)

This difference equation is a lift of the differential equation (
1.1
), i.e., if we let
q\rightarrow 1
, then (
1.6
) becomes (
1.1
). This phenomenon is called

which was studied first by J. Sauloy in 2000
[
15
]
. Under the above specific choice, the formula in Theorem
1.2
becomes

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)_{\infty}^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{\big(q,q,Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}Q,q/\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}Q\big);q\big)_{\infty}}{\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}},q/\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}\big),Q,q/Q;q\big)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P^{l_q(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}} Q^d=}{}\times\frac{\big(q^{-\frac{j}{5}}q;q\big)^{4}_{\infty}{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,q^{j/5}}(1/Q)}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{4}\big(q^{\frac{i-j}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}W_{j}(1/Q),

where
\{W_{j}(1/Q)\}_{j=1}^{4}
are the fundamental solutions at
Q=\infty
. If we expand two sides with respect to
K
-group basis
(1-P)^{k}
,
k=0,1,2,3
, we obtain the connection matrix, for more details, see Section

. Besides, the fundamental solutions of (
1.6
) at
Q=0
and

are confluent to the solutions of (
1.1
), finally, we compute the confluence of the connection matrix.

In this section, we define some basic notions in the theory of

[

.

Notations 2.1
.

Here are some standard notations of general use:

–

Q
and
q
are complex variables and
|q|<1
,
q\neq 0
,

–

\mathbb{C}(\{Q\})
is the field of meromorphic germs at 0, is the quotient field of
\mathbb{C}\{Q\}
,

–

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
is the field of meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
,

–

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*},0)
is the ring of germs at punctured neighborhood of
Q=0
,

–

\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\right)
is the field of meromorphic functions on elliptic curve
\mathbb{E}_{q}=\mathbb{C}^{*}/q^{\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e, the field of elliptic functions.

–

(a;q)_{d}=(1-a)(1-qa)\cdots\big(1-q^{d-1}a\big)
for
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
is the
q
-Pochhammer symbol.

Definition 2.2
.

A difference field is a pair
(K,{\sigma})
, where
K
is a field, and
\sigma
is a field automorphism of
K
.

Example 2.3
.

We will focus on the fields in the above notations,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*},0),

they are all endowed with the
q
-shift operator
\sigma_{q}:=q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\colon f(Q)\mapsto f(qQ)
. Let
K=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
or
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*},0)
. Usually, we denote the field of constants of the difference field
(K,\sigma_{q})
as
K^{\sigma_{q}}
. For example,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\sigma_{q}}=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\sigma_{q}}=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{E}_{q})
. This is the main reason that the modular form such as elliptic function appears naturally in the theory of
q
-difference equation.

2.1
Regular singular
\boldsymbol{q}
-difference equations

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(E_{q})\colon q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
be a
q
-difference system, with
A_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(K)
. We define the solution space of this
q
-difference equation by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sol}(E_{q})=\big\{X_{q}\in K^{n}\mid q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)\big\}.

Remark 2.5
.

From now on, we will focus on the local solutions at
Q=0
, and the results will also hold for
Q=\infty
. The reason why we don’t consider solutions at other singular points is that: if a function
f(Q)
is a solution of a
q
-difference equation
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}f(Q)=a(Q)f(Q)
and has a singularity at some
Q_{0}\neq 0,\infty
, then
f(Q)
has a singularity at any complex number
Q_{0}q^{k}
.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
16
, Theorem 2.3.1, p. 118]
)
.

Let
(E_{q})\colon q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
be a
q
-difference system. Then, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{E}_{q})}\big(\operatorname{Sol}(E_{q})\big)\leq\operatorname{rank}(A_{q}).

Definition 2.7
.

Let
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
be a
q
-difference system. A fundamental solution of this system is an invertible matricial solution
\mathcal{X}_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(K)
such that
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\mathcal{X}_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)\mathcal{X}_{q}(Q)
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
be a
q
-difference system. Consider a matrix
P_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(K)
. The gauge transform of the matrix
A_{q}
by the gauge transformation
P_{q}
is the matrix

\displaystyle P_{q}\cdot[A_{q}]:=\big(q^{Q\partial_{Q}}P_{q}\big)A_{q}P_{q}^{-1}.

A second
q
-difference system
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=B_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
is said to be equivalent (over
K
) by gauge transform to the first one if there exists a matrix
P_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(K)
such that

\displaystyle B_{q}=P_{q}\cdot[A_{q}].

Let us define the regular singular
q
-difference equation. We shall start from the local analytic study, i.e., taking field
\mathbb{C}(\{Q\})
, and then look for solutions in the field
K=\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
or
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*},0)
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
A_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}(\{Q\}))
, a system
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
is said to be regular singular at
Q=0
if there exists a
q
-gauge transform
P_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}(\{Q\}))
such that the matrix
(P_{q}\cdot[A_{q}])(0)
is well-defined and invertible:
P_{q}\cdot[A_{q}](0)\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Definition 2.10
.

Consider a regular singular
q
-difference system
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
. Suppose
A_{q}(0)\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and denote by
(\lambda_{i})
the eigenvalues of the matrix
A_{q}(0)
. This
q
-difference system is said to be non
q
-resonant if for every
i\neq j
, we have
\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}}\notin q^{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}
, where
q^{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}:=\big\{q^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}\big\}\subset\mathbb{C}
.

Let’s introduce some special functions which are needed to solve regular singular
q
-difference equations.

We define Jacobi’s theta function by

\displaystyle\theta_{q}(Q)=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}Q^{d}.

This function satisfies the
q
-difference equation
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\theta_{q}(Q)=\frac{1}{Q}\theta_{q}(Q)
. And it has a famous Jacobi’s triple identity

\displaystyle\theta_{q}(Q)=(q;q)_{\infty}(-Q;q)_{\infty}(-q/Q;q)_{\infty}.

In the following, we define two special functions which are essential in solving regular singular (irregular)
q
-difference equations.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\lambda_{q}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The
q
-character associated to
\lambda
is the function
e_{q,\lambda_{q}}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbb{C}^{*}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)=\frac{\theta_{q}(Q)}{\theta_{q}(\lambda_{q}Q)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*}).

The function
e_{q,\lambda_{q}}
satisfies the
q
-difference equation
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)=\lambda_{q}e_{q,\lambda_{q}}(Q)
.

Definition 2.12
.

The
q
-logarithm is the function
\ell_{q}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbb{C}^{*}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle\ell_{q}(Q)=-Q\frac{\theta_{q}^{\prime}(Q)}{\theta_{q}(Q)}.

By a little computation, one could know that the function
\ell_{q}
satisfies the following
q
-difference equation

\displaystyle q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\ell_{q}(Q)=\ell_{q}(Q)+1.

Now we can state the existence of a fundamental solution for regular singular
q
-difference equations under certain conditions.

For a
q
-difference system
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
, without loss of generality, we assume
A_{q}(0)\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and moreover that it is non-resonant. We can recursively build a gauge transform
F_{q}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}(\{Q\}))
which sends the matrix
A_{q}(0)
to the constant matrix
A_{q}(Q)
, for details, see
[
16
, Corollary 3.2.4]
. Then we take the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition of
A_{q}(0)=A_{s}A_{u}
, where
A_{s}
is semi-simple,
A_{u}
is unipotent and
A_{s}
,
A_{u}
commute.

Since
N=A_{u}-I_{n}
is nilpotent, we can define

\displaystyle A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}:=(I_{n}+N)^{\ell_{q}}:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{\ell_{q}}{k}N^{k},

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle\binom{\ell_{q}}{k}:=\frac{\ell_{q}(\ell_{q}-1)\cdots(\ell_{q}-(k-1))}{k!}.

Note that (
2.1
) is actually a finite sum and
A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}
is unipotent, and we have

\displaystyle q^{Q\partial_{Q}}A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}=A_{u}A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}=A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}A_{u}.

Thus we set

\displaystyle e_{q,A_{u}}:=A_{u}^{\ell_{q}}.

Take a basis change
P
to diagonalise
A_{s}=P^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{i})P
. We define

\displaystyle e_{q,A_{s}}:=P^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(e_{q,\lambda_{i}}(Q))P,

(2.2)

which satisfies

\displaystyle q^{Q\partial_{Q}}e_{q,A_{s}}=A_{s}e_{q,A_{s}}=e_{q,A_{s}}A_{s}.

Then one can check that the product
F_{q}e_{q,A_{s}}e_{q,A_{u}}=:\mathcal{X}_{q}(Q)
is a fundamental solution of the
q
-difference system
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
. We arrive at the following theorem.

Proposition 2.13

(
[
16
, Theorem 3.3.1]
)
.

The
q
-difference system
\sigma_{q}X_{q}(Q)=A_{q}(Q)X_{q}(Q)
, regular singular at
Q=0
, admits a fundamental matricial solution
\mathcal{X}:=Me_{q,C}\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}^{*},0))
, where
C\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and where
M\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{C}(\{Q\}))
. The
e_{q,C}
is defined by Jordan–Chevalley decomposition of
C
as above.

Remark 2.14
.

Let
A,P\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, one can check that

2.2
Monodromy of regular singular
\boldsymbol{q}
-difference equations

Definition 2.15
.

A
q
-difference system

is called fuchsian if it is regular singular both at
Q=0
and
Q=\infty
.

It is easy to see the difference equation (

) is not fuchsian since it is not regular singular at
Q=0
. But we will see it is regular singular at

(see (

)).

Definition 2.16
.

Let

be a fuchsian
q
-difference system. This
q
-difference system admits a fundamental solution
\mathcal{X}_{0}(Q)
at

and a second one
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}(1/Q)
at
Q=\infty
. Birkhoff’s connection matrix (or
q
-monodromy)

is the ratio

\displaystyle M_{q}(Q)=(\mathcal{X}_{\infty}(1/Q))^{-1}\mathcal{X}_{0}(Q).

Since the connection matrix relates two fundamental matrix solutions. It is invariant by difference operator
q^{Q\partial_{Q}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle M_{q}(Q)\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{E}_{q})).

However, it is not well defined: it depends on the choice of fundamental matrix solutions. To get rid of this dependence, we need to consider the following triple.

Definition 2.17
.

A Birkhoff connection triple is a triple

\displaystyle\big(A^{(0)},M_{q},A^{(\infty)}\big)\in{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\times{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{E}_{q})\times{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

up to certain equivalent. Where
A^{(0)}
and
A^{(\infty)}
are related to the fundamental solutions at
Q=0
and
Q=\infty
respectively, for more details, see
[
16
, p. 133]
.

The data of Birkhoff’s connection triples classifies fuchsian
q
-difference systems up to gauge transformations.

Proposition 2.18

(
[
16
, Theorem 3.4.9]
)
.

Rational classes
(
under rational equivalence, i.e., over field
\mathbb{C}(Q))
of fuchsian rational systems are in bijection with equivalence classes of Birkhoff connection triples.

3
The difference equation for quintic

3.1
General technique: Newton polygon

Let’s consider the equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}(Q)(\sigma_{q})^{i}f(Q)=0,

with

\displaystyle a_{i}(Q)=a_{i,0}+a_{i,1}Q+a_{i,2}Q^{2}+\cdots.

We call the following equation the
characteristic equation

\displaystyle a_{n,0}x^{n}+a_{n-1,0}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1,0}x+a_{0,0}=0,

(3.1)

which plays an important role in constructing solutions.

Denote by
a_{i,j_{i}}
the first nonzero coefficient in
a_{i}(Q)
, and choosing
i
- and
j
-axes as horizontal and vertical axes respectively, plot the points
(n-i,j_{i})
. Construct a broken line, convex downward, such that both ends of each segment of the line are points of the set
(n-i,j_{i})
. Then we obtain a Newton polygon as follows

Note that the horizontal segment corresponds to the characteristic equation

\displaystyle a_{k,0}x^{k}+a_{k-1,0}x^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{d,0}x^{d}=0.

The degree of the above characteristic equation is 1 less than the number of points on or above that segment.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the following equation:

\displaystyle\big[\big(Q^{4}+2Q^{7}\big)\sigma_{q}^{6}+\big(Q+3Q^{5}\big)\sigma_{q}^{5}+\big(3+2Q^{3}\big)\sigma_{q}^{4}+2\sigma_{q}^{3}+3Q\sigma_{q}^{2}+Q^{2}\sigma_{q}\big]f(Q)=0.

Then the associated Newton polygon is
(0,4)
(1,1)
(2,0)
(3,0)
(4,0)
(5,2)

The general technique to construct solutions is as follows:

•

Horizontal segment:
As mentioned above, it corresponds to characteristic equation. Using the non-zero roots, we could construct the associated solutions as regular singular cases.

•

Non-horizontal segment:
For each non-horizontal segment of slope
\mu
, a rational number.

–

If
\mu=r
is an integer, we consider a formal series solution of the form

\displaystyle\theta^{r}_{q}(Q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}(q)Q^{n}.

–

If
\mu=t/s
is a rational number with
s
positive, then we consider a formal series solution of the form

\displaystyle\theta_{q^{t/s}}\big(Q^{t/s}\big)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}(q)Q^{n/s}.

For more details, see
[
1
,
2
,
14
]
.

Remark 3.2
.

In Adams’ works, he used
q^{\frac{\mu}{2}(t^{2}-t)}
, where
t=\frac{\ln Q}{\ln q}
.

\displaystyle=e_{q,q^{\frac{l}{5}}\xi^{m}}(1/Q)\frac{\prod_{k=0}^{d-1}\big(1-q^{-\frac{l}{5}}\xi^{-m}q^{-k}\big)^{n}}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-q^{1-l}q^{-5k}\big)\cdots\big(1-q^{5-l}q^{-5k}\big)}Q^{-d}

\displaystyle=e_{q,q^{\frac{l}{5}}\xi^{m}}(1/Q)\sum_{d\geq 0}\frac{\prod_{k=0}^{d-1}\big(1-\xi^{-m}q^{-k-\frac{l}{5}}\big)^{5}}{\prod_{k=0}^{5d-1}\big(1-q^{-k-l}\big)}Q^{-d}

\displaystyle=W_{l,m}(1/Q).

4.2
Analytic continuation

For the sake of simplicity, we shall assume in this section that
0<q<1
and write

\displaystyle q={\rm e}^{-w},\qquad w>0.

The results can be extended to complex
q
in the unit disc using analytic continuation.

Consider the following contour integral. We follow the argument of
[
6
, pp. 115–118]
to show that this integral is well defined. For
|Q|<1
, we can close the contour to the right, it equals to (
4.1
),

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i};q)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)_{\infty}^{n}}\int_{C}\frac{\big(Pq^{s+1};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i}q^{s};q)_{\infty}}\frac{\pi(-Q)^{s}}{\sin\pi s}\frac{{\rm d}s}{-2\pi{\rm i}}.

(4.3)

Here we view
P={\rm e}^{-H}
. Although
H
is the hyperplane class, we consider it as a formal variable valued in
\mathbb{C}
.
C
is a curve from
-{\rm i}\infty
to
+{\rm i}\infty
such that only the non-negative zeros of
\sin\pi s
lie on the right side of
C
.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\big|1-\big|a\big|{\rm e}^{-\omega\operatorname{Re}(s)}\big|\leq|1-aq^{s}|\leq 1+|a|{\rm e}^{-\omega\operatorname{Re}(s)},

we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\big(Pq^{s+1};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i}q^{s};q)_{\infty}}\bigg|\leq\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(1+|P|{\rm e}^{-(k+1+\operatorname{Re}(s)w)}\big)^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-|P\alpha_{i}|{\rm e}^{-(k+\operatorname{Re}(s)w)}\big)},

which is bounded on the contour
C
. Hence the integral (
4.3
) converges if
|\arg(-Q)|<\pi
.

Let
C_{R_{+}}
be a large clockwise-oriented semicircle of radius
R
with a center at the origin that lies to the right of
C
. The semicircle is terminated by
C
and bounded away from the poles. Now consider the contour integral over
C_{R_{+}}
instead of
C
.

Setting
s=R{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}
, we have for
|s|<1
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\bigg[\log\frac{(-Q)^{s}}{\sin\pi s}\bigg]

\displaystyle=R[\cos\theta\log|Q|-\sin\theta\arg(-Q)-\pi|\sin\theta|]+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq-R[\sin\theta\arg(-Q)+\pi|\sin\theta|]+O(1).

Hence, when
|Q|<1
and
|\arg(-Q)|<\pi-\delta
,
0<\delta<\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(-Q)^{s}}{\sin\pi s}=O[\exp(-\delta R|\sin\theta|)],

as
R\rightarrow\infty
, then the integral on
C_{R_{+}}
tends to zero as
R\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, by applying Cauchy’s theorem, we can prove (
4.3
) equals to (
4.1
) through tedious computation.

Similarly, if we replace the contour
C
by a contour
C_{R_{-}}
consisting of a large counterclockwise-oriented semicircle of radius
R
with center at the origin that lies to the left of
C
. From an asymptotic formula

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}[\log(q^{s};q)_{\infty}]=\frac{\omega}{2}(\operatorname{Re}(s))^{2}+\frac{\omega}{2}\operatorname{Re}(s)+O(1),

as
R\rightarrow-\infty
. Without loss of generality, we assume
P=q^{h}
,
\alpha_{i}=q^{a_{i}}
and let
h
,
a_{i}
be real numbers.

Then

\displaystyle n(1+h+\operatorname{Re}(s))^{2}+n(1+h+\operatorname{Re}(s))-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big[(a_{i}+h+\operatorname{Re}(s))^{2}+(a_{i}+h+\operatorname{Re}(s))\big]

\displaystyle\qquad{}=2\bigg[n(1+h)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(a_{i}+h)\bigg]\operatorname{Re}(s)+(n-m)\big(\operatorname{Re}(s)+\operatorname{Re}^{2}(s)\big)+\rm{const}.

Note that
q={\rm e}^{-w}
,
w>0
, then the asymptotic formula for
(q^{s};q)_{\infty}
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\big(Pq^{s+1};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i}q^{s};q)_{\infty}}=O\Bigg(\bigg|q^{\frac{m-n}{2}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}P\alpha_{i}}{(Pq)^{n}}\bigg|^{\operatorname{Re}(s)}\Bigg),

when
\operatorname{Re}(s)\rightarrow-\infty
with s bounded away from the zeros and poles.

Similarly, it can be shown that if
|Q|
is big enough, we can close the contour the left, i.e., the integral (
4.3
) on
C_{R_{-}}
tends to zero as
R\rightarrow-\infty
. Thus, (
4.3
) equals to the sum of residues at

\displaystyle s=w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j}+l\cdot 2\pi{\rm i})-k\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=-1-h,

where
j=1,\dots,m
,
l\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n,h\in\mathbb{N}
. The residue at
s=-1-h
contains a term

\displaystyle(1-P)^{n}

from

\displaystyle\big(Pq^{s+1};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}.

And the residue at
s=w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j}+l\cdot 2\pi{\rm i})-k
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{s=w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j}+l\cdot 2\pi{\rm i})-k}\frac{\big(Pq^{s+1};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i}q^{s};q)_{\infty}}\frac{\pi(-Q)^{s}}{\sin\pi s}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{1-k};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}\big(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j}q^{-k};q\big)_{\infty}}\frac{(q^{k+1};q)_{\infty}}{(q,q;q)_{\infty}}(-1)^{k}q^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}\times\frac{\pi(-Q)^{w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j})-k}w^{-1}}{\sin\pi\big(w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j})-k+w^{-1}2l\pi{\rm i}\big)}\exp\big\{2l\pi{\rm i}w^{-1}\log(-Q)\big\}.

If we sum over
k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{1-k};q\big)_{\infty}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}\big(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j}q^{-k};q\big)_{\infty}}\frac{\big(q^{k+1};q\big)_{\infty}}{(q,q;q)_{\infty}}q^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}Q^{-k}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q;q\big)^{n}_{\infty}}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1};q^{-1}\big)_{k}^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j}q^{-1};q^{-1}\big)_{k}}Q^{-k}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q;q\big)^{n}_{\infty}}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,\alpha_{j}}(1/Q)W_{j}(1/Q).

If we sum over
l
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\big\{2l\pi{\rm i}w^{-1}\log(-Q)\big\}}{\sin(w^{-1}\big({-}H+\log\alpha_{j})\pi+2l\pi^{2}{\rm i}w^{-1}\big)}(-Q)^{w^{-1}(-H+\log\alpha_{j})}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{w(q,q,P\alpha_{j}Q,q/(P\alpha_{j}Q);q)_{\infty}}{\pi(P\alpha_{j},q/(P\alpha_{j}),Q,q/Q;q)_{\infty}},

which comes from the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
6
, equation (4.3.9), p. 119]
)
.

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{csc}\big(\alpha\pi-2m\pi^{2}{\rm i}w^{-1}\big)\operatorname{exp}\big\{2m\pi{\rm i}w^{-1}\operatorname{log}(-Q)\big\}(-Q)^{-\alpha}=\frac{w(q,q,aQ,q/(aQ);q)_{\infty}}{\pi(a,q/a,Q,q/Q;q)_{\infty}},

where
a=q^{\alpha}={\rm e}^{-w\alpha}
.

Summing up the above discussion, we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
\alpha_{i}\notin\alpha_{j}q^{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}
. For
m\geq n
, the
K\big(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\big)
valued
q
-series has the following analytic continuation:

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P{\alpha_{i}};q)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{n}}Q^{d}={}

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(P\alpha_{i};q)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)_{\infty}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(q,q,P\alpha_{j}Q,q/(P\alpha_{j}Q);q)_{\infty}}{(P\alpha_{j},q/(P\alpha_{j}),Q,q/Q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\big(\alpha_{j}^{-1}q;q\big)^{n}_{\infty}\cdot{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,\alpha_{j}}(1/Q)}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{m}(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}W_{j}(1/Q).

for
|Q|<1
and
|\arg(-Q)|<\pi-\delta
,
0<\delta<\pi
.

Remark 4.5
.

In general, the above formula only contains a part of solutions at
Q=0
.

5
A special fuchsian case

Consider the following difference equation:

\displaystyle\Big[\big(1-q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)^{4}-Q\big(1-q^{\frac{1}{5}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\big(1-q^{\frac{2}{5}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\big(1-q^{\frac{3}{5}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\big(1-q^{\frac{4}{5}}q^{Q\partial_{Q}}\big)\Big]F(Q)=0.

(5.1)

By definition, it is fuchsian. One could easily construct the solutions at
Q=\infty
, indeed, let
w=1/Q
, then (
5.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\Big[\big(1-q^{-\frac{1}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{2}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{3}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{4}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)-q^{2}w\big(1-q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)^{4}\Big]G(w)=0.

The characteristic equation of the above difference equation at
w=0
is

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{-\frac{1}{5}}x\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{2}{5}}x\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{3}{5}}x\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{4}{5}}x\big)=0,

with 4 different roots

\displaystyle x=q^{\frac{i}{5}},\qquad i=1,2,3,4.

So the difference equation is non-resonant. By using Frobenius method, we could construct solutions of the form

\displaystyle W_{i}(w)=e_{q,q^{\frac{i}{5}}}(w)\widetilde{W}_{i}(w)=e_{q,q^{\frac{i}{5}}}(w)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g^{i}_{n}w^{n}.

After a short computation one obtain four solutions as follows

\displaystyle W_{1}(1/Q)=e_{q,q^{\frac{1}{5}}}(1/Q){{}_{4}}\phi_{3}\big(q^{\frac{1}{5}},q^{\frac{1}{5}},q^{\frac{1}{5}},q^{\frac{1}{5}};q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{\frac{3}{5}},q^{\frac{2}{5}};q;q^{2}/Q\big),

(5.2)

\displaystyle W_{2}(1/Q)=e_{q,q^{\frac{2}{5}}}(1/Q){{}_{4}}\phi_{3}\big(q^{\frac{2}{5}},q^{\frac{2}{5}},q^{\frac{2}{5}},q^{\frac{2}{5}};q^{\frac{6}{5}},q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{\frac{3}{5}};q;q^{2}/Q\big),

(5.3)

\displaystyle W_{3}(1/Q)=e_{q,q^{\frac{3}{5}}}(1/Q){{}_{4}}\phi_{3}\big(q^{\frac{3}{5}},q^{\frac{3}{5}},q^{\frac{3}{5}},q^{\frac{3}{5}};q^{\frac{7}{5}},q^{\frac{6}{5}},q^{\frac{4}{5}};q;q^{2}/Q\big),

(5.4)

\displaystyle W_{4}(1/Q)=e_{q,q^{\frac{4}{5}}}(1/Q){{}_{4}}\phi_{3}\big(q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{\frac{4}{5}};q^{\frac{8}{5}},q^{\frac{7}{5}},q^{\frac{6}{5}};q;q^{2}/Q\big).

(5.5)

Remark 5.1
.

By a small trick, we could find the formula for
\widetilde{W}_{i}(w)
easily. Note that, for
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)^{k}e_{q,q^{\alpha}}(w)\widetilde{W}(w)=e_{q,q^{\alpha}}(w)\big(1-q^{\alpha}\cdot q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)^{k}\widetilde{W}(w).

For example,
\widetilde{W}_{1}(w)
satisfies the following difference equation

\displaystyle\Big[\big(1-q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{1}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{2}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)\big(1-q^{-\frac{3}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)-q^{2}w\big(1-q^{\frac{1}{5}}q^{w\partial_{w}}\big)^{4}\Big]\widetilde{W}_{1}(w)=0.

From the above explicit form, it’s quite easy to find a
q
-hypergeometric series representation for

.

Let’s consider the solutions of (
5.1
) at
Q=0
, the characteristic equation is as follows

\displaystyle(1-x)^{4}=0.

From the general theorem (see

\displaystyle G_{i}(Q)=l_{q}(Q)G_{i-1}(Q)+g_{i}(Q),\qquad i=2,3,4,

(5.6)

where

(5.7)

is a solution of (

) and

For general

-hypergeometric function

(

\displaystyle{{}_{4}}\phi_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4};b_{1},b_{2},b_{3};q;z)

5.1
Connection matrix

Notice that (
5.1
) is a special case of (
4.2
) with
n=m=4
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=q^{\frac{i}{5}},\qquad i=1,2,3,4,

then

\displaystyle F_{4,4}\big(\big\{q^{\frac{i}{5}}\big\}_{i=1}^{4},Q\big)=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}.

(5.9)

The solutions of difference equation (
5.1
) at
Q=0
are given by the expansion of
F_{4,4}\big(\big\{q^{\frac{i}{5}}\big\}_{i=1}^{4},Q\big)
with respect to
(1-P)^{i}
,
i=0,1,2,3
. From Theorem
4.4
, then we have

Corollary 5.3
.

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}{}

\displaystyle=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)_{\infty}^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{\big(q,q,Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}Q,q/\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}Q\big);q\big)_{\infty}}{}

\displaystyle\times{\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}},q/\big(Pq^{\frac{j}{5}}\big),Q,q/Q;q\big)_{\infty}}\frac{\big(q^{-\frac{j}{5}}q;q\big)^{4}_{\infty}{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,q^{j/5}}(1/Q)}{\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{4}\big(q^{\frac{i-j}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\times W_{j}(1/Q).

(5.10)

Remark 5.4
.

Taking
P=1
in the above formula, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(q^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(q;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}\frac{\big(q^{\frac{1}{5}}\cdots\hat{k}\cdots q^{\frac{4}{5}},q^{1-\frac{k}{5}},q^{1-\frac{k}{5}},q^{1-\frac{k}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}}{\big(q^{\frac{1-k}{5}},\dots\hat{k}\dots,q^{\frac{4-k}{5}},q,q,q;q\big)_{\infty}}\frac{\big(q^{\frac{k}{5}}Q;q^{\frac{5-k}{5}}/Q\big)_{\infty}}{(Q,q/Q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\times{{}_{4}}\phi_{3}\big(q^{\frac{k}{5}},q^{\frac{k}{5}},q^{\frac{k}{5}},q^{\frac{k-1}{5}+1};q^{\frac{k-2}{5}+1},\dots\hat{k}\dots,q^{\frac{k-4}{5}+1};q;q^{2}/Q\big).

It agrees with (
5.8
).

In order to obtain the connection matrix, we need to expand (
5.10
) with respect to
\{(1-P)^{k}\}^{3}_{k=1}
. Notice that

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}=(1-(1-P))^{l_{q}(Q)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\binom{\ell_{q}(Q)}{k}(1-P)^{k},

where

\displaystyle\binom{\ell_{q}(Q)}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{r=0}^{k-1}(\ell_{q}(Q)-r).

Then

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}=\sum_{m=0}^{3}\sum_{a+b=m}(-1)^{a}\binom{\ell_{q}(Q)}{a}X_{b}(q,Q)(1-P)^{m},

where
X_{b}(q,Q)
is the coefficient of

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}=\sum_{b=0}^{3}X_{b}(q,Q)(1-P)^{b}.

(5.11)

Let’s consider the expansion of
(Pq^{\alpha};q)_{\infty}
and
\big(P^{-1}q^{\alpha};q\big)_{\infty}
. By definition

\displaystyle(Pq^{\alpha};q)_{\infty}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(1-Pq^{\alpha+k}\big).

For
(1-Pq^{\alpha+k})
, we have

\displaystyle\big(1-Pq^{\alpha+k}\big)=\big(1-q^{\alpha+k}+q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)\big)=\big(1-q^{\alpha+k}\big)\bigg(1+\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}(1-P)\bigg).

Then

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{\alpha+k}\big)\bigg(1+\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}(1-P)\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(q^{\alpha};q)_{\infty}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[1+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}(1-P)+\sum_{i<j}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2\alpha+i+j}}{(1-q^{\alpha+i})(1-q^{\alpha+j})}(1-P)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+\sum_{i<j<l}^{\infty}\frac{q^{3\alpha+i+j+l}}{(1-q^{\alpha+i})(1-q^{\alpha+j})(1-q^{\alpha+l})}(1-P)^{3}+O\big((1-P)^{4}\big)\Bigg].

Similarly,

\displaystyle\big(1-P^{-1}q^{\alpha+k}\big)={}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-(1-P)}=\big(1-q^{\alpha+k}\big)-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)^{2}

\displaystyle-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)^{3}+O\big((1-P)^{4}\big).

Then

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(\big(1-q^{\alpha+k}\big)-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)^{2}-q^{\alpha+k}(1-P)^{3}+O\big((1-P)^{4}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(q^{\alpha};q)_{\infty}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}(1-P)

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+\Bigg(\sum_{i<j}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2\alpha+i+j}}{(1-q^{\alpha+i})(1-q^{\alpha+j})}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}\Bigg)(1-P)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+\Bigg({-}\sum_{i<j<l}^{\infty}\frac{q^{3\alpha+i+j+l}}{(1-q^{\alpha+i})(1-q^{\alpha+j})(1-q^{\alpha+l})}+2\sum_{i<j}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2\alpha+i+j}}{(1-q^{\alpha+i})(1-q^{\alpha+j})}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\alpha+k}}{1-q^{\alpha+k}}\Bigg)(1-P)^{3}\Bigg]+O\big((1-P)^{4}\big).

In order to simplify the computation, we introduce the following notations

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{xq^{k}}{1-xq^{k}},

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)=\sum_{i<j}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2}q^{i+j}}{(1-xq^{i})(1-xq^{j})},

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)=\sum_{i<j<l}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3}q^{i+j+l}}{(1-xq^{i})(1-xq^{j})(1-xq^{l})}

and

\displaystyle F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=-\sum_{k=1}^{4}f_{1}(x_{k}),

\displaystyle F_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=-\sum_{k=1}^{4}f_{2}(x_{k})+\sum_{i<j}^{4}f_{1}(x_{i})f_{1}(x_{j}),

\displaystyle F_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=-\sum_{k=1}^{4}f_{3}(x_{k})+\sum_{i<j}^{4}(f_{1}(x_{i})f_{2}(x_{j})+f_{2}(x_{i})f_{1}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle\hphantom{F_3(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4) = }{}-\sum_{i<j<l}^{4}f_{1}(x_{i})f_{1}(x_{j})f_{1}(x_{l}).

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{\infty}}{(Pq;q)^{4}_{\infty}}={}

Lemma 6.1
.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}=Q^{H}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(H+\frac{i}{5}\big)_{d}}{(H+1)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}\mod\big(H^{4}\big).

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-Pq^{k}\big)}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-q^{k}\big)}=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(H+k)}{d!},\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{(1-P)^{k}}{(1-q)^{k}}=H^{k},

and

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\binom{\ell_{q}(Q)}{k}(1-P)^{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(q-1)^{k}\binom{\ell_{q}(Q)}{k}\frac{(1-P)^{k}}{(1-q)^{k}}.

Then from Proposition
2.20

∎

The
q
-Gamma function is defined as follows

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(x)=\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{x};q)_{\infty}}(1-q)^{1-x}.

It has a nice property

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\Gamma_{q}(x)=\Gamma(x).

Using

\displaystyle P^{l_{q}(Q)}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(Pq^{\frac{i}{5}};q\big)_{d}}{(Pq;q)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=P^{l_{q}(Q)}\frac{\Gamma_{q}(H+1)^{4}}{\Gamma_{q}\big(H+\frac{1}{5}\big)\Gamma_{q}\big(H+\frac{2}{5}\big)\Gamma_{q}\big(H+\frac{3}{5}\big)\Gamma_{q}\big(H+\frac{4}{5}\big)}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\frac{\Gamma_{q}\big(\frac{1-k}{5}\big)\cdots\hat{k}\cdots\Gamma_{q}\big(\frac{4-k}{5}\big)}{\Gamma_{q}\big(1-\frac{k}{5}\big)^{4}}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}\times\Gamma_{q}\bigg(H+\frac{k}{5}\bigg)\Gamma_{q}\bigg({-}H+1-\frac{k}{5}\bigg)\frac{\theta_{q}\big({-}q^{H+k/5}Q\big)}{\theta_{q}(-Q)}{\rm e}^{-1}_{q,q^{k/5}}(1/Q)W_{k}(1/Q).

(6.1)

\displaystyle Q^{H}\sum_{d\geq 0}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{5d}(5H+k)}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(H+k)^{5}}\big(Q/5^{5}\big)^{d}

\displaystyle Q^{H}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\big(H+\frac{i}{5}\big)_{d}}{(H+1)_{d}^{4}}Q^{d}=Q^{H}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\bigg(\frac{\Gamma\big(H+\frac{i}{5}+d\big)}{\Gamma\big(H+\frac{i}{5}\big)}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\Gamma(H+1)}{\Gamma(H+d+1)}\bigg)^{4}Q^{d}.
