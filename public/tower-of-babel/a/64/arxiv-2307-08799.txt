Decoherence Time Scales and the Hörmander condition
Roman Schubert, Thomas Plastow

Abstract
We consider an open quantum system described by the GKLS equation and we are interested in the onset of decoherence. We are in particulary interested in situations where only some degrees of freedom of the system are coupled to the environment, and we want to understand if, and how fast, the noise travels through the system and eventually affects all degrees of freedom. We find that this can be understood in terms of the Hörmander condition, a condition on the commutators of the Hamiltonian vectorfields of the Lindbald operators and the internal Hamiltonian, which is a condition for hypoellipticity known from the theory of PDE’s. We show that for Gaussian quantum channels this condition leads to a delay in the onset of decoherence and can as well be used to detect decoherence free subsystems.



I
Introduction

We will consider open quantum systems whose time evolution can be described by the GKLS equation
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
.
An open quantum systems is a quantum system which is interacting with an environment, and in many situations when the coupling to the environment is weak and
memory effects in the environment can be neglected, the evolution of the density operator
\hat{\rho}
is subject to the GKLS equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007

\mathrm{i}\hbar\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}=[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}2\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\,\,.

(1)

Here the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is Hermitian and describes the internal dynamics of the system, and the Lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{k}
describe the influence of the environment on the system. Typical examples for Lindblad operators are
(i)
\hat{L}_{k}=\sqrt{\Lambda}\,\hat{q}_{k}
,
k=1,\cdots,d
, where
d
is number of degrees of freedom, which are used if the environment can be modelled by random scatterers, or (ii)
L_{k}=\gamma_{k}^{(-)}\hat{a}_{k}
and

L_{k+d}=\gamma_{k}^{(+)}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}
,
k=1,\cdots,d
, where
\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{k}
are creation and annihilation operators of the
k
’th mode respectively, which are used to model coupling to a heat bath,
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Joos et al. 2003
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007
;
Schlosshauer 2019
.

Decoherence is the suppression of interference effects due to the
influence of the environment,
Joos and Zeh 1985
;
Zurek et al. 1991
;
Joos et al. 2003
;
Hornberger 2009
;
Schlosshauer 2019
;
Zurek 2003
, it is an effect which typically sets in on very short time scales and it is a major obstacle for the practical implementation of
quantum computing, as the superposition principle is the main resource in quantum information. In this paper we will consider decoherence for continuous variable quantum systems, see
Braunstein and van
Loock 2005
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
;
Adesso et al. 2014
, and in particular we will be interested in the situation where the environment is only coupled to some degrees of freedom and the internal dynamics is needed to transport the effect of the noise through the whole system. We will give a general condition
which characterises the situations where decoherence eventually affects the whole system. Furthermore the methods we present can as well be used identify subsystems on which the onset of decoherence is delayed and decoherence free subsystems, related to
Lidar and Birgitta Whaley 2003
;
Lidar 2014
;
Yamamoto 2014
.

The Hilbert space of the systems we study is given by
L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
and we will assume that the operators
\hat{\rho},\hat{H}
and
\hat{L}_{k}
are given as
Weyl-quantisations (see
Dimassi and Sjöstrand 1999
;
Zworski 2012
)
of phase space functions
\rho(x),H(x)
and
L_{k}(x)
, where
x=(p,q)\in\mathds{R}^{n}_{p}\oplus\mathds{R}^{n}_{q}
denote momentum and position in phase space. We will focus on a class of systems for which the time evolution can be solved explicitly, namely we will assume that

H(x)=\frac{1}{2}x\cdot Qx\quad\text{and}\quad L_{k}(x)=x\cdot\Omega^{T}l_{k}

(2)

Here
Q
is symmetric and real valued,
\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}
and
l_{k}\in\mathds{C}^{2n}
, the peculiar parametrization of
L_{k}
with
\Omega
is chosen so that the Hamiltonian vectorfield of
L_{k}
,
X_{L_{K}}=\Omega\nabla_{x}L_{k}
, is given by
l_{k}
. As the examples after equation (
1
) show, this includes typical examples of Lindblad operators used in applications.
In this case the Lindblad equation can be rewritten as an equation for the Wignerfunction of
\hat{\rho}(t)
,
\rho(t,x)
,

\partial_{t}\rho=X_{0}\rho+\nabla X_{0}\,\rho+\frac{\hbar}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2K}X_{k}^{2}\rho\,\,,

(3)

where the vector fields
X_{k}
,
k=0,1,\cdots,2K
are given by

\begin{split}X_{0}=&-(Fx)\cdot\nabla_{x}\\
&+\sum_{k}\operatorname{Im}L_{k}(x)\operatorname{Re}l_{k}\cdot\nabla_{x}-\operatorname{Re}L_{k}(x)\operatorname{Im}l_{k}\cdot\nabla_{x}\,\,,\end{split}

(4)

with
F=\Omega Q
, and for
k=1,2,\cdots,K

X_{k}=\operatorname{Re}l_{k}\cdot\nabla_{x}\,\,,\quad X_{K+k}=\operatorname{Im}l_{k}\cdot\nabla_{x}\,\,.

(5)

The equation (
3
) has a transport part,
X_{0}
, and a diffusive part given by the sum of squares of
X_{k}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,2K
. Such equations occur in the
description of stochastic processes, and this connection is not surprising as we are in a situation where we treat the influence of the environment as noise.
In the theory of stochastic processes it is important to understand under which conditions on the vector fields
X_{0},X_{k}
the solutions to (
3
)
are smooth for
t>0
, even for singular initial conditions, this is a condition which is called hypoellipticity in the theory of Partial Differential Equations,
Bramanti 2014
.
There is a celebrated result by Hörmander
Hörmander 1967
which gives a criterion for hypoellipticity, it is formulated in terms of the commutators of the vector fields

X_{0},X_{k}
,
k=1,2,\cdots
.

Definition I.1
.

Let us consider the subspaces
V_{k}\subset\mathds{R}^{n}_{p}\oplus\mathds{R}^{n}_{q}
,
k=0,1,2,\cdots
, spanned by the
X_{j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots
and iterated commutators with
X_{0}
,

\displaystyle V_{0}:=

\displaystyle\langle X_{j}\,;\,\,j=1,\cdots,2K\rangle

(6)

\displaystyle V_{k}:=

\displaystyle\langle Y,[Y,X_{0}]\,;\,\,Y\in V_{k-1}\rangle\,\,.

(7)

We say that
X_{j}
,
j=0,1,\cdots,K
, satisfy the
Hörmander condition
if for some
k
we have
V_{k}=\mathds{R}^{n}_{p}\oplus\mathds{R}^{n}_{q}
.

See
Bramanti 2014
;
Agrachev et al. 2020
for more information on the geometry behind this condition. The Hörmander condition is in control theory and sub-Riemannian geometry sometimes as well called the bracket condition or the Chow condition.
To explain its meaning let us denote by
\phi_{k}^{t}
the flow generated by
X_{k}
, then we have,
Agrachev et al. 2020
,

\phi_{k}^{-t}\circ\phi_{k^{\prime}}^{-t}\circ\phi_{k}^{t}\circ\phi_{k^{\prime}}^{t}=t^{2}[X_{k},X_{k^{\prime}}]+O(t^{3})\,\,,

(8)

so by combining the flows of the vector fields
X_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,
, we can move in a direction given by a commutator
[X_{k},X_{k^{\prime}}]
.
By iterating this argument one can show that by suitably composition of the flows one can move in the direction of iterated commutators, too. This idea is formalised in the Chow Rashevsky Theorem, see
Bramanti 2014
;
Agrachev et al. 2020
.

In the phase space representation of the Lindblad equation, (
3
), we have transport in the direction of the vector field
X_{0}
and diffusion in the
direction of the vector fields
X_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,2K
, and so in view of the Chow Rashevsky Theorem it is natural to expect that the diffusion will affect all
parts of the system if Hörmander’s condition holds. This is particular interesting in situations where the environment couples only to some degrees of freedom of the system,
and we would like to understand under which conditions decoherence will affect all parts of the system, or only some parts. Hörmander’s condition will give a sufficient condition for
decoherence to spread through the whole system.

In order to illustrate the condition let us take as example a free particle in one-degree of freedom with collisional decoherence, i.e.,
H=\frac{1}{2m}p^{2}
and
L=\sqrt{\Lambda}q
. Then we have

X_{0}=\frac{1}{m}p\partial_{q}\,\,,X_{1}=\sqrt{\Lambda}\partial_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad[X_{1},X_{0}]=\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}}{m}\partial_{q}

(9)

and so
V_{0}=\mathds{R}_{p}\subset\mathds{R}_{p}\oplus\mathds{R}_{q}
and
V_{1}=\mathds{R}_{p}\oplus\mathds{R}_{q}
and the Hörmander condition holds. On the other hand side, if we choose
H=\lambda pq
, a Hamiltonian used for instance as a normal form near an unstable fixed point, and the same Lindblad term
L=\sqrt{\Lambda}q
, then we have

X_{0}=\lambda(p\partial_{p}+q\partial_{q})\,\,,X_{1}=\sqrt{\Lambda}\partial_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad[X_{1},X_{0}]=\lambda X_{1}

(10)

so
V_{0}=\mathds{R}_{p}
and
V_{1}=V_{0}
, and the Hörmander condition does not hold.

In the situation we consider, namely that the internal Hamiltonain is quadratic in
\hat{x}
and the Lindblad operators are linear in
\hat{x}
, the time evolution of the system generated by the GKLS equation is a one parameter semigroup of Gaussian Channels,
\mathcal{V}_{t}
,
Weedbrook et al. 2012
. A Gaussian Channel can be characterised by its action on the characteristic function of a state. Recall that if
\hat{\rho}
is a density operator, then its characteristic function is defined as

\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(\xi):=\operatorname{tr}[\hat{\rho}T(\xi)]\,\,,

(11)

where
T(\xi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\xi\cdot\hat{x}}
. The characteristic function is the Fourier transform of the Wigner function, hence the Wigner function can be computed from the characteristic function by the inverse Fourier transform. Now the action of a Gaussian Channel
\mathcal{V}
on the characteristic function is given by

\chi_{\mathcal{V}\hat{\rho}}(\xi)=\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(R^{T}\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}D(\xi)}

(12)

where
R:\mathds{R}^{2n}\to\mathds{R}^{2n}
is a linear map and
D(\xi)=\xi^{T}D\xi
is a quadratic form in
\xi
,
Holevo and Werner 2001
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
. The map
R
and the quadratic form
D
define the Gaussian Channel uniquely, and they have to satisfy the condition

D+i(\Omega-R\Omega R^{T})\geq 0

(13)

in order to guarantee complete positivity,
Holevo and Werner 2001
. Therefore the solution to the GKLS equation (
3
) is given in terms of the corresponding maps
R_{t}
and quadratic forms
D_{t}
, and our strategy to understand the onset of decoherence is to compute the short time behavior of
D_{t}
, since decoherence holds if
D_{t}>0
.

To demonstrate the presence of decoherence we will look at the evolution of off-diagonal terms in cat states. A cat state is a superposition of two coherent states
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|z_{1}\rangle+|z_{2}\rangle)
, centred at two phase space points
z_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1})
and
z_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})
, respectively. Its density matrix has 4 terms

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\big(|z_{1}\rangle\langle z_{1}|+|z_{2}\rangle\langle z_{2}|+|z_{1}\rangle\langle z_{2}|+|z_{2}\rangle\langle z_{1}|\big)

(14)

and one manifestation of decoherence is the suppression of the off-diagonal terms
|z_{i}\rangle\langle z_{j}|
,
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
, see
Zurek et al. 1991
;
Paz et al. 1993
;
Zurek 2003
. The corresponding characteristic functions are given by

\chi_{|z_{i}\rangle\langle z_{j}|}(\xi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bar{z}_{ij}\cdot\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\hbar}(\xi-\Omega\delta z_{ij})^{2}}

(15)

where
\delta z_{ij}=z_{i}-z_{j}
and
\bar{z}_{ij}=(z_{i}+z_{j})/2
. If we now apply a Gaussian channel to this term we obtain

\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bar{z}_{ij}\cdot R^{T}\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\hbar}[(R^{T}\xi-\Omega\delta z_{ij})^{2}+2\xi\cdot D\xi]}

(16)

so if
\delta z_{ij}\neq 0
and
D>0
then this term will be small, and this is how decoherence manifests itself on the characteristic function.
We will use the Hilbert Schmidt norm,

\lVert\hat{\rho}\rVert_{HS}:=(\operatorname{tr}[\hat{\rho}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}])^{1/2}\,\,,

(17)

as a measure of the size of these terms, this norm is particular convenient since we have

\lVert\hat{\rho}\rVert_{HS}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{n}}\int\lvert\chi(\xi)\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\,\,,

(18)

and
\lVert|z_{1}\rangle\langle z_{2}|\rVert_{HS}=1
.

We can now formulate one of our main results.

Theorem I.2
.

Suppose that
\hat{\rho}_{0}=|z_{1}\rangle\langle z_{2}|
and set
\delta z=z_{1}-z_{2}
, then we have for short times the estimate

\lVert\hat{\rho}_{t}\rVert_{HS}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}[d_{0}(\delta z)t+O(t^{2})]}(1+O(t))

(19)

and if
\Omega\delta z\in V_{j-1}^{\perp}
then the more precise estimate

\lVert\hat{\rho}_{t}\rVert_{HS}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}[d_{j}(\delta z)t^{2j+1}+O(t^{2j+2})]}(1+O(t))

(20)

holds, where for
j=0,1,2,\ldots

d_{j}(\delta z)=\frac{1}{(2j+1)(j!)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert L_{k}(F^{j}\delta z)\rvert^{2}\,\,,

(21)

and
F=\Omega Q
is the Hamiltonian map of
H
.

We will give a slightly more precise formulation in Theorem
III.2
in Section
III
. The proof is based on (
18
) and (
12
), and estimates on
D_{t}
we will develop in section
III
. One can derive similar upper bounds for the Wignerfunction
\rho_{t}(x)
based on
\lvert\rho_{t}(x)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{n}}\int\lvert\chi_{t}(\xi)\rvert\,d\xi
.

For the example (
9
) we find for short times

\lVert\hat{\rho}_{t}\rVert_{HS}\sim\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\Lambda\lvert\delta q\rvert^{2}t}&\delta q\neq 0\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\frac{1}{3m}\Lambda\lvert\delta p\rvert^{2}t^{3}}&\delta q=0\end{cases}

(22)

where
\delta z=(\delta p,\delta q)\neq 0
. We see that the onset of decoherence is delayed if
\delta q=0
, this can be explained by observing that the effects of noise on different regions in
q
are independent of each other, so if
\delta q\neq 0
the relative phase between the two coherent states will become random. But if
\delta q=0
and
\delta p\neq 0
, then the internal dynamics is needed to separate the two coherent states before the noise can randomise the relative phase. This effect was already observed and explained in
Paz et al. 1993
;
Zurek 2003
, and a quantitative estimate appeared in
Joos et al. 2003
.

The plan of the paper is as follows. In Section
II
we recall the explicit formulas for the time evolution and in Section
III
we use the Hörmander condition to derive explicit expression for the onset of decoherence at short times. We want to emphasise that the material in Sections
II
and
III
are based on similar results in
Kupcov 1972
;
Lanconelli and Polidoro 1994
for evolution equations of Fokker-Planck type. Our main contribution is to apply these ideas to the study of decoherence. In Section
IV
we apply our results to some examples to help illuminate their meaning and in Section
V
we summarise our results and indicate some future directions of study.

II
Background

In this section we will recall the explicit form for the time evolution generated by the Lindblad equation in the situation we consider.
With the notation from (
4
) and (
5
) we have

X_{0}\rho=-(Ax)\cdot\nabla\rho\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{2K}X_{k}^{2}\rho=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\rho\,\,,

(23)

where

A=F+N\Omega

(24)

with

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\operatorname{Re}l_{k}\operatorname{Im}l_{k}^{T}-\operatorname{Im}l_{k}\operatorname{Re}l_{k}^{T}\,\,,

(25)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\operatorname{Re}l_{k}\operatorname{Re}l_{k}^{T}+\operatorname{Im}l_{k}\operatorname{Im}l_{k}^{T}\,\,.

(26)

Notice that

\sum_{k}\bar{l}_{k}l_{k}^{T}=M+\mathrm{i}N

(27)

and
M
is symmetric, and
N
is anti-symmetric, respectively.

We can now write down an explicit solution to the Lindblad equation, expressed in terms of the characteristic function of
\hat{\rho}(t)
.

Theorem II.1
.

Set

R_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{tA}\quad\text{and}\quad D_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}R_{s}MR_{s}^{T}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\,,

(28)

and suppose
\rho(t,x)
is a solution to (
3
) with
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x)
, then for
t\geq 0

\chi(t,\xi)=\chi_{0}(R_{t}^{T}\xi)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot D_{t}\xi}\,\,.

(29)

We can rewrite this result for the Wigner function if we assume
D_{t}>0
for
t>0
, then we find by inserting the Fouriertransform that

\rho(t,x)=\int K(t,x,y)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,dy\,,

(30)

where the propagator is given by

K(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{n}\sqrt{\det D_{t}}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}(x-R_{t}y)D_{t}^{-1}(x-R_{t}y)}\,\,.

(31)

This is a classical result for Fokker-Planck type equations. A first special case with a degenerate
M
goes back to Kolmogorov
Kolmogoroff 1934
, the first time the general case appears seems to be
Kupcov 1972
, see as well

Lanconelli and Polidoro 1994
for a more recent study. In the physics literature see
Risken 1989
in the context of Fokker-Planck equations, and
Brodier and Ozorio de
Almeida 2010
specifically for the Lindblad equation.

Proof.

In the case of quadratic
H
and linear
L_{k}
the Lindblad equation (
3
) reduces to

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)=-(Ax)\cdot\nabla\rho(t,x)-\operatorname{tr}A\,\rho(t,x)+\frac{\hbar}{2}\nabla\cdot M\nabla\rho(t,x)\,\,,

(32)

which gives for the characteristic function
\chi(t,\xi)=\int\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}x\cdot\xi}\rho(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x
the equation

\partial_{t}\chi(t,\xi)=(A^{T}\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\chi(t,\xi)-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot M\xi\chi(t,\xi)\,\,.

(33)

If we make an Ansatz
\chi(t,\xi)=\chi_{0}(R_{t}^{T}\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot D_{t}\xi}
, with
R_{t}
and
D_{t}

2n\times 2n
matrices,
D_{t}
symmetric, with
R_{0}=I
and
D_{0}=0
, then the left hand side of
(
33
) is

(\partial_{t}R_{t}^{T}\xi)\cdot(\nabla\chi_{0})(R_{t}^{T}\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot D_{t}\xi}-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot\partial_{t}D_{t}\xi\chi(t,\xi)

(34)

whereas the right hand side gives

\begin{split}(A^{T}\xi)&\cdot(R_{t}\nabla\chi_{0})(R_{t}^{T}\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot D_{t}\xi}\\
&-\frac{1}{\hbar}(A^{T}\xi)\cdot D_{t}\xi\chi(t,\xi)-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\xi\cdot M\xi\chi(t,\xi)\,\,.\end{split}

(35)

With
(A^{T}\xi)\cdot D_{t}\xi=\xi\cdot AD_{t}\xi=\frac{1}{2}\xi\cdot(AD_{t}+D_{t}A^{T})\xi
we obtain the two relations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R_{t}

\displaystyle=AR_{t}

(36)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{t}

\displaystyle=AD_{t}+D_{t}A^{T}+M\,\,,

(37)

the first one is solved by
R_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{tA}
and the second by
D_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}R_{s}MR_{s}^{T}\,\mathrm{d}s
. To see this we observe that
D_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}R_{s}MR_{s}^{T}\,\mathrm{d}s
satisfies

\begin{split}AD_{t}+D_{t}A^{T}+M&=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\big(R(_{s}MR_{s}^{T}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s+M\\
&=R_{t}M_{t}R^{T}=\partial_{t}D_{t}\,\,.\end{split}

(38)

This finishes the proof of (
29
) since the solution to the initial value problem is unique.
∎

In the following we will abuse notation and denote the matrices
D
and
M
and the corresponding quadratic functions
D(\xi)=\xi^{T}D\xi
and
M(\xi)=\xi^{T}M\xi
be the same letter. Using this notation we can write

D_{t}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}M(R_{s}^{T}\xi)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert R_{s}l_{k}\cdot\xi\rvert^{2}\,ds

(39)

where we have used as well that
M(\xi)=\sum_{k}\lvert l_{k}\cdot\xi\rvert^{2}
.

If we insert (
29
) into (
18
) we get a Gaussian integral which we can compute and obtain the following result.

Lemma II.2
.

Suppose
\hat{\rho}_{0}=|z_{1}\rangle\langle z_{2}|
, then the time evolved
\rho_{t}
satisfies

\lVert\hat{\rho}_{t}\rVert_{HS}^{2}=\frac{\lvert\det R_{t}\rvert}{\sqrt{\det(I+2C_{t})}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}(\Omega\delta z)\tilde{C}_{t}\Omega\delta z}\,\,,

(40)

where
\delta z=z_{1}-z_{2}
and

\tilde{C}_{t}=C_{t}(I+2C_{t})^{-1}\,\,,

(41)

with
C_{t}=R_{-t}D_{t}R_{-t}^{T}
.

III
Hörmander Condition

By Lemma
II.2
we know that a system displays decoherence in phase space if, and only if, the matrix
D_{t}
is strictly positive for
t>0
. In this section we will show how this property is related to
the Hörmander condition.

Let us start by evaluating the Hörmander condition for the set of vectorfields

X_{0},X_{1},\cdots X_{2K}

(42)

defined in (
4
) and (
5
) in the form (
23
).

Lemma III.1
.

Let
V_{k}
be the subspaces defined by (
7
), then we have

V_{k}=V_{0}+FV_{0}+F^{2}V_{0}+\cdots+F^{k}V_{0}\subset\mathds{R}^{2n}\,\,,

(43)

for
k=1,2,\cdots
, and the vector fields
X_{0},X_{1},\cdots,X_{2K}
satisfy Hörmander’s condition if and only if there is a
r\leq 2n-1
such that
V_{r}=\mathds{R}^{2n}
.

Proof.

Since constant vector fields commute, we only have to consider
[X_{0},X_{j}]
, and a direct calculation gives for
k=1,2,\cdots,K

that
[X_{0},X_{k}]\rho=(A\operatorname{Re}l_{k})\cdot\nabla\rho
and
[X_{0},X_{K+k}]\rho=(A\operatorname{Im}l_{k})\cdot\nabla\rho
,
which are again constant vector fields, and hence commute among themselves and with the
X_{j}
,
j\geq 1
. So the only nontrivial commutators to consider are
j
-fold commutators with
X_{0}
, for
k=1,\cdots,K
we find

\displaystyle[X_{0},[X_{0},[\cdots,X_{k}]]]

\displaystyle=A^{j}\operatorname{Re}l_{k}\cdot\nabla\,\,,

(44)

\displaystyle[X_{0},[X_{0},[\cdots,Y_{K+k}]]]

\displaystyle=A^{k}\operatorname{Im}l_{k}\cdot\nabla\,\,.

(45)

Hence we find
V_{1}=V_{0}+AV_{0}
,
V_{2}=V_{0}+AV_{0}+A^{2}V_{0}
, and so on. But
A=F+N\Omega
and the image of
N
is contained in
V_{0}
,
hence
AV_{0}\subset V_{0}+FV_{0}
which gives
V_{1}=V_{0}+FV_{0}
, repeating this argument gives
V_{k}=V_{0}+FV_{0}+\cdots F^{k}V_{0}
. Hörmander’s condition is now equivalent to
V_{k}=\mathds{R}^{2n}
for
some
k
. But by the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem
F^{2n+r}
for
r\geq 0
can be expressed as a polynomial in
F
of order
2n-1
, hence
V_{k}=V_{2n-1}
for all
k\geq 2n
.
∎

Notice that
D_{t}(\xi)
is monotonically increasing, i.e., if
t\geq s\geq 0
then
D_{t}(\xi)\geq D_{s}(\xi)\geq 0
, i.e., if it is non-degenerate for
s
, then it is non-degenerate for all
t\geq s
. Therefore we will analyse the Taylor expansion of
D_{t}(\xi)
around
t=0
and check if the first non-zero term is positive. This will then imply positivity of
D_{t}(\xi)
for all
t>0
.

The following result is implicitly contained in
Hörmander 1967
, but the first explicit proof seems to be contained in
Kupcov 1972
, see as well
Lanconelli and Polidoro 1994
.

Theorem III.2
.

The quadratic form
D_{t}(\xi)
is nondgenerate for all
t>0
if, and only if, the
Hörmander condition
V_{2n-1}=\mathds{R}^{2n}
holds.

Furthermore, we have for
\xi\notin V_{0}^{\perp}
that
M(\xi)\neq 0
and

D_{t}(\xi)=M(\xi)t+O(t^{2})\,\,,

(46)

and for any
k\geq 1
with
V_{k-1}^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
that for
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}

D_{t}(\xi)=\frac{M((F^{T})^{k}\xi)}{(2k+1)(k!)^{2}}t^{2k+1}+O(t^{2k+2})\,\,.

(47)

where
M(\xi)=\sum_{m}\lvert L_{m}(\Omega^{T}\xi)\rvert^{2}
.

The strategy of the proof is to study the Taylor expansion of
D_{t}(\xi)
, as a function of
t
around
t=0
.
We will show that for any
\xi\neq 0
the first non-zero term in the Taylor expansion is positive, and this implies by the monotonicity
of
D_{t}(\xi)
that the quadratic form is non-degenerate and positive for all
t>0
.

The first step is provided by the following Lemma.

Lemma III.3
.

We have

D_{0}=0\,\,,

(48)

and for any
j\in\mathds{N}_{0}

\partial_{t}^{j+1}D_{t}|_{t=0}=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\binom{j}{l}A^{j-l}M(A^{T})^{l}\,\,.

(49)

Proof.

We have derived in Theorem
II.1
a formula for
D_{t}
as an integral, this immediately implies that
D_{0}=0
. For the study of the derivatives of
D_{t}
it is easier to use (
37
) and its derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D

\displaystyle=AD+DA^{T}+M\,\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{j+1}D

\displaystyle=A\partial_{t}^{j}D+\partial_{t}^{j}DA^{T}\quad j=0,1,2,\cdots.

(51)

With
D_{0}=0
these relations immediately give
\partial_{t}D_{0}=M
and (
49
) is then easily proved by induction.
∎

The next Lemma provides a technical step to evaluate
\partial_{t}^{j}D_{t}(\xi)
for
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}
.

Lemma III.4
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
, then for
j=0,1,\cdots,k-1

\xi^{T}A^{j}M=0\,\,

(52)

and

\xi^{T}A^{k}M=\xi^{T}F^{k}M\,\,

(53)

Proof.

Notice that

V_{k-1}^{\perp}=V_{0}^{\perp}\cap(FV_{0})^{\perp}\cap\cdots\cap(F^{k-1}V_{0})^{\perp}\,\,,

(54)

and hence
V_{j}^{\perp}\subset V_{k-1}^{\perp}
for
j\leq k-1
. As
\operatorname{range}M=V_{0}
, we find, as in the proof of Lemma
III.1
, that

\operatorname{range}A^{j}M\subset V_{j}
and therefore
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
implies
\xi^{T}A^{j}M=0
.

To prove the second relation we notice that by (
25
) we have
\ker N=V_{0}^{\perp}
and therefore
N\xi=0
. That implies
A^{T}\xi=F^{T}\xi+\Omega N\xi=F\xi

Using that
A^{k}=(F+N\Omega)^{k}
and that
\operatorname{range}(F^{j}N)\subset V_{k-1}
for
j\leq k-1
, which implies
\xi^{T}F^{j}N=0
, we find

\xi^{T}A^{k}=\xi^{T}F^{k}
, as all other terms obtained by expanding
(F+N\Omega)^{k}
are of the form
F^{j}NB
for some
j\leq k-1
, where
B
is product of terms involving
F
and
\Omega N
.
∎

Now we combine the previous two Lemmas.

Lemma III.5
.

(i)

Suppose
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
, then for all
j\leq 2k

\partial_{t}^{j}D_{t}(\xi)|_{t=0}=0

(55)

and

\partial_{t}^{2k+1}D_{t}(\xi)|_{t=0}=\binom{2k}{k}\xi^{T}F^{k}M(F^{T})^{k}\xi\,\,.

(56)

(ii)

Suppose for all
j\leq 2k+1
we have

\partial_{t}^{j}D_{t}(\xi)|_{t=0}=0\,\,,

(57)

then
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Part
(i)
follows directly by combining Lemma
III.3
and Lemma
III.4
.

Part
(ii)
we prove by induction over
k
. Suppose
k=0
, then we have to consider

D_{0}(\xi)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}D_{0}(\xi)=\xi^{T}M\xi\,\,,

(58)

which follow from Lemma
III.3
. And as
\xi^{T}M\xi=\sum_{j}(\xi^{T}\operatorname{Re}l_{j})^{2}+(\xi^{T}\operatorname{Im}l_{j})^{2}
we see that
D_{0}(\xi)=0
implies
\xi\in V_{0}^{\perp}
.

Now suppose the assertion holds for
k-1
, then we have to show that if
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
, then the further conditions
\partial_{t}^{2k}D_{0}(\xi)=\partial_{t}^{2k+1}D_{0}(\xi)=0

imply that
\xi\in(F^{k}V_{0})^{\perp}
, and hence
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}=V_{k-1}^{\perp}\cap(F^{k}V_{0})^{\perp}
. But from Lemma
III.3
and Lemma
III.4
we get
for
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
that
\partial_{t}^{2k}D_{0}(\xi)=0
and from part
(i)
we have for
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
that

\begin{split}\partial_{t}^{2k+1}D_{0}(\xi)&=\binom{2k}{k}\xi^{T}F^{k}M(F^{T})^{k}\xi\\
&=\binom{2k}{k}\sum_{j}(\xi^{T}F^{k}\operatorname{Re}l_{j})^{2}+(\xi^{T}F^{k}\operatorname{Im}l_{j})^{2}\,\,.\end{split}

(59)

Therefore
\partial_{t}^{2k+1}D_{0}(\xi)=0
implies that
\xi\in(F^{k}V_{0})^{\perp}
, and hence
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}
.
∎

Lemma
III.5
is the main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
III.2
which we can now provide.

Proof of Theorem
III.2
.

Suppose Hörmander’s condition holds, and assume there is a
\xi\in\mathds{R}^{2n}
such that
D_{t_{0}}(\xi)=0
for some
t_{0}>0
, and hence by monotonicity

D_{t}(\xi)=0
for all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
. This implies
\partial_{t}^{j}D_{0}(\xi)=0
for all
j\in\mathds{N}
, and hence
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}
for all
k\in\mathds{N}
. But since Hörmander’s condition holds

V_{2n-1}^{\perp}=\{0\}
, and so
\xi=0
. Therefore
D_{t}(\xi)
is non-degenerate, and as
D_{t}(\xi)\geq 0
, it follows that
D_{t}(\xi)>0
for
\xi\neq 0
and
t>0
.

To prove that the non-degeneracy of
D_{t}(\xi)
implies that Hörmander’s condition holds, we show that for all
t>0

V_{2n-1}^{\perp}\subset\ker D_{t}(\xi)\,\,.

(60)

Since by Lemma
III.1
the Hörmander condition is equivalent to
V_{2n-1}^{\perp}=\{0\}
this implies that if the the Hörmander condition does not hold, then

D_{t}(\xi)
cannot be non-degenerate for
t\geq 0
. We recall, as we pointed out in the proof of Lemma
III.1
, that by the Cayley Hamilton Theorem we have

V_{k}=V_{2n-1}
for all
k\geq 2n
, end hence if
\xi\in V_{2n-1}^{\perp}
, then
\xi\in V_{k}^{\perp}
for all
k\in\mathds{N}_{0}
. Part
(i)
of Lemma
III.5
then gives

\partial_{t}^{k}D_{0}(\xi)=0\,\,,\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in N_{0}\,\,,

(61)

and as
D_{t}(\xi)
is by construction an analytic function of
t
this implies that
D_{t}(\xi)=0
for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in V_{2n-1}^{\perp}
.

The formula for the leading order term in the Taylor expansion of
D_{t}(\xi)
follows then directly from part
(i)
of Lemma
III.5
, if
\xi\in V_{k-1}^{\perp}
then

D_{t}(\xi)=\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\partial_{t}^{2k+1}D_{t}(\xi)|_{t=0}+O(t^{2k+2})

(62)

and now we can insert (
56
) to obtain (
46
) and (
47
).

∎

Let us recall the expression for
D_{t}(\xi)=\xi\cdot D_{t}\xi

D_{t}(\xi)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\xi\cdot R_{s}l_{k}\rvert^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\,\,,

(63)

and similarly we have for
C_{t}(\xi)=D_{t}(R_{-}t^{T}\xi)

C_{t}(\xi)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\xi\cdot R_{-s}l_{k}\rvert^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}s\,\,,

(64)

which follows by substituting
s\to t-s
. But if we substitute
s\to-s
in (
64
) we find that

C_{t}(\xi)=-D_{-t}(\xi)\,\,.

(65)

As the leading order terms of
D_{t}(\xi)
for small
t
contain only odd powers of
t

this implies that they agree with the leading order terms of
C_{t}(\xi)
, i.e., we have

C_{t}(\xi)=D_{t}(\xi)(1+O(t))\,\,.

(66)

In the estimates of the rate of decoherence, Lemma
II.2
, contains the quadratic form

\tilde{C}_{t}(\xi)=\xi\cdot C_{t}(I+2C_{t})^{-1}\xi\,\,,

(67)

and the methods from the proof of Theorem
III.2
can be used as well to find the leading order behaviour for small
t
of

\tilde{C}_{t}(\xi)
.

Proposition III.6
.

We have

\tilde{C}_{t}(\xi)=C_{t}(\xi)(1+O(t))

(68)

and in particular if
\xi\in V_{j-1}^{\perp}
then

\tilde{C}_{t}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2j+1)(j!)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert\xi\cdot F^{j}l_{k}\rvert^{2}\,\,t^{2j+1}+O(t^{2j+2})\,\,.

(69)

Proof.

As
C_{t}=O(t)
, we can expand
\tilde{C}_{t}
for small
t
as

\tilde{C}_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}C_{t}^{n+1}\,\,.

(70)

Now (
47
) applied to the
n=0
term in the sum gives the first term in (
69
) and it remains to show that the terms

C_{t}^{n+1}
, are of higher order in
t
for
n=1,2,\cdots
. So let us assume
\xi\in V_{j-1}^{\perp}
, then we have by (
64
) that for any
\eta\in\mathds{R}^{2n}

\eta\cdot C_{t}\xi=\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\eta,R_{-s}l_{k}\rangle\langle\xi,R_{-s}l_{k}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s=O(t

(71)

Now we use that by Lemma
III.4

we have

\langle\xi,R_{-s}l_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-s)^{k}}{k!}\langle\xi,A^{k}l_{k}\rangle=O(s^{j})

(72)

and with
\langle\eta,R_{-s}l_{k}\rangle=O(1)
this gives
\eta\cdot C_{t}\xi=O(t^{j+1})
.
As
\eta
was arbitrary this means that
C_{t}\xi=O(t^{j+1})
and together with
C_{t}=O(t)
we obtain

\xi\cdot C_{t}^{n+1}\xi=(C_{t}\xi)\cdot C_{t}^{n-1}C_{t}\xi=O(t^{2j+1+n})\,\,.

(73)

∎

If we combine Lemma
II.2
and Proposition
III.6
we immediately get Theorem
I.2
since

\frac{\lvert\det R_{t}\rvert}{\sqrt{\det(I+2C_{t})}}=1+O(t)\,\,,

(74)

and

|\Omega\delta z\cdot F^{j}l_{k}|^{2}=\lvert L_{k}(F^{j}\delta z\rvert^{2}\,\,,

(75)

where we used in addition that
\Omega^{T}(F^{T})^{j}\Omega=(-1)^{j}F^{j}
which follows from
F=\Omega Q
and
\Omega^{T}=\Omega^{-1}=-\Omega
.

IV
Examples

In this section we will look at some examples to illustrate our results.
It will be useful to rewrite the nested sequence of subspaces
V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\cdots\subset V_{k}\subset\cdots
introduced in Definition
I.1
. Let us set
W_{0}:=V_{0}
and let
W_{k}
be the orthogonal complement of
V_{k-1}
in
V_{k}
, so that
V_{k}=V_{k-1}\oplus W_{k}
,
then we have

V_{k}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{k}\,\,\quad\text{and for $k\neq j$}\quad W_{k}\perp W_{j}\,\,.

(76)

If the Hörmander condition does not hold, then there is a smallest
r
such that
V_{r+1}=V_{r}
and
V_{r}\neq\mathds{R}^{2n}
, and we define
W_{DF}
to be the orthogonal complement of
V_{r}
in
\mathds{R}^{2n}
, so that

\mathds{R}^{2n}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{r}\oplus W_{DF}\,\,.

(77)

Here
W_{DF}
is the decoherence free subspace, and we can formally include the case that the Hörmander condition holds as we have
W_{DF}=\{0\}
then.

Let us first look a free particle with collisional decoherence, i.e.,
H=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}
and
L=\sqrt{\Lambda}\,\hat{q}
. Then we find
N=0
and
M=\Lambda\,e_{p}e_{p}^{T}
, where
e_{p}=(1,0)
is the unit vector in
p
direction, and
R_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t/m&1\end{pmatrix}
and so using (
64
) we find

D_{t}(\xi)=\Lambda t\xi_{p}^{2}+\frac{\Lambda}{m}t^{2}\xi_{p}\xi_{q}+\frac{\Lambda}{3m^{2}}t^{3}\,\xi_{q}^{2}\,\,.

(78)

We see that for small
t

D_{t}
grows linear in
t
if
\xi_{p}\neq 0
, but if
\xi_{p}=0
and
\xi_{q}\neq 0
then it grows with
t^{3}
, which means it is smaller for small
t
and hence the onset of decoherence is slower. This behavior has been observed in
Paz et al. 1993
;
Zurek 2003
when comparing the suppression of oscillatory terms in the Wignerfunction of a cat-state, as we discussed already at the end of Section
I
.

Now consider a Hamiltonian with potential,
H=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+V(q)
, and
L=\sqrt{\Lambda}\,\hat{q}
, then we find
[X_{0},X_{1}]=\sqrt{\Lambda}/m\,\partial_{q}
, and so in this case the Hörmander condition still holds, independently of the potential. The matrix
R_{t}
will of course depend on the potential, but with
Fe_{p}=(1/m)e_{q}
by Theorem
III.2
the leading order behavior of
D_{t}(\xi)
, and therefore the onset of decoherence, does not depend on the potential
V
.

Let us now look at how the damped harmonic oscillator in one-degree of freedom fits into our scheme, see
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. In that situation we have
L_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma(\bar{n}+1)}\,a
and
L_{2}=\sqrt{\gamma\bar{n}}\,a^{\dagger}
where
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{q}+\mathrm{i}\hat{p})
is the annihilation operator and
a^{\dagger}
the corresponding creation operator. Here
\gamma>0
is a dissipation constant and
\bar{n}=(\mathrm{e}^{\hbar\omega\beta}-1)^{-1}
is related to the temperature of the bath and
\omega
is the frequency of the oscillator
H=\hbar\omega(a^{\dagger}a+1/2)
. Then we find
V_{0}=\mathds{R}^{2}
, so the Hörmander condition is fulfilled without the need for any commutators, as expected, and we find

M=\frac{\gamma}{2}\coth(\hbar\omega\beta/2)I_{2}\,\,,\quad A=\omega\Omega_{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}I_{2}

(79)

and this leads to

R_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t)&-\sin(\omega t)\\
\sin(\omega t)&\cos(\omega t)\end{pmatrix}

(80)

and

D_{t}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t})\coth(\hbar\omega\beta/2)\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}\,\,.

(81)

With
(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t})=\gamma t+O(t^{2})
this matches the prediction from Theorem
III.2
.

We already mentioned the case
H=\lambda\hat{p}\hat{q}
and
L=\sqrt{\Lambda}\,\hat{q}
where the Hörmander condition is not fulfilled. For this case we find

D_{t}(\xi)=\frac{\Lambda}{2\lambda}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda t})\,\xi_{p}^{2}

(82)

and
R_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\end{pmatrix}
, so
D_{t}(\xi)
is degenerate for all
t\geq 0
and hence decoherence does not hold if
\delta q=0
.

We now look at a class of coupled harmonic oscillators with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\sum_{i}^{n}\omega_{i}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+1/2)+\sum_{i\neq j}\delta_{i,j}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})

(83)

where
a_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{q}_{i}+\mathrm{i}\hat{p}_{i})
,
a_{i}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{q}_{i}-\mathrm{i}\hat{p}_{i})
denote the creation and annihilation operates of the
i
’th oscillator, respectively, and the symmetric matrix
\Delta=(\delta_{ij})
, with
\delta_{ii}=0
and
\delta_{ij}\geq 0
, defines the coupling of the oscillators. We will write the phase space variables as

(p_{1},q_{1},p_{2},q_{2},\cdots,p_{n},q_{n})\subset\mathds{R}^{2}_{1}\oplus\mathds{R}_{2}^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathds{R}_{n}^{2}

(84)

and with this splitting we can write the symplectic form as
\Omega=I_{n}\otimes\Omega_{2}
, where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and
\Omega_{2}
is the symplectic form on
\mathds{R}^{2}
. Similarly we have
Q=Q_{n}\otimes I_{2}
where

Q_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\omega_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\omega_{n}\end{pmatrix}+\Delta

(85)

is an
n\times n
matrix. For these examples we choose to couple one oscillator of the system, the
k
’th one, to a thermal bath by choosing

L_{1}^{(k)}=\sqrt{\gamma(\bar{n}_{k}+1)}\,a_{k}
and
L_{2}^{(k)}=\sqrt{\gamma\bar{n}_{k}}\,a_{k}^{\dagger}
, where
\bar{n}_{k}=(\mathrm{e}^{\hbar\omega_{k}\beta}-1)^{-1}
as in the previous example. We are interested how the noise will spread through the system, and if it reaches all parts of it.

The first case we consider is a chain of
n
coupled oscillators with thermal noise coupled to the first oscillator,
k=1
. In this case
\Delta
is tri-diagonal with

\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}\delta&\lvert i-j\rvert=1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(86)

and
V_{0}=\mathds{R}^{2}_{1}
, and we find
V_{k}=\mathds{R}^{2}_{1}\oplus\mathds{R}^{2}_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathds{R}_{k+1}^{2}
, and so
V_{n-1}
is the whole phase space and Hörmander’s condition holds. If
\xi\in\mathds{R}_{k}^{2}
then we have

D_{t}(\xi)=\frac{\gamma\coth(\hbar\omega_{1}\beta)}{2(2k+1)(k!)^{2}}\,\delta^{k}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}\,t^{2k+1}(1+O(t))\,\,,

(87)

so the onset of decoherence is delayed.

In our next example we consider a chain of three harmonic oscillators and couple the thermal noise to the middle one. Then
V_{0}=\mathds{R}_{2}^{2}
and

V_{1}=V_{0}+FV_{0}=\{(x,y,x)\,;\,x,y\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}

(88)

and
V_{2}=V_{1}
, so the Hörmander condition does not hold. In the notation from (
77
) we set
W_{0}=V_{0}
and
W_{1}=\{(x,0,x)\,;\,x\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}
and

W_{DF}:=\{(x,0,-x)\,;\,x\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}
, the
V_{1}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}
and the phase space is decomposed into

W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus W_{DF}

(89)

where
W_{DF}
stands for the Decoherence free susbspace. Notice that

W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
and
W_{DF}
are symplectic subspaces, and that means there exists a proper subsystem which is decoherence free. This subsystem consists of the oscillator one and three states which are anti-symmetric under permutation of oscillator one and three.

Finally we look at the example of a chain of four oscillators and we couple the noise to oscillator two. In this situation we find
W_{0}=V_{0}=\mathds{R}^{2}_{2}
,
W_{2}=\{(x,0,x,0)\,;\,x\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}
,
W_{3}=\mathds{R}_{4}^{2}
and
W_{4}=\{(x,0,-x,0)\,;\,x\in\mathds{R}^{2}\}
so that
V_{1}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}
,
V_{2}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
and
V_{3}=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus W_{3}
is the whole phase space, so the Hörmander condition holds. Notice how
W_{2}
is the same space which appeared in the previous example as the decoherence free subspace
W_{DF}
.

The difference between the two previous examples is the presence of a symmetry which meant that certain states are affected identically by the noise and therefore superposition of such states don’t show decoherence relative to each other. This type of mechanism is a well known as a tool to create decoherence free subspaces and subsystems, see
Lidar et al. 1998
;
Braun et al. 2000
;
Lidar and Birgitta Whaley 2003
;
Lidar 2014
.

V
Summary and Outlook

We considered quantum systems coupled to an environment, and in particular situations where only some degrees of freedom of the system are coupled to the environment. Then the question arises if the influence of the environment will affect eventually the whole system, and how long it takes for noise to start affecting different parts of the system. We showed that for systems described by Gaussian Channels
\mathcal{V}_{t}
generated by the GKLS equation, this questions can be addressed using the so called Hörmander condition, a condition on the commutators of the classical Hamiltonian vectorfields of the internal Hamiltonian and the Lindblad terms.
Our main result is a condition on when the noise will affect eventually all of the system, and the identification of a set of time-scales for the onset of decoherence in different parts of the system

\lVert\mathcal{V}_{t}(|z\rangle\langle z^{\prime}\rangle)\rVert\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}d_{j}(z-z^{\prime})\,t^{2j+1}}\,\,,

(90)

where
j=0,1,2,3,\ldots
.

We then considered a couple of examples which suggested as well that the Hörmander condition can be used to identify decoherence free subsystems and subspaces as described in
Lidar et al. 1998
;
Lidar 2014
;
Yamamoto 2014
. It would be worthwhile to follow this up and explore the decomposition of phase-space into subspaces and how this might translate into a corresponding decomposition of the Quantum system into subsystems and subspaces.

We expect that the results can be extended to more general systems, where the Hamiltonian and the Lindblad operators don’t have to be quadratic or linear functions of
\hat{x}
, respectively, by using the methods developed in
Graefe et al. 2018
. Equations of the form (
3
), the phase space representation of the Lindblad equation, with the assumption that the Hörmander condition holds have been studies extensively in the mathematics literature. One of the main techniques developed is to model these equations on nilpotent Lie groups whose Lie algebra is related to the commutators in the Hörmander condition, and this in turn is related to sub-Riemannian geometry, see
Bramanti 2014
;
Agrachev et al. 2020
. We expect that these tools will prove to be fruitful in the study of decoherence, too.
