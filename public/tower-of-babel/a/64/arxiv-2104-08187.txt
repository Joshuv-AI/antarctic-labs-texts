The Nielsen realization problem for K3 surfaces
Benson Farb, Eduard Looijenga

Abstract
The smooth (resp. metric and complex) Nielsen Realization Problem for K3 surfaces M M asks: when can a finite group G G of mapping classes of M M be realized by a finite group of diffeomorphisms (resp. isometries of a Ricci-flat metric, or automorphisms of a complex structure)? We solve the metric and complex versions of Nielsen Realization, and we solve the smooth version for involutions. Unlike the case of 2 2 -manifolds, some G G are realizable and some are not, and the answer depends on the category of structure preserved. In particular, Dehn twists are not realizable by finite order diffeomorphisms. We introduce a computable invariant 𝐋 G \mathbf{L}_{G} that determines in many cases whether G G is realizable or not, and apply this invariant to construct an S 4 S_{4} action by isometries of some Ricci-flat metric on M M that preserves no complex structure. We also show that the subgroups of Diff ⁡ ( M ) \Diff(M) of a given prime order p p which fix pointwise some positive-definite 3 3 -plane in H 2 ​ ( M , ℝ ) H_{2}(M;\mathbb{R}) and preserve some complex structure on M M form a single conjugacy class in Diff ⁡ ( M ) \Diff(M) (it is known that then p ∈ { 2 , 3 , 5 , 7 } p\in\{2,3,5,7\} ).

Introduction

Let
M
be a closed, oriented, smooth manifold. The (smooth)
mapping class group
of
M
is the group
\Mod(M):=\pi_{0}(\Diff^{+}(M))
of smooth isotopy classes of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of
M
. The
Nielsen Realization Problem
asks if the natural map
\Diff^{+}(M)\to\Mod(M)
has a section over any finite subgroup
G\subset\Mod(M)
; that is, does any finite group of mapping classes arise as a finite group of diffeomorphisms? When there is such a section
we say that
G
is
realized
as a group of diffeomorphisms. Whenever
M
admits a particular kind of structure, there is a corresponding Nielsen realization problem. For example, one can also ask:

Metric Nielsen Realization:
Suppose that
M
admits a distinguished class
\mathcal{C}
of metrics. Given a
finite subgroup
G\subset\Mod(M)
, does there exist a lift of
G
to
\Diff(M)
, and a metric
g\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
G\subseteq{\rm Isom}(M,g)
?

Complex Nielsen Realization:
Given a finite subgroup
G\subset\Mod(M)
, does there exist a lift of
G
to
\Diff(M)
, and a complex structure
J
on
M
, such that
G\subseteq\Aut(M,J)
?

For
M
a closed, oriented surface
\Sigma_{g}
, a positive answer in all categories was given by Fenchel for cyclic
G
and by Kerckhoff in general: any finite subgroup

G\subset\Mod(\Sigma_{g})
is realized by a group of automorphisms of both a complex structure on
\Sigma_{g}
and its associated constant curvature metric.

In this paper we consider the Nielsen Realization Problem for K3 surfaces
M
. These admit both complex structures and Ricci-flat metrics. We solve the metric and complex
Nielsen Realization (Theorem
1.2
) problems; we solve the smooth version for involutions (Theorem
1.8
); and we give partial progress in general. Unlike the case of
2
-manifolds, some
G
are realizable and some are not, and the answer depends on the category of structure preserved. We introduce a computable invariant
\mathbf{L}_{G}
that determines in many cases if
G
is realizable or not. Two other results are: the classification of prime order subgroups of
\Diff(M)
that preserve some complex structure and pointwise fix a positive definite
3
-plane in
H_{2}(M;\mathbb{R})
; and a characterization of “Nikulin type” involutions in
\Diff(M)
.

1.1
A computable invariant

Recall that a
K3 surface
is a closed, simply-connected complex surface that admits a nowhere vanishing holomorphic
2
-form. It is known that any two K3 surfaces are diffeomorphic (see for example
[
BPHV
]
, Cor. 8.6); we call this diffeomorphism type the
K3 manifold
. We fix one such
M
throughout this paper. It is also true that, as Friedman-Morgan have shown (
[
FM
]
, Ch. VII Cor. 3.5), any complex structure on
M
turns it into a K3 surface. The manifold
M
has a natural fundamental class
[M]\in H_{4}(M;\mathbb{Z})
: it is the one for which the signature of
M
is
-16
. So every diffeomorphism of
M
is necessarily orientation-preserving.

It is known that
H_{2}(M):=H_{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{22}
, and that
H_{2}(M)
as a lattice equipped with the symmetric, bilinear intersection pairing (with respect to the above-mentioned orientation)

H_{2}(M)\times H_{2}(M)\to\mathbb{Z},\quad(a,b)\mapsto a\cdot b

is isomorphic to the
K3 lattice

\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}\oplus\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
, where
\mathbf{U}
denotes
the hyperbolic lattice (which has two isotropic generators
e,f
with
e\cdot f=1
), the term
\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)
denotes the
\mathbf{E}_{8}
-lattice whose pairing has been multiplied with
-1
, and
\oplus
stands for the orthogonal direct sum. In particular
H_{2}(M)
has signature type
(3,19)
. We will often write
\mathbf{\Lambda}
for the lattice
H_{2}(M)
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{R}
for
\mathbf{\Lambda}\otimes R
when
R
is an abelian group.
Note that the group
\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda})
of automorphisms of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is an arithmetic lattice in the real semisimple Lie group
\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

The natural action of
\Diff(M)
on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
factors through an orthogonal representation

\rho:\Mod(M)\to\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. According to Donaldson (
[
D-or
]
(3.20) and Definition (3.24)), the tautological
3
-plane bundle over the space
Gr^{+}_{3}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
of positive-definite
3
-planes in

\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
comes with a natural orientation (which we shall call a
spinor orientation
), so that
\rho
will take its values in the
subgroup of
g\in\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda})
that preserve this orientation. This subgroup is of index two in
\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda})
and will be denoted by
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. For example, if
v\in\mathbf{\Lambda}
is such that
v\cdot v=-2
(we will refer to such a
v
as a
(-2)
-vector
in
\mathbf{\Lambda}
), then the orthogonal reflection with respect to
v
is given by
x\in\mathbf{\Lambda}\mapsto x+(v\cdot x)v
and lies in
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. Such a reflection is the image under
\rho
of a “
2
-dimensional Dehn twist” about a
2
-sphere in
M
(see
[
Se
]
for a precise definition of these). Since these reflections generate all of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
, the representation
\rho
has image
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
(see
[
Bo
]
and
[
Ma1
]
).

The theory of complex K3 surfaces produces many finite subgroups
G\subset\Mod(G)
that are realizable by complex (even Kähler) automorphisms. The question is whether or not these are the only realizable finite subgroups
G\subset\Mod(M)
. We now introduce an invariant to help answer this question.

Let
G\subset\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
be a finite subgroup. Since
Gr_{3}^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
is the symmetric space of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
it is nonpositively curved, and so
G
will have a fixed point in this space. In other words,
G
will leave invariant a positive definite
3
-plane
P\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
G
also preserves the orientation of
P
, the representation
\rho
restricts to
a representation
\rho_{P}:G\to\SO(P)
. The image
\rho_{P}(G)
will be conjugate to one of the familiar groups: cyclic (this includes the trivial group), dihedral, tetrahedral, octahedral or icosahedral. Note that only the last three cases yield
an irreducible representation.

Let
\mathbf{I}_{G}\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}

be the sum of the irreducible
\mathbb{R}[G]
-submodules of the type that appear in
P
. As our notation intends to suggest, this subspace (in contrast to
P
) is
canonically associated with
G
.

Definition 1.1

(
The invariant
\mathbf{L}_{G}
).

Define
\mathbf{L}_{G}
to be the following sublattice of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
:

\mathbf{L}_{G}:=\mathbf{I}_{G}^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{\Lambda}.

The sublattice
\mathbf{L}_{G}
is clearly negative definite and
G
-invariant. It is easily computable.

1.2
Metric and complex Nielsen Realization

Siu
[
Siu
]
proved that every complex K3 surface
M
admits a Kähler metric and Yau
[
Yau
]
proved that for every Kähler metric on
M
there is a unique Ricci-flat Kähler metric in its cohomology class. These Ricci-flat metrics play the same role for K3 surfaces that constant curvature metrics play for Riemann surfaces.
If a finite group
G\subset\Diff(M)
preserves some complex structure on
M
then one can take a
G
-average of a Kähler metric and then apply Yau’s theorem to find a
G
-invariant, Ricci-flat Kähler metric. Thus there are basically three Nielsen Realization problems for K3 surfaces, each stronger than the previous: realization by diffeomorphisms of
M
; realization by isometries of some Ricci-flat metric on
M
; and realization by complex automorphism of
M
.

For the complex and Ricci-flat categories there is an associated Teichmüller space, which is particularly nice in the Ricci-flat case. For our purposes the latter case is the most relevant. Let
\Teich(M)
denote the
moduli space of smooth isotopy classes of unit volume, Ricci-flat metrics on
M
; thus two such metrics define the same point of
\Teich(M)
if one is the pullback of the other via a diffeomorphism of
M
isotopic to the identity. The space
\Teich(M)
comes equipped with a natural
\Mod(M)
-action. It also comes with a
period map
: for a (Ricci-flat) Riemann
metric on
M
, its space of self-dual harmonic
2
-forms defines a positive definite
3
-plane in
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{R})
;
its orthogonal complement is negative definite and is defined by the space anti-self-dual harmonic
2
-forms. Identifying
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
via Poincaré duality, this gives a map

\mathscr{P}:\Teich(M)\to Gr^{+}_{3}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}).

Since the codomain of
\mathscr{P}
is the symmetric space for
\Orth(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
, the group
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
acts properly discontinuously on it.

We will need some known properties of
\mathscr{P}
which we recall in more detail in Proposition
2.1
. The image of
\mathscr{P}
is the subset
U\subset Gr^{+}_{3}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
given by the set of all positive-definite
3
-planes not perpendicular to a
(-2)
-vector. This
U
is a simply-connected open subset of
Gr^{+}_{3}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
. The natural action of
\Diff(M)
on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
factors through a representation
\rho:\Mod(M)\to\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
whose kernel
\Tor(M):=\ker(\rho)
is called the
Torelli subgroup
of
\Mod(M)
. The map
\mathscr{P}
is obviously
\Mod(M)
-equivariant, with
\Mod(M)
acting on
Gr^{+}_{3}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}})
via
\rho
. It maps every connected component of
\Teich(M)
homeomorphically onto
U
,
thus making
\Teich(M)
a smooth manifold of dimension
3\cdot 19=57
. If
\Teich(M)_{o}
is a connected component of
\Teich(M)
, then
the
\Mod(M)
-stabilizer
\Mod(M)_{o}
of
\Teich(M)_{o}
maps isomorphically onto
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. Thus
\Mod(M)
acquires the structure of a semi-direct product

\Mod(M)=\Tor(M)\rtimes\Mod(M)_{o}\cong\Tor(M)\rtimes\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})

and gives a corresponding description of
\Teich(M)
as a
\Mod(M)
-manifold:

\Teich(M)\cong\Mod(M)\times_{\Mod(M)_{o}}U.

In particular,
\Tor(M)
permutes simply-transitively the connected components of
\Teich(M)
.

A finite subgroup of
\Diff(M)
that fixes some Ricci-flat metric on
M
always fixes a point of
\Teich(M)
and hence a connected component of

\Teich(M)
. In view of the preceding it is then natural to consider the metric Nielsen Realization problem for finite subgroups of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
, although this is clearly
a priori
not the same as the corresponding problem for finite subgroups of
\Mod(M)
. The following theorem solves this version of the Ricci-flat and complex versions of the Nielsen Realization problem for K3 surfaces. The answer to the homological version is in terms of the invariant
\mathbf{L}_{G}
only.

Theorem 1.2

(
Metric and complex Nielsen Realization
)
.

Let
M
be a K3 manifold and let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
.

1.

G
lifts to the group of isometries of a Ricci-flat metric on
M
if and only if
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no

(-2)
-vectors.

2.

G
lifts to the group of automorphisms of some complex structure on
M
endowed with a Ricci-flat Kähler metric (making it a

Kähler-Einstein
K3 surface) if and only if
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no

(-2)
-vectors and in addition the trivial representation appears in
\mathbf{L}_{G}^{\perp}
. In this case the complex structure can be chosen so that
M
is projective and
G
acts by algebraic automorphisms.

If instead
G
is given rather as a finite subgroup of
\Mod(M)
, then a necessary and sufficient condition for it to lift as in Case 1 (resp. Case 2) is that
G
satisfies the homological conditions stated in that case and also preserves a connected component of the Teichmüller space
\Teich(M)
.

Remark 1.3
.

We do not know whether conversely, a finite subgroup
G\subset\Diff(M)
necessarily preserves a connected component of
\Teich(M)
, or equivalently (by Theorem
1.2
above) a Ricci-flat metric. Perhaps this might be proved by choosing a
G
-invariant metric and then use the Ricci flow to deform it equivariantly into a
G
-invariant
Ricci-flat metric. (As Shing-Tung Yau pointed out to us, it suffices that one ends up with a metric of nonnegative scalar curvature, since for a K3 manifold this implies Ricci-flatness.)

The following result shows that the metric and complex versions of Nielsen Realization are different.

Theorem 1.4

(
Ricci-flat but not complex).

There exists a subgroup
G\subset\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
isomorphic to the alternating group
\mathcal{A}_{4}
that can be realized as a group of isometries of some Ricci-flat metric on
M
, but that preserves no complex structure on
M

contains no
(-2)

\mathbf{L}_{G}^{\perp}

1.3
(Non)realization for cyclic subgroups

Even in the smooth category, and even for involutions, there are obstructions for
G\subset\Mod(M)
to be realized.

Theorem 1.8

(
Smooth Nielsen (non)realization for involutions).

Let
G\subset\Mod(M)
be cyclic of order
2
. Then the nontrivial element of
G
is realized by a smooth involution of
M
if and only if
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no
(-2)
-vectors. In this case the involution preserves some Ricci-flat Kähler structure on
M
.

Remark 1.9
.

Note that Theorem
1.2
gives the ‘if’ part of the last sentence of Theorem
1.8
. The main content of Theorem
1.8
is therefore the ‘only if’ part.

An interesting example to which Theorem
1.8
applies is a
Dehn twist

T_{S}:M\to M
about an embedded
2
-sphere
S\subset M
with
S\cdot S=-2

(this intersection number is independent of the orientation of
S
). It is known (see, e.g.
[
Se
]
, Proposition 1.1) that
T_{S}^{2}
is smoothly isotopic to the identity. However, Theorem
1.8
(or its proof, for the stronger result claimed) implies the following (which we will prove in detail below.)

Corollary 1.10

(
Twists not realizable).

Let
T_{S}\in\Diff(M)
be the Dehn twist about an embedded
2
-sphere
S\subset M
with
S\cdot S=-2
. Although
T_{S}^{2}
is smoothly isotopic to the identity,
T_{S}
is not even topologically isotopic to any finite order diffeomorphism of
M
.

One can also show for example that products of disjoint twists cannot be smoothly realized as finite subgroups of
\Diff(M)
.

Involutions of Nikulin type.
Nikulin showed that the involutions of a
K3
-surface
X
that act as the identity on
H^{2,0}(X)
are all alike: they have eight fixed points, so that the orbit space will have eight-double points, and resolving these double points produces a
K3
-surface. The K3 surfaces endowed with such an involution make up a single connected family. As Morrison
[
Mor
]
observed, the action of the involution on their second homology is equivalent to the involution of the
K3
-lattice
\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}\oplus\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
that exchanges the last two summands. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.11
.

An element of order
2
in
\Diff(M)
is
topologically
resp.
differentiably of Nikulin type
if
f
has eight fixed points points and the orbit space is, after a simple resolution
of the resulting
8
quotient singularities, a closed
4
-manifold homeomorphic resp. diffeomorphic to a
K3
-surface.

The main ingredient in our proof of Theorem
1.8
is the following theorem, which characterizes involutions of Nikulin type in terms of purely homological data. We remind the reader that any finite order
diffeomorphism leaves invariant a positive
3
-plane in
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 1.12

(
Involutions of Nikulin type).

A smooth involution of the
K3
manifold
M
that fixes pointwise some positive
3
-plane in
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
is topologically of Nikulin type and acts on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
as a Nikulin involution.

p\geq 3

Unlike for the

1.4
Concluding remarks

We state here a basic result regarding the finite subgroups of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
, which although not used in this paper, might be helpful for what follows. Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. The reflections with respect to
(-2)
-vectors in the negative definite lattice
\mathbf{L}_{G}
generate a finite Coxeter subgroup
W\subset\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. If
C
is a connected component of the complement of the union of the reflection hyperplanes in
\mathbf{L}_{G}\otimes\mathbb{R}
with respect to the
(-2)
-reflections in
W
(a
chamber
), then
G=G_{C}\rtimes(W\cap G)
, where
G_{C}
denotes the
G
-stabilizer of
C
. The Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces implies that there exists a complex structure
J
on
M
such that
G_{C}
is realized as a group of automorphisms of
(M,J)
. Although Theorem
1.10
shows that any
(-2)
-reflection in
W
cannot be realized as a smooth involution, it is possible for
W\cap G
to be nontrivial without containing such elements and so we cannot exclude the possibility that
G
lifts to
\Diff(M)
, even when
W\cap G\not=\{1\}
.

Methods.
The methods used in this paper are varied. To constrain fixed sets we use the theory of finite group actions on manifolds
(e.g. the Hirzebruch
G
-signature theorem and Smith theory by way of the work of Edmonds), and an adjunction formula of Kronheimer-Mrowka
coming out of Seiberg-Witten theory. We analyze
\Mod(M)
via its action on an appropriate Teichmüller space, using a differential-geometric version of the Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces. Finally, much of the work in this paper involves lattice theory.

proved that the surjection

does not split over any finite index subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\Mod(M)
. Corollary
1.10
implies
this theorem when
\Gamma=\Mod(M)
; indeed it gives that
\pi
does not even
split over certain order
2
subgroups of
\Mod(M)
. We will see below, however, that
this corollary can also be derived from a theorem of Bryan
[
Bry
]
. We also mention that Baraglia and Konno
[
BK
]
recently
constructed a topological involution of
M
which is not conjugate to a differentiable involution.

Since all finite group actions on a K3 manifold that we are aware of fix a Ricci-flat metric we are led to ask:

Question 1.13
.

Does every finite subgroup of
\Diff(M)
leave invariant a Ricci-flat metric?

Another “question” (which we do not wish to advertise as explicitly as the one above, because it is too vague) is the following. As we indicated, the finite subgroups of
\Diff(M)
that fix a positive-definite
3
-plane in
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
pointwise correspond to what in the
K3 community are called symplectic automorphism groups.
A well-known theorem of Mukai
[
Mu
]
characterizes the isomorphism classes of such groups as the subgroups of the Mathieu group
M_{24}
subject to certain properties (
M_{24}
is a subgroup of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{24}
and the subgroups
G\subset M_{24}
in question
are those that fix an element and have at least
5
orbits). Since a clear conceptual link between these two classes of finite groups has not
been found yet, one may wonder whether the passage to
\Mod(M)
or
\Diff(M)
can shed some light on this.

Question 1.14
.

The subgroup of
\Mod(M)
generated by the Dehn twists defined by smoothly embedded
(-2)
-spheres in
M
generate a normal subgroup

\Dehn(M)\subset\Mod(M)
. It is not hard to see that

\Dehn(M)
contains the stabilizer of every connected component of
\Teich(M)
. This leads us to ask: what is
\Tor(M)\cap\Dehn(M)
?
Note that if that intersection is trivial, then we have a direct product decomposition
\Mod(M)=\Tor(M)\times\Dehn(M)
with
\rho
mapping

\Dehn(M)
isomorphically onto
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. But it could well be that
\Dehn(M)
is of finite index in
\Mod(M)
, if not all of
\Mod(M)
just as for closed, connected, oriented 2-manifolds.

2
Metric and complex Nielsen Realization

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem

and Theorem
1.4
.

Notation.

By a
lattice
we shall always mean a free abelian group
\mathbf{L}
of finite rank endowed with symmetric bilinear form

(v,v^{\prime})\in\mathbf{L}\times\mathbf{L}\mapsto v\cdot v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
. In this context a direct sum is always understood as an orthogonal direct sum.

Unless mentioned otherwise, we will assume that a lattice
\mathbf{L}
is nondegenerate, so that the associated map from
\mathbf{L}
to its
dual
\mathbf{L}^{\vee}:=\Hom(\mathbf{L},\mathbb{Z})
is injective. We then identify
\mathbf{L}^{\vee}
with the set of
x\in\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathbf{L}
for which
x\cdot v\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
v\in\mathbf{L}
.
The
discriminant group

\mathbf{L}^{\vee}/\mathbf{L}
is finite and inherits from
\mathbf{L}
a symmetric bilinear form that takes values in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
.
In case the form is even, so that it comes from a quadratic form (namely
v\in\mathbf{L}\to\frac{1}{2}v\cdot v\in\mathbb{Z}
) and the discriminant group inherits such a form
as well (again taking values in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
. If
n
is a nonzero integer, then we denote by
\mathbf{L}(n)
the lattice whose underlying group is
\mathbf{L}
, but
for which the form has been multiplied by
n
. So
\mathbf{L}(n)^{\vee}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{L}^{\vee}
.

We make the notational distinction between the type of a root system of equal root length
and the lattice it generates (for which all roots have square length
2
) by in the latter case using a bold font. For example,
\mathbf{E}_{8}
is an even unimodular positive definite lattice of rank
8
and
its vectors
v
with
v\cdot v=2
make up a root system of type
E_{8}
.

2.1
The Torelli Theorem for K3 surfaces

Before we begin the proof of Theorem
1.2
proper, we review the various formulations of the Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces (see for example
[
Lo
]
).
Suppose
M
is endowed with a complex structure
J
(so that by
[
FM
]
it becomes a K3 surface). It is known that the automorphism group
\Aut(M,J)
acts faithfully on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and so can be considered as a subgroup of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
. The complex structure gives rise to
a Hodge decomposition of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
with
\dim H^{2,0}(M,J)=1
, and basic Hodge theory tells us that this complex line is isotropic
for the complex-bilinear extension of the intersection pairing and positive definite for the hermitian extension. This implies
that
H^{2,0}(M,J)
maps under the projection
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
(taking the real part) onto a positive-definite
2
-plane
\Pi_{J}\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
. This
2
-plane
\Pi_{J}
inherits a natural orientation from
H^{2,0}(M,J)
, and as such it is easily seen to be a complete invariant of the Hodge structure.

The Torelli theorem states that more is true:

\Pi_{J}
determines
J
up to a diffeomorphism that fixes
\Pi_{J}
pointwise. It also tells us that every oriented, positive-definite
2
-plane
\Pi
so arises.
In other words, such a
\Pi
determines a complex structure
J
up to diffeomorphism that fixes
\Pi
pointwise. However that diffeomorphism may act nontrivially on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
. Indeed,
\Aut(M,J)
can be a proper subgroup of the
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
-stabilizer
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\Pi}
of
\Pi
.

In order to state a more precise result, first note that
the orthogonal complement
\Pi^{\perp}\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
has hyperbolic signature
(1,19)
, so the set of
x\in\Pi^{\perp}
with
x\cdot x>0
consists of a pair of antipodal cones. The spinor orientation and the given orientation of
\Pi
single out one of them, denoted
C_{\Pi}
, to which we will refer as the
positive cone
.
The subgroup
W_{\Pi}\subset\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\Pi}
generated by the orthogonal reflections in the
(-2)
-vectors in
\mathbf{\Lambda}\cap\Pi^{\perp}
preserves
C_{\Pi}
and acts on it as a Coxeter group. The collection of reflection hyperplanes is locally finite on
C_{\Pi}
and decomposes
C_{\Pi}
into faces.
The group
W_{\Pi}
acts simply transitively of the set of open faces. We refer to these as
\Pi
-chambers
.

One way to phrase a strong form of the Torelli theorem is the assertion that for every
\Pi
-chamber
K\subset C_{\Pi}
there exists
a complex structure
J
for which
\Pi_{J}=\Pi
and
K
is the set of classes of Kähler forms relative to
J
. This complex structure is indeed unique up to a diffeomorphism that acts trivially on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
. Moreover,
\Aut(M,J)
maps isomorphically onto the
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
-stabilizer of the pair
(\Pi,K)
.
In particular, to give a finite subgroup of
\Aut(M,J)
is to give a finite subgroup
G
of
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{\Pi,K}
. Since
K
is a convex (open) cone, this is equivalent to asking that
G
has fixed point
\kappa\in K
, which is then the class of a Kähler form.

There is a further refinement of the above that involves the twistor construction and that is relevant here. According to Yau’s solution of the Calabi conjecture,
\kappa

is representable by a unique Kähler form for which the associated metric
s
is Ricci-flat
(so that we have a Ricci-flat Kähler metric). As a consequence,
G
will fix this Ricci-flat Kähler metric. The harmonic
2
-forms that are self-dual for the
\star
-operator define the
3
-dimensional positive-definite space

P=\Pi+\mathbb{R}\kappa
. Note that by construction,
P
is not perpendicular to any
(-2)
-vector. The twistor construction (see for example
[
HKLR
]
) shows that the
\SO(P)
-orbit of the pair
(\Pi,\kappa)
defines a family of Ricci-flat Kähler structures on
M
for which
s
is the associated metric. This enables us to state the Torelli theorem in differential-geometric terms: For the purposes of reference we formulate this as a proposition.

Proposition 2.1

(
The
3
-plane of self-dual harmonic forms).

Given any Ricci-flat metric
s
on
M
for which
M
has unit volume, let
P_{s}
be the positive-definite
3
-plane of harmonic (in the
s
metric) self-dual
2
-forms.
Then :

1.

P_{s}
is not perpendicular to any
(-2)
-vector.

2.

P_{s}
determines
s
on
M
up to an element of the Torelli group.

3.

Any positive-definite
3
-plane
P\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
that is not perpendicular to a
(-2)
-vector so
arises.

For a proof of Proposition
2.1
, see for example
[
Lo
]
. The following is a corollary of the twistor construction.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
s
be a Ricci-flat metric on
M
and let
P_{s}\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the positive definite
3
-plane defined by its harmonic self-dual
2
-forms.
Then the isometry group of
(M,s)
is finite and its representation on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
identifies this group with the
\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
-stabilizer of
P_{s}
.

Proof.

Suppose
g\in\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
leaves
P_{s}
invariant. Then
g
must have finite order. It acts on
P_{s}
as an element of
\SO(P_{s})
and so
P_{s}^{g}\not=\{0\}
. For each
\kappa\in P_{s}^{g}\smallsetminus\{0\}
the natural orientation on
P_{s}
induces a natural orientation on
\Pi_{\kappa}:=\kappa^{\perp}\cap P_{s}
. By the twistor construction,
\Pi_{\kappa}
defines a unique complex structure
J_{\kappa}
on
M
for which
\Pi_{J_{\kappa}}=\Pi_{\kappa}
as oriented planes and for which
s
is a Kähler metric.

The Torelli theorem then asserts
that
g
has a unique representative
g_{\kappa}\in\Diff(M)
that preserves both
J_{\kappa}
and
s
(or equivalently,
\kappa
and
s
). Since the isometry group of
(M,s)
is discrete,

g_{\kappa}
is independent of the connected component of
\kappa
in
P_{s}^{g}\smallsetminus\{0\}
. If
P_{s}^{g}\smallsetminus\{0\}
is not connected then it is a punctured line
and
\kappa\mapsto-\kappa
exchanges its components. This has the effect of changing the orientation of
\Pi_{\kappa}
and hence replaces
the complex structure
J_{\kappa}
by its conjugate
\bar{J}_{\kappa}
. This will not affect
g_{\kappa}
(for
g_{\kappa}
also preserves
\bar{J}_{\kappa}
) and so there is a unique lift
\tilde{g}
of
g
to an isometry of
(M,s)
. Hence
g\mapsto\tilde{g}
defines a group homomorphism that is a section of the
map
\Aut(M,s)\to\Orth^{+}(\mathbf{\Lambda})_{[P_{s}]}
. The uniqueness assertion implies that the latter is injective.
∎

2.2
Completing the proofs of metric and complex Nielsen realization

With the above in hand we can complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We prove the nontrivial directions only (in the mapping class version we assume that
G
fixes a connected component of
\Teich(M)
). It then remains to prove that if
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no
(-2)
-vectors then
G
is realized as a group of isometries of a Ricci-flat metric. The other properties already follow from our discussion above.

In the Grassmannian of
G
-invariant
3
-planes
P\subset\mathbf{L}_{G}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{R}
, those
with the property that the rational hull of
P
is
\mathbf{L}_{G}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{R}
(or equivalently, for which
P^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{L}_{G}
) are dense. Hence we can find a
G
-invariant, positive-definite
3
-plane

P\subset\mathbf{L}^{\perp}_{G}\otimes\mathbb{R}
for which
P^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{L}_{G}
. In that case
P^{\perp}
contains no
(-2)
-vectors. By the differential-geometric version of the Torelli theorem, there exists then a Ricci-flat
metric
s
on
M
which in the mapping class version can be taken to be in the component of
\Teich(M)
fixed by
G
such that
P
is the space defined by the self-dual harmonic
2
-forms. Corollary
2.2
implies that
G
then lifts to a group of isometries of
(M,s)
.
∎

If
G
acts irreducibly on
P
, and if
P^{\perp}
does not contain any
(-2)
-vector, then
G
preserves a Ricci-flat metric without necessarily preserving a complex structure on
M
. This explains why we put the emphasis on the
differential-geometric version of the Torelli theorem. Theorem
1.4
, which we now prove, gives an example that shows that this possibility actually occurs.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We think of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{4}
as the Weyl group of a root system
R
of type
A_{3}
. It preserves the root lattice
\mathbf{A}_{3}
, a free abelian group of rank
3
.
\mathcal{S}_{4}
acts on the dual
\mathbf{A}_{3}^{\vee}
by means of the contragredient representation.
Since the reflection representation of
\mathcal{S}_{4}
is self-dual over
\mathbb{Q}
, these two representations are equivalent when tensored with
\mathbb{Q}
.
The direct sum
\mathbf{A}_{3}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{3}^{\vee}
comes with a natural quadratic form defined by
f(x,\xi):=\xi(x)
. This turns
\mathbf{A}_{3}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{3}^{\vee}

into a lattice isomorphic to
\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}
and thus produces a representation of the alternating group
\mathcal{A}_{4}
on
\Orth(\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3})
.

After tensoring with
\mathbb{R}
, this representation becomes a direct sum of two copies of the reflection representation, and so

\mathcal{S}_{4}
preserves a positive-definite
3
-plane
P\subset\mathbf{U}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\oplus 3}
. The subgroup
\mathcal{A}_{4}\subset\mathcal{S}_{4}
will act on
P
in an orientation-preserving manner. In order to extend this action to the
K3
lattice

\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}\oplus\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
, note that there exist two perpendicular copies of the
A_{3}
root system in an
E_{8}
root system
such that the associated embedding
\mathbf{A}_{3}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{E}_{8}
has the property that its orthogonal complement is spanned by two mutually perpendicular
(+4)
-vectors.
So if we let
\mathcal{A}_{4}
act on each summand of
\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
through this embedding, then the resulting action of
\mathcal{A}_{4}
on the

K3
-lattice
\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}\oplus\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\cong\mathbf{\Lambda}
has the property that
\mathbf{L}_{G}
is spanned by
4
mutually perpendicular
(-4)
-vectors.
Theorem
1.2
implies that
G
is realized by a group of isometries of
M
relative to some Ricci-flat metric, but cannot fix a complex structure.
∎

3
The fixed set of a diffeomorphism of prime order

The goal of this section is to prove some results on the structure of the fixed-point set of a finite subgroup
\Diff(M)
of prime order
p
. In the first two subsections we introduce some basic tools we need in order to do this.

Throughout this section,
N
will be a connected, closed, oriented, smooth
4
-manifold and
G\subset\Diff^{+}(N)
will stand for a finite cyclic group of prime order
p\geq 2
of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms.
Since
G
is a finite group acting by diffeomorphisms, the fixed set
N^{G}
is an embedded, closed, smooth submanifold of
N
.

3.1
The Hirzebruch
G
-signature theorem

The first tool we will need is the Hirzebruch
G
-signature theorem, which we now describe. The assumptions are as given in the previous paragraph.

Since the
G
-action on
N
preserves orientation,
each path component of
N^{G}
has dimension
0
or
2
. At an isolated fixed point
z\in N^{G}
, the
G
-action on the tangent space
T_{z}N

preserves a complex structure that is compatible with the given orientation, and is then given as a
2
-dimensional complex
G
-representation
by two nontrivial complex characters
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, so equivalent to the
complex representation

g\in G:(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(\chi_{1}(g)z_{1},\chi_{2}(g)z_{2}).

(3.1)

This is not unique: there is exactly one other complex structure with this property and that is the conjugate of this one for which the associated characters
are of course
(\bar{\chi}_{1},\bar{\chi}_{2})
. Therefore the
signature defect

{\rm def}_{z}
of the
G
-action at
z
defined by

\Def_{z}:=\sum_{g\in G\smallsetminus\{1\}}\frac{(1+\chi_{1}(g))(1+\chi_{2}(g))}{(1-\chi_{1}(g))(1-\chi_{2}(g))}.

(3.2)

is intrinsic. Since
G
is cyclic of prime order
p
,
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are generators of its group of complex characters and so
\chi_{2}=\chi_{1}^{q}
for some
q\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
. We can then also write this as

\Def_{z}=\sum_{\zeta\in\mu_{p}\smallsetminus\{1\}}\frac{(1+\zeta)(1+\zeta^{q})}{(1-\zeta)(1-\zeta^{q})}

Note that each term in this sum is real, and is the norm squared of
(1+\zeta)/(1-\zeta)
when
q=-1
. For future reference we observe that the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

2\frac{(1+\zeta)(1+\zeta^{q})}{(1-\zeta)(1-\zeta^{q})}\leq\frac{(1+\zeta)(1+\zeta^{-1})}{(1-\zeta)(1-\zeta^{-1})}+\frac{(1+\zeta^{q})(1+\zeta^{-q})}{(1-\zeta^{q})(1-\zeta^{-q})},

with equality holding only when
(1+\zeta^{q})/(1-\zeta^{q})=(1+\zeta^{-1})/(1-\zeta^{-1})
, which simply means that
q=-1
. It follows that
\Def_{z}
takes its maximal value for
q=-1
and for
q=-1
only.

For each
2
-dimensional path component
C\subseteq N^{G}
, the
signature defect
of the
G
-action at
Y
is defined by (cf.
[
HZ
]
, p.178, (15)):

\Def_{C}:=\frac{\displaystyle(p^{2}-1)}{\displaystyle 3}C\cdot C,

(3.3)

where
C\cdot C
denotes the intersection product in
H_{2}(N;\mathbb{Z})
: it is by definition zero unless
C
is orientable, in which case we choose a generator
[C]
of the infinite cyclic group
H_{2}(C;\mathbb{Z})
and then take
[C]\cdot[C]
. So when
p=2
, we get
\Def_{z}=0
and
\Def_{C}=C\cdot C
.

Since
N/G
is an oriented rational homology manifold, it has a well-defined signature
\sigma(N/G)
, but for our purpose we can just as well define it as the signature of the restriction of the intersection pairing to
H_{2}(N;\mathbb{R})^{G}
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Hirzebruch Signature Formula (see, e.g. (12) on page 177 of
[
HZ
]
)).

Let
N
be a closed, oriented
4
-manifold. Suppose that
G
is a finite subgroup of the group
\Diff^{+}(N)
of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of
N
of prime order
p
. Then

\textstyle p\cdot\sigma(N/G)=\sigma(N)+\sum_{z}{\rm def}_{z}+\sum_{C}{\rm def}_{C},

(3.4)

where
\sigma
denotes the signature, the first sum ranges over the
0
-dimensional path components
z
of
N^{G}
, and the second sum ranges over the
2
-dimensional path components
C
of
N^{G}
.

3.2
H_{2}(N;\mathbb{Z})
as a
\mathbb{Z}[G]
-module

When
N
is simply-connected, a result of Edmonds gives more precise information
about the
2
-dimensional components of
N^{G}
in terms of the
G
-action on
H_{2}(N;\mathbb{Z})
. In order to state this we will need a few facts regarding
\mathbb{Z}[G]
-modules.

If
G
is a finite cyclic group of prime order
p
, let
\mathcal{I}_{G}\subset\mathbb{Z}[G]
denote the augmentation ideal and let
\check{\varepsilon}_{G}:=\sum_{g\in G}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
be the co-augmentation. Then
\mathbb{Q}[G]=\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{I}_{G}\oplus\mathbb{Q}\check{\varepsilon}_{G}
decomposes the algebra into a direct sum of the cyclotomic field
of degree
p-1
(which we shall denote by
\mathbb{Q}(G)
) and
\mathbb{Q}
.
Any finitely-generated
\mathbb{Q}[G]
-module decomposes into a trivial module
and copies of the cyclotomic module
\mathbb{Q}(G)
.

There is an integral counterpart of this. First note that the image of
\mathbb{Z}[G]
in
\mathbb{Q}(G)=\mathbb{Q}[G]/\mathbb{Q}\check{\varepsilon}_{G}
is
its ring of integers, denoted here by
\mathbb{Z}(G)
. We shall refer to
\mathbb{Z}(G)
as the
basic integral cyclotomic module
. As recalled by Edmonds in
[
Ed
]
, every finitely-generated module
H
over the group-ring

\mathbb{Z}[G]
which is free abelian over
\mathbb{Z}
decomposes into a trivial module and
indecomposable
summands that are either isomorphic to an ideal in
\mathbb{Z}(G)
,
called
integral cyclotomic
or of
\mathbb{Z}
-rank
p
, called
regular
. For the primes of interest here (
p=2,3,5,7
) the ideal class group of
\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{p})
is trivial, so that then every indecomposable module is principal; that is, is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
,

\mathbb{Z}(G)
or
\mathbb{Z}[G]
, respectively. Beware however that a decomposition into isogeny types need not be unique.

Example 3.2
.

A Coxeter transformation
c\in\Orth(\mathbf{E}_{8})
is of order
30
and has as its
eigenvalues the eight primitive
30
th roots of unity.
So for
p=2,3,5
, the transformation
c^{30/p}
has prime order
p
and does not have
1
as an
eigenvalue. This makes
\mathbf{E}_{8}
a purely cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}(\mu_{p})
-module; its rank as a
\mathbb{Z}(\mu_{p})
-module is
8/(p-1)
, that is
8,4
and
2
, respectively.

The obvious ring homomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{Z}[G]\to\mathbb{Z}(G)\times\mathbb{Z}
satisfies

\psi(\check{\varepsilon}_{G})=(0,p)
and of course
\psi(1)=(1,1)
.
Thus
\psi
becomes a ring isomorphism if we invert
p
. In other words, a regular
\mathbb{Z}[p^{-1}][G]
module splits into a cyclotomic and a trivial module. Put differently, the distinction between a regular module and a sum of a cyclotomic module and a trivial module survives reduction modulo
p
. Indeed, since a
p
th root of unity in an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
equals
1
, tensoring with
\mathbb{F}_{p}
renders the
G
-action unipotent and an indecomposable regular resp. cyclotomic module is then given by a single Jordan block of size
p
resp.
p-1
. Here is simple, but useful observation.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
H
be a free abelian group of finite rank and
G\subset\GL(H)
a subgroup of prime order
p
. Assume that as a
\mathbb{Z}[G]
-module,
H
has only regular and trivial summands and that the ideal class group of
\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{p})
is trivial. If
g\in G
is a generator, then the image of

g-1:H\to H
is a direct summand of
H
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module (and equal to the intersection
H^{\not=1}
of
H
with the subspace of
H_{\mathbb{C}}
where
g
acts with eigenvalues
\not=1
).

If
H
is endowed with a
G
-invariant unimodular form, then
H^{\not=1}=(H^{G})^{\perp}
and has discriminant lattice an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space of dimension equal to the number of regular summands in
H
.

Proof.

Our assumptions imply that
H

3.5
Resolution of a spin orbifold of dimension 4

Let
G\subset\Diff(M)
be any finite group. When
M^{G}
has an isolated fixed point
p
then the quotient
M/G
is not a manifold, since a neighborhood of the image of
p
in
M/G
is the cone on a lens space. The goal of this section is to explain how one can sometimes resolve
M/G
to obtain a new smooth manifold
M^{\prime}
equipped with a smooth
G
-action. We then deduce properties of the original action from properties of
M^{\prime}
.

We begin with a general discussion of how to construct spin structures on certain resolutions. We will apply this to the resolved quotient of a K3 surface by a finite group of prime order.

Spin structures.
Recall that a spin structure on an oriented
4
-manifold
W
amounts to a lift of the structural group of its tangent bundle to the universal cover of
\textrm{SL}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
(which has degree
2
). In other words, if
\textrm{SL}_{W}\to W
denotes the bundle of oriented bases of the tangent spaces of
W
(this is a principal
\textrm{SL}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
-bundle), then a spin structure is given by a double cover
\widetilde{\textrm{SL}}_{W}\to\textrm{SL}_{W}
, called a
spinor bundle
, which defines a connected (and hence universal) cover of each
\textrm{SL}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
-orbit. A necessary and sufficient condition for its existence is the vanishing of the second Stiefel-Whitney class
w_{2}(W)
of the tangent bundle. It is unique up to isomorphism if and only if the first Stiefel-Whitney class also vanishes. We note here that if
W
is closed then the existence of a spin structure on
W
implies that its intersection form is even: this follows from
a special case of Wu’s formula, which states that
x\cup x=w_{2}(W)\cup x
for all
x\in H^{2}(W;\mathbb{F}_{2})
(see for instance
[
MS
]
).

Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\Diff(W)
that preserves the spin structure in the sense that
every element of
G
lifts to the spinor bundle
\widetilde{\textrm{SL}}_{W}\to W
. We say that
G
is
even
if
G
admits a lift
as a group
to
\widetilde{\textrm{SL}}_{W}

(so that there is no need to extend
G
with the covering transformation of
\widetilde{\textrm{SL}}_{W}\to\textrm{SL}_{W}
).

Suppose
W
is a complex manifold endowed with a nowhere zero holomorphic
2
-form
\omega
. Then
there is a reduction of the structural group of the tangent bundle to
\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
by letting
\textrm{SL}(\mathbb{C})_{W}
be the bundle of complex bases on which
\omega
takes the value
1
. Since
\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is simply connected, the inclusion
\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\subset\textrm{SL}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
lifts canonically to an embedding of
\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
in the universal cover of
\textrm{SL}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
, and hence this gives rise to a spin structure on
W
. So a finite group of diffeomorphisms
of
W
that preserves both the complex structure and the form
\omega
comes with a lift to the associated spinor bundle: it is even.

The resolved orbit space.

In what follows we consider the following special case:
G
is a nontrivial finite subgroup of
\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and

W
is an open contractible
G
-invariant neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, for example the unit ball with respect to a
G
-invariant inner product. Note that this contains the important case when
G
is of order two, its nontrivial element being the antipodal involution.
We observe that
G
then also leaves
(dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2})|_{W}
invariant.
The group
G
acts freely on

and so the
G
-orbit space

of

has, as a (normal) singular point, the image of the origin, which we shall denote by

-sphere by a free
G
-action.

This singularity has a well known resolution
\pi:W^{\prime}\to W_{G}
. Here
W^{\prime}
is a complex manifold of dimension
2
, the map
\pi
is holomorphic and is an isomorphism over
W_{G}\smallsetminus 0_{G}
and

is a normal crossing curve whose irreducible components are all copies of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
having self-intersection
-2

(we call such an irreducible component a
(-2)
-curve

A
,
D
or
E
.

The case that interests us most is when

is cyclic of order

is a chain of
(-2)
-curves,

. Moreover, the holomorphic form

as a

. This proves that

\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-reduction of the tangent bundle of the

-orbit space of

(which is just

.
In particular,

comes with a spin structure.

Conversely, suppose that we are given a pair

is a normal crossing divisor that is a deformation retract of the complex surface

and
is of the type above: a finite union of
(-2)
-curves meeting transversally so that the dual intersection graph is a

,
D
or
E
.
Then

as a complex normal variety that

and a holomorphic map

of

The normal crossing divisor

We now turn to the proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

The proof is in three steps.

Step 1: The action of

is via an element of the Weyl group

.

Let

.
So for some

, we will have

and it then follows that
Gv
generates an irreducible root system of rank
\leq p-1
.
(This must be of rank exactly

with

acting in a standard manner as there are no nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}[G]
-modules of lower rank.)

Let

be an irreducible component of

. From what we just proved it follows that

preserves

.
We claim that

acts on

as an element of the Weyl group
W(R_{o})
. If this were not so then it would act nontrivially on the Dynkin diagram of

.
The Dynkin diagrams of type

,
D
or

with a nontrivial automorphism are

(

),

(

) and

this
is an automorphism group is of order

(and so this cannot happen since we assumed

odd) and for

and the group in question is isomorphic to

. This last case cannot happen either, because then

and any order

Step 2:
\nu(p+1)\leq 24
and equality holds if and only if

and

(4.1)

Since by assumption

for each

As we have seen, each

As we already observed in §

On the other hand,

Suppose we have equality:

The content in the rest of Section
4
.

Of the two possibilities in the dichotomy of Theorem
4.1
, only the Coxeter case is explicit.
The other, more interesting case, is when
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no
(-2)
-vector. Since its existence was established by Nikulin, this lattice has been the subject of further study (see below for references). Yet such studies
have mostly been on a case-by-case basis, sometimes aided with computer calculations.

Our take here is somewhat different: it is entirely uniform (it is not based on a case-by-case treatment and includes the case
p=2
) and leads to a novel characterization of lattices of Nikulin type (see Proposition
4.9
ff). This will be the key to our proof that the finite prime order subgroups of
\Diff(M)
which leave invariant a complex structure make up a single conjugacy class (Theorem
1.6
). At the same time we analyze the homological aspects of the resolved orbit space construction. Indeed we arrive at the candidate for
\mathbf{L}_{G}
via this construction which involves another lattice that we shall refer to as the
Nikulin lattice
(for
p=2
this was exhibited by Morrison and was thus called by Van Geemen-Sarti). This lattice will also help us to identify the characters of
G
associated to its representation on the tangent space of a fixed point. We will thus see how close we come to eliminating the Coxeter type in Theorem
4.1
. We shall see for
p=2
that we will always have a Nikulin-type situation and indeed, we will find that then the answer is: quite close.

Theorem 4.1 of

Remark 4.11
.

Recall that the

\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space

comes with a nondegenerate quadratic form. There is an evident homomorphism

For

4.5
The case

Using what we have proved so far in this section, we can now prove Theorem

The following corollary illustrates the power of Proposition

Corollary 4.12
.

In case

. In particular,
\mathbf{L}_{G}
contains no
(-2)

via which

acts by exchanging the
\mathbf{E}_{8}(-1)
-summands.

Proof.

We found that the Nikulin lattice
\mathbf{N}_{2}
has a discriminant group an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space of dimension
6

\mathbf{U}(2)^{\oplus 3}
. It follows from Nikulin’s uniqueness theorem that in the situation of Propositions
4.4
and
4.5
, the orthogonal complement

of
\mathbf{N}_{2}
in

is isomorphic to

and hence
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{G}\cong\mathbf{U}^{\oplus 3}\oplus\mathbf{E}_{8}(-2)
by Proposition

\mathbf{L}_{G}
is a lattice whose discriminant form is that of

Since

has rank

\mathbf{L}_{G}(\frac{1}{2})
is unimodular negative definite. It cannot be odd, because then

as asserted.

It then also follows that the embedding

is isomorphic to

It is clear that via such an isomorphism,

∎

Since we arrived at this Corollary

Remark 4.13
.

This comes close to, but does not quite produce, a classification of

Proof of Theorem

Let

4.6
Nielsen realization relative to a Ricci-flat metric

In this subsection we deduce some geometric consequences of our study of

Lemma 4.14

(
Moduli space of Ricci-flat metrics).

Let

be a primitive embedding. Extend the

\mathbf{\Lambda}
by letting it act trivially on

. Denote by

the space of

on

for which

lies in the image of the isometry group of

. Then the orbit space of

with respect to the natural action of the Torelli group of

in the symmetric space of
positive

-planes in the orthogonal complement of

3(2\nu-5)
.
This manifold is the base of a fine moduli space: it supports a universal family of Ricci-flat K3 manifolds.

Conversely, if

is a Ricci-flat metric on

, then every cyclic group of isometries of
(M,s)
of order

that pointwise fixes a positive
3
-plane in

arises in this manner for some primitive embedding

Proof.

Since

acts trivially on

extends to
\mathbf{\Lambda}
by letting it act as the identity on the orthogonal complement of

is a subgroup of

If

and a necessary and sufficient condition that this group contains

is that the positive

defined by its self-dual harmonic

(so that is the space of antiselfdual harmonic

-vector and contains the image of

of the orthogonal group of

If

∎

Building on all of the above material, we can now finish the proof of Theorem

preserving some complex structure.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let us first observe that in the situation of Lemma

For the proof of Theorem

∎

4.7
Elliptic fibrations

As we saw, if a cyclic group
G
of prime order acts faithfully on a K3 surface, then
p\in\{2,3,5,7\}
. Van Geemen-Sarti (for
p=2
) and Garbagnati-Sarti (for

with
\nu=24/(p+1)

fibers of Kodaira type

and as many fibers of type

-fiber is for

a ‘cycle’ of
(-2)

is a rational curve with a node.) So the sum of the Euler characteristics of the singular fibers is

- the group of automorphisms that preserve each fiber and act on it as a translation.
Each nontrivial element of

acts in each

-fiber as a nontrivial rotation (but not necessary over the same angle, see below). It also acts nontrivially in each
I_{1}
-fiber and as observed earlier, the singular points of such fibers account for all the

-fixed points (see Figure
1
).

In what follows we give a homological description of the elliptic fibration (with and without a section) on which

acts. The geometrically most useful one is the case when

preserves each fiber, but as we will see for

, there exist also fibrations that permutes the fibers.

Elliptic fibrations with fiberwise

-action.
We here describe the homological model of an elliptic fibration that is fiberwise preserved by the

-action, as depicted in Figure

.

Let
\mathbf{Z}
be a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
, but regarded as a rank one lattice with zero quadratic form. Denote

. Define an embedding

and let

\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{p}:=i(\mathbf{N}_{p})+\mathbf{Z}\subset\mathbf{N}_{p}\oplus\tfrac{1}{p}\mathbf{Z}.

The definition of

shows that
\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{p}

meets the first summand in
\mathbf{L}_{p}
and the second summand in
\mathbf{Z}
. For

and

let

and
\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\tilde{D}_{i,j}=f

-action to
\mathbf{N}_{p}\oplus\tfrac{1}{p}\mathbf{Z}
by letting it act trivially on
\tfrac{1}{p}\mathbf{Z}
. Since

is an element of

,
and
\sigma-1
takes
\mathbf{L}_{p}^{\vee}
(and hence

) to
\mathbf{L}_{p}
, it follows that

takes its values in

. In other words,

is

-invariant.

Suppose we are given primitive embedding

.
If
M
is endowed with a complex structure

for which the image of this embedding is the Picard lattice (i.e., the orthogonal

(in order to make it compatible with the spinor orientation),

is the class of an elliptic fibration. This elliptic fibration has for each

whose irreducible components have as classes the images of

The Torelli theorem ensures that

uniquely lifts to the automorphism group of

as
in Figure (

.

Remark 4.15

If the singularities of the quotient

Elliptic fibrations with

-action permuting the fibers.

The advantage of the above model is that it helps us to understand the geometry of the

-action. But a K3 surface

with an elliptic fibration and an automorphism of order

is not necessarily of the above type,

(the algebraic counterpart of this observation is somewhat implicit in §5 of

[

We here use that for such

there exists an embedding

such that

contains a copy of

(as is shown in

be one of the isotropic generators of this copy so that

The Torelli theorem guarantees the existence of a complex structure

such that

has

\pm f

Since

cannot preserve each fiber, for then the number of fixed points would at least be equal to the Euler characteristic of

and this is
>\nu

acts nontrivially on the base

of the pencil.
In terms of an affine coordinate

(over

(over

and the number of the fixed points

(or

has

lie over free

For
(p,\nu)=(2,8)

and

and each must contain four fixed points,

For
(p,\nu)=(3,6)

and

must be smooth and each contains

For

At the cusp, we then choose complex-analytic coordinates

\mathbf{U}
, so that

\mathbf{K}_{p}=i(\mathbf{N}_{p})\oplus\mathbf{U}\cong\mathbf{N}_{p}\oplus\mathbf{U}.

Let us also determine the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{L}_{p}
in
\mathbf{K}_{p}
. This clearly contains
f
. Since for
x\in\mathbf{N}_{p}
,

0=s\cdot i(x)=s\cdot(x+\tfrac{1}{p}(\varrho\cdot x)f)=s\cdot x+\tfrac{1}{p}\varrho\cdot x=(s+\tfrac{1}{p}\varrho)\cdot x,

we see that
ps+\varrho\perp\mathbf{L}_{p}
. This also shows that
s\cdot\varrho=-\tfrac{1}{p}\varrho\cdot\varrho=2(p-1)
and so

(ps+\varrho)\cdot(ps+\varrho)=-2p^{2}+4p(p-1)-2(p-1)p=-2p.

Since
s\cdot f=1
, it follows that

e^{\prime}:=ps+\varrho+f
is isotropic and that
\mathbf{L}_{p}^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}e^{\prime}+\mathbb{Z}f
is a copy of
\mathbf{U}(p)
.
It also follows that the
C_{p}
-action on
\mathbf{L}_{p}
extends to
\mathbf{K}_{p}
by letting it fix
e^{\prime}
and
f
, for then

(\sigma-1)(s)=(\sigma-1)(-\varrho/p)
and we know that
\sigma-1
takes
\varrho/p\in\mathbf{L}_{p}^{\vee}
to
\mathbf{L}_{p}
.

The algebro-geometric interpretation is that for a primitive embedding of
\mathbf{K}_{p}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{\Lambda}
that is compatible with the spinor orientation there exist complex structures
J
on
M
for which the image of
\mathbf{K}_{p}
is the Picard lattice,
f
the class of an elliptic fibration, and
s
the class of a section. The group
C_{p}
then lifts uniquely to
(M,J)
so that every
C_{p}
-translate of this section is also one and the image
of
\mathbf{L}_{p}
is
\mathbf{L}_{C_{p}}
.
