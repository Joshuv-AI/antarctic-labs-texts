On the Width of Regular Classes of Finite Structures A preliminary version of this work was published in the proceedings of the 27th International Conference on Automated Deduction [ 1 ]
Alexsander Andrade de Melo Mateus de Oliveira Oliveira

Abstract
In this work, we introduce the notion of decisional width of a finite relational structure and the notion of decisional width of a regular class of finite structures. Our main result states that given a first-order formula ψ \psi over a vocabulary τ \tau , and a finite automaton ℱ \mathcal{F} over a suitable alphabet ℬ ⁡ ( Σ , 0 ​ p ​ t , τ ) \mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau) representing a width- 0 ​ p ​ t 0pt regular-decisional class of τ \tau -structures 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , one can decide in time f ⁡ ( τ , Σ , ψ , 0 ​ p ​ t ) ⋅ | ℱ | f(\tau,\Sigma,\psi,0pt)\cdot|\mathcal{F}| whether some τ \tau -structure in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} satisfies ψ \psi . Here, f f is a function that depends on the parameters τ , Σ , ψ , 0 ​ p ​ t \tau,\Sigma,\psi,0pt , but not on the size of the automaton ℱ \mathcal{F} representing the class. Therefore, besides implying that the first-order theory of any given regular-decisional class of finite structures is decidable, it also implies that when the parameters τ \tau , ψ \psi , Σ \Sigma and 0 ​ p ​ t 0pt are fixed, decidability can be achieved in linear time on the size of the input automaton ℱ \mathcal{F} . Building on the proof of our main result, we show that the problem of counting satisfying assignments for a first-order logic formula in a given structure 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} of width 0 ​ p ​ t 0pt is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to 0 ​ p ​ t 0pt , and can be solved in quadratic time on the length of the input representation of 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} . Keywords. Automatic Structures, Width Measures, First Order Logic

Introduction

Relational structures can be used to formalize a wide variety of mathematical constructions,
such as graphs, hypergraphs, groups, rings, databases, etc.
Not surprisingly, relational structures are a central object of study in several subfields
of computer science, such as database theory, learning theory
[
16
]
,
constraint satisfaction theory
[
8
,
23
]
,
and automated theorem proving
[
26
,
29
,
10
,
11
,
31
]
.

Given a class
\mathcal{C}
of finite relational structures and a logic
\mathcal{L}
,
such as first-order logic (FO) or monadic second-order logic (MSO),
the
\mathcal{L}
theory of
\mathcal{C}

is the set
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})
of all logical sentences from
\mathcal{L}
that
are satisfied by at least one structure in
\mathcal{C}
. The theory
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})

is said to be
decidable
if the problem of determining whether a given sentence
\varphi
belongs to

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})
is decidable. Showing that the
\mathcal{L}
theory of a particular
class of structures
\mathcal{C}
is decidable is an endeavour of fundamental importance because
many interesting mathematical statements can be formulated as the problem of determining whether some/every/no structure in
a given class
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures satisfies a given logical sentence
\varphi
.

The problem of deciding logical properties of relational structures has been studied extensively
in the field of automatic structure theory. Intuitively, a relational structure is automatic if its domain
and each of its relations can be defined by finite automata. Although the idea of representing certain
types of relational structures using automata dates back to seminal work of Büchi
and Rabin,
and it has been extensively applied in the field of automatic group theory
[
15
]
,
Khoussainov and Nerode were the first to define and investigate a general
notion of automatic structure
[
22
]
. One early use
of the notion of automatic structure is in providing a simplified proof
of a celebrated theorem of Presburger stating that the first-order theory of
(\mathbb{N},+)

is decidable.
In
[
25
]
Kruckman et al. introduced the notion of structure with advice and used it to show that
the first-order theory of
(\mathbb{Q},+)
is decidable. The theory of automatic structures with advice was extended
by Zaid, Grädel and Reinhardt and used to define classes of relational structures.
They showed that an automatic class of structures with advice has decidable first-order logic theory if and only
if the set of used advices has a decidable monadic second-order logic theory
[
31
]
.

Interesting classes of structures with decidable MSO theory can be obtained by combining automata theoretic
techniques with techniques from parameterized complexity theory. In particular, for each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\mathcal{C}
is the class of graphs of treewidth at
most
k
, and
\mathcal{L}
is the monadic second order logic of graphs with vertex-set and edge-set quantifications (MSO
2
logic),
then the
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})
is decidable
[
10
,
31
]
.
Given the expressive power of MSO
2
logic, this decidability result implies
that for each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the validity of several long-standing conjectures,
such as Hadwiger’s conjecture for
K_{r}
-free graphs (for each fixed
r\geq 7
) can be decided automatically when restricted to the class of graphs of
treewidth at most
k
. Similar decidability results can be obtained when
\mathcal{C}
is the class
of graphs of cliquewidth at most
k
, for fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathcal{L}
is the monadic second-order
logic of graphs with vertex-set quantifications (MSO
1
logic)
[
11
,
31
]
.
On the other hand, it is known that the MSO
1
theory of any class of graphs that contain arbitrarily large grids
is undecidable. Additionally, a celebrated long-standing conjecture due to Seese states that the
MSO
1
theory of any class of graphs of unbounded cliquewidth is undecidable
[
28
]
.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we introduce a new width measure for finite relational structures that builds on the
notion of ordered decision diagrams (ODDs). An ODD over an alphabet
\Sigma
is essentially an
acyclic finite automaton over
\Sigma
where states are split into a sequence of frontiers,
and transitions are split into a sequence of layers in such a way that transitions in layer
i

send states in frontier
i-1
to states in frontier
i
. We note that ODDs over a binary alphabet have
been extensively studied in the literature and are usually called

OBDDs (ordered binary decision diagrams)
. This formalism has been widely used in the field of symbolic computation
due to its ability to concisely represent certain classes of combinatorial structures with exponentially many elements in the domain

[
5
,
4
,
19
,
30
,
27
]
.

An important complexity measure when dealing with ODDs is the notion of
width
. This is simply the maximum number
of states in a frontier of the ODD. In this work, we will be concerned with relational structures that can be represented
using ODDs whose width is bounded by a constant
0pt
. More precisely, we say that a relational structure

\mathfrak{A}=(\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A}),\mathsf{R}_{1}(\mathfrak{A}),\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l}(\mathfrak{A}))

over a relational vocabulary
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
is

(\Sigma,0pt)
-decisional if its domain
\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})
can be represented by an ODD of width at most
0pt

over the alphabet
\Sigma
, and each of its relations
\mathsf{R}_{i}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})^{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}

can be represented by an ODD of width at most
0pt
over the alphabet
\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}}
, i.e. the
\mathfrak{a}_{i}
-fold
Cartesian product of
\Sigma
. If we let
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
denote the set of all layers whose frontiers have
size at most
0pt
, then an ODD over
\Sigma
can be represented as a word over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
.
Similarly, an ODD over
\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}}
of width at most
0pt
can be represented as a word over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}},0pt)
.
Therefore, a
(\Sigma,0pt)
-relational structure can be represented as a word over the alphabet

\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)\doteq\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)\otimes\mathcal{B}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{1}},0pt)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{B}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{l}},0pt)
. Following this point of view, one can define classes of finite
(\Sigma,0pt)
-structures over a vocabulary
\tau

using languages over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
. In particular, we will be concerned with classes of structures that
can be defined using regular languages over
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
. We say that that a class
\mathcal{C}
of finite relational structures
is
(\Sigma,0pt)
-regular-decisional
if there is a finite automaton
\mathcal{F}
over
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
whose words represent precisely those structures in
\mathcal{C}
.

In this work, we show that given a first order sentence
\psi
over a vocabulary
\tau
, an alphabet
\Sigma
and a constant
0pt
,
the class of
(\Sigma,0pt)
-decisional structures satisfying
\psi
is
(\Sigma,0pt)
-regular-decisional. Building on this result
we show that given a finite automaton
\mathcal{F}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
representing
a class
\mathcal{C}
of structures, one can decide in time
f(\tau,\Sigma,\psi,0pt)\cdot|\mathcal{F}|
whether
some structure defined by a word in
L(\mathcal{F})
satisfies
\varphi
. Finally, we show that given a formula
\psi
over
\tau
with
t
variables

x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
, and a presentation of a
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
as a tuple of ODDs of length
k
and width
0pt
, one can count in time

f(\tau,\Sigma,\psi,t,0pt)\cdot k
the number of assignments for
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
that satisfy
\psi
on
\mathfrak{A}
.
As a straightforward application of this result, we have that several counting problems
that are
\#W[1]
-complete or
\#W[2]
-complete on general graphs,
such as counting the number of independent sets of size
r
or the number of
dominating sets of size
r
(for fixed
r
), can be solved in FPT time on
(\Sigma,0pt)
-decisional graphs, provided a presentation is given at the input.

An interesting feature of our width measure for classes of structures is that it behaves very differently from
usual complexity measures for graphs studied in structural complexity theory. As an example of this fact, we
note that the family of hypercube graphs has regular-decisional width
2
, while it has unbounded width in
many of the studied measures, such as treewidth and cliquewidth. This class of graphs has also unbounded degeneracy,
and therefore it is not no-where dense. As a consequence, we have that most algorithmic meta-theorems proved so
far dealing with the interplay between first-order logic and structural graph theory

[
17
,
24
,
18
]
fail on graphs of constant
decisional-width. In this regard, it is interesting to note that our first-order decidability result cannot be
generalized to MSO
1
logic, since the class of grid graphs has constant decisional width, while the MSO
1
theory
of grids is known to be undecidable
[
28
,
20
]
.

Preliminaries

We denote by
\mathbb{N}\doteq\{0,1,\ldots\}
the set of natural numbers (including zero),
and by
\mathbb{N}_{+}\doteq\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
the set of positive natural numbers.
For each
c\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we let

[c]\doteq\{1,2,\ldots,c\}
and

\llbracket c\rrbracket\doteq\{0,1,\ldots,c-1\}
.
Given a set
S
and a number
a\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we let
S^{\times a}
be the
set of all
a
-tuples of elements from
S
.

Relational Structures.

A
relational vocabulary
is a tuple
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})

of
relation symbols
where for each
i\in[l]
, the relation symbol
\mathsf{R}_{i}

is associated with an arity
\mathfrak{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
Let
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
be a relational vocabulary.
A finite
\tau
-structure
is a tuple
\mathfrak{A}=(\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A}),R_{1}(\mathfrak{A}),\ldots,R_{l}(\mathfrak{A}))
such that

1.

\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})
is a non-empty finite set, called the
domain
of
\mathfrak{A}
;

2.

for each
i\in[l]
,
R_{i}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})^{\times\mathfrak{a}_{i}}
is an
\mathfrak{a}_{i}
-ary relation.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
be
\tau
-structures.
An
isomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
is a bijection
\pi\colon\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})\rightarrow\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A}^{\prime})
such that, for each
i\in[l]
,
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}})\in\mathsf{R}_{i}(\mathfrak{A})
if and only if
(\pi(u_{1}),\dots,\pi(u_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}))\in\mathsf{R}_{i}(\mathfrak{A}^{\prime})
.
If there exists an isomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
, then we say that
\mathfrak{A}
is
isomorphic
to
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
, and we denote this fact by
\mathfrak{A}\simeq\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
.

First-Order Logic.

Now, we briefly recall some basic concepts from first-order logic.
Extensive treatments of this subject can be found in
[
12
,
13
]
.

Let
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
be a relational vocabulary.
We denote by
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
the set of all
first-order logic formulas over
\tau
, i.e. the logic formulas comprising: variables to be used as placeholders for elements from the domain of a
\tau
-structure; the Boolean connectives
\vee
,
\wedge
,
\neg
,
\rightarrow
and
\leftrightarrow
; the quantifiers
\exists
and
\forall
that can be applied to the variables;
and the atomic logic formulas
x=y
, where
x
and
y
are variables, and
R_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}})
for some
i\in[l]
, where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}
are variables.

A variable
x
is said to be
free
in a formula
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
if some of its occurrences
is outside the scope of any quantifier. We let
\mathrm{freevar}(\psi)
denote the set of variables that
are free in
\psi
. We write
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
to indicate
that
\mathrm{freevar}(\psi)\subseteq\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
.
A
sentence
is a formula without free variables.

Let
\mathfrak{A}=(\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A}),\mathsf{R}_{1}(\mathfrak{A}),\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l}(\mathfrak{A}))
be a finite
\tau
-structure,
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\in\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}\in\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})
.
We write
\mathfrak{A}\models\psi[u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}]
to mean that
\mathfrak{A}
satisfies
\psi
when all the free occurrences of the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
are interpreted by the values
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}
, respectively.
In particular, if
\psi
is a sentence, then we may write
\mathfrak{A}\models\psi
to mean that
\mathfrak{A}
satisfies
\psi
.
In this case, we also say that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
model
of
\psi
.
If
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\equiv\mathsf{R}_{i}(x_{\beta(1)},\ldots,x_{\beta(\mathfrak{a}_{i})})
for some mapping
\beta\colon[\mathfrak{a}_{i}]\rightarrow[t]
and some
i\in[l]
, then
\mathfrak{A}\models\psi[u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}]
if and only if
(u_{\beta(1)},\ldots,u_{\beta(\mathfrak{a}_{i})})\in\mathsf{R}_{i}(\mathfrak{A})
.
The semantics of the equality symbol, of the quantifiers
\exists
and
\forall
, and of the Boolean connectives
\vee
,
\wedge
,
\neg
,
\rightarrow
and
\leftrightarrow
are the usual ones.

Languages.

An
alphabet
is any finite, non-empty set of symbols
\Sigma
.
A
string
over
\Sigma
is any finite sequence of symbols from
\Sigma
.
We denote by
\Sigma^{*}
the set of all strings over
\Sigma
, including
the empty string
\varepsilon
, and by
\Sigma^{+}
the set of all (non-empty) strings over
\Sigma
.
A
language
over
\Sigma
is any subset
L
of
\Sigma^{*}
.
In particular, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we let
\Sigma^{k}
be the language of all strings of length
k
over
\Sigma
, and we let
\Sigma^{\leq k}\doteq\Sigma^{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Sigma^{k}
be the language of all (non-empty) strings of length at most
k
over
\Sigma
.

For each alphabet
\Sigma
, we let
\square
be a special symbol, called the
padding symbol
, such that
\square\not\in\Sigma
, and we write
\Sigma\uplus\{\square\}
to denote the disjoint union between
\Sigma
and
\{\square\}
.
For each
\sigma\in\Sigma\uplus\{\square\}
and each
c\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we let
\sigma^{\times c}
denote the tuple
(\sigma,\ldots,\sigma)
composed by
c
copies of the symbol
\sigma
.

Tensor Product.

Let
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{c}
be
c
alphabets, where
c\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
The
tensor product
of
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{c}
is defined as the alphabet

\Sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{c}\doteq\{(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{c})\colon\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma_{i},\;i\in[c]\}\text{.}

In particular, for each
c\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and each alphabet
\Sigma
, we define the
c
-th tensor power
of
\Sigma
as the alphabet
\Sigma^{\otimes c}\doteq\overbrace{\Sigma\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma}^{c\;\mathit{times}}\text{.}

Note that the tensor product of
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{c}
(the
c
-th tensor power of
\Sigma
)
may be simply regarded as the Cartesian product of

\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{c}
(the
c
-ary Cartesian power of
\Sigma
, respectively).
Also, note that
\Sigma^{\otimes 1}=\Sigma
.

For each
i\in[c]
, let
s_{i}=\sigma_{i,1}\cdots\sigma_{i,k_{i}}
be a string of length
k_{i}
, where
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, over the alphabet
\Sigma_{i}
.
The
tensor product
of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{c}
is defined as the string

s_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{c}\doteq(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1,1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{c,1})\cdots(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1,k},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{c,k})\text{}

of length
k=\max\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{c}\}
over the alphabet
(\Sigma_{1}\uplus\{\square\})\otimes\cdots\otimes(\Sigma_{c}\uplus\{\square\})
such that, for each
i\in[c]
and each
j\in[k]
,

\widetilde{\sigma}_{i,j}\doteq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\sigma_{i,j}&\text{ if }j\leq k_{i}\\
\square&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

For instance, the tensor product of the strings
aabab
and
abb
over the alphabet
\{a,b\}
is the string
(a,a)(a,b)(b,b)(a,\square)(b,\square)
over the alphabet
(\{a,b\}\uplus\{\square\})^{\otimes 2}
.

For each
i\in[c]
, let
L_{i}\subseteq\Sigma_{i}^{+}
be a language over the alphabet
\Sigma_{i}
. The
tensor product
of
L_{1},\ldots,L_{c}
is defined as the language

L_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{c}\doteq\{s_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{c}\colon s_{i}\in L_{i},i\in[c]\}\text{.}

We remark that in the literature
[
2
,
3
,
21
,
31
]

the tensor product of strings (as well as the tensor product of languages) is also commonly called of
convolution
.

Finite Automata.

A
finite automaton
is a tuple
\mathcal{F}=(\Sigma,Q,T,I,F)
, where
\Sigma
is an alphabet,
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
{T\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q}
is a set of
transitions
,
I\subseteq Q
is a set of
initial states
and
F\subseteq Q
is a set of
final states
.
The size of a finite automaton
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\doteq\lvert Q\rvert+\lvert T\rvert\log|\Sigma|
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
s=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}\in\Sigma^{k}
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}

accepts

s
if there exists a sequence of transitions
\langle(q_{0},\sigma_{1},q_{1}),(q_{1},\sigma_{2},q_{2}),\ldots,(q_{k-1},\sigma_{k},q_{k})\rangle
, called an
accepting sequence
for
s
in
\mathcal{F}
, such that
q_{0}\in I
,
q_{k}\in F
and, for each
i\in[k]
,
{(q_{i-1},\sigma_{i},q_{i})\in T}
.
The language of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{F})
, is defined as the set of all strings accepted by
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{F})\doteq\left\{s\in\Sigma^{+}\colon s\text{ is accepted by }\mathcal{F}\right\}\text{.}

Regular Relations.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet.
A language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{+}
is called
regular
if there exists a finite automaton
\mathcal{F}
over
\Sigma
such that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{F})=L
.

For each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and each language
L\subseteq(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}})^{+}
over the alphabet
\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}}
, we let
\mathsf{rel}(L)\doteq\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}})\colon s_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{\mathfrak{a}}\in L\}
be the
relation associated with
L
.
On the other hand, for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and each
\mathfrak{a}
-ary relation
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{+})^{\times\mathfrak{a}}
, we let
\mathsf{lang}(R)\doteq\{s_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{\mathfrak{a}}\colon(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}})\in R\}
be the
language associated with
R
.
We say that such a relation
R
is
regular
if its associated language
\mathsf{lang}(R)
is a regular
subset of
(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}})^{+}
.

3
Ordered Decision Diagrams

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet and
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
A
(\Sigma,0pt)
-layer
is a tuple
B\doteq(\ell,r,T,I,F,\iota,\phi)
, where
\ell\subseteq\llbracket 0pt\rrbracket
is a set of
left states
,
r\subseteq\llbracket 0pt\rrbracket
is a set of
right states
,
T\subseteq\ell\times(\Sigma\uplus\{\square\})\times r
is a set of
transitions
,
I\subseteq\ell
is a set of
initial states
,
F\subseteq r
is a set of
final states
and
\iota,\phi\in\{0,1\}
are Boolean flags satisfying the following conditions:

i)

I=\emptyset
if
\iota=0
, and

ii)

F=\emptyset
if
\phi=0
.

We remark that it is possible that
\iota=1
and
I=\emptyset
. Similarly, it might be the case in which
\phi=1
and
F=\emptyset
.

In what follows, we may write
\ell(B)
,
r(B)
,
T(B)
,
I(B)
,
F(B)
,
\iota(B)
and
\phi(B)
to refer to the sets
\ell
,
r
,
T
,
I
and
F
and to the Boolean flags
\iota
and
\phi
, respectively.

We let
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
denote the set of all
(\Sigma,0pt)
-layers.
Note that,
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
is non-empty and has at most
2^{\mathcal{O}(\lvert\Sigma\rvert\cdot 0pt^{2})}
elements.
Therefore,
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
may be regarded as an alphabet.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. A
(\Sigma,0pt)
-ordered decision diagram
(or simply,
(\Sigma,0pt)
-
ODD
) of
length

k
is a string
D\doteq B_{1}\cdots B_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)^{k}
of length
k
over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

for each
i\in[k-1]
,
\ell(B_{i+1})=r(B_{i})
;

2.

\iota(B_{1})=1

For each
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-sentence

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi)

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi)\doteq\{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)\colon\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})\models\psi\}\text{.}

The next theorem
(Theorem

Theorem 6
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})

be a relational vocabulary. For each
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}

, the relation

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi)
is regular.

A constructive proof of Theorem
6
will be given in Section
6
.
As a consequence of Theorem
6
, we have that the problem of determining whether a given

\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-sentence
\psi
is satisfied by some structure
\mathfrak{A}
in the class of structures specified
by a given finite automaton
\mathcal{F}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
is
fixed-parameter linear in the size of
\mathcal{F}
when parameterized by
\tau
,
\psi
,
\Sigma

and
0pt
(Theorem

).

We note that in natural applications, the parameter
\tau
is often fixed.
For instance, if our goal is to decide properties about classes of directed graphs, then

\tau
is simply the vocabulary of directed graphs. Additionally, often the sentence

\psi
is fixed, as in the case where the goal is to determine whether all graphs in
a given class of graphs, specified by a finite automaton
\mathcal{F}

over the alphabet
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
,
are triangle-free. Finally, by Proposition
2
,
the alphabet
\Sigma
can be assumed to be the binary alphabet
\{0,1\}
, with only a moderate increase in width. Therefore,
in applications where
\tau
,
\psi
and

are fixed, the only relevant
parameter is the width parameter

. This parameter intuitively is a measure of
the complexity of each individual structure in the class specified by
\mathcal{F}
, while
the size of
\mathcal{F}
is a measure of the complexity of the class itself.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

\psi

\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}

-regular-decisional class of

\tau
-structure

Proof.

The proof of Theorem

over the alphabet

6
Proof of Theorem
6

We dedicate this section to the proof of Theorem
6
.
The proof follows a traditional strategy combined with new machinery for the implicit
manipulation of ODDs. More precisely, given an alphabet
\Sigma
,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, a relational vocabulary

\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
and an

\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
with
free variables
\mathrm{freevar}(\psi)\subseteq\,X_{t}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
,
we define
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t})
as the relation
containing precisely the tuples of the form
(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})

such that
\mathcal{D}=(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l})
is
(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
-structural
and
\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})\models\psi[u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}]
.
The Boolean connectives
\wedge,\vee
and
\neg
and the existential
quantification
\exists
are handled using closure properties from regular languages.

The technically involved part of the proof however will be the construction of an
initial
automaton which accepts precisely those
strings

D_{0}\otimes D_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes D_{l}\otimes u_{1}\ldots u_{t}

such that
(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l})
is a
(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
-structural tuple,
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}
belong to the domain
\mathcal{L}(D_{0})
.
In particular, we need to guaranteed that for each
i\in[l]
,
the language
\mathcal{L}(D_{i})
is contained in the tensored language
\mathcal{L}(D_{0})^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}}
.

6.1
Basic General Operations

In this section, we introduce some basic low-level operations that will be used repeatedly in
the proof of Theorem
6
. More precisely, we consider the following
operations:
projection
,
identification
,
permutation of coordinates
,
fold
,
unfold
,
direct sum
,
union
,
intersection
and
complementation
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{+})^{{\times}\mathfrak{a}}
be an
\mathfrak{a}
-ary relation.

For each permutation
\pi\colon[\mathfrak{a}]\rightarrow[\mathfrak{a}]
, we let
\mathsf{perm}(R,\pi)
be the relation obtained from
R
by permuting the coordinates of each tuple in
R
according to
\pi
. In other words,
\mathsf{perm}(R,\pi)\doteq\left\{\left(s_{\pi(1)},\ldots,s_{\pi(\mathfrak{a})}\right)\colon\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)\in R\right\}
.

For each
i\in[\mathfrak{a}]
, the
projection of the
i
-th coordinate of
R
is defined as the
(\mathfrak{a}-1)
-ary relation
\mathsf{proj}(R,i)\doteq\left\{\left(s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)\colon\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)\in R\right\}\text{}

obtained from
R
by removing the
i
-th coordinate of each tuple in
R
.
More generally, for each
J\subseteq[\mathfrak{a}]
, we let
\mathsf{proj}(R,J)
denote the relation obtained from
R
by removing all the
i
-th coordinates of each tuple in
R
, where
i\in J
.

For each
i,j\in[\mathfrak{a}]
, the
identification of the
i
-th and
j
-th coordinates of
R
is defined as the relation
\mathsf{ident}(R,i,j)\doteq\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}})\in R\colon s_{i}=s_{j}\}\text{}
obtained from
R
by removing each tuple
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}})\in R
such that
s_{i}\neq s_{j}
.
More generally, for each
J\subseteq[\mathfrak{a}]\times[\mathfrak{a}]
, we let
\mathsf{ident}(R,J)\doteq\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}})\in R\colon s_{i}=s_{j},(i,j)\in J\}
.

For each
i,j\in[\mathfrak{a}]
, with
i\leq j
, we let

\mathsf{fold}(R,i,j)\doteq\left\{\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{j},s_{j+1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)\colon\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)\in R\right\}\text{.}

On the other hand, if
R=\mathsf{fold}(R^{\prime},i,j)
for some relation
R^{\prime}
and some
i,j\in[\mathfrak{a}]
, with
i\leq j
, then we let
\mathsf{unfold}(R,i)=R^{\prime}
, i.e. the inverse operation of
\mathsf{fold}
.

Let
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
be two alphabets,
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
R_{1}\subseteq(\Sigma_{1}^{+})^{\times\mathfrak{a}_{1}}
be an
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
-ary relation and
R_{2}\subseteq(\Sigma_{2}^{+})^{{\times}\mathfrak{a}_{2}}
be an
\mathfrak{a}_{2}
-ary relation.
If
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are non-empty, then we define the
direct sum
of
R_{1}
with
R_{2}
as the
(\mathfrak{a}_{1}+\mathfrak{a}_{2})
-ary relation

R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\doteq\left\{\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}_{1}},s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{a}_{2}}\right)\colon\left(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}\right)\in R_{1},\left(s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{a}_{2}}\right)\in R_{2}\right\}\text{.}

Otherwise, we let
R_{1}\oplus\emptyset\doteq R_{1}
and
\emptyset\oplus R_{2}\doteq R_{2}
.

\Sigma_{1}

Lemma 15
.

and

consists of tuples of the form
(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{0},D_{l},D_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})
satisfying the conditions:

\left(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l}\right)\in\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)

and

Therefore,

is regular, since it may regarded as the relation

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
\tau
be a relational vocabulary, and

\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
be an
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formula, with
free variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
. Consider the following relation.

\begin{multlined}\textstyle\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))\doteq\big\{(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)\colon\\
\mathfrak{s}(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l})\models\psi[u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}]\}\text{.}\end{multlined}

In Theorem
16
, we show that

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is a regular relation. We remark that if
t=0
, then
\psi
is an
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-sentence and,
in this case, the relation
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

coincides with the relation
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi)
. Therefore,
Theorem
16
implies Theorem
6

The proof of Theorem
16
is by induction on the structure of the input formula

\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})

Theorem 16
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})

be a relational vocabulary and
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
be an

\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formula. The relation
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is regular.

Proof.

We prove by induction on the structure of the formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})

that the relation
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is regular.

Base case.
Suppose that
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
is an atomic formula.
Then, there are two subcases to be analyzed.
In the first subcase,
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\equiv(x_{i}=x_{j})

for some
i,j\in[t]
.
In this case, one can verify that

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t})

is equal to the relation

\mathsf{ident}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l+i+1,l+j+1})

which consists of the subset of tuples in
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)
where the
coordinate corresponding to variable
x_{i}
is equal to the coordinate corresponding to variable
x_{j}
.
We note that we need to add
l+1
to
i
and
j
because the first

l+1
coordinates of each tuple in
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)
are ODDs, while
the last
t
coordinates are strings representing assignments to the variables.

In the second subcase,
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\equiv\mathsf{R}_{i}(x_{\beta(1)},\ldots,x_{\beta(\mathfrak{a}_{i})})
for some
\beta\colon[\mathfrak{a}_{i}]\rightarrow[t]
and some
i\in[l]
.
Let
R=\mathsf{unfold}(\mathcal{R}_{\in}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}},0pt),2)
. Intuitively,
R
consists of all tuples of the form
(D,v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}})
such that
D\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}},0pt)^{\circ{k}}
and
v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\in\mathcal{L}(D)
.
Let
R^{\prime}=\mathsf{ident}(R\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)},1,i+\mathfrak{a}_{i}+2)
.
One can verify that
R^{\prime}
comprises all tuples of the form
(D_{i},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}},D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{i},\ldots,D_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})
such that
(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)}
and
v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\in\mathcal{L}(D_{i})
.
Let
R^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{ident}(R^{\prime},\{(j+1,\beta(j)+\mathfrak{a}_{i}+l+2)\colon j\in[\mathfrak{a}_{i}]\})
.
In other words,
R^{\prime\prime}
consists of all tuples
(D_{i},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}},D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{i},\ldots,D_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in R^{\prime}
such that
v_{j}=u_{\beta(j)}
for each
j\in[\mathfrak{a}_{i}]
, which implies
u_{\beta(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{\beta(\mathfrak{a}_{i})}\in\mathcal{L}(D_{i})
.
As a result, we obtain the regularity of
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t})
, since in this case
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t})

can be defined as the relation
\mathsf{proj}(R^{\prime\prime},\{1,\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{i}+1\})
.

Inductive step.
It suffices to analyze the following four subcases:
\psi\equiv\psi_{1}\lor\psi_{2}
,
\psi\equiv\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}
,
\psi\equiv\neg\psi_{1}
and
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\equiv\exists\,y\;\psi_{3}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t},y)
for

\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formulas
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
and
\psi_{3}
and variable
y

that is free in
\psi_{3}
.

First, assume that
\psi\equiv\psi_{1}\lor\psi_{2}
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

and
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{2},\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

are regular relations. This implies that

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is a regular relation, since it is equal to the union

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))\cup\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

of two regular relations. Similarly, assume that
\psi\equiv\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}
.
By the induction hypothesis, the relations

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

and
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

are regular. Then

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})

is a regular relation, since it is equal to the intersection

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))\cap\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

of two regular relations.

Now, assume that
\psi\equiv\neg\psi_{1}
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have that the relation

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is regular.
Therefore,
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is regular, since it is equal to the Boolean combination

\neg\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))\cap{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,t)}
of two regular relations.

Finally, assume that

\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\equiv\exists y\;\psi_{3}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t},y)
for some variable

y
that is free in
\psi_{3}
. By the induction hypothesis,

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{3}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t},y))

is a regular relation.
Therefore,
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}))

is regular, since it can be obtained from

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{3}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t},y))

by deleting the coordinate corresponding to
y
from each tuple in

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{3}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t},y))
.
In other words,
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t})=\mathsf{proj}(\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi_{3}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t},y)),l+t+2)
.
∎

7
Counting Satisfying Assignments

Let
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
be a relational vocabulary,

\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
be an
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formula
with

,
and let
\mathfrak{A}
be a finite
\tau

We say that an assignment
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathsf{R}_{0}(\mathfrak{A})^{t}

of the variables

satisfies
\psi
with respect to
\mathfrak{A}

if
\mathfrak{A}\models\psi[u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}]
.

The next theorem states that given a
(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
-structural tuple
\mathcal{D}
such that

\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})=\mathfrak{A}
, the problem of counting the number of satisfying assignments
of
\psi
with respect to
\mathfrak{A}

is fixed parameter tractable when parameterized by
\Sigma
,
0pt
,
\tau
,
\psi
and
t
.
More specifically, such a problem can be solved in time quadratic in the length of the
(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
-structural tuple
\mathcal{D}
.
We remark that, in most applications, the parameters
\Sigma,\tau,\psi,t
are naturally already fixed, and therefore the only complexity parameter that is indeed relevant in these situations is the width
0pt
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
0pt\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
\tau=(\mathsf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{R}_{l})
be a
relational vocabulary and
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
be an
\mathrm{FO}\{\tau\}
-formula.
Given a
(\Sigma,0pt,\tau)
-structural tuple
\mathcal{D}=(D_{0},D_{1}\ldots D_{l})

of length
k
, one can count in time
f(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,t)\cdot k^{2}
,
for some computable function
f
, the number of satisfying assignments for
\psi
with respect to
\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

Let
X_{t}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}
be the minimum deterministic finite automaton with language
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{F}_{1})=\mathsf{lang}(\mathcal{R}(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,X_{t}))
.

Assume that
D_{0}=B_{1}^{0}\cdots B_{k}^{0}\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma,0pt)^{\circ{k}}
, and
for each
i\in[k]
,
D_{i}=B_{1}^{i}\cdots B_{k}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma^{\otimes\mathfrak{a}_{i}},0pt)^{\circ{k}}
, where
\mathfrak{a}_{i}
denotes
the arity of
\mathsf{R}_{i}
.
Then, consider
the finite automaton
\mathcal{F}_{2}
defined as follows:

•

Q(\mathcal{F}_{2})=\{q_{0}\}\cup\{q_{i,[\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}]}\colon\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\in\Sigma\uplus\{\square\},\,i\in[k]\}\text{;}

•

\begin{aligned} T(\mathcal{F}_{2})&=\big\{(q_{0},(B_{1}^{0},B_{1}^{1},\ldots,B_{1}^{l},\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}),q_{1,[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}]})\colon\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}\in\Sigma\big\}\\[-2.15277pt]
&\cup\begin{multlined}\Big\{(q_{i,[\widetilde{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\nu}_{t}]},(B_{i+1}^{0},B_{i+1}^{1},\ldots,B_{i+1}^{l},\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}),q_{i+1,[\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}]})\colon\\[0.86108pt]
\hfill{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{\nu}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}\in\Sigma\uplus\{\square\}},\,(\widetilde{\nu}_{j}=\square\Rightarrow\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}=\square)\text{ for each }j\in[t],\hskip 17.22217pt\\[-4.30554pt]
\hfill i\in[k-1]\Big\}\text{;}\end{multlined}\end{aligned}

•

I(\mathcal{F}_{2})=\{q_{0}\}
;

F(\mathcal{F}_{2})=\{q_{k,[\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}]}\colon\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\in\Sigma\uplus\{\square\}\}
.

One can verify that
the language
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{F}_{2})
consists of all strings of the form

D_{0}\otimes D_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes D_{l}\otimes u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{t}

such that, for each
i\in[t]
,
u_{i}
is a string in
\Sigma^{\leq k}
.

Note that, the set of states of
\mathcal{F}_{2}

can be
split into
k
levels
such that
states in level
i
only have transitions to states in level
i+1
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is acyclic. Additionally, one can readily check that
\mathcal{F}_{2}

is deterministic and
that
the number of states in each level of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
can be described as
a function of
\lvert\Sigma\rvert
and
t
.

Now, consider the automaton
\mathcal{F}_{\cap}=\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}

whose language is the intersection of the languages of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
.
Note that
\mathcal{F}_{\cap}
accepts precisely the strings of the form
D_{0}\otimes D_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes D_{l}\otimes u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{t}

such that
(u_{1}\ldots u_{t})
is a satisfying assignment for

\psi
with respect to the
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})
.
Furthermore,
one can verify that
\mathcal{F}_{\cap}
is also
an acyclic deterministic automaton whose states can be split into
k
levels such that
there are only transitions going from level
i
to level
i+1
.

Therefore, counting the number of assignments
that satisfy

\psi
with respect to
\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{D})
amounts
to counting the number of accepting paths in
\mathcal{F}_{\cap}
.
This can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\cap}\rvert^{2})

using standard dynamic programming, starting from

the states of
\mathcal{F}_{\cap}
that are in the last
level and, then, recording, for each state of each level, the number of
paths from that state to a final state.

Since

\mathcal{F}_{\cap}
can be constructed in time

g(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,t)\cdot k
, for some computable function
g
,

the overall algorithm takes time
\mathcal{O}(g(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,t)^{2}\cdot k^{2})
. By setting

f(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,t)=c\cdot g(\Sigma,0pt,\tau,\psi,t)^{2}+d
for
some sufficiently large constants
c
and
d
, the theorem follows.∎

8
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced the notion of decisional-width of a relational structure,
as a measure that intuitively tries to provide a quantification of how difficult it is to
define the relations of the structure. Subsequently we provided a suitable way of defining
infinite classes of structures of small width. Interestingly, there exist classes of structures
of constant decisional-width that have very high width with respect to most width measures defined
so far in structural graph theory. As an example, we have shown that the class of hypercube graphs has
regular-decisional width
2
, while it is well known that they have unbounded treewidth and cliquewidth. Additionally,
this family is not nowhere-dense. Therefore, first-order model-checking and validity-testing techniques
developed for these well studied classes of graphs do not generalize to graphs of constant decisional
width. Other examples of families of graphs of constant decisional width
are paths, cliques (which have unbounded treewidth), unlabeled grids (which have unbounded treewidth and cliquewidth)
and many others. It is interesting to note that these mentioned classes have all a regular structure
and, therefore, are “easy” to describe.
