Bounded-error constrained state estimation of LTV systems in presence of sporadic measurements
Yasmina BECIS-AUBRY

Abstract
This contribution proposes a recursive set-membership method for the ellipsoidal state characterization for discrete-time linear time-varying models with additive unknown disturbances vectors, bounded by possibly degenerate zonotopes and polytopes, impacting respectively, the state evolution equation and the sporadic measurement vectors, which are expressed as linear inequality and equality constraints on the state vector. New algorithms are designed considering the unprecedented fact that, due to equality constraints, the shape matrix of the ellipsoid characterizing all possible values of the state vector is non invertible. The two main size minimizing criteria (volume and sum of squared axes lengths) are examined in the time update step and also in the observation updating , in addition to a third one, minimizing some error norm and ensuring the input-to-state stability of the estimation error. The author’s papers [ 1 ] and [ 2 ] were combined into this longer, more comprehensive version. It includes all the proofs and a few images and is meant to be a support for the reader. There is no introduction, no conclusion, and no application examples.

To grasp this idea, take any closed convex set

and a hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}
intersecting it. The intersection of
{\mathcal{S}}
with a halfspace
{\mathcal{G}}
delimited by

{\mathcal{G}}

{\mathcal{H}}

{\mathcal{S}}
(being its support hyperplane), then
{\mathcal{S}}

Remark 4.3

This algorithm is of low computational complexity :
O(n^{2})
. Indeed, all the operations are simple sums and products: they were optimized in this regard and are thence suitable for systems with a high dimensional state vector (big
n
), potentially many measurements (big
{p_{k}}
) and potentially many unknown inputs (big
m_{k}
). The intermediate variables
\alpha_{i}
,
\theta_{i}
,
\eta_{i}
,
\bm{\varphi}_{i}
were added on to perform redundant vector and matrix operations only once. Thereby noticing that
\bm{f}_{k_{i}}^{T}z_{i-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\bar{\rho}_{i}-\underaccent{\bar}{\rho}_{i})
allows to determine
\delta_{i}{\,:=\,}\tfrac{1}{2}({\bar{y}_{k_{i}}+\underaccent{\bar}{y}_{k_{i}}})-\bm{f}_{k_{i}}^{T}z_{i-1}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{\rho}_{i}{\,:=\,}\eta_{i}-\bm{f}_{k_{i}}^{T}z_{i-1}
using addition of scalars, in (
26j
) and (
26n
)
resp
., rather than multiplication of possibly high dimensional vectors.

Remark 4.4

In the case where
\mathscr{H}_{k}\neq\emptyset
, the matrix
\Pi_{i}
loses rank with each intersecting hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{i}}
,
i\in\mathscr{H}_{k}
, thusly entailing the progressive flattening of the ellipsoid
{\mathcal{E}}_{k_{i}}
. Depending on the rank of the matrix
R_{k}
(of which no assumption is made), the rank of
P_{k+1/k}
can be recovered at the time-update phase.
The value of
\rank(\Pi_{i})
is needed at each
i=1\ldots,m_{k}
for each time step
k
, whenever the volume of the ellipsoid
{\mathcal{E}}_{k}
is minimized. Therefore, keeping track of this parameter through simple relations, as (
11c
) during the time update and (
26q
) during the observation update, spares its recalculation at each step

, provided that
\rank(A_{0}P_{0}A_{0}^{T})
is given.

Appendix D
Cubic Equation

D.1
Reduced Cubic Equation

If, in the general cubic equation

\displaystyle\beta^{3}+b\beta^{2}+c\beta+d=0,

(102)

we set
\beta=\tilde{\beta}-b/3
, we obtain the
reduced cubic equation

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}^{3}+{s}\tilde{\beta}+{t}=0,

(103)

lacking the square of the unknown
\tilde{\beta}
, where

\displaystyle{s}=c-\frac{b^{2}}{3},\qquad{t}=d-\frac{bc}{3}+\frac{2b^{3}}{27}.

(104)

After finding the roots
\tilde{\beta}_{1}
,
\tilde{\beta}_{2}
,
\tilde{\beta}_{3}
of (
103
), we shall know the roots of (
102
):

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\tilde{\beta}_{1}-\frac{b}{3},\qquad\beta_{2}=\tilde{\beta}_{2}-\frac{b}{3},\qquad\beta_{3}=\tilde{\beta}_{3}-\frac{b}{3}.

(105)

D.2
Algebraic Solution of the Reduced Cubic Equation

We shall
employ the method which is essentially the same as that given by Vieta
in 1591. We make the substitution

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}=z-\frac{{s}}{3z}

(106)

in (
103
) and obtain

z^{3}-\frac{{s}^{3}}{27z^{3}}+{t}=0,

since the terms in
z
cancel, and likewise the terms in
1/z
. Thus

z^{6}+{t}z^{3}-\frac{{s}^{3}}{27}=0.

Solving this as a quadratic equation for
z^{3}
, we obtain

z^{3}=-\frac{{t}}{2}\textpm\sqrt{{u}},\qquad{u}=\left(\frac{{s}}{3}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{{t}}{2}\right)^{2}.

Any number has three cube roots, two of which are the products
of the remaining one by the imaginary cube roots of unity:

\omega=-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}i,\qquad\omega^{2}=-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}i.

We can choose particular cube roots

\displaystyle{v}=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{{t}}{2}+\sqrt{{u}}},\qquad{w}=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{{t}}{2}-\sqrt{{u}}},

(107)

such that
{v}{w}=-{s}/3
, since the product of the numbers under the cube
root radicals is equal to
(-{s}/3)^{3}
. Hence the six values of
z
are

\displaystyle{v},\quad\omega{v},\quad\omega^{2}{v},\quad{w},\quad\omega{w},\quad\omega^{2}{w}.

These can be paired so that the product of the two in each pair is
-{s}/3
:

{v}{w}=-\frac{{s}}{3},\qquad\omega{v}\cdot\omega^{2}{w}=-\frac{{s}}{3},\qquad\omega^{2}{v}\cdot\omega{w}=-\frac{{s}}{3}.

Hence with any root
z
is paired a root equal to
-{s}/(3z)
. By (
106
), the sum
of the two is a value of
\tilde{\beta}
. Hence the
three
values of
\tilde{\beta}
are

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{1}={v}+{w},\qquad\tilde{\beta}_{2}=\omega{v}+\omega^{2}{w},\qquad\tilde{\beta}_{3}=\omega^{2}{v}+\omega{w}.

(108)

It is easy to verify that these numbers are actually roots of (
103
). For
example, since
\omega^{3}=1
, the cube of
\tilde{\beta}_{2}
is

{v}^{3}+{w}^{3}+3\omega{v}^{2}{w}+3\omega^{2}{v}{w}^{2}=-{t}-{s}(\omega{v}+\omega^{2}{w})=-{t}-{s}\tilde{\beta}_{2},

by (
107
) and
{v}{w}=-{s}/3
.

The numbers (
108
) are known as
Cardan’s formulas
for the roots of a
reduced cubic equation (
103
). The expression
{v}+{w}
for a root was first
published by Cardan in his
Ars Magna
of 1545, although he had obtained
it from Tartaglia under promise of secrecy.

D.3
Discriminant

The product of the squares of the differences of
the roots of any equation in which the coefficient of the highest power of
the unknown is unity shall be called the
discriminant
of the equation.
For the reduced cubic (
103
), the discriminant is

\displaystyle(\tilde{\beta}_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{2})^{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{3})^{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{2}-\tilde{\beta}_{3})^{2}=-4{s}^{3}-27{t}^{2},

(109)

Remark D.1

The discriminant
\Delta
of the general cubic (
102
) is equal to the discriminant
of the corresponding reduced cubic (
103
).
For, by (
105
),

\displaystyle\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}=\tilde{\beta}_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{2},\qquad\beta_{1}-\beta_{3}=\tilde{\beta}_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{3},\qquad\beta_{2}-\beta_{3}=\tilde{\beta}_{2}-\tilde{\beta}_{3}.

(110)

Inserting in (
109
) the values of
{s}
and
q
given by (
104
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta=18bcd-4b^{3}d+b^{2}c^{2}-4c^{3}-27d^{2}.

(111)

It is sometimes convenient to employ a cubic equation

\displaystyle a\beta^{3}+b\beta^{2}+c\beta+d=0\quad(a\neq 0),

(112)

in which the coefficient of
\beta^{3}
has not been made unity by division. The product
\bar{\Delta}

of the squares of the differences of its roots is evidently derived from (
111
) by replacing

b
,
c
,
d
by
b/a
,
c/a
,
d/a
. Hence

a^{4}\bar{\Delta}=18abcd-4b^{3}d+b^{2}c^{2}-4ac^{3}-27a^{2}d^{2}.

This expression (and not
P
itself) is called the discriminant of (
112
).

D.4
Number of Real Roots of a Cubic Equation.

Theorem D.1

A cubic equation
with real coefficients has three distinct real roots if its discriminant
\Delta
is positive,
a single real root and two conjugate imaginary roots if
\Delta
is negative, and at
least two equal real roots if
\Delta
is zero.
