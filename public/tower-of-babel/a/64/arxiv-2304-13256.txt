Some counterexamples in surface homology
Peter Buser, Eran Makover, Bjoern Muetzel

Abstract
We present four counterexamples in surface homology. The first example shows that even if the loops inducing a homology basis intersect each other at most once, they still may separate the surface into two parts. The other three examples show some difficulties in working with minimal homology bases. Introducing hyperbolic ribbon graphs we modify the examples so as to have a hyperbolic metric. Keywords: surface topology, minimal homology basis. Mathematics Subject Classifications (2020): 14J80, 54G20 and 55N10.

Introduction

While working with surface homology in
[
BMM
]
we encountered a number of pitfalls and obstacles that easily lend themselves to be overlooked. The aim of this note is to clarify some of them for which we could not find a reference. We present our findings in four examples answering four questions. While the first question is of a more general nature the other three concern minimal homology bases. In the following
S
stands for a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
. We denote by
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
its first homology group with coefficients in a ring
{\mathbb{K}}
, where we are mainly interested in the cases
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. The questions are about bases of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
that are induced by simple closed curves. When length problems are addressed we assume that
S
is endowed with a length metric which must be such that in any homotopy class of closed curves there is one with minimal length. The length is denoted by
\ell
.

The first question is about configurations that have features of a canonical homology basis.

Question 1
.

Given a set of
2g
simple closed curves on
S
that pairwise intersect each other at most once and, furthermore, induce a basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{Z}})
, if we cut
S
open along these curves, will the resulting surface
S^{\prime}
be connected?

Though this is true in the case of a canonical homology basis, the answer is no in general.
Example 1
provides a configuration with five simple closed curves on a genus three surface whose complement is simply connected. By a general lemma (
Lemma
2.1
) the configuration can be completed into a homology basis and there is, in fact, an explicit one that provides a counterexample to
Question
1
. This example and the lemma are presented in Section
2
.

Section
3
is about minimal homology bases. There are several concepts of short or minimal homology bases on surfaces that have a length metric. For instance, one may request that the longest member in the basis must have minimal length, or that the sum of the lengths shall be minimal. Here we focus on
successive minimality
. We shall consider two variant definitions. The first is due to Gromov
[
Gr
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Successive minima I)
.

A set of curves
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
on
S
is called a set of successive minima (more precisely, of type I) for the homology
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
if it is the result of the following search procedure:

1)

take
\alpha_{1}
to be a shortest homologically non-trivial curve;

2)

for
j=2,\ldots,m
, take
\alpha_{j}
to be a shortest curve whose homology class
[\alpha_{j}]
is not a linear combination over
{\mathbb{K}}
of the homology classes of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{j-1}
.

As Gromov observes in
[
Gr
, sect. 5.1]
successive minima have the very useful property that they are simple and pairwise intersect at most once. Furthermore, they are
straight
in the sense that for any two points
p
,
q
on the curve the distance from
p
to
q
along the curve is the same as the global distance from
p
to
q
on the surface (see also
[
Gu
, Lemma 2.2]
). Hence,

Question 2
.

Does there always exist a basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
of successive minima of type I?

If
{\mathbb{K}}
is a field, e.g.
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, the answer is yes because there are always up to
m=2g=\text{rank\,}H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
successive minima and, since
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
is a vector space over
{\mathbb{K}}
they then form a basis. In the general case, however, the answer is again no. Counterexamples are given in
Examples 2
and
3
. There we construct two surfaces
S_{G}
and
S_{H}
of genus two with a Riemannian metric of variable curvature and a surface
\hat{S}_{G}
of genus twelve with a hyperbolic metric together with the respective loops.

This stands in contradiction with a statement in
[
Gr
, sect. 5.1]
at the bottom of p. 45, where the aim, however, only is to find
generating
successive minima, and the latter can indeed be achieved by extending the search procedure beyond
m=\text{rank\,}H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
(see
Remark
4.8
).

The examples also answer, in the affirmative, the following question raised in
[
BKP
, sect. 3.2]
that stands in connection with the difference between homology over a ring and homology over a field:

Question 3
.

Is it possible that
2g
simple closed curves that pairwise intersect each other at most once form a basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
but not so for
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{Z}})
?

As the search of successive minima does not always lead to a basis unless
{\mathbb{K}}
is a field one may resort to the following alternative which remedies this:

Definition 1.2

(Successive minima II)
.

A set of curves
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
on
S
is called a set of successive minima of type II for the homology
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
if it is the result of the following search procedure:

1)

take
\alpha_{1}
to be a shortest homologically non-trivial closed curve;

2)

for
j=2,\ldots,m
, take
\alpha_{j}
to be a shortest closed curve with the property that the homology classes of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{j-1},\alpha_{j}
can be extended to a basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
.

It is easily checked that whenever procedure I (with
m=2g
) does lead to a basis then procedures I and II come to the same result. In particular, when
{\mathbb{K}}
is a field the two definitions of successive minima coincide.

One may now call a basis
minimal
if it is a sequence of successive minima of type II. But “how minimal” is it from other points of view? In
[
Gu
, p. 1059]
Guth defines the following partial order on the set of all bases of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
: Let
A=\{[\alpha_{1}],\dots,[\alpha_{2g}]\}
,
B=\{[\beta_{1}],\dots,[\beta_{2g}]\}
be bases and assume that the representing curves are of minimal lengths in their homology classes and that the sequences are ordered by length:
\ell(\alpha_{1})\leq\dots\leq\ell(\alpha_{2g})
,
\ell(\beta_{1})\leq\dots\leq\ell(\beta_{2g})
. We say that
A
is
smaller or equal

B
if

\ell(\alpha_{k})\leq\ell(\beta_{k}),\quad\text{for all $k=1,\dots,2g$},

and
A
is
strictly smaller
than
B
if, in addition,
\ell(\alpha_{j})<\ell(\beta_{j})
for some
j
. Based on this partial order we call
A

locally minimal
if there is no strictly smaller basis and
globally minimal
if it is smaller or equal
B
for
any
other basis
B
. Of course, this prompts

Question 4
.

Does a globally minimal basis always exist?

It is not difficult to prove (see Section
4
) that if successive minima procedure I leads to a basis then this basis must be globally minimal. Hence, the answer to
Question
4
is yes if
{\mathbb{K}}
is a field; unfortunately, in the general case it is once again no. A counterexample on a genus four surface is given in
Example 4
. In addition, the example can be modified so that it is hyperbolic, albeit with a greater genus. This is carried out in
Remark
4.1
respectively,
Example 3
, where we introduce a general construction of hyperbolic ribbon graphs.

A word about how we arrived at the configurations shown in
Figures

,
3
and
7
. They gradually emerged after many futile attempts to prove the opposite and by modifying numerous sketches through trial and error. It would be interesting to find a technique that makes it possible to construct further examples in a systematic way.

Separating homology bases

This section is about separation properties of homology bases on surfaces.
Example 1
will show that even if the curves of a basis are simple and pairwise intersect each other at most once their union set may be separating the surface. To put this example into perspective we first prove a lemma that goes in the other direction saying that as long as a system of simple closed curves is
not
separating the system is always part of a homology basis. This is even so in cases where, like in
Figure
1
, the complement of the curve system is a simply connected domain. We could not find a reference for the lemma in the literature and we include a proof for the convenience of the reader.

In the following we call a
partial basis
a set of homology classes that can be completed into a homology basis.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S
be a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 1
and let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be distinct curves on
S
with the following properties

1.)

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are simple closed curves,

2.)

S\smallsetminus(a_{1}\cup\ldots\cup a_{n})
is connected.

Then
n\leq 2g
and the homology classes
[a_{1}],\ldots,[a_{n}]
form a partial basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
.

We sketch a proof. All arguments are standard.

Proof.

We assume
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}
and argue for general
{\mathbb{K}}
at the end. Without loss of generality we may assume that the system of curves is
maximal
in the sense that it cannot be extended to a system
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n+1}
satisfying 1.) and 2.). Then, cutting
S
open along
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
we obtain a topological sphere
S^{\prime}
with
m
holes (i.e. a surface of signature
(0;m)
) for some
m\geq 1
. In turn,
S
is the quotient

S=S^{\prime}/(\text{mod pasting})

“pasting” meaning the reverse of the cutting. Note that any pair of points
p_{1},p_{2}
on the boundary
\partial S^{\prime}
of
S^{\prime}
that come together in this pasting must belong to the
same
connected component of
\partial S^{\prime}
for otherwise we could draw a simple curve
a_{n+1}
from
p_{1}
to
p_{2}
on
S^{\prime}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n+1}
on
S
would still satisfy 1.) and 2.) contradicting the maximality.

The argument is via graphs. For this the curves are homotoped so that they pairwise intersect in at most finitely many points and
S
is viewed as polyhedral surface on which
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}

are edge paths that pairwise intersect only in vertices
. Note that this may be carried out such that conditions 1.) and 2.) are still satisfied.

For any connected component
c
of
\partial S^{\prime}
we let
F_{c}
be a small tubular neighbourhood of
c
in
S^{\prime}
, “small” meaning that all
F_{c}
are annuli and pairwise disjoint. For each
c
we set

S_{c}=F_{c}/(\text{mod pasting}).

This represents
S
as an
m
-holed sphere
S^{0}
to which
m
surfaces
S_{c},S_{c^{\prime}},\ldots
are attached along the holes. Since the latter are orientable the first homology group of
S
is the direct sum of the first homology groups of
S_{c},S_{c^{\prime}},\ldots
(to see this introduce canonical generators on the latter). Therefore we can inspect each
S_{c}
individually and hence, we may assume without loss of generality that
m=1
. Let

\mathcal{G}=c/(\text{mod pasting})

be the connected graph obtained by restricting the pasting to
c
. As a point set
\mathcal{G}
is the same as
a_{1}\cup\ldots\cup a_{n}
. Furthermore,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are naturally identified as cycles of
\mathcal{G}
.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is a deformation retract of
S_{c}
there is a natural isomorphism
\mathbf{j}:H_{1}(S_{c};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\mathcal{G};{\mathbb{Z}})
that acts as the identity on the homology classes of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
.

It remains to construct a basis of
H_{1}(\mathcal{G};{\mathbb{Z}})
via a spanning tree e.g.
[
St
, sect. 2.1.5]
. For this we delete from each cycle
a_{k}
some small arc
e_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, “small” meaning that the arc lies on some edge of the graph and does not meet the endpoints of this edge. As the
a_{k}
intersect only in vertices the
e_{k}
are pairwise distinct and the resulting graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is connected. If
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
still has cycles we delete successively further small arcs
f_{1},\ldots f_{q}
belonging to cycles
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
until the resulting graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}
is a tree. Then the homology classes of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
form a basis of
H_{1}(\mathcal{G};{\mathbb{Z}})
. Via the isomorphism
\mathbf{j}
it is identified with a basis of
H_{1}(S_{c};{\mathbb{Z}})
and
n+q=2g
. Finally,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
also induce a basis of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{Z}})
given that
S
is orientable and
S_{c}
is obtained from
S
by removing a disk.

This proves the lemma for
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}
, and for the general case it then holds as well because for orientable surfaces one has
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})=H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes{\mathbb{K}}
.

Remark 4.1

(Hyperbolic examples)
.

The construction of a hyperbolic ribbon graph around graph

that lead to

In this way one can show, in particular, that

.

Remark 4.2

(Sufficient condition for global minimum)
.

If successive minima procedure I yields a basis then this basis is globally minimal.

This is part of the next slightly more general lemma. The condition is not necessary, though, as is detailed in
Remark
4.6
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2g}\in H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
be obtained by successive minima procedure I. Then for any sequence of linearly independent
\delta_{1},\dots\delta_{2g}\in H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
ordered by increasing length we have

\ell(\gamma_{k})\leq\ell(\delta_{k}),\quad k=1,\dots,{2g}.

Proof.

Since
\gamma_{1}
is a systole the inequality holds for
k=1
. Now assume on the contrary that there is
j\in\{2,\dots,2g\}
with
\ell(\delta_{j})<\ell(\gamma_{j})
. Under this assumption we must have (with
span
over
{\mathbb{K}}
)

\delta_{i}\in\text{span}\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{j-1}\},\;\text{for}\;i=1,\dots,j,

(*)

for if some
\delta_{i}
were not linear combination of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{j-1}
, then by the minimal procedure and our assumption
\ell(\delta_{i})\geq\ell(\gamma_{j})>\ell(\delta_{j})
, where
i\leq j
, in contradiction to the increasing lengths of
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{2g}
. Hence, the assumption
\ell(\delta_{j})<\ell(\gamma_{j})
implies (*), but (*) cannot hold because
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{j}
are linearly independent.
∎

Remark 4.4

(Necessary condition for a global minimum)
.

If a globally minimal basis exists then successive minima procedure II yields it.

This is an immediate consequence of the definition of the procedure.

Remark 4.5

(Local minimum)
.

Successive minima II always yields a local minimum.
This too is an immediate consequence of the definition of the procedure.

Remark 4.8

(Straight generators)
.

We recall that a closed curve
c
on
S
is called
straight
if for any pair of points
p
,
q
on
c
the distance from
p
to
q
on
S
is equal to the length of the shortest arc on
c
from
p
to
q
.

The observation in
[
Gr
, sect. 5.1]
mentioned in the Introduction can be formulated as follows: If
L
is a subgroup of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
and
c
a shortest closed curve on
S
whose homology class
[c]
is not in
L
then
c
is simple and straight. The reason for this property is that otherwise
c
may be written as sum of shorter curves
c=c_{1}+c_{2}
whose homology classes thus both lie in
L
which is not possible because
[c]\notin L
. As a consequence successive minima I produces straight curves. The procedure can be continued until the resulting curves generate all of
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
. Hence,
H_{1}(S;{\mathbb{K}})
can always be generated by straight closed curves.

The question whether there always exist straight homology
bases
, however, seems to be open.
