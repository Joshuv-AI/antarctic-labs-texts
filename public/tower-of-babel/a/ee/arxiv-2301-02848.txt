Boundedness of the Fifth Derivative for the One-Particle Coulombic Density Matrix at the Diagonal
Peter Hearnshaw

Abstract
. Boundedness is demonstrated for the fifth derivative of the one-particle reduced density matrix for non-relativistic Coulombic wavefunctions in the vicinity of the diagonal. To prove this result, strong pointwise bounds are obtained for cluster derivatives of wavefunctions involving multiple clusters.

1.
Introduction and results

We consider the non-relativistic quantum system of
N\geq 2
electrons among
N_{0}\geq 1
nuclei, of fixed position, which represents the system of an atom or molecule. For simplicity we surpress spin coordinates and restrict ourselves to the case of an atom (
N_{0}=1
), although all results are applicable to the case where spin is included and also readily generalise to the molecular case. The electrons therefore have coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}),x_{k}\in\real^{3}
,
k=1,\dots,N
, and the nucleus has charge
Z>0
whose position we choose fixed at the origin in
3
. The corresponding Schrödinger operator is

(1.1)

H=-\Delta+V

where
\Delta=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_{x_{k}}
is the Laplacian in
3N
, i.e.
\Delta_{x_{k}}
refers to the Laplacian applied to the variable
x_{k}
, and
V
is the Coulomb potential given by

(1.2)

V(\mathbb{x})=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{Z}{|x_{k}|}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}

for
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
. This operator acts in
L^{2}(\real^{3N})
and is self-adjoint on
H^{2}(\real^{3N})
, see for example
[
1
, Theorem X.16]
. We consider solutions to the eigenvalue problem for
H
in the operator sense, namely

(1.3)

H\psi=E\psi

for
\psi\in H^{2}(\real^{3N})
and
E\in\real
.

There is a long history of regularity and differentiablity properties of wavefunctions
\psi
, a few selected references include
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
. In recent years there has been growing interest in the regularity of the reduced density matrices, which are objects derived from such wavefunctions. Of particular interest is the non-smoothness at the “diagonal”, which is a signature of electron-electron interation. In previous work by A.V. Sobolev and the current author, it was shown that the density matrix remains bounded at the diagonal upon taking up to four derivatives,
[
9
]
, and it was also suggested that this should remain true for five derivatives, see also
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. The current work demonstrates this to be correct.

We now introduce notation and define precisely our objects of study. For each
j=1,\dots,N
, we represent

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}),\quad(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x,x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N})

for
x\in\real^{3}
. We define the
one-particle reduced density matrix
, or simply
density matrix
, by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\ \quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{1}.

for
x,y\in\real^{3}
.
More commonly, the one-particle reduced density matrix is defined as the function

(1.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

However, since we are interested only in regularity properties we need only study one term of (
1.5
), hence our use of the definition (
1.4
). In fact, we have
\tilde{\gamma}(x,y)=N\gamma(x,y)
whenever
\psi
is totally symmetric or antisymmetric, although in this work we will assume no symmetry of
\psi
. An important related function is the
one-particle density
, or simply the
density
, which is defined here as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\gamma(x,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}|\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})|^{2}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\quad x\in\real^{3}.

Now suppose
\psi
is any eigenfunction obeying (
1.3
) with
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\real^{3N})}=1
, and let
\rho
and
\gamma
be the corresponding functions as defined in (
1.6
) and (

[

[

as a function of two variables in the set

\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y)\in\real^{3}\times\real^{3}:x\neq 0,y\neq 0,x\neq y\}.

In particular, real analyticity was not shown across the diagonal, that is where
x=y
. Despite this, real analyticity holds for the density
\rho
on the set
\real{}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
as shown in
[
14
]
, see also
[
15
]
. Therefore, there is real analyticity of
\gamma
in one variable along the diagonal

, excluding the point

. However, there is no smoothness of
\gamma
across the diagonal, as discussed in
[
9
, Remark 1.2(7)]
in relation to the polynomial decay of eigenvalues of the density matrix operator in
[

]
and
[

[

The first indication of non-smoothness at the diagonal seems to be from

[

[

. For
b\geq 0

, first define

\displaystyle h_{b}(t)=\begin{cases}t^{\min\{0,5-b\}}&\text{if }b\neq 5\\
\log(t^{-1}+2)&\text{if }b=5.\end{cases}

We denote
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For all

and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}

|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\partial_{y}^{\beta}\gamma(x,y)|\leq C\big(1+|x|^{2-|\alpha|-|\beta|}+|y|^{2-|\alpha|-|\beta|}\\
+h_{|\alpha|+|\beta|}(|x-y|)\big)\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(x,R))}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(y,R))},

and for all
|\alpha|\geq 1
there exists
C
such that

(1.8)

|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\gamma(x,y)|+|\partial^{\alpha}_{y}\gamma(x,y)|\leq C\big(1+|x|^{1-|\alpha|}+|y|^{1-|\alpha|}\\
+h_{|\alpha|}(|x-y|)\big)\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(x,R))}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(y,R))},

for all
x,y\in\real^{3}

x\neq 0
,
y\neq 0
and

. The notation

refers to the
\alpha

C
depends on
\alpha,\beta,R,N
and
Z
. The right-hand side is finite because
\psi
is normalised and hence
\rho\in L^{1}(\real^{3})
. The density matrix
\gamma
can be seen to be locally Lipschitz continuous in view of the local boundedness of the first derivative of
\gamma
on
6
. In addition, there is local boundedness of up to four derivatives at the diagonal.

Finally, we remark upon a recent result by T. Jecko,
[
20

, where it is shown that a formula can be produced in the special case of the (
N-1
)-particle density matrix which involves a term with a fifth order cusp along with a smoother remainder term. Due to the method of proof involving the analytic representation of

[

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\psi
be an eigenfunction of (
1.3
). Define
m(x,y)=\min\{1,|x|,|y|\}
. Then for all
|\alpha|+|\beta|=5
we have
C
, depending on
Z,N
and
E
, such that

(1.9)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{\beta}\gamma(x,y)|\leq\begin{cases}Cm(x,y)^{-3}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(x,1))}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(y,1))}\quad&\text{if }|\alpha|,|\beta|\geq 1\\
Cm(x,y)^{-4}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(x,1))}\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{1}(B(y,1))}\quad&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

for all
x,y\in\real^{3}
obeying
0<|x-y|\leq m(x,y)/2
.

Remark 1.2
.

(1)

The bound (
1.9
) naturally complements (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) for
|\alpha|+|\beta|\neq 5
.

(2)

As a consequence of
[
9
, Proposition A.1]
and Sobolev embeddings, the inequality (
1.9
) shows that
\gamma\in C^{4,1}\big((\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{0\})\big)
. The definition of this space is given after Remark
1.4
below.

(3)

In view of
[
9
, Remark 1.2(7)]
, the boundedness of the fifth derivative at the diagonal is consistent with the asymptotics of eigenvalue decay of the density matrix operator given in
[
17
]
. In addition, such analysis indicates the sixth derivative will in general be unbounded.

The idea of the proof is to turn partial derivatives of the density matrix
\gamma(x,y)
, with a suitable cutoffs, into certain directional derivatives, known as cluster derivatives, under the integral. The resulting integrals can then be estimated using pointwise bounds to cluster derivatives of
\psi
. This method builds upon similar strategies employed in
[
21
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
8
]
for differentiating the density
\rho
, and
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
for differentiating the density matrix. However, proving boundedness of the fifth derivatives of the density matrix requires a significant refinement of these techniques.

We will consider derivatives of
\gamma(x,y)
, not just in
x
and
y
but also in the direction
x+y
. Such derivatives will turn out not to contribute to the singularity at the diagonal. To show this, we will require bounds to cluster derivatives of
\psi
involving multiple clusters and which, crucially, will distinguish between the orders of each cluster derivative. The result is Theorem
1.3
and is proven in Section
2
. Cutoffs will be introduced in Section
3
, these are similar to those used in
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
but more sophisticated since particles are grouped up, not only into different so-called clusters associated with
x
,
y
, but crucially also associated to
x+y
. In Section
4
, we will show that, due to the results on the
(x+y)
-derivatives, it suffices to show boundedness at the diagonal for derivatives
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{\beta}\gamma
with
|\alpha|,|\beta|\geq 1
. For such derivatives we require a representation of cluster derivatives of
\psi
in terms of a “good” term and a “bad” term, see (
1.34
) of Theorem
1.3
. The bounds for the “good” term is stronger than in previous results, for example those obtained in
[
8
, Proposition 1.12]
and
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
. The additional strengthening is proven using the optimal multiplicative factors of
[
22
]
. The “bad” term cannot be bounded sufficiently, but it can be handled using a series of steps involving integration by parts.

Notation.

As mentioned earlier, we use a standard notation whereby
\mathbb{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\real^{3N}
,
x_{j}\in\real^{3}
, j=1, …, N, and where
N
is the number of electrons. In addition, define for
1\leq j,k\leq N
,
j\neq k
,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j}

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N})

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{N})

with obvious modifications if either
j,k
equals
1
or
N
, and if
k<j
.
We define
\mathbb{\hat{x}}=\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{1}
, which will be used throughout. Variables placed before
\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j}
and
\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j,k}
will be placed in the removed slots as follows, for any

(1.12)

\displaystyle(x,\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x,x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}),

(1.13)

\displaystyle(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j,k})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x,x_{j+1},\dots,x_{k-1},y,x_{k+1},\dots,x_{N}).

In this way,
\mathbb{x}=(x_{j},\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j})=(x_{j},x_{k},\mathbb{\hat{x}}_{j,k})

For variables
x,y\in\real^{3}
, the directional derivative
\partial_{x+y}
will be defined by

(1.14)

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{x+y}=\partial_{x}^{\alpha}+\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\quad\text{for }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3},\,|\alpha|=1.

Higher order derivatives

are defined by successive first order derivatives.

We define a
cluster
to be any subset
P\subset\{1,\dots,N\}
. Denote
P^{c}=\{1,\dots,N\}\backslash P
,
P^{*}=P\backslash\{1\}
. We will also need
cluster sets
,
\mathbb{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{M})
, where
M\geq 1
and
P_{1},\dots,P_{M}
are clusters.

First-order
cluster derivatives
are defined, for a non-empty cluster
P
, by

(1.15)

D^{\alpha}_{P}=\sum_{j\in P}\partial_{x_{j}}^{\alpha}\quad\text{for }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3},\,|\alpha|=1.

For
P=\emptyset
,
D^{\alpha}_{P}
is defined as the identity. Higher order cluster derivatives, for
\alpha=(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
with
|\alpha|\geq 2
, are defined by successive application of first-order cluster derivatives as follows,

(1.16)

D^{\alpha}_{P}=(D_{P}^{e_{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}(D_{P}^{e_{2}})^{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}(D_{P}^{e_{3}})^{\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime}}

where
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
are the standard unit basis vectors of
3
. Let
\mathbb{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{M})
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M})
,
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
1\leq j\leq M
, then we define the
multicluster derivative
(often simply referred to as cluster derivative) by

(1.17)

D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=D_{P_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots D_{P_{M}}^{\alpha_{M}}.

It can readily be seen that cluster derivatives obey a Leibniz rule.

Throughout, the letter
C
refers to a positive constant whose value is unimportant and may depend on
Z
,
N
and the eigenvalue
E
.

Distance function notation and elementary results.

For non-empty cluster
P
, define

(1.18)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{P}=\Big\{\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}:\prod_{j\in P}|x_{j}|\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in P\\
m\in P^{c}\end{subarray}}|x_{l}-x_{m}|=0\Big\}.

For
P=\emptyset
we set
\Sigma_{P}:=\emptyset
. Denote
\Sigma_{P}^{c}=\real^{3N}\backslash\Sigma_{P}
. For each
P
we have
\Sigma_{P}\subset\Sigma
where

(1.19)

\Sigma=\Big\{\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}:\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}|x_{j}|\prod_{1\leq l<m\leq N}|x_{l}-x_{m}|=0\Big\}

is the set of singularities of the Coulomb potential
V
, (
1.2
). For any cluster
P
we can define the following distances

(1.20)

\displaystyle d_{P}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle:=\text{dist}(\mathbb{x},\Sigma_{P})=\min\big\{|x_{j}|,\,2^{-1/2}|x_{j}-x_{k}|:j\in P,k\in P^{c}\big\}

(1.21)

\displaystyle\lambda_{P}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle:=\min\{1,\,d_{P}(\mathbb{x})\}

(1.22)

\displaystyle\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\min\{1;|x_{j}|:j\in P\}

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
.

Using the formula (
1.20
), see for example
[
9
, Lemma 4.2]
, it can be shown that

(1.23)

|d_{P}(\mathbb{x})-d_{P}(\mathbb{y})|,|\lambda_{P}(\mathbb{x})-\lambda_{P}(\mathbb{y})|,|\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})-\nu_{P}(\mathbf{y})|\leq|\mathbb{x}-\mathbb{y}|

for all
\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in\real^{3N}
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{M})
be a cluster set and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
be a multiindex. Define

(1.24)

\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\bigcup_{j\,:\,\alpha_{j}\neq 0}\Sigma_{P_{j}}

for non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and when
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=0
we set

\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\emptyset
. Denote
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c}=\real^{3N}\backslash\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. For non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
we can also define the distances

(1.25)

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle=\min\{d_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x}):\alpha_{j}\neq 0,\,j=1,\dots,M\},

(1.26)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle=\min\{\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x}):\alpha_{j}\neq 0,\,j=1,\dots,M\},

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
. Notice we also have the identity

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})=\text{dist}(\mathbb{x},\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}).

Indeed, since
\Sigma_{P_{j}}\subset\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
whenever

is such that
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
, we have
\text{dist}(\mathbb{x},\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\leq d_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})
and hence
\text{dist}(\mathbb{x},\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\leq d_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})
. Conversely for each

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Sigma_{P_{j}}

. Taking infimum over

we obtain the reverse inequality.

We will also use the following related quantities involving maxima of relevant distances for non-zero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,

(1.27)

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle=\max\{d_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x}):\alpha_{j}\neq 0,\,j=1,\dots,M\}

(1.28)

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})

\displaystyle=\max\{\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x}):\alpha_{j}\neq 0,\,j=1,\dots,M\}.

For
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=0

and
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\equiv 1
.

(1.29)

\displaystyle f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};r;u):=\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r))}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r))}

for
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
. The ball

is considered in

. Largely, this notation will be used for

(1.30)

\displaystyle f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};r):=f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};r;\psi).

Furthermore, later we will not track the dependence on

(1.31)

\displaystyle f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x}):=f_{\infty}\Big(\mathbb{x};\frac{1}{2};\psi\Big).

A pointwise cluster derivative bound.

To prove Theorem
1.1
we will state and prove a new pointwise bound to cluster derivatives of eigenfunctions
\psi
, which itself is of independent interest. It will be shown by elliptic regularity that for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
the weak cluster derivatives
D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathbb{P}}\psi
exist in the set
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c}
. We consider how such cluster derivatives behave as the set
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is approached.

Previously, S. Fournais and T. Ø. Sørensen have given bounds to local
L^{p}
-norms of cluster derivatives of
\psi
for a single cluster
P
. Indeed, in
[
8
, Proposition 1.12]
it is shown that for any multiindex
0\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
p\in(1,\infty]
and any
0<r<R<1
there exists
C
, depending on
r,R,p
and
\alpha
, such that

(1.32)

\left\lVert D_{P}^{\alpha}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{p}(B(\mathbb{x},r\lambda_{P}(\mathbb{x})))}\leq C\lambda_{P}(\mathbb{x})^{1-|\alpha|}\big(\left\lVert\nabla\psi\right\rVert_{L^{p}(B(\mathbf{x},R\lambda_{P}(\mathbf{x})))}+\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{p}(B(\mathbf{x},R\lambda_{P}(\mathbf{x})))}\big)

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma^{c}_{P}
. Notice that for every
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{P}^{c}
, we have
B(\mathbf{x},r\lambda_{P}(\mathbf{x}))\subset\Sigma^{c}_{P}
by the definition of
\lambda_{P}(\mathbf{x})
. The exponent
1-|\alpha|
is natural since both
\psi
and
\nabla\psi
are locally bounded, therefore we only get a singularity for
|\alpha|\geq 2
.

The objective of the following theorem is to extend the bounds (
1.32
) in the case of
p=\infty
and for cluster sets
\mathbb{P}
which may contain more than one cluster. Bounds for cluster sets are present in
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
, but here we obtain estimates which depend on the order of derivative on each cluster in
\mathbb{P}
. This will be vital in proving the boundedness of the fifth derivative of the density matrix at the diagonal. The symbol
\nabla
denotes the gradient operator in
3N
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every cluster set
\mathbb{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{M})
, multiindex
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
and any
R>0
there exists
C
, depending on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
R
, such that for
k=0,1
,

(1.33)

|D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\nabla^{k}\psi(\mathbf{x})|\leq C\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{1-k}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{M}|}f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};R)

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Furthermore, for each
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\geq 1
there exists a function
G_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\to\mathbb{C}^{3N}
such that

(1.34)

D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\nabla\psi=G_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+\psi\,D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\nabla F_{c},

where
F_{c}
is defined later in (
2.1
), and for each
0\leq b<1
there exists
C
, depending on
\boldsymbol{\alpha},R
and
b
, such that

(1.35)

|G_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})|\\
\leq C\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{b}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{M}|}f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};R)

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Remark 1.4
.

(1)

In the case of a single cluster and

, the bound (
1.33
) reestablishes (
1.32

(2)

The proof of Theorem

will adopt a similar strategy to that used originally in

, and developed in

[

upon multiplication by a factor, universal in the sense that the factor depends only on

and

.

We will require an elliptic regularity result, stated below, which will be used in the proofs. Beforehand, we clarify the precise form of definitions which we will be using. Let
\Omega
be open,
\theta\in(0,1]
and
k=\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We formally define the
\theta
-Hölder seminorms for a function
f
by

\displaystyle[f]_{\theta,\Omega}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\theta}},

\displaystyle[\nabla^{k}f]_{\theta,\Omega}=\sup_{|\alpha|=k}[\partial^{\alpha}f]_{\theta,\Omega}.

The space
C^{k,\theta}(\Omega)
is defined as all
f\in C^{k}(\Omega)
where
[\nabla^{k}f]_{\theta,\Omega^{\prime}}
is finite for each
\Omega^{\prime}
compactly contained in
\Omega
. In addition, the space
C^{k,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
is defined as all
f\in C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
where
[\nabla^{k}f]_{\theta,\Omega}
is finite. This space has a norm given by

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{k,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})}+[\nabla^{k}f]_{\theta,\Omega}.

For open
\Omega\subset\real^{n}
we can consider the following elliptic equation,

(1.36)

Lu:=-\Delta u+\mathbb{c}\cdot\nabla u+du=g

for some
\mathbb{c}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
d,g:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
. The corresponding bilinear form for operator
L
is defined formally as

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\int_{\Omega}\big(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\chi+(\mathbb{c}\cdot\nabla u)\chi+du\chi\big)\,dx

for all
u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
and
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. We say that a function
u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
is a
weak solution
to the equation (
1.36
) in
\Omega
if
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\int_{\Omega}g\chi\,dx
for every
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
.

The following theorem is a restatement of
[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
(
[
8
, Proposition A.2]
is similar), with additional Hölder regularity which follows from the proof.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
x_{0}\in\real^{n}
,
R>0
and
\mathbb{c},d,g\in L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},R))
and
u\in H^{1}(B(x_{0},R))
be a weak solution to (
1.36
) then for each
\theta\in[0,1)
we have
u\in C^{1,\theta}(B(x_{0},R))\cap H_{loc}^{2}(B(x_{0},R))
, and for any

we have

(1.37)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\theta}(\overline{B(x_{0},r)})}\leq C(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x_{0},R))}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},R))})

for
C=C(n,K,r,R,\theta)
where

\left\lVert\mathbb{c}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},R))}+\left\lVert d\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},R))}\leq K.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof will involve the use of a multiplicative factor, frequently called a
Jastrow factor
in mathematical literature, to improve the regularity of
\psi
. This is a strategy that has been used successfully in, for example,
[
21
]
,
[
4
]
to elucidate regularity properties of
\psi
. We start by defining a function
F=F(\mathbf{x})
, depending only on
N
and
Z
, such that the function
e^{-F}\psi
solves an elliptic equation with bounded coefficients. These coefficients behave suitably well under the action of cluster derivatives. This allows us to use elliptic regularity to produce bounds to the cluster derivatives of
e^{-F}\psi
and hence to
\psi
itself.

2.1.
Jastrow factors

We begin by defining

(2.1)

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=F_{c}(\mathbf{x})-F_{s}(\mathbf{x}),\quad F_{c}(\mathbb{x})=F_{c}^{(en)}(\mathbf{x})+F_{c}^{(ee)}(\mathbf{x})

where

(2.2)

\displaystyle F^{(en)}_{c}(\mathbb{x})=-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}|x_{j}|,\quad F^{(ee)}_{c}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{1\leq l<k\leq N}|x_{l}-x_{k}|,

(2.3)

\displaystyle F_{s}(\mathbb{x})=-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\sqrt{|x_{j}|^{2}+1}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{1\leq l<k\leq N}\sqrt{|x_{l}-x_{k}|^{2}+1},

for
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
.
The function
F
was used in
[
8
]
. We now detail some basic facts regarding these functions. Firstly,

(2.4)

\Delta F_{c}=V

where
V
is the Coulomb potential, (
1.2
). The function
F_{s}
has the same behaviour as
F_{c}
at infinity. However,
F_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\real^{3N})
and

(2.5)

\partial^{\alpha}F_{s}\in L^{\infty}(\real^{3N})\quad\text{for all }\,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3N},|\alpha|\geq 1.

Finally, the function
F
obeys

(2.6)

F,\nabla F\in L^{\infty}(\real^{3N}).

The function
F
is used to define the following object, which will be used throughout the proof:

(2.7)

\phi=e^{-F}\psi.

Using (
1.3
) the following elliptic equation can be shown to hold for
\phi
a weak solution,

(2.8)

-\Delta\phi-2\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi+(\Delta F_{s}-|\nabla F|^{2}-E)\phi=0.

Since all coefficients are bounded in
3N
we can see from Theorem
1.5
that
\phi\in C^{1,\theta}(\real^{3N})
for each
\theta\in[0,1)
. Furthermore, by the same theorem, for any pair
0<r<R
we can find constants
C,C^{\prime}
, dependent only on
N,Z,E,r,R
and
\theta
, such that

(2.9)

\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{C^{1,\theta}(\overline{B(\mathbb{x},r)})}\leq C\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
.

From the definition of
\phi
, (
2.7
), along with (
2.6
) there exists new constants
C<C^{\prime}
, dependent only on
Z
and
N
(hence independent of
R>0
), such that

(2.10)

C\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}\leq\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
.

2.2.
Derivatives of F

Informally, our objective is to take cluster derivatives of the elliptic equation (
2.8
) and apply elliptic regularity. To do so, we require bounds to the cluster derivatives of the coefficients present in this equation. This is the objective of the current section. To begin, we state and prove the following preparatory lemma involving the distances introduced in (

), (

) and (

).

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{M})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
we have for
k=0,1
,

(2.11)

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{k-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}\leq\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{k}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

for all
\mathbb{y}\in\real^{3N}
. Furthermore, for some
n\geq 1
let
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}
be an arbitrary collection of multiindices in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}+\dots+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, then

(2.12)

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}(\mathbb{y})\leq\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})

for all
\mathbb{y}\in\real^{3N}
.

Proof.

The results are trivial in the case of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0
therefore we assume in the following that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is non-zero. First, observe for all
j=1,\dots,M
,

\begin{split}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{j}|}&\leq\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{j}|}\\
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\sigma_{j}|}&\leq\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\sigma_{j}|}\quad\text{ if }\sigma_{j}\neq 0\end{split}

by the definition of
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
. Now perform the following trivial expansion of the product,

\begin{split}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}&=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\\
&\leq\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\end{split}

which proves (
2.11
) for
k=0
. For
k=1
, consider that for each
\mathbb{y}
we can find
l=1,\dots,M
such that
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})=\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})
and
\sigma_{l}\neq 0
. Then,

\begin{split}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}&=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\sigma_{l}|}\dots\mu_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\\
&\leq\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\sigma_{l}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\\
&=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\end{split}

as required.

Finally we prove (
2.12
). As above, take arbitrary
\mathbb{y}
and a corresponding
l
such that
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})=\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})
with
\sigma_{l}\neq 0
. For each
1\leq j\leq n
we denote the
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
-components of
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}
as
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}=(\beta^{(j)}_{1},\dots,\beta^{(j)}_{M})
. We know
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}+\dots+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}
so in particular,
\beta_{l}^{(1)}+\dots+\beta_{l}^{(n)}=\sigma_{l}
. Since
\sigma_{l}\neq 0
there exists at least one
1\leq r\leq n
such that
\beta^{(r)}_{l}\neq 0
. Hence by the definition of
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r)}}
,

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r)}}(\mathbb{y})=\min\{\lambda_{P_{s}}(\mathbb{y}):\beta_{s}^{(r)}\neq 0\text{, }s=1,\dots,M\}\leq\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y}).

The remaining factors
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}(\mathbb{y})
, for
j\neq r
, can each be bounded above by one.

The following lemma will be useful in proving results about taking cluster derivatives of

Lemma 2.2

Proof.

Take any

\alpha_{j}\neq 0
. For such

we have
x_{l}\neq 0

|x_{l}|\geq d_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})

B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x}))\subset\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{c}
for all
\nu\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\geq 1
and any
\nu\in(0,1)
there exists
C
, depending on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
\nu
, such that for
k=0,1
,

(2.22)

\left\lVert D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla^{k}F\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})))},\,\left\lVert D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla^{k}(e^{F})\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})))}\\
\leq C\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})^{1-k}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

(2.23)

\left\lVert D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}|\nabla F|^{2}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})))}\leq C\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{c}
. The bound to the first norm in (
2.22
) also holds when
F
is replaced by
F_{c}
.

Proof.

Take some
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{c}
. By (
1.23
), for each
\mathbb{y}\in B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x}))
we have

|\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})-\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{y})|\leq|\mathbb{x}-\mathbb{y}|\leq\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})\leq\nu\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})

for each
j=1,\dots,M
with
\sigma_{j}\neq 0
. The final inequality above uses the definition of
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
in (
1.26
). By rearrangement, we have

(2.24)

(1-\nu)\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})\leq\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{y}).

Therefore,

(2.25)

\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\leq(1-\nu)^{-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

for all
\mathbb{y}\in B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x}))
. We now prove an analogous inequality. First, we see that
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})=\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{x})
for some
1\leq l\leq M
with
\sigma_{l}\neq 0
which will depend on the choice of
\mathbb{x}
. We also note that
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})\leq\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})
for all
\mathbb{y}\in\real^{3N}
which follows from
\sigma_{l}\neq 0
and the definition of
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{y})\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{y})^{1-|\sigma_{l}|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}^{M}\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{y})^{-|\sigma_{j}|}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\nu)^{1-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}\lambda_{P_{l}}(\mathbb{x})^{1-|\sigma_{l}|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}^{M}\lambda_{P_{j}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{j}|}

(2.26)

\displaystyle=(1-\nu)^{1-|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x})\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}

for all
\mathbb{y}\in B(\mathbb{x},\nu\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbb{x}))
. In the second step we applied (
2.24
). The required bounds then arise from Lemma
2.3
followed by either (
2.25
) or (
2.26
) as appropriate.

∎

2.3.
Cluster derivatives of
\phi
and
\psi

We introduce the following notation. For cluster set
\mathbb{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{M})
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
define

\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi.

We have previously obtained estimates for the coefficients, and their derivatives, in equation (
2.8
). By taking cluster derivatives of this equation, we obtain elliptic equations whose weak solutions are
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. By a process of rescaling, using a method developed in

, we will apply elliptic regularity to give quantative bounds to the

-norms of
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and

Lemma 2.5
.

For every
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\geq 1
we have
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a weak solution to

(2.27)

-\Delta\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-2\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+(\Delta F_{s}-|\nabla F|^{2}-E)\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\neq 0\end{subarray}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\choose\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\big(2D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\Delta F_{s}-|\nabla F|^{2})\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\big)=:g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

in
\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, and therefore
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in C^{1}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c})\cap H^{2}_{loc}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c})
.

Proof.

We prove by induction, starting with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=1
. Let
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the bilinear form corresponding to the operator acting on
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
on the left-hand side of (
2.27
), as defined in (
1.36
). Since
\phi\in H^{2}_{loc}(\real^{3N})
we have
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\real^{3N})
. We use integration by parts to show that, for any
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
,

(2.28)

\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\chi)=-\mathcal{L}(\phi,D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\chi)+\int_{\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\chi\,d\mathbb{x}.

Now,
\mathcal{L}(\phi,D_{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\chi)=0
since
\phi
is a weak solution to (
2.8
), hence (
2.27
) holds. By Lemma
2.3
and that
\phi\in C^{1}(\real^{3N})
we see that
g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in L_{loc}^{\infty}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. Hence, by Theorems
1.5
,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in C^{1}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\cap H_{loc}^{2}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

Assume the hypothesis holds for all multiindices
1\leq|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq k-1
for some
k\geq 2
. Take some arbitrary multiindex
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=k
. We will prove the induction hypothesis for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. First, we state the useful fact that for any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
we have
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\subset\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. Take some
\boldsymbol{\eta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with
|\boldsymbol{\eta}|=1
. Notice, then, that
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in H^{2}_{loc}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
by the induction hypothesis, and hence
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. This allows
\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\chi)
to be defined for any
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. Integration by parts is then used on the
D^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathbb{P}}
-derivative, in a similar way to (
2.28
). Along with the induction hypothesis and further applications of integration by parts, we find that (
2.27
) holds for
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as a weak solution. Regularity for
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is shown in a similar way to the
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=1
case. Indeed, the induction hypothesis can be used to show
g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\Sigma^{c}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
.

∎

We now apply the
C^{1}
-elliptic regularity estimates of Theorem
1.5
, via a scaling procedure, to the equations (
2.27
).

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\geq 1
and
0<r<R<1
there exists
C
, dependent only on
E,Z,N,r
and

, such that

(2.29)

\left\lVert\nabla\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}\\
\leq C\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{-1}\big(\left\lVert\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}+\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{2}\left\lVert g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}\big)

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c}
. The function
g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
was given by (
2.27
).

Proof.

Fix any
\mathbb{x}\in\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{c}
and denote
\lambda=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})

\begin{split}w(\mathbb{y})&=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})\\
\mathbb{c}(\mathbb{y})&=-2\nabla F(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})\\
d(\mathbb{y})&=\Delta F_{s}(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})-|\nabla F(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})|^{2}-E\\
f(\mathbb{y})&=g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})\end{split}

for all
\mathbb{y}\in B(0,1)
. Then by Lemma
2.5
we have that
w
is a weak solution to

(2.30)

-\Delta w+\lambda\mathbb{c}\cdot\nabla w+\lambda^{2}dw=\lambda^{2}f

in

. By (
2.6
) and (
2.5
), and that

by definition, we obtain

\lambda\left\lVert\mathbb{c}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(0,1))}+\lambda^{2}\left\lVert d\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(0,1))}\leq K

for some

and in particular is independent of our choice of

1.5

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{C^{1}(\overline{B(0,r)})}\leq C(\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(0,R))}+\lambda^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(0,R))}).

Finally, we use
\nabla w(\mathbb{y})=\lambda\nabla\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x}+\lambda\mathbb{y})
by the chain rule, and rewrite (
2.31

2.29

∎

The following proposition uses induction to write estimates for both

\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

For any

, any

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{1-k}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{M}|}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})^{b}f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};R;\phi)

and

Suppose

For

Assume the hypothesis holds for all multiindices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with

for some

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with

and

for each

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}

2.27

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\sigma_{M}|}\big(\left\lVert\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r^{\prime}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}\\

b=0

, (
1.30

\boldsymbol{\alpha},r^{\prime}

(2.34)

\left\lVert g_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r^{\prime}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}\leq C\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{1}|}\dots\lambda_{P_{M}}(\mathbb{x})^{-|\alpha_{M}|}f_{\infty}(\mathbb{x};R;\phi)

, depending on

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbb{x})))}

3.
Cutoffs

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, partial derivatives of the density matrix will become cluster derivatives of
\psi
under the integral. In this section we introduce cutoff functions which will included in the density matrix. These can be made to form a partition of unity, and are present to ensure that the cluster derivatives of
\psi
are supported only away from their singularities.

The cutoffs introduced in this section are an extension of similar cutoffs used in
[
14
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
9
]
. They involve the variables (i.e. “particles”)
x,y
and
x_{j}
,
2\leq j\leq N
. For a given cutoff, we will define corresponding clusters
P
and
S
. Particles in
P
(or
S
) will be close to
x
(or
y
, respectively) on the support of the cutoff. The cluster
P
(or
S
) will eventually faciliate taking
x
- (or
y
-)derivatives of the density matrix.

We will also need to take derivatives of the density matrix in the variable
x+y
. For this, it will be natural to consider a larger cluster
Q
which contains both
P
and
S
, but can include other particles. The particles in
Q
are held close to both
x
and
y
, but potentially looser than how particles in
P
and
S
are held to
x
and
y
respectively.

3.1.
Definition of
\Phi

To begin, take some
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\real)
,
0\leq\xi(s)\leq 1
, with

(3.1)

\displaystyle\xi(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }|s|\leq 1\\
0&\text{ if }|s|\geq 2.\end{cases}

for
s\in\real
. For each
t>0
we can define the following two
cutoff factors

\zeta_{t}=C_{c}^{\infty}(\real^{3})
and
\theta_{t}\in C^{\infty}(\real^{3})
by

(3.2)

\zeta_{t}(z)=\xi\Big(\frac{4N|z|}{t}\Big),\qquad\theta_{t}(z)=1-\zeta_{t}(z),\qquad z\in\real^{3}.

We have the following
support criteria
for cutoff factors. For any
z\in\real^{3}
and
t>0
,

•

if
\zeta_{t}(z)\neq 0
then
|z|<(2N)^{-1}t
,

•

if
\theta_{t}(z)\neq 0
then
|z|>(4N)^{-1}t
.

Take any
\delta,\epsilon
with
0<2\delta\leq\epsilon
. We define our cutoff, which depends on
\delta
and
\epsilon
as parameters, as a function
\Phi=\Phi_{\delta,\epsilon}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})
given by

(3.3)

\Phi_{\delta,\epsilon}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}g^{(1)}_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}g^{(2)}_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq k<l\leq N}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l})

for
x,y\in\real^{3}
and
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
, and where
g^{(1)}_{j},g^{(2)}_{j},f_{k,l}\in\{\zeta_{\delta},\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon}\}
for
2\leq j\leq N
and
2\leq k<l\leq N
. In addition, for
2\leq k<l\leq N
we define
f_{l,k}=f_{k,l}
.

The idea is that on the support of
\Phi
we have upper and/or lower bounds to the distances between various pairs of particles. If we take the particles
x_{j}
and
x_{k}
, for example, we have that
\zeta_{\delta}(x_{j}-x_{k})
holds the two particles close relative to a distance
\delta
,

\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})
holds them apart relative to a distance
\epsilon
, and
(\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon})(x_{j}-x_{k})
keeps them within an intermediate distance apart.

The cutoffs,
\Phi
, defined in (
3.3
) form a partition of unity as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a collection
\{\Phi^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
of cutoffs (
3.3
), with integer
J
depending only on
N
, such that whenever
0<2\delta\leq\epsilon
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi^{(j)}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=1

for all
x,y\in\real^{3}
,
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
.

Proof.

From the definitions (
3.2
) and support criteria it is straightforward to verify that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\theta_{\delta}(z)\theta_{\epsilon}(z)=\theta_{\epsilon}(z),\qquad\zeta_{\delta}(z)\theta_{\epsilon}(z)=0,\qquad\zeta_{\delta}(z)\zeta_{\epsilon}(z)=\zeta_{\delta}(z)

for all
z\in\real^{3}
. It follows that on
3
,

(3.5)

1\equiv\big(\zeta_{\delta}+\theta_{\delta}\big)\big(\zeta_{\epsilon}+\theta_{\epsilon}\big)=\zeta_{\delta}+\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon}+\theta_{\epsilon}.

For any
A,B\subset\{2,\dots,N\}
with
A\cap B=\emptyset
we define

\tau_{A,B}(\mathbb{x})=\prod_{j\in A}\zeta_{\delta}(x_{1}-x_{j})\prod_{j\in B}(\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon})(x_{1}-x_{j})\prod_{j\in\{2,\dots,N\}\backslash(A\cup B)}\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{1}-x_{j})

for
\mathbb{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\real^{3N}
. Therefore,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\\
B\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\backslash A\end{subarray}}\tau_{A,B}(\mathbb{x})=\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}\big\{\zeta_{\delta}(x_{1}-x_{j})+(\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon})(x_{1}-x_{j})+\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{1}-x_{j})\big\}=1

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
.
Let
\Xi=\{(j,k):2\leq j<k\leq N\}
. For each
Y,Z\subset\Xi
with
Y\cap Z=\emptyset
we define

T_{Y,Z}(\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\prod_{(j,k)\in Y}\zeta_{\delta}(x_{j}-x_{k})\prod_{(j,k)\in Z}(\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon})(x_{j}-x_{k})\prod_{(j,k)\,\in\,\Xi\backslash(Y\cup Z)}\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})

for
\mathbb{\hat{x}}=(x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in\real^{3N-3}
. Therefore,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\,\subset\,\Xi\\
Z\,\subset\,\Xi\backslash Y\end{subarray}}T_{Y,Z}(\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\prod_{2\leq j<k\leq N}\big(\zeta_{\delta}(x_{j}-x_{k})+(\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon})(x_{j}-x_{k})+\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})\big)=1

for all
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
. Overall, for all
x,y\in\real^{3}
,
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\\
B\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\backslash A\end{subarray}}\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\\
D\subset\{2,\dots,N\}\backslash C\end{subarray}}\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\,\subset\,\Xi\\
Z\,\subset\,\Xi\backslash Y\end{subarray}}\tau_{A,B}(x,\mathbb{\hat{x}})\tau_{C,D}(y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})T_{Y,Z}(\mathbb{\hat{x}})=1,

which is a sum of cutoffs of the form (
3.3
).

∎

3.2.
Clusters corresponding to
\Phi

To each
\Phi
we now introduce three corresponding clusters. To define these, we first introduce index sets based solely on the choice of cutoff factors
g^{(1)}_{j},g^{(2)}_{j}
and
f_{k,l}
in the definition (
3.3
) of
\Phi
. The index sets and clusters are therefore not dependent on
x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}}
nor on the scaling parameters
\delta
and
\epsilon
.

We define the index set
L\subset\{(j,k)\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}:j\neq k\}
as follows.

•

We have
(j,k)\in L
if
f_{j,k}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
for
j,k=2,\dots,N
. Also
(1,j),(j,1)\in L
if
(g^{(1)}_{j},g^{(2)}_{j})\neq(\theta_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon})
for
j=2,\dots,N
.

Furthermore, we define two more index sets
J,K\subset\{(j,k)\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}:j\neq k\}
as follows.

•

We have
(j,k)\in J
if
f_{j,k}=\zeta_{\delta}
for
j,k=2,\dots,N
. Also
(1,j),(j,1)\in J
if
g^{(1)}_{j}=\zeta_{\delta}
for
j=2,\dots,N
.

•

We have
(j,k)\in K
if
f_{j,k}=\zeta_{\delta}
for
j,k=2,\dots,N
. Also
(1,j),(j,1)\in K
if
g^{(2)}_{j}=\zeta_{\delta}
for
j=2,\dots,N
.

These index sets obey
J,K\subset L
.
For an arbitrary index set
I\subset\{(j,k)\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}:j\neq k\}
we say that two indices
j,k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
are
I
-
linked
if either
j=k
, or
(j,k)\in I
, or there exist pairwise distinct indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}
for
1\leq s\leq N-2
, all distinct from
j
and
k
such that
(j,j_{1}),(j_{1},j_{2}),\dots,(j_{s},k)\in I
.

The cluster
Q=Q(\Phi)
is defined as the set of all indices
L
-linked to
1
. The cluster
P=P(\Phi)
is defined as the set of all indices
J
-linked to
1
. The cluster
S=S(\Phi)
is defined as the set of all indices
K
-linked to
1
. Since
J,K\subset L
we see that
P,S\subset Q
.

3.3.
Support of
\Phi

The following lemma shows that on the support of
\Phi
, the cluster
P^{*}:=P\backslash\{1\}
represents a set of particles whose positions
x_{j}
,
j\in P^{*}
, are close to
x
, and the cluster
S^{*}:=S\backslash\{1\}
represents a set of particles whose positions
x_{j}
,
j\in S^{*}
, are close to
y
. In both cases this closeness is with respect to the parameter
\delta
. On the support of
\Phi
, the cluster
Q^{*}:=Q\backslash\{1\}
(recall
P,S\subset Q
) represents a set of particles whose positions
x_{j}
,
j\in Q^{*}
, are close to both
x
and
y
, albeit held potentially looser since this closeness is with respect to the larger parameter
\epsilon
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=0
unless

(3.6)

\displaystyle|x-x_{k}|,\,|y-x_{k}|,\,|x_{j}-x_{k}|

\displaystyle>(4N)^{-1}\epsilon\qquad

\displaystyle j\in Q^{*},k\in Q^{c},

(3.7)

\displaystyle|x-x_{j}|,|y-x_{k}|

\displaystyle>(4N)^{-1}\delta\quad

\displaystyle j\in P^{c},\,k\in S^{c},

(3.8)

\displaystyle|x_{r}-x_{s}|

\displaystyle>(4N)^{-1}\delta\quad

\displaystyle r\in P^{*},\,s\in P^{c}\text{ or }r\in S^{*},\,s\in S^{c}.

Proof.

Take
j\in Q^{*}
and
k\in Q^{c}
. By the definition of the cluster
Q
we have
f_{j,k}=\theta_{\epsilon}
and
g^{(1)}_{k}=g^{(2)}_{k}=\theta_{\epsilon}
in the formula (
3.3
) defining
\Phi
. If we assume
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\neq 0
, then the support criteria for
\theta_{\epsilon}(x-x_{k}),\theta_{\epsilon}(y-x_{k})
and
\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})
give (
3.6
). The inequalities (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) are proven in a similar way.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
0<2\delta\leq\epsilon
. Then
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbb{x}})=0
unless

(3.9)

\displaystyle|x-x_{k}|<\delta/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in P^{*},

(3.10)

\displaystyle|y-x_{k}|<\delta/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in S^{*},

and if
|x-y|\geq\delta
then
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbb{x}})=0
unless

(3.11)

\displaystyle|y-x_{k}|>\delta/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in P^{*},

(3.12)

\displaystyle|x-x_{k}|>\delta/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in S^{*}.

Therefore, if
P^{*}\cap S^{*}\neq\emptyset
then
\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=0
for all
|x-y|\geq\delta
and
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
.

Furthermore, if
|x|,|y|\geq\epsilon
then
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbb{x}})=0
unless

(3.13)

\displaystyle\min\{|x-x_{k}|,\,|y-x_{k}|\}<\epsilon/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in Q^{*},

(3.14)

\displaystyle|x_{k}|>\epsilon/2\quad\text{ for }\quad k\in Q^{*}.

Proof.

By definition, if
k\in P^{*}
either
g^{(1)}_{k}=\zeta_{\delta}
or there exist pairwise distinct
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\in\{2,\dots,N\}
with
1\leq s\leq N-2
such that
g^{(1)}_{j_{1}}=\zeta_{\delta}
and
f_{j_{1},j_{2}},f_{j_{2},j_{3}},\,\dots,\,f_{j_{s},k}=\zeta_{\delta}
. In the former case, support criteria for
\zeta_{\delta}(x-x_{k})
gives
|x-x_{k}|<(2N)^{-1}\delta
. In the latter case, support conditions give
|x-x_{j_{1}}|,\,|x_{j_{1}}-x_{j_{2}}|,\,\dots,\,|x_{j_{s}}-x_{k}|<(2N)^{-1}\delta
and so by the triangle inequality
|x-x_{k}|<\delta/2
. Now,
|y-x_{k}|\geq|x-y|-|x-x_{k}|>\delta/2
by the reverse triangle inequality. The case of
k\in S^{*}
is analogous.

Now let
k\in Q^{*}
. First, we consider the case where either
g^{(1)}_{k}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
or
g^{(2)}_{k}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
, or both. Without loss, assume
g^{(1)}_{k}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
. Then either
g^{(1)}_{k}=\zeta_{\delta}
or
g^{(1)}_{k}=\theta_{\delta}\zeta_{\epsilon}
, but both give the inequality

|x-x_{k}|<(2N)^{-1}\epsilon

by support criteria. Hence (
3.13
) holds in this case.
Now suppose that
g^{(1)}_{k}=g^{(2)}_{k}=\theta_{\epsilon}
. Then there must exist pairwise distinct
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\in\{2,\dots,N\}
with
1\leq s\leq N-2
such that
f_{j_{1},j_{2}},f_{j_{2},j_{3}},\dots,f_{j_{s},k}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
and either
g^{(1)}_{j_{1}}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
or
g^{(2)}_{j_{1}}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
. Without loss take
g^{(1)}_{j_{1}}\neq\theta_{\epsilon}
, so that, as before,
|x-x_{j_{1}}|<(2N)^{-1}\epsilon
. Similarly, we get
|x_{j_{1}}-x_{j_{2}}|,\,\dots,\,|x_{j_{s}}-x_{k}|<(2N)^{-1}\epsilon
. Therefore, by the triangle inequality,
|x-x_{k}|<\epsilon/2
. Hence completing the proof of (
3.13
). Finally, (
3.14
) follows from (
3.13
) and
|x|,|y|\geq\epsilon
.
∎

3.4.
Factorisation of cutoffs

Let
\Phi
be given by (
3.3
). We can define a
partial product
of
\Phi
as a function of the form

(3.15)

\Phi^{\prime}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\prod_{j\in R_{1}}g^{(1)}_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{j\in R_{2}}g^{(2)}_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{(k,l)\in R_{3}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l})

where
R_{1},R_{2}\subset\{2,\dots,N\}
,
R_{3}\subset\{(k,l):2\leq k<l\leq N\}
.

We now define classes of partial products of
\Phi
which corresponding to a cluster. Let
T
be an arbitrary cluster with
1\in T
. Then,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in T^{*}}g^{(1)}_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{j\in T^{*}}g^{(2)}_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in T^{*}\\
k<l\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

(3.17)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T^{c})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in T^{c}\\
k<l\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

(3.18)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T,T^{c})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in T^{c}}g^{(1)}_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{j\in T^{c}}g^{(2)}_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in T^{*}\\
l\in T^{c}\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l}).

Formulae (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) are separate definitions in the cases where the cluster contains
1
and does not contain
1
, respectively.

If we consider both
x
and
y
to adopt the role of particle
1
then these functions can be interpreted as
\Phi(\,\cdot\,;T)
involving only pairs of particles in
T
,
\Phi(\,\cdot\,;T^{c})
involving only pairs in
T^{c}
, and
\Phi(\,\cdot\,;T,T^{c})
involving pairs where one particle lies in
T
and the other lies in
T^{c}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Given a cutoff
\Phi
of the form (
3.3
) and any cluster
T
with
1\in T
we have

(3.19)

\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T)\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T,T^{c})\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};T^{c}).

Proof.

This identity follows from the definitions (
3.16
)-(
3.18
) and the equality

\prod_{2\leq k<l\leq N}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in T^{*}\\
k<l\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in T^{*}\\
l\in T^{c}\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\in T^{c}\\
k<l\end{subarray}}f_{k,l}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

since for any
2\leq k<l\leq N
we have
f_{k,l}=f_{l,k}
by definition.
∎

Let
Q=Q(\Phi)
. Then the partial product
\Phi(\,\cdot\,;Q,Q^{c})
consists of only
\theta_{\epsilon}
cutoff factors, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
Q=Q(\Phi)
,

(3.20)

\Phi(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}};Q,Q^{c})=\prod_{j\in Q^{c}}\theta_{\epsilon}(x-x_{j})\prod_{j\in Q^{c}}\theta_{\epsilon}(y-x_{j})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in Q^{*}\\
l\in Q^{c}\end{subarray}}\theta_{\epsilon}(x_{k}-x_{l}).

Proof.

By the definition of the cluster
Q(\Phi)
, we have
g^{(1)}_{j}=g^{(2)}_{j}=\theta_{\epsilon}
for each
j\in Q^{c}
, and
f_{k,l}=\theta_{\epsilon}
for each
k\in Q^{*}
and

. The formula then follows from (
3.18
).
∎

3.5.
Derivatives

The definition of cluster derivatives, (
1.15
), is modestly extended to allow action on cutoffs. For any cluster
T
we define the following three cluster derivatives which can act on functions of
x
,
y
and
\mathbb{\hat{x}}
, such as
\Phi
. We set

(3.21)

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{x,T}=\partial^{\alpha}_{x}+\sum_{j\in T^{*}}\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad D^{\alpha}_{y,T}=\partial^{\alpha}_{y}+\sum_{j\in T^{*}}\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}}\quad\text{for }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3},\,|\alpha|=1,

(3.22)

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{x,y,T}=\partial^{\alpha}_{x}+\partial^{\alpha}_{y}+\sum_{j\in T^{*}}\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}}\quad\text{for }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3},\,|\alpha|=1,

which are extended to higher order multiindices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
by successive application of first-order derivatives, as in (
1.16
).

The following lemma gives partial derivative estimates of the cutoff factors. We will require the following elementary result. For each
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
and
s\in\real
there exists
C>0
such that for any
x_{0}\in\real^{3}
we get
\big|\partial_{x}^{\sigma}|x+x_{0}|^{s}\big|\leq C|x+x_{0}|^{s-|\sigma|}
for all
x\in\real^{3}
,
x\neq-x_{0}
. We use the standard notation
\mathds{1}_{S}
to denote the indicator function on a set
S
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
with
|\sigma|\geq 1
and any
t>0
there exists
C
, depending on
\sigma
but independent of
t
, such that

(3.23)

|\partial^{\sigma}\zeta_{t}(x)|,\,|\partial^{\sigma}\theta_{t}(x)|\leq Ct^{-|\sigma|}\mathds{1}_{\{(4N)^{-1}t<|x|<(2N)^{-1}t\}}(x)

for all
x\in\real^{3}
.

Proof.

Without loss we consider the case of
\zeta_{t}
, the case of
\theta_{t}
being similar. Recall
\xi=\xi(s)
was defined in (
3.1
) and we denote by
\xi^{(m)}
the
m
-th (univariate) derivative of
\xi
. Since
|\sigma|\geq 1
the chain rule shows that
\partial^{\sigma}\zeta_{t}(x)
can be written as a sum of terms of the form

(3.24)

(4Nt^{-1})^{m}\,\xi^{(m)}\big(4N|x|t^{-1}\big)\,\partial^{\sigma_{1}}|x|\,\dots\,\partial^{\sigma_{m}}|x|

where
1\leq m\leq|\sigma|
, and
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
are non-zero multiindices obeying

\sigma_{1}+\dots+\sigma_{m}=\sigma.

Since
m\geq 1
, we have that if
\xi^{(m)}(s)\neq 0
then
s\in(1,2)
. Therefore, for any term (
3.24
) to be non-zero we require that

(3.25)

(4N)^{-1}t<|x|<(2N)^{-1}t.

By the remark preceeding the current lemma, there exists
C
, dependent on
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}
, such that

\displaystyle\partial^{\sigma_{1}}|x|\,\dots\,\partial^{\sigma_{m}}|x|\leq C|x|^{m-|\sigma|}\leq C(4N)^{|\sigma|-m}t^{m-|\sigma|},

using (
3.25
). Therefore, the terms (
3.24
) can readily be bounded to give the desired result.
∎

We now give bounds for the cluster derivatives (
3.21
)-(
3.22
) acting on cutoffs.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
Q=Q(\Phi)
. Then
D^{\alpha}_{x,y,Q}\Phi(\,\cdot\,;Q)\equiv 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
.

Proof.

By the chain rule, each function in the product (
3.16
) for
\Phi(\,\cdot\,;Q)
has zero derivative upon action of
D^{\alpha}_{x,y,Q}
.
∎

For the next result we will use the following function, defined for all
t>0
by

(3.26)

\displaystyle M_{t}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})=\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\mathds{1}_{\{(4N)^{-1}t<|x-x_{j}|<(2N)^{-1}t\}}(x-x_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\mathds{1}_{\{(4N)^{-1}t<|y-x_{j}|<(2N)^{-1}t\}}(y-x_{j})+\sum_{2\leq k<l\leq N}\mathds{1}_{\{(4N)^{-1}t<|x_{k}-x_{l}|<(2N)^{-1}t\}}(x_{k}-x_{l})

for
x,y\in\real^{3}
and
\mathbb{\hat{x}}\in\real^{3N-3}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
0<2\delta\leq\epsilon
and
\Phi=\Phi_{\delta,\epsilon}
be a cutoff of the form (
3.3
). Let
Q=Q(\Phi)
. Then for any multiindex
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3N+3}
there exists
C
, dependent on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
but independent of
\delta
and
\epsilon
, such that for any partial products
\Phi^{\prime}
of
\Phi
we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\Phi^{\prime}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})|\leq\begin{cases}C\epsilon^{-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}&\text{ if }\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi(\,\cdot\,;Q,Q^{c})\\
C\big(\epsilon^{-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}+\delta^{-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}M_{\delta}(x,y,\mathbb{\hat{x}})\big)&\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Lemma
3.6
gives bounds for the partial derivatives of the functions
\zeta_{\delta},\theta_{\delta},\zeta_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon}
. Considering

Appendix A
Second derivatives of
\phi

In
[
22
]
it was shown that
\phi

2.7

1.3

The above authors introduce the following functions

\displaystyle\tilde{F}

\displaystyle=-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi(|x_{j}|)|x_{j}|+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\xi(|x_{j}-x_{k}|)|x_{j}-x_{k}|,

\displaystyle\tilde{G}

\displaystyle=C_{0}Z\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\xi(|x_{j}|)\xi(|x_{k}|)(x_{j}\cdot x_{k})\log\big(|x_{j}|^{2}+|x_{k}|^{2}\big),

where
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\real)
is defined in (
3.1
) and
C_{0}=(2-\pi)/12\pi
. The authors obtain
e^{-\tilde{F}-\tilde{G}}\psi\in W_{loc}^{2,\infty}(\real^{3N})
,
[
22

. We note that this is an improvement upon the well-known fact that
\phi\in C^{1,\theta}(\real^{3N})
for all
\theta\in(0,1)
, see Section
2

For our purposes, it will be convenient to restate their results using a slightly different form of the exponential factor. We define

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}=e^{-F-G}\psi

where
F=F_{c}-F_{s}
was defined in (
2.1
) and

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=C_{0}Z\sum_{j<k}(x_{j}\cdot x_{k})\log\big(|x_{j}|^{2}+|x_{k}|^{2}\big)-C_{0}Z\sum_{j<k}(x_{j}\cdot x_{k})\log\big(|x_{j}|^{2}+|x_{k}|^{2}+1\big)

\displaystyle=:G_{c}-G_{s}.

We can then write
\phi^{\prime}=e^{H}e^{-\tilde{F}-\tilde{G}}\psi
for

\displaystyle H=-F_{c}+F_{s}+\tilde{F}-G_{c}+G_{s}+\tilde{G}.

It can be verified, with the help of (
2.5
) and (
2.6

\partial^{\alpha}H\in L^{\infty}(\real^{3N})
for all
|\alpha|\leq 2

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3N}
. Therefore, we can restate
[
22
, Theorem 1.5]
as follows.

Theorem A.1

(S. Fournais, M. and T. Hoffmann-Ostenhof, T.Ø. Sørensen)
.

For all
0<r<R
we have a constant
C
, depending on
r
and
R
, such that

(A.1)

\left\lVert\phi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r))}\leq C\left\lVert\phi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}

for all
\mathbb{x}\in\real^{3N}
. The constant does not depend on
\mathbf{x}
.

Remark
.

We use the equivalence of the spaces
C^{1,1}(\overline{B})
and
W^{2,\infty}(B)
in the case of an open ball
B
, see for example
[
23
]
.

The following lemma can be verified by straightforward calculations. Here,
e_{i}
represents the
i
-th unit basis vector in
3
,
i=1,2,3
.

Lemma A.2
.

G,\nabla G\in L^{\infty}(\real^{3N})
. Also, for all
j,k=1,\dots N
and
r,s\in\{1,2,3\}
there exists some
C
such that

\displaystyle|\partial^{e_{r}}_{x_{j}}\partial^{e_{s}}_{x_{k}}G(\mathbf{x})|\leq\begin{cases}C\big(1-\log\big(\min\{1,\,|x_{j}|^{2}+|x_{k}|^{2}\}\big)\big)&\text{ if }j\neq k\text{ and }r=s\\
C&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
\phi
does not contain the additional exponential factor involving
G
, we do not expect it’s second order derivatives to be bounded. However, we obtain the following corollary of Theorem
A.1
to obtain pointwise bounds showing the second order derivatives of
\phi
have only logarithmic singularities. We recall that
\nu_{P}
was defined in (
1.22
).

Corollary A.3
.

For all
0<r<R<1
there exists
C
, depending on
r
and
R
, such that for any non-empty cluster
P
and any
\eta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
with
|\eta|=1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x})))}\leq C(1-\log\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x}))\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},R))}

for all
\mathbb{x}
with
\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})>0
.

Remark A.4
.

By the definitions, it is immediate that
\nu_{P}\geq\lambda_{P}
and hence, in particular, the above inequality holds on
\Sigma^{c}_{P}
.

Proof.

By the definition of cluster derivatives, (
1.15
), it follows from Lemma
A.2
that there is some
C
such that

(A.2)

\displaystyle|D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla G(\mathbf{x})|\leq C(1-\log(\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})))

for all
\mathbf{x}
with
\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})>0
.

By (
1.23
), we get that
(1-r)\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x})\leq\nu_{P}(\mathbb{y})
for all
\mathbb{y}\in B(\mathbb{x},r\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x}))
. Therefore, for
C
as in (
A.2
), we have for all
\mathbb{x}
with
\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})>0
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla G\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x})))}

\displaystyle\leq C(1-\log(1-r)-\log(\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})))

(A.4)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(1-\log(\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})))

for some
C^{\prime}
depending on
r
.

We recall from the definition, (
2.7
), that
\phi=e^{-F}\psi
. Therefore
\phi=e^{G}\phi^{\prime}
. Then, we have
\nabla\phi=e^{G}\phi^{\prime}\nabla G+e^{G}\nabla\phi^{\prime}
. And therefore the following formula holds,

\displaystyle D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla\phi=\big(D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla G+D_{P}^{\eta}G\,\nabla G\big)e^{G}\phi^{\prime}+e^{G}D_{P}^{\eta}\phi^{\prime}\,\nabla G+e^{G}D_{P}^{\eta}G\,\nabla\phi^{\prime}+e^{G}D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla\phi^{\prime}.

Taking the norm and using that
G,\nabla G\in L^{\infty}
by Lemma
A.2
we can then obtain
C
such that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{P}^{\eta}\nabla\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x})))}

\displaystyle\leq C(1-\log(\nu_{P}(\mathbf{x})))\left\lVert\phi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}(B(\mathbb{x},r\nu_{P}(\mathbb{x})))}.

Using that
\nu_{P}\leq 1
, we then apply Theorem
A.1
followed by a use of the equality
\phi=e^{G}\phi^{\prime}
to obtain the required result.
