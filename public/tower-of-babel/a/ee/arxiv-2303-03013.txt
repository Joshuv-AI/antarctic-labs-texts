Minimal rational curves on complete symmetric varieties
Michel Brion, Shin-young Kim, Nicolas Perrin

Abstract
We describe the families of minimal rational curves on any complete symmetric variety, and the corresponding varieties of minimal rational tangents (VMRT). In particular, we prove that these varieties are homogeneous and that for non-exceptional indecomposable wonderful varieties, there is a unique family of minimal rational curves, and hence a unique VMRT. We relate these results to the restricted root system of the associated symmetric space.

Introduction

Let
X
be a projective uniruled variety over the field of complex numbers.
An irreducible family
{\mathcal{K}}
of rational curves
on
X
is called
a
covering family
if there is a member of
{\mathcal{K}}

passing through a general point
x\in X
.
If in addition the subfamily
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
of curves in
{\mathcal{K}}

passing through
x
is projective, then
{\mathcal{K}}
is called a

family of minimal rational curves
.

These curves play a prominent role in the study of the variety
X
.
There is a rational map
\tau_{x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
sending a curve to its tangent direction at
x
and
the closure of its image
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
is an important invariant of
X
called
the variety of minimal rational tangents
or VMRT of
X
, see
[
Hw01
]
,
[
HM04
]
and references therein.

The families of minimal rational curves on projective rational homogeneous spaces
G/P
for
G
reductive and
P
a parabolic subgroup are well understood. For example, if
G/P
has Picard rank
1
, then there is a unique family of minimal rational curves which was used to prove its rigidity in a series of papers by Hwang and Mok (see e.g.
[
HM02
,
HM05
]
), with the unique exception of
B_{3}/P_{2}
which admits an explicit degeneration constructed in
[
PP10
]
(see also
[
HL24
]
).
If the Picard number of
G/P
is greater than
1
, there are several families of minimal rational curves.

In
[
BF15
]
, the authors
consider another case where
X
has a large Picard group, namely the wonderful compactifications of adjoint simple groups. Suprisingly, they prove that there is a unique family of minimal rational curves for any such wonderful compactification and also that the corresponding VMRT is a rational homogeneous variety. These results were used in
[
FL20
]
to prove the rigidity of wonderful compactifications of semisimple groups, under the condition that the special fiber is Fano.

In this paper we generalize the results of
[
BF15
]
and describe the families of minimal rational curves on any complete symmetric variety. Rigidity of symmetric varieties of Picard number
1
has already attracted some attention (see
[
KP19
]
and
[
CFL23
]
), we hope that our results will open new directions for higher Picard numbers.

To state our main results, we recall basic definitions and properties of complete symmetric varieties. Let
G
be a connected reductive group and let
\sigma
be a group involution of
G
. A symmetric subgroup is a closed subgroup

H\subset G
such that
G^{\sigma,0}\subset H\subset G^{\sigma}
,
where
G^{\sigma,0}
denotes the neutral component of the
algebraic group
G^{\sigma}
.
The homogeneous space
G/H
is a
symmetric space
.
We denote by
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
the Lie algebras of
G
and
H
.
Note the decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
as
H
-representations,
where
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{g}^{\sigma}
.

Consider the normalizer
N=\N_{G}(H)
; the quotient
G/N
is the

adjoint homogeneous space
of the symmetric space
G/H
.
The homogeneous space
G/N
admits by
[
deCP83
]

a unique wonderful compactification
X_{\rm ad}
.
This is a smooth projective
G
-variety having an open dense orbit

G\cdot x_{\rm ad}=X_{\rm ad}^{0}\simeq G/N
, such that the boundary

\partial X_{\rm ad}=X_{\rm ad}\setminus X^{0}_{\rm ad}
is a simple normal
crossing divisor:

\partial X_{\rm ad}=X_{\rm ad}^{1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{\rm ad}^{r}

where
X_{\rm ad}^{i}
is a prime
G
-stable divisor for all
i\in[1,r]
.
Furthermore for any
y,z\in X_{\rm ad}
we have
G\cdot y=G\cdot z

if and only if
\{i\ |y\in X_{\rm ad}^{i}\}=\{i\ |z\in X_{\rm ad}^{i}\}
.
The integer
r
is
the rank
of
G/N
.
A
complete symmetric variety
is a smooth projective
G
-variety

X
having a dense orbit
G\cdot x=X^{0}\simeq G/H

such that the natural map
G/H\to G/N\subset X_{\rm ad}

extends to a
G
-equivariant morphism
\pi:X\to X_{\rm ad}
.
(We do not assume that
X
contains a unique closed
G
-orbit).
The boundary
\partial X=X\setminus X^{0}
is also a simple
normal crossing divisor with
G
-stable prime components.

Let
X
be a complete symmetric variety with base point
x

and map
\pi:X\to X_{\rm ad}
,
and let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of minimal rational curves on
X
.
We will prove the following results.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is smooth and
\tau_{x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
is an isomorphism.

In particular, understanding the VMRT as an abstract variety is equivalent to understanding
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
. If the map
\pi
contracts curves of the family
{\mathcal{K}}
, then the description of the VMRT follows easily from the case of toric varieties treated in
[
CFH14
]
, see Lemma
3.4
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\pi
contracts a curve of
{\mathcal{K}}
, then
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
is a linear subspace
of
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma})
.

We are therefore left to consider curves not contracted by
\pi
.

Theorem 1.3

(Proposition
3.6
and Remark
4.24
)
.

Assume that
\pi
contracts no curve in
{\mathcal{K}}
and let
C\in{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
.

1.

There exists a unique family of minimal rational curves
{\mathcal{L}}

in
X_{\rm ad}
such that
\pi
maps curves of
{\mathcal{K}}
to curves of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

2.

We have

1\leq\partial X\cdot C\leq\partial X_{\rm ad}\cdot\pi(C)\leq 2
.

3.

If
\partial X\cdot C=\partial X_{\rm ad}\cdot\pi(C)
, then every component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is isomorphic to
a component of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
. Otherwise,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is isomorphic to
a divisor of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
.

One of the key ingredients for proving the above results consists
of the highest weight curves,
introduced in
[
BF15
, Section 2]
and studied further in Subsection
2.6
.
Given a Borel subgroup
B_{H}
of
H
, the Borel Fixed Point Theorem implies that every irreducible component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
contains a
B_{H}
-fixed point
C
. Moreover, if
C

is not contracted by
\pi
,
then
C
is mapped to a
B_{H}
-fixed point
C_{\rm ad}
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
that determines the associated component of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
. Furthermore,
the tangent space at
x_{\rm ad}
of the highest weight curve
C_{\rm ad}
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}

is a highest weight line in
T_{x_{\rm ad}}(X_{\rm ad})
.

In view of the above results, we focus on wonderful compactifications of adjoint symmetric spaces. Decomposing
G
into a product of indecomposable
\sigma
-stable factors, we obtain
a decomposition of
G/N
into a product of
indecomposable symmetric spaces
. There are three possible types for these indecomposable factors (see Subsection
2.5
for more details):

1.

Group type
:
(H\times H)/{\rm diag}(H)
, where
H
is simple.

2.

Hermitian type
:
G/\N_{G}(L)
, where
G
is simple and

L\subset G
is a Levi subgroup.

3.

Simple type
:
G/H
, where
G
is simple and
H^{0}
is semisimple.

Given a highest weight curve
C
on
X
, we prove that there is a unique
indecomposable factor
X_{C}
of
X_{{\rm ad}}
such that the composition of
\pi:X\to X_{{\rm ad}}
with the projection
X_{{\rm ad}}\to X_{C}
sends
C
isomorphically to its image. We may thus replace
X_{\rm ad}
by
X_{C}
and assume that
X_{\rm ad}
is indecomposable.
In particular
G/N
is as in one of the above three cases. To understand the geometry of the indecomposable factors, we use the
restricted root system
.

There exists a maximal torus
T_{{\rm s}}
, called of
split type
, such that
T_{{\rm s}}
is
\sigma
-stable and
the subtorus

S=\{t\in T_{{\rm s}}\ |\ \sigma(t)=t^{-1}\}^{0}
has maximal dimension. The root system
R
of
(G,T_{{\rm s}})
is stable under the action of
\sigma
and there is a basis
\Delta
of
R
such that, for
\alpha\in\Delta
, either
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
or
\sigma(\alpha)<0
. Set
\Delta_{1}=\{\alpha\in\Delta\ |\ \sigma(\alpha)<0\}
and
{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)
. The set
\overline{R}=\{{\overline{\alpha}}\ |\ \alpha\in R\}
is a (possibly non-reduced) root system with basis
\overline{\Delta}=\{{\overline{\alpha}}\ |\ \alpha\in\Delta_{1}\}
called the
restricted root system
of the symmetric space. The rank of
\overline{R}
is
the rank
r
of
G/H
. In Subsection
4.2
, we relate curves and divisors in
X_{\rm ad}
to the restricted root system (the results are probably well known to the experts but we could not find a convenient reference). Let
\overline{R}^{\vee}
be the dual root system of
\overline{R}

with basis
\overline{\Delta}^{\vee}
and
coroot lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\Delta}^{\vee}
, and denote by
A_{1}(X)
the Chow group of curves modulo rational equivalence. We prove the following result.

Proposition 1.4

(Proposition
4.16
)
.

There is a surjective
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear map

\psi:A_{1}(X_{\rm ad})\to{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\Delta}^{\vee}
such that:

1.

The image of the monoid of effective curves is the monoid generated by the positive coroots.

2.

The image of the monoid of curves having non-negative intersection with any component of
\partial X_{\rm ad}
is the intersection of
{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\Delta}^{\vee}
with the monoid of dominant cocharacters.

If the map
\psi
is not injective, then
X_{\rm ad}
is called
exceptional
(see Propositions
4.13
and
4.12
for more details on this case). Since the class of a curve
C
in a covering family
{\mathcal{L}}
of rational curves is effective and has non-negative intersection with any component of
\partial X_{\rm ad}
, it has to be contained in the intersection of the monoids generated by
the positive coroots and by the dominant cocharacters. There is a unique minimal such element
{\overline{\Theta}^{\vee}}
, the coroot of the highest root
{\overline{\Theta}}\in\overline{R}
. This gives a very natural candidate for classes of minimal rational curves. Indeed we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.5

(Corollary
4.17
)
.

Assume that
X_{\rm ad}
is indecomposable.

1.

If
X_{\rm ad}
is not exceptional, there is a unique family
of minimal rational curves
{\mathcal{L}}
and the class of any
C_{\rm ad}\in{\mathcal{L}}
satisfies
\psi([C_{\rm ad}])={\overline{\Theta}}^{\vee}
.

2.

If
X_{\rm ad}
is exceptional, then there are exactly two such families
{\mathcal{L}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{-}
and the class of any
C_{\rm ad}^{\pm}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}
satifies
\psi([C_{\rm ad}^{\pm}])={\overline{\Theta}}^{\vee}
.

Note that if
C_{\rm ad}
is a highest weight curve in
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
, then its

-orbit
H\cdot C_{\rm ad}
is contained in
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
. We describe the family
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
by comparing the dimension of this orbit with the dimension of the family
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{\rm ad}}
of curves whose class is described by the previous result. To compute the dimension of the
H
-orbits, we prove that the tangent line

lies in very specific nilpotent orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{\min}
be the minimal non-zero nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{g}
and

{\mathcal{O}}_{{{\rm sum}},\sigma}
be the nilpotent orbit of

e_{\Theta}-\sigma(e_{\Theta})
,
where
\Theta
is the highest root of

and

.
Let

, we prove
the following in Corollary

.

Proposition 1.6
.

We have

if

Using results of Kostant and Rallis

we prove that the orbit

is Lagrangian in the nilpotent orbit

(equipped with the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau invariant symplectic structure). Using this, we compute the dimension of these orbits and obtain:

(Theorem

)
.

1.

If the restricted root system

If

The orbit

Note that these Fano varieties have Picard number

, whereas there are examples of Fano

In Table

Rational curves and symmetric spaces

In this section, we recall basic results on rational curves on uniruled varieties and then specialise to the case of almost homogeneous varieties. We also introduce symmetric homogeneous spaces and their adjoint symmetric space, and we obtain
the existence of highest weight curves and their basic properties.

2.1
Families of rational curves

In this subsection, we recall some notions and results
on rational curves, after
[
Ko96
, Sections II.2.2 and II.2.3]

and
[
BK21
, Sections 2.1 and 2.2]
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety. Consider the scheme
of morphisms
\Hom({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
and the open subscheme

\Hom_{{\rm bir}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
consisting of morphisms which are
birational onto their image.
The (normalized)
space of rational curves

\RatCurves(X)
is the quotient of the normalization

\Hom^{{\rm n}}_{{\rm bir}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
by the free action of

{\rm Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})={\rm PGL}_{2}
via reparametrization. We have
a universal family

\rho:\Univ(X)\longrightarrow\RatCurves(X)

which is a
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundle, and an evaluation map

\mu:\Univ(X)\longrightarrow X

such that the morphism

\rho\times\mu:\Univ(X)\to\RatCurves(X)\times X

is finite.

Let
f\in\Hom_{{\rm bir}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
with image
C\subset X
.
We say that
C
is
free
if the pull-back

f^{*}(T_{X})
is globally generated, where
T_{X}
denotes
the tangent bundle. Every free morphism yields a smooth
point of
\Hom_{{\rm bir}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
, and hence of

\RatCurves(X)
. Also, we say that
C
is
embedded

if
f
is
an isomorphism to its image; equivalently,
C
is smooth.
The free (resp. embedded free)
curves form smooth open subschemes

\RatCurves_{{\rm emfr}}(X)\subset\RatCurves_{{\rm fr}}(X)

of the space of rational curves.

A
family of rational curves
on
X

is a component
{\mathcal{K}}
of
\RatCurves(X)
.
We then have a universal family

\rho:{\mathcal{U}}=\rho^{-1}({\mathcal{K}})\to{\mathcal{K}}
which is again
a
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundle, and an evaluation map

\mu:{\mathcal{U}}\to X
.
For any
x\in X
, let
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}=\mu^{-1}(x)
and

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}=\rho({\mathcal{U}}_{x})
; then
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is the subfamily
of curves through
x
. The restriction

\rho_{x}:{\mathcal{U}}_{x}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is finite, and is an isomorphism
above the smooth open subset of embedded free curves
(see
[
BK21
, Lemma 2.1]
).

The family
{\mathcal{K}}
is
covering
if
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is non-empty
for
x
general. If in addition
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is projective
for
x
general, we say that
{\mathcal{K}}
is a

family of minimal rational curves
.

By sending every embedded free curve in
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
to
its tangent direction at
x
, we obtain a morphism

\tau_{x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{{\rm emfr},x}\to{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
,
where
{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
denotes the projectivization of the
tangent space. We will view
\tau
as a rational map

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
, defined at every curve
which is smooth at
x
. The closure of the image
of
\tau
is denoted by
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
and called the

variety of tangents
of
{\mathcal{K}}
at
x
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of minimal rational curves on
X
.
By
[
Ke02
, Theorem 3.3]
,
for a general point
x
, there are only finitely
many curves in
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
which are singular at
x
.
Thus,
\tau_{x}
is defined along every
positive-dimensional irreducible component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
.
In view of
[
Ke02
, Theorem 3.4]
,

\tau_{x}
extends to a finite morphism

\tau_{x}^{{\rm n}}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{{\rm n}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X),

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{{\rm n}}
denotes the normalization.
Moreover,
\tau_{x}^{{\rm n}}
is birational onto its image by

[
HM04
, Theorem 1]
. The image
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
is called the

variety of minimal rational tangents
of
{\mathcal{K}}
at
x

(VMRT).

under a morphism of smooth projective varieties

\pi:X\to Y
. Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of rational curves
on
X
.
Assume that some

is represented by
a free morphism

onto its image, and such that

\pi\circ f:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to Y

birational onto its image as well.
Let

be the corresponding rational curve in
Y
,
and
{\mathcal{L}}
the family on

containing the free

. Finally,
let

Lemma 2.1

With the above notation and assumptions,

\pi_{*}:{\mathcal{K}}\dasharrow{\mathcal{L}},\quad\pi_{*,x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\dasharrow{\mathcal{L}}_{y}

.
If the differential

at

Proof.

Composing by

\Hom({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)\to\Hom({\mathbb{P}}^{1},Y)

This implies the final assertion

is injective on an open dense subset of

∎

In the opposite direction, assume that
\pi

With this terminology, we may recall a useful observation

Consider two smooth projective varieties

,
and let

with projections
p:X\to Y
,

q:X\to Z
.

1.

The pull-back map

p^{*}:\Hom({\mathbb{P}}^{1},Y)\times Z\to\Hom({\mathbb{P}}^{1},X)
,

(f,z)\to(t\mapsto(f(t),z))
induces a closed immersion

\RatCurves(Y)\times Z\to\RatCurves(X)

with image a union of components.

2.

The map
p^{*}
sends covering families
(resp. families of minimal rational curves) to covering
families (resp. families of minimal rational curves).

3.

A family of rational curves
{\mathcal{K}}
on
X

is the pull-back of a family on
Y
if and only if
q

contracts some curve in
{\mathcal{K}}
.

4.

Every family of minimal rational curves on
X

is the pull-back of a unique family of minimal rational curves
on
Y
or
Z
.

2.2
Almost homogeneous varieties

We now assume that
X
is
almost homogeneous
,
i.e., it is equipped with an action of a connected
linear algebraic group
G
, and contains an open

G
-orbit
X^{0}
. We recall and slightly generalize
results from
[
BF15
, Section 2]
and
[
BK21
, Section 2.3]
.

Choose a base point
x\in X^{0}
, and denote by

H=G_{x}
its isotropy group. Then the orbit

X^{0}=G\cdot x
is identified with the homogeneous
space
G/H
, and the pair
(X,x)
, with an equivariant
embedding of this homogeneous space. Denoting by

\mathfrak{g}
(resp.
\mathfrak{h}
) the Lie algebra of
G
(resp.
H
),
the tangent space
T_{x}X
is identified with the
quotient
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
as a representation of
H

(the
isotropy representation
).

Since
G
is a rational variety,
X
is unirational;
as a consequence, covering families exist. Also,

G
acts on
\RatCurves(X)
and on
\Univ(X)

so that
\rho
and
\mu
are equivariant. Since
G

is connected, it stabilizes every family
{\mathcal{K}}
, as
well as the open subset
{\mathcal{K}}^{0}
consisting of curves
which meet
X^{0}
. Every such curve is free (see e.g.

[
BF15
, Lemma 2.1(i)]
); thus,
{\mathcal{K}}^{0}
is smooth.
The subgroup
H\subset G
acts compatibly
on
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
,
{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}
.

We now obtain a variant of
[
BK21
, Lemma 2.4]
:

Lemma 2.3
.

A family of rational curves
{\mathcal{K}}
on
X
is covering
if and only if
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
is non-empty; equivalently,

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is non-empty. Under these assumptions,

{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
is smooth and its components are permuted
transitively by
H
.

Proof.

The morphism
\mu
restricts to a
G
-equivariant
morphism

\mu^{0}:{\mathcal{U}}^{0}=\mu^{-1}(X^{0})\longrightarrow X^{0}=G/H

with fiber at
x
being
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
. This yields an
isomorphism
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}\simeq G\times^{H}{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
,
where the right-hand side denotes the quotient
of
G\times{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
by the
H
-action via

h\cdot(g,z)=(gh^{-1},h\cdot z)
.
Since
{\mathcal{K}}^{0}
is smooth, so are
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}
and hence

{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
. Also,
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}
is irreducible; thus,

H
acts transitively on the components of
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
.
∎

Next, let
\pi:X\to Y
be a surjective morphism,
where
Y
is a smooth projective variety. Assume that

Y
is equipped with a
G
-action such that
\pi

is equivariant. Let
y=\pi(x)
and
Y^{0}=G\cdot y
;
then
Y^{0}=\pi(X^{0})
is open in
Y
. We now have
the following variants of

[
BK21
, Lemma 2.6, Remark 2.7]
:

Lemma 2.4
.

Keep the above notation and assumptions, and consider
a covering family of rational curves
{\mathcal{K}}
on
X
.
Assume that there exists
C\in{\mathcal{K}}^{0}
such that

\pi|_{C}
is birational onto its image

. Then:

1.

D\in{\mathcal{L}}
for a unique covering family
{\mathcal{L}}
of
rational curves on
Y
.

2.

\pi
induces a
G
-equivariant rational map

\pi_{*}:{\mathcal{K}}\dasharrow{\mathcal{L}}
,
which is defined at
C
and satisfies
\pi_{*}(C)=D
,
and an
H
-equivariant rational map

\pi_{*,x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\dasharrow{\mathcal{L}}_{y},\quad C\longmapsto D.

3.

We have a commutative diagram of
H
-equivariant
rational maps

\textstyle{{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{*,x}}
\scriptstyle{\tau_{x}}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{L}}_{y}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tau_{y}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d\pi_{x}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}(T_{y}Y).}

Proof.

(1) Replacing
C
with a translate
g\cdot C
for some

g\in G
, we may assume that
x\in C
. Then
the assertion follows from Lemma
2.3
.

(2) This is a consequence of Lemma
2.1
,
except for the equivariance assertions which are
easily checked.

(3) This follows readily from the definitions.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

If
\pi
is birational (equivalently, it induces an isomorphism
X^{0}\to Y^{0}
), then the assumptions of Lemma
2.4
hold and moreover
\pi_{*,x}
is an immersion.
Indeed,
\pi_{*,x}
is clearly an injective morphism.
Moreover, the differential of
\pi_{*,x}
at every

is injective by Lemma
2.1
.

Still considering a covering family of rational curves

{\mathcal{K}}
on
X
, we now assume that
\pi
contracts some
curve in
{\mathcal{K}}
, and hence all curves in
{\mathcal{K}}
. Let

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi^{\prime}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eta}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y

be the Stein factorization of
\pi
,
where
Y^{\prime}
is a normal projective variety (possibly
singular),
\pi^{\prime}
is a contraction (that is,

\pi^{\prime}_{*}({\mathcal{O}}_{X})={\mathcal{O}}_{Y^{\prime}}
), and
\eta
is finite surjective.
Then there is a unique action of
G
on
Y^{\prime}
such that

\pi^{\prime}
and
\eta
are equivariant. Let
y^{\prime}=\pi^{\prime}(x)

and
I=G_{y^{\prime}}
; then
H\subset I\subset G
and the
orbit
G\cdot y^{\prime}\simeq G/I
is open in
Y^{\prime}
. Also,
let
F=\pi^{\prime-1}(y^{\prime})
; then
F
is the connected
component of
x
in the fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)
, and hence
is a smooth projective variety
(by generic smoothness). Moreover,
F
is stable
by
I
and contains
I\cdot x
as its open orbit.
Clearly, every curve in
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is contained in
F
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Keep the above notation and assumptions, and
assume that
I
normalizes
H
. Then
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is
irreducible and there exists a unique covering family
of rational curves
{\mathcal{L}}
on
F
such that

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}={\mathcal{L}}_{x}
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{K}}^{0}=G\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{0}
.

Proof.

Note that
H
acts trivially on
I/H
, since

H\triangleleft I
. Thus,
H
acts trivially on
F
,
and hence on
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
. As
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}\simeq G\times^{H}{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
,
we obtain
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}\simeq G/H\times{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
. Since
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}

is irreducible, so are
{\mathcal{U}}_{x}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
.

The inclusion
\iota:F\to X
induces compatible
immersions

\RatCurves_{{\rm fr}}(F)\longrightarrow\RatCurves_{{\rm fr}}(X),\quad\Univ_{{\rm fr}}(F)\longrightarrow\Univ_{{\rm fr}}(X),

since they are injective and their differentials are

It follows that
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is an irreducible component
of
\mu_{F}^{-1}(x)
, where
\mu_{F}:\Univ(F)\to F

denotes the evaluation map. So
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is an irreducible
component of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
for a unique family of rational
curves
{\mathcal{L}}
on

. Since
(F,x)
is an equivariant embedding
of the homogeneous space

and

, we see that
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
is

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}={\mathcal{L}}_{x}
and

{\mathcal{L}}^{0}=I^{0}\cdot{\mathcal{L}}_{x}=I^{0}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
, so that

{\mathcal{K}}^{0}=G\cdot{\mathcal{K}}_{x}=G\cdot{\mathcal{L}}_{x}=G\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{0}
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The above assignement

yields a bijection
between covering families of rational curves on

which are contracted by

, and covering families of

. This restricts to a bijection

2.3
Symmetric spaces

In this subsection, we recall some basic facts
on symmetric spaces, after
[
Ti11
, Section 26]

and its references.
We begin with some notation and conventions
which will be used throughout the sequel.
We will consider linear algebraic groups; for any
such group
H
, we denote by
H^{0}
its neutral
component, i.e., the connected component of
H

containing the neutral element
e
.

Let
G
be a connected reductive algebraic group.
Let
T\subset G
be a maximal torus,
and
B\subset G
a Borel subgroup containing
T
.
We denote the character group of
T
by

\mathfrak{X}=\mathfrak{X}(T)
, and the root system of
(G,T)
by

R=R(G,T)\subset\mathfrak{X}
. The roots of

(B,T)
form the set of positive roots
R^{+}
,
with basis
\Delta
(the set of simple roots).
The Weyl group of
(G,T)
is denoted by
W
.

Recall the decomposition of Lie algebras

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.
For any
\alpha\in R
, we denote by
U_{\alpha}

the closed subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
, and by
G_{\alpha}
the
subgroup of
G
generated by
U_{\alpha}

and
U_{-\alpha}
. Then
G_{\alpha}
is
a closed subgroup of
G
,
isomorphic to
\SL_{2}
or
\PSL_{2}
.

Next, let
\sigma
be a group involution of
G
.
Denote by
G^{\sigma}
the fixed point subgroup,
and by
G^{\sigma,0}
its neutral component.
Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
such that

G^{\sigma,0}\subset H\subset G^{\sigma}
;
then we say that
H
is a
symmetric subgroup

of
G
, and the homogeneous space
G/H
is a

symmetric space
.

By
[
St68
, Section 8]
,
the group
H
is reductive; equivalently,
the variety
G/H
is affine. Also,

\sigma
induces an involution of the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}

that we still denote by
\sigma
for simplicity.
The Lie algebra of
H
satisfies

\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{g}^{\sigma}
and

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
, where

\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}~|~\sigma(x)=-x\}

is a
G^{\sigma}
-stable complement of
\mathfrak{h}

in
\mathfrak{g}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
is the isotropy
representation of the symmetric space
G/H
.
Note that
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{h}

with respect to any
(G,\sigma)
-invariant scalar
product on
\mathfrak{g}
, and hence is a self-dual
representation of
G^{\sigma}
.

The involution
\sigma
stabilizes a maximal torus

T
of
G
, as follows e.g. from

[
Ti11
, Lemma 26.5]
.
Thus,
\sigma
acts on the character
group
\mathfrak{X}
and stabilizes the root system
R
;
it also acts on the Weyl group
W
by conjugation.
We may choose a scalar product
(-,-)
on
the real vector space
\mathfrak{X}_{{\mathbb{R}}}=\mathfrak{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}

which is invariant under
W
and
\sigma
.

Definition 2.8
.

For
\alpha\in R
, one of the following cases occurs:

1.

\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha

and
\sigma

fixes pointwise

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. Then
\alpha
is called a

compact imaginary
root.

2.

\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha

and
\sigma
acts on
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
by
-1
. Then

\alpha
is
non-compact imaginary
.

3.

\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
. Then
\alpha

is
real
.

4.

\sigma(\alpha)\neq\pm\alpha
. Then

\alpha
is
complex
.

Recall that any two maximal tori of
G
are conjugate
and hence
\mathfrak{X}
,
R
and
W
are independent of
the choice of
T
. But the action of
\sigma
on these
objects depends on the choice of the
\sigma
-stable
torus
T
, up to conjugacy by
H
. We now consider
two special conjugacy classes of
\sigma
-stable maximal
tori, that we call of fixed (resp. split) type.
These are constructed as follows.

Maximal tori of fixed type.

Choose a maximal torus
T_{H}
of
H
; then its centralizer

T=\C_{G}(T_{H})
is a
\sigma
-stable maximal torus of
G

and we have
T_{H}=T^{\sigma,0}
.
Moreover,
T
is contained in a
\sigma
-stable Borel
subgroup
B
of
G
; then
B_{H}=B^{\sigma,0}

is a Borel subgroup of
H

(see
[
Ti11
, Lemma 26.7]
for these facts).
Thus,
B_{H}=U_{H}T_{H}
, where
U_{H}=U\cap H
is
a maximal unipotent subgroup of
H
.

Since
B
is
\sigma
-stable,
the action of
\sigma
on the root system
R

stabilizes
R^{+}
. In particular, there are no real roots.
The subset of simple roots
\Delta
is

\sigma
-stable as well.

Clearly, the maximal tori obtained in this way are
exactly those containing a
\sigma
-fixed torus
(i.e., a subtorus
S\subset G
such that

\sigma(s)=s
for all
s\in S
)
that is maximal for this property; they are
all conjugate under
H^{0}=G^{\sigma,0}
.
We call every such maximal torus
of fixed type

and denote it by
T_{{\rm f}}
.

Maximal tori of split type.

In the opposite direction, a subtorus
S\subset G

is called
\sigma
-split
if
\sigma(s)=s^{-1}
for all

s\in S
. Choose such a torus
S
maximal for this property.
Then
L=\C_{G}(S)
satisfies
[L,L]\subset H^{0}
;
as a consequence, every maximal torus of
G
containing

S
is
\sigma
-stable
(see
[
Vu74
, Proposition 2]
for these facts).
We call every such maximal torus

of split type
and denote it by
T_{{\rm s}}
.
We then have
L=T_{{\rm s}}(L\cap H)=S(L\cap H)
.
Also,
L
is a Levi subgroup of a minimal
\sigma
-split
parabolic subgroup
P
, that is,
P
is a parabolic subgroup
of
G
which is opposite to
\sigma(P)
, and minimal for
this property (see loc. cit., Section 1.2).
Moreover,
PG^{\sigma,0}
is open in
G
by loc. cit.,
Theorem 1; as a consequence,
PH
is open in
G
.
Also, recall from loc. cit., Section 1.3
that the maximal split tori are all conjugate
under
H^{0}
, as well as the minimal split parabolic
subgroups and the maximal tori of split type.

Denote by
x=H/H
the base point of the homogeneous
space
G/H
; then the orbit
P\cdot x
is
isomorphic to
P/P\cap H=PH/H
, and hence is
open in
G\cdot x=G/H
.
Moreover, we have

P\cap H=P\cap\sigma(P)\cap H=L\cap H
;
in particular,
P\cap H\subset L
.
Denote by
\R_{u}(P)
the unipotent radical of
P
,
so that we have the Levi decomposition

P=\R_{u}(P)\rtimes L
. Then the map

\R_{u}(P)\times L/L\cap H\to P/P\cap H
,

(g,z)\mapsto g\cdot z
is an isomorphism.
Thus, the map

i:\R_{u}(P)\times L/L\cap H\longrightarrow G/H,\quad(g,z)\longmapsto g\cdot z

is an open immersion with image
P\cdot x
.
Moreover, the natural map

S/S\cap H\to L/L\cap H
is an isomorphism
as
L=S(L\cap H)
. Also, note that
i
is
P
-equivariant, where

P
acts on
\R_{u}(P)\times S/S\cap H

via
(u,l)\cdot(g,z)=(ulgl^{-1},l\cdot z)
,
and on
G/H
via left multiplication.

The maximal tori of fixed type will be used in
the rest of this section and in Section

3
. Those of split type, and
the corresponding restricted root system,
feature prominently in the subsequent sections.

2.4
The normalizer of a symmetric subgroup

We keep the notation of Subsection
2.3
,
and obtain some auxiliary results on the structure of
the normalizer
N=\N_{G}(G^{\sigma})
.
For this, we introduce additional notation:
let
Z=Z(G)
be the center of

G
, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{z}
. Since
Z
is

\sigma
-stable, we have

\mathfrak{z}=(\mathfrak{z}\cap\mathfrak{h})\oplus(\mathfrak{z}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma})
.
Also, we denote by

q:G\longrightarrow G/Z=G_{{\rm ad}}

the quotient homomorphism, where
G_{{\rm ad}}

is the adjoint group.
The involution
\sigma
of
G
induces an
involution of
G_{{\rm ad}}
that we still denote by

\sigma
for simplicity.

Lemma 2.9
.

1.

N=\{g\in G~|~\sigma(g)g^{-1}\in Z\}
.

2.

q
induces an isomorphism

G/N\simeq G_{{\rm ad}}/G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}
.
Moreover,
G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}
is its own
normalizer in
G_{{\rm ad}}
.

3.

N=\N_{G}(G^{\sigma,0})=\N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})
.

4.

N^{0}=Z^{0}H^{0}
.

5.

Z^{0}T_{H}
is a maximal torus of
N
for any
maximal torus
T_{H}
of
H
.

Proof.

(1) This is obtained in
[
Vu90
, Lemma 1]

(see also
[
deCP83
, Section I.7]
); we recall the
argument for completeness.

Let
g\in G
such that
\sigma(g)g^{-1}\in Z
.
For any
h\in G^{\sigma}
, we have

\sigma(ghg^{-1})=\sigma(g)h\sigma(g)^{-1}=ghg^{-1}
, that is,
ghg^{-1}\in G^{\sigma}
.
So
g\in N
. For the converse, observe that

N
is reductive and normalized by
\sigma
.
The corresponding semi-direct product

N\rtimes\langle\sigma\rangle

is a reductive algebraic group, which acts
linearly on
\mathfrak{g}
and stabilizes
\mathfrak{h}
.
Thus,
\mathfrak{h}
has an

N\rtimes\langle\sigma\rangle
-stable
complement, which must be
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
. In particular,

\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
is
N
-stable; thus,
\Ad(N)
commutes
with
\sigma
. So

\Ad(\sigma(g)g^{-1})=\sigma\Ad(g)\sigma^{-1}\Ad(g)^{-1}={\rm id}

for any
g\in N
, that is,

\sigma(g)g^{-1}\in Z
.

(2) By (1), we have
N=q^{-1}(G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma})
;
this yields the first assertion. Applying (1) again
to
G_{{\rm ad}}
, we obtain the second assertion.

(3) Clearly, we have

N=\N_{G}(G^{\sigma})\subset\N_{G}(G^{\sigma,0})=\N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})
. Moreover,
\N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})
is reductive
and normalized by
\sigma
. Arguing as in the proof
of (1), it follows that
\sigma(g)g^{-1}\in Z

for any
g\in\N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})
, and hence
g\in N
.

(4) Denote by
\mathfrak{n}
the Lie algebra of
N
.
Then (1) yields that

\mathfrak{n}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}~|~\sigma(x)-x\in\mathfrak{z}\}
.
Using the
\sigma
-stable decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{z}\oplus[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
, it follows that

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{z}\oplus[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]^{\sigma}=\mathfrak{z}+\mathfrak{h}
.
This yields the assertion.

(5) This follows readily from (4).
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
S
be a maximal
\sigma
-split torus of
G
.

1.

N=G^{\sigma,0}(N\cap S)
.

2.

H=G^{\sigma,0}(H\cap S)

and
H\cap S
is an elementary abelian
2
-group.

3.

N=\N_{G}(H)
and

N/H\simeq N\cap S/H\cap S
. In particular,

N/H
is diagonalizable.

Proof.

(1) Let
P
be a minimal
\sigma
-split
parabolic subgroup of
G
containing
S
.
Then
P_{{\rm ad}}=P/Z
is a minimal
\sigma
-split
parabolic subgroup of
G_{{\rm ad}}
, containing

S_{{\rm ad}}=S/S\cap Z
which is a maximal

\sigma
-split torus of
G_{{\rm ad}}
.
As seen in the discussion of maximal tori
of split type in Subsection
2.3
, the map

\R_{u}(P_{{\rm ad}})\times S_{{\rm ad}}/S_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}\longrightarrow G_{{\rm ad}}/G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma},\quad(g,z)\longmapsto g\cdot z

is an open immersion.
Also, the isomorphism

G_{{\rm ad}}/G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}\simeq G/N

(Lemma
2.9
(2)) restricts to an
isomorphism

S_{{\rm ad}}/S_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}\simeq SN/N
.
Thus, the
multiplication map

\R_{u}(P)\times SN\to G
is an open immersion
as well. Its image is
\R_{u}(P)SN=PN
, the open
orbit of
P\times N
in
G
. Likewise,

PG^{\sigma,0}
is the open orbit of

P\times G^{\sigma,0}
in
G
. Let
g\in N
; then
the orbit
PgG^{\sigma,0}=PG^{\sigma,0}g

is open in
G
. So
g\in PG^{\sigma,0}
, and
hence
PN=PG^{\sigma,0}
. It follows that

SN=SG^{\sigma,0}
; this yields the assertion.

(2) The first assertion follows readily from (1).
For the second assertion, just note that every

g\in S\cap H
satisfies
g^{-1}=\sigma(g)=g
.

(3) Clearly, we have

\N_{G}(H)\subset\N_{G}(H^{0})=\N_{G}(G^{\sigma,0})
.
Moreover,
\N_{G}(G^{\sigma,0})=N
in view of
Lemma
2.9
(2). Also, by combining
(1) and (2) above, we see that
N
normalizes
H
,
since
N\cap S
normalizes
G^{\sigma,0}
and
centralizes
H\cap S
. Thus,
\N_{G}(H)=N
.
By (1) again, we have
N=H(N\cap S)
,
and hence
N/H\simeq(N\cap S)/(H\cap S)
is
diagonalizable.
∎

2.5
The adjoint symmetric space

Recall the adjoint group
G_{{\rm ad}}=G/Z

equipped with an involution
\sigma
.
The homogeneous space
G_{{\rm ad}}/G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}

is called an
adjoint symmetric space
;
it is isomorphic to
G/N
by Lemma
2.9
(2).
The semisimple adjoint group
G_{\rm ad}
is the product of its simple factors,
and these are permuted by
\sigma
. This gives a
decomposition into a product of simple adjoint groups

G_{\rm ad}=G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{m}\times(H_{1}\times H_{1})\times\cdots\times(H_{n}\times H_{n}),

where
\sigma
stabilizes the
G_{i}

and exchanges the two copies of the
H_{j}
.
Using the classification of symmetric spaces
(see
[
Ti11
, Section 26.5]
),
one arrives at a decomposition of
G/N
into a product of

indecomposable symmetric spaces
of the
following three types:

1.

(group)

(H\times H)/{\rm diag}(H)
, where
H
is simple adjoint.

2.

(Hermitian)

G/\N_{G}(L)
, where
G
is simple adjoint and

L\subset G
is a Levi subgroup.

3.

(simple)

G/H
, where
G
is simple adjoint and
H^{0}
is
semisimple.

In type (1), we have
\sigma(x,y)=(y,x)
for all

x,y\in H
. Thus,
G/N
is just the group
H
on which

H\times H
acts by left and right multiplication.
The isotropy representation
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
is the adjoint
representation of
H
in
\mathfrak{h}
. This is an irreducible
representation with highest weight the highest root

\Theta
.

In type (2), we have
L=P\cap Q
,
where
P
and
Q
are opposite maximal
parabolic subgroups of
G
.
Moreover,
\sigma
is the conjugation
{\rm Int}(c)
,
where
c\in Z(L)
and
c^{2}\in Z(G)
.
Denote by
\alpha
the unique simple root which
is not a root of
L
; then
\alpha
has coefficient
1

in the expansion of the highest root
\Theta

as a linear combination of simple roots.
We have
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}=\mathfrak{u}_{P}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
, where

\mathfrak{u}_{P}
(resp.
\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
) denotes the Lie algebra of
\R_{u}(P)

(resp.
\R_{u}(Q)
). Moreover, the representations
\mathfrak{u}_{P}
,

\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
of
L
are irreducible and dual to each other
(see e.g.
[
RRS92
, Section 5.5]
for these results).
Their highest weights relative to
L
are
\Theta
,

-\alpha
; they are linearly independent unless
G=\PSL_{2}
.

We say that the Hermitian symmetric space

G/\N_{G}(L)
is
exceptional
,
if
P
and
Q
are not conjugate in
G
.
Then
\N_{G}(L)=L
, and hence
G/\N_{G}(L)
may be
identified with the open
G
-orbit in
G/P\times G/Q

on which
G
acts diagonally.
In the
non-exceptional
case,
where
P
and
Q
are conjugate in
G
,
the group
\N_{G}(L)/L
has order
2
and exchanges

P
and
Q
. Moreover,
G/\N_{G}(L)
may be identified
with the open
G
-orbit in the symmetric square

(G/P)^{(2)}
, the quotient of
G/P\times G/P

by the involution
(y,z)\mapsto(z,y)
.

In type (3),
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
is irreducible as a representation of

H^{0}
, with a non-zero highest weight. (We do not know any uniform
proof of this fact, which can be checked on the classification
of symmetric spaces).

2.6
Highest weight curves

We still use the notation of Subsection
2.3
,
and choose a maximal torus
T_{H}\subset H
and a Borel
subgroup
B_{H}\subset H
containing
T_{H}
.
Recall that
T=\C_{G}(T_{H})
is a maximal
torus of fixed type of
G
.
We first obtain a generalization of
[
BF15
, Lemma 2.2]
:

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
C
be an irreducible
B_{H}
-stable curve in
G/H

through the base point
x
.

1.

Either
C
is contained in
Z^{0}\cdot x
,
or
B_{H}
acts non-trivially on
C
.

2.

In the latter case,
C
is smooth
and
B_{H}
-equivariantly isomorphic to its tangent
line at
x
, which is the
T_{H}
-weight space

\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{\lambda}
for a unique non-zero highest weight

\lambda
of
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
. Moreover,
\lambda
determines

C
uniquely, and the stabilizer of
C
in
H
equals
the stabilizer of the weight space
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
C
is fixed pointwise by
B_{H}
. Then
the orbit
H^{0}\cdot y
is complete for any
y\in C
.
Since
G/H
is affine, this orbit must be a point,
i.e.,
C
is fixed pointwise by
H^{0}
. Let
g\in G
such
that
y=g\cdot x
, then
g^{-1}H^{0}g\cdot x=x
,
i.e.,
g^{-1}H^{0}g\subset H
. So
g\in\N_{G}(H^{0})=N

(Lemma
2.9
). Thus,
C\subset N\cdot x
.
As
C
is connected and contains
x
, it follows that

C\subset N^{0}\cdot x
.
But
N^{0}\cdot x=Z^{0}\cdot x
by Lemma
2.9

again; this yields the assertion.

(2) This is obtained by arguing as in the proof of

[
BF15
, Lemma 2.2(i)]
. We provide details for the
reader’s convenience.

Since
C
is not fixed pointwise by
B_{H}
, it contains
an open orbit
B_{H}\cdot y
, where
y\neq x
.
Thus, the isotropy group
B_{H,y}
has codimension
1

in
B_{H}
. We thus have

B_{H,y}^{0}=U_{H,y}\rtimes S
for some subtorus
S

of
B_{H}
. Replacing
y
with a
B_{H}
-translate,
we may assume that
S\subset T_{H}
.

If
S=T_{H}
, then the orbit

B_{H}\cdot y=U_{H}\cdot y
is isomorphic to

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
and hence is closed in
G/H
, since the
latter is an affine variety.
But
x\in\overline{B_{H}\cdot y}\setminus\{y\}
,
a contradiction. For dimension reasons, it follows
that
S
is a subtorus of codimension
1
of
T_{H}
,
and
U_{H}\subset B_{H,y}
. As a consequence,

C
is fixed pointwise by
U_{H}
, since the latter
is a normal subgroup of
B_{H}
. Thus,
T_{H}\cdot y

is open in
C
.

In particular,
x\in\overline{H\cdot y}
. So
C
is
contained in the fiber at
x
of the geometric invariant
theory quotient
G/H\to H\backslash\!\!\backslash G/H

of the smooth affine
H
-variety
G/H
.
By a corollary of Luna’s slice theorem
(see
[
Lu73
, Sections II.1 and III.1]
), this fiber is

H
-equivariantly isomorphic to the nilcone
{\mathcal{N}}
of
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}

(the fiber at
0
of the quotient
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\to\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}/\!\!/H
;
it consists of the points
z\in\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
such that

0\in\overline{H\cdot z}
).
Thus,
C
is
B_{H}
-equivariantly isomorphic to
a
B_{H}
-stable curve
D
in
{\mathcal{N}}
. Moreover,
C
and
D

have the same stabilizer in
H
.

As
U_{H}
fixes
D
pointwise, we have

D\subset{\mathcal{N}}\cap(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma})^{U_{H}}
. Also,

\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}=(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap\mathfrak{z})\oplus(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])

and the projection
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\to\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap\mathfrak{z}
is

H
-invariant, hence sends
{\mathcal{N}}
to
0
.
it follows that

D\subset(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])^{U_{H}}
.

So we may assume that
G/H
is an adjoint symmetric space.
Using the product decomposition of these spaces, we see that

D
is a highest weight line from a unique indecomposable
factor of
G/H
, and is uniquely determined by its weight.
∎

We say that a curve
C
as in Lemma
2.11
(2)
is a
highest weight curve
. The corresponding
highest weight
\lambda
satisfies

\lambda=\alpha|_{T_{H}}
for some root
\alpha
,
since the non-zero weights of
T_{H}
in
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}

are restrictions of non-zero weights of
T
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
Let
S={\rm Ker}(\lambda)^{0}=({\rm Ker}(\alpha)\cap T_{H})^{0}
;
then
S
is a subtorus of codimension
1
of
T_{H}
,
and fixes
C
pointwise.
Thus, the centralizer
\C_{G}(S)
is a
\sigma
-stable
subgroup of
G
containing
T
;
also,
C_{G}(S)
is connected and reductive by

[
Sp98b
, Section 7.6.4]
.
Moreover,
\C_{H}(S)
is a symmetric subgroup of
\C_{G}(S)

containing
T_{H}
, and
C
is a highest weight curve
of the symmetric space
\C_{G}(S)/\C_{H}(S)
.

Recall the following easy result
(see
[
Sp83
, Section 2]
and
[
Br99
, Lemma 2.5]
):

Lemma 2.12
.

With the above notation and assumptions, the adjoint
symmetric space of
\C_{G}(S)/\C_{H}(S)
is one of
the following:

(
{\rm A}_{1}
)

\PSL_{2}/N
, where
N
denotes
the normalizer of the diagonal torus in
\PSL_{2}
.

(
{\rm A}_{1}\times{\rm A}_{1}
)

(\PSL_{2}\times\PSL_{2})/{\rm diag}(\PSL_{2})
.
Then
\sigma(\alpha)
is strongly orthogonal to
\alpha
.

(
{\rm A}_{2}
)

\PSL_{3}/\SO_{3}
, where
\SO_{3}
denotes the special
orthogonal group.

Moreover,
\alpha
is non-compact imaginary in types

({\rm A}_{1})
and
({\rm A}_{2})
. If the involution
\sigma
is inner
(equivalently,
T_{H}
is a maximal torus of
G
), then
only type
({\rm A}_{1})
occurs.

Still considering a highest weight curve
C
of weight
\lambda
,
we now obtain a description of
C
and its tangent line

T_{x}C\subset T_{x}G/H
(using the identifications

T_{x}G/H=\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}

Proposition 2.13
.

In type
({\rm A}_{1})
, there is a unique root
\alpha

such that
\lambda=\alpha|_{T_{H}}
.
Moreover,
C=U_{\alpha}\cdot x

and
T_{x}C=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.

In type
({\rm A}_{1}\times{\rm A}_{1})
, there are exactly two roots

\alpha
,
\beta
such that

\lambda=\alpha|_{T_{H}}=\beta|_{T_{H}}
.
Moreover,
\alpha
and
\beta=\sigma(\alpha)
are
the simple roots of
(\C_{G}(S),T)
. We have

C=U_{\alpha}\cdot x=U_{\beta}\cdot x

and
T_{x}C={\mathbb{C}}(e_{\alpha}-\sigma(e_{\alpha}))
,
where
e_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
.

In type
({\rm A}_{2})
, there is a unique root
\alpha

such that
\lambda=\alpha|_{T_{H}}
. Moreover,

\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, where

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}=\sigma(\alpha_{1})
are
the simple roots of
(\C_{G}(S),T)
. Also,

C=U_{\alpha}\cdot x
and
T_{x}C=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

In type
({\rm A}_{1})
, there are two highest weight
curves in
\C_{G}(S)/\C_{H}(S)
, namely,

U_{\alpha}\cdot x
and
U_{-\alpha}\cdot x
.

In type
({\rm A}_{1}\times{\rm A}_{1})
, recall that the adjoint
symmetric space of
\C_{G}(S)/\C_{H}(S)
is the group

(\PSL_{2}\times\PSL_{2})/{\rm diag}(\PSL_{2})=\PSL_{2}
.
So the roots of
(\C_{G}(S),T)
are
\pm\alpha
,

\pm\sigma(\alpha)
. Moreover,
U_{\alpha}\cdot x

is the unique highest weight curve; it is identified
with the standard unipotent subgroup
U\subset\PSL_{2}
,
and likewise for
U_{\sigma(\alpha)}\cdot x
.

In type
({\rm A}_{2})
, the adjoint symmetric space of

\C_{G}(S)/\C_{H}(S)
is
\PSL_{3}/\SO_{3}
; one checks that
the highest weight of its isotropy representation is

(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})|_{T_{H}}
, where

\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
are the simple roots of

\PSL_{3}
. It follows that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
,
and
\sigma(\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{2}
. Finally,

is an irreducible curve in

and fixed by

(since the latter

in

This yields the assertions on roots and highest weight

∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
C
be a highest weight curve of weight
\lambda
.
Then there exists
\alpha\in R
such that

\lambda=\alpha|_{T_{H}}
and

.

Corollary 2.15
.

We have the following alternative for a simple group
G
:

1.

T_{x}C=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for a long root
\alpha
, or

2.

T_{x}C=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for a short root
\alpha
, or

3.

T_{x}C
is spanned by
e_{\alpha}-\sigma(e_{\alpha})
,
where
\alpha\in R
is strongly orthogonal to

\sigma(\alpha)
. Moreover,

is simply laced.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
2.13
,
we only have to show that
G
is simply laced in case (3).
Then
\sigma(\alpha)\neq\alpha
,
and hence
\sigma
acts non-trivially on

.
As
\sigma
stabilizes the set
\Delta
of simple roots,

simply laced.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

The three cases in Corollary

do occur
(see Table
1
for the notation on types):

1.

In type AI with

2.

In type BII with

we have

with

3.

In type AII with

and

,

,

is the highest root and
\alpha_{1}
and

are simple roots labeled as in

.

4.

5.

In type

Indeed

for any root

Next, we assume that

is indecomposable;
in particular,

We show that

of a very special type, defined as follows:

Definition 2.17
.

Let

be an indecomposable symmetric space.

1.

If

, then set

{\mathcal{O}}_{\min}=G\cdot(e,-e)\subset\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{h}

2.

If

and a

.

.

With the above notation,

is contained in

or in
a nilpotent orbit of type

.

This follows by combining Corollary

,
Remark
2.16
(5) and the next result.

Lemma 2.20
.

Assume that
G
is not simply laced and not of type
G_{2}
.
Let
e_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}

with
\alpha
a short root. Then
e_{\alpha}
belongs to a nilpotent orbit
of type
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\rm sum}}
.

Proof.

Since all short roots are in the same orbit under the action
of the Weyl group, we may assume that
\alpha
is
the highest short root
\theta
.

There exists a simple root
\beta
such that

\theta+\beta
is a root; then
\theta+\beta
must be
a long root. We claim that

\langle\beta^{\vee},\theta+\beta\rangle=2
.
Indeed, since
\theta
is a dominant weight, we have

\langle\beta^{\vee},\theta\rangle\geq 0
and we get

\langle\beta^{\vee},\theta+\beta\rangle\geq\langle\beta^{\vee},\beta\rangle=2
.
Since
G
is not of type
G_{2}
, we must have equality:

\langle\beta^{\vee},\theta+\beta\rangle=2
.

We get
s_{\beta}(\theta+\beta)=\theta-\beta
; thus,

\theta-\beta
is a long root. Moreover, we have

\theta-\beta+\theta+\beta=2\theta
and
\theta+\beta-(\theta-\beta)=2\beta
. Thus
\theta-\beta
and
\theta+\beta
are strongly orthogonal.

We are left to prove that
e_{\theta}
and

e_{\theta-\beta}+e_{\theta+\beta}
are in the same

G
-orbit in
\mathfrak{g}
.
The group
G_{\beta}
(generated by
U_{\pm\beta}
)
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
and stabilizes the subspace

V=\mathfrak{g}_{\theta-\beta}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\theta+\beta}

on which it acts via the adjoint representation.
Moreover,
G_{\beta}
acts with two orbits in the
projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(V)
:
the minimal orbit and its complement.
The point
[e_{\theta}]
is in this last orbit,
which also contains

[e_{\theta-\beta}+e_{\theta+\beta}]
.
This yields the assertion, since nilpotent
orbits are stable under non-trivial homotheties.
∎

3
Complete symmetric varieties

In this section, we recall the notions
of wonderful symmetric varieties and complete symmetric varieties and we describe their relations, especially how to compare their respective families of minimal
rational curves.
We then recall the result of
[
BF15
]
about such families on wonderful compactifications of groups. We end the section by a description of the families of minimal rational curves on complete symmetric varieties in the group type, in the Hermitian type, and in some cases of simple type.

3.1
Wonderful and complete symmetric varieties

We use the notation of Subsections
2.3

and
2.4
.
In particular,
G
denotes a connected reductive group,

H
a symmetric subgroup relative to an involution

\sigma
, and
N
the normalizer of
H
in
G
.
We denote by
x
(resp.
x_{\rm ad}
) the base point of
the homogeneous space
G/H
(resp.
G/N
).
The natural morphism

\pi:G/H\longrightarrow G/N,\quad x\longmapsto x_{\rm ad}

is a principal bundle under
N/H
. Moreover,
N/H
is
diagonalizable by Lemma
2.10
.
We have the “Stein factorization” of
\pi
as

G/H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi^{\prime}}}{{\longrightarrow}}G/N^{0}H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eta}}{{\longrightarrow}}G/N,

where
\pi^{\prime}
is a principal bundle under the torus

N^{0}H/H\simeq Z^{0}/H\cap Z^{0}
(Lemma
2.9
),
and
\eta
is a principal bundle under
N/N^{0}H
,
a finite abelian group (Lemma
2.10
).

By
[
deCP83
]
, the adjoint symmetric space

G/N=G_{{\rm ad}}/G_{{\rm ad}}^{\sigma}

admits a wonderful equivariant embedding that we denote
by
X_{\rm ad}
, with base point
x_{\rm ad}
. We say that

X_{{\rm ad}}
is a
wonderful symmetric variety
.

We now recall from
[
LP90
, Section 3.3]
how to obtain

X_{{\rm ad}}
from the wonderful

G_{{\rm ad}}\times G_{{\rm ad}}
-equivariant embedding

\overline{G_{\rm ad}}
of
G_{\rm ad}=(G_{{\rm ad}}\times G_{{\rm ad}})/{\rm diag}(G_{{\rm ad}})
.
We begin with a general construction: the morphism

G\longrightarrow G,\quad g\longmapsto\sigma(g)g^{-1}

factors through a closed immersion

\iota:G/G^{\sigma}\to G

which sends the base point
x
to the neutral element

e
. The image of
\iota
is a connected component of
the fixed locus
G^{-\sigma}
, where
-\sigma

denotes the involution
g\mapsto\sigma(g^{-1})

of
G
(viewed as a variety); see

[
Ri82
, Lemma 2.4]

for these results. Note that
\iota
is
equivariant for the natural action of
G
on

G/G^{\sigma}
, and the
G
-action on itself
via twisted conjugation, defined by

g_{1}\cdot g_{2}:=\sigma(g_{1})g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}
.
Also, the differential of
\iota
at
x

is identified with the inclusion

\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
.

This construction applies to the involution
\sigma

of
G_{{\rm ad}}
; moreover,
-\sigma
extends uniquely
to an involution of
\overline{G_{\rm ad}}
that we still
denote by
-\sigma
, and
\iota
extends uniquely
to a closed immersion

\overline{\iota}:X_{{\rm ad}}\hookrightarrow\overline{G_{\rm ad}}

which identifies
X_{{\rm ad}}
with a component of

(\overline{G_{\rm ad}})^{-\sigma}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
complete symmetric variety
is a smooth
projective equivariant embedding
(X,x)
of
G/H

that is toroidal in the sense of

[
Ti11
, Section 29]
), i.e.,
the morphism
\pi:G/H\to G/N

extends to a morphism
X\to X_{\rm ad}
.
We still denote by

\pi:X\longrightarrow X_{\rm ad}

this extension,
which is of course unique and hence
G
-equivariant.
(We do not assume that
X
has a unique closed
G
-orbit).

If
X=X_{\rm ad}
is adjoint we will call
X
of group, simple, Hermitian or Hermitian exceptional type if
G/H
is of group, simple, Hermitian or Hermitian exceptional type.

For any complete symmetric variety
X
,
the boundary
\partial X=X\setminus X^{0}
is a divisor
with simple normal crossings. We will use the
following relation between the canonical divisors
of
X
and
X_{{\rm ad}}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

With the above notation, we have
the equality of divisor classes

K_{X}+\partial X=\pi^{*}(K_{X_{{\rm ad}}}+\partial X_{{\rm ad}}).

Proof.

Recall from
[
deCP83
]
that
X_{{\rm ad}}
is isomorphic
to the
G
-orbit closure of
\mathfrak{h}
in the Grassmannian
of subspaces of
\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover,

-K_{X_{{\rm ad}}}-\partial X_{{\rm ad}}
is the hyperplane
class
h
in the corresponding Plücker embedding
(see
[
Ti11
, Proposition 30.8]
). Also,

-K_{X}-\partial X=\pi^{*}(h)
by loc. cit.
∎

We will also use the following description of the
general fibers of
\pi
; by equivariance, it suffices
to describe the fiber at
x
. In view of the Stein
factorization,
\pi
is the composition of
a contraction
\pi^{\prime}:X\to X^{\prime}
as discussed after
Remark
2.5
, and a finite surjective
equivariant morphism
\eta:X^{\prime}\to X_{\rm ad}
.
The pair
(X^{\prime},x^{\prime}:=\pi^{\prime}(x))
is a normal projective
equivariant embedding (possibly singular) of

G/N^{0}H=G/Z^{0}H
(a symmetric space under
G/Z^{0}
).
Moreover, the fiber of
\pi
at
x
is isomorphic to
the associated bundle
N\times^{N^{0}H}F
, where

F
denotes the fiber of
\pi^{\prime}
at
x
. The group

N^{0}H
acts on
F
via its quotient torus

N^{0}H/H\simeq N^{0}/H\cap N^{0}\simeq Z^{0}/H\cap Z^{0}

(where the second isomorphism follows from
Lemma
2.9
),
and
F
is a smooth projective toric variety
under that torus.

3.2
Relation between families of minimal rational curves

In this subsection, we consider a complete symmetric
variety
X
with base point
x
. We will reduce somehow
the description of families of minimal rational curves
on
X
to the cases where
X
is a smooth projective
toric variety or a wonderful symmetric variety.
A key notion is that of a
highest weight curve
,
i.e., an irreducible curve
C\subset X
through
x

which is stable and not fixed pointwise by the Borel
subgroup
B_{H}
.
Equivalently,
C\cap X^{0}
is a highest weight curve
in the sense of Subsection
2.6
.

By Corollary
2.14
, we have

C=\overline{U_{\alpha}\cdot x}

for some root
\alpha
. In view of

[
BF15
, Lemma 2.1 (i), Lemma 2.4]
, this yields:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
C
be a highest weight curve.
Then
C
is an embedded free rational curve.

We now obtain an alternative for families of minimal
rational curves:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of minimal rational curves on
X
.

1.

Either each curve in
{\mathcal{K}}
is contracted by
\pi
,
or
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
contains a highest weight curve.

2.

In the former case,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is
a family of minimal rational curves on the toric variety
F
.
Moreover, the tangent map
\tau_{x}
is an isomorphism
of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
with a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap\mathfrak{z})
.

Proof.

(1) In view of Borel’s fixed point theorem,

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
contains a
B_{H}
-fixed point, i.e.
a
B_{H}
-stable curve
C
.
Then either
C
is contained in
Z^{0}\cdot x
,
or
C
is a highest weight curve by Lemma

2.11
(1).

(2) The first assertion is a consequence of Lemma

2.6
. The second assertion follows from

[
CFH14
, Corollary 2.5]
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
X=X_{{\rm ad}}
be a wonderful symmetric variety.

1.

A family
{\mathcal{K}}
of rational curves on
X

consists of minimal curves if and only if

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
contains a highest weight curve.

2.

If
X
is indecomposable and not Hermitian,
then it has a unique highest weight curve
(and hence a unique family of
minimal rational curves).

3.

If
X
is Hermitian, then it has two highest weight curves.
In the non-exceptional case, these are exchanged
by any element of
\N_{G}(L)\setminus L
,
and there is a unique family of minimal rational
curves. In the exceptional case, there are two such
families, and they are exchanged by an automorphism
of
X
fixing
x
.

Proof.

(1) If
{\mathcal{K}}
is a family of minimal rational curves,
then
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
contains a highest weight curve
by Lemma
3.4
.

For the converse, using Lemma
2.2
and the
structure of adjoint symmetric spaces (Subsection
2.6
),
we may assume that
X
is indecomposable.

(2) Since
X
is not Hermitian, there is a unique highest weight
curve
C
by Subsection
2.5
.
Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a family of minimal rational curves on
X
.
Then
C\in{\mathcal{L}}
by the above step, and hence
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{L}}
.

(3) Recall that
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}=\mathfrak{u}_{P}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
with
the notation of Subsection
2.5
.
As a consequence, there are two highest weight curves,
with highest weights
\Theta
and
-\alpha
.
Consider a Chevalley involution of
(G,T)
,
i.e., an involution
\tau
of
G
such that

\tau(t)=t^{-1}
for all
t\in T
. Then
\tau

commutes with
\sigma={\rm Int}(c)
(see Subsection
2.5

again for the definition of
c
),
since
\tau(c)=c^{-1}=cz
for some
z\in Z
.
Thus,
\tau
induces an involution
\tau_{X}
of
X

fixing
x
. Also,
\tau
sends every root to its opposite;
in particular,
\tau(\Theta)=-\Theta
.
Choose a representative
g\in\N_{L}(T)

of the longest element of the Weyl group of

(L,T)
. Then
g\circ\tau_{X}
is an automorphism
of
X
which fixes
x
and exchanges the two
highest weight curves (indeed
g\circ\tau_{X}

maps
\Theta
to
-\alpha
:
the involution
\tau_{X}
maps the weights of

\mathfrak{u}_{P}
to the weights of
\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
and reverses the order.
Since
g
is a representative of

w_{0,L}
, the longest element in
W_{L}
,
it reverses the order of the weights in
\mathfrak{u}_{P}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
;
thus,
g\circ\tau_{X}
maps the highest weight of
\mathfrak{u}_{P}

to the highest weight of
\mathfrak{u}_{Q}
). So each of these curves
is contained in a family of minimal rational curves,
and hence
{\mathcal{K}}
is such a family.

This completes the proof of the first assertion.
For the second assertion, it only remains to show
that there are two families of minimal rational curves
if
X
is Hermitian exceptional. In this case,
the map
G/L\to G/P
extends to a morphism

f_{P}:X\to G/P
. (Indeed, we may view
G/P
as the closed

G
-orbit in the projectivization of a simple
G
-module

V
with fundamental highest weight. Let
D
be the pull-back
of the
B
-stable hyperplane in
{\mathbb{P}}(V)
under the resulting
morphism
G/L\to G/P\to{\mathbb{P}}(V)
. Then the closure

\overline{D}\subset X
is a prime
B
-stable divisor,
and hence the translates
g\cdot\overline{D}
,
where
g\in G
, have no common point as
X
is toroidal.
The corresponding base-point-free linear system
yields a morphism
X\to{\mathbb{P}}(V)
extending

G/L\to{\mathbb{P}}(V)
).
Moreover, the morphism
f_{P}
contracts
the highest weight curve with weight

\Theta
but not the other one.
So these two curves cannot be in the
same family. In the non-exceptional case,
they are exchanged by any element of

\N_{G}(L)\setminus L
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of minimal rational curves
on
X
containing a highest weight curve
C
.

1.

{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
consists of embedded free curves
(in particular, of smooth curves).
Moreover,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is smooth and equidimensional,
of dimension
-K_{X}\cdot C-2
.

2.

There is a unique family of minimal rational curves

{\mathcal{L}}
on
X_{{\rm ad}}
and a commutative diagram of

H
-equivariant rational maps

\textstyle{{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{*,x}}

\scriptstyle{\tau_{x_{{\rm ad}}}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x}X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d\pi_{x}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}(T_{x_{{\rm ad}}}X_{{\rm ad}})}

where
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{x_{{\rm ad}}}
are finite and
birational onto their image,
and
\pi_{*,x}
is a finite morphism.
If
\pi
is birational, then
\pi_{*,x}
is finite and
birational onto its image as well.

3.

We have

1\leq\partial X\cdot C\leq\partial X_{{\rm ad}}\cdot\pi(C)
.
Moreover,

if and only if the image of

.

4.

If each connected component of

is
a unique

-orbit, then

\pi_{*,x}
sends each component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}

isomorphically to a component of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{{\rm ad}}}
.

Proof.

(1) The open subset
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\rm emfr},x}
is
B_{H}
-stable,
and contains every
B_{H}
-fixed point by Lemma

2.11
(2) and Lemma
3.3
.
Using Borel’s fixed point theorem,
it follows that
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\rm emfr},x}
is the whole
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
.
Thus,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is smooth; it is equidimensional by
Lemma
2.3
. The assertion on its dimension
follows from
[
Ko96
, Section II.3.2]
.

(2) By Lemma
2.11
(2) again,

\pi|_{C}
is birational to its image
D
.
In view of Lemma
2.1
,
this yields a commutative diagram of rational maps

\textstyle{{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

for a unique covering family of rational maps
{\mathcal{L}}
on

X_{{\rm ad}}
. Since
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is smooth,
\tau_{x}
is a
finite morphism, birational onto its image
(see
[
Ke02
]
and
[
HM04
]
). Also,

\pi_{*,x}
is a morphism since it is a
B_{H}
-equivariant
rational map, defined at every
B_{H}
-fixed point.
Moreover,
{\mathcal{L}}
contains the highest weight curve

,
and hence is a

3.5
. Thus,
\tau_{x_{{\rm ad}}}

The rational map

the fibers of
\pi_{*,x}
are affine; thus,
\pi_{*,x}

is a finite morphism.

The final assertion follows from Remark
2.5
.

(3) Since

is affine,

and hence
\partial X\cdot C\geq 1
.

By (1), we have

\dim({\mathcal{K}}_{x})=-K_{X}\cdot C-2
and

Since

(4) By assumption, each component of
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{{\rm ad}}}

is homogeneous under

(Lemma

). The corresponding isotropy group

; thus, it is connected.
As

is finite and

-equivariant,
this yields the assertion.
∎

Example 3.7
.

Let
G=\SO_{n}
, where
n\geq 3
, and let
\sigma
be
the conjugation by
c={\rm diag}(1,\ldots,1,-1)\in{\rm O}_{n}
.
Let
H=G^{\sigma,0}=\SO_{n-1}
. Then

N=G^{\sigma}={\rm O}_{n-1}
embedded in
\SO_{n}

via
g\mapsto(g,\det(g))
, and
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}={\mathbb{C}}^{n-1}

on which
{\rm O}_{n-1}
acts via its standard representation.
Also,
G/H
has a unique smooth projective equivariant
embedding: the quadric

{\rm Q}_{n-1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\oplus{\mathbb{C}})
,
where
\SO_{n}
acts on
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\oplus{\mathbb{C}})
via its
standard representation on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Moreover,
X_{{\rm ad}}={\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}={\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
and

\pi:X\to X_{{\rm ad}}
is a ramified double cover induced
by the linear projection

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\oplus{\mathbb{C}})\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
.

If
n\geq 4
then
X
has a unique family of
minimal rational curves
{\mathcal{K}}
; it consists of the lines
in
{\rm Q}_{n-1}
. Moreover,
\pi_{*}
sends
{\mathcal{K}}

to the family
{\mathcal{L}}
of lines in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
, and

\pi_{*;x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\to{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{{\rm ad}}}
is identified
with the inclusion

{\rm Q}_{n-3}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-2}={\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma})
,
compatibly with the action of
{\rm O}_{n-1}=N
.

If
n=3
then
X={\rm Q}_{2}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

has two families of minimal rational curves, the fibers
of the two projections to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. For both families,

\pi_{*,x}
identifies
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
with a point in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

3.3
Minimal rational curves for group and Hermitian types

We still consider a complete symmetric variety

X
with base point
x
, and a family of minimal rational
curves
{\mathcal{K}}
on
X
; we assume that
{\mathcal{K}}

contains a highest weight curve
C
.
By Lemma
2.2
, Lemma
3.4

and Proposition
3.5
,
there is a unique
indecomposable factor
X_{C}
of
X_{{\rm ad}}
such that the composition of

\pi:X\to X_{{\rm ad}}
with the projection
X_{{\rm ad}}\to X_{C}
sends
C

isomorphically to its image. In this subsection, we will handle in details
the cases where
X_{C}
is of group or Hermitian types.

We first handle the group type, where
X_{C}
is the
wonderful completion of an adjoint simple group
H_{C}
.
The Lie algebra of
H_{C}
is denoted by
\mathfrak{h}_{C}
, and we
still denote by
C
the highest weight curve in
X_{C}
.
By the main result of
[
BF15
]
,

X_{C}
has a unique family of minimal rational
curves
{\mathcal{L}}
. Moreover, the tangent map

\tau_{x_{C}}:{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{C}}\to{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{h}_{C})

is an
H_{C}
-equivariant isomorphism to its image

{\mathcal{C}}_{x_{C}}
.
If
H_{C}
is of type
{\rm A}_{r}
,
i.e.,
H_{C}\simeq{\rm PGL}(V)
where
V
is a vector
space of dimension
r+1
,
then
\mathfrak{h}_{C}\simeq{\rm End}(V)/{\mathbb{C}}\,{\rm id}
.
When
r=1
, we have
X_{C}={\mathbb{P}}({\rm End}(V))

and hence
{\mathcal{C}}_{x_{C}}={\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{h}_{C})
. On the
other hand, when
r\geq 2
, the VMRT

{\mathcal{C}}_{x_{C}}
is isomorphic to

{\mathbb{P}}(V)\times{\mathbb{P}}(V^{\vee})
embedded in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{h}_{C})

via the Segre embedding

{\mathbb{P}}(V)\times{\mathbb{P}}(V^{\vee})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(V\otimes V^{\vee})={\mathbb{P}}({\rm End}(V))

followed by the linear projection

{\mathbb{P}}({\rm End}(V))\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}({\rm End}(V)/{\mathbb{C}}\,{\rm id})
.
In all other types, we have

{\mathcal{C}}_{x_{C}}={\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{C,\min})
,
the projectivization of the minimal nilpotent
orbit in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{h}_{C})
.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
X_{C}
is of group type, then

\partial X\cdot C
equals
1
or
2
.
In the former case, every component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{C,\min})
.
In the latter case, we have
H_{C}\simeq{\rm PGL}(V)
;
moreover, every component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is isomorphic
to
{\mathbb{P}}(V)\times{\mathbb{P}}(V^{\vee})
if
\dim(V)\geq 3
,
and to
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{h}_{C})\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
if

\dim(V)=2
.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
3.6
that

1\leq\partial X\cdot C\leq\partial X_{{\rm ad}}\cdot\pi(C)
.
Moreover, the line bundle on
X_{{\rm ad}}
associated with
the divisor
\partial X_{{\rm ad}}
equals

{\mathcal{L}}_{X_{{\rm ad}}}(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r})

with the notation of
[
BF15
, Section 3]
. By using

[
BF15
, Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4]
, it follows that

\partial X_{{\rm ad}}\cdot\pi(C)=2
if
H_{C}
is
of type
{\rm A}_{r}
; otherwise,

\partial X_{{\rm ad}}\cdot\pi(C)=1
.

In the latter case, we must have

\partial X\cdot C=\partial X_{{\rm ad}}\cdot\pi(C)
.
So every component of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is isomorphic to
the orbit
H_{C}\cdot C
, by Proposition
3.6

again. Moreover,
H_{C}\cdot C={\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{C,\min})
.

In the former case, if
r\geq 2
then

{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{C}}={\mathbb{P}}(V)\times{\mathbb{P}}(V^{\vee})

consists of two orbits of
H_{C}={\rm PGL}(V)
:
a closed orbit of codimension
1
(the incidence variety,
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{C,\min})
),
and an open orbit isomorphic to

\SL_{r+1}/{\rm GL}_{r}
, and hence simply
connected. If
r=1
then
{\mathcal{L}}_{x_{C}}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}

is simply connected as well.

If
\partial X\cdot C=2
, then by Proposition

3.6
again, we get a finite
surjective
H
-equivariant morphism

\pi_{*,x}:{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\to{\mathbb{P}}(V)\times{\mathbb{P}}(V^{\vee})
.
Since the open orbit in the right-hand side is
simply connected, it follows that

\pi_{*,x}
is birational on each component,
and hence an isomorphism in view of Zariski’s main
theorem.

On the other hand, if
\partial X\cdot C=1
,
then the image of
\pi_{*,x}
is the closed orbit
and we conclude as above.
∎

Example 3.9
.

Assume that
\pi:X\to X_{{\rm ad}}

is the wonderful completion of
{\rm PGL}(V)

Next, we handle the Hermitian type, where

Proposition 3.10
.

If
X_{C}

.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.6
, we may assume that

X=X_{C}
, and hence
G=G_{C}
and

, where

are
opposite parabolic subgroups and
P\supset B
.
We now view

With the notation of Subsection

\overline{G}

If

Otherwise,

equivariantly for the action of

Consider first the exceptional case, where

.
Then we have a

with the variety
of lines in

through its base point. Since

we obtain

On the other hand, we have

Also, the boundary of

Next, we consider the non-exceptional case,

By

As a consequence, we have

where the latter equality is proved as in the exceptional

∎

4
Minimal rational curves on wonderful symmetric varieties

In this section, we deal with wonderful embeddings
of adjoint indecomposable symmetric spaces.
These are of the form
G_{{\rm ad}}/H
, where
G_{{\rm ad}}
is an
adjoint semisimple group and
H
is the fixed point
subgroup of an involution
\sigma
. Then
\sigma
lifts
to a unique involution of the universal cover

G=G_{\rm sc}
of
G_{{\rm ad}}
, that we still denote by
\sigma

for simplicity; moreover,
G^{\sigma}
is connected
(see
[
St68
]
for these results).
As seen in Subsection
2.5
, we have

G_{{\rm ad}}/H=G/N
, where

N=\N_{G}(G^{\sigma})
; moreover,
G/N
is of group,
Hermitian, or simple type. We will consider its
wonderful embedding

.

We start with reminders on restricted root systems
(Subsection
4.1
) and their connection
to curves and divisors on
X
(Subsection

4.2
).
Many results on these topics are well known but we could not find a good reference, so we included proofs for the convenience of the reader. From this we obtain an explicit description of the classes of
minimal rational curves in
X

(Subsection
4.3
).
We then compute the dimension of these families of minimal
rational curves
{\mathcal{K}}
using the contact structures on projectivised
nilpotent orbits (Subsection
4.4
). It turns out that in all cases except for
X
of restricted type
\textrm{A}_{r}
, the family
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
has the same dimension as the orbit
N\cdot C
where
C\in{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is a highest weight curve, which in turn implies that
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}=N\cdot C
.
We deal with
X
of restricted type
\textrm{A}_{r}
separately (Subsection
4.5
).
We conclude with a full description of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}

(Subsection
4.6
).

4.1
Restricted root system

Let us first recall a few facts on the restricted root system;
we refer to
[
Vu90
]
and
[
Ti11
]
for details.
Let
T_{{\rm s}}
be a maximal torus of split type, and

S\subset T_{{\rm s}}
its maximal split subtorus:

S=\{t\in T_{{\rm s}}\ |\ \sigma(t)=t^{-1}\}^{0}
.
Let
R
be the root system associated to the pair

(G,T_{{\rm s}})
. Then
\sigma
acts on
R
.
Set
\overline{S}=S/S^{\sigma}
,
\overline{\mathfrak{X}}=\mathfrak{X}(\overline{S})
and

\overline{\chi}=\chi-\sigma(\chi)
for
\chi\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}})
.
We have an identification

\overline{\mathfrak{X}}=\{\overline{\chi}\ |\ \chi\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}})\}
.
Define the subset
\overline{R}\subset\overline{\mathfrak{X}}
via

\overline{R}=\{{\overline{\alpha}}\ |\ \alpha\in R\}.

Then
\overline{R}
is an irreducible root system called the

restricted root system
. It may be non-reduced
(see Remark
4.1
below).

Recall from Subsection
2.3

that
L=\C_{G}(S)
is the Levi subgroup
containing
T_{{\rm s}}
of a parabolic subgroup
P\subset G

and that
\sigma(P)
is the opposite parabolic subgroup
to
P
with common Levi subgroup
L
.
Let
B_{{\rm s}}\subset P
be a Borel subgroup and let

\Delta\subset R^{+}\subset R
be the sets of simple roots
and positive roots defined by
B_{{\rm s}}
.
Then for
\alpha\in R^{+}
,
we have
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
if and only if
\alpha

is a root of
L
; moreover, if

\sigma(\alpha)\neq\alpha
, then

\sigma(\alpha)<0
. Set

\Delta_{1}=\{\alpha\in\Delta\ |\ \sigma(\alpha)<0\}

and
\Delta_{0}=\Delta\setminus\Delta_{1}
.
Then
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
for any

\alpha\in\Delta_{0}
. Define
\overline{\Delta}\subset\overline{\mathfrak{X}}
via

\overline{\Delta}=\{{\overline{\alpha}}\ |\ \alpha\in\Delta_{1}\}.

Then
\overline{\Delta}
is a basis of
\overline{R}\subset\overline{\mathfrak{X}}
. In particular

|\overline{\Delta}|={\rm rk}(\overline{\mathfrak{X}})=\dim T_{{\rm s}}=r
is the rank of
X
.
Furthermore, there exists a length-preserving involution

{\overline{\sigma}}
on
\Delta
, preserving
\Delta_{1}
and acting
as
-w_{0,L}
on
\Delta_{0}

(where
w_{0,L}
is the element of maximal length in
W_{L}
, the Weyl group of
L
), such that for any
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
, we have

\sigma(\alpha)+{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)=-\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\beta}\beta

with
c_{\beta}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
(see
[
deCS99
, Section 1.5]
for these facts). In particular, if

{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
, then

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle\geq 0
.
Note that for
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}
,
we have

{\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}\Leftrightarrow(\beta=\alpha\textrm{ or }\beta={\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha))
.

A root
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
such that

{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
and

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle\neq 0

is called

exceptional
.
If
\alpha
is exceptional, then

\sigma(\alpha)\neq-\alpha
. Moreover,

\beta={\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
is also exceptional
and one of the following two conditions is satisfied:
either
\sigma(\alpha)\neq-\beta
and

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
,
or
\sigma(\alpha)=-\beta
and

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle\neq 0
(see
[
deCS99
, Lemma 4.3]
).
If there exists an exceptional root, then
\overline{R}
and
X
are called
exceptional
. This definition is equivalent
to the one given in Subsection
2.5
,
see for example
[
deCS99
, Lemma 4.7]
.
Note that by loc. cit.,
there are at most two exceptional roots
(thus, of the form

\alpha
and
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
).

Remark 4.1
.

If
\overline{R}
is exceptional, it
is non-reduced.
In fact, for
\alpha
exceptional, we have

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle\neq 0
.
As
\alpha
and
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
are different
but of the same length, we have

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
.
Thus,
\gamma=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)\in R
and

{\overline{\gamma}}=2{\overline{\alpha}}
. In particular,

{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
and
\overline{R}
is non-reduced.

Example 4.2
.

There are non-reduced restricted root systems which are
non-exceptional (actually only two families: types CII and FII,
see Appendix). For example, if
G={\rm Sp}_{6}
,
there exists an involution
\sigma
such that

G^{\sigma}={\rm Sp}_{2}\times{\rm Sp}_{4}
and with the labeling
of the simple roots as in Bourbaki
[
Bo68
]
,
we have
\Delta_{0}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3}\}
and

\Delta_{1}=\{\alpha_{2}\}
. Set
\alpha=\alpha_{2}
, then

{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)=\alpha
,
\sigma(\alpha)=-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3})

and

{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=\gamma\in R
.
We have
{\overline{\gamma}}=2{\overline{\alpha}}
; thus,

\overline{R}=\{-2{\overline{\alpha}},-{\overline{\alpha}},{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\}

but

is not exceptional.

Let
\alpha\in R^{+}
such that
\sigma(\alpha)<0
.
The roots
\alpha
and
\sigma(\alpha)
have the same length.
As explained in
[
Vu90

, three cases occur
and the coroot

is defined accordingly:

If
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
, then

{\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\vee}
.

2.

If
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=0
, then

{\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\vee}-\sigma(\alpha)^{\vee})
.

3.

If
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
, then

{\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\alpha^{\vee}-\sigma(\alpha)^{\vee}
.

Case (3) above actually occurs if and only if
\overline{R}
is non-reduced, see Proposition
4.3
(5) below. In the next proposition, we summarise the results on restricted root systems needed for the study of curves and divisors on

4.2
Divisors and restricted root system

We relate the Picard group of
X
(viewed as the group of divisors up to linear equivalence), to the restricted root system
\overline{R}
. We will need some definitions from the theory of spherical varieties, we refer to
[
Pe14
]

or
[
Ti11
, Section 17]

for further details. The variety
X
is spherical: it is a normal
G
-variety such that
B_{{\rm s}}
has a dense orbit. This implies that
B_{{\rm s}}
acts on
X
with finitely many orbits. In particular there are finitely many prime
B_{{\rm s}}
-stable divisors in
X
. The boundary
\partial X=X_{1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{r}
with

the rank of

is the union of the prime
G
-stable divisors. The prime
B_{{\rm s}}
-stable divisors which are not

-stable are called colors. We denote by
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
the set of colors and by
{\mathcal{V}}_{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}

the set of prime
G
-stable divisors.

We start with a description of prime
G
-stable divisors.
Let
j:Y\to X
be the inclusion of the closed
G
-orbit in
X
.
Let
B^{-}_{{\rm s}}
be the Borel subgroup containing
T_{{\rm s}}
and
opposite to
B_{{\rm s}}
, and let
z\in Y
be the unique

B^{-}_{{\rm s}}
-fixed point in
Y
. Then the stabilizer of
z
in
G

is
\sigma(P)
, and this identifies
Y
with
G/\sigma(P)
.
For any character
\lambda
of
\sigma(P)
,
we have a homogeneous line bundle

{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}(\lambda)=G\times^{\sigma(P)}{\mathbb{C}}_{\lambda}

on
G/\sigma(P)
, where
{\mathbb{C}}_{\lambda}
is the
1
-dimensional

\sigma(P)
-representation of weight
\lambda
. We may now
state the following result (see

[
deCP83
, Proposition 8.1 and Corollary 8.2]
):

Proposition 4.5
.

1.

The map
j^{*}:{\rm Pic}(X)\to{\rm Pic}(Y)
is injective.

2.

For any
i\in[1,r]
, the torus
T_{{\rm s}}
acts on
T_{z}X/T_{z}X_{i}
with weight some
{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\in\overline{\Delta}
and the resulting map

{\mathcal{V}}_{X}\to\overline{\Delta},X_{i}\mapsto{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}
is bijective.

3.

We have
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X_{i})|_{Y}=\mathscr{L}_{Y}({\overline{\alpha}}_{i})
for any such
i
.

Remark 4.6
.

We set
X_{{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}}:=X_{i}
for

so that
X_{\overline{\beta}}
is well defined for
{\overline{\beta}}\in\overline{\Delta}
.

Next we want to relate colors and restricted roots. This is more difficult, since there may be more colors than restricted roots as we will see next. Recall the description of the
Picard group of
X
(see
[
Pe14
, Theorem 3.2.4]
):

Proposition 4.7
.

We have
{\rm Pic}(X)=\bigoplus_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\mathbb{Z}}[D]
.

There is a correspondence between colors and restricted roots that we describe now.
For
\alpha\in\Delta
, recall that
G_{\alpha}
denotes
the subgroup of
G
generated by
U_{\alpha}

and
U_{-\alpha}
, and
set
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)=\{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}\ |\ G_{\alpha}\cdot D\neq D\}
. Note that if
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
, then
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)=\emptyset
(see
[
Lu01
, Section 1.4]
).

Proposition 4.8
.

For
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
, the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
consists of a unique element
D_{\alpha}
.
Moreover, for any distinct
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}
,
we have
D_{\alpha}=D_{\beta}
if and only if
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
and
\sigma(\alpha)=-\beta
.

Proof.

The assertion that
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
has a unique element
follows from a result of Luna which holds true for any wonderful variety (see
[
Lu01
, Section 1.4]
).
Luna proves that three cases, called
(a)
,
(a^{\prime})
and
(b)
, occur. In cases
(a^{\prime})
and
(b)
, the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
consists of a unique element, while in case
(a)
the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
consists of two elements. We prove that case
(a)
does not occur: in this case, by
[
Lu01
, Section 1.4.(2)]
, we have
\alpha={\overline{\gamma}}=\gamma-\sigma(\gamma)
for some
\gamma\in\Delta
. Thus,
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
and
{\overline{\alpha}}=2\alpha=2{\overline{\gamma}}\in\overline{\Delta}
. In particular,
{\overline{\gamma}},2{\overline{\gamma}}\in\overline{\Delta}
, which contradicts the fact that
\overline{\Delta}
is a basis of
\overline{R}
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}
, there are, according to
[
Lu01
, Proposition 3.2]
, the following possibilities to have
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\beta)\neq\emptyset
:

•

Both
\alpha
and
\beta
are in
\overline{\Delta}
, in which case it may happen that
|{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\beta)|=3
.

•

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
and
\alpha+\beta\in\overline{\Delta}
or
\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)\in\overline{\Delta}
.

The first case does not occur by the above argument.
If
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
and

\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)\in\overline{\Delta}
or

\alpha+\beta\in\overline{\Delta}
, then there exists
\gamma\in\Delta_{1}
such that
{\overline{\gamma}}=\gamma-\sigma(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)
or
{\overline{\gamma}}=\gamma-\sigma(\gamma)=\alpha+\beta
. Write

\sigma(\gamma)+{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)=-\sum_{\delta\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\delta}\delta.

Then, we have

\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)=\gamma+{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)+\sum_{\delta\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\delta}\delta\ \ \ \textrm{ or }\ \ \ \alpha+\beta=\gamma+{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)+\sum_{\delta\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\delta}\delta.

In the former case,
this implies
\alpha=\beta
and

\gamma+{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)\leq\alpha
,
which is impossible.
In the latter case,
we get that
\gamma
equals
\alpha
or
\beta
. Assume for example that
\gamma=\alpha
, then we have
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)={\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)=\beta
and
c_{\delta}=0
for all
\delta\in\Delta_{0}
. We thus have
\sigma(\alpha)=\sigma(\gamma)=-{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)=-\beta
. Note that
{\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}
.

Conversely, if
\beta=-\sigma(\alpha)
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
, then
{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha+\beta
and by
[
Lu01
, Proposition 3.2]
again, we have
D_{\alpha}=D_{\beta}
.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

If
X
is the wonderful compactification of a nonadjoint symmetric space, then there may be some simple roots
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
with
|{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)|=2
. A typical example is the case
G=\SL_{2}
and
H=T
a maximal torus. Then
N=\N_{G}(T)
is the normalizer of the torus. There is a unique projective compactification of
G/H
given by
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with two
B
-stable divisors
D^{+}
and
D^{-}
and both are such that
\SL_{2}\cdot D^{\pm}=X
. We thus have
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)=\{D^{+},D^{-}\}
, where
\alpha
is the unique simple root of
G
. The wonderful compactification

of

is the quotient of

by the involution exchanging the two factors and is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
with a unique
B
-stable divisor

, so that
{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{\rm ad}}(\alpha)=\{D\}
.

The restricted root system is
\overline{R}=\{-{\overline{\alpha}},{\overline{\alpha}}\}
in both cases. But for a maximal split torus

, we have
\alpha\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}}/H\cap T_{{\rm s}})
while
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}}/N\cap T_{{\rm s}})
and
{\overline{\alpha}}=2\alpha\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}}/N\cap T_{{\rm s}})
.

In view of Proposition
4.8
, we may define a map
\zeta:\Delta_{1}\to{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
by
\alpha\mapsto D_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

The map
\zeta:\Delta_{1}\to{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
is surjective.

Proof.

For
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
, there exists
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
such that
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
. Indeed, since
G
is generated by the
G_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\Delta
and since
D
is not
G
-stable, there exists at least one
\alpha\in\Delta
with

. Furthermore, by
[
Lu01
, Section 1.4.(1)]
, we have
\alpha\not\in\Delta_{0}
; thus,
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}
with
\alpha\neq\beta
. Then
{\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}
if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)
is a pair of exceptional roots or (
\beta=-\sigma(\alpha)
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
).

Proof.

Write
{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha+{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)+\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\gamma}\gamma
with
c_{\gamma}\geq 0
. Since
\alpha
,
\beta
,
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
and
{\overline{\sigma}}(\beta)
lie in
\Delta_{1}
, the equality
{\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}
together with the condition
\alpha\neq\beta
implies that
\beta={\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
. If
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle\neq 0
, then
(\alpha,\beta)
is a pair of exceptional roots. Otherwise, we have

0=\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=-\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\gamma}\langle\alpha^{\vee},\gamma\rangle
.
This implies the vanishings
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
and
c_{\gamma}\langle\alpha^{\vee},\gamma\rangle=0
for all
\gamma
. Since
\beta={\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
, we have
\sigma(\beta)=-\alpha-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\gamma}\gamma
. This implies the equality
\sigma(\beta)=-\alpha
since otherwise, the support of the root
\sigma(\beta)
being connected, there would exist a
\gamma
with
c_{\gamma}\langle\alpha^{\vee},\gamma\rangle\neq 0
.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

The map
\tau:{\mathcal{D}}_{X}\to\overline{\Delta},D\mapsto{\overline{\alpha}}
, with
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
such that
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
, is well defined and surjective. Moreover, this map is injective except if
X
is exceptional, in which case the only non-trivial fiber is
\tau^{-1}({\overline{\alpha}})=\{D_{\alpha},D_{\beta}\}
, where
\alpha,\beta
are the exceptional roots.

Proof.

The restricted root
{\overline{\alpha}}
does not depend on the choice of
\alpha
with
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)
: if
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\beta)
, then
\beta=-\sigma(\alpha)
and
{\overline{\beta}}={\overline{\alpha}}
by Proposition
4.8
. For the surjectivity, note that the composition
\tau\circ\zeta
is surjective, since
\tau\circ\zeta(\alpha)={\overline{\alpha}}
.

Assume first that
\alpha
is exceptional and set
\beta={\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
. Then
\tau(D_{\alpha})={\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}=\tau(D_{\beta})
. If
D_{\alpha}=D_{\beta}
, then
{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{X}(\beta)\neq\emptyset
. We thus have
\beta=-\sigma(\alpha)
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=-\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
, a contradiction with the fact that
\alpha
is exceptional. Therefore,
D_{\alpha}\neq D_{\beta}
and
\tau
is not injective. Conversely, if
{\overline{\alpha}}={\overline{\beta}}
, then by Lemma
4.11
, we have that
(\alpha,\beta)
is a pair of exceptional roots or (
\beta=-\sigma(\alpha)
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle=0
). In the second case, we have

so that
\tau
is not injective exactly when

is exceptional and the only non-trivial fiber is
\tau^{-1}({\overline{\alpha}})=\{D_{\alpha},D_{\beta}\}
, where
\alpha,\beta
are the exceptional roots.
∎

We recover a classical result (see

Proposition 4.13

We have
{\rm Pic}(X)={\mathbb{Z}}^{r+s}
where

. Moreover,

Proof.

By Proposition
4.7

{\rm Pic}(X)=\bigoplus_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\mathbb{Z}}[D]

shows that

∎

We now compute the restrictions
j^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(D)
for
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
.
The next proposition is a direct application of results
in
[
Lu97
]
. For
\alpha\in\Delta

\varpi_{\alpha}
be the fundamental weight associated to
\alpha

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
and let
\lambda_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{X}(T_{{\rm s}})
be such that
j^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(D_{\alpha})=\mathscr{L}_{Y}(\lambda_{\alpha})
. Then we have

\lambda_{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\varpi_{\alpha}&\textrm{ if $\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha$,}\\
\varpi_{\alpha}+\varpi_{{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)}&\textrm{ if $\sigma(\alpha)=-{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)$ and $\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=0$,}\\
\varpi_{\alpha}&\textrm{ otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Proof.

This result is
[
Ti11
, Lemma 30.24]
, due to A. Foschi.
We provide details for the reader’s convenience.

For
\beta\in\Delta_{1}
, let
Y_{\beta}\subset Y
be the Schubert curve dual to
{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}(\varpi_{\beta})
. Then by
[

Y_{\beta}\cdot j^{*}D_{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\delta_{\alpha,\beta}&\textrm{ if $\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha$,}\\
\delta_{D_{\alpha},D_{\beta}}&\textrm{ otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

The result follows from this and the facts that if
|\zeta^{-1}(D_{\alpha})|>1

\zeta^{-1}(D_{\alpha})=\{\alpha,{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\}
and that this occurs if and only if
\sigma(\alpha)=-{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=0
(Proposition
4.8
).
∎

The above proof implicitly uses the fact
that there exists a family of irreducible

B
-stable curves
(C_{D})_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}
such that
the classes
[C_{D}]
in the Chow group
A_{1}(X)
form the dual
basis to the basis
([D])_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}
of
{\rm Pic}(X)
(see
[
Lu97
, Lemma 3.1.2]

{\widehat{\alpha}}
for
{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{\Delta}
and the notation
X_{\overline{\beta}}
for
{\overline{\beta}}\in\overline{\Delta}
from Remark

.
Also, denote

by

Corollary 4.15
.

We have
X_{\overline{\beta}}\cdot C_{\alpha}=\langle{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}
.

Proof.

Recall that
j^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(X_{\overline{\beta}})=\mathscr{L}_{Y}({\overline{\beta}})
.
Note that we have
{\overline{\beta}}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle\varpi_{\gamma}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{1}}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle\varpi_{\gamma}
since
\langle\gamma,{\overline{\beta}}\rangle=0
for
\gamma\in\Delta_{0}
. Define the following subsets of
\Delta_{1}
:

A=\{\gamma\in\Delta_{1}\ |\ \sigma(\gamma)=-\gamma\},

B=\{\gamma\in\Delta_{1}\ |\ \sigma(\gamma)=-{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)\textrm{ and }\langle\gamma^{\vee},\sigma(\gamma)\rangle=0\},

\text{ and }C=\Delta_{1}\setminus(A\cup B).

Since
{\overline{\sigma}}
induces a fixed point free involution on
B

and since

\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle=\langle{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma^{\vee}),{\overline{\beta}}\rangle

for
\gamma\in B
, Proposition
4.14
implies

{\overline{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in A}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle\lambda_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in B}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle\lambda_{\gamma}+\sum_{\gamma\in C}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle\lambda_{\gamma}.

By Proposition
4.8
,
D_{{\overline{\sigma}}(\gamma)}=D_{\gamma}
for
\beta\in B
, giving the following equality on the level of divisors:

X_{\overline{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{D=D_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in A}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle D_{\gamma}+\sum_{D=D_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in B}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle D_{\gamma}+\sum_{D=D_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in C}\langle\gamma^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle D_{\gamma}.

We get

X_{\overline{\beta}}\cdot C_{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle&\textrm{ if $\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha$,}\\
\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle&\textrm{ otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

We now compare the above values to
\langle{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle
. If
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
, then
{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee}={\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\vee}
proving the first case.
If
\sigma(\alpha)\neq-\alpha
, we have two possibilities: either
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=0
or
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
. In the former case, we have
{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee}={\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\vee}-\sigma(\alpha)^{\vee})
; thus,
\langle{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle+\langle-\sigma(\alpha)^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle)=\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\beta}}\rangle
. Finally, if

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
,
then
{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}{\overline{\alpha}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\vee}-\sigma(\alpha)^{\vee})
and the result follows as before.
∎

Recall from Proposition
4.7

{\rm Pic}(X)=\bigoplus_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\mathbb{Z}}[D]

.

The map
\psi:A_{1}(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\Delta}^{\vee}

The image of the monoid of effective curves is the monoid generated by the positive coroots.

The surjectivity follows from the surjectivity of

Recall that a curve class

\psi

and

\psi(\gamma^{+})={\overline{\Theta}}^{\vee}=\psi(\gamma^{-})

The following lemma generalizes

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\eta:{\mathbb{G}}_{m}\to\overline{S}
be a dominant cocharacter,
\eta:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to X
the corresponding morphism, and
C_{\eta}
its image.

1.

We have
\eta(0)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{I}
, where

I:=\{i\in[1,r]\ |\ \langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle\neq 0\}
.

2.

We have
\eta(\infty)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{J}
, where
J:=\{j\in[1,r]\ |\ \langle\eta,w_{0}({\overline{\alpha}}_{j})\rangle\neq 0\}

3.

The morphism
\eta:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to C_{\eta}
is an isomorphism if and only if there exists

such that
\langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle=1

4.

For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{X}_{X}(T_{{\rm s}})
, we have
{\rm deg}(\eta)^{*}\mathscr{L}_{X}(\lambda)=\langle\eta,\lambda-w_{0}\lambda\rangle
.

Proof.

(1) Since
\eta
is dominant, it extends to a morphism

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to X_{Y,B}\cap\overline{T_{s}\cdot x}

defined by
t\mapsto(t^{\langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle})_{i\in[1,r]}
, where
X_{Y,B}\cap\overline{T_{s}\cdot x}

is identified with
{\mathbb{A}}^{r}
as above. In particular,
\eta(0)\in X_{Y,B}
and vanishes on the coodinates with
indices in
\{i\in[1,r]\ |\ \langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle\neq 0\}
. Moreover, the morphism
\eta:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to C_{\eta}
is a local isomorphism at
\eta(0)
if and only if there exists
i
such that
\langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle=1
.

(2) Consider the open affine subset

w_{0}\cdot X_{Y,B}
of
X
. It is isomorphic to

\R_{u}(P)^{w_{0}}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{r}
with a linear action of
T_{{\rm s}}
on
{\mathbb{A}}^{r}
with weights
w_{0}(\overline{\Delta})
. All these weights are non-negative linear combinations of negative roots. In particular, the one-parameter subgroup
-\eta
acts with non-negative weights on
{\mathbb{A}}^{r}
, and hence extends to a morphism
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to w_{0}\cdot X_{Y,B}
,
t\mapsto(t^{\langle\eta,-w_{0}({\overline{\alpha}}_{i})\rangle})_{i\in[1,r]}
. It follows that
\eta(\infty)=(-\eta)(0)\in w_{0}\cdot X_{Y,B}
and as above,
\eta(\infty)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{J}
. Moreover, the morphism
\eta:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to C_{\eta}
is a local isomorphism at
\eta(\infty)
if and only if there exists
i
such that
\langle\eta,-w_{0}({\overline{\alpha}}_{i})\rangle=1
. Note that
-w_{0}({\overline{\alpha}}_{i})=-w_{0}(\alpha_{i})+w_{0}(\sigma(\alpha_{i}))=-\overline{w_{0}(\alpha_{i})}
and since

permutes

4.3
(1),
the previous condition is true if and only if there exists

such that
\langle\eta,{\overline{\alpha}}_{i}\rangle=1
.

(3) This follows from the above conditions at
\eta(0)
and
\eta(\infty)
.

(4) The pull-back of
\mathscr{L}_{X}(\lambda)
to

X_{Y,B}
(resp.
w_{0}\cdot X_{Y,B}
)
has a trivializing section of weight
\lambda

(resp.
w_{0}(\lambda)
). As a consequence,
the line bundle
(\eta)^{*}\mathscr{L}_{X}(\lambda)

is a
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-linearized line bundle on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with weights
\langle\eta,\lambda\rangle
at

and
\langle\eta,w_{0}\lambda\rangle
at

. Since the degree of such a line bundle is the difference of its weights, this yields our assertion.
∎

Remark 4.24

Note that the value of

Recall from Proposition

\dim{\mathcal{K}}_{x}=-K_{X}\cdot C-2

By the adjunction formula, we have

We compute the dimension of

(we thank an anonymous referee for the uniform proof presented here). Since

is a nilpotent element, there exist

such that

is an

-triple. Then

with

and

is the space of highest weight vectors of

acting on
\mathfrak{g}
. Its dimension is equal to the number of simple
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-modules thus equals
\dim\mathfrak{g}(0)+\dim\mathfrak{g}(1)
, since every simple
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-module intersects either
\mathfrak{g}(0)
or
\mathfrak{g}(1)
in dimension
1
(depending on the parity of the highest weight). We thus have
\dim G\cdot m=\dim\mathfrak{g}-\dim\mathfrak{g}(0)-\dim\mathfrak{g}(1)=\dim\mathfrak{g}(1)+2\sum_{k\geq 2}\dim\mathfrak{g}(k)
, since

. Since

or

by Proposition

, we have

, thus

.

In case

i.e.

while if

and in particular

. If

\langle h,\alpha\rangle=\langle h,\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=\langle\sigma(h),\alpha\rangle=-\langle h,\alpha\rangle=0
thus

\langle h,\rho_{L}\rangle=0
. If
\alpha\in R^{+}
is such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}(i)
for
i\geq 0
, then
\langle h,\alpha\rangle=i
, thus

\langle h,\kappa\rangle=\langle h,2\rho\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{\alpha\in R^{+},\ \mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}(i)}i=\dim\mathfrak{g}(1)+2\dim\mathfrak{g}(2).

We get
\dim G\cdot m=2\langle{\overline{\Theta}}^{\vee},\kappa\rangle
, proving the result.
∎

4.4
Contact structure

In this subsection, we compute the dimension of
H\cdot m
for
m\in T_{x}C\setminus\{0\}
,
C\in{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
a family of minimal rational curves.
We first gather some facts on orbits associated with symmetric spaces, and in particular prove that orbits of symmetric subgroups of
G
are Lagrangian subvarieties in nilpotent
G
-orbits. Recall the following general definitions.

Definition 4.25
.

Let
\widehat{M}
be a smooth complex variety of dimension
2n+2
and let
M
be a smooth complex variety of dimension
2n+1
.

1.

A symplectic structure
on
\widehat{M}
is
a closed skew form

\omega:T\widehat{M}\times_{\widehat{M}}T\widehat{M}\to\widehat{M}\times{\mathbb{C}}

which is everywhere nondegenerate.

2.

A contact structure on
M
is an everywhere non-vanishing map
\eta:TM\to\mathscr{L}
, where
\mathscr{L}

is a line bundle, such that the bilinear form
\theta_{\eta}:D\times D\rightarrow TM/D
defined by
(u,v)\mapsto[u,v]\ (\text{mod}\ D)
on
D:={\rm Ker}\,\eta
is non-degenerate for all
m\in M
.

If
\eta:TM\to\mathscr{L}
is a contact structure on
M
, then there is a natural symplectic structure
\omega
defined by
\omega=d(p^{*}\eta)
on
\widehat{M}=\mathscr{L}^{\times}
, where
\mathscr{L}^{\times}
is the
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-bundle over
M
with structure map
p:\widehat{M}\to M
, associated to
\mathscr{L}
and we identify
p^{*}\mathscr{L}
to the trivial line bundle over
\widehat{M}
.

Definition 4.26
.

We say that a symplectic structure
\omega
on
\widehat{M}
is induced by a contact structure

\eta:TM\to\mathscr{L}
on
M
if

\widehat{M}=\mathscr{L}^{\times}
and
\omega=d(p^{*}\eta)
.

The most famous examples of the above structures are given by coadjoint orbits in the dual
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of a connected reductive group
G
. For later purposes, we present a (non-canonical) version of
Kostant-Kirillov form
which takes place in
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie algebra and not
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
. If
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, the Killing form
K
identifies
\mathfrak{g}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
and the construction is canonical.

Example 4.27
.

Choose an invariant non-degenerate bilinear form
K
on
\mathfrak{g}

(e.g., the Killing form if
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple).
Let
m
be a non-zero element in
\mathfrak{g}
and let
\widehat{M}_{m}=G\cdot m
and
M_{m}=G\cdot[m]\subset{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})
be the orbits of
m\in\mathfrak{g}
and of
[m]\in{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})
under the adjoint action.
Let
G_{m}
be the isotropy subgroup of
G
at
m
, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
. Define the anti-symmetric bilinear form
K_{m}
on
\mathfrak{g}
by
K_{m}(y,z)=K(m,[y,z])
. The non-degeneracy of
K
and the equality
K_{m}(x,y)=K([m,y],x)
implies that we have
{\rm Ker}\,K_{m}=\mathfrak{g}_{m}
. Thus,
K_{m}
descends to a symplectic form

\omega_{m}\colon\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{m}\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
at
m\in\mathfrak{g}
. By the Jacobi identity, the form
\omega_{m}
is closed.

If
m
is such that the orbit
\widehat{M}_{m}=G.m
is the cone in
\mathfrak{g}
over
M_{m}=G\cdot[m]\subset{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})

(i.e., the affine cone minus the origin), then the arguments in

[
Be98
, Proposition 2.2]
adapt verbatim and yield a contact structure
\eta
on
M_{m}
which induces the symplectic form
\omega_{m}
. In particular, if

is a nilpotent element in
\mathfrak{g}
, then the existence of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple containing

ensures that
\widehat{M}_{m}
is the cone over
M_{m}
(see
[
Be98
, Paragraph (2.4)]
).

Given a symplectic structure on a variety
\widehat{M}
or a contact structure on a variety
M
, it is natural to ask for Lagrangian or Legendrian subvarieties; we recall their definitions. A Lagrangian subspace in a symplectic vector space
V
of dimension
2m
is an isotropic subspace of maximal dimension,
i.e.
, of dimension
m
.

Definition 4.28
.

Let
\widehat{M}
have a symplectic structure
\omega
. A smooth subvariety
\widehat{L}\subset\widehat{M}
is called

if, for all

,
the subspace
T_{m}\widehat{L}\subset T_{m}\widehat{M}
is Lagrangian for the symplectic form
\omega_{m}
on
T_{m}\widehat{M}
.

Definition 4.29
.

Let
M
have a contact structure
\eta
and let
p:\widehat{M}\to M
be the
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-bundle
\mathscr{L}^{\times}
associated to the line bundle
\mathscr{L}
with symplectic form
\omega=d(p^{*}\eta)
. A smooth subvariety
L\subset M
is called
Legendrian
if
\widehat{L}=p^{-1}(L)
is Lagrangian in
\widehat{M}
.

Example 4.30
.

Let
G
be simple and
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra. Let
m\in\mathfrak{g}
be a highest weight vector. Set
{\mathcal{O}}_{\min}=G\cdot m
and
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{\min})=G\cdot[m]\subset{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})
. The latter is called the
adjoint variety
of
G
. It is the unique closed orbit of
G
in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})
under the adjoint action.
Consider the Grassmannian variety
{\rm Gr}(2,\mathfrak{g})
of lines in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathfrak{g})
, and let

{\mathbb{L}}_{G}\subset{\rm Gr}(2,\mathfrak{g})
be the subset of lines contained in
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{O}}_{\min})

and passing through a given point of that variety. Then
{\mathbb{L}}_{G}
is a smooth Legendrian variety
in its linear span (in the Plücker embedding of

)
and is homogeneous under the isotropy subgroup

(see
[

, Theorem 1]
).
This Legendrian variety

is called
the subadjoint variety
. Note that in type

we have

: the subadjoint variety is empty, since

is the second Veronese embedding of

and hence contains no line. We will see in Subsection
6.3

that

(viewed as a subvariety of its linear span)
can be recovered as the VMRT of a specific wonderful adjoint
symmetric variety for

.

Let
H\subset G
be a symmetric subgroup with group involution
\sigma
.
The following result is well known, we include a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 4.31

(
[
KR71
, Proposition 5]
)
.

Let
m\in\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
be nonzero.
Set
\widehat{L}_{m}:=H\cdot m
and
L_{m}:=H\cdot[m]
.
Then the variety
\widehat{L}_{m}
is Lagrangian in
\widehat{M}_{m}
; in particular,
\dim G\cdot m=2\dim H\cdot m
.

If
\widehat{L}_{m}
is the cone over
L_{m}
(or equivalently is stable by non-trivial homotheties), then the variety
L_{m}
is Legendrian in
M_{m}
, in particular
\dim G\cdot[m]=2\dim H\cdot[m]+1
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma(m)=-m
, the action of
\sigma
on
\mathfrak{g}
restricts to an action on
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
. Since
\sigma
is semisimple, we thus have
\mathfrak{g}_{m}=\mathfrak{h}_{m}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{m}
and
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{m}=\mathfrak{h}/\mathfrak{h}_{m}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}/\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{m}
with
\mathfrak{h}_{m}=\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{m}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{m}=\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{m}
. Let
u,v\in\mathfrak{h}/\mathfrak{h}_{m}
(resp.
u,v\in\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}/\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{m}
) and let
y
and
z
in
\mathfrak{h}
(resp. in
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
) be representatives of
u
and
v
.
In Example
4.27
, the form
K
can be chosen to be
\sigma
-invariant. Using the fact that

\sigma(u)=\varepsilon u
,
\sigma(v)=\varepsilon v

for the same sign
\varepsilon
, and likewise for

\sigma(y)
,
\sigma(z)
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega_{m}(u,v)&=&\omega_{m}(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))\\
&=&B(m,[\sigma(y),\sigma(z)])\\
&=&-B(\sigma(m),[\sigma(y),d\sigma(z)])\\
&=&-B(\sigma(m),\sigma[y,z])\\
&=&-B(m,[y,z])\\
&=&-\omega_{m}(u,v).\end{array}

Hence
\omega_{m}(u,v)=0
and both
\mathfrak{h}/\mathfrak{h}_{m}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}/\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}_{m}
are isotropic and therefore Lagrangian in
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{m}
. This proves the first part.
If
H\cdot m
is stable under non-trivial homotheties, then the same holds true for
G\cdot m
.
The result follows from this and the first part.
∎

Also, recall from
[
KR71
, Proposition 4, Proposition 11]
that
the condition that
\widehat{L}_{m}
is the cone over

is equivalent to

being nilpotent. Together with

2.19
, this readily yields:

Corollary 4.32
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a family of minimal rational curves,
let

and let

. Then
\widehat{L}_{m}
is the cone over

, in particular
\dim H\cdot[m]=\frac{1}{2}\dim G\cdot m-1
.

Next, assume that
\overline{R}
is not of type
{\rm A}_{r}
and let
C\in{\mathcal{K}}_{x}

with
{\mathcal{K}}
a family of minimal rational curves. We obtain the following description of
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
.

Theorem 4.33
.

We have
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}=N\cdot C
.
Furthermore, if
X
is Hermitian non-exceptional, then
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
has two components. Otherwise,
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
is irreducible.

Proof.

Since
\overline{R}
is not of type
{\rm A}_{r}
, we have

\partial X\cdot C=1
(see Remark
4.24
). By Theorem
4.23
and Corollary
4.32
, we have
\dim N\cdot C=\dim{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
. If

is non-Hermitian or Hermitian exceptional, then there exists a unique highest weight curve and

is irreducible, proving the result. If

is Hermitian non-exceptional, then

contains two

∎

We conclude this subsection by the following related result,
which follows from
[
Ri82
, Theorem A]
;
we provide a direct proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 4.34
.

The orbit
H\cdot m
(resp.
H\cdot[m]
) is open in
(G\cdot m)^{-\sigma}
(resp.
(G\cdot[m])^{\sigma}
).
In particular,
H\cdot m
(resp.
H\cdot[m]
) is a union of connected components of
(G\cdot m)^{-\sigma}
(resp.
(G\cdot[m])^{\sigma}
).
Moreover,
\dim(H\cdot m)=\dim(G\cdot m)^{-\sigma}
and
\dim(H\cdot[m])=\dim(G\cdot[m])^{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Note that
m
is fixed for
-\sigma
; therefore,

\sigma([m])=[m]
. We thus have inclusions

H\cdot m\subset(G\cdot m)^{-\sigma}
and

H\cdot[m]\subset(G\cdot[m])^{\sigma}
.
To prove the openness, we only need to check that
the tangent spaces at their respective base points

m
and
[m]
agree.
We deal with
H\cdot[m]
, the other case works in a similar way.
The natural map

\mathfrak{g}\to T_{[m]}(G\cdot[m]),\xi\mapsto\xi\cdot[m]

is surjective, and
\sigma
-equivariant as
[m]

is
\sigma
-fixed. Since
\sigma
is semisimple,
this map induces a surjection on
\sigma
-fixed
subspaces, i.e.

(T_{[m]}(G\cdot[m]))^{\sigma}=(\mathfrak{g}\cdot[m])^{\sigma}=\mathfrak{g}^{\sigma}\cdot[m]=\mathfrak{h}\cdot[m]=T_{[m]}(H\cdot[m])
.
Moreover,

(T_{[m]}(G\cdot[m]))^{\sigma}=T_{[m]}((G\cdot[m])^{\sigma})

in view of the semisimplicity of
\sigma
again;
this gives the desired equality

T_{[m]}((G\cdot[m])^{\sigma})=T_{[m]}(H\cdot[m])
.

Since every
H
-orbit in
(G\cdot m)^{-\sigma}
is open,
there are only finitely many such orbits and these orbits are
also closed, proving the last statement.
∎

Remark 4.36
.

The above statements (1) and (2) can be proved in a uniform way
using Jordan algebras: the representation

has
the structure of a Jordan algebra with structure group

and the stabiliser of the unit element is

is then explained by the notion of rank for elements in a Jordan algebra.
We refer to

and
[

]
for more on Jordan
algebras. We were however not able to fully relate symmetric spaces
with restricted root systems of type

to Jordan algebras
without using a case by case check, so we refrained from using them.

Likewise, the above statement (3) can be deduced in a
uniform way from the equality
\dim G/N=\dim V_{\lambda_{1}}-1

by using embedding theory of spherical homogeneous spaces,
as suggested by an anonymous referee.
But the only proof of this equality that we know goes via a
case-by-case checking.

Appendix

The goal of this appendix is twofold: we first prove basic results on restricted root systems used to describe curves and divisors on wonderful compactifications. We also obtain characterisations of exceptional wonderful varieties useful to establish Table
1
. We then give an easy way to describe, using the Kac diagram of the symmetric space, the components of the
H
-orbit
H\cdot C
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
, where
C
is a highest weight curve.
Finally, in Table
1
, we give a list, based on the classification of symmetric spaces, of families of minimal rational curves and VMRT of wonderful symmetric varieties.

6.1
Restricted root systems

In this subsection we prove useful results on restricted root systems that might be well known to experts, but for which we could not find a good reference.

Lemma 6.1
.

The restricted root
{\overline{\Theta}}
is the highest root of
\overline{R}
.

Proof.

For
\alpha\in R
, write

\alpha=\sum_{\beta\in\Delta}c_{\beta}\beta

with all the
c_{\beta}
of the same sign. We have

{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{1}}c_{\beta}(\beta-\sigma(\beta))=\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{1}}c_{\beta}{\overline{\beta}}

and the result follows from this.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
w_{0}\in W
be the longest element, then the actions of
\sigma
and
w_{0}
on roots commute. In particular,
\sigma(w_{0})=w_{0}
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
Sp83
, Section 5.2]
, that there exists an involution
\tau
of
\Delta
such that
\tau(\Delta_{0})=\Delta_{0}
with
\sigma=w_{0,L}w_{0}\tau
and
{\overline{\sigma}}=-w_{0}\tau
(see also
[
deCS99
, Section 1.5]
). Note that
-w_{0}
,
{\overline{\sigma}}
and
\tau
are involutions preserving
R^{+}
and
\Delta
. Furthermore there is at most one non-trivial such involution except in type
D_{4}
(in type
D_{4}
, we have
-w_{0}={\rm id}
).
Thus,
-w_{0}
is trivial or equal to
{\overline{\sigma}}
or
\tau
. In particular
w_{0}(\Delta_{0})=-\Delta_{0}
and
w_{0}(L)=L
(this can also be easily checked using the classification).

Recall that
\sigma=-w_{0,L}{\overline{\sigma}}
where
w_{0,L}
is the longest element in the Weyl group
W_{L}
of the pair
(L,T_{{\rm s}})
, for this see
[
Ti11
, Page 149]
. Therefore
w_{0}\sigma=-w_{0}w_{0,L}{\overline{\sigma}}=-w_{w_{0}(L)}w_{0}{\overline{\sigma}}=-w_{0,L}{\overline{\sigma}}w_{0}=\sigma w_{0}
. The result follows.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

We have
w_{0}({\overline{\Theta}})=-{\overline{\Theta}}
.

Proof.

We have
w_{0}({\overline{\Theta}})=w_{0}(\Theta)-w_{0}(\sigma(\Theta))=w_{0}(\Theta)-\sigma(w_{0}(\Theta))=-\Theta+\sigma(\Theta)=-{\overline{\Theta}}
.
∎

We now prove a characterisation of non-reduced restricted root systems.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\alpha\in R\setminus R^{\sigma}
. We have the equivalence:
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}

\Leftrightarrow

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
.

Proof.

If
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
, then

{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\beta\in R

and
\sigma(\beta)=-\beta
. Thus,

{\overline{\beta}}=2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
.

Conversely, assume that
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
. Recall that for
\gamma\in R
, there are three possibilities:

({\overline{\gamma}},{\overline{\gamma}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}4(\gamma,\gamma)&\textrm{ if $\sigma(\gamma)=-\gamma$},\\
2(\gamma,\gamma)&\textrm{ if $\langle\gamma^{\vee},\sigma(\gamma)\rangle=0$},\\
(\gamma,\gamma)&\textrm{ if $\langle\gamma^{\vee},\sigma(\gamma)\rangle=1$}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Let
\gamma\in R
with
{\overline{\gamma}}=2{\overline{\alpha}}
, then
({\overline{\gamma}},{\overline{\gamma}})=4({\overline{\alpha}},{\overline{\alpha}})
is equal to
16(\alpha,\alpha)
,
8(\alpha,\alpha)
or
4(\alpha,\alpha)
if
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
,
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=0
or
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
. In the first case, we would have
(\gamma,\gamma)\geq 4(\alpha,\alpha)
which is impossible for a reduced root system. In the second case, we have
(\alpha,\sigma(\alpha))=0
and
\gamma=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)
is a root. This implies that
\alpha+\sigma(\alpha)
is a root but the corresponding root vector is
[e_{\alpha},e_{\sigma(\alpha)}]
which lies in
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
. This means that
\alpha+\sigma(\alpha)
is non-compact imaginary which is impossible since we work with a split torus, see
[
Ti11
, Page 149]
. We thus have
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

We have the equivalences:

\textrm{$\overline{R}$ is non-reduced }\Leftrightarrow\exists\alpha\in\Delta_{1},\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1\Leftrightarrow\exists\alpha\in R,\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1.

Proof.

If
\overline{R}
is non-reduced then it is of type
\textrm{BC}_{r}
and there exists
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
with
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
. The second implication from left to right is clear. If
\alpha\in R
is such that
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
, then
{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\gamma
is a root and
{\overline{\gamma}}=2\gamma=2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
; thus,
\overline{R}
is non-reduced.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
such that
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
.

1.

The root
{\overline{\alpha}}
is the unique root of
\overline{\Delta}
with
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
.

2.

The variety
X
is exceptional if and only if
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
is such that
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
. Then
{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{\Delta}
is the unique simple root whose double is a root in the root system
\textrm{BC}_{r}
and is therefore unique.

(2) If
X
is exceptional then for
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
an exceptional root, we have
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
by definition and
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
by Remark
4.1
.
For the converse,
if
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
is such that
{\overline{\alpha}},2{\overline{\alpha}}\in\overline{R}
, then
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=1
.
If furthermore
{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)\neq\alpha
then

is exceptional by definition .
∎

For
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
, write

{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha+{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)+\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{0}}c_{\beta}\beta

and define the support of
{\overline{\alpha}}
by

\supp({\overline{\alpha}})=\{\alpha,{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha),\beta\ |\ \beta\in\Delta_{0}\textrm{ with }c_{\beta}>0\}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
, then
{\overline{\alpha}}
is dominant on

\supp({\overline{\alpha}})
. More precisely, we have

\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle>0,\langle{\overline{\sigma}}(\alpha)^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle>0\textrm{ and }\langle\beta^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle=0\textrm{ for $\beta\in\supp({\overline{\alpha}})$}\cap\Delta_{0}.

Proof.

For
\beta\in\Delta_{0}
, we have

\langle\beta^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle=\langle\sigma(\beta)^{\vee},\sigma({\overline{\alpha}})\rangle=\langle\beta^{\vee},-{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle
;
thus,
\langle\beta^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle=0
.
We have
\langle\alpha^{\vee},{\overline{\alpha}}\rangle=\langle\alpha^{\vee},\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=2-\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle

and since
\alpha
and
\sigma(\alpha)

have the same length, we have

\langle\alpha^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle\leq 1

proving the result.
∎

Recall that
\Theta
denotes the highest root of
R
,
and
\theta
the highest short root (if
R
is simply laced,
then
\Theta=\theta
and all roots are long and short).

Lemma 6.8
.

We have the following equivalences

1.

\sigma(\Theta)=-\Theta

\Leftrightarrow

there exists a long root
\alpha
with
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
.

2.

\sigma(\theta)=-\theta

\Leftrightarrow

there exists a short root
\alpha
with
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
.

Proof.

The implications from left to right in (1) and (2) are clear. We prove the converse implications. The proofs in both cases are similar.

We first prove that the converse implications in (1) and (2) are implied by the following claim: if
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
and there exists
\beta\in\Delta
with
s_{\beta}(\alpha)>\alpha
, then there exists
w\in W
with
\sigma(w)=w
such that
w(\alpha)>\alpha

(recall that
\sigma
acts on
W
by conjugaison).

Assume that the above claim is true. We prove the converse implications in (1) and (2) by induction on roots for their natural order: if
\alpha
is a positive root such that
\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
and
\alpha
is not maximal, we produce a root
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
with the same length as
\alpha
and such that
\sigma(\alpha^{\prime})=-\alpha^{\prime}
. Indeed, if
\alpha
is not maximal, then there exists
\beta\in\Delta
with
s_{\beta}(\alpha)>\alpha
. By the above claim, there exists
w\in W
with
\sigma(w)=w
and
w(\alpha)>\alpha
. We thus have
\sigma(w(\alpha))=\sigma(w)(\sigma(\alpha))=w(-\alpha)=-w(\alpha)
with
w(\alpha)>\alpha
. Therefore, if the claim is true, we get by induction that
\sigma(\alpha^{\prime})=-\alpha^{\prime}
for
\alpha^{\prime}
the highest root with the same length as
\alpha
, proving the implication from right to left of (1) and (2).

We now prove our claim, so let
\alpha\in R
such that

\sigma(\alpha)=-\alpha
and
\beta\in\Delta
with

s_{\beta}(\alpha)>\alpha
. In particular

\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle<0
.
We have four possible cases:

\sigma(\beta)=\beta
,

\sigma(\beta)=-\beta
,

\langle\beta^{\vee},\sigma(\beta)\rangle=0

or
\langle\beta^{\vee},\sigma(\beta)\rangle=1
.

If
\sigma(\beta)=\beta
, then

\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle=\langle\sigma(\beta)^{\vee},\sigma(\alpha)\rangle=-\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle
; thus,

\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle=0
a contradiction,
so this case does not occur.

If
\sigma(\beta)=-\beta
, then
w=s_{\beta}

works since
\sigma(w)=w
.

If
\langle\beta^{\vee},\sigma(\beta)\rangle=0
,
then set
w=s_{\beta}s_{\sigma(\beta)}
.
Since
s_{\beta}
and
s_{\sigma(\beta)}
commute,
we have
\sigma(w)=w
. Furthermore, we have

w(\alpha)=\alpha-\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\beta-\langle\sigma(\beta)^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\sigma(\beta)=\alpha-\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\beta+\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\sigma(\beta)
.
But since
\sigma(\beta)\neq\beta
, we have

\sigma(\beta)<0
and
w(\alpha)>\alpha
.

Finally, if
\langle\beta^{\vee},\sigma(\beta)\rangle=1
, define

\delta=s_{\sigma(\beta)}(\beta)=\beta-\sigma(\beta)={\overline{\beta}}
;
then
\delta
is a root. Let
w=s_{\delta}
.
We have
\sigma(\delta)=-\delta
; thus,
\sigma(w)=w
.
Furthermore, we have

w(\alpha)=\alpha-\langle\delta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\delta=\alpha-\langle s_{\sigma(\beta)}(\beta)^{\vee},\alpha\rangle(\beta-\sigma(\beta))

and

\langle s_{\sigma(\beta)}(\beta)^{\vee},\alpha\rangle=\langle\beta^{\vee},s_{\sigma(\beta)}(\alpha)\rangle=\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha-\langle\sigma(\beta)^{\vee},\alpha\rangle\sigma(\beta)\rangle=\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle-\langle\sigma(\beta)^{\vee},\alpha\rangle=2\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle
. Thus,
w(\alpha)=\alpha-2\langle\beta^{\vee},\alpha\rangle(\beta-\sigma(\beta))
and since
\sigma(\beta)<0
, we get
w(\alpha)>\alpha
.
∎

Assume that

.

On the other hand if

Assume that
\sigma(\Theta)=-\Theta

and let

be a

Lemma 6.13
