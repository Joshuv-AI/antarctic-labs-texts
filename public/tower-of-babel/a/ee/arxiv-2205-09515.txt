Variational Inference for Bayesian Bridge Regression
Carlos Tadeu Pagani Zanini, Helio S. Migon, Ronaldo Dias

Abstract
The bridge approach for regularization of coefficients in regression models uses ℓ α \ell_{\alpha} norm, with α ∈ ( 0 , + ∞ ) \alpha\in(0,+\infty) , to define a penalization on large values of the regression coefficients. Particular cases include the lasso and ridge penalizations. In Bayesian models, the penalization is enforced by a prior distribution on the coefficients. Although MCMC approaches are available for Bayesian bridge regression, they can be very slow for large datasets, specially in high dimensions. This paper develops an implementation of Automatic Differentiation Variational Inference for Bayesian inference on semi-parametric regression models with bridge penalization. The non-parametric effects of covariates are modeled by B-splines. The proposed inference procedure allows the use of small batches of data at each iteration (due to stochastic gradient based updates), therefore drastically reducing computational time in comparison with MCMC. Full Bayesian inference is preserved so joint uncertainty estimates for all model parameters are available. A simulation study shows the main properties of the proposed method and an application to a large real dataset is presented.

1
Introduction and related works

It is not uncommon for an experimenter to be interested in understanding how covariates might explain a response variable. For this, one can assume a general non-parametric regression model:

y_{i}=g(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iD})+\epsilon_{i},

where
\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\phi^{-1})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

A usual approach to estimate the surface
g
is to consider the well-known (General) Additive Model, which briefly means that
g
can be decomposed as

g(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{D})=g_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}g_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}),

where
\mathbf{x}_{j}=(x_{1j},\ldots,x_{nj})^{\top}
, for
j=1,\ldots,D
and
i=1,\ldots,n
. Each univariate function
g_{j}
can be written as a linear combination of basis functions
B_{j,1},\ldots,B_{jK_{j}}
, such as B-splines, wavelets, Fourier basis, etc.
That is,
g_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}B_{jk}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\beta_{jk}
.

Throughout this work, the basis functions chosen to represent the univariate function
g_{j}
are the well-known B-splines. Thus, the surface regression model is:

y_{i}=g_{0}+\sum^{D}_{j=1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}B_{jk}(x_{ij})\beta_{jk}+\epsilon_{i}

(1)

Regularization plays a very important role in Statistics: penalizing overcomplex models often reduces the risk of overfitting and produces better generalization to hold out data. For this, under the frequentist point of view, one have to solve the following optimization problem:

\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\ \lVert\mathbf{y}-g_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}B_{jk}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\beta_{jk}\rVert^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\lambda_{j}\;{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha_{j}),

where
\lambda_{j}
is the smoothing parameter and
{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha_{j})
is the roughness penalty term.

Different types of penalties,
{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha)
can be applied. For instance,
{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha=1)=\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\rVert_{1}
,
\ell_{1}
-norm,

{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha=2)=\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\rVert^{2}_{2}
, the
\ell_{2}
-norm squared, the smoothing splines penalty,
{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha=2)=\int(\sum_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j}\ddot{B}_{j})^{2},
and P-spline,
{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\alpha=2)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{D}(\Delta^{k}\bm{\beta}_{j})^{2}
, where
\Delta^{k}
is the
k
-th order difference operator.

In particular, after writing (
1
) in matrix form, the semi-parametric regression model can be written as:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}_{0}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j}+\bm{\epsilon},

where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\phi^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, with
\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}B_{jk}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\beta_{jk}=\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j}
, where
\mathbf{X}_{j}
is the
n\times K_{j}
matrix
\mathbf{X}_{j}=[B_{j1}(\mathbf{x}_{j}),\ldots,B_{jK_{j}}(\mathbf{x}_{j})]
and
\bm{\beta}_{j}=(\beta_{j1},\ldots,\beta_{jK_{j}})^{\top}
.
Note that the matrix
\mathbf{X}_{0}
comprises possible parametric covariates and hence can also be viewed as Partial Splines
(
Silverman \BBA Green \APACyear1994
)
. In particular, if
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{1}
then
\mathbf{X}_{0}\bm{\beta}_{0}
plays the role of
g_{0}
from equation (
1
) (Additive Model).

Penalties defined in terms of
\ell_{\alpha}
norm in the context of Bayesian regression models include the ridge (
Hoerl \BBA Kennard \APACyear1970\APACexlab\BCnt1
,
Hoerl \BBA Kennard \APACyear1970\APACexlab\BCnt2
) (
\alpha=2
), the lasso
(
Park \BBA Casella \APACyear2008
)
(
\alpha=1
), elastic net
(
Q. Li \BBA Lin \APACyear2010
)
(convex combination of both
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
norms) and bridge
(
Polson \BOthers. \APACyear2014
;
Mallick \BBA Yi \APACyear2018
)
(
\alpha>0
), first presented by
Frank \BBA Friedman \APACyear1993
.
A possible bridge model for semi-parametric regression is a regularization problem defined as,

\argmin_{\bm{\beta}_{0},\ \bm{\beta}}\frac{1}{2}\lVert(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}_{0}\bm{\beta}_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{D}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j})\|^{2}+\sum^{D}_{j=1}\lambda_{j}\lvert\lvert\bm{\beta}_{j}\lvert\lvert^{\alpha_{j}}_{\alpha_{j}}

(2)

where
\lambda_{j}>0
is the penalty term that controls the strength of shrinkage over
\bm{\beta}_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}>0
is the concavity parameter of the penalty function.

The different choices of
\ell_{\alpha}
norm for the penalization imply different forms of shrinking the regression coefficients towards zero. While ridge regression does not zero out coefficients, the lasso penalization is capable of producing sparse solutions to the corresponding maximum a posteriori objective, therefore working as a variable selection procedure
(
Tibshirani \APACyear1996
;
Tibshirani \APACyear1997
)
. From a Bayesian perspective,
Hastie \BOthers. \APACyear2015
,
Casella \BOthers. \APACyear2010
and
Leng \BOthers. \APACyear2014
, highlight that one can interpret the term
\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\lVert^{\alpha_{j}}_{\alpha_{j}}
as proportional to the negative log-prior density of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
with the contours illustrated in Figure
1
representing the contours of the prior distribution. The case
\alpha_{j}<1
implies a non-convex prior that concentrates more mass along the coordinates’ axis, producing solutions with fewer nonzero coefficients and less shrinkage.

Figure 1:
Constraint regions
\sum^{p_{x}}_{j=1}\mid\beta_{j}\mid^{\alpha}\leq 1
for different values of
\alpha
. For
\alpha<1
, the constraint region is nonconvex. Figure extracted from
Hastie \BOthers. \APACyear2015
(adapted).

Other forms of penalization are also relevant. The elastic net provides sparsity of representation similarly to the lasso while also encouraging a grouping effect where strongly correlated predictors tend to be in or out of the model together
(
Zou \BBA Hastie \APACyear2005
)
. In Bayesian regression models, the horseshoe prior implies a form of variable selection that acts strongly on coefficients of small magnitude while having little influence on coefficients of large magnitude
(
Carvalho \BOthers. \APACyear2009
)
.

In regression models that include non-parametric effects, it is crucial to assign some form of penalization in order to calibrate the level of complexity of the relationships between the covariates and the response to avoid overfitting. Generalized additive models (GAM) from
Hastie \BBA Tibshirani \APACyear1986

models the non-parametric effects of covariates on the mean of a response variable belonging to the exponential family, with one spline being used for each covariate. For common choices of spline basis functions such as B-spline, see
Eilers \BBA Marx \APACyear1996
. The level of smoothness can be controlled by a penalization acting on the splines coefficients, see
Currie \BBA Durban \APACyear2002
. Some Bayesian approaches in this context include
Lang \BBA Brezger \APACyear2004
and
Hastie \BBA Tibshirani \APACyear2000
. In the context of bridge penalized linear regression with Gaussian response,
Polson \BOthers. \APACyear2014
and
Mallick \BBA Yi \APACyear2018
develop full Bayesian inference through fixed dimension MCMC based on variable augmentation schemes that guarantee conjugacy for the full conditional distributions.
Denison \BOthers. \APACyear1998
and
Dias \BBA Gamerman \APACyear2002
describe reversible jumps MCMC algorithms for selecting the number of knots in B-splines and P-splines. These MCMC approaches, however, are computationally very expensive when applied to large datasets.

This work proposes an alternative to MCMC for approximate inference on Bayesian bridge semi-parametric regression with B-splines based on the Automatic Differentiation Variational Inference (ADVI) by
Kucukelbir \BOthers. \APACyear2015
and
Kucukelbir \BOthers. \APACyear2017
. The proposed inference algorithm jointly estimates all parameters in the model (including
\alpha_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
) and drastically increases computational speed for large datasets in comparison with MCMC implementations since stochastic gradient updates involved in the underlying optimization process small batches of data at each iteration, rather than the entire dataset as required by MCMC schemes. A key point in variational inference concerns the choice of the variational family of distributions, which needs to be tractable while at the same time flexible enough to approximate the posterior distribution well.
Armagan \APACyear2009
describe a mean-field variational inference method for Bayesian bridge regression model with approximate inference for the bridge parameter (
\alpha
).
Alves \BOthers. \APACyear2021
also proposes a variational approach based on mean-field assumption for Bayesian inference in regression models with splines, however it is restricted to Lasso penalization. In contrast, the ADVI does not require the oversimplifying mean-field assumption for the variational family, as required by coordinate ascending variational inference
(
Blei \BOthers. \APACyear2017
)
, producing very close approximations to the full posterior distribution, as demonstrated in the simulation study. Previous gradient based variational procedures, such as
Ranganath \BOthers. \APACyear2014
or
Kingma \BBA Welling \APACyear2013
could be used, but they imply more restricted forms of dependence in the variational family and produce noisier estimates of the gradients when compared to ADVI. More recent approaches, such as
Yin \BBA Zhou \APACyear2018
, allow for more flexible variational families, however we found the simpler ADVI approach to produce already very good results in terms of computational speed and posterior approximation under the semi-parametric Bayesian bridge model.

To summarize, the main contribution of the paper is the development of a full Bayesian inference procedure based on variational inference for semi-parametric regression with bridge penalization. As strengths of the proposed methodology, we can list the following:

1.

The proposed inference method is flexible enough to capture the dependence structure in the target posterior distribution since the variational family does not require the mean-field assumption. More specifically, for the mean-field approach, the joint variational distribution of the parameters is the product of its marginals. On the other hand, ADVI admits dependence structure given by a transformation of a multivariate normal distribution with full covariance matrix.

2.

It enables full Bayesian inference on semi-parametric regression for large datasets at drastically lower computing times when compared with more traditional MCMC implementations due to batch processing of data by the stochastic gradient updates specially for large data.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The Bayesian bridge semi-parametric model is described in section
2
. Section
3
covers the MCMC scheme by
Mallick \BBA Yi \APACyear2018
, used for comparison with the proposed ADVI for Bayesian bridge semi-parametric regression defined in section
4
. Section
5
concerns a simulation study with focus on large data. Section
6
contains an application to real data on energy charges in Brazil and, finally, section
7
presents the conclusions and future works.

2
The Bayesian bridge model for semi-parametric regression

A Bayesian bridge model for multiple covariates, can be written in the form of a hierarchical model as follows:

(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{D},\phi)\sim\\
\sim N\left(\mathbf{X}_{0}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\sum^{D}_{j=1}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j},\ \phi^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right),\\
(\beta_{jk}\mid\lambda_{j},\phi,\alpha_{j})\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}GG\left(0,\ \lambda^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}_{j}\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}},\ \alpha_{j}\right),\\
\ j=1,\ldots,D,\ k=1,\ldots,K_{j}.

(3)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

N(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
denotes the multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma},

GG(\mu,\sigma,\alpha)
denotes the generalized Gaussian distribution
(
Subbotin \APACyear1923
)
with mean
\mu
, scale
\sigma
and shape
\alpha
. The generalized Gaussian density evaluated at
x\in\mathbb{R}
is denoted by

GG(x\mid\mu,\sigma,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma\Gamma(\alpha^{-1})}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{\mid x-\mu\mid}{\sigma}\right)^{\alpha}\right\}.

Equation (
3
) therefore implies the prior probability density function

p(\bm{\beta}_{jk}\mid\lambda_{j},\phi,\alpha_{j})=\\
=\frac{\alpha_{j}\lambda^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}_{j}\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2\Gamma(\alpha^{-1}_{j})}\exp\left\{-\lambda_{j}\left(\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}\mid\beta_{j}\mid\right)^{\alpha_{j}}\right\},

(4)

so that maximizing the posterior density
p(\bm{\beta}\mid\mathbf{y},\bm{\lambda},\phi,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}_{0})
over
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}
for fixed
(\bm{\lambda},\phi,\bm{\alpha})
is equivalent to solving (
2
), where
\bm{\beta}=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{D}),\ \bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{D}),\ \bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{D})
. The particular form of dependency of
\beta_{jk}
on
\lambda_{j}
and
\phi
expressed by equation (
4
) avoids undesired multimodality of the posterior distribution as pointed out by
Park \BBA Casella \APACyear2008
.

The proposed semi-parametric model splits the regressors into matrices
\mathbf{X}_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,D
of covariates with their own penalized effects and a matrix
\mathbf{X}_{0}
of covariates with unpenalized effects. In the proposed formulation,
\mathbf{X}_{j}
represents a spline basis function that implies non-parametric effects to the corresponding covariates. B-splines were chosen as the set of spline basis functions to build
\mathbf{X}_{j}
with the bridge penalization acting on the B-spline coefficients. Naturally, other types of spline basis functions could also be used.

Within the Bayesian framework, it is possible to provide joint (approximate) posterior estimates for all parameters, including
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\lambda}
by specifying a prior distribution on them, as will be shown further in sections
3
and
4
. Therefore, joint posterior inference can be done in a single-step procedure.

MCMC on Bayesian bridge regression

Both
Polson \BOthers. \APACyear2014
and
Mallick \BBA Yi \APACyear2018
propose MCMC schemes for Bayesian bridge regression for fixed
\alpha_{j}
based on different data augmentation schemes. The algorithm by
Mallick \BBA Yi \APACyear2018
was chosen for comparison with the proposed ADVI for Bayesian bridge, mostly because of the simplicity of its gamma-uniform variable augmentation in comparison with the one proposed by
Polson \BOthers. \APACyear2014
. Accordingly, one can recover (
3
) by specifying

\displaystyle(u_{jk}\mid\alpha_{j},\lambda_{j})\sim Ga\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}+1,\ \lambda_{j}\right),\ \ \ k=1,\ldots,K_{j},

\displaystyle(\beta_{jk}\mid u_{jk},\phi,\alpha_{j})\sim Unif\left(-u^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}_{jk}\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}},\ u^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}_{jk}\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ j=1,\ldots,D.

(5)

In fact,

\displaystyle p(\beta_{jk}

\displaystyle\mid\lambda_{j},\alpha_{j},\phi)=

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}p(\beta_{jk}\mid u_{jk},\phi,\alpha_{j})p(u_{jk}\mid\alpha_{j},\lambda_{j})du_{jk}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-\lambda_{j}\phi^{\frac{\alpha_{j}}{2}}\mid\beta_{jk}\mid^{\alpha_{j}}\}.

The model specification is completed by assigning independent priors
\phi\sim Ga(a_{\phi},b_{\phi}),

\bm{\beta}_{0}\sim N(\bm{\mu}_{0},\Sigma_{0}),\lambda_{j}\sim Ga(a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda}),
where
Ga(a,b)
denotes the Gamma distribution with mean
a/b
and variance
a/b^{2}
. The prior distribution on
\alpha_{j}
was defined by taking
\alpha_{j}=2.5\eta_{j}
where
\eta_{j}\sim Beta(a_{\eta},b_{\eta})
. The upper limit for
\alpha_{j}
in this work is fixed arbitrarily at 2.5 so that the lasso and the ridge penalizations can be approximately represented as special cases of bridge regression.

Gibbs sampler can be easily implemented for posterior inference on the Bayesian bridge regression model. Due to conjugacy results, the full conditional distributions of all parameters, except
\alpha_{j}
, are analytically available. Details concerning the MCMC implementation are described in the appendix
9
. In section
9.1
, the Gibbs sampler approach considering
\alpha_{j}
fixed is described. To include
\alpha_{j}
in the sampling scheme, one can carry out a Metropolis-Hastings step with a transformed random walk proposal as described in section
9.2.1
(when the auxiliary vector
\mathbf{u}
is marginalized) or in section
9.2.2
when
\mathbf{u}
is not marginalized). It was found that the marginalized random walk proposal produce well mixing Markov chains without much effort to tune the proposal variance. Naturally, other Metropolis-Hastings proposals could be used.

4
Variational Inference

The variational approach searches among a predefined variational family
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta}):\ \bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}\}
(where its members are densities indexed by the variational parameter
\bm{\psi}
) for the density
q_{\bm{\psi}^{*}}(\bm{\theta})
that best approximates the posterior
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
in terms of Kullback-Leibler divergence. For the proposed Bayesian semi-parametric bridge regression,
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma},\phi,\lambda,\alpha)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{p_{Z}}\times(0,\ 1)^{2}\times(0,\ 2.5).

Therefore, the variational objective is

\argmin_{\bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}}KL\left(q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})\mid\mid p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})\right),

which is equivalent to maximize the evidence lower bound (ELBO), i.e.
\argmax_{\bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}}ELBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi}),
where

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle LBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi}):=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\psi}}}[\log p(\mathbf{y},\bm{\theta})-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})]

\displaystyle=\int\left[\log p(\mathbf{y},\bm{\theta})-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})\right]q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})\ d\bm{\theta}.

One way to maximize the ELBO is to calculate its gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi})
and use it in a stochastic gradient ascend based algorithm, such as Adam (
Kingma \BBA Ba \APACyear2014
), Adagrad
(
Duchi \BOthers. \APACyear2011
)
, AdaDelta
(
Zeiler \APACyear2012
)
and others. The issue with such gradient ascending methods is evaluating the expectation in the ELBO, which is often intractable. In this case, a common solution involves rewriting the ELBO in a way that the gradient operator can switch order with the expectation so the resulting
\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi})
is an expected value with respect to the variational distribution. The resulting expectation can then be estimated via Monte Carlo methods.

This work implements ADVI (section
4.3
) to the semi-parametric Bayesian bridge regression described in section
2
. The variational approaches by
Ranganath \BOthers. \APACyear2014
and by
Kingma \BBA Welling \APACyear2013
are briefly presented for comparison and contextualization. A reader interested in variational inference for semi-parametric regression models may see
(
Luts \BBA Wand \APACyear2015
)
,
(
Menictas \BBA Wand \APACyear2015
)
,
(
Ong \BOthers. \APACyear2017
)
and
(
Wand \APACyear2017
)
to mention a few.

4.1
Score method

Ranganath \BOthers. \APACyear2014
introduced the black box variational inference (BBVI) in which the score method (also known as ”log trick”) allows the gradient of the ELBO to be written as an expectation with repect to the variational distribution. The resulting expectation can be estimated by Monte Carlo as follows

\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi})=\\
=\int\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\left\{[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})+\log p(\bm{\theta})+\right.\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})]q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})\right\}d\bm{\theta}\\
=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\psi}}}[(\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})+\log p(\bm{\theta})-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta}))\times\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})]\\
\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{m=1}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta}^{(m)})+\log p(\bm{\theta}^{(m)})+\right.\\
\left.-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta}^{(m)})\right]\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}),

(6)

where
\bm{\theta}^{(m)}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta}),\ m=1\ldots,M.

Evaluating (
6
) requires (i) the prior, likelihood and variational densities to be analytically available and (ii) the ability to draw samples from the variational distribution
q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})
. However, in many applications, the Monte Carlo estimate from (
6
) has high variance
(
Paisley \BOthers. \APACyear2012
)
, even when control variates and Rao-Blackwellization are used to reduce variance. It is simple to mitigate the mean field hypothesis in the BBVI method to some extent by grouping blocks of components within
\bm{\theta}
and specifying a dependent multivariate variational distribution within each block. However specifying a single dependent distribution for the entire
\bm{\theta}
is typically hard, specially when its components lie in different subspaces of
\mathbb{R}
(so a multivariate Gaussian or Student-t would be inappropriate choices for
q_{\bm{\psi}}
). We found it crucial to account for full dependence structure in the variational family for the case of semi-parametric Bayesian bridge model. In Appendix
10
, we provide the specifics on how one can apply the BBVI algorithm to the Bayesian bridge model.

4.2
Reparameterization gradient

An alternative way to derive Monte Carlo estimates for the gradient of the ELBO is described in
Kingma \BBA Welling \APACyear2013
. The authors present the reparameterization trick, which assumes that the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
can be analytically written as
\bm{\theta}=T_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\epsilon})
where
T_{\bm{\psi}}
is a differentiable deterministic transformation involving the variational parameters
\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{\epsilon}
is a random noise required to have a closed form distribution
q^{*}(\bm{\epsilon})
that does not depend on
\bm{\psi}
and is easy to sample from. For example, if
\bm{\theta}\sim N(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S}),
then we can write
\bm{\beta}=T_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S}}(\bm{\epsilon})=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon},
with
\mathbf{L}
being the Choleskey decomposition of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
(ie,
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}=\mathbf{S}
) and
\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(\bm{0},\mathbf{I}).
Another example of reparameterization is the log-Normal distribution: if
\theta\sim\log N(m,s^{2}),
one can write
\theta=T_{m,s}(\epsilon)=\exp\{m+s\times\epsilon\}
with
\epsilon\sim N(0,1).

The reparameterization of
\bm{\theta}
allows one to switch the order of the gradient with the expectation when deriving the updating equations for optimizing the ELBO. The gradient of the ELBO under reparameterization becomes

\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\bm{\psi})=\\
=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim q^{*}}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log p(\mathbf{y},T_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\epsilon}))\right]+\\
-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})}[\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})].

If a random sample
\bm{\epsilon}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\epsilon}^{(M)}
is drawn from
q^{*}(\bm{\epsilon}),
the Monte Carlo estimate for the gradient is obtained as

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\bm{\psi})=\\
\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{m=1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log p(\mathbf{y},T_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{(m)}))\\
-\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{m=1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(T_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{(m)})).

(7)

The variance of the Monte Carlo estimates for the reparameterization gradients tend to exhibit lower variance than the BBVI Monte Carlo estimates, but the difficulty of assuming a full dependence structure on the variational family for different entries of
\bm{\theta}
persists. In Appendix B an implementation of the reparameterization method for variational inference on the proposed semi-parametric Bayesian bridge regression model is briefly described.

4.3
Automatic differentiation variational inference

The implementation of ADVI method from
Kucukelbir \BOthers. \APACyear2015
and
Kucukelbir \BOthers. \APACyear2017
to the proposed semi-parametric Bayesian bridge model is briefly described in this section. The ADVI shares similarities with the reparameterization method of
Kingma \BBA Welling \APACyear2013
since it also makes use of reparameterization in order to write the gradient of the ELBO as an expectation to be approximated by Monte Carlo. The key distinction is that the method works in a transformed parameter space that is (ideally) suitable to be modeled as a multivariate normal distribution.

Suppose the original parameters
\bm{\theta}
vary on a subset
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\Theta\neq\mathbb{R}^{d}.
This happens for example when one or more entries of
\bm{\theta}
lie in constrained subsets of
\mathbb{R},
say
\mathbb{R}^{+}
or the interval
(0,1)
for instance. We consider the transformed parameter vector
\bm{\xi}=T(\bm{\theta})
where
T:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a is diffeomorphism map (differentiable and invertible transformation) such that
\bm{\xi}
lies in
\mathbb{R}^{d},
with no restrictions. For the mean-field approach, the joint variational distribution of the parameters is the product of its marginals:
q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\theta})=\prod^{K}_{j=1}q_{\psi_{j}}(\theta_{j})
for
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K})
. On the other hand, ADVI admits dependence structure given by
\bm{\theta}=T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})
where
\bm{\xi}\sim N(\mathbf{m},\bm{\Sigma})
with full covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
.

In the case of the Bayesian bridge model, we have the original parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{D},\phi,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{D},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{D})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{0}+K_{1}+\ldots+K_{D}}\times(0,\ +\infty)^{D+1}\times(0,\ 2.5)^{D}
. A possible choice for
T
is

T(\bm{\theta})=(\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{D},\log\phi,\log\lambda_{1},\ldots,\log\lambda_{D},\\
\log\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2.5-\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\log\frac{\alpha_{D}}{2.5-\alpha_{D}})^{\top}.

In the remainder of this section, we will denote
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}},\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{1}},\ldots,\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{d}},\xi_{\phi},\xi_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{\lambda_{D}},\xi_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha_{D}})^{\top},
where
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}=\bm{\beta}_{0},\ \bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{j}}=\bm{\beta}_{j},\ \xi_{\phi}=\log{\phi},\ \xi_{\lambda_{j}}=\log{\lambda_{j}},\ \xi_{\alpha_{j}}=\log\frac{\alpha_{j}}{2.5-\alpha_{j}},
for
j=1,\ldots,D
. The joint distribution
p(\mathbf{y},\bm{\theta})
is defined as in the right hand side of equation (
10
) with likelihood
(\mathbf{y}|\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{D},\phi)\sim N(\sum^{D}_{j=0}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{j},\phi^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{n})
and prior
p(\bm{\theta})=p(\bm{\beta}_{0})p(\phi)\prod^{D}_{j=1}p(\bm{\beta}_{j}|\lambda_{j},\phi,\alpha_{j})p(\lambda_{j})p(\alpha_{j}),

(\beta_{j}|\lambda_{j},\phi,\alpha_{j})\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}GG(0,\lambda_{j}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}},\alpha_{j})
,
\phi\sim Ga(a_{\phi},b_{\phi}),

\bm{\beta}_{0}\sim N(\bm{\mu}_{0},\Sigma_{0}),\lambda_{j}\sim Ga(a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda})
with
\alpha_{j}=2.5\eta_{j}
where
\eta_{j}\sim Beta(a_{\eta},b_{\eta})
for
j=1,\ldots,D.

The ADVI method redefines the joint model density in terms of the joint distribution of
\mathbf{y}
and the transformed parameter
\bm{\xi}
, here denoted as
\tilde{p}(\mathbf{y},\bm{\xi})
to distinguish it from the original joint density
p(\mathbf{y},\bm{\theta})
. It follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(\mathbf{y},\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{y},\bm{\theta})\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\theta}=T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\times\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{y},T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))\times\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid,

where
J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}
represents the Jacobian of the inverse transformation
T^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\Theta,

T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})=\left(\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}},\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{1}},\ldots,\bm{\xi}_{\bm{\beta}_{D}},e^{\xi_{\phi}},e^{\xi_{\lambda_{1}}},\ldots,e^{\xi_{\lambda_{D}}},\right.\\
\left.\frac{2.5}{1+e^{-\xi{\alpha_{1}}}},\ldots,\frac{2.5}{1+e^{-\xi{\alpha_{D}}}}\right)^{\top}

which in this case is
|J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}|=2.5^{D}e^{\xi_{\phi}}\prod^{D}_{j=1}e^{\xi_{\lambda_{j}}}e^{-\xi_{\alpha_{j}}}/(1+e^{-\xi_{\alpha_{j}}})^{2}
. A multivariate Gaussian variational distribution
q(\bm{\xi}\mid\mathbf{L},\ \mathbf{m})=N(\bm{\xi}\mid\mathbf{m},\ \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})
is specified for
\bm{\xi}
and the variational parameters are
\bm{\psi}=(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{L})
. By reparameterizing
\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\ \bm{\epsilon},
the ADVI method enables calculation of the gradient of the ELBO as an expectation, which can be approximated via Monte Carlo:

\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\mathbf{y},\bm{\psi})=\\
=\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\xi})}\left[\log\tilde{p}(\mathbf{y},\bm{\xi})-\log q_{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{\xi})\right]\\
=\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(\bf 0,\mathbf{I})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y},T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))+\right.\\
\left.+\log\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid-\log N(\bm{\xi};\ \mathbf{m},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon}}\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(\bf 0,\mathbf{I})}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y},T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))+\log\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\log N(\bm{\xi};\ \mathbf{m},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon}}\right]\right\}\\
\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{\ell=1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y},T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))+\log\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid\right.\\
\left.-\log N(\bm{\xi};\ \mathbf{m},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}}\right]

(8)

where
\bm{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(\bf 0,\mathbf{I})
,
\ell=1,\ldots,M
.

One can also easily compute the stochastic gradient approximation of (
8
) for a random minibatch
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\left(y_{i_{1}},\ldots,y_{i_{K}}\right)
where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}
represent a random subset of size
K
from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. There are many ways in which one can draw random minibatches
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
, as long as the Monte Carlo estimate for the gradient based on
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
is unbiased for the full gradient based on
\mathbf{y}
. In this work, we randomly permute the indexes of
\mathbf{y}
at the beginning of each epoch and pick blocks of
M
consecutive observations to form the random batches at each iteration. The Monte Carlo estimate for stochastic gradient of the ELBO becomes

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\bm{\psi}}ELBO(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\bm{\psi})\\
=\frac{n}{KM}\sum^{M}_{\ell=1}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\log p(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\mid T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}}\right]\ +\ \\
+\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{\ell=1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\left[\log p(T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))+\log\mid J_{T^{-1}(\bm{\xi})}\mid\right.\\
\left.-\log N(\bm{\xi};\ \mathbf{m},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{L}\bm{\epsilon}^{(\ell)}}\right].

(9)

In equation (
9
), the terms
p(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\mid T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))
and
p(T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}))
denote the densities
p(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\mid\bm{\theta})
and
p(\bm{\theta})
evaluated at
\bm{\theta}=T^{-1}(\bm{\xi}).
The multiplicative term
\frac{n}{K}
rescales the minibatch gradient to make it an unbiased estimate for the full posterior
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
based on
n
observations rather than the batch size posterior, which is based on
M
data points only. Algorithm

Real data application

This section illustrates the use of the proposed ADVI inference procedure for semi-parametric Bayesian bridge regression on a large real dataset. The data consists of hourly measured Energy Charges starting from 2014-04-06 (10pm) to 2022-03-31 (11pm) averaged over stations in the Northern region of Brazil (see Figure
11
). In total, there are
n=69717
obsevations. The dataset is maintained by Operador Nacional do Sistema Elétrico (ONS) and can be obtained at https://dados.ons.org.br/dataset/carga-energia .

The data exhibits strong seasonal patterns with multiple frequencies due to periodicities in energy consumption according with time of the day, day of the week, season of the year and possibly more. We model the seasonal harmonics as the cosine and sine Fourier basis representation for weekly periodicity (period =
24\times 7=168
) following
West \BBA Harrison \APACyear1997
. To capture overall level changes in the series, we included a cubic B-spline with one knot at every 100 hours for a total of 700 knots. The B-spline coefficients are subjected to bridge penalization while the coefficients of the Fourier harmonics basis functions are not penalized. In total, the resulting covariate matrix has
p=868
columns.

The proposed ADVI inference approach took approximately 831 seconds (less than 14min) to complete 2000 iterations using 100 Monte Carlo samples to estimate the gradient of the ELBO. On the other hand, MCMC takes 69239 seconds (19.23 hours) to run the same 2000 iterations. Posterior estimates for the beginning and end of the series are shown in Figures
12
and
13
.

7
Conclusions and future work

The present work developed a variational inference procedure based on ADVI for Bayesian inference in semi-parametric bridge regression models. The use of small batches of data at each iteration of the training algorithm reduces computational time in comparison with a more traditional MCMC approach. Full Bayesian inference is preserved so joint uncertainty estimates for all model parameters are available. It was verified in the simulation study that the joint posterior is well approximated by the proposed variational family.

Figure 13:
VB 95% credibility bands and posterior mean for the average response. Only the last 1000 observations of Energy Charge data are shown.

Some directions for future research include (i) the extension to non-Gaussian distribtutions for the response variable; (ii) expanding flexibility of the variational family with other approaches such as the semi implicit variational inference from
Yin \BBA Zhou \APACyear2018
and (iii) considering different spline basis functions to represent non-parametric effects of covariaties including GAM models with tensor splines avoiding MCMC and RJMCMC. See details in
Denison \BOthers. \APACyear1998
,
Dias \BBA Gamerman \APACyear2002
,
F. Li \BBA Villani \APACyear2013
and references therein.
