Maximum confidence measurement for qubit states
Hanwool Lee, Kieran Flatt, Carles Roch i Carceller, Jonatan Bohr Brask, Joonwoo Bae

Abstract
In quantum state discrimination, one aims to identify unknown states from a given ensemble by performing measurements. Different strategies such as minimum-error discrimination or unambiguous state identification find different optimal measurements. Maximum-confidence measurements (MCMs) maximise the confidence with which inputs can be identified given the measurement outcomes. This unifies a range of discrimination strategies including minimum-error and unambiguous state identification, which can be understood as limiting cases of MCM. In this work, we investigate MCMs for general ensembles of qubit states. We present a method for finding MCMs for qubit-state ensembles by exploiting their geometry, and apply it to several interesting cases, including ensembles two and four mixed states and ensembles of an arbitrary number of pure states. We also compare MCMs to minimum-error and unambiguous discrimination for qubits. Our results provide interpretations of various qubit measurements in terms of MCM and can be used to devise qubit protocols.



I
Introduction

One fundamental difference between classical and quantum physics is that, while all information about the physical state of a quantum system is captured by its quantum state, such states are in general not perfectly distinguishable. Specifically, no measurement can perfectly discriminate non-orthogonal quantum states. This is closely related to other fundamental results in quantum mechanics such as the impossibility of perfectly copying quantum states
Wootters and Zurek 1982
and of faster-than-light signalling
Gisin 1998
. The limits to discriminating between quantum states have numerous applications in quantum information science. Such limits are key to the security of quantum key distribution
Bennett and Brassard 1984
;
Bennett et al. 1992
; near-optimal state discrimination enables approximate quantum error correction
Barnum and Knill 2002
. They are also useful for operationally interpreting the differences between separable and entangled states
Bennett et al. 1999
;
Matthews et al. 2009
, see also
Spehner and Orszag 2013a
;
Spehner and Orszag 2013b
. For further examples of the wide impact of quantum state discrimination, see the related reviews Refs.
Chefles 2000
;
Bergou et al. 2004
;
Bergou 2007
;
Barnett and Croke 2009
;
Bergou 2010
;
Bae and Kwek 2015
;
Spehner 2014
.

If it is impossible to perfectly discriminate quantum states, the natural thing to ask is precisely how well one can do. This, in turn, introduces the need for different figures of merit, corresponding to variations of the discrimination task. In general, the task consists in identifying states drawn from some ensemble, given a single copy of the state and prior knowledge of the possible states. Two well-studied cases are minimum-error and unambiguous state discrimination (MED and USD, respectively). In MED, one aims to minimise the probability that the state is misidentified while forbidding inconclusive outcomes
Helstrom 1967
;
Helstrom 1968
;
Helstrom 1969
. In USD, one instead enforces that the state is never misidentified, at the price of allowing for a non-zero inconclusive-outcome rate, which one then aims to minimise
Ivanovic 1987
;
Dieks 1988
;
Peres 1988
. Both MED and USD are naturally formulated as statements about the conditional probabilities for observing certain outcomes, given that particular states were prepared.

Interestingly, distinct figures of merits in quantum state discrimination can be rephrased in terms of predictive and retrodictive formulations of quantum probabilities
Barnett et al. 2021
. Predictive probabilities are probabilities of future events conditioned on past events, which, in this context, are the probabilities of the outcomes conditioned on the input states. Retrodictive probabilities are probabilities of past events conditioned on future events occurring; here, this means the probabilities, conditioned on the observed outcomes, that particular input states were prepared. Predictive and retrodictive probabilities can be linked via Bayes’ theorem.

In this work, we focus on maximum-confidence discrimination, which is most naturally formulated in the retrodictive picture. The figure of merit here is the
confidence
, defined as the conditional probability that an input was prepared given that the corresponding outcome was observed. A maximum confidence measurement (MCM) is a measurement strategy which achieves the best possible confidence. MCMs were introduced in Ref.
Croke et al. 2006
. They unify the MED and USD settings of state discrimination. In particular, MCMs implement USD whenever USD is possible for the given ensemble and MED if a zero inconclusive rate is enforced and the maximum confidence considers an ensemble itself. In general, they make optimal use of detection events for guessing which states were prepared in the past
Herzog 2009
;
Herzog 2012a
;
Herzog 2012b
;
Bagan et al. 2012
;
Herzog 2015
;
Kenbaev and Kronberg 2022
.

We investigate MCMs for qubit states and determine general relations between a given ensemble and its MCM. We present a method for finding MCMs by exploiting the geometry of the Bloch sphere directly, without reference to the algebraic optimization problem, in a similar manner to geometric schemes for MED of
n
qubit states
Deconinck and Terhal 2010
;
Bae and Hwang 2013
;
Ha and Kwon 2013
. We then consider several particular ensembles of qubit states, derive their MCMs, and also compare to MED and USD.

The article is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, we start by briefly recalling the state discrimination problem in the simplest case of two pure states, and results for optimal MED and USD. In Sec.
III
, we summarise MCMs. In Sec.
IV
, we formulate the problem of identifying an optimal MCM for qubits as a semidefinite program (SDP) and present optimality conditions. The relations between state ensembles and MCMs are found by exploiting the Bloch sphere geometry. In Sec.
V
, various ensembles of qubit state ensembles are considered, and their MCMs are explicitly derived. We consider two mixed states, geometrically uniform states, tetrahedron states, and asymmetric states. In Sec.
VI
we conclude.

II
MED and USD for two pure states

Let us consider the simplest non-trivial ensemble, consisting of two pure states,
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi_{1}\rangle
, generated with
a priori
probabilities
q_{0}
and
q_{1}
, respectively. A measurement device receives state
|\psi_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle
with
\mathrm{x}\in\{0,1\}
, drawn from this ensemble, and provides an output
\mathrm{y}\in\{0,1,2\}
. The output can be understood as a guess for what input was prepared, i.e. for the value of
\mathrm{x}
, with
\mathrm{y}=2
denoting inconclusive outcomes. One can thus define an average error rate and an inconclusive rate, respectively, as

\eta_{err}=q_{0}\Pr(\mathrm{y}=1|\mathrm{x}=0)+q_{1}\Pr(\mathrm{y}=0|\mathrm{x}=1),

(1)

and

\eta_{inc}=q_{0}\Pr(\mathrm{y}=2|\mathrm{x}=0)+q_{1}\Pr(\mathrm{y}=2|\mathrm{x}=1),

(2)

where
\Pr(\mathrm{y}|\mathrm{x})
denotes the conditional probability of observing outcome
\mathrm{y}
given input
\mathrm{x}
.

In MED, the goal is to minimise
\eta_{err}
under the constraint that no inconclusive outcomes occur, i.e.,
\Pr(\mathrm{y}=2|\mathrm{x}=0)=\Pr(\mathrm{y}=2|\mathrm{x}=1)=0
. In this case, the minimal error rate is known as the Helstrom bound
Helstrom 1967
;
Helstrom 1968
;
Helstrom 1969

\eta_{err}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|q_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|-q_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|\|_{1},

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the trace norm.

This result applies to an arbitrary pair of quantum states and is found by a measurement with a construction as follows. As it is shown in Eq. (
3
), the optimal measurement can be found in the support of given states
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi_{1}\rangle
. Then, two optimal positive-positive-operator-valued-measure (POVM) elements
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
are found as projectors with positive and negative eigenvalues of the operator
(q_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|-q_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|)
.

One can also notice that, independently to a dimension of a Hilbert space where two states can be described, the two-state discrimination problem can be reduced to a two-dimensional space spanned by
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi_{1}\rangle
. In this sense, the two-state problem is equivalent to discrimination of two qubit states. Then, by referring to a Bloch sphere, an optimal measurement with POVM elements
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
can be found in a diameter of a half-plane due to the completeness, i.e.,
M_{0}+M_{1}=\mathbbm{I}
. The Helstrom bound in Eq. (
3
) clarifies that the diameter should be parallel to the difference
(q_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|-q_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|)
.

In USD, on the other hand, the goal is to minimise
\eta_{inc}
under the constraint that no errors occur, i.e.,
\Pr(\mathrm{y}=1|\mathrm{x}=0)=\Pr(\mathrm{y}=0|\mathrm{x}=1)=0
. In this case, the minimal inconclusive rate is

\eta_{inc}=2\sqrt{q_{0}q_{1}}|\langle\psi_{0}|\psi_{1}\rangle|

(4)

If one hopes to be certain about which state was prepared, it suffices to rule out the other option. If one measurement outcome is
|\bar{\psi}_{0}\rangle
, such that
\langle\psi_{0}|\bar{\psi}_{0}\rangle=0
, then that outcome can never occur when
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is measured. This means that the prepared state must have been
|\psi_{1}\rangle
. The same holds for the other state, so that the POVM must include among its elements the two states orthogonal to those in the ensemble. A measurement consisting of just those outcomes, however, will not be complete, and so the POVM must be completed by a third element, which is the inconclusive one. Each of the elements must be weighted by constant factors and that associated with the third outcome determines the inconclusive rate. It is thus minimised. In this manner, the rate Eq. (
4
) is attained.
Barnett and Croke 2009
.

III
Maximum confidence measurement

We not turn to the more general case of discriminating between an arbitrary number of states. Let
S
denote an ensemble of qubit states in which the states
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
are generated with
a priori
probabilities
q_{\mathrm{x}}
:

\displaystyle S=\{q_{\mathrm{x}},\rho_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{n-1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \rho=\sum_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{n-1}q_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}.

(5)

The most general measurement corresponds to an
n+1
-outcome positive-operator-valued-measure (POVM), denoted by
M=\{M_{\mathrm{y}}\}_{\mathrm{y}=0}^{n}
, where outcome
n
collects inconclusive events, while outcome
\mathrm{y}
for
\mathrm{y}=0,\cdots,n-1
denotes a guess that the input
\rho_{\mathrm{y}}
was prepared.

Let
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
denote a state of particular interest in the ensemble. The probability that the correct state is identified is the confidence associated with the measurement
Croke et al. 2006
,

\displaystyle C(\mathrm{x})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Pr(\rho_{x}|M_{x})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr(\rho_{x})\Pr(M_{x}|\rho_{x})}{\Pr(M_{x})}=\frac{q_{\mathrm{x}}\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{x}}M_{\mathrm{x}}]}{\mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\mathrm{x}}]},

where Bayes’ rule is applied and
\Pr(M_{\mathrm{x}}|\rho_{\mathrm{x}})
is the probability that the outcome associated with
M_{\mathrm{x}}
is triggered by the state
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
. For example,
C(\mathrm{x})=1
for some
\mathrm{x}
signifies unambiguous identification of the state
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
by a detection event on
M_{\mathrm{x}}
. Given a detection event, a state
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
is verified with certainty. Unambiguous discrimination of quantum states is achieved when
C(\mathrm{x})=1
for all
\mathrm{x}=0,\cdots,n-1
.

The confidence in Eq. (
6
) can be maximized by optimizing over each POVM element according to

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})=\max_{M_{\mathrm{x}}}\frac{q_{\mathrm{x}}\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{x}}M_{\mathrm{x}}]}{\mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\mathrm{x}}]},

(7)

where
0\leq M_{\mathrm{x}}\leq 1
. A valid POVM, which attains the optimum for all
\mathrm{x}
, can always be obtained by rescaling the
M_{\mathrm{x}}
and including one additional element
M_{\phi}
which collects inconclusive outcomes. Such a measurement is called an MCM. In general, we have
M_{\phi}\neq 0
.

As mentioned, when unambiguous discrimination is possible for an ensemble, the MCM is identical with the measurement giving unambiguous discrimination. An MCM for an ensemble of two pure states, for instance, will identify each state with perfect confidence. Note, however, that an MCM can be introduced for ensembles for which unambiguous discrimination is impossible, such as three qubit states.

One may consider the maximum confidence for an ensemble itself: writing by
p_{\mathrm{x}}
as the probability that a detector
M_{\mathrm{x}}
shows a detection event, i.e.,
p_{\mathrm{x}}=\mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\mathrm{x}}]
, the maximization

\displaystyle\max\sum_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{n-1}p_{\mathrm{x}}C(\mathrm{x})

(8)

over a complete measurement equals to the highest success probability in minimum-error state discrimination
Barnett and Croke 2009
. We remark that an MCM provides a unifying picture of different figures of merits in quantum state discrimination.

Note that the maximization in Eq. (
7
) is computationally feasible
Croke et al. 2006
. One can apply the transformation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}=\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}q_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{where}\penalty\ \penalty\ \rho=\sum_{j}q_{j}\rho_{j}

(9)

in order to rewrite the optimization problem in Eq. (
7
) as

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})=\max_{Q_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0,\penalty\ \mathrm{tr}[Q_{\mathrm{x}}]=1}\mathrm{tr}[\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}Q_{\mathrm{x}}].

(10)

It is not difficult to see that the maximum confidence above corresponds to the operator norm

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})=\|\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}q_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}\|_{op}

(11)

where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm
\|A\|_{op}=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|
. Once an optimal operator in Eq. (
10
), denoted by
Q_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
, is obtained, an optimal POVM element
M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
is found as

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}=c_{\mathrm{x}}\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\sqrt{\rho}^{-1}.

(12)

for some constant
c_{\mathrm{x}}>0
. Note that
\{c_{\mathrm{x}}\}
may be chosen such that
\sum_{\mathrm{x}}M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\leq\mathbbm{I}
.

IV
MCM for Qubit States

In this section, we approach the maximum confidence in Eq. (
7
) from the point of view of convex optimization. We first show a semidefinite program (SDP) for the optimization problem and then analyze the optimality conditions in order to show that a general structure relates the states to their MCM.

IV.1
Convex optimization

We begin with the maximization problem in Eq. (
10
), which is linear with respect to a state of interest. The optimization problem can be written as an SDP as follows,

\displaystyle p^{*}=\max

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}Q_{\mathrm{x}}]

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0\penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \mathrm{tr}[Q_{\mathrm{x}}]=1.

Its dual problem is found by constructing the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Q_{\mathrm{x}},\lambda_{\mathrm{x}},Z_{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}Q_{\mathrm{x}}]+\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}(1-\mathrm{tr}[Q_{\mathrm{x}}])+\mathrm{tr}[Q_{\mathrm{x}}Z_{\mathrm{x}}]\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

(14)

where
Z_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}
are dual variables. Maximizing this Lagrangian gives the dual function

\displaystyle g(\lambda_{\mathrm{x}},Z_{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{Q_{\mathrm{x}}}\mathcal{L}(Q_{\mathrm{x}},\lambda_{\mathrm{x}},Z_{\mathrm{x}})

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}+\max_{Q_{\mathrm{x}}}\mathrm{tr}[(\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}I+Z_{\mathrm{x}})Q_{\mathrm{x}}].

It can be seen that the function
g(\lambda_{\mathrm{x}},Z_{\mathrm{x}})
does not converge if
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}I+Z_{\mathrm{x}}\neq 0
. Therefore, the optimal dual parameters
Z_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{x}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\mathbbm{I}+Z_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}=0,

(16)

called the Lagrangian stability. We note that the primal problem is feasible. The next condition that optimal parameters satisfy is called the complementary slackness, given as

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[Z_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}Q_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}]=0.

(17)

The dual problem is then obtained as

\displaystyle d^{*}=\min

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}\rho-q_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0.

Since both problems are feasible, one can find the maximum confidence from both primal and dual problems above,
p^{*}=d^{*}=\max C(\mathrm{x})
.

The linear complementarity problem (LCP) approach may be used to understand the convex optimization problem’s structure
Cottle 2009
. Technically speaking, while an SDP, either primal or dual problem, is formed with inequalities, an LCP directly analyzes the optimality conditions, which are given in terms of equalities. Those primal and dual parameters satisfying the equalities automatically find an optimal solution.

We are now in a position to derive the optimality conditions in terms of an ensemble
\rho
and state of interest
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
. From the transformation in Eq. (
9
), we introduce new parameters
r_{\mathrm{x}}>0
and a state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
such that
r_{\mathrm{x}}\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}=\sqrt{\rho}Z_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\sqrt{\rho}
, so that the optimality conditions can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lagrangian\penalty\ stability\penalty\ }

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}\rho=q_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}+r_{\mathrm{x}}\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Complementary\penalty\ slackness\penalty\ }

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{x}}\mathrm{tr}[\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}M_{\mathrm{x}}]=0.

(20)

Since both primal and dual problems are feasible, those primal and dual parameters satisfying Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) automatically pinpoint the optimization problem’s solution. Once dual parameters are found from Eq. (
19
), the optimal POVM element is characterized by Eq. (
20
). Note that an optimal POVM element is found by the equalities given in the optimality conditions.

IV.2
MCM for qubit states

We now investigate the optimality conditions for qubit states and show how one can solve the optimization problem directly. Both the maximum confidence and optimal POVM elements can be found. Let us begin with the condition in Eq. (
20
). The product of a complementary state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
and an optimal POVM must be zero. Since the optimal measurement satisfies
M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\neq 0
for
\forall\mathrm{x}=0,\cdots,n-1
, it holds that both
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
and
M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
must be rank-one and orthogonal with each other.

Let us consider the Lagrangian stability in Eq. (
19
), which can be rewritten for all
\mathrm{x}=0,\ldots,n-1
as

\displaystyle\rho=\mu_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}}+(1-\mu_{\mathrm{x}})\sigma_{\mathrm{x}},\penalty\ \mathrm{where}\penalty\ \mu_{\mathrm{x}}=\frac{q_{\mathrm{x}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}}.

(21)

Note that decompositions above for qubit states have been also obtained in Ref.
Kenbaev and Kronberg 2022
. MCMs can be computed analytically from this relation, which implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{(1-\mu_{\mathrm{x}})^{2}}\mathrm{tr}[(\rho-\mu_{\mathrm{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{x}})^{2}].\penalty\

(22)

For qubit cases, the left-hand side is given by
1
since the complementary state is rank-one. It is straightforward to find the value
\mu_{\mathrm{x}}
as follows. Suppose that the state of interest
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
is pure, i.e.,
\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}]=1
. We then have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{x}}=\frac{1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{2}]}{2(1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho\rho_{\mathrm{x}}])},

(23)

which can be computed from an ensemble
\rho
and a state of interest
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
.

When a state
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
is not pure, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho\rho_{\mathrm{x}}])-\mathrm{Det}(\rho,\rho_{\mathrm{x}})}{1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}]}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{where}\penalty\

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Det}(\rho,\rho_{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho\rho_{\mathrm{x}}])^{2}-(1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{2}])(1-\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}])]^{1/2}.

The maximum confidence is obtained as

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})=\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}=\frac{q_{\mathrm{x}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{x}}},

(25)

when the state is prepared with
a priori
probability
q_{\mathrm{x}}
. We have therefore shown how to compute the maximum confidence for a state of interest. Once
\mu_{\mathrm{x}}
is found as above, one can find the complementary state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
in Eq. (
21
), from which the optimal POVM element is also found.

Example:
N

To illustrate our approach, let us consider an ensemble of
N

\displaystyle\left\{|\psi_{j}\rangle\right\}_{j=0}^{N-1},\penalty\ \mathrm{where}\penalty\ |\psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle\penalty\ \mathrm{and}

(26)

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi_{j}}\sin\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}|1\rangle\penalty\ \mathrm{for}\penalty\ j=1,\cdots,N-1.

Note that the angles (
\theta_{j}
,
\phi_{j}
) are arbitrary and the state of interest is denoted by

\displaystyle\max C(0)=\frac{2\left(1-\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho_{0}\rho_{M}\right]\right)}{N+1-(N-1)\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho_{M}^{2}\right]-2\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho_{0}\rho_{M}\right]}.\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

(27)

where

is an equally weighted mixture of

states

. It is seen that the maximum confidence depends on two parameters: the purity of an ensemble

and the fidelity between

In addition, as shown in Refs.

IV.3
Geometry of qubit states and an MCM

The general structure of qubit states and MCMs can be depicted on the Bloch sphere. We here analyze the optimality condition geometrically and present the structure. We also show forms of the maximum confidence different to Eq. (
25
).

Let us refer to Fig.
1
. Note that the natural distance measure in the Bloch sphere is given by the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, which turns out to be proportional to the trace norm for qubit cases

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}d_{HS}(\rho,\sigma)=\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}

(29)

where

2
and the Hilbert-Schmidt distance by
\sqrt{2}
. Thus, one can consider two measures interchangeably in the Bloch sphere and relate them by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

We begin by interpreting Eq. (
21
): an ensemble
\rho
is given as a convex mixture of a state of interest
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
and its complementary one
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
. This means that the Bloch vector of a state
\rho
lies on a line connecting two Bloch vectors of two states
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
. It also implies that the Bloch vector of a state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
can be found on a line connecting those of two states
\rho
and
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
. Let us recall from the optimality condition in Eq. (
20
) that a complementary state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
must be rank-one on the Bloch sphere. Therefore, one can find a complementary state
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
on the surface at which the line connecting two known states
\rho
and
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}
meets, see Fig.
1
. Once a complementary state is found, an optimal POVM element is obtained as the orthogonal complement,

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\propto\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}^{\perp}.

(30)

Both operators
M_{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}

US
is given by

\displaystyle\vec{r}=(\vec{n}(\rho)-\vec{n}(\rho_{\mathrm{x}}))t+\vec{n}(\rho)

(31)

for some
t\geq 0
. The complementary state’s Bloch vector
\vec{r}_{\mathrm{x}}
is found with
t_{\mathrm{x}}
, by which
\|\vec{r}\|=1
.

From the convex combination in Eq. (
21
), it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\rho-\rho_{x}\|_{1}}{\|\rho-\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\|_{1}}=\frac{1-\mu_{\mathrm{x}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{x}}}.

(32)

From the relation above, it is straightforward to find
\mu_{\mathrm{x}}=\|\rho-\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\|_{1}/\|\rho_{\mathrm{x}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\|_{1}
, so that

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q_{\mathrm{x}}\|\rho_{\mathrm{x}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\|_{1}}{\|\rho-\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\|_{1}}.\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

(33)

From Eq. (
31
), one can compute the maximum confidence above in terms of the Bloch vector. It follows that

\displaystyle\max C(\mathrm{x})=q_{\mathrm{x}}\left(1+\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{x}}}\right)

(34)

where
t_{\mathrm{x}}
is found from the constraint
\|\vec{r}\|=1
.

We have therefore shown that a complementary state can be directly found by exploiting the qubit state geometry, as well as an MCM. In summary, the maximum confidence for qubit states can be written in the various forms in Eqs. (
25
), (
33
) and (
34
).

IV.4
Minimizing the probability of inconclusive outcomes

Having found POVM elements for an MCM, let us consider the probability inconclusive outcomes. As it is mentioned, it is clear that a POVM element in an MCM is rank-one: for an ensemble in Eq. (
5
), let

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{x}}=a_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}

(35)

denote a POVM element for each state where
a_{\mathrm{x}}
is a non-negative constant and
\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}
a rank-one projector. The projectors
\{\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{N-1}
are immediately obtained such that they perform an MCM. Then, a set of constants
\{a_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{N-1}
is chosen to find the probability of inconclusive outcomes, for which the POVM element is denoted by

\displaystyle M_{\phi}=\mathbbm{I}-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}

(36)

so that its probability is given by
p_{inc}=\mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\phi}]
.

Remarks are in order. Firstly, an MCM for an ensemble (see Eq. (
5
)) varies by choosing different values
\{a_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{N-1}
, for all of which an MCM holds true. This immediately concludes that an MCM for an ensemble is not unique. Secondly, if the convex hull of POVM elements
\{\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}
performing an MCM contains the identity, i.e.,
\{a_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{N-1}
can be chosen such that
\sum_{\mathrm{x}=0}^{N-1}a_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}=\mathbbm{I}
, one can find an MCM that is also complete. Consequently, an inconclusive outcome does not occur, since
M_{\phi}=0
and
p_{inc}=0
.

Remark.
Let
\{\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}
denote a set of rank-one projectors and suppose that their convex hull contains the identity. Then, an MCM with the projectors,
\{a_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}
, can be constructed such that an inconclusive outcome does not occur.

Thirdly, if the convex hull of POVM elements
\{\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}
does not contain the identity, an optimization problem is introduced to minimize the probability of inconclusive outcomes. From a POVM in Eq. (
36
), the problem is defined as follows.

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\phi}]

(37)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to\penalty\ }

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0,\penalty\ \forall\mathrm{x},\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ M_{\phi}\geq 0.

The optimization problem may be approached by a Lagrangian,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{tr}[\rho M_{\phi}]-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}v_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}-\mathrm{tr}[KM_{\phi}]}

where
K\geq 0
and
v_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0
are dual parameters. The optimality conditions contain the Lagrangian stability, i.e,
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a_{\mathrm{x}}=0
, for all
\mathrm{x}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[\rho\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}]+v_{\mathrm{x}}-\mathrm{tr}[\rho\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}]=0,

(38)

and the complementary slackness,

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}=0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ {\mathrm{tr}}KM_{\phi}=0.

(39)

The optimization problem works for an arbitrary ensemble of quantum states. In what follows, let us rewrite the problem specifically for qubit states.

It is straightforward to find that Eq. (
39
) implies
M_{\phi}
is rank-one for qubit states. Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\big(\frac{M_{\phi}}{\mathrm{tr}[M_{\phi}]}\big)^{2}=\frac{M_{\phi}}{\mathrm{tr}[M_{\phi}]}

which is equivalent to, from Eq. (
36
),

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}}(1-\mathrm{tr}[\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{y}}])a_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{y}}=0.

(40)

In addition, also from Eq. (
36
), we have
\mathrm{tr}[M_{\phi}]\geq 0
, meaning that
2-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0
. With the constraints, the optimization problem in Eq. (
37
) for qubit states can be written as follows,

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{tr}[\rho(1-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{x}})]

(41)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to\penalty\ }

\displaystyle\forall\mathrm{x},\penalty\ a_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ 2-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathrm{and}

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}}(1-\mathrm{tr}[\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\Pi_{\mathrm{y}}])a_{\mathrm{x}}a_{\mathrm{y}}=0.

The probability of inconclusive outcomes for an MCM of qubit states can be generally obtained by solving the optimization problem. We reiterate that, once a set of projectors for an MCM
\{\Pi_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=1}^{N-1}
is obtained, the optimization problem above finds a set of optimal coefficients
\{a_{\mathrm{x}}\}_{\mathrm{x}=1}^{N-1}
to minimize the probability of inconclusive outcomes.

VI
Conclusion

In summary, we have investigated MCMs for qubit states. We have presented a simple scheme to find MCMs for qubit states when an ensemble and a state of interest are given. The scheme exploits the geometry in a Bloch sphere without resorting to the computational optimization problem. We then considered various qubit states. From the cases of two qubit states, it is shown that an MCM lies between two strategies, minimum-error and unambiguous discrimination. An MCM for geometrically uniform states generalizes an example from Ref.
Croke et al. 2006
. An MCM for tetrahedron states is identical to a measurement for minimum-error discrimination. Otherwise, when an ensemble does not contain any symmetry, it was seen that MCMs highly depends on the particular state of interest.

Our results elucidate the meanings of different qubit measurements, each of which may aim to maximize different figures of merit. Measurements for various qubit ensembles may also be used to devise quantum protocols to certify the properties of qubit states.
