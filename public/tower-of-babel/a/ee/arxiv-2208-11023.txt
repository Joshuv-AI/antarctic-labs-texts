Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the Grassmann tensor by tensor product of vectors and some basic terminology in tensor theory. Some basic properties of the Grassmann tensors are investigated and the tensor language is used to rewrite some relations and correspondences in the mutliview geometry. Finally we show that a polytope in the Euclidean space ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} can also be concisely expressed as the Grassmann tensor generated by its vertices.

Deep learning models are composed of layers each of which takes some input data and produces the output after some transformations. There are many types of layers, including convolutional layers, pooling layers and recurrent layers, which are fully connected and parameterized by tensors. Tensors are also used to represent the weights of neural networks. A weight tensor is simply a tensor that is used as a parameter in a layer. When we train a neural network, we need to optimize the values of these weight tensors to minimize some loss functions.

\textbf{a},\textbf{b},\textbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b})\wedge\textbf{c}=\textbf{a}\wedge(\textbf{b}\wedge\textbf{c})=\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b}\wedge\textbf{c}

(2.6)

Proof.

We need only to show

(\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b})\wedge\textbf{c}=\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b}\wedge\textbf{c}

(2.7)

Denote
A=(a_{ij})=\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b}
. By (
2.5
), the left hand side of (
2.7

\displaystyle A\wedge\textbf{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a})\times_{\hat{1}}\textbf{c}-(\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a})\times_{\hat{2}}\textbf{c}+(\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a})\times_{\hat{3}}\textbf{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{c}\times\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{c}\times\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a}-\textbf{a}\times\textbf{c}\times\textbf{b}+\textbf{b}\times\textbf{c}\times\textbf{a}+\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}\times\textbf{c}-\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a}\times\textbf{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}\times\textbf{c}+\textbf{b}\times\textbf{c}\times\textbf{a}+\textbf{c}\times\textbf{a}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{a}\times\textbf{c}\times\textbf{b}-\textbf{c}\times\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a}-\textbf{b}\times\textbf{a}\times\textbf{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{a}\wedge\textbf{b}\wedge\textbf{c}

The equality

can also be proved by similar arguments.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Let

be either a symmetric matrix or a rank-one matrix. Then

.

Proof.

First we assume that
\texttt{rank}(A)=1
. Then there exist some vectors
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

. Note that

(\alpha\times\beta)\times_{(1,3)}(\alpha\times\beta)=(\alpha\times\beta)\times_{(1,4)}(\alpha\times\beta)=\alpha\times\alpha\times\beta\times\beta,

by (

\displaystyle A\wedge A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha\times\beta)\wedge(\alpha\times\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2[(\alpha\times\beta)\times(\alpha\times\beta)-(\alpha\times\beta)\times_{(1,3)}(\alpha\times\beta)+(\alpha\times\beta)\times_{(1,4)}(\alpha\times\beta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\alpha\times\beta\times\alpha\times\beta)=2A\times A.

If

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i,j}(\alpha_{i}\times\alpha_{i})\wedge(\alpha_{j}\times\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\times\alpha_{i}\times\alpha_{j}\times\alpha_{j}=2(A\times A).

The last equation comes from the expansion of

Corollary 2.11

Lemma 2.12
.

Let

be linearly independent and

If

3
Applications of Grassmann tensors in multiview geometry and geometry

Tensors can be employed to express the correspondences in computer vision. They can also be used to estimate the fundamental matrix, which is crucial in 3D
reconstruction from two-views in the vision. A fundamental matrix
F
can be described as the homography transforming a point
x
in an image plane to a line

l^{\prime}=F\textbf{x}
in another plane, so

(\textbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}F\textbf{x}=0

(3.1)

holds for every corresponding point pair
(\textbf{x},\textbf{x}^{\prime})
. (
3.1
) can alternatively be written in tensor form as

F\times_{1}\textbf{x}^{\prime}\times_{2}\textbf{x}=0

(3.2)

where
F
is viewed as a second-order tensor. Note that here all points and planes are expressed in homogeneous coordinate system, i.e.,
\textbf{x},\textbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Another
expression for
F
is through the two camera matrices.

The Grassmann tensor can be used to simplify some expressions in the geometry. For example, a plane determined by three points at general positions can be
is usually expressed by a determinant equation as in the following.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
(
X_{k}
is 4-dimensional) be located in general positions, i.e., they are non-colinear. Then a point
X\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
lies on the plane determined by
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
if and only if
\det[X,X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]=0
.

By the Grassmann tensor, however, we can put it in a more concise form, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
be vectors representing respectively planes
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
and
\pi_{3}
located at general positions. Then these planes meet
at a unique point
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
if and only if
\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}=0
where
\mathcal{A}=\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3}\in\mathcal{T}_{3;4}
.

Proof.

We let
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
be the intersection point of the three planes
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
and
\pi_{3}
. By definition, we have

\alpha_{i}^{\top}\textbf{x}=0,\quad i=1,2,3.

(3.3)

Then by definition we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3})\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1}\times\alpha_{2}\times\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{2}\times\alpha_{3}\times\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}\times\alpha_{1}\times\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{2}\times\alpha_{1}\times\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{3}\times\alpha_{2}\times\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{1}\times\alpha_{3}\times\alpha_{2})\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{3}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}+(\alpha_{1}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3}+(\alpha_{2}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{3}\wedge\alpha_{1}

So we have

\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}=(\alpha_{3}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}+(\alpha_{1}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3}+(\alpha_{2}^{\top}\textbf{x})\alpha_{3}\wedge\alpha_{1}

(3.4)

Since
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are linearly independent, we know that
\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3},\alpha_{3}\wedge\alpha_{1}
are also linearly independent
by Lemma
2.12
. It follows from (
3.4
) that
\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}=0
is equivalent to (
3.3
).
∎

By the symmetry, we can deduce the following result which is analog to Theorem
3.14
.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
be vectors representing three points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
at general positions. Then they uniquely determine a plane
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Furthermore,
a point
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
lies on
\pi
if and only if
x
satisfies condition
\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}=0
where
\mathcal{A}=\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3}\in\mathcal{T}_{3;4}
.

By Corollary
3.15
, we call
\mathcal{A}=\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{3}
the
Grassmann tensor
or
Plücker tensor
associated with plane
\pi

which is determined by points
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. This is actually the extension of the
Plücker matrix
of a line, as explained in the follows.

Let
L(X,Y)
denote the line determined by two distinct points
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
which are homogeneous represented. The
Plücker matrix
associated with

X,Y
, denoted by
P:=P[X,Y]
, is defined as

P:=X\wedge Y=X\times Y-Y\times X=XY^{\top}-YX^{\top}

(3.5)

P[X,Y]\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
is a rank-2 anti-symmetric matrix. Different pairs
(X,Y)
may give the same Plücker matrix
P
. But we do have

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
(X,Y)
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be two point pairs with
X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Then
P[X,Y]=\lambda P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]
for some nonzero scalar
\lambda
if and only if there is a nonsingular matrix

Q=(q_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}X^{\prime}&=&q_{11}X+q_{12}Y,\\
Y^{\prime}&=&q_{21}X+q_{22}Y\end{array}\right.

(3.6)

Proof.

We let
M=[X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]
. Then
M\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
. We want to prove that
\texttt{rank}(M)=2
. We write
M_{1}=[X,Y]
and
M_{2}=[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]
, then we have

M_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}
. If
X,Y
are linearly dependent, then
P[X,Y]=0
, thus we may assume that
X,Y
(and
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
) are linearly independent. So

\texttt{rank}(M_{k})=2
for
k=1,2
.

Denote by
N(A)
the null space of a given matrix
A
. We now show that

\displaystyle N(P[X,Y])

\displaystyle=N(M_{1}^{\top})

(3.7)

\displaystyle N(P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=N(M_{2}^{\top})

(3.8)

To prove (
3.7
), we let
Z\in N(P[X,Y])
. Then

0=P[X,Y]Z=(XY^{\top}-YX^{\top})Z=(Y^{\top}Z)X-(X^{\top}Z)Y,

which implies
X^{\prime}Z=0
and
Y^{\prime}Z=0
, and thus
Z\in N(M^{\top})
. It follows that
N(P[X,Y])\subseteq N(M_{1}^{\top})
. Conversely, we can show

N(M_{1}^{\top})\subseteq N(P[X,Y])
by reversing the argument. Thus (
3.7
) holds. Similarly we can show (
3.8
).

To show the sufficiency of (
3.6
), we first note that

P[X,Y]=M_{1}DM_{1}^{\top},P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]=M_{2}DM_{2}^{\top}

(3.9)

where
D=E_{2}
is defined by (
2.4
). If (
3.6
) holds, i.e.,
M_{2}=M_{1}Q
with
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
nonsingular, then we have

P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]=M_{2}DM_{2}^{\top}=M_{1}QDQ^{\top}M_{1}^{\top}=M_{1}P[q_{1},q_{2}]M_{1}^{\top}

(3.10)

where
Q=[q_{1},q_{2}]
with
q_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(
k=1,2
). We note that
q_{1},q_{2}
are linearly independent due to the nonsingularity of
Q
, and thus
P[q_{1},q_{2}]

is not zero. Furthermore, we have
P[q_{1},q_{2}]=\det(Q)D
by simple computations. Consequently we get by (
3.10
)

P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]=\det(Q)M_{1}DM_{1}^{\top}=\det(Q)P[X,Y].

To prove the necessity, we assume w.l.g. that
\lambda=1
and
P[X,Y]=P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]
. By (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we have

N(M_{1}^{\top})=N(M_{2}^{\top})
. Thus

N(M^{\top})=N(M_{1}^{\top})\cap N(M_{2}^{\top})=N(M_{1}^{\top}),

which implies
\texttt{rank}(M)=\texttt{rank}(M^{\top})=\texttt{rank}(M_{1}^{\prime})=2
. Since
X,Y
are linearly independent, there exists a matrix
Q=(q_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
such that

M_{2}=M_{1}Q
. Similarly we have
M_{1}=M_{2}Q^{\prime}
for some
Q^{\prime}=(q^{\prime}_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
by swapping
(X,Y)
with
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
. It follows that

Q
(and also
Q^{\prime}
) is invertible (nonsingular). The proof is completed.
∎

Theorem
3.16
allows us to choose an invertible matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
such that
\left\{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\right\}
is orthognormal, i.e. ,

\left<X^{\prime},X^{\prime}\right>=\left<Y^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\right>=1,\quad\left<X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\right>=0

(3.11)

with
P[X,Y]=P[X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}]
. In fact, we can use the Schmidt orthognormal process to obtain
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}X^{\prime}&=\lambda_{1}X,&\lambda_{1}:=\frac{1}{\parallel\!X\!\parallel},\\
\bar{Y}&=Y-\left<X^{\prime},Y\right>X^{\prime},&\\
Y^{\prime}&=\lambda_{2}\bar{Y},&\lambda_{2}:=\frac{1}{\parallel\!\bar{Y}\!\parallel}\end{array}

We now show the following theorem.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
be orthognormal. Then the Plücker matrix
P=P[X,Y]
is the reflection with respect to
Y
.

Proof.

We first assume that
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are orthognormal vectors, i.e.,

\parallel\!X\!\parallel=\parallel\!Y\!\parallel=1,\left<X,Y\right>=X^{\top}Y=0,

where the norm is
2
-norm. Then we have

PX=(XY^{\top}-YX^{\top})X=-Y,\quad PY=(XY^{\top}-YX^{\top})Y=X,

Denote
\alpha=X+\imath Y,\beta=X-\imath Y
where
\imath:=\sqrt{-1}
. Then
\alpha,\beta
are orthognormal (thus also linearly independent). Furthermore, we can
check easily that

P[\alpha,\beta]=[\alpha,\beta]K,

where
K=\texttt{diag}(\imath,-\imath)
, i.e.,
P
maps
\alpha
to
\alpha^{\prime}=\imath\alpha
and
\beta
to
\beta^{\prime}=-\imath\beta
.
∎

The Plücker matrix

[

.

Let
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}

In order to generalize Proposition
3.18
, we first need the following lemma which is an extension of Lemma
2.12
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let vectors
\textbf{a}^{1},\textbf{a}^{2},\ldots,\textbf{a}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be linearly independent with
1<m\in[n]
. Then the
(m-1)
-vectors in set

\Gamma_{m-1}:=\left\{\textbf{a}^{i_{1}}\wedge\textbf{a}^{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{a}^{i_{m-1}}\colon\left\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m-1}\right\}\subset[m].\right\}

(3.12)

are linearly independent.

Proof.

The result is obvious for
m=1,2
, and it is also true for
m=3
by Lemma
2.12
. Now we assume that
m>3
(
m\leq n
). Denote

\alpha^{(k)}=\bigwedge\limits_{j\neq k,j=1}^{m}\textbf{a}^{j},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,m.

(3.13)

Then
\Gamma_{m-1}=\left\{\alpha^{(1)},\alpha^{(2)},\cdots,\alpha^{(m)}\right\}
. Suppose there are some
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, not all zeros, such that

\lambda_{1}\alpha^{(1)}+\lambda_{2}\alpha^{(2)}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}\alpha^{(m)}=0.

(3.14)

We may assume that the last nonzero scalar in
\left\{\lambda_{k}\right\}
is
\lambda_{m^{\prime}}
with

, i.e.,

0\neq\lambda_{m^{\prime}}
, and
\lambda_{m^{\prime}+1}=\lambda_{m^{\prime}+2}=\cdots=\lambda_{m}=0
(if

\lambda_{m}\neq 0

Then (
3.14

\alpha^{(m)}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\alpha^{(k)},\quad\lambda^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{m}},k\in[m-1].

(3.15)

By (

\alpha^{(m)}\wedge\textbf{a}^{m}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(\alpha^{k}\wedge\textbf{a}^{m})=0.

On the other hand, we have

\alpha^{(m)}\wedge\textbf{a}^{m}=\textbf{a}^{1}\wedge\textbf{a}^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{a}^{m}

Thus we have

To state our last result, we recall that a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
generated by a set of points
X:=\left\{\textbf{x}^{1},\textbf{x}^{2},\cdots,\textbf{x}^{m}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined

W=\texttt{Conv}(X):=\left\{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\textbf{x}^{j}\colon\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=1,\lambda^{j}\geq 0\ \forall j\in[m].\right\}

can also be represented as the bounded solution set of a finite system of linear inequalities, i.e.,

W=W(A,b):=\left\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:A^{\top}\textbf{x}\leq\textbf{b}\right\}

(3.17)

Here
A=[\textbf{a}^{1},\textbf{a}^{2},\cdots,\textbf{a}^{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m},\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We end the paper by the following theorem in which the Grassmann tensor is used to describe a polytope in geometry.

Theorem 3.20

Let
\hat{W}

(\textbf{a}^{i_{1}}\wedge\textbf{a}^{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{a}^{i_{r}})\textbf{x}=0

\left\{\textbf{a}^{i_{1}},\textbf{a}^{i_{2}},\cdots,\textbf{a}^{i_{r}}\right\}

X=\left\{\textbf{a}^{1},\textbf{a}^{2},\ldots,\textbf{a}^{m}\right\}
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that

since otherwise we can replace
A

whose column vectors form a basis
of
X:=\left\{\textbf{a}^{1},\textbf{a}^{2},\cdots,\textbf{a}^{m}\right\}

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{P}\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\textbf{a}^{1}\wedge\textbf{a}^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{a}^{m})\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{m-k}(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{a}^{k})\alpha^{k}

Since

is a set of linearly independent vectors,
