An algebraic framework for geometrically continuous splines
Angelos Mantzaflaris, Bernard Mourrain, Nelly Villamizar, Beihui Yuan

Abstract
Geometrically continuous splines are piecewise polynomial functions defined on a collection of patches which are stitched together through transition maps. They are called G r G^{r} -splines if, after composition with the transition maps, they are continuously differentiable functions to order r r on each pair of patches with stitched boundaries. This type of splines has been used to represent smooth shapes with complex topology for which (parametric) spline functions on fixed partitions are not sufficient. In this article, we develop new algebraic tools to analyze G r G^{r} -spline spaces. We define G r G^{r} -domains and transition maps using an algebraic approach, and establish an algebraic criterion to determine whether a piecewise function is G r G^{r} -continuous on the given domain. In the proposed framework, we construct a chain complex whose top homology is isomorphic to the G r G^{r} -spline space. This complex generalizes Billera-Schenck-Stillman homological complex used to study parametric splines. Additionally, we show how previous constructions of G r G^{r} -splines fit into this new algebraic framework, and present an algorithm to construct a bases for G r G^{r} -spline spaces. We illustrate how our algebraic approach works with concrete examples, and prove a dimension formula for the G r G^{r} -spline space in terms of invariants to the chain complex. In some special cases, explicit dimension formulas in terms of the degree of splines are also given.

Introduction

Spline functions are mathematical representations built upon simpler pieces (usually defined by low-degree polynomials)
which are glued together forming a smooth curve, surface or volume.
Splines constitute an appealing tool for shape representation not only for the simplicity of their construction
[
23
,
50
]
, but because they are a fundamental component of the approximation of solutions of partial differential equations by the finite element method
[
62
]
.
They play a central role in geometric modeling
[

, in approximation theory
[
38
]
, and in novel fields such as isogeometric analysis
[

,

Splines have been traditionally studied within the realm of numerical analysis and computational mathematics
[
18
,
38
]
.
They are defined as piecewise polynomial functions defined on a partition of a real domain which are continuously differentiable up to some order
r\geqslant 0
on the whole domain.
These splines are called
C^{r}
-splines, or
parametric

C^{r}
-continuous splines.
If the polynomial degree is bounded then the set of
C^{r}
-splines on a given domain is a finite dimensional real vector space.
In the areas where splines are applied, it is important to be able to construct a basis, often with prescribed properties, for the space of
C^{r}
-splines on a given partitioned domain and fixed maximal polynomial degree.
A more basic task which aids in the construction of a basis is simply to compute the dimension of the spline space.
A formula for the dimension of

-spline spaces defined on triangulations of planar domains was first proposed by Strang
[
61
]

and proved for polynomial degree

vertex positions by Billera in
[
8

.
The seminal work in
[
8
]
unveiled fascinating connections between the study of splines and homological algebra techniques,
putting spline theory at the interface between commutative algebra, geometric modelling, and numerical analysis.
The problem of computing the dimension of spline spaces on planar triangulations has received considerable attention using a wide variety of techniques.
Early works using Bernstein-Bézier methods from approximation theory include
[

,

,

Some extensions of the algebraic approach introduced in

One important feature of

The main contribution in this paper is to develop an algebraic framework to study spline functions which are defined on a collection of patches instead of fixed partitions of real domains.
The collection of patches are
stitched
together through transition maps, and the continuity conditions are imposed after composition of the piecewise functions with the transition maps.
The splines defined on this collection of patches are called
geometric continuous splines
or
G
-splines

If

is an integer, the set of

-splines which (after the composition with the transition maps) are continuously differentiable functions to order

Geometric continuity
is essential for Computer-Aided
Design (CAD), where typically a sophisticated design is obtained as a
smooth surface described by many patches joint together.
Indeed, the concept

[

Parametric continuity (of first order) is instead the condition
that the parametrization of the patches matches with continuity
of the first order derivatives in parametric
coordinates (corresponding to identity transition maps in our

Since parametric continuity is not a necessary nor a
sufficient condition for the smoothness of the represented

emerged as a tool for flexible representations of CAD models.
Geometrically,

-splines are defined by the property that
the tangent planes of each patch match at that interface.
These conditions involve the derivatives of the parametric coordinates
composed with the transition map assigned to each interface.

The concept of geometric continuity builds on the theory on differential manifolds.
The first general definition of splines based
on transition maps or reparametrizations was presented in

Research on geometrically continuous splines has been revived during the last decade due to its importance for solving partial differential equations by means of isogeometric analysis, see for instance

In particular, smooth splines are used for the discretization of partial differential equations and yield outstanding benefits compared to piecewise

In this article, we provide an
algebraic framework for geometrically continuous
G^{r}
-splines for any order of continuity

.
This method extends the results on
G^{1}
-splines in
[

[

We begin with a characterization of the domains over which the piecewise polynomials are defined.
We define them as a couple
(\Delta,\Phi)
and call them
G^{r}
-domains
.
They are composed by a cell complex
\Delta
with a coordinate system associated to each maximal face, and a collection
\Phi
of
algebraic transition maps
between adjacent patches. Unlike transition maps in differential geometry, which are diffeomorphisms between open sets, an algebraic transition map is a homomorphism between quotients of polynomial rings. It is a “stitch" rather than “gluing".
We define
G^{r}
-splines as geometrically continuous piecewise polynomial functions on
G^{r}
-domains.

This algebraic setting for
G^{r}
-splines has several benefits.
On one hand, it allows the use of algebraic tools to study the dimension and to construct a basis for
G^{r}
-spline spaces.
Namely, we construct a chain complex of vector spaces whose top homology is isomorphic to the
G^{r}
-spline space.
This chain complex allows us to analyze the dimension of
G^{r}
-spline spaces, as we illustrate for some special cases in Section
6
.
More importantly, the algebraic framework enables us to present an algorithm (Algorithm
1
) to construct a basis for
G^{r}
-spline spaces over any given
G^{r}
-domain.
Algorithm
1
is insensitive to the shape of patches in the sense that it works as well on other shapes as it does on triangles and rectangular patches, which makes the choice of domain very flexible.
On the other hand, we do not lose the perspective gained from the framework in
[
44
]
.
More precisely, we prove in Theorem
3.6
that if a
G^{1}
-domain is obtained from a topological surface, then the
G^{1}
-spline space in this paper and the space of differentiable functions on the topological space in
[
44
]
are the same.
Hence, the dimension formulas obtained in
[
44
]
still apply in the new context.
As a result,
G^{r}
-splines can be defined and studied in s pure algebraic setting.
In particular, the criterion for a function to be
G^{r}
-continuous is algebraic.
We formulate concrete examples of algebraic transition maps.
As a byproduct of exploring the relation between the concept of geometrically continuous splines in different frameworks, we establish a way of obtaining transition maps from certain kinds of differential manifolds.

2.1
The

Recall that a cell complex

If

Notice that

If

is the ideal of all polynomials in

In the case,

In particular, if the point

Note that we do not require any face

If

Definition 2.3

Let

(

In this section we assume that

By an abuse of notation, we denote the

-faces of
\Delta
relative to its boundary by
(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{n}=\Delta_{n}
and
(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}=\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
.
We call the faces in
\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
the
interior

k
-faces of
\Delta
.
Recall that to each
n
-face
\sigma_{i}\in\Delta_{n}
, we associate a system of coordinates
X(\sigma_{i})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
and denote by
\ambRing(\sigma_{i})
the polynomial ring in the coordinates

(u_{i1},\dots,u_{in})
of
X(\sigma_{i})
.
In the following we extend this construction and associate a ring to each face of the polyhedral complex
\Delta
.

If
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, and
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
is a
k
-face, we take
\ambRing(\alpha)
as the tensor product

\ambRing(\alpha)=\bigotimes_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\supseteq\alpha\\
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}\end{subarray}}\ambRing(\sigma).

(2)

For simplicity, we write
\ambRing(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})=\ambRing(\sigma_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes\ambRing(\sigma_{m})
, for
n
-faces
\sigma_{i}\in\Delta_{n}
.

Definition 2.8
.

If
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{n}
are two adjacent
n
-faces such that
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}=\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}
and
\phi_{12}\in\Phi
is a algebraic transition map from
\sigma_{1}
to
\sigma_{2}
, we define
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)\subseteq\ambRing(\tau)
as the ideal

\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\langle u_{1i}-\widetilde{\phi}_{12}(u_{1i})\bigr\rangle+\left(I_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)^{r+1}\cdot\ambRing(\tau)+I_{\sigma_{2}}(\tau)^{r+1}\cdot\ambRing(\tau)\right),

(3)

where
\widetilde{\phi}_{12}
is a lift of
\phi_{12}
in (
1
).
If
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-2
, we define

\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\supseteq\alpha\\
\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)\cdot\ambRing(\alpha).

(4)

For
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, and
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
, we put

\mathcal{F}^{r}(\alpha)=\ambRing(\alpha)/\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha).

(5)

For
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}
, put
\mathcal{F}^{r}(\sigma)=\ambRing(\sigma)
.

Remark 4
.

Notice that for an
(n-1)
-face
\tau=\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{n-1}
, the ideal
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
in Definition

does not depend on the choice of the lift of the algebraic transition map
\phi_{12}
.

For each pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Delta_{k}\times\Delta_{k-1}
such that
\beta\subseteq\alpha
, we have the inclusion

\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}\colon\ambRing(\alpha)\to\ambRing(\beta)\,,

(6)

for every
k=1,\dots,n
.
Suppose the
k
-th boundary map of the chain complex
\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}(\Delta,\partial\Delta)
is given by

\partial_{k}=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}\times(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k-1}}a_{\alpha,\beta}e_{\beta}\otimes e_{\alpha}^{*},

(7)

where
\{e_{\alpha}\}
and
\{e_{\beta}\}
are generators of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\Delta,\partial\Delta)
and
\mathcal{C}_{k-1}(\Delta,\partial\Delta)
, respectively, and
a_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We denote by
\delta_{k}
the
\mathbb{R}
-linear map

\delta_{k}\colon\bigoplus_{\alpha\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}}\ambRing(\alpha)\to\bigoplus_{\beta\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k-1}}\ambRing(\beta)

(8)

defined as

\delta_{k}=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}\times(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k-1}}a_{\alpha,\beta}\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}e_{\beta}\otimes e_{\alpha}^{*}.

We first check that
(\ambRing_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
is a chain complex.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\Delta
is an
n
-dimensional polyhedral complex, and
\delta_{k}
is the linear map in (
8
), then

\delta_{k+1}\circ\delta_{k+2}=0,

for
k=0,\dots,n-2
, and hence
(\ambRing_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
is a chain complex of
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces.

Proof.

Note that for faces
\gamma\in\Delta_{k}
and
\alpha\in\Delta_{k+2}
, if there are two faces
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{k+1}
such that
\gamma\subseteq\beta\subseteq\alpha
and
\gamma\subseteq\beta^{\prime}\subseteq\alpha
, then

\rho^{\beta}_{\gamma}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\rho^{\beta^{\prime}}_{\gamma}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta^{\prime}},

for the homomorphisms in (
6
).
Furthermore, for any
k=0,\dots,n-2
we have

\partial_{k+1}\circ\partial_{k+2}=0.

Equivalently, this means if
\partial_{k+2}=\sum a_{\alpha,\beta}e_{\alpha}\otimes e_{\beta}^{*}
and
\partial_{k+1}=\sum b_{\beta,\lambda}e_{\beta}\otimes e_{\lambda}^{*}
, then for faces
\alpha
and
\lambda
, such that
\gamma\subseteq\alpha
we have

\sum_{\alpha\subseteq\beta\subseteq\gamma}a_{\alpha,\beta}b_{\beta,\lambda}=0.

Therefore,

\bigl(\delta_{k+1}\circ\delta_{k+2}\bigr)_{\alpha,\lambda}=\sum_{\alpha\subseteq\beta\subseteq\gamma}a_{\alpha,\beta}b_{\beta,\lambda}\rho^{\beta}_{\gamma}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\Biggl(\sum_{\alpha\subseteq\beta\subseteq\gamma}a_{\alpha,\beta}b_{\beta,\lambda}\Biggr)\cdot\rho^{\beta^{\prime}}_{\gamma}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta^{\prime}}=0\,,

where
\beta^{\prime}
is a
(k+1)
-face between
\alpha
and
\lambda
.
Since

\delta_{k+1}\circ\delta_{k+2}=\sum_{(\alpha,\lambda)\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k+2}\times(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}}(\delta_{k+1}\circ\delta_{k+2})_{\alpha,\lambda}e_{\alpha}\otimes e_{\lambda}^{*},

then
\delta_{k+1}\circ\delta_{k+2}=0
.
∎

Note that the restriction of
\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}
in (
6
) to
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha)
gives us a map
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha)\to\mathcal{I}^{r}(\beta)
. Therefore,
\delta_{k}
restricts to
\oplus_{\alpha\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}}\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha)\to\oplus_{\beta\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k-1}}\mathcal{I}^{r}(\beta)
and it induces a natural map
\oplus_{\alpha\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}}\mathcal{F}^{r}(\alpha)\to\oplus_{\beta\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k-1}}\mathcal{F}^{r}(\beta)
.
Thus, Lemma
2.9
implies that both
(\mathcal{I}^{r}_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
and
(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
are chain complexes, and we have the following immediate consequence of our construction.

Theorem 2.10
.

The space
G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
of
G^{r}
-splines on a
G^{r}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
is the top homology
H_{n}(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{\bullet})
of the chain complex
(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
.

Proof.

By definition, a tuple of polynomials
(f_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}\in G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
if

\phi_{ij}(\bar{f}_{\sigma_{i}})=\bar{f}_{\sigma_{j}},

(9)

for each pair of adjacent
n
-faces
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}\in\Delta_{n}
.
Let
\tau=\sigma_{i}\cap\sigma_{j}\in\Delta_{n-1}
.
We only need to show that (
9
) holds if and only if

f_{\sigma_{i}}-f_{\sigma_{j}}\in\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau).

We know that

\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(g_{i})=g_{j}~\Leftrightarrow~g_{i}-g_{j}\in\left\langle u_{ik}-\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(u_{ik})\colon k=1,\dots,n\right\rangle,

(10)

for any pair of polynomials
(g_{i},g_{j})\in\ambRing(\sigma_{i})\times\ambRing(\sigma_{j})
, where
\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}
is a lifting of
\phi_{ij}
.
For
l=i,j
, denote by
\bar{f}_{\sigma_{l}}
be the quotient image of
f_{\sigma_{l}}
in
\ambRing(\sigma_{l})/I_{\sigma_{l}}(\tau)^{r+1}
. If (
9
) holds, then there exists
\widetilde{f}_{i}
and
\widetilde{f}_{j}
which are liftings of
\bar{f}_{\sigma_{i}}
and
\bar{f}_{\sigma_{j}}
, respectively, such that
\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(\widetilde{f}_{i})=\widetilde{f}_{j}
. Hence, (
10
) implies
\widetilde{f}_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{j}\in\left\langle u_{ik}-\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(u_{ik})\colon k=1,\dots,n\right\rangle
.
Since
\widetilde{f}_{i}
is a lifting of
\bar{f}_{\sigma_{i}}
, then
\widetilde{f}_{i}-f_{\sigma_{i}}\in I_{\sigma_{i}}(\tau)^{r+1}
. The same applies for
\widetilde{f}_{j}
and
f_{\sigma_{j}}
, hence
\widetilde{f}_{j}-f_{\sigma_{j}}\in I_{\sigma_{j}}(\tau)^{r+1}
.
Putting these together, we have
f_{\sigma_{i}}-f_{\sigma_{j}}\in\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
.

Conversely, if
f_{\sigma_{i}}-f_{\sigma_{j}}\in\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
, then there exist
\widetilde{f}_{i}
, and
\widetilde{f}_{j}
such that

f_{\sigma_{i}}-\widetilde{f}_{i}\in I_{\sigma_{i}}(\tau)^{r+1}
,
f_{\sigma_{j}}-\widetilde{f}_{j}\in I_{\sigma_{j}}(\tau)^{r+1}
, and
\widetilde{f}_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{j}\in\left\langle u_{ik}-\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(u_{ik})\colon k=1,\dots,n\right\rangle
. Again, by (
10
), we have
\widetilde{\phi}_{ij}(\widetilde{f}_{i})=\widetilde{f}_{j}
, which implies (
9
).
∎

The chain complex
(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
specializes to the Billera-Schenck-Stillman spline chain complex when all transition maps are identities.
This is a technical result which for completeness we have included in
A
.

2.3
Chain complex for
G^{r}
-splines of bounded polynomial degree

In the following we consider the subspace of
G^{r}
-splines over a given
G^{r}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
which are of degree less than or equal to a fixed
d\geqslant 0
.
For each
d
, we associate a chain complex to the subspace of
G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
of splines of degree at most
d
and relate it to the chain complex
(\mathcal{F}^{r}_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
defined in Section
2.2
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
d\geqslant 0
be an integer.
The set of
G^{r}
-spline of degree at most
d
over a
G^{r}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
, denoted

G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
, is the subset of splines
f\in G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
whose restriction to each
n
-face is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to
d
. Namely,

G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)=\bigl\{f\in G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)\colon\deg(f_{\sigma})\leqslant d~\mbox{for every}~\sigma\in\Delta_{n}\bigr\},

where
f=(f_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}
, and
f|_{\sigma}=f_{\sigma}
.

It is clear that
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
is a finite dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-vector space.
To find the dimension of this vector space, we construct a chain complex of finite dimensional vector spaces as follows.

If
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}
, denote by
\ambRing_{d}(\sigma)=\ambRing(\sigma)_{\leqslant d}
the vector space of polynomials of degree at most
d
in the coordinates
X(\sigma)\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For a
k
-face
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, let

\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)=\Biggl\{\sum_{\alpha\subseteq\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}f_{\sigma}\colon f_{\sigma}\in\ambRing_{d}(\sigma)\Biggr\}

(11)

be the image of the inclusion

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\ambRing_{d}(\sigma_{i})\xhookrightarrow{\phantom{in}}\ambRing(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{s})=\ambRing(\alpha),

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{s}\in\Delta_{n}
are the
n
-faces in
\Delta
containing
\alpha
.
Notice that the set
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)
is a subspace of the tensor product polynomial ring
\ambRing(\alpha)
defined in (
2
).
The polynomials in
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)
do not contain mixed terms i.e., each term is a monomial in one of the rings
\ambRing(\sigma)
for some
n
-face
\sigma\supseteq\alpha
.
If
\sigma
is an
n
-face, then
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\sigma)=\ambRing_{d}(\sigma)
.

Additionally, for each
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, we define

\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)\cap\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha),

(12)

and

\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)/\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d}(\alpha),

(13)

where
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\alpha)
is the ideal defined in (
3
) and (
4
) for
k=n-1
and
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-2
, respectively. If
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}
, we take
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d}(\sigma)=\mathcal{T}_{d}(\sigma)
.

Since the differential map
\delta
in the chain complex
\bigl(\ambRing_{\bullet},\delta\bigr)
restricts to

\delta_{k}\colon\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\alpha)\to\bigoplus_{\beta\in\Delta_{k-1}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\beta),

then
(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet},\delta)
is a chain complex, and hence so are
(\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet},\delta)
and
(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet},\delta)
.

As a corollary of Theorem
2.10
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\bigl(\Delta,\Phi\bigr)
be a
G^{r}
-domain and
d\geqslant 0
an integer, then

G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)=H_{n}\bigl(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{{d},\bullet}\bigr),

(14)

where
H_{n}\bigl(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{{d},\bullet}\bigr)
is the top homology of the chain complex
\bigl(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet},\delta\bigr)
.

Proof.

By definition,
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)=G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)\cap\oplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\sigma)
. By Theorem
2.10
,
G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
is the kernel of
\delta_{n}:\oplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}\ambRing(\sigma)\to\oplus_{\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}^{\circ}}\mathcal{F}^{r}(\tau)
. Hence,
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
is the kernel of
\delta_{n}
restricted to
\oplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\sigma)
, which is exactly
H_{n}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
.
∎

With Proposition
2.12
, we estimate
\dim G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
by comparing it to the
Euler characteristic

\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
of
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
, which is defined as

\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\cdot\sum_{\alpha\in(\Delta,\partial\Delta)_{k}}\dim\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d}(\alpha).

The dimension of
G^{r}
-spline spaces and Euler characteristic
\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
are related by the following corollary:

Corollary 2.13
.

For any integer
d\geqslant 0
, and any
G^{r}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
, we have

\dim G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)=(-1)^{n}\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n+k+1}\dim H_{k}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}).

(15)

Proof.

For any chain complex
C_{\bullet}
of finite dimensional vector spaces such that
C_{k}=0
if
k>n
or
k<0
, we have

\chi(C_{\bullet})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\dim C_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\dim H_{k}(C_{\bullet}).

In particular, this holds for
C_{\bullet}=\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
. Hence, (
14
) implies (
15
).
∎

Specializing to
n=2
cases, the construction and results in this section can easily be adapted to the bidegree case.
More precisely, if
\Delta
is a two dimensional complex, and
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
then a polynomial

is of bidegree

if
\deg f(u,1)
, and
\deg f(1,v)
are

.
We write
\ambRing_{(d,d)}(\sigma)
for the set of polynomials in

The space of bidegree

where
f=(f_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}}
, and

.

Similarly, we can adapt (
11
), (
12
), and (
13
) and define
\mathcal{T}_{(d,d)}(\alpha)
,
\mathcal{J}^{r}_{(d,d)}(\alpha)
, and
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{(d,d)}(\alpha)
, respectively, for
\alpha\in\Delta_{k}
for any

.
We refer to

,
\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}
,
\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet}

.

2.4
Constructing a basis for geometrically continuous spline spaces

This section is devoted to develop a general method to generate certain subspace of a quotient ring. In particular, it gives an algorithm to construct a basis for a
G^{r}
-spline space.
The main result in this section is Lemma
2.15
, which describes the method on generating certain subspaces of a quotient ring.
The algorithm for the
G^{r}
-spline basis will be an immediate consequence of this result.
Later in Section
5
, Lemma
2.15
will also play an important role for computing the dimension of the terms in (
15
).
Lemma
2.15
does not depend on our setting so we state it in general for a finitely generated real polynomial ring.
We introduce the following notation that will be helpful to define the subspaces of the quotient ring we want to describe.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
f\in S
be polynomial in a finitely generated polynomial ring
S
over
\mathbb{R}
, and let
I\subseteq S
be an ideal.
We define the
remainder of
f
with respect to
I
to be the remainder of
f
by performing the division algorithm to
f
with respect to the reduced Gröbner basis of
I
for a fixed monomial order
\succ
on
S
.
We denote this by
\mathfrak{r}(f,I)
, or simply
\mathfrak{r}(f)
if
I
is clear from the context.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
S
be a finitely generated polynomial ring over
\mathbb{R}
, with a fixed monomial order
\succ
, and let
W\subseteq S
is a finite dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-vector space with basis
\mathcal{B}
.
Assume
I\subseteq S
is an ideal.
Then, the linear span
\spanset\{\mathfrak{r}(b)\colon b\in\mathcal{B}\}\cong W/(W\cap I)
, where
\mathfrak{r}(b)
is the remainder of
b\in\mathcal{B}
with respect to
I
.

Proof.

Let
V=\spanset\{\mathfrak{r}(b)\colon b\in\mathcal{B}\}\subseteq S
.
Then we can define a map
\mathfrak{r}\colon W\to V
given by
f\mapsto\mathfrak{r}(f)
.
If
W\to W/(W\cap I)
is the quotient map
f\mapsto\overline{f}
, then there exists a map
V\to W/(W\cap I)
for which the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W/(W\cap I)\,.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{r}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

Indeed, for any
g\in V
, we may take
g^{\prime}\in W
such that
\mathfrak{r}(g^{\prime})=g
and define
h(g)=\overline{g^{\prime}}
.
This map is well-defined, because if
\mathfrak{r}(g^{\prime}_{1})=\mathfrak{r}(g^{\prime}_{2})=g
for
g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime}\in W
, then
g^{\prime}_{1}-g^{\prime}_{2}\in I
, which implies that
\overline{g^{\prime}_{1}}=\overline{g^{\prime}_{2}}
.
It is easy to verify this map is linear, injective and surjective.
Hence,
h
is an isomorphism of vector spaces.
∎

By Proposition
2.12
we know that
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
is the kernel of the map
\delta_{n}\colon\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,n-1}
, where

\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,k}
is the
k
-th terms in the chain complex
(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
.
Recall that by definition we take
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,n}=\mathcal{T}_{d,n}
, see (
11
) and (
13
).
By construction, for each
\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}^{\circ}
, the set
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\tau)
is a linear subspace of the ring
\ambRing_{d}(\tau)
, and
I=\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
is an ideal of
\ambRing_{d}(\tau)
. Hence, Lemma
2.15
implies
\mathfrak{r}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{d}(\tau)\bigr)\cong\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d}(\tau)
.

This allows to see
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
as the kernel of the map
\mathfrak{r}\circ\delta_{n}\colon\mathcal{T}_{d,n}\to\mathfrak{r}(\mathcal{T}_{d,n-1})
, where (by an abuse of notation) we write
\delta_{n}\colon\mathcal{T}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{T}_{d,n-1}
for the restriction of the differential map in
(\ambRing_{\bullet},\delta_{\bullet})
to
\mathcal{T}_{d,n}
.

We start the algorithm to compute a basis of
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
by fixing a maximal polynomial degree
d
.
Taking
k=n
in (
7
), the boundary map
\partial_{n}

can be written as a rectangular matrix
\left(a_{\sigma,\tau}\right)_{\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}^{\circ}\;\sigma\in\Delta_{n}}
, whose rows are labeled by the interior
(n-1)
-faces
and the columns by the maximal
n
-faces of
\Delta
.
We compute the reduced Gröbner basis of the ideal
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
, for each
\tau\in\Delta_{n-1}^{\circ}
.
Then, for every
n
-face
\sigma\supseteq\tau
, if
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
is the monomial basis of
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\sigma)
, we use the division algorithm to find the remainder of each polynomial in
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
with respect to the Gröbner basis of
\mathcal{I}^{r}(\tau)
.
By Lemma
2.15
, we know that the collection of those remainders generate
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,n-1}
.
Next we construct a matrix
A
which will encode the map
\delta_{n}\colon\mathcal{T}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,n-1}
,
Explicitly, if

3
Topological surfaces and
G^{r}
-domains

The notion of geometric continuity was originally defined by differential-geometric methods, see for example
[
30
]
and
[
48
]
.
In
[
44
]
, the space of
G^{1}
-spline functions is defined over a collection of 2-dimensional patches glued together by transition maps.
In this section, we explain how the notion of
G^{1}
-geometric continuity in our framework and that in
[
44
]
are related.

First, recall from
[
44
, Definition 2.1]
, a
topological surface

\mathcal{M}
is a collection
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of polygons together with a set of homeomorphisms between pairs of polygonal edges with the condition that each polygonal edge can be paired with at most one other edge, and it cannot be glued with itself.
Each polygon is assumed to be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
By an abuse of notation, two polygons are said to share an edge if there is such a homeomorphism between two edges one in each of the two polygons.
From this, it is clear that the collection of polygons and homeomorphisms between pairs of polygonal edges of a topological surface
\mathcal{M}
define a
2
-dimensional cell complex
\Delta
such that the set of 2-faces
\Delta_{2}=\mathcal{M}_{2}
.
The coordinates
X(\sigma)
associated to each polygon
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
are given by the embedding of
\sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
As in Section
2.1
, we denote the corresponding homeomorphism by
\psi_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\rightarrow X(\sigma)
.
Notice that these embeddings of the faces
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
as polygons in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
mean that every edge or
\sigma
is locally algebraic (see Definition
2.2
).

Suppose
\Delta
is such a 2-dimensional cell complex obtained from a topological surface
\mathcal{M}
, with coordinates
\bigl\{X(\sigma)\colon\sigma\in\Delta_{2}\bigr\}
.
The (geometric) transition maps associated to pairs of polygons in
\mathcal{M}
as introduced in
[
44
, Definition 2.2]
can be interpreted in our setting as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Geometric transition maps)
.

Let
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
two faces in
\Delta_{2}
, such that
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}=\tau\in\Delta_{1}
.
A
(geometric) transition map
associated to
\tau
consists of the following:

\blacksquare

for each face
\sigma_{i}\in\Delta_{2}
containing
\tau
, an open set
U_{\tau,\sigma_{i}}\subseteq X(\sigma_{i})
containing
\psi_{\sigma_{i}}(\tau)
;

\blacksquare

a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism between the open sets

\varphi_{21}\colon U_{\tau,\sigma_{1}}\to U_{\tau,\sigma_{2}}

(16)

such that

\varphi_{21}|_{\psi_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)}=\psi_{\sigma_{2}}\circ\psi_{\sigma_{1}}^{-1}|_{\psi_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)}
.

Remark 5
.

The map
\varphi_{21}
is called transition map in
[
44
]
, we called it here geometric transition map to distinguish it from the algebraic transition maps from Definition
2.3
.
Notice that the notion of geometric transition maps can be generalized to an
n
-dimensional cell complex
\Delta
, for this we can simply replace
\Delta_{2}
with
\Delta_{n}
, and
\Delta_{1}
with
\Delta_{n-1}
in Definition
3.1
.

Our aim is to obtain a
G^{r}
-domain from a given topological surface and a given collection of geometric transition maps. However, a geometric transition map associated to an edge
\tau
, which is
C^{r}
-continuous along
\tau
, is not necessarily polynomial and therefore it does not necessarily corresponds to an algebraic transition map.
To describe the type of geometric transition maps from which we are able to obtain algebraic ones, we introduce the property of being
almost algebraic
.
We need some preparations before we can state such a notion in Definition
3.3
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, and take
p\in U
.
If
\bm{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is a multiindex, henceforth we follow the convention that
\bm{x}^{\bm{j}}=x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}
and
|\bm{j}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}
.

Recall that if
s\leqslant r
, then the
s
-th order
jet

J^{s}_{p}(f)
of a function
f
which is
C^{r}
-continuous at the point
p
is defined as the truncated Taylor expansion of
f
at
p
of degree
s
. Explicitly, if the coordinates of
p
are
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\bm{a}
, then

\begin{split}J^{s}_{p}\colon C^{r}_{p}&\to{\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]}/{I(p)^{s+1}}\\
f&\mapsto\sum_{|\bm{j}|\leqslant s}\frac{\partial^{\bm{j}}f}{\partial\bm{x}^{\bm{j}}}(p)(\bm{x}-\bm{a})^{\bm{j}},\end{split}

(17)

where
C^{s}_{p}
is the set of all functions that are
C^{s}
-continuous at
p
, and

I(p)=\bigl\langle x_{1}-a_{1},\dots,x_{n}-a_{n}\bigr\rangle

(18)

is the ideal of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
generated by the polynomials
x_{i}-a_{i}
.

Denote by
C^{1}(U)
the set of all functions
f\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which are continuously differentiable functions on
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 3.2
.

If
f\in C^{1}(U)
, we say that
f

vanishes to order
s
at a point

if
f
is
C^{s}
-continuous at
p
and

J^{s}_{p}(f)=0.

If
S\subseteq U
, then we say
f

vanishes to order
s
on

if
f
vanishes to order
s
at
p
for each
p\in S
.

Since a geometric transition map
\varphi_{{21}}
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism, then it induces a homomorphism

\displaystyle\varphi_{{21}}^{*}\colon C^{1}(U_{\tau,\sigma_{1}})

\displaystyle\to C^{1}(U_{\tau,\sigma_{2}})

(19)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f\circ\varphi_{{21}}.

Definition 3.3

(Almost algebraic property)
.

Let
\varphi_{{21}}
be a geometric transition map across
\tau
as introduced in (
16
).
We say
\varphi_{{21}}
is
almost algebraic
(up to order

) if there exist a homomorphism

\phi_{21}\colon\ambRing(\sigma_{2})/I_{\sigma_{2}}(\tau)^{r+1}\to\ambRing(\sigma_{1})/I_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)^{r+1}

(20)

such that
\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\varphi^{*}_{{21}}\bigr)(f)
vanishes to order
r
along
\psi_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)
, for any

, where
\varphi^{*}_{21}
is the homomorphism in (
19
), and
\widetilde{\phi}_{21}\colon\ambRing(\sigma_{2})\to\ambRing(\sigma_{1})
is a lift of
\phi_{21}
. If this is the case, we call
\phi_{21}
the
G^{r}
-algebraic transition map associated to
\varphi_{21}
.

It is clear that Definition
3.3
does not depend on the choice of the lift
\widetilde{\phi}_{21}
. In Lemma

we prove that if there exists a

-algebraic transition map associated to an geometric transition map

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\tau=\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{1}^{\circ}
, for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{2}
. If

is an almost algebraic transition map up to order

, then the

-algebraic transition map

associated to

Proof.

Suppose both
\phi_{21}
and
\phi_{21}^{\prime}
are algebraic transition maps induced by
\varphi_{{21}}
.
If

, then

\displaystyle\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}\bigr)(f)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\varphi_{{21}}^{*}+\varphi_{{21}}^{*}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}\bigr)(f)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\varphi_{{21}}^{*}\bigr)(f)+\bigl(\varphi_{{21}}^{*}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}\bigr)(f).

Both
\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\varphi_{{21}}^{*}\bigr)(f)
and
\bigl(\varphi_{{21}}^{*}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}\bigr)(f)
vanishes to order

along
\tau
.
Hence so does their sum.
Because
\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}
is algebraic, then
\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{21}-\widetilde{\phi}_{21}^{\prime}\bigr)(f)\in I_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)^{r+1}
.
This holds for any

, so this implies
\phi_{21}=\phi_{21}^{\prime}
.
∎

.
In this case, we say that

is
associated to

and the

-domain

Definition 3.5
.

Let

be a topological surface and

.

such that

where

, as defined in (

The set of all differentiable functions

for each

is denoted

For a topological surface

, let

be a collection of geometric transition maps.
The relation between the notation of geometric continuity is the following.

Theorem 3.6
.

If the
G^{1}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
is obtained from a topological surface
\mathcal{M}
with geometric transition maps

, then

G^{1}(\Delta,\Phi)=\mathcal{S}^{1}(\mathcal{M}),\text{ and \, }G^{1}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)=\mathcal{S}^{1}_{d}(\mathcal{M}).

In particular,

We prepare the proof of Proposition
3.6
with the following two lemmas. Similarly as before, we denote by
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\bm{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
a multiindex, where
\bm{x}^{\bm{j}}=x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}
and
|\bm{j}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f,\ell\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, with
\ell
irreducible.
Then the following two conditions on
f
are equivalent:

1.

the jet
J^{r}_{p}(f)=0
whenever
\ell(p)=0
,

2.

f\in\langle\ell^{r+1}\rangle
.

Proof.

Since the ideal
\langle\ell^{r+1}\rangle\subseteq I(p)^{s+1}
for all
s\leqslant r
whenever
\ell(p)=0
, where
I(p)
is the ideal in (
18
), then clearly
2
implies
1
.
It remains to prove the converse.
We prove this by induction on the order of smoothness
r
.
Note that condition
1
is equivalent to saying that
\partial^{\bm{j}}f/\partial\bm{x}^{\bm{j}}(p)=0
for all
|\bm{j}|\leqslant r
whenever
\ell(p)=0
, which in turn, is equivalent to saying that
\partial^{\bm{j}}f/\partial\bm{x}^{\bm{j}}\in\langle\ell\rangle
for all
|\bm{j}|\leqslant r
.

We first prove that for any
s\geqslant 1
, if
f,{\partial f}/{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,~{\partial f}/{\partial x_{n}}\in\langle\ell^{s}\rangle
, then
f\in\langle\ell^{s+1}\rangle
.
Indeed, if
f=g\ell^{s}
for some
g\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, then
{\partial f}/{\partial x_{j}}=\bigl({\partial g}/{\partial x_{i}}\bigr)\cdot\ell^{s}+g\cdot s\ell^{s-1}\bigl({\partial\ell}/{\partial x_{j}}\bigr)
for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Since
\ell
is non-constant, then there exists at least one variable
x_{j}
such that
{\partial\ell}/{\partial x_{j}}\neq 0
, so we have
{\partial\ell}/{\partial x_{j}}\not\in\langle\ell\rangle
.
Note that
\langle\ell\rangle
is a prime ideal since
\ell
is irreducible, so
\partial f/\partial x_{j}\in\langle\ell^{s}\rangle
implies
g\in\langle\ell\rangle
.
Hence
f=g\cdot\ell^{s}\in\langle\ell^{s+1}\rangle
.
If follows that for
r=1
, condition
1
implies
2
.

Now assume that
1
implies
2
for any
r=1,\dots,s
.
For
j=1,\dots,n
, put
h_{j}=\partial f/\partial x_{j}
.
Note that for any point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
J^{s+1}_{p}(f)=0
then
J^{s}_{p}(h_{j})=0
.
Hence by the induction hypothesis,
h_{j}\in\langle\ell^{s}\rangle
, for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Since
J^{s+1}_{p}(f)=0
implies
J^{s}_{p}(f)=0
, then applying
1
to
f
and

yields
f\in\langle\ell^{s}\rangle
.
Thus, we have
f,~{\partial f}/{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,~{\partial f}/{\partial x_{n}}\in\langle\ell^{s}\rangle
.
Therefore,
f\in\langle\ell^{s+1}\rangle
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\Delta
be a cell complex obtained from a topological surface
\mathcal{M}
, with

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{2}
two adjacent faces such that
\tau=\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
.
Assume the transition map
\varphi_{{21}}
across
\tau
is almost algebraic, with associated algebraic transition map
\phi_{12}
, as given in (

).
Then, for polynomials

and

, the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

J^{r}_{p}(f\circ\varphi_{{21}})=J^{r}_{p}(g)
for each
p\in\tau
;

2.

\phi_{12}(\bar{f})=\bar{g}
, where
\bar{f}
and
\bar{g}
are the classes of
f
and
g
in
\ambRing(\sigma_{1})/I_{\sigma_{1}}(\tau)^{r+1}
and
\ambRing(\sigma_{2})/I_{\sigma_{2}}(\tau)^{r+1}
, respectively.

Proof.

Let

\widetilde{\phi}_{12}\colon\ambRing(\sigma_{1})\to\ambRing(\sigma_{2})
be a lift of
\phi_{12}
.
Then
2
holds if and only if

\widetilde{\phi}_{12}(f)-g\in I_{\sigma_{2}}(\tau)^{r+1}.

(21)

Since the edge
\tau
is locally algebraic, then by Lemma
3.7
, a polynomial

vanishes along
\tau
up to order

if and only if

.
Therefore, equation (

) is equivalent to saying that
\widetilde{\phi}_{12}(f)-g
vanishes along
\tau
up to order

.
Because

, then (

J^{r}_{p}\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{12}(f)\bigr)=J^{r}_{p}(g),~\mbox{for each}~p\in\tau.

(22)

Since

J^{r}_{p}\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{12}(f)\bigr)=J^{r}_{p}\bigl(f\circ\varphi_{{21}}\bigr)
for any

, then (

) holds if and only if condition

holds.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem

.

4.2
G^{r}
-domains obtained from differential manifolds with cellulation

We denote by
M
a differential
n
-manifold with a given atlas.
A
cellulation
of
M
is given by a homeomorphism
\text{{cel}}\colon\Delta\to M
, such that
\Delta
is a cell complex.
Assume
\Delta
is such a cell complex, and suppose for each
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}
there is a unique chart
U(\sigma)
in the given atlas such that
\text{{cel}}(\sigma)\subseteq U(\sigma)
.
Then a system of coordinates
X(\sigma)
on each
\sigma\in\Delta_{n}
is determined by the atlas in a natural way, and for every
(n-1)
-face
\tau
of
\Delta
which is common to two
n
-faces, a geometric transition map across
\tau
is defined by the atlas.
We denote this collection of geometric transition maps by
\Phi_{G}
.
We assume that every such
(n-1)
-face
\tau
of
\Delta
is locally algebraic (i.e., the ideal
I_{\sigma}(\tau)
is generated by an irreducible polynomial for every
n
-face
\sigma\supseteq\tau
, see Definition
2.2
), and each map in
\Phi_{G}
is almost algebraic (see Definition
3.3
).
Then, from
\Phi_{G}
, we can construct a set of compatible algebraic transition maps
\Phi
which lead us to a
G^{r}
-domain over
\Delta
.
In this case, we simply say that
\Phi
is
obtained from the atlas
of the manifold
M
, and that the
G^{r}
-domain
(\Delta,\Phi)
is
obtained from the cellulation
of
M
.

In this section we give some examples in which we start with a differential manifold and construct
G^{r}
-domain.
In the first example, we consider an

i.e., a differential manifold admitting an atlas where all transition maps are affine
[
29
]
.

.

-dimensional simplicial complex given by

leads us to the long exact sequence

0\to H_{2}(\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{2}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{1}(\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})\to\\
H_{1}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{0}(\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})\to H_{0}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})\to 0.

(37)

Thus, we can study
\dim H_{1}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
and
\dim H_{0}(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
by analyzing the terms in the sequence (
37
).
We start with the homologies of
\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}
.

Remark 8
.

Notice that the commutativity of the diagram (
36
) and the exactness of the long sequence (
37
) hold if we replace the chain complexes
\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}
,
\mathcal{J}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
by their bidegree counterparts
\mathcal{T}_{(d,d),\bullet}
,
\mathcal{J}^{r}_{(d,d),\bullet}
, and
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{(d,d),\bullet}
, for any

.
To simplify the exposition, we only introduce the notation in terms of the total degree case
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
, but we apply the construction and give explicit formulas for each case
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
and
G^{r}_{(d,d)}(\Delta,\Phi)
in the results and examples below.

For an integer
k\geqslant 0
and a 2-face
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
, we denote by
\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}
the set of homogeneous polynomials in
\ambRing(\sigma)
of degree
k
.
We write
(\ambRing_{\bullet})_{k}
for the chain complex of the
k
-graded pieces of
\ambRing_{\bullet}
, and take
(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}\cap(\ambRing_{\bullet})_{k}
.
Using this notation we can rewrite
\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}=\bigoplus_{0\leqslant k\leqslant d}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\Delta
is a 2-dimensional polyhedral complex with only one connected component and the number of 2-faces of
\Delta
is strictly greater than

1
, then

H_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{0}=H_{i}\bigl(\Delta,\partial\Delta;\mathbb{R}\bigr),

for
i=0,1,2
.
Moreover, for any
k\geqslant 1
, we have

H_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}^{\prime}}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k},\quad H_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}\setminus\Delta_{2}^{\prime}}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}^{m(\sigma)-1},\,\text{and\hskip 10.22217pt}H_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=0,

where
\Delta^{\prime}=\bigl\{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}\colon\sigma\subseteq\Delta\setminus\partial\Delta\bigr\}
, and
m(\sigma)
is the number of connected components of
\partial\Delta\cap\sigma
for any
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
.
(We always assume that a 2-face
\sigma\in\Delta
contains its edges and vertices.)
In the bidegree case, the corresponding formulas for
H_{i}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{(d,d),\bullet}\bigr)_{k}
hold.

Proof.

We only prove the formulas in the total degree case, the bidegree case follows by a similar reasoning.
It is clear that

(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{0}\colon\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}}\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{\delta_{2}}\bigoplus_{\tau\in\Delta_{1}^{\circ}}\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{\delta_{1}}\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Delta_{0}^{\circ}}\mathbb{R},

and hence
H_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{0}=H_{i}(\Delta,\partial\Delta;\mathbb{R})
, for
i=0,1,2
.

If
k\geqslant 1
, then we can rewrite
\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}
as a direct sum
(\mathcal{T}_{d}(\tau))_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\supseteq\tau}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}
for every edge
\tau\in\Delta_{1}^{\circ}
, and
(\mathcal{T}_{d}(\gamma))_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\supseteq\gamma}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}
for every vertex
\gamma\in\Delta_{0}^{\circ}
.
Thus, if
(\delta_{i})_{k}
denotes the boundary map in the chain complex
\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet}\bigr)_{k}
then, we can rewrite it as the direct sum

(\delta_{i})_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}}\bigl(\delta_{i}(\sigma)\bigr)_{k},

where for each 2-face
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}
, the map
(\delta_{i}(\sigma))_{k}
is the boundary map in the chain complex
\ambRing_{\bullet}(\sigma)_{k}
given by

\ambRing_{\bullet}(\sigma)_{k}\colon\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}\xrightarrow{\delta_{2}(\sigma)_{k}}\bigoplus_{\tau\subseteq\sigma\;\tau\in\Delta_{1}^{\circ}}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}\xrightarrow{\delta_{1}(\sigma)_{k}}\bigoplus_{\gamma\subseteq\sigma,\;\gamma\in\Delta_{0}^{\circ}}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}.

Therefore, we can write the chain complex
(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}}\ambRing_{\bullet}(\sigma)_{k}
.
Since

\ambRing_{\bullet}(\sigma)_{k}=\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}\otimes C_{\bullet}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma),

where
C_{\bullet}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)
is the quotient complex
C_{\bullet}(\sigma)/C_{\bullet}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)
of the chain complexes of
\sigma
and
\partial\Delta\cap\sigma
, respectively.
In particular,
H_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Delta_{2}}\ambRing(\sigma)_{k}\otimes H_{i}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)
.
Thus, to compute
H_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}
, we first consider
H_{i}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)
.
Notice that the sequence
0\to C_{\bullet}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to C_{\bullet}(\sigma)\to C_{\bullet}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to 0
yields the long exact sequence of homologies

0\to H_{2}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to H_{2}(\sigma)\to H_{2}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to H_{1}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to\\
H_{1}(\sigma)\to H_{1}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to H_{0}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to H_{0}(\sigma)\to H_{0}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to 0\,.

By hypothesis
\sigma\neq\Delta
, so

\displaystyle H_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(\sigma)=0,

\displaystyle H_{0}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle H_{2}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=0,

\displaystyle H_{0}(\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}^{m(\sigma)}.

From this we see that
H_{2}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=0
, which implies
H_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}=0
.
Furthermore, we can rewrite the last part of the sequence as

0\to H_{1}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to\mathbb{Z}^{m(\sigma)}\to\mathbb{Z}\to H_{0}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)\to 0.

Note that
\sigma\in\Delta_{2}^{\prime}
if and only if
m(\sigma)=0
, in which case
H_{1}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=0
and
H_{0}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=\mathbb{Z}
.
On the other hand, if
\sigma\notin\Delta^{\prime}
, or equivalently if
m(\sigma)\geqslant 1
, then the map
\mathbb{Z}^{m(\sigma)}\to\mathbb{Z}
(defined by multiplication with
(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m(\sigma)}
) is surjective.
Hence, for
\sigma\notin\Delta^{\prime}
we have
H_{1}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=\mathbb{Z}^{m(\sigma)-1}
and
H_{0}(\sigma,\partial\Delta\cap\sigma)=0
.
Putting these two cases together leads to the formulas for
H_{i}(\mathcal{T}_{d,\bullet})_{k}
for
i=0,1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

7
Concluding remarks

We have made a theoretical and algorithmic contribution to the study of geometrically continuous splines by developing an algebraic framework that extends Billera’s algebraic methods for parametric splines.
This framework has two advantages.
First, we can use algebraic tools to study geometrically continuous splines.
In fact, in this paper, we demonstrate this by applying chain complexes and the division algorithm to construct bases and compute dimensions for
G^{r}
-spline spaces.
Second,
G^{r}
-spline spaces have rich algebraic structures that can be further explored using this framework. Thus, this framework makes it easy for algebraists to explore the exciting research topic of geometrically continuous splines.

Extending the study from parametric splines to geometrically continuous splines enables us to construct shapes with more complex topology.
For example, Example
9
in Section
6.3
is one such shape that cannot be constructed within the scope of parametric splines.
However, this flexibility comes at a cost: we need a more complicated algebraic model to describe the spline space.
While parametric spline spaces have only one coordinate system for the whole domain,
G^{r}
-spline require several coordinate systems, one for each face. As a result, transition maps are needed to define the smoothness condition between different coordinate systems. In Section
4
, we have several examples of
G^{r}
-domains having non-trivial transition maps which fit in our framework.
Hence, unlike in the case of parametric splines, the
G^{r}
-spline space
G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
is not always a finitely generated module over a known polynomial ring. In fact, it is not even clear if
G^{r}(\Delta,\Phi)
is finitely generated as an
\mathbb{R}
-algebra in general. This will be of interest for future research.

We have obtained explicit dimension formulas for
G^{r}
-spline spaces over a two-patches configuration and a star of vertex in Section
6
. To obtain these formulas, we have written the dimension of
G^{r}_{d}(\Delta,\Phi)
into the sum of Euler characteristic
\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
and homologies of the chain complex
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
, as defined in Section
2.3
. Because the transition map does not always preserve degrees between polynomial rings, finding
\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
and homologies of
\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet}
is difficult. In fact, the whole Section
5
is devoted in computing
\chi(\mathcal{Q}^{r}_{d,\bullet})
for certain kinds of transition maps, where the division algorithm plays an important role in the computation. It may be of interest for future studies to find dimension formulas which work more generally.

The method used for the construction of the
G^{r}
-basis in Section
2.4
, particularly Lemma
2.15
, applies to a more general setting and provides a general method to generate certain subspaces of a quotient ring.
We have implemented Algorithm
1
in
Macaulay2

[
25
]
computation in Example
9
. It is of our interest to develop a software package based on this Algorithm
1
, which computes a basis for
G^{r}
-spline spaces over any given
G^{r}
-domain.

One issue that remains to be solved is the gluing data in this paper are assumed to be polynomials, while they may be in the form of rational functions as defined in the context of topological surfaces in
[
44
]
. We are confident that our framework can be extended in the future to cover those cases where transition maps are given by rational gluing data.
