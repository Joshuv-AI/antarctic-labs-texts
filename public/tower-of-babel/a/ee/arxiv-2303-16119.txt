Exploring the validity of the complete case analysis for regression models with a right-censored covariate
Marissa C. Ashner, Tanya P. Garcia

Abstract
Despite its drawbacks, the complete case analysis is commonly used in regression models with missing covariates. Understanding when implementing complete cases will lead to consistent parameter estimation is vital before use. Here, our aim is to demonstrate when a complete case analysis is appropriate for a nuanced type of missing covariate, the randomly right-censored covariate. Across the censored covariate literature, different assumptions are made to ensure a complete case analysis produces a consistent estimator, which leads to confusion in practice. We make several contributions to dispel this confusion. First, we summarize the language surrounding the assumptions that lead to a consistent complete case estimator. Then, we show a unidirectional hierarchical relationship between these assumptions, which leads us to one sufficient assumption to consider before using a complete case analysis. Lastly, we conduct a simulation study to illustrate the performance of a complete case analysis with a right-censored covariate under different censoring mechanism assumptions, and we demonstrate its use with a Huntington disease data example.

Introduction

In the ongoing conversation about regression models with missing covariates, complete case analyses, which remove all observations with missingness, almost always have a seat at the table. However, being perceived as the simple, convenient method with many drawbacks, complete case analyses typically take a back seat. Despite its drawbacks, this convenient approach is still used by many analysts, especially newcomers to the field of missing data. Additionally, the complete case analysis is often used as a comparison to novel methods or as an initial estimate for sophisticated approaches. The continual use of the complete case analysis should earn the method a central role in the missing covariate conversation to avoid often overlooked properties and non-unified language surrounding the topic.

Here, we focus on clarifying when and why the complete case analysis leads to a consistent estimator for a nuanced type of missing covariate: right-censored covariates. For a right-censored covariate, the true value of the covariate is unknown due to missingness but is known to be greater than some value. This aspect gives us partial information regarding the unknown truth, which is more than is obtained from a general missing covariate. The body of literature surrounding the statistical handling of censored covariates mentions complete case analyses frequently. However, there are slight variations across the literature in terms of the assumptions made to ensure that the estimator produced by the complete case analysis is consistent. Key differences in the assumptions stem from the relationships assumed between the censored covariate, the value at which it is censored, and the censoring indicator. Each source considers one assumption, but assumptions across sources have not been compared before, making it unclear how the assumptions are related, if at all.

Understanding the relationship between the assumptions that drive consistency for a complete case estimator is important for several reasons. First, it is important for more sophisticated procedures for handling censored covariates, like multiple imputation, which uses the partial information available from the censored subject. The consistency of a multiple imputation estimator depends on the correct specification of the “imputation model”
[
1
]
. For some proposed imputation procedures, the imputation model itself builds from an consistent initial estimator, and in practice, the complete case estimator is often that initial estimator
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. This initial use means that if the complete case estimator is not consistent, the imputation model will be misspecified, leading to potential bias in the final estimator. Second, the complete case analysis is the default method for handling any type of missing data in most software packages
[
5
]
. Many analysts will use these programs without a second thought, so we are advocating for further awareness of when the default is valid and how to ensure the censored data are set up properly to handle the default. Finally, when new methods are proposed, many statisticians use simulations to compare the performance of their method to others, including the complete case analysis, which serves as a useful baseline (e.g., see
[
6
,
7
]
). It is crucial to be aware of the properties of
all
methods considered in simulations to fully understand the results.

In this paper, we will lay a clear foundation for what assumptions drive consistency when using a complete case analysis for regression models with a censored covariate. We clarify that, in fact, the different assumptions are interconnected in a hierarchical fashion and share a common property: either the censoring value or the censoring indicator must be independent of the outcome, given the covariates. The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
details and condenses the censoring mechanism assumptions present in the existing literature and proves the relationship between the mechanism assumptions as well as the consistency of the complete case estimator under said assumptions; Section
3
contains simulation studies conducted under many settings to compare a complete case analysis to the analysis that would be done if there were no censoring; Section
4
applies the complete case analysis to a Huntington disease observational study with a censored covariate; and Section
5
presents a discussion of our findings.

Establishing consistency of the complete case estimator

2.1
The complete case estimator with a right-censored covariate

2.2
Connections to missing data

At first glance, a right-censored covariate seems no different than a missing covariate. Yet, even though censoring is a type of missingness, it is different. With general missingness, we have no information about the true covariate value. With censoring, though, we have
partial information
: if
X
is right-censored, its true value must be larger than
C
. Statistical methods for handling missing data do not adjust for such partial information
[
9
]
.

That said, methods and language used in the missing data context can serve as a starting point for handling right-censored covariates. For example, when dealing with missing data, it is fundamental to consider the so-called missingness mechanisms –assumptions that explore the relationship between the missingness variable (i.e.,
\Delta
) and the data values (i.e.,
X,Y,{\mathbf{Z}}
) –to explain why missing data occur
[
10
,
11
]
. The three most common missingness mechanisms are: (1) missing completely at random, when the distribution of the missing data indicator is independent of all other variables; (2) missing at random, when the distribution of the missing data indicator depends on fully observed variables; and (3) missing not at random, when the distribution of the missing data indicator depends on the missing values themselves.

Models with a right-censored covariate case fall into the category of missing not at random because the “missing data" indicator is
\mbox{$\Delta$}=I({X}\leq{C})
, which explicitly depends on incomplete
{X}
. Data that are missing not at random must be considered carefully, since many existing methods require missing at random data
[
12
]
. However, an underappreciated fact is that a complete case analysis can lead to consistent estimators for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
even when data are missing not at random
[
13
]
. This consistency holds so long as certain assumptions about the missingness mechanisms hold. From now on, we will refer to missingness mechanisms as “censoring mechanisms” in the context of censored covariates, and we clarify the censoring mechanisms that lead to consistent complete case estimators next.

2.3
Censoring mechanisms for consistent complete case estimators

2.4
Unbiasedness of the linear complete case estimator

Consistency is a desirable property for any estimator, since it ensures that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
converges to the true parameter value,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}
, in probability. Convergence in probability is a large sample property meaning that the estimator will have higher probability of being “close” to the true value as the sample size increases to infinity. But what happens when we have a finite sample size? At what point will it be large enough for the large sample property to start “working”? We show that when the regression model in equation (
1
) is linear, then the complete case estimator exhibits unbiasedness, which is a finite sample property.

Remark
1

Consider a linear regression model with a right-censored covariate where in equation (
1
),
m(X,{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}^{T}{\mathbf{Z}}
. Let

{\mathbf{U}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1&X_{i}&{\mathbf{Z}}_{i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
be the
i
th row of the design matrix associated with this linear regression model. If and only if exogenous censoring
(C1)
holds, then the complete case estimator found by solving Equation (
3
), which is the least squares solution
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{U}}_{i}^{T}{\mathbf{U}}_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{U}}_{i}^{T}Y_{i}
, is not only a consistent estimator but is also unbiased (i.e.,
E(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})={\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}
).

Remark
1
says that, for any finite sample, the expectation of the estimator
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
will equal the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}
. Similar to Corollary
2.1
for consistency, we have unbiasedness when any of the censoring mechanisms
(C1)
–
(C5)
hold. The proof of unbiasedness under exogenous censoring is similar to that used for linear regression models when no covariate is right-censored; see for example
[

]

Having this result allows us to investigate the bias that can occur when exogenous censoring
(C1)
does not hold. In Supplementary Materials

, we show that for a linear model with an intercept, only the parameter estimate for the intercept (i.e.,
\widehat{\beta}_{0}
) will be biased if exogenous censoring does not hold, and the other estimates remain unbiased. Additionally, if the intercept is removed from the model, as in
m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\beta_{1}X+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}{\mathbf{Z}}

and

2.5
Checking the censoring mechanism assumptions

While censoring mechanisms
(C1)
–
(C5)
help to ensure consistency of the complete case estimator, how can an analyst check when these censoring mechanisms hold? Unfortunately, verifying any assumptions made on the censoring mechanisms is nearly impossible based on the observed data alone, since they all rely on incomplete
{X}

[
21
]
.

However, we may still be able to gauge from contextual knowledge of the data whether or not these censoring mechanisms hold. One way to visually explore the assumptions about independence between variables is to use a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
[
13
]
. DAGs are commonly used in the context of causal inference and are a tool used to visualize our contextual knowledge and
a priori
assumptions about the causal relationships between the variables in our model. DAGs consist of nodes representing random variables and edges representing the relationships between these variables. The edges have arrows, meaning they imply a direction; an arrow from
{X}
to
{Y}
implies that
{X}
causes
{Y}
. After using this framework to lay out the hypothesized causal structure of the variables of interest, the DAG can be used to describe marginal and conditional independence between variables. To do so, let us define a few terms:

1.

A
path
between two variables on a DAG is a route that connects the two variables by following a sequence of edges;

2.

A
collider
on a path is a node on the path where two arrowheads collide.

Two variables are marginally independent if, for all paths between the two, there is at least one collider. Two variables are conditionally independent if, for all paths between the two, there is at least one collider and/or we condition on at least one other variable (a non-collider) on the path
[
22
]
.

This concept can be best understood through an example. Consider a longitudinal observational study of patients who tested positive for the genetic mutation that causes Huntington disease. Let
{X}
be the time to clinical diagnosis (in years, which may be right-censored).
\Delta
is the indicator of whether or not
{X}
is censored, and
{C}
is the time to censoring (in years). Let us define
{\mathbf{Z}}
as the number of cytosine, adenine, guanine (CAG) repeats in the patient’s huntingtin gene and their current age, which are both factors known to be associated with impairment in this disease
[
23
]
. We will look at two outcomes
{Y}
. First, let
{Y}
be the current level of apathy felt by the patient. Then, let
{Y}
be the current motor impairment in Huntington disease patients (called the total motor score), where a higher score means greater impairment. We hypothesize that some people dropped out of the study because their motor impairment prevented them from continuing. By this we mean, a patient’s motor impairment could eventually limit their physical ability to continue participating in the study.

Now, consider the DAG in Figure

{Y}

It is important to recognize that these conclusions are not formal, since the DAGs are made from

We also note that we cannot consider exogenous censoring from DAGs, which relies on an expectation assumption rather than an independence assumption. However, DAGs are still helpful for visualizing the mechanisms.

3
Simulation Study

We conduct a simulation study to empirically demonstrate the consistency of the complete case estimator. Namely, we generate data under six different combinations of censoring mechanism assumptions to show that a complete case estimator is consistent when at least mechanism
(C1)
, i.e., exogenous censoring, holds.

3.1
Data Generation and Metrics for Comparison

Our simulations use sample sizes

We generate data for two regression models in equation (

\widehat{Var}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})=\widehat{{\mathbf{A}}}^{-1}\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{A}}}^{-1})^{T}

4
Apathy in Huntington disease as it relates to clinical diagnosis time

4.1
Motivation and Data

Huntington disease is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by motor disturbances, cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms
[
24
]
. Huntington disease is fully penetrant, meaning that if someone has the causal gene mutation, they are guaranteed to develop the disease in their lifetime. Individuals with the gene mutation and some symptoms, but not yet displaying enough motor disturbances to merit a clinical diagnosis, are in the “prodromal” disease stage
[
23
]
. Treating Huntington disease patients in the prodromal stage is important for possibly delaying diagnosis, avoiding irreparable damage and/or slowing symptom progression
[
25
]
.

One of the most common prodromal symptoms is apathy, described as lacking feeling, emotion, interest, and/or concern
[
26
,
27
]
. Apathy is distressing not only to the patient but also to the caretaker, since apathy presents as stubbornness and hostility which can strain relationships
[
28
,
29
]
. There is currently no treatment for apathy symptoms in Huntington disease, despite efforts to develop one
[
30
]
. Therefore, one step in treating apathy as early as possible would be to characterize how it changes as a function of time to diagnosis, or disease progression, specifically
within
the prodromal stage. Many researchers have concluded that apathy does indeed appear to be greater on average for those post-diagnosis than for those in the prodromal disease stage
[
31
,
32
]
. We are interested in determining how apathy changes over time to see where on the disease path apathy begins to increase or worsen. Knowing this information would enable researchers to target this time of initial apathy increase for future treatments and interventions.

The Neurobiological Predictors of Huntington Disease (PREDICT-HD) study is a longitudinal, observational study designed to explore symptoms of Huntington disease in the prodromal stage
[
25
]
. In one study of apathy in the prodromal period using PREDICT-HD data
[
33
]
, a cross-sectional linear regression analysis was performed with data from the baseline visit and apathy as the outcome. One variable
[
33
]
included as a covariate in their model is a commonly used measure of disease burden called the CAG-Age-Product (CAP) score, which is a function of CAG repeat length and age
[
23
]
. The purpose of the CAP score is to predict “time to clinical diagnosis”, or briefly, “time to diagnosis”. Since
[
33
]
did not see a significant relationship between CAP score and apathy while controlling for other variables, they concluded that apathy may not be
directly
related to the progression of Huntington disease during the prodromal stage. This conclusion would mean that despite the literature arguing apathy increases across disease stages, the relationship may be more difficult to detect within the prodromal stage or may be confounded by other variables.

Given the longitudinal nature of PREDICT-HD, some of the participants were given a clinical diagnosis during the course of the study, and therefore their time to diagnosis was observed. Instead of using the CAP score to draw conclusions about the relationship between apathy and the progression of Huntington disease, we can use the patient’s actual observed time to diagnosis. This approach is important because CAP scores are known to be imprecise and do not always accurately reflect clinical diagnosis
[
34
]
. Therefore, running the analysis with the true time to diagnosis can more accurately assess whether the lack of relationship
[
33
]
drew between apathy and disease progression is valid. The time to diagnosis covariate, however, is right-censored, since not everyone was given a clinical diagnosis by the time the study was over. This case presents an opportunity to analyze these data with a complete case estimator, which is consistent under one of the censoring mechanism assumptions
(C1)
–
(C5)
.

4.2
Considering Censoring Mechanism Assumptions

As discussed in Section
2.5
, it is not possible to check whether censoring mechanism assumptions hold based on the observed data alone, due to the censoring of
{X}
. That being said, the dropout rate for PREDICT-HD was less than
5\%
per year, and sample size variation year to year was due to study design considerations rather than dropouts
[
35
]
. Keeping this in mind, we can look back at the DAG in Figure
1
to revisit our proposed relationships between these variables. We remember from Section
2.5
that
{Y}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}{C}|({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
as long as there is no direct path between
{Y}
and
{C}
. We mentioned the possibility that apathetic people may lose interest in the study and dropout, causing a relationship between apathy and the time to censoring. However, given such a low dropout rate, we are confident in assuming this path does not exist in the true DAG. Therefore, we assume censoring mechanism
(C3)
, which will lead to a consistent complete case estimator.

4.3
Analysis

We focus on analyzing individuals who were genetically confirmed to have the gene mutation for Huntington disease (i.e., a CAG repeat length of at least 36). We also remove anyone who was given a clinical diagnosis at the first visit because we wanted a subset of individuals in the prodromal stage at baseline. Furthermore, we run the analysis using data from the second visit, since some patients were diagnosed at the second visit, giving us a wider range for the time to diagnosis variable. Finally, we remove any individuals with
missing
outcome or covariate information, leaving us with an analysis set of 781, where 212 have an observed time to diagnosis. This is a censoring rate of about
73\%
.

The goal of this analysis is to quantify the linear association between apathy and time to clinical diagnosis (in years), while controlling for sex, depression, total motor score, years of education, single digit modalities test score (SDMT), CAG repeat length, and age, similar to the model construction by
[
33
]
. Apathy was measured using the Frontal Systems Behavior Scale, which measures behavior associated with frontal systems of the brain and has subscales for apathy, disinhibition, and executive function
[
36
]
. Depression was measured using the Symptom Checklist-90-R, which measures psychological distress and has several subscales including depression
[
37
]
. Both the patient and caretaker filled out these rating scales and we use the caretaker results. We chose a linear model to reflect the relationship between apathy and disease progression from previous studies
[
31
,
33
]
. This set of covariates has been shown to be related to apathy, and we want to control for them to see if time to diagnosis is directly related to apathy, rather than through a relationship with these control variables. We want to compare our results to the model from
[
33
]
to see if using time to diagnosis yields different results than the CAP score. If the results agree, we will conclude that there is no evidence for a strong, direct relationship between apathy and disease progression during the prodromal stage. If the results are different, there will be some evidence to suggest that apathy does change with disease progression when the true time to diagnosis variable is used.

We use the following linear regression model to test these relationships:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i}+\beta_{2}Z_{1i}+\beta_{3}Z_{2i}+\beta_{4}Z_{3i}+\beta_{5}Z_{4i}+\beta_{6}Z_{5i}+\beta_{7}Z_{6i}+\beta_{8}Z_{7i}+\epsilon_{i},

where
Y_{i}
is apathy score,
X_{i}
is time to diagnosis in years,
Z_{1i}
is biological sex indicator (1 if female),
Z_{2i}
is depression score,
Z_{3i}
is total motor score,
Z_{4i}
is number years of education,
Z_{5i}
is SDMT score,
Z_{6i}
is CAG repeat length and
Z_{7i}
is age in years, all at the second visit. All covariates, except time to diagnosis, were centered and scaled.

The results from the regression (Table
3
) show no evidence that our measure of disease progression (i.e., time to diagnosis) is associated with apathy after controlling for other variables. These results agree with the same regression replacing time to diagnosis with CAP score (Table
S.8
, Supplementary Materials
S.5
) and the results from the CAP score model in
[
33
]
. Therefore, our results agree with the conclusion made from the CAP score model, even if using CAP score may not have been a precise measure of disease progression. It is also worth noting that the only covariate whose confidence interval does not contain the null value in our time to diagnosis model is depression. The
[
33
]
model and our CAP score model also saw evidence of a relationship between apathy and total motor score, as well as cognitive control (SDMT score, here). One reason for this result could be the lack of efficiency that comes with a complete case analysis. Because we deleted so much data for the time to diagnosis analysis, our standard errors are quite large, despite being confident in having a consistent estimator. Overall, our analysis shows that it may be difficult to detect a change in apathy scores in Huntington disease patients during the prodromal change, despite the clear increase across disease stage groups presented in the literature. From these results, we cannot recommend a clear way to recruit patients for trials that would be expected to detect a change in apathy over time.

5
Discussion

This paper provides a unified summary of complete case estimator consistency for censored covariates, and we recommend that these properties are taken into consideration when using the method in any scenario. Despite its main drawback of lack of precision due to the discarding of data, the complete case estimator can serve as a powerful initial estimator for complex methods or as a baseline comparison when benchmarking novel methods. Additionally, using the complete case estimator does not require any model assumptions on the censored covariate, meaning there is less chance of model misspecification leading to bias.

We show here that for regression models with a right-censored covariate, the complete case estimator is consistent when any one of the five censoring mechanisms
(C1)
through
(C5)
hold. These assumptions revolve around the idea that the censoring variable (in terms of
{C}
or
\Delta
) is independent of the outcome
{Y}
, conditional or unconditional on covariates
{X}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
. Moreover, a consistent estimator is not guaranteed when the exogenous censoring assumption does not hold. Although it is impossible to verify the assumptions made, domain knowledge and DAGs can be used to help gain confidence. Lastly, while we presented results when a covariate is randomly right-censored, our conclusions similarly apply when the covariate is left-censored (i.e.,
\Delta=I(X\geq C
)) or when the censoring is due to a limit of detection (i.e., mechanisms
(C1)
–
(C2)
only because there is no random
C
).

S.1
Proof of Theorem 1

We prove Theorem
1
by proving four implications between the five censoring mechanisms. Each of these proofs is labeled below, where ‘
\implies
’ means ‘implies’.

Censoring mechanism
(C5)

\implies
Censoring mechanism
(C4)
:

Assuming censoring mechanism
(C5)
holds, i.e.,
{C}
is independent of
({X},{Y},{\mathbf{Z}})
, we have that

\displaystyle f_{{C},{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{C},{Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x,{\mathbf{z}})dydx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{C}}(c)f_{{Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}})dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}}(c)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,z)dydx=f_{{C}}(c)f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}(z).

The result above shows that the joint density of
{C}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
equals the product of their marginal distributions, which means
{C}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
are marginally independent. This marginal independence combined with censoring mechanism
(C5)
further implies that

\displaystyle f_{({C},{Y},{X})|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x|{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{{C},{Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x,{\mathbf{z}})}{f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}({\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{{C}}(c)f_{{Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}})}{f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}({\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}}(c)f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x|{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x|{\mathbf{z}}).

The second equality above holds because we assumed censoring mechanism
(C5)
, and the last equality holds because
{C}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
are marginally independent. We therefore showed
f_{({C},{Y},{X})|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x|z)=f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x|z)
, which proves
{C}
is independent of
({X},{Y})
given
{\mathbf{Z}}
.

Censoring mechanism
(C4)

\implies
Censoring mechanism
(C3)
:

Assuming censoring mechanism
(C4)
, i.e.,
{C}
is independent of
({X},{Y})
given
{\mathbf{Z}}
, we have that

\displaystyle f_{{C},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,x|{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{C},{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x|{\mathbf{z}})dy=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x|{\mathbf{z}})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x|{\mathbf{z}})dy=f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}}).

Having shown
f_{{C},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,x|{\mathbf{z}})=f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}})
, we proved that
{C}
and
{X}
are independent conditional on
{\mathbf{Z}}
. This conditional independence combined with censoring mechanism

implies that

\displaystyle f_{{C},{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y|{x},{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{{C},{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,y,x|{\mathbf{z}})}{f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y},{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x,y|z)}{f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y|x,{\mathbf{z}})f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}})}{f_{{X}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x|{\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y|x,{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{C}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(c|x,{\mathbf{z}})f_{{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(y|x,{\mathbf{z}}).

The second equality holds by censoring mechanism
(C4)
, and the last equality holds because
{C}
and
{X}
are independent given
{\mathbf{Z}}
. Because we showed that the conditional density of
({C},{Y})|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}
is equal to the product of the conditional densities of
{C}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}
and
{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}
, we proved
{C}
is independent of
{Y}
given
({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
.

Censoring mechanism
(C3)

\implies
Censoring mechanism
(C2)
:

Let

(C3)

\displaystyle F_{\mbox{$\Delta$},{\epsilon}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(d,e|X=x,{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{z}})=F_{\mbox{$\Delta$}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(d|X=x,{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{z}})F_{{\epsilon}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(e|X=x,{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{z}})

(1)

for all

(R1

(R1enumi)

First, we show

for all
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle E\{\mbox{$\Delta$}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(Y,{X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}=E[\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{A}}(X,{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\{Y-m(X,{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{{X},{\mathbf{Z}},\mbox{$\Delta$}}[\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{A}}(X,{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\{E_{Y|{X},{\mathbf{Z}},\mbox{$\Delta$}}(Y|{X},{\mathbf{Z}},\mbox{$\Delta$})-m(X,{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}]

(R2enumi)
:
Now, we will show

\displaystyle E\left\{\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}}\right\}

\displaystyle E\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{A}}({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\{{Y}-m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}}\right]

\displaystyle E\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{A}}({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\{{Y}-m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}-{\mathbf{A}}(\cdot)\frac{\partial m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\right],

where the second equality holds by the product rule for derivatives.
Consider the first term,
E\left[\partial{\mathbf{A}}({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})/\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\{{Y}-m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}\left(E_{{Y}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{A}}({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\{{Y}-m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}\right]\right)

We can simplify the expectation of the complete case estimating function the same way, and the first term will also equal
{\mathbf{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta{\mathbf{A}}(x,z;{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\frac{\partial m(x,z;{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}f_{{X},{\mathbf{Z}},\mbox{$\Delta$}}(x,z,\delta)d\delta dxdz

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbf{A}}(x,z;{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\frac{\partial m(x,z;{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}f_{{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(x,z)\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta f_{\mbox{$\Delta$}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}}(\delta|x,z)d\delta\right\}dxdz

\displaystyle E\left\{P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=1|{X},{\mathbf{Z}}){\mathbf{A}}({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\frac{\partial m({X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\right\},

(R3enumi)

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}

\displaystyle\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0})}\left|\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({Y},{X},{\mathbf{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}}\right|

S.3
Investigation of Bias for Linear Model

In this section, we explore the bias that can occur for a complete case analysis with a linear model if exogenous censoring
(C1)
does not hold. This follows the discussion from Section
2.4
. Without loss of generality, we will show the simplest case where there is an intercept but no other
{\mathbf{Z}}
covariates such that
m(\cdot)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X
. The estimating equation from Section
2.4
will have two components:

1.

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}(Y_{i}-\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}X_{i})=0
and

2.

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}(Y_{i}-\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}X_{i})=0
.

Considering the first component, we can isolate
\beta_{0}
to get
\widehat{\beta_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{1}X_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}
. Then, plugging
\widehat{\beta}_{0}
in for
\beta_{0}
in the second component, we can isolate
\beta_{1}
to get
\widehat{\beta}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}(Y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}Y_{i}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i})/\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i})^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\}
.

We will show that
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
is unbiased, even if exogenous censoring is broken, by showing the expectation is equal to
\beta_{1}
.

\displaystyle E(\widehat{\beta}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}(Y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}Y_{i}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i})^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{X,\Delta}\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\{E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i})^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}}\right].

Note that
E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})=E(\epsilon_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})+\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i}=E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})+\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i}
by our model assumptions in Section
2.1
. Now, plugging back in to the numerator of the expectation, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left\{E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left[E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})+\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left\{E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})+\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i}\right\}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right].

Considering the terms with
\beta_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left(\beta_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\beta_{0}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}(\beta_{0}-\beta_{0})=0.

Considering the terms with
\beta_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left(\beta_{1}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\beta_{1}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right)=\beta_{1}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\right)^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right\},

which will cancel out with the denominator from above leaving us with
\beta_{1}
. Finally, considering the constant terms (i.e., no parameters), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left\{E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right\}.

If
\Delta_{i}=0
, this whole term is
0
due to the first
\Delta_{i}
in the summation. If
\Delta_{i}=1
, then
E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i}=1)=c
where
c
is a non-zero constant. Then the above expression is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left(c-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}c/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\left(c-c\right)=0.

Now, we want to show that
\widehat{\beta}_{0}
may be biased when exogenous censoring doesn’t hold. We show the expectation may not equal
\beta_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle E(\widehat{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{X,\Delta}\left[E_{Y|X,\Delta}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{1}X_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left(1/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left[\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}E(Y_{i}|X_{i},\Delta_{i})\right\}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}E\left(\widehat{\beta}_{1}|X_{i},\Delta_{i}\right)\Delta_{i}X_{i}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left(1/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left[\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})+\Delta_{i}\beta_{0}+\Delta_{i}\beta_{1}X_{i}\right\}-\beta_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}+E(\epsilon|\Delta=1),

which is not equal to
\beta_{0}
unless
E(\epsilon|\Delta=1)=0
. If exogenous censoring does not hold, then
E(\epsilon|\Delta=1)=E\{E(\epsilon|\Delta=1,X)\}
is not necessarily
0
, since
E(\epsilon|\Delta=1,X)\neq 0
.

Finally, we consider what happens when there is no intercept, such that
m(\cdot)=\beta_{1}X
. The estimating equation from Section
2.4
will only have one component; namely, the second component from above, setting
\beta_{0}=0
. If we isolate
\beta_{1}
, we get
\widehat{\beta}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}Y_{i}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}
. To investigate potential bias, we consider

\displaystyle E(\widehat{\beta}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}Y_{i}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}E(Y_{i}|\Delta_{i},X_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}\{E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})+\beta_{1}X_{i}\}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}+E\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}X_{i}^{2}\right\},

which is equal to
\beta_{1}
when exogenous censoring holds (i.e.,
E(\epsilon_{i}|\Delta_{i})=0
), but may not be equal to
\beta_{1}
otherwise.

S.4
Justification for Simulation Settings

The following give justification to the data generation done for each simulation setting as shown in Table
1
. Each setting was chosen to satisfy (i) the respective combination of censoring mechanism assumptions and (ii) the chosen censoring rate, denoted as
r
.

(a.)

Exogenous Censoring does not hold:
Mechanism
(C1)
–
(C5)
do not hold.

(i.)

Mechanism
(C1)
, exogenous censoring, does not hold because
{{\mu}}=E({\epsilon}|\mbox{$\Delta$},{X},{\mathbf{Z}})\neq{\mathbf{0}}
for both values of
\Delta
. Specifically,
{{\mu}}=\sigma/8
when
\mbox{$\Delta$}=0
and
{{\mu}}=-\sigma/8\times r/1-r
when
\mbox{$\Delta$}=1
.

Even though exogenous censoring does not hold, the model errors must have conditional mean zero, i.e.,
E({\epsilon}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}})={\mathbf{0}}
. To see that the errors have conditional mean zero, note that

\displaystyle E({\epsilon}|{X},{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E({\epsilon}|\mbox{$\Delta$}=1,{X},{\mathbf{Z}})P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=1|{X},{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E({\epsilon}|\mbox{$\Delta$}=0,{X},{\mathbf{Z}})P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=0|{X},{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-\sigma}{8}\frac{r}{1-r}\times(1-r)+\frac{\sigma}{8}\times r={\mathbf{0}}.

(ii.)

Since
\mbox{$\Delta$}\sim\hbox{Bernoulli}(1-r)
, this implies the probability of being observed is
1-r
, satisfying the desired censoring rate.

(b.)

Strict Exogenous Censoring does not hold:
Mechanism
(C1)
holds and
(C2)
–
(C5)
do not hold.

(i.)

Mechanism
(C2)
, strict exogenous censoring, does not hold by design since
\Delta
is directly generated dependent on
{\epsilon}
and independent of covariates
({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
. Therefore, the two are not independent given
({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
. This implies mechanisms
(C3)
–
(C5)
also do not hold. However, exogenous censoring still holds because (1)
{\epsilon}
is marginally generated from a normal distribution with mean
{\mathbf{0}}
, and (2)
\Delta
is generated based on the
absolute value
of
\epsilon
, so the expectation of
\epsilon
given either value of
\Delta
will still be
0
.

(ii.)

We define
\mbox{$\Delta$}=0
when the absolute value of
{\epsilon}
is less than to the
r
th quantile of
|{\epsilon}|
. The proportion of observations that are less than the
r
th quantile is exactly
r
, by definition of a quantile. This ensures that
r
of the observations will have
\mbox{$\Delta$}=0
, satisfying the desired censoring rate.

(c.)

Conditional Independence given (X,Z) does not hold:
Mechanism
(C1)
–
(C2)
hold and
(C3)
–
(C5)
do not hold.

(i.)

In this setting,
{\epsilon}
is generated to be dependent on
{C}
, independent of covariates
({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
, so mechanisms
(C3)
–
(C5)
do not hold. Particularly,
{\epsilon}
is more variable at the extremes of
{C}
. However, this says little about the relationship between
\Delta
and
{\epsilon}
. If
\Delta_{i}=1
, then we know
X_{i}\leq C_{i}
, but even given
X_{i}
, this tells us nothing about whether or not
C_{i}\in\text{IQR}(C)
and therefore tells us nothing about
\epsilon_{i}
. Therefore,
\Delta
is independent of
{\epsilon}
given
({X},{\mathbf{Z}})
, so censoring mechanisms
(C1)
–
(C2)
do hold.

(ii.)

Now to see that we have generated the desired censoring rate, consider the following. Remember that
{X}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,3)
. We consider
r<0.5
and
r>=0.5
separately, since for
r<0.5
,
\max(C)>\max(X)
and vice versa for
r>0.5
. If
r<0.5
, we define
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,3/(2r))
. Then

\displaystyle P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({C}\leq{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{2r}{3\times 3}dcdx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\frac{2r\times x}{9}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r}{9}(3^{2}-0^{2})=r.

And if the censoring rate is
\geq 0.5
, then
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,(6-6r))
and

\displaystyle P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({X}\leq{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{6-6r}\int_{0}^{c}\frac{1}{3(6-6r)}dxdc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{6-6r}\frac{c}{3(6-6r)}dc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6(6-6r)}((6-6r)^{2}-0^{2})=(6-6r)/6=1-r.

(d.)

Conditional Independence given Z does not hold:
Mechanism
(C1)
–
(C3)
hold and
(C4)
–
(C5)
do not hold.

(i.)

{C}
is generated directly dependent on
{X}
, so Mechanism
(C4)
, conditionally independent censoring given
{\mathbf{Z}}
, and therefore mechanism
(C5)
, independent censoring, cannot hold. However, given
{X}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
,
{C}
is independent of
{Y}
, so mechanism
(C3)
and therefore
(C1)
–
(C2)
all hold.

(ii.)

To see that we have generated the desired censoring rate with
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,{X}/r)
,

\displaystyle P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({C}\leq{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{r}{3x}dcdx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\frac{r}{3}dx=\frac{r}{3}(3-0)=r.

(e.)

Independence does not hold:
Mechanism
(C1)
–
(C4)
hold and
(C5)
does not hold.

(i.)

{C}
’s generation is directly dependent on
{\mathbf{Z}}
, so mechanism
(C5)
, independent censoring, cannot hold. However
{C}
is independent of
{X}
and
{Y}
given
{\mathbf{Z}}
, so mechanism
(C4)
and therefore
(C1)
–
(C3)
all hold.

(ii.)

To see that we have generated the desired censoring rate, we consider the generation of
{C}
. When
r\geq 0.5
, 50% of the observations (those with
Z>\text{med}({\mathbf{Z}})
will be generated from
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,6-6r)
, which has censoring rate
r
as shown in (c.ii.). The other 50% (when
Z\leq\text{med}({\mathbf{Z}})
) will be generated from
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(\frac{3-6r}{2-2r},3)
. This also has censoring rate
r
as seen below:

\displaystyle P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({C}\leq{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\int_{\frac{3-6r}{2-2r}}^{3}\frac{1}{3\times(3-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})}dcdx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}\frac{x-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r}}{3(3-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(3-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})^{2}-(\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})^{2}}{2\times 3(3-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{9-6\frac{3-6r}{2-2r}}{2\times 3(3-\frac{3-6r}{2-2r})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3-2\frac{3-6r}{2-2r}}{6-2\frac{3-6r}{2-2r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3(2-2r)-2(3-6r)}{6(2-2r)-2(3-6r)}=\frac{6r}{6}=r.

Similarly, when
r<0.5
, half of the observations will be generated from
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(0,\frac{3}{2r})
, which has censoring rate
r
as shown in (c.ii). The other half will be generated from
{C}\sim\hbox{Uniform}(3-6r,3)
. This also has censoring rate
r
:

\displaystyle P(\mbox{$\Delta$}=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({X}\leq{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{3-6r}^{3}\int_{0}^{c}\frac{1}{3(6r)}dxdc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{3-6r}^{3}\frac{c}{3(6r)}dc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3^{2}-(3-6r)^{2}}{2*3(6r)}=\frac{-36r^{2}+36r}{36r}=1-r.

(f.)

Independence holds:
Mechanisms (1-5) hold.

(i.)

{C}
is generated independently of
{X}
,
{Y}
, and
{\mathbf{Z}}
, so mechanism
(C5)
holds by design. This implies mechanisms
(C1)
–
(C4)
also hold.

(ii.)

For justification how the choice of parameters satisfies the chosen censoring rate, see (c.ii.).
