Sharpness criterion proof and examples

2.1
Preliminary lemmas

We begin with two lemmas that reframe the combinatorial relationship (
1.1
) as an identity of analytic functions. The ideas presented here are well-known in the area of analytic combinatorics and originally arose in the context of tree enumeration. For a full treatment, the interested reader can consult
[

]
, particularly sections I.5, VII.4, and VII.5.

Let
A(x)=1+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}x^{n}
and
B(x)=1+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}b_{n}x^{n}
be power series satisfying (

). When

has a positive radius of convergence, we would like to know when the identity (
1.1
) of formal power series is also an identity of the complex analytic functions defined by these power series in a neighborhood of the origin, since then we may apply analytic methods. A sufficient condition is given by Lemma

below.

We will adopt a useful convention of setting

, whereby the identity (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

y(x)=xA(y(x)).

(2.1)

The coefficient sequence

of

is then given simply by

, for
n\geqslant 1
. Recall that if

is known, then the coefficients

are unique and can be determined from the Lagrange Inversion Theorem
[

, Ch. 5.4]

y_{n}=\frac{1}{n}[x^{n-1}](A(x)^{n}),

where

denotes the

When thinking of these power series as analytic functions, we will use the variable

will emphasize thinking of formal power series combinatorially, without considerations of convergence or analytic continuation. This distinction is more psychological than mathematical and could also just be ignored.

Let

Recall from Lemma

Toward proving the contrapositive, assume that

(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{r^{n}n^{3/2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}))=\frac{C\cdot A^{\prime}(\tau)^{n}}{n^{3/2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})),

[

Taken together, Theorems

and

imply the following dichotomy.

Theorem 2.6

(Criterion for sharpness, full version)
.

Suppose that
A(x)
and
y(x)
(with radii of convergence
R
and
r
, respectively) satisfy conditions (1) and (2) of Theorem
2.4
and the gcd condition of Theorem
2.5
. Then exactly one of the following is true:

(1)

A(z)-zA^{\prime}(z)
is non-vanishing for
z\in(0,R)
, and
y(r)=R
.

(2)

A(z)-zA^{\prime}(z)
vanishes at
z=y(r)
, and
y(r)<R
. Moreover,
z=y(r)
is the unique solution to
A(z)-zA^{\prime}(z)=0
on
(0,R)
, and
\displaystyle{y_{n}=Cr^{-n}n^{-3/2}(1+o(1))}
as

, for some constant

Proof.

A(z)-zA^{\prime}(z)
either vanishes on
(0,R)
or it does not. If it does not, then Theorem
2.4
implies that
y(r)=R
, which is case (1) of the dichotomy.
If it does vanish, then Theorem
2.5
applies, and case (2) is implied.
∎

In particular, the existence of the subexponential factor

in the asymptotic expansion of

is implied when

. The absence of the

factor therefore certifies that
{y(r)=R}
, and Theorem
1.4
immediately follows.

The generating functions

does not possess an additional combinatorial interpretation that we are aware of, and it is not presently clear how the example should generalize to a broader class of boundary case examples with natural combinatorial significance. As discussed further in the remark preceding it, Example

Example 2.7

(Catalan numbers)

Let

3.2

Proposition 3.1
.

As
n\to\infty
, the sequence
(b_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfies

b_{n}\sim K(7^{n}/n^{7}).

In the introduction we described a lattice walk model in which the

’s denote the number of
n

G_{2}
. This interpretation suggests that a local central limit theorem will apply for the return probabilities of random paths of length

[
GZ92
]
which allows one to express the return probabilities as local linear combinations of the probabilities that
unconstrained
walks in the weight lattice (
\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
) will end at various nearby points to the origin. Indeed, this method has been applied to derive asymptotic formulas
[
TZ04
, Thm. 8]
which imply that the sequence (
\text{dim Inv}_{L}(V^{\otimes n}))_{n=1}^{\infty}
, where
V
is any representation of a complex semi-simple Lie algebra

, is asymptotically equivalent to
C\dim(V)^{n}n^{-\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is half the dimension of
L
(in our case,
\alpha=14/2=7
), and the constant term
C
can also be computed but depends on the specific representation. We nonetheless supply a direct proof of Proposition
3.1
based on a saddle-point analysis. Similar analysis for other Weyl chambers was conducted in
[
Fei13
]
.

Proof.

The random walk model and the reflection trick mentioned above are encoded by the following formula from character theory
[
Kup94
, p.15]
:
b_{n}
is the coefficient of

in the Laurent polynomial

\displaystyle W(x,y)=x^{-2}y^{-3}(x^{2}y^{3}

\displaystyle-xy^{3}+x^{-1}y^{2}-x^{-2}y+x^{-3}y^{-1}-x^{-3}y^{-2}

\displaystyle+x^{-2}y^{-3}-x^{-1}y^{-3}+xy^{-2}-x^{2}y^{-1}+x^{3}y-x^{3}y^{2}).

We define

By Cauchy’s residue formula for Taylor coefficients, we have

(3.1)

The function
f

. It follows that

f(e^{iu},e^{iv})=-\frac{2}{7}u^{2}-\frac{2}{7}v^{2}-\frac{2}{7}uv+\mathcal{O}((u,v)^{3}),

as

.
The integral becomes

\frac{4\pi^{2}b_{n}n}{7^{n}}=\int_{-\sqrt{n}\pi}^{\sqrt{n}\pi}\int_{-\sqrt{n}\pi}^{\sqrt{n}\pi}\left[W\left(e^{ip/\sqrt{n}},e^{iq/\sqrt{n}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{2}{7}p^{2}-\frac{2}{7}q^{2}-\frac{2}{7}pq+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(p,q)^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\right]dpdq.

By standard estimates, which we omit (and which morally relate to the fact that there is a local central limit theorem for a lattice random walk with i.i.d. steps at work), the above integral can be approximated asymptotically by the completed integral over all of

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[T_{12}(pn^{-1/2},qn^{-1/2})\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{2}{7}p^{2}-\frac{2}{7}q^{2}-\frac{2}{7}pq\right)\right]dpdq

4.1
Evaluation of

and analytic continuation of

In the recent paper
[
BTWZ22
, p. 8]
is given the following remarkable closed formula for the generating function

in terms of hypergeometric series.

Theorem 4.1

(Bostan, Tirrell, Westbury, Zhang, 2019)

B(z)=\frac{1}{30z^{5}}\left[R_{1}(z)\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3};2;\phi(z)\right)+R_{2}(z)\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3};3;\phi(z)\right)+5P(z)\right],

where

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=(z+1)^{2}(214z^{3}+45z^{2}+60z+5)(z-1)^{-1},

\displaystyle R_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=6z^{2}(z+1)^{2}(101z^{2}+74z+5)(z-1)^{-2},

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle=27(z+1)z^{2}(1-z)^{-3},

\displaystyle P(z)

\displaystyle=28z^{4}+66z^{3}+46z^{2}+15z+1.

We will use this formula as the starting point for our analysis in the next section. As a quick application, we obtain the following result, which verifies (
1.6
).

Proposition 4.2
.

\rho=\frac{5\pi}{8575\pi-15552\sqrt{3}}.

Proof.

Evaluating (
4.1
) at
x=1/7
, the formula simplifies to

B\left(\frac{1}{7}\right)=\frac{7^{5}}{30}\left[\frac{-55296}{2401}\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3};2;1\right)+\frac{9216}{2401}\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3};3;1\right)+\frac{150}{7}\right].

It is now a matter of routine calculation to deduce the value in the proposition. One only needs standard facts about the gamma function, namely that
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
for
z\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
and the following
[

]
:

(1)

\displaystyle{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(a,b;c;1\right)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}\hskip 8.50012pt(\operatorname{Re}(c)>\operatorname{Re}(a+b)),

.

(2)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}\hskip 8.50012pt(z\in\mathbb{C}).

.

Using (1) and (2) we simplify the above expression for

and recall that
\rho=7/B(1/7)
.
∎

In deriving formula (

), the authors of
[

]
demonstrate that

is the solution of a linear differential equation of the form

where the coefficients

, are rational functions with poles at

and

. From this fact, as well as the fact that

is expressed on

) and is visibly analytic near

(by (

) and

can be continued analytically and uniquely along any path avoiding the set

(e.g.
[

, p.119]
). Therefore,

has a unique analytic continuation to the simply-connected doubly slit plane

\tilde{\Omega}=\mathbb{C}\setminus{\big((\infty,-1/2]\cup[1/7,\infty)\big)}.

In particular,

is continuable to a Delta-domain around
\Omega
.

Proposition 4.3

Lemma 4.4

For constants

The next step in finding a singular expansion for

Moving forward, we make the change of variable

Lemma 4.7

\psi

We begin with a numerical evaluation. (

\tilde{\Omega}

B(z)=f_{0}+7f_{1}(1/7-z)+\mathcal{O}((1/7-z)^{2}),

where
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
were defined in the proof of Proposition
4.3
. Recall that
1/\rho=y(1/7)=B(1/7)/7=f_{0}/7
, and
y^{\prime}(1/7)=f_{0}-f_{1}
.
Therefore, the following approximation holds for
y
:

\displaystyle y(1/7)-y(z)

\displaystyle=y(1/7)-zB(z)

\displaystyle=f_{0}/7+(1/7-z)B(z)-B(z)/7

\displaystyle=f_{0}/7+f_{0}(1/7-z)-(f_{0}/7+f_{1}(1/7-z))+(\mathcal{O}(1/7-z)^{2})

\displaystyle=(1/7-z)(y^{\prime}(1/7)+\mathcal{O}(1/7-z))

(4.16)

As a consequence, there exists
\epsilon>0

such that
the following properties hold for all
z\in\tilde{\Omega}
such that
|z-1/7|<\epsilon
, and in particular on
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
:

(1)

\frac{8y^{\prime}(1/7)}{9}<\frac{|y(z)-y(1/7)|}{|z-1/7|}<\frac{10y^{\prime}(1/7)}{9}

(2)

\left|\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{y(1/7)-y(z)}{1/7-z}\right)\right|<\pi/8

(3)

|y^{\prime}(z)-y^{\prime}(1/7)|<\frac{y^{\prime}(1/7)}{9}

The first two properties follow from (
4.3
), as the
\mathcal{O}(1/7-z)
term can be bounded in modulus by
y^{\prime}(1/7)/9
(implying (1)), which is less than
y^{\prime}(1/7)\tan(\pi/8)
(implying (2)). The third property follows from the continuity of
y^{\prime}
, as discussed before (
4.14
). Properties (1) and (2) will help us to understand the shape of
y(D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7))
, while (3) will help us to argue that
y
is injective on
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
.

Let
\gamma
be the straight line emanating from
1/7
, of the form
\gamma(t)=1/7+te^{i\pi/4}
, for
t\geqslant 0
.
Property (2) implies that

\pi/8<\operatorname{Arg}(y(\gamma(t))-y(1/7))<3\pi/8

for
t\in(0,\epsilon)
. Similarly, we also have
-\pi/8>\operatorname{Arg}(y(\overline{\gamma}(t))-y(1/7))>-3\pi/8
for
t\in(0,\epsilon)
, where
\overline{\gamma}(t)=1/7+te^{-i\pi/4}
. Moreover, property (1) implies, by continuity, that for
z\in\tilde{\Omega}
such that
|z-1/7|=\epsilon
, the following is true:

\frac{8y^{\prime}(1/7)\epsilon}{9}\leqslant|y(z)-y(1/7)|\leqslant\frac{10y^{\prime}(1/7)\epsilon}{9}\,

Thus, we have described the images of the two line segments and the circular arc that make up the boundary of
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
. Our description implies that the image
y(D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7))
contains
D_{\epsilon^{\prime},\delta}(1/\rho)
, for
\epsilon^{\prime}=8y^{\prime}(1/7)\epsilon/9
, and
\delta=3\pi/8
, as shown in Figure
4.1
. This proves the first statement in the lemma.

Figure 4.1
:
The line
\gamma
and the region
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
(left), with its image (shaded, right).

To prove the second part of the lemma, we argue that
y
is injective on
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
.
Let
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
be two points in
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
. If the line segment connecting
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
lies in
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
, then we estimate
|y(z_{1})-y(z_{2})|
as follows. Since

y(z_{1})-y(z_{2})=\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}y^{\prime}(w)\,dw=\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}y^{\prime}(1/7)\,dw+\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}[y^{\prime}(w)-y^{\prime}(1/7)]\,dw,

where the contour of integration is the line segment, it follows from property (3) above that

\displaystyle|y(z_{1})-y(z_{2})|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}y^{\prime}(1/7)\,dw\right|-\left|\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}[y^{\prime}(w)-y^{\prime}(1/7)]\,dw\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant y^{\prime}(1/7)|z_{1}-z_{2}|-\frac{y^{\prime}(1/7)}{9}|z_{1}-z_{2}|

\displaystyle=\frac{8y^{\prime}(1/7)}{9}|z_{1}-z_{2}|.

Thus, we see that
|y(z_{1})-y(z_{2})|=0
implies that
z_{1}=z_{2}
.

In the case that the line segment connecting
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
does not lie in
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
, then
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
must have non-zero imaginary parts of opposite sign, so that the line segment connecting
z_{1}
and
\overline{z_{2}}
does indeed lie in
D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
. Moreover, it must be that
{|\operatorname{Arg}(z-1/7)|\in(\pi/4,3\pi/4)}
for
z\!=\!z_{1}
and
z\!=\!\overline{z_{2}}
, and so for all other points
z
on this line segment. Suppose that
{y(z_{1})\!=\!y(z_{2})}
. Then
y(z_{1})=\overline{y(\overline{z_{2}})}
, by the Schwarz Reflection Principle, and so the imaginary part of
y(z)
takes opposite values at the two endpoints of the line segment connecting
z_{1}
and
\overline{z_{2}}
. Since the imaginary part of
y(z)
varies continuously as
z
moves along this line segment, there is a point
w
on the line segment at which
y(w)
is real.
It follows that

\left|\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{y(1/7)-y(w)}{1/7-w}\right)\right|=|\operatorname{Arg}(1/7-w)|\in(\pi/4,3\pi/4),

which contradicts property (2).

We conclude that
y
is injective on
\tilde{\Omega}\cap D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7)
and admits an inverse there, which we call
y^{-1}
. Observe that
y^{-1}
is analytic on
y(D_{\epsilon,\pi/4}(1/7))
, as the inverse of an analytic map
[

, Ch. 9.4]
, and hence
y^{-1}
is analytic on the subset
D_{\epsilon^{\prime},3\pi/8}(1/\rho)
. Moreover,

agrees with
\psi
on
\Lambda\cap D_{\epsilon^{\prime},3\pi/8}(1/\rho)
, by Lemma
4.8
. Consequently, we have, by the principle of permanence, the inverse relationship
(y\circ\psi)|_{D_{\epsilon^{\prime},3\pi/8}(1/\rho)}=\operatorname{Id}|_{D_{\epsilon^{\prime},3\pi/8}(1/\rho)}
, as was to be shown.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

To finish proving that
\psi
can be analytically continued to
\Delta_{1/\rho}
, it suffices, by the compactness of the set

\partial\Lambda\setminus{D_{\epsilon^{\prime},3\pi/8}(1/\rho)}
and in light of Lemma

,
to demonstrate the existence of an analytic continuation of
\psi
around each point in an arbitrary finite subcollection of
\partial\Lambda\setminus{\{1/\rho\}}
.

Let
\tilde{w}\in\partial\Lambda\setminus{\{1/\rho\}}
. The two supporting claims in the proof of Lemma
4.8
imply that
{\psi(\tilde{w})\in\tilde{\Omega}}
. We claim that
y^{\prime}(\psi(\tilde{w}))\neq 0
. To see why this is true, let
\gamma
be the straight line path from
0
to
\tilde{w}
.
If
y^{\prime}(\psi(\tilde{w}))=0
, then, recalling from Lemma
4.8
that

, we see that

y(\psi(w))=w
for

0=\lim_{w\to\tilde{w},w\in\gamma}|y^{\prime}(\psi(w))|=\lim_{w\to\tilde{w},w\in\gamma}1/|\psi^{\prime}(w)|,

which implies that
|\psi^{\prime}(w)|\to\infty
as

along
\gamma
. This contradicts the fact from Lemma
4.7
that
\psi^{\prime}
extends continuously to

and so is bounded there. So

By the inverse function theorem,

is locally invertible at
\psi(\tilde{w})
, and the local inverse extends
\psi
analytically to a neighborhood of
\tilde{w}
that maps into
\tilde{\Omega}
.

We have extended
\psi
to a Delta-domain
\Delta_{1/\rho}
by patching together

inverses of

. To see that
y
is a
global
inverse of
\psi
, one need only observe that
\psi(\Delta_{1/\rho})\subset\tilde{\Omega}
, by construction. It follows that
y
is analytic on
\psi(\Delta_{1/\rho})

{(y\circ\psi)|_{\Delta_{1/\rho}}=\operatorname{Id}|_{\Delta_{1/\rho}}}
. ∎

4.4
Asymptotic singular expansion of
\psi
near
y(1/7)

Let
\Delta_{1/\rho}
be the Delta-domain from Proposition
4.6
.
We apply a bootstrapping procedure to (
4.2
) to derive an asymptotic singular expansion for
\psi=y^{-1}
near the singularity
1/\rho
. The general idea of bootstrapping as well as some examples are discussed in
[
dB81
, Ch. 2]
. Precisely, we show the following, with
\lambda
as in (
4.13
).

Proposition 4.10
.

Set
V=1-\rho z
. The function
\psi
admits the following singular expansion: as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
, we have

\psi(z)=\gamma(V)+CV^{6}\log V+\mathcal{O}(V^{7}\log V),

(4.17)

where

C=\frac{7^{5}K\rho}{6!(7+\rho\lambda)^{7}},

and
\gamma
is a degree-six polynomial with
\gamma(0)=1/7
.
Furthermore, the error term
\psi(z)-\gamma(V)-CV^{6}\log V
is analytic in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
.

Proof.

With
Z=1-7z
, (
4.2
) implies that

y(z)=\frac{1-Z}{7}\left(p(Z)-\frac{K}{6!}Z^{6}\log Z+\mathcal{O}(Z^{7}\log Z)\right),

as
z\to 1/7\in\tilde{\Omega}
. By the identity
(y\circ\psi)|_{\Delta_{1/\rho}}=\operatorname{Id}|_{\Delta_{1/\rho}}
, if we evaluate both sides of the equation at
\psi(z)
, for
z
in a small neighborhood of
1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
,
and if we apply the change of variables
Y=1-7\psi(z)
, then we obtain

\frac{1-Z}{7}=z=y(\psi(z))=\frac{1-Y}{7}\left(p(Y)-\frac{K}{6!}Y^{6}\log(Y)+\mathcal{O}(Y^{7}\log Y)\right),

which implies

Z-1=(Y-1)\left(p(Y)-\frac{K}{6!}Y^{6}\log Y+\mathcal{O}(Y^{7}\log Y)\right),

(4.18)

as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
.
In (
4.9
) we see that
p(Y)=(-K/6!)\log(21/8)Y^{6}+\sum_{n=0}^{6}f_{n}Y^{n}
. Thus,
upon setting

W=\frac{Z+f_{0}-1}{f_{0}-f_{1}},

we find from (
4.18
) that as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
,

Y=W-(Q(Y)+cY^{6}\log Y+\mathcal{O}(Y^{7}\log Y)),

(4.19)

where

\displaystyle Q(Y)=\frac{{f_{1}-f_{2}}}{f_{0}-f_{1}}Y^{2}+\frac{{f_{2}-f_{3}}}{f_{0}-f_{1}}Y^{3}+\frac{{f_{3}-f_{4}}}{f_{0}-f_{1}}Y^{4}+\frac{{f_{4}-f_{5}}}{f_{0}-f_{1}}Y^{5}+\frac{f_{5}-f_{6}+\frac{K}{6!}\log\left(\frac{21}{8}\right)}{f_{0}-f_{1}}Y^{6},

and

c=\frac{K}{6!(f_{0}-f_{1})}=\frac{K}{6!(7/\rho+\lambda)}.

To simplify notation, define the list of constants
(a_{i})_{i=2}^{6}\subset\mathbb{R}
so that
Q(Y)=\sum_{i=2}^{6}a_{i}Y^{i}
.

W(z)
and
Y(z)

tend to
0
as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
. It follows then from (
4.19
) that
W\sim Y
as
z\to 1/\rho
.
Therefore,
Y=\mathcal{O}(W)
and
\log(Y)\sim\log(W)
(as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
, which is assumed for the rest of the proof).
These estimates for
Y
imply that

Q(Y)=a_{2}W^{2}+\mathcal{O}(W^{3}),

which, upon substitution into (
4.19
), yields

Y=W-a_{2}W^{2}+\mathcal{O}(W^{3}).

We substitute this new estimate for
Y
into
Q(Y)
, which yields

Q(Y)=a_{2}W^{2}+(a_{3}-2a_{2}^{2})W^{3}+\mathcal{O}(W^{4}),

and hence,
from (
4.19
),

Y=W-a_{2}W^{2}-(a_{3}-2a_{2}^{2})W^{3}+\mathcal{O}(W^{4}).

After another iteration, we obtain from (
4.19
) that

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=W-Q(Y)+\mathcal{O}(W^{5})

\displaystyle=W-a_{2}W^{2}-(a_{3}-2a_{2}^{2})W^{3}-(5a_{2}^{3}-5a_{2}a_{3}+a_{4}W^{4})+\mathcal{O}(W^{5}).

After a final iteration, we obtain from (
4.19
) that

Y=W-a_{2}W^{2}-\cdots-a_{6}^{\prime}W^{6}+\mathcal{O}(W^{7})-cY^{6}\log Y+\mathcal{O}(Y^{7}\log Y),

where
a_{6}^{\prime}
is an unimportant constant.
Since
Y=W+\mathcal{O}(W^{2})
, we have that the

\mathcal{O}(Y^{7}\log Y)
error term
is
\mathcal{O}(W^{7}\log W)
, and that
Y^{6}\log Y=W^{6}\log W+\mathcal{O}(W^{7}\log W)
.
We can therefore express
Y
in terms of
W
as follows:

Y=W-a_{2}W^{2}-\cdots-a_{6}^{\prime}W^{6}-cW^{6}\log W+\mathcal{O}(W^{7}\log W).

We substitute
1-7\psi(z)
for
Y
in the left side of the latter expression, obtaining

\psi(z)=\frac{1}{7}+\tilde{P}(W)+\frac{c}{7}W^{6}\log(W)+\mathcal{O}(W^{7}\log W),

(4.20)

where
\tilde{P}(W)
is a degree-six polynomial with
\tilde{P}(0)=0
.

To finish the proof, it only remains to rewrite (
4.20
) with
W
in terms of
V
:

W=\frac{Z+f_{0}-1}{f_{0}-f_{1}}=\frac{Z+7/\rho-1}{7/\rho+\lambda}=\frac{7/\rho-7z}{7/\rho+\lambda}=\frac{7}{7+\rho\lambda}(1-\rho z)=\frac{7}{7+\rho\lambda}V.

Substituting the last expression into (
4.20
) leads immediately to (
4.17
), with

\gamma(V)=\frac{1}{7}+\tilde{P}\left(\frac{7}{7+\rho\lambda}V\right),

and

C=\frac{c}{7}\left(\frac{7}{7+\rho\lambda}\right)^{6}=\frac{7^{5}K\rho}{6!(7+\rho\lambda)^{7}}.

The error term in (
4.20
) is analytic in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
since all the other terms are.
∎

4.5
Asymptotic singular expansion of

near

Since
\psi
is injective on
\Delta_{1/\rho}
by Proposition
4.6
, and

, we see that

extends to be analytic on
\Delta_{1/\rho}
. We seek its singular expansion near

C
be the constant from Proposition

Proposition 4.11
.

Set
V=1-\rho z
. The function

admits the following singular expansion:
as
z\to 1/\rho
in
\Delta_{1/\rho}

A(z)=\eta(V)-\frac{49C}{\rho}V^{6}\log V+\mathcal{O}(V^{7}\log V),

(4.21)

where
\eta
is a degree-seven polynomial.
The error term

A(z)+(49C/\rho)V^{6}\log V-\eta(V)
is analytic in
\Delta_{1/\rho}
.

Proof.

Since
A(z)=z/\psi(z)
, we need to obtain a useful expression for the reciprocal of
\psi
. Referring to Proposition
4.10
, let
E(V)
denote the error term in (
4.17
), namely,

E(V):=\psi(z)-\gamma(V)-CV^{6}\log V.

E(V)
is visibly analytic for
z\in\Delta_{1/\rho}
.

Next, define
T(V)
to be the degree-six Taylor approximation polynomial for
1/\gamma(V)
centered at
V=0
, i.e.
\gamma(V)T(V)=1+\mathcal{O}(V^{7})
as
V\to 0
.
Also define
S(V):=T(V)-49CV^{6}\log V
, which is analytic on
\Delta_{1/\rho}

\gamma(0)=1/7
and

\displaystyle\psi(z)S(V)

\displaystyle=(\gamma(V)+CV^{6}\log V+E(V))(T(V)-49CV^{6}\log V)

\displaystyle=1+E(V)T(V)-49CV^{6}\log V\cdot E(V)+\mathcal{O}(V^{7}\log V)

as

.

\mathcal{O}(V^{7}\log V)

\tilde{E}(V):=\psi(z)S(V)-1

We rewrite (

Since

\displaystyle A(z)=\frac{z}{\psi(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-V}{\rho}S(V)-A(z)\tilde{E}(V)

4.6
Asymptotic formula for
(a_{n})

Looking at (

does not contribute to the asymptotics of the sequence

. The asymptotic expression for

Appendix

We record here, as Theorem
4.12
, several “transfer theorems” of Flajolet and Odlyzko
[
FO90
]
, which we use to transfer asymptotic growth estimates of a function
f
near a singularity to asymptotic growth estimates for the function’s Taylor coefficients
(f_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
.

Assume the following setup:
f
is a function analytic at the origin, with radius of convergence
R>0
and Taylor expansion
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}z^{n}
, and
f
can be continued analytically to a “Delta-domain”
\Delta_{R}
, which is defined to be any open set of the form

\{z:|z|<R+\epsilon,|\operatorname{Arg}(z-R)|>\theta\},

for some
\epsilon>0
and some
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
. Here,
\operatorname{Arg}
denotes the principle value of the argument. In particular,
f
has a unique singularity on its disk of convergence, namely the point
R
.
We may refer to
\Delta_{R}
as a “Delta-domain around the disk of convergence of
f
.”
Often in practical applications of these transfer theorems, a function will admit analytic continuation to well-beyond a Delta-domain, e.g. to
\mathbb{C}\setminus{[R,\infty)}
.

Let
\log
denote the principle branch of the logarithm.

Theorem 4.12
.

[
FO90
, Cor. 2 and Thm. 2]

Assume that
f
is analytic in a Delta-domain
\Delta_{R}
.

1.

If
f(z)\sim K(1-z/R)^{\alpha}
as
z\to 1
in
\Delta_{R}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus{\{0,1,2,\dots\}}
, and
K\in\mathbb{C}
, then

f_{n}\sim\frac{1}{R^{n}}\cdot\frac{K}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}n^{-\alpha-1},\hskip 8.50012pt\text{ as }\,n\to\infty.

2.

If
f(z)=\mathcal{O}((1-z/R)^{\alpha}(\log(1-z/R))^{\gamma})
as
z\to R
in
\Delta_{R}
, where
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, then

f_{n}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-\alpha-1}(\log n)^{\gamma}}{R^{n}}\right),\hskip 8.50012pt\text{ as }\,n\to\infty.

3.

As a consequence, if
F(z)=g(z)+f(z)
, for
f
and
g
analytic on
\{z:|z|<R\}
, and furthermore
f(z)
is analytic in a Delta-domain
\Delta_{R}
and satisfies

f(z)=\mathcal{O}\big((1-z/R)^{\alpha}(\log(1-z/R))^{\gamma}\big),

as
z\to R
in
\Delta_{R}
,
then

F_{n}=g_{n}+f_{n}=g_{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-\alpha-1}(\log n)^{\gamma}}{R^{n}}\right),\hskip 8.50012pt\text{ as }\,n\to\infty.

Theorem
4.12
is used in Section
4
to obtain asymptotic estimates for
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, with
{\gamma\!=\!1}
in both cases.
The theorem is actually stated and proved in
[
FO90
]
for the special case
R=1
, and the adjustments for
R>0
are straightforward.
