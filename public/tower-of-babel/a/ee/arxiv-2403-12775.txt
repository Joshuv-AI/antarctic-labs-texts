Bootstrap Percolation on the Binomial Random k k -uniform Hypergraph
Mihyun Kang, Christoph Koch, Tamás Makai

Abstract
. We investigate the behaviour of r r -neighbourhood bootstrap percolation on the binomial k k -uniform random hypergraph H k ​ ( n , p ) H_{k}(n,p) for given integers k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 and r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 . In r r -neighbourhood bootstrap percolation, infection spreads through the hypergraph, starting from a set of initially infected vertices, and in each subsequent step of the process every vertex with at least r r infected neighbours becomes infected. For our analysis the set of initially infected vertices is chosen uniformly at random from all sets of given size. In the regime n − 1 ≪ n k − 2 p ≪ n − 1 / r n^{-1}\ll n^{k-2}p\ll n^{-1/r} we establish a threshold such that if the number of initially infected vertices remains below the threshold, then with high probability only a few additional vertices become infected, while if the number of initially infected vertices exceeds the threshold then with high probability almost every vertex becomes infected. In fact we show that the probability of failure decreases exponentially.



1.
Introduction and main result

The
r
-neighbourhood bootstrap percolation process, which we refer to as
r
-bootstrap percolation from this point on,
was introduced by Chalupa, Leath, and Reich
[
18
]
in the context of magnetic disordered systems.
Since then
r
-bootstrap percolation and its extensions have been used to describe several complex propogation phenomena: from neuronal activity
[
4
,
23
]
to the dynamics of the Ising model at zero temperature
[
27
]
.

In
r
-bootstrap percolation infection spreads over the vertices of a graph, step by step following a deterministic rule. Starting from a set of initially infected vertices, in each subsequent step every uninfected vertex, which has at least
r
infected neighbours, becomes infected. All other vertices remain uninfected. Once a vertex has become infected, it remains infected forever.

Kang, Koch and Makai
[
33
]
introduced an extension of
r
-bootstrap percolation to
hypergraphs
. Starting from a set of initially infected vertices in every subsequent step every uninfected vertex becomes infected if it has at least
r
infected neighbours within the hypergraph, that is, there are at least
r
infected vertices within the set of hyperedges containing the uninfected vertex. (We note that this definition generalises
r
-bootstrap percolation to the hypergraph setting in a weak sense, and a generalisation in a strong sense will be discussed in Section
1.2
.)

When considering
r
-bootstrap percolation the key question is the relation between the set of initially infected vertices and the set of vertices infected by the end of the process.
Janson, Łuczak, Turova, and Vallier
[
32
, Theorem 3.1]
analysed
r
-bootstrap percolation on the binomial random graph
G(n,p)
where every edge appears independently with probability
p
and the set of initially infected vertices, denoted by
A(0)
, is chosen uniformly at random from all vertex sets of size
a
. They showed, among others, that for
r\geq 2
and
n^{-1}\ll p\ll n^{-1/r}
there is a phase transition with threshold
(1-1/r)\left(\tfrac{(r-1)!}{np^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)}
in terms of the number of initially infected vertices. In
[
34
]
, this result was strengthened by providing an exponential bound on the error probability. More precisely, we have the following result on the size of
A_{f}
, the set of vertices which become infected during the percolation process.

Theorem 1

(
[
32
,
34
]
)
.

Given an integer
r\geq 2
, consider
r
-bootstrap percolation on
G(n,p)
when
n^{-1}\ll p\ll n^{-1/r}
. Assume that the initial infection set is chosen uniformly at random from the family of all sets of vertices of size
a=a(n)
. Then for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega\left((np^{r})^{-1/(r-1)}\right)\right)
, the following hold.

(i)

If
a\leq(1-\varepsilon)(1-1/r)\left(\tfrac{(r-1)!}{np^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)}
, then
|A_{f}|=O\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{np^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)}\right)
.

(ii)

If
a\geq(1+\varepsilon)(1-1/r)\left(\tfrac{(r-1)!}{np^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)}
, then
|A_{f}|=(1-o(1))\,n
.

In this paper we generalise this result for the binomial random
k
-uniform hypergraph
H_{k}(n,p)
, where every hyperedge (which is a
k
-element subset of the vertex set
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
) is present independently with probability
p
. In this setting, unlike the graph case, the infection of two different vertices is no longer independent, since hyperedges may contain multiple uninfected vertices. The major challenge lies in understanding these dependencies and analysing their impact on the infection process.

1.1.
Main result

Throughout the paper, for integers
k\geq 2
and
r\geq 2
we set

\eta:=\eta(k,r)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }r\geq 3,\\
2k-3&\mbox{if }r=2,\end{cases}

(1)

and define

a^{*}:=a^{*}(k,r,n,p)=\left(\frac{(r-1)!}{\eta n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)}.

(2)

In addition, we set

a_{c}:=a_{c}(k,r,n,p)=\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)a^{*}.

(3)

Theorem 2
.

Given integers
k\geq 2
and
r\geq 2
, consider
r
-bootstrap percolation on
H_{k}(n,p)
when
n^{-1}\ll n^{k-2}p\ll n^{-1/r}
. Assume the initial infection set is chosen uniformly at random from the family of all sets of vertices of size
a=a(n)
. Then for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(a^{*}))
the following hold.

(i)

If
a\leq(1-\varepsilon)a_{c}
, then
|A_{f}|\leq a^{*}
.

(ii)

If
a\geq(1+\varepsilon)a_{c}
, then
|A_{f}|=(1-o(1))\,n
.

Theorem
2
shows in particular that there is a phase transition with respect to the behaviour of
r
-bootstrap percolation when the number of initially infected vertices reaches
a_{c}
. If the number of initially infected vertices remains below the threshold
a_{c}
, then only a few additional vertices become infected. On the other hand, if the number of initially infected vertices is larger than the threshold, then almost every vertex becomes infected by the end of the percolation process.

1.2.
Related results

Recall that the key question concerning
r
-bootstrap percolation is to establish the behaviour of the final infection set with respect to the set of initially infected vertices.
More precisely the typical questions asked are: when does a random subset lead to (almost) every vertex becoming infected, what is the size of the smallest subset which leads to (almost) every vertex becoming infected, and the running time of the process.
These questions have been investigated over a wide range of graph classes.
Various deterministic graphs have been considered such as trees
[
13
,
26
]
, lattices
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
17
,
30
]
, hypercubes
[
9
]
and expanders
[
19
]
.
Several random graph models have also been investigated, including the binomial random graph
[
7
,
8
,
25
,
32
,
34
,
40
,
41
]
, the Chung-Lu model
[
5
,
6
,
28
]
, preferential attachment graphs
[
1
]
and many others
[
3
,
14
,
16
,
21
,
24
,
35
]
.

Less is known when the underlying structure for
r
-bootstrap percolation is a hypergraph. Recently Cooley and Zalla
[
20
]
analysed the high-order generalisation of
r
-bootstrap percolation in complete
k
-uniform hypergraphs.

A generalisation of
r
-bootstrap percolation to hypergraphs in the strong sense can also be defined. Again starting from a set of infected vertices, in each subsequent step every uninfected vertex
v
becomes infected if it is contained in at least
r
hyperedges, where every vertex other than
v
is infected.
When
r=1
this model was introduced, under the name of hypergraph bootstrap percolation by Balogh, Bollobás, Morris and Riordan
[
12
]
.
In addition to 1-boostrap percolation, hypergraph bootstrap percolation generalises a second bootstrap process called
graph bootstrap percolation
, which was introduced by Bolobás
[
15
]
under the name weak saturation in 1968. Graph bootstrap percolation acts on the edge set of a graph. More precisely, for a given graph
F
, in each step of the process every edge is added which is the last edge required to create a new copy of
F
. The connection between the above two percolation processes can be observed, when the edges in the graph correspond to vertices in the hypergraph and the hyperedges correspond to the edge sets in copies of
F
.

Graph bootstrap percolation, and consequently hypergraph boostrap percolation, have received significant attention: see e.g.,
[
7
,
29
,
36
,
38
]
and the references within.

1.3.
Heuristics for the percolation threshold

In the remainder of the paper we assume that
k,r\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
are given and
n\in\mathbb{N},p\in(0,1)
satisfy

n^{-1}\ll n^{k-2}p\ll n^{-1/r}.

We call a
k
-element subset of
[n]
simply a
k
-set
.

There is a natural heuristic argument for the evolution of the number of infected vertices over time, which leads to the percolation threshold
a_{c}
as in (
3
).

Recall that
A(0)
denotes the set of initially infected vertices. For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
A(t)
the set of vertices infected by the end of step
t
.
Our heuristic begins with an assumption that the evolution of
A(t)
would be well-approximated by a smooth function
\varphi
, whose behaviour characterises the existence of a
bottleneck
in the process. We provide a heuristic suggesting both what
\varphi
should be and why this leads to the choice of
a^{*}
and the percolation threshold
a_{c}
. For simplicity we focus on the case
r\geq 3
. In step
t+1\in\mathbb{N}
, a typical vertex becomes infected due to being contained in exactly
r
hyperedges where each of these hyperedges contains precisely one (distinct) vertex from
A(t)
and in addition at least one of these hyperedges contains a vertex in
A(t)\setminus A(t-1)
. Thus, at least heuristically speaking, the approximation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|A(t+1)|\left|\right.A(t),\ldots,A(0)\right]

\displaystyle\approx|A(t)|+n\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{(|A(t)|-|A(t-1)|)^{i}|A(t-1)|^{r-i}}{i!(r-i)!}\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}

should be valid as long as only a small fraction of the vertices are infected. In fact, the right-hand side simplifies to
|A(t)|+\frac{|A(t)|^{r}-|A(t-1)|^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}
. Iterating yields a telescoping sum and we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[|A(t+1)|\left|\right.A(t),\ldots,A(0)\right]\approx|A(0)|+\frac{|A(t)|^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r},

(4)

where we used
A(-1):=\emptyset
. Assuming further that
|A(t)|
will be well-approximated by its mean we obtain the forward difference equation

|A(\lfloor(x+1/T)T\rfloor)|-|A(\lfloor xT\rfloor)|\approx\frac{|A(\lfloor xT\rfloor)|^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}-|A(\lfloor xT\rfloor)|+|A(0)|,

(5)

where we have rescaled time as
t=\lfloor xT\rfloor
for some (arbitrary)
T=T(n)\gg 1
. Now recall that the
r
-bootstrap percolation process stops if and only if
|A(\lfloor(x+1/T)T\rfloor)|=|A(\lfloor xT\rfloor)|
, in other words, when the forward difference vanishes.
Rescaling by setting
\alpha(x):=|A(\lfloor xT\rfloor)|\left(n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}
, from (
5
) we obtain a simpler form

\alpha(x+1/T)-\alpha(x)\approx\frac{\alpha(x)^{r}}{r!}-\alpha(x)+\alpha(0).

(6)

Now we observe that if
\alpha
were to follow a smooth trajectory
\varphi\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\alpha(x)\approx\varphi(x)
, its derivative
g^{\prime}
should obey
\varphi^{\prime}(x)\approx\alpha(x+1/T)-\alpha(x)
and thus
\varphi
should satisfy the following differential equation

\varphi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\varphi(x)^{r}}{r!}-\varphi(x)+\varphi(0),

(7)

where

\varphi(0)=|A(0)|\left(n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}.

(8)

As mentioned before the process stops, when
\varphi^{\prime}(x)=0
and in the following we will examine for which values of
\varphi(0)
does
\varphi^{\prime}(x)
have a non-negative root.

In order to achieve this we will examine whether the process has a bottleneck, to be characterised by an inflection point, which would translate to an extremal point of
\varphi
satisfying
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x_{c})=0
.
By differentiating
\varphi^{\prime}(x)
once more, we obtain

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\left(\frac{\varphi(x)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}-1\right)\varphi^{\prime}(x).

Recalling our assumption that
\alpha(x)\approx\varphi(x)
and the fact that
\alpha(\cdot)
is positive and non-decreasing, we would also assume
\varphi^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
. In addition we expect the process to stop if
\varphi^{\prime}(x)=0
, so we are only interested in the behaviour of the function as long as
\varphi^{\prime}(x)>0
.
Therefore
the bottleneck point should be defined as the earliest point in time such that
\frac{\varphi(x)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}-1=0
, i.e.,

x_{c}:=\inf\left\{x\geq 0\colon\varphi(x)=\left[(r-1)!\right]^{1/(r-1)}\right\}.

As
r
-bootstrap percolation is monotone in the number of initially infected vertices, intuitively the threshold for almost every vertex becoming infected should be when the process stops right as it reaches the bottleneck. That is,

\frac{\varphi(x_{c})^{r}}{r!}-\varphi(x_{c})+\varphi(0)=0.

As we are assuming the bottleneck exists, that is,
x_{c}<\infty
, a simple calculation implies that this occurs when

\varphi(0)=\varphi_{c}:=\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\left[(r-1)!\right]^{1/(r-1)}.

In addition, the above implies that, if
\varphi(0)\leq\varphi_{c}
in (
7
) then
\varphi^{\prime}(x)
has at least one non-negative root, while if
\varphi(0)>\varphi_{c}
no such root exists.
Using (
8
), ultimately the above indicates a threshold for
r
-bootstrap percolation at

a_{c}=\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\left(\frac{(r-1)!}{n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}}\right)^{1/(r-1)},

matching the result from (
3
).

1.4.
Proof outline

When the number of infected vertices is small, that is,
o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
, typically infection occurs when
r
hyperedges intersect in exactly one uninfected vertex and each of these hyperedges contains exactly one infected vertex. For any
r\geq 2
this implies that the vertex in the intersection of the hyperedges becomes infected. However, if
r=2
, then in the following step both of these hyperedges will contain 2 infected vertices and thus every other vertex in these hyperedges will be infected as well, leading to
2k-3
infected vertices in total instead of one and to the definition of
\eta
in (
1
).

In the subcritical regime, when
a<a_{c}
,
our aim is to show that at most
a^{*}
vertices become infected.
We start in Section
3
by modifying the process, in a manner that leads to more infected vertices (Lemma
17
), but is easier to analyse.

In Section
4
we analyse the behaviour of this modified process.
The FKG inequality (Theorem
6
) implies that the number of vertices infected in a step is stochastically dominated by the sum of weighted Bernoulli random variables (Corollary
21
).
Next we provide a trajectory, which resembles the solution of the differential equation in (
7
), and prove that it also has a maximum (Claim
24
). As long as the expected number of vertices infected in each step is large, which occurs in the early stages of the process, we analyse the process step by step. In every step we show that, provided the number of infected vertices remained below the trajectory in each of the previous steps this will also hold in the following step with sufficiently large probability (Lemma
26
).

When the number of vertices infected in a single step is small this strategy no longer provides sufficiently tight probability bounds and we establish an upper bound on the total number of vertices infected after this point. Note that any vertex which becomes infected in step
t+1
must have a neighbour which became infected in step
t
.
Based on this we can construct a forest, by selecting exactly one neighbouring vertex which was infected in the previous step. This forest is generated by a branching process, where the number of offspring for the individual vertices depends on the size of the previous generations, that is, the previously infected vertices. Nevertheless, based on the Dwass identity for Galton-Watson branching processes (Theorem
5
) we show
that the total number of infected vertices remains small (Lemma
28
).

In the supercritical regime, when
a>a_{c}
, we differentiate between the vertices depending on the number of infected neighbours it has, more precisely we consider the sets of vertices with at least
0,\ldots,r-1,r
infected neighbours. Then the set of infected vertices is the union of the set of initially infected vertices and the set of vertices with at least
r
infected neighbours. Similarly to the subcritical regime we establish a trajectory for the number of vertices in each of these parts, where the trajectory for the number of infected vertices resembles (
7
). As a first step we show that until
(n^{k-2}p)^{-1}
vertices become infected the number of vertices in each part remain above their respective trajectories with sufficiently large probability. Note that the number of additional infected neighbours the individual vertices acquire in a given step are dependent random variables.
We adapt a Chernoff-type bound for
dependent
random variables (Lemma
4
) to establish the probability that the number of vertices in the individual parts remain above their trajectories after every step (Lemma
36
). The number of infected vertices will eventually exceed
(n^{k-2}p)^{-1}
(Lemma
33
), at which point our estimates break down. A slightly modified versions of the concentration result (Lemma
37
) and a martingale argument (Lemma
38
) imply that once the number of infected vertices reaches
(n^{k-2}p)^{-1}
almost every vertex becomes infected in the following two steps with sufficiently high probability. These results are shown in Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

The following concentration result of McDiarmid
[
37
]
will be used throughout the paper.

Theorem 3
.

[
37
, Theorem 2.7]

Let
(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n})
be a sequence of independent random variables. Define
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. If there exists an
M\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
X_{i}\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]+M
, then for all
\vartheta\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}[X\geq\mathbb{E}[X]+\vartheta]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2\left(\mathrm{Var}(X)+M\vartheta/3\right)}\right).

The above inequality holds for independent random variables. We can prove that a similar bound also holds for
dependent
Bernoulli random variables satisfying mild conditions, which strengthens an earlier result due to Panconesi and Srinivasan
[
39
]
.

Lemma 4
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
let
X_{i}
be (not necessarily independent) Bernoulli random variables. Assume that there exists a constant
\lambda>0
and a set of independent Bernoulli random variables
\widehat{X}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
such that for every
I\subseteq[n]
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}\right]\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in I}\widehat{X}_{i}\right].

Define
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\widehat{X}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{X}_{i}
. Then, for all
\vartheta\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}[X\geq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]+\vartheta]\leq\lambda\exp\left(-\frac{\vartheta^{2}}{2(\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})+\vartheta/3)}\right).

The proof of this result can be found in Appendix
A
.
The following result on the total number of progeny in a branching process also plays an important role.

Theorem 5

(Dwass Identity)
.

[
22
]

For an arbitrary Galton-Watson process let
Z_{i}
denote the number of individuals in the
i
-th generation
and let
Z:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}Z_{i}
. Then, for all integers
m,\ell\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}[Z=m|Z_{0}=\ell]=\frac{\ell}{m}\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}=m-\ell|Z_{0}=m].

We also apply the FKG inequality (see e.g.,
[
31
, Theorem 2.12]
) several times in our proofs. A hypergraph property is called
increasing
if it is preserved under the addition of hyperedges, and it is
decreasing
if it is preserved under the removal of hyperedges.

Theorem 6

(FKG inequality)
.

Let
A
be an increasing hypergraph property and
B
be a decreasing hypergraph property. Denote by
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
the events that, in the random
k
-uniform hypergraph where each hyperedge is selected independently with some probability
p_{e}
has property
A
and
B
, respectively. Then we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}]\leq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}].

Finally we will need the well-known Azuma’s inequality (see e.g.,
[
31
, Theorem 2.25]
)

Theorem 7

(Azuma’s inequality)
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a real-valued supermartingale and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
|M_{i}-M_{i-1}|\leq c_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq N
. Then for all
\vartheta\geq 0

3.
Preliminaries for subcritical regime: the query-process

In Sections
3
and
4
we consider the subcritical case, when the initial number of infected vertices is below
a_{c}
. More precisely, throughout these sections we assume that a constant
0<\varepsilon<1
is given and the size of the initial infection set satisfies

a:=|A(0)|=(1-\varepsilon)a_{c}.

In order to simplify the analysis, we modify the process slightly, in such a way that the modified process provides a
superset
of the
set of vertices infected in the original process (Section
3.1
).
We will refer to this modified process as the
query-process
.
The query-process queries collections of
k
-sets, where we establish whether every
k
-set in a collection is a hyperedge or not. Vertices become infected only if every hyperedge within a collection is present.

We show that starting from the same infection set every vertex which is infected via
r
-bootstrap percolation is also infected by the query-process (Lemma
17
). We also establish that the trajectory for the number of infected vertices in the query-process remains below
a^{*}
(Lemma
25
).

The main advantage of the modification is that the probability whether every hyperedge in a collection is present in the hypergraph is stochastically dominated by a sequence of independent random variables, which depend only on the hyperedge probability
p
and the number of hyperedges in the collection (Lemma
20
) and thus the number of infected vertices is stochastically dominated by the sum of weighted Bernoulli random variables (Lemma
21
).
However, the number of
queries
depends on the history of the process, more precisely it depends on the number of vertices infected in the previous step and the total number of vertices infected until this step. Based on this we can show that, as long as the number of infected vertices remains below a certain trajectory in each of the previous steps and the expected number of infected vertices is large enough, the number of infected vertices in the next step will also remain below the trajectory (Lemma
26
).

However, once the expected number of infected vertices becomes small, the previous method no longer provides a sufficiently tight concentration. Note that any vertex which becomes infected in step
t
must have a neighbour which became infected in step
t-1
.
In fact, once the number of vertices infected in a given step is sufficiently small compared to the total number of infected vertices, a typical vertex will have exactly one such neighbour. Based on this we can construct a forest, by connecting every infected vertex to exactly one of its neighbours which was infected during the previous step. This forest is generated by a process resembling a branching process, with the main difference that the number of offspring depends on the size of the previous generations. Using the Dwass identity (Theorem
5
) we show that the number of vertices which become infected during the remaining stages of the process is small (Lemmas
28
).

3.1.
An upper coupling

We start by describing an upper coupling, which we call the
query-process
, informally and thereby introducing terminologies that will be used later.

The query-process is also an infection process which starts out from a set of initially infected vertices.
At the beginning of each step we construct multiple families, where each family is a collection of
k
-sets.
The rest of the step is divided into rounds, where in each round we
examine
one collection in the family. First it is decided whether the collection of
k
-sets will be
queried
based on the results of all the previous queries made during the current and the previous steps. If a collection is not queried then it is
discarded
. A query is
successful
if every
k
-set in the collection is a hyperedge. Every hyperedge within a successful query is called
exposed
.

Now we turn to the construction of the family of collections of
k
-sets. Essentially our aim is to infect every vertex which has at least
r
infected neighbours or this should happen if every collection in the family was queried. In addition, certain collections are added to the family in order to compensate for vertices with
r
infected neighbours, which can be found in discarded collections. The constructions are displayed in Figure
1
.

We start by adding every collection of
r
many
k
-sets which intersect in exactly one vertex
v
and each
k
-set contains exactly one previously infected vertex. We call such a collection a
star
collection (e.g., the triple of the dotted
k
-sets in Figure
1
(a). Any successful query of a star collection will result either in every vertex within the
k
-set becoming infected (when
r=2
) or in the vertex
v
becoming infected (when
r\geq 3
). In a subcritical query-process
almost every vertex infected in a given step is contained in a star.

Now if a vertex has
r
infected neighbours, which are not in a star collection, then either two of the hyperedges overlap in more than one vertex or a hyperedge contains multiple infected vertices.

We add every collection of pairs of
k
-sets which overlap in at least 2 uninfected vertices and both hyperedges contain at least one infected vertex. Such a collection is called a
widely-overlapping
collection (e.g., the pair of the dotted
k
-sets in Figure
1

We will introduce a final criteria to reduce the number of times a collection of

If none of the collections has been discarded, or equivalently if every collection was queried, then every vertex which has at least

3.4.
Coupling
r
-boostrap percolation and the query-process

In this section we show that every vertex infected by

Roughly speaking the query-process focuses on adding the vertices infected through star collections one at a time. Recall that a star collection consists of

. Now
\mathcal{C}
will be contained in the family
\mathcal{S}(t)
for some
t\geq 1
. Let
\tau
be the first time this holds and without loss of generality assume that
\mathcal{C}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
.
A successful query of
\mathcal{C}
would have infected
u
, implying that either one of the
e_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
has been exposed previously (line
1
) or one of the
e_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
intersects an exposed hyperedge in an uninfected vertex (line
1
).

Now if
e_{i}
is exposed and
u
is uninfected, then every other hyperedge
e_{j}
will become a neutron-star-adjacent collection in some later step. Formally, for every
1\leq j\leq\ell
with
j\neq i
there is some step
t\geq 1
such that
e_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(t)
for some
t\geq 1
, contradicting Claim
18
. On the other hand, if
e_{i}
intersects a hyperedge in an uninfected vertex, then we have that
e_{i}\in\mathcal{N}(t)
for some
t\geq 1
, again contradicting Claim
18
. We conclude that no subset of
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}
contains
r
hyperedges which intersect only in
u
.

We have already established that each hyperedge in
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}
has at most one vertex in
B_{f}
. Together with the fact that other than
u
any pair of hyperedges in
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}
can intersect only in infected vertices, and no
r
of them are vertex disjoint except for
u
implies that
u
has less than
r
infected neighbours at the end of the process, contradicting our assumption that
u
has at least
r
infected neighbours.
∎

4.
Subcritical regime

4.1.
Properties of the query-process

In the following we examine the behaviour of the query-process when the underlying graph is the binomial random
k
-uniform hypergraph
H_{k}(n,p)
with vertex set
V=[n]
and hyperedge set
E
.
Recall that we only query collections, where none of the hyperedges have been exposed before. However the query may contain unexposed hyperedges which have been part of unsuccessfully queried collections.
Intuitively, if a collection contains
k
-sets which were part of an unsuccessful query, then the probability that the current query is successful decreases.
In the following lemma, we make this argument rigorous by providing an upper bound for the probability of a successful query, in a more general setting, which is independent of the result of all previous queries.

Lemma 20
.

Consider a process where collections of
k
-sets are subsequently queried, with a restriction that no queried collection may contain any
k
-set from a previously successfully queried collection, as in Algorithm 1. Then, conditional on the results of all previous queries, the probability that a collection
\mathcal{C}
of
k
-sets is successfully queried is at most
p^{|\mathcal{C}|}
.

Proof.

Denote by
E_{1},\ldots,E_{\ell}
the collections of
k
-sets which were unsuccessfully queried before
\mathcal{C}
was examined and by
S_{1},\ldots,S_{j}
the
k
-sets which were part of a successful query before
\mathcal{C}
was examined. Let
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}S_{i}
and define
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i}\backslash S
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. Since
E_{i}
was unsuccessfully queried we have
E_{i}\setminus S\neq\emptyset
.
The event
E_{i}\subseteq E
and
E_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq E
are denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{i}
and
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
respectively and define
\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{i}}
. Finally let
\mathcal{S}
be the event that
S\subseteq E
.

Note that for every
1\leq i\leq\ell
we have
\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}=\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{i}}
and thus
\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
.
In addition,
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E
and
\mathcal{S}
are increasing events, while the event
\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
is decreasing. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E,\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E,\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E,\mathcal{S}]\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle(\text{by FKG (Theorem~\ref{thm:FKG})}).

Using the fact that
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}S_{i}
is disjoint of both the collection
\mathcal{C}
and every
E_{i}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E,\mathcal{S}]\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S}]\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle(\text{because }\mathcal{C}\cap S=\emptyset)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle(\text{because }S\cap E_{i}^{\prime}=\emptyset).

Again using
\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subseteq E]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}]=p^{|\mathcal{C}|}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}].

We conclude that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}\subset E,\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}]\leq p^{|\mathcal{C}|}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}],

and result follows after dividing both sides by
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{S},\overline{\mathcal{E}}]
.
∎

Using the previous lemma we estimate the distribution of the infected vertices.

Corollary 21
.

Given
\mathcal{N}(t+1),\mathcal{H}(t+1),\mathcal{W}(t+1)
and
\mathcal{S}(t+1)
, the number of vertices infected in step
t+1
is stochastically dominated by

k^{2}\mathrm{Bin}(N(t+1),p)+k\mathrm{Bin}(H(t+1),p)+2k\mathrm{Bin}(W(t+1),p^{2})+\eta\mathrm{Bin}(S(t+1),p^{r}),

where
\eta:=(2k-3)\mathbbm{1}_{r=2}+\mathbbm{1}_{r\geq 3}
is as defined in (
1
).

Proof.

Lemma
20
implies that the number of successful queries within the individual families are stochastically dominated by
\mathrm{Bin}(N(t+1),p),\mathrm{Bin}(H(t+1),p),\mathrm{Bin}(W(t+1),p^{2})
and
\mathrm{Bin}(S(t+1),p^{r})
, respectively. A successful query leads to different number of infected vertices depending on which family the queried collection of
k
-sets was contained in. Since the hyperedges of
F(t)
overlap only in infected vertices we have that any
k
-set not contained in
F(t)
can share an uninfected vertex with at most
k-1
hyperedges in
F(t)
and thus every neutron-star-adjacent collection (comprised of a single
k
-set by definition), causes at most
k^{2}
vertices to become infected, after a successful query. When considering a heavily-infected collection (comprised of a single
k
-set by definition) only the vertices within the
k
-set become infected leading to at most
k
infected vertices, in case of a successful query. Any widely-over-lapping collection (comprised of two
k
-sets by definition) contains at most
2k
vertices, all of which are infected, after a successful query. Because
\eta=2k-3
when
r=2
and
\eta=1
when
r\geq 3
, the successful query of a star collection leads to exactly
\eta
infected vertices for any
r\geq 2
.
∎

4.2.
Bounding trajectory

In the following we establish the expected trajectory for the number of infected vertices.
Recall that

a:=|A(0)|=|B(0)|=(1-\varepsilon)a_{c}.

Throughout the remainder of this section we assume that
\delta>0
satisfies

1/(1+\delta)>(1-\varepsilon)^{r-1}.

For convenience of notation define
B(-1)=\emptyset
.
Based on the heuristic that (
4
) holds, and under the assumption that in every step the number of infected vertices exceeds its expectation by at most a
(1+\delta)
factor, we define the following bounding trajectory.

Definition 22

(Bounding trajectory)
.

We define the sequence
b(t)
by setting
b(-1)=0
,
b(0)=|B(0)|
and for each integer
t\geq 0

\displaystyle b(t+1)

\displaystyle:=(1+\delta)\eta\frac{b(t)^{r}}{r!}n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}+b(0).

Moreover, for every integer
t\geq-1
we set

\beta(t):=\frac{b(t)}{a^{*}},

where
a^{*}
is as defined in (
2
).
We call the function
\beta
the
trajectory associated to the query-process
.

In the following we express
\beta
using a recursion.

Claim 23
.

For every integer
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\beta(t+1)=(1+\delta)\frac{\beta(t)^{r}}{r}+\beta(0).

Proof.

By Definition
22
we have

\displaystyle\beta(t+1)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)\eta\frac{\beta(t)^{r}(a^{*})^{r-1}}{r!}n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}+\beta(0)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:scaling}}}{{=}}(1+\delta)\eta\frac{\beta(t)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{(r-1)!}{\eta n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}}\right)n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}+\beta(0)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)\frac{\beta(t)^{r}}{r}+\beta(0).

∎

Based on the heuristic in Section
1.3
we expect
\beta(t)
to also have a maximum. We will prove a slightly weaker statement, namely that
\beta\leq x_{0}
where
x_{0}
is the smallest positive solution of the equation:

x_{0}=(1+\delta)\frac{x_{0}^{r}}{r}+\beta(0).

(9)

First we prove that a solution exists.

Claim 24
.

If
\beta(0)=(1-\varepsilon)(1-1/r)
, then
x_{0}
, defined in (
9
), satisfies

\beta(0)<x_{0}<(1+\delta)^{-1/(r-1)}<1.

Proof.

First we observe that the function
h(x):=x-(1+\delta)x^{r}/r
is strictly increasing and continuous on the interval
(0,(1+\delta)^{-1/(r-1)})
. Since
h(\beta(0))\leq\beta(0)
and

h\left(\left(\frac{1}{1+\delta}\right)^{1/(r-1)}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{1+\delta}\right)^{1/(r-1)}\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)>(1-\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)=\beta(0),

such a solution exists.
∎

Now we can show that
\beta(t)<x_{0}
for every
t\geq 0
, and thus by Claim
24
we have
b(t)\leq a^{*}x_{0}<a^{*}
.

Lemma 25
.

Assume that
\beta(t)\leq(1-\xi)x_{0}
for some
0<\xi<1
. Then there exists a
0<\xi^{\prime}<1
such that
\beta(t+1)\leq(1-\xi^{\prime})x_{0}
.

Proof.

By Claim
23
and
\beta(t)\leq(1-\xi)x_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\beta(t+1)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)\frac{\beta(t)^{r}}{r}+\beta(0)\leq(1+\delta)\frac{(1-\xi)^{r}x_{0}^{r}}{r}+\beta(0).

Adding and subtracting
(1-\xi)^{r}\beta(0)
and using the definition of
x_{0}
from (
9
) gives

\displaystyle(1+\delta)\frac{(1-\xi)^{r}x_{0}^{r}}{r}+\beta(0)

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)^{r}\left((1+\delta)\frac{x_{0}^{r}}{r}+\beta(0)\right)+(1-(1-\xi)^{r})\beta(0)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:boundx}}}{{=}}(1-\xi)^{r}x_{0}+(1-(1-\xi)^{r})\beta(0).

Using
\beta(0)\leq\beta(t)\leq(1-\xi)x_{0}
and some simple algebra leads to

\displaystyle(1-\xi)^{r}x_{0}+(1-(1-\xi)^{r})\beta(0)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\xi)^{r}x_{0}+(1-(1-\xi)^{r})(1-\xi)x_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi(1-(1-\xi)^{r}))x_{0}.

Since
0<\xi<1
, we have
0<1-(1-\xi)^{r}<1
, and setting
\xi^{\prime}=\xi(1-(1-\xi)^{r})
completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Remaining below the trajectory

We have established that the trajectory for the number of infected vertices remains below
a^{*}
. In the remainder of the section we show that, with sufficiently high probability, the number of infected vertices remains bounded from above by the trajectory.

We start by showing that as long as the number of infected vertices does not significantly exceed its expectation in each of the previous steps, this also holds with sufficiently high probability in the following step. Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}
denote the event that for every
0<\tau\leq t
we have
|B(\tau)|-|B(\tau-1)|\leq b(\tau)-b(\tau-1)
.

Lemma 26
.

For every
t\geq 1
, conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{t}

we have

|B(t+1)|-|B(t)|\leq b(t+1)-b(t)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-\delta^{2}(b(t+1)-b(t))/(2k^{2}))
.

Proof.

We start by establishing an upper bound on the expected number of infected vertices.
By Claim
24
and Lemma
25
we have

b(t)\binom{n}{k-2}p\leq a^{*}\binom{n}{k-2}p=\Theta\left(\left(n\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{-1/(r-1)}\right)=o(1).

(10)

In addition, by Definition
22
and
b(0)\leq b(t)\leq b(t+1)
we have

\displaystyle b(t+1)-b(t)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)\eta\frac{b(t)^{r}-b(t-1)^{r}}{r!}n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(b(t)-b(t-1)\right)\Omega\left(b(0)^{r-1}n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}\right).

Recall that
b(0)=|A(0)|=\Omega(a^{*})
. Thus by (
2
)

\displaystyle b(t+1)-b(t)

\displaystyle=\left(b(t)-b(t-1)\right)\Omega\left((a^{*})^{r-1}n\left[\binom{n}{k-2}p\right]^{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(b(t)-b(t-1)).

(11)

In the following we consider the expected number of successful queries in each of the individual families.

We start with the family of neutron-star-adjacent collections. By Observation
16
we have

N(t+1)p\leq 2rk(|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|)|B(t)|\binom{n}{k-2}p.

By assumption we have
|B(\tau)|-|B(\tau-1)|\leq b(\tau)-b(\tau-1)
for every
\tau\leq t
implying that
|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|\leq b(t)-b(t-1)
and
B(t)\leq b(t)
. Therefore

N(t+1)p=O\left((b(t)-b(t-1))b(t)\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:exnn}}}{{=}}o(b(t)-b(t-1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sameorder}}}{{=}}o(b(t+1)-b(t)).

The following two calculations are done similarly.
When considering the number of heavily-infected collections we start from Observation
14
, which implies

\displaystyle H(t+1)p

\displaystyle\leq(|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|)|B(t)|\binom{n}{k-2}p=o(b(t+1)-b(t)).

In addition, if we consider widely-overlapping pairs of
k
-sets, then by Observation
13
we have

\displaystyle W(t+1)p^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|B(t)|(|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|)n^{2}\binom{n}{k-3}^{2}p^{2}=o\left(b(t+1)-b(t)\right).

Finally we consider stars. Similarly as before, by assumption we have
|B(\tau)|-|B(\tau-1)|\leq b(\tau)-b(\tau-1)
for every
\tau\leq t
and thus
|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|\leq b(t)-b(t-1)
,
B(t)\leq b(t)
and
B(t-1)\leq b(t-1)
. Therefore

\displaystyle|B(t)|^{r}-|B(t-1)|^{r}

\displaystyle=(|B(t)|-|B(t-1)|)\sum_{\rho=0}^{r-1}|B(t)|^{\rho}|B(t-1)|^{r-1-\rho}

\displaystyle\leq(b(t)-b(t-1))\sum_{\rho=0}^{r-1}b(t)^{\rho}b(t-1)^{r-1-\rho}

\displaystyle=b(t)^{r}-b(t-1)^{r}.

Together with Observation
12
this implies

\displaystyle S(t+1)p^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|B(t)|^{r}-|B(t-1)|^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}\right)^{r}p^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b(t)^{r}-b(t-1)^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}\right)^{r}p^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{b(t+1)-b(t)}{(1+\delta)\eta},

where the last equality follows from Definition
22
.

By Corollary
21
we have that
|B(t+1)|-|B(t)|
is stochastically dominated by

k^{2}\mathrm{Bin}(N(t+1),p)+k\mathrm{Bin}(H(t+1),p)+2k\mathrm{Bin}(W(t+1),p^{2})+\eta\mathrm{Bin}(S(t+1),p^{r}).

Together with the previous calculations this implies
\mathbb{E}[|B(t+1)|-|B(t)|]\leq(1+o(1))(b(t+1)-b(t))/(1+\delta)

and applying Theorem
3
gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[(|B(t+1)|-|B(t)|)\geq b(t+1)-b(t)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1+o(1))\delta^{2}(b(t+1)-b(t))^{2}}{2((b(t+1)-b(t))+k^{2}(b(t+1)-b(t))/3)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}(b(t+1)-b(t))}{2k^{2}}\right),

as required.
∎

The previous Lemma provides sufficiently high probabilities when the expected number of vertices infected in a given step is large, that is,
\Omega(a^{*})
. However, the expected number of infected vertices decreases gradually and thus after a while it will be impossible to establish tight concentration on a step by step basis, and we will require a different method.
In particular we approximate the newly infected vertices with a Galton-Watson branching process and show that it dies out quickly using the Dwass identity. We start by providing an upper bound on the size of a Galton-Watson process.

Lemma 27
.

Consider a Galton-Watson process where the number of offspring is the sum of weighted Bernoulli random variables, that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}Be(p_{i})
, such that
\mu:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}p_{i}<1
for
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
p_{i}\in(0,1)
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Set
M=\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}w_{i}
and denote by
Z_{j}
the number of individuals in the
j
-th generation and by
Z
the total number of individuals in the process. Then for any
\chi\geq\mu/(1-\mu)
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left[Z>(1+\chi)\ell\mid Z_{0}=\ell\right]\leq\frac{\exp\left(-(1-(1+\chi)^{-1}-\mu)^{2}(1+\chi)\ell/(3M)\right)}{1-\exp\left(-(1-(1+\chi)^{-1}-\mu)^{2}(1+\chi)/(3M)\right)}.

Proof.

By the Dwass identity (Theorem
5
) we have that for any
m>(1+\chi)\ell
,

\mathbb{P}\left[Z=m\mid Z_{0}=\ell\right]=\frac{\ell}{m}\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}=m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right]<\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}=m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right],

where the last inequality is due to
\frac{\ell}{m}<\frac{1}{1+\chi}\leq 1-\mu<1
, and thus

\mathbb{P}\left[Z>(1+\chi)\ell\mid Z_{0}=\ell\right]\leq\sum_{m>(1+\chi)\ell}\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}=m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right].

Using
m>(1+\chi)\ell
and that the expected number of offspring in the branching process is
\mu
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}=m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}\geq m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}-\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\mid Z_{0}=m\right]\geq m-\ell-\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\mid Z_{0}=m\right]\mid Z_{0}=m\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}-\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\mid Z_{0}=m\right]\geq m-\frac{1}{1+\chi}m-\mu m\mid Z_{0}=m\right].

Since
\chi\geq\mu/(1-\mu)
we have

1-\frac{1}{1+\chi}-\mu\geq 0

and thus Theorem
3
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{1}=m-\ell\mid Z_{0}=m\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-(1+\chi)^{-1}-\mu)^{2}}{3M}m\right),

where we use
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1}\mid Z_{0}=m)\leq M\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\mid Z_{0}=m\right]\leq Mm.

∎

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is the event that for every
0<\tau\leq t
we have
|B(\tau)|-|B(\tau-1)|\leq b(\tau)-b(\tau-1)
.

Lemma 28
.

Set
\chi=4/\delta
. Let
t_{0}
be such that
b(t_{0}-1)=(1-\xi)x_{0}a^{*}
where

\left[\left(\frac{1+\delta}{1+\delta/2}\right)^{1/(r-1)}-1\right]\frac{1}{\chi}>\xi.

(12)

Then

\mathbb{P}\left[|B_{f}|\geq\frac{a^{*}}{(1+\delta/2)^{1/(r-1)}}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t_{0}}\right]\leq\exp\Big(-\Omega\big(b(t_{0})-b(t_{0}-1)\big)\Big).

Proof.

By Lemma
25
and Definition
22
we have
b(t)\leq x_{0}a^{*}
for every
t\geq 0
, which together with
b(t_{0}-1)=(1-\xi)x_{0}a^{*}
implies
b(t_{0})-b(t_{0}-1)\leq\xi x_{0}a^{*}
, thus

b(t_{0}-1)+(1+\chi)(b(t_{0})-b(t_{0}-1))\leq(1-\xi)x_{0}a^{*}+(1+\chi)\xi x_{0}a^{*}=(1+\xi\chi)x_{0}a^{*}.

(13)

Using the upper bound on
x_{0}
from Claim
24
and (
12
) gives

\displaystyle(1+\xi\chi)x_{0}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Clm~\ref{lem:boundx}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1+\xi\chi}{(1+\delta)^{1/(r-1)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:xicond}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{(1+\delta/2)^{r-1}}.

Together with (
13
) this implies

b(t_{0}-1)+(1+\chi)(b(t_{0})-b(t_{0}-1))\leq\frac{a^{*}}{(1+\delta/2)^{r-1}}=:a^{\prime}.

(14)

Note that if
\mathcal{G}_{t_{0}}
holds then
B(t_{0}-1)\leq b(t_{0}-1)
,
therefore an upper bound on the probability that the total number of vertices infected after step
t_{0}-1
is at least
(1+\chi)(b(t_{0})-b(t_{0}-1))
is an upper bound on the probability that
|B_{f}|\geq a^{\prime}
.

Forest construction.
We construct a forest from the vertices in
B_{f}\backslash B(t_{0}-1)
. The roots of the individual trees in the forest are the vertices in
B(t_{0})\setminus B(t_{0}-1)
. For
t\geq t_{0}
we will assign each vertex infected in step
t+1
, that is, each vertex in the set
B(t+1)\backslash B(t)
, to at least one vertex infected in the previous step, that is, a vertex in
B(t)\backslash B(t-1)
, and then discard some edges to transform this into a tree.

In order to achieve this we will consider subsets of
\mathcal{S}(t+1),\mathcal{W}(t+1),\mathcal{H}(t+1)
and
\mathcal{N}(t+1)
associated to one vertex
u\in B(t)\setminus B(t-1)
as follows:

•

\mathcal{S}_{u}(t+1)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(t+1)
, where one of the
k
-sets in the collection contains
u
;

•

\mathcal{W}_{u}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{W}(t)
, where one of the
k
-sets in the pair contains
u
;

•

\mathcal{H}_{u}(t+1)\subseteq\mathcal{H}(t)
, where the
k
-set contains
u
;

•

\mathcal{N}_{u}(t+1)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(t+1)
be the set of
k
-sets satisfying one of the following conditions:

–

K\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(t+1)
and
u\in K
;

–

K\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(t+1)
and there exists
e\in F(t)\setminus F(t-1)
such that
K\cap e\cap B(t)^{C}\neq\emptyset
and
u\in e

5.
supercritical regime

In this section we turn our attention to the supercritical regime of Theorem
2
. Throughout this section we assume that a constant
0<\varepsilon<1
is given and the size of the initial infection set satisfies

a:=|A(0)|=(1+\varepsilon)a_{c}.

Similarly to the previous section we define a new process, called
mild
r
-bootstrap
percolation, which will infect overall fewer vertices than the original
r
-bootstrap percolation. In particular, in each step we activate the right amount of vertices from the set of infected vertices, which have never been active before, and only expose the hyperedges which contain exactly one vertex which was activated in this step and no additional infected vertices. When
r\geq 3
, for a given vertex we count the number of its active neighbours within the hypergraph spanned by the hyperedges exposed so far and infect it, when the number of active neighbours reaches
r
. When
r=2
the situation is slightly different. If a vertex has two active neighbours within the hyperedges exposed so far, then obviously it becomes infected.
Following the rules of
r
-bootstrap percolation, this implies that every vertex within its neighbourhood, with respect to the hyperedges exposed so far, would also become infected, as every hyperedge contains an active vertex which is infected. Therefore in each step we add every vertex which has two active neighbours, and every vertex in its neighbourhood, within
the hyperedges exposed until this step.

The majority of the proof for the supercritical regime of Theorem
2
considers the case when the number of infected vertices is small, that is,
o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
.
In order to establish the probability that a given vertex becomes infected, we will not only need to track the number of infected vertices, but also the number of vertices which have at least
i
active neighbours for
0\leq i\leq r
.
We start by establishing some properties of mild
r
-bootstrap percolation within one step. Then we establish for each
i
the trajectory for the number of vertices with at least
i
active neighbours and do similarly for the number of infected vertices. In addition, we prove that the typical behaviour of the process in fact follows these trajectories as long as the number of infected vertices remains small (Lemma
36
), which guarantees a steady increase in the number of infected vertices, until roughly
(n^{k-2}p)^{-1}
vertices become infected. After this point the process finishes in 2 steps (Lemmas
37
and
38
).

In mild

\displaystyle\geq(1-o(1))p^{g(u)}(1-p)^{|U|\binom{n}{k-2}}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\bigwedge_{i\in I\backslash U_{\mathcal{C}}}Z_{i}=1]}{\mathbb{P}[\bigwedge_{i\in I\backslash U_{\mathcal{C}}}Z_{i}=1]}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:noedge}}}{{\geq}}(1-o(1))p^{g(u)}.

Therefore in order to complete our estimate for the right hand side of (
18
) we only need to establish
|\mathcal{F}_{u}|
.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{u}

is the family of
g(u)
sized subsets of
\Phi_{u}
such that for every
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}_{u}
we have that for any pair of
k
-sets in
\mathcal{C}
are vertex disjoint except for
u
.

Since the size of the sets
U,V^{\prime}
and
\{w:g(w)=1\}
is
o(n)
, we have

|\Phi_{u}|\geq|U|\binom{(1-o(1))n}{k-2}.

This in turn implies

\binom{|\Phi_{u}|}{g(u)}\geq(1-o(1))\frac{|U|^{g(u)}}{g(u)!}\binom{n}{k-2}^{g(u)},

as
\binom{(1-o(1))n}{k-2}=(1-o(1))\binom{n}{k-2}
.

Since the number of sets in
\binom{\Phi_{u}}{g(u)}
where the two
k
-sets overlap in a vertex other than
u
is

O\left(\left.|U|^{g(u)}\binom{n}{k-2}^{g(u)}\right/|U|\right)=o\left(\binom{|\Phi_{u}|}{g(u)}\right),

we have

|\mathcal{F}_{u}|=(1-o(1))\frac{|U|^{g(u)}}{g(u)!}\binom{n}{k-2}^{g(u)}

and the statement follows.

∎

The previous lemma will be used to establish a lower bound on
C_{i}(t)
when
2\leq i\leq r
, and also for
C(t)
when
r\geq 3
. However, as mentioned earlier, a different approach is needed for
C_{1}(t)
and
C(t)
when
r=2
. Roughly speaking,
C_{1}(t)
increases in every step with the vertices contained in the hyperedges of
F^{\prime}(t+1)
, and when
r=2
,
C(t)
increases with vertices in the closed neighbourhoods of
C_{2}(t+1)\setminus C_{2}(t)
. By considering how hyperedges intersect, the following lemma ensures that a typical hyperedge contributes
k-1
vertices to
C_{1}(t)
and an element in
C_{2}(t+1)\setminus C_{2}(t)
typically contributes
2k-3
vertices to
C(t)
, when
r=2
.

For a positive integer
\ell\geq 2
a set of hyperedges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
is called an
intersecting
\ell
-tuple with respect to
U
, if for every
1\leq i<\ell
,
e_{i}
intersects
e_{i+1}
in a vertex in
V\setminus U
. If two hyperedges in an intersecting
\ell
-tuple intersect in more than one vertex, which may include a vertex in
U
, we call it a
strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuple with respect to
U
.

Lemma 30
.

Consider
H_{k}(n,p)
with vertex set
V=[n]
and hyperedge set
E
. Let
U,V^{\prime}\subseteq V
be two disjoint subsets with
|V^{\prime}|=o(n)
while
U
satisfies both
|U|=\Omega(a_{c})
and
|U|=o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
. Denote by
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
the set of hyperedges which contain exactly one vertex in
U
and no vertices in
V^{\prime}
. Assume that we are given a set of hyperedges
F
, each containing at least one vertex in
V^{\prime}
and are vertex disjoint in
V\setminus(U\cup V^{\prime})
. Furthermore assume that
|F|=O(|U|n^{k-1}p)
.

Then for any
\ell\leq r
with probability
1-o(\exp(-|U|)
we have that in the hypergraph spanned by
E^{\prime}\cup F
the number of intersecting
(\ell+1)
-tuples and the number of strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuples with respect to
U\cup V^{\prime}
is

o(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}).

Proof.

Consider the following process to establish the set of intersecting
(\ell+1)
-tuples and the strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuples.
Denote by
\Phi_{U}
the set of
k
-sets containing exactly one vertex in
U
, and no vertices in
V^{\prime}
. Next we create the family
\mathcal{F}_{0}
consisting of subsets of
\Phi_{U}\cup F
, which form an intersecting
(\ell+1)
-tuple or a strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuple.
In the following we will provide an algorithm which finds every intersecting
(\ell+1)
-tuple and strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuple reminiscent of the query-process in Section
3.2
.
Consider the elements
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
one by one, but we do not query every element we consider. An element is queried only if
\mathcal{C}\cap\Phi_{U}
does not contain a hyperedge, which can be found in a previously successfully queried element. In addition the element
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
is successfully queried if it was queried and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E^{\prime}\cup F
. The first stage of the process is complete, once every element in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
has been considered. Let
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
be the set of hyperedges, which are found in a successfully queried element.

For
i\geq 1
denote by
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
the set of hyperedges in
E^{\prime}\cup F
, which intersect a hyperedge from
\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}
in
V\setminus(U\cup V^{\prime})
. Note that any intersecting
(\ell+1)
-tuple and strongly-intersecting
\ell
-tuple must contain at least one hyperedge in
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
and any remaining hyperedges in
\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Y}_{i}
for some
i\leq\ell
.
Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq\Phi_{U}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{i-1}\mathcal{Y}_{j}\right)
the set of
k
-sets, which intersect a hyperedge in
\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}
in a vertex in
V\setminus(U\cup V^{\prime})
.
For
1\leq i\leq\ell
repeatedly query every element of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
to obtain
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
.

We start by obtaining an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{Y}_{0}|]
. Lemma
20
implies that
|\mathcal{Y}_{0}|
is stochastically dominated by independent Bernoulli random variables, and denote the sum of these random variables as
Y_{0}
. Now both
|\Phi_{U}|p=O(|U|n^{k-1}p)
and
|F|=O(|U|n^{k-1}p)
, which together with
|U|n^{k-2}p=o(1)
and
|U|=\omega(1)
implies:

\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}]=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right).

Together with the concentration result in Theorem
3
we then have

\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{0}=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right]=1-\exp\left(-\omega\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right).

Since
\ell\leq r
we have

\displaystyle|U|^{\ell-1}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\left(\frac{1}{nn^{r(k-2)}p^{r}}\right)^{(\ell-1)/(r-1)}(n^{k-2}p)^{\ell-1}n^{k-1}p\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\left(\frac{1}{n^{k-1}p}\right)^{(\ell-1)/(r-1)}n^{k-1}p\right)=\Omega(1),

(25)

and thus

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathcal{Y}_{0}|=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{0}=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right]=1-o\left(\exp\left(-|U|\right)\right).

Similarly as before by Lemma
20
we have
|\mathcal{Y}_{i}\cap E^{\prime}|
is stochastically dominated by the binomial random variable
Y_{i}=\mathrm{Bin}(|U|(k-1)|\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}|n^{k-2},p)
.
Now conditional on
|\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}|=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)
we have
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)
as
|U|n^{k-2}p=o(1)
. Together with Theorem
3
and (
25
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathcal{Y}_{i}\cap E^{\prime}|=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\mid|\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}|=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\geq\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{i}=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\mid|\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}|=o\left(|U|^{\ell}n^{\ell(k-2)+1}p^{\ell}\right)\right]=1-o\left(\exp\left(-|U|\right)\right).

In addition, as the hyperedges in
F
are vertex disjoint in
V\setminus(U\cup V^{\prime})
, we also have
|\mathcal{Y}_{i}\cap F|\leq k|\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}|
.
Therefore with probability
1-o(\exp(-|U|))
all the
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq\ell
have the right size and the result follows.
∎

5.3.
Bounding trajectory

Similarly to the subcritical regime we establish a trajectory for the number of infected vertices and in addition for the number of vertices with at least
i
active neighbours for
1\leq i\leq r
. Recall that
|A(0)|=|C(0)|=(1+\varepsilon)a_{c}
and let
0<\delta<1
satisfy
(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)>1
.

Definition 31
.

Let
\eta
and
a^{*}
be as defined in (
1
) and (
2
).
Set
c_{0}(t):=n
for all integers
t\geq 0
and for
1\leq i\leq r
let
c_{i}(0):=0
. Finally set
c(-1):=0
and
c(0):=|C(0)|
.
Then for all integers
t\geq 0
and
1\leq i\leq r-1
we define

\displaystyle c_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{j}(t)\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{i-j}}{(i-j)!}\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i-j}+c_{i}(t);

\displaystyle c_{r}(t+1)

\displaystyle:=(1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}c_{j}(t)\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{r-j}}{(r-j)!}\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r-j}+c_{r}(t);

\displaystyle c(t+1)

\displaystyle:=\eta c_{r}(t+1)+c(0).

Finally let
\gamma(t):=c(t)/a^{*}
for all integers
t\geq-1
and call
\gamma
the trajectory associated with mild
r
-bootstrap percolation.

We also provide a simpler form for these values.

Claim 32
.

For
0\leq i<r
and
t\geq-1
we have

c_{i}(t+1)=\frac{c(t)^{i}}{i!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i};

c_{r}(t+1)=(1-\delta)\frac{c(t)^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r};

and

c(t+1)=(1-\delta)\eta\frac{c(t)^{r}}{r!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{r}+c(0).

Proof.

The proof is by induction. Note that the statement holds when
t=-1
and when
i=0
. Now assume the statement holds for
t
. If
1\leq i<r
, then the induction hypothesis for the individual
c_{j}(t)
when
j\leq i
leads to

\displaystyle c_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{j}(t)\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{i-j}}{(i-j)!}\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i-j}+c_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{c(t-1)^{j}}{j!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{j}\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{i-j}}{(i-j)!}\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i-j}+\frac{c(t-1)^{i}}{i!}n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i}

\displaystyle=n\left(\binom{n}{k-2}p\right)^{i}\left(\frac{c(t-1)^{i}}{i!}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{c(t-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{i-j}}{(i-j)!}\right).

For the first statement it only remains to show

c(t-1)^{i}+i!\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{c(t-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{(c(t)-c(t-1))^{i-j}}{(i-j)!}=c(t)^{i},

which follows from the binomial theorem as the left hand side is equivalent to the expansion of
((c(t)-c(t-1))+c(t-1))^{i}
.
The proof for
c_{r}(t)
is analogous, from which
c(t)
follows immediately.
∎

By an argument analogous to the proof of Claim
23
, that is, replacing
1+\delta
and
\beta
with
1-\delta
and
\gamma
respectively, we have

\gamma(t+1)=(1-\delta)\frac{\gamma(t)^{r}}{r}+\gamma(0).

(26)

We expect that
\gamma(t)
does not have a maximum, or equivalently
\gamma(t+1)-\gamma(t)
is bounded from below.

From (
26
), we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle\gamma(t+1)-\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{\gamma(t)^{r}-\gamma(t-1)^{r}}{r}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)(\gamma(t)-\gamma(t-1))\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{\gamma(t)^{j}\gamma(t-1)^{r-j-1}}{r}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)(\gamma(t)-\gamma(t-1))\gamma(t-1)^{r-1}.

This implies that if there exists a
t
such that
\gamma(t-1)^{r-1}>(1-\delta)^{-1}
or equivalently
c(t-1)>a^{*}(1-\delta)^{-1/(r-1)}
, then the sequence
c(t)
converges to infinity. In fact, if
\gamma(t-1)^{r-1}\geq(1+\nu)(1-\delta)^{-1}
for some fixed
\nu>0
, then
c(\tau+1)-c(\tau)
increases by at least a factor of
1+\nu
in every step
\tau\geq t
.

In order to prove our result we require not only that
\gamma(t)
does not have a maximum, but also that the one step change in each step is sufficiently large to show appropriate tail bounds on a step by step basis.

Lemma 33
.

There exists
\Delta>0
independent of
n
such that for every
t\geq 0
we have
\gamma(t+1)-\gamma(t)>\Delta
.

Proof.

Using (
26
) and that
\gamma(0)=(1+\varepsilon)a_{c}/a^{*}=(1+\varepsilon)(1-r^{-1})
we have

\displaystyle\gamma(t+1)-\gamma(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:nuratio}}}{{=}}(1-\delta)\frac{\gamma(t)^{r}}{r}+\gamma(0)-\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{\gamma(t)^{r}}{r}-\gamma(t)+(1+\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right).

For
x\geq 0
the function
(1-\delta)x^{r}/r-x
has its minimum at
x=(1-\delta)^{-1/(r-1)}
, implying

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\frac{\gamma(t)^{r}}{r}-\gamma(t)+(1+\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)^{1/(r-1)}\left(\frac{1}{r}-1\right)+(1+\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\varepsilon-\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)^{1/(r-1)}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)

\displaystyle>\left(1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right).

Recall that
(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)>1
and thus

\Delta:=\left(1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)=\left(\frac{(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)-1}{1-\delta}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)>0.

∎

Therefore the value of
\gamma(t)
increases by at least
\Delta
in every step. Thus for any
\nu>0
, in a bounded number of steps, depending only on
\varepsilon,\delta
and
\nu
, we have
\gamma(t-1)>((1+\nu)/(1-\delta))^{1/(r-1)}
.

In the last claim of this section we show that if
c(t)-c(t-1)
is small then so is
c(t)
.

Claim 34
.

Assume that
c(t)=c(t-1)+o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
. Then we have

c(t)=o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1}).

Proof.

Using
c(t)\leq c(t-1)+o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
, either
c(t)=o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
or
c(t)=O(c(t-1))
.
We only have to show that the statement holds in the latter case. By Claim
32
we have

c(t)=\Theta\left(c(t-1)^{r}n(n^{k-2}p)^{r}\right),

as
c(0)=O(c(\tau)^{r}n((n^{k-2}p)^{r})
for any
\tau\geq 0
. Together with
c(t)=O(c(t-1))
this implies

c(t-1)^{r}=O\left(c(t-1)n^{-1}(n^{k-2}p)^{-r}\right)

or equivalently

c(t-1)=O\left((n^{k-2}p)^{-1}(n^{k-1}p)^{-1/(r-1)}\right)=o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1}),

and we can deduce
c(t)=o((n^{k-2}p)^{-1})
in this case as well.
∎

5.4.
Following the trajectory

In this section we show that as long as the number of vertices added to each of the classes in the previous steps stays above its trajectory this also holds in the next step.
We start by providing the definition for the size of
\widehat{C}^{\prime}(t)
,

|\widehat{C}^{\prime}(t+1)|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\{c(t),|C(t)|\}-|\widehat{C}(t)|&\mbox{ if }|C(t)\setminus\widehat{C}(t)|<(n^{k-2}p\zeta)^{-1}\\

where

Next we calculate the one step expected increase in the size of the sets

Assume that

.

\mathbb{P}[X\geq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]+t]=\mathbb{P}\left[e^{hX}\geq e^{h(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]+t)}\right]\\
\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{hX}]e^{-h\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]}}{e^{ht}}.

Due to the boundedness of
X
we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{hX}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}X^{j}}{j!}\right]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}\mathbb{E}[X^{j}]}{j!}.

Now
X^{j}=(\sum_{i=0}^{n}X_{i})^{j}
is the sum of terms of the form
\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}
for some
I\subseteq[n]
. Hence by linearity of expectation and our assumptions we have
\mathbb{E}[X^{j}]\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]^{j}
implying:

\mathbb{E}[e^{hX}]\leq\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]^{j}}{j!}=\lambda\mathbb{E}[e^{h\widehat{X}}].

Due to the independence of the
\widehat{X}_{i}
we have:

\mathbb{P}[X\geq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]+t]\leq\lambda\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{h\widehat{X}}]e^{-h\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]}}{e^{ht}}=\lambda\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[e^{h(\widehat{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}_{i}])}]}{e^{ht}}.

Since
\widehat{X}_{i}
is an indicator random variable, we have
h\widehat{X}_{i}\leq h
and Lemma
40
implies

\mathbb{E}[e^{h(\widehat{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}_{i}])}]\leq e^{g(h)\mathrm{Var}(h\widehat{X}_{i})}=e^{g(h)h^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X}_{i})}.

Setting
h=\ln(1+t/\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X}))
gives us that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X\geq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{X}]+t]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\exp\left(g(h)h^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})-ht\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\exp\left(\left(\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}-h\right)\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})-ht\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\exp\left(t-\ln\left(1+\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}\right)\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})-\ln\left(1+\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}\right)t\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\exp\left(-\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})\left(\left(1+\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}\right)-\frac{t}{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{X})}\right)\right),

where we substitute
g(h)
in the second step and all other occurrences of
h
in the third step.
The result follows from Lemma
39
.
∎
