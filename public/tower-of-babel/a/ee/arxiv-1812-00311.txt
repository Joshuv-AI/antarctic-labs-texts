Abstract
The Airy line ensemble is a central object in random matrix theory and last passage percolation defined by a determinantal formula. The goal of this paper is to provide a set of tools which allow for precise probabilistic analysis of the Airy line ensemble. The two main theorems are a representation in terms of independent Brownian bridges connecting a fine grid of points, and a modulus of continuity result for all lines. Along the way, we give tail bounds and moduli of continuity for nonintersecting Brownian ensembles, and a quick proof of tightness for Dyson’s Brownian motion converging to the Airy line ensemble.

Introduction

The Airy line ensemble is a central object in random matrix theory, last passage percolation, and more generally, for problems about the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class. It was first described by Prähofer and Spohn
[
31
]
as the scaling limit of the polynuclear growth model, see Section
1.1
for more details on its history and related work.

A brief description

The
parabolic Airy line ensemble

\mathcal{L}
is a decreasing sequence of nonintersecting continuous functions
\mathcal{L}_{1}>\mathcal{L}_{2}>\mathcal{L}_{3}\dots
where each
\mathcal{L}_{i}:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
. It is the unique process of nonintersecting continuous functions whose finite dimensional distributions form a determinantal process with kernel (
3
).

We use the term parabolic in front of Airy line ensemble to help distinguish the object from
\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
, which is known as the
Airy line ensemble
. The process
\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
is stationary. Also, for any fixed
t
, the distribution of
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t)+t^{2}
is a GUE Tracy-Widom random variable (see
[
31
]
) and

\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}\sim-(3\pi k/2)^{2/3}\qquad\text{ as }k\to\infty,

(1)

see Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.3
for more precise asymptotics.
The determinantal formula (
3
) is useful for proving convergence to
\mathcal{L}
and for proving some properties of fixed-time distributions. However, it is hard to deduce even the most basic path properties, such as continuity, from it directly: see
[
31
]
, Appendix A for the essential steps of a proof of continuity using just the determinantal formula.

A useful technique, called the Brownian Gibbs property, was developed by Corwin and Hammond
[
9
]
. The Brownian Gibbs property says that inside any region, conditionally on the outside of the region, the parabolic Airy line ensemble is just a sequence of independent Brownian bridges of variance
2
conditioned so that everything remains nonintersecting and continuous.

The Brownian Gibbs property implies that if the boundary of a region is well understood, then one can use properties of Brownian bridges to deduce path properties of
\mathcal{L}
. This is a big if: for a rectangular region, left and right boundary points can be jammed close together, making nonintersection difficult. Also, the bottom and top boundaries are paths, whose properties are not easily accessible from the determinantal structure. These problems are particularly difficult to tackle further into the large-
k
bulk of the Airy line ensemble, where lines are more closely spaced and the top boundary cannot be easily removed.
The goal of this paper is to tackle these issues in order to make the Airy line ensemble more amenable to probabilistic analysis.

Two basic tools

For any random object, the most fundamental tool for studying it is a construction with a rich structure of independence. In this paper we obtain such a construction – the bridge representation – that quantitatively relates the parabolic Airy lines to independent Brownian bridges. We also obtain tight control of the parabolic Airy line locations and exponential moment bounds for the number of lines that are close together at a given time, which makes the bridge representation useful in practice.

A second basic tool, which for the case of Brownian motion is often the first theorem in an introductory textbook, is a good modulus of continuity bound. By using the bridge representation, we obtain modulus of continuity bounds for parabolic Airy lines that are optimal up to a logarithmic factor in the number of lines.

The bridge representation and the strong modulus of continuity for parabolic Airy lines are crucial steps in the construction of the scaling limit of Brownian last passage percolation, known as the directed landscape
[
12
]
. In that paper, having a detailed and quantitative understanding of the parabolic Airy line ensemble in the bulk is essential for estimating a certain last passage value across
\mathcal{L}
. The upcoming work
[
13
]
also relies on the bridge representation to show that other models in the KPZ universality class converge to the directed landscape.

The bridge representation

The Brownian Gibbs property suggests that one could construct the top
k
lines of
\mathcal{L}
by sampling points on a fine space-time grid, then connecting them with independent Brownian bridges that will not intersect because of the fineness of the grid.
Indeed, we have such a result, with one difference: when a group of endpoints are close together, we have to condition the Brownian bridges between those endpoints not to intersect. However, we have good control over the size of these groups of close endpoints. In particular, they will remain bounded as we include more and more lines in the scales that we are working with. The close endpoint phenomenon is not a deficiency in our method; close endpoints really do exist in the parabolic Airy line ensemble at these scales.

To make this more precise, pick parameters
\ell,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t,\delta>0
. Let
s_{j}=tj/\ell
, and sample
\mathcal{L}
at grid points
\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,2k\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
. The bridges connecting these points will be indexed by

(i,j)\in S:=\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\}.

Let
G
be the random graph on
S
that connects
(i,j)
to
(i+1,j)

if

\min(|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j-1})|,|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j})|)\leq\delta.

Now for each
i
, connect up the points
\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})\}
with independent, variance
2
, Brownian bridges
B_{i,j}:[s_{j-1},s_{j}]\to\mathbb{R}
, where
B_{i,j}
and
B_{i+1,j}
are conditioned not to intersect whenever
(i,j)
and
(i+1,j)
are in the same component of
G
. This yields a new line ensemble
\mathcal{B}
, see Figure
1
for an illustration.

We call
\mathcal{B}
a bridge representation for
\mathcal{L}
. For the process
\mathcal{B}
to mimic
\mathcal{L}
, it must be nonintersecting with high probability. For this, by Brownian scaling, we need that
\delta\gg\sqrt{t/\ell}
or else the Brownian bridges coming from different components of
G
will intersect with nonnegligible probability.

Also, regardless of the parameter choices we should not expect that
\mathcal{B}|_{\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]}
are close in law since
\mathcal{B}
ignores the lower boundary
\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}
entirely. However, if we have chosen the grid finely enough so that edges in
G
are sparse (i.e. most bridges
B_{i,j}
are unconditioned Brownian bridges), then the effect of this lower boundary should be localized to the lowest lines. In particular, it is reasonable to expect that in the right parameter range, the top
k
lines of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{L}
are close in law.

The distance between the
k
th and
(k+1)
st Airy points is of order
k^{-1/3}
(see Equation (
1
)), so for edges in
G
to be sparse we need
\delta=o(k^{-1/3})
. Therefore by the rationale discussed above, the mesh size
t/\ell
should be
o(k^{-2/3})
. For this reason, we use a parameter
\gamma>0
so that the mesh size
t/\ell
is at most
k^{-2/3-\gamma}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma\in[c_{2}\log(\log k)/\log k,2]
, and let

k\geq 3,\qquad t>0,\qquad\delta=k^{-1/3-\gamma/4},\qquad\ell\geq tk^{2/3+\gamma}.

The total variation distance between
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{L}
restricted to the top
k
lines and the interval
[0,t]
satisfies

d_{TV}(\mathcal{B}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]},\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]})\leq\ell\exp(-c_{1}\gamma k^{\gamma/12}).

Here
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive universal constants.

The possibility of extreme events involving the Airy points or the Brownian bridges is what forces us to take
k^{\gamma}
and
\delta/\sqrt{t/\ell}
to be at least a power of
\log k
in Theorem
1.1
. We have not attempted to optimize the particular power.
Note that the use of
2k
lines in the bridge representation is chosen for convenience; a smaller value could be used without affecting the statement of Theorem
1.1
.

In practical applications, one has to choose the value
\gamma
judiciously. A small value gives more conflicts and larger groups of multiple paths, while a large value means more grid points need to be controlled.

We have good control over the behavior of the graph
G
used to construct the bridge representation. In particular, the probability that a typical vertex is not isolated is
O(k^{-3\gamma/4})

Proposition 1.2
.

Fix
\gamma>0

One of the main technical difficulties in proving Theorem
1.1
is understanding the distribution of pairs of points that are within
\delta
of each other in the Airy point process
\{\mathcal{L}_{k}(0):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. In an interval of the form
[-a,-a+q]
, where

and

of such pairs is typically of the order

a\geq 1
and

. Note that it is far from optimal when

.

Proposition 1.3
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that for all
a\geq 1

q\geq 0

\mathbb{P}(L>m\eta)\leq 2e^{-cm\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta+1}\right)}\qquad\text{ for all }m>0.

Similar results hold for pairs of close points in all determinantal processes with nice enough kernels, see Proposition

.

Modulus of continuity and a law of large numbers

The bridge representation yields a modulus of continuity bound for
\mathcal{L}
that is optimal up to the power of
\log k
.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a constant
d>0
so that for all
t>0
there is a random
C>0
so that a.s.

\frac{|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s+r)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)|}{\sqrt{r\log(1+1/r)}\log^{d}k}<C

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
,
s,s+r\in[0,t]
.

We have not tried to extract explicit tail bounds on the constant
C
in Theorem
1.4
. Along the way to proving the bridge representation, we also show a preliminary modulus on individual lines that does yield good tail bounds.

Theorem 1.5
.

There are
c,d>0
so that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a random constant
C_{k}
so that

|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s+r)|\leq C_{k}\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(2/r)

for all
r>0,s,s+r\in[0,1]
. Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(C_{k}>m)\leq e^{ck-dm^{2}}\qquad\text{for all }m>0.

Up to the constant
d
and the prefactor
e^{ck}
, the modulus of continuity in Theorem
1.5
is the same as that of a single Brownian motion without any nonintersection conditions. Theorem
1.5
immediately implies a statement in the form of Theorem
1.4
with
\log^{d}(k)
replaced by
\sqrt{k}
. Reducing this
k
-dependent factor requires the full power of the bridge representation.

Note that both Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
can be used to give modulus of continuity bounds over other intervals not anchored at
0
by using stationarity of
\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
.

Many authors have previously studied continuity properties of
\mathcal{L}
. Prähofer and Spohn outlined the essential steps of a proof of continuity in the appendix of
[
31
]
. Corwin and Hammond
[
9
]
showed that all parabolic Airy lines are locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion. Hammond
[
19
]
found explicit modulus of continuity bounds for more general line ensembles as part of a broader program. In the case of the parabolic Airy line ensemble these bounds grow exponentially in
k
.

After this paper was first posted, Calvert, Hammond, and Hegde
[
8
]
, Theorem 3.11, found explicit Radon-Nikodym derivative estimates for Airy lines versus Brownian motion. These estimates can be used to give a version of Theorem
1.5
where the random constants
C_{k}
satisfy
\mathbb{P}(C_{k}>m)\leq e^{c_{k}-dm^{2}}
with the optimal value of
d=1/4
and a sequence of exponentially growing constants
c_{k}
.

A good modulus of continuity bound can be naturally combined with pointwise bounds to give a uniform law of large numbers for the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

Theorem 1.6
.

For any sequences
\ell_{k},t_{k}>0
with
\log k=o(\ell_{k})
and
t_{k}=e^{o(\ell_{k})}
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{s\in[-t_{k},t_{k}]}\frac{k^{1/3}|{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(s)+s^{2}+(3\pi k/2)^{2/3}|}{\ell_{k}}=0\qquad\text{ almost surely.}

Theorem
1.6
only requires Theorem
1.5
, rather than the more precise bound in Theorem
1.4
.

Properties of nonintersecting Brownian ensembles

On the way to proving these results, we establish several properties of Brownian bridges conditioned not to intersect. A modulus of continuity bound can be
directly deduced from the following, see Proposition
3.5
.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
B_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots k\}
be independent Brownian bridges
with slope
b_{i}
on some time interval
[a,b]
conditioned not to intersect. Then for any
i
,
t>0
,
s,s+t\in[a,b]
we
have

\mathbb{P}(|B_{i}(s+t)-B_{i}(s)-b_{i}t|\geq m\sqrt{t})\leq e^{ck-dm^{2}}.

Here
c,d>0
are universal constants.

The
k
-dependent factor
e^{ck}
is sharp up to the value of the constant
c
.

By combining Proposition
1.7
with a stochastic domination argument and one-point bounds for the top lines in a Dyson’s Brownian motion
\{W^{n}_{1}>W^{n}_{2}>\dots>W^{n}_{n}\}
(i.e. a collection of
n
Brownian motions all started at
0
conditioned not to intersect on
(0,\infty)
), we get a short proof of a tail bound for Dyson’s Brownian motion at the edge. This next proposition was originally obtained by Hammond
[
19
]
, see Theorem 2.14.

Proposition 1.8
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N},c>0
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t>0,\;s\in(0,ctn^{-1/3}]
, and
m>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\left|W_{k}^{n}(t+s)-W_{k}^{n}(t)-s\sqrt{n/t}\right|>m\sqrt{s}\bigg)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}.

Here
c_{k},d_{k}
are
k
-dependent constants.

Significantly, the bound in Proposition
1.8
does not get worse with
n
at the scale where Dyson’s Brownian motion converges to the parabolic Airy line ensemble. Therefore together with the Kolmogorov-Centsov criterion and convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions to those of the parabolic Airy line ensemble, Proposition
1.8
immediately implies functional tightness of Dyson’s Brownian motion at the edge.

Theorem 1.9
.

Define

\mathcal{C}^{n}_{k}(t)=\left(W^{n}_{k}(1+2tn^{-1/3})-2\sqrt{n}-2tn^{1/6}\right)n^{1/6}.

For every
k
,
\{\mathcal{C}^{n}_{k},n\geq 1\}
is tight with respect to the uniform-on-compact topology.

Theorem
1.9
was first obtained by
[
9
]
.
Finally, we note that Proposition
1.8
can be used to give a type of law of the iterated logarithm for Dyson’s Brownian motion, see Proposition
4.3
.

Preliminaries

In this section we recall definitions related to Dyson’s Brownian motion and the parabolic Airy line ensemble.
Let

W^{k}=(W^{k}_{1},\dots,W^{k}_{k}):[0,\infty)\to\mathds{R}

be
k
independent Brownian motions started from the initial condition
W^{k}_{i}(0)=0
and conditioned not to intersect on
[\epsilon,t]
for some
\epsilon,t>0
. The limit as
\epsilon\to 0
and
t\to\infty
of this process exists (e.g. see
[
17
]
and Section 2 in
[
29
]
for details regarding this), and is known as a
k
-level
Dyson’s Brownian motion
.

A
k
-level Dyson’s Brownian motion has the same distribution as the eigenvalues of a matrix-valued Brownian motion in the space of
k\times k
Hermitian matrices with complex entries. This is a result that goes back to Dyson
[
15
]
, see also
[
17
]
for the connection with nonintersecting random walks. Note that just like with Brownian motion, the law of
W^{k}
is invariant under Brownian scaling and time inversion. To see this, we could simply apply Brownian scaling and time inversion prior to taking the
\epsilon\to 0,t\to\infty
limiting procedure above.

A
Brownian
k
-melon

B^{k}=(B^{k}_{1},\dots,B^{k}_{k}):[0,t]\to\mathds{R}^{k}

is a system of
k
independent Brownian bridges
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
with
B^{k}_{i}(0)=B^{k}_{i}(t)=0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, conditioned in a similar limiting fashion so that

B^{k}_{1}(s)>B^{k}_{2}(s)>\dots>B^{k}_{k}(s)

for all
s\in(0,t)
(the reason for the name is that nonintersecting Brownian bridges look like stripes on a watermelon). We note that analogously to the usual relationship between Brownian bridge and Brownian motion, we have that

B^{k}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{t-s}{\sqrt{t}}W^{k}\left(\frac{s}{t-s}\right),

(2)

where the equality above holds in distribution in the space of
k
-tuples of random continuous functions from
[0,t]
to
\mathds{R}
. This follows by relating nonintersecting Brownian motions to nonintersecting Brownian bridges before passing to the limit.

More precisely, if
W^{k}
is a collection of
n
independent Brownian motions with initial conditions
W^{k}_{i}(0)=0
conditioned not to intersect on
[\epsilon,t_{0}]
, then the process
B^{k}
defined from
W^{k}
as in (
2
) is a collection of
n
Brownian bridges with
B^{k}_{i}(0)=B^{k}_{i}(t)=0
, conditioned not to intersect on
[\epsilon t/(1+\epsilon),t_{0}t/(1+t_{0})]
. As we take
\epsilon\to 0,t_{0}\to\infty
,
W^{k}
converges to a Dyson’s Brownian motion and
B^{k}
converges to a Brownian
k
-melon.

We say that a Brownian motion or a Brownian bridge has
variance
v
if its quadratic variation in an interval
[s,t]
is equal to
v(t-s)
. We say that a Dyson’s Brownian motion or a Brownian
k
-melon has variance
v
if the component Brownian motions have variance
v
.

The top lines of an
n
-level Dyson’s Brownian motion (or alternatively, a Brownian
n
-melon) converge in law to a limit called the parabolic Airy line ensemble as
n\to\infty
. To state this convergence precisely, we first discuss line ensembles and define the limiting process.

Line ensembles

Definition 2.1
.

A
line ensemble

\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2},\dots)
is a possibly finite sequence of random functions where each
\mathcal{R}_{i}:I\to\mathds{R}
for some interval
I\subset\mathds{R}
. We say that a line ensemble is
ordered
if almost surely,

\mathcal{R}_{i}(x)\geq\mathcal{R}_{i+1}(x)\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathds{R}.

We say that
\mathcal{R}
is
strictly ordered
if strict inequality can replace weak inequality above for all
i,x
.

Definition 2.2
.

An ordered line ensemble
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the
Brownian Gibbs property
(with variance
v
) if the following holds for all
k\in\{0,1,\dots\},\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
[c,d]\subset I
for which
\mathcal{R}_{k+1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{k+\ell}
are all defined. Let

\mathcal{F}
be the

Then the conditional distribution of
\mathcal{R}|_{\{k+1,\dots,k+\ell\}\times[c,d]}
given
\mathcal{F}
is equal to the law of
\ell
independent, variance
v
, Brownian bridges
B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}:(c,d)\to\mathds{R}
with
B_{i}(c)=\mathcal{L}_{k+i}(c)
and
B_{k+i}(d)=\mathcal{R}_{i}(d)
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, conditioned on the event

\mathcal{R}_{k}(r)>B_{1}(r)>B_{2}(r)>\dots>B_{\ell}(r)>\mathcal{R}_{k+\ell+1}(r)\qquad\text{ for all }r\in(c,d).

(Again, this conditioning should be understood in the same limiting sense as in the definition of Dyson’s Brownian motion in the case when some endpoints are equal).
If
\mathcal{R}_{k}
or
\mathcal{R}_{k+\ell+1}
does not exist, then drop the corresponding inequality from the conditioning.

Rather than repeating the above statements throughout the paper to describe a sequence of Brownian bridges with the above properties, we will simply say that
B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}
is a sequence of Brownian bridges with endpoints
B_{i}(c)=\mathcal{R}_{k+i}(c)
and
B_{i}(d)=\mathcal{R}_{k+i}(d)
conditioned to avoid each other and the boundaries
\mathcal{R}_{k},\mathcal{R}_{k+\ell+1}
.

Note that both Dyson’s Brownian motion and Brownian
k
-melons are line ensembles with the Brownian Gibbs property.

Definition 2.3
.

The
parabolic Airy line ensemble

\mathcal{L}=\{\mathcal{L}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a continuous, strictly ordered line ensemble with lines

indexed by

defined by the requirement that the process
\mathcal{A}(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
is a determinantal process with kernel

K((x,s);(y,t))=\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\Ai(x+\lambda)\Ai(y+\lambda),\qquad&\;\text{if}\;s\geq t,\\
-\int_{-\infty}^{0}d\lambda e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\Ai(x+\lambda)\Ai(y+\lambda),\qquad&\;\text{if}\;s<t,\end{cases}

(3)

where

is the Airy function. The process
\mathcal{A}(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
is stationary, and is known simply as the

.

See
[
25
]
for general background on determinantal processes, including the definition of a determinantal point process from a kernel. We note that the kernel formula (

Note that the convergence in

Finally, assume that

. Then there exists a
2k
-tuple of random functions
(B_{1},\dots,B_{k},C_{1},\dots,C_{k})

Lemma 3.2
.

There exist constants

Proof.

By Brownian scaling, we may assume that

. We can further assume that

k

\begin{split}B_{i}(s+t)-B_{i}(s)\leq\frac{-B_{i}(s)t}{1-s}+C^{k-i+1}_{1}(s+t).\end{split}

(21)

Here
c_{1},d_{1}
are independent of
i,k
. To apply the bound (
7
) in Theorem
3.1
, we have used that
(m-2\sqrt{i})k^{1/6}\geq 5k^{2/3}
whenever
m\geq 7\sqrt{k}
. Now, we can choose
c
large enough, indepedent of
k
, so that
\frac{1}{2}e^{ck}\geq c_{1}
and

\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(ck-\frac{d_{1}}{2}m^{2}\right)\geq 1

(22)

for
m\leq 7\sqrt{k}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore by combining (
21
) and (
22
), we have that

\mathbb{P}(Y^{i}_{1}(1)\geq m)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(ck-\frac{d_{1}}{2}m^{2}\right)

(23)

for all

. The same bound holds for

, so by a union bound we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{t}\left(\max(Y^{i}_{1}(1),0)+\max(X_{1}^{k-i+1}(1),0)\right)\geq m\sqrt{t}\right)\leq\exp\left(ck-\frac{d_{1}}{2}m^{2}\right),

as desired.
∎

Modulus of continuity results naturally follow from statements such as the one in Lemma
3.2
. The classical example of this is Lévy’s modulus of continuity of Brownian motion. For future use (most notably in the paper
[
12
]
), we state this next lemma in greater generality than we need it here.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T=I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}
be a product of bounded real intervals of length
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
. Let
c,a>0
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a random continuous function from
T
taking values in a real vector space
V
with Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. Assume that for every
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, that there exist
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1),\beta_{i},r_{i}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{H}(t+e_{i}u)-\mathcal{H}(t)|\geq mu^{{\alpha_{i}}})\leq ce^{-a{m^{{\beta_{i}}}}}

(24)

for every coordinate vector
e_{i}
, every
m>0
, and every
t,t+ue_{i}\in T
with
u<r_{i}
. Set
\beta=\min_{i}\beta_{i},\alpha=\max_{i}\alpha_{i}
, and
r=\max_{i}r_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
. Then with probability one we have

|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|s_{i}|^{\alpha_{i}}\log^{1/\beta_{i}}\left(\frac{2r^{1/\alpha_{i}}}{|s_{i}|}\right)\right),

(25)

for every
t,t+s\in T
with
|s_{i}|\leq r_{i}
for all
i
(here
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})
).
Here
C
is random constant satisfying

\mathbb{P}(C>m)\leq\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{b_{i}}{r_{i}}\right]cc_{0}e^{-c_{1}m^{\beta}},

where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are constants that depend on
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d},d,
and
a
. Notably, they do not depend on
b_{1},\dots,b_{d},c
or
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}
.

Note that the above lemma can also be extended to the case when
\alpha_{i}=1
, but the power of
1/\beta_{i}
in the logarithm term changes to a power of
1+1/\beta_{i}
.

The proof of Lemma
3.3
mimics the proof of Levy’s modulus of continuity of Brownian motion. The piecewise linear approximations of Brownian motion used in that proof must be replaced by polynomial approximations in each variable. To set up these approximations, we will need an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
B=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[b_{i},c_{i}]
be any box in
\mathds{R}^{d}
with
b_{i}<c_{i}
for all
i
. Let
a_{v}
be real numbers indexed by
v\in\prod_{i=1}^{d}\{b_{i},c_{i}\}
. There exists a unique polynomial
h:B\to\mathds{R}
which is linear in each variable such that
h(v)=a_{v}
for all
v\in\prod_{i=1}^{d}\{b_{i},c_{i}\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may shift the box so that
b_{i}=0
for all
i
.
For
w\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
, let
x_{w}=\Pi_{i\in w}x_{i}
, and define
h(x)=\sum m_{w}x_{w}
, where the sum ranges over
w\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
.
We want to choose the coordinates
m_{w}
so that
h(v)=a_{v}
for all
v
. This gives a linear system of
2^{d}
equations with
2^{d}
unknowns
m_{w}
. Let
A
be the coefficient matrix of this linear system with rows indexed by
v
’s and columns indexed by
w
’s. An entry
A_{v,w}
is nonzero exactly when

w\subset\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:v_{i}=c_{i}\}.

Denoting the right hand side above by
w(v)
, we therefore have
\det A=\pm\prod_{v}A_{v,w(v)},
as no other permutations contribute to the determinant. Hence
A
is invertible, so the system of equations has a unique solution, yielding
h
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We first consider the case when
T=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,1]
and
r_{i}=1
for all
i
.

We will prove the bound when

is a multiple of the unit vector

for some

. Let

k\alpha_{j}>\alpha_{1}

For

for

For

, define approximations
\mathcal{H}_{n}
of
\mathcal{H}
, by setting
\mathcal{H}_{n}=\mathcal{H}
on
P_{n}
, and by requiring that
\mathcal{H}_{n}

\mathcal{H}
(note that the approximations agree where the boxes overlap by the uniqueness in Lemma
3.4

\mathcal{H}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}(0,\dots,0)

Now let

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{H}_{n}-\mathcal{H}_{n-1}

\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{n}(t+s)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
. Letting
||\cdot||
denote the uniform norm on functions, by linearity of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
in the first coordinate on every box

we have
\|\partial_{1}\mathcal{G}_{n}\|\leq 2^{n}\|\mathcal{G}_{n}\|
. Therefore since

only has a nonzero first coordinate, by the mean value theorem, we have

|\mathcal{G}_{n}(t+s)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)|\leq|s|2^{n}\|\mathcal{G}_{n}\|.

(26)

Additionally, we have the bound

|\mathcal{G}_{n}(t+s)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)|\leq|\mathcal{G}_{n}(t+s)|+|\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)|\leq 2\|\mathcal{G}_{n}\|.

(27)

Hence to estimate
\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)

\|\mathcal{G}_{n}\|
.

For
n\geq 1
and a given box

, the values of
\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
on

are convex combinations of the values of
\mathcal{H}
on the vertices of
S
. Also, the values of

\mathcal{H}_{n}
on

\mathcal{H}
on

\displaystyle\max_{t\in S}|\mathcal{H}_{n-1}(t)-\mathcal{H}_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t+s,t\in S\cap P_{n}}|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t+s,t\in P_{n},|s_{1}|\leq 2^{-(n-1)},|s_{j}|\leq 2^{-k(n-1)}}|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|.

Here the final inequality follows since

is a translate of the box
[0,1/2^{n-1}]\times\prod_{j=2}^{d}[0,1/2^{(n-1)k}]
.
Therefore for

,

||\mathcal{G}_{n}||\leq\max\{|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|:t,t+s\in P_{n},|s_{1}|\leq 2^{-(n-1)},|s_{j}|\leq 2^{-k(n-1)}\}.

(28)

Because of how we defined
\mathcal{H}_{-1}
, the above bound also holds for
\mathcal{G}_{0}

with
|s_{1}|\leq 2^{-(n-1)},|s_{j}|\leq 2^{-k(n-1)}

implies that

s=a_{1}2^{-n}e_{1}+\dots+a_{k}2^{-nk}e_{k}

where the

are coordinate vectors,

and
a_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{k}\}
for

\|\mathcal{G}_{n}\|\leq N_{n,1}+\cdots+N_{n,d},

where

\displaystyle N_{n,1}

\displaystyle=2\max\{|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|:t+s,t\in P_{n},|s_{1}|=2^{-n},s_{j}=0,j\geq 2\}\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle N_{n,j}

\displaystyle=2^{k}\max\{|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|:t+s,t\in P_{n},|s_{j}|=2^{-nk},s_{i}=0,i\neq j\}.

By (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n,1}>2^{1-n\alpha_{1}}m((n+1)kd)^{1/\beta_{1}}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n,i}>2^{k-nk\alpha_{i}}m((n+1)kd)^{1/\beta_{i}}\right)

A tail bound on

, and

and

are constants that depends on the terms

and

.
Now by using the bounds in (

) and (

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}(t+s)-\mathcal{H}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D(n+1)^{1/\beta_{1}}2^{-n\alpha_{1}}(|s|2^{n}\wedge 2)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}D|s|^{\alpha_{1}}\log^{1/\beta_{1}}\left(\frac{2}{|s|}\right)

and

Proposition 3.5
.

There exist universal constants

so that the following holds. Let

and let

,

be independent Brownian bridges with arbitrary start and end points and slope

on some time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, conditioned not to intersect. There are random constants
C_{i}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}(C_{i}>m)\leq e^{ck-dm^{2}}

for any

, so that for any

,

,
s,s+t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
we have

|B_{i}(s+t)-B_{i}(s)-b_{i}t|\leq C_{i}\sqrt{t}\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{2\;|t_{2}-t_{1}|}{t}\right).

Fix

, and consider the process

\tilde{B}_{i}(t)=B_{i}(a+t)-B_{i}(a)-b_{i}t

By Lemma
3.2
, the process

satisfies

in dimension

4
Modulus of continuity for top Dyson lines

Before proceeding with the main goal of the paper, which is to understand the Airy line ensemble, we give a short proof of tightness of the Dyson lines in the scaling (
4
). The ideas in this proof will be used later in the paper to give a similar modulus of continuity result for parabolic Airy lines. As discussed in the introduction, Proposition
4.1
is a restatement of Proposition
1.8
and was previously obtained in Hammond
[
19
]
, Theorem 2.14.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
c>0
. There exist constants
c_{k},d_{k}>0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t>0,\;s\in(0,ctn^{-1/3}]
, and
m>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\left|W_{k}^{n}(t)-W_{k}^{n}(t+s)-\frac{s\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{t}}\right|>m\sqrt{s}\bigg)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}.

Throughout the proof,
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
will be constants that depend only on
k
and
c
, but may change from line to line.

Proof.

We can assume that
m>4c^{3/2}
by possibly changing
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
.
By Brownian scaling, it suffices to prove the lemma for
t=1
. Now, by time inversion and Brownian scaling, for any times
p,q\in\mathds{R}
we have that

W^{n}_{k}(p)-W^{n}_{k}(q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{p}{q}}W^{n}_{k}(q)-\sqrt{\frac{q}{p}}W^{n}_{k}(p).

(31)

To apply this property, we first define the error
C^{n}_{k}(s)
by

W^{n}_{k}(1+s)=\left(2+s-\frac{s^{2}}{4}\right)\sqrt{n}+n^{-1/6}C^{n}_{k}(s).

Note that
(2+s-s^{2}/4)\sqrt{n}
is the second order Taylor expansion of
2\sqrt{1+s}\sqrt{n}
, so for small
s
it is a good approximation of
W^{n}_{k}(1+s)
. In particular, by Theorem
3.1
and Brownian scaling, the error term
C^{n}_{k}
satisfies the tail bound

\mathbb{P}(|C^{n}_{k}(s)|\geq r)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}r^{3/2}}

(32)

for all
s\in[0,ctn^{-1/3}]
. Using the property (
31
) applied to the difference

W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+s)

and Taylor expanding the resulting
\sqrt{1+s}
and
1/\sqrt{1+s}
terms gives that

W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}W^{n}_{k}(1+s)-s\sqrt{n}-W^{n}_{k}(1)-\phi_{n}(s),

(33)

where
\phi_{n}(s)
is a random error term that satisfies

|\phi_{n}(s)|\leq d\sqrt{n}|s|(s^{2}+n^{-2/3}|C_{k}^{n}(0)|+n^{-2/3}|C_{k}^{n}(s)|)

(34)

for a constant
d
that depends on the width
c
of the interval but not on
n
, or the point
s
. By using (
33
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(

\displaystyle\left|W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}\right|>m\sqrt{s}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}\left(W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}>\frac{m}{2}\sqrt{s}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\phi_{n}(s)>\frac{m}{2}\sqrt{s}\right).

By the bound on
\phi_{n}
, and the bound in (
32
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\phi_{n}(s)>\frac{m}{2}\sqrt{s}\right)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}.

It remains to bound

\mathbb{P}\left(W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}>\frac{m}{2}\sqrt{s}\right).

(35)

The method we use here will be applied again in Lemma
6.1
. Set

r=\frac{m}{2\sqrt{sn}},

and let
L
be the line with
L(1)=W_{k}^{n}(1)
and
L(1+r)=W_{k}^{n}(1+r)
. Note that our assumption that
m>4c^{3/2}
implies that
r>s
. The Brownian Gibbs property for Dyson’s Brownian motion and monotonicity (Lemma
2.5
) implies that in the interval
[1,1+r]
, the line
W_{k}^{n}
stochastically dominates
L+B
, where
B:[1,1+r]\to\mathds{R}
is the bottom line of a Brownian
k
-melon on
[1,1+r]
that is independent of
W^{n}_{k}(1)
and
W^{n}_{k}(1+r)
(this is what we get after moving the bottom boundary to
-\infty
and the top
k-1
boundary points to
L(1)
and
L(1+r)
). Hence (
35
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left([L+B](1)-[L+B](1+s)+s\sqrt{n}>\frac{m\sqrt{s}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(L(1)-L(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}>\frac{m\sqrt{s}}{4}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(B(1+s)<-\frac{m\sqrt{s}}{4}\right).

(36)

By Lemma
3.2
, the second term in (
36
) is bounded above by
c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{2}}
for constants
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
. For the first term, since
L
is linear and equal to
W^{n}_{k}
at
1
and
1+r
we can write

L(1)-L(1+s)+s\sqrt{n}=\frac{s}{r}\left(W^{n}_{k}(1)-W^{n}_{k}(1+r)+r\sqrt{n}\right).

Therefore the first term in (
36
) is equal to

\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1)-W_{k}^{n}(1+r)+r\sqrt{n}>\frac{mr}{4\sqrt{s}}\right).

By a union bound, this is bounded above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1)>2\sqrt{n}+\frac{mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(-W_{k}^{n}(1+r)+r\sqrt{n}>-2\sqrt{n}+\frac{3mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1)>2\sqrt{n}+\frac{mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1+r)<\left(2+r-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\right)\sqrt{n}-\frac{mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\right).

(37)

In the second above we have used that
\sqrt{n}r^{2}/4=mr/(8\sqrt{s})
. Now, since
s
is bounded above by
cn^{-1/3}
, we have that

\frac{mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\geq\frac{m^{2}}{32c}n^{-1/6}.

Hence the first probability in (
37
) is bounded above by
c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3}}
by the tail bounds on Dyson’s Brownian motion established in Theorem
3.1
. By Brownian scaling, the second term in (
37
) is equal to

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{1+r}W_{k}^{n}(1)<\left(2+r-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\right)\sqrt{n}-\frac{mr}{16\sqrt{s}}\right).

Since
2\sqrt{1+r}\geq 2+r-r^{2}/4,
this is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1)<2\sqrt{n}-mn^{-1/6}\frac{rn^{1/6}}{16\sqrt{s}\sqrt{1+r}}\right).

(38)

Now, using that
r=m/(2\sqrt{sn})
, we have the bound

\frac{rn^{1/6}}{16\sqrt{s(1+r)}}\geq\frac{rn^{1/6}}{16\sqrt{s}(1+r/2)}=\frac{mn^{1/6}}{32s\sqrt{n}+8m\sqrt{s}}.

(39)

Using that
s\leq cn^{-1/3}
, there exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that the right hand side of (
39
) is bounded below by

\frac{c^{\prime}mn^{1/6}}{n^{1/6}+m}\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}(m\wedge n^{1/6}).

Therefore (
38
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\left(W_{k}^{n}(1)-2\sqrt{n}<-\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}mn^{-1/6}(m\wedge n^{1/6})\right).

This is bounded above by
c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{2}}
for all
m
by Theorem
3.1
, giving that (
37
) is also bounded above by

c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{2}}

for any
s\in[0,cn^{-1/3}]
. This in turn bounds (
35
), completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Tightness of the finite-dimensional distributions of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
is well-known. It follows from tightness of the distribution of
\mathcal{C}^{n}(t)
for every fixed

. This can be obtained, for example, from Theorem
3.1
and Brownian scaling. Tightness of the whole ensemble
\mathcal{C}^{n}
then follows from Proposition
4.1
and the Kolmogorov-Centsov theorem (
[
26
]
, Corollary 16.9).
∎

We end this section by showing how Proposition
4.1
can be used in conjunction with Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
to prove a type of ‘law of the iterated logarithm’ for the top line in Dyson’s Brownian motion. Beyond being an interesting consequence of Proposition
4.1
, this result is necessary for the construction of the directed landscape in
[
12
]
.

To prove this law, we first state a simple corollary of Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
3.3
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For every
k
, there exist constants
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
such that for all
t>0,m>0
and all
n\geq 1
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\max_{s\in[0,tn^{-1/3}]}\left|W^{n}_{k}(t+s)-W^{n}_{k}(t)-\frac{s\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{t}}\right|\geq m\sqrt{t}n^{-1/6}\bigg)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}.

(40)

Proof.

Proposition
4.1
allows us to apply Lemma
3.3
with
d=1,\alpha_{1}=1/2,\beta_{1}=3/2,r_{1}=b_{1}=tn^{-1/3}
to the process
W^{n}_{k}
on the interval
[0,tn^{-1/3}]
. This gives that there exists a random constant
C>0
with
\mathbb{P}(C>m)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}
for all
m
such that

\left|W^{n}_{k}(t+s)-W^{n}_{k}(t)-\frac{s\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{t}}\right|\leq C\sqrt{s}\log^{2/3}\left(\frac{2tn^{-1/3}}{s}\right)

for all
s\in[0,tn^{-1/3}]
.
Using that
\log^{2/3}(y)\leq\sqrt{y/2}
for all
y>0
, we get that the right hand side above is bounded above by
C\sqrt{t}n^{-1/6}
for all
s\in[0,tn^{-1/3}]
. The bound in (
40
) follows since
\mathbb{P}(C>m)\leq c_{k}e^{-d_{k}m^{3/2}}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

There exist positive constants
b,c,
and
d
such that for all
m>0
and
n\geq 1
, the probability that

\displaystyle W^{n}_{1}(t)\leq 2\sqrt{nt}+\sqrt{t}n^{-1/6}[m+b\log^{2/3}(n^{1/3}\log(t\;\vee\;t^{-1})+1)]\;\;\forall t\in[0,\infty)

is greater than or equal to
1-ce^{-dm^{3/2}}
.
Here
x\vee y=\max(x,y)
.

Note that in Proposition
4.3
we get a
2/3
-power in the outer logarithm rather than the power of
1/2
seen in the usual law of the iterated logarithm. This is due to the fact that the top tail of the top line of Dyson’s Brownian has a Tracy-Widom
3/2
-exponent. Throughout the proof,
b,c,
and
d
are constants that may change from line to line.

Proof.

Set
a_{i}=(1+n^{-1/3})^{i}
for
i\in\mathds{Z}
.
By Theorem
3.1
and Brownian scaling, for all
i\in\mathds{Z}
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{n}_{1}(a_{i})\geq 2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{a_{i}}+[m+b\log^{2/3}(|i|+1)

\displaystyle]\sqrt{a_{i}}n^{-1/6}\Big)\leq c(|i|+1)^{-db}e^{-dm^{3/2}}.

By Corollary
4.2
, we can extend this to

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\exists&t\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}]:W^{n}_{1}(t)\geq 2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{a_{i}}+\frac{(t-a_{i})\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{a_{i}}}\\
&+[m+b\log^{2/3}(|i|+1)]\sqrt{a_{i}}n^{-1/6}\bigg)\leq c(|i|+1)^{-db}e^{-dm^{3/2}}\end{split}

(41)

as well. Now, by Taylor expansion and the definition of the sequence
a_{i}
, for
t\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
we have that

2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{a_{i}}+\frac{(t-a_{i})\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{a_{i}}}\leq 2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{t}+\frac{n^{-1/6}}{4},\;\;\sqrt{a_{i}}\leq 2\sqrt{t}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;|i|\leq 4n^{1/3}\log(t\vee t^{-1}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\exists t\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}]:W^{n}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{t}+[m+b\log^{2/3}(n^{1/3}\log(t\;\vee\;t^{-1})+1)]\bigg)

satisfies the same bound as (
41
). A union bound then proves the proposition.
∎

5
Properties of the Airy point process

In this section, we prove a few basic properties about the distribution of the points

(\mathcal{L}_{1}(0),\mathcal{L}_{2}(0),\dots).

This sequence of points is known as the
Airy point process
. It is determinantal with locally trace class kernel given by Equation (
3
) in the case
s=t
. To simplify notation in the following lemmas, we write
A_{i}=\mathcal{L}_{i}(0)
, and for
a\in\mathds{R}
, define

N_{a}=\left|\{i\in\mathbb{N}:A_{i}\in[-a,\infty)\}\right|.

We first recall facts about the expected location of the
k
th point in the Airy point process, the expectation and variance of
N_{a}
, and tail bounds for the location of
A_{1}
. The facts about expectation can be easily derived from standard formulas for the Airy point process density (i.e. see formula 1.17 in
[
32
]
and discussion thereafter). The variance bound is more involved, and is proven as Theorem 1 in
[
32
]
. For this next lemma and throughout this section, we define

\kappa=(3\pi/2)^{2/3}.

The tail bounds on
A_{1}
are well-known. See, for example, Exercise 3.8.2 in
[
3
]
(alternately, they follow from the tail bounds on the prelimit in Theorem
3.1
).

Lemma 5.1
.

(i)

\mathbb{E}A_{k}=-\kappa k^{2/3}+O(1)
as
k\to\infty
and
\mathbb{E}N_{a}=(a/\kappa)^{3/2}+O(1)
as
a\to\infty
.

(ii)

There exist constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that for all
a\geq 1
, we have
\Var(N_{a})\leq c_{1}\log a+c_{2}
.

(iii)

There exists constants
c,d>0
such that for all
a>0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(A_{1}>a)\leq c\exp(-da^{3/2})
and
\mathbb{P}(A_{1}<-a)\leq c\exp(-da^{3})
.

As a quick consequence of the above lemma and the determinantal structure of the Airy point process, we can bound the fluctuations of the number of Airy points in an interval.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists constants
\alpha,c>0
such that for every
x\in\mathds{R}
and
b>\alpha\log(x\vee 2)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|N_{x}-(x_{+}/\kappa)^{3/2}\right|\geq b\right)\leq ce^{-b}.

Here
x_{+}=\max(x,0)
.

Proof.

By the monotonicity of
N_{x}
in
x
, it is enough to prove the lemma when
x\geq 0
.
The number of points in any interval in a determinantal point process with a locally trace class kernel is equal in distribution to a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables (see
[
25
]
, Theorem 4.5.3). Therefore Bernstein’s inequality gives that

\mathbb{P}(|N_{x}-\mathbb{E}N_{x}|\geq b)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}b^{2}}{\Var(N_{x})+\frac{1}{3}b}\right).

Applying the bounds on
\mathbb{E}N_{x}
and
\Var(N_{x})
from Lemma
5.1
completes the proof. ∎

We also record a corollary which translates the above lemma into a bound on the Airy point locations.

Corollary 5.3
.

There exist
c,\beta>0
such that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
m>\beta\log(i+1)
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(|A_{i}+\kappa i^{2/3}|\geq mi^{-1/3})\leq ce^{-m/5}.

In the proof
c
is a constant that may change from line to line.

Proof.

Fix
m>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x_{m}=\kappa i^{2/3}+mi^{-1/3}
. We have that

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<-x_{m})=\mathbb{P}(N_{x_{m}}<i)=\mathbb{P}\left((x_{m}/\kappa)^{3/2}-N_{x_{m}}>(x_{m}/\kappa)^{3/2}-i\right).

(42)

We have the bound

(x_{m}/\kappa)^{3/2}-i>\frac{3}{2\kappa}m>\frac{m}{5}.

Moreover, letting
\alpha
be as in Lemma
5.2
, there exists a
\beta>0
such that

\frac{m}{5}>\alpha\log(x_{m}\vee 2)

whenever
m>\beta\log(i+1)
. Applying Lemma
5.2
then shows that

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}+\kappa i^{2/3}<-mi^{-1/3})\leq ce^{-m/5}.

Now let
y_{m}=\kappa i^{2/3}-mi^{-1/3}
and let
y_{m,+}=\max(y_{m},0)
. Observe that

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}>-\kappa i^{2/3}+mi^{-1/3})=\mathbb{P}(N_{y_{m}}>i)=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{y_{m}}-(y_{m,+}/\kappa)^{3/2}>i-(y_{m,+}/\kappa)^{3/2}\right).

(43)

We have that

i-(y_{m,+}/\kappa)^{3/2}\geq\min(i,\kappa^{-1}m)\geq\min(i,m/5).

Again letting
\alpha
be as in Lemma
5.2
, there exists a
\beta>0
such that right hand above is bounded below by
\alpha\log(y_{m}\vee 2)
whenever
5i\geq m>\beta\log i
. Applying Lemma
5.2
then gives

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}+\kappa i^{2/3}<-mi^{-1/3})\leq ce^{-m/5},

for
5i\geq m>\beta\log i
.
When
m\geq 5i
, by the standard Tracy-Widom tail bound (Lemma
5.1
(iii)), we have that

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}>-\kappa i^{2/3}+mi^{-1/3})\leq\mathbb{P}(A_{1}>-\kappa i^{2/3}+mi^{-1/3})\leq ce^{-\frac{4}{3}(2mi^{-1/3}/5)^{3/2}}\leq ce^{-m/5}.

Here we have used that
5-\kappa>2
, so
-\kappa i^{2/3}+mi^{-1/3}\geq 2mi^{-1/3}/5
.
∎

We now turn our attention to bounding the probability that Airy points are close together.
For a point process
\Pi
on
\mathds{R}
, we say that a point
x\in\Pi
is
\delta
-jammed if there is a point
y\in\Pi
such that
|x-y|\leq\delta
. We start with a proposition bounding the number of
\delta
-jammed points in general determinantal processes.

Proposition 5.4
.

Consider a deteminantal point process
\Pi
on an interval
[a,a+\ell]
with a
C^{2}
kernel
K
. Suppose that there exists a constant
b
such that for all
(x,y)\in[a,a+\ell]^{2}
,

|K(x,y)|\leq b,\quad\left|\partial_{x}K(x,y)\right|\leq b^{2},\quad\left|\partial_{y}K(x,y)\right|\leq b^{2},\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\left|\partial_{x,y}K(x,y)\right|\leq b^{3}.

Let
L
be the number of
\delta
-jammed points in
\Pi
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}{\lfloor L/3\rfloor\choose n}n!\leq(2nb^{4}\delta^{3}\ell)^{n}.

Here and throughout the remainder of the paper we use the convention that
{m\choose n}=0
whenever
n>m
.

To prove Proposition
5.4
, we start with a simple combinatorial lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\Pi
be a point process on
[a,a+\ell]
and let
L
be the number of
\delta
-jammed points in
\Pi
. Let
R
be the number of
2n
-tuples
(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{n})
of distinct elements of
\Pi
such that
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\delta
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We have that

R\geq{\lfloor L/3\rfloor\choose n}n!2^{n}.

Here we use the convention that
{k\choose n}=0
if
k<n
.

Proof.

Let
Z=\{z_{1}<z_{2}<\dots<z_{L}\}
be the set of
\delta
-jammed points. We can construct a partial matching on
Z
via the following greedy algorithm. At each step
i
, we match
z_{i}
to
z_{i+1}
if
z_{i}
was not matched to
z_{i-1}
and if
|z_{i}-z_{i+1}|\leq\delta
. This algorithm produces a matching with at least
\lfloor L/3\rfloor
pairs
(z_{i},z_{i+1})
. By counting the assignments of
(x_{i},y_{i})
among the pairs in this matching, we get the desired lower bound.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

Let
R
be as in Lemma
5.5
. We have that

\mathbb{E}R=\int_{S}\det Md\mu,\quad\text{where}\quad S=\left\{(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{n})\in[a,a+\ell]^{2n}:|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\delta\right\}

and
\mu
is Lebesgue measure (see Remark 1.2.3, Definition 4.2.1 in
[
25
]
).
In the above formula,
M
is a
2n\times 2n
matrix consisting of
n^{2}

2\times 2
blocks, where each block is of the form

M_{i,j}=\begin{bmatrix}K(x_{i},x_{j})&K(x_{i},y_{j})\\
K(y_{i},x_{j})&K(y_{i},y_{j})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We first bound
\det M
on the set
S
. To do this, we will compute
\det BMB^{t}
, where
B
is a block diagonal matrix consisting of
n

2\times 2
blocks of the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
(b\delta)^{-1}&-(b\delta)^{-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We can calculate the blocks of
BMB^{t}
by computing that

A\begin{bmatrix}e&f\\
g&h\\
\end{bmatrix}A^{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{e+f+g+h}{4}\quad&\quad\left(\dfrac{e-f}{\delta}+\dfrac{g-h}{\delta}\right)/2b\\[8.61108pt]
\left(\dfrac{e-g}{\delta}+\dfrac{f-h}{\delta}\right)/2b\quad&\quad\left(\dfrac{(e-g)}{\delta}-\dfrac{(f-h)}{\delta}\right)/(\delta b^{2})\\[8.61108pt]
\end{bmatrix}.

Substituting in the entries of
M_{i,j}
for
e,f,g,
and
h
above, the
(1,1)
-entry of the resulting matrix
AM_{i,j}A^{t}
is an average of values of
K
. The
(1,2)
-entry is an average of difference quotients of
K
, multiplied by
(\delta b)^{-1}(x_{j}-y_{j})
, and similarly the
(2,1)
-entry is an average of difference quotients of
K
, multiplied by
(\delta b)^{-1}(x_{i}-y_{i})
. Finally, the
(2,2)
-entry is a
second difference quotient, multiplied by
(\delta b)^{-2}(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{j}-y_{j})
.

The mean value theorem then implies that all entries of
BMB^{t}
are bounded by
b
on
S
since
|x_{j}-y_{j}|\leq\delta
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
on this set. Therefore

\det(M)=\frac{\det(BMB^{t})}{(\det B)^{2}}=(b\delta)^{2n}\det(BMB^{t})\leq(b\delta)^{2n}\sqrt{2nb^{2}}^{\,2n}.

The last inequality follows from Hadamard’s inequality and the fact that the length of each of the columns of
BMB^{t}
is bounded by
\sqrt{2nb^{2}}
.
Combining this inequality with the fact that the volume of
S
is less than
(2\delta\ell)^{n}
shows that
\mathbb{E}R\leq(4nb^{4}\delta^{3}\ell)^{n}
. Lemma
5.5
then completes the proof of Proposition
5.4
.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

There exists a constant
c
such that for any
a\geq 1,\;\ell>0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the number
L
of
\delta
-jammed points in the Airy point process in the interval
[-a,-a+\ell]
satisfies

\mathbb{E}{\lfloor L/3\rfloor\choose n}n!\leq(cna^{2}\delta^{3}\ell)^{n}.

Proof.

Recall from (
3
) that we have the following formula for the kernel of the Airy point process:

K(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\Ai(x+\lambda)\Ai(y+\lambda)d\lambda}.

(44)

To bound
K
and its derivatives, we use the following bounds that hold for all
x\geq 1
(see
[
1
]
, formulas 10.4.59-10.4.62).

\displaystyle|\sqrt{x}\Ai(-x)|,\;|\Ai^{\prime}(-x)|\leq

\displaystyle\;cx^{1/4}\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad|\sqrt{x}\Ai(x)|,\;|\Ai^{\prime}(x)|\leq cx^{1/4}e^{-(2/3)x^{3/2}}.

(45)

Here
c
is a positive constant. Also note that
\Ai(x)
and
\Ai^{\prime}(x)
are bounded on
[-1,1]
by continuity.
Since the Airy function is analytic and decays exponentially fast as
x\to\infty
, we can differentiate under the integral sign in (
44
) to get formulas for
\partial_{x}K,\partial_{y}K
and
\partial_{x,y}K
. By the bounds in (
45
), we get that

|K(x,y)|\leq m^{1/2},\quad|\partial_{x}K(x,y)|\leq m,\quad|\partial_{x,y}K(x,y)|\leq m^{3/2},

where
m=c^{\prime}\max(-x,-y,1)
for a constant
c^{\prime}
. Applying Proposition
5.4
with
b=c^{\prime}a^{1/2}
finishes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Corollary
5.6
, we can get the following tail bound on the number of
\delta
-jammed points in the Airy point process in a given interval. This next proposition is a restatement of Proposition
1.3
.

Proposition 5.7
.

There exists a constant
d>0
such that for all
a\geq 1,\;\ell,m>0
, and
\delta>0
, the number of
\delta
-jammed points
L
in the Airy point process in the interval
[-a,-a+\ell]
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(L>ma^{2}\delta^{3}\ell)\leq 2\exp\left(-dm\left(\frac{a^{2}\delta^{3}\ell}{a^{2}\delta^{3}\ell+1}\right)\right).

Proof.

Observe that for every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

m^{n}\leq 4^{n}{{\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}\choose n}n!+(12n)^{n}.

(46)

To see this, first note that
m^{n}\leq(12n)^{n}
if
m\leq 12n
. For
m\geq 12n
, we have that

4^{n}{{\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}\choose n}n!=4^{n}{\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}({\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}-1)\cdots({\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}-n+1).

Since
m\geq 12n,
we have that
{\left\lfloor m/3\right\rfloor}-n+1\geq m/4
, and so the right hand side above is greater that
4^{n}(m/4)^{n}=m^{n}
, yielding (
46
) in this case.
Therefore by Corollary
5.6
and Fubini’s Theorem, for any
b>0
we have that

\mathbb{E}e^{bL}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{b^{n}\mathbb{E}L^{n}}{n!}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(12nb)^{n}}{n!}+\frac{(4cna^{2}\delta^{3}\ell b)^{n}}{n!}\right).

Here the constant
c
is as in Corollary
5.6
. Therefore there is a universal constant
d>0
such that with
b=d/(a^{2}\delta^{3}\ell+1)
we have
\mathbb{E}e^{bL}\leq 2
.
Applying Markov’s inequality to the event
e^{bL}>e^{mba^{2}\delta^{3}\ell}
completes the proof.
∎

In order to prove the bridge representation of the parabolic Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{L}
, we first need to define associated graphs that record which points in
\mathcal{L}
are
\delta
-jammed.

Definition 5.8
.

Fix
t>0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Define
s_{i}=it/\ell
for all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
. For
k,\delta>0
, we define a random graph
G_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
on the set

S_{k}(\ell)=\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\},

where the points
(i,j)
and
(i+1,j)
are connected if either

|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j-1})|\leq\delta\quad\;\text{or}\;\quad|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j})|\leq\delta.

Let
M_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
be the size of the largest component of
G_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
.

The second important consequence of Corollary
5.6
gives a bound on the size of components in
G_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
. In other words, it allows us to bound the size of long chains of
\delta
-jammed points in the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

Proposition 5.9
.

There exists
c>0
so that for every
\delta\in(0,1]
,
\ell,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
we have that

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}(t,\ell,\delta k^{-1/3})\geq m)\leq\ell k[cm\log(k/\delta)]^{m}\delta^{3{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}.

(47)

Proposition
1.2
follows as a special case of Proposition
5.9
in the case when
\delta=k^{-\gamma/4},\ell={\left\lceil tk^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}
for fixed
t,\gamma
. With these parameter choices, for fixed
m
, as
k\to\infty
the right hand side of (
47
) is equal to

O(\log^{m}(k))k^{5/3+\gamma(1-3{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}/4)}.

(48)

The exponent in
k
is always negative if we take
m={\left\lceil 14(1+1/\gamma)\right\rceil}
, and hence (
48
) tends to
0
with
k
for this choice of
m
.

In the proof,
c
is a constant that may change from line to line.

Proof.

Fix
m,k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
with
k\geq 2
. We may assume that
6\leq m\leq k
since by increasing
c
, the statement is trivial for
m<6
, and the component sizes in
G_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
are deterministically bounded above by
k
. If
M_{k}(t,\ell,\delta k^{-1/3})\geq m
, then there must be some
(i,j)
with
i\geq m
in the set

\tilde{S}_{k}(\ell)=\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{0,\dots,\ell\}

such that there are at least
m/2
points in the set

\{\mathcal{L}_{i-m+1}(s_{j}),\dots,\mathcal{L}_{i-1}(s_{j}),\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})\}

that are
\delta k^{-1/3}
-jammed. Let
E_{k,b}
be the event where

|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})+s_{j}^{2}+\kappa i^{2/3}|<bi^{-1/3}\log(k+1)\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,j)\in\tilde{S}_{k}(\ell).

On the event
E_{k,b}
, we have that

\{\mathcal{L}_{i-m+1}(s_{j})+s_{j}^{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{i-1}(s_{j})+s_{j}^{2},\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})+s_{j}^{2}\}\subset[-a_{i},-a_{i}+\ell_{i}],

for all
(i,j)\in\tilde{S}_{k}(\ell)
, where

a_{i}=\kappa i^{2/3}+bi^{-1/3}\log(k+1)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\ell_{i}=\kappa mi^{-1/3}+2b(i-m+1)^{-1/3}\log(k+1).

Therefore letting
J_{i,j}
be the number of
\delta k^{-1/3}
-jammed points in the point process
\{\mathcal{L}_{r}(s_{j}):r\in\mathbb{N}\}

in the set
[-a_{i},-a_{i}+\ell_{i}]
, a union bound implies that

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)\geq m)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{k,b}^{c})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\tilde{S}_{k}(\ell)}\mathbb{P}(J_{i,j}\geq m/2).

(49)

We can bound
\mathbb{P}(E^{c}_{k,b})
and
\mathbb{P}(J_{i,j}\geq m/2)
by using that each of the processes

\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})+s_{j}^{2}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}

is an Airy point process.
Set
b=\max(\beta,15\gamma{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor})
, where
\beta
is the constant from Corollary
5.3
and
\gamma=-\log_{k}\delta
. A union bound implies that

\mathbb{P}(E_{k,b}^{c})\leq c(\ell+1)k^{1-b/5}\leq c\ell k^{1-3\gamma{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}.

(50)

It remains to bound
\mathbb{P}(J_{i,j}\geq m/2)
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(J_{i,j}\geq m/2)\leq\mathbb{P}\left({{\left\lfloor J_{i,j}/3\right\rfloor}\choose{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}>0\right)\leq\frac{1}{{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}!}\mathbb{E}{{\left\lfloor J_{i,j}/3\right\rfloor}\choose{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}!

(51)

In the first inequality in (
51
), we used that
m\geq 6
.
By applying Corollary
5.6
and using the bound
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
for all
n
, the right hand side of (
51
) is bounded above by

(ca_{i}^{2}\delta^{3}k^{-1}\ell_{i})^{{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}},

(52)

where
c
is a universal constant. Now, for a universal constant
c
, we have the bounds

b\leq c(1+m\gamma)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(i-m+1)^{-1/3}\leq i^{-1/3}+\frac{(m-1)}{i-1},

which gives that

\displaystyle\ell_{i}\leq cm(1+\gamma)\left(i^{-1/3}+\frac{(m-1)}{i-1}\right)\log(k+1)\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle a_{i}\leq\kappa i^{2/3}+c(1+m\gamma)i^{-1/3}\log(k+1).

Using this we get that for every
i\in[m,k]\cap\mathbb{N}
, that

a_{i}^{2}\ell_{i}\leq cm^{4/3}(1+\gamma^{3})k\log^{3}(k+1)

Using that
\gamma=-\log_{k}\delta
and that
k\geq 2
so
\log(k+1)\leq c\log k
, we can bound the right hand side of (
52
) above by

\left[cm^{4/3}\log^{3}(k/\delta)\delta^{3}\right]^{{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}.

Combining this with the bound in (
50
) bounds the right hand side of (
49
). This completes the proof.
∎

6
Preliminary modulus of continuity for parabolic Airy lines

In this section, we will obtain a modulus of continuity estimate for the parabolic Airy line ensemble. This will be improved later as a consequence of the bridge representation. However, we need to prove a preliminary estimate in order to prove that theorem. When combined with the pointwise bounds in Corollary
5.3
, this modulus of continuity estimate gives a bound on the maximum of the
k
th Airy line over an interval. This bound will be necessary for showing that boundary conditions don’t propagate upwards when applying the Brownian Gibbs property in a small region.

For this modulus of continuity, we will need a tail bound on the difference between values in the parabolic Airy line ensemble at two distinct times. The method of proof is a limiting version of the method used in Proposition
4.1
. It is cleaner than that proof because we can appeal to the stationarity of the Airy line ensemble.

Lemma 6.1
.

There are constants
c,d>0
such that for every
t\in\mathds{R},s\in(0,1),a>0,
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
th line
\mathcal{L}_{k}
in the parabolic Airy line ensemble satisfies

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|>a\sqrt{s})\leq e^{ck-da^{2}}.

(53)

Moreover, when
k=1
the above bound holds for all
t\in\mathds{R},s\in(0,\infty)
and
a>0
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
a>7s^{3/2}
. Set
b=a\sqrt{s}+s^{2}
. By stationarity of the Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
and the equality in distribution
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(-\;\cdot)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|>a\sqrt{s})

\displaystyle=2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)-s^{2}>a\sqrt{s})

\displaystyle=2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)>b).

Now set
r=b/(8s)
.
Since
a>7s^{3/2}
, we have that
r>s
. By the Brownian Gibbs property for
\mathcal{L}
, conditionally on the set

\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(0),\mathcal{L}_{i}(r):i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\}\cup\{\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(q):q\in[0,r]\},

the restriction
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,r]}
is equal in distribution to
k
Brownian bridges
(\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{k})
on
[0,r]
of variance
2
with endpoints given by
\mathcal{L}_{i}(0)
and
\mathcal{L}_{i}(r)
for all
i\in[1,k]
, conditioned to avoid
\mathcal{L}_{k+1}
. By Lemma
2.5
,
(\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{k})
stochastically dominates the sequence

(B_{1}+L,\dots,B_{k}+L),

where
(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
is a Brownian
k
-melon with variance
2
on
[0,r]
, and
L
is the affine function with
L(0)=\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)
and
L(r)=\mathcal{L}_{k}(r)
.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)>b)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}([B_{k}+L](0)-[B_{k}+L](s)>b)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(L(0)-L(s)>\frac{b}{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}(s)-B_{k}(0)<-\frac{b}{2}\right).

We can then relate the first term above to the values of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
at the times
0
and
r=b/(8s)
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(L(0)-L(s)\geq\frac{b}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(r)\geq\frac{br}{2s}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}(r)\leq-\kappa k^{2/3}-\frac{br}{4s}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)\geq-\kappa k^{2/3}+\frac{br}{4s}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}(r)+r^{2}\leq-\kappa k^{2/3}-\frac{b^{2}}{64s^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)\geq-\kappa k^{2/3}+\frac{b^{2}}{32s^{2}}\right).

(54)

Here
\kappa=(3\pi/2)^{2/3}
, and for the final equality we have used that
r=b/(8s)
.
We can bound the two probabilities in (
54
) using Corollary
5.3
, since
\mathcal{L}_{k}(r)+r^{2}
is a
k
th Airy point for any
r
. Also,
\mathbb{P}\left(B_{k}(s)-B_{k}(0)\leq-b/2\right)
can be bounded by Lemma
3.2
. Combining these bounds gives that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)>b)\leq ce^{-db^{2}/s^{2}}+e^{ck-db^{2}/s}

for constants
c
and
d
. Using that
b\geq a\sqrt{s}
proves the desired bound when
s\in(0,1)
. For the case when
k=1,s\geq 1,
and
a>7s^{3/2}
we can use the tail bounds on the Tracy-Widom random variable
\mathcal{L}_{1}(0)
from Lemma
5.1
(iii) instead of Corollary
5.3
to bound (
54
). This gives that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{1}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{1}(s)>b)\leq ce^{-db^{3}/s^{3}}+e^{c-db^{2}/s}.

Using the fact that
b\geq a\sqrt{s}
and that
a>7s^{3/2}
then gives the desired bound.

Now suppose that
a<7s^{3/2}
. When
s<1
, then
a<7
, so by increasing
c
we can guarantee that
e^{ck-da^{2}}>e^{c-7d}\geq 1
, and the bound follows trivially. For the
k=1
and
s\geq 1
case, Tracy-Widom tail bounds on the random variables
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t)+t^{2}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t+s)+(t+s)^{2}
(Lemma
5.1
(iii)) and a union bound imply that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{L}_{1}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{1}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|>a\sqrt{s})\leq ce^{-da^{3/2}s^{3/4}}

for some
c,d>0
.
Since
a<7s^{3/2}
, this is bounded above by
ce^{-d7^{-1/2}a^{2}}
, completing the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
6.1
, we get the following modulus of continuity for the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

Theorem 6.2
.

There are
c,d>0
so that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in\mathds{R}
, there exists a random constant
C_{k}
so that

|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|\leq C_{k}\sqrt{s}\log^{1/2}(2/s)

for all
t,t+s\in[a,a+1]
. Moreover

\mathbb{P}(C_{k}>m)\leq e^{ck-dm^{2}}\qquad\text{for all }m>0.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, we can apply Lemma
3.3
to the
k
th Airy line
\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}
on the interval
[a,a+1]
with
d=1,b_{1}=r_{1}=1,\alpha_{1}=1/2,
and
\beta_{1}=2
.
∎

Theorem
1.5
follows from Theorem
6.2
in the
a=0
case.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For
t,t+s\in[0,1]
, we have the deterministic bound

|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)|\leq|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|+4\sqrt{s}\log^{1/2}(2/s),

so

|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(t+s)|\leq(C_{k}+4)\sqrt{s}\log^{1/2}(2/s),

with
C_{k}
as in Theorem
6.2
. The constant
C_{k}+4
satisfies the same tail bounds as
C_{k}
with a larger value of
c
and a smaller value of
d
. This yields Theorem
1.5
.
∎

We record the the following corollary of Theorem
6.2
, which confines lines in the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

Corollary 6.3
.

There exist
\beta,c>0
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, any
t>0
, and any
m>\beta\log(k+1)
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)+s^{2}+\kappa k^{2/3}|>mk^{-1/3}\right)\leq(t+1)ce^{-m/10}.

In the proof,
c,d,
and
\beta
are positive constants that may change from line to line.

Proof.

It suffices to proves the statement for
t=1
, as for larger
t
, we can simply use a union bound and the stationarity of
\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)+s^{2}
, and for smaller
t
, the statement follows by modifying the constant
c
.
Let
\Pi=\{0,1/k^{2},2/k^{2},\dots,1\}
.
Letting
C_{k}
be as in Theorem
6.2
for the interval
[0,1]
, we have that

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)+s^{2}+\kappa k^{2/3}|\leq\sup_{s\in\Pi}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)+s^{2}+\kappa k^{2/3}|+C_{k}\frac{\log^{1/2}(2k^{2})}{k},

and so the probability in the statement of the corollary is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in\Pi}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)+s^{2}+\kappa k^{2/3}|>\frac{2mk^{-1/3}}{3}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(C_{k}>\frac{mk^{2/3}}{3\log^{1/2}(2k^{2})}\right).

(55)

When
m>\beta\log(k+1)
, the first term on the right hand side of (
55
) is bounded above by
ck^{2}e^{-2m/15}
by Corollary
5.3
and a union bound. The second term on the right hand side of (
55
) is bounded above by
e^{ck-dkm^{2}}
by Theorem
6.2
. This uses that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k^{2/3}/(3\log^{1/2}(2k^{2}))\geq dk^{1/2}
for some
d>0
. Now, as long as
\beta
is large enough, we have that

ck^{2}e^{-2m/15}+e^{ck-dkm^{2}}

is bounded above above by
ce^{-m/10}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N},m>\beta\log(k+1)
. This gives the desired bound.
∎

Corollary
6.3
immediately implies Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By stationarity of the Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{L}(t)+t^{2}
and Corollary
6.3
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[-t_{k},t_{k}]}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(t)+t^{2}+\kappa k^{2/3}|>\epsilon\ell_{k}k^{-1/3}\right)\leq(2t_{k}+1)ce^{-\epsilon\ell_{k}/10}

for a constant
c>0
.
Since
t_{k}=e^{o(\ell_{k})}
and
\log k=o(\ell_{k})
, the right hand side above is summable in
k
for every
\epsilon>0
. Hence Theorem
1.6
follows by the Borel-Cantelli lemma.
∎

7
The bridge representation

In this section we prove the bridge representation, Theorem
1.1
. After proving the bridge representation, we prove a small proposition that shows that at most locations, the bridge representation samples bridges without any nonintersection condition.

We begin with a definition. Let
t>0,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and let
s_{j}=jt/\ell
as in Section
5
. Recall that
G_{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
is a random graph on the set
\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\}
where vertices
(i,j)
and
(i+1,j)
are connected if either

|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j-1})|\leq\delta\qquad\;\text{or}\;\qquad|\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})-\mathcal{L}_{i+1}(s_{j})|\leq\delta.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The
bridge representation

\mathcal{B}^{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
of the parabolic Airy line ensemble is a line ensemble
(\mathcal{B}^{k}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}^{k}_{2k})
with domain
[0,t]
constructed as follows. For every
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, sample an independent Brownian bridge
B_{i,j}:[s_{j-1},s_{j}]\to\mathds{R}
with

B_{i,j}(s_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad B_{i,j}(s_{j})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j}),

where the bridges
B_{i,j}
and
B_{i^{\prime},j}
are conditioned not to intersect if
(i,j)
and
(i^{\prime},j)
are in the same component of
G_{2k}(t,\ell,\delta)
. We then define the
i
th line
\mathcal{B}^{k}_{i}:[0,t]\to\mathds{R}
of the line ensemble
\mathcal{B}^{k}(t,\ell,\delta)
by concatenating the bridges
B_{i,j}
. That is,
\mathcal{B}^{k}_{i}|_{[s_{j-1},s_{j}]}=B_{i,j}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
.

The next theorem is a restatement of our main theorem, Theorem
1.1
. It shows that
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{L}
are close in law when restricted to the set
\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]
in the appropriate parameter range.

Theorem 7.2
.

There exist constants
c,d>0
such that the following holds for all
k\geq 3
,
t>0
,
\gamma\in(c\log(\log k)/\log k,2]
and
\ell\geq tk^{2/3+\gamma}
. The total variation distance between the laws of
\mathcal{B}^{k}(t,\ell,k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]}
is bounded above by

\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}.

Theorem
7.2
can be thought of as giving a quantitative version of the Brownian Gibbs property. In particular, it allows us to apply the Brownian Gibbs property to large patches in
\mathcal{L}
without having to worry about boundary conditions or long-range interactions between lines (e.g. for a fixed value of
\gamma
, the components in
G_{k}(t_{k},tk^{2/3+\gamma},k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
are of bounded size as we take
k\to\infty
by Proposition
1.2
).

The lower bound on
\gamma
in Theorem
7.2
is optimal up to lowering the constant
c
; having a nearly optimal parameter range is essential in applications (e.g. Theorem
1.4
and the application of Theorem
7.2
in
[
12
]
). The upper bound on
\gamma
in Theorem
7.2
is quite artificial and is chosen purely for the sake of proof. For any
\gamma>c\log\log(k)/\log k
, we can get a similar total variation bound, though the exponent
\gamma/12
will be replaced by
b\gamma
for a constant
b
that decays as
\gamma\to\infty
. The restriction that
k\geq 3
is imposed purely to ensure that
\log\log k/\log k>0
. See the introduction for discussion about the heuristics behind the parameter ranges.

The first step in the proof of Theorem
7.2
is to show that when we apply the Brownian Gibbs property to
\mathcal{L}
on a region of the form
\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,k^{-2/3-\gamma}]
, that the lower boundary condition coming from the line
\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}
only affects nearby lines.

Lemma 7.3
.

Define the
\sigma
-fields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(k,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(s):(i,s)\in\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{0,t\}\;\text{ or }\;i=2k+1,s\in[0,t]\}\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\sigma\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(s):(i,s)\in\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{0,t\}\}.

k\geq 3

\gamma\in[c\log\log k/\log k,2]

Let
A=(A_{J},\dots,A_{2k})

Step 1: Defining

, and let

(this uses that

). To show that (

Step 2: Separating out Airy points.

3k^{2/3-\gamma/4}

then implies (

) as long as

is taken large enough (note that the weaker lower bound of

would also suffice). This completes the proof that

.

Now, for

, let

We claim that

for all

. If not, then
f(i)-i\geq m
for some

. The recursive definition of

Since

, this would imply that

are in the same component of

, contradicting condition (iii) on the set

.
Therefore for all

,

and so for such

h_{i}-\mathcal{L}_{i}(0)<mb\sqrt{t}\leq k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}.

In the second inequality we have again used that
b\leq k^{\gamma/4}/m
and

.
In particular, this bounds holds for

. By the definition of

, this implies (
57
).

We can define

in terms of the sequence

analogously to how we defined

. This gives an analogous inequality to (

) for

.

Step 3: A stochastic domination argument.
Now, by Lemma

, on the event

the distribution of

\mathcal{F}(k,t)
is stochastically dominated by the top line

of a system of Brownian bridges

of variance

on

with endpoints

In particular, on

\mathbb{P}\Big(\min_{s\in[0,t]}L_{J-1}(s)-B_{J}(s)\geq k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}(k,t)\Big)

(60)

Now the
B_{i}
can be realized by repeated sampling of independent Brownian bridges until the avoidance conditions are satisfied. Let
(C_{J},\dots,C_{2k})
denote the first sample, and let

be the event that it successfully satisfies all avoidance conditions. Then the event in (

) is implied by

This event is implied by the event

where all bridges

stay in a channel of width

about the line from

to

. Second, by (

), the analogous inequality for

, and the bounds on

, we have

This implies that

as well. The conditional probability of

can be controlled a standard bound on the maximum of a Brownian bridge and a union bound. This yields that on the event

we have

\mathbb{P}(R\;|\;\mathcal{F}(k,t))\geq 1-cke^{-db^{2}}

(61)

for universal constants

).

Step 4: Bounding
\mathbb{P}(E_{b,m})
:
We bound

assuming that

for some constant

. With this lower bound on

. These bounds yield

\mathbb{P}(E_{b,m})\geq 1-ce^{-b/10}-[c(1+\gamma/4)m\log k]^{m}k^{1-\frac{3\gamma}{4}{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}

for a constant

.

Finally, taking

)) is bounded below by

.

We are now ready to prove the bridge representation theorem. The basic idea of the proof is to construct a line ensemble
\mathcal{C}^{k}
on
\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]
that interpolates between
\mathcal{B}^{k}:=\mathcal{B}^{k}(t,\ell,k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
and
\mathcal{L}
as follows.

•

\mathcal{C}^{k}
and
\mathcal{B}^{k}
will have the same law on the set
\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]
.

•

The total variation distance between the
\mathcal{C}^{k}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]}
will be bounded above by
\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}
.

The line ensemble
\mathcal{C}^{k}
will be constructed by replacing the lines
\mathcal{B}^{k}_{k+1},\dots,\mathcal{B}^{k}_{2k}
with Brownian bridges
A_{i,j}
constructed via Lemma
7.3
. We will then be able to estimate the total variation distance between
\mathcal{C}^{k}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]}
by estimating the probability that
\mathcal{C}^{k}
intersects itself or the lower boundary
\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}
.

Throughout the proof,
b
and
d
will be positive constants that may change from line to line. We use the notation of Lemma
7.3
.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Set
\gamma\in[c\log\log k/\log k,2]
for a sufficiently large constant
c
. As with Lemma
7.3
, exactly how large we need to take
c
will be made clear in the proof.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the set of random variables

S=\{\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j}):(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{0,\dots,\ell\}\}\cup\{\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}|_{[0,t]}\}.

(62)

By Lemma
7.3
and a union bound, there exist random variables
J_{1},\dots,J_{\ell}\in(k,2k]
such that the following holds. For
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, define a sequence of Brownian bridges
A_{j}=(A_{J_{j},j},\dots,A_{2k,j})
on the interval
[s_{j-1},s_{j}]
as in the statement of Lemma
7.3
such that
A_{i,j}(s_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})
and
A_{i,j}(s_{j})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})
, and such that the bridges in
A_{j}
are conditioned to avoid each other and the boundary
\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}
. Also let
L_{j}
be the line with
L_{j}(s_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}_{J_{j}-1}(s_{j-1})
and
L_{j}(s_{j})=\mathcal{L}_{J_{j}-1}(s_{j})
.
Then with probability at least
1-\ell e^{-dk^{\gamma/12}}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(R|\;\mathcal{H}\right)&\leq\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/4}}\qquad\text{ where }\qquad\\
R&=\left\{\exists j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}:\min_{q\in[s_{j-1},s_{j}]}L_{j}(q)-A_{J_{j},j}(q)\leq k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}\right\}.\end{split}

(63)

We now define the line ensemble
\mathcal{C}^{k}
. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
i\in\{J_{j},\dots,2k\}
, set
C_{i,j}=A_{i,j}
. For
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,J_{j}-1\}
, set
C_{i,j}=B_{i,j}
, where the bridges
B_{i,j}
are as in Definition
7.1
. Concatenating the bridges
C_{i,j}
together for

gives the
i
th line
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{i}
in the line ensemble
\mathcal{C}^{k}
.

Now, the conditional distributions of both
\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,2k\}\times[0,t]}
and
\mathcal{C}^{k}
given
\mathcal{H}
are the laws of
2k\ell
independent Brownian bridges

\{Z_{i,j}:(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,2k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\}\}

with endpoints
Z_{i,j}(s_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1})
and
Z_{i,j}(s_{j})=\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j})
, conditioned on two different sets of nonintersection events.

Since the nonintersection conditions for
\mathcal{L}
are stronger than those of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, the total variation distance of their laws is simply the probability that
\mathcal{C}^{k}
does not satisfy the nonintersection conditions for
\mathcal{L}
. This is

\mathbb{P}(D),\;\;\text{ where }D=\{\text{$\mathcal{C}^{k}$ intersects itself or the lower boundary $\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}$}\}.

(64)

Because the bridges
A_{i,j}
were conditioned both to be nonintersecting and not to intersect
\mathcal{L}_{2k+1}
, the only way that the event in (
64
) can hold is if either

•

B_{J_{j}-1,j}
intersects
A_{J_{j},j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots\ell\}
, or

•

a pair of bridges
B_{i,j}
and
B_{i+1,j}
intersect for some
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},i\in\{1,\dots,J_{j}-2\}
where
(i,j)
and
(i+1,j)
are not connected by an edge in
G_{2k}(t,\ell,k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
.

Therefore letting
E_{i,j}
be the event where the bridge
B_{i,j}
leaves a channel of width
k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}/2
around the line between its endpoints, we have

D\subset R^{c}\cup\bigcup_{j\in[1,\ell],i\in[1,J_{j}-1]}E_{i,j}.

(65)

We now bound
\mathbb{P}(E_{i,j}\;|\;\mathcal{H})
. Let
Q_{i,j}
be the slope of the line segment connecting the points
(s_{j-1},\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j-1}))
and
(s_{j},\mathcal{L}_{i}(s_{j}))
and let
M
be the size of the largest component of
G_{2k}(t,\ell,k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
. Each of the bridges
B_{i,j}
has slope
Q_{i,j}
and is conditioned to avoid at most

other bridges. Therefore for all
j\in[1,\ell]
and
i\in[1,J_{j-1}]
, we can apply Proposition
3.5
to the bridge
B_{i,j}
on the interval
[s_{j-1},s_{j}]
to get that conditionally on
\mathcal{H}
, we have that

|B_{i,j}(s+r)-B_{i,j}(s)-Q_{i,j}r|\leq P_{i,j}\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{2t}{\ell r}\right)

(66)

for all

for a random constant
P_{i,j}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}\left(P_{i,j}\geq m\;|\;\mathcal{H}\right)\leq e^{cM-dm^{2}}.

The right hand side of (

) is always bounded above by
P_{i,j}\sqrt{t/\ell}
, so
E_{i,j}
implies the event
P_{i,j}\geq\frac{1}{2}k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}\sqrt{\ell/t}
. Therefore we get that

\mathbb{P}(E_{i,j}\;|\;\mathcal{H})\leq\exp\left(bM-dk^{-2/3-\gamma/2}\ell/t\right)\leq\exp\left(bM-dk^{\gamma/2}\right).

(67)

Taking a union bound over

in (

), on the
\mathcal{H}
-measurable event

we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j\in[1,\ell],i\in[1,J_{j}-1]}E_{i,j}\;\;\bigg|\;\;\mathcal{H}\right)\leq k\ell\exp\left(bk^{\gamma/12}-dk^{\gamma/2}\right)\leq b\ell k\exp\left(-dk^{\gamma/2}\right).

(68)

In the second inequality, we have used that
\gamma\geq c\log\log k/\log k
to ensure that
k^{\gamma/2}\gg k^{\gamma/12}
as

.
Now we bound
\mathbb{P}(M>k^{\gamma/12})
to ensure that (
68
) holds with high probability.
By Proposition
5.9
, for any
m>0
we have that

\mathbb{P}(M>m)\leq\ell[b(1+\gamma^{3})m\log k]^{m}k^{1-3(\gamma/4){\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}.

In particular, setting

we get that

\mathbb{P}(M>k^{\gamma/12})\leq\ell[b(1+\gamma^{3})k^{\gamma/12}\log k]^{k^{\gamma/12}}k^{1-3(\gamma/4){\left\lfloor k^{\gamma/12}/6\right\rfloor}}\leq\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}.

For the second inequality we have used that
\gamma\in[c\log\log k/\log k,2]
, which ensures that
k^{\gamma/12}\gg\log k\gg 1+\gamma^{3}
as

.

Hence by combining (
63
), (

), and (

), we have

\mathbb{P}(D\;|\;\mathcal{H})\leq\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}+b\ell ke^{-dk^{\gamma/2}}

with probability at least

. Averaging over
\mathcal{H}
shows that
\mathbb{P}(D)\leq\ell e^{-d\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}
, completing the proof of Theorem

.
∎

When using the bridge representation, it is important that we have a good understanding of the graph

. In this vein, we conclude this section with an estimate on the density of nonisolated vertices in

.

While this next proposition is not used in the remainder of the paper, we include it anyways for an important application in
[

]
and to demonstrate how edge locations in the graph

can be controlled. Note that we have not tried to optimize the parameter bounds in Proposition

. In particular, the restriction on

Proposition 7.4
.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,2]
, and let
k\in{\rm Nature},t\in(0,\infty),\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let the graph
G:=G_{2k}(t,\ell,k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
be as in Definition
5.8
. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, let

V_{j}=\{x\in\{1,\dots,2k\}:\deg_{G}(x,j)\geq 1\}.

In other words,
V_{j}
is the set of vertices in
G
with second coordinate
j
that are connected to at least one other vertex. Then for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
there exist constants
c_{\alpha},d_{\alpha}
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N},i\in\{{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}+1,\dots,2k\},
and
m\leq k^{\alpha/2}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(|V_{j}\cap\{i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor},\dots,i\}|>mk^{\alpha-3\gamma/4})\leq c_{\alpha}e^{-d_{\alpha}m}.

(69)

In the proof,
c^{\prime},d>0
are constants that may change from line to line.

Proof.

Fix
k_{\alpha}
large enough so that for all
k\geq k_{\alpha}
and
i\leq 2k
, we have
k^{\alpha/2}\geq\beta\log(i+1)
, where
\beta
is as in Corollary
5.3
. We first prove the proposition for
k<k_{\alpha}
. In this case,
m\leq k_{\alpha}^{\alpha/2}\leq k_{\alpha}^{1/2}
. Therefore we can always guarantee that the right hand side of (
69
) is greater than
1
by possibly modifying
c_{\alpha},d_{\alpha}
.

Now suppose that
k\geq k_{\alpha}
. Fix
i\in\{{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}+1,\dots,2k\}
, and let

a_{i}=\kappa i^{2/3}+k^{\alpha/2}i^{-1/3},\qquad\ell_{i}=ca_{i}^{-2}k^{1+\alpha},\qquad I_{i}=[-a_{i},-a_{i}+\ell_{i}],

for a large constant
c
to be determined in the proof. For any
s\in\mathds{R}
and
i\in\{{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}+1,\dots,2k\}
we claim that

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{L}_{i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}}(s)+s^{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{i}(s)+s^{2}\}\nsubseteq I_{i})\leq 2e^{-k^{\alpha/2}/5}.

(70)

By the ordering of the points
\mathcal{L}_{j}(s)
and a union bound, the left side of (
70
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{L}_{i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}}(s)+s^{2}>-a_{i}+\ell_{i})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{i}(s)+s^{2}<a_{i}).

(71)

To bound the two probabilities in (

), we will use Corollary
5.3
. In order to fruitfully apply this corollary to bound the first probability above, we will need a lower bound on

. We will show that

\ell_{i}\geq a_{i}-\kappa(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{2/3}+k^{\alpha/2}(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{-1/3}

(72)

for all

. First, since
i\geq k^{\alpha}\geq k^{\alpha/2}
, we have that

\ell_{i}=\frac{ck^{1+\alpha}}{a_{i}^{2}}\geq\frac{c}{(\kappa+1)^{2}}k^{1+\alpha}i^{-4/3}\geq\frac{c}{2(\kappa+1)^{2}}k^{\alpha}i^{-1/3}.

(73)

The second inequality uses that

\displaystyle a_{i}-\kappa(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{2/3}+k^{\alpha/2}(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{-1/3}

\displaystyle\leq 2k^{\alpha/2}(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{-1/3}+\kappa[i^{2/3}-(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{2/3}]

\displaystyle\leq 2k^{\alpha/2}(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{-1/3}+\kappa i^{-1/3}k^{\alpha}

) for all

as long as

Therefore (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i}(s)+s^{2}<-k^{\alpha/2}i^{-1/3}-\kappa i^{2/3}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor}}(s)+s^{2}>-\kappa(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{2/3}+k^{\alpha/2}(i-{\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor})^{-1/3}\right).

Since

Now using that

By combining this with the bound in (

8
A stronger modulus of continuity for parabolic Airy lines

In this section we give an application of the bridge representation to get a modulus of continuity bound on parabolic Airy lines that has a better
k
-dependence than Theorem
6.2
. Theorem
1.4
is an immediate corollary of our next theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

There exists a constant
d>0
such that for any
t>0
, we have

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,t]}\frac{|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+r^{-1})\log^{d}k}>1\right)<\infty.

(75)

Moreover, for any
t>0
, we have that

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,t]}\frac{|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+r^{-1})\log^{d}k}<\infty\qquad\text{ almost surely }.

The second statement follows from the first by the Borel-Cantelli lemma and Theorem
6.2
. Note that if we remove the supremum over
k
, then the second statement is implied by Theorem
6.2
. However, if we simply apply that theorem and take a union bound, we need to put a factor of
\sqrt{k}
in the denominator, rather than a power of
\log k
.

On small time intervals (i.e. if we only take the supremum over
s,s+r\in[0,t]
with

for some fixed
\gamma>0
), a variant of our proof will give the power
\log^{1/2}k
instead of
\log^{d}(k)
, which is the same as what one would get for sequences of independent Brownian motions.

Throughout the proof,
b
and
d
are large positive constants and
d_{0}
is a small positive constant. These may change from line to line. The constant
b
may depend on
t
, but
d
and
d_{0}
will not.

Proof.

We will show that there exists a
d>0
and a
t
-dependent constant
b
such that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,t]}\frac{|\mathcal{L}_{k}(s)-\mathcal{L}_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+r^{-1})\log^{d}k}>b\right)<\infty

(76)

This immediately implies (
75
) with

replaced by
d+1
.
Let
\mathcal{B}^{k}
denote the bridge representation

\mathcal{B}^{k}\left(t,\ell_{k},k^{-1/3-\gamma/4}\right),\qquad\text{ where }\ell_{k}={\left\lceil tk^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}.

with
\gamma=c\log\log(k)/\log(k)
. Here

is a constant that can be chosen to be large enough so that each of the subsequent steps in the proof goes through.
By Theorem
7.2
, for all

such that

, we can couple the bridge representations
\mathcal{B}^{k}
to
\mathcal{L}
so that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}^{k}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]}\neq\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]})\leq{\left\lceil tk^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}e^{-d_{0}\gamma k^{\gamma/12}}.

By noting that
k^{\gamma}=\log^{c}k
, as long as
c
was chosen large enough, the right hand side above is bounded by
k^{-2}
for all large enough
k
and so

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}^{k}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]}\neq\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,t]})<\infty.

In particular, this means that it suffices to prove (
76
) with the
k
th line of
\mathcal{B}^{k}
, denoted by
B_{k}
, in place of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
. Specifically, it is enough to show that for large enough
b
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,t]}\frac{|B_{k}(s)-B_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+r^{-1})\log^{d}k}>b\right)=O(k^{-2}).

(77)

Define

s_{i}=it/\ell_{k}\quad\;\text{for}\;\quad i\in\left\{0,\dots,\ell_{k}\right\}.

Let
L_{k}
denote the function which is equal to
B_{k}
at the grid points
s_{i}
and affine on each of the intervals
[s_{i-1},s_{i}]
and set
X_{k}=B_{k}-L_{k}
. By the triangle inequality and a union bound, it suffices to prove the bound in (
77
) for
L_{k}
and
X_{k}
separately. Letting
a_{k}(s)=\kappa k^{2/3}+s^{2}
, Corollary
5.3
and a union bound implies that there exists
c^{\prime}>0
such that for all large enough
b
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|B_{k}(s_{i})+a_{k}(s_{i})|>b\log(k)k^{-1/3}\text{ for some }i\in\left\{0,\dots,\ell_{k}\right\}\right)\leq te^{(c^{\prime}-b)\log k/5}

(78)

for all
k\geq 2
. For every
t
, there exists a
b_{0}
such that the right hand side of (
78
) is bounded above by
k^{-2}
for all
b\geq b_{0}
and
k\geq 2
.

Now, on the event in (
78
) with
b=b_{0}
, the slope of each of the linear pieces of
L
is bounded above by

2b_{0}\log(k)k^{-1/3}\ell_{k}/t+2t\leq 2b_{1}k^{1/3+\gamma}\log k,

(79)

where
b_{1}\geq b_{0}
is another
t
-dependent constant. Here the additive factor of
2t
comes from the parabolic shape.
Therefore on the event in (
78
), for
s,s+r\in[0,t]
with
r\leq k^{-2/3-2\gamma}
we have

|L_{k}(s)-L_{k}(s+r)|\leq 2b_{1}\sqrt{r}\log k.

(80)

Now, again on the event in (
78
) with
b=b_{0}
, we have that

|L_{k}(s)+a_{k}(s)|\leq b_{0}k^{-1/3}\log k+\frac{t^{2}}{4\ell_{k}^{2}}\leq b_{2}k^{-1/3}\log k.

(81)

for all
s\in[0,t]
, where
b_{2}\geq b_{0}
is a
t
-dependent constant. Therefore for all
r
, we have

|L_{k}(s)-L_{k}(s+r)|\leq 2tr+2b_{2}k^{-1/3}\log k.

where the factor of
2tr
again comes from the parabolic shape. Since
r\leq t
, we have
2tr\leq 2t^{3/2}r^{1/2}
. Therefore for
r\geq k^{-2/3-2\gamma}=k^{-2/3}\log^{-2c}(k)
and
k\geq 2
, we have

|L_{k}(s)-L_{k}(s+r)|\leq(2t^{3/2}+2b_{2}\log^{c+1}(k))\sqrt{r}\leq b_{3}\log^{c+1}(k)\sqrt{r}

(82)

where
b_{3}
is another
t
-dependent constant. Combining (
82
), (
80
), and the
O(k^{-2})
tail bound on (
78
) for
b\geq b_{0}
implies the bound in (
77
) for
L_{k}
.

To get a modulus of continuity bound on
X_{k}
, we use the modulus of continuity bounds on nonintersecting Brownian bridges established in Proposition
3.5
. First we need to bound the size of components in the underlying graph
G^{k}=G_{2k}(t,\ell_{k},k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})
that gives rise to the bridge representation
\mathcal{B}^{k}
. Letting
M^{k}
be the size of the largest component in
G^{k}
, applying Proposition
5.9
with
\ell={\left\lceil tk^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}
and
\delta=k^{-\gamma/4}
gives that for every
m>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}>m)\leq k^{2}[bm\log(k)]^{m}[\log(k)]^{-\frac{3c}{4}{\left\lfloor m/6\right\rfloor}}.

To get this bound we have again used the observation that
k^{\gamma}=\log^{c}k
, as well as the crude bound that
{\left\lceil tk^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}\leq k
for large enough
k
. In particular, setting
m=\log^{c/16}k
, as long as
c
is large enough we get that

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}>\log^{c/16}k)\leq k^{-2}

(83)

for all large enough
k
. Let
F_{k}
denote the event where
M_{k}\leq\log^{c/16}k
. Proposition
3.5
applied to the bridge
B_{k}
on the interval
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
implies that for every
m>0
and every
i\in\{0,\dots,\ell_{k}\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[s_{i},s_{i+1}]}\frac{|X_{k}(s)-X_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(2\ell_{k}/r)}>m\;\bigg|\;\;F_{k}\right)\leq e^{d\log^{c/16}(k)-d_{0}m^{2}}.

Taking
m=b\log^{c/32}k
in the above expression for a large
b
and simplifying, we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[s_{i},s_{i+1}]}\frac{|X_{k}(s)-X_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+1/r)\log^{1+c/32}(k)}>b\;\bigg|\;\;F_{k}\right)\leq k^{-d_{0}b}.

Taking a union bound over the intervals
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
and using that
X_{k}=0
at the grid points
s_{i}
then yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,1]}\frac{|X_{k}(s)-X_{k}(s+r)|}{\sqrt{r}\log^{1/2}(1+1/r)\log^{1+c/32}(k)}>b\;\bigg|\;\;F_{k}\right)\leq\ell_{k}k^{-d_{0}b}.

Combining this with (
83
) gives the bound in (
77
) for
X_{k}
, completing the proof of (
76
).
