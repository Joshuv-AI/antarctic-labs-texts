A type of algebraic structure related to sets of intervals Thanks: Readable at http://math.berkeley.edu/~gbergman/papers/ .
George M. Bergman

Abstract
. F. Wehrung has asked: Given a family 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of subsets of a set Ω , \Omega, under what conditions will there exist a total ordering on Ω \Omega with respect to which every member of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is convex? We look at the family 𝒫 \mathcal{P} of subsets of Ω \Omega generated by 𝒞 \mathcal{C} under certain partial operations which preserve convexity; we determine the possible structures of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} if 𝒞 , \mathcal{C}, and hence 𝒫 , \mathcal{P}, is finite, and note a condition on that structure that is necessary and sufficient for there to exist an ordering of Ω \Omega of the desired sort. From this we obtain a criterion which works without the finiteness hypothesis on 𝒞 . \mathcal{C}. We establish bounds on the cardinality of the set 𝒫 \mathcal{P} generated by an n \!n\! -element set 𝒞 . \mathcal{C}. We end by noting some other ways of answering Wehrung’s question, using results in the literature.

1.
Introduction

If
(\Omega,\leq)
is a totally ordered set, we shall call a subset

A
of
\Omega

convex
if for

p,\,q,\,r\in\Omega
with
p\leq q\leq r,
the conditions

p,\,r\in A
imply
q\in A.

(I use the word “interval” in the title, since
it concisely suggests “convex subset of a totally ordered set”;
but in general, I shall write “convex set”.
The word “interval” will, however, come up in §
7
,
in connection with results in the literature.)

This note answers the question:

(1)

(F. Wehrung, personal correspondence related to
[
8
]
;
slightly reworded.)
Let
\Omega
be a set (usually finite), and

\mathcal{C}
a set of subsets of
\Omega.

When does there exist a total order on
\Omega
with respect to
which every member of
\mathcal{C}
is convex?

In §
2
, we will show that given a subset

\mathcal{C}
of
\Omega,
there exists a total ordering
\leq

on
\Omega
having the
desired property for
\mathcal{C}
if and only if for every finite subset

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C},
there exists a total
ordering
\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
on
\Omega
having that property for
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.

Thus, the general problem reduces to the
corresponding problem for finite
\mathcal{C}.

In §
3
we note some natural partial operations on the
set of convex subsets of a totally ordered set
\Omega,

which can be described solely in set-theoretic
terms, and we call a set
\mathcal{P}
of
subsets of an arbitrary set
\Omega
which is closed under
these partial operations a “patchwork”.
Thus, a set
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
\Omega
has
all its members convex under some total
ordering
\leq
on
\Omega
if and only if the patchwork
\mathcal{P}

that it generates has the same property.
Moreover, the patchwork generated by a finite set
\mathcal{C}

of subsets of
\Omega
is contained in the Boolean ring
of sets generated by
\mathcal{C},
hence is again finite;
so question (
1
) for finite sets
\mathcal{C}

comes down to the same question for finite patchworks
\mathcal{P}.

In §
4
we determine the structures of all
finite patchworks
\mathcal{P},
and note for which of these
there exist orderings of
\Omega
making all members of
\mathcal{P}
convex.
In §
5
, we combine this result with that
of §
2
to
get a condition on a not necessarily finite patchwork
which is necessary and sufficient for the existence of such an ordering.

In §
6
we examine how many elements the
patchwork
\mathcal{P}
generated by an
\!n\!
-element set
\mathcal{C}
can have.

After an earlier draft of this note was sent
out, it was pointed out to me that there
are results in the literature that can be used to answer
question (
1
) in other ways.
These are noted in §
7
.
So perhaps the main value of this paper is the detailed
structure it reveals of the families
\mathcal{P}
arising from families
\mathcal{C}

for which question (
1
) has a positive answer – and
possibly also the corresponding information when
question (
1
) is not assumed to have a positive answer;
whether this has interesting applications I do not know.

We remark that the arguments of §
4
that give
the structures of all finite patchworks
are rather lengthy and intricate; but the reasoning is elementary.
In contrast, the argument below that reduces
question (
1
) to the finite case
calls on the Compactness Theorem of model theory,
or, alternatively, on ultraproducts.
So that proof, though brief, is the
one non-elementary piece of reasoning in this note.

2.
Reduction to the case of finite
\mathcal{C}

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Omega
be a set, and
\mathcal{C}
a set of subsets of
\Omega.

Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)
There exists a total order on
\Omega
with respect to
which every member of
\mathcal{C}
is convex.

(ii)
For each finite subset
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C},

there exists a total order on
\Omega
with respect to
which every member of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is convex.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii) is clear, since an ordering that works
for
\mathcal{C}
works for any subset
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}.

The reader familiar with the Compactness Theorem of model theory

[
3
, Corollary 5.6]
will see that that theorem implies the reverse implication.
(One uses a language with a
constant for each element of
\Omega,
a unary relation
for each member of
\mathcal{C},
and a binary relation
{\leq}.)

I sketch below a variant of this proof
that uses ultraproducts
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
rather than
the Compactness Theorem.

Assume (ii).
Let
X
be the set of all finite subsets
of
\mathcal{C},
for each
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in X
let
J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\subseteq X
be the
set of all
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}\in X
which contain
\mathcal{C}^{\prime},

and let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of all sets
J\subseteq X

which for some
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in X
contain
J_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}.

Since
J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\cap J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}}=J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cup\,\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}},
we see that

\mathcal{F}
is a filter on
X,
and since no
J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
is empty,

\mathcal{F}
does not contain the empty set,
i.e., it is a proper filter.

Hence we can choose an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on
X

containing
\mathcal{F}.

By (ii), we can choose for each
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in X
a total ordering

\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
on
\Omega
with respect to which all
members of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are convex.
The ultraproduct of the totally ordered sets

(\Omega,\,\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}})

(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in X)
with respect to
\mathcal{U}

will be a totally ordered set
(\Omega^{*},\,\leq^{*}),

such that for each

A\in\mathcal{C},
the ultrapower of
A
with respect to
\mathcal{U}

is a convex subset
A^{*}
of
\Omega^{*}.

(A^{*}
is convex because
A
is convex with respect to “almost all”
the chosen orderings
\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}};

i.e., the set of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
such that
A
is convex with
respect to
\leq_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
contains
J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}},
hence
belongs to
\mathcal{U}.)

There is a natural embedding of
\Omega
in
its ultrapower
\Omega^{*},
and for each
A\in\mathcal{C},

the inverse image in
\Omega
of
A^{*}\subseteq\Omega^{*}
is
A.

Hence the restriction of
\leq^{*}
to
\Omega
is a
total ordering
\leq
under which each
A\in\mathcal{C}
is convex, proving (i).
∎

3.
Patchworks

The kind of structure we will call a patchwork is motivated by

Lemma 2
.

If
(\Omega,\,\leq)
is a totally ordered set, and
A,~B

are convex subsets of
\Omega
which have nonempty intersection, and
neither of which contains the other, then
A\cap B,

A\cup B,
and
A\setminus B
are also convex.

Also,
\emptyset
and
\Omega
are convex.
∎

(Each of the conclusions of the first sentence of Lemma
2

needs less than the full set of assumptions on
A
and
B.

But the cases where the unnecessary assumptions fail are
trivial, so we lose nothing in using this formulation.)

Definition 3
.

Let
\Omega
be a set.

We will say that subsets
A,\,B\subseteq\Omega
overlap
if they have nonempty intersection, but neither contains the other.

For any set
\Omega,
a patchwork of subsets of
\Omega

(
which may be shortened to “a patchwork on
\Omega
” or
simply “a patchwork” when the context makes
\Omega
clear
)

will mean a set
\mathcal{P}
of subsets of
\Omega
such that
for every pair of overlapping sets

A,\,B\in\mathcal{P},
the set
\mathcal{P}
also contains
A\cap B,

A\cup B,
and
A\setminus B,
and such that

\emptyset
and
\Omega
also belong to
\mathcal{P}.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a patchwork on
\Omega,

members of
\mathcal{P}
will be called
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets.

We note

Lemma 4
.

The patchwork
\mathcal{P}
on a set
\Omega
generated by a
finite set
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
\Omega
is finite.

Proof.

\mathcal{P}
will be contained in the Boolean ring of subsets of
\Omega

generated by
\mathcal{C},
and a finitely generated Boolean ring is finite.
∎

4.
The case of finite
\mathcal{P}.

Convention 5
.

Throughout this section
\Omega
will be a fixed
set, and
\mathcal{P}
a finite patchwork on
\Omega.

A key to analyzing the structure of
\mathcal{P}
is
to look at those of its members to which its
partial operations
cannot
be applied:

Definition 6
.

A
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
A
will be called autonomous
if it is nonempty, and does not overlap any member of
\mathcal{P}

(
cf. Definition
3
)
.
We will generally shorten “autonomous
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set” to
“autonomous set”.

If
A\in\mathcal{P}
is autonomous,
then the set of maximal autonomous proper subsets of
A

will be called the cohort under
A.

Elements of
A
that are not in the union of the cohort
under
A
will be called non-cohort elements of
A.

Note that

Lemma 7
.

Any two autonomous members of a patchwork
\mathcal{P}

are either disjoint, or one contains the other.

Proof.

By the definition of “autonomous” this is, in fact, true of any
two
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets at least
one
of which is autonomous.
∎

Thus the autonomous members of
\mathcal{P}

form a tree under inclusion, branching
downward from the top element,
\Omega.

It follows that for every proper subset
A
of
\Omega,

there is a least autonomous
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
B
properly containing
A.

Note that every
minimal

autonomous set
A
consists entirely of non-cohort elements.
Non-minimal autonomous
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets necessarily have nonempty cohorts
under them; we shall see that
they may or may not also have non-cohort elements.
Each element of
\Omega
is a non-cohort element of a
unique autonomous set, namely, the least autonomous
set containing it.

Not every union of autonomous sets need be a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set, but
the converse is true:

Lemma 8
.

Let
A
be a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.
Then

(i)

A
is a union of autonomous sets; in fact
it is the disjoint union of the maximal
elements of the set of autonomous sets which it contains.

Moreover, if
A\neq\Omega,
then letting
B
be the least
autonomous set properly containing
A,
we have

(ii)
The maximal autonomous sets contained in
A

(
cf.
(i))

all belong to the cohort under
B.

(iii)
If
A^{\prime}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set which overlaps

A,
then the least
autonomous set properly containing
A^{\prime}
is also
B.

(iv)
If
A
is not autonomous, then
it can be written as the disjoint union of two proper
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subsets,
each of which is a union of subsets of the cohort under
B.

Proof.

(i): If
A
is autonomous this is
trivial: it is the union of the -element family
\{A\}.

If
A
is not autonomous,
assume inductively that all
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets properly contained in
A

are unions of autonomous sets.
Since
A
is not autonomous, it overlaps some
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
A^{\prime}.

Then
A\cap A^{\prime}
and
A\setminus A^{\prime}
are
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
properly contained in
A,
so by inductive hypothesis,
they are unions of autonomous sets, hence so is their union,
A.

That
A
is the union of the
maximal
autonomous sets
that it contains follows, since
\mathcal{P}
is finite.
These sets are disjoint by Lemma
7
.

(ii): If
A
is autonomous, then
A
is
the unique maximal autonomous subset of
A,

and the conclusion is immediate.

If
A
is not autonomous, suppose, by way of contradiction,
that some maximal autonomous subset
C
of
A
belonged to a different
cohort from the cohort under
B;
say the cohort
under an autonomous set
D.

This makes
D
the least autonomous set properly containing
C;

but
C\subset A\subset B,
so
B
is an
autonomous set properly containing
C,
so
D\subset B,

so as
B
is the least autonomous subset containing
A,

A
is not contained in
D.

Neither can
A
contain
D;
if it did,
C
would
not be a
maximal
autonomous subset of
A.

So
A
overlaps
D;
but
this contradicts the assumption that
D
is autonomous,
completing the proof.

(iii): We shall show that
B
contains
A^{\prime}.

Then, by the same argument with the roles
of
A
and
A^{\prime}
reversed, the least autonomous set
B^{\prime}

properly containing
A^{\prime}
contains
A.

Thus,
B
and
B^{\prime}
must be the same, giving the desired assertion.

Note that
A\cup A^{\prime}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set which is
not disjoint from
B,
so by the autonomy of
B,

it must either be contained in
B
or properly contain
B.

If the latter were true, then
A^{\prime}
would have elements outside

B,
and also contain the
nonempty set
B\setminus A;
but since it does
not contain all of
A\subset B,
it would not contain
B,

contradicting the autonomy of
B.

So
A\cup A^{\prime}
must be contained in
B,

so
A^{\prime}
is contained in
B,
as required.

(iv): Since
A
is nonautonomous, we can choose a

\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
C
which it overlaps.
Thus, by (iii), the least autonomous set containing
C
is
B,

so by (i) and (ii),
A
and
C
are both unions
of members of the cohort under
B.

Now
A
is the union of the disjoint nonempty
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets

A\cap C
and
A\setminus C;

so this is a decomposition of desired sort.
∎

The above results suggest
the question: Given an autonomous set with nonempty cohort under it,

which
unions of subsets of this cohort can be
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets?
We will use the following concept.
(We define it here for general
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets,
but until the last result of this section,
we will only use it for autonomous sets.)

Definition 9
.

Two
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
A
and
B
will be called adjacent
if they are disjoint, and their union is again a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.

The adjacency graph of a family of
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
will mean the graph having the members of this family as
vertices, and having an edge between a pair of such
vertices if and only if they are adjacent as
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets.

The word “adjacent” is, of course, motivated by the case where

\mathcal{P}
consists of sets convex under a total ordering
\leq
on
\Omega.

But our definition is not limited to that case;
and even in that case, two sets
A,\,B\in\mathcal{P}

that are adjacent as convex subsets of
\Omega
need not be
adjacent under our present definition, if our patchwork

\mathcal{P}
doesn’t happen to include
A\cup B.

Lemma 10
.

A subset
A
of
\Omega
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set if and only
if it can be written as the union of a family of
autonomous sets whose adjacency graph is connected.

Proof.

The statement is trivial if
A
is an autonomous
set or
\emptyset,
so assume that neither of these is the case.

To get the “if” direction, suppose
A
is the union of
a family of more than one autonomous sets which has
connected adjointness graph.
Note that the implication in this direction is, in fact, also
trivially true if the graph in question has exactly
2
vertices,
by the definition of adjacency;
so let us assume it has
\geq 3
vertices.
Let us also assume inductively that the corresponding implication
is true for every set
A^{\prime}
that can be
written as the union of a family of fewer
autonomous sets than we are using for
A.

Let us now choose a vertex
B
in the graph for
A

such that the subgraph obtained by removing
B,
i.e.,
the adjacency graph of the family with union
A\setminus B,

is still connected.
(Such a vertex exists by
[
1
, Theorem 3.2.10]
.)
Thus, by our inductive assumption,
A\setminus B
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.
Let
C
be a vertex of the graph for
A
which is adjacent to
B.

Since the graph for
A
has
\geq 3
vertices,
it has vertices other than
B
and
C.

Hence neither of the
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
A\setminus B
and
B\cup C

contains the other, moreover, they overlap in
C;
so their union,

A,
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set, as desired.

Conversely, let
A
be a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set which is neither autonomous
nor empty, and
B
be the least autonomous set containing
A.

By Lemma
8
(i) and (ii),
A
is the union
of a subset of the cohort under
B;
we wish to show that
the adjacency graph of this expression for
A
is connected.
Again, this is clear if that graph has
\leq 2
vertices,
so suppose it has at least
3,
and assume by induction
that every subfamily of the cohort under
B
whose union is
a proper
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subset of
A
corresponds to a connected
subgraph of that graph.

Since
A
is non-autonomous,
Lemma
8
(iv) gives us a decomposition of our
expression for
A
into two disjoint subfamilies, corresponding
to disjoint
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
with union
A.

By our inductive assumption, the graphs corresponding
to these
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets are connected, so to show that the
graph corresponding to
A
is connected, it will suffice
to find a connected subgraph thereof
that meets both the indicated subgraphs.
Again invoking our inductive assumption, we will have this if
we can find a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
A_{3}\subset A

which is a union of members of the cohort under
B,

and which meets both
A_{1}
and
A_{2}.

Since the graph associated with
A
had at least
3
vertices,
one of the two subgraphs we have obtained must have more than one
vertex; i.e., one of our two
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subsets,
say
A_{1},
must be non-autonomous.
Let
C
be a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set which overlaps
A_{1}.

By Lemma
8
(iii),
C
is also a union of
members of the cohort under
B.

If
C
also has nonempty intersection with
A_{2},

then we see that
A\cap C
will be a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subset
A_{3}

of the sort we need.
On the other hand, if
C\cap A_{2}=\emptyset,
then the
fact that
C\not\subseteq A_{1}
implies
C\not\subseteq A,

so
A\setminus C
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set, and will serve as our
A_{3}.

∎

(Remark: In in the above result, if
A
is not autonomous, then
it will have a
unique
expression of the indicated sort.
If it
is
autonomous, then as noted, the expression as the
union of the singleton
\{A\}
is always of the indicated sort;
in this case
A
may or may not have another such expression.
Namely, we will see later that an autonomous
set
A
may or may not be the union of
the cohort under it, and that if it is, the adjacency graph
for that decomposition may or may not be connected.)

To lead up to our criterion for the existence of an
ordering of
\Omega
making all
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets convex,
we look next at two conditions which (it is not hard to see)
are incompatible with the existence of such an ordering.

Lemma 11
.

If an autonomous set
A
is adjacent to more than two
other autonomous sets, then every two autonomous
sets adjacent to
A
are adjacent to one another.

In fact, when this is true, then
in the adjacency graph of the cohort containing
A,

the connected component of
A
has an edge between every two vertices.

Proof.

Let
B
be the least autonomous set properly containing
A;

thus, the lemma concerns the cohort under
B.

To get the first assertion, we first consider any
two autonomous sets
C\neq C^{\prime}
adjacent to
A.

By the adjacency assumption,
A\cup C
and
A\cup C^{\prime}

are
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets, and they clearly overlap,
so their union
A\cup C\cup C^{\prime}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.
Now by our hypothesis there is at least one other autonomous
set
C^{\prime\prime}
adjacent to
A,
so
A\cup C^{\prime\prime}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set
which overlaps
A\cup C\cup C^{\prime};

hence
(A\cup C\cup C^{\prime})\setminus(A\cup C^{\prime\prime})=C\cup C^{\prime}

is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set, i.e.,
C
and
C^{\prime}
are adjacent, as claimed.

To get the second assertion, let us first show that
in the situation of the above paragraph, if
C

is adjacent to yet another member
D
of the cohort under
B,

then
A
is also adjacent to
D.

Indeed, since
C
is adjacent to the three
sets
A,

C^{\prime}
and
D,
the first assertion of the lemma shows
that these are adjacent to one another,
in particular,
A
and
D
are adjacent.
By induction, all members of the connected component in question
are adjacent to
A,

hence by yet another application of the first assertion
of the lemma, they are all adjacent to one another.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}

(n\geq 3)
are distinct autonomous sets
forming a “cycle of adjacency”, i.e.,
such that
A_{i}
is adjacent to
A_{i+1}

(1\leq i<n),

and
A_{n}
to
A_{1}.

Then, again, in the adjacency graph of the cohort containing
the
A_{i},
the connected component containing
these elements has an edge between every two vertices.

Proof.

First suppose
n\geq 4.

Then
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}
and

A_{3}\cup\dots\cup A_{n}\cup A_{1}
will be overlapping
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets,
hence their intersection,
A_{1}\cup A_{3}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set; i.e.,

A_{1}
and
A_{3}
are adjacent.
Hence
A_{3}
is adjacent to at least the three sets

A_{1},

A_{2}
and
A_{4},
so the preceding lemma is applicable.

On the other hand, suppose
n=3.

If the connected component in question
is simply
\{A_{1},\,A_{2},\,A_{3}\},
we are done.
If not, then by connectedness,
some other member
C
of that connected component
must be adjacent to one of the
A_{i}.

This again makes that
A_{i}
adjacent to three distinct
members of the component, so again, the preceding lemma is applicable.
∎

We can now prove

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite patchwork, and
B\in\mathcal{P}
an autonomous set.
Then exactly one of the following statements is true:

(i)
The cohort under
B
has at least
3
members,
every two members of that cohort are adjacent,
and the union of the cohort is all of
B.

(ii)
The cohort under
B
has at least
2
members,
the adjacency graph of that cohort is a path

(
i.e., consists of elements
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
for some
n\geq 2,

with
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
adjacent if and only if
|i-j|=1),

and again, the union of the cohort is all of
B.

(iii)
No two members of the cohort under
B
are adjacent.

(
In this case,
B
may contain non-cohort elements.
If it does,
the cohort under
B
may be empty or nonempty.
)

Proof.

Consider the adjacency graph of the cohort under

If this has any edges, then the members of that cohort
comprising a nontrivial connected component of that
graph will have as union a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subset
A\subseteq B

by Lemma
10
.
If this
A
were not autonomous, then Lemma
8
(iii)
would give a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set

overlapping it,
which by parts (ii) and (i) of that lemma would also
be a union of members of the cohort under
B;
hence

A\cup A^{\prime}
would be a larger
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subset of
B,
and by
Lemma
10
the adjacency graph of that larger

\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set would be a larger connected subgraph of the adjacency

a contradiction.
So

is autonomous.

But as an autonomous
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-subset of

which
properly contains members of the cohort under

must equal

So we conclude that if the adjacency graph of the cohort under

has
any edges, then it is connected and
B
is the union of
that cohort; in particular,

has no non-cohort elements.

By Lemmas

and
12
, if the above
adjacency graph has either any “branching” or any cycles,
it must have an edge between every pair of points, and since
it will also have at least three vertices, it falls under case (i).
On the other hand, a connected graph with at least one edge
which has neither branching nor cycles is a path, so if that is so,
we are in case (ii).
Finally, if the adjacency graph of

has no edges, we are
in case (iii).

(As noted earlier, a minimal autonomous set

has empty cohort under
it, and so necessarily has non-cohort elements.
For examples of autonomous sets having both a nonempty cohort under

non-cohort elements, the reader can examine the case where
\Omega
is a
totally ordered set, and
\mathcal{P}
is the patchwork
generated by an interval

in

whose union is not all of
B.)

∎

We can now answer question (
1
) for finite patchworks.

Theorem 14
.

The following three conditions on a finite patchwork

\mathcal{P}
are equivalent:

(a)

\Omega
admits a total ordering
\leq

under which all
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets are convex.

(b)
No three nonempty
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets
are pairwise adjacent.

(c)
Every autonomous set
B\subseteq\Omega
falls under
case
(ii)
or
(iii)
of
Theorem
13
.

Proof.

(a)
\implies
(b) is intuitively clear.
To supply some details: if a convex set
A
is adjacent to
each of a pair of disjoint convex sets
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime},
then one
of
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
must lie above it and the other below it under
\leq,

otherwise
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
could not be disjoint.
Since
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
have the nonempty

A
between them, their union is not
convex, so they are not adjacent.

(b)
\implies
(c) is immediate, since if some
B

fell under case (i) of Theorem
13
, the
cohort under it would include three pairwise adjacent sets.

To prove (c)
\implies
(a) we shall, assuming (c),
construct recursively an ordering
\leq
as in (a).

Given an autonomous set
B\subseteq\Omega,
assume recursively
that for each
A
in the cohort under
B,
we have constructed
a total ordering on
A
under which all
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets contained
in
A
are convex.
(If
B
is minimal, the cohort under it is empty, and this assumption is
vacuous.)

If
B
falls under case (ii) of Theorem
13
,
then writing the members of the cohort under
B
as

A_{1},\dots,A_{n},
as in that theorem, we order their union

so that
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
form
successive
intervals of
B

(either putting
A_{i}
below
A_{i+1}
for all

or
putting
A_{i}
above
A_{i+1}
for all
i),

with each ordered internally
by the order previously constructed for it.

On the other hand, if

falls under case (iii),
we can order it by arranging the members of the cohort under

in any order, simply making each a convex subset of
B,
again
with the internal ordering previously constructed,
and likewise put the non-cohort elements of

(if any) in any order above, below or between those

We see that for every
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set

if

is the least

-set containing it,
then

becomes convex under the ordering so constructed on

and remains so as we extend this ordering to larger autonomous sets.
Hence when our construction reaches

we have established (a).
∎

Looking back at Theorem
13
, we can turn this upside down, and
describe, somewhat informally, how to construct all finite patchworks:

Start with a finite partially ordered set
T
having the form of
a downward-branching tree, with greatest element denoted
\Omega.

Choose any graph structure on the elements of
T

such that edges, if any, occur only among elements of
T

that lie immediately below a common element, and such that for
each element, the resulting
graph structure on the elements immediately
below it is either a complete graph, a path, or edgeless.
Now assign to each element of
T
such that the graph
structure we have given to the set of elements immediately below it
is edgeless a set that is to be its set of non-cohort
elements, assigning to distinct elements of
T
disjoint sets,
and making the set so assigned to every such
element of
T
having
\leq 1
elements of
T
immediately
below it nonempty.
Let each element of
T
become the name of the union
of the sets of non-cohort elements assigned to it and
to elements of
T

anywhere below it
, and call the
sets so named
autonomous sets
.
(Apologies for this informal step of turning
abstract elements into names for sets!)
In particular,
\Omega
is the name assigned to the set of

all
elements that have been introduced as non-cohort elements.
Finally, let the members of
\mathcal{P}
be the unions of those families
of autonomous sets which, under our graph structure on

form connected subgraphs.

The case of not-necessarily-finite

Returning to Theorem
14
, we can combine it with

and deduce:

Theorem 15
.

Let
\Omega

\mathcal{P}
a

(

)

Then
(

)
,
the following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

\Omega
admits a total ordering
\leq

under which all
\!\mathcal{P}\!

(b)
No three nonempty
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-sets are pairwise adjacent.

Proof.

(a)

.

Conversely, assume (b) holds.
Then the same condition holds for all finite

∎

We could not bring condition (c) of Theorem

into Theorem

on

to a larger one, sets that

For two contrasting examples, let

consist of

and all finite

and let

we could, with slightly

The “subsets of a set” definition seemed simplest for our

6.
The cardinality of a patchwork generated by
n
elements

We noted in Lemma
4
that every finitely generated
patchwork is finite.
Let us get some more precise bounds.

The proof of the next
result uses an observation that we have not yet
stated explicitly: any patchwork
\mathcal{P}
is closed under
pairwise intersections, and hence under all finite intersections.
Indeed, by definition
\mathcal{P}
is closed under intersections of
overlapping elements, while if two elements are comparable,
their intersection is one of them, and if they are disjoint,
their intersection is
\emptyset\in\mathcal{P}.

This gives us all pairwise intersections, and hence, as noted, all
finite intersections.

Proposition 16
.

If
n
is a nonnegative integer,
\Omega
a set, and
\mathcal{P}

a patchwork generated by
n
subsets of
\Omega,
then
\mathcal{P}

has at most
2^{2^{n}-1}+1
elements.
Moreover, for all
n\neq 2,
there exist examples achieving this bound.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}(n)
be the free Boolean ring on
n
generators

[
5
, Theorem 26, p.260, and Corollary 1]
.
This can be described as the Boolean ring of all subsets
of the set
2^{n}
of subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\};
its free
generators are the sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},
where
X_{i}
is
the set of those subsets containing
i.

Thus
\mathcal{B}(n)
has cardinality
2^{2^{n}}.

The nonunital subring
\mathcal{B}(n)^{\mathrm{o}}

of
\mathcal{B}(n)
generated by
the
X_{i}
consists of those members
of
\mathcal{B}(n)
which, like
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},
do
not have as a member the empty subset of
\{1,\dots,n\};

so it has cardinality
2^{2^{n}-1}.

Given any
n
subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
of a set
\Omega,

we can map the free Boolean
ring
\mathcal{B}(n)
homomorphically into the Boolean ring of
all subsets of
\Omega
so as to send each
X_{i}
to
A_{i}.

It is not hard to see that the partial binary operations
of Definition
3
carry the image of

\mathcal{B}(n)^{\mathrm{o}}
into itself, so the elements
of
\mathcal{P}
obtainable using those operations will lie
in that image, as will the element
\emptyset.

The one member of
\mathcal{P}
that may not be contained in
that image is
\Omega,
so counting this,
we see that
\mathcal{P}
must have cardinality
\leq 2^{2^{n}-1}+1.

To get an example where this value is achieved,
let us take
\Omega
to be
2^{n},
defined as above as the set
of all subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\},
and examine
the structure of the patchwork
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega
generated
by the
X_{i}.

Since every pair of
X_{i}
overlap,
\mathcal{P}
will contain
all the sets
X_{i}\setminus X_{j}

(i\neq j).

I now claim that for any
a\in 2^{n}

which contains some but not all of
1,\dots,n,

the singleton
\{a\}
is a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.
Indeed, it is the intersection of
those sets
X_{i}\setminus X_{j}
such that
i\in a
but
j\notin a.

The singleton whose unique member is the improper subset,

\{1,\dots,n\},
is also in
\mathcal{P},
being the intersection
of the
X_{i}
themselves.
On the other hand,
\emptyset\in\Omega

is not contained in any of the
X_{i},

so the singleton
\{\emptyset\}
is not a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set.

This gives us
2^{n}-1
singleton autonomous sets; let us
now look at adjacency relationships among them.
I claim that if two elements
a\neq\,b\in\Omega

are nonempty, and differ only in the presence or
absence of a single one of
1,\dots,n,
then

\{a,\,b\}\in\mathcal{P},
i.e.,
a
and
b
are adjacent.
Indeed, of the
n-1
elements with respect to which
a
and
b
agree,
they must agree in
containing
at least one, otherwise one
of
a
or
b
would be empty.
If they also agree in
not
containing at least one, then,
imitating the trick of the preceding paragraph, we can obtain

\{a,\,b\}
as an appropriate intersection
of sets
X_{i}\setminus X_{j},
while if they contain
all
n-1
elements at which they agree, then,
again using the idea of the preceding paragraph, we find that

\{a,\,b\}
will be an intersection of sets
X_{i}.

Using these adjacency relations,
we can get a chain of adjacency from any
nonempty
a
to the element
\{1,\dots,n\};
so the
adjacency graph of these
2^{n}-1
singleton autonomous
sets is connected.
Moreover, the
n
elements of cardinality
n-1
are all adjacent
to
\{1,\dots,n\},
so if
n\geq 3,
Lemma
11
tells
us that every two of these singletons are adjacent; so the union of
every subfamily of these singletons is
a
\!\mathcal{P}\!
-set, giving
2^{2^{n}-1}
elements.
Bringing in
\Omega
itself
(which is not such a union, because it contains
not only the
2^{n-1}
nonempty subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
but also
the empty subset), we have the asserted
2^{2^{n}-1}+1
elements.

We assumed in the next-to-last sentence that
n\geq 3.

Looking at lower values,
if
n=1,
so that
\{1,\dots,n\}
has a unique nonempty
subset
a,
we have
2^{2^{n}-1}+1=3,
and

\mathcal{P}
indeed has
3
elements,
\emptyset\subset\{a\}\subset\Omega.

If
n=0,
so that
\Omega=\{\{\emptyset\}\},
the empty
set
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
\Omega
generates the patchwork

\{\emptyset,\,\Omega\},
which has cardinality
2=2^{2^{n}-1}+1.

The case
n=2,
on the other hand will fall under the next result.
(See discussion after the proof thereof.)
∎

Proposition 17
.

If
n
is a nonnegative integer,
\Omega
a set, and
\mathcal{P}

a patchwork generated by
n
subsets of
\Omega
whose members
are all convex under some ordering
\leq
on
\Omega,

then
\mathcal{P}
has at most
\binom{2n}{2}+2=2n^{2}-n+2
elements; and
for all
n
there exist examples achieving this bound.

Proof.

To prove the stated bound, suppose
(\Omega,\leq)
is a totally ordered
set, and let us define a “cut”
\chi
in
\Omega
to be a pair

(\chi^{\ell},\,\chi^{u})
(the superscripts standing
for “lower” and “upper”) such that
\Omega
is the disjoint
union of
\chi^{\ell}
and
\chi^{u},
and every element of
\chi^{\ell}

is less than every element of
\chi^{u}.

(So such a cut is equivalent to an isotone map
from
\Omega
to
\{0,1\}.

Note that
\chi^{\ell}
or
\chi^{u}
may be empty.)
Let us call a cut
\chi
“higher than” a cut

\chi^{\prime}
if
\chi^{\ell}\supset{\chi^{\prime}}^{\ell};

clearly the cuts are totally ordered under this relation.

Observe that every nonempty convex subset
A

of
\Omega
is determined by two cuts,
\chi_{A\uparrow}

and
\chi_{A\downarrow},
the former characterized by the property that

\chi_{A\uparrow}^{u}
consists of all elements of
\Omega
that are
greater than all elements of
A
(equivalently, that

\chi_{A\uparrow}^{\ell}
consists of elements
\leq
at least one
element of
A),
the latter by the property that

\chi_{A\downarrow}^{\ell}
consists of the elements that are
less than all elements of
A
(equivalently, that

\chi_{A\downarrow}^{u}
consists of the elements
\geq
at least one
element of
A).

Thus,
A=\chi_{A\uparrow}^{\ell}\cap\chi_{A\downarrow}^{u}.

Given overlapping convex sets
A
and
B,
it is not hard to see
that for each of
A\cup B,

A\cap B,

A\setminus B
and

B\setminus A,
the two cuts determining the set in question
are taken from among the
four cuts
\chi_{A\downarrow},

\chi_{A\uparrow},

\chi_{B\downarrow},
and
\chi_{B\uparrow}.

(Which pair of cuts correspond to each of these depends on whether

A
extends upward from
A\cap B
and
B
downward therefrom,
or vice versa.
For instance, in the former case,
A\setminus B
is determined by
the two cuts
\chi_{A\uparrow}
and
\chi_{B\uparrow}.)

Hence if we start with a set
\mathcal{C}
of
n
convex sets, those of them
that are nonempty determine at most
2n
distinct cuts, and
applying our four partial binary operations recursively,
we get a set of nonempty convex sets each of which is determined by
one of the
\leq\binom{2n}{2}
unordered pairs of distinct
cuts from this set.
Bringing in the sets
\Omega
and
\emptyset
(which may or
may not have belonged to
\mathcal{C},
or, in the case of
\Omega,

arisen via our partial operations), we see that the patchwork
generated by
\mathcal{C}
can have at most
\binom{2n}{2}+2
elements.

Let us now show that for every
n,
this upper bound can be achieved.
For integers
i\leq j,
let us here write
[i,j]
for

\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\mid i\leq k\leq j\}.

Let
\Omega
be the set
[-n,\,n],

under the usual ordering of the integers, and
let
\mathcal{C}
consist of the
n
convex subsets

A_{i}=[-n+i,\,i-1]

(i=1,\dots,n),
each of which has
n
elements.
We shall show below that the closure

\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{C}
under the partial binary
operations in the definition of a patchwork consists of

all
the nonempty convex subsets of
[-n+1,\,n-1].

Note that the latter
\!2n{-}1\!
-element ordered set has
2n
cuts (one
“below” the least element,
2n-2
between successive
elements, and one “above” the greatest element),
hence it has
\binom{2n}{2}
nonempty convex subsets;
once we know that our partial binary operations give
us all these sets, then bringing in
\Omega

(which, since it contains
-n

and
n,
is not in that closure) and
\emptyset,

it will follow that the patchwork generated by
\mathcal{C}

indeed has
\binom{2n}{2}+2
elements.

To show that we get all convex sets

[i,\,j]
with
-n+1\leq i\leq j\leq n-1,

it will suffice to show that we get those of
the forms
[i,\,n-1]
and
[-n+1,\,j],

since
[i,\,j]=[i,\,n-1]\cap[-n+1,\,j].

To get
[i,\,n-1],
note that if
i<0,
that set is the
union of the overlapping sets
[i,\,i+n-1]=A_{i+n}
and

[0,n-1]=A_{n};

if
i=0,
it is
[0,\,n-1]=A_{n},
and if
i>0,
it is a
difference of overlapping sets,

[0,\,n-1]\setminus[-n+i,\,i-1]=A_{n}\setminus A_{i}.

The symmetric argument shows that we can get

[-n+1,\,j],
completing the proof.
∎

Let us observe that for
n\leq 2,
the members
of
every
family
\mathcal{C}
of
n
subsets of a set
\Omega

are convex under some ordering of
\Omega.

This is immediate for
n=0
and
n=1.

To see the case
n=2,
let
\mathcal{C}=\{A_{1},\,A_{2}\}.

If
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are disjoint or one contains
the other, it is again easy to see how to get such an ordering.
If they overlap, then by choosing any ordering in
which all elements of
A_{1}\setminus A_{2}
lie above
those of
A_{1}\cap A_{2},
and these lie above
all elements of
A_{2}\setminus A_{1},
while every element not
in
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
lies above or below all elements
of
A_{1}\cup A_{2},
we again get the desired orderability property.

For
n=0,\,1,\,2,
the upper bounds given by
Proposition
16
are
2,

3
and
9,
while
those given by Proposition
17
are
2,

3
and
8.

Thus, these functions differ first at
n=2.

By the above orderability observation for
n=2,

the upper bound given by Proposition
17
supersedes
that given by Proposition
16
.

A consequence of Proposition
17
is that given
a set
\mathcal{C}
of
n
subsets of a finite set
\Omega,
one can
determine in polynomial time whether there exists an ordering
of
\Omega
making all elements of
\mathcal{C}
convex.
The idea is that starting with a list of the
n
sets
in
\mathcal{C},
together with the sets
\Omega
and
\emptyset,

one searches the list for overlapping pairs

A,

B,
and when one finds such a pair, adds to
the list their union,
their intersection, and their relative complements,
and repeats this process recursively.
Within polynomial time, one will know whether this recursive process
terminates (i.e., gives a family closed under those partial operations)
before the cardinality of the list goes above
\binom{2n}{2}+2.

If it doesn’t so terminate,
then by Proposition
17

there can be no ordering of the desired sort.
If it does, then one has an enumeration of
\mathcal{P},

and one can work out its adjacency structure
and apply Theorem
14
(a)​
\iff
​(b)
to determine whether there exists such an ordering.
The time needed will be polynomial in
n
and the number of
elements in
\Omega.

(If, rather,
\Omega
is infinite, then whether one can do the same
in a time that is polynomial in
n
will depend on what sort of
descriptions we have of
\Omega
and the subsets
of
\Omega
that form
\mathcal{C}.)

7.
Other solutions to question (
1
), using
results in the literature

Given a set
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of a set
\Omega,

the graph having the members of
\mathcal{C}
as vertices, and
an edge between
A,B\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset,

is called the
intersection graph
of
\mathcal{C}.

There has been considerable study of the intersection graphs of finite
sets of intervals of the real line.
Graphs that can be so represented
are called
interval graphs
, and several
characterizations of such graphs have been established
(see
[
9
]
, and for some details,

[
1
, Theorem 9.4.4]
and
[
6
]
).

The literature varies, inter alia, as to whether intervals are assumed
to be closed or open
[
1
, Remark 9.4.2, point 2]
.
In fact, it is not hard to show that a finite graph is
an interval graph in either sense if and only if it satisfies
the formally weaker condition that for
some

totally ordered set
(\Omega,\leq),
the graph can be represented
as the intersection graph of a set of convex subsets of
\Omega.

However, criteria for a finite graph to be an interval graph
do not answer the finite-
\!\mathcal{C}\!
case of
question (
1
):
If the intersection graph of
\mathcal{C}
is
an interval graph, this only tells us that it is isomorphic to
the intersection graph of
some
set of convex subsets
of some totally ordered set.
Observe, for example, that the complete graph with
three vertices is the intersection graph of the family of three sets

\{1\}\subset\{1,2\}\subset\{1,2,3\},
which are convex under the
standard ordering of the integers, but is also the
intersection graph of
\{x,y\},

\{y,z\},

\{x,z\},

which are not simultaneously convex under any ordering of
\{x,y,z\}.

However, Dave Witte Morris (personal communication)
has pointed out that one can get around this difficulty by
bringing in the singleton subsets of
\Omega
alongside the elements
of
\mathcal{C}
(assuming for the moment that
\Omega
is finite).
We develop the result below.
Here we take an
interval
to mean
a set
[s,t]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid s\leq x\leq t\}
where
s<t\in\mathbb{R}.

(Because intervals are by definition nonempty, we shall, in
defining the set
\mathcal{C}^{+}
below, not only append to
\mathcal{C}
all singletons,
but also remove
\emptyset
if it belonged to
\mathcal{C}.)

Proposition 18

(D. W. Morris)
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set,
\mathcal{C}
a set of subsets
of
\Omega,
and
\mathcal{C}^{+}
the set
consisting of all members of
\mathcal{C}
other than
\emptyset,

and also all singletons
\{a\}

(a\in\Omega).

Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)
There exists a total ordering
\leq
of
\Omega
under
which all members of
\mathcal{C}
are convex.

(ii)
The intersection graph of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
is an
interval graph
(
i.e., is isomorphic
to the intersection graph of a set of
intervals of the real line
)
.

Thus, applying to
\mathcal{C}^{+}
any of
the criteria in
[
9
]
for a finite graph
to be an interval graph, one gets criteria for the
members of
\mathcal{C}
to be convex under some ordering of
\Omega.

Proof.

First assume we have an ordering
\leq
as in (i), and list
the elements of
\Omega
in order as
a_{1}<\dots<a_{n}.

Choose real numbers
s_{1}<t_{1}<s_{2}<t_{2}<\dots<s_{n}<t_{n}.

These determine disjoint intervals
I_{i}=[s_{i},t_{i}]

(i=1,\dots,n),
ordered like the elements of
\Omega.

To each nonempty convex subset
A
of
\Omega,
we can now associate
an interval
f(A)
of the real line: the convex
closure of
\bigcup_{a_{i}\in A}I_{i}.

It is straightforward to verify that for nonempty convex
subsets
A
and
B
of
\Omega,
we have
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
if
and only if
f(A)\cap f(B)\neq\emptyset.

(The forward implication is immediate; conversely, if

A
and
B
are disjoint, one can see from their
convexity that
f(A)
and
f(B)
will be disjoint.)
Applying
f
to the members of
\mathcal{C}^{+},
all of which
are convex in
(\Omega,\leq),
we get the desired representation
of the intersection graph of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
as an interval graph.

Conversely, suppose the intersection graph of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
is
an interval graph,
i.e., that there exists a map
f
carrying members of
\mathcal{C}^{+}

to intervals in the real line, such that

(2)

f(A)\cap f(B)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset.

Since distinct singletons
\{a\}
and
\{b\}

(a,b\in\Omega)

are disjoint, (
2
) implies that
the
f(\{a\})

(a\in\Omega)
will
be disjoint intervals, hence this family
of intervals has a natural ordering based on the ordering of
\mathbb{R}.

In other words, there is a way of listing
the elements of
\Omega
as
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
so that, writing

f(\{a_{i}\})=[s_{i},t_{i}],
we have
s_{1}<t_{1}<s_{2}<t_{2}<\dots<s_{n}<t_{n}.

Hence let us order
\Omega
by setting
a_{1}<\dots<a_{n}.

For each
a\in\Omega
and nonempty
A\in\mathcal{C},
note that

(3)

a\in A\ \iff\ \{a\}\cap A\neq\emptyset\ \iff\ f(\{a\})\cap f(A)\neq\emptyset,

where the second equivalence holds by (
2
).
Now the convexity of
f(A),

together with the fact that the
f(\{a_{i}\})

(i=1,\dots,n)

form an ordered string of disjoint intervals, shows that
those
f(a_{i})
that are contained in
f(A)
form a consecutive
substring of this string of intervals, whence by (
3
)
the set of
a\in A
is a consecutive string under our
ordering of
\Omega;
so every nonempty
A\in\mathcal{C}
is
indeed convex under that ordering.

\mathcal{C}
might also contain
\emptyset,
which we
had to exclude from
\mathcal{C}^{+};
but
\emptyset
is vacuously convex,
so our proof of (i) is complete.
∎

Note that since we have been considering finite graphs only,
in the above result we had to take not only

\mathcal{C},
but also
\Omega
to be finite; something I avoided
doing in earlier sections so that we could
make use of Lemma
1
.
However, given a not necessarily finite set
\Omega
and a finite
set
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
\Omega,
we can proceed as follows.
For
a,b\in\Omega,
let
a\sim b
if and only
if the set of members of
\mathcal{C}
containing
a
is the same
as the set of those containing
b.

This is an equivalence relation on
\Omega

with
\leq 2^{\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{C})}
equivalence classes.
Let
\Omega_{0}\subseteq\Omega
be a set of
representatives of these finitely many equivalence classes,
and define
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{A\cap\Omega_{0}\mid A\in\mathcal{C}\},

a set of subsets of
\Omega_{0}.

Then, on the one hand, if every member of
\mathcal{C}
is convex
under an ordering of
\Omega,
every
member of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is clearly convex
under the induced ordering of
\Omega_{0}.

On the other hand, if there is an ordering
\leq_{0}
of
\Omega_{0}

under which all members of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
are convex,
we can construct an ordering
\leq
of
\Omega
by putting an
arbitrary internal total ordering on
each equivalence class under
\sim,

and arranging those classes one above
another as their representatives are arranged in
\Omega_{0}.

Then we see that all members of
\mathcal{C}
will be convex under the
resulting ordering.

Now Proposition
18
gives criteria for
the members of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to be convex under an ordering of
\Omega_{0},

and by the above discussion this is equivalent to the existence of
an ordering of
\Omega
under which the members of
\mathcal{C}
are convex.

Finally, we can apply Lemma
1
to get criteria
for such an ordering to exist in the case of infinite
\mathcal{C}.

Two variants of the above approach were pointed out by
Martin Milanič (personal communication).

On the one hand, one may consider a family
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
a set
\Omega
as
determining a
bipartite
graph, where one family of vertices
corresponds to the elements of
\mathcal{C}
and the other
to the points of
\Omega,
and an edge connects (the vertex
corresponding to)
S\in\mathcal{C}
with (the one corresponding to)

p\in\Omega
if and only if
p\in S.

Results are known on when such a bipartite graph corresponds
to a family
\mathcal{C}
of convex sets under an ordering on

\Omega

[
2
, § 9.7.2]
.

On the other hand, a pair
(\Omega,\,\mathcal{C})
can be regarded
as a
hypergraph
,
with
\Omega
the set of vertices, and
\mathcal{C}
the set of hyperedges.
The question of when the hyperedges of a hypergraph are intervals
under some ordering of the vertex-set is studied
in
[
7
]
; see also
[
2
, § 8.7]
.
