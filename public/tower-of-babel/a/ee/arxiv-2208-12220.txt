On adiabatic theory for extended fermionic lattice systems
Joscha Henheik, Tom Wessel

Abstract
We review recent results on adiabatic theory for ground states of extended gapped fermionic lattice systems under several different assumptions. More precisely, we present generalized super-adiabatic theorems for extended but finite and infinite systems, assuming either a uniform gap or a gap in the bulk above the unperturbed ground state. The goal of this note is to provide an overview of these adiabatic theorems and briefly outline the main ideas and techniques required in their proofs.

1.1
Linear response and adiabatic theory

The formalism of linear response theory
[
21
]
has been widely used in physics to calculate the response of a system in thermal equilibrium to external perturbations.
Put briefly, linear response theory provides an answer to the following question: What is the response of a system described by a Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, that is initially in an equilibrium state
\rho_{0}
, to a small static perturbation
\varepsilon V
?
Or, in somewhat more mathematical terms: What is the change
1
1

1

To be consistent with the rest of the paper, we view states as linear functionals on the algebra of observables (see Section
2
).

\rho_{\varepsilon}(A)-\rho_{0}(A)=\varepsilon\,\sigma_{\mkern-4.0muA}+o(\varepsilon)

of the expectation value of an observable
A
induced by the perturbation
\varepsilon V
to leading order in its strength
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
?
Here,
\rho_{\varepsilon}
denotes the state of the system after the perturbation has been (adiabatically) turned on and
\sigma_{\mkern-4.0muA}
denotes the linear response coefficient.

The answer to this fundamental question of linear response clearly hinges on the problem of determining
\rho_{\varepsilon}
.
Although in few particular situations one expects
\rho_{\varepsilon}
to remain an equilibrium state for the perturbed Hamiltonian
H^{\varepsilon}=H_{0}+\varepsilon V
, the original linear response theory
[
21
]
was developed for situations where the system is driven out of equilibrium, i.e.
\rho_{\varepsilon}
being a resonance state.
As prominently formulated by Simon
[
37
]
in his ‘Fifteen problems in mathematical physics’ from 1984, the latter non-equilibrium situation causes the main challenges in a rigorous mathematical treatment.
However, in either case, the linear response coefficient
\sigma_{\mkern-4.0muA}
is customarily expected to be given by the celebrated
Kubo formula

[
21
]
, and rigorously justifying it was formulated as one of the problems by Simon
[
37
]
.
For a more detailed recent review on the (mathematical) problem of proving Kubo’s formula and its relevance in the context of quantum Hall systems, we refer to
[
17
]
.

In a nutshell, the problem of justifying linear response theory and proving Kubo’s formula is thus to verify that a system, initially in an equilibrium state
\rho_{0}
, is adiabatically driven by a small perturbation
\varepsilon V
into a non-equilibrium state
\rho_{\varepsilon}\approx\rho_{0}
.
Since the perturbation acts over a very long (macroscopic) time, this problem clearly supersedes standard perturbation theory: The change of the state being small is
not
a trivial consequence of the smallness of the perturbation
\varepsilon V
.
Instead, verifying that the two states,
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and
\rho_{0}
, are close to each other requires an adiabatic type theorem.

However, even in our rather simple setting (zero temperature, assuming that
\rho_{0}
is the gapped ground state of
H_{0}
describing an extended fermionic lattice system, the perturbation
\varepsilon V
might close the gap), the problem of justifying the linear response formalism also goes beyond standard adiabatic theory.
In fact, the applicability of the standard adiabatic theorem of quantum mechanics is rather restrictive for the following three reasons:

(i)

The standard adiabatic theorem requires the perturbation
\varepsilon V
to
not close the spectral gap
.
In that scenario, it asserts that
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is (close to) the gapped ground state of
H^{\varepsilon}=H_{0}+\varepsilon V
and as such a (nearly) equilibrium state.

(ii)

Even if we neglect the first issue, the usual adiabatic theorem estimates the difference between
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and the ground state of the perturbed Hamiltonian
H^{\varepsilon}
in
operator norm
, leaving the translation to local differences in expectation values as an additional and potentially non-trivial step.

(iii)

In general, extended systems are plagued by the
orthogonality catastrophe
: Whenever for single-particle states
\psi,\tilde{\psi}
we have
{\|\psi-\tilde{\psi}\|\sim\varepsilon}
, the non-interacting many-particle states
\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}\psi_{x}
and
\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}\tilde{\psi}_{x}
satisfy
\|\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}{\psi}_{x}-\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}\tilde{\psi}_{x}\|\sim\varepsilon|\Lambda|
, i.e. the norm-estimate deteriorates when
|\Lambda|\to\infty
.
This means that the approximation error in the standard adiabatic theorem grows with the systems size, and it is thus not applicable for macroscopic systems.

A major breakthrough in overcoming these obstacles has recently been achieved by Bachmann, De Roeck and Fraas
[
3
]
(see also their introductory lecture notes
[
4
]
).
They proved the first adiabatic theorem for extended (but finite) lattice systems with short-range interactions, thereby solving the second and third problem in the list above.
More precisely, their result concerns differences in expectation values and provides error estimates, which are uniform in the system size.

For these lattice systems with short-range interactions, well known
Lieb-Robinson bounds

[
22
,
29
,
30
]
ensure a finite speed of correlation and prevent build-up of long-range entanglement.
Having Lieb-Robinson bounds at hand allowed Bachmann et al.
[
6
]
to prove that the generator of the
spectral flow
, introduced by Hastings and Wen
[
15
]
, is an SLT operator and thus preserves good locality properties.
The general spectral flow technique can then be used to prove automorphic equivalence of two gapped ground states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
of Hamiltonians
H(0)
and
H(1)
, respectively: Given a smooth path
s\mapsto H(s)
of (uniformly) gapped SLT Hamiltonians, their ground states are automorphically equivalent (equal up to a conjugation by unitaries) with the generator of the automorphism being an SLT operator
[
6
]
.
This automorphic equivalence allowed Bachmann et al.
[
3
]
to prove a
super-adiabatic theorem
(see Section
1.2
for an explanation of this notion) for such systems, however, still requiring the spectral gap not only for
H_{0}
but also for
H^{\varepsilon}
, i.e. the gap must remain open.
2

2

A slight generalization of their result can be found in
[
26

, where the authors used an alternative gauge with a time-dependent vector potential for a quantum Hall model.

The four theorems presented in this article also solved the last remaining problem given under item (i) in the above list, i.e. they allow the perturbation
\varepsilon V
to close the spectral gap of
H_{0}
.
The main idea for establishing this generalization is that a spatially local gap should suffice for an adiabatic theorem to hold.
This underlies the space-time adiabatic perturbation theory originally developed for non-interacting fermions by Panati, Spohn and Teufel
[
34

33
]
, where one utilizes a gap that exists locally in space (and time) but does not exist globally.
It also underlies the recent results by De Roeck, Elgart and Fraas
[
10
]
, where an adiabatic theorem holds even if the ‘spectral gap’ is filled with eigenvalues, whose corresponding eigenvectors are spatially localized, leaving a gap (with smaller size) locally open.
Finally, this is also the idea behind the Theorems
III
and especially
IV
presented below, where one still has an adiabatic type theorem although the gap closes at the boundary of the lattices.

Combining the ideas from the space-time adiabatic perturbation theory with the methods invented in
[
3
]
, the first of the four theorems presented in this article was proven by Teufel
[
39
]

It concerns extended but finite systems and requires a spectral gap for

(GAP
unif

The precise statement is formulated in Theorem

In order to extend this result from finite lattices to an infinite system, Henheik and Teufel
[
18

adapted ideas from Nachtergaele, Sims and Young
[
30
]
on controlling the thermodynamic limit of automorphisms with SLT generators.
This result is formulated in Theorem
II

So far, all mentioned results were obtained under the assumption of a (uniform) spectral gap for the finite systems (which also implies a gap for the infinite system).
However, the recent result on automorphic equivalence with a
gap only in the bulk
(via the GNS construction) by Moon and Ogata
[
28
]
, opened the door for a new class of adiabatic theorems, where the unperturbed Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is no longer required to have a uniform spectral gap.
Instead, Theorem
III
, originally proven by Henheik and Teufel
[
19
]
, is a result for the infinite volume states and requires a gap in the bulk.
This technically means a gap for the infinite system (cf. Assumption
(GAP

)
) but can be understood as requiring a local gap in the interior of the finite lattices (cf. Remark
4

Moreover, by employing strong locality estimates from
[
30
,
28
]
and assuming fast convergence of ground states, Theorem
III
can be traced back to extended but finite systems which only have a gap in the bulk.
This was also proven in
[
19
]
and is formulated in Theorem

2.1
Algebra of observables

We consider fermions with

Let

For each

.
The

\mathcal{A}_{X}:=\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{F}_{X})

Whenever

For infinite systems, the

, are to show that (i) the property of having a RTDL
γ
is preserved under all necessary operations for the construction of the NEASS (similarly as for Theorem
II
) and (ii) having a RTDL
γ
for an interaction provides an explicit rate of convergence for the associated evolution family as in Proposition
3
.

4
Idea of the proofs

The goal of the present section is to convey the main ideas relevant for proving the individual theorems from Section
3
, where we already glimpsed the key steps required in their proofs.
For many technical details we refer the reader to the original works
[
39
,
18
,
19
]
.

4.1
Systems with a uniform gap

The fundamental conceptual idea behind the proof for all four variants of the generalized super-adiabatic theorems is a perturbative scheme, which was called
space-time adiabatic perturbation theory
in
[
34
,
33
]
.
The basic structure of this computation is most easily presented for finite systems, where no further technical difficulties arise since all appearing operators are in fact matrices and thus bounded.
However, it is still necessary to show that all estimates are uniform in the size of the system
{\Lambda_{k}}
.

4.1.1
Extended but finite systems: Proof of Theorem
I

The form in which we presented Theorem
I
differs slightly from the original result
[
39
, Theorem 5.1]
.
The original statement concerns a sequence
\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t):=\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)\circ\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)
of states on
{\Lambda_{k}}
(indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}
), where

\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)\llbracket A\rrbracket:={\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\mathcal{L}^{\Lambda_{k}}_{S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon\mathcal{L}^{\Lambda_{k}}_{S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{j}\mathcal{L}^{\Lambda_{k}}_{A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)}\,.

From this, Theorem
I
(and similarly all other three theorems) follows by a simple
resummation
of the
\varepsilon^{j}\smash{\mathcal{L}^{\Lambda_{k}}_{A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)}}
, which will be discussed in Section
4.3
below.

The main idea of the proof is to choose each operator
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)
,
j=1,\dotsc,n
, in such a way that the
j
th
-order term in the perturbative scheme vanishes.
For the
n
-dependent result (i.e. prior to resummation), we apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to get

\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)(A)=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\Bigl(\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr)

(13)

and then aim to bound the integrand.
Calculating the derivative by using the chain rule and Duhamel’s formula leaves us with

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\Bigl(\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\eta}\,\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\Bigl(\Bigl[Q^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}_{n}(s),\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr]\Bigr)\,,

(14)

where
Q^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}_{n}(s)
is a shorthand notation for

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{multlined}\eta\,\mathcal{I}_{s}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}\bigl(\dot{H}_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)\bigr)+\eta\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)}\,\varepsilon\dot{S}_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)}\\
+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)}\,\bigl(H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)+\varepsilon V^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)\bigr)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=:H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{j}R_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)+\varepsilon^{n+1}R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\,,

(17)

and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}=V_{v}^{\Lambda_{k}}+H_{1}^{\Lambda_{k}}
.
Here,
\mathcal{I}_{s}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}\bigl(\dot{H}_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)\bigr)
is the SLT generator of the parallel transport within the vector-bundle
\Xi_{0,I}^{\Lambda_{k}}
over
I
defined by
t\mapsto\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
.
14
14

14

Since we assumed uniqueness of the ground state
\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
, the vector-bundle
\Xi_{0,I}^{\Lambda_{k}}
is one-dimensional.
If this were not the case, one had to include further terms generating the internal dynamic in
\Xi_{0,I}^{\Lambda_{k}}
(see
[
39
,
18
]
).

This parallel transport is also known as the
spectral flow
, which plays a fundamental role in proving automorphic equivalence of gapped ground state phases (see e.g.
[
6
,
3
]
).
Moreover, the operator
\mathcal{I}_{s}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}\colon\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{k}}\to\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{k}}
is called the
quasi-local inverse of the Liouvillian
12

\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}(s)}^{\Lambda_{k}}
, since it satisfies
[
3
,
39
]

\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)\Bigl(\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}(s)}^{\Lambda_{k}}\circ\mathcal{I}_{s}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket B_{1}\rrbracket-\mathrm{i}\,B_{1},B_{2}\bigr]\Bigr)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{k}}\,,\quad s\in I\,,

(18)

and also preserves good localization of its argument (in particular, it maps SLT operators to SLT operators).
This combined property of
\mathcal{I}_{s}^{\Lambda_{k}}
heavily relies on the ground state
\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)
being gapped
[
3
,
39
,
30
]
and will be of fundamental importance in the following.

In the last line of (
17
), we expanded in powers of
\varepsilon
and
\eta
in the sense that
R_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
, for
j\leq n
, are polynomials in
\eta/\varepsilon
of order (at most)
j
with
\varepsilon
- and
\eta
-independent SLT operators as coefficients.
A more detailed step-by-step calculation can be found in the proof of Proposition 5.1 in
[
39
]
.
Let us here only report the general structure

R_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)=-\mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}(s)}^{\Lambda_{k}}\bigl(A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\bigr)+\tilde{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s),

(19)

where the first remainder term is given by

\tilde{R}_{1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)=\tfrac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\,\mathcal{I}_{s}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}\bigl(\dot{H}_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)\bigr)-V^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)

and all other
\tilde{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
are composed of iterated commutators of the operators
A_{i}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
and
\dot{A}_{i}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
, for
i<j\leq n
, with
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)
and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)
.
In contrast to general onsite potentials, the commutator of a Lipschitz potential with an SLT operator is an SLT operator itself
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
.
For the commutator of SLT operators, this is easy to see.

We now consider individual terms from (
17
) when plugged into (
14
).
The zero-order term vanishes, because
\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)
is the ground state of
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s)
.
By application of (
18
) we can iteratively choose

A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)=-\mathcal{I}_{s}^{\Lambda_{k}}\bigl(\tilde{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\bigr)

(20)

such that (
14
) vanishes up to

-\mathrm{i}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}}{\eta}\,\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\Bigl(\Bigl[R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s),\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr]\Bigr)\,.

(21)

Moreover, all the operations involved in calculating the
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
, i.e. taking commutators and applying the quasi-local inverse of the Liouvillian preserve the locality properties of the operators as shown in the appendices of
[
39
,
26
]
, which are heavily based on
[
3
]
.
Hence, also all
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
are SLT operators.

It turns out that also
R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
is a polynomial in
\eta/\varepsilon
of order at most
n+1
and its coefficients, as we just explained, are SLT operators
[
39
, Proof of Proposition 6.1]
.
Thus, the absolute value of (
13
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}}{\eta}\,\biggl\lvert\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\rho_{0}^{{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\Bigl(\Bigl[R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s),\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr]\Bigr)\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}}{\eta}\,\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert\sup_{s\in[t_{0},t]}\Bigl\lVert\Bigl[\bigl(\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\bigr)^{-1}(s)\bigl(R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)\bigr),\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\Bigr]\Bigr\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}}{\eta}\,\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert\,\biggl(1+\Bigl(\frac{\mathstrut\eta}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{n+1}\biggr)\biggl(1+\Bigl(\frac{\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert}{\eta}\Bigr)^{d}\biggr)\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lvert X\rvert^{2}

(22)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}+\eta^{n+1}}{\eta^{d+1}}\,\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert\,\bigl(1+\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert^{d}\bigr)\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lvert X\rvert^{2}\,,

where we essentially used a generalized Lieb-Robinson bound
[
39
, Lemma B.5]
to estimate the commutator.
Note that the
(1+\bigl(\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert/\eta\bigr){}^{d})
-factor comes from the Lieb-Robinson bound and the adiabatic
1/\eta
-scaling of the time evolution
\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}
.
The
(1+(\eta/\varepsilon)^{n+1})
-factor comes from bounding the interaction norm of
R_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(s)
by separating the polynomial dependence on
\eta/\varepsilon
such that
C_{n}
is independent of
{\Lambda_{k}}
,
\varepsilon
and
\eta
.
We have thus shown that the NEASS almost intertwines the time evolution, i.e. item
7
of Definition
1
.

We are left with discussing the remaining three characterizing properties of the NEASS given in Definition
1
:
By construction, all
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)
depend only on
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
and their
j
th
derivatives at time
t
.
This shows that the NEASS is local in time, i.e. item
2
.
Moreover, if all time derivatives of
H_{0}
and
V
vanish for some
t\in I
, then all non-constant (i.e. in front of some positive power of
\eta/\varepsilon
) coefficients in
R_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
vanish and
\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)=\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,0,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)
.
This shows that the NEASS is stationary whenever the Hamiltonian is stationary, i.e. item
8
.
If, for some
t\in I
,
\dot{H}_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
vanish, then
\tilde{R}_{1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
and thus
A_{1}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
vanish.
If additionally all derivatives of
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}
and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}
at
t
vanish, also
\tilde{R}_{j}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
and thus
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)
vanish.
Hence,
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{k}}
and the NEASS equals the ground state, i.e. item
4
holds.

The above listed general arguments immediately translate to the other three theorems.

4.1.2
Infinite systems: Proof of Theorem
II

Without any further assumptions, the sequence Hamiltonian
H^{\varepsilon,{\Lambda_{k}}}
and its constituents
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}
and
V^{\Lambda_{k}}
could have nothing in common for different lattice sizes
k
(they might even describe different physical systems), so taking the limit
{\Lambda_{k}}\nearrow{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
might not be well-defined.
In order to avoid this somewhat meaningless situation, we assumed that the building blocks of the Hamiltonian
have a TDL
(see Definition
2
and Assumption
(INT
2
)
) and also the sequence of ground states
\big(\rho_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges (Assumption
(S
unif
)
).
Since the property of having a TDL guarantees the existence of the thermodynamic limit for the corresponding evolution operators (see Proposition
3
and
[
30
]
), it remains to show that the operator sequences
\big(A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
j=1,\dotsc,n
, constructed in Section
4.1.1
have a TDL.
More precisely, one needs to show that taking time-derivatives, sums of commutators with the building blocks of
H^{\varepsilon}
(and
\dot{H}_{0}
), and the inverse of the Liouvillian (see (
19
)) leaves the property of having a TDL for SLT operators invariant, which is in fact the main point of the proof in
[
18
]
.
It is then straightforward to show that compositions of states and automorphisms, all having a thermodynamic limit, converge as
{\Lambda_{k}}\nearrow{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
.
Since the constant
C_{n}
from (
22
) is uniformly bounded in
k
, the (sketch of a) proof of Theorem
II
is complete.

4.2
Systems with a gap in the bulk

For systems having a spectral gap only in the bulk (i.e. for the GNS Hamiltonian), the characteristic (
18
) of
\mathcal{I}_{s}^{\Lambda_{k}}
, that it essentially inverts the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}(s)}^{\Lambda_{k}}
(and still maps SLT operators to SLT operators), is now only fulfilled for certain
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
in a dense domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
after taking the limit
{\Lambda_{k}}\nearrow{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
(see
[
19
, Proposition 3.3]
).
Presuming that the limit actually exists, this point is the main challenge in proving an adiabatic theorem under the less restrictive gap Assumption
(GAP
bulk
)
.

4.2.1
Infinite systems: Proof of Theorem
III

As just explained, the main difficulty in proving Theorem
III
is that (
18
) only holds if
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}
is gapped.
On top of that, we cannot handle the limit
{\Lambda_{k}}\nearrow{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
of the
\tilde{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
directly nor could they be used in the infinite volume version of (

) because it only holds for

.
However, the rest of the construction from Section
4.1.1
is still valid, but the lower order terms in (
13
) have a non-vanishing contribution in finite domains.
We thus repeat this construction but take coefficients
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t)
, which are built up from
H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(t)
but restricting the perturbations
\dot{H}_{0}(t)
and
V(t)
to
{\Lambda_{l}}
with

.
In this way, one can take the limit
{\Lambda_{k}}\nearrow{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
in (
18
) with
B_{1}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
(see (
24
) and (
25
) and the comment thereafter for technical obstructions in taking the limit).
Using this notational convention, we introduce the states

\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t)=\rho_{0}(t)\circ\beta^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t)\,,

where
\rho_{0}(t)
is the infinite volume ground state, and compare them to the actual objects in infinite volume while estimating

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert\leq{}

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle+\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert

(23)

\displaystyle+\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(t)(A)-\Pi_{n}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert

by means of the triangle inequality.
Since all the interactions (and the Lipschitz potential) have a TDL, one can prove

that the first and last summand in (

) can be made arbitrarily small for

large enough, and we can thus focus on the second summand.
However, since (

and also

is not necessarily a ground state of

) and (

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\rho_{0}(s)\Bigl(\bigl[H_{0}^{\Lambda_{k}}(s),\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr]\Bigr)=0

and

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\rho_{0}(s)\Bigl(\bigl[R_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(s),\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}(s)\circ\mathfrak{U}_{t,s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}},{\Lambda_{l}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr]\Bigr)=0

for all

(iii)), such that the limits really converge to the infinite volume version of (

4.2.2
Extended but finite systems: Proof of Theorem
IV

Let us briefly explain the strategy to prove Theorem
IV
.
In order to show (
12
), we first estimate

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert\leq{}

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle+\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert

(26)

\displaystyle+\Bigl\lvert\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)(A)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert

and treat the three summands separately.
The second summand corresponds to the infinite system and can be estimated by means of Theorem
III
, such that it accounts for the first contribution on the RHS of (
12
).
We are left with bounding the remaining two summands in (
26
).
These contribute the additional error term on the RHS of (
12
).
To estimate them, we need explicit control on the speed of convergence (it must be faster than any inverse polynomial) for the states (see Assumption
(S
bulk
)
) and automorphisms
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
and
\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
.
For the time evolution
\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
, the rapid convergence to
\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
is ensured by supposing that the building blocks of
H^{\varepsilon}
have a RTDL (see Definition
5
and Assumption
(INT
4
)
).
This was carried out in
[
19
, Appendix B]
, building on estimates from
[
30
, Section 3]
.
We remark that the adiabatic

-scaling of the time evolution is responsible for the factor
\eta
appearing in the additional error term in (

).
In order to show that also
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\to\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
sufficiently fast, we need to show that all
A_{j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
have a RTDL, i.e. the operations involved in constructing the generator of
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
leave the property of having a RTDL (essentially) invariant (see
[
19
, Appendix C]
).
This finishes the sketch of the proof of Theorem
IV
and we refer to
[
19
, Section 5.2]
for further details.

4.3
Resummation of the NEASS

As mentioned in the beginning of Section
4.1.1
, the statements formulated in Section
3
require a resummation, which we explain in the following.
First, note that the generator
\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
of
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
constructed above can be rewritten as
\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\varepsilon^{i}\eta^{j-1}A_{j,i}
, where the coefficients
A_{j,i}
are time-dependent SLT operators and independent of
\varepsilon
and
\eta
.
Now, it is easy to show (see
[
18
, Lemma E.1]
) that there exists a sequence
\delta_{j}\to 0
and constants
C_{n}
such that the resummed generator

\varepsilon S^{\varepsilon,\eta}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\chi_{[0,1]}(\varepsilon/\delta_{j})\,\chi_{[0,1]}(\eta/\delta_{j})\sum_{i=0}^{j}\varepsilon^{i}\eta^{j-i}A_{j,i}

(27)

satisfies

\bigl\lVert\varepsilon S^{\varepsilon,\eta}-\varepsilon S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\bigr\rVert_{\mathrm{SLT}}\leq C_{n}\,(\varepsilon^{n}+\eta^{n})\,,

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{SLT}}
denotes an interaction norm similar to (
5
).
Resummations of this type are standard, e.g., in microlocal analysis
[
24
]
and the above estimate immediately leads to the bounds (cf.
[
18
, Lemmata E.3, E.4]
)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in I}\Bigl\lvert\Pi^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)(A)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t)(A)\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}_{n}\,(\varepsilon^{n}+\eta^{n})\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lvert X\rvert^{2}\quad\text{and}

(28)

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Pi^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)-\Pi_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}(t_{0})\bigl(\mathfrak{U}_{t,t_{0}}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\bigr)\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{n}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{n}+\eta^{n}}{\eta^{d+1}}\,(1+\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert)^{d+1}\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lvert X\rvert^{2}\,,

(29)

uniformly in the size of the system
{\Lambda_{k}}
. In the context of Theorem
II
and Theorem
III
, corresponding estimates hold in infinite volume, i.e. without the subscript
{\Lambda_{k}}
.

Next, since the sum in (
27
) is finite for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, also the resummed generator
S^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
has a TDL as soon as
S_{n}^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
has a TDL.
Therefore, the states
\Pi^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
constructed using the
S^{\varepsilon,\eta,{\Lambda_{k}}}
