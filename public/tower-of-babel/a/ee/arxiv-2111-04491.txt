Dual Quaternions and Dual Quaternion Vectors
Liqun Qi, Chen Ling, and Hong Yan

Abstract
We introduce a total order and an absolute value function for dual numbers. The absolute value function of dual numbers takes dual number values, and has properties similar to the those of the absolute value function of real numbers. We define the magnitude of a dual quaternion, as a dual number. Based upon these, we extended 1 1 -norm, ∞ \infty -norm and 2 2 -norm to dual quaternion vectors. Key words. Dual number, absolute value function, dual quaternion, magnitude, norm.

A Total Order for Dual Numbers

Denote
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{D}
as the set of the real numbers, and the set of the dual numbers, respectively. A dual number
q
has the form
q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, where
q_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
are real numbers, and
\epsilon
is the infinitesimal unit, satisfying
\epsilon^{2}=0
. We call
q_{st}
the real part or the standard part of
q
, and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
the dual part or the infinitesimal part of
q
. The infinitesimal unit
\epsilon
is commutative in multiplication with real numbers, complex numbers and quaternion numbers. The dual numbers form a commutative algebra of dimension two over the reals. If
q_{st}\not=0
, we say that
q
is appreciable, otherwise, we say that
q
is infinitesimal.

We may define a total order
\leq
over
\mathbb{D}
. Suppose
p=p_{st}+p_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in\mathbb{D}
. We have
q<p
if
q_{st}<p_{st}
, or
q_{st}=p_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}<p_{\mathcal{I}}
. We have
q=p
if
q_{st}=p_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}=p_{\mathcal{I}}
. Thus, if
q>0
, we say that
q
is a positive dual number; and if
q\geq 0
, we say that
q
is a nonnegative dual number. Denote the set of nonnegative dual numbers by
\mathbb{D}_{+}
, and the set of positive dual numbers by
\mathbb{D}_{++}
.

For
p=p_{st}+p_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in\mathbb{D}
and a positive integer
k
, we have

p+q=p_{st}+q_{st}+(p_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon,

(1)

pq=p_{st}q_{st}+(p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}+p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st})\epsilon,

(2)

q^{k}=q_{st}^{k}+kq_{st}^{k-1}q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon.

(3)

Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{D}
and a positive integer
k
, we have the following conclusions.

1.
q^{2k}\in\mathbb{D}_{+}
;

2.

;

3. If
p,q\in\mathbb{D}_{+}
, then
pq\in\mathbb{D}_{+}
;

4. If
p,q\in\mathbb{D}_{++}
and at least one of them is appreciable, then
pq\in\mathbb{D}_{++}

Proof.

1. By (

), we have

q^{2k}=q_{st}^{2k}+2kq_{st}^{2k-1}q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon.

If
q_{st}\not=0
, then

. This implies
q^{2k}>0

q_{st}=0
, then
q^{2k}=0
.

2. By 1, we have
p^{2}+q^{2}-2pq=(p-q)^{2}\geq 0

3. If
p_{st}>0
and
q_{st}>0
, then
p_{st}q_{st}>0
. By (

),
pq>0
. If
p_{st}=0
and
q_{st}>0
, then
p_{\mathcal{I}}\geq 0
as

. By (

),
pq=p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st}\epsilon\geq 0
. Similarly, if
p_{st}>0
and
q_{st}=0
, then by (

),
pq=p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\geq 0
. If
p_{st}=q_{st}=0
, then by (

4. This may be proved similarly to 3.

∎

Clearly, many inequalities of real numbers can be extended to dual numbers without difficulty.

For

If

The Absolute Value Function of Dual Numbers

Recall that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

{\rm sgn}(u)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1,&\ {\rm if}\ u>0,\\
0,&\ {\rm if}\ u=0,\\
-1,&\ {\rm if}\ u<0.\end{aligned}\right.

We define the absolute value of
q\in\mathbb{D}
as

|q|=\left\{\begin{aligned} |q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,&\ {\rm if}\ q_{st}\not=0,\\
|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The mapping
|\cdot|:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}_{+}
. Suppose that
p,q\in\mathbb{D}
. Then,

1.
|q|=0
if and only if
q=0
;

2.
|q|=q
if
q\geq 0
,
|q|>q
otherwise;

3.
|q|=\sqrt{q^{2}}
if

is appreciable;

4.
|pq|=|p||q|
;

5.
|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
.

Proof.

By definition,
|\cdot|:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}_{+}
, and we may verify conclusions 1 and 2 directly.

Suppose that
q
is appreciable. Then
q_{st}\not=0
. We have

. This implies that

We have conclusion 3.

We have

pq=p_{st}q_{st}+(p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}+p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st})\epsilon.

Then,

|pq|=\left\{\begin{aligned} |p_{st}q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(p_{st}q_{st})(p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}+p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st})\epsilon,&\ {\rm if}\ p_{st}q_{st}\not=0,\\
|p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}+p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st}|\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

If
p_{st}\not=0
and
q_{st}\not=0
, then

{\rm sgn}(p_{st}q_{st})={\rm sgn}(p_{st}){\rm sgn}(q_{st}).

We have

\displaystyle|pq|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|p_{st}q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(p_{st}q_{st})(p_{st}q_{\mathcal{I}}+p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st})\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|p_{st}||q_{st}|+|p_{st}|{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon+{\rm sgn}(p_{st})p_{\mathcal{I}}|q_{st}|\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(|p_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(p_{st})p_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\right)\left(|q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|p||q|.

If
p_{st}=0
and
q_{st}\not=0
, then
pq=p_{\mathcal{I}}q_{st}\epsilon
. This implies that
|pq|=|p_{\mathcal{I}}||q_{st}|\epsilon
. On the other hand, we have
|p|=|p_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon
and
|q|=|q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
. Therefore,

|p||q|=|p_{\mathcal{I}}||q_{st}|\epsilon=|pq|.

Similarly, if
p_{st}\not=0
and
q_{st}=0
, then we have
|p||q|=|pq|
.

If
p_{st}=q_{st}=0
, then
pq=0
,
|p|=|p_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon
and
|q|=|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon
. We still have
|pq|=|p||q|
. Hence, conclusion 4 holds.

We now show conclusion 5. We have

|p+q|=|(p_{st}+p_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon)+(q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon)|=\left\{\begin{aligned} |p_{st}+q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(p_{st}+q_{st})(p_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon,&\ {\rm if}\ p_{st}+q_{st}\not=0,\\
|p_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

If
p_{st}\not=0
and
q_{st}\not=0
, then

|p|+|q|=|p_{st}|+|q_{st}|+({\rm sgn}(p_{st})p_{\mathcal{I}}+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon.

We have
|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
, as
|p_{st}|+|q_{st}|>0
and
|p_{st}+q_{st}|\leq|p_{st}|+|q_{st}|
.

If
p_{st}=0
and
q_{st}\not=0
, then

|p|+|q|=|q_{st}|+(|p_{\mathcal{I}}|+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})q_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon.

We have

|p+q|=|q_{st}|+{\rm sgn}(q_{st})(p_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon\leq|p|+|q|.

Similarly, if
p_{st}\not=0
and
q_{st}=0
, then we have
|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
.

If
p_{st}=q_{st}=0
, then

|p+q|=|p_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon\leq(|p_{\mathcal{I}}|+|q_{\mathcal{I}}|)\epsilon=|p|+|q|.

Thus, in any case, we have
|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
.
∎

We see that the absolute value concept of dual numbers extends the absolute value concept of real numbers.

In fact, for
p,q\in\mathbb{D}
,
|p-q|
defines the distance between
p
and
q
. This distance satisfies the triangular formula, and provides the basis for dual analysis.

Based on this distance measurement, we can define limits and continuous dual functions. A function
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{D}
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{D}
,
a<b
, is called a dual function. Then we see that the properties of dual functions are different from real functions. Consider
f(x)=x^{2}-\epsilon
, defined on
\mathbb{D}
. We have
f(0)=-\epsilon<0
and
f(1)=1-\epsilon>0
. However, we cannot find
x\in[0,1]\subset\mathbb{D}
such that
f(x)=0
.

Denote
\mathbb{Q}
as the set of the quaternions.
A quaternion

The conjugate of

It follows that the inverse of a nonzero quaternion

By Theorem 2.1 of
[

]
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any

1.
|q|=|q^{*}|
;

2.
q^{*}q=qq^{*}=|q|^{2}=q_{0}^{2}+q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}+q_{3}^{2}

|q|=0
if and only if
q=0

4.
|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
;

5.
|pq|=|p||q|
.

By direct calculation, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
p=p_{0}+p_{1}\mathbf{i}+p_{2}\mathbf{j}+p_{3}\mathbf{k},q=q_{0}+q_{1}\mathbf{i}+q_{2}\mathbf{j}+q_{3}\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Q}

p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\in\mathbb{R}

pq^{*}+qp^{*}=p^{*}q+q^{*}p=2p_{0}q_{0}+2p_{1}q_{1}+2p_{2}q_{2}+2p_{3}q_{3},

(6)

which is a real number.

q_{st}\not=0
, c.
p_{st}\not=0
and
q_{st}=0
, and d.
p_{st}=q_{st}=0
, to prove Conclusion 5. We omit the technical details here.
∎

If

, then

is called a unit dual quaternion, which plays an important role to represent the motion of a rigid body in 3D

,

,

]
. Thus,

is a unit dual quaternion if and only if

and

This matches the definition of unit dual quaternions in applications

6
Norms of Dual Quaternion Vectors

Denote the collection of
n
-dimensional quaternion vectors by
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
, and the collection of
n
-dimensional dual quaternion vectors by
{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
with
x_{i}=(x_{i})_{0}+(x_{i})_{1}\mathbf{i}+(x_{i})_{2}\mathbf{j}+(x_{i})_{3}\mathbf{k}
for
(x_{i})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2,\cdots,n,j=0,1,2,4
, denote

\mathbf{x}^{R}=((x_{1})_{0},(x_{1})_{1},(x_{1})_{2},(x_{1})_{3},(x_{2})_{0},\cdots,(x_{n})_{3})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4n}.

Then

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\equiv\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(x_{i})_{0}^{2}+(x_{i})_{1}^{2}+(x_{i})_{2}^{2}+(x_{i})_{3}^{2}\right]}\equiv\|\mathbf{x}^{R}\|_{2}.

This connects the
2
-norms of real vectors and quaternion vectors, and will be useful for us to discuss the
2
-norm of dual quaternion vectors.

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})^{\top},\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
, define
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{*}y_{i}
, where
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},\cdots,x_{n}^{*})
is the conjugate transpose of
\mathbf{x}
.
By Theorem
4.2
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

For
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{*}\mathbf{x}\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
, we know

x_{i}^{*}y_{i}+y_{i}^{*}x_{i}=2[(x_{i})_{0}(y_{i})_{0}+(x_{i})_{1}(y_{i})_{1}+(x_{i})_{2}(y_{i})_{2}+(x_{i})_{3}(y_{i})_{3}].

Consequently, it holds that

\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{*}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}^{*}y_{i}+y_{i}^{*}x_{i})=2(\mathbf{x}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{R}\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}^{R}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{R}\|_{2}=2\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

∎

For
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
, we may also write

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,

where
\mathbf{x}_{st},\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
are the standard part and the infinitesimal part of
\mathbf{x}
respectively.

A function
v:{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}\to\mathbb{D}
is called a norm on
{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
if it satisfies the following three properties:

1. For any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
,
v(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
, and
v(\mathbf{x})=0
if and only if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
;

2. For any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{DQ}
,
v(q\mathbf{x})=|q|v(\mathbf{x})
;

3. For any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
,
v(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\leq v(\mathbf{x})+v(\mathbf{y})
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})^{\top}
. We may extend the
1
-norm and
\infty
-norm to dual quaternion vectors as follows:

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|,

(10)

and

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i=1,2,\cdots,n}|x_{i}|.

(11)

For
i=1,2,\cdots,n
, we have

|x_{i}|=\left\{\begin{aligned} |(x_{i})_{st}|+{(x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*}\over 2|(x_{i})_{st}|}\epsilon,&\ {\rm if}\ (x_{i})_{st}\not=0,\\
|(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}|\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

Proposition 6.2
.

The
1
-norm and the
\infty
-norm, defined above satisfy the three properties of norms.

Proof.

Consider the
1
-norm first. Let
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
. If
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
, then
x_{i}=0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. By (
12
), we have
|x_{i}|=0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. By (
10
), we have
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=0
. On the other, if
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=0
, then by (
10
), we have
|x_{i}|=0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
, as
|x_{i}|\geq 0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. By Theorem
5.1
, we have
x_{i}=0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. Hence
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. This proves Property 1 for the
1
-norm. Now, let
q\in\mathbb{DQ}
. We have

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|qx_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|q||x_{i}|=|q|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|=|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}.

Then, Property 2 of the norm holds for the
1
-norm. Finally, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
, we have

\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}+y_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(|x_{i}|+|y_{i}|)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}|=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{1}.

This proves Property 3 for the
1
-norm. Hence, the
1
-norm is a norm.

We see that the proof for the
1
-norm is the same as in the real vector space. The proof for the
\infty
-norm is also the same as in the real vector space. Hence, we omit the details here.
∎

However, for
2
-norm, we may not simply define

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}}.

(13)

We should define
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
by (
13
) if not all of
x_{i}
are infinitesimal. If all
x_{i}
are infinitesimal, we have
x_{i}=(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. Then we define

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}|^{2}}\epsilon.

(14)

Proposition 6.3
.

For any
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
with
\mathbf{x}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
, it holds that

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon.

(15)

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
, by (
12
) and (
13
), we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}&=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}}\\
&=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{(x_{i})_{st}\neq 0}\left(|(x_{i})_{st}|+\frac{(x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*}}{2|(x_{i})_{st}|}\epsilon\right)^{2}+\sum_{(x_{i})_{st}=0}|(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon|^{2}}\\
&=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{(x_{i})_{st}\neq 0}\left(|(x_{i})_{st}|^{2}+((x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*})\epsilon\right)}.\end{array}

Since
(x_{i})_{st}^{*}=0
when
(x_{i})_{st}=0
, we further have

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}&=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(x_{i})_{st}|^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}((x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*})\right)\epsilon}\\
&=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}((x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*})\right)\epsilon}.\end{array}

Consequently, by (
4
) and Theorem
4.2
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}&=&\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}((x_{i})_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}^{*})}{2\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon\\
&=&\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}((x_{i})_{st}^{*}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}+(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}(x_{i})_{st})}{2\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon\\
&=&\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{st}}{2\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon\\
&=&\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon,\end{array}

which means that the equality in (
15
) holds. Finally, the inequality in (
15
) follows from the fact that
(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}
.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

The
2
-norm, defined by (
13
) and (
14
), satisfies the three properties of norms.

Proof.

By (
13
) and (
14
), if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
. On the other hand, assume that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
. If one of
x_{i}
is appreciable, by (
13
), we have

|x_{i}|=0
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. This implies that

for

. Hence
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. If all of
x_{i}
are infinitesimal, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
, then by (

), we have

|(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}|=0
for

, this means that
(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}=0
for

. Hence,
x_{i}=0
for

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}

-norm.

Assume that
q\in\mathbb{DQ}
is appreciable. If at least one

is appreciable, then the corresponding

is also appreciable. By (

),

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|qx_{i}|^{2}}.

By Theorem
5.1
,
|qx_{i}|=|q||x_{i}|
. Hence,

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{|q|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}}=\sqrt{|q|^{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}.

By Theorem
5.1
,
|x_{i}|\geq 0
for all
i
. Then
|x_{i}|^{2}\geq 0
for all
i
. By (
7
),
|x_{i}|
, hence
|x_{i}|^{2}
is appreciable, if
x_{i}
is appreciable. This implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}\geq 0
and is appreciable. Since
q
is appreciable, by (
7

|q|\geq 0
and is also appreciable.
Thus,
|q|^{2}\geq 0
and is appreciable. Then,
|q|^{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
and is also appreciable. We have

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{|q|^{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}=|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

If all
x_{i}
are infinitesimal, then all

are also infinitesimal. By (

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|q_{st}(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}|^{2}}\epsilon=|q_{st}|\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(x_{i})_{\mathcal{I}}|^{2}}\epsilon.

Since

Assume now that
q
is infinitesimal. Then
q=q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, which implies that all
qx_{i}=q_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}\epsilon
are infinitesimal. Consequently, by (
14
), we have

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|q_{\mathcal{I}}(x_{i})_{st}|^{2}}\epsilon=|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(x_{i})_{st}|^{2}}\epsilon=|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}\epsilon.

(16)

If
\mathbf{x}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then by (

6.3
, we have

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\left(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}-\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon\right)\epsilon=|q_{\mathcal{I}}|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\epsilon=|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

If
\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
, then

, which implies
\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
by (
14

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
is also infinitesimal, which implies, together with the fact that

, that
|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
. Hence
\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
. This proves that Property 2 holds for the
2
-norm.

Finally, let
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
. We wish to prove that

\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

(17)

By the properties of the
2
-norm for quaternions
[
15
]
, we have

\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}.

(18)

If

\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}<\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2},

(19)

then
\mathbf{x}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{y}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
. If
\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then by Proposition
6.3
, we have

\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}+u\epsilon,

where
u=(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R}+\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R})/\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{y}_{st}\neq\mathbf{0}
, by Proposition
6.3
, we have

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}+\left(\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}+\frac{(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}}\right)\epsilon.

By (
19
), we know that (
17
) holds. If
\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}=0<\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}
. Thus, (
17
) still holds.

If
\|\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}
, then by the argument at the beginning of this section, we know that

\|\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R}+\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R}\|_{2}.

By the properties of the
2
-norm of real vectors, either
\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{y}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
or there is a real positive number
t
such that
\mathbf{y}_{st}=t\mathbf{x}_{st}
. Hence, we divide this case to four subcases.

a.
\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{y}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
. Then
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
. We have
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon
, and by (

),

\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon\leq\left(\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\right)\epsilon=\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon+\|\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

b.
\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{y}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
. Then
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{st}+(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}})\epsilon
. Since
\mathbf{y}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
, by Proposition
6.3
, we have
\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}+v\epsilon
, where

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}+\frac{(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon+\frac{(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+\frac{(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2},

where the last second inequality is due to the fact that
(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}\leq\|\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{I}^{R}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{I}\|_{2}
.
Thus, we have (
17
).

c.
\mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{y}_{st}=\mathbf{0}
. By exchanging
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
in the subcase b, we also have (

).

d.
\mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
and

for a real positive number

. In this case, since

, by Proposition

, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=(1+t)\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R})}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon=\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}+\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon+\frac{(\mathbf{y}_{st}^{R})^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{R}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{st}\|_{2}}\epsilon,

where the second equality comes from
\mathbf{y}_{st}=t\mathbf{x}_{st}
. By Proposition
6.3
again, we know that (
17
) holds. Thus, Property 3 holds for the 2-norm.
∎

For any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
, it is not difficult to show that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
.

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})^{\top},\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
, let the conjugate transpose of
\mathbf{x}
be
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},\cdots,x_{n}^{*})
, and define
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{*}y_{i}
. If
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}=0
, then we say that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are orthogonal. It is not difficult to show that
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{x}=1
if and only if
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
. In this case, we say that
\mathbf{x}
is a unit dual quaternion vector. If
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\in{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
, and
\left(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right)^{*}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}=\delta_{ij}
for

, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol, then we say that
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{DQ}}^{n}
.
