Abstract

We generalize results on the
p
-adic valuations of
S(n,k)
, the Stirling number of the second kind and
s(n,k)
, the
Stirling number of the first kind.
We have several new estimates for these valuations, along with criteria for when the estimates are sharp.
The primary foci are the explicit evaluation of
\nu_{2}(S(n,k))
with
n=c2^{h},k=b2^{h}+a
,

a,b,c,h,k,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
1\leq a\leq 2^{h-1}
, and
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
when
n=cp^{h}
for an odd prime
p
.
We have strong new results, which generalize and strengthen previous results, for all primes.
We also have some new results on the
p
-adic valuations
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))
for all primes.
We generally assume that
p-1\mid n-k
for exact values of
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
or
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))
.
In addition, we have proved some new Amdeberhan-type identities for Stirling numbers of both kinds.
We also extend some recent results
and propose two new conjectures, as well as proofs and extensions of previous ones.

Some of these results are universal and deal with

The first efforts to analyze the

.
A special
2
-adic study of the Stirling numbers of the second kind was
initiated by Lengyel in 1994 (cf.
[
15
, Theorems 1 and 2]
), which established that
\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},k))=\sigma_{2}(k)-1
for all

and contained a conjecture that was proven by De Wannemacker in

[
26
]
in 2005:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
26
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
h,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k\leq 2^{h}
. Then we have

\nu_{2}(S(2^{h},k))=\sigma_{2}(k)-1.

(1.1)

Lengyel extended this theorem in

Theorem 1.2

(
[
16
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
c,k,h\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k\leq 2^{h}
, then

\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},k))=\sigma_{2}(k)-1.

(1.2)

He also provided alternative proofs in
[
17
]
.
The major development came in a sequence of papers by Adelberg (cf.
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
) that depended on his previous work on higher order Bernoulli numbers and polynomials,
and on the relation between higher order Bernoulli numbers and Stirling polynomials.
In
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
, Adelberg generalized (
1.1
) for
arbitrary primes and replaced the term
2^{h}
with the condition that
S(n,k)

is a so-called minimum zero case (MZC).
Note that
S(c2^{h},k),1\leq k\leq 2^{h}
with
c
odd is a MZC if
c=1
, but not otherwise.
A remarkable short and instructive proof of Theorem
1.1
is presented as a special case in
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
.
To deal with the case of
c>1
and odd, Adelberg also introduced the notion of almost
minimum zero case (AMZC) in
[
5
]
that resulted in a short proof of Theorem
1.2
.
We define these concepts in relation with inequality (
1.3
).
De Wannemacker’s useful inequality
[
26
, Theorem 3]
states that

\nu_{2}(S(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k)-\sigma_{2}(n)

(1.3)

with
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq k\leq n
.

Although (
1.3
) is general in
n
and
k
, it was Adelberg who developed a general theory in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
that removed the limitations due to the assumed nature of
n=c2^{h}
and
k:\,1\leq k\leq 2^{h}
in
S(n,k)
that were used in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Adelberg
called the estimate (
1.3
) the minimum zero estimate, which he
improved
in several ways. He generalized it to odd primes, and proved a criterion for when
it is sharp, i.e., when
S(n,k)
is a minimum zero case (MZC).
He also added an additional term to improve the estimate in all cases where it is not sharp, namely he proved in
[
5
, Theorem 3.3]
that

\nu_{2}(S(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k)-\sigma_{2}(n)+N,

(1.4)

where
N=\#\{[n]\bigcap[n-k]\}
, i.e., the number of
2
-powers common to the base 2
expansions of
n
and
n-k
,
which he called the almost minimum zero estimate. Adelberg showed that the MZC occurs if and only if
N=0
in (
1.4
), i.e.,
\nu_{2}(S(n,k))=\sigma_{2}(k)-\sigma_{2}(n)
.
He also showed that his new estimate is always nonnegative, unlike De Wannemacker’s estimate (1.3), which can be negative. If (
1.3
) is not sharp but (
1.4
) is sharp, we have an almost minimum zero case (AMZC).
In addition, he obtained certain “shifted” estimates and cases for
p=2
, which obviate the need for many inductions.
In this paper we generalize these concepts to arbitrary primes. In particular, the shifted cases give us insight into certain cases where
\nu_{p}(S(n+1,k+1))=\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
and where
\nu_{p}(s(n-1,k-1))=\nu_{p}(s(n,k))
, continuing the generalization that was done in
[
4
]
of what was originally a conjecture of Amdeberhan et al.; cf.
[
6
]
. We give geometric interpretations of all the cases in terms of the Newton polygons of certain higher order Bernoulli polynomials.

The cases when we get exact values for the
p
-adic valuations generally assume that
p-1\mid n-k
.
Our analysis is based on the “maximum poles” of higher order Bernoulli polynomials, which are the highest powers of
p
in the denominators of their coefficients.
Using these polynomials is a unique feature of the presented method, which generalizes the approach used in
[
5
]
for
p=2
.
The results for
p=2
are simpler and more explicit than the results for odd primes, primarily because the maximum pole is much easier to determine for
p=2
, where there is a simple formula depending on base 2 expansions.

In the analysis of
\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},k))
the range
k\in[1,2^{h+1}]
has been analyzed
in
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
with
c\geq 3
odd. In this paper we focus on different
ranges and sets of
k
values. Of course, Theorem
1.2
has already dealt
with the range
k\in[1,2^{h}]
and, as it turns out, the answer essentially relies
on the fact that
S(c2^{h},k)
is an AMZC. The upper range

[

Theorem 1.3

(
[
24

)

Let

.

\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},(c-1)2^{h}+a))=\sigma_{2}(a)-1.

The proof illustrates Adelberg’s method, which depends on the analysis of higher order Bernoulli numbers and polynomials.

An important result for the calculation of

-adic valuations

Summary of previous results

For

Theorem 2.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
)

With

and

(a)

\nu_{2}(b)<\nu_{2}(c)
or if
\nu_{2}(b)=\nu_{2}(c)

2^{\nu_{2}(c-b)}\in[b]

\lim_{h\to\infty}\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},b2^{h}))=\sigma_{2}(b)-\sigma_{2}(c)+\nu_{2}\left({2c-b\choose c}\right)

and this limit is attained if
2^{h-1+\nu_{2}(c-b)}\geq\nu_{2}\left({2c-b\choose c}\right)
;

(b)

if
\nu_{2}(c)<\nu_{2}(b)
or
\nu_{2}(c)=\nu_{2}(b)
and
2^{\nu_{2}(c-b)}\in[c]
, then

\lim_{h\to\infty}\nu_{2}(S(c2^{h},b2^{h}))=\sigma_{2}(b-1)-\sigma_{2}(c-1)+\nu_{2}\left({2c-b-1\choose c-1}\right).

Furthermore, if
\nu_{2}(c)<\nu_{2}(b)
, the limit is attained if
2^{h-1+\nu_{2}(c)}\geq\nu_{2}\left({2c-b-1\choose c-1}\right)
,
while if
\nu_{2}(c)=\nu_{2}(b)
and
2^{\nu_{2}(c-b)}\in[c]
, the limit is attained if
2^{h-1+\nu_{2}(c)}>\nu_{2}\left({2c-b-1\choose c-1}\right)
.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

Part (a) of the preceding theorem is characterized by
2^{\nu_{2}(c-b)}\in[b]
and part (b), by
2^{\nu_{2}(c-b)}\in[c]
.

(ii)

If
\nu_{2}(b)\leq\nu_{2}(c)

\nu_{2}({2c-b\choose c})=\nu_{2}(b)-\nu_{2}(c)+\nu_{2}({2c-b-1\choose c-1})

(iii)

The estimates given in Theorem

are not always sharp.

and

2.2
Estimates and cases for

In

4
]
Adelberg considered the minimum zero estimate (MZ)

\nu_{p}(S(n,k)\geq(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1).

(2.5)

He found simple necessary and sufficient conditions for when this estimate is sharp, called the minimum zero case (MZC), namely

r=(n-k)/(p-1)\in\mathbb{N}
, and
S(n,k)
is a MZC if and only if

He tied this result to higher order Bernoulli numbers by showing that
S(n,k)
is a MZC if an only if
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(-k)})=-\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)
.
In
[
5
]
, dealing only with the prime
p=2
, Adelberg found the additional shifted minimum zero estimate (SMZ)
\nu_{2}(S(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k-1)-\sigma_{2}(n-1)
, and showed that this is sharp, called shifted minimum zero case (SMZC) if and only if
2\nmid{n-1+r\choose r}
. In addition, he also found more refined estimates depending on the base 2 expansions of
n
,
k
, and
n-k
, called the almost minimum zero (AMZ) and shifted almost minimum zero (SAMZ) estimates, and defined the almost minimum zero case (AMZC) and shifted almost minimum zero case (SAMZC) as when these estimates are sharp.
He noted that these estimates
depended on
the maximum poles
of the associated higher order Bernoulli polynomials, i.e., the highest powers of 2 in the denominators of their coefficients.

In this paper, we are interested in extending these concepts
to all primes
p
, so we have to define them for odd
p
and establish basic properties. The key formulas and definitions are summarized in Section
5
. We have the connecting formulas (cf. (
5.2
))

S(n,k)={n\choose k}B_{n-k}^{(-k)}

(2.6)

and

S(n,k)={n-1\choose k-1}B_{n-k}^{(-k+1)}(1).

(2.7)

The second formula, called shifted, is obtained by replacing
(n,k)
by
(n-1,k-1)
in the higher order Bernoulli polynomial
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
, and then replacing the constant term
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}
by
B_{n-k}^{(-k+1)}(1)
, which is the constant term of
B_{n-k}^{(-k+1)}(x+1)
.
From the second formula we get the shifted minimum zero estimate (SMZ):

\nu_{p}(S(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1).

(2.8)

When this estimate is sharp we have the shifted minimum zero case (SMZC).

The SMZ estimate can be easily expressed in terms of the MZ estimate using (
2.8
), namely the SMZ estimate is
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+\nu_{p}(k)-\nu_{p}(n)

by (
5.1
),
so the two estimates are equal if and only if
\nu_{p}(k)=\nu_{p}(n)
and the SMZ estimate is better (bigger) if and only if
\nu_{p}(n)<\nu_{p}(k)
. In particular, we get the following result.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
S(n,k)
is a MZC then
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
, while if
S(n,k)
is a SMZC, then
\nu_{p}(n)\leq\nu_{p}(k)
.

Remark 2.10
.

Obviously, the SMZ estimate for
S(n+1,k+1)
is the same as the MZ estimate for
S(n,k)
. We will now establish simple criteria for the MZ and SMZ cases, and use that in Section
3.3
to determine conditions that insure
\nu_{p}(S(n+1,k+1))=\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
, which we call an “Amdeberhan-type identity.”

The following theorem follows easily from the discussion of poles summarized in Section
5.3
. Our unified treatment via higher order Bernoulli numbers and polynomials allows us to consider both kinds of Stirling numbers (cf. Section
4
).

Theorem 2.11
.

If
l\in\mathbb{Z}
the
{\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})=-\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)}
if and only if
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)}(1))={-\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

The condition that
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})=-\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
is equivalent to
B_{n}^{(l)}
has a pole of order
\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
, so
B_{n}^{(l)}
, which is the constant coefficient of the higher order Bernoulli polynomial

has a greater pole (smaller

\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)}(1))
, which is the sum of all coefficients, has the same pole
\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
. ∎

The equation
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})=-\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
is equivalent to

is a maximum pole case (MPC) as defined in
[

, and the equation

Corollary 2.12

3.2
The exact
p
-adic order

In this section we generalize some of the results for
odd primes
p
, although they may apply to
p=2
as well.
Much of the terminology is explained in Section
5
, and many of the proofs follow directly from the results in that section.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
p
be an odd prime.
Assume that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
and
r=(n-k)/(p-1)\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following are equivalent

(1)

S(n,k)
is an AMZC;

(2)

S(n+1,k+1)
is a SAMZC;

(3)

\nu_{p}({r+n\choose n})=\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)-M
, where
M
is the maximum pole of

B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
;

(4)

\nu_{p}(\tau_{u})=-M
, where
u_{p-1}=r
; cf. Section
5.3
.

Furthermore, if these conditions hold then

\nu_{p}(S(n+1,k+1))=\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+\nu_{p}({r+n\choose n})
.

The proof follows immediately from
Theorem
5.11
, where the partition
u^{\prime}
concentrated in place
p-1
with
u^{\prime}_{p-1}=r-1
is eliminated from consideration by the assumption that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
. If
\nu_{p}(k)>\nu_{p}(n)
, then
\nu_{p}(k)>0
and
\nu_{p}(k-1)=0
, so we can replace
(n,k)
by
(n-1,k-1)
and
M
by
M^{\prime}
, the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{-(k-1)}(x)
in
Theorem
3.6
.

For
p=2
there are small modifications: There are now 3 canonical partitions (cf. Theorem
5.13
), but the one with
u_{1}=n-k-1
has weight less than
n-k
, so is not involved in
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(1)
, and is also ruled out by the hypothesis that
\nu_{2}(k)\leq\nu_{2}(n)
. We then have conditions (1) and (2) are equivalent, and if they hold, then the Amdeberhan equality
(cf. Remark
2.10
)
holds. The other analysis can be changed to reflect that there are now two possible candidate partitions
u
and
u^{\prime}
with
u_{1}=n-k
and with
u^{\prime}_{1}=n-k-3
and
u^{\prime}_{3}=1
,
exactly one of which must work for an AMZC.

Remark 3.7
.

The preceding Theorem
3.6
contains some very general results that apply to our cases. In particular, the last formula gives a very simple value for
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
, which does not depend on the maximum pole of any higher order Bernoulli polynomials, which may be difficult to calculate. Of course, the AMZC and SAMZC implicitly involve the maximum poles, but the binomial coefficient encodes that, and demonstrates precisely how we have improved the MZ estimate. The situation is similar for the SMZ estimate.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

We use Theorem
5.11
, with
n:=n-k
and
l:=-k
.
Then, if
M
is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
, the
assumption that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
translates to
\nu_{p}(l)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
, so the partition
u
with
u_{p-1}=r
is the only
possible
candidate for
\nu_{p}((n-k)!t_{u})=-M
, among all partitions
u

with
w(u)\leq n-k
.
Thus,
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(-k)})=-M
if and only if

\nu_{p}(\tau_{u})=-M
, if and only if
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(1))=-M
.
The first of these equalities defines
S(n,k)
is a AMZC, and the
last equality defines
S(n+1,k+1)
is a SAMZC. Since

\tau_{u}=(n-k)!{l-(n-k)-1\choose d}/p^{r}
, we have

\nu_{p}(\tau_{u})=(n-k-\sigma_{p}(n-k))/(p-1)+\nu_{p}({n+r\choose n})-r=-\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)+\nu_{p}({n+r\choose n})
, so the middle equality
says that
\nu_{p}(\tau_{u})=-M
, i.e., that is the unique
candidate for the maximum pole.
Thus, conditions (1)-(4) are equivalent. If we now assume that they hold, then
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(-k)})=\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(-(k+1)+1)}(1))=-M
, and
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)-M=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+\nu_{p}({n+r\choose n})
.
∎

The nice thing about this formula for
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
is that it
does not involve any a priori value of the maximum pole. Of course,
it is only valid if we have the conditions (1)–(4), e.g., if
S(n,k)

is an AMZC, in addition to
p-1\mid n-k
and
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
.

We have a similar formula for
p=2
based on the definitions and
analysis in
[
5
]
. The situation is slightly more
complicated, because for
p=2
there are three canonical
partitions, and one of which is
ruled out by the assumption that

\nu_{2}(k)\leq\nu_{2}(n)
.

Theorem 3.8
.

For
p=2
, assume that
\nu_{2}(k)\leq\nu_{2}(n)
.
Then the
following are equivalent

(1)

S(n,k)
is an AMZC;

(2)

S(n+1,k+1)

is a SAMZC;

(3)

\nu_{2}({n+n-k\choose n})=\sigma_{2}(n-k)-M
or

\nu_{2}({n+n-k-2\choose n})=\sigma_{2}(n-k)-(M+1)
and
n-k
is odd, where the or is exclusive and

is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
;

(4)

If
u_{1}=n-k
and
u^{\prime}_{1}=n-k-3
,
u^{\prime}_{3}=1

\nu_{2}(\tau_{u})=-M
or
\nu_{2}(\tau_{u^{\prime}})=-M

Furthermore if these conditions hold then

\nu_{2}(S(n,k))=\nu_{2}(S(n+1,k+1))

by the assumption that

. The
one that works determines

. The
2
-adic values
differ by one.

Remark 3.9
.

For any prime
p
, if
\nu_{p}(k)>\nu_{p}(n)
then
\nu_{p}(k-1)=0\leq\nu_{p}(n-1)

3.6
and
3.8

The main theorems in this section follow from Theorem
3.6
.
Our primary task is showing that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.6

are satisfied. We define the least positive residue
k^{\prime}
of
k\bmod{(p-1)}
by
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq p-1
and

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
n=cp^{h}
where
c=\sum_{i\geq 0}c_{i}p^{i}
is the base
p
expansion of
c
. Let
k^{\prime}
be the least positive residue of
k\bmod{(p-1)}
.
Assume that
0<k\leq\min\{c_{0},k^{\prime}\}p^{h}
.

(a)

If
\sigma_{p}(n)\geq k^{\prime}
and
n^{\prime}
is the smallest segment of (the base
p
expansion of)
n
with
\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime})=k^{\prime}
, then the maximum pole
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
equals
\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime}-k)/(p-1)

and the almost minimum zero estimate is
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))\geq

(\sigma_{p}(k)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)
.

(b)

If
n\equiv k\bmod{(p-1)}
then
S(n,k)
is an AMZC
and
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)
.

Note that the estimate depends only on
k
and is independent of
n
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Suppose that
p^{h}\mid n
and
0<k\leq p^{h}
. If
p-1\mid n-k
then
S(n,k)
is an AMZC and

\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)=\lfloor(\sigma_{p}(k)-1)/(p-1)\rfloor
, using the floor function.

.

Theorem

,
which is a MZC as it is given in Theorem

[

Proof of Theorem
3.10
.

Follows easily from the material in maximum poles and the Kimura
N
-function in the Appendix, and from Theorem
3.6
.
First prove part (a). Note that since
n^{\prime}
is the bottom segment of the base
p
representation of
n
with
\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime})=k^{\prime}
, we see that
n^{\prime}
is a generalization of the Kimura
N
-function, namely if

p-1\mid k
then
n^{\prime}=N(n;p)
.

Let
N_{1}=N(n^{\prime}-k;p),N_{2}=N(n^{\prime}-k-N_{1};p),\dots,N_{M}=N(n^{\prime}-k-N_{1}-\dots-N_{M-1};p)
.
Then
\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}N_{i}=n^{\prime}-k
, and
\sigma_{p}(N_{i})=p-1
and
\sum\sigma_{p}(N_{i})
has no base
p
carries. This gives
M(p-1)=\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime}-k)
, i.e.
M=\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime}-k)/(p-1)
. If
i<M
, all
p
-powers in
N_{i}
are less than
p^{h}
, so
n+N_{i}/(p-1)
has no base
p
carries. On the other hand, since
\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime})\leq p-1
, it follows that
\nu_{p}(N_{M})<h
. But
N_{M}+N_{M}/(p-1)
has only a single base
p
carry, occurring in the lowest
p
-power of
N_{M}
. Hence
n+N_{M}/(p-1)
has no base
p
carry, and thus

n+(n^{\prime}-k)/(p-1)
has no base
p
carry.

On the other hand, if
N
is a segment of
n-k-(n^{\prime}-k)=n-n^{\prime}
, then
N+N/(p-1)
has a base
p
carry. But
N
is a segment of
n
, so
n+N/(p-1
) has a base
p
carry. Thus
N_{1},N_{2},\dots,N_{M}
is the (longest) Kimura chain, and
M
is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
.

The AMZ estimate is
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+(\sigma_{p}(n-k)-\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime}-k))/(p-1)=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n)+\sigma_{p}(n-n^{\prime}))/(p-1)=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime})/(p-1)=(\sigma_{p}(k)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)
.

For part (b), since
k\leq c_{0}p^{h}
, we have
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)=h
. To finish the proof, by Theorem
3.6
,
we have only to show that
\nu_{p}({n+r\choose r})=\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)-M=(\sigma_{p}(n-k)-\sigma_{p}(n^{\prime}-k))/(p-1)=\sigma_{p}(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)=(\sigma_{p}(n)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)
.

Since
n+r=n+(n-k)/(p-1)=n+(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)+(n^{\prime}-k)/(p-1)
, and
n+(n^{\prime}-k)/(p-1)
has no base
p
carries, it suffices to consider
n+(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)=n-n^{\prime}+(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)+n^{\prime}
. Since the number of base
p
carries for
n-n^{\prime}+(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)
is
\sigma_{p}(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)
, it follows that the number of carries is
\sigma_{p}(n-n^{\prime})/(p-1)=(\sigma_{p}(n)-k^{\prime})/(p-1)=\nu_{p}({n+r\choose n})
, so
S(n,k)
is an AMZC and the estimate in (a) is sharp.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

We note that it is easy to test if we have a MZC provided that
n\equiv k\bmod{(p-1)},k\leq n,
which is equivalent to
r=(n-k)/(p-1)\in\mathbb{N}
. Adelberg proved in
[

that

is an MZC if and only if
p\nmid{n+r\choose r}
. In this case, by definition, we can determine
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))
without any direct calculation with Stirling numbers since
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)
. For the SMZC we have the condition that
p\nmid{n-1+r\choose r}
, and then
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1)
; cf.
[

for the case of

and it holds for any odd prime, too.

The situation is similar for the AMZC and SAMZC conditions,
although the non-trivial calculation of the maximum pole

is required; cf. Theorem
3.6
.
With
r=(n-k)/(p-1)\in\mathbb{N}
, if we add the condition that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
then
we have an AMZC if and only if
\nu_{p}\left({n+r\choose r}\right)=\sigma_{p}(n-k)/(p-1)-M
where

is the maximum pole of

. Then, once

is determined,
but without any calculations with

, we obtain that
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=\nu_{p}\left({n\choose k}\right)-M=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+\nu_{p}({n+r\choose r})
. In a similar fashion,
with

, if we add the condition that
\nu_{p}(n)<\nu_{p}(k)

then we have a SAMZC if and only if

is the maximum pole of

is determined,

, we obtain that

Remark 3.18
.

Theorem
3.10
can be further generalized to give the following theorem. This theorem is more general, since it dispenses with the condition that

, and in part (b) offers an alternative test to show that

is an AMZC without apriori calculation for the maximum pole

. This theorem is stronger than Theorem
3.10
, and the hypotheses are more germane to the conclusion. It retains only the hypotheses that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
and
p-1\mid n-k
.
Theorem
3.10
is the special case of this theorem where
B=n^{\prime}
.

We need the notion of segments; cf. Definition
5.1
.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
B
be a bottom segment of
n
such that
0<k\leq B
. Suppose that
p-1\mid B-k
and
p\nmid{n+(B-k)/(p-1)\choose n}
.
Then if
M
is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(-k)}(x)
, we have

(a)

M=\sigma_{p}(B-k)/(p-1)
, and the first occurrence of this pole in the higher order Bernoulli polynomial is in degree

degree

;

(b)

furthermore, if

is an AMZC and
\nu_{p}(S(n,k))=(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(B))/(p-1)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.19
.

The proof is essentially identical to that of Theorem
3.10
, with the segment

replaced by

. The additional information about the first occurrence of the pole of order
M
being in degree

follows from the general theory of maximum poles, namely if

is the (longest) Kimura chain, then the first occurrence of the maximal pole is in degree

For part (b), the hypothesis guarantees that

is a MZC, which implies that
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(B)\leq\nu_{p}(n)

s(n,k)

4.1
Preliminaries-Estimates and cases

The in-depth study of
p
-adic evaluation of Stirling numbers of the first kind started by Lengyel in
[
19
]

who noticed the inherent and significant differences in the
p
-adic behavior of the Stirling numbers of the first and second kinds.

Further progress has been made by Leonetti and Sanna
[
20
]
, Komatsu and Young
[
14
]
, Adelberg
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, and Qui and Hong
[
12
]
and
[
23
]
.

As an example of our approach, we illustrate it by consideration of the following theorems.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

For arbitrary positive integers
h
and
k
such that
k\leq 2^{h}
, we have

\nu_{2}(s(2^{h}+1,k+1))=\nu_{2}(s(2^{h},k))
.

Note that they also proved

Theorem 4.2

(
[
23
, Corollary 1.3]
)
.

For arbitrary integers
h
and
k
such that
h\geq 2
and
2\leq k\leq 2^{h-1}+1
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(s(2^{h},2^{h}-k))=\begin{cases}h-1-\nu_{2}(k),&\text{ if }2\mid k,\\
2h-2-\nu_{2}(k-1),&\text{ if }2\nmid k.\end{cases}

Although with Adelberg’s technique we can prove only limited versions of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
,
our proofs are very short and transparent, and can provide the inductive basis of more general proofs.
They illustrate how the estimates and cases can be used, and also demonstrate the limitations of our methods in dealing with Stirling numbers of the first kind. The proofs in
[
23
]
are very long and involved, with many inductions.

Our method for dealing with Stirling numbers of the first kind is entirely analogous to the method for dealing with Stirling numbers of the second kind, since both depend entirely on the translation from higher order Bernoulli numbers and polynomials to Stirling numbers.

The basic translation formulas for Stirling numbers of the first kind are

s(n,k)={n-1\choose k-1}B_{n-k}^{(n)}

(4.1)

and the “shifted formula”

s(n,k)={n\choose k}B_{n-k}^{(n+1)}(1).

(4.2)

Comparison of formulas (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) for Stirling numbers of the first kind with formulas (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) for Stirling numbers of the second kind, illustrates the duality between the different types of Stirling numbers,
which arises
from the duality between Stirling polynomials of the first and second kinds.
This duality basically reverses the roles of
n
and
k
, reverses inequalities, and interchanges sums and differences. Essentially one interchanges the
n
and
k
, and replaces them by their negatives. This will be further illustrated by the estimates and cases, and by the applications.

Since most of our analysis of Stirling numbers of the first kind is almost the same as for Stirling numbers of the second kind, we will state the results, with proofs only when there are significant differences.

For a general prime
p
, Adelberg proved a series of interesting results in
[
4
]
.
He proved the MZ estimate
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1)
. He showed that
s(n,k)
is a MZC if and only if the estimate is sharp, which is in turn equivalent to
p\nmid{k-1\choose r}
, where
r=(n-k)/(p-1)\in\mathbb{N}

in
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
. He also proved in
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
that if
s(n,k)
a MZC then the Amdeberhan-type result

\nu_{p}(s(n-1,k-1))=\nu_{p}(s(n,k))
holds.

For
p=2
Adelberg introduced the MZ, SMZ, AMZ, and SAMZ estimates (
4.3
)–(
4.6
) for
s(n,k)
in
[
5
, (3.7)–(3.10)]
, in respective order:

\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k-1)-\sigma_{2}(n-1),

(4.3)

\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k)-\sigma_{2}(n),

(4.4)

\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k-1)-\sigma_{2}(n-1)+\#([n-k]-[k-1]),

(4.5)

and

\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k)-\sigma_{2}(n)+\#([n-k]-[k]).

(4.6)

Since for the higher order Bernoulli polynomial
B_{n-k}^{(n)}(x)
, using the notations of Section 5.3, we have
s=n-(n-k)-1=k-1
, by Remark
5.7

the maximum pole
M=\#([k-1]\bigcap[n-k])
, and similarly, the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(n+1)}(x)
is
M^{\prime}=\#([k]\bigcap[n-k])
, so we can reformulate the AMZ (almost minimum zero) estimate and SAMZ (shifted almost minimum zero) estimate as
\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\nu_{2}({n-1\choose k-1})-M
and
\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\nu_{2}({n\choose k})-M^{\prime}
, respectively.

If any of these estimates is sharp, we have a “case,” so again we have the cases MZC, SMZC, AMZC, and SAMZC, with the same geometric interpretations.

The reformulation can be taken to define the four estimates and cases for an arbitrary prime
p
. Alternatively, one can use Definition
5.8

with
n:=n-k
and
l:=n
. Since in
[
5
]
only
p=2
was considered, and the focus was on Stirling numbers of the second kind, the estimates and cases were given for Stirling numbers of the first kind only for
p=2
, but there was no further development.
Accordingly, we will now generalize the estimates and cases (when the estimates are sharp) for all primes
p
for Stirling numbers of the first kind.

For odd
p
, if
M
is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(n)}(x)
and
M^{\prime}
is the maximum pole of
B_{n-k}^{(n+1)}(x)
respectively, the MZ, SMZ, AMZ, and SAMZ estimates in respective order are given in (
4.7
)–(
4.10
):

\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1)

(4.7)

\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)

(4.8)

\nu_{p}(s(n,k)\geq(\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1)+(\sigma_{p}(n-k))/(p-1)-M

(4.9)

\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq(\sigma_{p}(k)-\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)+(\sigma_{p}(n-k))/(p-1)-M^{\prime}.

(4.10)

Observe that we have alternate formulations to the estimates (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), namely
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq\nu_{p}({n-1\choose k-1})-M
and
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))\geq\nu_{p}({n\choose k})-M^{\prime}
, respectively. Also
s(n,k)
is an AMZC if and only if
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(n)})=-M
, and
s(n,k)
is a SAMZC if and only if
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(n+1)}(1))=-M^{\prime}
.

Note that the AMZ estimate improves the MZ estimate if that estimate is not sharp, and similarly the SAMZ estimate improves the SMZ estimate if that one is not sharp. Also note that the shifted MZ estimate for
s(n,k)
is the same as the MZ estimate for
s(n+1,k+1)
, and similarly, the shifted AMZ estimate for
s(n,k)
is the same as the AMZ estimate for
s(n+1,k+1)
, so
s(n,k)
is a SMZC if and only if
s(n+1,k+1)
is a MZC, and if these cases hold, then
\nu_{p}(s(n+1,k+1))=\nu_{p}(s(n,k))
, which is equivalent to
\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(n+1)})=\nu_{p}(B_{n-k}^{(n+1)}(1))
. This result considerably strengthens
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
, namely if we replace
(n,k)
by
(n-1,k-1)
, then we now have that if
s(n,k)
is a MZC then
\nu_{2}(s(n,k))=\nu_{2}(s(n-1,k-1))
and
s(n-1,k-1)
is a SMZC, which we did not have previously. In particular, we get the following improvement of
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
1\leq a\leq p-1
and
k=ap^{h}
and assume that
p-1\mid n-a
and
k\leq n<kp
. Then
s(n,k)
is a MZC and
s(n-1,k-1)
is a SMZC and
\nu_{p}(s(n,k))=\nu_{p}(s(n-1,k-1))=((\sigma_{p}(k-1)-\sigma_{p}(n-1))/(p-1)
, which is an Amdeberhan-type result.

Observe that according to (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), the theorem is the dual of De Wannemacker’s theorem for Stirling numbers of the second kind as generalized to all primes in
[

.

We can also easily show as before that if

\nu_{p}(n)\leq\nu_{p}(k)
, while if

is a SMZC then
\nu_{p}(k)\leq\nu_{p}(n)
. Finally, we will prove the following theorem here, because the proof differs significantly from that of

Theorem 4.4
.

The AMZ and SAMZ estimates for

Proof of Theorem

p\nmid{k-1\choose N_{i}/(p-1)}
. Thus, if
p=2
, the maximum pole

is
\#([n-k]\bigcap[k-1])
, and the almost minimum zero estimate is
\nu_{2}(s(n,k))\geq\sigma_{2}(k-1)-\sigma_{2}(n-1)+\#([n-k]-[k-1])
.

Adelberg defined in
[
3
]
the concept of “maximum pole case” (MPC) for higher order Bernoulli polynomials,
when the maximum pole of

has order
\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
, which
led to the concept of “minimum zero case” (MZC) for Stirling numbers of both kinds in
[
4
]
. We now have the concepts of almost minimum zero, shifted minimum zero and shifted almost minimum zero cases for
p=2
for Stirling numbers of both kinds (cf.
[
5
]
), and we invert the process by now defining the concepts of almost maximum pole case, shifted maximum pole case, and shifted almost maximum case for higher order Bernoulli polynomials for general primes
p
.

Definition 5.8
.

B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
is an almost maximum pole case if the pole of
B_{n}^{(l)}
is the maximum pole of
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
;
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
is a shifted maximum pole case if the pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(1)
has order
\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
,
which is equivalent to the pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}

has order
\sigma_{p}(n))/(p-1)
;

B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
is a shifted almost maximum pole case if the pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(1)
is the maximum pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x+1)
, which is the maximum pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x)
.

Remark 5.9
.

Each of these cases corresponds to an estimate, namely the MP and SMP cases correspond to the estimates
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})
and
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l+1)}(1))\geq-\sigma_{p}(n)/(p-1)
, while the AMP and SAMP cases correspond to the estimates
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})\geq-M
and
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l+1)}(1))\geq-M^{\prime}
, where
M
is the maximum pole of
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
and where
M^{\prime}
is the maximum pole of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x)
.
Furthermore, using the standard translations (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) from higher order Bernoulli numbers and polynomials to Stirling numbers of each kind, these cases and estimates correspond to the cases and estimates for the Stirling numbers of each kind.

Remark 5.10
.

The motivation for the shifted cases is the recursion formula for higher order Bernoulli numbers. It should be noted that since the strictly decreasing portion of the Newton polygon of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x+1)
is the same as the strictly decreasing portion
of the Newton polygon of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x)
, these two polynomials have the same maximum pole. Thus, we have geometric interpretations of each of the four cases:

•

the maximum pole case (MPC) says that the Newton polygon of
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
is strictly decreasing;

•

the almost maximum pole case (AMPC) says that the Newton polygon of
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
has a horizontal final segment;

•

the shifted maximum pole case (SMPC) says that the Newton polygon of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x+1)
(or of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x)
) is strictly decreasing;

•

the shifted almost maximum pole case (SAMPC) says that the Newton polynomial of
B_{n}^{(l+1)}(x+1)
has a horizontal final segment.

It should be noted that our terminology of poles and maximum poles, which we think is illuminating, is not completely standard. An alternative is to extend the
p
-adic valuation to
\mathbb{Q}[x]

by

\nu_{p}(f(x))=\min\{\nu_{p}(a_{i})\}

if
f(x)=\sum_{i}a_{i}x^{n-i}
.
We could then express each of the cases in terms of this extension, e.g., the AMPC says that
\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)}(x))=\nu_{p}(B_{n}^{(l)})
. This approach necessitates dealing with negative signs for the poles, and appears to us to be less constructive.

Definition 5.15
.

xxx

(1)

The minimum zero estimate is
