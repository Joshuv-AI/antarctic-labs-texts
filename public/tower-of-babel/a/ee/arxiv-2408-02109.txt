Optimal Estimation of Structured Covariance Operators
Omar Al-Ghattas, Jiaheng Chen, Daniel Sanz-Alonso, Nathan Waniorek

Abstract
This paper establishes optimal convergence rates for estimation of structured covariance operators of Gaussian processes. We study banded operators with kernels that decay rapidly off-the-diagonal and L q L^{q} -sparse operators with an unordered sparsity pattern. For these classes of operators, we find the minimax optimal rate of estimation in operator norm, identifying the fundamental dimension-free quantities that determine the sample complexity. In addition, we prove that tapering and thresholding estimators attain the optimal rate. The proof of the upper bound for tapering estimators requires novel techniques to circumvent the issue that discretization of a banded operator does not result, in general, in a banded covariance matrix. To derive lower bounds for banded and L q L^{q} -sparse classes, we introduce a general framework to lift theory from high-dimensional matrix estimation to the operator setting. Our work contributes to the growing literature on operator estimation and learning, building on ideas from high-dimensional statistics while also addressing new challenges that emerge in infinite dimension.

Introduction

Across many problems in statistics, it is essential to constrain the model by imposing structural assumptions such as sparsity, smoothness, the manifold hypothesis, or group invariance. A vast body of work has demonstrated that these and other forms of structure facilitate inference of high-dimensional vectors, large matrices, graphs, networks, and functions
[
12
,
83
,
86
,
36
,
34
,
68
,
30
,
51
]
. This paper sets forth the study of operator estimation and its fundamental limits under natural structural assumptions.
We consider two classes of covariance operators: banded integral operators with kernels that decay rapidly off-the-diagonal, and a more flexible family of
L^{q}
-sparse operators where the kernel need not concentrate around its diagonal. For both classes, we establish optimal convergence rates using a general framework to lift theory from high-dimensional matrix estimation to the operator setting. In so doing, we identify the dimension-free quantities that determine the sample complexity. Additionally, we show that tapering and thresholding estimators achieve the minimax optimal rate.

Our motivation to study covariance operator estimation stems from the growing interest in data-driven regularizers for inverse problems in function space
[
81
]
. In imaging applications, unlabeled data are routinely used to learn Tikhonov regularizers and prior covariance models
[
7
]
.
Similarly, operational algorithms for numerical weather prediction rely on an ensemble of forecasts to estimate a background prior covariance
[
31
]
. In these applications and many others, the data used to specify the prior covariance represent finely discretized functions. As data resolution continues to improve, we wish to understand the fundamental dimension-free, discretization-independent quantities that determine the difficulty of estimating the prior covariance. Relatedly, operator learning, i.e. the task of recovering an operator from pairs of inputs and outputs or from trajectory data
[
53
,
44
,
58
,
80
,
25
,
40
,

,

]
, has also received increased attention motivated by recent machine learning techniques to solve partial differential equations, see e.g.
[
37
,
43
,
73
,
56
]
. We emphasize the importance of analyzing the operator version of the covariance estimation problem, since many scientific problems are most naturally formulated in function space. While computations will inevitably need to be carried out with discretizations, designing and analyzing algorithms and estimation procedures in the function space setting often yields scalable and discretization-robust theory and methods
[
23
,
26
,
33
,
76
]
.

Banded structure in operators arises naturally in time series analyses and spatial datasets as a consequence of decay of correlations in time or space. Our theory shows that tapering estimators, akin to popular covariance localization techniques in the geophysical sciences
[
32
,
39
]
that often rely on the Gaspari-Cohn tapering function
[
35
]
, are minimax optimal. In addition, tapering estimators are computationally appealing, as they only estimate the kernel around its diagonal, thus reducing computational and memory costs.
L^{q}
-sparsity structure in operators is a weaker requirement than bandedness, and, not surprisingly,
L^{q}
-sparse operators are more challenging to estimate.
While tapering estimators fail in general over the class of
L^{q}
-sparse operators, we show that thresholding estimators achieve the minimax rate. A caveat is that thresholding estimators require to pre-compute the full sample covariance and to threshold it point-wise at higher computational cost.

Covariance matrix estimation: Sparsity assumption

1.1.3
Covariance operator estimation: Unstructured setting

Let
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
be a centered square-integrable Gaussian process on a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The covariance function (kernel)
k:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
and operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
of
u
satisfy that, for any
x,y\in D
and
\psi\in L^{2}(D)
,

\displaystyle k(x,y):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(x)u(y)\bigr],\qquad(\mathcal{C}\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}k(\cdot,y)\psi(y)\,dy.

Given data
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
comprising
N
independent copies of
u,

the sample covariance function
\widehat{k}
and sample covariance operator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{k}(x,y):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}(x)u_{n}(y),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}(\cdot,y)\psi(y)\,dy,

where here and throughout this paper we will tacitly assume that point-wise evaluations of
u
are almost surely well-defined Lebesgue almost-everywhere.
The work
[
49
]
shows that, for any sample size
N,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{r(\mathcal{C})}{N}}\lor\frac{r(\mathcal{C})}{N},\qquad r(\mathcal{C}):=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})}{\|\mathcal{C}\|},

where
r(\mathcal{C})
is called the
effective dimension
, and
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})
denotes the trace of
\mathcal{C}
. It is also known that the term
\sqrt{r(\mathcal{C})/N}
appears in the minimax lower bound for covariance matrix estimation
[
59
, Theorem 2]
.
More recently,
[
38
, Theorem 2.3]
establishes an exact upper bound with optimal constants for the relative error
in (
1.6
) using Gaussian comparison techniques.

1.1.4
Covariance operator estimation: Banded and sparsity assumptions

1.3
Notions of dimension

In this paper, we study the estimation of the covariance operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
of a Gaussian process
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
defined on the domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
based on
N
independent copies
u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}.
It is important to clearly differentiate between the dimension of the parameter space, the dimension
d
of the domain
D
where the process
u
is defined, and the dimension of the data. We use the following conventions and terminology:

•

Parameter space dimension:

We will consider infinite-dimensional parameter spaces of banded and
L^{q}
-sparse operators on
L^{2}(D)
.
Our bounds are
dimension-free
in that they do not depend on the dimension of the parameter space —which is infinite— but only on fundamental finite quantities such as the
effective dimension

r(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})/\|\mathcal{C}\|
of the true covariance operator, the off-diagonal decay of its kernel, and the expected supremum of the process
u
. Dimension-free bounds are essential to obtain
discretization-independent
control of the estimation error; see e.g.
[
76
]
.

•

Physical dimension:
We use this term to refer to the dimension
d
of the domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
where the Gaussian process
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
is defined. Since in most scientific applications the physical dimension is small (typically
d\leq 3)
, we treat it as a constant in our analysis. We expect, however, our bounds to worsen in the large
d
asymptotic.

•

Data dimension:
In this work, the data
u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}
represent
N
independent copies of a Gaussian process. Each data point belongs to the infinite-dimensional space of square-integrable functions on
D=[0,1]^{d}
. Subsection
1.1.5
compares this setting with the partially observed setting in functional data analysis, where the data are finite-dimensional vectors.

Notation

For positive sequences

, we write

a_{n}\leq cb_{n}.
If both

For an operator

Estimating banded covariance operators

2.1
Upper bound

Motivated by the covariance matrix class (
1.1
), we work under the following assumption. Recall that
r(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})/\|\mathcal{C}\|
denotes the effective dimension.

Assumption 2.1
.

The data
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
consists of
N
independent copies of a real-valued, centered, square-integrable Gaussian process
u
on
D=[0,1]^{d}
with covariance function
k:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
and trace-class covariance operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D),
denoted
u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C}).
Moreover:

(i)

\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x)\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})
.

(ii)

There exists a positive and decreasing sequence
\{\nu_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
with
\nu_{1}=1
such that

∎

\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\{y:\|x-y\|>mr(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}\}}|k(x,y)|dy\lesssim\|\mathcal{C}\|\nu_{m},\qquad\forall\ m\in\mathbb{N}.

Remark 2.2
.

Notice that it always holds that
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})\leq\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x),
since

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C})}\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}=\int_{D}k(x,x)dx\leq\Big(\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x)\Big)\mathrm{Vol}(D)=\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x).

Assumption
2.1
(i) is satisfied when the reverse inequality also holds, in which case the marginal variance varies moderately across the domain
D
. Our analysis will show that this requirement plays a crucial role in linking global covariance estimation to local estimates.
The condition in Assumption
2.1
(ii) resembles that in the finite-dimensional counterpart (
1.1
), which restricts attention to the case
\nu_{m}=m^{-\alpha}
. As discussed in Section
2.3
, the quantity
r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}
in our assumption can be viewed as a natural
lengthscale
in the interesting regime where
r(\mathcal{C})\gg 1.
In that setting, the sequence
\{\nu_{m}\}
controls the tail decay of the covariance function. ∎

For a tunable parameter
\kappa>0,
we define the tapering estimator as

\widehat{k}_{\kappa}(x,y):=\widehat{k}(x,y)f_{\kappa}(x,y),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}_{\kappa}(\cdot,y)\psi(y)\,dy,\ \ \psi\in L^{2}(D),

where the tapering function
f_{\kappa}
is defined as

(2.1)

\displaystyle f_{\kappa}(x,y):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{(2\kappa-|x_{i}-y_{i}|)_{+}}{\kappa},1}}\right\}.

Remark 2.3
.

When
d=1
, the tapering function in (
2.1
) becomes

f_{\kappa}(x,y)=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{(2\kappa-|x-y|)_{+}}{\kappa},1}}\right\},

which is identical to the tapering function used in the matrix setting
[
19
]
. Therefore,
f_{\kappa}(x,y)
in (
2.1
) can be seen as a generalization of the tapering function in
[
19
]
to high physical dimension
d>1
. ∎

We next define two important quantities,
m_{*}
and
\varepsilon_{*},
that will be respectively used to specify the tapering radius
\kappa
and to control the estimation error.

Definition 2.4
.

Define the pair
m_{*}=m_{*}(\{\nu_{m}\},N,d)
and
\varepsilon_{*}=\varepsilon_{*}(\{\nu_{m}\},N,d)
as

\displaystyle m_{*}:=\min\bigg\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\nu_{m}\leq\sqrt{\frac{m^{d}}{N}}\bigg\},\qquad\varepsilon_{*}:=\max_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg\{\nu_{m}\land\sqrt{\frac{m^{d}}{N}}\bigg\}.

The quantity
m_{*}
is essentially the solution to the maximization problem in the
definition of
\varepsilon_{*}
, which can be conceptualized as the correct truncation order. This relationship is formalized in Lemma
A.3
. Similar quantities arise in various statistical problems, e.g. estimation and testing in sequence models
[
62
,
8
]
. In nonparametric regression and density estimation, the rate for Sobolev spaces with smoothness
\alpha
is
\varepsilon_{*}\asymp N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}
since
\nu_{m}\asymp m^{-\alpha}
, corresponding to the decay rate of the function’s coefficients when decomposed along some orthonormal basis.

Notice that if
m_{*}=1
, then
N=1
by Definition
2.4
, which is the trivial case. For simplicity, we assume
m_{*}\geq 2
throughout the paper.
We are now ready to state our first main result, which provides an upper bound on the estimation error.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds and set
\kappa:=m_{*}r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}.
Then,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\lesssim\varepsilon_{*}+\biggl(\sqrt{\frac{\log r(\mathcal{C})}{N}}\lor\frac{\log r(\mathcal{C})}{N}\biggr).

Example 2.6
.

If
\nu_{m}=m^{-\alpha}
, then
m_{*}\asymp N^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+d}}
and
\varepsilon_{*}\asymp N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}
; if
\nu_{m}=e^{-m^{t}}
, then
m_{*}\asymp(\log N)^{1/t}
and
\varepsilon_{*}\asymp\sqrt{(\log N)^{d/t}/N}
.

To our knowledge, Theorem
2.5
gives the first upper bound in the literature for tapering estimators under the natural bandedness Assumption
2.1
.
We establish a matching minimax lower bound in Theorem
2.8
, thus showing that tapering estimators attain the minimax optimal rate.

The upper bound in Theorem
2.5
is analogous to the matrix result in
[
19
]
; see also (
1.3
).
In particular, our choice of tapering radius
\kappa=m_{*}r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}
depends on
\{\nu_{m}\}
and on
r(\mathcal{C});
this is analogous to the dependence of
\kappa
on
\alpha
and
p
in
[
19
]
.
However, several key differences arise in our setting. First, our bound is
dimension-free
in the sense of Subsection
1.3
, and is stated in terms of
relative error
. Second, discretizing a covariance operator that satisfies Assumption
2.1
yields a banded covariance matrix only in physical dimension
d=1.
As a result, the analysis of tapering estimators for banded covariance matrices in
[
19
]
does not directly extend to our setting.

The proof of Theorem
2.5
contains several novel ideas that we now outline. To streamline the flow of the paper, we defer the full proof to Appendix
A.1
.
The argument begins with a bias-variance trade-off analysis. The main technical challenge lies in the variance analysis: specifically, reducing global estimates of the variance to
dimension-free
local estimates. A crucial observation is the following representation of the tapering function:

f_{\kappa}(x,y)=\kappa^{-d}\sum_{\sigma\in\{1,2\}^{d}}(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\sigma_{i}\kappa-|x_{i}-y_{i}|)_{+},

shown in Lemma
A.1
. This expression reveals that the tapering function is a sum of separable product terms of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\sigma_{i}\kappa-|x_{i}-y_{i}|)_{+}
. Moreover, each such product can be expressed as a mixture of indicator functions:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\sigma_{i}\kappa-|x_{i}-y_{i}|)_{+}=\int_{\bar{D}}\mathbf{1}\bigl\{x,y\in T(\theta)\bigr\}\,d\theta,

where
T(\theta):=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}[\theta_{i}-\frac{\sigma_{i}\kappa}{2},\theta_{i}+\frac{\sigma_{i}\kappa}{2}]
is a small box centered at
\theta
, and
\bar{D}
denotes a slightly enlarged version of the domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
. This representation enables a reduction from global to local variance analysis.
To transition from the continuous uniform mixture distribution over
\theta
to
disjoint local boxes, we introduce a measure
Q
under which the centers
\theta
are well separated (see Lemma
A.5
). We then apply a
change of measure
argument to complete the reduction step. For the analysis of the local variance, we apply dimension-free concentration bounds for sample covariance operators from
[
49
]
. To lift the resulting local estimates to local bounds we use Lemma
A.6
, which states that the operator norm of a sum of local covariance operators with disjoint supports equals the maximum of their individual operator norms. Finally, the parameter
\kappa
is chosen to optimally balance the bias and variance terms.

Remark 2.7
.

In the covariance matrix literature,
[
17
,
18
]
introduced a block-thresholding approach that adapts to
\alpha.
In our infinite-dimensional setting, adaptation to
\alpha,
or, more generally, to the decay rate of the sequence
\{\nu_{m}\},
is an interesting direction for future work. The papers
[
65
, Lemma 2.1]
(see also
[
3
, Lemma B.8]
) study estimation of
r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}
up to a multiplicative constant.

2.2
Lower bound

In this subsection, we formulate and prove a matching lower bound for the estimation of banded covariance operators. Consider, as in Assumption
2.1
, the banded class

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(r,\{\nu_{m}\})

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{\mathcal{C}:\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x)\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C}),r(\mathcal{C})\leq r,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\{y:\|x-y\|>mr(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}\}}|k(x,y)|dy\lesssim\|\mathcal{C}\|\nu_{m},\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\}.

The following theorem establishes a minimax lower bound over this class.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose
N>\log r>0
. The minimax risk for estimating the covariance operator over
\mathcal{F}(r,\{\nu_{m}\})
under the operator norm satisfies

\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}(r,\{\nu_{m}\})}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\gtrsim\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\varepsilon_{*}+\sqrt{\frac{\log r}{N}},\sqrt{\frac{r}{N}}}}\right\}.

The next proposition provides a general technique to reduce the
infinite-dimensional
operator estimation problem to a
finite-dimensional
matrix estimation problem. This proposition, proved in Appendix
A.2
, will be used to establish lower bounds for both banded and sparse covariance operators.

Proposition 2.9

(Lower bound reduction)
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{M}
be a uniform partition of
D=[0,1]^{d}
with
\mathrm{Vol}(I_{i})=\frac{1}{M}
. Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be a subset of positive semi-definite matrices. For every
\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}
, define

(c)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|\geq\frac{1}{M}\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|

where

is taken over kernel
integral operators

is a measurable function of

.

Proposition

formalizes the intuition that the infinite-dimensional covariance operator estimation problem is at least as hard as the finite-dimensional covariance matrix estimation problem. This proposition facilitates using existing information-theoretic lower bounds for high-dimensional covariance matrix estimation to prove lower bounds for operator estimation with relatively few modifications. 2.3
Lengthscale, effective dimension, and kernel decay

Our upper bound in Theorem
2.5
utilizes the tapering parameter
\kappa=m_{*}r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d},
while our matching lower bound in Theorem
2.8
demonstrates the fundamental role that
the effective dimension
r(\mathcal{C})
and the decay sequence
\{\nu_{m}\}
play in the estimation problem. In this section, we provide further intuition on the key quantities
r(\mathcal{C})
and
\{\nu_{m}\}
by relating them to the correlation lengthscale and to the tail decay of the kernel. First, we formalize the notion of correlation lengthscale by means of the following assumption, which, while restrictive, is often invoked in applications
[
79
,
90
]
.

Assumption 2.10
.

The kernel
k:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

(i)

k=k_{\lambda}
depends on a correlation lengthscale parameter
\lambda>0,
so that
k_{\lambda}(x,y)=\mathsf{K}(\|x-y\|/\lambda)
for an isotropic base kernel
\mathsf{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathsf{k}(x,y)=\mathsf{K}(\|x-y\|).

(ii)

The base kernel
\mathsf{k}
is positive, so that
\mathsf{k}(x,y)=\mathsf{K}(\|x-y\|)>0
. Further,
\mathsf{K}(r)
is differentiable, strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, and satisfies
\lim_{r\to\infty}\mathsf{K}(r)=0
.

Example 2.11
.

Many widely used families of kernels are parameterized by a lengthscale parameter, including the squared exponential
k^{\text{SE}}
and Matérn
k^{\text{Ma}}
covariance models
[
90
,
79
]

(2.5)

\displaystyle k^{\text{SE}}_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}}}\right),

(2.6)

\displaystyle k^{\text{Ma}}_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\zeta)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\zeta}}{\lambda}\|x-y\|}}\right)^{\zeta}K_{\zeta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\zeta}}{\lambda}\|x-y\|}}\right),\qquad\zeta>\frac{d-1}{2}\lor\frac{1}{2}.

Here,
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function,
K_{\zeta}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and
\zeta
controls the smoothness of sample paths in the Matérn model. In these and other examples, the lengthscale
\lambda
parameterizes the covariance function, and can be heuristically interpreted as the largest distance in physical space at which correlations are significant. Lengthscale parameters are also used to define covariance operators directly. For instance, in the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) approach
[
57
]
, Matérn-type Gaussian processes are defined by, see e.g. equation (2.4) in
[
77
]
,

(2.7)

u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C}),\quad\mathcal{C}=\tau^{2s-d}(\tau^{2}I-\mathcal{L})^{-s},

where
\tau=\lambda^{-1}
represents an inverse lengthscale,
\tau^{2s-d}
is a normalizing constant to ensure
\rm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})\asymp 1
as
\lambda\to 0,
and
s
is a smoothness parameter. In contrast to the setting in Assumption
2.10
, processes defined through the SPDE approach are typically nonstationary (and hence nonisotropic) due to boundary conditions and spatially-varying coefficients in the elliptic operator
-\mathcal{L}
. This perspective suggests via Karhunen–Loéve expansion
[
70
]
another interpretation of
\lambda
as determining the number of eigendirections that have significant variance, and thus the effective number of frequencies that are superimposed in sample paths from the process.

The following result shows that in the small lengthscale regime where Assumption
2.10
holds and
\lambda
is small, we have that
r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}\asymp\lambda.

This has several important implications. First, it motivates the condition in Assumption
2.1
(ii), where the domain of integration is then simply determined by the correlation lengthscale. Second, it intuitively explains the choice of tapering parameter
\kappa\asymp m_{*}r(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}\asymp m_{*}\lambda,
whose size is determined by the correlation lengthscale. Third, it allows us to clearly contrast the performance of the tapering and sample covariance estimators in the small lengthscale regime, as summarized in the following corollary:

Corollary 2.12
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.10
hold.
There exists a universal constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that, for any
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
, the
covariance operator satisfies
r(\mathcal{C})\asymp\lambda^{-d}.
Consequently, the sample covariance estimator and tapering estimator with
\kappa=m_{*}\lambda
satisfy

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}}\lor\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\kappa}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{*}+\biggl(\sqrt{\frac{\log(\lambda^{-d})}{N}}\lor\frac{\log(\lambda^{-d})}{N}\biggr).

In particular, if
\nu_{m}=m^{-\alpha}
then
\varepsilon_{*}\asymp N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}},
and if
\nu_{m}\asymp e^{-m^{t}}
then
\varepsilon_{*}\asymp\sqrt{(\log N)^{d/t}/N}.

Proof.

By
[
4
, Theorem 2.8]
, for any covariance operator
\mathcal{C}
with covariance function
k
satisfying Assumption
2.10
it holds that, for small
\lambda,

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})=1
,
\|\mathcal{C}\|\asymp\lambda^{d},
and
r(\mathcal{C})\asymp\lambda^{-d}.
The bound (
2.8
) follows directly by
[
4
, Theorem 2.8]
. The bound (
2.9
) follows by plugging the values of

into the bound derived in Theorem
2.5
.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

While we choose to work under Assumption

for exposition purposes, the scaling

∎

We conclude this section with a lemma which demonstrates that, in the small lengthscale regime, the sequence

\displaystyle\nu_{m}=c\int_{m}^{\infty}r^{d-1}\mathsf{K}(r)dr,\qquad m=1,2,\dots,

Proof.

As in the proof of Corollary

Therefore, to characterize the sequence

in Assumption

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\{y:\|x-y\|>mr(\mathcal{C})^{-1/d}\}}k_{\lambda}(x,y)\,dy=\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\{y:\|x-y\|>m\lambda\}}\mathsf{K}(\|x-y\|/\lambda)\,dy

\displaystyle\hskip 108.12054pt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{K}(\|y\|/\lambda){\bf{1}}_{\{\|y\|>m\lambda\}}(y)\,dy=\lambda^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{K}(\|y\|){\bf{1}}_{\{\|y\|>m\}}(y)\,dy,

For example, Lemma

3
Estimating sparse covariance operators

3.1
Upper bound

For a Gaussian process on
D=[0,1]^{d}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
with covariance function
k
, we define

\|k\|^{q}_{q}:=\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}|k(x,y)|^{q}dy,\qquad\|k\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x,y\in D}|k(x,y)|.

In this section, we invoke an approximate sparsity assumption that will be formalized through the following notions of
L^{q}
-sparsity
and
capacity
of a covariance operator.

Definition 3.1
.

The
L^{q}
-sparsity
for
q\in[0,1]
and
capacity
of a covariance operator
\mathcal{C}
with kernel
k
are defined respectively as

\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C}):=\frac{\|k\|_{q}^{q}\|k\|_{\infty}^{1-q}}{\|\mathcal{C}\|},\qquad\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C}):=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C})}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{\|k\|_{\infty}}}.

Notice that both
\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})
and
\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})
are
dimension free
and
scale invariant
. The following lemma, proved in Appendix
B
, shows that, similar to
r(\mathcal{C}),

\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})
is bounded below by
1.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
q\in[0,1]
, it holds that
\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})\geq\Gamma_{1}(1,\mathcal{C})\geq 1
.

Remark 3.3
.

The capacity
\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})
is closely related to the notion of
stable dimension
. Recall that for a compact set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})
, the stable dimension of
T

[
85
, Section 7]
is the squared ratio of its
Gaussian width
to its
radius
,

d(T):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\,\langle g,t\rangle}{\sup_{t\in T}\|t\|}}}\right)^{2}.

The squared version of
\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})
admits the analogous form
[
4
, Proposition 3.1]

\Gamma^{2}_{2}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C})}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}}\right)^{2}}{\|k\|_{\infty}}\asymp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\langle f,u\rangle}{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{\psi_{2}}}}}\right)^{2},

where
\mathcal{F}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\ell_{x}}}\right\}_{x\in D}
denotes the family of evaluation functionals, i.e.
\langle\ell_{x},u\rangle=u(x)
, and
\psi_{2}
denotes the Orlicz norm with Orlicz function
\psi(x)=e^{x^{2}}-1,
see e.g.
[
85
, Definition 2.5.6]
. Comparing
\Gamma_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{C})
with
d(T)
, it is clear that
\Gamma_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{C})
naturally generalizes the stable dimension as it characterizes the complexity for more general and abstract Gaussian processes as opposed to the canonical Gaussian process on
T
, i.e.
\langle g,t\rangle
. The capacity
\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})
is rooted in deep chaining results
[
64
, Theorem 1.13]
(see also
[
63
]
). The paper
[
49
]
used these empirical process results to obtain dimension-free bounds for the sample covariance operator
[
49
, Theorem 4]
. Subsequently,
[
5
,
4
,
6
]
used similar techniques to obtain dimension-free bounds for various thresholding matrix and operator estimators.
∎

Assumption 3.4
.

The data
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
consists of
N
independent copies of a real-valued, centered Gaussian process
u\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C})
that is Lebesgue-almost everywhere continuous on
D=[0,1]^{d}
with probability 1.
Moreover:

(i)

There exists a constant
C_{0}>1
such that
\Gamma_{1}(0,\mathcal{C})\exp(-C_{0}\Gamma_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{C}))\leq 1.

(ii)

The sample size satisfies

\sqrt{N}\geq\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.

For a tunable parameter
\rho>0,
we define the thresholding estimator as

\widehat{k}_{\rho}(x,y):=\widehat{k}(x,y){\bf{1}}_{\{|\widehat{k}(x,y)|\geq\rho\}}(x,y),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}_{\rho}(\cdot,y)\psi(y)\,dy,\ \ \psi\in L^{2}(D).

The upper bound achieved by this estimator is similar to
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
, but now written in terms of
\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})
and
\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C}),
the two quantities that determine the minimax complexity in the new lower bound in Section
3.2
. For completeness, we include a proof in Appendix
B

Theorem 3.5
.

Under Assumption
3.4
, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
\frac{C_{0}}{c}\leq c_{0}\leq\sqrt{N}
and set

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}:=\frac{c_{0}\sqrt{\|k\|_{\infty}}}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big).

Then,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\lesssim\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})}{\sqrt{N}}}}\right)^{1-q}.

Lower bound

Here, we prove a matching lower bound for covariance operator estimation over the approximate sparsity class

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma_{1}(q),\Gamma_{2}}}\right):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{C}:\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})\leq\Gamma_{1}(q),\ \Gamma_{2}(\mathcal{C})\leq\Gamma_{2}}}\right\}.

Theorem 3.6

Let

for some

,
\Gamma_{2}\geq 2
, and

in the operator norm satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}(\Gamma_{1}(q),\Gamma_{2})}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\gtrsim\Gamma_{1}(q)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}}}\right)^{1-q}.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

We mirror the high-dimensional analysis in
[

]
to construct a finite subclass of covariance operators

with

and
\text{diam}(I_{i})\asymp(r+1)^{-1/d}.
Let
r^{*}=\lfloor r/2\rfloor
and define
\Lambda

3.3
Lengthscale, sparsity, and capacity

As in the banded setting, the performance of the thresholding estimator can be studied in the small lengthscale regime by characterizing the scaling of the
L^{q}
-sparsity and the capacity. This analysis was developed in
[
4
, Section 2.2]
, and here we summarize the main result to provide a comparison with the banded case considered in Section
2.3
. Specifically, the following corollary is an analog of Corollary
2.12
. Similarly to the banded setting, the thresholding estimator has sample complexity that improves exponentially on that of the sample covariance. Notice that here the thresholding parameter is chosen through an empirical approximation to the expected supremum, providing an estimator that naturally adapts to the lengthscale.

Corollary 3.7

(
[
4
, Theorem 2.8]
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.10
and
3.4
hold. There is a universal constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that, for
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
and
N\gtrsim\log(\lambda^{-d})
, the covariance operator satisfies for any
q\in(0,1]
that
r(\mathcal{C})\asymp\lambda^{-d},

\Gamma_{1}(q,\mathcal{C})\asymp 1,
and
\Gamma_{2}(q,\mathcal{C})\asymp\log(\lambda^{-d})
. Consequently, the sample covariance estimator and thresholding estimator with

\widehat{\rho}:=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big)

satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}}\lor\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\,c(q)\biggl(\frac{\log(\lambda^{-d})}{N}\biggr)^{\frac{1-q}{2}},

where

is an absolute constant and

Proof.

The result follows directly by characterizing the bound in Theorem
3.5
in terms of the lengthscale parameter (see the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 2.8]
). In addition to the characterizations of
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})
and
\|\mathcal{C}\|
in Corollary

. We note that under Assumption

,
\|k\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in D}k(x,x)=\mathsf{K}(0)=1.

∎

Remark 3.8
.

Corollary
3.7
implies rate
N^{(q-1)/2}
for
q>0.
In contrast, Corollary
2.12
shows that tapering estimators can achieve rate
N^{-1/2}
up to a logarithmic factor, provided that the kernel has fast tail decay (e.g. for squared exponential kernels). Assumption
2.10
imposes a form of
ordered sparsity
in that the decay of the covariance function depends monotonically on the physical distance between its two arguments. On the other hand, the sparsity Assumption
3.4
imposes no ordering. We can therefore think of covariance operators satisfying Assumption

as fitting more naturally into the class of banded operators captured by Assumption
2.1
. Corollary
3.7
demonstrates that, while more broadly useful, thresholding estimators can still perform well in the ordered setting. In Section
4
, we compare the performance of tapering and thresholding estimators on ordered sparse covariance operators, and show that while both do well, the tapering estimators have a clear advantage as they utilize the additional structure provided by the ordered decay. In contrast, when ordered sparsity is not present, the numerical experiments demonstrate that thresholding still does well while the tapering estimator fails. ∎

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have established optimal convergence rates for estimation of banded and
L^{q}
-sparse covariance operators. To do so, we leveraged techniques from high-dimensional covariance matrix estimation while also addressing new challenges that emerge in the infinite-dimensional setting. Several questions stem from this work.

1.

Estimating covariance operators with non-Gaussian data (e.g., log-concave
[
2
]
and heavy-tailed distributions
[
1
]
) is an interesting future direction. There is a rapidly growing body of literature on understanding the statistical and computational complexity of such tasks in the high-dimensional setting
[
82
,
89
,
42
,
22
]
, as well as on closely related robust covariance estimation
[
21
,
65
,
28
]
.

2.

An important open direction is to investigate structured covariance operator estimation under other norms, beyond the operator norm we considered. For the sample covariance and in the unstructured setting,
[
71
]
studied covariance estimation in Hilbert-Schmidt norm. In contrast to the classical matrix estimation problem, the infinite dimensional operator estimation problem lends itself naturally to study covariance estimation under norms that may account for the smoothness of the Gaussian process data.

3.

Another direction for future work concerns operator estimation under other structural assumptions. A natural question is estimation of Toeplitz covariance operators building on existing matrix theory
[
15
,
45
]
, which may be of particular interest in time series analysis and in stationary spatial statistics.
Other covariance classes with broad applications include low-rank covariance in kriging for large spatial datasets
[
24
,
87
]
, and Kronecker-structured covariance models for multi-way and tensor-valued data
[
88
,
72
]
.

4.

Finally, we conjecture that it could be possible to establish a tight connection between covariance operator estimation and existing Fourier analysis techniques for the study of kernel density estimators, see e.g.
[
83
, Section 1.3]
. Such a connection may help intuitively explain the emergence of the nonparametric rate
N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}
in banded matrix and operator estimation.

Lemma A.5
.

There are two constants
\kappa_{0}
and

depending only on

such that the following holds. For any
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{0}
and

, define

Q(d\theta_{1},d\theta_{2},\ldots,d\theta_{S})=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}\mathbf{1}\{\theta_{i}\in\bar{D}\}d\theta_{i},

. Then,

(a)

Q
is a probability measure over
(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}
.

(b)

Let
Q_{0}
denote the marginal probability density of
Q
. It holds that
\inf_{\theta\in\bar{D}}Q_{0}(\theta)\gtrsim 1.

Proof.

(a) First, the normalization constant is finite, since

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}

Second, we prove that
Z
has a strictly positive lower bound. For any
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{S}\subseteq\bar{D}
,

(A.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{D}\backslash\cup_{i=1}^{S}B(\theta_{i},2\kappa)}}\right)\geq 1-S\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(B(0,2\kappa)\bigr)\geq 1-c_{0}\kappa^{-d}C^{\prime}\kappa^{d}=1-C^{\prime}c_{0},

where

is a constant depending only on

. Therefore, if

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bar{D}}\hskip-4.26773pt\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\bar{D}\backslash\cup_{i=1}^{S-2}B(\theta_{i},2\kappa)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\bar{D}\backslash\cup_{i=1}^{S-1}B(\theta_{i},2\kappa)}1d\theta_{S}}}\right)d\theta_{S-1}}}\right)\cdots d\theta_{1}\geq(1-C^{\prime}c_{0})^{S}>0.

is a well-defined probability measure over

(b)

by establishing the following three steps:

Step 1:

.

The conclusion follows directly from these three steps:

Q_{0}(a)\gtrsim\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=2}^{S}\sim P}\bigg(\sum_{i=2}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}}=0\bigg)\gtrsim\sum_{t=0}^{C}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=2}^{S}\sim P}\bigg(\sum_{i=2}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}}=t\bigg)=1.

Proof of Step 1:

By definition,

\displaystyle Q_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes(S-1)}}Q(a,\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{S})\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes{(S-1)}}}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-a\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes{(S-1)}}}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}^{c}}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes{(S-1)}}}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}^{c}}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{2\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}{\int_{(\bar{D})^{\otimes S}}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq S}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|>2\kappa}}\right\}\prod_{1\leq i\leq S}d\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(\bar{D})}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=2}^{S}\sim P}\bigg(\prod_{i=2}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}^{c}}=1\bigg)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(\bar{D})}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=2}^{S}\sim P}\bigg(\sum_{i=2}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}}=0\bigg).

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{S}
be a set of kernel integral operators on
L^{2}(D)
with kernel functions
\{k_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)\}_{\ell=1}^{S}
. For
1\leq\ell\leq S
, we denote the support of
k_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
by
T_{\ell}\times T_{\ell}
. If
\{T_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{S}\subseteq D
are disjoint, then

\Big\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\Big\|=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|.

Proof.

For any
\psi\in L^{2}(D)
with
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\bigg)\psi\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\int_{D}\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}k_{\ell}(x,y)\psi(y)dy\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}=\bigg\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\int_{D}k_{\ell}(x,y)\psi(y)dy\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\bigg\|\int_{D}k_{\ell}(x,y)\psi(y)dy\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\overset{\text{(ii)}}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\bigg\|\int_{T_{\ell}}k_{\ell}(x,y)\psi(y)dy\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|^{2}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(T_{\ell})}^{2}\leq\bigg(\max_{1\leq\ell\leq S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(T_{\ell})}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(iii)}}{\leq}\max_{1\leq\ell\leq S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|^{2},

where (i) follows from the fact that that
\big\{\int_{D}k_{\ell}(\cdot,y)\psi(y)dy\big\}_{\ell=1}^{S}
have disjoint supports, (ii) follows by
k_{\ell}(x,y)=0
if
y\notin T_{\ell}
, and (iii) follows by
\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(T_{\ell})}^{2}\leq\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}=1
.

Therefore,
\Big\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{S}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\Big\|\leq\max_{1\leq\ell\leq S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|
. Let
\ell^{\prime}:=\arg\max_{1\leq\ell\leq S}\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\|
, by taking
\psi\in L^{2}(D)
with support
T_{\ell^{\prime}}
and satisfying
\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell^{\prime}}\psi\|=\|\mathcal{C}_{\ell^{\prime}}\|\|\psi\|
, we conclude that the equality holds.
∎

A.2
Banded covariance operators: Lower bound

Proof of Proposition
2.9
.

(a)
Observe that, for any
\psi\in L^{2}(D)
,

\langle\psi,\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}=\int_{D\times D}k_{\Sigma}(x,y)\psi(x)\psi(y)\,dxdy=\langle\bar{\psi},\Sigma\bar{\psi}\rangle\geq 0,

where
\bar{\psi}=(\bar{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\bar{\psi}_{i}=\int_{I_{i}}\psi(x)dx
. We see that the operator
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
is
positive semi-definite
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
is
trace-class
, since

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma})=\int_{D}k_{\Sigma}(x,x)\,dx=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ii}\mathrm{Vol}(I_{i})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ii}<\infty.

(b) For
\psi\in L^{2}(D)
with
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
,

\|\bar{\psi}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}(\bar{\psi}_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bigg(\int_{I_{i}}\psi(x)dx\bigg)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bigg(\int_{I_{i}}\psi(x)^{2}dx\bigg)\mathrm{Vol}(I_{i})=\frac{1}{M}.

We use an equivalent characterization of the operator norm to write

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\|=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1}\langle\psi,\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1}\langle\bar{\psi},\Sigma\bar{\psi}\rangle\leq\sup_{\|v\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle=\frac{1}{M}\|\Sigma\|.

On the other hand, for any unit vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we set
\psi_{v}(x)=\sqrt{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}v_{i}\mathbf{1}\{x\in I_{i}\}
. Observe that
\|\psi_{v}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
, so

\|\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\|=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1}\langle\psi,\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}\geq\sup_{\|v\|=1}\langle\psi_{v},\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\psi_{v}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\frac{1}{M}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle=\frac{1}{M}\|\Sigma\|,

as desired.

(c) We write

(A.15)

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|=\inf_{\widehat{k}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1}\Big\|\int_{D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{k}(\cdot,y)-k_{\Sigma}(\cdot,y)}}\right)\psi(y)dy\Big\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

For any unit vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we set
\psi_{v}(x)=\sqrt{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}v_{i}\mathbf{1}\{x\in I_{i}\}
. Observe that
\|\psi_{v}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
. We then restrict the supremum over all
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
in the lower bound (
A.15
) to be a supremum over functions of the form
\psi_{v}
, which yields

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|\geq\inf_{\widehat{k}}\hskip-2.84544pt\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|v\|=1}\Big\|\int_{D}\hskip-2.84544pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{k}(\cdot,y)-k_{\Sigma}(\cdot,y)}}\right)\sqrt{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}dy\Big\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

To simplify this expression, we observe the following:

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{k}(\cdot,y)-k_{\Sigma}(\cdot,y)}}\right)\sqrt{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}dy\Big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\hskip-2.84544pt\bigg(\int_{D}\bigg(\widehat{k}(x,y)-\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ij}\mathbf{1}\{x\in I_{i}\}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}\bigg)\sqrt{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}dy\bigg)^{2}\hskip-2.84544ptdx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{I_{i}}\bigg(\int_{D}\bigg(\widehat{k}(x,y)-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ij}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}\bigg)\sqrt{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\mathbf{1}\{y\in I_{j}\}dy\bigg)^{2}\hskip-2.84544ptdx

\displaystyle=M\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{I_{i}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\Big(\int_{I_{j}}\widehat{k}(x,y)dy\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ij}v_{j}\Big)\bigg)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{\geq}M\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(I_{i})}\bigg(\int_{I_{i}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\int_{I_{j}}\widehat{k}(x,y)dy\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ij}v_{j}\Big)dx\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=M^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bigg(\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}\int_{I_{i}\times I_{j}}\widehat{k}(x,y)dxdy\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{ij}v_{j}\Big)\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\|(\widehat{k}_{M}-\Sigma)v\|^{2},

where (i) follows by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and we have defined

in the last step. Consequently, we have that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|\geq\inf_{\widehat{k}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|v\|=1}\frac{1}{M}\|(\widehat{k}_{M}-\Sigma)v\|=\frac{1}{M}\inf_{\widehat{k}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{k}_{M}-\Sigma\|,

where
\inf_{\widehat{k}}
ranges over all measurable functions of
\{u_{n}(\cdot)\}_{n=1}^{N}
.

We note that the Gaussian random function
u_{n}(\cdot)\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma})
is almost surely piecewise constant and can be written as

\displaystyle u_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{ni}\mathbf{1}\{x\in I_{i}\},

where the
M
-dimensional vector

. Therefore,
\widehat{k}_{M}
is simply a function of the multivariate Gaussians

, which yields that

\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|\geq\frac{1}{M}\inf_{\widehat{k}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{k}_{M}-\Sigma\|=\frac{1}{M}\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|.

Combining the argument above with

in (b), we conclude that
