Asymptotic fluctuations in supercritical Crump-Mode-Jagers processes
Alexander Iksanovlabel=e1]iksan@univ.kiev.ua, Konrad Koleskolabel=e2]konrad.kolesko@math.uni.wroc.pl, Matthias Meinerslabel=e3]matthias.meiners@math.uni-giessen.de

Abstract
Consider a supercritical Crump–Mode–Jagers process ( 𝒵 t φ ) t ≥ 0 (\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi})_{t\geq 0} counted with a random characteristic φ \varphi . Nerman’s celebrated law of large numbers [ Z. Wahrsch. Verw. Gebiete 57, 365–395, 1981] states that, under some mild assumptions, e − α ​ t ​ 𝒵 t φ e^{-\alpha t}\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi} converges almost surely as t → ∞ t\to\infty to a ​ W aW . Here, α > 0 \alpha>0 is the Malthusian parameter, a a is a constant and W W is the limit of Nerman’s martingale, which is positive on the survival event. In this general situation, under additional (second moment) assumptions, we prove a central limit theorem for ( 𝒵 t φ ) t ≥ 0 (\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi})_{t\geq 0} . More precisely, we show that there exist a constant k ∈ ℕ 0 k\in\mathds{N}_{0} and a function H ⁡ ( t ) H(t) , a finite random linear combination of functions of the form t j ​ e λ ​ t t^{j}e^{\lambda t} with α / 2 ≤ Re ⁡ ( λ ) < α \alpha/2\leq\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)<\alpha , such that ( 𝒵 t φ − a ​ e α ​ t ​ W − H ⁡ ( t ) ) / t k ​ e α ​ t (\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}-ae^{\alpha t}W-H(t))/\sqrt{t^{k}e^{\alpha t}} converges in distribution to a normal random variable with random variance. This result unifies and extends various central limit theorem-type results for specific branching processes.

Introduction

A general (Crump-Mode-Jagers) branching process starts at time
0
with a single individual,
the ancestor, who is alive in the random time interval
[0,\zeta)
for a random variable
\zeta
,
the life span, taking values in
[0,\infty]
.
The ancestor produces offspring born at the points of a reproduction point process
\xi
on
[0,\infty)
.
No particular assumption about the dependence structure between
\xi
and
\zeta

is made.
For each individual
u
that is ever born there is an independent copy
(\xi_{u},\zeta_{u})
of the pair
(\xi,\zeta)

that determines the birth times of the individual’s offspring relative to
u
’s time of birth
and its life span.

The general branching process encompasses e.g. the
Bienaymé-Galton-Watson process,
the Yule process,
the continuous-time Markov branching process,
the Sevastyanov process,
and the Bellman-Harris process.
We refer to
[
25
]
for a more detailed account of the history of the general branching process
and its predecessors.

The general branching process counted with a random characteristic at time
t

is the sum over all individuals ever born where the contribution
of each individual to the sum is determined by some random characteristic that may take into account
all aspects of the individual’s life such as its age at time
t
, its life span, etc.
This formulation makes it possible to treat at one go various quantities of interest derived from the general branching process
such as the number of births up to time
t
, the number of individuals alive at time
t
,
the number of individuals alive at time
t
which are
younger than a given threshold
a>0
, etc.
A formal description of the model will be given in Section
2
.

General branching processes serve as models of
biological populations such as humans, cells or plants

[
17
,
25
,
34
,
41
]
,
as models for tumor growth
[
15
,
34
]
,
but also for neutron chain reactions
[
3
]

or fragmentation
[
31
]
(after a change of time) to name but a few.
The general branching process is also an important tool within related fields of applied probability or theoretical computer science.
In fact, its applications in these fields are numerous and any attempt to give a complete survey here is hopeless.
We confine ourselves to mentioning its successful application
in the study of asymptotic properties of random graph growth models driven by preferential attachment dynamics

[
6
,
9
,
37
,
45
]

and particularly random tree growth models
[
14
,
22
,
23
,
32
,
35
,
42
]
.
It is also used as an approximation for epidemic models

[
10
,
47
]

and as a model of the initial phase of epidemics such as SARS, Ebola and SARS-CoV-2

[
8
,
11
,
12
]
,
during which the disease spreads exponentially fast
but the impact of population structure and preventive measures is
still small
[
47
]
.

The laws of large numbers of the supercritical general branching process
counted with a random characteristic are due to Nerman
[
38
,
39
]
in the single-type, non-lattice case,
that is, when the reproduction point process is not concentrated on any lattice.
There were earlier results for special cases, but here we refrain from sketching the history
and instead refer to the introduction of
[
39
]
.
The lattice version of Nerman’s law of large numbers was proved
by Gatzouras
[
16
]
.

In view of the relevance of the general branching process in applications
and the fact that the laws of large numbers date back as far as 1981, it is remarkable, and rather
surprising,
that there is no comprehensive
central limit theorem for the general process counted with a random characteristic in the literature.
However, there are results for related models indicating the intricate nature of the fluctuations that can occur.
For the multi-type continuous-time Markov branching process with finite type space
where individuals give birth only at the time of their death
Athreya
[
4
,
5
]
proved a central limit theorem
and Janson
[
29
]
proved a functional central limit theorem.
Asmussen and Hering
[
3
, Section VIII.3]
provide
results for the asymptotic fluctuations of multi-type Markov branching processes
with rather general type space. In principle, these results contain the single-type case
of the general branching process
since such a process can be seen as a Markov process
in which the type of an individual at time
t
is its entire life history up to time
t
.
However, this type space is large, and the assumptions of
[
3
]

are typically not satisfied except in special cases such as the case of the Galton-Watson process.
Recently, Janson studied the asymptotic fluctuations of single-type supercritical general branching processes
in the lattice case
[
30
]
.
For the non-lattice case, there is a second-order result by Janson and Neininger
[
31
]

for Kolmogorov’s conservative fragmentation model that may be translated into the language
of general branching processes. It gives a central limit theorem
for the number of individuals born up to time
t
,
but it requires that the offspring variable
N\vcentcolon=\xi([0,\infty))
be bounded
and the additional assumption that
\int e^{-x}\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=1
almost surely,
a rather restrictive assumption in the context of general branching processes.
Another related work is the paper by Charmoy, Croydon, and Hambly
[
13
]
,
where the authors investigate the fluctuations of the eigenvalue counting function related to certain random fractals.
This problem can be addressed using limit theorems for specific Crump-Mode-Jagers processes.
The random characteristics in this model are no longer assumed to be independent,
which takes it beyond the scope of the present paper.
It is worth noting that limit theorems for general branching processes were previously explored
by Jagers and Nerman
[
27
]
.
However, the conditions in this paper can be challenging to verify, even for relatively simple characteristics.
Another related result is the central limit theorem for Nerman’s martingale
[
24
]
.

In the present paper, we close the gap in the literature and present
a central limit theorem for the general branching process counted with a random characteristic.
Our main result, Theorem
2.15
, contains and extends all results for single-type processes
summarized above. A non-exhaustive list of applications given in Section
3

contains Galton-Watson processes, Nerman’s martingale and its complex-valued counterparts,
epidemic models, Crump-Mode-Jagers processes with homogeneous Poisson offspring process and general lifetimes,
and conservative fragmentation models.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we formally introduce the general branching process
counted with a random characteristic. We further state and discuss the assumptions we are working with.
In Section
2.3
, we state the main result, Theorem
2.15
, and its corollaries.
We then apply our general results to some specific models in Section
3
.
Section
4
contains some preliminaries for the proofs.
Nerman’s martingale and further related martingales play a crucial role in our theory.
All these martingales are introduced and discussed in Section
5
.
Section
6
is devoted to proving our main result, Theorem
2.15
.
Our central limit theorem is based on an asymptotic expansion
of the mean of a general branching process counted with a random characteristic.
Such asymptotic expansions are derived in Section
7
.
We close the paper with Section
8
, in which possible future research directions are outlined.

2
Setup, preliminaries and main results

We continue with a formal description of the general branching process.

2.1
The general branching process counted with a random characteristic

We introduce the general (Crump-Mode-Jagers) branching process following Jagers
[
25
,
26
]
.
The process starts with a single individual, the ancestor, born at time
0
.
The ancestor produces offspring born at the points of a reproduction point process

\xi=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{j}}
on
[0,\infty)

where
N=\xi([0,\infty))
takes values in
\mathds{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
with
\mathds{N}_{0}\vcentcolon=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}

and
X_{j}\vcentcolon=\inf\{t\geq 0:\xi([0,t])\geq j\}
.
Here and throughout the paper, the infimum of the empty set is defined to be
\infty
.
The ancestor has a random lifetime
\zeta
, which may be dependent on

\xi
.
Formally,
\zeta
is a random variable assuming values in
[0,\infty]
.

Individuals are indexed by
u\in\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\mathds{N}^{n}
according to their genealogy.
Here,
\mathds{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathds{N}^{0}\vcentcolon=\{\varnothing\}
is the singleton set containing only the empty tuple
\varnothing
.
We use the usual Ulam-Harris notation.
We abbreviate a tuple
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathds{N}^{n}
by
u_{1}\ldots u_{n}

and refer to
n
as the length or generation of
u
; we write
|u|=n
.
In this context, any
u=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
is called (potential) individual.
Its ancestral line is encoded by

\varnothing\to u_{1}\to u_{1}u_{2}\to\ldots\to u_{1}\ldots u_{n}=u

where
u_{1}
is the
u_{1}^{\mathrm{th}}
child of the ancestor,
u_{1}u_{2}
the
u_{2}^{\mathrm{th}}
child of
u_{1}
, etc.
If
v=v_{1}\ldots v_{m}\in\mathcal{I}
, then
uv
is short for
u_{1}\ldots u_{n}v_{1}\ldots v_{m}
.
For
u\in\mathcal{I}
and
i\in\mathds{N}
, the individuals
ui
will be called children of
u
.
Conversely,
u
will be called parent of
ui
.
More generally,

w
will be called descendant of
u
(short:
u\preceq w
)
iff
uv=w
for some
v\in\mathcal{I}
.
Conversely,
u
will be called an ancestor/progenitor of
w
.
We write
u\prec w
if
u\preceq w
and
u\not=w
.
Often, we shall refer to
\mathds{N}^{n}
as the (potential)
n^{\mathrm{th}}
generation (
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
).
With these notations, we have

|u|=n\quad\text{iff}\quad u\in\mathds{N}^{n}\quad\text{iff}\quad u\text{ is an $n^{\mathrm{th}}$ generation (potential) individual}.

For
u=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}\in\mathds{N}^{n}
and
k\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,
let
u|_{k}
denote the ancestor of
u
in the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
generation.
Formally,
u|_{k}
is the restriction of the vector
u
to its first
k
components:

u|_{k}=\begin{cases}\varnothing&\text{if $k=0$},\\
u_{1}\ldots u_{k}&\text{if $1\leq k\leq|u|$},\\
u&\text{if $k>|u|$}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

For typographical reasons, we may sometimes write
v|k
instead of
v|_{k}
.
For
u\in\mathcal{I}
let
u\mathcal{I}
denote the subtree of
\mathcal{I}
emanating from
u
,
that is,

u\mathcal{I}\vcentcolon=\{uv:\,v\in\mathcal{I}\}=\{w\in\mathcal{I}:\,w|_{|u|}=u\}.

For each
u\in\mathcal{I}
there is an independent copy
(\xi_{u},\zeta_{u})

of the pair
(\xi,\zeta)
that determines the birth times of
u
’s offspring relative to its time of birth,
and the duration of its life.
Quantities derived from
(\xi_{u},\zeta_{u})
are indexed by
u
.
For instance,
N_{u}
is the number of offspring of
u
and

X_{u,k}
is the difference between the birth-time of the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
child of
u
and
u
itself, etc.
The birth-times
S(u)
for
u\in\mathcal{I}
are defined recursively.
We set
S(\varnothing)\vcentcolon=0
and, for
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,

S(uj)\vcentcolon=S(u)+X_{u,j}\quad\text{for }u\in\mathds{N}^{n}\text{ and }j\in\mathds{N}.

The family tree of all individuals ever born is denoted by
\mathcal{T}\vcentcolon=\{u\in\mathcal{I}:S(u)<\infty\}.

We call

\mathcal{S}\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{n\in\mathds{N}}\{\#(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathds{N}^{n})\geq 1\}

the survival set and its complement
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{c}}=\cup_{n\in\mathds{N}}\{\#(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathds{N}^{n})=0\}
the extinction set.
The time of death of individual
u
is
S(u)+\zeta_{u}
.
An individual
u
is alive at time
t\geq 0
if it is born, but not yet dead at time
t
,
i.e., if

S(u)\leq t<S(u)+\zeta_{u}.

We now construct the canonical space for the general branching process.
For
u\in\mathcal{I}
, let
(\Omega_{u},\mathcal{A}_{u},P_{u})
be a copy of a given probability space

(\Omega_{\varnothing},\mathcal{A}_{\varnothing},P_{\varnothing})
,
the
life space
of the ancestor.
An element
\omega\in\Omega_{u}
is a possible life career for individual
u

and any property of interest of
u
like its mass at some age
or its life span is viewed as a measurable function on the life space.
In particular,
\xi
and
\zeta
,
the reproduction point process and the life span, are measurable functions defined on
(\Omega_{\varnothing},\mathcal{A}_{\varnothing})
.

From the life space, we construct the population space:

\textstyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})\vcentcolon=\big(\bigtimes_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\Omega_{u},\bigotimes_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{A}_{u},\bigotimes_{u\in\mathcal{I}}P_{u}\big).

For
u\in\mathcal{I}
, we write
\pi_{u}
for the projection

\pi_{u}:\bigtimes_{v\in\mathcal{I}}\Omega_{v}\to\Omega_{u}

and
\theta_{u}
for the shift
\theta_{u}((\omega_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{I}})=(\omega_{uv})_{v\in\mathcal{I}}
.
To formally lift an entity
\chi
defined on the life space, i.e. a function
\chi
on
\Omega_{u}
,
to the population space,
we define
\chi_{u}\vcentcolon=\chi\circ\pi_{u}
.
In particular,
\xi_{u}=\xi\circ\pi_{u}
and
\zeta_{u}=\zeta\circ\pi_{u}
.
In slight abuse of notation,
if
\chi
is defined on the life space,
when working on the population space, we write
\chi
instead of
\chi\circ\pi_{\varnothing}
.
For instance, we sometimes write
\mathds{P}(\zeta\leq t)

for
P_{u}(\zeta\leq t)=\mathds{P}(\zeta_{\varnothing}\leq t)
. A technical remark is in order. Sometimes random variables independent of
\mathcal{F}
appear.
This means that when required, we work on a suitable extension of the space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
.

We are interested in the general branching process counted with a random characteristic.
A
random characteristic

\varphi
is a random process on
(\Omega_{\varnothing},\mathcal{A}_{\varnothing},P_{\varnothing})

taking values in the Skorokhod space of right-continuous functions
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}^{d}

with existing left limits at every point in
\mathds{R}
. Such functions are called càdlàg for short.
The characteristic
\varphi
may also be viewed as a stochastic process
\varphi:\Omega_{\varnothing}\times\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}^{d}
,

(\omega,t)\mapsto\varphi(\omega,t)
with right-continuous paths and existing left limits.
Notice that unlike in some important references
[
16
,
39
]
,
we allow, and actually need at some places, that
\varphi(t)\not=0
for some
t<0
.
It is known that such a process is product-measurable.
Define
\varphi_{u}=\varphi\circ\pi_{u}
. By product measurability,
\varphi_{u}(t-S(u))
is a random variable.
Note that, for given
u\in\mathcal{I}
,
\varphi_{u}
is independent of
S(u)
.
However,
\varphi_{u}
and
S(v)
can be dependent, when
u
is an ancestor of
v
. The general branching process counted with characteristic
\varphi

is
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}=(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi})_{t\in\mathds{R}}
where
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}
is defined by

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}\vcentcolon=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\varphi_{u}(t-S(u)),\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

(2.2)

Here, we use the convention
\varphi(-\infty)\vcentcolon=0
and so the above sum involves only terms
associated with individuals that are eventually born.
In the special case
\varphi=\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}
,

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}}_{t}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta_{u})}(t-S(u))=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t<S(u)+\zeta_{u}\}},

(2.3)

i.e.,
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}}_{t}
is the number of individuals alive at time
t
.
Similarly,

N((t,t+a])\vcentcolon=\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,a)}}_{t+a}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{[0,a)}(t+a-S(u))=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{t<S(u)\leq t+a\}}

(2.4)

is the number of individuals born strictly after time
t
and up to and including time
t+a
,
a>0
.
The setup covers a wide range of possible applications.
Some special cases and specific examples are covered in Section
3
.

Notice that
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}
is not well-defined
a priori
.
Conditions for the finiteness of the general branching process are given in
[
25
, Section 6.2]
.
For instance, the existence of a Malthusian parameter, a condition formally stated as (A
1
) below
and assumed throughout this paper, implies that the number of individuals born up to and including time
t
is finite for all
t\geq 0

almost surely, see
[
25
, Theorem 6.2.3]
.
In particular, if (A
1
) holds, then
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}
is well-defined whenever the characteristic
\varphi

vanishes on the negative half-line since in this case, the sum on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) has only finitely many non-vanishing
summands almost surely.
As, in general, we allow the characteristic
\varphi
to be real-valued and do not require that it vanishes
on the negative half-line, we need a finiteness result that goes beyond
[
25
, Theorem 6.2.3]
.
Jagers and Nerman
[
28
]
work under their assumption (6.1),
which corresponds to our condition (A
4
) for
|\varphi|
below.
However, in our proofs, we shall require the well-definedness of
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi_{\lambda}}

for a specific centered characteristic
\chi_{\lambda}
, defined in Section
5
,
with
|\chi_{\lambda}|
not satisfying (A
4
).
Instead, we work under (A
4
) and (A
5
)
to ensure the general branching process counted with a random characteristic to be well-defined.
The corresponding result is Proposition
2.2
below.

2.2
Assumptions

We write
\mu(\cdot)
for the intensity measure
\mathds{E}[\xi(\cdot)]
of the point process
\xi(\cdot)
,
and
\mathcal{L}\mu
for its Laplace transform, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\vcentcolon=\int e^{-zx}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-zX_{j}}\bigg]

(2.5)

for all
z\in\mathds{C}
for which the above integral converges absolutely.

Throughout this paper we distinguish between the lattice and the non-lattice case.
Here, we say that
\xi
is
lattice
if
\mu([0,\infty)\setminus h\mathds{N}_{0})=0
for some
h>0
, and
we say that
\xi
is
non-lattice
, otherwise.
In the lattice case, without loss of generality, we assume that the lattice span is
1
,
\mu([0,\infty)\setminus\mathds{N}_{0})=0

and
\mu([0,\infty)\setminus h\mathds{N}_{0})>0
for all
h>1
.
We set
\mathds{G}\vcentcolon=\mathds{Z}
in the lattice case and
\mathds{G}\vcentcolon=\mathds{R}
in the non-lattice case.
We use the symbol
\ell
to denote the counting measure on
\mathds{Z}
in the lattice case and the Lebesgue measure in the non-lattice case, respectively.

For a function
f:\mathds{G}\mapsto\mathds{C}
we define the bilateral Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}f
of
f
at
z\in\mathds{C}
by

\mathcal{L}f(z)\vcentcolon=\int_{\mathds{G}}e^{-zx}f(x)\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

whenever the integral converges absolutely.

The following assumption is essential in the law of large numbers
[
16
,
39
]

and, therefore, also for the central limit theorem studied here.

(A1)

There exists a
Malthusian parameter

\alpha>0
, i.e., an
\alpha>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha)=\int e^{-\alpha x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=1\quad\text{and}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}e^{-\alpha X_{j}}\bigg]=-(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{\prime}(\alpha)=\vcentcolon\beta\in(0,\infty).

(2.7)

Notice that (A
1
) implies the supercriticality of the general branching process,
that is,
\mathds{E}[N]=\mu([0,\infty))\in(1,\infty]
, which, in turn, ensures that the underlying branching process survives with positive probability
meaning that
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{S})>0
. We stress that the case
\mathds{P}(N=\infty)>0
is allowed.
For the rest of the paper, we assume that (A
1
) is satisfied.

In our main results, we further assume that the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}\mu
is finite
on an open half-space
\mathrm{Re}(z)>\vartheta
for some
\vartheta<\frac{\alpha}{2}
:

(A2)

There exists
\vartheta\in(0,\frac{\alpha}{2})
such that

\mathcal{L}\mu(\vartheta)=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\vartheta X_{j}}\bigg]<\infty.

(2.8)

For the central limit theorem, we need a second moment assumption for the point process
\xi
.
Before we state it, we set
k^{*}
to be the maximum of all multiplicities of the roots of
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=1

on the critical line
\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}
or
k^{*}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}
if there is no such root.

(A3)

The random variable

\int(1+x^{k^{*}-\frac{1}{2}})e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}x}\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(1+X_{j}^{k^{*}-\frac{1}{2}}\big)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}X_{j}}

(2.9)

has finite second moment.

Remark 2.1
.

Notice that Condition (A6) in
[
30
]
, namely, the existence of a
\vartheta<\frac{\alpha}{2}
such that

\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\vartheta X_{j}}\bigg)^{\!\!2}\,\bigg]<\infty,

implies both our conditions (A
2.8
) and (A
3
).
Janson’s condition (A6) may be easier to check in cases where it holds.

The existence of the Malthusian parameter allows us to define a nonnegative martingale,
called
Nerman’s martingale
, namely,

\displaystyle W_{t}=W_{t}(\alpha)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)},\quad t\geq 0

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t}\vcentcolon=\{uj\in\mathcal{T}:S(u)\leq t<S(uj)\}

(2.11)

is the coming generation at time
t
. For the proof of the martingale property under (A
1
) see
[
39
, Proposition 2.4]
.
We denote the almost sure limit of Nerman’s martingale by
W
.
Martingale theory implies
that
\mathds{E}[W]=1
iff
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly integrable.
Sufficient conditions for

),
expansion (
2.20
) is derived in Lemma
7.6
.
There might be more examples of
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}
that are not covered by any of the three sufficient conditions,
even though the corresponding expansion of
\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}]
is of form (
2.20
).
For this reason, we formulate our main result, Theorem
2.15
,
for processes
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}
for which
\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}]
satisfies (
2.20
).
What is more, in some examples, it is in fact more convenient to directly check that (
2.20
) holds
rather than checking the assumptions of Theorem
2.9
or
2.10
,
see e. g. Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

From (
2.20
) we can obtain an asymptotic expansion
of
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}

where the principal terms are of the form a constant times
e^{\lambda t}t^{j}W^{(j)}(\lambda)

for
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
j=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
.
More precisely, the principal terms are given by the expression

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(t)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{\lambda t}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}a_{\lambda,l}\binom{l}{j}t^{j}W^{(l-j)}(\lambda).

(2.21)

If, additionally, there are roots
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
,
then the next terms in the expansion are given by the following deterministic sum

\displaystyle H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}e^{\lambda t}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{\lambda,l}t^{l},\qquad t\in\mathds{R}.

(2.22)

(Of course, if
\Lambda_{\geq}=\partial\Lambda\cup\{\alpha\}
,
the terms from
H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)
are the subleading terms.)
We set
H(t)\vcentcolon=H_{\Lambda}(t)+H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)
,
t\in\mathds{R}
.
Further, for any
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
and
l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
,
we define a random variable
R_{\lambda,l}
by

\displaystyle R_{\lambda,l}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=l}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{\lambda,j}\binom{j}{l}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-X_{k})^{j-l}e^{-\lambda X_{k}}.

(2.23)

Assumption (A
3
) guarantees that
R_{\lambda,l}\in L^{2}
for all
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda

and
l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
.
We may thus define

\rho_{l}^{2}\vcentcolon=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\partial\Lambda:\\
k(\lambda)>l\end{subarray}}\Var[R_{\lambda,l}]

where
\Var[R_{\lambda,l}]=\mathds{E}[|R_{\lambda,l}|^{2}]-|\mathds{E}[R_{\lambda,l}]|^{2}
.
In general, throughout the paper, if
Y
is a complex-valued random variable with finite mean,
we set
\Var[Y]\vcentcolon=\mathds{E}[|Y-\mathds{E}[Y]|^{2}]
.

As a final preparation for our main result,
we recall the fact that if a sequence of random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}

defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})

converges in distribution to some random variable
Y
, this convergence is said to be
stable

if for all continuity points
y
of the distribution function of
Y
and all
E\in\mathcal{F}
,
the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}(\{Y_{n}\leq y\}\cap E)
exists.
In this case, we write
Y_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{st\ }$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}Y
.
An alternative characterization is the following.
There is a copy
Y^{*}
of
Y
defined on some extension of the probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
such that, for every
\mathcal{F}
-measurable random variable
X
, it holds that

(Y_{n},X)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}(Y^{*},X)\text{ as }n\to\infty,

see
[
1
, Condition (B’)]
.
Without loss of generality we may and will assume that
Y=Y^{*}
whenever we write
Y_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{st\ }$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}Y
.
The second definition is more convenient as it allows to manipulate the sequence
Y_{n}
(e.g. by multiplying with another random variable)
without loosing the convergence in distribution. In Remark
2.17
below we prove (
2.27
)
using such an argument.

Remark 2.14
.

Although it is not stated explicitly but it follows from the proofs that the convergences in
Theorems
2.8
,
2.9
and
2.10
are in fact stable.

For a measurable function
f
and a measure
\nu
(possibly random) on the Borel
\sigma
-field,
we write

for the Lebesgue-Stieltjes convolution of

and

i.e.,

whenever the integral exists.
In particular,

f*\xi(t)=\int f(t-x)\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(t-X_{j}),\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

Remark 2.16
.

Observe that, in the non-lattice case, if
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
, then so is
\overline{\lambda}

and
k(\overline{\lambda})=k(\lambda)
. Moreover, as
m_{t}^{\varphi}
is real for any
t\in\mathds{R}
, we have

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{\lambda,l}t^{l}e^{\lambda t}+r(t)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{{\overline{\lambda}},l}t^{l}e^{\overline{\lambda}t}+r(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}\overline{a_{\overline{\lambda},l}}t^{l}e^{\lambda t}+\overline{r(t)}=\overline{m_{t}^{\varphi}},\quad t\in\mathds{R},

whence

\displaystyle 0=m_{t}^{\varphi}-\overline{m_{t}^{\varphi}}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}(a_{\lambda,l}-\overline{a_{\overline{\lambda},l}})t^{l}e^{\lambda t}+o(e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t})\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,\ t\in\mathds{R}.

Next, we can choose
h>0
such that the
e^{\lambda h}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
are distinct.
Recalling that
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
,
Lemma
.1
gives that
a_{\lambda,l}-\overline{a_{\overline{\lambda},l}}=0
,
that is,
\overline{a_{\lambda,l}}=a_{\overline{\lambda},l}
. In particular,
r(t)
is real for any
t\in\mathds{R}
.

A similar reasoning in the lattice case gives
\overline{a_{\lambda,l}}=a_{\overline{\lambda},l}
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
with
|\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)|<\pi

and
a_{\lambda,l}\in\mathds{R}
if
\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)=\pi
.

Remark 2.17
.

Observe that for
N(t)\vcentcolon=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}}
,
the number of individuals born up to and including time
t
, by (
2.13
), we have

e^{-\alpha t}N(t)\to\frac{1}{\beta}\int\limits_{[0,\infty)}e^{-\alpha x}\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\,W=\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\beta}W\quad\text{a.\,s.~as}~t\to\infty,~t\in\mathds{G},

where
c_{\alpha}=(1-e^{-\alpha})^{-1}
in the lattice case,

c_{\alpha}=\alpha^{-1}
in the non-lattice case.
The stable convergence in (
ii
) yields

a_{t}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha}}{N(t)}}\big(\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}-H(t)\big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{as }t\to\infty\quad\text{conditionally given }\mathcal{S}.

(2.27)

Indeed, with
G(t)\vcentcolon=a_{t}^{-1}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\big(\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}-H(t)\big)
,

(
2.27
) is equivalent to

\mathds{P}(\mathcal{S})^{-1}\cdot\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{c_{\alpha}e^{\alpha t}}{N(t)}}G(t)\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]\to\mathds{E}[g(\mathcal{N})]

as
t\to\infty
, for any continuous, nonnegative function
g
bounded by
1
.
With
F(t)\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha}We^{\alpha t}}{\beta N(t)}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}+\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}
,
which is well-defined since
N(t)>0
on
\mathcal{S}
,
the above convergence can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\mathcal{S})^{-1}\cdot\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\beta}\tfrac{F(t)G(t)}{\sqrt{W}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]\to\mathds{E}[g(\mathcal{N})]\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

(2.28)

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
\delta>0
such that
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{S}\cap\{W<\delta\})=\mathds{P}(0<W<\delta)\leq\varepsilon
and consequently

\displaystyle\Big|\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\beta}\tfrac{F(t)G(t)}{\sqrt{W}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]-\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\beta}\tfrac{F(t)G(t)}{\sqrt{W\vee\delta}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]\Big|\leq\varepsilon

as well as

\displaystyle\Big|\mathds{E}[g(\mathcal{N})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}]-\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}}{\sqrt{W\vee\delta}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]\Big|\leq\varepsilon.

On the other hand, (
ii
) yields

\displaystyle(G(t),W,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}})\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}(\sqrt{\tfrac{W}{\beta}}\mathcal{N},W,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}})\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Since
F(t)\to 1
almost surely, Slutsky’s theorem implies

\displaystyle(G(t),W,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}},F(t))\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}(\sqrt{\tfrac{W}{\beta}}\mathcal{N},W,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}},1)\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Taking advantage of the fact that the function
(u,v,x,y)\mapsto g(\sqrt{\beta}\frac{uy}{\sqrt{v\vee\delta}})(|x|\wedge 1)
is bounded and continuous, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\beta}\tfrac{F(t)G(t)}{\sqrt{W\vee\delta}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]\to\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}}{\sqrt{W\vee\delta}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]

as
t\to\infty
, and consequently

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\Big|\mathds{E}\Big[g\Big(\sqrt{\beta}\tfrac{F(t)G(t)}{\sqrt{W}}\Big)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big]-\mathds{E}[g(\mathcal{N})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}]\Big|\leq 2\varepsilon.

Letting now
\varepsilon
to 0 and using the independence of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}

we get (
2.28
) and thereby (
2.27
).

Remark 2.18
.

(i)
Notice that formula (
2.26
) is not well-defined in the case
n\geq 0
as then the integral diverges.
However, in this case, the exact value of
\sigma
is irrelevant and can be set to
\sigma\vcentcolon=0
.

(ii)
In the non-lattice case the variance
\sigma^{2}
given by (
2.26
) can be calculated using the bilateral Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}h^{\varphi}
of
h^{\varphi}
.
Indeed, by Plancherel’s theorem,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\Var[\varphi(x)+h^{\varphi}*\xi(x)]e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\big(\big(\varphi(x)\!-\!\mathds{E}[\varphi](x)+(h^{\varphi}*(\xi\!-\!\mu))(x)\big)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}x}\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\big|\mathcal{L}((\varphi(\cdot)\!-\!\mathds{E}[\varphi](\cdot))e^{-\alpha\cdot/2})(\mathrm{i}\eta)+\mathcal{L}\big(((\xi\!-\!\mu)*h^{\varphi})(\cdot)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot}\big)(\mathrm{i}\eta)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\big|\mathcal{L}(\varphi\!-\!\mathds{E}[\varphi])(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}\eta)+\mathcal{L}\big(((\xi\!-\!\mu)*h^{\varphi})(\cdot)\big)(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}\eta)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Var[\mathcal{L}\varphi(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\mathcal{L}h^{\varphi}(z)]\,|\mathrm{d}z|,

where the variance of a complex random variable is defined in terms of absolute squares.
An analogous formula holds in the lattice case, see
[
30
]
. We refrain from giving further details.

(iii)
Suppose now that
\varphi
vanishes on
(-\infty,0)
.
Then so do
m_{t}^{\varphi}
and the remainder function
r
from the expansion (
2.20
).
Additionally, assume that
r(t)=O(e^{(\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon)t})
for some
\varepsilon>0
,
which holds in typical cases (see Section
7
).
In the non-lattice case of Theorem
2.15
, if
all the roots in
\Lambda
are simple and there are no roots on the critical line
\{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\}
,
the bilateral Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}h^{\varphi}
of
h^{\varphi}
coincides on a neighborhood of
\{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\}

with the function

z\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\varphi])(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}.

To see this, notice that
r(t)=m^{\varphi}_{t}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}a_{\lambda,0}e^{\lambda t}\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)

and let
h_{r}(t)\vcentcolon=h^{\varphi}(t)-r(t)
.
The bilateral Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}r
is well-defined on
\{\mathrm{Re}(z)>\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon\}
.
Moreover, for
\mathrm{Re}(z)>\alpha
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}r(z)=\mathcal{L}m^{\varphi}(z)-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\lambda,0}}{z-\lambda}=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\varphi])(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\lambda,0}}{z-\lambda}.

The right-hand side, being holomorphic on
\{\mathrm{Re}(z)>\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon\}
(all the singularities are removable), coincides with
\mathcal{L}r
on that domain.
On the other hand, decreasing
\varepsilon
if needed we can and do assume that
\Lambda\subseteq\{\mathrm{Re}(z)>\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon\}
. In particular,
\mathcal{L}h_{r}(z)
is well-defined on
\{\mathrm{Re}(z)<\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon\}
and
equal to
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\lambda,0}}{z-\lambda}
.
As a result, on the set
\{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon<\mathrm{Re}(z)<\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon\}

we have
\mathcal{L}h^{\varphi}(z)=\mathcal{L}r(z)+\mathcal{L}h_{r}(z)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\varphi])(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}
.

Applications

3.1
The Galton-Watson process

Consider a supercritical Galton-Watson branching process,
i.e.,
\xi=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{1}=N\delta_{1}

where
N
is a random variable taking values in
\mathds{N}_{0}
with

m\vcentcolon=\mathds{E}[N]\in(1,\infty)
and
\mathds{E}[N^{2}]<\infty
.
Then
\xi
is lattice with span
1
.
Further,

\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{-\lambda}\bigg]=me^{-\lambda},\quad\lambda\in\mathds{C}.

The equation
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=1
is equivalent to
e^{z}=m
and has only one solution
in the strip
\mathrm{Im}(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
.
In particular,
(A
1
) holds,
i.e., there is a Malthusian parameter
\alpha>0
, namely,
\alpha=\log m
,
and
\Lambda_{\geq}=\{\alpha\}
. In this case the parameter
\beta
defined by (
2.7
) is equal to 1.
Then

\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{-\theta}\bigg)^{\!2}\bigg]=e^{-2\theta}\mathds{E}[N^{2}]<\infty\quad\text{for all }\theta\in\mathds{R}.

By Remark
2.1
, this implies that (A
2.8
)
and (A
3
) hold.

Consider the characteristic
\phi(t)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}_{[0,1)}(t)
.
Then for any
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\phi}
is the number of individuals in the
n^{\mathrm{th}}

generation and the corresponding Nerman’s martingale (
2.10
) is the size of
n^{\mathrm{th}}
generation
normalized by its expectation
m^{n}
, i.e.,
W_{n}=e^{-\alpha n}\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\phi}
.
Clearly,
\phi

satisfies (A
4
), (A
5
) and (A
6
). Therefore, we may apply the lattice version of Theorem
2.15
with
\rho_{-1}=0
, which yields

\displaystyle m^{-\frac{n}{2}}\big(m^{n}W_{n}-a_{\alpha}m^{n}W\big)=e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}n}\big(\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\phi}-a_{\alpha}e^{\alpha n}W\big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sigma\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty

where
\mathcal{N}
is standard normal and independent of
W
.
To calculate
a_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=a_{\alpha,0}
, we use (
2.20
):

m^{n}=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\phi}]=a_{\alpha}m^{n}
, i.e.,
a_{\alpha}=1
.
Further,
\sigma>0
is given by (
2.26
),
i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\Var\big[\phi(n)+h*\xi(n)\big]e^{-\alpha n}=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\Var\big[h*\xi(n)\big]m^{-n},

where

\displaystyle h(n)=m^{\phi}_{n}-a_{\alpha}e^{\alpha n}=m^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{n\geq 0\}}-m^{n}=-m^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{n<0\}}.

Now, since
h*\xi(n)=Nh(n-1)
we infer

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\sum_{n<1}\Var[N]m^{2n-2}m^{-n}=\frac{\Var[N]}{m^{2}-m}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle m^{\frac{n}{2}}\big(W_{n}-W\big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\Big(\frac{\Var[N]W}{m^{2}-m}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

We have thus just rediscovered Heyde’s classical central limit theorem for the martingale in the Galton-Watson process

[
20
]
.

We can also deal with the total number of individuals in the generations
0,\ldots,n
.
Indeed, this number is
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{f}
for
f(t)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)
,
t\in\mathds{R}
,
which satisfies (A
4
), (A
5
) and (A
6
).
Invoking once again Theorem
2.15
with
\rho_{-1}=0
we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}n}\big(\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{f}-a_{\alpha}e^{\alpha n}W\big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sigma\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

This time
a_{\alpha}
can be computed with the help of (
2.20
) as follows.
We have the asymptotic expansion

m_{n}^{f}=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{f}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}m^{k}=\frac{m^{n+1}-1}{m-1}=\frac{m}{m-1}e^{\alpha n}-\frac{1}{m-1},

for
n\geq 0
and 0 otherwise.
Consequently,
a_{\alpha}=\frac{m}{m-1}
and thereupon

\displaystyle m^{-\frac{n}{2}}\Big(\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{f}-\frac{m^{n+1}}{m-1}W\Big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sigma\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

This time
\sigma>0
is given by
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\Var[N]|h(n-1)|^{2}m^{-n}

with

\displaystyle h(n)=m_{n}^{f}-\frac{m}{m-1}e^{\alpha n}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\Var[N]\Big(\sum_{n<1}\Big(\frac{m}{m-1}m^{n-1}\Big)^{2}m^{-n}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\Big(\frac{1}{m-1}\Big)^{2}m^{-n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)^{2}}\Var[N]\bigg(\sum_{n\leq 0}m^{n}+\sum_{n>0}m^{-n}\bigg)=\frac{m+1}{(m-1)^{3}}\Var[N].

Epidemic models

In this section, we consider the epidemic model discussed in

In this model, the role of the ancestor is that of the first person in a community

Suppose that
\xi

[0,\infty)

R_{0}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

a,b>0

N

R_{0}

In the given situation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)

The real part of a root

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Var\Big[\mathcal{L}f(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}f(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}\Big]\,|\mathrm{d}z|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Big|\frac{1}{z(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}\Var[\mathcal{L}\xi(z)]\,|\mathrm{d}z|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4}{(\alpha^{2}+4t^{2})g(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t,

with

.

3.4
Supercritical binary homogeneous Crump-Mode-Jagers processes

In this section, we assume that

\xi\vcentcolon=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta]}(P_{j})\delta_{P_{j}}

where

(P_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are the arrival times of a homogeneous Poisson process with intensity
b>0
,
independent of the
[0,\infty]
-valued random variable
\zeta
.
We are interested in
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}}_{t}
the number of individuals alive at time
t
,
see (
2.3
). Thus, the corresponding characteristic
\phi
is given by
\phi(t)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}(t)
for
t\geq 0
.

We put
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)\vcentcolon=\mathds{E}[e^{-z\zeta}]
for
\mathrm{Re}(z)\geq 0
and start by noting that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta]}(P_{j})e^{-zP_{j}}\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta]}(x)e^{-zx}b\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg]=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zx}b\mathds{P}(\zeta\geq x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{b(1-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z))}{z},\quad\mathrm{Re}(z)>0.

The Malthusian parameter
\alpha
is the unique real number that satisfies

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{b},

and the parameter
\beta
is given by

\beta=\frac{1}{\alpha}(1+b\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}^{\prime}(\alpha))=\frac{1}{\alpha}(1-b\mathds{E}[\zeta e^{-\alpha\zeta}]).

Now we shall show
that
\alpha
is the only solution to
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=1
with
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
.
Indeed, for positive
\theta
and
\eta

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{L}\mu(\theta+\mathrm{i}\eta)\big)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(\eta x)e^{-\theta x}b\mathds{P}(\zeta\geq x)\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle=b\sum_{l\geq 0}\int\limits_{2\pi l/\eta}^{\pi(2l+1)/\eta}\sin(\eta x)\Big(e^{-\theta x}\mathds{P}(\zeta\geq x)-e^{-\theta(x+\pi/\eta)}\mathds{P}(\zeta\geq x+\pi/\eta)\Big)\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}>0

which, together with
\mathcal{L}\mu(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)}
, shows that

\Lambda_{\geq}\cap\{z:\mathrm{Re}(z)>0\}=\{\alpha\}
. Since
\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\phi])(\alpha)=b^{-1}\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha)=b^{-1}
,
an application of Theorem
2.8
yields

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\Big(\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}}_{t}-e^{\alpha t}\frac{W}{b\beta}\Big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{W}{\beta}}\mathcal{N}.

Next, we express the variance
\sigma^{2}
in terms of the parameters
b
,
\alpha
and
\beta
.
By Remark
2.18

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Var\Big[\mathcal{L}\phi(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\phi])(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}\Big]\,|\mathrm{d}z|.

For
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0

\displaystyle\Var\Big[\mathcal{L}\phi(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathds{E}[\phi])(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\Big[\Var\Big[\mathcal{L}\phi(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|{\zeta}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}+\Var\Big[\mathds{E}\Big[\mathcal{L}\phi(z)+\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|\zeta\Big]\Big]=\vcentcolon\mathit{I}+\mathit{I\!I}

with

\displaystyle\mathit{I}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\Big[\Var\Big[\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|\zeta\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[\Var\big[\mathcal{L}\xi(z)\big|\zeta\big]\big]\Big|\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}=\mathds{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\zeta}e^{-2\mathrm{Re}(z)x}b\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\Big|\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2},

where we have used properties of the Poisson process, and

\displaystyle\mathit{I\!I}

\displaystyle=\Var\Big[\frac{1-e^{-z\zeta}}{z}+\frac{b(1-e^{-z\zeta})}{z}\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big]=\Big|\frac{1}{z(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}\Var[e^{-z\zeta}].

Assuming now that
\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathit{I}=\Big|\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}

because

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\zeta}e^{-2\mathrm{Re}(z)x}b\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]=\mathds{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\zeta}e^{-\alpha x}b\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]=1,

and

\mathit{I\!I}=\Big|\frac{1}{z(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(\alpha)-|\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)|^{2}\big).

Observing that
\overline{z}=\alpha-z
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}=\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha-z)
whenever

\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}
, we further infer

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{z\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b}\Big|^{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(\alpha)-|\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|1-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)|^{2}+1-\frac{\alpha}{b}-|\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)+\overline{1-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)}-\tfrac{\alpha}{b}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{b}\big(z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)+(\alpha-z)(\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha-z)-1)\big),

which in turn gives

\displaystyle\mathit{I}+\mathit{I\!I}

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{1}{z(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\Big|^{2}\Big(\Big|\frac{z\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{b}\Big|^{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(\alpha)-|\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(z)|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)+(\alpha-z)(\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha-z)-1)}{bz(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)(\alpha-z)(\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha-z)-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b(\alpha-z)(\mathcal{L}\mu(\alpha-z)-1)}+\frac{1}{bz(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{b}\mathrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}\Big).

We can now compute the variance as follows

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{b\pi}\mathrm{Re}\bigg(\int\limits_{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{i}R}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}R}\frac{1}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}\,|\mathrm{d}z|\bigg)=\frac{1}{b\pi}\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathrm{Im}\bigg(\int\limits_{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{i}R}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}R}\frac{1}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b\pi}\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathrm{Im}\bigg(\int\limits_{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{i}R}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}R}\frac{1}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}+\frac{1}{z}\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)-\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{b\pi}\int\limits_{-R}^{R}\frac{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{(\tfrac{\alpha}{2})^{2}+t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b\pi}\mathrm{Im}\bigg(\lim_{R\to\infty}\int\limits_{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{i}R}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}R}\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)-\frac{1}{b}.

To calculate the limit, we use the residue theorem. For
R>\alpha
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{i}Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathcal{L}\mu(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\mathrm{d}\theta}{(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})(\mathcal{L}\mu(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})-1)}-\int\limits_{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{i}R}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\mathrm{i}R}\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\mathrm{d}z}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\underset{z=\alpha}{\Res}\frac{\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}{z(\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1)}.

It suffices to show that the integrand of the first integral decays to zero uniformly in
\theta

as
R
goes to infinity. In view of the inequality
|\mathcal{L}\mu(z)|\leq 2b|z|^{-1}
and its consequence
|\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1|\geq 1-2b|z|^{-1}

(both hold true for
\mathrm{Re}(z)\geq 0
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{R\mathcal{L}\mu(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})}{(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})(\mathcal{L}\mu(\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta})-1)}\bigg|\leq\frac{R}{|\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}|}\cdot\frac{2b}{|\frac{\alpha}{2}+Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}|-2b}=O(R^{-1})

as
R\to\infty
uniformly in
\theta\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
.
Finally,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\frac{2-\alpha\beta}{\alpha b\beta}

and thereupon

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\Big(\mathcal{Z}^{\mathds{1}_{[0,\zeta)}}_{t}-e^{\alpha t}\frac{W}{b\beta}\Big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{st\ }$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sqrt{\frac{(2-\alpha\beta)W}{\alpha b\beta^{2}}}\mathcal{N}.

An application of a similar argument as in Remark
2.17
enables us to conclude that the convergence mentioned above holds true conditionally given
\mathcal{S}
.
The distribution of
W
conditionally given
\mathcal{S}
is exponential with parameter
\alpha/b
.
Therefore, we have just reproved Henry’s central limit theorem
[
19
]
.

3.5
The conservative fragmentation model

In this section we consider the conservative fragmentation model
as discussed in
[
31
]
.
Let
b\geq 2
be integer and
(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{b})
a vector of nonnegative random variables such that

\sum_{j=1}^{b}V_{j}=1
a. s.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
V_{1},\ldots,V_{b}
have Lebesgue densities except possible atoms at
0
.
(Our theory would allow to cover more general cases, too.)
Starting with an object of mass
x\geq 1
, we break it into pieces with masses
(V_{1}x,V_{2}x,\ldots,V_{b}x)
.
Continue recursively with each piece of mass
\geq 1
,
using new and independent copies of the random vector
(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{b})
each time.
Once a fragment has mass
<1
, it is not further crumbled.
The process terminates a. s. after a finite number of steps, leaving a finite set of fragments of masses
<1
.

Denote by
n(x)
the random number of fragmentation events,
i.e., the number of pieces of mass
\geq 1
that appear during the process.
Further, let
n_{e}(x)
be the final number of fragments, i.e., the number of pieces of mass
<1
that appear.
A limit theorem for
n(x)
has been proved in
[
31
]
,
where it was shown that the asymptotic behavior of
n(x)
as
x
goes to infinity
depends on the position of the roots of the function
z\mapsto\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathds{E}[V_{j}^{z}]
.

Letting
\xi\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\mathds{1}_{\{V_{j}>0\}}\delta_{-\log V_{j}}
, we conclude that
the corresponding Malthusian parameter is 1, i.e.,
\alpha=1
and the limit of Nerman’s martingale satisfies
W=1
a. s. Further
\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\mathbb{E}[V_{j}|\log V_{j}|]\in(0,\infty).

Note also that
n(x)=N(\log x)
corresponds to the number of individuals born up to and including time
\log x

and similarly, we can represent
n_{e}(x)
as a general branching process,
namely,
n_{e}(x)=\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{\log x}
, with

\varphi(t)\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\mathds{1}_{\{V_{j}>0\}}\mathds{1}_{[0,-\log V_{j})}(t)\quad\text{for }t\in\mathds{R}.

Hence, our main result provides (precise) limit theorems for both
n
and
n_{e}
.
For instance, in the case when all root from
\Lambda
are simple, we infer from Theorem
2.9

(the constants
b_{\lambda,0}
in the theorem can easily be seen to equal

b_{\lambda,0}=-1/(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{\prime}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\Lambda

by Proposition
7.9
)

\displaystyle x^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\Big(n(x)+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\tfrac{W(\lambda)}{\lambda(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{\prime}(\lambda)}x^{\lambda}\Big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\beta}}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{if }\partial\Lambda\text{ is empty and}

\displaystyle x^{-1/2}\big(\log x\big)^{-k+1/2}

\displaystyle\Big(n(x)+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\tfrac{W(\lambda)}{\lambda(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{\prime}(\lambda)}x^{\lambda}\Big)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\frac{\rho_{k-1}}{\sqrt{(2k-1)\beta}}\mathcal{N}\quad\text{if }\partial\Lambda\text{ is non-empty},

where
k
is the largest multiplicity of a root on the critical line
\mathrm{Re}(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}
, and
\rho_{k-1}
is as in Theorem
2.15
.

4
Preliminaries for the proofs of the main results

In this section we gather facts from the literature, introduce some notation used throughout the paper
and perform some basic calculations.

4.1
Change of measure and the connection to renewal theory

The existence of the Malthusian parameter (i.e., (
2.6
)) enables us
to use a change-of-measure argument as follows.
We define a random walk

(S_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
with
S_{0}=0
on some probability space
with underlying probability measure
\mathsf{P}
and increment distribution given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}(S_{1}\in B)=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=1}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\mathds{1}_{B}(S(u))\bigg]=\int\limits_{B}e^{-\alpha x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}),\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathds{R}).

(4.1)

With this definition, the many-to-one formula (see, e.g.,
[
46
, Theorem 1.1]
) holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[f(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=n}e^{-\alpha S(u)}f(S(u|_{1}),\ldots,S(u))\bigg]

(4.2)

for all Borel measurable
f:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}
such that the expectation on the left- or right-hand side of (
4.2
)
is well-defined, possibly infinite.
In particular, under (
2.7
)

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[S_{1}]

\displaystyle=\beta\in(0,\infty).

(4.3)

In other words, the increments of the random walk

(S_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}

have positive, finite mean. As a consequence, the associated renewal measure

\mathsf{U}(\cdot)=\sum_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\mathsf{P}(S_{n}\in\cdot)

is uniformly locally finite in the sense that

\mathsf{U}([t,t+h])\leq\mathsf{U}([0,h])<\infty\qquad\text{for all }t,h\geq 0.

(4.4)

Indeed, if
\tau\vcentcolon=\inf\{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}:S_{n}\geq t\}
, then

\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{[t,t+h]}(S_{n})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{[t,t+h]}(S_{\tau+n})\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{[0,h]}(S_{\tau+n}-S_{\tau}).

Now take expectations and use the strong Markov property at
\tau
to infer (
4.4
).

By the many-to-one formula, (A
2.8
) implies
that the increments of the associated random walk
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}

have a finite exponential moment of order
\alpha-\vartheta>\alpha/2

since

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[e^{(\alpha-\vartheta)S_{1}}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\vartheta X_{j}}\bigg]=\mathcal{L}\mu(\vartheta)<\infty.

(4.5)

4.2
The expectation of the general branching process

There is a connection between the renewal measure
\mathsf{U}

and the expectation
m_{t}^{\varphi}=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}]
of the general branching process
counted with characteristic
\varphi
provided that
\varphi
satisfies suitable assumptions.
For instance, if
\varphi
is nonnegative and

t\mapsto\mathds{E}[\varphi(t)]e^{-\alpha t}
is a directly Riemann integrable function,
then we infer from the many-to-one formula

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\varphi}e^{-\alpha t}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}]e^{-\alpha t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=n}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\varphi_{u}(t-S(u))e^{-\alpha(t-S(u))}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=n}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\mathds{E}[\varphi](t-S(u))e^{-\alpha(t-S(u))}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{E}\big[\mathds{E}[\varphi](t-S_{n})e^{-\alpha(t-S_{n})}\big]

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{E}[\varphi](t-x)e^{-\alpha(t-x)}\,\mathsf{U}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}).

(4.6)

By the direct Riemann integrability of
t\mapsto\mathds{E}[\varphi(t)]e^{-\alpha t}

and (
4.4
), the function
t\mapsto m_{t}^{\varphi}e^{-\alpha t}
is
bounded and, moreover,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to\infty\\
t\in\mathds{G}\end{subarray}}e^{-\alpha t}m_{t}^{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\beta}\int\mathds{E}[\varphi](x)e^{-\alpha x}\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\frac{1}{\beta}(\mathcal{L}\mathds{E}[\varphi])(\alpha)

(4.7)

by the key renewal theorem, see
[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
in the non-lattice case
and
[
2
, Theorem 2.5.3]
in the lattice (and non-lattice) case.
Recall that, in the lattice case,
\mathcal{L}\mathds{E}[\varphi]
denotes the ‘discrete’ bilateral Laplace transform of
\mathds{E}[\varphi]
,

\mathds{G}=\mathds{Z}
and
\ell
is the counting measure on
\mathds{Z}
,
whereas in the non-lattice case,
\mathcal{L}\mathds{E}[\varphi]
is the ‘continuous’ bilateral Laplace transform,

\mathds{G}=\mathds{R}
and
\ell
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathds{R}
.

We need a lemma in preparation for the proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that (A
1
) holds and that
\chi

is a centered characteristic, i.e.,
\mathds{E}[\chi(t)]=0
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.
Fix
t\in\mathds{R}
and suppose that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\Var[\chi](t-S(u))\bigg]<\infty.

(4.8)

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
be an admissible ordering of
\mathcal{I}
(see the paragraph before Proposition
2.2
for the definition).
Define

M_{n}(t)\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{u_{j}}(t-S(u_{j}))

for
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.
Then
(M_{n}(t))_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
is a centered martingale and bounded in
L^{2}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle Z^{\chi}_{t}\vcentcolon=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\chi_{u}(t-S(u))

(4.9)

converges unconditionally in
L^{2}
and it is also the almost sure limit of
M_{n}(t)
as
n\to\infty
.
Further, for any (deterministic) sequence
(\mathcal{I}_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
with
\mathcal{I}_{n}\uparrow\mathcal{I}
,

M_{\mathcal{I}_{n}}(t)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\chi_{u}(t-S(u))\to\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}\quad\text{in $L^{2}$ as }n\to\infty.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\Var[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}]=\mathds{E}[(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi})^{2}]=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi^{2}}]<\infty.

(4.10)

Finally, (A
5
) is sufficient for (
4.8
) to hold
for every
t\in\mathds{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\sigma(\pi_{u_{j}}:j\leq n)
,
where it should be recalled that
\pi_{u}
is the projection onto the life space of individual
u
,
in particular,
(\xi_{u},\zeta_{u},\chi_{u})
is
\pi_{u}
-measurable.
Then
(M_{n}(t))_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
is adapted with respect to
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
as,
for any
u\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, both
S(u)
and
\chi_{u}
are
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-measurable.
Moreover, (
4.8
) implies that for any
u\in\mathcal{I}
,

\mathds{E}[|\chi_{u}(t-S(u))|]<\infty
. Hence
M_{n}(t)
is integrable for any
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.
The martingale property then follows since
S(u_{n+1})
is
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-measurable
whereas
\chi_{u_{n+1}}
is independent of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and since
\mathds{E}[\chi](x)=\mathds{E}[\chi(x)]=0
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
, so

\mathds{E}[\chi_{u_{n+1}}(t-S(u_{n+1}))|\mathcal{G}_{n}]=\mathds{E}[\chi](t-S(u_{n+1}))=0\quad\text{almost surely.}

Next, we observe that, since the increments of
L^{2}
-martingales are uncorrelated,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[M_{n}(t)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{u_{j}}^{2}(t-S(u_{j}))\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}](t-S(u_{j}))\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}](t-S(u))\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\Var[\chi](t-S(u))\bigg].

(4.11)

By (
4.8
),
the martingale
(M_{n}(t))_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
is bounded in
L^{2}
and thus converges in
L^{2}
and almost surely.
We denote the limit by
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
and view it as the limit of the series
on the right-hand side of (
4.9
).
This is justified by the following argument.
For any subset
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, finite or infinite, since for any
u\in\mathcal{J}
there is a unique
j\in\mathds{N}

with
u=u_{j}
and again since martingale increments are uncorrelated, we have

\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{J}}\chi_{u}(t-S(u))\bigg|^{2}\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{J}}\Var[\chi](t-S(u))\bigg].

From this and the Cauchy criterion,
on the one hand, we infer the unconditional convergence in
L^{2}
of the series in (
4.9
),
thereby justifying to write
Z_{t}^{\chi}
for the limit.
On the other hand, we conclude the convergence of
M_{\mathcal{I}_{n}}(t)
to
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
.
Since convergence in
L^{2}
implies convergence in
L^{1}
,

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
is centered.
Using this and again the convergence in
L^{2}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\Var[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi})^{2}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}[M_{n}(t)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\chi_{u}^{2}(t-S(u))\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\chi_{u}^{2}(t-S(u))\bigg],

i.e., (
4.10
) holds.
Finally, (A
5
) implies (
4.8
)
since, for any
t\in\mathds{R}
, by (
4.6
) and the subsequent arguments,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\Var[\chi](t-S(u))\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}](t-S(u))\bigg]

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha t}\int\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}](t-x)e^{-\alpha(t-x)}\,\mathsf{U}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\leq Ce^{\alpha t}

where as before
\mathsf{U}
is the renewal measure of the associated random walk
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}

and
C>0
is some finite constant.
∎

4.3
Matrix notation

For any
s\in\mathds{R}
and
\gamma\in\mathds{C}
we define the following lower triangular
k\times k
matrix

\displaystyle\exp(\gamma,s,k)\vcentcolon=e^{\gamma s}\times\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\dots\qquad&0\\
s&1&0&\dots\qquad&0\\
s^{2}&2s&1&\dots\qquad&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\qquad&\vdots\\
s^{k-1}\quad&{k-1\choose 1}s^{k-2}\quad&{k-1\choose 2}s^{k-3}\quad&\dots\qquad&1\end{pmatrix}.

(4.15)

The
(i,j)^{\mathrm{th}}
entry of the matrix is
e^{\gamma s}\binom{i-1}{j-1}s^{i-j}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k
,
where
\binom{i-1}{j-1}=0
for
j>i
should be recalled.
Matrices of this form will be very useful since they simplify the notation
and allow us to deal with polynomial terms with relative ease.
Indeed, for any
s,t\in\mathds{R}
and
\gamma\in\mathds{C}
,

\exp(\gamma,s,k)\cdot\exp(\gamma,t,k)=\exp(\gamma,s+t,k).

This can be seen from elementary but tedious calculations.
Alternatively, notice that

\displaystyle\exp(\gamma,s,k)=\exp(sJ_{\gamma,k})

where the matrix
J_{\gamma,k}
is defined by

J_{\gamma,k}\vcentcolon=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&&\\
1&\ddots&\mbox{0}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
\mbox{0}&&k\!-\!1&\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}}\exp(\gamma,x,k)=J_{\gamma,k}\exp(\gamma,x,k).

(4.16)

With
\|\cdot\|
denoting the operator norm and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{HS}}

denoting the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, the following (crude) bound holds
for every
\delta>0
:

\displaystyle\|\exp(\gamma,s,k)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\exp(\gamma,s,k)\|_{\mathsf{HS}}\leq C^{\prime}(1+|s|)^{k-1}e^{\mathrm{Re}(\gamma)s}\leq Ce^{\mathrm{Re}(\gamma)s+\delta|s|}

(4.17)

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending on
k
only and another constant
C>0

depending on
k
and
\delta>0
.
For a vector
x
, we write
x^{\mathsf{T}}
for its transpose.
Further, we write
\mathsf{e}_{1},\mathsf{e}_{2},\ldots
for the canonical base vectors in Euclidean space.
Here, for ease of notation, we are slightly sloppy as we do not specify the dimension of that space
(formally, all Euclidean spaces may be embedded into an appropriate infinite-dimensional space such as
\ell^{2}
).
Then, for instance,

\exp(\gamma,s,k)\cdot\mathsf{e}_{1}=e^{\gamma s}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
s\\
s^{2}\\
\vdots\\
s^{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Throughout the paper, for
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>\vartheta
,
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
and
k\in\mathds{N}
,
we denote by
Z_{n}(\lambda,k)
the following random matrix

Z_{n}(\lambda,k)\vcentcolon=\sum_{|u|=n}\exp(\lambda,-S(u),k).

(4.18)

We set
Z_{n}(\lambda)\vcentcolon=Z_{n}(\lambda,1)
for
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)\geq\vartheta
.
In particular,
\mu(\theta)=\mathds{E}[Z_{1}(\theta)]

and (A
2.8
) becomes
\mathds{E}[Z_{1}(\vartheta)]<\infty
.

5
Nerman’s martingales as general branching processes

Nerman’s martingale and its complex counterparts are crucial for the paper
as they constitute the building blocks for the asymptotic expansion of
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}
.
In the present section, we demonstrate how these martingales can be represented in terms
of Crump-Mode-Jagers processes and which characteristics come into play.

Suppose that (A
1
) holds and that
\mathcal{L}\mu(\vartheta)<\infty

for some
0<\vartheta<\alpha
. (Notice that the last condition is implied by (A
2.8
).)
Further, let
\lambda\in\mathds{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>\vartheta
be a root of multiplicity
k=k(\lambda)\in\mathds{N}
of the mapping
z\mapsto\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\lambda X_{j}}\bigg]=1,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu^{(l)}(\lambda)=(-1)^{l}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}^{l}e^{-\lambda X_{j}}\bigg]=0\quad\text{for }l=1,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\leq C\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{1 \leq i,j \leq N}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{[-1,X_{j}+1)}(t)(1+|t-X_{j}|)^{k-1}e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}(t-X_{j})}\bigg]e^{-\alpha t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}

For

\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t}\vcentcolon=\sigma(\{A\cap\{S(u)\leq t\}:u\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and }A\in\mathcal{G}_{u}\}).

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that (A
1
) holds

\mathcal{L}\mu(\vartheta)<\infty

Let
\lambda\in\mathds{C}

\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>\vartheta

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda,k)\vcentcolon=\exp({\lambda},-t,k)\cdot\mathcal{Z}^{\phi_{\lambda}}_{t},\qquad t\in\mathds{R}

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda,k)=I_{k}\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)+\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{\lambda,[-t,1)}}_{0}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\quad\text{a.\,s.}

\displaystyle m^{\phi_{\lambda}}_{t}=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\exp(\lambda,t,k),\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

Remark 5.3

It is worth mentioning that for

and the matrix-valued characteristic

obtained by taking the upper left

\displaystyle\phi_{\lambda,l}(t)

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda,l)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},l)\quad\text{a.\,s.}

In particular, if the above conditions hold with

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

(A
1
), (A
4
) and (A
5
) entail that, by Proposition

,
\mathcal{Z}^{\phi_{\lambda}}
is well-defined as an unconditional limit in

and that

is integrable for any

.

We boldly write

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle({\lambda},-t,k)\mathcal{Z}^{\phi_{\lambda}}_{t}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\mathds{1}_{[0,X_{u,j})}(t-S(u))\exp({\lambda,-S(uj)},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(uj)>t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(uj)},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\big(\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{S(uj)\leq t\}}\big)\exp({\lambda,-S(uj)},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(uj)},k)-\sum_{|u|\geq 1}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)

\displaystyle=I_{k}\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\big(Z_{1}(\lambda,k)\circ\theta_{u}-I_{k}\big),

(5.8)

where the rearrangements of the infinite series in the last two lines are justified by the fact that there are only finitely many non-zero terms almost surely.
Next, note that for any
t\in\mathds{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}\big\|\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\big\|\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\big\|\exp({\lambda,-X_{u,j}},k)\big\|+1\bigg)\bigg]<\infty.

(5.9)

Indeed, by (
4.17
),
the expectation in (

) can be bounded by a finite, deterministic constant times

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}e^{-\vartheta S(u)}\big(Z_{1}(\vartheta)\circ\theta_{u}+1\big)\bigg]\leq\big(\mathcal{L}\mu(\vartheta)\!+\!1\big)\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}e^{-\vartheta S(u)}\bigg]<\infty,

where we have used the independence between
S(u)
and
\xi_{u}
.
The finiteness of the last expectation follows from the many-to-one lemma
(Formula (
4.2
)), namely,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}e^{-\vartheta S(u)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}\bigg[\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\{S_{n}\leq t\}}e^{(\alpha-\vartheta)S_{n}}\bigg]\leq e^{(\alpha-\vartheta)t}\mathsf{U}([0,t])<\infty.

Further,
(W_{t}(\lambda,k))_{t\geq 0}
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
In order to show the martingale property note that, for
0\leq s<t
,

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda,k)-W_{s}(\lambda,k)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{s<S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\big(Z_{1}(\lambda,k)\circ\theta_{u}-I_{k}\big),

and by (
5.9
) it suffices to show that for any
u\in\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{s<S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\big(Z_{1}(\lambda,k)\circ\theta_{u}-I_{k}\big)\big|\mathcal{F}^{W}_{s}\big]=0\quad\text{a.\,s.}

Let

In particular, for such

and hence for any

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{s<S(u)\leq t\}}\exp({\lambda,-S(u)},k)\big(Z_{1}(\lambda,k)\circ\theta_{u}-I_{k}\big)\mathds{1}_{A\cap\{S(v)\leq s\}}\big]=0,

where

The argument carries over if we take a finite intersection of sets of the type

,

The

It remains to prove (

The first identity of this equation is (

Further, from the calculation leading towards (

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda,k)

5.2
Convergence of Nerman’s martingales

The following lemma implies Theorem

.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose that (A
1
) through (A
3
) hold
and let
\lambda

) with multiplicity

\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>\frac{\alpha}{2}

Then the process

where the series above converges unconditionally in

.
In particular, for every
0\leq j\leq k\!-\!1

the martingale
(W_{r}^{(j)}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
converges a. s. and in

.

Proof.

Fix

. It suffices to show the corresponding result for the martingale

.
By Lemma
5.1
the centered characteristics

x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,1)}y
,
x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}y
and
x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,[-t,1)}y

satisfy (A
5
) and (A
6
).
In particular, by Lemma
4.1
,
the general branching processes counted with these characteristics are well-defined as unconditional limits in
L^{2}
,
and, for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,-S(u),k)Y_{u}y=\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,1)}y}_{0}=\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}y}_{0}+\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,[-t,1)}y}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)>t\}}x^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,-S(u),k)Y_{u}y+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathds{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}x^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,-S(u),k)Y_{u}y.

Taking into account that, for any

,
t\geq-1
,

\displaystyle\Var[x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}y](s)\leq\Var[x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,1)}y](s),

and applying the identity (
4.10

the dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathds{E}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}y}_{0}\Big|^{2}\Big]=0.

In particular, in view of (

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}W_{t}(\lambda,k)y=x^{\mathsf{T}}y+\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,1)}y}_{0}-\mathcal{Z}^{x^{\mathsf{T}}\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}y}_{0}

converges in

as

.
∎

Suppose now that the martingale

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

By uniform integrability,

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that (A

In particular, for

Proof.

Note that for any

t\geq 0

\displaystyle\exp(\lambda,-t,k)\chi_{\lambda}(s)=\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}(s-t).

In particular,

\displaystyle\exp(\lambda,-t,k)\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{\lambda}}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{\lambda,(-\infty,-t)}}_{0},

6
Proofs of the main results

In this section we provide a proof of our main Theorem
2.15
.
We begin by outlining the main ideas and steps.

The basic step is to decompose a given general branching process
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}=H_{\Lambda}(t)+H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}.

into
H_{\Lambda}(t)
and
H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)
, see (
2.21
) and (
2.22
),
the leading terms in the expansion,
plus two general branching processes
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
,
the first one with mean roughly of the order
o(e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t})
as
t\to\pm\infty

and the second one with centered characteristic, i.e.,
\mathds{E}[\chi(t)]=0
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.

General branching processes with centered characteristics are studied in Section
6.1
.
Theorem
6.3
provides the fluctuations of
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}

for a centered characteristic
\chi
.
There are two different cases of interest.
First, when
\chi
satisfies (A
5
)
and second when
\int_{0}^{t}\Var[\chi(x)]e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\sim ct^{\theta}
for some
\theta\geq 0
and
c>0
.
In both cases,
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
, appropriately rescaled, is asymptotically normal.
The main tools to prove this are the martingale central limit theorem and Nerman’s strong law of large numbers
for supercritical general branching processes.
The second case requires the additional auxiliary Lemma
6.1

Characteristics such that the corresponding general branching process has mean function roughly of the order

o(e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t})
as
t\to\pm\infty
are treated in Section
6.3

Theorem
6.6

Section
6.2
provides a connection between the cases
studied in Sections
6.1
and
6.3
.
Roughly speaking, Lemma
6.4
enables us to rewrite the process
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{f}

for a deterministic characteristic

in the form

\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}+m^{f}_{t},

for an appropriately chosen centered characteristic
\chi_{f}

This enables us to reduce the case of general branching processes with mean function roughly of the order

In Section

We investigate the asymptotic behavior of the general branching process

In the first step, we prove convergence of Nerman’s martingales at complex parameters.

Lemma 6.1

For any

t\in\mathds{G}

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{t^{\theta+1}}\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\theta}\bigg(\frac{\mathds{E}[N(\{0\})]}{t}+\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{t^{\theta+1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}e^{\alpha(t-n)}(t-n)^{\theta}\mathds{E}[N((n,n+1])]\bigg)

is bounded for

.
It remains to show (

).
To this end, first assume that

In particular,

if

First notice that for any fixed

Hence, almost surely, the limiting behavior of

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha t}t^{-\theta-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{I}:\\
r<S(u)\leq t\end{subarray}}e^{\alpha(t\!-\!S(u))}f(t-S(u)).

(6.3)

Now first consider the lattice case and notice that by

for given

Next, we turn to the non-lattice case

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{t^{\theta+1}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{I}:\\

\displaystyle~=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\alpha t_{n}}}{t_{n}^{\theta+1}}\bigg(\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{I}:\\

\displaystyle~\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\alpha t_{n}}}{t_{n}^{\theta+1}}\bigg(\delta\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{I}:\\
S(u)\leq t_{n}\!-\!T\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!e^{\alpha(t_{n}\!-\!S(u))}(t_{n}-S(u))^{\theta}+\|\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}]\|_{[0,T]}\cdot N((t_{n}-T,t_{n}])\bigg)

\displaystyle~\leq\frac{\delta W}{\beta(\theta+1)}\qquad\text{a.\,s.}

by Lemma
6.1
with
f(t)=t^{\theta}
,
t\geq 0

and the fact that
e^{-\alpha t_{n}}N((t_{n}-T,t_{n}])
converges a. s. by
[
39
, Theorem 5.4]
in the non-lattice case
and by
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
in the lattice case.
Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary, we conclude that the limit is zero and, therefore, (
6.1
) holds true
in both cases.

It remains to justify that the convergence is stable
and that limiting random variable
\mathcal{N}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}
.
Although, this is not stated explicitly in
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
,
it follows from the proof of the preceding Lemma 3.1 of
[
18
]
, cf. Eq. (3.15) there,
that is, for any
E\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Big[e^{\mathrm{i}\theta M_{k_{n}}(t_{n})}\mathds{1}_{E}\Big]\to\mathds{E}\Big[e^{-W\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{E}\Big]

for every
\theta\in\mathds{R}
.
The latter is equivalent, by a standard approximation argument, to say that for any
\mathcal{F}
-measurable random variable
Y

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Big[e^{\mathrm{i}\theta M_{k_{n}}(t_{n})}e^{\mathrm{i}\eta Y}\Big]\to\mathds{E}\Big[e^{-W\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\eta Y}\Big]=\mathds{E}\Big[e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\sqrt{W}\mathcal{N}}e^{\mathrm{i}\eta Y}\Big]

for a standard normal variable
\mathcal{N}
independent of
(W,Y)
.
This also implies the stable convergence by
[
1
, Proposition 1]
.

6.2
Deterministic characteristics

Let
f
be a deterministic characteristic, i.e.,
a càdlàg function
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
.
We investigate the behavior of
\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}
as
t\to\infty

by means of an auxiliary centered random characteristic
\chi_{f}
defined by

\chi_{f}(t)\vcentcolon=f*\xi*V(t)-f*\mu*V(t)=m^{f}*\xi(t)-m^{f}*\mu(t),

(6.14)

where
V(\cdot)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{*n}(\cdot)=\mathds{E}[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\delta_{S(u)}(\cdot)]
and
*
denotes
Lebesgue-Stieltjes convolution.
For instance, for every
t\in\mathds{R}
,

f*V(t)=\int f(t-x)\,V(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}f(t-S(u))\bigg]=m_{t}^{f}

if the integrals are well-defined.
However, the latter is not guaranteed a priori.
The following lemma provides a sufficient condition along with an important connection
between
\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Assume that (A
1
) holds.
Let
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
be a deterministic càdlàg function such that
t\mapsto f(t)e^{-\alpha t}
is directly Riemann integrable.

(a)

The characteristic
\chi_{f}
given by (
6.14
) is well-defined and has almost surely càdlàg paths.

(b)

If (A
3
) holds and
the function
t\mapsto m_{t}^{f}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}(1+t^{2})
is bounded,
then the characteristic
\chi_{f}
satisfies (A
5
) and (A
6
).

(c)

If (A
3
) holds and
the function
t\mapsto m_{t}^{f}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}(1+t^{2})
is bounded, then for any
t\in\mathds{R}
,
\mathds{E}[\chi_{f}(t)]=0
and

\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}-m^{f}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}\qquad\text{a.\,s.\hskip 10.22217ptfor all }t\in\mathds{R}.

(6.15)

(d)

If
f
is supported on
[0,\infty)
, i.e., if
f(t)=0
for all
t<0
, then (
6.15
) also holds.

Proof.

A function
g:R\to\mathds{R}
is directly Riemann integrable if and only if
g_{+}
and
g_{-}
, the positive and negative part of
g
, respectively, are.
Hence, if
t\mapsto f(t)e^{-\alpha t}
is directly Riemann integrable, then so is
t\mapsto|f(t)|e^{-\alpha t}
.

(a)
In order to see that
\chi_{f}
is well-defined, it suffices to check that
|f|*\mu*V(t)
is finite for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.
Indeed, as
\mu*V\leq\delta_{0}+\mu*V=V
, putting
g(t)\vcentcolon=|f(t)|e^{-\alpha t}
we obtain

\displaystyle|f|*\mu*V(t)\leq|f|*V(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=n}|f|(t-S(u))\bigg]

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=n}e^{-\alpha S(u)}g(t-S(u))\bigg]=e^{\alpha t}g*\mathsf{U}(t)<\infty,

(6.16)

where we have used the many-to-one formula (
4.2
) in the next-to-last step
and the direct Riemann integrability of
g
in combination with (
4.4
) in the last.
To prove that
\chi_{f}
has càdlàg paths almost surely,
it suffices to show that
f^{*}*\mu*V
is finite.
This is justified by the fact that
f
has càdlàg paths together with the dominated convergence theorem.
Since
f^{*}*\mu*V\leq f^{*}*V
, we have to check that the latter is finite.
Further,
e^{-\alpha t}f^{*}(t)\leq e^{\alpha}g^{*}(t)
.
Therefore, by a calculation analogous to (
6.16
),
it is enough to show that
g^{*}*\mathsf{U}
is finite,
which, in view of (
4.4
),
is true if
g^{*}
is directly Riemann integrable.
This however follows from the converse part of Proposition
2.6
(a)
since
g
is directly Riemann integrable.

(b) By Proposition
2.6
(c), it suffices to show that

\int\mathds{E}\big[(\chi_{f}^{*})^{2}\big](x)e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}<\infty,

(6.17)

To this end, note that, since
\chi_{f}=m^{f}*\xi-m^{f}*\mu
,
we have, for any
x\in\mathds{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[|\chi^{*}_{f}(x)|^{2}\big]\leq 2\mathds{E}\Big[\sup_{|t-x|\leq 1}|m^{f}\!*\xi(t)|^{2}\Big]+2\!\!\!\sup_{|t-x|\leq 1}|m^{f}\!*\mu(t)|^{2}\leq 4\mathds{E}\Big[\sup_{|t-x|\leq 1}|m^{f}\!*\xi(t)|^{2}\Big],

where we have used Jensen’s inequality.
For
|t-x|\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1+(x-X_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=1+(t-X_{j}+x-t)^{2}\leq 1+2(t-X_{j})^{2}+2(x-t)^{2}\leq 3+2(t-X_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3(1+(t-X_{j})^{2})

for
j=1,\ldots,N
, and, therefore, with
C\vcentcolon=3e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sup_{t\in\mathds{R}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}(1+t^{2})|m_{t}^{f}|
,

\displaystyle|m^{f}\!*\xi(t)|^{2}=\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{t-X_{j}}^{f}\bigg|^{2}\leq C^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}(x-X_{i})}}{1+(x-X_{i})^{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}(x-X_{j})}}{1+(x-X_{j})^{2}}.

Thus, since

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{1+(x-X_{i})^{2}}\frac{1}{1+(x-X_{j})^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\leq\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}}{1+(x-X_{i})^{2}}=\pi,

we conclude

\displaystyle\int\mathds{E}\big[|\chi^{*}_{f}(x)|^{2}\big]e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\leq 4\pi C^{2}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}X_{i}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}X_{j}}\bigg]<\infty

from assumption
(A
3
).

(c) By part (a),
|f|*\mu*V(t)
is finite for all
t\in\mathds{R}
and, hence,

\mathds{E}[\chi_{f}](t)=\mathds{E}[f*\xi*V(t)]-f*\mu*V(t)=f*\mu*V(t)-f*\mu*V(t)=0
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.
Further, since
f
satisfies (A
4
) by assumption
and trivially also (A
5
),
Proposition
2.2
implies that
\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}

converges in
L^{1}
for every
t\in\mathds{R}
.
The characteristic
\chi_{f}
on the other hand satisfies (A
5
) by part (b)
and trivially also (A
4
) because it is centered.
Thus, Proposition
2.2
yields that also
the series defining
\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}
converges unconditionally in
L^{1}
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.

In particular, for any
n\in\mathds{N}
, the infinite series

\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}\chi_{f,u}(t-S(u))

also converges unconditionally in
L^{1}
and so is well-defined and converges to
\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}
as
n\to\infty
in
L^{1}
.
Moreover, due to the fact that
f
is deterministic,
\chi_{f}
is
\xi
-measurable.
For
u\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
\chi_{f,u}(t)=f*\xi_{u}*V(t)-f*\mu*V(t)
.
Using this and
V=\delta_{0}+\mu*V
, we infer

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}\chi_{f,u}(t-S(u))

\displaystyle~=\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}\big(f*\xi_{u}*V(t-S(u))-f*\mu*V(t-S(u))\big)

\displaystyle~=\sum_{1\leq|u|\leq n+1}\!f*V(t-S(u))-\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}f*\mu*V(t-S(u))

\displaystyle~=\sum_{1\leq|u|\leq n}\!f(t-S(u))-f*\mu*V(t)+\sum_{|u|=n+1}f*V(t-S(u))

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}\!f(t-S(u))-f*V(t)+\sum_{|u|=n+1}f*V(t-S(u)).

In the last line,
f*V(t)=m_{t}^{f}
.
The manipulations in the above chain of equalities are justified
by
the fact that

\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n+1}|f*V(t-S(u))|\bigg]<\infty.

The finiteness of the above expectation follows from

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=k}\!|f*V(t-S(u))|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|=k}\!|f|*V(t-S(u))\bigg]=\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{|u|\geq k}\!|f|(t-S(u))\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\!|f|(t-S(u))\bigg]=m^{|f|}_{t}<\infty.

The dominated convergence theorem yields

\sum_{|u|=n+1}f*V(t-S(u))\to 0\quad\text{and}\sum_{0\leq|u|\leq n}\!f(t-S(u))\to\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}

as
n\to\infty

in
L^{1}
.

(d) The last calculation carries over if
f(t)=0
for all
t<0
.
Indeed, the latter condition implies
\chi_{f}(t)=0
for all
t<0

and hence with probability one,
all sums have only finitely many non-vanishing terms
(since only finitely many individuals are born before any fixed time almost surely
by
[
25
, Theorem 6.2.3]
).
∎

Lemma
6.4
has the following corollary.

Corollary 6.5
.

If the assumptions of Lemma
6.4
(b) are satisfied,
then, for every
t\in\mathds{R}
,

\Var[\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}]=m^{\chi_{f}^{2}}_{t}<\infty.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
, we have

\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}=\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}+m^{f}_{t}
where
\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}
is centered
and

e^{-\alpha t}\Var[\chi_{f}](t)=e^{-\alpha t}\mathds{E}[\chi_{f}^{2}](t)

is directly Riemann integrable.
Using
\Var[\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}]=\Var[\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}+m^{f}_{t}]=\Var[\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t}]=\mathds{E}[(\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t})^{2}]

we infer with the help of (
4.10
) that

\mathds{E}[(\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}}_{t})^{2}]=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{\chi_{f}^{2}}_{t}]\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathds{R}.

We now treat the case where

Suppose that (A
1
) through (A
3
) hold,

\varphi

(i).

Similarly, putting

\displaystyle\psi_{\partial\Lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathds{E}[\phi_{\lambda}(t)]\mathsf{e}_{1},

again by Lemma
5.2
, we obtain,
for any
t\in\mathds{R}
,

\displaystyle m^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}]=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\phi_{\lambda}}]\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp{(\lambda,t,k(\lambda))}\mathsf{e}_{1}=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)H_{\partial\Lambda}(t),

(6.27)

where the definition of
H_{\partial\Lambda}
should be recalled from (
2.22
).
In view of Remark
2.16
, we have
\overline{\vec{a}_{\lambda}}=\vec{a}_{\overline{\lambda}}
,

\overline{\phi_{\lambda}}=\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}
and
\overline{\chi_{\lambda}}=\chi_{\overline{\lambda}}
.
We thus conclude that both characteristics
\psi_{\Lambda}
and
\psi_{\partial\Lambda}
are in fact real-valued.

Now write

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\Lambda}}+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho}=H_{\Lambda}(t)+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho},

(6.28)

where
\varrho\vcentcolon=\varphi-\psi_{\Lambda}-\psi_{\partial\Lambda}
.
Next, since
\psi_{\partial\Lambda}
is deterministic, we may consider
the associated centered characteristic
\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}

defined in (
6.14
), namely,

\displaystyle\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}(t)

\displaystyle=\xi*m^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}-\mu*m^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp{(\lambda,t-X_{j},k(\lambda))}\mathsf{e}_{1}\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t-X_{j})-\mu*m^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}.

If we set now

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-X_{j},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\vcentcolon=}~-\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-X_{j},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg],

\displaystyle\chi(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\psi_{\lambda}(t)

and

\displaystyle\phi_{\partial\Lambda}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-X_{j},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\mathds{1}_{[0,X_{j})}(t)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\vcentcolon=}~-\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-X_{j},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\mathds{1}_{[0,X_{j})}(t)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi_{\lambda}(t)\mathsf{e}_{1}-\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\mathds{E}\big[\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi_{\lambda}(t)\mathsf{e}_{1}\big].

we get the following decomposition

\displaystyle\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}(t)=\chi(t)-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}(t).

(6.29)

The fact that all the expectations above are finite and thus the characteristics are well-defined follows from (A
2.8
) and (A
7
).
Note also that, for every
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-X_{i},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\lambda(t-X_{i})}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{\lambda,l}(t-X_{i})^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\lambda(t-X_{i})}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}a_{\lambda,l}\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{l}{j}t^{j}(-X_{i})^{l-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}\sum_{l=j}^{k(\lambda)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\lambda X_{i}}a_{\lambda,l}\binom{l}{j}(-X_{i})^{l-j}t^{j}e^{\lambda t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}R_{\lambda,j}t^{j}e^{\lambda t}

by (
2.23
).
With this at hand, we infer

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}(t)=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}(R_{\lambda,l}-\mathds{E}[R_{\lambda,l}])t^{l}e^{\lambda t},\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

(6.30)

Using Lemma
6.4
(d) with
f=\psi_{\partial\Lambda}
(note here that such
f
fulfills the assumptions as for any
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
the characteristic
\phi_{\lambda}
vanishes on
(-\infty,0)
and satisfies (A
4
) by Lemma
5.1
), we get that

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}-m_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}}

and, hence,
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}}+m_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi_{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}}+H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)
for
t\geq 0
.
Therefore, from (
6.28
) and (
6.29
) we obtain the following decomposition,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}=H_{\Lambda}(t)+H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}+\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi},\quad t\geq 0.

(6.31)

It suffices to prove
the limit theorem for
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}
.
To this end, we invoke Theorem
6.6
for the first process
and Theorem
6.3
(ii) for the second as both characteristics are real-valued
as a consequence of Remark
2.16
.
We begin with
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}
and first notice that,
in view of Lemma
5.1
and Remark
2.5
,
the characteristic
\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}
has càdlàg paths
and satisfies (A
4
), (A
5
) and (A
6
).
Moreover, using the fact that
\phi_{\partial\Lambda}
is centered in combination with (
6.26
), (
6.27
) and
(
2.20
)
we infer

\displaystyle m^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=m^{\varrho}_{t}=m^{\varphi-\psi_{\Lambda}-\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}=m_{t}^{\varphi}-m_{t}^{\psi_{\Lambda}}-m_{t}^{\psi_{\partial\Lambda}}

(6.32)

\displaystyle=m_{t}^{\varphi}-\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\big(\mathds{E}\big[H_{\Lambda}(t)\big]+H_{\partial\Lambda}(t)\big)=r(t).

We may thus apply Theorem
6.6
to conclude that

e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\mathcal{Z}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}_{t}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{st\ }$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{W}{\beta}}\mathcal{N},

(6.33)

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int v(x)e^{-\alpha x}\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}),

(6.34)

and
v(t)\vcentcolon=\Var\big[\varrho(t)-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}(t)+r*\xi(t)\big]
.
The function
v
can be further simplified in the following way

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=\Var\big[\varphi(t)-\psi_{\Lambda}(t)-\psi_{\partial\Lambda}(t)-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}(t)+r*\xi(t)\big]

\displaystyle=\Var\bigg[\varphi(t)-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}(\phi_{\lambda}(t)+\chi_{\lambda}(t))\mathsf{e}_{1}-\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi_{\lambda}(t)\mathsf{e}_{1}+r*\xi(t)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\Var\bigg[\varphi(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigg(-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,X_{j})}(t)\exp({\lambda,t-X_{j}},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\Var\bigg[}-\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{[0,X_{j})}(t)\exp({\lambda,t-X_{j}},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}+r(t-X_{j})\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\Var\bigg[\varphi(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigg(-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp({\lambda,t-X_{j}},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\Var\bigg[}-\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp({\lambda,t-X_{j}},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}+m^{\varphi}(t-X_{j})\bigg)\bigg].

This proves the theorem under the assumption that

\rho_{l}=0
for all
l\geq 0
. Indeed, in this case

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}=0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{a}_{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp({\lambda,t-X_{j}},k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}
is a. s. constant for any
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda,t\in\mathds{G}
and
(
2.26
) follows.

Now, combining (
6.31
)
and (
6.33
), we arrive at (
ii
) if
\sigma^{2}>0
.
However, if
\sigma^{2}=0
, then for all
t\in\mathds{G}
,

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}
equals its expectation,
which is
r(t)
a. s., as shown by (
6.32
). This establishes (i).

It remains to prove the theorem in the case where
\rho_{l}>0
for some
l\geq 0
.
First notice that by (
6.33
),
the already established central limit theorem for
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}
,
we have
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varrho-\phi_{\partial\Lambda}}=o(t^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t})

as
t\to\infty
in probability. Let
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
be maximal with
\rho_{n}>0
.
We show that the characteristic
\chi
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6.3
(ii)
with
\theta=2n
.
Observe that, for any
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
,
l\leq k(\lambda)-1
and some constant
C_{\lambda,\vec{a}_{\lambda}}
depending on
\lambda,l
and
\vec{a}_{\lambda}
,

\displaystyle|R_{\lambda,l}|\leq C_{\lambda,\vec{a}_{\lambda}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(1+X_{j}^{k(\lambda)-1})e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}X_{j}}.

In view of assumption (A
3
) the random variable
R_{\lambda,l}
is square integrable.
Setting
R_{\lambda,l}\vcentcolon=0
for
l\geq k(\lambda)
,
we write

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\cdot\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(R_{\lambda,l}-\mathds{E}[R_{\lambda,l}])t^{l}e^{\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)t}.

An application on Lemma
6.7
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{2n+1}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\chi^{2}(x)]e^{-\alpha x}\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\to\frac{\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\Var[R_{\lambda,n}]}{2n+1}=\frac{\rho^{2}_{n}}{2n+1}

and

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[|\chi(t)|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|\chi(t)|^{2}>\varepsilon t^{2n+1}e^{\alpha t}\}}\big]=o(t^{2n}e^{\alpha t})\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,\;t\in\mathds{G}.

Finally, by Theorem
6.3
(ii),

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\rho^{2}_{n}t^{2n+1}}{2n+1}e^{\alpha t}\Big)^{\!-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\chi}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{st\ }$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}\sqrt{\tfrac{W}{\beta}}\mathcal{N},

which finishes the proof.

Remark 6.8
.

The proofs of Theorems
2.15
and
6.6
reveal that for any characteristic
\varphi

satisfying the assumptions (A
4
) through (A
6
), there exists a decomposition
\varphi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}+\varphi_{3}
, where each term also satisfies (A
4
) through (A
6
). Furthermore, for the corresponding Crump-Mode-Jagers processes, it is established that
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi_{1}}_{t}=H(t)
for
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi_{2}}_{t}
is centered, and
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi_{3}}_{t}
is a deterministic function equal to
r(t)
. In particular, for the characteristic
\tilde{\varphi}\vcentcolon=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{3}
, one obtains that for
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{Z}^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{t}=H(t)+r(t)
almost surely, indicating the lack of Gaussian fluctuations as

goes to infinity.

Proof of Corollary
2.20
.

First observe that linear combinations as well as the translations
\varphi(\cdot)\mapsto\varphi(\cdot-s)

preserve the conditions (A
4
), (A
5
) and (A
6
).
Moreover, for the characteristic
\psi(t)\vcentcolon=\varphi(t-s)

the mean function
m^{\psi}_{t}
has expansion
(
2.20
) with coefficients given by vectors

\big({\exp(\lambda,-s,k(\lambda))}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{a}_{\lambda}\big)
.
According to the Cramér–Wold device the convergence in distribution of

t^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\big(\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t-s_{1}}-H(t-s_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t-s_{n}}-H(t-s_{n})\big)

for all choices

,
and the latter convergence follows from Theorem

.
The covariance can be obtained by the polarization identity applied to the variance
and the fact that

.

7
Asymptotic expansion of the mean

In this section we are concerned with
the asymptotic expansion of the mean
m_{t}^{\varphi}=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}]

of a supercritical general branching process
(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi})_{t\geq 0}
as
t\to\infty
. Throughout the section, we assume that (A
1
) and
(A
2.8
) hold.
We fix some notation throughout the section.
By
\theta
we denote a parameter from
(0,\frac{\alpha}{2})
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(\theta)<\infty\text{ and }\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\neq 1\quad\text{whenever }\theta\leq\mathrm{Re}(z)<\tfrac{\alpha}{2}.

(7.1)

Such a
\theta
exists in all particular cases considered in this section
and may sometimes be enlarged in order to ensure the validity of additional conditions.
We also fix

\gamma>\frac{\alpha}{2}
such that
\gamma<\min\{\mathrm{Re}(\lambda):\lambda\in\Lambda\}
.

It’s worth noting that the mean
m^{\varphi}_{t}
depends on the underlying point process
\xi
only through its intensity measure
\mu
. Therefore, when analyzing
m^{\varphi}_{t}
, without loss of generality, we can assume that, additionally to (A
1
) and
(A
2.8
), the condition (A
3
) holds true. Otherwise, we can always replace
\xi
with the Poisson point process whose intensity measure is
\mu
.

In the non-lattice case we work with
the corresponding bilateral Laplace transforms whereas in the lattice case,
we use generating functions.

7.1
The lattice case

In the present subsection,
we assume that
\mu
is concentrated on the lattice
\mathds{Z}
(and not on a smaller lattice).
We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu(\{k\})z^{k}=\int z^{x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

(7.2)

for all
z\in\mathds{C}
for which the series is absolutely convergent. In particular,
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=\mathcal{G}\mu(e^{-z})
.
Note that, due to assumption (A
2.8
),
\mathcal{G}\mu(e^{-\vartheta})<\infty
and hence
the power series (
7.2
)
defines a holomorphic function on
\{|z|<e^{-\vartheta}\}
.
Further, by slightly increasing the value of
\vartheta
if necessary, we may assume
without loss of generality that there are only finitely many solutions of the equation
\mathcal{G}\mu(z)=1

in the disc
\{|z|<e^{-\vartheta}\}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume that (A
1
) and (A
2.8
) hold.
Let
\theta\in(\vartheta,\frac{\alpha}{2})
be such that
there are no solutions to
\mathcal{G}\mu(z)=1
in
\{z:e^{-\alpha/2}<|z|\leq e^{-\theta}\}
.
Then there are constants
b_{\lambda,l}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq},\,l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
such that,
for any characteristic
\varphi
with

\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|\mathds{E}[\varphi(n)]|(e^{-\theta n}+e^{-\alpha n})<\infty,

(7.3)

it holds that, for
t\in\mathds{Z}
,

\displaystyle m^{\varphi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}b_{\lambda,l}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\mathds{E}[\varphi(n)](t-n)^{l}e^{\lambda(t-n)}+O(e^{\theta t})&\text{as }t\to\infty\\
O(e^{\gamma t})&\text{as }t\to-\infty.\end{cases}

(7.4)

Remark 7.2
.

Defining
\vec{b}_{\lambda}\vcentcolon=(b_{\lambda,l-1})_{l=1,\ldots,k(\lambda)}
,
we may write (
7.4
) more compactly in the form

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\varphi}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\mathds{E}[\varphi(n)]{\vec{b}_{\lambda}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}+O(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\gamma t})

as
t\to\pm\infty
,
t\in\mathds{Z}
.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

For
r>0
, let
B_{r}=\{|z|<r\}
and
\partial B_{r}=\{|z|=r\}
.
Now fix
r<e^{-\alpha}
.
As
\mathcal{G}(\mu^{*l})=(\mathcal{G}\mu)^{l}
, for any
l\in\mathds{N}

and since
\mathcal{G}\mu
is holomorphic on
B_{e^{-\vartheta}}
,
we infer from Cauchy’s integral formula that

\displaystyle\mu^{*l}(\{n\})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{(\mathcal{G}\mu)^{l}(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[N(\{n\})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mu^{*l}(\{n\})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\!\frac{(\mathcal{G}\mu)^{l}(z)}{z^{n+1}}\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}}{(1-\mathcal{G}\mu(z))z^{n+1}}

where the last equality follows by Fubini’s theorem.
For
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
, let

\sum_{j=-k(\lambda)}^{-1}{b_{j}(\lambda)}{(z-e^{-\lambda})^{j}}

be the principle part of the Laurent expansion of the meromorphic function
(1-\mathcal{G}\mu(z))^{-1}
around
e^{-\lambda}
.
Then the function

\displaystyle H(z)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{1-\mathcal{G}\mu(z)}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{j=-k(\lambda)}^{-1}{b_{j}(\lambda)}{(z-e^{-\lambda})^{j}}

is holomorphic on
B_{e^{-\theta}}
.
On the other hand, for any
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{(z-e^{-\lambda})^{-d}}{z^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}=(-e^{\lambda})^{d}e^{\lambda n}{n+d-1\choose d-1}

by the residue theorem. Therefore,

\displaystyle G(z)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum_{j=-k(\lambda)}^{-1}{b_{j}(\lambda)}{(z-e^{-\lambda})^{j}}

satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{G(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}p_{\lambda}(n)e^{\lambda n}

where
p_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
, is a polynomial with complex coefficients of degree
k(\lambda)-1
.
From the analyticity of
H
, we infer

\bigg|\int\limits_{\partial B_{e^{-\theta}}}\frac{H(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}\bigg|=O(e^{\theta n})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

which in turn gives

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[N(\{n\})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{G(z)+H(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}+O(e^{\theta n})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty

for some
\vec{b}_{\lambda}=\sum_{l=1}^{k(\lambda)}b_{\lambda,l-1}\mathsf{e}_{l}\in\mathds{R}^{k(\lambda)}
.
In other words, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for any
n\in\mathds{Z}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathds{E}[N(\{n\})]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{n\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|\leq C(e^{\theta n}\wedge e^{\gamma n}).

(7.5)

Now we are ready to investigate
the asymptotic behavior of
m_{t}^{\varphi}
as
t\to\pm\infty
,
t\in\mathds{Z}
.
Since
m_{t}^{\varphi}=m_{t}^{\mathds{E}[\varphi]}
, we assume without loss of generality
that
\varphi=f
is a deterministic function satisfying

\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|(e^{-\theta n}+e^{-\alpha n})<\infty,

Then, for
t\in\mathds{Z}
, we have
m_{t}^{f}=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}f(n)\mathds{E}[N(\{t-n\})]
.
We write

\displaystyle\bigg|m^{f}_{t}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}f(n)\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|\bigg|\mathds{E}[N(\{t-n\})]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq n\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|

\displaystyle\hphantom{\leq}+\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|\bigg|\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}|\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq n\}}|\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-n,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|.

(7.6)

We use (
7.5
) to estimate the first sum on the right-hand side of (
7.6
) by

\displaystyle C\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|(e^{\theta(t-n)}\wedge e^{\gamma(t-n)})

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|e^{\theta(t-n)}\bigg)\wedge\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|e^{\gamma(t-n)}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\gamma t})\,C\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|(e^{-\theta n}+e^{-\alpha n}).

On the other hand,
we use (
4.17
) to conclude that for any
0<\epsilon<\alpha/2-\theta
and
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda

there is a constant
C_{\epsilon}\geq 0
such that

\|\exp(\lambda,n,k(\lambda))\|\leq C_{\epsilon}e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}n+\epsilon|n|}
.
Hence the second sum on the right-hand side of (
7.6
) is bounded by

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon}\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}|\vec{b}_{\lambda}|\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)||\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq n\}}|e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}(t-n)+\epsilon|t-n|}.

The latter sum can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)||\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq n\}}|e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}(t-n)+\epsilon|t-n|}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\mathds{N}_{0}}(t)\sum_{n>t}|f(n)|e^{(\frac{\alpha}{2}-\epsilon)(t-n)}+\mathds{1}_{\mathds{Z}\setminus\mathds{N}_{0}}(t)\sum_{n\leq t}|f(n)|e^{(\frac{\alpha}{2}+\epsilon)(t-n)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{1}_{\mathds{N}_{0}}(t)\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}|f(n)|e^{\theta(t-n)}+\mathds{1}_{\mathds{Z}\setminus\mathds{N}_{0}}(t)\sum_{n\leq t}|f(n)|e^{\alpha(t-n)}=O(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\alpha t}),

as
t\to\pm\infty
.
∎

7.2
The non-lattice case

We again work under the conditions
(A
1
) and (A
2.8
)
as in Section
7.1
,
but now we assume that
\mu
is non-lattice.

Similar to the lattice case, first we study the behavior of
\mathds{E}[N(t)]
.
This was already done in
[
31
, Theorem 3.1]
in the special case
where
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)-1
has only simple roots.
However, the proof given in the cited source can be adapted to the more general setting here.
In order to make this paper self-contained and for the reader’s convenience, we include the proof.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose that, besides (A
1
) and (A
2.8
),
the following condition holds:

\limsup_{\eta\to\infty}|\mathcal{L}\mu(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\delta+\mathrm{i}\eta)|<1

(7.7)

for some
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\vartheta]
.
Then
\Lambda_{\geq}
is finite. In fact, the function
\mathcal{L}\mu
takes the value
1

only at finitely many points in the strip
\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\delta<\mathrm{Re}(z)<\alpha
.
Then, for any root
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
of multiplicity
k(\lambda)\in\mathds{N}
,
there exist constants
c_{\lambda,l}
,
l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
such that,
for any
\theta\in(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\delta,\frac{\alpha}{2})
satisfying (
7.1
) it holds that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[N(t)]=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}e^{\lambda t}\sum_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}c_{\lambda,l}{t^{l}}+O(e^{\theta t})\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

(7.8)

Remark 7.4
.

Note that (
7.8
) can be rewritten in the form

\mathds{E}[N(t)]=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}+O(e^{\theta t})

as
t\to\infty
with
\vec{c}_{\lambda}\vcentcolon=\sum_{l=1}^{k(\lambda)}c_{\lambda,l-1}\mathsf{e}_{l}
.

Remark 7.5
.

Suppose that (A
1
) and (A
2.8
) hold
and that the intensity measure
\mu
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Then one can check using the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma that (
7.7
) holds for any
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\vartheta]
.
Hence, in this case, Lemma
7.3
applies.

Proof of Lemma
7.3
.

First, condition (
7.7
) implies that

\limsup_{\eta\to\infty}|\mathcal{L}\mu(\theta+\mathrm{i}\eta)|<1

for all
\theta\geq\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\delta
and that there are only finitely many roots
of the equation
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=1
in the strip
\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\delta\leq\mathrm{Re}(z)<\alpha
,
see Lemmas 2.1 and 2.3 in
[
31
]
(note that although the setup in
[
31
]
is slightly different,
the proofs carry over without changes).

Now let
f=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}
and recall
that
N(t)=\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{f}
, hence
V(t)\vcentcolon=\mathds{E}[N(t)]=m_{t}^{f}
for
t\in\mathds{R}
.
In analogy to the derivation of
[
31
, Eq. (3.11)]
,
we use the recursive structure of
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{f}
to obtain a renewal equation for
V(t)
as follows. We start with

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{f}=f(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{Z}_{j,t-X_{j}}^{f}

where
\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{1,t},\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{2,t}\ldots
are independent copies of
\mathcal{Z}^{f}_{t}
.
Taking expectations, then conditioning with respect to
\xi
,
the reproduction point process of the ancestor, we infer

m_{t}^{f}=f(t)+\int m_{t-x}^{f}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=f(t)+\mu*m_{t}^{f},\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

(7.9)

Our subsequent proof relies on a smoothing technique.
So let
\rho\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, we set

\textstyle\rho_{\varepsilon}(t)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho(\frac{t}{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{[0,\varepsilon]}(t),\quad t\in\mathds{R}.

Then for
f_{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=f*\rho_{\varepsilon}
(Lebesgue convolution), we have

f_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq f(t)\leq f_{\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon)

for all
t\in\mathds{R}
,
which in turn gives

m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}\leq m^{f}_{t}\leq m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t+\varepsilon}.

(7.10)

Also, one can check that
t\mapsto m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}
is a continuous function.
First, we find the asymptotic expansion of this function and then, we let
\varepsilon
tend to
0

in a controlled way while letting
t\to\infty
to deduce the asymptotic behavior of
V(t)=m_{t}^{f}
from that of
m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}
.
From the renewal equation (
7.9
) we conclude that for
\mathrm{Re}(z)>\alpha
it holds

\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\varepsilon}*m_{t}^{f})(z)=\mathcal{L}{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)+\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z),

hence,

\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\frac{\mathcal{L}{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}\quad\text{for }\mathrm{Re}(z)>\alpha.

The function

\frac{\mathcal{L}{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}=\frac{\mathcal{L}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(z)\mathcal{L}f(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}=\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon z}}{\varepsilon z^{2}(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}

defines a meromorphic extension of
\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}
on
\mathrm{Re}(z)>\vartheta
.
This function decays as
|\mathrm{Im}(z)|^{-2}
as
\mathrm{Im}(z)\to\pm\infty
and
\mathrm{Re}(z)
is constant,
hence, it is integrable along vertical lines.
Thus, for any
\tau>\alpha
, the Laplace inversion formula (see, for instance,
[
48
, Theorem 7.3 on p. 66]
) gives

\displaystyle m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}=\frac{m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t+}+m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t-}}{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\tau-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\tau+\mathrm{i}\infty}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z},\quad t>0.

To simplify notation, we assume without loss of generality that

\vartheta=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\delta
and that
\mathcal{L}\mu
is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\mathrm{Re}(z)\geq\vartheta
.
Then, for large enough
R
, an application of the residue theorem gives

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\tau-\mathrm{i}R}^{\tau+\mathrm{i}R}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\Res_{z=\lambda}\big(e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\big)+\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}R}^{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}R}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}R}^{\tau+\mathrm{i}R}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}-\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}R}^{\tau-\mathrm{i}R}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}.

Here,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int\limits_{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}R}^{\tau+\mathrm{i}R}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq e^{t\tau}\int\limits_{\vartheta}^{\tau}\Big|\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon(x+\mathrm{i}R)}}{\varepsilon(x+\mathrm{i}R)^{2}(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(x+\mathrm{i}R))}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{t\tau}\int\limits_{\vartheta}^{\tau}\Big|\frac{1}{\varepsilon(x+\mathrm{i}R)^{2}}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\underset{R\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

for some constant
C
that depends only on
\mu
.
Here we used the fact that, by Lemma 2.1 of
[
31
]
,
\mathcal{L}\mu(x+\mathrm{i}R)
for
x\geq\vartheta

and
R\geq R_{0}
is uniformly bounded away from
1
for some sufficiently large
R_{0}>0
.
The same bound holds for the second horizontal integral.
Therefore, by letting
R
tend to infinity we conclude

\displaystyle m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\Res_{z=\lambda}\big(e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\big)+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}\infty}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}.

(7.11)

Next, denoting by
\{b_{j}(\lambda)\}_{j\in\mathds{Z}}
the coefficients in the Laurent expansion
of the function
(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))^{-1}
at
z=\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}

(hence, in particular,
b_{j}(\lambda)=0
for
j<-k(\lambda)
), we have

\displaystyle\Res_{z=\lambda}\big(e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\big)

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=\lambda}\Big(e^{tz}\frac{\mathcal{L}{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)}{1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z)}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda t}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,l\geq 0\\
n+l<k(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\!\!\frac{t^{l}}{l!}\frac{(\mathcal{L}f_{\varepsilon})^{(n)}(\lambda)}{n!}b_{-1-n-l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda t}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,l\geq 0\\
n+l<k(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\!\!\frac{t^{l}}{l!}\frac{\int(-x)^{n}f_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-\lambda x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}}{n!}b_{-1-n-l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda t}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,l\geq 0\\
n+l<k(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\!\!\frac{t^{l}}{l!}\frac{\int(-x)^{n}f(x)e^{-\lambda x}\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}}{n!}b_{-1-n-l}(\lambda)+\varepsilon O(e^{\alpha t})

\displaystyle=\vcentcolon e^{\lambda t}\sum_{0\leq l<k(\lambda)}c_{\lambda,l}t^{l}+\varepsilon O(e^{\alpha t}),

(7.12)

where the implicit constant depends only on
\lambda
, not on
\varepsilon
.
It remains to estimate the second term in (
7.11
).
For
\varepsilon\leq\vartheta^{-1}
, using that
|1-e^{-z}|\leq|z|\wedge 2
for
\mathrm{Re}(z)\geq 0
,
we infer

\displaystyle\bigg|\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}\infty}e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq e^{\vartheta t}\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}\infty}\bigg|\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon z}}{\varepsilon z^{2}(1-\mathcal{L}\mu(z))}\bigg|\,|\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{\vartheta t}\int\limits_{\vartheta-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\vartheta+\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{|\varepsilon z|^{-1}\wedge 1}{|z|}\,|\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}|

\displaystyle=Ce^{\vartheta t}\int\limits_{\varepsilon\vartheta-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\varepsilon\vartheta+\mathrm{i}\infty}(|z|^{-1}\wedge|z|^{-2})\,|\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{\vartheta t}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{-1}\wedge x^{-2}\wedge(\varepsilon\vartheta)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}e^{\vartheta t}(|\log\varepsilon|+1)

(7.13)

for some constant
C^{\prime}
that
depends neither on
t
nor on
\varepsilon
.
Using (
7.12
) with
t+\varepsilon
instead of
t
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\big|\Res_{z=\lambda}\big(e^{(t+\varepsilon)z}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\big)-\Res_{z=\lambda}\big(e^{tz}\mathcal{L}m^{f_{\varepsilon}}(z)\big)\big|=\varepsilon O(e^{\alpha t}),

where we used
k(\lambda)=1
for
\lambda=\alpha
, and thereupon, by (
7.11
) and (
7.13
),

m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t+\varepsilon}-m^{f_{\varepsilon}}_{t}=O(\varepsilon e^{\alpha t}+|\log\varepsilon|e^{\vartheta t}).

Setting now
\varepsilon\vcentcolon=e^{-\alpha t}
, by (
7.10
), we infer

m_{t}^{f}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}e^{\lambda t}\sum_{0\leq l\leq k(\lambda)-1}c_{\lambda,l}t^{l}+O(te^{\vartheta t}),

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we are ready to provide the asymptotic expansion for the expectation function
of a general branching process counted with a random characteristic
\varphi
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that, besides (A
1
) and (A
2.8
),
condition (
7.7
) holds.
Then
\Lambda_{\geq}
is finite and
there are constants
b_{\lambda,l}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
,
0\leq l<k(\lambda)

such that, for any
\vartheta<\theta<\frac{\alpha}{2}

fulfilling (
7.1
) and any random characteristic
\varphi
satisfying (
2.19
), we have

\displaystyle m^{\varphi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k(\lambda)-1}b_{\lambda,l}\int(t-x)^{l}e^{\lambda(t-x)}\mathds{E}[\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}+O(e^{\theta t})&\text{as }t\to\infty\\
O(e^{\gamma t})&\text{as }t\to-\infty.\end{cases}

(7.14)

Remark 7.7
.

If we set
\vec{b}_{\lambda}\vcentcolon=(b_{\lambda,l})_{l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1}
, then formula
(
7.14
) can be written in the form

m_{t}^{\varphi}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\int\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\mathds{E}[\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}+O(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\gamma t})

as
t\to\pm\infty
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume that the characteristic
\varphi=f
is a deterministic function.
By Lemma
7.3

there exist constants
c_{\lambda,l}
,
l=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
,
\theta\in(\vartheta,\frac{\alpha}{2})
and a constant
C
such that, for any
t\in\mathds{R}
,

\bigg|\mathds{E}[N(t)]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|\leq C(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\gamma t})

(7.15)

and hence for the characteristic
f(t)=\mathds{1}_{[x,\infty)}(t)=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t-x)
, we find

\bigg|m^{f}_{t}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t-x\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|\leq C(e^{\theta(t-x)}\wedge e^{\gamma(t-x)}).

Suppose now that
f\geq 0
is a càdlàg, nondecreasing function with

\displaystyle\int f(x)(e^{-\alpha x}+e^{-\vartheta x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}<\infty.

(7.16)

Then
f
is the measure-generating function of a locally finite measure
\nu
on the Borel sets of
\mathds{R}
, namely, for any
y\in\mathds{R}
,

f(y)=\nu((-\infty,y])=\int\mathds{1}_{[x,\infty)}(y)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}).

For any
t\in\mathds{R}
, by an application of Fubini’s theorem, we infer

m^{f}_{t}=\int\mathds{E}[N(t-x)]\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}).

By (
4.16
) we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}}\exp(\lambda,x,k)=J_{\lambda,k}\exp(\lambda,x,k)
.
We show that (
7.14
) holds with

\vec{b}_{\lambda}\vcentcolon=J_{\lambda,k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
.
To this end, first notice that,
as
f
fulfills (
7.16
), another application of Fubini’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\int\exp(\lambda,-x,k)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})=\int J_{\lambda,k}\exp(\lambda,-x,k)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}.

We now write

\displaystyle\bigg|m^{f}_{t}-\int\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{b}_{\lambda}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))f(x)\mathsf{e}_{1}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg|

\displaystyle~=\bigg|m^{f}_{t}-\int\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}J_{\lambda,k(\lambda)}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))f(x)\mathsf{e}_{1}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg|

\displaystyle~=\bigg|\int\Big(\mathds{E}[N(t-x)]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\Big)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\bigg|

\displaystyle~\leq\int\bigg|\mathds{E}[N(t-x)]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t-x\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle\hphantom{~\leq}~+\sum_{\lambda\in\partial\Lambda}\int\big|\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{t-x\geq 0\}}\big|\big|\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\big|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}).

For the first term, by (
7.15
), we have

\displaystyle\int\Big|\mathds{E}[N(t-x)]-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\mathds{1}_{\{t-x\geq 0\text{ or }\lambda\in\Lambda\}}\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\Big|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle~\leq C\int(e^{\theta(t-x)}\wedge e^{\gamma(t-x)})\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle~\leq C\bigg(\int e^{\theta(t-x)}\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\wedge\int e^{\gamma(t-x)}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\bigg)

\displaystyle~\leq C(e^{\theta t}\wedge e^{\gamma t})\bigg(\theta\int f(x)e^{-\theta x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}+\gamma\int f(x)e^{-\gamma x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg).

Next, for
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda
, we estimate

\displaystyle\int\big|\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{t-x\geq 0\}}\big|\big|\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\big|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle~=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(t)\int\limits_{(-\infty,t]}\big|\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\big|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle\hphantom{~=}~+\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\int\limits_{(t,\infty)}\big|\vec{c}_{\lambda}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda,t-x,k(\lambda))\mathsf{e}_{1}\big|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle~\leq C\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(t)\int e^{\alpha(t-x)}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})+C\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\int e^{\vartheta(t-x)}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})

\displaystyle~\leq C^{\prime}\big(e^{\alpha t}\wedge e^{\vartheta t}\big),

where we have used (
4.17
) in the penultimate step.
This completes the proof of the theorem for non-decreasing
f\geq 0
.

Now let
f
be an arbitrary càdlàg function
satisfying the integrability condition (
2.19
) (with
f
in place of
\mathds{E}[\varphi]
).
Define

f_{\pm}(x)\vcentcolon=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(f(x_{j})-f(x_{j-1}))^{\pm}:-\infty<x_{0}<\ldots<x_{n}\leq x,\ n\in\mathds{N}\bigg\}

for
x\in\mathds{R}
.
Clearly,
f_{+},f_{-}:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
are nondecreasing with
f_{\pm}\geq 0
.
It is known that
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
.
(This is the Jordan decomposition of
f
on
\mathds{R}
.)
It is further known that
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are càdlàg since
f
is.
Further,
\mathrm{V}\!f(x)=f_{+}(x)+f_{-}(x)

and hence (
2.19
) implies that both,
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
satisfy (
7.16
).
The previous part of the proof thereby applies to
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
and, by linearity,
extends to
f
.
∎

,

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Theorem

2.15
.
To see this, we first check that the assumptions of Theorem

imply
those of Theorem

7.4
Determining the coefficients

Note that although the constants
\vec{c}_{\lambda}
and
\vec{b}_{\lambda}
are not given explicitly it is not hard to follow the proofs
and provide explicit expressions for them.
However, even for small
k(\lambda)
, this approach may lead to tedious calculations,
not to mention that there can also be several roots in the relevant strip.
It seems that a more efficient way to determine the constants
b^{\lambda}_{l}

is an application of Lemma
7.1
or
7.6
,
respectively, to a characteristic for which we explicitly know the asymptotic behavior of the
expectation of the associated general branching process.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
be a root of
\mathcal{L}\mu(z)=1
of multiplicity
k

(in the lattice case we also assume that
\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)\in(-\pi,\pi]
).
Then the vector
\vec{b}_{\lambda}
appearing in Lemma
7.1

or
7.6
, respectively,
is given by
M^{\lambda}\vec{b}_{\lambda}=\mathsf{e}_{k}
,
where
M^{\lambda}
is the
k\times k
upper triangular matrix such that for
j\geq i

(M^{\lambda})_{i,j}\vcentcolon=-\frac{(j-1)!(k-1)!}{(i-1)!(j-i+k)!}(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{(k+j-i)}(\lambda)

in the non-lattice case.
In contrast, in the lattice case,

(M^{\lambda})_{i,j}\vcentcolon=\binom{j-1}{i-1}P_{k,j-i}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}}\Big)\mathcal{L}\mu(z)_{|z=\lambda},

where the polynomials
P_{k,l}
are given by

P_{k,l}(y)\vcentcolon=(-1)^{l}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{m-1}y^{k-m}\frac{B_{l+m}(-y)-B_{l+m}(0)}{l+m},

and
B_{n}
is the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
Bernoulli polynomial.
In particular, in both cases,
as
(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{(j)}(\lambda)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
,

\det(M^{\lambda})=\left(\frac{-\mathcal{L}\mu^{(k)}(\lambda)}{k}\right)^{k}\neq 0

and the matrix
M^{\lambda}
is invertible.

For the proof, we need a lemma which essentially is Jensen’s inequality for the total variation operator
\mathrm{V}

defined in (
2.18
).

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\phi=(\phi(t))_{t\in\mathds{R}}
be a stochastic process with càdlàg paths
such that
\phi(t)\in L^{1}
for every
t\in\mathds{R}
and
t\mapsto\mathds{E}[\phi](t)
is again càdlàg.
Then, finite or not,

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}\!\mathds{E}[\phi](t)\leq\mathds{E}[\mathrm{V}\!\phi(t)]

(7.17)

for every
t\in\mathds{R}
.

Proof.

First notice that
\mathrm{V}\!\phi(t)
is a random variable.
Indeed, since the paths of
\phi
are cádlág, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}\!\phi(t)=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\phi(t_{j})-\phi(t_{j-1})|:-\infty<t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}\leq t,\;t_{0},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathds{Q},\ n\in\mathds{N}\bigg\},

which is measurable as the supremum of a family of random variables indexed by a countable set.
Since
\mathds{E}[\phi]
is also càdlàg, we infer

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}\!\mathds{E}[\phi](t)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\mathds{E}[\phi(t_{j})-\phi(t_{j-1})]|:t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}\leq t,\,t_{0},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathds{Q},\,n\in\mathds{N}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\bigg\{\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\phi(t_{j})-\phi(t_{j-1})|\bigg]:t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}\leq t,\,t_{0},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathds{Q},\,n\in\mathds{N}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\bigg[\sup\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\phi(t_{j})-\phi(t_{j-1})|:t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}\leq t,\,t_{0},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathds{Q},\,n\in\mathds{N}\bigg\}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[\mathrm{V}\!\phi](t).

∎

Proof of Proposition
7.9
.

For
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\geq}
, consider the characteristic

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\mathds{E}[\phi_{\lambda}(t)]\mathsf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\mathds{1}_{[0,X_{j})}(t)\exp(\lambda,t-X_{j},k)\mathsf{e}_{1}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}f_{X_{j}}(t)\bigg],\quad t\in\mathds{R}

where, for any
x\geq 0
,

f_{x}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\mathds{1}_{[0,x)}(t)\exp(\lambda,t-x,k)\mathsf{e}_{1}=\mathds{1}_{[0,x)}(t)(t-x)^{k-1}e^{\lambda(t-x)}.

By Lemma
5.2
(the assumption are fulfilled by Lemma
5.1
), the following holds for all

\displaystyle m^{\phi}_{t}=\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\exp({\lambda},t,k)\mathsf{e}_{1}.

(7.18)

We now aim to apply either Lemma
7.6
in the lattice case
or Lemma

in the non-lattice case to the characteristic
\phi

\phi
satisfies either (
2.19

Using equations (

) and (
4.17

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}\!f_{x}(t)

\displaystyle\leq|f_{x}(0)|\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge x}\big|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{s}}f_{x}(s)\big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{s}+\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq x\}}

\displaystyle\leq C\times\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{for }t<0,\\
e^{(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)-\delta)(t-x)}&\quad\text{for }0\leq t<x,\\

for some

and a constant

that does not depend on

.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{V}\!f_{x}(t)\big(e^{-\vartheta t}+e^{-\alpha t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}\leq 2\int\mathrm{V}\!f_{x}(t)e^{-\vartheta t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\vartheta x}

for some other constant
C^{\prime}
that also does not depend on
x
.
Now, observe that we can apply (
7.17
) to
\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\phi_{\lambda}\mathsf{e}_{1}
since both this characteristic and its expectation have càdlàg paths.
Only the latter requires a proof. By (

\displaystyle|\mathsf{e}_{k}^{\ \mathsf{T}}\phi_{\lambda}(t)\mathsf{e}_{1}|\leq C\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)e^{\vartheta t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\vartheta X_{j}}

for any

. Hence, (A

imply that

Using the subadditivity of

, (

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{V}\!\phi(t)\big(e^{-\vartheta t}+e^{-\alpha t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{V}\!\bigg(\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{X_{j}}\bigg]\bigg)(t)\big(e^{-\vartheta t}\!+\!e^{-\alpha t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{N}\int\mathrm{V}\!f_{X_{j}}(t)\big(e^{-\vartheta t}\!+\!e^{-\alpha t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}\bigg]\leq C^{\prime}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{N}e^{-\vartheta X_{j}}\bigg]<\infty.

A similar argument gives (
7.3
) in the lattice case.
An application of either Lemma
7.6

or Lemma

(as mentioned in Remark

to the characteristic
\phi

\displaystyle m^{\phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\vec{b}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\;\mathsf{T}}\int\exp(\lambda^{\prime},t-x,k)\phi(x)\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\mathsf{e}_{1}+O(e^{\theta t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\geq}}\bigg(\bigg(\int\exp(\lambda^{\prime},-x,k)\phi(x)\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})\bigg)^{\mathsf{T}}\,\vec{b}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\bigg)^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(\lambda^{\prime},t,k)\mathsf{e}_{1}+O(e^{\theta t}),

(7.19)

as

\displaystyle M^{\lambda}\vcentcolon=\int\exp(\lambda,-x,k)\phi(x)\,\ell(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}).

First, we deal with the non-lattice case.
For this purpose, recalling basic properties of the beta function

\displaystyle\int(-x)^{l}e^{-\lambda x}\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{X_{j}}x^{l}(x-X_{j})^{k-1}e^{-\lambda X_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\iint_{0}^{y}x^{l}(x-y)^{k-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}e^{-\lambda y}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{y})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l+k-1}\int B(l+1,k)y^{k+l}e^{-\lambda y}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{y})

\displaystyle=-\frac{l!(k-1)!}{(l+k)!}(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{(k+l)}(\lambda),

\displaystyle(M^{\lambda})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=-\mathds{1}_{\{j\geq i\}}\frac{(j-1)!(k-1)!}{(i-1)!(j-i+k)!}(\mathcal{L}\mu)^{(k+j-i)}(\lambda).

In the non-lattice case, invoking Faulhaber’s formula, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathds{Z}}(-x)^{l}e^{-\lambda x}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\mathds{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{0\leq x<X_{j}}x^{l}(x-X_{j})^{k-1}e^{-\lambda X_{j}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\int\sum_{0\leq x<y}x^{l}(x-y)^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{y})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\int\sum_{m=1}^{k}{k-1\choose m-1}(-y)^{k-m}\sum_{0\leq x<y}x^{l+m-1}e^{-\lambda y}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{y})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\int\sum_{m=1}^{k}{k-1\choose m-1}(-y)^{k-m}\frac{B_{l+m}(y)-B_{l+m}(0)}{l+m}e^{-\lambda y}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathit{y})

\displaystyle=P_{k,l}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}}\Big)\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\Big|_{z=\lambda},

which gives

\displaystyle(M^{\lambda})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\Big(\int\exp(\lambda,-x,k)\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}\Big)_{j,i}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{j\geq i\}}{j-1\choose i-1}P_{k,j-i}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{z}}\Big)\mathcal{L}\mu(z)\Big|_{z=\lambda}.

∎

8
Discussion and open problems

In this section we formulate several open problems which are closely related to the present framework.

Open problem 1
.

Prove a corresponding limit theorem for the multitype CMJ process.

Open problem 2
.

Provide functional versions of the theorems proved in this paper.

A drawback of our method in the non-lattice case is that,
in order to find the asymptotic of the mean
m^{\varphi}_{t}

we need to assume that the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous
with respect to Lebesgue measure
or that at least (
7.7
) holds.

Open problem 3
.

In the non-lattice case, work out
a proof that does not require (
7.7
).

The Gaussian fluctuations appearing
in our theorems are caused by
the finiteness of the second moment (
2.9
).
In the case where the condition is not satisfied one
still may ask for a generalization.

Open problem 4
.

Prove a version of the limit theorems with a stable limit.

One of the basic ingredients of the CMJ process is the underlying branching random walk

(S(u))_{u\in\mathcal{T}}
with positive increments.
However, the process
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}
can also be defined
for a branching random walk with two-sided increments and suitable
\varphi
.

Open problem 5
.

Investigate the behavior of
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}
for a
branching random walk
(S(u))_{u\in\mathcal{T}}
with two-sided increments.

A central limit theorem is usually complemented by a law of the iterated logarithm
(see, for instance,
[
24
]
for the central limit theorem
and the law of the iterated logarithm for Nerman’s martingale).
This motivates the following problem.

Open problem 6
.

Prove a corresponding law of the iterated logarithm for
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}
.

The martingale limits
W^{(j)}(\lambda)
play an important role
in the asymptotic behavior of the general branching process.
It is important to obtain more information about their distributions.
In particular, the following problem seems to be quite relevant.

Open problem 7
.

Derive the first-order asymptotic behavior of the tail probabilities

\mathds{P}(|W^{(j)}(\lambda)|>t)
as
t\to\infty
for
j=0,\ldots,k(\lambda)-1
.

We also believe that the approach developed in the present paper
might be useful for settling the following.

Open problem 8
.

Find large deviation estimates for
\mathcal{Z}^{\varphi}_{t}
.

An auxiliary result

Lemma .1
.

Let
r\in\mathds{N}
,
k_{j}\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,
j=1,\ldots,r
,
z_{1},\dots,z_{r}
be distinct complex numbers
with
|z_{j}|\geq\rho
and
b_{j,l}
,
j=1,\ldots,r
,
l=0,\ldots,k_{j}
complex numbers.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{l=0}^{k_{j}}b_{j,l}n^{l}z_{j}^{n}=o(\rho^{n})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,\ n\in\mathds{N}

(.1)

implies
b_{j,l}=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,r
,
l=0,\ldots,k_{j}
.

Proof.

We use induction on
K\vcentcolon=k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}
.
Suppose that
K=0
and denote by

V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})\vcentcolon=(z_{j}^{m-1})_{m,j=1,\ldots,r}
the Vandermonde matrix associated with
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
.
Then, putting
b_{j}\vcentcolon=b_{j,0}
, each component of the vector

V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}z_{1}^{n}\\
\vdots\\
b_{r}z_{r}^{n}\end{pmatrix}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{r}b_{j}z_{j}^{m-1}z_{j}^{n}\bigg)_{m=1,\ldots,r}

is
o(\rho^{n})
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
are distinct,
\det V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})\not=0
,
hence we may multiply the last displayed equation by the inverse of
V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})
from the left and conclude
that
b_{j}z_{j}^{n}=o(\rho^{n})
as
n\to\infty
,
which, in turn, gives
b_{j}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,r
.
For the induction step, we assume that the induction hypothesis holds
whenever
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{r}\leq K
. If now
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{r}=K+1
,
then there is exists some
j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
with
k_{j_{0}}>0
.
We define a linear operator
L
by

Lf(n)\vcentcolon=f(n)-z_{j_{0}}f(n-1)

for any
f:\mathds{Z}\mapsto\mathds{C}
.
If
f(n)=o(\rho^{n})
, then so is
Lf(n)
. Moreover, if
f(n)=p(n)z^{n}
for some polynomial
p
,
then
Lf(n)=\tilde{p}(n)z^{n}
for another polynomial
\tilde{p}
with
\deg\tilde{p}\leq\deg p

and if
f(n)=n^{l}z_{j_{0}}^{n}
, then
Lf(n)=(ln^{l-1}+p(n))z_{j_{0}}^{n}

for some polynomial
p
with
\deg p\leq l-2
.
Applying
L
to both sides of the relation (
.1
) we infer

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{l=0}^{\tilde{k}_{j}}\tilde{b}_{j,l}n^{l}z_{j}^{n}=o(\rho^{n})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,\ n\in\mathds{N}

for some
\tilde{k}_{j}\leq k_{j}
,
\tilde{k}_{j_{0}}=k_{j_{0}}-1
and
\tilde{b}_{j_{0},k_{j_{0}}-1}=k_{j_{0}}b_{j_{0},k_{j_{0}}}
.
The induction hypothesis gives that
\tilde{b}_{j_{0},k_{j_{0}}-1}=0

which implies that
b_{j_{0},k_{j_{0}}}=0
as well.
This allows us to replace
k_{j_{0}}
by
k_{j_{0}}-1
in (
.1
).
The claim now follows by induction.
∎
