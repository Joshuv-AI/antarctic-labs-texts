Heat capacity double transitions in time-reversal symmetry broken superconductors
Henrik S. Røising, Glenn Wagner, Mercè Roig, Astrid T. Rømer, Brian M. Andersen

Abstract
Standard superconductors display a ubiquitous discontinuous jump in the electronic specific heat at the critical superconducting transition temperature. In a growing class of unconventional superconductors, however, a second order parameter component may get stabilized and produce a second heat capacity jump at a lower temperature, typically associated with the spontaneous breaking of time-reversal symmetry. The splitting of the two specific heat discontinuities can be controlled by external perturbations such as chemical substitution, hydrostatic pressure, or uniaxial strain. We develop a theoretical quantitative multi-band framework to determine the ratio of the heat capacity jumps, given the band structure and the order parameter momentum structure. We discuss the conditions of the gap profile which determine the amplitude of the second jump. We apply our formalism to the case of Sr 2 RuO 4 , and using the gap functions from a microscopic random phase approximation calculation, we show that recently-proposed accidentally degenerate order parameters may exhibit a strongly suppressed second heat capacity jump. We discuss the origin of this result and consider also the role of spatial inhomogeneity on the specific heat. Our results provide a possible explanation of why a second heat capacity jump has so far evaded experimental detection in Sr 2 RuO 4 .

II.1
The heat capacity discontinuities

The heat capacity of a superconductor,
C(T)=T\partial S/\partial T

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{\mu,\boldsymbol{k}}\Big[n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]\ln n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]

(2)

\displaystyle+\big(1-n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]\big)\ln\big( 1 - n_F[E_{\mu}(\bo{k})] \big)\Big],

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=Q(\Delta_{0}X,T_{\mathrm{TRSB}})/Q(\Delta_{0},T_{c}),

II.3
Parameters controlling the magnitude of the second jump

The expression of Eq. (
3
), along with the Ginzburg–Landau result of Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), reveal the key knobs that dictate the size of the second heat capacity jump.

First,
\eta
is generally highly sensitive to the order of which the order parameter components onset. Intuitively, the more of the Fermi surface that is gapped-out by the primary component (at
T_{c}
), or correspondingly the fewer the quasiparticles left to be gapped-out by the secondary component (at
T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}
), the smaller the second jump. This can be inferred directly from Eq. (
3
) by the fact that
\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}
appears in the denominators, so the order of the components is decisive for the size of
\eta
.

Second,
\eta
is suppressed in cases where the two components share momentum structure over the Fermi surface. This can be seen from the behaviour of the dimensionless integral appearing in Eq. (
3
),
\pazocal{I}(z)\approx\exp(- 7 \zeta(3) z^2 / \pi^2)
(accurate to
\pazocal{O}(z^{2})
). The second and dominant term in Eq. (
3
) is therefore approximately proportional to
\langle\lvert f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\exp(-c \lvert f_a(\bo{p}) \rvert^2)\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}
, where
c
is a positive constant. This expression will be small if, for a given momentum on the Fermi surface,
\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert
is maximal (minimal) where
\lvert f_{b}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert
is maximal (minimal). Consequentially, if the two components have a similar momentum structure over the Fermi surface, the second heat capacity jump is suppressed. In cases where the Fermi velocity is anisotropic, the regions with minimal velocity (particularly in the vicinity of van Hove points) will dominate the Fermi surface average and dictate where a matching momentum structure is decisive to get a suppressed second jump. We note that this criterion of a matching momentum structure is generally not limited to nodal regions, which is commonly accepted, but applies in principle to the entire Fermi surface.

Third, but indirectly related to the second point, increasing the repulsion between the components reduces the second jump. The repulsion is controlled by
\beta_{ab}
, which
A
(in Eq. (
12
)) is directly proportional to. This is related to the second point because
\beta_{ab}
is maximized when the components
f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})
and
f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})
share momentum structure. Technically, the closer one is to saturating Hölder’s inequality
\beta_{ab}^{2}\leq\beta_{a}\beta_{b}
, the smaller the second jump.

Since
a
and
b
label different one-dimensional irreps both symmetry-breaking and symmetry-preserving perturbations (with respect to the point group), commonly labelled
\epsilon
, will
a priori
cause a splitting of
T_{c}
and
T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}
since
\partial T_{c1}/\partial\epsilon\neq\partial T_{c2}/\partial\epsilon
generally, and the ratio
T_{c}/T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}
largely controls
\eta
. In the limit
\frac{T_{c}}{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}\gg\frac{\alpha_{a}^{0}}{\alpha_{b}^{0}}\frac{\beta_{ab}}{\beta_{a}}
Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) tell us that
\eta\propto(T_{c}/T_{\mathrm{TRSB}})^{2}
.

Experimentally, natural symmetry-preserving perturbations to consider include, e.g., disorder, impurity doping
Zinkl and Sigrist 2021
, and hydrostatic pressure. In the case of Sr
2
RuO
4
, La substitution of Sr, taking the effective role of added disorder
Shen et al. 2007
, and moderate hydrostatic pressure were applied, both resulting in no detectable temperature splitting
Grinenko et al. 2021
. In this case, it would also be of interest to see the result of Co and Mn substitution of Ru since this has been shown to enhance ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic fluctuations, respectively
Ortmann et al. 2013
;
Sheng et al. 2022
. Symmetry-breaking perturbations of experimental relevance include strain, which fundamentally decomposes into five types in the case of
D_{4}
: two compressional modes preserve the crystal symmetries, and three shear modes reduce the crystal symmetries
Ghosh et al. 2020
. In Sr
2
RuO
4
the (strikingly different) effects of both symmetry-breaking (100) strain
Steppke et al. 2017
and symmetry-preserving (001) strain
Jerzembeck et al. 2022
have been studied.

In this section we demonstrate the basic principles stated in Sec.

for controlling the size of the second specific heat jump by calculating the heat capacity discontinuity from Eq. (

\Delta_{0}(T)=\Delta_{0a}(1-T/T_{c})^{1/2}
, and

\xi(\boldsymbol{k})=-2t(\cos{k_x}+\cos{k_y})-\mu
, in the low-filling limit
\mu\approx-4t
:
k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}=k_{F}^{2}
, with
k_{F}=\sqrt{4+\mu/t}

. In this case the Fermi velocity, Fermi surface area, and density of states simplify to

,
\lvert S\rvert=2\pi k_{F}
, and
\rho_{0}=k_{F}/(4\pi t)
, respectively. The Fermi surface averages reduce to
\langle f\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{1}{2\pi k_{F}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta~f(\theta)
, where
\theta
is the polar angle.

We consider a perturbation
\epsilon
that causes a splitting of
T_{c}
and
T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}
. In the unperturbed case we assume that the two temperatures
T_{c}^{\epsilon=0}\approx T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{\epsilon=0}
such that the splitting can be meaningfully expanded in the small quantity

. We further assume, as is standard, that the magnitudes of the order parameter components scale with the respective temperatures, i.e.,
\Delta_{0a}^{\epsilon}=\Delta_{0a}^{\epsilon=0}T_{c}^{\epsilon}/T_{c}^{\epsilon=0}
and
\Delta_{0b}^{\epsilon}=\Delta_{0b}^{\epsilon=0}T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{\epsilon}/T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{\epsilon=0}
. Consequentially, we find that
\eta
to leading order in

\eta=\left(\frac{\Delta_{0b}^{\epsilon=0}}{\Delta_{0a}^{\epsilon=0}}\right)^{2}\frac{\langle\lvert f_{b}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}\Big[1-y\frac{7\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0a}^{\epsilon=0}}{2T_{c}^{\epsilon=0}}\right)^{2}\frac{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\lvert f_{b}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}{\langle\lvert f_{b}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}+\pazocal{O}(y^{2})\Big].

(14)

To obtain the above expression we made use of the small argument expansion of the dimensionless integral,
\pazocal{I}(z)
, see Appendix
A.2
. Notably, we see that the
\pazocal{O}(y)
correction is negative semi-definite under the above conditions, so the leading effect of, e.g., uniaxial in-plane strain (
\epsilon=\varepsilon_{xx}
) is a reduction of the second heat capacity jump.

From here it is a simple task to calculate the ratios of Fermi surface averages appearing in Eq. (
14
) for, e.g., any harmonic of the one-dimensional
D_{4}
irreps, i.e.,
f_{A_{1}}^{(n)}(\theta)=\cos(4n\theta)

f_{A_{2}}^{(n)}(\theta)=\sin([4n+4]\theta)
,
f_{B_{1}}^{(n)}(\theta)=\cos([4n+2]\theta)
,
f_{B_{2}}^{(n)}(\theta)=\sin([4n+2]\theta)
. Focusing on the fundamental harmonic
n=0
we see that
\langle\lvert f_{a}^{(0)}(\theta)\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{1}{k_{F}},~\frac{1}{2k_{F}}
for
a=A_{1},~\{A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}\}
, respectively. Thus, the strongest suppression of the second jump at
y=0
occurs for
a=A_{1}
(fully gapped
s
-wave) and
b\in\{A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}\}
(

,
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
, and
d_{xy}
, respectively). The largest
\pazocal{O}(y)
corrections occur when the nodes are maximally shifted, e.g., for
a=B_{1}
and
b=B_{2}
. The
\pazocal{O}(y)
correction is minimal when the nodes are perfectly matching, e.g., for
a=b=B_{1}
.

III
Case study:
\mathrm{Sr}_{2}\mathrm{RuO}_{4}

Combined heat capacity and muon spin relaxation (
\mathrm{\mu}
SR) measurements have demonstrated that the onset of superconductivity and TRSB split under uniaxial (100) strain in SRO
Grinenko et al. 2021
. If this splitting is directly linked to the splitting of the critical temperatures of two uniform order parameter components, one generically expects a second heat capacity jump at the lower temperature scale, i.e., the onset of TRSB. However, despite intensive efforts from high-resolution heat capacity measurements resolved under strain, no second transition is observed
Li et al. 2021

Within recent spin-fluctuation based calculations for superconductivity in Sr

III.3
Discussion

Considering the results of the unstrained material in the top panel of Fig.
7
, neither order parameter candidate,
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
or
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
, provides a perfect match with the temperature dependence of the experimental data of Ref.
Deguchi et al. 2004
. However, given that we feed in microscopically computed form factors obtained from a specific set of parameters, we do not consider this discrepancy a fundamental concern. As demonstrated in Appendix
D
, the match to the experimental data can be improved by, e.g., redistributing the band-resolved gap magnitudes compared to the RPA solution.

Noticeably, in the unstrained case the second jump of the
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
order is very tiny, and an order of magnitude smaller than for
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
. Under uniaxial in-plane strain, the second jump is initially found to grow in the case of
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
before decreasing again for strains
p<-0.02
. For
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
the second jump evolves differently with strain but remains sizeable and outside the threshold for detection reported in Ref.
Li et al. 2021
.

The second jump evolving non-monotonically under strain in both order parameter scenarios points to the importance of the
A
-term in Eq. (
3
) since the simplified case of Eq. (
14
), in which the
A
-term is absent, predicts a monotonic decrease of the second jump with strain. As noted before, the
A
-term becoming sizeable is linked to a shared momentum structure of the form factors. Accommodating a small second jump under strain thus requires the accidental nodes, which generally shift on the Fermi surface under strain, to shift in a similar fashion for the two form factor components, which is highly constraining and
a priori
implausible. Any failure of this synchronized node-matching under strain can therefore be identified as an effect working against second jump reduction found in Eq. (
14
).

In both order parameter scenarios considered here, the
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
form factors exhibit accidental nodes in the vicinity of
k_{x},k_{y}=\pi/3
at zero strain. Figures
4
and
5
show that for
s^{\prime}
and
d_{xy}
these nodes shift in a rather modest way with strain compared to
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
and
g
, the latter two also develop additional nodal regions at the highest strains. It is also worth noting that the symmetry-protected zone-diagonal node in the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
scenario is lifted for finite strain, which can be seen in the low-temperature tail in Fig.
7
(d). We attribute the dissimilar behaviour of the second jump under strain of
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
vs.
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
to this different strain-induced node shifting.

When smearing is added in Fig.
8
, it is seen that a smearing width of
\sigma=100~\mathrm{mK}
is enough to bring the second jump into the undetectable regime for the
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
case; a case with
50~\mathrm{mK}
smearing is shown Appendix.
D
. In contrast, the second jump remains clearly resolvable for
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
. When comparing to the experimental data of Ref.
Li et al. 2021
, shown in Fig.
8
(c) for completeness, we conclude that
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
is a more likely order parameter candidate than
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
within the premises of our calculation. We also note that the results change drastically if one instead considers the reversed combination of orders, e.g.,
d_{xy}+is^{\prime}
as opposed to
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
. In Appendix
D
we show results for the
d_{xy}+is^{\prime}
case, which results in
\eta\geq 0.26
. Thus, as noted in Sec.
II.3
, the order in which the order parameters switch on has a large impact on the heat capacity jumps. The order parameter combination
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
where the
s^{\prime}
order condenses at the higher temperature has a small second jump, whereas the
d_{xy}+is^{\prime}
combination has a large second jump and can therefore be ruled out.

Among the aforementioned order parameter candidates recently proposed for Sr

;
Rømer et al. 2022

\epsilon

(100)

4

s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
and

Contrasting the focus on exotic multi-component orders relying on accidental degeneracies, the simpler explanation of the specific heat data of Sr

4

;
Benhabib et al. 2021

;

IV
Other time-reversal symmetry broken superconductors

In the heavy fermion compound UPt
3
there is convincing experimental evidence of a two-component order parameter deep in the superconducting phase. Two distinct transition temperatures are revealed by two jumps visible in the ultrasound velocity as a function of temperature
Bruls et al. 1990
. Furthermore, TRSB is observed at temperatures below the second transition. The Kerr effect has been observed
Schemm et al. 2014
, indicating TRSB below
T_{\textrm{TRSB}}\approx 0.46
K, while the critical temperature as measured by the drop in the resistance is
T_{c}\approx 0.55
K. In addition, TRSB is also seen in a
\mu
SR signal
Luke et al. 1993
(although we should caution that an independent
\mu
SR experiment failed to see any signature of TRSB
de Réotier et al. 1995
). A likely order parameter candidate is
f_{z(x^{2}-y^{2})}+if_{xyz}

Joynt and Taillefer 2002
;
Kallin and Berlinsky 2016
(where the degeneracy of the two-dimensional irrep is split by magnetism). In UPt
3
a very clear second heat capacity jump is observed
Fisher et al. 1989
. The Fermi surface of UPt
3
is complicated and contains five strongly three-dimensional sheets when the
5f
uranium electrons are included
McMullan et al. 2008
. For the
f_{z(x^{2}-y^{2})}+if_{xyz}
scenario, modulo all the details of the multiband fermiology and possible accidental nodes, the vertical symmetry-imposed nodes are maximally shifted when comparing the two components, which, according to Sec.
II.3
suggests that one can expect equally-sized heat capacity jumps. On the phenomenological level this agrees with what is observed experimentally
Fisher et al. 1989
;
Trappmann et al. 1991
.

UTe
2
has attracted significant attention recently as an unconventional superconductor. The Kerr effect indicating TRSB has been observed
Hayes et al. 2021
. However, a second heat capacity jump is only observed under hydrostatic pressure
Braithwaite et al. 2019
;
Thomas et al. 2020
while no second jump is seen under applied stress
Girod et al. 2022

V
Conclusions

We have provided a general theoretical framework for the specific heat transitions in multi-component time-reversal symmetry broken superconductors. This allowed a thorough discussion of the amplitudes of the specific heat jumps at the two transition temperatures. Specifically, one expects comparable specific heat discontinuities for most two-dimensional irreps due to the maximally misaligned nodes and the symmetry-imposed equality of order parameter component magnitudes. On the other hand, a significantly reduced second jump may be expected for TRSB superconductors that have competing order parameter components with similar momentum structure, and where the least nodal component onsets first. We applied the theoretical setup to perform a comprehensive discussion of the low-temperature electronic specific heat relevant for Sr
2
RuO
4
. While the specific heat data alone for this material are most straightforwardly explained by a nodal single-component superconductor, several other experimental probes point to TRSB multi-component superconductivity
Ghosh et al. 2020
;
Benhabib et al. 2021
;
Xia et al. 2006
;
Grinenko et al. 2021
. We have pursued the accidentally degenerate possibilities
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
and
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
with gap structures obtained from microscopic RPA calculations, and compared their specific heat behaviour with experimental data in both the absence and presence of uniaxial strain. Our results yield a better agreement between experimental data and
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
order, as compared to
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
superconductivity. Further, we conclude from these calculations that the currently-available experimental specific heat data are consistent with
s^{\prime}+id_{xy}
superconductivity in the unstrained case, and also with the uniaxially strained data when considering the role of strain inhomogeneity.

Appendix A
Heat capacity of superconductors

The specific heat for a multiband superconductor follows from the expression for the entropy of the fermion gas
Tinkham 1975
,

S=-2k_{B}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\mu}\Big[n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]\ln n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]+\big(1-n_{F}[E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})]\big)\ln\big( 1 - n_F[E_{\mu}(\bo{k})] \big)\Big].

(17)

Here,
E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})=\sqrt{\xi_{\mu}^{2}+\lvert\Delta_{\mu}(T,\boldsymbol{k})\rvert^{2}}
, and
n_{F}(E)=(1+\exp(\beta E))^{-1}
is the Fermi function.

A.1
Single-component order parameter

We first assume a single-component order parameter with temperature dependence
\Delta(T)
,

\Delta_{\mu}(T,\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta(T)f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(18)

where
f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})
is the order parameter form factor of band
\mu
normalized such that
\max_{\boldsymbol{k},\mu}\lvert f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert=1
. The specific heat follows from

\displaystyle C(T)

\displaystyle=T\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}=2\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\mu}E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{F}(E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k}))}{\mathrm{d}T}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k_{B}T^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi_{\mu}\hskip 2.84526pt\Big\langle\frac{\xi_{\mu}^{2}+\Delta(T)^{2}\lvert f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert^{2}-\frac{T}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta(T)^{2}}{\partial T}\lvert f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert^{2}}{4\cosh^{2}(\frac{E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2k_{B}T})}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}},

where the Fermi surface average is evaluated as

\langle A\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}\coloneqq\sum_{\mu}\int_{S_{\mu}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{A}{v_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})},

(20)

where
v_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})=\lvert\nabla\xi_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert
is the Fermi velocity and
\rho_{\mu}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{S_{\mu}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}/v_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})
is the density of states of band
\mu
.

By evaluating Eq. (
19
) in the normal state, for which
\Delta=0
, the specific heat per temperature, i.e., the Sommerfeld coefficient
\gamma_{n}
, becomes

\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\frac{C_{n}(T)}{T}=\frac{2}{k_{B}T^{3}}\sum_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\frac{\xi^{2}}{4\cosh^{2}(\frac{\xi}{2k_{B}T})}=4k_{B}^{2}\zeta(2)\sum_{\mu}\rho_{\mu},

(21)

with
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
. This recovers the multiband version of the well-known (electronic) linear contribution to the specific heat of a metal
Tinkham 1975
. It is convenient to normalise Eq. (
19
) by the normal-state value:

\frac{C(T)}{T\gamma_{n}}=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}(k_{B}T)^{3}\sum_{b}\rho_{b}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\hskip 2.84526pt\Big\langle\frac{\xi^{2}+\Delta(T)^{2}\lvert f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert^{2}-\frac{T}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta(T)^{2}}{\partial T}\lvert f_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\rvert^{2}}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{E_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2k_{B}T})}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}.

(22)

A.2
Two-component order parameter with simple temperature dependence

Following Ref.
Kivelson et al. 2020
we assume a TRSB two-component order parameter with the particular BCS-like temperature dependence

\displaystyle\Delta(\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{0,a}\sqrt{1-\frac{T}{T_{c}}}f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})+i\Delta_{0,b}\sqrt{1-\frac{T}{T_{\text{TRSB}}}}f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p}),

(23)

where
a
and
b
are irrep. labels. We shall use this ansatz in Eq. (
19
) and evaluate the heat capacity jump across
T_{c}
and
T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}
. At the onset of superconductivity we get

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta C_{\mathrm{SC}}}{T_{c}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{C(T\to T_{c}^{-})-C(T\to T_{c}^{+})}{T_{c}}

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{c}}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0,a}}{k_{B}T_{c}}\right)^{2}\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{4\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{2k_{B}T_{c}}\right)}

\displaystyle=k_{B}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0,a}}{k_{B}T_{c}}\right)^{2}\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}},

where the only contribution we pick up comes from the third term in the numerator of Eq. (
19
), due to its discontinuity at
T_{c}
. Repeating the calculation at the onset of the second order parameter component we now get

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta C_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{C(T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{-})-C(T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{+})}{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}

(25)

\displaystyle=k_{B}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0,b}}{k_{B}T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}\right)^{2}\Big\langle\lvert f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\cosh^{2}\left(\sqrt{u^{2}+z(\boldsymbol{p})^{2}}\right)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}},

where
z(\boldsymbol{p})=\frac{\Delta_{0,a}}{2k_{B}T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}{T_{c}}}f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})
was introduced. Combining these two expressions we get the ratio of the heat capacity jumps

\eta\coloneqq\frac{\Delta C_{\mathrm{TRSB}}/T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}{\Delta C_{\mathrm{SC}}/T_{c}}=\left(\frac{\Delta_{0,b}}{\Delta_{0,a}}\frac{T_{c}}{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}\right)^{2}\frac{\langle\lvert f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\pazocal{I}[z(\boldsymbol{p})]\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}},

(26)

where

\pazocal{I}(z)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u~\cosh^{-2}\left(\sqrt{u^{2}+z^{2}}\right).

(27)

We note that this function for small
z
can be approximated as
\pazocal{I}(z)=1-\frac{7\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}z^{2}+\pazocal{O}(z^{4})\approx\exp\left(-\frac{7\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}z^{2}\right)
.

A.3
Two-component order parameter with general temperature dependence

We go beyond the square root ansatz in Eq. (

\Delta(T,\boldsymbol{p})=\Delta_{0}(T)\left[f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})+iX(T)f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})\right].

(28)

Above we have left band indices on

implicit. Crucially, this ansatz allows for the two components to interact in the sense that
\Delta_{0}(T)
can, and generally will, be non-analytic at

where
X
onsets. This also results in two terms being picked up in the numerator of
\eta
, coming from

A\coloneqq\left[\frac{\partial\Delta_{0}(T)^{2}}{\partial T}\Big\rvert_{T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{-}}-\frac{\partial\Delta_{0}(T)^{2}}{\partial T}\Big\rvert_{T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{+}}\right]\big/D

(29)

and

B\coloneqq\left[\frac{\partial(\Delta_{0}(T)X(T))^{2}}{\partial T}\Big\rvert_{T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{-}}-\frac{\partial(\Delta_{0}(T)X(T))^{2}}{\partial T}\Big\rvert_{T\to T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}^{+}}\right]\big/D,

(30)

with

, respectively. Repeating the calculation of Sec.
A.2
now results in

\eta=\frac{T_{c}}{T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}\left(A\frac{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\pazocal{I}[z(\boldsymbol{p})]\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}+B\frac{\langle\lvert f_{b}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\pazocal{I}[z(\boldsymbol{p})]\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}{\langle\lvert f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})\rvert^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}}\right),

(31)

with
z(\boldsymbol{p})=\frac{\Delta_{0}(T_{\mathrm{TRSB}})}{2k_{B}T_{\mathrm{TRSB}}}f_{a}(\boldsymbol{p})
.
