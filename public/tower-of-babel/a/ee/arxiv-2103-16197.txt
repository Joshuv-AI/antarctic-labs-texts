Standard monomial theory and toric degenerations of Richardson varieties in flag varieties
Narasimha Chary Bonala, Oliver Clarke, Fatemeh Mohammadi

Abstract
We study standard monomial bases for Richardson varieties inside the flag variety. In general, writing down a standard monomial basis for a Richardson variety can be challenging, as it involves computing so-called defining chains or key tableaux. However, for a certain family of Richardson varieties, indexed by compatible permutations, we provide a very direct and straightforward combinatorial rule for writing down a standard monomial basis. We apply this result to the study of toric degenerations of Richardson varieties. In particular, we provide a new family of toric degenerations of Richardson varieties inside flag varieties.

Introduction

The geometry of the flag variety heavily depends on the study of its Schubert varieties. For example, they provide an excellent way of understanding the multiplicative structure of the cohomology ring of the flag variety. In this context, it is essential to understand how Schubert varieties intersect in a general position.
A Richardson variety in the flag variety is the intersection of a Schubert variety and an opposite Schubert variety. In
[
11
]
and
[
21
]
, the fundamental properties of these varieties are studied, including their irreducibility.
Many geometric properties of the flag variety and its subvarieties can be understood through standard monomial theory (SMT). For example, vanishing results of cohomology groups, normality and other singularities of Schubert varieties, see e.g.
[
22
, Chapter 3]
. Also, the relationship between
K
-theory of the flag variety and SMT is established in
[
19
]
.

Let
\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]
be the polynomial ring on the Plücker variables
P_{J}
for non-empty subsets
J
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and let
I_{n}\subset\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]
be the Plücker ideal of the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
.
The homogeneous coordinate ring of

\Flag_{n}
is given by
\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]/I_{n}
. We say that a monomial
P=P_{J_{1}}\dots P_{J_{d}}
is
standard for
\Flag_{n}
if
J_{1}\leq\dots\leq J_{d}
.
A standard monomial basis of
\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]/I_{n}
is a subset of standard monomials that forms a basis for
\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]/I_{n}
as a vector space.
Hodge in
[
15
]
provided a combinatorial rule to choose such basis for Grassmannians in terms of semi-standard Young tableaux. He also proved that such a basis is compatible with Schubert varieties. More precisely, the basis elements that remain non-zero after restriction form a basis for the quotient ring associated to the Schubert varieties.
Hodge’s work is generalised to flag varieties by Lakshmibai, Musili and Seshadri, see e.g.
[
22
]

for a more detailed exposition.

In this paper, we investigate when the standard monomials directly restrict to a monomial basis for the Richardson variety, thus providing a particularly simple rule for determining standard monomial bases for particular Richardson varieties. A standard monomial
P=P_{J_{1}}\dots P_{J_{d}}

restricts
to a non-zero function on the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
if and only if
v\leq J_{i}\leq w
for all
i
. We say that such a monomial restricts to the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
and we often write these restricted monomials as semi-standard Young tableaux, see Section
2.6
.
For Richardson varieties in the Grassmannians, the restricted standard monomials always form a monomial basis. However, this is not true for an arbitrary Richardson variety in the flag variety, see Example
3.3
. And so the conventional method for determining a monomial basis for the coordinate ring of a Richardson variety inside the flag variety is fairly complicated, see Section
2.7
. Other combinatorial methods for calculating these monomials have been explored using so-called
key tableaux

[
23
]
.
We restrict our attention to the family of Richardson varieties
X_{w}^{v}
, where
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
from (
3.1
).
Our main result is the following:

Theorem

(Theorem
3.12
)
.

The restriction of standard monomials for the flag variety forms a standard monomial basis for the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
, for all
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
from (
3.1
).

We also observe a surprising relation between the pairs in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and toric degenerations of Richardson varieties.
A toric degeneration of a variety
X
is a flat family
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{\!{1}}
, where the special fiber (over zero) is a toric variety and all other fibers are isomorphic to
X
. In particular, some of the algebraic invariants of
X
, such as the Hilbert polynomial, will be the same for all the fibers. Hence, we can do the computations on the toric fiber.
The study of toric degenerations of flag varieties was started in
[
12
]
by Gonciulea and Lakshmibai using
standard monomial theory. In
[
17
]
, Kogan and Miller obtained toric degenerations of flag varieties using geometric methods. Moreover, in
[
4
]
Caldero constructed such degenerations using tools from representation theory.
In
[
16
]
, Kim studied the Gröbner degenerations of Richardson varieties inside the flag variety, where the special fiber is the toric variety of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope; this is a generalisation of the results of
[
17
]
.
We notice that the corresponding ideals of many such degenerations
contain monomials and so their corresponding varieties are not toric.
Hence, we aim to characterise
such toric ideals.
In particular, we explicitly describe degenerations of
Richardson varieties inside
flag varieties, and provide a complete characterisation for permutations leading to
monomial-free ideals.

Theorem

(Theorem
5.2
)
.

Every pair of permutations
(v,w)
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
from (
3.1
) gives rise to a toric degeneration of the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
in the flag variety.

The toric degenerations that we construct are Gröbner degenerations of the ideal of the Richardson variety with respect to a weight vector which we define in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
.
Our goal is to study the initial ideal
\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
for each Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
with respect to this weight vector. To do this, we consider the result for the flag variety which is proved in
[
9
,
20
]
. More precisely, the corresponding initial ideal is
toric
(binomial and prime), see Theorem
2.3
. We write the initial of the Plücker ideal as
\init(I_{n})=\ker(\phi_{n})
, which is equal to the kernel of a monomial map
\phi_{n}
, see (
2.4
). For each Richardson variety

we construct the

of

to the variables

using Lemma
2.4
, in particular this ideal is generated by degree two monomials and binomials.
Our degeneration is known as the

[

]
which correspond to the

Gröbner degeneration. We show that our methods can be used to generalise these results and find further toric degenerations.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout we fix an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
and write
[n]
for the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. We denote the symmetric group on
n
symbols by
S_{n}
and for any
w\in S_{n}
we write
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
, where
w_{i}=w(i)
for each
i\in[n]
.
We fix

w_{0}:=(n,n-1,\ldots,2,1)
for the longest product of adjacent transpositions in
S_{n}
. The permutations of
S_{n}
act naturally on the left of subsets of
[n]
. So, for each
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subset[n]
, we have
w_{0}I=\{n+1-i_{1},\ldots,n+1-i_{k}\}
which is obtained by applying the permutation
w_{0}
element-wise to
I
.
We use
\leq
for the natural partial order on the subsets of
[n]
given by

\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\}\leq\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{t}\}\textrm{ if }s\geq t\textrm{ and }i_{1}\leq j_{1},\dots,i_{t}\leq j_{t}.

We recall the
Bruhat order
on
S_{n}
, which is given by

(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\leq(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\textrm{ if }\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}\leq\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}\textrm{ for all }k\in[n].

It is also convenient for us to define a comparison operator between subsets and permutations:

\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\leq(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\textrm{ if }\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\leq\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\},

(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\leq\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\textrm{ if }\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}\leq\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}.

Remark 2.1
.

The comparison
\leq
between subsets and permutations can be phrased purely in terms of the Bruhat order as follows. For each subset
I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\}\subseteq[n]
, let
\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{n-s}\}=[n]\backslash I
denote its complement.
Then for any pair of permutations
v,w\in S_{n}
we have:

I\leq w\Longleftrightarrow(i_{1},i_{2}\dots,i_{s},j_{1},\dots,j_{n-s})\leq w\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptv\leq I\Longleftrightarrow v\leq(i_{s},i_{s-1}\dots,i_{1},j_{n-s},\dots,j_{1}).

2.1 Flag varieties.

A full flag is a sequence of vector subspaces of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
:

\{0\}=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n-1}\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}

where
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{K}}(V_{i})=i
.
The set of all full flags is called the flag variety and denoted by
\Flag_{n}
, which is naturally embedded in a product of Grassmannians. Here, we consider the structure of algebraic variety on
\Flag_{n}
induced from the product of Grassmannians.
We view the full flag variety
\Flag_{n}
as a homogeneous space for the group SL
(n,\mathbb{K})
of complex
n\times n
matrices with determinant one. Precisely, there is a natural transitive action of SL
(n,\mathbb{K})
on the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
which identifies the variety
\Flag_{n}
with the set of left cosets SL
(n,\mathbb{K})/B
, where
B
is the stabiliser of the standard flag

0\subset\langle e_{1}\rangle\subset\cdots\subset\langle e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle=\mathbb{K}^{n}
. Here, note that

B
is the subgroup of SL
(n,\mathbb{K})
consisting of upper triangular matrices. Given a permutation
w\in S_{n}
, we denote by
\sigma_{w}
the
n\times n
permutation matrix with
1
’s in positions
(w(i),i)
for all
i
. By the Bruhat decomposition, we can write the aforementioned set of cosets as

\Flag_{n}={\rm SL}(n,\mathbb{K})/B=\coprod_{w\in S_{n}}B\sigma_{w}B/B.

The spaces
B\sigma_{w}B/B
are all affine and are called Bruhat cells.
Similarly, for the subgroup of lower triangular matrices
B^{-}
, the homogeneous space
\Flag_{n}
can be decomposed as

\Flag_{n}=\coprod_{v\in S_{n}}B^{-}\sigma_{v}B/B.

2.2 Richardson varieties.

Let
v,w\in S_{n}
.
We define the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}

associated to
v,w
as the intersection of Schubert variety
X_{w}
and opposite Schubert variety
X^{v}
inside the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
. More precisely, the Schubert and opposite Schubert varieties are defined as the Zariski closure of the corresponding cells in the aforementioned decomposition, namely:

X_{w}=\overline{B\sigma_{w}B/B}\subseteq\Flag_{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptX^{v}=\overline{B^{-}\sigma_{v}B/B}\subseteq\Flag_{n}.

Note that
X_{w}^{v}
is nonempty if and only if
v\leq w
with respect to the
Bruhat order
, see Section
2.6
. Moreover, the dimension of
X_{w}^{v}
is given by
\dim(X_{w}^{v})=N(w)-N(v)
, where
N(w)
is the
inversion number
of
w
, i.e. the total number of pairs
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
such that
i<j
and
w(i)>w(j)
.
which we denote by
N(w)
.

We also note that the opposite Schubert variety
X^{v}
can be observed as a translate
w_{0}X_{w_{0}v}
of the Schubert variety
X_{w_{0}v}
since
B^{-}=\sigma_{w_{0}}B\sigma_{w_{0}}
. Moreover,
X_{w}^{id}=X_{w}
and
X_{w_{0}}^{v}=X^{v}
.

2.3 Ideals of flag varieties and Richardson varieties.

Every point in the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
can be represented by an
(n-1)\times n
matrix
X=(x_{i,j})
of full rank.
Let

\mathbb{K}[x_{i,j}]
be the polynomial ring on the variables
x_{i,j}
.
The ideal of the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
, denoted by
I_{n}
, is the kernel of the polynomial map

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}:\ \mathbb{K}[P_{J}:\varnothing\neq J\subsetneq[n]]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[x_{i,j}]

(2.1)

sending each variable

P_{J}
to the determinant of the submatrix of
X
with row indices
1,\ldots,|J|
and column indices in
J
. We call the variables
P_{J}
of the ring Plücker variables and their images
\varphi_{n}(P_{J})
Plücker forms. We also call
I_{n}
the Plücker ideal of the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
.

Given
v\leq w
in
S_{n}
, we define the collection of subsets
T_{w}^{v}=\{J\subset[n]:v\leq J\leq w\}
and its complement
S_{w}^{v}=\{J\subseteq[n]\}\backslash T_{w}^{v}
. The comparison of subsets and elements of
S_{n}
, along with the Bruhat order on
S_{n}
is given in Section
2.6
. Then the associated ideal of the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
is

I(X_{w}^{v})=(I_{n}+\langle P_{J}:J\in S_{w}^{v}\rangle)\cap\mathbb{K}[P_{J}:J\in T_{w}^{v}]=(I(X_{w})+I(X^{v}))\cap\mathbb{K}[P_{J}:J\in T_{w}^{v}].

(2.2)

We now give an example of the subsets
S_{w}^{v}
and
T_{w}^{v}

(see
[
18
, §3.4]
for more details).

Example 2.2
.

Let
n=4
. Consider the permutations
v=(2314)
and
w=(4231)
. The subsets of
[n]
in
T^{v}_{w}
of size one are given by those entries that lie between
v_{1}=2
and
w_{1}=4
, which are
2,3
and
4
. The subsets of size two are those that lie between
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}=23
and
\{w_{1},w_{2}\}=24
which are
23
and
24
. The subsets of size three are those which lie between
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}=123
and
\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}=234
which are all possible three-subsets. So we have:

{T}^{(2314)}_{(4231)}=\{2,3,4,23,24,123,124,134,234\}\text{ and }S^{(2314)}_{(4231)}=\{1,12,13,14,34\}.

Let

Proposition 2.8

2.7 Standard monomials.

The Plücker algebra

\mathbb{K}[P_{J}]_{J\subset[n]}/I_{n}

We refer to
[

To simplify our notation, we identify the semi-standard Young tableau

. The standard monomials for Richardson varieties can be determined by minimum and maximum defining chains, as follows (see e.g.
[

).

Theorem 2.9
.

Let

be permutations. The collection of semi-standard Young tableaux

such that

and
v\leq w^{+}_{1}
forms a monomial basis for
X_{w}^{v}
, where
w_{-}(T)=(w^{-}_{1},\dots,w^{-}_{|T|})
and
w_{+}(T)=(w^{+}_{1},\dots,w^{+}_{|T|})
are the unique minimum and maximum defining chains for

3.1 Block structure on compatible permutations.

To prove Theorem

Definition 3.4
.

Let
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
v_{d}=w_{e}=n
for some

. A
block
of

is a pair of consecutive subsets of

and
w
on the same indices which we write as
(v,w)_{i}^{j}=(\{v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j}\},\{w_{i},w_{i+1},\dots,w_{j}\})
for some

n
.

•

(Creation) If
i=j=e=d
then
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is a block.

•

(Persistence) Assume that
n\notin\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}
and
n\notin\{w_{i},\dots,w_{j}\}
. If
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is a block for
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
then
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is a block for
(v,w)
.

•

(Expansion) Assume that
n\in\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}
and
n\in\{w_{i},\dots,w_{j}\}
. In addition, assume that
i<d
and
e<j
. If

(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}=(\{v_{i},\dots,v_{d-1},v_{d+1}\dots,v_{j}\},\{w_{i},\dots,w_{e-1},w_{e+1},\dots,w_{j}\})

is a block for
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
then
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is a block for
(v,w)
.

The
size
of a block
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is equal to
j-i+1
.

Example 3.5
.

Here we give two examples illustrating properties of blocks.

•

and

.

For

and

. Note that,

.

We say two distinct blocks

Proposition 3.7

Let
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, then the following hold:

(a)

For any block
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
we have
\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}=\{w_{i},\dots,w_{j}\}
and

(b)

Any pair of distinct blocks are non-crossing.

Proof.

For (a) we proceed by double induction: first on
n
and then on the size of the block which is
j-i+1
. If the block has size

, i.e.

, then the result holds trivially. So let us assume that

. If
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is a block for
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
then the results follows by induction on

(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is not a block of
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
then

must occur by the expansion criterion and so by induction the result holds.

Part (b) follows easily by induction

(\underline{v},\underline{w})
.

w_{e}>w_{e+1}>\dots>w_{j}

Proof.

Throughout the proof we write
w_{e^{\prime}}=v_{d^{\prime}}=n-1
for some
e^{\prime},d^{\prime}\in[n]
. We proceed by double induction, first by induction on
n
and then by induction on the size of the maximum block. For any
n
, if the maximum block has size one then the result trivially holds. For the induction step, assume the maximum block has size at least two. It immediately follows that
e<d
, so by compatibility of
v
and
w
, we have:
v_{e}<\dots<v_{d}
,
w_{e}>\dots>w_{d}
,
d^{\prime}\geq e
and
e^{\prime}\leq d
.

Let us write
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{k}^{\ell-1}
for the maximum block of
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
for some
k,\ell\in[n]
, which is the smallest block containing
n-1
. Since
d^{\prime}\geq e
and
e^{\prime}\leq d
, by the expansion criterion, we have that
(v,w)_{k}^{\ell}
is a block containing
n
. Since
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
is the maximum block of
(v,w)
we have that
(v,w)_{k}^{\ell}
is contained in
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
, i.e.

and
j\leq\ell
. Since
n
is contained in the maximum block of
(v,w)
, we must have that the maximum block is obtained from the block
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}
in
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
by the expansion criterion. Therefore,
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}
is a block contained in the maximum block of
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
. However, it can be shown directly from the definition that the maximum block does not properly contain any other blocks. Therefore,
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}=(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{k}^{\ell-1}
is the maximum block and so by induction we have
v_{i}<\dots<v_{d^{\prime}}
and
w_{e^{\prime}}>\dots>w_{j}
. By compatibility we have
v_{e}<\dots<v_{d}
and
w_{e}>\dots>w_{d}
. Since
d^{\prime}\geq e
and
e^{\prime}\leq d
, it follows that
v_{i}<\dots<v_{d}
and
w_{e}>\dots>w_{j}
.

∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
be the maximum block of
(v,w)
. Then we have
\{v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j}\}=\{w_{i},w_{i+1},\dots,w_{j}\}=\{v_{i},v_{i}+1,\dots,n\}=\{w_{j},w_{j}+1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.7
(a), it suffices to show that
\{v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j}\}=\{v_{i},v_{i}+1,\dots,n\}
. We proceed by induction on the size of the maximum block
j-i+1
. If the maximum block has size
1
, then
(v,w)_{i}^{j}=(\{n\},\{n\})
and the result holds trivially.

Assume that the maximum block has size
j-i+1\geq 2
and write
v_{d}=w_{e}=n
and
w_{e^{\prime}}=v_{d^{\prime}}=n-1
for some
d,d^{\prime},e,e^{\prime}\in[n]
. Since
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we have that
v\leq w
and so
e\leq d
. If
e=d
, then
(v,w)_{d}^{d}
is a block that is properly contained in the maximum block
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
, a contradiction. So we must have
e<d
. In particular,
e<j
and
i<d
. By the expansion criterion, we have that
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}
is a block for
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
.

We proceed by showing that
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}
is the maximum block for
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
. To do this, we first show that
n-1\in\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}
. Assume by contradiction that
n-1\notin\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}=\{w_{i},\dots,w_{j}\}
. Since
(v,w)
are compatible, we have that
d^{\prime}\geq e
and
e^{\prime}\leq d
. Therefore
d^{\prime}\geq j+1
and
e^{\prime}\leq i-1
. By the persistence criterion, we have that
\left(\underline{\underline{v}},\underline{\underline{w}}\right)_{i}^{j-1}
is a block for
\left(\underline{\underline{v}},\underline{\underline{w}}\right)
. We write
\left(\underline{\underline{v}},\underline{\underline{w}}\right)_{i}^{j-1}=(\{\tilde{v}_{i},\dots,\tilde{v}_{j-1}\},\{\tilde{w}_{i},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j-1}\})
. Since
i<d\leq j<d^{\prime}
, we have that
\{\tilde{v}_{i},\dots,\tilde{v}_{j-1}\}=\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}\backslash\{n\}
. Since
e^{\prime}<i\leq e<j
, we have that
\{\tilde{w}_{i},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j-1}\}=\{w_{i+1},\dots,w_{j+1}\}\backslash\{n\}
. By Proposition
3.7
(a) applied to
(v,w)_{i}^{j}
we have that
\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}=\{w_{i},\dots,w_{j}\}
. Since
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we have that
\left(\underline{\underline{v}},\underline{\underline{w}}\right)\in\mathcal{T}_{n-2}
. So, by Proposition
3.7
(a) applied to
\left(\underline{\underline{v}},\underline{\underline{w}}\right)_{i}^{j-1}
, we have that
\{\tilde{v}_{i},\dots,\tilde{v}_{j-1}\}=\{\tilde{w}_{i},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j-1}\}
. However, by the above we have
w_{i}\in\{\tilde{v}_{i},\dots,\tilde{v}_{j-1}\}
but
w_{i}\notin\{\tilde{w}_{i},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j-1}\}
, a contradiction.

Next, we show that
(\underline{v},\underline{w})_{i}^{j-1}
does not properly contain another block. Assume by contradiction that

is a block containing

and is properly contained in

and

, and so

. Similarly, by compatibility, we have

and

So we have shown that

∎

In this section we prove Theorem

Lemma 3.11

For a given pair

Proof.

We note that
\underline{v}=\underline{v}^{\prime}
and

and

4
Monomial-free ideals

We recall the definition of the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
from Sections
2.4
and
2.5
and the collection of pairs of permutations
\mathcal{T}_{n}\subseteq S_{n}\times S_{n}
from
(
3.1
). Our main result in this section is the following which gives a complete characterisation for monomial-free ideals of form
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free if and only if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Proof.

The proof follows directly from Lemmas
4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
. In particular, Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
show that if
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then
\init(I_{n-1})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}
is monomial-free and
(v,w)
is a compatible pair. And so, by induction on
n
, we have that if
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. On the other hand, Lemma
4.5
shows that if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
then
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free.

∎

Example 4.2
.

Let
v=(1,3,2)
and

, which are non-compatible permutations. Note that the ideal
\init(I_{2})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}=0
, in particular it is monomial-free. We also have

, where

vanishes in

We now proceed to prove the lemmas used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
We will first show, in Lemma
4.3
, that

\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}\text{ monomial-free }\Rightarrow\init(I_{n})|_{T^{\underline{v}}_{\underline{w}}}\text{ monomial-free.}

. We will show that compatibility is an essential ingredient in showing that

is monomial-free.
In Lemma

\init(I_{n})|_{T^{\underline{v}}_{\underline{w}}}\text{ monomial-free and }(v,w)\text{ compatible }\Rightarrow\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}\text{ monomial-free.}

For the following lemma, recall that for any pair of permutations
(v,w)\in S_{n+1}\times S_{n+1}

v_{t}=w_{t^{\prime}}=n+1
and
v_{s}=w_{s^{\prime}}=n
. In addition we write
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\!\!\uparrow
for the ordered list whose elements are
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}
taken in increasing order.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
v,w\in S_{n+1}
with
\underline{v}\leq\underline{w}
. If
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then
(v,w)
is a compatible pair.

Proof.

Note that if
t^{\prime}=t
then
(v,w)
is compatible. Since
\underline{v}\leq\underline{w}
, we may assume that
t^{\prime}<t
. We have that
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free,
so by Lemma
4.3
, we have that
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}
is monomial-free. We prove that if
(v,w)\in S_{n+1}\times S_{n+1}
is not compatible and
v<w
then
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
contains a monomial.
Let
v_{\ell}=n-1=w_{\ell^{\prime}}
.

Case 1.
Assume that
t<s^{\prime}
. By compatibility we have
t^{\prime}\leq t<s^{\prime}\leq s
. Suppose that
s^{\prime}=s
and
\ell^{\prime}>s
. Let

I=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{s^{\prime}}\}=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{s^{\prime}-2}<n<n+1\},\hskip 10.00002ptJ=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{s^{\prime}-3}<n-1\}.

Note that
\underline{v}\leq\underline{w}
and
s=s^{\prime}<\ell^{\prime}
therefore
i_{s^{\prime}-2}<n-1
. And so we have
v\leq I,J\leq w
. Let

I^{\prime}=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{s^{\prime}-3}<n-1<n<n+1\},\hskip 10.00002ptJ^{\prime}=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{s^{\prime}-2}\}.

By construction it is clear that
P_{I}P_{J}-P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
is a binomial in
\init(I_{n+1})
. Since
n-1\notin\{w_{1},\dots,w_{s^{\prime}}\}
, it follows that
I^{\prime}\not\leq w
. And so
P_{I}P_{J}
is a monomial in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. So we may now assume that either
\ell^{\prime}\leq s
or
s^{\prime}<s
. However, if
s^{\prime}<s
then, by induction, we have
\ell^{\prime}\leq s
. So we assume
\ell^{\prime}\leq s
.

Case 1.1.
Let
\ell^{\prime}>s^{\prime}
. Since
t^{\prime}\leq t<s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}\neq t
, we have
t^{\prime}<s^{\prime}-1
. Take

I=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{s^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow,\hskip 10.00002ptJ=\begin{cases}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{\ell^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~t^{\prime}>1,\\
w_{\ell^{\prime}}&\text{if}~t^{\prime}=1,\end{cases}

I^{\prime}=\begin{cases}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{\ell^{\prime}},w_{t^{\prime}},w_{t^{\prime}+2},\ldots,w_{s^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~t^{\prime}>1,\\
(w_{\ell^{\prime}},w_{t^{\prime}},w_{t^{\prime}+2},\ldots,w_{s^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~t^{\prime}=1\end{cases}\text{ and }

J^{\prime}=\begin{cases}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{t^{\prime}+1})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~t^{\prime}>1,\\
w_{t^{\prime}+1}&\text{if}~t^{\prime}=1.\end{cases}

It is clear from the construction that
P_{I}P_{J}-P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
is a binomial in
\init(I_{n+1})
and
v\leq I,J\leq w
. So
P_{I}P_{J}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. Since
I^{\prime}\nleq w
, then
P_{I^{\prime}}
vanishes in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. Therefore,
P_{I}P_{J}
is a monomial in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 1.2.

Let
\ell^{\prime}<s^{\prime}
. Let
k=\max\{\ell^{\prime},t\}
and
v_{r}=max\{v_{i}:1\leq i\leq k,i\neq t\}
. Now define

I=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\!\!\uparrow,\hskip 10.00002ptJ=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r-1},v_{r+1},\dots,v_{k-1},n-1)\!\!\uparrow,

I^{\prime}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r-1},v_{r+1},\ldots,v_{k},n-1,)\!\!\uparrow\text{ and }J^{\prime}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1})\!\!\uparrow

Consider the tableaux for
P_{I}P_{J}
and
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
. Note that all rows are the same except for the
(k-1)^{th}
row, and in this row we interchange
n-1
and
v_{r}
. Since
k<s^{\prime}\leq s
,
n-1\notin I
and
P_{I}P_{J}-P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
is a binomial in
\init(I_{n+1})
, it follows that
v\leq I,J\leq w
and so
P_{I}P_{J}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.
Since
k<s^{\prime}
and
n-1\in I^{\prime}
, we see that
I^{\prime}\nleq w
. So
P_{I^{\prime}}
vanishes in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 2.
Assume that
t^{\prime}>s
. Then we have
t\geq t^{\prime}>s\geq s^{\prime}
. Let
v_{r}=max\{v_{i}:1\leq i\leq t^{\prime},i\neq s\}
. We define

I=\begin{cases}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{s-1},n+1,v_{s+1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~s>1,\\
(n+1,v_{s+1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~s=1,\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002ptJ=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}-1})\!\!\uparrow,

I^{\prime}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r-1},v_{r+1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}},n+1)\!\!\uparrow\text{ and }J^{\prime}=\begin{cases}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{s-1},v_{s+1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}-1},v_{r})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~s>1,\\
(v_{s+1},\ldots,v_{t^{\prime}-1},v_{r})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~s=1.\end{cases}

By construction,
P_{I}P_{J}-P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
is a binomial in
\init(I_{n+1})
. Since
n-1,n\notin J^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}>s
, we have
v\nleq J^{\prime}
and
P_{I}P_{J}
is a monomial in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 3.
Assume that there exists
t^{\prime}\leq k<t
such that
v_{k}>v_{k+1}
.
We take

I=\begin{cases}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1},v_{k+1},n)\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~k>1,\\
(v_{k+1},n)\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~k=1,\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002ptJ=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\!\!\uparrow,

. Since

and

. Now we show that
I\leq w
. Since
\underline{v}\leq\underline{w}
and

, we have

(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k-1},v_{k+1})\!\!\uparrow\leq(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{t^{\prime}+1},\ldots,w_{k},w_{k+1})\!\!\uparrow.

(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k-1},v_{k+1},n)\!\!\uparrow\leq(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k},w_{k+1})\!\!\uparrow
. Then we see that
P_{I}P_{J}
is non-zero and
P_{J^{\prime}}
vanishes in
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. Hence,
P_{I}P_{J}
is a monomial in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 4.
Assume that there exists
t^{\prime}<k\leq t
with
w_{k}<w_{k+1}
. In this case, we choose:

I=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})\!\!\uparrow,\hskip 10.00002ptJ=\begin{cases}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{t^{\prime}+1},\ldots,w_{k-1},w_{k+1})\!\!\uparrow&\text{if}~t^{\prime}>1,\\

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
v,w\in S_{n+1}
. If
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}
is monomial-free and
(v,w)
is a compatible pair then
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free.

Proof.

We proceed by double induction, first on
n
and secondly on the dimension of the Richardson variety. By Lemma
4.4
, we may assume that for all
v,w\in S_{k}
where
k\leq n
we have
\init(I_{k})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free if and only if
(v,w)
is a compatible pair and
\init(I_{k-1})|_{T^{\underline{v}}_{\underline{w}}}

Fix

and let
P_{I}P_{J}-P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
be a binomial in the ideal
\init(I_{n+1})
and assume that
P_{I}P_{J}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. Without loss of generality we assume that
|I|=|I^{\prime}|\geq|J|=|J^{\prime}|
. We will show that
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}

t,t^{\prime}
.

Case 1.
Assume that
t,t^{\prime}
both lie in one of the following sets:
\{1,\dots,|J|\},\{|J|+1,\dots,|I|\}
or
\{|I|+1,\dots,n+1\}
. For each of these cases we deduce immediately which sets
I,I^{\prime},J,J^{\prime}
contain
n+1
. By removing
n+1
from these sets we obtain
\underline{I},\underline{I}^{\prime},\underline{J},\underline{J}^{\prime}
. We have that
P_{\underline{I}}P_{\underline{J}}-P_{\underline{I}^{\prime}}P_{\underline{J}^{\prime}}

\init(I_{n})
. Since
P_{I}P_{J}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}

P_{\underline{I}}P_{\underline{J}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}
. Since
(\underline{v},\underline{w})\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
we have that
P_{\underline{I}^{\prime}}P_{\underline{J}^{\prime}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{\underline{w}}^{\underline{v}}}

P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}

\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}

Case 2.
Assume that
t^{\prime}\in\{|J|+1,\dots,|I|\}
and
t\in\{|I|+1,\dots,n+1\}
.

Case 2.1
Assume that
n+1\in I
.
By Case i. of the claim on page
3.2
,

. By Lemma
3.11

(v^{\prime},w)\in\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
and so by induction on the dimension
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v^{\prime}}}
. Therefore,
v<v^{\prime}\leq I^{\prime},J^{\prime}\leq w
and so
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 2.2
Assume that
n+1\notin I
. By Case ii. of the claim on page
3.2
, we have that
I\leq w^{\prime}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{t^{\prime}-1},w_{t^{\prime}+1},w_{t^{\prime}},v_{t+1},\dots,w_{n+1})
. Since
|J|<|I|
we have
J\leq w^{\prime}
. By Lemma
3.11
we have that
(v,w^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
and so by induction on the dimension
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w^{\prime}}^{v}}

does not vanish in
\init(I_{n+1})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.

Case 3.
Assume that
t^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,|J|\}
and
t\in\{|J|+1,\dots,|I|\}
. This case is identical to Case 2, where we can use a similar argument to show that either
v^{\prime}\leq J
if
n+1\in J
or
J\leq w^{\prime}
if
n+1\notin J

Case 4.
Assume that
t^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,|J|\}
and

as above and conclude the result by induction on the dimension of

and

Let

be the maximum block of
(v,w)
. Then we have by Proposition
3.9

v_{i}<\dots<v_{t}=n+1

3.7

for all

. And so ordering the first

v
we get

\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t}\}=\{\widehat{v}_{1}<\dots<\widehat{v}_{t}\}=\{\widehat{v}_{1}<\dots<\widehat{v}_{i-1}<v_{i}<\dots<v_{t}\}.

And so for all
k
we have
\widehat{v}_{k}\leq i_{k}
and
\widehat{v}_{k}\leq j_{k}
, i.e. the ordering of the first
|I|
elements of
v
coincides with ordering of the first
|J|
elements of
v
. Since the tableaux representing
P_{I}P_{J}
and
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
are row-wise equal, it follows that
v\leq I^{\prime}
and
v\leq J^{\prime}
.

Next we show that
I^{\prime}\leq w
and
J^{\prime}\leq w
. By Proposition
3.9
we have
w_{t^{\prime}}>\dots>w_{j}
and by Proposition
3.7
we have that
w_{k}<w_{j}
for all
k>j
.

Claim.
For any
K\subseteq[n+1]
:
K\leq w
if and only if
K^{c}:=[n+1]\backslash K\geq ww_{0}=(w_{n+1},w_{n},\dots,w_{1})
.

To prove the claim, it is an easy observation that
K\leq w
if and only if
(K^{\uparrow},(K^{c})^{\uparrow})\leq w
. Then
(K^{\uparrow},(K^{c})^{\uparrow})\leq w
if and only if
(K^{\uparrow},(K^{c})^{\uparrow})w_{0}\geq ww_{0}
. Explicitly we have
(K^{\uparrow},(K^{c})^{\uparrow})w_{0}=((K^{c})^{\downarrow},K^{\downarrow})
. And so
(K^{\uparrow},(K^{c})^{\uparrow})w_{0}\geq ww_{0}
if and only if
K^{c}\geq ww_{0}
. This completes the proof of the claim.

Since the tableaux representing
P_{I}P_{J}
and
P_{I^{\prime}}P_{J^{\prime}}
are row-wise equal, it follows that the tableaux representing
P_{I^{c}}P_{J^{c}}
and
P_{I^{\prime c}}P_{J^{\prime c}}
are also row-wise equal. By the claim above we have that
ww_{0}\leq I^{\prime c}
and
ww_{0}\leq J^{\prime c}
. And so we have
I^{\prime}\leq w
and
J^{\prime}\leq w
.

∎

5
Toric degenerations

Recall the ideals
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
,
\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
and
\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}

from Sections
2.4
and
2.5
.
In this section, we will study
the relationships between these ideals with the goal of understanding the initial ideal
\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
. When this ideal is toric, we obtain a toric degeneration of the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
inside the flag variety.
We recall, by Theorem
2.3
, that the initial ideal
\init(I_{n})
is quadratically generated and is the kernel of the monomial map
\phi_{n}
in (
2.4
).
We will see that if the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then
\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is quadratically generated.
Furthermore, we prove that if
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then

\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}=\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
and

\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is a toric ideal.
We prove this by showing that if
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then it is equal to the ideal
\ker(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have the following:

(i)

The ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free if and only if it coincide with
\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
.

(ii)

\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}\subseteq\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.3
, there exists a set
G
of quadratic binomials which generate the initial ideal
\init(I_{n})
and by Lemma
2.4
(ii), the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is generated by
G|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. See (
2.6
).

(i) First note that
\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v}
is a monomial map, hence its kernel does not contain any monomials. So, if the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
contains a monomial then it is not equal to
\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
.
Now assume that the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
does not contain any monomials, therefore the set
G|_{T_{w}^{v}}
does not contain any monomials.
Since all binomials
m_{1}-m_{2}\in G|_{T_{w}^{v}}
lie in
\init(I_{n})
and contain only the non-vanishing Plücker variables
P_{J}
for
J\in T_{w}^{v}
, therefore
m_{1}-m_{2}\in\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
. And so we have
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}\subseteq\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
. Thus the proof of (i) follows.

(ii) Since
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}=\langle G|_{T_{w}^{v}}\rangle
, we take
\hat{g}\in G|_{T_{w}^{v}}
. So we have that
g\in G
and
there exists
f\in I(X_{w}^{v})
such that
\init(f)=g
.
The terms of
\hat{g}
are precisely the non-vanishing terms of the initial terms of
f
. Therefore,
\hat{g}=\init(\hat{f})\in\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
.
This completes the proof of lemma.

∎

Theorem 5.2
.

If the ideal
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free, then

\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is a toric ideal and it provides a toric degeneration of the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
.

Proof.

Let us consider
\mathcal{M}\subseteq R:=\mathbb{K}[P_{J}:J\in T_{w}^{v}]
be a collection of monomials which are linearly independent in
R/\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
. If the image of
\mathcal{M}
in
R/\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is a linearly dependent subset, then we have
\sum_{m\in M}c_{m}m\in\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
for some
c_{m}\in\mathbb{K}
. So by Lemma
5.1
we have
\sum_{m\in M}c_{m}m\in\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
,
and so the image of
\mathcal{M}
in
R/\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is linearly dependent, a contradiction.
Moreover, since
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free we have that
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}=\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
.
Hence, for all
d\geq 1
, any standard monomial basis for
R/\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
of degree
d
is linearly independent in
R/\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}=R/\ker{(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})}
.
Note that
\ker(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})
is generated by binomials which correspond to pairs of row-wise equal tableaux, whose columns
I
satisfy
v\leq I\leq w
. It is easy to see that every tableau is row-wise equal to a unique semi-standard Young tableau. Therefore, the semi-standard Young tableaux
SSYT_{d}(v,w)
form a monomial basis for the degree
d
part of
R/\ker(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})
.

Note that any Gröbner degeneration gives rise to a flat family, so the Hilbert polynomials of all fibers are identical. By Theorem
3.12
, the semi-standard Young tableaux
SSYT_{d}(v,w)
form a standard monomial basis for
R/I(X_{w}^{v})
.
Thus, the dimension of the degree
d
part of
R/\init(I(X_{w}^{v})
is equal to
|SSYT_{d}(v,w)|
. And so
\init(I_{n})|_{T_{w}^{v}}=\ker(\phi_{n}|_{w}^{v})=\init(I(X_{w}^{v}))
.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

Our methods can be used to produce other toric degenerations of Richardson varieties with respect to different weight vectors. For example, in
[
16
]
, Kim considers a weight on the polynomial ring
\mathbb{K}[x_{i,j}]
such that the leading term of any minor
\varphi_{n}(P_{J})
, see (
2.1
), is the antidiagonal term.
Explicitly, if
M^{\prime}
is the weight on
\mathbb{K}[x_{i,j}]
then

\init_{M^{\prime}}(\varphi_{n}(P_{J}))=x_{1,j_{t}}x_{2,j_{t-1}}\dots x_{t,j_{1}}\text{ for every }J=\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{t}\}.

We write
{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}
for the weight on the ring
\mathbb{K}[P_{J}:J\subseteq[n]]
induced by
M^{\prime}
.
In
[
16
]
, the pairs of permutations
(v,w)
are labelled by collections of so-called
pipe dreams
.
Each pair of reduced pipe dreams associated to
(v,w)
gives rise to a face of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope.
If
(v,w)
is labelled by a unique pair of reduced pipe dreams, then the initial ideal
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is toric.
However, if there are multiple pairs of pipe dreams associated to
(v,w)
, then this method cannot differentiate between toric and non-toric initial ideals. For each
(v,w)\in S_{4}\times S_{4}
we have calculated the ideals
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I_{4})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
.
We have confirmed that Theorem
5.2
holds in all these cases, i.e. if
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I_{4})|_{T_{w}^{v}}
is monomial-free then the initial ideal
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is toric. Our calculations are displayed in Table
1
. The symbol
*
appears in the table beside pairs of permutations for which the description by pipe dreams does not determine whether the corresponding ideal is toric or non-toric.

In many cases, it is possible to give an explicit description of the polytopes associated to toric degenerations. Any toric variety whose ideal is of the form
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I(X_{w}^{v}))
, for some
v
and
w
, is a toric subvariety of the toric variety associated to the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope. Therefore, the toric polytope associated to
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is a face of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope. Suppose that
\init_{{\bf w}_{M^{\prime}}}(I(X_{w}^{v}))
is toric. On the one hand, if there is a unique pair of reduced pipe dreams associated to
(v,w)
, then this face of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope is determined uniquely and is described in terms of Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns in
[
16
]
. On the other hand, if there does not exist a unique pair of pipe dreams associated to
(v,w)
, then determining the face of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope is a difficult computational task.

Computing polytopes of toric degenerations of the flag variety
\Flag_{n}
for large
n
is already very difficult. For example, all toric degenerations via Gröbner degenerations have been calculated up to
\Flag_{5}
, see
[
2
]
. One approach to understand these toric polytopes, and by extension their faces, is to first consider the Grassmannian and its toric degenerations studied in
[
7

3

2.4

[

combinatorial mutations

Example 5.4
.

Let
v=(2,3,4,1)
and
w=(4,2,3,1)
. Let us consider the antidiagonal term order from Remark
5.3
.
We have that
((2,3,4,1),(4,2,3,1))
is contained in Table
1
, and so the Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
degenerates to a toric variety, which we will call
T
.
However, the entry has an asterisk beside it in Table
1
,
so it is not possible to immediately determine the polytope associated to this degeneration of
X_{w}^{v}
using pipe dreams from
[
16
]
.
Let us calculate the polytope associated to this degeneration following
[
5
,
10
]
.

Recall that, via the Plücker embedding, the Flag variety
\Flag_{4}
is a subvariety of the product of projective spaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{5}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. The coordinates for each projective space are
P_{I}
where
I\subseteq[n]
is a fixed size. For instance
[P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4}]
are the homogeneous coordinates for the first copy of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. The Richardson variety
X_{w}^{v}
and its toric degeneration
T
naturally live in the product of projective spaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{0}
, with coordinates given by the non-vanishing variables which are
[P_{2},P_{3},P_{4}],[P_{23},P_{24}]
and
[P_{234}]
, respectively. The ideal of
T\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{0}

\varphi_{4}
, see Remark
5.3
, restricted to the non-vanishing variables. We can write
\varphi_{4}
as the following integer matrix, note that only the non-zero rows are included:

A=\ \ \bordermatrix{&P_{2}&P_{3}&P_{4}&P_{23}&P_{24}&P_{234}\cr x_{2}&1&&&&&\cr x_{3}&&1&&1&&\cr x_{4}&&&1&&1&1\cr y_{2}&&&&1&1&\cr y_{3}&&&&&&1\cr z_{2}&&&&&&1\cr}.

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{0}
under the Segre embedding is a toric subvariety
Y\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
. We label the coordinates for
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
by the corresponding products of variables:
P_{2}P_{23}P_{234},P_{2}P_{24}P_{234}
and so on.
The monomial map, with matrix
S
, associated to
Y
sends each coordinate of
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
