Chapter 1
Preliminaries

1.1
Basic notions from probability theory

We begin with a few basic notions from probability theory, and define Brownian motion. This chapter is largely meant for review and in order to fix notation; a reader unfamiliar with the topics discussed here is advised to consult an introductory probability textbook. A probability space is a triple
(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
. Here
\Omega
is any set whatsoever;
{\cal F}
is a
\sigma
-field: a collection of subsets of
\Omega
, containing
\Omega
itself, and which is closed under complements, finite intersections, and countable unions;
P
is a probability measure. To say that
X
is a random variable (abbreviated
r.v.
) on a probability space
(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
means that
X=X(\omega)
is a function on
\Omega
, with
X^{-1}(A)\in{\cal F}
for all Borel sets
A
. We will also say
X
is
{\cal F}
-measurable
. If
X
is a random variable, there will be a unique smallest
\sigma
-field upon which
X
is measurable; this is referred to as the
\sigma
-field generated by
X
, and is denoted
\sigma(X)
. Two
\sigma
-fields
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
are
independent
if
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
for all
A\in{\cal F},B\in{\cal G}
. Two random variables
X,Y
are independent if the
\sigma
-fields
\sigma(X),\sigma(Y)
are independent.

Suppose that
X
is a r.v. taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. If there is a function
f_{X}(t)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that, for any Borel set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

(1.1)

P(X\in A)=\int_{A}f_{X}(t)dt,

then we will call
f(x)
the
probability density function
(abbreviated as
p.d.f
) for
X
. If
g
is a measurable function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then we define the
expectation
of
g(X)
to be

(1.2)

E[g(X)]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g(t)f_{X}(t)dt.

If
X
takes values on
{\mathbb{R}}
, the
mean
of
X
is then
E[X]
, and the
variance
is
\mbox{Var}(X)=E[X^{2}]-E[X]^{2}
. If
X
and
Y
are independent, then
E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]
and
\mbox{Var}(X+Y)=\mbox{Var}(X)+\mbox{Var}(Y)
. The
normal
or
Gaussian distribution
with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
is a distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}
characterized by the p.d.f.

(1.3)

\rho(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

We will use the notation
X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
to signify that
X
is a normally distributed r.v. with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

A
stochastic process
is an indexed family of r.v.’s
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
and an increasing collection of
\sigma
-fields
\{{\cal F}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
(which means that
{\cal F}_{s}\subseteq{\cal F}_{t}
whenever
s\leq t
) so that each
X_{t}
is
{\cal F}_{t}
-measurable. The collection
\{{\cal F}_{t}\}
is called the
filtration
of the process, and in some sense represents the information available to an observer at any time. In general, we take
{\cal F}_{t}
to be the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{X_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}
.

1.2
Brownian motion

A stochastic process
X_{t}
represents a system of some sort changing over time. Brownian motion is the stochastic process which can most aptly be described as ”purely random motion”. That is, it represents a continuous motion with no memory or momentum. In probabilistic terms, this requires
X_{t}-X_{s}
to be independent of
X_{s}
for
s<t
: the process should have no memory of what it did to get to
X_{s}
in order to decide what to do to get to
X_{t}
. The process should also move in a uniform way over different time intervals; that is,
X_{t}-X_{s}
should have the same distribution as
X_{t-s}-X_{0}
, for instance. However, if these properties hold then we see that we can write
X_{t}-X_{0}=(X_{t/n}-X_{0})+(X_{2t/n}-X_{t/n})+\ldots(X_{nt/n}-X_{(n-1)t/n})
for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and thereby express
X_{t}
as the sum of a large number of i.i.d. random variables. This brings us to the realm of the Central Limit Theorem, which states that the sum of a large number of i.i.d. random variables with variances will be approximately normally distributed. It is therefore natural (and necessary) that our Brownian motion be normally distributed.

One dimensional Brownian motion

B_{t}
is defined to be a stochastic process with the following properties.

(i)

B_{t}:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous a.s.

(ii)

For
t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}
the random variables
B_{t_{1}},B_{t_{2}}-B_{t_{1}},\ldots,B_{t_{n}}-B_{t_{n-1}}
are independent.

(iii)

For
t^{\prime}<t^{\prime\prime}
,
B_{t^{\prime\prime}}-B_{t^{\prime}}
is a normally distributed variable with mean 0 and variance
t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime}
.

In general, we will assume that
B_{0}=a
a.s. for some constant
a
, and we will use the notations
P_{a}
and
E_{a}
to mean probability and expectation taken with respect to this initial condition. That Brownian motion even exists is nontrivial, but well-established, and its study is a fascinating and rich endeavor practiced by many. In this text we will focus on properties of Brownian motion relevant to complex analysis, but so that the reader may get a better feel for the process we briefly describe the trajectories (the image of
B_{t}
for a fixed
\omega
). With probability 1, Brownian paths are nondifferentiable for every
t
and unbounded but recurrent, which means that they return infinitely often to every point. Brownian motion satisfies
self-similarity
, which means roughly that the path looks identical regardless of the scale at which it is viewed; this can be seen by noting that
\frac{1}{c}B_{c^{2}t}
for
c\in(0,\infty)
satisfies the requirements above and is therefore a Brownian motion. The process is therefore revealed as possessing non-smooth, crinkled-up paths which travel slowly throughout
{\mathbb{R}}
but always return to their starting point.

A
stopping time

\tau
is a random variable taking values in
[0,\infty]
such that

(1.4)

\{\tau\leq t\}\in{\cal F}_{t}.

The most commonly used stopping times are
hitting times
, and we will make extensive use of them in this book. For a closed set
K
in
{\mathbb{C}}
, let
T_{K}=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{t}\in K\}
.
T_{K}
is the hitting time of
K
, and is a stopping time. In fact, strictly speaking the set
K
need not be closed in order for
T_{K}
to be a stopping time; however, we choose to require this because for an open set
\Omega
we define
T_{\Omega}=T_{\delta\Omega}=T_{\Omega^{c}}
, the hitting time of the complement of
\Omega
(or the
exit time
of
\Omega
). This is a bit of an abuse of notation; however, the point is that we will be generally running Brownian motion on open sets and stopping it when it exits the set or hits a particular closed set, and we tailor our definitions accordingly.

The importance of stopping times can be seen in the following theorem, as well as in Itō’s Theorem in the next chapter.

Theorem 1
(Strong Markov property)

If
\tau
is a finite stopping time, and
\hat{B}_{t}=B_{\tau+t}-B_{\tau}
, then
\hat{B}_{t}
is a Brownian motion independent of
\{B_{t}:0\leq t\leq\tau\}
.

The proof of this theorem is also nontrivial. The reader interested in seeing the finer details of the result, of the construction of Brownian motion, or of any of the other properties given here is referred to
as well as any number of other excellent texts on the subject available in any academic library.

The primary object of interest in this book will be
planar Brownian motion
, which is simply the process
(R_{t},I_{t})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, where
R_{t}
and
I_{t}
are independent one dimensional Brownian motions. Henceforth, any reference to Brownian motion will be to the planar variety, unless otherwise specified. The following important observation leads to the development in the rest of the book.

Theorem 2

(i)

Brownian motion is rotation invariant. That is, if
\Theta
is a rotation of the plane, then
Y_{t}=\Theta(B_{t})
is again a Brownian motion.

(ii)

If
c\in[0,\infty)
, then
Z_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}B_{ct}=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}R_{ct},\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}I_{ct})
is again a Brownian motion.

Proof:
That
Y_{t}
and
Z_{t}
are continuous with independent increments are clear. It is also clear that the components of
Z_{t}
are independent, and each component can be shown to be normally distributed with the correct mean and variance by a simple calculation. Since the components of Brownian motion are independent, the p.d.f. for
B_{t}
starting at 0 is simply the product of the p.d.f.’s of the components, namely
\rho_{t}(x,y)=\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-x^{2}/2t}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-y^{2}/2t}\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi t}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2t}
. Converting to polar coordinates, this is
\rho_{t}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi t}e^{-r^{2}/2t}
, which is rotation invariant (i.e. does not depend on
\theta
). Thus,
\Theta(B_{t})
has the same distribution as
B_{t}
, namely a bivariate normal with independent components which are normalized to have mean
0
and variance
t
. A similar argument holds if the Brownian motion starts at a point other than 0.

Let us examine this theorem more closely. A rotation by
\theta
takes the point
(r,\theta_{0})
in polar coordinates to
(r,\theta_{0}+\theta)
, or in rectangular coordinates takes
(r\cos\theta_{0},r\sin\theta_{0})
to
(r\cos(\theta_{0}+\theta),r\sin(\theta_{0}+\theta))
. Using the angle sum formulas for
\cos,\sin
, this is
(r\cos\theta_{0}\cos\theta-r\sin\theta_{0}\sin\theta,r\sin\theta_{0}\cos\theta+r\cos\theta_{0}\sin\theta)
. If we then multiply both components by
c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, we arrive at the point
(rc\cos\theta_{0}\cos\theta-rc\sin\theta_{0}\sin\theta,rc\sin\theta_{0}\cos\theta+rc\cos\theta_{0}\sin\theta)
, and we know that this composition of maps takes Brownian motion to Brownian motion (with the clock being changed according to Theorem
2
). Associating this map with the point
(c\cos\theta,c\sin\theta)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we are led naturally to the operation
(x,y)\dot{(}x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(xx^{\prime}-yy^{\prime},xy^{\prime}+x^{\prime}y)
, and we will call this
complex multiplication
. Associating the point
(1,0)
with the real number 1, and using the symbol
i
for
(0,1)
, we can write any vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
as
x+yi
, and complex multiplication becomes
(x+yi)(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}i)=(xx^{\prime}-yy^{\prime})+(xy^{\prime}+x^{\prime}y)i
. Note that
i^{2}=-1
, which is often taken as the starting point of an investigation into complex numbers. We will refer to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with this multiplication as the
complex plane
, and write
{\mathbb{C}}
to denote it.

1.3
Complex numbers

Let
z=x+yi\in{\mathbb{C}}
. We will refer to the real numbers
x
and
y
as the
real
and
imaginary parts
of
z
, and write
Re(z)=x,Im(z)=y
. Define the
modulus
of
z
to be
|z|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, the ordinary Euclidean metric. We define
\bar{z}=x-iy
and refer to
\bar{z}
as the
conjugate
of z; one of the primarily uses of
\bar{z}
lies in the identity
z\bar{z}=|z|^{2}
. This allows us to define quotients on
{\mathbb{C}}
by reducing to division by real numbers, since if
w\neq 0
then we may write

(1.5)

\frac{z}{w}=\frac{z\bar{w}}{|w|^{2}}=\frac{Re(z\bar{w})}{|w|^{2}}+i\frac{Im(z\bar{w})}{|w|^{2}}.

Armed with these definitions, it is straightforward to verify the following important facts.

Proposition 1

(i)

|zw|=|z||w|
.

(ii)

|\frac{z}{w}|=\frac{|z|}{|w|}
.

(iii)

(Triangle inequality)
|z+w|\leq|z|+|w|
.

(iv)

(Reverse triangle inequality)
|z\pm w|\geq||z|-|w||
.

(v)

|z\pm w|^{2}=|z|^{2}\pm 2Re(z\bar{w})+|w|^{2}
.

The proof of this proposition is left to the reader. Fundamental to the study of the complex numbers is
Euler’s formula
:

(1.6)

e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta.

This can be verified by considering the well-known Taylor series for the real-valued functions
e^{x},\cos x,\sin x
. This allows us to extend the domain of the exponential function to the entire complex plane by
e^{x+yi}=e^{x}(\cos y+i\sin y)
. The exponential so extended satisfies virtually every property one would like; the following proposition lists some of the more basic ones.

Proposition 2

(i)

If
z=x+yi
, then
|e^{z}|=e^{x}
, and
\frac{Im(e^{z})}{Re(e^{z})}=\tan y
.

(ii)

The function
e^{z}
is periodic with period
2\pi i
. That is,
e^{z}=e^{z+2\pi i}
.

(iii)

e^{z_{1}+z_{2}}=e^{z_{1}}e^{z_{2}}
.

(iv)

e^{\bar{z}}=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{e^{z}}}
.

Again, we leave the proof to the reader. Part
(i)
includes an important new fact, that the angle which the vector
e^{z}
makes with the positive real axis is controlled by
y
. We will define the
argument
of any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
to be
\mbox{arg }(z)=\tan^{-1}\frac{Im(z)}{Re(z)}
; this is clearly an ambiguous definition, since the value of
\tan^{-1}
is only defined up to multiples of
2\pi
, and so for exactitude we define the
principal argument
of
z
to be
\mbox{Arg }(z)=\mbox{Tan }^{-1}\frac{Im(z)}{Re(z)}
, where here
\mbox{Tan }^{-1}
is the branch of
\tan^{-1}
which takes values in
(-\pi,\pi]
. We see that
\mbox{Arg }z
is well defined for all
z\neq 0
, although the function
\mbox{Arg }z
is discontinuous on the negative real axis. We see that for any
z=x+yi\neq 0
we can write
z=re^{i\theta}
, where
r=|z|
and
\theta=\mbox{Arg }z
(or, for that matter, any branch of
\arg z
). We will consider this to be the polar form of
z
. Note that the properties in Propositions
1
and
2
show that
(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{1}})(r_{2}e^{i\theta_{2}})=r_{1}r_{2}e^{i(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}
.

Let us now consider a number of simple transformations of
{\mathbb{C}}
. We have seen that a rotation of the plane can be realized by multiplication by a complex number with modulus 1, which can be expressed as
e^{i\theta}
for some
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. That is, the function
f(z)=e^{i\theta}z
is simply a rotation of
{\mathbb{C}}
by
\theta
radians counterclockwise. By an
expansion
of
{\mathbb{C}}
, we mean a function of the form
f(z)=rz
, where
r\in[0,\infty)
; this fixes the arguments of all points in
{\mathbb{C}}
, and simply scales the moduli of all points by the multiplicative factor
r
. A
dilation
is the composition of a rotation and expansion, and can therefore be expressed as
f(z)=re^{i\theta}z
. A
translation
will be a map of the form
f(z)=z+c
, which simply shifts the entire plane by
c
. Composing a translation with a dilation gives a general
linear function

f(z)=az+c
. These are essentially the most basic functions from
{\mathbb{C}}
to itself, and it would be well for the reader to entirely familiarize themselves with the geometric properties of these transformations, as they will be important throughout.

The
stereographic projection
is a method of adding a ”point at infinity” to
{\mathbb{C}}
in order to make
{\mathbb{C}}
compact, which helps greatly in visualizing various transformations

Proposition 3

If
{\cal C}
is a circle on
{\cal S}
, then
\Theta({\cal C})
is either a circle or line in

. To be specific, if
{\cal C}
passes through

is a straight line in
{\mathbb{C}}
, while if
{\cal C}
does not pass through
(0,0,1)
then
\Theta({\cal C})
is a circle in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof:

below. However, we can give a simple analytic one as follows. A circle

on
{\cal S}
can be realized as the intersection of
{\cal S}
with a plane with equation
ax_{1}+bx_{2}+cx_{3}=l
, where
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}=1
and

. Substituting the formula for
\Theta^{-1}(z)
given above into this equation and simplifying leads to

(1.7)

(c-l)(x^{2}+y^{2})+2ax+2by=c+l.

If
c\neq l
, then this is the equation of a circle in
{\mathbb{C}}
(the bijective nature of
\Theta
shows that the circle does not degenerate). If, however,

, then it is the equation of a line, and note in this case that the point
(0,0,1)
lies on
{\mathbb{C}}
.

A set

. A set is

boundary
of

in

converging to

and also a sequence

in

converging to
z
. A set

is

if, for any

there is a path in
U
connecting them. Equivalently, an open set is connected if it cannot be expressed as the union of two nonempty, disjoint, open sets. A

is an open, connected subset of

. The generic picture of a domain therefore looks something like

; the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}=\{|z|<1\}

Chapter 2
Dynkin’s formula and Lévy’s Theorem

In this chapter we begin with the formula which governs the manner at which the expectation of
u(B_{t})
evolves over time, where
u
is any
C^{2}
function on a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}
. We will see that this formula, which we will refer to as
Dynkin’s formula
, leads naturally to the definitions of harmonic and analytic functions and the power series expansion of each. It may therefore be considered one of the fundamental formulas in complex analysis, in addition to its obvious importance in probability theory.

2.1
Dynkin’s formula

The
Laplacian differential operator

\Delta
is defined by
\Delta=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}
. This can be applied to a real-valued function on a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}
, and if
u
is complex-valued, so that
u=u_{1}+iu_{2}
with
u_{1},u_{2}
real-valued, then we simply have
\Delta u=(\Delta u_{1})+i(\Delta u_{2})
. The following theorem indicates the prominent position of the Laplacian in the study of Brownian motion.

Theorem 3

Suppose that
u(z)=u(x,y)
is a
C^{2}
function on a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
a\in\Omega
. Suppose
\tau<T_{\Omega}
with
E[\tau]<\infty
is a stopping time such that
u
and its derivatives up to order 2 are a.s. bounded on the set
\{B_{t}:0\leq t\leq\tau,B_{0}=a\mbox{ a.s.}\}
. Then

(2.1)

E_{a}[u(B_{\tau})]-u(a)=\frac{1}{2}E_{a}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Delta u(B_{s})ds.

If
u
extends continuously to
\delta\Omega
, then the same conclusion holds even if we relax the requirement
\tau<T_{\Omega}
to
\tau\leq T_{\Omega}
.

Proof:
By considering real and imaginary parts separately, we may assume
u
is real valued. We begin by partitioning
[0,\infty)
as
0=s_{0}<s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots
with
s_{n}\nearrow\infty
, and write using independence

(2.2)

\begin{split}E_{a}[u(B_{\tau})]-u(a)&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{a}[u(B_{s_{n+1}\wedge\tau})-u(B_{s_{n}\wedge\tau})]\\
&\approx\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{a}[(u(B_{s_{n+1}})-u(B_{s_{n}}))1_{\{\tau\geq s_{n}\}}]\end{split}

Taylor’s Theorem allows us to write

(2.3)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptu(B_{s_{n+1}})-u(B_{s_{n}})=

\displaystyle u_{x}(B_{s_{n}})(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})+u_{y}(B_{s_{n}})(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})+\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}(B_{s_{n}})(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle+u_{xy}(B_{s_{n}})(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})+\frac{1}{2}u_{yy}(B_{s_{n}})(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})^{2}+o(|B_{s_{n+1}}-B_{s_{n}}|^{2}),

where as before
R
and
I
are the real and imaginary parts of
B
, respectively. Note that
(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})
and
(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})
are independent of
1_{\{\tau\geq s_{n}\}}
, since
\tau
is a stopping time, and also independent of the terms of the form
u_{x}(B_{s_{n}}),u_{yy}(B_{s_{n}})
, etc. Thus, when we plug (
2.3
) into (
2.2
) we may factor the expectation and use the facts that
E_{a}[R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}}]=E_{a}[I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}}]=E_{a}[(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})]=0
and
E_{a}[(R_{s_{n+1}}-R_{s_{n}})^{2}]=E_{a}[(I_{s_{n+1}}-I_{s_{n}})^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}E_{a}[|B_{s_{n+1}}-B_{s_{n}}|^{2}]=(s_{n+1}-s_{n})
. We arrive at

(2.4)

E_{a}[u(B_{\tau})]-u(a)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{a}[(u_{xx}(B_{s_{n}})+u_{yy}(B_{s_{n}}))1_{\{\tau\geq s_{n}\}}](s_{n+1}-s_{n})+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}o(s_{n+1}-s_{n})E_{a}[1_{\tau\geq s_{n}}].

We now let the partition become finer, so that the mesh size goes to
0
. Since
E_{a}[\tau]<\infty
, the last term approaches 0, and the first term approaches
\frac{1}{2}E_{a}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Delta u(B_{s})ds
, giving (
2.1
). We made use of a number of approximations along the way, but since
u
and its derivatives are bounded in the interior of
\gamma
these approximations behave correctly in the limit, and the result follows.

Remark:
The boundedness assumptions on
u
and
\Omega
are important; see Exercise
2.5
. The requirement that
\tau
must be a stopping time is also important; see Exercise
2.5
.

The conditions on the stopping time in the statement of the theorem may appear to be difficult to check in practice. However, we will use primarily one type of stopping time in order to apply this theorem: the hitting time of a bounded, closed curve
\gamma
which lies in
\Omega
and separates
a
from
\delta\Omega
.

The fact that
u
is
C^{2}
implies easily that
T_{\gamma}
automatically satisfies the required boundedness condition on
u
and its derivatives, so that it must only be shown that
E[T_{\gamma}]<\infty
. Since any bounded curve is contained in a disk centered at
a
, this is an immediate consequence of the following proposition.

Proposition 4

E_{0}[T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}]=\frac{r^{2}}{2}
.

Proof:
Fix large
N>0
and let
\tau=\tau(N)=T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}\wedge N
. Note that
E[\tau]<N<\infty
, so we may apply Dynkin’s formula with
u(x+yi)=x^{2}+y^{2}
. We have
u(0)=0
and
\Delta u\equiv 4
; we therefore obtain

(2.5)

E_{0}[u(B_{\tau})]=\frac{1}{2}E_{0}\int_{0}^{\tau}4ds=2E_{0}[\tau].

Since
u\leq r^{2}
on
\{B_{t}:0\leq t\leq\tau\}
, we see that
E[\tau]\leq r^{2}/2
independently of
N
, and we may let
N\nearrow\infty
and apply monotone convergence to conclude that
E[T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}]<\infty
. This implies that
T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}<\infty
a.s. so that
E_{0}[u(B_{T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}})]=r^{2}
, and also allows us to apply Dynkin’s formula directly to
T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}
in order to conclude that
r^{2}=2E_{0}[T_{r{\mathbb{D}}}]
.

We now have a large class of stopping times for which we can apply Dynkin’s formula. However, for arbitrary
u
it is of course difficult to say much about the integral term
\int_{0}^{\tau}\Delta u(B_{s})ds
; nevertheless, there is one instance in which this expression is easy to determine, and this leads to an immensely valuable definition. We will say that the function
u
is
harmonic
if
\Delta u(z)=0
for all
z
in the domain of
u
. It is then immediate from Theorem
3
that
E_{a}[u(B_{\tau})]=u(a)
. Taking
u(x+yi)=x+yi
, which is clearly harmonic, gives

Corollary 2

Suppose
u
is harmonic and
\tau
is a stopping time satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3
. Then
E_{a}[u(B_{\tau})]=u(a)
.

In particular, if we apply this corollary to the identity function
u(z)=z
, which is trivially harmonic, we see that if
E[\tau]<\infty
is a stopping time such that
\sup\{|B_{t}|:0\leq t\leq\tau,B_{0}=a\mbox{ a.s.}\}<\infty
then
E_{a}[B_{\tau}]=a
. This fact admits a fruitful interpretation. If
f
is a function such that
f(B_{t})
is a Brownian motion, then we will have
E_{a}[f(B_{\tau})]=f(a)
. This will hold for a Brownian motion run at a different speed, since all that matters is the values of
B_{\tau}
, and therefore by Proposition
2
will hold for any linear function
f(z)=cz+b
; but since
\tau
is a variable time it will also hold for a Brownian motion run at a variable speed. In order to make this precise, we define a
time change
to be a family of stopping times
\{C_{t}:t\geq 0\}
with respect to a given filtration which is a.s. non-decreasing and right continuous in
s
. We will then say that a process
X_{t}
is a time changed Brownian motion if there is a time change
C_{t}
of
X_{t}
such that
X_{C_{t}}
is a Brownian motion; or, equivalently, there is a Brownian motion
B_{t}
and a time change
C_{t}
of
B_{t}
such that
B_{C_{t}}=X_{t}
a.s. The prior argument shows that if we have a function
f
such that
f(B_{t})
is a time changed Brownian motion, then
E_{a}[f(B_{\tau})]=f(a)
for all suitable stopping times
\tau
. It should be no great stretch of the imagination to suppose that this equation is extremely useful, though just how useful it will turn out to be may surprise the reader. Suffice it to say that a large percentage of what follows in this book can be reduced at its core to an application of this result. In any case, the next logical task is to identify a large class of functions which map Brownian motion to a time change of Brownian motion. This is the subject of the next section.

2.2
Analytic invariance of Brownian motion

We have seen in Proposition
2
that Brownian motion is invariant under dilations and translations, and therefore under linear maps
f(z)=cz+b
, although the time must be adjusted by the square of the expanding factor
|c|
. It turns out that this phenomenon holds for the much more general class of functions which are ”locally dilating”, in the following sense. We will call a function
f
on a domain
\Omega

analytic
if it can be expressed as

(2.6)

f(z)=f(z_{0})+c(z-z_{0})+o(|z-z_{0}|)

around any point
z_{0}\in\Omega
, where
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a constant (depending upon
z_{0}
). That is,
f
is a continuous function whose behavior close to
z_{0}
is dominated by a dilation term. Equivalently, we may say that
f(z)
is analytic if

(2.7)

\lim_{\Delta z\longrightarrow 0}\frac{f(z_{0}+\Delta z)-f(z_{0})}{\Delta z}

exists for all
z_{0}\in\Omega
. In other words, analytic functions are simply complex differentiable functions, and for that reason we will denote the limit in (
2.7
) as
f^{\prime}(z_{0})
; however, the reader is forewarned that the behavior of analytic functions is far more restricted than that of differentiable functions of a real variable, and not to attempt to analogize too much between these two types of differentiable functions. This will be revealed as we progress through the subject.

The primary connection between analytic functions and Brownian motion lies in the following beautiful result, which is known as Lévy’s Theorem.

Theorem 4

Let
f
be analytic and nonconstant on a domain
U
, and let
a\in U
. Let
B_{t}
be a planar Brownian motion which starts at
a
and is stopped at a stopping time
\tau
such that
\tau\leq T(U)
a.s. Set

\sigma(t)=\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}|f^{\prime}(B_{s})|^{2}ds.

for
t\in[0,\tau]
.
\sigma(t)
is a.s. strictly increasing and continuous, and we let
C_{t}=\sigma^{-1}(t)
on
[0,\sigma(\tau)]
. Then
\hat{B}_{t}=f(B_{C_{t}})
is a Brownian motion stopped at the stopping time
\sigma(\tau)
.

We will often refer to the new Brownian motion
\hat{B}_{t}
and stopping time
\sigma(\tau)
as the
projections
of
B_{t}
and
\tau
under
f
. An intuitive proof of the theorem is simple. We know that dilations preserve Brownian motion, but that the time scale must be adjusted by the square of the expanding factor. Therefore, locally dilating maps preserve Brownian motion as well, except that in the more general setting the expansion factor is different at different points in the domain. We must therefore scale the time of the transformed Brownian motion according to the accumulated square of the expansion factors at all of the points that the Brownian motion has visited, which is done via
\sigma_{t}
and its inverse
C_{t}
. Q.E.D.

A rigorous proof of Theorem
4
would require quite a bit of work, including the introduction of stochastic integration. Although this is a fascinating and important subject in its own right, we choose not to include it in our development. A reader unsatisfied with the intuitive explanation given here is advised to consult
[
41
]
,
[
38
]
, or
[
70
]
for complete proofs of the result.

Note also that, for a analytic function
f
, we have
f(a)=E_{f(a)}[B_{C_{\sigma_{T}}}]=E_{a}[f(B_{T})]
; this is the same formula which harmonic functions satisfy, though at this stage we do not know that analytic functions are harmonic, or even that they are
C^{2}
. As we will see in the last section of this chapter, however, analytic functions are indeed harmonic.

Now that we have the concept of analytic functions, a natural question arises: what functions are analytic? We begin by noting that the standard proofs from calculus that sums, differences, products, quotients (with nonzero denominators), and compositions of differentiable functions are again differentiable carry over directly into the complex setting, so that we may build analytic functions by combining others, with the formulas for the derivatives given by the ordinary sum, product, quotient, and chain rules. We may therefore commence with the observations that the constant function
f(z)=c
and identity function
f(z)=z
are trivially analytic, which immediately gives us all polynomials, with the derivative of
\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{n}z^{n}
given by
\sum_{n=1}^{d}na_{n}z^{n-1}
. It is natural to ask whether we may carry this over to ”infinite degree polynomials”; that is, if
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
is a power series, is
f(z)
analytic? The following proposition answers this question.

Proposition 5

Consider the expression
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
. Let
R\in[0,\infty]
be given by
\frac{1}{R}=\limsup_{n\longrightarrow\infty}|a_{n}|^{1/n}
.

(i)

The partial sums
\sum_{n=0}^{M}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
converge on
D(z_{0},R)
and do not converge on
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{36.0}}}{{D(z_{0},R)}}^{c}=\{|z-z_{0}|>R\}
(if
R=0
, this signifies convergence only at
z=z_{0}
, and if
R=\infty
this signifies convergence for all
z
).

(ii)

This convergence is uniform on
D(z_{0},r)
for any
r<R
.

(iii)

If we let
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}=\lim_{M\longrightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{M}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
, then
f
is analytic on
D(z_{0},R)
, and
f^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}na_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-1}
.

Proof:
If
|z-z_{0}|<r<R
, then

(2.8)

\Big|\sum_{n=N}^{M}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}\Big|\leq\sum_{n=N}^{M}(|a_{n}|^{1/n}|z-z_{0}|)^{n}\leq\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}((\frac{1}{R}+\varepsilon)r)^{n},

where
\varepsilon
can be made arbitrarily small by requiring
N
and
M
to be sufficiently large. We may therefore take
(\frac{1}{R}+\varepsilon)r<1
, and it follows that
\sum_{n=N}^{M}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}\longrightarrow 0
uniformly as
N,M\longrightarrow\infty
. This implies that
\sum_{n=0}^{M}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
is a Cauchy sequence, uniformly on
D(z_{0},r)
, proving
(ii)
and the first part of
(i)
. The second part of
(i)
follows easily in a similar manner. To prove
(iii)
, we use the identity
a^{n}-b^{n}=(a-b)(a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b+\ldots+ab^{n-2}+b^{n-1}
and the uniform convergence to calculate

(2.9)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z)&=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z+h-z_{0})^{n}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}((z+h-z_{0})-(z-z_{0}))\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}((z+h-z_{0})^{n-1}+(z+h-z_{0})^{n-2}(z-z_{0})+\ldots+(z-z_{0})^{n-1})}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}((z+h-z_{0})^{n-1}+(z+h-z_{0})^{n-2}(z-z_{0})+\ldots+(z-z_{0})^{n-1})\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}na_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-1}.\end{split}

We see that convergent power series give us analytic functions, so by taking the Taylor series of well-known transcendental functions, such as the exponential and trigonometric ones, we obtain a wealth of analytic functions. But what about analytic functions not given by power series? It turns out that there are none, at least in a local sense: every analytic function can be expressed as a convergent power series over any disc in its domain. Our next order of business will be to prove this important fact, which we will do in Section
2.4
. First, however, we must study a prominent class of analytic functions known as Möbius transformations.

2.3
Möbius transformations

This section is devoted to the class of analytic functions known as
Möbius transformations
. We will see that these functions allow us to transform any three points on the plane to any other three, leading to useful simplifications in many cases. This will lead naturally in the next section to an expression for the exit distribution of Brownian motion from a disc, which in turn leads to the Poisson integral formula and the expansion into power series for harmonic functions, two of of the cornerstones of complex analysis. Furthermore, the class of analytic self-maps of the Reimann sphere is coincident with the class of Möbius transformations. Fluency in these maps is therefore a must for any complex analyst.

A Möbius transformation is a function of the form
\phi(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
for
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{C}},ad-bc\neq 0
. The determinant condition
ad-bc\neq 0
is simply to ensure that
\phi(z)
is not a constant. We have seen two types of Möbius transformations already: the translation
\phi(z)=z+b
and the dilation
\phi(z)=az
. Another simple example is the
inversion

\phi(z)=1/z
. It is a standard and useful fact that these simple forms can be used to build all other Möbius transformations.

Lemma 1

Any Möbius transformation can be expressed as the composition of translations, dilations, and the inversion.

Proof:
Let
\phi(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
be an arbitrary Möbius transformation. If
c=0
then
\phi
is merely a linear map, which is clearly the composition of a translation and dilation. If
c\neq 0
, let
\phi_{1}(z)=z+d/c
,
\phi_{2}(z)=1/z
,
\phi_{3}(z)=\frac{(bc-ad)}{c^{2}}z
, and
\phi_{4}(z)=z+a/c
. Then the reader may check that
\phi=\phi_{4}\circ\phi_{3}\circ\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}
.

If
c\neq 0
then it may seem that
\phi
is undefined at
z=-d/c
; however, it is far more profitable to consider
\phi
as a map from the Riemann sphere to itself with the definitions
\phi(-d/c)=\infty
and
\phi(\infty)=a/c
. Defined as such, Möbius transformations are bijections from the Riemann sphere to itself (this is left to the reader to check, in Exercise
2.5
below), and as quotients of polynomials are analytic at all points as well (analyticity at
-d/c
means that
1/\phi
is analytic there, and analyticity at
\infty
means that
\phi(1/z)
is analytic at 0). If
c=0
, so that
\phi
is a linear map, we define
\phi(\infty)=\infty
, and likewise obtain a analytic bijection of the sphere to itself. Möbius transformations are most naturally understood on the Riemann sphere rather than in the plane, as the following important proposition indicates.

Proposition 6

Möbius transformations map circles to circles.

Remark:
This and ensuing statements about circles should be taken in the context of the Riemann sphere, and recall from Section
1.3
that a circle on the sphere is a circle or straight line in the plane. Thus, the image of a circle or straight line in the plane is either a circle or straight line in the plane, but a circle may be mapped to a line and vice versa.

Proof:
Lemma
1
shows us that we need only prove the proposition for translations, dilations, and the inversion. We will take it as evident for translations and dilations, and for the inversion we note that a circle on the sphere can be realized as all points
z
which satisfy

(2.10)

a|z|^{2}+bz+c\bar{z}+d=0,

for some
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{C}}
(a straight line in the plane occurs precisely when
a=0
). Replacing
z
by
\frac{1}{z}
and multiplying through by
|z|^{2}
gives

(2.11)

a+b\bar{z}+cz+d|z|^{2}=0,

again the equation of a circle on the sphere (see also Exercise
2.5
below).

Proposition 7

(i)

The composition of two Möbius transformations is again a Möbius transformation. Furthermore, if
\phi(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
is a Möbius transformation, then
\phi^{-1}
is as well, and is given by
\phi^{-1}(w)=\frac{dw-b}{-cw+a}
.

(ii)

If
(a,b,c),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
are a pair of sets of three distinct points on the sphere, then there is a Möbius transformation taking
a\longrightarrow a^{\prime},b\longrightarrow b^{\prime}
, and
c\longrightarrow c^{\prime}
.

(iii)

If
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are Möbius transformations which agree at three distinct points then they coincide for all
z
.

Remark:
The formula for the inverse in
(i)
may look familiar, and this is no coincidence; see Exercise
2.5
below.

Proof:

(i)
is a simple calculation. For
(ii)
, let
\phi_{a,b,c}=\frac{(b-c)(z-a)}{(b-a)(z-c)}
; here
\phi_{a,b,c}(a)=0,\phi_{a,b,c}(b)=1
, and
\phi_{a,b,c}(c)=\infty
. Define
\phi_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}
accordingly. Then
\phi_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\phi_{a,b,c}
takes
a\longrightarrow a^{\prime},b\longrightarrow b^{\prime}
, and
c\longrightarrow c^{\prime}
.
(ii)
allows us to pre- and postcompose by appropriate Möbius transformations in order to reduce
(iii)
to showing that a Möbius transformation which fixes
0,1,
and
\infty
must be the identity; however, a Möbius transformation fixing
\infty
is simply a linear map, and the only degree 1 polynomial with two fixed points is the identity, so the result follows.

Proposition
6
shows that circles are important when studying Möbius transformations, and Proposition
7
shows that triples of points are as well. This suggests that triples of points are closely connected with circles, and this is indeed so.

Proposition 8

If
a,b,c
are distinct points on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, then there is precisely one circle on the sphere which passes through all three.

Proof:
If one of the points is
\infty
, then it is easy to see that the circle must be the line in the plane through the other two. Otherwise, construct the perpendicular bisectors of the line segments
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hskip 3.61371pt\line{1}{0}{15.0}}}{{ab}}
and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hskip 3.61371pt\line{1}{0}{15.0}}}{{bc}}
. If the two bisectors are parallel, then
a,b,c
are colinear, and the circle on the sphere in question is the straight line through the three of them. If, on the other hand, the two bisectors intersect, then this point of intersection is the unique point in the plane equidistant from
a,b,c
, and is therefore the center of the unique circle passing through the three of them.

This and the previous propositions lead to an easy test as to whether a circle can be found which passes through four given points: simply find the Möbius transformation mapping three of the points to
0,1,\infty
, and see where it sends the fourth point. If the fourth point is sent to the real axis, then the four points lie on a circle, but if it is not then they do not. With this end in mind, define the
cross ratio
of the points
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}
as

(2.12)

(z_{1},z_{2};z_{3},z_{4})=\frac{(z_{1}-z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{4})}{(z_{2}-z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{4})}.

The reader may compare with the formula given in Proposition
7
to see that this is the image of
z_{4}
under the Möbius transformation taking
z_{1}\longrightarrow\infty
,
z_{2}\longrightarrow 0
, and
z_{3}\longrightarrow 1
. We see that four points lie on a circle if their cross ratio is real. Proposition
6
then implies that, for any Möbius transformation
\phi
,
(z_{1},z_{2};z_{3},z_{4})
is real if and only if
(\phi(z_{1}),\phi(z_{2});\phi(z_{3}),\phi(z_{4}))
is real. However, much more is true.

Proposition 9

For any Möbius transformation
\phi
and any four points
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}
,

(2.13)

(\phi(z_{1}),\phi(z_{2});\phi(z_{3}),\phi(z_{4}))=(z_{1},z_{2};z_{3},z_{4}).

Proof:
This is a simple calculation for translations, dilations, and the inversion. Proposition
1
therefore gives the result.

For
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
consider the Möbius transformation

(2.14)

\psi_{a}(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\bar{a}z}.

It can be shown that for
|z|\leq 1
we have
|\psi_{a}(z)|\leq 1
, with equality precisely when
|z|=1
(see Exercise
2.5
below). Thus,
\psi_{a}
is a analytic self-map of
{\mathbb{D}}
sending
a
to 0. This map will allow us to give an easy solution to an important problem. Suppose we distinguish an interval
{\cal I}
on the unit circle; for
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
, what is
P_{a}(B_{T_{\mathbb{D}}}\in{\cal I})
? That is, what is the probability that a Brownian motion starting at
a
will exit
{\mathbb{D}}
through
{\cal I}
? We first note that the rotation invariance of Brownian motion shows that, if
a=0
, then the quantity
P_{0}(B_{T_{\mathbb{D}}}\in{\cal I})
must be a rotation invariant measure on
\{|z|=1\}
; and this implies that it is equal to
\frac{m({\cal I})}{2\pi}
, where
m({\cal I})
is the Lebesgue measure, or length, of
{\cal I}
. For
a\neq 0
, we may simply use the map
\psi_{a}
to map
a
to
0
and use the fact that
\psi_{a}(B_{t})
is a time-changed Brownian motion; note that our question is concerned with the exit probability of the Brownian motion but not the time, and therefore we need not even bother with keeping track of the time change. Clearly, the Brownian motion
B_{t}
leaves
{\mathbb{D}}
through
{\cal I}
if, and only if, the Brownian motion
\psi_{a}(B_{t})
leaves
{\mathbb{D}}
through
\psi_{a}({\cal I})
:

We obtain the following identity:

(2.15)

P_{a}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}\in{\cal I})=P_{0}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}\in\psi_{a}({\cal I}))=\frac{m(\psi_{a}({\cal I}))}{2\pi}.

To calculate
m(\psi_{a}({\cal I}))
, note simply that the map
\psi_{a}
expands an infinitesimal portion of
{\cal I}
centered at a point
e^{it}\in{\cal I}
with expansion factor
|\psi_{a}^{\prime}(e^{it})|
. It follows that

(2.16)

P_{a}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}\in{\cal I})=\frac{m(\psi_{a}({\cal I}))}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\cal I}}|\psi_{a}^{\prime}(e^{it})|dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\cal I}}\frac{1-|a|^{2}}{|1-\bar{a}e^{it}|^{2}}dt.

In the next section we will explore applications of this formula for harmonic and analytic functions.

2.4
Poisson’s integral formula and power series

Let us suppose that we have a harmonic function
h
on
{\mathbb{D}}
which is continuous on
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{{\mathbb{D}}}}
. We then have by Dynkin’s formula, for any
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

(2.17)

h(a)=E_{a}[B_{T_{\mathbb{D}}}]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{it})P_{a}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}\in dt).

Recalling the formula for exit probabilities from the previous section, we let
{\cal I}
shrink to an infinitesimal interval to obtain the identity

(2.18)

P_{a}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}\in dt)=\frac{1}{2\pi}|\psi_{a}^{\prime}(e^{it})|dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1-|a|^{2}}{|1-\bar{a}e^{it}|^{2}}dt.

Let
{\cal P}_{r}(\theta)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{|1-re^{-i\theta}|^{2}}
; this is known as the
Poisson kernel
. If we set
a=re^{i\theta}
, then (
2.18
) gives us

Theorem 5
(Poisson integral formula)

Let
h
be a harmonic function on
{\mathbb{D}}
which is continuous on
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{{\mathbb{D}}}}
. Then

(2.19)

h(re^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{it}){\cal P}_{r}(\theta-t)dt.

There are several equivalent forms for the Poisson kernel (see Exercise
2.5
below), one of which is
{\cal P}_{r}(\theta)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta}
; this is an extremely useful fact, since it allows us to write

(2.20)

\begin{split}h(a)&=h(re^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{it})\Big(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r^{|n|}e^{in(\theta-t)}\Big)dt=\\
&=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{it})e^{-int}dt\\
&=\beta_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\bar{a}^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}a^{n},\end{split}

where

(2.21)

\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{it})e^{-int}dt.

Note that
h
is continuous on
\bar{\mathbb{D}}
and thus bounded there, so that interchanging summation and integration in (
2.20
) is justified. We see that
h
can be expressed as the sum of power series in
a
and
\bar{a}
. There is nothing special about the unit disk for the purposes of this argument; it applies equally well to disks with arbitrary centers and radii, and we therefore obtain

Theorem 6

Let
h(z)
be harmonic (or analytic) on
D(z_{0},R)
. Then we may write

(2.22)

h(z)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n},

for all
z\in D(z_{0},R)
, with
\beta_{n}
given by

(2.23)

\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi r^{n}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(z_{0}+re^{it})e^{-int}dt,

for any
0<r<R
. These series converge uniformly on
D(z_{0},R^{\prime})
for any
R^{\prime}<R
. Furthermore, the coefficients in the series are unique, and
h
is real valued if and only if
\beta_{-n}=\bar{\beta}_{n}
for all
n
.

Proof:
The first statement has already been proved, and the uniform convergence follows as in Proposition
5
. The final statement follows from a standard technique in Fourier analysis; see Exercise
2.5
below.

We will somewhat informally refer to the first sum in (
2.40
) as the
\bar{z}
-part
, and the second as
z
-part
, of the power series for
h(z)
. Since
z
and
\bar{z}
are simply linear combinations of
x
and
y
, we obtain

Corollary 3

If
h
is harmonic or analytic, then
h
is
C^{\infty}
.

Define the differential operators

(2.24)

\frac{d}{dz}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{d}{dx}-i\frac{d}{dy}),\qquad\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{d}{dx}+i\frac{d}{dy}).

These are known as the
Wirtinger derivatives
, and satisfy a number of nice properties, such as

Lemma 2

Suppose
f,g
are differentiable functions, and
a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Then

(i)

(Sum/difference rule)

(2.25)

\frac{d}{dz}(af+bg)=a\frac{df}{dz}+b\frac{dg}{dz},\qquad\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}(af+bg)=a\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}+b\frac{dg}{d\bar{z}}.

(ii)

(Product rule)

(2.26)

\frac{d}{dz}(fg)=f\frac{dg}{dz}+g\frac{df}{dz},\qquad\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}(fg)=f\frac{dg}{d\bar{z}}+g\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}.

(iii)

(Chain rule)

(2.27)

\frac{d}{dz}(f\circ g)=\Big(\frac{df}{dz}\circ g\Big)\frac{dg}{dz}+\Big(\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}\circ g\Big)\frac{d\bar{g}}{dz},\qquad\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}(f\circ g)=\Big(\frac{df}{dz}\circ g\Big)\frac{dg}{d\bar{z}}+\Big(\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}\circ g\Big)\frac{d\bar{g}}{d\bar{z}}.

(iv)

For integer
n\geq 1
, we have

(2.28)

\frac{d}{dz}z^{n}=nz^{n-1},\qquad\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}\bar{z}^{n}=n\bar{z}^{n-1},\qquad\frac{d}{dz}\bar{z}^{n}=\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}z^{n}=0.

(v)

If
h
is
C^{2}
, then

(2.29)

\Delta h=4\frac{d^{2}h}{dzd\bar{z}}=4\frac{d^{2}h}{d\bar{z}dz}.

The properties given in this lemma are all consequences of standard rules in multivariate calculus, and their proofs are left to the reader (Exercise
2.5
). An immediate consequence of the lemma is the following.

Proposition 10

Suppose
h(z)
is harmonic (or analytic) on
D(z_{0},R)
, and given by (
2.40
). Then

(2.30)

\frac{dh}{dz}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-1},\qquad\frac{dh}{d\bar{z}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n-1}.

On the other hand, we are now in a position to prove

Proposition 11

If
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{\tau})]
for any appropriate stopping time
\tau
, then
h
is harmonic.

Proof:
The fact that
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{\tau})]
means that the proof of Poisson’s Integral formula (
2.19
) applies, and in particular
h
can be expressed in a series expansion of the form (
2.40
). It is now clear that
\Delta h=4\frac{d^{2}h}{dzd\bar{z}}=0

The reader will note that the condition that
h
is analytic is relegated to parentheses in the statements of Theorem
6
and Proposition
10
; this is to signify that, while these results do hold for analytic functions, in fact much more is true. If
f
is analytic at
z_{0}
, then the limit in (
2.7
) must exist, regardless of the manner in which
\Delta z\longrightarrow 0
. In particular, we may let
\Delta z=x
be real, separate
f
into real and imaginary parts
u+iv
, and obtain

(2.31)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z_{0})&=\lim_{x\longrightarrow 0}\frac{f(z_{0}+x)-f(z_{0})}{x}=\lim_{x\longrightarrow 0}\frac{u(z_{0}+x)-u(z_{0})}{x}+\lim_{x\longrightarrow 0}\frac{v(z_{0}+x)-v(z_{0})}{x}\\
&=\frac{du}{dx}(z_{0})+i\frac{dv}{dx}(z_{0}).\end{split}

We may then conduct the similar operation where
\Delta z=iy
is purely imaginary, to obtain

(2.32)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z_{0})&=\lim_{y\longrightarrow 0}\frac{f(z_{0}+yi)-f(z_{0})}{yi}=\lim_{y\longrightarrow 0}\frac{u(z_{0}+yi)-u(z_{0})}{yi}+\lim_{y\longrightarrow 0}\frac{v(z_{0}+yi)-v(z_{0})}{yi}\\
&=-i\frac{du}{dy}(z_{0})+\frac{dv}{dy}(z_{0}).\end{split}

Equating real and imaginary parts of (
2.31
) and (
2.32
) gives the
Cauchy-Riemann equations
:

(2.33)

\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{dv}{dy},\qquad\frac{du}{dy}=-\frac{dv}{dx}.

Analytic functions satisfy these equations at all points in their domains. Note that we may write

(2.34)

\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}=\frac{du}{d\bar{z}}+i\frac{dv}{d\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{du}{dx}-\frac{dv}{dy}\Big)+\frac{i}{2}\Big(\frac{du}{dy}+\frac{dv}{dx}\Big)=0.

Thus, the second series in (
2.30
) is identically 0 for analytic functions, which means that all coefficients
\beta_{-n}
must be zero as well. Theorem
6
and Proposition
10
are therefore eclipsed for analytic functions by

Theorem 7

Let
f(z)
be analytic on
D(z_{0},R)
. Then we may write

(2.35)

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}.

This series converges uniformly on
D(z_{0},R^{\prime})
for any
R^{\prime}<R
. Furthermore,
f^{\prime}
is analytic as well, and we have

(2.36)

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{df}{dz}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-1}.

We also have
\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}=0
.

Remark:
The relationship between harmonic and analytic functions can now be clarified. If
f
is analytic then the series expansion (
2.35
) holds, and thus
Re(f)=\frac{f+\bar{f}}{2}
and
Im(f)=\frac{f-\bar{f}}{2i}
are both power series in
z
and
\bar{z}
and are therefore harmonic. On the other hand, if
f=u+iv
is
C^{2}
and satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations, then

(2.37)

\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}u}{dy^{2}}=\frac{d^{2}v}{dydx}-\frac{d^{2}v}{dxdy}=0,\qquad\frac{d^{2}v}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}v}{dy^{2}}=-\frac{d^{2}u}{dydx}+\frac{d^{2}u}{dxdy}=0,

so that
u
and
v
are each individually harmonic.
f
is therefore harmonic and admits the expansion given in (
2.40
), but with all coefficients
\beta_{-n}
equal to 0 by the Cauchy-Riemann equations; it is therefore analytic.

3.1
General properties of harmonic functions

We begin by studying
h(z)=\ln|z|=\frac{1}{2}\ln|z|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\ln(x^{2}+y^{2})
. A straightforward calculation reveals
h
to be harmonic (showing
\frac{d^{2}h}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}h}{dy^{2}}=0
is not difficult, but the slick way is to calculate
\frac{dh}{dzd\bar{z}}
and use Lemma
2
). Thus, for
r<R
the function
h_{r}^{R}(z)=\frac{\ln R-\ln|z|}{\ln R-\ln r}
is harmonic on
A=A_{r,R}:=\{r<|z|<R\}
, and it is equal to 1 on
\{|z|=r\}
and 0 on
\{|z|=R\}
. Fix
a
with
r<|a|<R
; by Itō’s formula we have
h_{r}^{R}(a)=E_{a}[h_{r}^{R}(B_{T_{A}})]=P(|B_{T_{A}}|=r)
. We therefore obtain the probability that
B_{t}
exits
A
at the inner circle
\{|z|=r\}
when started at
a
. We can now derive two important facts about planar Brownian motion by this observation. The first to notice is that, upon letting
R\nearrow\infty
, we see that
h_{r}^{R}(a)\nearrow 1
; this tells us that
B_{t}
is
recurrent
: regardless of the starting point,
B_{t}
will hit any open set eventually with probability
1
. On the other hand, if we hold
R
fixed and let
r\searrow 0
, then
h_{r}^{R}(a)\searrow 0
as well; this tells us that
P_{a}(B_{t}=0\mbox{ for some }t>0)=0
, and the same holds if 0 is replaced by any
b\neq 0
. This may seem counterintuitive, since after all
P(\cup_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\{B_{t}=z\mbox{ for some }t>0\})=P(B_{t}\mbox{ hits some point in }{\mathbb{C}})=1
, but since there are uncountably many points in
{\mathbb{C}}
we do not run into a contradiction by summing probabilities. This curious property of Brownian motion is commonly expressed by a phrase like, ”planar Brownian motion is recurrent but doesn’t see points”. We also can note that this phenomenon sheds new light the holomorphic invariance of Brownian motion, Theorem
4
, as the reader may have noticed a potential problem with the intuitive proof given for that result: a holomophic function is not a local dilation at a point at which its derivative is 0. However, we will see that the zero set of a holomorphic function is a countable set, and therefore Brownian motion avoids the zero set of the derivative with probability 1, and again we do not run into trouble.

Let us calculate the power series for
h(z)
around
z=1
. We have

(3.1)

\frac{d}{dz}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\ln z\bar{z}\Big)=\frac{1}{2z}=\frac{1}{2(1+(z-1))}=\frac{1}{2}(1-(z-1)+(z-1)^{2}-\ldots).

Proposition
10
tells us that integrating this series will give us the
z
-part of the series for
h
, while the final statement of Theorem
6
and the fact that
h
is real valued tell us that the
\bar{z}
-part is obtained by conjugation. Using
h(1)=0
, we arrive at the following expression:

(3.2)

h(z)=\ln|z|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-1)}}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(z-1)^{n}.

The reader will note that this reduces to the familiar series
\ln x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(x-1)^{n}
when
z=x
is real.

Let us now consider the function
\tilde{h}(z)=\mbox{Arg }z=\tan^{-1}\frac{y}{x}=\tan^{-1}-i(\frac{z-\bar{z}}{z+\bar{z}})
, defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash(-\infty,0]
. Another straightforward calculation shows that
\Delta\tilde{h}=4\frac{d^{2}\tilde{h}}{dzd\bar{z}}=0
, and
\tilde{h}
is harmonic. Suppose that
-\pi<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\pi
, and suppose
\theta_{1}<\mbox{Arg }a<\theta_{2}
. If we set
\tilde{h}_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}(z)=\frac{\theta_{2}-\mbox{Arg }z}{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}}
, we see that
\tilde{h}_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}
is equal to 1 on
\{\mbox{Arg }z=\theta_{1}\}
and equal to 0 on
\{\mbox{Arg }z=\theta_{2}\}
. Thus, if we set
W=W_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}=\{\theta_{1}<\mbox{Arg }z<\theta_{2}\}
, then
\tilde{h}_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}(a)=E_{a}[h_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}(B_{T_{W}})]=P_{a}(\mbox{Arg }(B_{T_{W}})=\theta_{1})
(there is a subtlety here: since the domain
W
is unbounded, there are many harmonic functions with the given boundary values; however, in Chapter
we will see that there is only one such function which remains bounded, and this must then be equal to
P_{a}(\mbox{Arg }(B_{T_{W}})=\theta_{1})
). That is, we obtain the probability that
B_{t}
hits the set
\{\mbox{Arg }z=\theta_{1}\}
before hitting
\{\mbox{Arg }z=\theta_{2}\}
when started at
a
, and this probability is linear in
\mbox{Arg }a
.

Let us compute the power series for
\tilde{h}
around
z=1
. We have

(3.3)

\frac{d}{dz}\Big(\tan^{-1}i(\frac{z-\bar{z}}{z+\bar{z}})\Big)=\frac{-i}{2z}=\frac{-i}{2(1+(z-1))}=\frac{-i}{2}(1-(z-1)+(z-1)^{2}-\ldots).

As before, Proposition
10
tells us that the
z
-part of the series for
\tilde{g}
comes by integrating this series, while the
\bar{z}
-part is again obtained by conjugation. Again we have
\tilde{h}(1)=0
, so we obtain

(3.4)

\tilde{h}(z)=\mbox{Arg }z=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-1)}}^{n}+\frac{-i}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(z-1)^{n}.

This looks suspiciously like the series for
\ln|z|
, and we arrive at an important idea. We note that we can write

(3.5)

h(z)+i\tilde{h}(z)=\ln|z|+i\mbox{Arg }z=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(z-1)^{n},

and Theorem
7
tells us that we have a holomorphic function. Thus, the seemingly unrelated harmonic functions
h
and
\tilde{h}
have an intimate relationship with each other, namely that they can be taken to be the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function. We will call any such pair
harmonic conjugates
. The reader may note a seeming incongruity with this example, however, which is that the functions
h
and
\tilde{h}
do not have the same domains of definition:
h
is harmonic on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
, while
\tilde{h}
can only be defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash(-\infty.0]
, as the function
\mbox{Arg }z
cannot be made to be continuous on

.

We are led immediately to a fundamental question: suppose
h(z)
is any real-valued harmonic function; can we find another real-valued harmonic function
\tilde{h}(z)
such that
h+i\tilde{h}
is analytic? We note that the previous example shows that this is not always the case for all domains, however we can show easily using power series that it is the case when the domain of definition is a disk.

Theorem 8

Suppose
h(z)
is a harmonic function on
D(z_{0},R)
. Then there is another harmonic function
\tilde{h}(z)
such that
h+i\tilde{h}
is holomorphic on
D(z_{0},R)
. This function is unique up to adding a constant.

Proof:
Although the argument is implicit in the example of the logarithm, we prove it in generality here. We know that we may write

(3.6)

h(z)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}.

\tilde{h}
must now be chosen such that the power series for
h+i\tilde{h}
contains no
\bar{z}
-part. If we write

(3.7)

\tilde{h}(z)=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n},

we see that we must have

(3.8)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}+i\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}=0,

and we must therefore have
\alpha_{-n}=i\beta_{-n}
. Having obtained this, the requirement that
\tilde{h}
be real-valued forces
\alpha_{n}=\bar{\alpha}_{-n}=-i\bar{\beta}_{-n}=-i\beta_{n}
. We therefore obtain the unique (up to choice of
\alpha_{0}
) harmonic conjugate as

(3.9)

\tilde{h}(z)=\alpha_{0}+i\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(z-z_{0})}}^{n}-i\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n},

and we have

(3.10)

h+i\tilde{h}=\beta_{0}+\alpha_{0}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}.

We will see later that a disk may be replaced by any simply connected domain.

Let us now establish some other properties of harmonic functions.

Theorem 9

[Mean value property]

Suppose
h
is harmonic on a domain
W
, and the set
\{|z-a|\leq r\}\subseteq W
. Then

(3.11)

h(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(a+re^{i\theta})d\theta.

Proof:
By Dynkin’s formula,
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T(D(a,r))}]
, and by the rotation invariance of Brownian motion the distribution of
B_{T(D(a,r))}
on
\{|z-a|=r\}
is uniform. The result follows.

In other words,
h(a)
is equal to the average of its values on a circle in
W
centered at
a
. Note that this result can also be obtained from the Poisson Integral Formula in Chapter 1.

Theorem 10
(Maximum/minimum principle)

Suppose
W
is a bounded domain. If
h(z)
is harmonic on
W
and continuous on
cl(W)
then

\inf_{w\in\delta W}h(w)\leq h(z)\leq\sup_{w\in\delta W}h(w)

for all
z\in W
. Furthermore, if
h
is nonconstant, then

\inf_{w\in\delta W}h(w)<h(z)<\sup_{w\in\delta W}h(w)

for all
z\in W
.

Note that if
W
is the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
, then this result follows from the Poisson Integral Formula (
2.19
) by virtue of the fact that
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\cal P}_{r}(t)dt=1
. The general result will be shown in the next section.

Theorem 11
(Uniqueness principle)

Suppose
W
is a bounded domain. If
h_{1}(z),h_{2}(z)
are harmonic on
W
and continuous on
cl(W)
with
h_{1}(z)=h_{2}(z)
for all
z\in\delta W
, then
h_{1}(z)=h_{2}(z)
for all
z\in W
.

Proof:
Apply the maximum/minimum principle to the harmonic function
h_{1}-h_{2}
.

Theorem 12

If
h(z)
is harmonic and bounded on
{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}=\{0<|z|<1\}
, then we can define
h(0)
such that
h(z)
so extended is harmonic on all of
{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof:
Dynkin’s formula implies that
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T({\mathbb{D}}^{\times})})]
. As was shown at the beginning of this chapter, Brownian motion does not see points, and thus
P_{a}(B_{T({\mathbb{D}}^{\times})}=0)=0
. The distribution of
B_{T({\mathbb{D}}^{\times})}
is therefore the same as that of
B_{T({\mathbb{D}})}
, and thus
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T({\mathbb{D}}^{\times})})]
. As was shown in Chapter 1,
h(z)
can therefore be expressed as a power series in
z
and
\bar{z}
, and this series provides the required extension to all of
{\mathbb{D}}
.

3.2
The Dirichlet Problem

The Dirichlet problem in
{\mathbb{C}}
is the following.

Dirichlet problem

If
U
is a bounded domain, and
h
a continuous real-valued function on
\delta U
, to extend
h
to a harmonic function on
U
which is continuous on the closure of
U
.

As we will see, in many cases the Dirichlet problem may be solved by running a Brownian motion. To be precise, we will show that
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})]
provides the desired solution whenever
U
is a sufficiently nice domain. However, there are domains, such as
{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}=\{0<|z|<1\}
, for which the Dirichlet problem is not solvable. To see this, suppose that
h
is harmonic on
{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
, continuous on its closure, with
h(z)=1
for
|z|=1
and
h(0)=0
. By Theorem
12
,
h
is in fact harmonic on all of
{\mathbb{D}}
, and thus by Theorem
11

0=h(0)\geq\inf\{|z|=1\}h(z)=1
, a contradiction. Nonetheless, we have the following classical result.

Theorem 13

The Dirichlet problem is solvable for any bounded simply connected domain
U
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof:
Since
U
is bounded,
T(U)<\infty
a.s. We can therefore extend
h
from
\delta U
to
cl(U)
by setting
h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})]
, and we must show that
h
so extended on
U
and continuous on
cl(U)
. Harmonicity follows from the Strong Markov property:

h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})]=E_{a}[E_{B_{T(D(a,r))}(\omega^{\prime})}[h(B_{T(U)}(\omega))]]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(a+re^{it})dt;

note that in the final expectation in this string of equalities, the inner expectation is taken with respect to
\omega\in\Omega
while the outer one is taken with respect to
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}
, where the probability spaces
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
are independent. To show continuity we must prove that a Brownian motion started near a boundary point of
U
is likely to exit near that point, in particular

Lemma 3

If
y
is a boundary point of a simply connected domain
U
, then given any
\varepsilon>0
we can find
\delta>0
such that
P_{a}(|B_{T(U)}-y|<\varepsilon)>1-\varepsilon
whenever
|a-y|<\delta
.

The continuity of
h
on
cl(U)
now follows from from this by writing

E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})]=E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})1_{\{|B_{T(U)}-y|<\varepsilon\}}]+E_{a}[h(B_{T(U)})1_{\{|B_{T(U)}-y|\geq\varepsilon\}}]

and noting that for
a
sufficiently close to
y
the first term on the right is as close as we like to
h(y)
and the second can be made as small as we like (using the boundedness of
h
).

Proof of Lemma
3
:
Consider the domain
V_{M}=\{Re(z)<M\}\backslash L
, where
L
is the ray in
{\mathbb{C}}
emanating from
2\pi i
horizontally to the left; see Figure
3.1
. We note that if
M
is sufficiently large then
P_{0}(B_{T(V_{M})}\in L)>1-\varepsilon
; this is evident, since as
M\nearrow\infty
we have
P_{0}(B_{T(V_{M})}\in L)\nearrow P_{0}(T(V_{\infty})<\infty)
, where
V_{\infty}={\mathbb{C}}\backslash L
, and
P_{0}(T(V_{\infty})<\infty)=1
(proof: by Lévy’s Theorem,
arg(B_{t}-2\pi i)
is a time-changed one-dimensional Brownian motion, is therefore recurrent, and thus is equal to
-\pi
at some finite time a.s.). We may therefore choose
\delta
sufficiently small so that
P_{0}(B_{T(V_{M})}\in L)>1-\varepsilon
when
M=\ln\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta}
, and ensure also that
\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
. We claim now that
P_{a}(B_{T(U)}\in B(y,\varepsilon))>1-\varepsilon
whenever
|a-y|<\delta
.

Figure 3.1:
The mapping by
f
from
V_{M}
to
\{|z-y|<e^{M}\}
.

To prove this, suppose that
|a-y|<\delta
, and let
w
be the point in
\delta U
closest to
a
; note that
w
is not necessarily the same as
y
, but
|a-w|<\delta
which implies by the triangle inequality that
|y-w|<2\delta<\varepsilon
. Consider now the analytic function
f(z)=(a-w)e^{z}+w
: it takes
0
to
a
, takes
L
to the line segment
[a,w)
connecting
a
and
w
, and wraps the vertical line
\{Re(z)=M\}
around the circle
\{|z-w|=|a-w|e^{M}=(\frac{|a-w|}{\delta})\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}
. We will realize a Brownian motion starting at
a
via Lévy’s Theorem as the image under
f
of a Brownian motion starting at
0
. The projection
\tau=\sigma(T(V_{M}))
under
f
of the stopping time
T(V_{M})
is again a stopping time; we can describe it as ”the first time when either
B_{t}
lies on the line segment
(a,w)
having wound once anticlockwise about
w
or
|B_{t}-w|=(\frac{|a-w|}{\delta})\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
”. Note that
P_{a}(B_{\tau}\in(a,w))=P_{0}(B_{T(V_{M})}\in L)>1-\varepsilon
. Furthermore, on the event
\{B_{\tau}\in(a,w)\}
we may connect
B_{\tau}
to
a
by a straight line segment and form a closed path which winds about the boundary point
w
; if this path remained entirely within
U
this would contradict simple connectivity, and since
(a,w)
lies within
U
we conclude that
B_{t}
intersects
\delta U
before
\tau
. Thus, on the event
\{B_{\tau}\in(a,w)\}
we have
T(U)<\tau\leq\inf\{t\geq 0:|B_{t}-w|=(\frac{|a-w|}{\delta})\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}
. The triangle inequality implies easily that
\{|z-w|<(\frac{|a-w|}{\delta})\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}\subseteq\{|z-w|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}\subseteq\{|z-y|<\varepsilon\}
, and we conclude that
P_{a}(T(U)<\inf\{t\geq 0:|B_{t}-y|=\varepsilon\})>1-\varepsilon
. Since
\{T(U)<\inf\{t\geq 0:|B_{t}-y|=\varepsilon\}\}\subseteq\{|B_{T(U)}-y|<\varepsilon\}
, the lemma is proved.

Remarks:

•

The ray
L
can be replaced in the proof by the union of the rays emanating to the left from all points of the form
n2\pi i
for integer
n\neq 0
. This would form an even smaller stopping time
\tau
, but would not change the proof in any meaningful way.

•

It may be interesting to note that the choice of
\delta
depends only on
\varepsilon
and not on
U
.

•

The proof given here can be easily extended to other situations, such as when the complement of
U
is formed of finitely many connected components, none of which is a single point.

•

The boundedness requirement on
U
can in many cases be relaxed, but the Dirichlet problem and even the maximum/minimum and uniqueness principles for harmonic functions are rather subtle for unbounded domains. See Chapter
8
for more details on this.

3.3
The Identity Theorem, Open Mapping Theorem, and Maximum Principle

We now discuss a number of results on general analytic and harmonic function. We begin with a result, commonly referred to as the Identity Theorem, which states that if two analytic functions coincide on a set which contains an accumulation point than they agree everywhere. Note that this is not true for real-valued differentiable functions on the line, as for instance the function
f(x)=e^{-1/x}
for
x>0
and
f(x)=0
for
x\leq 0
is
C^{\infty}
on the real line, agrees with the constant function 0 on the negative real axis, and yet is clearly not equal to 0 for all
x
.

Theorem 14

Let
f
be a nonconstant analytic function on a domain
W
, and let
{\cal Z}
be the zero set of
f
; that is, let
{\cal Z}=\{z\in W:f(z)=0\}
. Then
{\cal Z}
is a discrete set in
W
.

Proof:
Let
z_{0}\in{\cal Z}
. We know that in some disk centered at
z_{0}
we can write
f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
. Let
N\geq 1
be the smallest index so that
\beta_{N}\neq 0
; we can then write
f(z)=(z-z_{0})^{N}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-N}
. As
z\longrightarrow z_{0}
,
\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n-N}\longrightarrow\beta_{N}\neq 0
, and it follows by continuity that this expression is nonzero on some (possibly smaller) disk centered at
z_{0}
. Since
(z-z_{0})^{N}
is only 0 at
z_{0}
, we see that
f
is not zero in this disk except at
z_{0}
, and
z_{0}
is therefore not an accumulation point of
{\cal Z}
.

Corollary 4

If
f,g
are analytic on a domain
W
, and the set
\{z:f(z)=g(z)\}
has an accumulation point in
W
, then
f(z)=g(z)
for all
z
in
W
.

Proof:
Apply Theorem
14
to the analytic function
f-g
.

We now will prove that the image under a nonconstant analytic function of an open set is again open. This is commonly known as the
Open Mapping Theorem
. Note that, again, nothing like this is true for real-valued differentiable functions on the line, as for instance the image of the real line under the sine function is the closed interval
[-1,1]
. First, we need a lemma about Brownian motion.

Lemma 4

Let
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and for any
\delta>0
let
\tau_{\delta}=\inf\Big\{t\geq 0:B_{t}\in\{|z-a|=\delta\}\Big\}
. Fix
r>0
, and let
V
be an open set contained in
D(a,r)
, where
D(a,r)=\{|z-a|<r\}
. Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion starting at
a
. Then
P(B_{t}\in V\mbox{ for some }t\leq\tau_{r})>0
.

Proof:
We have

(3.12)

P_{a}(B_{t}\in V\mbox{ for some }t\leq\tau_{r})\geq P_{a}(B_{t}\in{\cal C}\mbox{ for some }t\leq\tau_{r})\geq P_{a}(B_{\tau_{r^{\prime}}}\in{\cal C})=\frac{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}}{2\pi}.

Theorem 15

Let
f
be a nonconstant analytic function on a domain
W
. Then
f(W)
is a domain.

Proof:
We must prove that
f(W)
is open and connected. Analyticity is not needed for connectedness: any continuous image of a connected set is connected, since if
A
and
B
disconnect
f(W)
then
f^{-1}(A)
and
f^{-1}(B)
disconnect
W
. Openness is a bit more tricky. Let
v\in f(W)
, and choose
a\in W
such that
f(a)=v
. By Corollary
4
we can choose a small
r>0
such that
\bar{D}(a,r)\subseteq W
and
f(w)\neq v
on
\{|w-a|=r\}
. If we start a Brownian motion at
a
and stop it at the hitting time
\tau
of
\{|w-a|=r\}
, then
\hat{B}_{t}=f(B_{C_{t}})
is a Brownian motion starting at
v
and stopped at the stopping time
C^{-1}_{\tau}
, which satisfies
f(\hat{B}_{C^{-1}_{\tau}})=f(B_{\tau})\in f(\{|w-a|=r\})
. Let
\gamma
denote the curve
f(\{|w-a|=r\})
. In traveling from
v
to
\gamma
,
\hat{B}_{t}
must cross first the circle
\{|w-v|=m\}
, where
m=\inf\{|w-v|:w\in\gamma\}>0
; so if we let
\hat{\tau}
be the first time
\hat{B}_{t}
hits
\{|w-v|=m\}
then
C^{-1}_{\tau}\geq\hat{\tau}
a.s.

Lemma
4
now shows that
\hat{B}_{t}=f(B_{C_{t}})
hits any open set in
\{|w-v|<m\}
with positive probability before time
\hat{\tau}
, and this shows in particular that
\{|w-v|<m\}
is contained in the closure of
f(\bar{D}(z,r))
. However,
\bar{D}(z,r)
is a compact set, so its image under the continuous map
f
is compact and thus closed, and therefore
\{|w-v|<m\}\subseteq f(\bar{D}(z,r))\subseteq f(W)
. This proves that
f(W)
is open.

Theorem 16

Let
f
be a nonconstant analytic function on a domain
W
. Then
f
does not attain its maximum value on
W
; that is, there is no point
z
such that
|f(z)|=\sup_{w\in W}|f(w)|
for all
w\in W
.

Proof:
Let
{\cal M}=\{z\in W:|f(z)|=\sup_{w\in W}|f(w)|\}
.
{\cal M}
is closed, being the preimage of a point under the continous map
f
; but we may argue that it is open as well, as follows. For
a\in{\cal M}
, choose a small
r>0
such that
\bar{D}(a,r)\subseteq W
, and let
\tau
be the first time Brownian motion hits
\{|w-a|=r\}
. Then
|f(a)|=|E_{a}[f(B_{\tau})]|\leq E_{a}[|f(B_{\tau})|]\leq\sup_{|w-a|=r}|f(w)|\leq|f(a)|
. All inequalities in this string must then be equalities, which can only happen if
|f(w)|=|f(a)|
almost surely on
\{|w-a|=r\}
. By continuity,
|f(w)|=|f(a)|
for all
w
on this circle, and by repeating this argument for any radii less than
r
we see that
|f(w)|=|f(a)|
for all
w\in D(a,r)
. This proves
{\cal M}
is an open set, and since it is both open and closed the connectedness of
W
now proves that
{\cal M}=\emptyset
or
{\cal M}=W
. If
{\cal M}=W
then
f(W)\subseteq\{|w|=const.\}
, but this implies by Theorem
15
that
f
is constant, a contradiction. Thus,
{\cal M}=\emptyset
, proving the theorem.

Corollary 5

Let
f
be a nonconstant analytic function on a domain
W
with
f(z)\neq 0
on
W
. Then
f
does not attain its minimum value on
W
; that is, there is no point
z
such that
|f(z)|=\inf_{w\in W}|f(w)|
for all
w\in W
.

Proof:
Apply Theorem
16
to the analytic function
1/f
.

We should mention that the Maximum Principle applies to real-valued harmonic functions as well. The proof given here carries over directly, with the only difference being that dealing with the modulus is no longer necessary, and this removes the requirement that the function never be zero to deduce the Minimum Principle.

Theorem 17

Let
h
be a nonconstant harmonic function on a domain
W
. Then
f
does not attain its maximum (resp. minimum) value on
W
; that is, there is no point
z
such that
h(z)=\sup_{w\in W}h(w)
(resp.
h(z)=\inf_{w\in W}h(w)
) for all
w\in W
.

3.4
The exponential and trigonometric functions

Let us examine the exponential function
e^{z}=e^{x+yi}=e^{x}(\cos y+i\sin y)
more closely. This is an analytic function mapping the plane to itself, with
|e^{z}|=e^{x}
and
\mbox{Arg }(e^{z})=y
. We see that this function and the logarithm defined earlier in the chapter are inverses wherever
Log
can be applied, in the sense that
e^{\mbox{Log }z}=e^{\ln|z|+i\mbox{Arg }z}=|z|e^{i\mbox{Arg }z}=z
, and
\mbox{Log }e^{z}=\ln e^{x}+i\mbox{Arg }(\cos y+i\sin y)=x+\tilde{y}i
, where
\tilde{y}
is the value in
(-\pi,\pi]
such that
y=\tilde{y}(\mbox{mod }2\pi)
. Since the argument of
e^{z}
can be any value, and the modulus of
e^{z}
can be any positive value, the image of the plane under this map is
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
. The vertical line
\{y=y_{0}\}
in the plane gets mapped to a curve of constant argument; that is, to the infinite ray connecting 0 to
\infty
, which can be parameterized as
\{te^{iy_{0}}:0<t<\infty\}
. The horizontal line
\{x=x_{0}\}
in the plane gets mapped to a curve of constant modulus; that is, to the circle centered at 0 of radius
e^{x_{0}}
. This geometric understanding of the function gives rise to several useful consequences for Brownian motion, as we now describe.

To begin with, it is a standard fact that one-dimensional Brownian motion is recurrent; in fact we showed in Section
3.1
that planar Brownian motion is recurrent, and it is immediate from this that the one-dimensional variety is as well by considering the real or imaginary part of the planar case. However, there are proofs of recurrence in one dimension which do not use work done in the plane. We may therefore deduce planar recurrence by noting that recurrence on the line shows that the real part of a Brownian motion
B_{t}
starting at any point
x_{0}+iy_{0}
will eventually hit any other point
x_{1}
, which means
B_{t}
hits
\{x=x_{1}\}
with probability 1. Taking the image under the exponential map,
B_{t}
is mapped to a time changed Brownian motion
\tilde{B}_{t}
, and
\{x=x_{1}\}
is mapped to
\{|z|=e^{x_{1}}\}
. We conclude that

(3.13)

P_{e^{x_{0}+y_{0}i}}(\tilde{B}_{t}\in\{|z|=e^{x_{1}}\}\mbox{ for some }t>0)=1,

which is merely a restatement of recurrence. However, with a bit more work we can obtain an extension of this result which will be important to us. Let
r<R
be two positive values, and define sequences of stopping times
\tau^{(r)}_{n}
and
\tau^{(R)}_{n}
as follows. Let
\tau^{(r)}_{1}=\inf\{t>0:B_{t}\in\{|z|=r\}\}
, then
\tau^{(R)}_{1}=\inf\{t>\tau^{(r)}_{1}:B_{t}\in\{|z|=R\}\}
. For larger
n
define these similarly as
\tau^{(r)}_{n}=\inf\{t>\tau^{(R)}_{n-1}:B_{t}\in\{|z|=r\}\}
and
\tau^{(R)}_{n}=\inf\{t>\tau^{(r)}_{n}:B_{t}\in\{|z|=R\}\}
. In words,
\tau^{(r)}_{1}
is the first time
B_{t}
hits
\{|z|=r\}
, then
\tau^{(R)}_{1}
is the first time after
\tau^{(r)}_{1}
that
B_{t}
hits
\{|z|=R\}
, then
\tau^{(r)}_{1}
is the first time after
\tau^{(R)}_{1}
that
B_{t}
hits
\{|z|=r\}
, etc. Recall Theorem
1
, the Strong Markov Property, from Chapter
1
, which states that a Brownian motion behaves identically following a stopping time to a Brownian motion starting at time 0; together with the recurrence already established this guarantees that the random variables
\tau^{(r)}_{1},\tau^{(R)}_{n}-\tau^{(r)}_{n},
and
\tau^{(r)}_{n}-\tau^{(R)}_{n-1}
are all finite almost surely, regardless of the starting point of
B_{t}
. This in turn guarantees the almost sure finiteness of
\tau^{(r)}_{n}
and
\tau^{(R)}_{n}
for all n; that is,
B_{t}
will make infinitely many trips back and forth between
\{|z|=r\}
and
\{|z|=R\}
. Let a small
\delta>0
be given, and apply our knowledge with
r=\delta/2,R=\delta
. Note that for the duration of a trip between
\{|z|=\delta/2\}
and
\{|z|=\delta\}
the Brownian motion
B_{t}
must reside entirely within the disk
D(0,\delta)
. Thus, we have

(3.14)

\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{D(0,\delta)}(B_{s})ds\geq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\tau^{(\delta)}_{n}-\tau^{(\delta/2)}_{n}).

Set
Y_{n}=(\tau^{(\delta)}_{n}-\tau^{(\delta/2)}_{n})
. It is not hard to see by the Strong Markov Property and rotation invariance that the
Y_{n}
’s form a sequence of independent, identically distributed, positive random variables. We therefore have, for any
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

(3.15)

E_{a}[e^{-\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{D(0,\delta)}(B_{s})ds}]\leq E_{a}[e^{-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_{n}}]=\lim_{n\longrightarrow\infty}E_{a}[e^{-Y_{1}}]^{n}=0.

This can only occur if
\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{D(0,\delta)}(B_{s})ds=\infty
a.s. By translating if necessary any open set
A
so that it contains
0
, we have proved the following.

Proposition 12

For Brownian motion starting at any point and for any open set
A
in
{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

(3.16)

\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{A}(B_{s})ds=\infty\mbox{ a.s.}

That is, Brownian motion spends an infinite amount of time in any open set with probability 1. This will be important in the next chapter, when we study general functions analytic on all of
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Now do winding and Spitzer.

3.5
Entire and almost entire functions

An
entire
function is a function analytic on all of
{\mathbb{C}}
. We will call a function
almost entire
if it is analytic on a domain
W
such that
W^{c}
is a countable, closed set. This is a natural definition in relation to Brownian motion, since a Brownian motion started in
W
will never leave
W
, just as a Brownian motion can never leave the domain of an entire function. If
f
is entire or almost entire, then
f(B_{t})
is a time-changed Brownian motion, which means that its paths are precisely the paths of a Brownian motion, provided that we can show that nothing strange happens with the time change
\sigma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}|f^{\prime}(B_{s})|^{2}ds
; that is, we need to know that
\sigma_{t}
doesn’t blow up for some
t
, and also doesn’t approach some finite limit as
t\longrightarrow\infty
(which would correspond to the image Brownian motion
f(B_{t})
stopping). That is, we need the following result.

Theorem 18

Let
f
be almost entire and nonconstant. Then
\sigma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}|f^{\prime}(B_{s})|^{2}ds
is almost surely finite for any
t
, and
\sigma_{t}\nearrow\infty
almost surely as
t\longrightarrow\infty
.

Proof:
Since
f
is nonconstant,
f^{\prime}
is not identically 0, which means we can choose
\varepsilon>0
so that the open set
A=\{z:|f^{\prime}(z)|>\varepsilon\}
is nonempty. Then

(3.17)

\int_{0}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(B_{s})|^{2}ds\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{2}1_{A}(B_{s})ds=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{A}(B_{s})ds=\infty\mbox{ a.s.}

by Proposition
12
. This proves the second part of the statement, and the first is immediate, since
f^{\prime}
is continuous and thus bounded on the a.s. compact sets
\{B_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}
.

We can use this fact to obtain some results on entire and almost entire functions. For instance, since Brownian motion is recurrent and the paths of
f(B_{t})
are the paths of a Brownian motion,
f(B_{t})
must be recurrent as well. This gives

Theorem 19

Let
f
be nonconstant and entire or almost entire. Then the image of
f
is dense in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

This gives several immediate corollaries which are quite important.

Corollary 6
(Liouville’s Theorem)

A bounded entire or almost entire function is a constant.

Corollary 7
(Fundamental Theorem of Algebra)

Suppose
p(z)=\beta_{k}z^{k}+\ldots+\beta_{0}
is a degree
k
polynomial, with
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\beta_{k}\neq 0
. Then
p
has a root; that is, there is an
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
f(a)=0
.

Proof:
Since
p({\mathbb{C}})
is dense in
{\mathbb{C}}
, we can find a sequence of points
\{z_{n}\}
such that
p(z_{n})\longrightarrow 0
. The compactness of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
allows us to extract a convergent subsequence, and passing to this subsequence if necessary allows us to assume that
z_{n}\longrightarrow a\in\hat{C}
. We must show that
a\neq\infty
. Note that we can write

(3.18)

|p(z)|=|z|^{k}\Big|\beta_{k}+\frac{\beta_{k-1}}{z}+\ldots+\frac{\beta_{0}}{z^{k}}\Big|.

As
z\longrightarrow\infty
, we have
|\beta_{k}+\frac{\beta_{k-1}}{z}+\ldots+\frac{\beta_{0}}{z^{k}}|\longrightarrow|\beta_{k}|\neq 0
, so that
|p(z)|\longrightarrow\infty
; thus,
a\neq\infty
. We then have
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and by continuity we obtain
f(a)=\lim_{n\longrightarrow\infty}f(z_{n})=0
.

In fact,
p(z)
will have
k
roots; see Exercise
3.6
below.

Theorem
19
is just the beginning for entire functions. We will show later using Brownian motion that, if
f
is entire, then
f({\mathbb{C}})
is all of
{\mathbb{C}}
with at most one point removed. This is known as Picard’s Little Theorem.

Simply connected domains

4.1
Contour integration and Cauchy’s Theorem

One of the most important theorems in complex analysis is Cauchy’s Theorem, which is the subject of this section. Ordinarily, Cauchy’s Theorem is formulated using contour integration, but there is an equivalent form which will be more useful for our purposes. First, we need a definition.

A domain
W
is
simply connected
if
W^{c}
is connected, where the complement is taken in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. It is important for this definition that
\infty
be considered a point in
W^{c}
, for the first domain in the picture below may appear to have a disconnected complement, but in fact it is connected in the sphere. Armed with this definition, we may state the theorem.

Theorem 20
(Cauchy’s Theorem)

Let
f
be an analytic function on a simply connected domain
W
. Then there is an analytic antiderivative of
f
on
W
; that is, there is an analytic function
F
on
W
such that
F^{\prime}(z)=f(z)
.

In order to prove this theorem, we introduce contour integration. Let
\gamma(t)
be a continuous, piecewise smooth function from an interval
[a,b]\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
into
{\mathbb{C}}
which is only non-smooth at finitely many points in
[a,b]
; we will refer to such a function and its image as a
rectifiable curve
. The image
\gamma([a,b])
will be a connected curve in
{\mathbb{C}}
, and we will abuse notation somewhat by simply referring to this curve as
\gamma
. The
contour integral along
\gamma

(also known as the
complex line integral
), denoted
\int_{\gamma}fdz
,
is defined by the formula

(4.1)

\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=\int_{a}^{b}f(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}(t)dt.

It can be shown easily that this quantity does not depend on the parameterization chosen (see Exercise
7.4
). A curve
\gamma
is
closed
if
\gamma(a)=\gamma(b)
. The following is an equivalent form of Cauchy’s Theorem.

Theorem 21
(Cauchy’s Theorem - contour integral form)

If
f(z)
is analytic on a simply connected domain
W
, and
\gamma
is a closed path in
W
, then

(4.2)

\int_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=0.

The two forms of Cauchy’s Theorem are equivalent by the following proposition.

Proposition 13

A function
f
on a domain
W
has an analytic antiderivative on
W
if, and only if,
\int_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=0
for any closed curve
\gamma\subseteq W
.

Remark:
Note that
W
is not assumed to be simply connected here.

Proof of Proposition
13
:
Suppose that
f
has an analytic antiderivative
F
on
W
. Then, for any closed curve
\gamma
, we have

(4.3)

\int_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=\int_{a}^{b}f(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}(t)dt=\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}F(\gamma(t))\Big)dt=F(\gamma(b))-F(\gamma(a))=0.

On the other hand, if (
4.2
) holds, then we may fix
z_{0}\in W
and, for any
z\in W
, define

(4.4)

F(z)=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}f(w)dw,

where the notation
\int_{z_{0}}^{z}
indicates that the integral is taken over any curve traveling from
z_{0}
to
z
. This is well defined, for if
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are two such such curves, then we can form a new curve
\gamma
which follows
\gamma_{1}
from
z_{0}
to
z
and then follows
\gamma_{2}
backwards from
z_{0}
to
z
;
\gamma
is a closed curve, which means that
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
, and the integral over
\gamma_{2}
traversed backwards is equal to the integral over
\gamma_{2}
traversed forward (see Exercise
7.4
); it follows that
\int_{\gamma_{1}}f(z)dz=\int_{\gamma_{2}}f(z)dz
. With this notation, it is clear from (
4.2
) and Exercise
7.4
that

(4.5)

\int_{z_{0}}^{z_{1}}f(w)dw+\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}f(w)dw+\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{0}}f(w)dw=0,

since a curve traveling from
z_{0}
to
z_{1}
to
z_{2}
and back to
z_{0}
is closed, and thus

(4.6)

\begin{split}\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}{h}&=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{\int_{z_{0}}^{z+h}f(w)dw-\int_{z_{0}}^{z}f(w)dw}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{z}^{z+h}f(w)dw.\end{split}

For
h
sufficiently small we can take this interval over the line segment connecting
z
and
z+h
, which we can parameterize as
\gamma(t)=\{z+th:0\leq t\leq 1\}
. We then have

(4.7)

\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}{h}=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{1}f(z+ht)hdt=\lim_{h\longrightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{1}f(z+ht)dt=f(z).

Thus,
F
is analytic in
W
, and
F^{\prime}(z)=f(z)
.

Corollary 8
(Morera’s Theorem)

If a continuous function
f
on a domain
W
satisfies
\int_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=0
for any closed curve
\gamma\subseteq W
then
f
is analytic.

Proof:
Proposition
13
shows that
f=F^{\prime}
for an analytic function
F
, and Theorem
7
then shows that
f
is itself analytic.

We will now prove Cauchy’s Theorem; in particular, we will prove the integral form, Theorem
21
.

Proof of Theorem
21
:
We will progress through a sequence of cases, ending with the general case.

Case 1:

\gamma
is contained in a disk
D(z_{0},r)
which is contained in
W
. Here we expand
f
in a power series around
z_{0}
,
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}
, and we are assured that the radius of convergence of the series is at least
r
. If we let
F(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{n+1}(z-z_{0})^{n+1}
, then the radius of convergence of the series for
F
is the same as the series for
f
(see Exercise
7.4
), and we can differentiate termwise to get
F^{\prime}(z)=f(z)
. By Proposition
13
,
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
.

Case 2:

\gamma
is a triangle. Exercise
7.4
shows that we can assume that
\gamma
is traversed counterclockwise. Here Case 1 does not necessarily directly apply, but the idea is to express
\gamma
as the sum of a finite number of smaller triangles, each of which is contained in a disk contained in
W
; note that it will be here that we make use of simple connectivity, since it guarantees us that the interior of
\gamma
is contained in
W
. Let
\gamma_{1}^{1}=\gamma
, then let
\gamma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\gamma_{4}^{2}
be the four triangles obtained by connecting the midpoints of the sides of
\gamma_{1}^{1}
, and then
\gamma_{1}^{3},\ldots,\gamma_{16}^{3}
be the sixteen triangles obtained by connecting the midpoints of the sides of each
\gamma_{j}^{2}
, and so forth.

If we sum the integrals of
f
over each of the triangles at a given level traversed counterclockwise, we see that all but the outside line segments are integrated over in both directions. Exercise
7.4
(ii) then shows that the contribution of each of these interior segments vanishes, and we obtain

(4.8)

\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=\sum_{j=1}^{4^{n-1}}\int_{\gamma_{j}^{n}}f(z)dz.

Let
d(z,\delta W)=\inf\{|z-w|:w\in\delta W\}
. By compactness (see Exercise
7.4
), there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
D(z,\varepsilon)\subseteq W
for all
z
on
\gamma
and in the interior of
\gamma
. We now need only choose
n
large enough so that the diameter of
\gamma_{j}^{n}
is less than
\varepsilon
, and we see that each of the triangles
\gamma_{j}^{n}
is contained in a disk contained in
W
. It follows from Case 1 that the integral over each
\gamma_{j}^{n}
is
0
, and thus
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
.

Case 3:

\gamma
is a simple polygon; that is,
\gamma
is a piecewise linear curve with no self-intersections. Again we can assume that
\gamma
is traversed counterclockwise. It is not hard to show that any such polygon admits a triangulation
{\cal T}
(see Appendix Jordan Curve), and the integral over each triangle is 0 by Case 2. If we sum the integrals over the triangles traversed counterclockwise, then as in Case 2 each interior line segment will be traversed once in each direction, and by canceling these quantities we see that
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz
is equal to the sum of the integrals over the triangles in
{\cal T}
. We conclude that
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
.

Case 4:

\gamma
is a polygonal path. Here
\gamma
has a finite set of intersections, and we can break
\gamma
into the union of a finite number of simple polygons. The integral over each of these components of
\gamma
is 0, and thus
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
.

Case 5:

\gamma
is arbitrary. Again by compactness there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
D(z,\varepsilon)\subseteq W
for all
z
on
\gamma
, and we can choose
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=b
such that
\gamma\subseteq\cup_{k=1}^{N}D(\gamma(t_{k}),\varepsilon)
. We can then choose
z_{k}\in D(\gamma(t_{k}),\varepsilon)\cap D(\gamma(t_{k+1}),\varepsilon)
for
k=1,\ldots,N
. We now let
\tilde{\gamma}
be the polygonal path formed by taking the union of the line segments from
z_{k}
to
z_{k+1}
.

Since the line segment connecting
z_{k}
and
z_{k+1}
lies in the disk
D(\gamma(t_{k+1}),\varepsilon)
, the integral over this segment is the same as the integral over the portion of
\gamma
connecting
z_{k}
to
z_{k+1}
by Case 1. It follows that
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}}f(z)dz
, and since
\tilde{\gamma}
is a polygonal path we have
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
by Case 4. Thus,
\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0
. This completes the proof of Cauchy’s Theorem.

We now deduce some important consequences.

Corollary 9

Let
f
be an analytic function on a simply connected domain
W
, with
f(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in W
. Then there is an analytic logarithm of
f
on
W
; that is, there is an analytic function
g
on
W
such that
e^{g}=f
.

Proof:
If
f\neq 0
on
W
, Cauchy’s theorem tells us that we may find an antiderivative
\tilde{g}
of the analytic function
\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}
. We then have

(4.9)

\frac{d}{dz}fe^{-\tilde{g}}=f^{\prime}e^{-\tilde{g}}+fe^{-\tilde{g}}\tilde{g}^{\prime}=f^{\prime}e^{-\tilde{g}}+fe^{-\tilde{g}}\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}=0.

It follows that
f=Ce^{\tilde{g}}
for some constant
C\neq 0
. Our desired logarithm is then
g=\tilde{g}+\tilde{C}
, where
\tilde{C}
is chosen so that
e^{\tilde{C}}=C
.

Corollary 10

Let
f
be an analytic function on a simply connected domain
W
, with
f(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in W
. Then there is an analytic
n
-th root of
f
on
W
; that is, there is an analytic function
g
on
W
such that
g^{n}=f
.

Proof:
Choose
\tilde{g}
so that
e^{\tilde{g}}=f
. The desired
n
-th root is then
g=e^{\tilde{g}/n}
.

Corollary 11

Let
h
be a (real valued) harmonic function on a simply connected domain
W
. Then there is a harmonic conjugate of
h
on
W
; that is, there is a harmonic function
\tilde{h}
on
W
such that
h+i\tilde{h}
is analytic.

Proof:
Let us first note that if
h
were the real part of an analytic function
f
, then by the Cauchy-Riemann equations we could write
f^{\prime}=h_{x}-ih_{y}
. We therefore begin by setting
g=h_{x}-ih_{y}
and letting
f
be an antiderivative of
g
. If
f=u+iv
, then the Cauchy-Riemann equations again show that
f^{\prime}=u_{x}-iu_{y}
, and it follows that
h_{x}=u_{x},h_{y}=u_{y}
. From this we conclude that there is a constant
c
such that
h+c=u
, and we see that we can take
\tilde{h}=v-ic
to obtain the conjugate to
h
.

4.2
Green’s function

In the field of analysis, Green’s function
G(x,y)
on regions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is formally defined to be the solution of
LG(x,y)=\delta(y-x)
, where
L
is a linear differential operator. In complex analysis, where the Laplacian is the differential operator of most importance, for a given domain
W\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
and
z\in W
the Green’s function of the Laplacian is generally defined by the following.

Definition 1

The Green’s function
G_{W}(z,w)
on a domain
W
is a function in
w
on
W\backslash\{z\}
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

G_{W}(z,w)
is harmonic and positive on
W\backslash\{z\}
.

(ii)

G_{W}(z,w)\longrightarrow 0
as
w\longrightarrow bd(W)
, where
bd(W)
is the set-theoretic boundary of
W
(Note that the boundary is to be taken in the Riemann sphere
\hat{\mathbb{C}}:={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
, so that if
W
is unbounded then
\infty\in W
).

(iii)

G_{W}(z,w)+\frac{1}{\pi}\ln|w-z|
extends to be continuous (and therefore harmonic) at
w=z
.

Note that the factor of
\frac{1}{\pi}
in
(iii)
is not standard but has been chosen to align with the probabilistic considerations to follow. Not every domain has a Green’s function as defined above, as for instance it can be shown that no such function can exist on the punctured disk
{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}=\{0<|z|<1\}
, or more generally on a domain with isolated singularities. The Green’s function is of tremendous importance in analysis on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, including complex analysis, and the question of what domains possess a Green’s function has been keenly studied by analysts over the years. As will be seen below, the existence of the Green’s function on simply connected domains leads to a proof of the celebrated Riemann mapping theorem.

We will now bring Brownian motion into the picture, which gives us an advantage in that we may attach Green’s function to a stopping time rather than to a domain, leading to a useful generalization. Let us begin by letting
\rho_{t}^{T(W)}(z,w)
be the probability density function at point
w
and time
t
of a Brownian motion started at
z
and stopped at the exit time
T(W)
of
W
. We then can calculate formally, for any suitable function
f
,

(4.10)

\begin{split}E_{z}\int_{0}^{T(W)}f(B_{s})ds&=\int_{0}^{T(W)}E_{z}[f(B_{s})]ds\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{W}f(w)\rho_{s}^{W}(z,w)dA(w)ds\\
&=\int_{W}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho_{s}^{W}(z,w)ds\Big)f(w)dA(w).\end{split}

We see that the function

(4.11)

G_{T(W)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho_{s}^{W}(z,w)ds,

is the density of the
occupation measure

\mu(A)=\int_{0}^{T(W)}1_{A}(B_{s})ds
. We will refer to (
4.11
) as the
Green’s function of
T(W)
,
and show that it satisfies properties
(i)
through
(iii)
above, but first let us note that the definition (
4.11
) makes sense if
T(W)
is replaced by an arbitrary stopping time
\tau
. Naturally, we will denote the resulting function as
G_{\tau}(z,w)
. Our first order of business is showing that
G_{\tau}(z,w)
is conformally invariant, and subsequently providing an extension of this statement to analytic functions which are not necessarily injective.

Proposition 14

Green’s function is conformally invariant. That is, if
f
is a conformal map on
W
, then
G_{T(W)}(z,w)=G_{T(f(W))}(f(z),f(w))
.

Proof:
As was described above,
G_{T(W)}(z,w)
is the density of the occupation measure
\mu_{z}(A)=\int_{0}^{T(W)}1_{A}(B_{s})ds
, where the subscript of
z
signifies that
B_{0}=z
a.s. If
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure in the plane, then we have

(4.12)

G_{T(W)}(z,w)=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{\mu_{z}(D(w,\delta))}{\lambda(D(w,\delta))};\qquad G_{f(\Omega)}(f(z),f(w))=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{\mu_{f(z)}(f(D(w,\delta)))}{\lambda(f(D(w,\delta)))}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Conformality implies that
f^{\prime}(w)\neq 0
, and that for sufficiently small
\delta
we have
D(f(w),|f^{\prime}(w)|\delta(1-\varepsilon))\subseteq f(D(w,\delta)))\subseteq D(f(w),|f^{\prime}(w)|\delta(1+\varepsilon))
, so that
\frac{\lambda(f(D(w,\delta)))}{\lambda(D(w,\delta))}\in(|f^{\prime}(w)|^{2}(1-\varepsilon)^{2},|f^{\prime}(w)|^{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{2})
. On the other hand, in Lévy’s Theorem the scaling factor for time is
|f^{\prime}|^{2}
as well; that is, we can choose
\delta
sufficiently small so that
\frac{|f^{\prime}(w^{\prime})|}{|f^{\prime}(w)|}\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)
for all
w^{\prime}\in D(w,\delta)
, and then we will have
\frac{\mu_{\Omega}(f(D(w,\delta)))}{\mu_{\Omega}(D(w,\delta))}\in(|f^{\prime}(w)|^{2}(1-\varepsilon),|f^{\prime}(w)|^{2}(1+\varepsilon))
. Combining these two estimates and letting
\varepsilon\longrightarrow 0
shows that
\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{\mu_{\Omega}(D(w,\delta))}{\lambda(D(w,\delta))}=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{\mu_{f(\Omega)}(f(D(w,\delta)))}{\lambda(f(D(w,\delta)))}
, and the result follows.

It is important to note, however, that Lévy’s theorem in fact does not require maps to be injective, permitting general nonconstant analytic functions as well. This allows us to extend the previous proposition (with essentially the same proof) as follows.

Theorem 22

Let
W
be a domain, and suppose
f
is a function analytic on
W
. Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion starting at
a
, and
\tau
a stopping time such that the set of Brownian paths
\{B_{t}:0\leq t\leq\tau\}
lie within
W
a.s. Let
\hat{\tau}=\sigma(\tau)
, where
\sigma
is defined by L evy’s Theorem. Then

(4.13)

G_{\hat{\tau}}(f(z),w)=\sum_{w^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(\{w\})}n(f,w^{\prime})G_{\tau}(z,w^{\prime}),

where
n(f,w^{\prime})
is the order of the zero of
f-w
at
w^{\prime}
.

The proof of the Proposition
14
applies to this more general statement at all points which are not images under
f
of a point
w^{\prime}
at which
f^{\prime}(w^{\prime})=0
(the
critical values
of
f
), with the only difference being that a point
w
with multiple preimages will accumulate mass in the projected Green’s function corresponding to mass accumulated at each of the preimages by the initial Brownian motion. The
n(f,w^{\prime})
term is not necessary in order to calculate a density of the occupation measure, as in fact
n(f,w^{\prime})=1
except on the zero set of

4.4
Schwarz-Christoffel transformations

Another important class of conformal maps are the
Schwarz-Christoffel transformations
. In the simplest form, this maps the upper half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}=\{Im(z)>0\}
conformally onto a polygon. It is easiest to understand this type of map by looking first at the map
f(z)=z^{m}
, for
0<m\leq 2
, which takes
{\mathbb{H}}
conformally to the domain
\{0<Arg(z)<m\pi\}
. We see that this function turns an angle of
\pi
(a straight angle) into an angle of
m\pi
. Note also that the derivative, up to a constant, is
z^{m-1}
.

Suppose now that we want to find a conformal map
f
from
{\mathbb{H}}
onto a polygon with vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
with corresponding angles (to the left) of
\alpha_{1}\pi,\ldots,\alpha_{n}\pi
. Such a conformal map will extend to a bijection between the boundaries of the domains, and there will therefore be points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
which are mapped to
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1}
, and we will arrange it so that
\infty
is mapped to
w_{n}
. Between any two of these points,
z_{j}
and
z_{j+1}
say, the argument of
f^{\prime}(z)
must remain constant, but then it must have a jump of
\alpha_{j}\pi
at
z_{j}
. This suggests that

f^{\prime}(z)=C(z-z_{1})^{\alpha_{1}-1}(z-z_{2})^{\alpha_{2}-1}\ldots(z-z_{n-1})^{\alpha_{2}-1}.

This turns out to be correct, and we have the following theorem.

Theorem 23

The conformal map
f
from
{\mathbb{H}}
onto a polygon with vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
with corresponding angles (to the left) of
\alpha_{1}\pi,\ldots,\alpha_{n}\pi
is given by

f(z)=A+C\int^{z}(\zeta-z_{1})^{\alpha_{1}-1}(\zeta-z_{2})^{\alpha_{2}-1}\ldots(\zeta-z_{n-1})^{\alpha_{2}-1}d\zeta,

for some complex constants
A,C
, where
w_{j}=f(z_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
.

Most explicit conformal maps that are known can be expressed as compositions of Möbius transformations and Schwarz-Christoffel transformations. On the other hand, the class of conformal maps is in fact very large (as is shown by the Riemann Mapping Theorem below), we just don’t have formulas for most of them.

The reflection principle and consequences

In this chapter we study a number of important consequences of the reflection principle for Brownian motion. Let

5.1

The reflection principle for Brownian motion leads immediately to the reflection principles for harmonic functions.

5.2
Picard’s Theorem

The reflection principle allows us to construct a remarkable analytic function from
{\mathbb{H}}
onto
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
known as the
modular function
. Let
T=\{|z|>1,y>0,-1<x<-1\}
.
T
is a curvilinear triangle on the sphere; that is, a region with three sides which are each segments of circles on the sphere. Let
f
be the conformal map from
T
onto
{\mathbb{H}}
which extends continuously to a map from
\bar{T}
to
\bar{\mathbb{H}}
fixing
-1,1,
and
\infty
; the existence of such a map
g
without the final condition is guaranteed by the Riemann Mapping Theorem and Carathéodory’s Theorem, and we may then let
f=\phi\circ g
, where
\phi
is the Möbius transformation taking
g(-1),g(1),
and
g(\infty)
to
-1,1,
and
\infty
, respectively. The boundary components
\{x=-1,y>0\},\{|z|=1,y>0\},
and
\{x=1,y>0\}
are then mapped under
f
to
\{x<-1,y=0\},\{-1<x<1,y=0\},
and
\{x>1,y=0\}
, respectively. These sets are all segments of circles on the sphere, so the reflection principle allows us to reflect
f
over each boundary component of
T
, extending
f
to a function defined on the sets
T_{1},T_{2},
and
T_{3}
as illustrated in the picture below. The image of each of these sets under
f
will be the reflection of
{\mathbb{H}}
over
{\mathbb{R}}
, which is the lower half-plane
\{y<0\}
. We then reflect again over the boundary arcs of
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
, extending
f
into more triangular domains whose image is now the upper half-plane again. Continuing in this manner, we are abe to tessellate
{\mathbb{H}}
with triangles, thereby extending
f
to a map from
{\mathbb{H}}
onto
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
, as desired. In the following picture, the three boundary components are colored red, blue, and green, with the colors corresponding to the boundary components in the image
{\mathbb{H}}
. The dotted colored lines correspond to the reflections in the domain of
f
, which are still mapped to the corresponding colors in the image.

Every point
w
in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
will have many preimages under
f
in
{\mathbb{H}}
, and if
\gamma
is a curve in
{\mathbb{H}}
connecting any two such preimages
z_{1},z_{2}
, then
f(\gamma)
will be a closed curve beginning and ending at
w
. However, if we take
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
then the curve
f(\gamma)
will in fact enclose one or both of
\{-1,1\}
. For instance, in the picture below,
f(\gamma)
first crosses
\{x<-1,y=0\}
from below and then
\{x>1,y=0\}
from above before returning to
w
and encircling both of
\{-1,1\}
.

The reader is invited to sketch more examples to get the idea. Essentially, if
f(\gamma)
is a closed curve starting and finishing at
w
, then
\gamma
connects two preimages of
w
, and the particular preimages which
\gamma
connects determines the exact manner in which
f(\gamma)
winds its way through around
-1
and
1
. We need a way to make this more precise.

Suppose
W
is a domain and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\longrightarrow W
are two curves such that
\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{2}(0),\gamma_{1}(1)=\gamma_{2}(1)
. We will say
\gamma_{1}

is homotopic to
\gamma_{2}
in
W
if there is a a continuous map
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\longrightarrow W
with the following properties:

(i)

\tilde{\gamma}(0,t)=\gamma_{1}(t)
for
t\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

\tilde{\gamma}(1,t)=\gamma_{1}(t)
for
t\in(0,1)
.

(iii)

\tilde{\gamma}(s,0)=\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{2}(0)
and
\tilde{\gamma}(s,1)=\gamma_{1}(1)=\gamma_{2}(1)
for
s\in(0,1)
.

The map
\tilde{\gamma}
is called a
homotopy
, and as
s
goes from
0
to
1
the curve
\tilde{\gamma}(s,t)
represents a continuous deformation of the curve
\gamma_{1}(t)
into
\gamma_{2}(t)
. In case of the existence of such a
\tilde{\gamma}
, we will write
\gamma_{1}\sim_{{}_{W}}\gamma_{2}
, or simply
\gamma_{1}\sim\gamma_{2}
when the domain
W
is understood. Note that in deforming
\gamma_{1}
into
\gamma_{2}
,
\tilde{\gamma}
is not allowed to leave
W
. This is the part of the definition which will allow us to make rigorous a statement like ”
f(\gamma)
encircles
\{-1,1\}
”. A constant function
\gamma_{2}(t)=w\in W
will be referred to as a
point
, and therefore the phrase
\gamma_{1}
is homotopic to a point
means simply that
\gamma_{1}
can be continuously contracted to a trivial curve.

Let us return to the modular function. If
\gamma
is a curve connecting two preimages
z_{1},z_{2}
of
w
under
f
, then as discussed before
f(\gamma)
is a closed curve beginning and ending at
w
, and it can be shown that
f(\gamma)
is homotopic to a point if, and only if,
z_{1}=z_{2}
. With
z_{1}
fixed, the many possible choices of
z_{2}
therefore correspond to different homotopy classes of closed curves beginning and ending at
w
. Let us see how this relates to Brownian motion. If we start a Brownian motion
B_{t}
at
a\in{\mathbb{H}}
and stop it upon hitting
{\mathbb{R}}
, then
f(B_{t})
is a time-changed Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
stopped upon exiting
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
. However, we know that with probability 1 a Brownian motion will never hit
\{-1,1\}
, so the process
f(B_{t})
in fact traces out a full Brownian path in
{\mathbb{C}}
as
t\nearrow T_{\mathbb{H}}
. Let
\delta>0
be chosen so that
D=D(f(a),\delta)\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
. We know from the recurrence of Brownian motion that
f(B_{t})
lies with
D
for values of
t
arbitrarily close to
T_{\mathbb{H}}
, which implies that
B_{t}
intersects components of the set
f^{-1}(D)
for values of
t
arbitrarily close to
T_{\mathbb{H}}
. However, as
t\nearrow T_{\mathbb{H}}
, Brownian motion leaves the component
O_{a}
of
f^{-1}(D)
containing
a
. Let us therefore suppose we have
t
such that
B_{t}\in f^{-1}(D)\backslash O_{a}
. We choose
\tilde{a}
in the component of
f^{-1}(D)
containing
B_{t}
, and let
\tilde{\gamma}
be a curve lying in
f^{-1}(D)
connecting
B_{t}
and
\tilde{a}
and
\gamma=\{B_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}+\tilde{\gamma}
.
\gamma
is a curve connecting two distinct preimages of
f(a)
in
{\mathbb{H}}
, so
f(\gamma)
is a closed curve beginning and ending at
f(a)
which is not homotopic to a point in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
. We have proved an important fact about planar Brownian motion.

Theorem 27

Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion starting at
a\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
, and let
D
be a small disk such that
a\in D\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{-1,1\}
. Then there is a stopping time
\tau<\infty
a.s. such that if
t>\tau
and
B_{t}\in D
, and
\gamma=\{B_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}+\tilde{\gamma}
where
\tilde{\gamma}\subseteq D
connects
B_{t}
to
a
then
\gamma
is not homotopic to a point.

That is,
B_{t}
eventually gets tangled about
\{-1,1\}
and never becomes untangled. Let us contrast this with the case in which only one point is removed from the plane.

Theorem 28

Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion starting at
a\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
, and let
D
be a small disk such that
a\in D\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
. Then a.s. there are arbitrarily large values of
t
such that
B_{t}\in D
and
\gamma=\{B_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}+\breve{\gamma}
,where
\breve{\gamma}\subseteq D
connects
B_{t}
to
a
, is homotopic to a point.

Proof:

B_{t}
can be realized as a time change of
e^{\hat{B}_{t}}
, where
\hat{B}_{t}
is a Brownian motion starting at some primage
\hat{a}
of
a
under the exponential map.
\hat{B}_{t}
returns to the component of
f^{-1}(D)
containing
\hat{a}
for arbitrarily large values of
t
, and for such values we can connect
\hat{B}_{t}
to
\hat{a}
by a curve
\hat{\gamma}
so that
\{\hat{B}_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}+\tilde{\gamma}
is homotopic to a point. This homotopy, call it
\tilde{\gamma}
, can be projected, so that in fact
e^{\tilde{\gamma}}
gives a homotopy of
\gamma
to a point in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
.

Thus, Brownian motion gets tangled around two points, but doesn’t around only one point. This property has a very interesting corollary known as
Picard’s Theorem
.

Corollary 12

Suppose
f(z)
is an entire function or an analytic function defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{b\}
, and suppose there are two points
w_{1}.w_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that the image of
f
is contained in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
. Then
f
is a constant.

Proof:
Suppose
f
is a counterexample to this statement. If
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion starting at a point in the domain of
f
, then we know from the previous results that we can find a
t
such that
\gamma\{\hat{B}_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}
is homotopic to a point but
f(\gamma)
is not. This is a contradiction, since we can project a homotopy through
f
.

5.3
Laurent series

Let
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
refer to
D(w,\delta)
with
w
removed; that is,
D^{\times}(w,\delta)=\{0<|z-w|<\delta\}
. If a function
f
is analytic on a domain
W
which does not contain
w
but which does contain
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
for some
\delta>0
, then we will say that
f
has a
singularity at
w
. We will examine the behavior of
f(z)
near
w
closely. Let us distinguish three cases.

Case 1

\lim_{z\longrightarrow w}f(z)
exists and equals some
v\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and if we define
f(w)=v
then
f
so extended is analytic on
W\cup\{w\}
. This is known as a
removable singularity
.

Case 2

\lim_{z\longrightarrow w}|f(z)|=\infty
. This is called a
pole
.

Case 3

Neither
(i)
nor
(ii)
occurs. This is called an
essential singularity
.

It is a tautological and trivial statement that one of these three must occur, but we will see in fact that functions behave in a very specific way in the neighborhood of an essential singularity. Before discussing this in more detail, let us discuss the terminology a bit. A removable singularity is so named because it is a singularity which is not really a singularity, since it can be removed. A pole is so named because if one were to graph the function
|f(z)|
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the graph would have a vertical asymptote at
z=w
, resembling a pole of infinite height. It will be seen, furthermore, that if
f
has a pole at
w
then
w
is a removable singularity for
\frac{1}{f}
; in that sense, a pole is not really a true singularity, since
f
can be thought of as analytic at
w
if we define
f(w)=\infty
. The true singularities, then, are the essential ones, which justifies their name.

The following important theorem goes a long way towards clarifying the behavior of analytic functions in a neighborhood of the various types of singularities. It is known as the Casorati-Weierstrass Theorem.

Theorem 29

Let
f(z)
be analytic on
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
for some
\delta>0
. Then the following hold.

(i)

If
f
is bounded on
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
then
f
has a removable singularity at
w
.

(ii)

If
f(D^{\times}(w,\delta))
is not dense in
{\mathbb{C}}
, then
f
has either a pole or removable singularity at
w
.

Proof:
Suppose first that
f
is bounded on
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
. Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion, let
a\in D^{\times}(w,\delta)
, and let
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
. Since
f
is analytic on the bounded set
D^{\times}(w,\delta^{\prime})
and continuous on
{\cal C}(D^{\times}(w,\delta^{\prime}))
, we may apply Itō’s Theorem, which tells us that
f(a)=E_{a}[T_{D^{\times}(w,\delta^{\prime})}]
. However,
B_{t}
does not see points, so that
P_{a}[B_{T_{D^{\times}(w,\delta^{\prime})}}=w]\leq P_{a}[B_{t}=w\mbox{ for some }t\geq 0]=0
, and we conclude that
f(a)=E_{a}[T_{D(w,\delta^{\prime})}]
. We may now follow the proof of Theorem
6
to conclude that
f
may be expanded into a power series

(5.10)

f(a)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(a-w)}}^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(a-w)^{n}.

However,
f
is analytic on
D^{\times}(w,\delta^{\prime})
, so
\frac{df}{d\bar{z}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\beta_{-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{30.0}}}{{(a-w)}}^{n-1}
vanishes on this domain, which implies that
\beta_{-n}=0
for
n\geq 1
, and therefore
f(a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(a-w)^{n}
. It is now clear that if we define
f(w)=\beta_{0}
, then
f
so defined is analytic on
D(w,\delta^{\prime})
. This proves
(i)
.

Now suppose
f(D^{\times}(w,\delta))
is not dense in
{\mathbb{C}}
. We can then find
v\in f(D^{\times}(w,\delta))^{c}
such that
D(v,\varepsilon)\subseteq f(D^{\times}(w,\delta))^{c}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then
g(z)=\frac{1}{f(z)-v}
is analytic on
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
and bounded in magnitude by
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, so by
(i)

g
extends to be analytic on
D(w,\delta)
. If
g(w)\neq 0
, then since
f(z)=\frac{1}{g(z)}+v
it is clear that
f
extends to be analytic on
D(w,\delta)
, and
f(w)=\frac{1}{g(w)}+v
. If, however,
g(z)=0
, then if we let
m
be the order of the zero of
g
at
w
we can write
g(z)=(z-w)^{m}h(z)
, where
h
is analytic and nonzero in a neighborhood of
w
. But then
|f(z)|=\Big|\frac{1}{g(z)}+v\Big|=\Big|\frac{1}{(z-w)^{m}h(z)}+v\Big|\longrightarrow\infty
as
z\longrightarrow w
. This proves (ii).

Corollary 13

Suppose
f
is analytic on
D^{\times}(w,\delta)
and has a pole at
w
. Then there is a unique integer
m>0
such that
g(z)=(z-w)^{m}f(z)
can be extended to be analytic on
D(w,\delta)
with
g(w)\neq 0
.
f(z)=\frac{g(z)}{(z-w)^{m}}
can therefore be expanded in a power series

(5.11)

f(z)=\sum_{n=-m}^{+\infty}\beta_{n}(z-w)^{n},

which converges on
D(w,\delta)
. Conversely, if
f
is given by (
5.11
) with
\beta_{-m}\neq 0
, and
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\beta_{n}(z-w)^{n}
converges in some neighborhood of
w
, then
|f(z)|\longrightarrow\infty
as
z\longrightarrow w
, and
f
therefore has a pole at
w
.

Proof:
The first statement is clear from the proof of Theorem
29
, while the second holds by expanding
g
into a power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\beta}_{n}(z-w)^{n}
on
D(w,\delta)
, then setting
\beta_{n}=\tilde{\beta}_{n+m}
and relabeling the indices of the series. If we suppose now that
f
is given by (
5.11
), then the analytic part
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(z-w)^{n}
of
f
approaches
\beta_{0}
as
z\longrightarrow w
, and we have

(5.12)

\begin{split}\Big|\sum_{n=-m}^{-1}\beta_{n}(z-w)^{n}\Big|&\geq\Big|\frac{\beta_{-m}}{(z-w)^{m}}\Big|-\Big|\frac{\beta_{-m+1}}{(z-w)^{m-1}}\Big|-\ldots-\Big|\frac{\beta_{-1}}{(z-w)}\Big|\\
&=\Big|\frac{1}{(z-w)^{m}}\Big||\beta_{-m}-\beta_{-m+1}(z-w)-\ldots-\beta_{-1}(z-w)^{m-1}|\longrightarrow\infty\end{split}

as
z\longrightarrow w
. Thus,
w
is a pole of
f
.

Remark:
The integer
m
is referred to as the
order
of the pole at
w
, and is the same as the order of the zero of
\frac{1}{f}
at
w
. The series (
5.11
) is what is referred to as a
Laurent series
: a power series with negative powered terms.

We have now obtained a clear characterization of the behavior of an analytic function in the neighborhood of a pole. The singularities for which we do not have a good understanding is then the essential variety. It turns out, though we will not prove it, that a function with an essential singularity can be expanded into a series such as (
5.11
) in a neighborhood of singularity, but with infinitely many nonzero negative powered terms. It also turns out that the Casorati-Weierstrauss Theorem can be strengthened to the following result, known as the
Great
or
Big Picard’s Theorem
(what we have referred to earlier as Picard’s Theorem is then demoted to the
Little Picard’s Theorem
).

Theorem 30

Suppose
f
is analytic and has an essential singularity at
w
. Then there is a point
b\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{b\}\subseteq f(U)
for every neighborhood
U
of
w
.

That is,
f
attains every value in
{\mathbb{C}}
with at most one exception on every neighborhood of
w
. A proof exists of this fact which uses Brownian motion, but we will not present it here.

Proposition 15

6.2
Carathéodory’s Theorem for Jordan domains

A
Jordan curve
is the homeomorphic image of a circle in
{\mathbb{C}}
. That is, if
\gamma_{t}
is a continuous and injective map from
[0,1]
to
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)
, then its image, which we will refer to as
\gamma
, is a Jordan curve. A domain whose boundary is a Jordan curve is called a
Jordan domain
. The
Jordan Curve Theorem
(see Appendix) tells us that any Jordan curve
\gamma
defines a unique, bounded, simply connected domain
W
which has
\gamma
as its boundary. The Riemann Mapping Theorem tells us that
W
is conformally equivalent to the unit disk. If
f
is the conformal map from
{\mathbb{D}}
to
W
, then
f
is, among other things, a homeomorphism between the two domains, and since we know that
\gamma
is the homeomorphic image of a circle a very natural question is whether
f
can be extended to a homeomorphism from
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{{\mathbb{D}}}}
to
\bar{W}
. The affirmative answer to this question is known as Carathéodory’s Theorem.

Theorem 33

Let
f
be a conformal map from
{\mathbb{D}}
onto a Jordan domain
W
. Then
f
extends continuously to a map sending
\delta{\mathbb{D}}
bijectively to
\delta W
, and
f
so extended is therefore a homeomorphism from
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{{\mathbb{D}}}}
to
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{W}}
.

Proof:
Fix
z_{0}\in\delta{\mathbb{D}}
. For
0<\delta<1/4
let
C_{\delta}
be the curve
{\mathbb{D}}\cap\{|z_{0}-z|=\delta\}
. The heart of the proof lies in showing that the length of
f(C_{\delta})
goes to 0 as
\delta\longrightarrow 0
. Let
L(\delta)
denote the length of
C_{\delta}
. The magnification factor of a curve at each point
z
is
|f^{\prime}(z)|
, so we have

(6.10)

L(\delta)=\int_{C_{\delta}}|f^{\prime}(z)||dz|.

It is also clear that the length of
C_{\delta}
is bounded by
\pi\delta
. Thus, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(6.11)

L(\delta)^{2}\leq\Big(\int_{C_{\delta}}1^{2}|dz|\Big)\Big(\int_{C_{\delta}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}|dz|\Big)\leq\pi\delta\int_{C_{\delta}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}|dz|.

We may therefore bound as follows.

(6.12)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1/4}\frac{L(\delta)^{2}}{\delta}d\delta&\leq\pi\int_{0}^{1/4}\int_{C_{\delta}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}|dz|d\delta\\
&=\pi\int\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{D}}\cap D(z_{0},1/4)}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}dA(z)\\
&=\pi Area(f({\mathbb{D}}\cap D(z_{0},1/4)))<\infty.\end{split}

If there was an
\varepsilon>0
such that
L(\delta)\geq\varepsilon
for all
\delta
, we would have
\int_{0}^{1/4}\frac{L(\delta)^{2}}{\delta}d\delta\geq\int_{0}^{1/4}\frac{d\delta}{\delta}=\infty
, so we conclude that there is no such
\varepsilon
. This does not tell us that
L(\delta)\longrightarrow 0
, but it does tell us that we can find a sequence
\delta_{n}\longrightarrow 0
such that
L(\delta_{n})\longrightarrow 0
as well. Since the length of
f(C_{\delta_{n}})
is finite, the curve reaches an endpoint in each direction. Let these endpoints be labeled
a_{n},b_{n}
, and let the corresponding endpoints of
C_{\delta}
be
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
.
a_{n}
cannot lie within
W
, since if it did there would be a

such that
f(z)=a_{n}
, which by the open mapping theorem would imply that
f(D(z,\varepsilon)\cap{\mathbb{D}})\cap f(D(\alpha_{n},\varepsilon)\cap{\mathbb{D}})\neq\emptyset
for any
\varepsilon>0
, and this contradicts injectivity since
z\neq\alpha_{n}
. Thus,
a_{n}\in\delta W
, and likewise for
b_{n}
.

Note that
|a_{n}-b_{n}|<diam(f(C_{n}))\longrightarrow 0
. We can conclude from this that, if we let
D_{n}=D(z_{0},\delta_{n})\cap{\mathbb{D}}
, we have
diam(f(D_{n}))\longrightarrow 0
. The set
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{25.0}}}{{f(D_{n})}}
is compact and nonempty, and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{35.0}}}{{f(D_{n+1})}}\subseteq\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{25.0}}}{{f(D_{n})}}
for all

; Exercise
6.3
below then implies that
\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{25.0}}}{{f(D_{n})}}\neq\emptyset
. However, the diameter of this set must be less than the diameter of any
f(D_{n})
, which implies that the diameter is 0. We conclude that the intersection consists of a single point,
w_{0}
. We define
f(z_{0})
to be

. It can now be checked that
f
, so extended to all of
\delta{\mathbb{D}}
, defines a continuous bijection from
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{{\mathbb{D}}}}
to
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{10.0}}}{{W}}
.

7.1
The expected exit time

Suppose that
W
is a bounded domain, and as usual let
T_{W}=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{t}\in W^{c}\}
be the exit time of Brownian motion from

E_{a}[T_{W}]
as a function of

as well as we can, and evaluate it if possible. Let us begin by noting that if we can find a real-valued function

which is continuous on
{\cal C}(W)
,

on
W

\Delta h=-2
, and 0 on
\delta W
, then we may apply Itō’s Theorem to obtain

(7.1)

-h(a)=E_{a}[h(B_{T_{W}})]-h(a)=\frac{1}{2}E_{a}\int_{0}^{T_{W}}\Delta h(B_{s})ds=-E_{a}\int_{0}^{T_{W}}ds=-E_{a}[T_{W}].

Thus,
h(a)=E_{a}[T_{W}]
. However, the function
E_{a}[T_{W}]
does not always satisfy these conditions; for example, if
W=D^{\times}(0,1)=\{0<|z|<1\}
, then since Brownian motion doesn’t hit points we have
E_{a}[T_{W}]=E_{a}[T_{\mathbb{D}}]
, and therefore
\lim_{a\longrightarrow 0}E_{a}[T_{W}]=E_{0}[T_{\mathbb{D}}]=\frac{1}{2}
even though
0\in W^{c}
. On the other hand, the following holds.

Proposition 16

If
W
is a bounded simply connected domain, then
h(a)=E_{a}[T_{W}]
is the unique continuous function on
{\cal C}(W)
which is 0 on
\delta W
and satisfies
\Delta h=-2
on
W
.

This follows from the properties of Green’s function discussed earlier. There is another way to evaluate
E_{a}[T_{W}]
which is of interest, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 17

Suppose
W
is a bounded simply connected domain, and
f:{\mathbb{D}}\longrightarrow W
is conformal with
f(0)=a
. Suppose further that
f
admits the power series expansion
f(z)=a+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{2}
. Then

(7.2)

E_{a}[T_{W}]=\frac{-|a|^{2}}{2}+\lim_{r\nearrow 1}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}.

Proof:
Apply Itō’s Theorem to the function
u(z)=|z|^{2}
, using
\Delta u=4
, to obtain

(7.3)

E_{a}[|B_{T_{W}}|^{2}]-|a|^{2}=2E_{a}\int_{0}^{T_{W}}ds=2E_{a}[T_{W}].

Now note that Lévy’s Theorem allows us to project a Brownian motion running on the disc onto
W
, and we obtain

(7.4)

E_{a}[|B_{T_{W}}|^{2}]=\lim_{r\nearrow 1}E_{0}[|f(B_{T_{D(0,r)}})|^{2}]=\lim_{r\nearrow 1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta.

Inserting this into (
7.3
) and dividing by 2 gives the first equality in (
7.2
), and the second follows from Parseval’s Identity, which is proved next.

Theorem 34

Suppose

is an analytic function on

which admits the Taylor series expansion

. Then

(7.5)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f_{a}(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}\quad\mbox{for }r<R.

Proof:
We have

(7.6)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f_{a}(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{a}(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\line{1}{0}{55.0}}}{{f_{a}(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})}}d\theta\\
&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}r^{n}e^{in\theta}\Big)\Big(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bar{a}_{m}r^{m}e^{-im\theta}\Big)d\theta\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\bar{a}_{m}r^{n+m}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(n-m)\theta}d\theta.\end{split}

(
7.5
) now follows by noting

if

and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(n-m)\theta}d\theta=0
if

.

Let us work a few examples using Proposition

Example 1:

7.2
Unbounded domains

The following lemma is basic but important for unbounded domains.

Lemma 6

(i)

Suppose
z\in W_{1}\subseteq W_{2}
. Then
E_{z}[T_{W_{1}}]\leq E_{z}[T_{W_{2}}]
.

(ii)

Suppose that
z\in W_{1}\subseteq W_{2}\subseteq\ldots
, and
W=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}W_{n}
. Then
E_{z}[T_{W_{n}}]\nearrow E_{z}[T_{W}]
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
.

Proof:

(i)
is obvious, and
(ii)
is a simple consequence of the Monotone Convergence Theorem.

The key to resolving the issues mentioned on unbounded domains is the following theorem, which is a special case of what we will refer to as
Burkholder’s Theorem
, which can also be thought of as a planar form of the Davis Inequality. To state the theorem, we define the supremum process
|B^{*}_{\tau}|=\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}|B_{t}|
for any stopping time
\tau
. We then have

Theorem 35

For any stopping time
\tau
we have

(7.14)

2E_{a}[\tau]+|a|^{2}\leq E_{a}[|B^{*}_{\tau}|^{2}]\leq 4(2E_{a}[\tau]+|a|^{2}).

Proof:
First let us note that it is enough to prove the result in the case that
|B^{*}_{\tau}|\leq M
a.s. This is because, if we do so, we can let
S_{M}=\inf\{t\geq 0:|B_{t}|=M\}
, apply the result to
\tau\wedge S_{M}
, and then let
M\nearrow\infty
, applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem to both
\tau\wedge S_{M}
and
|B^{*}_{\tau\wedge S_{M}}|^{2}
in order to obtain (
7.25
). With this condition on
\tau
we may apply Itō’s Theorem as in the proof of Proposition
17
to obtain
2E_{a}[\tau]+|a|^{2}=E_{a}[|B_{\tau}|^{2}]
. It is clear that
E_{a}[|B_{\tau}|^{2}]\leq E_{a}[|B^{*}_{\tau}|^{2}]
, and we obtain the first inequality in (
7.25
). The second follows similarly by utilizing the following important inequality.

Proposition 18

If
\tau
is a stopping time with
E[\tau]<\infty
then

(7.15)

E_{a}[|B^{*}_{\tau}|^{2}]\leq 4E_{a}[|B_{\tau}|^{2}].

To prove this, we need several lemmas.

Lemma 7

(7.16)

E_{a}[|B_{S_{M}}|1_{S_{M}\leq\tau}]\leq E_{a}[|B_{\tau}|1_{S_{M}\leq\tau}]

Proof:
Should follow from Dynkin’s formula.

One of the most exciting developments in probability theory in the early

8.1
Schwarz’s lemma and consequences

We will now work towards a discussion of Stochastic Loewner Evolution. We begin with an important property of harmonic functions can be proved using Brownian motion.

Theorem 42

[Mean Value Property]

Suppose

is harmonic on a domain

, and the set
\{|z-z_{0}|\leq r\}\subseteq W
. Then

(8.1)

u(z_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})d\theta.

That is,

is the average of the values of

near
z_{0}
. A consequence is the following.

Theorem 43

[Maximum/minimum principle]

Suppose
{\cal C}
is a simple closed curve with interior

. If

is harmonic on a domain

containing
{\cal C}
and

,
then

\min_{w\in{\cal C}}u(w)\leq u(z)\leq\max_{w\in{\cal C}}u(w)

for all
z\in U
. In fact, if

is nonconstant, then

\min_{w\in{\cal C}}u(w)<u(z)<\max_{w\in{\cal C}}u(w)

for all
z\in U
.

From this follows another important property of analytic functions.

Theorem 44

[Maximum Modulus Theorem]

Suppose
{\cal C}
is a simple closed curve with interior

. If
f(z)
is analytic in a domain
D
containing
{\cal C}
and

,
then

|f(w)|\leq\max_{z\in{\cal C}}|f(z)|

for all
w\in U
. If

is not a constant, then in fact
|f(w)|<\max_{z\in{\cal C}}|f(z)|
for all
w\in U
.

Note that there is no analog of this result in real calculus. This implies the following important result, known as
Schwarz’s Lemma
.

Theorem 45

Suppose
f(z)
is an analytic function defined on
{\mathbb{D}}
such that
f({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq{\mathbb{D}}
, i.e.
|f(z)|<1
for all

. Suppose also that

. Then
|f(z)|\leq|z|
for all

, and
|f^{\prime}(0)|\leq 1
. Furthermore, if equality holds in either case then

, where

is a complex number with

.

Proof:
Consideration of the power series expansion
f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
with
a_{n}=\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}
shows that the function
g(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z}
and

is analytic on
{\mathbb{D}}
. Note that

, since

, so the maximum principle implies that

, with equality only when

8.2
de Branges’ theorem and deterministic Loewner evolution

In the 1800’s, with the Riemann Mapping Theorem and other developments in complex analysis people began studying conformal maps deeply. Let
{\cal S}
be the set of all conformal maps
f

is called the

, and note that any conformal map

•

I(z)=z
, the identity.

•

, the Koebe function.

If

Theorem 47
(de Branges Theorem, also known as the Bieberbach Conjecture)

For any Schlicht function

, and equality holds for any

only for the Koebe function and its rotations.

Loewner’s idea was to look at only

{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\gamma

containing

, let

be

be the conformal map from

to

with

and

. Then

is increasing in

. Let

Theorem 48

f_{t}

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{t}(w)=-g_{t}(w)\frac{\lambda(t)+g_{t}(w)}{\lambda(t)-g_{t}(w)};\\

\end{gathered}

Sketch of proof:
Let

Derivation of the Loewner equation

Re(K(z))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}Re(K(e^{i\theta}))Re\Big(\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}\Big)d\theta.

Consider now the function

8.2
) follows; in fact, we have proved it only for the right hand derivative, but essentially the same argument shows gives proves it for the left hand derivative, and the second differential equation in (
8.2
) follows by inverting the first.

In fact, in the proof above we used the obvious-sounding fact that
\lambda(t)
is continuous in
t
; this is a true statement but it is not easy to prove. Full details can be found in the book
Conformal invariants: Topics in geoetric function theory
by Lars Ahlfors.

8.3
Stochastic Loewner evolution

In 2000, Oded Schramm realized that the Loewner differential equation was also useful in studying the evolution of random processes which don’t self-intersect. That is, in the Loewner equation
\lambda(t)
is defined in terms of
\gamma(t)
, but the reverse works as well, and if we put a random
\lambda(t)
into the Loewner equation we obtain a random output
\gamma(t)
which does not self-intersect. The natural choice for
\lambda(t)
is a 1-dimensional Brownian motion, and the resulting random curve
\gamma(t)
has been shown to be the continuous limit of a number of different discrete processes. In fact, what probabilists generally look at is a bit of a modification of the original Loewner evolution, with the curve growing into a specified domain. A common choice is the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}=\{|z|<1\}
, and the same general method as was used in the plane leads to the following differential equations.

(8.3)

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{t}(w)=g_{t}(w)\frac{\lambda(t)+g_{t}(w)}{\lambda(t)-g_{t}(w)};\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{t}(z)=-f_{t}^{\prime}(z)z\frac{\lambda(t)+z}{\lambda(t)-z};\\
\end{gathered}

The other common choice is the upper half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}=\{Im(z)>0\}
, and again the same method leads to the following differential equations.

(8.4)

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{t}(w)=\frac{2}{g_{t}(w)-\lambda(t)};\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{t}(z)=\frac{2f_{t}^{\prime}(z)}{\lambda(t)-z};\\
\end{gathered}

As a simple example, if
\lambda(t)=0
then

\begin{gathered}f_{t}(z)=\sqrt{z^{2}-4t}\\
g_{t}(z)=\sqrt{z^{2}+4t}\\
\gamma(t)=2i\sqrt{t}\end{gathered}

For
SLE
, we take
\lambda(t)=B_{\kappa t}(=\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t}
), and call this
SLE_{k}
. Some basic properties of the curve for
SLE_{k}
are as follows.

•

For
0\leq\kappa\leq 4
the curve
\gamma(t)
is simple a.s.

•

For
4<\kappa<8
the curve
\gamma(t)
intersects itself and every point is contained in a loop but the curve is not space-filling a.s.

•

For
\kappa\geq 8
the curve
\gamma(t)
is space-filling a.s.

SLE
has been identified as the scaling limit of a number of discrete processes:

•

\kappa=2
is the scaling limit of the loop-erased random walk.

•

\kappa=\frac{8}{3}
is the outer boundary of Brownian motion.

•

\kappa=3
is the limit of interfaces for the Ising model.

•

\kappa=4
is the scaling limit of the path of the harmonic explorer and contour lines of the Gaussian free field.

•

\kappa=6
is the scaling limit of critical percolation on the triangular lattice.

•

\kappa=8
corresponds to the path separating the uniform spanning tree from its dual tree.

A number of others are conjectured, but are still unproved.
