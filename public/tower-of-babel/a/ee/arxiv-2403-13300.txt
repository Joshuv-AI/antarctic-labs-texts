Kernel Multigrid: Accelerate Back-fitting via Sparse Gaussian Process Regression
Lu Zou lzou@connect.ust.hk, Liang Ding liang_ding@fudan.edu.cn

Abstract
Additive Gaussian Processes (GPs) are popular approaches for nonparametric feature selection. The common training method for these models is Bayesian Back-fitting. However, the convergence rate of Back-fitting in training additive GPs is still an open problem. By utilizing a technique called Kernel Packets (KP), we prove that the convergence rate of Back-fitting is no faster than ( 1 − 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 n ) ) t (1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n}))^{t} , where n n and t t denote the data size and the iteration number, respectively. Consequently, Back-fitting requires a minimum of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log n) iterations to achieve convergence. Based on KPs, we further propose an algorithm called Kernel Multigrid (KMG). This algorithm enhances Back-fitting by incorporating a sparse Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) to process the residuals after each Back-fitting iteration. It is applicable to additive GPs with both structured and scattered data. Theoretically, we prove that KMG reduces the required iterations to 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n) while preserving the time and space complexities at 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log n) and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) per iteration, respectively. Numerically, by employing a sparse GPR with merely 10 inducing points, KMG can produce accurate approximations of high-dimensional targets within 5 iterations.

Introduction

Additive Gaussian Processes (GPs) are popular approaches for nonparametric feature selection, effectively addressing high-dimensional generalized additive models
(
Hastie 2017
)
from a Bayesian perspective. This method posits that the high-dimensional observed data
\mathcal{G}
can be decomposed into an additive form of one-dimensional GPs, expressed as
\mathcal{G}=\sum_{d}\mathcal{G}_{d}
. It then leverages Bayes’ rule to infer the posterior distribution of the contributions
\mathcal{G}_{d}
from each dimension individually. Thus, additive GPs offer not only high interpretability but also uncertainty quantification of their outcomes by providing distributions for the posteriors.

Despite their considerable benefits, additive GPs come with significant drawbacks, in brief their computational complexity. Training an additive GP model necessitates performing matrix multiplications and inversions. For
n
training points in
D
-dimension, matrix operations demand
\mathcal{O}((Dn)^{3})
time and
\mathcal{O}(Dn^{2})
space. These complexities can be prohibitive. Given that additive GPs are typically employed in high-dimensional problems with a substantial number of observations, direct computations become impractical. This computational burden severely limits the practicality of additive GPs in addressing many real-world problems.

Considerable research has been dedicated to enhancing the efficiency of computing the posteriors
\mathcal{G}_{d}
of additive GPs. Among these efforts, Bayesian Back-fitting
(
Breiman and Friedman 1985
;
Hastie and Tibshirani 2000
;
Saatçi 2012
)
is one of the most commonly used algorithms for training additive GPs. Back-fitting, which allows a scalable fit over a single dimension of the input, can be used to fit an additive GP
over a
D
-dimensional space with the same overall asymptotic complexity. Specifically, Back-fitting iteratively conducts Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) for each dimension individually while holding the others fixed. Thanks to efficient algorithms for training one-dimensional GPR, such as the state-space model
(
Hartikainen and Särkkä 2010
)
or kernel packets (KP)
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Ding and Tuo 2024
)
, each Back-fitting iteration can be executed with efficient time and space complexities of
\mathcal{O}(Dn\log n)
and
\mathcal{O}(Dn)
, respectively.

However, the convergence rate of Back-fitting for additive GPs remains an open problem. Despite its remarkable performance in prediction and classification tasks, as shown in
(
Gilboa et al. 2013
;
Delbridge et al. 2020
)
, leading to speculation that Back-fitting may exhibit exponentially fast convergence for additive GPs, our analysis reveals a different picture. By employing a technique called Kernel Packet (KP), which provides an explicit formula for the inverse kernel matrix, we can prove that Back-fitting converges no faster than
(1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n}))^{t}
when focusing on convergence towards the posterior of each individual dimension. Here
n
is the data size and
t
is the iteration number. This finding implies that a minimum of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
iterations is necessary for Back-fitting to reach convergence. The intuition is that Back-fitting struggles to accurately distribute global features across the correct dimensions, although the composite structure of additive models for prediction or classification might compensate for errors resulting from such misallocations. Inspired by the Algebraic Multigrid method in numerical analysis, we introduce an algorithm named Kernel Multigrid (KMG). This approach refines Back-fitting by integrating a sparse GPR to handle residuals after each iteration. The inclusion of sparse GPR aids in efficiently reconstructing global features during each step of the iterative process, thereby effectively addressing the aforementioned limitation associated with Back-fitting. KMG is versatile, applicable to additive GPs across both structured and scattered data. If we impose a weak condition on the associated sparse GPR, we can prove that the algorithm significantly reduces the necessary iterations to
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
while maintaining the time and space complexities unchanged for each iteration. Furthermore, through numerical experiments comparing Back-fitting and KMG, we find that with merely 10 inducing points in the sparse GPR, KMG is capable of accurately approximating high-dimensional targets in 5 iterations.

In summary, we have established a lower bound for the convergence rate of Back-fitting, demonstrating that a minimum of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
iterations is required for convergence. To address this limitation, we introduce the KMG algorithm, a slight modification of Back-fitting that overcomes its shortcomings. Consequently, KMG achieves exponential convergence towards each one-dimensional target posterior
\mathcal{G}_{d}
.

1.1
Literature Review

Scalable GPs aim to improve the scalability of full GP models without compromising the quality of predictions. Two prominent approaches in this domain are sparse approximation and iterative methods. Sparse approximation
(
Seeger et al. 2003
;
Snelson and Ghahramani 2005
;
Quinonero-Candela and Rasmussen 2005
;
Titsias 2009
;
Eleftheriadis et al. 2023
)
rely on selecting a set of
m
inducing points from a large pool of
n
observation to effectively approximate the posterior at a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2})
. The main challenge lies in optimally selecting inducing points to strike a balance between computational efficiency and prediction accuracy. Recently, several work including
Wilson and Nickisch 2015
;
Hensman et al. 2018
;
Dutordoir et al. 2020
;
Burt et al. 2020
have attempted to investigate this issue. Typically, these approaches enable the utilization of a considerable number of inducing points with minimal cost; however, these methods require imposing certain restrictions to the subspaces used for approximation.

Iterative methods, particularly the Conjugate Gradients(CG) method
(
Lanczos 1950
;
Hestenes et al. 1952
;
Saad 2003
)
, provide an alternative strategy for efficient approximation in GPs. Recently, there has been a notable increase in the popularity of iterative methods
(
Cutajar et al. 2016
;
Cockayne et al. 2019
;
Bartels and Hennig 2020
)
, largely due to their precision and effective utilization of GPU parallelization
(
Gardner et al. 2018
)
. Numerous enhancements to CG method have been proposed to improve numerical stability and accelerate convergence speed. For example,
Potapczynski et al. 2021
propose random truncations method to reduce the bias of CG. Similarly,
Wenger et al. 2021
propose variance reduction techniques to reduce the bias of CG. Additionally,
Maddox et al. 2021
provided comprehensive implementation guidance for the CG method, assisting practitioners in consistently attaining robust performance. For literature that delves into theoretical analysis of CG methods in GPs, please refer to
(
Wenger et al. 2022a
;
Wenger et al. 2022b
)
. These iterative approaches have demonstrated effectiveness on large scale datasets
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
. The performance of the CG is critically dependent on the initial solution and the preconditioner. An ill-chosen preconditioner can significantly impede convergence
(
Cutajar et al. 2016
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
.

Multigrid methods
(
Saad 2003
;
Borzi and Schulz 2009
)
are classic computational techniques designed to solve partial differential equations (PDEs), serving as a complement to the CG methods in numerical computation. A key difference between Multigrid methods and the CG methods is that Multigrid methods cycle through various grid resolutions, transferring solutions and residuals between finer and coarser grids, whereas CG methods iteratively refines the solution by moving along conjugate directions. However, the application of Multigrid methods is constrained by their requirement for a grid-based domain and noiseless observations, which limits their suitability for GPR. Our method generalizes the idea of Multigrid methods for scatter datasets with noise.

Additive GP models
(
Duvenaud et al. 2011
;
Kaufman and Sain 2010
;
López-Lopera et al. 2022
;
Lu et al. 2022
)
have recently attracted significant interest due to their broad applicability in various fields. The structure of additive GPs allows for implementation of Back-fitting. A key advantage of Back-fitting is its ability to update the posterior for each dimension at one time, thereby avoiding the costly joint updates
(
Luo et al. 2022
)
. Furthermore, Back-fitting is also a popular methodology for training bayesian tree structure type model
(
Chipman et al. 2012
;
Linero 2018
;
Lu and McCulloch 2023
)
, demonstrating its flexibility and effectiveness in various scenarios. Recently, the idea of projection pursuit
Friedman and Stuetzle 1981
has been integrated into additive GPs, introducing greater flexibility and enhancing computational efficiency. For further reading on projection pursuit in additive GPs, please see
Gilboa et al. 2013
;
Delbridge et al. 2020
;
Chen and Tuo 2022
.

Back-fitting has also been extensively embraced in the context of additive models within the frequentist setting
(
Luo 1998
;
Sadhanala and Tibshirani 2019
;
El-Bachir and Davison 2019
)
. The consistency of the Back-fitting has been extensively studied in
Buja et al. 1989
;
Ansley and Kohn 1994
;
Mammen et al. 1999
. Recently,
Ghosh et al. 2022a
developed a Back-fitting algorithm for generalized least squares estimators, demonstrating its operation at a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n)
under certain asymptotic conditions. This methodology has been extended to generalized linear mixed models for logistic regression
(
Ghosh et al. 2022b
)
and to generalized linear mixed models that include random slopes
(
Ghandwani et al. 2023
)
. The existing literature on nonparametric additive models
(
Mammen and Park 2006
;
Yu et al. 2008
;
Fan and Jiang 2005
;
Opsomer 2000
)
primarily focuses on the asymptotic properties of Back-fitting estimators, rather than its convergence rate. To the best of our knowledge, research on the convergence rate of Back-fitting for nonparametric additive models is scarce.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we provide an overview of the background knowledge for GPs, the KP technique, and Bayesian Back-fitting. In Section
3
, we establish the lower bound of convergence for Bayesian Back-fitting. Section
4
introduces a novel algorithm named KMG, along with a discussion of its convergence properties. In Section
5
, the performance of KMG is evaluated on both synthetic and real-world datasets. Finally, we conclude our findings and propose directions for future research in Section
6
.

Preliminaries

GPs have emerged as a popular Bayesian method for nonparametric regression, offering the flexibility to establish prior distributions over continuous functions. In this section, we will introduce some foundational concepts in GPs relevant to our study.

2.1
General Gaussian Processes

A GP is a distribution on function
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
over an input space
\mathcal{X}
such
that the joint distribution of
\mathcal{G}
on any size-
n
set of input points
\bm{X}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
is described by a multivariate Gaussian density over the associated targets, i.e.,

\pr\left(\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{1}),\cdots,\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{n})\right)=\mathcal{N}(m(\bm{X}),k(\bm{X},\bm{X}))

where
m(\bm{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
n
-vector whose
i
-entry equals the value of a mean function
m
on point
\bm{x}_{i}
and
k(\bm{X},\bm{X})=[k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
an
n
-by-
n
kernel covariance matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry equals the value of a positive definite kernel
k
on
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{j}

(
Wendland 2004
)
. Accordingly, a GP can be characterized by the mean function
m:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and the kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Generally , the mean function is set as
0
when we have no prior knowledge of the true function. Therefore, we can use only the kernel
k
to determine a GP.

In this study, we will assume that the underlying GP is
stationary
, i.e., its covariance only depends on the difference:
k(x^{\prime},x)=k(x^{\prime}-x)
. GPs with stationary kernels have been widely used in time series (see for example
Brockwell and Davis 1991
) and spatial statistics (see for example
Cressie 2015
). For a one-dimensional stationary
k
, we can define its Fourier transform as follows:

\mathcal{F}[k](\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(x)e^{-i\omega x}dx.

Now we can impose the following specific assumption on the kernel function. This assumption is weak and includes a large number of kernel classes, such as the Matérn kernels and the compactly supported Wendland kernels
(
Wendland 2004
, Chapter 9)
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The kernel
k
is stationary and its Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}[k(\cdot)](\omega)
satisfies:

C_{1}(1+\omega^{2})^{-s}\leq\mathcal{F}[k(\cdot)](\omega)\leq C_{2}(1+\omega^{2})^{-s},\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R}

for some
s>1/2
where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are positive constants independent of s,
\omega
and kernel function k.

We consider the case that observation is noisy
\bm{y}_{i}=\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2})
is an i.i.d. Gaussian distributed error. Then, we can use standard identities of the multivariate Gaussian
distribution to show that, conditioned on data
(\bm{X},\bm{Y})=\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the posterior distribution at any point
\bm{x}^{*}
also follows a Gaussian distribution:
\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}^{*})|\{\bm{X},\bm{Y}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n}(\bm{x}^{*}),{s}_{n}(\bm{x}^{*}))
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\bm{x}^{*})=k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{X})\left[k(\bm{X},\bm{X})+\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}\right]^{-1}\bm{Y}

\displaystyle s_{n}(\bm{x}^{*})=k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x}^{*})-k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{X})\left[k(\bm{X},\bm{X})+\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}\right]^{-1}k(\bm{X},\bm{x}^{*})

(1)

where
k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{X})=(k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x}_{1}),\cdots,k(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x}_{n})^{T}
, and
k(\bm{X},\bm{X})=[k(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{x}_{l})]^{n}_{k,l=1}
.

The computation of (
1
) all require
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time complexcity and
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
space complexity due to the need for inverting dense matrices
k(\bm{X},\bm{X})+\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}
. When data size
n
is large, GPs are computational inefficient and unstable because the kernel covariance matrix is dense and nearly singular.

2.2
Additive GPs

A
D
-dimensional additive GP can be view as summation of
D
one-dimensional GPs. Specifically,

\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{G}_{d}(x_{i,d}),

where
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is a one-dimensional zero-mean GP characterized by kernel
k_{d}
and
x_{i,d}
is the
d
-th entry of the
D
-dimensional training point
\bm{x}_{i}
. So an additive GP has kernel function
k=\sum_{d=1}^{D}k_{d}
. We use the following model to describe the generation of observations
\bm{Y}
:

\bm{y}_{i}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{G}_{d}(x_{i,d})+\varepsilon_{i}.

(2)

An additive prior occupies a middle ground between a linear model, which is additive, and a nonlinear regression prior, allowing for the modeling of arbitrary interactions between inputs. While it does not model interactions between input dimensions, an additive model still provides interpretable results. For instance, one can easily plot the posterior mean of the individual
\mathcal{G}_{d}
to see how each predictor is related to the target.

The objective of additive GP is to estimate the posterior distribution given the observation data
(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
. Let
\bm{X}_{d}=\{x_{i,d}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the
d
-th dimension of all data points
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. By applying Bayes’ rule, we can first derive the distribution of
\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}
, which is also Gaussian:

\displaystyle\pr\left(\{\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})\}_{d=1}^{D}|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right)\propto

\displaystyle\pr\left(\bm{Y}|\bm{X},\{\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})\}_{d=1}^{D}\right)\pr\left(\{\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})\}_{d=1}^{D}|\bm{X}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d}),\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}\right)\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\bm{K}\right),

(3)

where
\bm{K}=\text{diag}[\bm{K}_{1},\bm{K}_{2},\cdots,\bm{K}_{D}]\in\mathbb{R}^{Dn\times Dn}
is a block diagonal matrix with one-dimensional kernel covariance matrices
\bm{K}_{d}=k_{d}(\bm{X}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})
on its diagonal. A direct result of (
3
) is that the posterior
\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}
is Gaussian with mean and variance as follows:

\displaystyle\E\left[[\mathcal{G}_{1}(\bm{X}_{1});\cdots;\mathcal{G}_{D}(\bm{X}_{D})]|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]=\left[\bm{K}^{-1}+\sigma_{y}^{-2}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]^{-1}\sigma_{y}^{-2}\bm{S}\bm{Y},

(4)

\displaystyle\Var\left[[\mathcal{G}_{1}(\bm{X}_{1});\cdots;\mathcal{G}_{D}(\bm{X}_{D})]|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]=\left[\bm{K}^{-1}+\sigma_{y}^{-2}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]^{-1},

(5)

where
\bm{S}=[\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n};\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n};\cdots;\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{Dn\times n}
.

Based on (
4
) and (
5
), we then can compute the posterior of each
\mathcal{G}_{d}
at a one-dimensional input point
x^{*}_{d}
, we can use the following the marginal distribution:

\displaystyle\pr\left(\mathcal{G}_{d}(x^{*}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right)=\int\pr\left(\mathcal{G}_{d}(x^{*}_{d})|\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})\right)\pr\left(\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right)d\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d}).

(6)

From direct calculations,
\mathcal{G}_{d}(x^{*}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}
is also Gaussian with mean and variance as follows:

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{G}_{d}(x^{*}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]=

\displaystyle k_{d}(x^{*}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}\E\left[\mathcal{G}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{d}(x^{*}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}\left[\bm{e}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\sigma_{y}^{2}\bm{K}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]^{-1}\bm{S}\bm{Y}\right]

(7)

\displaystyle\Var\left[\mathcal{G}_{d}(x^{*}_{d})|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=k_{d}(x_{d}^{*},x_{d}^{*})-k_{d}(x_{d}^{*},\bm{X}_{d})\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}k_{d}(\bm{X}_{d},x^{*}_{d})

\displaystyle\quad+k_{d}(x^{*}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}\left[\bm{e}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\bm{K}^{-1}+\sigma_{y}^{-2}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]^{-1}\bm{e}_{d}\right]\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}k_{d}(\bm{X}_{d},x^{*}_{d})

(8)

where

\bm{e}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}=[\underbrace{\bm{0},\bm{0},\cdots,\bm{0},\bm{0}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(d-1)\ \bm{0}^{\prime}{\rm s}\\
\text{where $\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$}\end{subarray}},\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n},\bm{0},\cdots,\bm{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times Dn}

is a block matrix for querying the
d
-th block of a
Dn\times n
block matrix. For notation simplicity, we write
\lambda:=\sigma_{y}^{-2}
in subsequent discussions.

To calculate (
7
) and (
8
), we will demonstrate in next subsection how the KP technique facilitates matrix operations of the form
k_{d}(x^{*}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})\bm{K}^{-1}_{d}\bm{v}
to be executed in
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
time and
\mathcal{O}(n)
space for any vector
\bm{v}
. However, it’s important to note that we must also perform matrix operations of the form
[\bm{K}^{-1}+\sigma_{y}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{-1}\bm{v}
for certain vectors
\bm{v}
, which demand significantly higher time and space complexities of
\mathcal{O}((Dn)^{3})
and
\mathcal{O}(Dn^{2})
, respectively. This presents considerable challenges in managing large datasets and addressing high-dimensional problems with additive GPs.

2.3
Kernel Packet

KP yields a sparse formulation for one-dimensional kernel function. Here, we will use the Matérn kernel with half-integer smoothness parameter as an example and kernels in the Matérn class are also our tool to prove the lower bound. Notably, the half-integer Matérn kernel can be expressed in a closed form as the product of an exponential function and a polynomial of order
s=\nu+1/2
:

k_{\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\exp({-w{|x-x^{\prime}|}})\frac{s!}{2s!}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{s}\frac{(s+l)!}{l!(s-l)!}({2w}{|x-x^{\prime}|})^{s-l}\right),

(9)

where
w>0
is called the scaled parameter. Any Matérn-
\nu
kernel satisfies Assumption
2.1
with
s=\nu+1/2
for
\nu=1/2,3/2,\cdots
, thereby indicating smoothness of
s
-th order. For general kernel,
Ding and Tuo 2024
has shown that KP factorization exists as long as the kernel satisfied some specific differential equation, which is a weak condition.

3
Back-fitting Convergence Lower Bound

In this section, we outline our strategy for establishing the lower bound of convergence for Bayesian Back-fitting, introduce the essential technique - a global feature that Bayesian Back-fitting fails to reconstruct efficiently, and conclude with a two-dimensional example to demonstrate the concept.

3.1
Proof Overview

As described by
Hastie and Tibshirani 2000
;
Saatçi 2012
, the Bayesian Back-fitting technique is analogous to the Gauss-Seidel method, an iterative algorithm used for addressing finite difference problems. In the domain of numerical analysis, it is well known that solving a partial differential equation discretized on a grid of size
\mathcal{O}(n)
via the Gauss-Seidel method requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
iterations (see
Strang 2007
or
Saad 2003
). This is due to Gauss-Seidel’s nature as a filter that rapidly reduce high-frequency noise while exhibiting a marked insensitivity to the solution’s low-frequency Fourier components
(
Strang 2007
;
Saad 2003
)
.

Unlike the Gauss-Seidel method, where observations are collected from a regular grid, the data points in Bayesian Back-fitting are scattered. Additionally, Gauss-Seidel aims to invert a finite difference matrix induced by noiseless observations on a regular grid while Bayesian Back-fitting aims to invert a kernel matrix induced by scattered, noisy data. As a result, the rationale behind the low convergence rate observed in the Gauss-Seidel approach does not directly applicable to Bayesian Back-fitting. To establish the lower bound for the convergence rate of Bayesian Back-fitting, we employ the KPs discussed in Section
2.3
. Through these, we construct global features to which Bayesian Back-fitting is insensitively adjusted.

In our counterexample, the covariates
\bm{X}
are sampled from a
Latin Hypercube
design (LHD)
(
McKay et al. 2000
)
, an experimental design frequently used for generalized linear models with regularization. More specifically, for each dimension of
\bm{X}
, the
n
elements are chosen such that one element comes from each number
\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots,1
, and these elements are then randomly permuted. Let
\bm{X}^{*}=[1/n,2/n,\cdots,1]
be a set consisting of sorted points. Then, in a LHD, the following identity holds for any
d

\bm{X}_{d}=\bm{P}_{d}\bm{X}^{*}

where
\bm{P}_{d}
is a permutation matrix uniform distributed on the permutation group. In each outer iteration
t
of Bayesian Back-fitting, the total
D
inner iterations can be summarized as

\bm{u}^{(t)}=[\bm{K}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}+\lambda\bm{L}]^{-1}\lambda\left(\bm{S}\bm{Y}-\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u}^{(t-1)}\right)

(14)

where
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dn\times Dn}
is a lower triangular matrix with zero diagonal as follows:

\bm{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\cdots&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}&\bm{0}&\cdots&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}&\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}&\cdots&\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}

Let
\bm{u}^{\infty}
denote the solution. It must be a stationary point of the iteration (
14

t

\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=\bm{u}^{(t)}-\bm{u}^{(\infty)}

14

\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=\left(-[\bm{K}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}+\lambda\bm{L}]^{-1}\lambda\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{t}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{t}\bm{S}\bm{Y}.

(15)

for
t=1,\cdots,T
. The analysis of the convergence for Bayesian Back-fitting then involves quantifying the distance between
\|\mathcal{S}_{n}^{T}\bm{S}\bm{Y}\|_{2}
and 0, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
represents the vector

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{1}=\bm{V}_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{X}^{*})=\bm{A}k_{d}(\bm{X}^{*},\bm{X}^{*})[1,1,\cdots,1]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(16)

where
\bm{1}=[1,\cdots,1]

\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}[k_{d}(\bm{X}^{*},\bm{X}^{*})]^{-1}\bm{V}_{*}\leq\mathcal{O}(1/n)

\lambda\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\left[k_{d}(\bm{X}^{*},\bm{X}^{*})\right]^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}\right]^{-1}\bm{V}_{*}\geq\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}-\lambda^{-1}\left[k_{d}(\bm{X}^{*},\bm{X}^{*})\right]^{-1}\right]\bm{V}_{*}\geq 1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda n}\right),

Theorem 1

Let
\bm{X}

20

\bm{U}
is then defined as

\hat{\bm{c}}=\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}[\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}]^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{v}

(21)

where

\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}=\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}.

In (
21
), the operation
\bm{v}_{m}=\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{v}
represents the projection of
\bm{v}
onto the
mD
-dimensional Hilbert space spanned by kernel functions
\mathcal{H}_{m}=\{k_{d}(\cdot,u_{i,d})\mid d=1,\ldots,D,i=1,\ldots,n\}
. This operation is analogous to the coarsening step in Algebraic Multigrid. By mapping
\bm{v}
to
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, a similar problem to the original one can be formulated in the coarser space.

The projection
\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}
is analogous to the Galerkin projection
(
Saad 2003
)
. We treat
\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
and its inverse as linear operators. By applying the concept of Galerkin projection, an equivalent operator can be defined in the coarser space
\mathcal{H}_{m}
as:

\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}[\bm{K}^{-1}_{n,n}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}=\bm{K}^{-1}_{m,m}+\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1},

which leads to a different penalty term
\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}
. Once the coarser problem is solved, the solution can be mapped back to the original function space by interpolation operator
\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}
to have an approximated solution of (
20
).

Obviously, the accuracy of the approximation (
21
) relative to the original problem (
20
) improves as the number of inducing points increases. As
\bm{U}=\bm{X}
, meaning all data points are selected as inducing points, (
21
) is reduced to the original problem (
20
). Identifying the ideal sparse GPR for our KMG algorithm involves pinpointing the optimal quantity and arrangement of inducing points. The following theorem offers guidance on selecting the necessary inducing points.

Theorem 7

(Approximation Property)
.

Suppose
\bm{c}=[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{-1}\bm{v}
for some vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dn}
and
k_{d}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
for all
d=1,\cdots,D
. Suppose inducing points
\bm{U}
satisfy

\max_{d=1,\cdots,D}\max_{x\in[0,1]}\min_{u\in\bm{U}_{d}}|x-u|\leq\mathcal{O}(h_{m})

(22)

for some
h_{m}=o(1)
.
Then the sparse GPR approximation
\hat{\bm{c}}
has the following error rate:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{c}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|_{2}\leq C^{*}[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\sqrt{\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}_{n,n}\bm{c}}

where
\kappa^{*}_{m}
and
\kappa^{*}_{n}
are called restricted isometry constants and only depend on the distributions of points
\bm{U}
and
\bm{X}
, respectively, and
C^{*}
is come universal constant independent of
n
,
m
,
D
, and
\lambda
.

Remark 8
.

The restricted isometry constant was first proposed in
Candes and Tao 2007
. It is an important geometry characteristics for the function spaces
\mathcal{H}_{m}
under the empirical distributions of data. This constant reflects the ”orthogonality” between the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHSs) induced by those one-dimensional kernel functions, such as
k_{d}
and
k_{d^{\prime}}
, for instance. For more details on RKHS, please refer to
Wendland 2004
;
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
;
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
.

Remark 9
.

Condition (
22
) can be readily met in a variety of scenarios due to its focus on the fill distance in a single dimension, in contrast to the definition of the original fill distance. For instance, if the input data points
\bm{X}
are uniformly distributed within the hypercube
[0,1]^{D}
, this condition can be naturally satisfied by selecting points uniformly.

We leave the proof of Theorem
7
in the appendix. Leveraging sparse GPR, we are now prepared to outline our algorithm and discuss its convergence properties.

4.2
Kernel Multigird

The basic idea of KMG relies on two facts. Firstly, Back-fitting can be interpreted as a filtering process that reduces high-frequency errors. Secondly, sparse GPRs are particularly effective in capturing low-frequency components by explicitly discarding high-frequency components, albeit at the cost of reduced accuracy compared to full GPRs. Consequently, by combining Back-fitting with sparse GPR, the KMG method effectively mitigates the weaknesses of each approach, resulting in enhanced overall performance. The details of the KMG algorithm are as shown in Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Kernel Multigrid

Input
: data points
\bm{X}
, vectors
\{\bm{v}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}_{d=1}^{D}
, number of inducing points
m

Output
: estimation of
[{\lambda^{-1}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{-1}[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}

Initialize
\bm{u}_{d}^{(0)}
,
d=1,\cdots,D

Uniformly select
m
inducing points
\bm{U}_{d}
from
\bm{X}_{d}
,
d=1,\cdots,D

for

t=1
to
T

do

for

d=1
to
D

do

\bm{u}_{d}^{(t)}=[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}+\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}\left[\bm{v}_{d}-\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\bm{u}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}-\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{u}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\right]

(23)

end for

\bm{r}_{n}=[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}-[{\lambda^{-1}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}][\bm{u}_{1}^{(t)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(t)},\cdots,\bm{u}_{D}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}
\triangleright
residual

\bm{r}_{m}=\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{r}_{n}

\triangleright
projection

\bm{\delta}_{m}=[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}]^{-1}\bm{r}_{m}
\triangleright
coarser problem

[\bm{u}^{(t)}_{1},\cdots,\bm{u}^{(t)}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}\leftarrow[\bm{u}^{(t)}_{1},\cdots,\bm{u}^{(t)}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}^{-1}_{m,m}\bm{\delta}_{m}
\triangleright

end for

return
\bm{u}=[\bm{u}^{(T)}_{1},\cdots,\bm{u}_{D}^{(T)}]^{\mathsf{T}}

Efficient computations of the KMG algorithm can be divided into two main parts. The first part encompasses the execution of the Back-fitting step, as described by equation (
23
). This phase requires solving
D
linear systems in the form
[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}+\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}\bm{u}
. Utilizing the KP technique
(
Ding and Tuo 2024
)
or state-space model
(
Hartikainen and Särkkä 2010
)
, as outlined in Section
2.3
, enables the resolution of all
D
linear systems within a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
and a space complexity
\mathcal{O}(n)
. We focus on the second part, which entails applying a sparse GPR to the residual. The second part can be further decomposed into four steps: computing the residual, performing projection, solving the coarser problem, and performing interpolation.

For computing the residual
\bm{r}_{n}
, we use KP factorization (
12
):

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{n}

\displaystyle=[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}-[{\lambda^{-1}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}][\bm{u}_{1}^{(t)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(t)},\cdots,\bm{u}_{D}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{D}]^{\mathsf{T}}-[\lambda^{-1}\bm{P}\bm{\Phi}^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{P}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}][\bm{u}_{1}^{(t)},\bm{u}_{2}^{(t)},\cdots,\bm{u}_{D}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}

(24)

where
\bm{A}=\text{diag}[\bm{A}_{1},\cdots,\bm{A}_{D}]
,
\bm{\Phi}=\text{diag}[\bm{\Phi}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\Phi}_{D}]
,
\bm{P}=\text{diag}[\bm{P}_{1},\cdots,\bm{P}_{D}]
and
\bm{A}_{d}
and
\bm{\Phi}=[\phi_{i}(\bm{x}_{j})]_{i,j}
are the KP matrices and
\bm{P}_{d}
is the permutation matrix, all induced by points
\bm{X}_{d}
. Here, matrix multiplications involving the sparse matrices
\bm{P}
,
\bm{A}
, and
\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
can all be computed in
\mathcal{O}(Dn)
time and space. Similarly, the matrix multiplication involving the inverse banded matrix
\bm{\Phi}^{-1}
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(Dn)
time and space complexities using a banded matrix solver.

For computing the projection and interpolation, recall that inducing point
\bm{U}_{d}
are selected from data points
\bm{X}_{d}
and
\bm{K}_{m,m}=\text{diag}[k_{1}(\bm{U}_{1},\bm{U}_{1}),k_{2}(\bm{U}_{2},\bm{U}_{2}),\cdots,k_{D}(\bm{U}_{D},\bm{U}_{D})]
so, using KP factorization,
\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}
can also be factorized as

\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}=\bm{P}_{m,m}\bm{\Phi}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{m}(\bm{X})

(25)

where
\bm{P}_{m,m}
,
\bm{\Phi}_{m,m}
, and
\bm{\phi}_{m}(\bm{X})
correspond to the matrices
\bm{P}
,
\bm{\Phi}
, and
\bm{\phi}
introduced in the previous step but induced by the inducing points
\{\bm{U}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
. Notably,
\bm{P}_{m,m}
is a permutation matrix,
\bm{\Phi}_{m,m}
is a banded matrix, and
\bm{\phi}_{m}(\bm{X})
is a sparse matrix with
\mathcal{O}(Dn)
non-zero entries due to the compact support property of KPs. As a result, any matrix multiplication of the form
\bm{v}_{m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}
or
\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{v}_{n}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(Dn)
time and space for any vector
\bm{v}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dm}
and
\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{Dn}
. This efficiency arises from the sparsity of
\bm{P}_{m,m}
and
\bm{\phi}_{m}(\bm{X})
, as well as the banded structure of
\bm{\Phi}_{m,m}
.

Finally, solving the coarser problem is notably more efficient due to its significantly smaller scale compared to the original problem. This step requires only
\mathcal{O}((Dm)^{3})
time and
\mathcal{O}((Dm)^{2})
space to complete, as it involves matrices of size
Dm
-by-
Dm
only.

In summary, each iteration of the KMG algorithm can be executed efficiently, with the overall time and space complexities for each iteration being
\mathcal{O}(Dn\log n+(Dm)^{3})
and
\mathcal{O}(Dn+(Dm)^{2})
, respectively.

4.3
Convergence Analysis

The KMG algorithm can be summarized as the following steps in each of the
t
-th iteration

1.

Smoothing:
\bm{u}_{n}^{(t+1/2)}=\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{u}_{n}^{(t)}

2.

Get residual:
\bm{r}_{n}=\bm{v}-[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]\bm{u}_{n}^{(t+1/2)}

3.

Coarsen:
\bm{r}_{m}=\bm{K}^{-1}_{m,m}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{r}_{n}

4.

Solve in the coarsen scale:
\bm{\delta}_{m}=[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}_{m,m}+\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}]^{-1}\bm{r}_{m}

5.

Correct:
\bm{u}_{n}^{(t+1)}=\bm{u}_{n}^{(t+1/2)}+\bm{K}_{n,n}\bm{K}^{-1}_{m,m}\bm{\delta}_{m}
.

These steps closely mirror those of the Algebraic Multigrid method, with the key difference being that our objective is GPR rather than solving a differential equation. It is clear that the result of one iteration of the above algorithm corresponds to
a iteration process of the form

\bm{u}^{(t+1)}_{n}=\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{u}_{n}^{(t)}

(26)

where

\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}=\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}-\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}]^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]

executes sparse GPR on the residual and subsequently incorporates the correction into the solution and
\mathcal{S}_{n}=[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}+\bm{L}]^{-1}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
is the back-fit operator. Both
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}
are linear operators. Thus, the convergence analysis of (
26
) simplifies to estimating the distance between
\|(\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}\mathcal{S}_{n})^{t}\bm{S}\bm{Y}\|_{2}
and
0
as before.

From the representation theorem, any function in the
Dn
-dimensional RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{n}=\text{span}\{k_{d}(\cdot,x_{i,d}):d=1,\cdots,D,i=1,\cdots,n\}
can be represented by the
Dn
-dimensional vector
\bm{v}
equipped with the RKHS norm:
\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}^{2}:=\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{v}
.

From
(
Wendland 2004
, Theorem 12.1)
, the RKHS norm for any
L^{2}
function
f
within
\mathcal{H}_{n}
can increase at a rate significantly beyond
n
. Assuming Assumption
2.1
,
there exists vector
\bm{v}
such that
\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{v}\geq\mathcal{O}\left({n^{2s-1}}\right)
. Given that our initial error is the noisy observation vector
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}=\bm{S}\bm{Y}
, it is reasonable to infer that the RKHS norm of
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
is significantly substantial. The smoothing function of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
can effectively diminish the error contributing to this substantial RKHS norm:

Lemma 10

(Smoothing Property)
.

Given error
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}
in the
t
-th iteration for any
t
, we have

\|\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}D\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}.

We refer Lemma
10
as the
smoothing property
of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, signifying that the Back-fit operator
\mathcal{S}_{n}
effectively smooths out any error with an RKHS norm exceeding
\sqrt{\lambda}D\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}
.

Remark 11
.

The smoothing property in our study is different from the one commonly used in solving numerical PDEs. This difference arises because numerical PDEs are typically approached as interpolation problems, whereas additive GPs are regression problems. In the scenario of interpolation, it becomes evident that we would have
\lambda=\sigma_{y}^{-2}=\infty
as per Lemma
10
, leading to the conclusion that the smoothing property is not applicable.

In addition to smoothing property of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have already proved the
approximation property
of
\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}
. As a result, by combining Theorem
7
and Lemma
10
, we can directly have the following estimate for any error
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{m}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle C^{*}\sqrt{n}[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\|\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{*}\sqrt{\lambda n}D[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}.

We then can use induction argument to have the following convergence rate of KMG:

Theorem 12
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. Let the inducing points
\bm{U}
satisfy

C^{*}\sqrt{\lambda n}D[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\leq 1-\delta

(27)

for some
\delta>0
independent of data size
n
. Then for any initial guess
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
, we have the following error bound for the error of KMG

\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{t}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\|_{2}.

Remark 13
.

According to our selection of inducting points, the error term
1-\delta
must be independent of
n
. For instance, we can choose inducing points in a manner that ensures
1-\delta\leq e^{-1}
. Under this scenario, for any
\epsilon>0
, merely
\epsilon\log n
iterations are required to attain an accuracy of the order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\epsilon})
.

Remark 14
.

Condition (
27
) is readily achievable for a wide class of data distributions. For instance, if
\bm{X}
follows a uniform distribution or represents a LHD over the space
[0,1]^{D}
, then selecting
m
points at random from
\bm{X}
will yield an inducing point set
\bm{U}
whose one-dimensional fill distance, as defined in (
22
), is approximately
h_{m}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)
(ignoring the potential log term). Under these conditions, it suffices to randomly select
m\approx n^{\frac{1}{2s}}
inducing points.

A simple example can demonstrate this phenomenon. Consider a model where

6
Conclusions and Discussion

In this study, we establish a lower bound for the convergence rate of Back-fitting, demonstrating that it struggles to effectively reconstruct global features, necessitating at least
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
iterations to converge. To address this limitation, we propose an enhancement by applying a sparse GPR to the residuals generated by Back-fitting in each iteration. This approach enables the reconstruction of global features and significantly reduces the required number of iterations for convergence to
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, offering a more efficient solution.

There are several directions that could be pursued in future research. Firstly, the KMG algorithm could be expanded into more advanced Multigrid techniques, such as F-cycles, W-cycles
(
Saad 2003
)
, or Multigrid Conjugate Gradients
(
Tatebe 1993
)
. For complex real-world challenges, which often involve millions of data points across thousands of dimensions, these potential research directions could yield practical benefits. From a theoretical perspective, the KMG presented in our study, which employs only a small number of inducing points, is sufficient to attain the theoretically fastest exponential convergence rate, while maintaining time and space complexities at minimal order.

Secondly, our focus has been on training additive GPs to reconstruct contributions from each dimension. However, a variety of kernel machines also aim at achieving similar objectives, yet they operate with distinct kernel structure, loss functions, and update mechanisms. Extending our algorithms and analysis to enhance the training efficiency of these diverse models remains an unexplored area.

Thirdly, numerous iterative methods exist for training kernel machines, yet analyses focusing on the requisite number of iterations for these training algorithms are scarce. We posit that there must exist simple modifications to many iterative kernel training methods that could significantly enhance their convergence rates. Similar to our study, it has been rigorously proved that training kernel ridge regression via gradient descent with data augmentation techniques as in
Ding et al. 2023
, also requires
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
iterations only. Thorough analysis regarding the required iteration number in many other kernel machine training algorithms are needed, presenting an opportunity to broaden our analytical framework and methodology to improve these algorithms.

Theorem
1
:

We only need to show that after

\|\mathcal{S}_{n}^{t}\bm{S}\bm{Y}\|_{2}\geq(1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\lambda n}))^{t}.

\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[\bm{0},\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{0},\cdots,\bm{0}],\quad\text{where}\ \bm{0},\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Recall that the Back-fit operator
\mathcal{S}_{n}=[\bm{K}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{L}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\lambda\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Let
\bm{u}_{d},\bm{w}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

n

Then, we can notice that the block Gauss-Seidel iteration gives

\bm{u}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}=\lambda\left(\bm{w}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}-\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{u}_{d^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)[\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}.

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda[\bm{K}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{L}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}=\left[\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{2},\bm{0},\cdots,\bm{0}\right]

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda^{2}[\bm{K}_{2}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}[\bm{K}_{1}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda[\bm{K}_{2}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}.

On the other hand
\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{V}_{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[\bm{1};\bm{0};\cdots;\bm{0}]

\displaystyle\left|\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{V}_{*}\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\bm{V}_{*}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda[\bm{K}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{L}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{V}_{*}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\bm{V}_{1}\frac{\bm{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\lambda^{2}[\bm{K}_{2}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}[\bm{K}_{1}^{-1}+\lambda\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}\bm{1}

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\lambda n})

is greater than

\max_{\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{v}\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\lambda n}).

Let

\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}
are low-rank kernel matrices:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}=\text{diag}[\tilde{k}_{1}(\bm{U}_{1},\bm{X}_{1}),\cdots,\tilde{k}_{D}(\bm{U}_{D},\bm{X}_{D})],

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}=\text{diag}[\tilde{k}_{1}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{1}),\cdots,\tilde{k}_{D}(\bm{X}_{D},\bm{X}_{D})],

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{d}(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,\bm{U}_{d})[k_{d}(\bm{U}_{d},\bm{U}_{d})]^{-1}k_{d}(\bm{U}_{d},x^{\prime}).

Let
\tilde{k}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\tilde{k}_{d}
and

\hat{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{X})[\tilde{k}(\bm{X},\bm{X})+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\tilde{k}(\bm{X},\bm{x}^{\prime}).

We can notice that the matrix
\bm{M}
in (
51
) has the following identities for all its entries

\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})=\left[\bm{S}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}-\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}_{m}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}\right]_{i,j}

and for any
\bm{u}_{i}
, we have

\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\cdot)=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\frac{1}{n}\|h(\bm{X})-\tilde{k}(\bm{X},\bm{u}_{i})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda n}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{2}

so we can bound the empirical norm of each
\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i})
as follows:

\frac{1}{n}\|\hat{k}(\bm{X},\bm{u}_{i})-\tilde{k}(\bm{X},\bm{u}_{i})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda n}\|\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i}),\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda n}\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{i}).

(52)

For any function
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}
, we define the following linear operator for notation convenient:

\mathcal{L}[f](\bm{x})=\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{X})[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}f(\bm{X})

Obviously,
f
is also in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\bm{\alpha}_{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{k}(\bm{U},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{2}=\bm{\alpha}_{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{k}(\bm{U},\bm{U})\bm{\alpha}_{f}=\bm{\alpha}_{f}^{\mathsf{T}}{k}(\bm{U},\bm{U})\bm{\alpha}_{f}=\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

Then using Lemma
19
, we have for any
\bm{x}_{j}\in\bm{X}
:

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})\right|^{2}=\left|\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})-\mathcal{L}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)](\bm{x}_{j})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L}\right)[\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)](\bm{x}_{j})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)-\mathcal{L}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{{1}/{s}}\left(\frac{1}{{n\kappa^{*}_{n}}}\|\hat{k}(\bm{X},\bm{x}_{j})-\tilde{k}(\bm{X},\bm{x}_{j})\|^{2}_{2}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left([\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L}]^{2}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)](\bm{x}_{j})\right)^{\frac{1}{2s}}\left(\frac{k(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}_{j})}{\lambda n\kappa^{*}_{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2s}}

(53)

where the last line is from (
52
) and direct calculations.

It is straightforward to check that
\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L}
is positive definite. So for any
\bm{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\leq\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[f](\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\leq\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}\langle(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[f],\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}

\displaystyle\leq\langle(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)],\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)](\bm{x}).

Also,
\|(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\leq\|\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}
because, obviously, spectrum of
\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L}
is less than 1. So

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})^{2}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)]

\displaystyle\leq\left[\max_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\leq\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[f]\right]\|(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}.

From previous calculations, we have already known
\|(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}^{2}=(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})^{2}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)](\bm{x})
. So

\|\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)-\mathcal{L}[\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\cdot)]\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{k}}}\leq(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)](\bm{x})

(54)

Substitute (
54
) into (
53
), and remind that
\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)=(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathcal{L})[\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\cdot)]
, we can have

\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{j})\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(\bm{x},\bm{x})}{\lambda n\kappa^{*}_{n}}}.

It is straightforward to check that for any
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}
,
\tilde{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-\hat{k}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
is a positive definite kernel. As a result, for any
\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}
, we can derive the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{i})-\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{i})\right)\left(\tilde{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}_{j})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\max_{\bm{x}}k(\bm{x},\bm{x})}{\lambda n\kappa^{*}_{n}}}.

(55)

Substitute(
55
) into (
51
), we can have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{m}\|\left(\bm{S}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}-\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}_{m}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}\right)\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})\right)[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}]_{j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\max_{i,j}\left|\tilde{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})-\hat{k}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})\right|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}]_{j}\right|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{h_{m}^{2s}{{\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}\bm{E}_{n}}}}{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}})

(56)

where the last line is from (
50
). Then we can apply Lemma
18
on (
56
) to have the following upper bound for (
49
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\|\tilde{D}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\|[\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}+\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}]^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa^{*}_{m}m}\|\left(\bm{S}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}-\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}_{m}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{m,n}\bm{S}[\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}+\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{Dn}]^{-1}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bm{S}\right)\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\frac{h_{m}^{2s}{{\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}\bm{E}_{n}}}}{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}).

(57)

The final part of our proof is to estimate
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{K}_{n,n}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\tilde{D}_{m}\|_{2}
in (
49
). Let

\mathcal{D}(\bm{x})=\bm{K}_{\bm{x},m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\tilde{D}_{m}=[\mathcal{D}_{1}(x_{1}),\cdots,\mathcal{D}_{D}(x_{D})]^{\mathsf{T}}

So
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{K}_{n,n}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\tilde{D}_{m}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{D}(\bm{X})\|_{2}
. From Theorem
16
, it is straightforward to derive the following sampling inequality

\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{D}(\bm{X})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{D}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\sum_{d=1}^{D}h_{n}^{2s}\|\mathcal{D}_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}\leq\frac{1}{m}\|\mathcal{D}(\bm{U})\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{d=1}^{D}2h_{m}^{2s}\|\mathcal{D}_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}

(58)

Obviously,
\frac{1}{m}\|\mathcal{D}(\bm{U})\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\|\tilde{D}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{h_{m}^{2s}{{\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}\bm{E}_{n}}}}{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}})
so we only need to estimate the RKHS norm of
\mathcal{D}
. From long but straightforward calculations, we can have

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}\|\mathcal{D}_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\bm{D}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}\bigg[\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}-\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}[\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}]^{-1}\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}

\displaystyle-\lambda^{-1}\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}[\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}]^{-2}\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\bigg]\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\bm{D}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}[{\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}-\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}[\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}]^{-1}\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}}]\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\bm{D}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}[\lambda{\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}-\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\left(-\lambda\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}\right)^{l}}]\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{D}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda\|\bm{D}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

(59)

where
\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\tilde{k}_{d}(\bm{X}_{d},\bm{X}_{d})
and the fourth line is from the Neumann series expansion for
[\bm{\mathrm{I}}+\lambda\bar{\bm{K}}_{n,n}]^{-1}
.

Substitute (
59
) and (
50
) into (
58
), we can have the following upper bound

\sum_{d=1}^{D}\|\mathcal{D}_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}\leq\lambda\|\bm{D}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\lambda h_{m}^{2s}n\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}\bm{E}_{n}\right)

(60)

and substitute (
60
) into (
58
), we can have the following estimate for (
49
):

\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\tilde{D}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{D}(\bm{X})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{h_{m}^{2s}\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}_{n,n}\bm{E}_{n}}{\lambda\kappa_{n}^{*}\kappa_{m}^{*}}+\lambda h_{m}^{4s}n\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}_{n,n}\bm{E}_{n})

(61)

Finally, substitute (
61
) and (
50
) into (
47
),we can have the desired result:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{K}_{n,m}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}[\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{m,m}]^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,m}^{-1}\bm{K}_{m,n}[\bm{K}_{n,n}^{-1}+\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}]\bm{E}_{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left([\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\sqrt{\bm{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}_{n,n}\bm{E}_{n}}\right).

∎

Appendix C
Convergence Rate of Kernel Multigird

C.1
Smoothing Property of
\mathcal{S}_{n}

Lemma
10
.

We first write the Back-fit operator
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in error form
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}
. Denote the error in the
t
-th iteration as
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=[\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}_{D}]
where
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}_{d}
corresponds to the error of dimension
d
. Then each Back-fitting iteration can be written a

\displaystyle-\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}=

\displaystyle[\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}_{d}+\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}\left(\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}+\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{d}[\bm{K}_{d}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}]^{-1}\left(\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}+\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\right).

(62)

From (
62
), we can notice that
-\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}
can be viewed as the values of a kernel ridge regression on
\bm{X}_{d}
:
h_{d}^{(t)}(\bm{X}_{d})=-\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}_{d}
such that

h_{d}^{(t)}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}\|h(\bm{X}_{d})-\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}-\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}^{2}.

(63)

Moreover, from the representation theorem, we must have the following equality regarding the RKHS norm of
h^{(t)}_{d}
:

\|h_{d}^{(t)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}^{2}=[\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}.

Obviously
h=0
is a feasible solution of (
63
). Because
h^{(t)}_{d}
is the minimum of (
63
), we must have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\|h_{d}^{(t)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{d}}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|0-\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}-\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D\left(\sum_{d^{\prime}<d}\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{d^{\prime}>d}\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{D}\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}=D\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}

(64)

where the second line is from Jensen’s inequality and the convexity of
l_{2}
norm and the last line is because Back-fitting converges so we must have
\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{d^{\prime}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}
for any
d^{\prime}
and
t
. As a result, the RKHS norm for the error of the
t
-th iteration is

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}=

\displaystyle[\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}_{d}^{-1}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda D\sum_{d=1}^{D}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}=\lambda D^{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}

where the last line is from (
64
).

According to definition,
\|\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}^{2}=[\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{K}^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}
, we can finish the proof.
∎

C.2
Proof of Theorem
12

Theorem
12
.

From Theorem
7
and Lemma
10
, we can conclude that the error form has the following induction property for any
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{(t)}
:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{m}\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{*}\sqrt{n}\left|[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\|\mathcal{S}_{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{*}\sqrt{\lambda n}D[\frac{h_{m}^{s}}{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa^{*}_{n}\kappa_{m}^{*}}}+\sqrt{\lambda n}h_{m}^{2s}]\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}

where the last line is from the condition (
27
) imposed on
h_{m}
. Then by induction, we can immediately have the result:

\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq(1-\delta)\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}\leq(1-\delta)^{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(t-2)}\|_{2}\leq\cdots\leq(1-\delta)^{t}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\|_{2}.

∎
