Seagull: Privacy Preserving Network Verification System
1 st Jaber Daneshamooz, 2 nd Melody Yu, 3 rd Sucheer Maddury

Abstract
The current routing protocol used in the internet backbone is based on manual configuration, making it susceptible to errors. To mitigate these configuration-related issues, it becomes imperative to validate the accuracy and convergence of the algorithm, ensuring a seamless operation devoid of problems. However, the process of network verification faces challenges related to privacy and scalability. This paper addresses these challenges by introducing a novel approach: leveraging privacy-preserving computation, specifically multiparty computation (MPC), to verify the correctness of configurations in the internet backbone, governed by the BGP protocol. Not only does our proposed solution effectively address scalability concerns, but it also establishes a robust privacy framework. Through rigorous analysis, we demonstrate that our approach maintains privacy by not disclosing any information beyond the query result, thus providing a comprehensive and secure solution to the intricacies associated with routing protocol verification in large-scale networks.



I
Introduction

Network configuration, encompassing the assignment of settings and policies, is vital for the proper functioning of the Internet. This involves the intricate task of configuring routers, which are central to maintaining internet connectivity and optimizing resource utilization. Among the various components, configuring routers with routing protocols stands out as both crucial and challenging, with the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) being the de facto standard in the backbone network.

In a backbone network, all routers communicate using the BGP protocol to exchange reachability information. Each router belongs to an autonomous system (AS), and administrators manually apply BGP configurations. The BGP configuration process involves defining and applying policies that determine the criteria for selecting the best path among multiple options. Manual configuration, performed independently in each AS, introduces the possibility of inconsistencies, potentially leading to internet outages—a nightmare for internet clients and e-commerce companies.

The vulnerabilities in BGP peering, including routing leaks and misconfigurations, pose significant challenges. Such issues can not only cause network outages but also be exploited by malicious third parties to disrupt connectivity and leak sensitive network information
[
2
]
.

Alarming statistics, such as the report by Alibaba on networking incidents in 2016 and 2017, indicate that 66% of incidents are caused by misconfigurations. These misconfigurations include mistakes in configuration updates (56%) and bugs triggered by hardware failures (10%)
[
19
]
. In 2019, a network misconfiguration by Verizon resulted in a substantial outage, redirecting web traffic meant for major online platforms through an unintended path
[
5
]
. The financial and reputational repercussions of such incidents, exemplified by Meta’s 2021 outage incurring an estimated 100 million dollars in lost advertising revenue, underscore the critical importance of robust network configurations
[
3
]
. The average cost of network outage downtime is also significant, with every minute costing an average of 9,000 dollars
[
4
]
.

To preemptively identify and prevent such issues, network verification processes come into play. However, the introduction of network verification raises privacy concerns. The input data required for verification, which may include sensitive information like BGP policies, is not something network administrators are willing to share openly. This is particularly challenging in the context of inter AS level verification, where multiple ASes need to contribute data, and privacy concerns become more pronounced.

In intra AS level verification, where all networking devices are managed by a single entity, the AS administrator has local access to all data necessary for verification. Consequently, privacy concerns are less pronounced in this context. However, in inter AS level verification, the verification system must collect data from multiple ASes, posing challenges as AS administrators are hesitant to share data due to economic and security considerations. Therefore, a robust inter AS level network verification system must provide a guaranteed level of privacy to encourage AS administrators to share the required data.

Border gateway protocol

The Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) stands as the predominant routing protocol within the backbone network, designed specifically for the exchange of routing and reachability information among autonomous systems. In BGP, the computation of routes and the selection of paths are determined by the following criteria

ASes utilizing the BGP protocol maintain three key routing tables: the BGP neighbor table, the BGP table (or BGP RIB - Routing Information Base), and the BGP routing table.

The BGP neighbor table catalogs information about BGP neighbors, outlining the connected peers in the network. The BGP table, also known as BGP RIB, stores Network Layer Reachability Information (NLRI), consolidating routes from all neighbors. Each destination in this table may have multiple routes with distinct attributes.

The BGP routing table selectively retains the best routes from the BGP table, determined based on BGP policies. Once chosen, these optimal routes are integrated into the Forwarding Information Base (FIB), directing the actual forwarding of network traffic. This streamlined organization of tables facilitates efficient route selection and propagation in BGP-enabled networks
[
7
]
.

BGP policies play a pivotal role in selecting the optimal path to a destination among multiple identified paths. Additionally, these policies govern the decision of whether to advertise a particular path to neighboring Autonomous Systems (ASes) or not. The intricate nature of these decisions is significantly influenced by the relationships that an AS maintains with its neighboring ASes
[
8
]
.

Two distinct types of relationships exist between neighboring Autonomous Systems (ASes) within the realm of BGP routing:

•

Customer-Provider Relationship:
In this scenario, the customer AS procures connectivity services from the provider AS. This entails the customer paying the provider for the transmission of network traffic.

•

Peering Relationship:
In a peering relationship, two ASes mutually agree to exchange each other’s traffic without any monetary transaction. This collaborative arrangement acts as a network shortcut, enhancing overall performance by facilitating direct traffic exchange between the involved ASes at no cost.

Based on Gao-Rexford rule

1.

A route through the customer has the highest preference(They get paid)

2.

A route through the peer is preferred to a route through provider

3.

A route through the provider has the lowest preference(They need to pay)

II-C

Existing problems

BGP misconfigurations give rise to a spectrum of critical challenges within network infrastructure. These issues include concerns related to reachability, the formation of loops in the network, BGP leaks, and the unauthorized hijacking of prefixes. Understanding and addressing these problems are paramount for maintaining the integrity and efficiency of BGP-based routing in complex network environments.

II-C
1
Reachability

In inter-AS level, network reachability refers to the ability of data packets to travel across different autonomous systems and reach their intended destination. It involves establishing communication paths between devices in distinct autonomous systems, considering independent policies and configurations.

Reachability encompasses various forms of misconfiguration. For instance, network loops can prevent certain ASes from accessing specific destinations, while prefix hijacking can render an AS unreachable if its prefix is hijacked. In this paper, we define reachability as the ability for a source
s
to access a specific destination
d

s
can successfully reach
d
within the network architecture under examination.

II-C
2
Loop freedom

Network loops in the inter-AS level often stem from BGP misconfigurations. In the presence of a BGP misconfiguration, packets may circulate endlessly within the loop until the Time-To-Live (TTL) value reaches zero, at which point the packets are dropped. Consequently, the intended destination remains unreachable as the packets are trapped in the loop.

As an illustration, consider figure
5

Node 2 is aware that nodes 3 and 6 have a route to 4 through the previous route advertisement. Similarly, node 3 knows that nodes 2 and 6 have a route to 4. If both nodes 2 and 3 prefer each other over node 6 as the new route to 4, it would lead to a routing cycle.

3
BGP route leak

A route leak occurs when routing announcements extend beyond their intended scope, violating the policies established by the sender, receiver, or any AS along the preceding AS path. The intended scope is typically defined by a set of local redistribution and filtering policies distributed among the involved ASes. These policies are often specified in terms of the pair-wise peering relationships between ASes, such as customer, transit provider, or peer relationships
[
25
]
.

The consequences of a route leak include the redirection of traffic through unintended paths, potentially exposing it to eavesdropping or traffic analysis. This redirection may or may not lead to network overload or a black hole. Route leaks can occur accidentally or through malicious intent, but more commonly, they result from unintentional misconfigurations
[
25
]
.

Prefix hijacking

A considerable number of routing incidents result from the inherent vulnerability of BGP, particularly in prefix hijacking. BGP lacks the capability to verify the legitimacy of announcements from ASes. In a prefix hijacking scenario, an AS can exploit this weakness by falsely advertising a prefix it does not own. This manipulation redirects traffic intended for the legitimate owner to the hijacking AS, enabling malicious actions such as blackholing or interception

Given the aforementioned considerations, caution must be exercised in implementing new policies within Autonomous Systems (ASes). Prior to executing any configuration updates, AS administrators should meticulously assess potential issues using network verification systems.

Within the networking domain, business owners, particularly those in the network sector, are wary of sharing proprietary commercial information. The potential leak of such confidential details, especially through the observation of Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) policies, poses a significant risk to their enterprises. Consequently, it is paramount for AS administrators to maintain strict confidentiality regarding their BGP routing policies to safeguard the integrity and security of their network infrastructure.

By addressing these critical network properties with a focus on scalability and privacy preservation, our system aims to enhance the robustness and security of network infrastructure.

III-B

Internet Topology Analysis

The shallow depth of the Autonomous System (AS) connection graph, as highlighted by RIPE’s analysis indicating an average AS path length of 4.5
[
53
]
, underscores the interconnected nature of the internet backbone. This interconnectedness implies that the forwarding graph resembles a wide, shallow tree structure.

In light of this observation, we establish a threshold of 15 as the maximum allowable number of ASes in the path between two nodes. This threshold reflects the practical considerations of the internet’s topology and the typical path lengths observed in real-world routing scenarios. If we take a path from a node and the length exceeds 15, and the destination is still not visited, we conclude that the destination is unreachable. However, it’s worth noting that this threshold can be adjusted as necessary based on specific network requirements or evolving internet infrastructure.

III-C

Input representation and sharing

The network verification system plays a crucial role in identifying potential policy conflicts within the network. Given that the AS level FIBs are populated based on BGP policies, any modifications to routing policies directly manifest in the FIB. Consequently, conflicts between BGP policies are readily apparent as discrepancies in the forwarding tables.

BGP policies, representing conceptual preferences for one route over another, are reflected in the FIB with concrete details such as the next-hop ID. The distinction lies in the fact that FIBs provide tangible information about each route, making it easier to detect conflicts and network issues, including loops. Seagull, recognizing the advantage of FIBs in this context, utilizes the FIB of each node for network verification. By leveraging FIB information across all network nodes, Seagull ensures a comprehensive and effective approach to verifying the integrity of the network.

The forwarding graph, derived from the Forwarding Information Base (FIB), serves as a network representation, as illustrated in Figure
7
. This graph which is represented as a list of edges, takes the form of a directed graph, where each edge connects a node to its designated next hop for a specific destination within the network. It’s important to note that there is one forwarding graph per destination.

In this graph, each node, except for the destination node, has an outdegree of exactly one, indicating a unique next hop in the FIB. This structured representation effectively captures the routing information for each specific destination, facilitating efficient and targeted routing within the network.

The absence of outgoing edges for a node in the forwarding graph indicates that the destination is unreachable via that specific node, implying a failure in the network’s reachability test. Detecting a node without an outgoing edge is an implementation detail signaling that the node has not shared its next hop for a specific prefix. This observation is crucial for maintaining accurate and effective routing within the network. It is noteworthy that if a node shares more than one next hop for a destination, only the newest one is accepted.

In the depicted setup illustrated in Figure

, the Autonomous Systems (ASes) share their Forwarding Information Bases (FIB) with the verifier. This transfer is meticulously designed to ensure that divulging the FIB to the verification section does not expose any confidential information. Employing the additive secret sharing method for this purpose
[
13
]
, each server in the verifier receives a share of every FIB entry. During the initial run of Seagull, all ASes send their entire FIB for every destination to the verifier, with the verifier preserving this data. Subsequently, ASes only transmit changes in their FIB resulting from updates in BGP configurations and policies.

To safeguard FIB privacy, Seagull employs multiparty computation (MPC) within the verifier. The verifier comprises three or more
parties that collaborate to confidentially execute the verification process. Seagull leverages the additive secret sharing method for MPC. In this approach, each data element in the FIB is broken into several shares, with each share allocated to one of the servers in the verifier. The summation of these shares reconstructs the original data. While the summation discloses the secret values, the security of additive secret sharing ensures that none of these servers gains information about the original data unless at least one of them refrains from compromising security.

III-D

Reachability

The reachability check evaluates whether a destination node, denoted as
d
, is accessible from a given source node,
s
. Leveraging the specific graph representation introduced earlier, this verification process is straightforward. Utilizing multiparty computation, we traverse the edges, commencing from node
s
and aiming to reach
d
. If the algorithm successfully reaches
d
, it returns true. Conversely, if we iterate more than 15 times, representing the maximum hop count, without visiting
d
, we infer that
d
is not reachable.

It is crucial to note that the access pattern to different sections of the shared graph might inadvertently unveil the association between secretly shared values and specific nodes. To mitigate this risk, we adopt a meticulous approach. Instead of prematurely stopping upon finding the next hop, we consistently iterate through the entire array. Additionally, we adhere to a predetermined limit of 15 iterations before returning the final verdict. This cautious methodology ensures that the reachability check maintains the confidentiality of shared values while accurately determining the accessibility between nodes in the shared graph.

III-E

Loop detection

As previously mentioned, a forwarding graph, where each node can access a destination, takes the form of a tree, with each node (excluding the destination) having a single outgoing edge. This can also be conceptualized as the destination having a loopback.

If a set of entries in the FIB creates a loop, that loop becomes evident in the forwarding graph. A loop-free forwarding graph is weakly connected, meaning that if we convert all directed edges in the forwarding graph to undirected edges, it results in a connected undirected graph. In simpler terms, if a set of FIB entries creates a loop, the equivalent undirected forwarding graph becomes a disconnected graph (see Figure
9
). Therefore, to assess the loop-free property of the network, we need only check whether the forwarding graph is connected or not.

It’s important to note that the conventional methods of identifying loops in a general graph, such as using Breadth-First Search (BFS)

This is primarily due to the privacy-preserving nature of the method and the unique characteristics of our graph, which resembles a tree structure. Therefore, our problem revolves around checking connectivity or identifying the connected components within the forwarding graph.

III-F

Algorithm improvements

III-F
1
Removing leaves

Before initiating the BFS, it is advantageous to traverse the reversed FIBand identify the leaf nodes. Leaf nodes are those without a next hop. Given that the indegree of each node (excluding the destination) in the reversed FIB is precisely one, we can deduce that the second column (next hop) in the reverse FIB contains a list of all unique AS numbers.

If an AS number in the second column does not appear in the first column (source node), we can conclude that the corresponding node is a leaf, and consequently, we can remove the associated edge. Given the nature of the forwarding graph, which resembles a wide and shallow tree, removing leaves substantially reduces the input size. This reduction in input size leads to more efficient and faster privacy-preserving BFS.

III-G

Discover several nodes at a time

In a typical BFS, each node is visited, and its unvisited neighbors are added to the queue before exploring the next node. This method is well-suited for a graph represented as an adjacency list, and its execution time is typically
O(n+e)
, where
n
is the number of nodes and
e
is the number of edges. However, our input graph is represented as a list of edges, and the traditional BFS method is not optimized for this representation.

In our approach, when visiting an edge, we check whether the source node is in the visited list. If the source node is already visited, we add the next hop to the visited list. This approach eliminates the need to iterate through the entire table for each node visited. Instead, we efficiently find the neighbors of several nodes simultaneously. Consequently, the number of times we need to iterate through the FIB table is on the order of the depth of the graph (a small number, e.g., 15) rather than on the order of the number of nodes. This optimization significantly enhances the efficiency of the algorithm.

This check is crucial to prevent revisiting a node and avoiding an infinite loop in the graph, particularly when dealing with back edges. However, in our FIB, the out-degree of each node is equal to 1. Since we are employing the BFS algorithm on the reverse FIB, the in-degree of each node in the input of the BFS algorithm is 1.

When the in-degree of every vertex is equal to 1, it implies that there are no back edges to the same node in our graph. As a result, the conditional statement for checking

becomes unnecessary, and we can omit running this if statement for efficiency purposes.

III-J

Prefix hijacking detection and prevention

Prefix hijacking poses a significant threat to network security, and Seagull employs a straightforward approach to detect and prevent such incidents without the need for intricate computations on the graph. The primary strategy involves checking that no more than one Autonomous System (AS) claims ownership of a prefix. In the Forwarding Information Base (FIB), owning a prefix manifests as a loopback, with the destination in the forwarding graph representing the prefix owner. By examining each FIB, if more than one node shares the same source and destination, it indicates a potential prefix hijacking incident. This detection process entails a single iteration through the FIB.

The Regional Internet Registry (RIR)
[
11
]
assigns different IP prefixes to various Autonomous Systems (ASes). To figure out if there’s any mischief going on, we secretly share a table that links AS numbers to their IP prefixes. If more than one AS says they own the same prefix, we can use this table to check who’s telling the truth and identify any potential troublemakers. This helps us make sure that IP addresses are in the right hands and keeps the network secure.

Another effective measure for preventing prefix hijacking is Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI)
[
12
]
. RPKI relies on digital certification, providing a means for anyone consulting its repository to verify the accuracy of the association between a prefix and an autonomous system. This ensures that the claimed ownership of IP prefixes aligns with authorized and validated records, adding an extra layer of security against potential hijacking attempts.

III-K

BGP route leak

BGP route leaks often occur due to unintentional misconfigurations, although malicious behavior can also be a contributing factor. Seagull is proficient in identifying BGP leaks resulting from prefix hijacking or misconfigurations leading to blackholes (e.g., loops or lack of reachability). In such cases, Seagull employs the aforementioned verification methods to detect and address the anomalies.

However, if the BGP leak stems from incorrect export/import policy setups in Autonomous Systems (ASes) or malicious activities that do not lead to unreachability (e.g., used for traffic analysis purposes), Seagull faces limitations in detection. This is because such issues cannot be identified solely through the next-hop information shared with the verifier. Seagull’s capabilities are optimized for scenarios where the impact on reachability is a key indicator of potential problems.

IV
Threat model and privacy

IV-A

Threat model

Seagull operates within the framework of the honest but curious (HBC) threat model, where parties may attempt to extract as much information as possible without resorting to manipulation or conducting faulty operations.

A protocol is considered
t
-private under the HBC model if a coalition of up to
t
parties, colluding at the conclusion of the protocol, cannot access the private data. Seagull achieves
(t-1)
-privacy, where
t
denotes the number of servers within the verifier. Consequently, even if one server refrains from collusion with others, the confidentiality of the data remains intact. Given the inherent lack of trust among entities in this scenario, an anti-trust threat model applies to the verifier. In this context, the presence of a single non-colluding server within the verifier suffices to uphold data privacy. Across the entire system, all entities are assumed to adhere to the principles of honesty but curiosity.

Our objective is to execute the aforementioned algorithms in a privacy-preserving manner, with the ultimate aim of safeguarding AS policies from exposure. We aim to demonstrate that AS policies can be inferred from Forwarding Information Base (FIB) data. Therefore, to ensure policy confidentiality, we must secure the FIB during computation. This entails maintaining the confidentiality of FIB data and ensuring that access patterns and frequency do not inadvertently disclose policy information. Additionally, we will establish that the outcome of computations does not divulge any details that could lead to policy identification. Consequently, the system’s only discernible output will be the result of the algorithm (True or False).

IV-B

Attack model

Seagull’s reliance on the Forwarding Information Base (FIB) for network verification introduces a potential vulnerability. While a typical adversary might only discern the best route from each node to a specific destination by accessing the FIB, an active adversary can exploit this information to unveil all possible routes with their respective rankings.

The adversarial strategy involves systematically removing links corresponding to the best path of a node in the FIB. As the BGP protocol dynamically adjusts to changes in network topology, the removal of links prompts the protocol to select the next best path. This process can be iteratively applied, allowing the adversary to uncover multiple routes to a specific destination, along with their ranking order. With knowledge of all possible routes and their rankings, the attacker gains insights into the BGP policies governing a node.

Consider a directed graph, denoted as
G
, constructed based on the BGP table for destination
D
. Here, an edge
E(u,v)
signifies a route from node
u
to
D
with the next-hop being node
v
. The Forwarding Information Base (FIB) constitutes a subgraph of
G
, wherein edges exclusively represent routes with the highest rank. Consequently, the FIB retains certain information about the BGP table.

In the event of an attacker gaining access to the forwarding graph, they can exploit the strategy outlined above. By selectively severing links between nodes and their next-hops to
D
, the attacker triggers the BGP protocol to recalculate the best route. Repeating this process multiple times reveals successive routes with varying rankings from a specific node
x
to destination
D
. The attacker must iterate this query for each neighbor of node
x
in graph
G
, with the number of iterations (
n

G

This methodological exploitation underscores the importance of safeguarding the FIB to prevent adversaries from deducing sensitive BGP policy information through iterative probing and analysis.

Some BGP tables are selectively accessible, with certain entries designated as public while others remain private or only partially disclosed. Given the crucial nature of this information for AS administrators, safeguarding its confidentiality is of paramount importance, as unauthorized access poses a significant threat. Seagull is specifically designed to address this challenge by focusing on concealing the Forwarding Information Base (FIB), ensuring that attackers cannot glean any information about the BGP table through observation of FIB.

The risk of private data leakage exists across various points within the system. An attacker might exploit vulnerabilities during the sharing of private data by the data provider, or a malicious insider could illicitly access stored data within the system. Additionally, there is a potential for the querier to obtain sensitive information from the query results.

IV-C

Privacy in data collection and storage

During the initial phase of data collection and storage, our approach involves the utilization of additive secret sharing, a cryptographic method that enhances privacy and security. While Shamir secret sharing is a viable alternative, the preference for additive secret sharing stems from its distinct advantages in our specific context.

IV-D

Privacy during computation

Throughout the computation phase, our approach employs multiparty computation (MPC), ensuring that the outcome of operations is the only information revealed. However, an additional crucial consideration is the enforcement of data-oblivious operations.

Data-oblivious execution, also referred to simply as oblivious execution, entails executing the same sequence of operations irrespective of the input data, and it involves data-independent memory accesses. This characteristic makes data-oblivious execution particularly suitable for tasks outsourced to external entities. While techniques like Oblivious RAM (ORAM) excel in protecting certain aspects, such as concealing the accessed index, they often fall short in hiding the number of read/writes to the underlying data structure.

d

In a nutshell, the security definition intuitively demands that the server gains no insight into the access pattern. In other words, no information should be disclosed regarding

•

Which specific data is being accessed.

•

The timestamp of the last access to the data.

•

The recurrence of accessing the same data (linkability).

•

The access pattern, be it sequential, random, or otherwise.

•

Whether the access operation pertains to a read or a write action.

Our implementation of the algorithm, coupled with the utilization of Scale-Mamba, which inherently employs Oblivious RAM (ORAM) by default, ensures the complete obliviousness of the computation.

IV-E

Privacy in sharing the result

The query results in our system are represented by binary values for all predefined query types. Through our analysis, we’ve identified that certain sensitive and repetitive queries might pose a risk of data leakage. To address this concern, we have taken the approach of either refraining from implementing such queries or introducing a privacy budget to limit repetitive queries and scanning activities.

It’s important to note that the binary nature of the query result ensures that it lacks specific information about the private data. Instead, it serves as a boolean value indicating whether a particular network property is satisfied or not. Given that the verification system necessitates exact results, differential privacy is not employed at any stage within Seagull. This strategic decision further strengthens our commitment to maintaining the integrity and precision of the verification process without compromising the confidentiality of the underlying data.

Every node in the network has a FIB which contains the necessary information for reaching all of the IP prefixes. In contrast to the source-based FIB, the destination FIB contains information for reaching an IP prefix by every node.

Random FIB generation

In order to generate a random destination-based FIB, we used BFS as a graph search algorithm. Since the AS network topology is a connected unweighted graph, the BFS search would generate a minimum spanning tree(forwarding graph). In addition, in an unweighted graph, the shortest path is the path with the least number of edges. Therefore, by running BFS on the AS network graph, we always reach the other node from the given source using the minimum number of edges.

VI
Related works

While most of the current works focus on the scalability of network verification in large-scale networks, privacy issues are usually undermined; a large-scale network verification system that ignores privacy issues is useless. Although it can verify a large network so fast, the ASes would avoid sharing the required information with the versifier. In other words, preserving privacy is as important as the scalability of these network versifiers. To the best of our knowledge, there is no prior work on privacy issues in network verification.

Network verification is an expensive operation. Therefore, if the privacy preserving operation is not designed efficiently, the system would be so costly and not applicable. Therefore, we can not blindly apply privacy preserving computation. We need to customize the algorithms for solving this specific problem.

Most of the works in privacy preserving systems try to remove the link between the data owner and the data. However, in network verification, we can not ignore or eliminate this bound. In network verification, the data records are related to each other and release some information about the network design.

Unlike many applications of privacy preserving systems where the program tolerates some level of inaccuracy, network verification systems are so sensitive and require a fully accurate response. Hence, we can not apply differential privacy and sacrifice accuracy to get privacy. Therefore, the common approaches like node differential privacy or edge differential privacy are not applicable here.

Additionally, BGP already includes mechanisms to prevent loops, however, these mechanisms have limitations
[
46
]
. For eBGP, or BGP protocol between ASes, there is an AS-path attribute to information packets that cause an AS to discard the packet if it sees its own AS number. For iBGP, or BGP protocol within ASes, split horizon rule states that packets from one iBGP party should not be sent to a neighbor. The main issue with these mechanisms is that they simply prevent the forwarding of a packet of looped information, without detecting the loop itself. The information in the eBGP case will simply be lost as it would be discarded by the original AS. Consequently, designing a preventative mechanism that can enable to seamless forwarding of the packet would be useful.

Huisan Xu et al. introduces a privacy-preserving system for interdomain configuration verification
[
43
]
, enabling Autonomous Systems (ASes) to simulate and verify their networks using data oblivious computation. This system relies on a simulation-based approach where ASes encode their BGP configurations into data-oblivious structures and collaboratively compute a secret network data plane, ultimately achieving a converged state when all nodes have determined their best routes. The system incorporates DO-Simulation for the primary algorithm and a DO-DPV algorithm for verifying network properties, utilizing n-party SMPC primitives and a DFS-based traversal. However, the system’s practicality is limited by its lengthy verification time (up to 51 minutes for 32 ASes), support for only basic BGP attributes, lack of incremental verification, and inability to differentiate between route and forwarding reachability.

Arnaud Dethise et al. address the challenges of integrating SDN with BGP at SDXs, focusing on how BGP deflections in SDN-enabled IXPs can lead to forwarding loops and explores solutions for loop-free routing

They introduce a path verifier that uses the Distinct-Match primitive and secure multi-party computation protocols to to determine if the new or modified routing policy will create a forwarding loop between the SDXes. However, its utility is confined to detecting loops in SDN contexts at IXPs, not extending to AS routing changes.

Gilad Asharov et al. advocate for the logical centralization of BGP route computation through Multi-Party Computation (MPC) among parties lacking mutual trust

They introduce two algorithms for AS-level interdomain routing: the first prioritizes routes based on AS business relationships, while the second allows ASes to choose next hops based on individual preferences. These algorithms, employing BFS for graph traversal, are translated into boolean circuits optimized for MPC, highlighting efficiency in large networks but limited by a semi-honest adversarial model reliant on two computational parties. While the paper suggests practical runtimes at regional scales, it lacks evidence for global scale performance and acknowledges limitations in protocol choices impacting broader applicability.

To minimize computational costs, their algorithm excludes stub ASs, which lack customers and do not handle transit traffic, thus simplifying the overall process and reducing the computational burden. Instead of outsourcing route computations, each AS forwards encrypted messages along all known links to a predefined destination, cumulatively summing weights for the links by using homomorphic encryption. On receipt of all messages, the destination AS can determine the shortest path, even though the intermediate ASs on the route are kept private. The main disadvantage of this work is its significant message overhead. It further requires a prior knowledge of candidate destinations for the creation of routes between ASs.

Using DFS and DAG are common approaches for finding loops in a graph. Running these recursive algorithms for network verification requires complete information about the graph connectivity and node policies. This issue leads to the leak of information even using privacy preserving systems. Current approaches are trying to run DFS in MPC without revealing information. Adding fake nodes and fake links to the graph is one of the approaches to hide private information in the process of running DFS in MPC. These approaches fail to keep the information private enough. Also, the process is so expensive in terms of computation and communication. Here, we present another approach to find loops in the network graph.
