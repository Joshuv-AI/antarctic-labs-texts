Krylov–Bellman boosting: Super-linear policy evaluation in general state spaces


Abstract
We present and analyze the Krylov–Bellman Boosting (KBB) algorithm for policy evaluation in general state spaces. It alternates between fitting the Bellman residual using non-parametric regression (as in boosting), and estimating the value function via the least-squares temporal difference (LSTD) procedure applied with a feature set that grows adaptively over time. By exploiting the connection to Krylov methods, we equip this method with two attractive guarantees. First, we provide a general convergence bound that allows for separate estimation errors in residual fitting and LSTD computation. Consistent with our numerical experiments, this bound shows that convergence rates depend on the restricted spectral structure, and are typically super-linear. Second, by combining this meta-result with sample-size dependent guarantees for residual fitting and LTSD computation, we obtain concrete statistical guarantees that depend on the sample size along with the complexity of the function class used to fit the residuals. We illustrate the behavior of the KBB algorithm for various types of policy evaluation problems, and typically find large reductions in sample complexity relative to the standard approach of fitted value iteration.

Introduction

The past decade has witnessed tremendous success with applications of
reinforcement learning (e.g.,
[
58
,
34
,
52
]
), but many challenges remain. Notably, many
procedures in reinforcement learning are very “data hungry”, meaning
that large sample sizes—which may or may not be available in a given
application—are often required. Given a data-dependent procedure,
its
sample complexity
is a function that characterizes the
number of samples required, as a function of the problem parameters,
to achieve a desired error tolerance. Given the cost associated with
sampling (or lack of availability of large sample sizes), an important
problem is to develop RL algorithms with the lowest possible sample
complexity.

In this paper, we focus on the problem of
policy evaluation
for
Markov decision processes defined over general state spaces. Policy
evaluation occupies a central role in reinforcement learning. It is
of interest both in its own right and as a key subroutine in many
algorithms. For instance, classical algorithms for computing optimal
policies, such as policy iteration
[
24
]
, require repeated
rounds of policy evaluation; iterative schemes like policy gradient
methods
[
64
,
53
,
55
,
28
]
and actor-critic
schemes
[
33
,
37
,
65
,
14
]
involve maintaining estimates of the value
function as a part of the algorithm.

Policy evaluation in discrete state spaces, known as the tabular
setting, is very well-understood (e.g.,
[
7
,
5
,
41
]
); notably, recent work has
exhibited various procedures, for both batch and on-line settings,
that achieve fundamental lower bounds in a strong instance-optimal
sense
[
31
,
46
]
. Far more
challenging are Markov decision processes involving continuous state
spaces. In this setting, there are at least two broad classes of
approaches. One approach is based on using a suitably “rich”
non-parametric function class, such as regression trees, reproducing
kernel Hilbert spaces, or neural networks, to repeatedly refit an
estimate of the value function; procedures of this type are known
collectively as instantiations of fitted value iteration
(FVI)
[
20
]
. The FVI algorithm along with its close
relative fitted
Q
-iteration (FQI), has been the focus of a long line
of work (e.g.,
[
2
,
40
,
51
,
17
,
23
,
18
]
).

Another classical approach to approximate policy evaluation, even
simpler than fitted value iteration, is via the
least-squares
temporal difference
method, or LSTD for
short
[
3
,
49
,
11
]
. Given a finite collection of features or basis
functions, it computes the value function within the associated linear
span that provides the “best fit”, as formalized in terms of a
projected fixed point (e.g.,
[
15
,
66
]
). There has been line of work analyzing algorithms
for computing the LSTD solution
[
59
,
54
,
13
,
35
]
, as well as more general settings
involving projected fixed-point equations
[
39
,
63
,
19
]
. This line of analysis, for the
most part, takes as input a fixed set of features. In practice, the
quality of the LSTD fit depends strongly on the given basis, and
finding a good basis is often a difficult task. Often, features are
chosen based on knowledge of the specific problem domain; there is
also a line of past work
[
30
,
38
]
on
automated procedures for feature construction.

1.1
Our contributions

In this paper, we propose and analyze a procedure for approximate
policy evaluation that combines the LSTD framework with the boosting
approach of fitting residuals. The method estimates the value
function by computing a sequence of LSTD solutions, where the basis
used at each round is augmented with a non-parametric estimate of the
Bellman residual from the previous round. The LSTD steps can be
interpreted as computing a sequence of Krylov subspaces, and
accordingly we refer to our method as the

Krylov–Bellman boosting
algorithm, or KBB for short.

We note that boosting methods—that is, using non-parametric
procedures to fit the Bellman residual—have been studied in past
work on reinforcement learning tasks
[
1
]
. In our
method, however, the combination with LSTD is equally important. As
shown in our theoretical analysis, it is the combination of boosting
with LSTD that allows us establish convergence guarantees for KBB that
are provably superior to those of fitted value iteration (FVI).
Consistent with this theory, we also see the gains of KBB over FVI in
our experimental studies. In the tabular setting, an idealized form of
the KBB algorithm with known transition dynamics—and hence no error
in fitting the residuals—has been studied in past
work
[
44
,
43
]
, in which context it is called
the method of Bellman error basis functions (BEBF). However for
general state spaces it is unrealistic to assume that residuals can be
computed without error; for continuous state spaces this task can be
challenging even if the model dynamics are known, since it involves
computing the integral that defines the Bellman update. Even more
challenging—and the focus of this paper—is the setting of unknown
dynamics, in which case any estimate of the residual is subject to
statistical error due to a finite sample size. It has been
noted
[
42
]
that the BEBF method is equivalent to a
Krylov subspace method
[
47
,
48
]
for
solving a finite-dimensional linear system. This connection to Krylov
methods plays an important role in our paper; as opposed to the
classical use of Krylov methods in solving finite-dimensional linear
systems, in this paper we tackle the more general problem of solving a
linear operator equation, which (for general continuous state spaces)
corresponds to an infinite-dimensional problem.

The primary contributions of this paper take the form of two theorems.
Our first main result, stated as
Theorem

1
, is a general
convergence guarantee for the KBB algorithm. It involves a sequence
of so-called restricted spectral values (cf.
equation

14
)
that track how the effective conditioning of the residual problem
improves as the algorithm proceeds. This guarantee should be viewed
as a “meta-result”, since it applies to arbitrary procedures used to
estimate the Bellman residual and to approximate the LSTD solutions,
with the convergence guarantee depending upon these two associated
errors. In particular,
Theorem

1
applies both when the
transition dynamics are known and numerical integration is used to
approximate the Bellman residual at each round, and in the
reinforcement learning setting where the dynamics are unknown but
samples from the Markov chain are available. Our second set of
results, stated as
Theorem

2
and
Corollary

1
,
provides guarantees for this latter RL setting, and in particular, for
independent samples of state, next-state and reward triples. Under
this set-up,
Theorem

2
provides an upper bound on the
regression error for a general function class, whereas

Corollary

1
combines this guarantee with
Theorem

1
to
provide an “end-to-end” guarantee for a particular instantiation of
the KBB algorithm.

\mu
, let
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)

denote the space of real-valued functions with domain
\mathcal{X}
,

\mu
. This space is
equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,\,g\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f(X)g(X)],

along with the norm
\|f\|_{\mu}=\sqrt{\langle f,\,f\rangle_{\mu}}
. Given a
subspace
\mathscr{S}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
,
we write
v\perp_{\mu}\mathscr{S}
to indicate that
v
is orthogonal to the

\mathscr{S}
with respect to the inner product

\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
, and
\mathscr{S}^{\perp}
to denote the set
of all such vectors
v
. For an operator
\mathcal{Q}
, we write
\mathcal{Q}\succeq 0
to mean
\langle f,\,\mathcal{Q}f\rangle_{\mu}\geq 0

for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
,
and we say that
\mathcal{Q}
is a
positive
operator
for short. Given two operators
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, we
write
\mathcal{P}\succeq\mathcal{Q}
to indicate that
\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q}\succeq 0
. Given samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we define
the
empirical norm

\|f\|_{n}\coloneq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(x_{i})}
; note that it is a random

2
Background

Here we describe some background necessary to explain and motivate our
results. In Section
2.1
we describe the basic setup of the
problem of interest, solving for the value function of a Markov reward
process, as well as the sampling model to which we have
access. Section
2.2
then describes an approximate method
for solving for the value function as linear combination of given
basis functions. Finally in Section
2.3
we describe
Krylov subspace methods for solving linear equations.

2.1
Markov decision and reward processes

A Markov decision process consists of a state space
\mathcal{X}
, an
action space
\mathcal{A}
, a reward function
R
, along with a
collection of probability transition functions. In this paper, we
focus exclusively on the policy evaluation procedure, in which case a
given policy is fixed up front. For a fixed policy, a Markov decision
process reduces to a
Markov reward process
(MRP), which can be
characterized more simply in terms of the state space
\mathcal{X}
,
along with a probability transition kernel
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}
, along with a
reward function.

In a Markov reward process, the states evolve over time according to a
collection of transition kernels
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}
. At some time
t=1,2,\ldots
, when in state
x_{t}
, the next state is generated by
sampling
X_{t+1}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
, where for
each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(\cdot\mid x)

defines a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
. Under standard
regularity assumptions, the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}
has a stationary measure

\mu
satisfying the consistency condition

\displaystyle\mu(x^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(x^{\prime}\mid X)].

The MRP also is equipped with a reward function
R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we assume to be uniformly bounded and

\mu
-measurable. At time
t=1,2,\ldots
, when in state

x_{t}
we receive a reward
R(x_{t})
.

The problem of policy evaluation corresponds to computing the

value function

\displaystyle V^{\star}(x)\coloneq\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\cdot R(X_{t})\mid x_{0}=x\big],

(1)

where the conditional expectation is taken over a trajectory

(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)
from the underlying Markov chain. Since
the reward function
R
is measurable and uniformly bounded, the
value function exists and is uniquely defined. Moreover, it is
measurable, uniformly bounded by
\frac{\|R\|_{\infty}}{1-\gamma}
, and therefore
V^{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.

Given a value function
V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
we define the Bellman
operator
\mathcal{T}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(V)(x)\coloneq R(x)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(\cdot\mid x)}[V(x^{\prime})]\qquad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$}.

(2)

By classical results on dynamic programming, the value function is a
fixed point of the Bellman operator—that is,
V^{\star}=\mathcal{T}(V^{\star})
. Moreover, the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}

is
\gamma
-contractive in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
; see the
standard references
[
45
,
49
,
6
]
for further background.

In order to clarify the connection to solving linear operator
equations, define the transition operator
\mathcal{P}(V)(x)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[V(x^{\prime})\big]
. Observing that
\mathcal{P}
is a linear
operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, we can rewrite the Bellman fixed point
relation as

\displaystyle V^{\star}=R+\gamma\mathcal{P}V^{\star}\iff(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})V^{\star}=R,

(3)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
. Thus,
computing the value function is equivalent to solving the linear
operator equation (
3
).

We conclude by describing the sampling model studied in this paper.
The operator
\mathcal{P}
and the reward function
R
are unknown
to us, but we have access to sample transition pairs
(x,r,x^{\prime})
generated as follows

\displaystyle x\sim\mu,\quad r=R(x),\quad\text{and}\quad x^{\prime}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}(\cdot\mid x).

(4)

To be concrete, intermediate stages of our procedure make use of a
dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{t},r_{t},x^{\prime}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{n}
of
n
samples.

2.2
Linear function approximation and LSTD

When the state space
\mathcal{X}
is sufficiently complex—either
finite but with a very large number of states, or continuous in
nature—any efficient scheme for policy evaluation requires some form
of function approximation. In loose terms, we choose some function
space, and then seek to find the “best” approximation to the value
function within this space.

A classical and widely-used approach is based on
linear function
approximation.
Given a collection of functions
\phi_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
for
j=1,\ldots,J
, known either as features or basis
functions, we use the subspace
\mathscr{S}=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{J}\}
to approximate the value function. An
attractive feature is that any function in the subspace
\mathscr{S}

can be written as
V_{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\phi_{j}
for
some vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
, so that computations can be reduced
to linear-algebraic operations over
\mathbb{R}^{J}
. One way to define the
best approximation is via the notion of a projected fixed point
(e.g.,
[
15
,
66
]
). Defining the
projection onto the subspace
\mathscr{S}
via

\displaystyle\Pi(f)\coloneq\arg\min_{V\in\mathscr{S}}\|f-V\|_{\mu},

we seek a function

2.3
Krylov subspace methods

Portions of our development rely on connections to Krylov subspace
methods for solving systems of linear equations, which we describe
briefly here. See the book by Saad
[
48
]
for more
details. Suppose we are interested in solving a linear system
Ax=b
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a matrix, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector. In many cases, the dimension
d
may be very
large so directly solving the system of equations is computationally
very expensive. Krylov subspace methods are based on approximating the
solution to such a linear system via an expanding sequence of
subspaces. For each positive integer
j=1,\ldots,d
, we define
the
j^{th}
-order Krylov subspace as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b)\coloneq\text{span}\big\{b,Ab,A^{2}b,\ldots,A^{j}b\big\}.

(8)

Using the subspace
\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b)
, we can define an
approximate solution
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b)

\displaystyle\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b)\qquad\text{such that}\qquad b-A\widehat{x}\perp\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b).

(9)

Many algorithms for computing approximations to
x
involve forming
the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{j}(A,b)
and then solving the
system (
9
); the conjugate gradient method is
a notable instance. In the setting of tabular reinforcement learning
the problem of policy evaluation is equivalent to solving a linear
system; thus Krylov methods are applicable, as has been noted in
past work
[
42
]
. Our method, to be described in
the next section, exploits the connection to Krylov theory at the more
abstract level of operators, and allows for statistical errors in the
updates.

3
Krylov–Bellman boosting and its guarantees

In this section, we first describe the Krylov–Bellman boosting (KBB)
procedure (
Section

3.1
), and then state some theoretical
guarantees on its performance (
Section

3.2
). The KBB
algorithm involves a sequence of LSTD solutions, along with a sequence
of fits to the Bellman residual. Our first result (
Theorem

1
) is
a general guarantee that allows for arbitrary errors in these
intermediate computations. In
Section

3.3
, we analyze a
general family of non-parametric procedures for fitting the residual,
and provide an end-to-end result that bounds the behavior of the KBB
procedure with statistical error.

3.1
Krylov–Bellman Boosting

In operational terms, the KBB procedure can be viewed as a procedure
for adaptively choosing features or basis function; each feature is a
function belonging to
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
. At each iteration
t=1,2,\ldots
, the KBB procedure maintains a collection of features

\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
that defines the subspace
\mathscr{S}_{t}=\text{span}\big\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{t}\}
. In the idealized
(but unimplementable) form of the procedure, there are two steps
involved in the update from iterate
t
to
t+1
:

•

Compute the population level LSTD
solution (
5
) defined by the subspace

\mathscr{S}_{t}
. Call it
{V}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}_{t}
.

•

Compute the Bellman residual
\mathcal{B}({V}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}_{t})
and
use it to form the augmented subspace

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{t+1}=\text{span}\big\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{t},\mathcal{B}({V}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}_{t})\big\}.

Suppose that we initialize this procedure with the subspace

\mathscr{S}_{1}=\text{span}\{R\}
, corresponding to the span of
the reward function. With this choice, the subspace
\mathscr{S}_{t+1}

is equivalent to the Krylov subspace (
8
) of
order
t+1
defined by the linear operator
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}
and the reward function
R
—more
specifically, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{t+1}=\mathcal{K}_{t+1}(\mathcal{Q},R)\equiv\text{span}\big\{R,\mathcal{Q}(R),\mathcal{Q}^{2}(R),\ldots,\mathcal{Q}^{t}(R)\big\}.

Moreover, observe that the orthogonality
condition (
6
) that defines the population LSTD
solution is equivalent to the orthogonality
condition (
9
) that defines the associated
Krylov subspace. Thus, this idealized algorithm is exactly an
abstract Krylov subspace method for computing the solution

V^{\star}
to the linear operator equation
(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})V^{\star}=R
over the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.

In the setting of unknown model dynamics and general state spaces,
this idealized scheme is unimplementable for two
reasons. First, the population LSTD solution cannot be computed
exactly; instead we must approximate it, typically with the empirical
LSTD solution (
7
). A second, more daunting,
challenge is the computation of the Bellman residual at each round.

In the Krylov–Bellman boosting procedure, we assume access to a
mechanism that provides data of the form
(x_{i},r_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})
generated according to the sampling
model (
4
), with which we estimate the desired
quantities. As described in Section
2.2
, a dataset of the
form
{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1118872}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1118872}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{983636}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{983634}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle LSTD$}}}}}}=\{(x_{j},r_{j},x^{\prime}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}

used to compute the empirical LSTD
estimate (

estimate

of the value function, consider the problem of
estimating the Bellman residual

{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788525}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788524}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle REG$}}}}}}=\{(x_{i},r_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

3.2
Convergence guarantees

We now provide some theoretical guarantees for the Krylov–Bellman
Boosting procedure. Our first result (
Theorem

1
) is of a
general nature: it allows arbitrary procedures to be used in computing
the LSTD solutions and residual fits at intermediate stages of the
algorithm. In fact, while we have described the KBB residual fits in
terms of non-parametric regression,
Theorem

1
actually applies
to other procedures that might be used for this task. (For instance,
if the model dynamics were known, one could use numerical procedures
to approximate the Bellman update.) Our second main result
(
Theorem

2
) is more specific in nature, in that it
provides bounds on the error in the LSTD and residual fitting steps
induced by using empirical samples, as used in our introduction of the
procedure.

For ease of notation, define the discount operator
\mathcal{Q}\coloneq\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}
. In the analysis given here, we assume that
the Markov chain is reversible.
1
1

1

Although our theory exploits
reversibility, it does not seem essential to the practical behavior of
the algorithm itself, as shown by the simulations to follow in the

self-adjoint operator on

\displaystyle\langle f,\,g\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}\coloneq\langle f,\,\mathcal{Q}g\rangle_{\mu},\quad\mbox{along with

(12)

Under certain regularity conditions, it can be shown

satisfies the relation

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\langle f,\,\mathcal{Q}f\rangle_{\mu}\leq(1+\gamma)\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}\qquad\text{for all $f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)$.}

(13)

In addition, the operator
\mathcal{Q}

{\epsilon}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}
-accurate LSTD implementation (cf.
LE

(

ϵ
LSTD

t

)

). Then
at each step
t=1,2,\ldots
, the error satisfies the
\mathcal{Q}
-norm
bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t+1}\|V_{t+1}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\leq\left(1-\tfrac{m_{t}^{2}}{8M_{t}}\right)\|V_{t}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}+\tfrac{10}{M_{t}}\cdot\delta_{t}^{2}+\tfrac{8M_{t}}{m_{t}^{2}}\cdot({\epsilon}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}_{t})^{2}.

See Section
5.1
for the proof.

Note that the function
t\mapsto\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{8M_{t}}
is
non-decreasing in
t
by definition, so convergence is at least
geometric, and can be faster if the ratio

\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{8M_{t}}
is growing quickly. Using the fact
that
m_{t}\geq 1-\gamma
and
M_{t}\leq 1+\gamma
, we have the contraction factor is at least
1-\frac{m_{t}^{2}}{8M_{t}}\leq 1-\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{8(1+\gamma)}
at every iteration. However
in the next section we illustrate that the convergence can be much
faster than this worst case behavior. In addition, we can control the
error terms
\delta_{t}^{2}
and
({\epsilon}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}}_{t})^{2}
by the number of
samples,
n_{t}
and
m_{t}
, used in the regression
procedure and LSTD estimate, respectively.

Some intuition:

Roughly speaking,
the idea underlying the proof is that one round of the KBB algorithm
is at least as good as a gradient-type update that is
restricted
to the subspace

\mathscr{C}_{t}^{\perp}
. For this reason, the
algorithm depends on the restricted spectral
values (
14
) over this subspace, which determine the
conditioning of the effective problem at each round.

In more detail, suppose both the LSTD and regression procedures are
exact, and consider the problem of minimizing the functional

\mathcal{F}(V)\coloneq\langle V-V^{\star},\,\mathcal{Q}(V-V^{\star})\rangle_{\mu}
. It has (Frechet) derivative
\nabla\mathcal{F}(V)=\mathcal{Q}\big(V-V^{\star}\big)
, and
moreover, since the operator
\mathcal{Q}
is positive and self-adjoint, this
is a convex optimization problem which achieves its (unique) minimum at
the true value function
V^{\star}
. Using these properties along
with conditions defining LSTD optimality, we can prove
(cf.
Lemma

1
) that, for any choice of stepsize

\alpha>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|V_{t+1}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\leq\|V_{t}-\alpha\mathcal{Q}(V_{t}-V^{\star})-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\qquad

Using the fact that
\mathcal{Q}(V_{t}-V^{\star})=\mathcal{B}(V_{t})
and then expanding the square yields

\displaystyle\|V_{t}-\alpha\mathcal{B}(V_{t})-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|V_{t}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}-2\alpha\langle V_{t}-V^{\star},\,\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}+\alpha^{2}\|\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\|_{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\|V_{t}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}-2\alpha\|\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\|\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2},

where step (i) makes use of the equivalence
\mathcal{Q}(V_{t}-V^{\star})=\mathcal{B}(V_{t})
. Since
V_{t}
is the LSTD
fit over
\mathscr{S}_{t}
, the optimality conditions for the projected
fixed point equation (
5
) ensure that

\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\perp\mathscr{S}_{t}
, or equivalently

\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\in\mathscr{S}_{t}^{\perp}
. As an important
consequence, we can control
\|\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\|_{\mu}
and

\|\mathcal{B}(V_{t})\|_{\mathcal{Q}}
in terms of the restricted spectral
values (
14
). The full proof in
Section

5.1

follows this outline.

3.3
Regression bounds and consequences for KBB

Here we provide some results on the regression procedure in
Algorithm
KBB
for completeness. Given data points
{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788525}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788524}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle REG$}}}}}}=\{(x_{i},r_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we can write the
regression problem in the generative form as
y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+w_{i}

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, where

\displaystyle f^{*}(x)=V(x)-\mathcal{T}V(x),\qquad\text{and}\qquad w_{i}=\gamma\left(\mathcal{P}V(x_{i})-V(x_{i}^{\prime})\right).

(15)

Here we have used the fact that the rewards are deterministic. It
should be noted that the “noise” variables
w_{i}
are actually
dependent on the states
x_{i}
, so some care is required in
the analysis.

We can write our regression procedure as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathpzc{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}.

Note that it need
not
be the case that
f^{*}\in\mathpzc{F}
.
In order to allow for the possibility of such mis-specification, we
define the
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
-projection
\Pi{F}
from

L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
onto
\mathpzc{F}
, which is well-defined when
\mathpzc{F}
is
closed and convex.

Our results involve a non-asymptotic upper bound on
\|\widehat{f}-\mathcal{B}(V)\|_{\mu}
; it is easy to convert it to a

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}}
-guarantee via
Lemma

7
at the cost of a

(1+\gamma)
- factor. Additionally, it involves a

statistical estimation error

\beta_{n}
that depends on the
function class
\mathpzc{F}
. This quantity decreases to zero as the
sample size
n
increases, but the rate of decrease depends on
the complexity of the function class
\mathpzc{F}
;
see
Section

3.4
for its precise definition, and
associated details.

Theorem 2

(Regression bound)
.

For a given function
V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, suppose that we
compute an estimate
\widehat{f}
of the Bellman residual

\mathcal{B}(V)
via the regression
procedure (
10
) over a
b
-bounded and convex
function class
\mathpzc{F}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{f}-\mathcal{B}(V)\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq c\Big\{\beta_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\left(b^{2}+\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)+\|\Pi{F}(\mathcal{B}(V))-\mathcal{B}(V)\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big\},

(16)

where the statistical estimation error
\beta_{n}^{2}
depends on
the complexity of
\mathpzc{F}
.

See Section
5.4
for the proof.

Note that our regression procedures depends on
\beta_{n}^{2}
, as
well as the approximation error

\|\Pi{F}(\mathcal{B}(V))-\mathcal{B}(V)\|_{\mu}^{2}
; such dependence is to be expected since we
are fitting
\widehat{f}
in some function class
\mathpzc{F}
that does not
necessarily contain
\mathcal{B}(V)
.

Combining
Theorems

1
and
2
yields the
following “end-to-end” guarantee on the KBB algorithm:

Corollary 1

(End-to-end guarantee for KBB)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorems

1
and
2
, there are
universal constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that the KBB update satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{t+1}\|V_{t+1}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\leq\left(1-\tfrac{m_{t}^{2}}{8M_{t}}\right)\|V_{t}-V^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}+\tfrac{c_{2}M_{t}}{m_{t}^{2}}\cdot({\epsilon}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1011348}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895128}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{895126}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LSTD}$}}}}}})^{2}\\
+\frac{c_{1}}{M_{t}}\Big\{\beta_{n}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{n}\left(b^{2}+\|V_{t}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)+\|\Pi{F}(\mathcal{B}V_{t})-\mathcal{B}V_{t}\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big\},

where
n=n_{t}=|{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788525}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788524}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle REG$}}}}}}_{t}|
.

3.4
Estimation error via localized complexities

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the statistical
estimation error
\beta_{n}^{2}
that appears
in
Theorem

2
.

3.4.1
Complexity measures and critical radii

The statistical estimation error can be decomposed as the sum

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{2}=\omega_{n}^{2}+\varepsilon_{n}^{2},

(17)

where each of the two quantities
(\omega_{n},\varepsilon_{n})
arise
from localized complexity measures, in particular sub-Gaussian or
Rademacher complexities, that play a central role in empirical process
theory
[
60
,
4
,
62
]
. When fitting
the residual
f^{*}=\mathcal{B}(V)
, the relevant set is the

shifted function class

\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}=\{f-\tilde{f}\mid f\in\mathpzc{F}\}
, where
\tilde{f}\coloneq\Pi{F}(f^{*})
denotes the

\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
-projection of
f^{*}
onto
\mathpzc{F}
.

In the non-parametric fit of the Bellman residual, there are two
sources of “noise”. The first is the Bellman noise, and it leads to
the
sub-Gaussian complexity
at scale
\delta>0
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{g\,\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}g(x_{i})\right|\right].

(18)

Here the
x_{i}
is generated i.i.d. from the stationary
distribution
\mu
, whereas the noise
w_{i}
is generated
according to the model (
15
).

The second form of “noise” has to do with the discrepancy betwen the
empirical norm
\|f\|_{n}\coloneq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(x_{i})}
and the population norm
\|f\|_{\mu}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(X)]}
. In order to prove a uniform bound on this
discrepancy, we make use of the
local Rademacher complexity

of
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
at scale
\delta>0
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{g\,\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\left|\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g(x_{i})\right|\right].

(19)

Here
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. samples from the
stationary distribution
\mu
, and
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables taking values in
\{-1,+1\}

with equal probabilities, independent of
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

The two quantities
\varepsilon_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
in the
decomposition (
17
) are both quantities known as

critical radii
in empirical process theory. They are defined,
respectively as the smallest positive solutions to the inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{G}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n},\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})}{\varepsilon_{n}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}(\omega_{n};\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})}{\omega_{n}}\leq\frac{\omega_{n}}{b}.

(20)

As long as the function class
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
is convex (as assumed
here), it can be shown that both of the functions
\delta\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)}{\delta}
and
\delta\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}(\delta)}{\delta}
are non-increasing on the
positive real line, and so the critical radii always exist. We refer
the reader to Chapters 13 and 14 of the book
[
62
]
for
more details on critical radii, and the arguments used to establish
claims of this type.

3.4.2
Examples of statistical error

Let us illustrate this general set-up by describing the form of the
critical radii for several different function classes. For
simplicity, we consider classes with
\|V\|_{\infty}=b=1
. Here we summarize the form of the critical radii;
see
Appendix

B
for the computations that
underlie these conclusions.

Polynomials of degree
d
:

As our first example,
suppose that
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
is the family of
d
-degree
polynomials defined on
\mathcal{X}=[-1,1]
uniformly bounded by

1
. For a given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, define

f_{\theta}(x)\coloneq\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}x+\ldots+\theta_{d+1}x^{d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}\coloneq\{f_{\theta}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}\text{ for some
$\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ such that $\|f_{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leq 1$ }\}.

Some straightforward calculations yield
\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d+1}{n}
, and
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d+1}{n}
, so we can conclude

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d+1}{n}.

Radial basis kernels:

Consider
\mathcal{X}=[-1,1]

and the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathbb{H}
given by
the Gaussian kernel
\mathpzc{K}(x,z)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-z)^{2}}

defined on the Cartesian product space
[-1,1]\times[-1,1]
.
Consider the unit ball in this RKHS, given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}=\{f\in\mathbb{H}\mid\big\|f\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1,\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}.

For this example, it can be shown that
\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}
, and hence

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}.

This is a simple example of a non-parametric class, since the
effective degrees of freedom (
\log(n+1)
in this case) grows
as a function of the sample size.

Convex Lipschitz functions:

We let
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]

and
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
be the class of convex
1
-Lipschitz functions,
i.e.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}\coloneq\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\mid f(0)=0,\text{ and $f$
is convex and $1$-Lipschitz}\}.

We can show
\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{4/5}}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{4/5}}
, giving

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{4/5}}.

4
Numerical results

We illustrate the behavior of our algorithm in a mixture of idealized
and semi-realistic settings.
Section

4.1
highlights the
behavior of our algorithm in the tabular setting, where
\mathcal{X}
is
finite, and
Section

4.2
illustrates the performance in the
linear-quadratic setting, where
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but the
transitions and costs have simple
representations.
Section

4.3
demonstrates the behavior
of our algorithm for a nonlinear system with more complicated
dynamics.
Section

4.4
presents our algorithm on another nonlinear system with
dynamics, the ARCH model used in econometrics with quadratic cost.

We compare our algorithm to value iteration and fitted value
iteration. Recall that the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
is

\gamma
-contractive with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
, so we can
iteratively apply
\mathcal{T}
and ensure convergence to the true
value function
V^{\star}
; this is the value iteration algorithm.
The fitted value iteration (FVI) algorithm applies when the Bellman
update
cannot
be computed
\mathcal{T}(V)
exactly, and
must be approximated. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},r_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a function class
\mathpzc{F}
, we
compute an approximation via

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{T}(V)}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathpzc{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[r_{i}+\gamma V(x_{i}^{\prime})-f(x_{i})\big]^{2}\right\}.

In fitted value iteration, we iterate repeating this regression
procedure for every step (potentially with a different
dataset). Algorithm
KBB
, in each iteration, computes the Bellman
residual
\mathcal{B}(V_{t})=V_{t}-\mathcal{T}V_{t}
,
which involves computing the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}V_{t}
.
Accordingly, we compare each iteration to one iteration of value
iteration and fitted value iteration, using the same regression
procedure and number of samples. In our implementations of
Algorithm
KBB
, we use
{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1118872}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1118872}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{983636}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle LSTD$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{983634}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle LSTD$}}}}}}_{t}={\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788525}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788524}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle REG$}}}}}}_{t}
—that is, the
same samples used in fitting the regression procedure are re-used for
the LSTD fit. When running the algorithm, we have observed that
ensuring the first regression is very accurate results in much better
convergence. In the first step, Krylov-Bellman is estimating the
reward function
R
. It is likely essential to an accurate
estimate of the reward function since it corresponds to the right-hand
side of the linear operator equation
\mathcal{Q}V=R
.

4.1
Tabular MRPs

In a tabular MRP, the state space
\mathcal{X}
is finite; for ease of
notation we represent it as
\mathcal{X}=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
where
d=|\mathcal{X}|
. We can then represent all
quantities of interest as either a matrix or a vector: the transition
operator
\mathcal{P}
can be written as a matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|}
and the reward function
R

can be identified with a vector in
R\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
via

\displaystyle R_{x}\coloneq R(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{P}_{x,x^{\prime}}=\mathcal{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x)\qquad\text{for all
$x\in\mathcal{X}$},

where
\mathcal{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
is the probability of transition
to
x^{\prime}
from
x
. Similarly we can represent a value
function
V
as an equivalent vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
. The
Bellman operator can then be written in the matrix form as
\mathcal{T}V=R+\gamma\mathbf{P}V
. In this setting
we perform the regression procedure over the function class
\mathpzc{F}=\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
, and the regression is equivalent to computing the
sample average for every state.

4.2
Linear Quadratic Regulator

The linear quadratic regulator (LQR) is a canonical model in optimal
control; it plays a central role in many applications including
aeronautics, robotics and industrial process control
[
8
,
29
]
. Here we consider a particular variant of the LQR
problem known as the linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG) problem. The
state space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and action space
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{v}
are continuous, and the transition dynamics starting
from state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and taking action
u\in\mathbb{R}^{v}
is given by

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\mathbf{A}x_{t}+\mathbf{B}u_{t}+W_{t},

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the transition
matrix,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times v}
is the action matrix,
and
w_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma})
. We formulate this problem in
terms of a cost function, rather than a reward function (although for
the purposes of policy evaluation it is a meaningless
distinction). Given symmetric positive semidefinite matrices

\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times v}
, the cost function at each stage is

\displaystyle\psi(x,u)=x^{T}\mathbf{Q}x+u^{T}\mathbf{R}u.

We restrict our attention to policies of the form
u_{t}=\mathbf{K}x_{t}
where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times d}
. We then have a Markov reward process with dynamics and
costs given by

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}\big)x_{t}+w_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}\big(\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{K}^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}\big)x_{t}.

Assume that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}
are
contained within the unit circle on the complex plane. The goal is to
solve for the value function
V^{\star}
of this MRP. The value
function can be written as

\displaystyle V^{\star}(x)=x^{T}\mathbf{P}^{*}x+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\cdot\text{trace}(\mathbf{P}^{*}\mathbf{\Sigma}),

where the matrix
\mathbf{P}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the
solution to the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{*}=\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{K}^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}+\gamma\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}\big)^{T}\mathbf{P}^{*}\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}\big).

Value iteration involves computing the update

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{t+1}=\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{K}^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}+\gamma\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}\big)^{T}\mathbf{P}_{t}\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{t+1}=\gamma c_{t}+\gamma\text{trace}(\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{\Sigma}).

The pair
(\mathbf{P}_{y},c_{t})
define the value function via

V_{t}(x)=x^{T}\mathbf{P}_{t}x+c_{t}
.
When
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}
has eigenvalues contained
within the unit circle, this update is guaranteed to be a contraction.

In the numerical simulations, we choose
d=5
and
v=3
. The matrices
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{K}
are chosen
to have i.i.d.
\text{Unif}\,(0,1)
entries, and then rescaled to
ensure that the eigenvalues are contained within the unit circle. The
symmetric matrices
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{R}
are computed by
generating matrices with i.i.d.
\text{Unif}\,(0,1)
random variables
and then left-multiplied by its transpose to ensure symmetry. The
regression steps in both fitted value iteration and Krylov–Bellman
Boosting use the XGBoost routine
[
16
]
to perform the
fitting. Result are given in Figure
5
. As before, we
observe a geometric (or linear) convergence rate for value iteration,
consistent with the
\gamma
-contractive nature of the update. We
also see approximately linear convergence of fitted value iteration,
albeit at a slower rate compared to value iteration. Krylov–Bellman
Boosting is again more rapidly convergent than these two procedures.
(We note that KBB hits an error floor due to the statistical sampling
involved, whereas exact value iteration, which is based on unrealistic
knowledge of the true dynamics, does not involve any such error.) For
a larger discount factor
\gamma=0.99
, the gains afforded by
the KBB procedure become more significant.

4.3
A non-linear example

Consider the nonlinear system in the
3
-dimensional state variable

x_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t,1}&x_{t,2}&x_{t,3}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{3}
and control vector

given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{t+1,1}-x_{t+1,2}^{2}\\

x_{t,2}\\
x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{B}u_{t}+w_{t}

\displaystyle u_{t}=\mathbf{K}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t,1}-x_{t,2}^{2}\\
x_{t,2}\\
x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mbox{and}\quad c_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t,1}-x_{t,2}^{2}\\

\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{K}

three-dimensional matrices, with each of

nonlinear, it is straightforward to perform exact value iteration via
a change of coordinates (see the book
[
27

we can verify the system has the following state space

\displaystyle x_{t+1,1}

\displaystyle=x_{t,2}+\left[x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}+u_{t,1}\right]^{2},

\displaystyle x_{t+1,2}

\displaystyle=x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}+u_{t,1},\qquad\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle x_{t+1,3}

\displaystyle=x_{t,1}+2x_{t,2}\left[x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}+u_{t,1}\right]^{2}+\left[x_{t,3}-x_{t,1}^{2}+u_{t,1}\right]^{4}.

4.4
ARCH

For the final example, we consider the autoregressive conditional
heteroskedasticity model (ARCH)
[
21
,
10
]
. The state vectors
X_{t}
evolve according
to the non-linear dynamics

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\mathbf{A}X_{t}+\sqrt{q+X_{t}\mathbf{Q}X_{t}}\cdot W_{t}

where
q\geq 0
is a scalar, the matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is positive semidefinite, and
W_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma})
is system noise. We turn this into a Markov reward process
by assigning a cost function
\psi(x)=x^{T}\mathbf{R}x
and adding discounting. The value function
V^{\star}

is the solution to the Bellman relation

\displaystyle V(x)=\mathcal{T}(V)(x)\coloneq x^{T}\mathbf{R}x+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[V(\mathbf{A}x+\sqrt{q+x^{T}\mathbf{Q}x}\cdot W)\right].

Under some regularity conditions on the triple
(q,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R})
, the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
is

\gamma
-contractive under the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
.
Consequently, we can solve for the value function via value iteration
in the same manner as previous settings. A straightforward calculation
yields

\displaystyle V^{\star}(x)=x^{T}\mathbf{P}^{*}x+\frac{\gamma q}{1-\gamma}\cdot\text{trace}(\mathbf{P}^{*}\mathbf{\Sigma})

where the matrix
\mathbf{P}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
solves the linear
system

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{*}=\mathbf{R}+\gamma\left\{\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{P}^{*}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{Q}\cdot\text{trace}(\mathbf{P}^{*}\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\}.

In our numerical simulations we choose
d=5
, and
q=0.5
. The
matrix
\mathbf{A}
is chosen to have i.i.d. Unif(0, 1) entries, and
then rescaled to ensure that the system is stable. The matrix

\mathbf{Q}
is computed by generating a matrix with
i.i.d.
\mbox{Unif}(0,1)
random variables, and then left-multiplying
by its transpose to ensure symmetry. The results are plotted in
Figure
7
. The behavior in this setting seems to be
the same as what we observed in the past few settings. Initially,
Krylov–Bellman Boosting is much faster than both fitted value
iteration and value iteration. In the setting of
\gamma=0.9
,
Krylov Bellman Boosting hits an error floor relatively early, and it
takes value iteration and fitted value iteration quite a while to
catch up. For
\gamma=0.99
, we observe a similar behavior,
but both value iteration and fitted value iteration take significantly
longer to match Krylov–Bellman Boosting in accuracy.

of

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{n}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{g\,\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\,\|g\|_{n}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}g(x_{i})\right|\bigm|\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right].

Here the samples

correspond to those in
regression data set
{\mathscr{D}}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{889129}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788525}{229375}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle REG$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{788524}{229376}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle REG$}}}}}}

and directly write

\mathbb{E}_{w}\coloneq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}]
in the
paper occasionally for convenience.

Relative to the population-level sub-Gaussian complexity defined
previously (
18
) (in which we take expectations
over state variables), the complexity function
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
treats
the data
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed where
\mathcal{G}

does not. In analogy to the critical radii (

defined at the population level, we also define a critical radius at
using
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
: more specifically, let
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}>0
be
the smallest positive solution to the inequality

\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})}{\delta}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
. Finally, recall that the dataset
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
defines the empirical norm
\|f\|_{n}^{2}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(x_{i})
.

With this set-up, our proof is broken into three main steps:

(i)

We control the empirical error
\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}

in terms of empirical sub-Gaussian critical radius
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}

and the approximation error
\|\tilde{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
, as stated
in

2
.

(ii)

We establish that
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}
is controlled by the
population critical radii
\varepsilon_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
, given
by
Lemma

3
.

(iii)

We use the critical radius
\omega_{n}
to establish
uniform control and connect
\|\cdot\|_{n}
with

\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
(cf.
Lemma

4
).

We begin by stating several technical lemmas. Recall that
\omega_{n}>0
and
\varepsilon_{n}>0
satisfies the critical inequalities
\mathcal{R}(\omega_{n};\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\leq\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{b}
and
\mathcal{G}(\varepsilon_{n};\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{2}
, respectively. The statement of this lemma treats
the observations
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed, and provides
control of
\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}
over the noise in
w_{i}

conditional on
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
t\geq\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}\leq c_{1}t\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}+c_{2}\|\tilde{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

with probability exceeding
1-e^{-c_{3}n\cdot\frac{t\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}}{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}}}
.

See
Section

5.5.1
for the proof.

In
Lemma

2
, we have a guarantee in terms of the
empirical critical radius
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}
, which is a random variable
dependent on
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
. The
following lemma controls the expectation of
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2}
.

Lemma 3
.

The empirical sub-Gaussian complexity
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}
has expectation
bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2}\right]\leq c_{1}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}+c_{2}\,\omega_{n}^{2}+\frac{c_{3}}{n}\left(b^{2}+\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)

for universal constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
.

See
Section

5.5.2
for a proof of

The next lemma allows us to convert our empirical norm guarantee to

in terms of
\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}

uniformly for all

; we require a uniform version

\widehat{f}
is a random quantity.

Lemma 4
.

For any
t\geq\omega_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}-\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\,\qquad\text{for
all $f\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}$}

(30)

with probability exceeding
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\frac{nt^{2}}{b^{2}}}
.

See Theorem 14.1 in the reference
[
62
]
for
the proof.

Note for an arbitrary real-valued random variable
Z
, the
inequality

allows us to write
\mathbb{E}[Z]\leq\mathbb{E}[\max\{Z,0\}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z\geq u)\,du
, so we can
upper bound
\mathbb{E}[Z]
by integrating an upper tail bound.

Let us apply this line of reasoning to the random variable

\displaystyle Z\coloneq\big|\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}-\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2}\big|-\frac{1}{2}\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2},

using the tail bound established in

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[Z]\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z\geq u)\,du&\leq\frac{1}{2}\omega_{n}^{2}+\int_{\omega_{n}^{2}/2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z\geq u)\,du\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\omega_{n}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}c_{1}e^{-2c_{2}\frac{nu}{b^{2}}}\,du\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{n}^{2}+\frac{c_{1}}{2c_{2}}\cdot\frac{b^{2}}{n}.\end{split}

(31)

Additionally, observe that
Lemma

2
treats
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed and the high probability is over
the randomness with respect to

conditional on
\{x_{i}\}
. Thus, integrating the tail bound again yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}\right]\leq c^{\prime}\Big\{\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2}+\frac{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{n}+\|\tilde{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\Big\}

for some universal constant

. Taking expectations over

then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq c^{\prime}\Big\{\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{n}+\|\tilde{f}-f^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big\}.

(32)

Finally,
combining
equations

31
and
32
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq c^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2}\right]+\omega_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\left(b^{2}+\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)+\|\tilde{f}-f^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big\}.

The theorem then follows from

Proof of

We begin with some straightforward algebraic manipulations. By
expanding the square, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)(\tilde{y}_{i}-f(x_{i}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2},

where we define
\tilde{y}_{i}\coloneq\tilde{f}(x_{i})+w_{i}
. Since the
rightmost term does not involve
f
, we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathpzc{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)(\tilde{y}_{i}-f(x_{i}))\right\}.

By optimality of
\widehat{f}
and feasibility of
\tilde{f}
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)(\tilde{y}_{i}-\widehat{f}(x_{i}))\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)(\tilde{y}_{i}-\tilde{f}(x_{i})).

Rearranging terms yields the

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2}&\leq T_{1}+T_{2},\end{split}

We bound each of

and

Bounding

:

By applying the Fenchel-Young

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{*}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}(x_{i})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f^{*}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\widehat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{n}^{2}.

Bounding
T_{1}
:

For this part, we condition on

,
i.e., we treat them as fixed and given to us. We make use of techniques for controlling
localized empirical processes; see Chapter 13 in the

[
62
]
for relevant background. Recall that by

satisfies the critical

For all

By

6
Discussion

In this paper, we presented and analyzed the Krylov–Bellman boosting
(KBB) procedure. It is an efficient algorithm for sample-based policy
evaluation, and applicable to general state spaces. It is built upon
the machinery of Krylov subspace methods for solving linear operator
equations. As opposed to an idealized Krylov method, our approach
allows for errors in both the LSTD steps and the fitting of the
Bellman residual, and we provide general convergence guarantees that
track the effect of such errors. On the empirical front, the KBB
algorithm typically exhibits convergence rates that are significantly
faster than the linear or geometric rate obtained by fitted value
iteration. Consistent with these empirical observations, our theory
also reveals such accelerated convergence. In particular, we show how
the KBB convergence rate is determined by the spectral properties of
the Bellman residual operator when restricted to a shrinking sequence
of subspaces. Finally, we analyzed the use of non-parametric
regression routines for fitting the Bellman residual. We proved a
general theorem that bounds the residual fitting error in terms of a
combination of approximation error, and statistical estimation error.

There exist many interesting avenues for future work. While the
current theory applies to reversible Markov chains, the method itself
exhibits rapid convergence for general Markov reward processes. It
would be interesting to close this gap between theory and practice by
developing theory applicable to non-reversible Markov chains. It is
also interesting to draw connections to related problems in
reinforcement learning and under different sampling models For
instance, can we fit the Bellman residuals using offline data, and
still provide reasonable guarantees? How do updates of the
Krylov–Bellman type interact with actor-critic style algorithms?

.

For any separable Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, the operator
(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})
is self-adjoint, invertible, and satisfies the
bounds

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\langle f,\,(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})f\rangle_{\mu}\leq(1+\gamma)\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}\qquad\text{for all $f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)$.}

(42)

The requirements for
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
to be separable is quite mild; in the
case where
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some dimension
d
, one sufficient
condition is that
\mu
is a continuous distribution.

Proof.

By definition of the stationary measure, the operator
\mathcal{P}

satisfies the bound
\|\mathcal{P}V\|_{\mu}\leq\|V\|_{\mu}
.
Thus, by classical functional analysis (e.g., see Chap. 8 in the
reference
[
32
]
), the inverse
(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})^{-1}
exists, and can be represented via the
Neumann series expansion

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{j}\mathcal{P}^{j}.

The other statements follow from using linearity of the inner product,
and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz in conjunction with the operator norm
bound of
\mathcal{P}
.

Since
\mathcal{Q}\coloneq\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}
is a positive
operator, it has a unique square root
(\mathcal{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P})^{1/2}
. In addition, since
\mathcal{Q}
is invertible, so is its
square root (which is also self-adjoint).
∎

A.2
Proof of
Lemma

5

This argument is inspired by the proof of Lemma 13.12 in the
book
[
62
]
, along with some new ingredients. We treat
the observations
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed here. Recall the definition of the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=\left\{\text{ $\exists\,g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}$ with
$\|g\|_{n}\geq u$ such that $\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|\geq 2\|g\|_{n}u$}\right\}.

Define
the empirical process

\displaystyle Z_{n}(u)\coloneq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\\
\|g\|_{n}\leq u\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|.

We claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(u)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(u)\geq 2u^{2}).

In order to establish this claim, suppose that there exists
g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
with
\|g\|_{n}\geq u
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|\geq 2\|g\|_{n}u.

Define the rescaled function
\tilde{g}=\frac{u}{\|g\|_{n}}g
;
observe
\tilde{g}\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
by convexity. We then have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,\tilde{g}(x_{i})\right|=\frac{u}{\|g\|_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|\geq 2u^{2},

as desired.

It remains to control the probability
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(u)\geq 2u^{2})
. If
we view
Z_{n}(u)
as a function of
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, it can then be seen that it has Lipschitz constant
of
\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}}
in the Euclidean norm and is separately
convex. Since
|w_{i}|\leq 2\|V\|_{\infty}
, we have by
Theorem 3.4 in the book
[
62
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(u)\geq\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}(u)]+s)\leq e^{-cn\cdot\frac{s^{2}}{u^{2}\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}}}.

Observe by definition that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}(u)]=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(u)
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(u)}{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n})}{\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}}{2}\leq\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n},

where step (i) uses Lemma 13.6 in the book
[
62
]
and the
fact that
u\geq\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}
, and step (ii) uses the critical
inequality (
20
). Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(u)\geq 2u^{2})\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(u)\geq u\widehat{\varepsilon}_{n}+u^{2})\leq e^{-cn\cdot\frac{u^{2}}{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}}},

as desired.

A.3
Proof of Lemma
6

To begin, we observe that the claim (
41
)
follows by applying
Lemma

4
, along with the fact that
u\geq\tfrac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n}}
implies that
\sqrt{u\varepsilon_{n}}\geq\omega_{n}
. In order to prove the
claim (
40
), assume that
u\geq\varepsilon_{n}
,
and define the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t)\coloneq\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq t}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|.

By the definition (
37
) of
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)
, we
have the equivalence

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[Z_{n}(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}(t)\mid\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}],

and
\mathbb{E}\left[\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}(t)]=\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
. The moment generating function of

\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.43501pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[5.68752pt]{4.27866pt}{0.3014pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t))}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\mathbb{E}_{w}[Z_{n}(t)]-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t))}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[e^{\lambda(Z_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t))}\right]\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(Z_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t))}\right],

where the inequality follows from a (conditional) Jensen’s
inequality. Consequently, we can control
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
by controlling
Z_{n}(t)-\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
using a Chernoff-type argument. We prove an
upper tail bound on
Z_{n}(t)
via Talagrand
concentration
[
56
]
which uses a Chernoff bound
in its proof; see Theorem 3.27 in the book
[
62
]
for
further details.

Define the random variable
\Sigma^{2}=\sup_{f\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|f\|_{\mu}\leq t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}f(x_{i})^{2}
. We then have by Talagrand concentration

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(t)\geq\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)+s)\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\cdot\frac{s^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\Sigma^{2}]+bs}\right)

where
c
is a universal constant. By Equation (3.84) from the book
[
62
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Sigma^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|f\|_{\mu}\leq t}\mathbb{E}[w^{2}f^{2}(x)]+4b\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq 4\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}\,t^{2}+4b\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{n}(t),

where we have used the fact
|w|\leq 2\|V\|_{\infty}
a.s. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{5.21706pt}{0.43057pt}}{Z}_{n}(9\sqrt{u\varepsilon_{n}})\geq\mathcal{G}_{n}(9\sqrt{u\varepsilon_{n}})+\frac{9u\varepsilon_{n}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\cdot\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}u\varepsilon_{n}+b\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{n}(9\sqrt{u\varepsilon_{n}})+b\|V\|_{\infty}u\varepsilon_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}2\exp\left(-c^{\prime}n\cdot\frac{u\varepsilon_{n}}{\|V\|_{\infty}^{2}+b\|V\|_{\infty}}\right),

where step (i) follows from the fact that
\mathcal{G}(9\sqrt{u\varepsilon_{n}})\leq\frac{9}{2}u\varepsilon_{n}
for
u\geq\varepsilon_{n}
.

Appendix B
Calculations for

In this section we provide the details behind the calculations in
Section

3.4
. Before proceeding to the computations, we have the following result relating the sub-Gaussian complexity
\mathcal{G}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})
and the Rademacher complexity
\mathcal{R}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})

Lemma 8
.

We have for some universal constant
c
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\leq c\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathcal{R}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}).

This lemma allows us to instantly obtain a bound for the sub-Gaussian complexity after computing the Rademacher complexity of the function class
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
. In
Section

3.4
, we assume
\|V\|_{\infty}=1
; consequently
\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
.

Proof.

We have by the tower property,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}g(x_{i})\right|\right]\right].

We can then bound, letting
\varepsilon_{i}
be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables independent of
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w}\big[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}g(x_{i})\big|\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}2\mathbb{E}_{w,\varepsilon}\big[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}g(x_{i})\big|\big]

\displaystyle=4\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{w,\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\frac{w_{i}g(x_{i})}{2\|V\|_{\infty}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}8\|V\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g(x_{i})\big|\big],

where step (a) follows from symmetrization (cf. Chapter 4 in the reference
[
62
]
) and step (b) follows from the bound
|w_{i}|\leq 2\|V\|_{\infty}
and the Ledoux-Talagrand contraction inequality
[
36
]
.

∎

B.1
Polynomials of degree
d

To simplify the calculations, let
\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{d}
be an
orthonormal basis of
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
with respect to

\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
. This basis exists via applying
Gram-Schmidt to the basis
\{1,x,\ldots,x^{d}\}
. We can then write
any polynomial function in
\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}}
as
f_{b}(x)=b_{0}\phi_{0}(x)+b_{1}\phi_{1}(x)+\ldots+b_{d}\phi_{d}(x)
, which satisfies
\|f_{b}\|_{\mu}=\|b\|_{2}
. Define the matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}
with
\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\phi_{j}(x_{i})
. We then have, letting (with mild abuse of
notation)
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f_{b}\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|f_{b}\|_{\mu}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f_{b}(x_{i})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\|b\|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M}b\right|\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{\delta}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M}\|_{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M}\|_{2}^{2}]},

where step (a) follows from the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is a dual norm to itself, and step (b) follows from Jensen’s inequality.

Manipulating further, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\text{trace}(\mathbf{M}^{T}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M})]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{trace}(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\varepsilon\varepsilon^{T}]\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{T})\right],

where we used the fact
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\cdot]\coloneq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}]
and
\mathbf{M}
is a function of
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon^{T}\mathbf{M}\|_{2}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[\text{trace}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{T})]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}(d+1)n,

where the equality (a) follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\varepsilon^{T}]=\mathbf{I}
, and the equality (b) follows from the fact that
\{\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{d}\}
is orthonormal. To identify the critical radius, we compute using the definition (
20
),

\displaystyle\frac{4\delta\sqrt{d+1}}{\delta\sqrt{n}}\leq\delta\implies\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d+1}{n}.

By
Lemma

8
, we have
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d+1}{n}
.

B.2
Radial basis kernels

By Mercer’s theorem (see Chapter 12 in the book
[
62
]
), we have the kernel integral operator
T{K}(f)(x)\coloneq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathpzc{K}(x,z)f(z)\,d\mu(z)
has a set of eigenvalues
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Then we have (cf. Corollary 14.5
[
62
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(\delta;\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\min\{\mu_{j},\delta^{2}\}}.

Since a reversible Markov chain has a uniform stationary distribution, the eigenvalues of
T{K}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}j\log j}\qquad\text{for $j=1,2,\ldots$}

for some universal constants
c_{0},c_{1}
(see Chapter 12 in the reference
[
62
]
).

Let
k
be the smallest positive integer such that
c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k\log k}\leq\delta^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\min\{\mu_{j},\delta^{2}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\min\{\delta^{2},c_{0}e^{-c_{1}j\log j}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{k\delta^{2}+c_{0}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}e^{-c_{1}j\log j}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{k\delta^{2}+c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k\log k}}\leq c_{0}\sqrt{k\delta^{2}},

where
c_{0},c_{1}
denote universal constants whose values may change from line to line. Some algebra then shows
\omega_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}
, as claimed.

B.3
Convex Lipschitz functions

We use results involving chaining and empirical process theory to compute the critical radii in this setting, see the references
[
62
,
60
,
57

for a full exposition. Recall the definition of the event

from
Equation

38

\omega_{n}
denote the critical radius. By an argument similar to that of the proof of
Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\mathpzc{F}},\|g\|_{\mu}\leq\omega_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,g(x_{i})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z]
