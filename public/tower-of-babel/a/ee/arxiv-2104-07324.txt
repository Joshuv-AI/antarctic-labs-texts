OneLog: Towards End-to-End Training in Software Log Anomaly Detection Conference: The 29th ACM Joint European Software Engineering Conference and Symposium on the Foundations of Software Engineering; 23 - 27 August, 2021; Athens, Greece
Shayan Hashemi, Mika Mäntylä

Abstract
. In recent years, with the growth of online services and IoT devices, software log anomaly detection has become a significant concern for both academia and industry. However, at the time of writing this paper, almost all contributions to the log anomaly detection task, follow the same traditional architecture based on parsing, vectorizing, and classifying. This paper proposes OneLog, a new approach that uses a large deep model based on instead of multiple small components. OneLog utilizes a character-based convolutional neural network (CNN) originating from traditional NLP tasks. This allows the model to take advantage of multiple datasets at once and take advantage of numbers and punctuations, which were removed in previous architectures. We evaluate OneLog using four open data sets Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS), BlueGene/L (BGL), Hadoop, and OpenStack. We evaluate our model with single and multi-project datasets. Additionally, we evaluate robustness with synthetically evolved datasets and ahead-of-time anomaly detection test that indicates capabilities to predict anomalies before occurring. To the best of our knowledge, our multi-project model outperforms state-of-the-art methods in HDFS, Hadoop, and BGL datasets, respectively setting getting F 1 F_{1} scores of 99.99, 99.99, and 99.98. However, OneLog’s performance on the Openstack is unsatisfying with F 1 F_{1} score of only 21.18. Furthermore, Onelogs performance suffers very little from noise showing F 1 F_{1} scores of 99.95, 99.92, and 99.98 in HDFS, Hadoop, and BGL. Our work demonstrates that character-level CNNs can successfully utilize multiple datasets that boost learning in log anomaly detection. Our F 1 F_{1} scores for HDFS, Hadoop, and BGL are almost too good to be true, thus upon acceptance of this paper, we offer our code for further replications. Finally, future work is needed in investigating the poor performance of the Openstack dataset.



1.
Introduction

Every instance of running software consists of a hidden state. However, there are software footprints that may indicate the hidden state of a running software instance. One of these footprints is log files. Logs are text files that include events occurring inside the software. Generally, a log event (line) indicates an event possessing multiple properties such as the event’s timestamp, process ID, thread ID, log level, logger component, and an unstructured natural language (English in most cases) log message.

This paper explores the possibility of using deep learning for detecting anomalies in logs

)
in an end-to-end fashion. Deep learning has flourished in recent years, and many software engineering studies have helped deep learning, such as testing deep learning libraries

(

(

Detecting anomalous situations has become a significant concern in software testing, debugging, and operations. In general, software operations’ role has increased due to the emergence of DevOps-oriented software development where the quality of operations is just as important as the quality of software development or testing.
Anomaly situations during operations may result in severe consequences like a loss of human life, financial losses, or reputational damages for companies.
Anomaly situations can be caused by a hardware failure, cyber attack, or software bug; for example, We think many of the damages caused by operational anomalies may be prevented if the software operators could be notified of anomalous situations.

Currently, log anomaly detection methods consisted of four components: preprocessor, parser, vectorizer, and classifier. First, the preprocessor prepares and cleans the text, but this part is given minimal emphasis in academic papers, yet more in-depth explanation may be found in
(
Sizhong Du and
Cao 2015
)
. Second, the parser extracts the event template and variable(s) from the log message. Some software engineering studies, such as
(
Zhu
et al. 2019
)
, focus on log parsing alone. Third, the vectorizer transforms the template and variables into vectors. Different types of vectorizers have been proposed, but the typical approach is to transform sequences into one-hot encoded vectors, mentioned in
(
Du
et al. 2017
)
for the first time. Some improvements have been proposed in some other works, such as
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
that proposes a new vectorization based on the semantics of pre-trained word embeddings.
Finally, the classifier, which is a machine learning or deep learning model, detects anomalous logs. Typical approaches for classification are deep learning models based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
(
Du
et al. 2017
;
Meng et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
or Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
(
Lu
et al. 2018
)
.

This paper proposes a new approach to merge the parser, vectorizer, and classifier into a single deep neural network to learn at the language level instead of separate stages, shown in Figure
1
. Our proposed method applies a character-based convolutional neural network shown to be on par with LSTM and other word-based approaches in traditional NLP tasks
(
Zhang
et al. 2015
)
. We have chosen this approach as the concept of words might be ambiguous in the context of log messages. Additionally, we can take advantage of all information in log messages, like numbers, signs, and punctuations, that might be missed with word-based approaches. This approach also reduces memory consumption in comparison to semantic word-based approaches as the number of characters is finite, which is not the same for words.

Figure 1.
An overall view of the OneLog idea.

Our experiments show that our approach not only reduces the engineering effort but also results in better accuracy. As the model learns at the language level, it is more robust to log template changes. Furthermore, the design and implementation of the one component require less engineering effort in comparison to three. Furthermore, through our approach, multiple datasets can be merged into a multi-project log dataset and used simultaneously to make a more generalized model. Moreover, transferring knowledge across datasets helps the model boost data datasets’ performance with lower data volumes.

The following list recapitulates the motivations behind the OneLog idea:

•

Less engineering effort is required as multiple components is merged into a machine learning model.

•

Large amount of data across multiple projects is available for training, boosting the model’s overall performance.

•

As more datasets are added to the multi-project dataset, the model’s knowledge for infrequent words, messages, and execution paths increases.

On the other hand, the following list presents the motivation to use a character-based model rather than a word-based one:

•

Words may take different forms in logs. For instance, the word "request" might be changed to "req" or "block" to "blk".

•

Numerical values such as request processing time may affect the decision-making of the model.

•

Punctuation marks information is kept.

•

Character-based dictionary size is crucially smaller than the Word-based vocabulary size.

•

Character-based models are frequently less complex in comparison to word-based models.

Although different approaches have been invented to address log anomaly detection, this section focuses on deep-learning-based models only as they have achieved the best performance in almost every dataset.

As one of the first deep-learning-based approaches, DeepLog
(
Du
et al. 2017
)
parses the sequences using the Spell log parsing algorithm, mentioned in
(
Du and Li 2016
)
, and uses Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
(
Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber 1997
)
to model the non-anomaly logs by predicting the next event in the sequence. After the training process, the model predicts anomaly sequences with low probability as it has converged on non-anomaly ones.

LogRobust
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
applies an attention model to Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) to classify the event sequences. However, the main contribution of this work is in the introduction of log evolution. LogRobust asserts that software logs evolve due to updates. Hence, it proposes a method for synthetically evolving log messages and sequences by adding noise. Furthermore, it introduces a new method of vectorization called “semantic vectorization”, which uses pre-trained word embeddings to construct a vector based on words’ semantic meanings in a log event.

LogAnomaly
(
Meng et al. 2019
)
also uses LSTM but presents a new vectorization technique called “template2vec’ that takes advantage of synonyms and antonyms. Additionally, LogAnomaly keeps track of message count in sequences to detect quantitive anomalies alongside the sequential ones.

In another innovation in the vectorizer component,
(
Lu
et al. 2018
)
proposes a technique to embed log keys to feature-rich vectors called “log-key2vec”. Besides, it uses Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
(
LeCun
et al. 2015
)
to classify sequences, making the model more computationally efficient in both training and inference time compared to LSTM based models.

Logsy
(
Nedelkoski et al. 2020
)
is a first paper proposing the use of Transformer
(
Devlin
et al. 2018
)
. Logsy embeds log messages into a vector space so that the non-anomaly messages congregate around the origin while anomaly messages embed with some distance from the origin. Its contributions also include a novel loss function, which makes the learning process of embedding operation possible.

Finally,
(
Hashemi and
Mäntylä 2021
)
utilizes the Siamese network
(
Bromley et al. 1994
)
with LSTM to embed log sequences into a vector space that keeps sequences of the same type (anomaly / non-anomaly) close to each other while maximizing distance from the other type. Additionally, the authors introduce other benefits that come with the Siamese network, such as more robust predictions, unsupervised evolution monitoring, and sequence visualization.

Table
1
summarizes the previous methods. All previous works almost follow the same traditional architecture with three components; parser, vectorizer, and classier.
As shown in the table, this paper proposes a new method that combines parser, vectorizer, and classier components into a single deep neural network.

HDFS:
Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) is a distributed file system that is significantly fault-tolerant and low-cost to deploy. The dataset, mentioned in
(
Xu
et al. 2009
)
and considered a benchmark in log anomaly detection domain, is produces running map-reduce tasks on more than 200 Amazon’s EC2 nodes and labeled via Hadoop domain experts. The sequences are created based on the
block\_id

Openstack:
Openstack is a cloud operating system that manages pools of hardware resources. The dataset, discussed in
(
Du
et al. 2017
)
, is generated on CloudLab, and anomaly samples are generated by injecting failures into the system. Same as
(
Du
et al. 2017
)
, we used
instance\_id
to produce our sequences. In the end, we ended up with 198 anomaly and 1872 non-anomaly sequences.

Hadoop:
Hadoop is a big data processing framework allowing distributed processing of extensive data. The dataset, stated in
(
Lin et al. 2016
)
, contains a five-machine Hadoop cluster log, each having an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-3770 CPU and 16GB of RAM. The logs are generated by running two separate applications, Word Count, which counts the number of words in the input, and Page Rank, an algorithm used by search engines. The anomalies in the dataset are machine down, network disconnection, and disk full. However, we merge these three types of anomaly into one. Furthermore, as some sequences in the dataset are too long, we produced subsequences from them using a sliding window(window size of 300 and stride of 30). The outcome was comprised of 4240 anomaly samples and 169 non-anomaly samples after the preprocessing. Though the number of anomaly samples was more than non-anomaly ones, when combined with other datasets, other datasets compensated for this inverted unbalancedness. Furthermore, in the single-project test, we invert the dataset (changing anomalies to non-anomalies and vice versa) to preserve the anomaly detection task’s nature.

BGL:
The dataset, mentioned in
(
Oliner and
Stearley 2007
)
, is collected from a BlueGene/L supercomputer at Lawrence Livermore National Labs (LLNL) in Livermore, California, with 131,072 CPUs and 32,768GB of RAM. The sequence creation process was based on the label locality of log events. As some sequences became too long, we generated subsequences utilizing a sliding window (windows size of 300 and stride of 50) from them. We ended up with 7632 anomaly and 72257 non-anomaly samples.

3.2.
Hierarchical CNN Model

Figure 2.
The Hierarchical CNN model visualization. It must be noted that within our experiments, though the sequence events windows size is 300, there is no limit for the number of characters in each log event (log line). So, the number 50 is just an example for the sake of interpretability.

As previously stated, OneLog takes its input in text format and learns to classify sequences during the training. So, the log anomaly detection problem turns into a sequence of text classification problem. Hence, there must be two sequence processing modules in the OneLog model architecture. The first one for processing the log events, and the second one for processing the sequences. Though there are various sequence processing layers available in neural networks, not all of them are suitable for this task. The RNN based layers are not suitable for this task as they are expensive to train and almost impractical on massive datasets. So, OneLog strives to only use convolutional layers for model construction.

Figure
2
show the steps in our Hierarchical CNN model. The input is a sequence of log events, while each log event is a sequence of characters (the leftmost rectangle in the figure). Hence, assuming that
B
is the batch size used in our model training,
L_{s}
is the maximum number of log events in a sequence, and
L_{l}
is the maximum number of characters in a log event (line), the input size is
(B,L_{s},L_{l})
. At first, OneLog embeds all characters into an arbitrary vector space with the dimension of
C_{e}
, which is the character embedding matrix’s dimensions, producing an intermediate tensor shape of
(B,L_{s},L_{l},C_{e})
. After that, three one-dimensional convolutional layers, event embedding network, are applied to each log event (the second dimension) in the tensor. Presuming that
C_{l}
is the number of output channels in the log event embedding network, the intermediate tensor shape is
(B,L_{s},L_{l},C_{l})
. The output is then aggregated by taking the maximums in the third dimension, delivering the intermediate tensor shape of
(B,L_{s},C_{l})
.

Following the aggregation, the intermediate tensor goes through three one-dimensional convolutional layers, sequence embedding network. Supposing the number of output channels in the sequence embedding network’s last layer is
C_{s}
, the intermediate tensor shape is
(B,L_{s},C_{s})
. Next, the output is aggregated by taking the maximums in the second dimension, making a tensor shape of
(B,C_{s})
. Finally, the intermediate tensor passes through multiple fully-connected layers activated by Rectifier Linear Unit (ReLU) following by a sigmoid activated one with only one output neuron, producing an output shape of
(B,1)
, which indicates the probability of each input sequence being anomalous. As the model hierarchically applies convolution, we called it Hierarchical Convolutional Neural Network (Hierarchical CNN).

Since the proposed model is entirely made of convolutional and fully-connected layers, the computations could be parallelized, making the inference and training drastically more efficient in comparison to RNNs based solutions. Furthermore, parallelism allows for higher quantities of training data that can boost performance and generalizability.

Our results show that models trained on single-project data experience significant performance drops on evolved sequences. On the other hand, the multi-project model is exceptionally resistant to the noise showing only extremely minor performance losses.

The sequence noise, such as duplication, removal, and shuffling, does not affect the model’s performance as the BGL dataset messages are labeled individually and independent from each other.

5.
Conclusion and Future Works

The paper presented OneLog, a novel method to detect anomalies in software logs. OneLog replaces the four-stage traditional architecture with a new fully-deep model. Furthermore, OneLog merges the parser, vectorizer, and classifier components into one deep neural network, allowing the model to learn at the language level. Moreover, the OneLog takes a character-based approach to process log events (lines). This fact contributes to higher performance as the model may take advantage of characters deleted in word-based approaches, such as numbers and punctuation. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates that models trained on multi-project datasets are not only more accurate in standard tests but also more robust to sequence evolutions and more accurate in ahead of time anomaly predictions.

Although the results of the model trained on the multi-project dataset are mesmerizing, it does not come without any room for improvements. As a matter of fact, the lack of data volume caused poor results for the Openstack dataset, even in our own evaluations. In future works, we believe that a bigger version of the dataset may be used instead of the current version of the Openstack dataset. Furthermore, additional datasets from different projects or even the same project from different versions may be added to the multi-project dataset as well. All in all, we believe scaling the multi-project dataset (in any aspect) may be the best contribution to the OneLog idea.

Moreover, the concept of transfer learning in log anomaly detection could be another research domain. More in-depth, the weights of the convolutional layers in a model trained on a large multi-project dataset may be transferred to another project to boost the performance without retraining the model on the large multi-project dataset.

Finally, in the area of model complexity, there is always room for improvement. The Hierarchical CNN model can take advantage of more complex models such as attention-based architectures or the Transformer. All these convoluted models might provide higher performance in both single project and multi-project datasets.
