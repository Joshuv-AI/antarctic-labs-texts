Abstract
. Pursuing a new approach to the study of infinite games in combinatorics, we introduce the categories 𝐆𝐚𝐦𝐞 A \mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}} and 𝐆𝐚𝐦𝐞 B \mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{B}} and provide novel proofs of some classical results concerning topological games related to the duality between covering properties of X X and convergence properties of C p ​ ( X ) \mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{p}}(X) that are based on the existence of natural transformations. We then describe these ludic categories in various equivalent forms, viewing their objects as certain structured trees, presheaves, or metric spaces, and we thereby obtain their arboreal, functorial and metrical appearances. We use their metrical disguise to demonstrate a universality property of the Banach-Mazur game. The various equivalent descriptions come with underlying functors to more familiar categories which help establishing some important properties of the game categories: they are complete, cocomplete, extensive, cartesian closed, and coregular, but neither regular nor locally cartesian closed. We prove that their classes of strong epimorphisms, of regular epimorphisms, and of descent morphisms, are all distinct, and we show that these categories have weak classifiers for strong partial maps. Some of the categorical constructions have interesting game-theoretic interpretations.



Keywords:
infinite game, game category, game tree, Banach-Mazur game, tightness game, covering game, selection principles, arboreal game, metric game, functorial game, multiboard game, exponential game, chronological map, game morphism, locally surjective map, (strict) quotient map, weak classifier.

An abstract infinite game with

One often finds in many areas of mathematics the problem of whether a sequence

Indeed, through the existence of winning strategies we can then pose the question of whether it is possible to construct a sequence
{\left({x_{n}:n<\omega}\right)}
with the desired properties
even with an opposing force (the other player) attempting to hinder this process
.

The Banach-Mazur game, for instance, was introduced in the study of Baire subspaces of the real line and, indeed, in
[
15
]
, Oxtoby showed that a space is Baire if, and only if,
Alice
has no winning strategy in

In addition to topological applications, games have been introduced and studied to classify Banach spaces in functional analysis (as seen in, e.g.,
[
8

), to explore properties of algebraic structures (as seen in
[

), and notably in mathematical logic with the Axiom of Determinacy.

The well-known categorical techniques used in Section

[

The topos

With the metric presentation of games at hand, in Section

While this result may be considered as an analogue of Cayley’s Theorem in group theory, thus putting Banach-Mazur games at par with symmetric groups, we show that our embedding result actually goes beyond this predecessor: under a restriction to so-called locally surjective game morphisms, the emdedding defines a natural transformation.

Aided by the various equivalent presentations of games, in Section

\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}\cong\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}

Of course, any given games lead to new ones. For instance, forming subgames proceeds as follows.

Definition 2.9
.

A game

is a

Briefly, subgames of

The next proposition follows trivially from Definition

Proposition 2.10

For any family
\left\{\,{G_{\alpha}=(T_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}):\alpha\in\kappa}\,\right\}

(\bigcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}T_{\alpha},\bigcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}A_{\alpha})
of

3.
Game morphisms

We now define the morphisms of our game categories.

Definition 3.1

(Chronological map)
.

For game trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, a mapping
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is
chronological
(in which case we write
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T_{2}
) if
f
preserves length and truncation of moments; that is: if for every
t\in T_{1}
,
|f(t)|=|t|
and
f(t\restrict k)=f(t)\restrict k
for all
k\leq|t|
.

One trivially has:

Proposition 3.2
.

Given game trees
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
, if
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T_{2}
and
T_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}T_{3}
, then
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g\circ f}}{{\to}}T_{3}
.

Moreover, the identity map over a game tree
T
is chronological.

We note that any chronological map
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
extends to a mapping
\overline{f}\colon{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{1})\to{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{2})
, uniquely determined by
\overline{f}(R)\restrict n=f(R\upharpoonright n)
for every
n<\omega
; we may write

\overline{f}=\lim_{\longrightarrow}f_{n},

with
f_{n}:T_{1}(n)\to T_{2}(n)
denoting the indicated restriction of
f
.

Definition 3.3

(Game morphisms)
.

Let
G_{1}=(T_{1},A_{1})
and
G_{2}=(T_{2},A_{2})
be games and
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T_{2}
be chronological. Then

(A)

f
is an
A
-
morphism
if
\overline{f}(R)\in A_{2}
for every run
R\in A_{1}
, and

(B)

f
is a
B
-
morphism
if
\overline{f}(R)\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{2})\setminus A_{2}
for every run
R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{1})\setminus A_{1}
.

We indicate these properties respectively by
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}_{\textup{{{A}}}}G_{2}
and
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}_{\textup{{{B}}}}G_{2}
, omitting the subscript to the arrow when the context provides sufficient clarity.

Intuitively, an
A
-morphism between
G_{1}=(T_{1},A_{1})
and
G_{2}=(T_{2},A_{2})
is a mapping
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
that
chronologically
respects the histories of the games and guarantees that, if
Alice
wins a run
R
of
G_{1}
, then
Alice
also wins in the run
\overline{f}(R)
, and likewise for
B
-morphisms.

Of course:

Proposition 3.4
.

A
-morphisms are closed under composition, and so are
B
-morphisms. The identity map of the game tree of a game is both an
A
-morphism and a
B
-morphism.

In view of Propositions
3.2
and
3.4
, we have three emergent categories:

•

\mathbf{Gme}
:

–

objects are game trees,

–

morphisms are chronological mappings.

•

\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
:

–

objects are games,

–

morphisms are
A
-morphisms.

•

\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
:

–

objects are games,

–

morphisms are
B
-morphisms.

Our primary interest lies in studying the categories
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
. Exploring their common “base category”
\mathbf{Gme}
assists in this pursuit, since its objects, considered as “games with forgotten

\mathbf{Gme}
.

The following proposition characterizes some special types of morphisms in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
. We prove the first statement since it may come as a surprise that there are non-injective monomorphisms in these categories, but we omit the elementary proofs of the other statements.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G_{1}=(T_{1},A_{1})
and
G_{2}=(T_{2},A_{2})
be games. Then:

(a)

An
A
-morphism
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}_{\textup{{{A}}}}G_{2}
is a monomorphism in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
if, and only if, the mapping
\overline{f}\colon{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{1})\to{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{2})
is injective. Likewise for
B
-morphisms.

(b)

An
A
-morphism
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}_{\textup{{{A}}}}G_{2}
is an epimorphism in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
if, and only if, the mapping
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is surjective. Likewise for
B
-morphisms.

(c)

A mapping
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
becomes an isomorphism
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}_{\textup{{{A}}}}G_{2}
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
if, and only if,
f
is a bijection that is both an
A
-morphism and a
B
-morphism. These poroperties characterize also the isomorphisms in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
.

(d)

If
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}G_{2}
and

, then
f[T]\leq G_{2}
.

(e)

If
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is injective and both an
A
-morphism and a
B
-morphism, then

{\mathrm{Run}}(f[T_{1}])\cap A_{2}=\overline{f}[A_{1}],

and
\left(f[T_{1}],\overline{f}[A_{1}]\right)
is a subgame of
G_{2}
that is
A
-isomorphic to
G_{1}
.

Proof.

(a) With
G
denoting the generating game
(\{*\}^{<\omega},\emptyset)
of Example
2.5
, one first observes that the A-morphisms
G\to G_{1}
are in bijective correspondence to the runs of the game
G_{1}
. Hence, assuming
f
to be monic in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
and letting
\overline{f}(R)=\overline{f}(R^{\prime})
for
R,R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Run}(T_{1})
, the corresponding A-morphisms
r,r^{\prime}:G\to G_{1}
satisfy
f\circ r=f\circ r^{\prime}
. Consequently,
r=r^{\prime}
, and then
R=R^{\prime}
follows.

Conversely, assuming
\overline{f}
to be injective, we consider any A-morphisms
g,h:G^{\prime}\to G_{1}
with
f\circ g=f\circ h
for some game
G^{\prime}=(T^{\prime},A^{\prime})
. Then
\overline{f}\circ\overline{g}=\overline{f}\circ\overline{h}
, and since any
t^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
may be “extended" to a run

in
G^{\prime}
with

where

, we obtain

and then

. Therefore,

.
∎

(1) Similarly as in the above proof one shows that the generating game

Definition 3.7
.

Let

and

Trivially, an isomorphism maps a winning strategy in the domain to a winning strategy in the range. Next we identify a much larger class of morphisms of games preserving winning strategies.

Definition 3.8
.

For game trees
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, we call a chronological map
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T_{2}

locally surjective at

if, for every element

with
f(t)^{\smallfrown}y\in T_{2}
, there is an element
x
with
t^{\smallfrown}x\in T_{1}
and
f(t^{\smallfrown}x)=f(t)^{\smallfrown}y
.
The mapping
f
is
locally surjective
if

•

T_{1}=\emptyset
implies
T_{2}=\emptyset
, and

•

f
is locally surjective at every
t\in T_{1}
.

A locally surjective

is necessarily surjective. (This is true by definition when
T_{1}=\emptyset
, and for
T_{1}\neq\emptyset
note in particular that
f({\left({\,}\right)})={\left({\,}\right)}
holds.)

The following proposition provides a categorical characterization of locally surjective morphisms. Recall that a family of morphisms
g_{i}:A_{i}\to B\;(i\in I)
in a category
\mathbf{C}
is
collectively epic in
\mathbf{C}
if, for any morphisms
f,f^{\prime}\colon B\to C
of
\mathbf{C}
with
f\circ g_{i}=f^{\prime}\circ g_{i}
for all
i\in I
, one necessarily has
f=f^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
be game trees and
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T_{2}
be chronological. Then
f
is locally surjective if, and only if, there is a collectively epic family
(T_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{i}}}{{\to}}T_{1}:i\in I)
of morphisms in
\mathbf{Gme}
such that
f\circ g_{i}=\id_{T_{2}}
for all
i\in I
.

Proof.

The assertion is trivial if
T_{1}=\emptyset
, so we may assume
T_{1}\neq\emptyset
throughout. Furthermore, one easily verifies that a family
(T_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{i}}}{{\to}}T_{1}:i\in I)
is collectively epic in
\mathbf{Gme}
if, and only if,
\bigcup_{i\in I}g_{i}[T_{2}]=T_{1}
. Now, given such a family with
f\circ g_{i}=\id_{T_{2}}
for all
i\in I
, we must confirm that
f
is locally surjective. Indeed,
considering
t\in T_{1}
and
y
with
f(t)^{\smallfrown}y\in T_{2}
, we first find some
i\in I
and
t^{\prime}\in T_{2}
with
t=g_{i}(t^{\prime})
. Since
f\circ g_{i}=\id_{T_{2}}
we get
f(t)=t^{\prime}
and then
g_{i}(f(t))=t
. Since
g_{i}
is chronological, this forces
g_{i}(f(t)^{\smallfrown}y)=t^{\smallfrown}x\in T_{1}
with some element
x
, and
f(t^{\smallfrown}x)=f(t)^{\smallfrown}y
follows.

Conversely, for
f
locally surjective, it suffices to construct a family
(T_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{t}}}{{\to}}T_{1}:t\in T_{1})
with
f\circ g_{t}=\id_{T_{2}}
and
t\in g_{t}[T_{2}]
, for all
t\in T_{1}
. So, fixing
t\in T_{1}
, we establish the chronological mapping
g_{t}\colon T_{2}\to T_{1}
by recursively defining
g_{t}(s)\in T_{1}
with
f(g_{t}(s))=s
, for all
s\in T_{2}=\bigcup_{n}T_{2}(n)
, as follows.

•

Put
g_{t}({\left({\,}\right)})={\left({\,}\right)}
.

•

Suppose that
g_{t}
is defined on
T_{2}\restrict n
as desired, and now consider
s\in T_{2}(n+1)
. If
s=f(t\restrict n+1)
, then simply put
g_{t}(s)=t\restrict n+1
. Otherwise, we write
s
as
s=s^{\prime\smallfrown}y
with
s^{\prime}\in T_{2}(n)
, and we can assume that
g_{t}(s^{\prime})\in T_{1}(n)
is already defined, satisfying
f(g_{t}(s^{\prime}))=s^{\prime}
. Now the local surjectivity of

gives us an

with
g_{t}(s^{\prime})^{\smallfrown}x\in T_{1}
and
f(g_{t}(s^{\prime})^{\smallfrown}x)=s^{\prime\smallfrown}y=s
. Hence, putting
g_{t}(s)=g_{t}(s^{\prime})^{\smallfrown}x
completes the induction step.

Clearly, the mapping
g_{t}
is chronological and satisfies
g_{t}(f(t))=t
and
f\circ g_{t}=\id_{T_{2}}
, as desired.
∎

If
G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}G_{2}

We saw in item (d) of Proposition

Example 3.11
.

Consider the set

of finite sequences that are constantly

4.

It is high time for us to point out a trivial fact:

Proposition 4.1

The categories

8.
The Universality of the Banach-Mazur game

Many classical results concern the existence of “universal” objects in a given category. For example, since by Cayley’s Theorem every group is isomorphic to
a subgroup of a symmetric group, symmetric groups may be considered universal in the category of groups. In this section we show that the Banach-Mazur games are universal in this sense in the category of games. More precisely:

Theorem 8.1

(Universality of the Banach-Mazur Game)
.

For every game
G
there is an ultrametric space
\tilde{\mathsf{K}}(G)
such that
G
is isomorphic to a subgame of the Banach-Mazur game over
\tilde{\mathsf{K}}(G)
.

In order to prove Theorem
8.1
, let us first consider the following functorial construction:

Definition 8.2
.

Let
{\mathrm{D}}_{\textup{{{B}}}}\colon\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
be the functor such that,

•

G=(T,A)
, with some element

define
\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}G=(\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}T,A)
by

(1)

\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}T=\begin{cases}T\cup\left\{\,{t^{\smallfrown}{\left({0_{G}:i\leq n}\right)}:t\in T\text{, $t$ is $\textup{{{Alice}}}$'s turn and $n<\omega$}}\,\right\},\text{ if $T\neq\emptyset$}\\
\left\{\,{{\left({0_{G}:i\leq n}\right)}:n<\omega}\,\right\},\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

•

on morphisms, for a
B
-morphism
(T,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}(T^{\prime},A^{\prime})
, let
(\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}T,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}f}}{{\to}}(\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}T^{\prime},A^{\prime})
be such that

(\mathrm{D}_{\textup{{{B}}}}f)(t)=\begin{cases}f(t),\text{ if $t\in T$},\\
f(s)^{\smallfrown}{\left({0_{G^{\prime}}:i\leq n}\right)},\text{ if $t=s^{\smallfrown}{\left({0_{G}:i\leq n}\right)}$ for some $s\in T$.}\end{cases}

Intuitively, given a non-empty game

,

the option to “quit” the game at every moment

In what follows we concentrate on the game-theoretic construction and interpretation of products and equalizers and their duals in

. As only some of these have appeared in the literature on infinite games, we give a summary of them, before turning to properties that are aided by, but do not automatically follow from, our various presentations of
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}

9.1.1.

We start by describing products of games in

(the games

9.1.2.
Coproducts

Coproducts of games are even easier to describe than products.
For a family
\left(G^{\alpha}=(T^{\alpha},A^{\alpha}):\alpha\in\kappa\right)
of games, one defines
\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}G^{\alpha}=(T,A)
by

\displaystyle T:=\{{\left({\,}\right)}\}\cup\left(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}\left(T^{\alpha}\setminus\{{\left({\,}\right)}\}\right)\right),\quad A:=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}A^{\alpha}.

Intuitively,
\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}G^{\alpha}
is the game in which
Alice
also chooses at her first move which of the games
G^{\alpha}
she and
Bob
will be playing from that moment onward. With the canonical injections defined (on non-empty moments) as in
\mathbf{Set}
, it is clear that
(T,A)
serves as the coproduct
\coprod_{\alpha\in\kappa}G^{\alpha}
of the given family of games, not only in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
, but also in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
.

9.1.3.
Equalizers and pullbacks

The construction of the equalizer in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
of a pair of morphisms
f,g\colon G\to G^{\prime}
with
G=(T,A)
and
G^{\prime}=(T^{\prime},A^{\prime})
is, in functorial terms, predicated by the coreflector as described before Corollary
5.3
. Transcribed in game-theoretic notation, we simply must consider the subgame

defined by

T^{\prime\prime}(n):=\{R\upharpoonright n:R\in\mathrm{Run}(T),\overline{f}(R)=\overline{g}(R)\}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\prime\prime}:=A\cap T^{\prime\prime}

for all

. Indeed, one easily sees that the inclusion map

serves as the desired equalizer.

The subgame

may be hard to play and win: at every moment of the game

one has to be sure that there is a run in

which, once mapped by both,
\overline{f}
and
\overline{g}
, results in the same run in
G^{\prime}
, and
Alice
wins such run in

if it is a winning run in

.

Of course, given

-morphisms

, their pullback is formed by taking the equalizer
of the pair

,
with the projections

\mathsf{Multi}_{\textup{{{A}}}}(G_{1},G_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{i}}}{{\to}}G_{,}\,i=1,2
.
Explicitly, in the pullback span

, the subgame

of

is
obtained by pruning the tree

\left\{\,{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathsf{Multi}_{\textup{{{A}}}}(G_{1},G_{2}):f(t_{1})=g(t_{2})}\,\right\},

and

is the restriction of the projection
\pi_{i}
. Equivalently, one forms the pullback of the cospan

in

9.1.4.
Coequalizers and pushouts

In order to form the coequalizer of a pair of

and

as the game arising from

in which all

for that run. The difficulty lies in determining all equivalent moves at every moment of the game

In
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{B}}}}
, the coequalizer

of the

The pushout
G_{1}\sqcup_{G}G_{2}
of

is formed by taking the coequalizer
of the pair

,
with the coproduct injections

.

9.2.
Infinitary extensiveness

Knowing how to construct coproducts and pullbacks makes it easy to prove that the category
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
has an important “space-like” property, shared with the category of topological spaces as well as with all categories of
\mathbf{Set}

Recall that a finitely complete category

with coproducts is

is determined by the family of morphisms given by its pullbacks along every coproduct injection; more precisely, in terms of the “slices” of the category

\prod_{j\in J}\mathbf{C}/A_{j}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}/\coprod_{j\in J}A_{j}

is an equivalence of categories.

.

The category

9.3.
Orthogonal factorization systems

Recall that an orthogonal factorization system
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M})
in a category
\mathcal{C}
is given by two classes of morphisms, both closed under composition with isomorphisms, such that every morphism
f
factors as
f=m\cdot e
with
e\in\mathcal{E},m\in\mathcal{M}
, and the diagonalization (or lifting) property holds: whenever
v\cdot e^{\prime}=m^{\prime}\cdot u
with
e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E},m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, then
t\cdot e^{\prime}=u
and
m^{\prime}\cdot t=v
for a unique morphism
t
(in which case one says that
e^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
m^{\prime}
). Such systems are completely determined by just one of the two classes since, when fixing one of them, one obtains the other as containing precisely those morphisms that are orthogonal to all morphisms in the class considered first. The system
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M})
is proper if
\mathcal{E}
is a class epimorphisms and
\mathcal{M}
is a class of monomorphisms in the category
\mathcal{C}
.

Here we will present four proper orthogonal factorization systems in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
, taking advantage of the fact that the forgetful functor

\mathsf{Sub}_{\mathrm{Run}}(\mathbf{Gme})=\mathbf{Game}_{\mathrm{A}}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Gme}

is topological (Proposition

). In fact, it is well known (see 21.14 in
[
1
]
or, more generally, II.5.7.1 in
[
11
]
) that for any topological functor
U:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{C}
and any (proper) orthogonal factorization system
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M})
in
\mathcal{C}
, in the category
\mathcal{T}
one then has the (proper) orthogonal factorization systems

(U^{-1}[\mathcal{E}],\,U^{-1}[\mathcal{M}]\cap\{U\text{-initial morphisms}\})\quad\text{and}\quad(U^{-1}[\mathcal{E}]\cap\{U\text{-final morphisms}\},\,U^{-1}[\mathcal{M}])

which, neglecting the functor

, we will allow ourselves to respectively denote simply by

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M}_{*})\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathcal{E}^{*},\mathcal{M}),

thus denoting the classes
U^{-1}[\mathcal{E}]
and
U^{-1}[\mathcal{M}]
in
\mathcal{T}
again by
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively.

Hence, for the forgetful functor

one has the following Lemma which the reader may also verify easily in a direct manner.

Lemma 9.4

and

\displaystyle=\left\{\,{(T,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}(T^{\prime},A^{\prime}):(T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M},\,A=\overline{f}^{-1}[A^{\prime}]}\,\right\},

\displaystyle=\left\{\,{(T,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}(T^{\prime},A^{\prime}):(T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E},\,A^{\prime}=\overline{f}[A]}\,\right\}.

We will now present two proper orthogonal factorization systems in
\mathbf{Gme}
, each of which giving two systems in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
by Lemma

Every chronological mapping

Theorem 9.14
.

In both categories,
\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
, the composite of regular epimorphisms may fail to be a regular epimorphism. As a consequence, these categories contain strong epimorphisms that fail to be regular, and their morphisms generally lack
(\{\text{regular epis}\},\{\text{monos}\})
-factorizations.

Proof.

A “folklore” fact of category theory says that, in any category with kernel pairs and their coequalizers, the following properties are equivalent:

(i)

Strong epimorphisms are regular.

(ii)

The class of regular epimorphisms is closed under composition.

(iii)

(\{\text{regular epis}\},\{\text{monos}\})
is an (orthogonal) factorization system.

Since a direct proof of this fact may not be found easily, let us sketch one here. Indeed, regular epimorphisms are always strong, and the class of strong epimorphisms is always closed under composition. This shows (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). For (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii), given a morphism
f
, one factors
f=m\cdot e
with
e
the coequalizer of the kernel pair of
f
and then factors the unique morphism
m=n\cdot d
in the same manner. Then, since by hypothesis (ii)
d\cdot e
is a regular epimorphism, its kernel pair factors through the kernel pair of
f
. This makes the coequalizer
d\cdot e
factor through
e
, so that
d
must be an isomorphism. Hence,
d
has a trivial kernel pair, which makes
m
a monomorphism. Finally, assuming (iii), one sees that even all extremal epimorphisms (
i.e.
, those, which admit only isomorphisms as a second monic factor) are regular. Since strong epimorphisms are trivially extremal, one obtains (i).

Equipped with this general fact, since Proposition
9.10
and Lemma
9.13
tell us that condition (i) fails in
\mathbf{Gme}
, we obtain all assertions of the Theorem for that category. Providing the principal witness

e=c\circ(q\sqcup q)\colon T^{0}\sqcup T^{1}\to C
with empty payoff sets, we conclude (see Remark
9.8
) that
e
maintains this role also in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
.
∎

9.5.
Coregularity, non-regularity, and descent

We will show that the categories
\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{A}
are coregular (
i.e.
, their duals are regular), so that, in addition to the existence of the (epi, regular mono)-factorizations, in these categories not only the class of epimorphisms, but also the class of regular monomorphisms is stable under pushout. By contrast, these categories fail badly to be regular: not only are they missing the needed factorizations (by Theorem
9.14
) but, as we will show, their classes of regular epimorphisms also fail to be stable under pullback.

Theorem 9.15
.

\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{A}

Proof.

We carry out the proof for

Since the cocomplete category

For games

In any category (with pullbacks), a pullback-stable regular epimorphism (so that its pullback along any morphism with the same codomain is again a regular epimorphism) is often called a

. In pursuit of the characterization of descent morphisms in the categories

and

Definition 9.16

\Delta(S,S^{\prime})=\Delta(R,R^{\prime})
.

Just invoking the relevant definitions, one immediately sees that strict quotient maps are in fact quotient maps. However, there are quotient maps which fail to be strict, as we show next.

Recall that a

\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
can be a quasitopos. But what about the other defining property of a quasitopos? We will show that, although these categories do not have a classifier for strong subobjects either, each one does possess an object satisfying the classifying property
weakly
, whereby this property is stripped of its uniqueness requirement.
In fact, more generally, we will see that they have weak classifiers for all strong partial morphisms, as defined below.

Definition 9.29
.

A
classifier for strong subobjects
in a finitely complete category
\mathbf{C}
is an object
\Omega
equipped with a
truth morphism

1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\to}}\Omega
from
\mathbf{C}
’s terminal object such that, for every strong monomorphism
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\to}}X
, there is a unique morphism
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\chi}}{{\to}}\Omega
such that
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces!}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}

is a pullback diagram.

Proposition 9.30
.

The categories
\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
do not have a classifier for strong subobjects.

Proof.

By Theorems
7.1
and
7.3
, it suffices to show the absence of a classifier for strong subobjects in the categories
\mathbf{SeqSpa}
and
\mathbf{MetGame}
.
Suppose we had such classifier
\{\ast\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\to}}\Omega
in
\mathbf{SeqSpa}
. We metricize the set
X=\left\{\,{x_{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}\sqcup\{\bar{x}\}
of pairwise distinct elements by
d_{X}(\bar{x},x_{n})=\frac{1}{n+1}
and
d_{X}(x_{n},x_{m})=\max\{\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{m+1}\}
for all
m,n<\omega
,
to obtain the object
X\in\mathbf{SeqSpa}
.
Its strong subobject
\{\bar{x}\}\hookrightarrow X
then comes with the“characteristic morphism”
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\chi}}{{\to}}\Omega
producing the pullback diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\bar{x}\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\ast\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces!}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}

Hence,
\chi^{-1}(\chi(\bar{x}))=\{\bar{x}\}
, which implies
\chi(x_{n})\neq\chi(\bar{x})
for all
n<\omega
. Furthermore, since
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bar{x}
in
X
, one has
\chi(x_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi(\bar{x})
.

But there is another morphism,
\chi^{\prime}
, which fits into the above pullback diagram in lieu of
\chi
. Indeed, the map
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi(x_{n+1}),}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bar{x}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi(\bar{x}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi^{\prime}}

is clearly 1-Lipschitz and satisfies
(\chi^{\prime})^{-1}(\chi^{\prime}(\bar{x}))=\{\bar{x}\}
, but it is distinct from
\chi
since, otherwise,
\chi
would be constant on
\{x_{n}:n<\omega\}
and thus preventing
(\chi(x_{n}))_{n<\omega}
to converge to
\chi(\bar{x})
in
\Omega
.

This concludes the non-existence proof for
\mathbf{SeqSpa}
. The claim for
\mathbf{MetGame}
follows (just as in the proof of Proposition
9.27
) when we choose the “pay-off sets” to be maximal.
∎

Note that, in the above proof, we relied exclusively on the non-uniqueness of the characteristic morphism
\chi
. Discarding that condition in Definition
9.29
and thus arriving at the notion of
weak classifier for strong subobjects
would indeed allow us to show the existence of such objects in
\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}
, as we will prove more generally now.

Definition 9.31
.

A
weak classifier for strong partial maps into
B
in a finitely complete category
\mathbf{C}
with
B\in\mathsf{Obj}\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)
is a morphism
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{B}}}{{\to}}B_{\perp}
such that, for every span
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\leftarrow}}A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}B
with a strong monomorphism
m
, there is a morphism
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f_{\perp}}}{{\to}}B_{\perp}
such that the following diagram is a pullback:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{\perp}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{\perp}}

(The notion of weak classifier for strong subobjects emerges when we specialize
B
to be the terminal object
1
, thus setting
\Omega=1_{\bot}
.) We say that
\mathbf{C}

has weak classifiers for strong partial maps
if a weak classifier for strong partial maps into
B
exists for all objects
B
in
\mathbf{C}
.

Theorem 9.32
.

The categories
\mathbf{Gme}
and
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
have weak classifiers for strong partial maps.

Proof.

For every game tree
T
in
\mathbf{Gme}
we let
\ast_{T}
be an element outside
\mathrm{M}(T)
and put

T_{\perp}=\left\{\,{t^{\smallfrown}{\left({\ast_{T}:i<n}\right)}:t\in T,n<\omega}\,\right\}.

Being injective, the inclusion map
i_{T}\colon T\to T_{\perp}
is trivially a strong monomorphism in
\mathbf{Gme}
(by Proposition
9.5
).
Given a span
\tilde{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\leftarrow}}S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T
with a strong monomorphism
m
,
for every
s\in\tilde{S}
we let
n_{s}=\max\{n\leq|s|:s\restrict n\in m[S]\}
and consider the map
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{\perp}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f(s\restrict n_{s})^{\smallfrown}{\left({\ast_{T}:i<|s|-n_{s}}\right)}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{\perp}}

Clearly,
f_{\perp}
is chronological. Moreover,
f_{\perp}(m(s))=i_{T}(f(s))
for every
s\in S
and
m[S]=f_{\perp}^{-1}(i_{T}[T])
, so we obtain the pullback diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{\perp}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{T}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{\perp}}

This completes the proof of the claim for
\mathbf{Gme}
. Augmenting it to the level of games, for
G=(T,A)
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
we consider
G_{\perp}=(T_{\perp},A_{\perp})
with

A_{\perp}=A\cup\left\{\,{{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{\perp})\setminus{\mathrm{Run}}(T)}\,\right\}.

In this way
i_{T}\colon T\to T_{\perp}
becomes a game embedding,
i.e.
, a strong monomorphism in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
.
Considering any span

(\tilde{S},\tilde{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\leftarrow}}(S,\overline{m}^{-1}[\tilde{A}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}(T,A)
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
with
m
injective,
the map
f_{\perp}:\tilde{S}\to T_{\perp}
becomes an
A
-morphism.
Indeed, given any
R\in\tilde{A}
, if
R\notin{\mathrm{Run}}(m[S])
, then
\overline{f_{\perp}}(R)\in A_{\perp}
, and if
R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(m[S])
, since
m
is a game embedding, we find
R^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Run}}(S)
with
R=\overline{m}(R^{\prime})
. Because
f
and
i_{T}
are
A
-morphisms, in this case
\overline{f_{\perp}}(R)=\overline{i_{T}}(\overline{f}(R^{\prime}))\in A_{\perp}
follows, which makes

f_{\perp}
an
A
-morphism. One concludes the argument for
\mathbf{Game}_{\textup{{{A}}}}
just like for
\mathbf{Gme}
.
∎
