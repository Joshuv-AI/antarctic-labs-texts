Geometric complexity theory for product-plus-power
Pranjal Dutta, Fulvio Gesmundo, Christian Ikenmeyer

Abstract
According to Kumar’s recent surprising result (ToCT’20), a small border Waring rank implies that the polynomial can be approximated as a sum of a constant and a small product of linear polynomials. We prove the converse of Kumar’s result and establish a tight connection between border Waring rank and the model of computation in Kumar’s result. In this way, we obtain a new formulation of border Waring rank, up to a factor of the degree. We connect this new formulation to the orbit closure problem of the product-plus-power polynomial. We study this orbit closure from two directions: 1. We deborder this orbit closure and some related orbit closures, i.e., prove all points in the orbit closure have small non-border algebraic branching programs. 2. We fully implement the geometric complexity theory approach against the power sum by generalizing the ideas of Ikenmeyer-Kandasamy (STOC’20) to this new orbit closure. In this way, we obtain new multiplicity obstructions that are constructed from just the symmetries of the polynomials.



Keywords
: border complexity, Waring rank, geometric complexity theory, Newton identities

Introduction

Waring rank is an important complexity measure for homogeneous polynomials, classically studied in geometry and invariant theory. In complexity theory, it defines a model of computation also known as the homogeneous diagonal depth-3 circuits, see e.g.
[
Sax08
]
. In addition, the

[
CHI+18
]

border
version to a new measure that we call

, inspired by the recent work of Kumar
[
Kum20
]

The

of

can be written as the limit of a sequence of polynomials

with

1.1
The product-plus-power model

Homogeneous models of algebraic computation and their border versions are conveniently described using the notions of restrictions and degenerations of homogeneous polynomials. Let
f(\mathbf{x}),g(\mathbf{x})
be homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
n
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We say that
g
is a
restriction
of
f
if there exist linear forms
\ell_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x})
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\ell_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}))
; in this case, write
g\leq f
. Equivalently, regarding
f,g
as polynomial functions on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have that
g\leq f
is there exist a linear map
A:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(A^{t}\mathbf{x})
. Here
A^{t}
is the transpose of the matrix
A
.

We say that
g
is a
degeneration
of
f
is there exist linear forms
\ell_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x})
, depending rationally on a parameter
\epsilon
, such that
g(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f(\ell_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}))
; in this case, write
g\trianglelefteq f
. Equivalently,
g(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f(A_{\epsilon}\mathbf{x})
where
A_{\epsilon}
is a linear map
A_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
depending on
\epsilon
. It is a classical fact that the entries of
A_{\epsilon}
, or equivalently the coefficients of
\ell_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, can be taken in the ring of Laurent polynomials
\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}]
, see e.g.
[
BCS97
, Section 20.6]
; in particular, the polynomial
f(A_{\epsilon}\mathbf{x})
is an element of
\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

The number of variables does not play a role, and we will occasionally use the slightly larger ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for notational convenience.

Define the product-plus-power polynomial

P^{[d]}_{1,1}=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}+x_{0}^{d}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}.

Given a homogeneous degree
d
polynomial
f
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
(without
x_{0}
), it is clear that if
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq m
then
x_{0}^{m-d}f
is a degeneration of
P^{[m]}_{1,1}
. Therefore, lower bounds on the smallest possible
m
such that
x_{0}^{m-d}f\trianglelefteq P^{[m]}_{1,1}
provide lower bounds on
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)
and hence on
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}
.

It is unclear to what extent the product-plus-power model is stronger than Kumar’s model, so we provide
debordering results
for the product-plus-power model, see
Section

1.2
.
We establish that this model is not much stronger, hence lower bounds are expected to exist.
To find such lower bounds on Kumar’s complexity, we implement the geometric complexity theory approach for the product-plus-power model, see
section

1.3
.

1.2
Debordering the product-plus-power model

Border Waring rank, and border Kumar complexity are
border
measures of complexity, that is they are defined in terms of degeneration.
It is often unclear what the gap between a non-border complexity measure and its border complexity measure can be.
Lower bounds of a border complexity measure in terms of a non-border measure are commonly called
debordering results
and they guarantee that introducing the degeneration procedure does not make a model of computation much stronger. We provide debordering results for the product-plus-power model and some generalizations.

We record one important debordering result for border Waring rank, in terms of the algebraic branching program width. Given a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
, the
algebraic branching program width
of
f
, denoted
\textup{{abpw}}(f)
, is the smallest
w
such that
f
can be expressed as a product
f(\mathbf{x})=A_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots A_{d}(\mathbf{x})
of matrices whose entries are linear forms, with
A_{1}
of size
1\times w
,
A_{k}
of size
w\times w
for
k=2,\ldots,d-1
and
A_{d}
of size
w\times 1
.
It is known that
\textup{{abpw}}(f)\leq\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)

[
For16
,
BDI21
]
, and further, these measures can be
exponentially
far (for e.g., see
[
CKW11
]
). In particular,
Theorem

1.1
can be interpreted as a debordering result for Kumar’s complexity, showing
\textup{{abpw}}(f)\leq\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)
.

Similarly, other debordering results for border Waring rank, such as
[
DGI+24
, Theorem 1]
, and more generally classification results for secant varieties as in
[
BL14
,
Bal17
,
BB13
]
, can be reinterpreted in terms of Kumar’s complexity via
Theorem

1.1
.

We obtain the following result for degenerations of the product-plus-power polynomial
P^{[d]}_{1,1}
:

1.3 Theorem

(Debordering product-plus-power)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
.
If
f\trianglelefteq P_{1,1}^{[d]}
, then

(i)

either
f\leq P_{1,1}^{[d]}

(ii)

or
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq O(d^{5})
.

Also, we will obtain debordering results for a variant of the product-plus-power polynomial, that is the product-plus-two-powers polynomial
P_{1,2}^{[d]}=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}+x_{0}^{d}+x_{d+1}^{d}
, as well. These debordering results are more fine-grained because the previous techniques would show that
\textup{{abpw}}(f)
is polynomially-bounded
[
DDS21
]
. In
section

3.2
, we show exponential lower bounds for the same (see
theorem

3.15
-
theorem

3.16
).

1.3
The GCT program

Mulmuley and Sohoni introduced the geometric complexity theory (GCT) program
[
MS01
,
MS08
]
as an approach to Valiant’s determinant vs permanent problem.
The approach aims to determine “representation theoretic obstructions” in order to prove lower bounds of certain complexity measures, typically by proving the impossibility of a degeneration.
Basic background knowledge on the representation theory of the general linear group and the symmetric group can for example be found in
[
FH91
]
and
[
Ful97
]
, and is especially valuable in
section

4
.

The group
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
has a natural action on
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
given by

g\cdot f=f\circ g^{t}
or equivalently
g\cdot f(\mathbf{x})=f(g^{t}\mathbf{x})
for any

f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
,
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Now, given two elements
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
, the condition that a polynomial
f_{1}
degenerates to a polynomial
f_{2}
is equivalent to the fact that
f_{2}\in\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}
; here
\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}=\{g\cdot f_{1}\mid g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}\}
denotes the orbit of
f_{1}
under the group action and the overline indicates the closure, equivalently in the Zariski or the Euclidean topology of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}

[
Kra84
, AI.7.2 Folgerung]
. Further, the membership statement
f_{2}\in\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}
is equivalent to the inclusion of orbit-closures
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{2}}\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}
.

Let
\mathbb{C}\big[\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}\big]
be the ring of polynomials over
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
: its elements are polynomials in the coefficients of the elements of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
. Given
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
, let
I(\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f})\subseteq\mathbb{C}\big[\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}\big]
denote the
vanishing ideal
of the algebraic variety
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f}
and let
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f}]=\mathbb{C}\big[\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}\big]/I(\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f})
be the quotient ring, called the
coordinate ring
of
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f}
. It turns out that
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f}]
is a graded ring; denote by
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f}]_{D}
its degree
D
component.
The condition
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{2}}\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}
is equivalent to the inclusion
I(\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}})\subseteq I(\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{2}})
, yielding a surjection of graded rings
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}]\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{2}}]
. In summary, if
f_{1}
degenerates to
f_{2}
, then for every degree
D
there is a surjective linear map

\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{1}}]_{D}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}\cdot f_{2}}]_{D}.

The group action of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
on
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
induces an action of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
on the coordinate rings via canonical pullback. This makes both
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{1}}]_{D}
and
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{2}}]_{D}
into finite dimensional representations of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
.
The surjection is
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
-equivariant, namely it commutes with the group action.
The group
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
is
reductive
, a condition that guarantees that both representations decompose into a direct sum of
irreducible representations
.
For each partition
\lambda
,
the
representation theoretic multiplicity

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}
counts the number of irreducible representations of type
\lambda
in such a decomposition; this number is independent of the decomposition. In the setting described here,

\lambda
is always a partition of
Dd
into at most
n
parts, and
Schur’s Lemma (see, e.g.,
[
FH91
, Lemma 1.17]
) implies an inequality of representation theoretic multiplicities:
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{2}}]_{D})\geq\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{1}}]_{D})
.

The GCT program aims to prove obstructions to the existence of the surjection by determining violations of these inequalities.
More precisely, the existence of any
\lambda
with
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{1}}]_{D})<\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{2}}]_{D})
would guarantee
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{2}}\not\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{1}}
and therefore show that
f_{2}
is
not
a degeneration of
f_{1}
. This is called a
representation theoretic multiplicity obstruction
. If additionally
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{n}f_{1}}]_{D})=0
, then we call this an
occurrence obstruction
.
It is a wide open question in geometric complexity theory in which situations orbit closure containment can be disproved by representation theoretic multiplicity obstructions.
Very few examples are known
[
BI11
,
BI13a
,
IK20
]
.
We determine multiplicity obstructions that show that the polynomial
x_{0}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d}^{d}
is not a degeneration of
P_{1,1}^{d}
(which for example could also be seen directly by comparing the dimensions of their orbit closures):

1.4 Theorem

(New obstructions)
.

Let
d\geq 3
, and let
\lambda=(5d-1,1)+((d+1)\times(10d))
.
These
\lambda
are representation theoretic multiplicity obstructions that show
x_{0}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d}^{d}\not\trianglelefteq P^{[d]}_{1,1}
:

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}\Bigl[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}\Bigr])\leq 4<5=\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}\Bigl[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{0}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d}^{d})}\Bigr]).

This result extends the result of
[
IK20
]
from the product polynomial to product-plus-power by exhibiting multiplicity obstructions that are based entirely on the symmetries of the two polynomials; see
Theorem

4.10
for details.

Indeed, from a representation-theoretic and a combinatorial point of view, the polynomial
P_{1,1}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}+x_{0}^{d}
looks very similar to the well-studied product polynomial
P_{1,0}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}
, which was the object of several GCT papers
[
Kum15
,
BI17
,
DIP20
,
IK20
]
. A system of set-theoretic equations for its orbit-closure was known for over a century, due to Brill and Gordon
[
Gor94
]
, and their representation theoretic structure has been recently described by Guan
[
Gua18
]
. We transfer as much as possible of the known theory for
P_{1,0}^{[d]}
to the setting of
P^{[d]}_{1,1}
, in order to mimic the proof technique of
[
IK20
]
.

In order to prove
Theorem

4.10
, we obtain several results about the more general polynomial
P^{[d]}_{r,s}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\textstyle\prod}_{j=1}^{d}x_{ji}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}^{d}
.

1.

We determine the stabilizer of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
under the action of the group
\operatorname{GL}_{rd+s}
, see
Theorem

4.2
.

2.

We use the stabilizer to determine the representation theoretic structure of the coordinate ring of the orbit of
P^{[d]}_{1,1}
, which is achieved in
Proposition

4.4
.

3.

We prove that
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
is
polystable
, in the sense of invariant theory, see
Proposition

4.5
.

4.

Polystability implies the existence of a fundamental invariant in the sense of
[
BI17
]
. In
Proposition

4.7
,
in the case
P^{[d]}_{1,1}
,
we show an interesting connection between the degree of this fundamental invariant and the famous Alon-Tarsi conjecture on Latin squares in combinatorics:
the fundamental invariant appears in degree
d+1
if and only if the Alon-Tarsi conjecture holds for
d
.

A
Latin square
is an
n\times n
matrix with entries
1,\ldots,n
such that each row and each column is a permutation. The
column sign
of a Latin square is the product of the signs of its column permutations. If
n
is odd, then there are exactly as many sign +1 Latin squares as sign
-1
Latin squares, and a sign-reversing involution is obtained by switching the first two rows.
The Alon-Tarsi conjecture states that for
n
even, the number of sign +1 and sign
-1
Latin squares are different. The main references on the Alon-Tarsi conjecture are
[
AT92
,
Dri97
,
Gly10
]
,
where it is shown that the conjecture is true for
n=p\pm 1
for all odd primes
p
.

1.4
Related work and context

Waring rank and border Waring rank are the objects of a long history of work in classical algebraic geometry and invariant theory, beginning in the nineteenth century
[
Cay45
,
Syl52
,
Cle61
]
. It is related to the classical study of secant varieties
[
Pal06
,
Ter11
]
and today it has strong connections to the study of the Gorenstein algebras
[
IK99
,
BB14
]
and the geometry of the Hilbert scheme of points
[
BB21
,
JM22
]
.

In complexity theory, one is interested in the growth of a complexity parameter in a sequence of polynomials. In this context, we say that a p-family is a sequence of polynomials
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the degree and the number of variables of
f_{n}
are polynomially bounded as functions of
n
. The complexity classes
\mathsf{VWaring}
and
\overline{\mathsf{VWaring}}
consist of all p-families
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, respectively,
\mathsf{WR}(f_{n})
or
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f_{n})
, are a polynomially bounded function of
n
. Important complexity classes include
\mathsf{VBP}
and
\mathsf{VNP}
, consisting of p-families with polynomially bounded determinantal complexity or permanental complexity, respectively.
The
determinantal complexity
of a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
in
n
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is the smallest
N
such that
x_{0}^{N-d}f
is a restriction of the determinant polynomial
\det_{N}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i,\sigma(i)}
, which is a polynomial of degree
N
in
N^{2}
variables.
The
permanental complexity
is defined similarly, in terms of the permanent polynomial
\textup{per}_{N}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i,\sigma(i)}
. It is known that
\mathsf{VBP}\subseteq\mathsf{VNP}

[
Val79
,
Tod92
]
, and Valiant’s determinant vs. permanent conjecture states that this inclusion is strict, that is
\mathsf{VNP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{VBP}
.

The border complexity classes are
\overline{\mathsf{VBP}}
and
\overline{\mathsf{VNP}}
are defined similarly, replacing the notion of restriction with the one of degeneration, that is allowing arbitrary close approximations of polynomials in terms of determinants or permanents, rather than an exact expression. These border complexity classes can be defined as topological closures as well, see
[
IS22
]
. A systematic study of border complexity classes was initiated in
[
MS01
,
Bü04
]
: the Mulmuley-Sohoni conjecture is a strengthening of Valiant’s conjecture, and it predicts that
\mathsf{VNP}\not\subseteq\overline{\mathsf{VBP}}
.

It is wide open whether
\mathsf{VBP}=\overline{\mathsf{VBP}}
, hence it is unclear to what extent the Mulmuley-Sohoni conjecture is stronger than Valiant’s conjecture.
As a first step towards resolving this question,
in
[
BIZ18
]
it is shown that border of width-2 algebraic branching programs define the same complexity class as algebraic formulas
\overline{\mathsf{VBP}_{2}}=\overline{\mathsf{VF}}
, over fields of characteristic
\neq 2
, and using
[
AW16
]
this implies that
\mathsf{VBP}_{2}\subsetneqq\overline{\mathsf{VBP}_{2}}
.
Resolving similar problems of inclusion between a border complexity class and a non-border complexity class is the goal of the debordering techniques. For instance, the already mentioned inequality
\textup{{abpw}}(f)\leq\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)
proves the inclusion
\overline{\mathsf{VWaring}}\subseteq\mathsf{VBP}

[
For16
,
BDI21
]
.
In
[
DDS21
,
DS22
]
, it is shown that
\overline{\Sigma^{[k]}\Pi\Sigma}\subsetneqq\mathsf{VBP}
, where
\Sigma^{[k]}\Pi\Sigma
is the class of p-families of polynomials which can be expressed as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j}\ell_{ij}
, for linear forms
\ell_{ij}
. Very recently,
[
DIK+24
]
showed that border width-2 ABPs over characteristic
2
can compute any algebraic formulas.

A major difficulty to achieve debordering results is that, in general, boundaries of orbits of algebraic groups may present strong geometric pathologies. In small cases, one can achieve
boundary classification
results. This was done in the case of border Waring rank at most
5

[
BL14
,
BB13
,
Bal17
]
, for the
3\times 3
determinant polynomial
\det_{3}

[
HL16
]
, and partially for the binomial
P_{2,0}^{[d]}=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}+x_{d+1}\cdots x_{2d}

[
Hü17
, Ch. II.9]
. There are however universal results
[
Kac80
,
Vak06
,
Jel20
]
hinting towards the difficulty of such a fine classification in general.

The GCT approach discussed in
Section

1.3
was introduced in
[
MS01
,
MS08
]
as a path toward the Mulmuley-Sohoni conjecture, and proposed to use occurrence obstructions to prove lower bounds on the determinantal complexity of the
padded
permanent
x_{0}^{N-n}\textup{per}_{n}
. The
no-go theorem

of
[
IP17
,
BIP19
]
proved that this is impossible by making use of the fact that
[
MS01
,
MS08
]
use the padded formulation of the Mulmuley-Sohoni conjecture. There exists
no such result
when the determinant is replaced, for instance, by the iterated matrix multiplication polynomial, so that the determinantal complexity is replaced by the algebraic branching program width. The potential of multiplicity obstructions is explored in
[
DIP20
,
IK20
]
: in particular, the GCT approach is used to prove that the power sum polynomial is not a product of homogeneous linear forms, although there are easier ways to prove this.

Friedman and McGuinness
[
FM19
]
give a survey about the Alon-Tarsi conjecture.
The GCT result in
[
Kum15
]
is based on the conjecture.
The conjecture has been generalized in numerous directions.
[
SW12
]
prove that Drisko’s proof method cannot be used without modifications to prove the Alon-Tarsi conjecture.
The same is true for results in
[
BI13b
,
BI17
]
, some of which are based on generalizations or variants of the conjecture.
The Polymath Project number 12 (
https://polymathprojects.org
) was devoted to the study of Rota’s basis conjecture, which for even

is implied by the Alon-Tarsi conjecture, see
[
HR94
]
.

[

]
proves an upper bound on the difference between the even and odd Latin squares.

2
Kumar’s complexity and border Waring rank

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.1
, connecting Waring and border Waring rank to
Kc
-complexity and its variants. To obtain the result, we observe that
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}
expressions fall into three different cases, depending on whether the scalar
\alpha(\epsilon)
in (
1.※
) converges to
0
, converges to a nonzero constant, or diverges. We study these three cases independently.
For the case where
\alpha(\epsilon)
converges to zero, it is easy to see that the resulting polynomial is a product of affine linear polynomials, see
Lemma

2.8
.
For the case where
\alpha(\epsilon)
converges to a nonzero value, we use the Newton Identities to obtain the desired lower bound given by the Waring rank, see
Proposition

2.9
.
The case where
\alpha(\epsilon)
diverges is the most interesting one as it is the one where cancellations occur in the limit; in this case the proof is obtained via a border version of Newton relations.

Let
e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
denotes the
k
-th
elementary symmetric
polynomial, defined by

e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\;:=\;\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}\;;

Recall that by definition
e_{0}=1
. First, we record an immediate observation that will be useful throughout:

2.1 Remark
.

It is easy to observe that

\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+x_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{m}e_{j}(\mathbf{x})

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
. In particular, given a homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
of degree
d
, if
f=\alpha(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1)
for homogeneous linear forms
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}
, then

\displaystyle e_{j}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all $j\neq d$},

\displaystyle e_{d}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{\alpha}}f.

Newton identities
are a central tool in this section; they relate the elementary symmetric polynomials and the
power sum
polynomial, defined as
p_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}):=x_{1}^{k}+\cdots+x_{n}^{k}
.

2.2 Proposition

(Newton Identities, see e.g.
[
Mac95
]
, Section I.2)
.

Let
n,k
be integers with
n\geq k\geq 1
. Then

k\cdot e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\;=\;\sum_{i\in[k]}\,(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\cdot p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\;.

In light of the
Remark

2.1
, the
Kc
model of computation is a sum of elementary symmetric polynomials. Shpilka
[
Shp02
]
studied a similar notion of circuit complexity called
s_{sym}
. For a polynomial
f
,
s_{sym}(f)
is defined as the smallest
m
such that
f=e_{d}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{m})
where
d=\deg(f)
and
\ell_{i}
are affine linear forms. It was proved in
[
Shp02
]
that
s_{sym}(f)
is always finite, moreover several upper and lower bounds for
s_{sym}(f)
were proven. The complexity
Kc
differs from
s_{sym}(f)
, as
Kc
can even be infinite.
In fact, the only homogeneous polynomials with finite
Kc
-complexity are powers of linear forms, as the following lemma shows.

2.3 Lemma
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
be a homogeneous polynomial such that
\textup{{Kc}}(f)<\infty
. Then
\textup{{Kc}}(f)=d
and
f
is a power of a linear form.

Proof.

If
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, then it is immediate that
\textup{{Kc}}(f)\geq\deg(f)
. Notice that for any linear form
\ell
, we have
\ell^{d}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\zeta^{i}\ell)-1
where
\zeta
is a primitive
d
-th root of
1
. This shows
\textup{{Kc}}(\ell^{d})\leq d
, hence equality holds.

Assume
f\in\mathbb{C}[x]_{d}
is a homogeneous polynomial with
\textup{{Kc}}(f)=m<\infty
. By definition
f=\alpha\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\right)
for some homogeneous linear forms
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]
. Write
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
. By
Remark

2.1
, we have,
e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\frac{1}{\alpha}f
and
e_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
for
j\neq d
.

First, observe
m=d
. Indeed, if
m>d
, we have
0=e_{m}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{m}
, which implies
\ell_{i}=0
for some
i
, in contradiction with the minimality of
m
. Since
\textup{{Kc}}(f)\geq\deg(f)
, we deduce
m=d
.

Now we show that if
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d})
satisfies
e_{1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\cdots=e_{d-1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
then
e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(-1)^{d-1}\cdot\ell_{d}^{d}
; in particular, by unique factorization, all
\ell_{i}
’s are equal up to scaling. Write
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d-1})
. We use induction on
j
to prove that
e_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})=(-1)^{j}\cdot\ell_{d}^{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,d-1
. For
j=1
, we have

0=e_{1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1})+\ell_{d}=e_{1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})+\ell_{d}

which proves the statement. For
j=2,\ldots,d-1
, consider the recursive relation

e_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=e_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})+\ell_{d}e_{j-1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}).

By assumption we have
e_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
and the induction hypothesis guarantees
e_{j-1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})=(-1)^{j-1}\cdot\ell_{d}^{j-1}
; we deduce
e_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})=-\ell_{d}\cdot(-1)^{j-1}\cdot\ell_{d}^{j-1}=(-1)^{j}\ell_{d}^{j}
which proves the statement. Finally, notice
f=\alpha e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\alpha\ell_{d}\cdot(-1)^{d-1}\cdot e_{d-1}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})=-\alpha\ell_{d}^{d}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

However, the model is complete if one allows approximations, as shown in
[
Kum20
]
. We introduce an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}]
: given two polynomials
f_{1},f_{2}
whose coefficients depend rationally on
\epsilon
, we write
f_{1}\simeq f_{2}
if
\lim_{\epsilon}f_{1},\lim_{\epsilon}f_{2}
are both finite and they coincide. We often use this notation with either
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
not depending on
\epsilon
: if, for instance,
f_{1}
does not depend on
\epsilon
, then
f_{1}\simeq f_{2}
means that
f_{2}=f_{1}+O(\epsilon)
.

2.4 Proposition

(
[
Kum20
]
)
.

For all homogeneous
f
we have

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq\deg(f)\cdot\mathsf{WR}(f)
.

Proof.

The proof is based on a construction by Shpilka
[
Shp02
]
. Let
\mathsf{WR}(f)=r
and write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\ell_{i}^{d}
.
Let
\zeta
be a primitive
d
-th root of unity.
Then one verifies that

f=-e_{d}(-\zeta^{0}\ell_{1},-\zeta^{1}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{1},\ldots\ldots,-\zeta^{0}\ell_{r},-\zeta^{1}\ell_{r},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})

and for all
0<i<d
we have

e_{i}(-\zeta^{0}\ell_{1},-\zeta^{1}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{1},\ldots\ldots,-\zeta^{0}\ell_{r},-\zeta^{1}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})=0.

Hence
f\simeq-\epsilon^{-d}\big(\big((1-\epsilon\zeta^{0}\ell_{1})\cdots(1-\epsilon\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})\big)-1\big)
.
Therefore

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq rd
.
∎

In fact, the following slightly stronger statement is true:

2.5 Proposition
.

For all homogeneous
f
we have

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq\deg(f)\cdot\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)
.

Proof.

Analogously to the proof in
proposition

2.4
, let
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)=r
and let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}
be linear forms depending rationally on
\epsilon
such that

f\simeq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\ell_{i}^{d}=-e_{d}(-\zeta^{0}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})
.
Moreover, for all
0<i<d
, we have

e_{i}(-\zeta^{0}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})=0
.

Choose
M
large enough so that for all
d<i\leq dr
we have that
\epsilon^{-Md}e_{i}(-\epsilon^{M}\zeta^{0}\ell_{1},\ldots,-\epsilon^{M}\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})\simeq 0
.
We obtain

f\simeq-\epsilon^{-Md}\big(\big((1-\epsilon^{M}\zeta^{0}\ell_{1})\cdots(1-\epsilon^{M}\zeta^{d-1}\ell_{r})\big)-1\big)
. Therefore

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq rd
.
∎

Proposition

2.4
and
Proposition

2.5
show that if
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)
is small then
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)
is small. However, there are polynomials with large Waring (border) rank but small Kumar complexity, such as products of linear forms. For instance
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})
is exponentially large: a lower bound of
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
can be easily shown by partial derivative methods, see e.g.
[
LT10
, Sec. 11]
,
[
CKW11
, Thm. 10.4]
. However every completely reducible form has small Kumar complexity:

2.6 Lemma
.

If
f=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}
is a product of homogeneous linear forms
\ell_{i}
, then
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)=d
.

Proof.

The lower bound is immediate because
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\geq\deg(f)
. For the upper bound, notice

f\simeq\epsilon^{d}\big(\big(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\epsilon^{-1}\ell_{i})\big)-1\big)
.
∎

The main result of this section is a converse of the above statements: informally, homogeneous polynomials with small border Waring rank and product of linear forms are the only homogeneous polynomials with small border Kumar complexity.
The following result explains the relation between border Waring rank and border Kumar’s complexity and completes the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

2.7 Theorem
.

If
f
is a product of homogeneous linear forms, then
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)=\deg(f)
.
For all other homogeneous
f
we have

\max\{\deg(f),\ \underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\}\ \ \leq\ \ \underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\ \ \leq\ \ \deg(f)\cdot\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f).

Proof.

The first statement is
lemma

2.6
.
The right inequality follows from
proposition

2.5
.
Clearly
\deg(f)\leq\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)
.
The inequality
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)
is a combination of
lemma

2.8
,

proposition

2.9
,
and
theorem

2.10

below.
∎

Note that in the definition of
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}
, the factor
\alpha
can be assumed to be a scalar times a power of
\epsilon
, because only the lowest power of
\epsilon
in
\alpha
would contribute to the limit. We distinguish three cases, depending on the sign of the exponent of
\epsilon
in
\alpha
.

•

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{+}(f)
is the smallest
m
such that
f\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{N}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\big)
for some
N\geq 1
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}]_{1}
; set
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{+}(f)=\infty
if such an
m
does not exist;

•

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{-}(f)
is the smallest
m
such that
f\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\big)
for some
M\geq 1
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}]_{1}
; set
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{-}(f)=\infty
if such an
m
does not exist;

•

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f)
is the smallest
m
such that
f\simeq\gamma\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\big)
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}]_{1}
; set
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f)=\infty
if such an
m
does not exist.

We observe that

\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)=\min\big\{\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{+}(f),\ \underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f),\ \underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{-}(f)\big\}
.

2.8 Lemma
.

For all homogeneous
f
, if
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{+}(f)
is finite, then
f
is a product of homogeneous linear forms.

Proof.

Let
f\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{N}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\big)
with
N\geq 1
.
Since
\epsilon^{N}\simeq 0
, we have
f\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})
, namely
f
is limit of a product of affine linear polynomials. The property of being completely reducible is closed, therefore we deduce that
f
is a product of affine linear polynomials. Since
f
is homogeneous, its factors are homogeneous as well.
∎

2.9 Proposition

(Newton Identities)
.

For all homogeneous
f
we have

\mathsf{WR}(f)\leq\textup{{Kc}}(f)=\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f)
.

Proof.

Let
d:=\deg(f)
. Suppose
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f)=m
and write

f\simeq f_{\epsilon}:=\gamma\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell_{i})-1\big)
. One can verify that if even one of the
\ell_{i}
diverges, then the
j
-th homogeneous part of
f_{\epsilon}
diverges, where
j
is the number of diverging
\ell_{i}
.
Hence all
\ell_{i}
converge and we can set
\epsilon
to zero.
Hence,
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{=}(f)=\textup{{Kc}}(f)
.
Now, since
f
is homogeneous, each homogeneous degree
i
part of
f_{\epsilon}
vanishes,
i<d
.
In other words,
e_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
for all
1\leq i<d
, where
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
. Hence
s(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
for
all
symmetric polynomials of degree
<d
.
Therefore the Newton identity
p_{d}=(-1)^{d-1}\cdot d\cdot e_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(-1)^{d+i-1}e_{d-i}\cdot p_{i}
gives that

e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
and
p_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
are same up to multiplication by a scalar.
Hence

\mathsf{WR}(f)\leq m
.
∎

2.10 Theorem

(Border Newton Identities)
.

For all homogeneous
f
:

\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}^{-}(f)
.

Proof.

Let
d:=\deg(f)
.
Let
f\simeq f_{\epsilon}:=\gamma\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\ell^{\prime}_{i})-1\big)
with
M\geq 1
.
From the convergence of
f_{\epsilon}
we deduce that for each
i
we have
\ell^{\prime}_{i}=\epsilon\ell_{i}
with
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon][\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}
, because otherwise the homogeneous degree
j
part diverges, where
j
is the number of
\ell^{\prime}_{i}
that do not satisfy this property.

Now, let
f_{\epsilon,j}
denote the homogeneous degree
j
part of
f_{\epsilon}
.
Since
f
is homogeneous of degree
d
, for
0\leq j<d
we have
f_{\epsilon,j}\simeq 0
. By expanding the product, observe that for all
0<j<d
we have
0\simeq f_{\epsilon,j}=\gamma\epsilon^{-M}e_{j}(\epsilon\ell_{1},\ldots,\epsilon\ell_{m})=\gamma\epsilon^{-M+j}e_{j}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
.
We now show by induction that for all
1\leq j<d
we have
\epsilon^{-M+j}p_{j}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})\simeq 0
.
This is clear for
j=1
, because
p_{1}=e_{1}
.
For the step from
j
to
j+1
we use Newton’s identities:

\textstyle p_{j+1}\;=\;(-1)^{j}\,(j+1)\,e_{j+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{j+i}e_{j+1-i}\cdot p_{i}.

Hence
\epsilon^{-M+(j+1)}p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\hskip 10000.0pt=(-1)^{j}\,(j+1)\,\underbrace{\epsilon^{-M+(j+1)}e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})}_{\simeq 0}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{j+i}\underbrace{\epsilon^{-M+(j+1)-i}e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})}_{\simeq 0}\cdot\underbrace{\epsilon^{M}}_{\simeq 0}\cdot\underbrace{\epsilon^{-M+i}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})}_{\simeq 0}\simeq 0.\hfilneg

This finishes the induction proof, now we use Newton’s identities again in the same way
to see that
\epsilon^{-M+d}p_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\simeq(-1)^{d-1}\cdot d\cdot\epsilon^{-M+d}e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
:

\epsilon^{-M+d}p_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(-1)^{d-1}\cdot d\cdot\epsilon^{-M+d}e_{d}(\boldsymbol{\ell})+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(-1)^{d-1+i}\underbrace{\epsilon^{-M+d-i}e_{d-i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})}_{\simeq 0}\cdot\underbrace{\epsilon^{M}}_{\simeq 0}\cdot\underbrace{\epsilon^{-M+i}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})}_{\simeq 0}.

We are done now, because
f\simeq f_{\epsilon,d}=\gamma\epsilon^{-M+d}e_{d}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{-M+d}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\cdot(-1)^{d-1}p_{d}(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
and
hence
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq m
.
∎

2.1
Linear approximations and Waring rank

We demonstrated the inequality
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}}(f)\leq\deg(f)\cdot\mathsf{WR}(f)

in
proposition

2.4
. In the proof of
proposition

2.4
, only “linear approximations” have been used; we prove here a converse of
proposition

2.4
in the restricted setting of linear approximation. Given a homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, let
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}(f)
be the smallest
m
such that there exist linear forms
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{1}
and
M\geq 1
such that
f\simeq\gamma\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon\ell_{i})-1\big)
.

2.11 Proposition
.

For any homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
, we have
\mathsf{WR}(f)\leq\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}(f)\leq d\cdot\mathsf{WR}(f)
.

Proof.

The inequality
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}(f)\leq d\cdot\mathsf{WR}(f)
is clear from the proof
of
proposition

2.4
, as there we obtained an expression of the
form described in the definition of
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}
. Suppose
\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}(f)=m
and write
f\simeq f_{\epsilon}:=\gamma\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon\ell_{i})-1\big)

with
M\geq 1
and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}
. It is immediate that
m\geq M
,
f=\gamma e_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
and
e_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
for
j<M
, where
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
. Via the Newton identity for the power sum polynomial, we have

\textstyle{p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(-1)^{M-1}Me_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell})+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}(-1)^{M+i-1}e_{M-i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\cdot p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell}).}

Since
e_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=0
for all
1\leq j<M
, we obtain:

\textstyle p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(-1)^{M-1}Me_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\frac{1}{\gamma}(-1)^{M-1}Mf.

We conclude
\mathsf{WR}(f)=\mathsf{WR}(p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell}))\leq\mathsf{WR}(p_{M})=m=\underline{\textup{{Kc}}^{-}_{1}}(f)
, as desired.
∎

3
Restricted binomials: debordering and lower bounds

In this section, we study restricted binomials. A binomial
\textup{bn}_{d}
is the polynomial
\textup{bn}_{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=P_{2,0}^{[d]}=x_{1}\ldots x_{d}+y_{1}\cdots y_{d}
.
Theorem

1.3
is based on the presentation of
P_{1,1}^{[d]}
and
P_{1,2}^{[d]}
as restrictions of the binomial
P_{2,0}^{[d]}
, which follows from the fact that both
x_{0}^{d}
and
x_{0}^{d}-x_{d+1}^{d}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{0}-\zeta^{i}x_{d+1})
are completely reducible; here
\zeta
is a primitive
d
-th root of
1
. Therefore, degenerations of
P_{1,1}^{[d]}
and
P_{1,2}^{[d]}
arise as limits of the sum of two products

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell_{i}(\epsilon)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\epsilon)\right)

where
\ell_{i}(\epsilon),\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\epsilon)
are linear forms depending rationally on
\epsilon
, and the second product is
restricted
, in the sense that, up to change of coordinates, it has either one or two variables.

In
section

3.1
, we deborder product-plus-power (
P_{1,1}^{[d]}
) and product-plus-two-powers models (
P_{1,2}^{[d]}
). In
section

3.2
, we show exponential gaps between product-plus-power, product-plus-two-powers, and binomials (in the affine sense). Identifying explicit polynomials which are hard to
approximate, and proving it remains a major template in algebraic and geometric complexity theory.
Often, proving lower bounds on the homogeneous model turns out to be easier than in its affine
model, because of the non-trivial cancellations in the latter model. However, in the restricted setting, we are able to show
optimal
lower bounds, see
theorem

3.15
&
theorem

3.16
.

3.1
Debordering: Characterizing special binomials

In this section we prove debordering results for product-plus-power and product-plus-two-powers models.
Our method applies also for more general computational model based on restricted binomials.
More specifically, we prove that polynomials obtained in the limit in our model have low border Waring rank.
One can then apply a debordering result for
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}
such as
\textup{{abpw}}(f)\leq\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)

[
BDI21
,
For14
]
or the results from
[
DGI+24
]
to get a complete debordering.

3.1 Definition

(Restricted binomial model)
.

We say that a homogeneous degree
d
polynomial
f
is in the
class
RB_{k}
if it can be presented as

f=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell_{i}+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell^{\prime}_{i}

for some linear forms
\ell_{i},\ell^{\prime}_{i}
such that
\mathrm{rank}(\ell^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\ell^{\prime}_{d})\leq k
.
We also define the corresponding approximate class
\overline{RB}_{k}
in the standard way: a homogeneous degree
d
polynomial
f
is in
\overline{RB}_{k}
if

f=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell_{i}(\epsilon)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\epsilon)\right)

(3.2)

for some
\ell_{i}(\epsilon),\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\epsilon)\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}]_{1}
such that
\mathrm{rank}(\ell^{\prime}_{1}(\epsilon),\dots,\ell^{\prime}_{d}(\epsilon))\leq k
for every
\epsilon\neq 0
.

The main theorem of this section is a debordering result of
\overline{RB}_{k}
in terms of border Waring rank.
Theorem

1.3
is a consequence of this result.

3.3 Theorem

(Debordering
\overline{RB}_{k}
)
.

Let
f
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
\overline{RB}_{k}
. Then either
f\in RB_{k}
, or
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq O(d^{3k+2})
.

To prove this theorem, we first need some basic lemmas which will be used in the proof. We will use non-homogeneous polynomials, so instead of Waring rank we will be working with the complexity of
\Sigma\Lambda\Sigma
-circuits.
Denote by
\Sigma^{[s]}\Lambda^{[e]}\Sigma
the class of (non-homogeneous) polynomials representable as a sum of
s
powers of affine linear forms with exponents not exceeding
e
, and by
\overline{\Sigma^{[s]}\Lambda^{[e]}\Sigma}
the corresponding class closed under approximation.
As the following lemma shows, for homogeneous polynomials this model is equal in power to border Waring rank.

3.4 Lemma
.

Let
f
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
. Then
f\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s]}\Lambda\Sigma}
if and only if
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq s

Proof.

Clearly, if
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq s
then
f\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s]}\Lambda\Sigma}
. For the converse, suppose
f\simeq\sum_{i\in[s]}(\alpha_{i}+\ell_{i})^{e_{i}}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}]
, and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}
. Taking the degree
d
part of each side, we obtain a border Waring rank decomposition
f\simeq\sum_{i\colon e_{i}\geq d}\binom{e_{i}}{d}\ell_{i}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{e_{i}-d}
with at most
s
summands.
∎

We recall a classical result on the border Waring rank of a binary monomial.

3.5 Proposition

(see, e.g.,
[
LT10
, Cor. 4.5]
)
.

If
a\leq b
, then
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(x^{a}y^{b})=a+1
.

The next lemma bounds the
\Sigma\Lambda\Sigma
complexity of a polynomial in terms of the complexity of polynomials obtained from it by substitution of variables.

3.6 Lemma

(Interpolation)
.

Let
f
be a polynomial of degree
d
such that
f(\gamma_{i},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s]}\wedge^{[e]}\Sigma}
for some distinct
\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{d}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
f\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s(d+1)^{3}]}\wedge^{[e+d]}\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Write
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=0}^{d}x_{1}^{j}f_{j}(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
.
By polynomial interpolation there exist
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
f_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{ij}f(\gamma_{i},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
.
By assumption,
f(\gamma_{i},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\simeq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{ij}^{e_{j}}
, where
\ell_{ij}
are affine linear forms with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}]
, and
e_{j}\leq e
.
Hence

f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{ij}\ell_{ij}^{e_{j}}\implies f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s(d+1)]}\wedge^{[e]}\Sigma}\;.

Note that for any affine linear polynomial
\ell
the polynomial
x_{1}^{j}\ell^{e}
can be approximated by a
\Sigma^{[d+1]}\wedge^{[e+j]}\Sigma
-circuit using the decomposition of the monomial
x^{j}y^{e}
with border Waring rank equal to
\min\{j+1,e+1\}\leq j+1\leq d+1
; this follows from
proposition

3.5
.
Therefore

x_{1}^{j}f_{j}\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s(d+1)^{2}]}\wedge^{[e+j]}\Sigma}
,
and

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{1}^{j}f_{j}\in\overline{\Sigma^{[s(d+1)^{3}]}\wedge^{[e+d]}\Sigma}
.
∎

Applying

3.6
several times we obtain the following result.

3.7 Corollary
.

Let

Additionally, we need the following statement similar to

3.8 Theorem
.

For any degree
d
polynomial

,
not necessarily homogeneous, suppose we have
f\simeq\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon a_{i})-\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon b_{i})\big)

for some linear forms
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon][\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}

with

.
Then
f\in\overline{\Sigma^{[2md]}\wedge^{[d]}\Sigma}

Proof.

Let
f_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-M}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon a_{i})-\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\epsilon b_{i})\big)
.
Denote by
f_{j}
and
f_{\epsilon,j}
the homogeneous degree
j

f
and
f_{\epsilon}
respectively.
Since

f_{j}\simeq f_{\epsilon,j}=\epsilon^{-M}\left(e_{j}(\epsilon a_{1},\ldots,\epsilon a_{m})-e_{j}(\epsilon b_{1},\ldots,\epsilon b_{m})\right)=\epsilon^{-M+j}\left(e_{j}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j}(\boldsymbol{b})\right),

\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
and similarly
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})

Note that since

converges,

for all

We now show by induction that for all

\displaystyle p_{j}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv

\displaystyle p_{j}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-j}\rangle

\displaystyle p_{j}(\boldsymbol{a})-p_{j}(\boldsymbol{b})\equiv

\displaystyle(-1)^{j-1}j\left(e_{j}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j}(\boldsymbol{b})\right)\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-j+1}\rangle

For the induction step from

{\textstyle p_{j+1}=(-1)^{j}(j+1)e_{j+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{j+i}e_{j+1-i}\cdot p_{i}.}

We obtain

\displaystyle p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})=

\displaystyle(-1)^{j}(j+1)\left(e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{j+i}\left(e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{a})\cdot p_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{b})\cdot p_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})\right).

(3.9)

\displaystyle p_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv

\displaystyle p_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-i}\rangle

\displaystyle e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv

\displaystyle e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-(j+1)+i}\rangle.

Since
M-j\leq M-i
and

\displaystyle p_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv

\displaystyle p_{i}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-j}\rangle

\displaystyle e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv

\displaystyle e_{j+1-i}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-j}\rangle.

From (
3.9

p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\equiv(-1)^{j}(j+1)\left(e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\right)\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-j}\rangle.

Weakening this to an equivalence
\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-(j+1)}\rangle

p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\equiv(-1)^{j}(j+1)\left(e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})-e_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\right)\equiv 0\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-(j+1)}\rangle,

or
p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{a})\equiv p_{j+1}(\boldsymbol{b})\bmod\langle\epsilon^{M-(j+1)}\rangle

Finally, we use the proved congruences to write an approximate decomposition of

which shows that

Note that

.

For any degree
d

Proof.

Let

be the homogeneous parts as in the proof of the preceding Theorem.

is the
(2d)
-th primitive root of unity. Further, it is easy to see that
\mathrm{rank}\left(\ell^{\prime}(\varepsilon)-\zeta\ell^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon),\cdots,\ell^{\prime}(\varepsilon)-\zeta^{2d-1}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon)\right)\leq 2
, for every
\varepsilon\neq 0
. Therefore, by definition
f\in\overline{RB}_{2}
. Using,
theorem

3.3
, we get that
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\leq O(d^{8})
.

This finishes the proof.
∎

3.2
Lower Bounds

In this section, we prove several exponential separations between related polynomials contained in the affine closure of binomials.

3.12 Lemma
.

The polynomial
P^{[d]}_{1,2}=\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}+x_{d+1}^{d}+x_{d+2}^{d}
cannot be written as a product of linear forms.

Proof.

For every homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
which is a product of linear forms, the space of first order partial derivatives has dimension at most
d
. But
\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}+x_{d+1}^{d}+x_{d+2}^{d}
clearly has
d+2
linearly independent partial derivatives.
∎

3.13 Lemma
.

The polynomial
P^{[d]}_{2,0}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}+\prod_{i=d+1}^{2d}x_{i}
cannot be written as a product of linear forms.

Proof.

It easily follows from a proof similar to that of
lemma

3.12
.
∎

3.14 Lemma
.

For the polynomial
P^{[d]}_{1,2}=\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}+x_{d+1}^{d}+x_{d+2}^{d}
, we have
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\geq 2^{\Omega(d)}
.

Proof.

Evaluating
x_{d+1}=x_{d+2}=0
, we obtain

\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(f)\geq\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(x_{1}\cdots x_{d})\geq\binom{d}{\lceil d/2\rceil}

where the second inequality follows computing the dimension of the space of partial derivatives of order
\lfloor d/2\rfloor
, see, e.g.,
[
LT10
, Prop. 11.6]
.
∎

For polynomials
f
and
g
(not necessarily homogeneous) we write

f\leq_{\textup{aff}}g

if there exists an affine linear map
A
with
f=g\circ A
.
We write
f\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}g

if there exist affine linear maps
A_{\epsilon}
with
f=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}g\circ A_{\epsilon}
.

3.15 Theorem

(First exp. gap theorem)
.

If
P_{1,2}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}P^{[e]}_{1,1}
, then
e\geq\exp(d)
.

We remark that by Kumar’s result
[
Kum20
]
, we know that there exists
e\leq\exp(d)
, such that
P_{1,2}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}P^{[e]}_{1,1}
. Therefore,
theorem

3.15
is
optimal
.

Proof of
theorem

3.15
.

Let
P_{1,2}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}P^{[e]}_{1,1}
. That means that there are affine linear forms
L_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\boldsymbol{x}]
such that
\prod_{i\in[d]}x_{i}+x_{d+1}^{d}+x_{d+2}^{d}+\epsilon\cdot S(\boldsymbol{x},\epsilon)=\prod_{i\in[e]}L_{i}+L_{e+1}^{e}
.
By substituting,
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}/x_{0}
, and multiplying both sides by
x_{0}^{e}
, we get that
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}+\epsilon\cdot\widehat{S}=\prod_{i\in[e]}\widehat{L}_{i}+\widehat{L}_{e+1}^{e}
, for homogeneous linear forms
\widehat{L}_{i}
, or, equivalently,
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq P^{[e]}_{1,1}
.

By
theorem

1.3
, we know that
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq P^{[e]}_{1,1}
implies either (i)
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}+\ell_{0}^{e}
, for some linear forms
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
, or (ii)
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]})=O(e^{5})
. We show that (i) is an impossibility while (ii) can happen only when
e\geq\exp(d)
.

Proof of Part (ii):
Fix a random
x_{0}=\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Note that, this implies that
P_{1,2}^{[d]}+\epsilon g=\sum_{i\in[k]}\ell_{i}^{e}
for some
affine forms
\widehat{\ell}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon^{\pm 1}][\boldsymbol{x}]
and
g\in\mathbb{C}[\epsilon][\boldsymbol{x}]
with
k\in O(e^{5})
. Since
P_{1,2}^{[d]}
is homogeneous, this also implies that
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(P_{1,2}^{[d]})\leq k
.
But then
lemma

3.14
implies that
k\geq 2^{\Omega(d)}
, which in turn implies that
e\geq 2^{\Omega(d)}
.

Proof of Part (i):
Let
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}+\ell_{0}^{e}
. The space of first order partials of the LHS has dimension at least
d+2
, while the one of the RHS has dimension at most
e+1
; since trivially
\prod_{i\in T}\ell_{i}
, for
T\subset[e]
, such that
|T|=e-1
, and
\ell_{0}^{e-1}
certainly span the space of single partial derivatives. Therefore,
e\geq d+1
. This will be important since we will use the fact that
e-d\geq 1
, in the below.

Further, we can assume that
x_{0}\nmid\ell_{0}
. Otherwise, say
\ell_{0}=c\cdot x_{0}
, for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
, which implies that
x_{0}^{e-d}\mid\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}
. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\ell_{i}=x_{0}
, for
i\in[e-d]
(we are assuming constants to be
1
, because we can always rescale and push the constants to the other linear forms). Therefore, RHS is divisible by
x_{0}^{e-d}
. By dividing it out and renaming the linear forms appropriately, we get

P_{1,2}^{[d]}\;=\;\prod_{i\in[d]}\widehat{\ell}_{i}+cx_{0}^{d}\;,

where
\widehat{\ell}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
. Further, we can put
x_{0}=0
. Note that,
x_{0}\nmid\widehat{\ell}_{i}
, for any
i
, since otherwise
x_{0}
divides RHS, but it doesn’t divide the LHS. After substituting
x_{0}=0
, we get that

P_{1,2}^{[d]}\;=\;\prod_{i\in[d]}\tilde{\ell}_{i}\;,

where
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+2}]\ni\tilde{\ell}_{i}=\widehat{\ell}_{i}\rvert_{x_{0}=0}\neq 0
. From
lemma

3.12
, it follows that this is not possible. A similar argument shows that
x_{0}\nmid\ell_{i}
, for any
i\in[d]
; because otherwise that implies
x_{0}\mid\ell_{0}
, and hence the above argument shows a contradiction.

Therefore, we assume that
x_{0}\nmid\ell_{i}
, for
i\in[0,d]
. Now, there are two cases – (i)
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{0}
, (ii)
x_{0}
does not appear in
\ell_{0}
.

If
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{0}
, then say
\ell_{0}=c_{0}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}
, for some
c_{0}\neq 0
. Note that
\widehat{\ell}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+2}]_{1}
is non-zero, since we assume that
x_{0}\nmid\ell_{0}
. Substitute
x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0}
(so that
\ell_{0}
vanishes). This implies:

(-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0})^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}\;=\;\prod_{i\in[e]}\,\widehat{\ell}_{i}\;,

where
\widehat{\ell}_{i}=\ell_{i}\rvert_{x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0}}
. Since LHS is non-zero, so is each
\widehat{\ell}_{i}
. Since, everything is homogeneous, and we have unique factorization, the above implies that up to renaming,
P_{1,2}^{[d]}=c\cdot\prod_{i\in[d]}\widehat{\ell}_{i}
, which is a contradiction by
lemma

3.12
.

If
x_{0}
does not appear in
\ell_{0}
, then there must exist an
i\in[e]
such that
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{i}
, otherwise RHS is
x_{0}
-free which is trivially a contradiction. We also know that
x_{0}
cannot divide
\ell_{i}
, by our assumption. So, say
\ell_{i}=c_{i}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{i}
, where
\widehat{\ell}_{i}
is
x_{0}
-free, and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
is a nonzero element. Substitute
x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{i}/c_{i}
, so that
\ell_{i}
vanishes. Since
\ell_{0}
is
x_{0}
-free, we immediately get that

(-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0})^{e-d}\cdot P_{1,2}^{[d]}\;=\;\ell_{0}^{e}\;.

Again, by unique factorization, we get that
P_{1,2}^{[d]}=c\cdot\ell_{0}^{d}
, for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
, which is a contradiction by
lemma

3.12
. This finishes the proof.
∎

3.16 Theorem

(Second exp. gap theorem)
.

If
P_{2,0}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}P_{1,2}^{[e]}
, then
e\geq\exp(d)
.

Proof.

Let
P_{2,0}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq_{\textup{aff}}P_{1,2}^{[e]}
. A similar formulation as above (in the previous theorem) gives us that

x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq P^{[e]}_{1,2}
.
By
theorem

3.11
, we know that
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}\trianglelefteq P_{1,2}^{[e]}
implies – either (i)
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}=g+h
, where
g=\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}
, for some linear forms
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}
, and
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(h)\leq 2
, or (ii)
\underline{\mathsf{WR}}(x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]})=O(e^{8})
. Similarly, as before, we show that (i) is an impossibility while (ii) can happen only when
e\geq\exp(d)
. Part (ii) proof is exactly to the argument in the proof of
theorem

3.15
.

To prove the Part (i), there are two cases – (a)
h=\ell_{0}^{e}+\ell_{e+1}^{e}
, for
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{1}
, or, (b)
h=\ell_{0}^{e-1}\cdot\ell_{e+1}
.

Case (a):
Let
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[e]}\,\ell_{i}+\ell_{0}^{e}+\ell_{e+1}^{e}
. We assume that
x_{0}
does not divide
\ell_{i}
, for some
i\in\{0,e+1\}
, and each
\ell_{i}
, for
i\in[e]
, otherwise, we can divide by the maximum power of
x_{0}
on both the sides.

The space of first order partial derivatives of the LHS has dimension at least
2d
whereas the one of the RHS has dimension at most
e+2
: since trivially
\prod_{i\in T}\ell_{i}
, for
T\subset[e]
, such that
|T|=e-1
, and
\ell_{0}^{e-1},\ell_{e-1}^{e}
certainly span the space of single partial derivatives. Therefore,
e\geq 2d-2
.

Now, we divide this into subcases:

(a1)
x_{0}
does not appear in
\ell_{i}
, for any
i\in[e]
,

(a2)
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{i}
, for some
i\in[e]
.

Case (a1):

x_{0}
does not appear in
\ell_{i}
, for
i\in[e]
. In that case, say
\ell_{0}=c_{0}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{0}
, and
\ell_{e+1}=c_{e+1}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}
, where
\widehat{\ell}_{0}
and
\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}
are
x_{0}
-free, and
c_{0},c_{e+1}
are constants (might be
0
as well, but both cannot be
0
since then RHS becomes
x_{0}
-free). Therefore, the coefficient of
x_{0}^{e-d}
(as a polynomial) in RHS is
\gamma_{0}\widehat{\ell}_{0}^{d}+\gamma_{e+1}\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}^{d}
, where
\gamma_{0}=\binom{e}{d}c_{0}^{e-d}
, and similarly
\gamma_{e+1}=\binom{e}{d}c_{e+1}^{e-d}
. Comparing with LHS, we get that
P_{2,0}^{[d]}=\gamma_{0}\widehat{\ell}_{0}^{d}+\gamma_{e+1}\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}^{d}
. Trivially, over
\mathbb{C}
,
\gamma_{0}\widehat{\ell}_{0}^{d}+\gamma_{e+1}\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}^{d}
is a product of linear forms, which is a contradiction, using
lemma

3.13
.

Case (a2):
If
x_{0}
appears in one of the
\ell_{i}
for
i\in[e]
, it can appear in two ways, either
\ell_{i}
is a constant multiple of
x_{0}
, or
\ell_{i}=c_{i}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{i}
, where
\widehat{\ell}_{i}
is a nonzero linear form which is
x_{0}
-free. Let
S_{1}\subseteq[e]
be such that
\ell_{i}=c_{i}\cdot x_{0}
, for
i\in S_{1}
, for some nonzero constant
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, and
S_{2}\subseteq[e]
be such that
\ell_{i}=c_{i}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{i}
, where
\widehat{\ell}_{i}
is nonzero.

Note that if
|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|<e-d
, then
x_{0}^{e-d}
cannot be contributed from the product and hence it only gets produced from
\ell_{0}^{e}+\ell_{e+1}^{e}
, and we get a contradiction in the same way as above. Hence, without loss of generality, assume that
|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|\geq e-d
.

If
S_{2}
is non-empty, say
j\in S_{2}
, then substitute
x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{j}/c_{j}
, so that
\ell_{j}
becomes
0
. This substitution gives us the following:

(-\widehat{\ell}_{j}/c_{j})^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}\;=\;\tilde{\ell}_{0}^{e}+\tilde{\ell}_{e+1}^{e}\;.

Since,
\tilde{\ell}_{0}^{e}+\tilde{\ell}_{e+1}^{e}
can be written as a product of linear forms, from the unique factorization, it follows that
f
must be a product of linear forms, which is a contradiction from
lemma

3.13
. Hence, we are done when
|S_{2}|
is non-empty.

If
S_{2}
is empty, since
|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|\geq e-d
by assumption, we have
|S_{1}|\geq e-d
. In particular,
x_{0}^{e-d}\mid
LHS
-\prod\ell_{i}\implies x_{0}^{e-d}\mid\ell_{0}^{e}+\ell_{e+1}^{e}=\prod_{i}(\ell_{0}-\zeta^{2i+1}\ell_{e+1})
, where
\zeta
is
2e
-th root of unity. Since,
e-d\geq 2
for
d\geq 4
, this simply implies that there are two indices
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
such that
\ell_{0}-\zeta^{i_{1}}\ell_{e+1}=c_{i_{1}}x_{0}
, and
\ell_{0}-\zeta^{i_{2}}\ell_{e+1}=c_{i_{2}}x_{0}
. Together, this implies that both
\ell_{0}
and
\ell_{e+1}
are multiples of
x_{0}
, which is a contradiction, since we assumed that
x_{0}
cannot divide each
\ell_{i}
, for
i\in[0,e+1]
. Hence, we are done with case (a).

Case (b):
Let
x_{0}^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}=\prod_{i\in[e]}\,\ell_{i}+\ell_{0}^{e-1}\cdot\ell_{e+1}
. We assume that
x_{0}
does not divide both
\ell_{i}
, for some
i\in[e]
, and one of the
\ell_{0}
or
\ell_{e+1}
, otherwise, we can divide by the maximum power
x_{0}
both side. Again, a similar argument shows that
e\geq 2d-2
.

Similarly, as before, we divide into subcases:
(b1)
x_{0}
does not appear in
\ell_{i}
, for any
i\in[e]
,

(b2)
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{i}
, for some
i\in[e]
.

Case (b1):
If
x_{0}
does not appear in the first product, i.e,. any of
\ell_{i}
, for
i\in[e]
, then it must appear in
\ell_{0}
(because if it only appears in
\ell_{e+1}
, the degree of
x_{0}
is
1
in RHS, a contradiction). Note that,
x_{0}\nmid\ell_{0}
(and similarly
\ell_{e+1}
), because otherwise, substituting
x_{0}=0
makes LHS
0
, while RHS remains
\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}
. Hence, let
\ell_{0}:=c_{0}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell_{0}}
, where
\widehat{\ell}_{0}
is
x_{0}
-free. Substitute
x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0}
, so that

(-\widehat{\ell}_{0}/c_{0})^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}\;=\;\prod_{i\in[e]}\ell_{i}\;.

This in particular implies that
P_{2,0}^{[d]}
is a product of linear forms, which is a contradiction by
lemma

3.13
.

Case (b2):
In this case, without loss of generality,
x_{0}
appears in
\ell_{1}
. Note that,
x_{0}
cannot divide
\ell_{1}
, because otherwise, it must divide LHS-
\prod_{i\in[e]}=\ell_{0}^{e-1}\ell_{e+1}
, which implies that
x_{0}
must divide one of the
\ell_{0}
or
\ell_{e+1}
, contradicting the minimality of
x_{0}
-division. Therefore,
\ell_{1}=c_{1}x_{0}+\widehat{\ell}_{1}
, where
c_{1}
is a nonzero constant, and
\widehat{\ell}_{1}
is a nonzero linear form which is
x_{0}
-free. Substitute
x_{0}=-\widehat{\ell}_{1}/c_{1}
, both side to get that

(-\widehat{\ell}_{1}/c_{1})^{e-d}\cdot P_{2,0}^{[d]}\;=\;\widehat{\ell}_{0}^{e-1}\widehat{\ell}_{e+1}\;.

Therefore, again by unique factorization, we get that
f
must a product of linear forms, which is a contradiction by
lemma

3.13
.

∎

4
Geometric complexity theory for product-plus-power

In this section, we study computational and invariant theoretic properties of the polynomial

P^{[d]}_{r,s}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{ki}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}^{d},

defined in
Section

1.3
; this is a polynomial of degree
d
in
rd+s
variables. The cases
(r,s)=(1,1),(1,2),(2,0),(0,r)
correspond, respectively, to the product-plus-power, the product-plus-two-powers, the binomial, and the power sum polynomial mentioned in the previous sections.

Theorem

4.2
determines the stabilizer of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
under the action of the group
\operatorname{GL}_{rd+s}
acting on the variables. The knowledge of the stabilizer
H=\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}}(P_{1,1}^{[d]})
allows us to determine the representation theoretic structure of the coordinate ring of the orbit of
P^{[d]}_{1,1}
, which is achieved in
Proposition

4.4
. Recall that the irreducible representations of
\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}
are indexed by partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
,
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...
, with
\ell(\lambda)\leq d+1
, see
section

4.2
.
Denote by
S_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{d+1})
the irreducible representation of type
\lambda
.
In this section we write
S^{d}V=S_{(d)}V
for the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
\dim V
variables.
For every integer
D
and every partition
\lambda
of
dD
, we obtain the following identity:

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])\;=\;\dim(S_{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{d+1})^{H}\;=\;\sum_{\delta=0}^{D}\ \sum_{\mu\vdash\delta d,\mu\preceq\lambda,\ell(\mu)\leq d}\ a_{\mu}(d,\delta)\,,

where
a_{\mu}(d,\delta)
is the
plethysm coefficients
, that is the multiplicity of
S_{\mu}(\mathbb{C}^{d+1})
in
S^{d}(S^{\delta}(V))
, see
Proposition

4.4
. We use this formula, and apply the
[
IK20
]
approach to lower bounds on
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P_{0,d}^{[d]}}])
,
to find a sequence of partitions where
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])<\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P_{0,d}^{[d]}}])
, see
Theorem

4.10
. This implies
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P_{0,d}^{[d]}}\not\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}
if
d\geq 3
.

We implement this approach explicitly and we determine via a computer calculation an abundance of multiplicity obstructions against generic polynomials, see
Appendix

A
.

In
Proposition

4.5
, we prove that
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
is polystable, in the sense of invariant theory. This guarantees the existence of a
fundamental invariant
, in the sense of
[
BI17
]
: in
Proposition

4.7
, we show a connection between the degree of this fundamental invariant and the Alon-Tarsi conjecture on Latin squares in combinatorics.

4.1
Stabilizer

Consider the action of the general linear group
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
on the homogeneous components of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, by linear change of variables, as described in
Section

1.3
. For a homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, write
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
for its stabilizer under this action. It is an immediate fact that
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
is a closed algebraic subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
. It may consists of several connected (irreducible) components: the
identity component
, denoted
\operatorname{Stab}^{0}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
is the connected component containing the identity;
\operatorname{Stab}^{0}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
is a closed, normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)

[
Ges16
, Lemma 2.1]
; the quotient
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)/\operatorname{Stab}^{0}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
is a finite group.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of an algebraic group
G
can be geometrically identified with the tangent space to
G
at the identity element. Moreover, if
G
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is naturally a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
; moreover
\mathfrak{g}
uniquely determined the identity component of
G
.

It is a classical fact that the Lie algebra of
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
is the annihilator of
f
under the Lie algebra action of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
; denote this annihilator by
\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}(f)
. Typically, in order to determine
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
, one first computes
\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}(f)
, which uniquely determines
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}^{0}(f)
. Then, one determines
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(f)
as a subgroup of the normalizer
N_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}^{0}(f)
. The last step is often challenging; some methods to do this systematically in simplified settings are presented in
[
GG15
,
Ges16
,
GIP17
]
.

First, we record a general result regarding the stabilizer of sums of polynomials in disjoint sets of variables. This is the symmetric version of
[
CGL+21
, Thm. 4.1(i)]
. We say that a polynomial
f\in S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
concise
(in
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}
) if the first order partials of
f
are linearly independent.

4.1 Lemma
.

Let
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
and let
f\in\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]_{d}=S^{d}V
be a homogeneous polynomial with
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, where
f_{i}\in S^{d}V_{i}
are both concise, with
d\geq 3
. Then

(i)

\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V)}(f_{1})=\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{1})}(f_{1})\oplus\textnormal{Hom}(V_{2},V)
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V)}(f_{1}+f_{2})=\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{1})}(f_{1})\oplus\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{2})}(f_{2})
.

Proof.

For both statements, the inclusion of the right-hand term into the left-hand term is clear. We prove the reverse inclusion.

For
X\in\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, write
X=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}X_{ij}
, with
X_{ij}\in\textnormal{Hom}(V_{i},V_{j})
.

The proof of (i) amounts to showing that if
X\in\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V)}(f_{1})
, then
X_{12}=0
and
X_{11}\in\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{1})}(f_{1})
. Suppose
X.f_{1}=0
. Notice
X.f_{1}=X_{11}.f_{1}+X_{12}.f_{1}
; here
X_{11}.f_{1}\in S^{d}V_{1}
and
X_{12}.f_{1}\in V_{2}\otimes S^{d-1}V_{1}
. In particular, both terms must vanish. The term
X_{12}.f_{1}
is a sum of at most
\dim V_{2}
linearly independent elements, each of which is a linear combination of first order partials of
f_{1}
. Therefore, all such linear combinations must be
0
, and since
f_{1}
is concise in
S^{d}V_{1}
, we obtain
X_{12}=0
. The condition
X_{11}.f_{1}=0
is, by definition, equivalent to
X_{11}\in\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{1})}(f_{1})
. This conclude the proof of (i).

To prove (ii), we show that if
X\in\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V)}(f)
, then
X_{12}=0
,
X_{21}=0
and
X_{ii}\in\mathfrak{ann}_{\mathfrak{gl}(V_{i})}(f_{i})
. Suppose
X.f=0
. We have
X.f=(X_{11}+X_{12}).f_{1}+(X_{21}+X_{22}).f_{2}
. Notice

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{11}f_{1}\in S^{d}V_{1},&&X_{12}f_{1}\in V_{2}\otimes S^{d-1}V_{1},\\
X_{21}f_{2}\in V_{1}\otimes S^{d-1}V_{2},&&X_{22}f_{2}\in S^{d}V_{2}.\end{array}

Since
d\geq 3
, the four terms are linearly independent, hence they all must vanish individually. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now determine the stabilizer of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
. Let
\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}}
denote the subgroup of diagonal elements in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}
. We use the
wreath product
notation: given a group
G
, the wreath product
G\wr\mathfrak{S}_{k}
for the semidirect product
G^{\times k}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{k}
where
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
acts on the direct product
G^{\times k}
by permuting the direct factors; we refer to
[
Rob96
, Sec. 1.6]
for details on this construction.

4.2 Theorem
.

For
d\geq 3
and for every
r,s
, we have

\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})=([\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}]\wr\mathfrak{S}_{r})\times(\mathbb{Z}_{d}\wr\mathfrak{S}_{s});

each copy of
\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}
acts by rescaling and permuting the variables in one of the
r
sets
\{x_{ji}:i=1,\ldots,d\}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
; the group
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
permutes (set-wise) these sets; the group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\wr\mathfrak{S}_{s}
acts by rescaling (by a
d
-th root of
1
) and permuting the variables in the set
\{y_{i}:i=1,\ldots,s\}
.

Proof.

It is clear that the group on the right-hand side is contained in the stabilizer
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
. We show the reverse inclusion.

First, we determine the identity component of
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
. By
Lemma

4.1
, the annihilator of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
in
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
is the direct sum of the annihilators of its summands. This guarantees that the identity component of the stabilizer is the product of the identity components for the summands of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
. The identity component for each square-free monomial is a copy of
\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}
, see, e.g.,
[
Lan17
, Sec. 7.1.2]
. The identity component of each power is trivial. Therefore, we deduce
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}^{0}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})=({\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}})^{\times r}
.

Since
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}^{0}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
is a normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
, we have

\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})\subseteq N_{\operatorname{GL}_{rd+s}}({\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}}^{\times r})=([\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}]\wr\mathfrak{S}_{r})\rtimes Q

where
Q
is the parabolic subgroup stabilizing the subspace spanned by the
x_{ij}
variables; here
N_{\operatorname{GL}_{rd+s}}(=)
denotes the normalizer subgroup.

In order to determine the discrete component, we follow the same argument as the one used for the power sum polynomial
P^{[d]}_{0,s}
in
[
Lan17
, Section 8.12.1]
. In particular,
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
stabilizes the Hessian determinant of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
, up to scaling. A direct calculation shows that this Hessian determinant, up to scaling, is

H=(\prod_{i,j}x_{ij}\prod_{k}y_{k})^{d-2}.

Unique factorization implies that
\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})\cap Q\subseteq\mathbb{T}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{s}
, where
\mathbb{T}
is the torus of diagonal matrices acting on the
y_{j}
variables. Hence this subgroup commutes with
[\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}]\wr\mathfrak{S}_{r}
and we deduce

\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}(V)}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})\cap Q=\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{s}}(y_{1}^{d}+\cdots+y_{s}^{d})=\mathbb{Z}_{d}\wr\mathfrak{S}_{s}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

In the context of geometric complexity theory it is important to know if the polynomial is characterized by its stabilizer
[
MS08
]
.
While this property fails for the polynomials
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
, a slightly weaker statement is true — every polynomial stabilized by
\operatorname{Stab}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
is in the orbit of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
or a very special restriction of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
.
This is similar to the properties of minrank tensors and slice rank tensors considered in
[
BIL+19
]
.

4.3 Theorem
.

If a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{11},\dots,x_{dr},y_{1},\dots,y_{s}]_{d}
is stabilized by
\operatorname{Stab}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
, then

f=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\textstyle\prod}_{j=1}^{d}x_{ji}+\beta\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}^{d},

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Partition the set of variables into the subsets
X_{i}=\{x_{1i},\dots,x_{di}\}
and
Y_{i}=\{y_{i}\}
.
Note that
\operatorname{Stab}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})
contains the transformation which scales all variables in one of the subsets by a
d
-th root of unity, acting as identity on all other variables.
It follows that each monomial of
f
contains variables from only one of the subsets, because if this was not the case then the transformation described above multiplies the monomial by a coefficient different from
1
.
Thus we have

f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}(x_{1i},\dots,x_{di})+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}y_{i}^{d}.

Since
f
is fixed under the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{s}
permuting
y_{1},\dots,y_{s}
, the coefficients
\beta_{i}
are all equal.
Since
f
is fixed under the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
permuting the subsets
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
, all the polynomials
f_{i}
also coincide.

Finally, the stabilizer group contains the transformations scaling
x_{j1}
by
\lambda
and
x_{k1}
by
\lambda^{-1}
.
This transformation scales a monomial
x_{j1}^{p_{j}}x_{k1}^{p_{k}}\dots
by
\lambda^{p_{j}-p_{k}}
. It follows that each monomial of
f_{1}
must have the same degree with respect to each variable, that is,
f_{1}=\alpha\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j1}
.
∎

4.2
Multiplicities in the coordinate ring of the orbit

A
partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
is a finite non-increasing sequence of nonnegative integers. We write
\ell(\lambda):=\max\{i\mid\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}
, and
\lambda\vdash D
means
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=D
.
To each partition
\lambda
we associate its Young diagram, which is a top-left justified array of boxes with
\lambda_{i}
boxes in row
i
. For example, the Young diagram of
\lambda=(4,4,3)
is

.
The transpose of the Young diagram is obtained by switching rows and columns.
Denote the partition corresponding to this Young diagram by
\lambda^{t}
, for example
(4,4,3)^{t}=(3,3,3,2)
.
A group homomorphism
\varrho:\operatorname{GL}_{D}\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
, where
V
is a finite dimensional complex vector space, is called a
representation
of
\operatorname{GL}_{D}
. A representation is
polynomial
if each entry of the matrix corresponding to the linear map
\varrho(g)
is given by a polynomial in the entries of the elements of
\operatorname{GL}_{D}
.
A linear subspace that is closed under the group operation is called a
subrepresentation
.
A representation with only the two trivial subrepresentations is called
irreducible
.
The irreducible polynomial representations of
\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}
are indexed by partitions
\lambda
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq d+1
, see for example
[
Ful97
, Ch. 8]
. Denote by
S_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{d+1})
the irreducible representation of type
\lambda
.
For a
\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}
-representation
V
, write
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(V)
to denote the multiplicity of
\lambda
in
V
, i.e., the dimension of the space of equivariant maps from
S_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{d+1})
to
V
, or equivalently, the number of summands of isomorphism type
\lambda
in any decomposition of
V
into a direct sum of irreducible representations.

In this section, we consider the representations given by the homogeneous components of the coordinate ring of
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}\cdot P_{1,1}^{[d]}}
. By
Theorem

4.2
, the stabilizer of
P_{1,1}^{[d]}
under the action of
\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}
is
H:=\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}}(P^{[d]}_{1,1})\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d})
.

The stabilizer is used to determine the multiplicities in the coordinate ring of the group orbit
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])
. It is a general fact that
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}]\simeq\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}]^{H}
: in other words the coordinate ring of the orbit coincides with the subring of
H
-invariant elements in the coordinate ring of the group
\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}
. This ring of
H
-invariants can be determined using the Algebraic Peter-Weyl Theorem
[
GW09
, Thm. 4.2.7]
, a powerful tool that allows us to compute the relevant multiplicities. In turn, we have

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])=\dim(S_{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{d+1})^{H}.

We determine the dimension of these invariant spaces by classical representation branching rules, see
Proposition

4.4
.

For partitions
\mu
and
\lambda
, define
\mu\preceq\lambda
if and only if the Young diagram of
\mu
is contained in the one of
\lambda
, (that is for every
i
, one has
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
) and the skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
given by the difference has at most 1 box in each column (that is
\lambda^{t}_{i}-\mu^{t}_{i}\leq 1
). In other words,
\lambda
can be obtained from
\mu
by adding a suitable number of boxes, with no two of them in the same column.
The plethysm coefficient is defined to be the multiplicity
a_{\mu}(d,D):=\textup{mult}_{\mu}(S^{d}(S^{D}(\mathbb{C}^{N})))
, and it does not depend on
N
as long as
N\geq d
.

4.4 Proposition
.

For
\lambda\vdash dD
we have

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])=\dim(S_{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{d+1})^{H}=\sum_{\delta=0}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash\delta d\\
\mu\preceq\lambda\\
\ell(\mu)\leq d\end{subarray}}a_{\mu}(d,\delta).

Proof.

(S_{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{d+1})^{H}=(S_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{d})\downarrow^{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}}_{\operatorname{GL}_{1}\times\operatorname{GL}_{d}})^{\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Pieri's rule}}}{{=}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\preceq\lambda\\
\ell(\mu)\leq d\end{subarray}}(S^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}\mathbb{C}^{1})^{\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\otimes(S_{\mu}\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}},

where Pieri’s rule is a well-known decomposition rule, see for example
[
FH91
, p. 80, Exe. 6.12]
.
Now,
\dim((S^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}\mathbb{C}^{1})^{\mathbb{Z}_{d}})=1
if and only if
|\lambda|-|\mu|
is a multiple of
d
if and only if
|\mu|
is a multiple of
d
.
Otherwise it is 0.
Hence

\dim(S_{\lambda}V)^{H}=\sum_{\delta=0}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash\delta d\\
\mu\preceq\lambda\\
\ell(\mu)\leq d\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\dim(S_{\mu}\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}}}_{=a_{\mu}(d,\delta)}

The last underbrace equality is Gay’s theorem
[
Gay76
]
.
∎

The condition that
\ell(\mu)\leq d
is not necessary, because if
\ell(\mu)>d
, then
a_{\mu}(d,\delta)=0
.

A computer calculation shows that this indeed gives multiplicity obstructions in the sense of

4.3
Polystability

A polynomial
f\in S^{d}V
is called
polystable
if its
\operatorname{SL}(V)
-orbit is closed.
Polystability is an important property in GCT, as it implies the existence of a
fundamental invariant
that connects the
\operatorname{GL}
-orbit with the
\operatorname{GL}
-orbit closure, see
[
BI17
, Def. 3.9 and Prop. 3.10]
. This connection can be used to exhibit multiplicity obstructions, as was done in
[
IK20
]
.

4.5 Proposition
.

Let
d\geq 2
. The polynomial
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
is polystable, i.e., the orbit
\operatorname{SL}_{rd+s}P^{[d]}_{r,s}
is closed.

Proof.

If
d=2
, then
P^{[2]}_{r,s}
is a polynomial of degree
2
defining a quadratic form of maximal rank. This is polystable.

Suppose
d\geq 3
. A criterion for polystability is given in
[
BI17
, Prop. 2.8]
, based on works of Hilbert, Mumford, Luna, and Kempf.

In order to apply this criterion, consider the group
R=\operatorname{Stab}(P^{[d]}_{r,s})\cap\mathbb{T}
, where
\mathbb{T}
denotes the torus of diagonal matrices in
\operatorname{GL}_{rd+s}
, in the basis defined by the variables. By
Theorem

4.2
, we deduce
R=(\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d}})^{\times r}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{\times s}
. This is a group consisting entirely of diagonal matrices and it is easy to verify that its centralizer in
\operatorname{SL}_{d+1}
coincides with
\mathbb{T}^{\operatorname{SL}_{d+1}}
. This proves the first property of the criterion.

For the second property, consider the exponent vectors of the monomials appearing in
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
. For a monomial
m
, write
\mathrm{wt}(m)
for its exponent vector. It is immediate to verify that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{wt}(x_{i1}\cdots x_{id})+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{wt}(y_{j}^{d})=(1,\ldots,1);

this shows that the vector
(1,\ldots,1)
lies in the convex cone generated by the exponent vectors of the monomials of
P^{[d]}_{r,s}
. This proves the second part of the criterion and concludes the proof.
∎

proposition

4.5
reduces to the following in the special case
r=s=1
:

4.6 Corollary
.

Let
d\geq 2
. The product-plus-power polynomial

is polystable, i.e., the orbit
\operatorname{SL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}

4.4
Fundamental invariants and the Alon-Tarsi conjecture

The
fundamental invariant

\Phi
of a polystable polynomial
f\in S^{D}V
is the unique (up to scaling) smallest degree
\operatorname{SL}(V)
-invariant function in
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}(V)f]}
, see Def. 3.8 in
[
BI17
]
.
It describes the connection between the orbit and the orbit-closure of
f
: more formally, the coordinate ring of the orbit
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}(V)f]
is canonically isomorphic to the localization at
\Phi
of the coordinate ring of the orbit-closure, that is
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}(V)f}]_{\Phi}
; see
[
BI17
, Pro. 3.9]
.
This connection can be used to exhibit multiplicity obstructions, as was done in
[
IK20
]
.

It is known that for even
d
the orbit closure

\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d}(x_{1}\cdots x_{d})}
of a squarefree monomial
has fundamental invariant of degree
d
if and only if the Alon-Tarsi conjecture on Latin squares holds for
d
; see
[
KL15
]
and
[
BI17
, Prop. 3.26]
; otherwise the fundamental invariant has higher degree. In this section we show an analogous result for the orbit closure
\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}\cdots x_{d}+x_{d+1}^{d})}
: if
d
is even this orbit closure has fundamental invariant of degree
d+1
if and only if the Alon-Tarsi conjecture on Latin squares holds for
d
; otherwise the fundamental invariant has higher degree.

.

Let

be even.

Proof.

We follow the presentation in

For a partition
\lambda

.

For two partitions

. We write
a\times b
for the partition
(b,b,\ldots,b)
of
ab
.
For example, if
d=3
, then the Young diagram to
\lambda:=(5d-1,1)+((d+1)\times(10d))
is the following:

This section is devoted to proving the following result, which is a restatement of
Theorem

1.4
.

4.10 Theorem
.

Let
d\geq 3
be even, and let
\lambda:=(5d-1,1)+\big((d+1)\times(10d)\big)
. Then we have representation theoretic multiplicity obstructions:

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\overline{\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}]})\leq 4<5=\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\overline{\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d+1}^{d})]}),

and hence

\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d+1}^{d})}\not\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}
.

We point out that these obstructions of
Theorem

4.10
are only based on the symmetries of the two polynomials as in
[
IK20
]
.

The upper and the lower bound are proved independently, see
Proposition

4.11
and
Proposition

4.12
, which proves the theorem. Fix the following notation:
\kappa:=(5d-1,1)
,
\blacksquare:=(d+1)\times(10d)
,

\square:=d\times(10d)
,

\Delta:=|\square|/d=10d
,
and

\lambda:=\kappa+\blacksquare
.

4.11 Proposition
.

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}])\leq\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])=\textup{mult}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])=4
.

Proof.

The ring
\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}]
is
a localization of the ring
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}]
, see
[
BI17
]
, which implies
\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}}])\leq\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])
.
We observe that
a_{\nu+\square}(d,i+\Delta)=a_{\nu}(d,i)
, because
\square
has an even number of columns and exactly
d
rows.
Then we calculate:

\displaystyle\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}P^{[d]}_{1,1}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta=0}^{5}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash\delta d\\
\mu\preceq\lambda\\
\ell(\mu)\leq d\end{subarray}}a_{\mu}(d,\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{(d)+\square}(d,1+\Delta)+a_{(d-1,1)+\square}(d,1+\Delta)+a_{(2d)+\square}(d,2+\Delta)

\displaystyle+a_{(2d-1,1)+\square}(d,2+\Delta)+a_{(3d)+\square}(d,3+\Delta)+a_{(3d-1,1)+\square}(d,3+\Delta)

\displaystyle+a_{(4d)+\square}(d,4+\Delta)+a_{(4d-1,1)+\square}(d,4+\Delta)+a_{(5d-1,1)+\square}(d,5+\Delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{(d)}(d,1)+a_{(d-1,1)}(d,1)+a_{(2d)}(d,2)+a_{(2d-1,1)}(d,2)+a_{(3d)}(d,3)

\displaystyle+a_{(3d-1,1)}(d,3)+a_{(4d)}(d,4)+a_{(4d-1,1)}(d,4)+a_{(5d-1,1)}(d,5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4,

because
a_{(nm)}(n,m)=1
, and
a_{(nm-1,1)}(n,m)=0
, because
(nm-1,1)
is of hook shape, see
[
BI18
, Prop. 19.3.20]
. Note that there is no summand
a_{(5d)}(d,5)
and no summand
a_{(0)}(d,0)
,
because
(5d)\not\preceq(5d-1,1)
, and
(0)\not\preceq(5d-1,1)
.
∎

4.12 Proposition
.

\textup{mult}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d+1}^{d})}])\geq 5
.

Proof.

We use the Main Technical Theorem 4.2 from
[
IK20
]
.
Consider all partitions
\varrho
of 5, and observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}2\lceil\frac{\varrho_{i}}{2(d-2)}\rceil\leq 10
.
In the notation of
[
IK20
]
, we set
e_{\Xi}:=10
, which is exactly how many
(d+1)\times d
blocks form
\blacksquare
.

For a partition
\varrho\vdash_{m}D
the
frequency notation

\widehat{\varrho}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
is defined via

\widehat{\varrho}_{i}:=|\{j\mid\varrho_{j}=i\}|
.
For example, the frequency notation of
\varrho=(3,3,2,0)
is
\widehat{\varrho}=(0,1,2,0)
.
We observe that
|\varrho|=\sum_{i}i\widehat{\varrho}_{i}
.
We first use Theorem 4.1 from
[
IK20
]
(with adjusted notation):

Let
m:=d+1
,
D:=5
,
\kappa=(5d-1,1)\vdash_{m}Dd
.
Define

b(\kappa,\varrho,d,D):=\sum_{\mu^{1},\mu^{2},\ldots,\mu^{D}\atop\mu^{i}\vdash di\widehat{\varrho}_{i}}c_{\mu^{1},\mu^{2},\ldots,\mu^{D}}^{\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{D}a_{\mu^{i}}(\widehat{\varrho}_{i},i\cdot d).

Then

\textup{mult}_{\kappa}\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{m}(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{m}^{d})]=\sum_{\varrho\vdash_{m}D}b(\kappa,\varrho,d,D).

For the multi-Littlewood-Richardson coefficient to be nonzero, it is necessary that all
\mu^{i}\subseteq(5d-1,1)
, so each
\mu^{i}
is either a single row or a hook
(\widehat{\varrho}_{i}\cdot i\cdot d-1,1)
.
But
a_{nm-1,1}(n,m)=0
and
a_{nm}(n,m)=1
, so we can assume that the sum has only the summand with
\mu^{i}=(\widehat{\varrho}_{i}\cdot i\cdot d)
and the product of plethysm coefficients is 1.
Hence, the multi-Littlewood-Richardson coefficient counts the number of semistandard tableaux of shape
(5d-1,1)
and content
(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{5})
.

It is instructive to look at all possible
\widehat{\varrho}
:

\widehat{(1,1,1,1,1)}=(5)
,

\widehat{(2,1,1,1)}=(3,1)
,

\widehat{(2,2,1)}=(1,2)
,

\widehat{(3,1,1)}=(2,0,1)
,

\widehat{(3,2)}=(0,1,1)
,

\widehat{(4,1)}=(1,0,0,1)
,

\widehat{(5)}=(0,0,0,0,1)
.
We observe that
\widehat{\varrho}
has exactly two nonzero entries in 5 cases, and only one nonzero entry in 2 cases. There are no semistandard tableaux of shape
(5d-1,1)
with only one entry, and there is exactly one semistandard tableaux of shape
(5d-1,1)
with two symbols and fixed content.
Hence

\textup{mult}_{\kappa}\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d+1}^{d})]=5.

Note that this argument works indeed for all
d\geq 3
, even though for
d=3
we do not have
\varrho=(1,1,1,1,1)
in the sum (because it has more than
d+1=4
rows, but its contribution is zero anyway).

We now apply Theorem 4.2 from
[
IK20
]
, which implies

\textup{mult}_{\blacksquare+\kappa}\mathbb{C}[\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{d+1}(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{d+1}^{d})}]\geq 5.\qed
