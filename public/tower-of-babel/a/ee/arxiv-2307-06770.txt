Current fluctuations in a partially asymmetric simple exclusion process with a defect particle
Ivan Lobaskin, Martin R Evans, Kirone Mallick

Abstract
We study an exclusion process on a ring comprising a free defect particle in a bath of normal particles. The model is one of the few integrable cases in which the bath particles are partially asymmetric. The presence of the free defect creates localized or shock phases according to parameter values. We use a functional approach to Bethe equations resulting from a nested Bethe ansatz to calculate exactly the mean currents and diffusion constants. The results agree very well with Monte-Carlo simulations and reveal the main modes of fluctuation in the different phases of the steady state.



I
Introduction

Models of interacting driven diffusive particles are a natural effective description for many systems found in nature, particularly in biology.
Cases to which such models have been applied include the motion of RNA polymerase during DNA translation
MacDonald et al. 1968
and ribosome dynamics in mRNA translation
Szavits-Nossan and Evans 2020
, traffic flow on a busy street
Wolf et al. 1996
;
Chowdhury et al. 2000
, and driven colloids in a narrow channel
Cividini et al. 2017
;
Miron et al. 2020
;
Miron et al. 2021
.
Moreover, these models have been shown to have links to many other problems in statistical physics, including disordered polymers in random media
Halpin-Healy and Zhang 1995
, surface growth models
Meakin et al. 1986
(notably, some of the models are known to lie within the KPZ universality class
Barabási and Stanley 1995
), diffusion in strongly anisotropic materials
Richards 1977
, equations in fluid dynamics, such as the Burgers equation
Rost 1981
, and certain combinatorial problems
Wood et al. 2020
.

Both in the mathematical and physics literature, there has been focus on one-dimensional systems, for which there are powerful exact methods.
In particular, the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) has become the prototype of driven diffusive systems.
The simplicity of the ASEP has allowed for many exact results for its stochastic dynamics to be derived.
See, for instance
Derrida 1998
;
Schütz 2001
;
Blythe and Evans 2007
;
Derrida 2007
;
Chou et al. 2011
for reviews.

A good understanding of fluctuations in such minimal models is important for several reasons.
As the systems to which these models are applied to, such as traffic on a highway, typically contain many fewer particles than conventional equilibrium systems, fluctuations may be important to account for finite-size effects.

In particular, analyzing the system at a microscopic level allows one to derive the properties of fluctuations without postulating their form, as would need to be done if starting from a hydrodynamic picture.
This type of microscopic analysis is especially relevant as these models are far from equilibrium, which means that standard tools for describing fluctuations, such as Onsager-Machlup theory, are not applicable. Conversely, one can use exact results from microscopic dynamics to see whether general results can be derived for nonequilibrium systems from first principles.

The two main approaches that have emerged in the literature for calculating current fluctuations in ASEPs exactly are matrix product states and the Bethe ansatz.
The matrix product approach was initially used to describe the spatial structure and mean current in the steady state
Derrida et al. 1993a
.
It has been extended to fluctuations in some cases
Derrida et al. 1993b
;
Derrida and Mallick 1997
, but this generalization has proved to be quite difficult.

On the other hand, the Bethe ansatz approach allows for direct calculations of the full current statistics.
To be precise, it is used to calculate the scaled cumulant generating function of particle displacement, which can be done to all orders in some simple cases
Derrida and Lebowitz 1998
;
Prolhac 2010
.

In this paper, we investigate a partially asymmetric simple exclusion process (PASEP) with a defect particle that has priority in the dynamics.
Recently, the steady state of this model was solved using a matrix product ansatz and the mean current at long times was calculated
Lobaskin et al. 2022
.
Moreover, it has also recently been shown that it can be solved using a coordinate Bethe ansatz
Lobaskin et al. 2023
.
Building on these results, we use a functional Bethe ansatz to re-derive the mean current and calculate exactly the current fluctuations around the mean, which can be related to the diffusion constant.
We emphasize that this is the first time the Bethe ansatz has been used to calculate current statistics in a partially asymmetric process with a phase transition.
As such, the calculations presented here involve a combination of the techniques developed for the homogeneous partially asymmetric case and totally asymmetric case with a defect.

The importance of the Bethe ansatz to the ASEP was first appreciated when it was realized that its transition rate matrix (Markov operator) has a very similar structure to that of a quantum spin chain Hamiltonian
Alexander and Holstein 1978
;
Dhar 1987
.
Indeed, both of them are naturally expressed as tensor products of Pauli matrices.
Consequently, the well-known Bethe ansatz techniques from the quantum spin chain case could be carried over to study the spectral properties of the asymmetric exclusion process
Dhar 1987
;
Gwa and Spohn 1992
;
Kim 1995
;
de Gier and Essler 2005
.
Furthermore, by using a nested Bethe ansatz, Bethe equations have been derived for a PASEP with particles of different sizes
Alcaraz and Bariev 1999
and multi-species hierarchical PASEPs
Alcaraz and Bariev 2000a
;
Alcaraz and Bariev 2000b
.
However, to our knowledge, the calculation of current statistics beyond the mean in these latter models remains an open problem.

A major technical advancement was a modification of the time evolution problem that allowed current statistics to be calculated easily
Derrida and Lebowitz 1998
.
It was shown that a deformation of the transition rate matrix that corresponds to conditioning on a large current gives the time evolution of the total particle displacement.
As this satisfies a large deviation principle in the long time limit, the cumulant generating function of the current in the steady state could thus be calculated using the Bethe ansatz.

This method was used to calculate the large deviation functions of the current in the TASEP
Derrida and Lebowitz 1998
, the TASEP with a defect particle
Derrida and Evans 1999
and the PASEP
Prolhac and Mallick 2008
.
For the PASEP, it proved to be useful to reformulate the problem as a functional Bethe ansatz
Prolhac and Mallick 2008
;
Prolhac 2008
;
Prolhac 2010
.
This simplified calculations to the extent that allowed the cumulants to all orders to be formally expressed in terms of combinatorial objects
Prolhac 2010
.
The case considered in this paper is an extension of these results to the PASEP with a defect particle, which requires a generalization of the methods for those earlier cases.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we define the model, review the known results obtained using matrix product states and the coordinate Bethe ansatz for this model, and state the new results for the diffusion constant.
In Sec.
III
, we reformulate the Bethe equations in a functional form, which allows the cumulants of the current to be calculated directly.
We perform these calculations in Sec.
IV
and Sec.
V
.
In Sec.
IV
, we show that the Bethe ansatz solution reproduces biased diffusion statistics for the defect particle, as expected.
In Sec.
V
, we calculate the cumulants of the displacement of normal particles to second order.
In Sec.
VI
, we make some final remarks.
We fill in the algebraic detail of some of the lengthier calculations in the appendices.

II
Summary of results

II.1
Model definition

We consider a ring with
L+1
sites,
M
normal particles and one defect particle.
The normal particles hop to the right and left with rates
p
,
q
respectively, and the defect particle hops to the right and left with rates
\alpha p
,
q/\alpha
respectively.
The defect also overtakes normal particles to its right and left with rates
\alpha p
,
q/\alpha
.

We can summarize the dynamics as follows, where we denote empty sites, the defect and normal particles as 0, 1, 2 respectively:

\displaystyle 10\underset{q/\alpha}{\overset{\alpha p}{\rightleftarrows}}01\quad;\quad 20\underset{q}{\overset{p}{\rightleftarrows}}02\quad;\quad 12\underset{q/\alpha}{\overset{\alpha p}{\rightleftarrows}}21.

(1)

Note that the defect (denoted by 1) does not distinguish between normal particles and empty sites, and can therefore be seen as having priority in the dynamics.
Because of this, one may think of the defect as a “first-class” particle, whereas the normal particles may be thought of as “second-class”.

It is convenient to introduce the normal particle density and asymmetry parameters,

\rho=M/L,\qquad x=q/p.

(2)

We will take
x<1
in this paper (but
\alpha
can have any positive value).

II.2
Large deviation theory

In investigating the long time current statistics, the central objects are the random variables
Y_{1}(t)
,
Y_{2}(t)
,
Y_{12}(t)
, which count the number of processes of type
10\to 01
,
20\to 02
,
12\to 21
respectively, minus the reverse processes, up to time
t
.

In the long time limit,
t\to\infty
, the joint moment generating function of these variables satisfies a large deviation principle,

\displaystyle\langle e^{\gamma_{1}Y_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}Y_{2}(t)+\gamma_{12}Y_{12}(t)}\rangle\sim e^{\lambda(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{12})t},

(3)

where
\gamma_{i}
are the conjugate variables of
Y_{i}(t)
and
\lambda
is the rate function.

The rate function
\lambda
thus directly gives the long time limit of the scaled cumulants of the hop-counting variables,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle(Y_{i}(t))^{n}\rangle_{c}}{t}=\left.\frac{\partial^{n}\lambda}{\partial\gamma^{n}_{i}}\right|_{\gamma_{1}=0,\gamma_{2}=0,\gamma_{12}=0}.

(4)

Although this formulation is general, we shall focus on two important cases: (
i
) the
motion of the defect
, which corresponds to
\gamma_{2}=0
,
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{12}\neq 0
; and (
ii
) the
motion of the normal particles
, which corresponds to
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{12}=0
,
\gamma_{2}\neq 0
.
Thus we will use the notation

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=a_{i}\gamma,

(5)

where
\gamma
is the single formal parameter conjugate to the relevant variable, and
a_{i}
are constants, which we can set to
a_{2}=0
,
a_{1}=a_{12}=1
to track the defect, or
a_{1}=a_{12}=0
,
a_{2}=1
to track the normal particles.

Then, for instance, the long time limit of mean current and diffusion constant of normal particles is obtained by setting
a_{1}=a_{12}=0
,
a_{2}=1
and evaluating

\displaystyle J=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle Y_{2}(t)\rangle}{t}=\left.\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\gamma}\right|_{\gamma=0},

(6a)

\displaystyle\Delta=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{{\rm Var}(Y_{2}(t))}{t}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda}{\partial\gamma^{2}}\right|_{\gamma=0}.

(6b)

II.3.2
Current

The current of normal particles in these phases was also calculated exactly in
Lobaskin et al. 2022
using the matrix product ansatz.
In the limit
L\to\infty
, with
\rho
held fixed, it is given to leading order in
L
by

\displaystyle J_{\cal L}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle Lp(1-x)\rho(1-\rho),

(9a)

\displaystyle J_{\cal S}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle Lp\left(\rho(\alpha-x/\alpha)+\frac{x(\alpha-1)^{2}}{\alpha(1-x)}\right).

(9b)

In the localized phases, the current is essentially the same as in a homogeneous PASEP.
In the shock phase, the defect throttles the current.
Observe that the currents in the shock phase and localized phases are equal at the points of phase transition (i.e., there is no discontinuity).
However, the current in the shock phase depends linearly on density, whereas in the localized phases, it is a concave function of density.
Thus the defect evidently makes the current smaller than that of a pure system.

The current in the shock phase can be further understood as follows.
Let
k
be the mean position of the shock front in the reference frame of the defect.
Defining
u=k/L
, total particle number conservation means,

\displaystyle\rho=(1-u)\rho_{1}+u\rho_{2}.

(10)

Substituting (
10
) in (
9b
), we can write the current in the shock phase as

\displaystyle J_{\cal S}\approx Lp(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})[\alpha(1-u)+(x/\alpha)u].

(11)

This suggests the following interpretation.
The current of normal particles is controlled by the defect.
The defect sees a normal particle density
\rho_{1}
behind itself and
\rho_{2}
in front of itself.
As the defect hops forward, with rate
\alpha p
, it creates a small region of density
\rho_{2}
behind itself.
This “hole” has to travel backwards
L(1-u)
sites to restore the steady-state profile.
Thus the defect hopping forward generates a net current of normal particles in the forward direction of magnitude
\alpha pL(1-u)(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})
.

Similarly, as the defect hops backwards, with rate
px/\alpha
, it creates a small region of density
\rho_{1}
in front of itself.
This has to travel forwards
Lu
sites to restore the steady-state profile, giving a net current of
(q/\alpha)Lu(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})
.

These two effects combine to give (
11
).

This result reveals the main sources of fluctuations in the two phases.
In the localized phases, the diffusion constant is the same as in a homogeneous PASEP with bulk density
\rho

Derrida and Mallick 1997
.
This intuitively makes sense, as in the localized phases, the defect does not have a macroscopic effect on the system.

In the shock steady state, we can write the diffusion constant as,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\cal S}\approx L^{2}p(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})^{2}[\alpha(1-u)^{2}+(x/\alpha)u^{2}].

(13)

This expression can be interpreted through the same picture as the current in this phase.
The fluctuations are controlled by the defect, which creates low density “holes” at rate
\alpha p
, that travel backwards, and high density waves at rate
px/\alpha
, that travel forwards.
This creates fluctuations, of magnitude
L(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})(1-u)
and
L(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})u
respectively, whose squares have to be added weighted by their rate of creation to obtain the total variance (
13

III
Functional Bethe ansatz calculation of the large deviations of the current

III.1
Bethe ansatz solution

In
Lobaskin et al. 2023
, it was shown that the rate function, as defined in (
3
), can be calculated for the model (
1
) using a coordinate Bethe ansatz.
This allows us to write the rate function as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\gamma)}{p}=-[M(1+x)+\alpha+x/\alpha]+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M}(z_{i}^{-1}+xz_{i}),

(14)

where
z_{i}
are Bethe roots.
They are to be found by solving the Bethe equations,

\displaystyle z_{i}^{L+1}=(-)^{M}\xi^{-1}\frac{1-B-z_{i}+Bxz_{i}}{1-Bx-z_{i}+Bx^{2}z_{i}}

\displaystyle\times\prod\limits_{k=0}^{M}\frac{1-xz_{i}z_{k}-(1+x)z_{i}}{1-xz_{i}z_{k}-(1+x)z_{k}},

(15)

where

\displaystyle\xi=e^{\gamma(a_{1}+La_{2}-a_{12})},

(16)

and
B
is a constant.
We remark that the structure of these equations is similar to the homogeneous PASEP case
Prolhac and Mallick 2008
, but with an additional constant,
B
, which is also a feature in the case of a TASEP with a defect
Derrida and Evans 1999
.

The constant
B
can be fixed by multiplying (
22
) for all
i
.
Then together with (
24
), we get

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i=0}^{M}\frac{1-B-z_{i}+Bxz_{i}}{1-Bx-z_{i}+Bx^{2}z_{i}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\alpha^{-(L+1)}e^{-\gamma[(L-M)(a_{1}-a_{2})+(M+1)a_{12}]}.

(17)

In addition to the Bethe equations, we need to enforce the conditions,

\displaystyle\alpha e^{\gamma(a_{1}+Ma_{2})}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{M}z_{i}=1,

(18)

\displaystyle\lambda\to 0\quad{\rm as}\quad\gamma\to 0,

(19)

where (
24
) is a consequence of the translational invariance of the steady state (we will henceforth refer to this as the periodicity condition); and (
19
) comes from the fact that
\lambda
can be identified with the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of the transition rate matrix in the master equation of this system, which must converge to the 0 eigenvalue in the unconditioned limit,
\gamma=0
.

III.1.1
Ground state solution

It is instructive at this point to consider the ground state,
\gamma\to 0
.
In this case, the Bethe roots,
z_{i}
, all converge to
1
, except one root (we may choose it to be
z_{0}
without loss of generality), which converges to a different finite value.
From (
24
) and the condition (
19
), it is evident that

\displaystyle z_{0}\to z_{0}^{(0)}=\alpha^{-1}.

(20)

We note that in the TASEP with a defect particle whose hopping rate in units of the normal particles’ hopping rate is
\alpha
, the phenomenon of all Bethe roots converging to the 1, except for one, which converges to
\alpha^{-1}
has also been observed
Derrida and Evans 1999
.

III.1.2
Change of variable

The equations take a more felicitous form if we consider the following transformation of the Bethe roots,

\displaystyle y_{i}=\frac{1-z_{i}}{1-xz_{i}}.

(21)

Such transformations have been used before in Bethe ansatz solutions for the homogeneous PASEP
Prolhac and Mallick 2008
;
Prolhac 2010
and the asymmetric XXZ spin chain
Kim 1995
.
We will refer to
y_{i}
as the Bethe roots from here on.
The Bethe equations (
15
) become,

\displaystyle\xi\left(\frac{1-y_{i}}{1-xy_{i}}\right)^{L+1}\prod\limits_{k=0}^{M}\frac{xy_{i}-y_{k}}{y_{i}-xy_{k}}=-\frac{B-y_{i}}{Bx-y_{i}},

(22)

the
B
-fixing equation (
17
) becomes,

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i=1}^{M+1}\frac{B-y_{i}}{Bx-y_{i}}=\alpha^{-(L+1)}e^{-\gamma[(L-M)(a_{1}-a_{2})+(M+1)a_{12}]},

(23)

and the periodicity condition (
18
) becomes,

\displaystyle\alpha e^{\gamma(a_{1}+Ma_{2})}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{M}\frac{1-y_{i}}{1-xy_{i}}=1.

(24)

Furthermore, the rate function (
14
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\gamma)}{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\alpha-1)(1-x/\alpha)

(25)

\displaystyle+(1-x)\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{1-y_{i}}-\frac{1}{1-xy_{i}}\right).

The ground state corresponds to
y_{i}\to 0
for
i=1,\dots,M
and

\displaystyle y_{0}\to y_{0}^{(0)}=\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-x},

(26a)

\displaystyle B\to B^{(0)}=y_{0}^{(0)}\frac{\alpha^{L+1}-x^{M}}{\alpha^{L+1}-x^{M+1}},

(26b)

where the first equation comes from (
20
) and (
21
) and the latter equation comes from plugging (
26a
) into (
23
).

The structure of the Bethe equations (
22
) is similar to the homogeneous PASEP case, which has been studied previously
Prolhac and Mallick 2008
;
Prolhac 2010
.
The approach that has proved to be most fruitful is to transform the equations into a set of functional equations, which can be solved directly to extract the behaviour of functions of the roots, like
\lambda
, without explicitly calculating the roots.

Inspired by this approach, we now reformulate (
22
) as a functional equation for a single-variable function.
Then, we further manipulate this equation in a procedure known as going “beyond the equator”, which ultimately allows direct calculation of
\lambda
.

III.2
One-variable function formulation

We first define the single-variable polynomial,

\displaystyle S(T)=x\xi h(T)\prod\limits_{k=0}^{M}(xT-y_{k})+h(xT)\prod\limits_{k=0}^{M}(T-xy_{k}),

(27)

where

\displaystyle h(T)=(Bx-T)(1-T)^{L+1}.

(28)

Then from (
22
), it follows that all Bethe roots
y_{i}
are roots of
S(T)
(i.e.,
S(y_{i})=0
).
However, the degree of
S(T)
is
L+M+3
, whereas there are only
M+1
Bethe roots.
This suggests that we should also consider the polynomial

\displaystyle Q(T)=\prod\limits_{k=0}^{M}(T-y_{k}).

(29)

Then as the Bethe roots
y_{i}
are roots of both
S(T)
and
Q(T)
, and the degree of
S(T)
is higher, we conclude that
S(T)
must be divisible by
Q(T)
as a polynomial.
This means that there exists some polynomial
R(T)
of degree
L+2
, for which,

\displaystyle Q(T)R(T)=\xi h(T)Q(xT)+x^{M}h(xT)Q(T/x).

(30)

This is a functional equation for
Q
.
As
Q
contains the same information as the Bethe roots
y_{i}
, in this functional formulation, this equation plays the same role as the Bethe equation (
22
).

Using
Q
also allows us to rewrite the
B
-fixing equation (
23
) as

\displaystyle\frac{Q(B)}{Q(Bx)}=\alpha^{-(L+1)}e^{-\gamma[(L-M)(a_{1}-a_{2})+(M+1)a_{12}]},

(31)

and the periodicity condition (
24
) as

\displaystyle\alpha e^{\gamma(a_{1}+Ma_{2})}\frac{Q(1)}{x^{M+1}Q(x^{-1})}=1.

(32)

Moreover, the rate function (
25
), can be written in terms of
Q
as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\gamma)}{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\alpha-1)(1-x/\alpha)

(33)

\displaystyle+(1-x)\left(\frac{Q^{\prime}(1)}{Q(1)}-\frac{Q^{\prime}(x^{-1})}{xQ(x^{-1})}\right).

Now equations (
30
–
32
) are self-consistent and can be used to solve for
Q(T)
(and therefore
\lambda
) without reference to the Bethe roots
y_{i}
.

III.3
Bethe equation beyond the equator

We now proceed to change (
30
) into the so-called “beyond the equator” form

;
Prolhac 2010
, which ultimately allows us to write an equation involving only one unknown function.

III.3.1
Bethe equation

The Bethe equation beyond the equator is given by

\displaystyle 2Ch(T)=P(T/x)Q(T)-x^{M}\xi^{-1}P(T)Q(T/x),

(34)

where
P
is a polynomial of degree
L-M+1
and
C
is a constant defined as

\displaystyle C=-\frac{1-x^{M}\xi^{-1}}{2x}\frac{y_{0}}{B}Q(0).

(35)

The derivation of (
34
) is given in Appendix
A
.

III.3.2
Ground state solution

We now consider again the ground state case,
\gamma=0
.
Recalling the remark about the Bethe roots in Sec.
III.1
, we can write down the following form of the ground state solution,

\displaystyle Q^{(0)}(T)=T^{M}(T-y_{0}^{(0)}),

(36a)

\displaystyle P^{(0)}(T)=T-y_{0}^{(0)},

(36b)

\displaystyle C^{(0)}=0.

(36c)

The form of
Q^{(0)}
follows from the location of the Bethe roots.
Then (
36c
) follows by evaluating (
34
) at
T=0
.
Putting this back into (

III.4
One function reformulation of Bethe equation

At this point, the Bethe equation (

III.4.1
Bethe equation

After some algebraic manipulations, which are outlined in Appendix

\displaystyle w(T)={\rm arcsinh}\left(C{\tilde{h}}(T)e^{-X[w](T)}\right).

Here,

\displaystyle{\tilde{h}}(T)=\frac{(Bx-T)(1-T)^{L+1}}{T^{M}(T-y_{0})(T/x-y_{0})},

We remark that (

However, there the analogue of

had the simpler form

In the present case,

has additional poles at

,

As we shall see, the additional poles imply the existence of phase transitions.
The equations to fix the constants

,

,

III.4.2
Additional equations

We now rewrite the remaining equations (

In doing this, it is helpful to define another operator

\displaystyle y_{0}=\frac{\alpha e^{\delta}-1}{\alpha e^{\delta}-x},

\displaystyle\{T^{0}\}w(T)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(a_{1}+La_{2}-a_{12}),

where the notation

The derivation of this is also given in Appendix

Now (

Lastly, to complete the formulation in terms of

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\gamma)}{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)\left.\frac{d{\cal P}[w](T)}{dT}\right|_{T=1}

\displaystyle+(1-e^{-\delta})(\alpha e^{\delta}-x/\alpha).

IV
Displacement statistics of defect particle

We now proceed to explicitly calculate the cumulants of the currents at long times.
We first consider the motion of the defect (

,

As the defect particle simply performs biased diffusion, we should recover Skellam statistics (i.e., the difference of two Poisson random variables).
This result is obvious even without using the Bethe ansatz, but it serves as a verification of the validity of the calculation.

Setting

\displaystyle\{T^{0}\}w(T)=0.

Then from (

This is precisely the cumulant generating function of a Skellam distribution with parameters

The cumulants are given by,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\langle(Y_{1}(t)+Y_{12}(t))^{k}\rangle_{c}}{t}=\begin{cases}\alpha-x/\alpha,&k\;{\rm odd}\\
\alpha+x/\alpha,&k\;{\rm even}\end{cases}.

To obtain derivatives of

V.3

For the current, given by (

Using these equations, and the integral expressions (

\displaystyle\frac{J}{Lp}=\rho(\alpha-x/\alpha)+F,

where

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{L,M}^{-1}\oint_{\Gamma}f(T){\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T)/T

(66)

\displaystyle f(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha-x/\alpha)\frac{T}{1-T}-(1-x)\frac{T}{(1-T)^{2}},

(67)

\displaystyle Z_{L,M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{\Gamma}\frac{{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T)}{T}\;.

The normalisation

We first use the definition of
{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}
, (

Then, we expand the numerator using the binomial theorem.
Crucially, we observe that the terms in the expansion with powers larger than

The remaining terms can be evaluated using the residues of the poles outside the contour (at

After some simplification, this gives,

\displaystyle Z_{L,M}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{M}(-)^{k}{L+1\choose k}\frac{\alpha^{L+1}-x^{k}}{\alpha^{L+1}-x^{M+1}}x

\displaystyle\times(y_{0}^{(0)})^{k-M-1},

where we have used the definition of

, (

The numerator in

can be computed similarly.

Putting this into (

This gives an alternative expression for the mean current, which was previously calculated in

There, the current was defined as the flux of normal particles across a given bond, whereas in this paper it was defined as the motion of all particles everywhere in the system, (

Hence, to compare the two expressions, we have multiplied the former by

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\rm MPA}}{Lp}=\rho(\alpha-x/\alpha)-\frac{{L-1\choose M-1}(\alpha^{L+1}-\alpha^{-1}x^{M+1})+(\alpha-1)x\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{m=0}^{l}{L-1-l\choose M-1-m}{l\choose m}\alpha^{L-1-l}x^{m}}{\sum_{l=0}^{L}\sum_{m=0}^{l}{L-l\choose M-m}{l\choose m}\alpha^{L-l}x^{m}},

(70)

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\rm BA}}{Lp}=\rho(\alpha-x/\alpha)+\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}(-)^{k}\left[(\alpha-x/\alpha){L\choose k}-(1-x){L-1\choose k}\right](\alpha^{L+1}-x^{k+1})(y_{0}^{(0)})^{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{M}(-)^{k}{L+1\choose k}(\alpha^{L+1}-x^{k})(y_{0}^{(0)})^{k}},

where

is defined by (

In particular, one can use a symbolic programming language like Mathematica with rational numbers or integers for all parameters, as this gives exact values, without any machine error.

V.3.1
Asymptotic expressions for
J
and phase diagram

The result given so far, (
71
), is exact and can be evaluated numerically for finite system sizes.
To make sense of it physically, it is beneficial to extract the asymptotic behaviour in the limit
L\to\infty
, with
\rho
held fixed.

The key quantity is
Z_{L,M}
, as defined in (
68
).
We can write it as

\displaystyle Z_{L,M}=\oint_{\Gamma}A(T)e^{L\phi(T)},

(72)

where

\displaystyle A(T)=\frac{(B^{(0)}x-T)(1-T)}{T(T-y_{0}^{(0)})(T/x-y_{0}^{(0)})},

(73)

\displaystyle\phi(T)=\ln(1-T)-\rho\ln T.

(74)

This integral has a saddle point at the solution of the equation
\phi^{\prime}(T)=0
.
This is found to be

\displaystyle T=T_{0}=-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.

(75)

We can always deform the contour of integration to pass through the saddle point.
In doing this, we may need to pass through the poles at
T=y_{0}^{(0)}
and
T=xy_{0}^{(0)}
, in which case the contributions of their residues must be subtracted.

The original contour is a small circle around
T=0
.
Hence, the poles must be subtracted if
0>y_{0}^{(0)}>T_{0}
and
0>xy_{0}^{(0)}>T_{0}
respectively.
Rearranging these inequalities, we see that we have 3 cases.

No poles.

When
\alpha>1
or
\rho<\rho_{2}
, both poles are outside the contour, so the integral is dominated by the saddle point.

Two poles.

When
\alpha<1
and
\rho>\rho_{1}
, both poles are inside the contour.
However, it can be verified that the contributions from their residues cancel exactly.
Therefore the integral is still dominated by the saddle point.

One pole.

When
\alpha<1
and
\rho_{1}>\rho>\rho_{2}
, the pole at
xy_{0}^{(0)}
is inside the contour but the pole at
y_{0}^{(0)}
is outside the contour.
In this case the integral is dominated by the residue from the pole at
xy_{0}^{(0)}
.

In summary, for
\rho_{2}<\rho<\rho_{1}
(i.e., in the shock phase), the integral is dominated by the pole at
T=xy_{0}^{(0)}
, and otherwise (in the localized phases) it is dominated by the saddle point.
This implies the phase diagram presented in Section
II.3
.
Using these results, we get to leading order

\displaystyle F\approx\begin{cases}f(T_{0}),&{{\cal L}}\\
f(xy_{0}^{(0)}),&{{\cal S}}\end{cases}.

(76)

Plugging this into (
65
), we get expressions that agree with (
9a
–
9b
).

V.4.1
Asymptotic expressions for
\Delta

The asymptotic analysis of
\Delta
requires us to extract the large

,

(which enter via

(

The asymptotic analysis is much less straightforward than it is in the calculations of the current.
However, guided by known results in related systems (for instance
Derrida et al. 1993b
;
Derrida and Mallick 1997
;
Derrida and Evans 1999
;
Boutillier et al. 2002
) as well as Monte Carlo simulations, one expects that
\Delta
has the scaling
L^{2}
in the shock phase and
L^{3/2}
in the localized phases.
This helps us to determine which terms will contribute to the asymptotic behavior.

The integrals still have no-, one- and two-pole regimes.
However, if one looks at certain terms (such as
G
) in isolation, the residues from the two poles do not cancel exactly and some super-dominant scaling seems to emerge.
These super-dominant terms ultimately cancel out when all the terms comprising
\Delta
are considered together.
Although we do not have a rigorous analytic proof of this cancellation mechanism, our results are supported by extensive numerical analysis, as well as agreement with the exact finite-size results.

Asymptotics of G.

Recall that the definition of
G
(
78
) contains
g
(
79
).
Importantly, we have
g(T_{0})=0
which implies that
G
does not contribute at leading order in the localized phases, when the integrals are dominated by the saddle point.
In the shock phase,
G
is dominated by the residue of the pole at
T=xy_{0}^{(0)}
, therefore we have

\displaystyle G\approx g(xy_{0}^{(0)})=\rho-\rho_{2}.

(82)

Asymptotics of
X[{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T)]
.

Splitting
X[{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T)]
in two parts, like (
63b
), we eventually see that the integral involving the kernel
K
never contributes at leading order.
Hence, to leading order

\displaystyle X[{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}](T)\approx{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T).

(83)

Finally,
{\tilde{h}}^{(1)}
, as given in (

B^{(1)}
.
However, we have verified that this term does not contribute in any phase.

Thus, in the localized phase, we can write
w^{(2)}(T)\approx-({\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T))^{2}
, whereas in the shock phase we have,
w^{(2)}(T)\approx{\tilde{h}}^{(1)}(T)-({\tilde{h}}^{(0)}(T))^{2}
.
Ultimately, the contributing terms to the diffusion constant in each phase are

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{\cal L}}{L^{2}p}

\displaystyle\approx

VI
Conclusion

We have used the Bethe ansatz to calculate the first two scaled cumulants of particle displacement in a PASEP with a defect particle that has priority in the dynamics.
The first scaled cumulant (mean current) (
9a
–
9b
) agrees with the known result obtained using a matrix product ansatz
Lobaskin et al. 2022
.
The second scaled cumulant (diffusion constant) (
12a
–
12b
) is a novel result, which is shown to agree with Monte Carlo simulations (see Figure
3
).

The asymptotics of the scaled cumulants in the limit
L\to\infty
with
\rho
held fixed were also calculated using asymptotic analysis of the underlying integral expressions.
The phase transitions were shown to correspond to a transition of the integrals being dominated by a saddle point (in the localized phases) and a pole (in the shock phases).

The asymptotic results indicate that in the localized phases, the system essentially does not feel the defect, with the current statistics being the same as those in a pure system.
This makes sense intuitively, as the effects of the defect are localized, so its presence is not expected to be manifested at a macroscopic level.

In the shock phase, we have argued that the current is controlled by the defect, which creates density waves in the shock profile.
These are small high density packets in the low density region and low density packets in the high density region.
The results derived using the Bethe ansatz are consistent with this picture and it would be of interest to investigate whether this holds for higher order statistics.

An interesting aspect of the result for the second order cumulants is that the scaling is different in the two phases.
In the localized phase, we have
\Delta\sim L^{3/2}
, whereas in the shock phase,
\Delta\sim L^{2}
.
Hence there is a jump discontinuity in
\Delta
in the thermodynamic limit at the phase transitions, whereas the current,
J
, is continuous, with its first derivative being discontinuous at the phase transitions.
We remark that a similar feature occurs in the open boundary TASEP, where a discontinuity of
\Delta
appears when crossing the shock line in the phase diagram
Derrida et al. 1995
.

In the shock phase the current fluctuations, given by
\Delta^{1/2}\sim L
, remain comparable to the mean current.
This is consistent with the picture we presented in Sec.
II.3
of the motion of the defect causing density fluctuations that must travel around the system and thus create current fluctuations of order
L
.
As the motion of the defect is independent of the system size, the relative fluctuations do not decrease in the limit of large
L
.

Appendix A
Derivation of Bethe equation beyond the equator

To reformulate the Bethe equation in the “beyond the equator” form, we begin by examining the degrees of the polynomials in the Bethe equation (
30
).
We have
\deg h=\deg R=L+2
and
\deg Q=M+1
.
Then by the rules of polynomial division, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{h(T)}{Q(T)Q(T/x)}=\frac{U(T)}{Q(T/x)}+\frac{V(T)}{Q(T)}+W(T),

(89)

where
U
,
V
are polynomials of degree at most
M
and
W
is a polynomial of degree
L-2M
.
In doing this, we assume that
L-2M>0
, though the solution will be general due to particle-hole symmetry.

Then dividing (

Q(T)Q(xT)Q(T/x)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{R(T)}{Q(xT)Q(T/x)}=\xi\left(\frac{U(T)}{Q(T/x)}+\frac{V(T)}{Q(T)}+W(T)\right)

\displaystyle+x^{M}\left(\frac{U(xT)}{Q(T)}+\frac{V(xT)}{Q(xT)}+W(xT)\right).

(90)

Generally,
Q(T)
will not share any roots with
Q(xT)
or
Q(T/x)
.
Therefore the only term that has poles at the zeros of

is
(\xi V(T)+x^{M}U(xT))/Q(T)

As the degree of the numerator is at most
M
, the only way the equation can be satisfied is if this term vanishes identically,

\displaystyle\xi V(T)+x^{M}U(xT)=0.

(91)

Using this to eliminate

in (

), we get

\displaystyle\frac{h(T)}{Q(T)Q(T/x)}=\frac{U(T)}{Q(T/x)}-\frac{x^{M}}{\xi}\frac{U(xT)}{Q(T)}+W(T).

(92)

Now we can always find a polynomial
{\tilde{W}}
such that

\displaystyle W(T)=x^{M}\xi^{-1}{\tilde{W}}(T)-{\tilde{W}}(T/x).

(93)

Indeed, this corresponds to simply rescaling the coefficients of
T^{k}
in
W(T)
by
(x^{M}\xi^{-1}-x^{-k})
.
Then we multiply (
92
) by
2C
, where
C
is an arbitrary constant and the factor of
2
is introduced to simplify expressions later on.
This allows us to write the Bethe equation beyond the equator (
34
),

\displaystyle 2Ch(T)=P(T/x)Q(T)-x^{M}\xi^{-1}P(T)Q(T/x),

(94)

where,

\displaystyle P(T)=2C[U(xT)-{\tilde{W}}(T)Q(T)].

(95)

Although in general
C
is an arbitrary constant, we can make particular choice to simplify later calculations.
Specifically, we fix
C
such that we have the identity,

\displaystyle P(0)=-y_{0}.

(96)

Evaluating (
94
) at
T=0
, we see that this can be achieved with the following choice of

,

\displaystyle C=-\frac{1-x^{M}\xi^{-1}}{2x}\frac{y_{0}}{B}Q(0).

(97)

Appendix D
Proof of integral formulae

Here, we prove the integral formulae presented in Sec.
V.2
, specifically (
63d
), (
63e
) and (
63f
).
We first make a comment on the operator
{\cal P}
.
The output of the operator given by the definition (
41
) is well-defined everywhere if the argument of the operator has a finite Laurent series expansion.
For rational functions, such as
{\tilde{h}}^{(0)}

Note that from (
106a
), we see that at each order in perturbation theory

is a rational function, as it is given by a finite sum of
p(T)
and
q(T)
, which are both rational functions.

Consider an arbitrary rational function
a(T)
with a pole of order
n
at
T=0
and a finite number of other poles at some other locations
T=a_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,m
.
Using the partial fraction decomposition for rational functions, we can write

as,

\displaystyle a(T)=b_{1}(T)+\frac{b_{2}(T)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(T-a_{i})}+\frac{b_{3}(T)}{T^{n}},

(125)

where
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
b_{3}
are (finite) polynomials in
T
, with
\deg b_{3}<n
.
Note that the first two terms are both analytic at
T=0
.
Then we define the action of the operator
{\cal P}
on

as,

\displaystyle{\cal P}[a](T)=-b_{1}(T)-\frac{b_{2}(T)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(T-a_{i})}+\frac{b_{3}(T)}{T^{n}}.

(126)

It is easy to check that this alternative definition agrees with (
41
) within the latter’s radius of convergence.

Now we wish to evaluate
a(T)
at some location
T_{*}
, where
a(T_{*})=0
.
This condition, together with (
125
) and (
126
), implies that

\displaystyle{\cal P}[a](T_{*})=2\frac{b_{3}(T_{*})}{T_{*}^{n}}.

(127)

At the same time, consider an integral of the type used in Sec.
V.2
, namely,
\oint_{\Gamma}a(T)/(T_{*}-T)
, where
\Gamma
is a small circle around the origin.
Out of the terms in the expansion (

), only the last one has a pole at

, so the other two vanish.
Then the integral of the last term can be evaluated using the sum of the residues of the poles outside the contour.
As

is a finite polynomial with

.
Thus we obtain overall,

\displaystyle\oint_{\Gamma}\frac{a(T)}{T_{*}-T}=\frac{b_{3}(T_{*})}{T_{*}^{n}}=\frac{1}{2}{\cal P}[a](T_{*}).

(128)

Then (
63d
) and (
63e
) are direct applications of this with

and

respectively.
The required identities,

and

) and the definition (

The identity (

Then we also need the identity

Strictly speaking, our proof only shows that the integral formulae hold up to any order in perturbation theory, as for any finite

However, this is sufficient for our purposes.

Appendix E
Asymptotics of
\Delta
in the localized phases

First we rewrite the expression (
84a
) in a form that is conducive to saddle point expansion.
The diffusion constant becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{\cal L}}{L^{2}p}\approx\left(\oint_{\Gamma}Ae^{L\phi}\right)^{-3}\left[\left(\oint_{\Gamma}Afe^{L\phi}\right)\left(\oint_{\Gamma}A^{2}Te^{2L\phi}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\left(\oint_{\Gamma}Ae^{L\phi}\right)\left(\oint_{\Gamma}A^{2}Tfe^{2L\phi}\right)\right],\qquad

(129)

where we have suppressed the arguments of
A(T)
,
\phi(T)
,

for compactness and these functions are defined in (
73
), (
74
), (
67
) respectively.

The terms in the numerator evidently cancel each other at first order in the saddle point.
Hence, we use the general formula for the first correction to the saddle point (see any standard textbook, for instance
(
Bender and Orszag 1999
, chapter 6)
).
For instance, we have (remembering the suppressed factor of
2\pi
),

\displaystyle\oint_{\Gamma}Ae^{L\phi}\approx-\frac{Ae^{L\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi L|\phi^{\prime\prime}|}}\left[1+\frac{1}{L}\left(-\frac{A^{\prime\prime}}{2A\phi^{\prime\prime}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.+\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}}{8(\phi^{\prime\prime})^{2}}+\frac{A^{\prime}\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{2A(\phi^{\prime\prime})^{2}}-\frac{5(\phi^{\prime\prime\prime})^{2}}{24(\phi^{\prime\prime})^{3}}\right)\right]\right|_{T=T_{0}}.

The overall factor of

arises because the saddle point

is on the negative real axis and the original contour around the origin is anticlockwise, therefore the steepest descent contour goes from

to
-i\infty+T_{0}

Applying this, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{\cal L}}{L^{2}p}\approx\left(-\frac{Ae^{L\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi L|\phi^{\prime\prime}|}}\right)^{-3}\left.\frac{A^{3}Tfe^{3L\phi}}{2^{3/2}\pi L^{2}|\phi^{\prime\prime}|}\times{\rm corr.}\right|_{T=T_{0}},

where the correction term (

Note that in the two terms in the numerator, the only difference is in the placement of the factors in front of the exponential.
Hence the terms in the general correction formula that only involve

This leaves us with

\displaystyle{\rm corr.}=\frac{1}{4\phi^{\prime\prime}}\left[2\left(-\frac{(Af)^{\prime\prime}}{Af}+\frac{(Af)^{\prime}\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{Af\phi^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{A^{\prime\prime}}{A}-\frac{A^{\prime}\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{A\phi^{\prime\prime}}\right)\right.
