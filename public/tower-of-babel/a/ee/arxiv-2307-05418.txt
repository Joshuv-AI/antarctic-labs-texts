Stability and genericity of bang-bang controls in affine problems Thanks:
Alberto Domínguez Corella, Gerd Wachsmuth

Abstract
We analyse the role of the bang-bang property in affine optimal control problems. We show that many essential stability properties of affine problems are only satisfied when minimizers are bang-bang. Moreover, we prove that almost any perturbation in an affine optimal control problem leads to a bang-bang strict global minimizer. We work in an abstract framework that allows to cover many problems in the literature of optimal control, this includes problems constrained by partial and ordinary differential equations. We give examples that show the applicability of our results to specific optimal control problems.



keywords

bang-bang, affine optimal control, stability, genericity

Introduction

The term
bang-bang
was coined a long time ago in control theory. The term is informal and has become widely adopted in the field to refer to a control that switches from one extreme value to another; in analogy with relays, that make a
bang
to change from
off
to
on
, and another
bang
to come back from
on
to
off
. The term also applies to controls that take several values (more than two), usually the vertices of some polygon or polyhedron.

One of the fundamental principles in the theory of mathematical control is the so-called
bang-bang principle
, this result asserts that for control systems, originating from an ordinary differential equation, any state that can be reached by a feasible control can also be attained by a bang-bang control, see, e.g.,
[
30
,
35
]
.
Bang-bang controls also arise in optimal control problems, specially in
affine problems
, where the control appears linearly (and hence the name). This is mainly because they lack the so-called Tykhonov regularization term, and hence some regularity of minimizers is lost, or better said, it was never there; it was artificially added by the
regularizer
.

In this paper, we study several aspects related to the stability of bang-bang minimizers, and moreover to instability phenomena. These aspects have many important uses, e.g., they help to deal with uncertainty of data, to understand the technicalities appearing in the numerical methods, to regularize problems, etc. In order to show that the phenomena studied here are independent of particular control systems, we work in very general framework that allows us to cover several optimal control problems. We illustrate this with a handful of examples.

Let us now comment a bit on the related literature. For optimal control problems governed by ordinary differential equations, the stability analysis of bang-bang minimizers started with
[
23
]
for linear quadratic problems, and continued with
[
24
]
for more general affine systems. After that, several refinements and analyses of numerical schemes came. In
[
2
,
32
]
, the accuracy of implicit discretization schemes was analysed under growth assumptions on the switching function; these same assumptions were used in
[
16
,
39
]
to prove the convergence of gradient methods. In
[
41
,
42
]
the stability of the first order necessary conditions was studied by means of the
metric regularity property
. In
[
18
,
36
]
more
natural
assumptions than previous papers were introduced to generate results about stability and Lipschitz rates of convergence; these assumptions involved
L^{1}
-growths, similar to the classic coercivity condition, but with some modifications. Recently, in
[
3
,
19
]
, the
metric subregularity
of the optimality mapping was used to prove the accuracy of the
model predictive control
algorithm.

The stability analysis of bang-bang minimizers for problems constrained by partial differential equations started with
[
8
,
15
]
, where elliptic optimal control problems were considered. Since then, there are several papers dealing other type of problems with bang-bang minimizers; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
17
,
29
,
40
]
. For parabolic problems, we mention
[
47
]
, where a study on the accuracy of variational discretization was carried out; and
[
12
]
, where stability with respect to initial data was analysed. In
[
9
]
, a fully discrete scheme is proposed and analyzed for a velocity tracking problem with bang-bang controls. Finally, we comment on the recent paper
[
33
]
, where bilinear problems with
L^{1}
–
L^{\infty}
constraints are considered; the authors proved that under certain hypotheses, the optimal controls must be bang-bang (our setting is different, but with a few changes applies to the problem considered there). We present a similar result regarding the bang-bang nature of optimal controls, but for linearly perturbed problems, see
Section

3
for more details.

We now describe the the organization of the paper and the contributions of each section.

In
Section

2
, we introduce the optimization problem and give the definition of the bang-bang property under consideration. The first result is the following characterization.

A control is bang-bang iff every sequence converging weakly to it converges strongly.

This result (
theorem

8
) is based on previous results from
[
13
]
and
[
45
]
. Since the proof is a bit involved and requires some external tools from measure theory and set-valued analysis, it is given in the Appendix. After that, we use the characterization to prove that strict local minimizers satisfy a growth condition iff they are bang-bang, see
Section

2.3
for more details and references.

In
Section

3
, we prove that an affine problem is
well-posed
in Tykhonov’s sense iff it possesses a bang-bang strict global minimizer. We also use a smooth variational principle to prove that this situation is generic, yielding a result of the following form.

Almost every linearly perturbed problem has a bang-bang strict global minimizer.

In
Section

4
, we prove that for strict local minimizers, hemicontinuity notions of stability under linear perturbations coincide, and all of them are equivalent to the bang-bang property; therefore, many undesirable instability phenomena can occur when the minimizer is not bang-bang. This shows that the study of problems with singular arcs is truly different in nature from the pure bang-bang case; this can already been seen in previous publications dealing with the stability of
bang-singular-bang
minimizers, see
[
25
,
26
]
. In
Section

5
, we continue the study of stability, but now for the first order necessary condition. This done by means of a modification of the
metric subregularity property
. We prove that for isolated critical points (isolated solutions of the first order necessary condition), the stability of the first order necessary conditions is equivalent to the bang-bang property. As an application of this, we prove a stability result concerning
L^{p}
-norm regularizations.

Section

6
is devoted to illustrate the applicability of our results by means of examples. The first example is an affine optimal control problems constrained by ordinary differential equations, being this the model for the theory here developed. The other examples are concerned with optimization problems constrained by partial differential equations; this includes a classical elliptic problem, and a velocity tracking one.

Finally, Appendix A gives alternative proofs to some of the results in
[
45
]
, which to the best of the author’s knowledge was not done before. We mention that some of the results in
[
45
]
contain flaws, although not substantial ones; see the beginning of Appendix A for more details.

2
The abstract optimal control problem

We consider an abstract optimization problem for which the bang-bang property can be defined. The feasible set resembles the usual
control sets
appearing in optimal control theory.

2.1
The model

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space and consider the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\{u\in{L^{1}(X)^{m}}:u(x)\in U\quad\text{a.e.~in }X\},

(1)

where
U
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We consider the abstract optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{J}(u),

(2)

where
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given real-valued function. The set in (
1
) is called the
feasible set
and the function in (
2
) is called the
objective functional
.

We consider problem (
1
)–(
2
) under the following standing assumption.

Assumption 1
.

We require that the following statements hold.

(i)

(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a finite and nonatomic measure space;

(ii)

U
is a convex compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that contains more than one element;

(iii)

\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is weakly sequentially continuous.

The previous assumption is very reasonable for affine optimal control problems. The reason is that in those problems, the control appears linearly (hence the name), and the weak sequential continuity of the objective functional can be expected. Moreover, under
1
, the feasible set enjoys many good properties.

Proposition 2
.

The feasible set
\mathcal{U}
is a nonempty, convex and weakly sequentially compact subset of
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

Proof.

It follows as a particular case of
[
37
, Theorem 6.4.23]
that
\mathcal{U}
is a nonempty convex weakly compact subset of
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. From the Eberlein–Šmulian Theorem, we can conclude that
\mathcal{U}
must be weakly sequentially compact.

From the previous proposition, for every sequence in the feasible set, we are able to choose a weakly convergent subsequence; which is extremely useful in existence arguments.

Definition 3
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. We define the minimality radius of
u^{*}
as

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{u^{*}}:=\sup\left\{\delta\geq 0:\,\,\mathcal{J}(u^{*})\leq\mathcal{J}(u)\,\,\,\,\text{for all $u\in\mathcal{U}$ with $|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta$}\right\}.

We say that
u^{*}
is a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if
\bar{r}_{u^{*}}>0
. We say that
u^{*}
is a global minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if
\bar{r}_{u^{*}}=+\infty
.

From the weak sequential compactness of the feasible set and the weak sequential continuity of the objective functional, the existence of minimizers follows trivially.

Proposition 4
.

Problem (
1
)–(
2
) has at least one global minimizer.

2.2
Bang-bang property

We give now a precise definition of the
bang-bang property
for elements of
\mathcal{U}
. We denote by
\operatorname{ext}U
the set of extreme points of
U
.

Definition 5
.

We say that
u\in\mathcal{U}
is bang-bang if

\displaystyle u(x)\in\operatorname{ext}U\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in X$.}

Elements of the feasible set with the bang-bang property are of general interest because they saturate the pointwise constraints. For example, in the particular case of a convex polytope, bang-bang elements take only values on the vertices of the polytope almost everywhere. Recall that a convex polytope is the convex hull of finitely many points. In the
2
-dimensional and
3
-dimensional cases, convex polytopes are exactly polygons and polyhedra, respectively.

We now will state a couple of results concerned with the sequences converging weakly to bang-bang elements. The following result was proved first in
[
45
, Corollary 2]
using
[
45
, Theorem 1]
; however there are some inconsistencies in some of the proofs of
[
45
]
. In the Appendix, we comment on some of the flaws in
[
45
]
, and give an alternative proof of this result and other related ones.

Proposition 6
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be bang-bang and
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a sequence. If
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
, then
|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0
.

Proof.

This is a particular case of
theorem

54
.

The phenomenon described in the previous proposition is well known in the calculus of variations, where weakly convergent minimizing sequences are usually also strongly convergent. However, this is not always the case; the existence of bad behaved sequences follows from the following
weak clustering principle
. For any element of the feasible set without the bang-bang property, it is possible to find a sequence in the feasible set converging to it such that the sequence clusters on a sphere of arbitrarily small radius.

Proposition 7
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. If
u^{*}
is not bang-bang,
there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for every
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
there exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
with the following properties.

(i)

|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}=\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

Proof.

This is a particular case of
theorem

57
.

The two previous results can be combined to obtain the following characterization of the bang-bang property.

Theorem 8
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

u^{*}
is bang-bang.

(ii)

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
implies
|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0
for any sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
.

The next result was proved in
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
for local minimizers, in the particular case when the constraints in (
1
) are box-like (in the one-dimensional case
m=1
) and under the additional assumptions of separability and completeness of the measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
.

Corollary 9
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. Suppose that
u^{*}
is not bang-bang.
Then, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
and
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
u\in\mathcal{U}

with

|{u-u^{*}}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}=\delta\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{J}(u)\leq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})+\varepsilon.

(3)

Proof.

This follows from
proposition

7
and the weak sequential continuity of the objective functional.

2.3
Growth of the objective functional at strict local minimizers

We begin recalling the definition of strict minimality, both local and global.

Definition 10
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. We define the strict minimality radius of
u^{*}
as

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{u^{*}}:=\sup\left\{\delta\geq 0:\,\mathcal{J}(u^{*})<\mathcal{J}(u)\,\,\,\,\text{for all $u\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{u^{*}\}$ with $|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta$}\right\}.

We say that
u^{*}
is a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if
\hat{r}_{u^{*}}>0
. We say that
u^{*}
is a strict global minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if
\hat{r}_{u^{*}}=+\infty
.

It was proved in
[
13
, Section 2.1]
that

no growth
of the objective functional at minimizer can occur if the minimizer is not bang-bang. The proof was given for Hölder-type growths, and they point out that the argument also works for more general type of growths. We give here the argument for completeness.

Proposition 11
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. Suppose that there exist
\delta>0
and a function
\omega:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})+\omega\big(|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\big)

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{u^{*}\}
with
|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta
. Then
u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). Moreover,
\delta\leq\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
.

Proof.

If
u\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{u^{*}\}
satisfies
|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})+\omega(|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}})>\mathcal{J}(u^{*}).

Thus,
u^{*}
must be a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) and
\delta\leq\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
.
Suppose that
u^{*}
is not bang-bang, and let
\delta_{0}
be the positive number in Proposition
7
. Then there exist
\eta\in(0,\min\{\delta,\delta_{0}\})
and a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
, satisfying
|u_{n}-u|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}=\eta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\geq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})+\omega(\eta)\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

Since
\mathcal{J}
is weakly sequentially continuous, we get
\omega(\eta)\leq 0
. A contradiction.

The converse of the previous result is also true, the objective functional must satisfy a growth condition at bang-bang strict local minimizers.

Proposition 12
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). Suppose that
u^{*}
is bang-bang. Then there exist
\delta\in(0,\hat{r}_{u^{*}})
and a non-decreasing function
\omega:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})+\omega\big(|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\big)

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{u^{*}\}
with
|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta
.

Proof.

Let
M:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|u-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
and
\delta\in(0,\min\{M,\hat{r}_{u^{*}}\})
be arbitrary. Let
\omega_{\delta}:(0,\delta]\to(0,\infty)
be given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\delta}(\eta):=\inf\left\{\mathcal{J}(u)-\mathcal{J}(u^{*}):u\in\mathcal{U}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta\leq|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta\right\}.

By construction,
\omega
is nonnegative and non-decreasing. Suppose that there exists
\eta\in(0,\delta]
such that
\omega(\eta)=0
. By definition of infimum, there would exist a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\eta\leq|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\delta\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\mathcal{J}(u_{n})-\mathcal{J}(u^{*})\leq\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

(4)

We can extract a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
to some
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{U}
. Since
\mathcal{J}
is weakly sequentially continuous, from (
4
), we get
\mathcal{J}(\hat{u})=\mathcal{J}(u^{*})
. Since
u^{*}
is a strict local minimizer and

\displaystyle|\hat{u}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}|u_{n_{k}}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta<\hat{r}_{u^{*}},

we conclude
\hat{u}=u^{*}
. This implies that
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
u^{*}
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
, and as
u^{*}
is bang-bang,
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
must converge to
u^{*}
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
; a contradiction. The result follows defining
\omega:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
as

\omega(\eta):=\omega_{\delta}(\min(\eta,\delta))
.

3
Genericity of the bang-bang property

3.1
The Radon–Nikodým property

One of the drawbacks of Bochner integration theory is that the Radon–Nikodým Theorem fails to hold in general. Sets for which this result still holds define an important class that has been studied extensively; see, e.g., the specialized book
[
6
]
.

In this subsection, we give a short review of these sets in the particular case where the underlying Banach space is
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. We begin with the definition of dentability; for the general definition see
[
5
, Definition 6.3.2]
or
[
38
, Section 1.7.1]
.

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a nonempty subset of
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. The elements of
\{S(\mathcal{W},\xi,\delta):\,\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}},\,\delta>0\}
are called slices of
\mathcal{W}
, where

\displaystyle S\big(\mathcal{W},\xi,\delta\big):=\{\,w\in\mathcal{W}:\,\xi w\leq\inf_{v\in\mathcal{W}}\xi v+\delta\}\quad\text{for $\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}$ and $\delta>0$}.

We say that the subset
\mathcal{W}
of
L^{1}(X)^{m}
is dentable if it admits arbitrarily small slices, i.e.,
for every
\varepsilon>0
there exist
\delta>0
and
\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
such that
\operatorname{diam}S(\mathcal{W},\xi,\delta)\leq\varepsilon
.

We now give the definition of the Radon–Nikodým Property (RNP). There are many different ones, as the property has been characterized in many ways, see, e.g., the book
[
6
]
or the survey
[
28
]
for geometrical characterizations. Here we give a definition based on dentability of sets, see
[
5
, Definition 6.3.3]
or
[
38
, Section 1.7.1]
.

Definition 14
.

A subset
\mathcal{V}
of
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
has the Radon–Nikodým Property if every nonempty bounded subset
\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{V}
is dentable.

We mention that the family of sets having the RNP is quite rich, it includes all reflexive spaces, see
[
6
, Corollary 4.1.5]
, in particular the
L^{p}
-spaces for
p\in(1,\infty)
. There are non-reflexive spaces without the RNP, such as
L^{1}([0,1])
, see
[
6
, Example 2.1.2]
. However, subsets of
L^{1}
-spaces might possess the property even if the whole space does not have it. For example, it is known that weakly compact convex subsets of Banach spaces have the RNP, see
[
6
, Theorem 3.6.1]
.

Proposition 15
.

The feasible set
\mathcal{U}
has the Radon–Nikodým Property.

Proof.

By
proposition

2
,
\mathcal{U}
is weakly sequentially compact, and hence by the Eberlein–Šmulian Theorem, weakly compact. We can use then
[
6
, Theorem 3.6.1]
to conclude that
\mathcal{U}
has the Radon–Nikodým Property.

3.2
Strong minimizers and well-posedness

This subsection is devoted to recall one of the classical concepts of well-posedness, the
Tikhonov
one. In order to do so, we first recall the definition of
strong minimality
, see
[
5
, Definition 6.3.4]
.

Definition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
be a functional and
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
a minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
. We say that
u^{*}
is a strong minimizer if

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(u_{n})\to\mathcal{F}(u^{*})\,\,\,\,\text{implies}\,\,\,\,|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\to 0

for any sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
.

Using
theorem

8
, we can easily characterize strong minimizers in terms of the bang-bang property.

Proposition 17
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
be a weakly sequentially continuous functional and
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

u^{*}
is a strong minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
u^{*}
is a strong minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
, and let
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a sequence converging weakly to
u^{*}
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is weakly sequentially continuous,
\mathcal{F}(u_{n})\to\mathcal{F}(u^{*})
, and thus
u_{n}\to u^{*}
. We can use
theorem

8
to conclude that
u^{*}
must be bang-bang. It is clear that strong minimizers are strict.

Conversely, suppose that
u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict minimizer, and let
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a sequence such that
\mathcal{F}(u_{n})\to\mathcal{F}(u^{*})
. Let
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We can extract a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k_{j}}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
to some
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{U}
. Since
\mathcal{F}(u_{n_{k_{j}}})\to\mathcal{F}(u^{*})
, we obtain
\mathcal{F}(\hat{u})=\mathcal{F}(u^{*})
, but since
u^{*}
is a strict minimizer, we must have
\hat{u}=u^{*}
. Since every subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has further a subsequence that converges weakly to
u^{*}
, we conclude that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
; but then, by
theorem

8
,
u_{n}\to u^{*}
strongly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

We now give the definition of well-posedness, see
[
38
, Section 1.7]
.

Definition 18
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
. The optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{F}(u)

is said to be well-posed if
\mathcal{F}
has strong minimizer.

We can use
proposition

17
to see how the well-posedness of problem (
1
)–(
2
) is related to the bang-bang property.

Corollary 19
.

Problem (
1
)–(
2
) is well-posed if and only if it possesses a bang-bang strict global minimizer.

3.3
Stegall’s principle and the bang-bang property

In nonlinear analysis and
optimization, the notions of variational principle, perturbation and well-posedness are intrinsically related. The concept of genericity is of fundamental importance in the interplay of these notions. In topology, a generic property is usually one that holds on a dense open set, however this can be a very strong requirement; for example, the irrational numbers are somehow generic among the real numbers, but they do not conform a open dense subset of the real line.
More generally, a generic property is one that holds on a residual set, being the dual concept a meager set. We recall now the definition of residuality in the particular case of the space
{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
.

Definition 20
.

Let
\Theta
be a subset of
{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
. We say that
\Theta
is residual if there exists a countable family of open dense sets
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\subset{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
such that

\displaystyle\Theta=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{n}.

A set is said to be meager if it is the complement of a residual set.

From Baire Category Theorem, it is clear that residual subsets of
{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
are dense. We now give the definition of genericity, see
[
38
, Section 7]
.

Definition 21
.

A subset of
{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
is said to be generic if it contains a residual set. A property is said to be generic if it holds on a generic set.

We come now to a classic in variational analysis, Stegall’s principle. This is a type of smooth variational principle over sets with the RNP. It appeared first in
[
44
, pp. 174-176]
, see
[
38
, Theorem 1.153]
or
[
5
, Theorem 6.3.5]
for book references. We state now a version of the theorem, as a particular case of
[
31
, Theorem 5]
.

Theorem 22
.

The set

\displaystyle\{\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}:\,\,\text{problem\, $\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\{\mathcal{J}(u)-\xi u\}$ is well-posed}\}

is generic. In other words, the well-posedness of linearly perturbed versions of problem (
1
)–(
2
) is generic.

Proof.

By
proposition

15
, the feasible set
\mathcal{U}
has the RNP. Since the objective functional
\mathcal{J}
is weakly sequentially continuous, it is in particular sequentially continuous, and hence continuous. We can then employ the implication
(v)\implies(iii)
of
[
31
, Theorem 5]
to conclude the result.

We are going now to reformulate the previous theorem in a way that makes transparent that the bang-bang property is generic.

Theorem 23
.

The set

\displaystyle\left\{\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}:\,\,\,\mathcal{J}-\xi\,\,\,\text{has a bang-bang strict global minimizer}\right\}

is generic. In other words, the existence of bang-bang strict global minimizers of linearly perturbed problems of problem (
1
)–(
2
) is generic.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be the set of
\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
such that
\mathcal{J}-\xi
has a bang-bang strict global minimizer.
By
theorem

22
, there exists a residual set
\Theta\subset{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
such that
problem
\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\{\mathcal{J}(u)-\xi u\}
is well-posed for
\xi\in\Theta
.
By
proposition

17
,
\Theta
is contained in
\Lambda
; thus
\Lambda
is generic.

Corollary 24
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
with
|\xi|_{{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}}\leq\varepsilon
such that
\mathcal{J}-\xi
has a bang-bang strict global minimizer.

Proof.

By
theorem

23
, there exists a residual set
\Theta\subset{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
such that for any
\xi\in\Theta
,
\mathcal{J}-\xi
has a bang-bang strict global minimizer. The result follows from Baire Category Theorem, as it implies that residual sets of
{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
are dense.

4
Stability under linear perturbations

We study the relation of linear perturbations to problem (
1
)–(
2
) and the bang-bang property. We begin by describing the solution mappings that we consider. We give new characterizations of the bang-bang property in terms of hemicontinuity of these mappings. Finally, in the last subsection, we reformulate the stability properties to make clearer their meaning.

4.1
Solution mappings

When studying stability of an optimization problem, it is often possible to prove that perturbed problems have solutions in a neighborhood of a reference solution. This type of solutions can serve as a first approach for the stability analysis.

Given
\xi\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
,
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\gamma>0
, we consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi):\quad\min\left\{\mathcal{J}(u)-\xi u:\,\,\,\,u\in\mathcal{U}\,\,\,\,\text{with}\,\,\,\,|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\gamma\right\}.

Before advancing further, let us mention that each problem has at least one solution.

Proposition 25
.

Each problem
\mathcal{P}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)
has at least one minimizer, i.e, there exists
u=u_{u^{*},\gamma,\xi}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)-\xi u\leq\mathcal{J}(w)-\xi w\quad\text{for all $w\in\mathcal{U}$ with $|w-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\gamma$}.

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{V}:=\{u\in\mathcal{U}:\,\,|u-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\gamma\}
is closed and convex, hence weakly closed. As by
proposition

2
,
\mathcal{U}
is weakly sequentially compact, so is
\mathcal{V}
. Clearly, each map
\mathcal{J}-\xi:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is weakly sequentially continuous; therefore, problem
\mathcal{P}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)
must have at least one global minimizer.

With each problem
\mathcal{P}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)
, we associate a
localized solution mapping
. This is a set-valued mapping, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}:{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}L^{1}(X)^{m}
and given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi):=\left\{u\in\mathcal{U}:\text{$u$ is a minimizer of problem $\mathcal{P}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)$}\right\}.

Proposition 26
.

Each set-valued mapping
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
takes nonempty closed values.

Proof.

It follows from
proposition

25
that each
S_{u^{*},\gamma}
takes nonempty values. It follows from the weak sequential continuity of the objective functional that each
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
takes closed values.

We can recover the usual solution mappings from the localized ones.
The
local solution mapping

\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}:{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}\to{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}(\xi):=\left\{u\in\mathcal{U}:\,u\in\mathcal{S}_{u,\gamma}(\xi)\,\,\,\,\text{for some $\gamma>0$}\right\}.

Each set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}(\xi)
consists of the local minimizers of
\mathcal{J}-\xi
on
\mathcal{U}
.
In the same fashion, we define the
global solution mapping

\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}:{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}\to{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi):=\left\{u\in\mathcal{U}:\,u\in\mathcal{S}_{u,\gamma}(\xi)\,\,\,\,\text{for all $\gamma>0$}\right\}.

Similarly, each set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi)
consists of the global minimizers of
\mathcal{J}-\xi
on
\mathcal{U}
.

4.2
Hemicontinuity

It is now time to study the continuity properties of the solution mappings described in the previous subsection. We do this by means of the notion of hemicontinuity; we use standard definitions, see
[
1
, Definition 17.2]
. The term semicontinuity is used sometimes instead of hemicontinuity; see e.g.,
[
4
, Section 1.4.1]
.

We begin studying the lower hemicontinuity properties of the mappings in relation with the bang-bang property; in order to do so, we employ the following sequential characterization of lower hemicontinuity, see
[
1
, Theorem 17.21]
.

Proposition 27
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:L^{\infty}(X)^{m}\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}L^{1}(X)^{m}
be a set-valued mapping. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{S}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
.

(ii)

For every sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
converging to
0
in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and every
u\in\mathcal{S}(0)
, there exists a subsequence
\{\xi_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{1}(X)^{m}
such that

\displaystyle u_{k}\in\mathcal{S}(\xi_{n_{k}})\,\,\,\,\text{for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{k}\to u\,\,\,\text{in $L^{1}(X)^{m}$}.

We are now ready for our first result. The proof consist of two main ingredients, the weak clustering principle (
proposition

7
) and the construction of adequate perturbations. For the latter, we use the celebrated Hahn-Banach Theorem.

Proposition 28
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). Suppose that there exists
\gamma>0
such that
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
. Then
u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Proof.

Suppose that
u^{*}
is not bang-bang. By
proposition

7
, there exists a positive number
\delta<\min\{\hat{r}_{u^{*}},\gamma\}
and sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle u_{n}\rightharpoonup u^{*}\,\,\,\,\text{weakly in $L^{1}(X)^{m}$}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{$|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}=\delta$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

By the Hahn-Banach Theorem, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\xi_{n}\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\xi_{n}(u_{n}-u^{*})=|\xi_{n}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\quad\text{and}\quad|\xi_{n}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}=\frac{4}{\delta}|\mathcal{J}(u_{n})-\mathcal{J}(u^{*})|

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\delta<\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
, it follows that
\mathcal{J}(u^{*})<\mathcal{J}(u_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence
|\xi_{n}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Also, from the weak sequential continuity of the objective functional, it follows that
\xi_{n}\to 0
in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
.

Now, as
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
and
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(0)=\{u^{*}\}
, there exists a subsequence
\{\xi_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
u^{*}
such that
w_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi_{n_{k}})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(w_{k})-\xi_{n_{k}}w_{k}\leq\mathcal{J}(u_{n_{k}})-\xi_{n_{k}}u_{n_{k}}\quad\text{for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(5)

Since
w_{k}\to u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|w_{k}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq 2^{-1}\delta<\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
for
k\geq k_{0}
, and hence
\mathcal{J}(u^{*})\leq\mathcal{J}(w_{k})
for
k\geq k_{0}
. Combing this with
eq.

5
, we get

\displaystyle-|\mathcal{J}(u^{*})-\mathcal{J}(u_{n_{k}})|=\mathcal{J}(u^{*})-\mathcal{J}(u_{n_{k}})\leq\mathcal{J}(w_{k})-\mathcal{J}(u_{n_{k}})\leq\xi_{n_{k}}\big(w_{k}-u_{n_{k}}\big)

for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Now, by construction of the sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{4}|\xi_{n_{k}}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{J}(u^{*})-\mathcal{J}(u_{n_{k}})|\geq\xi_{n_{k}}\big(u_{n_{k}}-w_{k}\big)

\displaystyle=\xi_{n_{k}}\big(u_{n_{k}}-u^{*}\big)+\xi_{n_{k}}\big(u^{*}-w_{k}\big)

\displaystyle\geq|\xi_{n_{k}}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}\big(\delta-|w_{k}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\big)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}|\xi_{n_{k}}|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}

for all
k\geq k_{0}
. This yields a contradiction.

We proceed now to analyze the upper hemicontinuity of localized solution mappings. The following sequential characterization will be of use, see
[
1
, Theorem 17.20]
.

Proposition 29
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:L^{\infty}(X)^{m}\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}L^{1}(X)^{m}
be a set-valued mapping. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{S}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
and
\mathcal{S}(0)
is compact.

(ii)

If
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{1}(X)^{m}
are sequences such that

\displaystyle u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\xi_{n})\,\,\,\,\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{n}\to 0\,\,\,\text{in $L^{\infty}(X)^{m}$},

then the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a limit point in
\mathcal{S}(0)
.

It turns out that the bang-bang property can imply upper hemicontinuity at a point when the localized solution mappings are single valued at that point.

Proposition 30
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) and
\gamma\in(0,\hat{r}_{u^{*}})
. If
u^{*}
is bang-bang, then
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
.

Proof.

Let
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
be a sequence such that
\xi_{n}\to 0
in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
a sequence such that
u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u_{n})-\xi_{n}u_{n}\leq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})-\xi_{n}u^{*}\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(6)

We can extract a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging weakly to some
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{U}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
.
Taking limit in
eq.

6
, we conclude that
\mathcal{J}(\hat{u})\leq\mathcal{J}(u^{*})
. As
\gamma<\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
and

\displaystyle|\hat{u}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\liminf_{k\to 0}|u_{n_{k}}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\gamma,

we conclude that
\hat{u}=u^{*}
. Hence,
u_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
, and as
u^{*}
is bang-bang,
u_{n_{k}}\to u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
; thus
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a limit point in
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(0)=\{u^{*}\}
.

We can now put together the two previous results in a single theorem characterizing the bang-bang property in terms of hemicontinuity.

Theorem 31
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
) and
\gamma\in(0,\hat{r}_{u^{*}})
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
.

(iii)

u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Proof.

The implication
(ii)\implies(iii)
follows from
proposition

28
and the implication
(iii)\implies(i)
follows from
proposition

29
. We proceed then to prove the implication
(i)\implies(ii)
. We first observe that
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(0):=\{u^{*}\}
since
\gamma<\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
. Let
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
be any sequence converging to zero in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
. There exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
satisfying
u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
As
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
,
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has
u^{*}
as limit point; hence there exists a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
.

We can also give a characterization in terms of the global solution mapping.

Corollary 32
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a strict global minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}
is lower hemicontinuous at
0
.

(iii)

u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Proof.

Choose
\gamma>0
such that
\mathcal{U}\subset\{u\in L^{1}(X)^{m}:\,\,|u-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\gamma\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi)=\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)\quad\text{for all $\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}$}.

Consequently, the result follows from
theorem

31
.

We close the subsection with the following result relating the bang-bang property to the upper hemicontinuity at zero of the local solution mapping.

Corollary 33
.

Suppose that problem (
1
)–(
2
) has unique local minimizer
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. If
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
, then
u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}(0)=\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(0)=\{u^{*}\}
.
Let
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
be sequence converging to zero in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
. There exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}(\xi_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As
\mathcal{S}_{\text{loc}}
is assumed to be upper hemicontinuous at
0
, it follows that
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
u_{n_{k}}\to u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
. Thus, we conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}
is lower hemicontinuous. Then, by
corollary

32
,
u^{*}
must be bang-bang.

4.3
Local stability of linear perturbations

In the previous subsection, the stability properties of problem (
1
)–(
2
) were studied in terms of hemicontinuity, whose sequential characterization involves subsequences and limit points.
In this subsection, we restate these results in terms that make clearer their meaning.

Definition 34
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). We say that problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
if there exists
\gamma>0
with the property that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\xi|_{{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}}<\delta\qquad\text{implies}\qquad|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}<\varepsilon

(7)

for any
u\in\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)
and any
\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
.
We define the stability radius of
u^{*}
as the positive number
\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}}:=\sup\left\{\gamma>0:\,\text{(\ref{Staprope}) holds}\right\}
.

The definition of local stability says that small perturbations in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
should imply that all solutions of localized perturbed problems be close to
u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
. This agrees with the common understanding of stability.

We come now to a characterization of local stability in terms of the bang-bang property; the result follows easily from the hemicontinuity properties studied in the previous subsection.

Theorem 35
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

Problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
.

(ii)

u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
).

Moreover, if problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
, then
\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}}=\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
. It follows immediately from the definition of local stability that
u^{*}
is strict local minimizer and that
\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}}\leq\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
. It is also easy to see that
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
for any
\gamma\in(0,\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}})
, and hence that
u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Suppose now that
u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). Suppose that
\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}}<\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
, and let
\gamma\in(\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}},\hat{r}_{u^{*}})
. Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
, a sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
converging to zero in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad u_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi_{n})\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

By
proposition

29
,
\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}
is upper hemicontinuous at
0
. Consequently,
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must have a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
u_{n_{k}}\to u^{*}
in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
; this yields a contradiction. We conclude that problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
and that
\hat{\gamma}_{u^{*}}=\hat{r}_{u^{*}}
.

We now pass to the global analysis. For the sake of clarity and transparency, we give a definition that reflects the intuitive understanding of global stability.

Definition 36
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(0)
. We say that problem (
1
)–(
2
) is globally stable at
u^{*}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\xi|_{{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}}<\delta\quad\text{implies}\quad|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}<\varepsilon

for any
\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and
u\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi)
.

From
theorem

35
, we can deduce immediately the following result.

Corollary 37
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a global minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

Problem (
1
)–(
2
) is globally stable at
u^{*}
.

(ii)

u^{*}
is a bang-bang strict global minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
).

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{U}\subset\{u\in L^{1}(X)^{m}:\,\,|u-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\gamma_{0}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{gbl}}(\xi)=\mathcal{S}_{u^{*},\gamma}(\xi)\quad\text{for all $\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}$ and all $\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}$}.

The result follows then from
theorem

35
.

5
Stability of the first-order necessary condition

We study now the stability with respect to perturbations of the first-order necessary condition of problem (
1
)–(
2
). In general, the first-order necessary condition (the local Pontryagin principle for optimal control problems) can be written as an inclusion, the so-called optimality system. Thus the stability properties of the first-order necessary condition can be directly analyzed from this inclusion. We will employ a concept of stability based on the so-called
strong metric subregularity
property. The definition of this property was given first in
[
21
, Definition 5.1]
. The recent paper
[
14
]
gives a good overview of the utility of this property and its role in variational analysis and optimization. For book references, see
[
20
, Lecture 12]
or
[
22
, Section 3I]
.

5.1
The first-order necessary condition

We recall briefly the fist order necessary conditions for problem (
1
)–(
2
).
We will employ the classic notion of (first-order) Gateaux differentiability.

Definition 38
.

The objective functional
{\mathcal{J}}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be Gateaux differentiable if for every
u\in\mathcal{U}
there exists
d\mathcal{J}(u)\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
such that

\displaystyle d{\mathcal{J}}(u)v=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{{\mathcal{J}}(u+\varepsilon v)-{\mathcal{J}}(u)}{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all $v\in L^{1}(X)^{m}$ with $u+v\in\mathcal{U}$.}

The first-order necessary condition is well known, see, e.g.,
[
20
, pp. 11-13]
.

Lemma 39
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is Gateaux differentiable. If
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
is a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
), then

\displaystyle d\mathcal{J}(u^{*})(u-u^{*})\geq 0\quad\text{for all $u\in\mathcal{U}$.}

(8)

In order to talk about the stability of the first-order necessary conditions, during this section, we will of course assume that the objective functional is Gateaux differentiable; and moreover, a
weak-strong
continuity property on the derivative. The following assumption is supposed to hold throughout the remainder of this section.

Assumption 40
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

The objective functional
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is Gateaux differentiable;

(ii)

the mapping
\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{U}\to L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
given by
\mathcal{Q}(u):=d\mathcal{J}(u)
is weakly-strongly sequentially continuous,
i.e.,

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
implies
\mathcal{Q}(u_{n})\to\mathcal{Q}(u)
in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}

for any sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
and any
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

In analogy with optimal control, we write
\sigma_{u}:=\mathcal{Q}(u)
for each
u\in\mathcal{U}
. The mapping
\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{U}\to L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
in
(ii)
of
40
is called the
switching mapping
.

Let us recall that the normal cone to
\mathcal{U}
at
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
is given by

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{U}}(u^{*}):=\left\{\xi\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}:\langle\xi,u-u^{*}\rangle\leq 0\quad\text{for all $u\in\mathcal{U}$}\right\}.

For
u^{*}\in L^{1}(X)^{m}\setminus\mathcal{U}
, we set
N_{\mathcal{U}}(u^{*})=\emptyset
.
We can then rewrite the first-order necessary condition as the inclusion

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma_{u}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(u).

(9)

The correspondence
\Phi:\mathcal{U}\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
given by
\Phi(u):=\sigma_{u}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(u)
is called
the optimality mapping
.
We now give a definition concerned with inclusion (
9
).

Definition 41
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be given.

(i)

u^{*}
is said to be a critical point of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if
0\in\Phi(u^{*})
;

(ii)

u^{*}
is said to be a locally isolated critical point of problem (
1
)–(
2
) if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\text{$0\in\Phi(u)$ implies $u=u^{*}$ for all $u\in\mathcal{U}$ with $|u-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\leq\delta$}.

(10)

The critical radius of
u^{*}
is given by
\check{r}_{u^{*}}:=\sup\left\{\delta>0:\,\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{iscrimin} holds}\right\}
.

5.2
Subregularity of the optimality mapping

We are now going to study the stability of inclusion (
9
) under perturbations. We will employ the following definition based on the notion of
strong metric subregularity
, see
[
14
, Introduction]
.

Definition 42
.

Let
u^{*}
be a critical point of problem (
1
)–(
2
). We say that the optimality mapping
\Phi:\mathcal{U}\to L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
if there exists
\kappa>0
with the property that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\xi|_{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}<\delta\quad\text{implies}\quad|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}<\varepsilon

(11)

for any
u\in\mathcal{U}
with
|u-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\kappa
and any
\xi\in\Phi(u)
.
We define the subregularity radius of
u^{*}
as
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}:=\sup\left\{\kappa>0:\text{ property (\ref{Stapropefo}) holds}\right\}
.

We state now a trivial consequence of the definition of subregularity.

Proposition 43
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
. Then
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}\leq\check{r}_{u^{*}}
. In particular, if the optimality mapping is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
, then
u^{*}
is a locally isolated critical point of problem (
1
)–(
2
).

The following theorem states the necessity of the bang-bang property.

Lemma 44
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). If the optimality mapping is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
, then
u^{*}
is bang-bang.

Proof.

The subregularity of the optimality mapping at
u^{*}
clearly implies that problem (
1
)–(
2
) is locally stable at
u^{*}
. By
theorem

35
,
u^{*}
must be bang-bang.

We arrive now to the main result of this section.

Theorem 45
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a local minimizer of problem (
1
)–(
2
). The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

The optimality mapping is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
.

(ii)

u^{*}
is a bang-bang locally isolated critical point of problem (
1
)–(
2
).

Moreover, if the optimality mapping is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
, then
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}=\check{r}_{u^{*}}
.

Proof.

The implication
(i)\implies(ii)
follows from
proposition

43
and
lemma

44
.

Let us prove now the implication
(ii)\implies(i)
. From
proposition

43
, we have
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}\leq\check{r}_{u^{*}}
.
Towards a contradiction, suppose that
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}<\check{r}_{u^{*}}
and let
\delta\in(\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}},\check{r}_{u^{*}})
. Then there exist a number
\varepsilon>0
, a sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
converging to zero in
L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
and a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle\delta\geq|u_{n}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{n}\in\sigma_{u_{n}}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(u_{n})\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

We can extract a subsequence
\{u_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging weakly to some
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{U}
. Now, since each
u_{n_{k}}
satisfies
\xi_{n_{k}}\in\sigma_{u_{n_{k}}}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(u_{n_{k}})
, taking limit, we obtain
0\in\sigma_{\hat{u}}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(\hat{u})
. As
u^{*}
is a locally isolated critical point of problem (
1
)-(
2
) and

\displaystyle|\hat{u}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}|u_{n_{k}}-u^{*}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\leq\delta<\hat{r}_{u^{*}},

we conclude that
\hat{u}=u^{*}
, and hence that
u_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup u^{*}
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. But, as
u^{*}
, is bang-bang, it must be that
u_{n_{k}}\to u^{*}
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
; a contradiction. Then
\hat{\kappa}_{u^{*}}=\check{r}_{u^{*}}
.

5.3
An application:
p
-regularization

We give now an application of the subregularity property concerned with
Tykhonov regularizations
.

Let
p>1
be given. For each
\eta>0
, we consider the following
regularized optimization problem
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{p}(\eta):\,\,\,\,\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{\mathcal{J}(u)+\frac{\eta}{p}\int_{X}|u(x)|^{p}\,d\mu(x).\right\}.

From subregularity, we can conclude the following regularization result.

Theorem 46
.

Let
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
be a critical point of problem (
1
)-(
2
). Suppose that optimality mapping is strongly subregular at
u^{*}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\eta<\eta_{\varepsilon}\,\,\,\,\,\text{implies}\,\,\,\,\,|u_{\eta}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}<\varepsilon

for any local minimizer
u_{\eta}\in\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\eta)
satisfying
|u_{\eta}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}<\hat{\kappa}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary, and
let
u_{\eta}\in\mathcal{U}
be any local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\zeta)
satisfying
|u_{\eta}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}<\hat{\kappa}
. The first order necessary condition can be written as

\displaystyle d\mathcal{J}(u_{\eta})(u-u_{\eta})+\eta\int_{X}|u_{\eta}(x)|^{p-2}u_{\eta}(x)\cdot(u(x)-u_{\eta}(x))\,d\mu(x)\geq 0\quad\text{ $\forall u\in\mathcal{U}$.}

This can be rewritten as the inclusion
\xi_{\eta}\in\sigma_{u_{\eta}}+N_{\mathcal{U}}(u_{\eta})
,
where
\xi_{\eta}\in L^{\infty}(X)^{m}
is given by
\xi_{\eta}(x):=\eta|u_{\eta}(x)|^{p-2}u_{\eta}(x)
.
By strong subregularity of the optimality mapping at
u^{*}
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that if
|\xi_{\eta}|_{L^{\infty}(X)}<\delta_{\varepsilon}
, then
|u_{\eta}-u^{*}|_{L^{1}(X)}<\varepsilon
. It is enough then to take
\eta_{\varepsilon}:=\delta_{\varepsilon}\big[\sup U\big]^{1-p}

We omit the case
p=1
as it is a bit more involved, however and identical result can be accomplished by means of subregularity.

6
Examples of the theory

We provide three examples of the class of optimal control problems studied in this paper. The first one is constrained by an ordinary differential equation, and the second and third ones by partial differential equations.

6.1
Affine optimal control problems constrained by ordinary differential equations

As a canonical example of the theory developed in previous sections, we consider the
affine optimal control problem
given by

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{s_{T}(y_{u}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\Big[g_{0}\big(t,y_{u}(t)\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\big(t,y_{u}(u)\big)u_{i}(t)\Big]\,dt\right\},

(12)

where for each control
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathcal{U}
, there is a unique state
y_{u}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{u}=f_{0}(\cdot,y_{u})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(\cdot,y_{u})u_{i},\qquad y_{u}(0)=y_{0}.

(13)

We give below the specifications of problem (
12
)–(
13
) and the technical details.

The number
T>0
is the (fixed)
time horizon
. The underlying measure space
([0,T],\mathcal{A}_{[0,T]},\mathcal{L})
consists of the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{[0,T]}
of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
[0,T]
, and
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,T]
.

For a compact convex set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the feasible set takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\left\{u\in L^{1}(0,T)^{m}:\,\,u(t)\in U\,\,\,\text{for a.e. $t\in[0,T]$}\right\}.

The functions
f_{0},\dots,f_{m}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are Carathéodory and satisfy

\displaystyle\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{i}(t,x)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|f_{i}(t,x_{1})-f_{i}(t,x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}<\infty

for each
i\in\{0,\dots,m\}
.
These functions and the
initial datum

y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
determine the dynamic for each control in the following way. For each
u\in\mathcal{U}
, by the classical global existence theorem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 2.1.1]
), there exists a unique state
y_{u}\in W^{1,1}\big([0,T]\big)^{n}
satisfying (
13
), i.e.,

\displaystyle y_{u}(t)=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big[f_{0}(s,y_{u}(s))+\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(s,y_{u}(s))u_{i}(s)\Big]\,ds\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

The
cost functions

g_{0},\dots g_{m}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are Carathéodory and the
scrap function

s_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.
The objective functional
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)=s_{T}(y_{u}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\Big[g_{0}\big(t,y_{u}(t)\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\big(t,y_{u}(u)\big)u_{i}(t)\Big]\,dt.

Clearly, problem (
12
)–(
13
) trivially satisfies
(i)
and
(ii)
of Assumption
1
. Item
(iii)
is well-known to hold for these type of problems. Indeed, it follows easily from the integral form of the Grönwall inequality that the
input-output mapping

\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{U}\to C\big([0,T]\big)^{n}
, given by
\mathcal{S}(u)=y_{u}
, is weakly-strongly sequentially continuous. From this and the affine structure of the problem, a few calculations yield that the objective functional
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is weakly sequentially continuous.

6.2
Elliptic optimal control problems

We consider now an
elliptic optimal control problem
.

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}L(x,y_{u}(x))\,dx\right\},

(14)

where for each control
u\in\mathcal{U}
, there is a unique state
y_{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta y_{u}+d(\cdot,y_{u})=u\quad\text{in $\Omega$},\quad\,y_{u}=0\quad\text{on $\Omega$}.

(15)

The specifications of problem (
14
)–(
15
) and the data assumptions are given below.

The underlying measure space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega},\mathcal{L})
consists of a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\Omega
, and
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
.

For numbers
u_{a},u_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
with
u_{a}<u_{b}
, the feasible set takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\left\{u\in L^{1}(\Omega):\,\,u(x)\in[u_{a},u_{b}]\,\,\,\text{for a.e. $x\in\Omega$}\right\}.

The function
d:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Carathéodory, monotone nondecreasing with respect to the second variable and satisfies

\displaystyle\esssup_{x\in\Omega}\sup_{y\in K}|d(x,y)|<\infty

for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
.
For each control
u\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a unique
y_{u}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})
(see, e.g.,

[
11
, Theorem 2.7]
) satisfying (
15
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big[\nabla y_{u}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)+d(x,y(x))\varphi(x)\Big]\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\quad\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

The function
L:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Carathéodory and bounded by below.
The objective functional
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)=\int_{\Omega}L(x,y_{u}(x))\,dx.

Clearly, problem (
14
)–(
15
) trivially satisfies
(i)
and
(ii)
of Assumption
1
. One can easily prove that the
control-to-state mapping

\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{U}\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap C(\Omega)
, given by
\mathcal{S}(u)=y_{u}
, is weakly-strongly sequentially continuous. For example, using the arguments given in
[
17
, Lemma 2.5]
and
[
17
, Proposition 2.12]
. From this and the form of the cost function, it is almost trivial that the objective functional
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is weakly sequentially continuous.

6.3
A velocity tracking problem

In this subsection, we discuss an optimization problem constrained by the Navier-Stokes equations. We skip technical details and rather point to the relevant references.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a domain with boundary of class
C^{2}
and
T>0
. Denote
Q:=\Omega\times(0,T)
,
\Sigma:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
and let
\bm{u}_{a},\bm{u}_{b}:Q\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be bounded functions. The feasible set is

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{\bm{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,\,\bm{u}_{a}(x,t)\leq\bm{u}(x,t)\leq\bm{u}_{b}(x,t)\,\,\,\,\text{for a.e. $(x,t)\in Q$}\right\}.

(16)

For a given
\bm{y}_{d}\in L^{\infty}(Q)
, the optimal control is given by

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\bm{y_{u}}-\bm{y}_{d}|^{2}\,dx\,dt\right\}.

(17)

subject to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\bm{y_{u}}-\nu\Delta\bm{y_{u}}+(\bm{y_{u}}\cdot\nabla)\bm{y_{u}}+\nabla\bm{p_{u}}=\bm{u}\,\,\,\,\text{in Q},\\
\\
\dive\bm{y_{u}}=0\,\,\,\,\text{in Q},\,\,\bm{y_{u}}=0\,\,\,\,\text{on $\Sigma$},\,\,\,\bm{y_{u}}(\cdot,0)=\bm{y}_{0}\,\,\,\text{in $\Omega$}.\end{array}\right.

We refer the reader to
[
9
, Section 2]
for technical details of this problem. It was proved in
[
9
, Corollary 2.4]
that the control-to-state operator maps weakly convergent sequences to strongly convergent ones. This can easily be used to conclude that weak sequential continuity of the objective functional.

Appendix A
Convergence under extremal conditions

There are spaces where weak convergence and strong convergence are equivalent, such spaces are said to have the Schur property; the canonical example being the space
l^{1}(\mathbb{N})
of summable sequences.

Unfortunately, general
L^{1}
-spaces may not possess the Schur property, being the space
{L^{1}([0,1])}
the classic counterexample.
In
[
45
]
several results regarding these type of properties for particular sequences in general
L^{1}
-spaces were given, being probably the most important the following one.

Theorem 47
.

[
45
, Theorem 1]

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a complete
\sigma
-finite measure space. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{1}(X)^{m}
be a sequence of functions converging weakly in
L^{1}(X)^{m}
to a function
f\in L^{1}(X)^{m}
. If

f(x)
is an extremal point of
K(x):=\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\Big(\{f(x)\}\cup\{f_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Big)

for a.e.
x\in X
, then
|f_{n}-f|_{L^{1}(X)^{m}}\to 0
.

Nevertheless, the notation in the proof
[
45
, Theorem 1]
is sloppy and quite confusing sometimes. Moreover, it ignores many measurability issues. In this paper, we need the following result, proved in
[
45
]
as a corollary of
[
45
, Theorem 1]
.

Corollary 48
.

[
45
, Corollary 2]

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a complete
\sigma
-finite measure space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values, and
f\in{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
such that
f(x)\in\operatorname{ext}F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
be a sequence of functions such that
f_{n}(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. If
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
, then
f_{n}\to f
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

We use the first subsection of this appendix to provide an alternative proof of
[

. We mention that the result we give (

) is somewhat different in the assumptions. We assume that the set-valued mapping in

with

In the second subsection of this appendix we give a different version of a result related to

A.1
The extremal Schur’s property

We first prove some preparatory lemmas. The first one is concerned with the
equi-integrability
of weakly precompact sets; we refer the reader to
[
46
, Section 15]
for the definition of equi-integrability and its consequences.

Lemma 49
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space and
\mathcal{V}
be a weakly relatively compact subset of
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.
The following statements hold.

(i)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0

such that

\displaystyle\mu(A)<\delta\quad\text{implies}\quad\sup_{f\in\mathcal{V}}\int_{A}|f(x)|d\mu(x)<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all $A\in\mathcal{A}$.}

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite measure set
B\in\mathcal{A}

such that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{V}}\int_{X\setminus B}|f|d\mu<\varepsilon.

Proof.

By the Dunford-Pettis Theorem, the set
\mathcal{V}
is a family of equi-integrable functions; see
[
46
, Theorem 15.4]
. We can then apply
[
46
, Lemma 15.3]
to conclude the result.

A proof of the next lemma, for the particular case of finite measure spaces, can be found in
[
27
, Proposition 2.3.38]
. We give a proof for the general case.

Lemma 50
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space,
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset{L^{1}(X)}
a sequence and
f\in{L^{1}(X)}
. Suppose that

\displaystyle f_{n}\rightharpoonup f\text{ weakly in ${L^{1}(X)}$}\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in X$.}

Then,
|f_{n}-f|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0
.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
v_{n}:=f_{n}-f
. Then
\liminf_{n\to\infty}v_{n}\geq 0
a.e. in
X
, and since
v_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)}
, the set
\{v_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a weakly relatively compact subset of
{L^{1}(X)}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Due to
lemma

49
,
there exists a finite measure set
B\in\mathcal{A}
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{X\setminus B}|v_{n}(x)|d\mu(x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A}\\
\mu(A)<\delta\end{subarray}}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{A}|v_{n}(x)|d\mu(x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

By Egorov’s theorem, we can find
A\subset B
with
\mu(A)<\delta
such that the convergence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{k\geq n}v_{k}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}v_{n}

is uniform in
B\setminus A
. In particular, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}

such that

v_{n}\geq-\varepsilon(8\mu(B\setminus A))^{-1}

a.e. on
B\setminus A
for all
n\geq n_{1}
.
It follows that

{|v_{n}|}\leq v_{n}+\varepsilon(4\mu(B\setminus A))^{-1}
a.e. in
B\setminus A
for all
n\geq n_{1}
.
By definition of weak convergence,
there exists
n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\int_{B\setminus A}v_{n}d\mu<\varepsilon/4

for all
n\geq n_{2}
.
Putting all together,

\displaystyle\int_{X}{|v_{n}(x)|}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\setminus B}{|v_{n}(x)|}d\mu(x)+\int_{B\setminus A}{|v_{n}(x)|}d\mu(x)+\int_{A}{|v_{n}(x)|}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\int_{B\setminus A}\Big[v_{n}(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4\mu(B\setminus A)}\Big]\,d\mu(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\leq\varepsilon

for all
n\geq\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. This shows that
|v_{n}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0
.

Recall that given a convex set
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
u\in C
, the set

\displaystyle N_{C}(u):=\{\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\,\nu\cdot(v-u)\leq 0\quad\text{for all $v\in C$}\}

is the normal cone to
C
at
u
. We now address some measurability issues arising from the normal cone. These are of technical nature and will be needed later on.
We use the standard definitions of measurability of set-valued mappings, see
[
37
, Definition 6.2.1]
.

Lemma 51
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable function such that
f(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
.
The set-valued mapping

\displaystyle x\mathrel{\mathrlap{\mapsto}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}N_{F(x)}\big(f(x)\big)\quad\text{from $X$ to $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ is measurable. }

Proof.

By
[
1
, Corollary 18.15]
, there exists a countable family
\{f_{n}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of measurable functions
f_{n}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle F(x)=\overline{\{f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\quad\text{for all $x\in X$.}

Let
K:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
be given by

\displaystyle K(x):=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\xi\cdot\big(f_{n}(x)-f(x)\big)\leq 0\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\right\}.

The measurability of
K
follows from
[
43
, Theorem 3]
. Finally, observe that

\displaystyle\text{$K(x)=N_{F(x)}\big(f(x)\big)$ for all $x\in X$}.

We now give a geometrical construction that will allow to extend
lemma

50
to the higher dimensional case. We proceed inductively, taking care of the measurability issues arising in the process.

Lemma 52
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values such that
0\in\operatorname{ext}{F(x)}
for a.e.
x\in X
. Then there exist measurable functions
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that for a.e.
x\in X
,
\{e_{1}(x)\dots,e_{m}(x)\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

\displaystyle 0\in e_{i}(x)+N_{F(x)\cap H_{i}(x)}(0)\quad\text{for all $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$},

(21)

where
H_{1}(x):=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

\displaystyle H_{i+1}(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\,e_{j}(x)\cdot v=0\quad\text{for all $j\in\{1,\dots,i\}$}\}\quad\text{for $i=1,\dots,m-1$}.

Proof.

We argue by induction.
The case
i=1
follows trivially from
lemma

51
, and the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski Selection Theorem, see
[
1
, Theorem 18.13]
.
Now, let
i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
and suppose that there exists a measurable function
e_{i}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle-e_{i}(x)\in N_{F(x)\cap H_{i}(x)}(0)\cap H_{i}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad|e_{i}(x)|=1\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in X$}.

By
[
43
, Corollary 3.6]
, the set valued mapping
x\mathrel{\mathrlap{\mapsto}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}H_{i+1}(x)
is measurable, hence as
F
is a compact-valued measurable mapping, it follows that the mapping
x\mathrel{\mathrlap{\mapsto}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}F(x)\cap H_{i+1}(x)
is measurable, see
[
37
, Proposition 6.2.21]
. As for a.e.
x\in X
,
f(x)
is an extreme point of
F(x)
, it follows that
0
is a boundary point of
F(x)\cap H_{i+1}(x)
. By
lemma

51
, the mapping
x\mathrel{\mathrlap{\mapsto}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}N_{F(x)\cap H_{i+1}(x)}(0)
is measurable. Define
K_{i+1}:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
by

\displaystyle K_{i+1}(x):=N_{F(x)\cap H_{i+1}(x)}(0)\cap H_{i+1}(x)\cap\{\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|\nu|=1\}

By
[
37
, Proposition 6.2.21]
,
K_{i+1}
is measurable; moreover it is clear that it takes nonempty closed values. We can then use the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski Selection Theorem to conclude the existence of a measurable selection
e_{i+1}
of
K_{i+1}
. This completes the induction step.

Finally, we note that as
e_{i}(x)\in H_{i}(x)\cap\{\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|\nu|=1\}
for a.e.
x\in X
and all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the set
\{e_{1}(x),\dots,e_{m}(x)\}
generates an orthonormal basis for a.e.
x\in X
.

We now proceed to a merely technical lemma that allows to localize an argument given in the main theorem.

Lemma 53
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values such that
0\in\operatorname{ext}{F(x)}
for a.e.
x\in X
. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
be a sequence converging weakly to zero in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
. Consider functions
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\in{L^{\infty}(X)^{m}}
satisfying
eq.

21
.
Let
i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
and suppose that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
,
e_{j}(x)\cdot f_{n}(x)\to 0
for a.e.
x\in X
. Then
e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n}\to 0
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathcal{A}
be a measure zero set such that

\displaystyle e_{j}(x)\cdot f_{n}(x)\to 0\quad\text{for all $x\in X\setminus N$}

(22)

holds for each
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
and such that
(
21
) is valid for all
x\in X\setminus N
.

Let
x\in X\setminus N
. Consider a subsequence
\{f_{n_{k}}(x)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{f_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{n}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{n_{k}}(x).

Since
F(x)
is compact, we can find a subsequence
\{f_{n_{k_{l}}}(x)\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{f_{n_{k}}(x)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to some
f_{x}\in F(x)
. Then, by (
22
), we get
e_{j}(x)\cdot f_{x}=0
for
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
and thus
f_{x}\in H_{i+1}(x)
. Since
-e_{i+1}(x)\in N_{F(x)\cap H_{i+1}(x)}(0)
, we get
e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{x}\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{n}(x)=\lim_{l\to\infty}e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{n_{k_{l}}}(x)=e_{i+1}(x)\cdot f_{x}\geq 0.

Since
x\in X\setminus N
was arbitrary, we conclude
\liminf_{n\to\infty}e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n}\geq 0
a.e.
x\in X
.
It follows then from Lemma
50
that
|e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0
.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection of the Appendix.

Theorem 54
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values, and
f\in{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
such that
f(x)\in\operatorname{ext}F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
be a sequence of functions such that
f_{n}(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. If
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
, then
f_{n}\to f
in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
f=0
.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the measurable functions given in
lemma

52
. We argue by induction that
|e_{i}\cdot f_{n}|_{{L^{1}(X)}}\to 0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. The case
i=1
follows trivially from Lemma
50
.
Let
i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
and suppose that

\displaystyle|e_{j}\cdot f_{n}|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}\to 0\quad\text{for all $j\in\{1,\dots,i\}$}.

Let
\{e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be any subsequence of
\{e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We can find a subsequence
\{f_{n_{k_{l}}}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{f_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
,

\displaystyle e_{j}(x)\cdot f_{n_{k_{l}}}(x)\to 0\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in X$}.

It follows by
lemma

53
that
\{e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n_{k_{l}}}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to 0 in
{L^{1}(X)}
. Since every subsequence of
\{e_{i+1}\cdot f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has further a subsequence converging to
0
in
{L^{1}(X)}
, it follows that the entire sequence converges to
0
in
{L^{1}(X)}
. This completes the induction step.

Finally, since
\{e_{1}(x),\dots,e_{m}(x)\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for a.e.
x\in X
,

\displaystyle\int_{X}|f_{n}(x)|\,d\mu(x)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{X}|e_{i}(x)\cdot f_{n}(x)|\,d\mu(x)\longrightarrow 0.

A.2
The weak clustering principle

Given
p\in[1,\infty]
and a set-valued mapping
G:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
from a measure space to the Euclidean space, we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{p}_{G}:=\{g\in L^{p}(X)^{m}:g(x)\in G(x)\text{ for a.e.\ }x\in X\}.

Lemma 55
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and
f\in L^{p}(X)^{m}
such that
f(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. Suppose there exists a set
E\subset X
of positive measure such that

\displaystyle f(x)\notin\operatorname{ext}F(x)\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in E$.}

Then there exist two distinct functions
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{S}_{F}^{p}
such that
f=\displaystyle\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
a.e. in
X
.

Proof.

Observe that
f\in\mathcal{S}^{p}_{F}
, and consequently
\mathcal{S}^{p}_{F}
is nonempty. As stated in
[
37
, Theorem 6.4.26]
, from the measurability of
F
, we can deduce that
\operatorname{ext}\mathcal{S}^{p}_{F}=\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{ext}F}^{p}
. Hence, as
f\not\in\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{ext}F}^{p}
,
f
is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{S}_{F}^{p}
; thus there must exist two distinct functions
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{S}_{F}^{p}
such that
f=2^{-1}(\alpha+\beta)
a.e.
x\in X
.

Lemma 56
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a non-atomic
\sigma
-finite measure space and
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking nonempty compact convex values. Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and consider
f\in L^{p}(X)^{m}
such that
f(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. There exists a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{p}(X)^{m}
with the following properties.

(i)

f_{n}(x)\in\operatorname{ext}F(x)
for a.e
x\in X
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{p}(X)^{m}
.

Proof.

Observe that
f\in\mathcal{S}_{F}^{p}
, and consequently
\mathcal{S}_{F}^{p}
is nonempty. According to
[
37
, Proposition 6.4.27]
, we have
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{ext}F}^{p}}^{w}=\mathcal{S}^{p}_{F}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{ext}F}^{p}}^{w}
denotes the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{ext}F}^{p}
with respect to the weak topology of
{L^{p}(X)^{m}}
. By the theorem of Eberlein–Šmulian (in the form of
[
34
, Theorem 2.8.6]
), every sequence in
\mathcal{S}^{p}_{\operatorname{ext}F}
has a weak limit point. We can then employ Day’s Lemma (
[
34
, Lemma 2.8.5]
) to find a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\text{ext F}}^{p}\subset{L^{p}(X)^{m}}
such that

f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
in
L^{p}(X)^{m}
.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 57
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a non-atomic
\sigma
-finite measure space,
F:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
a measurable set-valued mapping taking non-empty compact convex values, and
f\in{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
such that
f(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
. Suppose that there exists a set
E
of positive measure such that

\displaystyle f(x)\notin\operatorname{ext}F(x)\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in E$}.

Then there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for every
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
there exists a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
with the following properties.

(i)

f_{n}(x)\in F(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

|f_{n}-f|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}=\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
.

Proof.

By
lemma

55
, there exist two distinct functions
\alpha,\beta\in{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
such that
f(x)=2^{-1}\big(\alpha(x)+\beta(x)\big)
for a.e.
x\in X
.
Let
G:X\mathrel{\mathrlap{\rightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{m}
be given by
G(x):=[\alpha(x),\beta(x)]
.
By Lemma
56
, there exists a sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{L^{1}(X)}
converging weakly in
{L^{1}(X)^{m}}
to
f
such that

\displaystyle g_{n}(x)\in\{\alpha(x),\beta(x)\}\quad\text{for a.e $x\in X$ and all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Define
\delta_{0}:=2^{-1}|\beta-\alpha|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}
. For each
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
consider the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

\displaystyle f_{n}:=f+\frac{2\delta}{\hskip 8.5359pt|\beta-\alpha|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}}\big(g_{n}-f\big)\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then, by construction,
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
and
|f_{n}-f|_{{L^{1}(X)^{m}}}=\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies all the stated properties and the proof culminates.

Observe that the non-atomicity assumption in the previous proposition is needed as spaces like
l^{1}(\mathbb{N})
have the
Schur’s property
. Not to mention the
L^{1}
-spaces induced by the counting measure over a finite subset of the natural numbers, yielding finite dimensional spaces.

Finally,
we note that
theorem

57

yields the result of
[
45
, Proposition 1]

under a set of slightly different assumptions.
