1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a simple, undirected graph with non-negative weights
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. In this note, we consider weights
w
obtained by restricting
\tilde{w}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, where
\tilde{w}^{1/q}
embeds isometrically into Euclidean space for some finite
q\geq 1
. In other words, there is a function
f:V\to\mathbb{R}^{|V|}
such that for all
u,v\in V
,
\tilde{w}(u,v)=\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{q}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the usual Euclidean or
\ell_{2}
norm. The special case
q=2
is known as a metric of negative-type.

Recall that a fractional matching of a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
is a function
h:E\to[0,1]
such that for all
v\in V
,
\sum_{v\in e}h(e)\leq 1
. A matching corresponds to maps
h:E\to\{0,1\}
.

Let
M
(respectively,
M_{\operatorname{frac}}
) denote a maximum weight matching (respectively, fractional matching) of
(G,w)
. Here, the weight of a (fractional) matching
h:E\to[0,1]
is defined by

w(h):=\sum_{e\in E}h(e)w(e).

The weight of a maximum weight (fractional) matching of
(G,w)
is called the (fractional) matching number of
(G,w)
.

It follows from LP duality that the fractional matching number of
(G,w)
is equal to the value of the following linear program:

\displaystyle\min_{g:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{u\in V}g(u)

(1.1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad g(u)+g(v)\geq w(u,v)\quad\forall\{u,v\}\in E

Motivated by the problem of the Fastest Mixing Markov Chain (see
Section

1.1
), we consider the following natural dimension reduction question: what is the minimum dimension of a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for which there is a function
f^{\prime}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
(G,w^{\prime})=(V,E,w^{\prime})
, with
w^{\prime}(u,v)=\|f^{\prime}(u)-f^{\prime}(v)\|^{q}
, has approximately the same (fractional) matching number as
(G,w)=(V,E,w)
?

A direct application of the classical Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma shows that
m=O_{q}(\log{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
suffices to preserve the (fractional) matching number to within a multiplicative factor of
(1\pm\varepsilon)
. Our main result completely removes the dependence on the number of vertices, and shows that
m=O_{q}(\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2})
is sufficient, where
\Delta
denotes the maximum degree of
G=(V,E)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
K>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\ref{thm:FMMC}}=C_{\ref{thm:FMMC}}(K)\geq 1
for which the following holds. Let
G=(V,E,w)
with
|V|=n
and with
w=\tilde{w}|_{E}
, where
\tilde{w}(u,v)=\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{q}
for some

and

. Let
\Delta
denote the maximum degree of
G
. Let
M
(respectively,

) denote a maximum weight matching (respectively, fractional matching) of

.

For

and

, let
\Pi_{n,d}
be a

. For

Then, except with probability at most
\exp(-d\varepsilon^{2}/\sqrt{C_{\ref{thm:FMMC}}})
over the realisation

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon q}w(M)

\displaystyle\leq w_{\pi}(M^{\pi})\leq e^{\varepsilon q}w(M),

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon q}w(M_{\operatorname{frac}})

Remark
.

By considering a disjoint union of
n/(\Delta+1)
stars, each with
(\Delta+1)
vertices, and weights given by assigning a distinct unit coordinate vector in

to each vertex, we see that
\Omega_{q}(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon^{2})

(which holds with high probability in our setting; see

) then the factor of

in our bound on

cannot be improved (even for possibly non-linear dimension reduction schemes) under the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis (SSE) of Raghavendra and Steurer
[
6
]
, the reason being that an improved dependence on
\Delta
in
Theorem

1.1
translates to a corresponding improved dependence on
\Delta
in
Theorem

1.4
, which contradicts SSE (see the remark after
Theorem

1.4

1.1.
Fastest Mixing Markov Chain (FMMC)

In the Fastest Mixing Markov Chain (FMMC) problem, introduced by Boyd, Diaconis, and Xiao
[
1
]
, we are given a finite, undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and are asked to design a symmetric
|V|\times|V|
matrix
P
such that (i)
P_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j
and
P_{ij}>0
only if
\{i,j\}\in E
or
i=j
; (ii)
\sum_{j}P_{ij}=1
for all
i
; and (iii) denoting the eigenvalues of
P
in decreasing order by
\lambda_{1}(P)\geq\lambda_{2}(P)\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}(P)
, the second largest eigenvalue modulus (SLEM), defined by

\mu(P):=\max_{i=2,\dots,n}|\lambda_{i}(P)|,

is as small as possible. We denote the set of all symmetric
V\times V
matrices, satisfying properties (i), (ii), (iii) by
\mathcal{M}(G)
.

In words, we are asked to design a discrete-time, time-homogeneous Markov chain with state space
V
, whose transitions are supported on the edges of
E
and which is reversible with respect to the uniform distribution on
V
, with the smallest possible SLEM among all such Markov chains. Since
\mu(P)\leq 1
, this is equivalent to asking for the largest possible spectral gap, defined by

\gamma(P):=1-\mu(P).

Definition 1.2

(Optimal spectral gap)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, undirected graph. With notation as above, we define the optimal spectral gap (among reversible chains supported on
E
) by

\gamma^{*}(G):=\sup\{\gamma(P):P\in\mathcal{M}(G)\}.

For an extensive discussion of the history of this problem, we refer the reader to
[
5
, Section 1.4]
, limiting ourselves here to only the most relevant results. All of these results involve the vertex conductance of a graph, whose definition we now recall.

Definition 1.3

(Vertex conductance)
.

The vertex conductance
\Psi^{*}(G)
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is defined as

\Psi^{*}(G):=\min_{\emptyset\neq S\subseteq V:|S|\leq|V|/2}\frac{|\{v\notin S:\exists u\in S\text{ s.t. }\{u,v\}\in E\}|}{|S|}

Roch
[
7
]
showed that
\gamma^{*}(G)\lesssim\Psi^{*}(G)
, thereby identifying the vertex conductance of the host graph as a barrier to designing Markov chains with large spectral gaps. In the other direction, Cheeger’s inequality shows that
\gamma^{*}(G)\gtrsim\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/\Delta^{2}
, where
\Delta
denotes the maximum degree of
G
. Put together, we have

(1.2)

\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/\Delta^{2}\lesssim\gamma^{*}(G)\lesssim\Psi^{*}(G)

Simple examples (see
[
5
]
) show that the quadratic dependence on
\Psi^{*}(G)
in the lower bound and the linear dependence on
\Psi^{*}(G)
in the upper bound cannot be improved in general, leaving open the question of resolving the dependence on
\Delta
(or removing it altogether).

Recently, Olesker-Taylor and Zanetti
[
5
]
provided a new lower bound on
\gamma^{*}(G)
, where the quadratic dependence on
\Delta
is replaced by logarithmic dependence on
|V|
:

(1.3)

\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/\log|V|\lesssim\gamma^{*}(G)\lesssim\Psi^{*}(G),

thereby providing an exponential improvement over
Equation

1.2
for dense graphs. However, in general, the lower bounds in
Equations

1.2
and
1.3
are incomparable, and Olesker-Taylor and Zanetti asked if
\log|V|
in
Equation

1.3
may be replaced by
\log\Delta
, which would provide a common refinement of
Equations

1.2
and
1.3
and moreover, would be tight under the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis
[
6
]
(see the remark after
Theorem

1.4
). As an application of
Theorem

1.1
, we answer this question in the affirmative.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
. Then,
\gamma^{*}(G)
satisfies

\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/\log\Delta\lesssim\gamma^{*}(G)\lesssim\Psi^{*}(G)

Remark
.

Work of Louis, Raghavendra, and Vempala
[
3
]
shows that under the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis
[
6
]
, for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm to distinguish between
\Psi^{*}(G)\leq\varepsilon
and
\Psi^{*}(G)\gtrsim\sqrt{\varepsilon\log\Delta}
for graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
satisfying
\log\Delta\asymp 1/\varepsilon
. As noted in
[
5
]
, a lower bound of the form
\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/o(\log(\Delta))\lesssim\gamma^{*}(G)
would allow us to distinguish between the two scenarios (using
\gamma^{*}(G)\leq\varepsilon
versus
\gamma^{*}(G)=\omega(\varepsilon)
), which would contradict the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis since
\gamma^{*}(G)
can be computed in time polynomial in the size of the graph.

Example 1.5
.

A popular use of Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods is to sample from the uniform distribution on an exponentially sized subset
V
of a product space
\{1,\dots,r\}^{n}
(where
r\asymp 1
and
n
is large) using ‘local chains’. In our notation, the transitions of the graph are supported on
G=(V,E)
, where
E
connects vertices within Hamming distance
s
, where
s\asymp 1
. Thus,
\Delta\asymp\log|V|\asymp n
, so that
Equation

1.2
gives the lower bound
\gamma^{*}(G)\gtrsim\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/n^{2}
,
Equation

1.3
gives the lower bound
\gamma^{*}(G)\gtrsim\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/n
, whereas
Theorem

1.4
gives the lower bound
\gamma^{*}(G)\gtrsim\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/\log{n}
.

2.
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

We begin by recalling some preliminary notions related to dimension reduction.

Definition 2.1
.

[cf.
[
4
, Definition 2.1]
]
Let
\Pi_{n,d}
denote a distribution on linear maps from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For constants
q\geq 1
and
c,C>0
, we say that
\Pi_{n,d}
is a
(q,c,C)
-good random dimension reduction if for all
1/10>\varepsilon>\frac{C\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{d}}
, there exist
0<\delta,\rho<\exp(-c\varepsilon^{2}d)
satisfying the following properties.

(1)

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi\sim\Pi_{n,d}}[\|\pi(x)-\pi(y)\|\notin e^{\pm\varepsilon}\|x-y\|]\leq\delta.

(2)

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, letting
\mathcal{E}_{x,y}
be the event that
\|\pi(x)-\pi(y)\|\geq e^{\varepsilon}\|x-y\|
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\Pi_{n,d}}\left[1_{\mathcal{E}_{x,y}}\left(\frac{\|\pi(x)-\pi(y)\|^{q}}{\|x-y\|^{q}}-e^{\varepsilon q}\right)\right]\leq\rho.

Lemma 2.2

(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma C.1]
)
.

For every
K_{\ref{lem:JL}}>0
, there exist constants
c_{\ref{lem:JL}},C_{\ref{lem:JL}}>0
for which the following holds. Let
\Pi_{n,d}
denote a
d\times n
random matrix whose entries are i.i.d. copies of a centered random variable with variance
1/d
and sub-Gaussian norm at most
K_{\ref{lem:JL}}/\sqrt{d}
. Then,
\Pi_{n,d}
is a
(q,c_{\ref{lem:JL}},C_{\ref{lem:JL}})
-good random dimension reduction for all
q\geq 1
.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.1
. Below, we use the notation introduced in the statement of
Theorem

1.1
. For later use, we will prove a slightly stronger statement, which shows that in addition to the conclusion of
Theorem

1.1
, we also have that

\sum_{u,v\in V}\tilde{w}_{\pi}(u,v)\geq e^{-\varepsilon q}\sum_{u,v\in V}w(u,v).

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

We start by giving a high-level overview of the proof. It is quite immediate to show that the weight of any fixed matching (in particular, a maximum matching) is preserved with high probability under the passage from
w
to
w_{\pi}
. The challenge, then, is to show that there are no unexpectedly large matchings (in terms of additive error) corresponding to
w_{\pi}
(note that we cannot use simple union bound due to the extremely large number of matchings). To address this, we consider the (random) set of edges which have unexpectedly high weights under
w_{\pi}
and show that, with high probability, the total
w_{\pi}
-weight of this random set of edges is at most
\varepsilon/\operatorname{poly}(\Delta)
times the total
w
-weight of all edges and hence, at most
\varepsilon/\operatorname{poly}(\Delta)
times the
w
-matching number of the graph. Thus, while it is possible for the
w_{\pi}
-weight of a matching to be unexpectedly larger (in a multiplicative sense) than its
w
-weight, the magnitude of this increase is much smaller than the
w
-matching number of the graph and so, cannot significantly alter the
w_{\pi}
-matching number. We now proceed to the formal details.

By
Lemma

2.2
,
\Pi_{n,d}
is a
(q,c^{\prime},C^{\prime})
-good random dimension reduction, where
c^{\prime},C^{\prime}>0
depend only on
K
. Let
\pi\sim\Pi_{n,d}
, and
consider the following random subsets of
\binom{V}{2}
, corresponding to heavy edges, light edges, and light pairs.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\{\{u,v\}\in E:w_{\pi}(u,v)\geq e^{\varepsilon q}w(u,v)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\{u,v\}\in E:w_{\pi}(u,v)\leq e^{-\varepsilon q}w(u,v)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\{u,v\}\in\binom{V}{2}:w_{\pi}(u,v)\leq e^{-\varepsilon q}w(u,v)\right\}.

These random subsets naturally give rise to the following quantities corresponding to the excess cost of heavy edges, the total original weight of light edges, and the total original weight of light pairs:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{H}):=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{H}}\left(w_{\pi}(u,v)-e^{\varepsilon q}w(u,v)\right);\text{ }\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{1}):=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}w(u,v);\text{ }\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{2}):=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}}\tilde{w}(u,v).

We show that, in expectation, all of these quantities are sufficiently small. First, since any graph of maximum degree
\Delta
can be written as a disjoint union of at most
(\Delta+1)
matchings, it follows that
w(M)\geq\frac{1}{\Delta+1}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}w(u,v)
. Then, from
Lemmas

2.2
and
2.1
, it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{H})]

\displaystyle\leq\rho\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}w(u,v)\leq\rho(\Delta+1)w(M),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{1})]

\displaystyle\leq\delta\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}w(u,v)\leq\delta(\Delta+1)w(M),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{2})]

\displaystyle\leq\delta\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\tilde{w}(u,v)=\delta\tilde{w}\left(\binom{V}{2}\right).

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, the union bound, and using
\max{\delta,\rho}\leq\exp(-c^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}d)
, we see that except with probability at most
3\exp(-c^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}d/2)
, the following event holds:

\mathcal{G}=\left\{\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{H})\leq\sqrt{\rho}(\Delta+1)w(M),\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{1})\leq\sqrt{\delta}(\Delta+1)w(M),\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{2})\leq\sqrt{\delta}\tilde{w}\binom{V}{2}\right\}.

Let
\pi
be a realisation of
\Pi_{n,d}
for which
\mathcal{G}
holds. Then, for any (fractional) matching
M^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle w_{\pi}(M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}M^{\prime}(e)w_{\pi}(e)=\sum_{e\notin\mathcal{H}}M^{\prime}(e)w_{\pi}(e)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{H}}M^{\prime}(e)e^{\varepsilon q}w(e)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{H}}M^{\prime}(e)(w_{\pi}(e)-e^{\varepsilon q}w(e))

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon q}w(M^{\prime})+\operatorname{Diff}(\mathcal{H})\leq e^{\varepsilon q}w(M^{\prime})+\sqrt{\rho}(\Delta+1)w(M)

\displaystyle\leq(e^{\varepsilon q}+\sqrt{\rho}(\Delta+1))w(M).

Moreover, we see that for
M^{\prime}=M
or
M^{\prime}=M_{\operatorname{frac}}
, we have

\displaystyle w_{\pi}(M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\notin\mathcal{L}_{1}}M^{\prime}(e)w_{\pi}(e)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}M^{\prime}(e)w_{e}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}M^{\prime}(e)(w_{\pi}(e)-w(e))

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\varepsilon q}w(M^{\prime})-\operatorname{Cost}(\mathcal{L}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq(e^{-\varepsilon q}-\sqrt{\delta}(\Delta+1))w(M^{\prime}).

A similar computation shows that

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{\pi}\left(\binom{V}{2}\right)\geq(e^{-\varepsilon q}-\sqrt{\delta})\tilde{w}\left(\binom{V}{2}\right).

Finally, by taking
C
to be sufficiently large depending on
c^{\prime},C^{\prime}
, we can ensure that

\max\{\sqrt{\delta},\sqrt{\rho}\}(\Delta+1)\leq\varepsilon q/10,

so that the desired conclusion follows by rescaling
\varepsilon
. ∎

3.
Application to the Fastest Mixing Markov Chain: Proof of
Theorem

1.4

We will need the following proposition due to Olesker-Taylor and Zanetti.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple, undirected graph with
|V|=n
, vertex conductance
\Psi^{*}(G)
, and optimal spectral gap
\gamma^{*}(G)
. For
1\leq m\leq n
, let
\lambda_{m}^{*}
denote the optimum value of the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{f:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m},G:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\sum_{u\in V}g(u)}{\sum_{u\in V}\|f(u)\|^{2}}

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{v\in V}f(v)=0,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad g(u)+g(v)\geq\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{2}\quad\forall\{u,v\}\in E

Let
m^{*}=\min\{1\leq m\leq n:\lambda_{n}^{*}\leq\lambda_{m}^{*}\leq 2\lambda_{n}^{*}\}
. Then,

\Psi^{*}(G)^{2}/(\log{m^{*}})\lesssim\gamma^{*}(G)\lesssim\Psi^{*}(G).

Given the previous proposition,
Theorem

1.4
follows from
Theorem

1.1
and LP duality
Section

1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

By
Proposition

3.1
, and since
\lambda_{m}^{*}
decreases as
m
increases, it suffices to show that for
d=O(\log\Delta)
,
\lambda_{d}^{*}\leq 2\lambda^{*}_{n}
. To this end, let
F:V\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
\sum_{v\in V}F(v)=0
, and that the value of the program

\displaystyle\min_{g:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\sum_{u\in V}g(u)}{\sum_{u\in V}\|F(u)\|^{2}}

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad g(u)+g(v)\geq\|F(u)-F(v)\|^{2}\quad\forall\{u,v\}\in E

is
\lambda_{n}^{*}
.

By LP duality

1
,
\lambda_{n}^{*}
is the fractional matching number of the graph
G=(V,E,w/S_{F})
, where
w(u,v):=\|F(u)-F(v)\|^{2}
and
S_{F}:={\sum_{u,v\in V}\|F(u)-F(v)\|^{2}}
. Here, we have used that
\sum_{u,v\in V}\|F(u)-F(v)\|^{2}=\sum_{u\in V}\|F(u)\|^{2}
, which holds since
\sum_{v\in V}F(v)=0
.

We now appeal to the slight extension of
Theorem

1.1
(with
K=1
) proved in the previous section. Let
\varepsilon=1/100
,
d=2C_{\ref{thm:matching}}(1)\log(\Delta/2\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2}=O(\log\Delta)
, and
\pi\in\mathcal{G}
. Here
\mathcal{G}
is the event defined in the previous subsection, where it was also shown to be nonempty. Let

be defined by

. Then
\sum_{v\in V}f(v)=0
and hence,
\lambda_{d}^{*}
is at most

\displaystyle\min_{g:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\sum_{u\in V}g(u)}{\sum_{u,v\in V}\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{2}}

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad g(u)+g(v)\geq\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{2}\quad\forall\{u,v\}\in E.

Once again, by LP duality

1
,
\lambda_{d}^{*}
is at most the fractional matching number of the graph

, where

and
S^{\prime}_{f}:={\sum_{u,v\in V}\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{2}}
.

In the previous section, we showed that for
\pi\in\mathcal{G}
,

and the fractional matching number of

∎
