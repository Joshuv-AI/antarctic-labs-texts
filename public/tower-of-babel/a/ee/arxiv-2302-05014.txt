Abstract
. L 1 L^{1} based optimization is widely used in image denoising, machine learning and related applications. One of the main features of such approach is that it naturally provide a sparse structure in the numerical solutions. In this paper, we study an L 1 L^{1} based mixed DG method for second-order elliptic equations in the non-divergence form. The elliptic PDE in nondivergence form arises in the linearization of fully nonlinear PDEs. Due to the nature of the equations, classical finite element methods based on variational forms can not be employed directly. In this work, we propose a new optimization scheme coupling the classical DG framework with recently developed L 1 L^{1} optimization technique. Convergence analysis in both energy norm and L ∞ L^{\infty} norm are obtained under weak regularity assumption. Such L 1 L^{1} models are nondifferentiable and therefore invalidate traditional gradient methods. Therefore all existing gradient based solvers are no longer feasible under this setting. To overcome this difficulty, we characterize solutions of L 1 L^{1} optimization as fixed-points of proximity equations and utilize matrix splitting technique to obtain a class of fixed-point proximity algorithms with convergence analysis. Various numerical examples are displayed to illustrate the numerical solution has sparse structure with careful choice of the bases of the finite dimensional spaces. Numerical examples in both smooth and nonsmooth settings are provided to validate the theoretical results.

1.
Introduction

One of the main features of using
L^{1}
norm based optimizations is that the approximation naturally has a sparse representation under specified transformations or bases. This feature is particularly important in large scale computations. For instance, in multi-physics applications, one needs to use the solution from one model as inputs/parameters for the subsequent physical model.
A typical challenge lays in the transition of computation between models with different scales.
Due to the curse of dimensionality, the computational complexity grows exponantially as the number of independent inputs/paramters grow linearly. In other words, too many independent inputs will lead to extremely large system to solve. Therefore, it is desirable to develop
sparsity promoting
algorithms such that the input, the solution from the foremal model can be represented by a small number of basis functions.

In the area of numerical PDEs, numerical schemes based on
L^{1}
stablization are less explored. The main challenge is that the
L^{1}
norm based scheme will result in minimizing an non-differentiable functional. In
[
16
]
, the author studied a finite element method for solving first order PDEs under the
L^{1}
norm. The author overcome the difficulty of minimizing the non-differentiable functional by regularizing the functional with a sequence of differentiable minimization problems.

In this paper, we propose a numerical scheme with two main features: (1) using a sparsity promoting functional in the minimization scheme and adopting fixed-point proximity algorithm
[
22
,
23
,
20
,
32
]
with exact convergence analysis of non-smooth optimization; (2) A sparsity promoting base of the finite element space
[
5
,
6
,
24
,
25
]
. The first condition is met by using
L^{1}
stabilization while the second is met by using DG finite elements. In
[
7
]
the authors systematically studied the construction of multiscale bases under the DG setting. It is worth to mention that the current study is an ongoing effort to develop new numerical schemes such that the numerical solutions with sparse structure in the finite dimensional space. As a step-stone, we consider the following elliptic equations in non-divergence form:

(1.1)

\displaystyle A:D^{2}u

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(1.2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega.

Here the domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=2,3)
is an open and bounded polytope and the coefficient matrix
A=[A_{ij}]\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix with eigenvalues bounded between
(0,\Lambda)
with
\Lambda>0
.
D^{2}u=[\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}]
denotes the Hessian matrix of
u
and
A:D^{2}u:=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}
. Note that
A
is not necessarily differentiable. Indeed if
A\in[C^{1}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
we may recast the above system into a divergence form
[
30
]
. Hence, the standard steady-state diffusion problem is a special case of (
1.1
).

The PDEs in non-divergence form like (
1.1
) arises in the linearization of fully nonlinear PDEs such as stochastic control problems
[
13
]
, Monge-Ampére equations
[
27
]
, and the fully nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations
[
17
]
. One of the main challenges around this problem is the fact that the coefficient matrix
A(x)
in general is not smooth in many practical settings. In this case, the standard weak variational formulation of the PDE is no longer available. Indeed, analytical results on the PDE level such as the existence and uniqueness of solutions for (
2.1
) are often characterized in the classical or strong sense.

Due to the possible non-smooth cofficient matrix, developing standard numerical methods for problem (
1.1
) with variational form is very difficult. In
[
19
]
the authors study a mixed finite element method based on Galerkin framework by introducing a so-called “finite element Hessian” and recently in
[
9
]
they extend the idea with discontinuous Galerkin elements. The analysis of the DG method is provided in
[
26
]
.
In
[
11
,
12
]
the authors consider two DG type methods based on variational form. The key ingredient for the error estimates is on the discrete Calderon-Zygmond estimates which assumes a full
H^{2,p}
regularity for the PDE operator:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega},\quad\forall w\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\cap W^{2,p}(\Omega).

Here
1\leq p<\infty
. This regularity assumption holds if the coefficient matrix

A\in[C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})]^{d\times d}
and
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
.
In contrast, several methods are studied
[
34
,
14
]
for equations with non-smooth coefficient matrix. they considered DG method for discontinuous
A
with Cordés condition and convex polyhedral domain
\Omega
. A primal-dual weak Galerkin method is introduced by Wang and Wang
[
35
]
. Roughly speaking, Cordés condition requires that the possibly discontinuous coefficient matrix
A
is not too far away from identity matrix multiplied by a constant. Consequently the difference between the nondivergence form and a diffusive form is bounded by the
|D^{2}u|
pointwisely.
Recently, a
L^{p}
-weak Galerkin method for the problem is developed in
[
4
]
based on primal dual weak Galerkin framework. They recast the problem into a Min-Max optimization problem. All the above mentioned works require a full
W^{2,p}

regularity assumption (
1.3
) for the PDE operator in order to establish the stability of the methods (methods in
[
34
,
14
,
35
]
are valid only for
p=2
).

In a recent work by one of the authors Qiu
[
30
]
, they develop a first order system least squares method for (
1.1
). It is worth to mention that for the stability of the method, their method only requires
W^{1,2}
regularity of the PDE operator:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{1,2,\Omega}\leq C\|A:D^{2}w\|_{2,\Omega},

for any
w\in\{v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega):A:D^{2}v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
. All the above mentioned work are formulated and implemented as Galerkin type schemes. Nochetto and Zhang
[
29
]
studied a two-scale method, which is based on the integro-differential approach and focuses on
L^{\infty}
-error estimates.

In this paper we advocate a different approach to discretize the equation (
1.1
) under the minimization framework. Namely, we employ standard DG element for the primary unknown
u
and its gradient
\nabla u
. We augment the minimization problem with
L^{1}
-type penalty terms. Comparing with existing finite element methods for solving (
2.1
) based on variational formulations, our approach has a few distinct features. First, the discrete problem does not need to use integration by parts which fits the original PDE naturally. No artificial differential operators are needed. Consequently, the error estimates is relatively neat and simple. Secondly, we are able to relax the regularity requirement of the PDE operator from
W^{2,p}
(
1.3
) to
W^{1,2}
(
1.4
). We only need the exact solution of the PDE
u\in W^{2+\delta,2}
to achieve convergence result in the energy norm comparable with above mentioned existing work.
Furthermore, we establish pointwise error estimate based on the Aleksandrov-Bakelman-Pucci estimates
[
15
]
, when the domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}(d=2,3)
is an open and bounded polytope and the coefficient matrix
A=A(x)\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix with eigenvalues bounded between
(0,\Lambda)
with
\Lambda>0
(we need to assume
A
is piecewise polynomial if
d=3
). Finally, conventional methods such as least squares or Galerkin type methods based on variational forms are feasible for PDEs with smooth solutions. Nevertheless, when the PDE operator is lack of strong regularity such as (
1.1
) and/or the desired energy functional is not differentiable (
L^{1}
for instance), the weak formulation of a PDE is not application under such settings.

Unlike smooth optimization, the proposed
L^{1}
optimization cannot be solved via gradient-based algorithms due to its nondifferentiability. To overcome the difficulty brought by nonsmoothness, here we adopt the framework of Fixed-Point Proximity Algorithms (FP
2
​A) originated from image processing and data science
[
22
,
23
,
20
,
32
]
to solve the proposed
L^{1}
optimization problem. FP
2
​A first characterizes solutions of
L^{1}
optimization to be fixed-points of proximity equations of nonsmooth functions, then a matrix splitting technique provides a class of fixed-point proximity algorithms. The framework of firmly nonexpansive operators are then applied to prove the convergence of FP
2
​A. It is also proven in
[
20
]
that FP
2
​A covers popular algorithms such as first-order primal-dual algorithms and alternating direction method of multipliers.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the details of the mixed DG method for the problem (
1.1
) with
L^{1}
stabilization and the main error estimates results. In Section 3 we present the detailed proofs of the error estimstes. In Section 4, we review some preliminary approximation results and present the main error estimates of the method. Given the fact that the scheme is based on
L^{1}
stabilization, the implementation of far from trivial, in Section 5, we will present the details on the iterative algorithm for the scheme and present the convergence analysis of the algorithm. Several numerical examples will be performed in Section 5 with concluding remarks in Section 6.

2.
Mixed formulation for non-divergence form

In this section we present the mixed DG method for the problem (
1.1
) as a minimization problem. We begin by specializing (
1.1
) as a first order system:

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\nabla{\boldsymbol{q}}-u

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(2.1c)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega.

We define the finite element spaces for the numerical scheme for solving (
2.1
). We adopt the notation and norms for the spaces as in
[
1
]
.
We consider a family of conforming triangulations
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
made of shape-regular simplexes (triangles in 2D, tetrahedra in 3D). Here conforming means that for any two element
K,K^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
,
\overline{K}\cap\overline{K^{\prime}}
is either
\emptyset
, a common vertex, a common edge or a common face of both elements.
Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}
denote the set of all interior faces of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, and
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}
denotes the set of
all boundary faces. We define
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}:={\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}\cup{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}
. We use
h_{K}
to denote the diameter
of the element
K
, and
h_{F}
the diameter of the edge
F\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}
. The mesh size of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
is defined as

h:=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{K}
. We denote by
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{K}
the unit outward normal vector on
\partial K
. We will drop the sub-index
K
to denote a generic outer normal vector
{\boldsymbol{n}}
.
We also introduce the average and jump operators. Let
F=\partial K\cap\partial K^{\prime}
be
an interior face shared by
K
and
K^{\prime}
.

\phi
is a generic piecewise smooth function
(scalar- or vector-valued). We define the average of
\phi
on
F
as

\{\hskip-4.0pt\{\phi\}\hskip-4.0pt\}:=\frac{1}{2}(\phi+\phi^{\prime})

where
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
denote the trace of

\phi
from the interior of
K
and
K^{\prime}
respectively. Furthermore, let
w
be a piecewise smooth function
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
a piecewise smooth vector-valued field. Analogously, we define the following jumps on
F
:

\displaystyle\llbracket w\rrbracket:=w{\boldsymbol{n}}_{K}+w^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{n}}_{K^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket:={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{K}+{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{K^{\prime}}.

On a boundary face
F=\partial K\cap\partial\Omega
, we set accordingly

\{\hskip-4.0pt\{\phi\}\hskip-4.0pt\}:=\phi,\quad\llbracket w\rrbracket:=w{\boldsymbol{n}},\quad\text{and}\quad\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\rrbracket_{N}:={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}.

Let
P_{k}(D)
denote the space of polynomials of degree no more than
k
over the domain
D
. We define the following spaces for
k\geq 2
:

\displaystyle W_{h}

\displaystyle=\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega)|w_{K}\in P_{k}(K),\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{K}\in[P_{k-1}(K)]^{d},\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h},\},

\displaystyle W^{c}_{h}

\displaystyle=W_{h}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\quad{\boldsymbol{V}}^{c}_{h}={\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\cap H(\text{div};\Omega).

Finally, we use the standard notation for Sobolev norms:
\|\cdot\|_{k,p,D}
and
|\cdot|_{k,p}
are used for the standard
W^{k,p}(D)
norms and semi-norms, respectively. In addition, for discrete functions we define:

\|w\|_{k,p,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}:=\left(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|w\|^{p}_{k,p,K}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

\|w\|_{k,p,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}:=\left(\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}\|w\|^{p}_{k,p,F}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

\|w\|_{k,p,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}:=\left(\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}\|w\|^{p}_{k,p,F}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

We drop the index
k
when
k=0
and we drop both indices
k,p
for the standard
L^{2}
norm. i.e.

\|w\|_{p,D}:=\|w\|_{0,p,D}
and
\|w\|_{D}:=\|w\|_{0,2,D}
.

We next describe our numerical scheme.
For
(w,v)\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
, we introduce the
penalty term

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{v}},w):=(h^{-1}\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|\llbracket w\rrbracket\|_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}})

and the energy functional

\mathcal{J}_{h}(w,{\boldsymbol{v}}):=\|A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-f\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}-\nabla w\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\tau{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{v}},w),

where the
h
-independent parameter
\tau
can be chosen as any positive real number bigger or equal to
1
.
We seek an approximation
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
by solving the minimization problem

(2.3)

\displaystyle(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{(w,v)\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}}\mathcal{J}_{h}(w,{\boldsymbol{v}}).

The objective function of minimization problem (
2.3
) is convex and thus, it has a (global) minimizer. However, since it involves a non-smooth penalty term, standard gradient type algorithm cannot be applied to this problem. Fixed-point iterative algorithms will be developed in section 4 for solving this minimization problem.

Remark
2.1
.

For non-homogeneous boundary condition case, we should modify the penalty term (
2.2
) as:

{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{v}},w):=\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|\llbracket w\rrbracket\|_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|w-g\|_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}}.

Remark
2.2
.

Note that in the energy functional
\mathcal{J}_{h}
we can replace the
L^{2}
norm with any
L^{p}
norm with

1\leq p<\infty
; the error analysis should follow almost the same lines as
L^{2}
since we do not rely on variational formulations. Nevertheless, we use the
L^{2}
norm here for the sake of simplicity in the implementation. As part of our future work, we will develop efficient algorithms to treat general
L^{p}
norms in the functional.

Remark
2.3
.

We believe that the framework can be formulated with the primary variable
u
only. It should not have substantial difficulty in the analysis of the method. Nevertheless, for the sake of simplicity in the implementation step, we recast the problem into the first order system (
2.1
) and approximate
u
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}
together.

In this paper, we provide two types of error estimates of the above scheme. We first provide the error estimate with the standard discrete
H^{1}
norms. In this case we assume that the elliptic equation has the
H^{1}
regularity:
for any
w\in\{v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega):A:D^{2}v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
,

(2.4)

\|w\|_{1,2,\Omega}\leq C\|A:D^{2}w\|_{\Omega}.

It is worth to mention that the above regularity assumption is weaker than the well-known Cordés condition for the full
H^{2}
regularity
[
34
]
. Namely, Cordés condition requires
\Omega
must be convex polyhedral domain and
A\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is symmetric positive definite and there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1]
such that

\frac{\sum_{i,j}a^{2}_{ij}}{({\bf Tr}A)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{n-1+\epsilon}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad\Omega.

In contrast, the relaxed
H^{1}
regularity (
2.4
) holds in more general settings. For instance, if
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a polyhedral domain (possibly nonconvex), we can establish (
2.4
) with similar assumption as Condés condition as follows:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a polyhedral domain. If
A\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{3\times 3}
is symmetric positive definite and there exists
\mu>0
such that

\|A-\mu\text{Id}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\Omega},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
is a positive constant only depends on the domain
\Omega
. Then it holds that:

\|w\|_{1,2,\Omega}\leq C\|A:D^{2}w\|_{\Omega},

for all
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

By triangle inequality, for any
\mu>0
we have

\displaystyle\|\mu\Delta w\|_{p,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\|A:D^{2}w-\mu\Delta w\|_{p,\Omega}+\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega}\leq\|A-\mu\text{Id}\|_{\infty}\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}+\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega}.

If
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a polyhedral domain, in
[
8
]
it shows that for any
p\in[\frac{6}{5},\frac{4}{3})
, there exists
C_{p}>0
such that

\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\|\Delta w\|_{p,\Omega}.

Inserting this inequality in the previous step we have

C_{p}^{-1}\mu\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}\leq\|A-\mu\text{Id}\|_{\infty}\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}+\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega}.

If there exists a
\mu>0
such that
\|A-\mu\text{Id}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{2}C_{p}^{-1}\mu\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}
, we can have a bound:

\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}\leq\frac{2C_{p}}{\mu}\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega}.

Finally, by the Sobolev embedding, if
p=\frac{6}{5}
with
d=3
we have

\|w\|_{1,2,\Omega}\leq C\|w\|_{2,p,\Omega}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\|A:D^{2}w\|_{p,\Omega}.

Here
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
only depends on the domain
\Omega
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next we present the first error estimate of the scheme (
2.3
) which is summarized as follows:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(u,{\boldsymbol{q}})
be the exact solution of problem (
2.1
) that satisfies the regularity assumption (
2.4
) and
u\in H^{2+\delta}(\Omega)
with

\delta>0
. If
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
is a minimizer of (
2.3
), then

(2.5)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|{\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{\min\{k-1,\delta\}}\|u\|_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega}.

In addition, if the stronger regularity assumption (
1.3
) holds for the non-divergence operator, then we have the optimal convergence with respect to the
H^{2}
norm:

(2.6)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{2,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{\min\{k-1,\delta\}}\|u\|_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega}.

Here
\mathcal{C}_{f}:=\max(\tau^{-2}\|f\|_{\Omega}^{2},1)
.

Remark
2.4
.

Here we only assume the exact solution has the strong
H^{2+\delta}
regularity. The linear non-divergence operator
is only assumed to have a weak
H^{1}
regularity (
2.4
) for the energy norm error estimate. In addition if we have full

regularity for the nondivergence operator, we then obtain the optimal convergence under the discrete

norm.

Moreover, we can establish the error estimate with the well-known Aleksandrov-Bakelman-Pucci
estimates (ABP)
[
15
, Chapter 9, Theorem 9.1]
:
If
w\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})\cap W^{2,d}_{loc}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, then it holds:

(2.7)

\|w\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leq C\|A:D^{2}w\|_{d,\Omega}.

The above estimate holds if the domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=2,3)
is an open and bounded polytope, and the coefficient matrix

A=A(x)\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix with eigenvalues
bounded between
(0,\Lambda)
with

.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(u,{\boldsymbol{q}})
be the exact solution of the problem (
2.1
) and
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
be a minimizer of
(
2.3
). If

with

, then the following statements hold:

(i) In the 2D case with
any polynomial degree

we have:

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|_{\infty,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{\min\{k-1,\delta\}}\|u\|_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega}.

(ii) In the 3D case with

,
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
and

,
we have:

Remark
2.5
.

In the 3D case, we make two additional restrictions, one on the the polynomial degree

. This is due to the use of the discrete inverse inequality in the analysis. Nevertheless, for the 2D case, this requirement is not needed.

3.
Proofs of the error estimates

This section is devoted to providing the proof of the error estimates stated in the last section. We begin by gathering several auxiliary results to be used in the proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{J}_{h}
with
\tau>0
, then

\tau{\mathcal{S}}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\leq\|f\|^{2}_{\Omega}

and

\tau(h^{-2}\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-4}\|\llbracket u_{h}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}})\leq 2\|f\|^{2}_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}).

Proof.

Since
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{J}_{h}
, we observe that

\tau{\mathcal{S}}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\leq\mathcal{J}_{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\leq\mathcal{J}_{h}(0,{\bf{0}})=\|f\|^{2}_{\Omega}.

The second inequality is a direct consequence of the first assertion.
∎

For discrete functions, we frequently use the following inverse and trace inequalities
[
2
]
: For
w_{h}\in W(D)
with
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|w_{h}\|_{l,p,D}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{m-l+\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q}}\|w_{h}\|_{m,q,D},\quad\text{for all $K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}$,}\quad 0\leq m\leq l,1\leq p,q\leq\infty,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|w_{h}\|_{\partial K}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\|w_{h}\|_{K},\quad\text{for all $K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}$.}

The global version of the above inequalities are as follows: for
w_{h}\in W_{h}
it holds

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|w_{h}\|_{l,p,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{m-l+\min\{0,\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q}\}}\|w_{h}\|_{m,q,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}},\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq l,1\leq p,q\leq\infty,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|w_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}.

In the error estimates, the essential step is to bridging the discontinuous numerical solution
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
with the exact solution
(u,{\boldsymbol{q}})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H(\text{div};\Omega)
so that we can apply the regularity of the PDE (
2.4
) on the error. To this end, we use several lifting operators on discrete functions as follows.

The first lifting operator is to construct a
u^{c}_{h}\in W^{c}_{h}
such that their difference is controlled by the jumps of
u_{h}
. Namely, for an arbitrary
w_{h}\in W_{h}
, we can use the averaging operator introduced in
[
18
]
to define
w^{c}_{h}
such that
w^{c}_{h}\in W^{c}_{h}
and it satisfies the property that
[
18
]
[Theorem 2.1]:

\|w_{h}-w^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{2}\|\nabla(w_{h}-w^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq Ch\|\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}.

With the inverse inequality (
3.3
) for discrete functions, we have the following result:

(3.6)

\|w_{h}-w^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{2}\|\nabla(w_{h}-w^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\|\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}.

Likewise, for vector-valued functions
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
, we can construct a
H(\text{div})
conforming function
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{c}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\cap H(\text{div};\Omega)
such that the following approximation property is true:

(3.7)

\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{2}\|\nabla({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}.

Notice that
u^{c}_{h}\in W^{c}_{h}
is only in
H^{1}(\Omega)
which is not sufficient for the regularity condition
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),D^{2}w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Hence, we need further lift the function to a function in
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We will need two different lifting operators for the
H^{1}
and
L^{\infty}
error estimates respectively.

The first one is due to
[
3
]
. They established the following result:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any function
w^{c}_{h}\in W^{c}_{h}
, there exists
\widetilde{w}^{c}_{h}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that:

\|D^{l}(w^{c}_{h}-\widetilde{w}^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{\Omega}\leq Ch^{3-2l}\|\llbracket\nabla w^{c}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}},\quad l=0,1,2.

It is worth to mention that here the construction of
\widetilde{w}^{c}_{h}
is done in the context of conforming virtual element spaces. This leads to the fact that
\widetilde{w}^{c}_{h}
in general is not a piecewise polynomial in
W_{h}
. Nevertheless, our analysis for the first error estimate does not require the lifted function in the discrete space
W_{h}
.

The next auxiliary result is on the relation between
(w_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})
and its conforming counterpart
(w^{c}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
(w^{c}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h})\in W^{c}_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}^{c}_{h}
is the lifting of
(w_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
, then

\displaystyle\|\llbracket\nabla w^{c}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-\nabla w^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}},

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-\nabla w^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-\nabla w_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}).

Proof.

The first assertion is due to the fact that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{c}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}^{c}_{h}
and the trace inequality (
3.5
):

\|\llbracket\nabla w^{c}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}}=\|\llbracket({\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-\nabla w^{c}_{h})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}}\leq Ch^{-1}\|({\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-\nabla w^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}.

For the second assertion we begin with triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-\nabla w^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-\nabla w_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}_{h}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|\nabla(w^{c}_{h}-w_{h})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}})

\displaystyle\leq C(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-\nabla w_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}_{h}}),

the last step is due to the approximation property of the lifting operators (
3.6
), (
3.7
).
∎

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\mathcal{C}_{f}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}).

This completes the proof of (
3.8
). Finally, we recall that
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
is the minimizer of the functional

(

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}(w_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})

for all

\Pi_{W}u

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}(\Pi_{W}u,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{V}{\boldsymbol{q}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\mathcal{C}_{f}(\|A:\nabla(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{V}{\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{q}})\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{-2}\|(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{V}{\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{q}})-\nabla(\Pi_{W}u-u)\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}})

The last step we used the standard approximation properties of the interpolations
(\Pi_{W}u,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{V}{\boldsymbol{q}})

\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}
. For the error in

\|{\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}-\nabla u_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{\min\{k-1,\delta\}}\|u\|_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega}.

).

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{2,2,{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}

. The only term needs modification is
\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{\infty,\Omega}
. More precisely, we can apply the ABP estimate as:

C\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h}\|^{2}_{\infty,\Omega}\leq C\|A:D^{2}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{3,\Omega}.

Notice that if
A
is piecewise polynomial, the last term in the above estimate
A:D^{2}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h})
remains to be piecewise polynomials over
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, we can now apply the inverse inequality (
3.1
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\|A:D^{2}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{3,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1}\|A:D^{2}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h})\|^{2}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1}(\|A:D^{2}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{c}_{h}u-u)\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|A:D^{2}\widetilde{u}^{c}_{h}-f\|^{2}_{\Omega})

From here we can use the same argument to control the last term on the right hand side as in the previous case. Finally, due to the
h^{-1}
from the inverse inequality, we have the final estimates as:

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2\min\{k-1,\delta\}-1}\|u\|^{2}_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega}+C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\mathcal{C}_{f}h^{2\min\{k-1,\delta\}-1}\|u\|^{2}_{\min\{k+1,2+\delta\},2,\Omega},

if the exact solution is smooth enough
u\in H^{2+\delta}(\Omega)
for some
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
.
This completes the proof.

∎

4.
Fixed-Point Proximity Algorithms

In this section, we develop a fast numerical algorithm for optimization problem (
2.3
) based on the Fixed-Point Proximity Algorithms (FP
2
​A)
[
22
,
20
,
32
]
and establish a convergence theorem for the algorithm.

A challenge of solving problem (
2.3
) is that the
L_{1}
norm involved in its objective function is non-differentiable, making classical gradient-based methods invalid. To surmount this difficulty, we first characterize solutions of the optimization problem (
2.3
) as fixed-points of a nonlinear map involved
proximity operators
of functions appearing in the objective function. Based on the fixed-point equation, application of a matrix splitting technique to the fixed-point equation leads to the FP
2
​A with guaranteed convergence.

4.1.
An Equivalent Formulation of the Basic Optimization Problem.

We first reformulate the optimization problem (
2.3
) in a form convenient to apply FP
2
​A. To this end, we choose a basis of the finite dimensional space
W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
and denote it by
\mathcal{B}_{W_{h}\times V_{h}}
. The numerical solution
(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h})
is represented by its coefficients
x
with respect to this basis. Note the choice of the basis is crucial to obtain a sparse representation of the solution. We will demonstrate this point with two choices of bases: a standard Lagrange nodal basis and a
multiscale basis
.

We associate each element in
(w,{\boldsymbol{v}})\in W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which represents its coefficients in the basis. This association is one-to-one. Hence,
W_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Specifically, we use
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{W}}
to denote the basis of
W_{h}
and
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{V}}
the basis of
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
. Let

\displaystyle B_{11}

\displaystyle:=h^{-2}\begin{bmatrix}{\left(\nabla w_{i},\nabla w_{j}\right)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\end{bmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq N_{W}},

\displaystyle B_{12}

\displaystyle:=h^{-2}\begin{bmatrix}{\left(\nabla w_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\right)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\end{bmatrix}_{1\leq i\leq N_{W},1\leq j\leq N_{V}},

\displaystyle B_{22}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}{\left(A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i},A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\right)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}+h^{-2}{\left({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\right)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\end{bmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq N_{V}},

and define

B:=\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{\ast}&B_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

We also define

b:=\begin{bmatrix}{\left(-2{\left(A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j},f\right)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\right)}_{1\leq j\leq N_{V}}\\
0_{N_{W}}\end{bmatrix},

where
0_{N_{W}}
denotes the
N_{W}
-dimensional zero vector.
In terms of the symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
B
and the vector
b
defined above,
we write

{\left\|A:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-f\right\|}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}^{2}+h^{-2}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla w\right\|}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}^{2}-{\left\|f\right\|}^{2}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}=x^{\ast}Bx+b^{\ast}x.

Likewise, we rewrite the boundary terms in (
2.3
). To this end,
we introduce matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
by

(4.1)

L:=\begin{bmatrix}\tau h^{-1}L_{1}\\
\tau h^{-1}L_{2}\\
\tau h^{-2}L_{3}\end{bmatrix}

with
L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}|\times N}
,
L_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}|\times N}
and
L_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}|\times N}
. Here,
L_{1}x
contains all endpoint values of
{\left\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\right\rrbracket}
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}
,
L_{2}x
contains all endpoint and midpoint values of
{\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket}
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}
and
L_{3}x
contains all endpoint and midpoint values of
w
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}
.
We then introduce
d\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
by setting

d:=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}|}\\
0_{3|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{I}|}\\
d_{1}\end{bmatrix},

where
d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{{}_{2|{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}|}}
contains all endpoint and midpoint values of
g
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{B}
, with each element arranged according to
L_{3}x
.
In terms of the matrix
L
and the vector
d
, we identify

{\tau h^{-1}{\left\|{\left\llbracket{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\right\rrbracket}\right\|}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+\tau h^{-1}{\left\|{\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket}\right\|}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{I}_{h}}+\tau h^{-2}{\left\|w-g\right\|}_{1,{\mathcal{E}}^{B}_{h}}}={\left\|Lx-d\right\|}_{1}.

In this notation, problem (
2.3
) is reformulated as

(4.2)

\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\left\{x^{\ast}Bx+b^{\ast}x+{\left\|Lx-d\right\|}_{1}\right\}}.

For convenience of further analysis, we define functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(x)\coloneqq x^{\ast}Bx+b^{\ast}x,\ \ \mbox{for}\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

and
h:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(y)\coloneqq\|y-d\|_{1},\ \ \mbox{for}\ \ y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

With these definitions, we identify (
4.2
) as

(4.3)

\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\left\{g{\left(x\right)}+h{\left(Lx\right)}\right\}}.

Clearly, (
4.3
) is a convex optimization problem studied in
[
20
,
22
]
. Since the objective function of (
4.3
) is non-differentiable, the classical gradient-based method is not applicable.

4.2.
A Fixed-Point Formulation.

We follow
[
20
,
22
]
to reformulate (
4.3
) as a fixed-point problem.
To this end, we recall some terminologies of convex analysis
[
22
,
33
]
. We denote by
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the class of all lower semi-continuous and proper convex functions mapping
\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Notice that
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
. By
{\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle}
we denote the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For any function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we define the convex conjugate of
f
by

f^{\ast}{\left(u\right)}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\left\{{\left\langle{x},{u}\right\rangle}-f{\left(u\right)}\right\}}.

Let
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denote the collection of all symmetric positive definite matrices. The proximity operator of
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with respect to
P\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}
is a function mapping
\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, defined by

(4.4)

\prox_{f,P}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq\argmin_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\left\{f{\left(u\right)}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|u-x\right\|}_{P}^{2}\right\}},\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
\|{\cdot}\|_{P}^{2}\coloneqq{\left\langle{\cdot},{P\cdot}\right\rangle}
. It is worth remarking that for
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we always have
f^{\ast}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and there holds

(4.5)

\prox_{f^{\ast},P}=\Id-P^{-1}\circ\prox_{f,P^{-1}}\circ P.

By a direct application of relation between proximity operator and subdifferntial of functions in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we can easily derive the following characterization theorem
[
20
,
22
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
x_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a solution of (
4.3
), then there exists
y_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that for all
\alpha>0
,
P\in\mathbb{S}^{N}_{+}
and
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{M}_{+}
, there holds

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{\ast}&=\prox_{\alpha g,P}{\left(x_{\ast}-P^{-1}L^{\ast}y_{\ast}\right)},\\
y_{\ast}&=\prox_{(\alpha h)^{\ast},Q}{\left(Q^{-1}Lx_{\ast}+y_{\ast}\right)}.\end{aligned}\right.

Conversely, if there exist
P\in\mathbb{S}^{N}_{+}
and
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{M}_{+}
satisfying the fixed-point equations (
4.6
), then the corresponding
x_{\ast}
is a solution of (
4.3
).

It is worth noticing that due to the smoothness of function
g
, there is an alternative form of fixed-point characterization of solutions of (
4.3
) involving gradient of
g
. This gradient approach simplifies the fixed-point equation
Equation

4.6
, but at the same time requires exact estimation of Lipschitz constant of the gradient for convergence analysis, which is complicated for problem
eq.

2.3
. To have a clear convergence condition, we choose
eq.

4.6
for further discuss. For details of the gradient approach, readers are referred to
[
21
]
.

We can rewrite fixed-point equation (
4.6
) in a compact form. For
\alpha>0
,
P\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{+}
, we define block matrices

R\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}P&0\\
0&Q\end{bmatrix},\quad E\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}I&-P^{-1}L^{\ast}\\
Q^{-1}L&I\end{bmatrix},

and function

\Phi{\left(z\right)}\coloneqq\alpha g{\left(x\right)}+{\left(\alpha h{\left(y\right)}\right)}^{\ast}.

Then, (
4.6
) is equivalent to

(4.7)

z_{\ast}=\prox_{\Phi,R}{\left(Ez_{\ast}\right)},

where
z_{\ast}\coloneqq{\left(x_{\ast},y_{\ast}\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+M}
.
That is,
z_{\ast}
is a fixed-point of operator
\prox_{\Phi,R}\circ E
.

The fixed-point characterization (
4.7
) brings two advantages. First of all, this formulation provides a guidance for designing convergence-guaranteed algorithms. Moreover, by this characterization, the non-differentiability of
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is no longer an obstacle, as long as the proximity operator
\prox_{\Phi,R}
can be easily calculated. Indeed, as we will show next, operator
\prox_{\Phi,R}
has a closed-form.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
\alpha>0
,
P\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}
and
Q:={\rm diag}(q_{i})_{i=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{M}
, then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,

\prox_{\alpha g,P}{\left(x\right)}={\left(P+2\alpha B\right)}^{-1}{\left(Px-\alpha b\right)},

and

\prox_{(\alpha h)^{\ast},Q}{\left(y\right)}={\left[\Proj_{{\left[-\alpha,\alpha\right]}}{\left(y_{i}-d_{i}/q_{i}\right)}\right]}_{i=1}^{M},

where
\Proj_{{\left[-\alpha,\alpha\right]}}
denotes the projection operator onto
{\left[-\alpha,\alpha\right]}
.

Proof.

Both of these closed-form formulas can be shown by the definition of the proximity operator. By (
4.4
),

\prox_{\alpha g,P}{\left(x\right)}=\argmin_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\left\{\alpha u^{\ast}Bu+\alpha b^{\ast}u+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|u-x\right\|}^{2}_{P}\right\}},

whose right-hand side is a minimization of a quadratic function of
u
. Taking the gradient of the right-hand side and solving for the zero gradient, we obtain the first formula.

It remains to establish the formula for
\prox_{(\alpha h)^{\ast},Q}
. By identity (
4.5
), for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
we have that

\prox_{(\alpha h)^{\ast},Q}{\left(y\right)}=y-Q^{-1}\prox_{\alpha h,Q^{-1}}{\left(Qy\right)}.

By setting
\tilde{v}\coloneqq v-d
, we observe that

\displaystyle\prox_{\alpha h,Q^{-1}}{\left(Qy\right)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\left\{\alpha{\left\|v-d\right\|}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|v-Qy\right\|}^{2}_{Q^{-1}}\right\}}

\displaystyle=d+\argmin_{\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\left\{\alpha{\left\|\tilde{v}\right\|}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|\tilde{v}-{\left(Qy-d\right)}\right\|}^{2}_{Q^{-1}}\right\}}

\displaystyle=d+\prox_{\alpha{\left\|\cdot\right\|}_{1},Q^{-1}}{\left(Qy-d\right)}.

Since
Q
is diagonal, we obtain that

\prox_{\alpha{\left\|\cdot\right\|}_{1},Q^{-1}}{\left(Qy-d\right)}={\left[{\left({\left(y_{i}-\alpha\right)}q_{i}-d_{i}\right)}_{+}\sgn{\left({\left(y_{i}-\alpha\right)}q_{i}-d_{i}\right)}\right]}_{i=1}^{M},

where
(\cdot)_{+}\coloneqq\max\{\cdot,0\}
and
\sgn
is the sign function (for details, see
[
22
]
). Finally, combining the equations above yields the desired formula.
∎

With help of
4.2
, we can easily obtain the closed form of
\prox_{\Phi,R}
by the following calculation. For any
z={\left(x,y\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+M}
, by definition of proximity operator
eq.

4.4
, we have

\displaystyle\prox_{\Phi,R}{\left(z\right)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N},v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\left\{\alpha g{\left(u\right)}+{\left(\alpha h\right)}^{\ast}{\left(v\right)}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|u-x\right\|}^{2}_{P}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\|v-y\right\|}^{2}_{Q}\right\}}

\displaystyle={\left(\prox_{\alpha g,P}{\left(x\right)},\prox_{{\left(\alpha h\right)}^{\ast},Q}{\left(y\right)}\right)}.

Numerical Results Using a Multiscale Basis

In this section we perform examples of solving
eq.

1.1
using multiscale bases to illustrate the adaptivity/sparsity feature of
L^{1}
stabilization. We test
eqs.

4.9
and
4.10
with following example

(5.1)

u(x)=\begin{cases}t{\left({\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)}^{n+1}-\frac{x}{t}\right)},&0\leq x\leq t,\\
{\left(1-t\right)}{\left(\frac{1-x}{1-t}-{\left(\frac{1-x}{1-t}\right)}^{n+1}\right)},&t<x\leq 1,\\
\end{cases}

where
0<t<1
and
n>0
. The corresponding
q
has a kink at
x=t
:

q(x)=u^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}{\left(n+1\right)}{\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)}^{n}-1,&0\leq x\leq t,\\
{\left(n+1\right)}{\left(\frac{1-x}{1-t}\right)}^{n}-1,&t<x\leq 1.\\
\end{cases}

Now we introduce the multiscale piecewise polynomial space and basis on
[0,1]
once presented in
[
7
]
. Define
\mathbb{X}_{n}\coloneqq W_{2^{-n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and transformations of
f\in L^{2}{\left[0,1\right]}
as

{\left(\tau_{0}f\right)}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq f{\left(2x\right)}\chi_{{\left[0,1\right]}}{\left(2x\right)},\qquad{\left(\tau_{1}f\right)}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq f{\left(2x-1\right)}\chi_{{\left[0,1\right]}}{\left(2x-1\right)},

where
\chi_{{\left[0,1\right]}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
takes
1
on
{\left[0,1\right]}
and
0
otherwise. Then it is proven in
[
7
]
that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{X}_{n}=\mathbb{X}_{0}\oplus^{\perp}\mathbb{W}_{1}\oplus^{\perp}\cdots\oplus^{\perp}\mathbb{W}_{n},

where
\mathbb{W}_{1}
is defined by
\mathbb{X}_{2}=\mathbb{X}_{1}\oplus^{\perp}\mathbb{W}_{1}
and for
n\geq 2
,

\mathbb{W}_{n}\coloneqq\tau_{0}\mathbb{W}_{n-1}\oplus\tau_{1}\mathbb{W}_{n-1}.

Here ‘
\oplus^{\perp}
’ indicates addition between two orthogonal sets in
L^{2}
.

In
[
7
]
the multiscale piecewise quadratic polynomial basis is proposed as follows. There are three basis functions for
\mathbb{X}_{0}
, as

w_{00}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq 1,\quad w_{01}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq\sqrt{3}{\left(2x-1\right)},\quad w_{02}{\left(x\right)}\coloneqq\sqrt{5}{\left(6x^{2}-6x+1\right)},

and for
\mathbb{W}_{1}
,

\displaystyle w_{10}{\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}1-6x,&x\in{\left[0,1/2\right]},\\
5-6x,&x\in(1/2,1],\end{cases}

\displaystyle w_{11}{\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{91}}{31}{\left(240x^{2}-116x+9\right)},&x\in{\left[0,1/2\right]},\\
\frac{\sqrt{91}}{31}{\left(3-4x\right)},&x\in(1/2,1],\end{cases}

\displaystyle w_{12}{\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{91}}{31}{\left(4x-1\right)},&x\in{\left[0,1/2\right]},\\
\frac{\sqrt{91}}{31}{\left(240x^{2}-364x+133\right)},&x\in(1/2,1].\end{cases}

Define
M_{1}\coloneqq{\left\{w_{1i}\right\}}_{i=1}^{3}
and
M_{n}\coloneqq\tau_{0}M_{n-1}\cup\tau_{1}M_{n-1}
for all
n\geq 2
. Then
[
7
]
proves that
M_{n}
is a basis of
\mathbb{W}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
. Such multiscale basis has vanishing moment of
4
since by construction we have
M_{n}\perp\mathbb{X}_{0}
, and this gives us a sparse representation of solutions. Finally the elements of representation are truncated under certain threshold.

5.1
), we set
t=1/6
and

6.
Concluding Remark

In this paper, we propose a novel
DG
method for the second order elliptic equations in the non-divergence form with
L^{1}
stabilization. The motivation for such an approach is to develop numerical schemes for PDEs such that the resulting solutions have sparse representation. This work is the first attempt along this path. Due to the use of
L^{1}
stabilization in the minimization formulation, we are unable to use existing analytical tools for gradient based methods. We believe the analysis used in the error estimates and the
FP
2
​A algorithm can be applied to more challenging problems with low regularity settings. It is worth to point out that the original
L^{1}
regularization technique is different from what we proposed in this paper. Namely, the purpose of regularization is to relax the functional from ill-posedness. Meanwhile, in our work the
L^{1}
term is a stablization term which ensures the convergence and existence of the minimizer of the discrete problem without breaking the consistency with the PDE. In turn, the sparsity promoting feature of the multiscale piecewise polynomial basis is not fully utilized in the current formulation. Nevertheless, we believe that a possible way to improve the performance of the scheme is to add a regularization term such as
\|Bx\|_{1}
in this functional where
B
is the multiscale piecewise polynomial decomposition. This is subject to our ongoing work in this project.
