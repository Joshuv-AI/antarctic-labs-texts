Fujiki relations and fibrations of irreducible symplectic varieties
Martin Schwald

Abstract




Abstract.
This paper concerns different types of singular complex projective varieties generalizing irreducible symplectic manifolds. We deduce from known results that the generalized Beauville-Bogomolov form satisfies the Fujiki relations and has rank
\big(3,0,b_{2}(X)-3\big)
. This enables us to study fibrations of these varieties; imposing the newer definition from
[
GKP16
, Definition 8.16.2]
we show that they behave much like irreducible symplectic manifolds.

Keywords.
irreducible symplectic, primitive symplectic, symplectic singularities, Lagrangian fibrations, Fujiki relation

1. Introduction

1.1. Symplectic varieties

Irreducible symplectic manifolds
were introduced by Beauville
[
Bea83b
, p. 763f]
and play an important role in the structure theory of complex manifolds of Kodaira dimension zero as they occur as factors in the famous Beauville-Bogomolov decomposition theorem
[
Bea83b
, th. 2]
.
A singular version of the decomposition theorem was recently proved
[
HP19
, Theorem 1.5]
, building on the work of
[
GKP16
,
GGK19
,
DG18
,
Dru18
]
.
Remarkably, the class of singular symplectic factors emerging from this approach
[
GKP16
, Definition 8.16.2]
, we call them here
primitive symplectic varieties
, differs from the types of singular symplectic varieties studied before.

To understand the geometry of
primitive symplectic varieties
better, we want to classify their fibrations.
Our main results show that they behave much like irreducible symplectic manifolds and are arguably closer to them than other definitions. While we prove in Theorem
2
and
3
that many of the properties that are well known for irreducible symplectic manifolds hold for a bigger class of symplectic varieties, which we call here
irreducible symplectic varieties
, Theorem
4
holds for primitive symplectic varieties but not completely for
irreducible symplectic varieties
.

We briefly recall the relevant definitions required to state our main results. The different notions in the literature generalizing holomorphic symplectic manifolds to a singular setting agree in the existence of a
symplectic form
on the smooth locus, which is best seen as a
reflexive

2
-form.

Reflexive differential forms
are holomorphic
p
-forms on the smooth locus
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
of a normal variety
X
. We denote the associated sheaf on
X
by
{}^{[p]}_{X}
and get

{}^{[p]}_{X}\cong i_{*}{}^{p}_{X_{\mathrm{reg}}}\cong({}^{p}_{X})^{\ast\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}^{[p]}_{X})\cong\textup{H}^{0}(X_{\mathrm{reg}},\,{}_{X_{\mathrm{reg}}}^{p}),

where
i\colon X_{\mathrm{reg}}\inj X
is the inclusion. These forms satisfy good pull back properties, see Section
2.1
. We recommend
[
Har80
]
for a reference on
reflexive sheaves
and
[
GKKP11
, I–III]
,
[
KP16
, I]
for more information on reflexive differential forms.

A
quasi-étale
morphism
\pi\colon X^{\prime}\ra X
is a finite surjective morphism between complex varieties that is étale outside of an analytic subset
Z\subset X^{\prime}
with
\codim Z\geq 2

Definition 1

(Symplectic varieties)
.

Let

(1)

A

(2)

The pair

{\widetilde{\omega}\in\textup{H}^{0}(\widetilde{X},\,{}^{2}_{\widetilde{X}})}
that coincides with
\nu^{*}\omega
over
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
. We say that
\nu^{*}\omega

extends
to
\widetilde{\omega}
on
\widetilde{X}
.

(3)

A symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
is called

if its irregularity
\h^{1}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})
vanishes and if

\h^{0}(X,\,{}^{[2]}_{X})=1
.

(4)

We call an irreducible symplectic variety

if

is
\mathds{Q}
-factorial with

\codim_{\mathds{C}}X_{\mathrm{sing}}\geq 4
.

(5)

We call a symplectic variety

if for all quasi-étale morphisms
\pi\colon X^{\prime}\ra X
with
X^{\prime}
normal the reflexive
1

1

This reflexive pullback exists e.g. due to
[

generates the exterior algebra of reflexive forms on

, so

Section
2.5
reviews properties of these notions and discusses their relations. Symplectic varieties were introduced by Beauville
[
Bea00
, Definition 1.1]
.

Namikawa symplectic varieties, most prominently studied by Namikawa and Matsushita, have a well-behaved deformation theory
[
Nam01c
]
.
Primitive symplectic varieties are building blocks in a singular version of the Beauville-Bogomolov decomposition theorem.
Using
[
GKP16
, Proposition 6.9]
, we can consider irreducible symplectic varieties as a natural generalization of these two notions.

Irreducible symplectic manifolds
are by definition simply connected, smooth irreducible symplectic varieties
[
Bea83b
, p. 763f]
. Using the Beauville-Bogomolov decomposition theorem we show that a smooth irreducible symplectic variety is always either simply connected or an étale quotient of an Abelian variety by a finite group of biholomorphic automorphisms, see Lemma

Irreducible symplectic manifolds are always primitive symplectic by
[
Bea83b
, prop. 3]

. This is our motivation to study how their geometric properties differ.

1.2. Main results

On an irreducible symplectic variety

, Namikawa and Kirschner constructed in
[

, Theorem 8 (2)]

[

, Definition 3.2.7]
an important quadratic form

. Its definition will be recalled in Section

, Proposition 4.1]
,
[

, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 2

(Fujiki relations, index of the Beauville-Bogomolov form)
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a
2n
-dimensional, irreducible symplectic variety. The Beauville-Bogomolov form

has the following properties:

(1)

There is a number
c_{X}\in\mathds{R}^{+}
, called

, such that for all
{\alpha\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})}
the following, so-called

, holds,
2
2

2

We will recall integration on singular cohomology in Section
2.3
.

c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(\alpha)^{n}=\intop\nolimits_{X}\alpha^{2n}.

In particular for classes of Cartier divisors

, this relates the Beauville-Bogomolov form to the intersection product via
c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(d)^{n}=D^{2n}
.

(2)

The restriction of
q_{X}
to
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})\ra\mathds{R}
is a real quadratic form of
index

\big(3,0,b_{2}(X)-3\big)

Part (

-orthogonal decomposition
{\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}}
with
\dim_{\mathds{R}}V_{+}=3
, such that

is positive definite on

and negative definite on

, see Proposition

In the smooth case, these properties were proved by Fujiki and Beauville
[

, Theorem 4.7]
,
[

. Matsushita generalized them to Namikawa symplectic varieties
[

,

.

Matsushita uses his versions of Theorem

[

,

Theorem 3

(Fibrations of irreducible symplectic varieties)
.

Let

be an irreducible symplectic variety of complex

, together with a surjective morphism

with connected fibers onto a

(1)

The base variety

is an
n
-dimensional,

-factorial klt variety with Picard number

.

(2)

The singular locus of

.

(3)

The general fiber is an
n
-dimensional Abelian variety.

(4)

n

3

.
of

Theorem 4

(Fibrations of primitive symplectic varieties)
.

Let

(1)

The base variety

If

Both parts of Theorem

To prove the results on fibrations we work as much as possible on the singular variety

1.3. Outline of the paper

We present in
2.1
–
2.4
our most important methods to prove our main theorems. They are the general extension theorem for differential forms from
[
GKKP11
, Theorem 1.4]
, the existence of terminal models from
[
BCHM10
, Corollary 1.4.3]
and results on the Hodge theory of klt varieties that we explain in detail in
[
Sch17
]
. In particular we will need the Hodge decomposition of
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
and a singular version of the Hodge-Riemann bilinear relations.

After recapitulating well-known properties of symplectic varieties in
2.5
, we prove that terminal models of irreducible or primitive symplectic varieties are Namikawa or primitive symplectic, respectively. In
2.6
we prove in Theorem
15
that singular fibers of Lagrangian fibrations are automatically also Lagrangian, implying that Lagrangian fibrations are always equidimensional. This requires a careful revision of the definitions of Lagrangian subvarieties and a subtle inductive argument. Theorem
15
will be used to show that the general fiber of a Lagrangian fibration is an Abelian variety, which in fact holds for fibrations of every symplectic variety. Here we make use of a recently proven special case of the Lipman-Zariski conjecture, which makes the proof even easier and more geometric than Matsushita’s proof of
[
Mat01
, Corollary 1.4 (2)]
. We explain in Section
3
how the Beauville-Bogomolov form
q_{X}
is defined in the singular setting in a way that is most suitable for direct computations in
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
.

In Section
4
we finally prove our main results. We deduce the Fujiki relation directly from a terminal model, and use our Hodge theoretic methods to calculate the index of
q_{X}
by hand similarly like Matsushita. Theorem
2
is essential to prove the rest of Theorem
3
in the spirit of Matsushita’s proof of
[
Mat01
, Corollary 1.4]
. Proving that
B
is
\mathds{Q}
-factorial becomes particularly involved because handling Weil divisors is much harder in the singular case. The proof of Theorem
4
uses the branched covering trick and going over to a terminal model, which we have seen to be primitive symplectic again.

2. Methods

We recall here the extension theorem, terminal models, integration over singular cohomology classes and results on the Hodge theory for klt varieties. We recapitulate well-known properties of symplectic varieties that will
imply that terminal models of irreducible or primitive symplectic varieties are Namikawa or primitive
symplectic, respectively. Then we discuss the notions of Lagrangian subvarieties and fibrations and examine their singular fibers.

2.1. Extension Theorem

The extension theorem for differential forms
[
GKKP11
, Theorem 1.4]
shows that on a klt variety
X
the pullback of every reflexive form
{\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}^{[p]}_{X})}
to a resolution of singularities can be
extended
over the exceptional locus. This allows us to construct pullbacks of reflexive forms along more general morphisms.

Theorem 5

(Pullbacks of reflexive forms,
[
Keb13
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
f\colon Y\ra X
be a morphism between normal, complex quasi-projective varieties where
X
is a klt variety. Then for every
p
there is a natural pullback morphism
f^{*}{}^{[p]}_{X}\ra{}^{[p]}_{Y}
, consistent with the natural pullback of Kähler differentials on
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.∎

The consistency with the natural pullback of Kähler differentials is made precise in
[
Keb13
, Theorem 5.2]
. We will only consider the case when
f\colon Y\ra X
is surjective. Then this consistency means that the pullback morphism
f^{*}{}^{[p]}_{X}\ra{}^{[p]}_{Y}
agrees with the usual pullback of Kähler differentials wherever
X
and
Y
are smooth. In other words, for every
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}^{[p]}_{X})
there is an
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\,{}^{[p]}_{Y})
that restricts on
Y_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap f^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})
to the natural pullback
f^{*}({\left.\kern-1.2pt\alpha\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{\mathrm{reg}}}})
of Kähler differentials. We call
\widetilde{\alpha}
an
extension
of
f^{*}{\alpha}
to
Y
. Pulling back reflexive forms is contravariant functorial with respect to
f
.

2.2. Terminal models

While not every singular variety admits a crepant resolution, complex projective klt varieties have always a
terminal model
. For canonical singularities a terminal model is a
crepant partial resolution
. This is an easy special case of
[
BCHM10
, Corollary 1.4.3]
.

Theorem 6

(Terminal models,
[
BCHM10
, page 413]
)
.

Let
X
be a complex projective variety with canonical singularities. Then every resolution
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\ra X
of
X
factors as
{\widetilde{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\pi}}}{{\lra}}Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\lra}}X}
, where
\widetilde{\pi},\pi
are birational and
Y
is a
\mathds{Q}
-factorial variety with at most terminal singularities, such that
\pi^{*}K_{X}=K_{Y}
. We call
Y
a
terminal model
of
X
.
∎

2.3. Integration of singular top classes

Recall that we integrate a top singular cohomology class
\phi\in\textup{H}^{2n}(X,\,\mathds{C})
over an
n
-dimensional, compact, complex variety
X
as
\intop\nolimits_{X}\phi\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=[X]\cap\phi
. Here
[X]\in\textup{H}_{2n}(X,\,\mathds{Z})
denotes the canonical fundamental class of
X
, induced by the complex structure on its smooth locus
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
,
[
Sch17
, Section 2.5]
. This integration commutes with pullbacks by bimeromorphic maps.

Lemma 7

(Pullbacks of integrals,
[
Sch17
, Lemma 22]
)
.

If
f\colon X\ra Y
is a bimeromorphic morphism of compact, complex varieties, then
\intop\nolimits_{Y}\alpha=\intop\nolimits_{X}f^{*}\alpha
for all

\alpha\in\textup{H}^{2n}(Y,\,\mathds{C})
.∎

2.4. Hodge Theory for klt varieties

When
X
is a compact complex variety with rational singularities, Namikawa noted in
[
Nam06
, p. 143]
that for every resolution of singularities
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
the pullback morphism
\nu^{*}\colon\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})\to\textup{H}^{2}(\widetilde{X},\,\mathds{C})
is an embedding and therefore the Hodge structure on
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
is pure of weight two. By similar arguments like in
[
Kal06
, Lemma 2.7]
it can be seen that for every reflexive
2
-form
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}_{X}^{[2]})
for which
f^{*}\alpha
extends to a form
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\textup{H}^{0}(\widetilde{X},\,{}^{2}_{X})
there is a class
\underline{\alpha}\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
with
f^{*}\underline{\alpha}=[\widetilde{\alpha}]\in\textup{H}^{2}(\widetilde{X},\,\mathds{C})
. When we have an extension theorem for reflexive forms on
X
like Theorem
5
, it follows that
\alpha\mapsto\underline{\alpha}
defines an isomorphism
\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}_{X}^{[2]})\cong\textup{H}^{2,0}(X)
. The author worked this out and drew the following consequences in his thesis.

Theorem 8

(Hodge decomposition of
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
,
[
Sch17
, Theorem 37]
,
cf.
also
[
Kir15
, B.2.7–B.2.9]
,
[
GKP16
, Proposition 6.9]
)
.

When
X
is a complex projective klt variety, then we have
\textup{H}^{2,0}(X)\cong\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}_{X}^{[2]})
and
\textup{H}^{0,2}(X)\cong\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})
. For every resolution
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
the pullback morphism
\nu^{*}\colon\textup{H}^{a,b}(X)\to\textup{H}^{a,b}(\widetilde{X})
is bijective for
(a,b)=(2,0)
or
(0,2)
and injective for
(a,b)=(1,1)
.

Remark 9
.

Let
X
be a complex projective klt variety. Every reflexive two-form
\alpha
on
X
defines by Theorem
8
a unique cohomology class in
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
that we denote by
\underline{\alpha}
. When
f\colon Y\to X
is a morphism of complex projective klt varieties and
\widetilde{\alpha}
the extension of
f^{*}\alpha
to
Y
by Theorem

, then we have
\underline{\widetilde{\alpha}}=f^{*}\underline{\alpha}
in

Corollary 10

(Bilinear relations on klt varieties,
[
Sch17
, Corollary 42]
)
.

Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional, complex projective klt variety. Every ample class
{a\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})}
induces a sesquilinear form
\psi_{X,a}
on
\textup{H}^{k}(X,\,\mathds{C})
defined by

(v,w)\mapsto(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\cdot\intop\nolimits_{X}v\cup\overline{w}\cup a^{n-k}.

The so-called
Hodge-Riemann bilinear relation

i^{p-q}\cdot\psi_{X,a}(v,v)>0
holds for any non-zero
v
with
{v\cup a^{n-k+1}=0}
that can be written as the cup product of classes
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
.

We write
p\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sumop\displaylimits p_{j}
,
q\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sumop\displaylimits q_{j}
and
k\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=p+q
where
(p_{j},q_{j})
are such that the
\alpha_{j}
lie in the
\textup{H}^{p_{j},q_{j}}(X)
-part of the Hodge decomposition of
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
.
∎

2.5. Properties of symplectic varieties

A symplectic variety has trivial canonical class, as the top exterior power of the symplectic form trivializes the canonical sheaf. It follows that symplectic varieties have canonical singularities
[
Bea00
, Proposition 1.3]
,
[
KM98
, Corollary 5.24]
. Furthermore, Namikawa showed that a symplectic variety has at most terminal singularities if and only if its singular locus is at least of codimension four,
[
Nam01a
, Corollary 1]
.

We point out that Matsushita uses in
[
Mat01
]
the property
\h^{2}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})=1
of irreducible symplectic varieties instead of
\h^{0}(X,\,{}^{[2]}_{X})=1
. It follows from Theorem
8
that these two conditions are equivalent by the Hodge symmetry.

Proposition 11
.

Every terminal model
\pi\colon Y\ra X
of an irreducible symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
is a Namikawa symplectic variety with the extension
\omega^{\prime}\in\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\,{}^{[2]}_{Y})
of
\pi^{*}\omega
to
Y
from Theorem
5
.

Proof.

Namikawa proved that
Y
is again symplectic
[
Nam06
, Remark 1]
. Let
\widetilde{\pi}\colon\widetilde{X}\ra Y
be a resolution of
Y
. Then
\h^{1}(Y,\,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=\h^{1}(\widetilde{X},\,\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{X}})=\h^{1}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})=0
as the irregularity is a birational invariant for varieties with rational singularities and
\h^{0}(Y,\,{}^{[2]}_{Y})=\h^{0}(X,\,{}^{[2]}_{X})=1
by Theorem
5
. Hence
(Y,\widetilde{\omega})
is Namikawa symplectic by
[
Nam01a
, Corollary 1]
.
∎

Proposition 12
.

Every terminal model
\pi\colon Y\ra X
of a primitive symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
is primitive symplectic with the extension
\omega^{\prime}\in\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\,{}^{[2]}_{Y})
of
\pi^{*}\omega
to
Y
.

Proof.

Let
g\colon Y^{\prime}\ra Y
be quasi-étale with
Y^{\prime}
normal. Then the Stein factorization of
\pi\circ g
,
[
Har77
, Corollary III.11.5]
, gives a morphism
\pi^{\prime}\colon Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
with connected fibers and a finite morphism
h\colon X^{\prime}\to X
with
\pi\circ g=h\circ\pi^{\prime}
. Hence
\pi^{\prime}
is birational because
\pi\circ g
is generically finite. If
g

2.6. Lagrangian fibrations

We show in Theorem

that Lagrangian fibrations are equidimensional because also their singular fibers consist of Lagrangian subvarieties. A straightforward generalization of Matsushita’s proof of
[

, Corollary 1]
, see Proposition
16
, works for all fiber components that do not completely lie in the singular locus. To complete the proof of Theorem

we use an additional inductive argument to make sure that there are no fiber components in
X_{\mathrm{sing}}
.

Definition 14

(Lagrangian subvariety and fibration)
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a symplectic variety and

F\nsubset X_{\mathrm{sing}}
and
{\dim F=\frac{1}{2}\dim X}
. If on every
embedded
5
5

5

A resolution of singularities
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\to X

and for which the

\overline{\nu^{-1}(F\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}})}
of

is smooth

of

the extension

of

a

of

The notion of Lagrangian subvarieties does not depend on the chosen embedded resolution because for two resolutions we can go over to a common resolution. Our definition is consistent with Matsushita’s definition,

[

Lemma 15

(Criteria of Lagrangian subvarieties)

Let

with

and

(1)

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap F_{\mathrm{reg}}}}
vanishes as a Kähler differential on

(2)

{i^{*}\underline{\omega}=0\in\textup{H}^{2}(F,\,\mathds{C})}

Proof of (

Let

be an embedded resolution of

with strict transform
\widetilde{i}\colon\widetilde{F}\inj\widetilde{X}
of

of

to

is an isomorphism over a dense open subset, the assumption

By Remark

The following result is a straightforward generalization of

Proposition 16
.

Let

of the symplectic class vanishes in

The Leray spectral sequence for

and the sheaf

gives the map
d_{2}\colon\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})\ra\textup{H}^{0}(B,\,R^{2}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})

\underline{\overline{\omega}}
lies in
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})
. Let

be the fiber over a point

As the general fiber is Lagrangian, not completely contained in

and has canonical singularities by Lemma

vanishes at the general point

{d_{2}(\underline{\overline{\omega}})=0}

Theorem 17
.

Let
f\colon X\ra B
be a Lagrangian fibration of a symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
. Then every component of each fiber of

does not lie completely in

and is Lagrangian. In particular

is equidimensional.

Proof.

We prove this theorem by induction over the dimension of

. If
X
is a surface, all fibers of

are curves. As

is normal, it has only isolated singularities and no fiber component can lie in

. By Proposition
16
the symplectic class vanishes on every fiber component

. Hence

is a Lagrangian subvariety of

and the theorem is proven for

.

We assume now
2n\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\dim X>2
and that the theorem is already proven for all lower dimensional symplectic varieties. Let

be a component of a fiber

of

. Let

be an irreducible component of

. We consider the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{Z}}
and the lifted morphism
\overline{f}\colon\overline{Z}\ra\overline{f(Z)}
between the normalizations. Then by
[
Mat15
, Theorem 3.1]
the normalization

is a symplectic variety and
\overline{f}
is a Lagrangian fibration. By the induction hypothesis, we know that
\overline{f}
is equidimensional, so every fiber of
\overline{f}
has dimension at most

{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{Z}}

. However, by the fiber dimension theorem,

3. Generalized Beauville-Bogomolov form

Namikawa defined a generalized Beauville-Bogomolov form on every Namikawa symplectic variety
X
by pulling back everything to a resolution of singularities
\widetilde{X}
and then calculating Beauville’s formula
[
Bea83b
, p. 772]
. We prefer working with Kirschner’s idea to calculate all integrals directly on the singular cohomology of
X
, which makes explicit calculations easier. Both approaches are equivalent, compatible with pullbacks and, after a suitable normalizing, define for every irreducible symplectic variety a uniquely determined quadratic form
q_{X}
on
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
.

3.1. Definitions of the Beauville-Bogomolov form

Definition 18

(Beauville-Bogomolov form, cf.
[
Nam01c
, Theorem 8 (2)]
)
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a
2n
-dimensional, irreducible symplectic variety with a resolution
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\ra X
and extension
\widetilde{\omega}\in\textup{H}^{0}(\widetilde{X},\,{}_{\widetilde{X}}^{2})
of
\nu^{*}\omega
to
\widetilde{X}
. We consider the pullbacks
\nu^{*}\alpha=\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}\widetilde{\alpha}
of classes
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
as the classes of two-forms. Then the
Beauville-Bogomolov form
on
X
is the quadratic form
q_{X,\omega}\colon\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})\ra\mathds{C}
with

q_{X,\omega}(\alpha)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{\widetilde{X}}(\widetilde{\omega}\overline{\widetilde{\omega}})^{n-1}\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}+(1-n)\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\widetilde{X}}\widetilde{\omega}^{n}\overline{\widetilde{\omega}}^{n-1}\widetilde{\alpha}\Big)\cdot\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\widetilde{X}}\widetilde{\omega}^{n-1}\overline{\widetilde{\omega}}^{n}\widetilde{\alpha}\Big)

for all
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
. The products and powers denote wedge products of forms.

The form
q_{X,\omega}
does not depend on the chosen resolution. Given two resolutions
\nu,\,\nu^{\prime}
of
X
, this can be seen by going over to a common resolution factoring through
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
.

A resolution
\widetilde{X}
does not need to be symplectic again. Note that thus we cannot trivially deduce all properties of
q_{X,\omega}
from the smooth case by considering
\widetilde{X}
.

Definition 19

(Beauville-Bogomolov form, cf.
[
Kir15
, Notation 3.2.1.]
)
.

Let
X
be a
2n
-dimensional, complex projective variety. We define for every
class
{w\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})}
a quadratic form
q_{X,w}\colon\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})\ra\mathds{C}
with

q_{X,w}(v)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{X}(w\overline{w})^{n-1}v^{2}+(1-n)\Big(\intop\nolimits_{X}w^{n}\overline{w}^{n-1}v\Big)\cdot\Big(\intop\nolimits_{X}w^{n-1}\overline{w}^{n}v\Big)

for all
v\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
. Here the products and powers denote the cup product in the cohomology ring
\textup{H}^{*}(X,\,\mathds{C})
. Using our notation from Remark
9
, for an irreducible symplectic
variety
(X,\omega)
the quadratic form
q_{X,\underline{\omega}}
is called

Beauville-Bogomolov form
on
X
.

In order to obtain a uniquely determined quadratic form
q_{X}
on an irreducible symplectic variety
X
, it is convenient to normalize the symplectic class. The following Lemma ensures that the definition will be well defined.

Lemma 20

(Existence and uniqueness of the normalizing)
.

Let
X
be a
2n
-dimensional, irreducible symplectic variety
X
. For all
v\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
we denote
{I(v)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\intop\nolimits_{X}(v\overline{v})^{n}}
. Every symplectic class
{\underline{\omega}\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,{}^{[2]}_{X})\setminus\{0\}}
induces a
normalized class

{w\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=I(\underline{\omega})^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\cdot\underline{\omega}}
with
I(w)=1
. The corresponding Beauville-Bogomolov form

does not depend on the choice of
\underline{\omega}
.

Proof.

As
X
is irreducible symplectic, every symplectic class
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(X,\,{}^{[2]}_{X})\setminus\{0\}
on

differs only by a constant factor from the class

for

and
\omega
only differ by a factor
\frac{c}{|c|}
of absolute value one. We see directly from Definition

that this does not affect

.∎

Definition 21

(Normalized Beauville-Bogomolov form)
.

Let
X
be an irreducible symplectic variety. The
normalized Beauville-Bogomolov form
is defined as
q_{X}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=q_{X,w}
for any class

with

.

Remark 22

(The symmetric bilinear form)
.

The Beauville-Bogomolov form

on an irreducible symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
is induced by a symmetric bilinear form. Like Matsushita we also denote it by

, but with two arguments. We have the usual formula
q_{X}(a,b)=\frac{1}{2}\big(q_{X}(a+b)-q_{X}(a)-q_{X}(b)\big)
for all

.

3.2. Equivalence of the definitions and behaviour under pullbacks

To relate Namikawa’s and Kirschner’s definitions of the Beauville-Bogomolov form, we prove in the following lemma that the Definitions

and
21
behave well under pullbacks along birational morphisms.

Lemma 23
.

Let
{\pi\colon Y\ra X}
be a birational morphism from a normal, complex projective variety

to a symplectic variety
(X,\omega)
with an extension
\omega^{\prime}\in\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\,{}^{[2]}_{Y})
of

is normalized if and only if
\underline{\omega^{\prime}}
is normalized and we have

\displaystyle q_{X,\underline{\omega}}=q_{Y,\underline{\omega^{\prime}}}\circ\pi^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{X}=q_{Y}\circ\pi^{*}.

Proof.

We noted in Lemma
7
that the integration of top cohomology
classes is compatible with pullbacks under birational morphisms. For every

we can apply this to the occurring integrals in Definition
19

of the Beauville-Bogomolov form, such that we get the relation
q_{X,w}(v)=q_{Y,\pi^{*}w}(\pi^{*}v)
for all

. Together with
\pi^{*}\underline{\omega}=\underline{\omega^{\prime}}\in\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\,\mathds{C})
by Remark

, which concludes the proof.
∎

(Equivalence of the definitions, cf.
[

On every irreducible symplectic variety

Proof.

Let
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\ra X
be a resolution and

the extension of

to
\widetilde{X}
. Using the notation of Kirschner’s Definition

as
{q_{X,\omega}=q_{\widetilde{X},\widetilde{\underline{\omega}}}\circ\nu^{*}}
. This equals

by Lemma

.

4. Proofs of the main results

4.1. Fujiki relations and the index of the Beauville-Bogomolov form

A natural approach to prove Theorem
2

Proof of Theorem

We write

for a normalized symplectic class

{\intop\nolimits_{X}(\underline{\omega\overline{\omega}})^{n}=1}

Proposition 25

(Index of the Beauville Bogomolov form
q_{X}
)
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a
2n
-dimensional, irreducible symplectic variety with normalized Beauville-Bogomolov form
q_{X}=q_{X,w}
for
w\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
, and let
a\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})
be an ample class.

Restricting
q_{X}
gives a real quadratic form
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})\ra\mathds{R}
and we get the
q_{X}
-orthogonal decomposition
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}
, where
V_{+}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\langle w+\overline{w},\,iw-i\overline{w},\,a\right\rangle_{\mathds{R}}
is a 3-dimensional space on which
q_{X}
is positive definite and

V_{-}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=a^{\perp}\cap\textup{H}^{1,1}(X)\cap\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})=\{d\in\textup{H}^{1,1}(X)\cap\textup{H}^{2}(X,\mathds{R})\mid q_{X}(d,a)=0\}

is a space on which
q_{X}
is negative definite.

Proof that restricting
q_{X}
gives a real form.

An easy computation for arbitrary
{v\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\mathds{C})}
shows that
{q_{X,w}\big(\overline{v}\big)=\overline{q_{X,w}(v)}}
, using that the integrals are compatible with complex conjugation. Hence restricting gives a real quadratic form
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})\ra\mathds{R}
.

Proof of
V_{+}\perp V_{-}
and the positivity of
q_{X}
on
V_{+}

(
cf.
[
Mat01
, (2.1)]
)
.

We can use
\intop\nolimits_{X}(w\overline{w})^{n}=1
and the formula from Remark
22
to calculate the Beauville-Bogomolov bilinear form
q_{X}
on the classes
w+\overline{w},\,iw-i\overline{w},\,a
. The Hodge decomposition from Theorem
8
allows us to calculate the occurring integrals via the usual type considerations, which gives the relations
q_{X}(w+\overline{w})=q_{X}(iw-i\overline{w})=1
,
q_{X}(w+\overline{w},\,iw-i\overline{w})=0
,
q_{X}(w+\overline{w},\,v)=q_{X}(iw-i\overline{w},\,v)=0
for all
v\in\textup{H}^{1,1}(X)
, and
q_{X}(a)=\frac{n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{X}(w\overline{w})^{n-1}a^{2}
.

Therefore
w+\overline{w},\,iw-i\overline{w}
and
a
are orthogonal with respect to
q_{X}
. The two subspaces
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
of
\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
are likewise orthogonal. The restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptq_{X}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{V_{+}}}
is diagonal with eigenvalues
1,\,1,\,q_{X}(a)
. As
w^{n-1}\cup a^{3}=0
by type considerations, the Hodge-Riemann bilinear relations, Corollary
10

q_{X}(a)=\frac{n}{2}\psi_{X,a}(w^{n-1},w^{n-1})
is positive and therefore
q_{X}
is positive definite on
V_{+}
.

Proof that
V_{+}\oplus V_{-}=\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})
.

The classes
w+\overline{w},\,iw-i\overline{w}
and
a
are obviously real. On the other hand, as
X
is irreducible symplectic,
\{w,\overline{w}\}
is a
\mathds{C}
-basis of
\textup{H}^{2,0}(X)\oplus\textup{H}^{0,2}(X)
. Hence every real class

can be decomposed as
{v=\alpha w+v^{1,1}+\beta\overline{w}}
with
\overline{\alpha}=\beta\in\mathds{C}
and
{v^{1,1}=\overline{v^{1,1}}\in\textup{H}^{1,1}(X)\cap\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})}
. We note
{q_{X}(v^{1,1}-\lambda a,a)=0}
for
{\lambda\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{q_{X}(v^{1,1},a)}{q_{X}(a)}\in\mathds{R}}
. This gives the decomposition

v=\underbrace{Re(\alpha)\cdot(w+\overline{w})+Im(\alpha)\cdot(iw-i\overline{w})+\lambda a}_{\in V_{+}}+\underbrace{v^{1,1}-\lambda a}_{\in V_{-}}

Hence we get
{V_{+}+V_{-}=\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{R})}
. The directness of this sum decomposition will follow from the negativity of
q_{X}
on
V_{-}
.

Proof of the negativity of
q_{X}
on
V_{-}
.

(
Cf.
[
Mat01
, (2.4)]
) Every class
d
in the space

is a real class of type
(1,1)
with
q_{X}(d,a)=0
. We need to show

if

. We use the Fujiki relations on
X
to calculate for every
t\in\mathds{R}

\intop\nolimits_{X}(td+a)^{2n}=c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(td+a)^{n}=c_{X}\cdot\big(t^{2}q_{X}(d)+\underbrace{tq_{X}(d,a)}_{=0}+q_{X}(a)\big)^{n}.

Comparing the
t
and

-terms on both sides yields

and

We have

because

4.2. Fibrations of irreducible symplectic varieties

Let
(X,\omega)
be an irreducible symplectic variety of complex
dimension
2n
, together with a surjective morphism
f\colon X\ra B
with connected fibers onto a
normal, complex projective variety
B
with dimension
0<\dim B<2n
. We subdivide the proof of Theorem
3
into seven steps, which we will prove in the following order:

(1a)

\dim B=n
.

(4a)

The general fiber is a Lagrangian subvariety of
X
.

(4b)

Every fiber component of
f
is an
n
-dimensional Lagrangian subvariety of
X
and does not lie completely in
X_{\mathrm{sing}}
.

(3a)

The general fiber is smooth.

(3b)

The general fiber is an Abelian variety.

(2)

X
is smooth along the general fiber and
f(X_{\mathrm{sing}})\subset B
is a proper closed subset.

(1b)

B
is a
\mathds{Q}
-factorial klt variety with
\rho(B)=1
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

For the proof we choose very ample divisors
A
on
X
and
H
on
B
. We denote their first Chern classes by
a\in\textup{H}^{2}(X,\,\mathds{C})
and
h\in\textup{H}^{2}(B,\,\mathds{C})
. For every class
{d=c_{1}\big(\mathcal{O}_{B}(D)\big)}
that comes from a Cartier divisor
D
on
B
we get
(f^{*}D)^{2n}=0
. Moreover, the Fujiki relation
(f^{*}D)^{2n}=c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(f^{*}d)^{n}
gives

(1)

q_{X}(f^{*}d)=0.

Proof of (
(1a)
).

(
Cf.
[
Mat01
, (3.2)]
) We calculate for every
t\in\mathds{R}^{+}

(A+tf^{*}H)^{2n}=c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(a+tf^{*}h)^{n}=c_{X}\cdot\big(q_{X}(a)+2tq_{X}(a,f^{*}h)\big)^{n}

by Equation (
1
). We see from the
t
-coefficient that
q_{X}(a,f^{*}h)
is positive because
A
is ample:

(2)

q_{X}(a,f^{*}h)=\frac{A^{2n-1}.f^{*}H}{c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(a)^{n-1}}>0.

Therefore the

-coefficient

vanishes for

, but survives for

\binom{2n}{n}\cdot A^{n}.(f^{*}H)^{n}=2^{n}c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(a,f^{*}h)^{n}>0.

This shows

Proof of (
(4a)

(

[

We note

\displaystyle=c_{X}\cdot q_{X}(w+\overline{w}+sa+tf^{*}h)^{n}

Proof of (

4.3. Fibrations of primitive symplectic varieties

We give an example showing that part
1
of Theorem
4
can fail for an irreducible symplectic variety. The idea is due to Matsushita and was worked out by Sawon
[
Mat01
, p. 7f]
,
[
Saw14
, Lemma 15]
.

Example 26
.

Consider the elliptic curve
E=\mathds{C}/\Gamma
with the lattice
\Gamma=\mathds{Z}+\mathds{Z}\cdot\zeta_{6}
, where
\zeta_{6}
is a primitive sixth root of unity, and the
6
-torus
T\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=E^{6}
. The maps

\displaystyle\mathds{C}^{6}\ra

\displaystyle\;\mathds{C}^{6}

\displaystyle(z_{1},\ldots,z_{6})\mapsto

\displaystyle\;(\zeta_{6}z_{1},\zeta_{6}^{5}z_{2},-z_{3},-z_{4},\zeta_{6}^{2}z_{5},\zeta_{6}^{4}z_{6})

\displaystyle(z_{1},\ldots,z_{6})\mapsto

\displaystyle\;(z_{5},z_{6},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})

generate a group of automorphisms of
\mathds{C}^{6}
preserving
\Gamma^{6}\subset\mathds{C}^{6}
, which induces a subgroup
G\subset\Aut T
. Then
X\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T/G
is an irreducible symplectic variety with
{\omega\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}+dz_{3}\wedge dz_{4}+dz_{5}\wedge dz_{6}}
,
[
Bea00
, Proposition 2.4]
. However, it is not primitive symplectic, as it carries holomorphic 3-forms. The group
G
also acts on the subtorus
T^{\prime}
with coordinates
(z_{1},z_{3},z_{5})
and the quotient
B\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T^{\prime}/G
carries the holomorphic form
dz_{1}\wedge dz_{3}\wedge dz_{5}
, hence
K_{B}=0
, and
B
has canonical singularities due to the Reid–Tai criterion
[
Kol13
, Theorem 3.21]
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a primitive symplectic variety with a morphism
f\colon X\ra B
like in Theorem
4
. We have to show the following two parts.

(1)

The base variety
B
is Fano.

(2)

If
B
is smooth, then
B\cong\mathds{P}^{n}
.

Proof of (
1
).

As
B
is
\mathds{Q}
-factorial with
\rho(B)=1
by Theorem
3
, the canonical divisor
K_{B}
is
\mathds{Q}
-Cartier with
K_{B}\equiv tH
for a
t\in\mathds{Q}
. By the Iitaka conjecture, which due to Kawamata is known to hold in this special case, see Theorem
30
in the appendix, we have
\kappa(B)\leq 0
, so
t\leq 0
. Therefore we only need to exclude the case
t=0
. Up to here this is the same idea as in
[
Mat99
, step 5]
.

We assume that
K_{B}
is numerically trivial. Applying
[
Kaw85
, Theorem 8.2]
to a terminal model of
B
shows that there is a
{d\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}
, such that
\mathcal{O}_{B}(dK_{B})\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}
is trivial. As the canonical sheaf is a reflexive sheaf that is locally free on
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
, we can go over to a ramified cyclic covering like in
[
KM98
, 2.52–2.53]
. This is a finite morphism
p\colon\hat{B}\ra B
with
{p^{*}\mathcal{O}_{B}(K_{B})=\mathcal{O}_{\hat{B}}}
that is étale of degree
d
over
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
and possibly branched over
X_{\mathrm{sing}}
. Hence
p
is quasi-étale because
X
is normal. The variety
\hat{B}
is by
[
KM98
, Proposition 5.20]
also a klt variety. We consider the fiber product
X\times_{B}\hat{B}
and take the normalization
\hat{X}
of any component that lies over
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
. The projections from the fiber product give a lift
\hat{f}\colon\hat{X}\ra\hat{B}
of the morphism
f
with
K_{\hat{B}}=0
and a quasi-étale morphism
\pi\colon\hat{X}\ra X
. There is a non-zero reflexive form
\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(\hat{B},\,{}^{[n]}_{\hat{B}})
and, as
\hat{B}
is a klt variety, we can pull it back by Theorem
5
to a non-zero reflexive form
{\hat{f}^{*}\alpha\in\textup{H}^{0}(\hat{X},\,{}^{[n]}_{\hat{X}})}
. As
\hat{f}^{*}\alpha
is not zero,
n
has to be even and
\hat{f}^{*}\alpha
a multiple of
\pi^{*}(\omega)^{n/2}
because
X
is primitive symplectic. This is a contradiction because
\hat{f}^{*}\alpha^{2}=0
, but
\pi^{*}\omega^{n}
has no zeros.

Proof of (
2
).

As
X
is primitive symplectic, every terminal model
\pi\colon Y\ra X
of
X
is also primitive symplectic by Proposition
12
. Then
{\h^{p}(Y,\,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=\h^{0}(Y,\,{}_{X}^{[p]})}
for all
p
by Hodge symmetry,
[
GKP16
, Proposition 6.9]
, so
Y
is
cohomologically irreducible symplectic
in Matsushita’s terminology,
[
Mat15
, Definition 1.6]
. Applying Matsushita’s result
[
Mat15
, Theorem 1.10]
to
f\circ\pi\colon Y\ra B
shows
B\cong\mathds{P}^{n}
if
B
is smooth.
∎

Appendix

We explain here some probably well-known results used in the main proofs above. Lemma
27
can be seen as a special case of
[
Kol96
, Theorem I.6.5]
. Theorems
29
and
30
are easy but useful consequences of results of Kollár and Kawamata.

Lemma 27

(Smoothness at general fibers)
.

Let
f\colon X\ra Y
be a surjective morphism of complex varieties with a general fiber
F
. If
F
is smooth at a point
x\in F
, then
X
is smooth at
x
.

Proof.

Let
X,\,Y
be
n+r
and
n
-dimensional, respectively. A general fiber
F
lies over a smooth point
y\in Y
and has the dimension
r
by the fiber dimension theorem.
Consider a smooth point
x\in F
with maximal ideals
{\mathfrak{m}_{X,x}\subset\mathcal{O}_{X,x}}
and
{\mathfrak{m}_{F,x}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F,x}}
and the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{Y,y}\subset\mathcal{O}_{Y,y}
of
y
. As
y\in Y
and
x\in F
are regular, we can write
{\mathfrak{m}_{F,x}=(\overline{\phi_{1}},\ldots,\overline{\phi_{r}})}
and
{\mathfrak{m}_{Y,y}=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n})}
. The ideal sheaf sequence for
F\subset X
allows us to extend the
\overline{\phi_{i}}
to functions
{\phi_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{X,x}\subset\mathcal{O}_{X,x}}
. Then it can easily be checked that we get a short exact sequence

0\ra f^{*}\mathfrak{m}_{Y,y}\ra\mathfrak{m}_{X,x}\ra\mathfrak{m}_{F,x}\ra 0

that shows
\mathfrak{m}_{X,x}=(f^{*}\psi_{1},\ldots,f^{*}\psi_{n},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{r})
. Hence
x\in X
is regular and also smooth because we work over a perfect field.
∎

Lemma 28

(Singularities of general fibers)
.

Let
f\colon X\ra Y
be a surjective morphism of complex varieties with connected fibers, where
X
is normal. Then the general fiber
F
is irreducible with
{F_{\mathrm{sing}}=F\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}}
. When
X
has canonical (resp. terminal) singularities, the general fiber also has canonical (resp. terminal) singularities.

Proof.

A general fiber
F
lies over a smooth point
y\in Y
.
Let
L_{y}
denote the linear system of hyperplanes passing through
y
.
Then the fiber
F
is the base locus of the linear system
f^{*}L_{y}
.
The general member of
f^{*}L_{y}
is normal with singularities contained in
X_{\mathrm{sing}}

[
BS95
, Theorem 1.7.1.1]
.
It follows by induction over the codimension of
F
that
F
is normal with
F_{\mathrm{sing}}\subset F\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}
.
On the other hand, a smooth point of
F
is by Lemma
27
also a smooth point of
X
.
This gives the equality
F_{\mathrm{sing}}=F\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}
. As
F
is normal and connected, it is also irreducible.

When
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\ra X
is a resolution, the same argument shows that the strict transform
\widetilde{F}
of
F
is smooth.
The adjunction formula allows to compare the minimal discrepancies as
{\mathrm{discr}(F)\geq\mathrm{discr}(X)}
, which shows that
F
also has canonical (resp. terminal) singularities if
X
does.

∎

Theorem 29

(Kollár’s torsion-freeness)
.

Let
f\colon X\ra B
be a surjective morphism with connected fibers between complex projective varieties, where
X
has rational singularities. Then all higher direct image sheaves
R^{i}f_{*}\omega_{X}
for
i\geq 0
are torsion-free sheaves on
B
.

Proof.

Let
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\ra X
be a resolution of singularities. We consider the Grothendieck spectral sequence for the functors
\nu_{*}
,
f_{*}
and the canonical sheaf
\omega_{\widetilde{X}}={}^{n}_{\widetilde{X}}
.

E^{pq}_{2}=R^{p}f_{*}(R^{q}\nu_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}})\Ra R^{p+q}(f\circ\nu)_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}}

By Grauert-Riemenschneider vanishing all higher direct images
R^{q}\nu_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}}
vanish for
q>0
,
[
Laz04
, Theorem 4.3.9]
. Hence we are only left with the row
E^{p0}_{2}
and the spectral sequence degenerates already on
E_{2}
. As
X
has rational singularities, we have
{\nu_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}}=\omega_{X}}
by
[
KM98
, Lemma 5.12]
. This gives us

R^{p}f_{*}\omega_{X}\cong R^{p}f_{*}(\nu_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}})\cong R^{p}(f\circ\nu)_{*}\omega_{\widetilde{X}}.

The latter sheaf is torsion-free by Kollár’s result
[
Kol86
, Theorem 2.1]
.
∎

Theorem 30

(Reduction of the Iitaka conjecture to the minimal model program)
.

Let
f\colon X\ra B
be a surjective morphism with connected fibers between normal, complex projective varieties. If the general fiber
F
has a
good minimal model
, in other words
F
is birational to a variety with semiample canonical divisor, then
\kappa(X)\geq\kappa(F)+\kappa(B)
.

Proof.

We construct a commutative diagram like diagram
5
that lifts
f
to a morphism
\widetilde{f}
between resolutions
\widetilde{X},\widetilde{B}
of
X,B
. The fibers of
\widetilde{f}
are connected. The general fiber of
\widetilde{f}
is smooth by Lemma
28
and lies over the general fiber of
f
. As the Kodaira dimension is a birational invariant, Theorem
30
follows now from the smooth case that was proved by Kawamata,
[
Kaw85
, Corollary 1.2]
.
∎
