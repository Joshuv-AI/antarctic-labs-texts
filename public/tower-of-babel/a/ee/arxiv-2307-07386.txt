Real-time system identification of superconducting cavities with a recursive least-squares algorithm: closed-loop operation
V. Ziemann, Uppsala University, 75120 Uppsala, Sweden

Abstract
We simulate a recursive least-squares estimator to determine the bandwidth ω 12 \omega_{12} and the detuning Δ ​ ω \Delta\omega of a cavity that is controlled with a low-level RF feedback system and we present a comprehensive analysis of the convergence and asymptotic behavior of the algorithm for static and time-varying systems.



1
Introduction

Superconducting acceleration cavities are used to accelerate protons
[
1
,
2
]
,
electrons
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, and heavy ions
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, both
with pulsed
[
1
,
4
]
and with continuous beams
[
3
,
9
]
. Owing
to the low losses, the cavities have a very narrow bandwidth on the order of Hz for
bare cavities and a few 100 Hz for cavities equipped with high-power couplers.
In order to efficiently cool these cavities with liquid helium are the cavities
made of rather thin material, which makes them easily deformable and this changes
their resonance frequency, often by an amount comparable to their bandwidth.
In pulsed operation, the dominant deformation comes from the electro-magnetic
pressure of the field inside the cavity, the Lorentz-force detuning
[
10
]
,
while cavities operated continuously are perturbed by so-called microphonics
[
11
]
,
caused by pressure variations of the liquid helium bath or mechanical perturbations,
for example, by reciprocating pumps or by malfunctioning equipment. As a consequence
of these perturbations, the cavities are detuned and force the power generator to
increase their output to maintain fields necessary for stable operation of the beams.
This reduces the efficiency of the system and requires an, often substantial, overhead
of the power generation, forcing it to operate at a less than optimal working point.
To avoid this sub-optimal mode of operation and to compensate the detuning, many
accelerators employ active tuning systems that use stepper motors and
piezo-actuators
[
15
]
to squeeze the cavities back in tune, which requires
diagnostic systems to measure the detuning.
These measurements are usually
based on comparing the phase of the signal that excites the cavity, measured with
a directional coupler just upstream of the input coupler, to the phase of the
field inside the cavity, measured by a field probe or antenna inside the cavity.
Both analog
[
11
,
12
]
and digital
[
13
,
14
]
signal processing
systems are used; often as part of the low-level radio-frequency (LLRF) feedback
system that stabilizes the fields in the cavity. Even more elaborate systems,
based on various system identification algorithms, were used
[
16
,
17
]
.
All these algorithms normally rely on low-pass filtering the often noisy signals
from the directional couplers and antennas in order to provide a reliable estimate
of the cavity detuning and the bandwidth.

In this report, we focus on a complementary algorithm that exploits correlations
between the
changes
of the input signal that the LLRF system prescribes and
the ensuing
changes
of the fields, as measured by the field probe. Modern
digital LLRF systems often operate at digitization rates of several Msamples per
second, making a large number of “change measurements” available. We subject this
wealth of data to a recursive least-squares (RLS) algorithm to extract the bandwidth

\omega_{12}
and the detuning
\Delta\omega
of a cavity as fit parameters. Such algorithms can be
efficiently implemented inside the LLRF and require only moderate numerical
overhead. Moreover, the difference between the continuously improving estimates
of the fit parameters and the “true” values—the so-called estimation
error—approaches zero
[
18
]
! Finally, this type of algorithm actually
benefits from the measurement noise of the field probe, because it agitates the
feedback and then exploits the ensuing correlations between the input to the
cavity and the field inside. We must, however, point out that the slow convergence
of the algorithm, especially for low-noise systems, makes it more suitable for
continuous-wave operation than for pulsed operation.

In the following sections, we first introduce the model of the cavity and the
feedback and derive a simple proportional controller using optimal control theory
whereas the analysis of PID controllers is deferred to appendices. After
transforming the model to discrete time we develop the RLS algorithm to identify
the cavity parameters in Section
5
and analyze its convergence in
Section
6
. In the following two sections we generalize the method
to efficiently deal with time-varying parameters and analyze its convergence.
In Section
9
we briefly address pulsed systems before closing.

2
Model

Accelerating cavities can be described by an equivalent circuit composed of a
resistor
R
, an inductance
L
, and a capacitor
C
, all connected in parallel.
This circuit is then excited by a current
I
. In
[
19
]

the following differential equation for the envelope of the voltage
V
in the
cavity is derived

\frac{dV}{dt}=-\left(\frac{\hat{\omega}}{2Q_{L}}+\frac{i\hat{\omega}\delta}{2}\right)V+\frac{\hat{\omega}RI}{2Q_{L}(1+\beta)n}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\delta=\frac{\omega}{\hat{\omega}}-\frac{\hat{\omega}}{\omega}=-\frac{2\Delta\omega}{\hat{\omega}}\ ,

(1)

where
\omega
is the frequency of the generator and
\hat{\omega}^{2}=1/LC
is the
resonance frequency of the cavity. The two are usually close, but not necessarily
equal and
\Delta\omega=\hat{\omega}-\omega
is the difference between them.
Moreover,
Q_{L}=Q_{0}/(1+\beta)
is the loaded
Q
-value and
Q_{0}=R\sqrt{C/L}
is
what’s called the unloaded
Q
-value, whereas
\beta
is the coupling of the
antenna that feeds power into the cavity and
n
is the winding ratio of
the transformer that normally models the coupler. With the abbreviations

\hat{\omega}/2Q_{L}=\omega_{12}
and
\hat{\omega}\delta/2=\Delta\omega
, we can rewrite
Equation
1
in the standard form

\frac{dV}{dt}=-(\omega_{12}+i\Delta\omega)V+\omega_{12}\tilde{R}I

(2)

where we defined the abbreviation
\tilde{R}=R/(1+\beta)n
to simplify the notation.
Throughout this report, we assume that all currents and voltages are baseband
signals, that is after the down-mixer and before before the up-mixer.

By splitting the voltage and the current into real and imaginary part,
V=V_{r}+iV_{i}

and
I=I_{r}+iI_{i}
we obtain two equations, one for the real and one for the imaginary
part of the voltage. Assembling the two equations in the form of a matrix leads
us directly to the following state-space representation

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dV_{r}}{dt}\\
\frac{dV_{i}}{dt}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\omega_{12}&-\Delta\omega\\
\Delta\omega&-\omega_{12}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{r}\\
V_{i}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{12}\tilde{R}&0\\
0&\omega_{12}\tilde{R}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I_{r}\\
I_{i}\end{array}\right)

(3)

of the system that describes the dynamics of the cavity voltage powered by a
generator that provides the currents.

Normally we want to control a system around some desired voltage level
\hat{V}_{r}
and

\hat{V}_{i}
, which is the set point of the controller. The currents, we assume that they
already include the beam currents, that produce these voltages are easily found by
setting the left-hand side in Equation
3
to zero and solving for the currents.
This leads us to

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{I}_{r}\\
\hat{I}_{i}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\tilde{R}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\Delta\omega/\omega_{12}\\
-\Delta\omega/\omega_{12}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{V}_{r}\\
\hat{V}_{i}\end{array}\right)\ .

(4)

The dynamics around this operating point is then given by expanding the voltages
around the steady state with
V_{r}=\hat{V}_{r}+v_{r}
and
V_{i}=\hat{V}_{i}+v_{i}
, where

v_{r}
and
v_{i}
are small deviations from the desired set point that we want to
stabilize by changing the currents, which we represent likewise by
I_{r}=\hat{I}_{r}+i_{r}

and
I_{i}=\hat{I}_{i}+i_{i}
. Inserting in Equation
3
then leads us to

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dv_{r}}{dt}\\
\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\omega_{12}&-\Delta\omega\\
\Delta\omega&-\omega_{12}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{r}\\
v_{i}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{12}\tilde{R}&0\\
0&\omega_{12}\tilde{R}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}i_{r}\\
i_{i}\end{array}\right)\ ,

(5)

which describes essentially the same dynamical system as Equation
3
,
but now it describes the dynamics around the chosen set point
\hat{V}_{r}
and

\hat{V}_{i}
.
Equation
5
is in the standard form of a linear dynamical
system
\dot{\vec{v}}=\bar{A}\vec{v}+\bar{B}\vec{i}
where
\vec{v}
is the column
vector of voltages and
\vec{i}
to that of the currents. The matrices
\bar{A}

and
\bar{B}
correspond to those in Equation
5
and are given by

\bar{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\omega_{12}&-\Delta\omega\\
\Delta\omega&-\omega_{12}\end{array}\right)\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\bar{B}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{12}\tilde{R}&0\\
0&\omega_{12}\tilde{R}\end{array}\right)\ .

(6)

In order to control this system we use an optimal controller. The discussion of
PID-type regulators is deferred to the appendices.

3
Optimal control feedback

An optimal controller is characterized by minimizing the functional

J[\vec{v},\vec{i}]=\int\left[\vec{v}^{\top}Q_{v}\vec{v}+\vec{i}^{\top}Q_{i}\vec{i}\right]dt\ ,

(7)

where
Q_{v}
and
Q_{i}
are weights to specify whether the algorithm should favor
small values of the state
\vec{v}
or of the controller
\vec{i}
. We will
use
Q_{v}={\mathbf{1}}
and
Q_{i}=Z^{2}{\mathbf{1}}
, where
Z
is a constant with
the units of an impedance that makes the units of
\vec{v}
(volt) and
\vec{i}

(ampere) commensurate in the definition of
J
. Moreover,
\mathbf{1}
is the

2\times 2
unit matrix.

The theory to design optimal controllers
[
20
,
21
]
for time-invariant systems
relies on finding the solution
K
of the stationary Riccati equation

0=-Q_{x}-\bar{A}^{\top}K-K\bar{A}+K\bar{B}Q_{u}^{-1}\bar{B}^{\top}K\ ,

(8)

where
\bar{A}
and
\bar{B}
are defined in Equation
6
.
Since the detuning
\Delta\omega
is normally small and might even oscillate around zero
if it is caused by microphonics
[
11
]
, we will set
\Delta\omega
to zero when determining

K
. In that case all matrices appearing in Equation
8
are diagonal
and the equation separates into two identical scalar equations, which read

0=-1+\omega_{12}\kappa+\kappa\omega_{12}+(\omega_{12}^{2}\tilde{R}^{2}/Z^{2})\kappa^{2}

(9)

for
\kappa
defined by
K=\kappa\mathbf{1}
. Solving this quadratic equation
results in

\kappa=-\frac{Z^{2}}{\omega_{12}\tilde{R}^{2}}\left[1\mp\sqrt{1+\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{Z^{2}}}\right]\ .

(10)

The control law
[
20
]
relating
\vec{i}
and
\vec{v}
is then given by

\vec{i}=-Q_{u}^{-1}\bar{B}^{\top}K\vec{v}
or, in components, we obtain

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}i_{r}\\
i_{i}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\tilde{R}}\left[1\mp\sqrt{1+\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{Z^{2}}}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{r}\\
v_{i}\end{array}\right)\ .

(11)

We see that it depends on the ratio of
\tilde{R}
and
Z
. We can select it to
make the feedback work harder to minimize the voltages (small
Z
) or
to keep the control currents small (large
Z
).

It remains to determine which sign of the root to use.
Inserting the control law from Equation
11
into Equation
5
,
we eliminate the currents
i_{r}
and
i_{i}
in favor of the voltages
v_{r}
and
v_{i}

and, after some algebra, arrive at

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dv_{r}}{dt}\\
\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}\end{array}\right)=\mp\omega_{12}\sqrt{1+\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{Z^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{r}\\
v_{i}\end{array}\right)\ ,

(12)

which makes it obvious that we need to pick the negative sign of the root in order
to make the feedback system stable. The control law is thus given by Equation
11

with the negative sign of the root.

We can summarize the system we will analyze by

\dot{\vec{v}}=\bar{A}\vec{v}+\bar{B}\vec{i}+noise\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\vec{i}=K_{p}\vec{v}

(13)

where the feedback gain
K_{p}=\frac{1}{\tilde{R}}\left[1-\sqrt{1+\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{Z^{2}}}\right]

is given in Equation
11
, which turns out to be a proportional controller
that minimizes Equation
7
. We also added a noise source to the right-hand
side of the equation in order to account for the process noise in the system.

4
Simulation

For the simulations we will convert the continuous-time system from Equation
13

to discrete time with time step
\Delta t
, which corresponds to the sampling time if the
system is implemented digitally. By replacing the derivatives of the voltages by
finite-difference equations

\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}\to\frac{\vec{v}_{t+1}-\vec{v}_{t}}{\Delta t}

(14)

where we label the time steps by
t
. With these substitutions, Equation
5

becomes

\vec{v}_{t+1}=A\vec{v}_{t}+B\vec{i}_{t}+\vec{w}_{t}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\omega_{12}\Delta t&-\Delta\omega\Delta t\\
\Delta\omega\Delta t&1-\omega_{12}\Delta t\end{array}\right)\ ,

(15)

B=\omega_{12}\Delta t\tilde{R}\mathbf{1}
, and the process noise
\vec{w}_{t}
. We assume that the noise is
uncorrelated and has magnitude
\sigma
. It is thus characterized by its expectation
value
E\left\{\vec{w}_{t}\vec{w}_{s}^{\top}\right\}=\sigma^{2}\delta_{ts}\mathbf{1}
. The feedback
algorithm looks the same as for the continuous-time system

\vec{i}_{t}=\frac{1}{\tilde{R}}\left[1-\sqrt{1+\frac{\tilde{R}^{2}}{Z^{2}}}\right]\vec{v}_{t}\ .

(16)

Iterating the system of Equations
15
and
16
shows that the
system is indeed stabilized by the feedback algorithm and the rms magnitude
of the voltages compared to that of the currents is indeed given by the ratio

\tilde{R}/Z
in Equation
16
.

5
System identification

Now we turn to the task of extracting
\omega_{12}\Delta t
and
\Delta\omega\Delta t
from continuously
recording the voltages and currents. In order to isolate the sought parameters
we rewrite Equation
15
in the form

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left({\mathbf{1}}+F\right)\vec{v}_{t}+B\vec{i}_{t}\qquad\mathrm{with}\qquad F=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-\omega_{12}\Delta t&-\Delta\omega\Delta t\\
\Delta\omega\Delta t&-\omega_{12}\Delta t\end{array}\right)\ .

(17)

and
B=\omega_{12}\Delta t\tilde{R}\mathbf{1}
. For the time being we ignore the noise
\vec{w}_{t}

and rewrite this equation as

\vec{v}_{t+1}-\vec{v}_{t}=F\vec{v}_{t}+\omega_{12}\Delta t\tilde{R}\vec{i}_{t}\\
.

(18)

Moreover, we rewrite
F\vec{v}_{t}+\omega_{12}\Delta t\tilde{R}\vec{i}_{t}
as

\displaystyle F\vec{v}_{t}+\omega_{12}\Delta t\tilde{R}\vec{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\omega_{12}\Delta t\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{r}\\
v_{i}\end{array}\right)_{t}+\Delta\omega\Delta t\left(\begin{array}[]{r}-v_{i}\\
v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}+\omega_{12}\Delta t\left(\begin{array}[]{r}\tilde{R}i_{r}\\
\tilde{R}i_{i}\end{array}\right)_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-v_{r}+\tilde{R}i_{r}&-v_{i}\\
-v_{i}+\tilde{R}i_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\omega_{12}\Delta t\\
\Delta\omega\Delta t\end{array}\right)\ .

We now introduce the abbreviations

G_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-v_{r}+\tilde{R}i_{r}&-v_{i}\\
-v_{i}+\tilde{R}i_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\vec{y}_{t+1}=\vec{v}_{t+1}-\vec{v}_{t}

(31)

and stack Equation
18
for consecutive times on top of each other.
In this way, we obtain a vastly overdetermined system of equations to determine

\omega_{12}\Delta t
and
\Delta\omega\Delta t

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{y}_{2}\\
\vec{y}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
\vec{y}_{T+1}\end{array}\right)=U_{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\omega_{12}\Delta t\\
\Delta\omega\Delta t\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{with}\quad U_{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}G_{1}\\
G_{2}\\
\vdots\\
G_{T}\end{array}\right)

(32)

that we solve in the least-squares sense with the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse

\vec{q}_{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\omega_{12}\Delta t\\
\Delta\omega\Delta t\end{array}\right)_{T}=\left(U_{T}^{\top}U_{T}\right)^{-1}U_{T}^{\top}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{y}_{2}\\
\vec{y}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
\vec{y}_{T+1}\end{array}\right)\ .

(33)

Here we introduce the abbreviation
\vec{q}_{T}
to denote the estimated parameters
at time step
T
.

It turns out that we can avoid lengthy evaluations by calculating Equation
33

recursively. With the definition
P_{T}^{-1}=U^{\top}_{T}U_{T}
, its initial value

P_{0}=p_{0}{\mathbf{1}}
, and the definition of
U_{T}
from Equation
32
we
express
P_{T+1}
through
P_{T}
in the following way

\displaystyle P_{T+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U^{\top}_{T+1}U_{T+1}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{0}{\mathbf{1}}+G_{1}^{\top}G_{1}+G_{2}^{\top}G_{2}+\dots+G_{T}^{\top}G_{T}+G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{T}^{-1}+G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\ .

It turns out that retrieving
P_{T+1}
from inverting
P_{T}^{-1}+G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}
can be
efficiently done with the Woodbury matrix identity
[
22
]

(A+UV^{\top})^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}U(\mathbf{1}+V^{\top}A^{-1}U)^{-1}V^{\top}A^{-1}\ .

(35)

With the substitutions
A^{-1}=P_{T}
,
U=G^{\top}_{T+1}
, and
V^{\top}=G_{T+1}
we obtain

\displaystyle P_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{T}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}G_{T+1}P_{T}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{1}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}G_{T+1}\right]P_{T}\ ,

where the second representation is occasionally convenient to use.

\vec{q}=(\omega_{12}\Delta t,\Delta\omega\Delta t)
as a
function of the iterations for
\sigma=10^{-3}
(left) and
\sigma=10^{-2}
(right). We
parameterize the feedback by
Z/\tilde{R}=0.5
which makes
K_{p}=-1.24/\tilde{R}
.

We now turn to finding
\vec{q}_{T+1}
by writing Equation
33
for
T+1

\displaystyle\vec{q}_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{T+1}\left(G_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{2}+G_{2}^{\top}\vec{y}_{3}+\dots+G_{T}^{\top}\vec{y}_{T+1}+G_{T+1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{T+2}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{1}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}G_{T+1}\right]P_{T}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}^{\top}\vec{y}_{t+1}+G_{T+1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{T+2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{1}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}G_{T+1}\right]\left(\vec{q}_{T}+P_{t}G_{T+1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{T+2}\right)\ .

We point out that only the
2\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}
has to be
inverted in every time step. Equation
36
and
37
constitute the algorithm
to continuously update estimates for the two components of
\vec{q}
, the bandwidth

q(1)=\omega_{12}\Delta t
and the detuning
q(2)=\Delta\omega\Delta t
, as new voltage measurements
\vec{v}_{T+1}

and currents
\vec{i}_{T+1}
—both enter in
G_{T+1}
and
\vec{y}_{T+2}
—become available.
We refer to the code on github
[
23
]
for the details of the implementation.

We also point out that the structural similarity between Equation
3
and
5

implies that the algorithm works both for small changes around a set point and for large
excitations that occur in pulsed operation of cavities that we briefly address in
Section
9
. The algorithm encapsulated in Equations
36
and
37

just efficiently extracts the cavity parameters from the reaction of the output voltages
to excitations of the cavity. This works by either deliberately exciting the cavity in an
open-loop configuration or when it is controlled by a feedback system in a closed-loop
configuration. We will focus on the latter in most of this report, because this mode
of operation allows the system to operate parasitically during “production” runs of
the accelerator. Moreover, it permits a detailed analysis of the convergence of the
algorithm.

Figure

\vec{q}_{0}=(0.02,-0.01)

.

and its recursive versions Equation

and

.

motivates a number of questions about the convergence of the algorithm, such

\sigma
, that determine the rate of convergence.
Moreover, the asymptotically reachable precision is an important quantity to determine.

6
Convergence

In this section we use the methods, developed in
[
25
]
, to analyze a recursive
least-squares algorithm, its rate of convergence and its asymptotic behavior. We immediately
note that
\left(U_{T}^{\top}U_{T}\right)^{-1}\sigma^{2}=P_{T}\sigma^{2}
is the empirical covariance
matrix that contains the squares of error bars
[
20
]
of fit parameters
\vec{q}_{T}

on its diagonal.
The evolution of
P_{T}
can be analyzed with the help of Equation
36
. From numerical
experiments we know that
P_{T}
decreases quite rapidly such that, in this analysis, we
approximate
\mathbf{1}+G_{T+1}P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}
by
\mathbf{1}
which simplifies
Equation
36
to

P_{T+1}\approx P_{T}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}P_{T}\ .

(38)

Here
G_{t}
depends on the voltages
\vec{v}_{t}
and currents
\vec{i}_{t}
as defined in
Equation
31
. For the controller from Equation
13
the currents
are related to the voltages via
\vec{i}_{t}=K_{p}\vec{v}_{t}
, such that we can write the
matrix
G_{t}
in the form

G_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{r}&-v_{i}\\
(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}=\vec{v}_{t}^{\top}H=(v_{r},v_{i})_{t}H

(39)

where
H
is a
2\times 2\times 2
array that is defined as follows

H(1,:,:)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}-1+\tilde{R}K_{p}&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad H(2,:,:)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}0&-1\\
-1+\tilde{R}K_{p}&0\end{array}\right)\ .

(40)

Here we use MATLAB
[
24
]
notation with colons denoting the second
and third argument. In the same way, we can write the transpose
G_{t}^{\top}
as

G_{t}^{\top}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{r}&(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{i}\\
-v_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}=\bar{H}\vec{v}_{t}

(41)

with the
2\times 2\times 2
array
\bar{H}
defined by

\bar{H}(:,:,1)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}-1+\tilde{R}K_{p}&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\bar{H}(:,:,2)=\left(\begin{array}[]{rc}0&-1+\tilde{R}K_{p}\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)\ .

(42)

With these definitions we express
G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}
as

G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}=\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H\ .

(43)

In order to remove the dependence on the process noise
\vec{w}_{t}
we replace

G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}
by its expectation value
E\left\{G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\right\}=\bar{H}E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}\right\}H
and for this we need to
calculate
\vec{v}_{T+1}
in terms of all previous
\vec{w}_{t}
and then perform
the expectation value.

We obtain
\vec{v}_{T+1}
by repeatedly inserting Equation
16
into Equation
15
,
which leads to

\vec{v}_{T+1}=(A+K_{p}B)^{T+1}\vec{v}_{0}+\sum_{s=0}^{T}(A+K_{p}B)^{s}\vec{w}_{T-s}\ .

(44)

If the largest eigenvalue of
A+\kappa B
is smaller than unity, the initial value
\vec{v}_{0}

“dies out” after a while and we can neglect this term and obtain for

E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{T}\sum_{r=0}^{T}(A+K_{p}B)^{s}\vec{w}_{T-s}\vec{w}_{T-r}^{\top}\left((A+K_{p}B)^{\top}\right)^{r}\right\}

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sum_{s=0}^{T}\left((A+K_{p}B)(A+K_{p}B)^{\top}\right)^{s}+o(1)\ ,

where we used the statistics of the noise defined immediately after Equation
15
.
Here
o(1)
denotes a quantity that vanishes in the limit of large
T
. Noting that the
product of matrices in the sum is symmetric, we can diagonalize it and find

(A+K_{p}B)(A+K_{p}B)^{\top}=O\Lambda O^{\top}=\lambda\mathbf{1}\ ,

(46)

where
O=\mathbf{1}
, because the matrix on the left-hand side is anti-symmetric and,
additionally, has equal entries on the diagonal. Therefore also the two diagonal elements
of
\Lambda
are equal and we denote them by
\lambda
. Now inserting this expression into
Equation
45
, we obtain

E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}\right\}=\sigma^{2}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{T}\lambda^{s}\right)\mathbf{1}\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-\lambda}\mathbf{1}\ ,

(47)

where we use that the sum over powers of
\lambda
approximates a geometric series
of which we used the asymptotic value.
We point out that the right-hand side of Equation
47
no longer depends
on
T
and is therefore constant, such that the expectation value

Q=E\left\{G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\right\}
is given by

Q=E\left\{G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\right\}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-\lambda}\bar{H}H=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-\lambda}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})^{2}&0\\
0&2\end{array}\right)\ .

(48)

Here we we evaluate the product of
\bar{H}H
by denoting the matrix elements
of
\bar{H}
by
\bar{H}_{i\alpha\beta}
, those of
H
by
H_{\beta\alpha j}
, and then
summing over the indices
\alpha
and
\beta
in the following fashion

(\bar{H}H)_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2}\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\bar{H}_{i\alpha\beta}H_{\beta\alpha j}\ .

(49)

Writing down the four parts of the double sum and assembling the appropriate
matrix elements from Equation
40
and
42
directly leads
to the result stated in Equation
48
.

For convenience we introduce the abbreviations

\mu_{1}=\frac{2(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})^{2}}{1-\lambda}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mu_{2}=\frac{2}{1-\lambda}

(50)

such that the diagonal elements of
Q
are
\sigma^{2}\mu_{1}
and
\sigma^{2}\mu_{2}
.

We now return to equation

and replace
G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}
by its
asymptotic expectation value
Q
. We also note that both
Q
and the initial value
of
P_{0}=p_{0}\mathbf{1}
are diagonal. In other words the two degrees of freedom
do not mix and we can consider them independently. In the following paragraphs

p_{T}
denotes one of the diagonal elements of
P_{T}
and
\sigma^{2}\mu
is the
corresponding diagonal element in
Q
. The temporal evolution of
p_{T}
is
then given by

p_{T+1}\approx p_{T}-\sigma^{2}\mu p_{T}^{2}

that we solve in a smooth approximation as

\frac{p_{T+1}-p_{T}}{dT}=-\sigma^{2}\mu p_{T}^{2}\qquad\mathrm{or}\qquad\frac{dp_{T}}{dT}=-\sigma^{2}\mu p_{T}^{2}

(52)

where

. This equation has the solution

We find that
p_{T}
shrinks proportional to

, where the time scale

and only depends on the noise level

and
on

, which is defined in Equation

and encapsulates the essential

Let us now introduce the estimation error
\vec{a}_{T}=\vec{q}_{T}-\vec{q}_{0}

Moreover, by combining Equations

\vec{y}_{T+2}

Replacing
\vec{y}_{T+2}
in Equation

leads to

\displaystyle\vec{q}_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{1}-P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\right]\left(\vec{q}_{T}+P_{t}G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\vec{q}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

where we omitted the higher-order contribution with the product of two

\vec{q}_{0}

on both sides and introducing the estimation error

allows us to write

\vec{a}_{T+1}=\vec{a}_{T}+P_{T}G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}\vec{a}_{T}\ .

As previously, we now approximate
G_{T+1}^{\top}G_{T+1}
by its expectation value
Q
from
Equation
48
and obtain

\vec{a}_{T+1}\approx\vec{a}_{T}+P_{T}Q\vec{a}_{T}

(58)

Since both
P_{T}
and
Q
are diagonal the system separates into two independent equations
that differ only by their respective value of the parameter
\mu
from Equation
50
.
We therefore omit the arrow on the estimation error
a_{T}
; it is either one component or the
other that has to obey the equation

a_{T+1}=a_{T}-p_{T}\sigma^{2}\mu a_{T}\ .

(59)

As before, we transform this equation into its smooth approximation and obtain

\frac{da_{T}}{dT}=-\sigma^{2}\mu p_{T}a_{T}=-\sigma^{2}\mu\frac{p_{0}}{1+\sigma^{2}\mu p_{0}T}a_{T}\ ,

(60)

where we replaced
p_{T}
by its approximation from Equation
53
. Integrating
this equation is straightforward and leads to the solution

a_{T}=\frac{a_{0}}{1+p_{0}\sigma^{2}\mu T}\ ,

(61)

where
a_{0}
is the difference between the initial estimate of the corresponding component
of
\vec{q}
and the “true” value. Equation
61
then describes the temporal
evolution of the difference between the parameter estimate corresponding component of

\vec{q}_{T}
and that of the “true” value
\vec{q}_{0}
.

by its expectation value

appears to be

61
, the magnitude
of the estimation error follows the magnitude of the error bars, which scale as

. This behavior is also consistent with the very general analysis of the
asymptotic behavior of least-squares algorithms from
[
18
]
. Remarkably, the
error bars and with it the estimation errors will asymptotically approach zero, such
that the “true” system parameters are determined exactly. This is a consequence that
new information in the form of new measurements is added to improve the estimate

\vec{q}_{T}
until it reaches the “true” parameters.

As long as
a_{T}
is larger than the error bars, the approximation from Equation
61

works reasonably well. We estimate the number of iterations
N_{t}
where the transition
from the initial convergence to the asymptotic regime occurs by equating the estimation
error and the error bars given by
\sqrt{p_{t}\sigma^{2}}=\sqrt{p_{0}\sigma^{2}/(1+p_{0}\sigma^{2}\mu)}
.
We find the following value for
N_{t}

N_{t}=\frac{a_{0}^{2}-p_{0}\sigma^{2}}{\mu p_{0}^{2}\sigma^{4}}\ ,

(62)

where we see that
N_{t}
depends on the initial excess of the estimation error above the
noise level
a_{0}^{2}-p_{0}\sigma^{2}
and is inversely proportional to the noise level on the
measurement system.

So far, we assumed that the system parameters
\omega_{12}\Delta t
and
\Delta\omega\Delta t
are constant, which
ensures that the “true” system parameters are asymptotically approached. In most
systems, however, this assumption is not always valid, especially when the cavity is
affected by microphonics. We therefore adapt the algorithm in the
next sections to follow time-varying system parameters.

where we recall that

is one of the diagonal entries in

and

is
the corresponding eigenvalue of

from Equation

. With

which has the solution

for a large number of iterations.

of Equation
67

and favors large values of

no longer approach zero with increasing number of iterations, as they did in

, but remain finite. Remarkably, this asymptotic resolution

from Equation

10
Conclusion and Outlook

Figure 8:
The parameters
|p_{T}|
for infinite
N_{f}

from Equation
53
(left) and for
N_{f}=10^{5}
(right). In each
case, three values of
\mu=0.5
(red),
5
(black) and
50
(blue) are
shown, where larger values of
\mu
clearly cause faster convergence. On
the right-hand side the limiting values for large number of iterations
are clearly seen. In all plots we use
\sigma=0.01
.

We worked out an algorithm to determine the cavity bandwidth
\omega_{12}
and the detuning

\Delta\omega
from LLRF feedback data in real time. The calculations are very efficient and
given by Equations
36
and
37
for static parameters and by
Equations
63
and
64
for time-varying parameters. The latter
case showed a distinct tradeoff between the ability to follow fast changes and the
achievable resolution, whose limit is given by Equation
69
.

The evolution of the diagonal elements of
P_{T}
, which determines the data-driven
covariance matrix and thus the error bars, can be analytically described for
both cases. Its dynamics is entirely determined by the expectation value

Q=E\left\{G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}\right\}
and its eigenvalues
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.
We determine them for different regulators (optimal, PID) in the text and in
the appendices. The left-hand plot in Figure
8
shows the
evolution of one diagonal element of the covariance matrix
P_{T}
for three
values of
\mu
where the forgetting horizon
N_{f}=\infty
causes it
to decrease without limit. On the other hand, with
N_{f}=10^{5}
iterations
the diagonal elements of
P_{T}
saturate at finite values, and thereby finite
error bars, for a large number of iterations.

We found that the evolution of the estimation error
a_{T}
can also be
analytically estimated as long as it is larger than the error bars. In this
regime it follows a
1/T
dependence. Once it becomes comparable to the error
bars it still decreases, but with a
1/\sqrt{T}
dependence, which is
consistent with the general behavior expected
[
18
]
for least-squares
algorithms.

Despite being developed for continuously operated system, the algorithm also
works for pulsed systems and determines the system parameters during
times of change, such as the rise of the pulse. This stimulates the idea to
deliberately introduce small perturbations—so-called dithering—to improve
the convergence of the system identification
[
25
]
, albeit at the cost of
slightly deteriorating the performance of the feedback system. The algorithm
from Section
5
and
7
robustly filters out any useful
information to improve the estimate of the fit parameters. One might even say
that any process noise is good noise for system identification.

Appendix A
Convergence with a PD controller

In Section
6
we found that the convergence of the algorithm is governed by

Q
, the expectation value of
E\left\{G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}\right\}
and the parameters

\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
from Equation
50
. We therefore calculate these
parameters for a proprtional-differential (PD) controller in this appendix for which the
currents
i_{t}
are given by

\vec{i}_{t}=K_{p}\vec{v}_{t}-K_{d}(\vec{v}_{t}-\vec{v}_{t-1})

(75)

instead of Equation
16
. Here
K_{p}
is the proportional gain defined near
the end of Section
3
. Inserting this expression into Equation
15

gives us

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}\right)\vec{v}_{t}+BK_{d}\vec{v}_{t-1}\ .

(76)

Introducing the abbreviations
C=A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}
and
D=BK_{d}
this defines a
recursion relation that resembles the one for the Fibonacci sequence. Since linear
difference equations are solved by power laws, we use the Ansatz

\vec{v}_{t+1}=Z^{t}\vec{w}_{1}
and find that the matrix
Z
has to satisfy

Z^{2}-CZ-D=0

(77)

which is solved by

Z_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(C\pm\sqrt{C^{2}+4D}\right)\ ,

(78)

where the square root of the matrix
C^{2}+4D
can be evaluated in Matlab with the
function
sqrtm()
. Taking the square root can return a matrix with imaginary
entries, which forces us to use the hermitian conjugate (transpose and complex conjugate)
of a matrix
M
, denoted by
M^{\ast}
, instead of the transpose. For matrices with
real entries the hermitian conjugate reverts to the transpose.

With
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
known the voltages
\vec{v}_{t}
are given by

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(c_{1}Z_{1}^{t}+c_{2}Z_{2}^{t}\right)\vec{w}_{1}

with constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
that are determined from the initial conditions

\vec{v}_{1}=0
and
\vec{v}_{2}=\vec{w}_{1}
and lead to

0=\left(c_{1}Z_{1}^{0}+c_{2}Z_{2}^{0}\right)\vec{w}_{1}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\vec{w}_{1}=\left(c_{1}Z_{1}^{1}+c_{2}Z_{2}^{1}\right)\vec{w}_{1}\ .

(79)

Solving for the constants gives us
c_{1}=-c_{2}=\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}
such that

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(Z_{1}^{t}-Z_{2}^{t}\right)\vec{w}_{1}

(80)

which allows us to calculate the influence of the noise
\vec{w}_{1}
at
t=1
on all
subsequent voltages
\vec{v}_{t}
. Conversely, adding the contributions from all previous
noise sources from
t=1
to
t=T
, we find that
\vec{v}_{T+1}
is given by

\vec{v}_{T+1}=\sum_{s=0}^{T}\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(Z_{1}^{s}-Z_{2}^{s}\right)\vec{w}_{T+1-s}\ .

(81)

With the voltages known, we assemble
G_{T+1}
by inserting Equation
75
into
Equation
31
and find

\displaystyle G_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}(-1+\tilde{R}(K_{p}-K_{d}))v_{r}&-v_{i}\\
(-1+\tilde{R}(K_{p}-K_{d}))v_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}+\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}v_{r}&0\\
v_{i}&0\end{array}\right)_{t-1}

(87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{t}^{\top}H+\vec{v}^{\top}_{t-1}J

with
H
being defined analogously to Equation
40
with the substitution
K_{p}\to K_{p}-K_{d}

and
J
is defined by

J(1,:,:)=\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad J(2,:,:)=\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\ .

(88)

In the same fashion we define
\bar{H}
analogously to Equation
42
and
\bar{J}
by

\bar{J}(:,:,1)=\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\bar{J}(:,:,2)=\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\ .

(89)

which allows us to write

G^{\top}_{t}=\bar{H}\vec{v}_{t}+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{t-1}

(90)

With these definitions we can express
G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}
by

\displaystyle G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{T}\right)\left(\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J\right)

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J

and observe that, in order to determine
Q
we need to calculate the expectation values
of
\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}
,
\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}
,
\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}
, and

\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}
. Let us therefore consider the cross term
E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}\right\}

as an example. The calculations for the other expectation values work the same way

\displaystyle E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{T}\sum_{r=0}^{T-1}\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(Z_{1}^{s}-Z_{2}^{s}\right)\right.

(92)

\displaystyle\times\left.\vec{w}_{T+1-s}\vec{w}_{T-r}^{\top}\left(Z_{1}^{\ast r}-Z_{2}^{\ast r}\right)\left(Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{T-1}\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(Z_{1}^{r+1}-Z_{2}^{r+1}\right)\left(Z_{1}^{\ast r}-Z_{2}^{\ast r}\right)\left(Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{T-1}\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left[Z_{1}\left(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{r}-Z_{1}\left(Z_{1}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{r}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\left.-Z_{2}\left(Z_{2}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{r}+Z_{2}\left(Z_{2}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{r}\right]\left(Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left[Z_{1}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}-Z_{1}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{1}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-Z_{2}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{2}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}+Z_{2}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{2}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}\right]\left(Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}\ .

In the second equality we use
E\{\vec{w}_{T+1-s}\vec{w}_{T-r}^{\top}\}=\sigma^{2}\delta_{s(r+1)}\mathbf{1}

and in the last approximate equality we extend the sum to infinity and use the summation formula
for infinite geometric sums that only converges for
|Z_{1,2}|<1
, which defines the limit
of stability for the system. With the abbreviations
A_{ij}=\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{i}Z_{j}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}

and
S=\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}
we write the other expectation values as

\displaystyle E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\left[Z_{1}A_{11}Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{1}A_{12}Z_{2}^{\ast}-Z_{2}A_{21}Z_{1}^{\ast}+Z_{2}A_{22}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right]S^{\ast}

\displaystyle E\left\{\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\ast}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\left[A_{11}Z_{1}^{\ast}-A_{12}Z_{2}^{\ast}-A_{21}Z_{1}^{\ast}+A_{22}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right]S^{\ast}

(93)

\displaystyle E\left\{\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{\ast}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\left[A_{11}-A_{12}-A_{21}+A_{22}\right]S^{\ast}\ .

In Equation
91
these expectation values are sandwiched between the arrays
H
and
J
.
Those terms are evaluated by calculating, for example

(\bar{H}E\left\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}\right\}J)_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2}\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{2}\bar{H}_{i\alpha\beta}M_{\beta\gamma}J_{\gamma\alpha j}

with the
2\times 2
matrix

from the second line in Equation

and
\mu_{2}

[
23
]
for the details of the calculations.

Appendix B
Convergence with a PI controller

In this appendix we calculate
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
for a proportional-integral (PI) controller,
whose control law is defined by

\vec{i}_{t}=K_{p}\vec{v}_{t}-K_{i}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\vec{v}_{i}\ .

(95)

As for the PD controller, inserting Equation
95
into Equation
15

leads to

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(A+BK_{p}\right)\vec{v}_{t}-BK_{i}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\vec{v}_{i}\ .

(96)

In order to remove the dependence on the sum, we calculate the difference of voltages
on two consecutive time steps
t+1
and
t+2
. After some algebra we obtain

\vec{v}_{t+2}-\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(A+BK_{p}-BK_{i}\right)\vec{v}_{t+1}-\left(A+BK_{p}\right)\vec{v}_{t}\ .

(97)

Adding
\vec{v}_{t+1}
on both sides then leads to

\vec{v}_{t+2}=\left(\mathbf{1}+A+BK_{p}-BK_{i}\right)\vec{v}_{t+1}-\left(A+BK_{p}\right)\vec{v}_{t}\ .

(98)

Introducing the abbreviations
\hat{C}=\mathbf{1}+A+BK_{p}-BK_{i}
and
\hat{D}=A+BK_{p}
and using the
Ansatz
\vec{v}_{t+1}=Z^{t}\vec{w}_{1}
the charactersitic equation for this system becomes

Z^{2}-\hat{C}Z+\hat{D}=0

which has the solutions

Z_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{C}\pm\sqrt{\hat{C}^{2}-4\hat{D}}\right)

(100)

(101)

c_{1}
and
c_{2}
from matching the
initial values and, as before, find
c_{1}=-c_{2}=\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}
.
As a consequence of calulating the difference of
\vec{v}_{t+2}-\vec{v}_{t+1}
the
variable
\vec{y}_{t}
is the sum of all
\vec{v}_{i}
and we have to take the difference between
consecutive values in order to obtain

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\vec{y}_{t+1}-\vec{y}_{t}=\left(Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right)^{-1}\left[(Z_{1}-\mathbf{1})Z_{1}^{t-1}-(Z_{2}-\mathbf{1})Z_{2}^{t-1}\right]\vec{w}_{1}\ .

(102)

As for the PD controller, we assemble the matrix
G_{t}
from the voltages by inserting the
control law from Equation
95
into the definition of
G_{t}
from Equation
31

and obtain

\displaystyle G_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{r}&-v_{i}\\
(-1+\tilde{R}K_{p})v_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}-\tilde{R}K_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}y_{r}&0\\
y_{i}&0\end{array}\right)_{t}

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{t}^{\top}H-\vec{y}^{\top}_{t}K

where
\vec{y}_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{t}\vec{v}_{s}
and the array
K
is defined analogously to
J
in
Equation
88
in Appendix
A

K(1,:,:)=\tilde{R}K_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}1&0\\

1&0\end{array}\right)

(109)

and
\bar{K}
analogously to Equation
89
. With these definitions

G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}
is given by

G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}=\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H-\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}J-\bar{J}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\bar{J}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}J\ .

Calculating the expectation values is somewhat lengthy but follows the same
spirit as in the previous appendix and we refer to the code on
[
23
]
for the

\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}
reads

\displaystyle E\{\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\left[R_{1}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}Z_{1}^{\ast}-R_{1}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{1}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-R_{2}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{2}Z_{1}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}Z_{1}^{\ast}+R_{2}\left(\mathbf{1}-Z_{2}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}Z_{2}^{\ast}\right]\left(Z_{1}^{\ast}-Z_{2}^{\ast}\right)^{-1}

and

.

then results in

Appendix C
Convergence with a PID controller

For a PID regulator, the control law for the currents reads

\vec{i}_{t}=K_{p}\vec{v}_{t}-K_{d}(\vec{v}_{t}-\vec{v}_{t-1})-K_{i}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\vec{v}_{i}

(112)

which, upon inserting into Equation
15
, leads to

\vec{v}_{t+1}=\left(A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}\right)\vec{v}_{t}+BK_{d}\vec{v}_{t-1}-BK_{i}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\vec{v}_{i}\ .

(113)

As for the PI regulator, we remove the dependence on the sum by taking the difference

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{t+2}-\vec{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}-BK_{i}\right)\vec{v}_{t+1}

(114)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(A+BK_{p}-2BK_{d}\right)\vec{v}_{t}-BK_{d}\vec{v}_{t-1}

and, after adding
\vec{v}_{t+1}
to both sides of the equation and using the Ansatz

v_{t+1}=Z^{t}\vec{w}_{1}
, we arrive at the characteristic equation

Z^{3}-\left(\mathbf{1}+A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}-BK_{i}\right)Z^{2}+\left(A+BK_{p}-2BK_{d}\right)Z+BK_{d}=0\ .

(115)

In order to find the roots of this matrix-valued equation, we exploit the fact that all
matrices before the powers of
Z
can be diagonalized simultaneously with the matrix
V

\mathbf{1}+A+BK_{p}-BK_{d}-BK_{i}=V\Lambda V^{\ast}

(116)

where
\Lambda
is diagonal with complex-conjugate eigenvalues and
V^{\ast}
is the hermitian
conjugate of
V
. Using
V
we bring
A+BK_{p}-2BK_{d}
and
BK_{d}
to the same basis

S=V^{\ast}\left(A+BK_{p}-2BK_{d}\right)V\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad T=V^{\ast}BK_{d}V

(117)

are all diagonal with complex-conjugate values on the diagonals. This transforms
Equation
115
into two complex-valued equations, where one is the complex
conjugate of the other

z^{3}-\Lambda_{11}z^{2}+S_{11}z+T_{11}=0

(118)

whose roots
z_{1},z_{2},
and
z_{3}
can be found numerically, such that we can reassemble
the matrices
Z_{i}
with

Z_{i}=V\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}z_{i}&0\\
0&z_{i}^{\ast}\end{array}\right)V^{\ast}

(119)

and use them to obtain
\vec{y}_{t}

\vec{y}_{t+1}=V\left[C_{1}Z_{1}^{t}+C_{2}Z_{2}^{t}+C_{3}Z_{3}^{t}\right]V^{\ast}\vec{w}_{1}

(120)

from which we determine
\vec{v}_{t+1}=\vec{y}_{t+1}-\vec{y}_{t}
, as for the PI controller in
the previous appendix. We find the constants
C_{1}
from matching the boundary conditions

\vec{y}_{0}=\vec{y}_{1}=0
and
\vec{y}_{2}=\vec{w}_{1}
, by solving the following system of equations

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
z_{1}&z_{2}&z_{3}\\
z_{1}^{2}&z_{2}^{2}&z_{3}^{2}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
c_{3}\end{array}\right)

(121)

and assembling the
C_{i}
with

C_{i}=V\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{i}&0\\
0&c_{i}^{\ast}\end{array}\right)V^{\ast}\ .

(122)

For
\vec{v}_{t}
we obtain

\vec{v}_{t}=\left[C_{1}(Z_{1}-\mathbf{1})Z_{1}^{t-1}+C_{2}(Z_{2}-\mathbf{1})Z_{2}^{t-1}+C_{3}(Z_{3}-\mathbf{1})Z_{3}^{t-1}\right]\vec{w}_{1}

(123)

Analogously to the procedure in Appendix
A
and
B
, where we summed over all
earlier noise sources to determine the voltage at time
t
, we express
G_{t}
as

\displaystyle G_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}(-1+\tilde{R}(K_{p}-K_{d}))v_{r}&-v_{i}\\
(-1+\tilde{R}(K_{p}-K_{d}))v_{i}&v_{r}\end{array}\right)_{t}+\tilde{R}K_{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}v_{r}&0\\
v_{i}&0\end{array}\right)_{t-1}-\tilde{R}K_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}y_{r}&0\\
y_{i}&0\end{array}\right)_{t}

(131)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{t}^{\top}H+\vec{v}^{\top}_{t-1}J-\vec{y}^{\top}_{t}K\ .

H
and
J
are the same as used in Appendix
A
and
K
is defined in
Equation
109
.
Definitions for
\bar{K}
, analogous to those in Equation
89
, allow us to
write
G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}
as

\displaystyle G^{\top}_{T+1}G_{T+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\bar{H}\vec{v}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J-\bar{H}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}K

(132)

\displaystyle+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{T}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H+\bar{J}\vec{v}_{t}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J-\bar{J}\vec{v}_{T}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}K

\displaystyle-\bar{K}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T+1}^{\top}H-\bar{K}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{v}_{T}^{\top}J+\bar{K}\vec{y}_{T+1}\vec{y}_{T+1}^{\top}K\ .

with
\vec{y}_{t}
given by Equation
120
and
\vec{v}_{t}
by
123
, we evaluate all

in a similar way done in Equation

.
We refrain from presenting the lengthy calculations to determine

In this appendix we consider complementary definitions of

\vec{y}_{t+1}
to those from
Equation

. Let us first assume that the matrix
B

\omega_{12}

{\mathbf{1}}

where the second equality proves convenient in the following. This recursion is initialized

through
all equations, because it carries the inverse units of
\vec{v}_{T}^{2}
. In this simple case,
the update of
\vec{q}_{T+1}
in Equation
37
becomes

\vec{q}_{T+1}=\left[1-\frac{p_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{2}}{1+p_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{2}}\right]\left(\vec{q}_{T}+p_{t}G_{T+1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{T+2}\right)\ .

(137)

Equations
136
and
137
only require to calculate the inverse of
1+p_{T}\vec{v}_{T}^{2}
and
a few multiplications, rather than the inverse of a
2\times 2
matrix that is required in
Section
