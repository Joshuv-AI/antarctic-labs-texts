Designing two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillators with prescribed trajectories and phase-response characteristics
Norihisa Namura, Tsubasa Ishii, Hiroya Nakao

Abstract
We propose a method for designing two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillators with prescribed periodic trajectories and phase response properties based on the phase reduction theory, which gives a concise description of weakly-perturbed limit-cycle oscillators and is widely used in the analysis of synchronization dynamics. We develop an algorithm for designing the vector field with a stable limit cycle, which possesses a given shape and also a given phase sensitivity function. The vector field of the limit-cycle oscillator is approximated by polynomials whose coefficients are estimated by convex optimization. Linear stability of the limit cycle is ensured by introducing an upper bound to the Floquet exponent. The validity of the proposed method is verified numerically by designing several types of two-dimensional existing and artificial oscillators. As applications, we first design a limit-cycle oscillator with an artificial star-shaped periodic trajectory and demonstrate global entrainment. We then design a limit-cycle oscillator with an artificial high-harmonic phase sensitivity function and demonstrate multistable entrainment caused by a high-frequency periodic input.

II.1
Phase reduction theory

II.1.1
Asymptotic phase

We consider a limit-cycle oscillator described by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{X}(t)=\bm{F}(\bm{X}(t)),

(1)

where
\bm{X}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the system state at time
t
.
We assume that the system has an exponentially stable limit-cycle trajectory
\bm{X}_{0}(t)
with a natural period
T
and frequency
\omega=2\pi/T
, which is a
T
-periodic function of
t
satisfying
\bm{X}_{0}(t+T)=\bm{X}_{0}(t)
.

First, we assign a phase
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
for each state on the limit cycle, where
0
and
2\pi
are considered identical,
by choosing a state
\bm{X}_{0}(0)
on the limit cycle as the phase origin,
\theta=0
, and defining the phase of the state
\bm{X}_{0}(t)
at
t>0
as
\theta=\omega t\mod{2\pi}
.
We denote the state on the limit cycle with phase
\theta
by
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)=\bm{X}_{0}(\theta/\omega)
.

Next, we extend the definition of the phase to the basin of the limit cycle.
The phase of a system state
\bm{X}_{A}
in the basin is defined as
\theta

if
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|\Psi_{t,t_{0}}\bm{X}_{A}-\Psi_{t,t_{0}}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)\right\|=0
holds,
where
\Psi_{t,t_{0}}
is the flow of Eq. (
1
) and
\|\cdot\|
is the
L_{2}
norm.
That is, if the state
\Psi_{t,t_{0}}\bm{X}_{A}
started from
\bm{X}_{A}
at
t_{0}
converges to the state
\Psi_{t,t_{0}}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)
started from
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)
at
t_{0}
as
t\to\infty
, we consider that the state
\bm{X}_{A}
has the same phase
\theta
as
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)
.
This defines a phase function
\Theta
for all states
\bm{X}
in the basin, which assigns a phase value
\theta(t)=\Theta(\bm{X}(t))
to the state
\bm{X}(t)
and satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\theta(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\Theta(\bm{X}(t))=\omega.

(2)

The phase defined in this way is called the asymptotic phase,
and the level sets of the phase function are called “isochrons”
Kuramoto1984
;
Hoppensteadt1997
;
Winfree2001
;
Ermentrout2010
;
Nakao2016
.

II.1.2
Phase sensitivity function

The phase sensitivity function (PSF, also known as the infinitesimal phase resetting curve, iPRC) is a key quantity in analyzing synchronization of limit-cycle oscillators by weak inputs.
It is defined by the gradient of the phase function at
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)
on the limit cycle as

\displaystyle\bm{Z}(\theta)=\nabla\Theta(\bm{X})|_{\bm{X}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)}.

(3)

The PSF describes linear phase-response characteristics of the oscillator caused by weak external inputs
Kuramoto1984
;
Winfree2001
;
Brown2004
;
Ermentrout2010
.

The PSF cannot be obtained analytically in general, so it should be calculated numerically or measured experimentally.
If the mathematical model of the oscillator is known, the PSF
\bm{Z}(\theta)
can be obtained by numerically calculating the
2\pi
-periodic solutions to the following adjoint equation
Brown2004
;
Ermentrout2010
;
Kuramoto2019
:

\displaystyle\omega\frac{d}{d\theta}\bm{Z}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\bm{J}}^{\top}(\theta)\bm{Z}(\theta),

(4)

where
\tilde{\bm{J}}^{\top}(\theta)
denotes the transposed Jacobian matrix of the vector field
\bm{F}
at
\bm{X}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)
.
The PSF
\bm{Z}(\theta)
should satisfy
\bm{Z}(\theta)\cdot\bm{F}\left(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta)\right)=\omega
as a normalization condition.

In this study, we consider the PSF as a function with a time argument,

\displaystyle\bm{Z}(\theta)=\bm{Z}(\omega t)\coloneqq\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t).

(5)

For this
\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)
, the adjoint equation (
6
) and normalization condition (
7
) are given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)=-\bm{J}^{\top}(t)\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t),

(6)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)\cdot\bm{F}\left(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t))\right)=\omega,

(7)

where
\bm{J}^{\top}(t)=\tilde{\bm{J}}^{\top}(\theta(t))
is the transposed Jacobian matrix of
\bm{F}
evaluated at
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t))
on the limit cycle.

II.1.3
Phase reduction

Weakly-perturbed limit-cycle oscillators can generally be reduced to a one-dimensional phase equation by using the PSF
\bm{Z}(\theta)
.
Let us consider the dynamics of a weakly-perturbed limit-cycle oscillator described by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{X}(t)=\bm{F}(\bm{X}(t))+\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{X}(t),t),

(8)

where
\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{X},t)
denotes the weak input and
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter representing the intensity of the input.
Since
\varepsilon
is small, the deviation of the oscillator state
\bm{X}(t)
from the state
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t))=\bm{X}_{0}(t)
on the limit cycle with the same phase
\theta(t)
is
O(\varepsilon)
, i.e.,
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t))+O(\varepsilon)
.
The time evolution of the phase
\theta(t)=\Theta(\bm{X}(t))
of the limit-cycle oscillator is then described by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\theta(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}\Theta(\bm{X}(t))\\
&=\omega+\varepsilon\nabla\Theta(\bm{X}(t))\cdot\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{X}(t),t)\\
&=\omega+\varepsilon\nabla\Theta(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t)))\cdot\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(\theta(t)),t)+O(\varepsilon^{2})\\
&=\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta(t))\cdot\bm{\eta}(\theta(t),t)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).\end{aligned}

(9)

Therefore, the phase
\theta(t)
obeys the following approximate phase equation up to
O(\varepsilon)
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\theta(t)=\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta(t))\cdot\bm{\eta}(\theta(t),t).

(10)

The simplicity of this phase equation has facilitated detailed and extensive analysis of synchronization caused by weak inputs
Kuramoto1984
;
Winfree2001
;
Brown2004
;
Ermentrout2010
.

II.2
Synchronization

By using phase reduction, we can analyze the synchronization of limit-cycle oscillators with a weak periodic external input.
We consider a limit-cycle oscillator subjected to weak periodic external input described by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{X}(t)=\bm{F}(\bm{X}(t))+\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{q}}(\bm{X},t),

(11)

where
\tilde{\bm{q}}(\bm{X},t)
denotes a weak periodic external input with the period
\tau
and the frequency
\Omega=2\pi/\tau
satisfying
\tilde{\bm{q}}(\bm{X},t+\tau)=\tilde{\bm{q}}(\bm{X},t)
.
When the intensity
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, the time evolution of the phase
\theta(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\theta(t)=\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta(t))\cdot\bm{q}(\theta(t),t)

(12)

by phase reduction, where the external input is approximately evaluated on the limit cycle, namely,
\bm{q}(\theta(t),t)=\bm{q}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}(\theta(t)),t)
.

Assuming that the natural frequency
\omega
of the oscillator and the frequency
\Omega
of the periodic external input are sufficiently close,
we denote the frequency difference by
\varepsilon\Delta=\omega-\Omega
, where “
\varepsilon\Delta
” indicates that the frequency difference is
O(\varepsilon)
.
The oscillator phase relative to the external input,
\phi(t)=\theta(t)-\Omega t
, obeys

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\phi(t)}=\varepsilon\left[\Delta+\bm{Z}(\phi{(t)}+\Omega t)\cdot\bm{q}(\phi{(t)}+\Omega t,t)\right],

(13)

where the range of
\phi
is extended outside
[0,2\pi)
and
\bm{Z}
and
\bm{q}
are regarded as periodic functions of period
2\pi
.
Since the right-hand side is
O(\varepsilon)
and
\phi
is a slow variable, this equation can further be simplified by the averaging approximation
Kuramoto1984
;
Hoppensteadt1997
;
Nakao2016
,
namely, by averaging the right-hand side over one period of the external input while keeping
\phi(t)
fixed.
This yields a single-variable autonomous system,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\phi(t)}=\varepsilon\left[\Delta+\Gamma(\phi{(t)})\right],

(14)

where we defined a
2\pi
-periodic phase coupling function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma(\phi)&=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\bm{Z}(\phi+\Omega t)\cdot\bm{q}(\phi+\Omega t,t)dt\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{Z}(\phi+\psi)\cdot\bm{q}\left(\phi+\psi,\psi/\Omega\right)d\psi,\end{aligned}

(15)

where
\psi=\Omega t
is the phase of the external input.

We can analyze the entrainment of the oscillator to the weak periodic input by using this simplified equation.
Specifically, the stable fixed point of Eq. (
14
) corresponds to a phase-locking point, where the oscillator phase is entrained to the periodic input.

III
DESIGN OF OSCILLATOR DYNAMICS

In this section, we propose a method for designing a stable two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillator with a prescribed periodic trajectory and PSF.

III.1
Conditions for the periodic trajectory and PSF

Our aim is to design a vector field with a prescribed stable limit cycle
\bm{p}(t)
and PSF
\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)
of period
T
and frequency
\omega=2\pi/T
.
It is noted that the periodic trajectory and the PSFs are not completely independent.
Specifically, the PSFs should satisfy the normalization condition
\dot{\bm{p}}(t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)=\omega
.

We approximate the vector field of the oscillator by using polynomials as
namura2022estimating

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{X})=\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(\bm{X})\\
F_{2}(\bm{X})\end{bmatrix}\simeq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{X})\bm{\zeta}_{1}\\
\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{X})\bm{\zeta}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

where
\bm{X}=[x_{1}\ x_{2}]^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\bm{U}(\bm{X})=\begin{bmatrix}1&\overline{x_{1}}&\overline{x_{2}}&\overline{x_{1}^{2}}&\overline{x_{1}x_{2}}&\overline{x_{2}^{2}}&\cdots&\overline{x_{2}^{n}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}

(17)

is a vector of monomials,
n
is the maximum degree of the polynomial, and
\bm{\zeta}_{1},\bm{\zeta}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
are the coefficient vectors.
Here, the overline denotes standardization (
\overline{z}\coloneqq(z-\mu)/\sigma
), where
\mu
and
\sigma
are the mean and the standard deviation of
z
, respectively.

We assume that the system has a non-intersecting differentiable periodic trajectory
\bm{p}(t)=[p_{1}(t)\ p_{2}(t)]^{\top}
and impose the following condition on the vector field:

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{p}(t))=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{p}(t).

(18)

This equation can be expressed in the polynomial approximation as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{1}\simeq\frac{d}{dt}p_{1}(t),\\
\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{2}\simeq\frac{d}{dt}p_{2}(t).\end{aligned}

(19)

Introducing the coefficient
\bm{\xi}=\left[\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\top}\ \bm{\zeta}_{2}^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2P}
and
discretizing the time
t
into
L
points,
t_{k}=(k-1)\Delta t,\;(k=1,\dots,L)
with
\Delta t=T/L
,
we express the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\end{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}\coloneqq\bm{A}_{\bm{p},k}\bm{\xi}

(20)

for each
t_{k}
, where
\bm{A}_{\bm{p},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2P}
.
We also denote the right-hand side by

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{\bm{p},k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\dot{p}_{1}(t_{k})\\
\dot{p}_{2}(t_{k})\end{bmatrix},

(21)

where
\bm{b}_{\bm{p},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The difference between both sides of Eq. (
18
) under polynomial approximation is then expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\bm{p},k}\bm{\xi}-\bm{b}_{\bm{p},k}.

(22)

Next, we introduce the conditions for the PSF into the vector field.
Assuming that the oscillator has a PSF
\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)=[\tilde{Z}_{1}(t)\ \tilde{Z}_{2}(t)]^{\top}
, we require the adjoint equation (
6
), represented as a function of
t
, is satisfied.
This equation is expressed in the polynomial approximation as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{Z}_{1}(t)\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{1}+\tilde{Z}_{2}(t)\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{2}\simeq-\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{Z}_{1}(t),\\
\tilde{Z}_{1}(t)\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{1}+\tilde{Z}_{2}(t)\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{\zeta}_{2}\simeq-\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{Z}_{2}(t),\end{aligned}

(23)

where
\bm{U}_{i}(\bm{X})=\left(\nabla\bm{U}(\bm{X})\right)_{i}
.

Introducing the coefficient
\bm{\xi}
and
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{L}
,
we express the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{Z}_{1}(t_{k})\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))&\tilde{Z}_{2}(t_{k})\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\\
\tilde{Z}_{1}(t_{k})\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))&\tilde{Z}_{2}(t_{k})\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\end{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}\coloneqq\bm{A}_{\bm{Z},k}\bm{\xi}

(24)

for each
t_{k}
, where
\bm{A}_{\bm{Z},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2P}
,
and also denote the right-hand side as

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{\bm{Z},k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}-\dot{\tilde{Z}}_{1}(t_{k})\\
-\dot{\tilde{Z}}_{2}(t_{k})\end{bmatrix},

(25)

where
\bm{b}_{\bm{Z},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The difference between both sides of Eq. (
6
) under polynomial approximation is then expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\bm{Z},k}\bm{\xi}-\bm{b}_{\bm{Z},k}.

(26)

We seek the polynomial vector field that satisfies Eq. (
18
) and (
6
) as much as possible
by minimizing the overall approximation errors for the periodic trajectory and PSF over one period
T
.
By introducing matrices

\displaystyle\bm{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{\bm{p},1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{A}_{\bm{p},L}^{\top}&\bm{A}_{\bm{Z},1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{A}_{\bm{Z},L}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4L\times 2P}

(27)

and

\displaystyle\bm{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{\bm{p},1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{b}_{\bm{p},L}^{\top}&\bm{b}_{\bm{Z},1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{b}_{\bm{Z},L}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4L},

(28)

the sum of the squared errors in Eqs. (
22
) and (
26
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle E=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-\bm{b}\|^{2}.

(29)

This error is the objective function to be minimized in our method.

In addition to the above conditions, we introduce regularization
of the vector field by adding the squared norm
\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}
of the coefficient
\bm{\xi}
with an appropriate weight
\gamma
to the objective function in order to obtain a better model.
This regularization prevents the coefficients of the vector field from becoming excessively large,
which is expected to reduce the complexity of the vector field and ensures the uniqueness of the solution of the optimization problem.

III.2
Condition for the linear stability

In addition to the conditions for the trajectory and PSF, we also impose the condition for the stability of the periodic trajectory on the vector field.
From the Floquet theory
hartman1964ordinary
;
guckenheimer1983nonlinear
,
the two Floquet exponents
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
characterizing the linear stability of the periodic trajectory satisfy

\displaystyle\exp(\lambda_{1}T)\exp(\lambda_{2}T)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{J}(t))dt\right),

(30)

where
\bm{J}(t)
is the Jacobian matrix at
\bm{X}_{0}(t)
on the periodic trajectory and
\mathrm{tr}
denotes the trace of a matrix.
Taking the logarithm, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{J}(t))dt

(31)

since
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are real for two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillators.

From the condition (
18
) for the periodic trajectory, the first Floquet exponent
\lambda_{1}=0
, thus we only need to evaluate
\lambda=\lambda_{2}
, which should be negative for the stability.
Discretizing the time
t
into
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{L}
, we can approximate the above integral as

\displaystyle\lambda\simeq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(\bm{J}_{11}(t_{k})+\bm{J}_{22}(t_{k})\right),

(32)

which is expressed in the polynomial approximation as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda=\lambda_{2}&\simeq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\bm{\zeta}_{1}+\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\bm{\zeta}_{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))&\bm{U}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{p}(t_{k}))\end{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}\coloneqq\bm{C}\bm{\xi}.\end{aligned}

(33)

We impose the condition

\displaystyle\lambda=\bm{C}\bm{\xi}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{tol}}

(34)

for some maximum tolerance value
\lambda_{\mathrm{tol}}<0

so that the periodic trajectory is sufficiently stable.

III.3
Optimization problem

Summarizing the conditions for the periodic trajectory
\bm{p}(t)
, PSF
\tilde{\bm{Z}}(t)
, and maximal tolerance
\lambda_{\mathrm{tol}}
of the linear stability explained in the previous subsections, our optimization problem for the coefficient vector
\bm{\xi}
is formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\bm{\xi}^{*}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\xi}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-\bm{b}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\bm{C}\bm{\xi}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{tol}}.\end{gathered}

Since this is a quadratic programming problem for the parameter
\bm{\xi}
with a linear inequality constraint, which is convex,
it can be easily solved uniquely and globally.

In the numerical implementation, the weight parameter
\gamma
is chosen
so that the designed oscillator has a stable limit cycle with the prescribed properties and no unnecessary attractors arises around the limit cycle.
Also, to match the scales of the errors for the periodic trajectory and PSF in Eqs. (
22
) and (
26
),
the conditions for the PSF are multiplied by an appropriate normalizing constant.

IV.2.2
Global entrainment

Here, we demonstrate global entrainment of the star-shaped oscillator designed above.
We apply an external periodic input with a frequency
\omega
, which is equal to the natural frequency.
Assuming that the external periodic input has the form of
\bm{q}(t)=\left[-\sin(\omega t),\cos(\omega t)\right]^{\top}
,
we can write the reduced phase equation of the oscillator as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\theta(t)}=\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta{(t)})\cdot\bm{q}(t).

(41)

From Eq. (
14
), the time evolution of the relative phase
\phi{(t)}=\theta(t)-\omega t
obeys

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}{\phi(t)}&=\varepsilon\Gamma(\phi{(t)})\\
&=\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\cos(\phi{(t)}).\end{aligned}

(42)

The phase coupling function
\Gamma(\phi)

IV.3.2
Multistable entrainment

Here, we demonstrate multistable entrainment of the oscillator designed above.
We apply an external periodic input with a frequency
\Omega=5\omega
, which is five times the natural frequency
\omega
.
Assuming that the external periodic input has the form of
\bm{q}(t)=\left[\sin(\Omega t),0\right]^{\top}
, we can write the reduced phase equation of the oscillator as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\theta(t)}=\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta{(t)})\cdot\bm{q}(t).

(45)

Since the ratio of the frequency of the external periodic input to that of the oscillator is
5
,
5:1
entrainment is expected to occur.
Generalizing the analysis in Sec.
II
and defining the phase of the external input to be
\Omega t/5
, we introduce the phase difference

\displaystyle\phi{(t)}=\theta{(t)}-\frac{1}{5}\Omega t.

(46)

The time evolution of
\phi{(t)}
is then

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}{\phi(t)}&=\frac{d}{dt}{\theta(t)}-\frac{1}{5}\Omega\\
&=\omega-\frac{1}{5}\Omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}\left(\phi{(t)}+\frac{1}{5}\Omega t\right)\cdot\bm{q}(t)\\
&=\varepsilon\bm{Z}\left(\phi{(t)}+\frac{1}{5}\Omega t\right)\cdot\bm{q}(t),\end{aligned}

(47)

and by applying the averaging approximation
Kuramoto1984
;
Hoppensteadt1997
;
Nakao2016
,
\phi{(t)}
obeys

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}{\phi(t)}&=\varepsilon\Gamma(\phi{(t)})\\
&=\frac{\varepsilon}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\bm{Z}\left(\phi{(t)}+\frac{1}{5}\Omega t{{}^{\prime}}\right)\cdot\bm{q}(t{{}^{\prime}})dt{{}^{\prime}}\\
&=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cos(5\phi{(t)}).\end{aligned}

(48)

The phase coupling function
\Gamma(\phi)
is shown in Fig.
11
.
There are ten fixed points (five stable and five unstable) satisfying
\dot{\phi}=0
within
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
;
the five stable fixed points are
\phi{{}^{*}}=3\pi/10,\;7\pi/10,\;11\pi/10,\;3\pi/2
, and
19\pi/10
.

We performed numerical simulations to confirm that entrainment with multiple phase differences occurs.
We assumed
\varepsilon=0.05
and evolved
100
independent oscillators from
100

The oscillator states at time

As time passes, the phases of the oscillators gradually form clusters, and at

We note that synchronization with such many fixed points is rarely observed in natural limit-cycle oscillators.
Thus, by using the present method, we can design artificial oscillators with desirable synchronization properties.

V
CONCLUDING REMARKS

We proposed a method for designing two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillators that possess stable prescribed periodic trajectories and PSFs.
Using this method, we could design the vector fields that exhibit the periodic trajectories and PSFs of several types of existing or artificial oscillators.
Furthermore, we were able to design an artificial oscillator with a high-harmonic PSF that rarely exists in the real world, and were able to demonstrate multistable entrainment caused by a high-frequency periodic input.
The proposed method is simple and generally applicable to design the vector fields of various two-dimensional oscillators. Generalizations of the proposed method to higher-dimensional oscillators will be reported in our future study.
