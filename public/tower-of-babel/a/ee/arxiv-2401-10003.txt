Frequency conversion to the telecom O-band using pressurized hydrogen
Anica Hamer, Seyed Mahdi Razavi Tabar, Priyanka Yashwantrao, Alireza Aghababaei, Frank Vewinger, Simon Stellmer

Abstract
Large-scale quantum networks rely on optical fiber networks and photons as so-called flying qubits for information transport. While dispersion and absorption of optical fibers are minimum at the infrared telecom wavelengths, most atomic and solid state platforms operate at visible or near-infrared wavelengths. Quantum frequency conversion is required to bridge these two wavelength regimes, and nonlinear crystals are currently employed for this process. Here, we report on a novel approach of frequency conversion to the telecom band. This interaction is based on coherent Stokes Raman scattering (CSRS), a four-wave mixing process resonantly enhanced in a dense molecular hydrogen gas. We show the conversion of photons from 863 nm 863\text{\,}\mathrm{nm} to the telecom O-Band and demonstrate that the input polarization state is preserved. This process is intrinsically broad-band and can be adapted to any other wavelength.



I
Introduction

For quantum computing and communication purposes, state-preserving frequency conversion of single photons is indispensable in hybrid architecture with optical connections
Rütz et al. 2017
;
Zaske et al. 2012
;
Krutyanskiy et al. 2017
;
Fisher et al. 2021
;
Bell et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2014
;
Tyumenev et al. 2022
. The most widely used method of quantum frequency conversion is based on the
\chi^{2}
nonlinearity of crystals
Rütz et al. 2017
;
Zaske et al. 2012
;
Fisher et al. 2021
;
Bell et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2014
. This approach has been developed towards near-unit conversion efficiency and a drastically reduced incoherent background. In related work, the
\chi^{3}
nonlinearity of atomic gases has also been employed in combination with short-pulse lasers
R. and Matera 1994
;
Tamaki et al. 1998
;
Babushkin and Herrmann 2008
.

Still, this crystal-based approach comes with a range of limitations, including very narrow bandwidth, polarization-dependence of the conversion process, crystal absorption, and inevitable background emission.

In recent work, a new approach to frequency conversion based on resonantly enhanced nonlinear interaction in pressurized diatomic gases
Tyumenev et al. 2022
;
Aghababaei et al. 2023
has been demonstrated, based on a coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) “up-conversion" process in the UV spectral range. It was shown that this process is well suitable for single photon conversion in quantum information processing due to its intrinsically broad bandwidth and insensitivity to the input polarization. Furthermore, it is free of absorption losses, degradation, or power limitations. A wide selection of molecules, isotopic compositions, and transitions allows to cover a wide range of wavelengths.

Here, we present the extension of previous work to the corresponding "upconversion" process, known as coherent Stokes Raman scattering (CSRS)
Zheng and Katsuragawa 2015
, which also relies on a four-wave mixing process that is resonantly enhanced in the vicinity of a vibrational transition in a molecule.

In this letter, we demonstrate conversion from
863\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
to
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. These wavelengths are intentionally chosen to prepare for the conversion of the two entangled photons of the biexciton emission of indium arsenide (InAs)/gallium arsenide (GaAs) quantum dots
Gao et al. 2012
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Joecker et al. 2019
to the telecom O-band. This band includes the zero crossing of the dispersion in optical fibers and thus provides minimal signal distortion
Winzer et al. 2018
. Connecting well-established solid-state platforms to optical fiber networks paves the way towards long-distance quantum communication
Deutsch et al. 2023
.

II
Experimental setup

The concept of the experiment is shown in Fig.
1
. The frequency conversion process is based on two pump fields whose frequency difference at matched to the vibrational level at
124\text{\,}\mathrm{THz}
within the
Q_{1}(1)
branch of molecular hydrogen. Here, the wavelengths of the two pump fields are chosen to be
938\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and
1538\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
.

The
938\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
laser is an amplified diode laser and provides
0.65\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
to the experiment. The other pump laser at
1538\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
is a diode laser, boosted to
15\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
through a fiber amplifier. These two lasers are stabilized at the MHz-level by a wavelength meter.

The signal photons are derived from a diode laser operated near
863\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and attenuated to a typical power of a few
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro W}
. This wavelength is chosen to coincide with the emission of InAs/GaAs quantum dots. The beams are overlapped using dichroic mirrors, and focussed to waists of
80\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
within the hydrogen cell. The beam powers are monitored, and the polarization of the beams can be set individually.

Our hydrogen cell is a home-built, cylindrical high-pressure vessel made from stainless steel that can stand a pressure of up to
60\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
. The cell has a
12\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
bore and a length of
140\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
and is capped by two uncoated sapphire windows.

Behind the cell, the converted photons at
635\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
are separated using dichroic mirrors from the pump and signal light. Bandpass filters (
10\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
width and
93\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
transmission) provide further spectral purification.

Photons converted to
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
pass through a polarizing beamsplitter (PBS) and are detected by two avalanche photodiodes (APD, ID Qube NIR, dead time
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
) with a quantum efficiency of
12.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
. Photons at
635\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
follow a different path and are detected by a photomultiplier tube (PMT, Hamamatsu model H10682-210) with a quantum efficiency of
4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
. Corresponding photon count rates are measured with a single-photon counting module with a dead time of
6\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
.

III.2
Efficiency

As shown already in Fig.
2
, the conversion efficiency is pressure-dependent. This dependence is shown in Fig.
4
. In the CARS process to
635\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, a clear maximum near
8(\pm 0.2)\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
can be observed. For our experimental parameters stated above, the internal conversion efficiency is
8.1\text{\times}{10}^{-10}\text{\,}
and reduces to an external efficiency of
1.5\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}
, which takes into account the transmission losses of the optics and the
4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
efficiency of the detector.

The behaviour of the CSRS process to
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
is strikingly different. The efficiency does not show a pronounced maximum, but oscillates around an internal efficiency of about
1.1\text{\times}{10}^{-9}\text{\,}
for a pressure between about 5 and
20\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
(external efficiency:
9.0\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}
). We attribute the oscillations to fluctuations in the experimental parameters during the measurements, a behaviour we have already observed earlier, as each data point corresponds to a measurement time of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{min}

Aghababaei et al. 2023

The noise level,
i.e.,
the rate of undesired background photons originating from the interaction of the pump fields, is compatible with zero within our experimental uncertainty.

III.3
Preservation of polarization

Quantum frequency conversion requires a faithful preservation of the initial photon state, namely its polarization. In contrast to a crystal, the hydrogen gas itself is isotropic, and the reference frame is set by the light field. To test the preservation of polarization, the output state is read out for two polarization bases, namely linear and circular.

As the preservation of polarization in CARS processes has already been shown
Aghababaei et al. 2023
, we only investigate the CSRS process here. The detection unit involves waveplates at
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, a polarization beamsplitter (PBS), and detectors on the two outputs.

III.3.1
Linear polarization

To check for the preservation of the linear polarization, the pump polarization is set to horizontal and the polarization of the signal photons is rotated. The recorded polarization state of the converted photons shown in Fig.
5
faithfully follows the input polarization.

Deviations can be attributed to imperfect waveplates and beamsplitters, as well as leakage light from the pump fields.

III.3.2
Circular polarization

To check for preservation of the phase, the polarization of the signal photons is turned from linear to circular. Correspondingly, the circularly polarized signal photons at
1346\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
polarized diagonally before the detectors.

The results are shown in Fig.
6
. Again the output polarization faithfully follows the input polarization, and the small deviations from an ideal behaviour can be fully attributed to imperfect polarization optics.

III.3.3
Fidelity

We fit sine functions to the data sets presented in Figs.
5
and
6
and compute the contrast for each measurement. The contrast for these four data sets ranges between 79.1% and 99.3% and is limited by the background signal and imperfect polarization optics, which are different for the two detectors and which we do not correct for. Here, we determine the fidelity as the average of the four contrast values and arrive at a fidelity of 0.904.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have further developed our novel approach to frequency conversion. While our previous work focused on a CARS process in the UV range
Aghababaei et al. 2023
, we here presented a study on a CSRS process in the NIR range.

We have demonstrated frequency conversion from 863 nm to a telecom wavelength and have shown the a photon’s polarization state is preserved. The poor efficiency of this preliminary study can be readily increased though tighter focusing of the beams, increase of the pump powers, and better mode overlap. We anticipate an efficiency increase by orders of magnitude by employing hydrogen-filled hollow-core fibers. Near-unit efficiency has recently been shown in closely related work at comparable wavelengths
Tyumenev et al. 2022
. Dedicated polarization optics and improved spectral filters will reduce the background and thereby increase the process fidelity.
