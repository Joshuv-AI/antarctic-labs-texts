Derivative Preserving Conditions in Conditional Expectation Operator
Battulga Gankhuu

Abstract
In this paper, we consider conditions that a higher order derivative preserve in conditional expectation operator for a generic nonlinear random variable. Also, the paper introduces higher order derivatives of the Expected Shortfall for a generic nonlinear portfolio loss random variable.



1
Introduction

For any integrable random variable
H(x)
, it is clear that
\mathbb{E}[H(x)|H(x)=0]=0
. It is the well–known fact that a derivative of risk measure Value–at–Risk (VaR), which is commonly used in practice equals conditional expectation of the derivative of linear portfolio loss random variable given that the portfolio loss random variable is equal to VaR, see
Tasche ((1999))
and
Tasche ((2000))
. Therefore, if we take
H(x)=L(x)-q_{\alpha}(L(x))
, then we have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}H(x)\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0,

(1.1)

where
L(x)
is a linear portfolio loss random variable, depending on a weight vector
x
and
q_{\alpha}(L(x))
is the VaR at confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Thus, for the risk measure Value–at–Risk, the partial derivative preserve in the conditional expectation operator that depends on weight vector
x
. Then, a question arises: under what conditions, a higher order derivative preserve in conditional expectation operator, depending on the weight vector
x
. In section 2, we aim to answer this question for a generic (nonlinear) integrable random variable.

Artzner et al. ((1999))
suppose that every risk measure should satisfy four axioms, namely, monotonicity, translation invariance, subadditivity, and positive homogeneity. They refer to a risk measure, satisfying the axioms as a coherent risk measure. For linear portfolio loss random variable, VaR satisfies monotonicity, translation invariance, and positive homogeneity but does not satisfy subadditivity in general. However, Expected Shortfall (ES) is a coherent risk measure for linear portfolio loss random variable, see
Acerbi & Tasche ((2002))
. Section 3 is dedicated to higher order derivatives of the ES for generic nonlinear integrable portfolio loss random variable. In section 4, we summarize the results of the paper.

2
Derivatives of Conditional Expectation

Let
H:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an integrable random variable, which is defined on probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Here we assume that the random variable
H(x)
is sufficiently differentiable with respect to argument
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The following Lemma will be used to prove Propositions 1 and 2.

Lemma 1
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2},\dots
be a random sequence such that
|H_{m}|\leq Z
for all
m=1,2,\dots
, where
Z\geq 0
is an integrable random variable. If we assume that
\{A_{m}\}
is a monotone increasing sequence such that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[A_{m}]=0
, then we have that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[|H_{m}|1_{A_{m}}\big]=0

(2.1)

and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[H_{m}1_{A_{m}}\big]=0.

(2.2)

Proof.

Since for all
m=1,2,\dots
,
|H_{m}|
is bounded by
Z
, we get the following inequality

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[|H_{m}|1_{A_{m}}\big]\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z1_{A_{m}}\big].

(2.3)

To prove the Lemma, we use a method, which is referred to standard machine, see
Shreve ((2004))
. Firstly, let us assume that
Z
equals an any indicator function for a generic set
B\in\mathcal{F}
, that is,
Z=1_{B}
. Then,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[1_{B}1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[BA_{m}\big]\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[A_{m}\big]=0.

(2.4)

Secondly, let us assume that
Z
is an any simple random variable, that is,
Z=s:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}1_{B_{i}}
, where for
i=1,\dots,n
,
s_{i}\geq 0
, for
i,j=1,\dots,n
and
i\neq j
,
B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\O
, and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}=\Omega
. Then, we get that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[s1_{A_{m}}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[1_{B_{i}}1_{A_{m}}\big]=0.

(2.5)

Thirdly, let us assume that
Z
is an any nonnegative random variable. Then, there is a monotone increasing sequence of simple random variables that converges to
Z
, i.e.,
s_{k}\uparrow Z
, as
k\to\infty
, where for
k=1,2,\dots
,
s_{k}
are simple random variables, see
Klenke ((2013))
and
Kopp et al. ((2014))
. Thus, as
A_{m}
is the monotone increasing sequence,
\mathbb{E}\big[s_{k}1_{A_{m}}\big]
is a monotone increasing sequence with respect to both index
m
and
k
. Consequently, according to the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Big(\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[s_{k}1_{A_{m}}\big]\Big)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Big(\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[s_{k}1_{A_{m}}\big]\Big)=0.

(2.6)

Finally, let us assume that
Z
is an any real random variable. Then, the random variable
Z
can be written as
Z=Z^{+}-Z^{-}
, where
Z^{+}:=\max(0,Z)
and
Z^{-}:=\max(0,-Z)
are the positive and negative parts of the random variable
Z
. Consequently, since the positive and negative parts of
Z
are nonnegative, by equation (
2.6
), we have that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{+}1_{A_{m}}\big]-\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{-}1_{A_{m}}\big]=0.

(2.7)

As a result, according to equation (
2.3
), one obtains equation (
2.1
). To prove (
2.2
), observe that for
m=1,2,\dots
,
H_{m}=H_{m}^{+}-H_{m}^{-}
and
|H_{m}|=H_{m}^{+}+H_{m}^{-}
, where
H_{m}^{+}:=\max(0,H_{m})
and
H_{m}^{-}:=\max(0,-H_{m})
are the positive and negative parts of the random variable
H_{m}
. Since the positive and negative parts of
H_{m}
are nonnegative and integrable and the indicator random variable
1_{A_{m}}
is nonnegative, it follows from equation (
2.1
) that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[H_{m}^{+}1_{A_{m}}\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[H_{m}^{-}1_{A_{m}}\big]=0.

(2.8)

Consequently, one obtains equation (
2.2
).
∎

Here we consider Proposition, which tackles higher order derivatives of expectation of a generic (nonlinear) random variable.

Proposition 1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, for all
x\in I
,
H(x)
be an integrable random variable and its
n
–th order partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)
is continuous with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, and
Z:=\sup_{x\in I}\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|
be an integrable random variable. If on a set
\{H(x)=0\}
,
H(x)
or
|H(x)|
is a monotone decreasing function with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, then it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|1_{\{H(x)=0\}}\bigg]=0,

(2.9)

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg\{\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)=0\bigg\}\bigcap\{H(x)=0\}\bigg]=\mathbb{P}\big[H(x)=0\big],

(2.10)

and

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg)1_{\{H(x)=0\}}\bigg]=0.

(2.11)

Proof.

Let us fix
x\in I
,
r=0,\dots,n
and
i=1,\dots,d
. We suppose that
x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\in I
for all
m=1,2,\dots
and
r=1,\dots,n
, where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a unit vector, whose
i
–th component is one and others are zero. (
i
) Let us assume that on the set
\{H(x)=0\}
,
H(x)
is a monotone decreasing with respect to
i
–th argument
x_{i}
. Since
H(x)
continuous with respect to the argument
x
,
\big|H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\big|\leq|H(x)|
for
m=1,2,\dots
, and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\big\{H(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k}\big\}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\big\{H(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k}\big\}=\big\{H(x)>\frac{1}{k}\big\}
, due to the dominated convergence theorem, it is clear that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)1_{\{H(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k}\}}1_{\{H(x)=0\}}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\big[H(x)1_{\O}\big]=0

(2.12)

for all
k=1,2,\dots
. Let
B(x):=\{H(x)=0\}
and
B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x):=\big\{H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\geq\frac{1}{k}\big\}
for
m,k=1,2,\dots
. Then, we have

B_{m}^{i,r}(x):=\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)>0\bigg\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x).

(2.13)

As
\{B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\}
is a monotone increasing sequence with respect to the index
k
, it holds

\mathbb{P}\big[B_{m}^{i,r}(x)\big]=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\big]

(2.14)

Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m}^{i,r}(x)\big]=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\big].

(2.15)

Let us define a simple function
s_{m,k}(x):=\frac{1}{k}1_{B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)}
. Then, the following inequality is true

s_{m,k}(x)=\frac{1}{k}1_{B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)}1_{B(x)}\leq H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)1_{B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)}1_{B(x)}.

(2.16)

Therefore, one gets that

\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\big]=\int_{\Omega}s_{m,k}(x)d\mathbb{P}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)1_{B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)}1_{B(x)}\bigg].

(2.17)

As a result, it follows from equations (
2.12
) and (
2.17
) that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\cap B(x)\big]=0.

(2.18)

On the other hand, since on the set
B(x)
, the random variable
H(x)
is monotone decreasing function, we have

\bigg[H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m+1}e_{i}\bigg)-H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\bigg]1_{B(x)}\geq 0.

(2.19)

Thus, the set sequence
\{B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\}
is monotone increasing with respect to the both indexes
m
and
k
. Therefore, it follows from equations (
2.15
) and (
2.18
) that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m}^{i,r}(x)\big]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Big(\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\big]\Big)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Big(\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[B(x)\cap B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)\big]\Big)=0.

(2.20)

(
ii
) Now, we assume that
|H(x)|
is a monotone decreasing with respect to
i
–th argument
x_{i}
.
Since on the set
B(x)
,
\big|H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\big|\leq|H(x)|
for
m=1,2,\dots
and
H(x)
is the continuous function, it follows from dominated convergence theorem that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\bigg|1_{\{H(x)=0\}}\bigg]=0.

(2.21)

If we define
B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)=\big\{\big|H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\big|\geq\frac{1}{k}\big\}
for
m,k=1,2,\dots
, then as
B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x)
is monotone increasing with respect to the index
k
, we have

B_{m}^{i,r}(x):=\bigg\{\bigg|H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\bigg|>0\bigg\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{m,k}^{i,r}(x).

(2.22)

By replacing
H(x)
, which appears in (
i
) part of the Proposition with
|H(x)|
, it can be shown that equation (
2.20
) still holds for the monotone decreasing
|H(x)|
. It should be noted that the rest of the proof is exactly the same for both cases.

Let us introduce the following notations: for fixed
n=1,2,\dots
and arbitrary
m=1,2,\dots
,
A_{m}^{i,n}(x):=\bigcap_{r=1}^{n}\overline{B_{m,m^{n+1}}^{i,r}(x)}
and

\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-j}C_{n}^{j}H\bigg(x+\frac{j}{m}e_{i}\bigg),

(2.23)

where
C_{n}^{i}:=\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}
is the binomial coefficient and for a generic set
A\in\mathcal{F}
,
\overline{A}=\Omega\backslash A
is the complement of the set
A
. If we repeat the mean value theorem of calculus, then there is a sequence
\{x_{m}\}\in I
such that

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x_{m})=m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)~~~\text{and}~~~\lim_{m\to\infty}x_{m}=x.

(2.24)

Consequently,
\big|m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)\big|\leq Z
. Since on the set
B(x)\cap A_{m}^{i,n}(x)
,
\big|\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)\big|<\frac{2^{n}}{m^{n+1}}
, the following inequality is true

\mathbb{E}\big[\big|m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)\big|1_{B(x)}\big]<\frac{2^{n}}{m}+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big|m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)\Big|1_{\big\{B(x)\cap\overline{A_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\big\}}\bigg].

(2.25)

Observe that
\{B(x)\cap\overline{A_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\}
is monotone increasing sequence with respect to index
m
. Due to equations (
2.20
) and (
2.13
) or (
2.22
), we conclude that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[B(x)\cap\overline{A_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\Big]\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\Big[B(x)\cap B_{m,m^{n+1}}^{i,r}(x)\Big]\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}n\mathbb{P}\Big[B(x)\cap B_{m}^{i,r}(x)\Big]=0.

(2.26)

Therefore, it follows from equation (
2.25
) and (
2.26
), the dominated convergence theorem, and Lemma
1
that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\big|m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}H(x)\big|1_{B(x)}\big]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg|1_{B(x)}\bigg]=0.

(2.27)

Equation (
2.27
) implies that

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)1_{B(x)}=0\bigg]=1.

(2.28)

Thus, it holds

1=\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg\{\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)1_{B(x)}=0\bigg\}\bigcap B(x)\bigg]+\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg\{\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)1_{B(x)}=0\bigg\}\bigcap\overline{B(x)}\bigg].

(2.29)

As a result, equation (
2.10
) holds.

Let us denote for a generic random variable
X
, its positive and negative parts by
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
, respectively, that is,
X^{+}:=\max(0,X)
and
X^{-}:=\max(0,-X)
. Then, as the proof of Lemma 1,
|X|=X^{+}+X^{-}
and
X=X^{+}-X^{-}
. According to equation (
2.27
) and the fact that the positive and negative parts are nonnegative, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)^{+}1_{B(x)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)^{-}1_{B(x)}\bigg]=0.

(2.30)

Consequently, it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}H(x)\bigg)1_{B(x)}\bigg]=0.

(2.31)

This completes the proof of the Proposition.
∎

The following two Corollaries, dealing with higher order derivatives of discrete and absolute continuous portfolio loss random variables are immediate consequences of Proposition 1.

Corollary 1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, for all
x\in I
,
H(x)
be an integrable random variable and its
n
–th order partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)
is continuous with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, and
Z:=\sup_{x\in I}\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|
be an integrable random variable. If
\mathbb{P}\big[H(x)=0\big]>0
and on a set
\{H(x)=0\}
,
H(x)
or
|H(x)|
is a monotone decreasing function with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, then it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0,

(2.32)

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)=0\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=1,

(2.33)

and

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0.

(2.34)

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{P}\big[H(x)=0\big]>0
, if we divide the both sides of equations (
2.9
)–(
2.11
) by
\mathbb{P}\big[H(x)=0\big]
, then the proof of the Corollary holds.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, for all
x\in I
,
H(x)
be an absolute continuous integrable random variable and its
n
–th order partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)
is continuous with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, and
Z:=\sup_{x\in I}\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|
be an integrable random variable. If on a set
\{H(x)=0\}
,
H(x)
or
|H(x)|
is a monotone decreasing function with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, then it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0,

(2.35)

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)=0\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=1,

(2.36)

and

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0.

(2.37)

Proof.

Because
\big\{0\leq H(x)\leq\frac{1}{m}\big\}
is a monotone decreasing sequence and its limit is
\{H(x)=0\}
, by monotone convergence theorem for decreasing sequence, equation (
2.9
) can be written by

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|1_{\{H(x)=0\}}\bigg]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|1_{\{0\leq H(x)\leq\varepsilon\}}\bigg]=0.

(2.38)

Consequently, one has

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|1_{\{0\leq H(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\}}\big]}{\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|1_{\{0\leq H(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\}}\big]-2\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|1_{\{0\leq H(x)\leq\varepsilon\}}\big]}{\varepsilon^{2}}

(2.39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|1_{\{H(x)\leq t\}}\bigg]\bigg|_{t=0}.

Since the random variable
H(x)
is absolute continuous, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big[0\leq H(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\big]}{\varepsilon}=2f_{H(x)}(0),

(2.40)

where
f_{H(x)}(t)
is a density function of the random variable
H(x)
.
As a result, we find that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\bigg|\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)\big|1_{\{0\leq H(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\}}\big]}{\mathbb{P}\big[0\leq H(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\big]}=0.

(2.41)

Following the proof of Theorem
1
, one can prove equations (
2.36
) and (
2.37
). Thus, we prove the Corollary.

To relax the condition that on the set
\{H(x)=0\}
the random variable
H(x)
or
|H(x)|
is monotone decreasing with respect to its
i
-th
(i=1,\dots,d)
argument, we assume that the following assumption holds.

Assumption 1
.

Let us assume that for integrable random variables
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}H(x)
,
i=1,\dots,d
, the following condition holds

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]~~~\text{for all}~i,j=1,\dots,d,

(2.42)

If Assumption
1
holds, then the following Proposition is true.

Proposition 2
.

Let for all
x\in I
,
H(x)
be an integrable random variable and positive homogeneous function with respect to argument
x
, where
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open set. If Assumption
1
is true, then it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]=0~~~\text{for}~i=1,\dots,d.

(2.43)

Proof.

According to the Euler’s formula, it holds

H(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}x_{i}.

(2.44)

Since
\mathbb{E}\big[H(x)\big|H(x)=0\big]=0
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\mathbb{E}\big[H(x)\big|H(x)=0\big]=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]x_{j}\bigg\}

(2.45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]\bigg)x_{j}+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg].

As
\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\big|H(x)=0\big]
is a homogeneous function with degree of zero, it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|H(x)=0\bigg]\bigg)x_{j}=0.

(2.46)

Since Assumption
1
holds, it follows from equations (
2.45
) and (
2.46
) that
\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{\partial H(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\big|H(x)=0\big]=0
. That completes the proof.
∎

3
Derivatives of Expected Shortfall

Let
L:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonlinear portfolio loss random variable, defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and for a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
q_{\alpha}(x):=\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{R}\big|F_{L(x)}(t)\geq\alpha\big\}
be an
\alpha
–quantile (VaR) of the portfolio loss random variable
L(x)
, where
F_{L(x)}(t)
is a distribution function of
L(x)
. Here we assume that the random variable
L(x)
and the risk measure
q_{\alpha}(x)
are sufficiently differentiable with respect to argument
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In order to obtain higher order derivatives of the Expected Shortfall, we will follow ideas in
Acerbi & Tasche ((2002))
. Let
U
be an uniform random variable on
[0,1]
and for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
i=1,\dots,d
,
r=0,1,\dots,n
, and
m=1,2,\dots
,
q_{\alpha}\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)
be an
\alpha
–quantile of the loss random variable
L\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)
. To keep notations simple, we introduce
H(x):=q_{U}(x)-q_{\alpha}(x)
. Then, since the quantile function
q_{\alpha}(x)
is a monotone increasing function with respect to the parameter
\alpha
, it holds

\{U>\alpha\}\subset\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\geq 0\bigg\}~~~\text{and}~~~\{U\leq\alpha\}\subset\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\leq 0\bigg\}.

(3.1)

Consequently, one gets that

\{U\leq\alpha\}\bigcap\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\geq 0\bigg\}\subset\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)=0\bigg\}

(3.2)

for
r=0,1,\dots,n
. Note that by the quantile transformation lemma (see
McNeil et al. ((2015))
), for
r=0,1,\dots,n
,

\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)=0\bigg\}\overset{d}{=}\bigg\{L\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)=q_{\alpha}\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\bigg\},

(3.3)

where
d
means equal distribution. Let us denote the ES at confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
of the portfolio loss random variable
L(x)
by
\text{ES}_{\alpha}(x)
. Then, the following Proposition holds.

Proposition 3
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, for all
x\in I
,
H(x):=L(x)-q_{\alpha}(x)
be an integrable random variable and its
n
–th order partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)
is continuous with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, and
Z:=\sup_{x\in I}\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)\big|
be an integrable random variable. If on a set
C(x)
,
H(x)
is a monotone increasing function with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument
x_{i}
, then it holds

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}\mathrm{ES}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)1_{\{L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\}}\bigg]-\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(x)\Big(\mathbb{P}\big[L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\big]-(1-\alpha)\Big)\bigg),

(3.4)

where
C(x):=\{U\leq\alpha\}\cup\{H(x)\geq 0\}
.

Proof.

Let us fix
i=1,\dots,d
. According to the definition of the Expected Shortfall, we have

\text{ES}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{1}q_{u}(x)du,

(3.5)

see
Acerbi & Tasche ((2002))
. Therefore, by equation (
3.1
) (for
r=0
) we have

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}\text{ES}_{\alpha}(x)=\int_{\alpha}^{1}m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{u}(x)du=\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{\{U>\alpha\}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{\{H(x)\geq 0\}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{C(x)}\Big]

(3.6)

According to the mean value theorem of calculus, there is a sequence
\{x_{m}\}\in I
such that

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x_{m})=m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}L(x)~~~\text{and}~~~\lim_{m\to\infty}x_{m}=x.

(3.7)

Consequently,
\big|m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}L(x)\big|\leq Z
. For the first term of the right–hand side of equation (
3.6
), according to equation (
3.3
), one obtains that

\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{\{H(x)\geq 0\}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}L(x)1_{\{L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\}}\Big]

(3.8)

By the dominated convergence theorem, equation (
3.8
) converges to

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{\{H(x)\geq 0\}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)1_{\{L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\}}\bigg].

(3.9)

For the second term of the right–hand side of equation (
3.6
), by the quantile transformation lemma (equation (
3.3
)), we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{C(x)}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{C(x)\cap C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}L(x)1_{C(x)\cap\overline{C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}}\bigg],

(3.10)

where the set
C_{m}^{i,n}(x)
is defined by
C_{m}^{i,n}(x):=\bigcap_{r=1}^{n}\big\{H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\geq 0\big\}
. Due to equation (
3.2
), the first expectation of equation (
3.10
) can be written by

\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{C(x)\cap C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\Big]=m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{\alpha}(x)\mathbb{P}\Big[C(x)\cap C_{m}^{i,n}(x)\Big].

(3.11)

Since on the set
C(x)\subset\{L(x)=q_{\alpha}(x)\}
the random variable
H(x)
is a monotone increasing function with respect to
i
–th argument, for
r=1,\dots,n
, it holds

\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m+1}e_{i}\bigg)\geq 0\bigg\}\subset\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\geq 0\bigg\}~~~\text{for}~m=1,2,\dots.

(3.12)

That means a set sequence
\big\{H\big(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\big)\geq 0\big\}
is monotone decreasing with respect to
m
on the set
C(x)
. Thus, it holds

\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}C_{m}^{i,n}(x)=\bigcap_{r=1}^{n}\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigg\{H\bigg(x+\frac{r}{m}e_{i}\bigg)\geq 0\bigg\}=\big\{H(x)\geq 0\big\}.

(3.13)

As a result, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[C(x)\cap C_{m}^{i,n}(x)\Big]=\mathbb{P}\big[C(x)\big]

(3.14)

and a set sequence
\big\{\overline{C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\big\}
is monotone increasing and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[C(x)\cap\overline{C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\Big]=0.

(3.15)

Consequently, it follows from equations (
3.1
), (
3.11
), (
3.14
), and (
3.15
) and Lemma
1
that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}q_{U}(x)1_{C(x)\cap C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}\Big]

\displaystyle~=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(x)\mathbb{P}\big[C(x)\big]=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(x)\Big(\mathbb{P}\big[L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\big]-(1-\alpha)\Big)

(3.16)

and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[m^{n}\Delta_{m}^{i,n}L(x)1_{C(x)\cap\overline{C_{m}^{i,n}(x)}}\bigg]=0.

(3.17)

Therefore, by equations (
3.6
), (
3.9
) (
3.10
), (
3.16
), and (
3.17
) one conclude that

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}\text{ES}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)1_{\{L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\}}\bigg]-\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(x)\Big(\mathbb{P}\big[L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\big]-(1-\alpha)\Big)\bigg).

(3.18)

That completes the proof of the Theorem.
∎

The following Corollary, corresponding to the absolute continuous portfolio loss random variable is a direct consequence of Proposition 2.

Corollary 3
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, for all
x\in I
,
H(x):=L(x)-q_{\alpha}(x)
be an integrable absolute continuous random variable and its
n
–th order partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}H(x)
is continuous with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument, and
Z:=\sup_{x\in I}\big|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)\big|
be an integrable random variable. If on a set
C(x)
,
H(x)
is a monotone increasing function with respect to
i
–th (
i=1,\dots,d
) argument
x_{i}
, then it holds

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}\mathrm{ES}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i}^{n}}L(x)1_{\{L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\}}\bigg].

(3.19)

Proof.

Since
H(x)
is the absolute continuous random variable,
\mathbb{P}\big[L(x)\geq q_{\alpha}(x)\big]=1-\alpha
. Therefore, equation (
3.19
) follows from equation (
3.4
).
∎

4
Conclusion

The paper provides a Proposition that higher order derivatives of a generic nonlinear random variable preserve in a conditional expectation operator. Also, we consider results, which follows from Proposition for a generic absolute continuous random variable and discrete random variable. Further, the paper studies a derivative preserving condition for a positive homogeneous random variable. Finally, we consider conditions, which preserve higher order derivatives of the Expected Shortfall for a generic nonlinear portfolio loss random variable.
