Max Noether Theorem for Singular Curves
Edson Martins Gagliardi, Renato Vidal Martins

Abstract
. Max Noether’s Theorem asserts that if ω \omega is the dualizing sheaf of a nonsingular nonhyperelliptic projective curve, then the natural morphisms Sym n ​ H 0 ​ ( ω ) → H 0 ​ ( ω n ) \text{Sym}^{n}H^{0}(\omega)\to H^{0}(\omega^{n}) are surjective for all n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 . The result was extended for Gorenstein curves by many different authors in distinct ways. More recently, it was proved for curves with projectively normal canonical models, and curves whose non-Gorenstein points are bibranch at worse. Based on those works, we address the combinatorics of the general case and extend the result for any integral curve.



Introduction

In late century XIX, Max Noether proved in
[
29
]
a celebrated result establishing that every canonical curve is projectively normal. Consequently, one computes the space dimension of quadrics containing the curve to be
(g-2)(g-3)/2
where
g
is its genus, which is one of the versions by which his result is now known. The theorem was important for subsequent works by Enriques
[
18
]
and Babbage
[
4
]
, characterizing canonical curves which are not intersections of quadrics, by Petri
[
30
]
for a complete description of the canonical ideal in terms of its equations and by several authors in a similar vein, see for instance
[
3
,
28
,
33
,
36
,
39
]
.

From
[
2
, pp. 113-117]
, one sees that the result is a consequence of the canonical curves being extremal, i.e., they reach the Castelnuovo’s bound, and these are projectively normal. So it’s actually possible to extend the argument
verbatin
to Gorenstein singular curves, those for which the dualizing sheaf is a bundle, and a canonical morphism well defined.

For arbitrary integral curves, the problem is more subtle as the very notion of a canonical curve seems not to be so precise. In
[
32
]
, Rosenlicht generalizes this concept as follows: for a curve
C
with normalization
\overline{C}
, its
canonical model

C^{\prime}
is the image of the morphism
\overline{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
induced by the global sections of its dualizing sheaf. He proves that
C^{\prime}\cong C
if and only if the latter is non-hyperelliptic and Gorenstein, a result reobtained later on by distinct authors in many different ways, see for instance,
[
11
,
21
,
23
,
27
,
34
]
.

In
[
25
]
, S. L. Kleiman, along with the second named author proved that if
C
is non-Gorenstein, then
C^{\prime}
is projectively normal if and only if it is extremal, and characterized curves which are so, called
nearly Gorenstein
.
Highly based on this work, in
[
26
]
, Noether’s result is extended to those curves. In the same article, the general problem is addressed purely intrinsically, and the result proved for curves whose non-Gorenstein points are unibranch at worst. Later on, Feital in
[
19
]
, and along with Contiero and the second named author in
[
12
]
, led the study to an arbitrary number of branches and proved Noether’s statement in bibranch case.

This is the point of departure of this work. In fact, in
[
26
]
, Noether’s assertion was proved up to a combinatorial local result,

[
26
, Lem. 3.2]
(corresponding to our Lemma
2.1
), obtained just for unibranch singularities. In
[
12
]
, this local result was rephrased and proved in the bibranch case
[
12
, Lem. 3.2]
by means of a new combinatorial result for two-dimensional semigroups
[
12
, Lem. 3.1]
. The strategy here is essentially skip this step and address the combinatorial problem within another approach, which happens to be somewhat simpler and more general. Indeed, it works for an arbitrary number of branches, and for a general singularity, which need not to be non-Gorenstein, as in
[
12
,
26
]
. So we get the following result, proved in Section
2
, which is Noether’s statement in its full generality.

Theorem
.

Let
C
be a nonhyperelliptic integral and complete curve over an algebraic closed ground field, with dualizing sheaf
\omega
. Then maps

(1)

\mbox{Sym}^{n}H^{0}(C,\omega)\longrightarrow H^{0}(C,\omega^{n})

are surjective for every
n\geq 1
.

Afterwards, in Section
3
,
we complete equivalences already stated in
[
12
]
which connect Noether’s assertion with Rosenlicht’s result and Green’s conjecture at level zero, and which happen to be a multivalent characterization of non-hyperelliticity. Finally, in Section
4
, we discuss another possible via to get Noether’s statement linking the problem with a local theory developed by V. Barucci, M. D’Anna and R. Fröberg in
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

1.1.
Linear Systems and Canonical Models

For the remainder,
C
is an integral curve, complete over an algebraically closed ground field
k
, of arithmetic genus
g
, with structure sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{C}
, or simply
\mathcal{O}
.

A
linear system of dimension
r
and degree
d
on
C
is a formal pair
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F},V)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a torsion free sheaf of rank
1
on
C
,
V
is a vector subspace of

H^{0}(\mathcal{F})
with dimension
r+1
and
d:=\deg\mathcal{F}:=\chi(\mathcal{F})-\chi(\mathcal{O})
, where
\chi
is the Euler characteristic. The notation
g_{d}^{r}
stands for a linear system of degree
d
and dimension
r
. The linear system is said to be
complete
if
V=H^{0}(\mathcal{F})
, in this case one simply writes
\mathcal{L}=|\mathcal{F}|
.
When
\mathcal{F}
is a line bundle, then
\mathcal{L}
determines a morphism
C\to\mathbb{P}^{d}
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{L}
defines a pencil with
non-removable
base points, in the sense of
[
14
,
31
]
.

A curve
C
is said to be
hyperelliptic
if there is a morphism
C\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
2
or, equivalently, if it carries a base point free
g_{2}^{1}
. Now let
\omega
be dualizing sheaf of
C
, recall that a point
P\in C
is
Gorenstein
if the stalk
\omega_{P}
is a free
\mathcal{O}_{P}
-module. The curve
C
is said to be
Gorenstein
if all of its points are so, or equivalently, if
\omega
is invertible. When so, the series
|\omega|
induces a morphism
\kappa:C\to\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
. If, additionally,
C
is nonhyperelliptic then
\kappa(C)
is often referred as a
canonical curve
.

The latter notion can be extended as we will see right away. Before that, we just recall some helpful notation. Given a sheaf
\mathscr{G}
on
C
, if
\varphi:X\to C
is a morphism from a scheme
X
to
C
, one sets

\mathcal{O}_{X}\mathscr{G}:=\varphi^{*}\mathscr{G}/\rm{Torsion}(\varphi^{*}\mathscr{G})

and for each coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
C
one sets

\mathcal{F}^{n}:=\rm Sym^{n}\mathcal{F}/\rm Torsion(\rm{Sym}^{n}\mathcal{F}).

In particular,
if
\mathcal{F}
is invertible then clearly
\mathcal{F}^{n}=\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
.

Now let
\overline{C}
be the normalization of
C
and
\pi:\overline{C}\rightarrow C
the natural map. The linear
system
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O}_{\overline{C}}\omega,H^{0}(\omega))
induces a morphism
\psi:\overline{C}\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{g-1}
. Its image
C^{\prime}:=\psi(\overline{C})
is the
canonical model
of
C
, introduced by Rosenlicht. He proved in
[
32
, Thm. 17]
that if
C
is nonhyperelliptic, then there is a birational morphism

\pi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\rightarrow C

of which existence we will refer in Section
3
as
Rosenlicht’s statement
. In
[
25
]
one finds another characterization of
C^{\prime}
as follows. Let
\widehat{C}:=\text{Proj}(\oplus\,\omega^{n})
the
canonical blowup
of
C
, that is, the blowup of
C
along its dualizing sheaf
\omega
. Rosenlicht’s result easily implies the existence of a birational morphism
\widehat{C}\to C^{\prime}
, but as
\omega
is generated by global sections, then we actually have
C^{\prime}\cong\widehat{C}
.

Now we study the maps above from a local perspective. First, let
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
,
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
and
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
be the direct images on
C
of the structure sheaves of
\overline{C}
,
\widehat{C}
and
C^{\prime}
respectively,
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{H}\rm{om}(\overline{\mathcal{O}},\mathcal{O})

the conductor of
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
into
\mathcal{O}
, and set
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\omega:=\overline{\pi}_{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\overline{C}}\omega)
. Given
P\in C
, take
\lambda\in H^{0}(\omega)
such that
(\overline{\mathcal{O}}\omega)_{P}=\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\lambda
as in
[
25
, Lem. 2.8]
.
Let

\mathcal{W}:=\omega/\lambda

be the embedding of
\omega
on the constant sheaf of rational functions of
C
by means of
\lambda
.
By
[
25
, Lem. 6.1]
we have the following sequence of inclusions

(2)

\mathcal{C}_{P}\subset\mathcal{O}_{P}\subset\mathcal{W}_{p}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{P}=\mathcal{O}_{P}^{\prime}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}.

1.2.
Semigroups

Let
k(C)
be the field of rational functions of
C
. Given
P\in C
, let
\overline{P}_{1},\ldots,\overline{P}_{s}
be the points of
\overline{C}
over
P
, and
v_{\overline{P}_{1}},\ldots,v_{\overline{P}_{s}}
the corresponding valuations. For any
x\in k(C)^{*}
, set

\upsilon_{P}(x)=\upsilon(x):=(v_{\overline{P}_{1}}(x),\cdots,v_{\overline{P}_{s}}(x))\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}.

The
semigroup of values
of
P
is

{\rm S}:=\upsilon(\mathcal{O}_{P}).

Plainly, it is a subsemigroup of
\mathbb{N}^{s}
. Now for an arbitrary
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, let
a_{i}
be its
i
-th component.
We have that
{\rm S}
has the following additional properties

(i)

if
a,b\in\rm S
then
\min(a,b):=(\min(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,\min(a_{s},b_{s}))\in\rm S
;

(ii)

if
a,b\in\rm S
and
a_{i}=b_{i}
then there exists
\varepsilon\in\rm S
such that

\varepsilon_{i}>a_{i}=b_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{j}\geq{\rm min}(a_{j},b_{j})
(and equality holds if
a_{j}\neq b_{j}
);

(iii)

there is
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
such that
\beta+\mathbb{N}^{s}\subseteq{\rm S}
.

Properties (i)-(iii) make
{\rm S}
a
good semigroup
in the sense of
[
7
]
. It is also a
local semigroup
according to the same reference. As
0
is the only element in
{\rm S}
with a vanishing component, the term makes sense. Indeed, one may easily extend the above notion of semigroup of values to a semilocal ring
R
with
k\subset R\subset k(C)
just considering the
\overline{P}_{i}
’s as the points of
\overline{C}
such that
R\subset\mathcal{O}_{\overline{P}_{i}}
,
\forall i
. Then
R
is local if and only if
v(R)
is so.

For the remainder, we set

\alpha:={\min}({\rm S}\setminus\{0\})

which agrees with the mutiplicity of
P
, that is
\alpha=\dim(\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}/\mathfrak{m}_{P}\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P})
. On the other hand, the smallest element in
\mathbb{N}^{s}
satisfying (iii) is the
conductor
of
{\rm S}
, denoted by
\beta
as well. The relation with the conductor ideal is clear as

\beta={\min}(v(\mathcal{C}_{P}))

and the
Frobenius vector
of
\rm S
is
\gamma:=\beta-(1,\ldots,1)
.

A
relative ideal
is any
{\rm E}\subset{\rm S}
such that
{\rm S}+{\rm E}\subset{\rm E}
and (iii) holds for
{\rm E}
. If, moreover (i) and (ii) hold as well, then
{\rm E}
is said a
good
relative ideal. The point here is that for any
\mathcal{O}_{P}
-fractional ideal, its set of values is a good relative ideal of
{\rm S}
.

Now given
E
, one sets

\Delta^{{\rm E}}(a):=\{b\in{\rm E}\mid b_{i}=a_{i}\hskip 3.99994pt\mbox{for some }i\hskip 3.99994pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 3.99994ptb_{j}>a_{j}\mbox{ if }j\neq i\}.

The
canonical ideal
of
{\rm S}
is

(3)

{\rm K}:=\{a\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}\ |\ \Delta^{\rm S}(\gamma-a)=\emptyset\}.

It is a good relative ideal of
{\rm S}
and plays a crucial role here. The term makes sense as well as we will see later on (cf. Equation
10
).

Finally, we fix a notation used throughout this text. Given
a:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}
we denote

|a|:=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}.

Now take local parameters
t_{i}
for the
\mathcal{O}_{\overline{P}_{i}}
and set

(4)

t^{a}:=t_{1}^{a_{1}}\ldots t_{s}^{a_{s}}.

For instance, if so, one may write the conductor ideal as
\mathcal{C}_{P}=t^{\beta}\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}
.

2.
Proof of the Theorem

In this section we prove Max Noether’s statement for an arbitrary integral curve, that is, we get the result stated in the Introduction. As we said above,
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
and
[
12
]
lead to partial generalizations of Noether’s property. The main tool was the study of how global sections of the dualizing sheaf interact with each singularity, which gets harder as the number of branches grows. In this sense,
[
26
]
solves the problem extrinsically and when singularities are unibranch, while
[
12
]
extends the analysis to the bibranch case. Thus all the global (and even part of the local) analysis are already contained in those works.

So the work here will be essentially a task of extracting a small part of
[
12
]
and grafting in a new one. We recall all results needed from the prior papers, and develop the proof within the following steps.

Step 1: Global Part.
First off, follow the whole proof of
[
26
, Thm. 3.7]

make no sense if

is not unibranch; (b) the proof relies in four other results, namely
[
26

To solve (a), first, one just have to allow
\epsilon
be a vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and make

be as in (
4

[
26
, Lem. 3.2]
, which refers to this notation will be rephrased right below in Lemma
2.1
(as well as it was done in
[
12
, Lemma 3.2]
).

To start solving (b), first note that
[
26

hold no matter the number of branches is. On the other hand,
[
26
, Lem. 3.6]
could perfectly had been stated for a multibranch

, as long as it is at least triple. Indeed, in the proof, the point

, rather than

To finish (b), note that the unibranch assumption in

over

and force the function

3.
Characterizing Non-Hyperelliptic Curves

In this section we connect Noether, Rosenlicht and Green statements. The result we prove stands for a characerization of non-hyperellipticity. It appears in
[
12
, Thm. 4.1]
, but part of the equivalences relies on bibranch hypothesis. In order to state it, we recall a few concepts.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a torsion-free sheaf of rank 1 on
C
. Following
[
5
, p. 363 Dfn. 2.2 (7)]
, the
Clifford index
of
C
is

{\rm Cliff}(C)=\min\{\deg\,\mathcal{F}-2(h^{0}(\mathcal{F})-1)\,;\,h^{0}(\mathcal{F})\geq 2\ {\rm{e}}\ h^{1}(\mathcal{F})\geq 2\}.

Now following
[
1
,
22
]
, consider the complex

(16)

\wedge^{p+1}H^{0}(\mathcal{F})\otimes H^{0}(\mathcal{F}^{q-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi_{p,q}^{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\wedge^{p}H^{0}(\mathcal{F})\otimes H^{0}(\mathcal{F}^{q})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi_{p,q}^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\wedge^{p-1}H^{0}(\mathcal{F})\otimes H^{0}(\mathcal{F}^{q+1});

the quotient

K_{p,q}(C,\mathcal{F}):=\ker(\phi_{p,q}^{2})/{\rm im}(\phi_{p,q}^{1})

is the
(p,q)
-th Koszul cohomology
of
\mathcal{F}
. The approach here, as in
[
12
]
, allows torsion free sheaves on both definitions, and not just bundles as in the mentioned refrences.

Now
Green’s conjecture
links both concepts in the following statement
[
22
]
:

K_{p,2}(C,\omega)=0\Longleftrightarrow p<{\rm Cliff}(C).

It was proved for smooth curves in general by C. Voisin in
[
40
,
41
]
while in
[
6
,
20
]
, e.g., one can find such a study for classes of possibly singular curves. It is well known that, for regular curves, the conjecture at level
p=0
is essentially Max Noether statement, while level
p=1
corresponds to Enriques-Babbage
[
18
,
4
]
celebrated characterization of trigonal curves.

Now let us carefully look at the
p=0
case, assuming the curve might be singular. Taking
q=2
and
\mathcal{F}=\omega
in (
16
) we get

H^{0}(\omega)\otimes H^{0}(\omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi_{0,2}^{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{0}(\omega^{2})\longrightarrow 0

Thus
K_{0,2}=0
is equivalent to saying that
\phi_{0,2}^{1}
is surjective, that is, Noether holds for
n=2
. But we just saw in the previous section that once Noether’s statement holds for
n=2
one easily derives all other surjections. On the other hand, in
[
17
, App]
it is proved that
{\rm Cliff}(C)=0
if and only if
C
is either hyperelliptic or rational with a unique singularity whose maximal ideal agrees with the conductor. Curves with such a property appear in the literature in many different ways. They are called
curves defined by a module
in Serre’s
[
35
]
,
partitional
in Behnke-Christophersen

[
10
]
,
universal
in Steven
[
37
]
, and
nearly normal
in Kleiman-Martins
[
25
]
, the term we adopt below, and where is given a characterization of which in the rational case. So these curves happen to be the key ingredient to adjust Green’s conjecture to the integral case, as stated in the last two items of the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

C
is non-hyperelliptic;

(ii)

Rosenlicht’s statement holds: there is a birational morphism
C^{\prime}\to C
;

(iii)

Noether’s statement holds:
{\rm Sym}^{n}H^{0}(\omega)\to H^{0}(\omega^{n})
is surjective
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iv)

K_{0.2}(C,\omega)=0

(v)

{\rm Cliff}(C)>0
or
C
is rational nearly normal.

.

The previous discussion yields the equivalences (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv) and (iv)
\Leftrightarrow
(v). Now if
C
is hyperelliptic then
C^{\prime}
is the rational normal curve of degree
g-1
in
\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
, and in particular is not birationally equivalent to
C
, thus (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i); on the other hand, (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is Rosenlicht’s Theorem
[
32
, Thm. 17]
, so (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii). Also, if
C
is hyperelliptic, then
\dim(H^{0}(\omega)^{2})=2g-1
owing to
[
38
, p. 95 bot]
, while
h^{0}(\omega^{2})=3g-3
due to
[
26
, Lem. 3.4]
and Riemann-Roch, thus (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i); now (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is our main theorem, so (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii), and we are done with the proof of the above result. We just recall here
[
26
, Rmk. 2.10]
where is discussed a way of getting (ii) by means of (iii). So since we got Noether’s statement in this work, we also get an alternative way of proving Rosenlicht’s statement.

4.
Another Local Approach

In this section we briefly discuss a different way of addressing Noether’s result. It connects the subject with some local commutative algebra developed by Barucci, D’Anna and Fröberg in
[
9
,
7
,
8
]
. At the end we get the theorem for curves with bibranch non-Gorenstein points at worse, as in
[
12
]
; to go further, one needs
[
8
, Thm. 4.6, Cor. 4.7]
for an arbitrary number of branches. The gain is linking the problem with some alternative local theory in a way that Noether’s assertion can be derived from an independent local statement. Also, this approach congregates both intrisic and extrinsic points of view. Indeed, the extrinsic proof of Noether in
[
26
, Thm. 2.7]
is used here to lead us to a local, and hence intrinsic, problem of rings named almost Gorenstein, as we will see.

We start by adjusting
[
8
]
to our framework. The reader can check that all results mentioned here work perfectly to any local ring of a point in a curve.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
P\in C
be any point. Given
I,J
fractional ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{P}
, set

(I:J):=\{a\in k(C)\mid aJ\subseteq I\}

which agrees with the conductor
\mathcal{C}_{P}
when
I=\mathcal{O}_{P}
and
J=\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}
. Following
[
8
, Chp. 4]

P
is called

maximal with fixed conductor
if for every ring
T
such that

\mathcal{O}_{P}\varsubsetneq T\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}
,
we have a strict inclusion
(\mathcal{O}_{P}:\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P})\subsetneq\,(T:\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P})
.

And now we recall a couple of local properties with a strong geometric appeal as we will see right away.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
P\in C
be any point. Set

\eta_{P}:=\dim(\mathcal{W}_{P}/\mathcal{O}_{P})\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mu_{P}:=\dim({\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}/\mathcal{W}_{P})

and also

\eta:=\sum_{P\in C}\eta_{P}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mu:=\sum_{P\in C}\mu_{P}

Following
[
9
, pp. 418, 433, Prps. 21, 28]
call
P

Kunz
if
\eta_{P}=1
and,
accordingly, say
C
is
Kunz
if all of its non-Gorenstein points are Kunz. Following
[
25
, Dfn. 5.7]
call
C

nearly Gorenstein
if
\mu=1
.

The key fact that links both definitions is
[
8
, Thm. 4.6, Cor. 4.7]
from which we get that if
P
is bibranch at worst, then it is maximal with fixed conductor if and only if it is Gorenstein or Kunz. Moreover, if
P
is Kunz then it is almost Gorenstein owing to
[
9
, Prp. 21]
. On the other hand,
[
26
, Thm. 2.7]
provides a total geometric and extrinsic proof of Noether’s statement to nearly Gorenstein curves. The reason why is that such curves are precisely the ones for which the canonical model
C^{\prime}
is projectively normal.

So, for the remainder, we will merge the facts above to get a third proof of Noether if the singularities are bibranch at worse. But the reader should note that we are actually proving something else, i.e., assume maximal with fixed conductor implies almost Gorenstein, then Noether holds.

Let
P\in C
be a non-Gorenstein point, and
C^{\#}
be the curve obtained by resolving all non-Gorenstein singularities of
C
distinct from
P
. We have a birational morphism
\pi^{\#}:C^{\#}\to C
. Let
\omega^{\#}
be the dualizing sheaf of
C^{\#}
. Set
\mathcal{O}^{\#}=\pi^{\#}_{\ast}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\#}})
and
\mathcal{W}^{\#}:=\pi^{\#}_{\ast}(\omega^{\#})/\lambda
,
where
\lambda
, as before, is such that
(\overline{\mathcal{O}}\omega)_{P}=\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}\lambda
. Thus

(17)

H^{0}(\mathcal{W}^{\#})\subset H^{0}(\mathcal{W}).

Also, by
[
25
, Lems. 2.8 and 6.1]
, we have that
\mathcal{W}^{\#}_{P}
is determined by the conductor of the local ring. But, in this case,
\mathcal{O}^{\#}_{P}=\mathcal{O}_{P}
by construction, and hence

(18)

\mathcal{W}^{\#}_{P}=\mathcal{W}_{P}.

So we have the following morphisms

(19)

H^{0}(\mathcal{W})^{n}\supset H^{0}(\mathcal{W}^{\#})^{n}\longrightarrow(\mathcal{W}_{P}^{\#})^{n}/\penalty t^{-\epsilon}\mathcal{C}^{n}_{P}=\mathcal{W}_{P}^{n}/\penalty t^{-\epsilon}\mathcal{C}^{n}_{P}

where
\epsilon
is as in the statement of Lemma
2.1
.

So now we will prove this lemma by a different argument if
P
is bibranch at worst. First off, from the proof of
[
26
, Thm. 3.7]
one sees that Lemma
2.1
, besides sufficient, is also necessary to get
H^{0}(\mathcal{W})^{n}=H^{0}(\mathcal{W}^{n})
. So assume
P
is almost Gorenstein. Then
C^{\#}
is nearly Gorenstein and satisfies Noether statement owing to
[
26
, Tmh. 2.9]
. Therefore, the maps in (
19
) are surjective, which implies that the maps
H^{0}(\mathcal{W})^{n}\to\mathcal{W}_{P}^{n}/\penalty t^{-\epsilon}\mathcal{C}^{n}_{P}
are surjective as well.

Now take any
P\in C
. If
P
is maximal with fixed conductor, then it is almost Gorenstein and we are done, from what was said above. Otherwise there is and intermmediate ring
\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
such that

\mathcal{O}_{P}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}

and also

\mathcal{C}_{P}^{*}:=(\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}:\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P})=(\mathcal{O}_{P}:\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P})=\mathcal{C}_{P}.

Then exclusively either:

(i)

\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
is local and maximal with fixed conductor;

(ii)

\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
is not local;

(iii)

\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
is local, but not maximal with fixed conductor.

If (i) holds, consider the partial dessingularization
\phi:C^{*}\to C
such that:

\phi^{-1}(P)=\{P^{*}\}
with
\phi_{*}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{*},P^{*}})=\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
and such that
C^{*}\setminus\{P^{*}\}\cong C\setminus\{P\}
. Then
P^{*}
is almost Gorenstein since
\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*}
is maximal with fixed conductor; thus
C*
is nearly Gorenstein, and we get the lemma for
C^{*}
and
P^{*}
. But as
\mathcal{C}_{P}^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{P}
we can apply exactly what was said above replacing
C^{\#}
by
C^{*}
to get the lemma for
C
and
P
as well.

If (ii) holds, then
\upsilon_{P}(\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*})
has a nonzero element with a vanishing component. On the other hand, by
[
8
, Lem 4.1.]
we have
\upsilon_{P}(\mathcal{O}_{P}^{*})\subset{\rm K}
; and, in particular,
{\rm K}
has such an element and this case is trivial as noted in the proof of
[
12
, Lem. 3.1]
. And if (iii) holds, consider
C^{*}
and
P^{*}
as defined in (i). Replace
C
by
C^{*}
,
P
by
P^{*}
and restart the procedure, which naturally ends as
\dim(\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{P}/\mathcal{O}_{P})<\infty
.
