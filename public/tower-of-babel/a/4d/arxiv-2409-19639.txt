Zeros of planar Ising models via flat SU(2) connections
Marcin Lis

Abstract
. Livine and Bonzom recently proposed a geometric formula for a certain set of complex zeros of the partition function of the Ising model defined on planar graphs [ 3 ] . Remarkably, the zeros depend locally on the geometry of an immersion of the graph in the three dimensional Euclidean space (different immersions give rise to different zeros). When restricted to the flat case, the weights become the critical weights on circle patterns [ 15 ] . We rigorously prove the formula by geometrically constructing a null eigenvector of the Kac–Ward matrix whose determinant is the squared partition function. The main ingredient of the proof is the realisation that the associated Kac–Ward transition matrix gives rise to an SU ( 2 ) (2) connection on the graph, creating a direct link with rotations in three dimensions. The existence of a null eigenvector turns out to be equivalent to this connection being flat.

The study of zeros of the partition function of the Ising model has a rich history going back to the seminal work of Lee and Yang
[
13

[

20

These results are valid irrespectively of the underlying finite graph.

On the other hand the zeros in the coupling constans, also called
Fisher zeros
, are less understood (see
[
19
]
and the discussion therein).
In their recent work, Livine and Bonzom
[
3
]
, using nonrigorous methods, proposed a parametrisation of a certain class of Fisher zeros for Ising models
defined on planar graphs. Surprisingly the parametrisation concerns the geometry of embeddings (or as it turns out immersions) of the graph into the three dimensional Euclidean space. To the best of our knowledge no such
geometric formula had been proposed before. When the embedding is locally flat,
then locally the weights become the critical Ising weights on circle patters that were introduced by the author in
[
15
]

In this note we give a rigorous proof of the formula of Livine and Bonzom.

Let

Theorem 1.1

(Livine–Bonzom zeros)
.

In the setup as above we have

Z_{G^{*}}(y)=0.

Our approach (unlike the derivation of
[
3
]
that uses a relation to spin networks
[
1
]
) makes use of the Kac–Ward matrix
[
12
]
(or more precisely its conjugated version studied in
[
17
,
16
,
18
,
15
]
).
Its determinant is equal to the partition function squared, and hence to prove Theorem
1.1
it is enough to construct a vector in its kernel.
We do it by a suitable change of coordinates of the eigenvector which reveals a structure of an SU(2) connection on
G^{*}
. The existence of the null eigenvector turns out to be equivalent to
this connection being flat, i.e. having trivial holonomy around every loop.
Due to the relation between SU(2) and rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, this has a geometric interpretation in terms of the existence of an immersion of the graph.
As a byproduct we establish that the space of eigenvectors has two complex dimensions.
We give the details in the remainder of this note.

Remark 1
.

As described in
[
3
]
the result extends readily (with properly generalised weights) to immersions of graphs
G
where the dual
G^{*}
has higher degrees of vertices than three.
In that case one requires that the immersion maps the vertices lying around each face of
G
not only to a single plane but moreover to a single circle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(which for three vertices is the same condition).
If the immersion is locally flat (meaning that the angles
\vartheta
are locally zero) this corresponds to circle patterns with the critical weights from
[
15
]
.

Remark 2
.

As mentioned in
[
3
]
the dimension of the set of zeros given by formula (
1.1
) is half of the full dimension of the set of all zeros of
Z_{G^{*}}(x)
. It is natural to expect that a larger class of zeros
will be given by an appropriate three dimensional generalisation of the critical weights of the Ising model on
s-embeddings
defined by Chelkak
[
6
,
7
]
which in the flat case generalise the critical weights on circle patterns.

Remark 3
.

If
G^{*}
has the topology of a torus or a surface of higher genus, then the determinant of an associated Kac–Ward matrix is known to be equal to the square of the partition function of even subgraphs
counted
with additional signs depending on the homology class of the even subgraph
[
8
]
. Our proof shows that with the weights defined in the same way as above, this partition function also vanishes.

Remark 4
.

Lastly, we want to mention that as the planar Ising model is a special case of the dimer model
[
9
,
4
,
10
]
, this work also motivates the question of finding three dimensional geometric descriptions of zeros of the partition function of
the dimer model itself.

The Kac–Ward matrix

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})

For two adjacent vertices

We fix a

For two directed edges

\displaystyle\angle(v_{1}v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}v_{2}^{\prime})=\arg\Big(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{v_{2}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{v_{2}}}{\mathbf{z}_{v_{1}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{v_{1}}}\Big)\in(-\pi,\pi]

(see Figure

\displaystyle x_{\{v,v^{\prime}\}}=\vec{x}_{vv^{\prime}}\vec{x}_{v^{\prime}v}.

The

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}}(\vec{x})]_{v_{1}v^{\prime}_{1},v_{2}v^{\prime}_{2}}=\begin{cases}\vec{x}_{v^{\prime}_{1}v_{1}}\vec{x}_{v_{2}v^{\prime}_{2}}e^{\frac{i}{2}\angle(v_{1}v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}v_{2}^{\prime})}&\text{if }v_{1}^{\prime}=v_{2},\\

]

Theorem 2.1
.

\displaystyle{\det}(\mathrm{Id}-\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}}(\vec{x}))=Z_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}(x),

where

In particular the determinant is independent of a particular choice of the embedding

, and it depends on

It immediately follows that

if and only if there exists

such that

.
The rest of this note is devoted to the construction of such a vector in the (slightly modified) setting of Theorem
1.1
.

2.2.
A graph modification and planar decompositions

A change of coordinates and an SU(2) connection

Lemma 2.2

\displaystyle\rho^{2}_{uu^{\prime}}={|\mathbf{z}_{uv_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{uv_{2}}|}\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad e^{i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}}=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{uv_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{uv_{2}}}{{|\mathbf{z}_{uv_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{uv_{2}}|}}.

\tilde{\Lambda}
is block diagonal, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in U^{\dagger}}\|\varphi^{w}\|^{2}=\|\varphi\|^{2}=\|\tilde{\Lambda}\varphi\|^{2}=\sum_{w\in U^{\dagger}}\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}\varphi^{w}\|^{2},

where
\varphi^{w}
is the restriction of
\varphi
to the subspace indexed by
\text{Out}_{w}
, and
\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}\|
is the Euclidean operator norm of
\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}
.
Since the blocks
\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}
are Hermitian and their operator norm is equal to the spectral radius. If
w\in U
, then by Lemma
2.2
, the spectral radius of
\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}
is one.
On the other hand if
w\in\vec{E}^{*}
, then by (
2.2
) and (
2.1
),
\tilde{\Lambda}^{w}
is a
2\times 2
antidiagonal matrix with entries of modulus one, and hence also of spectral radius one.
We conclude that if
\Lambda\varphi=\varphi
, then for all
u\in U
,
\|\varphi^{u}\|=\|\tilde{\Lambda}\varphi^{u}\|
and hence by Lemma
2.2
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{uw}=\varphi^{u}_{uw}=\xi_{u}^{+}\varphi^{u,+}_{uw}+\xi_{u}^{-}\varphi^{u,-}_{uw}

for some
\xi_{u}^{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
.
We therefore also have

\displaystyle\varphi_{wu}=\Lambda\varphi_{wu}=J\tilde{\Lambda}^{u}\varphi^{u}_{wu}=J(\xi_{u}^{+}\varphi^{u,+}_{wu}-\xi_{u}^{-}\varphi^{u,-}_{wu})=\xi_{u}^{+}\varphi^{u,+}_{uw}-\xi_{u}^{-}\varphi^{u,-}_{uw}.

This in particular means that the new coordinates
\xi_{u}=(\xi_{u}^{+},\xi_{u}^{-})
,
u\in U
, fully determine the eigenvector
\varphi
around each
u\in U
, which in turn
determines the full eigenvector.

We now want to see how
\xi^{u}
and
\xi^{u^{\prime}}
are related
for neighbouring
{u,u^{\prime}\in U}
.
To this end, we note that when propagating the value
\varphi_{(u^{\prime}u)u^{\prime}}
to
\varphi_{u(uu^{\prime})}
(resp.
\varphi_{(uu^{\prime})u}
to
\varphi_{u^{\prime}(u^{\prime}u)}
) by applying
\Lambda
(left application of
\Lambda
moves us backwards along the directed path from
u
to
u^{\prime}
), one picks up the phase
e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}+\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})}
(resp.
e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}-\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})}
), and hence

\displaystyle\varphi_{u(uu^{\prime})}=\varphi_{(u^{\prime}u)u^{\prime}}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}+\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})}\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad\varphi_{u^{\prime}(u^{\prime}u)}=\varphi_{(uu^{\prime})u}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}-\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})}.

This together with the two displayed equations above and the explicit form of the eigenvectors from Lemma
2.2
give (after factoring out
\rho_{uu^{\prime}}
from both equations)

\displaystyle\xi_{u^{\prime}}^{+}-\xi_{u^{\prime}}^{-}e^{-i\beta_{u^{\prime}u}}

\displaystyle=(\xi_{u}^{+}+\xi_{u}^{-}e^{-i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}})e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(-\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}-\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})},

\displaystyle\xi_{u^{\prime}}^{+}+\xi_{u^{\prime}}^{-}e^{-i\beta_{u^{\prime}u}}

\displaystyle=(\xi_{u}^{+}-\xi_{u}^{-}e^{-i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}})e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}-\alpha_{uu^{\prime}})},

which in matrix form reads

\xi_{u^{\prime}}=\xi_{u}\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}},

where

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&ie^{i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}}\sin(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\\
-ie^{-i\beta_{u^{\prime}u}}\sin(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&-e^{i(\beta_{u^{\prime}u}-\beta_{uu^{\prime}})}\cos(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}}\cos(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&-ie^{\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}+i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}}\sin(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\\
-ie^{-\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}-i\beta_{uu^{\prime}}}\sin(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&e^{\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}}\cos(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\end{pmatrix}.

In the second identity we used (
2.3
). On can see that
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}\in\textnormal{SU}(2)
as it is of the form

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Upsilon=\begin{pmatrix}a+bi&c+di\\
-c+di&a-bi\end{pmatrix}

with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
and
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}=1
. By construction,
\Upsilon_{u^{\prime}u}=\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}^{-1}
, and therefore the matrices
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}
,
uu^{\prime}\in\vec{E}^{*}
, form an
\textnormal{SU}(2)
-connection on
G^{*}
.
If this connection is
flat
, meaning that the product of the matrices along any closed directed loop in
G^{*}
(called the
holonomy
of this loop) is the identity matrix, then one can
consistently recover all
\xi_{u}
from any fixed
\xi_{u_{0}}
. To prove Theorem
1.1
it is therefore enough
to show that
\{\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}\}_{uu^{\prime}\in\vec{E}^{*}}
is a flat connection.

2.4.
Euler angles and rotations in 3D

To this end, we will need a geometric interpretation of
\textnormal{SU}(2)
matrices.
Recall that the
Euler angles

\phi
and
\psi
associated with
U
as in (
2.4
) are given by

\psi+\phi=2\arg a\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\psi-\phi=2\arg b.

A computation yields that for
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}
, the Euler angles are

\psi_{uu^{\prime}}=\beta_{uu^{\prime}}-\frac{\pi}{2}\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\phi_{uu^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}-\beta_{uu^{\prime}}=\frac{3\pi}{2}-\beta_{u^{\prime}u},

where we again used (
2.3
). Hence we can decompose into elemental
\textnormal{SU}(2)
matrices:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{i}{2}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}+\phi_{uu^{\prime}})}\cos(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&e^{\frac{i}{2}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}-\phi_{uu^{\prime}})}\sin(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\\
-e^{-\frac{i}{2}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}-\phi_{uu^{\prime}})}\sin(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&e^{-\frac{i}{2}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}+\phi_{uu^{\prime}})}\cos(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\psi_{uu^{\prime}}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-i\frac{\psi_{uu^{\prime}}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&\sin(\frac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\\
-\sin(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})&\cos(\tfrac{\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}}{2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi_{uu^{\prime}}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-i\frac{\phi_{uu^{\prime}}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}

(2.5)

\displaystyle=:\Upsilon_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})\Upsilon_{x}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})\Upsilon_{z}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}}).

The notation
\Upsilon_{x}(\gamma)
and
\Upsilon_{z}(\gamma)
refers to rotations around the
x
and
z
axis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by an angle
\gamma
.
To justify it, we need to recall the connection between
\textnormal{SU}(2)
and
\textnormal{SO}(3)
– the group of rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We chose to use the representation of
\textnormal{SU}(2)
as the unit
quaternions
, i.e.
each
\Upsilon
as in (
2.4
) is associated with the quaternion
q_{\Upsilon}=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
. With the standard rules of quaternion multiplication
(
\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=\mathbf{k}^{2}=\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=-1
,
\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{k}\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}
)
the map
\Upsilon\mapsto q_{\Upsilon}
is a group isomorphism
between
\textnormal{SU}(2)
and quaternions
q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
of unit norm, i.e. such that
\|q\|^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}=1
.
Recall that
\bar{q}=a-b\mathbf{i}-c\mathbf{j}-d\mathbf{k}
and
q\bar{q}=\bar{q}q=\|q\|^{2}
. We call a quaternion
q

pure
if
\operatorname{Re}q:=\tfrac{1}{2}(q+\bar{q})=0
. The set of pure quaternions can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the map

b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}\mapsto(d,c,b)
(we chose this particular order for convenience so that
\mathbf{i}
corresponds to the
z
coordinate and
\mathbf{k}
to the
x
coordinate in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
). Each unit quaternion
q
can be written as

\displaystyle q=\cos\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)+\sin\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)p

where
p
is a pure unit quaternion. It is classical that if
p_{0}
is another pure quaternion, then

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{0}=qp_{0}q^{-1}

is the pure quaternion resulting from the rotation of
p_{0}
around the axis given by
p
by an angle of
\gamma
(note the doubling of the angle). Here the rotation is clockwise when looking in the direction of
p
.
This map
q\mapsto R_{p}(\gamma)
, where
R_{p}(\gamma)\in\textnormal{SO}(3)
is the corresponding rotation matrix, is a 2-to-1 group homomorphism from
\textnormal{SU}(2)
to
\textnormal{SO}(3)
.
The multiplicity of this map comes from the fact that
-q
gives rise to the same rotation as
q
. This will be enough knowledge about
\textnormal{SU}(2)
to finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
In particular, this justifies the notation from (
2.5
).

Before proceeding we still need to recall one classical fact, this time about rotations themselves.
Namely, a sequence of rotations around the coordinate axes of a fixed reference frame (
extrinsic rotations
) is equivalent to the reverse sequence of the same rotations but this time taken around the
corresponding axes of the moving (rotating) frame (
intrinsic rotations
). This follows from the fact that the change of basis operation is realised by matrix conjugation (here the change of basis matrices are the rotations themselves).
By (
2.5
),
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}
corresponds to the rotation resulting from the composition
R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})R_{x}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})R_{z}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})
,
where
x
(resp.
z
) stands for
e_{1}
(resp.
e_{3}
) in the standard frame
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
. Assuming the initial frame agrees with the standard frame, this corresponds to the composition

(2.6)

\displaystyle R_{z^{\prime\prime}}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})R_{x^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}),

where
x^{\prime}=R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})e_{1}
and
z^{\prime\prime}=R_{x^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})e_{3}
, are the
x
and
z
axes of the rotating frame after one and two rotations respectively.
This interpretation will be very convenient to show that the connection
\{\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}\}_{uu^{\prime}\in\vec{G}^{*}}
is flat. Before doing this, we will need one more definition.

2.5.
Framed immersions and flatness of the connection

Figure 6.
From left to right: the folding of a net (a special case of a flat decomposition) of a tetrahedron together with a constant vector field (red vectors) on the faces into a framed immersion of the tetrahedron.
To match our conventions, the bottom plane on which the net lies should be seen upside down so that the black vectors point upwards.
The red vectors in the plane are in our case taken to be
i
, and they get mapped to the
\mathbf{r}_{u}
vectors by the folding

Recall that for a face
u=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
,
\mathbf{p}_{u}
is the plane on which
\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{3}}
lie.
A
framed immersion
is an oriented immersion
\mathbf{x}
together with a choice of a unit vector
\mathbf{r}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
parallel to
\mathbf{p}_{u}
for each face
u\in U

(see Fig
6
for an illustration).

We now describe how to obtain a framed immersion associated with a planar decomposition. Before reading the formal definition the reader may look at Fig.
6
which
visualises this procedure readily in the case of the tetrahedron. To be more formal, consider a planar decomposition
\mathbf{z}
of an immersion
\mathbf{x}
. For each
u=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}\in U
, let

\iota_{u}
be the isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that maps
\mathbf{T}^{\mathbf{z}}_{u}
to
\mathbf{T}^{\mathbf{x}}_{u}
, and such that
\iota_{u}(\mathbf{z}_{u}+(0,0,1))-\iota_{u}(\mathbf{z}_{u})=\mathbf{n}_{u}
.
Here we still think of
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the
x-y
plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then we simply define
\mathbf{r}_{u}=\iota_{u}(i)
, where the complex unit
i
is identified with
(0,1,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In this setup we now make the following claims (that are visualised in Fig.
7
).
Assume without loss of generality that the initial frame is
(\mathbf{r}_{u},\mathbf{n}_{u}\times\mathbf{r}_{u},\mathbf{n}_{u})=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
(we will only care about
(\mathbf{r}_{u},\mathbf{n}_{u})
as the third axis is determined)
and consider the sequence of intrinsic rotations from (
2.6
):

\displaystyle R_{z^{\prime\prime}}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})R_{x^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}}).

We have that

•

R_{z}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})=R_{\mathbf{n}_{u}}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})
maps
(\mathbf{r}_{u},\mathbf{n}_{u})
to
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}_{u})
, where

\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}}|}.

Indeed

\displaystyle\psi_{uu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{uu^{\prime}}-\frac{\pi}{2}=\arg\Big(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{uv_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{uv_{2}}}{i}\Big)

is the rotation angle from
i
to
\mathbf{z}_{uv_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{uv_{2}}
, which by construction is the same as the rotation angle from
\mathbf{r}_{u}
to
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
.

•

R_{x^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})=R_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})=R_{\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})
maps
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}_{u})
to
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}})

by the definition of the angle
\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}}
.

•

R_{z^{\prime\prime}}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})=R_{\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}}}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})
maps
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{u},\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}})
to
(\mathbf{r}_{u^{\prime}},\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}})
. Indeed

\displaystyle\phi_{uu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\pi+\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta_{u^{\prime}u}=\pi+\arg\Big(\frac{i}{\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{2}}-\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{1}}}\Big)=\arg\Big(\frac{i}{\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{2}}}\Big)

is the rotation angle from
\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{1}}-\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}v_{2}}
to
i
, which by construction is the same as the rotation angle from
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
to
\mathbf{r}_{u^{\prime}}
.

Figure 7.
Two adjacent faces in a framed immersion obtained from a planar decomposition.
The composition of three intrinsic rotations
R_{x^{\prime\prime}}(\phi_{uu^{\prime}})R_{z^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{uu^{\prime}})R_{x}(\psi_{uu^{\prime}})
rotates the frame
(\mathbf{n}_{u},\mathbf{r}_{u})
to the frame
(\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}},\mathbf{r}_{u^{\prime}})
by the sequence of transformations

(\mathbf{n}_{u},\mathbf{r}_{u})\to(\mathbf{n}_{u},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to(\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to(\mathbf{n}_{u^{\prime}},\mathbf{r}_{u^{\prime}})
. The intermediate vector
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{u}
is equal to
(\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}})/|\mathbf{x}_{v_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{v_{2}}|

This means that
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}
acts by rotating the frame at
u
to the frame at
u^{\prime}
. Hence if we follow a closed loop starting at
u
and apply
\Upsilon
iteratively, the frame at
u
will be mapped to itself.
However, since the homomorphism from
\textnormal{SU}(2)
to
\textnormal{SO}(3)
is 2-to-1 it is still possible that the holonomy of the this loop is equal to minus the identity matrix (which also corresponds to the trivial rotation).

We will now argue why this is not the case. It is enough to show that the holonomy of elementary loops defining the faces of
G^{*}
is the identity (loops going around a single vertex
v
of
G
).
To this end note that in the flat case, i.e. when all the
\vartheta
angles along the loop are zero, a planar decomposition can be chosen so that the paths between
uu^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}u
have zero winding
\alpha_{uu^{\prime}}
(indeed they are trivial paths if
\mathbf{z}_{uu^{\prime}}=\mathbf{z}_{u^{\prime}u}
, or can be chosen to make two opposite right turns). In that case by (
2.3
) we have
e^{\beta_{uu^{\prime}}-\beta_{u^{\prime}u}}=-1
, and hence
e^{\psi_{uu^{\prime}}+\varphi_{u^{\prime}u}}=1
, which in turn
implies that
\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}=\mathrm{Id}
for all edges
uu^{\prime}
along the loop. In particular the product over the loop is the identity. One can now modify the picture by
continuously changing the angles
\vartheta
and
\varphi
around
v
. The holonomy along the loop is clearly a
continuous function of all the angles (as seen in (
2.5
)), and hence it cannot discontinuously change from
\mathrm{Id}
to
-\mathrm{Id}
. This ends the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 5
.

As mentioned in the introduction, from the proof it follows that any choice of
\xi_{u_{0}}
for some fixed
u_{0}\in U
gives rise to an eigenvector of
\Lambda
with eigenvalue one
(by propagating
\xi_{u_{0}}
to any other face by applications of
\Upsilon
). It also follows that any such eigenvector gives rise to such
\xi_{u_{0}}
, and hence the kernel of
\mathrm{Id}-\Lambda
has
two complex dimensions corresponding to the two coordinates of
\xi_{u_{0}}
.

Remark 6
.

The collection
\{\xi_{u}\}_{u\in U}
can be seen as a field of two-component
spinors
acted upon by the
\textnormal{SU}(2)
connection
\{\Upsilon_{uu^{\prime}}\}_{uu^{\prime}\in\vec{E}^{*}}
.
Each such field represents a null eigenvector of
\mathrm{Id}-\Lambda
.
