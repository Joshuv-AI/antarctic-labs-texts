Chern classes of tropical manifolds
Lucía López de Medrano, Felipe Rincón, Kris Shaw

Abstract
. We extend the definitions of Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson (CSM) cycles of matroids to tropical manifolds. To do this, we provide an alternate description of CSM cycles of matroids which is invariant under integer affine transformations. Utilising results of Esterov and Katz-Stapledon, we prove correspondence theorems for the CSM classes of tropicalisations of subvarieties of toric varieties. We also provide an adjunction formula relating the CSM cycles of a tropical manifold and a codimension-one tropical submanifold. Lastly, we establish Noether’s Formula for compact tropical surfaces with a Delzant face structure. This extends the class of surfaces for which the formula had been previously proved by the third author.



1.
Introduction

The Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson (CSM) cycles of a matroid
M
are a collection of tropical cycles introduced in
[
LdMRS20
]
.
When the matroid is realisable by a hyperplane arrangement in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
its CSM cycles encode the CSM classes of the complement of the arrangement inside its
wonderful compactification.

CSM cycles of matroids geometrically encode many properties of the matroid.
Their degrees, for example, are the coefficients of the shifted reduced characteristic
polynomial;
this was proved via deletion-contraction in
[
LdMRS20
]
, and later by
[
AB23
]
using a refined basis activities expansion of the Tutte polynomial.
The fact that the coefficients of the shifted reduced characteristic polynomial of a matroid have this tropical-geometric interpretation was used in

[
ADH23
]
to prove the log-concavity
of this sequence.
CSM cycles of matroids have also been related to tautological classes of matroids in
[
BEST23
]
.

In this paper we extend the definition of CSM cycles of matroids to (smooth) tropical manifolds,
and we give evidence that they behave like Chern classes of tangent bundles.
Tropical manifolds are topological spaces
equipped with an atlas of charts to Bergman fans of matroids
and transition maps that are integer affine maps;
see Definition
2.3
for more details.
Examples of tropical manifolds include tropical curves, tropical linear spaces,
non-singular tropical hypersurfaces, and integral affine manifolds; see Section
2.3
.

The CSM cycles of a matroid
M
are Minkowski weights supported on the different skeleta of the Bergman fan of
M
.
The weights assigned to the faces of the skeleta are given by products of beta invariants of matroid minors of
M
; see Definition
3.1
.
A priori, this means that the CSM cycles of
M
depend heavily on
M
.
There are examples of non-isomorphic matroids whose corresponding Bergman fans have the same support up to integer linear maps; see, for example, Proposition
3.7
.
As a first step in extending the definition of CSM cycles to tropical manifolds, we prove in Proposition
3.3
that the definition of CSM cycles of matroids
is
{\rm GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
invariant.
For this purpose, we show that the weights of the CSM cycles can be equivalently determined from the cosheaves arising in tropical homology
[
IKMZ
]
.

In Section
4
, we provide results that relate the CSM classes of algebraic varieties with the CSM cycles of their tropicalisations.
We first show that the
0
-th CSM cycle of the tropicalisation of a family of subvarieties of a toric variety recovers the topological Euler characteristic of a general member of the family.

Theorem
4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a meromorphic family of subvarieties of a toric variety
\mathcal{Y}
over the punctured disk
\mathcal{D}^{*}
, with general member
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
. Suppose that
\trop(\mathcal{X})=X
is a tropical submanifold in
Y=\trop(\mathcal{Y})
. Then

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\deg(\csm_{0}(X)),

where
\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})
denotes the topological Euler characteristic.

To prove the above theorem, we combine the correspondence results for CSM cycles of matroid fans from
[
LdMRS20
]
and
the description of the tropical motivic nearby fibre of Katz and Stapledon
[
KS12
]
.

In the case
\mathcal{Y}
is a non-singular projective toric variety, we relate the CSM cycles of
X=\trop(\mathcal{X})
to the CSM classes of a general member
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
of the family
{\mathcal{X}}
.
To do this, we combine the above theorem and the work of Esterov
[
Est18
]
, and relate the CSM class of
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
in the Chow ring of
\mathcal{Y}
to the CSM cycles of the tropicalisation of
{\mathcal{X}}
, as we now explain.

The Chow cohomology ring of a non-singular projective toric variety
\mathcal{Y}
defined by a fan
\Sigma
is isomorphic to the ring of Minkowski weights supported on
\Sigma

[
FS97
]
. We denote this graded ring by
\MW_{\ast}(\Sigma)
.
The group of Minkowski weights of dimension
k
is denoted
\MW_{k}(\Sigma)
, and is isomorphic to
\Hom({\mathcal{A}}_{n-k}(\mathcal{Y}),{\mathbb{Z}})
, where
n
is the dimension of
\mathcal{Y}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{n-k}(\mathcal{Y})
is the
(n-k)
-graded piece of the Chow group of
\mathcal{Y}
.
By Poincaré duality, taking the cap product with the fundamental class of
\mathcal{Y}
provides an isomorphism

\cdot\frown[\mathcal{Y}]\colon\MW_{k}(\Sigma)\xrightarrow{\,\,\cong\,\,}{\mathcal{A}}_{k}(\mathcal{Y}).

If
\mathcal{X}
is transverse to the toric boundary of
\mathcal{Y}
then the recession fan of
X
is supported on the fan
\Sigma
. Following
[
AHR16
]
, by taking the recession cycles
\rec(\csm_{k}(X))
of
\csm_{k}(X)
(see Definition
4.2
), we thus obtain a collection of Minkowski weights on
\Sigma
.

Theorem
4.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a meromorphic family of subvarieties of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{N}
over the punctured disk
\mathcal{D}^{*}
, with general member
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
.
Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a non-singular projective toric variety
with defining fan
\Sigma
, and let
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
be the closure of
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{D}^{*}
.
Suppose that
\overline{X}:=\trop(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
is a tropical manifold transverse to
\trop(\mathcal{Y})
. Then

\csm_{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{X}_{t}})\,=\,\rec(\csm_{k}(X))\frown[\mathcal{Y}]\,\,\in{\mathcal{A}}_{k}(\mathcal{Y}).

Theorems
4.1
and
4.4
generalize
[
LdMRS20
, Theorem 3.1]
, which relates the CSM class of the complement of a hyperplane arrangement in the Chow ring of its wonderful compactification
to the CSM cycles of the corresponding matroid.

In previous work,
Bertrand and Bihan equipped the skeleta of tropical complete intersections in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with integer weights
to produce balanced tropical cycles
[
BB13
]
.
In their construction, their weights are, up to sign, related to the Euler characteristic of a non-degenerate complete intersection
in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}

[
BB13
, Theorem 5.9]
.
The situation they consider overlaps with our own when the tropical complete intersections are also tropical manifolds. In the hypersurface case, this is equivalent to the dual subdivision being unimodular.

In Section
5
, we give a product formula for CSM cycles of matroids, and use it to prove an adjunction-like formula relating the codimension-1 CSM cycles of a tropical manifold and a submanifold of codimension 1. This generalises the adjunction formula for curves in tropical surfaces proved in
[
Sha
, Theorem 6]
.

Theorem
5.2

(Adjunction formula)
.

Let
X
be a tropical manifold of dimension
d
and
D\subset X
a tropical submanifold of codimension 1 in
X
.
Then

\csm_{d-2}(D)=(\csm_{d-1}(X)-D)\cdot D,

where
\cdot
denotes the tropical intersection product in
X
.

The notion of a tropical submanifold is more subtle than just being a tropical manifold contained in a tropical manifold; see Definition
2.14
. In Example
2.15
we recall a situation where being a tropical manifold of contained in
X
and having codimension 1 is not sufficient for Theorem
5.2
.

In the case
X
is a 2-dimensional compact tropical manifold, we prove a tropical version of Noether’s Formula expressing its topological Euler characteristic
\chi(X)
in terms of its CSM cycles and their intersections. This is proven under the assumption that
X
admits a Delzant face structure, i.e., a cell structure where every cell locally looks like a polytope in which the primitive vectors of the edges adjacent to any vertex can be extended to a basis of the ambient lattice; see Definition
6.1
.

Theorem
6.3

(Tropical Noether’s Formula)
.

Let
X
be a compact tropical surface admitting a Delzant face structure.
Then

\chi(X)=\frac{\deg(\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2})}{12}.

Noether’s formula had previously been proven for tropical surfaces arising from tropical toric surfaces via the operations of tropical modifications and summation
[
Sha
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Tropical manifolds

To define tropical manifolds we first introduce a partial compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
used in tropical geometry. Let
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and equip it with the topology whose basis consists of intervals
(a,b)
and
[-\infty,b)
where
a<b
. The real numbers
\mathbb{R}
will be equipped with the Euclidean topology. The spaces
\mathbb{T}^{r}
and
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
are equipped with the product topologies. All subsets of
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
are equipped with the subspace topologies.

The space
\mathbb{T}^{r}
is a stratified, where the strata are

\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\colon x_{i}=-\infty\text{ iff }i\in I\}

for

I\subset[n]
.
Given a stratum
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}
, we call
I
its
sedentarity
, and we say a point
x\in\mathbb{T}^{r}
is of sedentarity
I
if and only if it is in
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}
. Notice that we have

\mathbb{T}^{r}=\bigsqcup_{I\subset[r]}\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}

and
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}
can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{r-|I|}
.

We assume the reader is familiar with the definitions of tropical cycles in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, including weight functions and the balancing condition, and refer to
[
MS15
]
and
[
MR
]
for more background.
A
tropical cycle of sedentarity
I
in
\mathbb{T}^{r}
is the topological closure of a tropical cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}\cong\mathbb{R}^{r-|I|}
.
Notice that we do not ask for a cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}
of sedentarity
I
to satisfy a balancing condition on any new codimension-1 faces in the closure.
A tropical cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}
is a formal sum of tropical cycles of different sedentarities.

We can also extend the notion of sedentarity to spaces of the form
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A tropical cycle of sedentarity
I
is the closure in
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A
k
-dimensional tropical cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}
is again a formal sum of
k
-dimensional cycles of different sedentarities.

Example 2.1
.

[
Matroidal tropical cycles.
]
Denote by
\{e_{0},e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\}
the standard basis of the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
For any subset
S\subseteq\{0,\dotsc,n\}
, let
e_{S}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in S}e_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}
.
We denote
\mathbf{1}:=(1,1,\dots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
.

Suppose
M
is a loopless matroid of rank
d+1
on the ground set
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
The affine Bergman fan
\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}(M)
of
M

is the pure
(d+1)
-dimensional rational polyhedral fan in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
consisting of the collection of cones of the form

\sigma_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq-\cone(e_{F_{1}},e_{F_{2}},\dotsc,e_{F_{k}})+{\mathbb{R}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}

where

\mathcal{F}=\{\emptyset\subsetneq F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\dotsb\subsetneq F_{k}\subsetneq\{0,\dotsc,n\}\}
is a chain of flats in the lattice of flats
{\mathcal{L}}(M)
of
M
.
If
M
is a matroid with loops then we define
\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}(M)=\emptyset
.
The (projective) Bergman fan
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
of
M

is the pure
d
-dimensional rational polyhedral fan obtained as the image of
\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}(M)

in the quotient vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,{\mathbb{R}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}
.

The
matroidal tropical cycle

Z_{M}
associated to
M
is the tropical cycle in the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,{\mathbb{R}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose support is the projective Bergman fan

{\mathcal{B}}(M)
and all multiplicities on the maximal cones are equal to
1
.
Note that the product
X=\mathbb{T}^{r}\times Z_{M}
is a tropical cycle in
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
of sedentarity
\emptyset
— indeed,
X
is the closure of the matroidal tropical cycle
Z_{C_{r}\oplus M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
C_{r}
is the free matroid on
r
elements (i.e., having no circuits).

\diamondsuit

Spaces of the form
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times Z
for
Z
a matroidal cycle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be the local building blocks of tropical manifolds. Before we give the precise definition we must also define the notion of extended integer affine maps. Recall that an integer affine map
F:\mathbb{R}^{r^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the composition of an integer linear map with a translation defined over
\mathbb{R}
, that is, a map of the form
F(x)=Ax+b
with
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times r^{\prime}}
and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{r^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
be an integer affine map and let
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times r^{\prime}}
denote the integer matrix representing the linear part of
F
. Let
I
be the set of

i\in[r^{\prime}]
such that the
i
-th column of
A
has only non-negative
entries.
Then
F
can be extended to a map

\hat{F}\colon\left(\bigcup\limits_{J\subset I}\mathbb{T}^{r^{\prime}}_{J}\right)\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}}^{r}

by continuity.
The restriction of such an
\hat{F}
to an open subset
U^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{T}}^{r^{\prime}}
is called an
extended integer affine map
. Note that this only makes sense if we have
\text{sed}(x)\subset I
for all
x\in U^{\prime}
.

We are now ready to define tropical manifolds. These will be topological spaces with an atlas of charts to the supports of matroid fans and extended integer affine linear transition maps.
We follow the definitions from
[
MR
, Section 7]
for abstract tropical cycles and polyhedral spaces,
and collect the necessary properties from
[
MR
, Definition 7.4.1]
in the definition below.

Definition 2.3
.

A
d
-dimensional
tropical manifold
is a Hausdorff topological space
X
equipped with an atlas of charts
\{\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\rightarrow\Omega_{\alpha}\subset X_{\alpha}\}_{{\alpha}\in\mathcal{I}}
where
\mathcal{I}
is a finite set
and

(1)

X_{\alpha}=\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}\times Y_{\alpha}
with
Y_{\alpha}
a matroidal tropical cycle of dimension
d-r_{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}
and
\Omega_{\alpha}
is an open subset of
X_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
,

(2)

for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
the map
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\rightarrow\Omega_{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism,

(3)

the transition functions
\varphi_{\beta}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}
are extended integer affine maps,

(4)

for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
there exists an extension
\varphi_{\alpha}^{\prime}\colon U_{\alpha}^{\prime}\rightarrow\Omega_{\alpha}^{\prime}\subset X_{\alpha}
of
\varphi_{\alpha}
such that
\overline{\Omega}_{\alpha}\subset\Omega_{\alpha}^{\prime}
.

2.2.
The boundary of a tropical manifold

Points of the stratified space
\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
have an order of sedentarity defined by

s(x):=|\{x_{i}:x_{i}=-\infty\}|.

The order of sedentarity is preserved under invertible extended integer affine maps and hence this notion extends to tropical manifolds, as long as we assume that the chart chosen contains points of sedentarity
0
in their image.

Definition 2.4
.

For a point
x\in X
of a tropical manifold, the
order of sedentarity
of
x
is
s(x)=|s(\phi_{\alpha}(x))|
, where
\phi_{\alpha}:U_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is any chart satisfying
x\in U_{\alpha}
and with
\phi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})
containing points of sedentarity
0
.
The
boundary
of a tropical manifold
X
is

\partial X=\{x\in X:s(x)>0\}.

A
boundary divisor
of a tropical manifold
X
is
the closure of a connected component of the set
\{x\in X:s(x)=1\}
.
Every boundary divisor is of codimension 1 in
X
and is itself a tropical manifold;
[
Sha
, Proposition 1.2.8]
. Call the set of boundary divisors
{\mathcal{A}}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\}
the
arrangement of boundary divisors
of
X
.

For a tropical manifold
X
we denote the complement of its boundary by
X_{0}=X\backslash\partial X
. This is the collection of points of
X
with order of sedentarity
0
.
Note that
X_{0}
is also a tropical manifold and that
X
is the closure of
X_{0}
.
Each connected component of
\{x\in X:s(x)=k\}
is a tropical manifold of dimension
\dim X-k
, and so is its closure in
X
.

2.3.
Examples of tropical manifolds

Example 2.5
.

[
Abstract tropical curves.
] An abstract tropical curve is a
1
-dimensional finite simplicial complex equipped with a metric on the complement of its 1-valent vertices. Abstract tropical curves are exactly
1
-dimensional tropical manifolds, since in dimension
1
the metric is equivalent to an integer affine structure. Indeed, each point of a tropical curve comes equipped with a chart to a matroidal fan in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a loopless matroid of rank
2
or to a neighbourhood of
-\infty\in\mathbb{T}
.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.6
.

[
Integral affine manifolds.
]
An integral affine manifold is a manifold equipped with an atlas of charts such that the transition functions are affine transformations with linear part defined by an integral matrix.
An integer affine manifold is thus a tropical manifold where the tropical charts from Definition
2.3
satisfy
X_{\alpha}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
\alpha
. In dimension 2 the topological type of a compact integer affine manifold without boundary is either
S^{1}\times S^{1}
or the Klein bottle.

Tropical Abelian varieties are integer affine manifolds obtained as quotients
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Lambda
, where
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a full-rank sublattice satisfying certain conditions
[
MZ08
]
. An
n
-dimensional tropical Abelian variety is homeomorphic to
(S^{1})^{n}
.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.7
.

[
Tropical toric varieties.
]
Let
\Sigma
be a rational polyhedral fan in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
As a topological space, the associated tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
can be described as a quotient of the disjoint union of tropical tori
U_{\sigma}=\mathbb{T}^{\dim(\sigma)}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-\dim(\sigma)},
ranging over all cones
\sigma\in\Sigma

[
MR
, Definition 3.2.3]
.
From this description we obtain an atlas of charts
\{U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha},X_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in I}
where
I
is in bijection with the top dimensional cones of
\Sigma
and the charts
\phi_{\alpha}:U_{\alpha}\to\Omega_{\alpha}\subset X_{\alpha}
satisfy
X_{\alpha}=\mathbb{T}^{N}
for all
\alpha\in I
.
The tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
is a tropical manifold if and only if
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a unimodular fan, see
[
MR
, Section 3.2]
. The tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
is compact if and only if
\Sigma
is complete.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.8
.

[
Tropical projective space.
]
Projective space is a toric variety and
tropical projective space can be constructed using the same fan as projective space over a field. It can also be described as the following quotient:

\mathbb{TP}^{n}=\frac{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\backslash(-\infty,\dots,-\infty)}{(x_{0}:\dots:x_{n})\sim(a+x_{0}:\dots:a+x_{n})},

where
a\in\mathbb{T}\backslash\{-\infty\}
.
From the quotient description we obtain tropical homogeneous coordinates on
\mathbb{TP}^{n}
which we write as
[x_{0}:\dots:x_{n}]
.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.9
.

[
Tropical manifolds in tropical toric varieties
]
Let
\hat{X}
be a pure dimensional polyhedral complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The support
X=|\hat{X}|
is a tropical manifold if for every face
\tau\in\hat{X}
, the star fan
{\rm star}_{\tau}(\Sigma)
of
X
at
\tau
is the support of a Bergman fan of some matroid
M_{\tau}
up to an integer affine transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Suppose in addition that the collection of recession cones of all the cones
\tau\in\hat{X}
forms a fan
\Sigma
, called the recession fan of
\hat{X}
.
Let
\overline{X}
denote the closure of
X
in the tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
.
Then
\overline{X}
is a compact space and is the canonical compactification of
X
from
[
KSW
]
,
[
AP
]
. Moreover, the space
\overline{X}
is a tropical manifold in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.10
.

[
Tropical linear spaces.
]
A tropical Plücker vector is a vector
p\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\binom{[n+1]}{d+1}}

satisfying the tropical Plücker relations:
For every
A\in\binom{[n+1]}{d+2}
and
B\in\binom{[n+1]}{d}
, the maximum
\max_{i\in A\setminus B}(p_{A\setminus i}+p_{B\cup i})
is attained twice.
Any tropical Plücker vector
p
gives rise to a tropical linear space
\textstyle L(p)
defined as

\textstyle L(p):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,{\mathbb{R}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}:\max_{i\in S}(p_{S-i}+x_{i})\text{ is achieved twice for any }S\in\binom{[n+1]}{d+2}\}.

Tropical linear spaces are
d
-dimensional tropical manifolds: around any
x\in\textstyle L(p)
,
the tropical linear space
\textstyle L(p)
looks like a matroidal fan
[
Spe08
]
.

We can consider the closure of a tropical linear space in different tropical toric varieties. For example, the closure of
L(p)
in
\mathbb{TP}^{n}
is a tropical projective subspace; however, the closure
\overline{L(p)}
does not necessarily intersect the boundary of
\mathbb{TP}^{n}
transversely, and
\overline{L(p)}
will not be a tropical manifold in the sense of Definition
2.3
.
Nonetheless, the closure of
L(p)
in the tropical toric variety of
\Sigma=\rec(L)
where
\rec(L)
denotes the recession fan of
L
is a tropical manifold in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

\diamondsuit

Example 2.11
.

[
Non-singular tropical hypersurfaces.
]
A tropical hypersurface
X_{f}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the divisor
\text{div}_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(f)
of a tropical regular function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, see
[
MS15
]
,
[
Mik06
]
. It is a weighted polyhedral complex dual to a regular subdivision of the Newton polytope of
f
. If the dual subdivision is unimodular, meaning each polytope in the subdivision is a simplex with normalised volume equal to
1
, the hypersurface is called non-singular. Non-singular hypersurfaces locally look like matroidal tropical cycles of corank-
1
matroids, and are thus tropical manifolds.

Let
\Sigma
be the dual fan of the Newton polytope of
f
.
The recession fan of
X_{f}
is the codimension-1 skeleton of
\Sigma
. As in Example
2.9
, we can compactify
X_{f}
in
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
. If the dual fan
\Sigma
is unimodular then
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
and
\overline{X_{f}}
are both compact tropical manifolds in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

\diamondsuit

2.4.
Cycles in tropical manifolds

Here we recall the definitions of cycles in tropical manifolds from
[
Sha13
]
and
[
MR
]
.
As previously stated, a tropical cycle
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
is a formal sum of cycles
A=\sum_{I\subset[r]}A_{I}
where
A_{I}
is the closure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
of a tropical cycle in

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{r}_{I}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{r-|I|}
.

Definition 2.12
.

A
tropical
k
-cycle

Z
in a tropical manifold
X
is a subset
Z\subset X
equipped with a weight function
w:\Omega\to{\mathbb{Z}}
on an open dense subset
\Omega\subset Z
such that, for all charts
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\to Y_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}
of
X
, the image
\varphi_{\alpha}(Z\cap U_{\alpha})
is a tropical
k
-cycle in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}
with the weight function induced from the weight function on
Z\cap U_{\alpha}
.

Definition 2.13
.

A tropical
k
-cycle
A
of a tropical manifold
X
is
transverse to the boundary
of
X
if for every boundary stratum
D_{I}:=\bigcap_{i\in I}D_{i}
of
X
and for every chart
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\to Y_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}
, the image
\varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap A\cap D_{I})
is of codimension
|I|
in
\varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap A)
or it is empty.

The adjunction formula for tropical CSM cycles in Section
5
concerns the intersection of divisors in a tropical manifold.
Similarly, Noether’s Formula in Section
6
talks about the
intersection of
1
-cycles in tropical surfaces.
There are various approaches to intersection theory in tropical manifolds
[
Sha11
]
,
[
FR13
]
, all of them equivalent for our purposes.
For completeness, we now recall the theory of intersecting with tropical Cartier divisors.

A
tropical Cartier divisor
on a tropical manifold
X
equipped with charts
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}
is a collection of tropical rational functions
\{f_{\alpha}\}
where

f_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}\to\mathbb{TP}^{1}=[-\infty,\infty]
and such that
on the overlaps
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}
the difference
f_{\alpha}-f_{\beta}
is a
bounded integer affine function on
X_{\alpha}
, implying in particular that
f_{\alpha}-f_{\beta}
does not attain the values
\pm\infty
.
Every codimension-
1
tropical cycle
D
in a tropical manifold is a Cartier divisor
[
Sha13
, Lemma 2.23]
,
meaning that
there exists a tropical Cartier divisor
\{f_{\alpha}\}
such that
\varphi_{\alpha}(D\cap U_{\alpha})=\Div_{X_{\alpha}}(f_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha
.

Suppose
X
is a tropical manifold without boundary.
Then for
D
a codimension-
1
tropical cycle in
X
and
A
a tropical
k
-cycle in
X
, we can define the intersection of
D
and
A
by first expressing
D
as a Cartier divisor
f=\{f_{\alpha}\}
, and then setting

D\cdot A=\Div_{A}(f),

where
\Div_{A}(f)
is the tropical cycle which in each chart
U_{\alpha}
is equal to
\Div_{A\cap U_{\alpha}}(f_{\alpha})
.
The above recipe also works in the case
X
has a boundary and
D
does not contain any boundary divisors.

When the codimension-
1
cycle
D
contains components which are boundary divisors it is possible that some of the functions
\{f_{\alpha}\}
are identically equal to
\pm\infty
on
A
. This is the case, for example, when we wish to consider
D^{2}
for a boundary divisor
D
. In this situation we use the theory of tropical line bundles from
[
JSS19
]
. The charts of
X
restricted to a neighbourhood of
D
in
X
defines a line bundle on
D

[
Sha11
]
.
The Cartier divisor
\{f_{\alpha}\}
defines a tropical line bundle
L\in\Pic(X)=H^{1}(X,\Aff_{\mathbb{Z}})
and
all Cartier divisors rationally equivalent to
f
arise from tropical rational sections of
L
. By
[
JSS19
, Proposition 4.6]
, every tropical line bundle admits a non-zero section
s
. A choice of section produces another codimension-
1
cycle
D^{\prime}
in
X
which is rationally equivalent to
D
and is a sedentarity
0
cycle. This cycle can then be intersected with
A
, so we define
D\cdot A:=D^{\prime}\cdot A
.
Notice that in this case the intersection product is only well defined up to rational equivalence, since
D^{\prime}
depends on the choice of section of
L
.

2.5.
Tropical submanifolds

For a tropical subvariety to behave as a submanifold of a tropical manifold we require a compatibility condition on the corresponding charts, as described below.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
X
be a tropical manifold and let
W
be a tropical cycle in
X
of dimension
k
which has constant weight function equal to
1
.
Then
W
is a
tropical submanifold
of
X
if
there is an atlas of charts
\{U_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\}
for
X

such that
\{(U_{\alpha}\cap W),\varphi_{\alpha}|_{W}\}
is also an atlas of charts for
W
.

Note that, in particular, if the atlas for
X
has charts
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\to\Omega_{\alpha}\subseteq Y_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}
where
Y_{\alpha}
are matroid fans, then
\varphi_{\alpha}|_{W}\colon U_{\alpha}\cap W\to W_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T}^{r^{\prime}_{\alpha}}
, where
W_{\alpha}
are matroid fans such that
W_{\alpha}\subseteq Y_{\alpha}
and
r^{\prime}_{\alpha}\leq r_{\alpha}
. This implies that the matroid of
W_{\alpha}
must be a matroid quotient of the matroid of
Y_{\alpha}

[
Sha13
, Lemma 2.21]
,
[
FR13
, Proposition 3.3]
. Example
2.15
shows a pair of tropical manifolds
W
and
X
with
W\subset X
which violates this condition on the compatibility of the charts and hence
W
is not a submanifold of
X
in the sense of Definition
2.14
.

We say that
W
is a sedentarity-
0
submanifold of
X
if
r^{\prime}_{\alpha}=r_{\alpha}
in all charts
\varphi_{\alpha}
of
X
. By the condition imposed on the compatibility of the charts, a sedentarity-
0
submanifold of
X
is necessarily transverse to the boundary of
X
in the sense of Definition
2.13
when considered as a tropical cycle of
X
.

Example 2.15
.

As we have mentioned before, being a tropical submanifold depends on a compatibility condition on the charts.
For instance, there exist tropical surfaces
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of degree
\geq 3
which contain tropical lines
L
not satisfying Definition
2.14
.
These examples were discovered by Vigeland
[
Vig09
]
as infinite families of lines on tropical hypersurfaces.
Both the surface
X
and the line
L
are tropical manifolds;
however, the atlases for
X
and
L
are not compatible with each other. Moreover, these lines in tropical surfaces do not satisfy the adjunction-like formula that we present in Theorem
5.2
; see
[
BS15
, Section 7.3]
.

3.
CSM cycles of tropical manifolds

In this section we show that CSM cycles of matroids are invariant under invertible integer affine transformations of the underlying Bergman fan, which allows us to extend the definition of CSM cycles to tropical manifolds; see Definition
3.4
.
We start by recalling the definition from
[
LdMRS20
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
M
is a loopless rank
d+1
matroid on the ground set
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
For
0\leq k\leq d
, the
k
-dimensional
Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson (CSM) cycle

of
M
, denoted
\csm_{k}(M)
, is the tropical cycle in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
supported on the
k
-dimensional skeleton
of the Bergman fan
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
in which the weight of the top-dimensional cone
\sigma_{\mathcal{F}}
corresponding to a flag of flats
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{\emptyset=F_{0}\subsetneq F_{1}\subsetneq\dotsb\subsetneq F_{k}\subsetneq F_{k+1}=\{0,\dotsc,n\}\}
is

w_{\csm_{k}(M)}(\sigma_{\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq(-1)^{d-k}\prod_{i=0}^{k}\beta(M|F_{i+1}/F_{i}),

where
M|F_{i+1}/F_{i}
denotes the minor of
M
obtained by restricting to
F_{i+1}
and contracting
F_{i}
.
If
M
is a matroid with loops then we define
\csm_{k}(M)\coloneqq\emptyset
for all
k
. If
\Sigma_{M}
is the matroidal fan of
M
, we will also denote
\csm_{k}(\Sigma_{M}):=\csm_{k}(M)
.

The next definition extends the notion of CSM cycles to spaces of the form
X=\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\Sigma_{M}
.
The space
X
is the closure of the matroidal fan of the matroid
C_{r}\oplus M
, where
C_{r}
is the free matroid on
r
elements (i.e., the matroid with no circuits).
Note that for any
I\subset[r]
, the intersection
X\cap(\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is again the support of a matroid fan in
\mathbb{R}^{r+n-|I|}
where the underlying matroid is
C_{r-|I|}\oplus M
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma_{M}
be a matroid fan in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
k
-th Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson cycle of

\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\Sigma_{M}
is

\csm_{k}(\mathbb{T}^{r}\times\Sigma_{M})=\sum_{\emptyset\subset I\subset[r]}\csm_{k}(\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}\times\Sigma_{M}).

The following
proposition, whose proof we provide later in this subsection, shows that our definition of CSM cycles is well-behaved under extended integer affine maps. This allows us to define CSM cycles of general tropical manifolds in Definition
3.4
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
Y^{\prime}
and
Y
be matroidal cycles in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively, and suppose that there is an invertible map
\varphi:Y^{\prime}\to Y
which is induced by an affine linear map
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

\varphi(\csm_{k}(Y^{\prime}))=\csm_{k}(Y)

as tropical cycles.

Since tropical manifolds are defined as spaces with an atlas of charts in which the transition functions are invertible integer affine maps, Proposition
3.3
ensures the following notion of CSM cycles for tropical manifolds is well defined.

Definition 3.4
.

The
k
-th
Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson cycle

\csm_{k}(X)
of a tropical manifold
X
is the tropical cycle supported on
X
such that, in each chart
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
, the image of
\csm_{k}(X)
is
\csm_{k}(X_{\alpha})\cap\varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})
.

Remark 3.5
.

We remark that the CSM cycles of products
\Sigma_{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
, where
\Sigma_{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a matroid fan, are transverse to the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
, and hence CSM cycles of a tropical manifold
X
are transverse to its boundary.

Before proving Proposition
3.3
, we provide an interesting class of examples of non-isomorphic matroids whose matroidal cycles are related by invertible integer linear maps.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be two loopless matroids on the ground sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, respectively, and let
p_{1}\in E_{1}
and
p_{2}\in E_{2}
. The
parallel connection

P(M_{1},M_{2})
of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
at the basepoints
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
is a matroid on the ground set
E:=(E_{1}-p_{1})\sqcup(E_{2}-p_{2})\sqcup\{p\}
. If we make the identification
p_{1}=p_{2}=p
, the flats of

are those

such that

is a flat of
M_{1}
and
F\cap E_{2}
is a flat of
M_{2}
. Equivalently, the circuits of

are the subsets of

such that

is a circuit of
M_{1}
or
M_{2}
, or
C=I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}
with

a circuit of
M_{i}
; see
[
Whi86
, Proposition 7.6.6]
.

In general, the isomorphism class of the parallel connection of two matroids

and
M_{2}
depends on the choice of basepoints. It follows from the next proposition that the matroidal cycles of any two parallel connections of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are related by an invertible integer affine map, even though their underlying matroids might not be isomorphic.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
M
is the parallel connection of the matroids
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
(at any basepoints) then there exists an invertible map

\varphi:Z_{M}\to Z_{M_{1}}\times Z_{M_{2}}
, which is the restriction of an invertible integer linear map between the ambient vector spaces.

Proof.

Let
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
be the ground sets of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, respectively. Suppose
M
is the parallel connection of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
at the base points
p_{1}\in E_{1}
and
p_{2}\in E_{2}
, and let
E:=(E_{1}-p_{1})\sqcup(E_{2}-p_{2})\sqcup\{p\}
be the ground set of
M
. Consider the vector spaces
V_{1}:={\mathbb{R}}^{E_{1}}
,
V_{2}:={\mathbb{R}}^{E_{2}}
, and
V:={\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, and let
\Delta_{1}=(e_{a})_{a\in E_{1}}
,
\Delta_{2}=(e_{b})_{b\in E_{2}}
, and
\Delta=(e_{c})_{c\in E}
denote their standard bases, respectively. Let
\varphi:V/{\mathbb{R}}\cdot e_{E}\to(V_{1}/{\mathbb{R}}\cdot e_{E_{1}})\oplus(V_{2}/{\mathbb{R}}\cdot e_{E_{2}})
be the map defined by
\varphi(\bar{e}_{x}):=\bar{e}_{x}
if

and

. The function

if

\varphi^{-1}(\bar{e}_{p_{i}})=-\bar{e}_{E_{i}-p_{i}}
for

The support of the matroidal cycle

of any matroid

can be described as

\textstyle\bigl|Z_{M}\bigr|=\bigl\{\sum\alpha_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}:\forall\text{ circuits $C$ of $M$, }\min\{\alpha_{i}:i\in C\}\text{ is achieved at least twice}\bigr\},

see
[

.
Using the description of the circuits of

from Definition

if and only if

∎

Conjecture 3.8
.

If
M
and
M^{\prime}
are non-isomorphic matroids and there exists an invertible map
\phi:Z_{M}\to Z_{M^{\prime}}
which is the restriction of an invertible integer linear map between their ambient spaces, then

and

are parallel connections.

We will prove Proposition
3.3
by
giving an alternate description of the weights of the CSM cycles; see Lemma
3.12
.
It will be clear that this description is invariant under invertible integer affine maps.
For this purpose, we make use of the sheaves arising from tropical homology
[
IKMZ
]
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\Sigma
be a pure
d
-dimensional polyhedral fan in an
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
V
.
For
0\leq p\leq d
, define

{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma):=\langle v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{p}:v_{1},\dotsc,v_{p}\text{ are in a common cone }\sigma\in\Sigma\rangle\,\subseteq\,\bigwedge\nolimits^{p}V.

By convention, if
\Sigma\neq\emptyset
then
F_{0}(\Sigma)
is the one-dimensional vector space
\bigwedge\nolimits^{0}V
, and if
\Sigma=\emptyset
then
F_{0}(\Sigma)=0
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Sigma
be a polyhedral fan in an
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
V
. Then the vector spaces
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)
from Definition
3.9
are invariants of the support of
\Sigma
.
Moreover, if
\Sigma^{\prime}
is a polyhedral fan in an
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
V^{\prime}
and
\phi:\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\prime}

is an invertible map induced by a linear map
\hat{\phi}:V\to V^{\prime}
then
\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)=\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for all
p
.

Proof.

To prove that
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)
is an invariant of the support of
\Sigma
, it suffices to show that
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)={\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for
\Sigma^{\prime}
a refinement of
\Sigma
. Given a cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
, let
L(\sigma)
denote its linear span. Note that

{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)=\big\langle\bigwedge^{p}L(\sigma):\sigma\in\Sigma\rangle.

By definition of a refinement, every cone of
\Sigma^{{}^{\prime}}
is contained in a cone of
\Sigma
and thus we have an immediate inclusion
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})\subset{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)
.
On the other hand, given any cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
there must exist a cone
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{{}^{\prime}}
contained in
\sigma
such that
\dim(\sigma^{\prime})=\dim(\sigma)
and thus
L(\sigma^{\prime})=L(\sigma)
.
This provides the inclusion
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)\subset{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})
.

For the second part of the statement,
if
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are linearly isomorphic as described, the isomorphism
\hat{\phi}:V\to V^{\prime}
induces an isomorphism between
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)
and
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})
. In particular, these vector spaces have the same dimension for each

, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 3.11
.

If
\Sigma={\mathcal{B}}(M)

is the Bergman fan of a loopless rank-
(d+1)
matroid

then the polynomial

{\psi}_{\Sigma}(\lambda):=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\,(-1)^{i}\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{i}(\Sigma)\,\lambda^{d-i}

is equal to the reduced characteristic polynomial
\bar{\chi}_{M}(\lambda)
of
M
.

Proof.

Consider the dual vector spaces
{\mathbf{F}}^{p}(\Sigma):={\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)^{*}
. Together, the
{\mathbf{F}}^{p}(\Sigma)
form a graded algebra with the product induced by the wedge product
[
Zha13
, Lemma 2]
. Moreover, this algebra is naturally isomorphic to the Orlik-Solomon algebra of the matroid
M

[
Zha13
, Theorem 4]
. It follows that the dimensions of the graded pieces of this algebra are the coefficients of the reduced characteristic polynomial of
M
, as claimed.
∎

It follows from the above proposition that we can recover the weight of the
0
-th dimensional CSM cycle of
M
from the fan
\Sigma=B(M)
by setting
\lambda=1
in the polynomial
{\psi}_{\Sigma}(\lambda)
.
Our goal is to recover the weights of the faces in
\csm_{k}
in a similar fashion.

If
\Sigma
is a polyhedral fan in a vector space
V
and
\tau
is a cone of
\Sigma
, the

of

\tau
in
\Sigma
is the polyhedral fan
\starr_{\tau}(\Sigma)
consisting of all cones of the form

\tilde{\sigma}:=\{\lambda(x-z):\lambda\geq 0,x\in\sigma,\text{and }z\in\tau\}

for any cone
\sigma
of
\Sigma
containing
\tau
as a face.
The
lineality space
of
\Sigma
is the maximal linear subspace
L
such that

x+L\in|\Sigma|
for all

.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\Sigma={\mathcal{B}}(M)
be the Bergman fan of a loopless matroid

.
For any
k
-dimensional cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
the polynomial
\psi_{{\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma)}}(\lambda)

is divisible by
(\lambda-1)^{k-1}
and

(3.1)

w_{\csm_{k}(M)}(\sigma)=\left.\frac{\psi_{{\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma)}}(\lambda)}{(\lambda-1)^{k-1}}\right|_{\lambda=1}.

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma
is a
k
-dimensional face of
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
corresponding to the chain of flats

\mathcal{F}=\{\emptyset\subsetneq F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\dotsb\subsetneq F_{k}\subsetneq\{0,\dotsc,n\}\}
of

.
The fan
\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma)
is the Bergman fan of the matroid

M_{\sigma}:=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{k}M|F_{i+1}/F_{i}
; see for example
[
MS15
, Corollary 4.4.8]
.
By Proposition
3.11
, the polynomial
\psi_{\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma)}(\lambda)
is the reduced characteristic polynomial of the matroid
M_{\sigma}
.
The characteristic polynomial of
M_{\sigma}
is the product of the characteristic polynomial of its components
M_{i}:=M|F_{i+1}/F_{i}
, hence,

\psi_{\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma)}(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)^{-1}\prod_{i=0}^{k}\chi_{M_{i}}(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)^{k-1}\prod_{i=0}^{k}\bar{\chi}_{M_{i}}(\lambda).

As
\bar{\chi}_{M_{i}}(1)=(-1)^{r(M_{i})-1}\beta(M_{i})
, in view of Definition
3.1
this directly implies the desired result.
∎

We have now all the pieces for the proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

The result follows from Lemma
3.12
together with the second part of Lemma
3.10
, as the dimensions of the vector spaces
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\Sigma)
are invariant under invertible linear maps.
∎

We present the following lemma which relates the CSM cycles of the different strata of
\Sigma_{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
and will be useful in future sections.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\sigma
be a face of a matroid fan
\Sigma_{M}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and consider
the corresponding face
\sigma\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}
in
X:=\Sigma_{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}
. Then for subsets

we have

w_{\csm_{k}(X)}(\sigma\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I})=w_{\csm_{k+|I|-|J|}(X)}(\sigma\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{J}).

Proof.

We may suppose that
\dim(\sigma\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I})=k
otherwise the weights on both sides are
0
.
Let
N
be the matroid corresponding to the matroid fan
\starr_{\sigma}(\Sigma_{M})
.
The underlying matroid for the fan
\starr_{\sigma\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I}}(\Sigma_{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{r}_{I})
is
N\oplus C_{r-|I|}
, where
C_{r-|I|}
is the matroid which is a direct sum of

coloops.
The (non-reduced) characteristic polynomials of matroids are multiplicative under direct summation, so we obtain

\chi_{N\oplus C_{r-|I|}}(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)^{r-|I|}\chi_{N}(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)^{|J|-|I|}\chi_{N\oplus C_{r-|J|}}(\lambda),

since the characteristic polynomial of a single coloop is
\lambda-1
.
Now the formula for the weights of the CSM cycles follows
from their description in Lemma
3.12
and Proposition
3.11
.
∎

One could hope that the recipe for the weights of CSM cycles using the dimensions of the vector spaces

. However, this is not the case, as the next examples show.

Example 3.14

Consider the fan tropical hypersurface

The fan

is a

-dimensional fan consisting of

rays, with primitive

(0,0,-1),(-1,0,0),(0,-1,0),(1,0,1),(0,1,1),

-dimensional faces.
Using the definition of weights from Equation (

) on the

-skeleton of

does not produce a balanced tropical cycle.
If
\sigma
is any of the

therefore, Equation (

From the above list of directions of these rays
we see that constant weights do not produce a balanced

give canonical weights on the

Example 3.16
.

A naive approach to extend the definition of CSM cycles to more general balanced polyhedral fans would be to insist that the additivity property of classical
CSM classes should also hold in the tropical situation.
Concretely, if the indicator function of a balanced polyhedral fan
\Sigma
can be expressed as an integer linear combination of indicator functions of matroidal fans, we could try to define the CSM cycles of
\Sigma
as the corresponding linear combination of the CSM cycles of the matroidal fans.
However, this does not always produce well-defined tropical cycles, as the sum of the corresponding CSM cycles might depend on the chosen decomposition of
\Sigma
.

As an example, consider the
1
-dimensional fan
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with 1 vertex, 6 rays, and support
|\Sigma|=\bigcup_{i=0}^{2}\spann_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{e}_{i})
,
where
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1)
, and
\mathbf{e}_{0}=(-1,-1)
.
The fan
\Sigma
is balanced when equipped with weights equal to
1
on all top dimensional faces.
The indicator function of
\Sigma
can be decomposed as a signed sum of indicator functions of matroidal fans in two different ways.
On the one hand, it decomposes as the sum of
{\mathcal{B}}(U_{2,3})
and
\text{crem}({\mathcal{B}}(U_{2,3}))
minus the origin
\mathbf{p}
, where
\text{crem}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the linear map negating both coordinates.
On the other, it decomposes as the sum of the three lines
{\mathcal{B}}(M_{i}):=\spann_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{e}_{i})

for
i=0,1,2
, minus two times the origin
\mathbf{p}
.

Figure 1.
Two distinct decompositions of a balanced fan as a (signed) sum of matroidal fans.

However, the first decomposition yields
\csm_{0}(U_{2,3})+\text{crem}(\csm_{0}(U_{2,3}))-\csm_{0}(U_{1,3})=(-1-1-1)\mathbf{p}=-3\mathbf{p}
,
while the second yields
\sum_{i=0}^{2}\csm_{0}(M_{i})-2\csm_{0}(U_{1,3})=(0+0+0-2)\mathbf{p}=-2\mathbf{p}

4.
Correspondence theorems

In this section we establish Theorem
4.1
relating the Euler characteristic of complex algebraic varieties to the degree of
\csm_{0}
of their tropicalisation, in the case this tropicalisation is a tropical manifold.

For
X
a tropical manifold and
A
a
0
-dimensional tropical cycle in
X
, let
m_{x}(A)
denote the multiplicity of the point
x\in X
in
A
.
We denote

\deg(A):=\sum_{x\in A}m_{x}(A).

Note that in the case where
A=\csm_{0}(X)
,
the sign of the multiplicity
m_{x}(\csm_{0}(X))
depends only on the sedentarity of
x
,
and thus if
X
is of dimension
d
and has only points of sedentarity
0

then
(-1)^{d}\deg(\csm_{0}(X))>0
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a meromorphic family of subvarieties of a complex toric variety
\mathcal{Y}
defined over the punctured disc
\mathcal{D}^{*}
.
Let
Y
denote the tropicalization of the toric variety
\mathcal{Y}
and let
X=\trop(\mathcal{X})
be the extended tropicalisation of
\mathcal{X}
in
Y
in the sense of Payne
[
Pay09
]
.
Consider the fibre
\mathcal{X}_{t}:=f^{-1}(t)
for a generic
t\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
.
The following theorem relates the Euler characteristic of
\mathcal{X}_{t}
to
\csm_{0}(X)
when
X
is a tropical manifold.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a meromorphic family of subvarieties of a complex toric variety
\mathcal{Y}
over the punctured disk
\mathcal{D}^{*}
. Suppose that
X:=\trop(\mathcal{X})
is a tropical submanifold of
Y:=\trop(\mathcal{Y})
, then

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\deg(\csm_{0}(X)).

Proof.

Consider first the case when
\mathcal{X}
is a family of very affine subvarieties, namely is contained in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{N}\times\mathcal{D^{*}}
.
Then
\trop(\mathcal{X})=X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})
denote the vertex set of
X
when equipped with some polyhedral structure
\hat{X}
.
By
[
KS12
, Corollary 1.4]
we have

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}\chi(V_{\mathbb{C}}(\In_{v}\mathcal{X})),

where
\In_{v}\mathcal{X}
denotes the initial ideal of
\mathcal{X}
at the vertex
v
of
X
and
V_{\mathbb{C}}(\In_{v}\mathcal{X})
its variety over
\mathbb{C}
.

By
[
KP11
, Proposition 4.2]
, since
X
is a tropical manifold, the initial ideal
\In_{v}\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{N}^{\pm}]
must be generated by linear forms up to toric coordinate change. This means that
V_{\mathbb{C}}(\In_{v}\mathcal{X})
is the image under an invertible monomial map of the complement of a hyperplane arrangement. A monomial automorphism of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
is a homeomorphism, and thus the cohomology groups and hence Euler characteristic of the complex points of
V_{\mathbb{C}}(\In_{v}\mathcal{X})
are determined by the underlying matroid
M_{v}
; see
[
OT92
]
.
Namely, the Euler characteristic is up to sign the beta invariant of
M_{v}
. Therefore we obtain

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})=(-1)^{d}\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}\beta(M_{v})=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}m_{v}(\csm_{0}(X))=\sum_{x\in X}m_{x}(\csm_{0}(X)).

The last equality holds since
m_{x}(\csm_{0}(X))=0
if
x
lies in a positive-dimensional face of
\hat{X}
.
This proves the statement when
\mathcal{Y}
is a torus.

Consider now the case where
\mathcal{X}
is a meromorphic family in

\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{D}^{*}

where
\mathcal{Y}
is some complex toric variety.
Let
\Sigma
be the fan defining
\mathcal{Y}
. We will apply the above proof for subvarieties of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{N}
to the open strata
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma}:=\mathcal{X}\cap(\mathcal{Y}_{\sigma}\times\mathcal{D}^{*})
, where
\mathcal{Y}_{\sigma}
is the open toric strata of
\mathcal{Y}
corresponding to the cone
\sigma
of
\Sigma
. Let
X_{\sigma}
denote the tropicalisation of
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma}
in open toric strata

\mathcal{Y}_{\sigma}
corresponding to
\sigma
.
By our assumption that
X=\trop(\mathcal{X})
is a tropical submanifold of
Y=\trop(\mathcal{Y})
, our previous case implies that
\chi(\mathcal{X}_{\sigma,t})=\deg(\csm_{0}(X_{\sigma}))
.

Compactly supported Euler characteristics are additive over strata, namely

\chi^{c}(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\chi^{c}(\mathcal{X}_{\sigma,t}).

However, since each stratum is non-singular, Poincaré duality relates the usual and compactly supported cohomology groups. Therefore we obtain

\chi(V)=(-1)^{\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(V)}\chi^{c}(V)=\chi^{c}(V),
since the real dimensions of the complex varieties are even.
Hence we have

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\chi(\mathcal{X}_{\sigma,t})=\sum_{x\in X}m_{x}({\csm_{0}(X)})=\deg(\csm_{0}(X)),

and the proof is complete.
∎

Under the same notation as in the proof of Theorem

The next theorem recovers and generalises the correspondence theorem for CSM cycles of matroids and wonderful compactifications proved in

5.
Adjunction formula

In this section we give a product formula for CSM cycles of matroids, and use it to prove a tropical analogue of the adjunction formula for smooth algebraic varieties. The tropical adjunction formula will require us to restrict to codimension-1 tropical submanifolds in the sense of Definition
2.14
.

5.1.
Product formula for CSM cycles of matroids

We start with the following result about intersections of CSM cycles of matroids. If
M
is a loopless matroid on the ground set
\{0,\dots,n\}
and
\Sigma_{M}
is its corresponding matroidal tropical cycle in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}
, we consider the formal sum of tropical cycles

\csm(\Sigma_{M}):=\csm(M):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\csm_{i}(M).

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be two loopless matroids on the ground set
\{0,\dots,n\}
, with matroidal tropical cycles
\Sigma_{M}
and
\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/\,\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\csm(\Sigma_{M})\cdot\csm(\Sigma_{M^{\prime}})=\csm(\Sigma_{M}\cdot\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}),

where the products denote tropical (stable) intersection in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Any matroidal tropical cycle can be obtained as the intersection of divisors corresponding to codimension-many tropical rational functions
[
Sha13
]
,
[
FR13
, Corollary 3.11]
.
In other words, if
c
and
c^{\prime}
are the codimensions of
\Sigma_{M}
and
\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively, there are tropical rational functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{c}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{c^{\prime}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

[\Sigma_{M}]=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\cdot\dots\cdot f_{c}\cdot[\mathbb{R}^{n}]\quad\text{and}\quad[\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}]=g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\cdot\dots\cdot g_{c^{\prime}}\cdot[\mathbb{R}^{n}].

This implies that

[\Sigma_{M}\cdot\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}]=f_{1}\cdot\dots\cdot f_{c}\cdot g_{1}\cdot\dots\cdot g_{c^{\prime}}\cdot[\mathbb{R}^{n}].

Using the formula for CSM cycles of matroids in
[
Rau
, Proposition 2.3]
,
we have

\csm(\Sigma_{M})=\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{1}{1+f_{i}}\cdot[\Sigma_{M}]\quad\text{and}\quad\csm(\Sigma_{M^{\prime}})=\prod_{i=1}^{c^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1+g_{i}}\cdot[\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}],

and thus

\csm(\Sigma_{M})\cdot\csm(\Sigma_{M^{\prime}})=\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{1}{1+f_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1+g_{i}}\cdot[\Sigma_{M}\cdot\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}]=\csm(\Sigma_{M}\cdot\Sigma_{M^{\prime}}),

as claimed.
∎

5.2.
Adjunction in tropical manifolds

The following theorem relates the CSM cycles of a codimension-
1
tropical submanifold
D
to the CSM cycles of the ambient tropical manifold
X
using the tropical intersection theory in
X
.
Notice that we do not require that
X
be compact.
To prove the theorem we restrict to codimension one tropical submanifolds from Definition
2.14
.
Recall that Example
2.15
presented tropical manifolds (lines) which are also codimension one tropical subvarieties of tropical manifolds which do not satisfy the following theorem, due to the fact that they do not satisfy the compatibility condition to be a tropical submanifold.

Theorem 5.2

(Adjunction formula)
.

Let
X
be a tropical manifold of dimension
d
and
D\subset X
a tropical submanifold of codimension
1
in
X
.
Then

\csm_{d-2}(D)=(\csm_{d-1}(X)-D)\cdot D,

where
\cdot
denotes the tropical intersection product in
X
.

Proof.

We assume the submanifold
D
is connected, otherwise the statement can be proved on each connected component.
We denote by
D^{o}
(respectively,
X^{o}
) the strata of
D
(respectively,
X
) of sedentarity
0
.
We first prove the case when
D
is not a boundary divisor of
X
, in other words,
D
is the closure in
X
of
D^{o}
.

The cycle
D
is a Cartier divisor in
X
, meaning that there is a collection of tropical rational functions
\{f_{\alpha}\}
such that
\varphi_{\alpha}(D\cap U_{\alpha})=\divis_{X_{\alpha}}(f_{\alpha})
for every chart
\varphi_{\alpha}
. Moreover, in the chart indexed by
\alpha
, the manifold
X
itself is cut out by a collection of functions
g^{\alpha}_{1},\dots,g^{\alpha}_{c_{\alpha}}
. Therefore, in each chart there are collections of functions
g^{\alpha}_{1},\dots,g^{\alpha}_{c_{\alpha}}
cutting out
X^{o}_{\alpha}
, and
f_{\alpha},g^{\alpha}_{1},\dots,g^{\alpha}_{c_{\alpha}}
cutting out
D_{\alpha}:=D\cap U_{\alpha}
.
Following the proof of Proposition
5.1
and working locally in charts we have
that

\displaystyle\csm(D^{o}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+f_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{c_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{1+g^{\alpha}_{i}}\cdot[D^{o}_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=(1-f_{\alpha}+f^{2}_{\alpha}-\dotsb)\prod_{i=1}^{c_{\alpha}}(1-g^{\alpha}_{i}+(g^{\alpha}_{i})^{2}-\dotsb)\cdot[D^{o}_{\alpha}].

Looking only at the dimension-
(d-2)
part of this equation we obtain,

\displaystyle\csm_{d-2}(D^{o}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=-(f_{\alpha}+g^{\alpha}_{1}+\dots+g^{\alpha}_{c_{\alpha}})\cdot[D^{o}_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=-f_{\alpha}\cdot[D^{o}_{\alpha}]-(g^{\alpha}_{1}+\dots+g^{\alpha}_{c_{\alpha}})\cdot[D^{o}_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=-(D_{\alpha}^{o})^{2}+\csm_{d-1}(X^{o}_{\alpha})\cdot D^{o}_{\alpha}.

Therefore we can conclude that

\csm_{d-2}(D^{o})=(\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})-D^{o})\cdot D^{o}.

Notice that, by definition of the boundary of tropical manifolds in terms of sedentarity, when
D
is of sedentarity
0
in
X
we have
\partial D=\partial X\cap D
.
Since
D
is a submanifold of
X
of sedentarity
0
, it is transverse to the boundary
\partial X
of
X
.
Moreover,
\partial X
is a codimension-
1
tropical cycle when equipped with weights equal to
1
,
thus as tropical cycles we have

\partial D=\partial X\cdot D
.
We thus get

\displaystyle\csm_{d-2}(D)

\displaystyle=\csm_{d-2}(D^{o})+\partial D

\displaystyle=\csm_{d-2}(D^{o})+\partial X\cdot D

\displaystyle=(\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})-D^{o})\cdot D^{o}+\partial X\cdot D

\displaystyle=(\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})+\partial X)\cdot D-(D^{o})^{2}

\displaystyle=\csm_{d-1}(X)\cdot D-D^{2}.

The second to last equality holds since
\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})\cdot D^{o}=\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})\cdot D
and
(D^{o})^{2}=D^{2}
due to the transversality of
D
with the boundary of
X
. This finishes the case when
D
is not a boundary divisor of
X
.

Let
\partial X=\bigcup D_{i}
be the decomposition of the boundary of
X
into irreducible divisors. If
D
is a boundary divisor of
X
then

\csm_{d-1}(X)=\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})+\partial X=\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})+D+\sum_{D\neq D_{i}}D_{i}.

Therefore

\csm_{d-1}(X)\cdot D-D^{2}=\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})\cdot D+D\cdot\sum_{D\neq D_{i}}D_{i}.

By Lemma
3.13
we have

\csm_{d-1}(X^{o})\cdot D=\csm_{d-2}(D^{o})
,
where
D^{o}=D\setminus\bigcup_{D\neq D_{i}}D_{i}
.
Moreover, the intersection

D\cdot\sum_{D\neq D_{i}}D_{i}=\csm_{d-2}(\partial D)=\partial D
.
Combining all this we obtain

\csm_{d-1}(X)\cdot D-D^{2}=\csm_{d-2}(D^{o})+\csm_{d-2}(\partial D)=\csm_{d-2}(D),

which proves the formula.
∎

6.
Tropical Noether’s Formula

In this section we restrict to compact tropical manifolds of dimension
2
, which we call compact tropical surfaces.
In order to prove Noether’s formula for a compact tropical surface
X
,
we will assume the existence of a cellular structure
\hat{X}
on
X
satisfying the following properties.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
X
be a tropical manifold. A cellular structure
\hat{X}
supported on
X
is a
face structure
on
X
if
for each cell
\sigma
of
\hat{X}
there exists
a chart
\varphi:U_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}
of
X
such that
U_{\alpha}\supset\sigma
and
\varphi(\sigma)
is the closure of a polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{r_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}
which is transverse to the boundary of
\mathbb{T}^{r_{\alpha}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}}
.
The face structure
\hat{X}
is called
rational
if all these polyhedra are
{\mathbb{R}}
-rational polyhedra, i.e., their facets have normal vectors with entries in
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

A rational face structure
\hat{X}
on a
2
-dimensional tropical manifold is called
Delzant
if for any vertex
v
of
\hat{X}
of sedentarity 0 and any
2
-dimensional face
F
of
\hat{X}
with
v\in F
we have that, in any chart of
X
containing
v
, the primitive integer vectors
\mathbf{w}_{v}(e)
and
\mathbf{w}_{v}(e^{\prime})
of the two edges
e,e^{\prime}
of
F
containing
v
can be completed to a basis of the ambient lattice.

Remark 6.2
.

The notion of a face structure on a tropical manifold has previously appeared in
[
JSS19
]
.
We do not know exactly which tropical manifolds admit face structures, rational face structures, or Delzant face structures.
However, given a compact tropical surface with a rational face structure
\hat{X}
, we can use tropical wave front propagation from
[
MS
]
on each
2
-dimensional face of
\hat{X}
to pass to a finer face structure. This face structure is not necessarily Delzant, but it has the property that for each
2
-dimensional face
F
and each vertex
v
of
F
, the outgoing primitive normal vectors to the two edges adjacent to
v
in
F
span a triangle with no interior lattice points; see
[
MS
]
.

We denote the topological Euler characteristic of a tropical manifold
X
by
\chi(X)
.
Our main goal in this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6.3

(Tropical Noether’s Formula)
.

Let
X
be a compact tropical surface admitting a Delzant face structure.
Then

\chi(X)=\frac{\deg(\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2})}{12}.

In order to prove Noether’s Formula, we first recall formulas for the intersection numbers of
1
-cycles in a compact tropical surface. In a compact tropical surface, there are
1
-cycles of sedentarity
0
and boundary
1
-cycles. A boundary
1
-cycle is a linear combination of boundary divisors of
X
; see Subsection
2.2
.

Proposition 6.4
.

[
Sha
, Section 3.5]

Let
X
be a compact tropical surface and
A,B
two
1
-cycles in

Our strategy for proving Noether’s Formula begins by analysing the tropical

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
X
be a compact tropical surface with a Delzant face structure
\hat{X}
. Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}
denote the irreducible boundary divisors of
X
. Then for any vertex
v\in\hat{X}
we have

\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2}=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{X})}m_{v}\cdot v

with

m_{v}=12-6|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|-\delta(v)\cdot\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e),

where
\delta(v)=1
if
v
is of sedentarity
0
, and
\delta(v)=0
otherwise.

Proof.

The cycle
\csm_{0}(X)
is supported on the vertices of
\hat{X}
.
The cycle
\csm_{1}(X)
can be written as

\csm_{1}(X)=\csm_{1}(X_{0})+\csm_{1}(X_{1})=\csm_{1}(X_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}.

Therefore, we have

(6.2)

\csm_{1}(X)^{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}D_{i}^{2}=\csm_{1}(X_{0})^{2}+2\csm_{1}(X_{0})\csm_{1}(X_{1})+2\sum_{i<j}D_{i}D_{j}.

First suppose
v
is a vertex in
\hat{X}_{2}
.
In this case, the multiplicity of
v
in
\csm_{0}(X)
is equal to 1.
Among the terms on the right-hand side of Equation (
6.2
), the vertex
v
only appears with non-zero multiplicity in
2\sum_{i<j}D_{i}D_{j}
. Since
v
is the intersection of two unique boundary divisors
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
of
X
, we thus have

m_{v}=3
.
Moreover,
|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|=2
and
|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|=1
. Therefore, in this case we have

m_{v}=3=12-6|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|
,
and the claim holds.

Suppose now
v
is a vertex in
\hat{X}_{1}
. The multiplicity of
v
in
\csm_{0}(X)

is equal to
2-|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|
.
The only term on the right-hand side of
Equation (
6.2
) with non-zero multiplicity in
v
is
2\csm_{1}(X_{0})\csm_{1}(X_{1})
. Moreover, the cycle
\csm_{1}(X_{1})
is of weight 1 everywhere, and the weight of
\csm_{1}(X_{0})
along the unique edge
e
of
\hat{X}_{0}
whose boundary is

is

2-|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|
by Lemma
3.13
.
Therefore, in this case
m_{v}=3(2-|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|)
.
Since each vertex
v\in\hat{X}_{1}
is adjacent to exactly one edge in
X_{0}
and satisfies
|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|=|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|+1
, we can rewrite this as

m_{v}=3(2-|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|)=12-6|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|
,
and the claim holds.

Lastly, suppose
v\in\hat{X}_{0}
. The only term on the right-hand side of Equation (
6.2
) with non-zero multiplicity in
v
is

\csm_{1}(X_{0})^{2}
.
Using
[
Sha
, Proposition 3.18]
, the term
\csm_{1}(X_{0})^{2}
can be expressed as a cycle supported only on the vertices of
\hat{X}_{0}
, where a vertex
v\in\hat{X}_{0}
has multiplicity

m_{v}(\csm_{1}(X_{0})^{2})=10+N_{v}-5|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+2|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|+\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}-\sigma_{v}(e)

with
N_{v}
denoting the dimension of the affine span of the local fan
\starr_{\hat{X}}(v)
.

For a vertex
v
of
\hat{X}_{0}
, denote by
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(v)
the vector space
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(\starr_{\hat{X}}(v))
from Definition
3.9
.
By Lemma
3.12
, the multiplicity of a vertex
v\in\hat{X}_{0}
in the term
\csm_{0}(X_{0})
is

m_{v}(\csm_{0}(X_{0}))=\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{0}(v)-\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{1}(v)+\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{2}(v).

We have
\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{0}(v)=1
and
\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{1}(v)=N_{v}
.
The fan
\Sigma:=\starr_{\hat{X}}(v)
is matroidal, thus by
[
JSS19
, Theorem 5.3]
, it satisfies Poincaré duality for tropical homology with
\mathbb{Z}
and hence
\mathbb{Q}
coefficients. We refer the reader to
[
JSS19
, Section 5]
and Equations 5.1 and 5.3 there for the definitions of the chain complexes computing these groups. Tropical Poincaré duality implies that

H^{q}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}^{p})\cong H^{BM}_{2-q}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}_{2-p})

for all
0\leq p,q\leq 2
, where the left-hand side denotes tropical cohomology and the right-hand side tropical Borel-Moore homology.
From their definitions, we have
H^{q}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}^{p})=0
for

and
\dim H^{0}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}^{p})=\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{p}(v)
.
Since

H^{BM}_{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}_{0})=H^{BM}_{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{R})
and that

is equal to the number of

-dimensional faces of the fan

.
It follows that

\displaystyle\dim{\mathbf{F}}_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\dim H^{BM}_{2}(\Sigma;{\mathbf{F}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\dim C^{BM}_{0}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})-\dim C^{BM}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})+\dim C^{BM}_{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=1-|{\mathcal{E}}(v,\hat{X})|+|{\mathcal{F}}(v,\hat{X})|.

We conclude that the multiplicity of the vertex

in the cycle

is equal to

m_{v}(\csm_{0}(X))=2-N_{v}-|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|.

Combining this with the contribution of

from above, we obtain

m_{v}=12-6|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|-\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e),

and the claim is proved for all vertices of
\hat{X}
.
∎

Following Lemma

into account.
If

is an irreducible boundary divisor of a tropical surface

is only defined up to rational, homological, or numerical equivalence.
We are only interested in the self-intersection number, so any of these equivalences will do upon taking the degree. We abuse notation and let

denote the degree of the self-intersection. This degree can be computed in a variety of ways. Here we will do this by finding a section of the normal bundle of

in

and computing its degree
[
Sha
]
. The section we will use will be

in the sense of Definition

. This will not only help us to compute the degree of the normal bundle, but it will be used to construct smooth toric surfaces.

We briefly review the theory of line bundles, sections, and divisors on tropical curves.
A tropical curve
C
is a tropical manifold of dimension
1
. Here we will assume that
C
is compact.
Each boundary divisor
D
of a compact tropical surface
X
is a compact tropical curve; an atlas for
D
is obtained by restricting the atlas for
X
. Moreover, any face structure
\hat{X}
on
X
induces a face structure on
D
.

A line bundle on a tropical curve
C
is a
2
-dimensional tropical surface
L
together with a map
\pi:L\to C
such that for any point
p\in C
we have
\pi^{-1}(p)=\mathbb{T}
and furthermore, there exist local trivialisations: for any point
p\in C
there exists a neighbourhood
U_{p}
of
p
such that
\pi^{-1}(U_{p})=U_{p}\times\mathbb{T}
. If
\{U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\}
is a covering such that
\pi^{-1}(U_{\alpha})=U_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T}
then the line bundle
L
can be specified via integer affine transition functions
f_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
which obey the cocycle condition.
Let
\mathcal{O}^{*}
denote the sheaf of tropical invertible regular functions on
C
. Then line bundles are in correspondence with
H^{1}(C;\mathcal{O}^{*})

[
MZ08
]
.
The normal bundle
N_{X}(D)
of a boundary divisor
D
in a tropical surface
X
is the tropical line bundle on
D
where the transition functions
f_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}
are inherited from the transition functions from the charts of
X
in a neighbourhood of
D

[
Sha
, Section 3.5.4]
.

A section of a line bundle is a continuous function
s:C\to L
such that
\pi(s(p))=p
for all
p\in C
and in every trivialisation
U\subset C
with
\pi^{-1}(U)=U\times\mathbb{T}
the function
s|_{U}
is a tropical rational function
U\to\mathbb{T}
which is bounded. In particular, in a neighbourhood of each point
p
of
C
, the section
s
is given by a piecewise integer affine function.
For each
p\in C
of sedentarity
0
, we can consider the collection of outgoing primitive integer tangent vectors of
C
at
p
, call these
\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{1}),\dots,\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{{\rm val}(p)})
, where the
e_{i}
denote the edges of
C
adjacent to
p
and
{\rm val}(p)
denotes the valency of
p
in the underlying graph of
C
.
By the balancing condition for
C
at
p
we have that
\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm val}(p)}\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i})=0
.
Let
\frac{\partial s}{\partial\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i})}(p)
denote the slope of the integer affine function at
p
in the direction of
\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i})
. The order of vanishing
m_{p}(s)
of
s
along
p
is the order of vanishing of the tropical rational function determining
s
in a local trivialisation at
p
; more specifically,

m_{p}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm val}(p)}\frac{\partial s}{\partial\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i})}(p).

The
divisor associated to the section

s
is
(s):=\sum_{p\in C}m_{p}(s)p
.

At a point
p
of
C
we will also consider the primitive outgoing tangent vectors to the graph of the section
s
at the point
s(p)
; namely, the vectors
{{\bf t}}_{p}(e_{1}),\dots,{{\bf t}}_{p}(e_{\rm val(p)})

given by

(6.3)

{{\bf t}}_{p}(e_{i})=\left(\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i}),\frac{\partial s}{\partial\bar{{\bf w}}_{p}(e_{i})}(p)\right).

There is an equivalence between divisors on a tropical curve
C
and tropical line bundles on
C
together with a section (up to adding scalars)
[
MZ08
, Proposition 4.6]
.
Two sections of the same line bundle that differ only by adding a constant yield the same divisor.

A global tropical rational function on a curve
C
is a function
h:C\to\mathbb{T}
such that in each chart of
C
the function
h
can be expressed as the difference of two tropical polynomials. We can think of a tropical rational function
h
as a section of the trivial line bundle
h:C\to\mathbb{T}
. A divisor is
principal
if it arises from a global tropical rational function
h:C\to\mathbb{T}
considered as such a section. Rationally equivalent divisors differ by a principal divisor and thus have the same degree.

If
D
is a divisor in a compact tropical surface
X
and
s_{D}
is a section of its normal bundle, then by
[
Sha
, Section 3.5.4]
we have

(6.4)

D^{2}=\deg((s_{D}))=\sum_{p\in D}m_{p}(s_{D}).

To obtain the degree of the self-intersection of a boundary divisor
D
, any section
s_{D}
of the normal bundle will do. However, for our proof of Noether’s Formula, we will require the notion of a Delzant section.

Definition 6.6
.

Let
C
be a tropical curve with a face structure
\hat{C}
, and let
L
be a tropical line bundle on
C
. A section
s:C\to L
is
Delzant
if its associated divisor
Q=(s)
is supported on the bounded edges of
\hat{C}
, and for every bounded edge
e
of
\hat{C}
there is at most one point
p\in{\rm relint}(e)
such that
p\in{\rm Supp}(Q)
and if this point exists we have
m_{p}(Q)=1
.

We call such sections Delzant as for every bounded edge
e
of
\hat{C}
and every point
p\in{\rm relint}(e)
, the outgoing tangent vectors to the graph of
s
are either parallel (in the case
m_{p}(Q)=0
) or they are unimodular (in the case
m_{p}(Q)=1
).
In particular, if
s
is a Delzant section, any points with negative multiplicity in the associated divisor
Q=(s)
must be vertices of
\hat{C}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

For any tropical curve
C
with face structure
\hat{C}
and any line bundle
L
on
C
with local trivialisations over the edges of
\hat{C}
there exists a Delzant section
s:C\to L
.

Proof.

By
[
MZ08
, Proposition 4.6]
, there exists a section
\tilde{s}:C\to L
of
L
. Denote its associated divisor by
\tilde{Q}
.
Subtracting any principal divisor
P
from
\tilde{Q}
provides a linearly equivalent divisor and thus another section of
L
.

We now construct for every edge
e
of
\hat{C}
a certain tropical rational function
h_{e}:C\to\mathbb{T}
which is constant outside of
e
.
For
e
an unbounded edge of
C
, let
v
denote the unique vertex of
\hat{C}
which is of sedentarity
0
. Then we define
h_{e}(p)=-\tilde{s}(p)
if
p
is a point of
e
, and
h_{e}(p)=-\tilde{s}(v)
otherwise. Then
h_{e}:C\to\mathbb{T}
is a tropical rational function, and its associated divisor
(h_{e})
satisfies
m_{p}((h_{e}))=-m_{p}(\tilde{Q})
if
p\in{\rm relint}(e)
or if
p
is the vertex of
e
of sedentarity 1, and
m_{p}((h_{e}))=0
for all
p\notin e
.

For
e
a bounded edge of
\hat{C}
, let
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
denote its two vertices.
If
\tilde{s}(v_{1})=\tilde{s}(v_{2})
, we proceed as above and define
h_{e}(p)=-\tilde{s}(p)
if
p
is a point of
e
and
h_{e}(p)=-\tilde{s}(v_{i})
otherwise.
Then
h_{e}:C\to\mathbb{T}
is a tropical rational function satisfying
m_{p}((h_{e}))=-m_{p}(\tilde{Q})
for all
p\in{\rm relint}(e)
, and
m_{p}((h_{e}))=0
for all
p\notin e
.

Suppose that
\tilde{s}(v_{1})\neq\tilde{s}(v_{2})
.
Upon taking a chart we can suppose that
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and that
v_{1}<v_{2}
. Let

m=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\tilde{s}(v_{2})-\tilde{s}(v_{1})}{v_{2}-v_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad m+1=-\Bigl\lceil\frac{\tilde{s}(v_{2})-\tilde{s}(v_{1})}{v_{2}-v_{1}}\Bigr\rceil.

Let

be the tropical polynomial given by the piecewise integer affine function

It can be checked that there is a single zero

of

in
\mathbb{R}
and it satisfies

. Moreover, the tropical polynomial
f_{e}
satisfies

. Then we can cook up a tropical rational function

by setting

for

and
h_{e}(p)=0
otherwise. Notice that

is a tropical rational function and that

for all

except

, and
m_{p}((h_{e}))=0
for all

.

By construction, the divisor of the section

is Delzant in the sense of Definition
6.6
, and the lemma is proven.

Lemma 6.9

The next lemma provides an expression for the number of
2
-dimensional faces of
\hat{X}

arising from the Noether’s formulas satisfied by the toric surfaces defined by the face fans
\Sigma_{F}
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
X
be a compact tropical surface with a Delzant face structure
\hat{X}
.
Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}
denote the irreducible boundary divisors of
X
.
Then

12|{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})|=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|-\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X}_{0})}\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we provide a Noether-type formula for every face
F
of
\hat{X}
.
We do this by cases.

\bullet

Case
F
is bounded.
Suppose
F
is a bounded
2
-dimensional face of
\hat{X}
.
As
\hat{X}
is a Delzant face structure, the toric variety associated to the fan

\Sigma_{F}
is a non-singular toric surface.
Noether’s Formula for this toric surface (see
[
Ful93
, Section 2.5]
) says that

(6.6)

12=3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(\Sigma_{F})|+\sum_{\rho\in{\rm Rays}(\Sigma_{F})}D_{\rho}^{2},

where
{{\mathcal{F}}}(\Sigma_{F})
is the set of
2
-dimensional cones of
\Sigma_{F}
and
D_{\rho}
is the divisor in the toric surface corresponding to the ray
\rho
of
\Sigma_{F}
.
Since the fan
\Sigma_{F}
is unimodular, the self-intersection
D_{\rho}^{2}
is equal to
\tau_{F}(e)
where
e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(F)
is the edge of
F
corresponding to the ray
\rho
of
\Sigma_{F}

[
Ful93
, Section 2.5]
.
Taking into account that all edges of
F
are of sedentarity
0
, we obtain

12=3|{{\mathcal{V}}}(F)|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(F)\\
\sed(e)=0\end{subarray}}\tau_{F}(e).

\bullet

Case
F
is unbounded and contains no vertex of sedentarity 2.
Suppose
F
is a
2
-dimensional face of
\hat{X}
containing points of sedentarity 1 but no points of sedentarity
2
.
Let
e
be the unique edge of
F
of sedentarity 1 and
D_{e}
be the boundary divisor containing
e
.
The fan
\Sigma_{F}
is unimodular, so the corresponding toric surface satisfies Equation (
6.6
).
The number of
2
-dimensional cones of
\Sigma_{F}
is equal to

|{\mathcal{V}}(F)|+1
if the section
s_{D_{e}}
has a zero on
e

(in which case two extra rays were added to the edge fan of
F
to obtain
\Sigma_{F}
),
and equal to
|{\mathcal{V}}(F)|
if
s_{D_{e}}
has no zero on
e
(in which case only one ray was added to the edge fan of
F
).
Moreover, the self-intersections
D_{\rho}^{2}
are equal to
\tau_{F}(e)
if
\rho
is a ray of
\Sigma_{F}
corresponding to an edge
e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(F)
of sedentarity 0, and equal to
-1
if
\rho
is one of the extra rays added to the edge fan of
F
.
In all cases, we have

12=3|{{\mathcal{V}}}(F)|+m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(F)\\
\sed(e)=0\end{subarray}}\tau_{F}(e),

where
m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})=1
if
s_{D_{e}}
has a zero on
e
and
m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})=0
otherwise.

\bullet

Case
F
is unbounded and contains a vertex of sedentarity 2.

Suppose
F
is a
2
-dimensional face of
\hat{X}
containing a point of sedentarity
2
.
Again, the fan
\Sigma_{F}
is unimodular, and so the corresponding toric surface satisfies Equation (
6.6
). We have
|{\mathcal{F}}(\Sigma_{F})|=|{\mathcal{V}}(F)|
.
The self-intersections
D_{\rho}^{2}
are equal to
\tau_{F}(e)
if
\rho
is a ray of
\Sigma_{F}
corresponding to an edge

of sedentarity 0,
and equal to
0
if
\rho
is one of the two extra rays added to the edge fan of

We thus get

12=3|{{\mathcal{V}}}(F)|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(F)\\
\sed(e)=0\end{subarray}}\tau_{F}(e).

Now, we add all these equalities over all faces

of
\hat{X}
to obtain

12|{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})|=\sum_{F\in{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})}3|{{\mathcal{V}}}(F)|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(e)=1\end{subarray}}m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in{\mathcal{F}}(\hat{X})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(F)\\
\sed(e)=0\end{subarray}}\tau_{F}(e).

Using that
\sum_{F\in{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})}|{{\mathcal{V}}}(F)|=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|

12|{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})|=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(e)=1\end{subarray}}m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(e)=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in{\mathcal{F}}(\hat{X})\\

Applying Lemma
6.9
, we further obtain

12|{{\mathcal{F}}}(\hat{X})|=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(e)=1\end{subarray}}m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{v}\in{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(\bar{v})=1\end{subarray}}m_{\bar{v}}(s_{D_{\bar{v}}})-\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X}_{0})}\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e),

where

denotes the boundary divisor containing the vertex
\bar{v}
.
Finally, Equation (

) gives

\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(e)=1\end{subarray}}m_{e}(s_{D_{e}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{v}\in{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{X})\\
\sed(\bar{v})=1\end{subarray}}m_{\bar{v}}(s_{D_{\bar{v}}}),

which proves the lemma.
∎

We now combine these results together to complete the proof of Noether’s Formula.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

From Lemma
6.5
we obtain

(6.7)

\deg\left(\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}D_{i}^{2}\right)=\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}\left(12-6|{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})|+3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|\right)\\
-\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X}_{0})}\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e).

Adding
\deg(\sum_{i=0}^{k}D_{i}^{2})
to both sides, and given that each edge of
\hat{X}
is adjacent to exactly two vertices, we obtain

\deg\left(\csm_{1}(X)^{2}+\csm_{0}(X)\right)=12|{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})|-12|{{\mathcal{E}}}(\hat{X})|+\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})}3|{{\mathcal{F}}}(v,\hat{X})|\\
-\sum_{v\in{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X}_{0})}\sum_{e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}(v,\hat{X})}\sigma_{v}(e)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2}.

Finally, by applying Lemma
6.10
, we get

\deg(\csm_{1}(X)^{2}+\csm_{0}(X))=12|{{\mathcal{V}}}(\hat{X})|-12|{{\mathcal{E}}}(\hat{X})|+12|{\mathcal{F}}(\hat{X})|=12\chi(X),

and the proof is complete.
∎

We next present a corollary that proves Noether’s Formula for toric compactifications of tropical surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
To do this we consider the
combinatorial stratification

X^{c}
of
X
from
[
MZ08
, Section 1.5]
.
Suppose that a tropical surface
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has lineality space of dimension
0
.
Since
the local fans around all vertices of the combinatorial stratification
X^{c}
of
X
are matroidal fans, the cell structure
X^{c}
gives a polyhedral face structure of
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The collection of unbounded directions of
X
can also be stratified using the combinatorial stratification. We denote by
\rec{(X^{c})}
this stratified set; the next corollary assumes that this is a unimodular fan.

Corollary 6.11
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a tropical submanifold of dimension 2 with lineality space of dimension
0
such that
\Sigma=\rec{(X^{c})}
is a unimodular fan. Then the closure
\overline{X}\subset\mathbb{T}\Sigma
of
X
in the tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
is a compact tropical surface satisfying Noether’s Formula.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is unimodular, the tropical toric variety
\mathbb{T}\Sigma
is non-singular. Moreover, the closure
\overline{X}
is compact and the closure of each 2-dimensional face of the combinatorial stratification of
X
is either bounded or, up to extended integer linear transformation, equal to the compactification in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
of a polyhedron transverse to the boundary.
Therefore the combinatorial stratification of
\overline{X}
is a Delzant face structure, in the sense of Definition
6.1
. We can thus apply Theorem
6.3
to obtain the statement.
∎

Corollary
6.11
can also be extended to a larger class of 2-dimensional tropical submanifolds of toric varieties by applying tropical blowup operations to the surfaces described above and the fact that Noether’s Formula is preserved under these operations by
[
Sha
, Theorem 5.1]
.

We conclude with an example showing the meaning of Noether’s Formula in the particular case where
X
is a hypersurface of a tropical toric 3-fold.

Example 6.12
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is a non-singular tropical hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by a tropical polynomial
f
. Let
\Delta
be the Newton polytope of
f
and let
\Sigma
be the normal fan of
\Delta
. Consider the compact tropical surface
X
obtained as the compactification of
X_{0}
in the tropical toric variety
{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma
, as in Example
2.11
.

Topologically,

is a wedge of
|{\rm Int}(\Delta)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}|
spheres, so that

\chi(X)=1+|{\rm Int}(\Delta)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}|.

The tropical hypersurface
X
has a face structure
\hat{X}
dual to the subdivision of its Newton polytope. This face structure is Delzant.
We can use Lemma
6.5
to determine the local contribution of each vertex to
\deg(\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2}).
We have

m_{v}(\csm_{0}(X)+\csm_{1}(X)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}^{2})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }\sed(v)=0,\\
-3&\text{if }\sed(v)=1,\\
3&\text{if }\sed(v)=2.\end{cases}

To compute the self-intersection of the boundary divisors
D_{i}
we notice that the boundary divisors are in bijection with the facets of the polytope
\Delta

{\mathbf{n}}_{F}
, and two facets which intersect do so along an edge.
It can be shown that the self-intersection number
D_{i}^{2}
is equal to
\tau_{i}

-\tau_{i}\,{\mathbf{n}}_{F_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\Delta)\\
F\neq F^{\prime}\end{subarray}}L(F\cap F^{\prime})\,{\mathbf{n}}_{F^{\prime}},

where

denotes the lattice length of the intersection of the faces
F
and
F^{\prime}
. In particular, the lattice length is
0
if
F\cap F^{\prime}=\emptyset
or if
F\cap F^{\prime}
is a point.

Using the duality between
\hat{X}
and the unimodular subdivision of
\Delta
, Noether’s formula translates to a formula for the number of interior lattice points of a polytope in terms of its lattice volume

, the total lattice area of its

-dimensional faces
{\rm Area}(\Delta)
, the total lattice length of its edges

, and the numbers

12(1+|{\rm Int}(\Delta)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}|)=2{\rm Vol}(\Delta)-3{\rm Area}(\Delta)+3{\rm Peri}(\Delta)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}.

\diamondsuit

We conjecture a generalization of Theorem

The Todd class of a complex vector bundle

We define the Todd class of a tropical manifold

Conjecture 6.13
.

For a compact tropical manifold
X
of dimension

\chi(X)=\deg\Todd_{n}(X).
