Moderate deviations of many–server queues in the Halfin–Whitt regime and weak convergence methods
Anatolii A. Puhalskii

Abstract




1
Introduction

Many–server queues are important in applications but their analysis
beyond Markovian assumptions is difficult, see, e.g., Asmussen

[
2
]
. As a way to gain insight into the operation of
many–server queues,
various heavy–traffic
asymptotics have been explored when the arrival and service rates tend
to infinity.
Iglehart and Whitt
[
14
]

considered the number of servers,
n
,
fixed, assumed that the traffic intensity,
\rho
, tends to unity
and showed that properly normalized version of the queue
length process converges to a one dimensional
reflected Brownian motion, in analogy with the heavy–traffic limit
for a
GI/GI/1
queue.
As for the single server queue, the
probability of having at least one customer in the queue
converges to unity.
Many server queues with the numbers of servers going to infinity
for different
initial conditions and assumptions on the interarrival and service
time distributions, were considered
by Borovkov
[
5
,
6
]
, Iglehart
[
12
,
13
]
and
Whitt
[
31
]
.
The growth conditions imposed on
n
result in the queue being
asymptotically equivalent to an infinite server queue
so that the probability of wait tends to zero.

In order to capture the situation of
the probability of a customer wait being strictly between zero and
unity,
Halfin and
Whitt
[
11
]
proposed the setup where
service time distributions are held fixed, while
the number of servers and the arrival rate
grow without a bound in such a way that

\sqrt{n}(1-\rho)\to\beta\in\mathbb{R}\,.

With
Q(t)
denoting the number of customers present at
time
t
, assuming the initial conditions are suitably chosen, the
sequence of processes
(Q(t)-n)/\sqrt{n}
,
considered as random elements of the associated Skorohod space,
converges in law to a continuous–path process.
In their pioneering contribution,
Halfin and Whitt
[
11
]
considered the case of
exponentially distributed service times. The limit process then is an Ito
diffusion with a piecewise linear drift.
An extension to generally
distributed service times is more recent and is due to Reed

[
29
]
, see also Puhalskii and Reed
[
28
]
.
The limit process, however, is no longer generally Markov and is not
well understood, cf. Kaspi and Ramanan
[
17
]
and
Aghajani and Ramanan
[
1
]
, for related results.

In search of more tractable asymptotics, it was proposed in
Puhalskii
[
27
]
to study moderate deviations of
Q(t)
and a Large
Deviation Principle (LDP) was conjectured for the process

(Q(t)-n)/(b_{n}\sqrt{n})
under the heavy traffic condition

\sqrt{n}/b_{n}(1-\rho)\to\beta
,
where
b_{n}\to\infty
and
b_{n}/n\to 0
.
The
deviation function (a.k.a. rate function) was purported to solve
a convex variational problem with a quadratic objective function.
This paper confirms the conjecture and proves the LDP in question.
Furthermore, the deviation function is expressed in terms of
the solution to
a Fredholm
equation of the second kind which could facilitate
evaluating the deviation
function numerically.
It is noteworthy that although the LDP generally produces cruder
asymptotics than weak convergence, or convergence in law, it may capture exponents in the
distributions of corresponding weak convergence limits, cf.
Puhalskii
[
25
]
.

The main thrust of the proofs is to demonstrate the utility of
weak convergence methods for deriving large deviation asymptotics. As in Puhalskii
[
24
,
26
]
, the LDP is viewed
as an analogue of weak convergence, the cornerstone of the approach
being the following analogue of celebrated Prohorov’s tightness theorem: “A
sequence of probability measures on a complete separable metric space
is exponentially tight if and only if every its subsequence admits a further
subsequence that satisfies the LDP” (Theorem (P) in Puhalskii

[
23
]
).
Consequently, once exponential
tightness has been proved, the
proof of the LDP is achieved by identifying
a large deviation limit point. A possible identification tool is an
analogue of the method of finite–dimensional distributions.
It is what is used in
this paper in order to
derive
logarithmic asymptotics of
moderate deviations of
the sequential empirical process, which is a key element of the
proof of the main result, on the one hand,
and complements developments in Krichagina and Puhalskii

[
18
]
and in Puhalskii and Reed
[
28
]
, on the
other hand. In
order to use the analogy with weak convergence to the utmost,
this paper formulates the definition of the LDP for stochastic
processes as
large deviation (LD) convergence to
idempotent processes, see Puhalskii
[
26
]
and Appendix

A
for
definitions and properties. With tools for the study of
weak convergence properties of many–server queues
in heavy traffic being well developed,
the moderate deviation asymptotics are derived
by using similar methods, mutatis mutandis,
that proved their worth in the weak convergence
context. An important distinction on the technical side
is an extensive use of exponential martingales, as
establishing exponential tightness
is a more challenging task than establishing
tightness.
In addition, a study of
idempotent counterparts of
the standard Wiener process, the Brownian bridge and the
Kiefer process is carried out. The properties of those idempotent
processes are integral to the proofs.

The paper’s organisation
is as follows.
In Section

2
, a precise specification of
the model is provided, as well as the main result on the logarithmic
asymptotics of moderate deviations of the number–in–the–system
process.
The latter
is stated both in the
form of a trajectorial
LDP and in the form of LD convergence to an idempotent process.
The proofs are given in Section

3
.
Section
4
is concerned with evaluating the
deviation function by reduction to solving a Fredholm
equation of the second kind.
For the reader’s convenience,
Appendix
A

gives a primer on idempotent
processes and the use of weak convergence methods for proving the
LDP.
Appendix
B
is concerned with absolute
continuity of the solution to a
nonlinear renewal equation which is needed in Section
4
.

2
Trajectorial moderate deviation limit theorems

Assume as given a sequence of many server queues with unlimited
waiting room indexed by
n
, where
n

represents the number of servers. Service is performed on a first–come–first–served
basis. Let
Q_{n}(t)
denote the number of customers present at time

t
. Out of those,
Q_{n}(t)\wedge n

customers are in service and
(Q_{n}(t)-n)^{+}
customers are
in the queue.
The service times of the customers in the queue at
time
0
and the service times
of customers exogenously arriving after time
0
are denoted
by

\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots
in the
order of entering service and come from an
i.i.d. sequence of positive unbounded random variables with
continuous distribution function

F
. In terms of
F
, it is thus assumed that

F(0)=0\,,F(x)<1\,,\text{ for all }x\,.

(2.1)

The mean service time
\mu^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,dF(x)
is assumed to
be finite. Residual service times of the customers in service at
time
0
are denoted by

\eta^{(0)}_{1},\eta^{(0)}_{2},\ldots
and are assumed to be
i.i.d. with distribution
F_{0}
that is the
distribution of the delay in a stationary renewal
process with inter–renewal distribution
F
. Thus,

F_{0}(x)=\mu\int_{0}^{x}(1-F(y))\,dy\,.

(2.2)

Let
A_{n}(t)
denote the number of exogenous arrivals
by
t
, with
A_{n}(0)=0
. It is assumed that
A_{n}(t)
has unit jumps.
The entities
Q_{n}(0)
,

\{\eta^{(0)}_{1},\eta^{(0)}_{2},\ldots\}
,

\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots\}
, and
A_{n}=(A_{n}(t),\,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
are assumed to be
independent.
All stochastic processes are assumed to have
rightcontinuous paths with lefthand limits.
Let

{{\hat{A}}_{n}}(t)
denote the number of customers that enter service after time

0
and by time

t
, with

{{\hat{A}}_{n}}(0)=0
. Since the distribution function
of
\eta_{i}
is continuous,
\hat{A}_{n}
has
unit jumps a.s.
By considering the jumps of the processes on both sides of the next
equation, one can
see that

(Q_{n}(t)-n)^{+}+{\hat{A}}_{n}(t)=(Q_{n}(0)-n)^{+}+A_{n}(t)\,.

(2.3)

In addition, balancing the arrivals and departures yields the equation

Q_{n}(t)=Q_{n}^{(0)}(t)+(Q_{n}(0)-n)^{+}+A_{n}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{s+x\leq t\}}\,d\,\sum_{i=1}^{{{\hat{A}}_{n}}(s)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{i}\leq x\}}\,,

(2.4)

where

Q_{n}^{(0)}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{Q_{n}(0)\wedge n}\,\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta^{(0)}_{i}>t\}}\,\,,

(2.5)

which quantity represents the number of customers present at time
t
out
of those in service at time
0
, and

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{s+x\leq t\}}\,d\,\sum_{i=1}^{{{\hat{A}}_{n}}(s)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta_{i}\leq x\}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{A}_{n}(t)}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\hat{\tau}_{n,i}+\eta_{i}\leq t\}}\,

(2.6)

which quantity represents the number of customers that enter service after time
0

and depart by time
t
,
\hat{\tau}_{n,i}
denoting the
i
th jump
time of
\hat{A}_{n}
, i.e.,

\hat{\tau}_{n,i}=\inf\{t:\,\hat{A}_{n}(t)\geq i\}\,.

(2.7)

For existence and uniqueness of solutions to
(
2.3
)–(
2.5
), the reader is referred to
Puhalskii and Reed

[
28
]
.

Given a sequence
r_{n}\to\infty
, as
n\to\infty
, a sequence
P_{n}

of probability laws on the Borel
\sigma
–algebra of
a metric space
M
and a
[0,\infty]
–valued function
I

on
M
such that the sets
\{y\in M:\,I(y)\leq\gamma\}
are compact
for all
\gamma\geq 0
, the sequence
P_{n}
is said to obey
the LDP for rate
r_{n}
with deviation function
I
provided

\lim_{n\to\infty}1/r_{n}\,\ln P_{n}(W)=-\inf_{y\in W}I(y)\,,
for every Borel set
W
such that the infima of
I
over the
interior and closure of
W
agree.

The deviation function for the queue length process is introduced next.
For
T>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
and

\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{m})
represent the respective Skorohod spaces of rightcontinuous

\mathbb{R}^{m}
–valued
functions with lefthand limits defined on
[0,T]
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
respectively.
These spaces are
endowed with
metrics rendering them complete separable metric spaces, see Ethier and
Kurtz
[
9
]
, Jacod and Shiryaev
[
15
]
, for
more detail.
Given
q=(q(t)\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})\in\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
, let

I^{Q}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\{\int_{0}^{1}\dot{w}^{0}(x)^{2}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{w}(t)^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{k}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt\}\,,

(2.8)

the
\inf
being taken over
w^{0}=(w^{0}(x)\,,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}),w=(w(t)\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})\in\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
and

k=((k(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1])\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})\in\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+}))
such that

w^{0}(0)=w^{0}(1)=0
,
w(0)=0
,
k(x,0)=k(0,t)=k(1,t)=0
,

w^{0}
,
w
and
k
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measures
on
[0,1]
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, respectively, and, for
all
t
, with
q_{0}^{-}=(-q_{0})^{+}
,

q(t)=(1-F(t))q_{0}^{+}-(1-F_{0}(t))q_{0}^{-}-\beta F_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}q(t-s)^{+}\,dF(s)+w^{0}(F_{0}(t))\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1-F(t-s)\bigr)\sigma\,\dot{w}(s)\,ds+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{s+x\leq t\}}\,\,\dot{k}(F(x),\mu s)\,dF(x)\,\mu\,ds\,,

(2.9)

where

\dot{w}^{0}(x)
,
\dot{w}(t)
and
\dot{k}(x,t)

represent the respective Radon–Nikodym derivatives.
If
w^{0}
,
w
and
k
as indicated do not exist, then

I^{Q}(q)=\infty
.
Consequently,
I^{Q}(q)=\infty
unless
q(t)
is continuous as the
righthand side of (
2.9
) is a continuous function of
t
.
(It is proved in Lemma
B.1
that if
F
is, in addition, absolutely
continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, then

q(t)
in (
2.9
) is absolutely continuous too.)
By Lemma B.1 in
Puhalskii and Reed
[
28
]
, the equation
in (
2.9
) has a unique solution for
q(t)

as an element of
\mathbb{L}_{\infty,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
.

Let the processes
X_{n}=(X_{n}(t),t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
be defined by

X_{n}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\bigl(\frac{Q_{n}(t)}{n}-1\bigr)\,.

(2.10)

The next theorem confirms a conjecture in Puhalskii

[

.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose, in addition, that

A_{n}
is a renewal process of rate
\lambda_{n}
.
Let

,

,

and
\sigma>0

Suppose that,

and

for rate

with deviation function

I_{q_{0}}(y)

, for

.
Suppose that

\bigl(\sqrt{n}/b_{n}(A_{n}(t)/n-\mu\rho_{n}t),t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\bigr)

Remark 2.1
.

In order that the LDP for
\bigl(\sqrt{n}/b_{n}(A_{n}(t)/n-\mu\rho_{n}t),t\geq 0\bigr)

[

the result follows.

The plan is being implemented next.
Let random variables
\zeta_{i}
be independent and uniform on
[0,1]
.
Define a centred and normalised sequential empirical process by

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,t)=\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\bigl(\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{i}\leq x\}}\,-x\bigr)

(3.15)

and let

\displaystyle B_{n}(x,t)=\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\bigl(\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{i}\leq x\}}\,-\int_{0}^{x\wedge\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\bigr)\,,

(3.16)

where
x\in[0,1]
.
It is a simple matter to check that

K_{n}(x,t)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{K_{n}(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy+B_{n}(x,t)\,,\;\;t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,,x\in[0,1]\,.

(3.17)

Let
K_{n}=\bigl((K_{n}(x,t),\,x\in[0,1]),t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\bigr)

and
B_{n}=\bigl((B_{n}(x,t),\,x\in[0,1]),t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\bigr)
.
Let
K=((K(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1])\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
represent a Kiefer idempotent
process
and let
B=((B(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1])\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
represent a Brownian sheet
idempotent process, both being canonical coordinate processes on

\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{2}))
endowed with deviability
\Pi
such that

K(x,t)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{K(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy+B(x,t)\,,\;\;t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,,x\in[0,1]\,.

(3.18)

(That the solution of (
3.18
) is a Kiefer idempotent process
follows from Lemma
A.1
.)

Lemma 3.1
.

If the convergence in (
2.12
) holds, then
the sequence of processes
(K_{n},B_{n})

LD converges at rate
b_{n}^{2}

in
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{2}))
to the idempotent processes
(K,B)
.

Proof.

The proof is modelled on the proof of Lemma 3.1 in Krichagina and
Puhalskii
[
18
]
, see also
Jacod and Shiryaev
[
15
, Chapter IX, §4c]
.
Given
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
let
K_{n}(t)=(K_{n}(x,t),\,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

B_{n}(t)=(B_{n}(x,t),\,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

K(t)=(K(x,t),\,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+})

and
B(t)=(B(x,t),\,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+})\,.

It is proved, first, that, for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}
,
the sequence

(K_{n}(t_{1}),B_{n}(t_{1})),(K_{n}(t_{2}),B_{n}(t_{2})),\ldots,(K_{n}(t_{k}),B_{n}(t_{k}))

LD converges
to

(K(t_{1}),B(t_{1})),(K(t_{2}),B(t_{2})),\ldots,(K(t_{k}),B(t_{k})))

in
\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
, as
n\to\infty
.
Since both the stochastic processes

((K_{n}(t),B_{n}(t))\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
and the idempotent
process
((K(t),B(t))\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
have independent increments in
t
,
it suffices to prove convergence of one–dimensional
distributions, so,
((K_{n}(x,t),B_{n}(x,t))\,,x\in[0,1])

and
((K(x,t),B(x,t))\,,x\in[0,1])
are worked with, with
t
being held fixed.
The process
(B_{n}(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1])

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration
with the jump measure

\mu^{n,B}([0,x],\Gamma)=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{1/(b_{n}\sqrt{n})\in\Gamma\}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{i}\leq x\}}\,\,,

the predictable jump measure

\nu^{n,B}([0,x],\Gamma)=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{1/(b_{n}\sqrt{n})\in\Gamma\}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{x\wedge\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}

and the predictable quadratic variation process

\langle B_{n}\rangle(x,t)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu^{n,B}(dy,du)=\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}n}\,\nu^{n,B}([0,x],\{1/(b_{n}\sqrt{n})\})=\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{x\wedge\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\\
=\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{b_{n}^{2}n}\,x+\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\,K_{n}(x,t)-\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\,B_{n}(x,t)\,,

(3.19)

where
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
It is shown next that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert B_{n}(x,t)\rvert>r)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}=0\,.

(3.20)

An exponential martingale device is applied, which will be used
repeatedly.
Since, the process

\exp\bigl(b_{n}^{2}B_{n}(x,t)-\int_{0}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{b_{n}^{2}u}-1-b_{n}^{2}u)\nu^{n,B}(dy,du)\bigr)\\
=\exp\bigl(b_{n}^{2}B_{n}(x,t)-(e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{x\wedge\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\bigr)

is a local martingale with respect to
x
, see, e.g., Lemma 4.1.1 on p.294 in
Puhalskii
[
26
]
, for any stopping time
\tau
,

E\exp\bigl(b_{n}^{2}B_{n}(\tau,t)-(e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\bigr)\leq 1\,.

(3.21)

Lemma 3.2.6 on p.282 in Puhalskii
[
26
]
implies that, for

r>0
and
\gamma>0
,

P(\sup_{x\in[0,1]}e^{b_{n}^{2}B_{n}(x,t)}\geq e^{b_{n}^{2}r})\leq e^{b_{n}^{2}(\gamma-r)}+P\bigl(\exp\bigl((e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\bigr)\geq e^{b_{n}^{2}\gamma}\bigr)\\
\leq e^{b_{n}^{2}(\gamma-r)}+e^{-b_{n}^{2}\gamma}E\bigl(\exp\bigl((e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}})\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\int_{0}^{\zeta_{i}}\frac{dy}{1-y}\bigr)\bigr)\\
\\
=e^{b_{n}^{2}(\gamma-r)}+e^{-b_{n}^{2}\gamma}\bigl(1-(e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-b_{n}/\sqrt{n})\bigr)^{-\lfloor nt\rfloor}\,,

with the latter equality holding for all
n
great enough because

e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-b_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to 0
.
Hence, assuming that
e^{b_{n}/\sqrt{n}}-1-b_{n}/\sqrt{n}\leq 1/2
,

P(\sup_{x\in[0,1]}e^{b_{n}^{2}B_{n}(x,t)}\geq e^{b_{n}^{2}r})^{1/b_{n}^{2}}\leq e^{(\gamma-r)}+e^{-\gamma}2^{\lfloor nt\rfloor/b_{n}^{2}}\,.

Since
n/b_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, it follows that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\sup_{x\in[0,1]}B_{n}(x,t)\geq r)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}=0\,.

(3.22)

A similar convergence
holds with
-B_{n}(x,t)
substituted for
B_{n}(x,t)
.
The limit in (
3.20
) has been proved.

(3.24)

By (

(3.26)

If extended past

,
the process

By (

.
A similar argument is used as in the proof of (
3.23
). Defining

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\\
+\sup_{x\in[1/2,1-\delta]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)+\sup_{x\in[1-\delta,1]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\\

+P(\sup_{u\in[1-\delta,1]}|K_{n}(u,t)|\geq\eta/2)\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\\

+P(\sup_{u\in[0,\delta]}|\overline{K}_{n}(u,t)|\geq\eta/2)\,.

(3.28)

Since the random variables
\zeta_{i}

[0,1]

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\leq 2\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)\\
+P(\sup_{u\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(u,t)|\geq\eta/2)\,.

(3.29)

Since

\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}\lvert K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)\rvert\leq 3\delta\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert K_{n}(u,t)\rvert+\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}\lvert B_{n}(x+y,t)-B_{n}(x,t)\rvert\,.

(3.30)

Similarly,

\sup_{x\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x,t)|\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\,\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert K_{n}(x,t)\rvert+\sup_{x\in[0,\delta]}|B_{n}(x,t)|\,.

By
(

), (
3.30
),
(

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}\\
\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(3\delta\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert K_{n}(u,t)\rvert\geq\eta/2)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}+\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\,\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert K_{n}(u,t)\rvert\geq\eta/4)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}\\
+\Pi(\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}\lvert B(x+y,t)-B(x,t)\rvert\geq\eta/2)+\Pi(\sup_{u\in[0,\delta]}|B(u,t)|\geq\eta/4)\,.

(3.32)

By (
3.23
),

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}P(\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}|K_{n}(x+y,t)-K_{n}(x,t)|\geq\eta)^{1/b_{n}^{2}}\\
\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\Pi(\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}\sup_{y\in[0,\delta]}\lvert B(x+y,t)-B(x,t)\rvert\geq\eta/2)+\lim_{\delta\to 0}\Pi(\sup_{u\in[0,\delta]}|B(u,t)|\geq\eta/4)\,.

(3.33)

Since the collections of sets

which concludes the proof of (

\{y\in\mathbb{E}:\,\Pi(y)\geq\gamma\}
are compact for all
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
(One can also refer to
\Pi
as a maxi–measure or an idempotent probability.)
A deviability is a tight set function in the sense that

\inf_{K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{E})}\Pi(\mathbb{S}\setminus K)=0
,
where
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{E})
stands for the collection of compact
subsets of
\mathbb{E}
.
If
\Xi
is a directed set and
F_{\xi}\,,\xi\in\Xi\,,
is a net of closed
subsets of
\mathbb{E}
that is nonincreasing with respect to the
partial order on
\Xi
by inclusion, then
\Pi(\cap_{\xi\in\Xi}F_{\xi})=\lim_{\xi\in\Xi}\Pi(F_{\xi})
.
A property pertaining to elements of
\mathbb{E}
is said to hold

\Pi
–a.e. if the value of
\Pi
of the set of elements that do not
have this property equals 0 .

Function
f
from
\mathbb{E}
to metric space

\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is called an idempotent
variable.
The idempotent distribution of the idempotent variable
f
is defined
as the set function
\Pi\circ f^{-1}(\Gamma)=\Pi(f\in\Gamma),\,\Gamma\subset\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
.
If
f
is the canonical idempotent variable defined by

f(y)=y
, then it has
\Pi

as the idempotent distribution. The continuous images of deviabilities are deviabilities, i.e.,
if
f:\,\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is continuous, then
\Pi\circ f^{-1}
defined by

\Pi\circ f^{-1}(E^{\prime})=\Pi(f^{-1}(E^{\prime}))
is a deviability on
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
, where
E^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, this property
extends to the situation where
f:\,\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is
strictly Luzin, i.e., continuous when restricted to the set

\{y\in\mathbb{E}:\,\Pi(y)\geq a\}
, for arbitrary
a\in(0,1]
.
Thus, the idempotent distribution of a strictly Luzin idempotent
variables is a deviability. More generally,
f
is said to be a Luzin
idempotent variable if the idempotent distribution of
f
is a deviability. If

f=(f_{1},f_{2})
, with
f_{i}
assuming values in
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{i}
,
then the (marginal)
idempotent distribution of
f_{1}
is defined by

\Pi^{f_{1}}(y^{\prime}_{1})=\Pi(f_{1}=y^{\prime}_{1})=\sup_{y:\,f_{1}(y)=y^{\prime}_{1}}\Pi(y)
.
The idempotent
variables
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are said to be independent if

{\Pi}(f_{1}=y^{\prime}_{1},\,f_{2}=y^{\prime}_{2})=\Pi(f_{1}=y^{\prime}_{1})\Pi(f_{2}=y^{\prime}_{2})
for all
(y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2})\in\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{1}\times\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{2}
,
so, the joint distribution is the product of the
marginal ones.
Independence of finite collections of idempotent variables is defined
similarly.

A sequence
Q_{n}

of probability measures on the Borel
\sigma
–algebra of
\mathbb{E}
is said to large
dviation (LD) converge at rate
r_{n}
to deviability
\Pi
if
for every bounded continuous non-negative function
f
on
\mathbb{E}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{E}}f(x)^{n}\,Q_{n}(dx)\right)^{1/r_{n}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{E}}f(x)\Pi(x).

Equivalently, one may require that

\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(\Gamma)^{1/r_{n}}=\Pi(\Gamma)
for every Borel set
\Gamma
such that

\Pi
of the interior of
\Gamma
and
\Pi
of the closure of
\Gamma
agree.
If the
Q_{n}
LD converge to
\Pi
, then

\Pi(y)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}(Q_{n}(B_{\delta}(y))^{1/r_{n}}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}(Q_{n}(B_{\delta}(y))^{1/r_{n}}
, for
all
y\in\mathbb{E}
, where
B_{\delta}(y)
represents the open ball of
radius
\delta
about
y
. (Closed balls may be used as well.)
The sequence
Q_{n}
is said to be exponentially tight of order
r_{n}
if

\inf_{K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{E})}\limsup_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(\mathbb{E}\setminus K)^{1/r_{n}}=0
. If the sequence
Q_{n}
is exponentially tight of order

r_{n}
, then there exists subsequence
Q_{n^{\prime}}
that LD
converges at rate
r_{n^{\prime}}
to a deviability. Any such deviability will be
referred to as a Large Deviation (LD) limit point of the
Q_{n}
.
Given
\tilde{E}\subset\mathbb{E}
, the sequence
Q_{n}
is said to be

\tilde{E}
–exponentially tight if it is exponentially tight and

\tilde{\Pi}(\mathbb{E}\setminus\tilde{E})=0
, for any LD limit point of

Q_{n}
.

It is immediate that
\Pi

is a deviability if and only if
I(x)=-\ln\Pi(x)
is a tight deviation
function, i.e.,
the sets
\{x\in\mathbb{E}:\,I(x)\leq\gamma\}
are compact for all

\gamma\geq 0
and
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{E}}I(x)=0
, and that the
Q_{n}
LD
converge to
\Pi
at rate
r_{n}
if and only if they
obey the LDP for rate
r_{n}
with deviation function

I
, i.e.,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}(1/r_{n})\,\ln Q_{n}(G)\geq-\inf_{x\in G}I(x)
, for all open sets
G
, and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(1/r_{n})\,\ln Q_{n}(F)\leq-\inf_{x\in F}I(x)
, for all closed sets
F
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let

to

in

\mathbb{E}^{\prime\prime}
.

Collection

The functions

Idempotent processes are said to be independent if they are

Since this paper deals with stochastic processes having
rightcontinuous trajectories with lefthand limits, the underlying

Suppose,

with metric

and has rightcontinuous trajectories

\Pi(\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})\setminus\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime}))=0

The method of finite–dimensional distributions for LD convergence of
stochastic processes is summarised in the next theorem,

23
]

mimics that in weak convergence theory.

Theorem A.2
.

If, for all tuples

coming from a dense subset of

\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
the sequence of

(X_{n}(t_{1}),\ldots,X_{n}(t_{l}))

\mathbb{R}^{l}

to
(X(t_{1}),\ldots,X(t_{l}))
and the sequence

is

\mathbb{C}
–exponentially tight of order

X
is a continuous path idempotent process and the sequence

X_{n}
LD converges in
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
at rate
r_{n}
to
X
.

Theorem A.3
.

Suppose
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is, in addition, complete and separable.
The sequence
X_{n}
is
\mathbb{C}
–exponentially tight of order
r_{n}

if and only if

1.

the sequence
X_{n}(t)
is exponentially tight of order
r_{n}
,
for all
t
from a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and,

2.

for all
\epsilon>0
and
L>0
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,L]}P(\sup_{s\in[0,\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(t+s),X_{n}(t))>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}=0\,.

(A.1)

Proof.

The necessity of the condition follows from the continuity of the
projection mapping and the continuous mapping principle.
The proof of sufficiency is standard and is included for
completeness.
Let, for
L>0
,
\delta>0
and
X
from the Skorohod space

\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
,

w_{L}(X,\delta)=\sup_{t,s\in[0,L]:\,\lvert t-s\rvert\leq\delta}d^{\prime}(X(t),X(s))\,.

(A.2)

Since

P(w_{L}(X_{n},\delta)>\epsilon)\leq P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\lfloor L/\delta\rfloor+1}\{3\sup_{t\in[i\delta,(i+1)\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(t),X_{n}(i\delta))>\epsilon\}\\
\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor L/\delta\rfloor+1}P(3\sup_{t\in[i\delta,(i+1)\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(t),X_{n}(i\delta))>\epsilon)\leq(\lfloor\frac{L}{\delta}\rfloor+1)\sup_{t\in[0,L]}P(\sup_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t))>\frac{\epsilon}{3})\,,

the hypotheses imply that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(w_{L}(X_{n},\delta)>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}=0\,.

(A.3)

Let also

w^{\prime}_{L}(X,\delta)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}=L:\\
t_{j}-t_{j-1}>\delta\end{subarray}}\,\,\max_{j=1,\ldots,k}\sup_{u,v\in[t_{j-1},t_{j})}d^{\prime}(X(u),X(v))\,.

(A.4)

Since
w^{\prime}_{L}(X,\delta)\leq w_{L}(X,2\delta)
, provided
\delta<L/2
,
by (
A.3
),

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(w_{L}^{\prime}(X_{n},\delta)>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}=0\,.

(A.5)

As each
X_{n}
is a member of the Skorohod space
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}P(w_{L}^{\prime}(X_{n},\delta)>\epsilon)=0\,,

so, on recalling (
A.5
),

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{n}P(w^{\prime}_{L}(X_{n},\delta)>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}=0\,.

(A.6)

Let
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}
represent a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

X_{n}(t_{1}),X_{n}(t_{2}),\ldots
are exponentially tight of order
r_{n}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is complete and separable, every probability
measure on
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is tight, so, it may be assumed that
there exist
compact subsets
K_{1},K_{2},\ldots
such that, for all

n
,

P(X_{n}(t_{i})\notin K_{i})^{1/r_{n}}<\frac{1}{2^{i}}\,,i\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(A.7)

Choose positive
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots
such that

P(w_{i}^{\prime}(X_{n},\delta_{i})>\frac{1}{2^{i}})^{1/r_{n}}<\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i}}\,,i\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(A.8)

The set

A=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\{X:\,w^{\prime}_{i}(X,\delta_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2^{i}}\,,X(t_{i})\in K_{i}\}

(A.9)

has a compact closure, see, e.g.,
Theorem A2.2 on p. 563 in Kallenberg
[
16
]
, and

P(X_{n}\notin A)^{1/r_{n}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(w_{i}(X_{n},\delta_{i})>\frac{1}{2^{i}})^{1/r_{n}}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(X_{n}(t_{i})\notin K_{i})^{1/r_{n}}<2\epsilon\,.

(A.10)

Thus, the sequence
X_{n}
is exponentially tight of order
r_{n}
in

\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
. Let
\Pi
represent a deviability on

\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
that is an LD limit point of the distributions
of
X_{n}
. It is proved next that
\Pi(X)=0
if
X
is a
discontinuous function. Suppose
X
has a jump at
t
, i.e.,

d^{\prime}(X(t),X(t-))>0
.
Let
\rho
denote a metric in
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
.
It may be assumed that
if
\rho(X^{\prime},X)<\delta
, then there exists a continuous nondecreasing
function
\lambda(t)
such that
\sup_{s\leq t+1}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(\lambda(s)),X(s))<\delta
and
\sup_{s\leq t+1}\lvert\lambda(s)-s\rvert<\delta
, see, e.g., Ethier and Kurtz
[
9
]
,

[
15
]
.
Then, assuming that
2\delta<t
and
\delta<1
,

\inf_{s^{\prime}\in[s-\delta,s+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X(s^{\prime}),X(s))<\delta
, where
s\in[0,t+1]
.
Note that

d^{\prime}(X(t),X(t-))\leq d^{\prime}(X(t),X^{\prime}(s_{1}))+d(X^{\prime}(s_{1}),X^{\prime}(t))+d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(t),X^{\prime}(s_{2}))+d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s_{2}),X(s_{3}))\\
+d^{\prime}(X(s_{3}),X(t-))

so that, with
s_{1}\in[t-\delta,t+\delta]
such that
d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s_{1}),X(t))<\delta
,
s_{3}\in[t-\delta,t]
such that
d^{\prime}(X(s_{3}),X(t-))<\delta
, and

s_{2}\in[s_{3}-\delta,s_{3}+\delta]
such that

d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s_{2}),X(s_{3}))<\delta
,

d^{\prime}(X(t),X(t-))<3\delta+2\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t+\delta]}d(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))\,,

which implies that, for
\delta
small enough,

\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))>\frac{d^{\prime}(X(t),X(t-))}{3}\,.

(A.11)

Since

\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))\leq\sup_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))+\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t-\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))\\
+\sup_{s\in[t-\delta,t]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))\leq\sup_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t))+\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t-\delta]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t-2\delta))\\
+\sup_{s\in[t-\delta,t]}d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(t-\delta))+d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(t-\delta),X^{\prime}(t-2\delta))+2d^{\prime}(X^{\prime}(t),X^{\prime}(t-\delta))\,,

for
\epsilon=d^{\prime}(X(t),X(t-))/18
,

P(\rho(X_{n},X)<\delta)\leq P(\sup_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t))>\epsilon)+P(\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t-\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t-2\delta))>\epsilon)\\
+P(\sup_{s\in[t-\delta,t]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t-\delta))>\epsilon)\,.

Therefore, assuming
L\geq t
and
r_{n}\geq 1
,

P(\rho(X_{n},X)<\delta)^{1/r_{n}}\leq P(\sup_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t))>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}+P(\sup_{s\in[t-\delta,t]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(s),X_{n}(t-\delta))>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}\\
+P(\sup_{s\in[t-2\delta,t-\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(t),X_{n}(t-2\delta))>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}\leq 3\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,L]}P(\sup_{s\in[0,\delta]}d^{\prime}(X_{n}(t^{\prime}+s),X_{n}(t^{\prime}))>\epsilon)^{1/r_{n}}\,.

Since

\Pi(X)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}P(\rho(X_{n},X)<\delta)^{1/r_{n}}\,,

(
A.1
) implies that
\Pi(X)=0

The discussion below concerns the properties of
the idempotent processes that feature prominently in the paper.
All the processes assume values in
\mathbb{R}
.

W=(W(t)\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})

with idempotent distribution

\Pi^{W}(w)=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{w}(t)^{2}\,dt\bigr)

(A.12)

provided
w=(w(t)\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})\in\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})

, and

\Pi^{W}(w)=0
otherwise. The restriction to
[0,t]
produces a

\Pi_{t}^{W}(w)=\exp\bigl(-1/2\,\int_{0}^{t}\dot{w}(s)^{2}\,ds\bigr)
.
The Brownian bridge idempotent process on
[0,1]
denoted by

W^{0}=(W^{0}(x),\,x\in[0,1])
is defined as an
idempotent process with the idempotent distribution

\Pi^{W^{0}}(w^{0})=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{w}^{0}(x)^{2}\,dx\bigr)

(A.13)

provided
w^{0}=(w^{0}(x)\,,x\in[0,1])\in\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
is absolutely continuous and
w^{0}(0)=w^{0}(1)=0
, and

\Pi^{W^{0}}(w^{0})=0
otherwise.
The Brownian sheet idempotent process on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
denoted by
(B(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1],\,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is defined as a
two–parameter idempotent process with the distribution

\Pi^{B}(b)=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\dot{b}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt\bigr)

(A.14)

provided
b=(b(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1]\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]
and
b(x,0)=b(0,t)=0
, and

\Pi^{B}(b)=0
otherwise.
The Kiefer idempotent process on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
denoted by

(K(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1]\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}),
is defined
as a two–parameter idempotent process with the idempotent distribution

\Pi^{K}(k)=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\dot{k}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt\bigr)

(A.15)

provided
k=(k(x,t)\,,x\in[0,1]\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]
and
k(0,t)=k(1,t)=k(x,0)=0
, and

\Pi^{K}(k)=0
otherwise.
In this paper we view it as an element of
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}_{+}))
.
Furthermore, as the deviabilities that

,
W^{0}
,

, or

have discontinuous paths are equal to zero, these
idempotent processes can be considered as having paths from

\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbb{C}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}([0,1],\mathbb{R}))

and

\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}([0,1],\mathbb{R}))

Being LD limits of their stochastic prototypes, these idempotent
processes have similar properties, as summarised in the next lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

1.

For
x>0
,
the idempotent process
(B(x,t)/\sqrt{x}\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a
standard Wiener idempotent process.

2.

For
t>0
,
(K(x,t)/\sqrt{t}\,,x\in[0,1])
is a Brownian bridge
idempotent process. For
x\in(0,1)
, the idempotent process

(K(x,t)/\sqrt{x(1-x)}\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a standard Wiener idempotent
process.

3.

The Kiefer idempotent process can be written as

K(x,t)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{K(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy+B(x,t)\,,

(A.16)

where
B(x,t)
is a Brownian sheet idempotent process. Conversely, if

B(x,t)
is a Brownian sheet idempotent process and (
A.16
)
holds, then
K(x,t)
is a Kiefer idempotent process.
Also,

W^{0}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{W^{0}(y)}{1-y}\,dy+W^{\prime}(x)\,,

(A.17)

where
W^{\prime}(x)
is a standard Wiener idempotent process.

Proof.

Parts 1 and 2 are elementary. For instance,

\Pi((\frac{K(x,t)}{\sqrt{t}}\,,x\in[0,1])=(w^{0}(x)\,,x\in[0,1]))\\
=\sup_{k:\,k(x,t)=\sqrt{t}w^{0}(x)\,,x\in[0,1]}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{k}(x,s)^{2}\,dx\,ds\bigr)\,.

An application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that
the optimal
k
is

k(x,s)=\frac{s\wedge t}{\sqrt{t}}\,w^{0}(x)\,.

As for
(K(x,t)/\sqrt{x(1-x)}\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
, the optimal trajectory

(k(y,t)\,,y\in[0,1])
to get to
w(t)\sqrt{x(1-x)}
at
x
is

k(y,t)=\begin{cases}w(t)\sqrt{x(1-x)}\,\displaystyle\frac{y}{x}\,,&\text{ if
}y\in[0,x]\,,\\
\\
w(t)\sqrt{x(1-x)}\,\displaystyle\frac{1-y}{1-x}\,,&\text{ if
}y\in[x,1]\,.\end{cases}

In order to prove part 3, it suffices to show that if

k(x,t)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{k(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy+b(x,t)\,,

(A.18)

with
k
and
b
being absolutely continuous and with
\Pi^{B}(b)>0
, then

k(1,t)=0
and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{k}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{b}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt\,.

(A.19)

Solving (
A.18
) yields, for
x<1
,

k(x,t)=(1-x)\int_{0}^{x}\frac{b_{y}(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy\,,

(A.20)

where
b_{y}(y,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{b}(s,y)\,ds
.
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\lvert\int_{0}^{x}\frac{b_{y}(y,t)}{1-y}\,dy\rvert\leq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{(1-y)^{2}}\,dy}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}b_{y}(y,t)^{2}dy}\leq\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{1-x}}\,\sqrt{\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\dot{b}(y,s)^{2}\,dy\,ds}\,.

(A.21)

We obtain that
k(x,t)\to 0
as
x\to 1
provided
\Pi^{B}(b)>0
.

Let
k_{t}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{x}\dot{k}(y,t)\,dy
. We show that if

\Pi^{B}(b)>0
, then, a.e.,

\frac{1}{1-x}\,k_{t}(x,t)^{2}\to 0\text{ as }x\to 1\,.

(A.22)

Given arbitrary
a>0
, by (
A.20
),

\frac{k_{t}(x,t)^{2}}{1-x}=(1-x)\Bigl(\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\dot{b}(y,t)\,dy}{1-y}\Bigr)^{2}\leq 2(1-x)a^{2}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dy}{1-y}\Bigr)^{2}\\
+2(1-x)\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dy}{(1-y)^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}\dot{b}(y,t)^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\lvert\dot{b}(y,t)\rvert>a\}}\,\,dy\leq 2(1-x)\lvert\ln(1-x)\rvert^{2}a^{2}+2\int_{0}^{x}\dot{b}(y,t)^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\lvert\dot{b}(y,t)\rvert>a\}}\,\,dy\,.

Since
\int_{0}^{1}\dot{b}(y,t)^{2}\,dy<\infty
a.e., the latter rightmost
side tends to
0
a.e. , as
x\to 1
and
a\to\infty
.

We are now in a position to prove (
A.19
).
Owing to (
A.18
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{b}(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\dot{k}(x,t)^{2}+2\dot{k}(x,t)\frac{k_{t}(x,t)}{1-x}+(\frac{k_{t}(x,t)}{1-x})^{2}\Bigr)\,dx\,dt\,.

(A.23)

Integration by parts with the account of (
A.22
) yields, for
almost all
t
,

\int_{0}^{1}\dot{k}(x,t)\frac{k_{t}(x,t)}{1-x}\,dx=-\int_{0}^{1}k_{t}(x,t)\bigl(\frac{\dot{k}(x,t)}{1-x}+\frac{k_{t}(x,t)}{(1-x)^{2}})\,dx

so that

\int_{0}^{1}\dot{k}(x,t)\frac{k_{t}(x,t)}{1-x}\,dx=-\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k_{t}(x,t)^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}}\,dx\,.

Recalling (
A.23
) implies (
A.19
).∎

Appendix B
A nonlinear renewal equation

This section is concerned with the properties of the equation

g(t)=f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(t-s)^{+}\,dF(s)\,,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,.

(B.1)

It is assumed that
f(t)
is a locally bounded measurable
function and
that
F(t)
is a continuous distribution function
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
F(0)<1
.
The existence and uniqueness of an essentially locally bounded
solution
g(t)
to (
B.1
) follows from Lemma B.2 in Puhalskii and
Reed
[
28
]
.

Lemma B.1
.

If the functions
f
and
F
are absolutely continuous with
respect to Lebesgue measure and
F(0)=0
, then the function
g
is absolutely
continuous too.

Proof.

Use Picard iterations. Let
g_{0}(t)=f(t)
and

g_{k}(t)=f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g_{k-1}(t-s)^{+}\,dF(s)\,.

(B.2)

The functions
g_{k}
are seen to be continuous. Let
\epsilon>0
,

T>0
and
0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{l}\leq T
.
Since
g_{k}\to g
locally uniformly, see Lemma B.1 in
Puhalskii and Reed
[
28
]
,
the function
g
is continuous and
\sup_{k}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lvert g_{k}(t)\rvert\leq M
, for some
M>0
.
Note that

\lvert\int_{0}^{t_{i}}g_{k-1}(t_{i}-s)^{+}\,dF(s)-\int_{0}^{t_{i-1}}g_{k-1}(t_{i-1}-s)^{+}\,dF(s)\rvert\\
\leq\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\lvert g_{k-1}(t_{i}-s)\rvert\,dF(s)+\int_{0}^{T}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{s\leq t_{i-1}\}}\,\lvert g_{k-1}(t_{i}-s)-g_{k-1}(t_{i-1}-s)\rvert\,dF(s)\,.

(B.3)

Let

\psi(\delta)=\sup_{0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{l}\leq T}\{\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lvert f(t_{i})-f(t_{i-1}\rvert+M\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lvert F(t_{i})-F(t_{i-1})\rvert:\,\sum_{l=1}^{l}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})\leq\delta\}

(B.4)

and

\phi_{k}(\delta)=\sup_{0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{l}\leq T}\{\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lvert g_{k}(t_{i})-g_{k}(t_{i-1})\rvert:\,\sum_{l=1}^{l}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})\leq\delta\}\,.

(B.5)

By (
B.2
) and (
B.3
), for
k\geq 1
,

\phi_{k}(\delta)\leq\psi(\delta)+\phi_{k-1}(\delta)F(T)\,.

(B.6)

Let

\phi(\delta)=\sup_{0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{l}\leq T}\{\lvert g(t_{i})-g(t_{i-1})\rvert:\,\sum_{l=1}^{l}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})\leq\delta\}\,.

(B.7)

Suppose that
F(t_{0})<1
. Since
g_{k}\to g
locally uniformly, as
k\to\infty
,

\phi_{k}(\delta)\to\phi(\delta)
.
Letting
k\to\infty
in (
B.6
) implies that
\phi(\delta)\leq\psi(\delta)/(1-F(t_{0}))
so that
\phi(\delta)\to 0
, as

\delta\to 0
. Hence,
g(t)
is absolutely continuous on
[0,t_{0}]
.
Next, as in Puhalskii and Reed
[
28
]
, write, for

t\in[0,t_{0}]
,

g(t+t_{0})=f(t+t_{0})+\int_{t}^{t+t_{0}}g(t+t_{0}-s)\,dF(s)+\int_{0}^{t}g(t+t_{0}-s)\,dF(s)\,.

By what has been proved, the sum of the first two terms on the
righthand side is an absolutely continuous function of
t
on

[0,t_{0}]
. The preceding argument implies that
g(t+t_{0})
is
absolutely continuous in
t
on
[0,t_{0}]
. Iterating the argument
proves the absolute continuity of
g(t)
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
∎
