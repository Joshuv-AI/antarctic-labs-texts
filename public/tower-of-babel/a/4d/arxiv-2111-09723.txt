Coproducts in Categories of q q -Matroids
Heide Gluesing-Luerssen, and Benjamin Jany

Abstract
q q -Matroids form the q q -analogue of classical matroids. In this paper we introduce various types of maps between q q -matroids. These maps are not necessarily linear, but they map subspaces to subspaces and respect the q q -matroid structure in certain ways. The various types of maps give rise to different categories of q q -matroids. We show that only one of these categories possesses a coproduct. This is the category where the morphisms are linear weak maps, that is, the rank of the image of any subspace is not larger than the rank of the subspace itself. The coproduct in this category is the very recently introduced direct sum of q q -matroids.



Keywords:

q
-matroid, representability, strong maps, weak maps, coproduct.

1
Introduction

Due to their close relation to rank-metric codes,
q
-matroids have gained a lot of attention in recent years.
They were introduced in
[
15
]
, and their generalization to
q
-polymatroids appeared first in

[
11
]
and
[
19
]
.
While
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
-linear rank-metric codes induce
q
-matroids,
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear rank-metric codes give rise to the
more general
q
-polymatroids.

A
q
-matroid is the
q
-analogue of a classical matroid:
Its ground space is a finite-dimensional vector space over some finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and the rank function is defined on the
lattice of subspaces. Its properties are the natural generalization of the rank function for classical matroids.

Even though the theory of
q
-matroids is still in the beginnings, many results have already been derived.
Most importantly, an abundance of cryptomorphisms have been worked out in
[
3
]
(and partly in
[
15
,
2
]
).
They may be regarded as the natural, yet non-trivial,
q
-analogues of the well-known cryptomorphisms for classical matroids.

In this paper we will discuss an aspect of
q
-matroids where the theory diverges significantly from that for classical matroids:
maps between
q
-matroids and the resulting categories.
Our study of maps and categories is motivated by the question of whether
q
-matroid constructions can be characterized (or in fact found) via
suitable universal properties; see
[
13
]
for the case of classical matroids.
We will see that this is indeed the case for the direct sum.

Just like for classical matroids we define maps between
q
-matroids as maps between the ground spaces that respect the
q
-matroid
structures in a particular way.
We take the most general approach and do not require the maps to be linear.
However, in order to be compatible with
q
-matroid structures, they have to map subspaces to subspaces.
In other words, they induce maps between the corresponding subspace lattices.
We call such maps
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps
.
As for classical matroids (see
[
20
, Ch. 8 and 9]
), there are various ways how
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps can respect the
q
-matroid structure.
We call an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
weak
if for every subspace the rank is greater than or equal to the rank of its image, and it is

rank-preserving
if we have equality for all subspaces.
An
{\mathcal{L}}
-map is
strong
if the pre-image of a flat is a flat.
In addition to these properties we will also pay attention to the special case where the
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps are linear.
The theory of
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps and maps between
q
-matroids will be worked out in
Section

3
.

It is well known that the direct sum of classical matroids is a coproduct in both the category with strong maps as
morphisms and the category with weak maps as morphisms; see
[
20
, Ex. 8.6, p. 244]
(which refers to
[
5
]
).
In
Section

4
we will show that — in contrast to the matroid case — the category of
q
-matroids with strong maps does not
admit a coproduct, and the same is true for the category with linear strong maps and the category
with weak maps (trivially, the category with linear or non-linear rank-preserving maps does not admit a coproduct).

The main positive result of this paper concerns the direct sum of
q
-matroids presented in
[
4
]
.
In
Section

5
we prove that the direct sum is a coproduct in the category with linear weak maps.
The appearance of
[
4
]
, which happened while the first version of this paper was in preparation, is a very
fortunate incidence because the main motivation for our approach has in fact been the
quest for a direct sum.
For
q
-matroids this is not a straightforward concept; in particular, none of the many ways of defining the direct sum of
classical matroids has a well-defined
q
-analogue.
Furthermore, the negative results mentioned in the previous paragraph suggest that any meaningful notion of direct sum of
q
-matroids
does not behave well with respect to strong maps, i.e., with respect to flats.

The fact that the direct sum in
[
4
]
does behave well with respect to linear weak maps may be interpreted as follows.
For
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
consider the collection of all
q
-matroids with ground space
E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
and whose restriction to
E_{i}
equals
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
.
The coproduct property implies that in this collection the direct sum is the unique
q
-matroid with the most independent spaces,
that is, the unique one with the “least amount of conditions”.
This is indeed a property any notion of direct sum should satisfy.
Therefore, together with the evidence provided in
[
4
]
, our paper contributes to settling on the right notion
of direct sum for
q
-matroids.
Further properties of the direct sum have been established in
[
10
]
, where,
among other things, it has been shown that every
q
-matroid decomposes into a direct sum of
indecomposable
q
-matroids in a unique way.

Finally, in the short
Section

6
we discuss categories of
q
-matroids where the morphisms are the maps between subspace lattices
induced by
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.
It will be shown that this notion of maps between
q
-matroids is too weak to produce meaningful results.

Notation:

Throughout, let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
E
be a finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space.
We denote by
{\mathcal{L}}(E)=({\mathcal{L}}(E),\leq,+,\cap)
the subspace lattice of
E
.
A
k
-space is a subspace of dimension
k
.
For a matrix
M\in{\mathbb{F}}^{a\times b}
we denote by
\mbox{\rm rs}(M)\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{b})
and
\mbox{\rm cs}(M)\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{a})
the row space
and column space of
M
, respectively.
We use the standard notation
[n]
for the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For clarity we use throughout the terminology
classical matroid
for the ‘non-
q
-version’ of matroids,
that is, matroids based on subsets of a finite ground set.

2
Basic Notions of
q
-Matroids

Definition 2.1
.

A
q
-matroid with ground space
E
is a pair
(E,\rho)
, where
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
is a map satisfying

(R1)

Dimension-Boundedness:
0\leq\rho(V)\leq\dim V
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R2)

Monotonicity:
V\leq W\Longrightarrow\rho(V)\leq\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R3)

Submodularity:
\rho(V+W)+\rho(V\cap W)\leq\rho(V)+\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

The value
\rho(V)
is called the
rank
of
V
and
\rho(M):=\rho(E)
is the
rank
of the
q
-matroid.
If
\rho
is the zero map, we call
(E,\rho)
the
trivial
q
-matroid on

E
.

A large class of
q
-matroids are the representable ones.

Definition 2.2

(
[
15
, Sec. 5]
)
.

Consider a field extension
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and let
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
be a matrix of rank
k
.
Define the map
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{n})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
via

\rho(V)={\rm rk}(GY\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$}),\ \text{ where $Y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{y\times n}$ such that $V=\mbox{\rm rs}(Y)$}.

Then
\rho
satisfies (R1)–(R3) and thus defines a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{G}=({\mathbb{F}}^{n},\rho)
, which has rank
k
.
We call
{\mathcal{M}}_{G}
the
q
-matroid
represented by
G
.
Furthermore, a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathbb{F}}^{n},\rho)
of rank
k
is
representable over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
if
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{G}
for some

G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
and it is
representable
if it is representable over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
for some
m
.

Not every
q
-matroid is representable. The first non-representable
q
-matroids appeared in
[
8
, Sec. 4]
.
However, the smallest non-representable
q
-matroid is the following one, found just recently in
[
4
]
.

Example 2.3

(
[
4
, Sec. 3.3]
)
.

Consider
E={\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4}
and let

{\mathcal{V}}=\{\mbox{$\langle{1000,\,0100}\rangle$},\mbox{$\langle{0010,\,0001}\rangle$},\mbox{$\langle{1001,\,0111}\rangle$},\mbox{$\langle{1011,\,0110}\rangle$}\}

(or any other partial spread of size
4
). Define
\rho(V)=1
for
V\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
\rho(V)=\min\{2,\dim V\}
otherwise.
It follows from
[
8
, Prop. 4.7]
that this does indeed define a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4},\rho)
.
In
[
4
, Sec. 3.3]
it has been shown that
{\mathcal{M}}
is a non-representable
q
-matroid.
In
[
8
, Sec. 4]
we prove that
{\mathcal{M}}
is not even representable over
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4\times m}
for any
m
in the sense of

[
8
, Def. 4.1]
.

We return to general
q
-matroids.
In
[
3
]
(and partly in
[
2
]
) a variety of cryptomorphic definitions of
q
-matroids have been derived.
We will need the one based on flats.
In order to formulate the corresponding results, we recall the following notions from lattice theory.
Let
({\mathcal{F}},\leq)
be any lattice. We denote by
F_{1}\vee F_{2}
and
F_{1}\wedge F_{2}
the
join
and
meet
of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
,
respectively.
We say that
F_{1}

covers

F_{2}
if
F_{2}<F_{1}
and there exists no
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
F_{2}<F<F_{1}
.
The lattice is
semi-modular
if it satisfies: whenever
F_{1}
covers
F_{1}\wedge F_{2}
, then
F_{1}\vee F_{2}
covers
F_{2}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
2
, Prop. 3.8 and Thm. 3.10]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid. For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
define the
closure
of
V
as

\overline{V}=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{\dim X=1}{\rho(V+X)=\rho(V)}}\hskip-11.99998ptX.

V
is called a
flat
if
\overline{V}=V
.
Thus
V
is a flat if and only if
\rho(V)<\rho(W)
for every subspace
W
strictly containing
V
.
Denote by
{\mathcal{F}}:={\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}
the collection of all flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Then

(F1)

E\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

(F2)

If
F_{1},F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

(F3)

For all
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
and all
1
-dimensional subspaces
X\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}(F)
, there exists a unique cover of
F
in
{\mathcal{F}}
containing
X
.

The converse result tells us that any collection of subspaces satisfying (F1)–(F3) gives rise to a
q
-matroid.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
2
, Cor. 3.11 and Thm. 3.13]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}(E)
be a collection of subspaces satisfying (F1)–(F3) from
2.4
.
For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
set
\overline{V}=\bigcap_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{F\in\cF}{V\subseteq F}}F
.

(a)

({\mathcal{F}},\subseteq)
is a semi-modular lattice with the meet and join of two flats given by

F_{1}\wedge F_{2}=F_{1}\cap F_{2}
and
F_{1}\vee F_{2}=\overline{F_{1}+F_{2}}
, respectively.

(b)

By semi-modularity all maximal chains between any two fixed elements in
{\mathcal{F}}
have the same length
(Jordan-Dedekind chain condition).

(c)

For any
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
denote by
\text{h}(F)
the length of a maximal chain from
0_{{\mathcal{F}}}
to
F
.
Define the map
\rho_{{\mathcal{F}}}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\text{h}(\overline{V})
. Then

(E,\rho_{{\mathcal{F}}})
is a
q
-matroid.

The above two processes are mutually inverse in the following sense.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
2
, Thm. 3.13]
)
.

(a)

Let
(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid. Then
\rho_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}}=\rho
.

(b)

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a collection of subspaces satisfying (F1)–(F3). Then
{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho_{{\mathcal{F}}}}={\mathcal{F}}
.

This last result allows us to define
q
-matroids based on their flats, denoted by
(E,{\mathcal{F}})
, or based on the rank function, denoted by
(E,\rho)
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\dim E
. We denote by
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)
the uniform
q
-matroid of rank
k
on
E
, that is, its rank function is given by

\rho(V)=\min\{k,\dim V\}
. The flats are given by
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E))=\{V\leq E\mid\dim V\leq k-1\}\cup\{E\}
.
Note that
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E)
is the trivial
q
-matroid on
E
.
It is well known (
[
15
, Ex. 31]
or
[
11
, Cor. 6.6]
) that
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n})
is representable over any

{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
with
m\geq n
: choose any
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
that generates an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
-linear MRD code (of rank distance

n-k+1
).

3
Maps Between
q
-Matroids

In this section we introduce maps between
q
-matroids.
The candidates for such maps are (possibly nonlinear) maps between the ground spaces of the
q
-matroids with the
property that they map subspaces to subspaces.
Such maps will be called
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.
By definition,
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps induce maps between the associated subspace lattices.
As a consequence, one may choose
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps or their induced maps as maps between
q
-matroids.
In
Section

6
we will briefly discuss the second option, while for now we focus on
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps
themselves.
As maps between
q
-matroids, they should respect the
q
-matroid structure.
This can be achieved in various ways, and we will introduce the options later in this section.

Throughout this section let
E_{1},E_{2}
be finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector spaces.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be a map.
We call
\phi
an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
if
\phi(V)\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.
The induced map from
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
to
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
is denoted by
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
.
A bijective
{\mathcal{L}}
-map is called an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism
.
Finally,
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps
\phi,\,\psi
from
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
are
{\mathcal{L}}
-
equivalent
, denoted by
\phi\sim_{{\mathcal{L}}}\psi
, if
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}=\psi_{\mathcal{L}}
.

An
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
is thus a possibly nonlinear map that maps
subspaces of
E_{1}
to subspaces of
E_{2}
.
It satisfies
\phi(0)=0
and

\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\mbox{$\langle{\phi(v)}\rangle$}\ \text{ for all }\ v\in E_{1}.

(3.1)

This follows from the fact that
\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})
is a subspace of cardinality at most
q
containing
0
and
\phi(v)
.
Our definition of
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphisms is justified by the following simple fact.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be a bijective
{\mathcal{L}}
-map. Then
\phi^{-1}
is also an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.
To see this, note that
\dim E_{1}=\dim E_{2}
by bijectivity of
\phi
and thus the subspace lattices

{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
and
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
are isomorphic.
Hence
\phi^{-1}
is also an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.

Recall that a map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
is
{\mathbb{F}}
-semilinear if
\phi
is additive and there exists
\sigma\in\mbox{\rm Aut}({\mathbb{F}})
such that

\phi(cv)=\sigma(c)\phi(v)
for all
v\in E_{1}
and
c\in{\mathbb{F}}
.
Clearly, any semi-linear map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.
Here are examples of non-semi-linear
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps and of
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent maps.
A more general construction of
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps will be given in
3.9
(b).

Example 3.3
.

(a)

Let
X\lneq E_{1}
be any subspace and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\in E_{1}
be such that

\mbox{$\langle{v_{1}}\rangle$},\ldots,\mbox{$\langle{v_{\ell}}\rangle$}
are the distinct lines in
E_{1}
that are not contained in
X
.
Choose
z\in E_{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Set

\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2},\quad v\longmapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0,&\text{if }v\in X,\\[2.15277pt]
\lambda z,&\text{if $v=\lambda v_{i}$ for some $i\in[\ell]$}.\end{array}\right.

Then
\phi(V)=\{0\}
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
and
\phi(V)=\mbox{$\langle{z}\rangle$}
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})\setminus{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.
Thus,
\phi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.
Furthermore, the pre-image of any subspace is a subspace.
Indeed, let
W\leq E_{2}
. Then
\phi^{-1}(W)=X
if
z\not\in W
and
\phi^{-1}(W)=E_{1}
if
z\in W
.
Note that
\phi
depends on the choice of the representatives
v_{i}
for the distinct lines.
One can easily create examples where
\phi
is not semi-linear.
(In fact, one can show that there always exist choices such that
\phi
is not semi-linear
unless
\dim E_{1}=1
or
[\dim X=\dim E_{1}-1
and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}]
.)

(b)

Let
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{3}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}
be given by
\phi(v_{1},v_{2},0)=(v_{1},v_{2})
and
\phi(v_{1},v_{2},1)=(0,0)
for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
.
Then
\phi
is a nonlinear
{\mathcal{L}}
-map. In this case, the pre-image of a subspace is not necessarily a subspace, for instance

\phi^{-1}(\{(0,0)\})
.

(c)

Clearly, if
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map, and
\psi=\lambda\phi
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
, then
\phi
and
\psi
are

{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.

(d)

Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{4}=\{0,1,\alpha,\alpha^{2}\}
and consider the semi-linear map
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{2},\ (v_{1},v_{2})\longmapsto(v_{1}^{2},v_{2}^{2})
.
Furthermore, let

\psi:{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{2},\quad(v_{1},v_{2})\longmapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\alpha(v_{1}^{2},v_{2}^{2}),&\text{if }(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mbox{$\langle{(1,1)}\rangle$},\\[2.15277pt]
(v_{1}^{2},v_{2}^{2}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

One easily verifies that
\psi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map and
\phi
and
\psi
are
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent.

We now discuss the relation between
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps from
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
and lattice homomorphism from

({\mathcal{L}}(E_{1}),\leq,+,\cap)
to
({\mathcal{L}}(E_{2}),\leq,+,\cap)
.
To do so, recall the following notions and simple facts.
For further details see for instance
[
18
, Ch. 3]
or
[
6
, Ch. 2]
.

Remark 3.4
.

Let
({\mathcal{L}}_{i},\leq_{i},\vee_{i},\wedge_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be two lattices and
\phi:{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
be a map.
Then
\phi
is a
lattice homomorphism
if it is meet- and join-preserving, that is,

\phi(a\wedge_{1}b)=\phi(a)\wedge_{2}\phi(b)
and
\phi(a\vee_{1}b)=\phi(a)\vee_{2}\phi(b)
for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
.
If each
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
has a least element
0_{i}
and a greatest element
1_{i}
, we call

\phi
a
\{0,1\}
-
lattice homomorphism
if it is a lattice homomorphism satisfying
\phi(0_{1})=0_{2}
and

\phi(1_{1})=1_{2}
.
Finally, we call
\phi

order-preserving
if
a\leq_{1}b
implies
\phi(a)\leq_{2}\phi(b)
for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
.
We have the following facts.

(a)

Every lattice homomorphism is order-preserving
[
6
, Prop. 2.19]
. Clearly, every
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
\phi
induces an order-preserving map
\phi_{{\mathcal{L}}}
.

(b)

Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map and
\phi(v_{1})=\phi(v_{2})\neq 0
for some linearly independent vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in E_{1}
.
Then
\phi_{{\mathcal{L}}}
is not meet-preserving and thus not a lattice homomorphism.
An example of such a map
\phi
is in
3.3
(a) if
\dim E_{1}>1
.

(c)

A lattice homomorphism need not be a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism.
Consider for instance the embedding
\tau:{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{3},\,(x,y)\longmapsto(x,y,0)
, and the map

\phi:{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{3})
given by
\phi(V)=\tau(V)
.

(d)

A lattice isomorphism is a
\{0,1\}
-lattice isomorphism.

(e)

A
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism need not be a lattice isomorphism. Consider, for instance,
\phi:{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4})
,
which maps
0
to
0
and
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4}
and the three 1-spaces in
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}
to the subspaces
\mbox{$\langle{1000,0100}\rangle$},\,\mbox{$\langle{0010,0001}\rangle$}
, and
\langle{1010,0101}\rangle
.

Note that the map in
3.4
(e) is not induced by an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map (because
\dim V<\dim\phi(V)
).
For maps induced by
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps we have some stronger statements.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map and
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}:{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
be the induced map.

(a)

If
\phi
is injective, then
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a lattice homomorphism.

(b)

Suppose
E_{2}\neq 0
. Then
\phi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism
\Longleftrightarrow\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism

\Longleftrightarrow\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a lattice isomorphism.

Note that if
E_{2}=0
, then the zero map induces a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism; thus the exceptional case in (b).

Proof.

(a) Let
V_{1},V_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
and
\hat{V}_{i}=\phi(V_{i})
. Then clearly
\phi(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\subseteq\hat{V_{1}}\cap\hat{V_{2}}
. For the converse containment let
\hat{v}\in\hat{V_{1}}\cap\hat{V_{2}}
.
Then there exist
v_{i}\in V_{i}
such that
\phi(v_{1})=\hat{v}=\phi(v_{2})
and injectivity implies
v_{1}=v_{2}\in V_{1}\cap V_{2}
.
This shows
\phi(V_{1}\cap V_{2})=\hat{V_{1}}\cap\hat{V_{2}}
and thus
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
is meet-preserving.
Next, we clearly have
\hat{V_{1}}+\hat{V_{2}}\subseteq\phi(V_{1}+V_{2})
and equality follows from

\dim(\hat{V_{1}}+\hat{V_{2}})=\dim\hat{V_{1}}+\dim\hat{V_{2}}-\dim(\hat{V_{1}}\cap\hat{V_{2}})=\dim V_{1}+\dim V_{2}-\dim(V_{1}\cap V_{2})=\dim(V_{1}+V_{2})=\dim\phi(V_{1}+V_{2})
. Thus
\phi
is join-preserving.

(b) Let
E_{2}\neq 0
. The only implication that remains to be proven is that if
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism then
\phi
is bijective.
Thus, let
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
be a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism.
By assumption
\phi(E_{1})=E_{2}
, and thus
\phi
is surjective and
\dim E_{2}\leq\dim E_{1}
.
Let
e_{i}=\dim E_{i}
. We have to show that
e_{1}=e_{2}
.
Assume by contradiction that
e_{2}<e_{1}
.
We proceed in several steps.

i) Clearly
\phi
maps
1
-spaces of
E_{1}
to subspaces of
E_{2}
of dimension
1
or
0
.
Suppose
\phi(V)=\phi(W)
for some 1-spaces
V\neq W
.
Then the properties of a
\{0,1\}
-lattice homomorphism imply
\phi(V)=\phi(V)\cap\phi(W)=\phi(V\cap W)=\phi(0)=0
.
Since
E_{i}
has
(q^{e_{i}}-1)/(q-1)
1-spaces, we conclude that at most
(q^{e_{2}}-1)/(q-1)
1-spaces of
E_{1}
are mapped to
1
-spaces, and
thus
\phi
maps at least
(q^{e_{1}}-q^{e_{2}})/(q-1)
1-spaces to
0
.

ii) Let now
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
be a maximal-dimensional subspace such that
\phi(V)=0
.
Then
V\neq E_{1}
since
E_{2}\neq 0
.
Let
\dim V=v
.
The number of 1-spaces in
V
is
(q^{v}-1)/(q-1)
.
Using
e_{2}\leq e_{1}-1
as well as
v\leq e_{1}-1
, we compute

\frac{q^{e_{1}}-q^{e_{2}}}{q-1}-\frac{q^{v}-1}{q-1}\geq\frac{q^{e_{1}}-q^{e_{2}}}{q-1}-\frac{q^{e_{1}-1}-1}{q-1}=q^{e_{1}-1}-\frac{q^{e_{2}}-1}{q-1}\geq 1.

Now i) tells us that there exists at least one
1
-space
W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
such that
W\not\leq V
and
\phi(W)=0
.
Now the join-preserving property implies
\phi(V+W)=\phi(V)+\phi(W)=0
, in contradiction to the maximality of
V
.
All of this shows that
e_{1}=e_{2}
and thus
\phi
is bijective.
∎

The
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphisms from
3.5
(b) are, up to
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalence, semi-linear maps.
This is a consequence of the Fundamental Theorem of Projective Geometry;
see for instance
[
1
, Ch. II.10]
or
[
17
, Thm. 1]
(for the interesting original source of this theorem, which is attributed to von Staudt, one
may also consult
[
12
]
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\dim E_{1}=\dim E_{2}=n
, where
n\geq 3
. Let
\tau:{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
be a lattice isomorphism.
Then there exists a semi-linear map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
such that
\tau=\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\dim E_{1}=\dim E_{2}=n
, where
n\geq 3
or
q=n=2
.
Let
\tau:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map and
suppose there exist vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of
E_{1}
such that

\tau(v_{1}),\ldots,\tau(v_{n})
are linearly independent.
Then
\tau
is bijective and there exists a semi-linear isomorphism
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}

such that
\tau\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\phi
.
In particular, if
q=2
, then
\tau=\phi
is a linear isomorphism.

Proof.

Since
\tau(E_{1})
is a subspace and contains
\tau(v_{1}),\ldots,\tau(v_{n})
, we conclude that
\tau(E_{1})=E_{2}
.
Hence
\tau
is a bijection.
For
n=2=q
it is clear that
\tau
is a linear isomorphism
(any nonzero vector in
E_{1}
or
E_{2}
is the sum of the other two nonzero vectors).
For
n\geq 3
we may use
3.6
.
∎

We return to general
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps and list some basic facts.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\phi,\psi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps such that
\phi\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\psi
.

(a)

For all
v\in E_{1}
there exist
\lambda_{v}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
such that
\phi(v)=\lambda_{v}\psi(v)
.

(b)

\phi^{-1}(V)=\psi^{-1}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
.

(c)

If
\psi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism, then so is
\phi
, and
\phi^{-1}\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\psi^{-1}
.

(d)

If
\psi
is injective (resp. surjective), then so is
\phi
.

(e)

If
\phi
and
\psi
are linear, then
\phi=\lambda\psi
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
.

Proof.

(a) It suffices to consider
v\neq 0
.
Note that
\phi(v)=0\Longleftrightarrow\psi(v)=0
, and in this case we may choose
\lambda_{v}=1
.
In the case
\psi(v)\neq 0\neq\phi(v)
the result follows from (
3.1
).

(b) For
v\in E_{1}
let
\lambda_{v}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
be as in (a).
For any
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
we have

\phi^{-1}(V)=\{v\in E_{1}\mid\phi(v)\in V\}=\{v\in E_{1}\mid\lambda_{v}\psi(v)\in V\}=\{v\in E_{1}\mid\psi(v)\in V\}=\psi^{-1}(V)
.

(c) If
\psi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism, then the lattices
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
and
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
are isomorphic (see
3.2
).
Next,
E_{2}=\psi(E_{1})=\phi(E_{1})
, and thus
\phi
is bijective as well.
Furthermore,
\psi_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
are lattice isomorphisms from
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
to
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
satisfying

(\psi^{-1})_{\mathcal{L}}=(\psi_{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}=(\phi_{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}=(\phi^{-1})_{\mathcal{L}}
.

(d) The statement about surjectivity is clear since
\phi(E_{1})=\psi(E_{1})
. For injectivity use (c) with
E_{2}=\psi(E_{1})
.

(e)
Let
W=\ker\psi
, hence also
W=\ker\phi
, and let
E_{1}=V\oplus W
.
Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
be a basis of
V
.
By (a) there exist
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
such that
\phi(v_{i})=\lambda_{i}\psi(v_{i})
for
i\in[r]
.
Furthermore,
\phi(v_{1}+v_{i})=\hat{\lambda}\psi(v_{1}+v_{i})
for some
\hat{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
.
Linearity implies
\psi(\lambda_{1}v_{1}+\lambda_{i}v_{i})=\phi(v_{1}+v_{i})=\hat{\lambda}\psi(v_{1}+v_{i})=\psi(\hat{\lambda}v_{1}+\hat{\lambda}v_{i})
, and injectivity of
\psi
on
V
yields
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{i}=\hat{\lambda}
for all
i\in[r]
.
Hence
\phi|_{V}=\hat{\lambda}\psi|_{V}
.
Now we obtain
\phi(v+w)=\phi(v)+0=\hat{\lambda}\psi(v)+0=\hat{\lambda}\psi(v)+\hat{\lambda}\psi(w)=\hat{\lambda}\psi(v+w)
for all

v\in V
and
w\in W
, and this proves the desired statement.
∎

The following results show how we may alter an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map without changing the induced lattice homomorphism.
Part (a) also shows that
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps are
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent if they agree on the 1-spaces.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\psi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.

(a)

Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be a map such that
\phi(0)=0
and
\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})
for all

v\in E_{1}
. Then
\phi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map and
\phi\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\psi
.

(b)

Suppose
\psi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism. Fix
w\in E_{1}\setminus 0
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
and set
\hat{w}=\psi^{-1}(\tau\psi(w))
.
Then
\mbox{$\langle{\hat{w}}\rangle$}=\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$}
.
Define the map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
via

\phi(v)=\psi(v)\ \text{ for }v\in E_{1}\setminus\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$},\quad\phi(\mu w)=\psi(\mu\hat{w})\ \text{ for }\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}.

Then
\phi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism and
\phi\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\psi
.
If
\tau=1
, then
\phi=\psi
.

Proof.

(a) is immediate from
\phi(V)=\bigcup_{v\in V}\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\bigcup_{v\in V}\psi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\psi(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.

(b) Bijectivity of
\psi
implies
\hat{w}\neq 0
and
\psi(\hat{w})=\tau\psi(w)
, thus

\psi(\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$})=\mbox{$\langle{\psi(w)}\rangle$}=\mbox{$\langle{\psi(\hat{w})}\rangle$}=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{\hat{w}}\rangle$})
.
This in turn implies
\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$}=\mbox{$\langle{\hat{w}}\rangle$}
.
The map
\phi
is clearly bijective and satisfies
\phi(0)=0
.
We show that
\phi
satisfies the condition of (a).
First let
v\not\in\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$}
. Then also
\lambda v\not\in\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$}
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
and

\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\{\phi(\lambda v)\mid\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}\}=\{\psi(\lambda v)\mid\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}\}=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})
.
Next, for
v=\mu w
with
\mu\neq 0
we have

\phi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\{\phi(\lambda\mu w)\mid\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}\}=\{\psi(\lambda\mu\hat{w})\mid\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}\}=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{\hat{w}}\rangle$})=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$})=\psi(\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})
.
Thus we may apply (a) and the statement follows.
∎

We now turn to
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps between
q
-matroids. There are different options for such a map to respect the
q
-matroid structure.
For (a) and (b) below we adopt the terminology known for classical matroids; see, e.g.
[
20
, Def. 8.1.1 and Def. 9.1.1]
.
Our notion of rank-preserving maps in (c), however, is different from rank-preserving weak maps for classical matroids:
the latter are weak maps that preserve the rank of the matroid; see
[
20
, p. 260]
.
The definition below will be convenient for us.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
be
q
-matroids with flats
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}:={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
.
Let
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map. We define the following
types
.

(a)

\phi
is a
strong map
from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
if
\phi^{-1}(F)\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})

for all
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
(this implies in particular that
\phi^{-1}(F)
is a subspace of
E_{1}
).

(b)

\phi
is a
weak map
from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
if
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))\leq\rho_{1}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.

(c)

\phi
is
rank-preserving
from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
if
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))=\rho_{1}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.

For any
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
we will also use the notation
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
This allows us to discuss its type.

Note that each of the types above are actually properties of the induced map
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
.
This raises the question as to whether one should define maps between
q
-matroids
(E_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(E_{2},\rho_{2})
as maps
(with certain properties) between the underlying subspace lattices
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
and
{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
.
However lattice homomorphisms are too restrictive as they exclude some non-injective maps (see also
3.4
(b)), while
simply order-preserving maps appear to be too general.
In
Section

6
we will briefly consider the setting where the maps are those induced by
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.
Note that the distinction of maps between the ground spaces versus maps between the subspace lattices does not occur for classical
matroids because a map on a set
S
is uniquely determined by its induced map on the subset lattice of
S
.

We return to
3.10
.
Clearly, the composition of maps of the same type is again a map of that type.
Furthermore,
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps are of the same type (see
3.8
(b) for strong maps).
Note, however, that if
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a bijective strong (resp. weak) map, then
\phi^{-1}:{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{1}

may not be strong (resp. weak):
take for instance the identity map
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}({\mathbb{F}}^{n})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{U}}_{k-1}({\mathbb{F}}^{n})
.
Being rank-preserving and being strong are not related:
there exist strong maps that are not rank-preserving (e.g., the identity map from any nontrivial
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
to the trivial
q
-matroid on
E
)
and there exist rank-preserving maps that are not strong (e.g.,
\phi:{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2},\ (x,y)\longmapsto(x,0)
and where

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are the
q
-matroids on
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}
of rank
1
with
\langle{e_{2}}\rangle
and
\langle{e_{1}+e_{2}}\rangle
as the unique flat of rank
0
, respectively).
Unsurprisingly, weak maps are in general not strong: take for instance the identity on
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4}
, which induces a weak,
but not strong map from
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{4})
to the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
from
2.3
.
However, it can be shown that – just like in the classical case
[
20
, Lemma 8.1.7]
– strong maps are weak.
This and other properties and characterizations of strong maps can be found in
[
14
]
. We do not need those facts in this paper.
The following simple result will suffice for our considerations.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
be
q
-matroids and
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism.
Consider
\phi
as a map from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Then

\phi\text{ and }\phi^{-1}\text{ are weak maps}\Longleftrightarrow\phi\text{ is rank-preserving }\Longleftrightarrow\phi\text{ and }\phi^{-1}\text{ are strong maps.}

Proof.

Recall from
3.2
that
\phi^{-1}
is also an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map.
The first equivalence is clear. Consider the second equivalence.

“
\Rightarrow
” Let
\phi
be a rank-preserving isomorphism. Then
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))=\rho_{1}(V)
and
\dim\phi(V)=\dim V
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.
Using
2.4
we conclude that
V
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
iff
\phi(V)
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

“
\Leftarrow
” Let now
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
be strong maps.
Then

\phi({\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}))={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
, that is,
\phi
induces an isomorphism between the lattices of flats
of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Using the height function on these lattices (see
2.5
(c) and
2.6
(a))
we conclude that
\rho_{1}(F)=\rho_{2}(\phi(F))
for all
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})
and thus
\rho_{1}(V)=\rho_{2}(\phi(V))
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.
Hence
\phi
is rank-preserving.
∎

Just like there exist linear maps between any vector spaces (over the same field), there exist weak and strong maps between any
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

Example 3.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids.

(a)

The zero map
\phi_{0}
from
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
is a weak map from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. It is also a strong map since
\phi_{0}^{-1}(W)=E_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})
for all
W\leq E_{2}
.

(b)

Let
X=\{x\in E_{1}\mid\rho_{1}(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=0\}
, that is,
X
is the closure of
\{0\}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
in the sense of
2.4
.
In particular,
X\leq E_{1}
.
Let
z\in E_{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
As in
3.3
(a) choose vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\in E_{1}\setminus X
such that
\mbox{$\langle{v_{1}}\rangle$},\ldots,\mbox{$\langle{v_{\ell}}\rangle$}
are the distinct lines in
E
that are not in
X
and
consider the map
\phi
as in that example.
Then
\phi
is a weak map. Indeed, for any
V\leq X
we have
\rho_{1}(V)=0=\rho_{2}(\{0\})=\rho_{2}(\phi(V))
, while for

we have
\rho_{1}(V)\geq 1\geq\rho_{2}(\mbox{$\langle{z}\rangle$})=\rho_{2}(\phi(V))
.
Moreover, the pre-images in

(a) show that
\phi
is strong.
Note that if

, i.e.,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is the trivial

-matroid, then

is the zero map.

We now turn to minors of
q
-matroids and determine the type of the corresponding maps.
For restrictions and contractions of
q
-matroids, discussed next, see for instance
[
8
, Def. 5.1 and Thm. 5.2]
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and let
X\leq E
.

(a)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}|_{X}
be the restriction of
{\mathcal{M}}
to
X
.
Then the embedding
\iota:X\longrightarrow E,\ x\longmapsto x
, is a linear strong and rank-preserving (hence weak)
map from
{\mathcal{M}}|_{X}
to
{\mathcal{M}}
.

(b)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}/X
be the contraction of
X
from
{\mathcal{M}}
. Then the projection
\pi:E\longrightarrow E/X,\ x\longmapsto x+X
is a
linear strong and weak map from
{\mathcal{M}}
to
{\mathcal{M}}/X
.

Proof.

(a)
Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}|_{X}=(X,\hat{\rho})
, where
\hat{\rho}(V)=\rho(V)
for all
V\leq X
.
This shows that
\iota
is rank-preserving.
Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Then
\iota^{-1}(F)=F\cap X
and
\overline{F\cap X}^{{\mathcal{M}}}\subseteq\overline{F}^{{\mathcal{M}}}=F
, where
\overline{A}^{{\mathcal{M}}}

denotes the closure of the space
A
in the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
(for the closure see
2.4
).
Thus for any
v\in X
the identity
\rho((F\cap X)+\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})=\rho(F\cap X)
implies
v\in F
.
Hence
\overline{F\cap X}^{{\mathcal{M}}|_{X}}=F\cap X
and thus
\iota^{-1}(F)\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{X})
.

(b) Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}/X=(E/X,\tilde{\rho})
, where
\tilde{\rho}(\pi(V))=\rho(V+X)-\rho(X)
.
Thus by submodularity
\tilde{\rho}(\pi(V))\leq\rho(V)-\rho(V\cap X)
, showing that
\pi
is weak.
In order to show that
\pi
is strong, let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}/X)
. Thus

\tilde{\rho}(F+\mbox{$\langle{v+X}\rangle$})>\tilde{\rho}(F)
for all
v+X\in(E/X)\setminus F
.
Since
\pi^{-1}(F+\mbox{$\langle{v+X}\rangle$})=\pi^{-1}(F)+\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$}
, this implies
\rho(\pi^{-1}(F)+\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$})>\rho(\pi^{-1}(F))
for all

Restricting an

-map to its image does not change its type.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let

Definition 4.7
.

Let

be objects in

A coproduct of

It is well-known (and straightforward to verify) that if

and

and

\phi:M\longrightarrow\hat{M}

is also a coproduct of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.
Furthermore, every coproduct of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is of this form.

Our first result narrows down the ground space of a putative coproduct in an expected way.

Note that the maps in

b) Let

5
A Coproduct in
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}

In this section we establish the existence of a coproduct in the category
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}
.
In fact, we will show that the direct sum, introduced in
[
4
, Sec. 7]
is such a coproduct.
For the sake of self-containment we will first give a concise description of the direct sum and provide the necessary proofs.
The description, different from the original one in
[
4
]
, will make the presentation consistent with this paper and be helpful for proving the existence of a coproduct.
Alternative proofs of the statements below can be found at
[
4
, Prop. 22, Prop. 26, Def. 40]
.

We need to recall the notions of independent spaces and circuits of a
q
-matroid. Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid.
Then
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is
independent
if
\rho(V)=\dim V
and
dependent
otherwise.
A dependent space all of whose proper subspaces are independent is called a
circuit
.
We refer to
[
3
, Def. 7 and 14]
for the properties of the collections of independent spaces and the collection of circuits of a
q
-matroid.
We will only need the following two facts:
(a) every subspace of an independent space is independent;
(b) for every
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
there exists an independent space
I\leq V
such that
\rho(I)=\rho(V)
.

We start with the following result taken from
[
7
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
7
, Thm. 3.9 and its proof]
)
.

Let
\tau:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be a map satisfying (R2), (R3) from
2.1
.
Define
r_{\tau}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\min\{\tau(X)+\dim V-\dim X\mid X\in{\mathcal{L}}(V)\}
.
Then
r_{\tau}
satisfies (R1)–(R3) and thus defines a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,r_{\tau})
.
Furthermore, a subspace
V
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if
\tau(W)\geq\dim W
for all
W\in V
.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
4
, Sec. 7]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and set
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Let
\pi_{i}:E\longrightarrow E_{i}
be the corresponding projection onto
E_{i}
.
Define
\rho^{\prime}_{i}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\rho_{i}(\pi_{i}(V))
for
i=1,2
.
Then
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}=(E,\rho^{\prime}_{i})
is a
q
-matroid.
Furthermore, let

\rho:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\min\{\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)+\dim V-\dim X\mid X\in{\mathcal{L}}(V)\}.

(5.1)

Then
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
is a
q
-matroid.
It is called the
direct sum of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
The collection of circuits of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is given by

{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})=\bigg\{C\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\,\bigg|\,\begin{array}[]{l}C\text{ is inclusion-minimal subject to the}\\
\text{condition }\rho^{\prime}_{1}(C)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(C)\leq\dim C-1\end{array}\bigg\}.

Proof.

1) We show that
\rho^{\prime}_{1}
is indeed a rank function. Using that
\rho_{1}
is a rank function,
we have
0\leq\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V))\leq\dim\pi_{1}(V)\leq\dim V
, and this shows (R1). (R2) is trivial.
For (R3) let
V,W\in E
. Note first that
\pi_{1}(V\cap W)\subseteq\pi_{1}(V)\cap\pi_{1}(W)
. Thus

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{1}(V+W)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V+W))=\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V)+\pi_{1}(W))

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V))+\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(W))-\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V)\cap\pi_{1}(W))

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V))+\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(W))-\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(V\cap W))=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(V)+\rho^{\prime}_{1}(W)-\rho^{\prime}_{1}(V\cap W).

2) For the fact that
\rho
is a rank function consider first the function
\tau(V)=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(V)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(V)
.
It is clearly non-negative and one straightforwardly
shows that it satisfies (R2), (R3) from
2.1
; see also
[
4
, Thm. 28]
.
Hence
5.1
implies that
\rho
is a rank function and that the collection of circuits is as stated.
∎

The
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are naturally embedded in the direct sum.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
4
, Sec. 7]
)
.

Let the data be as in
5.2
and in particular
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Let
\iota_{i}
be as in (
4.1
). Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i})
. Then

\rho^{\prime}_{i}(\iota_{i}(V))=\rho_{i}(V)=\rho(\iota_{i}(V))\ \text{ and }\ \rho^{\prime}_{j}(\iota_{i}(V))=0\text{ for }j\neq i.

As a consequence,
\iota_{i}
induces linear rank-preserving isomorphisms between
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
and

{\mathcal{M}}|_{\iota_{i}(E_{i})}
and
({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i})|_{\iota_{i}(E_{i})}
.

Proof.

\rho^{\prime}_{i}(\iota_{i}(V))=\rho_{i}(V)
follows from
\pi_{i}(\iota_{i}(V))=V
and
\rho^{\prime}_{j}(\iota_{i}(V))=0
is clear because
\pi_{j}(\iota_{i}(V))=0
.
We show next the identity
\rho^{\prime}_{i}(\iota_{i}(V))=\rho(\iota_{i}(V))
for
i=1
.
Choose
X\leq\iota_{1}(V)
and write
\iota_{1}(V)=X\oplus Y
. Using that
\rho^{\prime}_{1}
is a rank function, we obtain

\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\iota_{1}(V))\leq\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{1}(Y)\leq\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\dim Y=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\dim\iota_{1}(V)-\dim X=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)+\dim\iota_{1}(V)-\dim X
,
where the last step follows from the fact that
X\in\iota_{1}(E_{1})
, hence
\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)=0
.
All of this shows that the minimum in (
5.1
) is
attained by
\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\iota_{1}(V))
, and therefore
\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\iota_{1}(V))=\rho(\iota_{1}(V))
.
The consequence is clear.
∎

Thanks to the above we may and will from now on identify subspaces
V
in
E_{i}
with their image
\iota_{i}(V)
.
Note that by the above,
E_{i}
is a loop space of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{j}
for
j\neq i
(by definition a loop space is a space with rank
0
).
The process from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{1}
is called
adding a loop space
in
[
4
]
.
It is a special instance of the direct sum because
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}\cong{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E_{j})
for
i\neq j
.

We wish to mention that the direct sum also satisfies
({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})/E_{i}\cong{\mathcal{M}}_{j}
for
j\neq i
.
This has been shown in
[
4
, Cor. 48]
with the aid of the identity

\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})=\rho(V_{1})+\rho(V_{2})
for all subspaces
V_{i}\leq E_{i}
; see
[
4
, Thm. 47]
.

We now turn to our main result stating that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a coproduct in
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}
.
We need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),i=1,2
, be
q
-matroids and
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
be a linear map.
Suppose
\phi
is not a weak map.
Then there exists a circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
such that

\rho_{2}(\phi(C))>\rho_{1}(C),\quad\dim C=\dim\phi(C),\quad\phi(C)\text{ is an independent space in~${\mathcal{M}}_{2}$.}

(5.2)

Proof.

Since
\phi
is not weak there exists an inclusion-minimal subspace
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
such that
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))>\rho_{1}(V)
.
Clearly
V\neq 0
.
We will show that
V
is the desired circuit and proceed in several steps.

1) We first establish the following identities

\rho_{1}(W)=\rho_{1}(V)=\rho_{2}(\phi(W))=\rho_{2}(\phi(V))-1\text{ for all $W\leq V$ with $\dim W=\dim V-1$}.

(5.3)

Let
W\leq V
with
\dim W=\dim V-1
. Write
V=W\oplus X
, thus
X
is a 1-space.
Then
\phi(V)=\phi(W)+\phi(X)
and therefore
\dim\phi(V)\leq\dim\phi(W)+1
.
Furthermore, by minimality of
V
we have
\rho_{2}(\phi(W))\leq\rho_{1}(W)
.
Using the properties of rank functions, we obtain

\rho_{2}(\phi(W))\leq\rho_{1}(W)\leq\rho_{1}(V)<\rho_{2}(\phi(V)),

(5.4)

and thus
\phi(W)\lneq\phi(V)
, which means
\dim\phi(V)=\dim\phi(W)+1
, and in fact

\phi(V)=\phi(W)\oplus\phi(X)
. This implies
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))\leq\rho_{2}(\phi(W))+1
.
Together with (
5.4
) this yields
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))=\rho_{2}(\phi(W))+1
as well as

\rho_{2}(\phi(W))=\rho_{1}(W)=\rho_{1}(V)
.
This establishes (
5.3
).

2) We show that
\phi|_{V}
is injective. Assume to the contrary that there exists
v\in V\setminus 0
such that
\phi(v)=0
.
Then
V=W\oplus\mbox{$\langle{v}\rangle$}
for some subspace
W
of
V
and thus
\phi(V)=\phi(W)
.
But this contradicts (
5.3
). Hence
\phi|_{V}
is injective and
\dim V=\dim\phi(V)
.

3) We show that
\phi(V)
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. Assume to the contrary that
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))<\dim\phi(V)
.
Then there exists an independent subspace
I\lneq\phi(V)
such that
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))=\rho_{2}(I)=\dim I
.
Since
\phi|_{V}
is injective, there exists a subspace
J\lneq V
such that
\phi(J)=I
and
\dim J=\dim I
.
Now we have
\dim J=\dim I=\rho_{2}(I)\leq\rho_{1}(J)\leq\dim J
, where the first inequality follows from the
minimality of
V
subject to
\rho_{2}(\phi(V))>\rho_{1}(V)
.
Thus we have equality throughout, which shows that
J
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
.
Furthermore, since
J\lneq V
there exists a subspace
W\lneq V
with
\dim W=\dim V-1
such that
J\leq W\lneq V
.
Applying
\phi
we arrive at
\rho_{2}(I)\leq\rho_{2}(\phi(W))\leq\rho_{2}(\phi(V))=\rho_{2}(I)
, and we have equality throughout.
But this contradicts (
5.3
) and therefore
\phi(V)
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

4) It remains to show that
V
is a circuit. (
5.3
) together with 2) and 3) implies that
for every hyperplane
W
of
V
we have

\rho_{1}(W)=\rho_{1}(V)=\rho_{2}(\phi(V))-1=\dim\phi(V)-1=\dim V-1=\dim W
.
This shows that
V
is dependent and
W
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
.
Since
W
was an arbitrary hyperplane of
V
, we conclude that
V
is a circuit.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=(E,\rho)
be the direct sum as defined in
5.2
.
Let
\iota_{i}
be as in (
4.1
).
Then
({\mathcal{M}},\iota_{1},\iota_{2})
is a coproduct of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in the category
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}
.

Proof.

First of all, thanks to
5.3
the maps
\iota_{i}:{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
are rank-preserving, thus weak.
Let
{\mathcal{N}}=(\tilde{E},\tilde{\rho})
be a
q
-matroid and
\alpha_{i}:{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}
be linear weak maps.
We have to show the existence of a unique linear weak map
\epsilon:{\mathcal{M}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}
such that
\epsilon\circ\iota_{i}=\alpha_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
Since
{\mathcal{M}}
has ground space
E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
it is clear that the only linear map satisfying
\epsilon\circ\iota_{i}=\alpha_{i}
is given by

\epsilon(v_{1}+v_{2})=\alpha_{1}(v_{1})+\alpha_{2}(v_{2})
for all
v_{i}\in E_{i}
; recall that we identify
E_{i}
with
\iota_{i}(E_{i})
.
Thus it remains to show that this map
\epsilon
is weak.
We will use
Lemma

5.4
.
Choose any circuit
C
in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Denote by
\rho^{\prime}_{i}
and
\pi_{i}
the maps as in
5.2
and set
X_{i}=\pi_{i}(C)
.
Then
C\leq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
and, since
X_{i}\leq E_{i}
, we obtain

\epsilon(C)\leq\epsilon(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})=\epsilon(X_{1})+\epsilon(X_{2})=\alpha_{1}(X_{1})+\alpha_{2}(X_{2})
.
Applying the rank function
\tilde{\rho}
and using the weakness of the maps
\alpha_{i}
, we compute

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(\epsilon(C))

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\rho}(\alpha_{1}(X_{1})+\alpha_{2}(X_{2}))\leq\tilde{\rho}(\alpha_{1}(X_{1}))+\tilde{\rho}(\alpha_{2}(X_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{1}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}(X_{2})=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(C)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(C)\leq\dim C-1,

where the last step follows from
5.2
because
C
is a circuit.
This shows that
\epsilon(C)
is not an independent space of
{\mathcal{N}}
with the same dimension as
C
.
Thus, no circuit in
{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies (
5.2
), and this shows that
\epsilon
is weak.
∎

Example 5.6
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and consider the
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}={\mathcal{U}}_{1}({\mathbb{F}}^{2})
,
i.e.,
\rho_{1}(V)=\rho_{2}(V)=\min\{1,\dim V\}
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{2})
.
The direct sum
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
has been determined in
[
4
, Ex. 48]
using the definition of
its rank function in (
5.1
).
In this example we will derive the same result by making use of the fact that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a coproduct in
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}
.
Let
\omega
be a primitive element of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{4}}
.
Then
G=(1\ \omega^{2})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{4}}^{1\times 2}
represents
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Consider
G^{(2)}
as in
4.5
and let
{\mathcal{N}}^{(2)}=({\mathbb{F}}^{4},\rho^{(2)})
be the
q
-matroid generated by
G^{(2)}
.
Thanks to
4.5
we have

\rho^{(2)}(V)=\min\{2,\dim V\}\text{ for }V\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{4})\setminus\{T_{1},T_{2}\}\text{ and }\rho^{(2)}(T_{1})=\rho^{(2)}(T_{2})=1,

where
T_{1},\,T_{2}
are as in
4.5
.
Furthermore,
4.4
provides us with the linear rank-preserving, hence weak, maps
\iota_{i}:{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}^{(2)}
for

i=1,2
.
As a consequence, the map
\epsilon:{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}^{(2)}
from the previous proof is the
identity map on
{\mathbb{F}}^{4}
. It thus induces a weak map from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
to
{\mathcal{N}}^{(2)}
, and this means
that the rank function
\rho
of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
satisfies

\rho(V)\geq\min\{2,\dim V\}\text{ for }V\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{4})\setminus\{T_{1},T_{2}\}\text{ and }\rho(T_{1})\geq 1,\,\rho(T_{2})\geq 1.

Using that
\iota_{i}
are also rank-preserving maps from
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
to
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
, see
5.3
, we obtain that

\rho(T_{1})=\rho(T_{1})=1
and
\rho({\mathbb{F}}^{4})=\rho(\iota_{1}({\mathbb{F}}^{2})\oplus\iota_{2}({\mathbb{F}}^{2}))\leq\rho(\iota_{1}({\mathbb{F}}^{2}))+\rho(\iota_{2}({\mathbb{F}}^{2}))=\rho_{1}({\mathbb{F}}^{2})+\rho_{2}({\mathbb{F}}^{2})=2
.
This implies that
\rho=\rho^{(2)}
and thus
{\mathcal{N}}^{(2)}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

We close this section with the following characterization of the direct sum.

Remark 5.7
.

The fact that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is a coproduct in
q
-
Mat
{}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\sf l-w}}
can be translated into the following
characterization of the direct sum.
For any finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space
E
define the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{E}=\{\rho\mid\rho\text{ is a rank function on }E\}
and the
partial order
\rho\leq\rho^{\prime}:\Longleftrightarrow\rho(V)\leq\rho^{\prime}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
Let now
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and set
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Then the set
\hat{{\mathcal{S}}}=\{\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}\mid\rho|_{E_{i}}\leq\rho_{i}\}
has a unique maximum, say
\hat{\rho}
,
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=(E,\hat{\rho})
.

6
q
-Matroids with
{\mathcal{L}}
-Classes

In this short section we discuss a different approach to maps between
q
-matroids.
Since the
q
-matroid structure is based on subspaces and different
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps may induce the same map on subspaces (i.e., are

{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent), one may define maps between
q
-matroids as maps between subspace lattices induced by
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.
Precisely, for an
{\mathcal{L}}
-map
\phi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
define the
{\mathcal{L}}
-class
as

[\phi]=\{\psi:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}\mid\psi\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\phi\}
.
Then

[\phi]:{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2}),\,V\longmapsto\phi(V),

(6.1)

is well-defined (and equals
\phi_{\mathcal{L}}
).
Since the type of a map (see
3.10
) is invariant under
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalence, this gives rise to strong, weak, and rank-preserving
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes between
q
-matroids.
Furthermore, we call an
{\mathcal{L}}
-class
linear
, if it contains a linear
{\mathcal{L}}
-map (not all maps in a linear
{\mathcal{L}}
-class are linear since by
3.9
we may tweak a linear map into an
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent nonlinear
{\mathcal{L}}
-map).
Setting
[\phi_{1}]\circ[\phi_{2}]:=[\phi_{1}\circ\phi_{2}]
, which is indeed well-defined,
we obtain categories

\mbox{$q$-{Mat}}^{[\Delta]}\text{ for }\Delta\in\{\textsf{s, rp, w, l-s, l-rp, l-w}\},

in which the morphisms are
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes of the specified type.
Being isomorphic or linearly isomorphic in the sense of
4.2
does not change when moving to
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes.

It turns out that
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes are not better behaved than
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps.
The following result suggests that in fact
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps, rather than
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes, are the appropriate notion of maps between
q
-matroids.

Theorem 6.1
.

None of the categories
q
-
Mat
{}^{[\Delta]},\,\Delta\in\{\textsf{s, rp, w, l-s, l-rp, l-w}\},
has coproduct except for the case

(q,[\Delta])=(2,[\textsf{l-w}])
.

The proof is for the most part straightforward, but tedious. We provide a sketch.

Sketch of Proof.

In essence one follows the proofs of Sections
4
and
5
and replaces
{\mathcal{L}}
-maps by their
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes.
Consequently, equality of maps (which is pointwise) needs to be replaced by equality of
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes on all 1-spaces
(see
3.9
(a)).
With this in mind, one verifies the following.

1) The proof of
4.8
reducing coproducts to the form
({\mathcal{M}},[\iota_{1}],[\iota_{2}])
carries through without additional changes.

2)
\Delta\in\{\textsf{s, rp, w}\}
. The proof of
4.10
also generalizes without additional changes.

3)
\Delta\in\{\textsf{l-s, l-rp}\}
. The proof of
4.9
needs a bit more care.
With the strategy described above we arrive at statement (
4.8
), which now reads as

for all
j\in\Omega
there exists a type-
[\Delta]
map
[\epsilon_{j}]
such that
[\epsilon_{j}\circ\iota_{i}]=[\iota_{i}]
for
i=1,2
.

Thus
[\epsilon_{j}](\mbox{$\langle{(v_{1},0)}\rangle$})=\mbox{$\langle{(v_{1},0)}\rangle$}
and
[\epsilon_{j}](\mbox{$\langle{(0,v_{2})}\rangle$})=\mbox{$\langle{(0,v_{2})}\rangle$}

for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{2}
and all
j\in\Omega
.
Since the
{\mathcal{L}}
-class
[\epsilon_{j}]
is linear,
3.8
(e) implies that, without loss of generality, there exist
a_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
such that

\epsilon_{j}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\mbox{\rm diag}(1,1,a_{j},a_{j})\text{ for all }(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{4},

where
\mbox{\rm diag}(1,1,a_{j},a_{j})
is the
4\times 4
-diagonal matrix with the specified diagonal entries.
In particular,
\epsilon_{j}
need not be the identity and in Diagram (
4.9
) we have potentially different maps

[\epsilon_{j}]:{\mathcal{M}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}^{(j)}
for each
j\in\Omega
.
Note that each
\epsilon_{j}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-isomorphism.
Now we consider the two types of
4.9
.

a) Let
\Delta=\textsf{l-rp}
.
Then
{\mathcal{M}}
is linearly isomorphic to each
{\mathcal{N}}^{(j)}
, contradicting
4.5
(d).

b) Let
\Delta=\textsf{l-s}
.
Consider the
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{N}}^{(j)}=({\mathbb{F}}^{4},\rho^{(j)})
.
We claim that

\rho^{(j)}(V)=\rho^{(j)}([\epsilon_{j}](V))
.
Indeed, let
V=\mbox{\rm rs}(Y)
for some
Y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{y\times 4}
.
Then
\epsilon^{(j)}(V)=\mbox{\rm rs}(Y\mbox{\rm diag}(1,1,a_{j},a_{j}))
.
The definition of
G^{(j)}
implies that
G^{(j)}\mbox{\rm diag}(1,1,a_{j},a_{j})=\mbox{\rm diag}(1,a_{j})G^{(j)}
, and thus

\rho^{(j)}(\epsilon_{j}(V))={\rm rk}\big(G^{(j)}\mbox{\rm diag}(1,1,a_{j},a_{j})Y\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$}\big)={\rm rk}\big(\mbox{\rm diag}(1,a_{j})G^{(j)}Y\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$}\big)={\rm rk}\big(G^{(j)}Y\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$}\big)=\rho^{(j)}(V).

With the aid of (
4.3
) we conclude that
[\epsilon_{j}]({\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}}^{(j)}))={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}}^{(j)})
for all
j\in\Omega
.
Now we are ready to return to the proof of
4.9
and specifically to the set

{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j\in\Omega}{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}}^{(j)})
.
We claim that
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Indeed, let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
.
Then
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}}^{(j)})
for some
j\in\Omega
and thus
[\epsilon_{j}](F)\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}}^{(j)})
.
Since
[\epsilon_{j}]
is strong, we conclude that
F=[\epsilon_{j}^{-1}]([\epsilon_{j}](F))
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Now the rest of the proof of
4.9
, starting at (
4.10
), generalizes to
{\mathcal{L}}
-classes as described above, and
leads to a contradiction.

4)
(q,\Delta)=(2,\textsf{l-w})
.
Note that for
q=2
linear maps are
{\mathcal{L}}
-equivalent if and only if they are equal (see
3.8
(e)).
Therefore the result from
5.5
remains valid for
q
-
Mat
{}^{[\textsf{l-w}]}
if
q=2
.

5)
q>2
and
\Delta=\textsf{l-w}
.
Following the proof of
5.5
, we see that the existence of a coproduct implies the existence of a unique
{\mathcal{L}}
-class

[\epsilon]
satisfying
[\epsilon]\circ[\iota_{i}]=[\alpha_{i}]
for
i=1,2
.
However, choosing the linear weak map
\epsilon
as in that proof, we can now take any linear map

\epsilon^{\prime}
such that
\epsilon^{\prime}|_{E_{1}}=\lambda_{1}\epsilon|_{E_{1}}
and
\epsilon^{\prime}|_{E_{2}}=\lambda_{2}\epsilon|_{E_{2}}
for some

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{*}
and obtain a linear weak map satisfying
[\epsilon^{\prime}]\circ[\iota_{i}]=[\alpha_{i}]
for
i=1,2
.
Choosing
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
we obtain
[\epsilon^{\prime}]\neq[\epsilon]
, which proves that the uniqueness of the map
[\epsilon]

fails. Therefore
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is not a coproduct of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}

and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in
q
-
Mat
{}^{[\textsf{l-w}]}
.
A similar reasoning shows that the two
q
-matroids do not have any coproduct in
q
-
Mat
{}^{[\textsf{l-w}]}
.

\square

Open Problems

(1)

In
[
9
]
it was shown that the direct sum of two representable
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is not necessarily representable.
However, if
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is representable, then it must represented via a block diagonal matrix
G:=\text{diag}(G_{1},G_{2})
, where

G_{i}
represents
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
. Establishing representability of
q
-matroids is a notoriously difficult problem.
It is therefore interesting to ask if the property of the direct sum established in
5.7
can help understand when a direct sum is representable?

(2)

Of course, many other categorical questions can be posed, such as the existence of products, equalizers, pullbacks and pushouts.
The article
[
13
]
, which discusses properties of the category of matroids with strong maps, appears to be a very useful guideline.
Considering the results of this paper, one can expect further significant differences to the case of classical matroids.
