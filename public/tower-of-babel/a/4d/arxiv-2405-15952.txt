Theoretical guarantees for lifted samplers
Philippe Gagnon, Florian Maire

Abstract
Lifted samplers form a class of Markov chain Monte Carlo methods which has drawn a lot attention in recent years due to superior performance in challenging Bayesian applications. A canonical example of lifted samplers is the one that is derived from a random walk Metropolis algorithm for a totally-ordered state space such as the integers or the real numbers. The lifted sampler is derived by splitting into two the proposal distribution: one part in the increasing direction, and the other part in the decreasing direction. It keeps following a direction, until a rejection occurs, upon which it flips the direction. In terms of asymptotic variances, it outperforms the random walk Metropolis algorithm, regardless of the target distribution, at no additional computational cost. Other studies show, however, that beyond this simple case, lifted samplers do not always outperform their Metropolis counterparts. In this paper, we leverage the celebrated work of Tierney (1998) to provide an analysis in a general framework encompassing a broad class of lifted samplers. Our finding is that, essentially, the asymptotic variances cannot increase by a factor of more than 2, regardless of the target distribution, the way the directions are induced, and the type of algorithm from which the lifted sampler is derived (be it a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, a reversible jump algorithm, etc.). This result indicates that, while there is potentially a lot to gain from lifting a sampler, there is not much to lose.

1
Introduction

In the context of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), a technique known as
lifting
is applied to induce directions to follow by a Markov chain. It dates back at least to
Horowitz (1991)
. In the 90’s, it also appeared in
Gustafson (1998)
(yielding what was referred to as the
guided walk
) and
Chen et al. (1999)
. It consists of a (simple) modification of a MCMC algorithm and an extension of a state-space
\mathcal{X}
, where each state
x\in\mathcal{X}
is duplicated and combined with a
direction

\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
. The
lifted
state-space is thus
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
and the resulting class of sampling algorithms is referred to as
lifted samplers
. A canonical example of a lifted sampler arises in the situation where the technique is applied to a random walk Metropolis–Hastings (MH,
Metropolis et al. (1953)
and
Hastings (1970)
) algorithm and
\mathcal{X}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, with
n
being a positive integer. Let us say that the random walk MH algorithm is used to sample from
\pi
, referred to as the
target distribution
in a sampling context, which is a probability distribution defined on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{X})
, where in this case
\mathcal{X}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
corresponds to the support of
\pi
,
\mathsf{X}
being a sigma-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
. In this situation, the random walk MH algorithm first proceeds with a proposal
y=x+\nu
with
\nu\sim\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
(except at the boundary, in which case a proposal outside of
\mathcal{X}
is replaced by
x
), where
\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
denotes the uniform distribution over the set
\{-1,+1\}
. This is followed by an accept/reject step in which the proposal is accepted with probability

1\wedge\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)},

where
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
. If the proposal is accepted, the next state of the Markov chain is
y
. In the case of a rejection, the Markov chain remains at
x
. The resulting Markov chain is
reversible
and exhibits a random walk behaviour. Such a behaviour may lead to significant auto-correlation in the Markov chain and an inefficiency of the MCMC estimators, often measured by the
asymptotic variances
.

By inducing directions to follow, the goal of the lifting technique is to generate persistent movement and thus to lose this random walk behaviour, thereby decreasing the auto-correlation and gaining efficiency. In the canonical situation described above, applying the lifting technique to the random walk MH algorithm produces a lifted sampler which proceeds, when at a current state
(x,\nu)\in\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
, with a
deterministic
proposal
y=x+\nu
(except at the boundary) which is accepted with the same probability as above, that is
1\wedge\pi(y)/\pi(x)
. If the proposal is accepted, the next state of the Markov chain is
(y,\nu)
. In the case of a rejection (or when at the boundary), the next state of the Markov chain is
(x,-\nu)
. The Markov chain thus keeps following a direction, until a rejection occurs, upon which the direction is flipped. The lifting technique can be seen as a way to equip the resulting stochastic process with some memory of its past (the direction it comes from), while retaining the Markov property (on the extended state-space). The resulting Markov chain is
non-reversible
and satisfies a
skew detailed balance
(a formal definition is presented in the following), crucially ensuring that the product measure
\pi\otimes\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
is an invariant distribution. The lifted sampler is as easy to implement on a computer as its MH counterpart and it has the same computational complexity, as is often the case with lifted samplers. In a situation which is essentially the same as the canonical one described above,
Diaconis et al. (2000)
proved that lifting the random walk MH algorithm significantly decreases the mixing time, from quadratic to linear, when
\pi=\mathcal{U}\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The lifting technique has drawn a lot attention in recent years due to success stories in challenging sampling problems (see, e.g.,
Okabe et al. (2001)
,
Sakai and Hukushima (2016a)
,
Sakai and Hukushima (2016b)
,
Vucelja (2016)
,
Power and Goldman (2019)
,
Herschlag et al. (2020)
,
Faizi et al. (2020)
,
Neal (2020)
,
Gagnon and Doucet (2021)
,
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
,
Syed et al. (2022)
,
Kamatani and Song (2023)
,
Biron-Lattes et al. (2024)
and
Ascolani and Zanella (2025)
). For instance, in the context of Bayesian nested model selection,
Gagnon and Doucet (2021)
proved that the lifted version of the reversible jump algorithm
(
Green, 1995
;
Green, 2003
)
is better. The result holds for any target distribution
\pi
, provided that the reversible jump algorithm with which the comparison is made explores the model space via a random walk. In
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
, a general result is proved. It states that any lifted sampler is better than a specific reversible counterpart, the latter corresponding to the version of the lifted sampler in which the direction variable
\nu\sim\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
is resampled at the beginning of each iteration. This specific reversible counterpart corresponds to the algorithm with which we wish to compare in the canonical situation described above and in
Gagnon and Doucet (2021)
, but it is often not the case. A motivation of our work is to go beyond this specific situation and to provide a theoretical result when the algorithm with which we wish to compare is
not
this specific reversible counterpart.

When going beyond this specific situation, one may encounter instances where lifted samplers are not better; see, e.g., Appendix A in
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
where the simulation of an Ising model is used to evaluate the performance of a MH algorithm and its lifted version. A partial order between the system states is exploited to induce directions to follow by the lifted sampler. It is shown that the lifted sampler outperforms its MH counterpart when the target distribution is not too concentrated and thus allows for persistent movement, whereas it is the opposite when the target distribution is too concentrated. The lifted sampler in
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
can be used to simulate any system formed from binary variables and thus finds application in Bayesian variable selection. This work focuses on comparing MCMC algorithms for finite state-spaces through a weaker version of the Peskun ordering
(
Peskun, 1973
)
.

With a counter-example like that in
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
, it becomes important to evaluate what can be the degree of inefficiency of lifted samplers relatively to the go-to reversible MCMC methods, given the versatility of the lifting technique and the appealing features of the resulting samplers. For instance, in the simulation of Ising models and in Bayesian variable selection, what can be the degree of inefficiency? And what about in Bayesian nested model selection when the point of comparison is the reversible jump algorithm which explores the model space via an
asymmetric
random walk? An asymmetric random walk proposes to subtract or add 1, in this case to the model indicator variable, but with different probabilities. It appears frequently in the reversible jump algorithm (see, e.g., the multiple change-point example in
Green (1995)
).

The main contribution of our work is to provide an answer to the aforementioned questions under arguably the most general framework. We proceed by leveraging the seminal work of
Tierney (1998)
to define a lifted version of a generalized MH algorithm in the context of general state-spaces. Virtually any (reversible) MCMC method can be seen as a special case of this generalized MH algorithm (possibly up to minor adjustments), ranging from the traditional MH algorithm to the reversible jump algorithm. Other popular special cases include multiple-try Metropolis
(
Liu et al., 2000
)
, pseudo-marginal
(
Beaumont, 2003
)
and simulated tempering
(
Geyer and Thompson, 1995
)
. Our main theoretical result allows for a comparison between the generalized MH algorithm and its lifted version in terms of asymptotic variances. It essentially guarantees that the asymptotic variance of MCMC estimators produced by the lifted version cannot be more than twice that of estimators produced by the generalized MH algorithm. This result indicates that,
while there is potentially a lot to gain from applying the lifting technique, there is not much to lose
. We also show that our result is optimal. The definition of the lifted version of the generalized MH algorithm allows to understand how a lifted sampler can be constructed under such a general framework, which adds a methodological contribution to our theoretical contribution.

The efficiency of MCMC algorithms is traditionally assessed by studying the characteristics of their Markov transition operators. To establish our main theoretical result, we need to connect the efficiency of two significantly different operators (those of the generalized MH and lifted algorithms): in addition to not be defined on the same domain, one is self-adjoint while the other is not, which further complicates the analysis. Another contribution of this work is to identify a specific auxiliary operator which acts a bridge and allows to connect the efficiency of the two aforementioned operators. This auxiliary operator is compared to the MH one through a Peskun-type ordering
(
Peskun, 1973
;
Tierney, 1998
)
established via a careful analysis of the Markov kernels, yielding sharp bounds. A key ingredient of this analysis is
Lemma 1
which is of independent interest as it states important properties of the class of functions
\varphi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
verifying
\varphi(r)=r\varphi(1/r)
for all
r\in(0,\infty)
that appeared in several sampling contexts, for instance as efficient
balancing functions
in
Zanella (2020)
. The connection between the generalized MH and lifted algorithms is completed by comparing the auxiliary operator with the lifted one using the result in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
.

We finish this section with a description of how the rest of the article is organized. In
Section 2
, we present the generalized MH algorithm, and define the generalized auxiliary and lifted samplers, each in a different sub-section, and our main theoretical result follows in the final sub-section. In
Section 3
, we make the framework of
Section 2
more concrete by presenting the special case corresponding to the traditional MH framework. The traditional MH algorithm is explicit and simple, and the same is true for the auxiliary and lifted samplers. In
Section 3
, within the concrete framework of that section, empirical results are also presented for an illustration of our main theoretical result. In particular, we revisit the Ising model example of
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
. As mentioned, in that paper, a methodology is introduced for applying the lifting technique in the context of partially-ordered finite state-spaces. Another contribution of this work is to highlight that this methodology is applicable in the general framework of
Section 2
whenever a partial order exists. The article finishes with a conclusion in
Section 4
.

2
MH and lifted algorithms: A general definition and comparison

In
Section 2.1
, we present the generalized MH algorithm, following
Tierney (1998)
. Essentially any
\pi
-reversible MCMC algorithm which proceeds with a proposal distribution
Q(x,\cdot\,)
and an accept/reject step can be seen as a special case of this generalized MH algorithm;
\pi
and
Q(x,\cdot\,)
thus do not necessarily admit densities with respect to a common dominating measure. From the generalized MH algorithm, we introduce the generalized auxiliary sampler in
Section 2.2
, which in turn is used to define the generalized lifted sampler in
Section 2.3
. In
Section 2.4
, we present the Peskun-type ordering, leading to our theoretical guarantees.

2.1
Generalized MH algorithm

Let
\pi
, the target, be a probability distribution on
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{X})
and
\{Q(x,\cdot\,):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
a collection of (proposal) probability distributions on
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{X})
. Let
\mu
and
\mu^{T}
be two probability measures on the product space
(\mathcal{X}^{2},\mathsf{X}^{\otimes 2})
defined as:

\mu(A\times B)=\int_{A}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q(x,B)\,,\qquad\mu^{T}(A\times B)=\int_{B}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q(x,A).

Also, let
\eta:=\mu+\mu^{T}
. From these definitions, it can be concluded that both
\mu
and
\mu^{T}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\eta
and that, by the Radon–Nikodym theorem, there exist two (essentially unique) measurable functions
h:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
and
h^{T}:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
such that

A\times B\in\mathsf{X}^{\otimes 2},\quad\mu(A\times B)=\int_{A\times B}h(x,y)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),\quad\mu^{T}(A\times B)=\int_{A\times B}h^{T}(x,y)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),

with the usual notation
\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\eta:=h
and
\mathrm{d}\mu^{T}/\mathrm{d}\eta:=h^{T}
.

The following proposition from
Tierney (1998)
provides a characterization of the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mathrm{d}\mu^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu
and an implicit form of it under the general framework of this section. This Radon–Nikodym derivative will then play a crucial role in the acceptance probability of the generalized MH Markov kernel, guaranteeing its
\pi
-reversibility.

Proposition 1

(
Tierney (1998)
)
.

1.

The function
h^{T}
is such that
h^{T}(x,y)=h(y,x)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
.

2.

The set

R:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}:h(x,y)>0\quad\text{and}\quad h(y,x)>0\},

is symmetric.

3.

The probability measures
\mu
and
\mu^{T}
are mutually absolutely continuous on
R
and mutually singular on the complement of
R
,
R^{\mathsf{c}}
.

4.

Let
r:R\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a function that verifies
r(x,y)=(\mathrm{d}\mu^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu)(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in R
. Then,

\displaystyle r(x,y)=\frac{h(y,x)}{h(x,y)},\quad\text{for all $(x,y)\in R$},

(1)

with
0<r(x,y)<\infty
and
r(x,y)=1/r(y,x)
for all
(x,y)\in R
.

Let
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
be the generalized MH kernel, defined for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
as

P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha(x,y)+\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\int(1-\alpha(x,u))\,Q(x,\mathrm{d}u),

where
\alpha:\mathcal{X}^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
is the acceptance probability.

The following theorem from
Tierney (1998)
states necessary conditions on the function
\alpha
for
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
to be
\pi
-reversible.

Theorem 1

(
Tierney (1998)
)
.

The Markov kernel
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
is
\pi
-reversible if and only if the following condition holds
\mu
-almost everywhere:

\alpha(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r(x,y)\,\alpha(y,x)&\text{if}\quad(x,y)\in R,\cr 0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(2)

In this paper, we restrict our attention to the case where
\alpha
is defined as follows:

\alpha(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi(r(x,y))&\text{if $(x,y)\in R$},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(3)

with
\varphi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
verifying
\varphi(r)=r\varphi(1/r)
for all
r\in(0,\infty)
. Such a function
\varphi
allows to verify the condition in
Theorem 1
: for
(x,y)\in R
,

\alpha(x,y)=\varphi(r(x,y))=r(x,y)\,\varphi(1/r(x,y))=r(x,y)\,\varphi(r(y,x))=r(x,y)\,\alpha(y,x),

by Result 4 of
Proposition 1
. The term
r(x,y)
in (
3
) can be seen as a
summary statistic
, in the sense that the dependence on
(x,y)
only appears through
r(x,y)
in the acceptance probability. Nevertheless, it is sufficient, when combined with a function
\varphi
, given that the condition in
Theorem 1
is satisfied. The class of functions
\varphi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
verifying
\varphi(r)=r\varphi(1/r)
for all
r\in(0,\infty)
allows for the usual acceptance probability
\varphi(r)=1\wedge r
and Barker’s acceptance probability
\varphi(r)=r/(1+r)

(
Barker, 1965
)
. From
Tierney (1998)
, we know that
\varphi(r)=1\wedge r
yields the optimal acceptance probability (in the sense of minimizing the asymptotic variances).

This class of functions has interesting properties, as stated in
Lemma 1
, which will be useful to prove our Peskun-type ordering in
Section 2.4
.

Lemma 1
.

For any function
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to[0,1]
verifying
\varphi(r)=r\varphi(1/r)
for all
r
, we have that:

1.

\varphi
is non-decreasing,

2.

\varphi
is continuous on
(0,\infty)
,

3.

\varphi(r)\downarrow 0
when
r\downarrow 0
,

4.

\varphi
is either a null function, that is
\varphi(r)=0
for all
r
, or it is a positive function,

5.

for any
a>0,b>0
,
\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
,

6.

for any
a>0
,
b\in(0,1]
,
\varphi(ab)\geq b\varphi(a)
.

The proof of
Lemma 1
is technical and thus deferred to
Appendix A
.

What can be difficult in practice to implement a generalized MH algorithm is the identification of explicit forms for the function
r
and the set
R
(when available). A strategy to identify an explicit form for the function
r
is to directly calculate the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mathrm{d}\mu^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu
, which represents a contribution of the articles introducing MCMC algorithms such as multiple-try Metropolis, pseudo-marginal, reversible jump and simulated tempering. Another strategy is to exploit the results in
Andrieu et al. (2020)
.

2.2
Generalized auxiliary sampler

We now introduce the generalized auxiliary sampler, constructed from the generalized MH algorithm presented in
Section 2.1
. The generalized auxiliary sampler will in turn be used to construct the generalized lifted sampler in
Section 2.3
. The presentation of the generalized auxiliary and lifted samplers consists essentially of definitions; it will allow to understand how a lifted sampler can be constructed under the general framework of
Section 2
.

The generalized auxiliary sampler uses a notion of direction, but does not exploit it; it will be seen to correspond to the version of the generalized lifted sampler which resamples the direction variable
\nu\sim\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
at the beginning of each iteration. As mentioned in
Section 1
, it acts as a bridge between the generalized MH algorithm and the generalized lifted sampler, allowing a comparison between the MCMC estimators in terms of asymptotic variances.

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define two measurable sets
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
which will be referred to as
directional neighbourhoods
. The collection of sets is assumed to satisfy two properties.

Assumption 1
.

(i)

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q(x,A)=Q(x,A\cap\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))+Q(x,A\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))
for all
A\in\mathsf{X}
.

(ii)

For any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
and
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
,
y\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)

if and only if

x\in\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y)
, and for such a related couple
(x,y)
,
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))>0
and
Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))>0
.

Let us consider the situation where
Q(x,\cdot\,)
has a support given by, say,
\mathrm{N}(x)
; in this situation, the sets
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
can be constructed by splitting into two
\mathrm{N}(x)
(this is what is done in our illustration in
Section 3
). It is always possible to perform such a split when the state-space exhibits either a total or a partial order (because
\mathrm{N}(x)
can be formed of states that can all be compared to
x
via the total or partial order). In the examples in
Section 3
, the state-space is either totally or partially ordered.

We define a notion of
interior
of
\mathcal{X}
(that depends on the choice of directional neighbourhoods
\{\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\}
and
\{\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)\}
) as
\mathring{\mathcal{X}}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))>0\}
. For instance,
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=0
when one of the two directional neighbourhoods is empty. It is possible to have empty directional neighbourhoods when, for instance,
\mathcal{X}
, or a component of it, is a bounded subset of the integers (in particular, when the current state
x
corresponds to the upper bound, we have that
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)=\mathrm{N}(x)=\{x-1\}
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\varnothing
, if
\mathrm{N}(x)
comprises integers at a distance of 1 from
x
in
\mathcal{X}
). Empty directional neighbourhoods thus arise in the context of Bayesian nested model selection discussed in
Section 1
. They also arise in the Ising model example in
Section 3
and in Bayesian variable selection.

In the spirit of our notion of interior of
\mathcal{X}
, we define a notion of
boundary
of
\mathcal{X}
:
\partial{\mathcal{X}}:=\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
. For all
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
and
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
, we denote the conditional proposal distribution given that the proposal belongs to
\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)
as
Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
which is such that

Q_{\nu}(x,A)=\frac{Q(x,A\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))},\quad A\in\mathsf{X}.

(4)

We define a probability distribution which will play the role of proposal distribution in the generalized auxiliary sampler. It essentially corresponds to a reweighing of the proposal distribution
Q
:

Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\cdot\,):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1/2)Q_{-1}(x,\cdot\,)+(1/2)Q_{+1}(x,\cdot\,)&\text{ if}\quad x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}},\cr(1/2)\delta_{x}+(1/2)Q(x,\cdot\,)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

We now present a lemma which is useful to prove that the Markov kernel of the generalized auxiliary sampler is
\pi
-reversible and which also allows to understand the implications of
Assumption 1
on
Q
and thus on
Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
Assumption 1
holds.

1.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q(x,\{x\})=0
.

2.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=0
.

3.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cup\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=1
.

4.

When
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
, there exists
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
such that
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
. Therefore,
Q_{\nu}
can be defined as in (
4
) and in this case
Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)=Q(x,\cdot\,)
.

Proof.

Under
Assumption 1
, we have that, if
x\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)
, then
x\in\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)
, and from this we can deduce that
Q(x,\{x\})=2Q(x,\{x\})=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, concluding the proof of Result 1.

We can also deduce that, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=2Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=0,

and similarly,

Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})=\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=0,

the latter implying that
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cup\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=1
. This concludes the proof of Results 2 and 3.

Regarding Result 4, when
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
, there exists
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
such that
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x))=0
, which implies that
Q(x,\mathcal{X})=Q(x,\mathcal{X}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
and that
Q(x,\cdot\,)=Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
.
∎

Similarly as in
Section 2.1
, we define

\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}(A\times B):=\int_{A}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,B)\,,\qquad\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}(A\times B):=\int_{B}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A).

We also define the following sets:
R_{\nu}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}:y\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\}
for
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
, and
\Delta:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}:x=y\}
. Finally, supposing that
Assumption 1
holds, we introduce a function
\beta:\mathcal{X}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
:

\beta(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if}\quad(x,y)\in R^{\mathsf{c}},\cr Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))/Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))&\text{ if}\quad(x,y)\in R_{\nu}\cap R_{-\nu}^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\Delta^{\mathsf{c}}\cap R\text{ for some $\nu\in\{-1,+1\}$},\cr 1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(5)

We now present a result which will allow to define a valid acceptance probability in the generalized auxiliary sampler, in the sense of leading to a Markov kernel which is
\pi
-reversible. It highlights that the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}
can be written as
\mathrm{d}\mu^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu
adjusted by the function
\beta
. It thus shows how to construct the acceptance probability in the generalized auxiliary and lifted samplers (as will be seen in
Section 2.3
) from that in the generalized MH algorithm.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
Assumption 1
holds. The restriction of the measures
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}
to
R
are mutually absolutely continuous and, for all
(x,y)\in R
,

r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y):=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}}(x,y)=r(x,y)\,\beta(x,y),

with
0<r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)<\infty
and
r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=1/r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(y,x)
.

Sketch of the proof.
The proof of
Proposition 2
is technical and thus deferred to
Appendix A
. Here, we present a sketch of the proof of
r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=(\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}/\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}})(x,y)=r(x,y)\,\beta(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in R
. To simplify, let us consider the situation where
R=\mathcal{X}^{2}
and
\mathring{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{X}
.

We exploit a known result which follows from, e.g., Proposition 3 in
Roberts and Rosenthal (2004)
: two measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are equal if and only if
\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}=\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}
for all bounded measurable functions
\phi
. The idea is thus to prove that, for any bounded measurable function
\phi:\mathcal{X}^{2}\rightarrow\re
,

\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}=\int\phi\,r_{\mathrm{rev.}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}.

Under the simplified situation, using the definition of
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}
we have that

\displaystyle\int\phi(x,y)\,\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}\int\phi(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}y)\,\frac{1}{2}Q_{\nu}(y,\mathrm{d}x).

We analyse each term of the sum separately:

\displaystyle\int\phi(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}y)\,\frac{1}{2}Q_{\nu}(y,\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\phi(x,y)\,\mathds{1}(x\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}{2Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}y)\,Q(y,\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\phi(x,y)\,\mathds{1}(x\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}{2Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}\,\mu^{T}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\phi(x,y)\,\mathds{1}(y\in\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x))}{2Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}\,r(x,y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x,y)\,r(x,y)\,\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x))}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,\frac{1}{2}Q_{-\nu}(x,\mathrm{d}y),

using the definitions of
Q_{\nu}
and
\mu^{T}
in the first and second equalities, respectively, and that
(y,x)\in R_{\nu}
if and only if
(x,y)\in R_{-\nu}
and
Proposition 1
in the third equality, by combining
\phi(x,y)
and
\mathds{1}(x\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))/(2Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y)))
into a new bounded measurable function. Note that
1/Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))
is not necessarily bounded, but we can limit ourselves to the case where
Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(y))
is bounded from below by an arbitrarily small constant and use a limiting argument with the monotone convergence theorem.

We obtain
\int\phi\,r_{\mathrm{rev.}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}
by summing over
\nu
(and after verifying technical details), recalling the definition of
r_{\mathrm{rev.}}
(see the statement of the proposition) and
\beta
in (
5
).
\blacksquare

Following
Tierney (1998)
,
Proposition 2
allows to state that the Markov kernel of the generalized auxiliary sampler defined as

P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}y):=Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)+\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\int(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,u))\,Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}u),\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$},

with the function
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}
given by

(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2},\quad\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))&\text{if $(x,y)\in R$},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

is in fact a generalized MH kernel, but one that uses
Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}
as proposal kernel. It is thus
\pi
-reversible by
Theorem 1
.
Lemma 2
indicates that, under
Assumption 1
, for
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}
-almost all
(x,y)
with
(x,y)\in R
, the acceptance probability
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)
is equal to

\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))=\varphi(r(x,y)\,\beta(x,y))=\varphi\left(r(x,y)\,\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))}\right)

for some
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
, except if
y=x
, in which case
\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))=1
.

2.3
Generalized lifted samplers

From the generalized auxiliary sampler introduced in
Section 2.2
, we define the generalized lifted sampler. For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
, let
T_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
be a sub-probability measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{X})
defined as:

A\in\mathsf{X},\quad T_{\nu}(x,A)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{A}\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)\,Q_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{d}y)&\text{if $x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}$ or $x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}$ with $Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Let
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
be a Markov kernel defined on the state-space
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
as: for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
,

P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}((x,\nu),\mathrm{d}(y,\nu^{\prime}))=T_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\delta_{\nu}(\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime})+\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\delta_{-\nu}(\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime})\,(1-T_{\nu}(x,\mathcal{X})).

Under
Assumption 1
, if
(x,\nu)
is such that
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
or
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
with
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
, the lifted sampler proceeds with a proposal
y\sim Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
such that, with probability 1,

\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))=\varphi\left(r(x,y)\,\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))}\right),

if
(x,y)\in R
or 0 if
(x,y)\notin R
, recalling the definition of
\beta
in (
5
). Indeed, under
Assumption 1
, if
(x,\nu)
is such that
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
or
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
with
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
, we have that
(x,y)\in R_{\nu}\cap R_{-\nu}^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\Delta^{\mathsf{c}}
for
Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
-almost all
y
because

Q_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\{x\}^{\mathsf{c}})=1.

The latter follows from

Q_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cup\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)\cup\{x\})\leq\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}+\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}+\frac{Q(x,\{x\}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}=0,

by the union bound and
Lemma 2
.

The following proposition states that
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
satisfies a skewed detailed balance
(
Andrieu and Livingstone, 2021
)
, implying that the product measure
\pi\otimes\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
is an invariant distribution. Following
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
,
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
is said to satisfy a skewed detailed balance if the part of the kernel associated to a move of the
X
component, that is
T_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{d}y)
, satisfies

\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,T_{+1}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\pi(\mathrm{d}y)\,T_{-1}(y,\mathrm{d}x).

More formally, it corresponds to proving that the two following measures on the product space
(\mathcal{X}^{2},\mathsf{X}^{\otimes 2})
are equal:

\mu_{+1}(A\times B)=\int_{A}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,T_{+1}(x,B)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{-1}(A\times B)=\int_{B}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,T_{-1}(x,A).

For completeness, the proof that a skewed detailed balance implies that the product measure
\pi\otimes\mathcal{U}\{-1,+1\}
is an invariant distribution is provided in
Appendix A
.

Proposition 3
.

Supposing that
Assumption 1
holds,
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
satisfies a skewed detailed balance.

Sketch of the proof.
As for
Proposition 2
, here we present a sketch of the proof of
Proposition 3
as the formal proof is technical and thus deferred to
Appendix A
. As in the sketch of the proof of
Proposition 2
, let us consider the simplified situation where
R=\mathcal{X}^{2}
and
\mathring{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{X}
.

The idea is to prove that, for any bounded measurable function
\phi:\mathcal{X}^{2}\rightarrow\re
,

\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{+1}=\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{-1}.

Under the simplified situation and using the definition of
\mu_{+1}
, we have that

\mu_{+1}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q_{+1}(x,\mathrm{d}y).

The measure
\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,Q_{+1}(x,\mathrm{d}y)
is only positive on the set where
(x,y)
is such that
y\in\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
. Thus, we can limit ourselves to this set, which corresponds to
R_{+1}
. Also, we can add
Q_{-1}(x,\mathrm{d}y)
because it is null on this set and obtain

\displaystyle\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{+1}

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{+1}}\phi(x,y)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,(Q_{+1}(x,\mathrm{d}y)+Q_{-1}(x,\mathrm{d}y))

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{+1}}2\,\phi(x,y)\,\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))\,\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{+1}}2\,\phi(x,y)\,\varphi(1/r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))\,r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)\,\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{+1}}2\,\phi(x,y)\,\varphi(1/r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))\,\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{+1}}\phi(x,y)\,\varphi(r_{\mathrm{rev.}}(y,x))\,\pi(\mathrm{d}y)\,(Q_{+1}(y,\mathrm{d}x)+Q_{-1}(y,\mathrm{d}x)),

using the definitions of
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}
in the second equality (see
Section 2.2
), the property of the function
\varphi
in the third equality,
Proposition 2
in the fourth equality (recalling that
\varphi
is bounded) and the definition of
\mu_{\mathrm{rev.}}^{T}
in the last equality. From there, we proceed symmetrically as above to obtain the result, noting that
(x,y)\in R_{+1}
if and only if
(y,x)\in R_{-1}
.

\blacksquare

Note that the definition of lifted sampler presented in this sub-section is in fact not the most general.
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
present a more general definition in which
Q_{-1}
and
Q_{+1}
are not necessarily defined through a decomposition of
Q
; they can be any proposal kernels. In such generality, it can be shown that it is not possible to establish a comparison with the MH algorithm (see
Appendix B
for a demonstration). At least, it is not possible using the route proposed in
Section 2.4
that allows for a comparison when considering the definition of
Q_{-1}
and
Q_{+1}
in
Section 2.2
. Note that most applications of the lifting technique in practice use this definition.

2.4
The comparison

Equipped with the definitions and results of Sections
2.1
-
2.3
, we present in this section our theoretical guarantees for the generalized lifted sampler. They take the form of an upper bound on the asymptotic variances of the associated MCMC estimators, where the bound is a function of the asymptotic variances of the MCMC estimators associated with the generalized MH algorithm.

Let us recall that, for an homogeneous Markov chain
\{X_{k}\}
of transition kernel
P
leaving
\pi
invariant started in stationarity, the asymptotic variance of a MCMC estimator of
\pi f
, the expectation of
f(X)
under
X\sim\pi
, is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(f,P):=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}T\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}f(X_{k})\right],

whenever the limit exists. When
P
is reversible and
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
, the limit always exists, but may be infinite
(
Tierney, 1998
)
. When
P
is additionally ergodic and the limit is finite,

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(f,P)=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X_{0})]+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[f(X_{0}),f(X_{k})],

(6)

by Theorem 4 of
Häggström and Rosenthal (2007)
.

A standard route to compare the asymptotic variances of MCMC estimators produced by two different samplers (e.g.,
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
) is by establishing a Peskun-type ordering between the Markov kernels. In our context, a challenge is that
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
are not defined on the same state-space and thus cannot be ordered. As mentioned in
Section 1
, Theorem 7 in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
allows to compare
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
with a
\pi
-reversible kernel corresponding to the version of
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
which resamples the direction variable at the beginning of each iteration. In our analysis,
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
plays the role of this
\pi
-reversible kernel. In Theorem 7 in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
,
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is seen as a Markov kernel that can also be defined on the extended state-space
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
and only estimators of expectations of functions of
X\sim\pi
are considered. When focusing on functions
f
of solely the first argument, extending the state-space to include the direction variable in
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is superfluous. Let us be more specific and consider such a function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}\rightarrow\re
with
f(x,\nu)=g(x)
. For such a function
f
, the asymptotic variance of an estimator produced by
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
, but when the latter is defined on the extended state-space, is equivalent to the asymptotic variance for
g
of an estimator produced by
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
defined on
\mathcal{X}
(as in
Section 2.2
). The kernel
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is thus used as an intermediate kernel through which comparison of the asymptotic variances of the MCMC estimators associated with
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
and
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
is possible. With an abuse of notation, we will use
f
to denote both a function acting on
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
of solely the first argument and a function acting on
\mathcal{X}
. Only such functions acting on
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
of solely the first argument will be considered in the following.

The proof of our theoretical result is thus based on establishing a Peskun-type ordering between
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
and
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
. The fact that the MCMC estimators produced by
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
cannot have smaller asymptotic variances than those produced by
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}

(
Andrieu and Livingstone, 2021
, Theorem 7)
concludes the argument. Intuitively, this latter fact can be seen as following from the observation that
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
proceeds as
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
with only
one
difference:
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
resamples the direction variable at the beginning of each iteration instead of keeping it fixed until a rejection occurs. The Markov kernel
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is thus seen as a reversible counterpart to
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
.

In our theoretical result, we use the notion of
\lambda
-asymptotic variance (as in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
) defined, for an homogeneous Markov chain
\{X_{k}\}
of transition kernel
P
leaving
\pi
invariant started in stationarity, as

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P):=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X_{0})]+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\,\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[f(X_{0}),f(X_{k})],\quad\lambda\in[0,1),

whenever
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
. Note that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P)=\mathrm{var}(f,P)
under some conditions (not requiring reversibility). A general condition is that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[f(X_{0}),f(X_{k})]|<\infty
, which can be established under fairly mild assumptions
(
Andrieu and Livingstone, 2021
, Corollary 3)
. A more concrete (but stronger) condition is uniform ergodicity
(
Gagnon and Maire, 2024
)
.

We are now ready to present our result.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
Assumption 1
holds. For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\in\mathsf{X}
, we have that

P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\}).

Therefore, for any
f
such that
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
and whenever
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})=\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})
,

\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})\leq\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{rev.}})\leq 2\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{MH}})+\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X)],\quad X\sim\pi.

Proof.

With some abuse of notation, let, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
R_{x}:=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:(x,y)\in R\}
. Let us first consider the case where
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
. For
A\in\mathsf{X}
, we have that

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})=\int_{A\setminus\{x\}}Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}\int_{A\setminus\{x\}}Q_{\nu}(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}\int_{A\cap R_{x}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\setminus\{x\}}Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))},

(7)

using the definitions of
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\cdot\,)
, that, for any
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
,
Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
assigns null measure to
(\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})^{\mathsf{c}}=\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\cup\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)
by definition and
Lemma 2
, and that
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=0
for all
(x,y)\notin R
.

For all
(x,y)
with
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
and
y\in R_{x}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\setminus\{x\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}=\frac{\varphi(r(x,y)\beta(x,y))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varphi(r(x,y)(1\wedge\beta(x,y)))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varphi(r(x,y))(1\wedge\beta(x,y))}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}

\displaystyle=\varphi(r(x,y))\left(\frac{1}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}\wedge\frac{1}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\geq\varphi(r(x,y))=\alpha(x,y),

using the definitions of
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
\beta
(see
Section 2.2
), and Results 1 and 6 of
Lemma 1
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}\int_{A\cap R_{x}\cap\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(x)^{\mathsf{c}}\setminus\{x\}}Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A\cap R_{x}\setminus\{x\}}\left(\mathds{1}(y\in\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})+\mathds{1}(y\in\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})\right)Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A\setminus\{x\}}Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\}),

using in the first equality that, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathds{1}(y\in\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})+\mathds{1}(y\in\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)^{\mathsf{c}})=1\quad\text{for \quad$Q(x,\cdot\,)$-almost all $y$},

which can be deduced from
Lemma 2
(the formal proof is similar to that of
Lemma 3
in
Appendix A
), and in the second equality that
\alpha(x,y)=0
for all
(x,y)\notin R
.

The case
x\notin\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
is done similarly after noticing that, in this case, there exists
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
such that
Q(x,A)=Q_{\nu}(x,A)
and
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
by
Lemma 2
.

The result

P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\}),\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and $A\in\mathsf{X}$},

implies that, for any
f
such that
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
,

\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{rev.}})\leq 2\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{MH}})+\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X)],\quad X\sim\pi,

by
Lemma 4
.

The proof of

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})\leq\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{\mathrm{rev.}}),

(9)

in the case where
\varphi(r)=1\wedge r
follows from Theorem 7 in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
. It can be readily verified that the result holds for any function
\varphi
considered here using the same proof technique as in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
. The proof is concluded by taking the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 1
on both side of the inequality in (
9
).
∎

We now make a few remarks about
Theorem 2
. Firstly, the result reflects a notion of
universality
: in addition to being stated in a context of general state-spaces, it is valid for
any
target distribution
\pi
,
any
proposal mechanism (defining the generalized MH algorithm from which

and
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
are constructed, be it the usual MH, multiple-try, pseudo-marginal or reversible jump), and for
any
way of inducing directions (through the definition of
\{\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\}
and
\{\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)\}
). Also, it holds for any function
f
belonging to a class of functions (the functions
f
such that
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
and
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})=\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})
). In different contexts, performance comparison has been established through an order between
average
asymptotic variances
(
Chen et al., 2012
)
and
maximal
asymptotic variances
(
Frigessi et al., 1992
)
; such an ordering is thus implied by results such as
Theorem 2
.

Secondly, we observe in the proof that the factor

\left(\frac{1}{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}\wedge\frac{1}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))}\right)

in (
8
) is an explanation of the difference between
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
. It is a measure of whether the directional neighbourhoods
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
(and
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(y)
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(y)
) are well balanced or not. When
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))=Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=1/2
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we say that we are in a situation of
perfect balance
. In this situation, that factor is always equal to 1 and
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\in\mathsf{X}
. We thus have a guarantee that the lifted sampler outperforms the MH one, that is
\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})\leq\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{MH}})
whenever
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})=\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{Lifted}})
. It is for instance the case with the guided walk, by symmetry of the proposal distribution in random walk MH. Theorem 7 in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
is stated under the general framework of this section (where
\pi
and
Q(x,\cdot\,)
do not necessarily admit densities with respect to a common dominating measure), but with the restriction to the situation of perfect balance and to the function
\varphi(r)=1\wedge r
. Note that before introducing the inequalities in the proof, we had that
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})=P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
under the condition
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))=Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=1/2
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
(see (
7
)). This indicates that the inequalities in the proof are tight because, under this condition, the inequalities yield
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
but we know that
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})=P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
.

Thirdly, in a context that fits within the framework of this section,
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
prove that
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})/P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\rightarrow 1/2
for some
x
and
A
, as a dimension parameter grows without bounds (Proposition 2 in that paper). We can therefore conclude that our ordering between
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
is (essentially) optimal, in the sense that it is (essentially) not possible to obtain a better ordering without additional assumptions. In
Appendix C
, we show that our bound on the asymptotic variances is (essentially) optimal.
Theorem 2
is thus (essentially) optimal.

Fourthly, in a context of comparison between two samplers, the magnitude of the asymptotic variances, which is proportional to
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X)]
(see (
6
)), is irrelevant. Thus, it is appropriate to focus on standardized functions. When
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[f(X)]=1
, the expression in (
6
) corresponds to the integrated autocorrelation time. In this case, the factor
2
and the additive term in the bound in
Theorem 2
on the asymptotic variances (and thus on the integrated autocorrelation times) are independent of all problem parameters, whether it is the dimension of the state-space, the sample size in Bayesian statistics contexts, etc. This implies that the generalized lifted sampler is
at worst
comparable to the generalized MH algorithm, in the sense that the asymptotic variances can be larger, but at worst, the factor
2
and the additive term explaining the difference between the asymptotic variances do not deteriorate when a problem parameter changes. All that suggests the following practical suggestion:
when one has a way of inducing directions which does not significantly increase the computational cost of the algorithm, better do it as there is not much to lose by lifting a sampler, but there is potentially a lot to gain (as observed many times in the past)
.

Finally, the Peskun-type ordering between
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
in
Theorem 2
also allows to obtain an order between the spectral gaps
(
Andrieu et al., 2018b
, Lemma 33)
: the spectral gap of
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is greater than or equal to half of that of
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
. This result leads to an order between the mixing times in total variation, provided that
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
has a spectral gap (see, e.g.,
Andrieu et al. (2018a)
). Therefore, if one has established an order between the mixing times of
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
and
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
, then an order between the mixing times of
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
follows. Lifted samplers do not dominate their reversible counterparts in terms of mixing times because the former can exhibit quasi-periodic or even periodic behaviour (see, e.g.,
Vialaret and Maire (2020)
).

3
Illustration: The case of common dominating measure

In this section, we make more concrete the framework of
Section 2
by considering the case where
\pi
and
Q(x,\cdot\,)
admit densities with respect to a common dominating measure. In this case,
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
corresponds to the usual MH kernel. It is simple and explicit, which allows simple and explicit forms of
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
. All these kernels are presented in
Section 3.1
. Next, we illustrate
Theorem 2
by presenting empirical results and instances of lifted samplers in two concrete situations fitting within the framework of this section: one where the state-space is finite and partially ordered (
Section 3.2
), and one where the state-space is the real numbers, and thus, totally ordered (
Section 3.3
). We use this illustration to highlight that, even in situations where it is suspected that the lifted sampler will not offer a great performance, the asymptotic variances are at worst comparable to those of MH, as guaranteed by
Theorem 2
. In particular, we show that, in very unfavourable situations, the upper bound in
Theorem 2
is attained. The code to reproduce our numerical results is available online (see ancillary files on
https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15952
).

3.1
MH, auxiliary and lifted samplers

As mentioned, we consider the case where
\pi
and
Q(x,\cdot\,)
admit densities with respect to a common dominating measure, denoted by
\mathrm{d}y
for simplicity. With an abuse of notation, we also denote these densities by
\pi
and
Q(x,\cdot\,)
. To simplify, we consider that
\mathcal{X}\subset\re^{d}
is the support of
\pi
, with
d
a positive integer, and that, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathrm{N}(x)\subset\mathcal{X}
is the support of
Q(x,\cdot\,)
, where
\{\mathrm{N}(x)\}
yields a neighbourhood structure on
\mathcal{X}
. We also consider that
Q(x,\{x\})=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
(i.e., the probability is equal to 0, but not necessarily the density). Finally, we consider that: for any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
,

y\in\mathrm{N}(x)\textit{ if and only if }x\in\mathrm{N}(y).

Under the framework just described, the conclusion of
Theorem 1
holds, meaning that
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
is
\pi
-reversible, in the case where
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
using the usual acceptance probability is defined for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
as

P_{\mathrm{MH}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=Q(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha(x,y)+\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\int(1-\alpha(x,u))\,Q(x,\mathrm{d}u),

with, for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
,
\alpha(x,y)=0
whenever
y\notin\mathrm{N}(x)
, and otherwise,

\alpha(x,y)=1\wedge\frac{\pi(y)\,Q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\,Q(x,y)},

noting that
R=\{(x,y):x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathrm{N}(x)\}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the MH algorithm thus proceeds with a proposal
y\sim Q(x,\cdot\,)
which is accepted with probability

\alpha(x,y)=1\wedge\frac{\pi(y)\,Q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\,Q(x,y)},

in which case the next state of the Markov chain is
y
. Otherwise, the proposal is rejected and the next state of the Markov chain is
x
.

In the auxiliary sampler, we define the directional neighbourhoods as follows: for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
are such that
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cup\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\mathrm{N}(x)
and either
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\varnothing
or
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\{x\}
. Typically, when the state-space is discrete,
x\notin\mathrm{N}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\varnothing
, as in our first example in
Section 3.2
. When the state-space is continuous,
x\in\mathrm{N}(x)
and
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)\cap\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\{x\}
, but
Q(x,\{x\})=0
, as in our second example in
Section 3.3
. We also require that the directional neighbourhoods satisfy the following: for any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
and
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
,
y\in\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x)
if and only if
x\in\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y)
, and for such a related couple
(x,y)
,
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))>0
and
Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))>0
.

Consequently,
Assumption 1
is satisfied and
Proposition 2
holds, implying that
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is
\pi
-reversible, where

P_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}y)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)+\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\int(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,u))\,Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\mathrm{d}u),\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$},

Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\cdot\,)=(1/2)Q_{-1}(x,\cdot\,)+(1/2)Q_{+1}(x,\cdot\,)
if
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\cdot\,)=(1/2)\delta_{x}+(1/2)Q(x,\cdot\,)
otherwise, recalling the definitions of
Q_{\nu}
and
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}
in
Section 2.2
. For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the auxiliary sampler thus proceeds with a proposal
y\sim Q_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,\cdot\,)
which is accepted with probability

\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=1\wedge\frac{\pi(y)\,Q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\,Q(x,y)}\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))},

for some
\nu\in\{-1,+1\}
, except if
(x,y)\notin R
, in which case
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y)=0
, or if
y=x
, in which case
\alpha_{\mathrm{rev.}}(x,y))=1
. As with the MH algorithm, if the proposal is accepted, the next state of the Markov chain is
y
; otherwise, the next state of the Markov chain is
x
.

Proposition 3
also holds, meaning that
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
satisfies a skewed detailed balance (recall the definition of
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
in
Section 2.3
). When the current state of the Markov chain
(x,\nu)\in\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
is such that
x\in\mathring{\mathcal{X}}
or
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
with
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=1
, the lifted sampler proceeds with a proposal
y\sim Q_{\nu}(x,\cdot\,)
which is accepted with probability

1\wedge\frac{\pi(y)\,Q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\,Q(x,y)}\frac{Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))}{Q(y,\mathrm{N}_{-\nu}(y))},

in which case the next state of the Markov chain is
(y,\nu)
. Otherwise, the proposal is rejected and the next state of the Markov chain is
(x,-\nu)
. If the current state of the Markov chain
(x,\nu)\in\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,+1\}
is such that
x\in\partial{\mathcal{X}}
with
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{\nu}(x))=0
, the next state of the Markov chain is automatically set to
(x,-\nu)
.

3.2
Example 1: Partially-ordered finite state-space

In this example, we consider that
\mathcal{X}
is a finite state-space which admits a partial order. This partial order will be exploited to induce directions to follow by the lifted sampler. We consider more precisely that
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, where each component
x_{i}
can be of two types. Many statistical contexts fit within this framework, such as the modelling of binary data using networks or graphs and in variable selection. Indeed, for the former,
\mathcal{X}
can be parameterized such that
\mathcal{X}=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, where for example for an Ising model,
x_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
represents the state of a spin. For variable selection,
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether or not the
i
-th covariate is included in the model employed.

The use of lifted algorithms to sample from distributions defined on partially-ordered finite state-spaces has been thoroughly studied in
Gagnon and Maire (2024)
. In particular, the Ising model has been studied. Here, we provide numerical results that are complementary to those presented there. The specific model that we study is a two-dimensional Ising model. For this model, the state-space
\mathcal{X}=(V_{\eta},E_{\eta})
is a
\eta\times\eta
square lattice regarded here as a square matrix in which each element takes either the value
-1
or
+1
. We write each state as a vector as above:
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, where
n=\eta^{2}
. The states can be encoded as follows: the values of the components on the first line are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\eta}
, those on the second line
x_{\eta+1},\ldots,x_{2\eta}
, and so on. The probability mass function is given by

\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}x_{i}+\lambda\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}x_{i}x_{j}\right),

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\re
and
\lambda>0
are fixed parameters,
Z
is the normalizing constant and the notation
\langle ij\rangle
indicates that sites
i
and
j
are nearest neighbours. The notion of neighbourhood on
(V_{\eta},E_{\eta})
should not be confused with that on
\mathcal{X}
on which the samplers rely. The neighbourhood of a site
i\in V_{\eta}
comprises, when they exist, its North-South-East-West neighbours on the lattice. The parameter
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is often referred to as the
external field
and
\lambda
plays the role of a
spatial correlation parameter
. The external field tends to polarize each spin, while the spatial correlation tends to create patches of identical spin states.

To simulate an Ising model, the MH algorithm typically proceeds by proposing to flip a single bit at each iteration (hence,
x\notin\mathrm{N}(x)
). More formally, the neighbourhood structure
\{\mathrm{N}(x)\}
is given by
\mathrm{N}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\sum_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|=2\}
. A uniform proposal distribution is often employed. This strategy has been shown to be often inefficient in
Zanella (2020)
, who proposed a generic approach to sample from distributions defined on discrete state-spaces. The approach leverages local target-distribution information and is thus less naive than a uniform approach. It possesses appealing properties and is referred to as
locally balanced
. The terminology
locally balanced
comes form the fact that, in the limit, when the state-space becomes larger and larger (but the neighbourhoods have a fixed size and proposed moves are thus local), there is no need for an accept-reject step anymore; the proposal distribution leaves the distribution
\pi
invariant. The locally-balanced proposal distribution that we use in this example is the
Barker proposal distribution
, with

q(x,y)=\frac{\pi(y)/\pi(x)}{1+\pi(y)/\pi(x)},\quad x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathrm{N}(x),

where the name is in reference to
Barker (1965)
’s acceptance probability choice. Note that the example fits within the framework of
Section 3.1
with the MH algorithm defined as in that section.

To explicitly define the directional neighbourhoods that are used in the lifted sampler, we leverage that
\mathcal{X}
admits a partial order. Indeed, an inclusion-based partial order on
\mathcal{X}
can be defined through a set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}:\{i\,:\,x_{i}=+1\}\subset\{i\,:\,y_{i}=+1\}\right\}.

Pairs
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
with
x\neq y
are said to be
comparable
when either
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
or
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and are said
incomparable
otherwise. The existence of incomparable pairs
(x,y)
with
x\neq y
(those with the same number of
+1
components) represents the difference with a totally-ordered set such as
\na
or
\re
in which every pair of different elements is comparable. We denote
x\prec y
whenever
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
and
x\neq y
. Hence, setting
\mathrm{N}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\sum_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|=2\}
yields neighbourhoods with states
y
that are all comparable with
x
. In the lifted sampler, we set
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)=\{y\in\mathrm{N}(x):y\prec x\}
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=\{y\in\mathrm{N}(x):x\prec y\}
. States
y
in
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)
, for instance, are equal to
x
except for one component which is flipped from
-1
to
+1
. Note that the directional neighbourhoods satisfy
Assumption 1
. Therefore, Propositions
2
and
3
hold, and
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is
\pi
-reversible and
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
satisfies a skewed detailed balance, with the auxiliary and lifted samplers defined as in
Section 3.1
.

We are now ready to present simulation results. They are about a common problem in statistical physics which is to estimate the average
magnetisation
of an Ising model, the magnetisation being defined as the mapping
x\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. The numerical results that we present are more specifically about the asymptotic variances of MCMC estimators of the expectation of the magnetisation, in fact, of the standardized version of the magnetisation, meaning that the expectation is subtracted from the mapping and the result of this subtraction is divided by the standard deviation. The standardized version is considered to allow comparable asymptotic variances when varying problem parameters, which is what we do in the numerical experiment, as described below. It also allows to fit within the framework of comparison between samplers described at the end of
Section 2.4
.

We consider a base target distribution for which
n=10^{2}
and the spatial correlation is moderate and more precisely
\lambda=0.5
. We set the
\alpha_{i}
as follows:
\alpha_{i}=-\mu+\epsilon_{i}
if the column index is smaller than or equal to
\lfloor\eta/2\rfloor
and
\alpha_{i}=\mu+\epsilon_{i}
otherwise, where
\mu=1
, the
\epsilon_{i}
are independent uniform random variables on the interval
(-0.1,+0.1)
and
\lfloor\,\cdot\,\rfloor
is the floor function. We can thus think of the external field as a matrix with negative values on the left, and positive ones on the right. The base target is moderately rough, in the sense that it concentrates on a subset of the state-space, but with directional neighbourhoods that have a smoothly varying mass on this subset (implying similar
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))
and
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))
).

The base target distribution described above is favourable for the lifted sampler with the locally-balanced proposal distribution which leverages variations in the target distribution and create persistent movement (as seen in
Figure 1
by looking at the starting points on the left of the plots). In
Figure 1
(a), we observe the impact of dealing with larger systems for a moderately rough target, by increasing
\eta
from
10
to
50
. Increasing
\eta
in this case leads to longer paths along which the state-space can be explored, which is again favourable for lifted samplers. In
Figure 1
(a), we observe that the lifted sampler scales better with the system size than the MH algorithm. When comparing the latter with the reversible counterpart to the lifted sampler, we observe a stable absolute difference of asymptotic variances, which translates into a diminishing relative difference. With
Figure 1
(b), we consider an opposite situation which is increasingly unfavourable for the lifted sampler, with a fixed system size of
\eta=10
but an increasing
\mu
yielding an increasingly rougher target distribution with an increasing level of concentration. As the level of concentration increases, the mass of the directional neighbourhoods becomes increasingly variable (implying increasingly different
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))
and
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))
). Also, when the mass is concentrated on few configurations, it leaves not much room for persistent movement for the lifted sampler, and it thus loses its advantage. Such a situation corresponds to one where it is suspected that the lifted sampler will not offer a great performance. When the lifted sampler is at the mode and leaves it, it “wastes” an iteration because it tries continuing in the same direction whereas the MH sampler has the possibility to return to the mode the following iteration. In
Figure 1
(b), we also observe an increasing difference in asymptotic variances between
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
and
P_{\mathrm{MH}}
, with that of
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
reaching the upper bound provided by
Theorem 2
of
2\mathrm{var}(f,P_{\mathrm{MH}})+1
. Note that the numerical results are based on 100 independent runs of 1,000,000 iterations for each algorithm and each value of
\eta
and
\mu
, with burn-ins of 100,000.

3.3
Example 2: Totally-ordered continuous state-space

In this section, we study the simple case where
\mathcal{X}=\re
. When the MH sampler is the random walk Metropolis algorithm, the lifted version described in
Section 3.1
corresponds to the
guided walk

(
Gustafson, 1998
)
if
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)=(-\infty,x]
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=[x,+\infty)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The guided walk has essentially the same computational cost as the random walk Metropolis algorithm, and has been proved to outperform the latter for any target distribution
\pi

(
Andrieu and Livingstone, 2021
, Theorem 7)
. This is a consequence of the symmetry of the normal distribution used in random walk Metropolis. This symmetry leads to a situation of perfect balance, as described in
Section 2.4
, where
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))=Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))=1/2
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

But what if the proposal distribution is not symmetrical? This is the case that we now study. In this case, one may suspect that the lifted sampler will not offer a great performance in situations of significant differences between
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))
and
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x))
. In our example, we consider that the proposal distribution is the continuous version of the Barker proposal distribution described in
Section 3.2
. It has recently been proposed in
Livingstone and Zanella (2022)
and has the following density:

Q(x,y)=2\,\frac{\varphi_{\sigma}(y-x)}{1+\exp\left(-(y-x)\,\nabla\log\pi(x)\right)},\quad x\in\re,y\in\mathrm{N}(x)=\re,

where
\varphi_{\sigma}
is the density of a normal distribution with a mean of
0
and a standard deviation of
\sigma>0
, and
\nabla\log\pi
is the gradient of the target log-density. The gradient injects skewness: the more substantial is
\nabla\log\pi(x)
, the more asymmetrical is the density
Q(x,\cdot\,)
. The scale parameter
\sigma
is a tuning parameter. We study the natural case where the target distribution is a standard normal. Note that the example fits within the framework of
Section 3.1
with the MH algorithm defined as in that section.

and

. The directional neighbourhoods are as above:
\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x)=(-\infty,x]
and
\mathrm{N}_{+1}(x)=[x,+\infty)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The directional neighbourhoods thus satisfy
Assumption 1
. Therefore, Propositions
2
and
3
hold, and
P_{\mathrm{rev.}}
is
\pi
-reversible and
P_{\mathrm{Lifted}}
satisfies a skewed detailed balance, with the auxiliary and lifted samplers defined as in
Section 3.1
. In
Table 1
, we present results for the MH sampler, its lifted version and the reversible counterpart of the latter. We more precisely present acceptance rates and asymptotic variances of the MCMC estimators of
\pi f
when

is the identity mapping
x\mapsto x
, for different values of
\sigma
. Note that the identity mapping is already standardized given that the target distribution is a standard normal. The values of
\sigma
in

represent optimal values for the different samplers (at least according to our grid search). For the MH algorithm, the optimal value is
2.5
, associated with an acceptance rate of
62\%
. When using this algorithm, the asymptotic variance of the MCMC estimator of the mean is
1.94
. The lifted sampler suffers from instability among the mass of the directional neighbourhoods
Q(x,\mathrm{N}_{-1}(x))
and

has to be reduced. The optimal value for this sampler is

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we leveraged the generalized MH algorithm of
Tierney (1998)
to introduce a generalized lifted sampler. This allowed to understand how to construct a lifted sampler under a general framework in which the target and proposal distributions do not necessarily admit densities with respect to a common dominating measure. In the same way the generalized MH algorithm of
Tierney (1998)
unified seemingly different reversible MCMC methods under the same framework, the generalized lifted sampler unifies seemingly different lifted samplers such as the non-reversible jump algorithm of
Gagnon and Doucet (2021)
and the non-reversible simulated tempering of
Sakai and Hukushima (2016b)
. Under this general framework, theoretical guarantees were provided: the generalized lifted sampler may produce MCMC estimators with larger asymptotic variances than the generalized MH algorithm, but the ratio of asymptotic variances is essentially bounded above by 2.

In
Appendix C
, we highlight that different choices of directions may lead to lifted samplers that are significantly different, ranging from a lifted sampler which is worst than its MH counterpart to a lifted sampler which is better. This raises the question of the existence of an optimal choice of directions and, in the affirmative, of a theoretical guarantee that the optimal lifted sampler is better than its MH counterpart.

The focus of this article has been on the lifting technique in the classical sense, following
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
, where the lifted sampler alternates between two sub-probability measures
T_{-1}
and
T_{+1}
which satisfy the skewed detailed balance. The terminology
lifting
is sometimes used in a more general context and refers to as a technique by which the state-space is
lifted
to include auxiliary variables for performance improvement
(
Apers et al., 2021
)
. From this perspective, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
Duane et al., 1987
;
Neal, 2011
)
and piecewise deterministic Monte Carlo methods
(
Vanetti et al., 2017
;
Bouchard-Côté et al., 2018
;
Bierkens et al., 2019
)
are lifted samplers. For such classes of Monte Carlo methods, whether it is possible or not to establish theoretical guarantees as in our article is an open question. A challenge in these cases seems to be the identification of a natural and direct point of comparison, meaning MCMC algorithms that have a strong connection with the lifted samplers (as in our article).

Drawing inspiration from the recent work of
Ascolani and Zanella (2025)
, we can extend the framework of
Section 2
to the case where the generalized auxiliary and lifted samplers use
D
directions by exploiting a mixture structure, that is the Markov kernels correspond to a mixture of
D
kernels, and
Theorem 2
still holds. In
Ascolani and Zanella (2025)
, a lifted sampler exploiting a mixture structure is introduced in the context of Bayesian finite mixture models and an upper bound is obtained on the asymptotic variances, in the same spirit as
Theorem 2
. In the special case where the mixture model in
Ascolani and Zanella (2025)
has two components,
D=1
and their sampler is a special case of the generalized lifted sampler introduced in
Section 2.3
. In this case, our generic upper bound in
Theorem 2
allows to retrieve that in
Ascolani and Zanella (2025)
derived within their specific framework.

Before presenting the proof of

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu\in\{-1,+1\}}\left(\mathds{1}(\nu^{\prime}=\nu)\int\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,T_{\nu}(x,A)+\mathds{1}(\nu^{\prime}=-\nu)\int\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\int_{A}\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)\,(1-T_{\nu}(x,\mathcal{X}))\right)

We finish this section with a lemma which is used in the proof of
Theorem 2
. Before presenting it, we introduce notation. For two functions
f,g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\re
, let
\langle\,f,g\,\rangle:=\int f\,g\,\mathrm{d}\pi
,
\|f\|:=\left[\int f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\right]^{1/2}
and
L^{2}(\pi):=\{f:\|f\|<\infty\}
. For
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, we can consider without loss of generality that it has a mean of 0. Let
L_{0}^{2}(\pi):=\{f:\|f\|<\infty\text{ and }\pi f=0\}
.
Lemma 4
below can be seen as an extension of Lemma 33 in
Andrieu et al. (2018b)
: in the latter,
(I-P)
, with
I
being the identity operator and
P
a
\pi
-reversible kernel, is assumed to be invertible, whereas we do not assume this, yet the same conclusion is obtained. The proof is strongly inspired from that of Lemma 33 in
Andrieu et al. (2018b)
, but it uses the
\lambda
-asymptotic variance, instead of using directly the asymptotic variance.

Lemma 4
.

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be two
\pi
-reversible Markov kernels such that, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\in\mathsf{X}
,
P_{2}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq\alpha P_{1}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then, for any
f\in L_{0}^{2}(\pi)
,

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{2})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P_{1})+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)\|f\|^{2},\quad\lambda\in[0,1).

Also, by taking the limit
\lambda\uparrow 1
, the same inequality holds for the asymptotic variances given that
\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P)=\mathrm{var}(f,P)
, whether the limit is finite or not, for any
\pi
-reversible kernel
P
.

Proof.

As mentioned in
Andrieu and Livingstone (2021)
, the
\lambda
-asymptotic variance can be written as

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P)=2\langle\,f,(I-\lambda P)^{-1}f\,\rangle-\|f\|^{2}.

(15)

The fact that
P
is Markov guarantees that
(I-\lambda P)
has a bounded inverse on
L^{2}(\pi)
. Indeed,
\langle\,f,(I-\lambda P)^{-1}f\,\rangle=(1/\lambda)\langle\,f,\{(1/\lambda)I-P\}^{-1}f\,\rangle
and
(1/\lambda)I-P
is invertible given that
1/\lambda>1
and
1
is an upper bound of the spectrum of
P
. Thus, using Lemma 16 in
Andrieu and Vihola (2016)
, we have that

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P)=2\sup\{2\langle\,f,g\,\rangle-\langle\,g,(I-\lambda P)g\,\rangle\,:\,g\in L_{0}^{2}(\pi)\}-\|f\|^{2}.

For convenience, let us define
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda P}(f):=\langle\,f,(I-\lambda P)f\,\rangle
, the generalization of the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{P}(f)=\langle\,f,(I-P)f\,\rangle
, so that

\mathrm{var}_{\lambda}(f,P)=2\sup\{2\langle\,f,g\,\rangle-\mathcal{E}_{\lambda P}(g)\,:\,g\in L_{0}^{2}(\pi)\}-\|f\|^{2}.

We now establish that
P_{2}(x,A\setminus\{x\})\geq\alpha P_{1}(x,A\setminus\{x\})
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\in\mathsf{X}
implies that
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda P_{2}}(g)\geq\alpha\mathcal{E}_{\lambda P_{1}}(g)
for any
g\in L_{0}^{2}(\pi)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda P_{2}}(g)

\displaystyle=\lambda\langle\,g,(I-P_{2})g\,\rangle+(1-\lambda)\|g\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\lambda\langle\,g,(I-P_{1})g\,\rangle+(1-\lambda)\|g\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda\|g\|^{2}-\alpha\lambda\langle\,g,P_{1}g\,\rangle+(1-\lambda)\|g\|^{2}

\sum_{k=1}^{T^{\prime}}f(\tilde{X}_{k})/\sqrt{T^{\prime}}
produced by the lifted sampler is asymptotically equivalent to
\sqrt{2}
times that produced by the MH algorithm, that is
\sum_{k=1}^{T}f(X_{k})/\sqrt{T}
, all the arguments presented above carry over to the lifted case.
∎
