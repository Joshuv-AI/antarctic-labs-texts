Adapting Deep Variational Bayes Filter for Enhanced Confidence Estimation in Finite Element Method Integrated Networks (FEMIN)
Simon Thel, Lars Greve, Maximilian Karl, Patrick van der Smagt

Abstract
The Finite Element Method (FEM) is a widely used technique for simulating crash scenarios with high accuracy and reliability. To reduce the significant computational costs associated with FEM, the Finite Element Method Integrated Networks (FEMIN) framework integrates neural networks (NNs) with FEM solvers. However, this integration can introduce errors and deviations from full-FEM simulations, highlighting the need for an additional metric to assess prediction confidence, especially when no ground truth data is available. In this study, we adapt the Deep Variational Bayes Filter (DVBF) to the FEMIN framework, incorporating a probabilistic approach to provide qualitative insights into prediction confidence during FEMIN simulations. The adaptation involves using the learned transition model for a predictive decoding step, generating a preliminary force prediction. This predictive force is used alongside the displacement and the velocity data from the FEM solver as input for the encoder model. The decoder reconstructs the likelihood distribution based on the posterior. The mean force of this distribution is applied to the FEM solver, while the predicted standard deviation can be used for uncertainty estimation. Our findings demonstrate that the DVBF outperforms deterministic NN architectures in terms of accuracy. Furthermore, the standard deviation derived from the decoder serves as a valuable qualitative metric for assessing the confidence in FEMIN simulations. This approach enhances the robustness of FEMIN by providing a measure of reliability alongside the simulation results.

Introduction

Crash simulations have become an indispensable tool in automotive engineering, crucial in vehicle safety evaluation and improvement
(
Klug et al., 2023
)
.
These simulations, traditionally based on the Finite Element Method (FEM), provide high-fidelity simulation results to meet stringent safety standards
(
National Center for Statistics and Analysis, 2022
)
.
However, the complexity of these models results in substantial computational costs, with simulations often taking hours or even days to complete, despite advances in parallel computing
(
Law, 1986
;
Mafi and Sirouspour, 2014
;
Xue et al., 2023
)
.

Our previous work introduced the Finite Element Method Integrated Networks (FEMIN)
(
Thel et al., 2024
)
, a novel framework that combines Neural Network (NN) models with FEM simulations.
FEMIN aims to reduce computational time by replacing entire regions in the simulation model with NN surrogates during runtime.
This approach is advantageous in scenarios where engineers focus on evaluating the crash performance of specific localized regions of a vehicle within a full-vehicle context.
FEMIN is based on the premise that the impact of design changes on mechanical behavior decreases as the distance from the modified part increases.
Then, the vehicle is divided into two main parts:

1.

A substructure close to the part undergoing design changes, where the influence of these changes is assumed to be significant.

2.

The remainder of the vehicle, where the influence of the design change is assumed to be minor.

The latter part is replaced by a reactive surrogate model based on the FEMIN approach, reducing computational cost while still allowing for detailed FEM analysis of the area of interest.

While FEMIN has shown promise in accelerating crash simulations, the integration of NNs with FEM solvers introduces new challenges, particularly in terms of accuracy and reliability.
One critical aspect that needs to be addressed is the assessment of prediction confidence, especially in scenarios where ground truth data is unavailable for validation.

This study presents an innovative adaptation of the Deep Variational Bayes Filter (DVBF) to the FEMIN framework.
This adaptation introduces a probabilistic approach to FEMIN, providing qualitative insights into prediction confidence during FEMIN simulations.
By incorporating DVBF, we aim to enhance the reliability and interpretability of FEMIN results, addressing one of the critical challenges in adopting machine learning techniques in safety-critical applications such as automotive crash simulations.

Our approach involves several key modifications to the standard DVBF architecture
(
Karl et al., 2017
)
to align it with the requirements of the FEMIN framework.
We use the learned transition model for a predictive decoding step, generating a preliminary force prediction. This predictive force in conjunction with the kinematics
1

The decoder then reconstructs the likelihood distribution based on the posterior, from which we derive the mean force applied to the FEM solver.
This paper expands on our previous work by:

1.

Detailing the adaptation of DVBF to the FEMIN framework, making the adapted DVBF a generative and simultaneously an autoregressive model.

2.

Comparing the performance of DVBF against deterministic NN architectures in terms of accuracy.

3.

Assessing the uncertainty of the decoder output as a qualitative metric to estimate the prediction confidence in FEMIN.

By addressing the critical issue of confidence estimation, this study aims to further enhance the robustness and reliability of the FEMIN approach, potentially accelerating its adoption in automotive design and safety evaluation processes. The integration of probabilistic methods not only improves the accuracy of simulations but also provides valuable insights into the reliability of predictions.

Related Work

While the FEMIN approach
(
Thel et al., 2024
)
directly integrates a NN into the FEM solver to replace entire regions of the mesh, other recent research focuses on integrating NN as a constitutive model
Logarzo et al. (2021)
;
Tancogne-Dejean et al. (2021)
.
For example,
Pantidis and Mobasher (2023)
embedded NNs within finite element stiffness functions to accelerate the damage computations.
Their physics-informed NN (PINN) predicts both strain and its gradient on an element basis, which leads to an accelerated construction of the Jacobian matrix.
NNs can also be used as a computationally efficient surrogate for FEM simulations
(
Liang et al., 2018
;
Kudela and Matousek, 2022
;
Deshpande et al., 2024
)
.

Gulakala et al. (2023)
used a graph NN (GNN) that directly predicts the von Mises stress of a FEM mesh.
The stress distribution was then used as input for a standard FEM post-processor.
The surrogate modeling approach by
Haghighat et al. (2021)
used PINNs to predict von Mises stress.
Their approach could be used for other field variables, and the results demonstrated transfer learning capabilities.

van de Weg et al. (2021)
predicted fracture of tensile specimens using an LSTM-based learning approach.
They showed excellent data efficiency while maintaining high accuracy.
Besides replacing the entire FEM simulations, NNs can also be used to accelerate multi-scale FEM simulations
(
Ghavamian and Simone, 2019
;
Danoun et al., 2024
)
or used as an element-based surrogate within a FEM simulation
(
Ouyang et al., 2024
;
Koeppe et al., 2020
)
.

Surrogate models are also widely used in uncertainty quantification for solid mechanics
(
Rocas et al., 2021
;
Wojtkiewicz et al., 2001
)

In this context, probabilistic NN can be beneficial:

Deshpande et al. (2022)
applied a Variational Bayes approach
(
Kingma and Welling, 2014
)
to the U-Net architecture
Ronneberger et al. (2015)
.
The probabilistic U-Net of
(
Deshpande et al., 2022
)
was trained to predict the displacement of a mesh given the force boundary values.
The predicted uncertainties of the network correlated well with the prediction errors.

Dang et al. (2021)
used a Bayesian NN for structural reliability analysis.
They reported a reduction in computation time up to three orders of magnitude compared to a classical approach using Monte Carlo sampling with FEM.

Van De Weg et al. (2024)
used a Bayesian approach to automatically determine the LSTM network size during training by capturing the uncertainties within the weights of the surrogate model.
Their approach was immune to overfitting and showed accurate results as a surrogate model for solid mechanics.
Other research used probabilistic NNs for material parameter identification
(
Rappel et al., 2020
;
Zeraatpisheh et al., 2021
)
or for topology optimization
(
Patel and Choi, 2012
)
.

In this paper, we adapt the probabilistic NN architecture "Deep Variational Bayes Filter," introduced by
Karl et al. (2017)
and enhanced by
Karl et al. (2017)
.
DVBF extends Kalman Filter
(
Kalman, 1960
)
to deep learning and improves the application to dynamical systems compared to
Krishnan et al. (2015)
and
Johnson et al. (2017)
.
The DVBF architecture used is mainly used in robotics
(
Becker-Ehmck et al., 2020
;
Karl et al., 2017
)
.
While a DVBF-inspired architecture has been applied to fluid mechanics
(
Morton et al., 2021
)
and fluid control
(
Morton et al., 2018
)
, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the DVBF to structural mechanics.

3.1
Finite Element Method Integrated Networks

(a)
Mesh for data generation: Multiple FEM simulations are performed with design changes in
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
. The displacement, velocity, and section forces are recorded at
\mathcal{N}_{\text{B}}
.

(b)
Mesh during FEMIN simulation: A NN replaces the nodes
\mathcal{N}_{\text{replaced}}
by applying the force
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{NN}}
on
\mathcal{N}_{\text{B}}
.

Figure 1:
Principle of FEMIN: A FEM simulation is accelerated by a NN, which replaces a portion of the FEM mesh.

In FEMIN, a portion of the FEM domain is replaced by a NN, altering the original boundary value problem.
The simplified equation of motion for highly dynamic FEM is
(
Pickett et al., 1990
)
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{ext}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{FEM}}\cdot n_{\text{DOF}}}

(1)

with

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}

mass matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}

stiffness matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}

displacement vector

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}

acceleration vector

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{ext}}

external force

n_{\text{FEM}}

total number of nodes in FEM simulation

n_{\text{DOF}}

number of degrees of freedom

The FEM mesh is partitioned into two subsets:
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
(nodes retained during a FEMIN simulation) and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{replaced}}
(nodes replaced by the NN).
The boundary nodes
\mathcal{N}_{\text{B}}
are a subset of
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
.
The modified boundary value problem for FEMIN becomes:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{\text{FEMIN}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{FEMIN}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{FEMIN}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{FEMIN}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{ext, FEMIN}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{NN}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{FEMIN}}\cdot n_{\text{DOF}}}

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{NN}}
is the force vector provided by the NN acting on the boundary nodes
\mathcal{N}_{\text{B}}
and
n_{\text{FEMIN}}
is the number of nodes in the FEMIN simulation
Since it is

n_{\text{FEMIN}}<n_{\text{FEM}}\,,

(3)

the boundary value problem for FEMIN can be solved with fewer computational resources.
The NN must approximate the section forces
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{B}}
acting between
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{replaced}}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{NN}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{B}}

(4)

The prediction problem is characterized by:

•

Partial observability: The NN must infer
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{B}}
based solely on boundary kinematic (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{B}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{B}}
).
The node set
\mathcal{N}_{\text{replaced}}
is not available during a FEMIN simulation and including more information from
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
would increase the complexity of the NN matrices, reducing and even negating the computational benefits of FEMIN.

•

State dependency: The mechanical behavior depends on both spatial state (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
) and system state (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
). The system state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
describes properties such as plasticity, hardening, and damage. These variables are not necessarily nodal properties. Since the prediction problem follows a node-based description, the system state cannot improve the prediction.

Training data is obtained from full FEM simulations, extracting boundary node kinematics and loads:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{B},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{B},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{B},t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{B}}\cdot n_{\text{DOF}}}\text{ with }t\in T\,

(5)

at every time step
t
.
The total number of time steps is denoted as
T
and the number of boundary nodes is
n_{\text{B}}
.
For data generation, design changes are made in
\mathcal{N}_{\text{FEMIN}}
.
If the design changes stem from a parametric model, these parameters can be used to improve the prediction.
These parameters can be used as the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
.
If the design changes are established manually or are not parametric (e.g., from Graph and Heuristic based topology optimization
(
Ortmann and Schumacher, 2013
)
or a generative approach
(
Siddiqui et al., 2023
)
), the parameter vector is
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
.

The prediction problem can now be written as a mapping between the kinematics to forces at time step
t

f_{\text{NN}}\!:\quad\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{B},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{B},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{NN},t}\,,

(6)

where
f_{\text{NN}}
described by an NN.
The FEM solver handles the time step evolution via its integration scheme.,

3.2
Deep Variational Bayes Filter

3.2.1
Original Fusion DVBF

The architecture is based on the Fusion DVBF presented by
Karl et al. (2017)
.
Displacement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{t}
, velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{t}
and force
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{t}
with time step
t
are concatenated to observation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{t}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

(7)

where
k
is the dimensionality of the observations.
The encoder
q_{\text{enc}}
encodes
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
to the latent state estimation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{enc},t}
:

q_{\text{enc}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t,\text{enc}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{enc},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}),\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{enc},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}))\in\mathbb{R}^{l}

(8)

where
l
is the dimensionality of the latent state.
The mean
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{enc},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
and the standard deviation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\text{enc},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
of the Gaussian normal distribution are represented by a NN.
The transition model provides an estimate based on the previous latent state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}
.
The transition estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
can be enhanced by the design parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
given their availability.

p_{\text{trans}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{trans},t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\,.

(9)

The prior is also given by the transition model.

p_{\text{prior}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{prior},t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{prior},t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\,.

(10)

While the mean
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans}}
is shared between the transition estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
and the prior, the standard deviations of the two distributions
p_{\text{trans}}
and
p_{\text{prior}}
distribution are different.
Using two different standard deviations for prior and transition improves optimization of the KL-divergence
(
Karl, 2020
)
.

The transition model is based on the locally linear transition from
Karl et al. (2017)
.

Watter et al. (2015)
demonstrated the superior performance of the locally linear transition for long-term prediction problems while maintaining computational efficiency.
The mean of the transition estimate and the prior is given by

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans},t}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}

(11)

The matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{t}
are constructed from base matrices:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{t}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{t}^{(i)}\tanh_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{(i)}\right)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{t}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{t}^{(i)}\tanh_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{(i)}\right)

(12)

where
M
denotes the number of linear systems.
The components of the base matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{(i)}
are learned during training.
The mixing factor
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{t}
is obtained from a neural network
f_{\alpha}
with final sigmoid activation:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{t}=f_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\,.

(13)

The scaled hyperbolic tangent limits the maximum values of the base matrices

\tanh_{s}(x)=s\tanh x

(14)

with scale
s
.
Note that, while the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
is constant over time, the matrix-vector product
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
in
eq.

11
is not because mixing factor
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{t}
depends on the latent state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}
(c.f.
eq.

13
).
Thus, the inclusion of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{t}
into the transition model controls when the information from the design parameters is needed.
The standard deviations of the transition model are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{t,\text{trans}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{t}^{(i)}\psi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{1}^{(i)}\right)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{t,\text{prior}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{t}^{(i)}\psi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{2}^{(i)}\right)

(15)

with the base vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{1}^{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{2}^{(i)}
and the softplus function
\psi
ensuring positive values

\psi(x)=\log\left(e^{x}+1\right)\,.

(16)

The posterior distribution combines the transition
p_{\text{trans}}
and encoder distributions
q_{\text{enc}}
. It follows the Markov assumption

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1:t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\propto q_{\text{enc}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{enc},t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\times p_{\text{trans}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{trans},t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{q,t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{q,t})

(17)

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{q,t}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{enc},t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans},t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{enc},t}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{enc},t}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t}},\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{q,t}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{enc},t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{enc},t}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t}}\,.

(18)

The latent state is sampled from this posterior distribution

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{q,t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{q,t})\,.

(19)

The reparametrization trick
(
Kingma and Welling, 2014
)
allows backpropagation through this sampling process
The decoder provides the likelihood distribution

p_{\text{lik}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{lik},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{lik},t})\,.

(20)

The DVBF is trained by minimizing the negative Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) function

\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1:T}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\lambda}}\right):=-\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}

(21)

with
N
number of samples.
Backpropagation through time provides the gradients necessary to perform stochastic gradient decent optimizing
\mathcal{L}
by varying the trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\lambda}}
.
The ELBO loss consists of the negative log-likelihood as the reconstruction loss

-\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon},t}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\ln(\sigma_{\text{lik},t,i}^{2})-\frac{(x_{t,i}-\mu_{\text{lik},t,i})^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{lik},t,i}^{2}}\right)-\frac{k}{2}\ln(2\pi)

(22)

and Kullback Leibler (KL) divergence

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL},t}=D_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\parallel p_{\text{prior}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\left(\frac{\sigma_{q,t,i}^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{prior},t,i}^{2}}+\frac{(\mu_{q,t,i}-\mu_{\text{trans},t,i})^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{prior},t,i}^{2}}+\ln\left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{prior},t,i}^{2}}{\sigma_{q,t,i}^{2}}\right)-1\right)\,.

(23)

The initial prior is a standard normal distribution

p_{\text{prior},1}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\,.

(24)

The loss terms
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon},t}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL},t}
are aggregated over time via average.

3.2.2
Modification of Fusion DVBF for FEMIN

Figure 2:
Flow chart of the modified DVBF for FEMIN. No backpropagation over dashed lines

DVBF is a generative model, meaning its input and output are on the same manifold.
This generative approach allows for holistic learning, using all available information to build the latent space.
However, our problem involves an FE solver that enforces a mapping between two distinct manifolds (kinematic to forces, see
eq.

6
).
To address this, we propose modifying the DVBF architecture to maintain holistic learning while respecting the mapping relationship imposed by the solver.

Our approach involves decoding the transition estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
provided by the transition model. Initially, a latent state estimate is sampled from the transition distribution:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{z}}}_{\text{trans},t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{\text{trans},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{\text{trans},t})

(25)

This sample then undergoes a preliminary decoding step:

p_{\text{lik}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{z}}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\mu}}}_{\text{lik},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{lik},t})

(26)

From the resulting distribution, we extract the components of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\mu}}}^{2}_{\text{lik}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\sigma}}}^{2}_{\text{lik}}
that correspond to the force prediction.
These extracted values replace the true force values in the input definition of
eq.

7
, redefining the DVBF input as:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\mu}}}_{\text{lik, force},t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\sigma}}}_{\text{lik, force},t}\right\}\,.

(27)

This modification eliminates the need for true force as input to the model while allowing the DVBF to learn from all three quantities: displacement, velocity, and force.
When the DVBF is coupled with the FEM solver (also referred to as "online application"), the preliminary force prediction closely approximates the actual force of the final decoding step applied to the solver.
Consequently, the input to the encoder — combined with the kinematics updated by the FEM solver — closely resembles the actual observation of the boundary, enhancing the model’s accuracy.
The modified DVBF architecture is depicted in
fig.

2
.

The use of both mean and standard deviation from the decoder to describe the force distribution provides valuable information to the model.
This approach informs the encoder about the model’s certainty in its force predictions, enabling it to weigh its belief more heavily on either the kinematic or the predicted force.
Another approach to implicitly provide the encoder with information about the model’s certainty in its force predictions would be to sample from preliminary force distribution.
However, during the initial training phases, the likelihood model’s standard deviations are typically large, reflecting the model’s lack of confidence in its outputs.
Sampling from such a broad distribution introduces excessive noise, potentially over-regularizing the model and reducing convergence.
Therefore, our approach of using both the mean and standard deviation strikes a balance between leveraging the full force distribution information and maintaining good learning performance.

Gradients are not propagated through the preliminary decoding step and are stopped at
eq.

27
.
This design choice offers two advantages.
Firstly, it improves training speed and memory efficiency by reducing the number of backpropagation steps.
Secondly, it enhances online accuracy.
By stopping the gradients, we effectively treat
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\mu}}}_{\text{lik, force}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\sigma}}}_{\text{lik, force}}
as standard inputs and not trainable parameters.
The decoder weights are only updated with respect to the final decoding step (c.f.
eq.

20
).
Since the output of this decoding step is applied to the FEM solver during the online application, placing greater emphasis on the final decoding step improves online accuracy.

3.2.3
Window-based training

Following the approach of
Thel et al. (2024)
, we implement window-based training.
This method is particularly useful for FEMIN, where time series can be extremely long.
Window-based training allows for effective backpropagation on smaller time series segments, mitigating issues with vanishing or exploding gradients
(
Bengio et al., 1994
)
.
Additionally, this approach will scale well to even larger load cases.

The large time series with
T
time steps is sliced into smaller, rolling windows of length
T^{*}
.
An initial network is applied to the first
K
time steps in each window:

q_{\text{init}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1:K},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{w},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{w})

(28)

Note that
q_{\text{init}}
still receives the true force values
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{1:K}
.
We then sample from this initial distribution

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}_{w},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{2}_{w})

(29)

and apply an initial nonlinear transition

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{1}=f_{\text{trans},0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}_{0})\,,

(30)

where
f_{\text{trans},0}
is a NN
(
Karl et al., 2017
)
.

3.2.4
Online inference

During the initialization of the online application, the input of
q_{\text{init}}
are the initial values of the simulation padded
K
-times.
Since these are non-physical values, it can be expected that the predicted force during the initial time steps deviates from the ground truth values and converge during the simulation’s progress.

The sampling processes in
eqs.

19
,
25
and
29
are replaced by directly choosing the mean of these distributions.
While this does not mean that these lead to the most probable outcomes in the likelihood distribution of
eq.

20
(the mapping from posterior to likelihood is nonlinear), it leads to a smooth latent trajectory and repeatable results.
The smooth latent state also leads to a smooth force trajectory, reducing vibrations during online application.
Similarly, the force that is applied to the FEM solver is the mean of the force distribution of the likelihood model
p_{\text{lik}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
.

4.2
Metrics

The data set split into the train, validation, and test data is the same as
(
Thel et al., 2024
)
, enabling direct comparison of results.
We evaluate accuracy and confidence metrics, reporting them on the test data set.
These metrics are computed between the predicted quantity of interest (QoI)
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\chi}}}_{n,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
and its true value
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\chi}}_{n,t}
with the corresponding predicted confidence denoted as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}

For accuracy assessment, we use two primary metrics.
First, the mean squared error (MSE):

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N\cdot T\cdot C}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{c=1}^{C}(\chi_{n,t,c}-\hat{\chi}_{n,t,c})^{2}\,.

(31)

Second, the coefficient of determination, or
R^{2}
score, is defined as:

R^{2}_{n,t}=1-\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\chi_{n,t,c}-\hat{\chi}_{n,t,c})^{2}}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\chi_{n,t,c}-\bar{\chi}_{n,t})^{2}}

(32)

where
\bar{\chi}_{n,t}
is the mean of the true values per time step
t
and sample
n
.
The
R^{2}
score ranges from
-\infty
to
1
, with
1
indicating perfect prediction.
This normalized metric allows for comparison across different load cases.
To account for variations in prediction accuracy throughout the simulation, we employ the variance-weighted
R^{2}
score:

R^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{\text{var}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\chi}}_{n,t})}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{var}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\chi}}_{n,t})}R^{2}_{n,t}\right)

(33)

This approach reduces the impact of small prediction errors in low-variance regions (e.g., during the beginning of the simulation), which could otherwise disproportionately affect the overall score.

For confidence assessment, we employ three metrics.
The negative log-likelihood (NLL) is the reconstruction loss of the DVBF

\text{NLL}=-\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}

(34)

The NLL weights prediction accuracy with prediction confidence.
A prediction that is inaccurate while the model is confident (low predicted uncertainty) will result in a larger value than an inaccurate prediction with low confidence.
Therefore, smaller values are desirable.
Note that the NLL is not bounded in either direction and can become negative.
Another confidence metric is the Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP), which offers a human-interpretable confidence score:

\text{PICP}=\frac{1}{N\cdot T\cdot C}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{c=1}^{C}\xi_{i,t,c},\qquad\xi_{i,t,c}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\ \chi_{i,t,c}\in[\hat{\chi}_{i,t,c}-1.96\sigma_{i,t,c},\quad\hat{\chi}_{i,t,c}+1.96\sigma_{i,t,c}]\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(35)

The PICP is the proportion of true values that fall within the
95\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
-confidence interval, with higher values indicating better confidence estimation.
To evaluate the model’s overall confidence, we calculate the average width of the confidence band:

\text{AC}=\frac{2\cdot 1.96}{N\cdot T\cdot C}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{c=1}^{C}\sigma_{i,t,c}\,.

(36)

The average confidence (AC) value is reported in the same unit as the quantity of interest
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}
.

Finally, to assess the correlation between two curves
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}\in\mathrm{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}\in\mathrm{R}^{n}
, we use the Pearson correlation coefficient
r
:

r=\frac{\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\phi_{i}-\bar{\phi})(\eta_{i}-\bar{\eta})}{\sqrt{\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\phi_{i}-\bar{\phi})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\eta_{i}-\bar{\eta})^{2}}}\,,

(37)

where
\bar{\phi}
and
\bar{\eta}
are the respective means.
A Pearson correlation of 1 indicates a perfect linear correlation.

Our analysis of confidence metrics focuses primarily on force predictions, as force is the only free variable in the FEMIN framework.
The displacement and velocity errors are constrained because they will start at zero and tend to increase from there.
In FEMIN, then NNs are trained offline with data extracted from previously run FEM simulations.
There is no coupling between NN and FEM during training, and the NN cannot learn how the specimen reacts to its force predictions.
Consequently, the uncertainty provided by the decoder may not necessarily reflect the expected uncertainty in the kinematics of the online application.

The PICP for force predictions is high, with
88.23\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{init}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{impactor}}\}
) and
91.08\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
) of true values falling within the
95\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
confidence.
This aligns well with theoretical expectations, indicating that the uncertainty estimation of the DVBF generalizes well to the online application.
The uncertainty estimation can also be seen in
figs.

6
and
7
.
In general, the predicted uncertainty of the force increases in areas where the predictions differ from the true values, especially in the regions where the force prediction smooths out small oscillations.
Contrary to the high PICP, the AC value is relatively small (

and
6.35\text{\,}\mathrm{kN}
) and only accounts for approximately
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the force trajectories range (
65\text{\,}\mathrm{kN}
).
The AC value provides a human-interpretable measure of confidence in the FEMIN simulation.
This metric is particularly valuable as it can be calculated without true values, offering insight into prediction reliability during the online application of new, unseen designs.

To further assess the relationship between prediction uncertainty and accuracy, we analyzed the correlation between the force standard deviation and the absolute error during online application.

Figure

8
illustrates this relationship, showing that the absolute error (light blue) follows the trend of the standard deviation but with more noise.
A moving average was applied to reduce these fluctuations.
The Pearson correlation coefficient was computed between the smoothed absolute error and the predicted force standard deviation.
The resulting correlations of
r=$60.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{init}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}_{\text{impactor}}\}
and
r=$61.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
(averaged over all test samples) further establish the standard deviation as a valuable metric for assessing uncertainty during FEMIN application.

In conclusion, the standard deviation provided by the DVBF proves to be a valuable tool for estimating confidence in FEMIN simulations.
However, it is important to note some limitations: the standard deviation is a trained parameter and may not accurately reflect uncertainty when the model is applied outside its training domain.
For example, if a part buckles in a FEMIN simulation that did not buckle in the FEM simulation used for the train data, it is not guaranteed that the predicted uncertainty increases.
A similar conclusion was made by
(
Deshpande et al., 2022
)
.
Despite these limitations, the DVBF’s ability to provide meaningful uncertainty estimates within the trained domain represents a significant advancement in applying machine learning techniques to crash simulations.

5
Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the successful adaptation of the Deep Variational Bayes Filter (DVBF) to the Finite Element Method Integrated Networks (FEMIN) framework, offering significant improvements in terms of accuracy while adding uncertainty estimation.
Key findings include:

1.

The DVBF consistently outperformed full-sequence and window-based LSTM architectures across multiple accuracy metrics.

2.

The probabilistic nature of DVBF provided valuable uncertainty estimates, with high Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) and meaningful Average Confidence (AC) scores.

3.

A strong correlation between predicted standard deviations and actual force prediction errors was observed, validating the DVBF’s uncertainty estimates as a reliable prediction confidence indicator.

4.

The DVBF demonstrated competitive computational efficiency in both training and inference.

These results highlight the DVBF’s potential to enhance the robustness and reliability of FEMIN simulations.

Future work should focus on scaling the DVBF approach to larger, more complex load cases and exploring methods to improve the alignment between offline training metrics and online performance.
Additionally, investigating the DVBF’s behavior in scenarios outside its training domain could provide insights into its generalization capabilities and limitations.
In conclusion, the integration of DVBF into FEMIN represents a significant step forward in combining the efficiency of neural networks with the reliability requirements of crash simulations.
