Classification and a priori estimates for the singular prescribing Q Q -curvature equation on 4-manifold
Mohameden Ahmedou, Lina Wu, Lei Zhang

Abstract
. On ( M , g ) (M,g) a compact riemannian 4 − 4- manifold we consider the prescribed Q − Q- curvature equation defined on M M with finite singular sources. We first prove a classification theorem for singular Liouville equations defined on ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} and perform a concentration compactness analysis. Then we derive a quantization result for bubbling solutions and establish a priori estimate under the assumption that cetain conformal invariant does not take some quantized values. Furthermore we prove a spherical Harnack inequality around singular sources provided their strength is not an integer. Such an inequality implies that in this case singular sources are isolated simple blow up points .

Introduction

The question of existence of conformal metrics of constant or more generally prescribed curvature on riemannian manifolds is a recurrent problem in differential geometry and geometric analysis. Indeed a positive or a negative answer to such a question has far reaching consequences on the geometry and topology of the underlying manifold.
The Poincaré uniformization’s theorem on closed surfaces, the Yamabe problem on riemannian manifolds of dimension
n\geq 3
and the Nirenberg’s problem on standard spheres
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, just to name a few, are well known and well studied mathematical problems.

A similar question, which goes back to Picard
[
37
,
38
]
deals with the existence of conformal metrics of constant or prescribed curvature on surfaces with conical singularities. After the pioneering work of Picard at the beginning of the last century, such a problem has been systematically investigated by Berger
[
8
]
, McOwen
[
41
,
42
]
and Troyanov
[
46
,
45
]
. More recently Bartolucci-deMarchis-Malchiodi
[
5
]
used a Morse theoretical approach to prove further existence and multiplicity results.

In this paper, a first part of a series of papers, we address the problem of existence of conformal conical metrics of constant, or more generally prescribed
Q-
curvature on four dimensional riemannian manifolds. In the following we will explain in some details the geometric context of such a problem:

Given
(M,g)
a compact four-dimensional Riemannian manifold, the
Q
-curvature and the Paneitz operator are defined respectively, by

(1.1)

Q_{g}=-\frac{1}{12}\big(\Delta_{g}R_{g}-R_{g}^{2}+3|\rm{Ric}_{g}|^{2}\big),

(1.2)

P_{g}\varphi=\Delta_{g}^{2}\varphi+{\rm div}_{g}\Big(\big(\frac{2}{3}R_{g}g-2{\rm Ric}_{g}\big)\nabla u\Big),

where
{\rm Ric}_{g}
is the Ricci tensor and
R_{g}
is the scalar curvature of
(M,g)
.

Similar to second order equations a natural question is the following uniformization statement:
given a four-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, is there a metric
\tilde{g}=e^{2u}g
in the conformal class of
g
with constant
Q
-curvature?

Under the conformal change of metric above, the Paneitz operator is conformally covariant:

(1.3)

P_{\tilde{g}}\varphi=e^{-4u}P_{g}\varphi,

and the
Q
curvature of
\tilde{g}
is given by

(1.4)

P_{g}u+2Q_{g}=2Q_{\tilde{g}}e^{4u}.

From (
1.3
) and (
1.4
), the question above is equivalent to the existence of solution to this fourth order equation:

(1.5)

P_{g}u+2Q_{g}=2\bar{Q}e^{4u},

where
\bar{Q}
is a real constant.

Integrating with respect to the volume element
{\rm d}V_{g}
, we can see that

\kappa_{P}=\int_{M}Q_{g}{\rm d}V_{g}

is a constant in the conformal class of
g
, here we also point out the Gauss-Bonnet-Chern formula that links the local curvature to the global topology of
M

\int_{M}\Big(Q_{g}+\frac{1}{8}|W_{g}|^{2}\Big){\rm d}V_{g}=4\pi^{2}\chi_{M},

where
W_{g}
denotes the Weyl’s tensor of
(M,g)
and
\chi_{M}
is the Euler characteristic of
M
. From this equality and the aforementioned conformal covariance property it is not hard to imagine that
P_{g}
and
Q_{g}
are related to a number of studies such as Moser-Trudinger type inequatilities, log-determinant formulas and the compactification of locally flat manifolds, see
[

In this paper consider the following prescribed

(1.9)

P_{g}u_{k}+2b_{k}=2h_{k}e^{4u_{k}}-8\pi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{j}\Big(\delta_{q_{j}}-\frac{1}{{\rm vol}_{g}(M)}\Big)\quad{\rm in}\ \,M,

One indispensable part of the blowup analysis for the Q curvature equation is the classification of global solutions on

\Delta^{2}\tilde{u}=6e^{4\tilde{u}}-8\pi^{2}\gamma\delta_{0},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}e^{4\tilde{u}}<\infty,

(iii)

Remark 1.5
.

Our quantization and classification results directly point to the following important open questions:
1. Theorem
1.3
does not cover the case that
\gamma>0
, except for the quantization result. The symmetry and profile for global solutions of (
1.12
) in this case are largely unknown.

2. If the sub-quadratic growth assumption (
u(x)=o(|x|^{2})
) is removed, it is not clear to us how the total integration of global solutions will change. It is proved in Lin
[
31
]
that for
\gamma=0
solutions with sub-quadratic growth give the largest integration. It is also proved by Hyder et al
[
27
]
that for
\gamma<0
there are non-radial solutions whose integration is greater than that of radial ones. It can be easily derived from our Theorem
2.10
that if
\gamma<0
and
a_{i}x_{i}^{0}=0
for all
i
, the one with the sub-quadratic growth gives the largest integration. However, no information is known for other situations.

When we were about to submit this article we heard that Jevnikar-Sire-Yang
[
28
]
are working on a similar project independently and their results are to be posted soon.

Here we briefly outline the strategy of the proofs in our paper. For the proof of the classification result for globally defined singular equation, we follow the argument of Lin
[
31
]
but we need to take care of all the complications caused by the singular source. In particular the method of moving planes relies crucially on the integral form of the global solutions. We are able to prove the complete classification for
\gamma\in(-1,0]
and a quantization result for all
\gamma>-1
. For blowup solutions we first use a small-energy lemma (Lemma
4.1
) to prove that there are at most finite blowup points. Then we take advantage of a Pohozaev identity established by Weinstein-Zhang
[
52
]
to describe a precise asymptotic behavior of blowup solutions around a blowup point. Then the total integration as well as precise asymptotic behavior of solutions can be further determined. With this information the critical set
\Gamma
can be identified and if the total integral of
2b
does not tend to
\Gamma
we obtain a priori estimate. The idea to prove Theorem
1.2
is as follows: If the spherical harnack inequality is violated,
there should be finite small bubbling circles around the singular
source all tending to the singular source. Around each tiny bubbling
disk there is a Pohozaev identity, and a “big” ball that includes
all these tiny balls also has a Pohozaev identity. The comparison of
these Pohozaev identities implies that the strength of the singular
source has to be an integer.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In section
2

Q
-curvature equation on 4-manifolds. Theorem

is also established in this section. Finally we provide is the appendix an useful estimate of the difference between the geodesic distance and the Euclidean one for nearby points on the manifold.

2.1.
Asymptotic behavior of entire solutions

Our argument is progressive in nature and we shall obtain a rough estimate of
u
at infinity. For this purpose we set

(2.1)

v(x):=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\log\big(\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y,

which is obviously a solution of

(2.2)

\Delta^{2}v(x)=-6|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(x)},\quad{\rm in}\ \;\mathbb{R}^{4}.

The asymptotic behavior of
u
has a large to do with that of
v
, so in the first lemma we derive a rough upper bound of
v
.
For convenience we set

(2.3)

\alpha=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}dy.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
u
is a solution of (
1.12
) and let
\alpha
be given as in (
2.3
). Then

(2.4)

v(x)\leq\alpha\log|x|+C

for some constant
C
.

Proof
.

Since the goal is to describe asymptotic behavior it is natural to assume
|x|>4
. For such
x
, we decompose
\mathbb{R}^{4}=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
, where

A_{1}=\Big\{y:|y-x|\leq\frac{|x|}{2}\Big\},\quad A_{2}=\Big\{y:|y-x|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}\Big\}.

For
y\in A_{1}
,
\log\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\leq 0
because
|y|\geq|x|-|x-y|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}\geq|x-y|
, Thus

\int_{A_{1}}\log\big(\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y\leq 0

and

v(x)\leq\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{A_{2}}\log\big(\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y\leq 0.

To evaluate the integral over
A_{2}
, we first make two trivial observations:

|x-y|\leq|x|+|y|\leq|x||y|,\quad\mbox{if}\quad|y|\geq 2,

\log|x-y|\leq\log|x|+C,\quad\mbox{if }\quad|y|\leq 2

where
|x|>4
is used. Consequently

\begin{split}v(x)&\leq\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{A_{2}}\log\big(\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y\\
&\leq\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\Big\{\log|x|\int_{A_{2}\cap\{|y|\geq 2\}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y+\int_{A_{2}\cap\{|y|\leq 2\}}\log\big(\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y\Big\}\\
&\leq\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\Big\{\log|x|\int_{A_{2}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y+C\int_{A_{2}\cap\{|y|\leq 2\}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y\\
&\qquad\quad-\int_{A_{2}\cap\{|y|\leq 2\}}\big(\log|y|\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y\Big\}\\
&\leq\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\log|x|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}y+C.\end{split}

Lemma
2.1
is established.
∎

Before proving a lower bound of
v(x)
we derive an expression of
\Delta u(x)
in integral form.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
u
is a solution of (
1.12
). Then there exists a constant
C_{1}\geq 0
such that

(2.5)

\Delta u(x)=-\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y-C_{1}.

Proof
.

Let
w(x)=u(x)+v(x)
. Then from the equations of
u
and
v
in (
1.12
) and (
2.2
), we have
\Delta^{2}w=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Hence,
\Delta w
is a harmonic function in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. By the mean value property of harmonic functions, we have, for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
r>0
,

\begin{split}\Delta w(x_{0})=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}r^{4}}\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\Delta w(y){\rm d}y=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}r^{4}}\int_{\partial B(x_{0},r)}\Delta w(y){\rm d}\sigma,\end{split}

). Taking
\Omega=B_{R}
, we immediately obtain (
2.33
) from (
2.32
). Note that the singularity at the origin is insignificant that contributes nothing to the final form of the Pohozaev identity.

∎

From the Pohozaev identity we shall determine the exact value of
\alpha
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.12
). Assume
|u(x)|=o(|x|^{2})
at
\infty
, then
\alpha=2(1+\gamma)
.

Proof
.

Taking
Q(x)=6|x|^{4\gamma}
in (
2.33
), we have

(2.35)

\begin{split}&6(1+\gamma)\int_{B_{R}}|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}{\rm d}x\\
=&\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial B_{R}}6r^{4\gamma+1}e^{4u}{\rm d}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}r|\Delta u|^{2}-2\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\Delta u-\int_{\partial B_{R}}r\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\frac{\partial\Delta u}{\partial r}.\end{split}

In view of Lemma
2.4
, we have obtained

u(x)=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\log\big(\frac{|y|}{|x-y|}\big)|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y+C_{0},

and
e^{u(y)}\geq|y|^{-4\alpha}
for
|y|
large enough. Then
|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}\geq|y|^{4(\gamma-\alpha)}
. Hence
|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
implies
\alpha>1+\gamma
. On the other hand, by the representation of
u
and direct calculations, there hold

\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}(x)=-\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{x\cdot(x-y)}{|x||x-y|^{2}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y,

\Delta u(x)=-\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y,

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Delta u(x)=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{x\cdot(x-y)}{|x||x-y|^{4}}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}{\rm d}y,

Recall the definication of
u
, Lemma
2.4
and
\alpha>1+\gamma
, then we have

(2.36)

\begin{split}&\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}=0,\quad\lim_{r\to+\infty}r\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}=-\alpha,\\
&\lim_{r\to+\infty}r^{2}\Delta u=-2\alpha,\quad\lim_{r\to+\infty}r^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Delta u=4\alpha,\end{split}

where
r=|x|
. Therefore, applying the Pohozaev identity (
2.35
) and (
2.36
), we have

8\pi^{2}(1+\gamma)\alpha=4\pi^{2}\alpha^{2},

which leads to
\alpha=2(1+\gamma)
.

∎

Now we can determine the asymptotic behavior of
u
at infinity using the exact value of
\alpha
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.12
) and suppose
|u(x)|=o(|x|^{2})
at
\infty
. Then there exist
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tau>0
such that for
|x|>1
,

(2.37)

u(x)=-2(1+\gamma)\log|x|+c_{0}+O(|x|^{-\tau}),\quad|x|>1

and

(2.38)

\nabla^{j}(u(x)+2(1+\gamma)\log|x|)=O(|x|^{-\tau-j}),\quad j=1,2,3,4,\quad|x|>1.

In particular

(2.39)

-\Delta u(x)=\frac{4(1+\gamma)}{|x|^{2}}+O(|x|^{-2-\tau}),\quad|x|>1.

Proof
.

Let
w(x)=u(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}})-2(1+\gamma)\log|x|
, then from the equation of
u
and the assumption, we see
w
satisfies

(2.40)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta^{2}w(x)=6|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4w(x)},\quad{\rm in}\ \,\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\},\\
|w(x)|=o(\log\frac{1}{|x|}),\quad|\Delta w(x)|=o(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}),\quad{\rm as}\ \,|x|\to 0.\end{array}\right.

Let
h(x)
be a solution of

(2.41)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta^{2}h(x)=6|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4w(x)},&&{\rm in}\ \,B_{1}\setminus\{0\},\\
h(x)=w(x),\quad\Delta h(x)=\Delta w(x),&&{\rm on}\ \,\partial B_{1}.\end{array}\right.

and
q(x)=w(x)-h(x)
. Then
q(x)
satisfies

(2.42)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta^{2}q(x)=0,&&{\rm in}\ \,B_{1},\\
q(x)=\Delta q=0,&&{\rm on}\ \,\partial B_{1},\\
|q(x)|=o(\log\frac{1}{|x|}),\quad|\Delta q(x)|=o(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}),&&{\rm as}\ \,|x|\to 0.\end{array}\right.

First for
\Delta q
, since its growth near the singular source is weaker than fundamental solutions, the singularity is removable, thus
\Delta q=0
in
B_{1}
.
By exactly the same reason we further conclude that
q\equiv 0
in
B_{1}
. That means
w(x)=h(x)\in C^{0,\tau}(\bar{B}_{1})
. It suffices to consider the regularity of
h
in
B_{1}
.

Note that

\begin{split}|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4w(x)}&=|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}})-8(1+\gamma)\log|x|}\\
&\sim|x|^{4\gamma}|x|^{-8(1+\gamma)}|x|^{4\alpha}\sim|x|^{4\gamma},\quad{\rm near}\ \,0,\end{split}

where we used Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.6
.

By standard elliptic estimate, if
\gamma>-\frac{3}{4}
,
w\in C^{1,\tau}
for some
\tau>0
, if
-1<\gamma\leq-\frac{3}{4}
we have

for
\tau_{1}<4(1+\gamma)
. So in either case we use
\tau\in(0,1)
to have the following expansion of

:

u(x)=-2(1+\gamma)\log|x|+c_{0}+O(|x|^{-\tau}),\quad|x|>1.

Based on this we can use standard elliptic estimate to obtain corresponding gradient estimates: now
|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}
can be written as

(2.43)

|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}=e^{c_{0}}r^{-8-4\gamma}+O(r^{-8-4\gamma-\tau}),\quad r=|x|>1

Using this in the expression of
u(x)
in (
2.26
) and

in (

) it is easy to obtain (

) for

. Then the asymptotic behavior for other derivatives in (

∎

Remark 2.8

It is important to observe from (

2.2.

Suppose that

1.12
) with
|u(x)|=o(|x|^{2})
at
\infty

in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and (
2.37
)
\sim
(
2.38

. Then by Lemma

(2.44)

v(x)=\frac{4(1+\gamma)}{|x|^{2}}+O(|x|^{-2-\tau}),\quad|x|>1.

First, we state some conventional notations for moving planes. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let

,
\Sigma_{\lambda}=\{x:x_{1}>\lambda\}

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma
2.6
establishes (i) in Theorem
1.3
. Next we aim to prove the radial symmetry of solutions by the method of moving planes.

Step 1:

We start moving planes along
x_{1}
-direction. For any
\lambda
, we consider
w_{\lambda}(x)=u(x)-u(x^{\lambda})
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. Then
w_{\lambda}(x)
satisfies

(2.45)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta^{2}w_{\lambda}(x)=b_{\lambda}(x)w_{\lambda}(x),&&{\rm in}\ \;\Sigma_{\lambda},\\
w_{\lambda}(x)=\Delta w_{\lambda}(x)=0,&&{\rm on}\ \;\partial T_{\lambda},\end{array}\right.

where

b_{\lambda}(x)=6\frac{|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(x)}-|x^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(x^{\lambda})}}{u(x)-u(x^{\lambda})}.

First we claim that there exists
\lambda_{0}<0
such that
w_{\lambda_{0}}>0
and
v(x)-v_{\lambda_{0}}(x)>0
in
\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}
.
Using the expression of
u
in (
2.26
) we can obviously write

as

(2.46)

u(x)-u_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\log\frac{|x^{\lambda}-y|}{|x-y|}|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}dy=\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\Sigma_{\lambda}},

where
u_{\lambda}(x)=u(x^{\lambda})
. Changing the integration over
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\Sigma_{\lambda}
to
\Sigma_{\lambda}
by a change of variable:

z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})=(2\lambda-y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})
we see that

(2.47)

u(x)-u_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\log\frac{|x^{\lambda}-y|}{|x-y|}(|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y^{\lambda})})dy.

Here we remark that writing
w_{\lambda}
in the form of (
2.47
) is crucial for our argument, it prevents us from very delicate asymptotic analysis for
u
and its derivatives.
From (
2.47
) we claim that when
\lambda
is very negative and when
x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
,
u-u_{\lambda}>0
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. Indeed, first it is obvious that
\log(|x^{\lambda}-y|/|x-y|)>0

B_{R}
and outside

|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}>c_{0}>0

|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y^{\lambda})}<c_{0}

\lambda

y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}

by Remark
2.8

|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}>|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y^{\lambda})}\mbox{ because }\,\,|y^{\lambda}|>|y|.

We also observe that for
v=-\Delta u
,

v(x)-v_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}})|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}dy.

After a similar transformation we have

v(x)-v_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}})(|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y)}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}e^{4u(y^{\lambda})})dy.

By exactly the same reasoning we see that for
\lambda
very negative,
v(x)>v_{\lambda}(x)
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
.

Let
\lambda_{0}
be the starting position for the moving plane process and let
\bar{\lambda}\leq 0
be the upper limit:

(2.48)

\bar{\lambda}:=\sup\big\{\lambda\leq 0:v(x^{\mu})<v(x)\quad{\rm for\ \,all}\ \,x\in\Sigma_{\mu}\ \,{\rm and}\ \,\mu\leq\lambda\big\}.

Next we claim that
\bar{\lambda}=0
. If this is not the case, we have
\bar{\lambda}<0
. From the equation for
w_{\bar{\lambda}}
:

(2.49)

\Delta^{2}w_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)=6|x^{\bar{\lambda}}|^{4\gamma}(e^{4u(x)}-e^{4u_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)})+6(|x|^{4\gamma}-|x^{\bar{\lambda}}|^{4\gamma})e^{4u(x)},\quad x\in\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}}.

we see that on one hand the continuity gives
\Delta w_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)\leq 0
, since
w_{\bar{\lambda}}\to 0
as
|x|\to+\infty
and
w_{\bar{\lambda}}\big|_{T_{\bar{\lambda}}}=0
. On the other hand we have
|x^{\bar{\lambda}}|>|x|
. This strict inequality and the strong maximum principle combined gives

w_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)>0,\quad\Delta w_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)<0,\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}}.

Then we claim that for
\epsilon>0
small we still have

(2.50)

v(x^{\mu})<v(x),\quad\mbox{for all }x\in\Sigma_{\mu}\quad\mbox{ and }\mu\leq\bar{\lambda}+\epsilon.

Clearly once (
2.50
) is verified, we obtain a contradiction to the definition of
\bar{\lambda}
.
To prove (
2.50
) we first make a trivial observation: For any fixed
R>>1
, Hopf lemma and
w_{\bar{\lambda}}>0
in
\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}}\cap B_{R}
means that if
\lambda
is slightly greater than
\bar{\lambda}
, we still have
w_{\lambda}>0
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}
. Thus we only consider
|x|>R
. We use two different expressions of
w_{\lambda}
: One is (
2.46
), which will be used for crude estimate, the other one is based on (
2.47
):

(2.51)

w_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\log\frac{|x^{\lambda}-y|}{|x-y|}\bigg(|y|^{4\gamma}(e^{4u}-e^{4u_{\lambda}})+(|y|^{4\gamma}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma})e^{4u}\bigg)dy.

Similarly for
v-v_{\lambda}
we have

(2.52)

\displaystyle v(x)-v_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}})\bigg(|y|^{4\gamma}(e^{4u}-e^{4u_{\lambda}})+(|y|^{4\gamma}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma})e^{4u}\bigg)dy.

Here it is important to point out that since
\bar{\lambda}<0
, a perturbation of
\epsilon
still satisfies
\bar{\lambda}+\epsilon<0
.
Writing
w_{\lambda}=w_{\lambda}^{+}-w_{\lambda}^{-}
, our goal is to prove that
w_{\lambda}^{-}\equiv 0
for
\lambda
slightly greater than
\bar{\lambda}
. We claim that there exist
\epsilon,C>0
such that

(2.53)

w_{\lambda}^{-}(x)\leq C|x|^{-1-\epsilon},\quad\mbox{for}\quad|x|>1

The proof of (
2.53
) is by iteration. Note that
w_{\lambda}^{-}(x)=0
when
|x|<R
. First using (
2.46
) we have

w_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\log(1+\frac{|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}-|x-y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}})|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}dy=\int_{E_{1}}+\int_{E_{2}}+\int_{E_{3}}.

where
E_{1}=B(0,|x|/2)
,
E_{2}=B(x,|x|/2)
and
E_{3}=\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})
. The estimate on
E_{1}
is

|\int_{E_{1}}|\leq\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{E_{1}}|\log(1+\frac{4(y_{1}-\lambda)(x_{1}-\lambda)}{|x-y|^{2}})|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4u}|dy.

Since
|x-y|\sim|x|
it is easy to obtain an upper bound of
O(|x|^{-4\mu})
. The integration on
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
has an upper bound
O(|x|^{-4\mu+\epsilon})
.
Thus

(2.54)

w_{\lambda}^{-}(x)\leq C|x|^{-4\mu+\epsilon}.

Using (
2.54
) in (
2.51
) we have

w_{\lambda}(x)\geq-\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\log\frac{|x^{\lambda}-y|}{|x-y|}4|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}w_{\lambda}^{-}(y)dy.

where
\xi
comes from the mean value theorem. For each
x
satisfying
w_{\lambda}(x)\leq 0
, we obtain from standard estimates that

w_{\lambda}^{-}(x)\leq C|x|^{-8\mu+\epsilon}.

After finite steps we have (
2.53
).

Now we use (
2.53
) to evaluate
v-v_{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle v(x)-v_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}})(4|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}w_{\lambda}^{-}(y)+(|y|^{4\gamma}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma})e^{4u}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{6}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{(y_{1}-\lambda)(x_{1}-\lambda)}{|x-y|^{2}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}}(4|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}w_{\lambda}^{-}(y)+(|y|^{4\gamma}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}))e^{4u}dy.

Note that
|y|^{4\gamma}-|y^{\lambda}|^{4\gamma}>0
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
and

in
\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}
for some positive

. After integrating the second term, which is positive, we have

v(x)-v_{\lambda}(x)\geq-\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(\frac{(y_{1}-\lambda)(x_{1}-\lambda)}{|x-y|^{2}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}})4|y|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}w_{\lambda}^{-}(y))dy+c_{0}(x_{1}-\lambda)|x|^{-4}

for

large. Then using (

is positive even for

slightly larger than
\bar{\lambda}
. This is certainly a contradiction to the definition of
\bar{\lambda}
. Thus we have proved that
\bar{\lambda}=0
, which
means
u(x)\geq u(x^{\lambda})
in the

direction. Applying the moving plane method to all directions we obtain the radial symmetry of

.

Step 2:

Now we prove the uniqueness of the solution of (
1.12
) modulus scaling in (
1.13
).
Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be two radial solutions of (
1.12
) satisfying
w_{1}(0)=w_{2}(0)
. Our goal is
w_{1}\equiv w_{2}
. First we make a remark about the smoothness of

. Indeed, by Lemma

we can find some large

such that

is integrable, which together with
\gamma>-1
implies that the right hand side is

for some

. Therefore,
\Delta w_{i}\in L^{q_{1}}
for
q_{1}=\frac{2p_{1}}{2-p_{1}}>2
. By Sobolev embedding theorems we obtain the regularity of
w_{i}
:
w_{i}\in C^{0,2-\frac{4}{q_{1}}}
if
q_{1}<4
, and
w_{i}\in C^{1,1-\frac{4}{q_{1}}}
if
q_{1}\geq 4
. Thus if we denote
\alpha_{1}=2-\frac{4}{q_{1}}=4-\frac{4}{p_{1}}
, we have

(2.55)

(w_{1}-w_{2})(x)=O(|x|^{\alpha_{1}}).

The equation of
w_{1}-w_{2}
reads

\Delta^{2}(w_{1}-w_{2})=6|x|^{4\gamma}(e^{4w_{1}}-e^{4w_{2}})=24|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}(w_{1}-w_{2})

where
\xi
comes from the mean value theorem. If
\gamma>\frac{1}{p_{1}}-1
, which means
4\gamma+\alpha_{1}>0
, we can choose some
q>2
such that the right hand side is
L^{q}
. By elliptic estimates, we obtain that
w_{1}-w_{2}\in W^{4,q}\subset C^{2}
. Otherwise when
-1<\gamma\leq\frac{1}{p_{1}}-1
, by using the same method, we know the right hand side of this equation is
L^{p_{2}}
integrable near the origin for some
p_{2}
in

(2.56)

p_{1}<p_{2}<\frac{1}{\frac{1}{p_{1}}-(1+\gamma)},\quad\mbox{if }\quad\frac{1}{p_{1}}>1+\gamma.

(
2.56
) leads to a better regularity of
(w_{1}-w_{2})(x)=O(|x|^{\alpha_{2}})
for some
\alpha_{2}=4-\frac{4}{p_{2}}>\alpha_{1}
. Obviously this boot-strap argument leads to
\{p_{k}\}
and
\{\alpha_{k}\}
such that if
|x|^{4\gamma}e^{4\xi}(w_{1}-w_{2})\in L^{p_{k}}
then
(w_{1}-w_{2})(x)=O(|x|^{\alpha_{k}})
. Moreover,
p_{k+1}>p_{k}
and
\alpha_{k}=4-\frac{4}{p_{k}}
. For given
\gamma>-1
, we can obtain some
p_{k_{0}}
such that
1+\gamma-\frac{1}{p_{k_{0}}}>0
after finite steps. Therefore, we can find some
q>2
such that the right hand side is
L^{q}
, which means
w_{1}-w_{2}\in W^{4,q}\subset C^{2}
as well. Note that since
w_{i}
are radial, we certainly have
(w_{1}-w_{2})^{\prime}(0)=0
. By the uniqueness of ODE, we only need to prove
(w_{1}-w_{2})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)=0
.

If
(w_{1}-w_{2})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)<0
,
w_{1}(r)<w_{2}(r)
for small
r>0
. We will prove
w_{1}(r)<w_{2}(r)
for all
r>0
. Suppose there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
w_{1}(r_{0})=w_{2}(r_{0})
and
w_{1}(r)<w_{2}(r)
for

. Then by (
1.12
), we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Delta(w_{1}(r)-w_{2}(r))=6r^{4\gamma}\big(e^{4w_{1}(r)}-e^{4w_{2}(r)}\big)<0,\quad 0<r\leq r_{0},

which together with the assumption implies

\Delta(w_{1}-w_{2})<0,\quad{\rm in}\ \,B(0,r_{0}).

Since
w_{1}(r_{0})-w_{2}(r_{0})=0
, from the maximum principle, we have
w_{1}(r)-w_{2}(r)>0
for

, which contradicts with
w_{1}(0)=w_{2}(0)
. Thus,
w_{1}(r)<w_{2}(r)
for all
r>0
. Hence
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Delta(w_{1}(r)-w_{2}(r))<0
for all
r>0
, which means
\Delta(w_{1}(r)-w_{2}(r))
is decreasing in
r
. Thus
w_{1}(r)-w_{2}(r)\leq-cr^{2}
as

for some constant
c>0
, which yields a contradiction to the assumption
w_{i}(r)=o(r^{2})
at
\infty
.

Similarly, it is impossible for
(w_{1}-w_{2})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)>0
. Thus, the radial solution of (
1.12
) is unique under the scaling
u_{\lambda}(x)=u(\lambda x)+(1+\gamma)\log\lambda
for some
\lambda>0
, and it is valid for (
2.14
) after scaling.

∎

Proof of Corollary
1.4
:
First we use the rough expansion of
u
in (
2.37
) to rewrite

as

Here we use
\mu=1+\gamma>0
for convenience.
Then for
v=-\Delta u
, we have (see (
2.39
))

\lim_{r\to\infty}v^{\prime}(r)r^{3}=-8\mu.

The equation for
v
can be written as

v^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{3}{r}v^{\prime}(r)=6e^{4c_{0}}r^{-4-4\mu}+O(r^{-4-4\mu-\tau}),\quad r>1.

Multiplying
r^{3}
to both sides and integrating from
r
to
\infty
, we have

r^{3}v^{\prime}(r)+8\mu=-\frac{6e^{4c_{0}}}{4\mu}r^{-4\mu}+O(r^{-4\mu-\tau}),\quad r>1.

Thus

v^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{8\mu}{r^{3}}-\frac{3e^{4c_{0}}}{2\mu}r^{-3-4\mu}+O(r^{-3-4\mu-\tau}),\quad r>1

and

v(r)=\frac{4\mu}{r^{2}}+\frac{3e^{4c_{0}}}{2\mu(2+4\mu)}r^{-2-4\mu}+O(r^{-2-4\mu-\tau}),\quad r>1,

where
\lim_{r\to\infty}v(r)=0
is used. Multiplying the expression of
v
, which is
-u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{3}{r}u^{\prime}
, we have

(2.57)

(r^{3}u^{\prime})^{\prime}(r)=-4\mu r-\frac{3e^{4c_{0}}}{2\mu(2+4\mu)}r^{1-4\mu}+E,

where
E=O(r^{1-4\mu-\tau})
. Here we discuss under two cases, either
\gamma>-\frac{3}{4}
(which is
\mu>\frac{1}{4}
) or
\mu\leq\frac{1}{4}
. In the first case
M=[\frac{1}{4\mu}]=0
, so our goal is to prove

(2.58)

u(r)=-2\mu\log r+c_{0}+O(|x|^{-1}),\quad|x|>1.

Integrating (
2.57
) from
1
to
r
, we have

u^{\prime}(r)=\frac{c}{r^{3}}-\frac{2\mu}{r}+\frac{1}{r^{3}}\int_{1}^{r}E(s)ds,

where
c
is a constant. Integrating the above again from
1
to
r
we obtain (
2.58
). Now we consider the case
\mu\leq\frac{1}{4}
. In this case
we use the fact that
\tau=4\mu-\epsilon
. Integration from
1
to
r
we have

(2.59)

u(r)=-2\mu\log r+c_{0}+c_{1}r^{-4\mu}+O(r^{-8\mu+\epsilon})+O(r^{-1}),\quad r>1

where

c_{1}=\frac{3e^{4c_{0}}}{32\mu^{2}(1+2\mu)(1-2\mu)}.

Thus if
\mu>\frac{1}{8}
the expansion has an error term
O(r^{-1})
and is finished (here we also note that in this case
M=[\frac{1}{4\mu}]=1
).
So we only need to consider the case
\mu\leq\frac{1}{8}
.
Now (
2.59
) has improved the estimate of
r^{4\gamma}e^{4u}
to

(2.60)

r^{4\gamma}e^{4u}=e^{4c_{0}}r^{-4\mu-4}+e^{4(c_{0}+c_{1})}r^{-4-8\mu}+O(r^{-4-12\mu+\epsilon}),\quad r>1.

Using (
2.60
) in computation we obtain

u(r)=-2\mu\log r+c_{0}+c_{1}r^{-4\mu}+c_{2}r^{-8\mu}+O(r^{-12\mu+\epsilon})+O(r^{-1}),\quad r>1

where

c_{2}=\frac{3e^{4(c_{0}+c_{1})}}{128\mu^{2}(1+4\mu)(1-4\mu)}.

This expression further improves the estimate of
r^{4\gamma}e^{4u}
.
In general

c_{l}=\frac{3e^{4(c_{0}+...+c_{l-1})}}{32l^{2}\mu^{2}(1-2l\mu)(1+2l\mu)}.

Obviously this process can be finished in finite steps as
\mu>0
.
Finally the expansions of
\Delta u
as well as other derivatives can be obtained in standard argument. Corollary
1.4
is established.
\Box

3.
Preliminaries for blowup analysis

Let
G:M\times M\setminus{\rm diag}
denote the Green’s function for the Paneitz operator

(3.1)

f(x)-\bar{f}=\int_{M}G(x,y)P_{g}f(y){\rm d}V_{g}(y),\quad\int_{M}G(x,y){\rm d}V_{g}(y)=0,

where
\bar{f}=\frac{1}{{\rm vol}_{g}(M)}\int_{M}f{\rm d}V_{g}
is the average of
f
over
M
. Then the weak form of (
3.1
) is

(3.2)

P_{g,y}G(x,y)=\delta_{x}-\frac{1}{{\rm vol}_{g}(M)}.

Set
R
be the regular part of the Green function. Then by the Appendix A in
[
52
]
, for
y
in a neighborhood of
x
,

(3.3)

G(x,y)=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log d_{g}(x,y)\chi+R(x,y).

where
\chi
is a cut-off function to avoid cut locus.
Using
G
we can decompose
u_{k}
as the sum of its regular part and singular part

(3.4)

u_{k}(x)=\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-8\pi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{j}G(x,q_{j}).

Then
\tilde{u}_{k}
satisfies

(3.5)

P_{g}\tilde{u}_{k}+2b_{k}=2H_{k}e^{4\tilde{u}_{k}}\quad{\rm in}\ \,M,

where

(3.6)

H_{k}(x)=h_{k}(x)\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-32\pi^{2}\gamma_{j}G(x,q_{j})}.

Clearly, (
1.9
) and (
1.11
) imply that

(3.7)

\int_{M}H_{k}e^{4\tilde{u}_{k}}{\rm d}V_{g}\leq C

We will work with
\tilde{u}_{k}
in the later blow-up analysis.

Similar with
[
52
]
, since the metric
g
may not be locally conformally flat, we will apply the
conformal normal coordinates
, whose existence has been proved in
[
29
]
. More specially, for
q\in M
, there exists a normal coordinate around
q
such that
g
can be deformed to
\mathfrak{g}
which satisfies
\det\,(\mathfrak{g})=1
. We use
R_{ijkl}
to denote the curvature tensor under
\mathfrak{g}
.

We will apply the expansions of the metric
\mathfrak{g}
and its derivatives in the conformal normal coordinates (seen in the Appendix B of
[
52
]
), which are

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\mathfrak{g}_{ab}(x)=\delta_{ab}+\frac{1}{3}R_{aijb}x^{i}x^{j}+O(r^{3}),\\
&\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(x)=\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{3}R_{aijb}x^{i}x^{j}+O(r^{3}),\\
&\partial_{c}\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(x)=-\frac{1}{3}(R_{acib}+R_{aicb})x^{i}+O(r^{2}),\\
&\partial_{ab}\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(x)=\frac{1}{3}R_{ia,a}x^{i}+O(r^{2}),\end{split}

In addition, the following Pohozaev identity from the Appendix D in
[
52
]
will play an important role when the blow-up analysis is carried out in the conformal normal coordinates.

Lemma 2
.

[
52
]

For equation
P_{g}u+2b=2he^{4u}
in
M
and
\Omega=B(0,r)
, there holds

(3.9)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\Big(2he^{4u}+\frac{1}{2}x^{i}\partial_{i}he^{4u}\Big)\\
=&\int_{\partial\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{2}x^{i}\nu_{i}he^{4u}-x^{k}\nu_{j}g^{ij}\partial_{i}(\Delta_{g}u)\partial_{k}u+\nu_{j}g^{ij}\Delta_{g}u\partial_{i}u+x^{k}\nu_{j}g^{ij}\Delta_{g}u\partial_{ik}u-\frac{1}{2}x^{i}\nu_{i}(\Delta_{g}u)^{2}\Big)\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\Big(\Delta_{g}u\partial_{i}g^{ij}\partial_{j}u+x^{k}\Delta_{g}u\partial_{ik}g^{ij}\partial_{j}u+x^{k}\Delta_{g}u\partial_{k}g^{ij}\partial_{ij}u-2bx^{i}\partial_{i}u\Big)\\
&+2\int_{\partial\Omega}\Big(R_{ij,l}(q)x^{l}x^{k}\nu_{i}\partial_{j}u\partial_{k}u+O(r^{3})|\nabla u|^{2}\Big)\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\Big(2R_{ij,l}(q)\big(x^{l}\partial_{j}u\partial_{i}u+x^{k}x^{l}\partial_{j}u\partial_{ik}u\big)+O(r^{2})|\nabla u|^{2}+O(r^{4})|\nabla^{2}u|\Big)\end{split}

Note that we use
B(p,r)
to denote a ball centered at
p
with radius
r
. Sometimes if the center is the origin, we use
B_{r}
instead of
B(0,r)
.

4.
Blow-up analysis near the singularity

In this section, we focus on the blow-up analysis near
q_{j}
, and to simplify the notation, we will omit the subscript
j
. Similar to the argument in
[
52
]
, we will work in the conformal normal coordinates near
q
from
[
29
]
. To be specific, we can find some function
w
defined on
M
, such that in a small neighborhood
B(q,\delta)
of
q
,
\delta>0
, we have

(4.1)

\det\,(\hat{g})=1

in the normal coordinates of the conformal metric
\hat{g}=e^{2w}g
. For convenience we just use
g
instead of
\hat{g}
. Note that in a neighborhood of
q
,

(4.2)

w(x)=O(d_{g}(x,q)^{2}).

where
d_{g}(x,q)
stands for the distance between
x
and
q
under metric
g
.

Using the conformal covariance property of
P_{g}

the function
\mathfrak{u}_{k}=\tilde{u}_{k}-w
satisfies

(4.3)

P_{g}\mathfrak{u}_{k}+2\mathfrak{b}_{k}=2H_{k}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}},

and

(4.4)

\int_{M}H_{k}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}V_{g}\leq C,

where
2\mathfrak{b}_{k}=P_{g}w+2b_{k}
and
\tilde{H}_{k}=H_{k}e^{4w}
. For simplity, we still denote
\tilde{H}_{k}
by
H_{k}
.

We still use
G
to denote the Green’s function for
P_{g}
. Then we have the following Green’s representation formula

(4.5)

\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)=\bar{\mathfrak{u}}_{k}+2\int_{M}G(x,y)H_{k}(y)e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(y)}{\rm d}V_{g}(y)-2\int_{M}G(x,y)\mathfrak{b}_{k}(y){\rm d}V_{g}(y),

where
\bar{\mathfrak{u}}_{k}
is the average of
\mathfrak{u}_{k}
over
(M,g)
. Using the expression of
G
in (
3.3
), we have

(4.6)

\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)=\bar{\mathfrak{u}}_{k}+2\int_{M}\Big(-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log d_{g}(x,y)\chi\Big)H_{k}(y)e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(y)}{\rm d}V_{g}(y)+\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(x),

where

(4.7)

\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(x)=2\int_{M}R(x,y)H_{k}(y)e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(y)}{\rm d}V_{g}(y)-2\int_{M}G(x,y)\mathfrak{b}_{k}(y){\rm d}V_{g}(y).

Note that
\det\,(g)=1
in
B(q,\delta)
, we have
{\rm d}V_{g}(y)={\rm d}y
in
B(q,\delta)
. Taking the difference of (
4.6
) evaluated at
x
and
q
, we get

(4.8)

\begin{split}&\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)-\mathfrak{u}_{k}(q)\\
=&\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M}\big(\chi(r_{q})\log|y-q|-\chi(r_{x})\log d_{g}(x,y)\big)H_{k}(y)e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(y)}{\rm d}V_{g}(y)+\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(x)-\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(q),\end{split}

where
r_{q}=|y-q|
and
r_{x}=d_{g}(x,y)
. Here since the coordinates are normal, we have
d_{g}(y,q)=|y-q|
.

Thanks to the cut-off function
\chi
, we can replace the integral over
M
by an integral over
B(q,2\delta)
:

(4.9)

\begin{split}&\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)-\mathfrak{u}_{k}(q)\\
=&\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{B(q,4\delta)}\big(\chi(r_{q})\log|y-q|-\chi(r_{x})\log d_{g}(x,y)\big)H_{k}(y)e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(y)}{\rm d}V_{g}(y)\\
&+\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(x)-\mathfrak{\phi}_{k}(q),\quad x\in B(q,2\delta).\end{split}

We next give an upper bound of the mass near
q
when
\mathfrak{u}_{k}
cannot blow up at
q
. Before stating such a small energy lemma we point out that the function
H_{k}
can be written in the neighborhood of a singular source
q
as

(4.10)

H_{k}(x)=\mathfrak{h}_{k}(x)d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma},

with
\mathfrak{h}_{k}(q)\neq 0
. Using this notation, our result states as follows:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
q
be a singular source with index
\gamma
. If

(4.11)

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B(q,2\delta)}2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)}{\rm d}V_{g}<\min\{8\pi^{2},8\pi^{2}(1+\gamma)\},

\mathfrak{h}_{k}
is defined in (
4.10
).
Then
\mathfrak{u}_{k}\leq C
in
B(q,\delta)
.

Proof.

Note that

P_{g}\mathfrak{u}_{k}=2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)}-2\mathfrak{b}_{k}.

We write
\mathfrak{u}_{k}=u_{1k}+u_{2k}
where
u_{1k}
is the solution of

(4.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta^{2}u_{1k}=2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)}&&{\rm in}\ \,B(q,2\delta)\\
u_{1k}(x)=\Delta u_{1k}(x)=0&&{\rm on}\ \,\partial B(q,2\delta).\end{array}\right.

By Lemma 2.3 of
[
31
]
we have

(4.13)

\int_{B(q,2\delta)}\exp\Big\{\frac{\tilde{\delta}|u_{1k}|}{\parallel 2H_{k}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}\parallel_{L^{1}(B(q,2\delta),g)}}\Big\}{\rm d}V_{g}\leq C,

with any
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,32\pi^{2})
and some constant
C=C(\tilde{\delta},\delta)
. On one hand in
B(q,2\delta)
,

(4.14)

u_{1k}(x)=\int_{B(q,\delta)}G_{\delta}(x,y)2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(\eta,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}\eta,\quad x\in B(q,2\delta)

where
G_{\delta}(x,y)
is the Green’s function of
\Delta^{2}
on
B(q,2\delta)
:

G_{\delta}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log|x-y|+R_{\delta}(x,y),

with

G_{\delta}(x,y)=\Delta_{y}G_{\delta}(x,y)=0,\quad\mbox{for }x\in B(q,2\delta),y\in\partial B(q,2\delta).

In particular for
x\in B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta)
,

u_{1k}(x)=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{B(q,2\delta)}\log|x-q|2\mathfrak{h}_{k}|\eta-q|^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}\eta+O(1),\quad x\in B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta).

On the other hand the Green’s representation formula of
\mathfrak{u}_{k}
gives

\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)=u_{1k}(x)+u_{2k}(x)=\overline{\mathfrak{u}_{k}}+\int_{M}G(x,\eta)2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}\eta.

Since the leading term of
G
and
G_{\delta}
are both
-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log d_{g}(x,q)
, we have

u_{2k}(x)=\overline{\mathfrak{u}_{k}}+O(1).

From
\int_{M}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}V_{g}\leq C
and Jensen’s inequality

e^{4\bar{\mathfrak{u}}_{k}}\leq\int_{M}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}}{\rm d}V_{g}\leq C.

Therefore,
u_{2k}\leq C
in
B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta)
. Now we focus on
u_{1k}
.

If
\gamma\geq 0
, (
4.11
) is

\int_{B(q,2\delta)}2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)}{\rm d}V_{g}<8\pi^{2}.

Since
u_{2k}
is bounded from above in
B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta)
, we see from
(
4.13
) that there exists some
p>1
to make
e^{4u_{1k}}\in L^{p}(B(q,2\delta))
:

(4.15)

\parallel 2\mathfrak{h}_{k}d_{g}(x,q)^{4\gamma}e^{4\mathfrak{u}_{k}(x)}\parallel_{L^{p}(B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta))}\leq C.

The estimate (
4.15
) leads to a
L^{\infty}
bound of
u_{1k}
in
B(q,\delta)
based on two reasons. First
the integration of (
4.14
) gives a
L^{1}
bound of
u_{1k}
in
B(q,\frac{3}{2}\delta)
:

(4.16)

An immediate consequence of Lemma

Theorem 4.2

(Concentration and Quantization)

We recall that
