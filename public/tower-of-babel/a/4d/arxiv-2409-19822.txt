Stability of vortex quadrupoles with odd-odd symmetry
Kyudong Choi, In-Jee Jeong, Yao Yao

Abstract
. For the 2D incompressible Euler equations, we establish global-in-time ( t ∈ ℝ t\in\mathbb{R} ) stability of vortex quadrupoles satisfying odd symmetry with respect to both axes. Specifically, if the vorticity restricted to a quadrant is signed, sufficiently concentrated and close to its radial rearrangement up to a translation in L 1 L^{1} , we prove that it remains so for all times. The main difficulty is that the kinetic energy maximization problem in a quadrant – the typical approach for establishing vortex stability – lacks a solution, as the kinetic energy continues to increase when the vorticity escapes to infinity. We overcome this by taking dynamical information into account: finite-time desingularization result is combined with monotonicity of the first moment and a careful analysis of the interaction energies between vortices. The latter is achieved by new pointwise estimates on the Biot–Savart kernel and quantitative stability results for general interaction kernels. Moreover, with a similar strategy we obtain stability of a pair of opposite-signed Lamb dipoles moving away from each other.

1.
Introduction

The spontaneous creation of large-scale vortex structures is one of the most fascinating features of two-dimensional incompressible fluid motion, clearly demonstrating the tendency of vorticity to concentrate. These structures often appear as nearly radial vortices or symmetric dipoles (a pair of counter-rotating vortices), which suggests a strong form of asymptotic stability. While it is highly challenging to establish the formation of such coherent vortex structures from generic initial data, a more feasible goal is to understand vortex dynamics near these configurations and establish their neutral (Lyapunov) stability.

A typical mathematical framework for construction and stability for vortex structures, including radial vortex and dipoles (See Figure
1
(a) for an illustration of the Lamb dipole), is to consider the kinetic energy maximization problem under appropriate constraints, for the planar incompressible Euler equations. In the case of dipoles, one may consider the Euler equations on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and impose a constraint on the impulse (vertical first moment).

Existing results on dipole stability, despite overcoming significant technical challenges, are often quite restrictive: not only must the vorticity be a small perturbation in
L^{p}

type norms of a certain single dipole, but it must also share the same sign as the dipole throughout
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. These assumptions are crucial within the variational framework, as otherwise the structure of the energy-maximizing set becomes genuinely different. Notably, both assumptions fail in the case of two dipoles with opposite signs, even when they are positioned far apart; see Figure
1
(b) for an illustration.

Figure 1.

(a) Illustration of a Lamb dipole, which is a traveling wave solution of the 2D Euler equation. (b) For a pair of opposite-signed Lamb dipoles far away from each other, there has been no stability result in the literature.

This paper takes a step beyond the traditional approach by establishing the dynamical stability of a pair of dipoles moving away from each other, and more generally, for vortex quadrupoles under odd-odd symmetry (odd symmetry with respect to both axes). In such configurations, the problem reduces to considering a single vortex in the first quadrant
Q:=(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
. However, this domain lacks translation symmetry, and kinetic energy maximizers do not exist, under constraints which are left invariant in time. Our new strategy is to incorporate dynamical information and derive quantitative estimates for possible energy variations of
individual vortices
along the Euler flow under odd-odd symmetry.

1.2.
Discussion

Our main results provide stability of several types of vortex quadrupoles (in the sense that the vorticity is highly concentrated near four points in the plane) under the odd-odd symmetry. This seems to be the first stability result for vorticities which are not relative equilibria of the Euler equations. More precisely, our first theorem, together with Remark
2.9
, gives stability for a pair of energy-maximizing dipoles, and the second theorem for odd-odd quadrupoles of radial and monotone decreasing vorticities. These two results are closely related, as energy maximizing dipoles are in general asymptotically radial and monotone in the large impulse limit (see
[
5
,
7
]
,
[
32
, Figure 7]
). The second main result can be also interpreted as a solution to the global desingularization problem for point vortex motion using any radial and monotone decreasing vorticity. To expand upon this point, let us compare our results with recent exciting developments in the desingularization problem under the odd-odd symmetry.

1.3.
Overview of the proof strategy

The basic idea behind our main results is that, if the initial vorticity consists of two dipoles faraway from each other, and each dipole is close to the kinetic energy maximizer under some constraint, then their shapes must remain close to the maximizer for all positive times – if not, it would lower the kinetic energy.

We start with a well-known observation that is used in both of our main results. Note that the total kinetic energy
E[\omega(t)]
is conserved, and in the odd-odd setting, by breaking any non-trivial vorticity into its left and right parts as

\omega(\cdot,t)=\omega(\cdot,t)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>0\}}+\omega(\cdot,t)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}<0\}}=:\omega^{r}(\cdot,t)-\omega^{l}(\cdot,t),

the kinetic energy can be decomposed into “dipole energy” and “interaction energy” as follows, where
N(x-y):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|
: (see Figure
4
for an illustration)

\begin{split}E[\omega(t)]&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\omega^{r}(x,t)-\omega^{l}(x,t)\right)\left(\omega^{r}(y,t)-\omega^{l}(y,t)\right)N(x-y)dxdy\\
&=2\underbrace{\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega^{r}(x,t)\omega^{r}(y,t)N(x-y)dxdy}_{=:E_{\text{dipole}}(t)=E[\omega^{r}(t)]}\,-\,2\underbrace{\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega^{r}(x,t)\omega^{l}(y,t)N(x-y)dxdy}_{=:E_{\text{inter}}(t)},\end{split}

where in the last step we used that
E[\omega^{l}(t)]=E[\omega^{r}(t)]
. Furthermore, in the odd-odd setting where
\omega_{0}\geq 0
in
Q
, it is not difficult to check that
E_{\text{inter}}(t)>0
for all

. So if
\omega_{0}
consists of two dipoles that are sufficiently far away such that

(1.8)

E[\omega^{r}(0)]-E[\omega^{r}(t)]\ll 1\quad\text{ for all }t,

where the right hand side can be made arbitrarily small by placing the two dipoles far away initially.

. (Note that this is the setting in Corollary
1.3
and illustrated in Figure
3
):
\omega_{0}|_{Q}=\mathbf{1}_{D}
where
D
is an open set with
|D|=1
.
Furthermore, it is conceptually simpler to divide the stability proof into two cases, depending on the location of the center
p_{0}=:(l_{0},h_{0})\in Q
: (i)
l_{0}\gg h_{0}
and (ii)
l_{0}\lesssim h_{0}
.

The first case is the heart of the proof, and we prove it in Theorem
3.3
. Again, our goal is to use (
1.8
) together with Lemma
1.4
to conclude that
\omega(t)|_{Q}
stays close to a disk patch for all
t\geq 0
. Recall that for a
single
disk patch in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(called the
Rankine vortex
), it is well-known to be the energy maximizer among all patches with the same area, and there is a quantitative energy estimate. Namely, denoting by
\bar{\omega}_{R}
the disk patch with area 1,
[
49
,
42
,
48
,
41
,
16
]
showed that among all
\omega=\mathbf{1}_{D}
with
|D|=1
, one has

(1.11)

\begin{split}E[\bar{\omega}_{R}]-E[\omega]\gtrsim\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\bar{\omega}_{R}(\cdot-a)-\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{2}.\end{split}

However, one cannot directly apply this stability result to (
1.8
), since
\omega_{r}
is a dipole, instead of a single vortex with a definite sign. One may be tempted to further decompose
E[\omega_{r}]
as the “self-energy” and the rest: denoting
\rho:=\omega\mathbf{1}_{Q}
, we have

E[\omega_{r}]=2\left(E[\rho]+\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)\rho(y)\log|x-\bar{y}|dxdy\right)=:2E[\rho]+2E_{\text{inter'}}\,,

where
\bar{y}=(y_{1},-y_{2})
, and the term
E_{\text{inter'}}
comes from the interaction between the 1st and 4th quadrant. By (
1.11
),
E[\rho]
achieves its maximum when
\rho
is a disk patch. Unfortunately, it does not maximize
E_{\text{inter'}}
, which is in fact unbounded from above among patches with the same center of mass and the same area. (To see this, one can stretch a disk patch horizontally while keeping its area and center. As its width being stretched to infinity,
E_{\text{inter'}}
goes to
+\infty
, whereas
E[\rho]
goes to
-\infty
.)

To overcome this difficulty, we keep the dipole energy as a single double-integral given by

E[\omega_{r}]=\frac{1}{\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)\rho(y)\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}dxdy,

and take a closer look at the kernel. At
t=0
, since our disk patch is centered at
(l_{0},h_{0})
with
h_{0}\gg 1
, we easily have

(1.12)

\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}=-\log|x-y|+\log(2h_{0})+O(h_{0}^{-1})\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}=B(p_{0},1),

In general, this estimate will not hold for future times, since
x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho(t)
may get very far. The key technical ingredient of our argument is the following pointwise estimate on the kernel (see Lemma
3.2
below), where the upper bound resembles (
1.12
). Namely, for
h_{0}\gg 1
, we have

(1.13)

\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}\leq\varphi(|x-y|)+g^{h_{0}}(2x_{2})+O(h_{0}^{-1})\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in Q,

where
\varphi(s)=-\log s
when
s\leq 3
, and
g^{h_{0}}(s)
is a concave function that is equal to
\log(2x_{2})
when
s>h_{0}
. Due to its concavity, as we integrate
g^{h_{0}}(2x_{2})
against
\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)dxdy
, using Jensen’s inequality with the fact that the vertical center of mass of
\rho
decreases in time by Lemma
1.4
, we can bound it from above by
\log(2h_{0})
.

Applying the estimates (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) to
E[\omega^{r}(0)]
and
E[\omega^{r}(t)]
respectively, and combining it with (
1.8
) and the fact that
h_{0}\gg 1
, we have

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(y)\hskip-14.22636pt\underbrace{(-\log|x-y|)}_{=\varphi(|x-y|)\text{ when }|x-y|\leq 3}\hskip-14.22636ptdxdy-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\varphi(|x-y|)dxdy\ll 1.

Since both integral contains the same kernel
\varphi(|x-y|)
(where we used the fact that
\rho_{0}
is supported in a disk with radius 1), we can apply a sharp estimate obtained recently in Yan–Yao
[
51
]
(which also holds for general densities, not just patch functions) to conclude that
\rho(\cdot,t)
stays close to a translation of
\rho_{0}^{*}
for all
t\geq 0
.

To obtain the stability statement in the case (ii), namely when
l_{0}\lesssim h_{0}
, we apply the classical desingularization results of Marchioro–Pulvirenti
[
35
,
36
]
. These results give convergence of evolution of highly concentrated vortex blobs to point vortices in the sense of measures, for a large but finite time interval. Even within a finite time window, we need to upgrade this statement to the following: approximately radial monotone vortices remains so as it moves along the point vortex orbit. (This type of strong desingularization result was already obtained in Davila–del Pino–Musso–Wei
[
18
]
but for a specific radial profile.) For this part we need to apply similar energetic considerations and aforementioned pointwise estimates again.

We prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
in Sections
2
and

, respectively. These two sections are logically independent of each other.

2.
Stability for a pair of Lamb dipoles

2.1.
Notations

We recall the notation
\mathcal{X}
:

{\mathcal{X}}:=\{\omega\in(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x||\omega(x)|\,dx<\infty\}.

For
\omega\in\mathcal{X}
, we define the stream function
\psi=\psi[\omega]
by

\psi(x):=[(-\Delta)^{-1}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega](x)\,dx=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(y)\log{|x-y|}\,dy

and the kinetic energy
E=E[\omega]
by

(2.1)

E[\omega]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi(x)\omega(x)\,dx=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(x)\omega(y)\log{|x-y|}\,dydx.

In this section, we always assume
odd symmetry for vorticities with respect to
x_{2}
-variable:

\omega(x_{1},-x_{2})=-\omega(x_{1},x_{2}).

For convenience, we define the subclasses
\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd}},\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}
of
\mathcal{X}
:

\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd}}:=\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}\,:\,\mbox{$\omega$ is odd symmetric with respect to $x_{2}$-variable}\}

and

\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}:=\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd}}\,:\,\omega\geq 0\mbox{ for }x_{2}>0\}.

For
\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd}}
, its kinetic energy is obtained by

\begin{split}E[\omega]=2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(x)G_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(x,y)\omega(y)dxdy,\end{split}

where

G_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(x,y):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\log\left(1+\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\right)>0\quad\mbox{for}\quad x\neq y.

We observe

E[\omega]\geq 0
for any
\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}
. For future use, we borrow the following estimate:

Lemma 2.1

(Proposition 2.1 of
[
1
]
)
.

For any
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\mathcal{X}_{\textup{odd}}
, we have

(2.2)

|E[\omega]-E[\tilde{\omega}]|\lesssim\|\omega-\tilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\cdot\|\omega-\tilde{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\cdot\|x_{2}(\omega+\tilde{\omega})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\cdot\|\omega+\tilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}.

2.2.
Lamb dipole (Chaplygin–Lamb dipole)

In this subsection, we consider the Lamb dipole (or Chaplygin–Lamb dipole)
\omega_{L}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}
introduced by
H. Lamb
[
31
, p231]
in 1906 and, independently, by S. A. Chaplygin in 1903
[
11
]
,
[
12
]

(also see
[
38
]
). It is simply defined by

(2.3)

\omega_{L}(x):=\begin{cases}g(r)\sin\theta,\quad&r\leq 1,\\
0,\quad&r>1,\end{cases}

in polar coordinates
x_{1}=r\cos\theta
and
x_{2}=r\sin\theta
, where
g
is defined by

g(r):=\left(\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\right)J_{1}(c_{L}r),\quad 0\leq r\leq 1.

Here,
J_{m}(r)
is the
m
-th order Bessel function of the first kind, the constant
c_{L}=3.8317\dots>0
is the first (positive) zero point of
J_{1}
, and
J_{0}(c_{L})<0
.
We note

g\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
and satisfies

g(0)=g(1)=0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad g(r)>0,\quad r\in(0,1).

The Lamb dipole
\omega_{L}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

\omega_{L}
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(b)

The support of
\omega_{L}
is the unit disk. More precisely,

\omega(x)\neq 0
if and only if
|x|<1
and
x_{2}\neq 0
.

(c)

Its
impulse
is
\mu_{L}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega_{L}(x)\,dx=\pi.

(d)

It has unit traveling speed
W_{L}=1
in the sense that
\omega_{L}(x-te_{1})
is a solution for the 2D Euler equations.
Indeed, it satisfies

(2.4)

\omega_{L}=(c_{L})^{2}f_{L}(\psi_{L}-x_{2})\quad\mbox{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x_{2}>0\},

where
f_{L}
is simply

f_{L}(s)=s^{+}=\begin{cases}s,\quad&s>0,\\
0,\quad&s\leq 0,\end{cases}

and the stream function
\psi_{L}
is defined by
\psi[\omega_{L}]
.

(e)

Let us denote
\kappa_{L}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(\omega_{L})^{2}\,dx\in(0,\infty)
.
Then
\omega_{L}
is a maximizer of the kinetic energy

E[\omega]

in the class

(2.5)

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}:\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega(x)\,dx=\pi\,\,\mbox{and}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega^{2}(x)\,dx=\kappa_{L}\right\}.

(f)

Every other maximizer of kinetic energy in
\mathcal{A}
is a
x_{1}
-translation of
\omega_{L}
.

The velocity
u_{L}
also can be written explicitly due to the relation

u_{L}=-\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{L}
(e.g. see
[
15
]
).

Remark 2.2
.

All the properties above can be easily found in literature including
[
31
,
1
]
except
(e)
. However, it is easy to deduce the property
(e)
from the result of
[
1
]
. Indeed,
[
1
, Theorem 1.5]
says, after adjusting to our notation, that
\omega_{L}
is the unique maximizer (up to
x_{1}
-translations) of the
penalized
energy

(2.6)

\hat{E}[\omega]:=E[\omega]-\frac{2}{(c_{L})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega^{2}\,dx

in the following (larger) admissible class

\hat{K}:=\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega(x)\,dx=\pi\right\}.

Since
\omega_{L}\in\mathcal{A}\subset\hat{K}
,
the property
(e)
is obtained.

By scaling, we have a two-parameter family of Lamb dipoles:

(2.7)

\omega_{L}^{a,b}(x)=b\cdot\omega_{L}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right),\quad a>0,\quad b>0.

Each dipole
\omega_{L}^{a,b}
is a maximizer of the kinetic energy in the following admissible class

\mathcal{A}_{\mu,\kappa}:=\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}:\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega(x)\,dx=\mu\mbox{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega^{2}(x)\,dx=\kappa\right\}

(e.g.
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\pi,\kappa_{L}}
) for the choice

\mu(a,b)=(a^{3}\cdot b)\pi\quad\mbox{and}\quad\kappa(a,b)=(a\cdot b)^{2}\kappa_{L},

or equivalently

(2.8)

a(\mu,\kappa)=(\mu/\pi)^{1/2}(\kappa_{L}/\kappa)^{1/4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b(\mu,\kappa)=(\pi/\mu)^{1/2}(\kappa/\kappa_{L})^{3/4}.

Then we get the traveling speed

W_{L}^{a,b}=abW_{L}=ab=(\kappa/\kappa_{L})^{1/2}
and the energy

(2.9)

E[\omega_{L}^{a,b}]=a^{4}\cdot b^{2}\cdot E[\omega_{L}]=(\mu/\pi)\cdot(\kappa/\kappa_{L})^{1/2}\cdot E[\omega_{L}].

We also note

(2.10)

\omega_{L}^{a,b}\to\omega_{L}\quad\mbox{in}\quad{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\quad\mbox{as}\quad(a,b)\to(1,1)\quad\mbox{or equivalently}\quad(\mu,\kappa)\to(\pi,\kappa_{L}).

Remark 2.3
.

The relation (
2.9
) gives the following consequence:

The dipole
\omega_{L}
is the maximizer of the kinetic energy up to
x_{1}
-translations in the admissible class

\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}:\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega(x)\,dx\leq\pi\,\,\mbox{and}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega^{2}(x)\,dx\leq\kappa_{L}\right\},

which is larger than the class
\mathcal{A}
in (
2.5
).

Remark 2.4
.

The constant
\kappa_{L}
can be explicitly obtained:

(2.11)

\kappa_{L}=\pi\cdot(c_{L})^{2}.

Indeed, when

\mu=\pi
with general
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
, which means

a=(\kappa_{L}/\kappa)^{1/4}
and

b=(\kappa/\kappa_{L})^{3/4}
, we have, by the fact that
\omega_{L}
minimizes (
2.6
),

\hat{E}[\omega_{L}^{a,b}]\leq\hat{E}[\omega_{L}]\quad\mbox{for any}\quad\kappa\in(0,\infty).

By (
2.9
), we get

\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{L}}}\cdot E[\omega_{L}]-\frac{2\kappa}{(c_{L})^{2}}\leq E[\omega_{L}]-\frac{2\kappa_{L}}{(c_{L})^{2}}\quad\mbox{for any}\quad\kappa\in(0,\infty).

It implies

E[\omega_{L}]=\frac{4\kappa_{L}}{(c_{L})^{2}}.

On the other hand, from (
2.4
),

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}E[\omega_{L}]&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\psi_{L}(x)\omega_{L}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(\psi_{L}(x)-x_{2})\omega_{L}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\omega_{L}(x)dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\omega_{L}(x)}{(c_{L})^{2}}\cdot\omega_{L}(x)dx+\pi=\frac{\kappa_{L}}{(c_{L})^{2}}+\pi,\end{split}

which gives (
2.11
).

2.3.
Stability on the Half-plane

For
\omega\in\mathcal{X}
, we use the following norm:

(2.12)

\|\omega\|_{\mathcal{X}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x_{2}||\omega(x)|\,dx+\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\omega(x)|^{2}\,dx}.

First, we borrow the following compactness statement from
[
1
]
:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\{\omega_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{A}
defined in (
2.5
) be a sequence of vorticities such that

\begin{split}\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad E[\omega_{n}]\to E[\omega_{L}]\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.\end{split}

Then
there exists a subsequence
\{\omega_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a sequence

\{\tau_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

\|\omega_{n_{k}}(\cdot+\tau_{k}e_{1})-\omega_{L}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

Here
\omega_{L}
is the Lamb dipole defined in (
2.3
). This compactness result is contained in
[
1
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5]
. We now show that such a compactness statement implies an
energy estimate
:

Proposition 2.6

(Energy estimate)
.

Let
\nu>0
be any constant satisfying
\nu\geq\|\omega_{L}\|_{L^{1}}
. Then there exists a
function
F_{\nu}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying

•

F_{\nu}
is monotonically increasing,

•

F_{\nu}(0)=0
and
F_{\nu}(s)>0
for any
s>0
,

•

Energy estimate: for all
\omega\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying
\|\omega\|_{L^{1}}\leq\nu
, we have

\begin{split}E[\omega_{L}]-E[\omega]\geq F_{\nu}(\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{L}-\omega(\cdot+\tau e_{1})\|_{\mathcal{X}}).\end{split}

Proof.

Let
\nu\geq\|\omega_{L}\|_{L^{1}}
.
First we note that for
\omega\in\mathcal{A}
,

\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{L}-\omega(\cdot+\tau e_{1})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\|\omega_{L}\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|\omega\|_{\mathcal{X}}<\infty.

For
s\geq 0
, we denote the subclass

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}^{s,\nu}=\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{A}:\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{L}-\omega(\cdot+\tau e_{1})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\geq s\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\omega\|_{L^{1}}\leq\nu\right\}.\end{split}

Then, we define

(2.13)

\begin{split}F_{\nu}(s):=\inf_{\omega\in\mathcal{S}^{s,\nu}}\left(E[\omega_{L}]-E[\omega]\right)\geq 0.\end{split}

Clearly,

F_{\nu}(s)\leq E[\omega_{L}]<\infty
,
F_{\nu}(0)=0
(due to
\omega_{L}\in\mathcal{S}^{0,\nu}
) and
F_{\nu}(\cdot_{s})
is monotonically increasing in
s\geq 0
, since
\mathcal{S}^{s,\nu}
is a smaller class of functions as
s
increases.

Now we show
F_{\nu}(s)>0
for all
s>0
. Assume that there is some
s_{1}>0

satisfying
F_{\nu}(s_{1})=0
. This means that there is a sequence
\{\omega_{n}\}
, belonging to the class
\mathcal{S}^{s_{1},\nu}\subset\mathcal{A}
, which verifies
E[\omega_{n}]\to E[\omega_{L}]
. However, thanks to the compactness (Proposition
2.5
),
the energy convergence implies that there is a subsequence which converges strongly in
\mathcal{X}
to
\omega_{L}
after proper translations. This is a contradiction to
\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{L}-\omega(\cdot+\tau e_{1})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\geq s_{1}>0
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

By scaling as in (
2.7
), we have the following energy estimate
for any
\nu\geq\|\omega_{L}\|_{L^{1}}
and
for any
\mu,\kappa\in(0,\infty)
: For any

\omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu,\kappa}
with

\|\omega\|_{L^{1}}\leq\sqrt{\mu/\pi}(\kappa/\kappa_{L})^{1/4}\nu
,
we have

(2.14)

\begin{split}E[\omega_{L}^{a,b}]-E[\omega]\geq(\mu/\pi)\cdot\sqrt{(\kappa/\kappa_{L})}\cdot F_{\nu}\left(\left(\max{\{(\mu/\pi),\sqrt{(\kappa/\kappa_{L})}\}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{L}^{a,b}-\omega(\cdot+\tau e_{1})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\right).\end{split}

where

\omega_{L}^{a,b}
is defined in
(
2.7
) with

a=a(\mu,\kappa),b=b(\mu,\kappa)
defined in (
2.8
).

2.4.
Orbital stability on the first quadrant

For the dynamics of non-negative vorticities on the first quadrant

Q
,
let us consider
nonnegative
vorticity
\rho
in
Q
:

\rho\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(Q)\quad\mbox{satisfying}\quad\int_{Q}|x|\rho(x)\,dx<\infty.

We denote
{\omega}
the odd-odd extension of
\rho
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We note that the extension
{\omega}
consists of two opposite dipoles
\omega^{{r}},\omega^{{l}}

in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the sense that

(2.15)

{\omega}=\omega^{{r}}-\omega^{{l}}\quad\mbox{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\omega^{{r}}:={\omega}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>0\}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\omega^{{l}}:=-{\omega}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}<0\}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{odd},+}.

We observe the first dipole
\omega^{{r}}
is supported in
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x_{1}>0\}
, and the second one
\omega^{{l}}
is the mirror image of the first one
\omega^{{r}}
with respect to
x_{2}
-axis:

\omega^{{l}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\omega^{{r}}(-x_{1},x_{2})\quad\mbox{for}\quad x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The conserved kinetic energy consists of two parts:

\begin{split}E[{\omega}]=E[\omega^{{r}}]+E[\omega^{{l}}]-2E_{\text{inter}}[\omega]=2(E[\omega^{{r}}]-E_{\text{inter}}[\omega]),\end{split}

where
the interaction energy is defined by

\begin{split}E_{\text{inter}}[\omega]:&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega^{{r}}(x)\big(\log{|x-y|}\big)\omega^{{l}}(y)dxdy\\
&=2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega^{{r}}(x)G_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(x,y)\omega^{{l}}(y)dxdy\\
&=2\int_{Q^{\prime}}\int_{Q}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})G_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(x,y)\rho(-y_{1},y_{2})dxdy>0\quad\mbox{when}\quad\rho\geq 0\quad\mbox{is non-trivial,}\end{split}

where
Q^{\prime}
is the second quadrant
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x_{1}<0,x_{2}>0\}
.
This
positive
quantity
E_{\text{inter}}[\omega]
is the
negative
contribution in energy of
\omega
from interaction between two dipoles
\omega^{{r}}
and
(-\omega^{{l}})
.

We easily observe that if two dipoles are far from each other, then
the interaction energy is negligible. More precisely, assuming that the support of
\rho
lies in
\{x\in Q\,:\,x_{1}\geq d\}
for
d>0
,
we have a simple estimate

(2.16)

\begin{split}E_{\text{inter}}[\omega]&\leq C\int_{Q^{\prime}}\int_{Q}\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\rho(-y_{1},y_{2})dxdy\\
&\leq\frac{C}{4d^{2}}\int_{Q^{\prime}}\int_{Q}x_{2}y_{2}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\rho(-y_{1},y_{2})dxdy=\frac{C\mu^{2}}{4d^{2}}\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\mu:=\int_{Q}x_{2}\rho(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x_{2}\omega^{{r}}(x)dx=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x_{2}\omega(x)|dx\geq 0.\end{split}

Recall that
\omega_{L}
is the (solo) Lamb dipole in (
2.3
). We call
\omega_{L,d}

a pair of two opposite Lamb dipoles, given by

\omega_{L,d}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\underbrace{\omega_{L}(x_{1}-d,x_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>0\}}}_{(\omega_{L,d})^{{r}}}-\underbrace{\omega_{L}(-x_{1}-d,x_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}<0\}}}_{(\omega_{L,d})^{{l}}}

for some
d>0
. In the theorem below, we
prove stability when
a pair of two opposite
general
dipoles is initially close to the pair
\omega_{L,d}
of two opposite

Like in Section

Due to the odd-odd symmetry of

(3.4)

K_{234}(p,p)=K_{234}(q,q).

It follows from a direct computation using the formula for
\mathcal{O}_{pv}(p)
given in (
1.3
). Alternatively,
K_{234}(p,p)
is the Hamiltonian for the point vortex motion of a single vortex in
Q
, which is conserved; see
[
52
, Section 2.3]
for details.

Viewpoint 2:
Decomposing
\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]
into “left dipole” and “right dipole” contributions.

\circ
Contribution from the dipole on the left:

For any
x,y\in Q
, one can easily check that
|x+y|\geq|x-\widetilde{y}|
, thus
K_{2}(x,y)+K_{3}(x,y)<0
. Therefore, if
\rho_{0}\geq 0
in
Q
, we have
\rho(\cdot,t)\geq 0
in
Q
, leading to

(3.5)

E_{2}[\rho(t)]+E_{3}[\rho(t)]\leq 0\quad\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

\circ
Contribution from the dipole on the right:

Next we move on to the sum
E_{1}+E_{4}
, which comes from the contribution from the right half plane, and it is a double integral with potential
K_{1}+K_{4}=\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}
. Let us state and prove the following lemma regarding a pointwise upper bound for this potential. Although elementary, it will play a central role in the proof.

Lemma 3.2

(Key Lemma)
.

Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{2}>0
.
For any
H>300
, we have

\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}\leq\varphi(|x-y|)+g^{H}(2x_{2})+\frac{300}{H},

where
\varphi\in C((0,+\infty))
is defined as

\varphi(s):=\begin{cases}-\log s&\text{ for }0<s<3,\\
-\log 3&\text{ for }s\geq 3,\end{cases}

and
g^{H}\in C((0,+\infty))
is defined as

(3.6)

g^{H}(s):=\begin{cases}\log s&\text{ for }s\geq H,\\
\log H+\frac{s-H}{H}&\text{ for }0<s<H.\end{cases}

Figure 5.

Illustrations of the functions
\varphi
and
g^{H}
in Lemma
3.2
. Note that both functions are continuous in
(0,\infty)
. In addition,
g^{H}
has its left and right derivative at
H
matching each other, and is concave in
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let us denote
d_{1}:=x_{1}-y_{1}
and
d_{2}:=x_{2}-y_{2}
. With such notations, we have

\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{d_{1}^{2}+(2x_{2}-d_{2})^{2}}{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}.

The proof is divided into the following two cases:

Case 1.

|x-y|=\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}<3
. In this case, note that
\varphi(|x-y|)=-\log|x-y|
, thus it suffices to prove that
\log|x-\bar{y}|\leq g^{H}(2x_{2})+\frac{300}{H}
.
To show this, we discuss two sub-cases depending on
x_{2}
:

•

If
x_{2}\geq\frac{H}{100}
, we rewrite
\log|x-\bar{y}|
as

\begin{split}\log|x-\bar{y}|&=\frac{1}{2}\log(d_{1}^{2}+(2x_{2}-d_{2})^{2})\\
&=\log(2x_{2}-d_{2})+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{(2x_{2}-d_{2})^{2}}\right)\\
&\leq\log(2x_{2}+3)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{3^{2}}{(2x_{2}-3)^{2}}\right)\\
&=\log(2x_{2})+\log\left(1+\frac{3}{2x_{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{3^{2}}{(2x_{2}-3)^{2}}\right).\end{split}

Since
H>300
and
x_{2}\geq\frac{H}{100}>3
, we have
2x_{2}-3\geq x_{2}
, so

\log|x-\bar{y}|\leq\log(2x_{2})+\frac{3}{2x_{2}}+\frac{9}{2x_{2}^{2}}\leq\log(2x_{2})+\frac{300}{H}.

•

If
x_{2}<\frac{H}{100}
, using that
(2x_{2}-d_{2})^{2}\leq 8x_{2}^{2}+2d_{2}^{2}
, we directly bound
\log|x-\bar{y}|
as

(3.7)

\begin{split}\log|x-\bar{y}|&\leq\frac{1}{2}\log(d_{1}^{2}+2d_{2}^{2}+8x_{2}^{2})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(18+\frac{8H^{2}}{10000}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{H^{2}}{1000}\right)\\
&<g^{H}(0)\\
&\leq g^{H}(2x_{2}).\\
\end{split}

Here the third inequality follows from the fact that
H>300
, and the last inequality follows from the fact that
g^{H}(s)
is an increasing function in
s
.

Case 2.

|x-y|=\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}\geq 3
. In this case, have

(3.8)

\begin{split}\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}&\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{d_{1}^{2}+2d_{2}^{2}+4x_{2}d_{2}+4x_{2}^{2}}{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2+\frac{4x_{2}d_{2}+4x_{2}^{2}}{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}\right).\\
\end{split}

Below we discuss two sub-cases:

•

If
\frac{x_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}}<\frac{H}{100}
, then (
3.8
) becomes

\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2+\frac{4H}{100}+\frac{4H^{2}}{10000}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{H^{2}}{1000}\right)\leq\varphi(|x-y|)+g^{H}(2x_{2}),

where the last step follows from a similar argument as the last three inequalities in (
3.7
), and we also use that
\varphi(|x-y|)\geq-\log 3
.

•

If
\frac{x_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}}\geq\frac{H}{100}
, then (
3.8
) becomes

\begin{split}\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}&\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\Big(4+\frac{500}{H}\Big)\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}\right)\\
&=\log(2x_{2})-\log(|x-y|)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{125}{H}\right)\\
&\leq\varphi(|x-y|)+g^{H}(2x_{2})+\frac{300}{H},\end{split}

where the last step follows from
\varphi(|x-y|)\geq-\log(|x-y|)
and
g^{H}(2x_{2})\geq\log(2x_{2})
.∎

3.2.
Forward-in-time orbital stability for far-away initial data

In this subsection, we prove a key result that lead to orbital stability for all positive times, under the assumptions that the initial vortex has compact support whose area is of order 1, and satisfies various inequalities of the energy functional
E_{1},\dots,E_{4}
. These assumptions look rather technical, but roughly speaking, they correspond to the setting where the initial data
\rho_{0}
is centered near a far-away point
(l_{0},h_{0})
with
l_{0}\gg h_{0}\gg 1
, and it is already very close to a translation of its radially decreasing rearrangement
\rho_{0}^{*}
. To help the readers visualize these assumptions, in Remark
3.4
afterwards, we give a concrete example of
\rho_{0}
that satisfies these assumptions.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\omega_{0}
be an odd-odd initial data to (
1.1
).
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{1000})
and
A\geq 1
, assume
\rho_{0}=\omega_{0}\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies the following four assumptions:

(3.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)dx=1,\quad 0\leq\rho_{0}\leq A\quad\text{ and }\quad|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}|\leq\pi;

(3.10)

E_{1}[\rho_{0}]-E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]\geq-\varepsilon;

(3.11)

X_{02}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)x_{2}dx>\varepsilon^{-1},\quad\text{and}\quad E_{4}[\rho_{0}]-\log(2X_{02})\geq-\varepsilon;

(3.12)

E_{2}[\rho_{0}]+E_{3}[\rho_{0}]\geq-\varepsilon.

Then for all
t\geq 0
,
\rho(\cdot,t):=\omega(\cdot,t)\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies

(3.13)

\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{0}\sqrt{A\varepsilon},

where
C_{0}
is a universal constant.
In addition, its vertical center of mass
X_{2}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)x_{2}dx
satisfies

(3.14)

\frac{X_{2}(t)}{X_{02}}\in(1-400\varepsilon,1]\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Proof.

From the conservation of kinetic energy, we have
\widetilde{E}[\rho_{0}]=\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In what follows, we shall obtain a lower bound of
\widetilde{E}[\rho_{0}]
, and an upper bound of
\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]
for
t\geq 0
.

Lower bound of energy.
To obtain a lower bound of
\widetilde{E}[\rho_{0}]
, we directly apply the assumptions (
3.10
)–(
3.12
) to obtain

(3.15)

\widetilde{E}[\rho_{0}]\geq E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]+\log(2X_{02})-3\varepsilon.

Upper bound of energy.
Next we will bound
\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]
from above for all
t\geq 0
. Using the observation (
3.5
), we have
E_{2}[\rho(t)]+E_{3}[\rho(t)]\leq 0
, therefore

\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]\leq E_{1}[\rho(t)]+E_{4}[\rho(t)]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\log\frac{|x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|}dxdy.

To control the right hand side, we apply Lemma
3.2
(with
H
replaced by
X_{02}
; recall that (
3.11
) gives
X_{02}\geq\varepsilon^{-1}>1000
) to obtain a pointwise upper bound on the integrand. This leads to

(3.16)

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]&\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\varphi(|x-y|)dxdy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)g^{X_{02}}(2x_{2})dx+\frac{300}{X_{02}}.\end{split}

We will obtain an upper bound for each of the three terms on the right hand side: For the last term, our assumption (
3.11
) directly lead to

(3.17)

\frac{300}{X_{02}}\leq 300\varepsilon.

For the second term, since
g^{X_{02}}
is concave and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)dx=1
, applying Jensen’s inequality, we have

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)g^{X_{02}}(2x_{2})dx\leq g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t)).

Finally, for the first term, let us first define
\tilde{\varphi}\in C^{1}((0,+\infty))
such that
\tilde{\varphi}(s)=\varphi(s)
for
0<s\leq 2
,
\tilde{\varphi}(s)\geq\varphi(s)
for
s\in[2,\infty)
, and
\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(s)<0
for all
s>0
. Let us also define
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{\Phi}(x):=\tilde{\varphi}(|x|)
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}\geq\varphi
and
\rho(\cdot,t)\geq 0
, clearly we have

(3.19)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\varphi(|x-y|)dxdy\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\tilde{\varphi}(|x-y|)dxdy=\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho(t)].

Such
\tilde{\Phi}
allows us to apply Theorem
3.1
to obtain

(3.20)

\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho^{*}(t)]-\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho(t)]\geq c(\tilde{\Phi},R_{*})\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-1}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho^{*}(\cdot-a,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},

where
R_{*}
is the radius of support of
\rho^{*}(\cdot,t)
.
Since
\rho(\cdot,t)
has the same distribution as
\rho_{0}
, we have
\rho^{*}(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}^{*}
for all
t
, and
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq A
(the last step is by (
3.9
)). Also by (
3.9
),
\rho_{0}^{*}
is supported in the unit disk (so
R_{*}=1
), therefore
c(\tilde{\Phi},R_{*})
is a universal constant since
\tilde{\Phi}
is fixed. Let us denote it by
c_{0}
. With these observations, we rewrite (
3.20
) as

(3.21)

\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho(t)]\leq\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho^{*}_{0}]-c_{0}A^{-1}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}.

Next we make another elementary but useful observation. Even though the interaction potential
\tilde{\Phi}(x-y)
is different from
K_{1}(x-y)
, they do agree with each other when
|x-y|\leq 2
(recall that
\tilde{\varphi}(s)=\varphi(s)=-\log s
when
0<s\leq 2
). Since
{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}^{*}\subset\overline{B(0,1)}
(which follows from the assumption
|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}|\leq\pi
in (
3.9
)), any two points
x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}^{*}
satisfy
|x-y|\leq 2
, therefore

(3.22)

E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]=\mathcal{E}_{K_{1}}[\rho_{0}^{*}]=\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\Phi}}[\rho_{0}^{*}].

Finally, we apply (
3.17
), (
3.18
), (
3.19
), (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) to (
3.16
), and arrive at

(3.23)

\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]\leq E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]-c_{0}A^{-1}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t))+300\varepsilon.

Completion of the proof
. Finally, let us put together the lower bound (
3.15
) and the upper bound (
3.23
), and use the fact that
\widetilde{E}[\rho_{0}]=\widetilde{E}[\rho(t)]
. They lead to

(3.24)

c_{0}A^{-1}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\log(2X_{02})-g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t))\leq 303\varepsilon.

By Lemma
1.4
, we have
X_{2}(t)\leq X_{02}
for all
t\geq 0
, therefore

g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t))\leq g^{X_{02}}(2X_{02})=\log(2X_{02})\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

where we used the definition of
g^{H}
in (
3.6
) with
H=X_{02}
.

Plugging this into (
3.24
) gives

c_{0}A^{-1}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\leq 303\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

leading to (
3.13
).

To show (
3.14
), note that (
3.24
) also implies

(3.25)

\log(2X_{02})-g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t))\leq 303\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

By the concavity of
g^{X_{02}}
and the facts that
g^{X_{02}}(2X_{02})=\log(2X_{02})
and
(g^{X_{02}})^{\prime}(2X_{02})=\frac{1}{2X_{02}}
, we have

g^{X_{02}}(s)\leq\log(2X_{02})-\frac{2X_{02}-s}{2X_{02}}\quad\text{ for all }s\in[0,2X_{02}].

In particular, since we have
X_{2}(t)\leq X_{02}
for all
t\geq 0
by Lemma
1.4
, we can set
s=2X_{2}(t)
in the above inequality, which becomes

g^{X_{02}}(2X_{2}(t))\leq\log(2X_{02})-\frac{X_{02}-X_{2}(t)}{X_{02}}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Plugging this into (
3.25
) yields

\frac{X_{2}(t)}{X_{02}}\geq 1-303\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

finishing the proof of (
3.14
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

To help the readers visualize the assumptions (
3.9
)–(
3.12
), we give a concrete example of
\rho_{0}
that satisfies them. Let us take any
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-p)
that satisfies (
3.9
). Since such
\rho_{0}
is a translation of
\rho_{0}^{*}
, (
3.10
) is automatically satisfied. Furthermore, assume
p=(l_{0},h_{0})
is such that
h_{0}\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}
and
l_{0}\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}h_{0}
. See Figure
6
for an illustration.

Since such
\rho_{0}
is supported inside
B(p,1)
, let us check that (
3.11
)–(
3.12
) are also satisfied: for (
3.11
), note that
X_{02}=h_{0}>\varepsilon^{-1}
, and for any
x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}
, we have
|x-\bar{y}|>2h_{0}-2
. This leads to

E_{4}[\rho_{0}]-\log(2X_{02})\geq\log(2h_{0}-4)-\log(2h_{0})\geq\log\left(1-\frac{1}{2h_{0}}\right)\geq-\varepsilon.

To check (
3.12

E_{3}[\rho_{0}]+E_{4}[\rho_{0}]\geq\inf_{x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}}\log\frac{|x-\widetilde{y}|}{|x+y|}\geq\log\frac{l_{0}-2}{l_{0}+h_{0}+2}\geq-\varepsilon,

where the last inequality follows from our assumptions
h_{0}\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1},l_{0}\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}h_{0}
.

We now derive a rescaled version of Theorem
3.3
for concentrated vortices supported in a small area. Note that the assumptions and conclusions are mostly identical to (
3.3
), except for the appearance of the scaling factor
\lambda
in (
3.26
) and (
3.28
).

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\omega_{0}
be an odd-odd initial data to (
1.1
).
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{1000}),A\geq 1
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, assume
\rho_{0}=\omega_{0}\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies the following four assumptions:

(3.26)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)dx=1,\quad 0\leq\rho_{0}\leq A\lambda^{-2}\quad\text{ and }\quad|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}|\leq\pi\lambda^{2};

(3.27)

E_{1}[\rho_{0}]-E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]\geq-\varepsilon;

(3.28)

X_{02}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)x_{2}dx>\varepsilon^{-1}\lambda,\quad\text{and}\quad E_{4}[\rho_{0}]-\log(2X_{02})\geq-\varepsilon;

(3.29)

E_{2}[\rho_{0}]+E_{3}[\rho_{0}]\geq-\varepsilon.

Then
\rho(\cdot,t):=\omega(\cdot,t)\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies

\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{0}^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{0}\sqrt{A\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

where
C_{0}
is a universal constant.
In addition, its vertical center of mass
X_{2}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)x_{2}dx
satisfies

\frac{X_{2}(t)}{X_{02}}\in(1-400\varepsilon,1]\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Proof.

We recall the following scale invariance of the 2D Euler equations: if
\omega(x,t)
is a solution of (
1.1
), then for any
\lambda>0
,

\begin{split}\widetilde{\omega}(x,t):=\lambda^{2}\omega(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)\end{split}

is again a solution.
Let us check that when
\rho_{0}=\omega_{0}\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies (
3.26
)–(
3.29
),
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}=\widetilde{\omega}_{0}\mathbf{1}_{Q}
satisfies all assumptions (
3.9
)–(
3.12
) of Theorem
3.3
:

•

One can easily check that such scaling leaves the
L^{1}
norm invariant, so

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{0}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}dx.

In addition, we have
\|\widetilde{\rho}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}=\lambda^{2}\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, and
|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\widetilde{\rho}_{0}|=\lambda^{-2}|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}|
. Hence if
\rho_{0}
satisfies (
3.26
), we have
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}
satisfies (
3.9
).

•

For the vertical center of mass, the scaling and (
3.28
) gives

\widetilde{X}_{02}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{0}(x)x_{2}dx=\lambda^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)x_{2}dx=\lambda^{-1}X_{02}>\varepsilon^{-1},

so
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}
satisfies the first assumption of (
3.11
).

•

For the energy, the scaling gives

\widetilde{E}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{0}(x)\widetilde{\rho}_{0}(y)K(x,y)dxdy=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(y)K(\lambda^{-1}x,\lambda^{-1}y)dxdy,

and likewise we can replace
K
by
K_{i}
to get the scaling for each
E_{i}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]
.
Namely, since
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
contains different signs of
\log
, we have

E_{i}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]=\begin{cases}E_{i}[\rho_{0}]+\log\lambda&\text{ for }i=1,3;\\
E_{i}[\rho_{0}]-\log\lambda&\text{ for }i=2,4.\end{cases}

Similarly, we also have
E_{1}[(\widetilde{\rho}_{0})^{*}]=E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]+\log\lambda
.

Combining these identities with the assumptions (
3.27
)–(
3.29
) yields

\begin{split}&E_{1}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]-E_{1}[(\widetilde{\rho}_{0})^{*}]=(E_{1}[\rho_{0}]+\log\lambda)-(E_{1}[\rho_{0}^{*}]+\log\lambda)\geq-\varepsilon;\\
&E_{4}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]-\log(2\widetilde{X}_{02})=(E_{4}[\rho_{0}]-\log\lambda)-\log(2\lambda^{-1}X_{02})=E_{4}[\rho_{0}]-\log(2X_{02})\geq-\varepsilon;\\[2.84544pt]
&E_{2}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]+E_{3}[\widetilde{\rho}_{0}]=(E_{2}[\rho_{0}]-\log\lambda)+(E_{3}[\rho_{0}]+\log\lambda)=E_{2}[\rho_{0}]+E_{3}[\rho_{0}]\geq-\varepsilon,\end{split}

so
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}
satisfies (
3.10
)–(
3.12
).

Now we have checked that
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}
satisfies all assumptions of Theorem
3.3
. Applying the theorem gives

\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\widetilde{\rho}(\cdot,t)-(\widetilde{\rho}_{0})^{*}(\cdot-a)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{0}\sqrt{A\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

where
C_{0}
is a universal constant, and

\frac{\widetilde{X}_{2}(t)}{\widetilde{X}_{02}}\in(1-400\varepsilon,1]\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Finally, we replace
\widetilde{\rho}(x,t)
by
\lambda^{2}\rho(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
in the above inequalities (again, recall that the
L^{1}
norm is preserved under the scaling) to get the desired estimates.
∎

Next we give a family of
\rho_{0}
that satisfies the assumptions (
3.28
)–(
3.29
). This result will be used later in the proof of our main theorem.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{1000})
, assume
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in Q
satisfies
p_{1}>8\varepsilon^{-1}p_{2}
, and
\lambda>0
is sufficiently small such that
\lambda<\frac{1}{4}p_{2}\varepsilon
. Then any
\rho_{0}\in L_{+}^{1}(Q)
with
\int_{Q}\rho_{0}dx=1
supported in
B(p,\lambda)
satisfies (
3.28
)–(
3.29
).

Proof.

First, note that since
\lambda<\frac{1}{4}p_{2}\varepsilon
and
{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}\subset B(p,\lambda)
, the vertical center of mass
X_{02}=\int\rho_{0}x_{2}dx
satisfies

X_{02}>p_{2}-\lambda>p_{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)>\frac{p_{2}}{2}>\varepsilon^{-1}\lambda,

and we also have the upper bound
X_{02}<p_{2}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)
. Also, for any
x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}\subset B(p,\lambda)
, we have
|x-\bar{y}|\geq 2p_{2}-2\lambda\geq 2p_{2}(1-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon)
, thus

\begin{split}E_{4}[\rho_{0}]&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(y)\log|x-\bar{y}|dxdy\\
&\geq\log(2p_{2})+\log\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon\right)\\
&\geq\log(2X_{02})-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon\right)\\
&\geq\log(2X_{02})-\varepsilon,\end{split}

where we used that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{1000}
in the last step. So we have verified that
\rho_{0}
satisfies (
3.28
).

To verify (
3.29
), recall that

E_{2}[\rho_{0}]+E_{3}[\rho_{0}]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)\rho(y)\log\frac{|x-\widetilde{y}|}{|x+y|}dxdy\geq\inf_{x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}}\log\frac{|x-\widetilde{y}|}{|x+y|}.

For any
x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}
, using that
\lambda<p_{2}
and
\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
, we have

\frac{|x-\widetilde{y}|}{|x+y|}\geq\frac{2(p_{1}-\lambda)}{2(\sqrt{p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}}+\lambda)}\geq\frac{p_{1}-p_{2}}{\sqrt{p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}}+p_{2}}\geq\frac{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{8}}{\sqrt{1+(\frac{\varepsilon}{8})^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon.

This leads to

\inf_{x,y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\rho_{0}}\log\frac{|x-\widetilde{y}|}{|x+y|}\geq\log\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right)\geq-\varepsilon,

finishing the proof.
∎
