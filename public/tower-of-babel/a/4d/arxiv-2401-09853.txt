Abstract
Supply chains transform raw materials into finished goods and distribute them to end consumers. The vast majority of products we use daily are supplied to us through complex global supply chains. This paper proposes a modelling methodology for dynamic competitive supply chains based on game theory and model predictive control. We model each manufacturer in the supply chain as a rational utility maximizing agent that selects their actions by finding an open-loop generalized Nash equilibrium of a multi-stage game. To react to competitors and the state of the market, every agent re-plans their actions in a receding horizon manner based on estimates of market and supplier parameters thereby creating an approximate closed-loop equilibrium policy. We demonstrate through numerical simulations that this modelling approach is computationally tractable and generates economically interpretable behaviors in a variety of settings such as demand spikes, supply shocks, and information asymmetry.

Introduction

Supply chains are complex socio-technical systems that transform raw materials into finished products and deliver these products to consumers. In modern market economies, supply chains are interconnected networks of suppliers, manufacturers, retailers, wholesalers, and consumers, each selfishly seeking to maximize their utility. Modelling and control of supply chains is crucial for tackling a variety of real-world challenges such as disruptions to product availability caused by supply shocks and labour shortages (e.g., due to the pandemic or geopolitical conflicts).
Supply chains are challenging to model since they are highly dynamic, subject to a variety of constraints, and comprised of multiple interlinked competitive actors.
As a result, supply chains present a unique and challenging setting for control in socio-technical systems.

Control engineering principles have been successfully applied to modelling and control of supply chains in previous works. Typical examples include inventory control, modelling industrial dynamics, and policy design for avoiding the
Bullwhip Effect

(
Vassian 1955
;
Towill 1982
;
Towill 1996
)
, mainly using frequency domain tools. Recent literature capitalizes on advances in optimal control, predominantly model predictive control (MPC), to tackle problems in real-time inventory management and supply chain optimization
(
Braun et al. 2003
;
Perea-Lopez et al. 2003
;
Subramanian et al. 2014
)
, remanufacturing

(
Li et al. 2021
)
, and production and marketing decisions
(
Pekelman 1974
;
Levin et al. 2008
)
.
A thorough survey can be found in
Ivanov et al. 2018
;
Sarimveis et al. 2008
.

The control-oriented works primarily focus on vertically integrated supply chains, where all agents are working toward a common goal, potentially in a decentralized way. However, in reality, there is competition at every stage of the chain starting from resource extraction, through production, wholesaling, distribution, and retail. Game theory is a powerful framework for modelling and analyzing supply chains with multiple agents that have conflicting interests
(
Cachon and Netessine 2006
)
. Network equilibrium models conceptualize supply chains as a network of interconnected producers, suppliers, and consumers
(
Nagurney et al. 2002
;
Nagurney 2006
;
Anderson and Bao 2010
)
and solve for (typically Nash) equilibria of network games by solving variational inequalities. These equilibria can computed for large networks using distributed algorithms
(
Belgioioso et al. 2022
)
and are flexible enough to model a wide variety of scenarios, e.g., they have been used to study the impact of waste from electric and electronic equipment disposal regulations
(
Hammond and Beullens 2007
)
and the consequences of labour constraints caused by the Covid-19 pandemic
(
Nagurney 2021
)
.

Dynamic game-theory
(
Başar and Olsder 1998
)
has been used to study a variety of scenarios involving dynamic multi-agent competition including optimal pricing and inventory replenishment policies
(
Netessine et al. 2006
;
Kirman and Sobel 1974
;
Bernstein and Federgruen 2004
)
, the impacts of technology acquisition
(
Gaimon 1989
)
, and capacity investment decisions based on reputational effects
(
Taylor and Plambeck 2007
)
. Dynamic games involve multi-period decision-making and typically require solving for feedback Nash equilibria (Nash equilibria over policies) which are very challenging to compute, limiting these methods to simple settings to remain analytically tractable. Instead, receding horizon games compute open-loop equilibria and introduce feedback by re-planning at every time step. Thus, harnessing the benefits of a feedback policy while remaining computationally tractable even for complex models with many agents
(
Hall et al. 2022
)
.

In this paper, we propose a method for modelling dynamic supply chains using receding horizon games. Specifically, we combine multi-period network equilibrium models with model predictive control to numerically construct approximate feedback-equilibrium policies. These policies then serve to form a closed-loop model of the supply chain. This approach is flexible and allows us to consider complex scenarios involving dynamics and delays, constraints, information asymmetry, external forcing, and forecasts that cannot be captured by either network equilibrium or traditional dynamic game theory approaches. We illustrate the efficacy of our method by studying demand and supply shocks, and the impact of information asymmetry. With out methodology we are able to replicate a variety of economically relevant behaviours: (i) we show that dynamic pricing can be used to manage demand and inventory, (ii) we show how dynamic effects propagate through the supply chain, and lastly (iii) that information offers a competitive advantage.

3
Game-theoretic MPC-based modelling

We want to model competitive dynamic supply chains. Therefore, we model each manufacturer as a rational agent seeking to minimize the following stage cost function:

\ell^{v}(x^{v},u^{v},u^{-v},w^{v})=\underbrace{\gamma^{v}\cdot(\xi^{v}-\bar{\xi}^{v})^{2}}_{\text{security stock}}\\
-\underbrace{\left(p^{v}\cdot d^{v}(p^{v},p^{-v},w^{v})-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\rho^{s}(o^{vs},o^{-vs})\cdot o^{vs}\right)}_{\text{net cash flow}}.

(9)

The first term with penalizes deviations from the safety inventory
\bar{\xi}^{v}
, which is a reference term used in inventory control laws (e.g.,
(
Spiegler et al. 2012
)
) to ensure that it is always possible to fulfill demand where
\gamma^{v}>0
, and the second term is net cash flow of manufacturer
v
.

The manufacturers incorporate the dynamics of the supply chain into their decision-making process by minimizing their incremental cost function over a planning horizon
N>0
. This yields the set of coupled optimal control problems (OCP)
\mathcal{P}_{N}(\theta,\mathbf{x},w):
for all
v\in\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\min_{x^{v}_{k},u^{v}_{k}}~

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\ell^{v}(x_{k}^{v},u_{k}^{v},u_{k}^{-v},w_{k}^{v})

(10a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle x^{v}_{k+1}=A^{v}x^{v}_{k}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}B^{vj}u_{k}^{j}+D^{v}w^{v}_{k},

(10b)

\displaystyle x_{0}^{v}=\mathbf{x}^{v},

(10c)

\displaystyle x_{k}^{v}\in\mathcal{X}^{v},~~u_{k}^{v}\in\mathcal{U}^{v},

(10d)

\displaystyle(u^{v}_{k},u^{-v}_{k})\in\mathcal{C}

(10e)

where
\mathbf{x}^{v}
is the initial condition,
\mathcal{U}^{v}=\{(p^{v},o^{vs})~|~0\leq p^{v},~0\leq o^{vs}~~\forall s\in\mathcal{S}\}
,
\mathcal{X}^{v}=\{(\xi,O,d)~|~0\leq\xi\leq\Xi^{v}\}

are the local constraints and

\mathcal{C}=\left\{o^{vs}~\bigg|~\sum_{v\in\mathcal{M}}o^{vs}\leq\bar{O}^{s}~~\forall s\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

(10f)

encodes the coupling constraint (
5
). The parameters of the market, suppliers, and manufacturers are collected in
\theta=(\rho^{s},\bar{O}^{s},\beta^{v},\alpha^{v},\gamma^{v},\Xi^{v})_{v\in\mathcal{M},s\in\mathcal{S}}
and the demand forecasts of each agent over the prediction horizon is
w^{v}=(w^{v}_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
. Together, the
n_{p}
coupled OCPs
\mathcal{P}_{N}
constitute a game.

In reality, there is information asymmetry (i.e., not all manufacturers have accurate information on the local parameters of others). To model these effects, we assume that each manufacturer is strategic, in the sense that they maintain models of their competitors. Specifically:

•

The form of the model is fixed and known and all agents plan over an
N
step horizon;

•

Each manufacturer maintains estimates of the the parameters of the game
\hat{\theta}^{v}
and demand forecasts for all agents
\hat{w}^{v}=(\hat{w}^{vj})_{j\in\mathcal{M}}
where
\hat{w}^{vj}=(\hat{w}^{vj}_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
denotes the forecast of agent
v
for agent
j
over the prediction horizon
N
;

•

The state of each agent
x^{v}_{t}
is measurable (or can be estimated).

To compensate for the finite planning horizon and inaccurate information about other, agents
re-plans
their actions each day. Specifically, they find a Nash equilibrium
(\bar{u}^{v}_{k},\bar{u}^{-v}_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
of
\mathcal{P}_{N}(\hat{\theta}^{v},x_{t},\hat{w}^{v}_{t})
based on the measured state
x_{t}
, their parameter estimates
\hat{\theta}^{v}
and their forecast
\hat{w}^{v}_{t}
of the base demand over the interval
[t~~t+N]
, and they apply the first element of their planned control sequence
u^{v}_{t}=\bar{u}^{v}_{0}(\hat{\theta}^{v},x_{t},\hat{w}^{v}_{t})
. For each agent, this re-planning procedure constitutes a model predictive controller where they use game-theory to model how their competitors will react to their own actions (e.g., if I play
u^{v}_{0}
it would be rational for my competitors to play
u^{-v}_{0}
). Similar ideas are used in multi-robot systems e.g., drone racing and autonomous driving
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Spica et al. 2020
;
Fridovich-Keil et al. 2020
)
and energy management applications
(
Hall et al. 2022
)
.

10

10a

10e

\forall v\in\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\min_{u^{v}}~~

\displaystyle J^{v}(u^{v},u^{-v},\theta,\mathbf{x},w^{v})

(11a)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle G(\theta)u\leq g(\theta,\mathbf{x},w)

(11b)

where
u^{v}=(u^{v}_{k})_{k=0}^{N-1}
,
u=(u^{v})_{v\in\mathcal{M}}
,
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{v})_{v\in\mathcal{M}}
, and
w=(w^{v})_{k\in\mathcal{M}}
. The feedback law
\kappa^{v}
is given by

u^{v}_{t}=\kappa^{v}(x_{t},\hat{\theta}^{v},\hat{w}^{v}_{t})=\Psi^{v}S(x_{t},\hat{\theta}^{v},\hat{w}^{v}_{t})

(12)

where

is the solution map of (

Our global supply chain model is given by the dynamics of the supply chain (
8
) in closed-loop with the feedback policies (

x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+B\kappa(x_{t},\hat{\theta},\hat{w}_{t})+Dw_{t}

(13)

where
\kappa(x_{t},\hat{\theta},\hat{w}_{t})=(\kappa^{v}(x_{t},\hat{\theta}^{v},\hat{w}_{t}^{v}))_{v\in\mathcal{M}}
, and the collected parameter estimate and demand forecasts are denoted by
\hat{w}_{t}=(\hat{w}_{t}^{v})_{v\in\mathcal{M}}
and

Our approach is algorithmic in the sense that we evaluate
\kappa^{v}
by solving for v-GNEs of (
11

is quadratic and convex in

for each agent if

, v-GNEs of the condensed game (

(

(
Liao-McPherson et al. 2018
)
to solve for the

of (

is that they “fairly” distribute the cost of enforcing the coupling constraints (

)
(
Belgioioso et al. 2022
)
.

4.2
Supply shock

As a second case study, we investigate the global supply chain behaviour in the presence of sudden shortages in supply of input materials. This may occur if e.g., supply routes are disrupted by geopolitical conflicts, labour shortages, or natural disasters such as droughts or flooding.

As in the previous case study, the manufacturers are given no warning of the sudden supply shock. The coupling constraint limit for supplier one
\bar{O}^{1}
is dropped on day 10 by 70%, as displayed in Figure
4

•

As manufacturers hit the supply limit
\bar{O}^{1}
for the cheaper supplier 1, they instantly order more from the other available supplier.

•

To compensate for the increase in raw material cost, they increase their prices, experiencing a drop in demand as the number of marginal consumers decreases.

•

Manufacturers also use prices to control demand (lower it) such that they do not run out of inventory as they can not order enough raw materials to keep up with demand.

Summarizing the three case studies we observe the following economically relevant behaviours in our dynamic supply chain model: (i) Agents use prices to control demand and to manage their inventory; (ii) increased demand and profits are passed throughout the supply chain; (iii) information is a major competitive advantage; (iv) agents without information suffer from information asymmetry.

5
Conclusion

We present a dynamic supply chain model to study the competition between manufacturers that share raw material suppliers and a common consumer base. The manufacturers compete to maximize their profit by setting prices and managing their inventory levels. We formalized a dynamic supply chain model as a game-theoretic MPC where the approximate equilibrium policies serve as a closed-loop model for the supply chain. We study the behaviour of the supply chain in a variety of scenarios such as demand spikes and supply shocks and demonstrate that economically intuitive behaviours emerge from our model of dynamic supply chain competition. Future work includes: game-theoretic inference for validation and parameter estimation, studying recursive feasibility under information asymmetry, understanding the impact of varying prediction horizons, as well as studying a variety of supply chain scenarios such as the design of market regulations and carbon reduction incentives.
