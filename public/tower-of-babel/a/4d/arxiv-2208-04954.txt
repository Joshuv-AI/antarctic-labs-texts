Explicit expressions for stationary states of the Lindblad equation for a finite state space
Bernd Fernengel, Barbara Drossel

Abstract
The Lindblad equation describes the time evolution of a density matrix of a quantum mechanical system. Stationary solutions are obtained by time-averaging the solution, which will in general depend on the initial state. We provide an analytical expression for the steady states of the Lindblad equation using the quantum jump unraveling, a version of an ergodic theorem, and the stationary probabilities of the corresponding discret-time Markov chains. Our result is valid when the number of states appearing the in quantum trajectory is finite. The classical case of a Markov jump-process is recovered as a special case, and differences between the two are discussed.

Introduction

The Lindblad equation is a quantum master equation describing the time evolution of a density matrix of an open quantum system. It is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\underbrace{-i[H,\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)]}_{\text{von Neumann term}}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{k\in I}\gamma_{k}\left(V_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\,V_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{V_{k}^{\dagger}V_{k},\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\right\}\right)}_{\text{dissipator term}}

(1)

\displaystyle=-i\left(H_{c}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)-\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\,H_{c}\right)+\sum\limits_{k\in I}\gamma_{k}\left(V_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\,V_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denote the commutator and anticommutator, respectively, and the so-called
conditional Hamiltonian

H_{c}
is defined as

\displaystyle H_{c}:=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum\limits_{k\in I}\gamma_{k}\left(V_{k}^{\dagger}\,V_{k}\right)=:H-\frac{i}{2}\,\Lambda.

(2)

We have set
\hbar=1
. An open quantum system is a quantum mechanical system in contact with an external environment.
The Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
of equation (
1
) is the most general form of an (infinitesimal) generator that ensures that the solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
remains a density matrix, in particular that is positive semi-definite (with respect to the complex numbers) and has trace one. This theorem has first been proven by Gorini, Kossakowski, and Sudarshan for the finite dimensional case, with Lindblad generalizing the statement to bounded generators
[
BP02
]
. At that time, generators of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
have already appeared multiple times in the physical literature
[
BP02
]
.
In addition to these previous theorems, which are based on quantum Markov semi-groups, there are two types of microscopic derivations of the Lindblad equation, namely the weak-coupling limit and the low-density limit. The basis for both derivations is the ad-hoc assumption that the combined system of system and bath can be written initially as a product state. From this initial state, one computes the dynamics and traces over the environmental degrees of freedom to arrive at the reduced density matrix of the system.

When conducting a numerical simulation of a specific model, one often relies on
unravellings
of the Lindblad equation, which are ensembles of stochastic trajectories whose average yields the solution of the Lindblad equation
[
BP02
]
. While different unravellings may yield qualitative different types of dynamics, all measurable quantities depend only on the solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
, while additional properties depending on
either a certain type of unravelling or a specific quantum trajectory
are not accessible to experimental observation.

The Lindblad equation contains the von Neumann equation, describing a closed quantum mechanical system, as a special case. The solution von the von Neumann equation is given by
\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H\,t}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,H\,t}
, with the self-adjoint Hamiltonian
H^{\dagger}=H
. Since
H_{c}
is not self-adjoint, the (conditional) time evolution operator
\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}
is not unitary.

The additional terms can be interpreted as modeling the influence of the environment, in particular by inducing transitions between states of the system. The so-called
Lindblad operators

\{V_{k}\,|\,k\in I\}
can be interpreted as causing transitions
\psi\to\frac{V_{k}\,\psi}{\|V_{k}\,\psi\|}
, where
\emptyset\neq I\subset\mathbb{N}
is a finite index set and
\gamma_{k}>\,0
are positive transition rates. We will restrict ourselves to the finite-dimensional case, that is
\boldsymbol{\rho},V_{k},H\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, with
N\in\mathbb{N},N\geq\,2
.

One important aspect in the study of quantum master equation is its long-term behaviour
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
and its steady states
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
( with
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}
defined by
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty})=0
), as this determines the state where a quantum mechanical system will eventually be. When certain algebraic conditions on the Lindblad operators are satisfied, the steady state is unique and asymptotically stable. The most prominent theorem about such conditions was given by Spohn
[
Spo77
]
. It requires that the set of Lindblad operators form a basis, i.e. that the environment couples to all degrees of freedom, which applies only to a limited set of physical systems and cannot easily be generalized.

Most papers studying the stationary states of the Lindblad equation require additional properties of the Lindblad operators. Bua and Prosen propose a method which assumes that the underlying symmetry of the system is already known
[
BP12
]
. While being numerically cheap, finding all the symmetries of an open system can be highly non-trivial, especially when both "strong" and "weak" symmetry is involved (see
[
TM21
]
for a summary).
Another approach was made by Trushechkina, where special kinds of Lindblad operators were considered, which allow a "backwards" transition for every "forward" transition (see
[
Tru18
]
. For the dimension
N=2
a full discussion can be found in
[
AIIN22
]
.

The general nature of the Lindbladian in equation (
1
) makes it difficult to compute
analytical expressions for all possible steady states. In this paper, we aim at
statements about the stationary states, without making additional assumptions on the Lindblad operators.
We will address the nature and number of these stationary states, how they can be determined, and how they are related to the long-term behaviour that results from a given initial state.

The standard way to compute the stationary state from a given initial state
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
would be to determine the solution
t\mapsto\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
of the initial value problem of equation (
1
) for all positive times
t\geq 0
and to evaluate the time average
\langle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t)\rangle_{t\geq 0}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t)\,\text{ d}\,t
.
We propose a different method: We express the density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
in terms of the average of an ensemble of stochastic quantum trajectories. Then we make use of the fact that the limit of the time average of a single quantum trajectory exists and that it may be interchanged with the ensemble limit
[
KM04
]
. We will show that both the time average and the ensemble average of a quantum trajectory can be explicitly evaluated with the theory of discrete-time Markov chains when certain conditions (in particular a finite state space) are satisfied.

2
The concept of an unravelling of the Lindblad equation

Unravellings are ensembles of stochastic quantum trajectories that are equivalent to the Lindblad equation in the sense that the state of the system
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
at a time
t\geq 0
is given by the average over all possible trajectories,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Theta(t,\omega)\text{ d}\,\mathcal{P}(\omega).

(3)

The parameter
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
labels the quantum trajectories
\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}\ni\,t\mapsto\Theta(t,\omega)\in\{\text{density matrices}\}
(see figure (
1
) for all illustration).

In particular, the expectation values of any quantum mechanical observable
A
, given the system is in the state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, can be computed from these unravelings, according to

\displaystyle E_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[A]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\boldsymbol{\rho}\,A]=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\text{Tr}[\Theta(\omega)\,A]\text{ d}\,\mathcal{P}(\omega).

(4)

Unravellings provide different perspectives on the subject of time evolution of open quantum systems and can yield new insights since they are tackled by a different set of mathematical tools: Instead of linear differential equations one has the theory of Markov chains, piecewise deterministic processes, and stochastic differential equations at hand
[
BP02
]
.

Moreover, they are often superior when it comes to numerical simulations of specific models as a Ket-state unravelling (see section
2.3
) requires less memory storage (linear with the dimension of the system, instead of quadratic
[
Lid19
]
).

While unravellings are usually done in terms of ket states, we want to focus on the so called
mixed-state unravellings
, which assume values in the set of density matrices
[
BP02
]
.

Among the different possible types of unravellings, we will use the
quantum-jump unravelling
. For a given Hamiltonian
H
and a given set of Lindblad operators
\{V_{k}\,:\,k\in I\}
, the quantum jump unravelling is a piecewise deterministic process (see
[
BP02
,
Lid19
]
) where the continuous, deterministic time evolution is interrupted by discontinuous quantum jumps at times
t_{n}
, where a Lindblad operator
V_{\pi_{n}}
is applied.

So a quantum trajectory is completely determined by the sequence of times
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
(which record when a quantum jump takes place) and the sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in
of indices of Lindblad operators (which records which operator has been applied at time
t_{n}
).
We define
\mathcal{U}
to be the set of all possible quantum trajectories,
\tau_{n}:=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
to be the
length of the time interval
between two jumps and
\Theta_{n}:=\Theta(t_{n})
the state of the unravelling at time
t_{n}
.

Note that we use with
\Theta(t,\omega)
a different symbol for a state in the quantum trajectory
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
, to distinguish it from the solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
of the Lindblad equation (
1
) at time
t_{n}
, which is the average over all quantum trajectories,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)=\langle\Theta(\omega,t)\rangle_{\omega\in\mathcal{U}}
.

2.1
Definitions and preparatory considerations

2.1.1
(Pseudo-) Time-evolution operators and quantum jump operators

To avoid unnecessarily complicated notation, we denote by
U_{t}
the (non-unitary) time evolution according to the conditional Hamiltonian
H_{c}
, and by

the quantum jump operator, irrespective of the nature of the quantum state, be it a density matrix or a ket state. Their action on these two types of quantum states is thus given by

\displaystyle U_{t},J_{k}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}

\displaystyle U_{t}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}\,\Theta\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,H_{c}^{\dagger}\,t}

\displaystyle J_{k}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=V_{k}\,\Theta\,V_{k}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle U_{t},J_{k}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}

(5)

\displaystyle U_{t}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}\,\psi

\displaystyle J_{k}(\psi)

\displaystyle=V_{k}\,\psi

During the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1})
between two quantum jumps there is a deterministic time evolution
\Theta(t)=\frac{U_{t-t_{n}}\,(\Theta_{n})}{\text{Tr}[\dots]}
, and directly after a quantum jump induced by the Lindblad operator
V_{k}
the state of the quantum trajectory is given by
\Theta(t_{n})=\frac{J_{k}(\Theta)}{\text{Tr}[\dots]}

\Bigl(
we write
\frac{A}{\text{Tr}[\dots]}
instead of
\frac{A}{\text{Tr}[A]}\Bigr)
.

2.1.2
The distribution of the waiting time

Given that the quantum trajectory is in state
\Theta
, the waiting time

for the next jump is distributed according to

\displaystyle f(t\,|\,\Theta)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{d}{dt}\text{Tr}\left[\underbrace{U_{t}(\Theta)}_{\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}\,\Theta\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,H_{c}^{\dagger}\,t}}\right]=-\text{Tr}\left[\underbrace{(-i\,H_{c})}_{-i\,H-\frac{1}{2}\,\Lambda}\,U_{t}(\Theta)+U_{t}(\Theta)\,\underbrace{(i\,H_{c}^{\dagger})}_{i\,H-\frac{1}{2}\,\Lambda}\right]=

(6)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{i\,\text{Tr}\left[H\,U_{t}(\Theta)\right]-i\,\text{Tr}\left[U_{t}(\Theta)\,H\right]}_{0}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\Lambda\,U_{t}(\Theta)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[U_{t}(\Theta)\,\Lambda\right]}_{\text{Tr}\left[\Lambda\,U_{t}(\Theta)\right]}=

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\Lambda\,U_{t}(\Theta)]{\mathrel{\to}^{\Lambda=\sum\limits_{k\in I}\gamma_{k}\,V_{k}^{\dagger}\,V_{k}}}=\sum\limits_{k\in I}\gamma_{k}\,\text{Tr}\left[\underbrace{V_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}\,\Theta\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,H_{c}^{\dagger}\,t}\,V_{k}^{\dagger}}_{J_{k}\circ U_{t}(\Theta)}\right]=

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in I}\underbrace{\gamma_{k}\,\text{Tr}\left[J_{k}\circ U_{t}(\Theta)\right]}_{f^{(k)}(t\,|\,\Theta)}=:\sum\limits_{k\in I}f^{(k)}(t\,|\,\Theta)

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(t\,|\,\Theta):=\gamma_{k}\,J_{k}\circ U_{t}(\Theta)=\gamma_{k}\,V_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,H_{c}\,t}\,\Theta\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,H_{c}^{\dagger}\,t}\,V_{k}^{\dagger}\;\;\text{ (see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{breuer2002theory}{}{}]})}.

(7)

Note that

need not be a probability distribution since

2.1.3
The time-weighted average state

For a finite waiting time
\tau<\infty
we define the time-weighted average state between two jumps as

\displaystyle I_{\Theta}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\tau}\,\frac{U_{t}(\Theta)}{\text{Tr}[U_{t}(\Theta)]}\,d\,t=\tau\cdot\langle\Theta(t)\rangle_{t\in[0,\tau]},

(8)

which is the waiting time
\tau
times the average state between two quantum jumps.

3
Computing the stationary solution of the Lindblad equation based on quantum-jump unravelling

The stationary solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
for an initial state
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
is obtained by taking the long-time average of expression (
3
)
and interchanging the time average with the ensemble average,

\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})=\int\limits_{\mathcal{U}}\,\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)\,\text{ d}\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega),

(15)

where the time average for a single trajectory is given by

\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)=\langle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\rangle_{t\geq 0}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\,\text{ d}\,t.

(16)

We therefore need to calculate the time averages of quantum trajectories and their probabilites.
Instead of computing the probability density function
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
separately, we will divide the set of all possible unravellings into a finite partition
\mathcal{U}=\dot{\bigcup\limits}_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathcal{U}(B_{i})
of
n\in\mathbb{N}
disjoint measurable sets, such that
\mathcal{U}(B_{i})
contains all quantum trajectories with the same long-term behaviour

\Theta_{B_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\;\,\int\limits_{\mathcal{U}(B_{i})}\underbrace{\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)}_{\Theta_{B_{i}}}\,\text{ d}\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}\Bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\,\bigl|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\Bigr)\,\Theta_{B_{i}}.

(17)

Calculating the different building blocks of this expression is the goal of the next subsections.

An explicit expression for the time average
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\cdot)
of a single quantum trajectory will be obtained in section
3.1
. To find the different possible long-time behaviors of quantum trajectories, we view an unravelling as a discrete-time Markov chain in section
3.2
, where the next state
\Theta_{n+1}
of the discrete quantum trajectory depends only on the previous state
\Theta_{n}
.
In section
3.3
we explicitly construct the set of possible states in the Markov chain
and the matrix of transition probabilities, and we illustrate this procedure by three examples in section
3.4
.
This gives us both the stationary states of the Markov chain and the probabilities
for the different long-term behaviours.

In section
3.5
, we will evaluate the time averaged states between jumps.
By combining all these results, we can obtain in section
3.6
the final expression for the stationary solutions of the Lindblad equation (
1
).

3.1
Time average of a single quantum trajectory

The time average of a single quantum trajectory is given by

\displaystyle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\,\text{ d}\,t=:\langle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\rangle_{t\geq 0}.

(18)

according to the theorem quoted in appendix

In order to evaluate this expression further, we denote by

\displaystyle J(T)

\displaystyle:=\left|\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,t_{n}\leq T\}\right|\text{ the number of jumps before the time $T>0$ , and and by }

(19)

\displaystyle J_{s}(T)

\displaystyle:=\left|\{n\in\{1,\dots,J(T)\}\,:\,\Theta_{n}=\Theta_{s}\}\right|\text{ the number of times the state $\Theta_{s}$ }

\displaystyle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrel{\to}^{\text{theorem }\eqref{ChangingLimits_TimeAndEnsembleAverage}}}\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\;\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\,d\,t=

(20)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\,\frac{U_{t-t_{n}}(\,\Theta_{n}\,)}{\text{Tr}\left[\dots\right]}\,d\,t+\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})=

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}\,\frac{U_{t}(\,\Theta_{n}\,)}{\text{Tr}\left[\dots\right]}\,d\,t}_{I_{\Theta_{n}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)}+\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})}{T}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{n}}(\tau_{n})\right)}_{\text{'jump part'}}+\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{T}\,I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})\right),}_{\text{'remainder part'}}

where the time-weighted average
I_{\Theta}(\tau)
was defined in equation (
8
).

The number of quantum jumps can be finite or infinite, so in the following we have to distinguish these two cases.
As we will see, the ’jump part’ vanishes in the case of finitely many jumps and the time average will be determined by the ’remainder part’, and vice versa when the number of jumps is infinite.

3.1.1
The number of quantum jumps is finite

When the number of quantum jumps is finite, we can set
J(T){\mathrel{\to}^{T\text{ large enough}}}J_{\text{Max}}=:J_{M}
. So the discrete quantum trajectory will stop at the state
\Theta_{\text{trap}}:=\Theta_{J_{M}}
.
As mentioned before, this can only happen if
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}}f(t\,|\,\Theta_{\text{trap}})\,\text{ d}t<1
.

We claim that the time average of this particular quantum trajectory equals
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{T}\,\int_{0}^{T}U_{t}(\Theta_{\text{trap}})\,\text{ d}t
.

Proof.

a)

The ’jump part’:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}\underbrace{I_{\Theta_{n}}(\tau_{n})}_{\leq\,\tau_{n}\,\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}]}\,\|I_{\Theta_{n}}(t)\|}\right\|\leq\,\max\limits_{n\in\{0,\dots,J_{M}-1\}}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}]}\|I_{\Theta_{n}}(t)\|\,\frac{t_{J_{M}}}{T}\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}0.

(21)

b)

The ’remainder part’:

\displaystyle\frac{I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})}{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{\Theta_{J_{M}}}(T-t_{J_{M}})}{T-t_{J_{M}}}\,\overbrace{\left(\frac{T-t_{J_{M}}}{T}\right)}^{\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}1}\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}I_{\Theta_{\text{trap}}}(T)=

(22)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{T}\,\int_{0}^{T}\frac{U_{t}(\Theta_{\text{trap}})}{Tr[]}\,\text{ d}t=\langle\Theta_{\text{trap}}(t)\rangle_{t\geq\,t_{J(T)}}.

∎

3.1.2
The number of quantum jumps is infinite

When the number of quantum jumps is infinite, the discrete quantum trajectory does not stop.
We show that the ’remainder part’ vanishes and derive an expression for the ’jump part’.

a)

The ’remainder part’:

The ’remainder’ term
\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})}{T}
tends to zero when
T
approaches infinity,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})}{T}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{I_{\Theta_{J(T)}}(T-t_{J(T)})}{T-t_{J(T)}}\,\left(\frac{T-t_{J(T)}}{T}\right)\right\|\,\leq

(23)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}]}\left\|\frac{U_{t}(\,\Theta_{n}\,)}{\text{Tr}\left[\dots\right]}\right\|\;\,\left(1-\frac{t_{J(T)}}{T}\right)\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}0,

where we used the fact that
\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{t_{J(T)}}{T}
= 1 (see appendix
A.7
).

b)

The ’jump part’:

For a fixed quantum trajectory
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
that does not stop we denote by
\alpha_{s}(k)\in\mathbb{N}
the position in that quantum trajectory where the corresponding state
\Theta_{s}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
appears for the
k
-th time, so

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min\limits_{M\in\mathbb{N}}\Bigl\{\,|\{\Theta_{n}\,:\,n\leq M,\Theta_{n}=\Theta_{s}\}|=k\,\Bigr\}.

(24)

Note that we define
\alpha_{s}(k)
only for those states that actually appear in
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
.

In particular, we have
\Theta_{\alpha_{s}(k)}=\Theta_{s}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and the waiting time
\tau_{\alpha_{s}}(k)
has the probability density function
f(\cdot\,|\,\Theta_{\alpha_{s}(k)})=f(\cdot\,|\,\Theta_{s})
(see equation (
6
) for a definition).

The sums of the form
\frac{1}{J(T)}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}x_{n}
, with
x_{n}\in\{\tau_{n},I_{\Theta_{n}}(\tau_{n})\}
in the ’jump part’ of equation (
20
) can be rearranged to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J(T)}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}x_{n}=\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}\,\frac{1}{J_{s}(T)}\,\sum\limits_{k=1}^{J_{s}(T)}x_{\alpha_{s}(k)}.

(25)

For all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
, both the waiting times
\{\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\,:\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
and the time-weighted average states

\{I_{\Theta_{s}}\left(\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\right)\,:\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}

are independent and identically distributed, and by the law of large numbers we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{K\to\infty}\overline{\tau_{s}},

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\,I_{\Theta_{s}}\left(\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\right)

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{K\to\infty}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}I_{\Theta_{s}}(\tau)\,f(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s})\,d\,\tau=:\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})},

\displaystyle\text{ pointwise, almost surely. }

The term
\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}
can be interpreted as the fraction of jumps in the quantum trajectory whose outcome yields the state
\Theta_{s}
.

For long times, the trajectory will eventually be in a minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
, which is a subset of states that is both
absorbing
(which means that there are no transitions out of this set) and
minimal
in the sense of set inclusions (when
B
is a minimal absorbing set and
A\subseteq B
is absorbing, we have
A=B
), (see section
3.2
and appendix
A.3
. Therefore, the fraction
\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}
of jumps landing in state
\Theta_{s}
converges to a stationary value
q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)
associated with this minimal absorbing set,

\displaystyle\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J(T)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J(T)-1}\delta_{\Theta_{n},\Theta_{s}}\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B),

(27)

with
q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)
being the
relative frequency
with which the state
\Theta_{s}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
appears in the discrete quantum trajectory
(\Theta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Since the numbers
\left(q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
are non-negative and add up to one, we can interpret
q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)
as the probability of state
\Theta_{s}
, provided the discrete quantum trajectory is eventually in the minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
.

This idea will be picked up in section
3.2
.

If the probability for a state
\Theta_{s}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
in a quantum trajectory is strictly positive, then it must appear infinitely often in the corresponding trajectory:

q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}>0\Longrightarrow\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,J_{s}(T)=\infty
.

Now we can compute the ’jump part’

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{n}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{J(T)}\,\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{n}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)}{\frac{1}{J(T)}\,\sum\limits_{n=0}^{J(T)-1}\tau_{n}+\left(\frac{T-t_{J(T)}}{J(T)}\right)}=

(28)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}\,\frac{1}{J_{s}(T)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J_{s}(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{\alpha_{s}(k)}}\left(\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\right)}{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}\,\frac{1}{J_{s}(T)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J_{s}(T)-1}\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}+\left(\frac{T-t_{J(T)}}{J(T)}\right)}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrel{\to}^{|\mathcal{S}|<\infty}}\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\overbrace{\left(\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}\right)}^{q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)}\,\overbrace{\left(\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{J_{s}(T)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J_{s}(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{\alpha_{s}(k)}}\left(\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\right)\right)}^{\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}}}{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\underbrace{\left(\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{J_{s}(T)}{J(T)}\right)}_{q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)}\,\underbrace{\left(\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{J_{s}(T)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J_{s}(T)-1}\tau_{\alpha_{s}(k)}\right)}_{\overline{\tau}_{s}}+\underbrace{\left(\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{T-t_{J(T)}}{J(T)}\right)\right)}_{0}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrel{\to}^{\eqref{LawOfLargeNumbers_Applied}\,+\,\eqref{Probability_P_s}}}\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}}{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\overline{\tau}_{s}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}}{\text{Tr}[\cdots]}=:\Theta_{B}.

3.1.3
Summarizing the two cases

When the number of quantum jumps is infinite, this minimal absorbing set is a subset of the possible states of the discrete quantum trajectory,
B\subseteq\bigcup_{\omega\in\mathcal{U}}\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)
.
When on the other hand the number of quantum jumps is finite, that is when the discrete quantum trajectory ends at a possible trapping state
\Theta_{\text{trap}}
, then
the discrete quantum trajectory also ends in a minimal absorbing set, which is in this case
given by
B=\bigl\{\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{T}I_{\Theta_{\text{trap}}}(T)\bigr\}
.

Taking the two expressions for the average of the quantum trajectory together, we obtain

\displaystyle\Theta_{B}:=\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)=\begin{cases}\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,I_{\Theta_{J_{M}}}(T),&\text{ for finitely many jumps}\\
\\
\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}}{\text{Tr}[\cdots]},&\text{ when the number of jumps is infinite. }\end{cases}

(29)

This result shows that the long-term behaviour of a quantum trajectory does not depend on the trajectory
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
itself, but only on the minimal absorbing set
B
it is eventually captured in.

3.2
Discrete-time Markov chains with finite state space

Now that we have computed an expression the time-averaged state
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)
for a fixed
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
, we need to evaluate two quantities in order to obtain the stationary states of the Lindblad equation:

(i)

First, the probability
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(B)\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
for a quantum trajectory to land in a minimal absorbing set
B
.

(ii)

Second, the expression for the stationary states
\Theta_{B}
, corresponding to the minimal absorbing state
B
.

Depending on the nature of the minimal absorbing set, we need for the expression of the stationary state either

(iia)

the probabilities
q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)
for the states
\Theta_{s}\in B

within
the minimal absorbing set
B
as well as their time-weighted ensemble average
\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}
or

(iib)

the long-term average
\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,I_{\Theta_{\text{trap}}}(T)
, where
\Theta_{\text{trap}}
is a possible trapping state.

Figure 4:
Illustrating the significance of a Markov chain in a quantum state unraveling: In order to compute the stationary state we need both the long-term behaviour
\Theta_{B_{i}}
of a single quantum trajectory contained in the minimal absorbing set
B_{i}
, as well as the probability
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(B_{i}))
for such a trajectory to be captured by this minimal absorbing set
B_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Both the probability for reaching each minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(B))\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
, as well as the probability for each state
q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)
within such a minimal absorbing set
can be obtained by considering only the states
\Theta_{s}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
of the discrete quantum trajectory and the transition probabilities between them. The discrete quantum trajectory is a Markov chain, since the probability for the next state
\Theta_{n+1}
depends only on the previous state
\Theta_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{n+1}\,|\,\Theta_{n},\Theta_{n-1},\dots,\Theta_{0})=\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{n+1}\,|\,\Theta_{n}).

(30)

We will therefore in the following summarize those results for classical discrete-time Markov chains that will be relevant for obtaining the two types of probabilities. The connection to the quantum trajectories and the evaluation of the time averages between jumps will follow in the subsequent subsections.

We consider a classical, discrete-time Markov process
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
taking values in the finite state space
\Omega
. The transition probabilities are defined via

\displaystyle Q_{ij}:=q_{j\to i}:=\mathcal{P}\left(X_{n+1}=i\,|\,X_{n}=j\right)=\mathcal{P}\left(X_{n+1}=i\,|\,X_{n}=j,\dots,X_{0}=i_{0}\right).

(31)

This specifies the transition matrix
Q\in\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}\right)^{|\Omega|\times|\Omega|}
, which is a column-stochastic matrix that satisfies

\displaystyle Q_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 0\text{ , for all }i,j\in\Omega\text{ and }

(32)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{|\Omega|}Q_{ij}

\displaystyle=1\text{ , for all }j\in\Omega.

The states and the nonzero transition probabilities form a network
(\Omega,\mathcal{E})
, with the states
\Omega
being the nodes and the nonzero transitions
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
being the directed links between them.
When there is no transition from state
i
to state
j
, the associated transition probability
q_{i\to j}
vanishes.
The transition probability indicate the strength of the link.

For each finite-size Markov chain there is an associated state transition network (a directed weighted graph). An example is given in Figure
5
:

.

For all subsets of states
B\subseteq\Omega
there is a corresponding subnetwork
(B,\mathcal{E}_{B})
, where
\mathcal{E}_{B}:=\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\,:\,i,j\in B\}
consists of all links in the subset
B\subseteq\Omega
.

We call a subset
B\subseteq\Omega

absorbing
if there are no edges pointing out of
B
, that is
q_{i\to j}=0
for all
i\in B
and
j\in B^{C}
. An absorbing subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is called
minimal
if for all absorbing subsets
C\subseteq\Omega
with
C\subseteq B
we have
C=B
. In
particular, there can be more than one minimal absorbing subset
[
FD22
]
.

We denote with
q_{n}(i)
the probability of the system to be in state
i
at the time step
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
q_{n}(i)\geq 0
,
\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}q_{n}(i)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Given an initial probability distribution
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in[0,1]^{|\Omega|}
, with
\|\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\|_{1}=1
, the probability distribution after
n\in\mathbb{N}
time steps is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0})

\displaystyle=Q^{n}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{|\Omega|}q_{0}(j)\,\underbrace{\hskip 11.38109ptQ^{n}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\hskip 11.38109pt}_{\left(\mathcal{P}(X_{n}=i\,|\,X_{0}=j)\right)_{i\in\Omega}}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{|\Omega|}q_{0}(j)\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,X_{0}=j)\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{P}(X_{n}=|\Omega|\,|\,X_{0}=j)\end{pmatrix},

(33)

\displaystyle\text{with }\mathcal{P}(X_{n}=i\,|\,X_{0}=j)

\displaystyle=\left(Q^{n}\right)_{ij}=\sum\limits_{\alpha_{1}\in\Omega}\dots\sum\limits_{\alpha_{n-1}\in\Omega}Q_{i,\alpha_{n-1}}\cdot\dotsc\cdot Q_{\alpha_{1},j}.

The probability that a probability starting in state
j\in\Omega
will be in state
i\in\Omega
after
n\in\mathbb{N}
steps is given by the sum over all possible trajectories
\bigl(j=X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{n}=i\bigr)\in\Omega^{n}
, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\omega=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\text{ with }X_{0}=i\text{ and }X_{n}=j)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(X_{n}=i\,|\,X_{0}=j)=\sum\limits_{\alpha_{1}\in\Omega}\dots\sum\limits_{\alpha_{n-1}\in\Omega}Q_{i,\alpha_{n-1}}\cdot\dotsc\cdot Q_{\alpha_{1},j}.

(34)

A
limiting distribution

\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}Q^{K}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
does not necessarily exist for a discrete-time Markov chain since oscillations are possible. However, the time average
\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}Q^{k}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
exists for all finite state space
\Omega
, and for all initial distributions
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
and converges to (one of) the
stationary solutions
, which are eigenvectors of the transition matrix
Q
to the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
, see appendix
A.4
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between minimal absorbing sets of the state-transition network and the stationary solutions of a Markov chain
[
FD22
]
: The number of minimal absorbing sets equals the number of (linearly independent) stationary solutions, and for every minimal absorbing set we can construct an associated eigenvector whose non-zero entries correspond to the states in the minimal absorbing network, see equation (
76
) in appendix
A.5
.

The algorithm for obtaining the stationary solution corresponding to the initial condition
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
is the following:

i)

Find all minimal absorbing sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
of the network
\Omega
, with
n\in\mathbb{N},\,n\leq|\Omega|
.

ii)

Compute probability vectors
\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
according to formula (
74
) in the appendix, which will be pairwise orthogonal.

iii)

Enlarge the orthonormal system
\left(\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{i})\right)_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
to an ordered basis

\displaystyle\bigl(\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{1}),\dots,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{n}),\boldsymbol{h}^{(n+1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}^{(|\Omega|)}\bigr),

where the first
n
basis vectors are
\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{1}),\dots,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{n})
.

iv)

When the basis representation of the initial condition is given by
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{B_{i}}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}\in B_{i})+\sum\limits_{i=n+1}^{|\Omega|}\mu_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}^{(i)}
, then the corresponding stationary solution is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{B_{i}}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{i})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}(B_{i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0})\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{i}).

(35)

An example is given in appendix
A.8
.

Note that while the focus in
[
FD22
]
was on continuous-time Markov chains,
the above steps for discrete-time Markov chains follow directly from there.
Stationary solutions of a discrete-time Markov chain are identical to the stationary solutions of the corresponding continuous-time Markov chain since eigenvectors to the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
of transition matrix
Q
of the discrete-time Markov chain are eigenvalues to the eigenvalue
\lambda=0
of transition matrix
\Gamma_{Q}=Q-\mathbb{1}
of the associated continuous-time Markov chain:
\Gamma_{Q}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}=\boldsymbol{0}=(Q-\mathbb{1})\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}
.

3.3
Constructing the Markov chain for quantum trajectories

In order to apply the theory of Markov chains, we need to determine both the states and the transition probabilities. Unlike in classical Markov chains, it can happen that an unravelling stops, as the column sum of the transition matrix need not be one.
This is the case for possible trapping states.
When constructing the matrix
Q
, this has to be taken into account. In the following, we will first compute the set of possible states of the Markov chain, before we derive expressions for the transition probabilities.

3.3.1
The states of the Markov chain

We denote the set of possible states in the Markov chain by

\displaystyle\{\Theta_{s}\,:\,s\in\mathcal{S}\}:=\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\omega\in\mathcal{U}}\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)\,\cup\,\left\{\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{I_{\Theta_{\text{trap}}}(T)}{T}\;:\;\Theta_{\text{trap}}\in\bigcup_{\omega\in\mathcal{U}}\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)\text{ and }\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\text{Tr}[U_{t}(\Theta_{\text{trap}})\,\neq\,0\right\}.

(36)

The last set is only relevant for the possible trapping states
\Theta_{\text{trap}}
with
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\text{Tr}[U_{t}(\Theta_{\text{trap}})\,\in(0,1]
. In that case, there is a non-zero chance for a transition from the state
\Theta_{\text{trap}}
to the minimal absorbing set
\left\{\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{I_{\Theta}(T)}{T}\right\}
(see figure (
6
) for an illustration). An explicit expression for the time average of a quantum trajectory that is captured in this minimal absorbing set is given below in section
3.5.2
.

We choose the initial distribution vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
to be the first unit vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
, corresponding to the initial state
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}=\Theta_{0}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}

3.3.2
The transition probabilities of the Markov chain

The transition probability

\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{s_{1}}\to\Theta_{s_{2}})
is the probability that the next state in the quantum trajectory equals
\Theta_{s_{2}}
, provided that the last state was
\Theta_{s_{1}}
. It can be computed to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{s_{2}}\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}}\underbrace{p(\tau,\Theta_{s_{2}}\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})}_{f(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})\,\cdot\,\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{s_{2}}\,|\,\tau,\Theta_{s_{1}})}\text{ d}\tau=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}}f(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})\,\cdot\,\underbrace{\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{s_{2}}\,|\,\tau,\Theta_{s_{1}})}_{\sum\limits_{k\in I(s_{1}\to s_{2})}\,\frac{f^{(k)}(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})}{f(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})}}\text{ d}\tau=

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in I(s_{1}\to s_{2})}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}}f^{(k)}(\tau\,|\,\Theta_{s_{1}})\text{ d}\tau,

where the sum goes over all Lindblad operators
V_{k}
that yield a transition from state
\Theta_{s_{1}}
to
\Theta_{s_{2}}
, or more formally:
I({s_{1}}\to{s_{2}}):=\{k\in I\,:\,J_{k}\circ U_{t}(\Theta_{s_{1}})=\Theta_{s_{2}}\text{ for some }t>0\}
.

3.5.2
Finitely many quantum jumps

When the number of jumps is finite, we have to evaluate
\Theta_{B}=\left\langle\frac{U_{t}(\Theta_{\text{trap}})}{\text{Tr}[]}\right\rangle_{t\geq 0}

3.6
The stationary solution of the Lindblad equation

Now that we have obtained all building blocks for the stationary solution, let us put them together. Our starting point was the quantum jump unravelling (section
2
) and the fact that the time and ensemble average can be exchanged (section
A.1
)
, due to the fact that the time average of a single trajectory exists.
We assumed that the space of density matrices occurring immediately after jumps is finite
(|\mathcal{S}|<\infty)
, so that a stationary solution
of the corresponding Markov chain
always exists.
We evaluated the time average
\Theta_{B}:=\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)=\langle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega,t)\rangle_{t\geq 0})
of a single trajectory
\omega\in\mathcal{U}

(see equation (
29
) in section
3.1
), and we showed that it depends only on the minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
the quantum trajectory is eventually captured in (section
3.2
). We therefore separated the integral over the different unravellings into a sum over the minimal absorbing sets in the network, where the integrand
\Theta_{B}
is constant over the set
\mathcal{U}(B)
of quantum trajectories that all land in the associated minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
.
Mathematically, all this gives the following steps for obtaining the formula for the limiting density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
, depending on the initial state
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\,\text{ d}\,t=\langle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t)\rangle_{t\geq\,0}=\langle\langle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\rangle_{\omega\in U}\rangle_{t\geq\,0}=

(50)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrel{\to}^{\eqref{ChangingLimits_TimeAndEnsembleAverage}}}\langle\;\underbrace{\langle\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(t,\omega)\rangle_{t\geq\,0}}_{\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)}\;\rangle_{\omega\in U}=\int_{\mathcal{U}=\dot{\bigcup}_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{U}(B_{i})}\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)\,\text{ d}\,\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrel{\to}^{|\mathcal{S}|<\infty}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{U}(B_{i})}\underbrace{\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}^{\infty}(\omega)}_{\Theta_{B_{i}}}\,\text{ d}\,\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}(\omega)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\bigr)\,\Theta_{B_{i}}.

By mapping the quantum trajectories onto discrete-time Markov chains (section
3.3
), we were able to compute the probability
\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\bigr)
of landing in the minimal absorbing set
B_{i}
as the coefficient
\mu_{B_{i}}
in an expansion of the initial probability distribution vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
into an ordered basis of generalized eigenvectors of the transition matrix
Q
(see section
3.2
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\mu_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}^{i}=:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{B_{i}}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}\in B_{i})+\sum\limits_{i=n+1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\mu_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}^{i}.

(51)

The first
n
generalized eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}\in B_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots n\}
are the actual eigenvectors of
Q
to the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
(see equation (
74
) in Appendix
A.5
).
The second term contains the remaining generalized eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{h}^{i}
, and it declines toward zero under repeated application of the transition matrix
Q
.

An explicit expression for
\Theta_{B}
was given in section
3.5
. It is
the stationary solution of the Lindblad equation that lies in the minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
.

3.7
The classical case

The classical case can be recovered when the Hamiltonian vanishes (
H=0
) and
\Lambda=\beta\,\mathbb{1}
is proportional to the identity operator for some real number
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq\,0}
. In this case, there are no possible trapping states, and we get for the stationary solution of a minimal absorbing set
B\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
and for the stationary solution of equation (
1
)

\displaystyle\Theta_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\overbrace{\overline{I_{s}(\tau_{s})}}^{\Theta_{s}/\beta}}{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\frac{1}{\beta}\,\underbrace{\text{Tr}[\Theta_{s}]}_{1}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\Theta_{s}\,\frac{1}{\beta}}{\,\frac{1}{\beta}}=\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B)\,\Theta_{s}

(52)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\bigr)\,\Theta_{B_{i}}\,=\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,\underbrace{\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\bigr)\,q(\Theta_{s}\,|\,B_{i})\right)}_{q(\Theta_{s})}\,\Theta_{s}=\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,q(\Theta_{s})\,\Theta_{s}.

4
Discussion and Conclusion

We have shown that the stationary states of the Lindblad equation (
1
) can be determined by using the concept of a
quantum jump unravelling
. The final formula is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\bigr)\,\Theta_{B_{i}},

(53)

where the sum goes over all minimal absorbing set of the discrete-time Markov chain associated with the quantum jump process, whose states
\{\Theta_{s}\,:\,s\in\mathcal{S}\}
and transition probabilities
\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{s_{1}}\to\Theta_{s_{1}})
are given in section
3.3.1
and
3.3.2
, respectively.

The factor
\Theta_{B_{i}}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\,\Theta(t,\omega)\text{ d}\,t
denotes the time average of all quantum trajectories
\omega\in\mathcal{U}
which are eventually captured by the minimal absorbing set
B_{i}\subseteq\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
. Explicit expressions for
\Theta_{B_{i}}
are derived in section
3.5
, depending on whether the number of quantum jumps is finite or infinite.

\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathcal{U}(B_{i})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\bigr)
denotes the probability for a quantum trajectory being captured by the minimal absorbing set
B_{i}
, provided the initial state is given by
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
. It can be computed from the coefficients
\mu_{B_{i}}
of the vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
(representing the initial state
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}
) when expressing it in a basis of generalized eigenvectors of the transition matrix
Q
of the discrete-time Markov chain, which is defined in section
3.3.2
.
When there is no change of the states between jumps, the classical case is recovered, see section
3.7
.

In the case of infinitely many states, such a methods is in general no longer applicable:
Although the existence of stationary density matrices
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
for the
Lindbladian
is guaranteed (see
[
KM04
]
), the existence of stationary vectors
\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
for the corresponding
Markov chain
is not, as shown in appendix.
A.6
. This limitation can only be dissolved if an analogous expression to formula (
76
), but for special kinds of Markov chains with infinite state space is known. One possible condition could be to require the Markov chain to be
positive recurrent
, in which case a stationary solution exists
[
Pri13
,
DMPS18
,
Bré13
]
. An interesting question for further research would be which conditions the Lindblad operators must have for the states of the quantum trajectory to be positive recurrent or whether this is a necessary condition for the Lindblad operators.

However, it is possible to compute the stationary density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\infty}
when the sequence of states in the quantum trajectory, together with the corresponding average waiting times, converges:
(\Theta_{k},\overline{\tau_{k}})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}(\Theta_{\infty},\overline{\tau_{\infty}})
. In this case, we can evaluate the time average of a quantum trajectory to

\displaystyle\langle\Theta(t,\omega)\rangle_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{J(T)}\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J(T)-1}I_{\Theta_{k}}(\tau_{k})}{\text{Tr}[]}=\frac{1}{\overline{\tau_{\infty}}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\tau_{\infty}}}\frac{U_{t}(\Theta_{\infty})}{\text{Tr}[]}\,\text{ d}t.

(54)

This means that while we can no longer use the formula for
\Theta_{B}
given by equation (
29
) when the minimal absorbing set
B
contains infinitely many states, we can can use equation (
53
) when the
number
of minimal absorbing sets is finite. We only have to substitute the expression of equation (
54
) for
\Theta_{B_{i}}
. Unfortunately, this is not quite the same as giving an explicit recipe for obtaining the steady state, as was possible in the case with finitely many states. But it might still be possible to obtain the stationary density matrices this way for many more cases that are physically relevant.

A.3
In a classical, discrete-time Markov chain with a finite state space a trajectory
(\Theta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will eventually be captured by a minimal absorbing set, that is
\mathcal{P}(\Theta_{n}\in\{\text{minimal absorbing sets}\})=1
.

Proof.

Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
be the minimal absorbing sets of the associated state transition network for the Markov chain. We know that for every state
s\in\Omega
in the network there is a path to a minimal absorbing set
B\subset\Omega
. This means that there is a finite number of states
s=s_{0},\dots,s_{L}\in B
with
q_{s_{j}\to s_{j+1}}>0

[
FD22
]
. We now let
l_{i}
be the length of the shortest (non-zero) path to the minimal absorbing set number
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
q_{i}
be the largest probability for reaching the set
B_{i}
in
l_{i}
time steps, namely

\displaystyle l_{i}

\displaystyle:=\min\limits_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left\{(\omega_{0}=s,\dots,\omega_{l}\in B_{i})\,:\,q_{\omega_{j}\to\omega_{j+1}}>0\text{ for all }j\in\{0,\dots,l\}\right\}

\displaystyle L:=\max\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\{l_{i}\}

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle:=\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega^{l_{i}+1}}\left\{\prod\limits_{j=0}^{l_{i}-1}q_{\omega_{j}\to\omega_{j+1}},\text{ with }\omega_{0}=s\text{ and }\omega_{l_{i}}\in B_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle q_{0}:=\min\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\{q_{i}\}.

Further, be let
L\,(q_{0})
be the largest (smallest) of the
l_{i}\,(q_{i})
. For a fixed natural number
m\in\mathbb{N}
we define the event that a trajectory
\omega\in\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
after
L\cdot m
time steps is not contained in a minimal absorbing set

\displaystyle A_{m}

\displaystyle:=\{\omega\in\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,\omega_{L\cdot m}\nin\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}\}.

(64)

The corresponding probability for
A_{m}
to occur can be estimated to
\mathcal{P}(A_{m})\leq(1-q_{0})^{m}
. Since the sum over all probabilities of
A_{m}
is finite
(\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{P}(A_{m})<\infty)
, we know by the lemma of Borel-Cantelli that the probability for
A_{m}
to occur infinitely often vanishes,
\mathcal{P}(A_{m}\text{ infinitely often })=0
.

∎

A.4
The time average of a classical, discrete-time Markov chain with finite state space

Let
Q
be the transition matrix of a discrete-time Markov chain that is the entries represent the transition probabilities
Q_{ij}=\mathcal{P}(j\to i)
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0})=Q^{k}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
is the probability distribution after
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
jumps. While the limit

need not exist, since oscillation are possible (consider the aperiodic Markov chain with the transition matrix

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}Q^{k}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}(B_{i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0})\,\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\in B_{i}),

(65)

where
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{n}
are the minimal absorbing sets of the associated state-transition network.

Proof.

If

is the Jordan normal form of the transition matrix, it can be shown by induction that the absolute value of the
i
-
j
-th entry of the
K
-th exponent of
J
is proportional to the
K
-th exponent of the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue of
Q
, that is

\displaystyle\left|(J^{k})_{ij}\right|\leq C_{ij}\,\cdot\,|\lambda_{S}|^{k+i-j}\,\cdot\,1_{\{i\leq j\leq i+k\}},

(66)

where
C_{ij}
is a constant that only depends on
i
and
j
and
\lambda_{S}:=\arg\max\{|\lambda|\,:\,\lambda\in\sigma(Q)\}
.

When
|\lambda_{S}|\leq 1
,
\lambda_{S}\neq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}J^{k}\,\right)_{i\,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\underbrace{\left|\,\left(J^{k}\right)_{i\,j}\,\,\right|}_{\leq\,C_{ij}\,\lambda_{S}^{k+i-j}}\leq C_{ij}\,\lambda_{S}^{i-j}\,\frac{1}{K}\underbrace{\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\lambda_{S}^{k}\right)}_{\frac{1-\lambda_{S}^{K}}{1-\lambda_{S}}}

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{K\to\infty}0.

(67)

Since we can interpret the matrix
\Gamma_{Q}:=Q-\mathbb{1}
as a transition matrix of a continuous-time Markov chain, and
\text{kern }(\Gamma_{Q}-\lambda\,\mathbb{1})=\text{kern }(Q-(\lambda+1)\,\mathbb{1})
we conclude that for the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
of

the algebraic multiplicity

must coincide with the geometric multiplicity

\displaystyle d_{\lambda=1}(Q)=d_{\lambda=0}(\Gamma_{Q}=Q-\mathbb{1}){\mathrel{\to}^{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{fernengel2022obtaining}{}{}]}}}a_{\lambda=0}(\Gamma_{Q}=Q-\mathbb{1})=a_{\lambda=1}(Q).

(68)

This means that we can write the transition matrix as

0&\tilde{J}\end{pmatrix}\,S
, with
\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\tilde{J}^{k}=0
.

When writing the initial state as a linear combination of generalized eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{|\Omega|}\mu_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\lambda}^{(i)}(Q)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0})

\displaystyle:=\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}Q^{k}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=S^{-1}\,\lim\limits_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\underbrace{J^{k}}_{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}&0\\

0&0\end{pmatrix}\,S\,\underbrace{\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{q}_{0}\hskip 8.53581pt}_{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{|\Omega|}\mu_{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\lambda}^{(i)}(Q)}=

(69)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{|\Omega|}\mu_{i}\,S\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}&0\\

∎

In the last step (*) we have used the fact that the probability

(70)

It can easily be verified that the coefficients

When the number of quantum jumps in a quantum trajectory is not bounded, then
\frac{t_{J(T)}}{T}\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}1
in probability.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
, then we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\left(\left|\frac{t_{J(T)}}{T}-1\right|>\epsilon\right)=\mathcal{P}\left(T-t_{J(T)}>\epsilon\,T\right)=\text{Tr}[U_{\epsilon\,T}\,(\Theta_{J(T)})]\xlongrightarrow{T\to\infty}0,

(81)

where we used the fact that
\Theta_{J(T)}
can never be a possible trapping state, since the number of quantum jumps is by assumption not bounded, so the quantum trajectory will almost surely jump at some point.

∎
