A Discrete Stochastic Interpretation of the Dominative p p -Laplacian
Karl K. Brustad, Peter Lindqvist, Juan J. Manfredi

Abstract




1.
Introduction

The interplay between Stochastic Game Theory and
nonlinear
Partial Differential Equations has been of increasing importance, beginning with the pioneering work of Kohn and Serfaty

[
KS09
,
KS10
]
and Peres, Schramm, Sheffield and Wilson
[
PS08
,
PSSW09
]
, involving discrete processes. We shall develop this connection for the
Dominative p-Laplace Equation
, which is akin to the well-known normalized p-Laplace Equation. Thus, we shall present a discrete stochastic interpretation and prove uniform convergence of the discretizations.

The Dominative
p
-Laplacian is the operator defined for
2\leq p<\infty
as follows:

(1.1)

\mathcal{D}_{p}u(x):=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{N-1}+(p-1)\lambda_{N},

where we have ordered the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix
D^{2}u(x)
as
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\ldots\leq\lambda_{N}
.
It was introduced by Brustad in
[
Bru18b
]
in order to give a natural explanation of the superposition principle for the
p
-Laplace equation (see
[
CZ03
]
and
[
LM08
]
). This operator is interesting in its own right. The case
p=2
reduces to the Laplacian
\Delta u(x)
.

It is also of interest to consider the case
p=\infty
with the following interpretation

(1.2)

\mathcal{D}_{\infty}u(x):=\lambda_{N}.

\mathcal{D}_{\infty}u=0
is the largest eigenvalue equation, or the equation for the concave envelope, which has been studied in
[
Obe07
]
and
[
OS11
]
. This equation is only
degenerate
elliptic, but viscosity solutions with
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary values are in the class
C^{1,\alpha}

[
OS11
]
.

The operator
\mathcal{D}_{p}
is sublinear, therefore convex, and uniformly elliptic for
p<\infty
. Thus, the viscosity solutions of the equation
\mathcal{D}_{p}u(x)=0
are locally in the class
C^{2,\alpha}
. See Chapter 6 in
[
CC95
]
for the regularity result and
[
Bru18a
]
for the general theory of sublinear operators.

Consider the following problem.
Suppose we have a domain
\Omega
and a function
F
defined on an
\epsilon
-strip along the
outside
of its boundary. Start at a point
x_{0}
in
\overline{\Omega}
. Now, you get to choose a
direction

\sigma=\sigma(x_{0})\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. Then, for a fixed
q\in(0,1]
, a new starting point
x_{1}\in\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
is picked according to the rule

(1.3)

\begin{cases}&\text{with probability $q$, $x_{1}\in B_{\epsilon}(x_{0})$ is selected at random}\\
&\text{with probability $\frac{1-q}{2}$ we set $x_{1}:=x_{0}+\epsilon\sigma$, and}\\
&\text{with probability $\frac{1-q}{2}$ we set $x_{1}:=x_{0}-\epsilon\sigma$.}\end{cases}

Observe that the probabilities sum up to 1, as they should. Also note that
x_{1}
does not depend on the sign of
\sigma
. Thus you are effectively only choosing a
line
through the origin.
Repeat the process until you leave
\overline{\Omega}
at, say, step
\tau
.
The value
F(x_{\tau})
is then defined and let us say you want it to be as large as possible. The challenge is therefore:
How to choose the directions
(\sigma(x_{k}))_{k=0}^{\tau-1}
in order to maximize the expected value of
F(x_{\tau})
?

We shall show that the maximized expected value
u_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sup_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}(F(x_{\tau}))
, obtained from starting at
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, satisfies a
non-linear mean value property
, or Dynamic Programming Principle,

\displaystyle{u_{\epsilon}(x)=q\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB_{\epsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}}u_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy+\!\!\left(1-q\right)\sup_{|\xi|=1}\frac{u_{\epsilon}(x+\epsilon\xi)+u_{\epsilon}(x-\epsilon\xi)}{2}},

where we have used the notation

\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}f(y)\,dy=\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}f(y)\,dy

for the average of an integrable function
f
on a ball
B
.

Moreover, we will prove that
u_{\epsilon}\to u
uniformly
in
\overline{\Omega}
, where the limit function
u
is the unique solution of the Dirichlet problem

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclc}\mathcal{D}_{p}u(x)&=&0&\text{ for }x\in\Omega\\
u(x)&=&F(x)&\text{ for }x\in\partial\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

for the Dominative
p
-Laplace Equation.
The relation between
p\in[2,\infty)
and
q
is

(1.5)

q=\frac{N+2}{N+p},\qquad 1-q=\frac{p-2}{N+p}.

We shall assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain and that the prescribed boundary values

F\colon\partial\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}

are Lipschitz continuous.

2.
Statements of Results

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
.
For a fixed
{\varepsilon}>0
we set

\Omega_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon d(x,\Omega)\leq\epsilon\}\text{ and }\Gamma_{\epsilon}:=\Omega_{\epsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

Note that, by our definition,
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
does not contain the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Also, for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, we always have
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(x)\subset\Omega_{\epsilon}
.
We may extend the given bounded Lipschitz function

F\colon\partial\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
to
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
, preserving the same Lipschitz constant.

Fix
p\in[2,\infty)
and recall the relation (
1.5
) with
q
. We define a
non-linear
Mean Value Operator
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}
from the set of bounded and integrable Borel functions in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, to the set of bounded functions in
\overline{\Omega}

as

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v(x):=q\fint_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}v(y)\,dy+(1-q)\sup_{|\xi|=1}\frac{v(x+\epsilon\xi)+v(x-\epsilon\xi)}{2}.

For
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
we shall also write

\mathcal{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}v(x):=q\fint_{B_{\epsilon}(x)}v(y)\,dy+(1-q)\frac{v(x+\epsilon\xi)+v(x-\epsilon\xi)}{2}

and thus
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v=\sup_{|\xi|=1}\mathcal{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}v
.
If
v_{1},v_{2}
are two such functions in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
c
is a non-negative constant, one may easily check that

(1)

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[cv_{1}](x)=c\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}(x)
,

(2)

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[v_{1}+v_{2}](x)\leq\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}(x)+\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{2}(x)
,

(3)

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[v_{1}-v_{2}](x)\geq\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}(x)-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{2}(x)
,

(4)

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}(x)\leq\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{2}(x)
whenever
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
in
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(x)
,

for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[a+v_{1}]=a+\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}
in
\overline{\Omega}
for any affine function
a
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, and
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[\phi+v_{1}]=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\phi+\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{\xi}\phi+\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v_{1}
for any paraboloid
\phi(x)=\alpha|x-x_{0}|^{2}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
.
Also,
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}
is translational invariant, meaning that if
\theta_{h}(x):=x+h
, then
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[v\circ\theta_{h}]=(\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v)\circ\theta_{h}
in the proper domains.

Next, we show that

(2.1)

C_{N,p}\frac{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\phi(x)-\phi(x)}{\epsilon^{2}}=\mathcal{D}_{p}\phi,\qquad C_{N,p}:=2(N+p),

for second order polynomials
\phi
.

Let
\phi(x):=c+b^{T}x+{\frac{1}{2}}x^{T}Ax
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
A
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix. First, we verify (
2.1
) at
x=0
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\phi(0)-\phi(0)

\displaystyle=q\fint_{B_{\epsilon}(0)}{\frac{1}{2}}y^{T}Ay\,dy+\frac{1-q}{2}{\varepsilon}^{2}\sup_{|\xi|=1}\xi^{T}A\xi

\displaystyle=q\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2(N+2)}\tr A+\frac{1-q}{2}{\varepsilon}^{2}\lambda_{N}(A)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\frac{q}{2(N+2)}\Delta\phi+\frac{1-q}{2}\lambda_{N}(D^{2}\phi)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2(N+p)}\left(\Delta\phi+(p-2)\lambda_{N}(D^{2}\phi)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2(N+p)}\mathcal{D}_{p}\phi(0).

Next, for
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
\phi(x+h)-\phi(h)=h^{T}Ax+\phi(x)-\phi(0)
and so
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\phi(x+h)-\phi(h)=h^{T}Ax+\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\phi(x)-\phi(0)
by translational invariance and the property for affine functions. The identity (
2.1
) follows by setting
x=0
and replacing
h
with
x
.

In particular, by a Taylor expansion about
x\in\Omega
, (
2.1
) implies that

(2.2)

C_{N,p}\frac{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v(x)-v(x)}{\epsilon^{2}}=\mathcal{D}_{p}v(x)+O({\varepsilon}),\qquad\text{as ${\varepsilon}\to 0$,}

for every
v\in C^{3}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of Borel functions
v\colon\Omega_{\epsilon}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the conditions

(1)

v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\epsilon})
,
and

(2)

v=F
on
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
.

The next Lemma allows us to circumvent the question whether
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v
is measurable.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
v
is bounded and lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) in
\Omega_{\epsilon}
, then

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v
is bounded and l.s.c. in
\overline{\Omega}
.

(See §
3.1
below for the proof.)

Recall that for a bounded function
v
the lower semi-continuous envelope
v_{*}
is given by

v_{*}(x):=\sup\{\phi(x)\colon\phi\leq v\text{ and }\phi\text{ is l.s.c.}\}.

Define the iteration operator
\mathcal{T}^{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathcal{A}
as follows

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccl}\text{for }x\in\overline{\Omega},&\mathcal{T}^{\varepsilon}v(x)&:=&\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}[v_{*}](x)\\
\text{for }x\in\Gamma_{\epsilon},&\mathcal{T}^{\varepsilon}v(x)&=&F(x).\end{array}\right.

Its fixed point is of interest.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a unique function
v_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{T}^{\varepsilon}v_{\epsilon}(x)=v_{\epsilon}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, the function
v_{\varepsilon}
is l.s.c. in
\overline{\Omega}
.

(See §
3.2
below for the proof.)

We keep the subindex
\epsilon
to emphasize the dependence on the step-size.
We call
v_{\epsilon}
the
\epsilon
-mean value solution
.

Given a fixed Borel measurable control
\sigma\colon\overline{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
and a stepsize
{\varepsilon}>0
, we define a discrete random process according to the rule (
1.3
).

More precisely, fix
x_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and let

X^{\infty,x_{0}}:=\{\omega=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\colon x_{n}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\}

be the space of possible outcomes.
Set
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{0}
to be the trivial sigma-algebra
\{X^{\infty,x_{0}},\emptyset\}
, and for
n\geq 1
let
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}
be the sigma-algebra generated by the cylinders

\begin{array}[]{rl}A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots&\times A_{n}\times\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cdots\\
&=\{\omega\in X^{\infty,x_{0}}\colon x_{i}\in A_{i},i=1,\ldots,n\}\\
&=A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots\times A_{n}\quad\textrm{(abuse of notation)},\end{array}

where the
A_{i}\subset\Omega_{\varepsilon}
are Borel sets.

Clearly we have

\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}\subset\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n+1}
so that
\left\{\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is a filtration of the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}
generated by

\bigcup_{{n\geq 1}}\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}.

The coordinate functions
\textbf{x}_{n}(\omega)=x_{n}
are
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}
measurable.

Let
\tau_{\sigma}\colon X^{\infty,x_{0}}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be the random variable

\tau_{\sigma}(\omega)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon x_{n}\in\Gamma_{\epsilon}\},

where we follow the convention
\min\emptyset=\infty
. We say that
\tau_{\sigma}
is a
stopping time
with respect to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
.

For
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
define the
transition probability measures

\gamma[x]
as

\gamma[x](A):=\begin{cases}\delta_{x}(A),&\text{if $x\in\Gamma_{\epsilon}$,}\\
q\,\dfrac{|B_{\epsilon}(x)\cap A|}{|B_{\epsilon}(x)|}+\dfrac{1-q}{2}(\delta_{x+\epsilon\sigma(x)}(A)+\delta_{x-\epsilon\sigma(x)}(A)),&\text{if $x\in\overline{\Omega}$.}\end{cases}

We see that the mapping
x\to\gamma[x](A)
is Borel measurable for a fixed Borel set
A\subset X
. Indeed, the first term is, in fact, continuous and the second one is easily seen to be Borel measurable, since
x\to\sigma(x)
is so.

For
n\geq 1
define the probability measures
\mathbb{P}^{n,x_{0}}_{\sigma}
on the measurable space

(X^{\infty,x_{0}},\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n})
as follows:

\mathbb{P}^{1,x_{0}}_{\sigma}(A_{1}):=\gamma[x_{0}](A_{1})=\int_{A_{1}}1\,d\gamma[x_{0}](y_{1}),

(Note that
x_{0}
is fixed and the integration variable
y_{1}\in A_{1}
. )

\mathbb{P}^{2,x_{0}}_{\sigma}(A_{1}\times A_{2}):=\int_{A_{1}}\left(\int_{A_{2}}1\,d\gamma[y_{1}](y_{2})\right)d\gamma[x_{0}](y_{1})

In the general case we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n,x_{0}}_{\sigma}(A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n})

\displaystyle:=\int_{A_{1}}\!\!\left(\int_{A_{2}}\!\!\left(\cdots\int_{A_{n}}1\,d\gamma[y_{n-1}](y_{n})\right)\cdots d\gamma[y_{1}](y_{2})\right)\!d\gamma[x_{0}](y_{1}).

The family of probabilities
\left\{\mathbb{P}^{n,x_{0}}_{\sigma}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is consistent in the sense of Kolmogorov. Thus the limit probability

\mathbb{P}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{n,x_{0}}_{\sigma}

exists and we have

\mathbb{P}^{n,x_{0}}_{\sigma}(A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n})=\mathbb{P}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}(A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n})

for all cylinders
A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}
.

The following lemma tells us that the conditional expectation of the process at step
n
relative to its past history, reflected in the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n-1}
, is precisely the integral of
v
with respect to the transition probability from step
n-1
to
n
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
v\colon\Omega_{\varepsilon}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function. Then, whenever
x_{n-1}\in\overline{\Omega}
, we have

\mathbb{E}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}\left[v(x_{n})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n-1}\right]\!\!(x_{n-1})=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{\sigma(x_{n-1})}v(x_{n-1}),

and thus

\sup_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}\left[v(x_{n})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n-1}\right]\!\!(x_{n-1})=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v(x_{n-1}).

(See §
3.3
below for the proof.)

Lemma 2.4
.

For any fixed
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and every control
\sigma
the sequence of random variables

\{|x_{n\wedge\tau_{\sigma}}-y_{0}|^{2}-c_{N,p}(n\wedge\tau_{\sigma})\epsilon^{2}\}_{n\geq 1}

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration

\left\{\mathcal{F}^{x_{0}}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}.

Here,
c_{N,p}:=\frac{N+p-2}{N+p}
.

(See §
3.4
below for the proof.)

Applying Doob’s optional stopping to the finite stopping times
\tau_{\sigma}\wedge n:=\min\{\tau_{\sigma},n\}
and letting

n\to\infty
, we have

(2.4)

\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}^{x_{0}}[\tau_{\sigma}]\leq C(N,\Omega)

and the
process ends almost surely:

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}(\{\tau_{\sigma}<\infty\})=1.

Therefore, when we run the process we will hit
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
almost surely. Thus, the random variable

F(x_{\tau_{\sigma}})
is well defined. Averaging over all possible runs we get the expected value

(2.6)

u^{\sigma}_{\epsilon}(x_{0}):=\mathbb{E}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}[F(x_{\tau_{\sigma}})].

Optimizing over all strategies we get

(2.7)

u_{\epsilon}(x_{0}):=\sup_{\sigma}\left(u^{\sigma}_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right)=\sup_{\sigma}\left(\mathbb{E}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma}[F(x_{\tau_{\sigma}})]\right),

which we call the
\epsilon
-stochastic solution
.

Recall the
\epsilon
-mean value solution
v_{\varepsilon}
defined in Lemma
2.2
.

Theorem 2.1
.

We have

u_{\varepsilon}=v_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{in $\Omega_{\varepsilon}$.}

That is, the
{\varepsilon}
-stochastic solution
u_{\epsilon}
also satisfies the dynamic programming principle
u_{\epsilon}(x_{0})=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}u_{\epsilon}(x_{0})
.

(See §
3.5
below for the proof.)

The following comparison principle for
\epsilon
-mean value solutions follows at once from
formula (
2.7
) and Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
v_{\epsilon}
be the
\epsilon
-mean value solution with boundary values
F
and
let
w_{\epsilon}
be the
\epsilon
-mean value solution with boundary values
G
. If

F\leq G
on
\partial\Omega
(extended so that we still have
F\leq G
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
), then
v_{{\varepsilon}}\leq w_{{\varepsilon}}
in
\overline{\Omega}
.

We next adapt the Barles and Perthame procedure as in
[
BS91
]
of semi-continuous regularizations. We remark that in
[
BS91
]
the domain
\Omega
must be of class
C^{2}
and the equation must satisfy a strong uniqueness property involving the viscosity interpretation of the boundary Dirichlet data. We replace the strong uniqueness property with uniform boundary estimates for the discretizations
u_{\epsilon}
to reach the same uniform convergence conclusion as in
[
BS91
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Given
\eta>0
we can find
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that whenever

y_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
we have

|u_{\epsilon}(x)-F(y_{0})|\leq\eta

for
x\in B_{\epsilon_{2}}(y_{0})\cap\overline{\Omega}
.

(See §
3.6
below for the proof.)

For
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and
0<\delta\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
consider the sets

(2.8)

S(x,\delta):=\left\{u_{\epsilon}(y)\colon\epsilon<\delta\text{ and }|y-x|\leq\delta\right\}.

and the functions

U_{\delta}(x):=\sup S(x,\delta).

Note that the set
\sup S(x,\delta)
is bounded above, that

S(x,\delta_{1})\subset S(x,\delta_{2})
, and

(2.9)

u_{\epsilon}(x)\leq U_{\delta_{1}}(x)\leq U_{\delta_{2}}(x)

whenever
\epsilon<\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
.
Thus, the following limit is always well-defined

(2.10)

\overline{u}(x):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}U_{\delta}(x)

The function
\overline{u}\colon\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the half-relaxed upper limit of the family
\{u_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
when

\epsilon\to 0
and it is always u.s.c.

Similarly, we consider the functions

U^{\delta}(x):=\inf S(x,\delta),

so that

(2.11)

u_{\epsilon}(x)\geq U^{\delta_{1}}(x)\geq U^{\delta_{2}}(x)

whenever
\epsilon<\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
.
Thus, the following limit is always well-defined

(2.12)

\underline{u}(x):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}U^{\delta}(x)

The function
\underline{u}\colon\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the half-relaxed lower limit of the family
\{u_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
when

\epsilon\to 0
and it is always l.s.c.

Since
U^{\delta}\leq U_{\delta}
by definition, also
\underline{u}\leq\overline{u}
in
\overline{\Omega}
. On the boundary
\partial\Omega
, the oposite inequality
\underline{u}\geq\overline{u}
holds, because
Lemma
2.6
implies

(2.13)

\overline{u}(y)\leq F(y),

(2.14)

\underline{u}(y)\geq F(y),

for every
y\in\partial\Omega
.

Lemma 2.7
.

\overline{u}
is a viscosity subsolution and
\underline{u}
is a viscosity supersolution of
\mathcal{D}_{p}u=0
in
\Omega
.

(See §
3.6.1
below for the proof.)

.

We have
\overline{u}=\underline{u}
, denoted by
u
. It is the unique solution to the Dirichlet problem
(
1.4
).
Moreover
u_{\epsilon}\to u
uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}

(See §
3.6.2
below for the proof.)

3.
Proofs

3.1.
Proof of Lemma
2.1
. (l.s.c. of
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v
):

Let
v
be bounded and l.s.c. in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. That
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v
is bounded in
\overline{\Omega}
, is clear. For
x\in\overline{\Omega}
we can write

\mathcal{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}v(x)=\int v(x+y)\,d\gamma_{\xi}(y)

where

\gamma_{\xi}(A):=q\frac{|B_{\varepsilon}(0)\cap A|}{|B_{\varepsilon}(0)|}+(1-q)\frac{\delta_{\xi}(A)+\delta_{-\xi}(A)}{2}.

Now,

\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}\int v(x+y)\,d\gamma_{\xi}(y)\geq\int\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}v(x+y)\,d\gamma_{\xi}(y)\geq\int v(x_{0}+y)\,d\gamma_{\xi}(y)

by Fatou’s Lemma. Thus
\mathcal{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}v
is l.s.c. in
\overline{\Omega}
for each
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, and so is

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}v=\sup_{|\xi|=1}\mathcal{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}v

Proof of Lemma

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Certainly, if

\alpha v^{\varepsilon}+\beta m\leq U_{k}+\gamma\quad\text{in}\quad B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})\cap\Omega,

for
{\varepsilon}\leq{\varepsilon}_{k+1}
.

From the comparison principle (Lemma
2.5
) we get

\partial_{\varepsilon}(B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})\cap\Omega)\subseteq\Gamma_{1}^{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{2}^{\varepsilon},

where
\Gamma_{1}^{\varepsilon}=B_{\delta_{k}/2+{\varepsilon}}(z_{k})\cap\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\varepsilon}=(B_{\delta_{k}/2+{\varepsilon}}(z_{k})\setminus\overline{B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})})\cap\Omega
.

On
\Gamma_{1}^{\varepsilon}
, we have
u^{\varepsilon}=F\leq m
, hence:

\alpha u^{\varepsilon}+\beta m\leq m=\inf_{R_{k}}U_{k}\leq U_{k}\leq U_{k}^{\varepsilon}+\gamma,

since
\Gamma_{1}^{\varepsilon}\subset R_{k,{\varepsilon}}
.

On
\Gamma_{2}^{\varepsilon}
, we have
v^{\varepsilon}\leq M_{k}
by assumption, because
B_{\delta_{k}/2+{\varepsilon}}(z_{k})\subset B_{\delta_{k}}(y)
.
For
x\in\partial B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})
, we have
|x-z_{k}|=\delta_{k}/2
, hence:

(3.6)

\begin{split}U_{k}(x)&=\frac{M_{k}-m}{\delta_{k}^{b}-(\mu\delta_{k+1})^{b}}\left(|\delta_{k}/2|^{b}-(\mu\delta_{k+1})^{b}\right)+m\\
&=\frac{M_{k}-m}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}\left((1/2)^{b}-(\mu/4)^{b}\right)+m\\
&=\frac{1-(1/2)^{b}}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}m+\frac{(1/2)^{b}-(\mu/4)^{b}}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}M_{k}\\
&=\alpha m+\beta M_{k}\end{split}

and by monotonicity

U_{k}\geq\alpha m+\beta M_{k}
in
\Gamma_{2}^{\varepsilon}
.
Hence:

\alpha m+\beta v_{{\varepsilon}}\leq\alpha m+\beta M_{k}\leq U_{k}\leq U_{k}^{\varepsilon}+\gamma

in
\Gamma_{2}^{\varepsilon}
.
In conclusion, we have:

\alpha m+\beta v_{{\varepsilon}}\leq U_{k}^{\varepsilon}+\gamma\quad\text{in}\quad\partial_{\varepsilon}(B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})\cap\Omega),

and the claim follows again by the comparison principle Lemma
2.5
.

Consider next the intersection
B_{\delta_{k+1}}(y)\cap\Omega
. We have
B_{\delta_{k+1}}(y)\subset B_{(2-\mu)\delta_{k+1}}(z_{k})
and for
x\in B_{(2-\mu)\delta_{k+1}}(z_{k})
we have:

(3.7)

\begin{split}U_{k}(x)&\leq\frac{M_{k}-m}{\delta_{k}^{b}-(\mu\delta_{k+1})^{b}}\left(((2-\mu)\delta_{k+1})^{b}-(\mu\delta_{k+1})^{b}\right)+m\\
&=\frac{M_{k}-m}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}\left(((2-\mu)/4)^{b}-(\mu/4)^{b}\right)+m\\
&=\alpha^{\prime}m+\beta^{\prime}M_{k},\end{split}

where we have set

\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{1-((2-\mu)/4)^{b}}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{\prime}=\frac{(2-\mu)/4)^{b}-(\mu/4)^{b}}{1-(\mu/4)^{b}}.

Also, note that

B_{\delta_{k+1}}(y)\subset B_{\delta_{k}/2}(z_{k})
,
hence by (
3.1
) we get:

(3.8)

\alpha m+\beta v^{{\varepsilon}}\leq U_{k}+\gamma\quad\text{in}\quad B_{\delta_{k+1}}(y)\cap\Omega.

Combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), for
p\in B_{\delta_{k+1}}(y)\cap\Omega
and
{\varepsilon}<{\varepsilon}_{k+1}
, we get:

\begin{split}v^{\varepsilon}(p)&\leq\frac{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}{\beta}m+\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}M_{k}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\\
&=m+\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}(M_{k}-m)+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}.\end{split}

Observe that
\beta^{\prime}/\beta\in(0,1)
and that
\beta^{\prime}<\beta
. Recall that we have chosen

\gamma=\frac{(1/2)^{b}-((2-\mu)/4)^{b}}{8}\eta\leq\frac{(1/2)^{b}-((2-\mu)/4)^{b}}{2}(M_{k}-m).

Thus, we get

\begin{split}v^{\varepsilon}(p)&\leq m+\left(\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}+\frac{(1/2)^{b}-((2-\mu)/4)^{b}}{2\beta}\right)(M_{k}-m),\end{split}

and setting

(3.9)

\theta=\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}+\frac{(1/2)^{b}-((2-\mu)/4)^{b}}{2\beta}

we get

v^{\varepsilon}(p)\leq m+\theta(M_{k}-m)\leq m+\theta^{k+1}(M^{\epsilon}-m).

∎

The next Corollary, whose proof follows in a standard way from Lemma
3.2
, implies one half of Lemma
2.6
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Given
\eta>0
, there exist
\delta=\delta(\eta,F,\bar{\delta})
,
k_{0}=k_{0}(\eta,\mu,p,F)
,
{\varepsilon}_{0}={\varepsilon}_{0}(\eta,\delta,\mu,k_{0})
such that:

u^{\varepsilon}(x)-F(y)\leq\frac{\eta}{2},

for all
y\in\partial\Omega
,
x\in B_{\delta/4^{k_{0}}}(y)\cap\overline{\Omega}
and
{\varepsilon}\leq{\varepsilon}_{0}
.

To prove the (easier) lower bounds we observe that if
v
is a harmonic function, then it is also a subsolution of the equation
\mathcal{D}_{p}u(x)=0
. Thererefore if
u
and
v
agree on the boundary of a domain, we must have
v\leq u
in the domain. Thus, lower bounds for
u
follow from lower bounds for
v
. This suggest building barriers using the fundamental solution of the Laplacian. Repeating the argument of the proof of Lemma
3.2

with minima in lieu of maxima and using the fundamental solution of the Laplacian as barriers, we get the the analogue of Lemma
3.2
for lower bounds, and the other half of Lemma
2.6
.

3.6.1.
Proof of Lemma
2.7

Let us prove that
\overline{u}
is a viscosity subsolution; that is, it satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{p}\overline{u}\geq 0
in the viscosity sense. Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
and choose
\phi\in C^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\phi
touches
\overline{u}
from above at
x_{0}
; i.e. we have
\overline{u}(x_{0})=\phi(x_{0})
and

\overline{u}(x)<\phi(x)
for
x\in\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}
.
The following proposition is standard (see Lemma 4.2 in
[
Bar94
]
and the Mathoverflow discussion
[
MOF16
]
). We include the proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
\overline{B(x_{0},r)}\subset\Omega
. Then, there exists a sequence of numbers
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
and a sequence of points
y_{n}\to x_{0}
such that

u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})\to\overline{u}(x_{0})
and the function
\phi-u_{\epsilon_{n}}
has an approximate minimum in
\overline{B(x_{0},r)}
at the point
y_{n}
; that is, we have:

(3.10)

\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})\leq\phi(y)-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y)+\epsilon_{n}^{3}

for all
y\in B(x_{0},r)

Proof.

Choose sequences of numbers
\epsilon_{n}
and points
x_{n}
such that
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
,
x_{n}\to x_{0}
, and

u_{\epsilon_{n}}(x_{n})\to\bar{u}(x_{0})
as
n\to\infty
. Select a point
y_{n}\in\overline{B(x_{0},r)}
such that

(3.11)

\inf_{y\in\overline{B(x_{0},r)}}\,\,\phi(y)-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y)\geq\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n}))-\epsilon_{n}^{3}.

Select convergent subsequences
(\epsilon_{n},x_{n},y_{n})\to(0,x_{0},y_{0})
that we relabel with the index
n
again.
We have, using the defintion of
\bar{u}(x_{0})
, that

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}0&=&\phi(x_{0})-\bar{u}(x_{0})&=&\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(x_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(x_{n})\\
&&&\geq&\liminf_{n\to\infty}\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})-\epsilon_{n}^{3}\\
&&&\geq&\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0,y\to\hat{y}}\phi(y)-u_{\epsilon}(y)-\epsilon^{3}\\
&&&=&\phi(y_{0})-\bar{u}(y_{0}),\end{array}

which would be positive, unless we have
y_{0}=x_{0}
.
The proposition then follows from (
3.11
).
∎

To continue proving Lemma
2.7

start with

\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})\leq\phi(x)-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(x)+\epsilon_{n}^{3}

and for
n
large, noting that
B(y_{n},\epsilon_{n})\subset B(x_{0},r)
, integrate over the ball
B(y_{n},\epsilon_{n})
to get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})&\leq&\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}_{\xi}\left[\phi-u_{\epsilon_{n}}\right](y_{n})+\epsilon_{n}^{3}\\
&=&\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}_{\xi}\phi(y_{n})-\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}_{\xi}u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})+\epsilon_{n}^{3}\end{array}

Therefore, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(y_{n})-u_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}(y_{n})+\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}_{\xi}u_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}(y_{n})&\leq&\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}_{\xi}\phi(y_{n})+\epsilon_{n}^{3},\\
\end{array}

and taking supremum among all
|\xi|=1
we get

\phi(y_{n})-u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})+\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})\leq\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}\phi(y_{n})+\epsilon_{n}^{3}

from which we, using the fact that

u_{\epsilon_{n}}(y_{n})=\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}u_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}(y_{n}),

conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(y_{n})&\leq&\mathcal{M}^{\epsilon_{n}}\phi(y_{n})\\
&=&\phi(y_{n})+\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{2}}{C_{N,p}}\,\mathcal{D}_{p}\phi(y_{n})+o(\epsilon_{n}^{2})+\epsilon_{n}^{3}.\end{array}

Therefore, letting
n\to\infty
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{p}\phi(x_{0})\geq 0
and thus

\mathcal{D}_{p}\overline{u}(x_{0})\geq 0
in the viscosity sense.

A similar proof shows that
\underline{u}
is a viscosity supersolution.

3.6.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Given the boundary estimates
(
2.13
) and (
2.14
), we use the comparison principle for viscosity solution of
\mathcal{D}_{p}u=0
to conclude that

\overline{u}=\underline{u}

and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}u_{\epsilon}=\overline{u}=\underline{u}=u

uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
,
where
u
is the unique solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.4
).
