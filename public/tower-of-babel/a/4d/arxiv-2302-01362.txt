Signature SDEs from an affine and polynomial perspective
Christa Cuchiero, Sara Svaluto-Ferro, Josef Teichmann

Abstract
Signature stochastic differential equations (SDEs) constitute a large class of stochastic processes, here driven by Brownian motions, whose characteristics are linear maps of their own signature, i.e. of iterated integrals of the process with itself, and allow therefore for a generic path dependence. We show that their prolongation with the corresponding signature is an affine and polynomial process taking values in the set of group-like elements of the extended tensor algebra. By relying on the duality theory for affine or polynomial processes, we obtain explicit formulas in terms of converging power series for the Fourier-Laplace transform and the expected value of entire functions of the signature process’ marginals. The coefficients of these power series are solutions of extended tensor algebra valued Riccati and linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs), respectively, whose vector fields can be expressed in terms of the characteristics of the corresponding SDEs. We thus construct a class of stochastic processes which is universal (in a sense specified in the introduction) within Itô-diffusions with path-dependent characteristics and allows for an explicit characterization of the Fourier-Laplace transform and hence the full law on path space. The practical applicability of this affine and polynomial approach is illustrated by several numerical examples.



Keywords:
Signature SDEs, affine and polynomial processes, entire functions on path spaces and groups, power series expansions for Fourier-Laplace transforms, expected signature

Introduction

A plethora of stochastic models used in diverse areas, like mathematical finance, biology or physics, stems from the class of affine and polynomial processes. This is not always visible at first sight, because many stochastic models only appear as projections of infinite dimensional processes, and the corresponding affine or polynomial lift is often not the first object of attention.

Recognizing whether a Markovian semimartingale model falls in the class of
finite dimensional affine or polynomial processes,
as systematically introduced in
Duffie et al. 2003
and
Cuchiero et al. 2012
;
Filipović and Larsson 2016
, respectively, is usually simple and consists in verifying the properties of its semimartingale characteristics.
Indeed, in a diffusion setup, the polynomial property holds true if the drift is affine and if the instantaneous covariance matrix is at most quadratic in the state variables. If the latter is also affine, then
the affine property is additionally satisfied.
Even though this appears to be a rather narrow definition, solely the finite dimensional setting already contains many well known processes, e.g. Lévy processes, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, Feller-type diffusions, Wishart processes (see
Bru 1991
;
Cuchiero et al. 2011
), the Black-Scholes model, the Fisher-Snedecor process, or the Wright-Fisher diffusion, also called Kimura diffusion (see
Kimura 1964
) or Jacobi process, as well as all possible combinations thereof.

Many of those processes also have
infinite dimensional
analogs, which can often be realized as
measure valued processes
, in particular when their state space corresponds to some positive cone or bounded domain, such as probability measures. We refer to
Li 2011
;
Cuchiero et al. 2019
;
Cuchiero et al. 2024a
, where it is shown that many well-known examples from the literature, including the Dawson-Watanabe superprocess and the Fleming-Viot process, are of affine or polynomial type.
Another possibility to move to an infinite dimensional setup is to consider
Hilbert space valued processes
which have recently been treated in
Schmidt et al. 2020
;
Cox et al. 2022
for the affine case.

Beyond these measure valued and Hilbert space valued processes, a wide variety of finite dimensional models, in particular non-Markovian ones, appear as projections of infinite dimensional affine or polynomial processes. This applies in particular to
Markovian lifts
of stochastic Volterra processes (see e.g.
Protter 1985
) and Hawkes processes (see
Hawkes 1971
). As a consequence many examples in the field of rough volatility and rough covariance modeling, such as the rough Heston model (see
El Euch and Rosenbaum 2019
) or the rough Wishart processes (see
Cuchiero and Teichmann 2019
;
Abi Jaber 2022
)
can be viewed as infinite dimensional affine or polynomial processes, as shown e.g. in
Abi Jaber and El Euch 2019
;
Cuchiero and Teichmann 2020
.

Such Markovian lifts are of course not only restricted to Volterra-type processes, but can generically be applied by
linearizing the models’ characteristics
, making a multitude of stochastic processes a projection of sequence-valued affine and polynomial processes. This suggests a certain
universality of the affine and polynomial class
in the space of
all
stochastic processes driven by Brownian motion (or also other semimartingales, see
Cuchiero et al. 2024c
). This universality comes at the cost of more involved infinite dimensional state spaces, such as
\{(x^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, than the canonical one
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
considered initially in
Duffie et al. 2003
.

One purpose of this article is to make certain aspects of this universality mathematically precise. Indeed, we shall “linearize” generic classes of stochastic differential equations, called
signature SDEs
(introduced precisely in Section
4.2

\displaystyle dX_{t}={b}(\mathbb{X}_{t})dt+\sqrt{{a}(\mathbb{X}_{t})}dB_{t},\quad X_{0}=x,

(Sig-SDE)

where

and

in the extended tensor algebra, the state space of the signature process

, rigorously introduced in Section

and Definition

The invoked universality of signature SDEs holds on the level of the characteristics as all continuous path functionals can be approximated by linear and thus entire functions of the time-extended signature on compacts (see e.g.

As a special case we consider in Section

one-dimensional signature SDEs which translate – using a reparametrization of the signature – to

The prolongation of these processes are then simply sequences of monomials that are again affine and polynomial processes whose corresponding deterministic dual processes are sequence-valued Riccati and linear ODEs, respectively. In this setup we can give concrete specifications under which the required integrability conditions for the respective transform formulas hold true.

Both, in the one-dimensional and the general setup, our approach efficiently calculates time-dependent expansion coefficients of solutions of the Kolmogorov backward equations, i.e. certain parabolic partial differential equations (or their Cole-Hopf transforms), where expansion is meant with respect to state variables, here the signature components. This is reminiscent of Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theory. 2
Preliminaries

This section introduces affine and polynomial processes in the simplest possible setup, i.e. the
one
-dimensional setting. We also recall the fundamental notion of signature in the setting of continuous semimartingales.

2.1
Affine and polynomial processes from a duality point of view

Consider on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
, an Itô diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the form

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{a(X_{t})}dB_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}

(2.1)

with
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
continuous functions and
B
a standard Brownian motion.

Definition 2.1
.

A weak solution
X
of (
2.1
) is called
polynomial process
if
b
and
a
admit the representations
b(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x
and
a(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}
, respectively, for some constants
b_{0},b_{1},a_{0},a_{1}
, and
a_{2}
.
If additionally
a_{2}=0
, then the process is called affine.

Note that in this diffusion setting all affine processes are polynomial. In general this only holds true under auxiliary conditions on the moments of the jump measure.

Remark 2.2
.

Alternatively, such processes can be equivalently defined in via the martingale problem approach (as we will do later on in Section
3
).
In particular, denoting by

\mathcal{A}f(x)=f^{\prime}(x)b(x)+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)a(x).

the generator of a diffusion of the form (
2.1
) with domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, then
the process

N_{t}^{f}:=f(X_{t})-f(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}f(X_{s})ds

is a local martingale for every
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
.

Processes satisfying definition
2.1
have remarkable properties, namely that all marginal moments of a polynomial process, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{n}]
can be computed by solving a system of linear ODEs. In the case of affine processes, we additionally get that exponential moments
\mathbb{E}[e^{uX_{t}}]
for
u\in\mathbb{C}
can be expressed in terms of solutions of Riccati ODEs whenever
\mathbb{E}[|e^{uX_{t}}|]<\infty
.

We here present these implications from the point of view of dual processes (see e.g. Chapter 4 in
Ethier and Kurtz 1993
),
which has not been considered in this form in the original papers
Duffie et al. 2003
and
Cuchiero et al. 2012
;
Filipović and Larsson 2016
, where affine and polynomial processes were first systematically introduced.
Denote again by
\mathcal{A}
the (extended) generator given by (
2.2
).
We can then distinguish two different ways to compute
\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})]
. They are given by

(i)

the Kolmogorov backward equation:
\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})]=g(t,x)
, where

\partial_{t}g(t,x)=\mathcal{A}g(t,x),\quad g(0,x)=f(x).

(ii)

the duality method:
let
(U_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be an independent Markov process with state space
U
and infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{B}
. Assume that there is some
f:S\times U\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{A}(f(\cdot,u))(x)=\mathcal{B}(f(x,\cdot))(u),\quad\text{for all }x\in S,\,u\in U,

then (modulo technicalities)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X_{t},u)]=\mathbb{E}_{u}[f(x,U_{t})].

This duality relationship can then be used as a numerical method if the Kolmogorov backward equation for the dual process, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,u)=\mathcal{B}v(t,u),\quad v(0,u)=f(x,u)

(2.2)

with

v(t,u)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X_{t},u)]
is easy to solve or if the dual process

U
is easy to simulate or even deterministic.

The latter is the case for affine and polynomial processes, because (one choice of) the dual process is deterministic and corresponds to the solution of ODEs, explaining the often stated tractability properties.
In the case of affine processes, the function family consists of
exponentials
,
in the case of polynomial processes processes they are
polynomials.

Since these families are distribution determining (in the polynomial case under exponential moment conditions), existence of the dual process implies uniqueness of the solution of the martingale problem for the primal process
X
.

Affine case

As we shall only treat
linear processes
below, meaning that the characteristics are linear instead of affine, we here also set
b_{0}=0
and
a_{0}=0
in the definition of the affine process. The corresponding state space is given by
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

The
dual affine operator

\mathcal{B}
acting on
u\mapsto\exp(ux)
is defined via

\mathcal{B}(\exp({\,\cdot\,}x))(u):=\mathcal{A}(\exp(u{\,\cdot\,}))(x),

for each
x\in S
, which explicitly reads
{\mathcal{B}}g(u)=R(u)g^{\prime}(u)
for
g=\exp(\cdot x)
and

R(u):=\frac{1}{2}a_{1}u^{2}+b_{1}u.

From this we can see that

\mathcal{B}
is the restriction of the transport operator and
the corresponding (deterministic) dual process is a solution of the following ODE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(t)=R(\psi(t)),\quad\psi(0)=u,\qquad t\in[0,T],

(2.3)

which is of Riccati type due to the quadratic form of

.
The duality method
(ii)
with the deterministic dual process

yields the original formulation of the affine transform formula while (
2.2
) for computing expectations via the Kolmogorov equation for the dual process, leads to the corresponding transport equation formulation.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T>0
be fixed and let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be an affine process. Let

\mathbb{E}[|\exp(uX_{T})|]<\infty

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\exp(uX_{T})]=\exp(\psi(T)x),

where

solves the Riccati ODE given by (

). Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\exp(uX_{T})]=v(T,u),

where

is a solution to the following linear PDE of transport type

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}v(t,u)=R(u)\partial_{u}v(t,u),\quad v(0,u)=\exp(ux),\quad t\in[0,T].

Note that the advantage of the transport formulation is that

, which necessarily corresponds to an explosion of

In Section
3

T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
introduced below. They will then be applied to signature SDEs in Section

Polynomial case

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
denote by
\mathcal{P}_{k}:=\{x\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}x^{i}\colon u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
of polynomials up to degree
k
.
Moreover, for a coefficients’ vector

p(x,u):=\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}x^{i}

Note that for a polynomial process, the generator
\mathcal{A}
maps

\mathcal{P}_{k}
to
\mathcal{P}_{k}
, which is an alternative defining property of polynomial processes. Define a
dual polynomial operator
\mathcal{B}

acting on

as

\mathcal{B}(p(x,\cdot))(u):=\mathcal{A}(p(\cdot,u))(x),

for every
x\in S
, which explicitly reads

\mathcal{B}g(u)=(L_{k}u)^{\top}\nabla g(u)

for
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(p(\cdot,u))(x)=p(x,L_{k}u).

The corresponding
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
-valued (deterministic) dual process solves the linear ODE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c(t)=L_{k}c(t),\quad c(0)=u,\qquad t\in[0,T],

(2.4)

which describes the evolution of the coefficients vector.
Applying the duality method
(ii)
, thus yields the following theorem, which was initially proved in
Cuchiero et al. 2012
;
Filipović and Larsson 2016
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Fix
T>0
and let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a polynomial process. Denote by
c(t)=(c_{0}(t),\ldots,c_{k}(t))^{\top}
the solution of the linear ODE
(
2.4
).
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}X_{T}^{i}]=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}(T)x^{i}.

Remark 2.5
.

In this case (
2.2
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}X_{T}^{i}]=v(t,u),
where
v(t,u)

\partial_{t}v(t,u)={\mathcal{B}}v(t,u)=(L_{k}u)^{\top}\nabla_{u}v(t,u).

Inserting
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}X_{t}^{i}]
for

thus gives

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}u_{i}X_{t}^{i}]=\langle L_{k}(u),\mathbb{E}[(1,X_{t},\ldots,X_{t}^{k})^{\top}]\rangle

and in turn

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[(1,X_{t},\ldots,X_{t}^{k})^{\top}]=L_{k}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[(1,X_{t},\ldots,X_{t}^{k})^{\top}].

In the terminology of
Cuchiero and Svaluto-Ferro 2021
, this corresponds to the so-called bidual moment formula.

2.2
Signature of continuous semimartingales

Our approach how to pass from the narrow definition of one-dimensional affine and polynomial processes to a universal class is based on the signature process, which was first studied by
Chen 1957
;
Chen 1977
and plays a prominent role in
rough path theory
introduced in
Lyons 1998
(see also the monographs by
Friz and Victoir 2010
;
Friz and Hairer 2014
).

Following the lines of Section 2.1 in
Cuchiero et al. 2023
we start here by introducing some basic notions related to the signature of an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued continuous semimartingale. Similar introductions to the concept of signature can be found e.g. in Section 2.2. of
Bayer et al. 2023
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
consider the
n
-fold tensor product of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 0}:=\mathbb{R},\qquad(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}:=\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}}_{n}.

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the extended tensor algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

T((\mathbb{R}^{d})):=\{\textbf{a}:=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n},\dots):a_{n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\}.

Similarly we introduce the truncated tensor algebra of order
N\in\mathbb{N}

T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\textbf{a}:=(a_{0},\dots,a_{N},0,\dots):a_{n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\}.

and the tensor algebra

T(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\bigcup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
For
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
we set

\pi_{n}(\textbf{a}):=a_{n},

{\mathbf{a}}^{\leq N}:=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}),

\textbf{a}+\textbf{b}:=(a_{0}+b_{0},\dots,a_{n}+b_{n},\dots),

\lambda\textbf{a}:=(\lambda a_{0},\dots,\lambda a_{n},\dots),

\textbf{a}\otimes\textbf{b}:=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n},\dots),

where
c_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\otimes b_{n-k}
. Whenever needed, we identify
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{N})\in T({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},0,\ldots,0)\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
.
Observe that
(T((\mathbb{R}^{d})),+,\cdot,\otimes)
is a real algebra with
d
free generators
(0,e_{i},0,\ldots,0,\dots)
, where
e_{i}
denotes the canonical basis element of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The neutral element is identified via
(1,0,\dots,0,\dots)
. The tensor algebra
T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is a subalgebra and isomorphic to
the
real free algebra with
d
generators. Note that
T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an algebra as well, namely
the
real free
N
-nilpotent algebra. It appears as factor algebra of
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
by the ideal of elements with entries only after
N
. We shall need these algebraic structures to introduce signature properly.

For a multi-index
I:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in{\{1,\ldots,d\}}^{n}
we denote its length by
|I|:=n
. We also consider the empty index
I:=\emptyset
and set
|I|:=0
. For each multiindex
I
with
|I|>0
we define

I^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{|I|-1})&\text{if }|I|\geq 2,\\
\emptyset&\text{if }|I|=1,\end{cases}

(2.5)

which just leaves away the last letter. Similarly we set
I^{\prime\prime}:=(I^{\prime})^{\prime}
if
|I|>1
. Finally, we denote by
I^{ord}
the multi-index obtained by sorting the entries of
I
and define
I!:=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(I(k)!)

where
I(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{|I|}1_{\{i_{j}=k\}}
counts the number of times the letter
k
appears in multiindex
I
.
Next, for each
|I|\geq 1
we set

e_{I}:=e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}},

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
denote the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denoting by
e_{\emptyset}
the basis element corresponding to
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes 0}
, each element of
\textbf{a}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
can thus be written as formal series

\textbf{a}=\sum_{|I|\geq 0}\mathbf{a}_{I}e_{I},

for some
\mathbf{a}_{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Notice that convergence is not just formal here but componentwise (which is actually the standard locally convex structure on the sequence space) and whence properly defined – still we maintain the wording of formal series. To extract the
I
-th component from
a
, we shall either write
\textbf{a}_{I}
or
\langle\textbf{a},e_{I}\rangle
. For each
\textbf{a}\in T({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and each
\textbf{b}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
we set

\langle\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\rangle:=\sum_{|I|\geq 0}\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{I}.

Observe that the set
\{e_{I}\colon|I|=n\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}
with respect to this
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. In particular,
\mathbf{b}_{I}=\langle e_{I},\textbf{b}\rangle
.

Finally, we denote by
({\,\cdot\,})^{(1)}:T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to(T((\mathbb{R}^{d})))^{d}
and
({\,\cdot\,})^{(2)}:T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to(T((\mathbb{R}^{d})))^{d\times d}
the shifts given by

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\Big(\sum_{|I|\geq 1}\mathbf{u}_{I}e_{I^{\prime}}\Big)(1_{\{i_{|I|}=1\}},\ldots,1_{\{i_{|I|}=d\}})^{\top},

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\Big(\sum_{|I|\geq 2}\mathbf{u}_{I}e_{I^{\prime\prime}}\Big)\begin{pmatrix}1_{\{i_{|I|-1}=i_{|I|}=1\}}&\cdots&1_{\{i_{|I|-1}=1,i_{|I|}=d\}}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1_{\{i_{|I|-1}=d,i_{|I|}=1\}}&\cdots&1_{\{i_{|I|-1}=i_{|I|}=d\}}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, we define

\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u}^{(1)}\rangle:=\sum_{|I|\geq 1}\big(\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(I1)},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(Id)}\big)^{\top}\quad\text{ and }\quad\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u}^{(2)}\rangle:=\sum_{|I|\geq 2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(I11)}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(I1d)}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(Id1)}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{u}_{(Idd)}\end{pmatrix}

for each
\mathbf{a}\in T({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Here, we use the notation
Ik
for the multiindex
I
prolonged by
k
.

Throughout, the paper we consider a filtered probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}})
with a right continuous filtration. Whenever not specified, stochastic processes are always supposed to be defined thereon.
We are now ready to define the signature of an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued continuous semimartingale.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued semimartingale. Its
signature
is the
T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
-valued process
t\mapsto{\mathbb{X}}_{t}
whose components are recursively defined as

\langle e_{\emptyset},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle:=\textup{1},\qquad\langle e_{I},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle:=\int_{0}^{t}\langle e_{I^{\prime}},\mathbb{X}_{r}\rangle\circ\mathrm{d}X_{r}^{i_{|I|}},

for each
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
t\in[0,T]
, where
\circ
denotes the Stratonovich integral.
Its projection
{\mathbb{X}}^{\leq N}
on
T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

\mathbb{X}_{t}^{\leq N}=\sum_{|I|\leq N}\langle e_{I},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle e_{I}

and is called
signature of
X
truncated at level
N
.

Observe that the signature of
X
and the signature of
X-c
coincide for each
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and are given by iterated Stratonovich integrals

\langle e_{I},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle=\int_{0\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots t_{n}\leq t}\circ dX^{i_{1}}_{t_{1}}\cdots\circ dX^{i_{n}}_{t_{n}}\quad.

In order to describe the algebraic properties of the signature we need the shuffle product, which is nothing else than a concise expression of the classical integration by parts formula, which holds for Stratonovich integrals.

Definition 2.7
.

For every two multi-indices
I:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
J:=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})
the
shuffle product
is defined recursively as

\displaystyle e_{I}\shuffle e_{J}:=(e_{I^{\prime}}\shuffle e_{J})\otimes e_{i_{n}}+(e_{I}\shuffle e_{J^{\prime}})\otimes e_{j_{m}},

with
e_{I}\shuffle e_{\emptyset}:=e_{\emptyset}\shuffle e_{I}=e_{I}
. It extends to
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in T({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
as

\textbf{a}\shuffle\textbf{b}=\sum_{|I|,|J|\geq 0}\mathbf{a}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{J}(e_{I}\shuffle e_{J})

and to
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
as

\langle e_{I},\textbf{a}\shuffle\textbf{b}\rangle=\langle e_{I},\mathbf{a}^{\leq|I|}\shuffle\mathbf{b}^{\leq|I|}\rangle.

Remark 2.8
.

The shuffle product has many important properties:

(i)

(T({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),+,\shuffle)
is a commutative algebra, which in particular means that the shuffle product is associative and commutative.

(ii)

e_{I}\shuffle e_{J}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes(|I|+|J|)}
, which in particular implies that
e_{I}\shuffle e_{J}=\sum_{|H|=|I|+|J|}\lambda_{H}e_{H}
for some
\lambda_{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(iii)

For each multi-index
I
it holds

\sum_{J^{ord}=I^{ord}}e_{J}=\frac{1}{I!}(e_{i_{1}}\shuffle\ldots\shuffle e_{i_{|I|}})
.

(iv)

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued semimartingale and
I,J
be two multi-indices. Then

\langle e_{I},\mathbb{X}\rangle\langle e_{J},\mathbb{X}\rangle=\langle e_{I}\shuffle e_{J},\mathbb{X}\rangle\ a.s.
(see
Ree 1958
or
Lyons et al. 2007
).

(v)

Due to current notation we do not introduce co-products (whose adjoint the shuffle product appears to be) and group-like elements via them, but we can state the algebraic counterpart of Remark
(iv)
, namely

\langle\textbf{a},\mathbb{X}\rangle\langle\textbf{b},\mathbb{X}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}\shuffle\mathbf{b},\mathbb{X}\rangle

for
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and piecewise linear curves
X
(which are of course deterministic, continuous semimartingales).

In the following definition we introduce the notion of the exponential shuffle, similarly as in Definition 6.1 of
Bayer et al. 2023
. Note that in contrast to the definition there we consider it also for elements in the (complexified) extended tensor algebra and not only on
T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The complexification of the extended tensor algebra is denoted by
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))+\text{i}T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Note that we still apply
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as introduced above to both the real and imaginary entries.

Definition 2.9
.

For each
\mathbf{u}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))+\text{i}T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
we set
\overline{\mathbf{u}}:=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\emptyset}e_{\emptyset}
and consider the following quantities.

•

\mathbf{u}^{\shuffle 0}=e_{\emptyset}
,
\mathbf{u}^{\shuffle 2}:=\mathbf{u}\shuffle\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\shuffle k}:=\mathbf{u}^{\shuffle(k-1)}\shuffle\mathbf{u}
.

•

\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{u}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\shuffle k}}{k!}
. Since the right hand side can be written as
\exp(\mathbf{u}_{\emptyset})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\shuffle k}}{k!}
, we can see that every component of
\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{u})
corresponds to a finite sum.

•

\log(\shuffle\mathbf{u}):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!}{\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\shuffle k}}
, whenever
\mathbf{u}_{\emptyset}=1
.

Lemma 2.10
.

For
\mathbf{u}_{0}:=0
it holds
\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{u}_{0})=e_{\emptyset}
and for each
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
it holds

\displaystyle\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{u})\shuffle\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\exp(\shuffle(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})).

Moreover, for each
\mathbf{u}
such that
\mathbf{u}_{\emptyset}=1
it holds

\exp(\shuffle\log(\shuffle\mathbf{u}))=\mathbf{u}.

One of the most important properties of the signature states that every continuous functional on a compact set of continuous semimartingales’ trajectories can be approximated by a linear map of the time extended trajectory’s signature, evaluated at its final time. This universal approximation result is based on the properties of the so-called Lyons lift (see for instance Theorem 2.2.1 in
Lyons 1998
and Theorem 9.5 and Exercise 17.2 in
Friz and Victoir 2010
) and on the Stone-Weierstrass theorem. It uses the fact that two trajectories cannot share the same value of the time-extended signature at final time and that linear maps on the signature form an algebra. As this result is not used directly in this paper and stating it would require to specify the involved spaces and topologies we direct the reader to the following references
Kalsi et al. 2020
;
Bayer et al. 2023
;
Cuchiero et al. 2023
;
Cuchiero and Möller 2023
;
Cuchiero et al. 2025
.

3
Affine and polynomial processes on
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))

We introduce here the notion of affine or polynomial processes (which appear often
both
just as linear processes) taking values in a subset of the extended tensor algebra
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

3.1
State space and spaces of dual elements

The goal of this section is to define a vector space of dual elements needed for the definition of affine and polynomial processes. We shall first introduce vector spaces of linear functionals
\mathcal{S}\ni\mathbf{x}\mapsto\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle
restricted to some state space
\mathcal{S}
, which can be related in certain cases to power series. Sometimes these vector spaces already form a point-separating algebra, sometimes we have to form one, for instance by considering
\mathcal{S}\ni\mathbf{x}\mapsto\exp\big(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle\big)
: in both cases this will provide us with large enough sets of test functions for the martingale problem approach, which we use to define stochastic processes.

•

State space of the stochastic process:
\mathcal{S}\subseteq T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. The set
{\mathcal{S}}
is endowed with the sigma-algebra induced by the product sigma-algebra on
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

•

\Pi:=\{\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\Pi_{3},\ldots\}
denotes a partition of the set
\{I\colon|I|\geq 0\}
of all multi-indices. We assume that each
\Pi_{k}
just contains indices of the same length.

•

For each
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathbf{u}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))+\text{i}T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
we set

|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big|\sum_{I\in\Pi_{k}}{\mathbf{u}}_{I}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}\big|.

(3.1)

•

\mathcal{S}^{*}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))+\text{i}T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\colon|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}<\infty\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}\}
.

•

For
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
we set

\displaystyle\langle{\bf u},{\bf x}\rangle:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\langle\pi_{n}({\mathbf{u}}),\pi_{n}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle.

(3.2)

Remark 3.1
.

The choice of avoiding partitions with sets containing indices of different length guarantees that
the limit defining
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle
is well defined for all
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
. With a different order of summation,
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
could potentially be larger but the limit might no longer be well-defined.

Observe that
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
is a point-separating vector space. Point separation is already given by dual elements, where only finitely many components are non zero. Given
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
it holds
\lambda\mathbf{u}+\mu\mathbf{v}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and
\lambda\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\mu\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\langle\lambda\mathbf{u}+\mu\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle
for each
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

Finally, note that there is only one choice of partition for
d=1
.

Example 3.2
.

We discuss now some choices for
\Pi
.

(i)

\Pi:=\{\{I\}\colon|I|\geq 0\}
. In this case

|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{|I|\geq 0}|{\mathbf{u}}_{I}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}|.

If every entry of
\mathbf{x}
is nonzero
|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}
is a norm. As we shall see, this partition is however not shuffle-compatible in the sense of Definition
4.2
.

(ii)

\Pi:=\{\{I\colon I^{ord}=J\}\colon J=J^{ord}\}
. In this case

|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{I\colon I=I^{ord}}\Big|\sum_{J\colon J^{ord}=I}{\mathbf{u}}_{J}{\mathbf{x}}_{J}\Big|,

where
I^{ord}
is the ordered version of
I
.
The advantage of this semi-norm lies in the connection with power series explained in Lemma
4.3

(iv)
below.
In particular, this partition is the finest shuffle-compatible partition (see Definition
4.2
).

(iii)

\Pi:=\{\{I\colon|I|=n\}\colon n\geq 0\}
. In this case

|\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\langle\pi_{n}({\mathbf{u}}),\pi_{n}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle|.

This is the smallest semi-norm satisfying the necessary properties, thus the one corresponding to the largest
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
. This partition is also shuffle-compatible (see Definition
4.2
).

3.2
Martingale problems

Let
M({\mathcal{S}})
be the set of maps
f:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and fix a linear subset
D({\mathcal{L}})\subseteq M({\mathcal{S}})
. Consider a linear operator
\mathcal{L}:D(\mathcal{L})\to M({\mathcal{S}})
and a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
with right continuous filtration.
An
\mathcal{S}
-valued stochastic process
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is called a
solution to the martingale problem for
\mathcal{L}
if

(i)

\mathbb{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
for some initial value
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}
,

(ii)

there exists a version of
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
(f(\mathbb{X}_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}
and
({\mathcal{L}}f(\mathbb{X}_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}

are adapted càdlàg processes for every
f\in D({\mathcal{L}})

and

(iii)

the process
N^{f}
given by

N^{f}_{t}:=f(\mathbb{X}_{t})-f(\mathbb{X}_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}f(\mathbb{X}_{s})ds

(3.3)

defines a local martingale for every
f\in D({\mathcal{L}})
.

Note that we write
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
for a general
\mathcal{S}
-valued process which in the present context does not necessarily denote the signature process of some underlying process.

3.3
The affine case

We now use the martingale problem formulation to introduce affine processes on
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Fix
D({\mathcal{L}}):=\textup{span}\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)\colon\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}\}
for some subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and let
\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}:D({\mathcal{L}})\to M({\mathcal{S}})
be a linear operator.
We say that
{\mathcal{L}}
is
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-
affine type
if there exists
a map
R:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{S}^{*},\mathbf{u}\mapsto R(\mathbf{u})

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle)(\mathbf{x})=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}\rangle

(3.4)

for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Any solution
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of a martingale problem corresponding to an operator
{\mathcal{L}}
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine type is then called
\mathcal{S}
-valued
{\mathcal{U}}
-
affine process.

Remark 3.4
.

Observe that a solution to the
martingale problem is supposed to take values in
{\mathcal{S}}
and that there is no cemetery state which would allow for a killing. This assumptions has some useful consequences.
Suppose for a moment that
0\in{\mathcal{U}}
and thus that the map
f\equiv 1
belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
. Letting
({\mathbb{X}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the corresponding affine process with initial condition
\bf x
, the process
(\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{L}}f({\mathbb{X}}_{s})ds)_{t\in[0,T]}

is a local martingale of finite variation and thus a constant process.
Since
({\mathcal{L}}f({\mathbb{X}}_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}
is assumed to be càdlàg, we can conclude that

{\mathcal{L}}f({\bf x})=0
and hence that
\langle R(0),{\bf x}\rangle=0
for all
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
This motivates the assumption
R(0)=0
, whenever this is needed.

With these definitions at hand and certain integrability conditions we can now prove a first version of the affine transform formula in the present setting.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix
T>0
and let
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be an
\mathcal{S}
-valued
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine process with initial value
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and operator
\mathcal{L}
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine type as of Definition
3.3
.
Fix
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and suppose that the Riccati ODE

{\bm{\psi}}(t)=\mathbf{u}+\int_{0}^{t}R({\bm{\psi}}(s))ds,

(3.5)

admits an
\mathcal{U}
-valued solution
({\bm{\psi}}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
such that

\int_{0}^{T}|R(\bm{\psi}(s))|_{\mathbf{x}}ds<\infty

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover, assume that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]<\infty,\quad\text{ and}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle R({\bm{\psi}}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]<\infty.

Then, it holds

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{T}\rangle)]=\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(T),\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle).

Proof.

We verify the conditions of Lemma
A.1
for

X(s,t)=\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\quad\text{and}\quad Y(s,t)=\langle R({\bm{\psi}}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle).

As
({\mathbb{X}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is an
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine process, we have by definition that

M^{\mathbf{u}}_{t}=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)-\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},{\bf x}_{0}\rangle)-\int_{0}^{t}\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle)ds,

(3.7)

is a local martingale.
Using that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|M^{\mathbf{u}}_{t}|]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]+T\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]+T\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle R({\bm{\psi}}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)|]<\infty

holds by (
3.6
), we can conclude that
(M^{\mathbf{u}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a càdlàg martingale.
Furthermore, since
(\bm{\psi}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
solves the Riccati ODE (
3.5
) componentwise we have that

\langle{\bm{\psi}}(t),\mathbf{x}^{\leq N}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}^{\leq N}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle R({\bm{\psi}}(s)),\mathbf{x}^{\leq N}\rangle ds=0,

for each
N
. The dominated convergence theorem with the bound
|R(\bm{\psi}(s))|_{\mathbf{x}}
yields then

\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(t),\mathbf{x}\rangle)-\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-\int_{0}^{t}\langle R({\bm{\psi}}(s)),\mathbf{x}\rangle\exp(\langle{\bm{\psi}}(s),\mathbf{x}\rangle)ds=0,

which is thus a constant martingale for each
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Due to (
3.6
) all the assumptions of Lemma
A.1
are satisfied and the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

An inspection of the proof and Lemma

shows that (

(i)

Next, we show that

Corollary 3.7
.

Fix

Proof.

Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem

As in the one dimensional case we can now define the dual operator for affine processes.

Remark 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an operator of
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine type. The
dual
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine operator
is the linear operator
{\mathcal{B}}

acting on
D({\mathcal{B}}):=\textup{span}\{g:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\colon g({\bf u})=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{S}}}\}
such that

\mathcal{B}\exp(\langle{\,\cdot\,},\mathbf{x}\rangle)({\bf u)}:=\mathcal{L}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle)({\bf x}),\quad\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

Clearly

\mathcal{B}\exp(\langle{\,\cdot\,},\mathbf{x}\rangle)({\bf u})=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}\rangle
for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
To show that

\mathcal{B}
can – similarly as in the one dimensional situation – be interpreted as the restriction of a transport operator,
suppose that for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a solution
(\bm{\psi}(t))_{t\in[0,{\varepsilon}]}

to the Riccati ODE (
3.5
), such that
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|R(\bm{\psi}(s))|_{\mathbf{x}}ds<\infty
.
Then the linear operator
{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies

\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{|g({\bm{\psi}}({\varepsilon}))-g({\bf u})-{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{B}}g({\bf u})|}{{\varepsilon}}=\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{|g({\bf u}+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}R({\bm{\psi}}(s))ds)-g({\bf u})-{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{B}}g({\bf u})|}{{\varepsilon}}=0,

for each
g\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{B}})
.
This shows that
{\mathcal{B}}g({\bf u})
can be seen as the directional derivative of
g
at
{\bf u}
in direction
R({\bf u})
.

In order to get formulations that do not depend on the existence of the Riccati ODEs, we introduce the following property for
\mathcal{U}
allowing for componentwise differentiability.

Definition 3.9
.

We say that the set
{\mathcal{U}}
is componentwise-semi-open if for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
|I|\geq 0
the set
{\mathcal{U}}_{I,{\bf u}}:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}\colon{\bf u}+ye_{I}\in{\mathcal{U}}\}
is semi-open
3
3

3

A set
A
is semi-open if there is an open set
O
such that
O\subseteq A\subseteq\overline{O}
where
\overline{O}
denotes the closure of
O
.
. For a componentwise-semi-open set
{\mathcal{U}}
we say that a map
v:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is componentwise-differentiable at
{\bf u}
if each function
v_{I,{\bf u}}:{\mathcal{U}}_{I,{\bf u}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by
v_{I,{\bf u}}(y):=v({\bf u}+ye_{I})
is differentiable at
y=0
(if 0 lies in the interior of
{\mathcal{U}}_{I,{\bf u}}
) or if it is one-side differentiable at
y=0
(if 0 lies in the boundary of
{\mathcal{U}}_{I,{\bf u}}
). In this case we write

\nabla_{{\bf u}}v({\bf u}):=\sum_{|I|\geq 0}v_{I,{\bf u}}^{\prime}(0)e_{I}
.

Remark 3.10
.

For a componentwise-semi-open set
\mathcal{U}
, the dual
\mathcal{U}
-affine operator can therefore be written as

\mathcal{B}g({\bf u})=\langle R({\bf u}),\nabla_{{\bf u}}g({\bf u})\rangle

for
g\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{B}})
. As the right hand side makes sense for all componentwise-differentiable functions,
{\mathcal{B}}
can be extended to these functions.

The following theorem provides now a second formulation of the affine transform formula, namely a classical transport version, under slightly different assumptions. Note that in contrast to Corollary
3.7
, we here do not need to assume the existence of a solution to the Riccati ODE.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be an
\mathcal{S}
-valued
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine process for some componentwise-semi-open set
{\mathcal{U}}
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}
be its initial value and
\mathcal{L}
the corresponding operator of
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine type.
Set

r(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}):=\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Big|\sum_{|I|\leq N}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}\langle e_{I},R(\mathbf{u})\rangle\Big|,\qquad{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}},{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}.

Fix
T>0
and suppose that for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
|I|\geq 0
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}r(\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t})|\exp(\langle{\bf u},{{\mathbb{X}}_{t}}\rangle)|]

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{and}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}(1+|\langle e_{I},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle|)|\exp(\langle{\bf u},{{\mathbb{X}}_{t}}\rangle)|]

\displaystyle<\infty.

(3.10)

Then

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{T}\rangle)]=v(T,\mathbf{u}),
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
,
where
v(t,\mathbf{u})
is a solution to the following transport equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{B}v(t,\mathbf{u})=\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\nabla_{\bf u}v(t,\mathbf{u})\rangle,\quad v(0,{\bf u})=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle),\quad t\in[0,T].

(3.11)

Proof.

Fix
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
. We know from the definition of an affine process that the process
M^{\mathbf{u}}
given by

dM^{\mathbf{u}}_{t}=d\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)-\mathcal{L}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)dt,\qquad M^{\mathbf{u}}_{0}=0,

is a local martingale. Since conditions (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) imply condition (
3.6
), we can proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
to conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|M^{\mathbf{u}}_{t}|]<\infty

and thus that
M^{\bf u}
is a true martingale.
Therefore and due to Fubini’s-Tonelli’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)\big]

\displaystyle=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle)\Big]ds

\displaystyle=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{|I|\leq N}\langle e_{I},R(\mathbf{u})\rangle\langle e_{I},\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle\Big)\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle)\Big]ds

\displaystyle=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle)+\int_{0}^{t}\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{X}_{s}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{s}\rangle)\Big]\rangle ds,

(3.12)

where the last equality follows from dominated convergence with (
3.9
).
This justifies also the application of Fubini-Tonelli’s theorem.
We now claim that the map

t\mapsto\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{X}_{t}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]\rangle=\mathbb{E}[\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]

is continuous. Let
t_{n}\to t
. Then, using (
3.9
) again we have by dominated convergence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle)]=\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle)].

From the càdlàg assumption of
(\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)_{t\in[0,T]}
and
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle_{t\in[0,T]}
, it follows that

\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle)
converges to

\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)
if
t_{n}\downarrow t
or to

\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t-}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t-}\rangle)
if
if
t_{n}\uparrow t
. Since they are
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. equal, we can conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t_{n}}\rangle)]=\mathbb{E}[\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)],

whence the asserted continuity follows. This together with (
3.12
) implies

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]=\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{X}_{t}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]\rangle.

Since
\mathbf{u}\mapsto\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
is componentwise differentiable and
\mathbf{x}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)=\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
,
it remains to argue that we can interchange expectation and differentiation, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bf u}\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]=\nabla_{\bf u}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)].

Indeed, consider the partial derivative with respect to the component
\mathbf{u}_{I}
of
\mathbf{u}
corresponding to the multi-index
I
and observe that for each
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathcal{U}}
with
\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{v}_{I}-\mathbf{u}_{I})e_{I}
,
|\mathbf{v}_{I}-\mathbf{u}_{I}|\leq{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}

|\partial_{\mathbf{u}_{I}}\exp(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle)|\leq|\langle e_{I},\mathbf{x}\rangle||\exp(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle)|\leq|\langle e_{I},\mathbf{x}\rangle|(|\exp(\langle\mathbf{u}+{\varepsilon}e_{I},\mathbf{x}\rangle)|+|\exp(\langle\mathbf{u}-{\varepsilon}e_{I},\mathbf{x}\rangle)|).

By (
3.10
) and the Leibniz integral rule we can conclude that

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]=\langle R(\mathbf{u}),\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]\rangle,

proving that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle)]
is a solution to (
3.11
), which exists globally. Note that the equality with the dual operator follows from Remark
3.10
. This proves the the assertion.
∎

3.4
The polynomial case

In analogy to affine processes, polynomial processes are defined as follows.

Definition 3.12
.

Fix
D({\mathcal{L}}):=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle\colon\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}\}
for some linear subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
and let
\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}:D({\mathcal{L}})\to M({\mathcal{S}})
be a linear operator.
We say that
{\mathcal{L}}
is
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial type
if there exists
a map
L:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{S}^{*},\mathbf{u}\mapsto L(\mathbf{u})

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\langle\mathbf{u},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle)(\mathbf{x})=\langle L(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}\rangle

(3.13)

for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Any solution
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of a martingale problem corresponding
to an operator
\mathcal{L}
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial type is called
\mathcal{S}
-valued
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial process
.

Remark 3.13
.

(i)

Observe that in the literature of polynomial processes operators of polynomial type are typically called
polynomial operators
and the operator
L
appearing in Definition
3.12
is called
1st-dual operator
(see e.g.
Cuchiero and Svaluto-Ferro 2021
).
We here opted for the notion “operator of
polynomial type
”
to be consistent with the terminology used for affine processes. Since in the present paper we are interested in spaces where higher order polynomials can be written as linear functions (see for instance Lemma
4.3
), we do not need to define higher order dual operators.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{L}
is an operator of
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial type, then we can also associate a dual operator. Indeed, the

dual
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial operator
is the linear operator
{\mathcal{B}}

acting on
D({\mathcal{B}}):=\{g:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\colon g({\bf u})=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle
with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{S}}}\}
such that

\mathcal{B}(\langle{\,\cdot\,},\mathbf{x}\rangle)({\bf u)}:=\mathcal{L}(\langle\mathbf{u},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle)({\bf x}),\quad\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

Thus
\mathcal{B}(\langle{\,\cdot\,},\mathbf{x}\rangle)({\bf u})=\langle L(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}\rangle
for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
. It can again be interpreted as the restriction of a transport operator which acts on componentwise-differentiable functions
g
defined on componentwise-semi-open sets
{\mathcal{U}}
via

\mathcal{B}g(\mathbf{u})=\langle L(\mathbf{u}),\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}g(\mathbf{u})\rangle.

In the following proposition we establish a relation between affine and polynomial processes. We will see (Proposition
4.6
), that for particular state spaces affine and polynomial processes coincide.
For general state spaces
\mathcal{S}\subseteq T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
this does not need to be the case.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be an
\mathcal{S}
-valued continuous
{\mathcal{U}}
-affine process
with
R:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
as defined in (
3.4
). Assume that for each
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
there is some
\lambda\notin\{0,1\}
such that
\lambda{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Then
{\mathbb{X}}
is an
\mathcal{S}
-valued
\textup{span}({\mathcal{U}})
-polynomial process
with
L|_{\mathcal{U}}:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
given by

L({\bf u}):=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}R({\bf u})-\frac{1}{\lambda(\lambda-1)}R(\lambda{\bf u}).

Proof.

Define
D({\mathcal{L}}):=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle\colon\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}\}
,
{\mathcal{L}}:D({\mathcal{L}})\to M({\mathcal{S}})
as

\mathcal{L}(\langle\mathbf{u},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle)(\mathbf{x})=\langle L(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}\rangle,

and note that it is of
\textup{span}({\mathcal{U}})
-polynomial type. To prove that
{\mathbb{X}}
is a
\textup{span}({\mathcal{U}})
-polynomial process we verify that it solves the martingale problem for
{\mathcal{L}}
. Properties
(i)
and
(ii)
are clear. For property
(iii)
, note that since the process
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
given by
Y_{t}:=\exp(\langle{\bf u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)
is continuous, an application of the Itô formula yields

dY_{t}^{\lambda}=\lambda Y_{t}^{\lambda-1}dY_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)Y_{t}^{\lambda-2}d[Y]_{t}.

On the other hand, since
{\bf u},\lambda{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
by definition of martingale problem we also have that

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp(\langle\mathbf{u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\langle R(\mathbf{u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle dt+d(\text{local martingale}),

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\exp(\langle\lambda\mathbf{u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\langle R(\lambda\mathbf{u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle dt+d(\text{local martingale}).

We can thus conclude that

\displaystyle d[Y]_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\lambda(\lambda-1)Y^{\lambda-2}_{t}}\big(\exp(\langle\lambda{\bf u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\langle R(\lambda{\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle-\lambda Y_{t}^{\lambda-1}\exp(\langle{\bf u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\langle R({\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle\big)dt

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2Y_{t}^{2}}{\lambda(\lambda-1)}\langle R(\lambda{\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle-\frac{2Y_{t}^{2}}{(\lambda-1)}\langle R({\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle\Big)dt.

An application of Itô’s formula yields

d\log(Y_{t})=\frac{1}{Y_{t}}\exp(\langle{\bf u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle)\langle R({\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle dt-\frac{1}{2Y_{t}^{2}}d[Y]_{t}+d\text{(local martingale)},

which can be rewritten as

\langle{\bf u},{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle-\langle{\bf u},{\bf x}_{0}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle L({\bf u}),{\mathbb{X}}_{s}\rangle ds=\text{(local martingale)}.

∎

The analog of the affine transform formula for polynomial processes is now given by the following moment formula.

Theorem 3.15
.

Fix
T>0
and let
(\mathbb{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be an
\mathcal{S}
-valued
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial process with initial value
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and operator
\mathcal{L}
of
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial type as of Definition
3.12
.
Fix
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and suppose that the extended tensor algebra valued linear ODE

\mathbf{c}(t)=\mathbf{u}+\int_{0}^{t}L(\mathbf{c}(s))ds

(3.14)

admits an
\mathcal{U}
-valued solution
({\bf c}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}

such that
\int_{0}^{T}|L({\bf c}(t))|_{\mathbf{x}}dt<\infty

for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{S}
.
If

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle\mathbf{c}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle L(\mathbf{c}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]<\infty,

(3.15)

then it holds that

\mathbb{E}[\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{T}\rangle]=\langle\mathbf{c}(T),\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle.

Proof.

We verify the conditions of Lemma
A.1
for

X(s,t)=\langle{\bf c}(s),{\mathbb{X}}_{t}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad Y(s,t)=\langle L({\bf c}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle.

By definition of
{\mathcal{U}}
-polynomial processes, the process
(N_{t}^{\mathbf{u}})_{t\in[0,T]}
given by

dN_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}:=d\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle-\langle L(\mathbf{u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle dt,\qquad N_{0}^{\mathbf{u}}=0,

(3.16)

is a local martingale.
Using that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|N^{\bf u}_{t}|]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|\langle{\bf u},\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]+T\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}|\langle L({\bf u}),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle{\bf c}(s),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]+T\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s,t\leq T}|\langle L({\bf c}(s)),\mathbb{X}_{t}\rangle|]<\infty

holds by (
3.15
), we can conclude that
(N^{{\bf u}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a true martingale for all
{\bf u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
.
Furthermore, for each
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{S}}
by (
3.14
) and
\int_{0}^{T}|L({\bf c}(t))|_{\mathbf{x}}dt<\infty
we have

\langle\mathbf{c}(t),\mathbf{x}\rangle-\langle{\bf u},\mathbf{x}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle L(\mathbf{c}(s)),\mathbf{x}\rangle ds=0,

which is thus a constant martingale.
Due to (
3.15
) all the assumptions of Lemma
A.1
are thus satisfied and the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

Also in this case an inspection of the proof and Lemma
A.1
shows that (
3.15
) can be replaced by the following two properties.

(i)

The process
(N_{t}^{{\bf u}})_{t\in[0,T]}

(ii)

4
Signature SDEs and their signature process

We introduce signature SDEs as (Itô) stochastic processes with characteristics depending in a kind of entire way (made precise below) on its signature components. The first two subsections are devoted to a detailed analysis of the state space of signature processes and functions thereon.
Then we are led to our main results: such processes appear as linear projections of affine or polynomial processes taking values in subsets of so-called group-like elements. This class of processes is large (and to some extent universal) on the one hand, on the other hand affine or polynomial processes allow for very explicit descriptions of their laws in terms of Fourier-Laplace transforms.

4.1
State space of signature processes, shuffle-compatible spaces of dual elements and entire functions

Fix some subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We start by introducing the state space of the signature process (see Definition
2.6
) of a stochastic process
X
satisfying
X_{t}-X_{0}\in S
for each
t\in[0,T]
.

Definition 4.1
.

For a given subset
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(S)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{x}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))\colon\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\in S,\ {\mathbf{x}}_{\emptyset}=1,\text{ and }{\mathbf{x}}_{I}{\mathbf{x}}_{J}=\langle e_{I}\shuffle e_{J},\mathbf{x}\rangle\ \quad\forall|I|,|J|\geq 0\}.

The set
{\mathcal{S}}(S)
corresponds to the set of the so-called group-like elements of
T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
whose first level lies in
S
. More precisely, define the commutator via
[e_{i},e_{j}]:=e_{i}\otimes e_{j}-e_{j}\otimes e_{i}

(which extends to all elements
\mathbf{a}\in T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathbf{a}_{\emptyset}=0
) and consider

g^{N}:=0\oplus S\oplus[\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}]\oplus\cdots\oplus\underbrace{[\mathbb{R}^{d},[\ldots,[\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}]]]}_{N-1\text{ brackets}}\subseteq T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Denoting by
G^{N}:=\{\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\otimes k}}{k!}\colon\mathbf{u}\in g^{N}\}
its exponential image truncated at level
N
we get

{\mathcal{S}}(S)=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d})):\mathbf{x}^{\leq N}\in G^{N},\ \pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\in S\}.

In the current setting we shall choose the semi-norms defined in (
3.1
) such that if
I\in\Pi_{k}
then
\sigma(I)\in\Pi_{k}
for each permutation
\sigma
.
Under this assumption the set
{\mathcal{S}}(S)^{*}
is indeed closed with respect to the shuffle product (see Lemma

).
Recall that

\Pi=\{\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\Pi_{3},\ldots\}
denotes a partition of the set
\{I\colon|I|\geq 0\}
and all
\Pi_{k}
just contain indices of the same length.

Definition 4.2
.

We say that a partition
\Pi
of multi-indices is

if for each

there is a

such that for each

we can write

e_{I_{1}}\shuffle e_{I_{2}}=\sum_{I\in\Pi_{k}}\lambda_{I}e_{I}

for some
\lambda_{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In this case we write
k=k_{1}\shuffle k_{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

The following results hold.

(i)

If the process

takes value in

takes value in

(ii)

, and

for each
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}(S)
.

(iii)

Suppose that
\Pi
is shuffle-compatible.
Then given
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{S}}(S)^{*}

for each

.

(iv)

Suppose that
\Pi
is given by Example
3.2
(ii)
and

\mathbf{u}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))+\text{i}T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))

for every

converges absolutely for each

it holds

(v)

Suppose that
\Pi
is shuffle-compatible,
\mathbf{u}
satisfies (

\mathbf{u}_{\infty}:=\sup_{I}|\mathbf{u}_{I}|<\infty

\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{S}}(S)^{*}

and

|\mathbf{x}|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\exp(|\mathbf{x}_{(1)}|+\cdots+|\mathbf{x}_{(d)}|).

Proof.

(i)
follows directly from Remark

(iv)
.
For
(ii)

observe that

\displaystyle\langle e_{J},\mathbf{u}\shuffle\mathbf{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J_{1}|,|J_{2}|\geq 0}{\mathbf{u}}_{J_{1}}{\mathbf{v}}_{J_{2}}\langle e_{J},e_{J_{1}}\shuffle e_{J_{2}}\rangle,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{J_{1}}{\mathbf{x}}_{J_{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{J_{1}}\shuffle e_{J_{2}},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\sum_{|J|\geq 0}{\mathbf{x}}_{J}\langle e_{J_{1}}\shuffle e_{J_{2}},e_{J}\rangle.

For fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\Pi_{k}}\langle e_{I},\mathbf{u}\shuffle\mathbf{v}\rangle{\mathbf{x}}_{I}

\displaystyle|\mathbf{u}\shuffle\mathbf{v}|_{\mathbf{x}}

For
(iii)

Concerning

4.2.3

where sum and derivation can be exchanged since the sum has finitely many nonzero elements.
This in particular yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}),e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\langle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}^{\shuffle k}}{k!},e_{I}\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}\langle\frac{\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}^{\shuffle k}}{k!},e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle\frac{\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}^{\shuffle(k-1)}}{(k-1)!}\shuffle\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))},e_{I}\rangle=\langle\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)})\shuffle\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))},e_{I}\rangle,

where again we use that the appearing sum has finitely many nonzero elements. Since

\langle\exp(\shuffle\bm{\psi}(t)),e_{I}\rangle=\langle\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}),e_{I}\rangle\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})

and

\partial_{t}\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})=\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})(R(\bm{\psi}(t)))_{\emptyset}

we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\exp(\shuffle\bm{\psi}(t)),e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)})\shuffle\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))},e_{I}\rangle\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})+\langle\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}),e_{I}\rangle\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})(R(\bm{\psi}(t)))_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle=\langle\exp(\shuffle\bm{\psi}(t))\shuffle(\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))}+(R(\bm{\psi}(t)))_{\emptyset}e_{\emptyset}),e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\exp(\shuffle\bm{\psi}(t))\shuffle R(\bm{\psi}(t)),e_{I}\rangle.

The result now follows directly from Theorem
4.13
and Lemma
4.17
.
∎

Finally, we explain how to move from the moment formula to the generalized affine-transform formula provided in the last theorem.

Theorem 4.19
.

Fix
\mathbf{u}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))+iT(({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
and suppose that
({\bf c}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.13
for
{\bf c}(0)=\exp(\shuffle\mathbf{u})
. Assume that
{\bf c}(t)_{\emptyset}\neq 0

for each
t\in[0,T]
and set

\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset}=\mathbf{u}_{\emptyset}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{L{\bf c}(s)_{\emptyset}}{{\bf c}(s)_{\emptyset}}ds

and

\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)}:=\log(\shuffle{\bf d}(t))
for
{\bf d}(t):={{\bf c}(t)}/{{\bf c}(t)_{\emptyset}}
.
Then
\bm{\psi}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.18
.

Proof.

First observe that

\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})={\bf c}(t)_{\emptyset}
and Lemma
2.10
yields

\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)})={\bf d}(t).

This implies that

\exp(\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset})\exp(\shuffle\overline{\bm{\psi}(t)})={\bf c}(t)

(4.12)

which together with Lemma
4.17

yield

\bm{\psi}(t)_{\emptyset}=\bm{\psi}(0)_{\emptyset}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\exp(\bm{\psi}(s)_{\emptyset})R(\bm{\psi}(s))_{\emptyset}}{\exp(\bm{\psi}(s)_{\emptyset})}ds=\bm{\psi}(0)_{\emptyset}+\int_{0}^{t}R(\bm{\psi}(s))_{\emptyset}ds.

Finally, proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
4.18
, for each
|I|>0
we can conclude that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}(t)_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\partial_{t}\langle\overline{{\bf d}(t)}^{\shuffle k},e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{k+1}}\langle\overline{{\bf d}(t)}^{\shuffle(k-1)}\shuffle\Big(-\overline{{\bf d}(t)}R(\bm{\psi}(t))_{\emptyset}+\overline{{\bf d}(t)\shuffle R(\bm{\psi}(t))}\Big),e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{k+1}}\overline{{\bf d}(t)}^{\shuffle(k-1)}\shuffle\Big((e_{\emptyset}+\overline{{\bf d}(t)})\shuffle\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))}\Big),e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{(-1)^{k}}\overline{{\bf d}(t)}^{\shuffle k}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{k}}\overline{{\bf d}(t)}^{\shuffle k}\Big)\shuffle\overline{R(\bm{\psi}(t))},e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=R(\bm{\psi}(t))_{I}.

The claim now follows by Lemma
4.17
and (
4.12
).
∎

5
Power sequences of one dimensional diffusions with real-analytic characteristics

In this section we consider the case

and a process

given as the sequence of powers of some stochastic process

\displaystyle{\mathbb{X}}=(1,X,X^{2},\ldots,X^{n},\ldots).

(5.1)

In order to simplify the notation for

we set

Fix

and consider a one-dimensional diffusion process

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{a(X_{t})}dB_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

and
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{a}\in T((\mathbb{R}))

({\bf u}\star{\bf v})_{n}:=\sum_{n_{1}+n_{2}=n}{\bf u}_{n_{1}}{\bf v}_{n_{2}},\qquad{\bf u},{\bf v}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}))

Finally, we often consider the component-wise open set

and for each

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{[1]}

\displaystyle:=(\mathbf{u}_{1},2\mathbf{u}_{2},3\mathbf{u}_{3},\ldots,k\mathbf{u}_{k},\ldots)

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{[2]}

\displaystyle:=(2\mathbf{u}_{2},6\mathbf{u}_{3},12\mathbf{u}_{4},\ldots,k(k-1)\mathbf{u}_{k},\ldots).

Observe that these elements of the extended tensor algebra correspond to

and

when the shuffle product is replaced by the discrete convolution.

Remark 5.1
.

By the properties of power series in their convergence domain we have that
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}(S)^{*}
. Moreover, for each
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{V}}
the map

is smooth on

, both

and

Note also that it can be of interest to consider power series expansions around points different than 0. The whole theory of this section can then be applied considering the shifted process

for some

. This is for instance used to apply the results of Theorem

to the shifted Jacobi diffusion in Section

Finally, note that
the processes analyzed in this section do not correspond to signatures of one dimensional processes, which would take values in

{\mathcal{S}}(S-x_{0})=\{\mathbf{x}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}))\colon{\bf x}=(1,x-x_{0},\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{2},\frac{(x-x_{0})^{3}}{3!},\ldots),\ x\in S\}.

Moreover, since property (
4.1

, for each

and

With this parametrization, the assertions of Remark

read

and
g^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u}}=g_{\mathbf{u}^{(2)}}.

5.3
Concrete specifications and a “counterexample”

This section is dedicated to consider concrete specifications, where we obtain
existence of the Riccati and linear ODEs of
Theorem
5.2

and Theorem
5.5
, respectively. We start with Brownian motion, consider then affine processes and finally a rich specification of SDEs on compact state spaces, in general beyond the polynomial class, but including also Jacobi-type diffusions. Each of these specifications comes with a class of entire functions for which the moment formula and the affine transform formula hold true.

We conclude this section with an example of an SDE with entire drift and diffusion coefficients, for which
x\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{x}[X_{t}]
is not real analytic. This means that the moment formula and the affine transform
formula can only hold asymptotically as outlined in the introduction. Nevertheless, we can find a dense class of functions for which we can prove their exact validity.

Throughout this section we work here in the one-dimensional setup of Section
5
and consider the power sequence expansion, denoted by

\mathbb{X}_{t}^{pow}:=(1,X_{t},X_{t}^{2},\ldots)
.

5.3.1
Brownian motion

Let
X
be a one-dimensional Brownian motion (starting at

) and denote by

its generator

{\mathcal{A}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
.
Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the set of entire functions

{\mathcal{D}}:=\{f\in{\mathcal{B}}\colon f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}\}.

Consider the semigroup

given by

for

.
For this class of function we get the following results whose proof can be found in Appendix

.

Lemma 5.7
.

The following holds.

(i)

to itself for each

(ii)

(iii)

, and

.

(iv)

For each

is continuously differentiable and it holds

Observe that the Riccati operator and the linear operator in the Brownian case are

\displaystyle R^{pow}(\mathbf{u})_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big((k+1)(k+2)\mathbf{u}_{k+2}+\sum_{i+j=k}(i+1)(j+1)\mathbf{u}_{i+1}\mathbf{u}_{j+1}\Big),

\displaystyle L^{pow}(\mathbf{u})_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(k+1)(k+2)\mathbf{u}_{k+2}.

4

Proposition 5.8
.

Fix

and

.

5.3.2
Affine processes

In the case of affine processes we can identify another family of entire functions for which we get existence of the Riccati and linear ODEs. Similarly as in the Brownian case, we provide this for the power sequence expansion, but a similar result also holds for the signature expansion.

The next proposition
is an adaptation of Proposition 3.33
in
Cuchiero et al. 2024c

Proposition 5.11
.

Let

\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle:=\{h\in\mathcal{E}\colon h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\},\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{U}}^{pow}=\{\mathbf{u}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}))\colon h_{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{D}}\}.

(5.17)

Fix

Remark 5.12

As in the case of Brownian motion

Proof.

5.3.3
Beyond polynomial processes

This section is dedicated to specify a class of SDEs of the form (

such that
a solution to the linear ODE (

x\in S
.
We here work again with power sequence expansion and consider operators
\mathcal{A}
which act on

as

\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k}(\cdot)^{k})(x)=\mathcal{G}(\lambda_{\cdot}x^{(\cdot)})(k),\quad x\in S,\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

for some operator

{\mathcal{G}}
acting on functions

as

\mathcal{G}f(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{kj}(f(j)-f(k))+\beta_{k}f(k).

Assuming that
\beta_{kj}\geq 0
and
\beta_{k}\leq 0
, we can see that
{\mathcal{G}}
corresponds to the generator of a jump process
Z
with state space
\mathbb{N}_{0}
that jumps from
k
to
j
with intensity
\beta_{kj}
and can be killed with rate
\beta_{k}
depending on its position
k
.
The process
Z
is thus dual to the original process
X
and under appropriate integrability conditions (see Theorem 4.11 in
Ethier and Kurtz 2005
) we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Z_{0}}X_{T}^{Z_{0}}]=\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Z_{T}}x_{0}^{Z_{T}}].

(5.18)

Set then
\mathbf{\mu}_{k}(t):=\mathbb{P}[Z_{t}=k]
,
\mathbf{u}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}(0)
, and note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Z_{0}}X_{T}^{Z_{0}}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Z_{0}}X_{T}^{Z_{0}}|Z_{0}=k]P(Z_{0}=k)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}(0)\mathbb{E}[X_{T}^{k}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{u}_{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{T}^{k}]

and similarly

\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Z_{T}}x_{0}^{Z_{T}}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}(T)x_{0}^{k}.

By (
5.18
) we therefore have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{u}_{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{T}^{k}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}(T)x_{0}^{k}.

If the sum and the expected value can be interchanged this then implies that

\mathbf{c}_{k}(T)=\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}(T).

Since
\mu(t)
solves the Fokker Planck equation

\partial_{t}\mu(t)=\mathcal{G}^{*}\mu(t),

which reduces to infinite dimensional linear ODE in this case, we can identify
L
with
\mathcal{G}^{*}
.

Let again
{\mathcal{V}}
be as defined in (
5.4
) and in order to simplify the notation set

{\mathcal{R}}_{C}:=\{{\bf u}\in T(({\mathbb{R}}))\colon{\bf u}_{k}\geq 0,\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\bf u}_{k}\leq C\},\qquad{\mathcal{R}}:=\bigcup_{C>0}{\mathcal{R}}_{C}.

(5.19)

Theorem 5.14
.

Fix

,
\lambda\in T(({\mathbb{R}}))
, and

such that

.
Assume that

{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda_{k}({\,\cdot\,})^{k})(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{kj}(\lambda_{j}x^{j}-\lambda_{k}x^{k})+\beta_{k}\lambda_{k}x^{k}

Proof.

Without loss of generality assume that

The shifted Jacobi diffusion

5.3.4
An example underlying the importance of the technical conditions

It could be tempting to imagine that,
if the coefficients
b
and
a
of (
5.2
) are entire maps which are bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, the corresponding solution of the backward Kolmogorov equation
with sufficiently regular initial functions
depends in a real analytic way on the initial state.
The next example (derived from
Ishiwata and Taniguchi 1999
) illustrates that this is actually not the case even though the corresponding stochastic flows are real analytic.

Fix
d=1
and
S={\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
X_{t}^{x_{0}}
be a process given by (
5.2
) for
b(x)=\cos(2x)\sin(2x)
and
a(x)=\sin^{2}(2x)
and initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}
. The vector fields are obviously entire functions and globally bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. The solution of this SDE is given by
X_{t}^{x_{0}}=x_{0}
for each
X_{0}=x_{0}=k\pi/2
and

X_{t}^{x_{0}}=\arctan(\exp(2B_{t})\tan(x_{0}))+k\pi

for all
x_{0}\in(-\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi,\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi)
. For all
t>0
the map
x_{0}\mapsto X_{t}^{x_{0}}
is almost surely not entire, but real analytic with a positive radius of convergence depending on the Brownian path
B
. The expectation of this quantity is, however, not real analytic at
x_{0}=0
(the radius of convergence equals to
0
), since otherwise also its derivative would be real analytic, which is the expectation of

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}X_{t}^{x_{0}}=\frac{\exp(2B_{t})}{1+(\exp(4B_{t})-1)\sin^{2}(x_{0})}\,\text{ for }t>0\text{ and }x_{0}\in(-\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi,\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi).

Indeed the functional series, which represents the flow’s derivative for small
x_{0}

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\exp(2B_{t}){(-1)}^{i}{\big(\exp(4B_{t})-1\big)}^{i}\sin^{2i}(x_{0})

is absolutely minorized by

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\exp(2B_{t}){(-1)}^{i}{(\exp(4B_{t})-1)}^{i}|{(\lambda x_{0})}^{2i}

for some
0<\lambda<1
and
x_{0}
in an interval around
0
such that
\lambda x_{0}\leq\sin(x_{0})
. The expectation of the minorizing series, however, has a radius of convergence
0
, since the highest order term diverges to infinity like
\exp(8ti)
as
i\to\infty
.

Even though real analyticity of the expected value fails for the identity map, there is a real-analytic family of functions on
(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
(and similarly for every shift of
k\pi
), dense in
C(K)
for compact sets
K\subset(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
where it works. Indeed,

\text{span}\{x\mapsto\tan^{j}(x)\,|\,j=0,1,\ldots\}

is by the Stone–Weierstrass Theorem dense in
C(K)
and since

\mathbb{E}[\tan^{j}(X_{t}^{x_{0}})]=\tan^{j}(x_{0})\mathbb{E}[\exp(2jB_{t})]=\tan^{j}(x_{0})\exp(2j^{2}t),

the real analyticity of the expected values is clear.

\displaystyle R^{sig}(\mathbf{u})_{k}

Scheme 6.1
.

(i)

Set a truncation level
K
.

(ii)

Define the truncated operators

and
R^{pow,K}

(iii)

Consider the truncated initial conditions

\mathbf{u}^{pow,\leq K}

(iv)

Employ a standard ODE solver to solve the ODEs

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}^{sig,K}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{pow,K}(0)

The expected representations are then given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u}^{sig},{\mathbb{X}}_{T}^{sig}\rangle)]

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(\langle\mathbf{u}^{pow},{\mathbb{X}}_{T}^{pow}\rangle)]

Appendix A
Adaptation of Theorem 4.4.11 of
Ethier and Kurtz 2005

Lemma A.1
.

Consider a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
and two measurable maps
X,Y:[0,T]\times[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Assume that both

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[X(s,t)-X(0,t)-\int_{0}^{s}Y(u,t)du\Big]=0\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\Big[X(s,t)-X(s,0)-\int_{0}^{t}Y(s,u)du\Big]=0

for each
s,t\in[0,T]
. If
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[|Y(s,t)|]dsdt<\infty
, then

{\mathbb{E}}[X(0,T)]={\mathbb{E}}[X(T,0)].

Proof.

We follow the proof of Theorem 4.4.11 in
Ethier and Kurtz 2005
. Set
f(s,t):={\mathbb{E}}[X(s,t)]
and note that the martingale properties and Fubini yield

f(s,t)-f(0,t)-\int_{0}^{s}{\mathbb{E}}[Y(u,t)]du=f(s,t)-f(s,0)-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[Y(s,u)]du=0,

for each
s,t\in[0,T]
. This shows that
f(s,t)
is absolutely continuous in
s
for each fixed
t
and in
t
for each fixed
s
. By Lemma 4.4.10 in the same book we can conclude that

f(T,0)-f(0,T)=0
and the result follows.
∎

\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}f_{0}(x_{0})=f_{0}(x_{0})=f(x_{0})
. Since by the dominated convergence theorem we also have

\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}f_{c}(x_{0})=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2t\pi}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}f_{c}(x)\exp\Big(-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{2t}\Big)dx=0,

we can conclude that
\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}f(x_{0})=P_{0}(x_{0})
.

(iii)
The claim follows as there is an
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}|g|(x_{0})ds
is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\exp(a(|{\,\cdot\,}|+1))(x_{0})ds\leq e^{a}\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\exp(a({\,\cdot\,}))(x_{0})+P_{s}\exp(-a({\,\cdot\,}))(x_{0})ds<\infty.

(iv)

Observe that for each
f\in{\mathcal{D}}
by the Itô formula the process
M^{f}
given by

M^{f}_{t}:=f(X_{t})-f(x_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})ds=\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(X_{s})dX_{s},

is a local martingale.
By definition of
{\mathcal{B}}
we have that
g^{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}
for each
g\in{\mathcal{B}}
. This in particular implies that
(f^{\prime})^{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}
and using
(iii)
we can thus compute

{\mathbb{E}}[|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}M^{f}_{t}|^{2}]\leq C\int_{0}^{T}P_{s}(|f^{\prime}|^{2})(x_{0})ds<\infty,

showing that
M^{f}
is a true martingale. As
{\mathcal{A}}f=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}
, by
(iii)
we know that
\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}|{\mathcal{A}}f|(x_{0})ds
is finite.
Taking expectations of
M_{t}^{f}
and applying Fubini we thus get that

P_{t}f(x_{0})-f(x_{0})=\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}{\mathcal{A}}f(x_{0})ds

for each
f\in{\mathcal{D}}
.
To conclude, observe that by
(ii)
(together with the fundamental theorem of calculus) yields

\partial_{s}P_{s}f(x_{0})=P_{s}{\mathcal{A}}f(x_{0})
that we already show to coincide with
{\mathcal{A}}P_{s}f(x_{0})
.

B.2
Proof of Lemma
5.13

(i)

Note first that the exponential moment condition on
X
implies that the affine transform formula, i.e.,

{\mathbb{E}}[\exp((\varepsilon+iu)X_{t})]=\exp(\phi(t,\varepsilon+iu)+\psi(t,\varepsilon+iu)x_{0}),

holds for all
u\in[-\tau,\tau]
and
t\in[0,T]
, which follows from Theorem 3.7 in
Spreij and Veerman 2010
.
By Fubini’s theorem we have that

P_{t}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\Big[\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\exp((\varepsilon+iu)X_{t})g(u)du\Big]=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\exp(\phi(t,\varepsilon+iu)+\psi(t,\varepsilon+iu)x)g(u)du.

We now apply Morera’s theorem and show that

\int_{\triangle}P_{t}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x)dx=0

for any triangle
\triangle
in
\mathbb{C}
, which implies that
P_{t}h_{\mathbf{u}}

is entire. First note that

x\mapsto P_{t}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x)
can be extended to
\mathbb{C}
since this is the case for

x\mapsto\exp(\phi(t,\varepsilon+iu)+\psi(t,\varepsilon+iu)x)g(u)
.
Again, by Fubini’s theorem
we have

P_{t}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x)dx=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\int_{\triangle}\exp(\phi(t,\varepsilon+iu)+\psi(t,\varepsilon+iu)x)dxg(u)du=0,

where the last equality follows from Goursat’s theorem and the fact that
x\mapsto\exp(\phi(t,\varepsilon+iu)+\psi(t,\varepsilon+iu)x)
is entire.

(ii)
For this property we only need that
h_{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{D}
which implies that
h_{\mathbf{u}},h^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u}},h^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u}}
can be bounded by some polynomial of some degree
n
. As diffusion and drift coefficient of affine diffusions are affine, this yields that
{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{E}}
and
the assertion then follows by the polynomial property of the affine process
X
(see e.g., Lemma 2.17
Cuchiero et al. 2012
).

(iii)
Here, we need again only that
h_{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{D}
.
By dominated convergence in holds that

P_{t}\mathcal{A}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x_{0})=\mathbb{E}[\lim_{N\to\infty}-N\vee(\mathcal{A}h_{\mathbf{u}}\wedge N)]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[-N\vee(\mathcal{A}h_{\mathbf{u}}\wedge N)]

and by the Feller property of affine processes (see e.g., Theorem 2.7 in
Duffie et al. 2003
)
x_{0}\mapsto\mathbb{E}[-N\vee(\mathcal{A}h_{\mathbf{u}}\wedge N)|X_{0}=x_{0}]

is continuous.
Now observe that

|P_{t}{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x_{0})-{\mathbb{E}}[-N\vee({\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})\wedge N]|\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|1_{\{|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|>N\}}],

which by Cauchy-Schwartz and Markov’s inequality can be bounded by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(\{|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|>N\})^{1/2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{t})|]^{1/2}}{N^{1/2}}.

By the polynomial property of
X
, proceeding as in
(ii)
we get that the convergence is uniform on compacts and thus
x\mapsto P_{t}{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}(x)
is continuous.

(iv)
Similarly, again by the polynomial property of
X
we get that for each
x\in S
,
P_{t}|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}|(x)
is bounded by a continuous function in
t
and thus
\int_{0}^{T}P_{t}|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}|(x)dt<\infty
for each
x\in S
. The same argument implies also that
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}P_{t}P_{s}|{\mathcal{A}}h_{\mathbf{u}}|(x)dsdt<\infty
.
