1.
Introduction

We are interested in counting solutions to Diophantine equations involving products of polynomial values.
Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a polynomial.
Consider the equation

(1.1)

\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}P(x_{i})=\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}P(y_{j})\neq 0

for a given integer
k\geq 1
.
For integers
N\geq 1
, let
[N]:=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
and define

A_{P,2k}([N]):=\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{k})\in[N]^{2k}:\text{solutions to \eqref{eqn: S}}\}.

Call a solution to (
1.1
)
trivial
if the first
k
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
equal the last
k
variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{k}
in pairs.
We say (
1.1
) has a

of nontrivial solutions in
[N]^{2k}
as
N\to+\infty

if almost all solutions are trivial,
or equivalently, if
\lim_{N\to+\infty}(A_{P,2k}([N])/N^{k})=k!
holds.
One might expect a typical equation of the form (
1.1
) to have this paucity property,
which is a problem raised and studied in some special cases of
P(x)
by Najnudel in
[
18
, §5]
.
A recent result of Klurman, Shkredov, and Xu
[
16

confirms that such phenomena exist in the
k=2

1.1.
Main results

In this paper, we prove the paucity phenomenon occurs for all
P(x)
satisfying mild necessary conditions.
Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
have degree

d\geq 2
and at least two distinct complex roots.
Let
e_{P}
be the maximum multiplicity of any complex root of
P
; then
1\leq e_{P}\leq d-1
.
(For example, if
P(x)=x^{2}(x+1)
, then

.)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P,d,e_{P}
be as above.
Then for integers
k,N\geq 1
, we have

A_{P,2k}([N])=k!N^{k}+O_{P,k,\varepsilon}(N^{k-1/6e_{P}+\varepsilon}).

We prove Theorem
1.1
in §
2
.
Related paucity results (for linear
P(x)=x-\alpha
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}

irrational
) were obtained independently by Bourgain, Garaev, Konyagin, and Shparlinski
[
3

and Heap, Sahay, and Wooley
[
13
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]
.
By considering certain algebraic norms, it may be possible to use our result (Theorem
1.1
) to obtain a version of those results (of
[
3
]
and
[
13
]
), weaker in the error term.
However, we are not aware of any known or easy implication in the other direction.

1.3.
Application to random multiplicative functions

One motivation for the study of Diophantine equations of the form (
1.1
) (when
P(x)=x
) comes from the moments of the Steinhaus random multiplicative function:
let
f(p)
be independent random variables (for all primes
p
) taking values uniformly from the complex unit circle, and define
f(n)
completely multiplicatively for all
n
.
We can use orthogonality to derive that

\mathbb{E}_{f}\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)\Bigr|^{2k}=A_{P(n)=n,2k}([N]).

Computing the moments here is exactly the same as counting solutions to (
1.1
).
As mentioned before, we have now a good understanding of the high moments of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)
.
When the support of
f(n)
is the full set
[N]
, these moments blow up and grow like powers of
\log N
as
k
grows.
Our recent work
[
19
]
with Pandey proves that when
f(n)
is supported on short intervals
[N,N+H]
with
H\to+\infty
and
H\ll N/(\log N)^{Ck^{2}}
as
N\to+\infty
, these moments are consistent with Gaussian moments for all
k\geq 1
.

Theorem
1.1
can be interpreted as the following moment asymptotic for the Steinhaus random multiplicative function
f
.
Suppose that
P(n)
has a positive leading coefficient;
then there exists a constant
n_{0}\geq 0
such that
P(n)>0
for all
n>n_{0}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}_{f}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n_{0}<n\leq N}f(P(n))\Bigr|^{2k}=k!+O_{k,P,\varepsilon}(N^{-1/6e_{P}+\varepsilon}).

The moments of random sums are strongly connected to their limiting distribution.
In particular, to prove the random sums have Gaussian limiting distribution, it is sufficient to show that all the moments match the Gaussian moments (although this is not a necessary condition).
Thus, Theorem
1.1
potentially gives a way to establish the central limit theorem for

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n\leq N}f(P(n))
subject to establishing the odd moments,

2

2

Very recently, Besfort Shala noted that the odd moments are trivial to handle by the divisor bound.

which could likely be done either by the methods of the present paper, or by adapting the treatment of analogous odd moments in
[
19
, end of §3]
.
In fact, Klurman, Shkredov, and Xu
[
16
]
already proved that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n\leq N}f(P(n))
has Gaussian limiting distribution by exploiting the martingale structure, which successfully reduces the task of establishing a central limit theorem to a fourth moment estimate. There is a lot more literature on the study of the distribution when

is linear and we refer readers to

Proof of the main result

The proof of Theorem

shares some ideas with the authors’ recent work
[

]
with Pandey, particularly the use of “factored” congruence conditions and induction on

3
3

3

The work
[

More specifically, our argument requires us to separately consider three types of solutions: trivial solutions (for which

We now build up to a proof of Theorem

.
Let
P,d,e_{P}
be as in Theorem

.
Furthermore, assume that
P
has positive leading coefficient.
(Theorem

Now fix an integer

such that
P(n)>0
for all

.
By the divisor bound, (

) has at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{k-1+\varepsilon})
solutions in
[N]^{2k}
with
\min(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\leq n_{0}
.
Therefore, we may and do restrict attention to

.
By replacing

with

, we may then assume

Let

be a polynomial, with no repeated complex roots, such that

(2.1)

Q(x)\mid P(x)\mid Q(x)^{e_{P}}.

Let
\Delta_{Q}\neq 0
be the discriminant of

.
(On a first reading, we suggest restricting to the special case

considered by Najnudel in
[

, where

and one can take
Q(x)=x(x+1)

The following result on polynomial congruences is due to Huxley
[

See also the stronger result in Stewart

Lemma 2.1

(Huxley)
.

Let
P,d,e_{P},Q,\Delta_{Q}
be as above.
Let
\ell\geq 1
be an integer.
Then

(2.2)

\#\{x\bmod{\ell}:Q(x)\equiv 0\bmod{\ell}\}\leq d^{\omega(\ell)}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{1/2}.

Proof.

Since
P
has
\geq 2
distinct complex roots, the condition (
2.1
) implies
\deg{Q}\geq 2
.
Since
\deg{Q}\geq 2
, the bound (
2.2
) then follows directly from
[
15
]
.
∎

This result leads to the following proposition:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
P,d,e_{P},Q,\Delta_{Q}
be as above.
Let
z,N\geq 1
be integers.
Then

(2.3)

\#\{x\in[N]:z\mid P(x)\}\leq d^{\omega(z)}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{1/2}\cdot(1+N/z^{1/e_{P}}).

Proof.

Let
\ell
be the smallest positive integer such that
z\mid\ell^{e_{P}}
.
Clearly
\ell\mid z
and
\ell\geq z^{1/e_{P}}
.

If
x\in[N]
and
z\mid P(x)
, then
z\mid Q(x)^{e_{P}}
by (

), so
\ell\mid Q(x)
.
This implies that

(2.4)

\#\{x\in[N]:z\mid P(x)\}\leq\#\{x\in[N]:\ell\mid Q(x)\}.

But a short calculation using Lemma
2.1
gives the bound

(2.5)

\#\{x\in[N]:\ell\mid Q(x)\}\leq d^{\omega(\ell)}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{1/2}\cdot\lceil N/\ell\rceil\leq d^{\omega(\ell)}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{1/2}\cdot(1+N/\ell).

(For
N=\ell
, the bound (
2.5
) follows directly from Lemma
2.1
;
for
N<\ell
it then follows by enlarging
N
to
\ell
,
while for
N>\ell
it follows by reduction modulo
\ell

Since
\ell\mid z
and
\ell\geq z^{1/e_{P}}
, the proposition follows from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).
∎

Remark 2.3
.

By using
[
17

instead of “enlarging

This would save roughly a factor of

But (

We next deduce the following general counting result.
Here and later, we let

(2.6)

G_{P,\lambda}([N],z):=\#\{(x,a,b)\in[N]\times[\lambda]^{2}:a\cdot z=b\cdot P(x)\text{ and }a<b\},

whenever
z,N,\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
P,d,e_{P},Q,\Delta_{Q}
be as above.
Let
k,z,N\geq 1
be integers.
Let

(2.7)

\begin{split}T:=\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})\in[N]^{k}:{}&z\mid P(x_{1})P(x_{2})\cdots P(x_{k}),\\
&P(x_{1}),P(x_{2}),\dots,P(x_{k})<z\}.\end{split}

Then for any integer
\lambda\geq 1
, we have

(2.8)

T\leq k\cdot G_{P,\lambda}([N],z)\cdot N^{k-1}+\tau_{k}(z)\cdot O(d^{\omega(z)})^{k}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{k/2}\cdot\left(\frac{N^{k}}{z^{1/e_{P}}}+\frac{N^{k-1}}{\lambda^{1/e_{P}}}+N^{k-2}\right).

Proof.

For each
j\in[k]
, let
I_{j}
be the contribution to
T
from tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
\operatorname{gcd}(P(x_{j}),z)\geq z/\lambda
.
Note that if
x\in[N]
has the properties
\operatorname{gcd}(P(x),z)\geq z/\lambda
and
P(x)<z
,
then
\operatorname{gcd}(P(x),z)=z/b
for some positive integer
b\leq\lambda
dividing
z
, and thus
P(x)=a\cdot z/b
for some positive integer
a<b
,
since
P(x)>0
.
It follows that for each
j\in[k]
, we have

(2.9)

I_{j}\leq G_{P,\lambda}([N],z)\cdot N^{k-1}.

Now let
S
be the contribution to
T
from tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
\operatorname{gcd}(P(x_{j}),z)<z/\lambda
for all
j\in[k]
.
Given positive integers
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
with
u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{k}=z
,
let
S_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}}
be the contribution to
T
from tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
\operatorname{gcd}(P(x_{j}),z)<z/\lambda
and
u_{j}\mid P(x_{j})
for all

.
Then
S_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}}=0
unless
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}<z/\lambda
, in which case we must have

and (by Proposition

)

\begin{split}S_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}}&\leq O(d^{\omega(z)})^{k}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{k/2}\cdot\frac{N^{k}}{\prod_{j\in[k]}\min(N,u_{j}^{1/e_{P}})}\\
&\leq O(d^{\omega(z)})^{k}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{k/2}\cdot\left(N^{k-2}+\frac{N^{k-1}}{\min_{i\in[k]}(z/u_{i})^{1/e_{P}}}+\frac{N^{k}}{z^{1/e_{P}}}\right),\end{split}

because

is either

,
or equal to

\prod_{j\in[k]}u_{j}^{1/e_{P}}=z^{1/e_{P}}

(2.10)

S_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}}\leq O(d^{\omega(z)})^{k}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{k/2}\cdot\left(N^{k-2}+\frac{N^{k-1}}{\lambda^{1/e_{P}}}+\frac{N^{k}}{z^{1/e_{P}}}\right),

.

S\leq\tau_{k}(z)\cdot O(d^{\omega(z)})^{k}\lvert\Delta_{Q}\rvert^{k/2}\cdot\left(N^{k-2}+\frac{N^{k-1}}{\lambda^{1/e_{P}}}+\frac{N^{k}}{z^{1/e_{P}}}\right).

, the desired result follows from (

) and (

Lemmas

has no linear factor, and therefore has few integral zeros in boxes.
Lemma

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
P,d
be as above.
Let
a,b
be distinct positive integers.
Then the polynomial
a\cdot P(y)-b\cdot P(x)
has no factor in
\mathbb{C}[x,y]
of degree
1
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
a\cdot P(y)-b\cdot P(x)
has a linear factor, say
fx+gy+h
(with
f,g,h\in\mathbb{C}
and
(f,g)\neq(0,0)
).
By symmetry, we may assume
g\neq 0
.
By scaling, we may then assume
g=-1
.
The factor theorem in
(\mathbb{C}[x])[y]
now implies

aP(fx+h)=bP(x).

Thus
f^{d}=b/a
(so
|f|=(b/a)^{1/d}\notin\{0,1\}
),
and the (invertible) affine map
L(x):=fx+h
on
\mathbb{C}
permutes the roots of
P
.
Thus each root of
P
is fixed by
L^{d!}
.
But since
|f|\neq 1
, the map
L^{d!}=f^{d!}x+O_{d,f,h}(1)
has at most one fixed point.
Thus
P
has at most one root, contradicting our original requirement that
P
have at least two distinct roots.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Bombieri–Pila
[
2
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an absolutely irreducible curve (over the rationals) of degree

.
If
N\geq\exp(r^{6})
,
then the number of integral points on
\mathcal{C}
and inside the square

does not exceed
N^{1/r}\exp(12\sqrt{r\log N\log\log N})
.

Recall the definition of
G_{P,\lambda}([N],z)
from (

).
The following lemma shows that

is not large when we average over

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
P,d
be as above.
Let

be integers.
Then

\sum_{y\in[N]}G_{P,\lambda}([N],P(y))\ll_{d,\varepsilon}\lambda^{2}\cdot N^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Plugging in (
2.6
), we see that the lemma concerns the number of solutions
(x,y)\in[N]^{2}
to
a\cdot P(y)=b\cdot P(x)
in total over positive integers
a,b\leq\lambda
with
a<b
.
Consider any such integers

.
By Lemma
2.5
,
each irreducible complex component of the curve

has degree
\geq 2
, and thus by Lemma

, at most

.
The lemma follows upon summing over

.
∎

Then by symmetry,

(2.14)

N_{P,2k}([N])\leq k^{2}\cdot R_{P,2k}([N])+2k\cdot N^{\prime}_{P,2k}([N]).

Since
R_{P,2k}([N])\leq N\cdot N_{P,2k-2}([N])
and
R_{P,2}([N])=0
, it follows from (
2.14
) that

(2.15)

N_{P,2k}([N])\leq k\cdot\max_{1\leq j\leq k}((k^{2}N)^{k-j}\cdot 2j\cdot N^{\prime}_{P,2j}([N])).

We now bound
N^{\prime}_{P,2k}([N])
.
Let
M(P)
be a positive integer (depending only on
P
) such that for all integers
n\geq M(P)
, we have

(2.16)

P(n)>\max(P(0),P(1),\dots,P(n-1))\qquad\text{and}\qquad P(n)\geq n^{d}/2.

Let
M\geq M(P)
and
\lambda\geq 1
denote integers to be chosen later.
Then by Corollary
2.4
(applied with
z:=P(y_{k})
for
y_{k}\geq M
, using the conditions (
2.13
) and (
2.16
)) and the divisor bound, the quantity
N^{\prime}_{P,2k}([N])
is at most

(2.17)

M^{2k}+O_{k,d,\Delta_{Q},\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})\cdot\sum_{M\leq y\leq N}\left(N^{k-1}G_{P,\lambda}([N],P(y))+\frac{N^{k}}{y^{d/e_{P}}}+\frac{N^{k-1}}{\lambda^{1/e_{P}}}+N^{k-2}\right).

By Lemma
2.7
, we conclude that

(2.18)

N^{\prime}_{P,2k}([N])\leq M^{2k}+O_{k,d,\Delta_{Q},\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})\cdot(\lambda^{2}N^{k-1/2}+N^{k}M^{1-d/e_{P}}+N^{k}\lambda^{-1/e_{P}}+N^{k-1}).

Choosing
M:=\lfloor M(P)\cdot N^{1/4}\rfloor
(say) and
\lambda:=\lfloor N^{1/6}\rfloor
, we get (since
1-d/e_{P}\leq-1/e_{P}
)

(2.19)

N^{\prime}_{P,2k}([N])\leq O_{k,d,\Delta_{Q},M(P),\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})\cdot(N^{k/2}+N^{k-1/4e_{P}}+N^{k-1/6e_{P}})\ll_{k,P,\varepsilon}N^{k-1/6e_{P}+\varepsilon}.

Finally, plug (
2.19
) into (
2.15
) to get
N_{P,2k}([N])\ll_{k,P,\varepsilon}N^{k-1/6e_{P}+\varepsilon}
.
This, plus the estimate (
2.12
) for trivial solutions, establishes the theorem.
∎
