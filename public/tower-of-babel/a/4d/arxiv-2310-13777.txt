Discrete and Continuous Caching Games
Áron Jánosik, Csenge Miklós, Dániel G. Simon, Kristóf Zólomy

Abstract
Alpern’s Caching Game is played by 2 2 players. Player 1 1 is a squirrel, who is hiding his d d nuts in n n different holes, with the restriction that he can only dig down a distance of 1 1 metre altogether. After that, Player 2 2 wants to find all the nuts, and she is allowed to dig down a distance of k k metres altogether. Player 2 2 wins after finding all the nuts, if she fails then the squirrel wins. We investigate a discrete version of the game, finding strategies and statements for both smaller and general values of n , d , k n,d,k . In particular, we answer a question of Pálvölgyi, by exhibiting an example, where the value of the game can change depending on which nut the squirrel reveals, when he has multiple options in the discrete game. We also investigate and invent other continuous versions of the game, one of them having a connection to the Manickam-Miklós-Singhi Conjecture. Keywords: game theory, probability, strategies, caching games, 2-person game MSC: 91A05

Introduction

Alpern’s Caching Game, introduced in a paper of Alpern, Fokkink, Leszek and Lindelauf
[
1

d

n

1

2

k

n

For example, she can change strategy after finding a nut in one of the holes. The searcher can only win if she finds all the nuts in the holes. The squirrel wins the game if at least one of the nuts stays hidden. An example game can be seen on Figure

Call these numbers the values of the corresponding games.

2
The Multiple Caching Game

2.1
Intuitive helpful lemmas

We proved some simple lemmas and theorems, which will be useful in the later parts of the paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

Getting a "helpful nut" at any point in the game does not decrease the value of the game.
A helpful nut is a nut who’s position is revealed by the squirrel as a help.

Proof.

If the squirrel tells the position of a nut, the searcher can forget the bonus information and play following his original strategy. Therefore the value of the game cannot decrease.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Getting a "gift nut" at any point in the game does not decrease the value of the game.
A gift nut is given to the searcher without any query. It still decreases the searcher’s remaining question number.

Proof.

If the squirrel gives the searcher a gift nut, without loss of generality we can call its hole the first hole.
The searcher can follow a strategy that gives at least the value her original game would give:
She will follow her original optimal strategy with one change. The first time she would question the first hole after the gift nut, she just omits the question and imagines that she asked it and was given a nut from the first hole.
This way, the winning probability stays the same, as her strategy was the optimal one, which means the squirrel’s nut-giving strategy cannot decrease the winning probability of the searcher under the game’s value. Therefore, getting the nut from the first hole is not making her chances worse compared to the original strategy.
If the rest of her queries would never ask the first hole, then originally the winning probability would be zero, in this case, so then following any new strategy will not give a smaller winning chance.
This new strategy, therefore, has at least the same winning percentage as the optimal one without the gift nut.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

The value of the Multiple Caching Game is monotonically decreasing in parameter
d
:
v_{M}(n,d+1,k)\leq v_{M}(n,d,k)
for any
n,d,k
integers.

Proof.

Suppose that the
(d+1)
-nut game has value
v_{M}(n,d+1,k)
. For any starting hiding position, we can force the squirrel to give a gift nut to the searcher. By Lemma
2.2

v_{M}(n,d+1,k)
. Now we just need to show that this game is equivalent to the
d
-nut game, which is obvious since the squirrel has a nut that he gives away anyways, and is only able to hide
d
nuts in the holes. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
.
v_{M}(n,d+1,k)\leq v_{M}(n,d,k)
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

When looking for an optimal strategy, it is enough to consider strategies with all questions asking exactly
k
holes.

Theorem 2.5

(Csóka
[
3
]
)
.

For any integers
n,d,k
,
v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
.

Proof.

The number of all the possible hiding variations is
\binom{n+d-1}{d}
since the squirrel hides
d
nuts in
n
holes, and multiple nuts can go into the same hole.
If the squirrel hides by choosing any of the hiding variations with the same probability, so he chooses uniformly between them, then the searcher can win with probability at most
\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
. That is because the searcher can only win in
k^{d}
different ways depending on which hole she gets the nuts from in each query, hence she can win at most
k^{d}
different hiding variations out of all.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

That upper bound holds in Alpern’s Caching Game too, see Csóka’s paper for proof:

Proof.

(Pálvölgyi)

We define a strategy in Alpern’s Caching Game that finds the nut with at least the same probability as the value of the game in the Multiple Caching Game.
We use the optimal searcher strategy from the Multiple Caching Game:
If our next query asks
k
holes, in our game the searcher will start digging on these
k
holes simultaneously, with equal speed in all of them. As soon as she finds a nut, she can just imagine that the squirrel chose to give it to her (since the squirrel could indeed choose that one in the other game). If there are more nuts at the same depth, she just takes one of them, and only digs the other out later if a query asks the hole. The searcher then continues the strategy with the next question and so on. If she ever asks only nut-free holes, then she loses the game as in the Multiple Caching Game. If she could find all the nuts with this method in the discrete game, she finds them here too. She has dug at most
1
unit altogether for the nuts themselves, and for every query, she dug at most
k
times the required amount, so she dug at most
k
distance in the holes. Therefore with this strategy, the searcher wins precisely when she would win in the Multiple Caching Game with nuts placed in the same holes. So the value in Alpern’s Caching Game is at least the value in the Multiple Caching Game.
∎

The following statement was proven in several other papers.

Theorem 2.8
.

For any integers
n,d,k
,
v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq\frac{k}{n}

Proof.

If the squirrel hides all his nuts in the same hole, then the searcher’s first query finds a nut with at most
\frac{k}{n}
probability, therefore the value must be
v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq\frac{k}{n}
.
∎

Observation 3.1
.

In this strategy, the searcher never asks a hole if she has not received a nut from it during a previous question. (1-\frac{nk^{2}}{\binom{n+2}{3}})\cdot k^{2}(k-2)

Hence we proved that the value of the game is
\frac{k^{3}}{\binom{n+2}{3}}

3.3
Evil, Kind, and Random Squirrel

After arguing strategies in the last section, one could be curious to see if it is significant, whether the squirrel can choose which nut he gives the searcher. To figure that out, we define two other versions of the Multiple Caching Game.

The game itself does not change in any of the cases, the only thing that changes is how the squirrel chooses the nut to give away when multiple nuts are found in a single question.

The value of each game is the winning probability of the searcher if both players play optimally.

Definition 3.7
.

If the searcher guesses more nuts in a single question, the
Evil Squirrel
chooses one to give her, following his strategy to win the game.
This is the original version of the Discrete Caching Game.

We still call the value of that game
v_{M}(n,d,k)
.

Definition 3.8
.

If the searcher guesses more nuts in a single question, the
Random Squirrel
gives the searcher one of them with equal probability.

We call the value of that game
v_{Mr}(n,d,k)
.

Definition 3.9
.

If the searcher guesses more nuts in a single question, the
Kind Squirrel
gives the searcher a nut, following a strategy discussed with the searcher in advance. So basically the searcher decided in advance how to receive the nut.

We call the value of that game
v_{Mj}(n,d,k)
.

Theorem 3.10
.

For any integer triplet
(n,d,k)
,
v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq v_{Mr}(n,d,k)\leq v_{Mj}(n,d,k)\leq\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
2.5
.  we only used the hiding strategy and never mentioned how the squirrel chooses which nut to give. Therefore the upper bound holds for all defined versions of the game, even with the kind squirrel.

v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq v_{Mr}(n,d,k)
, since the evil squirrel can choose to follow the random squirrel strategy, which gives him a maximal winning chance at least as large as the random squirrel has. Therefore the value of the random squirrel game is at least as large as the evil squirrel game.

v_{Mr}(n,d,k)\leq v_{Mj}(n,d,k)
, since the searcher can just tell the kind squirrel to apply the random nut-giving strategy, which shows that she can get the same winning probability as in the random squirrel case.
∎

Up until now, all the triplets we checked turned out to be accurate, therefore
v_{M}(n,d,k)=v_{Mr}(n,d,k)=v_{Mj}(n,d,k)
was true in the previously discussed games.

We managed to find a triplet, for which these 3 games differ in value. This is the
(3,3,2)
game. Pálvölgyi in
[
5
]
discussed this case, but he made a mistake since he stated that
v_{M}(3,3,2)=\frac{2}{3}
. In reality, this value can only be reached in the kind squirrel game, and it is also the largest possible value because of Theorem
2.8
.

To find the values of the other two games we used a computer. The method was the following: We made choice variables for every single choice the squirrel or the searcher has during the game. (including hiding strategy, questioning strategy, nut-giving strategy (for which it was fixed for the random squirrel as an equal probability for all included nuts)), these variables ranged between
0
and
1
as they described the probability of choosing the given option. From all of these variables, one can calculate the searcher’s winning probability with these parameters. Now we shall be aware that the squirrel wants to minimize that probability, and the searcher wants to maximize it. Hence to get the value of the game, the squirrel’s choices will always be such that they minimise the probability with the given searcher parameters, and we need to find the searcher strategy for which this minimised value is maximal. The equations were linear in each variable, except the minimums and maximums. Here we just used a simple program to numerically maximize our long function of these parameters.

This maximization must be a lower bound for the game value since for the search strategy it gives, it defined a hiding strategy that makes the winning probability equal to the estimate we got.

We also calculated the other way, so the searcher optimized her steps parametrically based on the squirrel moves, and the program looked for the squirrel choices that minimize the searcher’s winning probability. The probability this approach gives must be an upper bound for the value since for that hiding strategy it also gives a searching strategy for which the squirrel loses with the given probability.

Finally, we got the following results:

0.6312\leq v_{Mr}(n,d,k)\leq 0.6316

0.5999\leq v_{M}(n,d,k)\leq 0.6001

The exact hiding and searching strategies for these bounds can be found in Appendix (
A
.).

3.4
Lower bound for
v_{M}(n,d,k)
as
d\to\infty

We proved that
v_{M}(n,d,k)
is monotonically decreasing with respect to
d
. This makes one wonder, whether as
d\to\infty
how does the value of the Multiple Caching Game behave? We show that the value does not converge to
0
if
k\geq 2
. Hence there is a positive probability, such that no matter how many nuts the squirrel hides, the searcher can find all of them. Now we restate and prove the main theorem of this subsection:

See
1.10

Proof.

Fix
n
and
k\geq 2
. We define a strategy that finds all the nuts with a probability independent from
d
: In the first step, the searcher asks
k
arbitrarily chosen holes. In all the subsequent steps, she asks the hole she received the nut from in the previous step and asks
k-1
more holes chosen uniformly out of all the other holes. In the first step, she loses with probability at most
\frac{n-k}{n}
since there must be at least one hole with a nut. In a general step, the searcher can only lose, if the last nut she received was the last in its hole. If before the step there were
i-1
empty holes, and after the step there are
i
empty holes, then in the next step the searcher loses with probability
\frac{\binom{i-1}{k-1}}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}
, since she guesses
k-1
random holes out of all the remaining ones. Therefore the win probability of the searcher with this strategy is at least

c(n,k)=\left(1-\frac{n-k}{n}\right)\cdot\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\binom{i-1}{k-1}}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}\right)>0
.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

For any
k\geq 2
,
v_{A}(n,d,k)\geq c(n,\lfloor k\rfloor)>0
, where
c(n,\lfloor k\rfloor)
is a positive constant only depending on
n
and
\lfloor k\rfloor
.

Proof.

Using Theorem
2.7
.  for any integer
k
,
v_{A}(n,d,k)\geq c(n,k)
. For non-integer
k
values,
v_{A}(n,d,k)\geq v_{A}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor)\geq c(n,\lfloor k\rfloor)
using the game’s definition.
∎

4
Continuous versions

4.1
Discrete game for real
k
values

We can define the game for non-integer values of
k
. The searcher is allowed to use a probabilistic strategy, giving a probability for each possible query, such that the expected number of chosen holes is
k
.

In this whole section, we assume
k
to be any positive real number.

Theorem 4.1
.

\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
is an upper bound for the value of the Discrete Caching Game with arbitrary positive real
k
values.

Proof.

We want to use the proof of Theorem
2.5
and show that the expected number of hiding positions the searcher can find is at most
k^{d}
.
We argue the following way. We do not know how many allocations of
d-1
nuts the searcher can find in the first
d-1
steps, so we call it
E_{d-1}
, but we do know that in the last step, the searcher is expected to find a nut in at most
k
positions. So the expected value of all the cases the searcher can find (call it
E_{d}
) is certainly
E_{d}\leq kE_{d-1}
.
Similarly, we call the expected number of
d-2
nut allocations the searcher can find in the first
d-2
questions
E_{d-2}
, and notice that in the
d-1
-th question she is expected to ask
k
holes. Therefore it is sure that
E_{d-1}\leq kE_{d-2}
. Continuing that argument until
E_{1}=k
, and substituting every inequality in the last one we get
E_{d}\leq k^{d}
. Therefore the proof of Theorem
2.5
works here too.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

If
n
is large enough compared to
d
and
k
, then the value of the game is
\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
for any positive real
k
.

Proof.

We already have the upper bound from the previous theorem.

For the lower bound, we use a search strategy similar to the one in the paper of Pálvölgyi.
[
5
, Proof of Theorem 2.]
.
In each step, the searcher will ask
\lfloor k\rfloor
holes with probability
p
and
\lceil k\rceil
holes with probability
1-p
such that the expected value is
k
.
We define
p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)
the same way Pálvölgyi did in his paper.

For this, we need to revisit the strategy of the searcher if she can only ask 1 hole in each step. Then her strategy is the following:

She picks randomly a starting allocation
\mu
of the nuts, uniformly distributed among all the
\binom{n+d-1}{d}
possibilities. Then she will ask the hole with the most nuts in
\mu
. She asks it as many times, as many nuts are there in that hole in
\mu
.
Then she asks the hole with the second most nuts and asks just enough times to make it empty assuming
\mu
. She repeats that until she asked all her questions. If several holes have the same amount of nuts, she picks one of them with equal probability. This way, if we count the found nuts in the holes she picked in order, we get a Young diagram
\lambda
at any point of the game.
For the outside observer, who does not know
\mu
, the Young diagram
\lambda
tells a probability of the searcher asking the same hole again in the next question. (provided that the observer knows the strategy, but does not know the
\mu
, he can calculate conditional probabilities of all possible Young diagram shapes.). This probability with a given Young table
\lambda
is called
p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)
.
Now we define
p_{\lambda}(n,d,s)=s\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)
.

We now have to assume that
n
is large enough, such that
p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil)\leq 1
, and also such that
n\geq d\cdot\lceil k\rceil
.

Now we can define the strategy of the searcher. The strategy will be such a strategy, that the found nuts will form a Young diagram at any point. If
k
is an integer, use the same strategy Pálvölgyi described in his paper. If
k
is not an integer, then the searcher will ask:

•

The hole she received the last nut from, and
\lfloor k\rfloor-1
brand new, previously untouched holes with
p\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor)
probability.

•

\lfloor k\rfloor
brand new, previously untouched holes with
p\cdot(1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor))
probability.

•

The hole she received the last nut from, and
\lceil k\rceil-1
brand new, previously untouched holes with
(1-p)\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil)
probability.

•

\lceil k\rceil
brand new, previously untouched holes with
(1-p)\cdot(1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil))
probability.

Now we need to show that this strategy wins with
\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
probability. If any question asks more than one hole that contains nuts, the searcher has already lost. Therefore, it is enough to show that with this strategy, each step has
k
times the probability of finding the door
i
with a nut than the corresponding step in the
k=1
case.

Here we have 2 cases to consider: If door
i
is the door she last received a nut from, then if
k=1
, she had a probability
p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)
to find it. In the current strategy, her chance to find the hole is:

p\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor)+(1-p)\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil)=p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)\cdot(p\lfloor k\rfloor+(1-p)\lceil k\rceil)=k\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)

which we wanted to show.

If door
i
is a new door and
k=1
she had probability
1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)
to find it.
In the current strategy, her chance to find the hole is:

(\lfloor k\rfloor-1)\cdot p\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor)+\lfloor k\rfloor\cdot p\cdot(1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor))+(\lceil k\rceil-1)\cdot(1-p)\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil)+\lceil k\rceil\cdot(1-p)\cdot(1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil))=\\
(\lfloor k\rfloor p+\lceil k\rceil(1-p))-(p\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lfloor k\rfloor)+(1-p)\cdot p_{\lambda}(n,d,\lceil k\rceil))=k\cdot(1-p_{\lambda}(n,d,1)

which we wanted to show.

If
k
was
1
, the searcher could win the game with probability
\frac{1}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
. Therefore since the game consists of
d
steps, and the chance of finding a new nut in each step is
k
times the chance of finding it with
1
-sized queries, we get that the winning probability of the searcher is
\frac{k^{d}}{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
.
∎

4.2
Peanut butter

We also generalised the Multiple Caching Game for non-integer nut numbers. In this game the squirrel is not hiding nuts, he is hiding peanut butter in the holes. He has a jar of
d
units of peanut butter which he digs into the holes entirely, putting into each hole some non-negative real quantity of peanut butter. Here the searcher can choose
k
holes for a query, and if those
k
holes contain together at least
1
unit of peanut butter, then the squirrel gives her
1
unit of peanut butter out of those holes as he wants. Otherwise, the searcher lost.

Even if the searcher only wants to find
1
unit of peanut butter, the game is still not trivial. Hence we considered that case first. Here the searcher does not have a strategy, she just chooses
k
out of the
n
holes and wins if these holes contain at least
1
unit of peanut butter. The hider wants to spread the peanut butter in a way to minimise the sets of
k
holes that contain
1
unit of butter added together.

If
d\geq n
then no matter how the squirrel hides, there will be a hole with at least
1
unit of peanut butter. Therefore the squirrel has the best winning probability if he hides all the peanut butter in that single hole. In this case, the searcher has a win probability of
\frac{k}{n}
. If
d<\frac{n}{k}
then the squirrel can just hide the same amount of peanut butter in each hole, making it impossible to find
k
holes that sum up to
1
unit of nut.
The interesting cases to see are when
d
is between these two values.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
k\mid n
, then there is no in-between case, so whenever
d\geq\frac{n}{k}
, the searcher will win with probability at least
\frac{k}{n}
.

Due to András Imolay, personal communication.

To prove that, we use the Baranyai Theorem
[
6
]
: We can partition the set of all
k
-element subsets of
[n]
in a way such that each partition contains
\frac{n}{k}
elements, whose disjoint union is precisely
[n]
. Since altogether there are at least
\frac{n}{k}
units of peanut butter in the holes, in each partition at least one of the
k
-element sets must contain at least
1
unit of peanut butter by the pigeonhole principle. Therefore at least
\frac{1}{\frac{n}{k}}
fraction of all
k
-elemented sets have at least
1
unit of peanut butter, so the searcher wins with a probability at least
\frac{k}{n}
.
∎

If
k\nmid n
, usually there are cases in-between:

Example 4.4
.

If
n=5
,
k=2
and
\frac{5}{2}\leq d<3
, the best hiding strategy of the squirrel gives a chance of
\frac{3}{10}
for the searcher to win.

Proof.

If the squirrel hides
\frac{d}{3}
units in
3
holes, and nothing in the other holes, the searcher can only win if she guesses
2
of these
3
holes. She has
\frac{3}{10}
chance to do so.

Now we show that the hider cannot achieve
\frac{2}{10}
probability of the searcher succeeding.
Suppose there is such a hiding allocation. Let’s say that the holes contain
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq a_{3}\geq a_{4}\geq a_{5}
units of peanut butter respectively. Since precisely
2
of the pairwise sums add up to at least
1
unit, these sums must be
a_{1}+a_{2}
and
a_{1}+a_{3}
.
Hence the other sums must be strictly less than
1
, which means
a_{1}+a_{4},a_{1}+a_{5},a_{4}+a_{5}
are all less than
1
. Therefore
a_{1}+a_{4}+a_{5}<\frac{3}{2}
. We also know
a_{2}+a_{3}<1
. Therefore
d=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4}+a_{5}<\frac{5}{2}
, which is not a possible value for
d
.
∎

Our question is quite similar to the Manickam-Miklós-Singhi Conjecture, which can be found in Csóka’s paper
[
3
]
. It was stated there for a different reason because it has a similar limes problem to a limes problem of Alpern’s Caching Game.

Question 4.5
.

Let
n,k
be fixed integers. Let
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be a
k
elemented subset, chosen uniformly. Try to find
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
real numbers, such that

P\left(a_{i_{1}}+a_{i_{2}}+\dots+a_{i_{k}}>\frac{k}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)

(1)

is maximized.

Conjecture 4.6
.

(Manickam–Miklós–Singhi)

If
4k\leq n
, then the maximum probability is
1-\frac{k}{n}
.

Notice that if we ask about maximizing

P\left(a_{i_{1}}+a_{i_{2}}+\dots+a_{i_{k}}<\frac{k}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)

instead, we get an equivalent question since if we have an optimal solution for one of them, we can just reflect all of the numbers over their average to get a solution to the other question. This new question is then equivalent to minimizing

P\left(a_{i_{1}}+a_{i_{2}}+\dots+a_{i_{k}}\geq\frac{k}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)

. Based on the conjecture, the optimal solution would give
\frac{k}{n}
whenever
4k\leq n
.

We can call the units of the peanut butter in each corresponding hole as
a_{1},a_{2}\dots,a_{n}
, and their sum as
d
. If
\frac{k}{n}d=1
, then we get an equivalence between the question of the Manickam-Miklós-Singhi Conjecture and the game with
d=\frac{n}{k}
. So if the MMS conjecture is true, it would mean that in our game, whenever
4k\leq n
there are no cases between
d<\frac{n}{k}
and
d\geq n
.

For all the games that had in-between cases, the best hiding strategy was always of a specific kind, so we conjecture that this is always true:

Conjecture 4.7
.

No matter what the parameters are, there is always an optimal hiding strategy of the following form: The hider chooses
r
holes and hides exactly
\frac{d}{r}
units of peanut butter in each of them. The rest of the holes remain empty.
