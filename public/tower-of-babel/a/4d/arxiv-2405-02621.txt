Introduction

For integers
a\leq b
, put
[a,b]:=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
, and denote
[n]:=[1,n]
for shorthand. For a set
X
, denote by

its power set and, for integer
k\geq 0
, denote by
{X\choose k}({X\choose\leq k}
) the collection of all (at most)
k
-element subsets (
k
-sets
) of
X
. A
family
is simply a collection of sets. We call a family
intersecting
, if any two of its sets intersect. A ‘trivial’ example of an intersecting family is the
full star
: the family of all sets containing a fixed element. The
covering number

\tau(\mathcal{F})
of a family
\mathcal{F}
is the size of the smallest

such that

for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Each such

we call a

or a

. An intersecting family is

or a

if it has covering number

. We call an intersecting family

2
.

One of the oldest and most famous results in extremal combinatorics is the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
6
]
, which states that for
n\geq 2k>0
an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
satisfies
|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}
. Thus, the extremal example is a full star. Answering a question of Erdős, Ko, and Rado, Hilton and Milner
[
22
]
found the size and structure of the largest non-trivial intersecting families of
k
-sets. It has size
{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1
and has an element that intersects all but one set of the family.

For a family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}
and
i\in[n]
, the
degree
of
i
in
\mathcal{F}
is the number of sets from
\mathcal{F}
containing
i
. Let
\Delta(\mathcal{F})
stand for the

of an element in
\mathcal{F}
. The

\gamma(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is the quantity
|\mathcal{F}|-\Delta(\mathcal{F})
. One may think of diversity as of the distance from
\mathcal{F}
to the closest star. Frankl
[
10
]
proved a far-reaching generalization of the Hilton–Milner theorem. We present its slightly stronger variant due to Zakharov and the author
[

]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let

and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be an intersecting family. If
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}
for some real

, then

(1.1)

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}+{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}-{n-u-1\choose k-1}.

We note that the Hilton–Milner theorem is included in Theorem
1.1
: simply put

. Theorem
1.1
provides essentially the strongest possible stability result for the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem in the regime when the intersecting family is large (more precisely, for the families of size at least
{n-2\choose k-2}+2{n-3\choose k-2}
). There are several other stability results for the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, see, e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
19
]
. Recently, there were a series of results
[
21
,
23
]
in which the authors found a more fine-grained relationship between the structure of the intersecting family and its size (again, for large intersecting families). See the papers of the author
[
25
,
26
]
for the most general and conclusive results.

Theorem

is stated in terms of diversity. The largest bound on diversity it gives is for

. Actually, for relatively large

for

Another natural measure of how far the intersecting family is from the trivial family is the covering number. Intersecting families of
k
-sets with fixed covering number were studied in several classical works. The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem shows that the largest intersecting family of

-element sets has covering number

. The result of Hilton and Milner

(note that it is independent of

. Later, both lower
[

]
and upper
[

In
[

(cf. also
[

]

Let us define the following important family.

(1.2)

\mathcal{T}_{2}(k):=\big\{[k]\big\}\cup\big\{\{1\}\cup[k+1,2k-1]\big\}\cup\big\{\{2\}\cup[k+1,2k-1]\big\}.

It is easy to see that

is intersecting, moreover,

Define

is isomorphic to

Theorem 1.2

(

]

Let

Frankl proved this theorem using the Delta-system method, which was behind many of the breakthroughs in extremal set theory in the 1970s and 80s. If one analyzes the method used by Frankl directly, then the bound on

is doubly exponential in

if one analyzes the proof in

. Using a refined variant of the

-system method, one can get the bound down to

Theorem 1.3
.

The conclusion of Theorem
1.2
holds for any

,
n>2k
.

The case

In the paper

A weaker version of Theorem

Remark 2:

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

2.1.
Outline of the approach

Take an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
with covering number
3
. The proof of the theorem bifurcates based on whether
\gamma(\mathcal{F})
is large or small. (Concretely, whether
\gamma(\mathcal{F})>{n-5\choose k-3}
or not.)

In the case of large diversity, we show that
\mathcal{F}
is significantly smaller than the family
\mathcal{C}_{3}(n,k)
that is expected to be extremal. For
n<2k^{2}
the family
\mathcal{C}_{3}(n,k)
is still quite large, and it is sufficient to use the bound (
1.1
) to show that
|\mathcal{F}|
is small.

For
n>2k^{2}
, we use the recently developed ‘peeling’ procedure, which is an independent and apparently useful part of the spread approximation technique
[
30
]
, which was recently upgraded to be much more efficient
[
28
]
. We say that a (non-uniform) family is maximal intersecting if no set can be replaced by a proper subset without violating the intersection property. Peeling is an iterative procedure in which we replace the family by a maximal intersecting family and then remove (peel) the layer of the largest sets. It allows for an efficient control of the structure of the family. In a way, it is an improved analogue of the Delta-system method as used by, say, Frankl in
[
8
]
. Back to the proof, the fact that
\mathcal{F}
has large diversity implies that we can peel the family up to a constant layer without the remaining family getting trivial (i.e., being replaced by one singleton). This at the end allows us to efficiently control the number of different
3
-element covers of
\mathcal{F}
, which turns out to be much smaller than that for
\mathcal{C}_{3}(n,k)
. As a result, the size of
\mathcal{F}
is much smaller than that of
\mathcal{C}_{3}(n,k)
.

The second case is small diversity. The key idea in this case is an extension of the bipartite switching idea, which was introduced in
[
29
]
(similar ideas appeared earlier in
[
13
]
). Its application is based on Corollary
2.1
for cross-intersecting families (essentially, the two cross-intersecting families are restrictions of
\mathcal{F}(1)
and
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
, where
1
is the element with the highest degree). We carefully change the family
\mathcal{F}
so that its size does not decrease and the covering number is preserved, but
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
gets smaller and smaller. We actually start with a minimal subfamily
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
with covering number
2
and at the end of the procedure we get that
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
coincides with
\mathcal{M}
.

The last step is to show that the optimal minimal
\mathcal{M}
for
\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}_{2}(k)
. This is done in Lemma
2.2
. In order to show this, we found an elegant way to upper bound the size of
\mathcal{F}
doing a specific size count of the size of the family
\mathcal{F}(1)
that cross-intersects
\mathcal{M}
and compare it term by term with an analogous count for
\mathcal{T}_{2}(k)
.

2.2.
Preliminaries

For a family
{\mathcal{A}}\subset 2^{X}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(Y)=

\displaystyle\big\{F\setminus Y:Y\subset F,F\in{\mathcal{A}}\big\},

\displaystyle\big\{F:Y\subset F,F\in{\mathcal{A}}\big\},

Note the difference between

The families

Corollary 2.1

(
[

Let

be a pair of cross-intersecting families. Denote

Then, if

, then

(2.1)

|{\mathcal{A}}|+|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n\choose a}.

Moreover, the displayed inequality is strict unless
|{\mathcal{B}}|=0
.

If
{n-j\choose b-j}\leq|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n-t\choose a-1}
for integer

(2.2)

|{\mathcal{A}}|+|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n\choose a}-{n-j\choose a}+{n-j\choose b-j}.

Moreover, if the left inequality on

.

The result in
[

]
did not explicitly treat the equality case. However, it is clear that strictness of (

), and the equality case in (

Let us recall the definition of shifting. For a given pair of indices
1\leq i<j\leq n
and a set
A\subset[n]
, define its
(i,j)
-shift

S_{ij}(A)
as follows. If
i\in A
or
j\notin A
, then
S_{ij}(A)=A
. If
j\in A,i\notin A
, then
S_{ij}(A):=(A-\{j\})\cup\{i\}
. That is,
S_{ij}(A)
is obtained from
A
by replacing
j
with
i
.
The
(i,j)
-shift

of a family
\mathcal{A}
is as follows:

S_{ij}(\mathcal{A}):=\{S_{ij}(A)\ :\ A\in\mathcal{A}\}\cup\{A\ :\ A,S_{ij}(A)\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Shifting is a very useful operation in the study of a class of extremal set theoretic problems. We refer to
[
9
]
for a survey. Shifting preserves the sizes of sets and the size of the family:
|S_{ij}({\mathcal{A}})|=|{\mathcal{A}}|
. Importantly, if
{\mathcal{A}}
is intersecting then
S_{ij}({\mathcal{A}})
is intersecting. Unfortunately, shifting can potentially reduce the covering number and is thus not directly applicable to our problem. However, with a certain amount of care, it is still possible to use, which we do in this paper.

\mathcal{F}(\bar{1})
