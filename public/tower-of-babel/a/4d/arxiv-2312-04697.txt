Abstract
. We investigate the geometry of a family of equations in two dimensions which interpolate between the Euler equations of ideal hydrodynamics and the inviscid surface quasi-geostrophic equation. This family can be realised as geodesic equations on groups of diffeomorphisms. We show precisely when the corresponding Riemannian exponential map is non-linear Fredholm of index 0. We further illustrate this by examining the distribution of conjugate points in these settings via a Morse theoretic approach.



1.
Introduction

In the early 1990s Constantin et al.
[
6
]
investigated a 2D hydrodynamic model known as the surface quasi-geostrophic (SQG) equation. One motivation for this was a striking mathematical analogy with the 3D Euler equations exhibited by the authors via several key analytic similarities. These investigations led to the study of a whole family of inviscid SQG-type equations which can be viewed as interpolating between the standard SQG equation and the 2D Euler equations, cf.
[
5
]
.

On the other hand, it is well known from the work of Arnold and his school that the motions of an ideal fluid have a beautiful geometric description as geodesic equations on the group of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms of the fluid domain
[
1
,
2
]
. A key distinction between two and three dimensional hydrodynamics comes from examining the Fredholm properties of the associated Riemannian exponential maps. In 2D the exponential map is a non-linear Fredholm map of index zero, but in 3D it is not
[
8
,
11
,
16
]
.

It was shown by Washabaugh in
[
21
]
that the standard SQG equation can also be reformulated as a geodesic equation on the group of exact volume-preserving diffeomorphisms equipped with a metric given by the homogeneous Sobolev
\dot{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
inner product. He further illustrated via an explicit example that the exponential map in this setting fails to be Fredholm. Recently one of the authors and Vu showed that the entire generalized SQG family, given by

(1.1)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\theta+u\cdot\nabla\theta=0\\
&u=\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\beta}{2}-1}\theta,\qquad 0\leq\beta\leq 1\end{split}

can be viewed as geodesic equations
[
13
]
. This opens up a possibility to investigate, using Riemannian geometric tools, the transition from 2D Euler to the surface quasi-geostrophic equations.

The central focus of this paper is to understand how the parameter
\beta
affects properties of the corresponding Riemannian exponential maps. In particular, we prove that for
\beta<1
these exponential maps are non-linear Fredholm of index
0
, illustrating that the value
\beta=1
is critical. In the former case we establish, via a Morse theoretic argument, a finite upper bound on the number of conjugate points along an arbitrary geodesic segment. As the interpolation parameter approaches the critical value, this upper bound approaches infinity. We then provide an explicit example to show that this bound is attained.

2.1.
Configuration Spaces

Let

We define the set

to consist of all functions from

H^{s}

H^{s}
-diffeomorphisms
\mathscr{D}^{s}(M)=\{C^{1}\text{ diffeomorphisms of }M\}\cap H^{s}(M,M)
inherits a smooth submanifold structure as an open subset of
H^{s}(M,M)
. Its tangent space at the identity,
T_{e}\mathscr{D}^{s}(M)=H^{s}(TM)

(2.1)

H^{s}(TM)=\mathcal{H}\oplus\nabla H^{s+1}(M)\oplus\nabla^{\perp}H^{s+1}(M)

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the finite-dimensional subspace of harmonic vector fields on
M
.

The subgroup
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
of exact diffeomorphisms is then defined to be the submanifold whose tangent space at the identity consists of those divergence-free vector fields with zero harmonic component, i.e.

(2.2)

T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)=\nabla^{\perp}H^{s+1}(M)=\{u\ |\ u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{u}\ \text{with}\ \psi_{u}\in H^{s+1}(M)\}.

We refer to
\psi_{u}
as the
stream function
for

. This configuration space will be the central focus of the paper, however we will also consider its smooth counterpart
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}(M)=\{C^{\infty}\text{ diffeomorphisms of }M\}\cap\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
where
T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}(M)=\nabla^{\perp}C^{\infty}(M)
.

It is worth noting at this point that the homogeneous Sobolev

\displaystyle\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\left\langle u,\Delta^{s}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}},

when restricted to

H^{s}
topology.

\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)

and the Lie algebra adjoint

2.2.
Right-Invariant Metrics and their Exponential Maps

We now recall the abstract geometric framework of V. Arnold
[
1
,
2
]
.

Let
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
be an inner product on
T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
which we extend to a Riemannian metric on
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
via right translation. For a linear operator
L:T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)\rightarrow T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
, we denote by
L^{*}
its adjoint with respect to the metric, i.e,
\left\langle Lu,v\right\rangle=\left\langle u,L^{*}v\right\rangle
for all
u,v\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
. As their definitions involve both the Lie and Riemannian structure, the geometry is in some sense encoded in the operators

\left\langle\Ad^{*}_{\eta}u,v\right\rangle=\left\langle u,\Ad_{\eta}v\right\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle\ad_{u}^{*}v,w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,\ad_{u}w\right\rangle

associated with the Lie group and Lie algebra coadjoint representations. Geodesics
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
in
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
of the metric induced by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
are critical paths for the energy functional. The corresponding geodesic equation, when reduced to the Lie algebra, is referred to as an
Euler-Arnold
equation which takes the form:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u=-\ad^{*}_{u}u\\
&u=\dot{\gamma}\circ\gamma^{-1}.\end{split}

If we further impose an initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}
, then we obtain an initial value problem which enjoys the following (coadjoint) conservation law

(2.6)

\Ad^{{*}}_{\gamma(t)}u(t)=u_{0}.

For a comprehensive list of notable examples of Euler-Arnold equations, cf.
[
2
,
11
,
20
]
. The second equation in (
2.5
) is simply the flow equation, which provides a link between the (Eulerian) solution map
u_{0}\mapsto u
and the (Lagrangian) solution map
u_{0}\mapsto(\gamma,\dot{\gamma})
. The latter has a clear geometric interpretation as a Riemannian exponential map on
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
. Namely, we have

(2.7)

u_{0}\mapsto\exp_{e}(tu_{0})=\gamma(t)

where
\gamma(t)
is the unique geodesic with
\gamma(0)=e
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=u_{0}
.

2.3.
The Jacobi Equation

Assume now that the metric induced by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
has a well defined smooth exponential map on
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
. For fixed
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}(M)
and
T>0
, we have the geodesic segment,
\gamma|_{[0,T]}
, where
\gamma(t)=\exp_{e}(tu_{0})
. The Jacobi field along
\gamma
with initial conditions
J(0)=0
and
\dot{J}(0)=w_{0}
is then given by
J(t)=d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw_{0}
. If we left-translate back to the Lie algebra via
v_{L}(t)=d_{e}L_{\eta^{-1}(t)}J(t)
, the field
v_{L}
satisfies the system

(2.8)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}v_{L}=w\\
&\partial_{t}(\Ad^{*}_{\gamma}\Ad_{\gamma}w)+\ad^{*}_{w}(u_{0})=0\end{split}

with initial data
v_{L}(0)=0
,
\partial_{t}v_{L}(0)=w_{0}
. We denote the solution operator of this Cauchy problem by

(2.9)

\Phi(t):T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}\rightarrow T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s},\qquad\Phi(t)w_{0}=v_{L}(t)

and recall the following result from
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}
with
\Lambda(t)
and
K_{0}
the operators defined by the formulas

(2.10)

w\mapsto\Lambda(t)w=\Ad^{*}_{\gamma(t)}\Ad_{\gamma(t)}w\qquad\text{ and }\qquad w\mapsto K_{0}(w)=\ad^{*}_{w}u_{0}.

Then the solution operator
w\mapsto\Phi(t)w=td_{e}L_{\gamma(t)}^{-1}d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})w
can be decomposed as

(2.11)

\Phi(t)=\Omega(t)+\Gamma(t)

where

(2.12)

\Omega(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda(\tau)^{-1}\ d\tau\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Gamma(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda(\tau)^{-1}K_{0}\Phi(\tau)\ d\tau.

3.2.
Fredholm Properties of the Exponential Maps

Recall that a bounded linear operator
F
between Banach spaces is Fredholm if its range is closed and its kernel and cokernel are finite-dimensional. The difference of the dimensions of these two spaces is a topological invariant known as the index of
F
. The operator is semi-Fredholm if either of the finiteness requirements on its kernel or cokernel is dropped. Throughout the proceeding arguments, we will also use
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X}
to denote the operator norm on the space of bounded linear operators on
X
, which should be clear from the context.

A map
f
between Banach manifolds is said to be Fredholm if its differential is a Fredholm operator on corresponding tangent spaces. If the source manifold is connected then we define the index of
f
to be the index of its differential
df
. Standard references for the above are
[
9
,
17
]
.

The goal of this section is to establish the following theorem, which extends to fractional orders the results of
[
11
]

Theorem 3.2
.

For
s>3
and
0\leq\beta<1
, the exponential map on the group of exact volume-preserving diffeomorphisms,
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
, induced by the metric (
3.1
) is a non-linear Fredholm map of index 0.

The value
\beta=1
is critical in the sense that the exponential map fails to be Fredholm. We will return to this in the following section.

(3.11)

\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\gtrsim c_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-C_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}-\left\lVert\Gamma(t)w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}},

for
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s^{\prime}}
and
w\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
.

If we now assume that
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
we may approximate
u_{0}
by a more regular field
\tilde{u}_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s^{\prime}}
such that
\left\lVert u_{0}-\tilde{u_{0}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}<\varepsilon
. As the exponential map is smooth in the
\dot{H}^{s}
topology, its derivative
u_{0}\mapsto d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})
must depend smoothly on
u_{0}
and therefore be locally Lipschitz. Hence, from the triangle inequality and (
3.11
) with appropriately modified
\tilde{c}_{T}
,
\tilde{C}_{T}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(t\tilde{u}_{0})tw\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw-d\exp_{e}(t\tilde{u}_{0})tw\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\tilde{c}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-\tilde{C}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}-\big\lVert\tilde{\Gamma}(t)w\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad-t\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})-d\exp_{e}(t\tilde{u}_{0})\right\rVert_{L(\dot{H}^{s})}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{c}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-\tilde{C}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}-\big\lVert\tilde{\Gamma}(t)w\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-T\mathcal{C}_{T}\varepsilon\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

where
\mathcal{C}_{T}
denotes a uniform (in time) upper bound on the Lipschitz constant of
u_{0}\mapsto d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})
. As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary we then have

(3.12)

\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\gtrsim\tilde{c}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}-\tilde{C}_{T}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}-\big\lVert\tilde{\Gamma}(t)w\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

With this estimate in hand, it now follows that
d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})
has finite-dimensional kernel and closed range in
T_{\gamma(t)}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
. The argument is standard. To see why this is true note that, if
d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})w_{k}
converges in
\dot{H}^{s}
with
w_{k}
a bounded sequence in
\dot{H}^{s}
, then
w_{k}
must contain a subsequence which converges in
\dot{H}^{s-1}
as well as a subsequence for which
\tilde{\Gamma}(t)w_{k}
converges in
\dot{H}^{s}
. We may assume without loss of generality that both subsequences are simply
w_{k}
. Substituting
w_{N}-w_{M}
into (
3.12
), we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert w_{N}-w_{M}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw_{N}-d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})tw_{M}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert w_{N}-w_{M}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}+\big\lVert\tilde{\Gamma}(t)(w_{N}-w_{M})\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

Consequently
w_{k}
is Cauchy in
\dot{H}^{s}
and hence converges. Now, if the kernel were infinite-dimensional it would contain an orthonormal sequence with a convergent subsequence, which is a contradiction. Hence,
\dim\ker d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})<\infty
.

Next we decompose
T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
into a
\dot{H}^{s}
orthogonal sum
\ker(d\exp_{e}(tu_{0}))\oplus X^{s}_{u_{0}}
. There exists a constant
c>0
such that, for all
w
in
X^{s}_{u_{0}}
, we have
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq c\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}
which implies closed range. If such a constant did not exist then there would be a sequence
w_{k}
in
X^{s}_{u_{0}}
such that
\left\lVert w_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}=1
and
\left\lVert d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})w_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}<\frac{1}{k}
containing a convergent subsequence. Passing to the limit and using the fact that
X^{s}_{u_{0}}
is closed, this would imply that there exists
w_{\infty}\in X^{s}_{u_{0}}
with
\left\lVert w_{\infty}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}=1
and
d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})w_{\infty}=0
, a contradiction.

The above shows that, for
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
, the operator
d\exp_{e}(tu_{0})
is semi-Fredholm. Finally, as at
t=0

d\exp_{e}(0)
is the identity on
T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
, the theorem follows from the fact that the index function is continuous on the space of semi-Fredholm operators.
∎

3.3.
Conjugate Points and the Breakdown of Fredholmness

As mentioned in the Introduction, the distinction between two- and three-dimensional ideal hydrodynamics can be observed through the Fredholm properties of the
L^{2}
exponential map. For example, Fredholmness in the 2D case precludes accumulation of conjugate points along finite geodesic segments, while such clustering is known to occur in the 3D setting, cf.
[
8
,
14
]
. In this section we amplify Theorem
3.2
by establishing, via the Hydrodynamical Morse Index Theorem, cf.
[
11
]
, an upper bound on the number of conjugate points along a finite geodesic segment for the
\beta<1
metrics; precluding clusters of such points. As the interpolation parameter approaches the critical value
\beta=1
, this upper bound approaches infinity. We also provide an explicit example to illustrate that this bound is attained. This shows that the breakdown of Fredholmness occurs precisely at
\beta=1
; in analogy with 3D ideal hydrodynamics.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
0\leq\beta<1
. Given any initial velocity
u_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
let
\gamma(t)
be the corresponding geodesic of the metric (
3.1
), which exists for at least some time
T>0
. The number of conjugate points along the segment
\gamma|_{[0,T]}
is bounded above by
\aleph_{\beta}=\aleph_{\beta}(u_{0},T)<\infty
.

As in
[
11
]
consider the set of smooth (in time) vector fields along
\gamma|_{[0,T]}
which vanish at the endpoints

(3.13)

\Big\{V:[0,T]\xrightarrow{C^{\infty}}T\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}\ |\ V(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}\text{ with }V(0)=V(T)=0\Big\}.

Let
\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}
denote the completion of this space under the norm induced by the inner product

\langle V,W\rangle_{\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}}=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}V,\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}W\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\,dt

where
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}
denotes the covariant derivative operator of the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
metric in
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
. The index form along
\gamma
is then a symmetric bilinear operator on
\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}\times\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}
given by

(3.14)

I_{\beta}(V,W)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\left\langle\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}V,\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}W\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}-\left\langle\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(V),W\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\Big)\ dt

where
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\cdot)=\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\dot{\gamma})\dot{\gamma}
is the curvature tensor of the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
metric along
\gamma
in
\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
. We recall the following result from
[
14
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The index form (
3.14
) can be rewritten as

(3.15)

I_{\beta}(V,W)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\left\langle\Ad_{\gamma}\partial_{t}v,\Ad_{\gamma}\partial_{t}w\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}+\left\langle K_{0}(v),\partial_{t}w\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\Big)\,dt

where
V=d_{e}L_{\gamma}v
,
W=d_{e}L_{\gamma}w
with
v,w\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
and
K_{0}
is given in (
2.10
).

As in finite-dimensional Riemannian geometry, the Hydrodynamical Morse Index Theorem in
[
11
]
states that the number of conjugate points along the geodesic segment
\gamma\big|_{[0,T]}
is equal to the dimension of the space on which this index form is negative definite. Hence, it will suffice to construct a finite-dimensional subspace,
X_{\beta}
, of
\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}
such that the index form
I_{\beta}
is non-negative on its
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
-orthogonal complement,
Y_{\beta}
. We proceed to the proof of the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Using Lemma
3.4
, for
W=d_{e}L_{\gamma}w\in\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}
we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(W,W)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\left\lVert\Ad_{\gamma}\partial_{t}w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}+\left\langle K_{0}(w),\partial_{t}w\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\Big)\ dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\delta\left\lVert\partial_{t}w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}+\left\langle K_{0}(w),\partial_{t}w\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\Big)\ dt

where
\displaystyle\delta=\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\Ad_{\gamma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{-2}>0
is independent of
\beta
.

In order to simplify the above expression we let
w(t)=e^{-\frac{t}{2\delta}K_{0}}v(t)
for some vector field
v(t)
. Clearly both fields vanish on the same subset of
[0,T]\times M
. Furthermore, since
K_{0}
is anti-symmetric in the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
inner product, we have that
e^{-\frac{t}{2\delta}K_{0}}
is an orthogonal operator and hence preserves the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
norm. Hence from (
3.16
) we have

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(W,W)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta\Big\lVert e^{-\frac{t}{2\delta}K_{0}}\Big(\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{2\delta}K_{0}(v)\Big)\Big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\langle e^{-\frac{t}{2\delta}K_{0}}K_{0}(v),e^{-\frac{t}{2\delta}K_{0}}\Big(\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{2\delta}K_{0}(v)\Big)\Big\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\bigg)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta\big\lVert\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{2\delta}K_{0}(v)\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}+\big\langle K_{0}(v),\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{2\delta}K_{0}(v)\big\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}\bigg)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta\left\lVert\partial_{t}v\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\left\lVert K_{0}(v)\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}\bigg)\ dt.

Expanding
\displaystyle v(t,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi t}{T}\right)v_{k}(x)
where
v_{k}
are time-independent fields we then have

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(W,W)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi t}{T}\right)\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi t}{T}\right)\left\lVert K_{0}(v_{k})\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}\bigg)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\left\lVert K_{0}(v_{k})\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}\right).

Expressing each
v_{k}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{k}
and using an analogous estimate to (
3.2
), namely

(3.17)

\displaystyle\left\lVert K_{0}w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\lVert\nabla(-\Delta)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\psi_{u_{0}}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{\rho}}\big\lVert\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{w}\big\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}

for any
w\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
and
\rho>1+\frac{\beta}{2}
, we have

(3.18)

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(W,W)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\left\lVert\psi_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1-\beta/2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\left\lVert K_{0}(\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{k})\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\left\lVert\psi_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1-\beta/2}}^{2}-\frac{C}{4\delta}\left\lVert\psi_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{\beta/2}}^{2}\right),

with
C>0
is a constant (independent of
\beta
) and
u_{0}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{0}\in T_{e}\mathscr{D}_{\mu,\text{ex}}^{s}
.

Finally, we expand each
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{k,n}\phi_{n}(x)
where
a_{k,n}
are constants and
\phi_{n}
are non-constant eigenfunctions of the Laplacian with eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{n}\nearrow\infty
, normalized so that
\left\lVert\phi_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1
and for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi_{k}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\psi_{k}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\Big\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{k,n}\lambda_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\phi_{n}\Big\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{k,n}^{2}\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}.

The estimate (
3.18
) becomes

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(W,W)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{k,n}^{2}\lambda_{n}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\lambda_{n}^{1-\beta}-\frac{C}{4\delta}\right).

It follows that if

\frac{\delta k^{2}\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}\lambda_{n}^{1-\beta}-\frac{C}{4\delta}\geq 0

for each
(k,n)
such that
a_{k,n}\neq 0
, then we have
I_{\beta}(W,W)\geq 0
. This condition is equivalent to

(3.19)

\lambda_{n}\geq\left(\frac{CT^{2}}{4\delta^{2}k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}.

Notice that, for fixed
\beta<1
, as
\lambda_{n}\nearrow\infty
and since the right hand side of (
3.19
) is a decreasing function of
k
, there can be only finitely many
k
, say
1\leq k\leq k_{\text{max}}
, for which (
3.19
) fails to hold for all
n\geq 1
. If we consider such
k
, we can approximate the number of
n
for which (
3.19
) does not hold using the Weyl Law
[
22
]
which states that

N(\lambda)=\#\{n\ |\ \lambda_{n}\leq\lambda\}\approx\frac{\mu(M)}{2\pi}\lambda+O(1).

Let
A=\big\{(k,n)\ |\ \eqref{lambda requirement}\text{ does not hold}\big\}
and
X_{A}=\text{span}\big\{\sin\left(\frac{k\pi t}{T}\right)\phi_{n}\big\}_{(k,n)\in A}
with closure under the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
norm denoted by
X_{A,\beta}
. Observe now that

\displaystyle\aleph_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\dim X_{A,\beta}=\#A=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\text{max}}}N\left(\left(\frac{CT}{4\delta^{2}k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}\right)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\text{max}}}\left(\frac{CT}{4\delta^{2}k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}+O(1)<\infty.

Finally, since we have the
\dot{H}^{-\beta/2}
orthogonal decomposition
\dot{\mathscr{H}}^{1}_{\beta}=X_{A,\beta}\oplus Y_{A,\beta}
with
I_{\beta}\big|_{Y_{A,\beta}}\geq 0
, the number of conjugate points along
\gamma|_{[0,T]}
is bounded above by
\aleph_{\beta}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above is

Corollary 3.5
.

We have

\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 1}\aleph_{\beta}=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 1}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\text{max}}}\left(\frac{CT}{4\delta^{2}k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-2\beta}}+O(1)=\infty

We conclude with an example illustrating the number of conjugate points along a finite geodesic segment approaching infinity as
\beta\rightarrow 1
.

Example 3.6
.

Following Washabaugh
[
21
]
we consider the case of
M={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
with spherical coordinates
(\vartheta,\varphi)
. The rotation
\partial_{\vartheta}
is then a steady state solution to (
1.1
) for any
0\leq\beta\leq 1
with corresponding stream function
-\cos(\varphi)
. Note that this is in fact an eigenfunction of the Laplacian with eigenvalue
2
. Letting
w=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
,
v_{L}=\nabla^{\perp}\sigma
and noting that
\gamma(t,\vartheta,\varphi)=(\vartheta+t,\varphi)
, (
2.8
) now becomes

(3.20)

\partial_{t}\sigma=\psi,\qquad\partial_{t}(-\Delta)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\psi+2^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\partial_{\vartheta}\psi=0

with initial condition
\sigma(0)=0
.

Taking
\psi(t,\vartheta,\varphi)=\xi(t)\phi_{n}(\vartheta,\varphi)
where
\phi_{n}
is a spherical harmonic such that
(-\Delta)\phi_{n}=n(n+1)\phi_{n}
and
\partial_{\vartheta}\phi_{n}=in\phi_{n}
then gives:

(3.21)

\partial_{t}\sigma=\psi,\qquad(n(n+1))^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\partial_{t}\xi+in2^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\xi=0

whose solutions, with
\xi(0)=1
, are given by

(3.22)

\xi(t)=e^{-int\left(\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}}

and

(3.23)

\sigma(t)=\left(\frac{i}{n}\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\right)\left(e^{-int\left(\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}}-1\right)\phi_{n}.

Notice now that
\sigma
vanishes at
T_{n}(\beta)=\frac{2\pi}{n}\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}
and hence
\gamma(T_{n}(\beta),\vartheta,\varphi)
is conjugate to the identity. In particular if
0\leq\beta<1
we see these points are spread out, whilst if
\beta=1
, we have
T_{n}(1)=\pi\sqrt{\frac{2(n+1)}{n}}
; forming a sequence of times, and indeed conjugate points, which cluster at
T=\pi\sqrt{2}
.

There are several natural continuations of this work. One is the study of degeneracy phenomena of the induced geodesic distance. For the

[

. This raises the question: for which
