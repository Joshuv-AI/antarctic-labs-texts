A Study of Continual Learning Under Language Shift
Evangelia Gogoulou, Timothée Lesort, Magnus Boman, Joakim Nivre

Abstract
The recent increase in data and model scale for language model pre-training has led to huge training costs. In scenarios where new data become available over time, updating a model instead of fully retraining it would therefore provide significant gains. In this paper, we study the benefits and downsides of updating a language model when new data comes from new languages – the case of continual learning under language shift. Starting from a monolingual English language model, we incrementally add data from Norwegian and Icelandic to investigate how forward and backward transfer effects depend on the pre-training order and characteristics of languages, for different model sizes and learning rate schedulers. Our results show that, while forward transfer is largely positive and independent of language order, backward transfer can be either positive or negative depending on the order and characteristics of new languages. To explain these patterns we explore several language similarity metrics and find that syntactic similarity appears to have the best correlation with our results.



1
Introduction

Large-scale pre-training on large amounts of text data is the current paradigm for training language models. Even though these models have been shown to have good generalization capabilities for various downstream tasks, they will sooner or later become obsolete given the current rate of producing new data sources that differ from the data used to train the model
Lazaridou et al. 2021
. Continual learning methods aim to update a model with new data while retaining the previous model knowledge in different scenarios of data distribution shift
Parisi et al. 2019
;
Hadsell et al. 2020
;
Lesort et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
.

In this paper, we focus on the specific case of language shift, where each new data source is in a different language, and study how the model learns and forgets when sequentially trained on multiple languages. More precisely, we study the training of generative language models with standard next-word prediction objectives. We evaluate their capacity to predict the next token in an increasing number of languages. From a continual learning perspective, this multilingual scenario is particularly interesting since the distribution shift happening between linguistically dissimilar languages is expected to have a big impact on the model knowledge. In addition, it allows us to study the cross-lingual transfer effects as a monolingual model becomes progressively multilingual.

The main question we investigate in this paper is: How do the characteristics of languages and their order in the task sequence influence model performance? We focus on how the model performs on new languages – forward transfer – and on previous languages – backward transfer.
We do this by training generative language models on data from English, Icelandic and Norwegian, in different sequential orders. Furthermore, we investigate whether increasing the model capacity alleviates catastrophic forgetting of previous languages or changes the transfer effects in other respects. Before we study the transfer effects in depth, we investigate what learning rate policy is best suited for the language shift scenario. Finally, in an attempt to explain the transfer effect patterns, we explore several language similarity metrics, including a novel metric based on token distribution similarity.

Our contributions are the following:

•

We propose a multilingual scenario as a challenging corner case
for the study of continual pre-training of language models.

•

We show that, in this scenario, despite the distribution shifts, there is in general positive forward transfer from earlier to later languages, improving over monolingual baselines.

•

We also show that, by contrast, the existence of positive or negative backward transfer is dependent on the specific languages and the order in which they are added.

•

We find that, in our setup, the optimal learning rate policy for continual learning coincides with the optimal policy for transfer.

•

We find that increasing model size improves the final performance on all languages, but does not significantly impact backward transfer.

•

We study the relation between transfer effects and language similarity and find that syntactic similarity is best aligned with our results.

2
Related Work

Our work investigates continual pre-training of multilingual models. In this section, we first review related work on continual learning, move on to continual pre-training of language models, and finally discuss related work on multilingual models with a focus on continual multilingual approaches.

Continual Learning

Continual learning in the deep neural network literature generally addresses the problem of learning without forgetting on sequences of datasets
French 1999
;
Lange et al. 2019
;
Lesort et al. 2020
;
Hadsell et al. 2020
.
The most classical set-up for studying this problem is image classification
Rebuffi et al. 2017
;
Aljundi et al. 2019
;
Douillard et al. 2021
;
Hayes et al. 2020
.
However, recent progress in natural language processing has led to an increased interest in continual learning also in this area
Caccia et al. 2021
;
Ke and Liu 2022
;
Scialom et al. 2022
;
Winata et al. 2023
;
de Masson d'Autume et al. 2019
.
Notably, the increased availability of large pre-trained models, such as BLOOM
(
Scao et al. 2022
)
, Gopher
(
Rae et al. 2021
)
, Pythia
Biderman et al. 2023
) and LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, provides good foundations for continual learning where just slight adaptations are needed for downstream tasks.
Nevertheless, those large pre-trained models still have shortcomings when sequences of tasks are too different from the pre-training data or learning objectives
Ostapenko et al. 2021
;
Abnar et al. 2021
.
Consequently, in this paper, we are interested in the continual pre-training of language models where the whole model is continuously trained. We also study the influence of model size on continual performance as in several previous studies
Ramasesh et al. 2022
;
Ostapenko et al. 2021
;
Mirzadeh et al. 2021
;
Lesort et al. 2022
.

Continual Language Pre-Training

Continual pre-training of language models has been applied for example to data that evolves through time
Han et al. 2021
;
Jin et al. 2022
;
Jang et al. 2021
;
Jang et al. 2022
;
Loureiro et al. 2022
. In this setup, one of the challenges is to disentangle knowledge that is outdated and can be updated from the knowledge that is time-invariant.
In our setup, we assume all the factual knowledge to be invariant and are interested in incorporating more and more knowledge while minimizing forgetting as in domain adaptation pre-training
Ke et al. 2023
;
Scialom et al. 2022
;
Gururangan et al. 2021
;
Qin et al. 2022
.
One of the advantages of continual pre-training is that the learning objective is self-supervised, therefore it could lead to a scalable approach that could automatically accumulate knowledge through long sequences of tasks
Lesort et al. 2022
;
Scialom et al. 2022
.
As the capacity of algorithms to continuously learn highly depends on the data distribution shift between training stages
Lesort et al. 2021
, we study the sequential multilingual setup, as a corner case where the input distribution significantly changes between training stages. Indeed, the change of language will deeply change the token distribution. We argue that this type of scenario can be used as a benchmark to evaluate continual pre-training algorithms. The harder distributional shift between languages is expected to make pre-training more challenging than with monolingual data.

Multilingual Language Models

Multilingual Transformer-based language models are typically pre-trained jointly on massive amounts of data in multiple languages. Standard examples of multilingual Transformer encoder models are mBERT
Devlin et al. 2019
and XLM-R
Conneau et al. 2020
, while mT5
Xue et al. 2021
is an encoder-decoder architecture for multiple languages. Following the recent development of Transformer decoder architectures, several multilingual GPT-style
Radford et al. 2018
models have been proposed, such as BLOOM
Scao et al. 2022
, a
176B
parameter Transformer decoder trained on
59
different languages and GPT-SW3
Ekgren et al. 2023
, a suite of generative language models, ranging from
126M
to
40B
parameters, which are trained on
320B
tokens from a mix of Nordic languages and English.
Joint pre-training is used as a baseline for measuring the transfer effects between languages in our setup, while our work focuses on multilingual models where each new language is added to the model one by one, starting from scratch. This setup is motivated by the huge potential cost benefit of updating a large-scale language model with neither forgetting nor re-training. Our model architecture, data, and training process closely follow GPT-SW3
Ekgren et al. 2023
.

Related to continual learning applied to pre-trained multilingual models,
Badola et al. 2022
propose a method for continual fine-tuning of mBERT on new multilingual training data from the same downstream task but with an unbalanced distribution between languages.
Garcia et al. 2021
study the scenario of continual machine translation for multiple languages, while
Coria et al. 2022
investigate catastrophic forgetting in the setup of continual fine-tuning mBERT on two sequence labelling tasks, one language at a time. Related to that,
Liu et al. 2021
propose a method based on gradient episodic memory that retains the language modeling performance of mBERT, while fine-tuning the model for sentence retrieval.

Mao et al. 2022
propose a novel continual multilingual fine-tuning method, applied to XLM-R, which opts for both minimum forgetting in the source languages and reduction of the fine-tuning loss.
Concurrent with our work,
Winata et al. 2023
studies continual pre-training of language models. However, they mostly aim at improving algorithmic solutions while we investigate the transfer effects between languages with simple continual training techniques.

Method

We start with the learning scenario and general setup and then move on to technical details concerning model architecture, training and evaluation procedures, datasets, and learning rate scheduling. We conclude by introducing the language similarity metrics used in our analysis of the experimental results. More details about hyperparameter settings and datasets can be found in Appendix
A
–
B
, while information about computational infrastructure and resource consumption is given in Appendix
D
.

3.1
Learning Scenario and Setup

We consider a scenario where a generative language model is trained in successive stages given a data input stream
D={D_{1},D_{2},\ldots D_{n}}
. In this scenario, each
D_{i}
contains data from a different language. After each stage, we evaluate the model performance on each of the languages seen during training by computing the cross-entropy loss on the corresponding hold-out test sets. In order to study how forward and backward transfer effects depend on the order and characteristics of the languages involved, we run multiple experiments where we systematically vary the order of languages.

In this study, we experiment with data from three languages, namely English (en), Icelandic (is), and Norwegian (no). We consistently take the initial data
D_{1}
from English, and then add Icelandic and/or Norwegian in all possible orders, which gives us four different training sequences: en_is, en_is_no, en_no, en_no_is.
All models are randomly initialized before being trained on the first language of the sequence. For each successive continual learning stage
i
(
i>1
), all model parameters are initialized from the last checkpoint obtained after stage
i-1
and then updated using the standard next-step prediction language modeling objective with data batch
D_{i}
as input.

The size of the training sets is constant across languages, which allows us to study the effect of language shift independently from data set size. Regarding tokenization, we make the assumption that a multilingual tokenizer, covering all the languages studied, is available to us before training. This is often the case in practice as well, given the abundance of openly available tokenizers with large language coverage
Conneau et al. 2020
;
Scao et al. 2022
, although it is an unrealistic assumption for many low-resource languages.

For comparison, we also train two types of baseline models: one monolingual model for each language (en, is, no), trained on the corresponding training batch
D_{i}
, and one multilingual model (en+is+no) trained on the concatenation of the three training sets
D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3}
.

3.4
Learning Rate

As part of our experiments, we investigate which learning rate policy works best for our continual learning setup. We are interested in both the policy that leads to the lowest overall loss (on all languages), known as the continual performance, and the loss on the latest language learned, the local performance or transfer.

An important question is how to resolve the different practices adopted in continual learning and NLP. In the continual learning tradition, the learning rate schedule is normally either kept constant
Ke et al. 2023
;
Lesort et al. 2022
;
Scialom et al. 2022
or progressively decreased
Winata et al. 2023
;
Mirzadeh et al. 2020
.
By contrast, in the NLP setup, the learning rate is often first warmed up and then decreased
Ruder 2016
.

In our experiments, we are not interested in a consistent decrease in learning rate, since it could not be scaled to a high number of tasks without vanishing. Therefore, we experiment with constant learning rate and cosine annealing for the second and third training stages of the
126
M models (see Figure
1
). The learning rate used in the first training stage always follows the cosine annealing policy. Following
Ekgren et al. 2023
for the cosine annealing scheduler, we start by warming up the learning rate for
250
steps, increasing its value to the maximum value,
6\mathrm{e}{-4}
and then decreasing it according to the cosine decay function until reaching its minimum value,
6\mathrm{e}{-5}
, where it will stay constant for the last
4900
steps. The second learning rate policy tested is keeping it constant at
6\mathrm{e}{-5}
, which is the last learning rate value of the cosine annealing scheduler.

Our hypothesis here is that the constant learning rate will keep the model parameters closer to the model initialization at the beginning of the training stage, leading to a smaller catastrophic forgetting effect in the previous languages.

3.5
Language Similarity Metrics

One of our hypotheses is that the degree of shift happening when moving from one language to another will have a significant effect on the model knowledge in the different languages. In order to investigate this hypothesis, we explore two types of metrics for estimating the similarity between languages and their distributions.

First, the linguistic similarity between two languages is used, as estimated by the precomputed distance
2
2

2

http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dmortens/projects/7_project/
of their corresponding
lang2vec
language vectors which are part of the URIEL database
Littell et al. 2017
. Different types of features are considered, as follows:
syntax
(SYN) vectors that encode syntactic features,
inventory
(INV) vectors that include phonetic features,
phonology
(PHON) vectors that represent phonological properties of the language.
3
3

3

While phonetic and phonological features are not directly relevant for written texts, they can be seen as proxies for orthographical features.

Second, we introduce a data-driven metric of similarity between two language corpora, which we call
token distribution similarity
(TDS). The idea is that similarity should take into account the difference in token distributions between two datasets. Hence, we create one vector per dataset, counting the number of occurrences for each token, and then compute the cosine similarity between vectors of different datasets. Note that we use this metric in a multilingual setup, but it could also be used to compare datasets in the same language. This is similar to the metric of
Garcia et al. 2021
, which estimates the similarity between two vocabularies by the percentage of token overlap between them. The advantage of our method is that we do not take into account only the list of common tokens, but also the frequency of their occurrence.

Given a sample of
50K
sequences for each language, we first tokenize it using the model tokenizer and then create a vector where position
i
corresponds to the frequency of vocabulary token
i
in the sample. The next step is to compute the cosine similarity of the two token frequency vectors, which is used as a proxy for language similarity.

The similarity values between pairs of languages en, no, and is are shown in Table

. Interestingly, the degree of similarity of each pair of languages varies with the metric. Norwegian and Icelandic have the highest similarity according to the TDS metric, but their similarity value according to the URIEL features is mixed: their syntactic similarity is the lowest of all three language pairs, but they have highly similar phonological and phonetic features. Our hypothesis is that positive transfer will be observed for languages with the highest TDS value, as this metric derives from the overlap between the data inputs, but we also expect syntactic similarity to be relevant for next-token prediction.

4.2.1
Forward Transfer

One part of our main research question is whether pre-training on the first two languages helps the third one, independently of order. Figure
2
illustrates the progression of model test loss on Icelandic and Norwegian when learned in the second or third training stage, in comparison to the monolingual baseline (first stage). This shows evidence of forward transfer from past languages to new languages as performance improves when the language is learned late in the sequence. The only case of negative transfer is when Icelandic is the second language as input to the
126
M model. We hypothesize that this happens because of the low similarity with the first language (English), at least the syntactic one as discussed in Section
3.5
, in combination with the limited model capacity.

Clear improvement in the loss is observed when the language is learned later in the sequence, with the exception of Icelandic for the

4.2.2
Backward Transfer

The second part of our main research question is what happens to previously seen languages when a new language is learned. Figure
3
visualizes the evolution of model test loss in English for the models trained on sequences en_is_no and en_no_is. The first observation we can make is that the loss follows a pattern that does not relate to the position of the language in the sequence, but to the choice of language. More specifically, training the model on Icelandic leads to an increase in the English test loss, which is a sign of catastrophic forgetting. On the other hand, we observe that training on Norwegian transfers positively to English, leading to a reduction in English test loss. In the interesting case of en_is_no, the model is able to recover its language modeling performance in English at the third training stage, even after the performance degradation caused by training on Icelandic. As seen in Figure
4
, Norwegian and Icelandic transfer negatively to one another independently of the order they are positioned in the second and third places in the sequence.

5
Discussion

One of our main results is that the specific ordering of languages in the sequence matters, especially for backward transfer. Icelandic stands out as the language with the strongest forgetting effect on both other languages. Interestingly, Icelandic and Norwegian have the highest overlap in their token distributions, as measured by the novel TDS metric introduced in Section
3.5
. This contradicts our initial hypothesis that the forgetting effects will be weaker for pairs of languages with high TDS similarity, and we have to conclude that the TDS metric is not predictive of the backward transfer observed in our continual learning setup. Instead, we find that syntactic similarity (SYN), as reflected in the metric derived from URIEL
Littell et al. 2017
, is a more relevant factor since it indicates that English and Norwegian are more similar to each other than to Icelandic. By contrast, phonetic (INV) and phonological (PHON) similarity, which is high between Icelandic and Norwegian for historical reasons, is clearly not relevant.

One potential risk factor that affects the interpretation of our experimental results, and especially the positive backward transfer from Norwegian to English, is language contamination.
Blevins and Zettlemoyer 2022
find that standard English corpora contain a significant amount of non-English text, which is enough to enable the cross-lingual transfer of English models to other languages. The surprisingly low test loss of the Norwegian monolingual model on English, as seen in Table
4
, could indicate the presence of English text in the Norwegian corpus, despite the basic language identification applied to the corpus used
Öhman et al. 2023
. At present, we cannot say whether this is the case and, if so, whether its effect is stronger than that of syntactic similarity and other linguistically motivated factors.
Finding ways to disentangle these factors will be important going forward.

In our experimental setup, we opted for consistently using the existing multilingual GPT-SW3 tokenizer
Ekgren et al. 2023
because it is openly available and our corpus is included in the data used for training the tokenizer. However, a multilingual tokenizer typically comes with the drawback of some languages, most often the low-resource ones, being underrepresented in the vocabulary.
Stollenwerk 2023
evaluate the quality of tokenization of the GPT-SW3 tokenizer measured as the average number of tokens that a word is decomposed to (fertility score) and report that the tokenization quality of Icelandic text is clearly lower than for English and Norwegian texts. Conclusion

In this paper, we study forward and backward transfer in a language modeling task – standardly used for pre-training of large language models – in a novel multilingual continual learning scenario. Our experimental results with two model sizes (
126
M and
356
M) and three languages (English, Norwegian, Icelandic) indicate that there is a consistent positive forward transfer that facilitates learning new languages through time. However, depending on the choice of languages, and the order in which they are learned, new languages may result in either positive or negative backward transfer. In an attempt to further analyze the transfer patterns observed, we look into the similarity between the language distributions from both a data-driven and linguistic perspective and find that syntactic similarity correlates best with the backward transfer effects. When increasing the model size, we find that, while model performance improves for all languages, forgetting is not reduced.

We hope that our findings will help in the design of large-scale continual learning for language models either in multilingual or monolingual setups. The next step would be to explore longer task sequences with larger models to assess how our findings hold at different scales. In addition, we need to explore a larger and more diverse set of languages to assess the generality of the transfer patterns. As a more long-term goal, this could pave the way for realizing the potential to improve resource efficiency while reducing negative transfer in the computationally extensive setup of continual language model pre-training.
