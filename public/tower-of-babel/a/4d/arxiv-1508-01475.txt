Abstract
We present results for Drell-Yan production from the Geneva Monte-Carlo framework. We combine the fully-differential NNLO calculation with higher-order resummation in the 0 0 -jettiness resolution variable. The resulting parton-level events are further combined with parton showering and hadronization provided by Pythia 8. The 0 0 -jettiness resummation is carried out to NNLL ′ , which consistently incorporates all singular virtual and real NNLO corrections. It thus provides a natural perturbative connection between the NNLO calculation and the parton shower regime, including a systematic assessment of perturbative uncertainties. In this way, inclusive observables are correct to NNLO, up to small power corrections in the resolution cutoff. Furthermore, the perturbative accuracy of 0 0 -jet-like resummation variables is significantly improved beyond the parton shower approximation. We provide comparisons with LHC measurements of Drell-Yan production at 7 ​ TeV 7\,\mathrm{TeV} from ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb. As already observed in e + ​ e − e^{+}e^{-} collisions, for resummation-sensitive observables, the agreement with data is noticeably improved by using a lower value of α s ​ ( M Z ) = 0.1135 \alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.1135 .

Introduction

There are several different ways to obtain theoretical predictions for collider processes involving strongly interacting particles, namely fixed-order (FO) perturbation theory, resummed perturbation theory, and predictions using parton shower algorithms.

In FO perturbation theory, the perturbative expansion is carried out to a given order in the strong coupling constant
\alpha_{\rm s}
. The leading order (LO) corresponds to the lowest order in
\alpha_{\rm s}
required to obtain the desired process. The next-to-leading order (NLO) then includes all terms of
\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{\rm s})
relative to the LO result, and so on.

When the process or observables in consideration involve large ratios of physical scales, FO perturbation theory can become unreliable or even break down, since for each order in
\alpha_{\rm s}
one can encounter up to two powers of logarithms of large scale ratios. In this case, the most precise perturbative predictions are obtained by resumming the logarithmic terms to all orders in
\alpha_{\rm s}
. At leading logarithmic (LL) order one includes the most dominant terms of order
\alpha_{\rm s}^{n}\ln^{2n}
. The next-to-leading logarithmic (NLL) order also includes the next-largest subleading logarithmic terms, and so on. Like at FO, the resummed perturbative expansion happens in a systematic fashion, which is described in more detail later on.

Parton shower (PS) algorithms formally work in the strongly-ordered limit, where each subsequent emission happens at a smaller resolution scale than the previous one. They start from a given LO prediction and effectively multiply it by splitting probabilities for each additional emission. This allows in principle any observable to be predicted at LL (and including some NLL effects) using a probabilistic Markov-Chain algorithm.

Each type of prediction has its advantages as it provides the most accurate description in the limit where other predictions are not applicable. FO predictions are necessary for a precise description of additional hard (i.e. energetic wide-angle) emissions. These are not correctly described even at LO by either resummation or parton showers, since both of these approaches rely on a kinematic expansion in a small resolution variable. For observables sensitive to many soft and collinear emissions generating large logarithms, FO predictions are not suitable and resummation and parton showers are necessary. Resummed perturbation theory allows one to systematically carry out the resummation beyond the LL or strongly-ordered limit and is therefore more accurate than parton showers. On the other hand, the higher-order resummation applies at the level of observables that are still sufficiently inclusive, i.e., that marginalize over many emissions and are only sensitive to a small number of physical scales. To obtain a prediction of the final state that is fully exclusive in all emissions, parton showers are required. In particular, parton shower predictions allow one to further attach a hadronization model to generate fully exclusive hadron-level events. These are an essential requirement for experiments to be able to simulate their data and study detector effects, and allow for the most direct comparison with experimental data.

Clearly, to obtain the best possible predictions, it is desirable to combine the different theoretical descriptions in such a way that one benefits from the advantages of each. In particular, this allows for reliable predictions also in the transition regions in between different parametric limits, which are often important in practice. The (often difficult to answer) question which parametric regime is the most appropriate in a given phase-space region then becomes much less relevant for practical purposes.

Combining resummed and fixed-order perturbation theory is a standard procedure in higher-order resummed calculations, where it is well understood how to systematically match the pure resummed result to the fixed-order result in the appropriate fixed-order limit.

The goal of combining higher-order perturbative calculations with a parton shower is to obtain fully-exclusive hadronized events that also have the perturbative accuracy of the higher-order calculations. The perturbative matching essentially happens now from FO to resummation to parton shower. This provides several key benefits:

•

A systematic combination of NNLO calculations with parton showers, which extends to arbitrary processes.

•

An improved perturbative accuracy of (sufficiently inclusive) resummation observables,

•

an improved description of the matching/transition between resummation and fixed-order regimes,

•

a systematic assessment of perturbative uncertainties in the resummation and matching.

2
Theoretical Framework

2.1
General Setup

In this subsection we discuss the general construction we use. Our discussion here follows that of Ref.
Alioli et al. 2014
, and we refer to that paper for more details.

Drell-Yan production at NNLO receives contributions from partonic processes with up to two final-state partons. In a standard FO calculation, the phase space for these partonic contributions is integrated over in such a way that the cancellation of virtual and real IR divergences happens at the level of observables.

In contrast, an event generator is meant to produce physical events, which means that all IR divergences should cancel on a per-event basis. This implies that an
N
-parton event should correspond to an IR-finite partonic
N
-jet cross section, which is fully-differential in the corresponding partonic
N
-jet phase space. That is, each generated event represents a point in an
N
-jet phase space, rather than an
N
-parton phase space, and each four-momentum in the event provides the energy and direction of a partonic jet.

Therefore, the basis of the
Geneva
MC framework is the formulation of the perturbative inputs in terms of “Monte Carlo (MC) cross sections”, which are well-defined partonic jet cross sections according to which the events are distributed, and which can be systematically computed to the desired perturbative accuracy in FO and resummed perturbation theory
Bauer et al. 2008a
;
Bauer et al. 2008b
;
Alioli et al. 2013
.

The MC cross sections are defined in terms of an
N
-jet resolution variable
\mathcal{T}_{N}
and formally include the contributions of an arbitrary number of unresolved emissions below a resolution cutoff

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,.

\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2}))=\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})\,.

(13)

In other words, if this was not the case, we would not obtain the correct nonsingular corrections for the

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{resum}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})+\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{match}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,.

Here,

For the

At this level, we can write the resummed contributions as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{resum}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}};\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\geq 1}^{C}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}\,U_{1}(\Phi_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{resum}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\geq 1}^{C}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}\,U_{1}^{\prime}(\Phi_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})\Big|_{\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})}\!\!\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{2})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,.

(15)

Here,
U_{1}(\Phi_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})
denotes the Sudakov factor that resums the dependence on
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}}
to at least LL accuracy, and

denotes its derivative with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}}

\mathcal{T}_{1}

The differential

The

(Its full form will include the

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\geq 2}^{\mathrm{match}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{B_{2}(\Phi_{2})\,[1-\Theta^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{1}<\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})]

2.2
Implementation Details

2.2.1
Choice of the jet resolution variables

We choose
N
-jettiness
Stewart et al. 2010b
as our
N
-jet resolution variables. It is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{N}=\sum_{k}\min\Bigl\{\hat{q}_{a}\cdot p_{k},\hat{q}_{b}\cdot p_{k},\hat{q}_{1}\cdot p_{k},\ldots,\hat{q}_{N}\cdot p_{k}\Bigr\}\,,

(22)

where the sum over
k
runs over all final-state particles, excluding the vector boson and all its decay products. We use a canonical geometric measure, where
\hat{q}_{i}=n_{i}=(1,\vec{n}_{i})
are lightlike reference vectors along the jet and beam directions.

N
-jettiness is a global event shape that is explicitly designed as an
N
-jet resolution variable, as it measures the degree to which the final state is
N
-jet-like for a given
N
and automatically clusters the final state into
N
jet and beam regions in an IR-safe way and without any dependence on an additional jet clustering algorithm. Furthermore, it is theoretically well understood and its all-orders singular structure and resummation are known.

For Drell-Yan, we need
0
-jettiness
\mathcal{T}_{0}
and
1
-jettiness
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, and we always evaluate Eq. (
22
) in the longitudinally boosted frame where the vector boson has zero rapidity. The reference vectors for the beam directions are always along the beam directions, so
\vec{n}_{a}=\hat{z}
and
\vec{n}_{b}=-\hat{z}
. For
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, we also need to define a jet direction
\vec{n}_{J}
. For our calculation we only need to define it for a
\Phi_{2}
event, in which case we can choose
\vec{n}_{J}
by minimizing
\mathcal{T}_{1}
.
We define a clustering metric

\displaystyle d(p)

\displaystyle=\lvert\vec{p}\rvert-\lvert p^{z}\rvert\,,

\displaystyle d(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\lvert\vec{p}_{1}\rvert+\lvert\vec{p}_{2}\rvert-\lvert\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2}\rvert\,,

(23)

and find the smallest of
d(p_{1})
,
d(p_{2})
, and
d(p_{1},p_{2})
, where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are the two parton momenta in the
\Phi_{2}
event. If
d(p_{i})
is smallest, we can think of
p_{i}
being clustered with one of the beams, and then
\vec{n}_{J}=\vec{p}_{j}/\lvert\vec{p}_{j}\rvert
with
j\neq i
. If
d(p_{1},p_{2})
is the smallest, we can think of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
being clustered together, and then
\vec{n}_{J}=(\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2})/\lvert\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2}\rvert
. It is not hard to show that this minimizes
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and its final value is determined by the minimum metric as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(\Phi_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}(E_{k}-\lvert p_{k}^{z}\rvert)+\min\bigl\{d(p_{1}),d(p_{2}),d(p_{1},p_{2})\bigr\}\,.

(24)

2.2.2
The
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum at NNLL
′
from SCET

The all-orders parton-level factorization theorem for
0
-jettiness (aka beam thrust) is given by
Stewart et al. 2010a
;
Stewart et al. 2011

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm SCET}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{ij}^{B}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}H_{ij}(Q^{2},\mu)\int\!\mathrm{d}t_{a}\,\mathrm{d}t_{b}\,B_{i}(t_{a},x_{a},\mu)

\displaystyle\quad\times B_{j}(t_{b},x_{b},\mu)\,S\Bigl(\mathcal{T}_{0}-\frac{t_{a}+t_{b}}{Q},\mu\Bigr)\,.

(25)

Here,
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{ij}^{B}/\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}
is the Born cross section for the
ij\to Z/\gamma^{*}\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
hard scattering. The hard function
H_{ij}(Q)
contains the corresponding Born and virtual squared matrix elements, and the sum runs over all possible
q\bar{q}
pairs
ij=\{u\bar{u},\bar{u}u,d\bar{d},\bar{d}d,\ldots\}
. The
B_{i}(t,x)
are inclusive (anti)quark beam functions
Stewart et al. 2010a
, with the momentum fractions
x_{a,b}
given in terms of the total rapidity
Y
and invariant mass
Q\equiv m_{\ell^{+}\ell^{-}}
of the vector-boson final state by

x_{a}=\frac{Q}{E_{\rm cm}}\,e^{Y}\,,\qquad x_{b}=\frac{Q}{E_{\rm cm}}\,e^{-Y}\,.

(26)

The beam functions are computed perturbatively in terms of standard PDFs
f_{j}
, schematically
B_{i}=\sum_{j}\mathcal{I}_{ij}\otimes f_{j}
. Finally,
S(k)
is the quark hemisphere soft function for beam thrust.

Equation (
2.2.2
) is derived using Soft Collinear Effective Theory (SCET)
Bauer et al. 2000
;
Bauer et al. 2001
;
Bauer and Stewart 2001
;
Bauer et al. 2002a
;
Bauer et al. 2002b
. Its key feature is that each of
H
,
B
,
S
only depend on a single characteristic scale. Therefore, there are no large logarithms in their perturbative expansion when each is evaluated at its own characteristic scale, which is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{H}=Q\,,\quad\mu_{B}=\sqrt{Q\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,,\quad\mu_{S}=\mathcal{T}_{0}\,.

(27)

In Eq. (
2.2.2
), all ingredients have to be evaluated at a common scale
\mu
, which is arbitrary and whose dependence exactly cancels between the different functions. The renormalization group evolution in the effective theory is then used to evolve each function from its own scale to
\mu
. This gives the resummed
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum used in Eq. (
2.1.1
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{ij}^{B}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}H_{ij}(Q^{2},\mu_{H})\,U_{H}(\mu_{H},\mu)

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigl[B_{i}(x_{a},\mu_{B})\otimes U_{B}(\mu_{B},\mu)\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigl[B_{j}(x_{b},\mu_{B})\otimes U_{B}(\mu_{B},\mu)\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\otimes\bigl[S(\mu_{S})\otimes U_{S}(\mu_{S},\mu)\bigr]\,,

(28)

where we have now written the convolutions between the different functions in schematic form.

The RGE factors
U_{X}(\mu_{X},\mu)
resum the large logarithmic terms, and the functions evaluated at their own scale provide the boundary conditions for the evolution. At NNLL
′
accuracy, we need the boundary conditions at 2-loop order, and the evolution to 3(2)-loops in the cusp (noncusp) anomalous dimensions. All required expressions are known in the literature
Idilbi et al. 2006
;
Becher et al. 2007
;
Stewart et al. 2010c
;
Monni et al. 2011
;
Kelley et al. 2011
;
Hornig et al. 2011
;
Gaunt et al. 2014a
;
Gaunt et al. 2014b
;
Kang et al. 2015
;
Gaunt et al. 2015
and we do not reproduce them here.

In practice, the canonical scales in Eq. (
27
) are appropriate in the resummation region, where the singular corrections dominate. In Fig.
1
, we compare the singular, nonsingular, and full results for the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
distribution at NNLO
0
. We can see that up to
\mathcal{T}_{0}\lesssim 50\,\mathrm{GeV}
the singular dominate and the nonsingular are suppressed by an order of magnitude or more. On the other hand, in the FO region for larger values of
\mathcal{T}_{0}\sim Q
, the resummation must be turned off since here the singular terms being resummed become meaningless and there are large cancellations between the singular and nonsingular terms, which must be preserved to reproduce the correct FO result. This is clearly visible in Fig.
1
for
\mathcal{T}_{0}\gtrsim 80\,\mathrm{GeV}
, where the magnitude of the singular and nonsingular are larger than the full result (the singular turn negative at the dip at
\mathcal{T}_{0}\simeq 70\,\mathrm{GeV}
). For this reason the resummation must be turned off in this region. This is done by taking all scales to be equal to the common FO scale
\mu_{S}=\mu_{B}=\mu_{H}=\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}
. A smooth transition between the canonical and FO limits is achieved by using profile scales
Ligeti et al. 2008
;
Abbate et al. 2011
, where
\mu_{B}
and
\mu_{S}
are smooth functions of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, interpolating between the exact canonical scaling in Eq. (
27
) and the FO scale. The uncertainties from the resummation and the transition between resummation and FO can be estimated by choosing different sets of profile scales, which provide a sensible variation around the central scale choice. In other words, for each event we perform the calculation of its MC cross section with a different set of profile scales, producing different weights for the event, and the variation in these define our event-by-event uncertainties as described next.

Profile scales and their variations are used in many SCET calculations and have been shown to provide reliable perturbative uncertainty estimates for resummed and matched predictions for observables in a variety of contexts, see e.g. Refs.
Abbate et al. 2011
;
Berger et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2014
;
Jouttenus et al. 2013
;
Gangal et al. 2015
;
Neill et al. 2015
. For our central profiles we take

\displaystyle\mu_{H}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{S}(\mathcal{T}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}f_{\rm run}(\mathcal{T}_{0}/Q)\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{B}(\mathcal{T}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}\sqrt{f_{\rm run}(\mathcal{T}_{0}/Q)}\,,

(29)

where the common profile function
f_{\rm run}(x)
is as in Ref.
Stewart et al. 2014
,

\displaystyle f_{\rm run}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}x_{0}\bigl[1+(x/x_{0})^{2}/4\bigr]&x\leq 2x_{0}\,,\\
x&2x_{0}\leq x\leq x_{1}\,,\\
x+\frac{(2-x_{2}-x_{3})(x-x_{1})^{2}}{2(x_{2}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{1})}&x_{1}\leq x\leq x_{2}\,,\\
1-\frac{(2-x_{1}-x_{2})(x-x_{3})^{2}}{2(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}&x_{2}\leq x\leq x_{3}\,,\\
1&x_{3}\leq x\,.\end{cases}\,,

(30)

which has strict canonical scaling below
x_{1}
and the resummation is turned off above
x_{3}
. From Fig.
1
we choose for our central scale the parameters

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}

\displaystyle=Q\,,\qquad x_{0}=2.5\,\mathrm{GeV}/Q\,,

\displaystyle\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}

\displaystyle=\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}\,.

(31)

For the FO variations we vary
\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}
up and down to
2Q
and
Q/2
, taking the maximal absolute deviation in the results from the central value as the FO uncertainty. For our resummation uncertainties, we use the profile variations designed for
\mathcal{T}
-like resummation variables from Ref.
Gangal et al. 2015
, which give us four independent up and down variations for
\mu_{B}
and
\mu_{S}
that probe deviations from the canonical scaling but never violate the parametric scaling
\mu_{B}^{2}\sim\mu_{S}\mu_{H}
and turn off beyond
x_{3}
. In addition, we include two more profiles where we vary all
x_{i}
transition points by
\pm 0.05
, giving us a total of 6 resummation profile variations. We then take the maximal absolute deviation in the result from the central value among all 6 profiles as the resummation uncertainty. The total perturbative uncertainty is obtained by adding the FO and resummation uncertainties in quadrature. Note that the profile scales depend on the underlying
\Phi_{0}
point through the value of
Q
, but we choose them to be independent of
Y
.

The same resummation formalism can be used to obtain an expression for the resummed cumulant of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum in Eq. (
2.1.1
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(32)

Here, we first integrate the resummed expression for the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
distribution in Eq. (
2.2.2
), and then choose the scales using the same profile scales but as a function of
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
. In particular, in the resummation region, the canonical values are

\displaystyle\mu_{H}=Q\,,\quad\mu_{B}=\sqrt{Q\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}}\,,\quad\mu_{S}=\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}\,.

(33)

Since the operations of integrating the factorization theorem and choosing the scales do not commute with one another, the resulting expression for the cumulant is not exactly equal to the integral of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum, where the scales would be chosen before performing the integration. The resulting differences are formally always of higher order, beyond NNLL
′
in our case (see e.g. Ref.
Almeida et al. 2014
for a detailed discussion). However, they can be numerically relevant, especially if one aims at preserving the NNLO
0
total cross section as accurately as possible.

In principle, one could enforce the equality between the cumulant and the integral of the spectrum in Eq. (
32
) exactly, by simply defining the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum as the derivative of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
cumulant. However, this can give rise to unreliable uncertainty estimates in the spectrum as well as a poor description of the region of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
where the resummation is turned off and FO predictions are valid
Abbate et al. 2011
. In
Geneva
we circumvent these problems by adding to the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum the quantity

\displaystyle\kappa(\mathcal{T}_{0})\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}(\mathcal{T}_{0},\mu_{h})-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}(\mu_{h})\right]\,,

(34)

which is explicitly beyond NNLL
′
order. The profile scales
\mu_{h}
are chosen to turn off the resummation somewhat earlier than the profile scales for the rest of the resummed calculation, in order to preserve the accurate spectrum prediction in the tail of the distribution. Furthermore,
\mu_{h}
and its derivative are smooth functions of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, which is needed because the difference of resummed terms in Eq. (
34
) is proportional to
\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}

Almeida et al. 2014
. The function
\kappa(\mathcal{T}_{0})
is a function that smoothly interpolates from a constant value for
\mathcal{T}_{0}\ll Q
to zero at the point where
\mu_{h}(\mathcal{T}_{0})=Q
, and further reduces the effects of the higher order correction terms in the tail of the distribution. By adjusting the constant value at small
\mathcal{T}_{0}
within
\mathcal{O}(1)
factors, the overall size of these terms can be adjusted (in practice this constant value is approximately 2).

These higher-order terms may be used to capture the correct FO scale uncertainties in the inclusive cross section upon integrating over the full spectrum. We use the same profile for each of the scale variation described above, but scaled by the value of
\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}/Q
(so 1 for the central scale and resummation scale variations, 2 for the up FO scale variation, and 1/2 for the down FO scale variation). By adjusting the constant value of
\kappa(\mathcal{T}_{0}\to 0)
separately for each
\mu_{\mathrm{FO}}/Q
value, we can correctly obtain the total integrated FO cross section for each FO scale variation.

Technically, each of the resummation scale variations should yield strictly the same total cross section, such that the resummation uncertainties vanish for inclusive observables. However, in practice, the profile scale variations are primarily meant and designed to capture the resummation uncertainties in the peak and transition regions. Implementing a constraint that they all reproduce exactly the same numerical integral of the resummed spectrum as the central profile is very challenging, especially since the resummation uncertainties in the peak region can be sizeable, while the inclusive cross section uncertainties are very small. (Alternatively, we could separately finetune the
\kappa(\mathcal{T}_{0})
function for each of the different resummation profiles.) Instead, what we will do is to simply drop the resummation uncertainties whenever we consider fully inclusive observables.

2.2.6
NLO
1
calculation and phase-space map

The last point addressed in this section concerns the phase-space map
\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})
that is used to project a partonic phase-space point
\Phi_{2}
onto the
\Phi_{1}
point in the NLO
1
calculation, see Eqs. (
2.1.1
) and (
2.1.2
). It is also used in the
\mathcal{T}_{1}
resummation in Eq. (
2.1.2
). Just requiring to have some IR-safe map as usual for fixed-order calculations (like for example the FKS map
\Phi_{1}^{C}(\Phi_{2})
used in the
C_{2}
subtraction term in Eq. (
2.1.2
)) is not enough anymore.

As discussed around Eq. (
13
), we have the additional constraint that the
\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}
map must preserve the value of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
in order for the NLO
1
calculation to be consistent with the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
resummation. This property was also important for the
1/2
-jet separation to preserve the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
resummation.

Technically, the only real-emission integral that we have to perform as part of the NLO
1
calculation is the integral in Eq. (
2.1.2
). We perform this integral numerically by Monte-Carlo integration over the radiation phase space for a given
\Phi_{1}
event. The
B_{2}
and
C_{2}
terms in Eq. (
2.1.2
) are allowed to use different maps, since all IR-safe maps agree in the IR-singular limit. However, we have to be able to invert both maps, i.e., we have to reconstruct all
\Phi_{2}
points that would project to the given
\Phi_{1}
point. We then parametrize the radiation phase space for both maps in terms of a common set of radiation variables, which are then Monte-Carlo sampled. While the usual FKS maps are designed to be invertible, the invertibility turns out to be a very nontrivial requirement on the
\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})
map.

Formally, we only need to preserve
\mathcal{T}_{0}
for the singular terms, i.e., we can use any map which preserves a variant of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
with the same singular structure to the order we want to carry out the resummation, while any nonsingular difference is captured by the nonsingular corrections. Therefore, to enable us to invert the map, we hold a recursive definition of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
fixed, which is effectively defined by the action of the map,

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\rm FR}(\Phi_{2})=\mathcal{T}_{0}[\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})]\,.

(41)

The map itself is constructed as follows: We first cluster (by simply adding/subtracting four-momenta) the two partons in
\Phi_{2}
that set the
\mathcal{T}_{1}
value, i.e., that are closest according to the
N
-jettiness metric. If the two final-state partons are clustered (FSR clustering), then this yields a
\Phi_{1}
point with an off-shell final-state momentum. If one of the final-state partons in
\Phi_{2}
is closest to a beam direction (ISR clustering), then this yields a
\Phi_{1}
point with one of the incoming momenta off shell (and off axis). Next, we compute
\mathcal{T}_{0}
on this off-shell
\Phi_{1}
point. Finally, we construct an on-shell
\Phi_{1}
point that has the same
\mathcal{T}_{0}
value and the same
q^{\mu}
of the vector boson. With
q^{\mu}
and
\mathcal{T}_{0}
given, the resulting
\Phi_{1}
point is fully determined by four-momentum conservation and on-shell conditions.
Hence, by construction, the map also preserves the momentum of the vector boson
q^{\mu}(\Phi_{2})=q^{\mu}(\Phi_{1}^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2}))
.
It is easy to see that this map is IR safe, and it is sufficiently simple that it can be inverted.

For all ISR clusterings as well as FSR clusterings with both partons in the same hemisphere (defined by the vector-boson rapidity) this definition of
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\rm FR}
is identical to the exact value of
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})
. The only case where it differs is for FSR clusterings where the two final-state partons are in opposite hemispheres.
In the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
-singular limit, this can only happen for soft emissions, and hence will in principle affect the constant terms in the two-loop soft function. (Collinear emissions in the beam functions always correspond to ISR clusterings.) However, this can only happen in a small region of phase space where the soft emitter was already accidentally close in rapidity to the vector boson. We therefore expect this to be a small effect, which we have verified numerically. We can therefore safely ignore it for our resummation and simply take it into account as part of the nonsingular matching corrections.

Finally, we need to comment on the
\Theta^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})
appearing in Eq. (
12
). It is easy to see that an on-shell massless
\Phi_{1}
point must satisfy
\mathcal{T}_{0}\leq\lvert\vec{q}_{T}\rvert
. However, there can be
\Phi_{2}
points that can have a larger
\mathcal{T}_{0}
value, and hence such
\Phi_{2}
points cannot be projected onto a massless
\Phi_{1}
point. The
\Theta^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})
is defined to be
1
for points that can be projected and
0
otherwise. Equivalently, these points also cannot be reached by the inverse map from splitting a
\Phi_{1}
point, and so are never part of the integral in Eq. (
2.1.2
). Since the map is IR safe, the nonprojectable
\Phi_{2}
region is nonsingular and is included in Eq. (
2.1.2
) by the
1-\Theta^{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{2})
constraint.

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{N\to N+1}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{N+1}}(\mathcal{T}_{N}>\Lambda_{N},\mathcal{T}_{N}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{N}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{N\to N}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{N}}(\mathcal{T}_{N}^{\mathrm{cut}};\mathcal{T}_{N})\right]\,\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{N+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\theta(\mathcal{T}_{N}^{\mathrm{cut}}>\mathcal{T}_{N}>\Lambda_{N})\,,

(47)

using a Sudakov factor
U_{N}
and splitting probability
\mathcal{P}
to define a branching and no-branching probability as in a unitary parton shower. The first term is now an exclusive
N
-jet cross-section with a jet resolution at
\Lambda_{N}
, which can be much smaller than the
\mathcal{T}_{N}^{\mathrm{cut}}
value used in
Geneva
. This process can also be iterated further.

Applying this to our
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{1}
events, and adding all emissions up to two partons, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}};\Lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,U_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\Lambda_{0})\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\Lambda_{0},\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}},\Lambda_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})\,U_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}},\Lambda_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{0}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}};\mathcal{T}_{0})\,\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{1})\,\theta(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}>\mathcal{T}_{0}>\Lambda_{0})\,U_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\rm max},\Lambda_{1})\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\Lambda_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\Lambda_{1},\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{\geq 2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}>\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{1}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\textsc{mc}}_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}>\Lambda_{0},\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}},\mathcal{T}_{1})\,\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{2})\,\theta(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{\max}>\mathcal{T}_{1}>\Lambda_{1})\,.

(50)

The
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{\max}
in the theta-function in the last line above is either
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathrm{cut}}
or
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\rm max}
, depending on whether the derivative is on the first or the second term of Eq. (
49
). Once the first emissions have been performed as described above, the only remaining constraint on the parton shower is what is given in the last column of Table
1
, namely the constraint on the jet resolution variable. In practice, we will only apply this to the
\Phi_{1}
events, since as discussed below, the showering of the
\Phi_{0}
events is not affecting the accuracy of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
distribution.

In the remainder of this section we give an argument why the parton shower acting on the jet events as described above does not affect the NNLL
{}^{\prime}+
NNLO accuracy.
The
\Phi_{0}
events include all phase-space points for which
\mathcal{T}_{0}<\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
, and by definition
Geneva
is only predicting the normalization, and not the distribution in
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Therefore, as long as we constrain the parton shower to only shower these events up to
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
, the shower will exactly provide the missing
\mathcal{T}_{0}
shape below
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
which is integrated over by
Geneva
. On the other hand, due to the unitarity constraint, the shower will not change the normalization of the events below
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\mathrm{cut}}
, which is thus still the NNLL
{}^{\prime}+
NNLO
0
one predicted by
Geneva
.

By performing the first emission of our
\Phi_{1}
events as described above, and taking
\Lambda_{1}
as small as possible (we use a value of
0.1
GeV), the remaining 1-jet cross section is only about
0.1\%
of the total cross section.
The theoretical problem of how the shower affects the accuracy of this small contribution to the total cross section, which requires imposing all the constraints detailed above, can then be ignored for practical purposes.

What is therefore left to show is that the shower does not affect the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
distribution when showering
\Phi_{2}
events.
The
\mathcal{T}_{0}
distribution of the original
2
-body phase space point is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})]\,,

(51)

while the expression after the first emission done by the shower is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,U_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\Lambda_{2})\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})]

\displaystyle\quad+\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}_{3}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}}\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{3})]\,.

(52)

Clearly, thanks to the infrared safety of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, in the soft and collinear limit of
\Phi_{3}
one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{3})=\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})\,.

(53)

If the phase-space map of the parton shower were to preserve the value of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, the relation Eq. (
53
) would remain exact even away from the collinear and soft limits. The first emission done by the shower would then not change the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(54)

If the phase-space map of the shower does not preserve
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, instead, a difference between these two values exists. We can assume this difference to be proportional to
\mathcal{T}_{2}(\Phi_{3})
, which measures the distance from the soft and collinear limits

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{3})

\displaystyle=a(\Phi_{3})\,\mathcal{T}_{2}(\Phi_{3})\,.

(55)

Here
a(\Phi_{3})
is well behaved in the singular limit
of
\Phi_{3}
, because all the singular behavior is
incorporated in
\mathcal{T}_{2}(\Phi_{3})\to 0
. For the sake of simplicity
and to avoid rendering the notation too heavy we will drop the

(\Phi_{3})
dependence of
a
and treat it as constant in the remainder
of this section. The reader can easily convince themselves that the
argument given below will not be affected by this approximation.

This allows us to write

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,U_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\rm max},\Lambda_{2})\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0}]

\displaystyle\quad+\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}_{3}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}}\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0}+a\,\mathcal{T}_{2}(\Phi_{3})]\,.

(56)

Thanks to the normalization of the splitting probabilities, one can easily perform the integrals over the two radiation variables other than
\mathcal{T}_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}U_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\Lambda_{2})\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0}]

\displaystyle\quad+\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,\int_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\max}}\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\,U_{2}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\mathcal{T}_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0}+a\,\mathcal{T}_{2}]\,.

(57)

Next, the delta function is Taylor expanded to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=a\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,\delta^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})]\,

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\max}}\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{2}\,U_{2}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\mathcal{T}_{2})\,

\displaystyle=a\int\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}}\,\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime})\,\delta^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=-a\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle(\mathcal{T}_{0})\right]\,,

(58)

where we have defined the average
\mathcal{T}_{2}
value

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle\equiv\int_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\max}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{2}\,U_{2}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\mathcal{T}_{2})\,.

(59)

In the last lines of Eq. (
3
), we have written
\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle(\mathcal{T}_{0})
to make the dependence of the average value of
2
-jettiness on
\mathcal{T}_{0}
explicit.
In fact, one can derive this dependence using a simple LL Sudakov factor for the parton shower. Let us first consider a single emission governed by

\displaystyle U(\mathcal{T}^{\max},\mathcal{T})\sim\exp\left[-C\,\frac{\alpha_{\rm s}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\frac{\mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{T}^{\max}}\right]\,,

(60)

with
C
the appropriate color factor. We can now easily show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}^{\max}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}\,\mathcal{T}\,U^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}^{\max},\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle\sim\,\mathcal{T}^{\max}\left[1-\frac{e^{\pi/(4\alpha_{\rm s}C)}\pi\,{\rm Erfc}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm s}C}}\right)}{2\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm s}C}}\right]

\displaystyle\sim 2\,\frac{C\,\alpha_{\rm s}}{\pi}\,\mathcal{T}^{\max}+{\cal O}(\alpha_{\rm s}^{2})\,.

(61)

Iterating this over two emissions, we find

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{1}\,U_{1}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1})\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{2}\,U_{2}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\,2\,\frac{C_{2}\alpha_{\rm s}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{1}\,\mathcal{T}_{1}\,U^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\,4\,\frac{C_{1}C_{2}\,\alpha_{\rm s}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}_{0}+{\cal O}(\alpha_{\rm s}^{3})\,,

(62)

where we used the scaling
\mathcal{T}_{N}^{\max}\sim\mathcal{T}_{N-1}
.

We can also numerically extract the dependence of
\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle
on
\mathcal{T}_{0}
from an actual shower in the following way: Starting from the showered expression
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{3}^{S}/\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}
defined as in Eq. (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{3}^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}}\,\delta(\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0})\,\mathcal{T}_{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}}\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})]\,\int_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\max}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{2}\,U_{2}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{\rm max},\mathcal{T}_{2})\,

\displaystyle\quad=\int\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}}\,\delta[\mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime}]\,\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle(\mathcal{T}_{0})\,.

(63)

This gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle=\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\,\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{3}^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{3}}\,\delta(\mathcal{T}_{0}(\Phi_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{0})\,\mathcal{T}_{2}\,.

(64)

Taking the
\Phi_{2}
events from
Geneva
, running them through
Pythia
8, and calculating this ratio for various values of
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, we obtain a very good fit to a straight line with

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle\approx 0.06\,\mathcal{T}_{0}\,.

(65)

Taking advantage of the linear scaling of
\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle
with
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, and dropping the explicit dependence in the notation from here forward, we can now rewrite Eq. (
3
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=-a\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,\mathcal{T}_{0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\right]\,\frac{\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle}{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(66)

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}

\displaystyle=f(\mathcal{T}_{0})\frac{\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle}{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,,

(67)

where

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{T}_{0})

\displaystyle\equiv-a\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\ln\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\right]\,.

(68)

Since the dominant contribution to
\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\sim-\alpha_{\rm s}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}\,e^{-\alpha_{\rm s}\ln^{2}\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,,

(69)

we find

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{T}_{0})\sim\frac{1}{\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(70)

This gives our final result for the change of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum after the first emission done by the parton shower

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{S}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}\sim\frac{1}{\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\frac{\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\rangle}{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\sim\frac{\alpha_{s}^{2}}{\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(71)

Since the parton shower is strongly ordered in
\mathcal{T}_{N}
, in the sense discussed at the beginning of this section, all the subsequent emissions will not alter this result to the order we are working.

This result can be compared to the relative uncertainty in our NNLL
′
calculation of the
\mathcal{T}_{0}
spectrum, which can be estimated considering the dominant term beyond the NNLL
′
order. This term scales as
\alpha_{\rm s}^{3}/\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Comparing this to the first dominant one that we include in the NNLL
′
prediction, we find the relative uncertainty to be

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm NNLL^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{0}}}\sim\frac{\alpha_{\rm s}^{3}/\mathcal{T}_{0}}{\alpha_{\rm s}\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}/\mathcal{T}_{0}}\sim\frac{\alpha_{\rm s}^{2}}{\ln\mathcal{T}_{0}}\,.

(72)

Upon inspection of Eq. (
71
) and Eq. (
72
), we can see that the shift in the spectrum due to
Pythia
’s showering is of the same size as the missing higher-order terms in the perturbative calculation. Therefore, the showering only affects our prediction beyond the claimed accuracy.

In addition, we also include in our data comparisons
Geneva
results with a lower value of
\alpha_{\rm s}(M_{Z})=0.1135
, since as we will see, the value of
\alpha_{\rm s}(M_{Z})
can have a noticeable effect on resummation-sensitive observables. In the following, the results with
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.118
will be shown by the orange bands, while those with
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.1135

\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})

3
3

3

We hasten to add that using a different
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})
than the default value provided by the PDF set is not problematic as it may seem at first. In the resummation-dominated region, this only leads to a small inconsistency inside the beam functions, where the terms that are supposed to cancel the PDF’s
\mu_{F}
dependence will now use a different
\alpha_{s}
value leading to a small noncancellation in the coefficients of some single-logarithmic terms. It is safe to assume that this will not be numerically relevant compared to the dominant
\alpha_{s}
dependence coming from the double-logarithmic beam function terms, as well as the hard and soft function, and all the resummation kernels. All of these pieces are completely unrelated to the PDFs, and are in fact the same as for the corresponding
\mathcal{T}_{2}
resummation in
e^{+}e^{-}
collisions.

This value is motivated by the fact, that all
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})
determinations from LEP data that include higher-order resummation together with some form of analytic treatment of nonperturbative hadronization effects, find considerably lower values of
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})
than
0.118

Davison and Webber 2009
;
Gehrmann et al. 2010
;
Abbate et al. 2011
;
Abbate et al. 2012
;
Gehrmann et al. 2013b
;
Hoang et al. 2015a
;
Hoang et al. 2015b
, see also Ref.
Alekhin et al. 2012
for determinations from DIS.
Consistent with these findings, in Ref.
Alioli et al. 2013
it was observed that the higher-order resummation using
\alpha_{\rm s}(M_{Z})=0.1135
coupled with
Pythia
8’s hadronization model gave excellent agreement with data for
e^{+}e^{-}\to 2
-jet resummation observables, namely thrust,
C
-parameter, heavy-jet mass, and jet broadening.
On the other hand, the predictions with
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.118
gave consistently spectra that were somewhat too hard, i.e., shifted from lower to higher values compared to data. We will observe precisely the same qualitative trend here.

Aad et al. 2012

6
Conclusions

We have presented a combination of the fully-differential NNLO calculation for Drell-Yan production
pp\to\gamma/Z\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
combined with the NNLL
′
resummation of
0
-jettiness and interfaced with the parton shower provided by
Pythia
8 within the
Geneva
Monte-Carlo framework. The starting point of
Geneva
is the formulation of Monte-Carlo cross sections, which are IR safe partonic jet cross sections, defined using a jet resolution variable
\mathcal{T}_{N}
, which is chosen to be
N
-jettiness in our implementation. We include
0
-jet,
1
-jet, and
2
-jet cross sections at NNLO
0
, NLO
1
and LO
2
. Furthermore, the dependence on the 0-jet resolution variable
\mathcal{T}_{0}
is resummed to NNLL
′
accuracy, while that of the 1-jet resolution variable
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is presently resummed to LL.

Interfacing these partonic results with a parton shower such as
Pythia
8 requires a careful treatment. First, one needs to deal with the fact that the parton shower evolution variable is different from
N
-jettiness. Second, care has to be taken that the parton shower’s lower resummation accuracy and different phase-space map does not destroy the higher logarithmic accuracy of the calculated Monte-Carlo cross sections. We discussed in detail how to solve these issues by performing the first two shower emissions by hand using the jet-resolution parameter as ordering variable. We then showed that any subsequent showering by
Pythia
8 does not affect the formal perturbative accuracy included in
Geneva
.

We have validated the FO perturbative accuracy by comparing our results for inclusive
0
-jet observables to
Dynnlo
. We agree with this dedicated NNLO calculation within the small perturbative (and statistical) uncertainties. We also studied how
Geneva
’s improved perturbative accuracy in the resummation region from resumming
0
-jettiness to NNLL
′
, translates to other
0
-jet resummation variables, such as the transverse momentum of the vector boson
q_{T}
, the
\phi^{*}
between the two leptons, and the exclusive
0
-jet cross section as a function of the jet
p_{T}^{\mathrm{cut}}
. Since
Geneva
partially relies on the parton shower for these observables, they formally do not have NNLL
′
accuracy. Nevertheless, we find that
Geneva
reproduces dedicated NNLL resummations for these three observables rather well, and in particular the
Geneva
predictions are much closer to the exact NNLL
+
NLO
1
results than to the NLL
+
LO
1
results. This is a clear indication that the gain in resummation accuracy for
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, when implemented into a fully exclusive prediction, also translates into more accurate predictions for other
0
-jet resolution variables.

Finally, we presented a comparison of
Geneva
with measurements by ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb using the
7\,\mathrm{TeV}
LHC data. Since
Geneva
agrees with the NNLO results for the rapidity distribution of the vector boson, the agreement between
Geneva
and the LHC data is very similar to that observed when comparing to other NNLO calculations. Here, we note that experimental measurements of the lepton
p_{T\ell}
spectrum would be very valuable.

We find good agreement between
Geneva
and the ATLAS measurement of exclusive jet cross sections and of the transverse momentum distribution of the hardest jet. For the transverse momentum of the vector boson or the
\phi^{*}
between the leptons our predictions when using the default value of
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.118
are lower in the peak region and higher in the transition region behind the peak compared to the measurements. The same trend has been observed before by other MC predictions and also dedicated resummed calculations for these observables. When using a lower value
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.1135
, we observe a noticeable improvement in agreement with data for essentially all resummation-sensitive observables. A similar effect was observed previously for a variety of
e^{+}e^{-}

2
-jet resummation observables. We believe this deserves further attention.

We encourage the experiments to also measure
\mathcal{T}_{0}
-like jet-based variables (see Ref.
Gangal et al. 2015
) and to also perform differential measurements of resummation observables at much higher
m_{\ell^{+}\ell^{-}}
values. Since the resummation type and regime for these cases are very different, they would add valuable complementary information for theory comparisons. In general, continued precise measurements of resummation observables at the LHC are essential for our understanding of higher-order QCD effects in exclusive and differential observables, which ultimately will also provide important inputs for the theoretical interpretation of Higgs measurements and new-physics searches.
