Rate-Optimal Robust Estimation of High-Dimensional Vector Autoregressive Models
Di Wang, Ruey S. Tsay

Abstract
High-dimensional time series data appear in many scientific areas in the current data-rich environment. Analysis of such data poses new challenges to data analysts because of not only the complicated dynamic dependence between the series, but also the existence of aberrant observations, such as missing values, contaminated observations, and heavy-tailed distributions. For high-dimensional vector autoregressive (VAR) models, we introduce a unified estimation procedure that is robust to model misspecification, heavy-tailed noise contamination, and conditional heteroscedasticity. The proposed methodology enjoys both statistical optimality and computational efficiency, and can handle many popular high-dimensional models, such as sparse, reduced-rank, banded, and network-structured VAR models. With proper regularization and data truncation, the estimation convergence rates are shown to be almost optimal in the minimax sense under a bounded ( 2 + 2 ​ ϵ ) (2+2\epsilon) -th moment condition. When ϵ ≥ 1 \epsilon\geq 1 , the rates of convergence match those obtained under the sub-Gaussian assumption. Consistency of the proposed estimators is also established for some ϵ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \epsilon\in(0,1) , with minimax optimal convergence rates associated with ϵ \epsilon . The efficacy of the proposed estimation methods is demonstrated by simulation and a U.S. macroeconomic example.



Keywords
: Autocovariance, high-dimensional time series, minimax optimal, robust statistics, truncation

Introduction

1.1
High-dimensional vector autoregression

Vector autoregressive (VAR) models are arguably the most commonly used multivariate time series models in practice; see, e.g.,
Lütkepohl, 2005
,
Tsay, 2013
, and the references therein. Applications of the model can be found in a wide range of fields, such as economics and finance
(
Wu and Xia, 2016
)
, time-course functional genomics
(
Michailidis and d’Alché Buc, 2013
)
, and neuroimaging
(
Gorrostieta et al., 2012
)
.
Consider a
p
-dimensional zero-mean VAR model of order
d
, i.e., VAR(
d
) model,

\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{y}_{t-2}+\cdots+\mathbf{A}_{d}\mathbf{y}_{t-d}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},~~~~t=1,2,\dots,T,

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1t},\dots,y_{pt})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the observed time series,
\mathbf{A}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the lag-
j
coefficient matrix, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\varepsilon_{1t},\dots,\varepsilon_{pt})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a serially uncorrelated white noise innovation. We assume that all solutions of the determinant equation
|\mathbf{A}(z)|=0
are outside the unit circle, where
\mathbf{A}(z)=\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{A}_{1}z-\cdots-\mathbf{A}_{d}z^{d}
is referred to as the AR matrix polynomial in
z
. In modern applications, the dimension
p
is often large. However, since the number of coefficient parameters is
p^{2}d
and those coefficients are often highly correlated, an unrestricted VAR(
d
) model is likely to encounter the difficulty of over-parameterization and, hence, cannot provide reliable estimates nor accurate forecasts without further restrictions.

Estimation consistency of high-dimensional VAR models is achievable under some structural assumptions, provided that certain regularity conditions are satisfied. For example, if the coefficient matrices have an unobserved low-dimensional structure, such as sparsity or low-rankness, the structure-inducing regularization methods, including Lasso
(
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
)
, Dantzig selector
(
Han et al., 2015
)
, and nuclear norm penalty
(
Negahban and Wainwright, 2011
)
, give consistent estimates under the Gaussian assumption of the time series. Recently,
Zheng and Raskutti, 2019
,
Zheng and Cheng, 2021
, and
Wang et al., 2021b
developed novel technical tools to relax the distributional assumption from Gaussian to sub-Gaussian.

However, in real applications, time series data often contain aberrant observations, which can occur in many ways, such as missing values, measurement error contamination, and heavy-tailed distribution. Those aberrant observations, if overlooked, can lead to biased estimates, erroneous inference, and sub-optimal forecasts. The situation can easily be further exacerbated when the dimension
p
is large. It is, therefore, important to study robust estimation of high-dimensional VAR models. In addition, the true data generating process is unlikely to follow a VAR model. Model uncertainty, including using a high-dimensional VAR(
d
) model as an approximation to the true model, also deserves a careful investigation.

Recently there have been emerging interests in studying high-dimensional VAR models with non-
i.i.d.
and/or non-sub-Gaussian innovations. For example,
Wu and Wu, 2016
studied theoretical properties of Lasso and constrained
\ell_{1}
minimization estimators for a VAR model with weakly correlated and heavy-tailed
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
.
Wong et al., 2020
investigated the estimation and prediction performance of Lasso for the sub-Weibull time series data under a
\beta
-mixing condition. Both theoretical and numerical results in the literature show that the performance of standard
\ell_{1}
regularized estimators deteriorates substantially when the data have heavy tails. For robust estimation of high-dimensional heavy-tailed time series data,
Qiu et al., 2015
developed a quantile-based Dantzig selector for the class of elliptical VAR processes.
Han et al., 2020
proposed a robust estimation method for high-dimensional sparse generalized linear models with temporal dependent covariates. However, the existing literature on robust estimation for time series data focuses on sparse models. To the best of our knowledge, there is no unified solution to address the robust estimation problem for a large class of high-dimensional VAR models.

Our proposed robust estimation procedure is built on two key ingredients: the constrained Yule–Walker estimator and the robust autocovariance matrix estimator. The first ingredient, the constrained Yule–Walker estimator, provides a general and flexible estimation framework for two classes of high-dimensional models, namely the approximately low-dimensional VAR models and linear-restricted VAR models. The second ingredient, the robust autocovariance matrix estimator, is easy to implement by truncating the time series data. However, based on the specific model structure, we need to adapt the data truncation methods. With a large class of distributions having bounded second or higher-order moments, the proposed estimators are shown to be consistent under high-dimensional scaling. To be specific, we summarize the main contributions of this paper as follows:

(i)

For various high-dimensional VAR models, the paper provides a simple and general estimation procedure robust to model misspecification, heavy-tailed noise contamination, and conditional heteroskedasticity. Our proposal can handle many popular high-dimensional VAR models, such as sparse, reduced-rank, banded, and network VAR models. An efficient and scalable alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm is developed.

(ii)

The proposed methodology enjoys statistical optimality in the minimax sense. Our theoretical framework deals with heavy-tailed distributions with bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moments for any
\epsilon>0
. It results in a phase transition on the rates of convergence: for
\epsilon\geq 1
, the estimator achieves the same convergence rates as those obtained under the Gaussian or sub-Gaussian distribution, while consistency is also established with a slower rate for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. By establishing the matching minimax lower bounds, we show that the proposed estimators for high-dimensional VAR models and autocovariance matrices are rate-optimal.

1.2
Related literature

This work is related to a huge body of literature on the robust estimation of high-dimensional regression and covariance matrices. The early developments in robust statistics were pioneered by
Huber, 1964
and
Hampel, 1971
;
Hampel, 1974
; see also the overview by
Hampel, 2001
and the references therein. For time series data, robust estimation methods were developed for univariate and multivariate ARMA models
(
Martin, 1981
;
Muler et al., 2009
;
Muler, 2013
)
. For high-dimensional
i.i.d.
data, inspired by
Catoni, 2012
, the non-asymptotic deviation analysis for heavy-tailed variables and robust
M
-estimators for high-dimensional regression were proposed by
Fan et al., 2017
,
Loh, 2017
,
Sun et al., 2020
,
Wang et al., 2020
, and many others. These recent robust estimation methods and their theoretical results were further extended to high-dimensional covariance and precision matrix estimation problems, such as
Avella-Medina et al., 2018
,
Minsker, 2018
, and
Zhang, 2021
. Another research line of robust regression is the least absolute deviation loss, and more generally, quantile loss, which have been studied extensively; see, e.g.,
Wang et al., 2007
,
Belloni and Chernozhukov, 2011
, and
Wang et al., 2012
. Recently,
Fan et al., 2021
and
Ke et al., 2019
independently proposed truncated variants of the sample covariance, which are easy to implement and motivate us to develop the robust estimators of high-dimensional autocovariance matrices.

The upper and lower bound development for high-dimensional estimation problems under heavy-tailed distributions is another emerging and important research topic. Under heavy-tailed distributions, the phase transition phenomenon in the rate of convergence was previously discovered by
Bubeck et al., 2013
,
Avella-Medina et al., 2018
,
Sun et al., 2020
,
Tan et al., 2022
, and others. For the lower bound development, the phase transition phenomenon was first established by
Devroye et al., 2016
for univariate mean estimation problem, and was extended to the fixed and high-dimensional linear regression problems in
Sun et al., 2020
. Compared with the existing literature, our minimax lower bound results are established under a much more complicated setting. First, all existing lower bound results are developed for
i.i.d.
data, but we allow weak serial dependency and establish the minimax lower bounds under strong mixing conditions. Second, for the lower bounds of the linear regression problem in
Sun et al., 2020
, a finite moment condition is imposed on the random error terms while the covariates are assumed to be light-tailed. Without any assumption on data generating process, we consider the finite moment condition on the observed time series data that serve as both predictors and responses in the autoregressive models, which is fundamentally different from the conventional linear regression problem.

2
Constrained Yule–Walker Estimation

2.1
Yule–Walker equation

Consider a general mean-zero and covariance stationary process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In many applications, it is common to model the time series data and predict their future values using a linear VAR model.
The VAR(
d
) model in (
1
) can be rewritten as

\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{t-d}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}
is the predictor vector and
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{A}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
is the combined parameter matrix.
Throughout this paper, we consider that the lag order
d
is fixed.
Given the stationarity of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
, the parameter matrix of interest
\mathbf{A}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
is defined as the minimizer of the risk function

\mathbf{A}^{*}:=\underset{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}}{\arg\min}~\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(3)

For any integer
\ell
, denote the lag-
\ell
autocovariance matrix of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
by
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t-\ell}^{\top}]
.
By simple algebra,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=&\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\textup{vec}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top})\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})\|_{2}^{2}\\
=&~\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t}-2\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})+\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})\\
=&~\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t}-2\textup{vec}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top})^{\top}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})+\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}),\end{split}

(4)

which implies that
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}=\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
or
\mathbf{A}^{*}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}
if
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
is invertible, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]=[\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1},\mathbf{\Gamma}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d}]
and

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d-1}\\
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{1}^{\top}&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d-1}^{\top}&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d-2}^{\top}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

This relationship between the parameter matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}
and autocovariance matrices is well known as the multivariate Yule–Walker equation for VAR(
d
) models
(
Lütkepohl, 2005
;
Tsay, 2013
)
.
When the data generating mechanism of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
is not the VAR(
d
) process, the Yule–Walker equation still holds if VAR is used as a running model.

The existence of aberrant observations, such as missing values or data contaminated by measurement errors, is ubiquitous in high-dimensional macroeconomic, environmental, and
genetic data, among many other applications. In a VAR process, when some of the variables are removed as they cannot be observed or measured, the rest of the available variables generally would not follow any finite-order VAR model
(
Lütkepohl, 2005
)
. In addition, if the true signal of the time series follows a VAR process and the observed time series
\mathbf{y}_{t}
is a contaminated version of the true signal plus a measurement error,
\mathbf{y}_{t}
no longer follows a VAR process.
Hence, the VAR model assumption commonly used in real applications is questionable, but the Yule–Walker equation which holds without any data generating mechanism assumption can be utilized to construct estimation methodology for the general covariance stationary time series.

In addition to the sub-Gaussian innovation assumption, another key limitation in the existing literature is the
i.i.d.
assumption for the innovation series
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
. The conditional heteroskedasticity is often observed in financial time series, and violation of the homogeneous error assumption can affect the performance of standard estimation methods. Another major advantage of our moment-based methodology is that the
i.i.d.
assumption for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
can be relaxed to a serially uncorrelated, but weakly stationary condition. Specifically, our theoretical analysis allows some serial dependence in
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, dependence between
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, and conditional heteroskedasticity in
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
. Note that this setting of innovations includes many important conditional heteroskedasticity models, such as GARCH models
(
Engle, 1982
;
Bollerslev, 1986
)
and double AR models
(
Ling, 2004
;
Zhu et al., 2018
)
.

Based on the Yule–Walker equation, we propose a constrained minimization estimation approach, named the constrained Yule–Walker estimation, for two classes of high-dimensional VAR(
d
) models. In Section
2.2
, we consider the VAR(
d
) model, where
\mathbf{A}^{*}
in (
3
) can be approximated by a matrix with some latent low-dimensional structure. In Section
2.3
, we consider the VAR(
d
) model whose coefficients are subject to some linear restrictions. These two classes of models are shown to encompass many commonly-used high-dimensional VAR models in practice.

2.2
Approximately low-dimensional VAR

We first consider the situations where the dimension
p
is relatively large compared to the sample size
T
and the parameter matrix of interest
\mathbf{A}^{*}
in (
3
) can be well approximated by a matrix with certain types of low-dimensional structure. The approximately low-dimensional structure, such as weak sparsity and approximate low-rankness, is general and natural in high-dimensional time series modeling.

Based on the Yule–Walker equation, we consider a general class of constrained estimation:

\mathbf{\widehat{A}}=\underset{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}}{\arg\min}~\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})~~~\text{such that}~~\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0})\leq\lambda,

(6)

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is a matrix norm as the regularization function,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)
is its dual norm as the constraint function,
\lambda
is the constraint parameter, and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}
are robust autocovariance estimators to be specified later in Section
3
. When
\lambda
is sufficiently small, the constraint function
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)
guarantees that the sample version of the Yule–Walker equation holds roughly, and the regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
induces the low-dimensional structure and improves the estimation efficiency.

Following the framework for high-dimensional analysis in
Negahban et al., 2012
, we consider a decomposable regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
. For a generic low-dimensional structure, define
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{M}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is referred to as the model subspace to represent the specific model constraints; for instance, it can be the subspace of low-rank matrices (see Example
1
). The orthogonal complement of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}
, is the associated perturbation subspace to capture the deviation from the model subspace and is adopted to measure the error of approximation to the low-dimensional structure. We assume that the true value
\mathbf{A}^{*}
can be decomposed into its projections onto
\mathcal{M}
and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{A}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}+\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}}
.

We call a regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
decomposable with respect to a pair of subspaces
(\mathcal{M},\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp})
, if for any
\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}
,

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{W}_{2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}_{1})+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}_{2}).

(7)

Many of the commonly-used convex regularizers, such as the nuclear norm for low-rankness, are shown to be decomposable; see
Negahban et al., 2012
for more details of decomposable regularizers. To measure the magnitude of the low-dimensional structure, define by
\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}})
the associated constant such that
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})\leq\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}})\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W})
for any
\mathbf{W}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
. In our theoretical analysis, we consider another auxiliary matrix norm
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
such that
(\mathcal{R}(\cdot),\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot),\mathcal{C}(\cdot))
satisfy that

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{W}_{2})\leq\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}_{1})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{W}_{2})
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{W}_{2})\leq\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}_{1})\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}_{2})
for any symmetric matrix
\mathbf{W}_{2}
and any compatible matrix
\mathbf{W}_{1}
.
The following example illustrates the suitable choice of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)
, and
(\mathcal{M},\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp})
for a reduced-rank VAR model.

Example 1

(Reduced-rank VAR)
.

The reduced-rank VAR model is an important approach to modeling high-dimensional time series by imposing a low-rank structure on
\mathbf{A}
; see also
Velu and Reinsel, 2013
,
Basu et al., 2019
, and
Wang et al., 2021a
. To induce low-rankness, we use the nuclear norm as the regularizer and the operator norm as the constraint function; that is,
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{nuc}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
. By the submultiplicative property of the operator norm,
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
. For the approximately low-rank matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}
, denote by
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
the subspace spanned by its leading left and right singular vectors, respectively. Define the model subspace

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=\{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}:\text{col}(\mathbf{W})\subseteq\mathcal{U},~\text{col}(\mathbf{W}^{\top})\subseteq\mathcal{V}\},

(8)

and the perturbation space

\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=\{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}:\text{col}(\mathbf{W})\perp\mathcal{U},~\text{col}(\mathbf{W}^{\top})\perp\mathcal{V}\}.

(9)

For the general mean-zero and covariance stationary process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, if we model the data by a VAR(
d
) model in (
1
) and apply the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
6
) with robust autocovariance estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}
, we have the following upper bounds for the estimation error between the estimated matrix
\mathbf{\widehat{A}}
and the true value
\mathbf{A}^{*}
defined in (
3
).

Proposition 1
.

For the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
6
),
suppose that
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\leq\zeta_{0}
,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})\leq\zeta_{1}
, and
\lambda\geq\zeta_{0}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*})+\zeta_{1}
. Then, for
(\mathcal{M},\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp})
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{\widehat{A}}-\mathbf{A}^{*})&\leq 2\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\lambda,\\
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\widehat{A}}-\mathbf{A}^{*})&\leq 4\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}})\lambda+2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}}),\\
\text{and}~~\|\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq 8\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})^{2}\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}})\lambda^{2}+4\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}})\lambda.\end{split}

(10)

This proposition presents the deterministic estimation error bounds in
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}
, given that the estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}
satisfy some regularity conditions.
The estimation error bounds are related to
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})
, and these terms could be bounded or diverge slowly as the dimension
p
increases; see more discussions for each specific model in Section
3
.
When the true parameter matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}
admits a low-dimensional approximation rather than an exact structure,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}})
is strictly positive, and the second terms in the second and third upper bounds are related to the approximation error.
In this case, the upper bounds in this proposition hold uniformly for a class of low-dimensional structures with respect to
(\mathcal{M},\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp})
, and the class of upper bounds can be minimized by balancing the estimation error on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
and the approximation error on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\perp}
.

Moreover, in some cases, it is of interest to consider the approximate low dimensionality in a more structured manner. In particular, based on the multi-response nature, the VAR model in (
2
) can be split into
p
sub-models:

y_{it}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\varepsilon_{it}

(11)

where
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}
is the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
, for
i=1,\dots,p
. For these sub-models, as each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is a
pd
-dimensional vector, we may consider some low-dimensional structures, such as sparsity, on
\mathbf{a}_{i}
. Then, we consider the constrained estimation framework for each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
:

\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{i}=\argmin_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{a})~~\text{such that}~~\mathcal{R}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1i}-\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}\mathbf{a})\leq\lambda,

(12)

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is a vector norm,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)
is its dual norm, and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1i}^{\top}
is the
i
-th row of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}
. For simplicity, we consider the same constraint parameter
\lambda
for all sub-problems. In this framework, these
p

sub-problems can be handled in parallel and thus can be solved efficiently.

In an analogous fashion, we consider a pair of subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{pd}
,
(\mathcal{M}_{i},\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp})
, for each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{a}^{*}_{i}=(\mathbf{a}^{*}_{i})_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}+(\mathbf{a}^{*}_{i})_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp}}
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{1})+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{2})
for any
\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp}
, for all
i=1,\dots,p
. In addition, define by
\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i})
the constant such that
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v})\leq\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i})\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{v})
for any
\mathbf{v}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
. Moreover, for theoretical analysis, we consider the auxiliary matrix norms
\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
satisfying that
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{v})\leq\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\mathbf{W})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v})
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{v})\leq\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{W})\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{v})
for any compatible matrix
\mathbf{W}
and vector
\mathbf{v}
. The following example shows that the
\ell_{1}
regularization can be applied to the sparse VAR model.

Example 2

(Sparse VAR)
.

The sparse VAR model assumes that each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is strictly or weakly sparse, so that each response variable
y_{it}
is only related to a subset of lagged values
y_{j,t-\ell}
. Though the overall sparsity in
\mathbf{A}
has been considered in many existing literature, such as
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
and
Kock and Callot, 2015
, the sparsity structure in rows seems more natural in the time series context. For example, in the diagonal VAR(1) model, the total degree of sparsity grows with
p
, while the sparsity level in each row remains fixed. To induce sparsity in each row, as in
Han et al., 2015
, we consider
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, and
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1,\infty}
. For the weakly sparse
\mathbf{a}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,p
, denote by
S_{i}\subset\{j:1\leq j\leq pd\}
any index set corresponding to those coefficients significantly distant from zero in
\mathbf{a}_{i}
. For each
i
, define the model subspace associated with the chosen index set
S_{i}
as

\mathcal{M}_{i}(S_{i})=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}:\mathbf{v}_{j}=0~\text{for all}~j\notin S_{i}\},

(13)

and the perturbation subspace

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp}(S_{i})=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}:\mathbf{v}_{j}=0~\text{for all}~j\in S_{i}\}.

(14)

For the general mean-zero and covariance stationary process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, if we separate the VAR(
d
) model to
p
sub-problems in (
11
) and apply the separated constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
12
) with robust autocovariance estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1i}
, we have the following upper bounds for the estimation error between
\mathbf{\widehat{a}}_{i}
and the true value
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{*}
.

Proposition 2
.

For the separated Yule–Walker estimators in (
12
), suppose that
\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\leq\zeta_{0}
,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1i}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1i})\leq\zeta_{1i}
, and
\lambda\geq\max_{1\leq i\leq p}[\zeta_{0}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{*})+\zeta_{1i}]
. Then, for
(\mathcal{M}_{i},\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp})
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{*}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{i}^{*})&\leq 2\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\lambda,\\
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{i}^{*})&\leq 4\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i})\lambda+2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp}}),\\
\text{and}~~\|\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{i}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}&\leq 8\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})^{2}\phi(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i})\lambda^{2}+4\hskip 0.72229pt\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\perp}})\lambda\end{split}

(15)

for
i=1,\dots,p
.

For the approximately low-rank and sparse VAR models discussed in Examples
1
and
2
, we can obtain a sharp upper bound by appropriately selecting the matrix rank or the degree of sparsity. More concrete results are given in Section
3
.

2.3
Linear-restricted VAR

In modern high-dimensional time series modeling, domain knowledge and prior information are commonly available in addition to the time series data. In many such cases, prior information can be formulated as linear restrictions on the parameter matrix, and in this subsection, we apply the constrained Yule–Walker estimation approach to the high-dimensional VAR models with linear restrictions.

As discussed in
Tsay, 2013
, a general linear parameter restriction can be expressed as
\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})=\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}
,
where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}d\times r}
is a prespecified constraint matrix,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}d}
is a known constant vector, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the unknown parameter vector.
Note that the linear constraint representation is not unique; that is, for any nonsingular matrix
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{O})(\mathbf{O}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Thus, we require that
\mathbf{C}
is a tall orthonormal matrix, i.e.,
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Otherwise, we can apply singular value decomposition or QR decomposition to
\mathbf{C}
.

For simplicity, we consider the case where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{0}
. In this case, we can rewrite the VAR(
d
) model in (
2
) to

\mathbf{y}_{t}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}.

(16)

Similarly to the discussions in Section
2.1
, for the general stationary time series
\mathbf{y}_{t}
which may not strictly follow a linear-restricted VAR process, the parameter of interest
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
is defined as

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(17)

It implies the linear-restricted version of the Yule–Walker equation

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=[\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}]^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}^{\top})~~\text{or}~~\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}^{\top})=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},

(18)

provided that
\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}
is invertible.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. For
\mathbf{\Omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, suppose that we have robust estimators
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
. Then, we consider the constrained Yule–Walker estimator

\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}=\arg\min\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\infty}~~~\textup{subject to}~~\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda,

(19)

where
\lambda
is the constraint parameter and the
\ell_{\infty}
norm provides both element-wise regularization on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and element-wise constraint on the sample version of Yule–Walker equation. Decompose the restriction matrix
\mathbf{C}=[\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{p}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where each
\mathbf{B}_{i}
is a
pd\times r
matrix. Let
\mathbf{B}=[\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{pd\times pr}
and then the estimator of
\mathbf{A}
is
\mathbf{\widehat{A}}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}})^{\top}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
.

The linear-restricted model encompasses several important recent developments in high-dimensional vector autoregression.

Example 3

(Banded VAR)
.

The banded VAR(1) proposed by
Guo et al., 2016
has the following banded coefficient structure:

\mathbf{A}_{ij}=0,~~\text{for all }|i-j|>k_{0},

(20)

where
k_{0}
is called the bandwidth parameter.
The banded structure is a special sparse structure, so we can formulate it to a linear constraint
\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})=\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where each column of
\mathbf{C}
is a coordinate vector corresponding to one nonzero index in
\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})
.

Example 4

(Network VAR)
.

Zhu et al., 2017
proposed the network VAR model for the network time series data
\mathbf{y}_{t}
, which has an observable network structure with the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{W}
. The network VAR model assumes the form

\mathbf{y}_{t}=\beta_{1}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\beta_{2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}.

(21)

In other words, it is a VAR(1) model with
\mathbf{A}=\beta_{1}\mathbf{I}_{p}+\beta_{2}\mathbf{W}
, and the associated coefficient vector can be written as

\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})=\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\mathbf{C}=(p^{-1/2}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{I}_{p}),\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}^{-1}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{W}^{\top}))
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(p^{1/2}\beta_{1},\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}\beta_{2})^{\top}
. In this parameterization,
\mathbf{C}
is an orthonormal matrix.

For a general zero-mean and covariance stationary time series
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, if we model the data by the linear-restricted VAR(
d
) model in (
16
) and apply the linear-restricted Yule–Walker estimator in (
19
) with the robust estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Omega}}
and
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}
, we have the upper bounds for the estimation error between the estimated parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}
and the true vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
defined in (
17
).

Proposition 3
.

For the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
19
), suppose that
\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Omega}}-\mathbf{\Omega}\|_{1,\infty}\leq\zeta_{1}
,

\|\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta_{2}
, and
\lambda\geq\zeta_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}+\zeta_{2}
. Then,

\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\lambda\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}.

(22)

This proposition presents the
\ell_{\infty}
norm estimation error bounds of the low-dimensional parameter
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}
, which is a new result for the linear-restricted VAR models. Similarly to Proposition
1
, the upper bound is proportional to
\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
that could be related to the dimension
p
, depending on the specific linear restrictions. The
\ell_{\infty}
upper bound directly implies that
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{r}\lambda\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
, and some sharper bounds may be obtained based on the the special structure of
\mathbf{C}
. More concrete results and discussions are provided in Section
3.3
.

Remark 1
.

For the upper bound result in Proposition
3
, the terms
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}
and
\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
may vary or even diverge with the dimension
p
. Indeed, the term
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}
for the network VAR model in Example
3
naturally diverges at a rate
\sqrt{p}
. The explicit rate of
\lambda\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
depends on the specific linear restriction structure and further assumptions. More discussions about these terms are given in Section
3.3
for some specific models.

The deterministic upper bounds in Propositions
1
-
3
imply that the performance of parameter estimation of the constrained Yule–Walker estimators hinges on the accuracy of autocovariance matrix estimation. It suffices to find reliable robust autocovariance estimators, which will be introduced in the next section.

3
Robust Autocovariance Estimation

3.1
Element truncation estimator

Fan et al., 2021
and
Ke et al., 2019
proposed a simple robust estimation approach via appropriate truncation on data and showed that the truncated covariance estimator can achieve the optimal rate as that under the sub-Gaussian distribution for independent samples. We first apply the truncation method to estimate the elements of autocovariance matrices.

We consider the element-wise truncated data
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\text{E}}(\tau)=(y_{1t}(\tau),y_{2t}(\tau),\dots,y_{pt}(\tau))^{\top}
, where
y_{it}(\tau)=\text{sign}(y_{it})(\tau\wedge|y_{it}|)
, for
1\leq i\leq p
, and
\tau>0
is the truncation parameter. Based on this truncation scheme,
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\ell}
can be estimated by
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Gamma}}_{\ell}^{\text{E}}(\tau)=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\text{E}}(\tau)\mathbf{y}_{t-\ell}^{\text{E}}(\tau)^{\top}
, for any integer
\ell\geq 0
. The corresponding autocovariance estimators are

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\text{E}}(\tau)=[\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{1}(\tau),\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{2}(\tau),\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{d}(\tau)],

(23)

and

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\text{E}}(\tau)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{0}(\tau)&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{1}(\tau)&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{d-1}(\tau)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{-1}(\tau)&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{0}(\tau)&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{d-2}(\tau)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{-d+1}(\tau)&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{-d}(\tau)&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{0}(\tau)\end{bmatrix},

(24)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{-j}(\tau)=\widetilde{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\text{E}}_{j}(\tau)^{\top}
, for
j\geq 1
. The element-wise truncation can control the deviation of
y_{it}y_{jt}
or
y_{it}y_{j,t-\ell}
and the truncation parameter
\tau
allows us to balance the tradeoff between the truncation bias and robustness.

Note that no data generating mechanism assumption is imposed, and we adopt the
\alpha
-mixing condition to quantify the serial dependency. For any stochastic process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}
, the lag-
\ell

\alpha
-mixing dependence coefficient is defined as

\alpha(\ell):=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=s+\ell}^{\infty})
, where

\alpha(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=r}^{\infty})=\sup|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|,

(25)

the supremum being taken over all events
A\in\sigma(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s})
and
B\in\sigma(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=r}^{\infty})
, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigma field generated by the process. To derive the theoretical guarantees of the autocovariance estimation, we have the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}
is weakly stationary and
\alpha
-mixing with the mixing coefficients
\alpha(\ell)=O(r^{\ell})
, where
r=r(p)
is a sequence possibly depending on
p
such that
0\leq r\leq\bar{r}
for some constant
\bar{r}<1
.

Assumption 2
.

For
1\leq i\leq p
,
\mathbb{E}[|y_{it}|^{2+2\epsilon}]\leq M_{2+2\epsilon}
, for some
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.

Assumption
1
states the weak stationarity and geometrically decayed
\alpha
-mixing, rather than assuming that the true data generating process (DGP) of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
is a VAR(
d
) process. If
\mathbf{y}_{t}
truly follows a VAR(
d
) model in (
1
), Assumption
1
holds by the stationarity and geometric ergodicity of the VAR process; see Proposition 2 in
Liebscher, 2005
. Assumption
2
relaxes the commonly-used sub-Gaussian condition in the existing literature to the bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition, where
M_{2+2\epsilon}
may diverge to infinity with the dimension
p
. For the strong mixing time series, we denote
n_{\text{eff}}=T/\log(T)^{2}
as the effective sample size representing the number of effectively independent samples from
T
observations. Note that
n_{\text{eff}}
is of almost the same rate as
T
since
T^{1-\delta}=o(n_{\text{eff}})
for any
\delta>0
.

Proposition 4
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, if
T\gtrsim\log(p^{2}d)
and

\tau\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}}}{\log(p^{2}d)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},

(26)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(T)\log(p^{2}d)]
,

\begin{split}&\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\textup{E}}(\tau)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and~~}&\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\textup{E}}(\tau)-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(27)

This proposition presents the
\ell_{\infty}
bounds for the element-wise truncation autocovariance estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}^{\text{E}}_{0}(\tau)
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}^{\text{E}}_{1}(\tau)
. If the truncation parameter
\tau
is chosen appropriately and the moment bound
M_{2+2\epsilon}
is fixed, the convergence rate of autocovariance matrix estimation in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm is
O([\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)})
.
When
\epsilon=1
, the data has a bounded fourth moment and the convergence rates of the autocovariance matrices scale as
\sqrt{\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}}
. When
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the convergence rates have a smooth phase transition phenomenon, decreasing from
[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{1/2}
to
[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
.

Remark 2
.

For robust covariance estimators based on data truncation or shrinkage, the existing theoretical analysis in
Fan et al., 2021
and
Ke et al., 2019
focused on the data with a bounded fourth moment. Our results in Proposition
4
relax the fourth moment condition to the
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition and can effectively handle a much larger class of distributions. In addition,
Avella-Medina et al., 2018
proposed rank-based and adaptive Huber regression methods for robust covariance estimation and obtained a similar phase transition in the upper bound with respect to
\epsilon
.

For the weakly sparse VAR model in Example
2
,
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\text{E}}(\tau)
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\text{E}}(\tau)
can be used as robust autocovariance estimators in the constrained Yule–Walker estimation in (
12
) with
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
The resulting estimator for the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
is denoted as
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
and the sparse estimator is
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{S}}(\lambda,\tau)=[\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{1}(\lambda,\tau),\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{p}(\lambda,\tau)]^{\top}
.

Define an
\ell_{q}
-“ball” with radius
s_{q}
as
\mathbb{B}_{q}(s_{q})=\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}:\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{pd}|\mathbf{A}_{ij}|^{q}\leq s_{q}\}
. Note that when
q=0
,
\mathbb{B}_{0}(s_{0})
is the set of all
p
-by-
pd
matrices whose rows are at most
s_{0}
-sparse. For
q>0
,
\mathbb{B}_{q}(s_{q})
requires that the absolute values of entries in
\mathbf{A}
decay sufficiently fast, which is more general than the exact sparsity assumption. Given the weak sparsity in the rows of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
, it is natural to assume that
\|\mathbf{A}^{*\top}\|_{1,\infty}
is bounded.

We are ready to present the theoretical properties of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{S}}(\lambda,\tau)
.

Theorem 1

(Sparse VAR upper bounds)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{*}\in\mathbb{B}_{q}(s_{q})
for some
q\in[0,1)
,
\|\mathbf{A}^{*\top}\|_{1,\infty}\leq C<\infty
, and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If
T\gtrsim\log(p^{2}d)
,

\tau\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}}}{\log(p^{2}d)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},

(28)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(T)\log(p^{2}d)]
,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{S}}(\lambda,\tau)-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}^{\top}_{\textup{S}}(\lambda,\tau)-\mathbf{A}^{*\top}\|_{1,\infty}&\lesssim s_{q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}^{1-q}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{(1-q)\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}^{\top}_{\textup{S}}(\lambda,\tau)-\mathbf{A}^{*\top}\|_{2,\infty}&\lesssim\sqrt{s_{q}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p^{2}d)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\left(1-\frac{q}{2}\right)\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(29)

This theorem presents non-asymptotic estimation upper bounds in various matrix norms under the weakly sparse structure. If both
M_{2+2\epsilon}
and
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
are fixed, the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
,
\|\cdot\|_{1,\infty}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}
upper bounds scale as
[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
,
s_{q}[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{(1-q)\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, and
\sqrt{s_{q}}[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{(1-q/2)\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, respectively.
If the data have a bounded fourth moment with
\epsilon=1
, the first two rates of convergence match those of Gaussian sparse VAR in
Han et al., 2015
. When the true model has a strict sparsity structure with
q=0
and sparsity level
s_{0}
, based on the
\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}
bound, the sample size requirement is
n_{\text{eff}}\gtrsim s_{0}\log(p^{2}d)
. That is, if the sparsity level in each row of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is finite, the dimension
p
is allowed to be exponentially large compared to
n_{\text{eff}}
. If the data do not have a bounded fourth moment but only a bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the proposed estimator is still consistent but the rate of convergence in the
\ell_{2,\infty}
norm decreases from
\sqrt{s_{q}}[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{1/2}
to
\sqrt{s_{q}}[\log(p^{2}d)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
.

Remark 3
.

The boundedness of
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
is widely assumed in the existing literature of the Dantzig selector for sparse VAR models; see, for example,
Han et al., 2015
and
Wu and Wu, 2016
. Indeed, if
\mathbf{y}_{t}
follows a VAR(1) process in (
1
), the covariance of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
has an explicit form
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{A}^{*\top})^{i}
, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
is the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, and
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
can be verified to be independent of
p
given that
\mathbf{A}^{*}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
satisfy certain structures. For example, if both
\mathbf{A}^{*}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
are block diagonal with a fixed block size,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}
is also block diagonal and its
\ell_{1,\infty}
norm is independent of
p
. If
\mathbf{A}^{*}
has a banded structure as in Example
3
,
(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1})_{ij}
shrinks to zero exponentially as
|i-j|
increases to infinity, and hence the boundedness of
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
can be expected.

Remark 4
.

The robust estimation of sparse linear regression has been investigated in
Fan et al., 2017
,
Sun et al., 2020
, and
Fan et al., 2021
, among many others. We highlight some main differences between conventional linear regression and vector autoregression in terms of robust estimation. First, for linear regression models, the heavy-tailed distribution condition is considered on the random errors; that is, the response is conditionally heavy-tailed given the predictors. In our analysis, as no data generating mechanism assumption is imposed, the heavy-tailed distributional assumption, i.e. the moment condition in Assumption
2
, is directly considered on the observed data
\mathbf{y}_{t}
. Second, when the design matrix of the linear regression model satisfies some regulatory conditions, the
(1+\epsilon)
-th moment condition is considered for the random errors in
Sun et al., 2020
. However, for the VAR models, as both predictor and response are simultaneously heavy-tailed, a more stringent
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition has to be imposed in our theoretical analysis. Though the distributional assumptions are different in these two problems, the similar phase transition phenomenon can be obtained for two regimes
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
\epsilon\geq 1
.

3.2
Vector truncation estimator

In this subsection, we propose and study an autcovariance estimator that is robust in the operator norm. Intuitively, in order to achieve the operator norm robustness, we need to control the spectrum of
\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}
. Note that
\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\|_{\textup{op}}=\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\|_{\textup{op}}=\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}
. Therefore, we propose the truncation method to the whole vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}
in the
\ell_{2}
norm.
To construct robust estimators for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
of a VAR(
d
) model, we consider the vector-truncated responses and predictors
\mathbf{y}^{\text{V}}_{t}(\tau_{1})=(\tau_{1}\wedge\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\|_{2})\mathbf{y}_{t}/\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\|_{2}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\text{V}}_{t}(\tau_{2})=(\tau_{2}\wedge\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2})\mathbf{x}_{t}/\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}
, for
1\leq t\leq T
, where two truncation parameters
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are adopted as
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
are of different dimension when
d>1
. For the case with
d=1
, we can use a single parameter by setting
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
.
Based on the vector truncation of the data, the corresponding truncation autocovariance estimators are defined as

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\text{V}}_{0}(\tau_{2})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{2})\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{2})^{\top},~~\text{and}~~\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\text{V}}_{1}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{1})\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{2})^{\top}.

(30)

Remark 5
.

Fan et al., 2021
proposed a robust covariance estimator based on the vector-wise truncation in the
\ell_{4}
norm and investigated its theoretical properties under the finite fourth moment condition. However, as we are interested in the relaxed setting with the finite
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment, the truncation in the
\ell_{4}
norm is not appropriate in our setting.
Ke et al., 2019
proposed the spectrum-wise truncation for robust estimation of covariance matrix, and our method shares the same idea.

For the vector-wise truncation estimator, we adopt the
\beta
-mixing condition to quantify the serial dependency. Specifically, for any stochastic process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}
, the lag-
\ell

\beta
-mixing dependence coefficient is defined as

\beta(\ell):=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=s+\ell}^{\infty})
, where

\beta(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=r}^{\infty})=\sup\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left|\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{j})-\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\mathbb{P}(B_{j})\right|,

(31)

the supremum being taken over all pairs of partitions
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{I}\}
and
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{J}\}
such that
A_{i}\in\sigma(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s})
and
B_{j}\in\sigma(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=r}^{\infty})

. To study the theoretical properties of the vector-wise truncation estimators, we need the following assumptions.

Assumption 3
.

The process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}
is weakly stationary and
\beta
-mixing with the mixing coefficients
\beta(\ell)=O(r^{\ell})
, where
r=r(p)
is a sequence possibly depending on
p
such that
0\leq r\leq\bar{r}
for some constant
\bar{r}<1
.

Assumption 4
.

For any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}
such that
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1
and some
\delta>0
,
\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{2+2\epsilon+\delta}]\leq M_{2+2\epsilon}
.

Note that the
\beta
-mixing condition in Assumption
3
is slightly stronger than the
\alpha
-mixing condition in Assumption
1
for the element-wise truncation estimators. Here we adopt the
\beta
-mixing condition in order to apply the Bernstein-type inequality for
\beta
-mixing random matrices developed by
Banna et al., 2016
. For the VAR process with independent and identically distributed
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, the absolute regularity with geometrically decayed
\beta
-mixing coefficients is equivalent to the geometric ergodicity
(
Liebscher, 2005
)
. The bounded
(2+2\epsilon+\delta)
-th moment condition in Assumption
4
is also slightly stronger than the bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition in Assumption
2
. Here
\delta
is only for the technical purpose and can be arbitrarily small.

Proposition 5
.

Under Assumptions
3
and
4
, if
T\gtrsim pd
,

\tau_{1}\asymp(p^{\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}}~~\text{and}~~\tau_{2}\asymp(p^{\epsilon}d^{\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},

(32)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(T)]
,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\textup{V}}(\tau_{2})-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}\|_{\textup{op}}&\lesssim\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\textup{V}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{\textup{op}}&\lesssim\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(33)

This proposition presents the operator norm bounds for the vector truncation autocovariance estimators
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{2})
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\text{V}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
.
Similarly to the element truncation estimator in Section
3.1
, we define
n_{\text{eff}}=T/\log(T)^{2}
. If the moment bound
M_{2+2\epsilon}
is fixed, the operator norm convergence rates of both autocovariance matrix estimators scale as
(pd/n_{\text{eff}})^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
. When the time series data has a bounded fourth moment, that is
\epsilon=1
, our results are almost the same as those of covariance matrix estimators for
i.i.d.
data in
Ke et al., 2019
and
Fan et al., 2021
. Smooth transition on the operator norm convergence rate is observed when the fourth moment condition is relaxed to the
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition.

Based on the rates of the vector truncation autocovariance matrices, we derive the estimation rate of the nuclear norm constrained Yule–Walker estimator for the weakly low-rank VAR model. Define an
\ell_{q}
-“ball” for the singular values with radius
r_{q}
as
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}_{q}(r_{q})=\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{q}(\mathbf{A})\leq r_{q}\}
. When the singular values of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
are weakly sparse, it is natural to assume that
\|\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{nuc}}
, the sum of singular values of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
, is bounded. Denote by
\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\text{RR}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
the reduced-rank constrained Yule–Walker estimator with the regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{nuc}}
, the constraint function
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
, the constraint parameter
\lambda
, and the vector truncation autocovariance estimators in (
30
) with truncation parameters
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
.

Theorem 2

(Reduced-rank VAR upper bounds)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{*}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}_{q}(r_{q})
for some
q\in[0,1)
,
\|\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{nuc}}\leq C<\infty
, and Assumptions
3
and
4
hold. If
T\gtrsim p
,

\tau_{1}\asymp(p^{\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},~~\tau_{2}\asymp(p^{\epsilon}d^{\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},~~\text{and}~~\lambda\asymp\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},

(34)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(T)]
,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{RR}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{op}}&\lesssim\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{RR}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{nuc}}&\lesssim r_{q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|^{1-q}_{\textup{op}}\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{(1-q)\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{RR}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}&\lesssim\sqrt{r_{q}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|^{1-\frac{q}{2}}_{\textup{op}}\left(\frac{pM_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{q}{2}\right)\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(35)

This theorem presents the non-asymptotic estimation upper bounds in the operator norm, nuclear norm, and Frobenius norm, respectively. If
M_{2+2\epsilon}
is fixed, the convergence rates of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{RR}}
in the operator norm and Frobenius norm scale as
(p/n_{\text{eff}})^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
and
\sqrt{r_{q}}(p/n_{\text{eff}})^{(1-q/2)\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
. Specifically, when
\epsilon=1
, that is the time series have a bounded fourth moment, the Frobenius norm convergence rate of the robust estimator is nearly the same as those obtained by the standard nuclear norm penalized estimators for Gaussian VAR model in
Negahban and Wainwright, 2011
and
Basu et al., 2019
. When the time series only has a bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the estimation error rates decrease from
\sqrt{r_{q}}(p/n_{\text{eff}})^{1/2}
to
(p/n_{\text{eff}})^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
. Note that though the estimation convergence rates decrease, if
r_{q}
is fixed, the sample size requirement
n_{\text{eff}}\gtrsim pd
for estimation consistency remains unchanged when the moment condition is relaxed.

Remark 6
.

The boundedness of
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{\textup{op}}
is equivalent to that the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
is bounded away from zero. If
\mathbf{y}_{t}
follows a stationary VAR model in (
1
), this condition can be guaranteed if the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
is bounded away from zero, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
is the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
.

3.3
Linear-restricted truncation estimator

For the linear-restricted VAR model in (
16
), the linear transformations of autocovariance matrices are defined as

\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t})(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{C}]~~\text{and}~~\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{y}_{t}].

(36)

Motivated by the element and vector-wise truncation in the previous subsections, in order to robustly estimate each element of
\mathbf{\Omega}
, we apply the truncation to
(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{c}_{i}
, where each
\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}d}
is the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{C}
. When each
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is highly sparse, e.g.,
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is an coordinate vector for the banded VAR in Example
3
, the vector
(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{c}_{i}
is also highly sparse. In this case,let
S_{i}
be the non-zero index set of
(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{c}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{w}_{it}=[(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{c}_{i}]_{S_{i}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{it}=(\mathbf{y}_{t})_{S_{i}}
be the sub-vectors. It is obvious to check that
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t})(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathbf{c}_{j}=\mathbf{w}_{it}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{jt}
and
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{w}_{it}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{it}
, for
1\leq i,j\leq r
.

Therefore, we consider the vector-wise truncation
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{it}(\tau_{1})=(\tau_{1}\wedge\|\mathbf{w}_{it}\|_{2})\mathbf{w}_{it}/\|\mathbf{w}_{it}\|_{2}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{it}(\tau_{2})=(\tau_{2}\wedge\|\mathbf{z}_{it}\|_{2})\mathbf{z}_{it}/\|\mathbf{z}_{it}\|_{2}
, and the elements of
\mathbf{\Omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
can be estimated by the linear-restricted truncated data

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{ij}(\tau_{1})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{it}(\tau_{1})^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{jt}(\tau_{1})~~\text{and}~~\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{it}(\tau_{1})^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{it}(\tau_{2}),

(37)

for
1\leq i,j\leq r
.

Remark 7
.

For the special matrix
\mathbf{C}
whose columns are coordinate vectors corresponding to the non-zero entries in
\textup{vec}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})
, the linear-restricted estimators with
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
are equivalent to the linear transformations of element truncation estimators, namely
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\tau_{1})=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\text{E}}(\tau_{1}))\mathbf{C}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})=\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textup{vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\text{E}}(\tau_{1})^{\top})
.

For the linear-restricted models, we consider the following moment conditions.

Assumption 5
.

For
1\leq i\leq r
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{w}_{it}\|_{2}^{2+2\epsilon}]\leq M_{1,2+2\epsilon}
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{z}_{it}\|_{2}^{2+2\epsilon}]\leq M_{2,2+2\epsilon}
.

The moment conditions in Assumption
5
can be viewed as an extension of the element-wise moment condition in Assumption
2
. In addition, due to the normalization of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
, the moment bounds
M_{1,2+2\epsilon}
and
M_{2,2+2\epsilon}
are allowed to vary with the dimension
p
. More discussions on the moment bounds are presented below for each specific linear-restricted model. For the linear-restricted model in (
16
) with
r>1
, the general results of the autocovariance estimators are given as follows.

Proposition 6
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
5
, if
T\gtrsim\log(r)
,

\tau_{1}\asymp\left[\frac{M_{1,2+2\epsilon}n_{\text{eff}}}{\log(r)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},~~\text{and}~~\tau_{2}\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2,2+2\epsilon}n_{\text{eff}}}{\log(r)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},

(38)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(r)\log(T)]
,

\begin{split}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\tau_{1})-\mathbf{\Omega}\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\left[\frac{M_{1,2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(r)}{n_{\text{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\left[\frac{M_{1,2+2\epsilon}^{1/(2\epsilon)}M_{2,2+2\epsilon}^{1/(2\epsilon)}\log(r)}{n_{\text{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(39)

This proposition presents the estimation upper bounds for
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\tau_{1})
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\tau_{2})
in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Following this proposition, we first consider the banded VAR model with the bandwidth
k_{0}
in Example
3
, denote by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{B}}(\lambda,\tau)
and
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{B}}(\lambda,\tau)
the linear-restricted constrained Yule–Walker estimators with robust autocovariance estimators
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\tau)
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\tau,\tau)
. Note that as each
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is a coordinate vector, both
\mathbf{w}_{it}
and
\mathbf{z}_{it}
are 1-dimensional, and the Assumption
5
reduces to the element-wise moment bound in Assumption
2
. The following estimation upper bounds can be derived.

Theorem 3

(Banded VAR upper bounds)
.

For the banded VAR model with a bounded bandwidth
k_{0}
in Example
3
, suppose that
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C<\infty
, and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If
T\gtrsim\log(p)
,

\tau\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}}}{\log(p)}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}},~~\text{and}~~\lambda\asymp\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}},

(40)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(p)\log(T)]
,

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}_{\textup{B}}(\lambda,\tau)-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{B}}(\lambda,\tau)-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{op}}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}_{\textup{B}}(\lambda,\tau)-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{B}}(\lambda,\tau)-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}&\lesssim\sqrt{p}\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\left[\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}\log(p)}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(41)

When
M_{2+2\epsilon}
and
\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
are fixed, the upper bounds in this theorem imply that asymptotically the rates of convergence in the operator norm and Frobenius norm scale as
[\log(p)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
and
[p\log(p)/n_{\text{eff}}]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, respectively. In other words, the sample size requirement in the operator norm is
n_{\text{eff}}\gtrsim\log(p)
. When
\epsilon=1
, our results are comparable and consistent with those in
Guo et al., 2016
up to a logarithm factor.
However, in
Guo et al., 2016
, they imposed the
i.i.d.
condition and sub-exponential tail condition for
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
when
p\gtrsim T
. Hence, under high-dimensional scaling, both the operator norm and Frobenius norm convergence rates of the robust constrained Yule–Walker estimator for heavy-tailed data with a bounded fourth moment are almost the same as those of the standard ordinary least squares under the sub-exponential tail condition. In sum, the convergence rates obtained under the sub-exponential distribution can be achieved by the robust estimator under a much relaxed fourth moment condition, and we also establish the estimation consistency and the rates of convergence under the
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition.

Remark 8
.

The stationarity of the VAR(1) model requires that the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
in terms of absolute value is strictly smaller than one, and hence the absolute value of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{i}
is expected to be bounded.
As
\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}
and the columns of
\mathbf{C}
are all coordinate vectors,
\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
is bounded by
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
, and the boundedness of
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}
has been discussed in Remark
3
.

Next, we consider another case of the linear-restricted VAR models, the network VAR model in Example
4
. In this model,
\mathbf{C}
is a
p^{2}
-by-2 matrix with
\mathbf{c}_{1}=p^{-1/2}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{I}_{p})
and
\mathbf{c}_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}^{-1}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{W}^{\top})
being the restrictions associated with the nodal effect and network effect, respectively. Accordingly,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(p^{1/2}\beta_{1},\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}\beta_{2})^{\top}
. As
\mathbf{C}
needs to be normalized fro identification purpose, we assume that the unscaled parameters,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are constants independent of
p
. In addition, we assume that each node in the network is only connected to a fixed number of nodes, so the network adjacency matrix satisfies
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}\asymp\sqrt{p}
.

According to the linear restriction matrix
\mathbf{C}
of the network VAR model, it can be verified that
\mathbf{w}_{1t}=p^{-1/2}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}
,
\mathbf{w}_{2t}=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}^{-1}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}
, and
\mathbf{z}_{1t}=\mathbf{z}_{2t}=\mathbf{y}_{t}
. Hence, if
\max_{i=1,2}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{w}_{it}\|_{2}^{2+2\epsilon}=M_{2+2\epsilon}
which is independent of
p
, then,
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}_{1t}\|_{2}^{2+2\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}_{2t}\|_{2}^{2+2\epsilon}\leq p^{1+\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}
. For the network VAR model, denote by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{N}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
the constrained Yule–Walker estimator with the constraint parameter
\lambda
and the autocovariance estimators
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\tau_{1})
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
. The estimation upper bounds of the network VAR model can be derived as follows.

Theorem 4

(Network VAR upper bounds)
.

For the network VAR model in Example
4
with
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\textup{F}}\asymp\sqrt{p}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}\asymp\sqrt{p}
, under Assumptions
1
and
5
with
M_{1,2+2\epsilon}=M_{2+2\epsilon}
and
M_{2,2+2\epsilon}=p^{1+\epsilon}M_{2+2\epsilon}
, if

\tau_{1}\asymp(M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},~~\tau_{2}\asymp\sqrt{p}(M_{2+2\epsilon}n_{\textup{eff}})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}},~~\text{and}~~\lambda\asymp\sqrt{p}\left(\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},

(42)

then, with probability at least
1-C\exp[-C\log(T)]
,

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}_{\textup{N}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\sqrt{p}\left(\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\\
\text{and}~~\|\mathbf{\widehat{A}}_{\textup{N}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathbf{A}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}=\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}_{\textup{N}}(\lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\sqrt{p}\left(\frac{M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}}{n_{\textup{eff}}}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(43)

The upper bounds in this theorem are new for the network VAR model under the non-asymptotic scheme.
Due to the normalization for the linear restriction matrix
\mathbf{C}
, both the true value
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}
and estimated value
\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}\|_{2}
will diverge at a rate of
\sqrt{p}
as the dimension
p
increases to infinity. Hence, the
\ell_{2}
rate of convergence for the normalized coefficients
p^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\theta}
scales as
n_{\text{eff}}^{-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, independent of the dimension
p
, and our result with
\epsilon=1
is comparable to that obtained under the sub-Gaussian condition in
Zheng and Cheng, 2021
. The convergence rates under a bounded
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition are established under a phase transition pattern.

4
Minimax Lower Bounds

In this section, we investigate theoretical properties on the lower bounds of estimation tasks for heavy-tailed high-dimensional time series data, including estimation of the high-dimensional VAR models and the autocovariance matrices. The lower bound analysis shows that the rates of convergence obtained in Section
3
are optimal in the minimax sense: there exists a distributional setting for the time series process for which the upper bounds obtained cannot be improved without further distributional assumptions.

4.1
Lower bounds of VAR estimation

Similarly to the upper bound analysis, no assumption on the data generating mechanism is imposed on the time series data in the lower bound analysis. We consider the VAR(
d
) model and denote the distribution of
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
as
\mathbb{P}
. By the Yule–Walker equation, the true value of the VAR model coefficient matrix is defined as
\mathbf{A}^{*}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(\mathbb{P})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbb{P})
, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]
.

The first case we consider is the sparse VAR model, where all row vectors of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
are strictly sparse. For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{P}_{\text{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
denote the class of all joint distributions for the
\alpha
-mixing stochastic process
\mathbf{v}_{t}=(v_{1t},\dots,v_{pt})^{\top}
with the element-wise moment condition, such that they satisfy
\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\mathbb{E}|v_{it}|^{2+2\epsilon}=M
and
\alpha(\{\mathbf{v}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{v}_{t}\}_{t=s+\ell}^{\infty})=O(r^{\ell})
for any integer
s
and lag order
\ell>0
. The moment parameter
M
is imposed on the element of
\mathbf{v}_{t}
, which is consistent with Assumption
2
.

For
\mathbf{A}^{*}
belonging to the sparse set
\mathbb{B}_{0}(s_{0})
defined in Section
3.1
and the process
\mathbf{y}_{t}
following the distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, we have the minimax lower bound for the VAR estimation.

Theorem 5

(Sparse VAR lower bound)
.

For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in(0,1)
, suppose that
T/\log_{r^{-2}}T\geq 2s_{0}
and the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
. Then, for any estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})
which depends on the observations from
\mathbf{y}_{1}
to
\mathbf{y}_{T}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{E}}(M,\epsilon,r),\\
\mathbf{A}^{*}(\mathbb{P})\in\mathbb{B}_{0}(s_{0})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{*}(\mathbb{P})^{\top}\|_{2,\infty}\right]\gtrsim\sqrt{s_{0}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbb{P})\|_{1,\infty}\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(44)

The minimax lower bound in this theorem matches the
\ell_{2,\infty}
upper bound in Theorem
1
with
q=0
up to a logarithm factor that is negligible compared with
T
. Hence, for the strictly sparse VAR model, the constrained Yule–Walker estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\text{S}}(\lambda,\tau)
introduced in Section
3.1
is nearly rate-optimal when the regularization parameter
\lambda
and truncation parameter
\tau
are properly chosen. In addition, the effective sample size in the lower bound is
T/\log(T)
, where the
\log(T)
factor can be referred to as the price paid for the serial dependency in the data.

Moreover, it is clear that the banded VAR model with the bandwidth
k
is a special case of the strictly sparse VAR model with the sparsity level
s_{0}=2k+1
. When
k
is fixed, Theorem
5
directly implies that the minimax lower bound of the banded VAR model is of rate
\|\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}[M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)/T]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
in the operator norm, matching the upper bound of the banded VAR in Theorem
3
.

The second case considered is the reduced-rank VAR model where
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is of low rank. For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{P}_{\text{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
denote the class of all joint distributions for the
\alpha
-mixing stochastic process
\mathbf{v}_{t}=(v_{1t},\dots,v_{pt})^{\top}
with the vector-wise moment condition, such that
\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{u}|^{2+2\epsilon}=M
and
\alpha(\{\mathbf{v}_{t}\}_{-\infty}^{s},\{\mathbf{v}_{t}\}_{s+\ell}^{\infty})=O(r^{\ell})
for any integer
s
and lag order
\ell>0
. The moment condition defined on the whole vector is similar to Assumption
3
for the reduced-rank VAR model, but the technical term
\delta
is omitted in the lower bound analysis.

For
\mathbf{A}^{*}
belonging to the low-rank set
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}_{0}(r_{0})
defined in Section
3.2
and the process
\mathbf{y}_{t}
following the distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, the lower bound in terms of Frobenius norm is derived.

Theorem 6

(Reduced-rank VAR lower bound)
.

For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in(0,1)
, suppose that
p\geq 20
,
T>p/128
, and the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
. Then, for any estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})
which depends on the observations from
\mathbf{y}_{1}
to
\mathbf{y}_{T}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{V}}(M,\epsilon,r),\\
\mathbf{A}^{*}(\mathbb{P})\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}_{0}(r_{0})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{*}(\mathbb{P})^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}\right]\gtrsim\sqrt{r_{0}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbb{P})\|_{\textup{op}}\left(\frac{pM^{1/\epsilon}}{T}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(45)

This theorem presents the minimax lower bound of the reduced-rank VAR model with the exact low-rankness in terms of Frobenius norm, which matches the upper bound in Theorem
2
up to a logarithm factor.

Remark 9
.

The minimax lower bounds in Theorems
5
and
6
are developed for the VAR models with an exact low-dimensional structure, such as the strict row-wise sparsity and exact low-rankness. The lower bounds for the high-dimensional regression and covariance estimation with the weakly sparse coefficients have been investigated by
Raskutti et al., 2011
,
Cai and Zhou, 2012
, and others under the
\ell_{q}
ball constraints. However, the theoretical techniques in their proofs rely heavily on the Gaussian distributional assumption, and hence cannot be applied to the heavy-tailed setting. The minimax lower bounds for the VAR estimation under the weak sparsity and approximate low-rankness are left for future research.

4.2
Lower bounds of autocovariance estimation

For any process
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}
following distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, let
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{t-d}^{\top})^{\top}
, and a by-product of our lower bound analysis is the minimax lower bounds for the autocovariance estimation. First, we develop the minimax lower bounds in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm for estimating the autocovariance matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]
.

Proposition 7
.

For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in[0,1)
, suppose that the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
. Then, for any covariance estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}:=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})
which depends on the observations from
\mathbf{y}_{1}
to
\mathbf{y}_{T}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}(\mathbb{P})\|_{\infty}\right]\gtrsim\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\quad k=0,1.

(46)

The minimax lower bound matches the upper bounds in Proposition
4
up to a logarithmic factor
[\log(p^{2}d)\log(T)]^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, indicating the nearly minimax optimality of the proposed shrinkage estimator
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}^{\text{E}}(\tau)
with
\tau
chosen properly. Compared with the upper and lower bounds of the covariance matrix estimation for the
i.i.d.
data in
Avella-Medina et al., 2018
and
Devroye et al., 2016
, our minimax lower bound involves a factor
\log(T)^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, which is due to the weak serial dependency under the geometrically decayed
\alpha
-mixing condition. It is also noteworthy that the
\log(T)
factor can also be found in the upper bound analysis in
Zhang, 2021
under the functional dependence measures
(
Wu, 2005
)
.

In addition, this minimax lower bound can easily be extended to the linear transformations of autocovariance matrices
\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{C}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textup{vec}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})
, where
\mathbf{C}
consists of coordinate vector columns. In other words, Proposition
7
implies that the upper bounds in Proposition
6
are rate-optimal up to a logarithm factor.

Moreover, the minimax lower bound in the operator norm is also established.

Proposition 8
.

For any
M>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
r\in[0,1)
, suppose that
p\geq 20
,
T\geq p/128
, and the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
. Then, for any covariance estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}:=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})
which depends on the observations from
\mathbf{y}_{1}
to
\mathbf{y}_{T}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}(\mathbb{P})\|_{\textup{op}}\right]\gtrsim\left(\frac{pM^{1/\epsilon}}{T}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\quad k=0,1.

(47)

This lower bound matches the upper bound in Proposition
5
up to a logarithm factor
\log(T)^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
, indicating that the vector-wise truncated estimators
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\text{V}}_{k}(\tau)
introduced in Section
3.2
are nearly rate-optimal when
\tau
is chosen properly.

Remark 10
.

To the best of our knowledge, for covariance or autocovaraince estimation problems, the minimax lower bound in Proposition
8
is the first operator norm lower bound result with the phase transition phenomenon. This lower bound is sharper than that in
Fan et al., 2021
by constructing a special multivariate discrete distribution with the
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment condition; see Appendix
B
for details.

5
Algorithm and Implementation

5.1
Linearized ADMM algorithm

The constrained Yule–Walker estimators in (
6
), (
12
) and (
19
) are convex optimization problems as each of them consists of a convex objective function and a convex constraint function. For the constrained problem in (
6
), define the constraint set
\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(\lambda)=\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}:\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathbf{M})\leq\lambda\}
and the optimization problem can be rewritten as

\min_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}),\quad\text{subject to }\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}~\text{and}~\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(\lambda).

(48)

The augmented Lagrangian form is given as

\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D};\mathbf{W})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})-\langle\mathbf{W},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{D}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{D}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},

(49)

subject to
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{C}(\lambda)
, where
\mathbf{W}
is the Lagrangian multiplier and
\rho
is the regularization parameter. The augmented Lagrangian form can be solved by the alternating direction method of multipliers
(
Boyd et al., 2011
, ADMM)
with the iterative updates of
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{W})
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{A}^{(j+1)}&=\underset{\mathbf{A}}{\argmin}~\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{D}^{(j)}-\mathbf{W}^{(j)}/\rho\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},\\
\mathbf{D}^{(j+1)}&=\underset{\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(\lambda)}{\argmin}\|\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(j+1)}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}+\mathbf{W}^{(j)}/\rho\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},\\
\mathbf{W}^{(j+1)}&=\mathbf{W}^{(j)}+\rho(\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(j+1)}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}+\mathbf{D}^{(j+1)}).\end{split}

(50)

The efficiency of the ADMM algorithm depends largely on the complexity of the resulting subproblems.
Note that
\mathbf{A}
-update in the ADMM algorithm is a regularized least squares problem of
\mathbf{A}
, which might not have an explicit solution and can be computationally expensive.

To alleviate the computational burden in
\mathbf{A}
-update, we use an inexact proximal method to minimize

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rho}^{(j)}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D}^{(j)};\mathbf{W}^{(j)})\\
=&\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})+\frac{\rho\mu}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}-\left[\mathbf{A}^{(j)}-\frac{2}{\mu}(\mathbf{A}^{(j)}\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}-\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{D}^{(j)}-\mathbf{W}^{(j)}/\rho)\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}\right]\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\end{split}

(51)

where
\mu
is another prespecified parameter.
As in
Wang and Yuan, 2012
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(j)}_{\rho}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D}^{(j)};\mathbf{W}^{(j)})
can be viewed as a linearized approximation of
\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D}^{(j)};\mathbf{W}^{(j)})
.

For the separated constrained Yule–Walker estimation in (
12
), the linearized ADMM algorithm can be developed in similar fashion. For the
\ell_{1}
norm and nuclear norm regularized estimators, in
\mathbf{D}
-update and the proximal variant of
\mathbf{A}
-update, the soft-thresholding operator and truncation operator can be applied to the elements and singular values, respectively. The proposed ADMM algorithm can be illustrated as a particular application of the inexact Bregman ADMM algorithm
(
Wang and Banerjee, 2014
)
. To guarantee the global convergence,
\mu/2
needs to be greater than the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{\widetilde{\Sigma}}_{0}^{2}
.

In summary, the ADMM algorithm has explicit updates and can be solved efficiently. The details of the linearized ADMM algorithm for sparse and reduced-rank VAR models are presented in Appendix
C
.

5.2
Linear and semidefinite programmings

Some specific cases of the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
6
) can be formulated as linear programmings (LP) or semidefinite programs (SDP), and can thus be efficiently solved by any of the standard LP or SDP solvers.

As discussed in Example
2
, for sparse VAR models, a separated constrained minimization framework in (
12
) is proposed with
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Following the lead of Dantzig selector
(
Candès and Tao, 2007
)
, each of the sub-problems can be recast to an LP:

\begin{split}\text{minimize}&~\mathbf{1}_{pd}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\\
\text{subject to}&~-\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{a}_{i}\leq\mathbf{u}~\text{and}~-\lambda\cdot\mathbf{1}_{pd}\leq\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1i}-\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}\mathbf{a}_{i}\leq\lambda\cdot\mathbf{1}_{pd},\end{split}

(52)

where the optimization variables are
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}
and
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}
. As discussed in
Candès and Tao, 2007
, there is a large class of efficient algorithms for solving such problems.

Similarly, the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
6
), with
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{nuc}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
, can be used to estimate the reduced-rank VAR models. As introduced by
Candès and Plan, 2011
, the nuclear norm minimization problem can be recast to a SDP with a linear matrix inequality constraint:

\begin{split}\text{minimize}&~[\text{tr}(\mathbf{W}_{1})+\text{tr}(\mathbf{W}_{2})]/2\\
\text{subject to}&~\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{1}&\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{A}^{\top}&\mathbf{W}_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,~\text{and}~\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p}&\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{A}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\top}-\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}\mathbf{A}^{\top}&\lambda\mathbf{I}_{pd}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\end{split}

(53)

with optimization variables
\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd\times pd}
, and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
. Interior point methods or first-order methods, such as splitting cone solver, can be applied to efficiently solve large-scale SDP.

For linear-restricted VAR models, the
\ell_{\infty}
optimization problem of the constrained Yule–Walker estimator in (
19
) can also be formulated as a LP:

\begin{split}\text{minimize}&~t\\
\text{subject to}&~-t\cdot\mathbf{1}_{r}\leq\boldsymbol{\theta}\leq t\cdot\mathbf{1}_{r},~\text{and}~-\lambda\cdot\mathbf{1}_{r}\leq\mathbf{\widetilde{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}\leq\lambda\cdot\mathbf{1}_{r},\end{split}

(54)

with optimization variables
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, and can be solved by standard LP solvers.

As the data truncation and robust autocovariance estimator calculation are computationally cheap, the operation time of ADMM, LP and SDP algorithms mainly depends on the dimension
p
and AR order
d
. As shown in the simulation experiments in Section
6
, the proposed ADMM algorithm is much more efficient than the LP and SDP solvers, especially when
p
is large.

5.3
Tuning parameter selection

For the constrained Yule–Walker estimators, both the robustification parameter(s)
\tau
(or
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
) and constraint parameter
\lambda
need to adapt properly to the dimension
p
, sample size
T
and moment condition bound
M_{2+2\epsilon}
to achieve optimal trade-off between the truncation bias and tail robustness. However, based on the conditions and convergence rates in Section
3
, the optimal value of both parameters rely on some unknown parameters
\epsilon
and
M_{2+2\epsilon}
. In the literature on robust estimation of
i.i.d.
data, cross-validation is an intuitive data-driven method to select robustification and regularization parameters. To accommodate the intrinsically ordered nature of time series data, we use a rolling forecasting validation, one of the standard approaches to tuning parameter selection for time series data.

To simultaneously select robustification and constraint parameters, a two-dimensional or multidimensional grid of tuning parameters is constructed.
The search of the constraint parameter
\lambda
starts from a predetermined
\lambda_{\max}
and decreases in log-linear increments. For the robustification parameter
\tau
, grid search on the data-driven interval
[\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}]
is suggested, where
\tau_{\min}
and
\tau_{\max}
are empirical quantiles of the data. For instance, to tune
\tau
for the element truncation estimators, we may set
\tau_{\min}
and
\tau_{\max}
to be the median and maximum of
|y_{it}|
, for all
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq t\leq T
, respectively. If at least one of
p
and
T
is very large, we may calculate the quantiles of some randomly sampled
|y_{it}|
.

For the dataset with time series observations from
t=1
to
T
, we determine a validation period
[T_{\text{val}},T]
whose length
T-T_{\text{val}}
is supposed to be slightly smaller than
T
. Given the constraint and robustification parameters
(\lambda,\tau)
, at any time point
t\in[T_{\text{val}},T-1]
, we use all the historical data to calculate the constrained Yule–Walker estimates
\mathbf{\widehat{A}}(\lambda,\tau)
and the corresponding one-step-ahead forecast
\mathbf{\widehat{A}}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{x}_{t}
. The performance evaluation is based on the mean squared forecast error (MSFE)

\text{MSFE}(\lambda,\tau;T_{\text{val}},T)=\frac{1}{T-T_{\text{val}}}\sum_{t=T_{\text{val}}+1}^{T}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{\widehat{A}}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

(55)

and the tuning parameters are selected by minimizing the
\text{MSFE}(\lambda,\tau;T_{\text{val}},T)
over the grid points.

Simulation Study

6.1
VAR estimation

In this subsection, we compare the performance of the robust procedure and two standard estimation procedures for sparse, reduced-rank, and banded VAR models in finite samples. For each model, we consider three innovation settings: standardized
t_{2.1}
-distributed innovations, standardized log-normal innovations, and standard Gaussian innovations. They represent heavy-tailed symmetric distributions with finite second moments, heavy-tailed asymmetric distributions with finite fourth moments, and light-tailed symmetric distribution, respectively. The data are drawn from the VAR(1) process
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
with the following structures of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
.

DGP-1: Sparse VAR with
\mathbf{A}^{*}
specified as
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0.5
if
i=j
,
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0.4
if
i-j=1
,
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=-0.4
if
i-j=-1
, and
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0
otherwise. We consider
p\in\{30,50\}
and

.

DGP-2: Reduced-rank VAR with
\mathbf{A}^{*}
having a low-rank singular value decomposition
\mathbf{A}^{*}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
. Specifically, we consider a fixed
\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(1.5,0.8)
, and generate random orthonormal
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}
from the first two leading singular vectors of a random
p
-by-
p
Gaussian matrix. We consider
p\in\{30,50\}
and
T\in\{300,350,400,450,500\}
.

DGP-3: Banded VAR with a banded
\mathbf{A}^{*}
having bandwidth
k=2
. Specifically,
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0.5
if
i=j
;
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0.3
if
i-j=1
;
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=-0.3
if
i-j=-1
;
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0.2
if
|i-j|=2
; and
\mathbf{A}^{*}_{ij}=0
for all
|i-j|>2
. We consider
p\in\{50,100\}
and
T\in\{100,150,200,250,300\}
.

We first specify the robust estimation procedure, denoted as ROB, for these three DGPs. For DGP-1, as discussed in Example
2
, we apply the split estimation method in (
12
) with
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, and the element-wise truncation autocovariance estimators
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}^{\text{E}}(\tau)
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}^{\text{E}}(\tau)
specified in Section
3.1
. For DGP-2, as discussed in Example
1
, we apply the estimation method in (
6
) with
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{nuc}}

\tau_{1}
on

,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}\right|>T\varepsilon\right)&\leq C[1+T/q+\mu(\varepsilon)]\exp[-q\mu(\varepsilon)]\\
&+CT\left[1+5\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbb{E}x_{t}^{k})^{1/(2k+1)}\right]\alpha\left(\left[\frac{T}{q+1}\right]\right)^{2k/(2k+1)}.\end{split}

(130)

Since there exists a constant
\bar{r}<1
such that
r(p)<\bar{r}
for all
p
, letting
q=T/(c_{1}\log(T))
for sufficiently large
c_{1}>0
, we have
\alpha(T/(q+1))\asymp T^{-c_{2}}
, where
c_{2}
can be arbitrarily large as long as
c_{1}
is sufficiently large. Therefore, for any fixed
\varepsilon
and when
T\to\infty
, the second term of the upper bound in (
130
) can be arbitrarily small. In this case, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}\right|>T\varepsilon\right)\leq C[\log(T)+\mu(\varepsilon)]\exp\left[-\frac{T\mu(\varepsilon)}{\log(T)}\right].

(131)

∎

We state a covariance inequality for
\alpha
-mixing random variables from
Doukhan, 1994
.

Lemma 4
.

If
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}+|Y|^{q}]<\infty
for some
p,q\geq 1
and
1/p+1/q<1
, it holds that

|\textup{Cov}(X,Y)|\leq 8\alpha^{1/r}[\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}]^{1/p}[\mathbb{E}|Y|^{q}]^{1/q},

(132)

where
r=(1-p^{-1}-q^{-1})^{-1}
and
\alpha
is the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient between
X
and
Y
.

The definition of
\beta

\mathbf{y}_{t}

We then have a Bernstein-type concentration inequality for geometrically
\beta

Lemma 5
.

Let
\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be a family of self-adjoint stationary

-mixing random matrices of size

with mean zero and mixing coefficient
\beta(\ell)\leq r^{\ell}
for some
r\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\leq M
almost surely, then for any

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq d\exp\left(-\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{v^{2}T+c^{-1}M^{2}+\varepsilon M\gamma(c,T)}\right),

(133)

where

v^{2}=\sup_{K\subset\{1,\dots,T\}}\frac{1}{\textup{Card}(K)}\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t\in K}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{2}\right)

(134)

and

\gamma(c,T)=\frac{\log(T)}{\log(2)}\max\left(2,\frac{32\log(T)}{c\log(2)}\right).

Appendix B
Proof of Lower Bound Results

In this section, we provide proofs of the minimax lower bound results for the estimation problems of autocovariance matrices and VAR models. We prove the lower bounds for autocovariance estimation in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm and sparse VAR estimation in Section
B.1
and those of the operator norm lower bound for autocovariance estimation and the Frobenius norm lower bound for reduced-rank VAR estimation in Section
B.2
. Some auxiliary lemmas are presented in Section
B.3
.

We start with some notations. For any real number
x
, the ceiling function
\lceil x\rceil
is defined as the smallest integer not smaller than
x
and the floor function
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the largest integer not larger than
x
. For any binary vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, the Hamming distance of
\mathbf{v}_{1}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}
, denoted by
d_{H}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})
, is defined as the number of different entries in the two vectors.

B.1
Maximum Norm Lower Bounds and Sparse VAR

We start with constructing minimax lower bounds of autocovariance matrix estimation in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

The proof consists of four steps. The first three steps present the minimax lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
with
d=1
. Specifically, in the first step, a pair of discrete distributions are constructed. In the second step, we consider a pair of
\alpha
-mixing time series
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}\}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}^{\prime}\}
with the mixing coefficients decayed geometrically. In the third step, we show that with probability at a constant level, it is impossible to obtain an estimation error smaller than the minimax lower bound for
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}\}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}^{\prime}\}
simultaneously.
In the last step, the minimax lower bound is extended to the case of
d>1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
.
Note that we establish the lower bounds in a non-asymptotic sense, where all parameters including
p
,
T
and
M
are assumed to be finite.

Step 1. Specify a pair of bivariate distributions

First, motivated by
Devroye et al., 2016
, we construct a pair of discrete bivariate distributions for
(y_{1},y_{2})^{\top}
. Specifically, for the given moment bound
M
, we consider the bivariate distribution
\mathcal{P}_{c,\gamma}=\{P_{+},P_{-}\}
with some positive constants
c>0
and
0<\gamma<1
such that
c^{2+2\epsilon}\gamma=M
, in which

\begin{split}&P_{+}(y_{1}=c,y_{2}=c)=P_{+}(y_{1}=-c,y_{2}=-c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\quad P_{+}(y_{1}=y_{2}=0)=1-\gamma,\\
&P_{-}(y_{1}=c,y_{2}=-c)=P_{-}(y_{1}=-c,y_{2}=c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},~~\text{and}~~P_{-}(y_{1}=y_{2}=0)=1-\gamma.\end{split}

(136)

It is simple to check that under both distributions
P_{+}
and
P_{-}
,
\mathbb{E}(y_{1})=\mathbb{E}(y_{2})=0
and
\mathbb{E}(|y_{1}|^{2+2\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}(|y_{2}|^{2+2\epsilon})=c^{2+2\epsilon}\gamma=M
. In addition,
P_{+}(y_{1}y_{2}=c^{2})=\gamma
,
P_{+}(y_{1}y_{2}=0)=1-\gamma
,
P_{-}(y_{1}y_{2}=-c^{2})=\gamma
, and
P_{-}(y_{1}y_{2}=0)=1-\gamma
. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{+}(y_{1}y_{2})=c^{2}\gamma
and
\mathbb{E}_{-}(y_{1}y_{2})=-c^{2}\gamma
.

Step 2. Construct an
\alpha
-mixing sequence

Let
v=r^{-2}>1
. For
t=1,2,\dots,T
, consider
\widetilde{T}:=\lceil(T/\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor)\rceil
time index groups
I_{1}=\{1,\dots,\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor\}
,
I_{2}=\{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor+1,\dots,2\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor\}
,
\cdots
,
I_{\widetilde{T}}=\{(\widetilde{T}-1)\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor+1,\dots,\widetilde{T}\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor\}
. For each
1\leq k\leq\widetilde{T}
, let
\{y_{1t},y_{2t},y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime}\}_{t\in I_{k}}
be random variables with the joint distribution

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\{y_{1t}=y_{2t}=y_{1t}^{\prime}=y_{2t}^{\prime}=0\}\right)=1-\gamma,\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\{y_{1t}=c,y_{2t}=c,y_{1t}^{\prime}=c,y_{2t}^{\prime}=-c\}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\\
\text{and }&\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\{y_{1t}=-c,y_{2t}=-c,y_{1t}^{\prime}=-c,y_{2t}^{\prime}=c\}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{2}.\end{split}

(137)

Marginally, it is easy to check that
(y_{1t},y_{2t})\sim P_{+}
and
(y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime})\sim P_{-}
. For the variables in different time index groups,
\{y_{1t},y_{2t},y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime}\}
are independent.

For the rest of variables other than the first and second ones, we assume that
y_{it}=y_{it}^{\prime}
almost surely for
i\in\{3,4,\dots,p\}
and they are independent with
y_{1t}
and
y_{2t}
. In addition, they are independent at different time points. Though we only consider the data from
t=1
to
t=T
, we assume that the above distribution applies to all time indexes
t
so that we can study the mixing property of the stochastic process.

Let
\gamma=(2\widetilde{T})^{-1}\log(1/(2\delta))\leq 1/2
with some constant
\delta\in[\exp(-\widetilde{T})/2,1/2)
. Now we verify that both
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1t},y_{2t},y_{3t},\dots,y_{pt})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}=(y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime},y_{3t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{pt}^{\prime})^{\top}
are
\alpha
-mixing with mixing coefficients geometrically decayed. As
\{(y_{3t},\dots,y_{pt})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{(y_{3t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{pt}^{\prime})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are independent series and are independent with
\{y_{1t},y_{2t},y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime}\}
, it suffices to show that
\{(y_{1t},y_{2t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{(y_{1t}^{\prime},y_{2t}^{\prime})\}_{t=1}^{T}
satisfy the
\alpha
-mixing condition.

Without loss of generality, we first consider
t_{1},t_{2}\in I_{1}
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
. By definition, it can easily be checked that,

\begin{split}&\alpha(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{t_{1}},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\infty})=\alpha\left(\{(y_{1t},y_{2t})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{t_{1}},\{(y_{1t},y_{2t})^{\top}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor}\right)\\
=&\sup|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|,~~A\in\sigma(\{(y_{1t},y_{2t})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{t_{1}})~\text{and}~B\in\sigma(\{(y_{1t},y_{2t})^{\top}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor})\\
=&\max\{(1-\gamma)-(1-\gamma)^{2},\gamma/2-\gamma^{2}/4,\gamma(1-\gamma)\}\\
\leq&\gamma=\frac{\log(1/(2\delta))}{2\widetilde{T}}=Cr^{2\log_{v}\widetilde{T}}\leq Cr^{\log_{v}T}\leq Cr^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor}\leq Cr^{t_{2}-t_{1}}.\end{split}

(138)

In addition, for
t_{1}<t_{2}
in different time index groups, it is obvious that

\alpha(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{t_{1}},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\infty})=0,

(139)

because the variables in different groups are independent.
Therefore, the constructed
p
-dimensional series
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{t}
are both
\alpha
-mixing with the mixing coefficients decayed geometrically.

Step 3. Establish the lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
with
d=1

As
1-\gamma\geq\exp[-\gamma/(1-\gamma)]
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})=(1-\gamma)^{\widetilde{T}}\geq\exp\left(\frac{-\gamma\widetilde{T}}{1-\gamma}\right)\geq\exp(-2\gamma\widetilde{T})=2\delta.

(140)

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\cdot)
be any mean estimator of
(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})_{1,2}
. Then,

\begin{split}&\max\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-c^{2}\gamma\right|>c^{2}\gamma\right),\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})+c^{2}\gamma\right|>c^{2}\gamma\right)\right]\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-c^{2}\gamma\right|>c^{2}\gamma,\text{ or }\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})+c^{2}\gamma\right|>c^{2}\gamma\right]\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})\right]\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T}\right]\geq\delta,\end{split}

(141)

where
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})
, following directly from
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

Taking
\delta=1/3
, we have that

c^{2}\gamma=M^{1/(1+\epsilon)}\gamma^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}=\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(1/(2\delta))}{2\widetilde{T}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\asymp\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(142)

Hence, we have

\begin{split}\max&\left\{\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-\mathbb{E}[y_{1t}y_{2t}]\right|\gtrsim\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right],\right.\\
&\quad\left.\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{1,2}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})-\mathbb{E}[y_{1t}^{\prime}y_{2t}^{\prime}]\right|\gtrsim\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right]\right\}\geq 1/3.\end{split}

(143)

Therefore, as
\mathcal{P}_{c,\gamma}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{E}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, the minimax lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm can be established.

Step 4. Extension to the case of
d>1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}

Since the analysis in the first three steps is independent of
p
, the above minimax lower bound result can be extended directly to the
pd
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top},\mathbf{y}_{t-2}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{t-d}^{\top})^{\top}
and the
p(d+1)
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{z}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top},\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top})^{\top}
. Hence, the minimax lower bound results for the case of
d>1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
can be obtained in a similar fashion, so the details are omitted for brevity.

∎

Next, we apply the techniques developed above to the estimation of sparse VAR models.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Similarly to the proof of Proposition
7
, we focus on the case of
d=1
, as the proof can readily be extended to the general case of
d>1
. The proof consists of three steps.
In the first step, a pair of joint distributions of
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}\}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}^{\prime}\}
are constructed. In the second step, we verify that the constructed time series are
\alpha
-mixing with mixing coefficients decayed geometrically. In the final step, we show that with probability at a constant level, it is impossible to obtain an estimation error smaller than the minimax lower bound for
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}\}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{T}^{\prime}\}
simultaneously.

Step 1. Specify a class of distribution

For simplicity, for given
0<\epsilon\leq 1
, sparsity level
s_{0}=s
,
c>0
, and
0<\gamma<1/s
, we consider the bivariate distribution
\mathcal{P}_{c,\gamma,s,\epsilon}=\{P_{+},P_{-}\}
for random variables
\{x,y\}
, where

\begin{split}&P_{+}(x=cs^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)},y=c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\quad\quad P_{+}(x=-cs^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)},y=-c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\\
&P_{+}(x=0,y=c)=\frac{(s-1)\gamma}{2},\quad\quad P_{+}(x=0,y=-c)=\frac{(s-1)\gamma}{2},\\
&P_{+}(x=0,y=0)=1-s\gamma,\end{split}

(144)

and

\begin{split}&P_{-}(x=cs^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)},y=-c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\quad\quad P_{-}(x=-cs^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)},y=c)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\\
&P_{-}(x=0,y=c)=\frac{(s-1)\gamma}{2},\quad\quad P_{-}(x=0,y=-c)=\frac{(s-1)\gamma}{2},\\
&P_{-}(x=0,y=0)=1-s\gamma.\end{split}

(145)

We have that under both distributions,
\mathbb{E}(x)=\mathbb{E}(y)=0
,
\mathbb{E}(x^{2})=c^{2}s^{2\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}\gamma
,
\mathbb{E}(y^{2})=c^{2}s\gamma
,
\mathbb{E}|x|^{2+2\epsilon}=c^{2+2\epsilon}s^{2\epsilon}\gamma:=M
,
\mathbb{E}|y|^{2+2\epsilon}=c^{2+2\epsilon}s\gamma
. Also,
\mathbb{E}_{+}(xy)=c^{2}s^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}\gamma
and
\mathbb{E}_{-}(xy)=-c^{2}s^{\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}\gamma
.

Step 2. Construct an
\alpha
-mixing sequence

Note that
y_{s+1,t+1}
is only correlated with
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(y_{1t},\dots,y_{st})^{\top}
, and
y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}
is only correlated with
\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}=(y_{1t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{st}^{\prime})^{\top}
. Let
v=r^{-2}>1
. For
t=1,2,\dots,T
, consider
\widetilde{T}:=\lceil(T/\log_{v}T)\rceil
index groups
I_{1}=\{1,\dots,\lceil\log_{v}T\rceil\}
,
I_{2}=\{\lceil\log_{v}T\rceil+1,\dots,2\lceil\log_{v}T\rceil\}
,
\dots
,
I_{\widetilde{T}}=\{(\widetilde{T}-1)\lceil\log_{v}T\rceil+1,\dots,\widetilde{T}\lceil\log_{v}T\rceil\}
. For each
1\leq k\leq\widetilde{T}
, let
\{\mathbf{x}_{t},y_{s+1,t+1},\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}\}_{t\in I_{k}}
be random variables with the joint distribution

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t}=cs^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\mathbf{e}_{i},y_{s+1,t+1}=c,\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}=cs^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\mathbf{e}_{i},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}=-c\right\}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{2},~~1\leq i\leq s,\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t}=-cs^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\mathbf{e}_{i},y_{s+1,t+1}=-c,\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}=-cs^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\mathbf{e}_{i},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}=c\right\}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{2},~~1\leq i\leq s,\\
&\text{and}~~\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{t\in I_{k}}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0},y_{s+1,t+1}=y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}=0\right\}\right)=1-s\gamma.\end{split}

(146)

Marginally, it is easy to check that each pair of
(y_{it},y_{s+1,t+1})\sim P_{+}
and
(y_{it}^{\prime},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime})\sim P_{-}
, for
i=1,\dots,s
, respectively. In addition, for any
1\leq i<j\leq s
,
\text{Cov}(y_{it},y_{jt})=0
.

For the rest of variables other than the first
s+1
variables, we assume that
y_{it}=y_{it}^{\prime}
almost surely for
i\in\{s+2,\dots,p\}
and they are independent with
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
y_{s+1,t}
. Let
\gamma=(2s\widetilde{T})^{-1}\log(1/(2\delta))\leq 1/2
with some constant
\delta\in[\exp(-\widetilde{T}/2,1/2))
. Since both
\{(y_{s+2,t},\dots,y_{pt})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{(y_{s+2,t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{pt}^{\prime})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are independent series and they are independent with the first
s+1
variables, it suffices to show that
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top},y_{s,t})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\prime}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{{}^{\prime}\top},y_{s,t}^{\prime})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
satisfy the
\alpha
-mixing condition.

Without loss of generality, we first consider
t_{1},t_{2}\in I_{1}
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
. By definition, it can be checked that

\begin{split}&\alpha(\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{t_{1}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\infty})=\alpha(\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{t_{1}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor})\\
=&\sup|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|,~~A\in\sigma(\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{t_{1}}),~~B\in\sigma(\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor})\\
=&\max\{(1-s\gamma)-(1-s\gamma)^{2},s\gamma/2-s^{2}\gamma^{2}/4,s\gamma(1-s\gamma)\}\\
\leq&s\gamma=\frac{\log(1/2\delta)}{2\widetilde{T}}=Cr^{2\log_{v}\widetilde{T}}\leq Cr^{\log_{v}T}\leq Cr^{\lfloor\log_{v}T\rfloor}\leq Cr^{t_{2}-t_{1}}.\end{split}

(147)

In addition, for
t_{1}<t_{2}
in different index groups, as the variables in different index groups are independent, we have

\alpha(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=-\infty}^{t_{1}},\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=t_{2}}^{\infty})=0.

(148)

Therefore, the constructed
p
-dimensional series
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}
are both
\alpha
-mixing with the mixing coefficients decayed geometrically.

Step 3. Establish the lower bound

For the constructed sequence
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}
, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\{y_{1t},\dots,y_{st},y_{s+1,t+1}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{y_{1t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{st}^{\prime},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})=(1-s\gamma)^{\widetilde{T}}\\
\geq&\exp\left(\frac{s\gamma\widetilde{T}}{1-s\gamma}\right)\geq\exp(-2s\gamma\widetilde{T})=2\delta.\end{split}

(149)

We consider the estimation task of coefficients of the predictors
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(y_{1t},\dots,y_{st})^{\top}
and the response
y_{s+1,t}
. By the Yule–Walker equation, the true values of the coefficient vector are defined as
\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}y_{s+1,t}]
and
\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{t}\})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{{}^{\prime}\top}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t}y^{\prime}_{s+1,t}]
, respectively. As
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime\top}]
is a diagonal matrix, it can be obtained that
\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}c^{2}s^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}}\gamma
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\cdot)
be any mean estimator of
\mathbf{a}_{s+1}
. Then,

\begin{split}&\max\left\{\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{y_{t}\})\right\|_{2}>\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})\|_{2}\right],\right.\\
&\quad\quad\quad\left.\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})+\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\right\|_{2}>\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\|_{2}\right]\right\}\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})\right\|_{2}>\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})\|_{2},\right.\\
&\left.\quad\quad\quad\text{ or }\left\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})+\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\right\|_{2}>\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\|_{2}\right]\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s+1}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T})\right]\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T}\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\{y_{1t},\dots,y_{st},y_{s+1,t+1}\}_{t=1}^{T}=\{y_{1t}^{\prime},\dots,y_{st}^{\prime},y_{s+1,t+1}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T}\right]\geq\delta,\end{split}

(150)

Taking
\delta=1/3
, we have that

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\})\|_{2}=&\|\mathbf{a}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}\})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}(c^{2}s^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\gamma^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}})\sqrt{s}\gamma^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}\sqrt{s}\gamma^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\\
=&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}\sqrt{s}\left[\frac{\log(1/(2\delta))}{2\widetilde{T}}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\asymp\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\sqrt{s}\left[\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}\log(T)}{T}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.\end{split}

(151)

Therefore, the minimax lower bound for
\mathbf{a}_{s+1}
in terms of
\ell_{2}
norm can be derived, and the
\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}
lower bound can be developed accordingly.

Finally, for
d>1
, we consider the same data generating process and use the VAR(
d
) model as the running model. As the coefficient vector in the VAR(1) model is a sub-vector of that in the VAR(
d
) model, the lower bound result for the VAR(1) model can be extended directly to the VAR(
d
) model.

∎

B.2
Operator Norm Lower Bounds and Reduced-Rank VAR

In this subsection, we establish the minimax lower bounds of the autocovariance estimation in the operator norm and estimation of the reduced-rank VAR.

Proof of Proposition
8
.

The proof consists of three steps. The operator norm minimax lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
with
d=1
is developed in the first three steps. In the first step, we construct a class of multivariate distributions with some moment conditions. In the second step, we apply Fano’s inequality (Lemma
7
) to show that with probability at a constant level, it is impossible to obtain an estimation error in the operator norm smaller than the minimax lower bound. Finally, the lower bound result is extended to the case of
d>1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
in the third step.

Step 1. Construct a class of discrete multivariate distributions

Without loss of generality, we assume that the dimension
p
is a multiple of 2, i.e.,
p=2h
, where
h
is a positive integer. Consider
2^{p}
vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{2^{p}}
where each
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\{p^{-1/2},-p^{-1/2}\}^{p}
, for
i=1,\dots,2^{p}
.

For any
\mathbf{v}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 2^{p}
, it can be obtained that

\sum_{j=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}=2^{p}/p
. Based on
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, define the corresponding multivariate distribution
P_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}
, where

\begin{split}&P_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{j})=\frac{\gamma}{p}+\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}\gamma,\quad j=1,\dots,2^{p}\\
\text{and}~&P_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}_{p})=1-\frac{2^{p}}{p}\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}\gamma=1-\frac{2^{p+1}}{p}\gamma,\end{split}

(152)

with the parameters
c
and
\gamma
satisfying that
c^{2+2\epsilon}\gamma p^{-(2+\epsilon)}2^{p+3}=M
and
\gamma<2^{-(p+1)}p
.

Under each distribution
P_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}
, it can be checked that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}[\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{0}_{p}
,

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\top}]=\left[\frac{2^{p}}{p^{2}}+\frac{2^{p}(p-2)}{p^{3}}\right]c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{I}_{p}+\frac{2^{p+1}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},

(153)

and

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}\left[|\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{y}|^{2+2\epsilon}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{p}}|\langle c\mathbf{v}_{j},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle|^{2+2\epsilon}P_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{j})\\
=&c^{2+2\epsilon}\frac{\gamma}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{p}}|\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle|^{2+2\epsilon}+c^{2+2\epsilon}\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{2^{p}}|\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle|^{4+2\epsilon}\\
\leq&c^{2+2\epsilon}\gamma p^{-2-\epsilon}2^{p+3}=M.\end{split}

(154)

For
h=p/2
, by Lemma
6
, there exist
N\geq\exp(h/8)
binary vectors
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{N}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
such that
d_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{k})\geq h/4
for all
1\leq j\neq k\leq h
, where
d_{\text{H}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Hamming distance that measures the number of different entries in two binary vectors.

For each
\mathbf{z}_{j}=(z_{j1},\dots,z_{jh})^{\top}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, let

\mathbf{w}_{j}=p^{-1/2}\left(z_{j1}(1,1)+(1-z_{j1})(1,-1),\dots,z_{jh}(1,1)+(1-z_{jh})(1,-1)\right)^{\top}

(155)

and note that
\mathbf{w}_{j}\in\{p^{-1/2},-p^{-1/2}\}^{p}
.

Consider a class of
N
multivariate distributions
\mathcal{P}_{c,\gamma}=\{P_{\mathbf{w}_{1}},\dots,P_{\mathbf{w}_{N}}\}
. For the operator norm lower bound, we consider the independent setting, which is also a special case of strong mixing. Specifically, denote the distribution of
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}^{T}
with
i.i.d.
data, where each
\mathbf{y}_{t}
follows the distribution
P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}
.

Step 2. Establish the lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
with
d=1

For the multivariate distributions
P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}
and
P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}
, the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence of these two distributions is defined as

\begin{split}\text{KL}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}},P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})=&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})\log\left(\frac{P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}{P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}\right)\\
=&\frac{\gamma}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\log\left(\frac{P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}{P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}\right)+\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2}\log\left(\frac{P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}{P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}\right).\end{split}

(156)

Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\log\left(\frac{P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}{P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i})}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}=c\mathbf{v}_{i}))=0.

(157)

In addition, as
\log(1+t)\leq t
for
t\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2}\log\left(\frac{P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i})}{P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i})}\right)\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2}[\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i}))-\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i}))]\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2}\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2}\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i}))\\
+&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}(\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2}-\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2})\log(P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}(\mathbf{y}-c\mathbf{v}_{i}))\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}(\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2}-\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2})\log(1+p\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2})\\
\leq&p\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}(\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2}-\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle^{2})\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{2}\\
\leq&p\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle^{4}=\frac{2^{p+1}}{p^{2}}(1.5p-1)\leq 2^{p+2}p^{-1}.\end{split}

(158)

Hence, we have that
\text{KL}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}},P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})\leq\gamma p^{-1}2^{p+2}
.

For
1\leq j\neq k\leq N
,
d_{H}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{k})\geq h/4=p/8
. Hence, we have

\begin{split}&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}\|_{\textup{op}}^{2}=\frac{2^{2p+2}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\|\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\|_{\textup{op}}^{2}\\
\geq&\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\|\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\|(\sqrt{p}\mathbf{w}_{j})(\sqrt{p}\mathbf{w}_{j})^{\top}-(\sqrt{p}\mathbf{w}_{k})(\sqrt{p}\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\frac{32}{p^{2}}\sum_{1\leq s,r\leq h}1\{z_{js}\neq z_{ks},z_{jr}\neq z_{kr}\}\\
\geq&\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\frac{32}{p^{2}}\left(\frac{p}{8}\right)^{2}=\frac{2^{2p-4}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}.\end{split}

(159)

Since
p<128T
, we take
\gamma=p^{2}2^{-(p+8)}T^{-1}
and have that

\frac{2^{p-2}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma=\frac{1}{32}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}(p^{-1}2^{p+3}\gamma)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}=\frac{1}{32}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}\left(\frac{p}{32T}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\geq\frac{1}{1024}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}\left(\frac{p}{T}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(160)

Also, for any
1\leq j,k\leq N
,

\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{T}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}},\mathbb{P}^{T}_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})=T\cdot\text{KL}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}},P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})\leq T\gamma p^{-1}2^{p+2}=\frac{p}{64}.

(161)

By the Fano’s inequality in Lemma
7
, when
p\geq 20>64\log 2
, we have

\begin{split}&\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\mathbb{P}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}\|_{\textup{op}}\geq\frac{1}{1024}\left(\frac{M^{1/\epsilon}p}{T}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right]\\
\geq&1-\frac{N^{-2}\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq N}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{T}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}},\mathbb{P}^{T}_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})+\log 2}{\log N}\\
\geq&1-\frac{(p/64)+\log 2}{h/8}\geq 1-\frac{(p/64)+(p/64)}{p/16}=\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(162)

Therefore, as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{1}}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{2}}^{T},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{N}}^{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, the required minimax lower bound for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
with
d=1
is obtained.

Step 3. Extension to the case of
d>1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}

For the case of
d>1
, consider the proposed distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{1}}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{2}}^{T},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{N}}^{T}
with
i.i.d.
samples. Let
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{t-d}^{\top})^{\top}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}]
is a block diagonal matrix with
d
blocks
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}
. Hence, the minimax lower in the operator norm can be obtained accordingly.

For
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
, without loss of generality, assume that
p
is a multiple of 4, i.e.,
p=4h
, where
h
is a positive integer. Let
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top})^{\top}
, where each
\mathbf{y}_{it}
is a
2h
-dimensional vector. Assume that
\mathbf{y}_{1t}=\mathbf{y}_{2,t-1}
almost surely, and
\mathbf{y}_{1t}
is a sequence of

random vectors. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top}]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{2,t-1}^{\top}]\\
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{2,t-1}]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top}]\\
\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}\end{bmatrix}.

(163)

Hence, we can apply the constructed distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{1}}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{2}}^{T},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{N}}^{T}
to
\mathbf{y}_{1t}
, where each
\mathbf{w}_{j}\in\{(2h)^{-1/2},(2h)^{-1/2}\}^{2h}
. The required minimax lower bound can be obtained similarly and the proof is omitted for brevity.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The proof consists of three steps. In the first step, for the special case of
r_{0}=1
and
d=1
, we apply the proposed multivariate discrete distributions to construct a special time series process. In the second step, we apply Fano’s inequailty to obtain the minimax lower bound. In the last step, the lower bound result is extended to the general case of
r_{0}>1
and
d>1
.

Step 1. Construct a class of discrete time series

For simplicity, we first focus on the case with
r_{0}=1
and
d=1
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
p
is a multiple of
4
, that is,
p=4h
, where
h
is a positive integer. For
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we split it as
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top})^{\top}
, where each
\mathbf{y}_{it}
is a
2h
-dimensional vector.

Consider the following data generating process of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
:
\mathbf{y}_{1t}
is independent with
\mathbf{y}_{2t}
and all historical information
\{\mathbf{y}_{s}\}_{s<t}
, and
\mathbf{y}_{2t}
is only related to
\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}
. Let
\mathbf{u}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Similarly to the step 1 in the proof of Proposition
8
, we consider the multivariate distribution
P_{\mathbf{v}_{1}},\dots,P_{\mathbf{v}_{2^{p}}}
. As discussed in the proof of Proposition
8
,
P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\textup{V}}(M,\epsilon,r)
, for
j=1,\dots,N
. For
h=p/4
, by Lemma
6
, there exist
N\geq\exp(h/8)
binary vectors
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
such that
d_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{k})\geq h/4
for all
1\leq j\neq k\leq N
, where
d_{\text{H}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Hamming distance that measures the number of different entries in two binary vectors.

For each
\mathbf{z}_{j}=(z_{j1},\dots,z_{jh})^{\top}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, let

\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}=p^{-1/2}(z_{j1}(1,1)+(1-z_{j1})(1,-1),\dots,z_{jh}(1,1)+(1-z_{jh})(1,-1))^{\top}

(164)

and
\mathbf{w}_{j}=(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}_{j},\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}_{j})^{\top}\in\{p^{-1/2},-p^{-1/2}\}^{p}
.

Based on the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{N}
, consider
N
distributions
P_{\mathbf{w}_{1}},\dots,P_{\mathbf{w}_{N}}
for the vector
\mathbf{u}_{t}
. According to the proof of Proposition
8
, under the distribution
P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\top}]=&\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top}]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top}]\end{bmatrix}\\
=&\left[\frac{2^{p}}{p^{2}}+\frac{2^{p}(p-2)}{p^{3}}\right]c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{I}_{p}+\frac{2^{p+1}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\\
=&\left[\frac{2^{p}}{p^{2}}+\frac{2^{p}(p-2)}{p^{3}}\right]c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{I}_{p}+\frac{2^{p+1}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}&\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\\
\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}&\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(165)

Hence, we can further obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}]=&\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top}]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{\top}]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}}&\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(166)

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top}]=&\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{1t}\mathbf{y}_{2,t-1}^{\top}]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{1,t-1}^{\top}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{2t}\mathbf{y}_{2,t-1}^{\top}]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&c^{2}\gamma 2^{p+1}p^{-2}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(167)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}}=\left[\frac{2^{p}}{p^{2}}+\frac{2^{p}(p-2)}{p^{3}}\right]c^{2}\gamma\mathbf{I}_{2h}+\frac{2^{p+1}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}:=c^{2}\gamma(C_{1}\mathbf{I}_{2h}+C_{2}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top})\end{split}

(168)

and

\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}}^{-1}=c^{-2}\gamma^{-1}C_{1}^{-1}[\mathbf{I}_{2h}-2C_{2}/(2C_{1}+C_{2})\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}].

(169)

Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}^{T}
the distribution of
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{u}_{t}
follows the distribution
P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}
.
By Yule–Walker equation,

\mathbf{A}^{*}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}]^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbf{A}^{*}_{1,2}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})\\
\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}&\mathbf{0}_{2h\times 2h}\end{bmatrix}

(170)

is a rank-1 matrix, where

\begin{split}&\mathbf{A}^{*}_{1,2}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})=c^{2}\gamma 2^{p+1}p^{-2}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}}^{-1}=\frac{2C_{2}}{2C_{1}+C_{2}}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\\
=&\left[c^{-2}\gamma^{-1}C_{1}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{2C_{2}}{2C_{1}+C_{2}}\right)\right]c^{2}\gamma C_{1}\left(1-\frac{2C_{2}}{2C_{1}+C_{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{2C_{2}}{2C_{1}+C_{2}}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\\
=&\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}]^{-1}\right\|_{\textup{op}}C_{1}C_{3}c^{2}\gamma\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\end{split}

(171)

and
C_{3}=2C_{2}/(2C_{1}-C_{2})=p/(p-2)
. In addition, note that
\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}[\mathbf{y}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}]^{-1}\|_{\textup{op}}:=C_{4}
is a constant independent of the choice of
\mathbf{w}_{j}
.

Step 2. Establish the lower bound with
r_{0}=1
and
d=1

For
1\leq j\neq k\leq N
, as
d_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{w}_{j},\mathbf{w}_{k})\geq h/4=p/16
,

\begin{split}&\|\mathbf{A}^{*}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})-\mathbf{A}^{*}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{k}})\|_{\textup{op}}^{2}\\
=&C_{1}^{2}C_{3}^{2}C_{4}^{2}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}-\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&C_{1}^{2}C_{3}^{2}C_{4}^{2}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}-\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&C_{4}^{2}\frac{2^{2p}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\|(\sqrt{p}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j})(\sqrt{p}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j})^{\top}-(\sqrt{p}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}){\sqrt{p}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\top}}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&C_{4}^{2}\frac{2^{2p}}{p^{4}}c^{4}\gamma^{2}.\end{split}

(172)

Similarly to the proof of Proposition
8
, note that

\frac{2^{p}}{p^{2}}c^{2}\gamma=\frac{1}{8}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}(p^{-1}2^{p+3}\gamma)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(173)

Taking
\gamma=p^{2}2^{-(p+8)}T^{-1}
, we have that for any
1\leq j,k\leq N
,

\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}^{T})=T\gamma p^{-1}2^{p+2}=\frac{p}{128}.

(174)

By Fano’s inequality in Lemma
7
, when
p\geq 39>128\log(2)
,

\begin{split}&\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\mathbb{P}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}^{*}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})\|_{\textup{F}}\geq\frac{1}{256}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}(P_{\mathbf{w}_{j}})^{-1}\|_{\textup{op}}M^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}(p/T)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right]\\
\geq&1-\frac{N^{-2}\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq N}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}^{T})+\log 2}{\log N}\\
\geq&1-\frac{p/128+\log(2)}{p/32}\geq\frac{1}{2},\end{split}

(175)

which concludes the proof of the case with
r_{0}=1
and
d=1
.

Step 3. Extension to the general case of
r_{0}>1

For the case of
r_{0}>1
, we consider that
\mathbf{y}_{t}
can be split into
2r_{0}
components
\mathbf{y}_{t}=[\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(1)\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(1)\top},\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(2)\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(2)\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(r_{0})\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(r_{0})\top}]
, where
\{(\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(j)\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(j)\top})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{(\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(k)\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(k)\top})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are independent for
1\leq j,k\leq r_{0}
. Then, we can apply the same technique in the steps 1 and 2 to each
\{(\mathbf{y}_{1t}^{(j)\top},\mathbf{y}_{2t}^{(j)\top})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and obtain the required lower bound. In addition, the lower bound result for
d=1
directly implies that for
d>1
, so the proof of
d>1
is omitted for brevity.

B.3
Auxiliary Lemmas

In this section, we present some auxiliary lemmas used in the proofs of lower bound results.
The first lemma presents the number of vectors satisfying a certain Hamming distance, which is known as the Gilbert–Varshamov Lemma in
Massart, 2007
.

Lemma 6
.

There exist binary vectors
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
such that

1.

d_{H}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{k})\geq p/4
for all
j\neq k
;

2.

m\geq\exp(p/8)
.

The second lemma is a well known result from information theory called Fano’s inequality
(
Wainwright, 2019
)
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{M}
be
M
probability distributions,
M\geq 2
. Then,

\inf_{\psi}\max_{1\leq j\leq M}P_{j}[\psi(X)\neq j]\geq 1-\frac{M^{-2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{M}\textup{KL}(P_{j},P_{k})+\log 2}{\log M}

(176)

where
\textup{KL}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence of two distributions and the infimum is taken over all tests with values in
\{1,2,\dots,M\}
.
