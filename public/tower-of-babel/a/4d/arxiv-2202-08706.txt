Distributed Machine-Learning for Early HARQ Feedback Prediction in Cloud RANs PubID: pubid: 10.1109/TWC.2023.3275296 © 2023 IEEE
Barış Göktepe, Cornelius Hellge, Thomas Schierl, and Slawomir Stanczak

Abstract
In this work, we propose novel HARQ prediction schemes for Cloud RANs (C-RANs) that use feedback over a rate-limited feedback channel (2 - 6 bits) from the Remote Radio Heads (RRHs) to predict at the User Equipment (UE) the decoding outcome at the BaseBand Unit (BBU) ahead of actual decoding. In particular, we propose a Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model (DA2SGMM) that is trained in an end-to-end fashion over the whole C-RAN setup. Using realistic link-level simulations in the sub-THz band at 100 GHz, we show that the novel DA2SGMM HARQ prediction scheme clearly outperforms all other adapted and state-of-the-art schemes. The DA2SGMM shows a superior performance in terms of blockage detection as well as HARQ prediction in the no-blockage and single-blockage cases. In particular, the DA2SGMM with 4 bit feedback achieves a more than 200 % higher throughput in average compared to its best alternative. Compared to regular HARQ, the DA2SGMM reduces the maximum transmission latency by more than 72.4 %, while maintaining more than 75 % of the throughput in the no-blockage scenario. In the single-blockage scenario, DA2SGMM significantly increases the throughput for most of the evaluated Signal-to-Noise-Ratios (SNRs) compared to regular HARQ.



I
Introduction

The emergence of new services, such as Vehicle-To-Everything, Virtual Reality, Ultra-Reliable Low Latency Communication and many more, has increased the need for higher data rates and extremely low latencies. This has directed the interest of mobile communication standards to cover new and higher frequency bands. The Fifth Generation standardization body, the 3rd Generation Partnership Project, has recently finished a new work item targeting frequencies up to 71 GHz for access
[
1
]
. In particular, recent advances in hardware have paved the way for using these bands. The sub-THz and THz bands, which reach from 100 GHz up to 3 THz, are now in the focus for beyond Fifth Generation technologies
[
2
,
3
]
. However, the use of high-frequency bands has the disadvantage of being highly dependent on an unobstructed Line-Of-Sight path and having significantly shorter channel coherence times, which require a higher control signaling overhead due to more frequent channel measurements
[
2

. Especially, the latter poses a bottleneck for the Channel State Information at the Transmitter, which arrives with a delay
[
4

. Channel State Information at the Transmitter is essential to estimate the appropriate transmission parameters, such as the Modulation and Coding Scheme, precoding, etc. Especially, for highly mobile User Equipments, such as cars or trains, the Channel State Information at the Transmitter is already outdated when it is available at the transmitter. Although, exploiting geometrical properties of the environment and employing Machine Learning enables predicting the Channel State Information at the Transmitter over larger time windows
[
4
]
, the fast fading behavior of the channel may still make the channel estimation inaccurate.

To cope with inaccurate Channel State Information at the Transmitter, physical layer retransmission mechanisms, such as Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest, are used. However, Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest, also known as reactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest, increases the end-to-end latency because the transmitter requires feedback from the receiver after each transmission round in form of an ACKnowledgment or Non-ACKnowledgment. Especially, for Fifth Generation Ultra-Reliable Low Latency Communication use cases with end-to-end latency requirements of down to 1 ms
[
5
]
, reactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest poses a limitation. For Sixth Generation use cases, where end-to-end latency requirements even down to 100 µs are foreseen
[
6
]
, this becomes even more an issue. This drawback is compensated by proactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest that continuously transmits further retransmissions until an ACKnowledgment is received
[
7
,
8
]
. Proactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest combines high reliability with extremely short latencies
[
9
]
. Nevertheless, it trades these advantages for a degraded spectral efficiency due to unnecessary retransmissions
[
10
]
.

The dependence on the Line-Of-Sight path also poses a major issue for reliable communication, as any obstruction by an object causes a severe degradation of the channel quality. As a remedy, Cloud Radio Access Network architectures with multiple reception points at different locations, i.e. Remote Radio Heads, are foreseen for sub-THz and THz communications
[
11
]
. The BaseBand Unit, which is responsible for higher layer processing, decodes the packet by combining all received signals from the different Remote Radio Heads. However, in the context of Cloud Radio Access Network architectures, the aforementioned drawbacks of reactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest and proactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest become even more critical due to the significantly larger feedback delay
[
12

. In particular, a fronthaul latency of up to 250 µs is assumed
[

However, in Cloud Radio Access Network architectures, the Remote Radio Heads only have partial knowledge and further, the feedback channels to the User Equipment are rate-limited. Hence, in Cloud Radio Access Network architectures, Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes that consider the locality of the information are required. Due to the rate-limitation of the feedback channels, schemes also have to develop efficient representations of the local feedback and rules on how to combine these. Furthermore, even for the Signal-to-Noise Ratio-based approach, no evaluation using realistic link-level simulations in the Cloud Radio Access Network context, in particular considering blockage, is available. Against this background, the contributions of this paper are summarized in the following:

•

To address the Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction problem in Cloud Radio Access Networks in a holistic manner, we present a novel Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model that exploits subcode features as well as channel estimation features. In particular, we also consider the single-blockage case, where one Remote Radio Head is blocked. We show that the Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model clearly outperforms all other schemes in all experiments.

I-A

Related work on HARQ feedback prediction

As mentioned in the previous section, different Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes have been proposed and studied in the literature. The variety of schemes reaches from simple thresholding, e.g.
[
12
]
, up to complex machine learning schemes, e.g.
[
22
]
and
[
25
]
. In particular, the latter has gained interest recently, also in the context of the new Rel. 18 standardization
[
26
]
. In the following, we classify these schemes into three categories:

1.

Channel-estimation-based feedback prediction:
In
[
15
]
, Makki et al. investigated a mixture of proactive and reactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest protocols to reduce the expected latency. In the proposed scheme, the receiver accumulates the received signal until the sum channel gain that is estimated over quantization regions, exceeds a certain threshold associated with a sufficiently high decoding probability. In case of a negative prediction, i.e. the transmitted redundancy is not sufficient for successful decoding, the receiver switches to a reactive Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest approach. In
[
12
]
, Rost and Prasad and, in
[
14
]
, Khalili and Simeone put the channel-estimation-based prediction schemes into the context of Cloud Radio Access Networks and showed the benefits of early feedback in Cloud Radio Access Networks with non-ideal backhaul.

Besides that, in
[
19
]
, AlMarshed et al. proposed a Deep Machine Learning scheme that uses the received complex signal to estimate the decodability of a packet. Although being different from the previously described schemes, we categorize the Deep ML as a channel-estimation-based approach because it does not involve the computation of Log-Likelihood Ratios or any other channel-code-aware features.

2.

Log-Likelihood Ratio-based feedback prediction:
In
[
20
]
and
[
21
]
, Berardinelli et al. used a Bit Error Rate estimate based on Log-Likelihood Ratios to predict the decoding outcome ahead of the actual decoding. They empirically computed a threshold for the Bit Error Rate estimate to predict the decodability. In contrast to the channel-estimation-based schemes, the Log-Likelihood Ratios inherently contain a reduced form of the channel estimates. Nevertheless, different from the first category, the Log-Likelihood Ratio-based schemes consider the whole received data signal for the prediction instead of relying only on pilots used for the channel estimation. As an improvement over the simple thresholding that was used by Berardinelli et al., Hummert et al. proposed in
[
22
]
to use a neural network, designated as NN ForeCast, that can mimic the decoder. A hybrid pathway was proposed by AlMarshed et al. in
[
23
]
. They combined both Log-Likelihood Ratio- and channel-estimation-based features using a logistic regression. The proposed logistic regression showed a significant enhancement over other approaches that use only one of both.

3.

Subcode-based feedback prediction:
In
[
10
,
24
]
and
[
25
]
, the authors proposed a feedback prediction mechanism that observes the partial decoding behavior of so-called subcodes. The subcodes reflect dependencies between received symbols arising from the structure of the channel code. Similar to the Log-Likelihood Ratio-based feedback prediction, this approach uses the Log-Likelihood Ratios as a basis. However, in contrast to these, subcode-based schemes apply additional processing on the Log-Likelihood Ratios based on the knowledge of the code structure. In
[
24
]
, Göktepe et al. empirically determined thresholds for these code-aware features. As an improvement over this thresholding approach, in
[
25
]
, the authors applied machine learning techniques, i.e. logistic regression, random forests, isolation forests and supervised autoencoders, on the Log-Likelihood Ratio and subcode features. Especially, the logistic regression and the supervised autoencoder have proven to be fruitful approaches to enhance the feedback prediction.

Apart from the previously described Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes that are mainly designed for single reception points,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
presented similar approaches for implementing the channel-estimation-based feedback prediction in Cloud Radio Access Networks. The works proposed collecting the channel gains and the Signal-to-Noise Ratios over the received Redundancy Versions, respectively. In
[
12
]
, Rost and Prasad applied Gallager’s error exponent

to convert the channel estimation into an estimated error probability

[

]
for more details):

\displaystyle\epsilon(R,\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}e^{-NE_{r}(R,\gamma)}\,,

(1)

\displaystyle E_{r}(R,\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{0}(\gamma)-R\,,

After calculating Gallager’s error exponent locally at the Remote Radio Heads, they proposed applying a threshold to generate a positive or negative feedback. However, they ignored the case of multiple Remote Radio Heads receiving the same packet. This was investigated by Khalili and Simeone in
[

III
Distributed early HARQ strategies

In this paper, we consider early Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest strategies that attempt to predict the decodability of a packet ahead of the actual decoding. In particular, we take only a part of the whole transmitted signal vector into account. In contrast to early Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest strategies that use the whole signal vector, this approach allows for providing the feedback at an earlier stage, which is crucial especially for latency-constrained use cases. In the broadest sense, the decodability prediction can be interpreted as binary statistical hypothesis testing, where the early Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest predictor tries to discriminate between two probability distributions
P
and
Q
: the probability distribution of decodables and the probability distribution of undecodables. The feedback maps
T^{(1)}
and
T^{(2)}
have to be chosen such that the two distributions become as distinguishable as possible. Ideally, these maps are sufficient statistics to the statistical hypothesis testing problem. However, in practice, depending on the type of feedback, it is a notoriously difficult problem to exactly characterize these distributions and hence, also finding sufficient feedback maps.

In terms of the system model, the prediction is based on
p
modulation symbols with
p<n
.
Hence, given
\mathrm{proj}_{p}{Y}^{(1)}
and
\mathrm{proj}_{p}{Y}^{(2)}

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{(T^{(1)}(\mathrm{proj}_{p}Y^{(1)}),T^{(2)}(\mathrm{proj}_{p}Y^{(2)}))|D=\textrm{ACK}}

(4)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{(T^{(1)}(\mathrm{proj}_{p}Y^{(1)}),T^{(2)}(\mathrm{proj}_{p}Y^{(2)}))|D=\textrm{NACK}}\,,

(5)

where
D\in\{\mathrm{ACK},\mathrm{NACK}\}
respresents the decoding outcome at the BaseBand Unit with

III-C

Subcode-based HARQ prediction

As Log-Likelihood Ratio-based schemes, subcode-based Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest predictors take the Log-Likelihood Ratios as a basis. However, instead of assuming i.i.d. components of the signal vector

, the subcode-based prediction considers constraints defined by the parity-check matrix
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{L\times nM}

\boldsymbol{b}:=(b_{i,j})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{nM}

\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{b}^{\mathrm{T}}=\boldsymbol{0}

Message passing decoders, such as the min-sum implementation, iteratively update the Log-Likelihood Ratios based on
\boldsymbol{P}

-th iteration with
\Lambda_{l,0}^{(i)}:=\Lambda_{l}^{(i)}
. In contrast to tree codes where message passing decoders always converge to the best solution, for modern Low-Density Parity-Check codes, the evolution of the Log-Likelihood Ratios can be interpreted as a sequence which may or may not converge to a ”degraded” marginalization
[
35
]
. Compared to considering only the received Log-Likelihood Ratios, this behavior provides additional information on the healthiness of the received codeword.

In the machine learning literature, autoencoders are well established for unsupervised anomaly detection tasks

III-E

Data transmission model

Fig. 5:
Data transmission model incorporating the feedback delay.

We assume an incremental redundancy Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest protocol with up to four Redundancy Versions. In our simulation setup, an Redundancy Version spans over 14 Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing symbols in time, which is equivalent to 15.63 µs.
The User Equipment transmits the Redundancy Versions in a consecutive manner, as depicted in Fig.
5
. After receiving each Redundancy Version, the Remote Radio Heads provide feedback using the previously described prediction schemes. The User Equipment decides based on a combination rule
F
whether further Redundancy Versions are required or not. This approach enables a good trade-off between reliability and spectral efficiency since some Redundancy Versions are omitted, if an early decoding is successfully predicted. After having received all Redundancy Versions from the User Equipment, the Remote Radio Heads forward the received signals to the BaseBand Unit where a single decoding attempt is conducted.
In this work, we simulate four Redundancy Versions; hence, there are three prediction points:

1.

the
first prediction point (Pos#1)
, which uses the received Redundancy Version#0 to decide whether Redundancy Version#2 is required or not,

2.

the
second prediction point (Pos#2)
, which uses Redundancy Version#0 and Redundancy Version#1 to decide whether Redundancy Version#3 is required or not.

3.

the
blockage prediction point (Pos#3)
, which uses Redundancy Version#0-2 to decide whether blockage is detected or not.

A positive prediction at the first prediction point causes the User Equipment to stop transmitting further Redundancy Versions. Hence, the second prediction point would not be reached in that case. However, this depends on the particular prediction scheme and modeling this accurately would require to incorporate the prediction directly into the link-level simulations, which increases the complexity extremely. Instead, we assume that all prediction schemes correctly identify transmissions that are already decodable with the first Redundancy Version and only the remaining transmissions reach the second prediction point.

III-F

Evaluation methodology

In addition to achieving the reliability and the latency targets which are mandatory requirements, the performance of the Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes can be compared in terms of their achieved throughput. In contrast to commonly used classifier performance metrics, such as precision and recall, the throughput provides a measure with direct takeover to practical scenarios. Especially, when considering edge cases with extremely low-reliability requirements, common classifier metrics may not provide a good metric for comparison
[
25
]
.

Based on the renewal-reward theorem
[
44
]
, the throughput is expressed as
\eta=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}]}{\mathbb{E}[L(T)]}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}]
is an expected reward and,
\mathbb{E}[L(T)]
is the expected transmission latency with
L(T)
as defined in (
18
) and (
19
) and
T\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
being the number of requested Redundancy Versions. Furthermore, the reward is
\mathcal{R}:=0
in case the transmission failed within the latency budget. In case the transmission was successful,
\mathcal{R}
is
N_{\mathrm{packet}}
, the size of the packet in nats. Hence, the expected reward is given as
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}]:=(1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}})N_{\mathrm{packet}}
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
is the associated total error probability. The transmission latency of the prediction schemes is composed of multiple components:

L_{\mathrm{e}}(T)=\begin{cases}\!\begin{aligned} &T\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}+(T-1)\delta_{\mathrm{exc}}\end{aligned}&\text{if }1\leq T\leq T_{\mathrm{max}}\\
\!\begin{aligned} &T_{\mathrm{max}}\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}+L_{\mathrm{blk}}\\
&+(T_{\mathrm{max}}-1)\delta_{\mathrm{exc}}\end{aligned}&\text{if }T>T_{\mathrm{max}}\end{cases}\,,

(18)

where
\delta_{\mathrm{exc}}:=\max(\delta_{\mathrm{proc}}+\delta_{\mathrm{fb}}-\delta_{\mathrm{RV}},0)
and
\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}
is the time to transmit an Redundancy Version,
\delta_{\mathrm{proc}}
is the processing time required by the specific prediction scheme and
\delta_{\mathrm{fb}}
is the time required to transmit the feedback. Furthermore,
L_{\mathrm{blk}}
is a latency penalty. For simplicity, we assume
L_{\mathrm{blk}}=T_{\mathrm{blk}}\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}
, where
T_{\mathrm{blk}}
is a spectral efficiency penalty when blockage is detected. In contrast to that, the latency of a regular Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest system is composed as:

L_{\mathrm{r}}(T)=\begin{cases}T\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}+(T-1)(\delta_{\mathrm{fh}}+\delta_{\mathrm{fb}})&\text{if }1\leq T\leq T_{\mathrm{max}}\\
\!\begin{aligned} &T_{\mathrm{max}}\delta_{\mathrm{RV}}+L_{\mathrm{blk}}\\
&+(T_{\mathrm{max}}-1)(\delta_{\mathrm{fh}}+\delta_{\mathrm{fb}})\end{aligned}&\text{if }T>T_{\mathrm{max}}\end{cases}\,,

(19)

where
\delta_{\mathrm{fh}}
is the fronthaul round-trip time, which is the time required for transporting the received signal vectors to the BaseBand Unit, decoding the packet at the BaseBand Unit, and sending the result back to the Remote Radio Heads.

The probability distribution of
T
for a feedback delay of
\delta:=1
is determined by the performance of the different estimators:

\mathbb{P}[T=t]=\begin{cases}\!\begin{aligned} &p_{t-2}\epsilon_{t-1}\alpha_{t-1}\\
&+p_{t-2}(1-\epsilon_{t-1})\\
&\cdot(1-\beta_{t-1})\end{aligned}&\text{if }2\leq t\leq T_{\mathrm{max}}\\
p_{T_{\mathrm{max}}-1}&\text{if }t=T_{\mathrm{max}}+T_{\mathrm{blk}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(20)

with

p_{t}:=\prod_{j=1}^{t}\epsilon_{j}(1-\alpha_{j})+(1-\epsilon_{j})\beta_{j}\,,

(21)

where
T_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum number of transmissions,
T_{\mathrm{blk}}
is an additional spectral efficiency penalty in case blockage is detected,
\epsilon_{j}
,
j\in\{1,2,...,T_{\mathrm{max}}-1\}
, are the error probabilities at the
(j+1)
-th Redundancy Version given that previous Redundancy Versions were unsuccessful,
\alpha_{j}
,
j\in\{1,2,...,T_{\mathrm{max}}-1\}
, are the false positive probabilities, i.e. predicting an unsuccessful decoding as an ACK, and
\beta_{j}
,
j\in\{1,2,...,T_{\mathrm{max}}-1\}
, are the false negative probabilities, i.e. predicting a successful decoding as a NACK. The false-positive and false-negative probabilities are defined as

\alpha_{j}:=P_{D_{j+1}=\mathrm{NACK}|F_{j}=\mathrm{ACK}}\,,

(22)

and

\beta_{j}:=P_{D_{j+1}=\mathrm{ACK}|F_{j}=\mathrm{NACK}}\,,

(23)

where
D_{j+1}
is the decoding outcome with
(j+1)
Redundancy Versions and
F_{j}
is the outcome of the
j
-th prediction.

The total error performance
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
is determined by the error probabilities
\epsilon_{i}
but also the false positive error probability
\alpha_{i}
. The total error performance in a non-blockage scenario is given by
[
10
]
:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}|\mathrm{nb}}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{max}}-2}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}\epsilon_{j}(1-\alpha_{j})\right)\epsilon_{t}\alpha_{t}\right)\\
+\left(\prod_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{max}}-2}\epsilon_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})\right)\epsilon_{T_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\,,

(24)

where the false-positive, false-negative and error probabilities are estimated from non-blockage scenarios.
However, in a blockage scenario a sufficiently low error probability cannot be maintained and hence, alternative procedures, e.g. additional redundancy, switching to a lower frequency or adapting the beam, have to be initiated. However, instead of making an assumption on the specific blockage recovery scheme, we use the spectral efficiency penalty
T_{\mathrm{blk}}
to model the blockage case. This also means that an effective blockage, i.e. non-decodability of the
T_{\mathrm{max}}
Redundancy Versions, has to be detected with the same target error probability. The probability of blockage misdetection is given by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{blk}|\mathrm{sb}}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{max}}-3}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}\epsilon_{j}(1-\alpha_{j})\right)\epsilon_{t}\alpha_{t}\right)\\
+\left(\prod_{j=1}^{T_{\mathrm{max}}-3}\epsilon_{j}(1-\alpha_{j})\right)\epsilon_{T_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\alpha_{T_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\,,

(25)

where the false-positive, false-negative and error probabilities are derived from single-blockage scenarios.

The false-positive probabilities and the false-negative probabilities behave in a conflicting manner, where the trade-off between both can be controlled by adjusting the bias
s
of the respective predictor. However, the functional relation between
\alpha_{i}(s)
and
\beta_{i}(s)
,
i=1,...,T_{\mathrm{max}}-1
, is not known. Hence, we determine 1000 admissible pairs
(\alpha_{i}(s_{j}),\beta_{i}(s_{j}))
,
j=1,2,...,1000
, from the link-level simulations and interpolate them piece-wise linearly. Obviously, any false-positive false-negative curve of a reasonable predictor is a convex function, where at the extreme case when no prediction is possible, this function becomes a straight line connecting the points
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
. Hence, a piece-wise linear interpolation is a conservative approximation, where the actual performance of the predictors at an interpolated point is expected to be better than the approximated value.

With
\boldsymbol{s}:=(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{T_{\mathrm{max}}-1})
being the vector of biases per prediction, we derive the following optimization problem for the expected number of Redundancy Versions under no blockage:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{s}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\boldsymbol{s}),\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\boldsymbol{s}))]

(26)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot|nb}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\boldsymbol{s}))\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}

\displaystyle\text{and}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{blk|sb}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\boldsymbol{s}))\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}\,,

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}
corresponds to the overall reliability requirement. We use the Sequential Least Squares Programming algorithm
[
45
]
with a Monte-Carlo approach to numerically find a valid solution to the aforementioned optimization problem.

IV
Simulation Results

In this section, we present the results for the different Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction approaches. We train all schemes jointly on all Signal-to-Noise Ratios except 6 dB. The Signal-to-Noise Ratio of 6 dB is not used during training but only used for testing to evaluate the generalization performance of the models.

IV-A

False-positive and false-negative performance

The false-positive and false-negative mispredictions determine the performance of the predictor, as we can see from Eq. (
20
) and (
24
). Especially, the regime of low false-positives is of special interest because the cost of a false-positive misprediction is significantly higher than the cost of a false-negative misprediction. However, due to the finite size of the test sets, we have to deal with false-positives of zero, which makes a logarithmic scale unusable. Hence, a symmetrical logarithmic scale has been chosen for the false-positive axis. This scaling puts emphasis on the lower regime of the false-positive probabilities while keeping the zero point interpretable; however, special care has to be given to the linear scaling between the zero and the first step.

(b)

V
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we proposed novel machine-learning assisted Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes and evaluated them within the context of a Cloud Radio Access Network scenario in the sub-THz regime consider also blockage using link-level simulations. In particular, we extended the Log-Likelihood Ratio- and subcode-based approaches proposed in
[
25
]
enabling their usage in a Cloud Radio Access Network setup by introducing quantization and a feedback combination module using a logistic regression and we proposed a novel end-to-end Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model architecture that exploits Signal-to-Noise Ratio and subcode features. Using realistic link-level simulations, we showed that the proposed Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model clearly outperforms other prediction mechanisms in no-blockage as well as in single-blockage scenarios within the context of the Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest evaluation methodology. In particular, we present that the Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction achieves a more than 200 % higher throughput compared to other Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest prediction schemes at Signal-to-Noise Ratios ranging from 4 dB to 7 dB and target error rates from
1\cdot 10^{-4}
to
3.5\cdot 10^{-6}
, if single-blockage is considered. Even without blockage, we show that the throughput of the Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model is approx. 29 % higher compared to the Logistic Regression on LogLikelihood Ratios and even 45 % higher compared to the Quantized Signal-to-Noise-Ratio. Compared to regular Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest, our proposed Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model with a sufficient feedback size suffers only by a throughput reduction of approx. 20 % while reducing the maximum transmission latency by a factor larger than 4. Furthermore, we show that 4 bits for the feedback transmission is sufficient and the schemes do not benefit from more bits. In future research, the impact of double-blockage as well as a setup with more than two Remote Radio Heads may be studied. Furthermore, the impact of non-ideal channel estimation on the performance of the different schemes has to be evaluated in further studies.

Dual-Input Denoising Autoencoder

The network configuration for the encoders at each Remote Radio Head is [FCL(
d
,25), FCL(25,10), FCL(10,3)] and for the decoder [FCL(3,15), FCL(15,40), Lin(40,
d
)], where FCL(x,y)
\equiv
[Lin(x,y), BN, L-ReLU] and
d
is the input dimension. Furthermore, Lin(x,y) denotes a linear transformation layer, BN a Batch Normalization-layer, L-ReLU a Leaky ReLU activation layer with a slope of
0.01
.
Each Remote Radio Head further contains a classifier that operates on the compressed form of the subcode features. The network configurations of the Remote Radio Head classifiers each read as [FC(5,10), FC(10,15), FC(15, 15), FC(15, 10), Lin(10, 2), SM], where FC(x,y)
\equiv
[Lin(x,y), BN, ReLU] where ReLU is a ReLU activation layer and SM is a softmax activation layer. The classifiers each receive the compressed representation of
\overline{\boldsymbol{s}}^{(i)}
and the received Signal-to-Noise Ratio
\gamma^{(i)}
as input. To prevent drifting off of the two ”arms” of the network, the local encoders and classifiers are tied together. In particular this means, the weights and the biases of the linear layers are updated equally at both Remote Radio Heads. Furthermore, we implement the quantization layer by a FakeQuantize layer with a MinMaxObserver in PyTorch using quantization-aware training
[
49
]
. Lastly, the network configuration of the User Equipment classifier is given by [FC(2,20), FC(20,10), FC(10,5), Lin(5,2), SM]. We train the Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model in an end-to-end fashion using a loss function
L
that is composed by the
L_{2}
norm:

L_{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}||\boldsymbol{s}^{(i)}_{k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{(i)}_{k}||_{2}^{2}\,,

(27)

and the cross-entropy between the predicted output
\hat{d}
and the actual decoding outcome
d
:

L_{\mathrm{ce}}=\frac{1}{1+\omega_{\mathrm{ACK}}}\left(d_{k}\log(\hat{d}_{k})+\omega_{\mathrm{ACK}}(1-d_{k})\log(1-\hat{d}_{k})\right)\,,

(28)

where
N
is the number of samples in a batch and
\omega_{\mathrm{ACK}}
is a weight class for ACKnowledgments. The two loss functions are combined as:

L=L_{2}+\lambda L_{\mathrm{ce}}\,,

(29)

where
\lambda
is a fixed weight factor. The fixed weight factor and the batch size are found to give the best results at
15.0
and
15,000
, respectively, for all prediction points. For the first and the blockage prediction point, the ACKnowledgment weight class
\omega_{\mathrm{ACK}}
achieves at
0.5
the best performance. For the second prediction point, the ACKnowledgment weight class is chosen to be
0.1
. To train Dual Autoencoding 2-Stage Gaussian Mixture Model under the given loss function, we use the Adam optimizer
[
50
]
at a learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay of
10^{-5}
. We initialize the parameters of the whole network with the Kaiming normal initialization
[
51
]
. Due to the nature of the sample data, the ratio between ACKs and NACKs is heavily imbalanced. Hence, we undersample the majority class, i.e. ACKs, to create a balance between ACKs and NACKs.
