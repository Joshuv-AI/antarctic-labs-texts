Spherical and Plane Symmetric Solutions in Macroscopic Gravity
Anish Agashe, Mustapha Ishak

Abstract
The theory of macroscopic gravity provides a formalism to average the Einstein field equations from small scales to largest scales in space-time. It is well known that averaging is an operation that does not commute with calculating the Einstein tensor and this leads to a correction term in the field equations known as back-reaction. In this work, we derive exact solutions to the macroscopic gravity field equations assuming that the averaged geometry is plane or spherically symmetric, and the source is taken as vacuum, dust, or perfect fluid. We then focus on the specific cases of spherical symmetry and derive solutions that are analogous to the Schwarzschild, Tolman VII, and Lemaître-Tolman-Bondi solutions. The geodesic equations and curvature structure are contrasted with the general relativistic counterparts for the Schwarzschild and Lemaître-Tolman-Bondi solutions.



Keywords
: Averaging problem, macroscopic gravity, back-reaction, inhomogeneous cosmology

An outstanding problem in general relativity (GR) is how the small scale lumpy universe is averaged to be homogeneous at the largest scales. Let there be a geometric object (vector, tensor etc.)
p^{\alpha}_{\beta}(x)
defined on an
n

(\mathcal{M},\textnormal{{g}}_{\alpha\beta})
. Then, the space-time averaged value of this object over a compact region
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{M}
with a volume
n

, is defined as,

\left\langle p^{\alpha}_{\beta}(x)\right\rangle=\dfrac{\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}_{\mu^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})p^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{A}^{\nu^{\prime}}_{\beta}(x^{\prime},x)\sqrt{-\textnormal{{g}}^{\prime}}\ \textrm{d}^{n}x^{\prime}}{\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{-\textnormal{{g}}^{\prime}}\ \textrm{d}^{n}x^{\prime}}

(2.1)

where
\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{-\textnormal{{g}}^{\prime}}\ \textrm{d}^{n}x^{\prime}
is the volume (
V_{\Sigma}
) of the region
\Sigma
and
\textnormal{{g}}^{\prime}=\det\left[\textnormal{{g}}_{\alpha\beta}(x^{\prime})\right]
. The integration is done over all the points
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma
. The integrand
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}_{\mu^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})p^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{A}^{\nu^{\prime}}_{\beta}(x^{\prime},x)
is called the
bilocal extension
of the object
p^{\alpha}_{\beta}(x)
; and the objects
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}_{\mu^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{A}^{\nu^{\prime}}_{\beta}(x^{\prime},x)
are called the
bilocal averaging operators
.

Then, bilocal objects
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
are defined in terms of these averaging operators as,

{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho}=\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\left(\partial_{\rho}\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}_{\beta}+\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}_{\beta}\mathcal{A}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\rho}\right)

(2.2)

These objects behave as connection coefficients at
x
and hence, can be considered as the bilocal extension of the microscopic connection coefficients. The averages of these objects,
\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho}\right\rangle
serve as the affine connection coefficients of the averaged space-time.

Further, the average of the microscopic Riemann curvature tensor,
\left\langle{r^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho\sigma}\right\rangle
is written as
{R^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho\sigma}
. A curvature tensor corresponding to the macroscopic connection can be calculated, and turns out to be related to the average of the microscopic Riemann curvature tensor through the following formula,

{M^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho\sigma}={R^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho\sigma}+2\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\delta}}_{\beta[\rho}{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\delta}\sigma]}\right\rangle-2\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\delta}}_{\beta[\rho}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\delta}\sigma]}\right\rangle

(2.3)

Then, in order to define splitting rules for averaging the differential Bianchi identities, correlation tensors need to be defined. The connection correlation is a six rank tensor which takes into account the non-commutativity of averaging and calculating the Einstein tensor. It is defined as,

{Z^{\alpha}}_{\beta[\gamma}{{}^{\mu}}_{\underline{\nu}\sigma]}=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\beta[\gamma}{\mathcal{F}^{\mu}}_{\underline{\nu}\sigma]}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\beta[\gamma}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\mu}}_{\underline{\nu}\sigma]}\right\rangle

(2.4)

Note that the second term on the right hand side in equation (
2.3
) is merely a contraction of the connection correlation and can be written as,
{Q^{\alpha}}_{\beta\rho\sigma}~=~2\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\delta}}_{\beta[\rho}{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\delta}\sigma]}\right\rangle-2\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\delta}}_{\beta[\rho}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\delta}\sigma]}\right\rangle=2{Z^{\epsilon}}_{\beta[\rho}{{}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\epsilon}\sigma]}
. This tensor is like a curvature tensor in its mathematical construction and follows the algebraic properties of one. It can be considered as a ‘curvature deformation’ tensor since it measures the difference between the macroscopic curvature tensor and the average of the microscopic curvature tensor. Higher order correlations also exist (restricted up to the dimensionality of the macroscopic space-time), but can be taken to be zero in a self consistent manner
[
13
,
14
]
. This makes the formalism more practical with equations involving only the connection correlation.

The final form of the averaged Einstein field equations is given by,

{E^{\epsilon}}_{\gamma}=G^{\beta\epsilon}M_{\beta\gamma}-{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{\epsilon}_{\gamma}G^{\mu\nu}M_{\mu\nu}=\kappa{T^{\epsilon}}_{\gamma}+\left({Z^{\epsilon}}_{\mu\nu\gamma}-{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{\epsilon}_{\gamma}Q_{\mu\nu}\right)G^{\mu\nu}

(2.5)

where,
{T^{\epsilon}}_{\gamma}=\left\langle{t^{\epsilon}}_{\gamma}\right\rangle
is the average of the energy-momentum tensor for the microscopic matter distribution,
G^{\mu\nu}
is the inverse of the macroscopic metric tensor,
M_{\beta\gamma}
is the macroscopic Ricci tensor,
{Z^{\epsilon}}_{\mu\nu\gamma}~=~2{Z^{\epsilon}}_{\mu[\alpha}{{}^{\alpha}}_{\underline{\nu}\gamma]}
is a Ricci-tensor-like object for the connection correlation and
Q_{\mu\nu}~=~{Q^{\epsilon}}_{\mu\epsilon\nu}
.

Therefore, in the theory of macroscopic gravity, averaging out the Einstein field equations introduces additional terms on right hand side of these equations, constituting various traces (contractions) of the connection correlation. We call these additional terms as the MG correction term, denoted as,

{C^{\epsilon}}_{\gamma}=\left({Z^{\epsilon}}_{\mu\nu\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\epsilon}_{\gamma}Q_{\mu\nu}\right)G^{\mu\nu}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=-{Z^{\alpha}}_{\beta\sigma}{{}^{\mu}}_{\nu\gamma}

\displaystyle=-{Z^{\mu}}_{\nu\gamma}{{}^{\alpha}}_{\beta\sigma}

(2.7b)

\displaystyle{Z^{\alpha}}_{\beta[\gamma}{{}^{\mu}}_{\nu\sigma]}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{Z^{\epsilon}}_{\epsilon\gamma}{{}^{\mu}}_{\nu\sigma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{{{Z^{\alpha}}_{\beta\mu}}^{\gamma}}_{\delta\nu}u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0

f=\left[(k+\mathcal{B})-\frac{2m}{x^{1}}\right]
. Therefore, the vacuum solution is now modified to have the back-reaction term in the line element. This metric has a coordinate singularity at
x^{1}=\frac{2m}{(k+\mathcal{B})}

4.1
The MG-Schwarzschild Exterior Solution

The static spherically symmetric case (
k=1
) gives us the MG-Schwarzschild (exterior) solution. The line element, in coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\phi)
, then, looks like,

\textrm{d}s^{2}=-\left[(1+\mathcal{B})-\frac{2m}{r}\right]\textrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{1}{\left[(1+\mathcal{B})-\frac{2m}{r}\right]}\textrm{d}r^{2}\\
+r^{2}\textrm{d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\textrm{d}\phi^{2}

(4.4)

It reduces to the usual Schwarzschild exterior solution of GR when we take the back-reaction term
\mathcal{B}
to be zero. The line element in equation (
4.4
) clearly represents a geometry that is different from that of the Schwarzschild solution. We know that in the GR-Schwarzschild geometry, the Ricci tensor and hence the Ricci scalar is zero. But this is not the case in the MG-Schwarzschild geometry where,
{R^{\mu}}_{\nu}~=~{\rm diag}[0,0,-\frac{\mathcal{B}}{r^{2}},-\frac{\mathcal{B}}{r^{2}}]
and
R~=~-\frac{2\mathcal{B}}{r^{2}}
. The Kretschmann scalar is also modified, and is given by,
K~=~\frac{48m^{2}}{r^{6}}~+~\frac{4\mathcal{B}r(\mathcal{B}r-4m)}{r^{6}}
. This is exactly the same result as in
[
24
]
.

As explained above, since the geometry is not Schwarzschild anymore, the geodesic equations will be modified as well. Using the line element in equation (
4.4
), the Lagrangian density will become,

2\mathcal{L}=-f(r,\mathcal{B})\left(\frac{\textrm{d}t}{\textrm{d}\lambda}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{f(r,\mathcal{B})}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\lambda}\right)^{2}+r^{2}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}\phi}{\textrm{d}\lambda}\right)^{2}

(4.5)

where,
\lambda
is an affine parameter and
f=\left(1+\mathcal{B}-\frac{2m}{r}\right)
. Using the Euler-Lagrange equations, we get,
\frac{\textrm{d}t}{\textrm{d}\lambda}~=~\frac{\gamma}{f(r,\mathcal{B})};\ \frac{\textrm{d}\phi}{\textrm{d}\lambda}~=~\frac{l}{r^{2}}
, where,
\gamma
and
l
are constants. We know that for geodesics
2\mathcal{L}=\varepsilon
, where,
\varepsilon=-1,0,+1
correspond to time-like, null and space-like geodesics, respectively. Using this, we can obtain the following orbital equation for the respective type of particles,

\left(\frac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}\phi}\right)^{2}=2mu^{3}-(1+\mathcal{B})u^{2}-\frac{2m\varepsilon}{l^{2}}u+\frac{\gamma^{2}+(1+\mathcal{B})\varepsilon}{l^{2}}

(4.6)

which gives,

\frac{\textrm{d}^{2}u}{\textrm{d}\phi^{2}}=3mu^{2}-(1+\mathcal{B})u-\frac{m\varepsilon}{l^{2}}

(4.7)

where,
u\equiv\frac{1}{r}

\phi
and mass

as
\overline{\phi}=\sqrt{1+\mathcal{B}}\phi
and
\overline{m}=\frac{m}{1+\mathcal{B}}
, respectively. The solution to this equation is, then, given by,

u=\frac{-m\varepsilon}{(1+\mathcal{B})l^{2}}\left[1+\alpha\cos\left\{\phi\left(\sqrt{1+\mathcal{B}}+\frac{3m^{2}\varepsilon}{(1+\mathcal{B})^{\frac{3}{2}}l^{2}}\right)\right\}\right]

(4.8)

where,
\alpha
is a small parameter (
|\alpha|<<1
) that quantifies the deviation from the Newtonian solution.

It is a fruitful exercise to write the metric in equation (
4.4

\overline{x}~=~\overline{r}\sin\theta\cos\phi

\overline{y}~=~\overline{r}\sin\theta\cos\phi

\overline{z}~=~\overline{r}\cos\theta

(4.10)

where,

5.2.1
The MG-LTB Solution

Our analysis until this point is done for both plane and spherical symmetry in a combined way. Although there has been some interest in the plane symmetric cosmological models in the past, none of these models has stood the tests of precision cosmology as well as the spatially homogeneous and isotropic FLRW model. The only exciting alternative to the FLRW model is the Lemaître-Tolman-Bondi (LTB)
[

It is only radially inhomogeneous (due to spherical symmetry) with the spatial hypersurfaces spherically symmetric about a central worldline. In this section, we will look at the LTB model in the framework of macroscopic gravity – the MG-LTB model. To do this, we choose spherical coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\phi)
and

in equations (

The dynamical equations governing this model would be given by equations (

Further, we require that
\textrm{e}^{P(r_{b})}\left|{}_{\rm int}\right.~=~\textrm{e}^{P(r_{b})}\left|{}_{\rm ext}\right.

Then, equation (

\textrm{e}^{P}={\frac{1}{2}}\left[3\left\{(1+\mathcal{B})-\frac{8\pi}{3}\overline{\rho}r_{b}^{2}\right\}^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\{(1+\mathcal{B})-\frac{8\pi}{3}\overline{\rho}r^{2}\right\}^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]

+\frac{1}{\left[(1+\mathcal{B})-\frac{8\pi}{3}\overline{\rho}r^{2}\right]}\textrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\textrm{d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\textrm{d}\phi^{2}

The obvious solution to look at, after the Schwarzschild solution, is the Tolman VII solution

Writing the differential terms to be with respect to the variable,

6.2.1
Solutions with
\Theta=0
;
{\sigma^{\alpha}}_{\beta}=0

The vanishing shear and expansion, through equations (

6.2.2
Solutions with
\Theta\neq 0
;
{\sigma^{\alpha}}_{\beta}=0

The most interesting and well studied case is that of expanding, shear free solutions. We will look at this case in some detail since it has the FLRW solution as a subcase. Vanishing shear, through equation (
6.33c
), implies
\dot{Q}=\frac{\dot{R}}{R}
. This can be integrated to give,

R=X(x^{1})\textrm{e}^{Q}

(6.34)

where,
X
is an arbitrary function arising from integration. Using this in equation (
3.8b
) and then integrating, we get,

\textrm{e}^{P}=\dot{Q}\textrm{e}^{-Y(x^{0})}

(6.35)

where,
Y
is another arbitrary function coming from integration. Then, using this and equation (
6.34
), the expansion scalar becomes,
\Theta=3\textrm{e}^{Y}
.

Using equations (
6.34
) and (
6.35
) in equations (
3.8a
) and (
3.8d
), the energy density and pressure can be written as,

3\textrm{e}^{2Y}-\frac{\textrm{e}^{-2Q}}{X^{2}}\left(2XX^{\prime\prime}+{X^{\prime}}^{2}+2X^{2}Q^{\prime\prime}\right.\\
\left.+4XX^{\prime}Q^{\prime}+X^{2}{Q^{\prime}}^{2}+k+\mathcal{B}\right)=8\pi\rho

(6.36)

and,

\frac{\textrm{e}^{-3Q}}{\dot{Q}X^{2}}\partial_{0}\left[\textrm{e}^{Q}\left\{\left(X^{\prime}+XQ^{\prime}\right)^{2}-(k+\mathcal{B})\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\textrm{e}^{2(Q+Y)}\right\}\right]=8\pi p

(6.37)

The isotropy condition,
{E^{1}}_{1}-{E^{3}}_{3}=\frac{\mathcal{B}}{R^{2}}
, gives,

\partial_{0}\left[\textrm{e}^{Q}\left\{{X^{\prime}}^{2}-X\left(X^{\prime\prime}+X^{\prime}Q^{\prime}\right)\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.+X^{2}\left({Q^{\prime}}^{2}-Q^{\prime\prime}\right)-(k+\mathcal{B})\right\}\right]=0

(6.38)

Further, the metric becomes,

\textrm{d}s^{2}=-\dot{Q}^{2}\textrm{e}^{-2Y}(\textrm{d}x^{0})^{2}+\textrm{e}^{2Q}(\textrm{d}x^{1})^{2}\\
+\textrm{e}^{2Q}X^{2}\left[(\textrm{d}x^{2})^{2}+S^{2}(x^{2},k)(\textrm{d}x^{2})^{2}\right]

(6.39)

Equation (
6.38
) can be integrated and then further solved to produce several classes of solutions
[
28
]
, especially in the case of spherical symmetry. The one that is of interest to us is the spherically symmetric solution
((k=1);\ x^{\mu}=(t,r,\theta,\phi))
with a homogeneous distribution of matter density and pressure
(\rho~\equiv~\rho(t);\ p~\equiv~p(t))
. The homogeneity of the pressure, through equation (
3.7b
), implies
P^{\prime}~=~0
. As explained in section
5
, this allows one to put
P=0
and write the metric coefficient
Q
as in equation (
5.9
). Further equation (
6.35
), then, implies
\dot{Q}^{\prime}~=~0
. Using this, we can write,

Q~=~Q_{1}(t)+Q_{2}(r)

(6.40)

Using this in equation (
6.34
), we get,

R^{\prime}=Z^{\prime}a(t)

(6.41)

where,
Z(r)=X\textrm{e}^{Q_{2}(r)}
and
a(t)=e^{Q_{1}(t)}
.

Then, using equations (
5.9
) and (
6.41
), the metric in equation (
6.39
) (for the spherically symmetric case) becomes,

\textrm{d}s^{2}=-\textrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{{Z^{\prime}}^{2}a^{2}}{F^{2}(r)}\textrm{d}r^{2}+Z^{2}a^{2}\left(\textrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\textrm{d}\phi^{2}\right)

(6.42)

where, the function
F
is an arbitrary function that comes from integration. Now, since

are functions of only

, we can perform a coordinate transformation

such that the above metric becomes,

\textrm{d}s^{2}=-\textrm{d}t^{2}+a^{2}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}r^{2}}{F^{2}(r)}+r^{2}\textrm{d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\textrm{d}\phi^{2}\right)

(6.43)

This is nothing but the spatially homogeneous and isotropic FLRW metric.

The function
F
can be (conveniently) redefined such that
F^{2}(r)~=~(1~+~\mathcal{B})~-~Kr^{2}
. Given this redefinition, the MG field equations reduce to the usual Friedmann equations. On the other hand, if
F^{2}
is taken to be simply

, we recover the usual FLRW geometry, but now, its evolution is affected by the back-reaction term. This highlights the obvious, albeit important, point that we discussed briefly in the case of LTB solution, which is, in MG, the FLRW geometry evolves differently than in GR and the geometry that evolves like a FLRW geometry is different from FLRW.

Since the integration of this equation depends on what kind of a function

i)
When

-8\pi\rho=\frac{k+\mathcal{B}}{C^{2}}=8\pi p

(6.45)

Using this, equation (
3.8e
) can be solved to find
Q
. Since
P~\equiv~P(x^{0})
, the time coordinate can be rescaled such that
P=0
(similar to what we had in the case of dust). The line element, in this case, looks like,

\textrm{d}s^{2}=-(\textrm{d}x^{0})^{2}+S^{2}\left\{\frac{x^{0}}{C},-(k+\mathcal{B})\right\}(\textrm{d}x^{1})^{2}\\
+C^{2}\left[(\textrm{d}x^{2})^{2}+S^{2}(x^{2},k)(\textrm{d}x^{3})^{2}\right]

(6.46)

ii)
When
R~\neq~\textrm{constant}
;
R^{\prime}~=~0
, one can choose
R~=~x^{0}
. The field equations then give,

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{e}^{-2P}}{(x^{0})^{2}}\left\{1+2x^{0}\dot{Q}+(k+\mathcal{B})\textrm{e}^{2P}\right\}

\displaystyle=8\pi\rho

(6.47a)

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{e}^{-2P}}{(x^{0})^{2}}\left\{1-2x^{0}\dot{P}-(k+\mathcal{B})\textrm{e}^{2P}\right\}

\displaystyle=8\pi p

(6.47b)

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{e}^{-2P}}{(x^{0})^{2}}\left\{1-(x^{0})^{2}\left(\ddot{Q}+\dot{Q}^{2}-\dot{P}\dot{Q}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=4\pi(\rho+3p)

(6.47c)

These equations can be solved to find one of the two functions
P,Q
by prescribing the other one. We know that due to zero acceleration,
P^{\prime}~=~0
. However, it should be noted that, even though there is no
Q^{\prime}
in the field equations, it is not zero in general.

iii)
When
R^{\prime}~\neq~0
, one can choose
P~=~0
. Then, this case becomes similar to the corresponding case with dust. Equation (
6.44
) can be integrated to find,

\textrm{e}^{2Q}=\frac{{R^{\prime}}^{2}}{(k+\mathcal{B})-\varepsilon f^{2}}\qquad\quad\varepsilon=0,\pm 1

(6.48)

The energy density and pressure can be determined from the field equations, which reduce to,

\displaystyle\frac{{\dot{R}}^{2}+\varepsilon f^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{2\dot{R}\dot{R}^{\prime}+2\varepsilon ff^{\prime}}{RR^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=8\pi\rho

(6.49a)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{R}^{2}+\varepsilon f^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{2\ddot{R}}{R}

\displaystyle=-8\pi p

(6.49b)

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{R}^{\prime}}{RR^{\prime}}+\frac{2\ddot{R}}{R}

\displaystyle=-4\pi(\rho+3p)

(6.49c)

The first equation above is the same as the one in the case of dust and the third equation comes from differentiating the second one with respect to
x^{1}
. The metric takes the same form as the one on equation (
5.15
).

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have analysed the effects of averaging on plane and spherically symmetric macroscopic geometries within the framework of macroscopic gravity. We found that the back-reaction takes a form of an anisotropic fluid and enters the field equations in the form of an additional spatial curvature. This was consistent with the findings in
[
25
,
26
,
24
]
for spherically symmetric geometries. Here, we have extended the analysis further to include plane symmetric geometries as well. We categorised the solutions based on the type of source and then analysed various subcases within.

Taking the source to be vacuum led to the MG-Schwarzschild solution. Similarly, for dust, the non-static solutions led to the MG-LTB solution and for a perfect fluid, we derived Schwarzschild interior and Tolman VII (static case) and some non-static solutions. Our approach in this work was different from how back-reaction has been treated in the literature in that we considered the influence of back-reaction on the geometry instead of the dynamics. Since in MG, the back-reaction modifies the field equations, it will influence the geometry and not just the dynamical evolution.
