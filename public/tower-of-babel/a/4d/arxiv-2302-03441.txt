A Nonlocal diffusion model with H 1 H^{1} convergence for Dirichlet Boundary Thanks: This work was supported by National Natural Science Foundation of China under grant 12071244.
Tangjun Wang, Zuoqiang Shi

Abstract
In this paper, we present a nonlocal model for Poisson equation and corresponding eigenproblem with Dirichlet boundary condition. In the direct derivation of the nonlocal model, normal derivative is required which is not known for Dirichlet boundary. To overcome this difficulty, we treat the normal derivative as an auxiliary variable and derive corresponding nonlocal approximation of the boundary condition. For this specifically designed nonlocal model, we can prove its well-posedness and convergence to the counterpart local model. The nonlocal model is carefully designed such that coercivity and symmetry are preserved. Based on these good properties, we can prove the nonlocal model converges with first order rate in H 1 H^{1} norm. Our model can be naturally extended to Poisson problems with Robin boundary and corresponding eigenvalue problem.

Nonlocal models play a crucial role in many fields and have been widely studied, such as in peridynamical theory of continuum mechanics, nonlocal wave propagation and nonlocal diffusion process

. We are particularly interested in the problems where nonlocal operators have compact support

,

]
, parameterized by the nonlocal horizon
\delta
. In this paper, we consider the nonlocal diffusion model, whose local counterpart is the Poisson equation.
There have been a large literature on nonlocal diffusion models

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. Such models usually appear in the field of peridynamics

, where the singularity of materials can be effectively captured. They are also closely related to a meshless numerical methods called smoothed particle hydrodynamics

. Nonlocal models also play important role in machine learning especially in semi-supervised learning

,

,

.

However, when boundary emerges, how to find a proper nonlocal analogous of local boundary conditions becomes a major issue. The traditional local boundary condition is not effective since the boundary is measure zero. One idea is to extend the boundary to a small volume adjacent to the boundary which is so-called volume constraints
[
10
]
. Compared to
O(\delta^{2})
convergence in the interior, naive extension only achieve
O(\delta)
convergence on the boundary
[
21
,
12
]
. High order extension gives high accuracy, but it is difficult to construct and requires high order derivatives of the local solution. Efforts have been made to properly design the nonlocal models or volume constraints in order to achieve better convergence rate, such as for Neumann boundary in one dimension
[
33
]
and two dimension
[
41
]
, which can obtain
O(\delta^{2})
convergence in
L^{2}
norm. The point integral method
[
19
,
28
]
also proposes a nonlocal model to approximate the local Poisson equation with Neumann boundary, and proves
O(\delta)
convergence in
H^{1}
norm.

In parallel with the works on Neumann boundary, the Dirichlet boundary is also an intriguing problem. The most straightforward approach is the constant extension method
[
21
]
, which assume that Dirichlet boundary condition is constantly extended to a small neighborhood outside the domain, but it only provides first order convergence. To achieve higher order convergence, the predominant approaches in this field enforce the no-slip condition (analogy of Dirichlet boundary condition in fluid dynamics) by extrapolating the velocity of a particle across its tangent plane
[
24
,
40
,
16
]
. These methods need to calculate or approximate the distance between particles and the domain boundaries, which can be costly. Recently,
[
39
]
proves
O(\delta^{2})
convergence given the first-order derivatives on the boundaries, which is generally not given a priori. Lee and Du
[
18
]
introduce a nonlocal gradient operator to mimic the extrapolation of the boundary data to the volumetric data, and obtain optimal
O(\delta^{2})
convergence in
L^{2}
norm, but it is analysed only in one dimension. Shi and Sun use point integral method
[
28
]
, and approximate the Dirichlet boundary condition by Robin boundary condition
[
29
]
. In this approach, the proved convergence rate depends on the weighted parameter in Robin boundary condition, which is far from sharp. Zhang and Shi
[
43
]
propose a
O(\delta^{2})
convergent model in
H^{1}
norm, but its model contains extra terms including curvature and normal vector, and requires more regularity and curvature of the boundary, which is hard to obtain in many applications.

In this paper, we propose a new approach to handle Dirichlet boundary condition.
We consider the Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.1
).

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u(\mathbf{x})&=f(\mathbf{x}),&&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
u(\mathbf{x})&=b(\mathbf{x}),&&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Based on the point integral method
[
19
]
, the Poisson equation is well approximated by an integral equation,

\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}=\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

(1.2)

for some kernel functions
R_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)
and
\bar{R}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)
specified later.

However, we cannot directly enforce the Dirichlet boundary condition on the integral equation (
1.2
), since the normal derivative
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}
is not given explicitly. We therefore treat
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}
as a new variable,
v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
.
To close the system, we need more equation about
u
and
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}
. This will be given by careful approximation of the Dirichlet boundary condition.
The idea is to also apply integral approximation (
1.2
) for
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
.
To get symmetry and coercivity, we need to modify the integral approximation on the boundary. First, to preserve the symmetry, we choose the integral kernel to be
\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
which implies the integral equation on the boundary as following

\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)(b(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}=\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}},~\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(1.3)

Here
\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is also a kernel function defined later.

To get coercivity, we further simplify the second term of the left hand side of (
1.3
) by moving
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}
out of the integral.

\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)(b(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-2\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}=\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}},~\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(1.4)

Eventually, we get a nonlocal model as follows

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{G}_{\delta}v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})&=F_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}),&&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\[5.0pt]
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\delta}v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})&=F_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x}),&&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

where the operators are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left(u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}},\quad\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left(b(\mathbf{x})-u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\delta}v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})v_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}},\quad\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\delta}v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}},\quad F_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}}

Our goal in this paper is to present that model (
1.5
) assures a unique solution
\left(u_{\delta},v_{\delta}\right)
and it converges to the solution
\left(u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\right)
of problem (
1.1
) as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

Remark 1.1
.

We can set the right-hand side of
Equation

1.4
to zero, i.e.,
F_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x})=0
, without affecting the main results in the paper, as explained in the proof of
Theorem

7.4
.

The main contribution of this paper is that, for Poisson equation with Dirichlet condition, we prove that the solution computed by the nonlocal model (
1.5
) converges to the counterpart local solution with the rate of
O(\delta)
in
H^{1}
norm. We use standard techniques in numerical analysis, which combine consistency and stability to prove convergence. We prove the coercivity of the nonlocal operator, which implies the stability of our method. Together with the estimate of the truncation error, we get the convergence of our nonlocal model to the local Poisson equation. Our nonlocal model applies in any smooth domain in any dimension even in smooth manifold. This gives a very general approach to handle the Dirichlet boundary condition in nonlocal diffusion model.

Preliminaries

First we state some basic assumptions used in our analysis.

{assumption}
[Assumptions on the domain]

\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, bounded and connected.
\partial\Omega
is

smooth.

{assumption}

[Assumptions on the kernel function]

(a)

Smoothness:

;

(b)

R(r)\geq 0
for
\forall r\geq 0
;

(c)

Compact support:
R(r)=0
for
\forall r>1
;

(d)

Nondegeneracy:
\exists\gamma_{0}>0
so that

for

.

\bar{R}
and
\bar{\bar{R}}
are defined as

Then the rescaled kernel function

are given as

\tilde{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=C_{\delta}\tilde{R}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}\right)

for
\tilde{R}\in[R,\bar{R},\bar{\bar{R}}]
. The constant

is a normalization factor so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\alpha_{n}S_{n}\int_{0}^{1}R(\frac{r^{2}}{4})r^{n-1}\mathrm{d}r=1

with
S_{n}
denotes the surface area of unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Here we provide some basic estimates that we use later.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\tilde{R}
be a kernel function satisfying
Section

2
(a)(b)(c) and

\tilde{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\alpha_{n}\delta^{-n}\tilde{R}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}\right)

Then there exists a constant

and

,

\frac{C_{1}}{3}<\int_{\Omega}\tilde{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\leq C_{1},\qquad\frac{C_{2}}{3\delta}<\int_{\partial\Omega}\tilde{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\delta}

where

C_{1}=\alpha_{n}S_{n}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{R}(\frac{r^{2}}{4})r^{n-1}\mathrm{d}r,\qquad C_{2}=\alpha_{n}S_{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{R}(\frac{r^{2}}{4})r^{n-2}\mathrm{d}r

\delta
.

Proof 2.2
.

See
Section

A.1

Clearly
\tilde{R}\in[R,\bar{R},\bar{\bar{R}}]

w_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},\quad\bar{w}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},\quad\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

Finally, to simplify the notation and make the proof concise, we consider the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u(\mathbf{x})&=f(\mathbf{x}),&&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\

and consequently the operator

Theorem 3.3

4.1
Existence and uniqueness

In this subsection, we will prove the first part of
Theorem

3.1
, i.e. the existence and uniqueness of solution
\left(u_{\delta},v_{\delta}\right)
to
Equation

1.5
, by Lax-Milgram Theorem. We observe that in the second equation of
Equation

1.5
,

\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\delta}v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}=2\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})

Thus we can eliminate
v_{\delta}
in the first equation by noticing that

v_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}\int_{\Omega}\left(\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})+\delta^{2}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega

(4.6)

So the first equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})f(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

(4.7)

We introduce the bilinear form
B:L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle B[u_{\delta},p_{\delta}]={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

and denote

(Continuity)

For any

Proof 4.2
.

\displaystyle B[u_{\delta},p_{\delta}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

Recall the estimates we have established in
Proposition

2.1
. The first term

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\left(\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\vphantom{\int}C\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq C\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|u_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

The second term can be bounded by using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\left[\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{1}\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u_{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle C_{1}\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}R_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)u_{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle C\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\int_{\Omega}u_{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=C\left\|p_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|u_{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

The third term

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\bar{\bar{w}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\left(\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\right)\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{1}\delta\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})p_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})u_{\delta}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\right)\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bar{R}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})u(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=r_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

We have already proved that

\|r_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

and the other term is a lower order term

\left\|\int_{\Omega}\bar{\bar{R}}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\delta}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\delta}\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

The dominating term now becomes
O(1/\delta)
. Nonetheless, the convergence results still hold due to the high order of
\delta
prefactors in
Corollary

5.3
. The
\delta^{3}
prefactor before
\|r_{\mathrm{bd}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
now becomes
\delta^{2}
due to the extra

, which gives us

\|e_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\delta\|e_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+C\delta^{2}\|u\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}

Then we still have

\left\|u-u_{\delta}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\delta\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

Thus

\left\|T-T_{\delta}\right\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\delta

Finally, we are ready to derive the convergence results for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The following results are stated with the help of the Riesz spectral projection. Let
X
be a complex Banach space and
L:X\rightarrow X
be a compact linear operator. The resolvent set
\rho(L)
is given by the complex numbers
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
z-L
is bijective. The spectrum of
L
is
\sigma(L)=\mathbb{C}\backslash\rho(L)
. It is well known that
\sigma(L)
is a countable set with no limit points other than zero. All non-zero values in
\sigma(L)
are eigenvalues. If
\lambda
is a nonzero eigenvalue of
L

\Gamma\subset\rho(L)
which encloses the eigenvalue
\lambda
and no other elements of
\sigma(L)
, the Riesz spectral projection associated with
\lambda
is defined by

E(\lambda,L)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}(z-L)^{-1}\mathrm{~d}z

Then we have the following theorem, giving us the convergence results of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The proof is similar to Theorem 4.3 in
[
31
]
. Nonetheless, we provide the proof in
Section

E.2
for completeness.

(

]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{m}
be the m-th largest eigenvalue of

with multiplicity
\alpha_{m}
and

Conclusion

In this work, we propose a nonlocal model to enforce the local Dirichlet boundary condition on Poisson equations. We prove the well-posedness of the proposed model and the first order convergence in
H^{1}
norm. Our model provides a general framework to handle Dirichlet boundary condition for nonlocal diffusion problem with smooth domain in any dimension. The nonlocal model and the analysis can be naturally extended to manifold also.
The convergence rate of the proposed nonlocal model is first order in
H^{1}
norm which is not optimal (second order). We are also working on the nonlocal diffusion model with optimal convergence rate for Dirichlet boundary condition. The results will be reported in the future paper.
