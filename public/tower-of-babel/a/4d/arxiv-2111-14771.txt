A fast algorithm on average for solving the Hamilton Cycle problem
Michael Anastos

Abstract
We present CertifyHAM, a deterministic algorithm that takes a graph G G as input and either finds a Hamilton cycle of G G or outputs that such a cycle does not exist. If G ∼ G ⁡ ( n , p ) G\sim G(n,p) and p ≥ 100 ​ log ⁡ n n p\geq\frac{100\log n}{n} then the expected running time of CertifyHAM is O ⁡ ( n p ) O(\frac{n}{p}) which is best possible. This improves upon previous results due to Gurevich and Shelah, Thomason and Alon, and Krivelevich, who proved analogous results for p p being constant, p ≥ 12 n − 1 / 3 p\geq 12n^{-1/3} and p ≥ 70 n − 1 / 2 p\geq 70n^{-1/2} respectively.

1.1
Our contribution

In this paper, we introduce CertifyHAM, an algorithm that solves HAM fast in expectation.
It implements in succession 2 algorithms that solve HAM, first the MatrixCerHAM algorithm and then the DCerHAM algorithm. MatrixCerHAM is a fast algorithm that solves HAM with high probability while DCerHAM solves HAM slower but in expectation. This paper is devoted to presenting and analyzing DCerHAM. The description of MatrixCerHAM and proof of Theorem
1.2

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm MatrixCerHAM that takes as input the adjacency matrix of a graph

1.2
The RCerHAM algorithm

In this subsection, we give a sketch of the RCerHAM algorithm. For its description, we introduce the definition of a full path packing and the Pósa rotations procedure. We then present the main elements of its analysis. We also describe how to derandomize it to obtain DCerHAM.

RCerHAM first generates a subset of

of size at most
|S|
until no such a set exists. As soon as we identify such a set, we add it to
S
and then continue our search. Once our search is finished, the set
S
has the property that no set

is non-expanding. The next time (II) occurs a non-expanding set

is identified, thus
|T|>|S|
. It follows that after each time (II) occurs the set
S
doubles in size, thus
\Delta\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\log_{2}n}2\cdot 2^{i}\leq 4n
. Initially,
|\mathcal{P}|=n
and
|\mathcal{P}|=0
implies that we have identified a Hamilton cycle. Each time an attempt to improve
|\mathcal{P}|
is made
|\mathcal{P}|
is decreased by at least
1
with probability
0.5p
. As
\Delta\leq 4n
we may improve on
|\mathcal{P}|
at most
5n
times until it reaches
0
. Thus the probability
0.01n^{2}
attempts to improve
\mathcal{P}
are made is
\operatorname{\bf Pr}(Bin(0.01n^{2},0.5p)<5n)
and the corresponding event is very unlikely, causing CerHAM to exit the while-loop. This completes the description of RCerHAM.

The single point in which DCerHAM and RCerHAM differ is in generating
G_{0}
. Later on, at the execution of DCerHAM we derandomize this step by letting
F_{0}
be the edge set of
H_{n}
and defining
G_{0}
by
V(G_{0})=[n]
and
E(G_{0})=F_{0}\cap E(G)
.
H_{n}
will be a well-chosen pseudorandom graph of linear in
n
minimum degree containing a constant fraction of the edges of

. Roughly speaking, a graph

is pseudorandom if the edge counts between any two linear sets is close to what one would expect it to be in
G\sim G(n,p)
with

. Substituting

with

will suffices for our calculations to go through.

1.2.1
Comparison to previous work

Our algorithm does not build upon the previous algorithms of Gurevich and Shelah, Thomason and Alon and Krivelevich. For example, the algorithm given by Alon and Krivelevich first builds a cycle that covers all the vertices of degree at most
np/2
and then grows this cycle by sequentially absorbing the rest of the vertices using local alterations. For example given a cycle

of

on

vertices and a vertex

outside

their algorithm may look for vertices

that appear in this order on the cycle such that if we let

be the vertices preceding them on

then

are neighbors of

and
a^{-}b,b^{-}c,c^{-}d^{-}\in E(G)
. In this case
(E(c)\cup\{a^{-}b,b^{-}c,c^{-}d^{-},av,vd\})\setminus\{aa^{-},bb^{-},cc^{-},dd^{-}\}
is a Hamilton cycle. Their analysis is based on the fact that with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
there are at most
np/20
vertices of degree smaller than

. This statement is not true for
p=o(n^{-0.5)}
. Thus the vertex
v
has at least
np/10
neighbors

such that each of

has at least

neighbors

and

. This gives

quadruples

such that for each quadruple there exists a unique edge

which can be used to absorb

into

. On the other hand, the bottleneck of our algorithm is found in the fact that the statement “the probability there exists a set

of size

such that

, where

2.1
The Inclusion-Exclusion HAM algorithm

For a graph
G
,
u,v\in V(G)
,
S\subset V(G)
and
e\in E(G)
denote by
G-S
the subgraph of
G
induced by
V(G)\setminus S
, by
G-e
the graph obtained by removing
e
from
G
, by
A_{G}
the adjacent matrix of
G
and finally by
A_{G}(v,u)
the entry of
A_{G}
that corresponds to the pair of vertices
v,u
. The Inclusion-Exclusion HAM algorithm is based on the observation that
A_{G}^{n-1}(u,v)
equals the number of walks from
v
to
u
of length
n-1
and that the number of Hamiltonian paths from
v
to
u
can be obtained from the Inclusion-Exclusion principle and the matrices
\{A_{(G-uv)-S}^{n-1}\}_{S\subseteq V(G)\setminus\{v,u\}}
.

More concretely, for each
e\in E(G)
the Inclusion-Exclusion HAM algorithm executes the following steps. It lets
F=G-e
,
e=\{v,u\}
and calculates,

H(v,u)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(-1)^{k}\sum_{S\in\binom{V(F)\setminus\{v,u\}}{k}}A_{F-S}^{n-1}(v,u).

If
H(v,u)\neq 0
it then outputs that
G
has a Hamilton cycle that passes through the edge
e=\{v,u\}
.
If
H(v,u)=0
for every
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
then it outputs “
G
is not Hamiltonian”.

For the correctness of the algorithm observe that
A_{F-S}^{n-1}(v,u)
equals the number of walks from
v
to
u
of length
n-1
that do not pass through
S
. Thus, by the inclusion-exclusion principle,
H(v,u)
equals to the number of Hamiltonian paths of
G
from
v
to
u
. Finally as for each edge at most
n2^{n}
matrix multiplications are performed its worst-time running complexity is
O(|E(G)|\cdot n^{3}\cdot n2^{n})=O(n^{6}2^{n})
.

A graph

Lemma A.1

•

For a graph
