A unified approach to the small-time behavior of the spectral heat content for isotropic Lévy processes
Kei Kobayashi, Hyunchul Park

Abstract
This paper establishes the precise small-time asymptotic behavior of the spectral heat content for isotropic Lévy processes on bounded C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} open sets of ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} with d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , where the underlying characteristic exponents are regularly varying at infinity with index α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in(1,2] , including the case α = 2 \alpha=2 . Moreover, this asymptotic behavior is shown to be stable under an integrable perturbation of its Lévy measure. These results cover a wide class of isotropic Lévy processes, including Brownian motions, stable processes, and jump diffusions, and the proofs provide a unified approach to the asymptotic behavior of the spectral heat content for all of these processes.

1
Introduction

The spectral heat content (SHC) measures the total heat contained in a domain
D
at a given time
t>0
when the initial temperature is set to be one and the temperature outside
D
is kept at zero.
The SHC contains geometric information of the domain and spectral information of the infinitesimal generator of the underlying process.
The SHC for Brownian motions was studied intensively more than three decades ago, while the investigation into the SHC for jump processes started within the last decade. In
[
2
,
3
]
, the author established the small-time asymptotic behavior of the SHC for isotropic stable processes on bounded
C^{1,1}
open sets and provided two-term upper and lower bounds. Later in
[
14
]
, the precise two-term asymptotic expansion of the SHC for the same processes was established.
Various questions about the SHC, including those for other jump processes or for higher order terms, have been answered in
[
7
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

The investigations to be conducted in this paper are natural continuation of those in
[
7
,
14
]
. In
[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
, the authors established the two-term small-time asymptotic expansion of the SHC for Lévy processes on
{\mathbb{R}}
whose characteristic exponents are regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(1,2]
, whereas in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
, the same problem was answered for isotropic (rotationally invariant) stable processes on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
with characteristic exponents
|\xi|^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
. On the other hand,
Theorem
3.1
of the current paper establishes the two-term small-time asymptotic expansion of the SHC for isotropic Lévy processes on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whose characteristic exponents are regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(1,2]
, thus extending
[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
to higher dimensions while generalizing
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
to cover a large class of Lévy processes, including non-stable ones. Moreover, Corollary
3.2
establishes that this asymptotic expansion is stable when the Lévy measure is perturbed so that the difference of the original Lévy measure and the new one is integrable. This makes our asymptotic result applicable to the so-called
truncated jump processes
, where all large jumps (say, jump sizes bigger than 1) are removed.

Let us clarify the contributions of this paper to the existing literature. First, the main theorem, Theorem
3.1
, is proved under minimal assumptions. We only require that the Lévy process be isotropic (rotationally invariant) and its characteristic exponent be regularly varying at infinity with index
\alpha\in(1,2]
; no additional assumptions are imposed on the exponent or the associated Lévy measure. Thus, it covers a large class of Lévy processes including Brownian motions, stable processes, relativistic stable processes (also called tempered stable processes), mixed stable processes, jump diffusions, and their truncated versions. A list of concrete examples is provided in Section
4
.
Second, the proof of Theorem
3.1
is robust. In fact, when the two-term asymptotic behavior of the SHC for Brownian motions was obtained in
[
4
, Theorem 6.2]
, the independence of the components played a critical role in their proof, but the latter approach does not work for jump processes since the components are no longer independent. Instead, we will build our discussion upon the methods used to derive
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
for stable processes, while significantly modifying and sophisticating their arguments to overcome the challenges caused by the lack of self-similarity for non-stable Lévy processes. Consequently, the methods to be employed in this paper can be regarded as an alternative, intricate approach to deriving both
[
4
, Theorem 6.2]
and
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Now we briefly illustrate our strategy to prove Theorem
3.1
, which indicates that the
heat loss

|D|-Q_{D}^{X}(t)

(representing the total amount of heat that has been lost from the domain
D
by time
t>0
due to the zero exterior condition) decays at the rate of
\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}
, where
\psi
denotes the regularly varying characteristic exponent of the underlying Lévy process
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
We first verify in Lemma
3.3
that for heat particles starting at points well inside the domain
D
, the heat loss is of order
O(t)
, which is negligible when compared to the above-mentioned rate function
\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}
.
Next, for heat particles starting at points near the boundary of the domain, we analyze the heat loss by utilizing the
scaled process

\widetilde{X}^{(t)}=\{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
defined by
\widetilde{X}_{u}^{(t)}=\psi^{-1}(1/t)X_{tu}
(see Equation (
3.1
)).
For stable processes, this scaled process is equal in law to the original stable process due to self-similarity, so computations are often carried out in a straightforward manner. In our case, however, this is no longer the case, and as a result, various aspects of our discussion not only become complicated but also require new estimates that were not present in
[
14
]
in the case of stable processes.
The sharp asymptotic upper bound of the heat loss is established via Lemmas
3.4
,
3.5
, and
3.6
, while the sharp asymptotic lower bound is derived via Lemmas
3.4
,
3.7
, and
3.8
.
For the upper bound, we use the
interior ball condition
of the
C^{1,1}
open set
D
; for any
x\in D
close enough to the boundary, there exists a ball of a fixed radius
R>0
that contains
x
and is contained in
D
.
This property, together with Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
, allows us to translate the problem about the heat loss from the domain
D
into the problem about the heat loss from the upper half-space
H=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{d}>0\}
.
For the lower bound, in contrast, we use the
exterior ball condition
; for any
x\in D
close enough to the boundary, there exists a ball of a fixed radius
R>0
that is contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D}
.
Using Lemmas
3.7
and
3.8
, we again analyze the heat loss from
D
by connecting it with the heat loss from the half-space.

The organization of this paper is as follows. Section
2
sets up notations and recalls necessary facts. The main results, Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
, are presented and proved in Section
3
. Section
4
illustrates concrete examples of Lévy processes to which our results are applicable.
The notation
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
stands for the law of the underlying process started at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
or
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
depending on the context, and
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
stands for expectation under
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
.
For simplicity, we write
{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{P}}_{0}
and
{\mathbb{E}}={\mathbb{E}}_{0}
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
.
By the Lévy-Khintchine theorem (
[
16
, Theorem 8.1]
),

{\mathbb{E}}[e^{i\xi\cdot X_{t}}]=e^{-t\psi(\xi)},\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

with

\psi(\xi)=-i\xi\cdot\gamma+\xi\cdot A\xi-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(e^{i\xi\cdot x}-1-i\xi\cdot xI_{|x|\leq 1}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
A
is a symmetric, nonnegative-definite matrix, and
\nu
is a measure satisfying
\nu(\{0\})=0
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(1\wedge|x|^{2})\nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
.
The law of
X
is uniquely characterized by the characteristic exponent
\psi(\xi)
, and the triplet
(A,\gamma,\nu)
is called the Lévy triplet of
X
.
Throughout the paper,
X
is assumed to be isotropic (i.e., rotationally invariant), which implies that
A=aI
, a constant multiple of the identity matrix, and that
\psi
is a radial function, so we will write
\psi(b)=\psi(\xi)
for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|\xi|=b
.

An isotropic stable Lévy process with stability index
\alpha
is a Lévy process with
A=0
and
\psi(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}
.
It is well-known that
\alpha\in(0,2]
, and when
\alpha=2
, it is a Brownian motion that runs twice as fast as the standard Brownian motion.
In this paper, an isotropic stable process with stability index
\alpha
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
is denoted by
X^{(\alpha)}=\{X_{t}^{(\alpha)}\}_{t\geq 0}
, whereas a symmetric stable process in
{\mathbb{R}}
is denoted by
Y^{(\alpha)}=\{Y_{t}^{(\alpha)}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

A real valued function
f
is said to be regularly varying at infinity with index
\alpha
if

\lim_{y\to\infty}\frac{f(xy)}{f(y)}=x^{\alpha}\text{ for all }x>0.

The class of regularly varying functions at infinity with index
\alpha
is denoted by
{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
.
For the rest of this paper, we assume that
X
is an isotropic Lévy process in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the characteristic exponent
\psi(\xi)\in{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
with some
\alpha\in(1,2]
.
Notice that the asymptotic behavior of the spectral heat content when
\alpha\in(0,1)
was already studied in
[
7
]
, and hence, we are left with the case
\alpha\in[1,2]
. In the present paper, however, we restrict our discussion to
\alpha\in(1,2]
since Theorem
3.1
requires the expectation appearing in (
2.6
) to be finite. On the other hand, the threshold case
\alpha=1
is worth investigating, and this will be explored in a future project.

Let
D
be an open set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and let
\tau^{X}_{D}=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}\notin D\}
be the first exit time of an isotropic Lévy process
X
from
D
. The spectral heat content
Q^{X}_{D}(t)
of
X
at time
t>0
is defined by

Q^{X}_{D}(t)=\int_{D}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}^{X}>t)\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau^{X}_{D}>t)
is the unique solution to the following Cauchy problem:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\mathcal{L}u(t,x),&(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times D,\\
\lim_{t\to 0}u(t,x)=1&x\in D,\\
u(t,y)=0,&t>0,\,y\in D^{c},\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{L}
is the infinitesimal generator of the killed process
X^{D}
. Hence, the spectral heat content
Q^{X}_{D}(t)
measures the total heat that remains in
D
at time
t>0
when the initial temperature of
D
is set at one and the temperature of
D^{c}
is kept identically zero.

An open set
D
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is said to be
C^{1,1}
if
there exist a localization radius
R>0
and a constant
\Lambda>0
that satisfy the following condition (see e.g.,
[
14
]
):
for every
z\in\partial D
, there exist a
C^{1,1}
function
\phi=\phi_{z}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
\phi(0)=0
,
\nabla\phi(0)=(0,\ldots,0)
,

\|\nabla\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\Lambda
,

|\nabla\phi(x_{1})-\nabla\phi(x_{2})|\leq\Lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|
for
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
, and an orthonormal coordinate system
CS_{z}:y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1},y_{d})=(\tilde{y},y_{d})

with origin at
z
such that

B(z,R)\cap D=B(z,R)\cap\{y=(\tilde{y},y_{d})\mbox{ in }CS_{z}:y_{d}>\phi(\tilde{y})\}.

The pair
(R,\Lambda)
is called the
C^{1,1}
characteristics of the
C^{1,1}
open set
D
.
It is well-known that any
C^{1,1}
open set
D
with
C^{1,1}
characteristics
(R,\Lambda)
satisfies the uniform interior and exterior
R
-ball condition
(see
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
):
for any
z\in\partial D
, there exist open balls
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of the same radius
R
such that

\displaystyle B_{1}\subset D,\ B_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D},\ \textrm{and}\ \partial B_{1}\cap\partial B_{2}=\{z\}.

(2.1)

For
x\in D
, let
\delta_{D}(x)
denote the Euclidean distance from
x
to the boundary
\partial D
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta_{D}(x)=\inf\{|x-y|:y\in\partial D\}.

(2.2)

For each
a\in(0,R/2]
, let

\displaystyle D_{a}=\{x\in D:\delta_{D}(x)>a\},

(2.3)

which is a region obtained by removing points near the boundary
\partial D
from
D
.
Note that the
C^{1,1}
condition with
C^{1,1}
characteristics
(R,\Lambda)
implies that the set
D_{a}
is non-empty.
It also follows from
[
4
, Lemma 6.7]
that if the
C^{1,1}
open set
D
is bounded, then

|\partial D|\left(\frac{R-a}{R}\right)^{d-1}\leq|\partial D_{a}|\leq|\partial D|\left(\frac{R}{R-a}\right)^{d-1},

(2.4)

where
|\partial D|
and
|\partial D_{a}|
denote the perimeters (or the Lebesgue measures) of the sets
\partial D
and
\partial D_{a}
, respectively.
In particular,
|\partial D_{a}|\leq 2^{d-1}|\partial D|
for any
a\in(0,R/2]
.
The reader is advised to note that the same notation
|\cdot|
is used throughout the paper to denote the Euclidean norm.

For
x\in D\setminus D_{R/2}
(so that
\delta_{D}(x)\leq R/2
), let
z_{x}
be the unique point in
\partial D
such that
|x-z_{x}|=\delta_{D}(x)
.
Let
\mathbf{n}_{z_{x}}=\frac{z_{x}-x}{|z_{x}-x|}
be the outward unit normal vector to
\partial D
at the point
z_{x}
. For an isotropic
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
X^{(\alpha)}
starting at
x
, let

Y^{(\alpha)}_{t}=(X^{(\alpha)}_{t}-x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{z_{x}}

be the one-dimensional projection of
X^{(\alpha)}_{t}-x
onto the direction of
\mathbf{n}_{z_{x}}
.
Then the process
Y^{(\alpha)}=\{Y^{(\alpha)}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a symmetric
\alpha
-stable process in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, in the particular case when
X^{(\alpha)}
starts at a point
(\tilde{0},v)=(0,\ldots,0,v)
in the upper half-space
H=\{x=(\tilde{x},x_{d})=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{d}>0\}
, the relationship

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{H}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)={\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}\geq v)

(2.5)

holds, where
{\mathbb{P}}
without the subscript means that the process starts from the origin, and

\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{t}:=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}Y^{(\alpha)}_{s}

represents the supremum process of
Y^{(\alpha)}
; see the proofs of
[
14
, Lemma 3.1, Proposition 3.3]
for details. By
[
5
, Proposition 4 in Section VIII.1]
, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}>u)\sim{\mathbb{P}}(Y^{(\alpha)}_{1}>u)\sim C_{\alpha}u^{-\alpha}\text{ as }u\to\infty.

The identity
{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}>u)\mathrm{d}u
implies that

{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]<\infty
if
\alpha\in(1,2),
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]=\infty
if
\alpha\in(0,1].

The case
\alpha=2
corresponds to Brownian motion, and it is well-known that
{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(2)}]={\mathbb{E}}[|Y_{1}^{(2)}|]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x}{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}<\infty
.
Hence,

{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]<\infty\text{ if and only if }\alpha\in(1,2].

(2.6)

3
Main Results

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 3.1

Let
D
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
open set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
.
Let
X
be an isotropic Lévy process in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the characteristic exponent
\psi\in{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
,
\alpha\in(1,2]
.
Then

\lim_{t\to 0}\psi^{-1}(1/t)(|D|-Q_{D}^{X}(t))=|\partial D|{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}],

where
\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}
is the supremum process of a symmetric
\alpha
-stable process
Y^{(\alpha)}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem
3.1
continues to hold for a Lévy process
U
whose Lévy measure is close to that of
X
in the sense that the difference of the two is a finite signed measure.
In this case, one can use
[
7
, Theorem 5.1]
whose main idea is to decompose the process
U
as an independent sum of the process
X
and a compound Poisson process. Here is a precise statement:

Corollary 3.2

Let
D
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
open set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
.
Let
U
be an isotropic Lévy process in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with Lévy triplet
(aI,0,\nu^{U})
. If there exists another isotropic Lévy process with Lévy triplet
(aI,0,\nu^{X})
and the characteristic exponent
\psi\in{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
,
\alpha\in(1,2]
, such that
\sigma(\mathrm{d}x)=\nu^{U}(\mathrm{d}x)-\nu^{X}(\mathrm{d}x)
is a finite signed measure, then

|D|-Q_{D}^{U}(t)=|\partial D|{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}]\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}+o(\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}).

Proof.
The condition
\psi\in\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(\infty)
implies
\psi^{-1}\in\mathcal{R}_{1/\alpha}(\infty)
due to
[
6
, Theorems 1.5.3 and 1.5.12]
, and in particular,

t=o(\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1})
as
t\to 0
.
Hence, the desired conclusion follows from
[
7
, Theorem 5.1]
and Theorem
3.1
.

\Box

The remainder of the section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
Our discussion builds upon the approach taken in
[
14
]
for stable processes, but we will modify that approach substantially to cover more general isotropic Lévy processes.

The first lemma below shows that the contribution of points inside the domain
D
to the amount of heat loss
|D|-Q_{D}^{X}(t)=\int_{D}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x
is at most of order
t
.
On the other hand, since
\psi^{-1}\in\mathcal{R}_{1/\alpha}(\infty)
when
\psi\in\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(\infty)
due to
[
6
, Theorems 1.5.3 and 1.5.12]
, it follows that

t=o(\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1})

as
t\to 0
.
Hence, it is concluded that the contribution to the heat loss from inside the domain
D
is negligible when it is compared to
\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}
. The lemma will be used later in the derivation of the upper bound for Theorem
3.1
.
Recall the notation
D_{a}
introduced in (
2.3
).

Lemma 3.3

Let
D
and
X
be as in Theorem
3.1
, where the
C^{1,1}
characteristics of
D
are given by
(R,\Lambda)
. Then for any
a\in(0,R/2]
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\int_{D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x\leq c|D|t

for all
t>0
.

Proof.
For any
x\in D_{a}
, by
[
15
, Equation (3.2)]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|X_{s}-x|\geq a\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{s\leq t}|X_{s}|\geq a\right)\leq ct,

where
c>0
is a constant independent of
x
.
In fact,
[
15
, Equation (3.2)]
assumes that the Gaussian part of
X
vanishes, but one can include a Gaussian part given by a constant multiple of a Brownian motion (recall that
X
is assumed to be isotropic) since the tail of its supremum
process has an upper bound that decays exponentially as
t
decreases; see
[
9
, Chapter 2, Equation (8.3)’]
.
Therefore,

\int_{D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x\leq c|D_{a}|t\leq c|D|t,

which completes the proof.

\Box

The next lemma is similar to
[
14
, Proposition 3.3]
.
Let

H=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{d}>0\}

be the upper half-space.
In the remainder of the paper, we use the notation
\tilde{0}
to represent the zero vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
.

Lemma 3.4

Let
D
and
X
be as in Theorem
3.1
, where the
C^{1,1}
characteristics of
D
are given by
(R,\Lambda)
.
Then there exists
A=A(\psi)\in(0,R/2]
such that for any
a\in(0,A]
,

\lim_{t\to 0}\psi^{-1}(1/t)\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau^{X}_{H}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u={\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}].

Proof.
For each
t>0
, define the scaled process
\widetilde{X}^{(t)}=\{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{u}^{(t)}=\psi^{-1}(1/t)X_{tu}.

(3.1)

Referring to the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 4.5]
, one can verify that the characteristic exponent
t\psi(\psi^{-1}(1/t)\xi)
of
\widetilde{X}^{(t)}
converges to the characteristic exponent
|\xi|^{\alpha}
of an isotropic
\alpha
-stable process
X^{(\alpha)}
as
t\to 0
. By
[
8
, Exercise 16.10]
, the latter implies that
\widetilde{X}^{(t)}
converges weakly to
X^{(\alpha)}
in the Skorokhod space equipped with the
J_{1}
-topology.
Note that

\displaystyle\tau_{H}^{X}

\displaystyle=\inf\{u:X_{u}\notin H\}=\inf\{tu:\psi^{-1}(1/t)X_{tu}\notin\psi^{-1}(1/t)H\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{tu:\psi^{-1}(1/t)X_{tu}\notin H\}=t\inf\{u:\widetilde{X}_{u}^{(t)}\notin H\}=t\tau_{H}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}},

where we used the fact that the upper half-space
H
is invariant under the multiplication by any positive constant. The latter
shows that the law of
\tau_{H}^{X}
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
is equal to the law of
t\tau_{H}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\psi^{-1}(1/t)x}
.
Hence, for a fixed
a\in(0,R/2]
, by the change of variables
v=\psi^{-1}(1/t)u
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau_{H}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},\psi^{-1}(1/t)u)}(\tau_{H}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{a\psi^{-1}(1/t)}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{H}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v.

(3.2)

By (
2.6
),
{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}\geq v)\mathrm{d}v<\infty
for
\alpha\in(1,2]
. Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N=N(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\int_{0}^{N}{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}\geq v)\mathrm{d}v>{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]-\varepsilon.

Let

\widetilde{Z}_{u}^{(t)}=\widetilde{X}_{u}^{(t)}\cdot e_{d}=\psi^{-1}(1/t)X_{tu}\cdot e_{d}

be the projection of

t\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\psi^{-1}(1/t)x}
, where
\widetilde{X}^{(t)}
is the scaled process defined in (
3.1
).
Hence, by the change of variables
v=\psi^{-1}(1/t)u
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau^{X}_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},\psi^{-1}(1/t)u)}(\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{a\psi^{-1}(1/t)/2}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\geq\psi^{-1}(1/t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v

(3.16)

for all sufficiently small
t>0
(since
\psi^{-1}(1/t)\to\infty
as
t\to 0
).

Now we construct an increasing sequence of domains
E(n)
by

E(n)=\left\{x=(\tilde{x},x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}:|\tilde{x}|<n,\,-n<x_{d}<0,\,\cos^{-1}(-\vec{\bf{n}}\cdot\frac{x}{|x|})<\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\right\},\quad-\vec{\bf{n}}=(\tilde{0},-1).

n
-n
0
E(n)

Since
E(n)
increases to the lower half-space
H^{\prime}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):x_{d}<0\}
as
n\to\infty
, it follows from the bounded convergence theorem that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{E(n)^{c}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v=\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{(H^{\prime})^{c}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v=\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{\overline{H}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v.

Since the latter sequence converges increasingly, we may take an integer
N=N(\varepsilon)
such that

\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{E(N)^{c}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v>\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{\overline{H}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v-\varepsilon.

(3.17)

Since
B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)
increases to
H^{\prime}
as
t\to 0
, we can take
t_{0}=t_{0}(N)>0
such that for any
0<t\leq t_{0}
,

E(N)\subset B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),

which implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-\psi^{-1}(1/t)R),\psi^{-1}(1/t)R)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{E(N)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1).

(3.18)

Combining (
3
)–(
3.18
) and using Fatou’s lemma and the weak convergence of
\widetilde{X}^{(t)}
to
X^{(\alpha)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\psi^{-1}(1/t)\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau^{X}_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{E(N)^{c}}^{\widetilde{X}^{(t)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{E(N)^{c}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle>\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},v)}(\tau_{\overline{H}}^{X^{(\alpha)}}\leq 1)\mathrm{d}v-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}>v)\mathrm{d}v-\varepsilon>{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}_{1}^{(\alpha)}]-2\varepsilon,

where the last line follows from (
3.15
).
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\liminf_{t\to 0}\psi^{-1}(1/t)\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau^{X}_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u\geq{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Y}^{(\alpha)}_{1}],

as desired.

\Box

Recall that for a starting point
x\in D\setminus D_{R/2}
,

denotes the half-space containing the interior

z_{x}
and tangent to

\tilde{B_{x}}

.

Lemma 3.8

Let
D
and
X
be as in Theorem

, where the

are given by

.
Let

.
Then for any

,

\lim_{t\to 0}\psi^{-1}(1/t)\int_{D\setminus D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{\tilde{B_{x}}^{c}}^{X})\mathrm{d}x=0.

Proof.
By (

),

|\partial D_{a}|\leq 2^{d-1}|\partial D|
for all

. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{D\setminus D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{\tilde{B_{x}}^{c}}^{X})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}|\partial D_{u}|{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau_{H}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}^{X})\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}|\partial D|\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau_{H}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}^{X})\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}|\partial D|\left(\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau_{H}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{a}{\mathbb{P}}_{(\tilde{0},u)}(\tau_{B((\tilde{0},-R),R)^{c}}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}u\right).

Applying Lemmas
3.4
and

, which extends

for

to any isotropic Lévy processes with characteristic exponents

We remark that there is a minor mistake in the proof of

.
The authors there claimed that

Derivation of the lower bound for Theorem
3.1
.

As we did in the derivation of the upper bound for Theorem
3.1
, for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, take
a=a(\varepsilon)\in(0,A]
such that (
3.12
) holds, which results in (
3.14
).

Under
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
with
x\in D\setminus D_{a}
,

\displaystyle\{\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t\}

\displaystyle\subset\{\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t,\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t\}\cup\{\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{D}^{X}\}

\displaystyle\subset\{\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t\}\cup\{\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{D}^{X}\}

\displaystyle\subset\{\tau_{D}^{X}\leq t\}\cup\{\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{\tilde{B_{x}}^{c}}^{X}\},

(3.19)

where
H_{x}
and

are the ones defined right above the statement of Lemma

The latter implies that

\displaystyle|D|-Q^{X}_{D}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau^{X}_{D}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{D\setminus D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau^{X}_{D}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{D\setminus D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{D\setminus D_{a}}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{H_{x}}^{X}\leq t<\tau_{\tilde{B_{x}}^{c}}^{X})\mathrm{d}x.

4
Examples

This section illustrates the fact that our results, Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
, are applicable to a broad range of Lévy processes.

Example 4.1
(Pure Jump Process)

Theorem
3.1
applies to the following pure jump processes:

1.

Stable Process
\psi(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in(1,2)
.

2.

Mixed Stable Process
\psi(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{\beta}
, where
0<\beta<\alpha
and
\alpha\in(1,2)
.

3.

Relativistic Stable Process
\psi(\xi)=(|\xi|^{2}+m^{2/\alpha})^{\alpha/2}-m
, where
m>0
and
\alpha\in(1,2)
.

4.

\psi(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}(\log(|\xi|^{2}+1))^{\beta/2}
, where
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
0<\beta<2-\alpha
.

5.

\psi(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}(\log(|\xi|^{2}+1))^{-\beta/2}
, where
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
0<\beta<\alpha
.

More generally, let
X:=\{W_{S_{t}}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a subordinate Brownian motion, where
S_{t}
is a subordinator (an increasing Lévy process on
{\mathbb{R}}
) with Laplace exponent
\phi\in{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha/2}(\infty)
with
\alpha\in(1,2]
that is independent of a Brownian motion
W
. Then the characteristic exponent
\psi
of
X
takes the form
\psi(\xi)=\phi(|\xi|^{2})
, and in particular,
\psi\in{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha}(\infty)
. Therefore, Theorem
3.1
is applicable to a large class of subordinate Brownian motions.
For more details on subordinators, readers are referred to
[
17
]
.

Example 4.2
(Jump Diffusion)

Let
\psi_{1}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}+\psi(\xi)
, where
\psi
is the characteristic exponent in Example
4.1
.
This corresponds to the independent sum of a Brownian motion and a pure jump process. Clearly,
\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(\infty)
, so Theorem
3.1
is applicable.

Example 4.3
(Truncated Jump Process and Truncated Jump Diffusion)

Let
\nu
be the Lévy measure corresponding to a characteristic exponent
\psi
appearing in Example
4.1
. Define a new Lévy measure
\mu
by

\mu(\mathrm{d}y):=\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\cdot 1_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}.

The Lévy process with Lévy measure
\mu
corresponds to a truncated jump process (a process whose jump sizes are at most 1).
Since any Lévy measure is integrable away from the origin,
\nu-\mu
is a finite (non-negative) measure, and hence, Corollary
3.2
is applicable.
A similar discussion applies to truncated jump diffusions constructed from jump diffusions in Example
4.2
.
