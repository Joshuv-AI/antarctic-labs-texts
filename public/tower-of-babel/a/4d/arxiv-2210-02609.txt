Scattering theory and an index theorem on the radial part of SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
H. Inoue, S. Richard

Abstract
We present the spectral and scattering theory of the Casimir operator acting on the radial part of SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}) . After a suitable decomposition, these investigations consist in studying a family of differential operators acting on the half-line. For these operators, explicit expressions can be found for the resolvent, for the spectral density, and for the Moeller wave operators, in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function. An index theorem is also introduced and discussed. The resulting equality links various asymptotic behaviors of the hypergeometric function.

Introduction

In this paper, we provide a self-contained presentation of the spectral and scattering theory for the family of differential expressions

D_{\mu,\nu}:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\left(\mu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{1}{\sinh(x)^{2}\cosh(x)^{2}}+(\mu^{2}-\nu^{2})\frac{1}{\cosh(x)^{2}},

(1.1)

with
\mu,\nu\geq 0
.
These operators appear naturally via the Cartan decomposition of the Casimir operator of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
acting on
L^{2}\big(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\big)
, see
[
33
, Sect. 8.1]
and Appendix
A
.
Self-adjoint realizations of these operators in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
are obtained by prescribing suitable boundary conditions at
x=0
.
The analysis of (
1.1
) relies on properties of the Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
, and several asymptotic behaviors of this function are involved in our study.
An index theorem, linking some of these asymptotic expressions, is also provided. Up to the best of our knowledge, and despite a huge literature on the Gauss hypergeometric function, it seems that this index theorem went unnoticed so far.

This work can be approached from different angles:

1.

The integrable models’ perspective: The operator (
1.1
) is one member of a larger family of solvable models, as presented for example in
[
7
]
. For this model, we provide the full spectral and scattering theory.

2.

The special functions’ perspective: The analysis is heavily relying on Gauss hypergeometric functions, and several asymptotic behaviors of these functions are recast in the framework of scattering theory. For instance, the uniform asymptotic expansion of these functions with respect to large parameters, as recently achieved in
[
19
]
, is playing a central role in our work.

3.

The Levinson’s perspective: For the last 15 years, it has been shown that an equality discovered by N. Levinson in
[
23
]
and relating the number of eigenvalues of a differential equation to its scattering part, corresponds to an index theorem in scattering theory
[
26
]
. For self-adjoint
realizations of (
1.1
), the equality between the number of eigenvalues and a suitable winding number can be computed explicitly, see Section
7
.

4.

The Lie groups’ perspective: The family of operators (
1.1
) is obtained by reduction of the Casimir operator
acting on
L^{2}\big(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\big)
to some invariant subspaces. Thus, this work also corresponds to the first attempt to consider Levinson’s theorem in the framework
of semisimple Lie group, a research topic triggered by discussions with N. Higson, see also
[
8
]
.

Clearly, none of these subjects is new, but combining all of them together seems unique.
For example, scattering theory on symmetric spaces has been developed by several authors, see for example
[
12
,
29
,
30
]
and references therein.
In the physics literature, we also mention
[
17
,
18
]
in which a link is established between
scattering theory and representation theory of semisimple Lie group.
Among a huge literature linking representation theory and special functions, let us just mention
[
20
]
which has been a source of inspiration for our investigations.
Finally, the role of
C^{*}
-algebras for a topological version of Levinson’s theorem has been initially exhibited in
[
14
,
15
]
and a review presentation is provided in
[
26
]
.

Let us now describe the content of this paper.
In Section
2
we introduce more precisely the differential expressions
D_{\mu,\nu}
and endow them with a boundary condition such that they become self-adjoint
operators
H_{\mu,\nu}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
The study of the equation
D_{\mu,\nu}f=-\zeta^{2}f
for
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
with
\Re(\zeta)>0
is carried out in Section
3
. The solutions of this equation involve
Gauss hypergeometric functions. Based on these solutions, the resolvent

R_{\mu,\nu}(-\zeta^{2}):=(H_{\mu,\nu}+\zeta^{2})^{-1}

of the operator
H_{\mu,\nu}
is computed and information about the point spectrum of

H_{\mu,\nu}
is provided.
In Section
4
, we show the existence of boundary values
R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i0)
for
k>0
as bounded operators between appropriate weighted Hilbert spaces.
Such a result is often referred to as a limiting absorption principle in spectral theory.
In terms of the boundary values, one then infers that the spectral density

p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\big(R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}+i0)-R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}-i0)\big),\qquad k>0

is well-defined and the spectrum of
H_{\mu,\nu}
is purely absolutely continuous
on
(0,\infty)
.

The content of the previous three sections corresponds to spectral information, let us move
to scattering results.
In Section
5
we introduce the generalized Fourier kernels
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)
,
for
x,k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and study some of their properties.
These kernels are expressed in terms of the hypergeometric function together with some normalizing factors. Various asymptotic behaviors of these kernels are either computed or recalled.
These kernels define the generalized Fourier transforms
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}
studied in Section
6
.
Note that these operators can be used for proving Plancherel theorem, as exhibited in

[
20
, Thm. 2.3 & Thm. 2.4]
. In our framework, the operators
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}

are needed for defining the Møller wave operators

W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D}):=\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}
,
where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{D}}
denotes the sine transform in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
These operators provides an intertwining relation between the two operators
H_{\mu,\nu}
and
the Dirichlet Laplacian
H_{\rm D}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, namely

W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})H_{\rm D}=H_{\mu,\nu}W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D}).

The equality of these wave operators with their time dependent analog is proved.
As a by-product of this construction, the scattering operator
S_{\mu,\nu}
is also defined.
This unitary operator involves the product of four gamma functions and their complex conjugates.

The last section is more of a
C^{*}
-algebraic nature. We introduce a
C^{*}
-algebra
related to the wave operators, and show how this algebraic formalism leads us to the definition
of a continuous function defined on the edges of a square. This function is unitary-valued, and contains the scattering operator, among other contributions. The explicit computation of its winding number and its equality with the eigenvalues of
H_{\mu,\nu}
correspond to the new index theorem.
However, due to the complicated asymptotic behaviors of the hypergeometric function, one key but technical result has not been obtained, see Conjecture
7.1
. Fortunately, it does not impact any other result provided in this paper (even the index theorem), but it would certainly be more satisfactory to prove this affiliation statement. Nevertheless, the scattering theory part, the
C^{*}
-algebraic framework, and the properties of the hypergeometric function, complement and stimulate each other.

In two appendices, we finally provide a few additional computations. The first one is related to the reduction of the Casimir operator to the differential expression (
1.1
) in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
The second one is a detailed computation for relating the differential equation
D_{\mu,\nu}f=-\zeta^{2}f
to the hypergeometric equation
(
3.2
). Through this computation, the hypergeometric functions appear naturally in our investigations.

This work is centered on the Lie group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
It would be of great interest to further extend these investigations to other semisimple Lie groups.
Also, from the point of view of representation theory, the link between the intertwining operators, introduced in
[
21
]
and further studied in
[
28
]
for
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
,
and our generalized Fourier transforms
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}
should be further investigated.
We hope that this initial work will trigger further projects at the interplay between group theory,
special functions,
C^{*}
-algebras, and scattering theory.

The self-adjoint realization

D_{\mu,\nu}:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V_{\mu,\nu}(X)

\dom(D_{\mu,\nu}):=C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

Here,
V_{\mu,\nu}(X)
denotes the multiplication operator by the function
V_{\mu,\nu}
.
We also consider the auxiliary operator

defined by

D_{\mu}:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V_{\mu}(X),

(2.1)

with
\dom(D_{\mu}):=C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and with
V_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
given
for

V_{\mu}(x):=\left(\mu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{1}{x^{2}}.

Since
V_{\mu,\nu}-V_{\mu}
corresponds to a bounded function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
there exists a one-to-one correspondence
between closed extensions of
D_{\mu,\nu}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and closed extensions
of
D_{\mu}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
In particular, the same one-to-one correspondence holds for self-adjoint extensions.

In
[
5
]
, closed extensions of
D_{\mu}
have been extensively studied,
for any
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(\mu)>-1
. These investigations led to two families of closed operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, each of them corresponding to a specific boundary condition
at
0
.
Clearly, similar families of operators could be defined for
D_{\mu,\nu}
, and the full
program of
[
5
]
could be repeated in the current framework.
However, since our goal is different, we shall consider here only one distinguished
self-adjoint realization. We recall its construction for the auxiliary operator
D_{\mu}
,
since the addition of
V_{\mu,\nu}(X)-V_{\mu}(X)
does not change the domain of the operator.

For
\mu\geq 0
the self-adjoint extension
H_{\mu}
of
D_{\mu}
is constructed as follows:
Let
D_{\mu}^{\min}
denote the minimal operator associated with
D_{\mu}
,
namely the closure of
D_{\mu}
, and let
D_{\mu}^{\max}
be the maximal operator, with domain

\displaystyle\dom(D_{\mu}^{\max}):=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\mid D_{\mu}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\Big\}.

These operators satisfy
\big(D_{\mu}^{\min}\big)^{*}=D_{\mu}^{\max}
.
For
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
, we say that
g(x)\in\dom(D_{\mu}^{\min})
near
0
if there exists
\chi\in C_{\rm c}^{\infty}\big([0,\infty)\big)
with
\chi(0)=1
such that
\chi g\in\dom(D_{\mu}^{\min})
. The operator
H_{\mu}
is then defined as the restriction of
D_{\mu}^{\max}
to

\dom(H_{\mu}):=\left\{f\in\dom(D_{\mu}^{\max})\mid\exists c\in\mathbb{C}\ \text{s.t.}\ f(x)-cx^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\in\dom(D_{\mu}^{\min})\ \text{near}\ 0\right\}.

(2.2)

The resulting operator
H_{\mu}
is self-adjoint, and corresponds in
[
5
]
to the operator

H_{\mu,0}
, and to
H_{0}^{\infty}
in the special case
\mu=0
,
see also
[
2
]
for an earlier construction in line with our notations.
Note also that
H_{\frac{1}{2}}=:H_{\rm D}
coincides with the Dirichlet Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Based on this construction, we now define the self-adjoint operator of interest, namely

H_{\mu,\nu}
. This operator can either be constructed as the operator
H_{\mu}
, or defined by setting

H_{\mu,\nu}:=H_{\mu}+V_{\mu,\nu}(X)-V_{\mu}(X)

with domain
\dom(H_{\mu,\nu}):=\dom(H_{\mu})
.
In particular, since
\dom(D_{\mu,\nu}^{\max})=\dom(D_{\mu}^{\max})

and
\dom(D_{\mu,\nu}^{\min})=\dom(D_{\mu}^{\min})
, it follows that the elements of
\dom(H_{\mu,\nu})
behave near
0
as prescribed in (
2.2
).

Lemma 3.1

(Resolvent)
.

For fixed
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
with
\Re(\zeta)>0

We denote by

Proposition 3.2

(Number of bound states)
.

The number of eigenvalues of

is given by

\displaystyle\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{\mu,\nu})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad 1+\mu>\nu,\\
\left\lceil\frac{\nu-\mu-1}{2}\right\rceil&\text{if}\quad 1+\mu\leq\nu,\end{cases}

(3.12)

where
\lceil\cdot\rceil

Proof.

Since
H_{\mu,\nu}
is self-adjoint, its eigenvalues are real. By the limiting absorption principle provided in the next section, we shall infer that the possible eigenvalues are all located in
(-\infty,0]

In
(-\infty,0)

One infers that they correspond to the simple zeros of the function
\zeta\mapsto W_{\mu,\nu}(\zeta)
.
Hence,
-\zeta^{2}
is an eigenvalue of
H_{\mu,\nu}
if
\zeta>0
and
\beta+\zeta/2
belongs to
-\mathbb{N}

If
\beta=\frac{1+\mu-\nu}{2}>0
, then the previous condition is never satisfied.
If
\frac{1+\mu-\nu}{2}\leq 0
, then the previous condition is
satisfied whenever
\frac{\nu-\mu-1}{2}>n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
\zeta=0
, the above approach does not hold, and one has to look for
solutions of the equation
D^{\max}_{\mu,\nu}f=0
with
f\in\dom(H_{\mu,\nu})
.
The two functions
L_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,0)
and

M_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,0)
are solutions of
D^{\max}_{\mu,\nu}f=0
, but none of
them is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
For the first one, this follows from
[
1
, 15.3.10]
, while for the second
it follows from its asymptotic near
\infty
, as already mentioned before.
When
\beta=0
, these two functions are equal to
\tanh(\cdot)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}
,
and we need another linearly independent solution.
For that purpose, observe firstly that
\beta=0
implies that
\alpha=1+\mu
.
Thus, we end up considering the cases
7
(when
\mu\not\in\mathbb{N}
) and
21
(when
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
) of the list of solutions to the hypergeometric differential equation provided by
[
6
, Sec. 2.2.2]
.
In the first case, this leads to the second function

\displaystyle O_{\mu,\nu}(x,0)

\displaystyle:=z^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\mu}{2}}F(-\mu,1;1-\mu;z)=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mu}F\bigl(-\mu,1;1-\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\bigr)

while in the second case this leads to the second function

\displaystyle O_{\mu,\nu}(x,0)

\displaystyle:=z^{-\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\mu}{2}}F(1,1+\mu;2+\mu;z^{-1})=\tanh(x)^{-\frac{3}{2}-\mu}F\bigl(1,1+\mu;2+\mu;\tanh(x)^{-2}\bigr).

In the first case, when
\mu\not\in\mathbb{N}
, it again follows from
[
1
, 15.3.10]
that this function is not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
In the second case, when
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
, one directly observes that this function
is not in
L^{2}
near infinity.
As a consequence,
\zeta=0
is never an eigenvalue of
H_{\mu,\nu}
.
We then infer from the first paragraph that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{\mu,\nu})

\displaystyle=\left\{-\zeta^{2}\mid\zeta:=\nu-\mu-1-2n>0\hbox{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

which leads directly to (
3.12
).
∎

4
Limiting absorption principle and spectral density

We now look at the functions introduced in the previous section when the parameter
\zeta
approaches the line
i\mathbb{R}
in
\mathbb{C}
.
For that purpose, one first easily observes from the properties of the gamma function that
the function
\zeta\mapsto W_{\mu,\nu}(\zeta)
is analytic for
\Re(\zeta)>0
.
Similarly, for any fixed
x>0
the functions
\zeta\mapsto L_{\mu,\nu}(x,\zeta)
,

\zeta\mapsto M_{\mu,\nu}(x,\zeta)
, and

\zeta\mapsto N_{\mu,\nu}(x,\zeta)
are analytic in
\zeta
with
\Re(\zeta)>0
.
In addition, the boundary values of these functions exist, namely by setting
\zeta=\sqrt{-(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)}
with
k>0
and
\varepsilon>0
and by letting
\varepsilon\searrow 0
the expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(k)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}W_{\mu,\nu}\big(\sqrt{-(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}L_{\mu,\nu}\big(x,\sqrt{-(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}M_{\mu,\nu}\big(x,\sqrt{-(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}N_{\mu,\nu}\big(x,\sqrt{-(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)}\big)

are well defined for any
x>0
.
Note that we consider the principal branch of the square root, which means that
\Re(\sqrt{z})>0
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
(and for
z\in(-\infty,0]
we consider the limit from above). It turns out that the above expressions are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(1\mp ik)}{\Gamma(\alpha\mp ik/2)\Gamma(\beta\mp ik/2)},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{\pm ik}F\big(\alpha\mp ik/2,\beta\mp ik/2;1+\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{\mp ik}F\big(\alpha\pm ik/2,\beta\pm ik/2;1+\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{\pm ik}F\big(\alpha\mp ik/2,\beta\mp ik/2;1\mp ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mu}\cosh(x)^{\pm ik}F\big(1-\alpha\mp ik/2,1-\beta\mp ik/2;1\mp ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{\mp ik}F\big(\alpha\pm ik/2,\beta\pm ik/2;1\pm ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}-\mu}\cosh(x)^{\mp ik}F\big(1-\alpha\pm ik/2,1-\beta\pm ik/2;1\pm ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big).

It is then easily observed that

\mathcal{L}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,k)=\mathcal{L}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,k)=\overline{\mathcal{L}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,k)}

which means that
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,k)
correspond to the same real function
that we shall simply denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot,k)
.
One also easily infers that

\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=\overline{\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=\mathcal{N}^{\mp}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k).

(4.2)

For the Wronskian, the relation
\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(k)=\overline{\mathcal{W}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(k)}

holds, and one has
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(k)\neq 0
for any
k>0
.

With the functions introduced above we can look at the boundary value of the resolvent
of the operator
H_{\mu,\nu}
.
For the next statement we introduce for
s\geq 0
the weighted Hilbert space

L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):=\langle X\rangle^{-s}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
\langle x\rangle:=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
and

\langle X\rangle
the corresponding multiplication operator. We also set

L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
for its dual space, and recall that this space can be identified with
\langle X\rangle^{s}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Proposition 4.1

(Limiting absorption principle)
.

For
k>0
the boundary values of the resolvent

R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i0):=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)

exist in the sense of operators from
L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
to
L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

for any
s>\frac{1}{2}
,
uniformly in
k
on each compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In addition, their
kernels are given by

\displaystyle R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i0;x,y)=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(k)}\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)&\text{if}\ 0<x<y,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)&\text{if}\ 0<y<x.\end{cases}

(4.3)

Before the proof, let us recall that a direct consequence
of a limiting absorption principle is the local absolute continuity
of the spectrum of the underlying operator.
Thus, one infers from this statement that the operator
H_{\mu,\nu}

has purely absolutely continuous spectrum on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

For
k>0
and for
\varepsilon>0
let us consider the operator

\langle X\rangle^{-s}R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon)\langle X\rangle^{-s}
whose kernel is given by

\langle x\rangle^{-s}R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon;x,y)\langle y\rangle^{-s}.

(4.4)

If
\mu>0
one infers from (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\big|\langle x\rangle^{-s}R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon;x,y)\langle y\rangle^{-s}\big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mu,\nu}\bigl(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}\bigr)\big|W_{\mu,\nu}\bigl(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}\bigr)\big|^{-1}\langle x\rangle^{-s}\langle y\rangle^{-s}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\tanh(y)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Re(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon})|x-y|},

(4.5)

and similarly if
\mu=0
one infers from (
3.9
) that

\displaystyle\big|\langle x\rangle^{-s}R_{0,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon;x,y)\langle y\rangle^{-s}\big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{0,\nu}\bigl(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}\bigr)\big|W_{0,\nu}\bigl(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}\bigr)\big|^{-1}\langle x\rangle^{-s}\langle y\rangle^{-s}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\tanh(y)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\big(1+\big|\ln\bigl(\tanh(x)\bigr)\big|\big)\big(1+\big|\ln\bigl(\tanh(y)\bigr)\big|\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\Re(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon})|x-y|}.

Since
\big|\mathrm{e}^{-\Re(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon})|x-y|}\big|\leq 1
,
one deduces that these two kernels belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which means that the corresponding operators are Hilbert-Schmidt.

Let us now look at the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
.
One firstly observes that

\big|W_{\mu,\nu}\bigl(\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}\bigr)\big|^{-1}

has a limit as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, uniformly in
k
inside any compact subset
of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, see the expressions (

) and (

In order to eventually apply a Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem, one also has to study the behavior of the constant

as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
.
This easily reduces to investigating the two factors

F\big(\alpha-\zeta/2,\beta-\zeta/2;1+\mu;\tanh(\cdot)^{2}\big)\quad\hbox{and}\quad F\big(1-\alpha+\zeta/2,1-\beta+\zeta/2;1+\zeta;\cosh(\cdot)^{-2}\big).

(4.6)

For the first one, we infer from (
3.5

\displaystyle F\big(\alpha-\zeta/2,\beta-\zeta/2;1+\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(\zeta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\zeta/2)\Gamma(\beta+\zeta/2)}\big(1+O(\mathrm{e}^{-2x})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\cosh(x)^{-2\zeta}\frac{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(-\zeta)}{\Gamma(\alpha-\zeta/2)\Gamma(\beta-\zeta/2)}\big(1+O(\mathrm{e}^{-2x})\big)

as
x\to\infty
.
Note that for
\zeta=\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}
,
the expression for

can be chosen locally uniformly in

and independently of
\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon
small enough.
For the second factor in (

), one also infers from (

) that
for

one has

\displaystyle F\bigl(1-\alpha+\zeta/2,1-\beta+\zeta/2;1+\zeta;\cosh(x)^{-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\zeta)\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\zeta/2)\Gamma(\beta+\zeta/2)}\big(1+O(x^{2})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Gamma(1+\zeta)\Gamma(-\mu)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha+\zeta/2)\Gamma(1-\beta+\zeta/2)}\big(1+O(x^{2})\big)

as

.
Note that for
\zeta=\sqrt{-k^{2}\mp i\varepsilon}

the expression for

can be chosen locally uniformly in
k
and independently of
\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon
small enough.
As a consequence, one infers that for
\mu\not\in\mathbb{N}
, the constants

have limits as

, uniformly in

inside any compact subset
of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
For

, the same approach holds, by using the asymptotic expansion provided in
[

instead of formula (

In the special case

\frac{1}{1+\big|\ln\big(\tanh(x)\big)\big|}F\big((1+\nu+\zeta)/2,(1-\nu+\zeta)/2;1+\zeta;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)

As a consequence, one has

\displaystyle\big|\langle x\rangle^{-s}R_{0,\nu}(k^{2}\pm i\varepsilon;x,y)\langle y\rangle^{-s}\big|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}_{0,\nu}(k)\langle x\rangle^{-s}\langle y\rangle^{-s}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\tanh(y)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(1+\big|\ln\bigl(\tanh(x)\bigr)\big|\big)^{2}\big(1+\big|\ln\bigl(\tanh(y)\bigr)\big|\big)^{2},

(4.7)

where the constant

can be chosen locally uniformly in
k
and independently of
\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon
small enough. Note that the r.h.s. still belongs to

.

By summing up, the expression provided in (
4.4
)
belongs to

For

∎

Let us now set

\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k):=k\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(k)}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

and observe that for
0<x<y
one has

\displaystyle R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}+i0;x,y)-R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}-i0;x,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(k)}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(k)}\bigg(\mathcal{W}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(k)\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)-\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(k)\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{ik}\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\overline{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)},

where we have used for the last equality that

\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)=\frac{i}{2k}\bigg(\mathcal{W}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(k)\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)-\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(k)\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\bigg)

as a limiting case of (
3.6
) for
\zeta=\sqrt{-(k^{2}+i\varepsilon)}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, together with the relations (
4.2
).
Note that for
0<y<x
a similar computation leads to the expression

-\frac{2}{ik}\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\overline{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)}
.

By putting these results together one deduces the following statement:

Proposition 4.2

(Spectral density)
.

For
k>0
the spectral density

p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\big(R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}+i0)-R_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2}-i0)\big)

exists as a bounded operator from
L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
to
L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
for any
s>1/2
, and has kernel

p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2};x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi k}\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\overline{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)}=\frac{k}{\pi}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{\mu,\nu}^{+}(k)|^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k).

Proof.

The existence of the limit directly follows from Proposition
4.1
.
The expression for the kernel of this operator is a consequence of the previous
computations together with the equality

\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\overline{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)}=\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\overline{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)}

which can be easily checked.
∎

5
Generalized Fourier kernels

We now consider a slightly different factorization of the spectral density,
and recall several of its asymptotic behaviors. Consequences on scattering theory
will be provided in the following section.
For any
x,k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we define the generalized Fourier kernels by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}(x,k)

\displaystyle:=-2^{\pm ik}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{k}{\mathcal{W}_{\mu,\nu}^{\mp}(k)}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\pm ik}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}k\frac{\Gamma(\alpha\pm ik/2)\Gamma(\beta\pm ik/2)}{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(1\pm ik)}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{ik}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\beta-ik/2;1+\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\big)

(5.1)

and observe that the following relations hold:

2k\;\!p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2};x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\mathcal{F}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)=\mathcal{F}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)

and

\mathcal{F}^{+}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=\overline{\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)}.

As a consequence of the second relation, we shall mainly
concentrate on the expression for
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}
.

In order to understand the behavior of
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)
as
x\to\infty
, we
consider again the linear transformation formula (
3.5
), and infer that

\displaystyle\cosh(x)^{ik}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\beta-ik/2;1+\mu;\tanh(x)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(ik)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(\beta+ik/2)}\cosh(x)^{ik}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\beta-ik/2;1-ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(-ik)}{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)}\cosh(x)^{-ik}F\big(\alpha+ik/2,\beta+ik/2;1+ik,\cosh(x)^{-2}\big).

Motivated by this expression we also define

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k):=

\displaystyle-4^{-ik}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)\Gamma(ik)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(\beta+ik/2)\Gamma(-ik)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)\Gamma(1+ik/2)\Gamma(1/2+ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(\beta+ik/2)\Gamma(1-ik/2)\Gamma(1/2-ik/2)},

(5.2)

where the duplication formula
[
1
, 6.1.18]
has been used for
\Gamma(\pm ik)
.
Thus, by inserting these expressions in the definition of
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}
one gets

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}2^{-ik}\bigg\{4^{ik}\cosh(x)^{ik}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\beta-ik/2;1-ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\cosh(x)^{-ik}F\big(\alpha+ik/2,\beta+ik/2;1+ik,\cosh(x)^{-2}\big)\bigg\}.

By setting

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}(\mathrm{e}^{x}+\mathrm{e}^{-x})^{ik}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\beta-ik/2;1-ik;\cosh(x)^{-2}\big),

then the above expression simply reads

\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=-i\big\{\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)-\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)}\big\}.

(5.3)

From these various expressions it is now easy to deduce the asymptotic behaviors in

x
. The proof of the following statement is an easy application of the power series of hyperbolic functions, starting from (
5.3
) for the limit at
\infty
, and starting from
(
5
) for the limit at
0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any fixed
k>0
one has as
x\to\infty

\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{ikx}\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)-\mathrm{e}^{-ixk}\Big)+O(\mathrm{e}^{-2x}\big)

(5.4)

and as
x\searrow 0

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(x,k)=2^{-ik}k\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)}{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(1-ik)}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\big(1+O(x^{2})\big),

where both remainder terms are locally uniformly
k
-dependent.

Let us now mention another asymptotic of
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}

which is closer to the expansion of the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
in terms of Bessel functions, see
[
35

for the original result and also
[

In the framework of the analysis of noncompact semisimple Lie groups,

.
Namely, one infers from the latter reference that for any fixed

one has

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(\epsilon x,k/\epsilon)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(\mu-\frac{1}{2})}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(xk),

(5.5)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
denotes the Bessel function for dimension 1, as introduced in
[
5

and defined by

\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(x):=\sqrt{\frac{\pi x}{2}}J_{\mu}(x)

with

the usual Bessel function.
However, note that this expansion is only part of a more general result developed
by several authors over the last couple of decades, see for example
[

]

and references therein. In the next paragraph, we show that the leading term provided in
[

leads also to (

Let us firstly introduce another representation of
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{\mu,\nu}
​:
By taking the equality

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(x,k)=

\displaystyle 2^{-ik}k\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)}{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(1-ik)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\tanh(x)^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\cosh(x)^{2\alpha}F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\alpha+ik/2;1+\mu;-\sinh(x)^{2}\big).

Then, it follows from

\displaystyle F\big(\alpha-ik/2,\alpha+ik/2;1+\mu;-\sinh(x)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\mu)\Gamma(\alpha-\mu-ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)}\bigg(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\tanh(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\mu}\cosh(x)^{-2\alpha}I_{\mu}(-ikx)+\mathcal{O}\big(\Phi_{1}(-ik/2,2x)\big)\bigg)

where the identifications

\frac{1-z}{2}:=-\sinh(x)^{2}
, and
\zeta:=\ln\big(z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\big)=2x

have been taken into account.
In this expression,
I_{\mu}
denotes the modified Bessel function, and
\Phi_{1}(-ik/2,2x)

represents a remainder term with a precise decay property, see
[
19
, Eq. (3.7)]
. As a consequence, the main term in the expansion of

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(x,k)
reads

\displaystyle 2^{-ik}k\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\beta-ik/2)\Gamma(\alpha-\mu-ik/2)}{\Gamma(1-ik)}x^{\frac{1}{2}}I_{\mu}(-ikx)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}2^{-ik}\sqrt{k}\frac{B\big(\beta-ik/2,\alpha-\mu-ik/2\big)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi kx}{2}}I_{\mu}(-ikx)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(\mu-\frac{1}{2})}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\bigg[\sqrt{k}\frac{B\big(\beta-ik/2,\alpha-\mu-ik/2\big)}{\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\;\!2^{ik}\;\!\sqrt{2\pi}}\bigg]\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(kx),

where
B(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Beta function, see
[
1
, 6.2.2]
.
If one sets

b(k):=\sqrt{k}\frac{B\big(\beta-ik/2,\alpha-\mu-ik/2\big)}{\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\;\!2^{ik}\;\!\sqrt{2\pi}}

and takes the asymptotic expansion of the Beta function into account, one easily gets that

\lim_{k\to\infty}b(k)=1
.

If we summarize this finding and add another similar result one gets:

Lemma 5.2
.

For any fixed
x,k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
one has

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(\epsilon x,k/\epsilon)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(\mu-\frac{1}{2})}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(xk),

and

\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(\epsilon x,k/\epsilon)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{ikx}\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(0)-\mathrm{e}^{-ixk}\Big)

with

\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(0)=\begin{cases}-1&\hbox{ if }\beta\in-\mathbb{N},\\
1&\hbox{ if }\beta\not\in-\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

(5.6)

The second statement is a direct consequence of (
5.4
) together with
a careful analysis of the expression for
\sigma_{\mu,\nu}
provided in (
5
).

6
Scattering theory

Motivated by the previous computations,
let us introduce the integral operators
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}
defined for any compactly supported
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
k>0
by

[\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}f](k):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}(x,k)}f(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\mp}(x,k)f(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

with
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}(x,k)
provided in (
5
).
These transforms are often referred to as the
generalized Fourier transforms
.

Recall also that the spectral density has been introduced in Proposition
4.2
.
It then follows from Stone’s formula that for
0<a<b
the expression

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}(H_{\mu,\nu}):=\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{\sqrt{b}}p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2})\;\!\mathrm{d}(k^{2})=2\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{\sqrt{b}}p_{\mu,\nu}(k^{2})\;\!k\;\!\mathrm{d}k

exists and defines the spectral projection of the operator
H_{\mu,\nu}
on the interval
[a,b]
. The kernel of this operator is given for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}(H_{\mu,\nu})(x,y)=\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{\sqrt{b}}\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k)\mathcal{F}^{\mp}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}^{\pm}_{\mu,\nu}(x,k){\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}(k^{2})\mathcal{F}^{\mp}_{\mu,\nu}(y,k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k,

where
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}
denotes the characteristic function on
[a,b]
in the last expression.
Then, since the kernel of
\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}(x,k)
for
x,k>0
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}(x,k)
,
one deduces that

{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}(H_{\mu,\nu})=\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[a,b]}(X^{2})\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}.

(6.1)

We also infer from these relations that the equalities

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{[0,\infty]}(H_{\mu,\nu})={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{p}(H_{\mu,\nu})

(6.2)

hold, with
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}_{p}(H_{\mu,\nu})
the projection on the space spanned by the eigenfunctions of
H_{\mu,\nu}
.
Note that these relations were already mentioned (in a slightly different language) in
[
20
, Thm. 2.3 & Thm. 2.4]
.

We now recall the definition of the cosine and sine transforms
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, namely

\displaystyle[\mathcal{F}_{\rm N}f](k)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(kx)f(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}f](k)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(kx)f(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

Similarly, the Hankel transform is given by

[\mathcal{F}_{\mu}f](k):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(kx)f(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

These maps maps are firstly defined on
f\in C_{\rm c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, but are known to extend continuously to unitary maps in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Based on the definitions introduced so far, we can now define the Møller wave operators for the pair of operators
(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})
, namely

W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D}):=\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}\big)^{*}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}.

The first task is to show that this operator corresponds to the usual wave operators defined with the time dependent scattering theory, namely:

Proposition 6.1
.

The following equalities hold:

W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})=\mathop{\rm s\mathchar 45lim}\limits_{t\to\pm\infty}\mathrm{e}^{itH_{\mu,\nu}}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\rm D}},

where
\mathop{\rm s\mathchar 45lim}\limits
means the limit in the strong topology.

The following proof is inspired from the proof of
[
36
, Lem. 3.2]
.
Note that we only show the statement for
W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})
since the other
statement can be proved similarly.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}^{*}
, one easily observes that the statement holds
for
W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})
if

\lim_{t\to-\infty}\big\|\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\rm D}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}f-\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\mu,\nu}}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}f\big\|=0

(6.3)

is satisfied for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. By using the intertwining property of
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}
, which can be inferred from (
6.1
), one gets for
x>0

\big[\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\rm D}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}f-\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\mu,\nu}}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}f\big](x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sin(kx)-\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(x,k)\bigg]\mathrm{e}^{-itk^{2}}f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k.

(6.4)

Successive integrations by parts show that for any fixed
x
, the r.h.s. of (
6.4
) decays faster than any power of
|t|^{-1}
as
t\to-\infty
.
Then, by the asymptotic expansion provided in (
5.4
) one infers that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sin(kx)-\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{\pm}(x,k)\bigg]\mathrm{e}^{-itk^{2}}f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\mathrm{e}^{ikx}\big(\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)-1\big)+O(\mathrm{e}^{-2x})\Big]\mathrm{e}^{-itk^{2}}f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k

(6.6)

where the remainder term is locally uniformly
k
-dependent. Observe now that

\mathrm{e}^{i(kx-tk^{2})}=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial_{k}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i(kx-tk^{2})}}{x-2tk}\bigg)+i\frac{1}{x-2tk}\mathrm{e}^{i(kx-tk^{2})}\frac{2t}{x-2tk}.

Thus, one infers that (
6.6
) is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial_{k}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i(kx-tk^{2})}}{x-2tk}\bigg)\big(\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)-1\big)f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k

(6.7)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big[\frac{1}{x-2tk}\mathrm{e}^{i(kx-tk^{2})}\frac{2t}{x-2tk}+O(\mathrm{e}^{-2x})\Big]f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k.

(6.8)

By an integration by parts, it follows that
|
(
6.7
)
|
decays like
\frac{1}{x}
for
x\to\infty
, uniformly in
t<0
. Since
f
has a compact support away from
0
, one directly infers
that
|
(
6.8
)
|
is also decaying like
\frac{1}{x}
for
x\to\infty
, uniformly in
t<0
.
It follows that
|
(
6.5
)
|
is bounded by a function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
independent of
t
.
The statement in (
6.3
) follows then by an application of Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Based on the previous equality of the two definitions for
W_{\pm}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})
, we mention a standard result from scattering theory, namely:

Lemma 6.2
.

The following equality holds:

\mathop{\rm s\mathchar 45lim}\limits_{t\to-\infty}\mathrm{e}^{itH_{\rm D}}\;\!W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\;\!\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\rm D}}=1

while

\mathop{\rm s\mathchar 45lim}\limits_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{itH_{\rm D}}\;\!W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\;\!\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{\rm D}}=S_{\mu,\nu}

where
S_{\mu,\nu}:=W_{+}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})^{*}\;\!W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})

denotes the scattering operator.

Let us observe that the scattering operator mentioned in the previous statement can be expressed in terms of the function
\sigma_{\mu,\nu}
introduced in the previous section, namely

S_{\mu,\nu}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(K)\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}=\sigma_{\mu,\nu}\big(\sqrt{H_{\rm D}}\big)

(6.9)

with
\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(K)
the multiplication operator by the function

\sigma_{\mu,\nu}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
Indeed, one easily observes that

\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(x,k)=\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{+}(x,k)
for any
x,k>0
.
Then, from the definition of
S_{\mu,\nu}
one infers that

S_{\mu,\nu}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{+}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(K)\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(K)\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D},

where the first relation of (
6.2
) has been used for the last equality.
Note that the second equality in (
6.9
) corresponds to the diagonalization of the operator
H_{\rm D}
.

We still introduce another operator which frequently appears in the framework of scattering theory of Schrödinger operators. This operator will also play a central role in the next section.
Let
\{U_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}
denote the unitary group of dilations
acting on
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

as
\big(U_{\tau}f\big)(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\tau/2}f(\mathrm{e}^{\tau}x)
.
The self-adjoint generator of the strongly continuous dilation group is denoted by
A
.

It turns out that the conjugation of the wave operator by this group has a quite interesting feature. More precisely, one easily observes that the following equality holds:
U_{\tau}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!U_{\tau}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}
for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus one infers that

U_{-\tau}\;\!W_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\;\!U_{\tau}=U_{-\tau}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\;\!U_{-\tau}\;\!U_{\tau}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\;\!U_{\tau}=U_{-\tau}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\;\!U_{-\tau}\;\!\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}.

Finally, the action of the operator
U_{-\tau}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu^{-}}\big)^{*}\;\!U_{-\tau}
can be computed explicitly. Indeed, on any compactly supported
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and for
x>0

one has:

\displaystyle\big[U_{-\tau}\;\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\;\!U_{-\tau}f\big](x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau/2}\big[\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big)^{*}\;\!U_{-\tau}f\big](\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}x,k)[U_{-\tau}f](k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}x,k\big)f(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}^{-}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}x,\mathrm{e}^{\tau}k\big)f(k)\;\!\mathrm{d}k.

If we set
\epsilon:=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, one recognizes the kernel already
mentioned and studied in Lemma
5.2
.

Remark 6.3
.

Based on the above expression and on the content of Lemma
5.2
, we expect
the following convergences in a suitable topology (strong, weak, or even weaker) but we were
not able to prove them:

U_{-\tau}\;\!W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\;\!U_{\tau}\longrightarrow\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(\mu-\frac{1}{2})}\mathcal{F}_{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}\qquad\hbox{ as }\tau\to\infty

(6.10)

and

U_{-\tau}\;\!W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\;\!U_{\tau}\longrightarrow\begin{cases}i\mathcal{F}_{\rm N}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}&\hbox{ if }\beta\in-\mathbb{N},\\
1&\hbox{ if }\beta\not\in-\mathbb{N},\end{cases}\qquad\hbox{ as }\tau\to-\infty.

(6.11)

In fact, the main difficulty for getting these limits is coming from the remainder term

\Phi_{1}
appearing in the expansion provided in
[
19
, Thm. 3.1]

and already mentioned in the previous section.

Conjecture 7.1

For any

\displaystyle:=\varphi_{4}(0)-\varphi_{4}(+\infty).

Then, the full winding number is given by

\wn(\Lambda_{\mu,\nu})=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}+\omega_{4}

Clearly,
\omega_{4}=0
,
and also

\omega_{1}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}&\hbox{ if }\beta\in-\mathbb{N},\\
0&\hbox{ if }\beta\not\in-\mathbb{N},\end{cases}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\omega_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu-\frac{1}{2}\Big).

Note that the technique for the computation of
\omega_{3}
can be borrowed from

[
25
, Sec. III]
.

For the contribution of
\Lambda_{2}
, different cases have to be considered, depending on the value of the parameter
\beta
. First, by a slight adaptation of the proof of
[
13
, Lem. 4]
,
one infers that for
a,b>0
the function

[0,+\infty]\ni k\mapsto\frac{\Gamma(a-ik/2)\Gamma(b+ik/2)}{\Gamma(a+ik/2)\Gamma(b-ik/2)}\in\mathbb{T}

provides a partial signed contribution equal to
\frac{1}{2}(a-b)
.
As a consequence, for
\beta>0
one gets

\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\alpha+\beta-1-\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu-\frac{1}{2}\Big).

If
\beta=-n+\varepsilon
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
observe that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(-n+\varepsilon-ik/2)\Gamma(1+ik/2)\Gamma(1/2+ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(-n-\varepsilon+ik/2)\Gamma(1-ik/2)\Gamma(1/2-ik/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(\varepsilon-ik/2)\Gamma(1+ik/2)\Gamma(1/2+ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(\varepsilon+ik/2)\Gamma(1-ik/2)\Gamma(1/2-ik/2)}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{-\ell+\varepsilon+ik/2}{-\ell+\varepsilon-ik/2}.

As a consequence, one obtains

\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\alpha-1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\Big)+\frac{n}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu+n-\frac{1}{2}\Big)+\frac{n}{2}=n+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu-\frac{1}{2}\Big),

since
\beta=-n+\varepsilon\Longleftrightarrow\nu=\mu+1+2n-2\varepsilon
.
Finally, when
\beta=-n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\nu}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(-n-ik/2)\Gamma(1+ik/2)\Gamma(1/2+ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(-n+ik/2)\Gamma(1-ik/2)\Gamma(1/2-ik/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-ik/2)\Gamma(1/2+ik/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+ik/2)\Gamma(1/2-ik/2)}\prod_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{-\ell+ik/2}{-\ell-ik/2}

from which one infers that

\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\Big)+\frac{n}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu+n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)+\frac{n}{2}=n+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\Big).

By collecting the information obtained above, one finally gets

Theorem 7.2
.

For any
\mu,\nu\geq 0
one has

\wn(\Lambda_{\mu,\nu})=\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{\mu,\nu})=-\ind\big(W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\big),

where
\wn(\Lambda_{\mu,\nu})
denotes the winding number of the function
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}
, and
\ind\big(W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})\big)
the Fredholm index
of the wave operators
W_{-}(H_{\mu,\nu},H_{\rm D})
.

As already mentioned, this statement is independent of Conjecture
7.1
, since
its proof is based on an explicit computation.

Proof.

The proof of the first equality consists simply in comparing the result obtained in
Proposition
3.2
with the sum of the contributions

\omega_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. The three different cases for
\beta>0
,
\beta\in-\mathbb{N}
, and
\beta\leq 0
but
\beta\not\in-\mathbb{N}
have to be checked separately.
The second equality is a standard result of scattering theory.
∎

Appendix A
Reduction

In this appendix, we recall the decomposition of the Casimir operator of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
acting on
L^{2}\big(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\big)
. This decomposition leads to the operators (
1.1
).

Let
G
be the group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(G,\mathrm{d}g)
with
\mathrm{d}g
the Haar measure on
G
.
For
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
we say that a function
f:G\to\mathbb{C}
is a
spherical function of type
(m,n)
if
it satisfies

f(u_{\theta_{1}}\;\!g\;\!u_{\theta_{2}})=\mathrm{e}^{i(m\theta_{1}+n\theta_{2})}f(g)

(A.1)

for any
g\in G
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,2\pi)
, where

u_{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&\sin(\theta)\\
-\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\\
\end{pmatrix}\in K:=\mathrm{SO}(2).

We set
\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
for the subspace of
\mathscr{H}
consisting of functions of type
(m,n)
.
However, note that if
m-n\notin 2\mathbb{Z}
, then any
f\in\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
vanishes everywhere.
Indeed, in such a case one has for any
g\in G

f(g)=f(u_{\pi}\;\!g\;\!u_{\pi})=\mathrm{e}^{i(m+n)\pi}f(g)=(-1)^{m+n}f(g)=(-1)^{2n+(m-n)}f(g)=-f(g).

Also, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
let us set

a_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{x}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-x}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Then by considering
\theta_{1}=\pi/2
and
\theta_{2}=3\pi/2
in equality (
A.1
), one gets the relation

f(a_{-x})=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi i}{2}(m-n)}f(a_{x}),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the function
x\mapsto f(a_{x})
is even if
f\in\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
with
m-n\in 4\mathbb{Z}
, and odd otherwise.

We now recall the Cartan decomposition:
for any
g\in G\setminus K
there exist unique
x>0
,
\theta_{1}\in[0,2\pi)
and
\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi)

such that

g=u_{\theta_{1}}\;\!a_{x}\;\!u_{\theta_{2}}

and the corresponding expression for the Haar measure is

\mathrm{d}g=\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}.

We refer to
[
22
, Sec. VII.2]
and to
[
33
, Sec. 6.2 & 6.5]
for the details.
Then, we have the following inner orthogonal sum decomposition of
\mathscr{H}
:

\mathscr{H}=\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z},m-n\in 2\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{H}_{m,n}.

(A.2)

One easily observes that elements in
\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
are uniquely determined by their values on
A_{+}:=\{a_{x}\mid x>0\}
, and therefore we identify
\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}x)
through the unitary map
\mathcal{U}_{m,n}:\mathscr{H}_{m,n}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}x)
defined by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{m,n}f](x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{\sinh(2x)}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}f_{m,n}(a_{x})

for
f\in\mathscr{H}_{m,n}
and a.e.
x>0
, with

f_{m,n}(g):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{[0,2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-i(m\theta_{1}+n\theta_{2})}f(u_{\theta_{1}}\;\!g\;\!u_{\theta_{2}})\;\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2},\qquad\text{for a.e.~}g\in G.

Note that if
f\in C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(G)
, then
f_{m,n}
is a function of type
(m,n)
. In addition, the function
x\mapsto f_{m,n}(a_{x})
belongs to
C_{\rm c}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\right)
, and there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that

[\mathcal{U}_{m,n}f](x)=cx^{\frac{1}{2}}+o\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad x\searrow 0.

(A.3)

Let us now recall that the (normalized) Casimir operator
\Omega
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is an element in the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
defined by

\Omega=H^{2}+2XY+2YX+1

(A.4)

where

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(A.5)

see for example
[
33
, Sec. 5.1]
.
Since
\Omega
belongs to the centre of the universal enveloping algebra,
\Omega
can be realized as an essentially self-adjoint second order differential operator on
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(G)
with domain

\mathscr{H}^{\infty}:=\left\{f\in\mathscr{H}\mid G\ni g\mapsto f(g^{-1}\ \cdot)\in\mathscr{H}\ \text{is strongly smooth}\right\}

(A.6)

by passing through the differential representation of the left regular representation,
see
[
34
, Sec. 4.4.1 & 4.4.4]
.
Note that
C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(G)
is a subspace of
\mathscr{H}^{\infty}
.

Let us set
\mathsf{H}
for the self-adjoint extension of
-\Omega
.
In the coordinate
(\theta_{1},x,\theta_{2})
associated with the Cartan decomposition,
\mathsf{H}
is expressed as

-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\left[\frac{1}{\sinh(2x)^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{2}^{2}}\right)-2\frac{\cosh(2x)}{\sinh(2x)^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{1}\partial\theta_{2}}\right]-2\frac{\cosh(2x)}{\sinh(2x)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-1,

see
[
33
, Lem. 26, p190]
.
Then, the decomposition (
A.2
) reduces the operator
\mathsf{H}
and we have

\mathsf{H}\cong\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z},~m-n\in 2\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{H}_{m,n}.

(A.7)

By using the unitary transform
\mathcal{U}_{m,n}
one finally gets on
C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\mathcal{U}_{m,n}\;\!\mathsf{H}_{m,n}\;\!\mathcal{U}_{m,n}^{*}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V_{m,n}(X),

(A.8)

where
V_{m,n}
is given for any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

V_{m,n}(x)=\frac{m^{2}+n^{2}-1-2mn\cosh(2x)}{\sinh(2x)^{2}}.

By setting
\mu:=\frac{|m-n|}{2}
and
\nu:=\frac{|m+n|}{2}
, and using hyperbolic trigonometric identities one gets the operator
D_{\mu,\nu}
on
C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Remark A.1
.

Because of the above construction, let us observe that we could consider the operators
D_{\mu,\nu}
with
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{N}
only. However, for the analysis performed in this work, considering
\mu,\nu\in[0,\infty)
does not make the investigations more complicated.

Let us finally complement the information provided in Section
2
.
Recall that the operator
D_{\mu}
has been introduced in (
2.1
), with the domain
\dom(D_{\mu})=C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
In addition, for any
\mu\geq 0
, self-adjoint realizations of
D_{\mu}
and of
D_{\mu,\nu}

have also been provided in Section
2
.
Now, for
\mu\geq 1
, the operator
D_{\mu}
is already essentially self-adjoint, see
[
5
]
for more information on this operator. As a consequence of the relation between
D_{\mu,\nu}

and
D_{\mu}
, the same property holds for
D_{\mu,\nu}
.
Thus, according to Remark
A.1
, the only remaining tricky question is about the relation between the self-adjoint operator
\mathsf{H}_{m,m}
and the self-adjoint operator
H_{0,|m|}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. The next statement
shows that the self-adjoint extension for
D_{0}
(and therefore for
D_{0,|m|}
) introduced in
Section
2
is the correct one.
For that purpose, we recall from
[
5
, Sec. 2.3]
that all self-adjoint extensions of

are given by
H_{0}
and by the following one parameter family of self-adjoint opeators
\{H_{0}^{\kappa}\}_{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}}
with

\dom(H_{0}^{\kappa}):=\left\{f\in\dom(\mathsf{D}_{0}^{\max})\mid\exists c\in\mathbb{C}\ \text{s.t.}\ f(x)-cx^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\kappa+\ln(x)\right)\in\dom(\mathsf{D}_{0}^{\min})\ \text{near}\ 0\right\}.

(A.9)

Proposition A.2
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has

\mathcal{U}_{m,m}\;\!\dom(\mathsf{H}_{m,m})=\dom({H_{0,|m|}}).

Proof.

For proving the statement, we shall show that there exists
f\in\dom(\mathsf{H}_{m,m})
satisfying (
A.3
) with
c\neq 0
. In particular, this rules out any self-adjoint extension of
D_{0}
of the form (
A.9
).

By an abuse of notation, we keep writing
\Omega
for the extension of
\Omega\restriction_{C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(G)}
to the space
C^{\infty}(G)
.
Then, all eigenfunctions of
\Omega
are obtained as matrix elements of irreducible unitary representations of
G
.
Indeed, following
[
33
, Sec. 8.1]
let
\pi
be a irreducible unitary representation of
G
with infinitesimal character
\chi_{\pi}
, and let
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be an orthonormal basis of the space of
\pi
with
\pi(u_{\theta})e_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{in\theta}e_{n}
.
If
\chi_{\pi}(\Omega)=-\xi^{2}
, then
the function
u_{m,n}(\cdot,\xi):x\mapsto\langle e_{m},\pi(x)e_{n}\rangle
satisfies

\Omega\;\!u_{m,n}(\cdot,\xi)=-\xi^{2}\;\!u_{m,n}(\cdot,\xi)
.
Moreover,
u_{m}(\cdot,\xi):=u_{m,m}(\cdot,\xi)
is the unique eigenfunction of
\Omega
satisfying (
A.1
) with

and

.
Therefore, any eigenfunction of type
(m,m)
with eigenvalue

is proportional to

, see
[

, Thm. 8.2.3]
.
Note that an explicit expression for the eigenfunction

can be found for example in
[

and that this function is analytic over

.

Let us now consider a function
\chi\in C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\chi(0)=1
,
and define a spherical function

of type

for

(which defines

Then, for any fixed

with

by setting

.
One observes that

) with

.
This finishes the proof.

) by
