Solvable and non-solvable finite groups of the same order type
Paweł Piwek

Abstract
. We construct two finite groups of size 2 365 ⋅ 3 105 ⋅ 7 104 2^{365}\cdot 3^{105}\cdot 7^{104} : a solvable group G G and a non-solvable group H H , such that for every integer n n the groups have the same number of elements of order n n . This answers a question posed in 1987 by John G. Thompson.



1.
Introduction

The following meta-question has been of considerable interest
in the field of finite group theory.

Meta-question
.

Which properties of a group
G
can we discern from
knowing only the orders of its elements?

While some addressed this question by looking at
just the
set
of orders of elements of
G
–
see
[
11
]
,
we are interested in also using
the
multiplicity
of these orders,
which prompts the following definition.

Definition 1
.

For a group
G
we define its
order type
to be the function

o_{G}:n\mapsto|\{g\in G\ |\ g\text{ has order }n\}|.

It isn’t difficult to give examples
of an abelian group
G
and a non-abelian group
H

such that
o_{G}=o_{H}
.
Indeed, take
G=C_{3}\times C_{3}\times C_{3}

and
H
to be the Heisenberg group

(C_{3}\times C_{3})\rtimes C_{3}
,
which is non-abelian of exponent
3
.

On the other hand, the order type allows us to count
the number of elements of order
p^{\alpha}
for
p
a prime,
so it can also tell us if a
p
-Sylow subgroup is normal.
This implies that the order type can distinguish nilpotent groups
from non-nilpotent ones.
A similar argument can be given for super-solvable groups.

A problem posed in 1987 by John G. Thompson
in a private communication with Wujie Shi
(see
[
9
,
10
]
;
also Question 2.13 in
[
3
]
,
and Problem 12.37
in
[
5
]
)
is: which is the case for solvability?

Question
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group
and
H
be any finite group
such that
o_{G}=o_{H}
.
Is
H
necessarily solvable?

It was shown that the answer is positive
if we assume that the prime graph of
G
is disconnected
– see
[
9
]
,
or in the case where there are at most three different orders of elements
– see
[
8
]
.
Some works also showed results about solvability of groups
(and super-solvability, nilpotence and cyclicity too)
based on the average element order
– see
[
4
,
6
]
.

In this paper, we give a negative answer to Thompson’s question.
No claim is made
that the presented examples
are of minimal size.

.

G_{i}
and
H_{i}

Tables

2.
Proof of
Theorem

A

Before presenting the proof, let’s first rephrase the question slightly.

Definition 2
.

For a group
G
we define its
exponent type
to be the function

e_{G}:n\mapsto|\{g\in G\ |\ g^{n}=1\}|.

Lemma 1
.

For groups
G
and
H
we have:
o_{G}=o_{H}\iff e_{G}=e_{H}
.

Proof.

g^{n}=1
if and only if
\text{ord}(g)
divides
n
.
Thus
e_{G}(n)=\sum_{d|n}o_{G}(d)
,
and
o_{G}(n)=\sum_{d|n}e_{G}(n/d)\cdot\mu(d)
,
where
\mu
is the Möbius function.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
G
and
H
be any groups. Then
e_{G\times H}=e_{G}\cdot e_{H}
.

Proof.

For
(g,h)\in G\times H
we have
(g,h)^{n}=(g^{n},h^{n})
,
so
(g,h)^{n}
is trivial if and only if
g^{n}
and
h^{n}
are trivial.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

A
.

With the help of
MAGMA
computational package
[
2
]

we can check that—with the notable exception of
H_{16}
—all of the groups
G_{i}
and
H_{i}
are solvable.
This means that
G
is solvable as a product of solvable groups,
and
H
isn’t as it contains a non-solvable subgroup,
namely
H_{16}\cong\mathrm{GL}(3,2)
.

To prove
e_{G}=e_{H}
we need to compute
the exponent types
e_{G_{i}}
and
e_{H_{i}}
,
and check that
\prod e_{G_{i}}^{m_{i}}=\prod e_{H_{i}}^{n_{i}}
.

The exponent types of groups
G_{i}
and
H_{i}

were computed with the help of
MAGMA
too
(see the ancillary file
exponents_computation.m
)
and are listed in
Tables

2
and
4
.
Only the columns labelled with divisors of
168
were included
as
e_{G}(n)=e_{G}\left(\gcd(n,E_{G})\right)

for
E_{G}
being the exponent of the group
G
.
Since the products
\prod e_{G_{i}}(n)^{m_{i}}

and
\prod e_{H_{i}}(n)^{n_{i}}

become unmanageably large,
we factorise them into their prime factors
to compute the exponent types of
G
and
H

listed in
Tables

3
and
5
.
This computation is done in the ancillary file

exponents_verification.ipynb
.
∎

3.
How these groups were found

Finding groups
G
and
H
presented in
Theorem

A
manually
would be very difficult.
Instead, we employed a computer-based search method outlined below.

Step 1.

We used
MAGMA

to access the
Small Groups
database
and to we go through all groups of size at most 2000
excluding groups of size divisible by 128,
and to compute for each group

(1)

whether it is solvable—using
IsSolvable
function,

(2)

whether it is a non-trivial direct product—using a custom function
IsDirectProduct

(see the ancillary file
functions.m
),
which searches for two normal subgroups with trivial intersection
and whose sizes multiply to the size of the investigated group,

(3)

its order type—using a simple custom function
OrderType
.

Step 2.

We parsed the outputs into a
.csv
table
and handled the remaining tasks using various
Python
libraries.
Given our objective of constructing an example
through the direct product of groups,
we focused solely on those groups that were not direct products themselves.

Step 3.

We rephrased the question as follows.

(1)

First we converted the order types
o_{G}
to exponent types
e_{G}

as described in
Section

2
.

(2)

Then we used multiplicative Möbius inversion on
e_{G}

defining the
revolved exponent type

r_{G}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
as

r_{G}(n):=\prod_{d|n}e_{G}(n/d)^{\mu(d)}.

The advantage of doing so was that

r_{G}(n)=1

if
n\nmid E_{G}
.
Indeed, for
k\mid E_{G}
and
\gcd(m,E_{G})=1

we get the following.

\displaystyle r_{G}(mk)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d|mk}e_{G}(mk/d)^{\mu(d)}=\prod_{e|m}\prod_{f|k}e_{G}(m/e\cdot k/f)^{\mu(ef)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{e|m}\prod_{f|k}e_{G}(k/f)^{\mu(e)\mu(f)}=\prod_{e|m}\Big(\prod_{f|k}e_{G}(k/f)^{\mu(f)}\Big)^{\mu(e)}

\displaystyle=r_{G}(k)^{\sum_{e|m}\mu(e)}=\begin{cases}r_{G}(k)&\text{ if }m=1,\\
1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Additionally, we maintained the multiplicativity
with respect to direct products:

r_{G_{1}\times G_{2}}=r_{G_{1}}\cdot r_{G_{2}}
.
In order to operate with rational numbers
we used a Python datatype
Fraction
.

(3)

Finally, we factorised the revolved exponent types into their prime factors by defining

v_{G}(n,p)
to be the exponent of a prime
p

in the factorisation of
r_{G}(n)
into prime factors.
Thus, for
G=\prod G_{i}^{m_{i}}
we have

v_{G}(n,p)=\sum m_{i}\cdot v_{G_{i}}(n,p).

Additionally, for a fixed
G
,
the values of
v_{G}(n,p)
are zero for all but finitely many combinations
(n,p)
.

At this point the question became:
is there a positive rational linear combination

\sum k_{i}\cdot v_{N_{i}}

for non-solvable groups
N_{i}

in the rational span of
v_{S_{i}}

for
S_{i}
solvable groups?

Step 4.

An even simpler question,
although not equivalent, arises:
is any
v_{N}
for a non-solvable group
N

in the span of
v_{S_{i}}
for solvable groups
S_{i}
?
This question is equivalent to
determining whether the equation
Vx=v_{N}
has a solution,
where
V
is a matrix of
v_{S_{i}}(n,p)

with rows indexed by pairs
(n,p)

and columns indexed by solvable groups
S_{i}
.
A significant challenge lies in that the matrix
V

were in our case of size
9945\times 100972
,
albeit very sparse.

Step 5.
We first attempted solving this numerically
using Least Squares methods
of the
SciPy
library
[
12
]
,
which gave two conclusions.

(1)

For many of the non-solvable groups considered (e.g.
N=A_{5}
)
the (numerical) projection of their
v_{N}

onto the column space of
V

was at distance about
1
from
v_{N}
,
meaning that
v_{N}
was almost certainly not in the span.

(2)

For some of the groups (e.g. the
\mathrm{GL}(3,2)
) the algorithm
converged to a solution up to a small error of
10^{-6}
.
This lead us to search for an exact solution to
Vx=v_{N}

for these groups.

Step 6.
For
N=\mathrm{GL}(3,2)
we solved
Vx=v_{N}
exactly
using the symbolic computation library

SymPy

[
7
]
.
A family of solutions was found; we chose one of them.

Step 7.
We converted the solution back into the language of groups
and checked that it holds using more direct methods.
