Abstract
This paper studies sample average approximation (SAA) in solving convex or strongly convex stochastic programming (SP) problems. In estimating SAA’s sample efficiency, the state-of-the-art sample complexity bounds entail metric entropy terms (such as the logarithm of the feasible region’s covering number), which often grow polynomially with problem dimensionality. While it has been shown that metric entropy-free complexity rates are attainable under a uniform Lipschitz condition, such an assumption can be overly critical for many important SP problem settings. In response, this paper presents perhaps the first set of metric entropy-free sample complexity bounds for the SAA under standard SP assumptions—in the absence of the uniform Lipschitz condition. The new results often lead to an O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) -improvement in the complexity rate than the state-of-the-art. From the newly established complexity bounds, an important revelation is that SAA and the canonical stochastic mirror descent (SMD) method, two mainstream solution approaches to SP, entail almost identical rates of sample efficiency, lifting a theoretical discrepancy of SAA from SMD also by the order of O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) . Furthermore, this paper explores non-Lipschitzian scenarios where SAA maintains provable efficacy but the corresponding results for SMD remain mostly unexplored, indicating the potential of SAA’s better applicability in some irregular settings. Our numerical experiment results on SAA for solving a simulated SP problem align with our theoretical findings.

Introduction.

This paper is focused on a convex or strongly convex stochastic programming (SP) problem of the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\,F({\mathbf{x}}):={\mathbb{E}}[f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)],

(1)

where
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a non-empty, closed, and convex feasible region with integer
d
being the number of decision variables (a.k.a., dimensionality),
\xi
is a random vector of problem parameters whose probability distribution
P
is supported on
\Xi\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and the cost function
f:\,{\mathcal{X}}\times\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is deterministic and measurable. Throughout this paper, we assume that the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}\left[f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\right]=\int_{\Xi}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\,\text{d}P(\xi)
is well defined for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the population-level cost function
F
admits a finite minimizer
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
with a finite optimal cost, and
f(\cdot,\xi)
is lower semi-continuous for all
\xi\in\Xi
.
As formally stated in the latter part of the paper, the cost function
f(\cdot,\xi)
is further assumed to be convex and, in some cases, strongly convex. We also assume the existence of a deterministic and measurable function
G:{\mathcal{X}}\times\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)
for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and almost every
\xi\in\Xi
. Here,
\partial_{{\mathbf{x}}}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)
is the subdifferential of
f(\,\cdot\,,\xi)
at
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
for a given
\xi\in\Xi
.

The SP problem above has been widely applied and much discussed
(e.g., by
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Birge 1997
,
Birge and Louveaux 2011
,
Ruszczyński and Shapiro 2003
,
Lan 2020
, to name only a few)
. Particularly, it has extensive connections with many statistical and machine learning problems
(
Bartlett et al. 2006
,
Liu et al. 2019
, as per, e.g.,)
. Indeed, the suboptimality gap in solving (
1

This paper revisits one of the most traditional but popular solution methods for SP called
sample average approximation
(SAA). Following its literature
(
Dupacová and Wets 1988
,
Ruszczyński and Shapiro 2003
,
Kleywegt et al. 2002
,
Shapiro et al. 2021
,

,

, we particularly focus on both the canonical formulation of SAA and one of its simple, regularized variations:

(i)

Formulation 1:
In particular, the canonical SAA is as below:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\,F_{N}({\mathbf{x}}):=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}:=(\xi_{j}:\,j=1,...,N)
is an i.i.d. random sample of
\xi
. Our analysis on this formulation is centered around its effectiveness for strongly convex SP problems. Here, positive integer
N
is the sample size.

(ii)

Formulation 2:
On top of (
2
), we also consider the SAA variation that incorporates a Tikhonov-like regularization in the following:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\,F_{\lambda_{0},N}({\mathbf{x}}):=F_{N}({\mathbf{x}})+\lambda_{0}V_{q^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}),

(3)

where
\lambda_{0}\geq 0
is a tuning parameter, and
V_{q^{\prime}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, given a user’s choice of
q^{\prime}
-norm (with
q^{\prime}\in(1,2]

\displaystyle V_{q^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{0}\|_{q^{\prime}}^{2},

(4)

for any initial guess
{\mathbf{x}}^{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
(though many of our results can be easily extended to any choice of
{\mathbf{x}}^{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
). Particularly in the case of
q^{\prime}=2
and
{\mathbf{x}}^{0}=\mathbf{0}
, we have
V_{q^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})=0.5\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}
, which becomes the canonical Tikhonov regularization
(
Tikhonov et al. 1995
,
Golub et al. 1999
)
commonly employed in ridge regression
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
)
. The same type of regularization approach has been discussed in the SAA theories for (non-strongly) convex SP, among others, by
Hu et al. 2020
,
Feldman and Vondrak 2019
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2009
and
Lei and Ying 2020
. Similarly in this paper, we also study SAA (
3
) in convex SP problems. Because
V_{q^{\prime}}
is
(q^{\prime}-1)
-strongly convex w.r.t. the
q^{\prime}
-norm, additionally incorporating this term as in (
3

would not add significant computational burden to solving the SAA formulation.

Throughout this paper, we refer to both formulations (
2
) and (
3
) as SAA; or, if there is ambiguity, “SAA (
2
)” and “SAA (
3
)”, respectively. Both versions of SAA avoid the multi-dimensional integral involved in (
1

(
Shapiro et al. 2021
)
, often leading to a substantial improvement in tractability.

Hereafter, for a given sample
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}
and given scalars
q\geq 1
and
\delta>0
, we refer to the random vector
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}:=\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})\in{\mathcal{X}}
as a
(\delta,q)
-approximate solution
to SAA (
2
) (or SAA (
3
)) if
\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}:\,\Xi^{N}\rightarrow{\mathcal{X}}
is a deterministic and measurable function
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{g},\,{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})\right\rangle\geq-\delta\cdot\left\|{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})\right\|_{q},~~\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}},

(5)

for some
{\mathbf{g}}\in\partial F_{N}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}))
(or
{\mathbf{g}}\in\partial F_{N,\lambda_{0}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}))
, respectively). Clearly, under convexity,
\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})
must also satisfy the below for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F_{N}({\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}))\geq-\delta\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})\|_{q},&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA}};\\
F_{N,\lambda_{0}}({\mathbf{x}})-F_{N,\lambda_{0}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}))\geq-\delta\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})\|_{q},&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA-ell2}}.\end{cases}

(6)

By (
6
), if
\delta=0
, then
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}:=\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N})
becomes an exact optimal solution to SAA, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{opt}
hereafter.

The existence of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
can be inferred by current results for the measurability of exact optimal solutions to SAA
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Rockafellar and Wets 2009
,
Krätschmer 2023
, e.g., by)
. Meanwhile, the quality of solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
in approximating a solution to the genuine SP problem in (
1
) has also been much studied
(
Artstein and Wets 1995
,
Dupacová and Wets 1988
,
King and Rockafellar 1993
,
King and Wets 1991
,
Pflug 1995
,
Pflug 1999
,
Pflug 2003
,
Shapiro 1989
,
Shapiro 1993
,
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Liu et al. 2016
,
Liu et al. 2022
, e.g., by)
. Following many works in this body of literature, this current paper is focused on SAA’s (non-asymptotic) sample complexity; namely, how large the sample size
N
should be in order to ensure that
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
approximates an optimal solution
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
to (
1
), meeting the user-prescribed thresholds for accuracy and probability. Although this is a well-visited topic, existing sample complexity bounds commonly carry an undesirable term of metric entropy, such as the example below.

Typical non-asymptotic sample complexity under light tails
(when results, e.g., by
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
, are applied to our settings)
:

Under the
Lipschitz assumption that, for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and every
\xi\in\Xi
,

\displaystyle|f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-f({\mathbf{y}},\xi)|\leq M(\xi)\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{q},

(7)

where
M:\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is some deterministic and measurable function and
\|\cdot\|_{q}
is the
q
-norm (
q\geq 1
), the optimal solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{opt}
to SAA (
2
) satisfies the following: For any given
\epsilon>0,\,\,\beta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{opt})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\leq\epsilon\right]\geq 1-\beta,~~\text{if }N\geq O\left(\frac{\varphi^{2}\mathcal{D}_{q}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left[\Gamma_{\epsilon}({\mathcal{X}})+\ln\frac{1}{\beta}\right]\right),

(8)

Here,
\Gamma_{\epsilon}({\mathcal{X}})
is the logarithm of the covering number of
{\mathcal{X}}
—one common form of the said metric entropy,
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is the
q
-norm diameter of
{\mathcal{X}}
, and
\varphi
is the parameter related to the sub-Gaussian distribution assumed for
M(\xi)
.

Other than the logarithm of covering number above, in some other existing works, metric entropy may be calculated in different ways, including the “generic chaining”-based complexity measures as in the analysis by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
. Regardless of how the metric entropy is quantified, it generally causes the sample complexity bounds to exhibit an elevated dependence on problem dimensionality
d
. For example, because the covering number grows exponentially with
d
in general, the logarithm of covering number
\Gamma_{\epsilon}({\mathcal{X}})
is then polynomial in
d
, leading to the following more explicit representation of the complexity rate than (
8
):

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\varphi^{2}\mathcal{D}_{q}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left[d\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}_{q}\varphi}{\epsilon}\right)+\ln\frac{1}{\beta}\right]\right).

(9)

Changing ways to account for the metric entropy, such as via the said “generic chaining”-based argument, does not avoid the aforementioned polynomial growth rate with
d
, except when some special structural assumptions hold (e.g., the feasible region is a simplex). While uniform stability arguments for SAA have led to sample bounds that are free of metric entropy terms, these arguments rely on a uniform Lipschitz condition, which is often overly critical for many SP problems. (See Section
2
for more detailed discussions).

The influence of metric entropy such as in (
8
) (and thus in (
9
)) seems particularly overt when SAA is compared with the stochastic mirror descent (SMD) methods (aka, the stochastic approximation or the stochastic first-order methods) as discussed, e.g., by
Nemirovski et al. 2009
,
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
, and
Lan 2020
. SMD is a mainstream alternative to SAA in solving an SP problem. For a convex or strongly convex SP problem under comparable, and sometimes weaker, assumptions than those by the existing SAA theories, SMD can often achieve metric entropy-free sample complexity rates. As a consequence, comparisons between the current theories for both methods would suggest a significant performance gap in sample efficiency—SMD would be substantially more efficient than SAA by a margin of order
O(d)
. With such a stark contrast, one would expect SMD to exhibit a significantly better solution quality than SAA in practice, when both methods are operating under the same sample size constraints. Yet, this theoretical discrepancy, though being persistent in the literature, seems to have never been confirmed in empirical studies. On the contrary, in many reported experiments
(
Nemirovski et al. 2009
, such as those by)
, SAA achieves comparable, if not better, solution accuracy than SMD, given the same sample size. The aforementioned inconsistency between the theoretical predictions and numerical results underscores a critical literature gap, to which this paper seeks to respond by studying a directly related question:

The open question of our focus:

Under typical assumptions in the SP literature, does SAA, or its simple variations, admit sample complexity bounds that are completely free from any quantification of metric entropy?

As mentioned, while this question has been studied under a uniform Lipschitz condition
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2009
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
,
Feldman and Vondrak 2018
,
Feldman and Vondrak 2019
, e.g., by)
, this assumption tends to be overly critical for many important SP formulations such as stochastic linear programming with Gaussian cost coefficients. (See Section
2
for more discussions). In a standard SP setting—which is typically in the absence of the uniform Lipschitz condition or its comparable variations—the said research question remains largely open. In response, this current paper presents perhaps the first affirmative answer through the following three sets of results on
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
with some properly small
\delta\geq 0
as defined in (
5
):

(a)

We show, perhaps for the first time, that SAA’s sample complexity matches exactly with that of the canonical SMD
(
Nemirovski et al. 2009
,
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
,
Lan 2020
)
, under comparable assumptions commonly imposed for the latter. More specifically, when the subgradient
of
f(\cdot,\xi)
admits a bounded variance of no more than
\sigma_{p}^{2}
—thus the underlying randomness is heavy-tailed—and the population-level objective function

comprises of an

-smooth term and an

-Lipschitz term, we prove in Theorems

that, for any given

\mathbb{E}[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})]\leq\epsilon,\\
\text{if~}N\geq\begin{cases}O\left(\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\mu},~\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu\cdot\epsilon}\right\}\right)&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA} in $\mu$-strongly convex SP};\\
O\left(\frac{V_{q^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})}{q^{\prime}-1}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\epsilon},~\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\}\right)&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA-ell2} in (non-strongly) convex SP},\end{cases}

(10)

where we recall the definition of
V_{q^{\prime}}
in (
4

This set of results are identical to the best-known sample complexity bounds for the conventional SMD, providing perhaps the first theoretical explanation on the comparable empirical performance between SAA and SMD. Furthermore, (
10

Oliveira and Thompson 2023
. In particular, with the aforementioned assumption on the bounded-ness of variance, our results apply to the “most heavy-tailed” scenario considered by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023

8

10

d

\mathcal{M}

(b)

In Theorem
3.3
of Section
3.2

Suppose that (
7

therein is sub-Gaussian with a parameter
\varphi>0

\epsilon\in(0,1]

\beta\in(0,1)

to SAA (

{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\leq\epsilon\right]\geq 1-\beta,~~\text{if~}N\geq O\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}_{q^{\prime}}^{2}\cdot\varphi^{2}}{(q^{\prime}-1)\cdot\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\min\left\{\ln^{3}\frac{\Phi}{\beta},~~\ln\left(d\Phi\ln\frac{e}{\beta}\right)\cdot\ln^{2}\frac{\Phi}{\beta}\right\}\right),

where

is the
q^{\prime}

(c)

In Theorems

We would like to re-iterate that all our theoretical results share the advantage of being independent from any form of metric entropy, implying the SAA’s innate, SMD-comparable dimension-insensitivity. Under the common assumptions of SP literature (which typically are beyond the uniform Lipschitz condition), the same level of dimension-insensitivity, to our knowledge, has not been uncovered thus far.

While

Our observations in numerical experiments with synthetic data in Section

and
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy 2021
. Their results can also provide metric entropy-free complexity bounds for SAA under the stipulation of more critical conditions. More specifically, through the argument of uniform stability or its variations,
it has been proven
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2009
,
Hu et al. 2020
, e.g., by)
that an optimal solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{opt}
to SAA satisfies the below:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{opt})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right]\leq\epsilon,~~\text{if }~N\geq\begin{cases}O(\frac{M}{\mu\epsilon})&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA} in $\mu$-strongly convex SP};\\
\\
O(\frac{MV_{q^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})}{\epsilon^{2}})&\text{for SAA \eqref{Eq: SAA-ell2} in convex SP,}\end{cases}

where
V_{q^{\prime}}(\cdot)
is defined as in (
4
) with
{\mathbf{x}}^{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
when the following, what we call,
uniform Lipschitz condition
holds:
For all
{\mathbf{x}},\,{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\xi\in\Xi
, there exists some
\xi
-invariant constant
M>0
such that, for some
q\geq q^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle|f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-f({\mathbf{y}},\xi)|\leq M\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{q}.

(14)

Furthermore, high probability bounds that are logarithmic in
1/\beta
are also obtained under the same condition
(e.g., by
Feldman and Vondrak 2018
,
Feldman and Vondrak 2019
,
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
,
Feldman and Vondrak 2018
,
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy 2021
, when their results are applied to the analysis of (
2
) or (
3
))
.
Nonetheless, the uniform Lipschitz condition in (
14
) can be overly critical for many applications of the SP; because
M
is
\xi
-invariant, this quantity can be undesirably large and even unbounded under the more common conditions, such as (
7
), in the SAA literature. To see this, one may consider a simple stochastic linear program of
\min\{{\mathbb{E}}[\alpha^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}]:\,{\mathbf{x}}\in[-1,1]^{d}\}
, where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is some Gaussian random vector. While many applications can be subsumed by simple variations of this SP problem, it hardly satisfies (
14
) for a finite
M
. One may easily extend this linear program to identify typical SP problems where (
14
) fails to apply. Indeed, a closer examination can reveal that (
14
) is an arguably restrictive special case of (
7
) when
M(\xi)
in the latter is assumed with a bounded support set. In contrast, our results are based on more flexible conditions common to the SP literature and subsume the said stochastic linear programming problem. Moreover, our results do not assume a bounded support set for
M(\xi)
.

Some recent works on SAA for high-dimensional SP study the implications of special problem structures in reducing the growth rate of the metric entropy terms w.r.t. their dependence on
d
. Along this direction,
Liu et al. 2022
,
Liu et al. 2019
and
Lee et al. 2023
study the implications of sparsity and low-rankness.
Lam and Li 2022
consider the influence of low Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimensions.
Bugg and Aswani 2021
investigate the dimension-independent budget constraints. As is also discussed by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
, when the feasible region is (representable by) a simplex, the generic chaining-based metric entropy exhibits a logarithmic growth rate with
d
. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, those results may not apply beyond the corresponding special structural assumptions.

Birge 2023
shows that careful designs with sub-sample estimates can promote sample efficiency particularly in terms of the dependence on the number of random parameters. Yet, the current theories therein are mostly focused on some more special (but still widely applicable) SP problems.

This current paper frequently refers to the existing complexity bounds, e.g., by
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
, and
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
as benchmarks in order to explain the claimed advantages of our results. Yet, it is worth noting that those concurrent works apply to more general settings than this paper. For instance, the SAA theories by
Shapiro et al. 2021
and
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
can handle nonconvex problems. The findings by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
further admit stochasticity in the feasible region. Nonetheless, when applied to the settings of our consideration—SAA for convex SP problems with deterministic constraints—the results by
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
, and
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
are known to be the best available benchmarks. We would like to also argue that the SP problems considered herein are still flexible enough to cover a very wide spectrum of applications, and our proof arguments, which seem to differentiate from most SAA literature, may be further extended to nonconvex problems and scenarios with uncertain constraints.

3
Main Results.

This section presents the formal statements of our three sets of results. First, Section
3.1
provides the sample complexity bounds that match with those of the canonical SMD under comparable assumptions. Second, Section
3.2
shows our large deviations-type, metric entropy-free bounds under the standard Lipschitz condition as in (
7
). Lastly, Section
3.3
discusses our findings in non-Lipschitzian settings.

3.1
SMD-comparable sample complexity of SAA.

We start by introducing our assumptions. First, we formalize the previously mentioned structure of a composite objective function in the genuine SP problem (
1
) as below:

Assumption 1

For a given
q\geq 1
, let
\varrho=q/(q-1)
. There exist two deterministic functions, denoted by
F_{1}:\,{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and
F_{2}:\,{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}})=F_{1}({\mathbf{x}})+F_{2}({\mathbf{x}}),

where
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
satisfy the below:

(a)

Function
F_{1}
is everywhere differentiable. Meanwhile, for some
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla F_{1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-\nabla F_{1}({\mathbf{x}}_{2})\|_{\varrho}\leq\mathcal{L}\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|_{q},~~\forall({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2};

(15)

(b)

For any given
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and every
{\mathbf{g}}_{F_{2}}\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}})-\nabla F_{1}({\mathbf{x}})
, there exists some
\mathcal{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{g}}_{F_{2}}\|_{\varrho}\leq\mathcal{M}.

(16)

We sometimes refer to this condition as “Assumption
1
w.r.t. the
q
-norm”. Here, (
15
) means that the first component of the population-level objective function
F_{1}
is
\mathcal{L}
-smooth; that is, it admits an
\mathcal{L}
-Lipschitz continuous gradient. Meanwhile, (
16
) essentially imposes that the second component of the population-level objective function
F_{2}
is
\mathcal{M}
-Lipschitz continuous. Results that apply to such a composite objective function subsume the special cases of
F
being smooth (with
F_{2}\equiv 0
) and
F
being Lipschitz (with
F_{1}\equiv 0
). Conditions closely similar to, if not more critical than, Assumption
1
have been considered in much SP literature
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2012
,
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
,
Nemirovski et al. 2009
,
Rakhlin et al. 2011
,
Lan 2020
, such as)
.

Our assumption on the underlying randomness is the everywhere bounded-ness of the variance of some stochastic subgradient. We formalize this condition in the following, where we recall that
G
is a deterministic and measurable function such that
G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)
for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and almost every
\xi\in\Xi
:

Assumption 2

For every
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)]
is well defined and, for a given
p\geq 1
and some
\sigma_{p}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-{\mathbb{E}}[G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)]\|_{p}^{2}\vphantom{V^{V^{V}}_{V_{V}}}\right]\leq\sigma_{p}^{2}.

Hereafter, this condition is sometimes called “Assumption
2
w.r.t. the
p
-norm”, which is common in the SP literature, especially in the discussions of SMD
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
, e.g., by)
. The stipulation of
\sigma_{p}\geq 1
is non-critical; it is only for the simplification of notations in our results.

We formalize our assumptions of strong convexity and convexity in Assumptions
3
and
4
, respectively, as below:

Assumption 3

Function
f(\,\cdot\,,\xi)
is
\mu
-strongly convex on
{\mathcal{X}}
w.r.t. the
q
-norm for almost every
\xi\in\Xi
; namely,
f(\,\cdot\,,\xi)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\cdot\|_{q}^{2}
is convex on
{\mathcal{X}}
for almost every
\xi\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

As an immediate result of this assumption, the following inequality holds for every pair of solutions
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2}
and almost every
\xi\in\Xi
:

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\xi)-f({\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi)\geq\langle{\mathbf{g}},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|^{2}_{q},

for all
{\mathbf{g}}\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}}f({\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi)
and some given
\mu>0
and
q\geq 1
.

Remark 1

We refer to the above as “Assumption
3
w.r.t. the
q
-norm” or “
\mu
-strong convexity”, which is common in the SAA literature
(
Milz 2023
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
, e.g., in)
. Some SP literature
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2012
, e.g., by)
assumes a relatively more flexible version of strong convexity than Assumption
3
as in the following:

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-F({\mathbf{x}}_{2})\geq\langle\mathbf{g}_{F},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|^{2}_{q},~~\forall({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2}~\text{and}~\mathbf{g}_{F}\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}}_{2}).

(17)

This condition is considered mostly in the discussions of SMD. We argue that the seemingly higher stringency in Assumption
3
relative to (
17
) does not make the SP problem much easier. Indeed, lower complexity bounds for SMD
(
Rakhlin et al. 2011
,
Agarwal et al. 2009
, such as by)
are derived based on the identification of adversarial problems that satisfy Assumption
3
. Thus, when solving an SP problem that satisfies Assumption
3
instead of (
17
), the typical SMD schemes cannot achieve faster sample complexity rates in general.

Some results in this section (as well as in several latter parts of this paper) consider the condition of (non-strong) convexity below:

Assumption 4

Function
f(\,\cdot\,,\xi)
is convex on
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
for almost every
\xi\in\Xi
.

As an immediate implication of this assumption, the following inequality holds for every
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2}
, all
{\mathbf{g}}\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}}f({\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi)
, and almost every
\xi\in\Xi
:

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\xi)-f({\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi)\geq\langle{\mathbf{g}},\,{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\rangle.

We would like to compare the above with a counterpart assumption that the population-level objective
F(\cdot)
is convex, which is, again, a common condition in the literature on SMD
(
Nemirovski et al. 2009
,
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
, e.g., by)
. Relative to this counterpart condition, the incremental stringency in Assumption
4
does not make the SP problems much easier; this is because, again, the adversarial problem instances used to prove lower performance limits for SMD in solving the convex SP problems
(
Agarwal et al. 2009
, such as those constructed by)
often satisfy Assumption
4
.
From such analysis, one can see that switching from the assumption of
F
being convex into Assumption
4
does not allow SMD to achieve a better sample efficiency in general.

We are now ready to formalize the promised sample complexity bounds in both strongly convex and convex cases below.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
3
hold both w.r.t. the
q
-norm for a given
q\geq 1
, and that Assumption
2
holds w.r.t. the
p
-norm for some
p:\,1\leq p\leq\frac{q}{q-1}
. Then any
(\delta,q)
-approximate solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
to SAA (
2
) with
\delta\leq 1/N
satisfies the below: For any given
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right]\leq\epsilon,~~~\text{if }N\geq C_{1}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\mu},\,\frac{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu\epsilon}\right\};

(18)

and, meanwhile, for any given
\epsilon>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\leq\epsilon\vphantom{V^{V^{V}}_{V_{V}}}\right]\geq 1-\beta,~~\text{if }N\geq C_{1}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\mu},\,\frac{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu\epsilon\beta}\right\}.

(19)

Here,
C_{1}>0
is some universal constant.

Proof

We first prove (
18
) through two steps.

Step 1.
Observe that Eq. (
6
) (as an implication of the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
as in (
5
)) implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right]=

\displaystyle\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})+\delta\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{4}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right].

(20)

Invoking Assumption
3
, we have that
F
is also
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t. the
q
-norm. Consequently,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mu}{2}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{4}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right]\Longrightarrow{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mu}{4}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mu}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right].

This combined with (
20
) implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right]\leq\frac{2\delta^{2}}{\mu}+2{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right].

(21)

Therefore, it suffices to establish an upper bound on
{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right]
, which is the focus of Step 2 in this proof.

Step 2.
Below, we let
H({\mathbf{x}}):={\mathbb{E}}[G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)]
for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. By Assumption
3
, it holds that
f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-f({\mathbf{y}},\xi)\geq\langle G({\mathbf{x}},\xi),{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle
for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and almost every
\xi\in\Xi
. Thus,
F({\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{y}})={\mathbb{E}}[f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)]-{\mathbb{E}}[f({\mathbf{y}},\xi)]\geq{\mathbb{E}}[\langle G({\mathbf{x}},\xi),{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle]=\langle{\mathbb{E}}[G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)],{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\langle H({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Consequently,
H({\mathbf{x}})\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
.

With the observation from Step 1, we construct a sequence of alternative formulations of SAA (
2
) with
F_{N}^{(j)}({\mathbf{x}}):=\frac{1}{N}\left(f({\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})+\sum_{\iota\neq j}f({\mathbf{x}},\xi_{\iota})\right)
, where
\xi_{j}^{\prime}
is an i.i.d. copy of
\xi
, for all
j=1,...,N
. Denote that
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N}=(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{j-1},\xi_{j}^{\prime},\xi_{j+1},...,\xi_{N})
. Correspondingly, we let
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}:=\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N})
following the notation in (
5
).
Below, we establish an overestimate on
N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}\right]
. This overestimate is to play a key role in bounding
{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right].

To that end, we first observe that

\displaystyle F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})=

\displaystyle\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})}{N}{+}\sum_{\iota\neq j}\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{\iota})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{\iota})}{N}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})}{N}-\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})}{N}+F_{N}^{(j)}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-F_{N}^{(j)}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})

(23)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})}{N}-\frac{f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})}{N}+\delta\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}

(24)

Here, (
22
) and (
23
) are by the definitions of
F_{N}
and
F_{N}^{(j)}
, and (
24
) is due to the construction of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}:=\widetilde{{\mathbf{x}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N})
(cf. (
6
)).
By Assumption
3
and
G({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\in\partial f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)
, we have
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})\leq\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\rangle
for almost every
\xi_{j}\in\Xi
, as well as
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}^{\prime})\leq\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\rangle
for almost every
\xi_{j}^{\prime}\in\Xi
. Combining this with (
24
) leads to the below:

\displaystyle F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\cdot\left\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{N}\cdot\left\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\right\rangle+\delta\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q},~~a.s.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\cdot\left\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{N}\cdot\left\langle G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\cdot\left\langle H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle+\delta\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}.

(25)

Since Assumption
1
leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla F_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-\nabla F_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}),\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle+\left\langle[H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-\nabla F_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})]-[H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-\nabla F_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})],\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\|_{q}^{2}+2\mathcal{M}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\|_{q},

(26)

we may continue from the above to obtain, through Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, for all
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\alpha\mu N^{2}}\cdot\left\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})\right\|^{2}_{p}+\frac{1}{2\alpha\mu N^{2}}\cdot\left\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right\|^{2}_{p}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{N}+\alpha\mu\right)\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}+\frac{16\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu N^{2}}+\frac{\mu}{16}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}+\frac{4\delta^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{16}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2},~~~a.s.

(27)

By strong convexity of
F_{N}
(Assumption
3
) as well as the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
in (
5
), we have that

\displaystyle F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\geq

\displaystyle-\delta\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}+\frac{\mu}{2}\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{q},~~~a.s.,

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{4}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{q}.

(28)

Combining (
27
) and (
28
), we immediately obtain the below after some re-organization and simplification:

\left[\left(\frac{1}{8}-\alpha\right)\cdot\mu-\frac{\mathcal{L}}{N}\right]\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2N^{2}\mu\alpha}\cdot\left\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})\right\|^{2}_{p}+\frac{1}{2N^{2}\mu\alpha}\cdot\left\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right\|^{2}_{p}+\frac{16\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu N^{2}}+\frac{5\delta^{2}}{\mu}
, almost surely.
Note that
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}
and
\xi_{j}
are independent, so are
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
and
\xi^{\prime}_{j}
. We therefore have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)})\|^{2}_{p}]\leq\sigma^{2}_{p}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})-H(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\|^{2}_{p}]\leq\sigma^{2}_{p}
under Assumption
2
. Further because we may let
\alpha=1/16
and it is assumed that
N\geq\frac{C^{\prime}\mathcal{L}}{\mu}
(where we may as well let
C^{\prime}\geq 32
), we then have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}\right]\leq\left[\left(\frac{1}{8}-\alpha\right)\cdot\mu-\frac{\mathcal{L}}{N}\right]^{-1}\cdot\left(\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}}{N^{2}\mu\alpha}+\frac{16\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mu N^{2}}+\frac{5\delta^{2}}{\mu}\right)\leq 512\cdot\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{N^{2}\mu^{2}}+\frac{160\delta^{2}}{\mu^{2}};

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}\right]\leq 512\cdot\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{N^{2}\mu^{2}}+\frac{160\delta^{2}}{\mu^{2}}.

(29)

Because
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j}^{\prime})
and
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})
are identically distributed — so are
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})
and
f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}^{\prime})
— we then obtain that
{\mathbb{E}}[f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi^{\prime}_{j})]={\mathbb{E}}[f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j})]
and that
{\mathbb{E}}[f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\xi_{j})]={\mathbb{E}}[f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)},\xi_{j}^{\prime})]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[F(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})-F_{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})]

\displaystyle=

Remark 2

An important component of this proof is to establish an upper bound on

as in (

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}

Remark 8

Remark 9

In some applications, the variance

may also depend on dimensionality

and every

is concave in ‘

Namely, in this case, one may let

in our bounds, whose dependence on dimensionality reduces when

becomes dimension-free.

An important component of our proofs resorts to a seemingly novel argument based on the “average-replace-one (average-RO) stability”

)

)

)

)
. While it is known that the average-RO stability can lead to error bounds for learning algorithms
(

)
, seldom is there a sample complexity bound for SAA based on such a stability type in comparable settings of our consideration. In contrast, as mentioned in Section

3.2
Large deviations-type, metric entropy-free complexity bounds.

Eq. (
19
) and (
35
) in the previous subsection provide sample complexity bounds at the rate of
O(1/\beta)
in ensuring a significance level of
\beta\in(0,1)
. While we expect this rate to be hardly improvable given only the assumption of a bounded variance, imposing stronger conditions such as the bounded-ness of higher moments should expectedly lead to a better dependence on
\beta
. In particular, when the underlying randomness is light-tailed, many existing results, such as in (
8
) and (
9
), are (poly-)logarithmic in
1/\beta
(although most of these results are polynomial in some metric entropy terms). It then prompts the question whether metric entropy-free bounds can also be derived while preserving a (poly-)logarithmic rate with
1/\beta
under similar conditions. Our affirmative answer to this question is presented in this subsection.

The assumptions of consideration include a formalized statement of the Lipschitz condition in (
7
) and that of the underlying randomness, both provided in the below:

Assumption 5

Let
M:\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a deterministic and measurable function.

(a)

Given
q>1
, for any
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2}
and every

|f({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-f({\mathbf{y}},\xi)|\leq M(\xi)\cdot\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{q}.

(b)

It holds that
\|M(\xi)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\psi_{M}
for some
\psi_{M}\geq 1
and
p>2
.

Assumption
5
.(a) is a very common condition in the SAA literature
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
)
. Compared to the uniform Lipschitz condition as in Eq. (
14
), Assumption
5
.(a) does not impose that

should be
\xi

5
.(b) means that
M(\xi)
should have a finite
p
th moment, though this random variable can still be heavy-tailed. A related condition is also imposed by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
.
The specification that
q>1
(and thus excluding the case of
q=1
) is non-critical due to the same argument as in Remark
6
.

For perhaps more interesting results of this section, we impose light-tailed counterparts to the above as stated in Assumptions
6
and
7
below:

Assumption 6

Let
M:\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a deterministic and measurable function.

(a)

Assumption
5
.(a) holds; and

(b)

For some
\varphi\geq 1
and all

, it holds that

{\textnormal{Prob}}[M(\xi)\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-{t}/{\varphi}\right).

Assumption
6
.(b) imposes subexponential tails, a common form of light-tailed assumptions, for the underlying distribution and is comparable to, if not weaker than, the counterpart conditions imposed by
Shapiro 2003
,
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
and
Shapiro et al. 2021
.

Assumption 7

Let
M:\Xi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a deterministic and measurable function.

(a)

Assumption
5
.(a) holds; and

(b)

For some
\varphi\geq 1
and all

{\textnormal{Prob}}[M(\xi)\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-{t^{2}}/{\varphi^{2}}\right).

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Upsilon_{j}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right\|_{L^{p}}^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mu}{8}\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{q}^{2}\right]+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mu}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}]\leq\frac{16}{\mu^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Upsilon_{j}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right\|_{L^{p}}^{2}\right]+\frac{8\delta^{2}}{\mu^{2}}.

By Lemma
1
in Section

, since
{2}\leq p<\infty
, if we denote by

\Upsilon_{j}

\displaystyle\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Upsilon_{j}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right\|_{L^{p}}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Upsilon_{j,i}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\vphantom{V^{V}_{V_{V_{V}}}}\right)^{2/p}\leq\left(\left(\tilde{C}\sqrt{p\cdot N^{-1}}\right)^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\|\Upsilon_{1,i}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\|_{L^{p}}\vphantom{V_{V_{V_{V}}}}\right]^{p}\right)^{2/p}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}\cdot p}{N}\psi_{p}^{2}.

for some universal constant
\tilde{C}>0
.
We may then continue from (

) to obtain

for some universal constant

, which immediately leads to the desired result in (

) (and thus the first part of the theorem).

To show (

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}^{2}\leq\frac{16}{\mu^{2}}\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Upsilon_{j}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\right\|^{2}_{p}+\frac{8\delta^{2}}{\mu^{2}},~~~~a.s.

Remark 13

Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
above provide formal statements of the promised sample complexity as in (
12
) and (
13
) of Section
1
, respectively. Again, no metric entropy term is in presence.

Another potentially desirable feature of these complexity bounds is that they do not depend on any Lipschitz constants of
F
and
f(\cdot,\xi)
, nor those of their (sub)gradients. This result predicts SAA to be effective even if the Lipschitz constants are undesirably large and even potentially unbounded (due to the possibly unbounded feasible region). In contrast, most existing SAA’s sample complexity bounds, e.g., by
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Hu et al. 2020
,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
, and
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
, grow polynomially with the some Lipschitz constants. To our knowledge, the only SAA result under similar conditions is provided by
Milz 2023
, whose findings imply the same error bound of Theorem
3.4
in the
2
-norm setting. Nonetheless, our analysis in Theorem
3.4

presents an alternative proof and generalizes from the
2
-norm setting to more general
q
-norm (
1\leq q\leq 2
) settings. Further, Theorem
3.5
applies to SP problems not necessarily strongly convex, which are not discussed by
Milz 2023
.

To our knowledge, there currently is limited theory for SMD’s effectiveness when none of the Lipschitz constants of
F
,
f(\cdot,\xi)
, or their (sub)gradients admits a known upper bound. Comparing with SMD, our results show SAA’s potential for better applicability to the SP problems in less desirable settings.

Remark 14

It is also worth noting that both Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
explicate the evolution of the complexity rate w.r.t.
\beta
, as the underlying distribution gradually admits more and more bounded central moments (and thus the tail becomes lighter and lighter). Once it is admissible to let
p\geq c\ln(1/\beta)
for some constant
c>0
, the complexity then becomes logarithmic in
1/\beta
.

Similar to Remark
12
, Theorem
3.5
does not provide a guarantee on the solution’s suboptimality gap. Yet, such a guarantee is, as we suspect, hardly available due to the limited regularities in the non-Lipschitzian settings of consideration.

Conclusion.

This paper revisits the sample complexity bounds for SAA in both strongly convex and (non-strongly) convex SP problems. Under regularity conditions that are typical to the SP literature (and beyond the uniform Lipschitz condition), our findings show, perhaps for the first time, that SAA achieves sample complexity rates completely free from any metric entropy terms. This represents a deviation from the benchmark results where the inclusion of those metric entropy terms has been seemingly inevitable. Generally, as these terms elevate the dependence of the sample requirement on the problem dimensionality, our newly established sample complexity bounds are less sensitive to the increase of dimensionality compared to the state-of-the-art results in many scenarios. In particular, under assumptions comparable to those in the existing literature on the SMD methods, an mainstream alternative solution approach to SP, part of our results provides the first revelation that SAA and the canonical SMD entail identical sample efficiency, closing a seemingly persistent theoretical gap of the order
O(d)
between these two methods. Furthermore, we also identify some non-Lipschitzan cases where SAA can be shown to retain effectiveness but, in contrast, the results for SMD are currently unavailable to our knowledge. The paper also reports preliminary numerical experiments, from which our observations were consistent with our theoretical predictions.

Proposition 2

Let
\beta\in(0,1)
be any scalar. Let
A:\Xi^{N}\times\Xi\rightarrow[0,U]
be a meaurable and deterministic function for some
U>0
. Denote that

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N}=(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N})
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N}=(\xi_{1},...,\,\xi_{j-1},\xi_{j}^{\prime},\xi_{j+1},...,\xi_{N})
; that is, the two vectors are different only in the
j
th entry.

For a given
\gamma\geq 0
, assume that

\displaystyle|A(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi)-A(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N},\xi)|\leq\gamma,~~\forall(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi^{\prime}_{j},\xi)\in\Xi^{N}\times\Xi\times\Xi,~~j=1,...,N.

(107)

Then, it holds that

{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[A(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi)]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi_{j})\right|\geq c\left(\gamma\ln N\cdot\ln\frac{N}{\beta}+U\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\beta)}{N}}\right)\right]\leq\beta,

for some universal constant
c>0
.

Proof

This is a straightforward result of Theorem 1.1 by
Feldman and Vondrak 2019
. More specifically, let
A^{\prime}:\Xi^{N}\times\Xi\rightarrow[0,1]
be defined as
A^{\prime}:=U^{-1}\cdot A
. By assumption, it holds that

\displaystyle|A^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi)-A^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{1,N},\xi)|\leq\gamma/U,~~\forall(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi^{\prime}_{j},\xi)\in\Xi^{N}\times\Xi\times\Xi,~~j=1,...,N.

Invoking Theorem 1.1 in
Feldman and Vondrak 2019
(where
A
and
\gamma
therein are specified as
A^{\prime}
and
\gamma/U
, respectively), we have

\displaystyle\beta\geq

\displaystyle\,{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[A(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi)]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi_{j})\right|\geq c\left(\gamma\ln N\cdot\ln\frac{N}{\beta}+U\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\beta)}{N}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[U\cdot\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[A^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi)]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,N},\xi_{j})\right|\geq c\cdot U\left(\frac{\gamma}{U}\ln N\cdot\ln\frac{N}{\beta}+\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\beta)}{N}}\right)\right],

which immediately leads to the desired result.

Lemma 1

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
. Denote by
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N}{\in{\mathbb{R}}}
an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with
{\mathbb{E}}[\xi_{1}]=0
. Then
\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\cdot\sqrt{pN^{-1}}\cdot\left\|\xi_{1}\right\|_{L^{p}}
for some universal constant
C>0
.

Proof

This lemma is largely based on the approach used in the proof of Proposition 1 by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
. By Marcinkiewicz’ inequality, for some universal constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq

\displaystyle N^{-1}C\cdot\sqrt{p}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}^{2}\right\|_{L^{p/2}}^{1/2}\leq N^{-1}C\cdot\sqrt{p}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\xi_{j}^{2}\right\|_{L^{p/2}}}=C\cdot\sqrt{p\cdot N^{-1}}\cdot\left\|\xi_{1}\right\|_{L^{p}},

as desired.

Lemma 2

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
. Denote by
{\mathbf{v}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{v}}_{N}{\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
an i.i.d. sequence of
d
-dimensional random vectors with
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{v}}_{1}]=\mathbf{0}
. Then, for any
t>0
, it holds that

{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}\geq t\right]\leq\left(\widetilde{C}\cdot\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\right\|_{L^{p}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{p}{Nt}}\right)^{p},

for some universal constant
\widetilde{C}>0
.

Proof

The proof slightly strengthens that of Proposition 1 by
Oliveira and Thompson 2023
and is a quick result of the Markov’s inequality. Let
v_{ij}
be the
i
th component of
{\mathbf{v}}_{j}
. Then, it holds, for any
t>0
, that

\displaystyle\,{\textnormal{Prob}}\left[\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}\geq t\right]={\textnormal{Prob}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{ij}\right|^{p}\right)^{2/p}\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Markov's}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\,\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{ij}\right|^{p}\right]}{t^{p/2}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left\|N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{ij}\right\|_{L^{p}}\right)^{p}}{t^{p/2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma \ref{useful lemma 2}}}}{{\leq}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\widetilde{C}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{p}{Nt}}\|v_{i1}\|_{L^{p}}\right)^{p}

Appendix C
Comparison with an existing lower bound on sample complexity

Below we restate Example 5.21 of
Shapiro et al. 2021
, which provides a lower sample complexity for SAA. Consider a special SP problem (
1
) with
f({\mathbf{x}},\xi):=\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2m}-2m\xi^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}
, where
m>0
and
{\mathcal{X}}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
. Suppose, further, that random vector
\xi
has normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, where
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
and
I_{d}
is the
d
-by-
d
identity matrix. Without loss of generality, we let
d\geq 3
. We also denote by
O(1)
’s universal constants.

As per
Shapiro et al. 2021
, the event that an exact optimal solution to SAA (
2
) ensures
\epsilon
-suboptimality with
\epsilon\in(0,1)
in solving the population-level SP problem coincides with the event that
\{\chi_{d}^{2}\leq N\epsilon^{2/v}/\sigma^{2}\}
, where
v:=\frac{2m}{2m-1}
and
\chi_{d}^{2}
denotes the chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom. Thus, if we let
m=\ln(1/\epsilon)
, one can derive the following lower bound—the sample size is required to satisfy:

\displaystyle N>\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2/v}}=\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2-1/\ln(1/\epsilon)}}=\frac{e\cdot d\cdot\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}},

(111)

in order to ensure that the SAA solution is an
\epsilon
-suboptimal solution to the true problem with probability at least
1-\alpha
. The above then identifies an “adversarial” case of the sample complexity, showing that SAA’s sample complexity should grow no slower than the rate of
O(d)
in general.

The lower bound in (
111
) is not at odds with our results. For instance, when invoking Part (b) of Theorem
3.3
, one may let
q^{\prime}=2
and
R^{*}=1
. It then can be verified that
c_{l}\cdot m^{2}d\sigma^{2}\leq\varphi^{2}\leq c_{u}\cdot m^{2}d\sigma^{2}
for some constants
c_{u}\geq c_{l}>0
. As a result, the sample complexity becomes:

\displaystyle O(1)\cdot\frac{m^{2}d\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\min\left\{\ln^{3}\frac{\Phi_{1}}{\beta},~~\ln\left(d\Phi_{1}\ln\frac{e}{\beta}\right)\cdot\ln^{2}\frac{\Phi_{1}}{\beta}\right\},

(112)

where
\Phi_{1}\leq\frac{O(1)m\sigma\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon}
. Here (
112
)
is consistent with the sample complexity lower bound in (
111
).

The analysis above does not rule out possibility of a better-than-polynomial growth rate with
d
under some additional problem structural assumption. Indeed, below we are able to identify an important special case where our new sample bounds can be dimension insensitive and thus surpass (
111
). Consider a modification to the example above—We assume instead that
{\mathcal{X}}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\leq 1\}
and keep all other settings the same. In evaluating the conditions for Part (b) of Theorem
3.3
for this modified example, we let
R^{*}=e
and
q^{\prime}=1+(\ln(d)-1)^{-1}
. One may also verify that

\displaystyle M(\xi)=\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in X}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\|_{\infty}=\left\|2m\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2m-2}{\mathbf{x}}-2m\xi\vphantom{V^{V^{V}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2m+2m\|\xi\|_{\infty}.

(113)

Then, the quantity
\varphi
admits the an overestimate of
\varphi^{2}\leq O(1)\cdot m^{2}\sigma^{2}\ln d.

Consequently, the sample complexity bound becomes

\displaystyle O(1)\cdot\frac{m^{2}\sigma^{2}\ln d}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\min\left\{\ln^{3}\frac{\Phi_{2}}{\beta},~~\ln\left(d\Phi_{2}\ln\frac{e}{\beta}\right)\cdot\ln^{2}\frac{\Phi_{2}}{\beta}\right\},

(114)

where
\Phi_{1}\leq\frac{O(1)m\sigma\sqrt{\ln d}}{\epsilon}
. The sample complexity result in (
114
) surpasses the lower bound in (
111
). In further contrast, the current SAA’s sample complexity benchmark in (
9
) yields the following:

\displaystyle O(1)\cdot\frac{m^{2}\sigma^{2}\ln d}{\epsilon^{2}}\left[d\cdot\ln\left(\frac{O(1)\cdot m\sigma\ln d}{\epsilon}\right)+\ln\frac{1}{\beta}\right].

(115)

One can see that (
112
) is more efficient than (
115
) for higher dimensional problems.

A closely similar arguments as above can show that our findings are not at odds with, and can sometimes even surpass, the lower sample complexity bounds by
Guigues et al. 2017
.
