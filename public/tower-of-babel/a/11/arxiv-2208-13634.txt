Trade-off relations between measurement dependence and hidden information for factorizable hidden variable models
Ryo Takakura, Kei Morisue, Issei Watanabe, Gen Kimura

Abstract
The Bell theorem is explored in terms of a trade-off relation between underlying assumptions within the hidden variable model framework. In this paper, recognizing the incorporation of hidden variables as one of the fundamental assumptions, we propose a measure termed ‘hidden information’ taking account of their distribution. This measure quantifies the number of hidden variables that essentially contribute to the empirical statistics. For factorizable models, hidden variable models that satisfy ‘locality’ without adhering to the measurement independence criterion, we derive novel relaxed Bell-Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (Bell-CHSH) inequalities. These inequalities elucidate trade-off relations between measurement dependence and hidden information in the CHSH scenario. It is also revealed that the relation gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the measures to be realized by a factorizable model.

To address the Bell theorem, adopting a local hidden variable model is essential: A set of ‘hidden’ variables is introduced that satisfies certain assumptions to explain the empirically observed statistics.
One of the crucial assumptions behind the Bell theorem is

The violation of the Bell inequality thus implies that at least one of the underlying assumptions is violated.

Despite the recognition that the introduction of hidden variables is one of the fundamental assumptions behind the Bell Theorem, these studies have not considered the quantitative analysis of this assumption.
In our previous paper

However, the measure of hiddenness is limited to only discrete values and does not adequately reflect the statistics of the hidden variable model.
In this paper, we address this issue by introducing a refined entropic measure, termed

We then derive a novel relaxed Bell inequality (more precisely, a relaxed Bell-CHSH inequality as in the previous studies) for any factorizable model in the CHSH scenario (Theorem
3.1

This inequality establishes a trade-off relation between measurement dependence and hidden information.
Moreover, we demonstrate that the set of inequalities that we found completely characterizes factorizable models: any point satisfying the inequality can be realized by a suitably chosen factorizable model (Theorem
3.2

Our results give a different evaluation of the CHSH value of a factorizable model, which makes it possible in another way to interpret a violation of the usual Bell-CHSH inequality in terms of the measurement dependence and the introduction of hidden variables itself.

2
Preliminaries

Consider two spacelike separated parties, Alice and Bob, who perform measurements
x
and
y
on each subsystem to obtain outcomes
a
and
b
respectively.
In the CHSH scenario, there are two choices of measurements
x,y\in\{0,1\}
with two outcomes
a,b\in\{-1,1\}
for each system.
The state, called a
behavior

[
1
]
, is a set of probabilities
s=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}_{a,b\in\{-1,1\},x,y\in\{0,1\}}
, where
p(a,b|x,y)
denotes the joint probability of observing outcomes
a
and
b
when measurements
x
and
y
are performed by Alice and Bob respectively.
We say that a behavior
s=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}_{a,b\in\{-1,1\},x,y\in\{0,1\}}
admits a
hidden variable model
(HV model) if there exists a set
\Lambda
of ‘hidden’ variables through which each joint probability is given as the marginal distribution

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}p(a,b,\lambda|x,y),

(1)

where
p(a,b,\lambda|x,y)
is a joint probability of
a,b
and a hidden variable
\lambda\in\Lambda
conditioned on
x,y
.
The equation (
1
) can be rewritten by using the conditional probabilities as

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda|x,y)p(a,b|x,y,\lambda).

(2)

Although in this paper we basically treat a discrete set of hidden variables, one can consider continuous cases as well just by replacing the summation by an integration.
In the following, we sometimes refer to the sets
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda}\}_{x,y}
and
O=\{\{p(a,b|x,y,\lambda)\}_{a,b}\}_{{x,y,\lambda}}
as the
input
and
output
of the underlying HV model respectively.
We note that once two sets of probabilities
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda}\}_{x,y}
and
O=\{\{p(a,b|x,y,\lambda)\}_{a,b}\}_{{x,y,\lambda}}
are given, an HV model
s=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}_{a,b,x,y}
can be defined through (
2
).
Thus we write a behavior
s
also as
s=(I,O)
by means of its input
I
and output
O
.

In this paper, we consider
factorizable
models.
An HV model
s=(I,O)
is called factorizable if it satisfies the factorization condition

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y,\lambda)=p(a|x,\lambda)p(b|y,\lambda)

(3)

characterized by local distributions
\{\{p(a|x,\lambda)\}_{a}\}_{x,\lambda}
and
\{\{p(b|y,\lambda)\}_{b}\}_{y,\lambda}
.
It is worthwhile to elucidate the reasons behind this terminology.
These models were referred to as
measurement dependent local
in
[
39
]
(also see
[
38
]
): the condition (
3
) is equivalent to parameter independence and outcome independence, which typically reflect the locality condition
[
4
,
29
]
.
However, as pointed out in
[
37
]
, relaxing measurement independence while maintaining parameter independence can cause violations of the no-signaling condition
[
40
]
in the empirical distributions. This observation led the authors in
[
37
]
to call these models
separable
, but the term ‘separable’ is already widely recognized in the field of quantum information theory as referring quantum separable states.
To avoid confusion and more accurately reflect the characteristic that an outcome probability is factorizable under the hidden variables, in this paper we adopt the term
factorizable
for the models instead.

If the input
I
of
s
in addition satisfies the
measurement independence
between every measurement context
(x,y)
and underlying variable
\lambda
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p(\lambda|x,y)=p(\lambda),

(4)

then the model is called
Bell-local
.
Hence the underlying assumptions for Bell-local models are three independencies: (i) parameter, (ii) outcome, and (iii) measurement independencies of the underlying (hidden) variables.
Bell-local models are the local HV models that have been adopted traditionally when discussing the Bell theorem.

For a behavior
s=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}_{a,b\in\{-1,1\},x,y\in\{0,1\}}
, its CHSH value
S(s)
is defined as

\displaystyle S(s)=

\displaystyle\max\Bigl\{|\left\langle{00}\right\rangle+\left\langle{01}\right\rangle+\left\langle{10}\right\rangle-\left\langle{11}\right\rangle|,|\left\langle{00}\right\rangle+\left\langle{01}\right\rangle-\left\langle{10}\right\rangle+\left\langle{11}\right\rangle|,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad|\left\langle{00}\right\rangle-\left\langle{01}\right\rangle+\left\langle{10}\right\rangle+\left\langle{11}\right\rangle|,|-\left\langle{00}\right\rangle+\left\langle{01}\right\rangle+\left\langle{10}\right\rangle+\left\langle{11}\right\rangle|\Bigr\}

(5)

with

\displaystyle\left\langle{xy}\right\rangle=\sum_{a,b\in\{-1,1\}}ab\penalty\ p(a,b|x,y)\quad(x,y\in\{0,1\}).

(6)

Notice that the CHSH value
S(s)
can be directly accessed by experiments.
There is a trivial upper bound

S(s)\leq 4

since
|\left\langle{xy}\right\rangle|\leq 1
for any
x,y\in\{0,1\}
.
It is known that for a Bell-local behavior
s=(I,O)

\displaystyle S(s)=S(I,O)\leq 2

(7)

holds, which is the famous Bell-CHSH inequality
[
1
,
2
]
.
The violation of this inequality thus implies that at least one of the assumptions (i), (ii), or (iii) of a Bell-local model is violated.
The most important theory that violates the Bell-CHSH inequality is quantum theory.
There, by means of a suitable choice of measurements and an entangled state, the Bell-CHSH inequality is violated
[
2
,
9
]
(in fact it can reach the Tsirelson bound
2\sqrt{2}

[
41
]
), and thus quantum behaviors cannot be described by Bell-local models.

Let us focus on the CHSH value
S(s)
of a factorizable model
s=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}_{a,b,x,y}=(I,O)
that is not necessarily measurement independent (i.e, not necessarily Bell-local).
For the CHSH value, Hall proved a simple evaluation in terms of the measurement dependence of the model defined as
[
21
,
31
]

\displaystyle M(s)=\max_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|x,y)-p(\lambda|x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|.

(8)

It is easy to see that
0\leq M(s)\leq 2
and
M=0
if and only if the model is measurement independent.
Introducing measures of indeterminisim and signaling as well, Hall obtained relaxed Bell inequalities (relaxed Bell-CHSH inequalities) that give trade-off relations among these measures and CHSH values
[
31
]
.
In particular, for the factorizable model
s
, the inequality gives

\displaystyle S\leq 2+\min[3M,\ 2].

(9)

Note that here and in the following
S(s)
and
M(s)
are denoted simply as
S
and
M
respectively when the behavior of interest is obvious.
The inequality (
9
) can be regarded as a generalization of the Bell-CHSH inequality.
In fact, if
M=0
holds for a factorizable model, then the model reduces to a Bell-local model, and the Bell-CHSH inequality is reproduced as expected.

3
Results

Although a broad class of HV models has been investigated and several relaxed Bell inequalities were derived in the previous studies
[
21
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
,
no attempt has been made to quantify the assumption of introducing hidden variables.
Motivated by this observation, in this section we introduce a measure termed
hidden information
, which quantifies the number of hidden variables essentially required to account for the empirical statistics.
In terms of this measure, we derive a new set of relaxed Bell inequalities for factorizable models as trade-off relations between measurement dependence and hidden information for factorizable models.
The expression of hidden information used in our argument is a refined version of the
hiddenness
defined originally in the previous paper
[
38
]
, and thus contributes to obtaining refined inequalities.
The new inequalities are also examined as trade-off relations between measurement dependence and hidden information.
We remark that in our argument we only consider the case where the number of the hidden variables is finite, i.e., the set of the hidden variables is written as
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
(
n
: finite) as well as its limiting case
n\to\infty
.

We begin with reviewing the previous result in
[
38
]
.
There was defined a measure of
hiddenness
simply by the number (the cardinality) of hidden variables
\#(\Lambda)
.
That is, for an HV model
s
with a set of hidden variables
\Lambda
, its hiddenness
H^{\prime}
is defined as

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=\#(\Lambda)-1.

(10)

We note that here we again follow the notation in (
9
) and write
H^{\prime}(s)
simply as
H^{\prime}
.
By means of this
H^{\prime}
and the measurement dependence
M
given by (
8
), a refined version of (
9
) for a factorizable model
s
was derived in
[
38
]
as

\displaystyle S\leq 2+\min\Bigl[\min[H^{\prime},3]M,\ 2\Bigr].

(11)

We remark that this inequality gives a trade-off relation between the measurement dependence and the hiddenness:
the less the measurement dependence
M
is, the more the hiddenness
H^{\prime}
is, and vice versa.
In
[
38
]
, there was also demonstrated that any triple
(M,H^{\prime},S)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying the inequality (
11
) is realized by a factorizable model.

While the above result enables us to interpret a violation of the Bell-CHSH inequality for a factorizable model in terms of its hiddenness and measurement dependence, the measure (
10
) would not be appropriate for quantifying hiddenness.
Two reasons can be given to show this:
First,
H^{\prime}
is a discrete (integer-valued) quantity.
Second, it does not always represent properly the amount of the underlying hidden variables that essentially contribute to the statistics.
To understand this, consider a factorizable model where the set of hidden variables
\Lambda
has a large number of elements but the probability
p(\lambda)
for each hidden variable is negligible for almost all
\lambda
’s except for small numbers of elements.
In this case, although
H^{\prime}
is inevitably estimated to be large, it is more natural to assume that there are fewer hidden variables used intrinsically.
To reflect these considerations, we introduce a new entropic measure
H
termed
hidden information
by

\displaystyle H=1-\exp(-H_{\infty}(\lambda))=1-\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda),

(12)

where
H_{\infty}(\lambda)
is the min-entropy for the hidden variable
[
42
]
.
Note here that the unbiased settings are concerned where measurements
x\in\{0,1\}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
are chosen randomly:
p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}
,

\displaystyle H=1-\frac{1}{4}\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left(\sum_{x,y}p(\lambda|x,y)\right).

(13)

This quantity
H=1-\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda)
can be regarded as describing the ‘width’ of the probability distribution
\{p(\lambda)\}_{\lambda}

[
43
,
44
]
and thereby it appropriately captures the necessity of hidden variables, taking the statistical distribution into account.
It is easy to see that
H\geq 0
and
H=0
if and only if there is an element
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda
such that
p(\lambda^{*})=1
, which essentially corresponds to the case without the introduction of hidden variables (or with a single variable).
In our finite model with arbitrary
\#(\Lambda)=n
, it holds that
H\leq 1-1/n\ (<1)
, and the upper bound is attained if and only if
p(\lambda)=1/n
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
Note that the measurement dependence
M
and the hidden information
H
are functionals of the input
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda}\}_{x,y}
of the underlying HV model.
They are not completely independent of each other but intrinsically satisfy

\displaystyle H\geq\frac{M}{8}

(14)

(the proof for this relation is given in Appendix
B
).
This means that large measurement dependence requires large hidden information, and also
H=0
implies
M=0
.
Now our main finding of relaxed Bell inequalities for factorizable models is presented as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any factorizable model, a relaxed Bell inequality

\displaystyle S\leq 2+\frac{3}{4}M+2H

(15)

holds.

This inequality is a novel trade-off relation between
M
and
H
for
S
that refines the previously obtained inequality (
11
).
Combining this theorem with (
11
), we obtain the tightest trade-off relation.

Theorem 3.2
.

The measurement dependence
M
, the hidden information
H
, and the CHSH value
S
for any factorizable model satisfy

\displaystyle S\leq 2+\min\Bigl[3M,\ \frac{3}{4}M+2H,\ 2\Bigr].

(16)

Conversely, any triple
(M,H,S)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
that satisfies the inequality (
16
) together with the intrinsic relations

\displaystyle 0\leq M\leq 2,\quad\frac{M}{8}\leq H<1,\quad S\geq 0

(17)

is realized by a factorizable model.

This theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a triple
(M,H,S)
to be realized by a factorizable model.

1
(a) shows the allowed region of
(M,H,S)
for all factorizable models determined by (
16
) and (
17
), which forms a polyhedron with
10
vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

:

(M,H,S)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
determined by (
16
) and (
17
), which is a polyhedron with 10 vertices.
The faces indicated by (
16
) is expressed in red and the others by (
17
) in yellow.

(M,H)
in

S=k+2

.
To illustrate how (
16
) gives a trade-off relation between
M
and
H
, assume that a CHSH value
S=2+k
(
-2\leq k\leq 2
) is observed in an experiment.
Then, from Theorem
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle k\leq\min\Bigl[3M,\ \frac{3}{4}M+2H,\ 2\Bigr].

(18)

It follows that the allowed region
W_{k}
of
(M,H)
is given by

\displaystyle W_{k}=\left\{(M,H)\mid\frac{k}{3}\leq M,\ -\frac{3}{8}M+\frac{k}{2}\leq H,\ 0\leq M\leq 2,\ \frac{M}{8}\leq H<1\right\}.

(19)

(b), the allowed regions
W_{k}

k=2
(the maximal violation of the Bell-CHSH inequality),
0<k<2
(a violation of the Bell-CHSH inequality including the quantum case, i.e., the Tsirelson bound), and
k\leq 0
(no violation of the Bell-CHSH inequality) are plotted in light blue.
It implies that if the Bell-CHSH inequality is not violated (
k\leq 0
), then there are no constraints between
H
and
M
but the intrinsic relations (
17
).
On the other hand, if the Bell-CHSH inequality is violated (
k>0
), then there appears a trade-off relation between
M
and
H
given by (
15
): the less the measurement dependence
M
is, the more the hidden information
H
is, and vice versa.
In this case, we can also see that there is a lower bound for the measurement dependence
M
given by (
18
), which means that sufficiently large measurement dependence is needed.

4
Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a measure of hidden information where the distribution of hidden variables is taken into account.
We showed a novel relaxed Bell-CHSH inequality for a given factorizable model in the CHSH scenario (Theorem
3.1
), which gives a non-trivial trade-off relation between the measurement dependence and the hidden information for that model.
The result can be considered as another version of the inequality in
[
38
]
in terms of another measure for hiddenness.
Combining our novel inequality with the known trade-off relations in
[
31
,
38
]
, we also derived the tightest relations between measurement dependence, hidden information, and CHSH values (Theorem
3.2
) in the sense that they give necessary and sufficient conditions for these measures to take arbitrary values realized by a factorizable model.
Future studies will be needed to reveal how our inequalities can be re-expressed when the no-signaling condition is imposed on the empirical distribution as well as for non-factorizable hidden variable models.
It may also be interesting to rewrite our inequalities using different measures, such as other entropic quantities.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

To prove Theorem
3.1
, it is convenient to introduce the optimal CHSH value
S_{\rm opt}
for factorizable models
[
38
]
defined by

\displaystyle S_{\rm opt}(I)=\sup_{O\mathrm{:\penalty\ separable}}S(I,O).

(20)

There was also proven that for
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda}\}_{x,y}

\displaystyle S_{\rm opt}(I)=4-2\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\min_{x,y}p(\lambda|x,y)

(21)

holds and there exists an output
O^{*}
such that
S(I,O^{*})=S_{\rm opt}(I)
(see Lemma 1 in
[
38
]
).
Then, because
S(s)=S(I,O)\leq S_{\rm opt}(I)
holds for any factorizable model
s=(I,O)
, our proof for Theorem
3.1
proceeds by showing that the inequality

\displaystyle S_{\rm opt}(I)\leq 2+\frac{3}{4}M(I)+2H(I)

(22)

holds for any input
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\}_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}
(note that the quantities
M
and
H
depend only on
I
, and thus here we write them by
M(I)
and
H(I)
respectively).
In the following, we represent the set
\Lambda
of hidden variables as
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
with
n=\#(\Lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
(including the limiting case
n\to\infty
), and use the relabeling
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
for the measurement contexts
(x,y)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
in this order.
We also write the input
I=\{\{p(\lambda|x,y)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\}_{x,y\in\{0,1\}}
simply as
I=\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda,i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}}
.
By the definitions (
8
) and (
13
) as well as the optimal CHSH value (
21
), the inequality (
22
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle 2\tilde{K}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)+\frac{3}{4}\tilde{M}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{H}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)\geq 0,

(23)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\tilde{K}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)=\sum_{\lambda}\min_{i}p(\lambda|i),\\
&\tilde{M}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)=\max_{i,j}\left(\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|\right),\\
&\tilde{H}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)=\max_{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i}p(\lambda|i)\right).\end{aligned}

(24)

Thus, if we introduce

\displaystyle F(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}):=2\tilde{K}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})+\frac{3}{4}\tilde{M}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{H}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),

Note that

as well as the label
\lambda
.
In the following, we shall prove that (

.

The cases

When

, and

.
Thus we have

\displaystyle F\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)=2\cdot 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4=0,

When

\hline\cr\lambda_{1}&p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}&p_{4}\\
\lambda_{2}&1-p_{1}&1-p_{2}&1-p_{3}&1-p_{4}\end{array}\right].

By the invariance of the function

under permutations of the labels

Direct calculations of the functions (

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=p_{1}+(1-p_{4}),\ \tilde{M}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=2(p_{4}-p_{1}),\ \tilde{H}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=4-\sum_{i}p_{i},

With

(A)

If there exists

&q(\lambda|i)-q\quad&&(\mbox{$\lambda=\overline{\lambda^{*}},i\in I$})\\
&q(\lambda|i)+q\quad&&(\lambda=\lambda_{3})\\
&q(\lambda|i)\quad&&(\mbox{otherwise}),\end{aligned}\right.

(28)

and go to (C)

We can see that the algorithm is designed to make the newly obtained input

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}_{\theta}|i)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &r(\lambda_{1}|i)+r(\lambda_{2}|i)\quad&&(\theta=1)\\
&r(\lambda_{\theta+1}|i)\quad&&(\theta=2,\ldots,k).\end{aligned}\right.

(29)

\displaystyle F(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\geq F(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\geq F(\{r(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\geq F(\{r^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}).

Let us show these by examining each transformation one by one.
For the first transformation, it is easy to see that

\tilde{K}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{K}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),\ \tilde{M}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{M}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),

and

\tilde{H}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{H}(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})+4p

By the definition (

, we have
F(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\geq F(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})
.
For the second transformation, it is enough to confirm that

holds in step (B).
We prove this by considering the following three cases: (i)

and

.
In case (i),

holds clearly, and thus

is concluded.
In case (ii), we can demonstrate easily that

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{K}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),

\displaystyle\tilde{M}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{M}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{H}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})+4q

hold.
It follows that
F(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=F(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})-2q\leq F(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})
.
For case (iii), we need a slightly complicated argument.
Let us first investigate
\tilde{K}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})
.
Since

\displaystyle\min_{i}q^{\prime}(\lambda^{*}|i)=\min_{i}q(\lambda^{*}|i)=0,

\displaystyle\min_{i}q^{\prime}(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)\leq\min_{i}q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i),

\displaystyle\min_{i}q^{\prime}(\lambda_{3}|i)=\min_{i}q(\lambda_{3}|i)+q,

\displaystyle\min_{i}q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)=\min_{i}q(\lambda|i)\quad(\lambda\in\{\lambda_{4},\ldots,\lambda_{k+1}\}),

we have
\tilde{K}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\leq\tilde{K}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})+q
.
We next focus on
\tilde{M}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})
.
The following observations are important (remember that

for

\displaystyle|q^{\prime}(\lambda^{*}|i)-q^{\prime}(\lambda^{*}|j)|=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|q(\lambda^{*}|i)-q(\lambda^{*}|j)|\quad(\mbox{$i,j\in I$ or $i,j\in I^{c}$})\\
&|q(\lambda^{*}|i)-q(\lambda^{*}|j)|-q\quad(\mbox{$i\in I$ and $j\in I^{c}$}),\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle|q^{\prime}(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)-q^{\prime}(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|j)|=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)-q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|j)|\quad(\mbox{$i,j\in I$ or $i,j\in I^{c}$})\\
&|q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)-q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|j)\pm q|\quad(\mbox{$i\in I$ and $j\in I^{c}$}),\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle|q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)-q^{\prime}(\lambda|j)|=|q(\lambda|i)-q(\lambda|j)|\quad(\mbox{for all $i,j$ and $\lambda\in\{\lambda_{3},\ldots,\lambda_{k+1}\}$}).

Because
|q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)-q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|j)\pm q|\leq|q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|i)-q(\overline{\lambda^{*}}|j)|+q
, we can see from these observations that

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)-q^{\prime}(\lambda|j)|\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|q(\lambda|i)-q(\lambda|j)|

holds for all
i,j
, which implies
\tilde{M}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})\leq\tilde{M}(\{q(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})
.
For

, we obtain easily
\tilde{H}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=\tilde{H}(\{q^{\prime}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})+4q
.
Therefore, we can conclude that

holds also in case (iii).
The third inequality in (
30

is shown as follows.
First, it is easily verified

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\min_{i}r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}|i)=\sum_{\theta=3}^{k+1}\min_{i}r(\lambda_{\theta}|i)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\min_{i}r(\lambda|i),

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\{r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}|i)\}_{\lambda^{\prime},i})=\tilde{K}(\{r(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}),

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(\{r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}|i)\}_{\lambda^{\prime},i})\geq\tilde{H}(\{r(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}).

\displaystyle|r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}_{\theta}|i)-r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}_{\theta}|j)|=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|(r(\lambda_{1}|i)+r(\lambda_{2}|i))-(r(\lambda_{1}|j)+r(\lambda_{2}|j))|\quad&&(\theta=1)\\
&|r(\lambda_{\theta+1}|i)-r(\lambda_{\theta+1}|j)|\quad&&(\theta=2,\ldots,k),\end{aligned}\right.

and

\displaystyle|(r(\lambda_{1}|i)+r(\lambda_{2}|i))-(r(\lambda_{1}|j)+r(\lambda_{2}|j))|\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\leq|r(\lambda_{1}|i)-r(\lambda_{1}|j)|+|r(\lambda_{2}|i)-r(\lambda_{2}|j)|,

we have

and thus
F(\{r^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}|i)\}_{\lambda^{\prime},i})\leq F(\{r(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})

Together with the assumption

.
Since

and

, we can conclude

.

Finally, we prove that the claim

holds also for

with countably infinite

(note that in this case the definition of

To see this, we first confirm that all the quantities in (
24

.
In fact, for example, we can see that the right hand side of

is definite because
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\min_{i}p(\lambda|i)\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda|i)=1
.
Now we construct a family of tables
\{\{p_{\alpha}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda,i=1,\ldots,4}\}_{\alpha=1,2,\ldots}
by

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(\lambda|i)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &p(\lambda_{\alpha}|i)\quad&&(\lambda=\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\alpha-1})\\
&1-\sum_{\beta=1}^{\alpha-1}p_{\alpha}(\lambda_{\beta}|i)\quad&&(\lambda=\lambda_{\alpha})\\
&0\quad&&(\lambda=\lambda_{\alpha+1},\lambda_{\alpha+2},\ldots).\end{aligned}\right.

We note that each

.
Then, because

\displaystyle\tilde{K}\left(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\min_{i}p(\lambda|i)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\min_{i}p_{\alpha}(\lambda|i)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\tilde{K}\left(\{p_{\alpha}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}\right)

holds for example, we have
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}F(\{p_{\alpha}(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})=F(\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i})

is concluded.

In this part, we prove the existence of a factorizable output

We first consider auxiliary outputs

\displaystyle\underline{O}^{{M,H,S}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\left\{\underline{p}^{{M,H,S}}\left(a|x,\lambda\right)\right\}_{a},\left\{\underline{p}^{{M,H,S}}\left(b|y,\lambda\right)\right\}_{b}\right),

\displaystyle\overline{O}^{{M,H,S}}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S\left(I^{M,H,S},\underline{O}^{M,H,S}\right)&=0,\\
S\left(I^{M,H,S},\overline{O}^{M,H,S}\right)&=\bar{S}\end{aligned}

The first auxiliary output
\underline{O}^{M,H,S}
is constructed via

\displaystyle\underline{p}\left(a|x,\lambda\right)=1/2\ \ ,\underline{p}\left(b|y,\lambda\right)=1/2,

\displaystyle\forall a,b=\pm 1,\forall x\in\left\{0,1\right\},\forall\lambda=\lambda_{1},\dots.\lambda_{n},

because it lets all the expectation values in (

On the other hand, since we have

\displaystyle\min_{i}{p^{M,H,S}(\lambda_{l}|i)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}p^{M,H,S}(\lambda_{l}|1)&\left(1\leq l\leq n_{0}\right)\\

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(I^{M,H,S}\right)

\displaystyle=4-2\sum_{l=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}p^{{M,H,S}}\left(\lambda_{i(n_{0}-1)+l}|i\right)

\displaystyle p^{{M,H,S}}_{t_{A}}\left(a|x,\lambda\right)

Appendix B
Proof of (
14
)

In this appendix, under the same notation as Appendix
A
, we prove that

\displaystyle H\left(\left\{p\left(\lambda|i\right)\right\}_{\lambda,i}\right)\geq\frac{1}{8}M\left(\left\{p\left(\lambda|i\right)\right\}_{\lambda,i}\right)

(37)

holds for any input
\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}
.
If
n:=\#(\Lambda)=1
, then, as we have seen in Appendix
A
,
H=M=0
holds, and thus (
37
) is verified.
The proof for
n\geq 2
is again given by induction for the number of the hidden variables
n
.
We note that the claim (
37
) can be rewritten explicitly as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\max_{\lambda}\sum_{i}p(\lambda|i)+\frac{1}{8}\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|\leq 1,

(38)

and it is easy to see that the quantities
\max_{\lambda}\sum_{i}p(\lambda|i)
and
\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|
are invariant under permutations of the labels
i
and
\lambda
.
We first examine the case
n=2
.
Any input
I=\{\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},i=1,2,3,4}
is expressed as

\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}&i=1&i=2&i=3&i=4\\
\hline\cr\lambda_{1}&p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}&p_{4}\\
\lambda_{2}&1-p_{1}&1-p_{2}&1-p_{3}&1-p_{4}\end{array}\right].

In this expression, we can assume
p_{1}\geq p_{2}
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}\leq 4-\sum_{i}p_{i}
, and
\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|=\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|
without loss of generality.
Then, since

\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|=|p_{1}-p_{2}|+|p_{1}-p_{2}|=2(p_{1}-p_{2})

holds, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\max_{\lambda}\sum_{i}p(\lambda|i)+\frac{1}{8}\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i}p_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{4}(p_{1}-p_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{4}(2p_{2}+p_{3}+p_{4})\leq 1.

We next suppose that (
38
) holds for any inputs with
n=k\ (\geq 2)
, and consider an input
\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}
with
n=k+1
.
By means of suitable permutations, this input
\{p(\lambda|i)\}_{\lambda,i}
can be assumed to satisfy
\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|=\sum_{\lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|
, and
p(\lambda_{1}|1)\geq p(\lambda_{1}|2)
and
p(\lambda_{2}|1)\geq p(\lambda_{2}|2)
.
We note that the latter condition is valid because
n=k+1\geq 3
.
Now we define an input
\{p^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}|i)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{\prime},i=1,\ldots,4}
with
\Lambda^{\prime}=\{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\}
(
n=k
) by

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}_{\theta}|i)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &p(\lambda_{1}|i)+p(\lambda_{2}|i)\quad&&(\theta=1)\\
&p(\lambda_{\theta+1}|i)\quad&&(\theta=2,\ldots,k).\end{aligned}\right.

It follows easily from this definition of
\{p^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}_{\theta}|i)\}_{\lambda^{\prime},i}
that

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{i}p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)\geq\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sum_{i}p(\lambda|i).

(39)

To evaluate the second term of the l.h.s. of (
38
),
it should be noted that

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|j)|

\displaystyle=|p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|j)|+\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}\backslash\{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|j)|

\displaystyle\leq|p(\lambda_{1}|1)-p(\lambda_{1}|2)|+|p(\lambda_{2}|1)-p_{i}(\lambda_{2}|2)|+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\backslash\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|

holds for any
(i,j)
, where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality and the second one from the assumption imposed on
\{p(\lambda_{\theta}|i)\}_{\lambda,i}
.
While this implies

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|j)|\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|,

we can show

\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|1)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|2)|=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|,

i.e.,

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|j)|=\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|.

\displaystyle|p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|1)-p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|2)|

\displaystyle=|(p(\lambda_{1}|1)+p(\lambda_{2}|1))-(p(\lambda_{1}|2)+p_{i}(\lambda_{2}|2))|

\displaystyle=|(p(\lambda_{1}|1)-p(\lambda_{1}|2))+(p(\lambda_{2}|1)-p_{i}(\lambda_{2}|2))|

\displaystyle=|p(\lambda_{1}|1)-p(\lambda_{1}|2)|+|p(\lambda_{2}|1)-p_{i}(\lambda_{2}|2)|

holds due to the initial assumption
p(\lambda_{1}|1)\geq p(\lambda_{1}|2)
and
p(\lambda_{2}|1)\geq p(\lambda_{2}|2)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|1)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|2)|

\displaystyle=|p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|1)-p(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}|2)|+\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}\backslash\{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|1)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|2)|

\displaystyle=|p(\lambda_{1}|1)-p(\lambda_{1}|2)|+|p(\lambda_{2}|1)-p_{i}(\lambda_{2}|2)|+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\backslash\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}}|p(\lambda|i)-p(\lambda|j)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|p(\lambda|1)-p(\lambda|2)|.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\max_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{i}p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)+\frac{1}{8}\max_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|p(\lambda^{\prime}|i)-p(\lambda^{\prime}|j)|

\displaystyle\leq 1.
