Electroweak Monopole-Antimonopole Pair Production at LHC
Petr Beneš, Filip Blaschke, Y. M. Cho

Abstract
One of the urgent issues in high energy physics is the experimental confirmation of the electroweak monopole predicted by the standard model, and currently MoEDAL at LHC is actively searching for the monopole. However, the present LHC cannot produce the monopole if the mass is bigger than 7 TeV, while the monopole mass is expected to be around M W / α ≃ 11 ​ TeV M_{W}/\alpha\simeq 11~\text{TeV} . In this paper we discuss how LHC could circumbent this energy constraint and produce the monopole even when the mass becomes bigger than 7 TeV, based on the following ideas. First, in the topological production of the monopole the baby monopole mass at creation could be considerably smaller than the adolescent mass. Second, the binding energy of the monopole-antimonopole pair could effectively reduce the mass of the bound state. We discuss how these ideas can actually be realized at LHC to produce the monopole pairs. In particular, we argue that LHC could produce the baby electroweak monopoles whose mass could be around 5.3 TeV, smaller than the adolescent monopole mass around 11.0 TeV. Moreover, we show that LHC could produce the monopolium bound state with mass around 2.5 TeV, even when the total mass of the baby monopole-antimonopole pair becomes around 10.6 TeV. Our analysis could play an important role for MoEDAL experiment.

Introduction

With the advent of the Dirac’s monopole the magnetic monopole has become an obsession in physics, experimentally as well as theoretically
Dirac 1931
;
Dirac 1948
;
Cabrera 1982
. After
the Dirac monopole we have had the Wu-Yang monopole,
the ’t Hooft-Polyakov monopole, the grand unification
monopole

;
Wu and Yang 1975
;
Cho 1980
;
Cho 1982
;
’t Hooft 1974
;
Polyakov 1974
;
Prasad and Sommerfield 1975
;
Dokos and Tomaras 1980
.
But the electroweak monopole (also known as the Cho-Maison monopole), despite the initial skepticism on the existence
of such monopole in the standard model
Coleman 1985
;
Vachaspati and Barriola 1992
, stands out as
the most realistic monopole that could exist
in nature and could actually be detected
Cho and Maison 1997
;
Yang 1998
;
Yang
.

Indeed the Dirac monopole in electrodynamics should transform
to the electroweak monopole after the unification of
the electromagnetic and weak interactions, and the Wu-Yang
monopole in QCD is supposed to make the monopole condensation
to confine the color. Moreover, the ’tHooft-Polyakov monopole
exists only in an hypothetical theory, and the grand unification monopole which could have been amply produced
at the grand unification scale in the early universe probably has become completely irrelevant at present universe after
the inflation
Guth 1981
;
Linde 1982
.

This makes the experimental confirmation of the electroweak
monopole one of the most urgent issues in the standard
model after the discovery of the Higgs particle. In fact
the detection of this monopole, not the Higgs particle,
should be regarded as the final (and topological) test
of the standard model. For this reason the newest MoEDAL
detector at LHC is actively searching for the monopole
Acharya et al. 2017
;
Acharya et al. 2019
;
Aad et al. 2020
;
Acharya et al. 2022
.

To detect the electroweak monopole at LHC,
we need to recall basic facts about the monopole
Cho and Maison 1997
;
Yang 1998
;
Cho et al. 2015
;
Ellis et al. 2016
;
Blaschke and Beneš 2018
;
Blaschke and Beneš 2022
;
Cho 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020
. First,
this is the monopole which exists within (not beyond)
the standard model as the electroweak generalization of
the Dirac monopole, which can be viewed as a hybrid
between Dirac and ’t Hooft-Polyakov monopoles. Second,
the magnetic charge of the monopole is not
2\pi/e
but
4\pi/e
, twice that of the Dirac monopole. This is
because the period of the electromagnetic U(1) subgroup
of the standard model is
4\pi
, since the electromagnetic
U(1) (partly) comes from the U(1) subgroup of SU(2).
Third, the mass of the monopole
M
is of the order of several TeV, probably between 4 to 10 TeV. This is because the mass basically comes from the same Higgs mechanism
which makes the W boson massive, except that here
the coupling is magnetic (i.e.,
4\pi/e
). This makes
the monopole mass
1/\alpha
times heavier than
the W boson mass, around 11.0 TeV. Despite this, the size
of the monopole is set by the weak boson masses. This is because the monopole solution has the Higgs and W boson dressing which has the exponential damping fixed by
the weak boson masses.

Because of these distinctive features, MoEDAL detector
could identify the monopole without much difficulty,
if LHC could produce it. However, the 14 TeV LHC may have
no chance to produce the monopole if the mass becomes
larger than 7 TeV. This is problematic, because the monopole
mass could turn out to be bigger than this, around 11.0 TeV.
If this is the case, we may have little chance to detect
the monopole at LHC. In this case we may have to try
to detect the remnant monopoles at present universe produced
in the early universe
Cho 2019
, or wait for the next
upgrade of LHC.

However, two observations could make the monopole signal
at LHC non-zero even when the mass becomes over 7 TeV.
First, the monopole has to be created in pairs at LHC.
This implies that at the initial stage, the monopoles
could appear in the form of the atomic monopolium states
made of the monopole and antimonopole, or the quantized
magnetic flux of the monopole-antimonopole pair. In this
case there is the possibility that the binding energy
could reduce the bound state of monopole-antimonopole
pair below 14 TeV, even if the monopole mass becomes
bigger than 7 TeV.

The second point is related to the electroweak monopole
production mechanism at LHC. At present there are three
contending monopole production mechanisms. Two popular
ones in the high energy physics community are the Drell-Yan
(and two photon fusion) process
Acharya et al. 2017
;
Acharya et al. 2019

and the Schwinger mechanism
Acharya et al. 2022
;
Schwinger 1951
;
Gould et al. 2019
.
The third one is the topological monopole production
mechanism, in which the thermal fluctuation of the Higgs
vacuum which takes place during the electroweak phase
transition produces the monopole
Cho 2019
. LHC could
produce the monopole with this mechanism because
the fireball of the p-p collision could re-create
the electroweak phase transition in the early universe,
and thus produces the monopoles topologically by
the thermal fluctuation of the Higgs vacuum.

A unique feature of this topological thermal fluctuation mechanism is that this could allow LHC to produce
the electroweak monopole even when the mass becomes heavier
than 7 TeV. To understand this point we first have to
keep in mind that the monopole mass we have been talking
about is the mass at the zero temperature. However, in this
thermal production the monopole mass depends on
the temperature, so that at the creation the initial
monopole mass could be smaller than the zero temperature
mass
Cho 2019
. This tells that we have to distinguish the finite temperature monopole mass
from the zero temperature monopole mass. So, from
now on we will call the initial monopole mass (the monopole mass at birth) as the “baby” monopole mass and
the zero temperature mass as the “adolescent” monopole
mass.

The purpose of this paper is to discuss
how LHC can accommodate the above ideas and produce
the electroweak monopoles even when the monopole mass
exceeds 7 TeV. We show that in the topological thermal production the monopole production at LHC starts
from
T_{c}\simeq 147~\text{TeV}
and stops at

T_{min}^{LHC}\simeq 119~\text{TeV}
before
the Ginzburg temperature
T_{G}\simeq 57.5~\text{GeV}
,
with the monopole mass around 5.3 TeV. Moreover, we
show that LHC could produce the monopolium bound
states of mass around 5.3 TeV, even though the mass
of the monopole-antimonopole pair becomes 22.0 TeV.

As we will see, just after the phase transition the baby monopole mass could be as low as 1.0 TeV. This would
have been impossible if LHC produces the monopole by Drell-Yan or Schwinger mechanism, where the concept
of the temperature dependent monople mass does not exist.
On the other hand, we emphasize that the monopole
production in the early universe and the monopole
production at LHC have important differences. First,
the monopole production in the early universe stops
around the Ginzburg temperature, while the monopole production at LHC stops at higher temperature. This is because at the present LHC the monopole mass can not
exceed 7 TeV. So LHC has less monopole production time. Second, the size of the thermal radiation bath in
the early universe is limited only by the horizon
distance, but the size of the fireball at LHC
in p-p collision is fixed by the proton size which is
very small. Because of this, the monopole in the early
universe has unlimited energy nearby but the monopole
at LHC has very limited energy coming from the p-p
fireball. These differences play important roles in
the monopole production at LHC.

The existence of a monopole-antimonopole bound state,
the metastable quantized magnetic flux made of Cho-Maison monopole-antimonopole pair, has recently been confirmed
Zhu et al. 2022
. It has the expected pole separation length around
8.2/M_{W}
and has the magnetic dipole
moment roughly
8.6/M_{W}
. This extended size (length)
of the magnetic flux suggests that the energy of the pair
might be bigger than the sum of the monopole and
antimonopole masses. This implies that LHC might need
more energy to produce the magnetic flux made of
the monopole-antimonopole pair than to produce
the monopole and antimonopole separately.

This makes the monopolium bound state a more probable
signal for the monopole production at LHC.
The monopolium itself has been studied before for various reasons
Hill 1983
;
Epele et al. 2008
;
Nambu 1977
. For example, Nambu proposed to view the monopolium as a model
of strong interaction, because the magnetic coupling of
the monopole-antionopole pair drastically increases
the energy spectrum to the order of hundred MeV, the mass
scale of the low lying hadrons
Nambu 1977
. But
the electroweak monopolium made of the Cho-Maison monopole
pair has not been studied before.

Our study on the electroweak monopolium tells the followings. First, the naive atomic model of monopolium
does not describe a realistic monopolium, because it
has unrealistically big binding energy and too small
size. This is because the magnetic Coulombic coupling
of the monopole-antimonopole pair is too strong and
the monopole mass is too big.

But we could avoid these difficulties and have a realistic
electroweak monopolium modifying the magnetic potential
at short distance. As we will see, in this modification
we can show that the binding energy reduces the monopolium mass from 22.0 TeV to 5.3 TeV. This is precisely what we hoped for. With this we could conclude that LHC could produce the electroweak monopolium of the mass considerably lighter than 14 TeV without much difficulty. In this case we could try to detect the monopolium bound state experimentally detecting the decay modes.

The standard model provides another monopole production mechanism,
the topological monopole production generated by the thermal
fluctuation of the Higgs vacuum which induces the change of topology,
which is the electroweak monopole production mechanism in the early
universe

produced in the course of the electroweak phase transition by
thermal fluctuations of the Higgs vacuum, which takes

A remarkable feature of this mechanism is that the monopole
production is not controlled by any fundamental interaction,
but by thermal fluctuation which does not involve any fundamental
constant. Perhaps more importantly, in this thermal fluctuation
the monopole mass at creation (the baby monopole mass) could be
considerably smaller than the adolescent monopole mass. This is
because in this thermal production, the monopole mass depends on
the temperature at creation. As we will see in the following this
could have a deep impact on the monopole production at LHC.

If so, what is the the monopole production mechanism
at LHC? We do not know yet. On the other hand, it has
often been claimed that LHC should reproduce the early
universe. If this is true, the monopole production mechanism
at LHC could be topological, just like the electroweak
monopole production the early universe. In this paper
we will adopt this view and discuss how LHC could produce
the electroweak monopole. For this we have to understand
the electroweak monopole production in the early universe.

III
Monopole Production in Early Universe: A Review

It has generally been believed that the monopole production
in the early universe critically depends on the type of
the phase transition. In the first order phase transition
the vacuum bubble collisions in the unstable vacuum are
supposed to create the monopoles through the quantum
tunneling to the stable vacuum during the phase transition,
so that the monopole production is supposed to be suppressed
exponentially by the vacuum tunneling
Guth 1981
.
On the other hand the monopole production in the second
order phase transition is supposed to be described by
the Kibble-Zurek mechanism with no exponential suppression
Kibble 1976
;
Zurek 1996
.

We emphasize, however, that this popular view may have
a critical defect
Cho 2019
. This is because in
the topological monopole production mechanism the thermal fluctuation of the Higgs vacuum which provides the seed
of the monopoles continues untill the temperature drops
to the Ginzburg temperature
Ginzburg 1960
. This means that, even in the first order phase transition we can have
the monopole production after the vacuum bubble tunneling without the exponential suppression, if the Ginzburg temperature becomes less than the critical temperature.
In this case the monopole production in the first order phase transition becomes qualitatively the same as in
the second order phase transition. This tells that
the popular exponential suppression of the monopole production in the first order phase transition is only
half of the full story which could be totally misleading.

In the second order phase transition the thermal fluctuation of the Higgs vacuum could also modify
the Kibble-Zurek mechanism considerably, because it provides more time for the monopole production as
the thermal fluctuation could continue long after
the phase transition. This tells that what is important
in the monopole production in the early universe is
the Ginzburg temperature, not the type of the phase transition.

IV
Electroweak monopoles from a fireball at LHC

If the fireballs made of the p-p (and heavy ion) collisions at LHC can reproduce the hot thermal bath of radiation
of the early universe as it is commonly believed, we
may assume that LHC produces the electroweak monopoles
by the same mechanism that the early universe does. In
this case, the above discussion suggests that LHC could produce the baby electroweak monopole of mass around
1.0 TeV just after
T_{c}
and produce the last monopole
of mass 10.4 TeV at the Ginzburg temperature
T_{G}
.

However, the 14 TeV LHC can not produce the monopole
of mass 10.4 TeV. This means that the present LHC
can not produce the monopole exactly as the early
universe does. In other words, although LHC could
reproduce the early universe, it could do so only
in a limited sense. This is because, unlike the big
bang fueled by the infinite energy, LHC can provide
only a finite energy. So we have to take care of this
energy constraint at LHC.

To do that, we estimate the temperature at which
the monopole production stops at the present LHC. Since
LHC should produce the monopole in pairs, the minimum temperature
T_{min}^{LHC}
at the 14 TeV LHC for
the monopole production must satisfy the energy condition

\displaystyle M_{max}=7~\text{TeV}\simeq\frac{\bar{M}_{W}(T_{min}^{LHC})}{\alpha}=\frac{g}{2}\frac{\rho_{+}(T_{min}^{LHC})}{\alpha}.

(37)

Solving this we find

\displaystyle T_{min}^{LHC}\simeq 119.4~{\rm GeV}.

(38)

This means that the energy condition to produce
the monopole mass no more than 7 TeV forces the monopole production at LHC to stop at 119.4 GeV, much higher that
the Ginzburg temperature. The effective potential at

T_{min}^{LHC}
is also shown in Fig.
3
for comparison.

If so, at LHC the electroweak monopole production starts
at
T_{c}
around 146.7 GeV and stops at
T_{min}^{LHC}

around 119.4 GeV, not at the Ginzburg temperature.
In average the monopole production temperature
at LHC is given by

\displaystyle T_{i}^{LHC}=\frac{T_{c}+T_{min}^{LHC}}{2}\simeq 132.9~\text{GeV},

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(T_{i}^{LHC})\simeq 119.2~\text{GeV},

(39)

not at
T_{i}
given by (
28
). This tells that
LHC starts to produce the electroweak monopole with
mass 1.0 TeV at around 146.7 GeV, and
stops producing the monopole with mass 7 TeV at
119.4 TeV. In average LHC produces the infant
electroweak monopole mass around 5.3 TeV, much less
than the adolescent mass 11.0 TeV. This implies that
the 14 TeV LHC could actually produce the electroweak monopole pair even when the mass of the monopole pair
becomes bigger than 14 TeV. This is remarkable.

It is generally believed that in the Kibble-Zurek mechanism we are supposed to have one monopole per one correlation volume. This assumotion, however, may have
a critical defect. This is because the correlation
length is fixed by the electroweak scale but
the monopole mass is given by the
1/\alpha
times
bigger than the electroweak scale, so that the energy
in one correlation volume may not be enough to make up
the monopole mass. A natural way to cure this defect is
to enlarge the correlation length
\xi=1/\bar{M}_{H}
to

\bar{\xi}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}=(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{1/3}~\xi\simeq 5.16\times\frac{1}{\bar{M}_{H}}.

(40)

With this we have the new correlation volume at

T_{i}^{LHC}
,

\displaystyle V_{c}\simeq\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}\bar{\xi}^{3}\simeq 0.76\times 10^{-43}cm^{3}.

(41)

In comparison the p-p fireball volume at the 14 TeV LHC
is given by (with the proton radius
0.87\times 10^{-13}~cm
)

\displaystyle V_{pp}\simeq\frac{0.938}{7000}\times\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}~(0.87)^{3}\times 10^{-39}cm^{3}

\displaystyle\simeq 1.16\times 10^{-42}cm^{3},

(42)

where the first term represents the Lorentz contraction
of the p-p fireball. Notice that
V_{c}
is about thirteen times bigger than
V_{pp}
. This tells that the p-p
fireball at LHC has just enough size to produce
the monopole.

We could translate the monopole production process
at LHC in time scale. From (
29
) we can say that
the electroweak monopole production at LHC starts at
146.7 GeV for the period of
0.7\times 10^{-11}~sec
.
This tells that the monopole production lasts only very
short period at LHC. So one might wonder if we have
enough thermal fluctuations of the Higgs vacuum during
this period. We can estimate how many times the Higgs
vacuum fluctuates from
\rho_{+}(T_{LHC})
to zero in
average. From the uncertainty principle the time

\Delta t
for one fluctuation is given by

\displaystyle\Delta t\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta E}\simeq 4.7\times 10^{-27}~sec,

(43)

so that the number of the fluctuation
N_{f}
of the Higgs
vacuum is given by

\displaystyle N_{f}\simeq\frac{\bar{t}}{\Delta t}\simeq 3.1\times 10^{16}.

(44)

This assures that we have enough fluctuations of
the Higgs vacuum to produce the monopoles at LHC.

The above discussion tells that LHC could produce the electroweak baby monopole which could have the mass considerably less than
the adolescent mass of 11.0 TeV, if we assume that the monopole
production mechanism at LHC is topological. This is unexpected.

If so, can we really detect the infant electroweak monopole
at LHC whose mass is smaller than
the adolescent mass? To answer this we have to keep
in mind that in the early universe most of
the monopole-antimonopole pairs are quickly annhilated
as soon as they are produced. This is because at
the production temperature the capture radius of
the monopole and anti-monopole is almost hundred times
bigger than the correlation length. As the result most
of the monopole-antimonopole pairs are annihilated
as soon as they are produced, and only about
10^{-5}

of the monopoles are survived in the early
universe
Cho 2019
. So one might wonder if
we could have the same annihilation of
the monopole-antimonopole pairs at LHC.

Actually, this annhilation is not expected to play
an important role at LHC. This is because (unlike
the early universe) the size of the p-p fireball at LHC
is only about thirteen times bigger than the correlation
volume, so that many of the monopoles could quickly escape
the fireball before they are annhilated. This must be
clear from (
41
) and (
42
). Moreover, once
they escape from the fireball they are quickly freezed out (i.e., “quenched”). And as far as we know, nothing can destroy these
quenched monopoles after they escape from the fireball. In this
case MoEDAL should be able to detect these infant monopoles
in principle.

On the other hand, some of the infant monopole-antimonopole pairs at LHC could settle down to form a monopolium bound state before they escape from the fireball. This necessiate us to study the monopolium bound state which we discuss in
the following.

V
Electroweak Monopolium: Atomic Model

Let the monopole mass be
M
and consider the Schrödinger
equation of the monopolium wave function

\displaystyle\Big(-\frac{1}{2\mu}\nabla^{2}+V\Big)\Psi=E~\Psi,

\displaystyle V=-\frac{4\pi}{e^{2}}\frac{1}{r},

(45)

where
\mu=M/2
is the reduced mass and
V
is
the Coulombic magnetic potential of
the monopole-antimonopole pair. Obviously this is
formally identical to the Schrödinger equation of
the Hydrogen atom, except that the coupling strenth
of the potential is replaced by
4\pi/e
and
the electron mass is replaced by
\mu
.

From this we have the energy spectrum

\displaystyle E_{n}=-\frac{M}{4\alpha^{2}n^{2}}=-\frac{4,692.3}{n^{2}}\times M

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{51,615.3}{n^{2}}~{\rm TeV},

(46)

where
\alpha=e^{2}/4\pi
is the fine structure constant
and we have put
M=M_{W}/\alpha\simeq 11.0~\text{TeV}
.
So the Rydburg energy of the monopolium becomes
51.615 TeV. Moreover, the Bohr radius
R
of
the monopolium is given by

\displaystyle R=\frac{\alpha}{\mu}\times\alpha^{2}=\frac{2\alpha^{3}}{M}\simeq\frac{5.6\times~10^{-9}}{M_{W}}

\displaystyle\simeq 1.4\times 10^{-24}~\mbox{cm},

(47)

Notice, however, that these results are totally
unreasonable. First, the above Rydburg energy is too
big (roughly 2,346 times bigger than two monopole
masses), which means that the monopole mass simply
can not provide the binding energy necessary to make
the monopolium. Second, the monopolium size (i.e.,
the Bohr radius) is
10^{-8}
times smaller than
the monopole size.

This could mean the followings. First, the monopolium
quickly annihilates itself, long before it reaches to
the ground state. In fact (
46
) suggests that
the monopolium could exist only at highly excited
levels, probably with
n\geq 49
, because below this
level the monopole-antimonopole mass can not provide
the necessary binding energy. Or else, this could mean
that the naive atomic description of the monopolium is
not acceptable. In this case we have to find a more
realistic model of the monopolium.

The reason for the above results originates from two facts.
First, the magnetic coupling of the monopole is too strong.
Second, the monopole mass is too big. We could make
the atomic model more realistic by improving on these points.
For example, we could modify the magnetic Coulombic potential
at short distances for
r\leq 1/M_{W}
, to accommodate
the fact that the monopole has a finite size
1/M_{W}
. Moreover, we could make the monopole mass less than
11 TeV because the monopole, at the initial stage of
the formation, has a temperature dependent mass that is
much less than the adolescent mass.

On the other hand, the above exercise has one positive side,
because it implies that the binding energy of the electroweak
monopolium could be big, of the order of TeV. This could make
the monopolium mass considerably lighter than
2M
, so that
we could have the monopolium of mass less than 14 TeV even
when the mass of monopole-antimonopole pair is heavier
than 14 TeV. With these modifications, we could have
a more realistic model of the electroweak monopolium
that we discuss now.

VI
Electroweak Monopolium: A Realistic Model

To have a realistic model of the electroweak monopolium,
we have to remember that intuitively the size of the electroweak
monopolium can not be smaller than
2/M_{W}
, because the monopole
(and antimonopole) has the size of
1/M_{W}
. This means that
at short distances the singularity of the magnetic potential
in (
45
) should be regularized.

So we modify the potential
V
at short distance with
a harmonic oscillator potential smoothly connected at
r_{0}
,
and let

\displaystyle V=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dfrac{2\pi}{e^{2}r_{0}}\Big(\dfrac{r^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}-3\Big),&~~~r<r_{0},\\
\\
-\dfrac{4\pi}{e^{2}}\dfrac{1}{r},&~~~r\geq r_{0},\end{array}\right.

where
r_{0}
is of the order of
1/M_{W}
. The potential is shown
in Fig.
6
in the unit of
r_{0}
.

The significance of this potential is that the coupling constant

. This immediately tells that

M_{W}/2\alpha=M/2
,

46

the Schrödinger equation keeping the reduced mass

\mu

allowing
\mu

The first step is the usual separation of variables
for wave functions in the regions
r<r_{0}
(region I)

r>r_{0}
(region II) as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm{I,II}}(r,\varphi,\theta)\equiv\frac{R_{\rm{I,II}}(r)}{r}Y_{l}^{m}(\varphi,\theta)\,,

(51)

where

are the spherical

\displaystyle r=\frac{2\alpha}{M}x\,~~~~r_{0}=\frac{2\alpha}{M}x_{0},~~~~E\equiv-\frac{M}{4\alpha^{2}}\epsilon,

(52)

where we denoted dimensionless binding energy as

\epsilon>0
. In these coordinates, the relevant Schrödinger equations for the radial parts read

\displaystyle\ddot{R}_{\rm I}-\Big(\frac{l(l+1)}{x^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{x_{0}^{3}}-\frac{3}{x_{0}}+\epsilon\Big)R_{\rm I}=0,

\displaystyle\ddot{R}_{\rm II}-\Big(\frac{l(l+1)}{x^{2}}-\frac{2}{x_{0}}+\epsilon\Big)R_{\rm II}=0.

(53)

Both of these can be mapped to the Kummer’s equation,

\displaystyle z\frac{d^{2}w(z)}{dz^{2}}+(b-z)\frac{dw(z)}{dz}-aw(z)=0,

(54)

by suitable choices of
z\equiv z(x)
and parameters

a

In particular, this mapping is facilitated via assignments,

\displaystyle R_{\rm I}=x^{l+1}\exp\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2x_{0}^{3/2}}\Big)w(x^{2}/x_{0}^{3/2}),

\displaystyle a_{\rm I}=\frac{2l+3+x_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon-3\sqrt{x_{0}}}{4},~~~~b_{\rm I}=l+3/2,

\displaystyle R_{\rm II}=x^{l+1}\exp\big(-\sqrt{\epsilon}x\big)w(2\sqrt{\epsilon}x),

\displaystyle a_{\rm II}=l+1-\epsilon^{-1/2},\quad b_{\rm II}=2l+2.

(55)

As is well-known, the Kummer’s equation has two independent solutions, the confluent hypergeometric function
M(a,b,z)\equiv\,_{1}F_{1}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\bigr|z\bigr)
and the Tricomi’s
function
U(a,b,z)
.

In the region I (
x<x_{0}
), we need to satisfy
the regularity condition at the origin
x=0
, which
force us to exclude
U(a,b,z)
as it has a singularity
at
z=0
in contrast to
M(a,b,z)
, which is an entire
function in
z
. Hence, the general solution in
the first region that is regular at the origin is
given by

\displaystyle R_{\rm I}=N_{\rm I}\,x^{l+1}\exp\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2x_{0}^{3/2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\times_{1}F_{1}\Big(\begin{matrix}\frac{l}{2}+\frac{3}{4}+\frac{x_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon-3\sqrt{x_{0}}}{4}\\
l+\frac{3}{2}\end{matrix}\Bigr|\tfrac{x^{2}}{x_{0}^{3/2}}\Big),

(56)

where

is an arbitrary constant at the moment.

In the region II (

that behaves as

Notice that this asymptotic behaviour holds only if

is not a negative integer (and hence

blows

For our modified potential, however, the spectrum
differs and hence

is a positive integer.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle U(a,b,x)\sim x^{-a}\,_{2}F_{0}\bigl(\begin{matrix}a&a-b+1\\
&\end{matrix}\bigr|-1/x\bigr),

(57)

as
x
goes to infinity. Hence, the general solution in
the second region has a following form,

\displaystyle R_{\rm II}=N_{\rm II}\,x^{l+1}\exp\big(-\sqrt{\epsilon}x\big)

\displaystyle\times U\Big(\begin{matrix}l+1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\\
2l+2\end{matrix}\Bigr|2\sqrt{\epsilon}x\Big),

(58)

where

is again an arbitrary constant.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{HO}=\frac{3\sqrt{x_{0}}-3-2l-4n}{x_{0}^{3/2}},~~~~n=0,1,\ldots

(61)

The value of
x_{0}
we are interested is given by

r_{0}\sim 1/M_{W}
, or

\displaystyle x_{0}=\frac{M}{2\alpha}r_{0}\simeq 7,767,173.

(62)

For such a high values of
x_{0}
, the binding energies are
almost indistinguishable from binding energies of harmonic oscillator.

This means that the ground state energy of the monopolium (for
l=0
) is roughly

\displaystyle E_{0}=-\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{4\alpha^{2}}~M\simeq-4,692\times(M/\text{TeV})~\epsilon_{HO}^{n,l=0}

\displaystyle\simeq-16.8~\text{TeV}.

(63)

where we have put
M\simeq 11.0~\text{TeV}
. Compared
with (

), this is roughly
3\times 10^{-4}

times reduction of the naive estimate of the binding
energy given by hydrogen-like atomic model of
the monopolium. In particular, this tells that
the monopole-antimonopole pair with mass 22.0 TeV
makes the monopolium bound state of mass of 5.2 TeV
at the ground state. This is precisely what we have
hoped for.

Furthermore, the Bohr radius of the monopolium, which

R_{\rm II}
from (

\displaystyle R=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|E_{0}|}}\simeq 1.5\times 10^{-18}~cm.

(64)

This is
0.6\times 10^{-2}
times smaller than
the monopole size given by

1/M_{W}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-16}~cm
. Again, this is
definitely much more realistic than (

confirms that the monopolium bound state with the modified potential (

energy which could help LHC to produce the bound state
even when the monopole mass becomes bigger than 7 TeV.

Notice that in the above analysis we have assumed
that the monopole mass is 11 TeV. However, at LHC
the monopole mass at
T_{min}^{LHC}
becomes 5.3 TeV,
so that the monopolium at LHC is expected to be

It should be mentioned that a different model of

\displaystyle V=-\frac{4\pi}{e^{2}}\frac{1}{r}\Big(1-\exp(-r/2r_{0})\Big),

VII
Discussion

An urgent task at LHC is to discover the electroweak monopole. Because of the unique charactistics of
the electroweak monopole, the MoEDAL detector may have
no difficulty to detect the monopole, if LHC could
produce it. But the problem is that the present 14 TeV
LHC may not be able to produce the monopole, if
the monopole mass becomes bigger than 7 TeV.
And indeed, if we suppose the monopole mass is

1/\alpha
times the W boson mass, LHC cannot produce
it. In this case the detection of the monopole at LHC
may become hopeless, and we may have to look for
the remnant electroweak monopoles which could have been produced in the early universe after the electroweak
phase transition.

Our work in this paper tells that, if LHC produces the monopole by the topological thermal fluctuation of
the Higgs vacuum, LHC could circumbent this energy
constraint and produce the electroweak monopole even
when the monopole mass becomes 11.0 TeV. This is because
in this thermal production the monopole mass depends
on the temperature at production. In this case the mass
of the monopoles produced right after the electroweak
phase transition (the baby monopole mass) could be as
low as 1.0 TeV, even when the monopole mass at zero temperature (the adolescent monopole mass) becomes
11.0 TeV.

So, if the monopole production mechanism
at LHC is the thermal fluctuation of the Higgs vacuum
(the same mechanism as the monopole production in
the early universe), the present 14 TeV LHC could produce
the electroweak monopole, and thus MoEDAL could detect it.
Our results suggest that LHC could produce the infant monopoles of mass around 5.3 TeV in average (assuming
the adolescent monopole mass is 11 TeV). This is really remarkable, because this implies that there is a finite possibility that the MoEDAL detector at LHC could
actually detect the infant electroweak monopoles, even though it is not likely to detect the adolescent (i.e., grown-up) monopoles.

It should be emphasized that this is a unique feature
of the finite temperature physics which is absent in
the zero temperature physics. In particular, this is impossible if the monopole production mechanism
at LHC is the Drell-Yan or Schwinger mechanism, where
only the adolescent monopole enters. From this we can
definitely say that the detection of the infant monopole
at LHC should be interpreted as the confirmation that
the electroweak monopole production mechanism is indeed topological.

Another important result of our work is that there is
a real possibility for LHC to produce the monopoles
in the form of the monopolia. When LHC produces
the monopoles, it should produce them in pairs. But
since they have strong magnetic attraction some of
them could form bound states, although many of
the monopole-antimonopole pairs could quickly annihilate.
An interesting feature of this monopolium is that
the mass could be considerably lighter than the mass
of the monopole-antimonopole pair, because
the monopolium binding energy could be of the order
of ten TeV. In fact, our study in this paper suggests
that the binding energy of the ground state could be
as much as 16.7 TeV, so that the monopole-antimonopole pair of the mass 22.0 TeV could form a bound state
of the mass 5.3 TeV. This provides another way for LHC
to circumbent the energy constraint and produce
the monopole pairs.

This tells that there are two ways
for LHC to produce the monopole pairs, the monopole
and antimonopole as independent particles or
the monopole-antimonopole pair together as a monopolium
bound state. So MoEDAL and ATLAS could try to detect
the monopole (and antimonopole) separately, or together
as
a
monopolium.

Detecting the monopole (and antimonopole) should be straightforward in principle, but detecting
the monopolium could be tricky. This is because
the monopolium has zero magnetic charge, so that we
have to look for the magnetically neutral particles
of mass of few TeV. Moreover, the monopole and
antimonopole inside the monopolium could easily
annihilate to photons and electron-positron pairs.
In this case we may have to look for the decay modes
of the monopolium.

One might wonder if we could also have the infant monopoles in cosmology produced in the early universe. This may not be possible, because the monopoles
in the early universe are in contact with the hot thermal radiation bath which feeds up the energy and allows them
to grow up. This means that the remnant monopoles from
the early universe that we could detect at present
are expected to be the adolescent monopoles.
This implies that the quenched infant monopole at LHC
is not a generic phenomenon but an outcome of the limited energy and size of the p-p fireball at LHC.

Admittedly the idea of the infant monopole at LHC sounds strange and appears hard to swallow. We emphasize, however, that this idea actually comes from the fact
that in the electroweak phase transition the W (and Higg) boson mass becomes temperature dependent. Once we accept this, the idea of the infant monopole follows naturally. Moreover, it appears that (as far as we know) logically
it is difficult to exclude this possibility. Ultimately, of course, only experiment could tell whether this idea
is realistic or not.

We hope that our results in this paper could help
MoEDAL to detect the electroweak monopole at LHC.
