Maximal intrinsic randomness of a quantum state
Shuyang Meng, Fionnuala Curran, Gabriel Senno, Victoria J. Wright, Máté Farkas, Valerio Scarani, Antonio Acín

Abstract
One of the most counterintuitive aspects of quantum theory is its claim that there is ‘intrinsic’ randomness in the physical world. Quantum information science has greatly progressed in the study of intrinsic, or secret, quantum randomness in the past decade. With much emphasis on device-independent and semi-device-independent bounds, one of the most basic questions has escaped attention: how much intrinsic randomness can be extracted from a given state ρ \rho , and what measurements achieve this bound? We answer this question for three different randomness quantifiers: the conditional min-entropy, the conditional von Neumann entropy and the conditional max-entropy. For the first, we solve the min-max problem of finding the projective measurement that minimises the maximal guessing probability of an eavesdropper. The result is that one can guarantee an amount of conditional min-entropy H min ∗ = − log 2 ​ P guess ∗ ​ ( ρ ) H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}=-\log_{2}P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho) with P guess ∗ ​ ( ρ ) = 1 d ​ ( tr ⁡ ρ ) 2 P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}\,\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2} by performing suitable projective measurements. For the conditional von Neumann entropy, we find that the maximal value is H ∗ = log 2 ​ d − S ⁡ ( ρ ) H^{*}=\log_{2}d-S(\rho) , with S ⁡ ( ρ ) S(\rho) the von Neumann entropy of ρ \rho , while for the conditional max-entropy, we find the maximal value H max ∗ = log 2 ​ d + log 2 ​ λ max ​ ( ρ ) H^{*}_{\textnormal{max}}=\log_{2}d+\log_{2}{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho) , where λ max ​ ( ρ ) {\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho) is the largest eigenvalue of ρ \rho . Optimal values for H min ∗ H^{*}_{\textrm{min}} , H ∗ H^{*} and H max ∗ H^{*}_{\textnormal{max}} are achieved by measuring in any basis that is unbiased with respect to the eigenbasis of ρ \rho , as well as by other, less intuitive, measurements.



I
Introduction

One of the core differences between classical and quantum physics is the latter’s probabilistic character, which is irreducible to ignorance of underlying variables. This difference has fundamental implications for our worldview, but it is also attractive as a natural source of randomness for practical uses. Indeed, Geiger counting was already used as a source of physical randomness in the second half of the 20th century. In the past two decades, with the development of quantum information science, a large number of quantum random number generators (QRNGs) have been designed, and many have been implemented, usually with light (see
Herrero-Collantes and Garcia-Escartin 2017
;
Vaisakh Mannalath 2022
for comprehensive reviews). The
amount of randomness
is naturally captured by the
guessing probability

P_{\textnormal{guess}}
: the higher the probability that the random variable is guessed, the smaller the randomness. This intuitive characterisation was found to have operational meaning: the
min-entropy

H_{\textrm{min}}=-\log_{2}P_{\textnormal{guess}}
quantifies (informally) the fraction of perfect coin tosses that can be extracted from a string generated by the available source. But randomness is not an absolute notion: one has to specify
for whom
the source should be partly unpredictable. For mere sampling purposes, it might be sufficient to take the observed probabilities at face value; for cryptographic applications, however, one needs to estimate the probability that
an adversary, Eve,
guesses the outcomes. The resulting randomness is called
secret randomness
, or
intrinsic randomness
.

The computation of intrinsic randomness using quantum resources and against a quantum adversary has been studied from different perspectives. When considering a user with classical data correlated with quantum information in the hands of an adversary, the min-entropy quantifies the amount of perfect random bits that the user can establish
Konig et al. 2009
. The question was also addressed for the task of quantum key distribution, which is the extraction of secret
shared
randomness. It was in this context that the idea of device-independent certification was born: the possibility of bounding the amount of randomness in a black-box setting, based on the observation of Bell-nonlocal correlations
Acín et al. 2007
. Next, it was noticed that device-independent certification can be performed for randomness as well
Colbeck 2006
;
Pironio et al. 2010
, providing the first disruptive case for quantum randomness in a non-shared setting
1
1

1

As long as
process randomness
requires characterised devices, classical and quantum RNGs compete on the same grounds for speed, stability, practicality etc. But, given an alleged RNG as a black box, on classical devices, one can only test product randomness with statistical tests. While process randomness implies product randomness, the opposite is certainly not true: one could have recorded a long enough list of random numbers, and the device under study may just be reading deterministically from that record.
. This breakthrough happened as the race to demonstrate loophole-free Bell tests was taking up speed. There followed an explosion of designs and implementations of QRNGs certifiable under various assumptions, from device-independent (disruptive, but hard to implement), to semi-device-independent in various forms, to fully characterised (practical and fast, but requiring a precise modelling of the setups). For these developments, we refer to the reviews
Herrero-Collantes and Garcia-Escartin 2017
;
Bera et al. 2017
;
Acín and Masanes 2016
;
Vaisakh Mannalath 2022
.

In this flurry of activity, one of the most basic questions
was somehow left out:
how much secret randomness can be extracted from a known state
\rho
. In this paper, we solve this problem for three of the most natural and operational measures of randomness: the conditional min-entropy, the conditional von Neumann entropy and the conditional max-entropy. For the first, we show that the answer is
H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}=-\log_{2}P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)
, with

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}\,\big(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\big)^{2}\,,

(1)

where
d
is the dimension of the Hilbert space of the system, assumed to be finite. We find a family of measurements that generate this amount of randomness, which is closely related to the concept of ‘pretty good measurements’
Hausladen and Wootters 1994
, originally used as a close-to-optimal way to distinguish an ensemble of states. For the second, we find the maximal value

\displaystyle H^{*}=\log_{2}d-S(\rho)\,,

(2)

where
S(\rho)=-\tr\Big(\rho\log_2 \rho\Big)
is the von Neumman entropy of
\rho
, while for the third, we find

\displaystyle H^{*}_{\textnormal{max}}=\log_{2}d+\log_{2}{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)\,,

(3)

where
{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
.
Interestingly, for
d>2
, we find that some measurements maximise one of
H_{\textrm{min}}
,
H
and
H_{\textnormal{max}}
, but not the other two.

II
Qubit example

A case study will help to introduce the main ideas. Alice has a source that produces a qubit. She has characterised its state to the best of her knowledge and found it to be

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{1}+m\sigma_{z})=\frac{1+m}{2}\outerproduct{0}{0}+\frac{1-m}{2}\outerproduct{1}{1}

(4)

for some
0\leq m\leq 1
. If she measures
\sigma_{x}
, her observed statistics will be those of a perfect unbiased coin:
P_{A}(+1)=P_{A}(-1)=\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose now that what the source really does is produce a pure state in each round, specifically half of the rounds
\ket{\chi_+}
and half of the rounds
\ket{\chi_-}
, with

\ket{\chi_{\pm}}=\sqrt{\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}{2}}\ket{+x}+\sqrt{\frac{1\mp\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}{2}}\ket{-x}

(5)

(indeed,
\frac{1}{2}\ket{\chi_+}\bra{\chi_+}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{\chi_-}\bra{\chi_-}=\rho
). If Eve knows the working of the source exactly, she will guess
i=+1
(
i=-1
) in the rounds when the source sent out
\ket{\chi_+}
(
\ket{\chi_-}
). Her guess will then be correct with probability

P_{\textnormal{guess}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\Big)\,,

(6)

which is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{2}
when
m<1
(i.e. when
\rho
is mixed). Thus, the intrinsic randomness of Alice’s protocol is less than her apparent perfect randomness. In particular, there is no secret randomness in the state
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}
, since
P_{\textnormal{guess}}=1
for
m=0
.

As will be expanded on in what follows, two things are already known about this case study and its generalisation to higher dimensions. First: we presented this example with Eve having perfect classical information about the source, in the sense that she knows at each instance which state has been prepared and accordingly makes her guess on Alice’s measurement outcome. However, the result is unchanged if Eve holds quantum side-information. Eve then holds a purification of Alice’s state, and she measures her own system to guess Alice’s result. Since the two scenarios are equivalent in terms of the guessing probability, we will move from one to the other when convenient for the argumentation. Second: having fixed Alice’s protocol (both the state and the measurement), the maximisation of
P_{\textnormal{guess}}
over all decompositions of
\rho
is a known semidefinite program (SDP)
Law et al. 2014
; in the case study, we have presented the optimal decomposition. What is not known is whether
\sigma_{x}
is the best measurement for Alice, even in the presence of Eve: could another measurement on the same state
\rho
decrease Eve’s guessing probability, at the expense of biasing the observed
P_{A}
?
We set out to solve this min-max problem, and thus determine the maximal amount of secret randomness that can be extracted from
\rho
.

III
Setting of the problem

Alice holds a quantum state
\rho
from a Hilbert space of dimension
d
. We want to determine how much intrinsic randomness she can extract from
\rho
and which measurement achieves this maximum. We consider only
measurements
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{i}\}_{i}
which are projective, i.e.
M_{i}M_{j}=\delta_{ij}M_{i}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta (we discuss general POVMs at the end of this section). To quantify how intrinsically random, that is, how unpredictable, Alice’s measurement outcome is, one considers the existence of an eavesdropper, Eve, who has a more detailed knowledge than Alice about the process, but cannot actively influence it (she is ‘outside the lab’). Concretely, in every round, Eve knows the true state
\rho_{c}
produced by the source. Given this knowledge, she guesses the most likely outcome
i=i(c)
for that round. Without loss of generality, we can group together all of Eve’s states that lead to the same guessed outcome, since Eve does not gain anything in treating them as distinct. We denote by
\rho_{i}
the states seen by Eve, sub-normalised such that
q_{i}=\tr\rho_{i}
is the probability that Eve’s most likely outcome is
i
. These states must satisfy
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=\rho
.

Having set this stage, Eve’s average guessing probability is
P_{\textrm{guess}}\big(\{\rho_{i}\},\mathcal{M}\big)=\sum_{i}\tr(M_{i}\rho_i)
. Since we don’t know the true states
\rho_{i}
, we need to consider the worst case scenario, i.e. the decomposition that maximises Eve’s guessing probability,

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}}\sum_{i}\tr(M_i\rho_i)

(7)

s.t.

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\geq 0\,,\;\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=\rho\,.

This optimisation is an SDP, and so can be solved efficiently. In order to determine,
the maximal amount of secret randomness that can be extracted from the known state
\rho
, one need to optimize Eq. (
7
) over Alice’s measurement, i.e. compute

P_{\textrm{guess}}^{*}(\rho)=\min_{\mathcal{M}\in\Pi}P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})\,,

(8)

where
\Pi
is the set of all projective measurements.
Our main result is to show that Eq. (
1
) is the solution to the optimisation (
8
).

The search for an optimal measurement could have been extended to the larger set of Positive Operator-Valued Measures (POVMs), but the operational interpretation in our context is unclear. Recall that our goal is to quantify the secret randomness
in the state
\rho
. When implementing a POVM, however, the projective measurement acts on the given state
\rho
plus an auxiliary system, so part of the obtained randomness may come from the latter. In fact, for extremal measurements minimising the guessing probability, the auxiliary system has to be in a pure state, say
\ket{a}
, of dimension
d_{A}

Senno et al. 2023
. It follows from our main result that the maximal amount of randomness obtained when implementing a projective measurement on the global state is
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}\big(\rho\otimes\outerproduct{a}{a}\big)
. It is easy, however, to see that
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}\big(\rho\otimes\outerproduct{a}{a}\big)=\frac{1}{d_{A}}P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)
, that is, the optimal guessing probability is equal to that obtained by performing the corresponding optimal projective measurements independently on the system and the auxiliary.

Thus, the extra randomness supplied by using
the optimal POVM is exactly equal to the
intrinsic randomness of the auxiliary system used to implement the POVM, so we view
it as arising from the auxiliary rather than from
\rho
itself.

IV
Main results

Theorem 1
.

The maximal amount of secret randomness that can be extracted from a quantum state
\rho
using a projective measurement is given by
H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}=-\log_{2}P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)
with
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}\,\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2}
.

Without loss of generality, one can restrict the optimisation to rank-one projective measurements (see Appendix
B.1
). In what follows, we outline a proof that uses notions from state discrimination and the resource theory of coherence, with full details in Appendix
C
. An alternative proof using properties of the min-entropy and semidefinite programming is provided in Appendix
D
. We prove the theorem by first proving the lower bound
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{d}\,\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2}
(Lemma
1
) and then showing that there exist measurements that achieve that bound (Lemma
2
).

Lemma 1
.

The lower bound
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{d}\,\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2}
holds for every state
\rho
.

Proof.

Using the fact that rank-one measurements are optimal for Alice, from
(
Coles 2012
, Theorem 1, (iii))
, we find

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})=

\displaystyle\max_{\{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\}}F(\rho,\sigma)\,,

(9)

where
F
is the Uhlmann fidelity and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
is the set of states that are diagonal in the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_i}\}
. Notice that
\mathbb{1}/d\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
for all
\mathcal{M}\in\Pi
, so
P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})\geq F(\rho,\mathbb{1}/d)=\frac{1}{d}(\tr\sqrt{\rho})^{2}
for all
\mathcal{M}
. Hence,
P_{\textnormal{guess}}^{*}(\rho)
cannot be smaller than
\frac{1}{d}(\tr\sqrt{\rho})^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

A projective measurement
\mathcal{M}
in the basis
\{\ket{m_i}\}
achieves the bound
P_{\textnormal{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{d}(\tr\sqrt{\rho})^{2}
if and only if
\matrixelement{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\rho}
for all
i=1,...,d
.

Proof.

The details missing here are provided in Appendix
C.1
. In the quantum side-information scenario, any
k
-outcome rank-one measurement,
\mathcal{M}
, by Alice steers
k
pure states on Eve. To optimise her guess, Eve has to measure her system to optimally discriminate among these
k
states. It is known
Eldar et al. 2003
that the best discrimination of a set of pure states is obtained with rank-one measurements: thus, Eve will also perform a rank-one measurement. In turn, Eve’s measurement defines an ensemble realising the mixed state
\rho
. This ensemble
consists of
k
pure states
\rho_{i}=\outerproduct{\tilde{\psi_i}}{\tilde{\psi_i}}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is projective, we have
k=d
and it follows from
Hughston et al. 1993
that any decomposition of
\rho
in
d
pure states is defined by the choice of an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{i}\}
through

\displaystyle\tilde{\ket{\psi_i}}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{i}

with

\displaystyle\innerproduct{i}{i'}=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\,,\;i,i^{\prime}=1,...,d\,.

(10)

Inserting all these observations in (
7
),
we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})=\max_{\{\ket{i}\}}\sum_{i}\absolutevalue{\matrixel{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{i}}^{2}\,.

(11)

The r.h.s. has been called the geometric coherence of
\rho

Streltsov et al. 2015
and was shown in
Xiong and Wu 2018
to be equivalent to
\max_{\{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\}}F(\rho,\sigma)
, with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
the set of states diagonal in the basis
\{\ket{m_i}\}
.
If we rewrite (
11
) as

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i}}\sum_{i}\tr\big(\Pi_i\dyad{\tilde{\gamma_i}}\big)

(12)

s.t.

\displaystyle\Pi_{i}\geq 0\,,\;\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}=\mathbb{1}\,,

the r.h.s defines the optimal discrimination of the sub-normalised states
\ket{\tilde{\gamma_i}}:=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_i}
with a projective measurement
\Pi_{i}=\outerproduct{i}{i}
. One then checks (see Appendix
C.2
) that, under the assumption that

\displaystyle\matrixelement{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\rho}

for all

\displaystyle i=1,...,d\,,

(13)

the choice
\ket{i}=\ket{m_i}
fulfills all the conditions for optimal discrimination of the
\ket{\tilde{\gamma_i}}

Holevo 1973
;
Helstrom 1969
;
Yuen et al. 1975
. Thus, for measurements satisfying (
13
), it holds that
P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})=\sum_{i}\absolutevalue{\matrixel{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}(\tr\sqrt{\rho})^{2}
. Furthermore, we prove (see Appendix
C.3
) that the condition (
13
) is also necessary for a projective measurement to achieve the optimal guessing probability. What remains to be proven is that there exist measurements satisfying condition (
13
). An example of such a measurement valid for any state is the one defined by a basis
\{\ket{m_i}\}
that is unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
, that is, all moduli of inner products between elements of the two different bases equal
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
.
However, as we discuss in Section
V
, one can find other measurements satisfying condition (
13
)
when
d>2
.
∎

Notice that, when Alice uses measurements satisfying (
13
), the decomposition (
10
) that is optimal for Eve is
\tilde{\ket{\psi}_{i}}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_i}
. If
\rho
is full rank,
M_{i}=\rho^{-1/2}\rho_{i}\rho^{-1/2}
is the ‘pretty good measurement’
Hausladen and Wootters 1994
for the ensemble
\{q_{i},\rho_{i}/q_{i}\}
steered by Eve. This measurement is known to be optimal when special symmetries like (
13
) are present in the problem
Dalla Pozza and Pierobon 2015
(in the notation of that work, the Gram matrix has entries
G_{ij}=\matrixelement{m_i}{\rho}{m_j}
). Moreover, when Alice’s measurement satisfies (
13
) and when
\rho
is full-rank, we can show (see Appendix
C.4
) that Eve’s optimal measurement to discriminate her local states is also a ‘pretty good’ measurement.

After solving the problem for the guessing probability, we now move to the von Neumann entropy of the measurement outcomes conditioned on Eve’s side information, a quantity of relevance in the multi-round setting
Tomamichel et al. 2009
;
Dupuis et al. 2020
;
Dai et al. 2023
.

Theorem 2
.

The maximal conditional entropy that can be extracted from a quantum state
\rho
using a projective measurement is
H^{*}=\log_{2}d-S(\rho)
, where
S(\rho)=-\tr\rho\log_{2}{\rho}
is the von Neumann entropy.

Proof.

From
(
Coles 2012
, Theorem 1, (i))
, we have that the entropy
H(Z|E)
of Alice’s measurement outcomes
Z
conditioned on Eve’s side information
E
is

\displaystyle H(Z|E)=D\big(\rho\,||\sum_{z}M_{z}\rho M_{z}\big)\,,

(14)

where
\{M_{z}\}_{z}
is Alice’s projective measurement and
D(\rho||\sigma)
is the quantum relative entropy between the states
\rho
and
\sigma
,

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma)=\tr\Big( \rho\big(\log_2{\rho}-\log_2{\sigma} \big)\Big)\,,

(15)

which is defined when the support of
\rho
is contained within the support of
\sigma
. In Appendix
B.2
, we show that: 1) a rank-one measurement is optimal for Alice to maximise
H(Z|E)
for a given
\rho
, and 2) that

\displaystyle D\big(\rho\,||\sum_{z}M_{z}\rho M_{z}\big)=S\big(\sum_{z}M_{z}\rho M_{z}\big)-S(\rho)\,.

(16)

In
Yuan et al. 2019
, the r.h.s. is shown to be equivalent to the relative entropy of coherence of
\rho
with respect to the measurement basis, which is used as a quantifier of randomness.

The maximum von Neumann entropy of a state of dimension
d
is
\log_{2}d
and is achieved only for maximally mixed states, so we can upper bound Eq. (
16
) with

\displaystyle H(Z|E)\leq\log_{2}d-S(\rho)\,,

(17)

with equality reached if and only if Alice’s measurement basis
\{\ket{m_z}\}_{z}
leaves her system in the maximally mixed state, i.e. if the condition

\displaystyle\matrixelement{m_z}{{\rho}}{m_z}=\frac{1}{d}

for all

\displaystyle z=1,...,d\,

(18)

is satisfied.
∎

As in the case for the condition (
13
) for
H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}
, suitable measurements satisfying (
18
) include bases
\{\ket{m_z}\}
that are unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
, implying the tightness of (
17
). However, when
d>2
we can find other suitable measurements, as discussed in Section
V
. The quantity
\log_{2}d-S(\rho)
is defined in
Zurek 2001
as the total information of
\rho
and it is used in
Horodecki et al. 2003
as a measure of the objective information of
\rho
.

We now consider the conditional max-entropy of the measurement outcomes conditioned on Eve’s side information. This quantity has been interpreted as the security of Alice’s measurement outcomes when used as a secret key
Konig et al. 2009
.

Theorem 3
.

The maximal conditional max-entropy that can be extracted from a quantum state
\rho
using a projective measurement is
H^{*}_{\textnormal{max}}=\log_{2}d+\log_{2}{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)
, where
{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
.

Proof.

The details missing here are given in Appendix
E
. Without loss of generality, we restrict Alice to performing rank-one projective measurements (see Appendix
B.1
). In the case where Alice makes a rank-one projective measurement, the conditional max-entropy of her outcomes conditioned on Eve can be formulated
Konig et al. 2009
as

\displaystyle{H_{\textnormal{max}}}\left(A|E\right)=\log_{2}{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}\,,

(19)

where

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{{p_{x}}\tr\left({\sigma\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}}\right)}\right)^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}\quad\sigma\geq 0\,,\;\tr\sigma=1\,,

(21)

where
\{\ket{\psi_{x}^{E}}\}
are Eve’s post-measurement states and
p_{x}=\langle m_{x}|\rho|m_{x}\rangle
. By applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and identifying the semidefinite optimisation problem for the maximum eigenvalue of a quantum state, we find

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}\leq d{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)\,.

(22)

In the case where the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
is unique, the bound (
22
) is reached if and only if the condition

\displaystyle{\lvert\langle m_{x}|{u_{\textnormal{max}}}\rangle\rvert}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}

for all

\displaystyle x=1,...,d\,

(23)

is satisfied, where
\ket{\umax}
is the eigenvector of
\rho
corresponding to its largest eigenvalue. The optimal measurements in the case where the maximum eigenvalue of
\rho
is degenerate are discussed in Appendix
E
.
∎

As in the case of
H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}
and
H^{*}
, suitable measurements satisfying (
23
) include bases
\{\ket{m_x}\}
that are unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
, but, as before, when

we can find other suitable measurements, as discussed in Section

.

V
Two case studies

Let us now study measurements that satisfy (
13
), (
18
) or (

) but which are not unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
. For one qubit, it is quickly verified that all measurements that satisfy (

with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}

\{M_{i}=\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}\}_{i=1,2,3}

\displaystyle\ket{m_1 }

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{3}}\ket{1}+\sqrt{\frac{1+b}{3}}\ket{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{3}}\ket{3}\,,

\displaystyle\ket{m_2 }

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{3}}\ e^{i\theta_{1}}\ket{1}+\sqrt{\frac{1+b}{3}}\ket{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{3}}\ e^{i\theta_{2}}\ket{3}\,,

{H_{\textnormal{max}}}
is satisfied when
\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{3}
, so we see that, for qutrits at least, there exist non-unbiased measurements that achieve maximal randomness for every
\rho
for all three of our quantifiers of randomness. Interestingly, though, these three conditions are inequivalent in general, so one can choose parameters
\{\gamma_{i}\}
such that the measurement maximises any one of the entropies but not the other two.

The second case study uses
two qubits
. It is based on the observation (proved in Appendix
F.2
) that there is no product basis unbiased to the basis

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_1}

\displaystyle=\ket{00}

(25)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\omega\ket{10}+\omega^{2}\ket{11})

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\omega^{2}\ket{10}+\omega\ket{11})\,,

where
\omega=e^{i\,2\pi/3}
. Consider a state
\rho=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\lambda_{k}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
diagonal in this basis. To extract the maximal randomness with an unbiased measurement, one must be able to perform entangled measurements. This is not a conceptual problem in our setting, since there is no reason why the two qubits should be far apart; nonetheless, such measurements may be more challenging to perform than basic single-qubit measurements. The question is: can one extract maximal randomness from
\rho
by using a product basis? The answer seems to be positive. While we do not have an analytical proof, for a large number of choices of
\lambda
, we performed a heuristic optimisation over product bases, both general (
\{\ket{a,b},\ket{a,b^\perp},\ket{a^\perp,c},\ket{a^\perp,c^\perp}\}
, with six free parameters) and restricted to proper product measurements (
\{\ket{a,b},\ket{a,b^\perp},\ket{a^\perp,b},\ket{a^\perp,b^\perp}\}
, with four free parameters). In both cases and for all states that we probed, we numerically found measurements satisfying
\sum_{i}\Big(\langle m_{i}|\sqrt{\rho}|m_{i}\rangle-\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho}}{4}\Big)^{2}\leq 10^{-15}
,
\sum_{i}\Big(\langle m_{i}|{\rho}|m_{i}\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\Big)^{2}\leq 10^{-15}
or
\sum_{i}\Big(\absolutevalue{\langle m_i | \umax\rangle}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)^{2}\leq 10^{-15}
, which suggests that there exist product measurements satisfying the conditions (
13
), (
18
) and (
23
), respectively.
In this family of examples, therefore, the freedom to choose a measurement basis that is not unbiased may lead to a practical advantage: it allows one to obtain maximal randomness with product measurements.

VI
Conclusion

It is well known that quantum physics contains an intrinsic form of randomness, but, somewhat surprisingly, given a quantum state, it is unknown what is the optimal measurement to extract from it the maximum amount of such randomness. In this work, we concentrate on three different quantifiers of the amount of randomness in a measurement’s outcomes conditioned on an adversary’s side information: the conditional min-entropy, the conditonal von Neumann entropy and the conditional max-entropy.
As one might have expected, all measurements in a basis that is unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
maximise all three of these conditional entropies. However, we also find other measurements that achieve the optimal values, providing a flexibility that may have practical implications, as in the second case study reported. In fact, beyond its fundamental motivation, our analysis is also relevant for the design of device-dependent QRNGs, for which the quantum state is fully characterised. Interestingly, we find measurements in the qutrit case that maximise one of the three conditional entropies considered, but which are not optimal for the other two.

Appendix B
Proof of optimality of rank-one measurement operators

In this appendix we prove the optimality of rank-one measurements for

and

(

B.1
Optimality of rank-one measurements for
H^{*}_{\textrm{min}}
and
H^{*}_{\textnormal{max}}

We show that Alice’s optimal measurement can be assumed to be rank-one. First, notice that any measurement can be obtained by coarse-graining a rank-one measurement, since, given some coarse-grained (i.e. not rank-one) measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{coarse}}=\{M_{i}\}_{i}
, we can represent each of its elements in its spectral decomposition as
M_{i}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{ij}\outerproduct{f^i_j}{f^i_j}
, with
\lambda_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i
,
j
.
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{coarse}}
can then be seen as a coarse-graining of the fine-grained measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{fine}}=\{\outerproduct{f^i_j}{f^i_j}\}_{i,j}
.
If we restrict Alice to performing some projective measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{coarse}}
, the corresponding
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{fine}}
will also be projective.

The coarse-graining of
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{coarse}}
can be seen as a deterministic post-processing of the outcomes of
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{fine}}
. Consider the classical-quantum state (
30
) formed by the classical information of Alice’s measurement outcomes and Eve’s quantum states in the case of
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{fine}}
. The cq-state for any coarse-graining of
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{fine}}
can be found by applying a deterministic function
f(x)
to the classical register
X
,

\displaystyle\rho_{XE}^{\textrm{coarse}}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\outerproduct{f(x)}{f(x)}\otimes\rho_{E}^{x}\,.

(32)

Applying a function to a classical register cannot increase either the conditional min-entropy or the conditional max-entropy (see, e.g.,
(
Tomamichel 2016
, Proposition 6.20)
). Therefore,

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{min}}(X|E)_{\rho_{XE}^{\textrm{coarse}}}

\displaystyle\leq H_{\textrm{min}}(X|E)_{\rho_{XE}^{\textrm{fine}}}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{max}}(X|E)_{\rho_{XE}^{\textrm{coarse}}}

\displaystyle\leq H_{\textrm{max}}(X|E)_{\rho_{XE}^{\textrm{fine}}}\,.

(34)

Then, by (
31
), it is optimal for Alice to choose a rank-one measurement in order to minimise Eve’s guessing probability and to maximise the conditional max-entropy of her measurement outcomes.

B.2
Optimality of rank-one measurements for
H^{*}

We know from
(

, Theorem 1, (i))
that the conditional entropy of the classical-quantum state formed by Alice’s outcomes
Z
and Eve’s post-measurement states is

\displaystyle H(Z|E)=D\big(\rho\,||\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)\,,

(35)

where
\{M_{i}\}_{i}
is Alice’s projective measurement and

is the quantum relative entropy between the states

and
\sigma

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma)=\tr\Big( \rho\big(\log_2{\rho}-\log_2{\sigma} \big)\Big)\,,

(36)

and is defined when the support of
\rho
is contained within the support of
\sigma
.
D\big(\rho\,||\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)
can be written as

\displaystyle D\big(\rho\,||\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)=-S(\rho)+\tr\Big(\rho\log_2 \big( \sum_{i}M_i \rho M_i\big)\Big)\,.

(37)

Since
\{M_{i}\}_{i}
is projective, we have
\log_{2}\big(\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)=\sum_{i}\log_{2}\big(M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)

\Big(\log_{2}\big({M_{i}\rho M_{i}}\big)\Big)M_{j}=\delta_{ij}\log_{2}\big({M_{i}\rho M_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle\tr\Big(\rho\sum_{i} \log_2 \big(M_i \rho M_i\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\tr\Big(\sum_j M_j \rho M_j \sum_{i} \log_2 \big(M_i \rho M_i \big)\Big)

\displaystyle=-S\big(\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}\big)\,,

and we recover Equation (
16

\displaystyle S(\rho_{\textrm{fine}})\geq S(\rho_{\textrm{coarse}})\,.

(39)

Note that the state

is the average state after performing the measurement

.
Projective measurements cannot increase the von Neumann of a state (see, e.g.,
(

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{AEX}=\sum_{x}\outerproduct{\psi^x}{\psi^x}\otimes\outerproduct{x}{x}\,..

To prove our target bound (
1
), we let
\rho_{AE}
be the initial pure state shared by Alice and Eve and
\mathcal{M}=\{\outerproduct{u_x}{u_x}\}_{x}
be Alice’s rank-one projective measurement. Noting that
H_{\textrm{max}}(X)=\log_{2}\textrm{rank}(\tilde{\rho}_{X})\leq\log_{2}d

and that for any pure state
\rho_{AE}

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{min}}(A|E)=-2\log_{2}\sqrt{\rho_{A}}\,,

(59)

returning to our usual notation with
\rho_{A}=\rho
and
d_{A}=d
, we get from Lemma
5
the following upper bound on
H_{\textrm{min}}(A|E)_{\tilde{\rho}_{AE}}
,

\displaystyle\log_{2}d-2\log_{2}\tr\sqrt{\rho}\geq H_{\textrm{min}}(A|E)_{\tilde{\rho}_{AE}}\,.

(60)

Finally, notice that, since Alice’s post-measurement states form an orthonormal basis, the states
\tilde{\rho}_{XE}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{AE}
are related by a unitary on the first subsystem. Therefore, using twice the data processing inequality for conditional min-entropies
Renner 2006
, one can replace
H_{\textrm{min}}(A|E)_{\tilde{\rho}_{AE}}
with
H_{\textrm{min}}(X|E)_{\tilde{\rho}_{XE}}
in (
60
) and, together with (
31
), obtain

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})\geq\frac{1}{d}\Big(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\Big)^{2}.

(61)

D.2
Proof that the bound (
1
) can be reached

Here, we provide an alternative proof using semidefinite programming that the lower bound (
1
) for Eve’s optimal guessing probability can be reached when Alice performs measurements satisfying (
13
).
We consider the maximisation problem (
7
) for the guessing probability and reduce our study to the set of projective rank-one measurements
M_{i}=\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}
in dimension
d
that satisfy
\tr\Big(\ketbra{m_i}{m_i} \sqrt{\rho} \Big)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\rho}
for all
\ket{m_i}
(the set is certainly not empty, since all measurements in a basis that is unbiased with the eigenbasis of
\rho
satisfy this condition).
From hereon, we assume without loss of generality that
\rho
is full-rank, as we note that only the projection of the measurement elements
M_{i}
onto the support of
\rho
plays a role in (
7
). More precisely, defining
\Pi_{\rho}
as the orthogonal projector onto the support of
\rho
and
M_{i}^{\prime}:=\Pi_{\rho}M_{i}\Pi_{\rho}
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}}\sum_{i}\tr(M_i\rho_i)

\displaystyle=\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}}\sum_{i}\tr(M_i\,\Pi_{\rho}\rho_i\Pi_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}}\sum_{i}\tr(M_i'\rho_i)\,.

(62)

Note too that

\displaystyle\tr\Big(M_i \sqrt{\rho} \Big)=\tr\Big(M_i' \sqrt{\rho} \Big)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\rho}\,.

(63)

Then, in the case where
\rho
is not rank-one, we can consider the problem projected onto the support of
\rho
, using the rank-one (not necessarily projective) measurement with elements
M_{i}^{\prime}=\outerproduct{m_i'}{m_i'}
in (
7
), where
M_{i}^{\prime}
is in dimension
r=\textrm{rank}(\rho)
, has
d
outcomes and satisfies
\tr\Big(M_i' \sqrt{\rho} \Big)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\rho}
.

Now with the full-rank
\rho
assumption, we note that, since (
7
) is a semidefinite programming problem, we can define its corresponding minimisation (or dual) problem, which is given by

\displaystyle\beta(\rho,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\underset{X}{\textrm{min}}\tr\big( X\rho\big)\,,\qquad\textrm{s.t.}\,\,\,X\geq M_{i}\,.

(64)

The set of states
\{\rho_{i}=\rho/d\}
and the matrix
X=2\mathbb{1}
define strongly feasible points (i.e. points that satisfy the necessary constraints with strict inequalities) on the dual and primal problems respectively, so (
7
) and (
64
) both return
P_{\textrm{guess}}(\rho,\,\mathcal{M})
. For further details on semidefinite programming problems, see, for example,
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
. Given the measurement
\mathcal{M}
, we can use the primal and dual problems to set upper and lower bounds respectively on Eve’s optimal guessing probability,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\tr\Big(\rho_{i} M_i \Big)\leq P_{\textnormal{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})\leq\tr\Big( X \rho\Big)\,,

(65)

where
\{\rho_{i}\}
is any set of subnormalised states satisfying
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=\rho
and
X
is any positive-semidefinite matrix satisfying
X-M_{i}\geq 0
for all
M_{i}
. The set of states
\rho_{i}=\sqrt{\rho}\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}\sqrt{\rho}
recovers the lower bound (
1
).
We now show that the matrix
X=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho}}{d}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}

satisfies

\displaystyle X-\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho}}{d}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}\geq 0\qquad\forall\,\,i\,.

(66)

We can define any vector in dimension
d
as
\sqrt{\rho}\ket{\phi}
for some
\ket{\phi}
. To prove (
66
), it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\matrixelement{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}\matrixelement{\phi}{\sqrt{\rho}}{\phi}-\matrixelement{\phi}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}\matrixelement{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{\phi}\geq 0\,.

(67)

In analogy with the proof in
C.2
, we see that (
67
) is true by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the vectors
\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}\ket{m_i}
and
\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}\ket{\phi}
.

Using
X=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho}}{d}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in (
65
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\Big(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\Big)^{2}\leq P_{\textnormal{guess}}(\rho,\mathcal{M})\leq\frac{1}{d}\Big(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\Big)^{2}\,.

(68)

This shows that the bound (
1
) can be saturated by measurements satisfying (
13
), so we have that the optimal guessing probability for Alice given the state
\rho
is

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{guess}}^{*}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}\Big(\tr\sqrt{\rho}\Big)^{2}\,.

(69)

Appendix E
Technical details in the proof of Theorem
3

We denote the Schmidt decomposition of the pure state
\ket{\Psi}_{AE}
shared by Alice and Eve by

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{AE}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\ket{u_k}_{A}\ket{v_k}_{E}\,,

(70)

where
\rho=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}\outerproduct{u_k}{u_k}
and
\{\ket{v_k}\}
is an orthonormal basis
in which Eve’s reduced state in diagonal. When Alice measures in an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{m_x}\}
with dimension
d
, we can write Eve’s post-measurement states
\{\ket{\psi_{x}^{E}}\}
as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{x}^{E}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{x}}}\langle m_{x}|\Psi_{AE}\rangle\,,\quad p_{x}=\langle m_{x}|\rho|m_{x}\rangle\,.

(71)

Denoting Eve’s average state post-measurement as
\tilde{\rho}_{E}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{E}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}\,,

(72)

we note that

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{E}=\sum_{x}\langle m_{x}|\Psi_{AE}\rangle\langle\Psi_{AE}|m_{x}\rangle=\tr_{A}\outerproduct{\Psi_{AE}}{\Psi_{AE}}:=\rho_{E}\,,

(73)

where
\rho_{E}
is Eve’s local state before the measurement. The following expression for
{H_{\textnormal{max}}}\left(X|E\right)
in the special case of classical-quantum states is given in
Konig et al. 2009
,

\displaystyle{H_{\textnormal{max}}}\left(A|E\right)=\log_{2}{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}\,,

(74)

where

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}=\max_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{x}}F\left(\rho_{x}^{E},\sigma\right)\right)^{2}\,,

(75)

where
\sigma
is a quantum state (
\sigma\geq 0
and
\tr\sigma=1
) and

\displaystyle F\left(\rho,\sigma\right)=\tr\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}

(76)

(note that
F\left(\rho,\sigma\right)
is symmetric in
\rho
and
\sigma
). Since, in our case, the states
\rho_{x}^{E}
are pure, the expression (
75
) reduces to

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{{p_{x}}\langle\psi_{x}^{E}|\sigma|\psi_{x}^{E}}\rangle\right)^{2}

(77)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{{p_{x}}\tr\left({\sigma\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}}\right)}\right)^{2}\,.

(78)

We define by
\sigma^{*}
the state
\sigma
that achieves the maximisation in (
75
). By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{{p_{x}}\tr\left({\sigma^{*}\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}}\right)}\right)^{2}

(79)

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{x}p_{x}\tr\left({\sigma^{*}\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}}\right)=d\tr\left(\sigma^{*}{\rho}_{E}\right)\,.

(80)

The inequality is saturated if and only if all of the terms inside the sum are identical,

\displaystyle p_{x}\tr\left({\sigma^{*}\outerproduct{\psi_{x}^{E}}{\psi_{x}^{E}}}\right)=\frac{\tr\big( \sigma^{*} {\rho}_E \big)}{d}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;x=1,...,d\,.

(81)

We find a second inequality for
{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}
by noting that

\displaystyle\tr\big( \sigma^{*} {\rho}_E \big)\leq\max_{\sigma}\tr\big( \sigma{\rho}_E \big)={\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho_{E})\,,

(82)

where the second term is a known SDP that returns the largest eigenvalue of
\rho_{E}
. In the case where the largest eigenvalue of
{\rho}_{E}
is non-degenerate, the maximisation is achieved only by
\sigma=\outerproduct{\vmax}{\vmax}
, where
\ket{\vmax}
is the vector from Eve’s local basis
\{\ket{v_i}\}
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. Restricting for now to the case where the largest eigenvalue is non-degenerate, we see that the bound (
82
) is reached if and only if
\sigma^{*}=\outerproduct{\vmax}{\vmax}
and (
81
) is satisfied. We are free to combine these conditions such that the necessary and sufficient conditions for the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_x}\}
to achieve the bound (
82
) are, from (
81
),

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \langle m_x | \umax\rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;x=1,...,d\,,

(83)

where
\ket{\umax}
is the vector from Alice’s local basis
\{\ket{u_i}\}
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. In the case where the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
is degenerate, denote by
\{\ket{\vmax^{(i)}}\}
the set of vectors in Eve’s local basis corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. The optimal
\sigma
in (
82
) is now of the form

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\outerproduct{\vmax^{(i)}}{\vmax^{(i)}}\,,\quad\gamma_{i}\geq 0\,,\quad\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1\,.

(84)

Now the necessary and sufficient conditions for
\{\ket{m_x}\}
to achieve the bound are

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\gamma_{i}}\absolutevalue{\langle{m_x}|{\umax^{(i)}\rangle}}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;x=1,...,d\,,

(85)

where
\{\gamma_{i}\}
is any set of non-negative numbers summing to 1 and
\{\ket{\umax^{(i)}}\}
is the set of vectors in Alice’s local basis corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue.

Note, finally, that
{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho_{E})={\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)
, so in terms of Alice’s state
\rho
we have

\displaystyle{p_{\textnormal{secr}}}^{*}=d{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)

(86)

and

\displaystyle{H_{\textnormal{max}}}^{*}\left(A|E\right)=\log_{2}d+\log_{2}{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}(\rho)\,.

(87)

F.1
Parameters for qutrit measurement (
V
)

For convenience, we restate the measurement (
V
) in the following. Consider the measurement basis
\{M_{i}=\outerproduct{m_i}{m_i}\}_{i=1,2,3}
, with

\displaystyle\ket{m_1 }

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{3}}\ket{1}+\sqrt{\frac{1+b}{3}}\ket{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{3}}\ket{3}\,,

\displaystyle\ket{m_2 }

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{3}}\ e^{i\theta_{1}}\ket{1}+\sqrt{\frac{1+b}{3}}\ket{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{3}}\ e^{i\theta_{2}}\ket{3}\,,

(88)

and
\ket{m_3}
defined by the normalization condition
\sum_{i}M_{i}=\mathbb{1}
,
where
a=-({\gamma_{2}}-{\gamma_{3}})k,\,b=({\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{3}})k,\,c=-({\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{2}})k,\,k\in\mathbb{R}
and each
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
with
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\gamma_{3}
.
To ensure that the square root terms in the coefficients of
\ket{m_1}
and
\ket{m_2}
are well-defined, we need that
1+x\geq 0
,
x\in\{a,b,c\}
, which imposes the following constraint on
k
,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{{\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{3}}}\leq k\leq\frac{1}{\max\{{\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{2}},{\gamma_{2}}-{\gamma_{3}}\}}\,.

(89)

Furthermore, imposing
\langle{m_{1}|m_{2}}\rangle=0
sets the following restriction,

\displaystyle\frac{1+a}{3}e^{i\theta_{1}}+\frac{1+b}{3}+\frac{1+c}{3}e^{i\theta_{2}}=0\,.

(90)

Considering each of the terms in the sum as vectors in the complex plane, the sum
\frac{1+a}{3}e^{i\theta_{1}}+\frac{1+b}{3}
can take any absolute value in the range
\frac{1}{3}|b-a|
to
\frac{1}{3}|2+a+b|
by taking an appropriate choice of
\theta_{1}
. We then see that in order to satisfy (
90
),
\frac{1+c}{3}
must fall in this range, i.e.

\displaystyle|b-a|\leq 1+c\leq 2+a+b\,.

(91)

This gives the following restriction on
k
,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{{\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{3}}}\leq k\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{{\gamma_{1}}-{\gamma_{3}}}\,,

(92)

which is a tighter bound than the constraint (
89
). Notice that the tightest constraint is
-\frac{1}{2}\leq k\leq\frac{1}{2}
and is obtained for pure states.

For
k\neq 0
, this measurement basis is unbiased to the eigenbasis of
\rho
if and only if
a=b=c=0
i.e. when
\rho
is the maximally mixed state.
When we set
\{\gamma_{i}\}=\{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\}
, it is easy to show that
\matrixelement{m_i}{\sqrt{\rho}}{m_i}=\frac{1}{3}\tr\sqrt{\rho}
for
i=1,2,3
, so condition (
13
) for maximal
H_{\textnormal{min}}
is satisfied.
However, this measurement does not satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition (
18
) to maximise the conditional entropy, except in the special case where

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{3}})-\lambda_{2}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{3}})-\lambda_{3}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})=0\,.

(93)

Moreover, we can also consider this qutrit measurement taking
\{\gamma_{i}\}=\{\lambda_{i}\}
. Similarly, it is straightforward to see that this measurement satisfies (
18
) and thus achieves the maximal conditional entropy
H^{*}
.
It does not, however, satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition (
13
) to achieve
H^{*}_{\textnormal{min}}
except in the special case where

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3})-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3})+\sqrt{\lambda_{3}}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})=0\,.

(94)

Finally, when
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
(i.e. the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
is not degenerate), the condition (
23
) for the measurement (
V
) to produce maximal
{H_{\textnormal{max}}}
is satisfied if and only if
a=0
, so
\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{3}
. This condition is, in general, inequivalent to the conditions for maximal
H_{\textnormal{min}}
and
H

F.2
A two-qubit basis that has no unbiased product basis

We will show that there is no orthonormal basis of two-qubit product states that is unbiased to the basis:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_1}

\displaystyle=\ket{00}

(95)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\omega\ket{10}+\omega^{2}\ket{11})

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{01}+\omega^{2}\ket{10}+\omega\ket{11}),

where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
. Firstly, we note that any orthonormal two-qubit product basis can be expressed as either

(i)

\left\{\ket{a}\ket{A},\ket{a}\ket{A^\perp},\ket{a^\perp}\ket{B},\ket{a^\perp}\ket{B^\perp}\right\}
or

(ii)

\left\{\ket{a}\ket{A},\ket{a^\perp}\ket{A},\ket{b}\ket{A^\perp},\ket{b^\perp}\ket{A^\perp}\right\}

for some single qubit states
\ket{a},\ket{A},\ket{b},\ket{B}
, where
\perp
denotes the unique orthogonal state to a given qubit state. Consider Case (
i
). If this basis is unbiased to the vectors (
95
), we have

\absolutevalue{\braket{00}{aA}}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\braket{00}{aA^\perp}}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}.

(96)

Letting
\ket{A}=A_{0}\ket{0}+A_{1}\ket{1}
, etc, with
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
A_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, it follows that
A_{0}=A^{\perp}_{0}={1}/{2a_{0}}
. In order for
\ket{A}
and
\ket{A^\perp}
to be orthogonal and have the same coefficient of
\ket{0}
, both states must lie on the
XY
-plane of the Bloch sphere, i.e.
\ket{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+e^{iy}\ket{1})
and
\ket{A^\perp}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}-e^{iy}\ket{1})
for some
0\leq y<2\pi
. The relation between
a_{0}
and
A_{0}
implies that
\ket{a}
also lies on this plane and we can take
\ket{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+e^{ix}\ket{1})
for some
0\leq x<2\pi
. Similar reasoning shows that all the states
\ket{a},\ket{A},\ket{b},\ket{B}
must lie in the
XY
-plane, in both Cases (
i
) and (
ii
).

We now show the states
\ket{aA}
and
\ket{aA^\perp}
cannot be mutually unbiased to both
\ket{\psi_2}
and
\ket{\psi_3}
. Firstly, the equalities
\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_2}{aA}}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_2}{aA^\perp}}^{2}=1/4
give

\displaystyle\cos(x)+\cos(y)+\cos(x-y)=

\displaystyle\cos(x)-\cos(y)-\cos(x-y)=0\,.

(97)

Thus, we find
\cos(x)=0
, giving four possibilities for the values of
(x,y)
: either
(\pi/2,3\pi/4)
,
(\pi/2,7\pi/4)
,
(3\pi/2,\pi/4)
, or
(3\pi/2,5\pi/4)
. Substitution shows that none of these pairs give
\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_3}{aA}}^{2}=1/4
. A similar argument shows that bases of the form in Case (
ii
) also cannot be mutually unbiased to the vectors (
95
).
