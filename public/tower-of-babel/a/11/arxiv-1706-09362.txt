Sample-based high-dimensional convexity testing
Xi Chen, Adam Freilich, Rocco A. Servedio, Timothy Sun

Abstract
In the problem of high-dimensional convexity testing , there is an unknown set S ⊆ ℝ n S\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n} which is promised to be either convex or ε \varepsilon -far from every convex body with respect to the standard multivariate normal distribution 𝒩 ​ ( 0 , 1 ) n {\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n} . The job of a testing algorithm is then to distinguish between these two cases while making as few inspections of the set S S as possible. In this work we consider sample-based testing algorithms, in which the testing algorithm only has access to labeled samples ( 𝒙 , S ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) ) (\bm{x},S(\bm{x})) where each 𝒙 \bm{x} is independently drawn from 𝒩 ​ ( 0 , 1 ) n {\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n} . We give nearly matching sample complexity upper and lower bounds for both one-sided and two-sided convexity testing algorithms in this framework. For constant ε \varepsilon , our results show that the sample complexity of one-sided convexity testing is 2 Θ ~ ​ ( n ) 2^{\tilde{\Theta}(n)} samples, while for two-sided convexity testing it is 2 Θ ~ ​ ( n ) 2^{\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})} .

1.3
Our techniques

One-sided lower bound.

Our one-sided lower bound has a simple proof using only elementary geometric and probabilistic arguments. It follows from the fact (see Lemma
29
) that if
q=2^{\Theta(n)}
many points are drawn independently from
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n}
, then with probability
1-o(1)
no one of the points lies in the convex hull of the
q-1
others. This can easily be shown to imply that more than
q
samples are required (since given only
q
samples, with probability
1-o(1)
there is a convex set consistent with any labeling and thus a one-sided algorithm cannot reject).

Two-sided lower bound.

At a high-level, the proof of our two-sided lower bound
uses the following standard approach. We first define two distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}

over sets in
\mathds{R}^{n}
such that
(i)
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
is a distribution over convex sets only, and (ii)

\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
is a distribution such that

\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
is

\varepsilon_{0}
-far from convex with probability at least
1-o(1)
for some positive constant
\varepsilon_{0}
.
We then show that every sample-based,
q
-query algorithm
A
with
q=2^{0.01n}

must have

\Ppr_{\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}};\hskip 1.42271pt\bm{x}}\big[\text{$A$ accepts $(\bm{x},\bm{S}(\bm{x}))$}\big]-\Ppr_{\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}};\hskip 1.42271pt\bm{x}}\big[\text{$A$ accepts $(\bm{x},\bm{S}(\bm{x}))$}\big]\leq o(1),

(1)

where
\bm{x}
denotes a sequence of
q
points drawn from
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n}
independently and

(\bm{x},\bm{S}(\bm{x}))
denotes the
q
labeled samples from
\bm{S}
.
Theorem
3
follows directly from (
1
).

To draw a set
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
, we sample a sequence of
N=2^{\sqrt{n}}
points

\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{N}
from the sphere
S^{n-1}(r)
of radius
r
for some
r=\Theta(n^{1/4})
.
Each
\mathbf{y}_{i}
defines a halfspace
\bm{h}_{i}=\{x:x\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i}\leq r^{2}\}
.

\bm{S}
is then the intersection of all
\bm{h}_{i}
’s. (This is essentially a construction used by Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
to exhibit a convex set that has large Gaussian surface area, and used by
[
KOS07
]
to lower bound the sample complexity of learning convex sets under the Gaussian distribution.) The most challenging part of the two-sided lower bound proof is to show that,
with
q
points
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{q}\leftarrow{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n}
,
the
q
bits
\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{q})
with
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
are “almost” independent.
More formally, the
q
bits
\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{q})
with
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}

have
o(1)
-total variation distance from

q
independent bits with the
i
th bit drawn from the marginal distribution
of
\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{i})
as
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
.
On the other hand, it is relatively easy to define a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
that satisfies (ii)
and at the same time,
\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,\bm{S}(\bm{x}_{q})
when
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
has
o(1)
-total variation
distance from the same product distribution.
(
1
) follows by combining the two parts.

Structural result.

Our algorithms rely on a new structural result which we establish for convex sets in
\mathds{R}^{n}
. Roughly speaking, this result gives an upper bound on the Gaussian volume of the “thickened surface” of any bounded convex subset of
\mathds{R}^{n}
; it is inspired by, and builds on, the classic result of Ball
[
Bal93
]
that upperbounds the Gaussian surface area of any convex subset of
\mathds{R}^{n}
.

One-sided upper bound.

Our one-sided testing algorithm employs a “gridding-based” approach to decompose the relevant portion of
\mathds{R}^{n}
(namely, those points which are not too far from the origin) into a collection of disjoint cubes. It draws samples and identifies a subset of these cubes as a proxy for the “thickened surface” of the target set; by the structural result sketched above, if the Gaussian volume of this thickened surface is too high, then the one-sided algorithm can safely reject (as the target set cannot be convex). Otherwise the algorithm does random sampling to probe for points which are inside the convex hull of positive examples it has received but are labeled negative (there should be no such points if the target set is indeed convex, so if such a point is identified, the one-sided algorithm can safely reject). If no such points are identified, then the algorithm accepts.

Two-sided upper bound.

Finally, the main tool we use to obtain our two-sided testing algorithm is a
learning
algorithm for convex sets with respect to the normal distribution over
\mathds{R}^{n}.
The main result of
[
KOS07
]
is an (improper) algorithm which learns the class of all convex subsets of
\mathds{R}^{n}
to accuracy
\varepsilon
using
n^{O(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})}
independent samples from
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n}
. Using the structural result mentioned above, we show that this can be converted into a
proper
algorithm for learning convex sets under
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{n}
, with essentially no increase in the sample complexity. Given this proper learning algorithm, a two-sided algorithm for testing convexity follows from the well-known result of
[
GGR98
]
which shows that proper learning for a class of functions implies (two-sided) testability.

Algorithm

:

1.

, where each

In words, Algorithm

works as follows: first, in Step 1 it draws enough samples so that (with very high probability) it will receive at least one sample in each cube (if the low-probability event that this does not occur takes place, then the algorithm outputs “accept” since it can only reject if it is impossible for

we must show that (i) algorithm

\varepsilon

\varepsilon

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{convex}}

\Ppr_{\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}};\hskip 1.42271pt\bm{x}}\big[\text{$A^{\prime}$ accepts $(\bm{x},\bm{S}(\bm{x}))$}\big]-\Ppr_{\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}};\hskip 1.42271pt\bm{x}}\big[\text{$A^{\prime}$ accepts $(\bm{x},\bm{S}(\bm{x}))$}\big]\geq 1/3-o(1).

(2)

Let

(or
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}
) be the distribution of

, where

(or
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
, respectively). Both of them are distributions over sequences of
q

labeled samples.
Then the LHS of (

), for any deterministic sample-based,
q
-query algorithm

, is
at most the total variation distance between
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}
.

Lemma 13
.

The total variation distance between
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}
is
o(1)
.

The total variation distance between

.

Lemma 15

The total variation distance between

and

5.2
The Distributions

Let
r=\Theta(n^{1/4})
be a parameter to be fixed later, and let
N=2^{\sqrt{n}}
.
We start with the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
.
A random set
\bm{S}\subset\mathds{R}^{n}
is drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
using the following procedure:

1.

We sample a sequence of
N
points
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{N}
from
{{S^{n-1}(r)}}
independently and uniformly at random.
Each point
\mathbf{y}_{i}
defines a halfspace

\bm{h}_{i}=\big\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n}:x\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i}\leq r^{2}\big\}.

2.

The set
\bm{S}
is then the intersection of
\bm{h}_{i}
,
i\in[N]
(this is always nonempty as
indeed
\mathrm{Ball}(r)
is contained in
\bm{S}
).

It is clear from the definition that
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
is always a convex set.

Next we define
{{\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}^{*}}}
(instead of
{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
), a distribution over sequences of
q
labeled samples
(\bm{x},\mathbf{b})
.
To this end, we use
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
to define a function
\rho:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
as follows:

\rho(t)=\Ppr_{\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\text{yes}}}\Big[(t,0,\ldots,0)\in\bm{S}\Big].

Due to the symmetry of
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
, the value
\rho(t)
is indeed the probability that a point
x\in\mathds{R}^{n}
at distance
t

from the origin lies in
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
.
To draw a sequence of
q
labeled samples
(\bm{x},\mathbf{b})\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}^{*}
, we first independently draw
q
random points
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{q}\leftarrow{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
and then independently set each
\mathbf{b}_{i}=1
with probability
\rho(\|\bm{x}_{i}\|)
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}=0
with probability
1-\rho(\|\bm{x}_{i}\|)
.

Given
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
{{\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}^{*}}}
,
Lemma
15
shows that information-theoretically no sample-based algorithm
can distinguish a sequence of
q
labeled samples
(\bm{x},\mathbf{b})
with
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
,

\bm{x}\leftarrow({\mathcal{N}^{n}})^{q}
, and
\mathbf{b}=\bm{S}(\bm{x})

from a sequence of
q
labeled samples drawn from
{{\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}^{*}}}
.
While the marginal distribution of each labeled sample is the same for the two cases,
the former is generated in a correlated fashion using the underlying random convex
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
while the latter
is generated independently.

Finally we define the distribution
{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
, prove Lemma
14
, and
show that a set drawn from
{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
is far from convex with high probability.
To define
{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
, we let
M\geq 2^{\sqrt{n}}
be a large enough integer to be specified later.
With
M
fixed, we use

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M-1}<t_{M}={{2\sqrt{n}}}

to denote a sequence of numbers such that the origin-centered ball
\mathrm{Ball}({{{2\sqrt{n}}}})
is partitioned into

M

shells

\mathrm{Ball}(t_{i})\setminus\mathrm{Ball}(t_{i-1})
,
i\in[M]
,
and all the
M
shells have the same probability mass under
{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
.
By spherical coordinates, it means that
the following integral takes the same value for all
i

\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\phi(x,0,\ldots,0)x^{n-1}dx,

(3)

where
\phi
denotes the density function of
{\mathcal{N}^{n}}
.
We show below that when
M
is large enough, we have

|\rho(x)-\rho(t_{i})|\leq 2^{-\sqrt{n}},

(4)

for any
i\in[M]
and any
x\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]
.
We will fix such an
M
and use it to define
{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
. (Our results are not affected by the size of
M
as a function of
n
; we only need it to be finite, given
n
.)

To show that (
4
) holds when
M
is large enough, we need the continuity of the function
\rho
, which follows
directly from the explicit expression for
\rho
given later in
(

).

Lemma 16
.

The function
\rho:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
is continuous.

Since
\rho
is continuous, it is continuous over
[0,{{2\sqrt{n}}}]
.
Since
[0,{{2\sqrt{n}}}]
is compact,

\rho
is also uniformly continuous over
[0,{{2\sqrt{n}}}]
.
Also note that
\max_{i\in[M]}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})
goes to
0
as
M
goes to
+\infty
.
It follows that (
4
) holds when
M
is large enough.

With
M\geq 2^{\sqrt{n}}
fixed,
a random set
\bm{S}\leftarrow{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
is drawn as follows.
We start with
\bm{S}=\emptyset
and for each
i\in[M]
,
we add the
i
th shell
\mathrm{Ball}(t_{i})\setminus\mathrm{Ball}(t_{i-1})
to
\bm{S}
independently
with probability
\rho(t_{i})
.
Thus an outcome of
\bm{S}
is a union of some of the shells and

{{\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}}}
is supported over
2^{M}
different sets.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\textsf{no}}
using
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
. We now prove Lemma
14
.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})
be a sequence of
q
points in
\mathds{R}^{n}
.
We say
x
is
bad
if either (1) at least one point lies outside of
\mathrm{Ball}(2\sqrt{n})
or (2)
there are two points that lie in the same shell of
\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
; we say
x
is
good
otherwise.
We first claim that
\bm{x}\leftarrow({\mathcal{N}^{n}})^{q}
is bad with probability
o(1)
.
To see this, we have from Lemma
5
that event (1) occurs with probability
o(1)
, and
from
M\geq 2^{\sqrt{n}}
and
q=2^{0.01\sqrt{n}}
that event (2) occurs with probability
o(1)
.
The claim follows from a union bound.

Given that
\bm{x}\leftarrow({\mathcal{N}^{n}})^{q}
is good with probability
1-o(1)
, it suffices to show that
for any good
q
-tuple
x
, the total variation distance between
(1)
\bm{S}(x)
with
\bm{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\textsf{no}}
and (2)

\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{q})
with each bit
\mathbf{b}_{i}
being

with probability
\rho(\|x_{i}\|)
independently, is
o(1)
.
Let
\ell_{i}\in[M]
be the index of the shell that

lies in.
Since

is good (and thus, all points lie in different shells),

\bm{S}(x)
has the
i
th bit being

independently with probability
\rho(t_{\ell_{i}})
;
for the other distribution, the probability is
\rho(\|x_{i}\|)
.
Using the subadditivity of total variation distance (i.e., the fact that
the

between two sequences of independent random variables
is upper bounded by the sum of the

between each pair) as well as (

), we have

\smash{d_{\text{TV}}(\bm{S}(x),\mathbf{b})\leq q\cdot 2^{-\sqrt{n}}=o(1).}

This finishes the proof.
∎

\varepsilon_{0}

Lemma 17

There exist a real value

Proof.

We need the following claim but delay its proof to the end of the subsection:

Claim 18

There exist an

with

and a constant

c<\rho(x)<1-c,\quad\text{for all $x\in\left[\sqrt{n}-{{10}},\sqrt{n}+{{10}}\right]$}.

Let

denote the set of all integers

Observe that (1) the total probability mass of all shells

Consider the following

|K|

We prove the following claim:

Claim 19

The random set
\bm{T}

\Omega(M)

\bm{T}
,

\Omega(M)

First note that it suffices to consider intervals

(In other words, for a given outcome

of
\bm{T}

On the other hand, if

We return to the

As a result, Claim

,

is
\varepsilon_{0}
-far from any convex set for some constant

.

Proof of Claim
18
.

We start with the choice of
r
. Let

\alpha=\sqrt{n}-{{10}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\sqrt{n}+{{10}}.

Let
\mathrm{cap}(t)
denote the fractional surface area of the spherical cap
S^{n-1}\cap\{x:x_{1}\geq t\}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{cap}(t)=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\bm{x}\leftarrow S^{n-1}}\big[\bm{x}_{1}\geq t\big].

So
\mathrm{cap}
is a continuous, strictly decreasing function over
[0,1]
.
Since
\mathrm{cap}(0)=1/2
and
\mathrm{cap}(1)=0
, there is a unique
r\in(0,{{\alpha}})
such that

{{\mathrm{cap}}}(r/\alpha)=1/N=2^{-\sqrt{n}}
.
Below we show that
r=\Theta(n^{1/4})
and fix it in the rest of the proof.
First recall the following explicit expression (see e.g.
[
KOS07
]
):

\mathrm{cap}(t)=a_{n}\int_{t}^{1}\left(\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\right)^{n-3}dz,

where
a_{n}=\Theta(n^{1/2})
is a parameter that only depends on
n
.
Also recall the following inequalities from
[
KOS07
]
about
\mathrm{cap}(t)
:

\mathrm{cap}(t)\leq e^{-nt^{2}/2},\quad\text{~for all $t\in[0,1]$};\quad\quad\mathrm{cap}(t)\geq\Omega\left(t\cdot e^{-nt^{2}/2}\right),\quad\text{for $t=O(1/n^{1/4})$}.

(5)

By our choice of
\alpha
and the monotonicity of the cap function,
this implies that
r=\Theta(n^{1/4})
and

1/N=\mathrm{cap}(r/\alpha)\geq\Omega(1/n^{1/4})\cdot e^{-n(r/\alpha)^{2}/2}\geq\Omega(1/n^{1/4})\cdot e^{-(r^{2}/2)(1+O(1/\sqrt{n}))}=\Omega(1/n^{1/4})\cdot e^{-r^{2}/2}

(using
r=\Theta(n^{1/4})
for the last inequality), and thus, we have
e^{r^{2}/2}\geq N/n
.

Next, using the function
\mathrm{cap}
we have the following expression for
\rho
:

\rho(x)=\left(1-\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{x}\right)\right)^{N}.

(6)

As a side note,
\rho
is continuous and thus, Lemma
16
follows.
Since
\mathrm{cap}
is strictly decreasing, we have that
\rho
is strictly decreasing as well.
To finish the proof it suffices to show that there is a constant

such that

\rho(\alpha)<1-c
and
\rho(\beta)\geq c
.
The first part is easy since

\rho(\alpha)=\left(1-1/N\right)^{N}\approx e^{-1}

by our choice of
r
.
In the rest of the proof we show that

\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\right)\leq a\cdot\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)=\frac{a}{N},

(7)

for some positive constant

.
It follows immediately that

\rho(\beta)=\left(1-\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\right)\right)^{N}\geq\left(1-\frac{a}{N}\right)^{N}\geq\left(e^{-2a/N}\right)^{N}=e^{-2a},

using

for

,
and this finishes the proof of the claim.

.
Below we show that

(8)

for some positive constant

.
It follows that

\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\right)-\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)\leq a^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{cap}\left(\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)

Lemma 34

If

Since

Again by the theorem from John (see the discussion in
[

]

. Now consider the set

n\rho+\alpha
. This is a “thickened hyperplane” which contains

and its Gaussian volume is given by

\mathrm{Vol}(H)=\int_{-(n\rho+\alpha)}^{(n\rho+\alpha)}\varphi(x)\,dx,

where
\varphi(x)
is the density function of a univariate normal distribution as defined in Section
2
. We know that
\phi
is bounded from above by

so this integral is at most

Lemma 35
.

Let

Let

. Let

Lemma 36
