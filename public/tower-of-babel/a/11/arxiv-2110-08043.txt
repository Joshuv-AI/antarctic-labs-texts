Phase field models for thermal fracturing and their variational structures Journal: Journal of L a T e X Templates
S. Alfat, M. Kimura, Alifian. M. M

Abstract
It is often observed that thermal stress enhances crack propagation in materials, and conversely, crack propagation can contribute to temperature shifts in materials. In this study, we first consider the thermoelasticity model proposed by M. A. Biot (1956) and study its energy dissipation property. The Biot thermoelasticity model takes into account the following effects. Thermal expansion and contraction are caused by temperature changes, and conversely, temperatures decrease in expanding areas but increase in contracting areas. In addition, we examine its thermomechanical properties through several numerical examples and observe that the stress near a singular point is enhanced by the thermoelastic effect. In the second part, we propose two crack propagation models under thermal stress by coupling a phase field model for crack propagation and the Biot thermoelasticity model and show their variational structures. In our numerical experiments, we investigate how thermal coupling affects the crack speed and shape. In particular, we observe that the lowest temperature appears near the crack tip, and the crack propagation is accelerated by the enhanced thermal stress.

Introduction

Cracking is a phenomenon that occurs everywhere in our lives, but if it is allowed to continue, it can cause fatal damage. A crack in a material occurs when the material experiences a continuous overload. However, several other factors, such as thermal expansion and contraction due to temperature changes
[
29
,
31
,
35
]
, fluid pressure (e.g., in hydraulic fracturing)
[
26
]
, the diffusion of hydrogen (or hydrogen embrittlement)

28
]
, chemical reactions
[

]
, and humidity
[
31

, cause cracks in materials. In particular, among these phenomena, cracks due to thermal expansion are interesting to study from the viewpoint of the energy balance between elastic, thermal, and surface energies.

M. A. Biot proposed a theoretical framework for coupled thermoelasticity based on the principle of minimum entropy production
[
5
]
. Biot’s model is now widely known as the traditional coupled thermoelasticity model, and it has been extended to dynamical theory
[
27
]
and to various other situations
[
14
,
20
,
21
,
25
,
36
,
37
]
. As shown in Section 2.2, it satisfies an energy balance equality between the elastic and thermal energies.

In fracture mechanics, especially in the modeling and simulation of crack propagation,
a phase field approach has been recently recognized as a powerful tool. The phase field model (PFM) for fractures was first proposed by Bourdin et al.
[
6
]
and Karma et al.
[
23
]
. Then, based on the framework of variational fracture theory
[
8
,
15
]
, the techniques and applications of PFM have been extensively developed, for example
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
30
,
34
]
. We refer to
[
24
]
for further information on the development of PFM for fracture mechanics. PFM for fracture mechanics is derived as a gradient flow of the total energy, which consists of the elastic energy and the surface energy and is known to be consistent with the classical Griffith theory
[
6
,
24
]
. It allows us to handle the complex geometry of multiple, kinked, or branching cracks in both 2D and 3D without a crack path search. Comparisons with the experimental results are investigated in
[
33
]
.

In this study, we deal with the modeling of thermal fracturing in an isotropic and homogeneous body by coupling the Biot thermoelasticity model and PFM. Naturally, three kinds of energy, i.e., elastic, thermal, and surface energies, appear in our stage, and the exchange and dissipation of those energies are the main interests of our research. Thermoelasticity Model

2.1
Formulation of the problem

M.A. Biot
[
5
]
proposed the following mathematical model for coupled thermoelasticity:

\displaystyle-\mbox{div}\sigma{[u]}=\beta\nabla\Theta

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~\Omega\times[0,T],

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\chi\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\Theta=\kappa_{0}\Delta\Theta-\Theta_{0}\beta\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mbox{div}u)

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~\Omega\times(0,T],

(2.1b)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2
or
3
). We suppose that
\Omega
is an isotropic elastic body and consider the thermoelastic coupling between the mechanical deformation and the thermal expansion in
\Omega
. The constant
\beta
is defined by
\beta:=a_{L}(d\lambda+2\mu)
with
a_{L}>0
as the coefficient of linear thermal expansion and
\mu(>0)
;
\lambda(>-\frac{2\mu}{d})
are Lamé’s constants.

The unknown functions in (
2.1a
) and (
2.1b
) are the displacement
u(x,t)=(u_{1}(x,t)

,\cdots,u_{d}(x,t))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the temperature
\Theta(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, the constant
\Theta_{0}>0
is a fixed reference temperature. Similarly, strain
e[u]
and stress tensors
\sigma[u]
are defined as

\displaystyle e[u]:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla u^{T}+(\nabla u^{T})^{T}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\sigma[u]:=Ce[u]=\lambda(\mbox{div}u){I}+2\mu e[u]\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d},

(2.2b)

where
C:=(c_{ijkl}),~c_{ijkl}=\lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})
is an isotropic elastic tensor and
{I}
is the identity matrix of size
d
. From (
2.2b
), (
2.1a
) is also written in the form

\displaystyle-\mu\Delta u-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mbox{div}u)=\beta\nabla\Theta.

The term
\beta\nabla\Theta
in (
2.1a
) and the term
\Theta_{0}\beta\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mbox{div}u)
in (
2.1b
) represent the body force due to thermal expansion and the heat source due to the volume change rate, respectively. We remark that when
a_{L}=0
, (
2.1a
) and (
2.1b
) are decoupled.

It is convenient to introduce the following strain and stress tensors, including the thermal effect.

\displaystyle e^{*}[u,\Theta]:=e[u]-a_{L}(\Theta(x,t)-\Theta_{0})I\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{sym},

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:=Ce^{*}[u,\Theta]=\sigma[u]-\beta(\Theta(x,t)-\Theta_{0})I\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{sym}.

Using the thermal stress tensor
\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]
, (
2.1a
) can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle-\mbox{div}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]=0.

This means that the force
\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]
is in equilibrium in
\Omega
. In the preceding equation, (
2.1
) represents the force balance and the thermal diffusion in
\Omega
, respectively.

The system in (
2.1
) is complemented by the following boundary and initial conditions:

\displaystyle u=u_{D}(x,t)

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{D}^{u}\times[0,T],

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]n=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{N}^{u}\times[0,T],

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\Theta=\Theta_{D}(x,t)

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~{\Gamma}_{D}^{\Theta}\times[0,T],

(2.4c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial n}=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~{\Gamma}_{N}^{\Theta}\times[0,T],

(2.4d)

\displaystyle\Theta(x,0)=\Theta_{*}(x)

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~\Omega,~\hskip-2.0pt\qquad\qquad

(2.4e)

where
n
is the outward unit normal vector along the boundary,
\Gamma=\Gamma_{D}^{u}~\cup~\Gamma_{N}^{u}~(\Gamma=\Gamma_{D}^{\Theta}~\cup~\Gamma_{N}^{\Theta})
with
\Gamma_{D}^{u}~\cap~\Gamma_{N}^{u}~=\emptyset~({\Gamma}_{D}^{\Theta}~\cap~{\Gamma}_{N}^{\Theta}=\emptyset)
. The boundaries
\Gamma_{D}^{u}
and
\Gamma_{N}^{u}
(
\Gamma_{D}^{\Theta}
and
\Gamma_{N}^{\Theta}
) are the Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries for
u
(for
\Theta
), respectively. We suppose that the
(d-1)
-dimensional volume of
\Gamma_{D}^{u}
is positive for the solvability of
u
.

Remark 2.1

Instead of boundary conditions (
2.4a
) and (
2.4b
), we can also consider the following mixed-type condition. When
d=2
, on a part of the boundary (which we denote by
\Gamma_{DN}^{u}
),
u=(u_{1},u_{2})^{T}
and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{1}=u_{D1}&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{DN}^{u},\\
(\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]n)\cdot e_{2}=0&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{DN}^{u},\end{array}\right.

or

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{2}=u_{D2}&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{DN}^{u},\\
(\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]n)\cdot e_{1}=0&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{DN}^{u},\end{array}\right.

where
u_{Di}:=\Gamma_{DN}^{u}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a given horizontal or vertical displacement and
e_{1}=(1,0)^{T}
,
e_{2}=(0,1)^{T}
. These types of mixed boundary conditions are considered in Section
2.3.3
and Section
4.4.1
. Even for these mixed-type boundary conditions, we can easily extend the following arguments on weak solutions, variational principles, and energy equalities.

2.2
Variational principle and energy equality

This section aims to show a variational principle and provide an energy equality that implies the energy dissipation property for the system (
2.1
). In linear elasticity theory, a weak form of the boundary value problem for
u_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D}^{u};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mbox{div}\sigma[u]=0&\qquad\mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
u=u_{D}&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{D}^{u},\\
\sigma[u]n=0&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{N}^{u},\end{array}\right.

which is given by

\displaystyle u\in V^{u}(u_{D}),~\int_{\Omega}\sigma[u]:e[v]~dx=0\quad\text{for all}~v\in V^{u}(0),

where

\displaystyle V^{u}(u_{D}):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d});~u\big|_{\Gamma_{D}^{u}}=u_{D}\right\}.

(2.12)

A weak solution uniquely exists and is given by

u=\operatorname*{argmin}_{v\in V^{u}(u_{D})}{E_{el}(v)},

where

\displaystyle E_{el}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma[v]:e[v]~dx\quad(v\in H^{1}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)

(2.13)

is an elastic energy. This is known as a variational principle
[
10
,
12
]
. For a fixed
\Theta(x)
, a weak form for
u
of (
2.1a
) and its variational principle are derived as follows.

Proposition 2.2

For
u\in H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Theta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-{\mbox{div}}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]=0&\qquad\mbox{in}~\Omega,\\
u=u_{D}&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{D}^{u},\\
\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]n=0&\qquad\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{N}^{u},\end{array}\right.

is equivalent to the following weak form:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[v]~dx=0&\quad\mbox{for all}~v\in V^{u}(0),\\[8.0pt]
u\in V^{u}(u_{D}).&\end{array}\right.

Proof

For
v\in V^{u}(0)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(-\mbox{div}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]\right)\cdot v~dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[v]~dx-\int_{\Gamma_{N}^{u}}(\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]n)\cdot v~ds.

The equivalency immediately follows from this equation. ∎

Proposition 2.3
(Variational principle)

For a given
\Theta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D}^{u};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a unique weak solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
that satisfies (
2.2
). Furthermore, the solution
u
is a unique minimizer of the variational problem:

u=\operatorname*{argmin}_{v\in V^{u}(u_{D})}{E_{el}^{*}(v,\Theta)},

where

\displaystyle E_{el}^{*}(v,\Theta)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[v,\Theta]:e^{*}[v,\Theta]~dx.

(2.19)

We remark that
E_{el}^{*}(v,\Theta)
represents thermoelastic energy.

Proof

The unique existence of a weak solution for
u
is shown by the Lax-Milgram theorem
[
10
]
since (
2.2
) is written as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma[u]:e[v]~dx=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}v~dx,\\[3.0pt]
u\in V^{u}(u_{D})\qquad(\mbox{for all}~v\in V^{u}(0)).\end{array}\right.

The coercivity of the above weak form is known as Korn’s second inequality
[
10
]
:

{}^{\exists}a_{0}>0~\text{such that}~\int_{\Omega}\sigma[v]:e[v]~dx\geq a_{0}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\mbox{for all}~v\in V^{u}(0).

For a weak solution
u
and any
v\in V^{u}(0)
, using the equalities

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}[u+v,\Theta]=\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]+\sigma[v],

\displaystyle e^{*}[u+v,\Theta]=e^{*}[u,\Theta]+e[v],

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[v]=e^{*}[u,\Theta]:\sigma[v],

we have

\displaystyle E_{el}^{*}(u+v,\Theta)-E_{el}^{*}(u,\Theta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u+v,\Theta]:e^{*}[u+v,\Theta]~dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e^{*}[u,\Theta]~dx\qquad

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[v]~dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma[v]:e[v]~dx

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sigma[v]:e[v]~dx\geq 0.

This shows that
u
is a minimizer of
E^{*}_{el}(u,\Theta)
among
V^{u}(u_{D})
.

On the other hand, if
u
is a minimizer, the first variation of
E_{el}^{*}
vanishes at
u
; i.e., for all
v\in V^{u}(0)
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{ds}E^{*}_{el}(u+sv,\Theta)\big|_{s=0}=\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[v]~dx.

Hence,
u
is a weak solution. Summarizing the above, there exists a unique weak solution to (
2.13
), and
u
is a weak solution if and only if it is a minimizer of
E^{*}_{el}
among
V^{u}(u_{D})
. ∎

The next theorem represents a dissipation of the sum of the elastic and thermal energies during the thermomechanical process. We define thermal energy as

\displaystyle E_{th}(\Theta):=\frac{\chi}{2\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\Theta(x)-\Theta_{0}\right|^{2}~dx.

(2.22)

Theorem 2.4
(Energy equality for Biot’s model)

Let
(u(x,t),\Theta(x,t))
be a sufficiently smooth solution to (
2.1
) and (
2.4
). In addition, we suppose that
u_{D}
does not depend on
t
and
\Theta_{D}=\Theta_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(E_{el}(u(t))+E_{th}(\Theta(t))\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\Theta(t)\right|^{2}~dx\leq 0.

(2.23)

Proof

Since

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma[u]:e[u]\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma[u]:e[\dot{u}]

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]+\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})I):e[\dot{u}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]-\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u}

(2.24)

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{el}(u(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sigma[u]:e[u]\right)~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[\dot{u}]~dx+\int_{\Omega}\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})(\mbox{div}\dot{u})~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})(\mbox{div}\dot{u})~dx.

(2.25)

Substituting (
2.1b
) into (
2.25
) and using the boundary conditions (
2.4c
) and (
2.4d
) for
\Theta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{el}(u(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{\Theta_{0}}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\Big\{\kappa_{0}\Delta\Theta-\chi\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}\Big\}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Gamma}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial n}ds-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla\Theta\big|^{2}dx-\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\frac{\chi}{2\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\big|\Theta-\Theta_{0}\big|^{2}dx\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla\Theta\big|^{2}dx-\frac{d}{dt}E_{th}(\Theta(t)).

This gives the energy equality for (
3.2
). ∎

As shown in Proposition
2.3
and Theorem
2.4
, Biot’s thermoelasticity model is related to both energies
E_{el}(u)
and
E_{el}^{*}(u,\Theta)
. We denote their energy densities as follows:

\displaystyle{W}(u):=\sigma[u]:e[u],

(2.26)

\displaystyle{W}^{*}(u,\Theta):=\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]:e^{*}[u,\Theta],

(2.27)

where
{W}(u)
and
{W}^{*}(u,\Theta)
are the elastic and thermoelastic energy densities, respectively.

2.3
Numerical Experiment

2.3.1
Nondimensional setting

In the following numerical examples, we introduce a nondimensional form of Biot’s model. We consider the following scaling for
x
,

Crack Propagation under Thermal Stress

This section is devoted to the phase field models for thermal fracturing, which are the main purpose of this paper.

3.1
Fracturing phase field model (F-PFM)

According to the works
[
34
,
24
]
, we introduce fracturing PFM (we call it F-PFM) in this section. Let
\Omega
be a bounded (uncracked) domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}^{u}\cup\Gamma_{N}^{u}
, similar to Section
2
. In F-PFM, a crack in
\Omega
at time
t
is described by a damage variable
z(x,t)\in[0,1]
for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
with space regularization. The cracked and uncracked regions are represented by
z\approx 1
and
z\approx 0
, respectively, and
z\in(0,1)
indicates slight damage. A typical example of a straight crack in a square domain is illustrated in Figure
9
.

The F-PFM is described as:

\displaystyle-{\mbox{div}}\left((1-z)^{2}{\sigma}[{u}]\right)=0

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~{\Omega}\times[0,{T}],

(3.1a)

\displaystyle{\alpha}\frac{\partial z}{\partial{t}}=\left({\epsilon}~{\mbox{div}}\left({\gamma_{*}}{\nabla}z\right)-\frac{{\gamma_{*}}}{{\epsilon}}z+(1-z){W}(u)\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~{\Omega}\times[0,{T}],

(3.1b)

with the following boundary and initial conditions:

\displaystyle u=u_{D}(x,t)

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{D}^{u}\times[0,{T}],

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\sigma[u]n=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~\Gamma_{N}^{u}\times[0,{T}],

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on}~\Gamma\times[0,{T}],

(3.2c)

\displaystyle z({x,0})=z_{*}(x)

\displaystyle\mbox{in}~\Omega,

(3.2d)

where the displacement
u:\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the damage variable
z:\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]\mapsto[0,1]
are unknowns. The parameters
\alpha>0
and
\epsilon>0
are small numbers related to regularization in time and space, respectively. The critical energy release rate is denoted by
\gamma_{*}
(which is often denoted by
\text{G}_{\text{c}}
), and the elastic energy density is defined by
{W}={W}(u):=\sigma[u]:e[u]
. In (
3.1b
), the term
{W}
works as a driving force for
z
.

Figure 9
:
Illustration of the phase field approximation of the cracked surface in an elastic body.

The symbol
(~)_{+}
on the right-hand side in (
3.1b
) denoted the positive part
(s)_{+}:=\text{max}(s,0)
, and it represents the irreversible property of crack growth.

F-PFM is derived as a unidirectional gradient flow of the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z)+E_{s}(z)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(1-z)^{2}\sigma[u]:e[u]~dx,

(3.3)

\displaystyle E_{s}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{*}\left(\epsilon\big|\nabla z\big|^{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)dx.

(3.4)

More precisely,
u(t)
obeys the following variational principle:

u(t)=\operatorname*{argmin}_{u\in V(u_{D}(t))}{\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z(t))},

(3.5)

and (
3.1b
) becomes a gradient flow of the energy
\min\limits_{u}\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z)+E_{s}(z)
.

We remark that
\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z)
is a modified elastic energy, which corresponds to the elastic energy with a damaged Young’s modulus
\tilde{E}_{\text{Y}}=(1-z)^{2}E_{\text{Y}}
. The energy
E_{s}(z)
is regularized surface energy, which approximates the crack area (
d=3
) or length (
d=2
) as
\epsilon\mapsto 0
. Please see
[
24
]
for more details. The following energy equality for F-PFM is shown in
[
24
]
(
[
34
]
for the antiplane setting).

Theorem 3.1
(Energy equality for F-PFM)

Let
(u(x,t),z(x,t))
be a sufficiently smooth solution to (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). If
u_{D}
is independent of
t
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{el}(u(t),z(t))+E_{s}(z(t))\right)=-\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\dot{z}\right|^{2}dx\leq 0.

(3.6)

Proof

Differentiating the total energy in
t
and applying integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-12.0pt\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{el}(u(t),z(t))+E_{s}(z(t))\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(1-z)^{2}\sigma[u]:{e}[\dot{u}]~dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma_{*}\epsilon\nabla z\cdot\nabla\dot{z}+\big(\frac{\gamma_{*}}{\epsilon}z-(1-z){W}(u)\big)\dot{z}\right)~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}(1-z)^{2}\underbrace{\left(\sigma[u]n\right)}_{0}\cdot\dot{u}~ds-\int_{\Omega}\underbrace{\mbox{div}\left((1-z)^{2}\sigma[u]\right)}_{0}\cdot\dot{u}~dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma_{*}\epsilon\underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}}_{0}\dot{z}~ds-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}\dot{z}~dx,

(3.7)

where we define
\mathcal{H}:=\epsilon\mbox{div}\left(\gamma_{*}\nabla z\right)-\frac{\gamma_{*}}{\epsilon}z+(1-z){W}(u)
. Since (
3.1b
) is written as
\alpha\dot{z}=(\mathcal{H})_{+}
, using the equality
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{H})_{+}=(\mathcal{H})_{+}^{2}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{el}(u(t),z(t))+E_{s}(z(t))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}\dot{z}~dx=-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}\frac{(\mathcal{H})_{+}}{\alpha}~dx=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\mathcal{H})_{+}^{2}}{\alpha}~dx=-\int_{\Omega}\alpha\left|\dot{z}\right|^{2}dx.\qquad\text{\qed}

Thermal fracturing phase field model 2 (TF-PFM2)

In the previous section, we proposed TF-PFM1 based on the thermoelastic energy
E_{el}^{*}(u,\Theta)
. We proved a variational principle but proved only partial energy equality.
As shown in Section
2.2
, the Biot model is related to both energies
E^{*}_{el}(u,\Theta)
and
E_{el}(u)
. The variational principle holds for
E_{el}^{*}(u,\Theta)
(Proposition
2.3
), and the energy equality holds for
E_{el}(u)
(Theorem
2.4
). This motivates us to consider another type of thermal fracturing PFM based on elastic energy
E_{el}(u)
.
We call the following thermal fracturing model TF-PFM2:

\displaystyle-{\mbox{div}}\left((1-z)^{2}{\sigma}^{*}[{u},{\Theta}]\right)=0

\displaystyle\hskip-16.0pt\mbox{in}~{\Omega}\times[0,{T}],

(3.14a)

\displaystyle{\alpha}\frac{\partial z}{\partial{t}}=\left({\epsilon}~{\mbox{div}}({\gamma_{*}}{\nabla}z)-\frac{{\gamma_{*}}}{{\epsilon}}z+(1-z){W}(u)\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\hskip-16.0pt\mbox{in}~{\Omega}\times[0,{T}],

(3.14b)

\displaystyle\chi\frac{\partial{\Theta}}{\partial{t}}=~{\mbox{div}}\left({\kappa(z)}{\nabla}{\Theta}\right)-\Theta_{0}(1-z)^{2}\beta\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}({\mbox{div}}{u})

\displaystyle\hskip-16.0pt\mbox{in}~{\Omega}\times(0,{T}].

(3.14c)

The associated boundary and initial conditions are given by (
3.11
). For this model, we can show the following energy equality.

Theorem 3.3
(Energy equality for TF-PFM2)

We suppose that
(u(x,t),

\Theta(x,t),z(x,t))
is a sufficiently smooth solution for (
3.14
) and (
3.11
). If
u_{D}
is independent of
t
and
\Theta_{D}=\Theta_{0}
, then the following energy equality holds:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{el}(u(t),z(t))+E_{s}(z(t))+E_{th}(\Theta(t))\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=-\frac{1}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(z)\left|\nabla\Theta\right|^{2}~dx-\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\dot{z}\right|^{2}dx\leq 0.

(3.15)

Proof

Since the relation in (
2.24
) is written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}W(u)\right)=\sigma^{*}[u,\Theta]-\beta(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u},

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-z)^{2}W(u)\right)=\sigma_{z}^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[\dot{u}]+\beta(1-z)^{2}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u}-(1-z)\dot{z}W(u).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{el}(u(t),z(t))+\frac{d}{dt}E_{s}(z(t))

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\Omega}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-z)^{2}W(u)\right)~dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\epsilon\mbox{div}(\gamma_{*}\nabla z)-\frac{\gamma_{*}}{\epsilon}z\right)\dot{z}~dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{z}^{*}[u,\Theta]:e[\dot{u}]~dx}_{0}+\int_{\Omega}\beta(1-z)^{2}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u}~dx-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}\dot{z}~dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\Omega}\beta(1-z)^{2}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u}~dx-\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\dot{z}|^{2}~dx,

(3.16)

where
\mathcal{H}=\epsilon\mbox{div}(\gamma_{*}\nabla z)-\frac{\gamma_{*}}{\epsilon}z+(1-z)W(u)
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{th}(\Theta(t))=\frac{\chi}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\dot{\Theta}~dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=\frac{1}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\left\{\mbox{div}(\kappa(z)\nabla\Theta)-\Theta_{0}\beta(1-z)^{2}\mbox{div}\dot{u}\right\}~dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=-\frac{1}{\Theta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(z)|\nabla\Theta|^{2}~dx-\int_{\Omega}\beta(1-z)^{2}(\Theta-\Theta_{0})\mbox{div}\dot{u}~dx.

(3.17)

Taking a sum of these equalities (
3.16
)-(
3.17
), we obtain the energy equality (
3.15
). ∎

5
Summary and Future Works

We proposed two thermal fracturing phase field models, TF-PFM1 and TF-PFM2, by coupling the Biot thermoelasticity model
[
5
]
and the fracturing phase field model (F-PFM) by Takaishi-Kimura
[
24
,
34
]
.

For the Biot model, we studied a variational principle (Proposition
2.3
) and energy equality (Theorem
2.4
), which were related to different energies
E^{*}_{el}(u,\Theta)
and
E_{el}(u)+E_{th}(\Theta)
, respectively (see Tables
1
and
2
).

On the other hand, F-PFM has a gradient flow structure with respect to the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{el}(u,z)+E_{s}(z)
and admits energy equality (Theorem
3.1
).

As the first model, TF-PFM1 was derived based on the variational principle of the Biot model and the gradient flow structure of F-PFM, while TF-PFM2 is based on the energy equalities of the Biot model and F-PFM. The difference between them is the driving force term for the crack:
{W}^{*}(u,\Theta)
in TF-PFM1 (
3.10b
) and
W(u)
in TF-PFM2 (
3.14b
).

Consequently, we established partial energy equality for TF-PFM1 (Theorem
3.2
) and energy equality for TF-PFM2 (Theorem
3.3
). From the viewpoint of energy consistency, both models are satisfactory, but TF-PFM2 is more energetically consistent than TF-PFM1.

Based on the obtained numerical experiments, the following conclusions can be drawn.

1.

The thermoelastic coupling parameter
\delta
in TF-PFM1 and TF-PFM2 enhances crack propagation (Figure
11
).

2.

TF-PFM1 accelerates the crack speed more than TF-PFM2 (Figure
12
). On the other hand, the effect of the temperature gradient on the crack path in TF-PFM2 is larger than that in TF-PFM1 (Figure
18
-
20
).
