Integral Transforms and 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric Hamiltonians


Abstract
Motivated by the fact that twice the Fourier transform plays the role of parity operator. We systematically study integral transforms in the case of 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric Hamiltonian. First, we obtain a closed analytical formula for the exponential Fourier transform of a general 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric Hamiltonian. Using the Segal-Bargmann transform, we investigate the effect of the Fourier transform on the eigenfunctions of the original Hamiltonian. As an immediate application, we comment on the holomorphic representation of non-Hermitian spin chains, in which the Hamiltonian operator is written in terms of analytical phase-space coordinates and their partial derivatives in the Bargmann space rather than matrices in the vector Hilbert space. Finally, we discuss the effect of integral transforms in the study of the Swanson Hamiltonian.

Integral Transforms of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians

The integral equation problem is to find the solution to:

h(x)f(x)=g(x)+\lambda\int_{a}^{b}K(x,y)f(y)dx,

(2.1)

We are given
h(x),g(x),K(x,y)
and want to determine
f(x)
. The quantity
\lambda
is a parameter that may be complex in general, and
K(x,y)
is called the kernel of the integral equation. If
h(x)=0
, we may take
\lambda=-1
without loss of generality, and we end up with a Fredholm equation of the first kind or an integral transform.

Definition 2.1.

The integral transform of a function
f(x)
in the interval
a\leq x\leq b
denoted by
\mathcal{I}\{f(x)\}=F(k)
is
[
44
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\{f(x)\}=F(k)=\int^{b}_{a}K(x,k)f(x)dx,

(2.2)

where
K(x,k)
is called the kernel of the transform and
\mathcal{I}
is the transform operator
.

The integral transform for a function
f({\bf x})
with several variables is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\{f({\bf x})\}=F({\bf k})=\int_{D}K({\bf x},{\bf k})f({\bf x})d{\bf x},

(2.3)

where
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
,
{\bf k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n})
and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Apparently
\mathcal{I}
is a linear operator since

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\{\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)\}=\int_{a}^{b}\{\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)\}K(x,k)dx

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathcal{I}\{f(x)\}+\beta\mathcal{I}\{g(x)\}.

(2.5)

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the Fourier transform of
f(x)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is a linear transform which has eigenfunctions obeying

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\psi]=\lambda\psi,

(2.6)

with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Specifying the Fourier transform between position and momentum spaces, we define the transform as an integral of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{x}[f(x)](k)=F(k)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x)\;e^{-ikx}dx.

(2.7)

where the kernel of the Fourier transform (in our convention) is
e^{-ikx}
.
Clearly,
|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ikx}f(x)dx|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|dx
. This implies that the integral
2.7
exist for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
. One interesting feature of the Fourier transform is the generalized Parseval relation. Let
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\displaystyle\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\overline{g}\;dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F\overline{G}\;dk=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle F|G\rangle

(2.8)

Furthermore, if we define the convolution
f*g
of two functions to be the integral

\displaystyle f*g(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-y)g(y)dy.

(2.9)

Then, the Fourier transform is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[f*g(x)]=\mathcal{F}[f]\mathcal{F}[g].

(2.10)

In other words, the Fourier transform of a convolution of two functions
f,g
is the product of
their Fourier transforms. Other useful identities are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[fg]=\mathcal{F}[f]*\mathcal{F}[g],

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}(f)\mathcal{F}(g)]=f*g,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}(f)*\mathcal{F}(g)]=fg.

(2.13)

It is not difficult to realize that Fourier transform can be viewed as a special case of the bilateral Laplace transform with imaginary argument,

\displaystyle F(k)=\mathcal{F}_{x}[f(x)]=\mathcal{L}\{f(x)\}|_{s=ik}

(2.14)

where
\mathcal{L}\{f(x)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-sx}f(x)dx
. The definition
2.2
of integral transforms holds for quantum mechanical systems where the arguments of these transforms are in principle functions of canonical variables
F(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{i})
satisfying in general the canonical commutation relation
[\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}]=i\hbar\delta_{ij}
( in our case, we simply chose these canonical variables to be the position and momentum operators
x
and
p
defined in the continuous Hilbert space).

The Fourier transform is a four-periodic operator and has an intimate connection with the parity operator
\mathcal{P}f(x)=f(-x)
since

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{0}=\mathrm{id},

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{1}=\mathcal{F},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{2}=\mathcal{P},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{3}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}=\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{F},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{4}=\mathrm{id},

(2.19)

where
\mathrm{id}
is the identity operator. From the above relations, we find that applying twice the Fourier transform is identical to the action of the parity operator.

A Hamiltonian is called pseudo-Hermitian if there exists a Hermitian operator
\eta
such that
[
9
]

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}=\eta H\eta

(2.20)

The operator
\eta
is sometimes referred to as the metric operator.
In this case, the Hermitian indefinite inner product
\langle\langle\;\;|\;\;\rangle\rangle_{\eta}
is defined by
[
9
]

\displaystyle\langle\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\rangle_{\eta}:=\langle\psi_{1}|\eta|\psi_{2}\rangle

(2.21)

which is invariant under the time translation generated by the Hamiltonian
H
if and only if
H
is
\eta
-pseudo Hermitian. Obviously, when
\eta
is the identity operator, i.e.,
\eta=\mathrm{id}
, the condition
2.20
reduces to the Hermitian case. Since the parity operator is Hermitian, it can be used as a metric operator. In this case, the condition
2.20
becomes

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}H\mathcal{P}

(2.22)

Hence , in terms of the Fourier operator, it becomes

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}=\mathcal{F}^{2}H\mathcal{F}^{2}

(2.23)

The next step is to derive the expression for the Fourier transform of a general
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian. The exponential Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
of the operator

is
[

,

\mathcal{F}_{x}[\hat{f}(x)]=\hat{F}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(x)e^{-ik\cdot x}dx

and the inverse Fourier transform is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}^{-1}[\hat{F}(k)]=\hat{f(x)}=2\pi\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\hat{F}(k)e^{ikx}dk

(2.25)

where

are operators in the continuous Hilbert space.
Under parity (spatial reflection)

and under time-reversal

and

-symmetric Hamiltonian (in units

\displaystyle H=p^{2}+V_{{\mathrm{R}e}}(x)+iV_{{\mathrm{I}m}}(x)

(2.26)

where the real part of the potential

is even and the imaginary part
V_{{\mathrm{I}m}}
is odd under parity transformation. Note that both

. We define their exponential Fourier transforms as

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{R}e}(p)=2\int^{\infty}_{0}V_{\mathrm{R}e}(x)\cos(p\cdot x)\;dx,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{{\mathrm{I}m}}(p)=-2i\int^{\infty}_{0}V_{{\mathrm{I}m}}(x)\sin(p\cdot x)\;dx.

(2.28)

We plug
3.1
,
3.2
in the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\tilde{H}\phi(p)=p^{2}\phi(p)+2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{V}(p-p^{\prime})\phi(p^{\prime})dp^{\prime},

(2.29)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(p)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(V_{\mathrm{Re}}\left(x\right)\cos\left(x\cdot p\right)+V_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(x\right)\sin\left(x\cdot p\right)\right)dx.

(2.30)

Interestingly, performing the exponential Fourier transform of the Hamiltonian
2.26
removes the imaginary unit

in front of
V_{\mathrm{Im}}
and thus the Hamiltonian becomes apparently Hermitian in the momentum-space representation. Therefore, the non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian becomes Hermitian after performing the exponential Fourier transform, as seen in
2.29
. Due to the unitarity of the Fourier transform, one could easily prove that in the case of broken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry, complex pairs appear in the Hamiltonian written in the momentum space, and thus the discrete symmetries of a given Hamiltonian are unaffected by unitary transformations as expected.
In the above we have considered Hamiltonian with one-dimensional position spaces. However, the generalization to higher-dimensional spaces is straightforward.

Next, we need to investigate the effect of the exponential Fourier transform on the orthonormal basis attached to a quantum system . Consider, for example, the Hermite polynomials ,

H_{n}(x)=(-)^{n}\;e^{x^{2}}\;(\frac{d}{dx})^{n}(e^{-x^{2}})

(2.31)

which appears in many quantum mechanical problems, such as quantum harmonic oscillator. The Fourier transform of Hermite polynomials is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{e^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x)\}=(-i)^{n}e^{-p^{2}/2}H_{n}(p)

It is worthy to mention that the imaginary coefficient that appears in the front of the second-hand side
(-i)^{n}
has no effect on the measured energy eigenvalues. To prove this we compute the Segal-Bargmann transform of

. Let

be a function in the momentum Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(f(z))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-(z\cdot z-2\sqrt{2}z\cdot p-p\cdot p)/2}f(p)\;dp.

(2.33)

The Segal-Bargmann transform of Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
are simply complex monomials of the form
\{\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\}
. These monomials form an orthonormal basis since

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dz\;e^{-|z|^{2}}\;\overline{z}^{n}z^{m}=n!\;\delta_{mn},

(2.34)

where

Definition 2.2.

The Bargmann space, also known as the Fock-Bargmann or Segal-Bargmann space, denoted by
\mathcal{H}L^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu\right)
, is the space of holomorphic functions with Gaussian integral measure
\mu\left(z\right)=(\pi)^{-n}e^{-|z|^{2}}
and
|z|^{2}=|z_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|z_{n}|^{2}
.

Any entire analytic function
f\left(z\right)
in this space satisfy a square-integrability condition of the type
[
51
]

||f||^{2}:=\langle f|f\rangle_{\mu}=\left(\pi\right)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}|f\left(z\right)|^{2}e^{-|z|^{2}}dz<\infty,

(2.35)

where
dz
is the
2n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

Remark :
The inner-product between analytic functions
f\left(z\right)
and
g\left(z\right)
in the Bargmann space
\mathcal{H}L^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu\right)
is

\langle f|g\rangle_{\mu}=\left(\pi\right)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\overline{f}\left(z\right)g\left(z\right)e^{-|z|^{2}}dz.

(2.36)

Definition 2.3.

The Segal-Bargmann transform is an integral transformation from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathcal{H}L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu)
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(f(z))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{n}(z,x)f(x)d^{n}x\;\;\;for\;z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\;and\;f(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(2.37)

with
K_{n}(z,x)=e^{\left(z\cdot z-2\sqrt{2}z\cdot x-x\cdot x\right)/2}
the kernel integral.

Theorem 2.1
(Unitarity of the Segal-Bargmann transform).
The Segal-Bargmann transform defined by
2.37
is a unitary mapping between the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{H}L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu)
.

In Bargmann space
\mathcal{H}L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mu)
, the creation and annihilation operators of the harmonic oscillator are
z
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
since

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial z},z\right]f(z)=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(zf(z))-z\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z}=1

(2.38)

similar to the canonical commutation relation
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
. In this representation, the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian operator is
H=\hbar\omega\left(z\frac{d}{dz}+\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Applying to energy eigenstates (Fock states), we find

\displaystyle H|n\rangle=\hbar\omega\left(z\frac{d}{dz}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)|n\rangle.

(2.39)

Non-Hermitian Spin Chains

For a given entire analytic function
f_{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)=\frac{z^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\frac{w^{\beta}}{\sqrt{\beta!}}
, we define the spin operators in the two-dimensional Bargmann space
\mathcal{H}L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mu)
using Bargmann representation of the Jordan-Schwinger map as
[
53
]

\displaystyle S_{x}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial w}+w\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right),

(3.1)

\displaystyle S_{y}=\frac{\hbar}{2i}\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial w}-w\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right),

(3.2)

\displaystyle S_{z}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-w\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\right).

(3.3)

These operators belong to the
SU(2)
Lie algebra and obey the commutation relations
[S_{i},S_{j}]=i\hbar\;\varepsilon_{ijk}S_{k}
since the only non-trivial commutators between
z
,
w
and their partial derivatives are
\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial z},z\right]=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial w},w\right]=1
. The total number operator is

N=n_{z}+n_{w},

(3.4)

where
n_{z}=z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
and
n_{w}=w\frac{\partial}{\partial w}
.

The functions
\{f_{\alpha,\beta}\}
form an orthonormal basis in the two-dimensional Bargmann space since

\displaystyle\langle f_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(z,w)|f_{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)\rangle_{\mu}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int dzdw\;\exp[-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}]\overline{f}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(z,w)f_{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}\delta_{\beta^{\prime},\beta}

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
is the set of all natural numbers including zero i.e.
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

One advantage of Bargmann representation of spin operators appears clearly in the study of spin chains.
Consider the non-Hermitian

spin chain Hamiltonian

54
]
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\left[S_{j}^{x}S^{x}_{j+1}+S^{y}_{j}S^{y}_{j+1}+ig\left(S^{z}_{j}-S^{z}_{j+1}\right)\right],

(3.6)

where for small values of the coupling constant

[
54

.

In Bargmann representation,

can be written analytically as

\displaystyle H_{s}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\left(z_{j+1}w_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{j}\partial w_{j+1}}+z_{j}w_{j+1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{j}\partial z_{j+1}}\right)

(3.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{i\hbar g}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\left(z_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}-z_{j+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j+1}}+w_{j+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{j+1}}-w_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{j}}\right).

Since the Segal-Bargmann transform is unitary, the time evolution for spin chain Hamiltonians is the same both in operator and analytical form

The Swanson Hamiltonian is
[

,

,

,
59
]
,

\displaystyle H(\omega,\alpha,\beta)=\hbar\omega\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\hbar\alpha\;\hat{a}^{2}+\hbar\beta\;\hat{a}^{\dagger\;2},

(4.1)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are real unequal constants in general. When
\alpha=\beta
,

Eq.

can be written in terms of position and momentum operators by plugging the exact form of creation and annihilation operators,

\displaystyle\hat{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{x}{\xi_{0}}+i\frac{\xi_{0}}{\hbar}p\right),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{x}{\xi_{0}}-i\frac{\xi_{0}}{\hbar}p\right),

(4.3)

with

being the characteristic length scale of the system. We obtain

\displaystyle H(\omega,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\hbar\left(\omega+\alpha+\beta\right)\left(\frac{x}{\xi_{0}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hbar\left(\omega-\alpha-\beta\right)\left(\frac{\xi_{0}p}{\hbar}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hbar\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\left(\frac{2i}{\hbar}xp+1\right)

(4.4)

It is convenient in our analysis to make the Swanson-harmonic Hamiltonian mapping. This can be done with proper definitions of the position and momentum operators. Following
[
58
]
, we have

P=\left(p+i\hbar\frac{\alpha-\beta}{(\omega-\alpha-\beta)\xi_{0}^{2}}x\right),

(4.5)

X=x.

(4.6)

It is straightforward to verify the commutation relation
[X,P]=i\hbar
.

In terms of
X
and

, equation

becomes

H=\frac{1}{2m}P^{2}+\frac{k}{2}X^{2}

(4.7)

where

\displaystyle m=\frac{\hbar}{(\omega-\alpha-\beta)\xi_{0}^{2}}\in\mathbb{R},

(4.8)

\displaystyle k=m\Omega^{2}=m\sqrt{\omega^{2}-4\alpha\beta}\in\mathbb{C}.

(4.9)

As shown by Swanson in
[
56
]
,
4.1
posses real and positive energy eigenvalues whenever the condition
\omega^{2}-4\alpha\beta\geq 0
(
\Omega^{2}\geq 0
)
is satisfied. Indeed, the limiting case when
\Omega=0
and
\omega-\alpha-\beta\neq 0
corresponds to a free particle
[

]
.
The creation and annihilation operators are

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sqrt{\frac{m\Omega}{2\hbar}}\left(X+\frac{i}{m\Omega}P\right),

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}=\sqrt{\frac{m\Omega}{2\hbar}}\left(X-\frac{i}{m\Omega}P\right)

Since

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{2\hbar}}\left(Xe^{i\theta/2}+\frac{i}{m|\Omega|}Pe^{-i\theta/2}\right),

(4.12)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}=\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{2\hbar}}\left(Xe^{-i\theta/2}-\frac{i}{m|\Omega|}Pe^{i\theta/2}\right),

(4.13)

Obviously
[\hat{A},\hat{A}^{\dagger}]=1
. By virtue of Euler’s formula, i.e.,

,

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{A}\\

-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{b}\\
\hat{b}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}={\bf M}\cdot\vec{{\bf b}}

(4.14)

where

. When

N
is integer,
\mathrm{det}({\bf M})=1
and this corresponds to unitary transformation, the energy spectrum in this case is real. The operators
\hat{b}
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}
are the usual creation and annihilation operators for a harmonic oscillator with frequency
|\Omega|
and generalized momentum
P
. Next, consider
|\tilde{n}\rangle
to be the energy eigenvectors for
4.7
and
|n\rangle
to be the energy eigenvectors for a harmonic oscillator with frequency
|\Omega|
, then we have

\displaystyle\hat{A}|\tilde{n}\rangle=\sqrt{\tilde{n}}|\tilde{n}-1\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{b}|n\rangle+i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{b}^{\dagger}|n\rangle

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle+i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}|\tilde{n}\rangle=\sqrt{\tilde{n}+1}|\tilde{n}+1\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{b}^{\dagger}|n\rangle-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{b}|n\rangle

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle

From these results we notice that when
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=0
, the Hamiltonian
4.7
assumes real energy eigenvalues. The potential term in
4.7
can acquire an imaginary value and thus break the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry. Its exponential Fourier transform has the form

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(P)=\frac{m|\Omega|^{2}}{2}e^{2i\theta}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X^{2}e^{-iP\cdot X}dX=-\pi m|\Omega|^{2}e^{2i\theta}\delta^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(P)

(4.17)

where
\delta^{\prime\prime}
is the second-derivative of the Dirac delta function. The non-zero phase
\theta
breaks the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry in the Swanson model. When
\theta=0
, the energy eigenvalues of the Swanson Hamiltonian are real and the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry is exact.

The creation and annihilation operators
4.12
,
4.13
can be written in terms of the operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
as

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{\hbar}}\left(\left(s\xi_{0}+\frac{\hbar e^{-i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\hat{a}+\left(s\xi_{0}-\frac{\hbar e^{-i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),

(4.18)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{\hbar}}\left(\left(s\xi_{0}+\frac{\hbar e^{i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\left(s\xi_{0}-\frac{\hbar e^{i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\hat{a}\right)

(4.19)

where
s=\left(e^{i\theta/2}-\frac{\hbar(\alpha-\beta)e^{-i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|(\omega-\alpha-\beta)\xi_{0}^{2}}\right)
.

The operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{A}^{\dagger}
belong to pseudo-boson annihilation and creation operators introduced in
[
60
]
. Using Bargmann representation, we may write
\hat{A}
and
\hat{A}^{\dagger}
respectively, as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{\hbar}}\left(\left(s\xi_{0}+\frac{\hbar e^{-i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\left(s\xi_{0}-\frac{\hbar e^{-i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)z\right),

(4.20)

\displaystyle w=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m|\Omega|}{\hbar}}\left(\left(s\xi_{0}+\frac{\hbar e^{i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)z+\left(s\xi_{0}-\frac{\hbar e^{i\theta/2}}{m|\Omega|\xi_{0}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right),

(4.21)

with
[\frac{\partial}{\partial w},w]=1
. The eigenfunctions of the Swanson model are monomials of the phase-coordinate
w
i.e.
\frac{w^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}
. While the eigenfunctions associated with operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are
\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}
which by construction are the ordinary harmonic oscillator energy states.

5
Concluding Remarks

We have investigated the behavior of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians under integral transforms. The fact that twice the Fourier transform serves as a parity operator is used in the inner products of pseudo-Hermitian Hamiltonians. We discussed the effect of applying the exponential Fourier transform to a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian and investigated the resulting eigenfunctions in the momentum space. More interestingly, we applied another type of integral transform called the Segal-Bargmann transform to write the Hamiltonian in terms of the phase-space coordinates starting from the Hamiltonians in the position space. This was applied to the case of non-Hermitian
XX
spin chains which is spectral equivalent to their Hermitian counterparts in the weak coupling regime. As a model example, we studied the Swanson model in detail from integral transforms point of view. One possible application of the Fourier transforms in the context of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians can be found in the study of double well delta function potentials
[
61
]

V(x)=\left(1+i\Gamma\right)\delta\left(x+a\right)+\left(1-i\Gamma\right)\delta\left(x-a\right)

(5.1)

which has applications in the study of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Bose-Einstein condensates.
