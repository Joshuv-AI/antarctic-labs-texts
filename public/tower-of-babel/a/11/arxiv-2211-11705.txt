Spin Hall effect of radiofrequency waves in magnetized plasmas
Yichen Fu, I. Y. Dodin, Hong Qin

Abstract
In inhomogeneous media, electromagnetic-wave rays deviate from the trajectories predicted by the leading-order geometrical optics. This effect, called the spin Hall effect of light, is typically neglected in ray-tracing codes used for modeling waves in plasmas. Here, we demonstrate that the spin Hall effect can be significant for radiofrequency waves in toroidal magnetized plasmas whose parameters are in the ballpark of those used in fusion experiments. For example, an electron-cyclotron wave beam can deviate by as large as ten wavelengths ( ∼ 0.1 ​ m \sim 0.1\,\text{m} ) relative to the lowest-order ray trajectory in the poloidal direction. We calculate this displacement using gauge-invariant ray equations of extended geometrical optics, and we also compare our theoretical predictions with full-wave simulations.



I
Introduction

Precise modeling of radiofrequency (RF) waves in magnetic-fusion experiments is essential for many applications, including cyclotron heating, current drive
Fisch 1987
;
Prater et al. 2008
;
Figini et al. 2012
, and suppression of tearing modes
Reiman 1983
;
Petty et al. 2004
. Waves with short wavelengths, particularly those in the electron-cyclotron and lower-hybrid frequency range, are commonly modeled with ray-tracing codes
Kritz et al. 1982
;
Pereverzev 1998
;
Smirnov and Harvey 2001
;
Poli et al. 2001
;
Farina 2007
;
Marushchenko et al. 2014
, which are based on geometrical optics (GO)
Kravtsov and Orlov 1990
;
Tracy et al. 2014
. In the traditional, lowest-order GO, the evolution of the ray coordinate
\mathbf{x}
and momentum
\mathbf{k}
(wavevector) are governed by the Hamiltonian that equals the local dispersion function and ignores the local gradients of the medium parameters
Stix 1992
. However, the evolution of the wave polarization in an inhomogeneous medium produces corrections that make rays deviate from the trajectories predicted by this lowest-order Hamiltonian
Littlejohn and Flynn 1991
;
Weigert and Littlejohn 1993
.
This effect, which is similar to the spin-orbital interaction in quantum mechanics and the spin-orbital-like coupling in atomic systems
Zygelman 1990
;
Zygelman 2013
, is known as the spin Hall effect (SHE) of light. It is being actively studied as an important photonic effect in various physical systems
Onoda et al. 2004
;
Bliokh and Bliokh 2006
;
Bliokh et al. 2008
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
;
Ruiz and Dodin 2015a
;
Ruiz and Dodin 2015b
;
Ling et al. 2017
;
Perez et al. 2021
;
Yamamoto 2018
;
Oancea et al. 2020
. Plasma was recently identified as a medium where the SHE manifests
Ruiz and Dodin 2017
;
Ruiz 2017
;
Dodin et al. 2019
. But the importance of the SHE for practical plasma applications has not been explored, and the common ray-tracing codes used in fusion research ignore the SHE entirely
foo
. In this paper, we show that the SHE can be significant for RF waves in magnetized plasmas whose parameters are in the ballpark of those used in fusion experiments. The results reported enrich the understanding of the SHE and demonstrate the importance of an interdisciplinary approach to wave effects found in various media.

We calculate the SHE using equations of “extended GO” (XGO) as formulated in Refs.
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Ruiz and Dodin 2017
. By comparing XGO predictions with two-dimensional (2-D) full-wave (FW) simulations, we show that the ray equations that account for the SHE describe waves in fusion plasmas more accurately than the traditional ray equations. For three-dimensional (3-D) toroidal plasmas, we show that electromagnetic waves in the electron-cyclotron frequency range can deviate by as large as ten wavelengths in the poloidal plane relative to the GO predictions. Displacements of this size can be important in practice, for example, because processes like collisionless absorption and mode conversion can be very sensitive to the spatial distribution of the wave energy.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce basic theory. In Sec.
III
, we apply this theory to waves in cold magnetized plasma, specifically, in a plasma slab (Sec.
III.2
) and toroidal plasma (Sec.
III.3
). In Sec.
IV
, we conclude our results. Auxiliary calculations are presented in appendices.

II
Basic equations

Let us start by briefly restating the derivation of the XGO ray equations
Ruiz 2017
;
Dodin et al. 2019
. Consider a multicomponent wave field
\Psi
on a given space (or spacetime)
x^{\mu}
and suppose that this wave is governed by an equation of the form
\hat{D}\Psi=0
, where
\hat{D}
is some linear dispersion operator generally of integro-differential form. Assume that the wave has an eikonal form
\Psi(x^{\mu})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(x^{\mu})}\psi(x^{\mu})
. Here, the scalar function
\theta
is a fast real phase and
\bar{k}_{\mu}\doteq\partial_{\mu}\theta
is the associated wavevector, which is generally a field on
x^{\mu}
. (The symbol
\doteq
denotes definitions,
\partial_{\mu}\doteq\partial/\partial x^{\mu}
, and
\partial^{\mu}\doteq\partial/\partial k_{\mu}
.) The multicomponent function
\psi
represents a slow complex envelope governed by
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\psi=0
, where
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\doteq\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\hat{D}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. We assume that the least scale
L
of the envelope dynamics is much smaller than the local wavelength
\lambda\doteq 2\pi/\bar{k}
, yielding a small parameter
\epsilon\doteq\lambda/L\ll 1
(the “GO parameter”). Then, assuming Euclidean or pseudo-Euclidean (e.g., Minkowski) coordinates for simplicity, one obtains
Dodin et al. 2019

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}\approx\mathsf{D}(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{V}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}(\partial_{\mu}\mathsf{V}^{\mu})/2.

(1)

Here, the “local dispersion tensor”
\mathsf{D}
, which is a matrix function on the ray phase space
(x^{\mu},k_{\mu})
, is the Weyl symbol of
\hat{D}
, and
\mathsf{V}^{\mu}\doteq(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}(x^{\mu},k_{\mu}))|_{k_{\mu}=\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu})}
. We assume that the Hermitian part of the dispersion tensor,
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}\doteq(\mathsf{D}+\mathsf{D}^{\dagger})/2
, is
\mathcal{O}(1)
, while its anti-Hermitian part,
\mathsf{D}_{\rm A}\doteq(\mathsf{D}-\mathsf{D}^{\dagger})/(2\mathrm{i})
, is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, so one can use
\mathsf{V}^{\mu}(x^{\mu})\approx(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}(x^{\mu},k_{\mu}))|_{k_{\mu}=\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu})}
. Then,
\mathsf{D}_{\rm A}
determines local damping, while the wave propagation is determined entirely by
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}
, namely, as follows.

To the zeroth order in
\epsilon
, Eq. (
1
) gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))\psi(x^{\mu})=0,

(2)

so
\psi
is the eigenvector of
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}
corresponding to an eigenvalue
\Lambda
that is zero on the solution at
\epsilon\to 0
. To eliminate mode conversion, which makes the analysis more complicated
Dodin et al. 2019
, let us assume that
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}(x^{\mu},k_{\mu})
has only one eigenvalue that is zero at
k_{\mu}=\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu})
; we call it an active mode. Then, Eq. (
2
) can be restated as follows. Consider the corresponding unit eigenvector
\eta
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}\eta=\Lambda\eta,\quad\eta^{\dagger}\eta=1,\quad\Lambda=\eta^{\dagger}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}\eta,

(3)

where all quantities are considered as functions of
(x^{\mu},k_{\mu})
. Then,
\Lambda(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))=0

\psi(x^{\mu})=\eta(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))a(x^{\mu})
, where

is a scalar amplitude. To separate the phase dynamics from the amplitude dynamics completely, we attribute the
whole

\theta
and
\eta

is real by definition. We note that
\eta
is defined only up to

\varphi
is real and slow but otherwise arbitrarily. This constitutes a U(1) gauge symmetry of XGO, with
\varphi
being the gauge potential (see below).

To the first order in
\epsilon
, Eq. (
1
) gives
\psi(x^{\mu})=\eta(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))a(x^{\mu})+\psi_{\perp}(x^{\mu})
, where
\psi_{\perp}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
is perpendicular to
\eta
. Then, projecting Eq. (
1
) on
\eta
gives
Dodin et al. 2019

\displaystyle(\Lambda-U)a-\mathrm{i}[V^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+(\partial_{\mu}V^{\mu})/2-\Gamma]a=0.

(4)

Here,
\Lambda
is as in Eq. (
3
),
\Gamma\doteq\eta^{\dagger}\mathsf{D}_{\rm A}\eta
,
U\doteq\im[(\partial_{\mu}\eta^{\dagger})\mathsf{V}^{\mu}\eta]
, and
\eta=\eta(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))
, so
\partial_{\mu}
applies to both its arguments. Since
a
is real by definition, the imaginary part of Eq. (
1
) gives an amplitude equation
[V^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+(\partial_{\mu}V^{\mu})/2-\Gamma]a=0
, and the real part of Eq. (
1
) gives a dispersion relation
\mathcal{H}(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))=0
, where
\mathcal{H}\doteq\Lambda-U
and
Dodin et al. 2019

\displaystyle U=U_{0}-\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\,\partial^{\mu}\Lambda+\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\Lambda,

(5)

\displaystyle U_{0}\doteq\im\left[\left(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}}\right)\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\partial_{\mu}\eta\right)\right],

(6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\doteq\im\left(\eta^{{\dagger}}\partial_{\mu}\eta\right),\quad\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\doteq\im\left(\eta^{{\dagger}}\partial^{\mu}\eta\right).

(7)

As usual
Tracy et al. 2014
, the corresponding ray equations are

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{\mu}=\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{H},\quad\dot{k}_{\mu}=-\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{H},

(8)

where the dot denotes
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\tau
,
\tau
is a parameter along the ray, and
k_{\mu}
represents
\bar{k}_{\mu}
(the bar is omitted from now on for brevity). These correspond to the canonical Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}=k_{\mu}\dot{x}^{\mu}-\mathcal{H}
. But note that
\dot{x}^{\mu}=\partial^{\mu}\Lambda+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
,
\dot{k}_{\mu}=-\partial_{\mu}\Lambda+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
,
\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, and
\mathsf{A}^{\mu}_{(k)}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, so
U\approx U_{0}-\dot{x}^{\mu}\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}-\dot{k}_{\mu}\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. Omitting
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, which is negligible within the assumed accuracy, one arrives at an equivalent noncanonical Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\left[\dfrac{k_{\mu}}{2}-\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\right]\dot{x}^{\mu}-\left[\dfrac{x^{\mu}}{2}+\mathsf{A}^{\mu}_{(k)}\right]\dot{k}_{\mu}-(\Lambda-U_{0}),

(9)

where an insignificant full derivative
\mathrm{d}(k_{\mu}x^{\mu}/2)/\mathrm{d}\tau

\mathsf{A}
define the Berry connections
Simon 1983
. It plays the role of the Abelian vector potential in the present context. The corresponding Euler–Lagrange equations are as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=+\partial^{\mu}(\Lambda-U_{0})+{\mathsf{F}^{\mu}}_{\nu}\dot{x}^{\nu}+\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}\,\dot{k}_{\nu},

(10a)

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\mu}(\Lambda-U_{0})-{\mathsf{F}_{\mu}}^{\nu}\dot{k}_{\nu}-\mathsf{F}_{\mu\nu}\,\dot{x}^{\nu},

(10b)

where the functions
\mathsf{F}
(with various indices) are understood as the Berry curvatures:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}^{\mu}}_{\nu}

\displaystyle\doteq 2\im[(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}})\partial_{\nu}\eta],\,\,\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}\doteq 2\im[(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}})\partial^{\nu}\eta],

(11a)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}_{\mu}}^{\nu}

\displaystyle\doteq 2\im[(\partial_{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}})\partial^{\nu}\eta],\,\,\mathsf{F}_{\mu\nu}\doteq 2\im[(\partial_{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}})\partial_{\nu}\eta].

(11b)

This result generalizes the corresponding equations in Refs.
Perez et al. 2021
to arbitrary dispersive operators
\hat{D}
. In particular, one can show (see Appendix
A
) that Eqs. (
10
) are gauge-invariant, i.e., unaffected by a variable transformation
\eta\to\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\eta
. Still, Eqs. (
6
) and (
11
) can be inconvenient for numerical integration. Assuming that
\eta
is provided by a generic eigensolver, having its phase arbitrary makes
\eta(x^{\mu},k_{\mu})
discontinuous, resulting in large
\mathsf{F}
. This undermines both the accuracy of the perturbation model and the numerical stability of the integrator. A more practical form of
U_{0}
and
\mathsf{F}
is derived in Appendix
B
and generalize the corresponding formulas from Refs.
Berry 1984
;
Perez et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle U_{0}=\mathrm{Im}\sum_{m}\dfrac{\eta^{{\dagger}}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{{\dagger}}(\partial_{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H})\eta}{\Lambda_{m}},

(12a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}=2\mathrm{Im}\sum_{m}\dfrac{\eta^{{\dagger}}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{{\dagger}}(\partial^{\nu}\mathsf{D}_{\rm H})\eta}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}.

(12b)

Lowering the indices
\mu
and
\nu
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
12b
) yields the corresponding components of
\mathsf{F}
with lower and mixed indices. Here,
\eta_{m}
and
\Lambda_{m}
are the unit eigenvectors and the corresponding eigenvalues of
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}
that correspond to modes with
\Lambda_{m}\neq\Lambda
; we call them passive modes. These equations have the benefit of “numerical gauge invariance” in that changing
\eta\to\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\eta
leaves both
U_{0}
nor
\mathsf{F}
intact. It is also seen from Eqs. (
12
) that the SHE is amplified when one or more of the passive modes is in resonance with the active mode, namely,
\Lambda_{m}\approx 0
. In this regime, one can also expect mode conversion, i.e., tunneling of the wave action between separate dispersion surfaces. However, this tunneling scales with
\Lambda_{m}
exponentially
Tracy et al. 2014
;
Dodin et al. 2017
, while the SHE scales with
\Lambda_{m}
algebraically, so it can be amplified at small
\Lambda_{m}
while the mode conversion remains negligible.

The SHE in the present study originates from the internal degree of freedom associated with vector components of the photon field in plasmas. It is also similar to the spin-orbital-like coupling that appears in the non-Abelian gauge field theory for electron wave functions in atomic systems
Zygelman 1990
;
Zygelman 2013
, but the mechanism there is different. Another difference is that the gauge potential in the present context is Abelian, since we only study here the propagation of one single mode away from the mode-conversion region, and the eigenmode is non-degenerate. For the plasma waves near the mode conversion region, the effects of eigenmode degeneracy are important
Ruiz and Dodin 2017
. Then a non-Abelian gauge theory similar to that in atomic systems
Zygelman 1990
;
Zygelman 2013
is necessary.

III
Waves in cold magnetized plasma

III.1
Basic equations

Let us apply the above results to a cold-plasma model, which is commonly used for ray tracing in fusion devices
Prater et al. 2008
. For simplicity, let us neglect the ion response, plasma flows, and dissipation, and let us also assume that the waves have a fixed frequency
\omega
, as usual. Then, the linear-wave equations can be written as

, where
\mathsf{D}=\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}=H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})-\omega

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\mathbf{x},-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mathbf{x}})=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\mathbf{x})\times&\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{x})&0\\
-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{x})&0&\mathrm{i}c\,\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\times\\
0&-\mathrm{i}c\,\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\times&0\end{pmatrix},

(13)

and
\Psi(t,\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})^{\intercal}
is a 9-dimensional column vector (the symbol

denotes transposition) that includes the properly normalized velocity
\mathbf{v}
, the wave electric field
\mathbf{E}
, and the wave magnetic field
\mathbf{B}

Dodin and Startsev 2021
. Also,
\times
denotes vector product, as usual;
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{x})=[4\pi q^{2}\bar{n}(\mathbf{x})/m]^{1/2}
is the plasma frequency;

m

are the electron charge, mass, and background density, correspondingly;

\bar{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x})
is the background magnetic field; and

is the speed of light. In fusion applications of the electron waves governed by Eq. (

\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}

Typically, Berry curvature arises from degeneracy points in the dispersion relation that behave as magnetic monopoles

. In the cold plasma model considered here, there are four degeneracy points known as Weyl points

;
Fu and Qin 2021
. The spatial locations where
\omega_{\rm p}
and
\Omega
are zero also serve as degeneracy points. In addition, Berry curvature has a contribution from infinite
k

\epsilon\ll 1

III.2
SHE in a plasma slab

IV
Conclusions

Here, we present the first systematic study of the SHE for magnetized plasmas. We start with the XGO formulation of the SHE and derive a gauge-invariant form of the ray equations that describe SHE for general waves [Eq. (
10
)]. We also express the right-hand side in a form that is better suited for simulations due to its “numerical gauge invariance” [Eqs. (
12
)]. Then, we perform ray-tracing simulations based on these equations for electromagnetic waves in a cold magnetized collisionless electron plasma slab and compare them with 2-D FW simulations. We show that the FW simulations are in better agreement with XGO, which retains the SHE, than they are with GO simulations, which ignores the SHE. Finally, we present an example of a large SHE in toroidal plasma, where a wave beam deviates from the GO trajectory by a distance of roughly ten wavelengths. In fusion devices, such a large deviation can significantly affect resonant absorption of radiofrequency waves. Therefore, typical ray-tracing codes that are based on GO instead of XGO are at risk of missing important physics, even if
usually
the SHE is less pronounces than in our example.

Appendix A
Gauge invariance of the ray Lagrangian

In this section, we prove the gauge invariance of the ray Lagrangian defined in Eq. (
9
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\left[\dfrac{k_{\mu}}{2}-\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\right]\dot{x}^{\mu}-\left[\dfrac{x^{\mu}}{2}+\mathsf{A}^{\mu}_{(k)}\right]\dot{k}_{\mu}-(\Lambda-U_{0}),

where

\displaystyle U=U_{0}-\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\,\partial^{\mu}\Lambda+\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\Lambda,

(16)

\displaystyle U_{0}\doteq\im\left[\left(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{{\dagger}}\right)\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\partial_{\mu}\eta\right)\right],

(17)

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\doteq\im\left(\eta^{{\dagger}}\partial_{\mu}\eta\right),\quad\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\doteq\im\left(\eta^{{\dagger}}\partial^{\mu}\eta\right).

(18)

As a reminder, we assume the notation
\partial_{\mu}\doteq\partial/\partial x^{\mu}
and
\partial^{\mu}\doteq\partial/\partial k_{\mu}
, and
\eta
denotes a unit polarization vector of the active eigenmode, which is also an eigenvector of
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}\eta=\Lambda\eta,\quad\eta^{\dagger}\eta=1,\quad\Lambda=\eta^{\dagger}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}\eta,

(19)

and
\Lambda
is the corresponding eigenvalue.

Let us consider a gauge transformation
\eta\to\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\eta
, where
\varphi(x^{\mu},k_{\mu})
is a real function. This transformation does not affect
\Lambda
, while
U_{0}
is transformed as follows:

\displaystyle U_{0}\to\im\big[(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{\dagger})\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\partial_{\mu}\eta)+(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\Lambda+\Phi\big],

(20)

\displaystyle\Phi=\mathrm{i}\Lambda\big[(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)(\partial^{\mu}\eta^{\dagger})\eta-(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)\eta^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}\eta)\big].

(21)

Since both
\varphi
and
\Lambda
are real, the term
(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\Lambda
is real and therefore does not contribute to
U_{0}
. Also,

\displaystyle 0=\partial(\eta^{\dagger}\eta)=(\partial\eta^{\dagger})\eta+\eta^{\dagger}(\partial\eta),

(22)

so
(\partial\eta^{\dagger})\eta
is purely imaginary. Here and further,
\partial
denotes a derivative with respect to any coordinate in the ray phase space
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})
. Hence,
\Phi
is real and therefore does not contribute to
U_{0}
either. Then, according to Eq. (
20
), the gauge transformation leaves
U_{0}
intact.

Now let us consider the Berry connections
\mathsf{A}
. These functions transform as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{(x)}_{\mu}\to\im\left(\eta^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\eta+\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\right)=\mathsf{A}^{(x)}_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\varphi,

(23)

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\dot{x}^{\mu}

\displaystyle+\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\dot{k}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\dot{x}^{\mu}+\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\dot{k}_{\mu}+(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\dot{x}^{\mu}+(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)\dot{k}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{A}_{\mu}^{(x)}\dot{x}^{\mu}+\mathsf{A}_{(k)}^{\mu}\dot{k}_{\mu}+\dot{\varphi}.

(24)

The extra term
\dot{\varphi}
is a total time derivative and thus does not contribute to the equation of motion. Therefore, up to this insignificant full time derivative, the non-canonical Lagrangian (
9
) is gauge-invariant and so are the corresponding ray equations.

Appendix B
Derivation of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}
and
\boldsymbol{U_{0}}

The formulas for
\mathsf{F}
and
U_{0}
are well-known in condensed matter physics, but not so well known for general waves and in plasma physics in particular, so it is instructive to present their general derivation. Here, we do so by adapting the corresponding calculation from Ref.
Berry 1984
.

Because
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}
is a Hermitian matrix, one can choose its orthonormal eigenvectors to form a complete basis. Let us consider any one of them,
\eta_{n}
, and decompose its derivative (with respect to any given parameter) in this basis:

\displaystyle\partial\eta_{n}=(\eta_{n}^{\dagger}\partial\eta_{n})\eta_{n}+\sum_{s\neq n}(\eta_{s}^{\dagger}\partial\eta_{n})\eta_{s}.

(25)

To calculate the coefficients in the latter sum, let us differentiate
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}\eta_{n}=\Lambda_{n}\eta_{n}
and multiply the result by
\eta_{m}^{\dagger}
with
m\neq n
from the left. This gives

\displaystyle\eta_{m}^{\dagger}\partial\eta_{n}=\dfrac{\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{\Lambda_{n}-\Lambda_{m}}\quad(m\neq n),

(26)

assuming
\Lambda_{n}\neq\Lambda_{m}
. Hence, one can rewrite Eq. (
25
) as

\displaystyle\partial\eta_{n}=\left(\eta_{n}^{\dagger}\partial\eta_{n}\right)\eta_{n}+\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{\Lambda_{n}-\Lambda_{m}}\eta_{m}.

(27)

Plugging this into Eq. (
17
) yields

\displaystyle U_{0}=\im\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{n}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{(\Lambda_{m}-\Lambda_{n})^{2}/\Lambda_{m}}.

(28)

Similarly, plugging Eq. (
27
) into

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}\doteq 2\im[(\partial^{\mu}\eta_{n}^{{\dagger}})\partial^{\nu}\eta_{n}]

(29)

and making use of the fact that
(\partial\eta_{n}^{\dagger})\eta_{n}
is purely imaginary [cf. Eq. (
22
)], leads to

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}=2\im\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{n}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\nu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{(\Lambda_{n}-\Lambda_{m})^{2}}.

(30)

Finally, let us choose
\eta_{n}
to be the polarization vector of the active mode,
\eta_{n}=\eta
, so
\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda
. By the dispersion relation, one has
\Lambda=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, so within the assumed accuracy Eqs. (
28
) and (
30
) can as well be written as follows:

\displaystyle U_{0}=\im\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{n}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{\Lambda_{m}},

(31)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mu\nu}=2\im\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{n}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\mu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{m}\eta_{m}^{\dagger}(\partial^{\nu}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}})\eta_{n}}{\Lambda_{m}^{2}}.

(32)

Similar formulas for
{\mathsf{F}^{\mu}}_{\nu}
,
{\mathsf{F}_{\mu}}^{\nu}
, and
\mathsf{F}_{\mu\nu}
are derived in the same way.

Appendix C
Construction of wave packets

Within XGO, terms
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
in expressions for the field are not negligible
Dodin et al. 2019
. In particular, they must be retained when constructing initial wave packets for full-wave simulations if the spin Hall effect must be retained. In this section, we derive an explicit approximation for these terms and apply it to cold-plasma waves.

A slow complex envelope
\psi(x^{\mu})
is governed by

\hat{\mathcal{D}}\psi=0,

(33)

where
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
is approximated by Eq. (1) of the main article up to insignificant terms
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. This operator can be represented as
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\approx\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))+\hat{\mathscr{D}}
, where
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\hat{\mathscr{D}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
under the assumptions specified in the main article. Then, one can expand
\psi
in the basis of orthonromal eigenvectors of
\mathsf{D}_{\rm H}(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu}))
as

\psi(x^{\mu})=\sum_{m}\eta_{m}\left(x^{\mu},\bar{k}_{\mu}(x^{\mu})\right)a_{m}(x^{\mu}),

(34)

where
a_{n}
are scalar amplitudes. Then, Eq. (
33
) gives

\sum_{m}\Lambda_{m}\eta_{m}a_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(35)

whence
\Lambda_{m}a_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
for each
m
. Consider the region where
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}
has only one eigenvalue that is close to zero, say
\Lambda_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, i.e., only the
n
th mode is on-shell (propagating wave that satisfies a dispersion relation). Such mode may have
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and is called an active mode. The remaining modes, for which
\Lambda_{m}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, have to have
a_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and are called passive modes
Dodin et al. 2019
.

Although small, passive modes must be accounted in simulations that are intended to capture the SHE. In particular, the initial wave envelope
\psi(t=0)
must be constructed with accuracy not less than
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. To do, let us represent the envelope as
\psi=\eta_{n}a_{n}+\psi_{\perp}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\perp}\doteq\sum_{m\neq n}\eta_{m}a_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(36)

Then,

\displaystyle(\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}+\hat{\mathscr{D}})(\eta_{n}a_{n}+\psi_{\perp})=0,

(37)

where an error
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
in the approximation of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
has been ignored. Since
\hat{\mathscr{D}}\psi_{\perp}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, this term can be ignored as well. Then, multiplying the equation by
\eta_{m}^{\dagger}
with
m\neq n
from the left, one obtains

a_{m}=-\dfrac{\eta_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\eta_{n}a_{n})}{\Lambda_{m}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(38)

Therefore, up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, the total wave field
\Psi
can be expressed in the following form parameterized by a single function,
a_{n}
:

\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\left(a_{n}\eta_{n}-\sum_{m\neq n}\dfrac{\eta_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\eta_{n}a_{n})}{\Lambda_{m}}\right).

(39)

For cold plasma, the dispersion operator is
\hat{D}=\hat{H}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}
, where
\hat{H}(\mathbf{x},-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mathbf{x}})
is defined in Eq. (12) of the main article. Using
\theta=k_{\mu}x^{\mu}
and
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}=H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})-\omega
, the operator
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{H}}+\hat{\mathscr{D}}
can be calculated exactly:

\hat{\mathscr{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&\mathrm{i}c\,\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\times\\
0&-\mathrm{i}c\,\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\times&0\end{pmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t},

(40)

where
\partial_{t}=0
for stationary waves.
