2.
Intermittency of the Anderson parabolic problem

The intermittency phenomenon is associated with the progressive growth of moment functions

The existence-uniqueness theorem for stochastic differential equations governing

in a weighted Hilbert space, along with the Itô formula, supports the derivation of equations (

of the stochastic differential equation (

are finite. Furthermore, these moment functions satisfy the

-particle type Schrödinger equation (
1.9
)
Since
\|\varkappa\mathcal{L}\|\leq\varkappa
and
\max\limits_{x\in Z^{d}}|B(x)|=\max\limits_{x\in Z^{d}}B(x)=B(0)

in (

Note also that since

,

\displaystyle m_{p}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{p})

\displaystyle=<u(t,x_{1})u(t,x_{2})\cdots u(t,x_{p})>

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{p}<u^{p}(t,x_{1})+\cdots+u^{p}(t,x_{p})>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}<u^{p}(t,x_{j})>=m_{p}(t,0,0,\cdots,0)

Due to Kac-Feynman formula,

(2.2)

\displaystyle m_{p}(t,0,\cdots,0)=E_{(0,0,\cdots,0)}e^{\int_{0}^{t}V_{p}(x_{1}(s),x_{2}(s),\cdots,x_{p}(s))ds}

Remark 2.1
.

The field
u(t,x)
, representing solutions to the Anderson parabolic problem, is both homogeneous and ergodic. The correlation functions of any order are finite. Specifically, it leads to the following ergodic theorem,

(2.4)

\displaystyle<u^{p_{0}}(t,x)>=<u^{p_{0}}(t,0)>=\lim\limits_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{(2L)^{d}}\sum\limits_{|y|\leq L}u^{p_{0}}(t,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\log}}{{\sim}}e^{\gamma_{p_{0}}t},\quad t\to\infty.

Let
0<\delta<\gamma_{p_{0}}/p_{0}
. Chebyshev’s inequality and the relation
<u(t,y)>\equiv 1,~y\in Z^{d},
imply

\displaystyle P(\frac{\ln u(t,y)}{t}\geq\frac{\gamma_{p_{0}}}{p_{0}}-\delta)=P(u(t,y)\geq e^{t(\frac{\gamma_{p_{0}}}{p_{0}}-\delta)})\leq<u(t,y)>e^{-t(\frac{\gamma_{p_{0}}}{p_{0}}-\delta)}=e^{-\delta_{1}t},~~\delta_{1}>0.

For a fixed
\delta
and a large
L
, consider the cube
Q_{L}=\{y:|y|<L\}
which we can partition into the set
Q_{L}^{\delta}=\{y\in Q_{L}:u(t,y)\leq e^{-t(\frac{\gamma_{p_{0}}}{p_{0}}-\delta)}\}
and the complement set containing large values of
u
. In a logarithmic sense, we have
\frac{1}{(2L)^{d}}\sum\limits_{Q_{L}^{\delta}}<u^{p_{0}}(t,x)>\leq e^{t(\gamma_{p_{0}}-p_{0}\delta)}
, which is significantly smaller than the logarithmic asymptotic
e^{\gamma_{p_{0}}t}
of
<u^{p_{0}}(t,0)>
. This indicates that the primary contribution to
<u^{p_{0}}(t,0)>
arises from the sparse high peaks where
u(t,x)>e^{-t(\frac{\gamma_{p_{0}}}{p_{0}}-\delta)}
. The density
\pi(t,\delta)=|Q_{L}\setminus Q_{L}^{\delta}|/|Q_{L}|
of such points in
Q_{L}
,
L\rightarrow\infty
, is exponentially small in
t
:

\displaystyle\pi(t,\delta)\leq e^{-t\delta_{1}}.

A classic example of
intermittent field
is the magnetic field of the Sun and a diverse array of white and yellow stars. The energy of magnetic field
\vec{H}(t,x)
is proportional to the 2nd moment
<\vec{H}(t,x_{1})\vec{H}(t,x_{2})>
. The Lyapunov exponents of

satisfy the same relation

for hot stars including the Sun.
Nearly all magnetic energy of

is concentrated within the black spots, covering extremely small regions of the Sun’s surface, as discussed in
[
20
]
. The lattice parabolic Anderson model, introduced in
[
3
]
, serves as a simplified representation of the Maxwell equations governing the magnetic field in the turbulent flow of solar plasma.

Theorem 2.2
.

The field

, representing the solution to the equation (

. This holds without any additional restrictions on the jump distribution

Proof.

Due to (

\displaystyle M_{p}(t)=\max\limits_{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{p}}m_{p}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{p})=M_{p}(t,0,\cdot 0)\leq\exp\{\frac{p(p-1)}{2}B(0)t\},

i.e.,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{p}}{p}\leq\frac{p-1}{2}B(0),p=2,3,\cdots~.

At the same time,

\displaystyle M_{p}(t,0,\cdots,0)\geq E_{0,0,\cdots,0}[I_{\{y_{1}(s)=y_{2}(s)=\cdots=y_{p}(s)=0,s\in[0,t]\}}]e^{\frac{p(p-1)}{2}B(0)t}

\displaystyle=\exp\{-p\varkappa dt+\frac{p(p-1)}{2}B(0)t\},

i.e.,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{p}}{p}\geq\frac{p-1}{2}B(0)-\varkappa d.

Thus,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{p}}{p}>0\quad\,\,\text{if}\quad\,\,p>2+\frac{2\varkappa}{B(0)}.

This implies the weak intermittency of the field

and provides an estimate from below on the borderline value of

3.
Spectral Analysis of
H_{2}
and the strong intermittency

The analysis of the second Lyapunov exponent
\gamma_{2}
(or the top of the spectrum of the operator
H_{2}
) depends significantly on the properties of the random walk
x(t)
associated with “Laplacian"
\varkappa\mathcal{L}
. If
p(t,x,y)=P\{x(t)=y|x(0)=x\}
,
t>0,x,y\in Z^{d}
, then
p(t,x,y)
is the fundamental solution of the following parabolic problem:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dt}

\displaystyle=2\varkappa\mathcal{L}p,t>0,\quad p(0,x,y)=\delta_{y}(x).

The process
x(t)
is called recurrent if
\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,x)dt=\infty
, otherwise, it is transient.

Using the Fourier transform, we obtain:

(3.2)

p(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{T^{d}}e^{2\varkappa\hat{\mathcal{L}}(k)t}e^{ik(x-y)}dk,\,\,\,k\in T^{d}=[-\pi,\pi]^{d}.

Here,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(k)=\sum\limits_{z\neq 0}(\cos(k,z)-1)a(z)=\hat{a}(k)-1
represents the Fourier symbol of the operator
\mathcal{L}
.
When
x=y
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,x)dt=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{T^{d}}\frac{dk}{2\varkappa(1-\hat{a}(k))}.

If
E[x^{2}(t)]<\infty
, i.e.,
\sum\limits_{z\in Z^{d}}|z|^{2}a(z)<\infty
, then
\hat{a}(k)=1-\frac{1}{2}(Dk,k)+O(|k|^{2})
, where
D
is the matrix of the second moments of
x(t)
. In this case
p(t,x,x)\sim\frac{c}{t^{d/2}},~t\to\infty,
, and the process
x(t)
is transient for dimensions
d\geq 3
and recurrent for
d=1,2
.

We consider a more general situation when the second moment of
x(t)
is infinite. We impose a certain regularity condition on the symbol
-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(k)=1-\hat{a}(k)
of the operator
\mathcal{L}
. We assume that

(3.3)

\hat{a}(k)=e^{-\beta(\dot{k})|k|^{\alpha}(1+o(|k|))},\quad|k|\rightarrow 0,

where
\dot{k}=\frac{k}{|k|}
,
0<\alpha\leq 2
, and
\beta(\dot{k})=\beta(-\dot{k})>0
is a symmetric and positive function on
T^{d}
. Both functions
\beta
on the sphere and the remainder term in the exponent are assumed to be sufficiently smooth. Corresponding conditions on the jump distribution
a(x)
can be found in
[
1
]
.

Under these conditions,
p(t,x,x)\sim t^{-d/\alpha}
as
t\to\infty
. Since
p(t,x,y)\leq 1
, we have
p(t,x,x)\leq Ct^{-d/\alpha},~t\geq 0,
and the process
x(t)
is recurrent for
d=2,\alpha=2
, and for
d=1,1\leq\alpha\leq 2
. It is transient in the case of
d=2,\alpha<2
and
d=1,\alpha<1
.

We continue to study the question of the existence of a positive eigenvalue for the Schrödinger operator

Since an analysis of the ground states

]
, see Theorem 1.2:

N_{0}(V)\leq 1+2\sum_{x\in Z^{d}}|V(x)|\lim_{\lambda\to+0}[R_{\lambda}(x)-R_{\lambda}(0)]|,

where
R_{\lambda}(x-y)
is the kernel of the resolvent of operator
\mathcal{L}
.
The limit above does not exceed

\int_{T^{d}}|e^{ikx}-1|[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(k)]^{-1}dk\leq\int_{T^{d}}|kx|[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(k)]^{-1}dk.

The last two relations imply that
N_{0}(V)<2
when
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
. Thus
H
has at most one eigenvalue in this case, and it exists due to Theorem
3.2
.
∎

3.3.
Zero average potentials, one-dimensional case

The goal of this subsection is to expand the important part of Theorem
3.2
on the existence of positive eigenvalues to a particular case of operators
H
with
\sum_{x\in Z^{d}}V(x)\geq 0
where the sum can be equal to zero. The operator
H
will be a perturbation of the second derivative on the one-dimensional lattice. We would like to get this result also in the continuous case and therefore, we will consider simultaneously operators
H
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and in
L^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by the relations

(3.12)

H\psi=\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\sigma V(x)\psi(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}~{\rm or}~\mathbb{Z},\quad{\rm where}\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(1+|x|)|V(x)|dx<\infty,~~V\not\equiv 0,

Here and below,
\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)
and the integrals must be replaced by
\psi(x-1)+\psi(x+1)-2\psi(x)
and the corresponding infinite sums, respectively, when
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let us stress that the assumption on the behavior of the potential at infinity in (
3.12
) is stronger than in (
3.7
).

Theorem 3.5
.

If
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x)dx\geq 0,~~V\not\equiv 0,
then operator
H
defined in (
3.12
) has a positive eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda(\sigma)
for each
\sigma>0
.

Remark 3.6
.

The proof below will be given in the continuous case
x\in\mathbb{R}
, but one needs only to replace integrals with sums for the proof to be valid in the lattice case.

Remark 3.7
.

This statement for the operator on the half-axis
x>0
with the boundary condition
\psi^{\prime}(0)=0
was proved in
[
13
]

We will need the following lemma (a similar statement can be found in
[
13
]
).

Lemma 3.8
.

When
|\sigma|\ll 1
, there are solutions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
of the equation
H\psi=0
on the half axis
x\geq 0
, where
H
is defined in (
3.12
), such that

(3.13)

\psi_{1}(x)=1+O(\sigma),\quad\psi_{1}^{\prime}(x)=O(\sigma),\quad x\geq 0;~\quad\psi_{1}(0)=1,\quad\psi_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\alpha(\sigma),

(3.14)

\psi_{2}(x)=x(1+O(\sigma)),\quad\psi_{2}^{\prime}(x)=1+O(\sigma),\quad x\geq 0;~\quad\psi_{2}(0)=0,\quad\psi_{2}^{\prime}(0)=1.

Here the remainder terms are uniform in
x
and

(3.15)

\alpha(\sigma)=\sigma b+\sigma^{2}c^{2}+O(\sigma^{3}),\quad b=\int_{0}^{\infty}V(\xi)d\xi,\quad c^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\eta}^{\infty}V(\xi)d\xi\right)^{2}d\eta.

Proof.

We look for a complementary bounded solution
\psi
in the form
\psi=1+z(x)
. Then
z^{\prime\prime}+\sigma V(x)z=-\sigma V(x)
, and this equation can be reduced to the integral equation

(3.16)

z(x)+\sigma Pz=-\sigma\int_{x}^{\infty}(\xi-x)V(\xi)d\xi,\quad x\geq 0,\quad{\rm where}\quad Pz=\int_{x}^{\infty}(\xi-x)V(\xi)z(\xi)d\xi.

Operator
P
in the space
C
of continuous bounded functions on the semi-axis
x\geq 0
is bounded, and the right-hand side in (
3.16
) has the from
-\sigma f
, where
f\in C
. Thus equation (
3.16
) is uniquely solvable in
C
when
|\sigma|\ll 1
and the solution is analytic in
\sigma
. Hence,

(3.17)

\psi=1-\sigma f+\sigma^{2}Pf+O(\sigma^{3}),

where
-\sigma f
is the right-hand side in (
3.16
). Since (
3.16
) also admits the differentiation in
x
, the latter expansion can be differentiated in
x
. This implies that
\psi
satisfies the first two relations in (
3.13
) and has the following initial data:

\psi(0)=1-\sigma a+O(\sigma^{2}),\quad\psi^{\prime}(0)=\sigma b+\sigma^{2}(-ab+c^{2})+O(\sigma^{3}),

where
a=\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi V(\xi)d\xi,
and
b,~c^{2}
are defined in (
3.15
).
The evaluation of the coefficient for
\sigma^{2}
in the expansion of
\psi^{\prime}(0)
is based on (
3.17
) but it also involves an integration by parts, and more details can be found in
[
13
]
(see Lemma 3.3) if needed.

Function
\psi_{1}
can be defined as
\psi_{1}(x)=\psi(x)/\psi(0)
.
Function
\psi_{2}
can be constructed as

\psi_{2}(x)=\psi_{1}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\psi_{1}^{-2}(\xi)d\xi,\quad x\geq 0,\quad|\sigma|\ll 1.

Then (
3.14
) follows from (
3.13
).

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.
Theorem 3.1 from
[
12
]
implies that the positive spectrum of
H
is discrete. Thus it is enough to show that the positive spectrum is not empty. It is enough to show the existence of a positive spectrum of
H
for arbitrarily small
\sigma>0
since it will imply its existence for all
\sigma>0
, see the paragraph before the statement of the theorem. Hence, the theorem will be proven if, for some sequence
\sigma=\sigma_{n}\to 0,~n\to\infty,
we construct functions
\phi=\phi_{n}(x)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with compact supports such that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[-\phi^{\prime 2}(x)+\sigma_{n}V(x)\phi^{2}(x)]dx>0.

We define solutions
\psi_{3}(x),\psi_{4}(x)
of equation (
3.12
) on the negative half-axis
x\leq 0
similar to
\psi_{1}(x),\psi_{2}(x)
constructed in Lemma
3.8
on the semi-axis
x\geq 0
. In particular,

(3.18)

\psi_{3}(0)=1,\quad\psi_{3}^{\prime}(0)=\beta(\sigma),\quad\psi_{4}(0)=0,\quad\psi_{4}^{\prime}(0)=1,

where

\beta=-\sigma b_{-}-\sigma^{2}(c_{-})^{2}+O(\sigma^{3}),\quad b_{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}V(\xi)d\xi,\quad(c_{-})^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\eta}V(\xi)d\xi\right)^{2}d\eta.

For example, one can find
\psi_{1}(x),\psi_{2}(x)
on
x\geq 0
for the potential
V(-x)
and put

\psi_{3}(x)=\psi_{1}(-x),\quad\psi_{4}(x)=\psi_{2}(-x),\quad x\leq 0.

Let
m=[\sigma^{-3}]
be the integer part of
\sigma^{-3}
, where
0\leq\sigma\ll 1
.
We put

(3.19)

\phi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{1}(x)-\varepsilon_{1}\psi_{2}(x),&0\leq x\leq m,\\
\psi_{3}(x)+\varepsilon_{2}\psi_{4}(x),&-m\leq x\leq 0,\\
0,&~~~|x|>m,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\varepsilon_{1,2}
are positive solutions of the equations
\psi_{1}(m)-\varepsilon_{1}\psi_{2}(m)=0,~\psi_{3}(-m)+\varepsilon_{2}\psi_{4}(-m)=0
, respectively. From the asymptotics of
\psi_{i}(x),~1\leq i\leq 4,
at infinity, it follows that solutions
\varepsilon_{1,2}
exist and
\varepsilon_{1,2}=m^{-1}(1+O(\sigma))=O(\sigma^{-3})
. Note that
\phi(\pm m)=0
.

Since
H\phi(x)=0,~0<|x|<m,
and
\phi(x)
is continuous with
\phi(0)=1
, we have

H\phi=\varepsilon\delta(x),\quad|x|<m,\quad\varepsilon=\phi^{\prime}(+0)-\phi^{\prime}(-0),

where
\delta(x)
is the delta function, and therefore,

\int_{|x|<m}(H\phi(x))\phi(x)dx=\varepsilon.

On the other hand, integration by parts implies

\int_{|x|<m}(H\phi(x))\phi(x)dx=\int_{|x|<m}[-\phi^{\prime 2}(x)+\sigma V(x)\phi^{2}(x)]dx-\varepsilon.

Thus

(3.20)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}([\phi^{\prime 2}(x)-\sigma V(x)\phi^{2}(x)]dx=2\varepsilon.

Asymptotics of
\psi_{i}(x),~1\leq i\leq 4
as
x\to\pm 0
, see (
3.13
), (
3.14
), (
3.18
), lead to

\varepsilon=\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)dx+\sigma^{2}(c^{2}+(c_{-})^{2})+O(\sigma^{3})-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}

=\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)dx+\sigma^{2}(c^{2}+(c_{-})^{2})+O(\sigma^{3}).

The coefficient for
\sigma
here is not negative, and the coefficient for
\sigma^{2}
is positive if
V\not\equiv 0
. Hence,
\varepsilon>0
when
\sigma>0
is small enough. Thus, for that
\sigma
, the quadratic form in the left-hand side of (
3.20
) is positive, and therefore the positive spectrum of
H
is not empty.

∎
