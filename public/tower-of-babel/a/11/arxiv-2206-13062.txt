High-frequency solutions to the constraint equations
Arthur Touati

Abstract
We construct high-frequency initial data for the Einstein vacuum equations in dimension 3+1 by solving the constraint equations on ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . Our family of solutions ( g ¯ λ , K λ ) λ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] (\bar{g}_{\lambda},K_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in(0,1]} is defined through a high-frequency expansion similar to the geometric optics approach and converges in a particular sense to the data of a null dust. In order to solve the constraint equations, we use their conformal formulation and the main challenge of our proof is to adapt this method to the high-frequency context. In particular, the parameters of the conformal formulation are oscillating. The main application of this article is our companion article [ Tou22 ] where we construct high-frequency gravitational waves in generalised wave gauge.



1
Introduction

In this article, we construct high-frequency initial data for the Einstein vacuum equations

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}(g)=0,

(1.1)

where
R_{\mu\nu}(g)
is the Ricci tensor of
g
, a Lorentzian metric on the manifold
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Initial data for (
1.1
) on
\Sigma_{0}=\{t=0\}
are given by a Riemannian metric
\bar{g}
and a symmetric 2-tensor
K
. In the spacetime
([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{3},g)
that
(\Sigma_{0},\bar{g},K)
gives birth to,
\bar{g}
will be the restriction of
g
to
\Sigma_{0}
and
K
will be the second fundamental form of
\Sigma_{0}
, that is
K=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{T}g
where
T
is the unit normal to
\Sigma_{0}
for
g
. A necessary condition for
(\bar{g},K)
to be the set of initial data to a solution of (
1.1
) is that
(\bar{g},K)
solves the following
vacuum constraint equations
:

\displaystyle R(\bar{g})+(\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}}K)^{2}-|K|^{2}_{\bar{g}}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}}K+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}}K

\displaystyle=0,

(1.3)

where
R(\bar{g})
denotes the scalar curvature of
\bar{g}
. Equation (
1.2
) is the Hamiltonian constraint, and equation (
1.3
) is the momentum constraint. Together, they form a coupled system of non-linear elliptic partial differential equations and we refer to Chapter 7 of
[
CB09
]
for a complete presentation of their mathematical study.

1.1
Backreaction for the constraint equations

We construct a family
(\bar{g}_{\lambda},K_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
of solutions to (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) which oscillate at frequency
\lambda^{-1}
. The mean feature of this family is its high-frequency limit, i.e its behaviour when
\lambda
tends to 0. It converges in the following weak sense

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\to\bar{g}_{0},\quad\text{uniformly in $L^{\infty}$,}

\displaystyle\nabla\bar{g}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\nabla\bar{g}_{0},\quad\text{weakly in $L^{2}_{loc}$,}

(1.4)

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup K_{0},\quad\text{weakly in $L^{2}_{loc}$,}

to a fixed solution
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0})
of the
null dust maximal constraint equations
:

\displaystyle R(\bar{g}_{0})-|K_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}

\displaystyle=2|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}F_{0}^{2},

(1.5)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}_{0}}K_{0}

\displaystyle=|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}F_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}u_{0},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}K_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.7)

where
u_{0}
and
F_{0}
are scalar functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore, this article provides the first example of
backreaction
for the constraint equations: a sequence of solutions to the
vacuum
equations describes, in the high-frequency limit, a matter model.

This phenomenon is at the heart of Burnett’s conjecture in general relativity. In
[
Bur89
]
, he conjectured that if a sequence
(g_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of solutions to (
1.1
) converges weakly in
H^{1}_{loc}
to a metric
g_{0}
, then
g_{0}
solves the massless Einstein-Vlasov system

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}(g_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{g_{0}^{-1}(p,p)=0}f(x,p)p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}\mathrm{d}\mu_{g_{0}},

(1.8)

\displaystyle p^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}f-p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}\Gamma(g_{0})^{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{p^{\rho}}f

\displaystyle=0,

(1.9)

for a density
f
defined on the tangent bundle and where
\mathrm{d}\mu_{g_{0}}
is the measure on the tangent bundle. The second equation is the Vlasov equation for the density of massless particles
f
. In his article, Burnett also asked the reverse question : given
g_{0}
a solution to (
1.8
)-(
1.9
), can one construct a sequence
(g_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of solutions to (
1.1
) converging weakly in
H^{1}
to
g_{0}
? The present article provides a positive answer to this question at the level of the spacelike data for (
1.1
) and for a discrete version of the massless Einstein-Vlasov system, namely the Einstein-null dust system

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}(g_{0})

\displaystyle=F_{0}^{2}\partial_{\alpha}u_{0}\partial_{\beta}u_{0},

(1.10)

\displaystyle g_{0}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}u_{0},\mathrm{d}u_{0})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.11)

\displaystyle 2g_{0}^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\rho}u_{0}\partial_{\sigma}F_{0}+(\Box_{g_{0}}u_{0})F_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

(1.12)

The equations (
1.5
)-(
1.7
) are the constraint equations that spacelike data for the system (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) need to solve, in the particular case where the initial hypersurface is maximal. This explains why we refered to the system (
1.5
)-(
1.7
) as the null dust maximal constraint equations. Note that we slightly abuse notations: in this article the scalar functions
F_{0}
and
u_{0}
will be defined on
\Sigma_{0}
only, while in (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) they are defined on a whole spacetime.

The two aspects of Burnett’s conjecture, the direct one and the indirect one, have been studied in several works and different settings. Assuming
\mathbb{U}(1)
symmetry, Huneau and Luk address the direct conjecture by means of microlocal defect measure in
[
HL19
]
(see also
[
GdC21
]
). Under the same symmetry, they construct high-frequency vacuum spacetimes converging to
N
null dusts in
[
HL18
]
. The first result without symmetry assumptions was obtained by Luk and Rodnianski in
[
LR20
]
, where they address both sides of Burnett’s conjecture in double null gauge. This choice of gauge restricts the class of kinetic spacetimes they consider to 2 null dusts.

In a companion paper
[
Tou22
]
, the author constructs high-frequency vacuum spacetimes in generalised wave gauge, paving the way to a proof of Burnett’s conjecture in this gauge. This motivates the need for the high-frequency initial data solving (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) provided by this article. The link between this article and
[
Tou22
]
and its mathematical implications will be further discussed in Section
2.3
, after we state the main result in Section
2.2
.

Finally, note that Burnett’s conjecture and the backreaction it describes for the Einstein vacuum equations falls into the widest category of non-linear effects induced by homogenization, that is through the interaction of multiple scales. This type of effects can be encountered in the study of virtually all non-linear equations coming from physics. Examples are porous media, hydrodynamics, quantum mechanics etc. We refer to
[
Tar09
]
for a rich presentation of the field of homogenization.

1.2
The conformal method

The constraint equations (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) are underdetermined, and in order to solve them we use the conformal method. Introduced by Lichnerowicz in
[
Lic44
]
, this method is based on a conformal formulation of the data
(\bar{g},K)
and it identifies free parameters. In particular, this method transforms (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) into a determined system of equations composed of a vectorial equation and a scalar equation. The idea giving its name to the method is to prescribe the conformal class of
\bar{g}
, i.e to fix a Riemannian metric
\gamma
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and to solve for the scalar function
\varphi
such that

\displaystyle\bar{g}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{4}\gamma.

(1.13)

We say that a tensor is a TT-tensor if it is traceless divergence free and symmetric and one can show that the space of TT-tensor depends only on the conformal class of the metric. Therefore, the next step in the conformal method is to decompose the symmetric 2-tensor
K
in connection with its trace and divergence features. More precisely we fix a scalar function
\tau
and a TT-tensor
\sigma
for the metric
\gamma
and solve for the vector field
W
such that

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-2}(\sigma+L_{\gamma}W)+\frac{1}{3}\varphi^{4}\gamma\tau

(1.14)

where
L_{\gamma}W
is defined in (
4.30
). The exponents appearing in (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) are linked to the dimension of the manifold, here 3, see Chapter 6 of
[
CB09
]
for their general expression. Once the parameters
\gamma
,
\tau
and
\sigma
are chosen, the constraint equations (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) rewrite as the following coupled system of non-linear elliptic equations for
(\varphi,W)
:

\displaystyle 8\Delta_{\gamma}\varphi

\displaystyle=R(\gamma)\varphi+\frac{2}{3}\tau^{2}\varphi^{5}-\left|\sigma+L_{\gamma}W\right|^{2}_{\gamma}\varphi^{-7},

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}W

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\varphi^{6}\mathrm{d}\tau.

(1.16)

A wealth of literature has been produced over the years on the construction of solutions to (
1.15
)-(
1.16
) on various Riemannian manifolds. Note that if the mean curvature
\tau
is constant (CMC setting), then (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) decouple and the construction of solutions is simplified. The results on (
1.15
)-(
1.16
) can thus be categorized into CMC, near CMC (where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}
is small) or far from CMC results, where no assumption is made on
\tau
. On compact manifolds, CMC results are obtained in
[
Ise95
]
. Near CMC results on asymptotically Euclidean manifolds are obtained in
[
CBIY00
]
, where the authors also treat the constraint equations with matter. For far from CMC results, we refer the reader to
[
Max09
]
and
[
DIMM14
]
, where the case of compact manifolds and asymptotically Euclidean manifolds are respectively treated.

Our main challenge is to adapt the conformal method to the high-frequency context, i.e choose the parameters so that the resulting solution
(\bar{g}_{\lambda},K_{\lambda})
displays the behaviour (
1.4
). Not only will the solutions
(\varphi,W)
be defined by high-frequency ansatz, but also the parameters
\gamma
,
\tau
and
\sigma
. In particular, we will construct an oscillating TT-tensor, i.e solve
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}\sigma=0
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\sigma=0
, with
\gamma
itself oscillating.

1.3
Preliminaries

We fix here our notations and present the analytic setting of this article.

1.3.1
Geometric notations

Throughout this article, the notation
\Sigma_{0}
refers to the manifold
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. On
\Sigma_{0}
we consider the usual Euclidean coordinates
x=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
, and we denote by
e
the standard Euclidean metric, i.e

\displaystyle e=\left(\mathrm{d}x^{1}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathrm{d}x^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathrm{d}x^{3}\right)^{2}.

Latin indices are used for the Euclidean coordinates and therefore runs from 1 to 3. In this article, repeated indices are always summed over. A second frame adapted to the background structure will be defined in Section
2.1
.

If
f
is a scalar function, we define its gradient by
\nabla f=(\partial_{1}f,\partial_{2}f,\partial_{3}f)
. If
h
is a Riemannian metric on
\Sigma_{0}
we define

\displaystyle|\nabla f|_{h}^{2}=h^{ij}\partial_{i}f\partial_{j}f.

In the particular case of the Euclidean metric we simply write
|\nabla f|^{2}
.
Moreover if
T
and
S
are two symmetric 2-tensors on
\Sigma_{0}
, we define
|T\cdot S|_{h}=h^{ij}h^{k\ell}T_{ik}S_{j\ell}
and
|T|^{2}_{h}=|T\cdot T|_{h}
. The trace of
T
with respect to
h
is defined by
\mathrm{tr}_{h}T=h^{ij}T_{ij}
.

1.3.2
Function spaces and asymptotically Euclidean manifold

If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we set
\langle x\rangle\vcentcolon=(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
with
|x|=\sqrt{(x^{1})^{2}+(x^{2})^{2}+(x^{3})^{2}}
. We define the following weighted Sobolev spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 1.1

(Weighted Sobolev spaces)
.

For
1\leq p<+\infty
,
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define the space
W^{k,p}_{\delta}
as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}
for the norm

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{W^{k,p}_{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta+|\alpha|}\nabla^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{p}},

where the
L^{p}
norm is defined with the Euclidean volume element. We extend this definition to tensors of any type by summing over all components in the Euclidean coordinates. Some special cases are
H^{k}_{\delta}\vcentcolon=W^{k,2}_{\delta}
and
L^{p}_{\delta}\vcentcolon=W^{0,p}_{\delta}
.

We also define the following
L^{\infty}
-based spaces:

Definition 1.2
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
we define
C^{k}_{\delta}
as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}
for the norm

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{C^{k}_{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta+|\alpha|}\nabla^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

In the following proposition we recall some useful facts about these spaces (see
[
CB09
]
for the proofs).

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
s,s^{\prime},s_{1},s_{2},m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta,\delta^{\prime},\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
1\leq p<+\infty
.

1.

If
s\leq\min(s_{1},s_{2})
,
s<s_{1}+s_{2}-\frac{3}{p}
and
\delta<\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\frac{3}{p}
we have the continuous embedding

\displaystyle W^{s_{1},p}_{\delta_{1}}\times W^{s_{2},p}_{\delta_{2}}\subset W^{s,p}_{\delta}.

2.

If
m<s-\frac{3}{p}
and
\beta\leq\delta+\frac{3}{p}
we have the continuous embedding

\displaystyle W^{s,p}_{\delta}\subset C^{m}_{\beta}.

The background metric and the solution of the constraint equations we will produce are asymptotically Euclidean, meaning that they converge in some sense to the Euclidean metric at infinity. We give the definition of
[
CBIY00
]
.

Definition 1.3

(Asymptotically Euclidean initial data)
.

Let
\bar{g}
be a metric on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
K
a symmetric 2-tensor on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
k>\frac{5}{2}
and
\delta>-\frac{3}{2}
, we say that the pair
(\bar{g},K)
is
H^{k}_{\delta}
asymptotically flat if

\displaystyle\bar{g}-e\in H^{k}_{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad K\in H^{k-1}_{\delta+1}.

Note that the restrictions on
k
and
\delta
in the previous definition ensure that
\bar{g}
is
C^{1}
and
\bar{g}-e
tends to 0 at infinity, since Proposition
1.1
implies
H^{k}_{\delta}\xhookrightarrow{}C^{1}_{\delta+\frac{3}{2}}
.

Remark 1.1
.

While the assumptions in Definition
1.3
are adapted to the inversion of elliptic operators, the decay assumption
\delta>-\frac{3}{2}
is too weak to be able to define the ADM mass of
\bar{g}
. Introduced in
[
ADM08
]
, the ADM mass of an asymptotically Euclidean manifold is defined by

\displaystyle M(\bar{g})=\frac{1}{16\pi}\lim_{R\to+\infty}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\sum_{i,j}\left(\partial_{i}\bar{g}_{ij}-\partial_{j}\bar{g}_{ii}\right)\nu_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
\nu
is the outgoing normal to the spheres
\partial B_{R}
and
\mathrm{d}\mu
the measure on these spheres. Since the volume of the spheres
\partial B_{R}
grows like
R^{2}
, we need to ask for
\delta\geq-\frac{1}{2}
in Definition
1.3
in order to be able to define
M(\bar{g})
in general. See also Remark
2.1
.

1.3.3
Elliptic estimates

Solving the constraint equations with the conformal method requires to invert the elliptic operators
\Delta_{\gamma}
and
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
, for
\gamma
a Riemannian metric. The first one is the Laplace-Beltrami operator acting on scalar functions and the second one is the conformal Laplacian acting on vector field, see Section
4.2
for their exact definition. The metric
\gamma
will be defined in Section
4.1
but we can already say that it will be close to a background metric
\bar{g}_{0}
, itself close to the Euclidean metric
e
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We will benefit from this fact and invert
\Delta=\Delta_{e}
and
\mathrm{div}_{e}L_{e}
rather than
\Delta_{\gamma}
and
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
, which have oscillating coefficients. The following proposition gives the desired inversion properties. The proof of its first part can be found in
[
McO79
]
while the second part is proved in
[
CBIY00
]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

If
-\frac{3}{2}<\delta<-\frac{1}{2}
then
\Delta:H^{2}_{\delta}\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\delta+2}
and
\mathrm{div}_{e}L_{e}:H^{2}_{\delta}\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\delta+2}
are isomorphisms.

1.3.4
High-frequency notations

In this article we consider high-frequency quantities, i.e tensors of all types including scalar functions, metrics, 1-forms etc. A quantity is said to be high-frequency if it admits an expansion in powers of the small parameter
\lambda
with coefficients of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)f

(1.17)

where
f
depends only on
x\in\Sigma_{0}
and
\mathrm{T}
is an oscillating function, i.e an element of

\displaystyle\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\sin(k\theta)~\middle|~k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\cos(k\theta)~\middle|~k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

(1.18)

When considering a high-frequency quantity such as a tensor
S
, we denote by
S^{(i)}
the coefficients of
\lambda^{i}
in the expansion defining
S
, which thus expands formally as

\displaystyle S=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda^{i}S^{(i)}.

Note that
S^{(i)}
is a tensor of the same type as
S
. Moreover, if
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we define
S^{(\geq j)}
by
S^{(\geq j)}=\sum_{k\geq j}\lambda^{k-j}S^{(k)}
. This allows us to clearly troncate high-frequency expansions at a fixed order as in

\displaystyle S=\sum_{k\leq j-1}\lambda^{k}S^{(k)}+\lambda^{j}S^{(\geq j)}.

To emphasize the fact that a high-frequency coefficient
S^{(i)}
of a tensor
S
is oscillating we will often write
S^{(i)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
instead of just
S^{(i)}
.

In order to describe concisely the
oscillating behaviour
of a high-frequency coefficient
S^{(i)}
of a tensor
S
, we write for
\mathcal{A}
a finite subset of (
1.18
)

\displaystyle S^{(i)}\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;\sum_{\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{T}(\theta)

if there exists tensors
(S^{(i)}_{\mathrm{T}})_{\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{A}}
of the same type as
S^{(i)}
such that

\displaystyle S^{(i)}=\sum_{\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{T}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)S^{(i)}_{\mathrm{T}}.

This notation allows us to compute the oscillating behaviour of non-linear quantities without caring too much about the non-oscillating coefficients
S^{(i)}_{\mathrm{T}}
. Note that
S^{(i)}\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;1
simply means that
S^{(i)}
is non-oscillating, i.e does not depend on
\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}
.

In terms of derivation, we use the symbol
\theta
for the derivation with respect to the
\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}
variable. For example, if
f
is a scalar function and if
g=\mathrm{T}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)f
then

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}g

\displaystyle=\mathrm{T}^{\prime}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)f.

2
Statement of the results

In this section, we give the assumptions on the background, state our main result and discuss its main application.

2.1
The background

The background metric and 2-tensor
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0})
solve (
1.5
)-(
1.7
) with
F_{0}
and
u_{0}
two scalar functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The full background solution is then
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0},F_{0},u_{0})
and we make some assumptions on it.

•

Assumptions on
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0})
.
Even though
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0})
satisfies non-vacuum constraint equations, the sources are compactly supported (see (
2.2
) below) and we assume that
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0})
corresponds to asymptotically Euclidean and highly regular initial data. By this we mean that there exists
\delta>-\frac{3}{2}
, a large integer
N\geq 10
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{g}_{0}-e\right\|_{H^{N+1}_{\delta}}+\left\|K_{0}\right\|_{H^{N}_{\delta+1}}\leq\varepsilon.

(2.1)

We denote by
\bar{\mathbf{D}}
the covariant derivative associated to
\bar{g}_{0}
.

•

Assumptions on
F_{0}
.
The density
F_{0}
is supported in a ball of size
R>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(F_{0})\subset B_{R}\vcentcolon=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~\middle|~|x|\leq R\right\}.

(2.2)

It also enjoys some regularity:

\displaystyle\left\|F_{0}\right\|_{H^{N}}\leq\varepsilon

(2.3)

where
\varepsilon
is defined above.

•

Assumptions on
u_{0}
.
There exists a constant non-zero vector field
\mathfrak{z}=(\mathfrak{z}_{1},\mathfrak{z}_{2},\mathfrak{z}_{3})
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u_{0}-\mathfrak{z}\right\|_{H^{N}_{\delta+1}}\leq\varepsilon

(2.4)

where
\nabla u_{0}=(\partial_{1}u_{0},\partial_{2}u_{0},\partial_{3}u_{0})
is the euclidean gradient of
u_{0}
. By taking
\varepsilon
small enough in (
2.4
) we can assume that
|\nabla u_{0}|
is uniformly bounded from below, which implies that
u_{0}
has no critical points. Moreover, the level hypersurfaces of
u_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle P_{0,u}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~\middle|~u_{0}(x)=u\right\}

foliates
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and have the topology of planes thanks to (
2.4
). This allows us to define a particular frame at each point of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We define the vector field

\displaystyle N_{0}=-\frac{\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}}{|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}}.

It satisfies
\bar{g}_{0}(N_{0},N_{0})=1
and is orthogonal to the hypersurface
P_{0,u}
. We consider at each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
an orthonormal basis
(e_{\mathbf{1}},e_{\mathbf{2}})
of
T_{x}P_{0,u}
for the metric
\bar{g}_{0}
. The frame
(N_{0},e_{\mathbf{1}},e_{\mathbf{2}})
will play a crucial role in our construction. While we reserve the usual latin indices for the coordinates system
(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
, i.e
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
, the bold latin indices are used for the frame
(e_{\mathbf{1}},e_{\mathbf{2}})
, i.e
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2}\}
.

In this article, we don’t prove that a background solution
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0},F_{0},u_{0})
solving (
1.5
)-(
1.7
) and satisfying the above assumptions exists. We refer to
[
CBIY00
]
for the details of how one can solve the constraint equations with sources in the asymptotically Euclidean setting.

2.2
Solving the constraint equations

The following theorem is the main result of this article.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0},F_{0},u_{0})
be the solution of the maximal constraint equations coupled with a null dust described in Section
2.1
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be the smallness threshold. There exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\delta,R)>0
such that if
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, there exists for all
\lambda\in(0,1]
a solution
(\bar{g}_{\lambda},K_{\lambda})

2.3
Application to high-frequency vacuum spacetimes

Even though Theorem
2.1
is formulated in a purely elliptic way, its main purpose is to provide spacelike initial data for the high-frequency solutions to (
1.1
) constructed in
[
Tou22
]
in generalised wave gauge. More precisely, in this article we construct a family of Lorentzian metric
(g_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
of the form

\displaystyle g_{\lambda}=g_{0}+\lambda g^{(1)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda^{2}g^{(2)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right)

(2.14)

where the
g^{(i)}
are periodic and smooth functions of their argument
\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}
and where
(g_{0},F_{0},u_{0})
is a given solution of the Einstein-null dust system (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) with initial data on
\Sigma_{0}
given by
(\bar{g}_{0},K_{0},F_{0},u_{0})
described in Section
2.1
. In this section, let us discuss how it affects the construction of Theorem
2.1
.

Since
\bar{g}_{\lambda}
is the induced metric of
g_{\lambda}
on
\Sigma_{0}
, the oscillating behaviours of
g^{(1)}
and
g^{(2)}
in
[
Tou22
]
explains the high-frequency expansion of
\bar{g}_{\lambda}
in (
2.5
). In particular, we have
g^{(1)}=\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)F^{(1)}
with
F^{(1)}\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}=\bar{F}^{(1)}
. The fact that
\bar{F}^{(1)}
is
\bar{g}_{0}
-traceless and tangential to
P_{0,u}
translates as an initial polarization condition for
F^{(1)}
equivalent to
R_{\mu\nu}(g_{\lambda})=O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
.

Remark 2.2
.

These conditions correspond exactly to the definition of the TT gauge in the linearized gravity setting for a plane wave propagating in the
N_{0}
direction. Note that
N_{0}
is not a constant vector field, therefore strictly speaking a plane wave can’t propagate in the
N_{0}
direction. However, as (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) show,
N_{0}
is close to the
\mathfrak{z}
direction and the analogy with the TT gauge of linearized gravity is thus valid.

In
[
Tou22
]
, these polarization conditions are propagated by the transport equation that
F^{(1)}
must satisfy in the spacetime so that
R_{\mu\nu}(g_{\lambda})=O\left(\lambda^{1}\right)
, namely

\displaystyle-2\mathbf{D}_{L_{0}}F^{(1)}+(\Box_{g_{0}}u_{0})F^{(1)}=0,

(2.15)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the covariant derivative associated to
g_{0}
and
L_{0}=-g_{0}^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}u_{0}\partial_{\nu}
is the spacetime gradient of
u_{0}
. Moreover, in
[
Tou22
]
we assume without loss of generality that
\partial_{t}
is the unit normal to
\Sigma_{0}
for
g_{0}
and that
u_{0}
solves the eikonal equation (see (
1.11
)), which thus give
\partial_{t}u_{0}=|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}
and
L_{0}=|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}(\partial_{t}+N_{0})
on
\Sigma_{0}
. This also implies that on
\Sigma_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\lambda g^{(1)}_{ij}\right)=-\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}F^{(1)}_{ij}+\lambda\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\partial_{t}F^{(1)}_{ij}

(2.16)

where
\partial_{t}F^{(1)}_{ij}
on
\Sigma_{0}
is directly given by the first order transport equation (
2.15
). Note in particular that thanks to our previous geometric assumption on
\partial_{t}
and
u_{0}
on
\Sigma_{0}
and thanks to the wave gauge condition that
g_{0}
is also assumed to satisfy in the spacetime, we can completely rewrite
\partial_{t}F^{(1)}_{ij}\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}
given by (
2.15
) in terms of
\bar{g}_{0}
,
K_{0}
and
\bar{F}^{(1)}
. For instance, we can show that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\Box_{g_{0}}u_{0}\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}=(K_{0})^{k\ell}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\ell}u_{0}+\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\ell}|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}u_{0}.

A similar treatment can be applied to all the terms in (
2.15
). Now, on the one hand,
K_{\lambda}
must be the second fundamental form
-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{T_{\lambda}}g_{\lambda}
of
\Sigma_{0}
, where
T_{\lambda}
is the unit normal to
\Sigma_{0}
for
g_{\lambda}
. On the other hand, we choose data in
[
Tou22
]
such that
T_{\lambda}=\partial_{t}+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right)
. Therefore, the oscillating parts of
K^{(0)}_{\lambda}
and
K^{(1)}_{\lambda}
must be consistent with (
2.16
), which explains expressions (
2.7
) and (
2.10
).

Remark 2.3
.

This entanglement between the construction of the data and the evolution equations in the spacetime is characteristic of the present high-frequency setting. Indeed, the spacelike constraint equations give two initial data, roughly
g\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}
and
\partial_{t}g\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}
, while the oscillating terms in the spacetime ansatz satisfy first order transport equations which only require
g\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}
and prescribe, in part,
\partial_{t}g\restriction{\Sigma_{0}}
.

Finally, note that the use of Sobolev spaces instead of Hölder spaces in Theorem
2.1
also comes from the application to
[
Tou22
]
, since Sobolev spaces are more suited to non-linear wave equations. Similarly, the constraint
N\geq 10
allows us to perform a bootstrap argument for the remainder in
[
Tou22
]
using only
L^{2}-L^{\infty}
estimates.

3
Strategy of proof

In this section, we present the strategy of the proof of Theorem
2.1
, which consists mainly in an adaptation of the conformal method to the high-frequency setting.

Remark 3.1
.

From now on and until Section
7
we drop the index
\lambda
in the solution
(\bar{g}_{\lambda},K_{\lambda})
obtained in Theorem
2.1
and simply write
(\bar{g},K)
.

3.1
High-frequency expansion of the parameters

We start by giving and motivating the expansions of the parameters of the conformal method, i.e the metric
\gamma
, the scalar function
\tau
and the TT-tensor
\sigma
.

Since the metric
\bar{g}
obtained in Theorem
2.1
will ultimately inherit its properties from the metric
\gamma
defining the conformal class,
\gamma
needs needs to fulfill two requirements: it needs to oscillate and to be close to
\bar{g}_{0}
. Therefore, we define
\gamma
by

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}+\lambda\gamma^{(1)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda^{2}\gamma^{(2)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right),

(3.1)

where we recall that the notation
\gamma^{(i)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
is used to emphasize the fact that
\gamma^{(i)}
is a linear combination of terms of the form (
1.17
). See Section
4.1
for the exact definition of
\gamma^{(1)}
and
\gamma^{(2)}
.

Similarly, we want
K
to be close to
K_{0}
(in a weak sense since it is at the level of one derivative of the metric, see (
1.4
)) and
K_{0}
is assumed to be
\bar{g}_{0}
-traceless. We thus define
\tau
to be of order
\lambda^{1}
, i.e

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\lambda\tau^{(1)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right).

(3.2)

The fact that the high-frequency perturbations don’t contribute to the mean curvature at the
\lambda^{0}
order will be justified in the proof, but this can already be seen in Theorem
2.1
: the contribution at
\lambda^{0}
order to
K
is given by
\frac{1}{2}\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}
(see (
2.7
)), and
\bar{F}^{(1)}
is
\bar{g}_{0}
-traceless.

The definition of the TT-tensor
\sigma
requires a special construction linked to expressions (
2.10
)-(
2.11
). Since it involves also the expansion of the solution
\varphi
given in the next section, we postpone the discussion of this special construction to Section
3.3
. However, we can already give its high-frequency expansion:

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(0)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda\sigma^{(1)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda^{2}\left(\sigma^{(2)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\widetilde{\sigma}\right).

(3.3)

Here,
\widetilde{\sigma}
is a non-oscillating remainder whose role is to solve the equations defining the class of TT-tensors, that is
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}\sigma=0
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\sigma=0
, with
\gamma
defined by (
3.1
).

3.2
High-frequency expansion of the solutions

The solutions
(\varphi,W)
need to solve equations (
1.15
)-(
1.16
), where the parameters defined above appear as coefficients. To understand the expansions for
(\varphi,W)
, let us look at the effect of the expansions (
3.1
)-(
3.2
)-(
3.3
) on the equations (
1.15
)-(
1.16
).

A standard feature of high-frequency quantities is that they lose one power of
\lambda
per derivative. In (
1.15
), derivatives of the metric
\gamma
only appear in its scalar curvature
R(\gamma)
and we can derive from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle R(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}}{\lambda}\partial^{2}_{\theta}\left(\gamma^{(1)}_{N_{0}N_{0}}-\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\gamma^{(1)}_{ij}\right)+O\left(\lambda^{0}\right).

(3.4)

Therefore, if
\gamma^{(1)}
is well-chosen, the RHS of (
1.15
) is
O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
. This implies that the first oscillating term in
\varphi
must be at order
\lambda^{2}
, since
\Delta_{\gamma}
is a second order elliptic operator. More precisely we choose

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=1+\lambda^{2}\left(\varphi^{(2)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\widetilde{\varphi}\right)+\lambda^{3}\varphi^{(3)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right),

(3.5)

where
\widetilde{\varphi}
is a non-oscillating remainder. The constant coefficient in (
3.5
) ensures that
\bar{g}_{\lambda}=\bar{g}_{0}+O\left(\lambda\right)
. Even though
\varphi^{(3)}
appears after the remainder
\widetilde{\varphi}
in the ansatz for
\varphi
, it appears before
\widetilde{\varphi}
in the hierarchy obtained from (
1.15
), again since
\Delta_{\gamma}
is a second order elliptic operator.

Similarly, (
3.2
) and (
3.5
) imply that
\varphi^{6}\mathrm{d}\tau=O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
and the ellipticity of
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
allows us to choose
W
of the following form

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\left(W^{(2)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)+\widetilde{W}\right)+\lambda^{3}W^{(3)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right),

(3.6)

where
\widetilde{W}
is a non-oscillating remainder.

As is standard when considering high-frequency expansions, we obtain a hierarchy of equations. Very schematically, one can say that
\varphi^{(2)}
and
\varphi^{(3)}
(resp.
W^{(2)}
and
W^{(3)}
) will solve the orders
\lambda^{0}
and
\lambda^{1}
of (
1.15
) (resp. (
1.16
)), while the non-oscillating remainder
\widetilde{\varphi}
(resp.
\widetilde{W}
) will solve the orders higher than
\lambda^{2}
.

Even though the operators
\Delta_{\gamma}
and
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
are both second order elliptic operators, they depend differently on
\gamma
. Indeed, the coefficients of
\Delta_{\gamma}
depend on
\gamma
and
\nabla\gamma
only, while the coefficients of
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
also depend on
\nabla^{2}\gamma
. From (
3.1
), we thus see that
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}
loses inherently one power of
\lambda
. The analysis of the last equations in the hierarchy will therefore differ from the hamiltonian to the momentum constraint.

3.3
The TT-tensor

In this section, we give more details on the definition of
\sigma
, and in particular the first two terms in its high-frequency expansion (
3.3
), i.e
\sigma^{(0)}
and
\sigma^{(1)}
. Thanks to (
3.5
) we have
\varphi=1+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right)
which implies that

\displaystyle K^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(0)}+(L_{\gamma}W)^{(0)},

(3.7)

\displaystyle K^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(1)}+(L_{\gamma}W)^{(1)}+\frac{1}{3}\bar{g}_{0}\tau^{(1)},

(3.8)

where we used (
1.14
), (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). As explained in Section

, the expressions of

and
K^{(1)}
are actually prescribed the application of Theorem

to the definition of initial data for (

). Therefore, (
2.7
)-(
2.11
) are used to define

and
K^{(1)}
, while
\sigma^{(0)}
and

are defined

such that (

)-(

) hold. We are then left with the task of showing that

defines an approximate TT-tensor and of constructing an exact one, with the help of

and the remainder

introduced in (

). The full construction of

is the content of Sections

and

.

This procedure goes against the standard conformal method, where one starts by defining the parameters, then solve for the

via (

)-(

). This is another characteristic feature of the high-frequency setting, which originates in the redundancy described in Remark

\displaystyle\quad+\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}\right),

and

\displaystyle\left|R^{(1)}\right|+\left|R^{(\geq 2)}\right|\lesssim\left|\gamma^{-2}\partial^{2}\gamma\right|+\left|\gamma^{-3}(\partial\gamma)^{2}\right|.

(4.11)

Moreover

\displaystyle R^{(1)}\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;\cos(\theta)+\sin(2\theta)+\cos(3\theta).

(4.12)

Proof.

We recall the definition of the scalar curvature

\displaystyle R(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}_{jk}+\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}_{k\ell}\Gamma(\gamma)^{\ell}_{ij}-\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}_{i\ell}\Gamma(\gamma)^{\ell}_{jk}\right).

We start by giving an estimation up to second order in
\lambda
of the inverse of
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}-\lambda\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{2}\left(\cos^{2}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{ik}(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{j\ell}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{k\ell}-\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\omega^{(2)})^{ij}\right)+O\left(\lambda^{3}\right),

where on the RHS all the inverses are taken with respect to the background metric
\bar{g}_{0}
. We now expand the Christoffel symbols of
\gamma
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})^{k}_{ij}+(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}+\lambda(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right),

(4.14)

with

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\left(\partial_{(i}u_{0}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j)}-\partial_{\ell}u_{0}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{ij}\right),

(4.15)

\displaystyle(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\left(\partial_{(i}u_{0}\omega^{(2)}_{\ell j)}-\partial_{\ell}u_{0}\omega^{(2)}_{ij}\right)

(4.16)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\sin\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{k\ell}\partial_{(i}u_{0}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j)}+\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{Q}^{k}_{ij},

where we defined

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\left(\partial_{(i}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j)}-\partial_{\ell}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{ij}\right)-\frac{1}{2}(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{k\ell}\left(\partial_{(i}(\bar{g}_{0})_{\ell j)}-\partial_{\ell}(\bar{g}_{0})_{ij}\right).

By using
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}=0
, we can compute useful contractions of
\bar{Q}^{k}_{ij}
. We first look at
\bar{Q}^{k}_{jk}
which vanishes thanks to
\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}=0
:

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{k}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{g}_{0}^{k\ell}\partial_{j}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell k}-(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{k\ell}\partial_{j}(\bar{g}_{0})_{\ell k}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}=0.

(4.17)

Using in addition
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
we have:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(N_{0})_{k}\bar{Q}^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}N_{0}^{\ell}\left(\partial_{i}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\ell}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}j}-\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}

\displaystyle=\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}\right).

(4.18)

Since the scalar curvature contains first derivatives of the Christoffel symbols,
R(\gamma)
contains
a priori
a
\lambda^{-1}
contribution from
\partial_{\theta}\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)}
, but thanks to the properties of
\bar{F}^{(1)}
we can see that it vanishes. Indeed we have

\displaystyle R^{(-1)}=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{jk}\right),

and
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}=0
imply that

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}=0\quad\text{and}\quad(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{jk}=0.

(4.19)

Let us now look at the
\lambda^{0}
terms in
R(\gamma)
. Using again (
4.19
) we obtain:

\displaystyle R^{(0)}-R(\bar{g}_{0})

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{k}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})^{\ell}_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{jk}\right)-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{\ell}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{k}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})^{\ell}_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{\ell}_{jk},

where we used
(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}=0
. We set

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\left(\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}-\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{jk}\right),

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{k}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})^{\ell}_{jk},

\displaystyle III

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{\ell}_{jk},

and compute further each of these terms. The term

where we used that the diagonal coefficients of

\displaystyle=-(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{i\ell}\left(\partial_{k}\bar{g}_{0}^{i\ell}+2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})^{\ell}_{jk}\right)=0,

4.2
Expansion of differential operators

The equations (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) involve differential operators depending on the metric
\gamma
. When they are applied to high-frequency quantities, we need to take into account the expansion (
4.1
) affecting the coefficients of the operators as well as the expansion of the quantities themselves. In this section we compute such expansions for all the operators involved, that is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, the divergence operator, the conformal Killing operator and the conformal Laplacian.

In terms of notation, if
P_{\gamma}
is a differential operator acting on tensors of any type and whose coefficients depends on
\gamma
, we can formally obtain an expansion of
P_{\gamma}(T)
of the form

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\lambda^{k}P^{[k]}_{\gamma}(T).

where the previous sum has finite support. The bracket notation
[k]
thus plays the same role for differential operators as the parenthesis notation
(i)
introduced in Section
1.3.4
for tensors.

Moreover we can mix the two cases, i.e apply differential operators
P_{\gamma}
depending on
\gamma
to oscillating tensors
T\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
. The expansion of
P_{\gamma}(T)
then depends on the order of
T\longmapsto P_{\gamma}(T)
and also
\gamma\longmapsto P_{\gamma}(T)
. By the order of
\gamma\longmapsto P_{\gamma}(T)
, we simply mean the top derivative of
\gamma
appearing in the coefficients of
P_{\gamma}
. Since both oscillating and non-oscillating terms appear in the expansions for the parameters and the unknowns of the conformal method (see Section
3.1
), we make a difference between the expansions for
P_{\gamma}(T)
when
T
is non-oscillating and when
T
is oscillating. Usual capital letters are used in the first case and bold capital letters in the second case, i.e

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\lambda^{k}P^{[k]}_{\gamma}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\gamma}\left(T\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)=\sum_{k}\lambda^{k}\mathbf{P}^{[k]}_{\gamma}(T),

where the support of the two previous finite sums are
a priori
different, depending on
T
and
P_{\gamma}
. This explains the difference between Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
below.

4.2.1
The Laplace-Beltrami operator

The only differential operator in the hamiltonian constraint (
1.15
) is the Laplace-Beltrami operator associated to
\gamma
. If
h
is a generic Riemannian metric on
\Sigma_{0}
, we define its Laplace-Beltrami operator by

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}f=h^{ij}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f-\Gamma(h)_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}f\right),

for
f
a scalar function. Note that
\Delta_{e}
is the usual Laplacian operator on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and is denoted by
\Delta
in this article. Since
\gamma=O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
and
\partial\gamma=O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
, if
f
does not admit a high-frequency expansion then

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma}f=O\left(\lambda^{0}\right).

If
f
admit a high-frequency expansion we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
f\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
an oscillating scalar function we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma}\left(f\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{H}^{[-2]}(f)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{H}^{[-1]}(f)+\mathbf{H}^{[\geq 0]}(f),

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{[-2]}(f)

\displaystyle=|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}\partial_{\theta}^{2}f,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{[-1]}(f)

\displaystyle=2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}\partial_{\theta}f+(\tilde{\Delta}_{\bar{g}_{0}}u_{0})\partial_{\theta}f-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}f,

(4.22)

and

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{H}^{[\geq 0]}(f)\right|\lesssim\left|\gamma^{-1}\partial^{2}f\right|+\left|\gamma^{-2}\partial\gamma\partial f\right|.

(4.23)

Proof.

We start with the definition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator :

\Delta_{\gamma}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)=\gamma^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)-\gamma^{ij}\Gamma(\gamma)_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right).

Using the expansion of the inverse of
\gamma
and
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}|\nabla u_{0}|_{\gamma}^{2}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\varphi+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(2\gamma^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}\partial_{\theta}\varphi+(\tilde{\Delta}_{\gamma}u_{0})\partial_{\theta}\varphi\right)+O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\varphi+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(2\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}\partial_{\theta}\varphi+(\tilde{\Delta}_{\bar{g}_{0}}u_{0})\partial_{\theta}\varphi\right)+O\left(\lambda^{0}\right),

where
\tilde{\Delta}_{h}=h^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
. Moreover from the decomposition of the Christoffel symbols (
4.14
) and (
4.19
) we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}\Gamma(\gamma)_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g}_{0})_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}\varphi+O\left(\lambda\right).

The estimate (
4.23
) simply comes from the definition of
\Delta_{\gamma}
.
∎

4.2.2
The divergence operator

The divergence operator appears in the momentum constraint (
1.16
) but also in the definition of the parameter
\sigma
, which in particular needs to be a divergence free tensor for the metric
\gamma
. Recall that if
h
is a Riemannian metric on
\Sigma_{0}
and if
\mathbf{D}^{(h)}
denotes the covariant derivative associated, then
\mathrm{div}_{h}A=h^{k\ell}\mathbf{D}^{(h)}_{k}A_{\ell}
and
(\mathrm{div}_{h}B)_{i}=h^{k\ell}\mathbf{D}^{(h)}_{k}B_{\ell i}
for
A
a 1-tensor and
B
a 2-tensor. The divergence operator only depends on first derivatives of
\gamma
which are
O\left(\lambda^{0}\right)
so we only need an expansion when the tensor on which it acts is itself oscillating.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
A_{ij}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
an oscillating symmetric 2-tensor we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}A\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{d}^{[-1]}_{\ell}(A)+\mathbf{d}^{[0]}_{\ell}(A)+\lambda\mathbf{d}^{[\geq 1]}_{\ell}(A),

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{[-1]}_{\ell}(A)

\displaystyle=-|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\partial_{\theta}A_{N_{0}\ell},

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{[0]}_{\ell}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}_{0}}A_{\ell}-(\bar{g}_{0})^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{i\ell}A_{aj},

(4.25)

and

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{d}^{[\geq 1]}_{\ell}(A)\right|\lesssim\left|\gamma^{-1}\partial A\right|+\left|\gamma^{-2}\partial\gamma A\right|.

(4.26)

Moreover

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{[0]}(A)

\displaystyle\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;(1+\sin(\theta))A,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{[1]}(A)

\displaystyle\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;\sin(\theta)\partial_{\theta}A+(\cos(\theta)+\sin(2\theta))A.

(4.28)

Proof.

We start by using the expansion of the Christoffel’s symbols from (
4.14
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(\gamma)}_{i}A\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)_{j\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}A_{j\ell}+\bar{\mathbf{D}}_{i}A_{j\ell}-(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{i(j}A_{a\ell)}-\lambda(\Gamma^{(1)})^{a}_{i(j}A_{a\ell)}+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right).

We now use the expansion of the inverse of
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}A\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{ij}\mathbf{D}^{(\gamma)}_{i}A\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)_{j\ell}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda}|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\partial_{\theta}A_{N_{0}\ell}+\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}_{0}}A_{\ell}-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{i(j}A_{a\ell)}-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}A_{j\ell}

(4.29)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\left[\cos^{2}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{ik}(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ja}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{ka}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}A_{j\ell}-\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\omega^{(2)})^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}A_{j\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\left(\bar{\mathbf{D}}_{i}A_{j\ell}-(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{i(j}A_{a\ell)}\right)-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\Gamma^{(1)})^{a}_{i(j}A_{a\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\lambda^{2}\right).

This concludes the proof, using
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
and
\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{ij}=0
. The estimate (
4.26
) just comes from the definition of the divergence. The oscillating behaviours (
4.27
) and (
4.28
) can be directly read on (
4.29
).
∎

4.2.3
The conformal Killing operator

In order to compute
L_{\gamma}W
in the ansatz for
K
(
1.14
), we need to expand the conformal Killing operator defined by

\displaystyle(L_{h}A)_{ij}=\mathbf{D}^{(h)}_{(i}A_{j)}-\frac{2}{3}(\mathrm{div}_{h}A)h_{ij},

(4.30)

where
A
is a 1-tensor. Note that
L_{h}A
is a symmetric 2-tensor traceless with respect to
h
. As for the divergence operator,
L_{\gamma}
only depends on first derivatives of
\gamma
so only an expansion of
L_{\gamma}W
with
W
oscillating is necessary.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
be an oscillating 1-form. We have

L_{\gamma}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{ij}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[-1]}(W)+\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[0]}(W)+\lambda\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[\geq 1]}(W),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[-1]}(W)

\displaystyle=\partial_{(i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}W_{j)}+\frac{2}{3}|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}(\bar{g}_{0})_{ij}\partial_{\theta}W_{N_{0}},

(4.31)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[0]}(W)

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{D}}_{(i}W_{j)}-2W_{k}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}+\frac{2}{3}\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{ij}\partial_{\theta}W_{N_{0}}-\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}_{0}}W(\bar{g}_{0})_{ij},

(4.32)

and

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{[\geq 1]}(W)\right|\lesssim\left|\partial W\right|+\left|\gamma^{-1}\partial\gamma W\right|.

(4.33)

The following hold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.34)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\mathbf{K}^{[0]}(W)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{F}^{(1)}}\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W).

(4.35)

The following oscillating behaviour holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{[0]}(W)

\displaystyle\;\overset{\mathrm{osc}}{\sim}\;(1+\sin(\theta))W+\cos(\theta)\partial_{\theta}W.

(4.36)

Proof.

We start with the covariant derivative of the oscillating 1-form
W^{(\cdot)}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(\gamma)}_{i}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}W_{j}+\bar{\mathbf{D}}_{i}W_{j}\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)-W_{k}\left((\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}+\lambda(\Gamma^{(1)})^{k}_{ij}+\lambda^{2}(\Gamma^{(2)})^{k}_{ij}+\cdots\right),

which also gives us the divergence

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{ij}\mathbf{D}^{(\gamma)}_{i}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\bar{g}_{0})^{k\ell}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}W_{\ell}-\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{k\ell}\partial_{k}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}W_{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}_{0}}W-(\bar{g}_{0})^{k\ell}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{k\ell}W_{a}+O\left(\lambda\right).

We conclude the proof of (
4.31
)-(
4.32
) with

\displaystyle L_{\gamma}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}^{(\gamma)}_{(i}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{j)}-\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)\gamma_{ij},

and
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
and
(\bar{g}_{0})^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{a}_{ij}=0
. The trace identities (
4.34
)-(
4.35
) and the oscillating behaviour (
4.36
) follows directly from (
4.31
)-(
4.32
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

In the previous lemma we used the notation
\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{F}^{(1)}}\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W)
to denote

(\bar{F}^{(1)})^{ij}\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}_{ij}(W)=\bar{g}_{0}^{ik}\bar{g}_{0}^{j\ell}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{k\ell}\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}_{ij}(W),

even though
\bar{F}^{(1)}
is not a Lorentzian metric.

4.2.4
The conformal Laplacian

The conformal Laplacian associated to a Riemannian metric
h
is the operator
\mathrm{div}_{h}L_{h}
acting on 1-tensor. It is the only operator considered in this article depending on
\partial^{2}\gamma
, i.e second derivatives of the metric
\gamma
. Indeed
L_{\gamma}
contains the Christoffel symbols of
\gamma
through the covariant derivative, and they are differenciated once by the divergence. Since
\partial^{2}\gamma=O\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)
, this a major difference with the Laplace-Beltrami operator or the divergence operator from the point of view of expanding quantities in powers of
\lambda
. Indeed, this implies that even if the 1-form
\beta
is not oscillating, the quantity
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}\beta
still loses one power of
\lambda
. If
\beta
is oscillating, then
\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}\beta
loses two powers of
\lambda
since the conformal Laplacian is a second order operator. This explains the two following lemmas.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\beta
be a 1-form. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}M^{[-1]}(\beta)+M^{[\geq 0]}(\beta),

with

\displaystyle M^{[-1]}_{\ell}(\beta)

\displaystyle=|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{i\ell}\beta_{j},

(4.37)

and

\displaystyle\left|M^{[\geq 0]}(\beta)-\mathrm{div}_{e}L_{e}\beta\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\gamma^{-1}-e^{-1}\right||\partial^{2}\beta|+\left|\gamma^{-2}\partial\gamma\right|\left|1+\gamma^{-1}\gamma\right||\partial\beta|

(4.38)

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\gamma^{-3}(\partial\gamma)^{2}+\gamma^{-2}(\partial^{2}\gamma)^{(\geq 0)}\right|\left|1+\gamma^{-1}\gamma\right||\beta|.

Proof.

Let
\bar{g}
be any Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}}L_{\bar{g}}\beta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}^{ij}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{(j}\beta_{\ell)}-\frac{2}{3}\partial_{\ell}\partial_{i}\beta_{j}\right)+\beta_{k}\left(-2\bar{g}^{ij}\partial_{i}\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{j\ell}+\frac{2}{3}\partial_{\ell}\left(\bar{g}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{ij}\right)\right)

(4.39)

\displaystyle\quad-\bar{g}^{ij}\left(2\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{j\ell}\partial_{i}\beta_{k}+\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{div}_{\bar{g}}\beta\partial_{i}\bar{g}_{j\ell}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\left(-\partial_{i}\beta_{j}\partial_{\ell}\bar{g}^{ij}+\bar{g}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{ij}\partial_{\ell}\beta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\bar{g}^{ij}\Gamma(\bar{g})^{k}_{i(j}L_{\bar{g}}\beta_{k\ell)}.

In the case of the high-frequency metric
\gamma
, the only terms loosing
\frac{1}{\lambda}
, i.e contributing to
M^{[-1]}(\beta)
, are the
\gamma^{-2}\partial^{2}\gamma\beta
. More precisely, it concerns terms involving
\partial\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)}
, and since
\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{ij}=0
, the only contribution is

\displaystyle M^{[-1]}_{\ell}(\beta)

\displaystyle=-2\beta_{k}\bar{g}^{ij}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{\theta}(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(0)})^{k}_{j\ell}=|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\cos\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{i\ell}\beta_{j},

where we used (
4.15
) and
\bar{F}^{(1)}_{N_{0}i}=0
. From (
4.39
) we also get

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{e}L_{e}\beta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=e^{ij}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{(j}\beta_{\ell)}-\frac{2}{3}\partial_{\ell}\partial_{i}\beta_{j}\right),

which concludes the proof of (
4.38
).
∎

The estimation (
4.38
) will allow us to invert the operator
M^{[\geq 0]}
, since we know how to invert
\mathrm{div}_{e}L_{e}
(see Proposition
1.2
) and since we gain a smallness constant
\varepsilon
in front of
\partial^{2}\beta
thanks to (
2.1
) and (
4.13
). We now state the final lemma of this section, which will allow us to construct high-frequency solutions of the momentum constraint. The proof is left to the reader since it follows from Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)
be an oscillating 1-form. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}\left(W\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\right)_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{M}^{[-2]}_{\ell}(W)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{M}^{[-1]}_{\ell}(W)+\mathbf{M}^{[\geq 0]}_{\ell}(W),

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{[-2]}_{\ell}(W)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}^{[-1]}_{\ell}\left(\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W)\right),

(4.40)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{[-1]}_{\ell}(W)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}^{[-1]}_{\ell}\left(\mathbf{K}^{[0]}(W)\right)+\mathbf{d}^{[0]}_{\ell}\left(\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W)\right).

(4.41)

The following oscillating behaviour holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{[-1]}(W)

\displaystyle\sim\cos(\theta)W+\cos(\theta)\partial^{2}_{\theta}W+(1+\sin(\theta))\partial_{\theta}W.

(4.42)

From (
4.24
), (
4.31
) and (
4.40
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{[-2]}_{\ell}(W)

\displaystyle=|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}W_{\ell}+\frac{1}{3}(N_{0})_{\ell}\partial^{2}_{\theta}W_{N_{0}}\right).

(4.43)

In order to solve the momentum constraint, we need to invert this operator. This is done in the following simple lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are 1-forms such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ell}+\frac{1}{3}(N_{0})_{\ell}\alpha_{N_{0}}=\beta_{\ell},

(4.44)

then

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ell}=\beta_{\ell}-\frac{1}{4}(N_{0})_{\ell}\beta_{N_{0}}.

Proof.

We contract (
4.44
) with the vector field
N_{0}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}\alpha_{N_{0}}=\beta_{N_{0}}.

Inserting this into (
4.44
) concludes the proof.
∎

(see Lemma

where

\displaystyle K^{(0)}

\displaystyle=K_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)},

(5.2)

following the discussion of Section
3.3
. As the parameter

is the trace with respect to
\gamma

\displaystyle\tau^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{\bar{g}_{0}}K^{(0)}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.3)

where we used (
1.7

5.1
) rewrites

\displaystyle 8|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}\partial^{2}_{\theta}\varphi^{(2)}=R^{(0)}-\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}.

The LHS of (

Lemma 5.1
.

We have

\displaystyle R^{(0)}-\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}-K_{0}^{\ell j}\right)-6\cos\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2}.

Proof.

Let us first expand
\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}
using (
5.2
) and (
2.9
):

\displaystyle\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}

\displaystyle=|K_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}+|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2}-\cos\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2}+\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\left|\bar{F}^{(1)}\cdot K_{0}\right|_{\bar{g}_{0}}.

We use now (
4.10
) to compute the full RHS of (
5.4
):

\displaystyle R^{(0)}-\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}

\displaystyle=R(\bar{g}_{0})-|K_{0}|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}-2|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\left(\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}\right)-\left|\bar{F}^{(1)}\cdot K_{0}\right|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-6\cos\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2}.

Using the background Hamiltonian constraint (
1.5
) to cancel the non-oscillating term, we are left with

\displaystyle R^{(0)}-\left|K^{(0)}\right|^{2}_{\bar{g}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\left(\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(-\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}\right)-\left|\bar{F}^{(1)}\cdot K_{0}\right|_{\bar{g}_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-6\cos\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}^{2}F_{0}^{2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

This lemma allows us to solve (

\displaystyle\varphi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}}\sin\left(\frac{u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}\left(\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}-\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}+K_{0}^{\ell j}\right)+\frac{3}{16}\cos\left(\frac{2u_{0}}{\lambda}\right)F_{0}^{2}.

Now that

\displaystyle\bar{F}^{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{F}^{(1)}_{\ell j}}{2|\nabla u_{0}|_{\bar{g}_{0}}}\left(\bar{g}_{0}^{ij}\partial_{i}N_{0}^{\ell}-\frac{1}{2}N_{0}\bar{g}_{0}^{\ell j}+K_{0}^{\ell j}\right)\bar{g}_{0}+\omega^{(2)},

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}F_{0}^{2}\bar{g}_{0}.

These definitions are consistent with (

and

Lemma 5.2

The symmetric 2-tensors

and

are supported in

and the following holds

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{F}^{(2,1)}\right\|_{H^{N-1}}+\left\|\bar{F}^{(2,2)}\right\|_{H^{N-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon,

(5.8)

with a constant depending only on

Proof.

5.2

Remark 5.1
.

(5.19)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}

\displaystyle=K^{(1)}-\frac{1}{3}\tau^{(1)}\bar{g}_{0}-\mathbf{K}^{[-1]}(W^{(2)}),

where

,

In this section, we prove that

a TT-tensor, that is

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\left(\sigma^{(0)}+\lambda\sigma^{(1)}\right)=O\left(\lambda^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{div}_{\gamma}\left(\sigma^{(0)}+\lambda\sigma^{(1)}\right)=O\left(\lambda\right).

Using the expansion of

Remark 5.2
.

The cancellation of the

5.4
An exact TT-tensor

In the next section, we are going to solve completely the constraint equations, i.e solve for the remainders in the high-frequency ansatz (
3.5
)-(
3.6
). We will thus need the full expression of the parameters of the conformal method. While
\gamma
and
\tau
are already fully defined,
\sigma
is only partially known yet and is only an

TT-tensor, as it was shown in the previous section. In this section we finish the construction of
\sigma
. We choose the following ansatz

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(0)}+\lambda\sigma^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\left(\sigma^{(2)}+L_{\gamma}Y\right)+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{3}\mathfrak{f}\gamma.

In this expression,
\sigma^{(0)}
and
\sigma^{(1)}
are given by (

) and (

) and
\sigma^{(2)}
,

and

are yet to be defined such that

(5.29)

Let us explain the ansatz (

), we can’t modifiy the order
\lambda^{0}
and
\lambda^{1}
of

and the scalar function
\mathfrak{f}
ensures

We now derive the equations for

,

and
\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\mathbf{d}^{[1]}(\sigma^{(0)})+\mathbf{d}^{[0]}(\sigma^{(1)})+\mathbf{d}^{[-1]}(\sigma^{(2)})+M^{[-1]}(Y)\right)\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

,

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma^{(2)}}\sigma^{(0)}-\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma^{(1)}}\sigma^{(1)},

(5.30)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma^{(\geq 1)}}\sigma^{(2)}-\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma^{(\geq 2)}}\sigma^{(1)}-\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma^{(\geq 3)}}\sigma^{(0)},

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{d}^{[\geq 2]}(\sigma^{(0)})-\mathbf{d}^{[\geq 1]}(\sigma^{(1)})-\mathbf{d}^{[\geq 0]}(\sigma^{(2)})-\frac{\lambda}{3}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{f}.

This discussion proves that

\displaystyle\left\|\text{RHS of \eqref{eq Phi tffi}}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\delta+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim C(C_{1})\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon.

(6.19)

Using the first part of Proposition

Therefore, if we take

In order to show that
