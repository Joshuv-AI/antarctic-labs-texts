Dilogarithm identities in cluster scattering diagrams
Tomoki Nakanishi

Abstract
. We extend the notion of y y -variables (coefficients) in cluster algebras to cluster scattering diagrams. Accordingly, we extend the dilogarithm identity associated with a period in a cluster pattern to the one associated with a loop in a cluster scattering diagram. We show that these identities are constructed from and reduced to trivial ones by applying the pentagon identity possibly infinitely many times.

1.
Introduction

In the seminal paper by Gross-Hacking-Keel-Kontsevich
[
GHKK18
]
,
it was revealed that there is an intimate
relation between cluster algebras and scattering diagrams.
The underlying cluster pattern for a cluster algebra is constructed by
mutations

[
FZ02
]
,
while a scattering diagram is constructed based on the
consistency

[
KS06
,
GS11
]
.
In spite of the difference of their principles, the whole information of a given cluster pattern

\mathbf{\Sigma}

is contained in a certain scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}
called
a
cluster scattering diagram
(CSD, for short).

Let us briefly summarize the relation between
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\mathfrak{D}
.
Let
\Delta(\mathbf{G}^{t_{0}})
be the fan (the
G
-fan
) spanned by the cones of
G
-matrices
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
with the initial vertex
t_{0}
.
The
G
-fan
\Delta(\mathbf{G}^{t_{0}})
is isomorphic to the
cluster complex

\Delta(\mathbf{\Sigma})
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
in
[
FZ03
]

as a simplicial complex.
Then,
the union of the codimension one cones of

\Delta(\mathbf{G}^{t_{0}})
is embedded into the support
\mathrm{Supp}(\mathfrak{D})
of
\mathfrak{D}
.
Also, it coincides with
\mathrm{Supp}(\mathfrak{D})

if and only if
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is of finite type.
In general,
the structure of
\mathfrak{D}
outside
the support
of the
G
-fan
\Delta(\mathbf{G}^{t_{0}})
is very complicated.

So, at least superficially, a cluster pattern carries only a partial information of
\mathfrak{D}
.
However, looking at the construction of
\mathfrak{D}
in

[
GHKK18
]
,
we recognize that
\mathfrak{D}
is uniquely constructed (up to isomorphism) only from
the
initial exchange matrix

B_{t_{0}}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
Thus, we may think that a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}

and a CSD
\mathfrak{D}
are actually
one inseparable object

associated with
B_{t_{0}}
.

There is one great advantage of working with
\mathfrak{D}
rather than only
\mathbf{\Sigma}
;
namely, it has an underlying group
G
which we call the
structure group
for
\mathfrak{D}
.
Since
G
is defined by the inverse limit, the infinite product is well-defined.
Then, first of all, it is possible to consider an
infinite product of mutations
,
which is nothing but a
path-ordered product
in
\mathfrak{D}
.
Moreover, the group
G
controls the whole structure of
\mathfrak{D}
locally and globally.
In particular,
the following facts were presented somewhat implicitly in
[
GHKK18
]
,
and described more explicitly in
[
Nak23
]
:

(a).

There are some distinguished elements of
G
called
the
dilogarithm elements
, and they satisfy the
pentagon relation
.

(b).

Up to equivalence,
\mathfrak{D}
consists of walls whose wall elements
are dilogarithm elements with positive rational exponents.

(c).

Dilogarithm elements act as
mutations

on cluster variables in
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

(d).

Every
consistency relation
in
\mathfrak{D}
is constructed from and reduced to a trivial one
(i.e.,
g=g
with
g\in G
) by applying the pentagon relation (and commutative relations) possibly infinitely many times.

Thus, the dilogarithm elements and the pentagon relation
are everything for a CSD; moreover, they build a bridge over the gap between the principles
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\mathfrak{D}
, namely, mutations and consistency.
We note that
the importance of the dilogarithm in scattering diagrams was already noticed in
[
KS08
]
. Also, the construction of scattering diagrams by the pentagon relation appeared in
[
GPS10
]
in a geometrical setting.

With this perspective in mind,
it is natural to extend basic notions for a cluster pattern to a CSD.
The notion of a
seed
, which is most fundamental for a cluster pattern,
cannot be extended to the entire CSD,
because there is no global chamber structure in a CSD in general
[
GHKK18
]
.
However, the notions of cluster variables (
x
-variables),
coefficients (
y
-variables), and their mutations can be extended to the entire CSD.
In fact, the
theta functions
in
[
GHKK18
]
is an extension of
the notion of
cluster monomials
.
In this paper we extend the notion of
y
-variables
to a CSD.
Accordingly, we also extend the
dilogarithm identity
(DI, for short) associated with a

period
in a cluster pattern
[
Nak11
]

to the one associated with a
loop
in a CSD
based on the classical mechanical method in
[
GNR17
]
.
This is our first main result (Theorem
3.6
).

Even though the structure of a CSD is very complicated as already mentioned,
it is tractable in some sense
thanks to the property (d).
As a result, the above DIs
are also constructed from and
reduced to trivial ones by applying the celebrated
pentagon identity
(the
five-term relation
) of the dilogarithm function

[
Lew81
]

possibly infinitely many times
in a parallel way.
(See
[
Zag07
, §2.A]
for the background of the reducibility problem of the dilogarithm identities
to the pentagon identity.)
This also provides an alternative and independent proof of the DIs.
This is our second main result (Theorem
4.6
).
We also give examples of DIs for rank 2 CSDs of affine type.

We conclude by giving some remarks on related works.

(i). The
y
-variables are also known as
cluster
\mathcal{X}
-variables

[
FG09a
]
.
The corresponding theta functions were studied via the CSDs with principal coefficients in
[
GHKK18
]
and via the scattering diagrams with the Langlands dual data
(
\mathcal{X}
scattering diagrmas) in
[
CMNC22
,
CKM22
]
.
Apparently our
y
-variables in CSDs are defined in a different way,
and we do not know how
they are related with the ones in the above works. See also Remark
3.2
(c).
The results in this paper show that our
y
-variables behave nicely at least in view of
dilogarithm identities.

(ii).
The
quantum analogue
of a CSD (QCSD) was constructed and studied by

[
Man21
,
DM21
,
CFMM20
]
in the skew-symmetric case,
and it is naturally extended to the skew-symmetrizable case as well.
From their construction,
we immediately obtain the
quantum DI (QDI)

(possibly with infinite product)
associated with a loop in a QCSD for the quantum dilogarithm of
[
FV93
,
FK94
]
,
which are the extension of the QDIs for a cluster pattern
studied in
[
FG09b
,
Kel11
,
Nag11
,
KN11
]
.
They are clearly the counterpart of the DIs in this paper.
However, we stress that the main results in this paper
are
not
straightforwardly obtained from the quantum case
by two reasons.
Firstly, the DIs here should be obtained from QDI
in their semiclassical limits in the sense of

[
KN11
]
; however, the method therein is only heuristic and not rigorous
even for the finite product case.
Secondly, it is not yet known that every constancy relation in a QCSD
is reduced to a trivial one by the pentagon relation.

2.
Cluster scattering diagrams

In this section we quickly recall basic notions for CSDs in
[
GHKK18
]
.
See also
[
Nak23
]
for a review, which is closer to the present context.

2.1.
Structure group

Let
\Gamma
be a
fixed data
consisting of the following:

•

a lattice
N
of rank
r
,

•

a skew-symmetric bilinear form
\{\cdot,\cdot\}:N\times N\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
,

•

a sublattice
N^{\circ}\subset N
of finite index such that
\{N^{\circ},N\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
,

•

positive integers
\delta_{1}
, …,
\delta_{r}
such that
there is a basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})
of
N
, where
(\delta_{1}e_{1},\dots,\delta_{r}e_{r})

is a basis of
N^{\circ}
,

•

M=\mathrm{Hom}(N,\mathbb{Z})
and
M^{\circ}=\mathrm{Hom}(N^{\circ},\mathbb{Z})
.

Let
M_{\mathbb{R}}=M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
.
Let

\langle n,m\rangle
denote the canonical paring either for
N^{\circ}\times M^{\circ}

or for
N\times M_{\mathbb{R}}
.
For
n\in N
,
n\neq 0
, let
n^{\perp}:=\{z\in M_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\langle n,z\rangle=0\}
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})
be a
seed
for
\Gamma
,
which are a basis of
N
such that
(\delta_{1}e_{1},\dots,\delta_{r}e_{r})
are a basis of
N^{\circ}
.
Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle N^{+}=\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}e_{i}\mid a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}>0\Biggr\}

be the set of the positive vectors of
N
with respect to
\mathfrak{s}
.
Let
N^{+}_{\mathrm{pr}}
denote the set of primitive elements in
N^{+}
.
The degree function
\deg:N^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is defined by
\deg(\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}e_{i}):=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}
.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the
N^{+}
-graded Lie algebra defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{n\in N^{+}}\mathbb{Q}X_{n},\quad[X_{n},X_{n^{\prime}}]:=\{n,n^{\prime}\}X_{n+n^{\prime}}.

Let
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
be the completion of
\mathfrak{g}
with respect to
\deg
, and let
G=G_{\Gamma,\mathfrak{s}}=\exp(\widehat{\mathfrak{g}})

be the exponential group of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Namely,
an element of
G
is given by a formal symbol
\exp(X)
(
X\in\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
)
and the product is defined by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula.
We call
G
the
structure group
for the forthcoming scattering diagrams.
Let
G^{>\ell}
be the normal subgroup of
G
generated by the elements with degrees greater than
\ell
,
and let
G^{\leq\ell}:=G/G^{>\ell}
be its quotient.
For each
n\in N_{\mathrm{pr}}^{+}
, let
G_{n}^{\parallel}
be the abelian subgroup of
G

generated by elements
\exp(c_{jn}X_{jn})
(
j>0
,
c_{jn}\in\mathbb{Q}
)
admitting the infinite product.

2.2.
Initial seed for cluster pattern

Let
(e^{*}_{1},\dots,e^{*}_{r})
be the basis of
M
which is dual to
\mathfrak{s}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})
.
Let
f_{i}=\delta_{i}^{-1}e^{*}_{i}
.
Then,
(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
is a basis of
M^{\circ}
, which is dual to
the basis
(\delta_{1}e_{1},\dots,\delta_{r}e_{r})
of
N^{\circ}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
,
B=B_{\Gamma,\mathfrak{s}}=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{r}
with

(2.3)

\displaystyle x_{i}=x^{f_{i}},\quad y_{i}=y^{e_{i}},\quad b_{ij}=\{\delta_{i}e_{i},e_{j}\},

where
x
and
y
are formal variables for formal exponentials.
Then,
B
is a skew-symmetrizable integer matrix with
a left skew-symmetrizer
\Delta^{-1}
, where we set

\Delta=\mathrm{diag}(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{r})
.
Thus,
\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
is naturally identified with
the
initial
seed for the cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathfrak{s}}
corresponding to
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
.
Conversely, for a given skew-symmetrizable integer matrix
B
, there is a (not unique) pair of
\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{s}
such that
B=B_{\Gamma,\mathfrak{s}}
.
Moreover,
two groups
G_{\Gamma,\mathfrak{s}}
and
G_{\Gamma^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}
are isomorphic
if
B_{\Gamma,\mathfrak{s}}=B_{\Gamma^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}
.
In this paper, we concentrate on
y
-variables only.

2.3.
y
-representation

We introduce a monoid
Q:=N^{+}\sqcup\{0\}\subset N
,
the monoid algebra
\mathbb{Q}[Q]
of
Q
over
\mathbb{Q}
,
and its completion
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]
with respect to
\deg
.
An element of
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]
is written as a formal infinite sum with a formal variable
y
as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in Q}c_{n}y^{n},\quad(c_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}).

It is also regarded as a formal power series of the

initial
y
-variables

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{r})

in (
2.3
).
We consider an action of
X_{n}
(
n\in N^{+}
) on
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]

(2.5)

\displaystyle X_{n}(y^{n^{\prime}})=\{n,n^{\prime}\}y^{n^{\prime}+n},

which is a derivation,
and we linearly extend it to the action of any
X\in\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Then, the resulting map
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Der}(\mathbb{Q}[[Q]])

is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
Moreover, its exponential action

(2.6)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Exp}\,X)(y^{n^{\prime}}):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}X^{j}(y^{n^{\prime}})\quad(X\in\widehat{\mathfrak{g}})

is well-defined on
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]
.
Then,
\mathrm{Exp}\,X

is an algebra automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]

(e.g.,
[
Jac79
, §1.2]
).
Thus, we have a representation of
G

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\rho_{y}:&G&\rightarrow&\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}[[Q]])\\
&\exp X&\mapsto&\mathrm{Exp}\,X.\end{matrix}

We call it the
y
-representation
of
G
.

2.4.
Dilogarithm elements and pentagon relation

Let us introduce some distinguished elements in
G
.

Definition 2.1

(Dilogarithm element)
.

For any
n\in N^{+}
, we define

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Psi[n]:=\exp\Biggl(\,\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j^{2}}X_{jn}\Biggr)\in G_{n_{0}}^{\parallel},

where
n_{0}\in N_{\mathrm{pr}}^{+}
is the one such that
n=jn_{0}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
We call it the
dilogarithm element
for
n
.

The element
\Psi[n]
acts on
\mathbb{Q}[[Q]]
under
\rho_{y}
as

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi[n](y^{n^{\prime}})&=\exp\Biggl(\,\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j^{2}}X_{jn}\Biggr)(y^{n^{\prime}})\\
&=y^{n^{\prime}}\exp\Biggl(\{n,n^{\prime}\}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}y^{jn}\Biggr)\\
&=y^{n^{\prime}}(1+y^{n})^{\{n,n^{\prime}\}}.\end{split}

Observe that
this is essentially the automorphism part of the Fock-Goncharov decomposition
of mutations of
y
-variables
[
FG09a
, §2.1]
.

It is easy to see that
the dilogarithm elements
\Psi[n]^{c}
(
n\in N^{+}
,
c\in\mathbb{Q}
) are generators of
G

admitting the infinite product.
Moreover, they satisfy the following remarkable relations.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\in N^{+}
.
The following relations hold in
G
.

(a). If
\{n_{2},n_{1}\}=0
,
for any
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Psi[n_{2}]^{c_{2}}\Psi[n_{1}]^{c_{1}}=\Psi[n_{1}]^{c_{1}}\Psi[n_{2}]^{c_{2}}.

(b).
(Pentagon relation

[
GHKK18
, Example 1.14]
,
[
Nak23
, Prop. III.1.13]
.)
If
\{n_{2},n_{1}\}=c

(c\in\mathbb{Q},c\neq 0)
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\quad\Psi[n_{2}]^{1/c}\Psi[n_{1}]^{1/c}=\Psi[n_{1}]^{1/c}\Psi[n_{1}+n_{2}]^{1/c}\Psi[n_{2}]^{1/c}.

Proof.

The equality (
2.10
) is clear by (
2.9
).
One can prove (
2.11
) by comparing the actions of both sides on
y^{n^{\prime}}

using (
2.9
).
∎

2.5.
Cluster scattering diagrams

A wall
\mathbf{w}=(\mathfrak{d},g)_{n}
for
\mathfrak{s}
is a triplet such that

n\in N_{\mathrm{pr}}^{+}
, a cone
\mathfrak{d}\subset n^{\perp}
of codimension 1, and
g\in G_{n}^{\parallel}
.
We call
n
,
\mathfrak{d}
,
g
, the
normal vector
, the
support
, the
wall element

of
\mathbf{w}
, respectively.
Let
p^{*}:N\rightarrow M^{\circ}
,
n\mapsto\{\cdot,n\}
.
We say that a wall
\mathbf{w}=(\mathfrak{d},g)_{n}
is
incoming
if
p^{*}(n)\in\mathfrak{d}
holds.

Definition 2.3

(Scattering diagram)
.

A
scattering diagram

\mathfrak{D}=\{\mathbf{w}_{\lambda}=(\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda},g_{{\lambda}})_{n_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
for
\mathfrak{s}
is a collection of walls for
\mathfrak{s}

satisfying the following finiteness condition:

For any degree
\ell
,
there are only finitely many walls such that
\pi_{\ell}(g_{\lambda})\neq\mathrm{id}
,
where
\pi_{\ell}:G\rightarrow G^{\leq\ell}
is the canonical projection.

For a scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}
, we define

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Supp}(\mathfrak{D})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda},\quad\mathrm{Sing}(\mathfrak{D})=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\partial\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda}\cup\bigcup_{\scriptstyle\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda\atop\scriptstyle\dim\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda}\cap\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=r-2}\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda}\cap\mathfrak{d}_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

A curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{R}}
is

admissible
for
\mathfrak{D}

if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

The end points of
\gamma
are in
M_{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\mathrm{Supp}(\mathfrak{D})
.

(ii)

It is a smooth curve, and it intersects
\mathrm{Supp}(\mathfrak{D})
transversally.

(iii)

\gamma
does not intersect
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathfrak{D})
.

For any admissible curve
\gamma
,
the path-ordered product
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}}\in G
is defined
as the product of
the wall elements
g_{{\lambda}}^{\epsilon_{\lambda}}
of walls
\mathbf{w}_{\lambda}
of
\mathfrak{D}
intersected by
\gamma

in the order of intersection, where
\epsilon_{\lambda}
is the
intersection sign

defined by

(2.13)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}1&\langle n_{\lambda},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle<0,\\
-1&\langle n_{\lambda},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle>0,\end{cases}

and
\gamma^{\prime}
is the velocity vector of
\gamma
at the wall
\mathbf{w}_{\lambda}
.
The product
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}}
is an infinite one in general,
and it is well-defined in
G
due to the finiteness condition.
See
[
GHKK18
, §1.1]
for a more precise definition.
We say that a pair of scattering diagrams
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}}=\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}}

for any admissible curve
\gamma
for both
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
.
We say that a scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}
is
consistent
if
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}}=\mathrm{id}

for any admissible loop (i.e., closed curve)
\gamma
for
\mathfrak{D}
.

Definition 2.4

(Cluster scattering diagram)
.

A
cluster scattering diagram

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
(CSD, for short) for
\mathfrak{s}
is a consistent scattering diagram
whose set of incoming walls are given by

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{In}_{\mathfrak{s}}:=\{\mathbf{w}_{e_{i}}=(e_{i}^{\perp},\Psi[e_{i}]^{\delta_{i}})_{e_{i}}\mid i=1,\dots,r\}.

For
n\in N^{+}
, let
\delta(n)
be the smallest positive rational number
such that
\delta(n)n\in N^{\circ}
,
which is called the
normalization factor
of
n
.
For example,
\delta(e_{i})=\delta_{i}
.
Note that
\delta(tn)=\delta(n)/t
holds for any
n\in N^{+}_{\mathrm{pr}}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Also,
\delta(n)
is an integer for any
n\in N^{+}_{\mathrm{pr}}
,

The following is the most fundamental theorem on CSDs.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
GHKK18
, Theorems 1.12 & 1.13]
)
.

(a). There exists a CSD
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
uniquely up to equivalence.

(b).
There exists a (still not unique) CSD
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
such that
every wall element has
the form

(2.15)

\displaystyle\Psi[tn]^{s\delta(tn)}\quad(n\in N_{\mathrm{pr}}^{+},\ t,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}).

In this paper we exclusively use
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
given in Theorem
2.5
(b),
which we call a
positive realization
a CSD
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
.

Remark 2.6
.

The fact
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is the key to prove the
Laurent positivity
of cluster variables and
theta functions
in
[
GHKK18
]
, though we do not use this connection in this paper.

3.
Dilogarithm identities in CSDs

3.1.
y
-variables for CSD

Let us extend the notion of
y
-variables (coefficients) for a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathfrak{s}}
to a CSD
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
.
We say that a curve is
weakly admissible
for
\mathfrak{D}
if it satisfies the
conditions (ii) and (iii) for an admissible curve.
The definition of the path-ordered product
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma,\mathfrak{D}}
is extended to a weakly admissible curve
\gamma

by ignoring the contribution from the walls at the end points.

Definition 3.1

(
y
-variable/
c
-vector for CSD)
.

Let
\mathbf{w}=(\mathfrak{d},g)_{n}
be any wall of
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}

with
g=\Psi[tn]^{s\delta(tn)}
as in (
2.15
).
Let
z\in\mathfrak{d}

with
z\not\in\mathrm{Sing}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}})
.
Let

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{+}:=\{z\in M_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\langle e_{i},z\rangle\geq 0\ (i=1,\dots,r)\}.

Let
\gamma_{z}
be any weakly admissible curve in
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}

from
z
to any point in
\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{C}^{+})
.
Then, we define a
y
-variable
y_{z}[tn]
at
z
with the
c
-vector

tn
by

(3.2)

\displaystyle y_{z}[tn]:=\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma_{z},\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}}(y^{tn})\in\mathbb{Q}[[Q]],

where the path-ordered product
\mathfrak{p}_{\gamma_{z},\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}}\in G
acts on
y^{tn}

under the
y
-representation
\rho_{y}
.

Since any
g\in G_{n}^{\parallel}
acts trivially on
y^{tn}
,
y_{z}[tn]
is independent of the choice of

\gamma_{z}
due to the consistency of
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{s}}
.
Also, due to our assumption on

3.2.

We define the Euler dilogarithm

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}x^{j}\quad(|x|<1)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\log(1-y)}{y}\,dy\quad(x\leq 1),

The celebrated

(0\leq x,y<1)

\displaystyle L(x)+L(y)=L(xy)+L\biggl(\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}\biggr)+L\biggl(\frac{y(1-x)}{1-xy}\biggr).

We introduce a variant of the Rogers dilogarithm,
which we call the
modified Rogers dilogarithm
, as follows:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}(x):

\displaystyle=L\left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)=-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-x)-\frac{1}{2}\log x\log(1+x)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}\left\{\frac{\log(1+y)}{y}-\frac{\log y}{1+y}\right\}dy\quad(0\leq x).

The function

expansion
around
x=0

with
\log

:

(3.10)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j^{2}}x^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\log x\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}x^{j}.

The pentagon identity (

) is expressed in terms of

[

, §5.3]

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{L}(y_{2}(1+y_{1}))+\tilde{L}(y_{1})\\
&\qquad=\tilde{L}(y_{1}(1+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2})^{-1})+\tilde{L}(y_{1}y_{2}(1+y_{2})^{-1})+\tilde{L}(y_{2}).\end{split}

The above arguments in

y
-variables with positive

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\begin{pmatrix}\framebox{$y_{1}$}\\
y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{{\mu_{1}}}}\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}^{-1}\\

3.11
)
possibly infinitely many times.

Thus, our DIs have the
infinite reducibility

in view of the reducibility problem of the DIs to the pentagon relation
[
Zag07
, §2.A]
.

Remark 4.9
.

(a).
The above corollary does
not
imply that
any
\sigma
-period of a cluster pattern is reduced to a trivial one
by applying the square and pentagon periodicities
in the cluster pattern itself
.
In fact, Example
4.7
is such a case,
where there are no square and pentagon periodicities at all
in the cluster pattern.
Some other examples are also known
[
FST08
,
KY20
]
.

(b) Even if the corresponding DI for a cluster pattern has only finitely many terms,
it might be necessary to apply the pentagon relation
infinitely many times

to reduce it to a trivial one, in general.
