Relation between Hardy components for locally supported vector fields on the sphere
C. Gerhards, X. Huang, A. Kegeles

Abstract




Abstract.

Given a function in the Hardy space of inner harmonic gradients on the sphere,
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
, we consider the problem of finding a corresponding function in the Hardy space of outer harmonic gradients on the sphere,
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, such that the sum of both functions differs from a locally supported vector field only by a tangential divergence-free contribution. We characterize the subspace of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
that allows such a continuation and show that it is dense but not closed within
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. Furthermore, we derive the linear mapping that maps a vector field from this subspace of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
to the corresponding unique vector field in
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
. The explicit construction uses layer potentials but involves unbounded operators. We indicate some bounded extremal problems supporting a possible numerical evaluation of this mapping between the Hardy components. The original motivation to study this problem comes from an inverse magnetization problem with localization constraints.

Keywords.

Hardy spaces, Constrained approximation, Vector field decomposition, Locally divergence-free fields, Spatially localized fields

1
Introduction

The space of square-integrable vector fields on a sphere
\mathbb{S}
can be written as a direct sum

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})=H_{+}(\mathbb{S})\oplus H_{-}(\mathbb{S})\oplus H_{df}(\mathbb{S}),

(1.1)

where
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
is the space of divergence-free vector fields, and
H_{+}(\mathbb{S}),H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
are the (spherical) Hardy spaces (see below for more details).
For a generic
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
its Hardy components
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
are independent, implying that either one of them is not enough to reconstruct the other without knowing the entire field
\mathbf{f}
.
However, if
\mathbf{f}
is localized, meaning that it vanishes on an open domain of
\mathbb{S}
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
13
, Cor. 2.6]
)
.

If
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is localized
then one Hardy component of
\mathbf{f}
uniquely determines the other.

This result is useful for the following type of inverse magnetization problems:
Consider a magnetic sphere and use
\mathbf{f}
to describe its magnetization.
It is known that outside the sphere only the Hardy component

\mathbf{f}_{+}
generates a measurable magnetic field, whereas the Hardy component
\mathbf{f}_{-}
is invisible (e.g.,
[
11
,
15
]
). Consequently, it is impossible to determine the magnetization of the sphere by measuring only its outer magnetic field.
Nevertheless, if the magnetization is a priori known to vanish on some open subset of
\mathbb{S}
, Theorem
1.1
implies that
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
are both determined by the magnetic field outside the sphere and thus the entire magnetization
\mathbf{f}
can be recovered (up to a divergence-free part which is never visible).
This particular setup appears in geomagnetism, where one typically models the Earth’s crust as a magnetized sphere and measures its outer magnetic field on a satellite orbit (for more details
see e.g.,
[
4
,
6
,
11
,
12
]
).

Devoid of the geomagnetic context, Theorem
1.1
states that the Hardy components of a localized vector field are dependent, but its proof in
[
13
]
is not constructive and it does not state how to obtain one component from the other.
Furthermore, to relate the Hardy components we need to know a priori that the given
\mathbf{f}_{+}
belongs to a localized vector field
\mathbf{f}
. Nevertheless, Theorem
1.1
does not characterize Hardy components that appear from such localized vector fields. Thus, when looking only at a single Hardy component, we lack the means to verify whether the assumption of the theorem can be satisfied.

In this paper we close both of the above shortcomings.
In Section
2
, we rephrase the problem into a more convenient setting in terms of locally divergence-free vector fields. Subsequently, we characterize the set of Hardy components that belong to strictly localized fields and provide explicit linear mappings to reconstruct one component from the other. Nevertheless, these mappings are unbounded
and thus difficult to evaluate numerically. For that reason, in Section
3
we suggest several bounded extremal problems to approximate the reconstruction procedure.

Notation.

By
\mathbb{B}
we will denote the open unit ball in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 3
). Its boundary, the unit sphere, will be denoted by
\mathbb{S}
.
The space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
will be the space of square-integrable (with respect to the surface measure) functions on
\mathbb{S}
, with the canonical scalar product
\big\langle f,g\big\rangle
and the norm
\|f\|=\big\langle f,f\big\rangle^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
for
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. For an open set
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{S}
we will consider
L^{2}(\Sigma)
as a subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
of functions with essential support in
\Sigma
.
If
H
is a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, we will write
H/\langle 1\rangle
to denote the subspace of functions in
H
that satisfy
\big\langle f,1\big\rangle=0
for
f\in H
, i.e., functions in
H
with zero mean.
If
A
is a linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
then
\textnormal{dom}(A)
will denote its domain and for
H\subseteq\textnormal{dom}(A)
we will write
A\big(H\big)=\{Af\colon\ f\in H\}
for the image of
H
under
A
, and
A|_{H}
for the operator
A
restricted to the subspace
H
.
The space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is identified with the tensor product
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
, referring to the space of square integrable vector fields on
\mathbb{S}
. For
\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we will denote their scalar product by
\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle
and the norm by
\|\mathbf{f}\|=\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.
The sphere
\mathbb{S}
admits an outward-pointing unit normal vector field
\boldsymbol{\eta}
. This field is continuous, so that for every
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
the (Euclidean) scalar product
\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\mathbf{f}
defines a function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
which we call the normal of
\mathbf{f}
.
If
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a vanishing normal, we call
\mathbf{f}
a tangent vector field. If
\mathbf{f}
is a tangent vector field, we will write
\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{f})
to denote the surface divergence of
\mathbf{f}
in the distributional sense.
The space
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
will be the space of divergence-free vector fields, i.e., the space of vector fields
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
that have vanishing normal and whose surface divergence
\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{f})
vanishes as a distribution on the entire sphere.
If
\Sigma
is an open subset of
\mathbb{S}
, we will write
\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{f})|_{\Sigma}
to denote the distribution on
\Sigma
that results from restricting the distribution
\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{f})
to this subset (no confusion with the operator restriction shall arise since the difference will be clear from the context).
The space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
will refer to the Sobolev space of functions
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
whose surface gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}f
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
; equipped with the graph norm
\|f\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})}=\|f\|+\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, the space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
is itself a Hilbert space. Furthermore, if
\Sigma
is an open subset of
\mathbb{S}
, then
C^{\infty}_{0}(Z)
will be the space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{S}
with compact support in
\Sigma
. The closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)
in the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})}
is the Sobolev space
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)
which is a closed subset of
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
.

Layer potentials and Hardy spaces.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
we write its single layer potential as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}f(x)=\frac{-1}{\omega_{d}(d-2)}\ \int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{d-2}}\ d\omega(y)\qquad\big(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\big),

(1.2)

where
\omega
is the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}
, the constant
\omega_{d}=\omega(\mathbb{S})
is the surface area of the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean vector norm. The function
\mathcal{S}f
is continuous on the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, harmonic on
\mathbb{B}
, and harmonic on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}
while vanishing at infinity.
The trace of
\mathcal{S}f
to the sphere,
\trace(\mathcal{S}f)
, defines the (boundary) single layer potential
S\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
via

Sf(x)=\frac{-1}{\omega_{d}(d-2)}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{d-2}}\ d\omega(y)=\trace(\mathcal{S}f)(x)\qquad\big(x\in\mathbb{S}\big).

The operator
S
is invertible, and (on the sphere) it preserves constant functions, meaning that
Sf
is a constant function if and only if
f
is constant. By definition, every function
f
in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
has a surface gradient on the sphere
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}f
that is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore, the mapping
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S
from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
defines a bounded linear operator, whose adjoint
(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}
is a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.

Remark 1.2
.

If
W^{-1,2}(\mathbb{S})
denotes the dual space of the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
, then we can define the Banach space adjoint of
S
as the mapping
S^{*}\colon W^{-1,2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. In this case, we can identify
(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}
with the mapping

\displaystyle S^{*}\circ\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to W^{-1,2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}).

(1.3)

However, for the following analysis it will be more convenient to stay in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and to not switch between Sobolev spaces. For this reason we will use the operators
(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}
instead of
S^{*}\circ\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}
, even though the latter may be more familiar to some readers.

The normal derivative trace
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{+}}\mathcal{S}f
and
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{-}}\mathcal{S}f
to the sphere from the inside and the outside, respectively, define the operators

\displaystyle K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle,

\displaystyle K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}),

(1.4)

respectively,
where
I
is the identity operator and
K
is the singular double layer potential

Kf(x)=\textnormal{p.v.}\,\frac{-1}{\omega_{d}}\ \int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}(y)\cdot(x-y)}{|x-y|^{3}}f(y)\ d\omega(y)\qquad\big(x\in\mathbb{S}\big).

Both operators in (
1.4
) are invertible and self-adjoint (on the sphere). The operator
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
preserves constant functions, while the operator
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I
, extended to act on constant functions, annihilates them. For more information on the layer potentials we refer e.g., to
[
8
,
24
]
and references therein.

Hardy spaces and the Hardy-Hodge decomposition.

Using the single and the double layer potentials, we now define the required Hardy spaces. Our exposition of Hardy spaces is slightly unconventional but it serves the purpose to introduce them without additional analytical tools that are necessary for the original and conventional definition (e.g.,
[
23
]
and Appendix
A.1
). We define the Hardy spaces
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
as the range of the operators

\displaystyle B_{+}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle B_{-}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})

(1.5)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\boldsymbol{\eta}\ \big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)f+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\boldsymbol{\eta}\ \big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)f+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf.

Both operators in (
1.5
) are bounded, have closed range in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and on that range they are bijective and thus (boundedly) invertible. The range of
B_{+}
defines the Hardy space
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
of non-tangential limits of harmonic gradients from within the ball
\mathbb{B}
to the sphere, while the range of
B_{-}
defines the Hardy space
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
of non-tangential limits of harmonic gradients from outside the ball
\mathbb{B}
to the sphere.

The Hardy-Hodge decomposition of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the orthogonal direct sum (
1.1
).
Thus, every vector field
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be uniquely written as

\mathbf{f}=B_{+}\varphi+B_{-}\phi+\mathbf{f}_{df}
for some unique
\varphi,\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
with
\big\langle\varphi,1\big\rangle=0
. For brevity, we will sometimes write
\mathbf{f}_{+}
for
B_{+}\varphi
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
for
B_{-}\phi
, and refer to
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
as to the Hardy components of
\mathbf{f}
.
Since the Hardy-Hodge decomposition is orthogonal, it follows that for every
\varphi,\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
we have

\displaystyle\big\langle B_{+}\phi,B_{-}\varphi\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0.

(1.6)

By inserting the definition of the operators
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
in this equation and using the adjoint of
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S
we get the operator identity

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S=-\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big).

(1.7)

We will use this relation several times in the following analysis. It is worth to point out that this relation holds only on a sphere, since on other surfaces the Hardy-spaces (if defined) are not orthogonal.
Further details regarding Hardy spaces and the Hardy-Hodge decomposition can be found, e.g., in
[
1
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
11
]
, which also consider more general surfaces than the sphere.

1.1
Localized and locally divergence-free vector fields

Theorem
1.1
discusses localized fields – fields that vanish on an open set of
\mathbb{S}
. For the further analysis, however, it appears convenient to rephrase this condition solely in terms of Hardy components of a field. We observe that writing
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the Hardy-Hodge decomposition and assuming that
\mathbf{f}
vanishes on the open set
\Sigma
yields
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}=-\mathbf{f}_{df}
on
\Sigma
. In other words, if a vector field vanishes on an open domain of a sphere, then on this domain the sum of its Hardy components must have a vanishing normal and it must be divergence-free (in the distributional sense). We call such fields locally divergence-free on
\Sigma
.

The converse statement holds in the following form: if
\mathbf{f}
is a locally divergence-free field on
\Sigma
, then for every open set
O\subset\Sigma
with a sufficiently regular boundary in the sense the boundary separate
\mathbb{S}
into two Lipschitz domains, there exists a
\mathbf{g}\in H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
that is divergence-free on the entire sphere such that
\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{g}
vanishes on
O
. This statement follows from the fact that if the boundary of
O
satisfies the above conditions, then we can always extend a locally divergence-free field to a divergence-free field on the entire sphere (for the convenience of the reader we include the necessary extension result and its proof in the Appendix
A.2
).
Since
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
is orthogonal to the Hardy spaces, adding an appropriate
\mathbf{g}\in H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
to
\mathbf{f}
leaves the Hardy components of
\mathbf{f}
unchanged. Thus, looking only at Hardy components of a vector field, it makes no difference whether we assume the field
\mathbf{f}
to vanish on an open set or to be locally divergence-free. We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
be a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary. If
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
vanishes on
\Sigma
, then the sum of its Hardy-components
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
is locally divergence-free on
\Sigma
. Conversely, if for given
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
the sum
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
is locally divergence-free on
\Sigma
, then there exists a field
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
that vanishes on
\Sigma
and has Hardy-components
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
.

If
\Sigma
is open in
\mathbb{S}
, we can always choose a subset
O
of
\Sigma
with a sufficiently regular boundary to satisfy the requirement of the proposition above. Thus, with the aid of Proposition
1.3
our initial goal of finding a relation between the Hardy components of strictly localized vector fields becomes equivalent to finding a relation between Hardy components
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
under the assumption that their sum is locally divergence-free on
\Sigma
. This is what we investigate in the remaining part of the paper.

2
Hardy components of locally divergence-free vector fields

Our goal of this section is to prove Theorem
2.6
, which defines an “if and only if” condition for a vector field to be locally divergence-free on an open subset of a sphere.
To this aim, the following space will be pivotal:
for an open set
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{S}
we define

\displaystyle\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\colon\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf)|_{\Sigma}=0\text{ as distribution on }\Sigma\big\}.

(2.1)

This space is non-empty as it contains constant functions: if
c
is a constant function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, then
Sc
is again constant and
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sc=0
. Moreover,
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
is closed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. To see this consider a Cauchy sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
that converges to
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. Then for

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)
, it holds

\displaystyle\big|\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf)(\varphi)\big|=\big|\big\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\big\rangle\big|=\big|\big\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S(f-f_{n}),\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\big\rangle\big|\leq C\big\|f-f_{n}\big\|,

(2.2)

where
C
is a finite constant that depends on
\varphi
. The right-hand side of
(
2.2
) goes to zero as
n
grows to infinity,
implying that
f
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
and that
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.

The space
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
defines functions
that we call
S
-harmonic
on
\Sigma
.
If
f
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, then its surface gradient is naturally a distribution and thus not necessarily square integrable.
The function
Sf
, on the other hand, is in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
, and
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf
is always a square integrable field in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, since
S
is one-to-one and onto
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
, we can unambiguously define
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf
as an
L^{2}
-surface gradient
of
f
. Thus,
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
defines functions with a divergence-free
L^{2}
-surface gradient on
\Sigma
. Consequently, if
f
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
then vanishing divergence of
\nabla Sf
implies that
Sf
is weakly harmonic on
\Sigma
. By Weyl’s Lemma
Sf
is harmonic on
\Sigma
, which we summarize by saying that
f
is
S
-harmonic there.

The orthogonal complement of
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
will also be important in what follows. It is characterized by the following lemma that we prove in the Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
be open and
\mathbb{S}\setminus\Sigma
have positive surface measure, then the orthogonal complement of
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
is the set

\displaystyle\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}^{\perp}=\big\{\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)(S^{-1}\varphi)\colon\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)\big\}.

(2.3)

In the above Lemma we require the mild condition that
\mathbb{S}\setminus\Sigma
must have positive measure. However, in the following it will be necessary to strengthen this requirement and to demand that
\mathbb{S}\setminus\Sigma
contains an open set. To ensure this, we will write
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
, meaning that
\Sigma
and its closure
\overline{\Sigma}
are both proper subsets of
\mathbb{S}
. The following theorem indicates why this additional condition on the closure of
\Sigma
is important.
Moreover, for the rest of the paper we will use the following notation: if
\Sigma
is an open subset of
\mathbb{S}
, then
P_{\Sigma}
will denote the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
onto
L^{2}(\Sigma)
. If the set
\Sigma
is clear from the context, we will write
P
instead of
P_{\Sigma}
and call it
the corresponding projection
to the set
\Sigma
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{S}
be open and
P
be the corresponding projection in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.
The operators
P\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right):\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle\to L^{2}(\Sigma)
and
P\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right):\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}\to L^{2}(\Sigma)
are injective. If additionally
\overline{\Sigma}
is a proper subset of
\mathbb{S}
, then the range of both operators is dense in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
, but neither range is closed.

Proof.

For injectivity of
P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)
, assume a
g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
such that

P\left(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)g=0.

(2.4)

For an open cone
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)=\{r\xi:\xi\in\Sigma,\ r\in(0,\infty)\}
set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)\cap\mathbb{B}
and define two functions
u
and
w
as

\displaystyle u(r\xi)\coloneqq\mathcal{S}g(r\xi),

\displaystyle w(r\xi)\coloneqq Sg(\xi)\qquad\big(\xi\in\Sigma,\ r\in\mathbb{R},\ r\xi\in{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)}\big).

(2.5)

Observe that by the definition of layer potentials in (
1.4
), condition (
2.4
) states that the normal derivative of
u
from the inside to the sphere,
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{+}}u
, vanishes on
\Sigma
.
Since
g
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
, it is
S
-harmonic.
Consequently,
\Delta w(r\xi)=r^{-2}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}Sg(\xi)=0
, and the function
w
is harmonic in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)
. It follows that the functions
u
and
w
are both harmonic in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)
, coincide on
\Sigma
, and have a vanishing normal derivative on
\Sigma
.
Set
v=u-w
. Then
v
is continuous on
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
, vanishes on
\Sigma
, and its normal derivative also vanishes on
\Sigma
.
Therefore, extending
v
by zero to the whole cone
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)
defines a harmonic function. This function vanishes on an open set
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})
and coincides with
v
on
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)
. By analyticity of harmonic functions,
v
must be identically zero,
which yields
u(r\xi)=w(r\xi)=Sg(\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Sigma
and
r\in(0,1)
. By continuity of
u
at the origin, we have

\displaystyle Sg(\xi)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}u(r\xi)=u(0)\qquad\big(\xi\in\Sigma\big),

(2.6)

and hence,
u
must be constant in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)
.
Because
u
coincides with
\mathcal{S}g
on the non-empty open set
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{S}g
is harmonic in all of
\mathbb{B}
, it follows that
\mathcal{S}g
must be a constant function on
\mathbb{B}
. Taking the trace to the sphere yields that
Sg
is constant on
\mathbb{S}
. Since the single layer potential is injective and on the sphere preserves constant functions,
g
must be constant, and since the only constant function in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
is the zero function, this proves injectivity.

The injectivity of
P\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)
follows the same line of reasoning as above, after exchanging the role of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{B}}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})
, and replacing the limit
r\to 0
by
r\to\infty
. Since the region
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})
is unbounded, the preceding argument implies that
\mathcal{S}g
is not only constant but indeed zero outside the ball
\mathbb{B}
, and consequently
Sg
must vanish on
\mathbb{S}
. Since
S
is injective it follows that
g
must be identically zero.

If
\omega
is an open subset of
\Sigma
and
P_{\omega}
is the corresponding projection then the above argument implies that
P_{\omega}\left(K\pm\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)g
is injective. Thus, the functions
\left(K\pm\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)g
cannot vanish on any open subset of
\Sigma
unless being identically zero. Hence, the range of
P\left(K\pm\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)
is not the whole space
L^{2}(\Sigma)
.

To prove the density of the range, let
f\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
be such that

\displaystyle 0=\big\langle f,P\left(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)g\big\rangle=\big\langle\left(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)f,g\big\rangle\qquad\big(g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\penalty\langle 1\rangle\big),

(2.7)

where the second equality holds because
K
is self-adjoint and
Pf=f
. Thus,
(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)f
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}^{\perp}
(since constant functions are not in the range of
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I
). From (
2.3
), there exists a function
\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)
such that

(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)\big((K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}\varphi-f\big)=0
.
Since
\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)
is invertible on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, there must exists a constant
c
such that
(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}\varphi+c=f
. Since
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
and
S
preserve constant functions, there is a (possibly different) constant
c
with

\displaystyle\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}(\varphi+c)=f.

(2.8)

If
\overline{\Sigma}
is a proper subset of
\mathbb{S}
, then
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}
is open and the corresponding projection onto
L^{2}(\Gamma)
is
I-P
.
Now, the function
S^{-1}(\varphi+c)
is in the set
V_{\Gamma}
since
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S(S^{-1}(\varphi+c))=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}(\varphi+c)=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi
vanishes on
\Gamma
.
Moreover, since
f
also vanishes on
\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle(I-P)\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}(\varphi+c)=(I-P)f=0.

(2.9)

From the first part of the proof we know that
(I-P)(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)
is injective on
V_{\Gamma}
and thus (
2.9
) implies that
S^{-1}(\varphi+c)
must be zero. From (
2.8
) we therefore get
f=0
. This shows that the orthogonal complement of the range of
P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)
contains only the zero function, i.e., the range is dense in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
. At the same time, the range cannot be closed since it is not the whole
L^{2}(\Sigma)
(as we have noticed in the paragraph after proving injectivity). This proves the claim for the operator
P\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)
.

To show density for the operator
P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)
we follow similar arguments. Assume an
f\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
such that

\displaystyle 0=\big\langle f,P\left(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)g\big\rangle=\big\langle\left(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)f,g\big\rangle\qquad\big(g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}\big).

(2.10)

Then
(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)f
must be in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}^{\perp}
. Again, from (
2.3
) and the fact that
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
is invertible on the entire
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, there must be a
\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)
such that

\displaystyle\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}\varphi=f.

(2.11)

With the same argument as above, the function
S^{-1}\varphi
is in
V_{\Gamma}
and
(I-P)f=0
. By injectivity of the operator
(I-P)(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)
on
V_{\Gamma}/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, the function
S^{-1}\varphi
must be constant and
f=0
from (
2.11
) (since
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I
annihilates constant functions). Consequently, the range of
P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
but, as already stated, it is not the whole
L^{2}(\Sigma)
.
∎

To avoid pathological cases, we will henceforth assume
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
. Then, the above theorem asserts that the operators
P\left(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)
and
P\left(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\right)
(acting on their corresponding domains) are invertible on their dense ranges, but the inverses are unbounded.
This observation leads us to define the following spaces: for
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
open, we define

\displaystyle D_{+,\Sigma}=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\colon\ P_{\Sigma}f=P_{\Sigma}(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)g,\text{ for some }g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}\big\}/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle,

(2.12)

and

\displaystyle D_{-,\Sigma}=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\colon P_{\Sigma}f=P_{\Sigma}(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)g,\text{ for some }g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle\big\}.

(2.13)

By the injectivity from Theorem
2.2
, if a
g
in the above definition exists, it must be unique;
by the density assertion of the same theorem, the space
D_{+,\Sigma}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
and the space
D_{-,\Sigma}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. We use these spaces to define the following operators.

Definition 2.3
.

For
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
open, we define the operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle&\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\\
f&\mapsto\left[P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)\right]^{-1}Pf-f,\end{split}

(2.14)

acting on the dense domain
\textnormal{dom}(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}})=D_{+,\Sigma}
,
and the operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})&\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\\
f&\mapsto-\left[P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)\right]^{-1}Pf-f+\big\langle f,1\big\rangle,\end{split}

(2.15)

acting on the dense domain
\textnormal{dom}(\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}})=D_{-,\Sigma}
.

Both operators are unbounded, as the operators
P(K\pm\tfrac{1}{2})
have no closed range.

Remark 2.4
.

For better readability, we mention that the indices on the operators
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
stand for “plus to minus” or “minus to plus”, which will indicate the mappings between Hardy components.

We have the following relation between the domains of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
open, we have
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\big(D_{+,\Sigma}\big)\subseteq D_{-,\Sigma}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\big(D_{-,\Sigma}\big)\subseteq D_{+,\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be in
D_{+,\Sigma}
, and set

\displaystyle\phi=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)=\Big[P\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\Big]^{-1}P\varphi-\varphi.

(2.16)

From the definition of the space
D_{+,\Sigma}
, we have
P\varphi=P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)g
for some
g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
. Using this fact in (
2.16
) and applying
P
on both sides of the equation, we get

\displaystyle P\phi=Pg-P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)g=-P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)g=-P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)(g-\big\langle g,1\big\rangle),

(2.17)

where the last equality follows since
\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)
annihilates constant functions. Since
g
and
\big\langle g,1\big\rangle
(viewed as a constant function) are both in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
, we have
g-\big\langle g,1\big\rangle\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
and consequently

\phi\in D_{-,\Sigma}
.

For the second inclusion, let
\phi
be in
D_{-,\Sigma}
and set

\displaystyle\varphi=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)=-\Big[P\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\Big]^{-1}P\phi-\phi+\big\langle\phi,1\big\rangle.

(2.18)

From the definition of
D_{-,\Sigma}
, we have
P\phi=P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)g
for some
g\in\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
. Using this fact in (
2.18
), we get
\big\langle\varphi,1\big\rangle=-\big\langle g,1\big\rangle=0
, and moreover,

\displaystyle P\varphi=-Pg-P(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)g+P\big\langle\phi,1\big\rangle=-P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)g+P\big\langle\phi,1\big\rangle.

(2.19)

Since
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
preserves constant functions, the above equation implies
P\varphi=-P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)(g+c)
for some constant function
c
. Moreover, since constant functions are in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
and
g
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, the function
g+c
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
.
Therefore,
\varphi
is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
.
∎

We now state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
be open.
If
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is tangent on
\Sigma
and
\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{f})|_{\Sigma}=0
,
then its Hardy component
\mathbf{f}_{+}
is in
B_{+}\big(D_{+,\Sigma}\big)
and its Hardy component
\mathbf{f}_{-}
is in
B_{-}\big(D_{-,\Sigma}\big)
.
Conversely, for every
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in B_{+}\big(D_{+,\Sigma}\big)
there is a unique
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in B_{-}\big(D_{-,\Sigma}\big)
such that
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
. The last statement also holds with
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}
interchanged.

Proof.

Using the operators from (
1.5
) we can express the Hardy-Hodge components of
\mathbf{f}
as
\mathbf{f}_{+}=B_{+}\varphi
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}=B_{-}\phi
for some unique
\varphi,\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
with
\big\langle\varphi,1\big\rangle=0
.

For the first assertion, set
P=P_{\Sigma}
and assume that
\mathbf{f}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
. Using (
1.5
), the normal and the tangent components of
\mathbf{f}
restricted to
\Sigma
read

\displaystyle P\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\varphi+P\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\phi=0,

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S(\varphi+\phi)\big)|_{\Sigma}=0.

(2.20)

From the second equation in (
2.20
) it follows that the function
\varphi+\phi
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
. Writing
\phi=(\varphi+\phi)-\varphi
, inserting it into the first equation in (
2.20
) and rearranging the terms yields

\displaystyle P\varphi=P\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)(\varphi+\phi).

(2.21)

Hence
\varphi
is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{f}_{+}=B_{+}\varphi
is in
B_{+}(D_{+,\Sigma})
.
To see that also the
\mathbf{f}_{-}
component is in the desired space, let
c=\big\langle\phi,1\big\rangle
be the mean of
\phi
. Then, writing
\varphi=(\varphi+\phi-c)-(\phi-c)
, performing the same manipulations as above, and using the fact that
\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)c=0
, yields

\displaystyle P\phi=-P\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)(\varphi+\phi-c).

(2.22)

The function
\varphi+\phi
and the constant function
c
are both in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
. And since
\varphi+\phi-c
has zero mean, it is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
,
implying that
\phi
is in
D_{-,\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}=B_{-}\phi
is in
B_{-}(D_{-,\Sigma})
. Clearly, both components are unique by the direct-sum property of the Hardy-Hodge decomposition.

For the converse statement, assume an
\mathbf{f}_{+}=B_{+}\varphi
with
\varphi\in D_{+,\Sigma}
and set

\displaystyle\phi=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)=\Big[P\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\Big]^{-1}P\varphi-\varphi.

(2.23)

By Lemma
2.5
we have
\phi=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)\in D_{-,\Sigma}
,
and choosing
\mathbf{f}_{-}=B_{-}\phi
, we get from (
2.23
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}

\displaystyle=B_{+}\varphi+B_{-}\phi

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}\,(I-P)\Big[\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\varphi+\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\phi\Big]+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S(\varphi+\phi).

(2.24)

Thus, the normal component of
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
vanishes on
\Sigma
. Furthermore, it holds that
\varphi+\phi
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
because (
2.23
) and the definition of
D_{+,\Sigma}
imply the existence of a
g
in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
with

\displaystyle\varphi+\phi=\Big[P\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\Big]^{-1}P\varphi=g.

(2.25)

Eventually, (
2
) implies that
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
as asserted.

Let
\phi
be as in (
2.23
) and assume that there is another
\tilde{\phi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
leading to the field
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}=B_{-}\tilde{\phi}
such that
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
. Then, from the first part of the proof, (
2.21
) holds for both,
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
, and thus

\displaystyle P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)(\varphi+\phi)=P\varphi=P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)(\varphi+\tilde{\phi}).

(2.26)

By injectivity of
P(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)
from Theorem
2.2
, it follows that
\tilde{\phi}=\phi
.

Using the same arguments with obvious modifications, we see that for every
\mathbf{f}_{-}=B_{-}\phi
with
\phi\in D_{-,\Sigma}
, the field
\mathbf{f}_{+}=B_{+}\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)
satisfies the stated conditions and is also unique.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

For
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
open, the space
B_{+}(D_{+,\Sigma})
is dense in
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and the space
B_{-}(D_{-,\Sigma})
is dense in
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
.

Proof.

Since
D_{+,\Sigma}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle
and the operator
B_{+}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\to H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
is bounded and invertible, the image
B_{+}\big(D_{+,\Sigma}\big)
is dense in
B_{+}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\big)=H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. Similar for
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

For
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
open,
\varphi\in D_{+,\Sigma}
, and
\phi\in D_{-,\Sigma}
, the functions
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
(resp.
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)
)is the unique function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
(resp.
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
)such that the fields

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=B_{+}\varphi+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=B_{+}\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)+B_{-}\phi

(2.27)

are tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

This is shown in the calculation after (
2.23
) and in the last sentence of that proof.
∎

The mappings stated in Corollary

are the desired mappings between the Hardy components of a locally divergence-free vector field. Moreover, for a given Hardy field

we only need to check whether or not

. Yet, the operators

and

are unbounded rendering the reconstruction of one Hardy component from the other unstable.
In particular, the operators are difficult to evaluate numerically. Therefore, in the following section, we will introduce two variational approaches that are better suited for numerical applications.

3
Bounded extremal problems for Hardy components

In the previous section we showed that the map between Hardy components is unbounded and thus not continuous.
The question is then, under what conditions and in what sense can we compute
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
(resp.
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)
) taking into account that the input data
\varphi
(resp.
\phi
) are typically corrupted by noise.
In this section, we address this question by analyzing some related bounded extremal problems. We focus on the operator
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
only, but an analogous analysis can be applied to
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
without significant difference (noting that the operator
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
is the one most important, e.g., for applications to planetary magnetic fields, since measurements are typically only available outside the planets).
Opposed to many other inverse problems, our case is special as both operators
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and its inverse are unbounded.

3.1
Properties of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
,
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
, and the first bounded extremal problem

Given the input
\varphi
, numerical calculations of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
can be challenging. Then, it is sometimes convenient to use an
approximation sequence
of functions
\varphi_{n}
that converge to
\varphi
and for which the functions
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{n})
enjoy desirable numerical properties.
However, since
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
is unbounded, convergence of
\varphi_{n}
to
\varphi
does not guarantee that the sequence
(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{n}))
approaches
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
, nor that it converges at all. To avoid this issue, we consider the following bounded extremal problem that stems form the theory of constrained approximations in Hilbert spaces.

Bounded Extremal Problem 1.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and
c>0
be fixed. Find a
\varphi
in
D_{+,\Sigma}
with
\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)\|\leq c
such that

\displaystyle\|\varphi-f\|=\inf\left\{\|g-f\|:\,g\in D_{+,\Sigma},\,\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(g)\|\leq c\right\}.

(
BEP_{1}
)

In order to address the problem above, in particular the existence of its solution, we first require some additional properties of the operators
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
be open and denote the identity operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
by
I
. Then
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
is the unbounded two-sided inverse of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
in the following sense

i )

we have
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}=I|_{D_{+,\Sigma}}
and
D_{+,\Sigma}=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\big(D_{-,\Sigma}\big)
;

ii )

we have
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}=I|_{D_{-,\Sigma}}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\big(D_{+,\Sigma}\big)
.

Proof.

For
i )
: if
\varphi
is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
, then
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
is in
D_{-,\Sigma}
by Lemma
2.5
. Thus,
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
is well-defined on
D_{+,\Sigma}
. Consider the mapping sequence
\varphi\mapsto\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)\mapsto\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
, and set for brevity
\phi=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
,
\tilde{\varphi}=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
. Then, by Corollary
2.8
,
B_{+}\varphi
and
B_{+}\tilde{\varphi}
are unique fields such that both
B_{+}\varphi+B_{-}\phi
and
B_{+}\tilde{\varphi}+B_{-}\phi
are locally divergence-free.
From the uniqueness assertion, it follows that
B_{+}(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})=0
. Since
B_{+}
is injective on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
and since functions in
D_{+,\Sigma}
have zero mean, this yields
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi
and
\varphi=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(\phi)=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi)
. This shows that if
\varphi
is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
then
\varphi=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\phi)
for some
\phi\in D_{-,\Sigma}
and thus,
D_{+,\Sigma}\subseteq\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\big(D_{-,\Sigma}\big)
. Lemma
2.5
provides the opposite inclusion. This implies
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}=I|_{D_{-,\Sigma}}
.
The proof for the corresponding statement in
ii )
follows the same reasoning.
∎

We will sometimes write
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{-1}=\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
, to refer to the statement of the Proposition
3.1
. However, one should keep in mind that both operators are unbounded and their product is defined only on subdomains of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.

In the following theorem we establish that the operators
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
are closed. This property is paramount for what follows.

Theorem 3.2
.

The graph of the operator
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
(resp.
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
) is weakly closed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle\times L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
(resp.
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
). In particular,
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
are closed operators.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
be open. It is known that the space of tangent and divergence-free vector fields on
\Sigma
is weakly closed.
Now, let
(\varphi_{n},\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in the graph of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
that converges weakly to
(f,h)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle\times L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. Then in particular, the sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
D_{+,\Sigma}
converges weakly to
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, and the sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. From Proposition
3.1
we know that
\phi_{n}=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{n})
is in
D_{-,\Sigma}
for each
n
; and by Corollary
2.8
we know that for each
n
the field
B_{+}\varphi_{n}+B_{-}\phi_{n}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
. Moreover, since it holds for every
\mathbf{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
that

\displaystyle|\big\langle B_{+}\varphi_{n}+B_{-}\phi_{n},\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|\leq|\big\langle\varphi_{n},B_{+}^{\ast}\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|+|\big\langle\phi_{n},B_{-}^{\ast}\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|,

(3.1)

the sequence
\big(B_{+}\varphi_{n}+B_{-}\phi_{n}\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to some
\mathbf{f}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Due to the weak closure of the space of tangent and divergence-free vector fields,
\mathbf{f}
is tangent and divergence-free on
\Sigma
. From Theorem
2.6
and Corollary
2.8
, there is a unique
\varphi_{0}\in D_{+,\Sigma}
such that the Hardy components of
\mathbf{f}
are
B_{+}\varphi_{0}
and
B_{-}\big(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{0})\big)
.
It follows that for every
\mathbf{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle|\big\langle B_{+}\big(\varphi_{n}-\varphi_{0}\big)+B_{-}\big(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{n})-\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{0})\big),\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|\longrightarrow\ 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(3.2)

In particular, using the fact that the Hardy spaces are orthogonal, we see

\displaystyle|\big\langle B_{+}\big(\varphi_{n}-\varphi_{0}\big),\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|=|\big\langle\varphi_{n}-\varphi_{0},B_{+}^{\ast}\mathbf{g}\big\rangle|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(3.3)

for every
\mathbf{g}\in H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
.
Since
B_{+}
is invertible, so is
B_{+}^{\ast}
. Especially,
B_{+}^{\ast}
is onto
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, and thus,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
\varphi_{0}
. By the uniqueness of limit points, we have
\varphi_{0}=f
.
Choosing
\mathbf{g}
in (

) to be in
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
and using the same arguments, we see that
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{n})
converges weakly to
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\varphi_{0})=h
. Consequently, the graph of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
is weakly closed. Moreover, weakly closure and norm closure are equivalent for convex set in Hilbert space(e.g.,
[
20

). Hence,
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
is closed, and thus so is
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma\Subset\mathbb{S}
be open. A function

is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
if and only if there exists a sequence of functions

\left(\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{n})\|\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded; in that case,
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{n})
converges to
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)
in the weak sense.

Proof.

For the “only if” statement it is enough to choose the sequences of functions

, which satisfies the desired properties.

For the “if” statement, let

be a sequence in

that converges to

and assume that

), which implies that

Let

is weakly closed by Theorem

is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and thus

and
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{n})

\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)
.
∎

which gives

Let

A.1
Hardy spaces

We briefly recapitulate the classical definition of Hardy spaces as mentioned, e.g., in
[
23
]
. Letting
\textnormal{Harm}(\mathbb{B})=\{u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}):\Delta u=0\textnormal{ in }\mathbb{B}\}
and
\textnormal{Harm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})=\{u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}):\Delta u=0\textnormal{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}},\,\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|u(x)|=0\}
be the spaces of harmonic functions on the corresponding domains, we set

\displaystyle H_{+}(\mathbb{B})

\displaystyle=\left\{\nabla u:u\in\textnormal{Harm}(\mathbb{B}),\,\sup_{r\in[0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}|\nabla u(y)|^{2}d\omega(y)<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle H_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})

\displaystyle=\left\{\nabla v:v\in\textnormal{Harm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}),\,\sup_{r\in(1,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}|\nabla v(y)|^{2}d\omega(y)<\infty\right\}.

The nontangential limits to the sphere of any vector fields in
H_{+}(\mathbb{B})
or
H_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})
are known to lie in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The space of boundary fields obtained by taking the non-tangential limits define the Hardy spaces on the sphere, such that

\displaystyle H_{+}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\ \mathbf{f}(y)=\operatorname*{nt-lim}_{\mathbb{B}\ni x\to y}\nabla u(x)\text{ at almost every $y\in\mathbb{S}$},\nabla u\in H_{+}(\mathbb{B})\},

\displaystyle H_{-}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\ \mathbf{g}(y)=\operatorname*{nt-lim}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}\ni x\to y}\nabla u(x)\text{ at almost every $y\in\mathbb{S}$},\nabla u\in H_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}})\},

where
\operatorname*{nt-lim}
denotes the non-tangential limits to the sphere.
It has been shown in
[
5
]
that the definition of Hardy spaces that we use in this paper (via the operators
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
from (
1.5
)) are equivalent to the standard definition of Hardy spaces as presented above.

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}h,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\rangle_{\mathbb{S}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}}=\big(-\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{f},\trace(\varphi)\big)_{\partial\Sigma}\qquad\big(\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}\setminus\overline{\Sigma})/\langle 1\rangle\big),

i.e.

A.3
Proof of Lemma
2.3

Proof.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
be open with
\mathbb{S}\setminus\Sigma
having positive Lebesgue measure. First, we observe that we can phrase
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
as the orthogonal complement of the set

\displaystyle G=\big\{(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\colon\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)\big\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{S}).

(A.4)

This is because for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\mathbb{S}}(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf)(\varphi)=\big\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\big\rangle=\big\langle f,(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\varphi\big\rangle\qquad\big(\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)\big).

(A.5)

Thus,
f
is in
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
(left-hand side of (
A.5
) vanishes) if and only if
f
is orthogonal to
G
(right-hand side of (
A.5
) vanishes). In particular,
G
is orthogonal to constant functions and thus, it is a subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
.
The orthogonal complement of
\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}
is then the
L^{2}
-closure of
G
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{V}_{\Sigma}^{\perp}=(G^{\perp})^{\perp}=\overline{G}.

(A.6)

Moreover, inserting the identity
SS^{-1}=I\colon W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})\to W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
in (
A.4
) and using (
1.7
) we can rewrite
G
as

\displaystyle G=\big\{(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{\ast}(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)S^{-1}\varphi\colon\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)\big\}=\big\{\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}\varphi\colon\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)\big\}.

(A.7)

To prove the assertion of the lemma, it remains to show that the closure of
G
is given by

\displaystyle H=\big\{\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}\varphi\colon\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)\big\}.

(A.8)

For this assume a Cauchy sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
G
that converges to
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
; for each
f_{n}
let
\varphi_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)
be the corresponding smooth function as in (
A.7
) with the mean
c_{n}=\big\langle\varphi_{n},1\big\rangle
.
Since
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy and the operators
K\pm\tfrac{1}{2}I
are both isomorphisms on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
, the sequence of functions
S^{-1}\varphi_{n}
is Cauchy in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.
Correspondingly, the sequence of functions
S^{-1}\varphi_{n}-S^{-1}c_{n}
converges in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\penalty\big\langle 1\big\rangle
(since
S^{-1}c_{n}
is the mean of
S^{-1}\varphi_{n}
due to the fact that
S
preserves constant functions).
Because
S\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
is bounded, it further follows that the sequence
(\varphi_{n}-c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must be Cauchy in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
.
In particular, it converges in the
L^{2}
-sense on every subdomain of
\mathbb{S}
that has positive measure — especially on
\mathbb{S}\setminus\Sigma
, where it is a sequence of constant functions
c_{n}
(since each
\varphi_{n}
is compactly supported on
\Sigma
). Clearly,
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must then also converge on the entire sphere in the
L^{2}
-sense and in the
W^{1,2}
-sense. It follows that both sequences,
(\varphi_{n}-c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, are individually Cauchy in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
. Correspondingly, the sequence of functions
\varphi_{n}=(\varphi_{n}-c_{n})+c_{n}
converges in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S})
and its limit is a function
\varphi_{0}
in
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)
(since by definition
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Sigma)
is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma)
in the Sobolev norm).
Moreover, we have

\|\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}\varphi_{0}-f_{n}\|\to 0
for
n\to\infty
, and thus,

\displaystyle f=\big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)\big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)S^{-1}\varphi_{0},

(A.9)

implying that
\overline{G}\subset H
. The inverse inclusion is trivial, since all involved operators are isomorphic on the corresponding spaces. Thus,
\overline{G}=H
which concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Properties of
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{*}
,
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}^{*}
, and a quantification of weak convergence

From the standard theory of operators we know that
the adjoint operators of densely defined operator are always closed (e.g.,
[

).
Since the operators

and

\displaystyle\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}^{\ast}=(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{-1})^{\ast}=(\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{\ast})^{-1}

\displaystyle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{\ast}=(\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}^{-1})^{\ast}=(\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}^{\ast})^{-1}.

We can now formulate the following bounded extremal problem.
