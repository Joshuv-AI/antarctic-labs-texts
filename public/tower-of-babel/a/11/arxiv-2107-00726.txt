1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, let
X
be a nonempty set and let
Y
be a fixed nonempty subset of
X
. Denote by
T(X)
the full transformation semigroup on
X
. It is well-known that
T(X)
is regular
[
8
, p. 63, Exercise 15]
, and
T(X)
is unit-regular if and only if
X
is finite
[
3
, Proposition 5]
. A characterization of Green’s relations on
T(X)
is well-known
[
8
, p. 63, Exercise 16]
. In
1952
, Mal’cev
[
13
]
determined ideals of
T(X)
. A
transformation semigroup
is a subsemigroup of
T(X)
. Various transformation semigroups have been introduced and extensively studied for several decades, as every semigroup is isomorphic to a transformation semigroup
[
8
, Theorem 1.1.2]
.

In
1966
, Magill
[
12
]
introduced and studied an interesting transformation semigroup
\overline{T}(X,Y)
defined by

\overline{T}(X,Y)=\{f\in T(X)\colon Yf\subseteq Y\},

where
Yf
is the image of
Y
under
f
. In
2005
, Nenthein et al. proved that
\overline{T}(X,Y)
is regular if and only if
X=Y
or
|Y|=1

[
14
, Corollary 2.4]
, and they characterized regular elements in
\overline{T}(X,Y)

[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
. Honyam and Sanwong
[
6
]
determined Green’s relations on
\overline{T}(X,Y)
and characterized its ideals. Sarkar and Singh gave a characterization of unit-regular elements in
\overline{T}(X,Y)

[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
and also proved that
\overline{T}(X,Y)
is unit-regular if and only if
X
is finite, and
|Y|=1
or
Y=X

[
16
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Denote by
S(X)
(resp.
I_{X}
) the symmetric group (resp. identity map) on
X
. Honyam and Sanwong
[
7
]
and Laysirikul
[
11
]
introduced, respectively, the transformation semigroups
\text{Fix}(X,Y)
and
\overline{S}(X,Y)
defined by

\text{Fix}(X,Y)=\{f\in T(X)\colon f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=I_{Y}\}\hskip 8.5359pt\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359pt\overline{S}(X,Y)=\{f\in T(X)\colon f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)\},

where
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\colon Y\to Y
is the map that agrees with
f
on
Y
.
Honyam and Sanwong
[
7
]
observed that
\text{Fix}(X,Y)
is regular and characterized its Green’s relations and ideals. Chaiya et al.
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
proved that
\mbox{ Fix}(X,Y)
is unit-regular if and only if
X\setminus Y
is finite. Laysirikul
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
showed that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is regular. Sommanee
[
17
]
gave a description of Green’s relations on
\overline{S}(X,Y)
and characterize its ideals.

Denote by
\Omega(X)
the semigroup under composition of all surjective transformations on
X
. In
[
9
]
, Konieczny characterized Green’s relations on
\Omega(X)
and investigated its kernel. In particular, Konieczny
[
9
]
proved that the kernel of
\Omega(X)
exists and determined its elements and cardinality.

Let

\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\{f\in T(X)\colon Yf=Y\}.

Clearly
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is a subsemigroup of
\overline{T}(X,Y)
. If
Y=X
, then
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\Omega(X)
. Therefore we may regard
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
as a generalization of
\Omega(X)
. Moreover, if
Y
is finite, then
\overline{S}(X,Y)=\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
(cf.
[
4
, Proposition 1.1.3]
). By definition of
\overline{T}(X,Y)
,
\overline{S}(X,Y)
,
\text{Fix}(X,Y)
, and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, we can see that

\text{Fix}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{S}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{T}(X,Y).

The main purpose of this paper is to study the semigroup
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. We divide the paper into five sections.
In Section
2
, we present basic definitions and introduce remaining notation used within the paper. In Section
3
, we first describe regular elements in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
and then show that
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is regular if and only if
Y
is finite. Next, we characterize unit-regular elements in both semigroups
\overline{S}(X,Y)
and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, and show that the sets of all unit-regular elements in
\overline{S}(X,Y)
and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
are equal.
Also, we prove that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
(resp.
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
) is unit-regular if and only if
X\setminus Y
(resp.
X
) is finite. In Section
4
, we characterize Green’s relations on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
and prove that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
Y
is finite.
In Section
5
, we characterize ideals of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
and determine its kernel.

2.
Preliminaries and Notation

Let
A
be a set. We denote by
|A|
the cardinality of
A
and write
A\setminus B=\{x\in A\colon x\notin B\}
, where
B
is a set. A
partition
of
A
is a collection of pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets of
A
, called
blocks
, whose union is
A
. A
transversal
of an equivalence relation
\rho
on
A
is a subset of
A
which contains exactly one element of each
\rho
-class. We denote by
I_{A}
the identity map on
A
.

We denote the composition of maps by juxtaposition, and we compose maps from left to right. Let
f
be a map. We write
\dom(f)
(resp.
\codom(f)
) to denote the domain (resp. codomain) of
f
. For
x\in\dom(f)
, we write
xf
for the image of
x
under
f
. If
B\subseteq\dom(f)
and
C\subseteq\codom(f)
, we let
Bf=\{xf\colon x\in B\}
and
Cf^{-1}=\{x\in\dom(f)\colon xf\in C\}
. We simply write
xf^{-1}
instead of
Cf^{-1}
when
C=\{x\}\subseteq\codom(f)
. Let
f
be a map and
\dom(f)=A
. We denote by
\ker(f)
the equivalence relation on
A
defined by
(a,b)\in\ker(f)
if and only if
af=bf
. We write
T_{f}
to denote a transversal of
\ker(f)
. Note that
|T_{f}|=|Af|
. Let
f\colon A\to A
be a map and
B\subseteq\dom(f)
. The
restriction
of
f
to
B
is a map
f_{|_{B}}\colon B\to A
defined by
x(f_{|_{B}})=xf
for all
x\in B
. If
Bf\subseteq B
, we denote by
f_{\upharpoonright_{B}}
the map from
B
to
B
which agrees with
f
on
B
. Note that if
f\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
(resp.
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
), then
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
(resp.
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
).

Let
S
be a semigroup with identity. Denote by
U(S)
the set of all units in
S
.
An element
x\in S
is called
regular
(resp.
unit-regular
) if
xyx=x
for some
y\in S
(resp.
y\in U(S)
). Let
\reg(S)
(resp.
\ureg(S)
) denote the set of all regular (resp. unit-regular) elements in
S
. The semigroup
S
is said to be
regular
(resp.
unit-regular
) if
\reg(S)=S
(resp.
\ureg(S)=S
).
Note that
U(T(X))=S(X)
, and if
f\in U(\overline{T}(X,Y))
, then
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
.

Let
S
be a semigroup. As usual, we denote Green’s relations on
S
by
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{D}
, and
\mathcal{J}
. If
a\in S
and
\mathcal{T}
is one of Green’s relations on
S
, then we denote by
T_{a}
the equivalence class of
a
with respect to
\mathcal{T}
. An ideal
I
of
S
is said to be
minimal
if there is no ideal of
S
that is strictly contained in
I
. Note that a semigroup contains at most one minimal ideal. However, if a semigroup
S
contains a minimal ideal, then such unique minimal ideal of
S
is called the
kernel
of
S
and is denoted by
K(S)
.

For all undefined notions and notation, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
8
]
.

3.
Regular and Unit-regular elements

In this section, we first describe regular elements in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
and show that
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is regular if and only if
Y
is finite. Next, we characterize unit-regular elements in both semigroups
\overline{S}(X,Y)
and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, and prove that the sets of all unit-regular elements in
\overline{S}(X,Y)
and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
are equal. We prove that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
(resp.
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
) is unit-regular if and only if
X\setminus Y
(resp.
X
) is finite. Finally, we give new proofs of known results about regularity and unit-regularity of
\mbox{ Fix}(X,Y)
.

The following simple lemma is included here for completeness.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to X
be a map and let
T_{f}
be a transversal of
\ker(f)
. Then

(i)

f
is injective if and only if
X\setminus T_{f}=\emptyset
;

(ii)

f
is surjective if and only if
X\setminus Xf=\emptyset
;

(iii)

f
is bijective if and only if
X\setminus T_{f}=\emptyset
and
X\setminus Xf=\emptyset
;

(iv)

if
f
is injective but not surjective, then
|X\setminus T_{f}|\neq|X\setminus Xf|
.

In general, the subset
\reg(S)
of a semigroup
S
does not form a subsemigroup of
S
.
However, in the next result, we show that
\reg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
is a subsemigroup of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have
\reg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))=\overline{S}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Note that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is a regular semigroup
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
. Since
\overline{S}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, it follows that
\overline{S}(X,Y)=\reg(\overline{S}(X,Y))\subseteq\reg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
. To show the reverse inclusion, let
f\in\reg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
. Then there exists
g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
such that
fgf=f
. This gives
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
and so
Y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
is a transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
by
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
, it is clear that
Y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=Y
. Since
Y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
is a transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
and
Y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=Y
, it follows that

Y\setminus Y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=\emptyset
and so
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
is injective by Lemma
3.1
(i). Hence
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
and subsequently
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
as required.
∎

Note that
\overline{S}(X,Y)=\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
Y
is finite (cf.
[
4
, Proposition 1.1.3]
). Using
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
together with Theorem
3.2
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

The semigroup
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is regular if and only if
Y
is finite.

In the next result, we characterize unit-regular elements in
\overline{S}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Then
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
if and only if
|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|
for some transversal
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
such that
Y\subseteq T_{f}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
. Since
\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))\subseteq\ureg(\overline{T}(X,Y))
, we have
f\in\ureg(\overline{T}(X,Y))
. Then, by
[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
, there exists a transversal
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
such that
Y\cap T_{f}
is a transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
and

|(X\setminus T_{f})\setminus(Y\setminus T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}})|=|(X\setminus Xf)\setminus(Y\setminus Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})|

(1)

where
T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}}=Y\cap T_{f}
is a transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, the set
Y
is the only transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. This implies that
Y=Y\cap T_{f}
and so
Y\subseteq T_{f}
. Further, since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
(iii) that

Y\setminus T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}}=\emptyset
and
Y\setminus Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=\emptyset
. Then, by using equation (
1
), we obtain

|X\setminus T_{f}|=|(X\setminus T_{f})\setminus(Y\setminus T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}})|=|(X\setminus Xf)\setminus(Y\setminus Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})|=|X\setminus Xf|.

Conversely, suppose that
|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|
for some transversal
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
such that
Y\subseteq T_{f}
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, the set
Y
is the only transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Write
T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}}=Y
. By hypothesis, since
Y\subseteq T_{f}
, we then get
T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}}=Y\cap T_{f}
. Further, since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
(iii) that
Y\setminus T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}}=\emptyset
and
Y\setminus Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=\emptyset
. Therefore, by hypothesis, we obtain

|(X\setminus T_{f})\setminus(Y\setminus T_{f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}})|=|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|=|(X\setminus Xf)\setminus(Y\setminus Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})|.

In addition, since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, we simply have
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\ureg(T(Y))
and
Yf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=Y\cap Xf
.
Therefore, by
[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
, we get
f\in\ureg(\overline{T}(X,Y))
.
Since
U(\overline{T}(X,Y))\subseteq\overline{S}(X,Y)
, we conclude that
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
.
∎

In the next result, we show that
\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
and
\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
are equal.

Theorem 3.5
.

We have
\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))=\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{S}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, it follows that
\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))\subseteq\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
. To show the reverse inclusion, let
f\in\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
. Then
f\in\reg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
and so
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
by Theorem
3.2
. Since
f\in\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
, there exists
g\in U(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
such that
fgf=f
. Note that
U(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))\subseteq U(\overline{S}(X,Y))
. Therefore
g\in U(\overline{S}(X,Y))
and so
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
as required.
∎

Using Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
f\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
f\in\ureg(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))
if and only if
|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|
for some transversal
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
such that
Y\subseteq T_{f}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
X\setminus Y
is an infinite set, then there exists
f\in\text{Fix}(X,Y)
such that
f\notin\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
.

Proof.

If
X\setminus Y
is an infinite set, then there exists a map
\alpha\colon X\setminus Y\to X\setminus Y
which is injective but not surjective. Define
f\colon X\to X
by

\displaystyle xf=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if $x\in Y$},\\
x\alpha&\text{ if $x\in X\setminus Y$.}\end{cases}

It is obvious that
f\in\text{Fix}(X,Y)
. Since
\alpha
is injective, it follows that
f
is injective and so
X
is the only transversal of
\ker(f)
. Write
T_{f}=X
. Then
Y\subseteq T_{f}
and
|X\setminus T_{f}|=0
. In addition, since
\alpha
is not surjective, the map
f
is not surjective and so
|X\setminus Xf|\geq 1
. This implies that
|X\setminus T_{f}|\neq|X\setminus Xf|
where
T_{f}
is the only transversal of
\ker(f)
and
Y\subseteq T_{f}
. Hence
f\notin\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
by Theorem
3.4
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Our next result characterizes unit-regularity of
\overline{S}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 3.8
.

The semigroup
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is unit-regular if and only if
X\setminus Y
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is unit-regular. On the contrary, assume that
X\setminus Y
is infinite. Then, by Lemma
3.7
, there exists
f\in\text{Fix}(X,Y)
such that
f\notin\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
. However, since
\text{Fix}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{S}(X,Y)
, we have
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
and so
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
by hypothesis. This is a contradiction of the fact that
f\notin\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
. Hence
X\setminus Y
is finite.

Conversely, suppose that
X\setminus Y
is finite and let
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Clearly
Y\subseteq Xf
and therefore
X\setminus Xf,Xf\setminus Y\subseteq X\setminus Y
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
, it is clear that
Y
is the only transversal of
\ker(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
and there exists a transversal
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
such that
Y\subseteq T_{f}
. Then
X\setminus T_{f},T_{f}\setminus Y\subseteq X\setminus Y
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\colon Y\to Y
and
f_{|_{T_{f}}}\colon T_{f}\to Xf
are bijections, it follows that
|T_{f}\setminus Y|=|Xf\setminus Y|
. Further, since
X\setminus Y
is finite, the sets
X\setminus Xf
,
Xf\setminus Y
,
X\setminus T_{f}
, and
T_{f}\setminus Y
are finite. Therefore

|(X\setminus Y)\setminus(T_{f}\setminus Y)|=|X\setminus Y|-|T_{f}\setminus Y|=|X\setminus Y|-|Xf\setminus Y|=|(X\setminus Y)\setminus(Xf\setminus Y)|

and so

|X\setminus T_{f}|=|(X\setminus Y)\setminus(T_{f}\setminus Y)|=|(X\setminus Y)\setminus(Xf\setminus Y)|=|X\setminus Xf|
.
This implies that
f\in\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
by Theorem
3.4
. Since
f
is arbitrary, we conclude that
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is unit-regular. ∎

Using Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.8
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 3.9
.

The semigroup
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is unit-regular if and only if
X
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is unit-regular. Then
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is regular and so
Y
is finite by Proposition
3.3
. Therefore
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Since

\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is unit-regular and
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\overline{S}(X,Y)
, it follows that
X\setminus Y
is finite by Theorem
3.8
. Moreover, since
Y
is finite, we conclude that
X
is finite.

Conversely, suppose that
X
is finite. Then
X\setminus Y
is finite and so
\overline{S}(X,Y)
is unit-regular by Theorem
3.8
. Again, by hypothesis, the set
Y
is finite and so
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Hence
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is unit-regular.
∎

Now, we recall the notion of pre-inverses introduced by Hickey
[
5
, p. 372]
in 1983.

Definition 3.10
.

[
5
, p. 372]

Let
S
be a semigroup and
a\in S
. An element
b\in S
is said to be a
pre-inverse
of
a
if
aba=a
.

We denote by
\text{Pre}(a)
the set of all pre-inverses of
a
of a semigroup
S
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
f\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
.

(i)

If
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
, then
\text{Pre}(f)\subseteq\overline{S}(X,Y)
.

(ii)

If
f\in\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
, then
\text{Pre}(f)\subseteq\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
g\in\text{Pre}(f)
. This means
fgf=f
. It follows that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Since
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
, we have
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in S(Y)
. Therefore
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=I_{Y}
and
g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=I_{Y}
. Hence
g\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
as required.

(ii)

Let
g\in\text{Pre}(f)
. This means
fgf=f
. It follows that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Since
f\in\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
, we have
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=I_{Y}
. Therefore
g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=I_{Y}
and hence
g\in\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
as required.

∎

Next, we give a new proof of the next result which was first appeared in

Proposition 3.12
.

The semigroup
Fix
(X,Y)
is regular.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
. Then
f\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
. It follows from
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
that
fgf=f
for some
g\in\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Then, by Lemma
3.11
, we have
g\in\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
and so
f\in\reg(\mbox{Fix}(X,Y))
. Hence
\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
is regular.
∎

The following result was first proved by Chaiya

in

Proposition 3.13
.

The semigroup
\text{Fix}(X,Y)
is unit-regular if and only if
X\setminus Y
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)
is unit-regular. On the contrary, assume that
X\setminus Y
is infinite. Then, by Lemma
3.7
, there exists
f\in\text{Fix}(X,Y)
such that
f\notin\ureg(\overline{S}(X,Y))
. Since
\mbox{Fix}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{S}(X,Y)
, it follows that
f\notin\ureg(\mbox{Fix}(X,Y))
. However, since
f\in\text{Fix}(X,Y)
, we get

by hypothesis. This is a contradiction. Hence

is finite.

Conversely, suppose that

is finite and let

is finite, we get

for some

by Theorem

as required.
∎

4.
Green’s Relations on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)

In this section, we give a characterization of Green’s relations on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
and prove that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
Y
is finite. We refer the reader to
[
8
, Chapter 2]
for definitions and notation of Green’s relations.

In order to characterize
\mathcal{L}
-relation on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
f=hg
for some
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)

if and only if

(i)

Xf\subseteq Xg
;

(ii)

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f=hg
for some
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

(i)

Since
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{T}(X,Y)
, we have
f,g,h\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
and so
Xf\subseteq Xg
by
[
6
, Lemma 2]
.

(ii)

Note that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
and
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Then, by
[
9
, Theorem 2.1(1)]
, we get
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
.

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. First, we recall (ii). Then, by
[
9
, Theorem 2.1(1)]
, there exists
\gamma\in\Omega(Y)
such that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=\gamma g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Next, by (i), there exists
x^{\prime}\in X
such that
xf=x^{\prime}g
for all
x\in X\setminus Y
. Therefore, for each
x\in X\setminus Y
, we may choose such an element
x^{\prime}\in X
. Now, we define
h\colon X\to X
by

\displaystyle xh=\begin{cases}x\gamma&\mbox{ if }x\in Y,\\
x^{\prime}&\mbox{ if }x\in X\setminus Y.\end{cases}

It is clear that
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Finally, we show that
f=hg
. Let
x\in X
. Consider two possible cases.

Case 1:

x\in Y
. Then

x(hg)=(xh)g=(x\gamma)g=(x\gamma)g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=(x(\gamma g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=xf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=xf
.

Case 2:

x\in X\setminus Y
. Then
x(hg)=(xh)g=x^{\prime}g=xf
.

Hence, from both cases, we conclude that
f=hg
as required.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
(f,g)\in\mathcal{L}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
Xf=Xg
and
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}
be partitions of a set

, and let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be subcollections of blocks in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}

We say that
\mathcal{A}

refines

\mathcal{B}
, denoted by
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
, if for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
A\subseteq B
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\preceq\mathcal{A}
, then we write
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
.

For
f\in T(X)
and
A\subseteq\codom(f)
with
Xf\cap A\neq\emptyset
, we let

\pi_{f}(A)=\{xf^{-1}\colon x\in Xf\cap A\}.

We will write
\pi(f)
instead of
\pi_{f}(X)
. Note that
\pi(f)
is a partition of
X
induced by
f
.

To characterize
\mathcal{R}
-relation on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
f=gh
for some
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if

(i)

\pi(g)\preceq\pi(f)
;

(ii)

\pi_{g}(Y)\preceq\pi_{f}(Y)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f=gh
for some
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Clearly
f,g,h\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
. Then, by
[
6
, Lemma 3]
, the statements (i) and (ii) hold.

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. Clearly
f,g\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
. Then, by
[
6
, Lemma 3]
, there exists
h\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
such that
f=gh
. Now, we show that
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Note that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
and
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}

and so

(cf.
[
10

with

as required.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
(f,g)\in\mathcal{R}

in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
\pi(f)=\pi(g)

\pi_{f}(Y)=\pi_{g}(Y)

It follows immediately from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

.

(i)

(ii)

(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\in\mathcal{R}

(iii)

(i)

(ii)

It is a dual of the proof of (i) and so we omit the details.

(iii)

Since

∎

In the next result, we characterize

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

(f,g)\in\mathcal{D}

(i)

|Xf\setminus Y|=|Xg\setminus Y|

(ii)

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|(y\alpha)(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

Suppose that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{D}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

(i)

Since
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{T}(X,Y)
, it follows that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{D}
in
\overline{T}(X,Y)
and so
|Xf\setminus Y|=|Xg\setminus Y|
by
[
6

(ii)

Then, by Lemma

(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\in\mathcal{D}
in
\Omega(Y)
. Hence, by
[

\alpha\in S(Y)
such that
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|(y\alpha)(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. By (i), there exists a bijection
\beta\colon Xg\setminus Y\to Xf\setminus Y
. Now, we recall (ii). Define

by

\displaystyle xh=\begin{cases}a_{i}\alpha^{-1}&\text{if $x\in X_{i}$ where $X_{i}\in\pi_{g}(Y)$ and $X_{i}g=a_{i}$},\\

It is clear that
h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. First, we observe that

and
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|y(h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all

.
Therefore
(f,h)\in\mathcal{L}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
by Theorem

.
Next, we see that
\pi(h)=\pi(g)
and
\pi_{h}(Y)=\pi_{g}(Y)
. Therefore
(h,g)\in\mathcal{R}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
by Theorem
4.5
. Since
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}
, we conclude that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{D}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.
∎

In order to characterize
\mathcal{J}
-relation on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
f=hgh^{\prime}
for some
h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if

(i)

|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|
;

(ii)

there exists a partition
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
such that

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\displaystyle\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

for all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f=hgh^{\prime}
for some
h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

(i)

Since
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{T}(X,Y)
, we have
f,g,h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
and so
|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|
by
[
6
, Theorem 5]
.

(ii)

Note that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
and
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Hence, by
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
, there exists a partition
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
such that
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
.

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. Since
Yf=Y
and
Yg=Y
, we have
|Yf|=|Yg|
. Now, we recall (i). Then we get
|(Xf\setminus Y)\cup Y|\leq|(Xg\setminus Y)\cup Y|
which simply gives
|Xf|\leq|Xg|
. Thus, by
[
6
, Theorem 5]
, there exist
\alpha,\gamma\in\overline{T}(X,Y)
such that
f=\alpha g\gamma
.

Now, we recall (ii). Then,
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
, there exist
\beta,\delta\in\Omega(Y)
such that
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\delta
. Let
x\in X\setminus Y
such that
xf\in Y
. Write
xf=y
. Since
Yf=Y
, it follows that
Y\cap yf^{-1}\neq\emptyset
. Therefore we may choose an element
x^{\prime}\in Y\cap yf^{-1}
. Define
h,h^{\prime}\colon X\to X
by

\displaystyle xh=\begin{cases}x\beta&\text{if $x\in Y$},\\
x^{\prime}\beta&\text{if $x\in X\setminus Y$ and $xf\in Y$},\\
x\alpha&\text{if $x\in X\setminus Y$ and $xf\in X\setminus Y$}.\end{cases}\;\hskip 28.45274ptxh^{\prime}=\begin{cases}x\delta&\text{if $x\in Y$},\\
x\gamma&\text{if $x\in X\setminus Y$}.\end{cases}

Clearly
h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Finally, we show that
f=hgh^{\prime}
. Let
x\in X
. Consider three possible cases.

Case 1:

x\in Y
. Then
x(hg)=(xh)g=(x\beta)g=(x\beta)g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=x(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Since
x(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\in Y
, we obtain

x(hgh^{\prime})=(x(hg))h^{\prime}=(x(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}))h^{\prime}=(x(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}))\delta=x(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\delta)=xf_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=xf.

Case 2:

x\in X\setminus Y
and
xf\in Y
. Then
x(hg)=(xh)g=(x^{\prime}\beta)g=(x^{\prime}\beta)g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=x^{\prime}(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Since
x^{\prime}(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\in Y
, we obtain

x(hgh^{\prime})=(x(hg))h^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}))h^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}))\delta=x^{\prime}(\beta g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\delta)=x^{\prime}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=x^{\prime}f=xf.

Case 3:

x\in X\setminus Y
and
xf\in X\setminus Y
. Then
x(hg)=(xh)g=(x\alpha)g=x(\alpha g)
. We now claim that
x(\alpha g)\in X\setminus Y
. We assume by contradiction that
x(\alpha g)\in Y
. Then
xf=x(\alpha g\gamma)=(x(\alpha g))\gamma\in Y
which is a contradiction of the assumption
xf\in X\setminus Y
. Hence
x(\alpha g)\in X\setminus Y
. Using these facts, we obtain

x(hgh^{\prime})=(x(hg))h^{\prime}=(x(\alpha g))h^{\prime}=(x(\alpha g))\gamma=x(\alpha g\gamma)=xf.

Hence, from all three cases, we conclude that
f=hgh^{\prime}
as required.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then
(f,g)\in\mathcal{J}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
|Xf\setminus Y|=|Xg\setminus Y|
, and there exist partitions
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
and
\{Q_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of

such that

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\hskip 11.38092pt\text{ and }\hskip 11.38092pt|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in Q_{y}}|w(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

for all

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemma
4.9
.
∎

It is well-known that
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
on every semigroup, and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on

. However, the following example shows that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on

is not always true.

Example 4.11
.

Let
Y=\mathbb{N}
be the set of positive integers, and let
X=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Define
f,g\colon X\to X
by

xf=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x\in\{0,1\}\cup(2\mathbb{N}+1$)},\\
\frac{x}{2}+1&\text{if $x\in 2\mathbb{N}$}.\end{cases}\hskip 11.38092ptxg=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x\in\{1\}\cup(4\mathbb{N}+1$)},\\
2&\text{if $x\in\{0\}\cup(4\mathbb{N}-1$)},\\
\frac{x}{2}+2&\text{if $x\in 2\mathbb{N}$}.\end{cases}

Obviously
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. First, we show that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{J}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Observe that
Xf=Xg=Y
and so
|Xf\setminus Y|=|Xg\setminus Y|
. Now, consider the partition
\{\{y\}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
. Then, for all
y\in Y
, we obtain
|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in\{y\}}|w(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
. Next, consider the partition
\{\{1,2\},\{y\}\colon y\in Y\setminus\{1,2\}\}
of
Y
. Then, for all
y\in Y
, we obtain
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
.
Using these facts, we conclude from Theorem
4.10
that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Second, we assume by contradiction that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{D}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then, by Theorem
4.8
, there exists a bijection
\alpha\in S(Y)
such that
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|(y\alpha)(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
. Now, we consider two possible cases of

.

Case 1:

1\alpha=1
. Then for

, we get
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=1
and

Case 2:

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=1
and

|(y\alpha)(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|1(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=\infty
, a contradiction.

Hence we conclude that
(f,g)\notin\mathcal{D}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

In the next result, we give a necessary and sufficient condition for
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 4.12
.

We have
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
Y
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. On the contrary, assume that
Y
is infinite. Then there exist two disjoint subsets
A,B\subseteq Y
such that
|Y|=|A|=|B|=|Y\setminus(A\cup B)|
. Let
p,q\in Y
be distinct elements. Note that
|Y\setminus(A\cup B)|=|Y\setminus\{p,q\}|
and
|Y\setminus(A\cup B)|=|Y\setminus\{p\}|
. It follows that there exist bijections
\alpha\colon Y\setminus(A\cup B)\to Y\setminus\{p,q\}
and
\beta\colon Y\setminus(A\cup B)\to Y\setminus\{p\}
. Define
f,g\colon X\to X
by

\displaystyle xf=\begin{cases}p&\text{if $x\in A\cup(X\setminus Y)$},\\
q&\text{if $x\in B$},\\
x\alpha&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\;\hskip 8.5359pt\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359ptxg=\begin{cases}p&\text{if $x\in A\cup B\cup(X\setminus Y)$},\\
x\beta&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear that
f,g\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. First, we show that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{J}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Observe that
|Xf\setminus Y|=|Xg\setminus Y|
. Now, consider the partition
\{\{y\}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
. Then, for all
y\in Y
, we obtain
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in\{y\}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
.
Next, consider the partition
\{\{p,q\},\{y\}\colon y\in Y\setminus\{p,q\}\}
of
Y
. Then, for all
y\in Y
, we obtain
|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in Q_{y}}|w(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
. Using these facts, we conclude from Theorem
4.10
that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Second, observe that the number of blocks of infinite cardinalities in the partitions
\pi(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
and
\pi(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
of
Y
are two and one, respectively. Therefore there does not exist any bijection
\alpha\in S(Y)
such that
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|(y\alpha)(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
. It follows from Theorem
4.8
that
(f,g)\notin\mathcal{D}
in
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
which is a contradiction of the assumption
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Hence
Y
is finite.

Conversely, suppose that
Y
is finite. Then
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\overline{S}(X,Y)
. Hence, by
[
17
, Theorem 3.1(4)]
, we have
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)

∎

Because of Example 2.3 of
[
9
]
, we remark that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\Omega(X)
if and only if
X
is finite. Using this, we have the following corollary of Theorem
4.12
.

Corollary 4.13
.

We have
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
if and only if
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
on
\Omega(Y)
.

5.
Ideals of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)

In this section, we present a characterization of ideals of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Moreover, we determine the kernel of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. To prove the main results of this section, we need the next five lemmas.

For an infinite set
A
and a map
\alpha\in\Omega(A)
, let

N(\alpha)=\{a\in A\colon|a\alpha^{-1}|<|A|\}

and
n(\alpha)=|N(\alpha)|
. It is obvious that
0\leq n(\alpha)\leq|A|
for all
\alpha\in\Omega(A)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
A
is an infinite set and
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega(A)
, then
n(\alpha\beta)\leq\min\{n(\alpha),n(\beta)\}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
n(\alpha\beta)\leq n(\beta)
. For this, let
a\in N(\alpha\beta)
. Then
|a(\alpha\beta)^{-1}|<|A|
and so
|a\beta^{-1}|\leq|(a\beta^{-1})\alpha^{-1}|=|a(\alpha\beta)^{-1}|<|A|
. Therefore
a\in N(\beta)
and so
N(\alpha\beta)\subseteq N(\beta)
. Hence
n(\alpha\beta)\leq n(\beta)
.

Next, we show that
n(\alpha\beta)\leq n(\alpha)
. For this, let
a\in N(\alpha\beta)
. Then
|a(\alpha\beta)^{-1}|<|A|
. Let
x\in a\beta^{-1}
. Then
|x\alpha^{-1}|\leq|(a\beta^{-1})\alpha^{-1}|=|a(\alpha\beta)^{-1}|<|A|
. This implies that
x\in N(\alpha)
and so
a=x\beta\in N(\alpha)\beta
. Therefore
N(\alpha\beta)\subseteq N(\alpha)\beta
and so
n(\alpha\beta)\leq|N(\alpha)\beta|
. Since
|N(\alpha)\beta|\leq n(\alpha)
, we subsequently have
n(\alpha\beta)\leq n(\alpha)
. Thus
n(\alpha\beta)\leq\min\{n(\alpha),n(\beta)\}
. ∎

For two cardinal numbers
s
and
t
with
0\leq s\leq|Y|
and
0\leq t\leq|X\setminus Y|
, let

J(s,t)=\{f\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\colon n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq s\mbox{ and }|Xf\setminus Y|\leq t\}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
s
and
t
be cardinal numbers such that
0\leq s\leq|Y|
and
0\leq t\leq|X\setminus Y|
. If
Y
is an infinite subset of
X
, then
J(s,t)
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

First, we show that
J(s,t)\neq\emptyset
. For this, consider a map
f\in T(X)
such that
Xf=Y
and
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|Y|
for all
y\in Y
. Obviously
f\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Also, it is clear that
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=0
and
|Xf\setminus Y|=0
. Therefore
f\in J(s,t)
and so
J(s,t)\neq\emptyset
.

To show that
J(s,t)
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, let
f\in J(s,t)
and
g,h\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then, by definition of
J(s,t)
, we have
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq s
and
|Xf\setminus Y|\leq t
. Note that

(gfh)_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}=g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

n((gfh)_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq s.

Since
Yh=Y
, we have
X(fh)\setminus Y\subseteq(Xf\setminus Y)h
. Also, since
Xg\subseteq X
, we have

X(gfh)\subseteq X(fh)
. Using these facts, we obtain

|X(gfh)\setminus Y|\leq|X(fh)\setminus Y|\leq|(Xf\setminus Y)h|\leq|Xf\setminus Y|\leq t.

Hence, by definition of
J(s,t)
, we have
gfh\in J(s,t)
as required.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
s
and
t
be cardinal numbers such that
0\leq s\leq|Y|
and
0\leq t\leq|X\setminus Y|
. Then

\bigcup_{f\in J(s,t)}J_{f}=J(s,t).

Proof.

Write
\bigcup_{f\in J(s,t)}J_{f}=J
. It is obvious that
J(s,t)\subseteq J
. To show the reverse inclusion, let
g\in J
. Then
g\in J_{f}
for some
f\in J(s,t)
. Therefore, by Theorem
4.10
, we have
|Xg\setminus Y|=|Xf\setminus Y|\leq t
. Also, by Theorem
4.10
, there exist partitions
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
and
\{Q_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
such that, for all
y\in Y
,

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt|y(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{v\in Q_{y}}|v(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|.

(2)

Now, we show that
n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. For this, let
x\in N(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Then
|x(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|<|Y|
. Let
w\in P_{x}
. Then, from the first inequality of equation (
2
), we get

|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|<|Y|
and so
w\in N(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. It follows that
P_{x}\subseteq N(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Since
|P_{x}|\geq 1
, we have
|N(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})|\leq|N(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})|
and subsequently
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
.

Similarly, by using the second inequality of equation (
2
), we can obtain
n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Thus
n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})
. Since
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq s
, it follows that
n(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})\leq s
. Hence, by definition of
J(s,t)
, we have
g\in J(s,t)
and so
J\subseteq J(s,t)
as required. ∎

For a nonempty subset
F
of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, let

\begin{split}J(F)&=\big\{f\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)\colon\text{ there exist }g\in F\text{ and a partition }\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}\text{ of }Y\text{ such that }\\
&\quad\hskip 11.38092pt|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|\text{ and }|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\text{ for all }y\in Y\big\}.\end{split}

It is clear that
F\subseteq J(F)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
F
is a nonempty subset of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, then
J(F)
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Note that
F\subseteq J(F)
. Since
F\neq\emptyset
, we have
J(F)\neq\emptyset
. To show that
J(F)
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, let
f\in J(F)
and
h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. Then, by definition of
J(F)
, there exist
g\in F
and a partition
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
such that
|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|
and for all
y\in Y

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|.

(3)

First, note that
Yh^{\prime}=Y
and
X(hfh^{\prime})\subseteq X(fh^{\prime})
. Therefore we obtain

|X(hfh^{\prime})\setminus Y|\leq|X(fh^{\prime})\setminus Yh^{\prime}|\leq|(Xf\setminus Y)h^{\prime}|\leq|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|.

Second, let
y\in Y
. Since
f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}},h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}\in\Omega(Y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle|y(h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

\displaystyle=|(y(h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}^{\prime})^{-1}(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1})(h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

\displaystyle\geq|y(h_{\upharpoonright_{Y}}^{\prime})^{-1}(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in y(h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}}|x(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x\in y(h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}}\sum_{w\in P_{x}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ by equation }(\ref{ineq-JF})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in Q_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ where }\hskip 5.69046ptQ_{y}=\bigcup_{x\in y(h^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}}P_{x}.

Observe that
\{Q_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
is a partition of
Y
.
Thus, by definition of
J(F)
, we have
hfh^{\prime}\in J(F)
as required.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

If
F
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, then
F=J(F)
.

Proof.

From definition of
J(F)
, we have
F\subseteq J(F)
. To show the reverse inclusion, let
f\in J(F)
. Then, by definition of
J(F)
, there exist
g\in F
and a partition
\{P_{y}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
such that
|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|
and

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{y}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|
for all
y\in Y
. Then, by Lemma
4.9
, there exist
h,h^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
such that
f=hgh^{\prime}
. Since
F
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
, it follows that
f=hgh^{\prime}\in F
and so
J(F)\subseteq F
as required.
∎

Combining Lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
, we obtain a characterization of ideals of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
as follows.

Theorem 5.6
.

An ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
is precisely of the form
J(F)
for some nonempty subset
F
of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

If
Y=X
, then
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\Omega(X)
. The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.6
.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
X
be an infinite set. Then an ideal of
\Omega(X)
is precisely

\begin{split}&\big\{f\in\Omega(X)\colon\text{ there exist }g\in F\text{ and a partition }\{P_{x}\colon x\in X\}\text{ of }X\text{ such that }\\
&\quad\hskip 51.21504pt|xf^{-1}|\geq\sum_{w\in P_{x}}|wg^{-1}|\text{ for all }x\in X\big\}\end{split}

for some nonempty subset
F
of
\Omega(X)
.

In the next result, we determine the kernel of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
Y
be an infinite subset of
X
. Then
K(\overline{\Omega}(X,Y))=J(0,0)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.2
, the subset
J(0,0)
is an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
. To show that
J(0,0)
is minimal, let
I
be an ideal of
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)
that is contained in
J(0,0)
. Let
f\in J(0,0)
. Then, by definition of
J(0,0)
, we have
|Xf\setminus Y|=0
and
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=0
.

Let
g\in I
. Obviously
|Xg\setminus Y|\geq 0
. Since
|Xf\setminus Y|=0
, it is clear that
|Xf\setminus Y|\leq|Xg\setminus Y|
. Let
y\in Y
. Since
n(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})=0
, we have
|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|Y|
and so

|y(f_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|=|Y|\geq\sum_{w\in\{y\}}|w(g_{\upharpoonright_{Y}})^{-1}|

for the partition
\{\{y\}\colon y\in Y\}
of
Y
. It follows from definition of
J(I)
that
f\in J(I)
and so
f\in I
by Lemma
5.5
. Therefore
J(0,0)\subseteq I
and hence
J(0,0)=I
as required.
∎

If
Y=X
, then
\overline{\Omega}(X,Y)=\Omega(X)
. The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.8
, which was first proved by Konieczny
[
9
, Theorem 3.2(1)]
in
2019
.

Corollary 5.9
.

If
X
is an infinite set, then

K(\Omega(X))=\{\alpha\in\Omega(X)\colon n(\alpha)=0\}
.
