Dynamics of wave packets in the functional mechanics
Zharinov V.V

Abstract
In these notes we consider the mathematical aspects of the functional mechanics proposed and developed by I. V. Volovich.



keywords:
functional mechanics, wave packet, Liouville equation,
dispersion, Hamiltonian dynamics, Lagrangian dynamics, divergence free dynamics.

2
Wave packets.

A wave packet is a mapping

R:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad(t,x)\mapsto R(t,x),\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(1)

In the functional mechanics it is assumed that the wave packet
satisfies the Liouville equation

\partial_{t}R+\partial_{x^{\mu}}\big(v^{\mu}\cdot R\big)=D_{t}R+\mathrm{div\,}v\cdot R=0,

(2)

where
v:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given vector field,
and
\mathrm{div}\,v=\partial_{x^{\mu}}v^{\mu}(x)
is its divergence.

The Liouville equation (
2
) describes the conservation law
for the pair
(R,vR)
, hence, the integral

\int R(t,x)d^{m}x=\mathrm{const}
,
and one may assume that it is equal to 1.
Indeed,

\frac{d}{dt}\int R(t,x)d^{m}x=\int\partial_{t}R(t,x)d^{m}x=-\int\partial_{x^{\mu}}\big(v^{\mu}(x)R(t,x)\big)d^{m}x=0,

assuming
|v(x)R(t,x)|\to 0
sufficiently fast,
when
|x|\to\infty
.

Below, we assume
\int R(t,x)d^{m}x=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In this case, the average value of a function
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(t,x)\mapsto f(t,x)
, is defined as

\overline{f}(t)=\int f(t,x)R(t,x)d^{m}x,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

We assume, also, that the wave packet
R(t,x)
satisfies
the Liouville equation (
2
). Then the time derivative

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{f}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\big(\partial_{t}f(t,x)\cdot R(t,x)+f(t,x)\cdot\partial_{t}R(t,x)\big)d^{m}x=(\text{see }(\ref{LE}))

\displaystyle=\int\big(\partial_{t}f\cdot R-f\cdot\partial_{x^{\mu}}(v^{\mu}\cdot R)\big)d^{m}x=(\text{integration by parts})

\displaystyle=\int\big(\partial_{t}f+v^{\mu}\partial_{x^{\mu}}f\big)R\,d^{m}x=\int D_{t}f\cdot R\,d^{m}x.

We summarize these considerations as the following

Proposition 1
.

If a wave packet
R(t,s)
satisfies the Liouville equation
(
2
), then the time derivative

\dot{\overline{f}}(t)=\int D_{t}f\cdot R\,d^{m}x,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular,
\overline{f}(t)=\mathrm{const}
,
if
D_{t}f(t,x)=0
.

We are most interested in the average values of the coordinates

\overline{x^{\mu}}(t)=\int x^{\mu}R(t,x)d^{m}x,\quad\mu\in\overline{1,m},

and the dispersion

\overline{\Delta x^{2}}(t)=\int(\Delta x)^{2}R(t,x)d^{m}x=\overline{x^{2}}(t)-\big(\overline{x}(t)\big)^{2},

where

\Delta x=x-\overline{x}(t)
,
\overline{x^{2}}(t)=\int x^{2}R(t,x)d^{m}x
.

Proposition 2
.

In the above settings,

•

\dot{\overline{x}}(t)=\overline{v}(t)
, where
\overline{v}(t)=\int v(x)R(t,x)d^{m}x
;

•

\dot{\overline{\Delta x^{2}}}(t)=2\overline{\langle\Delta x,\Delta v\rangle}(t)=2\big(\overline{\langle x,v\rangle}(t)-\langle\overline{x}(t),\overline{v}(t)\rangle\big)
,

\Delta v=v-\overline{v}(t)
.

3
Hamiltonian dynamics.

In the Hamiltonian situation,
m=2n
,

x=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{n}
, the dynamics is governed
by a Hamiltonian
H=H(q,p)
, the vector field

v=v(q,p)=\big(V,F\big):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{n},

where

V=\big(V^{\alpha}=\partial_{p_{\alpha}}H\big)
is the generalized velocity,

F=\big(F_{\alpha}=-\partial_{q^{\alpha}}H\big)
is the generalized force,

\alpha\in\overline{1,n}
, the total time derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+V^{\alpha}\partial_{q^{\alpha}}+F_{\alpha}\partial_{p_{\alpha}}.

Proposition 3
.

In the Hamiltonian dynamics, the divergence

\partial_{x^{\mu}}v^{\mu}=\partial_{q^{\alpha}}(\partial_{p_{\alpha}}H)+\partial_{p_{\alpha}}(-\partial_{q^{\alpha}}H)=0,

and the Liouville equation (
2
) has the familiar look

\partial_{t}R+\big\{H,R\big\}=0,\qquad\big\{H,R\big\}=\partial_{p_{\alpha}}H\cdot\partial_{q^{\alpha}}R-\partial_{q^{\alpha}}H\cdot\partial_{p_{\alpha}}R.

(4)

In particular, if
H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+U(q)
,

p^{2}=\langle p,p\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}(p_{\alpha})^{2}
,
then

\partial_{t}R+\{H,R\}=\partial_{t}+\sum_{\alpha}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}p_{\alpha}\cdot\partial_{q^{\alpha}}R-\partial_{q^{\alpha}}U\cdot\partial_{p_{\alpha}}R\bigg).

Here (we set
dq=d^{n}q
,
dp=d^{n}p
, for brevity),

\displaystyle\overline{q}(t)=\int qR(t,q,p)dqdp,\quad\dot{\overline{q}}(t)=\int D_{t}q\cdot R(t,q,p)dqdp=\overline{V}(t);

\displaystyle\overline{p}(t)=\int pR(t,q,p)dqdp,\quad\dot{\overline{p}}(t)=\int D_{t}p\cdot R(t,q,p)dqdp=\overline{F}(t).

Thus, in the functional Hamiltonian dynamics we have
the familiar equations for the average values

\dot{\overline{q}}=\overline{\partial_{p}H}=\overline{V},\qquad\dot{\overline{p}}=-\overline{\partial_{q}H}=\overline{F}.

Further,

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta q^{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\Delta q^{2}R(t,q,p)dqdp=\int(q-\overline{q}(t))^{2}R(t,q,p)dqdp;

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{\Delta q^{2}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int D_{t}(q-\overline{q}(t))^{2}\cdot R(t,q,p)dqdp

\displaystyle=2\int\langle(q-\overline{q}(t)),D_{t}(q-\overline{q}(t))\rangle\cdot R(t,q,p)dqdp

\displaystyle=2\int\langle q-\overline{q}(t),V-\overline{V}(t)\rangle\cdot R(t,q,p)dqdp.

Thus,

\dot{\overline{\Delta q^{2}}}(t)=2\overline{\langle\Delta q,\Delta V\rangle}(t),\quad\Delta q=q-\overline{q}(t),\ \Delta V=V(q)-\overline{V}(t).

In the same way,

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta p^{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\Delta p^{2}R(t,q,p)dqdp=\int(p-\overline{p}(t))^{2}R(t,q,p)dqdp;

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{\Delta p^{2}}}(t)

\displaystyle=2\overline{\langle\Delta p,\Delta F\rangle}(t),\quad\Delta p=p-\overline{p}(t),\ \Delta F=F(q)-\overline{F}(t).

Proposition 4
.

If the Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=T(p)+U(q)
,
where
T(p)
is the kinetic energy,
U(q)
is the potential energy,
while the wave packet is factorised, i.e.,

R(t,q,p)=Q(t,q)\cdot P(t,p),\quad\int Q(t,q)dq=\int P(t,p)dp=1.

Then,

\dot{\overline{\Delta q^{2}}}(t)=\dot{\overline{\Delta p^{2}}}(t)=0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Indeed, in this case,

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\Delta q,\Delta V\rangle}(t)=\bigg\langle\int\Delta q\cdot Q(t,q)dq,\int\Delta V\cdot P(t,p)dp\bigg\rangle=\langle\overline{\Delta q},\overline{\Delta V}\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\Delta p,\Delta F\rangle}(t)=\bigg\langle\int\Delta p\cdot P(t,p)dp,\int\Delta F\cdot Q(t,q)dq\bigg\rangle=\langle\overline{\Delta p},\overline{\Delta F}\rangle=0,

because here, for example,

\displaystyle\overline{q}(t)=\int qQ(t,q)dq\cdot\int P(t,p)dp=\int qQ(t,q)dq,

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta q}(t)=\int(q-\overline{q}(t))Q(t,q)dq=\overline{q}(t)-\overline{q}(t)=0.

Remark 1
.

The problem here is the existence of a suitable
factorised wave packet satisfying the Liouville equation (
2
).

4
Lagrangian dynamics.

In the Lagrangian dynamics,
m=2n
,
x=(q,\dot{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the dynamics is governed by a Lagrangian
L=L(q,\dot{q})
.
The total time derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\dot{q}^{\alpha}\partial_{q^{\alpha}}+\ddot{q}^{\alpha}\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}},

where the coefficients

\ddot{q}^{\alpha}(q,\dot{q})
,
\alpha\in\overline{1,n}
,
should be found from the variational equations (Euler-Lagrange equations)

\delta_{q^{\alpha}}L=\partial_{q^{\alpha}}L-\big(\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}}L\big)^{.}=\partial_{q^{\alpha}}L-\partial^{2}_{q^{\beta}\dot{q}^{\alpha}}L\cdot\dot{q}^{\beta}-\partial^{2}_{\dot{q}^{\beta}\dot{q}^{\alpha}}L\cdot\ddot{q}^{\beta}=0.

The crucial role here is played by the Hessian

H=\det\big(\partial^{2}_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}\dot{q}^{\beta}}L\big)
.
If the Hessian is non-degenerate then the function
\ddot{q}
is unequivocally defined by the variational equations,
in the opposite case one should impose some additional constraints.
Note, if the Hessian is not-degenerate one can pass from the variables
q,\dot{q}
to the variables
q,p
, from the Lagrangian
L(q,\dot{q})

pass to the Hamiltonian
H(q,p)
, and come to the Hamiltonian dynamics.

After the above problem is solved, the vector field takes the form

v=(\dot{q},\ddot{q}(q,\dot{q}))
, and the Liouville equation for the
wave packet
R(t,q,\dot{q})
is written as

\partial_{t}R+\partial_{q^{\alpha}}\big(\dot{q}^{\alpha}\cdot R\big)+\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}}\big(\ddot{q}^{\alpha}\cdot R\big)=D_{t}R+\mathrm{div}\,v\cdot R=0,

where the divergence
\mathrm{div}\,v=\partial_{q^{\alpha}}\dot{q}^{\alpha}+\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}}\ddot{q}^{\alpha}=0+\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}}\ddot{q}^{\alpha}=\partial_{\dot{q}^{\alpha}}\ddot{q}^{\alpha}
.

5
Divergence free dynamics.

Here we suppose that the vector field
v:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

is divergence free, i.e.,
\mathrm{div\,}v=0
.
In this case, the Liouville equation takes the form

D_{t}R=\partial_{t}R+v^{\mu}\partial_{x^{\mu}}R=0,

(5)

and according to the theory of the equations in the
partial derivatives of the first order, has the general solution

R(t,x)=\rho(S(t,x)),\quad R(0,x)=\rho(x),

where the profile
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
s\mapsto\rho(s)
,
is arbitrary, while the flow

S:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad(t,x)\mapsto s=S(t,x),\quad S(0,x)=x.

In more detail,
S=(S^{\mu}(t,x))
, where the components
S^{\mu}(t,x)
,

\mu\in\overline{1,m}
, are the first integrals of the equation (
5
).

We also assume that
\int\rho(S(t,x))d^{m}x=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In this case, the average value (
3
) reduces to

\overline{f}(t)=\int f(t,x)\rho(S(t,x))d^{m}x,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

If the induced mapping
S(t):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

x\mapsto s=S(t,x)
, is invertible for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
there exists the inverse mapping
X(t):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

s\mapsto x=X(t,s)
, and the Jacobian

J(t,s)=\det\|\partial_{s^{\mu}}X^{\nu}(t,s)\|\neq 0
,
then the average value can be written as follows

\overline{f}(t)=\int f(t,X(t,s))J(t,s)\cdot\rho(s)d^{m}s,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Proposition 5
.

In the above settings, let

S=A(t)x+a(t),\quad D_{t}S=\dot{A}(t)x+\dot{a}(t)+A(t)v(x)=0,

where

•

s=(s^{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
S=(S^{\mu}(t,x)):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

•

A=(A^{\mu}_{\nu}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{m}
,
x=(x^{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

a=(a^{\mu}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

A(0)
is the identity matrix,
\det A=1
,
a(0)=0
;

•

v=(v^{\mu}(x)):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\mapsto v(x)
,

\mathrm{div\,}v=0
.

Then

•

the induced mapping
S(t):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\mapsto s=S(t,x)
is invertible for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e there is defined the inverse induced mapping

X(t):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

s\mapsto x=X(t,s)
,
X(t,s)=B(t)s+b(t)
,
where
B=(B^{\mu}_{\nu}(t))=A^{-1}
,
b=-Ba
,
J(t,s)=\det B=1
;

•

\overline{x}(t)=B(t)\overline{s}+b(t)
,
\overline{s}=\int s\rho(s)d^{m}s
;

•

\Delta x=x-\overline{x}(t)=B(t)\Delta s
,
\Delta s=s-\overline{s}
;

•

\Delta x^{2}=\langle\Delta x,\Delta x\rangle=\sum_{\lambda}B^{\lambda}_{\mu}(t)B^{\lambda}_{\nu}(t)\cdot\Delta s^{\mu}\Delta s^{\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}(t)\cdot\Delta s^{\mu}\Delta s^{\nu}
;

•

\overline{\Delta x^{2}}=g_{\mu\nu}(t)\cdot\overline{\Delta s^{\mu}\Delta s^{\nu}}=g_{\mu\nu}(t)\cdot\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{Tr}(g\sigma)\geq 0
;

•

g_{\mu\nu}(t)=\sum_{\lambda}B^{\lambda}_{\mu}(t)B^{\lambda}_{\nu}(t)
,

\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\overline{\Delta s^{\mu}\Delta s^{\nu}}=\overline{s^{\mu}\cdot s^{\nu}}-\overline{s^{\mu}}\cdot\overline{s^{\nu}}
;

•

the matrices
g=(g_{\mu\nu}(t))
and

\sigma=(\sigma^{\mu\nu})

are positive definite,
g(0)
is the identity matrix.

The matrix
g(t)
characterises the dynamics of the wave packet,
while the matrix
\sigma
characterises its original profile.

6
Examples.

Example 1
.

Let
n=1
,
H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}
,
m>0
, then the total time derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}
, the Liouville equation takes the form

D_{t}R=\partial_{t}R+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}R=0,

and has the general solution
R(t,q,p)=\rho(r,s)
,
where
\rho=\rho(r,s)
,
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
, is an arbitrary function, and

r=q-\frac{p}{m}t,\quad s=p
.
Thus, here

\begin{cases}r=q-\frac{t}{m}p.\\
s=p;\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}q=r+\frac{t}{m}s,\\
p=s;\end{cases}\qquad J(t;q,p)=1.

In particular, the conditions of Proposition
5

are fulfilled with

x=\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix},\quad s=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
s\end{pmatrix},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{t}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{t}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad a=b=0.

The average value of a function
f(t,q,p
takes the form

\overline{f}(t)=\int f\big(t,r+\frac{t}{m}s,s\big)\cdot\rho(r,s)drds.

In particular,

•

\overline{q}(t)=\overline{r}+\frac{t}{m}\overline{s},\quad\overline{p}(t)=\overline{s}
;

•

\overline{\Delta q^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta r^{2}}+2\frac{t}{m}\cdot\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}+\big(\frac{t}{m}\big)^{2}\cdot\overline{\Delta s^{2}},\quad\overline{\Delta p^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta s^{2}}
;

•

\overline{r}=\int r\rho(r,s)drds
,
\overline{s}=\int s\rho(rs)drds
;

•

\overline{\Delta r^{2}}=\int\Delta r^{2}\rho(r,s)drds
;

\overline{\Delta s^{2}}=\int\Delta s^{2}\rho(r,s)drds
,

\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}=\int\Delta r\Delta s\,\rho(r,s)drds
, .

So,

\overline{\Delta x^{2}}=\overline{\Delta q^{2}}+\overline{\Delta p^{2}}=g_{\mu\nu}\cdot\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{Tr}(g\sigma)
,
where

g=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{t}{m}\\
\frac{t}{m}&1+\big(\frac{t}{m}\big)^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Delta r^{2}}&\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}\\
\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}&\overline{\Delta s^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\det g=1,\quad\det\sigma>0.

Example 2
.

Let
n=1
,
H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-Fq
,
F=const
,
then the total time derivative
D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}+F\partial_{p}
,
while the Liouville equation takes the form

D_{t}R=\partial_{t}R+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}R+F\partial_{p}R=0,

and has the general solution
R(t,q,p)=\rho(r,s)
,
where
\rho=\rho(r,s)
,
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
, is an arbitrary function, and

r=q-\frac{pt}{m}+\frac{Ft^{2}}{2m},\quad s=p-Ft.

Thus, here

\begin{cases}r=q-\frac{t}{m}p+\frac{Ft^{2}}{2m},\\
s=p-Ft;\end{cases}\quad\begin{cases}q=r+\frac{t}{m}s+\frac{Ft^{2}}{2m},\\
p=s+Ft;\end{cases}\quad J(t;r,s/q,p)=1.

The average value of a function
f(t,q,p
takes the form

\overline{f}(t)=\int f\big(t,r+\frac{t}{m}s+\frac{Ft^{2}}{2m},s+Ft\big)\cdot\rho(r,s)drds.

In particular,

•

\overline{q}(t)=\overline{r}+\frac{\overline{s}t}{m}+\frac{Ft^{2}}{2m}
,

\overline{p}(t)=\overline{s}+Ft
;

•

\overline{\Delta q^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta r^{2}}+2\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}\cdot\frac{t}{m}+\overline{\Delta s^{2}}\big(\frac{t}{m}\big)^{2},\quad\overline{\Delta p^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta s^{2}}
,

like in the previous example, because here
\overline{F}=F
,
so
\Delta F=0
.

Example 3
.

Let
n=1
,
H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{kq^{2}}{2}
,
k>0
,
then the total time derivative
D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}-kq\partial_{p}
,
the Liouville equation takes the form

D_{t}R=\partial_{t}R+\frac{p}{m}\partial_{q}R-kq\partial_{p}R=0,

and has the general solution
R(t,q,p)=\rho(r,s)
,
where
\rho=\rho(r,s)
,
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
, is an arbitrary function, and

r=q\cos\omega t-\frac{p}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\quad s=m\omega q\sin\omega t+p\cos\omega t,

\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
,
m\omega=\sqrt{mk}
.
Thus, here

\begin{cases}r=q\cos\omega t-p\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\\
s=q\,m\omega\sin\omega t+p\cos\omega t;\end{cases}\quad\begin{cases}q=r\cos\omega t+s\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\\
p=-r\,m\omega\sin\omega t+s\cos\omega t.\end{cases}

The average value of a function
f(t,q,p
is written in the form

\overline{f}(t)=\int f\big(t,r\cos\omega t+s\,\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,-r\,m\omega\sin\omega t+s\cos\omega t\big)\cdot\rho(r,s)drds.

In particular,

•

\overline{q}(t)=\overline{r}\cos\omega t+\overline{s}\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t
,
\overline{p}(t)=-\overline{r}\,m\omega\sin\omega t+\overline{s}\cos\omega t
;

•

\overline{\Delta q^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta r^{2}}\cos^{2}\omega t+\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}\,\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin 2\omega t+{\overline{\Delta s^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{(m\omega)^{2}}\sin^{2}\omega t
;

•

\overline{\Delta p^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta r^{2}}\,(m\omega)^{2}\sin^{2}\omega t-\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}\,m\omega\sin 2\omega t+{\overline{\Delta s^{2}}}\cos^{2}\omega t
.

Thus, here the conditions of Proposition
5
are fulfilled with

x\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}\!,s\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}r\\
s\end{pmatrix}\!,A\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t\!\!&-\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t\\
m\omega\sin\omega t\!\!&\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}\!,B\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t\!\!&\frac{1}{m\omega}\sin\omega t\\
-m\omega\sin\omega t\!\!&\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}\!.

So, here

\overline{\Delta x^{2}}=\overline{\Delta q^{2}}+\overline{\Delta p^{2}}=g_{\mu\nu}\cdot\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{Tr}(g(t)\sigma)
,
where

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}\omega t+(m\omega)^{2}\sin^{2}\omega t&\big(\frac{1}{m\omega}-m\omega\big)\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\\
\big(\frac{1}{m\omega}-m\omega\big)\cos\omega t\sin\omega t&\cos^{2}\omega t+\big(\frac{1}{m\omega}\big)^{2}\sin^{2}\omega t\end{pmatrix},\quad\det g(t)=1,

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Delta r^{2}}&\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}\\
\overline{\Delta r\cdot\Delta s}&\overline{\Delta s^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\det\sigma>0.

Note,

\overline{\Delta x^{2}}(t)=\overline{\Delta r^{2}}+\overline{\Delta s^{2}}

if
mk=1
.

Example 4
.

In the Lagrangian situation (see Section
4
), let

n=2
,
q=(q^{1},q^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
L(q,\dot{q})=(\dot{q}^{1})^{2}\big/2-U(q)
.
Then, the Euler-Lagrange equations take form

\ddot{q}^{1}=-\partial_{q^{1}}U
,
0=-\partial_{q^{2}}U
.
They have the constraint (the solvability condition)
U=U(q^{1})
,
so here the vector field
v=(v^{1},v^{2})
, where
v^{1}(q^{1})=-\partial_{q^{1}}U(q^{1})
,
while
v^{2}(q^{1},q^{2})
is arbitrary. In particular,
we have no reason to expect that the vector field
v
is divergence free.

Example 5
.

Let
x=(x^{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
v:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\mapsto x
,
(in other words,
v=(v^{\mu}(x))
,
v^{\mu}(x)=x^{\mu}
), the divergence

\mathrm{div\,}v=\partial_{x^{\mu}}x^{\mu}=m
,
the total time derivative
D_{t}=\partial_{t}+x^{\mu}\partial_{x^{\mu}}
.
In this case, the Liouville equation

\partial_{t}R+\partial_{x^{\mu}}\big(x^{\mu}\cdot R\big)+\mathrm{div\,}v\cdot R=D_{t}R+m\cdot R=0,

has the general solution

R(t,x)=R(t,x^{1},\dots,x^{m})=\mathrm{e}^{-mt}\rho(s)=\mathrm{e}^{-mt}\rho(s^{1},\dots,s^{m}),

where
\rho(s)
is an arbitrary function, we assume
\rho\geq 0

and
\int\rho(s)d^{m}s=1
, while

s=x\mathrm{e}^{-t},\quad(x=s\mathrm{e}^{t})

are the solutions of the equation
D_{t}s=0
.
The average value of a function
f(t,x)
here is written in the form

\overline{f}(t)=\int f\big(t,s\mathrm{e}^{t}\big)\rho(s)d^{m}s.

In particular,

\displaystyle\overline{x}(t)=\int s\mathrm{e}^{t}\rho(s)d^{m}s=\mathrm{e}^{t}\cdot\overline{s},

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta x^{2}}(t)=\int\big(s\mathrm{e}^{t}-\overline{s}\mathrm{e}^{t}\big)^{2}\rho(s)d^{m}s=\mathrm{e}^{2t}\cdot\overline{\Delta s^{2}}.

7
Conclusion.

From the above considerations and simple examples one can see
that functional mechanics has the solid mathematical foundations.
Thus, the problem left is the metaphysical foundations of its relevance
to the physical laws and the experimental interpretation.
In particular, one should have the clear answers to the
natural questions listed below.

•

What is the origin of the vector field
v:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

in the conception of the functional mechanics?
Is it governed by the standard classical mechanics,
Hamiltonian or Lagrangian?

•

If the vector field
v
is governed by the Hamiltonian
mechanics, then it is divergence free, i.e.,
\mathrm{div\,}v=0
,
(see Proposition
3
), the wave packet
R(t,x)=\rho(S(t,x))
,
where the profile
\rho(s)
is arbitrary while the flow
s=S(t,x)

is given by the first integrals of the Liouville equation.
One may interpret this situation as follows:
the dynamics is governed by the usual Hamiltonian equations, while
the profile
\rho
characterises measurement.

•

If the vector field
v
is governed by the Lagrangian mechanics,
then there is no reason to expect that the vector field

v
is divergence-free (see Example
4
), so the dynamics
is governed directly by the Liouville equation (
2
),
the wave packet
R=R(t,x)
does not split into the profile and
the flow.

•

Most mathematical models of the modern mathematical physics
are subject to the Lagrangian approach and are formulated as
extremals of the appropriate functionals (actions).
It is naturally requires the applicability of the functional
mechanics to variational problems.

Clear, this list is far from to be complete, so the functional mechanics
has a long way ahead. Let us hope it will be fruitful and successful.
