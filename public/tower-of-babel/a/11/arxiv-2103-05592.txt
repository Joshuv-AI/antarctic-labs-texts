One-Sided k k -Orthogonal Matrices Over Finite Semi-Local Rings And Their Codes
Virgilio P. Sison

Abstract
Let R R be a finite commutative ring with unity 1 R 1_{R} and k ∈ R k\in R . Properties of one-sided k k -orthogonal n × n n\times n matrices over R R are presented. When k k is idempotent, these matrices form a semigroup structure. Consequently new families of matrix semigroups over certain finite semi-local rings are constructed. When k = 1 R k=1_{R} , the classical orthogonal group of degree n n is obtained. It is proved that, if R R is a semi-local ring, then these semigroups are isomorphic to a finite product of k k -orthogonal semigroups over fields. Finally, the antiorthogonal and self-orthogonal matrices that give rise to leading-systematic self-dual or weakly self-dual linear codes are discussed.



Keywords
: orthogonal matrices, semi-local rings, self-dual codes.

Mathematics Subject Classification (2020)
: 15B10, 13M99, 94B60.

Introduction

This study extends the classical theory of orthogonal matrices over fields to the so-called one-sided (left or right)
k
-orthogonal matrices over rings, where
k
is some fixed scalar element. This new class of one-sided
k
-orthogonal matrices is examined in the context of finite commutative rings with unity. In particular, we focus our attention to finite semi-local rings with at least two maximal ideals in order to fully utilize the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Various properties of
k
-orthogonal matrices are described and applications of these matrices in constructing linear codes are explored. It is important to note at this point that we have given a different definition of
k
-orthogonal matrices which may vary from those found in other literature.

The material is organized as follows: Section
2
provides the important conceptual framework while Section
3
contains the main results. MAGMA
®
routines are designed to generate new examples of one-sided and two-sided
k
-orthogonal matrices and to construct the codes from these matrices. A simple algorithm to generate the set of idempotent elements of an arbitrary finite ring is also written. Section
4
gives suggestions for further research.

2.1
Ring of matrices and the orthogonal group

Let
R
be a commutative ring with unity
1_{R}\neq 0
and
M_{n}(R)
the unital noncommutative ring of square
n\times n
matrices over
R
. A matrix
A\in M_{n}(R)
is
nonsingular
or
invertible
if and only if
\det(A)
is a unit in
R
, or equivalently, there exists
B\in M_{n}(R)
such that
AB=BA=I_{n}
, where
I_{n}
is the identity matrix. The set of invertible matrices in
M_{n}(R)
forms a noncommutative multiplicative group and is denoted by
GL_{n}(R)
. A matrix
A
is
symmetric
if
A=A^{T}
, where
A^{T}
is the transpose of
A
. It is called
orthogonal
provided the following holds.

A^{T}A=AA^{T}=I_{n}.

(1)

In other words, an orthogonal matrix is an invertible matrix whose unique two-sided inverse is its transpose. In fact the set of orthogonal matrices over
R
, denoted
O_{n}(R)
, is a (not necessarily normal) subgroup of
GL_{n}(R)
, and is called the
orthogonal group of degree
n
. It should be remarked that, if
A\in O_{n}(R)
, then
\det(A)
is either the identity or an involution, but not conversely.

Let
A=[a_{ij}]
,
B=[b_{ij}]\in M_{n}(R)
, and
I
an ideal of
R
. Then
A
is said to be
congruent
to
B
modulo
I
, written as
A\equiv_{n}B\pmod{I}
, if and only if
a_{ij}\equiv b_{ij}\pmod{I}
, that is
a_{ij}-b_{ij}\in I
, for all
i,j=1,2,...,n
. This is simply component-wise application of the usual additive subgroup relation on an ideal. It is shown in
[
2
]
that
\equiv_{n}
is, quite expectedly, a congruence relation in
M_{n}(R)
.

Two ideals
I
and
J
of
R
are said to be
coprime
if
I+J=R
. A set of nontrivial ideals
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{m}\}
in
R
is
pairwise coprime
if
I_{j}+I_{k}=R
for all
j,k=1,2,...,m
with
j\neq k
. Note that, if
\mathcal{I}
consists of distinct maximal ideals, then necessarily
\mathcal{I}
is pairwise coprime. The Chinese Remainder Theorem gives the following powerful result for matrix rings.

Theorem 2.1
(Han, 2006)

Let
m
and
n
be any positive integers and
I_{1},

I_{2},
…,
I_{m}
be ideals in a ring
R
.Then there is a monomorphism of rings

\theta:M_{n}\bigl(R/I_{1}\cap I_{2}\cap...\cap I_{m}\bigr)\rightarrow M_{n}(R/I_{1})\times M_{n}(R/I_{2})\times\ldots\times M_{n}(R/I_{m})

If
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{m}
are pairwise coprime, then
\theta
is an isomorphism.

This result extends naturally to the general linear group
GL_{n}(R)
, and when
R
is finite and the ideals
I_{j}
are maximal, it gives a convenient formula for
|GL_{n}(R)|
as a product of the
m
factors
|GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})|
, in terms of the residue fields
\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}}
, given by

|GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})|=q_{j}^{\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(q_{j}^{i}-1),j=1,2,\ldots,m

(2)

2.2
Finite semi-local rings

A
semi-local ring
is a commutative ring
R
with unity with a finite number of maximal ideals. In particular, if
R
has a unique maximal ideal, then
R
is said to be a
local ring
. Hence a local ring is semi-local but not conversely. The quaternary ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2}
, where
v^{2}=v
, is a semi-local ring with two maximal coprime ideals
(v)
and
(1+v)
. The ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
of integers modulo
6
is also a semi-local ring with two maximal coprime ideals
(2)
and
(3)
. A finite commutative ring with unity whose ideals are linearly ordered by inclusion is a
finite chain ring
. The ring
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r}}
of integers modulo
p^{r}
,
p
prime and
r\geq 1
an integer, is a local ring with unique maximal ideal
(p)
, and is a finite chain ring of length
r
.

An
idempotent
of a ring is an element
a
with the property that
a^{2}=a
, that is, its square is itself. A
Boolean ring
is a ring in which all its elements are idempotent. The ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2}
, where
v^{2}=v
, is an example of a Boolean ring. It is also known that, if
a_{1},a_{2},..,a_{m}
are idempotent elements of rings
R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{m}
, respectively, then the
m
-tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{m})
is idempotent in the cross product
R_{1}\times R_{2}\times...\times R_{m}
. The integer ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
has
2^{k}
idempotent elements where
k
is the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Specifically, the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
is not Boolean but it has
4
idempotents out of
6
elements, namely,
\{0,1,3,4\}
. Finite local rings with unity contain no idempotents except the trivial elements
0
and
1
.

2.3
Orthogonal codes

Let
k<n
be positive integers. A rate-
k/n

linear code

\mathcal{C}
of length
n
over
R
is an
R
-submodule of
R^{n}
given by the set
\mathcal{C}=\{v\in R^{n}\;|\;v=uG,u\in R^{k}\}
, where
G
is a
k\times n
matrix whose rows span the elements of
\mathcal{C}
which are called
codewords
. If no proper subset of the rows of
G
generates
\mathcal{C}
, then
G
is called a
generator matrix
of
\mathcal{C}
. The code
\mathcal{C}
is a
leading-systematic code
if it has a systematic generator matrix of the form
G=[I_{k}:A]
, where
I_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix and
A
is a
k\times(n-k)
matrix, in which case,
\mathcal{C}
is a
free code
since the rows of
G
form a basis for
\mathcal{C}
. We recall that
\mathcal{C}
is a
weakly self-dual code
if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, where
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\{u\in R^{n}\;|\;\langle u,v\rangle=0,\;\text{for all}\;v\in\mathcal{C}\}
is the orthogonal or dual code of
\mathcal{C}
, and that
\mathcal{C}
is
self-dual
if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.

Massey in
[
5
]
gives specific notions of orthogonal matrices over fields depending on the result of the product of a given matrix with its transpose. An
n\times n
matrix
O
is an
antiorthogonal matrix
if
O^{T}O=-I_{n}
, and is a
self-orthogonal matrix
if
O^{T}O=0_{n}
, where
0_{n}
is the all-zero matrix. Orthogonal, antiorthogonal and self-orthogonal matrices are then used to construct certain linear codes over fields. It is shown in
[
5
]
that a leading-systematic linear code is self-dual if and only if the matrix
A
in its generator matrix
G=[I:A]
is an antiorthogonal matrix. The conditions for the existence of antiorthogonal matrices over a Galois field are stated in
[
6
]
.

A
linear complementary dual code

\mathcal{C}
(or LCD code) is a code satisfying
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Given the generator matrix
G
of a code
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{C}
is self-dual if and only if
GG^{T}
is a nonsingular matrix, as shown in
[
7
]
for the field case. Moreover, a leading systematic linear code with
G=[I:A]
is an LCD code if
A
is a row self-orthogonal matrix, or equivalently, if
G
is a row-orthogonal matrix.

3
Results and discussion

We extend the theory of orthogonal matrices by considering left or right sided
k
-orthogonal matrices over a finite commutative ring
R
with unity, where
k
is some fixed element of
R
. We begin with the structure of one-sided
k
-orthogonal matrices in
M_{n}(R)
.

3.1
k
-orthogonal matrices

Definition 3.1

Let
k
be a fixed element of
R
. A matrix
A\in M_{n}(R)
is said to be left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal if and only if
A^{T}A=kI_{n}
[resp.
AA^{T}=kI_{n}
], where
I_{n}
is the identity of
M_{n}(R)
. A matrix
A
is said to be two-sided
k
-orthogonal (or simply,
k
-orthogonal) if it is both left
k
-orthogonal and right
k
-orthogonal.

Example 3.2

Given the matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&5\\
1&2\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{6})
. Then
A^{T}A=AA^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\
0&5\end{bmatrix}
so that
A
is a two-sided
5
-orthogonal matrix.

Obviously the transpose of a left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal matrix is right [resp. left]
k
-orthogonal. Further, if
A
is left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal, then
[\det(A)]^{2}=k^{n}
, and specifically, if
R
is Boolean,
\det(A)=k
. However, a left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal matrix is not necessarily right [resp. left]
k
-orthogonal, as given in the following example.

Example 3.3

Throughout this paper we will denote by
\mathcal{R}_{2}
the Boolean ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{0,1,v,1+v\}
, where
v^{2}=v
. Given
A=\begin{bmatrix}v&0\\
1+v&1\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
, then
AA^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}v&0\\
0&v\end{bmatrix}
so that
A
is right
v
-orthogonal. The determinant of
A
is
v
, that is,
A
is not a unit in
M_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
. Furthermore,
A^{T}A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1+v\\
1+v&1\end{bmatrix}
, thus
A
is not left
v
-orthogonal.

Example 3.4

The matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}1+v&1\\
1&1+v\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
satisfies
A^{T}A=AA^{T}=vI_{2}
and hence is two-sided
v
-orthogonal.

Example 3.5

The matrix
B=\begin{bmatrix}1+v&0&1+v\\
1&v&1+v\\
v&v&0\end{bmatrix}\in M_{3}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
satisfies
B^{T}B

=BB^{T}=0_{3}
, where
0_{3}
is the
3\times 3
all-zero matrix. Therefore,
B
is a two-sided
0
-orthogonal matrix.

It is important to note that, if
k\in R
is idempotent, the scalar matrix
kI_{n}
is both left
k
-orthogonal and right
k
-orthogonal. The statement is false when
k
is not idempotent. For instance, the matrix
2I_{n}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{6})
. One can easily verify that this matrix is neither left
2
-orthogonal nor right
2
-orthogonal. We extend this statement in the proposition below.

Proposition 3.6

Let
k
be an idempotent element. If
A
is left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal, then
kA
is also left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal, but not conversely.

Proof:
Quite easily,
(kA)^{T}(kA)=k^{2n}A^{T}A=k^{2n+1}I_{n}=kI_{n}.

\Box

Example 3.7

The matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1+v\\
1&v\end{bmatrix}
is right
(1+v)
-orthogonal. Then
(1+v)A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1+v\\
1+v&0\end{bmatrix}
is also right
(1+v)
-orthogonal. However, the reversal matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
is not right
(1+v)
-orthogonal.

We then construct the set of left
k
-orthogonal matrices and the set of right
k
-orthogonal matrices, and denote these sets by
LO_{n}(k,R)
and
RO_{n}(k,R)
, respectively. When
k
is idempotent, these sets are multiplicative semigroups.

Proposition 3.8

Let
k
be an idempotent element of
R
, and
n
be a positive integer,
n\geq 2
. The sets
LO_{n}(k,R)
and
RO_{n}(k,R)
of left and right
k
-orthogonal matrices in
M_{n}(R)
, respectively, are semigroups under matrix multiplication.

Proof:
The set
LO_{n}(k,R)=\{A\in M_{n}(R)\;|\;A^{T}A=kI_{n}\}
is nonempty because the scalar matrix
kI_{n}
is an element. Since
LO_{n}(k,R)\subseteq M_{n}(R)
, associativity is inherent. We just need to show that
LO_{n}(k,R)
is closed under matrix multiplication. Let
A,B\in LO_{n}(k,R).
Then
(AB)^{T}(AB)

=(B^{T}A^{T})(AB)

=B^{T}(kI_{n})B

=k(B^{T}B)

=k(kI_{n})
=k^{2}I_{n}

=kI_{n}
. The proof for
RO_{n}(k,R)
is analogous.
\Box

The identity
I_{n}
of
M_{n}(R)
is not in
LO_{n}(k,R)
and in
RO_{n}(k,R)
in general and hence these semigroups are not monoids. The set of two-sided
k
-orthogonal matrices, denoted
O_{n}(k,R)
, satisfies by definition,

O_{n}(k,R)=LO_{n}(k,R)\cap RO_{n}(k,R)

(3)

and therefore is a nonempty multiplicative semigroup as well, as
kI_{n}\in O_{n}(k,R)
. The semigroup
O_{n}(1_{R},R)
is actually the orthogonal group
O_{n}(R)
.

Example 3.9

The orthogonal group

Proposition 3.10

Let

Suppose

and
RO_{n}(1_{R},R)
are groups.

Proof:
Let the set
LO_{n}(1,R)=\{A\in M_{n}(R)\;|\;A^{T}A=I_{n}\}
. Clearly,
I_{n}\in LO_{n}(1,R)
and is the two-sided identity element, in particular,
I_{n}
is the left identity element. Let
A\in LO_{n}(1,R)
. Since
A^{T}A=I_{n}
, then
A^{T}
is the left inverse of
A
. Hence
LO_{n}(1_{R},R)
contains a left identity element and each element has a left inverse. Therefore,
LO_{n}(1,R)
is group. The proof for
RO_{n}(1,R)
is analogous.
\Box

Corollary 3.17

For any commutative ring
R
with unity
1\neq 0
and positive integer
n\geq 2
, we have
LO_{n}(1,R)=RO_{n}(1,R)=O_{n}(R)
.

Proof:
Suppose
A\in LO_{n}(1,R)
. Then
A^{T}A=I_{n}
. Now we take the product
(AA^{T})(AA^{T})=A(A^{T}A)A^{T}=A(I_{n})A^{T}=AA^{T}
. Since
LO_{n}(1,R)
is a group, then by cancellation,
AA^{T}=I_{n}
so that
A\in RO_{n}(1,R)
. Hence,
LO_{n}(1,R)\subseteq RO_{n}(1,R)
. Similarly,
RO_{n}(1,R)\subseteq LO_{n}(1,R)
. The second equality is a mere consequence of (
3
).
\Box

The converse of Theorem
3.16
is obviously true, as shown below.

Proposition 3.18

Let
R
be any commutative ring with unity
1\neq 0
. If
O_{n}(k,R)
is a group, then
k=1
.

Proof:
Since
O_{n}(k,R)
is a subgroup of
GL_{n}(R)
, necessarily the matrix
I_{n}
, which is in
O_{n}(1,R)
, is also the identity of
O_{n}(k,R)
, and we are done.
\Box

3.2
Semigroup isomorphisms

Analogous semigroup isomorphisms for the left [resp. right]
k
-orthogonal semigroups
LO_{n}(k,R)
[resp.
RO_{n}(k,R)
] are deduced and specific examples of these isomorphisms are presented in this section. But initially, we give an easy but useful corollary to Theorem
2.1
. On the side, we give a product formula for the cardinality of
O_{n}(R)
.

Corollary 3.19

For any positive integers
m,n\geq 2
, if
R
is a finite semi-local ring with maximal ideals
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{m}
, then

O_{n}(R)\cong O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})\times O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})\times\ldots\times O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}})

(5)

where
\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}}
are Galois fields of order
q_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,m
.

Proof:
In Theorem
2.1
,
GL_{n}(R)
is isomorphic to the product
GL_{n}(R/I_{1})\times GL_{n}(R/I_{2})\times...\times GL_{n}(R/I_{m})
. Since
R
is finite and
I_{j}
is maximal, then
R/I_{j}
is a Galois field
\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}},
for some
q_{j}
a power of a prime. That is,

GL_{n}(R)\cong GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})\times GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})\times\ldots\times GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}})

Now,
O_{n}(R)
is a subgroup of
GL_{n}(R)
, and
O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})
is a subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})
, for
j=1,2,\ldots,m
. By extension and the correspondence theorem, the result (
5
) follows.
\Box

Consequently the number of
n\times n
orthogonal matrices over
R
is given by

|O_{n}(R)|=|O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})|\cdot|O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})|\cdot....\cdot|O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}})|

(6)

where each factor in the product is computed using the formula from
[
1
]
.

Example 3.20

We have
GL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\cong GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\times GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
Using the formula in (
2
) we get
|GL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{2})|=6\cdot 6=36
. Further,
O_{2}(1,\mathcal{R}_{2})\cong O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\times O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
, which is a Klein-
4
group. Since
|O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})|=2
from
[
1
]
, therefore
|O_{2}(1,\mathcal{R}_{2})|=4
, as we already know.

Example 3.21

We have
O_{2}(1,\mathbb{Z}_{6})\cong O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\times O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
Now,
|O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})|=2
and
|O_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})|=8
from the formulas in
[
1
]
. Hence,
\lvert O_{2}(1,\mathbb{Z}_{6})\rvert=16
as earlier mentioned in Example
3.9
. In fact,
O_{2}(1,\mathbb{Z}_{6})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times D_{4}
, where
D_{4}
is the dihedral group of degree
4
.

Corollary 3.22

Let
R
be a finite semi-local ring and
k\neq 1
be an idempotent element of
R
. For positive integers
m,n\geq 2
, we have

LO_{n}(k,R)\cong LO_{n}(a_{1},\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})\times LO_{n}(a_{2},\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})\times\ldots\times LO_{n}(a_{m},\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}})

(7)

where the
q_{i}
’s are powers of primes, and the
a_{i}
’s are determined by the mapping from
R
to
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\times\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}}
. Analogously for
RO_{n}(k,R)
.

Proof:
We consider the isomorphism
\theta
in Theorem
2.1
as an isomorphism of multiplicative semigroups and follow the same reasoning in the previous corollary. Since
R
is finite and
I_{j}
is maximal, then
R/I_{j}
is a Galois field
\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}},
for some
q_{j}
a power of a prime. Now,
LO_{n}(k,R)
is a subsemigroup of
M_{n}(R)
, and
LO_{n}(a_{j},\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})
is a subsemigroup of
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}})
for
j=1,\dots,m
. By extension and the correspondence theorem for semigroups, the result (
7
) follows.
\Box

Consequently the number of left
k
-orthogonal matrices over
R
is given by

|LO_{n}(R)|=|LO_{n}(a_{1},\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})|\cdot|LO_{n}(a_{2},\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})|\cdot....\cdot|LO_{n}(a_{m},\mathbb{F}_{q_{m}})|

(8)

Example 3.23

The Boolean ring
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
v^{2}=v
is isomorphic to the product
\mathbb{F}_{2}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
via the map
a+vb\mapsto(a+b,a)
. Hence,

LO_{n}(a+vb,\mathcal{R}_{2})\cong LO_{n}(a+b,\mathbb{F}_{2})\times LO_{n}(a,\mathbb{F}_{2}).

In particular, we have the following isomorphisms.

LO_{n}(0,\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong LO_{n}(0,\mathbb{F}_{2})\times LO_{n}(0,\mathbb{F}_{2})

LO_{n}(v,\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\times LO_{n}(0,\mathbb{F}_{2})

LO_{n}(1+v,\mathbb{F}_{2}+v\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong LO_{n}(0,\mathbb{F}_{2})\times O_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})

Example 3.24

The integer ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}

Example 3.25

The set

\langle v\rangle=\{xv\;|\;x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{r}}\}

Example 3.26

For
p

\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}+v\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}=\{a+vb\;|\;a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}},\;v^{2}=1\}

\langle 1+v\rangle=\{x(1+v)\;|\;x\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}\}

3.3
Codes from
k
-orthogonal matrices

We study how
(-1)
-orthogonal matrices and
0
-orthogonal matrices can give rise to leading-systematic self-dual or weakly self-dual linear codes. The construction of these classes of codes is illustrated in some examples.

In this section, we let
R
to be a finite commutative ring with unity. Following the discussion in the previous section, we use the terminology in
[
5
]
to say that a matrix
A\in M_{n}(R)
is left [resp. right] antiorthogonal if and only if
A
is left [resp. right]
(-1)
-orthogonal. If
A
is both left antiorthogonal and right antiorthogonal, then
A
is said to be an
antiorthogonal
matrix. However, the following proposition says that one-sided antiorthogonal matrices over
R
coincide.

Proposition 3.27

A left antiorthogonal matrix is a right antiorthogonal matrix, and conversely.

Proof:
Suppose
AA^{T}=-I_{n}
. Then
\det A
is a unit in
R
so that
A\in GL_{n}(R)
in which left and right inverses coincide. The result follows.
\Box

The matrix in Example
3.2
is an antiorthogonal matrix. Clearly, an antiorthogonal matrix is orthogonal if and only if the characteristic of
R
is
2
. Consequently, Proposition
1
of
[
5
]
can be stated more generally as follows.

Proposition 3.28

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring with unity. A rate-
k/n
leading-systematic linear code over
R
is self-dual if and only if, in its generator matrix
G=[I_{k}:A]
, the matrix
A
is antiorthogonal.

It should be noted that, in this case,
n=2k
so that
A
is also a
k\times k
matrix.

The MAGMA
®
routines that we have developed help us to find the one-sided
k
-orthogonal matrices, and in particular, the antiorthogonal matrices which subsequently give the self-dual codes, as illustrated in the example below. The self-duality is checked further in MAGMA
®
.

Example 3.29

Consider the two-sided
5
-orthogonal matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\
1&4\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{6})
. Then form the
2\times 4
generator matrix

G=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&A\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&4&5\\
0&1&1&4\end{bmatrix}.

The leading-systematic code over
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
generated by
G
is self-dual.

Example 3.30

The rate-
4/8
octacode
\mathcal{O}_{8}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
is a self-dual leading-systematic linear code with generator matrix
G=[I_{4}:A]
where

A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1&2&1\\
1&2&3&1\\
3&3&3&2\\
2&3&1&1\end{bmatrix}

is a two-sided
3
-orthogonal matrix. The Lee distance is
6
and the Hamming distance is
4
.

The antiorthogonality condition can be extended to non-square matrices. A
k\times m
matrix
A
is said to be
right row-antiorthogonal
if
AA^{T}=-I_{k}
. Deleting rows of an antiorthogonal matrix will result to a right row-antiorthogonal matrix, but not every right row antiorthogonal matrix can be constructed. We claim an extension of Proposition
2
in
[
5
]
as follows.

Proposition 3.31

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring with unity. A leading-systematic linear code over
R
is weakly self-dual if and only if, in its generator matrix
G=\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}&A\end{bmatrix}
, the matrix
A
is right row-antiorthogonal.

Example 3.32

With the matrix
A
of the octacode
\mathcal{O}_{8}
in Example
3.30
, delete the fourth row to get
B=\begin{bmatrix}3&1&2&1\\
1&2&3&1\\
3&3&3&2\end{bmatrix}
. It can be verified that
B
is a right row-antiorthogonal matrix. We then construct the leading-systematic rate-
3/7

\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear code using the generator matrix

G=\begin{bmatrix}I_{3}&B\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&3&1&2&1\\
0&1&0&1&2&3&1\\
0&0&1&3&3&3&2\end{bmatrix}.

The code generated by
G
is weakly self-dual. The Lee distance is
6
and the Hamming distance is
4
.

A matrix
A\in M_{n}(R)
is said to be left [resp. right] self-orthogonal if and only if
A
is left [resp. right]
0
-orthogonal. If
A
is both left self-orthogonal and right self-orthogonal, then the matrix
A
is simply called
self-orthogonal
. The matrix in Example
3.5
is a self-orthogonal matrix. Unlike the case of antiorthogonal matrices, left self-orthogonal matrices and right self-orthogonal matrices do not necessarily coincide.

Example 3.33

The matrix in
M_{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{6})
given by

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&3&3\\
4&2&4\\
2&1&5\end{bmatrix}

is right self-orthogonal but not left self-orthogonal. We adopt the analogous idea of a row right self-orthogonal matrix. Deleting any row of
A
, say, the third row, we get the
2\times 3
matrix
G=\begin{bmatrix}0&3&3\\
4&2&4\end{bmatrix}
, which is a row right self-orthogonal matrix. The matrix
G
generates a weakly self-dual code over
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
.

This example is generalized in the proposition below.

Proposition 3.34

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring with unity. A linear code over
R
with generator matrix
G
is weakly self-dual if and only if
G
is row right self-orthogonal.

4
Conclusion and recommendations

For further research it is interesting to derive explicit formulas for the cardinality of the finite one-sided
k
-orthogonal semigroups. It is also worthy to consider applying the results of this paper to other classes of finite semi-local rings with multiple idempotents such as those given in Examples
3.25
and
3.26
. More specific and relevant structural properties of the matrix semigroups can be explored.

We have also proved that there exist no
3\times 3
antiorthogonal matrices over the integer ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
. This is accomplished by using the map from
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
onto
\mathbb{F}_{2}\times\mathbb{F}_{3}
in Example
3.24
to show that indeed
-1
is a non-square in
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
, and by
[
6
]
that, for any odd integer
n
, there exist no
n\times n
antiorthogonal matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. This specific result is likewise verified through exhaustive search. The reader is encouraged to determine the conditions for the existence of antiorthogonal matrices over finite semi-local rings.

Finally, the connections of the above defined matrices to the characterization of LCD codes over finite commutative rings with unity can be studied.
