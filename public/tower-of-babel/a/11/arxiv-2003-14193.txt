On Hom-Groups and Hom-Group actions
Liangyun Chen, Tianqi Feng, Yao Ma, Ripan Saha, Hongyi Zhang

Abstract
A Hom-group is the non-associative generalization of a group, whose associativity and unitality are twisted by a compatible bijective map. In this paper, we give some new examples of Hom-groups, and show the first and the second isomorphism fundamental theorems of homomorphisms on Hom-groups. We also introduce the notion of Hom-group action, and as an application, we show the first Sylow theorem for Hom-groups along the line of group actions.



Key words:
Hom-groups; Hom-subgroups; Hom-quotient groups; Isomorphism; Hom-group actions; First Sylow theorem.

MSC(2010):
17A99, 17B61, 20B99, 20D20, 20N05.

Introduction

Hartwig, Larsson, and Silvestrov
[
9
]
firstly introduced the notion of Hom-Lie algebras during the study of deformations of the Witt and the Virasoro algebras. In a Hom-Lie algebra, the Jacobi identity is twisted by a linear map, called the Hom-Jacobi identity in
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. A quantum deformation or a
q
-deformation of an algebra of vector fields is obtained when a usual derivation was replaced by a derivation
d_{\sigma}
that satisfies a twisted Leibniz rule
d_{\sigma}\left(fg\right)=d_{\sigma}f\left(g\right)+\sigma\left(f\right)d_{\sigma}\left(g\right)
, where
\sigma
is an algebra endomorphism of a commutative associative algebra. The set of
\sigma
-derivations with the classical bracket is a new type of algebra that does not satisfy the Jacobi identity. However, it satisfies Hom-Jacobi identity and it is called Hom-Lie algebras. Because of close relation of discrete and deformed vector fields and differential calculus in
[
9
,
12
,
13
]
, more people pay special attention to the algebraic structure. The corresponding associative algebras called Hom-associative algebras, were introduced in
[
14
]
. A Hom-associative algebra with a bracket
\left[a,b\right]=ab-ba
is a Hom-Lie algebra. Other-nonassociative objects such as Hom-coalgebras, Hom-bialgebras and Hom-Hopf algebras were introduced and studied in
[
4
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Much work has been done between Hom-Lie algebras and Hom-Hopf algebras. Due to the lack of Hom-type of notions for groups and group algebras, Hom-groups which naturally are appearing in the structure of the group-like elements of Hom-Hopf algebras came into the context of Hom-type objects to complete some relations
[
8
]
. Having this notion will help us to find out more about Hom-Hopf algebras. The twisting map
\alpha
of a Hom-group
G
does not need to be invertible in the original works
[
6
,
8
]
, however many interesting results in
[
5
]
, including the main results of this paper, are obtained in the case
\alpha
is invertible. The author in
[
5
,
6
]
has introduced some basics of Hom-groups, their representations, Hom-group (co)homology, and Lagrange’s theorem for Hom-groups. In
[
11
]
, the authors defined a notion of Hom-Lie groups and introduced an action of Hom-group on smooth manifolds.

In this paper, we assume that the twisting map
\alpha
is bijective. Then we promote the results of groups to Hom-groups. In Section
2
, we introduce some new examples of Hom-groups. In Section
3
, we discuss some fundamental notions of Hom-groups such as Hom-subgroups and the law of cancellation of Hom-groups. In addition, we show the intersection of two Hom-subgroups is a Hom-group. In Section
4
, we define Hom-normal subgroups by Hom-cosets. Then we give Hom-quotient groups and prove that a Hom-quotient group is a Hom-group. In Section
5
, we use Hom-quotient groups to show that the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms of Hom-groups is valid. Then we obtain the first and the second isomorphism theorems. In Section
6
, we introduce a notion of Hom-group action and study some properties of Hom-group actions. In the final Section
7
, we prove the first Sylow theorem for Hom-groups using Hom-group actions.

Throughout the paper, we assume the map
\alpha
is invertible, then some properties can be obtained by Hom-associativity when
\alpha
is invertible.

2
New examples of Hom-groups

In this section, we recall the basic notions and properties of Hom-groups and provide some new examples of Hom-groups.

Definition 2.1
.

[
5
]

A Hom-group consists of a set
G
together with a distinguished member
1\in G
, a bijective set map
\alpha:G\longrightarrow G
, a binary operation
\mu:G\times G\longrightarrow G
, where these pieces of structure are subject to the following axioms:

(1)
The product map
\alpha:G\longrightarrow G
satisfies the Hom-associativity property

\mu\left(\alpha\left(g\right),\mu\left(h,k\right)\right)=\mu\left(\mu\left(g,h\right),\alpha\left(k\right)\right).

For simplicity when there is no confusion, we omit the multiplication sign
\mu
.

(2)
The map
\alpha
is multiplicative

\alpha\left(gh\right)=\alpha\left(g\right)\alpha\left(h\right).

(3)
The element
1
is called unit and it satisfies the Hom-unitarity conditions

g1=1g=\alpha\left(g\right).

(4)
For every element
g\in G
, there exists an element
g^{-1}\in G
such that

gg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=1.

G
is called a Hom-semigroup if the conditions
(1)
,
(2)
are satisfied. A Hom-semigroup satisfying the condition
(3)
is called a Hom-monoid.

Definition 2.2
.

A Hom-group
(G,\alpha)
is called an involutive Hom-group if
\alpha^{2}=\alpha
.

Example 2.3
.

Every group
(G,\mu)
together with an automorphism
\alpha:G\to G
has a Hom-group structure
(G,\mu_{\alpha},\alpha)
, where
\mu_{\alpha}=\alpha\circ\mu
. Thus, every group can be thought of as a Hom-group by considering
\alpha
as the identity map. In this way, the notion of a Hom-group is a generalized notion of a group.

Example 2.4
.

(\mathbb{R},+,0,\text{Id})
is a Hom-group. Note that this example is a special case of the previous example.

Example 2.5
.

Averaging operation
\oplus
on
\mathbb{R}
, that is,
a\oplus b=\frac{a+b}{2}
is a non-associative operation. Therefore,
(\mathbb{R},\oplus)
is not a group. We show
(\mathbb{R},\oplus,0,\alpha)
is a Hom-group, where
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
\alpha(a)=\frac{a}{2}
. It is trivial to check that
\alpha
is a multiplicative bijective map on
\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\alpha(a)\oplus(b\oplus c)=\frac{a}{2}\oplus\frac{b+c}{2}=\frac{\frac{a}{2}+\frac{b+c}{2}}{2}=\frac{a+(b+c)}{4}

\displaystyle(a\oplus b)\oplus\alpha(c)=\frac{a+b}{2}\oplus\frac{c}{2}=\frac{\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{c}{2}}{2}=\frac{(a+b)+c}{4}.

Thus,
\oplus
is Hom-associative as usual addition
+
of real numbers is associative.

Now,

\displaystyle a\oplus 0=\frac{a}{2}=\alpha(a).

This implies
0
is the Hom-identity. Hence,
(\mathbb{R},\oplus,0,\alpha)
is a Hom-group.

Example 2.6
.

a\otimes b=\sqrt[3]{ab}
is a non-associative operation on
\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\}
. Therefore,
\left(\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\},1\right)
is not a group. We show
\left(\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\},\otimes,1,\alpha\right)
is a Hom-group, where
\alpha:\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\}
is defined as
\alpha\left(a\right)=\sqrt[3]{ab}
. It is easy to check that
\alpha
is a bijective map on
\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\}
.

We have

\alpha\left(a\otimes b\right)=\alpha\left(\sqrt[3]{ab}\right)=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt[3]{ab}}=\sqrt[9]{ab}

\alpha\left(a\right)\otimes\alpha\left(b\right)=\sqrt[3]{a}\otimes\sqrt[3]{b}=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt[3]{a}\sqrt[3]{b}}=\sqrt[9]{ab}

which imply
\alpha
is multiplicative.

Moreover,

\alpha\left(a\right)\otimes\left(b\otimes c\right)=\sqrt[3]{a}\otimes\left(\sqrt[3]{bc}\right)=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt[3]{a}\sqrt[3]{bc}}=\sqrt[9]{abc}

\left(a\otimes b\right)\otimes\alpha\left(c\right)=\left(\sqrt[3]{ab}\right)\otimes\sqrt[3]{c}=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt[3]{ab}\sqrt[3]{c}}=\sqrt[9]{abc}

a\otimes 1=\sqrt[3]{a1}=\sqrt[3]{a}=\alpha\left(a\right)=\sqrt[3]{1a}=1\otimes a

Therefore,
\otimes
satisfies Hom-associative and Hom-identity. And it is easy to check that
\frac{1}{a}
is the inverse of every
a\in\mathbb{R}-\left\{0\right\}
.

Example 2.7
.

The additive group of integers
(\mathbb{Z},+)
has a Hom-group structure with
\oplus
and
\alpha:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
is defined as

a\oplus b=-(a+b),~\alpha(a)=-a.

Note that this is an example of an involutive Hom-group.

Example 2.8
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set and
\beta
be a bijective map on
X
. Let
\bf{Sym(X)}
be the set of all bijective maps on
X
. Then
(\bf{Sym(X)},\mu,\alpha_{\beta})
is a Hom-group, where

\mu(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=\beta\circ\sigma_{1}\circ\beta^{-1}\circ\sigma_{2}\circ\beta^{-1},~~~\alpha_{\beta}(\sigma)=\beta\circ\sigma\circ\beta^{-1}.

It is easy to check that
(\bf{Sym(X)},\mu)
satisfies Hom-associative condition. Note that
\beta
is the Hom-identity of
\bf{Sym(X)}
, and the inverse of
\sigma_{1}
under this binary operation is
\beta\circ\sigma^{-1}_{1}\circ\beta
. Thus,
(\bf{Sym(X)},\mu,\beta,\alpha_{\beta})
is a Hom-group.

.

This example is a particular case of the above example.
Consider
X=\{1,2,3\}
, and
\beta=(1~2)
. We have

\bf{Sym(X)}=\{id,(1~2),(1~3),(2~3),(1~2~3),(1~3~2)\}

Using the binary operation
\mu
on
\bf{Sym(X)}
, we get the following table:

3
Fundamental properties of Hom-groups

In this section, we introduce several new notions of Hom-groups such as the law of cancellation and equivalent descriptions of Hom-groups.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
G
is a Hom-monoid, then the unit is unique. Therefore, there is a unique unit in Hom-groups.

Proof.

Let
1^{\prime}
be another unit of
G
, then
1^{\prime}=\alpha\left(1^{\prime}\right)=1^{\prime}1=\alpha\left(1\right)=1.

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a Hom-group. Then

(1)
[
11
]

\alpha(1)=1
.

(1)
[
5
]
For each
g\in G
, the inverse element
g^{-1}
is unique.

(2)
[
6
]
For each
g\in G
,
\left(g^{-1}\right)^{-1}=g
.

(3)
[
5
]
For
g,h\in G
,
\left(gh\right)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}
.

(4)
[
6
]
For each
g\in G
,
\left(\alpha(g)\right)^{-1}=\alpha(g^{-1})
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a Hom-group. If there is an element
g\in G
such that
gg=\alpha\left(g\right)=g1
, then
g=1
.

Proof.

Assume that
g\in G
satisfies
gg=\alpha\left(g\right)=g1
. Then

\displaystyle\left(gg\right)\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)=\alpha\left(g\right)\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)=\alpha\left(gg^{-1}\right)=\alpha\left(1\right)=1,

\displaystyle\left(gg\right)\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(gg^{-1}\right)=\alpha\left(g\right)1=\alpha^{2}\left(g\right),

which imply that
\alpha^{2}\left(g\right)=1
. Hence
g=\alpha^{-2}\left(1\right)=1
by
\alpha
being invertible.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If the elements
g,h,k
in a Hom-group satisfy
gh=gk
or
hg=kg
, then
h=k
.

Proof.

Assume that
gh=gk
. By left multiplying
\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)
to both sides of
gh=gk
the equality
\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)\left(gh\right)=\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)\left(gk\right)
is obtained, so
\left(g^{-1}g\right)\alpha\left(h\right)=\left(g^{-1}g\right)\alpha\left(k\right)
by the law of Hom-associativity. Hence
1\alpha\left(h\right)=1\alpha\left(k\right)
by the definition of Hom-invertibility. By Hom-unitarity we have
\alpha^{2}\left(h\right)=\alpha^{2}\left(k\right)
. Then
h=k
, since
\alpha
is invertible. The same argument shows that
hg=kh
implies
h=k
.∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
be a Hom-group. Then

(1)
For all
g,h\in G
,
\alpha^{-1}\left(gh\right)=\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(h\right)
.

(2)
For all
g,h,k\in G
,
\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)h\right)k=g\left(h\alpha^{-1}\left(k\right)\right)
.

Proof.

(1) For all
g,h\in G
,

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(gh\right)\right)=gh=\alpha\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\right)\alpha\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(h\right)\right)=\alpha\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(h\right)\right).

We have
\alpha^{-1}\left(gh\right)=\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(h\right)
, since
\alpha
is invertible.

(2) For all
g,h,k\in G
, since
\alpha
is invertible, there exists
h^{\prime}
such that
\alpha^{-1}\left(h^{\prime}\right)=h
, and

\displaystyle\alpha\left(g\right)\left(h^{\prime}k\right)=\left(gh^{\prime}\right)\alpha\left(k\right),

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\left(\alpha\left(g\right)\left(h^{\prime}k\right)\right)=\alpha^{-1}\left(\left(gh^{\prime}\right)\alpha\left(k\right)\right),

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\left(\alpha\left(g\right)\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(h^{\prime}k\right)=\alpha^{-1}\left(gh^{\prime}\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(\alpha\left(k\right)\right),

\displaystyle g\alpha^{-1}\left(h^{\prime}k\right)=\alpha^{-1}\left(gh^{\prime}\right)k,

\displaystyle g\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(h^{\prime}\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(k\right)\right)=\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(h^{\prime}\right)\right)k,g\left(h\alpha^{-1}\left(k\right)\right)=\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)h\right)k,

which complete the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G
be a Hom-semigroup. Then
G
is a Hom-group if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(1)
There exists an element
1\in G
such that
1g=\alpha\left(g\right)
,
\alpha\left(1\right)=1
(left unit).

(2)
For each
g\in G
, there exists an element
g^{-1}\in G
such that
g^{-1}g=1
(left inverse).

Proof.

\left(\Longrightarrow\right)
Trivial.

\left(\Longleftarrow\right)

We need to prove:
1
is the right unit, and
g^{-1}
is the right inverse, i.e.
gg^{-1}=1
.
Let
h=gg^{-1}
, then

\displaystyle hh

\displaystyle=\left(gg^{-1}\right)\left(gg^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(gg^{-1}\right)\alpha\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(gg^{-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(g^{-1}\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(gg^{-1}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(g^{-1}\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g\right)\alpha^{-1}\left(g^{-1}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(g^{-1}g\right)g^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(\alpha^{-1}\left(1\right)g^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\left(1g^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(g\right)\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(gg^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(h\right).

Moreover, we have
h=\alpha^{-1}(hh)=\alpha^{-1}(h)\alpha^{-1}(h)
and
\alpha\left(1\right)=1=h^{-1}h=h^{-1}\left(\alpha^{-1}(h)\alpha^{-1}(h)\right)=\left(\alpha^{-1}(h)\alpha^{-1}(h)\right)h=\alpha^{-1}\left(1\right)h=1h=\alpha\left(h\right)=\alpha\left(gg^{-1}\right)
.

1=gg^{-1}
since
\alpha
is invertible. Thus
g^{-1}
is the right inverse.

In addition,
g1=g\alpha^{-1}\left(1\right)=g\alpha\left(g^{-1}g\right)=g\left(\alpha^{-1}(g^{-1})\alpha^{-1}(g)\right)=\left(\alpha^{-1}(g)\alpha^{-1}(g^{-1})\right)g=\alpha^{-1}\left(gg^{-1}\right)g

=\alpha^{-1}\left(1\right)g=1g=\alpha\left(g\right)
. Thus
1
is the right unit.

This shows that
G
is a Hom-group.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G
be a nonempty Hom-semigroup with
|G|<+\infty
. Then
G
is a Hom-group if and only if
G
satisfies the law of left and right cancellation.

Proof.

Let
G=\left\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\right\}
. Since
\alpha
is invertible, we have

G=\left\{a_{1},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\right\}=\left\{\alpha\left(a_{1}\right),\alpha\left(a_{2}\right),\cdots,\alpha\left(a_{n}\right)\right\}.

For any
k\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,n\right\}
, by right multiplying
a_{k}
to every element of
G
,
G^{\prime}=\left\{a_{1}a_{k},a_{2}a_{k},\cdots,a_{n}a_{k}\right\}
is obtained.

We claim that
G=G^{\prime}
. By the closure of binary operation, we have
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
. We need to show that
|G^{\prime}|=n=|G|
, otherwise there exist
1\leq i,j\leq n
such that
a_{i}a_{k}=a_{j}a_{k}
.
a_{i}=a_{j}
by the law of right cancellation, which leads to a contraction. Thus
|G^{\prime}|=n=|G|
. The claim is proved.

There exists
r\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,n\right\}
such that
\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)=a_{r}a_{k}
by
G=G^{\prime}
. So
\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{k}a_{k}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)a_{r}a_{k}=\left(a_{k}a_{r}\right)\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)
. By the law of right cancelation, we get
\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)=a_{k}a_{r}
. We claim that
a_{r}
is the left unit of
G
. For any
\alpha\left(a_{i}\right)\in G
,
\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)\alpha\left(a_{i}\right)=\left(a_{k}a_{r}\right)\alpha\left(a_{i}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{k}\right)\left(a_{r}a_{i}\right)
. By the law of left cancellation, we get
\alpha\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{r}a_{i}
. In addition,
\alpha\left(a_{r}\right)=a_{r}
, otherwise assume that
\alpha\left(a_{r}\right)=a_{s}\neq a_{r}
, we have
a_{r}\left(a_{r}a_{s}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{r}a_{s}\right)=\alpha\left(a_{r}\right)\alpha\left(a_{s}\right)=a_{s}\left(a_{r}a_{s}\right)
. By the law of right cancelation, we get
a_{r}=a_{s}
, leading to contradiction. Therefore
a_{r}
is the left unit of
G
. This proves the claim.

Since
G^{\prime}=G
, there exists
j\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,n\right\}
such that
a_{r}=a_{j}a_{k}
, that is,
a_{j}
is the left inverse of
a_{k}
with respect to the left unit
a_{r}
in
G
. Therefore,
G
is a Hom-group.
∎

Definition 3.8
.

[
6
]

Let
\left(G,\alpha\right)
be a Hom-group and
H
a nonempty subset that is closed under the product in
G
. If
\left(H,\alpha\right)
is itself a Hom-group under the product in
G
, then
H
is said to be a Hom-subgroup of
G
, denoted by
H\preceq G
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
H
be a Hom-subgroup of
G
. Then

(1)

1_{H}=1_{G}
.

(2)
For each
g\in H
,
g^{-1}_{H}=g^{-1}_{G}
.

Proof.

(1)
1_{H}1_{H}=\alpha\left(1_{H}\right)=1_{H}=\alpha\left(1_{H}\right)=1_{G}1_{H}
, so
1_{H}=1_{G}
by the law of cancellation in
G
.

(2) For each
g\in H
, we have
gg^{-1}_{H}=1_{H}=1_{G}=gg^{-1}_{G}
. Hence
g^{-1}_{H}=g^{-1}_{G}
by the law of cancellation in
G
.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
H
,
K
be two Hom-subgroups of
G
. Then

(1)

H\cap K\preceq G
;

(2)

H\cup K\preceq G
if and only if
H\subset K
or
K\subset H
.

Proof.

(1) Obviously, we have
1_{G}\in H\cap K\not=\emptyset
by Proposition
3.9
. For any
g,h\in H\cap K
, we have
g,h\in H
and
g,h\in K
. So we have
gh^{-1}\in H
and
gh^{-1}\in K
by
H\preceq G
and
K\preceq G
. Therefore,
gh^{-1}\in H\cap K
, which implies
H\cap K\preceq G
.

(2)
\left(\Longleftarrow\right)
Assume that
H\subset K
or
K\subset H
, we have
H\cup K=K
or
H
, then
H\cup K\preceq G
.

\left(\Longrightarrow\right)
Suppose that
H\cup K\preceq G
, we need to prove
H\subset K
or
K\subset H
. Suppose that
H\subsetneq K
and
K\subsetneq H
, then there exists
g\in H
while
g\not\in K
, and there exists
h\in K
while
h\not\in H
. Hence
g\in H\cup K
and
h\in H\cup K
. Then
gh\in H\cup K
follows from
H\cup K\preceq G
. Thus
gh\in H
or
gh\in K
. Suppose that
gh\in H
. Since
H\preceq G
, we have
\alpha\left(g^{-1}\right)\in H
. The equality

Let

be a Hom-normal subgroup of a Hom-group

Since
\pi(xy)=(xy)H=(xH)(yH)=\pi(x)\pi(y)

\pi(\alpha(x))=\alpha(x)H=\tilde{\alpha}(xH)=\tilde{\alpha}(\pi(x))

\pi

\pi

By reference
[
5

\pi(x)=(x)H=H=1_{G/H}

Theorem 5.9
.

(Fundamental Theorem of Homomorphisms of Hom-groups)
Let
G
,
H
be two Hom-groups. If
f:(G,\beta)\longrightarrow\left(H,\alpha\right)
is an epimorphism, then
G/Kerf\cong H
.

Proof.

By proposition
5.4
, we have
gKerf=Kerfg
, which implies that
G/Kerf
is a Hom-group.

Define
\overline{f}:G/Kerf\longrightarrow H
, where
xKerf\longmapsto f(x)
.

First, we verify
\overline{f}
is well-defined:

\displaystyle xKerf=yKerf

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle x^{-1}y\in Kerf

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle f(x^{-1}y)=1_{H}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle f(x)^{-1}f(y)=1_{H}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha\left(f(x)\right)\left(f(x)^{-1}f(y)\right)=\alpha(f(x))1=\alpha^{2}(f(x)).

Since
\alpha\left(f(x)\right)\left(f(x)^{-1}f(y)\right)=\left(f(x)f(x)^{-1}\right)\alpha(f(y))=1_{H}\alpha(f(y))=\alpha^{2}(f(y))
, we have
\alpha^{2}(f(x))=\alpha^{2}(f(y))
. Therefore
f(x)=f(y)
, which implies that
\overline{f}(\overline{x})=\overline{f}(\overline{y})
.

Moreover, we need to prove
\overline{f}
is a homomorphism of Hom-groups:
note that

\overline{f}((xKerf)(yKerf))=\overline{f}(xyKerf)=f(xy)=f(x)f(y)=\overline{f}(xKerf)\cdot\overline{f}(yKerf),

\overline{f}(\tilde{\beta}(xKerf))=\overline{f}(\beta(x)Kerf)=f(\beta(x))=\alpha(f(x))=\alpha(\overline{f}(xKerf)),

which implies that
\overline{f}\circ\tilde{\beta}=\alpha\circ\overline{f}
. Hence,
\overline{f}
is a homomorphism of Hom-groups.

Finally, we will prove
\overline{f}
is a bijective map:

(1)
\overline{f}
is a surjection: since
f
is an epimorphism, for any
h\in H
, there exists
x\in G
such that
f(x)=h
, then
\overline{f}(xKerf)=f(x)=h
, hence
\overline{f}
is a surjection.

(2)
\overline{f}
is an injection:
we need to prove
Ker\overline{f}=\{1_{G/Kerf}\}
.

Suppose that
xKerf\in Ker\overline{f}
, then
\overline{f}(xKerf)=1_{H}=f(x)
, that is
f(x)=1_{H}
, so
x\in Kerf
. Therefore,
xKerf=Kerf=Ker\overline{f}=\{1_{G/Kerf}\}
.
∎

6
Hom-group actions

In this section, we introduce a notion of Hom-group action and study some properties of Hom-group actions. To get nice results on Hom-group actions, we need to consider only involutive Hom-groups.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(G,\alpha)
be a Hom-group and
(X,\beta)
be any non-empty Hom-set. An action of a Hom-group
G
on
X
is a map

\theta:~G\times X\to X,~~~~~~\theta(g,x)=gx,

satisfying

1.

1x=\beta(x),~\text{for all}~x\in X
and
1
is the Hom-identity of
G
;

2.

(g_{1}g_{2})x=\alpha(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})x)),~~~\text{for all}~~~g_{1},g_{2}\in G,~x\in X.

Example 6.2
.

Let
(G,\alpha)
be a Hom-group and
(H,\alpha)
be a Hom-subgroup of
G
. Then there is a regular left
H
action on
G

\theta(h,g)=hg
.

The following is an equivalent definition of Hom-group action.

Proposition 6.3
.

A Hom-group
(G,\alpha)
is acting on a Hom-set
(X,\beta)
if and only if there exist a weak Hom-group homomorphism

\psi:G\to\bf{Sym(X)}.

Proof.

First assume that
G
is acting on the Hom-set
(X,\beta)
.
For all
g\in G
, we define

\displaystyle\phi_{g}:X\to X,~~x\mapsto\theta(g,x)=gx.

Note that

\phi_{1}=\beta,~~~\text{and}~~~\phi_{\alpha^{-1}(gg^{-1})}=\phi_{g}\circ\beta^{-1}\circ\phi_{g^{-1}}=\phi_{1}.

This implies
\phi_{g}\circ\beta^{-1}\in\bf{Sym(X)}
. Thus, we have
\phi_{g}\in\bf{Sym(X)}
. Now we define

\displaystyle\psi:~G\to\bf{Sym(X)},~~~g\mapsto\phi_{g}.

We show that
\psi
is a weak Hom-group homomorphism. We have

\displaystyle\psi(1)=\phi_{1}=\beta,

\displaystyle\psi(g_{1}g_{2})(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{g_{1}g_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=(g_{1}g_{2})x=\alpha(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})x))

\displaystyle=\psi(\alpha(g_{1}))\circ\beta^{-1}\circ\psi(\alpha(g_{2}))(x)

Thus,
\alpha_{\beta}\circ\psi(g_{1}g_{2})=\psi(\alpha(g_{1}))\psi(\alpha(g_{2}))
.

Conversely, suppose
\psi:G\to\bf{Sym(X)}
is a weak Hom-group homomorphism. We show there is an action of
G
on
X
. We define

\displaystyle\theta:~

\displaystyle G\times X\to X,

\displaystyle(g,x)\mapsto\theta(g,x)=gx:=\psi(g)(x),~~~\text{for all}~~~g\in G,x\in X.

First observe that
1x=\theta(1,x)=\psi(1)(x)=\beta(x)
. We also have

\displaystyle(g_{1}g_{2})x

\displaystyle=\theta(g_{1}g_{2},x)

\displaystyle=\psi(g_{1}g_{2})(x)

\displaystyle=\psi(\alpha(g_{1}))\circ\beta^{-1}\circ\psi(\alpha(g_{2}))(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})x))

Thus, there is an action of
G
on
X
.
∎

Definition 6.4
.

Let
(X,\beta)
be a Hom-set equipped with a Hom-group action of
(G,\alpha)
. Suppose
x
is an element of
X
. We define an orbit of
x
, denoted by
G^{\alpha}(x)
as follows:

\displaystyle G^{\alpha}(x)=\{\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)x)\mid g\in G\}.

The stabilizer of
x
is defined by

\displaystyle G^{\alpha}_{x}=\{g\in G\mid\alpha(g)x=\beta(x)\}.

Note that when Hom-group
(G,\alpha=id)
acts on the Hom-set
(X,\beta=id)
, we get the usual orbit and stabilizer of
x
corresponding to a group action.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose
(G,\alpha)
is an involutive Hom-group acting on a non-empty Hom-set
(X,\beta)
. Then the stabilizer of
x
is a Hom-subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

As
(G,\alpha)
is an involutive Hom-group, we have
\alpha^{2}=\alpha
. First we need to check that
\alpha(G_{x}^{\alpha})\subseteq G_{x}^{\alpha}
. Suppose
g\in G_{x}^{\alpha}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\alpha(\alpha(g))x=\alpha^{2}(g)x=\alpha(g)x=\beta(x).

Thus,
\alpha(g)\in G^{\alpha}_{x}
. This implies
\alpha(G_{x}^{\alpha})\subseteq G_{x}^{\alpha}
.
Note that
1\in G^{\alpha}_{x}
as
1x=\beta(x)
.
Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in G^{\alpha}_{x}
. We have

\displaystyle\alpha(g_{1}g_{2})x

\displaystyle=(\alpha(g_{1})\alpha(g_{2}))x

\displaystyle=\alpha(\alpha(g_{1}))(\beta^{-1}\alpha(\alpha(g_{2}))x)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}\alpha^{2}(g_{2})x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}\alpha(g_{2})x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(g_{1})((\beta^{-1}\beta)x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(g_{1})x

\displaystyle=\beta(x).

Therefore,
g_{1}g_{2}\in G^{\alpha}_{x}
. Suppose
g\in G^{\alpha}_{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\beta(x)=1x=(g^{-1}g)x=\alpha(g^{-1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)x))=\alpha(g^{-1})x.

Thus,
g^{-1}\in G_{x}^{\alpha}
. Therefore,
G^{\alpha}_{x}
is a Hom-subgroup of
G
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

(Orbit-Stabilizer theorem)
Let
(G,\alpha)
be a finite involutive Hom-group. Suppose the Hom-group
G
is acting on non-empty Hom-set
(X,\beta)
. Then for all
x\in X
,

\displaystyle\mid G\mid=\mid G^{\alpha}_{x}\mid~\mid G^{\alpha}(x)\mid.

Proof.

We define a map

\displaystyle\phi:~

\displaystyle\frac{G}{G^{\alpha}_{x}}\to G^{\alpha}(x),~~~\phi(gG_{x}^{\alpha}):=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)x)~~~.

We show that
\phi
is a bijection.

Suppose
\phi(g_{1}G_{x}^{\alpha})=\phi(g_{2}G_{x}^{\alpha})
. We have

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{1})x)=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})x),

\displaystyle\alpha(g^{-1}_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{1})x))=\alpha(g^{-1}_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})x)),

\displaystyle(g^{-1}_{1}g_{1})x=(g^{-1}_{1}g_{2})x,

\displaystyle ex=(g^{-1}_{1}g_{2})x,

\displaystyle\beta(x)=\alpha(\alpha^{-1}(g^{-1}_{1}g_{2}))x.

This implies
\alpha^{-1}(g^{-1}_{1}g_{2})\in G_{x}^{\alpha}
. As
G_{x}^{\alpha}
is a Hom-subgroup,
g^{-1}_{1}g_{2}\in G_{x}^{\alpha}
. This implies

g_{1}G_{x}^{\alpha}=g_{2}G_{x}^{\alpha}.

Thus,
\phi
is one-one. Clearly,
\phi
is onto as
\phi(gG_{x}^{\alpha})=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)x)
. Therefore,
\phi
is a bijection.
∎

Suppose Hom-group
(G,\alpha)
is acting on a Hom-set
(X,\beta)
. We define a relation
\sim
on
X
as
x\sim y
if
x=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)y)
for some
g\in G
.

Note that relation
\sim
is reflexive as
x=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(1)x)
. Suppose
x\sim y
, this implies,
x=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)y)
for some
g\in G
. Observe that

\displaystyle\alpha(g^{-1})x=\alpha(g^{-1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g)y)),

\displaystyle\alpha(g^{-1})x=(g^{-1}g)y=1y=\beta(y),

\displaystyle y=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g^{-1})x).

This implies
y\sim x
. Thus, the relation
\sim
is symmetric.

Suppose
x\sim y
and
y\sim z
. We show
x\sim z
. For some
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, we have

\displaystyle x=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{1})y),~~~y=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})z).

Observe that,

\displaystyle x=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{1})(\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})z))=\beta^{-1}((g_{1}g_{2})z)=\beta^{-1}(\alpha(\alpha^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}))z).

This implies the relation is transitive.
Thus, the relation
\sim
is an equivalence relation. Hence, the relation
\sim
partition
X
into its equivalence classes. Note that an equivalence class is nothing but an orbit
G^{\alpha}(x)
for some
x\in X
. Being an equivalence class, orbits partitions Hom-set
X
into pairwise disjoint subsets.

7
First Sylow theorem for Hom-groups

In this final section, we prove first Sylow theorem for Hom groups using Hom-group actions.

Lemma 7.1
.

[
10
]

Let
p
be a prime number and
k\geq 0
and
m\geq 1
be integers. Then

\displaystyle{p^{k}m\choose p^{k}}\equiv m~~~\text{mod p}.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
(G,\alpha)
be an involutive finite Hom-group, and let
p
be a prime divisor of the order of
G
. Then
G
has a
p
-sylow Hom-subgroup.

Proof.

Let
\mid G\mid=p^{k}m
, where
k\geq 0
and
p
does not divide
m
. Let
S
be the collection of all subsets of
G
having number of elements
p^{k}
.
Define a bijective map on
S
as follows:

\beta:S\to S,~~~\beta(A)=\alpha(A)=\{\alpha(a)\mid a\in A\}.

Thus,
(S,\beta)
is a Hom-set.
Now we first show
G
acts on
S
by regular left multiplication.

\displaystyle\theta:

\displaystyle~G\times S\to S,

\displaystyle(g,A)\mapsto gA=\{ga\mid a\in A\}.

We have

1.

1A=\{1a\mid a\in A\}=\{\alpha(a)\mid a\in A\}=\alpha(A)=\beta(A)
,

2.

(g_{1}g_{2})A=\{(g_{1}g_{2})a\mid a\in A\}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(g_{2})A=\{\alpha(g_{2})a\mid a\in A\},

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})A)=\{\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})a)\mid a\in A\}=\{g_{2}\alpha^{-1}(a)\mid a\in A\},

\displaystyle\alpha(g_{1})\beta^{-1}(\alpha(g_{2})A)=\{\alpha(g_{1})(g_{2}\alpha^{-1}(a))\mid a\in A\}=\{(g_{1}g_{2})a\mid a\in A\}.

Therefore,
G
acts on
S
by regular left multiplication. This action partition
S
into disjoint orbits and
|S|
is the sum of the orbit sizes. Using Lemma
7.1
,

\displaystyle|S|={p^{k}m\choose p^{k}}\equiv m\neq 0~~~\text{(mod p)}.

This means
|S|
is not divisible by
p
. Hence, there is some orbit
G^{\alpha}(X)
such that
|G^{\alpha}(X)|
is not divisible by
p
.

Suppose
Y\in G^{\alpha}(X)
and
H=G_{Y}^{\alpha}
be the stabilizer of
Y
in
G
. From the orbit-stabilizer theorem,

\displaystyle|G^{\alpha}(X)|=\frac{|G|}{|G_{Y}^{\alpha}|}.

Since
p^{k}~\mid|G|
and
p~\nmid|G^{\alpha}(X)|
, this implies,
p^{k}~|~|G_{Y}^{\alpha}|
. Thus,
p^{k}
divides
|H|
. This implies
p^{k}\leq|H|
. Since
H
is a stabilizer of
Y
under left multiplication, if
y\in Y
, then
Hy\subseteq Y
. Now,

\displaystyle|H|=|Hy|\leq|Y|=p^{k}.

Therefore,
|H|=p^{k}
. As stabilizer
H
is a Hom-subgroup of
G
of highest power of
p
,
H
is a
p
-Sylow Hom-subgroup of
G
.
∎
