Random commuting matrices
John E. M c Carthy

Abstract
We define a random commuting d d -tuple of n n -by- n n matrices to be a random variable that takes values in the set of commuting d d -tuples and has a distribution that is a rapidly decaying continuous weight on this algebraic set. In the Hermitian case, we characterize the eigenvalue distribution, and derive the limit as n n tends to infinity for a Gaussian weight. In the non-Hermitian case, we get a formula that holds if the set is irreducible. We show that there are qualitative differences between the single matrix case and the several commuting matrices case. † † footnotetext: AMS Subject Class: 60B20, 15B52.

The field has many applications in mathematics and theoretical physics.
Typically the entries of the matrix are chosen independently from each other (or for Hermitian matrices the upper triangular entries are chosen independently) according to some probabilitly distribution, and then one tries to characterize the distribution of specific properties of the matrix, such as the eigenvalues, or the norm, or the singular values, both in the finite case and in the limit as the size of the matrix goes to infinity. The best understood case in random matrix theory is when the entries of the matrix are chosen i.i.d. from a Gaussian distribution.

What would it mean to choose random matrices subject to some algebraic constraint?
For a specific example, what would it mean to choose a random pair
(X,Y)
of commuting
n
-by-
n
matrices?
One approach would be to first choose
X
by some random method, and then to choose something in its commutant.
Generically,
X
will have
n
different eigenvalues, and
Y
will then have the same eigenvectors, so it would only be necessary to describe the distribution of the eigenvalues of
Y
. But what should that distribution be? Should it depend on the eigenvalues of
X
? And should the matrices
X
be chosen in such a way that ones with a ‘larger’ commutant are more heavily weighted?
If the possible entries of
X
and
Y
came from a large but finite set, one could in principle choose a pair independently, and then
keep it if and only if the pair commutes. But if the distribution is continuous, then the probability that any given pair would commute is zero, and one does not want to condition on a zero-probability event.

The purpose of this note is first to describe a systematic way to make sense of the notion of a random
d
-tuple of matrices that
satisfies certain algebraic relations, and which can reduce to the i.i.d. Gaussian model if the relations are absent.
Secondly, we shall explore this theory in the particular case of random commuting
d
-tuples. In the Hermitian case, we can derive the limiting distribution for a Gaussian weight; in the non-Hermitian case our results are far less complete.

1.

Large random Hermitian matrices were studied by Wigner and Dyson as models for the Hamiltonian of a complicated system with many weakly interacting parts, such as heavy atoms, and the eigenvalues were the possible energy levels.
If one wished to study a collection of

2.

Compact Lie groups have Haar measure as a natural probability measure. The construction in Subsection

2
Main results

In this note, we shall consider random commuting
d
-tuples.
There are two natural cases to consider, the Hermitian and the general case, which we denote by

{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
and
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
.
Since commuting matrices have common eigenvectors,
the eigenvalues of an element of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
are points in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and for an element of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
they are points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We would like to understand how they are distributed, both for fixed
n
and as
n
tends to infinity.

For an alternative approach, where the matrices are not required to commute exactly but are penalized for having large commutators, see the recent paper by Guionnet and Maurel-Segala
[
22
]
.

2.1
Main results: Hermitian case

We only consider weights which are unitarily invariant, i.e.
w(UXU^{*})=w(X)
.
We shall call unitarily invariant functions
class functions
.
By Proposition
4.2
, with probability
1
an
X
in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
will have exactly
n
distinct eigenvalues.
Since the unitary orbits of
X
in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
are uniquely determined by their eigenvalues, we can define a
function
{\tilde{w}}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
by
{\tilde{w}}(\lambda)=w(X)
, where
X
is any element of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
that has
\lambda

as its eigenvalues. Writing
\lambda=\lambda_{j}^{r}
for
1\leq j\leq n
and
1\leq r\leq d
, the order of the
j

but the order of the
r

Our first theorem is a

We write

to denote the Euclidean distance between
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{j}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Theorem
4.5

Let

be a continuous integrable class function,
and assume
\int_{{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}}w(X)dX=1
.
Let random matrices in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
be chosen with distribution

.
Then the density of their eigenvalues is given by

\rho_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ {\beta_{n}}\ {\tilde{w}}(\lambda)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

(2.1)

Note that the repulsion between the eigenvalues is the square of the Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, not of the components.

Using Johansson’s large deviation estimate
[
30

, we show that as

the distribution tends to the equilibrium measure for the logarithmic energy. These have been described explicitly for

a certain range of
n
when
d=3
(see Subsection
5.1
for details). We restrict our attention to the irreducible case, which happens when the elements with
n
eigenvalues are generic.
In this case we can write a generic
X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
as

X\ =\ UQU^{*}\ =\ UADA^{-1}U^{*},

where
U
is a unitary in
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
,
Q
is an upper triangular commuting
d
-tuple,

D
is a diagonal
d
-tuple and
A
is an upper triangular unipotent matrix.

In Proposition
5.3
we describe how to take advantage of the unitary invariance of
w_{n}
to decompose

w_{n}(X)dX
as
w_{n}(Q)\kappa(Q)dQd\nu
, where
\nu
is the invariant measure on
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}

and
\kappa
is a function described in Subsection
5.2
.

In Theorem

we describe how to get the eigenvalue distribution of a random element in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
by integrating with respect to both
U
and
A
.
The formula is unfortunately complicated. In Theorem
5.5
we calculate the distribution explicitly for commuting
d
-tuples of
2

2

Theorem
5.5
:

Let
X
be a random element of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{2}
chosen with distribution

w(X)\ =\ Ce^{-\gamma\|X\|_{F}^{2}}.

Then the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
X
in
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{2}
have distribution

\rho^{d}(\lambda)\ =\ C^{\prime}_{d}\ e^{-\gamma(|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2})}|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{1}{(d-1-j)!}\frac{2^{j}}{\gamma^{j+1}}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2j}}.

For

we get respectively

\displaystyle\rho^{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle C_{1}^{\prime}e^{-\gamma(|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2})}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\rho^{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle C_{2}^{\prime}e^{-\gamma(|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2})}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\rho^{3}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle C_{3}^{\prime}e^{-\gamma(|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2})}\left[\frac{1}{2\gamma}|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{4}{\gamma^{3}}|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{-2}\right].

Notice that, unlike in the Hermitian case or the case

3
Known results

3.1
The Gaussian Unitary Ensemble

The Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (or GUE) is the matrix ensemble in which the strictly upper triangular entries

(\xi_{ij})_{i<j}
are chosen i.i.d.
N_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
(ie with a complex normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
), the diagonal entries
\xi_{ii}
are chosen independently from
N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1)
, and the
lower triangular entries are defined by
\xi_{ji}=\overline{\xi_{ij}}
.
This gives an ensemble of Hermitian matrices, parametrized by
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
. The probability measure
is given by

C_{n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|X_{ij}|^{2}}dm(X)

(3.1)

where
m
is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
, and
C_{n}
is the appropriate normalizing constant.

The Ginibre formula
[
21
]
asserts that the density function of the eigenvalues
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\rho_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ C_{n}^{\prime}e^{-|\lambda|^{2}/2}\prod_{1\leq 1<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

(3.2)

The Wigner semi-circular law says that if one defines a measure by putting a point mass of weight
1/n
at each eigenvalue of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X
then these measures converge weakly to the semi-circular distribution on
[-2,2]
given by

\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}dx.

See e.g.

[
13
, Thm. 6.96 and Prop. 6.156]

for a proof.

If instead of choosing each matrix entry independently one chooses Hermitian
matrices with a distribution that replaces (
3.1
) by

C_{n}e^{-{\rm tr}(Q(X))}dX,

where
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{\alpha}+O(|x|^{\alpha-1})
for some positive constants
\alpha,\gamma
, then the Ginibre formula still holds, becoming

\rho_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ C_{n}^{\prime}e^{Q(\lambda)}\prod_{1\leq 1<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}

[
13
, Thm. 5.22]
. The semi-circle distribution is replaced by the equilibrium measure for
Q

[
13
, Thm. 6.96]
(see Subsection
3.2
for a definition of equilibrium measure).

3.2
Equilibrium measures for the logarithmic potential

Let
Q(x)
be an external field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a non-negative continous function that goes to infinity
like some positive power of
|x|
.
For
\mu
a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the logarithmic energy by

I^{Q}(\mu)\ =\ \int\int\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+\int Q(x)d\mu(x).

(3.3)

The equilibrium measure for
Q
is the unique probability measure
\mu^{Q}
that minimizes

I^{Q}(\mu)
(for existence and uniqueness, see
[
7
, Thm. 4.4.14]
).

Let us restrict to the case
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{\alpha}
.
When
\alpha=2
, the equilibrium measures are known for all
d
.
Let
\sigma^{d}
be area measure on the unit sphere
S^{d}
, normalized to have total mass
1
,
and
\sigma^{d}_{R}
be normalized area measure on the sphere of radius
R
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{2}
. Then the equilibrium measure is given by

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi R_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{(R_{1}^{2}-x^{2})_{+}}\ dx,

\displaystyle\quad R_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=1;

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi R_{2}^{2}}1_{|x|<R_{2}}\ dx^{1}dx^{2},

\displaystyle R_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=2;

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}R_{3}^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-|x|^{2}}}1_{|x|<R_{3}}\ drd\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle R_{3}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=3;

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sigma^{d-1}_{R_{d}},

\displaystyle R_{d}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}},

\displaystyle d\geq 4.

(3.7)

Result (
3.4
) is the semi-circle law, first obtained by Wigner
[
47
]
.
For other values of
\alpha
when
d=1
, see
[
13
, Sec. 6.7]
.
Result (
3.5
) is the circular law for random normal matrices; it was proved by Elbau and Felder
in
[
19
]
;
for another proof see
[
42
]
.
Results (
3.6
) and (

) are due to Chafai, Saff and Womersley
[
8
,
9
]
.
Among other results they also show that the equilibrium for all
\alpha\geq 2
and all
d\geq 4
is given by
\sigma^{d-1}_{(1/\gamma\alpha)^{1/\alpha}}
.

The Hoffman-Weilandt inequality

says that if we let
\mu_{j}(A)
denote the

largest eigenvalue of the self-adjoint matrix

, then
for any self-adjoint

4
Random commuting Hermitian matrices

4.1
Generic
d
-tuples

In this section, we shall study random
d
-tuples in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
.
All matrices in this section are Hermitian, and dimension always means real dimension.
First we shall prove that the set of
X
for which any of the matrices has a repeated eigenvalue is of lower dimension than
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
, so will have measure
0
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
Y
be a self-adjoint matrix. We shall say
Y
has banner
(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{p})
, where

r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\dots\geq r_{p}\geq 1
are integers, if
Y
has
p
distinct eigenvalues, with multiplcities
r_{1},\dots,r_{p}
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(d,n)
denote the complex Grassmanian of all
d
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It has dimension
2d(n-d)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
d\geq 2
, the set
\{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}:\ {\rm banner}(X^{1})=(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\}

has dimension

n^{2}+(d-2)n+p
.

Proof.

We shall prove this by induction on
d
.
First, let
d=1
.
To choose a matrix with banner
(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})
, one can first choose an
r_{1}
dimensional subspace, and the first eigenvalue, then an
r_{2}
dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n-r_{1}}

and the second eigenvalue, and so on. This yields a space of dimension

2r_{1}(n-r_{1})+2r_{2}(n-r_{1}-r_{2})+\dots+2r_{p-1}(n-r_{1}-\dots-r_{p-1})+p\\
\begin{aligned} &=\ 2n(n-r_{p})-2\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}r_{i}r_{j}+p\\
&=\ 2n(n-r_{p})-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}r_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}r_{i}^{2}+p\\
&=\ 2n(n-r_{p})-(n-r_{p})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{2}+r_{p}^{2}+p\\
&=\ n^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{2}+p.\end{aligned}

(4.1)

Note that
n^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{2}+p
is always
\leq n^{2}
, and equals
n^{2}
if each
r_{i}
is
1
(which means
p=n
).

Now assume
d\geq 2
,
and that we have shown that if
d^{\prime}<d
, the dimension of

{\mathfrak{C}}^{d^{\prime},H}_{n}
is
n^{2}+(d^{\prime}-1)n
. Note that (
4.1
) proves this assertion for
d^{\prime}=1
,
and if
d^{\prime}>1
it follows from the inductive hypothesis,
since
the maximum value that

n^{2}+(d^{\prime}-2)n+p
can attain is
n^{2}+(d^{\prime}-1)n
, which occurs when
p=n
.
To calculate the dimension of
\{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}:\ {\rm banner}(X^{1})=(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\}
, note that once
X^{1}
has been chosen, then the dimension of
\{X^{2},\dots,X^{d}\}

is
\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\rm dim}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d-1,H}_{r_{i}})
. By the inductive hypothesis, this gives

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}:\ {\rm banner}(X^{1})=(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle n^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{2}+p+\sum_{i=1}^{p}(r_{i}^{2}+(d-2)r_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{2}+(d-2)n+p.

This proves the proposition for
d
, and in particular shows that the dimension of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}

is
n^{2}+(d-1)n
.
∎

It follows from Proposition
4.2
that the set of
X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}

for which each
X^{r}
has
n
distinct eigenvalues is of full measure. Let us call these the generic elements, and write

{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}\ =\ \{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}:\ {\rm each\ }X^{r}\ {\rm has\ }n\ {\rm distinct\ eigenvalues}\}.

Note that at any point where the eigenvalues of
X
are distinct (in particular
at the generic elements) the algebraic set
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
is smooth,
since coordinates can be given by specifying an orthonormal frame for the eigenvectors and a choice of corresponding eigenvalues.

We could choose to order the eigenvalues of
X
, which are points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by, for example, putting the eigenvalues in dictionary order. However, we will not do this, and
so the eigenvalues of elements of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}
will be invariant under the symmetric group

\mathfrak{S}(n)
, and when we list them as
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
this is with some random ordering.

4.2
Ginibre formula

Next we prove the
d
-dimensional version of the Ginibre formula (
3.2
).
Both the proof in
[
42
, Sec. 2.6.1]
and in

[
13
, Thm. 5.22]
of the one dimensional case can be adapted to
d
dimensions, though some care must be
taken since the tangent space to
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
can no longer be naturally identified with
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
.
In the
d
-variable case, the eigenvalues of an elements of
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
will be points in

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
, and we will write them as
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
where each
\lambda_{i}
is a
d
-tuple

\lambda_{i}=(\lambda_{i}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{i}^{d})
.

If
w
is a real-valued function on
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}
that is invariant under the action of the unitary group
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
(ie.
w(X)=w(U^{*}XU)
for all
U\in{\mathcal{U}}(n)
) then
w(X)

can only depend on the eigenvalues of
X
, since they completely determine the unitary equivalence class of
X
. Such functions are called
class functions.

If
w
is a class function, there
is a function
{\tilde{w}}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is invariant with respect to the action of

\mathfrak{S}(n)
so that

w(X)\ =\ {\tilde{w}}(\lambda),

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
is some ordering of the eigenvalues of
X
.
Moreover, if
w
is continuous, so is
{\tilde{w}}
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we shall use
|x|
to mean the Euclidean norm
\sqrt{\sum_{r=1}^{d}|x^{r}|^{2}}
.
We shall write
dX
to mean
d{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm dim}{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}}(X)
.
We shall use the subscript
F
for the Frobenius (or Hilbert-Schmidt) norm on
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
, viz.

\|X\|_{F}^{2}\ =\ \sum_{r=1}^{d}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|X_{ij}^{r}|^{2}.

Let
v(k)
denote the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Conjugating a diagonal
d
-tuple by a unitary gives the most general Hermitian
d
-tuple, but
diagonal unitaries have no effect, so we must understand
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}
, where we identify a point
t\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
,
the set of
n
-tuples of unimodular complex numbers, with the diagonal unitary
D_{t}
that has the entries of
t
on the diagonal.
We shall write
1_{n}
for the identity
in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
.
The tangent space at the identity of
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
is the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}(n)
which consists of the skew-Hermitian matrices (matrices satisfying
S^{*}=-S
). Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}
denote the subspace of skew-Hermitian matrices that are
0
on the diagonal.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
g:{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}\to{\mathcal{U}}(n)
be defined by
g(S,t)=e^{S}D_{t}
. Then
dg:{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathfrak{u}(n)
is an isomorphism between the tangent space of
g:{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}

at
(0,1_{n})
and the tangent space of
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
at the identity.

Proof.

Note that

dg|_{(0,1_{n})}:(S,\theta)\ \mapsto\ S+D_{i\theta},

and that this map is an isomorphism.
∎

In particular, by the inverse function theorem one can parametrize a neighborhood of
[\mathbb{T}^{n}]
in

{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}
as
\{e^{S}\}
where
S
ranges over a neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}
.
Define a constant
{\beta_{n}}
by

{\beta_{n}}\ =\ \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{{\rm vol}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}}\{S:S\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n},\|S\|_{F}<\varepsilon\}}{{\rm vol}_{{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}}\{e^{S}:S\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n},\|S\|_{F}<\varepsilon\}}.

(4.2)

The number
\frac{1}{{\beta_{n}}}
is the Jacobian determinant of
e^{S}
, normalized by the requirement that the total Haar measure of
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
is
1
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}
. The tangent space
T_{X}{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
is the set
Z=(Z^{1},\dots,Z^{d})

of
d
-tuples of self-adjoint matrices that satisfy

[X^{r},Z^{s}]\ =\ [X^{s},Z^{r}]\qquad\forall\ 1\leq r\neq s\leq d.

(4.3)

If
e_{j}
is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
X
with corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
,
then with respect to this basis equations (
4.3
) become

Z^{s}_{ij}(\lambda_{j}^{r}-\lambda_{i}^{r})\ =\ Z^{r}_{ij}(\lambda_{j}^{s}-\lambda_{i}^{s})\qquad\forall\ 1\leq r\neq s\leq d.

(4.4)

This result has been known for a long time—we do not know where it first appeared.
For the convenience of the reader, we include the proof from

[
1
, Lemma 7.3]
. (One could also
adapt the proof of Lemma
5.2
below).

Proof.

We want to show that there exists a
C^{1}
curve
X(t)
of commuting self-adjoints
with
X(0)=X
and

X^{\prime}(0)=Z
if and only if (
4.4
) holds.

(
\Rightarrow
): If
X(t)=X+tZ+o(t)
is commutative, calculate

[X^{r}(t),X^{s}(t)]\ =\ t\left([X^{r},Z^{s}]-[X^{s},Z^{r}]\right)+o(t).

The coefficient of
t
must vanish, giving (
4.4
).

(
\Leftarrow
):
Suppose
(
4.4
) holds. This means

Z^{s}_{ij}(\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i})\ =\ Z^{r}_{ij}(\lambda^{s}_{j}-\lambda^{s}_{i})\qquad\forall\,r\neq s,

so

Z^{r}_{ij}\frac{1}{\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}}\ =\ Z^{s}_{ij}\frac{1}{\lambda^{s}_{j}-\lambda^{s}_{i}}\qquad{\rm if\ }\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}\neq 0\neq\lambda^{s}_{j}-\lambda^{s}_{i}.

(4.5)

Define a skew-selfadjoint matrix
Y
by

Y_{ij}\ =\ Z^{r}_{ij}\frac{1}{\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}}\qquad{\rm for\ any\ }r\ {\rm such\ that\ }\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}\neq 0.

(4.6)

For any
i\neq j
, there is some
r
with

\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}\neq 0
, so (
4.6
) defines
Y_{ij}
;
and (
4.5
)
says it doesn’t matter which
r
we choose.
Let all the diagonal terms of
Y
be
0
.

Define

X^{r}(t)\ =\ e^{tY}(X^{r}+t\ {\rm diag}(Z^{r}))e^{-tY}.

Since
e^{tY}
is a unitary matrix and
X^{r}+t\ {\rm diag}(Z^{r})
is diagonal,
X(t)\in CXAM_{n}^{d}
and

\frac{d}{dt}X^{r}(t)|_{t=0}=[Y,X^{r}]+{\rm diag}(Z^{r})=Z^{r}.

∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
w:{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous integrable class function,
and assume
\int_{{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}}w(X)dX=1
.
Let random matrices in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
be chosen with distribution
w(X)dX
.
Then the density of their eigenvalues is given by

\rho_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ {\beta_{n}}\ {\tilde{w}}(\lambda)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

(4.7)

where
{\beta_{n}}
is given by (
4.2
).

The theorem asserts that if we wish to integrate some class function with respect
to
w(X)dX
, we can first integrate over
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}
, and then integrate over the eigenvalues.
This gives the decomposition

w(X)dX\ =\ \rho_{n}(\lambda)d\nu d\lambda

(4.8)

where
d\lambda
is Lebesgue measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
and
d\nu
is volume measure on
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}
, with

\rho_{n}
given by (
4.7
). Notice that the repulsion between the eigenvalues is based on their Euclidean separation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
E_{ij}
denote the matrix which is
1
in the
(i,j)
entry and
0
elsewhere.

Proof.

The idea is to decompose
w(X)dX
as in (
4.8
).

As every element in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
is unitarily equivalent to a diagonal tuple, it is sufficient to look at
a neighborhood of a diagonal tuple.
Fix some orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and choose a diagonal tuple
D
in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}
with diagonal entries
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
. Let
\varepsilon
be a sufficiently small positive number,
and consider
\{X:\|X-D\|_{F}<\varepsilon\}
.

By the Hoffman-Wielandt inequality (
3.8
), the eigenvalues of
X

can be ordered as
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})

where
\kappa_{j}
is the eigenvalue of
X
closest to
\lambda_{j}
, and

\sum_{r=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\kappa_{j}^{r}-\lambda_{j}^{r}|^{2}\ \leq\ \|X-D\|^{2}_{F}\ <\ \varepsilon^{2}.

By Lemma
4.3
, we can parametrize a small neighborhood of
D
by

X\ =\ G(\theta,S)\ =\ e^{S}(D+D_{\theta})e^{-S}

where
S
ranges over a neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}
and
\theta
over a neighborhood
of
0
in
{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}
. The derivative
dG
at
(0,0)
is the map between the tangent spaces

\displaystyle dG:{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}

\displaystyle\ \to\

\displaystyle T_{D}{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}

\displaystyle(\theta,S)

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\

\displaystyle SD-DS+D_{\theta}.

To calculate the Jacobian determinant of
G
,
let
R_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{ij}-E_{ji})
and

S_{ij}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{ij}+E_{ji})
.
Then we can use the real coordinates
\theta
for
{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}

and
\{R_{i,j},S_{ij}:i<j\}
for
{\mathcal{A}}_{0,n}
.
We have

\displaystyle dG:(\theta,0)

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle dG:(0,R_{ij})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle-i(\lambda_{j}^{r}-\lambda_{i}^{r})S_{ij}

\displaystyle dG:(0,S_{ij})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle i(\lambda_{j}^{r}-\lambda_{i}^{r})R_{ij}.

Therefore the Jacobian of
G
is

\sqrt{|\det(dG^{*}dG)|}\ =\ \prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

This gives

dX\ =\ \prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\ d\theta dS.

By Lemma
4.3
,
dS
is locally
{\beta_{n}}d\nu
.
Thus we have shown that

w(X)dX={\beta_{n}}{\tilde{w}}(\lambda)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}d\lambda d\nu,

which gives (
4.7
).
∎

Remark 4.6
.

As the constant
{\beta_{n}}
depends only on the unitary group
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
and not on
d
or
w
,
it can be calculated in a number of ways; see e.g.
[
21
,
42
]
.

4.3
Density of eigenvalues

In Theorem
4.5
we gave a formula for the joint distribution of eigenvalues in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
. Now we ask what is the density of eigenvalues in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as
n\to\infty
. This will depend on the equilibrium measure of
\log{\tilde{w}}
, and choosing the correct scaling.
We shall restrict ourselves to weights of the following form, where
\alpha,\gamma>0
:

Q(x)\ =\ \gamma(\sum_{r=1}^{d}|x^{r}|^{2})^{\alpha/2}.

(4.9)

The following theorem is a special case of
[
7
, Thm. 4.4.14]
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
Q
be as in (
4.9
), and let the logarithmic energy be defined as in
(
3.3
). Then there is a unique probability measure
\mu^{Q}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
\inf I^{Q}(\mu)

is attained, and moreover
\mu^{Q}
is compactly supported.

We call
\mu^{Q}
the equilibrium measure (for
Q
) and set

E^{Q}\ =\ I^{Q}(\mu^{Q}).

We define
w_{n}
on
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
by

w_{n}(X)\ =\ C_{n}\ e^{-{\rm tr}\ Q(X)},

where
C_{n}
is a normalizing constant to ensure

\int_{{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}}w_{n}(X)dX=1
.
If
X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n,{\rm gen}}
and
\lambda
is some ordering of the eigenvalues, then

{\tilde{w}}_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ C_{n}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(\lambda_{j})}.

Let
\rho=\rho_{n}
be as in (
4.7
), so

\rho_{n}(x)\ =\ {\beta_{n}}C_{n}\,\,e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(x_{j})}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}.

To find the distribution of a single eigenvalue, we must integrate
out the other
n-1
eigenvalues. First we rescale by
y=n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}x
, defining a new probability density on

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
by

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)\ =\ D_{n}\,e^{-n\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(y_{j})}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|y_{i}-y_{j}|^{2}.

(4.10)

Here
D_{n}
is a normalizing constant to make
\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)dy=1
.
It is related to the previous constants by

D_{n}\ =\ \left(n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)^{n^{2}+(d-1)n}\ {\beta_{n}}C_{n}.

If
\phi
is a bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define an expectation by

{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\phi)\ :=\ \int_{{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)dy.

The principal result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
Q
be as in
4.9
, and let
\phi
be a bounded and continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\phi)\ =\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(x)d\mu^{Q}(x).

(4.11)

Theorem
4.8
was proved
in the
d=1
case by K. Johansson
[
30
]
,
and his proof generalizes to the
d
-dimensional case.
For the convenience of the reader we shall give a complete proof, following the exposition
in Section 6.4 of the beautiful monograph
[
13
]
, but we emphasize that generalizing to
d
dimensions
requires little more than notational changes.

We start with a large deviation estimate, which for
d=1
is Lemma 4.2 in
[
30
]

or Lemma 6.67 in
[
13
]
.
Our proof is a straightforward generalization, but since the proof that Claim
4.13
holds
must be modified for
d\geq 2
, we include the whole proof for completeness.

Define, for
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,

k(s,t)\ :=\ \log\frac{1}{|s-t|}+\frac{1}{2}(Q(s)+Q(t)),

and
for
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}
,

\displaystyle K_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}k(y_{i},y_{j})

(4.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log|y_{i}-y_{j}|^{-2}+(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(y_{i}).

Let

A_{n,\eta}\ :=\ \{y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}:\frac{1}{n^{2}}K_{n}(y)\leq E^{Q}+\eta\}.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)
be the probability density given by (
4.10
).
Let
\eta,a>0
.
Then there exists
n_{0}
which depends on
\eta
but not on
a
so that

\int_{{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\setminus A_{n,\eta+a}}1\ d{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\ \leq\ e^{-an^{2}},\quad n\geq n_{0}.

Proof.

Assume
d\geq 2
, as the
d=1
case is proved in
[
30
]
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<\eta/2
.
For
\delta>0
, let

\psi_{\delta}(y)\ =\ \frac{1}{v(d)\delta^{d}}\int_{B(y,\delta)}d\mu^{Q}(x),

where
v(d)
denotes the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
B(y,\delta)
means the ball centered at
y
of radius
\delta
.
Then
\psi_{\delta}
is a compactly supported function, and

\psi_{\delta}(y)dy
tends vaguely to
\mu^{Q}
as
\delta\to 0
.
Warning:
\psi_{\delta}
need not be continuous, for example if
\mu^{Q}
puts mass on a sphere of radius
\delta
.

Claim: For
\delta
sufficiently small, we have

I^{Q}(\psi_{\delta})\ \leq\ E^{Q}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(4.13)

Proof of Claim:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log|x-y|\psi_{\delta}(x)\psi_{\delta}(y)dxdy

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int\int\left[\frac{1}{v(d)^{2}\delta^{2d}}\int_{B(0,\delta)}\int_{B(0,\delta)}\log|x-y+s-t|\ dsdt\right]d\mu^{Q}(x)d\mu^{Q}(y).

Since
d\geq 2
, the function
\log|x|
is subharmonic.
Applying subharmonicity of
\log|x|
twice, we get

\frac{1}{v(d)^{2}\delta^{2d}}\int_{B(0,\delta)}\int_{B(0,\delta)}\log|x-y+s-t|\ dsdt\ \geq\ \log|x-y|.

Therefore we conclude

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\psi_{\delta}(x)\psi_{\delta}(y)dxdy\ \leq\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu^{Q}(x)d\mu^{Q}(y).

As

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int Q(x)\psi_{\delta}(x)dx\ =\ \int Q(x)d\mu^{Q}(x),

we have proved the claim.

The rest of the proof proceeds exactly as in
[
13
]
.
Fix
\delta
so that (
4.13
) holds. Let
E
be the compact support of
\psi_{\delta}
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}e^{-K_{n}(y)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(y_{i})}dy

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{E^{n}}e^{-K_{n}(y)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\psi_{\delta}(y_{i})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\delta}(y_{i})dy.

Fix some positive
\delta
for which (
4.13
) holds.
Applying Jensen’s inequality to the probability measure
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\delta}(y_{i})dy
we get

\displaystyle\log D_{n}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\int\left(K_{n}(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(y_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\psi_{\delta}(y_{i})\right)\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\delta}(y_{i})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n(n-1)I^{Q}(\psi_{\delta})+n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Q(x)dx+n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\log\psi_{\delta}(x))\psi_{\delta}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{2}I^{Q}(\psi_{\delta})+O(n)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{2}(E^{Q}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})+O(n).

Therefore there exists
n_{1}
so that if
n\geq n_{1}
, then

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\log D_{n}\ \leq\ E^{Q}+\varepsilon.

So for
n\geq n_{1}
, and recalling that
\varepsilon<\eta/2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}\setminus A_{n,\eta+a}}1\ dy

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle D_{n}\int_{\{K_{n}(y)\geq n^{2}(E^{Q}+\eta+a\}}e^{-K_{n}(y)-\sum Q(y_{i})}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}}e^{-\sum Q(y_{i})}e^{n^{2}(E^{Q}+\varepsilon)}e^{-n^{2}(E^{Q}+\eta+a)}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-Q(x)}dx\right)^{n}e^{-\eta n^{2}/2}\right]e^{-an^{2}}.

Now choose
n_{0}\geq n_{1}
so that the term in brackets is bounded by
1
, and the lemma is proved.
∎

With Lemma
4.9
in hand, the proof of Johansson’s theorem
[
30
, Thm. 2.1]
generalizes to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, the proof of
[
13
, Thm. 6.96]
applies with only notational changes.

Proof of Theorem
4.8
.

First, we will show that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\phi)\ \leq\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(x)d\mu^{Q}(x).

(4.14)

Fix
\eta>0
. Since
\phi
is bounded, it follows from Lemma
4.9
that the left-hand side of (
4.14
)
is the same as

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{A_{n,2\eta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)dy.

Let
y*=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{n}^{*})
be a point where the function
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i})
attains its
maximum on the compact set
A_{n,2\eta}
.
Let
\nu_{n}
be the probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
that has mass
\frac{1}{n}
at each point
y_{i}^{*}
.
We have

\int_{A_{n,2\eta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)dy\ \leq\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(t)d\nu_{n}(t).

If
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
then
|s-t|\leq\sqrt{(1+|s|^{2})(1+|t|^{2})}
, so

\displaystyle(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(y_{i}^{*})-(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum\log(1+|y_{i}^{*}|^{2})

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle K_{n}(y^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{2}(E^{Q}+2\eta).

Therefore

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}Q(t)-\log(1+|t|^{2})\ d\nu_{n}(t)\ \leq\ \frac{n}{n-1}(E^{Q}+2\eta).

(4.15)

It foilows from (
4.15
) that the sequence
\nu_{n}
is tight.
(Recall that a sequence of probability measures
\nu_{n}
is called tight if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact
set whose complement has measure less than
\varepsilon
for all sufficiently large
n
).
Since the sequence is tight, there is some subsequence
\nu_{n_{j}}
that converges weakly to some probability measure

\nu^{\eta}
for which

\int\phi d\nu^{\eta}\ =\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\phi d\nu_{n}.

(First extract a subsequence whose limit is the limsup, then extract a further subsequence that converges weakly).

Observe that for any
L>0
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}K_{n}(y^{*})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}k(y_{i}^{*},y_{j}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\min(L,k(y_{i}^{*},y_{j}^{*})-nL\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\min(L,k(s,t))d\nu_{n}(s)d\nu_{n}(t)-\frac{L}{n}.

Since
y^{*}\in A_{n,2\eta}
, we get

\int\int\min(L,k(s,t))d\nu_{n}(s)d\nu_{n}(t)\ \leq\ \frac{L}{n}+E^{Q}+2\eta.

(4.16)

Apply (
4.16
) with
n=n_{j}
, let
j\to\infty
and then let
L\to 0
.
This gives

\int\int k(s,t)d\nu^{\eta}(s)d\nu^{\eta}(t)\ \leq\ E^{Q}+2\eta.

(4.17)

Now let
\eta_{j}
be a sequence that decreases to
0
.
From (
4.15
) we see that
\nu^{\eta_{j}}
is tight, so it has a subsequence that converges weakly to some measure

\nu
.
From (
4.17
), we see that
\nu
must be the equilibrium measure
\mu^{Q}
.
So we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(\phi)\ \leq\ \int\phi\ d\nu^{\eta_{j}}

for all
j
, and letting
j\to\infty
we get (
4.14
).

Repeating the argument with
y_{*}
where
\frac{1}{n}\sum\phi(y_{i})
attains its minimum, we get the reverse inequality
for the liminf, and hence we get (
4.11
).

4.4
Gaussian Case

Let
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{2}
.
(The positive constant
\gamma
is just a simple scaling, and plays no important rôle.
But as some authors default to
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
and others to
\gamma=1
, we include it to make the formulas seem more familiar.)
Let
X_{n}
be chosen in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
with distribution

C_{n}e^{-{\rm tr}(Q(X))}
, with
C_{n}
the normalizing constant.
Let
E_{n}
denote expectation with respect to this probability.
Then for
\phi
a continuous bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{n}\left[\frac{1}{n}\ {\rm tr}\,(\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ X_{n}))\right]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\ {\rm tr}\,(\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X))w_{n}(X)dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda_{i})\rho_{n}(\lambda)d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)R_{1,n}(x)dx.

So combining Theorem
4.8
with Theorem
3.1
, we get the following result in the Gaussian case.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{2}
. Let
X_{n}
be chosen in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
with distribution

C_{n}e^{-{\rm tr}(Q(X))}
. Then for all
\phi
that are continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}\left[\frac{1}{n}\ {\rm tr}\,(\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ X_{n}))\right]\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)d\mu^{Q}(x),

where the equilibrium measure
\mu^{Q}
is given by Theorem
3.1
.

Let us consider what happens
when we project
the equilibrium measures for the Gaussian case
onto the
x_{1}
axis.
For
d=2
, the density is

\frac{1}{\pi R_{2}^{2}}\int_{-\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}}^{\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}}1dx_{2}\ =\ \frac{2}{\pi R_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}.

For
d=3
, it is the weighted area of a disk of radius
\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}
, where the weight
at radius
s
is
\frac{1}{\pi^{2}R_{3}^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-s^{2}}}
.
Switching to polar coordinates on this disk, the density is

\frac{2}{\pi R_{3}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}}\frac{s}{\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-s^{2}}}ds\ =\ \frac{2}{\pi R_{3}^{2}}\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}.

For
d\geq 4
, we project normalized area measure for the
(d-1)
sphere
S^{d-1}
with radius
R_{d}
.
Let us change variables to
x_{1}=R_{d}\cos\theta
for
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
, where
\theta

represents the angle with the positive
x_{1}
axis.
The area of the shell between
\theta
and
\theta+\Delta\theta
is approximately

\Delta\theta
times the
(d-2)
-dimensional area of a sphere of radius
\sin\theta
,
which is
cR_{d}^{d-2}\sin^{d-2}\theta
. As
\Delta x=-R_{d}\sin\theta\Delta\theta
, we get that the
area of the shell between
x
and
x+\Delta x
is approximately
\Delta x
times

cR_{d}^{d-3}\sin^{d-3}\theta\ =\ c\left(\sqrt{R_{d}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d-3}.

Letting
\Delta x\to 0
, and plugging in the
normalizing value for
c
which comes from the identity

\int_{-1}^{1}(\sqrt{1-x^{2}})^{d-3}dx\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\ \Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})},

which is valid for
d>1
, we get that the density is

\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}R_{d}^{d-2}\ \Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}\ \left(\sqrt{R_{d}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d-3}.

For
d=2,3,4
we still get a semi-circular distribution, though the radius
R_{d}
shrinks as
d
increases from
1
to
4
. For
d>4
the radius
R_{d}
stays
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}
, but the eigenvalues of
X^{1}

cluster more around
0
. We have proved the following.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
X_{n}
be chosen in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d,H}_{n}
with distribution

C_{n}e^{-\gamma\,{\rm tr}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(X^{j})^{2})}
. Then for all
\phi
that are continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}
,
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}\left[\frac{1}{n}\ {\rm tr}\,(\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ X^{1}_{n}))\right]\ =\ \int_{-R_{d}}^{R_{d}}\phi(x)f_{d}(x)dx,

where
R_{d}
are as in Theorem
3.1

and

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi R_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{R_{1}^{2}-x^{2}}

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi R_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-x^{2}}

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi R_{3}^{2}}\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-x^{2}}

\displaystyle f_{d}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}R_{d}^{d-2}\ \Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}\ \left(\sqrt{R_{d}^{2}-x^{2}}\right)^{d-3},\qquad d\geq 4.

5
Random Commuting Matrices: the non-Hermitian case

5.1
Generic
d
-tuples

In this section, we shall drop the assumption that the matrices are Hermitian, and work with random matrices from
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
.

There is a qualitative difference between the algebraic sets
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
for different values of
d,n
.
In particular, there are major differences between the reducible and irreducible cases.
Motzkin and Taussky proved that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{2}_{n}
is irreducible and of dimension
n^{2}+n
for all
n

[
35
]
.
Gerstenhaber reproved this, and proved that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{4}_{4}
is reducible
[
20
]
.
Guralnick proved that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
is irreducible for all
d
if
n\leq 3

[
23
]
.

When
d=3
, Guralnick proved that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{3}_{32}
is reducible
[
23
]
.
It is now known that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{3}_{n}
is reducible if
n\geq 29

[
28
,
36
]
,
and irreducible if
n\leq 10
(see
[
24
,
28
,
37
,
25
,
40
,
41
]
for

n
going from
4
to
10
.)

Let us call
X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}

generically diagonalizable
if each component
X^{r}
has
n
distinct eigenvalues, and define

{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}}\ =\ \{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}:\ {\rm each\ }X^{r}\ {\rm has\ }n\ {\rm distinct\ eigenvalues}\}.

When are the generically diagonalizable
d
-tuples dense in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
?
In
[
28
]
, Holbrook and Omladic showed that the answer is yes
if and only if
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
is irreducible. In the irreducible case, the
non-diagonalizable elements are of smaller dimension than the diagonalizable ones. Guralnick proved that
{\rm dim}\ {\mathfrak{C}}^{3}_{n}>{\rm dim}\ {\mathfrak{C}}^{3}_{n,{\rm gen}}
if
n\geq 32
.
For a study of the components in the non-irreducible case, see
[
29
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
be irreducible. Then

{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}\setminus{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}})\ <\ {\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}})=n^{2}+(d-1)n.

(5.1)

Proof.

Let us write
{\mathcal{W}}^{r}=\{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}:X^{r}{\rm\ does\ not\ have\ }n\ {\rm distinct\ eigenvalues}\}
.
Let
q^{r}
be the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of
X^{r}
, thought of as a polynomial
in the
dn^{2}
entries of an element of
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{d}
, and let
q=q^{1}\cdots q^{d}
. Then

{\mathcal{W}}^{r}\ =\ \{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}:q^{r}(X)=0\},

and

{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}\setminus{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}}\ =\ \cup_{r=1}^{d}{\mathcal{W}}^{r}\ =\ \{X\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}:q(X)=0\}.

As
q
does not vanish identically on
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
, assumed irreducible, it follows that

{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}\setminus{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}})\ <\ {\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}).

When
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
is irreducible, it is shown in
[
24
]
that
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n})=n^{2}+(d-1)n
.
It is obvious that
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}})=n^{2}+(d-1)n
, so we get (
5.1
).
∎

5.2
Ginibre formula

Throughout the remainder of Section
5

we shall assume that
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
is irreducible.

Assumption I:
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
is irreducible.

(If this is not the case, then we must replace
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
by its principal component, that is the closure of the set of jointly diagonalizable matrices).
Let
w:{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function that satisfies
w(U^{*}XU)=w(X)
for all unitary
U\in{\mathcal{U}}(n)
, and is normalized so

Let

By Proposition

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
Q
be in
{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}}
.
Then the tangent space to
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
at
Q
is

\{Z\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{d}:[Q^{r},Z^{s}]=[Q^{s},Z^{r}],\quad\forall\ 1\leq r\neq s\leq d\}.

(5.2)

The tangent space to
{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}
at
Q
is
\{Z\in{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}:\eqref{eqd31}{\rm\ holds}\}
.
The real dimension of the first tangent space is
2n^{2}+2(d-1)n
, and of the second is

n^{2}+(2d-1)n
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
\gamma(t)
is a
C^{1}
curve in
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
with
\gamma(0)=Q

and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=Z
.
Then
\gamma^{r}(t)=Q^{r}+tZ^{r}+o(t)
. As

0\ =\ \gamma^{r}(t)\gamma^{s}(t)-\gamma^{s}(t)\gamma^{r}(t)\ =\ t\left([Q^{r},Z^{s}]-[Q^{s},Z^{r}]\right)+o(t),

we conclude that
[Q^{r},Z^{s}]=[Q^{s},Z^{r}]
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose
Z
is in (
5.2
).
Let
A
be an upper triangular matrix that diagonalizes
Q
:
Q=ADA^{-1}
.
Let
Y=A^{-1}ZA
. Then

[D^{r},Y^{s}]\ =\ [D^{s},Y^{r}]\quad\forall\ 1\leq r\neq s\leq d.

(5.3)

Let
\lambda_{j}^{r}
be the diagonal entries of
D^{r}
. Then (
5.3
) says

Y^{s}_{ij}(\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i})\ =\ Y^{r}_{ij}(\lambda^{s}_{j}-\lambda^{s}_{i})\quad\forall\ 1\leq r\neq s\leq d.

(5.4)

Define a matrix
B
by

B_{ij}\ =\ \begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda^{r}_{j}-\lambda^{r}_{i}}Y^{r}_{ij},\qquad&i\neq j\\
0\qquad&i=j\end{cases}.

By (
5.4
) the definition does not depend on
r
, and since
Q
is generically diagonalizable
we never divide by zero. Let
E^{r}
be the diagonal of
Y^{r}
.
Define a curve
\gamma(t)
by

\gamma^{r}(t)\ =\ Ae^{tB}(D^{r}+tE^{r})e^{-tB}A^{-1}.

Then
\gamma(t)\in{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
, and if

(and hence

) is in
{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}
, then so is

for all

.
Moreover,
\gamma(0)=Q
and

\gamma^{\prime}(0)\ =\ Ad_{A}([B,D]+E)\ =\ Z.

So the tangent space is as claimed.

To calculate the dimensions of the tangent spaces, note that
Ad_{A}
is an isomorphism from
the tangent space at
D
to the tangent space at
Q
, so it suffices to calculate the dimension of the tangent space at
D
.
In
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
, one can choose the diagonal entries freely, which gives

dimensions, and
the off diagonal entries for

dimensions.
In the upper-triangular case, one can again choose the diagonal entries freely, and one has

off diagonal dimensions.
∎

Let us fix
{Q_{0}}\in{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}\cap{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n,{\rm gen}}
.
We shall use

to mean tangent space.
Let

eigenvalue of

. Let

be an upper triangular factoring of

As in Theorem

by

\displaystyle g:{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}\times\exp({\mathcal{A}}_{0,n})

\displaystyle\ \to\

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}

\displaystyle(Q,U)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle UQU^{*}\ =\ {\rm Ad}_{U}(Q).

We want to decompose

For

Decompose

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume

Case: the

Let

.

Case:

. The space

(5.7)

5.4
From upper triangular to diagonal

We are interested in determining the distribution
\rho_{n}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of the eigenvalues.
We shall mainly consider Gausssian weights on
{\mathfrak{C}}^{d}_{n}
, that is weights
of the form

w(X)\ =\ Ce^{-\gamma{\rm tr}\sum_{r=1}^{d}(X^{r})^{*}X^{r}}\ =\ Ce^{-\gamma\|X\|_{F}^{2}}.

(5.8)

The important property of Gaussian weights that we shall use, in addition to their unitary invariance, is that for upper triangular
d
-tuples
Q
they factor as

w(Q)\ =\ w_{1}(\|D\|_{F}^{2})w_{2}(\|Q-D\|_{F}^{2}),

(5.9)

where
D
is the diagonal of
Q
.

When
d=1
,
\rho
can be derived from
\kappa
in a straightforward way.
Indeed, the off-diagonal terms can be chosen independently, and since the weight factors as in (
5.9
),
one can integrate out all the off-diagonal terms, and since
\kappa
from (
5.6
) depends only on the eigenvalues, we get that the density of the eigenvalues for

is

\rho_{n}(\lambda)\ =\ C\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}e^{-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}}.

See
[
21
]
or
[
42
, 2.6.2]
.

For

, we proceed as follows.
Let
{\rm Diag}^{d}_{n}
denote the
d
-tuples of diagonal
n
-by-
n
matrices.
To make reading easier, we shall drop the subscript
0
and fix an element

.
We have

for some

and

\displaystyle G:{\rm Diag}^{d}_{n}\times F_{n,1}

\displaystyle\ \to\

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto

at

is

.
We shall work in complex coordinates, so the Jacobian will be
\det(dG)^{*}dG

without a square root.
We have

\displaystyle dG_{(D,A)}:{({\mathbb{C}}^{d})^{n}}\times F_{n,0}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle T_{Q}{\mathcal{T}}^{d}_{n}

\displaystyle dG_{(D,A)}(E,B)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}[(A+tB)(D+tE)(A+tB)^{-1}-{Q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Ad}_{A}([A^{-1}B,D])+{\rm Ad}_{A}(E).

As a basis for
{({\mathbb{C}}^{d})^{n}}
we can choose
\{\xi_{p}^{r}:1\leq p\leq n,1\leq r\leq d\}

elsewhere.

We have

to be the complex linear endomorphism of

\Gamma_{Q}\ =\ dG_{(D,A)}^{*}dG_{(D,A)}.

(5.11)

We have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{Q}((\xi_{p_{1}}^{r_{1}},E_{i_{1}j_{1}}),(\xi_{p_{2}}^{r_{2}},E_{i_{2}j_{2}}))

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\left[\delta_{r_{1},r_{2}}A_{kp_{1}}A^{-1}_{p_{1}l}\overline{A_{kp_{2}}}\overline{A_{p_{2}l}^{-1}}\ +\ \sum_{q_{2}=1}^{n}A_{kp_{1}}A^{-1}_{p_{1}l}\overline{A_{kq_{2}}}\overline{\lambda^{r_{1}}_{q_{2}}}\overline{A^{-1}_{q_{2}i_{2}}}\overline{A^{-1}_{j_{2}l}}\right.

5.5
Density of eigenvalues

We would like to find an explicit formula for the density of the eigenvalues, which would come from
evaluating the right-hand side of (
5.13
). We shall do this first in the known case
d=1
, as an illustration. Then we shall do it in the case of
d
arbitrary, but with
n=2
.

Case:
d=1
. We have from (
5.10
)

\displaystyle dG_{(D,A)}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\times F_{n,0}

\displaystyle\ \to\

\displaystyle T_{Q}{\mathcal{T}}^{1}_{n}

\displaystyle(E,B)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle Ad_{A}([A^{-1}B,D]+E).

Both the domain and codomain can be identified with the upper triangular matrices
{\mathcal{T}}^{1}_{n}
.
As
A
is upper triangular with
1
’s on the diagonal, multiplication by
A
and
A^{-1}

are unipotent linear operators on
{\mathcal{T}}^{1}_{n}
, and hence have determinant
1
.
So the Jacobian of
G
is the same as the Jacobian of the map

(E,B)\ \mapsto\ [A^{-1}B,D]+E.

Decomposing
{\mathcal{T}}^{1}_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{d}\oplus F_{n,0}
, this has the same determinant as

B\ \mapsto\ [A^{-1}B,D].

As multiplication by
A^{-1}
is unipotent on
F_{n,0}
, this in turn has the same Jacobian as

B\ \mapsto\ [B,D],

which is

\Delta(\lambda)\ :=\ \prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

So if we take (
5.12
) and write it as

w(X)dX\ =\ w_{1}(\|D\|_{F}^{2})w_{2}(\|ADA^{-1}-D\|^{2}_{F})\kappa(ADA^{-1})\Delta(\lambda)d\lambda dAd\nu,

integrate with respect to
\nu
and
A
and use (
5.6
), we get

\rho(\lambda)\ =\ c_{n}w_{1}(\|D\|^{2}_{F})\Delta(\lambda)\int_{F_{n,1}}w_{2}(\|ADA^{-1}-D\|^{2}_{F})\Delta(\lambda)dA.

(5.14)

(We can pull
\kappa(ADA^{-1})=c_{n}\Delta(\lambda)
out of the integral since it does not depend on
A
.)
As

ADA^{-1}-D\ =\ [A,D]A^{-1}\ =\ ad_{A}(D)A^{-1},

we can make the change of variables

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle[A,D]A^{-1}

\displaystyle dB

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(\lambda)dA

to conclude that the integral in
(
5.14
) equals
\int_{F_{n,0}}w_{2}(\|B\|^{2}_{F})dB
, which is some constant.
So we get Ginibre’s density formula
[
21
]
for some constant
C^{\prime}
:

\rho(\lambda)\ =\ C^{\prime}\Delta(\lambda)w_{1}(\|D\|_{F}^{2}),

which if we assume

is Gaussian as in (
5.8
) becomes

\rho(\lambda)\ =\ C^{\prime}\Delta(\lambda)e^{-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}}.

Case:

n=2
,
d
arbitrary. We shall restrict to the weight
w
being as in (
5.8
). We can write

A\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

From (

), the matrix for
\Gamma_{Q}
has the form

\bordermatrix{~&\xi^{r}_{1}&\xi^{r}_{2}&E_{12}\cr\xi^{s}_{1}&\delta_{rs}(1+|\alpha|^{2})&-\delta_{rs}|\alpha|^{2}&-\overline{\alpha}(\lambda_{2}^{s}-\lambda_{1}^{s})\cr\xi^{s}_{2}&-\delta_{rs}|\alpha|^{2}&\delta_{rs}(1+|\alpha|^{2})&\overline{\alpha}(\lambda_{2}^{s}-\lambda_{1}^{s})\cr E_{12}&-\alpha(\bar{\lambda}_{2}^{r}-\bar{\lambda}_{1}^{r})&\alpha(\bar{\lambda}_{2}^{r}-\bar{\lambda}_{1}^{r})&|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}\cr}

(5.15)

To calculate the determinant of (

) we shall use the Schur complement.
The first

-by-

) has determinant
(1+2|\alpha|^{2})^{d}

The Schur complement with respect to the bottom right-hand entry is

|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}|^{2}-\sum_{r=1}^{d}\langle\begin{pmatrix}1+|\alpha|^{2}&-|\alpha|^{2}\\
-|\alpha|^{2}&1+|\alpha|^{2}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{\alpha}(\lambda_{2}^{r}-\lambda_{1}^{r})\\
\overline{\alpha}(\lambda_{2}^{r}-\lambda_{1}^{r})\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{\alpha}(\lambda_{2}^{r}-\lambda_{1}^{r})\\

Multiplying these together, we get

Using this and (

Integrate first with respect to

,then use polar coordinates

The following identity is elementary:

I_{d}\ :=\ \int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2d+1}e^{-kr^{2}}dr\ =\ \frac{d!}{2k^{d+1}}.

Using(

Theorem 5.5
.

Let

Remark 5.6
.

The negative powers in (

However, once

the negative powers show that, unlike in the Hermitian case, there is an
attraction
between the eigenvalues, not a repulsion.
