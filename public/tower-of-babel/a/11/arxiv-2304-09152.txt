Monte-Carlo method for incompressible fluid flows past obstacles
By V. Cherepanov and Z. Qian

Abstract
We establish stochastic functional integral representations for incompressible fluid flows occupying wall-bounded domains using the conditional law duality for a class of diffusion processes. These representations are used to derive a Monte-Carlo scheme based on the corresponding exact random vortex formulation. We implement several numerical experiments based on the Monte-Carlo method without appealing to the boundary layer flow computations, to demonstrate the methodology. Key words : diffusion processes, incompressible fluid flow, Monte-Carlo simulation, random vortex method MSC classifications : 76M35, 76M23, 60H30, 65C05, 68Q10.

Introduction

In this paper we aim to develop Monte-Carlo schemes for the numerical analysis of incompressible flows past solid obstacles, that is flows occupying certain domains with a nontrivial boundary. These fluid flows satisfy the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, and we derive exact functional integral representations for their solutions. Phrasing these in terms of random vortex formulations, we are able to establish corresponding numerical schemes and conduct simulations for such flows.

The flows in question are characterised by their velocity
u(x,t)
for
x\in D
, where
D
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with nontrivial boundary
\partial D
. The velocity then follows the Navier-Stokes equations:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u+(u\cdot\nabla)u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla P=F,

(1.1)

\nabla\cdot u=0

(1.2)

in
D
, and the no-slip condition for
u
is satisfied, i.e.
u(x,t)=0
for
x\in\partial D
. The constant
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity of the fluid,
P
is the pressure and
F
denotes an external force.

One can get another important formulation of the problem, writing it in terms of different dynamical variables, namely the velocity
u
and the vorticity
\omega=\nabla\wedge u
, the curl of the velocity. For simplicity, we assume in the following derivation that the domain
D
is two-dimensional — we refer the reader to Section
2
for the three-dimensional case. We also assume that the velocity
u
is extended to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\nabla\cdot u=0
in distribution (e.g., by letting
u=0
outside of the domain
D
, see Sections
2
and
4
for details).

Indeed, the equation (
1.1
) implies the vorticity transport equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega-\nu\Delta\omega=G

(1.3)

in
D
, where
G=\nabla\wedge F
. Notice, however, that in general the value of the vorticity
\omega
is non-zero along the boundary
\partial D
, and we denote
\theta=\left.\omega\right|_{\partial D}
. It is well-known that the solution to equations of the form (
1.3
) can be expressed in terms of functional integrals — this idea may trace back to Feynman
[
13
]
and Kac
[
25
]
. However, we first have to introduce a perturbation
W_{\varepsilon}
of the vorticity
\omega
such that
W_{\varepsilon}=\omega
everywhere except a thin
\varepsilon
-layer of the boundary
\partial D
, and
\left.W_{\varepsilon}\right|_{\partial D}=0
. One way of defining such perturbation is given in
[
38
]
using a smooth cutoff function, which we also utilise in the subsequent sections.

Therefore, for
W_{\varepsilon}
the vorticity equation (
1.3
) implies

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W_{\varepsilon}+(u\cdot\nabla)W_{\varepsilon}-\nu\Delta W_{\varepsilon}=g_{\varepsilon}

(1.4)

in
D
where
\left.W_{\varepsilon}\right|_{\partial D}=0
and
g_{\varepsilon}
is the corresponding perturbation of
G
. Introducing the Green function
p(s,x,t,y)
for the forward parabolic operator
\nu\Delta-u\cdot\nabla-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
in
D
subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition, we write

W_{\varepsilon}(y,t)=\int_{D}p(0,\xi,t,y)W_{\varepsilon}(\xi,0)\textrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}p(s,\xi,t,y)g_{\varepsilon}(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s

(1.5)

which we can then use to recover the unperturbed vorticity
\omega
. Note that for incompressible flows one then recovers the velocity by

u(x,t)=\int_{D}K(x,y)\omega(y,t)\textrm{d}y

(1.6)

where
K
is the Biot-Savart kernel of the domain
D
(assuming both the velocity and the vorticity decay to zero at infinity sufficiently fast).

To give a formulatic expression, we assume throughout the introduction that a representation similar to (
1.5
) holds for the unperturbed vorticity
\omega
, that is

\omega(y,t)=\int_{D}p(0,\xi,t,y)\omega(\xi,0)\textrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}p(s,\xi,t,y)g(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s.

(1.7)

Then for the velocity, one has

u(x,t)=\int_{D}\int_{D}K(x,y)p(0,\xi,t,y)\omega(\xi,0)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\int_{D}K(x,y)p(s,\xi,t,y)g(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s\textrm{d}y.

(1.8)

We stress again that even though the above formulae hold for the case of unbounded flows, in the case of nontrivial boundary the actual representations essentially involve the boundary vorticity
\theta
, see Sections
2
and
4
for exact expressions.

It turns out one can formulate the above as stochastic representations due to the following observation. Noting again that the vector field
u
is divergence-free, we have that the function
p(s,x,t,y)
is also the transition density for the diffusion process with infinitesimal generator
\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla
. This means that for the family of stochastic processes
X^{\xi,s}
given by

\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\xi,s}=u(X^{\xi,s}_{t},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t}

(1.9)

for
t\geq s
and
X^{\xi,s}_{t}=\xi
for
t\leq s
, we have that

p(s,\xi,t,y)\textrm{d}y=\mathbb{P}\left[X^{\xi,s}_{t}\in\textrm{d}y\right].

(1.10)

The processes are called Taylor’s diffusions (due to Taylor
[
43
]
), one interprets the processes as "imaginary" Brownian particles following the flow with velocity
u(x,t)
.

Let us view the integral representations we have so far in terms of expectations with respect to the family of Taylor’s diffusions introduced above. Indeed, it is easy to see that the integrals in the representation (
1.8
) can be viewed in terms of the expectations with respect to
X^{\xi,s}
, that is

u(x,t)=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K(x,X^{\xi,0}_{t})\right]\omega(\xi,0)\textrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K(x,X^{\xi,s}_{t})\right]g(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s.

(1.11)

Having discussed the above representations, we propose the following numerical scheme. We couple the processes
X^{\xi,s}
with the velocity
u(x,t)
and update them
according to approximations given by
(
1.9
) and
(
1.11
) respectively. Indeed, the former depends only on the velocity
u(x,t)
for which one can take the approximation given by the latter. Note that, in turn, the velocity is determined by the diffusions
X^{\xi,s}
and the presence of expectations in the representation
(
1.11
) allows for Monte-Carlo-type schemes (we refer the reader to the Section

5
for particular schemes we used in simulations).

One of the drawbacks for the scheme described above is that it involves Taylor’s diffusions
(
1.9
) started at every time
s\in[0,t]
. This implies that the memory required for the computation and the computation complexity itself are increasing with time
t
. In Section
2
, we overcome this difficulty by deriving representations different from
(
1.7
) and
(
1.8
) using the duality of the conditional laws of Taylor’s diffusions proved in
[
37
]
. Although the representations in this case involve more complicated integrands (see Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and their two-dimensional versions, Theorems
2.3
,
2.4
), we are able to devise the schemes with non-increasing memory and computational complexity in Section
5
.

We also have to mention again that the above representations (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) in the stated form hold for domains without boundary. In the case of domains with nontrivial boundary, the representations are derived from (
1.5
) for the perturbed vorticity
W_{\varepsilon}
. These depend on the boundary vorticity
\theta
which poses an additional difficulty in simulations as we are unable to compute the values of
\theta
using the expressions for
\omega
. However, as in practice we use a desingularised kernel
K_{\delta}
to compute the velocity, we write the formal derivative of the representation for
u(x,t)
to compute the vorticity
\omega
used in plots and the boundary vorticity
\theta
.

Note also that as the expression
(
1.5
) for the perturbed vorticity
W_{\varepsilon}
depends on the boundary layer thickness parameter
\varepsilon
, it is interesting to consider the limit of the representations derived from
(
1.5
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We do the corresponding computations in Section
4
for different domains and notice that many terms in fact do not contribute to the limit. This observation turns out to be useful in Section
5
as it simplifies the numerical schemes significantly.

Let us remark that the discussed above limiting representations obtained in Section
4
have a certain interest on their own as they display how the boundary vorticity
\theta
influences the velocity
u
(see Section
4
for details), which seems to be in accordance with Prandtl’s boundary layer theory
[
35
]
. In some of the experiments reported in Section
5
we omit the terms involving
\theta
when we compute the velocity
u
and notice that they essentially contribute to the chaotic behaviour of the flow close to the boundary.

We also note that we derive these representations in Section
4
for two particular domains (namely, the ones for flows passing a flat plate and a wedge) and for general two-dimensional domains
D
conformally equivalent to the half-plane with smooth boundary
\partial D
. However, we believe that similar representations should hold for more complicated domains as well (e.g., not necessary simply connected domains with piecewise smooth boundary).

2
Random vortex for wall-bounded flows

In this section, we follow the approach proposed in
[
36
]
and
[
38
]
. In the latter paper the random
vortex dynamics has been established for viscous fluid flows moving
along with a solid wall modelled by the two dimensional Navier-Stokes
equations. Let
u=(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
denote the velocity of an
incompressible viscous fluid flow constrained in the upper half space

D
where
x_{3}\geq 0
. The velocity
u
satisfies the no-slip
condition, i.e.
u(x,t)=0
for
x\in\partial D
and
t>0
. Let us assume
that there is an external force
F=(F^{1},F^{2},F^{3})
supplying the energy
to the fluid dynamical system. Therefore the velocity
u
and the pressure
P

are evolved according to the Navier-Stokes equations:

\frac{\partial u^{i}}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}u^{j}\frac{\partial u^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\nu\Delta u^{i}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{i}}-F^{i}=0\quad\textrm{ in }D

(2.1)

for
i=1,2,3
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial u^{j}}{\partial x_{j}}=0\quad\textrm{ in }D.

(2.2)

The initial velocity is denoted by
u_{0}(x)=u(x,0)
. In the following we will often omit the summation over repeated indices.

To proceed our discussion, we assume that the velocity
u
is
smooth inside
D
and is
C^{3}
up to the boundary
\partial D
.
This assumption is although technical, but in no means
it is trivial and apparent. Indeed it remains and will still be stand
as an open problem in mathematics for establishing the regularity
for solutions to the general three dimensional Navier-Stokes equations.
We do not pursue this line of research which lies outside the scope
of current project.

We introduce the vorticity
\omega=\nabla\wedge u
,
whose components
\omega^{i}=\varepsilon^{ijk}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}u^{k}
.
In our study the vorticity transport equations play a crucial role.
The vorticity transport equations are evolution equations for
\omega
, which are given as the
following

\frac{\partial\omega^{i}}{\partial t}+u^{j}\frac{\partial\omega^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\nu\Delta\omega^{i}+\omega^{j}S_{j}^{i}-G^{i}=0\quad\textrm{ in }D

(2.3)

where
S_{j}^{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial u^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u^{j}}{\partial x_{i}})

is the symmetric tensor of rate-of-strain, and
G=\nabla\wedge F
.

We adopt the simple idea in
[
38
]
of extending the
definition of the velocity
u=(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
to the whole space

\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the reflection principle, that is

u^{i}(\bar{x},t)=u^{i}(x,t)\quad\textrm{ for }i=1,2;\quad u^{3}(\bar{x},t)=-u^{3}(x,t)

for
x_{3}<0
. Since
u
satisfies the no-slip condition, this
yields a crucial fact that the extended velocity
u
is divergence
free in the distribution sense on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore the adjoint
of the heat operator
\nu\Delta-u\cdot\nabla-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

is just the heat operator
\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
.
Therefore the Green function
\Gamma_{D}(\tau,x,t,y)
to the Dirichlet
boundary problem of the (forward) parabolic equation

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla\right)f=0&\textrm{ in }D,\\
f(x,t)=0&\textrm{ if }x_{3}=0\end{cases}

coincides with the transition probability density of the diffusion
process with its infinitesimal generator
\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla

killed on leaving the domain
D
. This leads to the following construction.
Let
X
be the diffusion process with infinitesimal generator
\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla
,
which is a diffusion with state space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, without “killing” at the boundary
x_{3}=0
. That
is,
X
is a weak solution of the stochastic differential equation

\textrm{d}X=u(X,t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B.

(2.4)

Let
p(\tau,x,t,y)
(for
t>\tau\geq 0
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
)
be the transition probability density function, which is positive
and Hölder’s continuous in all arguments, as long as
u
is bounded
and Borel measurable. Formally

p(\tau,x,t,y)\textrm{d}y=\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}\in\textrm{d}y|X_{\tau}=x\right]

and therefore it is clear that

p(\tau,x,t,y)=p(\tau,\overline{x},t,\overline{y})\quad\textrm{ for }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Hence, by applying the reflection principle we have therefore the
following representation

\Gamma_{D}(\tau,x,t,y)=p(\tau,x,t,y)-p(\tau,x,t,\overline{y})\quad\textrm{ for }x,y\in D.

(2.5)

We next apply this representation to the study of the vorticity
\omega
. The
vorticity
\omega
may be considered as a solution of the linear
parabolic equation (
2.3
) if
u
is supposed as a given
fluid dynamic variable. However,
\omega
has nontrivial boundary
value in general, so let
\theta
be the trace of
\omega
along the
boundary
\partial D
(note that the vorticity boundary values may be identified with the stress of the fluid flow immediately injected to the wall, cf.
[
40
]

[
44
]
). Then it is easy to see that
\omega^{3}

has trace zero, and due to the no-slip condition imposed on
u
,

\theta
can be identified with the normal part of the stress applied
to the boundary
\partial D
. Note that
\theta
is a time dependent
vector field on the boundary
\partial D
.

To handle the no vanishing boundary vorticity
\theta
, we employ the same technique used in
[
38
]
. Let
\phi
be a smooth cut-off function defined on
[0,\infty)
with values in
[0,1]
, such that
\phi(r)=1
for
r\in[0,1/3]
and
\phi(r)=0
for
r\geq 2/3
, and define
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\phi(x_{3}/\varepsilon)\theta(x_{1},x_{2},t)
for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in D
. Let

W_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\omega(x,t)-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x,t).

(2.6)

Then

\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{i}}{\partial t}+u^{j}\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\nu\Delta W_{\varepsilon}^{i}-W_{\varepsilon}^{j}\frac{\partial u^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-g_{\varepsilon}^{i}=0\quad\textrm{ in }D,

(2.7)

for
i=1,2,3
, and

\left.W_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right|_{x\in\partial D}=0\textrm{ }\quad\textrm{ for }x\in\partial D,

(2.8)

where

g_{\varepsilon}^{i}=G^{i}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{i}-u^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{i}+\nu\Delta\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{i}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{j}S_{j}^{i}

(2.9)

for
i=1,2,3
.

2.1
Random vortex for wall-bounded flows

According to the Feynman-Kac formula for forward heat equations established in
[
36
]
we can represent
W_{\varepsilon}
in terms of the distribution of the Taylor diffusion. Here we present a slightly different approach. To this end, we have to introduce some notation. Let
\mathbb{P}^{\xi}
denote the distribution of the diffusion process with infinitesimal generator
\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla
(where
u
is extended on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the reflection principle) started from
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
at time
0
, and
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}
the conditional law of
\mathbb{P}^{\xi}\left[\cdot|\psi(t)=\eta\right]
where
\psi
is the coordinate process (i.e. canonical element) on the path space
C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

We recall that
p_{b}(s,x,t,y)
denotes the transition probability density function of the diffusion with generator
\nu\Delta+b\cdot\nabla
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X^{\eta}
be the Taylor diffusion:

\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\eta}=u(X_{t}^{\eta},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}^{\eta}=\eta

(2.10)

for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For each pair
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

and
t>0
, define
s\mapsto Q(\eta,t;s)
to be the unique solution
of the differential equations:

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}s}Q_{j}^{i}(\eta,t;s)=-Q_{k}^{i}(\eta,t;s)1_{D}(X_{s}^{\eta})A_{j}^{k}(X_{s}^{\eta},s),\quad Q_{j}^{i}(\eta,t;t)=\delta_{ij}

(2.11)

where
A_{j}^{k}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u^{k}
and
i,j,k=1,2,3
. Then

\displaystyle\omega^{i}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,t)+\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\eta,t;0)1_{\{t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\eta,t;s)1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s

(2.12)

for every
\xi\in D
and
t>0
,
i=1,2,3
, where
\zeta(\psi)=\inf\left\{s:\psi(s)\notin D\right\}
and
\tau_{t}
denotes the time reversion operator on the path space
C([0,t];\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
q_{j}^{i}(x,t)=1_{D}(x)A_{j}^{i}(x,t)
, where
i,j=1,2,3
.
Let
T>0
be fixed. Recall that
u(x,t)
is extended for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

such that
\nabla\cdot u=0
in distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
\tilde{X}^{\xi}
be the solution to the stochastic differential
equation

\textrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi}=-u(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},\quad\tilde{X}_{0}^{\xi}=\xi

(2.13)

which is understood as the stochastic integral equation

\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi}=\xi-\int_{0}^{t\wedge T}u(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T}\textrm{d}B_{s}\quad\textrm{ for all }t\geq 0.

(2.14)

Let

T_{\xi}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi}\notin D\right\}

be the first time the process
\tilde{X}^{\xi}
leaves the region

D
. Then
t\rightarrow\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi}
is a diffusion
process too, and

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\xi-\int_{0}^{t\wedge T\wedge T_{\xi}}u(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T\wedge T_{\xi}}\textrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle=\xi-\int_{0}^{t}1_{\left\{s<T\wedge T_{\xi}\right\}}u(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t}1_{\left\{s<T\wedge T_{\xi}\right\}}\textrm{d}B_{s}

(2.15)

for all
t\geq 0
. According to Feynman-Kac, we define

\textrm{d}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)=\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(s)q_{j}^{k}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s,\quad\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(0)=\delta_{ij}

(2.16)

where
i,j=1,2,3
. Let
Y_{t}^{j}=W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

which is well defined, as according to our assumption,
W_{\varepsilon}

is
C^{2}
on
\overline{D}
. Since
W_{\varepsilon}
vanishes
along the boundary
\partial D
, so that

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{j}

\displaystyle=W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)=1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)+1_{\{t\geq T_{\xi}\}}W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

\displaystyle=1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)

for all
t\leq T
. Moreover, according to Itô’s formula,

\textrm{d}Y_{t}^{j}=\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)\cdot\textrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi}-\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t+\nu\Delta W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t.

Since
W_{\varepsilon}
vanishes identically on
\partial D
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)+1_{\left\{t\geq T_{\xi}\right\}}\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}(\tilde{X}_{T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)

and similarly

\displaystyle\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t),

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-t)

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\Delta W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)

for all
t\leq T
. Therefore

\displaystyle\textrm{d}Y_{t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\nu}1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\cdot\textrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle+1_{\left\{t<T_{\xi}\right\}}\left(\nu\Delta W_{\varepsilon}^{j}-(u\cdot\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j})-\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{j}}{\partial t}\right)(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t

(2.17)

for all
t\leq T
. By (
2.7
) which may be written as
the following

\left(\nu\Delta-u\cdot\nabla-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)W_{\varepsilon}^{j}=-A_{k}^{j}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}-g_{\varepsilon}^{j}\quad\textrm{ in }D

(2.18)

hence, by substituting this into (
2.17
), it follows that

\textrm{d}Y_{t}^{j}=\sqrt{2\nu}1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\cdot\textrm{d}B_{t}-1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}\left(A_{k}^{j}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}+g_{\varepsilon}^{j}\right)(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t.

(2.19)

Let
M^{i}=\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}Y^{j}
. Using stochastic integration
by parts one obtains

\displaystyle M_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(0)Y_{0}^{j}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)\textrm{d}Y_{s}^{j}+\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}^{j}\textrm{d}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(0)Y_{0}^{j}+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\cdot\textrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\left(A_{k}^{j}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}+g_{\varepsilon}^{j}\right)(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s\wedge T_{\xi}}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(0)Y_{0}^{j}+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\cdot\textrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\left(-\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(s)q_{j}^{k}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s+\textrm{d}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)\right)

here the last equality follows from the fact that on
\left\{s<T_{\xi}\right\}
, the process

\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi}
takes values in
D
, so that
A_{j}^{i}

coincides with
q_{j}^{i}
. This computation holds for any differentiable,
adapted processes
\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}
. In particular, if
\tilde{Q}

solves (
2.16
), then

\displaystyle M_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=Y_{0}^{i}+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\cdot\textrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}1_{\{s<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(s)g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{s}^{\xi},T-s)\textrm{d}s

which yields that

\displaystyle M_{T\wedge T_{\xi}}^{i}

\displaystyle=W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)-\int_{0}^{T\wedge T_{\xi}}1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2\nu}\int_{0}^{T\wedge T_{\xi}}1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)\nabla W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\cdot\textrm{d}B.

(2.20)

Since

M_{T\wedge T_{\xi}}^{i}=1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi},0),\quad Y_{0}^{i}=W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)

so that, after taking expectation of the both sides of (
2.20
) to
obtain that

W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi},0)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t\right].

(2.21)

By taking conditional expectation on the event that
\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi},0)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{-u_{T}}(0,\xi,T,\eta)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge T_{\xi}}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\textrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]p_{-u_{T}}(0,\xi,T,\eta)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t

where
p_{-u_{T}}
is the transition probability density function
of the diffusion
\tilde{X}^{\xi}
, which is a diffusion process
with infinitesimal generator
\nu\Delta-u_{T}\cdot\nabla
, where

u_{T}(x,t)=u(x,T-t)
. Since
\nabla\cdot u=0
, we have that
p_{-u_{T}}(0,\xi,T,\eta)

coincides with
p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)
. Therefore

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(T)1_{\{T<T_{\xi}\}}\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)1_{\{t<T_{\xi}\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi},T-t)\right|\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t.

(2.22)

Let us rewrite the representation (
2.22
) in terms of the
distributions of the Taylor diffusion. To this end, let us use
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\xi}

to denote the law of
\tilde{X}^{\xi}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}

to denote the conditional law, or the diffusion bridge measure of
\tilde{X}^{\xi}

given the terminal value that
\tilde{X}_{T}^{\xi}=\eta
. The conditional
law
\mathbb{\tilde{P}}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}
can be considered as
a probability measure on the path space
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{3})

canonically. For any continuous path
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\tilde{Q}(\psi;t)
denotes the solution to the following linear
ordinary differential equations

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi;t)=\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(\psi;t)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(t),T-t),\quad\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi;0)=\delta_{j}^{i}

(2.23)

for
i,j=1,2,3
. Then
\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\tilde{X}^{\xi};t)
gives
rise a version of the gauge functional defined above
\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(t)
.
Therefore, under the notations we just set up, the representation
(
2.22
) may be written as

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{\tilde{P}}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}\left[\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi;T)1_{\{T<\zeta(\psi)\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{\tilde{P}}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}\left[\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi;t)1_{\{t<\zeta(\psi)\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\psi(t),T-t)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t

(2.24)

where

\zeta(\psi)=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\psi(t)\notin D\right\}.

Next we apply the fundamental duality for conditional laws: since

\nabla\cdot u=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the distribution sense,
so that

\mathbb{\tilde{P}}^{\xi\rightarrow\eta}=\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\circ\tau_{T},

cf.
[
37
]
. Let
Q(\psi,T;t)=\tilde{Q}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};T-t)
.
Since

\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi;s)=\delta_{ij}+\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(\psi;r)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(r),T-r)\textrm{d}r.

(2.25)

Apply this to
\psi\circ\tau_{T}
one obtains that

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};s)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};r)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(T-r),T-r)\textrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+\int_{T-s}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};T-r)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(r),r)\textrm{d}r

so that

\tilde{Q}_{j}^{i}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};T-s)=\delta_{ij}+\int_{s}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{k}^{i}(\psi\circ\tau_{T};T-r)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(r),r)\textrm{d}r.

This means that
t\mapsto Q(\psi,T;t)
is the unique solution to
the ordinary differential equation:

dQ_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;t)=-Q_{k}^{i}(\psi,T;t)q_{j}^{k}(\psi(t),t)dt,\quad Q_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;T)=\delta_{ij}.

By definition,
\tilde{Q}(\psi;t)=Q(\psi\circ\tau_{T},T;T-t)
for
every
t\in[0,T].
Hence, by the duality of the conditional laws,
we may rewrite (
2.24
) in terms of the law of the Taylor diffusion:

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}^{i}(\xi,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;0)1_{\{T<\zeta(\psi\circ\tau_{T})\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;T-t)1_{\{t<\zeta(\psi\circ\tau_{T})\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\psi(T-t),T-t)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;0)1_{\{T<\zeta(\psi\circ\tau_{T})\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{j}^{i}(\psi,T;t)1_{\{T-t<\zeta(\psi\circ\tau_{T})\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\psi(t),t)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,T,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}t

which yields the claim.
∎

We are now in a position to derive an important stochastic representation
formula.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X^{\eta}
be the Taylor diffusion:

\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\eta}=u(X_{t}^{\eta},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}^{\eta}=\eta

(2.26)

for each
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The following stochastic representation
holds:

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{D}K^{j}(x,\eta)\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{k}(\eta,t)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;0)1_{\{t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}K^{j}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{l}(\eta,0)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;s)1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}K^{j}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})g_{\varepsilon}^{l}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s

for any
t>0
and
x\in D
, where
i=1,2,3
.

Proof.

Recall the Biot-Savart law

u(x,t)=\int_{D}K(x,\xi)\wedge\omega(\xi,t)\textrm{d}\xi

where
K
is the singular kernel of the half-space
x_{3}>0
.
By Theorem
2.1

\displaystyle\omega^{k}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{k}(\xi,t)+\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;0)1_{\{t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{l}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;s)1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}g_{\varepsilon}^{l}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s.

(2.27)

Integrating with
\varepsilon^{ijk}K^{j}(x,\cdot)
and using Fubini
theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{D}K^{j}(x,\eta)\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{k}(\eta,t)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{j}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;0)1_{\{t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}^{l}(\eta,0)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{ijk}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{l}^{k}(\eta,t;s)1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}K^{j}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})g_{\varepsilon}^{l}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s

which completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Two dimensional wall-bounded flows

There is a simplified version for two dimensional case.
For two dimensional flows, the non-linear stretching term
A^{i}_{j}\omega^{j}
vanishes identically, so that we can take
Q^{i}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
. Therefore we have the following two dimensional representation formula.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
d=2
, so that
\omega=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}u^{2}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}u^{1}

is a scalar function. Then

\displaystyle\omega(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\xi,t)+\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\right]W_{\varepsilon}(\eta,0)p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}^{\eta\rightarrow\xi}\left[1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}g_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]p_{u}(0,\eta,t,\xi)\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s

(2.28)

for every
\xi\in D
and
t>0
, where
\zeta(\psi)=\inf\left\{s:\psi(s)\notin D\right\}

and
X^{\eta}
is the Taylor diffusion:

\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\eta}=u(X_{t}^{\eta},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}^{\eta}=\eta

(2.29)

for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

As a consequence, we have the following representation for the velocity
u
in two dimensional case.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
d=2
, and let
K=(K^{1},K^{2})
be the Biot-Savart
kernel for the half-plane
D:x_{2}>0
. Then

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}K^{i}(x,\eta)\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\eta,t)\textrm{d}\eta+\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})1_{\left\{t<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\right\}}\right]W_{\varepsilon}(\eta,0)\textrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{t-s<\zeta(X^{\eta}\circ\tau_{t})\}}K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\eta})g_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\eta},s)\right]\textrm{d}\eta\textrm{d}s

(2.30)

for every
x\in D
and
t>0
, where
\zeta(\psi)=\inf\left\{s:\psi(s)\notin D\right\}

and
X^{\eta}
is the Taylor diffusion:

\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\eta}=u(X_{t}^{\eta},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}^{\eta}=\eta

(2.31)

for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

3
2D wall-bounded flows

In the subsequent parts we aim to present a stochastic formulation of a viscous
fluid flow, with kinematic viscosity constant
\nu>0
, which passes
through obstacles, such as a thin plane plate and a wedge. These flows have
been studied as important and classical examples which demonstrate
boundary layer phenomena. Turbulence may be build up near the solid
obstacle when the Reynolds number becomes large.

For simplicity we take a two dimensional model, although we admit
that the three dimensional model is more sophisticated and
will be studied in a future work. Therefore the fluid flow in
question is described by its velocity, a time dependent vector field

u=(u^{1},u^{2})
in the domain
D
.

3.1
A general case

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a general simply connected domain
which is not the whole plane. For such domain, according to Riemann mapping
theorem, there is a one-to-one and onto conformal mapping
T:D\rightarrow\mathbb{H}

where
\mathbb{H}
is the upper half-plane (the mapping is unique up to a rotation
at a point whose image is assigned to
i
for example). Then the Green function for
such domain
D
is given by

G_{D}(x,y)=\Gamma(T(x),T(y))-\Gamma(T(x),\overline{T(y)})

(3.1)

for
x,y\in D
, where
\Gamma(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|
. While the boundary
\partial D
may be complicated,
so some care is needed, although formally
G
satisfies the Dirichlet
boundary condition.

Notice also that
\omega=\nabla\wedge u
and
\nabla\cdot u=0
imply

\Delta u^{1}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\omega,\quad\Delta u^{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\omega\quad\text{ in }D,

(3.2)

and, as
u^{i}
are subject to the no-slip condition, the Green formula implies the Biot-Savart law

u^{i}(x,t)=\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)\omega(y,t)\textrm{d}y,

(3.3)

where the kernel
K
is given by

K^{1}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{2}}G_{D}(x,y),\quad K^{2}(x,y)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1}}G_{D}(x,y).

(3.4)

Hence, given the Green function as in (
3.1
), we might state the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The kernel
K
can be computed as follows

\begin{split}K^{1}(x,y)&=k^{-}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{2}}(y)-k^{+}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{2}}(y),\\
K^{2}(x,y)&=-k^{-}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{1}}(y)+k^{+}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{1}}(y),\end{split}

(3.5)

where the vector functions
k^{-}(x,y),k^{+}(x,y)
are given by

\begin{split}k^{-}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x),T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))}{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))^{2}},\\
k^{+}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x),T^{2}(y)+T^{2}(x))}{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)+T^{2}(x))^{2}},\end{split}

(3.6)

and
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{1}}(y)
and
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y_{2}}(y)
denote the vectors
(\frac{\partial T^{1}}{\partial y_{1}}(y),\frac{\partial T^{2}}{\partial y_{1}}(y))
and
(\frac{\partial T^{1}}{\partial y_{2}}(y),\frac{\partial T^{2}}{\partial y_{2}}(y))
respectively.

As for the numerical simulations we also need to compute the boundary stress
\theta
, we do so by differentiating the representation (
3.3
) with respect to
x
(and taking limits as
x
approaches the boundary). Note that in practice we have to desingularise the kernels (see Section
5
for details), which insures we can differentiate the singular integral representation. Therefore, we can write the representation, according to the lemma above, in terms of derivatives of
k^{-},k^{+}
given explicitly in the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

The derivatives
\dfrac{\partial k^{-}}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial k^{+}}{\partial x}
are given by

\begin{split}\dfrac{\partial(k^{-})^{1}}{\partial x_{i}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigg(\frac{-\frac{\partial T^{1}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)}{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))^{2}}+2(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))\\
\cdot\frac{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))\frac{\partial T^{1}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))\frac{\partial T^{2}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)}{\big((T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))^{2}\big)^{2}}\Bigg),\\
\dfrac{\partial(k^{-})^{2}}{\partial x_{i}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigg(\frac{-\frac{\partial T^{2}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)}{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))^{2}}+2(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))\\
\cdot\frac{(T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))\frac{\partial T^{1}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))\frac{\partial T^{2}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)}{\big((T^{1}(y)-T^{1}(x))^{2}+(T^{2}(y)-T^{2}(x))^{2}\big)^{2}}\Bigg),\end{split}

(3.7)

and

Flows past a thin plate

In terms of real coordinates

3.3
Flows past a wedge obstacle

Suppose the wedge with angle
2\alpha
at its tip (where
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
)
is modelled by

\Lambda=\left\{x=r\textrm{e}^{\textrm{i}\theta}:r\geq 0\textrm{ and }-\alpha\leq\theta\leq\alpha\right\}

so that the fluid occupies the region
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Lambda
. It is again conformally equivalent to the upper half space
\mathbb{H}
,
the conformal mapping
T:D\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is given explicitly as follows:

T(x)=\left(\textrm{e}^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)^{\beta}=\exp\left[\beta\ln|x|+\textrm{i}\beta\arg(\textrm{e}^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x)\right]\quad\textrm{ for }x\in D

where

\beta=\frac{\pi}{2(\pi-\alpha)}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\alpha\beta=\frac{\pi\alpha}{2(\pi-\alpha)}

Therefore

T(x)=\exp\left(\textrm{i}\beta\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)\right)|x|^{\beta}\quad\textrm{ for }x\in D.

In terms of real coordinates we have

T(x)=\left(|x|^{\beta}\cos\left(\beta\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)\right),|x|^{\beta}\sin\left(\beta\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)\right)\right)

x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in D
, where
\arg x\in(0,2\pi)
, so that

\arg(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}\arctan\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}&\textrm{if }x_{1}>0,x_{2}\geq 0,\\
\frac{\pi}{2}&\textrm{if }x_{1}=0,x_{2}>0,\\
\pi+\arctan\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}&\textrm{if }x_{1}<0,\\
\frac{3\pi}{2}&\textrm{if }x_{1}=0,x_{2}<0,\\
2\pi+\arctan\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}&\textrm{if }x_{1}>0,x_{2}\leq 0.\end{cases}

Since
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
\arg x\in(\alpha,2\pi-\alpha)
for
x\in D

\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)=\arg x-\alpha,

hence the real and imaginary parts

G(x,y)=\Gamma(Tx,Ty)-\Gamma(Tx,\overline{Ty})

which vanishes for
x
such that

Tx=\overline{Tx}

|x|^{\beta}\sin\left(\beta\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)\right)=-|x|^{\beta}\sin\left(\beta\arg\left(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x\right)\right)

so that either
x=(0,0)
or

\sin\left(\beta\arg(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x)\right)=0.

That is

\beta\arg(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x)=n\pi

so that

\arg(e^{-\textrm{i}\alpha}x)=2n(\pi-\alpha).

That is

\arg x-\alpha=2n(\pi-\alpha)\quad\textrm{ and }0<\arg x-\alpha<2\pi.

The requirement

0<2n(\pi-\alpha)<2\pi

gives rise to the constraint that
n=0,1
only that is,
\arg x=\alpha

and
\arg x=2\pi-\alpha
which are the edges of the wedge.

, we write the partial derivatives of the map

4
Limiting stochastic representations

The goal of this section is to derive representations similar to those in Section
2
for different domains described above. Note that so far we have a stochastic representation for the velocity
u
, e.g., the one given in Theorem
2.4
, for the half-plane. This representation contains the time-integral with the term
g_{\varepsilon}
given by

g_{\varepsilon}=G-\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-(u\cdot\nabla)\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\nu\Delta\sigma_{\varepsilon},

(4.1)

where
\varepsilon
defines the width of the cutoff modification in
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
. It is therefore interesting theoretically to consider the limits of the representations as the cutoff width parameter
\varepsilon

g_{\varepsilon}
do not contribute to the limit, we are able to simplify the expressions used for simulations in Section
5
.

4.1
Flat-plate case

Let us deal with the flows passing a flat-plate first, that is
D=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}<0\textrm{ or }x_{2}\neq 0\}
.
The flat-plate is modelled by the boundary
\partial D=\{x_{2}=0,x_{1}\geq 0\}
, and the flow is split into upper flow and lower
flow after hitting the plate. The velocity
u
has to satisfy the
no-slip condition, that is,

\lim_{x_{2}\rightarrow 0\pm}u(x_{1},x_{2},t)=0\quad\textrm{ for }x_{1}\geq 0\textrm{ and }t>0.

(4.2)

Therefore there is non-trivial stress on both sides of the plate.
The stress at the upper side of the plate is denoted by
\theta_{+}

and the one at the lower side by
\theta_{-}
, that is

\theta_{\pm}(x_{1},t)=\begin{cases}-\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}u^{1}(x_{1},x_{2},t)\right|_{x_{2}=0\pm}&\textrm{ for }x_{1}\geq 0,\\
0&\textrm{ for }x_{1}<0.\end{cases}

(4.3)

We make a technical assumption that
\theta_{+}
and
\theta_{-}
have continuous derivatives up to second order except at
x_{1}=0
. We also assume that the limit of
-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}u^{1}(x_{1},x_{2},t)
exists as
(x_{1},x_{2})\to(0,0)
in
D
, therefore
\theta_{+}(0,t)=\theta_{-}(0,t)
, for all
t\geq 0
, denoted
\theta(t)
in this case.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a cut-off function which
is smooth on
(0,\infty)
such that
\phi(r)=0
for
r<0
,
\phi(r)=1

for
0\leq r<1/3
and
\phi(r)=0
for
r\geq 2/3
. Define

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},t)=\theta_{+}(x_{1},t)\phi(x_{2}/\varepsilon)+\theta_{-}(x_{1},t)\phi(-x_{2}/\varepsilon)+\theta(t)\phi(|x|/\varepsilon)1_{\{x_{1}<0\}}

(4.4)

for
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Let
W_{\varepsilon}=\omega-\sigma_{\varepsilon}
. Then

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla-\nu\Delta\right)W_{\varepsilon}=G+\rho_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ in }D,

(4.5)

satisfying the boundary condition
\left.W_{\varepsilon}\right|_{\partial D}=0

in the sense that

\lim_{x_{2}\rightarrow 0\pm}W_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},t)=0\quad\textrm{ for }x_{1}\geq 0,

(4.6)

where

\rho_{\varepsilon}=\nu\Delta\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-(u\cdot\nabla)\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Taking into account that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is given as in (
4.4

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x_{2}/\varepsilon)\theta_{+}(x_{1},t)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\phi^{\prime}(x_{2}/\varepsilon)u^{2}(x,t)\theta_{+}(x_{1},t)

\displaystyle+\phi(x_{2}/\varepsilon)\left(\nu\frac{\partial^{2}\theta_{+}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x_{1},t)-\frac{\partial\theta_{+}}{\partial t}(x_{1},t)\right)-\phi(x_{2}/\varepsilon)u^{1}(x,t)\frac{\partial\theta_{+}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},t)

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(-x_{2}/\varepsilon)\theta_{-}(x_{1},t)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\phi^{\prime}(-x_{2}/\varepsilon)u^{2}(x,t)\theta_{-}(x_{1},t)

\displaystyle+\phi(-x_{2}/\varepsilon)\left(\nu\frac{\partial^{2}\theta_{-}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x_{1},t)-\frac{\partial\theta_{-}}{\partial t}(x_{1},t)\right)-\phi(-x_{2}/\varepsilon)u^{1}(x,t)\frac{\partial\theta_{-}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},t)

\displaystyle+\Big(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)\frac{u^{1}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{1}+u^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{2}}{|x|}

\displaystyle-\theta^{\prime}(t)\phi(|x|/\varepsilon)+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)\frac{1}{|x|}\Big)1_{\{x_{1}<0\}}

(4.7)

for any

(4.8)

for
x\in D

The velocity
u(x,t)
is extended to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{2}

trivially by defining
u(x,t)=0
if
x\notin D

\nabla\cdot u=0

\mathbb{R}^{2}
in distribution sense. Let

denote the transition probability density function of the diffusion

\nu\Delta+u\cdot\nabla

p^{D}(s,\xi,t,\eta)
be the transition probability density of the

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\left(\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)p^{D}(0,\xi,t,y)\textrm{d}y\right)\omega_{0}(\xi)\textrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\left(\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)p^{D}(s,\xi,t,y)\textrm{d}y\right)G(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}p^{D}(s,(\xi_{1},0+),t,y)\textrm{d}y\right)\theta_{+}(\xi_{1},s)\textrm{d}\xi_{1}\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}p^{D}(s,(\xi_{1},0-),t,y)\textrm{d}y\right)\theta_{-}(\xi_{1},s)\textrm{d}\xi_{1}\textrm{d}s

(4.25)

for every
x\in D
and
t>0
.

Proof.

As noticed above, the formula is implied by the Biot-Savart law (
3.3
). Note that the following term
(\omega_{0}(y_{1},0+,t)+\omega_{0}(y_{1},0-,t))1_{\{y\in\partial D\}}
does not contribute to the representation as it is zero outside the boundary.
∎

Thanks to the integral representation (
4.25
), we may establish
the random vortex dynamics as follows.

Firstly extend
u(x,t)
to be zero if
x\notin D
, and define the
Taylor diffusion process family
X^{\xi,s}
as the weak solution
to the stochastic differential equation

\begin{cases}\textrm{d}X_{t}^{\xi,s}=u(X_{t}^{\xi,s},t)\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\nu}\textrm{d}B_{t},&\textrm{ for }t\geq s,\\
X_{t}^{\xi,s}=\xi,&\textrm{ for }t\leq s.\end{cases}

(4.26)

Let

\tau_{\xi,s}=\inf\left\{t\geq s:X_{t}^{\xi,s}\in\partial D\right\}.

Then

\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)p^{D}(s,\xi,t,y)\textrm{d}y=\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right],

\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}p^{D}(s,(\xi_{1},0+),t,y)\textrm{d}y=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right|_{\xi_{2}=0+}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right]

and

\int_{D}K^{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}p^{D}(s,(\xi_{1},0-),t,y)\textrm{d}y=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right|_{\xi_{2}=0-}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right].

Therefore the velocity
u(x,t)
can be written as the following

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,0}):t<\tau_{\xi,0}\right]\omega_{0}(\xi)\textrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right]G(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right|_{\xi_{2}=0+}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right]\theta_{+}(\xi_{1},s)\textrm{d}\xi_{1}\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right|_{\xi_{2}=0-}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{i}(x,X_{t}^{\xi,s}):t<\tau_{\xi,s}\right]\theta_{-}(\xi_{1},s)\textrm{d}\xi_{1}\textrm{d}s.

(4.27)

4.2
Flows past a wedge type obstacle

In this subsection we derive a representation similar to the above for the wedge case. Here we will use the local coordinates for boundary components, and this idea will be used to derive a representation for more general domains subsequently.

Recall that the wedge obstacle is given by
\Lambda=\left\{x=r\textrm{e}^{\textrm{i}\theta}:r\geq 0\textrm{ and }-\alpha\leq\theta\leq\alpha\right\}
for a fixed
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, so the domain
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Lambda
. Note that the boundary
\partial D=\{x=re^{i\theta}:r\geq 0,\theta=\pm\alpha\}
has two components denoted
\partial D^{\pm}
correspondingly. We also introduce normal coordinates
n^{\pm},\tau^{\pm}
in neighbourhood of
\partial D^{\pm}
which are given by the following transformation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tau^{\pm}=x_{1}\cos\alpha\pm x_{2}\sin\alpha,\\
&n^{\pm}=-x_{1}\sin\alpha\pm x_{2}\cos\alpha.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.28)

In these coordinates, we denote the components of the vector field
u
by
u^{\tau^{\pm}},u^{n^{\pm}}
. Note that the boundary components are given by
\partial D^{\pm}=\{\tau^{\pm}\geq 0,n^{\pm}=0\}
. In this case, the no-slip condition reads

u(x,t)=\lim_{n^{\pm}\to 0}u(\tau^{\pm},n^{\pm},t)=0,

(4.29)

for any
x\in\partial D^{\pm}
. As in (
4.3
), the stress at the boundary is given by

\theta_{\pm}(\tau^{\pm},t)=\begin{cases}-\left.\frac{\partial u^{\tau^{\pm}}}{\partial n^{\pm}}(\tau^{\pm},n^{\pm},t)\right|_{n^{\pm}=0+}&\textrm{ for }\tau^{\pm}\geq 0,\\
0&\textrm{ for }\tau^{\pm}<0,\end{cases}

(4.30)

as the tangential derivatives
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tau^{\pm}}
vanish due to the no-slip condition. Note that the derivative operators are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau^{\pm}}=\cos\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\pm\sin\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}},\\
&\frac{\partial}{\partial n^{\pm}}=-\sin\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\pm\cos\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}};\end{aligned}\right.

(4.31)

due to the definition (
4.28
).

We introduce the extension
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
as follows

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},t)=\begin{cases}\theta_{+}(\tau^{+},t)\phi(n^{+}/\varepsilon),&\text{ if }\arg x\in[\alpha,\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2}],\\
\theta(t)\phi(|x|/\varepsilon),&\text{ if }\arg x\in(\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha),\\
\theta_{-}(\tau^{-},t)\phi(n^{-}/\varepsilon),&\text{ if }\arg x\in[\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha,2\pi-\alpha];\end{cases}

(4.32)

where the coordinates
n^{\pm},\tau^{\pm}
are given as functions of
x_{1},x_{2}
in (
4.28
), and
\theta(t)
denotes the value of
\theta_{+}(0,t)=\theta_{-}(0,t)
. Then
W^{\varepsilon}=\omega-\sigma_{\varepsilon}
as before satisfies the following equation

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla-\nu\Delta\right)W^{\varepsilon}=G+\rho_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ in }D,

(4.33)

with homogeneous boundary condition

\lim_{n^{\pm}\to 0+}W^{\varepsilon}(\tau^{\pm},n^{\pm},t)=0\quad\textrm{ for }\tau^{\pm}\geq 0,

(4.34)

and initial data

\displaystyle W_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})-\theta_{+}(\tau^{+},0)\phi(n^{+}/\varepsilon)1_{\{\arg x\in[\alpha,\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2}]\}}

\displaystyle-\theta_{-}(\tau^{-},0)\phi(n^{-}/\varepsilon)1_{\{\arg x\in[\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha,2\pi-\alpha]\}}

\displaystyle-\theta(0)\phi(|x|/\varepsilon)1_{\{\arg x\in(\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha)\}},

(4.35)

for
x\in D
.

The function
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\rho_{\varepsilon}=\nu\Delta\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-u\cdot\nabla\sigma_{\varepsilon},

(4.36)

and, using the definition of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, can be written as follows

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Big(\nu\frac{\partial^{2}\theta_{+}}{\partial(\tau^{+})^{2}}(\tau^{+},t)\phi(n^{+}/\varepsilon)+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\theta_{+}(\tau^{+},t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(n^{+}/\varepsilon)-\frac{\partial\theta_{+}}{\partial t}(\tau^{+},t)\phi(n^{+}/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle-u^{\tau^{+}}(\tau^{+},n^{+},t)\frac{\partial\theta_{+}}{\partial\tau^{+}}(\tau^{+},t)\phi(n^{+}/\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}u^{n^{+}}(\tau^{+},n^{+},t)\theta_{+}(\tau^{+},t)\phi^{\prime}(n^{+}/\varepsilon)\Big)1_{\{\arg x\in[\alpha,\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2}]\}}

\displaystyle+\Big(\nu\frac{\partial^{2}\theta_{-}}{\partial(\tau^{-})^{2}}(\tau^{-},t)\phi(n^{-}/\varepsilon)+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\theta_{-}(\tau^{-},t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(n^{-}/\varepsilon)-\frac{\partial\theta_{-}}{\partial t}(\tau^{-},t)\phi(n^{-}/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle-u^{\tau^{-}}(\tau^{-},n^{-},t)\frac{\partial\theta_{-}}{\partial\tau^{-}}(\tau^{-},t)\phi(n^{-}/\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}u^{n^{-}}(\tau^{-},n^{-},t)\theta_{-}(\tau^{-},t)\phi^{\prime}(n^{-}/\varepsilon)\Big)1_{\{\arg x\in[\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha,2\pi-\alpha]\}}

\displaystyle+\Big(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)\frac{u^{1}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{1}+u^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{2}}{|x|}-\theta^{\prime}(t)\phi(|x|/\varepsilon)+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon^{2}}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\theta(t)\phi^{\prime}(|x|/\varepsilon)\frac{1}{|x|}\Big)1_{\{\arg x\in(\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}-\alpha)\}}.

(4.37)

As before, we write the representation for the solution
W^{\varepsilon}
to (

which implies

(4.38)

for
y\in D
and
t>0
.

(4.50)

4.3
General case

We consider a flow in
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
assuming it is a proper simply connected domain. Let us fix a conformal mapping
T:D\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
. Notice that in this case we can parameterise the domain
D
using the coordinates
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{H}
, i.e. the transform is given by
x=T^{-1}(z)
for
x\in\mathbb{H}
. The boundary in this coordinates is given by
\partial D=\{z_{2}=0\}
, and the no-slip condition is written as

\lim_{z_{2}\to 0+}u(z_{1},z_{2},t)=0.

(4.55)

Notice that as the velocity
u
is a vector field, its components
u^{z_{1}},u^{z_{2}}
in new coordinates are transformed correspondingly. We again introduce the boundary stress as

\theta(z_{1},t)=-\left.\frac{\partial u^{z_{1}}}{\partial z_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2},t)\right|_{z_{2}=0+}.

(4.56)

Note that in the subsequent argument we have to assume that the boundary
\partial D
is sufficiently regular, in particular,
T
gives a smooth parametrisation of the boundary (except, maybe, one point in
\partial D
which is sent to infinity by
T
). However, as for the plate and wedge cases we were able to take care of singularities at the boundary
\partial D
, we expect a similar formula to hold more generally, say, for domains with piecewise smooth boundary.

Let us define the extension
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
in new coordinates by

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{2},t)=\theta(z_{1},t)\phi(z_{2}/\varepsilon),

(4.57)

and introduce
W^{\varepsilon}=\omega-\sigma_{\varepsilon}
. It has to satisfy

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla-\nu\Delta\right)W^{\varepsilon}=G+\rho_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ in }D,

(4.58)

with homogeneous boundary condition
\left.W^{\varepsilon}\right|_{\partial D}=0
written in new coordinates as

\lim_{z_{2}\to 0+}W^{\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{2},t)=0

for all
z_{1}
. The initial data for
W^{\varepsilon}
is

W_{0}^{\varepsilon}(z)=\omega_{0}(z_{1},z_{2})-\theta(z_{1},0)\phi(z_{2}/\varepsilon),

(4.59)

and
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\rho_{\varepsilon}=\nu\Delta\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-u\cdot\nabla\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

(4.60)

Therefore, in new coordinates

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}(z,t)=-\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}(z_{1},t)\phi(z_{2}/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle-u^{z_{1}}(z_{1},z_{2},t)\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z_{1}}(z_{1},t)\phi(z_{2}/\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}u^{z_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2},t)\theta(z_{1},t)\phi^{\prime}(z_{2}/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle+\nu|T^{\prime}|^{2}\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial z_{1}^{2}}(z_{1},t)\phi(z_{2}/\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\theta(z_{1},t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(z_{2}/\varepsilon)\Big),

(4.61)

where
T^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
T
as a complex function.

Writing the solution to (
4.58
) in terms of the transition density
p^{D}(s,\xi,t,\eta)
we have

\omega(y,t)=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y,t)+\int_{D}p^{D}(0,\xi,t,y)W^{\varepsilon}_{0}(\xi)\textrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}p^{D}(s,\xi,t,y)(G(\xi,s)+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\xi,s))\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s,

(4.62)

for
y\in D
and
t>0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have the following representation:

\displaystyle\omega(y,t)=\omega(y,t)1_{\{y\in\partial D\}}

\displaystyle+\int_{D}p^{D}(0,\xi,t,y)\omega_{0}(\xi)\textrm{d}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}p^{D}(s,\xi,t,y)G(\xi,s)\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}\Big|_{z_{2}=0}p^{D}(s,z,t,y)\theta(z_{1},s)\textrm{d}z_{1}\textrm{d}s,

(4.63)

for

Experiment 1.
We consider the flow past the flat-plate, i.e.
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(x_{1},0):x_{1}\geq 0\}
. The lattice points are given by
(
5.3
) and
(
5.4
) with
N_{0}=15,N_{1}=30,N_{2}=45
. Therefore, we use 961 points in the outer layer and 1,426 points for each of the two thin boundary layers. With
H=6
and
H_{0}=0.1
determining the size of the domain and the thickness of the thin layers respectively, we find the mesh constants
h_{0}=\frac{H}{N_{0}}=0.4,h_{1}=\frac{H}{N_{1}}=0.2,h_{2}=\frac{H_{0}}{N_{2}}\approx 0.0022
. We set the parameter
\varepsilon=0.05
for the boundary sums in this simulation.

We initialise the vector field
u_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=(U_{0}|x_{2}|(6-|x_{2}|)/9,0)
for
-H\leq x_{1},x_{2}\leq H
, where
U_{0}=\frac{\nu Re}{L}=125
for
\nu=0.1,L=6
and
Re=7500
. Thus, the initial vorticity
\omega_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=-U_{0}(-2|x_{2}|+6)/9\cdot\textrm{sgn}(x_{2})
, where for
x_{2}=0
and
x_{1}<0
we assume
\omega_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
, and the initial boundary stress
\theta_{+}(x_{1},0)=-2U_{0}/3
and
\theta_{-}(x_{1},0)=2U_{0}/3
. The external force
G
is taken to be identically zero.

Experiment 2.
In this experiment, the initial velocity is again taken to be
u_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})=(U_{0}|x_{2}|(6-|x_{2}|)/9,0)
and
G\equiv 0
. The lattice points and other parameters are taken as before, however, we alter the representation for
\hat{u}
. We ignore the boundary sums in this case, that is we compute

\hat{u}(x,t_{k+1})=\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in D}A_{i_{1},i_{2}}\omega_{i_{1},i_{2}}K_{\delta}(x,X^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{t_{k}})1_{\{t_{k}<\zeta(X^{i_{1},i_{2}}\circ\tau_{t_{k}})\}}.

Therefore, we have to use a variant of Algorithm
1
with the above representation for
\hat{u}
. The results are shown in Figures
5.6
,
5.7
and
5.8
for the outer flow and boundary flows respectively, and Figures
5.9
and Figures
5.10
for boundary stress values.

We notice that this version of the numerical scheme still allows to capture some boundary phenomena as the boundary flows display certain chaotic behaviour. However, at large times
t
one has large regions close to the boundary where the velocity does not change direction. We conclude therefore that for simulating the boundary flows it is essential to use the boundary sums in the velocity representation as they contribute to the chaotic turbulent motion.
