Abstract
. The most simple and famous divergent power series coming from ODE may be the so-called Euler series ∑ n ≥ 0 ( − 1 ) n ​ n ! ​ x n + 1 \sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}\,n!\,x^{n+1} , that, as well as all its positive powers, is Borel-summable in any direction excepted the negative real half-axis (see [ 2 ] or [ 7 ] ). By considering a family of linear q q -difference operators associated with a given first order non-homogenous q q -difference equation, it will be shown that the summability order of q q -analoguous counterparties of Euler series depends upon of the degree of power under consideration.



1.
Introduction

It is well-known that, given any direction starting from the origin of the complex plan, the set of all Borel-summable series in this direction forms a differential algebra; see
[
7
, Proposition 1.3.4.2]
or
[
2
, Theorem 36]
. This is not the same for the summability with respect to the power series solutions of
q
-difference equations. Indeed, if
q>1
and

(1.1)

\hat{E}_{q}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}x^{n}\,,

the divergent power series
\hat{E}_{q}(x)
is
Gq
-summable of only one level but its square
\hat{E}_{q}(x)^{2}
is
Gq
-summable of two levels; see
[
11
, §4.3.8]
and
[
8
, Théorème 2.2.1]
. The first purpose of this paper is to obtain a linear
q
-difference equation for every positive power of the power series
\hat{E}_{q}(x)
. This will allow one to see the different level of summability for each of these corresponding power series.

In what follows, let
q
be a given nonzero complex number, and let
\sigma_{q}
be the associated
q
-difference operator defined by
\sigma_{q}f(x)=f(qx)
. By direct computation, one finds the series defined by (
1.1
) in the above satisfies term by term the following linear
q
-difference equation :

(1.2)

(x\sigma_{q}+1)y=1\,.

Now, write (
1.2
) into the form
x\sigma_{q}y=1-y
and then square both sides. As
(\sigma_{q}y)^{2}=\sigma_{q}(y^{2})
, one obtains that

x^{2}\sigma_{q}(y^{2})-y^{2}=1-2y
. By using again (
1.2
), one gets the following equation:

(x\sigma_{q}+1)(x^{2}\sigma_{q}-1)(y^{2})=x+1-2(x\sigma_{q}+1)y\,,

what implies that

(1.3)

(x\sigma_{q}+1)(x^{2}\sigma_{q}-1)\hat{E}_{q}(x)^{2}=x-1\,.

We shall explain how to obtain a linear
q
-difference equation for
\hat{E}_{q}(x)^{n}
. For doing that, we shall start by recalling some non-commutative rings of
q
-difference operators and then arrive at a
n
-th order such operator associated with any given
n
-th power of a power series solution of some first order non-homogenous
q
-difference equation. See Theorem
2.1
in §
2
and its generalisation stated in Theorem
4.1
in §
4
. Section
3
is reserved to the proofs of Theorem
2.1
and two related Lemmas. In the last section, we shall apply, respectively, both Theorems
2.1
and
4.1
to the above-mentioned power series
\hat{E}_{q}(x)
and an other
q
-analog
\hat{E}(x;q)
of the classic Euler series. At the end of the paper will be outlined some results about the summability of both
\hat{E}_{q}(x)
and
\hat{E}(x;q)
when
q>1
; see Theorem
5.1
.

Throughout the present paper, we shall limit ourself to the
q
-summation method studied in our previous works
[
11
]
and
[
8
]
, by means of a
q
-Laplace integral involving the multivalued function
e^{\log^{2}x/2\ln q}
. There exists another summation, using a Jacobi theta function; see
[
10
]
. Comparing both summations can be seen as part of Stokes analysis, and this would be interesting to find identities on the associated special functions.

2.
Notation and statements

As usual, we will denote by
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
or
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
the integral domain constituting of the germs of holomorphic functions at
x=0
in the complex plan or that of the power series of indeterminate
x
, respectively. Their respective corresponding field of fractions will be denoted by
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}]\{x\}
or
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
. One has
\mathbb{C}\{x\}\subset\mathbb{C}[[x]]
, as well as
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}]\{x\}\subset\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
, in a natural way via the classic Taylor or Laurent series.

Let
R
be one of the above-mentioned
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
,
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
,
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}]\{x\}
and
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
. By replacing
x
with
qx
,
the
q
-difference operator
\sigma_{q}
acts as being an automorphism over
R
. Let
\sigma_{q}^{k}=\sigma_{q^{k}}
for any integer
k
; in particular,
\sigma_{q}^{0}
is simply the identity map on
R
. We will denote by
R[\sigma_{q}]
the set of the (linear)
q
-difference operators whose coefficients belong to
R
,
i.e.

L\in R[\sigma_{q}]
if
L=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\sigma_{q}^{k}
, where
a_{k}\in R
. By identifying each element of
R
with the corresponding multiplication map in
R
, the set
R[\sigma_{q}]
constitutes itself a non-commutative ring for the compoisition of operators. Given
L_{1}
,
L_{2}\in R[\sigma_{q}]
, we will write
L_{1}L_{2}
in stead of
L_{1}\circ L_{2}
.

Furthermore, we will denote by
\nu_{0}(f)\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
the valuation of any given element
f
of the field
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
“at
x=0
”. This means that (i)
\nu_{0}(f)=\infty
iff
f
is the identically vanishing series, and (ii)
v_{0}(f)=\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
iff
f\equiv c\,x^{\nu}

\bmod

x^{\nu+1}\,\mathbb{C}[[x]]
for some nonzero complex number
c
.

Throughout the whole section,
P
will be some given nonzero power series belonging to the field
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
. Define the associated family of power series
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows:
P_{0}=0
,
P_{1}=P
, and

(2.1)

P_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-x\sigma_{q})^{k}P\quad\textrm{for}\quad n\geq 2.

By noticing that
(-x\sigma_{q})^{k}P=(-x)^{k}\,q^{k(k-1)/2}\,\sigma_{q}^{k}P
, it follows from
(
2.1
) that, for any pair
(n,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle P_{n+\ell}-P_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{n+\ell-1}(-x)^{k}\,q^{k(k-1)/2}\,\sigma_{q}^{k}P

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{n}\,c\,q^{n\nu+n(n-1)/2}x^{\nu+n}\ \bmod\ x^{\nu+n+1}\,\mathbb{C}[[x]]\,,

where
c\not=0
and
\nu=\nu_{0}(P)\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This implies that

(2.2)

P_{m}\not=P_{n}\qquad\textrm{if}\qquad m\not=n.

Given any positive integer
n\geq 1
, let
\{L_{n,k}^{P}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}\subset\left(\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]\right)[\sigma_{q}]
be the associated family of
q
-difference operators defined in the following manner:

(2.3)

\displaystyle L_{n,1}^{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P_{1}}\left(x^{n}\sigma_{q}-(-1)^{n}\right)=\frac{1}{P}\left(x^{n}\sigma_{q}-(-1)^{n}\right);

(2.4)

\displaystyle L_{n,k+1}^{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P_{k+1}}\left(x^{n-k}\sigma_{q}-(-1)^{n-k}\right)\,L_{n,k}^{P},\quad 1\leq k<n\,.

Specifically, letting
k=n
, one gets the following form of the
n
-th order
q
-difference operator
L_{n,n}^{P}\in\left(\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]\right)[\sigma_{q}]
:

(2.5)

L_{n,n}^{P}=\frac{1}{P_{n}}\left(x\sigma_{q}+1\right)\frac{1}{P_{n-1}}\left(x^{2}\sigma_{q}-1\right)\cdots\frac{1}{P_{1}}\left(x^{n}\sigma_{q}-(-1)^{n}\right)\,.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n
be an integer
\geq 1
, let
P
,
f\in\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
with
P\not=0
, and suppose that the following
q
-difference equation is satisfied:

(2.6)

x\sigma_{q}f+f=P\,.

Then:

(2.7)

L_{n,n}^{P}(f^{n})=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\,,

where
L_{n,n}^{P}
is the
q
-difference operator defined by (
2.5
).

Theorem
2.1

will be proved with the help of the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(n,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
1\leq k\leq n
, and let
P
,
f
be as in Theorem
2.1
. For
{0\leq j\leq k}
, define
A_{k;j}\in\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}][[x]]
by

(2.8)

A_{k;j}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\not=j\\
0\leq\ell\leq k\end{subarray}}\left(P_{j}-P_{\ell}\right).

One has:

(2.9)

L_{n,k}^{P}(f^{n})=(-1)^{k(2n-k+1)/2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{A_{k;j}}\left(f-P_{j}\right)^{n}\,.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n
be a nonnegative integer,
\mathfrak{C}
an extension field of
\mathbb{C}
, and let
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})\in\mathfrak{C}^{n+1}
such that
\alpha_{j}\not=\alpha_{\ell}
for

. Define the
(n+1)
-uplet
(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{n})\in\mathfrak{C}^{n+1}
as follows:

a_{j}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\not=j\\
0\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}\left(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{\ell}\right)

(
a_{0}=1
if

).
The following identity holds in the ring
\mathfrak{C}[T]
of the polynomial functions over
\mathfrak{C}

(2.10)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{j}}\,(T-\alpha_{j})^{n}=(-1)^{n}\,.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n,k}=(-1)^{k(2n-k+1)/2}

B_{n,k;j}=x^{n-k}\sigma_{q}\left(\frac{1}{A_{k;j}}\left(f-P_{j}\right)^{n}\right)

.

Our next remark goes to another
q
-analog of the following so-called Euler series:

(5.2)

\hat{E}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}\,n!\,x^{n+1}\,.

This power series is divergent for all
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
but Borel-summable in every direction excepted
\mathbb{R}^{-}
. It satisfies the following first order ODE:

(5.3)

(x\,\delta+1)y=x,\quad\delta=x\partial_{x}=x\frac{d\ }{dx}.

Letting
Y=\hat{E}(x)^{2}
, one can check that

(5.4)

(x\,\delta+1-x)\,(x\,\delta+2)Y=2x^{2}.

Indeed, if
y=\hat{E}(x)
, one deduces from (
5.3
) that
x\delta(y^{2})=2x\,y\,\delta y=2y(x-y)=2xy-2y^{2}
. This means that
(x\delta+2)(y^{2})=2x\,y
. Thus, applying again (
5.3
) yields that

\displaystyle(x\,\delta+1)\,\frac{1}{2x}\,(x\,\delta+2)\,y^{2}=x\,.
In this way, one gets (
5.4
), using the identity
\displaystyle(x\,\delta+1)\,\frac{1}{2x}=\frac{1}{2x}\,(x\,\delta+1-x)
in the non-commutative ring
\mathbb{C}(x)[\delta]
of the differential operators over
\mathbb{C}(x)
.

Besides, the correspondances

\displaystyle n\leftrightarrow\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}

\hat{E}(x;q)=x+\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}\frac{(1-q)...(1-q^{n})}{(1-q)^{n}}x^{n+1}.

This can be written in term of a basic hypergeometric series as follows:

\hat{E}(x;q)=\displaystyle x\,{}_{2}\phi_{1}(q,q;0;q,-\frac{x}{1-q})=x\,{}_{2}\phi_{0}(q^{-1},q^{-1};-;q^{-1},\frac{x}{1-q^{-1}}).
