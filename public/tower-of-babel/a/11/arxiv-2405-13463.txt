Exploring Convexity in Normed Spaces
Ryan L. Acosta Babb

Abstract
Normed spaces appear to have very little going for them: aside from the hackneyed linear structure, you get a norm whose only virtue, aside from separating points, is the Triangle Inequality. What could you possibly prove with that? As it turns out, quite a lot. In this article we will start by considering basic convexity properties of normed spaces, and gradually build up to some of the highlights of Functional Analysis, emphasizing how these notions of convexity play a key role in proving many surprising and deep results.



¿Dónde vas con la desigualdad triangular?

(“Where do you think you’re going with just the triangle inequality?”)

– Rafael Payá Albert

1
The triangle inequality.

The basic (one might add “only”) tool in a normed vector space
(X,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is the
Triangle Inequality

\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert\leqslant\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\left\lVert y\right\rVert.

(1)

Its meaning should be evident to anyone who has tried crossing a rectangular field “the long way”
as opposed to cutting diagonally across:
the longest side of a triangle is never greater than the sum of the lengths of the remaining sides.

This may look innocent enough, but as we shall see throughout this article,
the triangle inequality (and some of its cousins) play a crucial role in many types of arguments in functional analysis.
After exploring the geometric consequences of the triangle inequality in some detail,
we shall show how it features in proofs of the uniqueness of Hahn–Banach extensions,
that it can upgrade weak convergence to norm convergence, and even show that a
a mild strengthening of the triangle inequality implies reflexivity—a profound notion with far-reaching consequences in the study of Banach Spaces.
But first,
we wish to bring the reader’s attention to a different geometric interpretation of the triangle inequality:

convexity
.

Recall that a set is
convex
if it contains all line segments between any two of its points.
Formally, we say that
C\subset X
is convex if for any
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
,
tx+(1-t)y\in C
.
Inequality (
1
) is then
equivalent
to the convexity of the closed unit ball in
X
, that is

\operatorname{ball}{X}:=\{x\in X:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leqslant 1\}.

Proposition 1
.

A function
p\colon X\to[0,\infty)
with the property that
p(\lambda x)=\left|\lambda\right|p(x)

for all
x\in X
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfies (
1
)
if, and only if, the set
\{x\in X:p(x)\leqslant 1\}
is convex.

Proof.

Let
B_{p}:=\{x\in X:p(x)\leqslant 1\}
.
It is straightforward to check that
B_{p}
is convex if
p
satisfies the triangle inequality.
Conversely, assume that
B_{p}
is convex and consider
x,y\in X
with
p(x),p(y)\neq 0
.
If we then let

t:=\frac{p(x)}{p(x)+p(y)}\quad\text{so}\quad 1-t=\frac{p(y)}{p(x)+p(y)},

then
t\in(0,1)
.
Since
x/p(x)
and
y/p(y)
lie in
B_{p}
, it follows by convexity that

tx/p(x)+(1-t)y/p(y)\in B_{p}
.
Unraveling the definitions, this is equivalent to

p\left(\frac{x}{p(x)+p(y)}+\frac{y}{p(x)+p(y)}\right)\leqslant 1,

and the triangle inequality follows.
∎

If by “ball” we mean the usual (i.e., Euclidean) disk or ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively, then this should be fairly clear.
But what happens at the boundary? Take two points
x
and
y
with
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}=1
.
Then it is clear that no point on the line joining
x
and
y
will meet the boundary again.
In particular, their midpoint
\tfrac{x+y}{2}
does not lie on the boundary, i.e.,

\left\lVert\frac{x+y}{2}\right\rVert_{2}<1.

In other words, the triangle inequality is strict:

\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert_{2}<\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}\quad\text{whenever}\quad x\neq y\text{ on }\partial(\operatorname{ball}{\mathbb{R}^{2}}).

(2)

Such spaces are called
strictly convex
.

Figure 1:
Strict convexity of the Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: the midpoint
z=\tfrac{x+y}{2}

never lies on the circle.

Definition 2
.

A normed space
(X,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is
strictly convex
if
whenever
x,y
lie on the boundary of
\operatorname{ball}{X}
with
\left\lVert\tfrac{x+y}{2}\right\rVert=1
, we have
x=y
.

Figure
1
suggests a slightly stronger statement.
Let
z=\tfrac{x+y}{2}
. If we separate
x
and
y
enough along the circle,
we can make
\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}
(i.e., the segment
Oz
) as small as we like.
In fact, more is true: given any separation
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}
, we can ensure a uniform upper bound on
\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}
.
This follows from the
Law of Cosines
.

Proposition 3
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,2]
there is a
\delta\in(0,1)
such that,
for all
x,y
on the unit circle with
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}>\varepsilon
, we have
\left\lVert\tfrac{x+y}{2}\right\rVert_{2}<\delta
.

Proof.

In Figure
1
, apply the Law of Cosines to the triangles
\triangle Oxy
and
\triangle Oxz
to see

\cos(\angle x)=\frac{\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert_{2}},

which, upon noting that
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}=1
and
x-z=\tfrac{x-y}{2}
, yields

\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=1+\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Thus, if
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}>\varepsilon
, then
\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}<\delta
provided that
\delta>\sqrt{1-\tfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{2}}
.
∎

A trivial modification to the above argument shows that we can even take
x
and
y
to lie anywhere
in the ball, not only on the boundary. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4
.

A normed vector space
(X,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is
uniformly convex
if for every
{\varepsilon\in(0,2]}

there is a
\delta\in(0,1)
such that, whenever
x,y\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
with
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert>\varepsilon
,
we have
\left\lVert\tfrac{x+y}{2}\right\rVert<\delta
.

Intuitively, the convexity is
uniform
in the sense that given any two points with a prescribed separation,
we have a uniform bound on their midpoint.

Thus, Proposition
3
tells us that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Euclidean norm is uniformly convex.
(In fact, the argument clearly generalizes to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
n\geqslant 2
, and the statement is trivial for
n=1
.)
The next corollary is immediate.

Corollary 5
.

Any uniformly convex space is strictly convex.

Whence we derive a rigorous proof of our earlier equation (
2
).

It should not have escaped the attentive reader that such convexity properties speak more to the virtues of the
norm

than the underlying vector space.

Figure 2:
Balls in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{p})
for various values of
p>0
.
(Modified from Go Further, CC BY 4.0
\langle
https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0
\rangle
, via Wikimedia Commons).

Indeed, if we replace the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
1
-norm or “Manhattan Norm”

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|x_{k}\right|,

then we lose uniform convexity.
For
n=2
this is geometrically obvious: the unit ball is now a square (see Figure
2
),
so that strict convexity fails along the “straight edges” of the boundary.
Hence,
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1})
is not uniformly convex, thanks to Corollary
5
.
We encourage the reader to prove the generalization of this unfortunate fact to
n\geqslant 2
.

After warming up in finite dimensions, the next “nice” example of a normed space is the inner product space.
A moment’s reflection on the proof of Proposition
3
should convince the reader that such spaces are indeed uniformly convex.

Proposition 6
.

If
(X,\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle)
is an inner product space with norm
\left\lVert x\right\rVert:=\sqrt{\left\langle{x,x}\right\rangle}
, then
(X,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is uniformly,
hence also strictly convex.

The proof is a standard exercise in the use of the Parallelogram Rule, which functions as a substitute for the Law of Cosines.
1
1

1

In fact, one can use the Law of Cosines to
define
angles in a real inner product space as follows:
given linearly independent vectors
x,y
, their
angle
is the unique
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
such that

\cos\alpha=\frac{\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}}{2\left\lVert x\right\rVert\left\lVert y\right\rVert}=\frac{\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\left\lVert y\right\rVert}.

Using this fact we can easily devise a norm that is strictly but not uniformly convex.

Example 7
.

On the vector space
\ell^{1}
, define the norm

\left\lVert x\right\rVert:=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}}+\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}.

If
x
and
y
are distinct points on the ball of
(\ell^{1},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
, then we have

\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}<\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}},

as a consequence of Proposition 6. Hence,

\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert<\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}}\right)+\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}\right)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\left\lVert y\right\rVert=2.

Thus, the space is strictly convex. However, if for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define
two
\ell^{1}
sequences
x_{N}:=(x_{N,k})_{k}
and
y_{N}:=(y_{N,k})_{k}
by

x_{N,k}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\leqslant N,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{N,k}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }N<k\leqslant 2N,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

then

\left\lVert x_{N}\right\rVert=\left\lVert y_{N}\right\rVert=N+\sqrt{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert x_{N}-y_{N}\right\rVert=2N+\sqrt{2N},

but

\left\lVert\frac{x_{N}+y_{N}}{2}\right\rVert=\frac{2N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2N}}{2};

dividing throughout by
N+\sqrt{N}
shows that we can make
\tfrac{\left\lVert x_{N}+y_{N}\right\rVert}{2(N+\sqrt{N})}

as close as we like to 1, while keeping
\frac{\left\lVert x_{N}-y_{N}\right\rVert}{N+\sqrt{N}}\geqslant\sqrt{2}
.
Hence,
(\ell^{1},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is not uniformly convex.

Luckily,
most

\ell^{p}
—and, indeed,
L^{p}(\mu)
—spaces are uniformly convex.

Theorem 8
.

If
1<p<\infty
, then
L^{p}
is uniformly convex.

Proof.

See Theorem 4.7.15 in
[
1
]
.
∎

It is not hard to construct counterexamples to strict convexity in
L^{1}
and
L^{\infty}
.

Example 9
.

Consider the following elements in
\ell^{\infty}
:

x:=(1,1,0,\ldots)\quad\text{and}\quad y:=(0,1,1,0,\ldots).

Then, clearly

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}}=\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}}=1,\quad\text{but}\quad\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}}=2.

Hence,
\ell^{\infty}
is not strictly convex
and so, by Corollary 5, it is also not uniformly convex.

2
Beyond the triangle inequality.

So what can one do with these various flavors of convexity? As it turns out, quite a lot!

In this section, we will pick three more advanced topics—Hahn–Banach extensions,
weak convergence, and reflexivity—motivating and defining each one as we go.
We will then see how convexity and its cousins interact with these notions
in surprising ways.

First we need to (re)acquaint ourselves with the (topological) dual of a normed vector space.
A linear
functional
on
X
is just a fancy name for a linear map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{K}
,
where
\mathbb{K}
is the field of the vector space
X
(usually
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
).
Such a map is continuous if, and only if, it is
bounded
in the sense that
the restriction of
f
to
\operatorname{ball}{X}
is bounded.
2
2

2

Of course, linear maps cannot literally be bounded since
\left|f(nx)\right|=n\left|f(x)\right|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
!
But this is “cheating”:
f
is “really” only interesting on
\partial(\operatorname{ball}{X})
, i.e., when
\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1
,
since
f(x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert f(x/\left\lVert x\right\rVert)
(
x\neq 0
).

The set of bounded linear functionals on
X
is called the
topological dual
or
dual
, for short,
denoted by
X^{*}
.
(The “algebraic dual” is just the set of linear functionals, no continuity required.)
If you haven’t ever done so, go ahead and check that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}}:=\sup\{\left|f(x)\right|\colon\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1\}

is a norm on
X^{*}
—I’ll wait.

It is common to write
\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle
for
f(x)
, since this gives us a pleasing symmetry:
we can think of
f\in X^{*}
as a function on
X
:
x\mapsto f(x)
;
or we can think of
x\in X
as a function on
X^{*}
:
f\mapsto f(x)
(evaluation at
x
).
We easily check that this is a bounded linear functional, and so belongs to the dual of
X^{*}
:
the
double dual
,
X^{**}
.

Why should we care about dual spaces? Because they are often much more helpful
than the original vector spaces!

As a warm up, recall from linear algebra that the
only
linear functionals
are the maps
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
T(v)=u\cdot v
for some vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
(This is obvious if you remember that
T
has to be an
n\times 1
matrix!)

This is also true in a general inner product space…provided we assume that it is
complete with respect to the norm
\left\lVert u\right\rVert=\sqrt{\left\langle{u,u}\right\rangle}
.
We call a complete inner product space a
Hilbert space
,
and it comes with the following powerful theorem.

Theorem 10
(Riesz Representation Theorem).

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with the inner product
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle
,
and consider a bounded linear functional
T\colon H\to\mathbb{K}
.
Then, there is a unique vector
u\in H
such that
T(v)=\left\langle{u,v}\right\rangle

for all
v\in H
. Furthermore,
\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{H^{*}}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert
.

Proof.

See Theorem I.3.4. in
[
2
]
.
∎

Example 11
.

Let
f\in C^{2}[0,1]
and consider the ODE

-u^{\prime\prime}+u=f\quad\text{with}\quad u(0)=u(1)=0.

(3)

Suppose we have a solution
u\in C^{2}[0,1]
.
If we multiply by any
\varphi\in C^{2}[0,1]
with
\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)=0

and integrate by parts, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}(u^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}+u\varphi)=\int_{0}^{1}f\varphi.

(4)

If we can solve
this
equation for some
u
(and arbitrary
\varphi
as above),
then we will have solved (
3
).
To do this, define the inner product and norm

\left\langle{u,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\star}:=\int_{0}^{1}(u^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}+u\varphi)\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert^{2}_{\star}=\int_{0}^{1}\left[(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}+\varphi^{2}\right],

and consider
H_{0}^{1}[0,1]
, the completion of

C_{0}^{2}[0,1]:=\{\varphi\in C^{2}[0,1]:\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)=0\}

with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\star}
.
(Note that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\star}
still makes sense for
f\in C^{2}[0,1]
,
since
f
and
f^{\prime}
are continuous and bounded on
[0,1]
.)

As discussed, the only bounded linear functionals on
H_{0}^{1}
are those of the form

\varphi\mapsto\left\langle{u,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\star}
for some
u\in H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
.
What about the functional
\varphi\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}f\varphi
?
This is clearly linear, and bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\int_{0}^{1}f\varphi\leqslant\left(\int_{0}^{1}f^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\star}\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\star}.

Hence, by the Riesz Representation Theorem there is a
u\in H_{0}^{1}[0,1]
such that

\left\langle{u,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\star}=\int_{0}^{1}f\varphi\quad\text{for all}\quad\varphi\in C^{2}_{0}[0,1].

Thus,
u
is a solution to (
4
).
The extra step of showing that
u\in C^{2}[0,1]
actually solves (
3
) would take
us too far afield. (The interested reader may consult chapter 6 of
[
3
]
.)

The moral of this example is that working with linear functionals makes our life easier:
by constructing an appropriate functional in the dual space,
we can deduce the existence of a solution to a problem in the original space.

Hahn–Banach Extensions.

What if we don’t have an inner product, but just a norm?
We will still assume completeness, of course; a complete normed space is a
Banach space
.

While we cannot say that
every
functional in
X^{*}
arises from an “inner product”
(because we don’t have inner products), we can often construct linear functionals
by defining them on smaller subsets and then extending them to the whole space.

A famous result called the
Hahn–Banach Theorem
implies that we can find an extension
of
f
to the whole of
X
without decreasing its norm.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X
be a Banach space with norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
and
U\subset X
be a subspace.
If
f\colon U\to\mathbb{K}
is a bounded linear functional on
U
,
then
f
admits an extension
g\in X^{*}
with the following two properties:

1.

g
agrees with
f
on
U
:
g|_{U}\equiv f
; and

2.

g
has the same norm as
f
:
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{X^{*}}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{U^{*}}
.

A functional
g
as above is called a
Hahn–Banach extension of
f
.
(For a proof, as well as a discussion of the Hahn–Banach Theorem and its consequences,
see section III.6 in
[
2
]
.)

Let us see how this works in an example.

Example 13
.

Let
x\in X
. We seek a functional
g\in X^{*}
that tells us what the norm of
x
is.
For instance, we could consider the 1-dimensional subspace
Y:=\left\{\alpha x:\alpha\in\mathbb{K}\right\}

and the functional
f\colon Y\to\mathbb{K}
defined as follows:

f(\alpha x):=\alpha\left\lVert x\right\rVert.

Now,
f(x)=f(1\cdot x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
.
Furthermore,
\left|f(\alpha x)\right|=\left|\alpha\right|\left\lVert x\right\rVert=\left\lVert\alpha x\right\rVert
.
On the other hand,
f(\frac{1}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\cdot x)=\frac{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}=1
.
Hence,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{Y^{*}}=1
.
But
f\notin X^{*}
since
f
is only defined on
Y
.
Here is where the Hahn–Banach Theorem comes to the rescue,
as it guarantees that there is an extension
g\colon X\to\mathbb{K}

of
f
with the same norm, i.e.
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{Y^{*}}=\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
.

In a Hilbert space, such extensions are unique, as can be seen from the Riesz Representation Theorem.
As it turns out,
strict convexity
(hence also uniform convexity) of the dual space
is enough to yield uniqueness in a general Banach space.

Theorem 14
.

Let
X
be a Banach space with strictly convex dual
X^{*}
.
Suppose
U\subset X
is a subspace and
f:U\to\mathbb{K}
a bounded linear functional.
Then
f
has a unique Hahn–Banach extension in
X^{*}
.

Proof.

The existence of an extension follows from the Hahn–Banach Theorem.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert=1
.
So suppose that
g,h\in X^{*}
and that both extend
f
with norm
1
. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert g+h\right\rVert_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{ball}{X}}\left|\left\langle{g,x}\right\rangle+\left\langle{h,x}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{x\in\operatorname{ball}{U}}\left|\left\langle{g,x}\right\rangle+\left\langle{h,x}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=2\sup_{x\in\operatorname{ball}{U}}\left|\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle\right|=2.

Thus,
\left\lVert g+h\right\rVert=2
, so
g=h
by the strict convexity of
X^{*}
.
∎

Corollary 15
.

Hahn–Banach extensions are unique in
L^{p}
(
1<p<\infty
).

This theorem not only grants us uniqueness in
L^{p}
,
but it also gives us a clue about where to find counter-examples to uniqueness if we relax the assumption
of strict convexity.

Example 16
.

Recall that
(\ell^{1})^{*}=\ell^{\infty}
.
We will exploit Example
9
to find our extensions.
Consider the space
U
to be the span of
e_{2}=(0,1,0,\ldots)
.
Define the functional
f\colon\ell^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\left\langle{f,(x_{k})}\right\rangle:=x_{2},

which is clearly bounded with norm
1
over
U
.
(As an element of
\ell^{\infty}
,
f=e_{2}
, since
\left\langle{(f_{k}),(x_{k})}\right\rangle=\sum f_{k}x_{k}
,
and
f
is picking out the 2nd element of the sequence
(x_{k})
.)
Now take

a:=(1,1,0,\ldots)\quad\text{and}\quad b:=(0,1,1,0,\ldots).

Both are in
\operatorname{ball}\ell^{\infty}
and, letting
g
and
h
represent the functionals
induced by
a
and
b
, we have

\left\langle{g,x}\right\rangle:=\sum_{k}a_{k}x_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle{h,x}\right\rangle:=\sum_{k}b_{k}x_{k},

which both agree with
f
on
U
.

Weak Convergence.

Another nifty trick used by analysts to find solutions to problems
is to apply some notion of compactness.

Example 17
(Mini Extreme Value Theorem).

Suppose we have a bounded, continuous function
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let
m=\inf_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)
, which exists since
f
is bounded.
We want to find a point
x^{*}\in[a,b]
where
m
is attained, i.e.,
f(x^{*})=m
.
Well, there is a sequence
x_{n}\in[a,b]
such that
f(x_{n})\to m
.
But
[a,b]
is sequentially compact (or has the Bolzano–Weierstrass property).
Hence, there is a convergent subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
of
(x_{n})_{n}
with a limit
x^{*}\in[a,b]
.
Since
f
is continuous, it follows that
f(x_{n_{k}})\to f(x^{*})
.
On the other hand,
f(x_{n_{k}})\to m
. Hence,
m=f(x^{*})
since limits are unique.

Neat! However, in an infinite-dimensional vector space,
bounded sequences don’t always have convergent subsequences.

Example 18
.

Consider the sequence of sequences
e_{n}
with a
1
in the
n
th place
and 0s everywhere else.
In
\ell^{2}
, this sequence is clearly bounded:
\left\lVert e_{n}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}=1
for all
n
.
But whenever
n\neq k
, the vectors
e_{n}
and
e_{k}
form a right-angle triangle
in the
(e_{n},e_{k})
-plane, and so
\left\lVert e_{n}-e_{k}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}=\sqrt{2}
.
Thus,
(e_{n})
has no Cauchy subsequences in
\ell^{2}
, and therefore no convergent subsequences.

Once again, linear functionals come to the rescue.

Definition 19
.

We say that a sequence
x_{n}\in X

converges weakly
to
x\in X
if

\left\langle{f,x_{n}}\right\rangle\to\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in X^{*}.

We write
x_{n}\rightharpoonup x
.

Oftentimes, weak convergence has nicer properties than norm convergence.
For instance, in
L^{p}
spaces (
1<p<\infty
), bounded sequences have
weakly
-convergent subsequences,
and this fact can be used to solve problems using the method form Example
17
.
(This is explored, e.g., in chapter 8 of
[
3
]
.)

Clearly, norm convergence implies weak convergence.
The converse is not true in general. (Otherwise there would be little point to weak convergence, wouldn’t there?)
However,
sometimes
one can bump up weak convergence to norm convergence.
For instance, it is easy to show that, in a Hilbert space

x_{n}\rightharpoonup x\text{ and }\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert\to\left\lVert x\right\rVert\quad\text{together imply}\quad x_{n}\to x.

(5)

(The convergence on the right is norm convergence:
\left\lVert x_{n}-x\right\rVert\to 0
.)
A space with this property is called a
Riesz–Radon
space.
Thus, all Hilbert spaces are Riesz–Radon spaces.
We note that the converse to (
5
) holds in
any
Hilbert or Banach space.

It turns out that uniformly convex spaces are also Riesz–Radon.
To show this, we record a simple technical lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Let
(X,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
be a uniformly convex space and
x_{n},y_{n}\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
such that

\left\lVert\tfrac{x_{n}+y_{n}}{2}\right\rVert\to 1
. Then
\left\lVert x_{n}-y_{n}\right\rVert\to 0
.

Proof.

Supposing
\left\lVert x_{n}-y_{n}\right\rVert\not\to 0
, and passing to a subsequence if necessary,
there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\left\lVert x_{n}-y_{n}\right\rVert\geqslant\varepsilon
for all
n\geqslant 1
.
Since the space is uniformly convex, there is a
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

1=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert\frac{x_{n}+y_{n}}{2}\right\rVert\leqslant\delta<1,

which is patent nonsense.
∎

Corollary 21
.

If
x_{n}\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
, in a uniformly convex space
X
, are such that
\left\lVert\tfrac{x_{n}+x_{m}}{2}\right\rVert\to 1
as
n,m\to\infty
,
then
(x_{n})_{n}
is Cauchy in
X
.

Theorem 22
.

Every uniformly convex Banach space is a Riesz–Radon space.

Proof.

We may assume that
x\neq 0
alongside the hypotheses that

x_{n}\rightharpoonup x\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert\to\left\lVert x\right\rVert.

Thus (eventually)
x_{n}\neq 0
and we may define

y_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}}{\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert}\quad\text{and}\quad y:=\frac{x}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}.

Clearly
y,y_{n}\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
and also satisfy

y_{n}\rightharpoonup y\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert\to\left\lVert y\right\rVert.

By the Hahn–Banach Theorem, there is a functional
f\in X^{*}
such that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle{f,y}\right\rangle=1.

Since
y_{n}\rightharpoonup y
, it follows that

1=\left\langle{f,y}\right\rangle=\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\left|\left\langle{f,\tfrac{y_{n}+y_{m}}{2}}\right\rangle\right|\leqslant\left\lVert\frac{y_{n}+y_{m}}{2}\right\rVert\leqslant 1.

Thus, by the previous lemma,
(y_{n})
is Cauchy, so
y_{n}\to z
for some
z\in X
.
But then
y_{n}\rightharpoonup z
.
Again by the Hahn–Banach Theorem, weak limits are unique,
so
y=z
, i.e.,
y_{n}\to y
in the norm topology of
X
.
It is now easy to conclude that
x_{n}\to x
.
∎

We immediately gain a nice property from Hilbert spaces in all
L^{p}
spaces, even if
p\neq 2
.

Corollary 23
.

Every
L^{p}
space (
1<p<\infty
) is a Riesz–Radon space.

Reflexivity.

It’s easy to move “up” the scale of duality: from
X
to its dual
X^{*}
, to
its
dual,
X^{**}
,
and so on.
What is not so clear is how to climb back down.
In other words, given a description of
X^{*}
, can we deduce what
X
must have looked like?

Recall that in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, every functional in the dual is just an evaluation map,
i.e.,
T(v)=\left\langle{u,v}\right\rangle
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In general, without an inner product, elements of
X
cannot be made to act on
X
itself,
but they
do
act on
X^{*}
:
for given
x\in X
, define the
evaluation

\hat{x}\colon X^{*}\to\mathbb{K}
via

\hat{x}(f)\equiv\left\langle{\hat{x},f}\right\rangle:=\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle\equiv f(x).

It is easy to see that such evaluation functionals are bounded, and so we see that

\hat{X}:=\left\{\hat{x}:x\in X\right\}\subset X^{**}.

In fact, the mapping
\hat{\cdot}\colon X\to X^{**}
is linear, injective and preserves the norm:

\left\lVert\hat{x}\right\rVert_{X^{**}}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert.

This last point follows from the Hahn–Banach theorem:
given any
x\in X
there is an
f\in X^{*}
with
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}}=1
and
\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
.

Now, the million-dollar question is: when does
\hat{X}=X^{**}
, or, when is
x\mapsto\hat{x}
surjective?

Definition 24
.

A Banach space
X
is
reflexive
if
\hat{X}=X^{**}
.

Clearly all finite-dimensional spaces as well as Hilbert spaces are reflexive,
as are
L^{p}
for
1<p<\infty
, since
(L^{p})^{*}=L^{q}
when
\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{q}=1
,
so exchanging
p
and
q
yields the desired isomorphism.
(This is a little too quick, how can you make it rigorous?)
However, neither
L^{1}
nor
L^{\infty}
is reflexive.

Since
\hat{X}
is “the same as” (isomorphic to)
X
,
in reflexive spaces we can recover
X
from
X^{**}
, the dual of
X^{*}
—hurrah!

“Is that it? Just one dual space and then back where we started? Not very exciting…”
(Some people you just cannot please…)
You have a point. Vector spaces are basically just algebra.
To make things interesting (read: “do analysis”) we need some topology.

Recall (from above) that a sequence
f_{n}\in X^{*}
converges
weakly
to
f\in X^{*}
if

\left\langle{\Phi,f_{n}}\right\rangle\to\left\langle{\Phi,f}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad\Phi\in X^{**}.

(6)

If
X
is reflexive, then this becomes

\left\langle{\hat{x},f_{n}}\right\rangle\to\left\langle{\hat{x},f}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in X,

or, in other words,

\left\langle{f_{n},x}\right\rangle\to\left\langle{f,x}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in X.

(7)

In general,
\hat{X}
will be smaller than
X^{**}
, so (
7
)
will be weaker than (
6
)

Definition 25
.

The convergence in (
7
) is called
weak* convergence
,
and its underlying topology, the
weak* topology
.
(FYI: it’s
star
,
weak star
—not “weak asterisk”!)

The weak* topology is important because of the following classical theorem.

Theorem 26
(Banach–Alaoglou).

The unit ball in
X^{*}
is weak*-compact.

The next result ties all of this together.

Theorem 27
.

A Banach space
X
is reflexive if, and only if, the weak and weak* topologies on
X^{*}
coincide.
Hence, if
X
is reflexive, then
\operatorname{ball}{X^{*}}
is weakly compact.
Furthermore, if
X
has a countable dense subset, then the weak topology on
\operatorname{ball}{X}
is metrizable,
and so bounded sets in
X^{*}
are sequentially compact.

Example 28
.

(Weak compactness in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
).
As noted earlier,
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
spaces are reflexive and themselves dual spaces.
Hence, the ball in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e., the ball in
(L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{*}
if
1/p+1/q=1
,
is weakly compact.
Hence, bounded sets in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are sequentially compact.
As mentioned above, this is a powerful tool in the analysis of PDE and the Calculus of Variations.

A fuller discussion of reflexivity and weak topologies, as well as proofs of the above theorems
may be found in Chapter V of
[
2
]
.

Returning to convexity, it is a remarkable fact that uniformly convex spaces are automatically reflexive.
(Or perhaps you are beginning to wonder what, if anything, is
not
true in uniformly convex spaces?)
This result is known as the
Milmann–Pettis Theorem
,
although the slick and short proof we offer here is due to Ringrose
[
4
]
.

Theorem 29
(Milmann–Pettis).

Any uniformly convex Banach space is reflexive.

In the proof below we use the following, equivalent characterization of uniform convexity:
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,2]
there is a
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
whenever
x,y\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
with
\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert>2-\delta
, we have
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert<\varepsilon
.
(That this is equivalent to Definition 4 is a straightforward exercise in notation juggling.)

Proof.

Suppose
X
is uniformly convex but not reflexive, so
X^{**}\setminus\hat{X}
is non-empty.

Recall (or take on faith) that the image of
\operatorname{ball}{X}
under
\hat{\cdot}

U:=\{\hat{x}\in X^{**}:x\in\operatorname{ball}{X}\}

(8)

is dense in
\operatorname{ball}{X^{**}}

with respect to the weak* topology
on

X^{**}

(see Proposition V.4.1 and the surrounding discussion in
[
2
]
).
It follows that
B:=\operatorname{ball}{X^{**}}
is precisely the weak*-closure of
U
.

We first note that
U
is a norm-closed subspace of
B
.
Indeed, suppose
(\hat{x}_{n})_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
U
.
Then,
(x_{n})_{n}
is Cauchy in
\operatorname{ball}{X}
since

\left\lVert\hat{x}_{n}-\hat{x}_{m}\right\rVert_{X^{**}}=\left\lVert x_{n}-x_{m}\right\rVert.

Hence,
x_{n}\to x
for some
x\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
, and so, clearly,
\hat{x}_{n}\to\hat{x}\in U
.

Now, if
X
is
not
reflexive, then
U
is a
proper
closed subset of
B
,
whence we can find a
\Phi\in B\setminus U
with unit norm such that

\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{2}d(\Phi,U)>0.

(9)

Since
\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{X^{**}}=1
, there is a functional
f\in X^{*}
with
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}}=1

such that

\left|\Phi(f)-1\right|<\delta/2,

(10)

where
\delta>0
is chosen in accordance with the uniform convexity of
X
for our choice of
\varepsilon
.
(This is just a consequence of the definition of the norm on
X^{**}
.)

Now consider the open set

V:=\{\Psi\in X^{**}:\left|\Psi(f)-1\right|<\delta/2\}.

If
\Psi,\Psi_{1}\in V\cap U
, then
\left|\Psi(f)+\Psi_{1}(f)\right|>2-\delta
.
Since
\Psi,\Psi_{1}\in U
, they are of the form
\hat{x},\hat{x}_{1}
for some
x,x_{1}\in\operatorname{ball}{X}
.
Thus,

\left\lVert x+x_{1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\Psi+\Psi_{1}\right\rVert_{X^{**}}\geqslant 2-\delta,

and so, by uniform convexity of
X
,

\left\lVert\Psi-\Psi_{1}\right\rVert_{X^{**}}=\left\lVert x-x_{1}\right\rVert<\varepsilon.

In other words, for a fixed
\Psi\in V\cap U
, we have

V\cap U\subset\Psi+\varepsilon U,

and the latter set is closed, and hence weak*-closed.

We now show that
\Phi\in\Psi+\varepsilon U
.
To do so, recall that
U
is weak*-dense in
B
, so we can find a sequence
\hat{x}_{n}\in U

such that
\hat{x}_{n}\to\Phi
in the weak* sense.
But
\Phi\in V
by (
10
), and
V
is a weak*-open set.
Hence, eventually,
\hat{x}_{n}\in V
. This shows that
\Phi
lies in the weak*-closure of
V\cap U
,
and therefore in the weak*-closed set
\Psi+\varepsilon U
.
But this means that
\left\lVert\Phi-\Psi\right\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon
for some
\Psi\in U
, contradicting (
9
).
∎

Hence, we have another proof of the reflexivity of
L^{p}
spaces (
1<p<\infty
).

As a nice cap to our discussion, we deduce the following result from the reflexivity of
L^{p}
,
which shows how another basic geometric fact about Hilbert spaces still holds in reflexive spaces.

Corollary 30
.

Let
Y
be a closed subspace of
L^{p}
(
1<p<\infty
).
Then, for any
f\notin Y
there is a unique closest point
g\in Y
, to
f
i.e.,

\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=\inf_{h\in Y}\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

Proof.

Since
L^{p}
is reflexive, a point
g
as above exists (see Proposition V.4.7 in
[
2
]
).
Suppose
h\in Y
were another closest point, i.e.,

\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert=\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert=d(f,Y).

Since
Y
is a subspace,
\tfrac{1}{2}(g+h)\in Y
and so

\left\lVert f-\frac{g+h}{2}\right\rVert=\frac{\left\lVert(f-g)+(f-h)\right\rVert}{2}<d(f,Y)

by the strict convexity of the norm on
L^{p}
,
contradicting the fact that
g\in Y
is the closest point to
f
.
∎
