A Simple Proof of the Mixing of Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm under Smoothness and Isoperimetry
Yuansi Chen, Khashayar Gatmiry

Abstract
We study the mixing time of Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) for sampling a target density on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . We assume that the target density satisfies ψ μ \psi_{\mu} -isoperimetry and that the operator norm and trace of its Hessian are bounded by L L and Υ \Upsilon respectively. Our main result establishes that, from a warm start, to achieve ϵ \epsilon -total variation distance to the target density, MALA mixes in O ⁡ ( ( L ​ Υ ) 1 2 ψ μ 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 ϵ ) ) O\left(\frac{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\psi_{\mu}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right) iterations. Notably, this result holds beyond the log-concave sampling setting and the mixing time depends on only Υ \Upsilon rather than its upper bound L ​ d Ld . In the m m -strongly logconcave and L L -log-smooth sampling setting, our bound recovers the previous minimax mixing bound of MALA [ WSC22 ] .



1
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to clarity what current proof techniques can show about the mixing time of Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) for sampling a smooth target density on
d
that satisfies an isoperimetry. Although the mixing time of MALA is well studied in the case of log-concave distributions, there is a question regarding how the mixing properties change when a slightly weaker assumption, such as isoperimetry, is made. In the study of Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA), Vempala and Wibisono
[
VW19
]
questioned the impact of the isoperimetry assumption on mixing. We would like to ask the same question for MALA. In particular, we believe that existing proof techniques such as truncated conductance and multivariate integration by parts that appeared in
[
WSC22
]
are sufficient to answer this question.

Freund et al.
[
FMZ22
]
investigated when the mixing time of Langevin algorithms becomes independent of the dimension. They show that the mixing time of such algorithms can be made to depend on the trace of the Hessian of the target density, rather than the dimension itself. Consequently, when combined with composite sampling, this leads to an algorithm with dimension-independent mixing time. We aim to explore whether the mixing time of MALA can similarly be shown to depend solely on the trace of the Hessian, instead of the
d^{\frac{1}{2}}
dependency in
[
WSC22
]
.

Based on the two motivations above, we investigate the mixing time of MALA to sample a target density
\mu
which satisfies the Cheeger’s isoperimetric inequality with coefficient
\psi_{\mu}
and has the operator norm and the trace of its Hessian bounded by
L
and
\Upsilon
respectively. We present a simple proof that shows from a warm start, MALA mixes in
O\left(\frac{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\psi_{\mu}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right)
iterations to achieve
\epsilon
-total variation distance to the target density. Compared to the existing work
[
WSC22
]
, our result demonstrates that the existing proof techniques presented there such as truncated conductance and multivariate integration by parts are sufficient to deal with sampling on a smooth target density with isoperimetry. When applied to sampling from an
m
-strongly logconcave and
L
-log-smooth density, our mixing time upper bound recovers the previous minimax mixing bound of MALA
[
WSC22
]
. Additionally, the dependency on
\Upsilon
in our mixing time bound allows us to get weaker dimension dependency in several scenarios.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the MALA algorithm and provide necessary background knowledge to establish mixing time of Markov chains.

2.1
Markov chain basics

We consider the problem of sampling from a target measure
\mu
with density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
d
. Given a
Markov chain with transition kernel

P:\real^{d}\times\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})\to\real_{\geq 0}
where
\mathcal{B}(\real^{d})
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
d
, the
k
-step transition kernel
P^{k}
is defined recursively by
P^{k}(x,dy):=\int_{z\in\real^{n}}P^{k-1}(x,dz)P(z,dy)
.
The Markov chain is called
reversible
with respect to the target measure
\mu
if

\displaystyle\mu(dx)P(x,dy)=\mu(dy)P(y,dx).

One can associate the Markov chain with a transition operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}(\nu)(S):=\int_{y\in\real^{n}}d\nu(y)P(y,S),\quad\forall S\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n}).

In words, when
\nu
is the distribution of the current state,
\mathcal{T}_{P}(\nu)
gives the distribution of the next state. And
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{n}(\nu):=\mathcal{T}_{P^{n}}(\nu)
is the distribution of the state after
n
steps.

s
-conductance.

For
s\in(0,1)
, we define the
s
-conductance
\Phi_{s}
of the Markov chain
P
with its stationary measure
\mu
as follows

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(P):=\inf_{S:s<\mu(S)<1-s}\frac{\int_{S}P(x,S^{c})\mu(dx)}{\min\left\{\mu(S),\mu(S^{c})\right\}-s}.

(1)

When compared to conductance (the case
s=0
),
s
-conductance allows us to ignore small parts of the distribution where the conductance is difficult to bound.

Lazy chain.

Given a Markov chain with transition kernel
P
. We define its lazy variant
P^{\text{lazy}}
, which stays in the same state with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, as

\displaystyle P^{\text{lazy}}(x,S):=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x\to S}+\frac{1}{2}P(x,S).

Here
\delta_{x\to\cdot}
is the Dirac measure at
x
.
Since the lazy variant only slows down the convergence rate by a constant factor, we study lazy Markov chains in this paper to simplify our
theoretical analysis.

Total-variance distance.

Let the total variation (TV) distance between two probability distributions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
be

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})}\left|\mathcal{P}_{1}(A)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(A)\right|,

where
\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})
is the Borel sigma-algebra on
n
. If
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
admit densities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
respectively, we may write
{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|p_{1}(x)-p_{2}(x)\right|dx
.

Warm start.

We say an initial measure
\mu_{0}
is
M
-warm
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})}\frac{\mu_{0}(S)}{\mu(S)}\leq M.

Mixing time.

For an error tolerance
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the total variance distance
\epsilon
-mixing time of the Markov chain
P
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and target distribution
\mu
is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{mix}}^{P}(\epsilon,\mu_{0},\mu):=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}^{n}_{P}(\mu_{0}),\mu\right)\leq\epsilon\right\}.

2.2
MALA basics

Given a state
x_{k}
at the
k
-th iteration, MALA proposes a new state
y_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{k}-h\nabla f(x_{k}),2h\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Then to obtain the next state, it decides to accept or reject
y_{k+1}
using a Metropolis-Hastings correction. We use
\mathcal{P}_{x}=\mathcal{N}(x-h\nabla f(x),2h\mathbb{I}_{d})
to denote the proposal kernel of MALA at
x
.

One step of MALA can be seen as one step of Metropolized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) with a single leapfrog step
[
Nea11
]
. Using the notation from HMC, MALA works as follows: At each iteration, given the step-size
\eta=(2h)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and the current state
q_{0}\in\real^{d}
, draw an independent Gaussian
p_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, the proposal for the next state is
q_{\eta}
defined as

\displaystyle q_{\eta}

\displaystyle=q_{0}+\eta p_{0}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\nabla f(q_{0})

\displaystyle p_{\eta}

\displaystyle=p_{0}-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{0})-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{\eta}).

(2)

A Metropolis-Hastings correction is used to ensure the correct stationary measure, with acceptance probability

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(q_{0},p_{0})

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{1,\frac{\exp\left(-f(q_{\eta})-\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{\eta}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(-f(q_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}\right\}.

(3)

See
[
Nea11
,
WSC22
]
for more details about the connection between MALA and HMC. In the following, we adopt the Metropolized HMC with a single leapfrog step notation with
\eta=(2h)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

2.3
Assumptions on the target density

Here are the regularity assumptions that one could impose on the target density.

•

A twice differentiable function
f:\real^{d}\to\real
is
L
-smooth
if
\nabla^{2}f_{x}\preceq L\mathbb{I}_{d},\forall x\in\real^{d}
. A target density
\mu\propto e^{-f}
is called
L
-log-smooth
is
f
is
L
-smooth.

•

A target density
\mu\sim e^{-f}
satisfies the
Cheeger’s isoperimetric inequality
with coefficient
\psi_{\mu}
if for any partition
(S_{1},S_{2},S_{3})
of
d
, the following inequality is satisfied

\displaystyle\mu(S_{3})\geq\psi_{\mu}\cdot\mathfrak{D}(S_{1},S_{2})\cdot\mu(S_{1})\mu(S_{2}).

where the distance between sets
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
is defined as
\mathfrak{D}(S_{1},S_{2})=\inf_{x\in S_{1},y\in S_{2}}\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}
.

•

A target density
\mu\propto e^{-f}
is
subexponential-tailed
if there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\to\infty}e^{-\lambda\left\|x\right\|_{2}}e^{-f(x)}\to 0.

Note that if
\mu
satisfies the Cheeger isoperimetric inequality, then it is also subexponential-tailed. More precisely, the Poincaré constant of
\mu
follows from Cheeger’s isoperimetric coefficient by Cheeger’s inequality
[
Che15
,
Maz60
]
(see also Theorem 1.1 in
[
Mil09
]
), and the Poincaré constant of
\mu
implies subexponential tail bounds following Gromov and Milman
[
GM83
]
(see also Theorem 1.7 of
[
GRS15
]
).

3
Main results

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mu\propto e^{-f}
be a
L
-log-smooth target density on
d
, with a Cheeger’s isoperimetric coefficient
\psi_{\mu}
. Let
\Upsilon=\sup_{x\in\real^{d}}\trace(\nabla^{2}f_{x})
. Then there exists universal constants
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
such that for any error tolerance
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, from any
M
-warm initial measure
\mu_{0}
, the
\epsilon
-mixing time of lazy MALA with step-size

\displaystyle h=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}=\frac{c_{0}}{\max\left\{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}},L\log\left(\frac{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\psi_{\mu}}\frac{M}{\epsilon}\right)\right\}}

satisfies

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{mix}}(\epsilon,\mu_{0},\mu)\leq c_{1}\frac{\max\left\{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}},L\log\left(\frac{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\psi_{\mu}}\frac{M}{\epsilon}\right)\right\}}{\psi_{\mu}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{M}{\epsilon}\right).

The theorem suggests that taking a step-size of order
\frac{1}{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
results in a mixing time of order
\frac{(L\Upsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\psi_{\mu}^{2}}
, after ignoring the logarithmic factors.
Note that under the
L
-log-smooth assumption, the supremum over all traces of Hessian
\Upsilon
is always upper bounded by
Ld
. In particular, in the setting of sampling
m
-strongly logconcave and
L
-log-smooth density in
[
WSC22
]
, our mixing time upper bound is
d^{\frac{1}{2}}\kappa
where
\kappa=\frac{L}{m}
because the isoperimetric coefficient for
m
-strongly logconcave density is
m^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Our mixing time upper bound recovers the previous minimax mixing bound of MALA
[
WSC22
]
up to constant factors. Furthermore, here is a log-concave sampling example where our bound is better: let the Hessian satisfy

\displaystyle\diag\left(\frac{L}{d},\frac{L}{2d},\ldots,\frac{L}{2d}\right)\preceq\nabla^{2}f_{x}\preceq\diag\left(L,\frac{L}{d},\ldots,\frac{L}{d}\right).

A naive application of
[
WSC22
]
gives a dimension dependency of
d^{\frac{3}{2}}
for the mixing time because
\kappa
is of order
d
and it enforces a smaller step size. However, the bound in Theorem
1
gives a mixing time of order
d
because
\Upsilon
is only of order
2L
.

Remark that in the log-concave sampling setting, a warm start stated in Theorem
1
can be found algorithmically via running underdamped Langevin algorithms for several steps
[
AC23
]
.

Lemma 1

(Lovasz and Simonovits

Consider a reversible lazy Markov chain with kernel

\mu

be an
M

0<s<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{P}^{n}(\mu_{0}),\mu\right)\leq Ms+M\left(1-\frac{\Phi_{s}^{2}}{2}\right)^{n}.

Using

[
WSC22
]

[
Lov99
]
(see also Lemma 2 in

]

{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x},\mathcal{T}_{y}\right)\leq 1-\rho

in a high probability region, we have
\Phi_{s}\geq\frac{\rho\Delta}{\psi_{\mu}}
. Therefore, it remains to bound the transition overlap, namely the TV-distance between
\mathcal{T}_{x}
and
\mathcal{T}_{y}
for two close points

We bound the TV-distance via the following triangle inequality,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x},\mathcal{T}_{y}\right)\leq{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x},\mathcal{P}_{x}\right)+{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{x},\mathcal{P}_{y}\right)+{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{y},\mathcal{T}_{y}\right).

\mathcal{P}_{x}
and
\mathcal{P}_{y}
for two close points
