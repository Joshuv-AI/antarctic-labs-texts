SICs and the Triangle Group ( 𝟑 , 𝟑 , 𝟑 ) \boldsymbol{(3,3,3)}
Danylo YAKYMENKO

Abstract
The problem of existence of symmetric informationally-complete positive operator-valued measures (SICs for short) in every dimension is known as Zauner’s conjecture and remains open to this day. Most of the known SIC examples are constructed as an orbit of the Weyl–Heisenberg group action. It appears that in these cases SICs are invariant under the so-called canonical order-three unitaries, which define automorphisms of the Weyl–Heisenberg group. In this note, we show that those order-three unitaries appear in projective unitary representations of the triangle group ( 3 , 3 , 3 ) (3,3,3) . We give a full description of such representations and show how it can be used to obtain results about the structure of canonical order-three unitaries. In particular, we present an alternative way of proving the fact that any canonical order-three unitary is conjugate to Zauner’s unitary if the dimension d > 3 d>3 is prime.

Introduction

Let
H=\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a complex Hilbert space of finite dimension
d
.
A
positive operator-valued measure
(
POVM for short
)
is
a measure whose values are positive semi-definite Hermitian matrices

M_{i}
on
H
. That is,

M_{i}^{\dagger}=M_{i}\geqslant 0
and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}=I

if the set
\{M_{i}\}
has a finite size
n
.

A POVM is a model of a general measurement that one can perform on a quantum system
with
d
degrees of freedom. Matrices
M_{i}
correspond to distinguishable outcomes
labelled by the indices
i
. If
\rho
is a density matrix on
H
,
which encodes a state of the quantum system, then the outcome labelled
i

appears with probability

{\rm Prob}(i)=\tr(\rho M_{i})

in a measurement event.
The von Neumann (projective or PVM) measurements are a special case
of POVMs, where each
M_{i}
is the projection onto the
i
-th subspace in
an orthogonal decomposition
H=\oplus_{i}H_{i}
.

A
symmetric informationally-complete POVM
(
SIC-POVM or just SIC
)

is a POVM
\{M_{i}\}
of size
n=d^{2}
, where each matrix
M_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\left|{v_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{v_{i}}\right|

has rank one and for each
i\neq j
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(M_{i}M_{j})={\rm const}=\frac{1}{d+1}.

It corresponds to a set of
d^{2}
pure states
\left|{v_{i}}\right\rangle\in H
such that

\displaystyle|\left\langle{v_{i}}\middle|{v_{j}}\right\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{d+1},\quad i\neq j,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\left|{v_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{v_{i}}\right|=dI.

By knowing the probabilities
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho M_{i})=\langle\rho,M_{i}\rangle_{\rm HS}
of the outcomes
\{i\}

(here
{\rm HS}
stands for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product),
we can completely reconstruct the quantum state
\rho
, which justifies the naming.
Note that it is not possible with a POVM of a lesser size
n<d^{2}
.
In a real scenario of measuring copies of
\rho
multiple times, we get
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho M_{i})

as an estimate based on the frequency of outcome
i
.
This gives us an estimate
\hat{\rho}
of
\rho
.
One can show that the expectation of
{||\rho-\hat{\rho}||^{2}_{\rm HS}}
,
averaged over all
\rho
which are unitary equivalent to a fixed one,
is minimized exactly for SICs amongst other minimal informationally-complete POVMs
[
18
]
.
This makes SICs the best choice for
a “standard” unbiased measurement in quantum mechanics.

The problem is that we do not know how to construct them in general.
Initially, SICs’ existence in every dimension
d
was conjectured
by Zauner in
[
21
]
(and independently by Renes et al. in
[
16
]
).
Since then a vast body of research was carried out
(e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
17
,
19
,
22
]
).
Either exact or numerical solutions have been
found in all dimensions
d\leq 193
and some others. The current state-of-the-art
was achieved in
[
4
,
5
]
, where authors proposed a recipe for finding SICs
in dimensions of the form
d=n^{2}+3
and verified it in a number of dimensions,
including
{d=39604}
. However, it remains unproven in general,
and it is also related to unsolved problems in number theory.

Most known SICs are constructed as an orbit of the action
of the
Weyl–Heisenberg group

{\rm WH}(d)
.
It is a group generated by the
clock and shift matrices
defined by

Z\left|{k}\right\rangle=\omega^{k}\left|{k}\right\rangle,\qquad X\left|{k}\right\rangle=\left|{k+1}\right\rangle,

respectively, where
\omega=\exp\bigl(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{d}\bigr)

and
\{\left|{k}\right\rangle\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\}
is the standard basis in
H
.

For a unit vector
\left|{f}\right\rangle\in H
, the set
\bigl\{X^{k}Z^{l}\left|{f}\right\rangle\mid k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\bigr\}
is called a

WH SIC
if it is a SIC, and
\left|{f}\right\rangle
is called a
fiducial vector
in such a case.

In any dimension
d
, there exists an order-three unitary
\mathfrak{Z}
, called
Zauner’s unitary
,
which gives an automorphism of
{\rm WH}(d)
under the conjugation (see Section
2
for details).
The strong version of Zauner’s conjecture states that in every dimension
d
there exists
a fiducial vector
\left|{f}\right\rangle
which is an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{Z}
.
This means that the corresponding WH SIC is invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{Z}
.
Moreover, the collected evidence suggests that any WH SIC is invariant under a

canonical order-three unitary
, which is related to
\mathfrak{Z}
anyway.
This makes it essential for SICs’ constructions, yet we do not have an explanation for this either.
Canonical order-three unitaries are also important in other problems, see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.

In this paper, we show that
\mathfrak{Z}
along with the group
{\rm WH}(d)

appear in projective unitary representations of the ordinary triangle group
\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
,
which can be abstractly defined by generators and relations

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}=\big\langle a,b,c\mid a^{3}=b^{3}=c^{3}=1,\,abc=1\big\rangle.

Such representations were first obtained in
[
14
]
,
but here we give their simplified description with a modified proof in Section
3
.
We then show how to use it to investigate properties of canonical order-three unitaries
in Section
4
. In particular, we give an alternative way of proving the fact that any
canonical order-three unitary is conjugate to Zauner’s unitary if the dimension
d>3
is prime.
We hope that the symmetric nature of

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
could provide insights into the importance of
\mathfrak{Z}
for SIC constructions.

2
Weyl–Heisenberg group and its symmetries

In this section, we mainly follow Appleby
[
2
]
.
Recall that the Weyl–Heisenberg group is generated by
the clock and shift matrices
Z
,
X
:

Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\omega&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\omega^{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\omega^{d-1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\omega=\exp\bigl(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{d}\bigr)
.
They satisfy
Z^{d}=X^{d}=I
and a kind of commutation relation
ZX=\omega XZ
.
It is easy to see that each element of the generated group has the
form
\omega^{m}X^{k}Z^{l}
, where
k,l,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
For further constructions, it is convenient to also add the factor
\tau=-\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{d}\bigr)
to it.
We thus define the group by
{\rm WH}(d)=\langle X,Z,\tau I\rangle
.
Note that
\tau^{2}=\omega
and if
d
is odd then
\tau=\omega^{\frac{d+1}{2}}

so it adds nothing, but doubles the group in even dimensions.
In any case, for the quotient over the group center, denoted by
\langle\xi I\rangle
, we have
{\rm WH}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

The
d^{2}

displacement matrices
for
{\bf a}=(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
defined by

D_{\bf a}=\tau^{a_{1}a_{2}}X^{a_{1}}Z^{a_{2}}\in{\rm WH}(d)

are cosets representatives of the quotient group
{{\rm WH}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle}
.
They satisfy the relations
D_{\bf a}D_{\bf b}=\tau^{\langle\bf a,b\rangle}D_{\bf a+b}
,
where
\langle{\bf a,b}\rangle=a_{2}b_{1}-b_{2}a_{1}
is the symplectic form.
In particular,
D_{\bf a}^{\dagger}=D_{-\bf a}
.

The Clifford group
{\rm Cli}(d)
is defined as the group of unitary automorphisms
of the Weyl–Heisenberg group. That is,

{\rm Cli}(d)=\bigl\{A\in{\rm Uni}(d)\mid A^{\dagger}{\rm WH}(d)A={\rm WH}(d)\bigr\}.

Its importance comes from the fact that if
\left|{f}\right\rangle
is a WH SIC fiducial vector,
then so is
A\left|{f}\right\rangle
(for a different SIC, in general).
Thus WH SIC solutions are naturally grouped in orbits of the Clifford group action.

The
{\rm Cli}(d)
group has the following structure.
Let
\bar{d}=d
if
d
is odd and
\bar{d}=2d
otherwise.
By
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
, we denote the group of
2\times 2
symplectic matrices

M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&m_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
with
entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}}
and condition
{\rm det}(M)=1
.
For a symplectic matrix
M
,
its
symplectic unitary
is defined (up to a scalar factor) by

A_{M}=\sum_{r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\tau^{m_{2}^{-1}(m_{1}s^{2}-2rs+m_{4}r^{2})}\left|{r}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{s}\right|

if
m_{2}^{-1}
(a multiplicative inverse) exists in
\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}}
. Otherwise,
one has to decompose
M
into a product of matrices in which
m_{2}^{-1}
exists
and then take the product of its symplectic unitaries.

The group
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
acts naturally on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
as
on vectors of size
2
, therefore we can define
the semidirect product
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
with respect to this action.
It can be checked that
\forall M\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
,
\forall{\bf a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}

A_{M}D_{\bf a}A_{M}^{\dagger}=D_{M{\bf a}}.

This implies
A_{M}\in{\rm Cli}(d)
.
Define
A_{[M,{\bf b}]}=D_{{\bf b}}A_{M}
,
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
.
It was proved
[
2
, Theorem 1]
that the map

[M,{\bf b}]\longrightarrow A_{[M,{\bf b}]}

is a surjective homomorphism from

{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
to
{\rm Cli}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle
,
it is an isomorphism in odd dimensions and has a kernel of size
8
in even dimensions.

Note that
A_{[M,{\bf 0}]}=A_{M}
just permutes the set of displacement matrices,
while
A_{[M,{\bf a}]}
with
{\bf a}\neq{\bf 0}
also adds phases to them.
For
A\in{\rm Cli}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle
, by

M_{A}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
we denote
a symplectic matrix such that
A=A_{[M_{A},{\bf a}]}

for some
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
(
M_{A}
is not unique in even dimensions,
but this will be irrelevant to us).

A prominent example of an automorphism of
{\rm WH}(d)
is the discrete Fourier transform matrix

F=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{rs}\left|{r}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{s}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&\omega&\omega^{2}&\cdots&\omega^{d-1}\\
1&\omega^{2}&\omega^{4}&\cdots&\omega^{2(d-1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\omega^{d-1}&\omega^{2(d-1)}&\cdots&\omega^{(d-1)(d-1)}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding symplectic matrix of
F
is

M_{F}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
F=A_{M_{F}}
.
It exchanges the clock and shift matrices,

FXF^{\dagger}=Z
,
FZF^{\dagger}=X^{-1}
, and has order four for
d>2
.

On the other hand, Zauner’s unitary
\mathfrak{Z}
has the symplectic matrix

M_{\mathfrak{Z}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\mathfrak{Z}=A_{M_{\mathfrak{Z}}}
,
and up to a scalar equals to

\mathfrak{Z}=\frac{{\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi(d-1)}{12}}}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{r,s=0}^{d-1}\tau^{r^{2}+2rs}\left|{r}\right\rangle\left\langle{s}\right|={\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi(d-1)}{12}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\tau&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\tau^{4}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\tau^{(d-1)^{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}F,

where the multiplier
{\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi(d-1)}{12}}
ensures that
\mathfrak{Z}^{3}=I
.
The matrix
\mathfrak{Z}
permutes
{\rm WH}(d)
by the rules

\mathfrak{Z}X\mathfrak{Z}^{\dagger}=Z,\qquad\mathfrak{Z}Z\mathfrak{Z}^{\dagger}=\tau X^{-1}Z^{-1},\qquad\mathfrak{Z}D_{(a_{1},a_{2})}\mathfrak{Z}^{\dagger}=D_{(-a_{2},a_{1}-a_{2})}.

For a unitary
A\in{\rm Cli}(d)
, its
Clifford trace
is defined by

\tr_{{\rm c}}(A)=\tr(M_{A})\bmod d
.
This is well-defined because it does not depend on the choice of
M_{A}
.
Clearly, Zauner’s unitary and its conjugates in the Clifford group have Clifford trace
-1
.
In general, if
\tr_{{\rm c}}(A)=-1
then
A
is of order three
(i.e., we can pick a phase such that
A^{3}=I

and we always assume so in what follows),
unless
A=I
in dimension three. Such unitaries are called
canonical order-three unitaries
.

While Zauner conjectured that a WH SIC fiducial vector
\left|{f}\right\rangle
can be
found as an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{Z}
, the collected evidence suggests
that in any case
\left|{f}\right\rangle
is an eigenvector of a canonical order-three unitary.
This observation became known as the Appleby–Zauner conjecture.
It was proved in
[
10
]
that any canonical order-three unitary is conjugate
to Zauner’s if the dimension
d>3
is prime. It is not true in general.
Theorem 9.1 in
[
8
]

gives the characterization of their conjugacy classes. They are given by the sets of
representatives

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{Z}\},\quad d\neq 0\mathrm{~mod~}3,\qquad\bigl\{\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z}^{2}\bigr\},\quad d=0\mathrm{~mod~}9\quad\text{or}\quad d=3,

\displaystyle\bigl\{\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z}^{2},A_{M_{1}}\bigr\},\qquad d=3\mathrm{~mod~}9\quad\text{and}\quad d\neq 3,

\displaystyle\bigl\{\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z}^{2},A_{M_{2}}\bigr\},\qquad d=6\mathrm{~mod~}9,

where

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
\frac{4d-3}{3}&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
\frac{2d-3}{3}&-2\end{pmatrix}\mod\bar{d}.

Note that the conjugacy class
\{M_{1}\}
corresponds to
F_{a}
in
[
17
,
19
]
, where
WH SIC fiducial vectors, which are eigenvectors of
A_{M_{1}}
, found for all small
d=3(3k+1)
.
For some reason, no WH SIC fiducials are found as eigenvectors of
A_{M_{2}}
when
d=3(3k+2)
.

In addition to Clifford unitaries, we can consider anti-unitary operators that also
define
{\rm WH}(d)
automorphisms. Let
\hat{J}
be the anti-unitary operator that
acts on
H
by the rule

\hat{J}\bigg(\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}c_{r}\left|{r}\right\rangle\bigg)=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\bar{c}_{r}\left|{r}\right\rangle.

Its conjugate action on
{\rm WH}(d)
is described by

\hat{J}D_{(a_{1},a_{2})}\hat{J}^{\dagger}=D_{(a_{1},-a_{2})},\qquad\forall(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}.

In fact, the disjoint union

{\rm ECli}(d)={\rm Cli}(d)\cup\bigl\{\hat{J}A\mid A\in{\rm Cli}(d)\bigr\}

forms the
extended Clifford group
.
In this group a product of two anti-unitaries is a unitary operator,
while anti-unitary times unitary remains anti-unitary.
If we denote by
{\rm ESL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
the group of symplectic matrices
with
{\rm det}(M)=\pm 1
, then by
[
2
, Theorem 2]

the extended map
{[M,{\bf a}]\longrightarrow D_{\bf a}A_{M}}
is a surjective
homomorphism from

{\rm ESL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}

to
{\rm ECli}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle
,
under which
\hat{J}
corresponds to
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\hphantom{-}0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and anti-unitaries in general to
M
with
{\rm det}(M)=-1
.

3
Triangle group and its representations

The
ordinary triangle group

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}

(such groups are also known as von Dyck groups)
can be defined abstractly by the generators and relations

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}=\big\langle a,b,c\mid a^{3}=b^{3}=c^{3}=1,\,abc=1\big\rangle.

It has a nice realization as motions of the real plane
[
15
]
.
Elements
a
,
b
,
c
can be represented by
2\pi/3
rotations around the vertices
of an equilateral triangle with side length 1, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The realization of
\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
as motions of the plane.

The elements
u=a^{2}b
,
v=aba
,
w=ba^{2}=u^{-1}v^{-1}
correspond to the translations
of the pattern (see Figure
1
) by one step of length
\sqrt{3}

in the directions north
\uparrow
,
southwest
\swarrow
(
240^{\circ}
north) and
southeast
\searrow
(
120^{\circ}
north), respectively.
They generate the abelian subgroup
\langle u,v,w\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}

of translations.
Note that the horizontal shift of length
1
preserves the alternating triangles pattern,
but not the additional hexagonal. This is not an element of
\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
.

One can see that

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}/\langle u,v,w\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.
Moreover,

\Delta_{(3,3,3)}\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ltimes\langle u,v,w\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ltimes(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}),

where
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
acts by shifting the generators
u\rightarrow v\rightarrow w\rightarrow u
.
It follows from the identities
a^{-1}ua=v
,
a^{-1}va=w
and
a^{-1}wa=u
.

We are interested in projective unitary representations of
\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
.
Denote the set of complex units by
{\rm S}^{1}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{C}\mid|\xi|=1\}
.

A
projective unitary representation
of a group
G

is a homomorphism from
G
to the group

{\rm PUni}(n)={\rm Uni}(n)/\langle\xi I\rangle
of projective unitaries.
Alternatively, it is a map
\pi\colon G\longrightarrow{\rm Uni}(n)
that satisfies

\displaystyle\pi(xy)=\pi(x)\pi(y)\cdot\xi(x,y),\qquad\xi(x,y)\in{\rm S}^{1}.

Let
\pi
be any such representation of
\Delta_{(3,3,3)}
.
It is easy to see that
(\pi(a))^{3},(\pi(b))^{3},(\pi(c))^{3}\in\bigl\{\gamma I\mid\gamma\in{\rm S}^{1}\bigr\}
.
Hence, we can choose scalars
\xi_{a},\xi_{b},\xi_{c}\in{\rm S}^{1}
such that

\displaystyle A^{3}=B^{3}=C^{3}=I,\qquad ABC=\lambda I,

(3.1)

where
A=\xi_{a}\pi(a)
,
B=\xi_{b}\pi(b)
,
C=\xi_{c}\pi(c)
and
\lambda\in{\rm S}^{1}
.
Henceforth, we focus on solving equation (
3.1
).

Recall that two solutions
(A,B,C)
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})

are unitary equivalent if and only if

U(A,B,C)U^{\dagger}=\bigl(UAU^{\dagger},UBU^{\dagger},UCU^{\dagger}\bigr)=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})

for some
U\in{\rm Uni}(n)
.
A unitary representation
(A,B,C)
is irreducible if and only if its not equivalent to

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\oplus(A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime},C^{\prime\prime})=(A^{\prime}\oplus A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime\prime},C^{\prime}\oplus C^{\prime\prime})
.
Equivalently, there is no proper subspace which is invariant for all
A
,
B
,
C
.

In fact, the irreducible unitary representations of equation (
3.1
) were
classified by Livinskyi and Radchenko in
[
14
]
. But here we present a modified and more
symmetric exposition of the results and also show the relation with Zauner’s unitary.
Additionally, note that there is a general (Mackey’s) theory of finding representations
of virtually abelian groups
[
12
]
.

By computing the determinants of expressions from equation (
3.1
), one can conclude that
\lambda^{3n}=1
,
so
\lambda
is a root of unity.
Denote
\mu=\lambda^{3}
and let

\displaystyle U=A^{2}B,\qquad V=ABA,\qquad W=BA^{2}.

From equation (
3.1
), it follows that

\displaystyle UV=\mu VU,\qquad VW=\mu WV,\qquad WU=\mu UW,\qquad UVW=\mu I,\qquad WVU=I.

(3.2)

The key idea is that we find irreducible representations of the equation (
3.2
)
at first.
Then we find how
(U,V,W)
, that comes from an irreducible solution
(A,B,C)
,
can split into irreducible parts.
The representation theory of the relation
UV=\mu VU
is well known
[
9
,
20
]
.
If
\mu=\exp(2\pi k{\rm i}/d)=\omega^{k}
,
{\rm gcd}(k,d)=1
,
then there are only
d
-dimensional irreducible representations which can be given by

U_{\alpha}=\alpha Z^{k},\qquad V_{\beta}=\beta X,

where
\alpha,\beta\in{\rm S}^{1}
are parameters of the representation.
Two such representations are equivalent if and only if

\bigl(\alpha^{\prime d},\beta^{\prime d}\bigr)=\bigl(\alpha^{d},\beta^{d}\bigr)
. Thus the pair
\bigl(\alpha^{d},\beta^{d}\bigr)

specifies a representation uniquely.
Packed together, the irreducible
d
-dimensional solutions for
U
,
V
,
W
in equation (
3.2
)
can be described by

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}=\alpha Z^{k}=\alpha D_{(0,k)},\qquad V_{\beta}=\beta X=\beta D_{(1,0)},\qquad W_{\gamma}=\gamma\tau^{k}X^{-1}Z^{-k}=\gamma D_{(-1,-k)},

(3.3)

where
\alpha\beta\gamma=\tau^{k}
.
And thus
\alpha^{d}\beta^{d}\gamma^{d}=\tau^{dk}=(-1)^{k(d-1)}
.

Two representations
(U_{\alpha^{\prime}},V_{\beta^{\prime}},W_{\gamma^{\prime}})
and
(U_{\alpha},V_{\beta},W_{\gamma})

are equivalent if and only if

\bigl(\alpha^{\prime d},\beta^{\prime d},\gamma^{\prime d}\bigr)=\allowbreak\bigl(\alpha^{d},\beta^{d},\gamma^{d}\bigr).

Let us return to the initial problem of finding irreducible solutions
(A,B,C)
of equation (
3.1
).
In general, the triple
(U,V,W)=\bigl(A^{2}B,ABA,BA^{2}\bigr)
, up to equivalence,
decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible summands given by equation (
3.3
).
It corresponds to a decomposition of
H
into invariant subspaces of
(U,V,W)
,
although this decomposition may not be unique if the summands are equivalent.
Let
H_{1}\subseteq H
be one of the invariant subspaces of dimension
d
.
Then the subspaces
H_{2}=A(H_{1})
and
H_{3}=A^{2}(H_{1})
are also invariant for
(U,V,W)
,
because
U(H_{2})=\bigl(AVA^{-1}\bigr)(H_{2})=AV(H_{1})=A(H_{1})=H_{2}
and similarly for
H_{3}
and
V,W
.
Since
H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{3}
is invariant for
A
as well as for
U
,
V
,
W
we immediately obtain
that irreducible
(A,B,C)
are at most
3d
-dimensional.

Let us be more specific. Pick an orthonormal basis in
H_{1}
so that

(U,V,W)_{|_{H_{1}}}=(U_{\alpha},V_{\beta},W_{\gamma}),

for some parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
as in equation (
3.3
).
This also fixes bases in
A(H_{1})
and
A^{2}(H_{1})
accordingly.
Then

\displaystyle(U,V,W)_{|_{H_{2}}}=\bigl(A(V,W,U)A^{-1}\bigr)_{|_{A(H_{1})}}=(V,W,U)_{|_{H_{1}}}=(V_{\beta},W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha}),

\displaystyle(U,V,W)_{|_{H_{3}}}=\bigl(A^{2}(W,U,V)A^{-2}\bigr)_{|_{A^{2}(H_{1})}}=(W,U,V)_{|_{H_{1}}}=(W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha},V_{\beta}).

Consider the symplectic matrix

M_{\mathfrak{Z}_{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-k^{-1}\\
k&-1\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})

along with the symplectic unitary
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\in{\rm Cli}(d)
it generates
(we take a scalar such that
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{3}=1
).

\mathfrak{Z}_{k}
has order three and permutes
X\rightarrow Z^{k}\rightarrow D_{(-1,-k)}\rightarrow X
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{k}(V_{\beta},W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha})\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{\dagger}=(U_{\beta},V_{\gamma},W_{\alpha}),\qquad\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}(W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha},V_{\beta})\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2{\dagger}}=(U_{\gamma},V_{\alpha},W_{\beta}).

What we get is that the shift

(U_{\alpha},V_{\beta},W_{\gamma})\rightarrow(V_{\beta},W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha})

in a solution (
3.3
) corresponds to
the change of parameters

({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma})\rightarrow({\beta},{\gamma},{\alpha})
.
Three shifts
(U_{\alpha},V_{\beta},W_{\gamma})
,
(V_{\beta},W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha})
,

(W_{\gamma},U_{\alpha},V_{\beta})
are inequivalent unless

\alpha^{d}=\beta^{d}=\gamma^{d}
, which can happen only in three cases
where
\alpha^{d}=\sqrt[3]{(-1)^{k(d-1)}}
for three cubic root choices.
If shifts are inequivalent, then
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
,
H_{3}
should be orthogonal to each other
according to the representation theory.
In this case, the representation of
(A,B,C)
is unique for

\bigl({\alpha^{d}},{\beta^{d}},{\gamma^{d}}\bigr)
and can be written as

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&I\\
I&0&0\\
0&I&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&W_{\gamma}\\
U_{\alpha}&0&0\\
0&V_{\beta}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&0&U^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\\
V^{\dagger}_{\beta}&0&0\\
0&W^{\dagger}_{\gamma}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(3.4)

in the chosen above basis in
H_{1}\cup A(H_{1})\cup A^{2}(H_{1})
, with

U=\begin{pmatrix}U_{\alpha}&0&0\\
0&V_{\beta}&0\\
0&0&W_{\gamma}\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad V=\begin{pmatrix}V_{\beta}&0&0\\
0&W_{\gamma}&0\\
0&0&U_{\alpha}\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad W=\begin{pmatrix}W_{\gamma}&0&0\\
0&U_{\alpha}&0\\
0&0&V_{\beta}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let us consider the remaining situation
where
\alpha^{d}=\beta^{d}=\gamma^{d}=\sqrt[3]{(-1)^{k(d-1)}}
,
which we call
the singularity case
.
We can assume that
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\sqrt[3]{\tau^{k}}=\omega_{3}^{l}\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi{\rm i}k(d+1)}{3d}\bigr)
,
l=0,1,2
,
where
\omega_{3}=\exp\bigl(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}\bigr)
. Define

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\prime}=\bigl\{\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-1}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-2}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle\mid\left|{f}\right\rangle\in H_{1}\bigr\}\subset H,

\displaystyle H_{2}^{\prime}=\bigl\{\left|{f}\right\rangle+\omega_{3}A^{-1}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+\omega_{3}^{2}A^{-2}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle\mid\left|{f}\right\rangle\in H_{1}\bigr\}\subset H,

\displaystyle H_{3}^{\prime}=\bigl\{\left|{f}\right\rangle+\omega_{3}^{2}A^{-1}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+\omega_{3}A^{-2}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle\mid\left|{f}\right\rangle\in H_{1}\bigr\}\subset H.

One can see that
H_{1}^{\prime}
is invariant for
A

and

\displaystyle U(\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-1}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-2}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=U\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-1}W\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-2}V\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-1}W_{\alpha}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-2}V_{\alpha}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}\left|{f}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-1}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}U_{\alpha}\left|{f}\right\rangle+A^{-2}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{2}U_{\alpha}\left|{f}\right\rangle\in H_{1}^{\prime},

so it is invariant for
U
and thus for

and
W
too. The same holds
for
H_{2}^{\prime}
,
H_{3}^{\prime}
. Note that
H_{1}^{\prime}+H_{2}^{\prime}+H_{3}^{\prime}=H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{3}
and
\dim H_{i}^{\prime}
is
either
d
or
0
for each
i
due to invariance.
Hence,
\dim H_{i}^{\prime}=d
for at least one index
i
.
But this means that irreducible representations of
(A,B,C)

are at most
d
-dimensional in this case and we should have
H_{1}=H_{2}=H_{3}

from the beginning.
It follows that
A
is an automorphism of
\langle U,V,W\rangle=\langle U_{\alpha},V_{\alpha},W_{\alpha}\rangle

since
V=A^{-1}UA
,
W=A^{-2}UA^{2}
.
From the description of
{\rm Cli}(d)
, we know that
A
coincides with
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}
up to
\omega_{3}
factor.
We conclude that there are three inequivalent representations

\displaystyle A=\omega_{3}^{j}\mathfrak{Z}_{k},\qquad B=\omega_{3}^{j}\mathfrak{Z}_{k}U_{\alpha},\qquad C=\lambda\omega_{3}^{j}U_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathfrak{Z}_{k},

\displaystyle U=U_{\alpha},\qquad V=V_{\alpha},\qquad W=W_{\alpha},

(3.5)

where
j=0,1,2
for each
\alpha=\sqrt[3]{\tau^{k}}
, so that is 9 in total.
Moreover, the representation (
3.4
) decomposes exactly into
the three parts that correspond to the values of

each, where the invariant subspaces are

H^{\prime}_{j+1}
.
We summarize this in the following theorem, which refines
[
14
, Theorem 5]
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu=\lambda^{3}=\exp(2\pi k{\rm i}/d)=\omega^{k}
,
{\rm gcd}(k,d)=1
.
Consider the set of parameters
{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in{\rm S}^{1}\times{\rm S}^{1}\times{\rm S}^{1}}

with
\alpha\beta\gamma=\tau^{k}
and
the equivalence relation
\bigl(\alpha^{\prime d},\beta^{\prime d},\gamma^{\prime d}\bigr)=\bigl(\alpha^{d},\beta^{d},\gamma^{d}\bigr)
.
Then equations (
3.4
)
define inequivalent irreducible
3d
-dimensional representations of (
3.1
),
except three cases where
\alpha^{d}=\beta^{d}=\gamma^{d}=\sqrt[3]{(-1)^{k(d-1)}}=\omega_{3}^{l}(-1)^{k(d-1)}
,
l=0,1,2
.
In each of those cases, equations (
3.4
) split into three inequivalent irreducible

d
-dimensional representations (
3.5
), generating nine in total.

In the simplest case,
\lambda^{3}=1
and
d=k=1
,
\omega=\tau=1
. For three complex units with

\alpha\beta\gamma=1
, we have
U_{\alpha}=\alpha
,
U_{\beta}=\beta
,
U_{\gamma}=\gamma
and
equation (
3.4
) reads as

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\gamma\\
\alpha&0&0\\
0&\beta&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\bar{\alpha}\\
\bar{\beta}&0&0\\
0&\bar{\gamma}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

This solution will be reducible if
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\omega_{3}^{l}
.
Since
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}=1
(up to
\omega_{3}
factor), equations (
3.5
) give the irreducible parts where
j=0,1,2
,

A=\omega_{3}^{j},\qquad B=\omega_{3}^{j+l},\qquad C=\lambda\omega_{3}^{j-l}.

We remark that in general
U_{\alpha}^{d}=\alpha^{d}I
and similarly for
\beta
,
\gamma
.
This helps to understand how, in a general representation, the tuple
(U,V,W)
decomposes into
irreducible parts. We will look at the spectral decompositions of

,
V^{d}
and
W^{d}
.

4
Canonical order-three unitaries

The matrix
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}
is a canonical order-three unitary and
we have just seen its connection to
\Delta{(3,3,3)}
.
In this section, we show that any canonical order-three unitary appears
in representations of the triangle group
\Delta{(3,3,3)}
and explain
how this can be used.

The following lemma can be checked by a straightforward computation.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A_{M}\in{\rm Cli}(d)
be a canonical order-three unitary with

M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&m_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{d}})
, so that

m_{1}+m_{4}=-1
,
m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3}=1
,
M^{3}=I

\mathrm{~mod~}\bar{d}
.
Then
\forall{\bf a}=(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}

\displaystyle(D_{\bf a})\bigl(A_{M}D_{\bf a}A_{M}^{\dagger}\bigr)\bigl(A_{M}^{2}D_{\bf a}A_{M}^{2{\dagger}}\bigr)=\tau^{-a_{1}^{2}m_{3}+a_{1}a_{2}(m_{1}-m_{4})+a_{2}^{2}m_{2}}I,

\displaystyle(D_{\bf a})\bigl(A_{M}^{2}D_{\bf a}A_{M}^{2{\dagger}}\bigr)\bigl(A_{M}D_{\bf a}A_{M}^{\dagger}\bigr)=\tau^{-(-a_{1}^{2}m_{3}+a_{1}a_{2}(m_{1}-m_{4})+a_{2}^{2}m_{2})}I.

In particular,

\displaystyle(Z)\bigl(A_{M}ZA_{M}^{\dagger}\bigr)\bigl(A_{M}^{2}ZA_{M}^{2{\dagger}}\bigr)=\tau^{m_{2}}I,\qquad(Z)\bigl(A_{M}^{2}ZA_{M}^{2{\dagger}}\bigr)\bigl(A_{M}ZA_{M}^{\dagger}\bigr)=\tau^{-m_{2}}I.

Note that this lemma does not hold for non-canonical order-three unitaries, since
there would be no scalar operator on the right-hand side.
It also can be generalized for
A_{[M,\bf b]}
,
but we will try to keep things simpler here.

Let us put
A=A_{M}
,
U=\epsilon Z
and
correspondingly
B=AU
,
C=\lambda U^{-1}A
, where
\epsilon,\lambda\in{\rm S}^{1}
.

We have that
A^{3}=I
and

\displaystyle B^{3}=\bigl(AUA^{-1}\bigr)\bigl(A^{2}UA^{-2}\bigr)(U)=\epsilon^{3}\tau^{m_{2}}I,

\displaystyle C^{3}=\lambda^{3}\bigl(\bigl(A^{2}UA^{-2}\bigr)\bigl(AUA^{-1}\bigr)(U))^{-1}=\lambda^{3}\epsilon^{-3}\tau^{m_{2}}I,\qquad ABC=\lambda I.

By setting
appropriate
\epsilon
and
\lambda
, we conclude the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

For a canonical order-three unitary
A_{M}\in{\rm Cli}(d)
, the tuple

(A,B,C)=\bigl(A_{M},\epsilon A_{M}Z,\lambda\epsilon^{-1}Z^{-1}A_{M}\bigr)

with the corresponding

\displaystyle(U,V,W)=(\epsilon Z,\epsilon D_{(-m_{2},m_{1})},\epsilon D_{(m_{2},m_{4})})

(4.1)

is a solution of equation (
3.1
) if

\epsilon^{3}=\tau^{-m_{2}}
,
\lambda^{3}=\omega^{-m_{2}}
.

One can find that this theorem can be generalized for
A_{[M,{\bf b}]}
with
{\bf b}\neq 0
,
so that any canonical order-three unitary gives rise to a representation of the triangle group.
But the case of matrices
A_{[M,{\bf 0}]}
is principal.
We now use Theorem
3.1
to investigate their properties.

At first, observe that

\displaystyle(U,W)=\bigl(\epsilon Z,\epsilon A_{M}ZA_{M}^{-1}\bigr)=(\epsilon Z,\epsilon D_{(m_{2},m_{4})})=\bigl(\epsilon Z,\epsilon\tau^{m_{2}m_{4}}X^{m_{2}}Z^{m_{4}}\bigr).

(4.2)

It follows immediately that if
m_{2}
is coprime with
d

then
U
and
W
generate the Weyl–Heisenberg group up to phases,
that is,
\langle U,W\rangle/\langle\xi I\rangle={\rm WH}(d)/\langle\xi I\rangle
.
Thus
(U,V,W)
should be irreducible, and since
\mu=\lambda^{3}=\omega^{-m_{2}}

is a root of 1 of order
d
, we have the singularity case here.
From equation (
3.5
), it follows that there is a unitary
T

that satisfies

A_{M}=T\bigl(\omega_{3}^{j}\mathfrak{Z}_{-m_{2}}\bigr)T^{\dagger},\qquad\epsilon Z=T(\alpha Z^{-m_{2}})T^{\dagger},\qquad\epsilon D_{(m_{2},m_{4})}=T(\alpha D_{(-1,m_{2})})T^{\dagger}

for some
j=0,1,2
and
\alpha^{3d}=(-1)^{-m_{2}(d-1)}
.
Hence
T\in{\rm Cli}(d)
and
A_{M}
is conjugate to
\mathfrak{Z}_{-m_{2}}

(up to
\omega_{3}
factor) in the Clifford group.

If
d>3
is prime and
m_{2}=0\mod d
, then we can consider the conjugation

\begin{pmatrix}a&1\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&0\\
m_{3}&m_{4}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&1\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}*&a(m_{4}-m_{1})-m_{3}\\
*&*\end{pmatrix}\mod d.

We have that
m_{4}-m_{1}\neq 0\mathrm{~mod~}d
. So there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
such that
a(m_{4}-m_{1})-m_{3}=-1\mathrm{~mod~}d
.
And so we have the conjugation to
\mathfrak{Z}_{1}
by the previous consideration.

Observe that

\begin{pmatrix}l^{-1}&0\\
0&l\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-k^{-1}\\
k&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}l&0\\
0&l^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-k^{-1}l^{-2}\\
kl^{2}&-1\end{pmatrix}=M_{\mathfrak{Z}_{kl^{2}}}\mathrm{~mod~}d,

hence
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{kl^{2}}
, where by “
\simeq
” we denote equivalence in the Clifford group.
This means
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{1}
if
k\neq 0
is a quadratic residue modulo
d

and
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{k_{0}}
for some fixed non-residue
k_{0}
otherwise.
But we also have

\begin{pmatrix}a&1\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}&-1\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}*&-\bigl(a^{2}+a+1\bigr)\\
*&*\end{pmatrix},

which implies
\mathfrak{Z}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{a^{2}+a+1}
for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
If we prove that
a^{2}+a+1
can be a quadratic non-residue for some
a
then
the conclusion is
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{1}
for any
k\neq 0
.
Assume the contrary, that
a^{2}+a+1
is a residue for any
a
.
Consider
4\bigl(a^{2}+a+1\bigr)=(2a+1)^{2}+3
. This means that
x^{2}+3
is a residue for any
x
,
i.e., the set of residues is periodic with period 3, and so any number is a residue,
a contradiction.
We thus showed that for a prime
d>3
any canonical order-three unitary
A_{M}
is conjugate
to Zauner’s unitary
\mathfrak{Z}_{1}
in the Clifford group, which was proved in
[
10
]
by relying
on the number theory more heavily.
If
d=3
, then one can find that
\mathfrak{Z}_{1}\not\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{2}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{1}^{2}
.

In the general case, where
m_{2}
and
d
are not coprime, the described approach still can be useful.
Assume that
n=\gcd(m_{2},d)
.
Then
\mu=\omega^{-m_{2}}=\omega_{d/n}^{-m_{2}/n}
.
Again, let us look at the decomposition of
(U,W)
from equation (
4.2
).
Up to phases,
\langle U,W\rangle=\langle Z,X^{m_{2}}\rangle
which decomposes into a sum
of
n

d/n
-dimensional irreducible parts.
Let those parts correspond to the parameters

\bigl(\alpha_{i}^{d/n},\beta_{i}^{d/n},\gamma_{i}^{d/n}\bigr)
,

i=0,1,\dots,n-1
as in equation (
3.3
).
By looking at the spectrum of
(\epsilon Z)^{d/n}
, we find that

\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl\{\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{i}\bigr\}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl\{\alpha_{i}^{d/n}\bigr\}.

Hence, we may think that
\forall i

\alpha_{i}^{d/n}=\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{i}
.
It follows that those parts are inequivalent.
It is easy to see that they correspond to the invariant subspaces

\displaystyle H_{i}=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{d/n-1}\{\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle\}

(4.3)

since

(\epsilon Z)^{d/n}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle=\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{d}^{(i+m_{2}l)d/n}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle=\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{i}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle(\epsilon D_{(-m_{2},m_{1})})^{d/n}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle=\epsilon^{d/n}\tau^{-m_{2}m_{1}(d/n)^{2}}\omega_{n}^{im_{1}}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle(\epsilon D_{(m_{2},m_{4})})^{d/n}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle=\epsilon^{d/n}\tau^{m_{2}m_{4}(d/n)^{2}}\omega_{n}^{im_{4}}\left|{i+m_{2}l}\right\rangle.

Recall that
\tau^{d}=(-1)^{d-1}
. In total, for any
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
,

\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{i}^{d/n},\beta_{i}^{d/n},\gamma_{i}^{d/n}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{i},\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{im_{1}}(-1)^{(d-1)(m_{2}/n)m_{1}(d/n)},\epsilon^{d/n}\omega_{n}^{im_{4}}(-1)^{(d-1)(m_{2}/n)m_{4}(d/n)}\bigr).

For each
i
, it is either the singularity case, i.e.,

\alpha_{i}^{d/n}=\beta_{i}^{d/n}=\gamma_{i}^{d/n}
,
or non-singularity, but those should come in triples which are
shifts of each other.

As an example, let us look at the case where dimension
d=3(3k+1)>3
is odd and

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
\frac{d-3}{3}&-2\end{pmatrix}\mod d.

It is known that in these dimensions any canonical order-three unitary is
conjugate to either
\mathfrak{Z}
,
\mathfrak{Z}^{2}
or
A_{M}

[
8
]
.
SIC fiducial vectors were found in the eigenspace of
A_{M}
in small dimensions
(this case is denoted by
F_{a}
in
[
19
]
).

By Theorem
4.2
, we can set
\epsilon=\tau^{-1}
,

\lambda=\omega^{-1}
and so
\epsilon^{d/3}=\omega_{3}
,

\mu=\lambda^{3}=\omega_{d/3}^{-1}
.
We have that there are
3
irreducible
d/3
-dimensional parts
in the decomposition of
(U,V,W)
that come from equation (
4.1
).
For
i=0,1,2
, we obtain

\bigl(\alpha_{i}^{d/3},\beta_{i}^{d/3},\gamma_{i}^{d/3}\bigr)=\bigl(\omega_{3}^{i+1},\omega_{3}^{i+1},\omega_{3}^{i+1}\bigr),

hence it is the singularity case. This means that invariant subspaces

H_{i}
from equation (
4.3

A_{M}

and that on each

unitary
A_{M}
is
conjugate to
\mathfrak{Z}_{-1}
from
{\rm Cli}(d/3)
.

Another way to see this is to consider the tensor decomposition of
H
under which

{\rm WH}(d)
splits as a tensor product of
{\rm WH}(d/3)
and
{\rm WH}(3)
.
In general, for odd and relatively prime
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
, we can define
the tensor product
\hat{\otimes}
for
\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}n_{2}}
by

\left|{i\mathrm{~mod~}n_{1}n_{2}}\right\rangle=\left|{i\mathrm{~mod~}n_{1}}\right\rangle\hat{\otimes}\left|{i\mathrm{~mod~}n_{2}}\right\rangle,

which corresponds to a permutation of the standard basis in
\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}n_{2}}
.
Under this tensor product, we have that for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}n_{2}}

D_{(a,b)}^{(n_{1}n_{2})}=D_{(a,n_{2}^{-1}b)}^{(n_{1})}\hat{\otimes}D_{(a,n_{1}^{-1}b)}^{(n_{2})},

where
n_{2}^{-1}
is inverse of
n_{2}\mathrm{~mod~}n_{1}
and
n_{1}^{-1}
is inverse of
n_{1}\mathrm{~mod~}n_{2}
(see
[
1
,
3
]
).
Conversely, for any
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}
,
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{n_{2}}

D_{(p,q)}^{(n_{1})}\hat{\otimes}D_{(r,s)}^{(n_{2})}=D_{(pn_{2}n_{2}^{-1}+rn_{1}n_{1}^{-1},qn_{2}+sn_{1})}^{(n_{1}n_{2})}.

Moreover, under this tensor product the Clifford group splits as well.
One can verify that for
{M^{\prime}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}})}
,
M^{\prime\prime}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{n_{2}})
,
we have

A_{M}=A_{M^{\prime}}\hat{\otimes}A_{M^{\prime\prime}}

up to a complex unit factor, where

\displaystyle M=n_{2}\begin{pmatrix}n_{2}^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}M^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&n_{2}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}+n_{1}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}M^{\prime\prime}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&n_{1}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}n_{2}}),

(4.4)

by looking at the action of
A_{M}
on the displacement operators.

In our example, we have
n_{1}=d/3=3k+1
,
n_{2}=3
and thus
n_{1}^{-1}=1
,
n_{2}^{-1}=-k
.
From equation (
4.4
), we conclude that

M^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-k\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
\frac{d-3}{3}&-2\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{~mod~}d/3\right)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&9\\
k&-2\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{3k+1})

and

M^{\prime\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
\frac{d-3}{3}&-2\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{~mod~}3\right)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{3}),

implying
A_{M}=A_{M^{\prime}}\hat{\otimes}I_{3}
for
M^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&9\\
k&-2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mathrm{~mod~}3k+1
.
Note that
H_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{d/3}\otimes\left|{i}\right\rangle=\mathbb{C}^{d/3}\hat{\otimes}\left|{i}\right\rangle
, hence

A_{M}
acts as the canonical order-three unitary
A_{M^{\prime}}\in{\rm Cli}(3k+1)
on each
H_{i}

which fits our conclusion obtained before. And we also get that
A_{M^{\prime}}\simeq\mathfrak{Z}_{-1}
in an alternative
way.

As a final remark, we add that there is also a notion of
the
full triangle group

\overline{\Delta}(3,3,3)
.
This group can be obtained from the ordinary
\Delta(3,3,3)

by adding a reflection along the side of fiducial triangle in Figure
1

(and so any other reflection along triangles sides is there).
In fact,
\big|\overline{\Delta}(3,3,3):{\Delta}(3,3,3)\big|=2
.
One can see an analogy between reflected motions in

\overline{\Delta}(3,3,3)
and anti-unitaries from
{\rm ECli}(d)
.
Perhaps, this connection with motions of the plane could be utilized
for the SIC constructions.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have related canonical order-three unitaries
to representations of the triangle group
{(3,3,3)}
.
We gave the full characterization of those representations
and showed how they can be used to derive properties of
canonical order-three unitaries. In particular, we have proved some
results about them which previously relied on number theory.
We hope this could generate insights about their importance for
the SICs existence problem.
