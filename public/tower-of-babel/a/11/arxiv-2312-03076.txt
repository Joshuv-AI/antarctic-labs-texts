XOR Lemmas for Communication via Marginal Information
Siddharth Iyer, Anup Rao

Abstract
We define the marginal information of a communication protocol, and use it to prove XOR lemmas for communication complexity. We show that if every C C -bit protocol has bounded advantage for computing a Boolean function f f , then every Ω ~ ​ ( C ​ n ) \widetilde{\Omega}(C\sqrt{n}) -bit protocol has advantage exp ⁡ ( − Ω ⁡ ( n ) ) \exp(-\Omega(n)) for computing the n n -fold xor f ⊕ n f^{\oplus n} . We prove exponentially small bounds in the average case setting, and near optimal bounds for product distributions and for bounded-round protocols.

Introduction

If a function is hard to compute, is it even harder to compute it many times? This old question is often challenging, and new answers are usually accompanied by foundational ideas. We give new answers in the framework of communication complexity, accompanied by a new measure of complexity called
marginal information

A wide variety of important lower bounds in computer science ultimately rely on information theoretic lower bounds in communication complexity, including lower bounds on the depth of monotone circuits

1.1
The evolution of information complexity

Marginal information is the most recent advance in an evolution of definitions about information. We relate bounds on the communication and advantage for computing
f
to the corresponding parameters for

via a scheme that has been applied many times before. We prove:

Step 1

Every protocol computing

with significant advantage and small communication has small marginal information; see
Theorem

5

Step 2

Marginal information is subadditive, so the marginal information for computing

is smaller by a factor of

Theorem

6

Step 3

Small marginal information can be compressed to give protocols with small communication; see
Theorems

7

10

Definitions of information are famously subtle. In order to make this strategy work, the marginal information needs to permit all 3 steps, and even minor changes to the definition can make one of the steps infeasible.

Our current definition builds on important insights and intuitions developed in theoretical computer science over a period of decades. An early precursor to the use of information theory in computer science is the work of Kalyanasundaram and Schnitger, who used Kolmogorov complexity to prove lower bounds on the randomized communication complexity of the disjointness function
[
SK87
]

[
Raz92
]

[

]

2
bits

The next chapter of the story was written during the study of parallel repetition, a vital tool in the development of probabilistically checkable proofs. Raz
[
Raz95
]
proved the first exponentially small bounds in this context using the Kullback-Liebler divergence as a measure of information. Given a distribution

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(xy|W)}\Big[\mathsf{D}(p(x|yW)||p(x|y))+\mathsf{D}(p(y|xW)||p(y|x))\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(xy|W)}\Big[\log\Big(\frac{p(x|yW)}{p(x|y)}\cdot\frac{p(y|xW)}{p(y|x)}\Big)\Big].

(1)

Lemma 29
.

For every
1>\kappa>0
, if
a(xym),b(xym)
are two distributions, there exists a rectangular set

such that
a(T)\geq 1-3\kappa
and for all

\frac{a(xm|T)}{b(xm)},\frac{a(ym|T)}{b(ym)},\frac{a(m|T)}{a(m)}\geq\kappa.

Proof.

The set
T
is constructed by an iterative process. Initially,
T
is the set of all triples
xym
. In each iteration, if there is
xm
such that

\displaystyle\frac{a(xm|T)}{b(xm)}<\kappa,

(39)

then delete
xm
from the support of
T
, if there is
ym
such that

\frac{a(ym|T)}{b(ym)}<\kappa,

then delete
ym
from the support of
T
, and if there is
m
such that

\frac{a(m|T)}{a(m)}<\kappa,

then delete
m
from the support of
T
. The process halts when there are no more elements to delete. Because the distributions we are working with have finite support, this process must eventually terminate. Initially,
T
is rectangular, and each deletion step leaves us with another rectangular set
T
, so the final
T
is also rectangular.

Let us bound
a(T)
. For each pair
xm
that was deleted from the support of
T
because of
Equation

39
, let
T_{xm}
denote the set
T
right before
xm
was deleted. If
xm
was not deleted, let
T_{xm}
denote the empty set.

The total mass deleted using

is exactly

\displaystyle\sum_{xm}a(xmT_{xm})=\sum_{xm}a(T_{xm})\cdot a(xm|T_{xm})<\sum_{xm}\kappa\cdot b(xm)=\kappa.

Similarly, the total mass deleted using each of the other rules is also at most

. By the union bound, this proves that

when the process terminates.
∎

(by
Equation

67

\displaystyle\leq q(t\neq\bot)\cdot(1+O(2^{-3\mathsf{M}/I}))\cdot(1+O(\varepsilon\mathsf{M}/I)),

(by
33

and

\displaystyle q(t\neq\bot)=\sum_{j=0}^{C}q(t=j)

(72)

\displaystyle\Gamma\bigg(\sum_{i=2}^{C}E_{i}>2^{7\mathsf{M}/I}\cdot\sqrt{C\mathsf{M}}\Big|QS\mathcal{Z},t=\bot\bigg)<O(2^{-\mathsf{M}/I}),

which would complete the proof of

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{C}\frac{\Gamma(E_{i}=1,QS\mathcal{Z},t=\bot)}{\Gamma(QS\mathcal{Z},t=\bot)}

(by

8
Smoothing protocols

A smooth protocol is a protocol where each message bit is close to being uniformly distributed:

Definition 39
.

Given a protocol distribution
p(xym)
with
C
messages satisfying
m_{2},\dotsc,m_{C}\in\{0,1\}
, we say that the distribution is
\beta
-smooth if for all
i>1
,
|p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})-1/2|\leq\beta
.

Here we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 40
.

For every Boolean function
f
, every protocol distribution
p(xym)
with
C
messages satisfying
m_{2},\dotsc,m_{C}\in\{0,1\}
, and every
\beta>0
, assuming that
\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p,f)
is finite, there is a
\beta
-smooth protocol
p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
with
C^{\prime}\leq O(C\cdot\log(IC)/\beta^{2})
messages such that
\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p^{\prime},f)\leq\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p,f)+1
, and
m^{\prime}_{2},\dotsc,m^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Let
q(xym)
be a rectangular distribution realizing
\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p,f)
. Let
L>1
be a large odd number to be determined. Define the pair of distributions
q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime}),p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
as follows. Let
m^{\prime}_{0},m^{\prime}_{1}
have the same support as
m_{0},m_{1}
, and let
m^{\prime}_{2},\dotsc,m^{\prime}_{C}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
. In
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
, the
i
’th message will correspond to
m^{\prime}_{i}
.

For
a\in\{0,1\}
, define the following distributions supported on
\{0,1\}^{L}
:

\displaystyle s_{a}(r)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{1}{2}+(-1)^{a+r_{j}}\cdot\beta

\displaystyle t_{a}(r)

\displaystyle=s_{a}\Big(r\big|(-1)^{a}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{L}(-1)^{r_{j}}\geq 0\Big)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}_{a}(r)

\displaystyle=s_{a}\Big(r\big|(-1)^{a}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{L}(-1)^{r_{j}}<0\Big).

In words,
s_{a}(r)
is the distribution of
L
independent bits that are biased towards being equal to
a
,
t_{a}(r)
is this distribution conditioned on the event that the majority of the bits is equal to
a
and
t^{\prime}_{a}(r)
is the distribution conditioned on the event that the majority is not
a
.

Now we define a protocol distribution
p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
and a rectangular distribution
q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
. Given
m^{\prime}
, let
D(m^{\prime}_{i})
denote the unique string satisfying
D(m^{\prime}_{0},m^{\prime}_{1})=(m^{\prime}_{0},m^{\prime}_{1})
, and

(-1)^{D(m^{\prime}_{i})}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{L}(-1)^{m^{\prime}_{i,j}}\geq 0.

In other words,
D
decodes each block of
L
bits by taking the majority. Below we abuse notation and write
D(m)=D(m_{0}),D(m_{1}),\dotsc,D(m_{C})
.

Define

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})

\displaystyle=q(xyD(m^{\prime}))\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{C}t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i}).

The definition ensures that
q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
is rectangular, and that conditioned on
D(m^{\prime})
,
xy
is independent of
m^{\prime}
. Define the distribution
p^{\prime}(xymm^{\prime})
as follows:

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime}_{0}m^{\prime}_{1})

\displaystyle=p(xym^{\prime}_{0}m^{\prime}_{1}),

and for
i>1
,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(m_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}|xym_{<i}m^{\prime}_{<i})

\displaystyle=p(m_{i}|xy,m_{<i}=D(m^{\prime})_{<i})\cdot s_{m_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})

In words, in the protocol
p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})
, the parties privately sample each message bit
m_{i}
according to the protocol distribution
p
. However, instead of sending this sampled bit, they send
m^{\prime}_{i}
sampled according to
s_{m_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})
. After this transmission, they continue the protocol using
D(m^{\prime})_{<i}
. Strictly speaking, in order to ensure that the new protocol is a protocol distribution, we require that all the odd bits are transmitted by Alice and all even bits are sent by Bob. This can be easily achieved by inserting random bits into the transcript, but we leave out the details here.

There is some small chance that for
i>1
,
D(m^{\prime})_{i}\neq m_{i}
, but by the Chernoff bound,

p^{\prime}(D(m^{\prime})_{i}\neq m_{i})\leq\exp(-\Omega(\beta^{2}L)).

We have that for all
xym^{\prime}
in the support of
q^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})}{p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})}=\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))}{p^{\prime}(xyD(m^{\prime}))}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{p^{\prime}(m^{\prime}_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{\leq i})}.

For any
xyD(m^{\prime})
such that
q(xyD(m^{\prime}))>0
, we can bound

\displaystyle\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))}{p^{\prime}(xyD(m^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))}{p(xyD(m^{\prime}))}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{p(D(m^{\prime})_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{<i})}{p^{\prime}(D(m^{\prime})_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{<i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))}{p(xyD(m^{\prime}))}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{1}{1-\exp(-\Omega(\beta^{2}L))},

where we assumed that
p(xyD(m^{\prime}))>0
; if
p(xyD(m^{\prime}))=0
then
q(xyD(m^{\prime}))=0
for otherwise the marginal information cost would be unbounded. Next,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{p^{\prime}(m^{\prime}_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{\leq i})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p^{\prime}(m_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{\leq i})}[p^{\prime}(m^{\prime}_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{\leq i},m_{i})]}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p^{\prime}(m_{i}|xyD(m^{\prime})_{\leq i})}[s_{m_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i}|D(m^{\prime}_{i}))]}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{p^{\prime}(m_{i}=D(m^{\prime})_{i})\cdot t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})+p^{\prime}(m_{i}\neq D(m^{\prime})_{i})\cdot t^{\prime}_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(m_{i}=D(m^{\prime})_{i})+p^{\prime}(m_{i}\neq D(m^{\prime})_{i})\cdot\frac{t^{\prime}_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(m_{i}=D(m^{\prime})_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=2}^{C}\frac{1}{1-\exp(-\Omega(\beta^{2}L))}.

So, we obtain the bound:

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})}{p^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})}=\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))}{p(xyD(m^{\prime}))}\cdot(1+2C\exp(-\Omega(\beta^{2}L))).

Moreover, for all
xym^{\prime}
in the support of
q^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(xy|m^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{\prime}(xym^{\prime})}{q^{\prime}(m^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(xyD(m^{\prime}))\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{C}t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}{q(D(m^{\prime}))\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{C}t_{D(m^{\prime})_{i}}(m^{\prime}_{i})}

\displaystyle=q(xy|D(m^{\prime})).

Finally, since
q^{\prime}(xy|m^{\prime})=q(xy|D(m^{\prime}))
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q^{\prime}(xy|m^{\prime})}[(-1)^{f}]\Big|

\displaystyle=|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q^{\prime}(xy|D(m^{\prime}))}[(-1)^{f}]\Big|.

Thus, we get that

\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p^{\prime},f)\leq\mathsf{M}^{\mathsf{ext}}_{I}(p,f)+IC\cdot\exp(-\Omega(\beta^{2}L)).

Setting
L=O(\log(IC)/\beta^{2})
proves the theorem.
∎

Smooth protocols have the feature that the log-ratios of the information terms are tightly concentrated. To explain this phenomenon, we need to introduce a few definitions. For every
xym
in the support of
p
, and
j\geq 2
, define the
j
-th divergence costs:

\displaystyle d^{A}_{j}(xm)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq j\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m_{i}|xm_{<i})}\Big[\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xm_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{<i})}\Big],

\displaystyle d^{B}_{j}(ym)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq j\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m_{i}|ym_{<i})}\Big[\log\frac{p(m_{i}|ym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{<i})}\Big],

\displaystyle d_{j}(xym)

\displaystyle=d^{A}_{j}(xm)+d^{B}_{j}(ym).

By the non-negativity of divergence, the divergence costs are monotone i.e.
d_{j}(xym)\leq d_{j+1}(xym)
. Since the protocol is
\beta
-smooth, we have

\displaystyle d_{j+1}^{A}(xm)-d_{j}^{A}(xm)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1/2+\beta}{1/2-\beta}\leq 5\beta,

\displaystyle d_{j+1}^{B}(ym)-d_{j}^{B}(ym)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1/2+\beta}{1/2-\beta}\leq 5\beta.

(76)

We say a function
r(xym)
taking values in
\{1,\ldots,C\}
is a
frontier
if every
m
contains exactly one prefix of the type
m^{\prime}_{\leq r(xym^{\prime})}
, and that is the prefix
m_{\leq r(xym)}
. Alternatively, for every
m,m^{\prime}
such that
r(xym)\neq r(xym^{\prime})
, it holds that both
r(xym)
and
r(xym^{\prime})
are larger than the length of the longest common prefix of
m
and
m^{\prime}
.
Given a frontier
r(xym)
, define

\displaystyle F_{r,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Big\{xym:\Big|\sum_{i\geq 2}^{r(xym)}\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{<i})}-d_{r(xym)}(xym)\Big|\geq\alpha\Big\},

\displaystyle F^{A}_{r,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Big\{xym:\Big|\sum_{i\geq 2\text{ odd}}^{r(xym)}\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{\leq i})}-d^{A}_{r(xym)}(xm)\Big|\geq\alpha\Big\},

\displaystyle F^{B}_{r,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Big\{xym:\Big|\sum_{i\geq 2\text{ even}}^{r(xym)}\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{\leq i})}-d^{B}_{r(xym)}(ym)\Big|\geq\alpha\Big\}.

(77)

Lemma 41
.

Let
r(xym)
be a frontier such that for every
xym
, it holds that
d_{r(xym)}(xym)\leq\tau
.
Then
p(F_{r,\alpha}),p(F^{A}_{r,\alpha})
and
p(F^{B}_{r,\alpha})
are all at most
2\exp(-\Omega(\alpha^{2}/\tau))
.

Proof.

We prove the inequality for
p(F_{r,\alpha})
; the proofs for the other two terms are similar.
Define the random variable
z_{0},z_{1}\ldots
where
z_{0}=z_{1}=0
and for every
i\geq 2
,

\displaystyle z_{i}=\begin{cases}\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{<i})}&\text{if $i\leq r(xym)$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and let
t_{i}=z_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}[z_{i}]
. Then by definition
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[t_{i}|t_{<i}]=0
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sup(z_{i}|xym_{<i})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{m_{i}}\Big\{\log\frac{p(m_{i}|xym_{<i})}{p(m_{i}|m_{<i})}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1/2-\beta+\sqrt{d_{i}(xym)-d_{i-1}(xym)}}{1/2-\beta}

\displaystyle\leq O(\sqrt{d_{i}(xym)-d_{i-1}(xym)}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\inf(z_{i}|xym_{<i})

\displaystyle\geq\log\frac{1/2-\beta}{1/2-\beta-\sqrt{d_{i}(xym)-d_{i-1}(xym)}}

\displaystyle\geq-O(\sqrt{d_{i}(xym)-d_{i-1}(xym)}).

So, if we define
L
as below, we have

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\sup_{xym}\sum_{i=2}^{C}(\sup(t_{i}|xym_{<i})-\inf(t_{i}|xym_{<i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{xym}\sum_{i=2}^{C}(\sup(z_{i}|xym_{<i})-\inf(z_{i}|xym_{<i}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq O(\tau).

It is well known that if
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[t_{i}]=0
, then
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(t_{i})]\leq\exp((\sup(t_{i})-\inf(t_{i}))^{2}/8)
(see Lemma 2.6 in
[
JHM+98
]
). We can use this inequality to bound:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m|xy)}\Big[\exp(\frac{4\alpha}{L}\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{C}t_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m_{\leq 2}|xy)}\Big[\exp(\frac{4\alpha}{L}t_{2})\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p(m|xym_{<3})}\Big[\exp(\frac{4\alpha}{L}\cdot\sum_{i=3}^{C}t_{i})\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\dotsc

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{(4\alpha/L)^{2}\sup_{xym}\sum_{i=2}^{C}(\sup(t_{i}|xym_{<i})-\inf(t_{i}|xym_{<i}))^{2}}{8}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(2\alpha^{2}/L\Big).

So by Markov’s inequality, we get:

\displaystyle p(\sum_{i=2}^{C}t_{i}>\alpha)\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\exp(\frac{4\alpha}{L}\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{C}t_{i})\Big]\cdot\exp(-4\alpha^{2}/L)\leq\exp(-\Omega(\alpha^{2}/\tau)).

Applying the same argument with
t_{i}=-t_{i}
proves the other inequality. Defining
z_{i},t_{i}
appropriately proves the other inequalities using the same proof.
∎
