Properties of functions on a bounded charge space Journal: TBD
Jonathan M. Keith

Abstract
A charge space ( X , 𝒜 , μ ) (X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu) is a generalisation of a measure space, consisting of a sample space X X , a field of subsets 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} and a finitely additive measure μ \mu , also known as a charge . Key properties a real-valued function on X X may possess include T 1 T_{1} -measurability and integrability. These properties are generalisations of corresponding properties of real-valued functions on a (countably additive) measure space. However, these properties are less well studied than their measure-theoretic counterparts. This paper describes new characterisations of T 1 T_{1} -measurability and integrability in the case that the charge space is bounded, that is, μ ⁡ ( X ) < ∞ \mu(X)<\infty . These characterisations are convenient for analytic purposes; for example, they facilitate simple proofs that T 1 T_{1} -measurability is equivalent to conventional measurability and integrability is equivalent to Lebesgue integrability, if ( X , 𝒜 , μ ) (X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu) is a complete measure space. Several additional contributions to the theory of bounded charges are also presented. New characterisations of equality almost everywhere of two real-valued functions on a bounded charge space are provided. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the function space L 1 ​ ( X , 𝒜 , μ ) L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu) to be a Banach space are determined. Lastly, the concept of completion of a measure space is generalised for charge spaces, and it is shown that under certain conditions, completion of a charge space adds no new equivalence classes to the quotient space ℒ p ​ ( X , 𝒜 , μ ) {\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu) .

Introduction

A
field of sets

{\mathcal{A}}
, also known as a
field of subsets
or an
algebra of sets
, is a subset of the power set
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
of some
sample space

X
such that:

1.

\emptyset\in{\mathcal{A}}
,

2.

A^{c}\in{\mathcal{A}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, and

3.

A\cup B\in{\mathcal{A}}
for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

A
charge

\mu
defined on
{\mathcal{A}}
is a real-valued function such that:

1.

\mu(\emptyset)=0
, and

2.

\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B)
for all disjoint
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

A charge that takes only non-negative values is sometimes called a
content
or
finitely additive measure
. However, in this paper the term
charge
is preferred, and may be assumed to refer to a non-negative valued charge unless otherwise stated. A triple
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
of such objects is called a
charge space
.

There is a little used but well developed theory of integration for real-valued functions on charge spaces. This theory actually predates the Lebesgue integral, beginning with the finitely additive measure theory developed by Giuseppe Peano (1858-1932) and independently by Camille Jordan (1838-1922) (see
[
1
]
for a nice historical account). The Lebesgue integral based on countably additive measures has proved to be more convenient and popular, but integration based on charges remains substantially more general. A brief review of this theory is provided in Section
2
, including definitions of the function spaces
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, comprised of integrable functions on a charge space.

One reason finitely additive measure and integration theory have not become as popular as their countably additive counterparts is that definitions of the key properties of
T_{1}
-measurability and integrability are a little more complicated than corresponding properties in the countably additive context, and initially seem less convenient for analysis. In particular, conventionally measurable functions are pointwise approximable by a non-decreasing sequence of simple functions. It is not immediately clear whether this useful property holds in general for
T_{1}
-measurable functions.

A goal of this paper is to facilitate analysis of functions on charge spaces, by providing more convenient characterisations of
T_{1}
-measurability and integrability. In Section
3
, two new characterisations are developed for each of these properties, for the special case of a bounded charge space, that is, one for which
\mu(X)<\infty
. First,
T_{1}
-measurability is characterised in terms of inverse images of rays, using a criterion resembling a frequently used characterisation of conventional measurability, and an additional smoothness condition on the tails of the function. A characterisation of integrability is obtained by strengthening this smoothness condition. Second,
T_{1}
-measurability can be characterised in terms of pointwise approximation by an increasing sequence of simple functions, and integrability can be characterised by imposing an additional condition on this sequence. Section
3
also contains two useful new characterisations of equality almost everywhere of functions on a charge space.

To demonstrate the utility of the new characterisations, a number of simple corollaries are also proved in Section
3
. In particular, it is shown that the
L_{p}
spaces defined in Section
2
are equivalent to conventional Lebesgue function spaces for charge spaces that are also complete measure spaces.

Another factor inhibiting the analysis of functions on charge spaces is that
L_{p}
spaces are not necessarily complete in this context, unlike Lebesgue function spaces. To partially address this deficiency, Section
4
identifies necessary and sufficient conditions for
L_{p}
spaces to be Banach spaces.

The concept of a complete measure space is generalised for charges in Section
5
. This section also introduces a construction that is here called
null modification
(see Lemma
33
and proof). Null modification is used to show that, under certain condtions, expanding a field by adding additional null sets leaves the corresponding
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
spaces unchanged up to isomorphism.

2
Integration theory for charges

As some readers may be unfamiliar with integration with respect to a charge, this section reviews the relevant theory based on the concise summary given in
[
2
]
and the more comprehensive treatment in
[
3
]
. Theorems and lemmas in this section that are stated without proof are proved in the references, but a few simple proofs are provided for results that are known but not explicitly proved in the literature. For simplicity, all charges and charge spaces mentioned in this paper are assumed to be non-negative and bounded, unless otherwise stated.

Definition 1

A charge
\mu
on a field of subsets
{\mathcal{A}}
of a set
X
can be extended to an outer charge on
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
as follows

\mu^{*}(A):=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{A}},A\subseteq B\}

for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X)
.

Outer charges are sub-additive, that is,
\mu^{*}(A\cup B)\leq\mu^{*}(A)+\mu^{*}(B)
for all
A,B\subseteq X
(Proposition 4.1.4 of
[
3
]
).

Note the outer charge depends on the field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}
. It is therefore sometimes useful to write
\mu^{*}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
to avoid ambiguity when working with nested fields. Nevertheless, the more concise notation
\mu^{*}
will be used when the field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}
is clear from the context.

Definition 2

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. A null set is any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X)
for which
\mu^{*}(A)=0
. A null function is a function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\mu^{*}(\{x\in X:|f(x)|>\epsilon\})=0

for all
\epsilon>0
.

The following notion of equivalence between functions plays an important role throughout this paper.

Definition 3

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. Two functions
f,g:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
are said to be equal almost everywhere (abbreviated as
f=g
a.e.) if
f-g
is a null function.

This terminology can be somewhat misleading. For example, consider the charge space
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
, where
{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the natural numbers (excluding zero),
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(X)
is the field of sets comprised of the finite subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
and their complements (cofinite sets), and
\nu
is the charge obtained by setting
\nu(A)=0
if
A\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is finite and
\nu(A)=1
if
A
is cofinite. The functions
f(n)=1/n
and
g(n)=0
are equal a.e., since
\{x\in{\mathbb{N}}:|f(x)-g(x)|>\epsilon\}
is finite, and therefore has zero charge for any
\epsilon
, even though
f(n)\neq g(n)
for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

A related definition is the following.

Definition 4

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. A function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be dominated almost everywhere by
g:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
(abbreviated as
f\leq g
a.e.) if
f\leq g+h
, where
h
is a null function.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\sigma
-field and
\mu
a measure, then
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a null function if and only if
\mu^{*}(\{x:f(x)\neq 0\})=0
(Proposition 4.2.7 of
[
3
]
). In that case, the use of the term “almost everywhere” in the preceding two definitions corresponds to the conventional sense of this expression in measure theory.

Whether a function
f
is a null function, and whether
f
is equal to or dominated almost everywhere by another function
g
, depends on the field
{\mathcal{A}}
via the outer charge
\mu^{*}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Thus where there is potential ambiguity regarding the field, it is helpful to say that
f
is a null function
with respect to

{\mathcal{A}}
, or that
f=g
a.e. or
f\leq g
a.e.
with respect to

{\mathcal{A}}
. Similar terminology should be used for all definitions that depend on an outer charge.

The following mode of convergence generalises convergence in probability and plays an important role in the definition of integrals over a charge space.

Definition 5

The mode of convergence
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
is read as “
f_{n}
converges to
f
hazily” and means that for every
\epsilon>0
,

\mu^{*}(\{x:|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|>\epsilon\})\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The limit of a sequence that converges hazily is not in general unique, but nevertheless the following result, adapted from Proposition 4.3.2 of
[
3
]
, holds.

Theorem 1

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
f
and
g
be real-valued functions on
X
. If a sequence of real-valued functions
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
on
X
converges hazily to
f
, and
f=g
a.e., then
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
g
hazily. Conversely, if
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to both
f
and
g
hazily, then
f=g
a.e.

For two fields of subsets
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
of a set
X
with
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
and a charge
\mu
defined on
{\mathcal{A}}
, ambiguity can arise as to whether hazy convergence is with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
or
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. In what follows, the notation
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f
is used wherever there is potential ambiguity regarding the field of sets with respect to which hazy convergence occurs.

The following lemma confirms an expected property of dominating functions: that the limit of a dominated sequence is also dominated.

Lemma 2

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space, and consider a sequence of real-valued functions
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
on
X
and real-valued functions
f
and
g
on
X
such that
f_{k}\xrightarrow{h}f
and
f_{k}\leq g
a.e. for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
f\leq g
a.e.

Proof 1

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a null function
h_{k}
such that
f_{k}\leq g+h_{k}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
h_{k}\geq 0
(noting
f_{k}\leq g+h_{k}^{+}
, where
h_{k}^{+}:=\max\{0,h_{k}\}
). Then

f\leq f_{k}+|f_{k}-f|\leq g+h_{k}+|f_{k}-f|,

and thus
f\leq g+h
, where
h:=\inf_{k}\left(h_{k}+|f_{k}-f|\right)\geq 0
. Now for any
x\in X
and
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle h(x)>\epsilon

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{k}(x)+|f_{k}-f|>\epsilon,\mbox{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{k}(x)>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\mbox{ or }|f_{k}-f|>\frac{\epsilon}{2},\mbox{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Hence for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\mu^{*}(\{x:h(x)>\epsilon\})\leq\mu^{*}(\{x:h_{k}(x)>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\})+\mu^{*}(\{x:|f_{k}-f|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\}).

The first term on the right hand side is zero, and the second goes to zero as
k\rightarrow\infty
, implying
\mu^{*}(\{x:h(x)>\epsilon\})=0
for any
\epsilon>0
. That is,
h
is a null function.
∎

As in standard Lebesgue integration, the integral is defined with reference to simple functions.

Definition 6

A simple function on a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
of the form
\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I_{A_{k}}
, for
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
, real numbers
c_{1},\ldots,c_{K}
and a partition of
X
into subsets
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
. (For unbounded charges, simple functions must also satisfy
\mu(A_{k})<\infty
whenever
c_{k}\neq 0
.)

Simple functions are used to define a notion of measurability of functions on a charge space.

Definition 7

A function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
T_{1}
-measurable on a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
if there is a sequence of simple functions
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
. It is said to be
T_{2}
-measurable if, given any
\epsilon>0
, there is a partition of
X
into a finite number of sets
\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(A_{0})<\epsilon
and
|f(x)-f(y)|<\epsilon
for every
x,y\in A_{k}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

These two definitions of measurability are equivalent (Theorem 4.4.7 of
[
3
]
).

Definition 8

The set of functions of the form
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
that are
T_{1}
-measurable with respect to a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is denoted
L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

A pseudo-metric can be defined for
L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
as follows:

d(f,g):=\inf\{\epsilon>0:\mu^{*}(\{x:|f(x)-g(x)|>\epsilon\})<\epsilon\}.

for all
f,g\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

It is sometimes helpful to write
d_{{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
to clarify the field of sets used to define the outer charge
\mu^{*}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
. It is immediate that
d_{{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)=0
if and only if
f=g
a.e. with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
, and that a sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
converges to
f\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
in the pseudo-metric
d_{{\mathcal{A}}}
if and only if
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f
.

Define
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
to be the collection of equivalence classes of
L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
under the equivalence relation
f\sim g\iff d(f,g)=0
. Then the function
d([f],[g]):=d(f,g)
is a a metric for
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, where
[f]
and
[g]
are the equivalence classes of
f
and
g
respectively.

Any
T_{1}
-measurable function has the following property.

Definition 9

A function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be smooth if, given any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
k>0
such that

\mu^{*}(\{x\in X:|f(x)|>k\})<\epsilon.

Although
T_{1}
-measurability implies smoothness (Corollary 4.4.8 of
[
3
]
), the converse is not true in general.

Other key properties of
T_{1}
-measurable functions include the following, adapted from Corollary 4.4.9 of
[
3
]
.

Theorem 3

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
f
and
g
be real-valued functions on
X
. Let
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function. Then

1.

If
f
and
g
are
T_{1}
-measurable and
c,d\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then
cf+dg
and
fg
are
T_{1}
-measurable.

2.

If
f
is
T_{1}
-measurable, then
\psi(f)
is
T_{1}
-measurable.

3.

If
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of
T_{1}
-measurable functions converging to
f

f
is
T_{1}
-measurable and
\{\psi(f_{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges to
\psi(f)
hazily.

Definition 10

Any simple function is integrable with integral

\int\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I_{A_{k}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\mu(A_{k}).

A more general function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be integrable if there is a sequence of simple functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that:

1.

f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
, and

2.

\int|f_{n}-f_{m}|d\mu\rightarrow 0
as
n,m\rightarrow\infty
.

Such a sequence is said to be a determining sequence for
f
. The integral is then given by

\int fd\mu:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f_{n}d\mu.

This integral is well defined, that is the limit has the same value for any determining sequence (Theorem 4.4.10 in
[
3
]
).

For two fields of sets
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
with
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
and a charge
\mu
defined on
{\mathcal{A}}
, ambiguity can arise as to whether a function is integrable with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
or
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. Where necessary to distinguish the two integrals, they are denoted
\int fd\mu_{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\int fd\mu_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
respectively. Where the relevant field of sets is clear from the context, the subscript can be omitted.

The following theorem, adapted from Theorem 4.4.13 of
[
3
]
, enumerates some key properties of integrable functions.

Theorem 4

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
f
and
g
be real-valued functions on
X
. Then

1.

If
f
and
g
are integrable and
c
and
d
are real numbers, then
cf+dg
is integrable and
\int(cf+dg)d\mu=c\int fd\mu+d\int gd\mu
.

2.

f
is integrable if and only if
f^{+}:=\max\{f,0\}
and
f^{-}:=\max\{-f,0\}

3.

If
f
and
g
are integrable and

\int fd\mu\leq\int gd\mu
.

4.

If
f
is integrable and
g=f

g
is integrable and
\int gd\mu=\int fd\mu
.

Another important property of integrals with respect to charges is given by Theorem 4.4.18 of
[
3
]

Theorem 5

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
f
and
g
be real-valued functions on

T_{1}

Definition 11

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, the function space
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is the set of all
T_{1}
-measurable functions

The following theorem paraphrases Theorem 4.6.7 of
[

Theorem 6

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and

One can construct a normed vector space from

as follows.

The following simple lemma is frequently useful.

Lemma 8

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and

. Then the following three statements are logically equivalent.

f\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)

2.

(f^{+})^{p},(f^{-})^{p}\in L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

3.

(1\implies 2)

(2\implies 3)

(3\implies 1)
This follows by Theorem

The additional statements follow by Theorem

.

(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)

\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

f_{k}\xrightarrow{h}f
if and only if
f\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
and
\|f_{k}-f\|_{p}\rightarrow 0
.

The simple functions in
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
have the following property, adapted from Theorem 4.6.15 of
[
3
]
.

Theorem 12

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. The simple functions on
X
with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
are dense in
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
for every
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
.

This is proved for the case
p\in[1,\infty)
in
[
3
]
. The case
p=0
follows from the fact that a sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges hazily to
f
if and only if
d(f_{n},f)\rightarrow 0
(see Section 1.1 of
[
2
]
).

An important tool in the analysis of
L^{p}
spaces with respect to a charge is the Peano-Jordan completion (
[
2
,
4
,
5
]
), defined as follows.

Definition 13

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. The Peano-Jordan completion of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the charge space
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
where

\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{A\subseteq X:\forall\epsilon>0,\exists B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\mbox{ such that }B\subseteq A\subseteq C\mbox{ and }\mu(C\setminus B)<\epsilon\}

and

\overline{\mu}(A):=\sup\{\mu(B):B\subseteq A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}\}=\inf\{\mu(C):A\subseteq C,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\}.

A charge space is said to be Peano-Jordan complete if it is equal to its Peano-Jordan completion.

It is straightforward to show
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is a field and
\overline{\mu}
is a charge. One can also show Peano-Jordan completion is an idempotent operation, that is
\overline{\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}}=\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\overline{\overline{\mu}}=\overline{\mu}
. Two more useful properties are that
\mu_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{*}(A)=\overline{\mu}(A)
for
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, and that
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
and
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
have the same null sets.

If
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
and
(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
are charge spaces with
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, and
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}})
and
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}},\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}})
are their respective Peano-Jordan completions, then it is straightforward to show
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(A)=\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. Thus the subscripts can safely be omitted from
\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
without ambiguity. However, it is useful to retain the subscripts on the inverse set functions
\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{-1}
and
\overline{\mu}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}^{-1}
, since these are not in general equal. Note also that
\mu^{*}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(A)=\mu^{*}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A)=\overline{\mu}(A)
for
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, and the subscripts may therefore be omitted without ambiguity in this case as well.

The following lemma establishes that Peano-Jordan completeness generalises the concept of completeness for measure spaces.

Lemma 13

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a measure space. Then it is Peano-Jordan complete if and only if it is a complete measure space.

Proof 3

(\implies)
Let
N\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(N)=0
, and consider
A\subset N
. Then
\emptyset\subseteq A\subseteq N
and
\mu(N\setminus\emptyset)=0
, implying
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}={\mathcal{A}}
.

(\impliedby)
Consider
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Then there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
B\subseteq A\subseteq C
and
\mu(C\setminus B)=0
. (To see this, use the definition of
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
to construct
B_{n},C_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
B_{n}\subseteq A\subseteq C_{n}
and
\mu(C_{n}\setminus B_{n})<1/n
, then set
B:=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}B_{n}
and
C:=\cap_{i=1}^{\infty}C_{n}
, noting
{\mathcal{A}}
is closed under countable unions and intersections.) Thus
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, since the measure space is complete.
∎

Peano-Jordan completion of a charge space leaves its
L_{p}
spaces unchanged, as the following theorem, adapted from Proposition 1.8 of
[
2
]
, states.

Theorem 14

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
be its Peano-Jordan completion. Then

1.

For
A\subseteq X
,
\mu^{*}(A)=\overline{\mu}^{*}(A)
.

2.

A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
if and only if
I_{A}\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

3.

L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)=L_{0}(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
.

4.

L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)=L_{p}(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
.

5.

For
f\in L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
,
\int fd\mu=\int fd\overline{\mu}
.

The above theorem has the following consequence.

Corollary 15

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. Then
f\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
if and only if
fI_{A}\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
for all
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Proof 4

Suppose
f\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
and
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
. By Theorem
14
,
I_{A}
is
T_{1}
-measurable, and by Theorem
3
,
fI_{A}
is
T_{1}
-measurable. This is all that is required for the case
p=0
. For
p\in[1,\infty)
, Theorem
5
gives
|fI_{A}|^{p}\in L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, that is,
fI_{A}\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

The converse is trivial, since
X\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
∎

One final theorem, useful for showing that an
L_{p}
space is complete, is the following, adapted from Theorem 3.4 of
[
2
]
.

Theorem 16

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete.

2.

L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete.

3.

If
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\ldots
are sets in
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\sup_{k\geq 1}\mu(A_{k})<\infty
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
D\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(A_{k}\setminus D)=0
for all

and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(A_{k})\leq\mu(D)\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(A_{k})+\epsilon
.

3
T_{1}
-measurability, integrability, and equality almost everywhere

This section presents a contribution to the general theory of bounded charges. New characterisations of
T_{1}
-measurability, integrability, and equality almost everywhere of functions on a charge space are deduced. Among other corollaries, it follows that
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
spaces defined as in Section
2
are equivalent to conventional Lebesgue function spaces when
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a complete measure space (Corollaries
21
and
22
). The latter result is known, or at least generally assumed, but proof seems to be absent from the literature.

The following two lemmas are fundamental to the results in this section.

Lemma 17

Consider
f\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, where
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space. Then for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
,
f^{-1}(-\infty,y]\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
f^{-1}[y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Moreover,
f^{-1}(y)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
with
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y))=0
.

Proof 5

Suppose
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the condition of the lemma, fix
\epsilon>0
and select
\delta>0
and
D\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta]\subseteq D
with
\mu(D)<\epsilon/2
. Since
f
is
T_{2}
-measurable, there exists a partition of
X
into sets
\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(A_{0})<\epsilon/2
and
|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|<2\delta
for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{k}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, exactly one of the following is true:

1.

A_{k}\subseteq f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta]
,

2.

A_{k}\cap f^{-1}(-\infty,y-\delta)\neq\emptyset
, or

3.

A_{k}\cap f^{-1}(y+\delta,\infty)\neq\emptyset
.

Define

B:=\bigcup\{A_{k}:k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},A_{k}\cap f^{-1}(-\infty,y-\delta)\neq\emptyset\}\setminus D

and

C^{c}:=\bigcup\{A_{k}:k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},A_{k}\cap f^{-1}(y+\delta,\infty)\neq\emptyset\}\setminus D.

Then
B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
B\subseteq f^{-1}(-\infty,y]\subseteq C
and
C\setminus B=(B\cup C^{c})^{c}\subseteq A_{0}\cup D
. Hence
\mu(C\setminus B)\leq\mu(A_{0})+\mu(D)<\epsilon
, and
f^{-1}(-\infty,y]\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
. The proof that
f^{-1}[y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is similar.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and choose
\delta>0
such that
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])<\epsilon
. Then
f^{-1}(y)=f^{-1}(-\infty,y]\cap f^{-1}[y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
with
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y))\leq\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])<\epsilon
. Let
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
to obtain
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y))=0
.
∎

Note the proof of Lemma
17
does not require
\mu
to be bounded.

Lemma 18

Consider
f\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, where
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space. There are at most countably many
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])>0
.

Proof 6

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, suppose there exist distinct
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta,y_{i}+\delta])>\frac{\mu(X)}{n}

for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Choose
\delta>0
with
\delta<\min\{|y_{i}-y_{j}|:i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}

and note
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/4,y_{i}+\delta/4])>\mu(X)/n
for each
i
. Then choose
\epsilon>0
such that
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/4,y_{i}+\delta/4])>\mu(X)/n+\epsilon
for each
i
.

Since
f
is
T_{2}
-measurable, there exists a partition
\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
of
X
such that
\mu(A_{0})<\epsilon
and
|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|<\delta/4
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{j}
and all
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define

B_{i}:=\bigcup\{A_{j}:k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},A_{j}\cap f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/4,y_{i}+\delta/4]\neq\emptyset\}.

Then
B_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/4,y_{i}+\delta/4]\subseteq B_{i}\cup A_{0}
, and hence

\mu(B_{i})\geq\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/4,y_{i}+\delta/4])-\mu(A_{0})>\mu(X)/n.

Moreover,
B_{i}\subseteq f^{-1}[y_{i}-\delta/2,y_{i}+\delta/2]
for each
i
, implying
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint sets in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and thus
\mu(\cup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(B_{i})>\mu(X)
, a contradiction. It follows there can be at most finitely many (fewer than
n
) elements
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])>\mu(X)/n
, and hence at most countably many
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])>0
.
∎

Putting the above two lemmas together gives that
T_{1}
-measurable functions produce chains of inverse images in
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, after excluding a countable set. As the following lemma states, that countable set may be chosen to correspond precisely with the sets at which
\overline{\mu}
is in a certain sense discontinuous on the chain.

Lemma 19

Consider
f\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, where
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space. There exists a countable set
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}
such that

1.

f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
, and

2.

y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
if and only if

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\supset A\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\subset A\}

where
{\mathcal{T}}:=\{f^{-1}(y,\infty):y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C\}
.

Proof 7

By Lemma
18
, the set

C:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}:\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])>0\}

is countable. By Lemma
17
,
f^{-1}(y,\infty)=(f^{-1}(-\infty,y])^{c}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
.

Suppose
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
and fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])<\epsilon
, and
y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
such that
y<y^{\prime}<y+\delta
. It follows that

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))-\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y^{\prime},\infty))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y,\infty)\setminus f^{-1}(y^{\prime},\infty))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,y^{\prime}])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\epsilon.

But then

\displaystyle\sup\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\supset A\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y^{\prime},\infty))+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\supset A\}+\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives

\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))=\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))=\sup\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\supset A\}.

The proof that
\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))=\inf\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\subset A\}
is similar.

Conversely, suppose

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\supset A\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\overline{\mu}(A):A\in{\mathcal{T}},f^{-1}(y,\infty)\subset A\}.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
C
is countable, one may choose
\delta>0
so that
y-\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
and
y+\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
with
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y-\delta,\infty))<\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))+\epsilon/2
and
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y+\delta,\infty))>\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))-\epsilon/2
. Note
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,\infty))=\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y-\delta,\infty))
, since
\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y-\delta))=0
by Lemma
17
. Hence

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,\infty))-\overline{\mu}(f^{-1}(y+\delta,\infty))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle[\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))+\epsilon/2]-[\mu^{*}(f^{-1}(y,\infty))-\epsilon/2]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon.

Hence
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mu^{*}(f^{-1}[y-\delta,y+\delta])=0
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
.
∎

By similar reasoning, the preceding lemma also holds if one replaces
f^{-1}(y,\infty)
with
f^{-1}(-\infty,y)
,
f^{-1}[y,\infty)
, or
f^{-1}(-\infty,y]
.

The condition on chains of inverse images identified in Lemma
19
, together with smoothness, turns out to characterise
T_{1}
-measurable functions.

Theorem 20

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space and let
f
be a real-valued function on
X
. Then the following three statements are logically equivalent.

1.

f\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

2.

f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the following:

(a)

there exists a countable set
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
, and

(b)

f
is smooth.

3.

There exist sequences of simple functions (with respect to
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
)
\{f^{+}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{f^{-}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

f^{+}_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}-1}y_{n,j}I_{(f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n,j},y_{n,j+1}]}+y_{n}I_{(f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n},\infty)}

and

f^{-}_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}-1}y_{n,j}I_{(f^{-})^{-1}(y_{n,j},y_{n,j+1}]}+y_{n}I_{(f^{-})^{-1}(y_{n},\infty)}

where

(a)

K_{n}:=n2^{n}
,

(b)

y_{n,j}\in((j-1)2^{-n},j2^{-n}]
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,K_{n}\}
, with
y_{n}:=y_{n,K_{n}}
,

(c)

(f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n,j},\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
(f^{-})^{-1}(y_{n,j},\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,K_{n}\}
,

(d)

\{y_{n,j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{n}}\subset\{y_{n+1,j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{n+1}}
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(e)

f^{+}_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f^{+}
and
f^{-}_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f^{-}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof 8

(1\implies 2)
Property 2a is immediate from Lemma
19
, and any
T_{1}
-measurable function is smooth.

(2\implies 3)
Let
D:=\{|y|:y\in C\}
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,K_{n}\}
, inductively choose
y_{n,j}
to be any element of
((j-1)2^{-n},j2^{-n}]\setminus D
, unless there is already an element of that set in
\{y_{n-1,j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{n-1}}
, in which case set
y_{n,j}
equal to that element. Properties 3a to 3d are immediate consequences of this construction, and then
\{f^{+}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{f^{-}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are simple functions with respect to
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

For any
x\in(f^{+})^{-1}[0,y_{n}]
,
|f^{+}_{n}(x)-f^{+}(x)|<2^{1-n}
. Also,
f^{+}
is smooth since
f^{+}\leq f
. Thus for any
\epsilon>0
and
n>1-\log_{2}\epsilon
,

\mu^{*}(\{x\in X:|f^{+}_{n}(x)-f^{+}(x)|>\epsilon\})\leq\mu^{*}((f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n},\infty))\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Hence
f^{+}_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f^{+}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and similarly
f^{-}_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f^{-}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(3\implies 1)
Property 3c implies
\{f^{+}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of simple functions with respect to
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, and by 3e and Theorem
14
,
f^{+}_{n}\xrightarrow{h,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}}f^{+}
. Hence
f^{+}\in L_{0}(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})=L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, and similarly
f^{-}\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
. By Theorem
3
,
f=f^{+}-f^{-}\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.
∎

Note that
f^{+}_{n}
and
f^{-}_{n}
can be alternatively defined as

f^{+}_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}-1}y_{n,j}I_{(f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n,j},y_{n,j+1}]}

and

f^{-}_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}-1}y_{n,j}I_{(f^{-})^{-1}(y_{n,j},y_{n,j+1}]}

and the proof goes through unchanged. In fact, it doesn’t matter what constant values are assigned to
(f^{+})^{-1}(y_{n},\infty)
and
(f^{-})^{-1}(y_{n},\infty)
. The alternative definitions just provided are used only in the proof of part of Theorem
25
, whereas the definitions given in Statement 3 above are used in other proofs.

The above theorem determines the following conditions under which
T_{1}
-measurability is equivalent to conventional measurability, and integrability with respect to a charge is equivalent to conventional Lebesgue integrability.

Corollary 21

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a complete measure space. Then a function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is
T_{1}
-measurable if and only if it is measurable in the conventional sense.

Proof 9

(\implies)
By Lemma
13
,
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}={\mathcal{A}}
. By Theorem
20
,
f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in{\mathcal{A}}
for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C
. But then
f^{-1}[y,\infty)\in{\mathcal{A}}
for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, using the closure of
{\mathcal{A}}
under countable intersections. Hence
f
is measurable.

(\impliedby)
Any measurable function immediately satisfies Statement 2a of Theorem
20
with
C=\emptyset
. Moreover, any measurable function is smooth by Proposition 4.2.17 of
[
3
]
.
∎

Corollary 22

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a complete measure space. Then a function
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is integrable (in the sense of Definition
10
) if and only if it is Lebesgue integrable. Moreover, the two types of integral are equal.

Proof 10

It is sufficient to prove the corollary for non-negative functions, since then it will apply to
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
, and by Theorem
4
, to
f
. Hence assume
f\geq 0
.

Recall that for a complete measure space,
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}={\mathcal{A}}
by Lemma
13
, so simple functions with respect to
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
are also simple with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover, all simple functions are trivially integrable in both senses, with the two types of integral equal. Also note
f
is both
T_{1}
-measurable and measurable in the conventional sense if it is integrable in either sense, by Corollary
21
.

(\implies)
By Theorem
4
, any simple function
s

Proof 11

4
Complete
L_{p}
spaces

It is not in general true that the metric space
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete, where
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space and
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. See
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
and especially
[
2
]
for helpful discussions of complete
L_{p}
spaces. However, the wider literature on completeness of
L_{p}
spaces should be approached with caution, as it contains a number of errors. For example, Proposition 5.2 in a paper by Armstrong
[
7
]
claims the following are logically equivalent for a bounded charge space: (i)
L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete, (ii)
\mu
induces a countably additive set function on the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
, where
{\mathcal{M}}:=\mu^{-1}(0)
is the
kernel
of
\mu
, and (iii)
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is a
\sigma
-field. However, as Basile and Bhaskara Rao
[
2
]
show, the only true implication in this proposition is (i)
\implies
(ii); counter-examples are available for each of the others. This error has been propagated across multiple papers. Another characterisation of completeness, due to Green
[
8
]
, was shown to be false in
[
9
]
.

This section contains a new characterisation of completeness for
L_{p}
spaces constructed on a bounded, non-negative charge space (Theorem
29
), in terms of a representation of
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
on a field of sets. In addition, a sufficient condition for completeness is proved, correcting the false condition (ii)
\implies
(i) of Armstrong just mentioned. The correction involves the auxiliary condition that
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
be a countably complete Boolean algebra.

Since this section makes frequent reference to Boolean algebras and their representations, the following brief discussion of relevant terminology and notation, with particular reference to charge spaces, may be helpful.

Any field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
on an arbitrary set
X
forms a Boolean algebra with pairwise intersection
\cap
as the
meet
operator
\wedge
, pairwise union
\cup
as the
join
operator
\vee
, set complement
c
as the Boolean complement operator
′
, the empty set
\emptyset
as the zero and
X
as the unit. Note also that the symmetric set difference
p\triangle q
is an exclusive disjunction
p+q:=(p\wedge q^{\prime})\vee(p^{\prime}\wedge q)
and the subset relation
p\subseteq q
is a Boolean partial order
p\leq q
.

Stone’s representation theorem (originally proved in
[
10
]
, but
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
contain helpful expositions) is a fundamental result asserting that every abstract Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is isomorphic to a field of sets. More specifically,
{\mathcal{A}}
can be embedded in
{\mathcal{P}}(S_{{\mathcal{A}}})
, where
S_{{\mathcal{A}}}
is a set called the
Stone space
of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Stone spaces can be constructed in various equivalent ways, but the details are not needed here. It will be sufficient throughout what follows to work with an unspecified
representation
of
{\mathcal{A}}
, that is, a Boolean isomorphism
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
embedding
{\mathcal{A}}
into the power set of some set
Y
.

For any charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, the set
{\mathcal{M}}:=\mu^{-1}(0):=\{A\in{\mathcal{A}}:\mu(A)=0\}
, called the
kernel
of
\mu
, is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and the collection of
null sets

{\mathcal{N}}:=\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\mu^{*}(A)=0\}
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}
is a Boolean algebra and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Let
[A]\in{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
denote the equivalence class of
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
under the equivalence relation
A\sim B\iff A+B\in{\mathcal{M}}
. If the quotient space
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
is potentially ambiguous, the notation
[A]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}}
will be used to clarify.

Suppose
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
is an embedding of the Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
in the power set of some set
Y
(which may or may not be the Stone space
S_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}}
). Define
\langle A\rangle:=\phi([A])
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
. If the embedding
\phi
is potentially ambiguous, it will be indicated by a subscript as
\langle A\rangle_{\phi}
.

For any
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
(not necessarily a sub-algebra), define
[{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}]:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\}
. Similarly, define
\langle{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\rangle:=\{\langle A\rangle:A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\}
.

If
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space and
{\mathcal{M}}
is the kernel of
\mu
, the induced function
\mu:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
given by
\mu[A]:=\mu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
is finitely additive. Similarly, if
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
is a representation of
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
, one may define a charge
\mu
on
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle_{\phi}
by
\mu\langle A\rangle_{\phi}:=\mu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, so that
(Y,\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle_{\phi},\mu)
is a charge space. Note that wherever possible in what follows, parentheses enclosing a function argument will be omitted when that argument is already enclosed in square or angled brackets; for example,
\mu[A]
and
\mu\langle A\rangle
will be preferred to
\mu([A])
and
\mu(\langle A\rangle)

A Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is said to be
countably complete
if every countable subset
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
has a least upper bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A
countably additive function

\mu
on a countably complete Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is a generalisation of a non-negative measure: it is a function
\mu:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
such that
\mu(0)=0
and
\mu(\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(p_{k})
for any pairwise disjoint sequence
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
. In this paper, it will be assumed that a countably additive function is bounded, that is,
\mu(1)<\infty
, unless otherwise stated.

First it will be useful to show that a representation of a countably complete Boolean algebra can be transformed into a measure space as follows.

Lemma 28

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a countably complete Boolean algebra and let
\mu:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a countably additive function. Suppose
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
is a representation of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
(Y,\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})},\overline{\mu})
is a complete measure space, where
\mu:\phi({\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is the charge defined by
\mu(\phi(A)):=\mu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof 16

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})}
be pairwise disjoint, and set
A:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
B_{k},C_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\phi(B_{k})\subseteq A_{k}\subseteq\phi(C_{k})
and
\mu(C_{k})-\mu(B_{k})<(\epsilon/2)2^{-k}
. Then
\{B_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise disjoint, and
\mu(\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(B_{k})
. Choose
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu(B_{k})>\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(B_{k})-\epsilon/2
, and set
B:=\vee_{k=1}^{K}B_{k}
and
C:=\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}C_{k}
. Then
\phi(B)\subseteq A\subseteq\phi(C)
, where the latter containment follows because
\phi(C)
is an upper bound for
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, but
A
is the least upper bound for
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
. Moreover,

\mu(C)-\mu(B)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mu(C_{k})-\mu(B_{k}))+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(B_{k})-\mu(B)\right)<\epsilon.

Hence
A\in\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})}
, and
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})}
is a monotone class and therefore also a
\sigma
-field (see
[
13
, Thm. A, p. 27]
). Also note

\overline{\mu}(A)\leq\mu(C)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(C_{k})<\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\mu}(A_{k})+\epsilon/2

and

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\mu}(A_{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\overline{\mu}(\cup_{j=1}^{k}A_{k})\leq\overline{\mu}(A),

implying
\overline{\mu}
is countably additive on
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})}
. Thus
(Y,\overline{\phi({\mathcal{A}})},\overline{\mu})
is a measure space. It is a complete measure space by Lemma
13
.
∎

The preceding lemma provides a sufficient condition for an
L_{p}
space to be complete, described in the following theorem, which also identifies a condition that is both necessary and sufficient.

Theorem 29

Consider a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, and let
{\mathcal{M}}:=\mu^{-1}(0)
. Suppose
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
is a representation of
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete if and only if
(Y,\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle_{\phi}},\overline{\mu})
is a measure space, where
\mu:\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle_{\phi}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is the charge defined by
\mu\langle A\rangle_{\phi}:=\mu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

2.

If
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
is a countably complete Boolean algebra and the function
\mu:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, defined by
\mu[A]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}}:=\mu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, is a countably additive function, then
L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete.

Proof 17

First consider the claimed necessary and sufficient condition.

(\implies)
Since
\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
is a field, it is a
\sigma
-field if it is closed under countable disjoint unions
[
13
, Thm. A, p. 27]
. Consider pairwise disjoint
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
, and fix
\epsilon>0
. For each
k
, there exist
B_{k},C_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\langle B_{k}\rangle\subseteq A_{k}\subseteq\langle C_{k}\rangle
and
\mu(C_{k})-\mu(B_{k})<2^{-k}\epsilon/3
. Note that the increasing sequence
\{\cup_{j=1}^{k}B_{j}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded above by
\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}B_{j}
, hence one may choose
K
so that
\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{K}B_{j})>\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}B_{j})-\epsilon/3
, and set
B:=\cup_{j=1}^{K}B_{j}\in{\mathcal{A}}
. By Theorem
16
, there exists
C\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j}\setminus C)=0
for each
k
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j})\leq\mu(C)\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{k})+\epsilon/3
. It follows that
C_{k}\setminus C\in{\mathcal{M}}
and thus
C_{k}=(C_{k}\cap C)\cup(C_{k}\setminus C)\in[C_{k}\cap C]
, giving
\langle C_{k}\rangle=\langle C_{k}\cap C\rangle\subseteq\langle C\rangle
for all
k
. Putting all this together gives
\langle B\rangle\subseteq\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\subseteq\langle C\rangle
and

\displaystyle\mu(C)-\mu(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(C)-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j})

\displaystyle+\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j})-\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}B_{j})\right)

\displaystyle+\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}B_{j})-\mu(B)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\epsilon.

Hence
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
. Moreover,
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(\cup_{j=1}^{k}C_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(C_{k})
, giving

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu\langle C\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu\langle C_{k}\rangle+\epsilon/3

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\overline{\mu}(A_{k})+2^{-k}\epsilon/3\bigg)+\epsilon/3

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\mu}(A_{k})+2\epsilon/3.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives that
\overline{\mu}
is countably additive on
\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
.

(\impliedby)
Consider an increasing sequence
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\ldots
in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Since
\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
is a
\sigma
-field,
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A_{k}\rangle\in\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
, implying that for any
\epsilon>0
there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
\langle B\rangle\subseteq\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A_{k}\rangle\subseteq\langle C\rangle
and
\mu(C)-\mu(B)<\epsilon
. For each
k
,
\mu(A_{k}\setminus C)=\mu(\langle A_{k}\rangle\setminus\langle C\rangle)=0
since
\langle A_{k}\rangle\subseteq\langle C\rangle
. Since
\overline{\mu}
is countably additive on
\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle}
,
\overline{\mu}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A_{k}\rangle)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu(A_{k})
. Combining this with

\overline{\mu}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A_{k}\rangle)\leq\mu\langle C\rangle<\overline{\mu}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A_{k}\rangle)+\epsilon

gives the conditions of Theorem
16
, hence
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete.

For the second condition, note that if
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
is countably complete and
\mu
is countably additive on
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
, then
(Y,\overline{\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle},\overline{\mu})
is a measure space by Lemma
28
, implying
L_{1}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete.
∎

Thus one strategy for constructing a complete
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
space is to ensure
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
is countably complete and the induced function
\mu
is countably additive on
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}
.

5
L_{p}
spaces over complete charge spaces

Complete measure spaces play an important role in measure theory. This section generalises the concept for charge spaces, and characterises those charge spaces
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
for which completion leaves the corresponding
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
spaces unchanged.

Definition 14

A charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete if it contains its null sets, that is, if
{\mathcal{N}}:=\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\mu^{*}(A)=0\}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
.

If
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a measure space, then the above definition is equivalent to the standard definition of a complete measure space, since in that case if
A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X)
with
\mu^{*}(A)=0
, then
A\subseteq B
for some
B\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
\mu(B)=0
. Moreover, for measure spaces, completeness is equivalent to Peano-Jordan completeness by Lemma
13
. However, in general, completeness in the above sense is a weaker condition than Peano-Jordan completeness: it is straightforward to show
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is complete if it is Peano-Jordan complete, but the converse does not hold in general. A counter-example to the converse is discussed below, after the proof of Lemma
31
.

The following notation and lemma will be useful in this section.

Definition 15

For any algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, let
\alpha({\mathcal{C}})
denote the subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
generated by
{\mathcal{C}}
, that is, the smallest subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
containing
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Lemma 30

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are Boolean algebras with
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(B\wedge M^{\prime})\vee N:B\in{\mathcal{B}},M,N\in{\mathcal{M}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{A\in{\mathcal{A}}:A+B\in{\mathcal{M}}\mbox{ for some }B\in{\mathcal{B}}\}.

Proof 18

The collection
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{(B\wedge M^{\prime})\vee N:B\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},M,N\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
is a subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
. To see this, note:

1.

X=(X\wedge 0^{\prime})\vee 0\in{\mathcal{C}}
,

2.

((B_{1}\wedge M^{\prime})\vee N)^{\prime}=(B_{1}^{\prime}\wedge N^{\prime})\vee(M\wedge N^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and

3.

((B_{1}\wedge M_{1}^{\prime})\vee N_{1})\vee((B_{2}\wedge M_{2}^{\prime})\vee N_{2})=(B_{3}\wedge M_{3}^{\prime})\vee N_{3}\in{\mathcal{C}}
where
B_{3}:=B_{1}\vee B_{2}
,

M_{3}:=(M_{1}\wedge M_{2})\vee(M_{1}\wedge B_{2}^{\prime})\vee(M_{2}\wedge B_{1}^{\prime})

and
N_{3}:=N_{1}\vee N_{2}
,

for any
B_{1},B_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
M_{1},N_{1},M_{2},N_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Thus
{\mathcal{C}}
is a Boolean algebra containing
{\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}}
, hence
\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}})\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
. But also
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}})
, hence
{\mathcal{C}}=\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}})
.

Consider
A\in\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{M}})
. Then there is
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
M,N\in{\mathcal{M}}
such that
A:=(B\wedge M^{\prime})\vee N
. But then
A+B\leq M\vee N\in{\mathcal{M}}
, implying
A+B\in{\mathcal{M}}
. On the other hand, if
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
A+B\in{\mathcal{M}}
for some
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
, then
A=(B\wedge M^{\prime})\vee N
where
M:=B\wedge A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
N:=A\wedge B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}
.
∎

Any charge space can be completed by adding null sets, in the following manner.

Lemma 31

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(X)
. Then
\mu
has a unique extension to a charge on
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Moreover,
(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
is a complete charge space with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proof 19

Since
{\mathcal{N}}
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, Lemma
30
gives

\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})=\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}\mbox{ for some }B\in{\mathcal{A}}\}.

Thus for any
A\in\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, there is
B\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. One may define
\mu(A):=\mu(B)
. This extension of
\mu
is well defined, since if there is another element
C\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
A\triangle C\in{\mathcal{N}}
, then
B\triangle C\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and
\mu(B)=\mu(C)
. It is then straightforward to show
(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
is a charge space.

Suppose there is another charge
\mu^{\prime}
on
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
that agrees with
\mu
on
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then for
A\in\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, there is
B\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Hence
\mu^{\prime}(A)=\mu(B)=\mu(A)
, implying the extension is unique.

To see that
(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
is complete, consider any null set
N
of this charge space and fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists
A\in\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
with
N\subseteq A
and
\mu(A)<\epsilon/2
. Moreover, there exists
B\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. But then there exists
C\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
A\triangle B\subseteq C
and
\mu(C)<\epsilon/2
. It follows that
N\subseteq B\cup C\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
\mu(B\cup C)\leq\mu(B)+\mu(C)<\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
N\in{\mathcal{N}}\subset\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, implying
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
is complete, with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
.
∎

The following example demonstrates that a charge space can be complete and yet not Peano-Jordan complete. Consider the charge space
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
, where
{\mathbb{N}}
is the natural numbers excluding 0 and
{\mathcal{A}}
is the collection of periodic sets in
{\mathbb{N}}
. Here a set
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is said to be
periodic
if the binary sequence
(I_{A}(1),I_{A}(2),\ldots)
is periodic, where
I_{A}
is the indicator function for set
A
. The charge
\nu
is defined by

\nu(A):=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{A}(n)

for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
. One can show (proof omitted) that
\nu({\mathcal{A}})
is the set of rational numbers in
[0,1]
, whereas
\nu(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})=[0,1]
, so
{\mathcal{A}}
is not Peano-Jordan complete. Nor is
{\mathcal{A}}
complete, since the set
{\mathcal{N}}
of null sets includes the finite sequences, which are not periodic. However, the charge space
({\mathbb{N}},\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\nu)
described in Lemma
31
contains its null sets and is therefore complete, whereas Lemma
30
implies
\nu(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
is still the set of rational numbers in
[0,1]
, implying
({\mathbb{N}},\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\nu)
is not Peano-Jordan complete.

The rest of this section characterises those charge spaces
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
for which completion does not add any new equivalence classes to
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
spaces, though it may add functions to those equivalence classes. To begin, Lemma
33
describes a construction that is here called a
null modification
, mapping chains (totally ordered sets) from a charge space into a subspace, by adding and removing null sets. The proof of Lemma
33
relies on the following real analysis lemma.

Lemma 32

Totally ordered sets have the following properties.

1.

Any
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
a\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
b,c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
b\leq a\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon
.

2.

Consider a totally ordered set
{\mathcal{T}}
and a strictly increasing function
\mu:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
B\leq A\leq C
and
\mu(C)-\mu(B)<\epsilon
.

Proof 20

For 1, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
be a countable, dense subset of
{\mathcal{T}}
. (Such a subset exists since any subset of the reals is separable. Standard proofs of this invoke the axiom of countable choice.) Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c-\epsilon/2,c)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c,c+\epsilon/2)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\}.

Then
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
are both countable, since there cannot be an uncountable number of pairwise disjoint intervals of non-zero width contained in
{\mathbb{R}}
(each must contain a distinct rational). Thus
{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{C}}_{0}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is a countable set with the claimed property.

Claim 2 then follows by applying Claim 1 to
\mu({\mathcal{T}})
, and noting
\mu
is one-to-one and order preserving, as a consequence of being strictly increasing.
∎

It will be convenient to introduce the following notation. Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}(A,B)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}:A\subset C\subset B\}

to represent
sub-intervals
of a chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
. Here
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}
but are not necessarily elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Lemma 33

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a Peano-Jordan complete charge space with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
, and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is a sub-field of
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Given a chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, there exists a map
\phi:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
such that:

1.

for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

2.

for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle[A]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{N}}}=[B]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{N}}}

\displaystyle\iff\phi(A)=\phi(B)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle[A]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{N}}}<[B]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{N}}}

\displaystyle\iff\phi(A)\subset\phi(B).

Proof 21

First note that since
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is Peano-Jordan complete, it contains its null sets and hence
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let
[{\mathcal{C}}]
be the countable subchain of
[{\mathcal{T}}]:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{T}}\}
obtained by applying Lemma
32
(2) to the totally ordered set
[{\mathcal{T}}]
and the strictly increasing function
\nu:[{\mathcal{T}}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
given by
\nu[A]:=\nu(A)
. Then let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
be obtained by selecting exactly one element of
{\mathcal{T}}
from each of the equivalence classes in
[{\mathcal{C}}]
. (This implicitly invokes the axiom of countable choice, in general.)

Arbitrarily order the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
as a sequence
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. (If
{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, the proof below still holds with minimal modifications.) For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
B_{k}
to be the largest set in the sequence
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}
that is a proper subset of
C_{k}
, if such a set exists (that is,
B_{k}:=\bigcup\{C_{j}:j<k,C_{j}\subset C_{k}\}
). Otherwise, set
B_{k}:=\emptyset
. Similarly, define
D_{k}
to be the smallest set in the sequence
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}
that properly contains
C_{k}
, if such a set exists (that is,
D_{k}:=\bigcap\{C_{j}:j<k,C_{j}\supset C_{k}\}
). Otherwise, set
D_{k}:=X
.

Set
\phi_{0}(C)=C
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and suppose inductively that
\phi_{k-1}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. This is trivially true for
k=1
, since
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. Sequentially define
\phi_{k}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

1.

Select
E_{k}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
such that
E_{k}\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

2.

Form the set
F_{k}:=\big(E_{k}\cup\phi_{k-1}(B_{k})\big)\cap\phi_{k-1}(D_{k})
.

3.

For each
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, define

\phi_{k}(C):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F_{k}&\mbox{ if }C=C_{k}\\
\phi_{k-1}(C)\cap F_{k}&\mbox{ if }C\in{\mathcal{C}}(B_{k},C_{k})\\
\phi_{k-1}(C)\cup F_{k}&\mbox{ if }C\in{\mathcal{C}}(C_{k},D_{k})\\
\phi_{k-1}(C)&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The set
E_{k}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
selected in Step 1 exists because, by assumption,
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, hence Lemma
30
applies. Notice that for each
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
\phi_{k}(C)
is formed from sets of
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
using finitely many basic set operations, hence
\phi_{k}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
.

It is straightforward to show (by induction) that
\phi_{k}
is an order preserving map for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, in the sense
B\subseteq C\implies\phi_{k}(B)\subseteq\phi_{k}(C)
for all
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and that
\phi_{j}(C_{k})=\phi_{k}(C_{k})
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\geq k
.

Define
\phi^{\prime}(C_{k}):=\phi_{k}(C_{k})
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
\phi^{\prime}
is order preserving, since if
C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}
,

\phi^{\prime}(C_{j})=\phi_{\max\{j,k\}}(C_{j})\subseteq\phi_{\max\{j,k\}}(C_{k})=\phi^{\prime}(C_{k}).

Thus
\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{C}})
is a chain.

Assume
\phi_{k-1}(C_{j})\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, noting this is trivially satisfied when
k=1
(because
\{1,\ldots,k-1\}=\emptyset
). Then

\phi_{k-1}(B_{k}),\phi_{k-1}(D_{k})\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, since for
k=1
,
\phi_{0}(B_{1})=\emptyset\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
and
\phi_{0}(D_{1})=X\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, and for
k>1
,

B_{k},D_{k}\in\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}\}
. It follows that
\phi_{k}(C_{k})=F_{k}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, since
E_{k}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
. Moreover,
\phi_{k}(C_{j})=\phi_{k-1}(C_{j})\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
. Thus, by induction,
\phi_{k}(C_{j})\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Thus
\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

For all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
\phi_{k}(C)\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C)\in{\mathcal{N}}
. To see this, note firstly that it is trivially true for
C\not\in{\mathcal{C}}(B_{k},D_{k})
. For
C=C_{k}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus\phi_{k}(C_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus F_{k}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus\big(E_{k}\cap\phi_{k-1}(D_{k})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus E_{k},

and

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(C_{k})\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{k}\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\big(E_{k}\cup\phi_{k-1}(B_{k})\big)\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{k}\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k}).

For
C\in{\mathcal{C}}(B_{k},C_{k})
,

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(C)\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C)\setminus F_{k}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus F_{k}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus E_{k},

and for
C\in{\mathcal{C}}(C_{k},D_{k})
,

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(C)\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{k}\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C)

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle F_{k}\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle E_{k}\setminus\phi_{k-1}(C_{k}).

Hence in all cases
\phi_{k}(C)\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C)\subseteq E_{k}\triangle\phi_{k-1}(C_{k})\in{\mathcal{N}}
. It follows that
\phi^{\prime}(C)
differs from
C
by the addition of at most
k
null sets and/or the removal of at most
k
null sets, hence
\phi^{\prime}(C)\triangle C\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
. That is, Property 1 holds on
{\mathcal{C}}
. This also implies
\mu(\phi^{\prime}(C))=\mu(C)
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

Define
\phi(A):=\bigcup\{\phi^{\prime}(C):C\in{\mathcal{C}},[C]\leq[A]\}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then
\phi(C)=\phi^{\prime}(C)
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover,
\phi(A)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
. To see this, note either
[A]=[C]
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, which implies
\phi(A)=\phi^{\prime}(C)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, or for any
\epsilon>0
there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
B\subset A\subset C
and
\mu(C)-\mu(B)<\epsilon
, which implies
\phi(B)\subseteq\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(C)
and
\mu(\phi(C))-\mu(\phi(B))<\epsilon
, and thus again
\phi(A)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

Note also that for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle[A]=[B]

\displaystyle\implies\phi(A)=\phi(B)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle[A]<[B]

\displaystyle\implies\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B).

Hence
\phi
is order preserving.

Property 1 can be extended from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\mathcal{T}}
as follows. Consider
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
. There exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
[B]\leq[A]\leq[C]
and
\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon/2
. If
[A]=[B]
then
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
\phi(A)=\phi^{\prime}(B)
. Moreover,
\phi^{\prime}(B)\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Hence
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Similarly, if
[A]=[C]
then
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Suppose
[B]<[A]<[C]
, implying
B\subset A\subset C
. Then
\phi^{\prime}(B)\subseteq\phi(A)\subseteq\phi^{\prime}(C)
, since
\phi
is order preserving, and
\nu(\phi^{\prime}(C))-\nu(\phi^{\prime}(B))<\epsilon/2
. It follows that

\phi(A)\triangle A\subseteq(\phi^{\prime}(B)\triangle B)\cup(\phi^{\prime}(C)\setminus\phi^{\prime}(B))\cup(C\setminus B),

and

\nu^{+}(\phi(A)\triangle A)\leq\nu(\phi(B)\triangle B)+\nu(\phi^{\prime}(C))-\nu(\phi^{\prime}(B))+\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Property 2 follows from Property 1 and the fact
\phi
is order preserving. Note

\phi(A)=\phi(B)\implies[\phi(A)]=[\phi(B)]\implies[A]=[B],

since
A\in[\phi(A)]
and
B\in[\phi(B)]
. This in turn gives

[A]<[B]\implies\phi(A)\neq\phi(B)\implies\phi(A)\subset\phi(B)

since it is already established that
[A]<[B]\implies\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\phi(A)\subset\phi(B)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\phi(A)\neq\phi(B)\mbox{ and }\phi(B)\not\subset\phi(A)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle[A]\neq[B]\mbox{ and }[B]\not<[A]

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle[A]<[B].

Lemma 34

The main reason for performing null modification operations on chains is that real-valued functions on a space
X
generate chains of inverse images, and thus null modification of those chains can be used to construct functions with desired properties. However, the missing ingredient is a way to generate a function from a chain. The following lemma identifies a class of chains that can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with positive real-valued functions.

Lemma 35

Consider a set
X
and a chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
of the form
{\mathcal{T}}:=\{A_{y}:y\in(0,\infty)\}
. Then the following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

There is a function
f:X\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
A_{y}=f^{-1}(y,\infty)
for each
y\in(0,\infty)
.

2.

{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

A_{y}=\bigcup\{A_{z}\in{\mathcal{T}}:z>y\}
for each
y\in(0,\infty)
, and

(b)

\bigcap{\mathcal{T}}=\emptyset
,

Proof 23

(\implies)
Statement 2a follows from the fact that for any
y\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle x\in A_{y}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)>y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)>z\mbox{ for some }z>y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle x\in A_{z}\mbox{ for some }z>y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle x\in\bigcup\{A_{z}\in{\mathcal{T}}:z>y\}

Statement 2b holds because for any
x\in X
, there exists
y>f(x)
, hence
x\notin A_{y}
.

(\impliedby)
Define

f(x):=\inf\{z\in(0,\infty):x\notin A_{z}\},

noting the set on the right of this expression is not empty, since
\bigcap{\mathcal{T}}=\emptyset
(that is, there is no
x\in X
with
x\in A_{y}
for all
y\in(0,\infty)
). Then

\displaystyle x\in f^{-1}(y,\infty)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)>y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle y<\inf\{z\in(0,\infty):x\notin A_{z}\}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists z\in(y,\infty)\mbox{ with }x\in A_{z}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle x\in\bigcup\{A_{z}\in{\mathcal{T}}:z>y\}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle x\in A_{y}

for each
y\in(0,\infty)
, giving
A_{y}=f^{-1}(y,\infty)
.
∎

Consequently, one can use null modification to transform
T_{1}
-measurable or integrable functions on the completion of a charge space to functions on the charge space itself, as described in the following lemma. Thus under the conditions of the lemma, completing the charge space has not added any new equivalence classes of functions.

Lemma 36

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a Peano-Jordan complete charge space with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is a sub-field of
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Then for every
f\in L_{0}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
, there exists
h\in L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
such that
f=h
a.e. (with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
). Moreover, if
f\in L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, then
h\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
.

Proof 24

It will be sufficient to prove the theorem assuming
f:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, since if the result holds for
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
, it holds for
f
by Lemma
3
.

By Theorem
20
, there exists a countable set
C\subset(0,\infty)
such that for
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
,
f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. Define

\displaystyle A_{y}

\displaystyle:=f^{-1}(y,\infty)\mbox{ for each }y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C,\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle:=\{A_{y}:y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C\}.

Let
\phi:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
be the order preserving map asserted in Lemma
33
, as it applies to
{\mathcal{T}}
. Define

\displaystyle B_{y}

\displaystyle:=\phi(A_{y})\mbox{ for each }y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle:=\{B_{y}:y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C\},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{y}

\displaystyle:=\{B\in{\mathcal{S}}:B\subset B_{y}\}\mbox{ for each }y\in(0,\infty).

Then, for each
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
,
B_{y}\triangle A_{y}\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

Before constructing
h
, the elements of
{\mathcal{S}}
must be further modified to satisfy the conditions of Statement 2 of Lemma
35
, as follows. Define:

\displaystyle C_{y}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}_{y}\setminus\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\mbox{ for each }y\in(0,\infty),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle:=\{C_{y}:y\in(0,\infty)\},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{y}

\displaystyle:=\{C\in{\mathcal{R}}:C\subset C_{y}\}\mbox{ for each }y\in(0,\infty).

Then the following properties hold

1.

C_{y}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
for each
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
,

2.

C_{y}=\bigcup{\mathcal{R}}_{y}
for each
y\in(0,\infty)
,

3.

\bigcap{\mathcal{R}}=\emptyset
, and

4.

C_{y}\triangle A_{y}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for each
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
.

To see 1, note that since
f
is smooth, for any
\epsilon>0
there is
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
such that
\mu(B_{y})=\mu(A_{y})<\epsilon
. But
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq B_{y}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
for all
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
, hence
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
with
\mu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})=0
. By Lemma
19
(2), for any
y\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
there exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in(0,\infty)\setminus C
such that
A_{y_{1}}\subset A_{y}\subset A_{y_{2}}
and
\mu(A_{y_{2}}\setminus A_{y_{1}})<\epsilon
. But then
B_{y_{1}},B_{y_{2}}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
with
B_{y_{1}}\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}_{y}\subseteq B_{y_{2}}
and

. Hence
\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}_{y}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, and
C_{y}\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

Properties 2 and 3 follow immediately from the definition of
C_{y}
.

To see 4, note for each

since
\mu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})=0
. Also note

, since

\mu(B_{y}\setminus\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}_{y})\leq\mu(B_{y_{2}}\setminus B_{y_{1}})<\epsilon

for any

, where

are as defined in the proof of 1. Finally, recall

to obtain the result.

By Lemma
35
, there is a function
h:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

for all

. Thus

for each

(2),

a.e. By Theorem

.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int|h|^{p}I_{|h|^{-1}(y,\infty)}d\mu_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int|f|^{p}I_{|f|^{-1}(y,\infty)}d\mu_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\epsilon,

where the first equality follows by Lemma

(4) and the second by Theorems

.

It is not in general true that
L_{0}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)=L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
, as may be understood by the following example. Take any
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
0<\nu(A)<1
, and form the
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\emptyset,A,A^{c},{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Let
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
be any null set contained in
A^{c}
, and let
B=A\cup B^{\prime}
. Then
I_{B}\in L_{0}({\mathbb{N}},\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\nu)
but
I_{B}\not\in L_{0}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
. However, the following theorem identifies necessary and sufficient conditions to have
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)=L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
and necessary and sufficient conditions to have
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
. Here the symbol ‘
\cong
’ is used to represent an isometric vector space isomorphism.

Theorem 37

Suppose
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a Peano-Jordan complete charge space with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is a sub-field of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Let
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. Then
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
is a dense subspace of
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
. Moreover, the following statements hold.

1.

L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)=L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
if and only if
{\mathcal{N}}\subset\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

2.

The following statements are logically equivalent:

(a)

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
with the isomorphism given by
[f]_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\mapsto[f]_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
.

(b)

\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
.

Proof 25

Lemma
30
gives that for every
A\in\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
there is
B\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
such that
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. It follows that
I_{A}=I_{B}
a.e. with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, and hence that for every simple function
s
with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, there is a simple function
s^{\prime}
with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
, such that
\|s-s^{\prime}\|_{p}=0
for
p\in[1,\infty)
or
d_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}(s,s^{\prime})=0
for
p=0
. Simple functions with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
are dense in
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
by Theorem
12
, hence so are simple functions with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
, and
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
is dense in
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
.

To show 1, it will be sufficient to show
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)=L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
if and only if
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
, by Lemma
34
. First suppose
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
. Then Theorem
14
gives

L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)=L_{p}(X,\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})},\mu)=L_{p}(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}},\mu)=L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu).

Conversely, suppose
L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)=L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
, and consider
A\in\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. Then
I_{A}\in L_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
by Theorem
14
, hence
I_{A}\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
. By Theorem
20
, there is
y\in(0,1)
such that
A=I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

For 2, first suppose
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Then Lemma
36
implies the injective vectorspace homomorphism
[f]_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\mapsto[f]_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
is surjective. This mapping is an isometry by Lemma
9
for
p\in[1,\infty)
, and by Corollary
24
for
p=0
. Conversely, suppose
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
with the isomorphism given by
[f]_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\mapsto[f]_{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, and consider
A\in\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. Then there exists
f\in L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
such that
f=I_{A}
a.e. with respect to
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. By Theorems
20
and
27
, there is
y\in(0,1)
such that
I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)\triangle f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
f^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
. But
I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)=A
, hence
A\in\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
by Lemma
30
. Thus
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}\subseteq\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Moreover,
\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, since
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
and
{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, giving
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
.
∎

The requirement in several of the results in this section that
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a Peano-Jordan complete charge space, is not as restrictive as may at first appear. By Lemma
31
, one may take any charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
with null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
and form its completion
(X,\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}}),\mu)
, where now the domain of
\mu
is extended to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. One can then form the charge space
(X,\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{N}})},\overline{\mu})
for use as the Peano-Jordan complete charge space in the above results. However, the above results are more general, in that they allow
{\mathcal{N}}
to be larger than the null sets of
(X,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\mu)
.

This section has characterised
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
spaces for which forming the completion of the underlying charge space adds no new equivalence classes. The value of this is that in such spaces one may effectively assume
{\mathcal{A}}
contains the null sets of
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, at least for the purpose of analysing the properties of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.
