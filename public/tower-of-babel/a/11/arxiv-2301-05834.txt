On lattice tilings of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} by limited magnitude error balls ℬ ⁡ ( n , 2 , 1 , 1 ) \mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
Tao Zhang, Yanlu Lian, Gennian Ge

Abstract
Limited magnitude error model has applications in flash memory. In this model, a perfect code is equivalent to a tiling of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} by limited magnitude error balls. In this paper, we give a complete classification of lattice tilings of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} by limited magnitude error balls ℬ ⁡ ( n , 2 , 1 , 1 ) \mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1) .



I
Introduction

In the asymmetric limited magnitude error model, a symbol
a\in\mathbb{Z}
may be modified to
b
during transmission, and the error magnitude
|a-b|
is likely to be bounded by certain threshold. One of applications of asymmetric limited magnitude error model is flash memory
[
2
]
. Moreover, for an information
\bf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, a common noise affects only some of the entries. Hence, for integers
n\geq t\geq 1
and
k_{+}\geq k_{-}\geq 0
, we define the
(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
-error ball as

\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-}):=\{{\bf{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\ a_{i}\in[-k_{-},k_{+}],\ \text{wt}({\bf{a}})\leq t\},

where
\text{wt}({\bf{a}})
denotes the Hamming weight of
{\bf{a}}
. Under this setting, it is easy to see that an error correcting code is equivalent to a packing of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
, and a perfect code is equivalent to a tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
. Moreover, a linear perfect code is equivalent to a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
.

When
n=t
, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
is a cube
[-k_{+}-k_{-},k_{+}+k_{-}]^{n}
, hence we will only consider
1\leq t\leq n-1
.
Tilings (or packings) of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k_{+},k_{-})
have been intensively studied in recent years due to their own interests and applications in flash memory. See
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
23
]
for the researches on cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,k)
and semi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,0)
. Later, these two shapes are extended to quasi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k_{+},k_{-})
by Schwartz
[
11
]
, and immediately, quasi-cross was received a lot of attentions
[
12
,
24
,
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
. For
t\geq 2
, there are only a few results. Tilings (or packings) of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,n-1,k,0)
are considered in
[
1
,
7
,
16
]
. In
[
21
,
22
]
, the authors studied the tiling and packing problem in the general case. In particular, Wei and Schwartz
[
21
]
gave a complete classification of the lattice tilings with
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,0)
and
\mathcal{B}(n,2,2,0)
. Recently, Wei and Schwartz
[
20
]
considered perfect burst-correcting codes for the limited magnitude error channel.

The goal of this paper is to continue this research. Since the lattice tilings with
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,0)
and
\mathcal{B}(n,2,2,0)
have been completely classified, and
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
is the next open case. In this paper, we will solve this case. The main result of this paper is the following one.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 3
, there exists a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
if and only if
n=11
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we will give the group ring representation of lattice tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
. In Section
III
, we prove our main result.

II
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{Z}[G]
denote the group ring of
G
over
\mathbb{Z}
, where
G
is a finite abelian group (written multiplicatively). For any
A\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
,
A
can be written as formal sum
A=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
, where
a_{g}\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
A=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

A^{(t)}=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g^{t}.

Addition, subtraction, multiplication and scalar multiplication in group ring are defined as:

\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\pm\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g=\sum_{g\in G}(a_{g}\pm b_{g})g,

\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g=\sum_{g\in G}(\sum_{h\in G}a_{h}b_{h^{-1}g})g,

and

\lambda\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g=\sum_{g\in G}(\lambda a_{g})g,

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g,\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
. For any multiset
A
of
G
, we can identify
A
with the group ring element
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
, where
a_{g}
is the multiplicity of
g
appearing in
A
. We also define
\text{supp}(A)
to be the support of
A=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
, i.e.,
\text{supp}(A)=\{g\in G:a_{g}\neq 0\}
. For any
A=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
, we denote

A^{*}=\sum_{g\in G\backslash\{e\}}a_{g}g.

The following theorem establishes the connection between lattice tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and finite abelian groups.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
4
]

Let
V
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Then there is a lattice tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
V
if and only if there are both an abelian group
G
of order
|V|
and a homomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow G
such that the restriction of
\phi
to
V
is a bijection.

Now we translate the existence of lattice tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
into group ring equations.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
, then there exists a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
if and only if there exists a finite abelian group
G
of order
2n^{2}+1
and a subset
T\subseteq G
satisfying

(1)

|T|=2n+1
,

(2)

e\in T
,

(3)

T=T^{(-1)}
,

(4)

T^{2}=2G+T^{(2)}+(2n-2)e
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
.
Let
e_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
, be a fixed orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
By Theorem
2.1
, there are both an abelian group
G
of order
2n^{2}+1
and a homomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow G
such that the restriction of
\phi
to
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
is a bijection. Since the homomorphism
\phi
is determined by the values
\phi(e_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
, then there exists an
n
-subset
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}\subset G
(let
\phi(e_{i})=a_{i}
) such that

\displaystyle G=\{e\}\cup\{a_{i},a_{i}^{-1}:\ 1\leq i\leq n\}\cup\{a_{i}a_{j},a_{i}a_{j}^{-1},a_{i}^{-1}a_{j},a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}^{-1}:\ 1\leq i<j\leq n\}.

In the language of group ring, the above equation can be written as

\displaystyle G=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})(a_{j}+a_{j}^{-1}).

Let
T=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})
, then
|T|=2n+1
,
e\in T
and
T^{(-1)}=T
. Moreover,

\displaystyle T^{2}=

\displaystyle(e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}^{2}+a_{i}^{-2})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})(a_{j}+a_{j}^{-1})+2ne

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2G+T^{(2)}+(2n-2)e.\qed

III
Proof of the main theorem

In this section, we will prove our main result. The main method was developed by Leung and Zhou
[
9
]
, which completely solved the existence of linear perfect Lee codes with radius 2. This method was also used to determine the existence of almost perfect linear Lee codes of radius 2
[
26
,
34
]
and linear diameter perfect Lee codes with diameter 6
[
33
]
.

The sketch of our proof is: we will first investigate
T^{(2)}T\pmod{3}
, we can get some restrictions on the coefficients of elements of
T^{(2)}T
. This will solve the cases
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
except
n=5,11
. By studying
TT^{(3)}\pmod{3}
, we solve the case
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
except
n=4
.
For the case
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, we will also study
TT^{(4)}\pmod{5}
and
TT^{(5)}\pmod{5}
. Small dimensions will be solved by symmetric polynomial method, which was developed by Kim
[
5
]
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer. Suppose that
G
is a finite abelian group with order
2n^{2}+1
and
T\subset G
satisfying conditions in Theorem
2.2
. Recall that
T=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})
and

\displaystyle G=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{-1})(a_{j}+a_{j}^{-1}).

(1)

The following lemma is directly from Condition (4) of Theorem
2.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
t\neq e
, we have

|\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in T\times T:t_{1}t_{2}=t\}|=\begin{cases}3,&\mbox{if }t\in T^{(2)};\\
2,&\mbox{if }t\notin T^{(2)}.\end{cases}

From Condition (4) in Theorem
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle TT^{(2)}=T^{3}-(4n+2)G-(2n-2)T.

(2)

We write

\displaystyle TT^{(2)}=\sum_{i=0}^{M}iX_{i},

(3)

where
X_{i}

(i=0,1,\dots,M)
form a partition of group
G
, i.e.,

\displaystyle G=\sum_{i=0}^{M}X_{i}.

(4)

Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{M}i|X_{i}|=4n^{2}+4n+1
,

(2)

\sum_{i=0}^{M}|X_{i}|=2n^{2}+1
,

(3)

\sum_{i=1}^{M}|X_{i}|=1-\beta+\sum_{i\geq 3}\frac{(i-1)(i-2)}{2}|X_{i}|
, where
2\beta=|T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}|
.

Proof.

The first two equations are directly from Equations (
3
) and (
4
). Now we count the size of
\text{supp}(T^{(2)}T)
.
We write
T^{(2)}=\sum_{i=0}^{2n}b_{i}
, where
b_{0}=e
and
b_{i}^{-1}=b_{n+i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we have

|b_{i}T\cap b_{j}T|=\begin{cases}3,&\mbox{if }b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}\in T^{(2)};\\
2,&\mbox{if }b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}\notin T^{(2)}.\end{cases}

Let
\alpha=|\{(i,j):0\leq i<j\leq 2n,b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}\in T^{(2)}\}|
and
N(t)=|\{(i,j):0\leq i<j\leq 2n,b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}=t\}|
, then
\alpha=\sum_{t\in T^{(2)}}N(t)
. Since
\gcd(2n^{2}+1,2)=1
, then
T^{(2)}
also satisfies conditions in Theorem
2.2
. By the definition of
b_{i}
and Lemma
3.1
, we have

N(t)+N(t^{-1})=\begin{cases}3,&\mbox{if }t\in T^{(2)*}\cap T^{(4)*};\\
2,&\mbox{if }t\in T^{(2)}\backslash T^{(4)}.\end{cases}

Hence,
\alpha=|T^{(2)}\backslash T^{(4)}|+\frac{3|T^{(2)*}\cap T^{(4)*}|}{2}=|T^{*}\backslash T^{(2)*}|+\frac{3|T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}|}{2}=2n+\beta
.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}|X_{i}|=

\displaystyle|\text{supp}(T^{(2)}T)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{2n}|b_{i}T|-\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq 2n}|b_{i}T\cap b_{j}T|+\sum_{r\geq 3}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq 2n}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T\cap\dots\cap b_{i_{r}}T|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4n^{2}+4n+1-3\alpha-2(\binom{2n+1}{2}-\alpha)+\sum_{r\geq 3}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq 2n}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T\cap\dots\cap b_{i_{r}}T|.

Suppose that
g\in b_{i_{1}}T\cap\dots\cap b_{i_{r}}T
for some
r\geq 3
, then
g\in X_{i}
for some
i\geq 3
. The contribution for
g
in the sum
\sum_{r\geq 3}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq 2n}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T\cap\dots\cap b_{i_{r}}T|
is

(-1)^{3-1}\binom{i}{3}+(-1)^{4-1}\binom{i}{4}+\cdots=\binom{i}{2}-\binom{i}{1}+\binom{i}{0}=\frac{(i-1)(i-2)}{2}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}|X_{i}|=

\displaystyle 4n^{2}+4n+1-3\alpha-2(\binom{2n+1}{2}-\alpha)+\sum_{r\geq 3}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq 2n}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T\cap\dots\cap b_{i_{r}}T|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4n^{2}+4n+1-3\alpha-2(\binom{2n+1}{2}-\alpha)+\sum_{i\geq 3}|X_{i}|\frac{(i-1)(i-2)}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\beta+\sum_{i\geq 3}\frac{(i-1)(i-2)}{2}|X_{i}|.\qed

Lemma 3.3
.

e
appears
2\beta+1
times in
T^{(3)}
, where
2\beta=|T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}|
.

Proof.

If there exist
a_{i},a_{j}\in T^{*}
with
j\neq i
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}^{2}\in T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}
, then
a_{j}=a_{i}a_{j}^{-1}
. By Equation (
1
), it can be seen that
a_{j}
appears twice in
G
, which is a contradiction.

If there exists
a_{i}\in T^{*}
such that
a_{i}=a_{i}^{2}\in T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}
, then
a_{i}=e
, which is a contradiction again.

If there exists
a_{i}\in T^{*}
such that
a_{i}=a_{i}^{-2}\in T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}
, then
a_{i}^{3}=e
.

On the other hand, if there exists
a_{i}\in T^{*}
such that
a_{i}^{3}=e
, then
a_{i}=a_{i}^{-2}\in T^{*}\cap T^{(2)*}
. Hence
e
appears
2\beta+1
times in
T^{(3)}
.
∎

\displaystyle T^{(2)}T\equiv T^{(3)}+2G+T\pmod{3}.

(5)

\displaystyle T^{4}=

.

III-D

Some sporadic cases

For
n=11
, Wei and Schwartz
[
21
]
have constructed a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(11,2,1,1)
.
For
n=3,4,5
, the nonexistence of lattice tiling with
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
follows from the following proposition.

Proposition 3.16
.

Suppose that
2n^{2}+1=mp
where
p
is a prime and
p>2n+1
. Define
a=\min\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\ p\mid(4^{k}-4n-2)\}
and
b
is the order of
4
modulo
p
(If there is no
k
with
p\mid(4^{k}-4n-2)
, then let
a=\infty
). Assume that there is a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,2,1,1)
, then there exists at least one
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{2}}\rfloor\}
such that the equation

a(x+1)+by=n-\ell

has nonnegative integer solutions.

Proof.

The proof is similar as
[
5
]
, for readers convenience, we keep the proof.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group (written additively) with order
2n^{2}+1
, and
0
be its identity element.
By Theorem
2.1
, there exists
T=\{t_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\}\subseteq G
such that

\{0\},\{\pm t_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\},\{\pm t_{i}\pm t_{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}

form a partition of
G
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
of index
p
. Let
\rho:G\rightarrow G/H
be the canonical homomorphism and
a_{i}=\rho(t_{i})
. Then the multisets

\{0\},\{\pm a_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\},\{\pm a_{i}\pm a_{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}

form a partition of
mG/H
.

Let
k
be an integer. By calculation,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((a_{i}^{2k}+(-a_{i})^{2k}\right)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left((a_{i}+a_{j})^{2k}+(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2k}+(-a_{i}+a_{j})^{2k}+(-a_{i}-a_{j})^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-4^{k}+4n+2)S_{2k}+2\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{2k}{2t}S_{2t}S_{2(k-t)}

where
S_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{t}
. Since this is also the sum of the
2k
-th powers of every element in
mG/H
,

(-4^{k}+4n+2)S_{2k}+2\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{2k}{2t}S_{2t}S_{2(k-t)}=\begin{cases}0,&p-1\nmid 2k,\\
-m,&p-1\mid 2k.\end{cases}

(27)

Let
a
and
b
be the least positive integers satisfying
p\mid(-4^{a}+4n+2)
and
p\mid(4^{b}-1)
. Define

X=\{ax+by:x\geq 1,y\geq 0\}.

We prove the following two claims by induction on
k
.

Claim 1:
If
1\leq k<\frac{p-1}{2}
is not in
X
, then
S_{2k}=0
.

Suppose that
S_{2k}=0
for each
k\leq k_{0}-1
that is not in
X
. Assume that
k_{0}\notin X
. As
X
is closed under addition, for any
t
, at least one of
t
and
k_{0}-t
is not in
X
.

For any integer
k
, if
p\mid(-4^{k}+4n+2)
, then
k
must be of the form
a+by
whence
k\in X
. This implies that
p\nmid(-4^{k_{0}}+4n+2)
. By (
27
) and the induction hypothesis,

0=(-4^{k_{0}}+4n+2)S_{2k_{0}}+2\sum_{t=1}^{k_{0}-1}\binom{2k_{0}}{2t}S_{2t}S_{2(k_{0}-t)}=(-4^{k_{0}}+4n+2)S_{2k_{0}}.

Thus
S_{2k_{0}}=0
.

Let
e_{k}
be the elementary symmetric polynomials with respect to
a_{1}^{2}
,
a_{2}^{2}
,
\cdots
,
a_{n}^{2}
.

Claim 2
: If
1\leq k\leq n<\frac{p-1}{2}
is not in
X
, then
e_{k}=0
.

Suppose that
e_{k}=0
for each
k\leq k_{0}-1
not in
X
and
k_{0}\notin X
. As
X
is closed under addition, for each
0<t<k_{0}
, at least one of
t
and
k_{0}-t
is not in
X
. By Claim 1 and the inductive hypothesis,
e_{t}=0
or
S_{2(k_{0}-t)}=0
.
Together with Newton identities on
a_{1}^{2},\dots,a_{n}^{2}
, we have

k_{0}e_{k_{0}}=e_{k_{0}-1}S_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{i+1}e_{k_{0}-i}S_{2i}+\dots+(-1)^{k_{0}-1}S_{2k_{0}}=(-1)^{k_{0}-1}S_{2k_{0}}=0.

Therefore
e_{k_{0}}=0
.

Suppose that
0
appears
\ell
times in
\rho(T)
, and let
a_{i_{1}}=a_{i_{2}}=\dots=a_{i_{\ell}}=0
, then
-a_{i_{j}}=0
and
\pm a_{i_{j}}\pm a_{i_{k}}=0
for
1\leq j<k\leq\ell
. This means that the number of
0
in multiset
\rho(G)
is at least
1+2\ell+4\binom{\ell}{2}=2\ell^{2}+1
.
As
0
appears exactly
m
times in
mG/H
, then
2\ell^{2}+1\leq m
, i.e.,
\ell\leq\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{2}}
. Note that
e_{n-\ell}
is the product of those nonzero
a_{i}^{2}
’s, whence
e_{n-\ell}\neq 0
. By Claim 2,
n-\ell
is in
X
. This completes the proof.
∎
