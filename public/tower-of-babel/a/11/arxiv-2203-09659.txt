Low-Degree Learning and the Metric Entropy of Polynomials
Lauritz Streck

Abstract
Let ℱ n , d \mathscr{F}_{n,d} be the class of all functions f : { − 1 , 1 } n → [ − 1 , 1 ] f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1] on the n n -dimensional discrete hypercube of degree at most d d . In the first part of this paper, we prove that any (deterministic or randomized) algorithm which learns ℱ n , d \mathscr{F}_{n,d} with L 2 L_{2} -accuracy ε \varepsilon requires at least Ω ​ ( ( 1 − ε ) ​ 2 d ​ log ⁡ n ) \Omega((1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})2^{d}\log n) queries for large enough n n , thus establishing the sharpness as n → ∞ n\to\infty of a recent upper bound of Eskenazis and Ivanisvili (2021). To do this, we show that the L 2 L_{2} -packing numbers 𝖬 ( ℱ n , d , ∥ ⋅ ∥ L 2 , ε ) \mathsf{M}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},\varepsilon) of the concept class ℱ n , d \mathscr{F}_{n,d} satisfy the two-sided estimate c ( 1 − ε ) 2 d log n ≤ log 𝖬 ( ℱ n , d , ∥ ⋅ ∥ L 2 , ε ) ≤ 2 C ​ d ​ log ⁡ n ε 4 c(1-\varepsilon)2^{d}\log n\leq\log\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2^{Cd}\log n}{\varepsilon^{4}} for large enough n n , where c , C > 0 c,C>0 are universal constants. In the second part of the paper, we present a logarithmic upper bound for the randomized query complexity of classes of bounded approximate polynomials whose Fourier spectra are concentrated on few subsets. As an application, we prove new estimates for the number of random queries required to learn approximate juntas of a given degree, functions with rapidly decaying Fourier tails and constant depth circuits of given size. Finally, we obtain bounds for the number of queries required to learn the polynomial class ℱ n , d \mathscr{F}_{n,d} without error in the query and random example models.

Introduction

For any function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, there exist unique real coefficients
\{\hat{f}(S)\}_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}
such that

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad f(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}\hat{f}(S)w_{S}(x),

(1)

where the Walsh functions
w_{S}:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
are defined by
w_{S}(x)=\prodop\displaylimits_{i\in S}x_{i}
. We say that the function
f
has degree at most
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
if
\hat{f}(S)=0
for all sets
S
with
|S|>d
.

1.1
Learning functions on the hypercube

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a class of functions on the discrete hypercube of dimension
n
. The learning problem for the class
\mathscr{F}
can be described as follows. Consider an unknown function
f\in\mathscr{F}
. Given access to
examples

(X_{1},f(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f(X_{Q}))
, the goal is to algorithmically construct a
hypothesis function

h:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which effectively approximates
f
. Different access models to examples give rise to concrete versions of the learning problem. The two most standard such models are the
query
model, in which the algorithm can sequentially request the values of
f
at any
Q
-tuple of points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
from
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, and the
random example
model, in which the data points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
are generated uniformly and independently from
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. In the query model, the goal is to construct a function
h
satisfying
\|h-f\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
whereas in the random example model, the desired output is a
random
function
h
satisfying
\|h-f\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\varepsilon,\delta\in[0,1)
are pre-fixed accuracy and confidence parameters respectively. The least number
Q
of examples required to solve the learning problem in each case is called the
query complexity
of the model and shall be denoted by
\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{F},\varepsilon)
for the query model and
by
\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F},\varepsilon,\delta)
for the random example model.

The query complexity of learning problems has been studied extensively for various classes
\mathscr{F}
of functions on the discrete hypercube (see
[
33
,
37
]
). One of the first rigorous results of this kind is the
Low-Degree Algorithm
of Linial, Mansour and Nisan
[
31
]
, who considered the class

\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]:\ f\mbox{ has degree at most }d\big\}

(2)

and showed the estimate
\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{2n^{d}}{\varepsilon}\log(\tfrac{2n^{d}}{\delta})
for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. In the recent work
[
16
]
, which followed an intermediate
O_{d,\varepsilon,\delta}(n^{d-1}\log n)
asymptotic

The first goal of the present paper is to investigate lower bounds for the query complexity, which in particular imply that (
3
) is asymptotically optimal as
n\to\infty
, that is

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)=\Theta_{d,\varepsilon,\delta}(\log n).

Consider the class

\mathscr{B}_{n,d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}:\ f\mbox{ has degree at most }d\big\}\subset\mathscr{F}_{n,d}.

We will prove the following lower estimate for the complexities of this class.

Theorem 1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\varepsilon)\geq\max\left\{(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})2^{d-2}\log_{2}n-(d+1)2^{d-2},d\log_{2}\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right\}

(6)

and

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\geq\max\left\{(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})2^{d-2}\log_{2}n-(d+1)2^{d-2},d\log_{2}\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right\}+\log_{2}(1-\delta).

(7)

The equivalence (
4
) now follows due to the inequality
\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)

The second goal of this paper is to show a more robust version of the upper bound (
3
) that applies to different concept classes
\mathscr{F}
which are not necessarily of bounded degree. In order to present this result we shall need some terminology (see also
[

\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|>d}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq t.

(8)

More generally, given a family
\mathscr{S}
of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
we say that the spectrum of
f
is
\eta
-concentrated on
\mathscr{S}
if

\sumop\displaylimits_{S\notin\mathscr{S}}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\eta.

(9)

Our main upper bound for learning is the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
t,\eta\in[0,1)
. Let
\mathscr{F}
be a class of bounded functions
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
such that the Fourier spectrum of any

is
t
-concentrated up to degree
d
and is
\eta
-concentrated on a family

of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
satisfying
\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq m
. Then,

\forall\ \varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1),\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F},\eta+t+\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\left\lceil\frac{18m}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\right)\right\rceil.

(10)

In Remark

below we will see how this statement implies the estimate (

) of

1.2
Fourier concentration and learning upper bounds

In this section we shall present concrete applications of Theorem
2
for various concept classes
\mathscr{F}
. We start with the class of approximate juntas of a given degree. Recall that a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
(k,\eta)
-junta if there exists a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\sigma|\leq k
and a map
g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
depending only on the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}
such that
\|f-g\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\eta
. Consider the class

\mathscr{J}_{n,k,\eta}=\big\{f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]:\ f\mbox{ is a }(k,\eta)\mbox{-junta}\big\}.

(11)

We shall prove the following estimate on the randomized query complexity of
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\cap\mathscr{J}_{n,k,\eta}
.

Corollary 3
.

In the setting above, for
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
we have

\mathsf{Q}_{r}\big(\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\cap\mathscr{J}_{n,k,\eta},2\eta+\varepsilon,\delta\big)\leq\left\lceil\frac{18}{\varepsilon}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{\min\{d,k\}}\binom{k}{r}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{\min\{d,k\}}\binom{n}{r}\right)\right\rceil.

(12)

In particular, choosing
d=n
, we get

\mathsf{Q}_{r}\big(\mathscr{J}_{n,k,\eta},2\eta+\varepsilon,\delta\big)\leq\frac{2^{k+5}}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{k}\binom{n}{r}\right).

(13)

Corollary
3
can be concretely applied in view of the large available literature on junta theorems in Boolean analysis. To motivate a first application along these lines, observe that the upper bound (
3
) of
[
16
]
differs from that of
[
31
]
in its dependence on
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. While we do not know whether the
\varepsilon^{-d-1}
asymptotic behavior is needed to learn
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
, Corollary
3
combined with a structural result of Nisan and Szegedy
[
36
]
gives the following upper bound for the complexity of
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}
, alas with a somewhat worse dependence on
d
.

Corollary 4
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{36\cdot d2^{d^{2}}}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right).

(14)

Combining Theorem
2
with a deep junta theorem of Dinur, Friedgut, Kindler and O’Donnell
[
13
]
, we will deduce that bounded functions which are sufficiently close to polynomials of degree
d
can be learned from
O_{d}(\log n)
samples. For
t\geq 0
, consider the class
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t)
consisting of functions
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
whose spectra are
t
-concentrated up to degree
d
.
In other words,
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t)
consists of all bounded functions which are
\sqrt{t}
-close (in
L_{2}
) to a polynomial of degree at most
d
. Corollary
3
has the following consequence.

Corollary 5
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
t\in[0,1)
and
\eta\geq\frac{Cd^{2}{\log d}}{{\log(1/t)}}
, we have

\forall\ \varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1),\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r}\big(\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t),\eta+\varepsilon,\delta\big)\leq\frac{2^{Cd^{2}}}{\eta^{2d}\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right).

(15)

It is worth emphasizing that as
t\to 0^{+}
, we can also take
\eta=\eta(t)\to 0^{+}
in the statement above. Corollary
5
is a robust version of the main theorem of
[
16
]
. On the one hand, the method of
[
16
]
seems unfit to provide estimates for the complexity of
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t)
as it uses the Bohnenblust–Hille inequality
[
10
]
, which heavily relies on the fact that the unknown function is a bounded polynomial. On the other hand (see also Remark
14
), Corollary
5
gives a worse estimate on the complexity of
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}=\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(0)
in terms of
d,\varepsilon
than the bound (
3

[
16
]

To the best of our knowledge, Corollary

31

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t),t+\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{2n^{d}}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{2n^{d}}{\delta}\right)

Corollary 6
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
t\in[0,1)
and
\eta\geq t^{1+o(1)}d^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}

\forall\ \varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1),\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r}\big(\mathscr{B}_{n,d}(t),\eta+\varepsilon,\delta\big)\leq\frac{2^{Cd^{2}}}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right).

(16)

Finally, we present a concrete application of Corollary
6
to Boolean functions which can be represented by constant depth circuits. We refer to
[
37
, Chapter 4]
for the relevant definitions. For readers which are unfamiliar with this class, we just point out that DNF formulas, i.e. functions which are representable as logical
\lor
of terms, each of which is a logical
\land
of variables

or their negations
\lnot x_{i}
, are circuits of depth 2. Similarly, CNF formulas, in which the roles of
\lor
and

are reversed, are also circuits of depth 2. Corollary
6
combined with estimates on the Fourier concentration of constant depth circuits
[

,

has the following consequence.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\mathscr{C}_{n,d,s}
be the class of all Boolean functions on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
computable by a depth-

. Then, for every
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{C}_{n,d,s},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\exp\big(O(\log(s/\varepsilon))^{d-2}\cdot\log s\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\big)\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right).

(17)

Learning constant depth circuits (also known as
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits) in quasi-polynomial time is the main focus of the seminal work
[
31
]
of Linial, Mansour and Nisan which prompted them to design the Low-Degree Algorithm. Moreover, it is known (see
[
22
]
) that quasi-polynomial time is also
necessary
to learn this class, conditionally on some standard cryptographic assumptions. The contribution of Corollary

is the fact that the query complexity of this learning problem is (exponentially) smaller than the corresponding running time
[
31
]
for large enough

. Choosing

, Corollary
7
implies that DNF formulas of polynomial size can be learned from polynomially many samples up to constant error (see
[
26
]
and the references therein for the long-standing open problem of understanding the running time required to learn DNF). It is worth emphasizing that the reason Corollary
7
follows from Corollary
6
is that
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
circuits have strong enough Fourier concentration. It remains an interesting problem to understand whether Corollary
6
can be boosted to encapsulate classes of Boolean functions with weaker concentration such as linear threshold functions or functions of many hyperplanes
[
2
,
39
]
.

1.3
Metric entropy and learning lower bounds

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
be a metric space and
\varepsilon>0
. A subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is an
\varepsilon
-packing of
\mathcal{X}
if for any
p\neq p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
d_{\mathcal{X}}(p,p^{\prime})>\varepsilon
. The largest size of an
\varepsilon
-packing is called the
packing number
of
\mathcal{X}
and is denoted by
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\varepsilon)
. A subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is an
\varepsilon
-cover of
\mathcal{X}
if for any
q\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists some
p\in\mathcal{C}
with
d_{\mathcal{X}}(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
. The smallest size of an
\varepsilon
-cover is called the
covering number
of
\mathcal{X}
and is denoted by
\mathsf{N}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\varepsilon)
. The quantity
\log_{2}\mathsf{N}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\varepsilon)
is called the
\varepsilon
-
metric entropy
of
\mathcal{X}
. It is well known (see
[
43
, Lemma 4.2.8]
) that packing and covering numbers are closely related via the elementary inequalities

\forall\ \varepsilon>0,\qquad\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},2\varepsilon)\leq\mathsf{N}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\varepsilon)\leq\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}},\varepsilon).

(18)

The pertinence of metric entropy in the context of learning lower bounds stems from the classical observation that concept classes with large covering (or packing) numbers cannot be efficiently learned from few queries (see, for instance, the works
[
4
,
32
,
14
]
). In our setting, we shall need the following concrete estimate which we could not locate in the literature.

Proposition 8
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathscr{B}
be a class of Boolean functions on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. Then,

\forall\ \varepsilon>0,\qquad\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{B},\varepsilon)\geq\log_{2}\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{B},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},2\sqrt{\varepsilon})

(19)

and

\forall\ \varepsilon>0\mbox{, }\forall\ \delta\in(0,1),\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{B},\varepsilon,\delta)\geq\log_{2}\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{B},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},2\sqrt{\varepsilon})+\log_{2}(1-\delta),

(20)

where
\|\phi-\psi\|_{L_{2}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x}(\phi(x)-\psi(x))^{2}}
is the
L_{2}
-norm with respect to the uniform probability measure.

The class
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}
defined in (
5
) contains all Walsh functions
\{w_{S}\}_{|S|\leq d}
and thus

\forall\ \varepsilon\in(0,\sqrt{2}),\qquad\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},\varepsilon)\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{d}\binom{n}{k}\geq\frac{n^{d}}{d^{d}},

(21)

as
\|w_{S}-w_{T}\|_{L_{2}}=\sqrt{2}
for any
S\neq T
. Combining this simple lower bound with Proposition
8
we already deduce the asymptotic sharpness of (
3
) as
n\to\infty
. In order to derive a sharper estimate for
\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon)
as a function of the degree
d
, we shall prove the following improved lower bound on the packing numbers of
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}
along with a qualitatively matching upper bound for the metric entropy of
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
.

Theorem 9
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\leq\log_{2}n
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, we have

\log_{2}\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},2\varepsilon)\geq(1-\varepsilon)2^{d-2}\log_{2}n-(d+1)2^{d-2}

(22)

Moreover,

\log_{2}\mathsf{N}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2^{Cd}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log n+\kappa(d,\varepsilon),

(23)

where
C>0
is a universal constant and
\kappa(d,\varepsilon)>0
depends only on
d
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Inequality (
6
) is a direct consequence of (
19
), (
21
) and (
22
), while (
7
) follows from (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
).
∎

Having presented Theorem
9
, some observations related to the implicit dependencies in (
4
) are in order. In
[
16
]
it was shown that

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{e^{8}d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{d+1}}(B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}})^{2d}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right),

(24)

where
B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}
is an important approximation theoretic parameter called the
Bohnenblust–Hille
constant of the hypercube (see
[
5
,
11
]
). While it is widely believed that
B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}
grows at most polynomially in
d
, the best known upper bound due to
[
10
]
states that
B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}\leq\exp(C\sqrt{d\log d})
for some universal constant
C>0
which, combined with (
24
), leads to (
3
). A polynomial bound on
B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}
combined with (
24
) would almost match, up to a logarithmic term in the exponent, the asymptotic behavior as
d\to\infty
of the lower bound (
7
) of Theorem
1
. We mention at this point that we are not aware of any non-constant (as
d\to\infty
) lower bound for the constant
B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}
.

The existence of a large separated set attaining the lower bound (
22
) is proven via a probabilistic construction of random decision trees with prescribed depth. On the other hand, the upper bound (
23
) is a consequence of the deep junta theorem of
[
13
]
but, to the extent of our knowledge, had not been previously observed in the literature. It is quite surprising that while
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
lies in a
O_{d}(n^{d})
-dimensional space, its metric entropy is logarithmic in the dimension of this space rather than polynomial. The reason for this is the strong restriction that
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
consists of functions which are bounded in
L_{\infty}
-norm yet it is endowed with the Hilbertian
L_{2}
-metric. The existence of such
small nets
is often useful in theoretical computer science and probability theory, in particular in the derandomization literature
[
34
,
40
,
29
]
and in the study of suprema of stochastic processes
[
43
, Chapters 7-8]
.

1.4
Exact learning

Having established reasonable bounds on the number of queries required to learn a function in
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
up to error
\varepsilon>0
, we proceed to investigate the exact case
\varepsilon=0
. As it turns out, the number of random queries required to learn a function
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
up to a constant error
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
using the classical Low-Degree Algorithm
[
31
]
is in fact the same (up to constants depending only on
d
) as the number of queries required to
exactly
learn the concept class
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
. Formally, our results is this setting are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Fix

2
Metric entropy and query complexity

We start by formalizing the concepts introduced earlier. A
learning algorithm
on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
using
Q
queries is a mapping
H:\left(\{-1,1\}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\right)^{Q}\to L_{2}(\{-1,1\}^{n})
which, given input of the form
(X_{1},f(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f(X_{Q}))
produces a hypothesis function for
f
. In this terminology, the randomized query complexity
\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F},\varepsilon,\delta)
of a class of functions
\mathscr{F}
on the hypercube is the smallest
Q\in\mathbb{N}
for which there exists a learning algorithm
H
with the following property:

\forall\ f\in\mathscr{F},\qquad\mathbb{P}_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\Big\{\big\|H\big((X_{1},f(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f(X_{Q}))\big)-f\big\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\Big\}\geq 1-\delta.

(27)

In the case of non-randomized algorithms, we need to ensure that the query points are chosen consistently with respect to the previous data. In other words,
X_{1}
is always a fixed point on the hypercube and for any
q\geq 2
, there exists a function
\varphi_{q}:(\{-1,1\}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})^{q-1}\to\{-1,1\}^{n}
associated to
H
determining the
q
-th query point as a function of the previous data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{q-1}
and the values
y_{1},\ldots,y_{q-1}
of the unknown function on these points. Given a learning algorithm
H
and an unknown function
f\in\mathscr{F}
, we shall denote by
X_{1}[f],X_{2}[f],\ldots
the sequence of points that
H
queries in order to construct a hypothesis function for
f
. In this terminology, the deterministic query complexity of the class
\mathscr{F}
is the least
Q\in\mathbb{N}
for which there exists a learning algorithm
H
using
Q
queries satisfying the following property:

\forall\ f\in\mathscr{F},\qquad\big\|H\big(\big(X_{1}[f],f(X_{1}[f])\big),\ldots,\big(X_{Q}[f],f(X_{Q}[f])\big)\big)-f\big\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

(28)

Having properly defined these notions, we may proceed to the proof of Proposition
8
. The argument relies on an information-theoretic consideration: given samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
, the outputs
f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{Q})
provide
Q
bits of information for
f
and thus cannot distinguish more than
\log_{2}Q
functions which are reasonably far apart.

Proof of Proposition
8
.

Let
M=\mathsf{M}(\mathscr{B},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},2\sqrt{\varepsilon})
and consider
f_{1},\ldots,f_{M}\in\mathscr{B}
with
\|f_{i}-f_{j}\|_{L_{2}}>2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for all
i\neq j
. We start with the lower bound (
19
) in the deterministic case. Denote by
Q=\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{B},\varepsilon)
and let
X_{1}[f],X_{2}[f],\ldots,X_{Q}[f]
be samples satisfying (
28
) for some learning algorithm
H
and all functions
f
in the class
\mathscr{B}
. Consider the set

\Sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\big(f_{i}(X_{1}[f_{i}]),\ldots,f_{i}(X_{Q}[f_{i}])\big):\ i=1,\ldots,M\big\}.

(29)

Claim.

|\Sigma|=M
.

Proof.
Clearly
|\Sigma|\leq M
. If
|\Sigma|<M
, then there exist
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
for which we have

\forall\ k\in\{1,\ldots,Q\},\qquad f_{i}(X_{k}[f_{i}])=f_{j}(X_{k}[f_{j}]).

(30)

As
X_{1}[f_{i}]=X_{1}[f_{j}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}X_{1}
by definition of
H
, (
30
) gives
f_{i}(X_{1})=f_{j}(X_{1})
which then, by consistency of the algorithm, implies that
X_{2}[f_{i}]=X_{2}[f_{j}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}X_{2}
. Continuing iteratively, we deduce that
X_{k}[f_{i}]=X_{k}[f_{j}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}X_{k}
for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}
and thus the common output function

h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}H\big((X_{1},f_{i}(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f_{i}(X_{Q}))\big)=H\big((X_{1},f_{j}(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f_{j}(X_{Q}))\big)

(31)

satisfies
\|h-f_{i}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
and
\|h-f_{j}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
which is a contradiction as
\|f_{i}-f_{j}\|_{L_{2}}>2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
\square

Finally, observe that as the class
\mathscr{B}
consists of Boolean functions, we have the trivial inclusion
\Sigma\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{Q}
which implies that
M=|\Sigma|\leq 2^{Q}
and the proof is complete.

In the random case, denote by
Q=\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F},\varepsilon,\delta)
and let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q})
where
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent random vectors, each uniformly distributed on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, satisfying (
27
) for some learning algorithm
H
. For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, consider the event

B_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Big\{\big\|H\big((X_{1},f_{i}(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f_{i}(X_{Q}))\big)-f_{i}\big\|_{L_{2}}^{2}>\varepsilon\Big\},

(32)

which has probability
\mathbb{P}\{B_{i}\}\leq\delta
by (
27
) and, as before, consider the (random) set

\Sigma(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\big(f_{i}(X_{1}),\ldots,f_{i}(X_{Q})\big):\ i=1,\ldots,M\big\}.

(33)

Claim.

\mathbb{E}|\Sigma(X)|\geq(1-\delta)M
.

Proof.
Consider the partition
\{1,\ldots,M\}=\sigma_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\sigma_{|\Sigma(X)|}
depending on
X
such that for every
r\in\{1,\ldots,|\Sigma(X)|\}
and all
i,j\in\sigma_{r}
, we have
f_{i}\equiv f_{j}
on
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}\}
. Now, suppose that there exist two distinct
i\neq j\in\sigma_{r}
such that
X\notin B_{i}
and
X\notin B_{j}
. Then, the function

h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}H\big((X_{1},f_{i}(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f_{i}(X_{Q}))\big)=H\big((X_{1},f_{j}(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{Q},f_{j}(X_{Q}))\big)

(34)

satisfies
\|h-f_{i}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
and
\|h-f_{j}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon
which contradicts
\|f_{i}-f_{j}\|_{L_{2}}>2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, for any
r
and any
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q})
, there exists a subset
\tau_{r}\subseteq\sigma_{r}
with
|\tau_{r}|\geq|\sigma_{r}|-1
such that
X\in B_{i}
for all
i\in\tau_{r}
. Adding up these inequalities and taking the expectation, we deduce that

M-\mathbb{E}|\Sigma(X)|=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sumop\displaylimits_{r=1}^{|\Sigma(X)|}\big(|\sigma_{r}|-1\big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sumop\displaylimits_{r=1}^{|\Sigma(X)|}|\tau_{r}|\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sumop\displaylimits_{r=1}^{|\Sigma(X)|}\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in\sigma_{r}}{\bf 1}_{B_{i}}(X)\big]=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}\{B_{i}\}\leq\delta M,

(35)

which is the desired inequality.
\square

As the class
\mathscr{B}
consists of Boolean functions, we have
(1-\delta)M\leq\mathbb{E}|\Sigma(X)|\leq 2^{Q}
.
∎

2.1
Decision trees

In this section we will prove the lower bound (
22
) for the packing numbers of
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}
and
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
. First, we introduce some necessary background. Following
[
37
, §3.2]
, we define a decision tree
T
to be a representation of a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as a rooted binary tree in which the internal nodes are labeled by Boolean variables
x_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the edges are labeled by -1 and 1 and the leaves are labeled by real numbers. It is required that no Boolean variable
x_{i}
appears more than once on any root-leaf path. On input
y\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, the tree
T
computes the value
f(y)
in the following way. Starting from the root, when the computation path reaches a node labeled by
x_{i}
, it follows the unique edge labeled by the value
y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. The output
f(y)
of
T
is the label of the leaf reached by this path. It is a classical fact (see
[
37
, Proposition 3.16]
) that if a function
f
can be represented by a decision tree of depth
d
, then
f
has degree at most
d
.

In order to prove the lower bound (
22
) on the packing number of
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}
, we shall need the following combinatorial lemma on large families of sets with pairwise small intersections.

Lemma 12
.

Fix
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<m
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists
t\geq(2k)^{-k/2}m^{(1-\varepsilon)k/2}
and subsets
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
of size
k
satisfying

\forall\ i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,t\},\qquad|\sigma_{i}\cap\sigma_{j}|<(1-\varepsilon)k.

(36)

Proof.

We shall use the probabilistic method. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a uniformly chosen random subset of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
of cardinality
k
. Then, we have

\mathbb{P}\big\{|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cap\{1,\ldots,k\}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)k\big\}=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{k}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j\leq\varepsilon k}\binom{k}{k-j}\binom{m-k}{j}\leq\frac{k^{k}}{m^{k}}m^{\varepsilon k}\sumop\displaylimits_{j\leq\varepsilon k}\binom{k}{k-j}\leq(2k)^{k}m^{-(1-\varepsilon)k},

(37)

where we used the fact that
\frac{r^{s}}{s^{s}}\leq\binom{r}{s}\leq r^{s}
. If
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}
are i.i.d. copies of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, then by homogeneity

\forall\ i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,t\},\qquad\mathbb{P}\big\{|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\cap\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)k\big\}\leq(2k)^{k}m^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}

(38)

and thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{i\neq j:|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\cap\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)k\}\right]\leq\binom{t}{2}(2k)^{k}m^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}<t^{2}(2k)^{k}m^{-(1-\varepsilon)k}.

(39)

Therefore, if
t\leq(2k)^{-k/2}m^{(1-\varepsilon)k/2}
, there exist
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
with the desired property.

2.2
Juntas

In this section we will prove the upper bound (
23
) for the metric entropy of the class
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
. A general principle in analysis on the hypercube asserts that functions whose spectrum is not spread out, effectively depend only on few variables. Many concrete instances of this phenomenon have been studied for Boolean functions, such as the important works
[
17
,
8
,
18
,
25
]
. The definitive junta theorem for general
bounded
functions, is the following deep result of Dinur, Friedgut, Kindler and O’Donnell (DFKO)
[
13
]
(see also
[
38
]
for a quantitatively sharp statement in terms of the dependence on
d
).

Theorem 13
.

Fix
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon>0
and let
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be a function satisfying

\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|>d}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\exp\big(-C(d^{2}\log d)/\varepsilon^{2}\big)

(42)

for a large enough universal constant
C>0
. Then, there exists a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\sigma|\leq\tfrac{2^{Cd}}{\varepsilon^{4}}
and a function
g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
depending only on the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}
such that
\|f-g\|_{L_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof of (
23
).

Let
\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}
be the size of the smallest
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
-net on the space of all bounded functions
h:\{-1,1\}^{k_{d,\varepsilon}}\to[-1,1]
, where
k_{d,\varepsilon}=\tfrac{2^{Cd+2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}
, equipped with the
L_{2}
-metric and let
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}}\}
be such a net. For a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
of cardinality
k_{d,\varepsilon}
and
s\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}\}
, define

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad h_{s}^{\sigma}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}h_{s}\big((x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}\big).

(43)

Claim.
The set
\{h_{s}^{\sigma}:\ s=1,\ldots,\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}\ \mbox{and}\ \sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\mbox{ with }|\sigma|=k_{d,\varepsilon}\}
is an
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
-covering of
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
.

Proof.
Indeed, let
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be a function of degree at most
d
. By Theorem
13
, there exists a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\sigma|\leq k_{d,\varepsilon}
and a function
g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
depending only on the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}
such that
\|f-g\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
. Notice that without loss of generality we can assume that
g
takes values in
[-1,1]
as we can otherwise define
\tilde{g}:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
by

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad\tilde{g}(x)=\begin{cases}g(x),&g(x)\in[-1,1]\\
\mathrm{sign}(g(x)),&g(x)\notin[-1,1]\end{cases}

(44)

and observe that
\|\tilde{g}-f\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|g-f\|_{L_{2}}\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
. Therefore, by definition of the covering
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}}\}
there exists
s\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}\}
such that
\|g-h_{s}^{\sigma}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
and hence
\|f-h_{s}^{\sigma}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
\square

To conclude, for every
s
and
\sigma
, choose an arbitrary point
p_{s}^{\sigma}\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
satisfying
\|p_{s}^{\sigma}-h_{s}^{\sigma}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, provided that such exists (in the opposite case the corresponding ball can be omitted from the cover). Then,

\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\subseteq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{s,\sigma}\mathrm{Ball}(h_{s}^{\sigma},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\subseteq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{s,\sigma}\mathrm{Ball}(p_{s}^{\sigma},\varepsilon),

(45)

thus proving that
\mathsf{N}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}},\varepsilon)\leq\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}\binom{n}{k_{d,\varepsilon}}\leq\mathsf{m}_{d,\varepsilon}n^{\frac{2^{Cd+2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3
Fourier concentration and learning

To prove Theorem
2
, we shall employ a modification of the algorithms of
[
31
,
30
]
with one important twist from the analysis of
[
16
]
. We include the argument in full detail for completeness.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Fix a parameter
b\in(0,\infty)
to be determined later and denote by

Q_{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\lceil\frac{2}{b^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\right)\right\rceil.

(46)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q_{b}}
be independent random vectors, each uniformly distributed on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. For a subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|S|\leq d
, consider the empirical Walsh coefficient of
f
given by

\alpha_{S}=\frac{1}{Q_{b}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{Q_{b}}f(X_{j})w_{S}(X_{j}).

(47)

As
\alpha_{S}
is a sum of bounded i.i.d. random variables and
\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{S}]=\hat{f}(S)
, the Chernoff bound gives

\forall\ S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\mbox{ with }|S|\leq d,\qquad\mathbb{P}\big\{|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|>b\big\}\leq 2\exp(-Q_{b}b^{2}/2).

(48)

Therefore, using the union bound, we get

\mathbb{P}\underbrace{\big\{|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|\leq b,\ \mbox{for every subset }S\ \mbox{with}\ |S|\leq d\big\}}_{G_{b}}\geq 1-2\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\exp(-Q_{b}b^{2}/2)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:defQ}}}{{\geq}}1-\delta.

Consider the random collection of sets given by

\mathscr{T}_{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}:\ |S|\leq d\mbox{ and }|\alpha_{S}|\geq 2b\big\}.

(49)

Observe that if the event
G_{b}
holds, then

\forall\ S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}\mbox{ with }|S|\leq d,\qquad|\hat{f}(S)|\leq|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|+|\alpha_{S}|\leq 3b

(50)

and

\forall\ S\in\mathscr{T}_{b},\qquad|\hat{f}(S)|\geq|\alpha_{S}|-|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|\geq b.

(51)

Now, consider the random function
h_{b}:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad h_{b}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sumop\displaylimits_{S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}}\alpha_{S}w_{S}(x)

(52)

and write

\begin{split}\|&h_{b}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\sumop\displaylimits_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}\big|\hat{h}_{b}(S)-\hat{f}(S)\big|^{2}=\sumop\displaylimits_{S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}}|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}\\
&=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\in\mathscr{S}(f)\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\notin\mathscr{S}(f)\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\!\!\!\!\!\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\in\mathscr{S}(f),\ |S|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\!\!\!\!\!\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\notin\mathscr{S}(f),\ |S|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|>d}\hat{f}(S)^{2}.\end{split}

On the event
G_{b}
we then have

\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\in\mathscr{S}(f)\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\!\!\!\!\!\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\in\mathscr{S}(f),\ |S|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:notinS}}}{{\leq}}(9b^{2})\cdot\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq 9b^{2}m.

(53)

On the other hand, as
|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|\leq b\leq|\hat{f}(S)|
for
S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}
, we get

\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\notin\mathscr{S}(f)\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{S}-\hat{f}(S)|^{2}+\!\!\!\!\!\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\notin\mathscr{T}_{b}\\
S\notin\mathscr{S}(f),\ |S|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:inS}}}{{\leq}}\sumop\displaylimits_{S\notin\mathscr{S}(f)}|\hat{f}(S)|^{2}\leq\eta

(54)

by the Fourier concentration property. Combining the above with the assumption that the spectrum of
f
is
t
-concentrated up to degree
d
, we conclude that

\|h_{b}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\eta+t+9b^{2}m\leq\eta+t+\varepsilon

(55)

for
b^{2}\leq\varepsilon/9m
. Plugging this choice of
b
in (
46
), we get the conclusion.
∎

We are now well-equipped to prove Corollary
3
.

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\cap\mathscr{J}_{n,k,\eta}
. Then, there exists a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\sigma|\leq k
and a function
g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
depending only on the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}
such that
\|f-g\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\eta
. Then,
f
is
\eta
-concentrated on the collection
\mathscr{S}(f)=\{S\subseteq\sigma:\ |S|\leq d\}
, as

\sumop\displaylimits_{S\nsubseteq\sigma}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\|f-g\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\eta.

(56)

Similarly, the spectrum of
f
is
\eta
-concentrated up to degree
\min\{d,k\}
and the conclusion of the corollary follows from Theorem
2
since
\#\mathscr{S}(f)=\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{\min\{d,k\}}\binom{k}{r}
.
∎

We emphasize that Corollary
3
does not make any claim about the
running time
required to learn approximate juntas. This is a notoriously difficult problem even for actual juntas that has been investigated in a series of important works, see for instance
[
35
,
27
,
42
]
.

Corollary
4
follows from Corollary
3
combined with a classical theorem of Nisan and Szegedy
[
36
]
, asserting that a Boolean function of degree
d
depends on at most
d2^{d-1}
variables. We note in passing that this result has recently been improved in important work of Chiarelli, Hatami and Saks
[
9
]
(see also
[
44
]
for the best known value of the implicit constant) who derived the optimal conclusion that such a function only depends on
O(2^{d})
variables, but this refinement will be immaterial for our considerations.

Proof of Corollary
4
.

By
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
, we have the set inclusion
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\cap\mathscr{J}_{n,k,0}
where
k=d2^{d-1}
. Therefore, by Corollary
3
, we conclude that

\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{B}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{18}{\varepsilon}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{d}\binom{d2^{d-1}}{r}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\right)\leq\frac{36\cdot d2^{d^{2}}}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right)

(57)

where the last inequality follows by elementary estimates.
∎

We now proceed to prove Corollary
5
, which relies on Theorem
2
and
[
13
]
.

Proof of Corollary
5
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(t)
and
\eta\geq\frac{Cd^{2}{\log d}}{{\log(1/t)}}
so that

\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|>d}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq t\leq\exp\big(-C(d^{2}\log d)/\eta\big).

(58)

Instead of using Theorem
13
directly, we will use a stronger statement from its proof. In
[
13
, p. 405]
, it was shown that there exists a function
h
of degree at most
d
which depends only on the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\sigma}
for a subset
\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\sigma|\leq 2^{O(d)}/\eta^{2}
such that
\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\eta
. Choosing
\mathscr{S}(f)=\{S\subseteq\sigma:\ |S|\leq d\}
, we deduce that
f
is
\eta
-concentrated up to degree
d
and on the collection
\mathscr{S}(f)
. The conclusion follows from Theorem
2
as
\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq 2^{O(d^{2})}/{\eta^{2d}}
.
∎

We note in passing that one can replace the use of the DFKO theorem with a result of O’Donnell and Zhao
[
38
, Corollary 3.5]
to improve the dependence of
\eta
on
d
to
\eta\geq\frac{Cd^{2}}{{\log(1/t)}}
at the expense of an exponentially worse dependence of the complexity on
d
and
\varepsilon
.

Remark 14
.

Choosing
t=0
in Corollary
5
provides a different proof of the main result of
[
16
]
, i.e. that
\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)=O_{d,\varepsilon,\delta}(\log n)
, using the DFKO theorem. Indeed, by Theorem
13
, we have
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}=\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\cap\mathscr{J}_{n,k(d,\eta),\eta}
for any
\eta>0
, where
k(d,\eta)=\big\lceil 2^{Cd}/\eta^{2}\big\rceil
. Plugging this in the bound (
12
) and optimizing over
\eta
, we deduce that there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\forall\ \varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1),\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\frac{2^{Cd^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2d+1}}\log\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right).

(59)

It is worth emphasizing that, while (
59
) captures the correct dependence on the dimension
n
, it is asymptotically worse than the bounds (
3
), (
24
) both as
d\to\infty
and as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

On the other hand, inequality (
24
) is a special case of the bound (
10
), up to lower order terms depending only on the degree
d
. Let
B_{d}=B_{d}^{\{\pm 1\}}
the Bohnenblust–Hille constant of the discrete hypercube. If for a function
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
we define
\mathscr{S}(f)
to be the collection of subsets
S
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
for which
|\hat{f}(S)|\geq\varepsilon^{\frac{d+1}{2}}B_{d}^{-d}
, then we have

\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq\varepsilon^{-d}B_{d}^{\frac{2d^{2}}{d+1}}\sumop\displaylimits_{S\in\mathscr{S}(f)}|\hat{f}(S)|^{\frac{2d}{d+1}}\leq\frac{B_{d}^{2d}}{\varepsilon^{d}}

(60)

and

\sumop\displaylimits_{S\notin\mathscr{S}(f)}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\varepsilon B_{d}^{-\frac{2d}{d+1}}\sumop\displaylimits_{S\notin\mathscr{S}(f)}|\hat{f}(S)|^{\frac{2d}{d+1}}\leq\varepsilon

(61)

by two applications of the Bohnenblust–Hille inequality. Thus (
24
) follows from (
10
) with
t=0
.

To prove Corollary
6
, we will use a deep junta theorem of Bourgain
[
8
, Proposition]
. The quantitative version which we employ below follows from
[

, Theorem 7.1]
(see also
[

,

]
).

Theorem 15
.

Fix
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in(0,1)
. For any Boolean function
f\in\mathscr{B}_{n,d}(t)
and any parameter
\eta\geq\exp\big(C\sqrt{\log(2/t)\log\log d}\big)\big(t\sqrt{d}+\tfrac{1}{2^{d}}\big)
there exists a collection of subsets
\mathscr{S}(f)
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq 2^{O(d^{2})}
such that the spectrum of
f
is
\eta
-concentrated on
\mathscr{S}(f)
.

To see how Theorem
15
follows from
[
23
, Theorem 7.1]
, choose
\beta=2^{-\Omega(d)}
in that statement and consider
\mathscr{S}(f)
to be the collection of subsets
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|S|\leq d
and
S\subseteq J_{\beta}
. The fact that
\#\mathscr{S}(f)\leq 2^{O(d^{2})}
then follows since
|J_{\beta}|\leq 2^{O(d)}
and the Fourier concentration property on
\mathscr{S}(f)
follows from the conclusion of
[
23
, Theorem 7.1]
. We note that the lower order terms on the size of
\eta
with respect to
t,d
can be removed from Theorem

in view of a result of Kindler and O’Donnell

]
at the expense of a worse dependence of
\#\mathscr{S}(f)
on

.

Proof of Corollary
6
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{B}_{n,d}(t)
. If
\eta\geq t^{1+o(1)}d^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}
in the precise sense of Theorem
15
, we have that the spectrum of
f
is
\eta
-concentrated on a collection of subsets with cardinality

. Therefore the conclusion follows from Theorem

since also

as

.
∎

Remark 16
.

It is worth emphasizing that the constraint
\eta\geq t\sqrt{d}
which follows from
[
8
,
24
]
is in some sense optimal if one wishes to learn the class
\mathscr{B}_{n,d}(t)
from logarithmically many samples. A linear threshold function (LTF) is a Boolean function of the form
f(x)=\mathrm{sign}\langle x,\theta\rangle
, where
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a fixed vector. A well-known theorem of Peres
[
39
]
(see also
[
2
]
) asserts that any LTF on
n
variables belongs in
\mathscr{B}_{n,\Omega(1/t^{2})}(t)
for every
t\in(0,1)
. We shall argue that there exist
2^{\Omega(n)}
LTFs which are pairwise
\Omega(1)
-apart which, in view of Proposition
8
, will imply that the class of LTFs requires at least
\Omega(n)
samples to be learned with accuracy
\tfrac{1}{4}
and confidence
\tfrac{3}{4}
. Equivalently, we will show that there exist
N=2^{\Omega(n)}
vectors
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that

\forall\ i\neq j,\qquad\big\|\mathrm{sign}\langle x,\theta_{i}\rangle-\mathrm{sign}\langle x,\theta_{j}\rangle\big\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=8\mathbb{P}\big\{\big(\langle x,\theta_{i}\rangle,\langle x,\theta_{j}\rangle\big)\in U\big\}=\Omega(1),

(62)

where
U
is the second quadrant
\{(s,t):\ s\leq 0\leq t\}
. The corresponding estimate in Gauss space follows from classical computations (see, e.g.,
[
28
, Lemme 1]
) as

\forall\ u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\qquad\big\|\mathrm{sign}\langle g,u\rangle-\mathrm{sign}\langle g,v\rangle\big\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=2-2\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{sign}\big(\langle g,u\rangle\cdot\langle g,v\rangle\big)\big]=2-\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\langle u,v\rangle,

(63)

where
g\sim N(0,\mathsf{Id}_{n})
is a standard Gaussian random vector, and thus it suffices to choose the vectors
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to form an
\Omega(1)
-net in the unit sphere. To pass from the Gaussian statement implied by (
63
) to the corresponding discrete inequality (
62
) we shall use a classical (multivariate) Berry–Esseen theorem (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
). In order to apply this result to the random vectors
(\langle x,\theta_{i}\rangle,\langle x,\theta_{j}\rangle)
, it suffices to find an
\Omega(1)
-separated set
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with
N=2^{\Omega(n)}
points such that
\|\theta_{i}\|_{\ell_{\infty}^{n}}\leq\tau
for some small enough universal constant
\tau>0
. The existence of such a set can be proven by the probabilistic method in view of standard concentration estimates of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-norms on
\ell_{q}^{n}
-spheres, see for instance
[
41
, Remark 2 in p. 223]
and
[
1
, Theorem 1]
.

Finally, we prove Corollary
7
on the complexity of constant depth circuits.

Proof of Corollary
7
.

By the main result of
[
20
]
(which slightly improves
[
31
, Main Lemma]
; see also the exposition in
[
37
, Section 4.5]
), every
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n,d,s}
also belongs in
\mathscr{B}_{n,m(d,s,t)}(t)
for every
t>0
, where
m(d,s,t)=O(\log(s/t))^{d-2}\cdot\log s\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)
. The conclusion follows by choosing

and

and applying Corollary

.
∎

Running time considerations

While all the results of this section estimate the query complexity of various concept classes on the hypercube, it is worth emphasizing that they are also algorithmic in nature. For instance, the algorithm of Theorem
2
(which generalizes the one of
[
16
]
) has effectively the same running time (at least for constant
\varepsilon>0
) as the classical algorithm of Linial, Mansour and Nisan
[
31
]
, yet it offers an exponential improvement to the number of queries which are required as input.

4
Exact learning

In this section, we shall prove Theorem
10
. We start with the deterministic case.

Proof of (
25
).

Let
Q=\mathsf{Q}(\mathscr{F}_{n,d},0)
. For the upper bound on
Q
, consider an enumeration
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
of the points in the closed Hamming
\mathrm{Ball}({\bf 1},d)
, where
{\bf 1}=(1,\ldots,1)
and
k=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j}
.

Claim.
If
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
, then the values
f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{k})
completely determine
f
.

Proof.
As usual, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we denote by

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad\partial_{i}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\ldots,-x_{i},\ldots,x_{n})}{2}

(64)

the discrete partial derivative of
f
. It is straightforward to see that if
f=\sumop\displaylimits_{S}c_{s}w_{S}
, then

\forall\ x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\qquad\partial_{i}f(x)=\sumop\displaylimits_{S:\ i\in S}c_{S}w_{S}(x).

(65)

In particular, if
f
has degree at most
d
and
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}
has cardinality
d
, then

\partial_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\partial_{i_{d}}f({\bf 1})=c_{S}.

(66)

On the other hand, (
64
) implies that
\partial_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\partial_{i_{d}}f({\bf 1})
is a linear combination of
f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{k})
. In other words, knowing
f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{k})
, we can reconstruct the top-order Walsh coefficients
\{c_{S}\}_{|S|=d}
. To conclude, we consider the function
f-\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|=d}c_{S}w_{S}
and iterate.
\square

Therefore, the claim implies that if the algorithm queries the values of
f
at
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
, then the function can be fully reconstructed, thus proving that
Q\leq k=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j}
.

The lower bound is a simple dimension counting argument. Assume, for contradiction, that
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
can be learned exactly using
Q
queries where
Q<k=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j}
. Then, for any fixed points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, the linear system

\forall\ r=1,\ldots,Q,\qquad\sumop\displaylimits_{|S|\leq d}c_{S}w_{S}(X_{r})=0

(67)

with
k
unknowns
\{c_{S}\}_{|S|\leq d}
and
Q
equations has at least one nonzero solution. In other words, there exists a nonzero function
g\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
which vanishes on
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}\}
. If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
are the points queried by the algorithm in order to learn
g
, then (
67
) shows the same points need to be queried to learn the zero function
{\bf 0}
. This is a contradictions as
H
would produce the same hypothesis function for both and
g
is not identically zero.
∎

Finally, we prove the upper bound (
26
) for the random example model.

Proof of (
26
).

For points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
on the hypercube, consider the (linear) evaluation operator
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}:\mathscr{F}_{n,d}\to\mathbb{R}^{Q}
given by
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}(f)=(f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{Q}))
. In order to prove the upper bound on the query complexity of the random example model without error, it suffices to show that if
Q
is large enough and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
are independent and uniformly distributed random vectors on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, then the operator
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}
is injective with high probability. Indeed, if this is the case then the values of any function
f\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}
on a random sequence of samples uniquely determine
f
with high probability and thus the function can be fully reconstructed by solving a system of linear equations with respect to its Walsh coefficients.

To show that
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}
is injective with high probability, fix points
P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and let
X
be a uniform random vector on the hypercube. Suppose that
\Phi_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}}
is not injective and choose a nonzero function
g\in\mathrm{ker}\Phi_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathrm{dim\ ker}\Phi_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{q},X}<\mathrm{dim\ ker}\Phi_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}}\big\}\geq\mathbb{P}\{g(X)\neq 0\}\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}},

(68)

where the last inequality is a classical property satisfied by nonzero functions of degree at most
d
which can be proven inductively, see
[
37
, Lemma 3.5]
.

To conclude the proof, consider
Q>k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j}
and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
be independent uniformly random points on the hypercube. Suppose that the operator
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}
is not injective. Then, at least
Q-k+1
steps in the following chain of inequalities are in fact equalities:

k\geq\mathrm{dim\ ker}\Phi_{X_{1}}\geq\mathrm{dim\ ker}\Phi_{X_{1},X_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq\mathrm{dim\ ker}\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}.

(69)

By inequality (
68
) and the independence of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\big\{\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}\ \mbox{is not injective}\big\}\leq\binom{Q}{Q-k+1}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2^{d}}\big)^{Q-k+1}\leq Q^{k-1}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2^{d}}\big)^{Q-k+1}\leq(2Q)^{k-1}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2^{d}}\big)^{Q}.

Choosing
Q=C2^{d}k\log\big(\frac{k}{\delta}\big)
for a large enough universal constant
C>1
ensures that
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Q}}
is injective with probability at least
1-\delta
, thus completing the proof as
k\leq(d+1)n^{d}
.
∎

Remark 17
.

We point out that the query complexity estimate (
26
) can be realized algorithmically. At every step of the algorithm, one has to compute the rank of the matrix
\Phi_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{q}}
until it becomes full-rank. Then, the unknown function
f
can be recovered by solving a system of linear equations with respect to its Walsh coefficients.
