Sparse Higher Order Čech Filtrations
Mickaël Buchet, Bianca B. Dornelas, Michael Kerber

Abstract
For a finite set of balls of radius r r , the k k -fold cover is the space covered by at least k k balls. Fixing the ball centers and varying the radius, we obtain a nested sequence of spaces that is called the k k -fold filtration of the centers. For k = 1 k=1 , the construction is the union-of-balls filtration that is popular in topological data analysis. For larger k k , it yields a cleaner shape reconstruction in the presence of outliers. We contribute a sparsification algorithm to approximate the topology of the k k -fold filtration. Our method is a combination and adaptation of several techniques from the well-studied case k = 1 k=1 , resulting in a sparsification of linear size that can be computed in expected near-linear time with respect to the number of input points. Our method also extends to the multicover bifiltration, composed of the k k -fold filtrations for several values of k k , with the same size and complexity bounds.

Introduction

Persistent homology

[
11
,
24
,
25
]
is a major branch of topological data analysis with applications, for instance, in shape recognition
[
6
]
, material science
[
38
]
and biology
[
31
,
39
]
. It studies the homological properties of sequences of topological spaces. A standard construction is to take the homogeneous union of balls, with increasing radius, centered at finitely many points of
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
We call these points
sites
and refer to that filtration as the
union-of-balls filtration
. For computational purposes, one considers the homologically equivalent
Čech filtration
, which is a sequence of simplicial complexes that captures the intersection patterns of the balls in the union-of-balls filtration
[
24
, Chap.3]
[
32
]
.

The drawback of the Čech filtration (as well as of the closely-related Vietoris-Rips filtration) is that for
n
sites, it consists of up to
\binom{n}{m+1}

m
-simplices because every
(m+1)
-subset of balls intersects at a sufficiently large radius. A technique to overcome this large size is to
approximate
the Čech (or Vietoris-Rips) filtration with another, much smaller simplicial filtration with similar topological properties. Technically, that means that the persistence modules induced by the homology of the Čech filtration and its approximation are
\epsilon
-interleaved for an arbitrary
\epsilon>0

[
13
]
. Several strategies have been devised to construct such approximations with total size linear in
n
for any fixed
\epsilon
(see related work).
Many of these approaches work by selecting only a subset of the simplices of the Čech filtration, in which case we refer to the approximation as a
sparsification
.

The union-of-balls filtration is a special case of the
k
-fold filtration
built upon the
k
-fold cover
. For
n
sites in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k\geq 1
fixed, the
k
-fold cover is the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
consisting of points contained in at least
k
balls of radius
r
centered at the sites. Besides being a natural extension,
k
-fold filtrations are tightly related with the
k
th neighbor distance that arises in the context of outlier removal and processing of non-homogeneous data densities
[
14
,
27
,
46
,
48
]
. For that reason, they have received increased attention recently, both with regards to computational
[
18
,
26
]
and structural aspects
[
4
]
.

For fixed
k
, the
k
-fold filtration can be equivalently expressed by its nerve, which is a simplicial filtration called the
k
th order Čech filtration
. It captures the intersection patterns of all
k
-wise intersections of balls, which we call
lenses
. The aforementioned size issue for Čech filtrations is even more important in the
k
th order case: the filtration is defined over
\binom{n}{k}
vertices (one for each
k
-subset of sites) and consequently consists of
\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\binom{n}{k}}{m+1}\right)

m
-simplices, making it unrealistic to compute even for small values of
n
. Therefore we need to reduce its size considerably while maintaining a good approximation quality.

The
k
-fold cover and the higher order Čech complexes are also studied with relation to
multiparameter persistence
: considering the order
k
as a second varying parameter, we obtain the
multicover bifiltration
, and the task of approximating the
k
-fold filtration extends to the question of whether there exists a structure that approximates
the entire multicover bifiltration at once (up to some maximal value
\mu
). The natural idea of combining sparsified versions of the
k
th order Čech filtration for different
values of
k
bears the technical problem that the set of vertices in order
k
and
\ell
are disjoint for
k\neq\ell
.
Hence, the approximation scheme must be altered to get compatible approximations for different values of
k
.

Contributions.

We propose the first sparsification of the
k
-fold filtration for a fixed
k
. It is a simplicial filtration that, for
n
sites in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with constant
d
) and a given parameter
\epsilon>0
, is (multiplicatively)
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved with the
k
-fold filtration. The number of
m
-simplices in our sparsification is

\mathcal{O}\left(nk^{k(m+1)}\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{\delta k(m+1)}\right),

(1)

where
\delta
is the doubling dimension of
P
. We point out that for constant
k
and
\epsilon
, the size of the filtration is linear in the number of sites. This is remarkable because the
k
th order Čech filtration, which captures the
k
-fold filtration exactly, already contains
\binom{n}{k}
vertices. Hence our construction avoids including the vast majority of lenses into the sparsification.

We give an output-sensitive algorithm to compute our sparsification up to dimension
m_{max}
in

\mathcal{O}\left(nk\log n\log\Phi+Xk^{k+1}\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{k\delta}\cdot(m_{max}+1)\right)

(2)

expected time. Here
\Phi
is the spread of the point set (i.e., the ratio of diameter and smallest distance of two distinct points)
and
X
is the size of the output complex, upper bounded by (
1
) with
m
replaced by
m_{max}
.
Again considering everything but
n
as constant, we get a running time of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

We extend our approach to approximate the multicover bifiltration that contains the
k
-fold filtrations for all
k
up to some
\mu
.
We compute a simplicial bifiltration that is
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved with the multicover, with the same total size
and the same computational complexity as given in (
1
) and (
2
), respectively,
replacing
k
with
\mu
.

Techniques

The seminal work by Sheehy
[
49
]
was the first one to introduce a sparsification technique for Vietoris-Rips filtrations yielding linear size
and
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
running time (assuming all other parameters as constant). His technique extends to Čech complexes as well with minor adaptations.
Subsequent work
[
7
,
9
,
12
,
21
,
50
]

introduces several extensions, variations, and simplifications of Sheehy’s original sparsification; all these works share essentially the same size and complexity bounds.

Our results are achieved by combining several of these techniques used for approximating in the case
k=1
,
which required non-trivial adaptation for larger values of
k
.
The main idea is that for every site
p
, we define a
removal radius
such that, for radii larger than this removal radius,
all lenses involving
p
are ignored. That means, for larger and larger radii, we construct simplicial complexes with fewer and fewer sites
to keep the size small. To determine the removal radii of sites, we introduce the
k
-distance permutation
which is an ordering of the sites based on the distance to the
k
th closest neighbor. The
k
-distance permutation is a generalization of the farthest point sampling
[
33
]
used in some sparsification schemes
[
21
,
50
]
and induces covering and packing properties analogous to those of
\epsilon
-nets.

The idea of removing a lens beyond a certain radius is justified geometrically by the fact that the remaining lenses cover its entire area after a certain radius.
This is only true, however, if we
freeze
a lens before removing it, that is, keep it unchanged for a short time while the surrounding lenses keep growing.
This concept was already introduced in
[
12
]
,
from where we also adapt the elegant technique of lifting the lenses to convex
cones
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
This lift allows us to reformulate the construction without really removing objects, as removals are not possible in filtrations.

The extension to multicovers up to
\mu
is based on two observations: first, the
\mu
-distance permutation can be used
to approximate all
k
-fold covers for
k=1,\ldots,\mu
simultaneously, with comparable quality bounds. Second,
at level
k
, we do not take the nerve only of all
k
-wise intersections, but of all
\ell
-wise intersections with
k\leq\ell\leq\mu
.
This makes the simplicial filtrations at different levels compatible; perhaps surprisingly, this enlarged nerve does not cause
any asymptotic increase in the size bound and the computation time.

The major geometric predicate for our computation is to determine for a set of growing balls
the smallest radius
r
of intersection.
The aforementioned freezing of lenses makes this problem
technically more challenging, as the balls can grow with different speeds.
The question of how to determine the first intersection in Euclidean space seems to be unaddressed in previous work on sparsifications.
We provide a solution by dualizing to the computation
of the minimal enclosing ball of the ball’s centers
[
23
,
29
,
30
,
42
]
.

Further related work

An alternative line of research by Choudhary et al.
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
defines approximations of Čech complexes
which are not sparsifications. They arrive at slightly improved bounds than the sparsification for
k=1
.
Approximate filtrations are also actively researched in practice
[
3
,
8
,
22
,
41
,
45
]
.

The multicover bifiltration is actively studied as well:
Blumberg and Lesnick
[
4
]
survey different multiparameter persistence approaches and show a particularly strong stability
result for multicovers.
Sheehy
[
48
]
introduces the barycentric bifiltrations, which is equivalent to the multicover but whose size is prohibitively large.
The question of computing the multicover bifiltration exactly has been studied by Edelsbrunner and Osang
[
27
]
, whose results have been refined by Corbet et al.
[
18
]
.
The latter authors obtain an equivalent bifiltration to the multicover one but has total size (over all choices of
k
)
\mathcal{O}(n^{d+1})
for
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
18
, Prop. 5]
.
Their construction rely on using
higher order Voronoi diagrams
and
Delaunay complexes

[
28
]
. That reduces size of Čech complexes, but cannot lead to linear size without further improvements: the Delaunay filtration’s
d
-skeleton is of size
\mathcal{O}(n^{\lceil d/2\rceil})

[
47
]
, which is a substantial improvement over the
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
size of the Čech
d
-skeleton, but still super-linear for
d\geq 3
.
Our approximation foregoes those constructions to reduce the size dependency on
n
further, with the trade-off that we get an exponential dependency on
k
.

Given a point set

an element

is called a

. The

Filtrations and equivalence.

A collection of topological spaces (e.g., subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)

\mathcal{X}=\{X_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
is called a
filtration
if for all
r\leq r^{\prime},
it holds that
X_{r}\subseteq{X}_{r^{\prime}}
.
In this paper, we will use the caligraphic font throughout to denote filtrations.
The letter
r
denotes the
scale parameter
of the filtration.
For
P
and
k
fixed, the previously defined sets
\operatorname{L}_{r}
for
r\geq 0
yield a filtration
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}
, the
k
-fold cover filtration
.

We recall some basic notions from algebraic topology
[
44
, Chap. 9]
[
37
, Chap. 0]
:
Two continuous maps
f,g:X\to Y
between topological spaces are
homotopic
,
f\simeq g
, if there exists a continuous map
H:X\times I\to Y

with
H(\cdot,0)=f
and
H(\cdot,1)=g
(
H
is called a
homotopy
). A map
f:X\to Y
is called a
homotopy equivalence of
X
and
Y
if there exists
a map
g:Y\to X
such that
f\circ g\simeq\operatorname{id}_{Y}
and
g\circ f\simeq\operatorname{id}_{X}
, where
\operatorname{id}_{\cdot}
is the identity function on the corresponding space.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be two filtrations. We say that
\mathcal{X}
is
(homotopy) equivalent
to
\mathcal{Y}
if there exists a family of maps
\{f_{r}\colon X_{r}\rightarrow Y_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
that are homotopy equivalences of spaces and additionally commute with the
inclusion maps of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.

Nerves.

An
abstract simplicial complex

K
over a vertex set
V
is a collection of non-empty subsets, called
simplices
such that whenever a set
\sigma\subseteq V

is in
K
, every non-empty subset of
\sigma
is also in
K

[
24
, Chap. 3]
. Note that an abstract simplicial complex can be geometrically
realized in a sufficiently high-dimensional (Euclidean) space, embedding every abstract simplex as a geometric simplex. Hence, abstract simplicial
complexes are topological spaces. A simplicial complex
K^{\prime}\subseteq K
is called a
subcomplex
of
K
.

For a finite collection
C
of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can define a simplicial complex with vertex set
C
, called the
nerve
of
C
, written as
\operatorname{Nrv}(C)
,
as the set of all subsets of
C

that have a non-empty mutual intersection. Note that the nerve can contain simplices of larger dimension than
d
.
As an example, taking
C:=\{\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)\mid A\in\binom{P}{k}\}
for
r
fixed, we obtain a simplicial complex called the
k
-th order Čech complex with radius
r
.

The importance of the nerve construction stems from the
Nerve Theorem
(also called
Nerve Lemma
),
which we state in a simplified form.
Given a collection
C=\{U^{1},\ldots,U^{m}\}
of closed and convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\bigcup C
the union
of all
U^{i}
. Then, there is a homotopy equivalence between
\bigcup C
and
\operatorname{Nrv}(C)
.
Informally, this means that the nerve of
C
contains the same topological information as the union of the sets.
This is useful, because the nerve is a combinatorial object (an abstract simplicial complex) that is more viable
for computation than a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Nerve theorem extends to filtrations, in the form of the Persistent Nerve Theorem
[
2
, Thm. 3.9]
.
Here, we consider a finite collection of filtrations
\{U^{i}_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
i
ranging from
1
to
m
.
On the one hand, defining
U_{r}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}U^{i}_{r}
, we obtain a filtration of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, called the
union filtration
.
On the other hand, writing
N_{r}:=\operatorname{Nrv}\{U^{i}_{r}\mid i=1,\ldots,m\}
yields a filtration of simplicial complexes,
called the
nerve filtration
. The Persistent Nerve Lemma states that if
U^{i}_{r}
is closed and convex for every choice of
i
and
r
,
then the union filtration and the nerve filtration are equivalent.

As an example, we pick the lenses
\{\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)\}_{r\geq 0}
with
A
ranging over all
k
-subsets of
P
. This yields closed and convex
filtrations, the union filtration is the
k
-fold cover
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}
, and the nerve filtration is the collection of Čech complexes,
called the
k
-th order Čech filtration. The Persistent Nerve Lemma states that these filtrations are equivalent, so the Čech filtration
is a combinatorial description of the
k
-fold cover that we are interested in. Unfortunately, the Čech filtration is too large
for practical purposes.

Geometric notions.

The
doubling constant

\Delta
of a metric space is the minimal integer
\Delta
such that any ball of arbitrary radius
r
can be covered with at most
\Delta
balls of radius
r/2
. The
doubling dimension
of the metric space is
\delta:=\log_{2}\Delta
. The Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}

has a doubling dimension of order
\Theta(d)
and hence constant in this paper.
However, interpreting a finite point set
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a finite metric space, its doubling dimension can be significantly smaller than
d
, for instance if the points all lie close to a low-dimensional subspace.

To cover a ball
B
of radius
r
with balls of radius
r/4,
one needs at most
\Delta^{2}
balls; with balls of radius
r/8
one needs
\Delta^{3}
balls and so on. Thus, to cover
B
with balls of radius
r^{\prime},
we have to find the smallest
t
such that
r/2^{t}\leq r^{\prime}
. That is
t=\lceil\log_{2}r/r^{\prime}\rceil.

Then,

\Delta^{t}\leq\Delta^{\log_{2}r/r^{\prime}+1}=2^{\delta}\left({r}/{r^{\prime}}\right)^{\delta}

and
(2r/r^{\prime})^{\delta}
balls of radius
r^{\prime}
are sufficient to cover
B
.

The
spread
of a point set
P
, denoted by
\Phi
, is the ratio of the diameter of the point set to the shortest distance between two
distinct points in the point set. Note that the spread can be arbitrarily large already for three points and is therefore
a quantity that is independent of the cardinality of
P
. The spread appears often in the analysis of geometric approximation
algorithms that are based on hierarchical grids (such as quadtrees) because it typically determines the number
of resolutions that need to be considered by the approximation algorithm – see, for instance,
[
35
]
for details.

Quadtreaps.

A
quadtreap

[
43
]
is a dynamic data structure for spherical range search in Euclidean space. We summarize its
properties in a simplified form suitable for us: for a set
X
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d
constant), it can be built in
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
expected time.
It supports insertions and deletions of points in
X
in expected
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time.
Moreover, given a query point
q
and a radius
r
, it returns a list
S\subseteq X
which is guaranteed to contain all
points in
X
of distance
\leq r
from
q
, and is guaranteed not to contain any point in
X
of distance
\geq 2r

from
q
. The running time for such a query is
\mathcal{O}(\log n+|S|)
.

3
k
-distance permutation

Given some integer
k\geq 1
and a finite data set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

of
n\geq k
sites, we
define an order on the points in
P
in which the sites
are denoted by
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
. Writing
P_{i}:=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}\}
,
our order ensures that the
k
-fold cover over
P_{i}
approximates
the
k
-fold cover over
P
, with increasing approximation quality
when
i
increases.

The
k
-distance
of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{d}^{k}(x,P)
,
is the distance from
x
to its
k
th closest neighbor in
P
.
We define the
k
-distance permutation
incrementally as follows:
we choose
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
as arbitrary, pairwise distinct sites from
P
.
If
p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1}
are chosen for
k<i\leq n
, we set

p_{i}:=\underset{q\in P\setminus P_{i-1}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\operatorname{d}^{k}(q,P_{i-1}).

Note that for
k=1
, we obtain the well-known farthest point sampling.
We also define

\lambda_{i}:=\operatorname{d}^{k}(p_{i},P_{i-1})

for
k+1\leq i\leq n
and set
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
to
\infty
, so that the sequence
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
is non-increasing.
The next two properties of the
k
-distance permutation are reminiscent of the packing and covering properties of
\epsilon
-nets
[
51
, Chap. 14]
.

Lemma 1

(Covering)
.

Let
\operatorname{L}_{r}|_{P_{i}}
be the
k
-fold cover of
P_{i}
. Then, for all
k\leq i\leq n-1
,

\operatorname{L}_{r}|_{P_{i}}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r+\lambda_{i+1}}|_{P_{i}}.

Proof.

P_{i}\subseteq P
immediately implies
\operatorname{L}_{r}|_{P_{i}}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}.
Consider
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r}.
Then,
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)
for some
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}\subseteq P
. If
A\subseteq P_{i}
, the result follows. Otherwise, without loss of generality let
a_{1}\notin P_{i}
. By definition of
\lambda_{i+1}
,
\operatorname{d}^{k}(a_{1},P_{i})\leq\lambda_{i+1}
and hence there are sites
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\in P_{i}
with
\operatorname{d}(a_{1},b_{j})\leq\lambda_{i+1}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
. Consequently,
\operatorname{d}(x,b_{j})\leq\operatorname{d}(x,a_{1})+\operatorname{d}(a_{1},b_{j})\leq r+\lambda_{i+1}

and the
k
closest sites to
x
in
P_{i}
are within distance
r+\lambda_{i+1}
of
x
, implying
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r+\lambda_{i+1}}|_{P_{i}}
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Packing)
.

For all
k+1\leq i\leq n
, each
p\in P_{i}
has

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i}\setminus\{p\})\geq{\lambda_{i}}/{2}
.

Proof.

We do induction on
i
. For
i=k+1
, let
q
be the
k
th closest neighbor of
p_{k+1}
in
P_{k}
. We have

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p_{k+1},P_{k+1}\setminus\{p_{k+1}\})=\lambda_{k+1}\geq\lambda_{k+1}/2

and, for any
p\in P_{k+1}\setminus\{p_{k+1}\}
,

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{k+1}\setminus\{p\})=\max\limits_{p^{\prime}\in P_{k+1}\setminus\{p\}}\operatorname{d}(p,p^{\prime})\geq\dfrac{\operatorname{d}(p,q)+\operatorname{d}(p,p_{k+1})}{2}\geq\dfrac{\operatorname{d}(q,p_{k+1})}{2}=\dfrac{\lambda_{k+1}}{2}.

Hence the statement is true for
i=k+1
. Next we assume, for some
i\geq k+1
, that for every
p\in P_{i},

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i}\setminus\{p\})\geq\lambda_{i}/2
, and show the statement for
i+1
.

For
p_{i+1}
, we have
\operatorname{d}^{k}(p_{i+1},P_{i+1}\setminus\{p_{i+1}\})=\lambda_{i+1}\geq\lambda_{i+1}/2
and the statement follows.
Consider
p\in P_{i+1}\setminus\{p_{i+1}\}
. If
p_{i+1}
is not among the
k
nearest neighbors of
p
in
P_{i+1}
, then

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i+1}\setminus\{p\})=\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i}\setminus\{p\})\geq\dfrac{\lambda_{i}}{2}\geq\dfrac{\lambda_{i+1}}{2}

by the induction hypothesis and because the
\lambda
-values are non-increasing.
Otherwise,
p_{i+1}
is among the
k
nearest neighbors of
p
in
P_{i+1}\setminus\{p\}
and

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i+1}\setminus\{p\})\geq\operatorname{d}(p,p_{i+1}).

If
\operatorname{d}(p,p_{i+1})\geq\lambda_{i+1}/2
, the claim follows. Otherwise, every site at distance smaller than
\lambda_{i+1}/2
of
p
is at distance smaller than
\lambda_{i+1}
of
p_{i+1}
. Since
\lambda_{i+1}=\operatorname{d}^{k}(p_{i+1},P_{i})
, there can be at most
k-2
sites of
P_{i}\setminus\{p\}
at distance smaller than
\lambda_{i+1}
of
p_{i+1}
.
Thus, counting
p_{i+1}
as well, there can be at most
k-1
sites of
P_{i+1}\setminus\{p\}
at distance smaller than
\lambda_{i+1}/2
of
p
and it follows that
\operatorname{d}^{k}(p,P_{i+1}\setminus\{p\})\geq\lambda_{i+1}/2
.
∎

Computation.

We give an algorithm for computing the
k
-distance permutation that is inspired by the algorithm given by Har-Peled and Mendel
[
36
, Sec. 3.1]

for the case
k=1
. We call a site
ordered
if it has already been assigned its index in the
k
-distance permutation and
unordered
otherwise.

For initialization, pick
k
sites
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
arbitrarily and compute, for each
y\in P\setminus P_{k}
, the distances from
y
to
p_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.
For each
y
, we store these distances in a max-heap
[
19
, Chap. 6]

T_{y}
that also has a fixed label identifying
y
.
Store these elements
T_{y}
in another max-heap
M
, whose priority value for each
T_{y}
is the maximal value in the heap
T_{y}
.
Also, initialize a quadtreap
Q
(see Section
2
) with the
(n-k)
-unordered sites.
Up until this point we need
\mathcal{O}(n(\log n+k\log k))
time.

The algorithm will proceed in iterations, choosing the next
p_{i}
to be ordered in every iteration.
It will maintain the invariant that, at the beginning of an iteration,
M
and
Q
contain exactly one element for each unordered site
y
,
and the corresponding max-heap
T_{y}
in
M
contains the distances to the
k
closest ordered sites from
y
.

To describe an iteration, pick the maximal
T_{y}
in
M
and set
p_{i}:=y
. Let
\lambda_{i}
be the maximal value in
T_{y}
. Remove
T_{y}
from
M
and
y
from
Q
.
Then, query
Q
for
p_{i}
and radius
\lambda_{i}
, obtaining a list
S_{i}
of sites that contains all sites that are at most
\lambda_{i}
away from
p_{i}
, and only contains sites
that are at most
2\lambda_{i}
away from
p_{i}
.
For each site
x\in S_{i}
, add the distance of
x
to
p_{i}
in
T_{x}
. Then, remove the maximal element from
T_{x}
. This ends the description of the algorithm.

For correctness, the invariants imply that the
p_{i}
as chosen is indeed the unordered site with the largest
k
-distance to the set of ordered sites,
as required. It is clear that
M
and
Q
are correctly updated, and for every site
x
in
S_{i}
, the heap
T_{x}
is correctly updated.
It remains to argue that for a site
x\notin S_{i}
, the addition of
p_{i}
to the ordered set does not affect
T_{x}
.
But that follows from the fact that for such an
x
, we have
\operatorname{d}^{k}(x,P_{i-1})\leq\lambda_{i}
,
so there are
k
sites in
P_{i-1}
in distance
\lambda_{i}
or closer to
x
. Since
d(x,p_{i})\geq\lambda_{i}
,
the heap
T_{x}
still contains the
k
closest distances in
P_{i}
.

We proceed to the complexity analysis. In iteration
i
, we have a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
to update
M
and
Q
,
\mathcal{O}(\log n+|S_{i}|)

to generate
S_{i}
and
\mathcal{O}(|S_{i}|\log n)
to update the heaps
T_{s}
(which requires an update of
M
, too). Clearly, the last
complexity dominates the others, and we obtain a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|S_{i}|\log n)
per iteration.
Writing
\Sigma
for sum of all
|S_{i}|
in the algorithm,
the expected running time is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(nk\log k+n\log n+\Sigma\log n)
(with a constant that depends exponentially on
the doubling dimension of
P
). It remains to bound
\Sigma
. Recall that
\Phi
is the spread of
P
.

Lemma 3
.

Every site appears in at most
\mathcal{O}(k\log\Phi)
sets
S_{i}
.

Proof.

Consider the interval
(2^{s},2^{s+1}]
for some integer
s
and the sites
p_{i},\ldots,p_{j}
with
i\leq j

with
\lambda_{i},\ldots,\lambda_{j}\in(2^{s},2^{s+1}]
. Note that there are at most
\log\Phi
intervals of that form
with at least one
\lambda
-value inside. Thus it is enough to show that each site appears in at most
\mathcal{O}(k)
sets among
S_{i},\ldots,S_{j}
.

Let
p
be an arbitrary site and fix
i\leq\ell\leq j
, meaning that
\lambda_{\ell}\in(2^{s},2^{s+1}]
. To be in the list
S_{\ell}
,
p
must be at distance at most
2\cdot 2^{s+1}
from
p_{\ell}
.
Since this holds for all
i\leq\ell\leq j
, all sites among
p_{i},\ldots,p_{j}
for which
p
is reported lie in a ball
B
of radius
2^{s+2}
(centered at
p
).
The (discrete) ball
B\cap P
can be covered by
32^{\delta}=\mathcal{O}(1)
(discrete) balls of radius
2^{s-2}
(see
Doubling Dimension
in Section
2
). We show next that every such ball of radius
2^{s-2}
contains at most
k
sites of
p_{i},\ldots,p_{j}
, implying the desired bound.
Let
B^{\prime}
be one the balls of radius
2^{s-2}
covering
B
and
p_{\ell}
be inside
B^{\prime}
. If the
k
closest sites of
p_{\ell}
in
P_{j}
are also in
B^{\prime}
, we have that

\operatorname{d}^{k}(p_{\ell},P_{j}\setminus\{p_{\ell}\})\leq 2\cdot 2^{s-2}=\dfrac{2^{s}}{2}<\dfrac{\lambda_{j}}{2}
, which is a contradiction to the Packing property (Lemma
2
).
∎

The lemma yields the bound
\Sigma=\mathcal{O}(nk\log\Phi)
, which implies our final complexity bound.

Theorem 4
.

The
k
-distance permutation can be computed in expected time

\mathcal{O}(nk\log n\log\Phi),

with
\Phi
the spread of the point set.

A sparse union of lenses

In the following two sections, we will
define several filtrations to arrive at our
final approximation.

and
r\in(\operatorname{frz}\left(d\right),(1+\epsilon)\operatorname{frz}\left(d\right)]

Recall that the
k
-fold cover is defined as the union of all lenses at radius
r
, where every lens is given by
k
sites.
For large values of
r
, most of these lenses intersect, yielding a size explosion in their nerve.
At the same time, many lenses are eventually covered by the union of other lenses and so may be removed from consideration.

To define the precise threshold for removal of a lens, recall that in Section
3

we ordered the sites as
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
and obtained values
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}=\infty,\,\lambda_{k+1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}
.
Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.
The
freezing radius
of a site
p_{i}
is

\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right):=\dfrac{(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{i}}{\epsilon}.

We extend the definition to lenses by setting

\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)=\min\limits_{p\in A}\operatorname{frz}\left(p\right).

Then at radius
r
we only consider lenses whose freezing radius is at least
r
and set

U_{r}:=\bigcup_{\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\geq r}\operatorname{L}_{r}(A).

Notice that
\{U_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
is
not
a filtration: Figure
3

illustrates that
U_{r}
might not be a subset of
U_{r^{\prime}}
for
r<r^{\prime}
. Even so,
we show a useful result to relate
\operatorname{L}_{r}
and
U_{r}
.

Lemma 5
.

U_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}\subseteq U_{(1+\epsilon)r}.

Proof.

The first inclusion is clear. For the second inclusion, consider
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r}
and let
i
be the maximal index such that

r\leq\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\epsilon}.

If
i=n
, then there is
A\subseteq P_{n}
with
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)
because
x\in\operatorname{L}_{r}
and
P=P_{n}
. By definition of the freezing radius and
inequality
(\ast)
,

\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\geq\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)=(1+\epsilon)\lambda_{i}/\epsilon\geq r(1+\epsilon)
and thus
\operatorname{L}_{r(1+\epsilon)}(A)\subseteq U_{(1+\epsilon)r}
. Since
\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{(1+\epsilon)r}(A)
, the result follows.

For
i<n
, notice that the Covering Property (Lemma
1
) guarantees that

x
is contained in a lens
\operatorname{L}_{r+\lambda_{i+1}}(A)
for some
A\subseteq P_{i}
.
Since
i
is maximal,
\lambda_{i+1}/\epsilon<r
and so

\operatorname{L}_{r+\lambda_{i+1}}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{(1+\epsilon)r}(A)
.
Moreover,
A\subseteq P_{i}
and inequality
(\ast)
imply
\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\geq\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)\geq(1+\epsilon)r
.
Hence the lens of
A
contributes to
U_{(1+\epsilon)r}

and as it contains
x
, the statement follows.
∎

We next adjust the definition of
U_{r}
to obtain an actual filtration.
To do that, we slightly delay the removal of lenses. More precisely, we define

\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A):=\begin{cases}\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)&r<\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\\[6.45831pt]
\operatorname{L}_{\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)}(A)&\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\leq r\leq(1+\epsilon)\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)\\[6.45831pt]
\emptyset&(1+\epsilon)\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)<r.\end{cases}

We will sometimes call
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
a
freezing lens
and
\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)
a
non-freezing lens
.
One can visualize the evolution of a freezing lens as a continuous process for increasing
r
: the lens
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}
grows until it reaches its freezing radius and remains unchanged (it is “frozen”) for the interval
[\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right),(1+\epsilon)\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)]
. Afterwards it completely disappears. We call
(1+\epsilon)\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)
the
removal radius
of
A
. The construction is an adaptation of a similar one by Sheehy
[
49
]
.

We write
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}
for the union of
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
over all
A\in\binom{P}{k}
and
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}:=\{\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
.

Lemma 6
.

\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
is a filtration, i.e., for any
r\leq r^{\prime},

\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

If the interval
(r,r^{\prime}]
does not contain any removal radius,

\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r^{\prime}}
because the inclusions hold lens-wise. Since the number of different removal radii is bounded by the number of sites and hence finite, it suffices to show that at a removal radius
s
, any lens that is removed is already covered by lenses that are not being removed at
s
.
In fact, we show that such a lens is covered by lenses that are not yet frozen at
s
.

Let
A
be the
k
-subset associated with a lens being removed at
s
and
x\in\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{s}(A)
.
By definition,
s=(1+\epsilon)t
with
t=\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)
.
This implies that
x\in\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{t}(A)=\operatorname{L}_{t}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{t}
because the lens is frozen from radius
t
on.
By Lemma
5
, it follows that
x\in U_{(1+\epsilon)t}=U_{s}
, and therefore
x
is contained in a lens
\operatorname{L}_{s}(B)
with
\operatorname{frz}\left(B\right)\geq s
.
Thus
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{s}(A)
is covered by lenses
\operatorname{L}_{s}(B)
with
\operatorname{frz}\left(B\right)\geq s
and
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
is a filtration.
∎

Lemma 7
.

\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}
are
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved.

Proof.

We show that
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{(1+\epsilon)r}
.
The first inclusion is clear by definition. For the second inclusion, observe that
U_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}

follows directly from their definition. Then, Lemma
5

yields
\operatorname{L}_{r}\subseteq U_{(1+\epsilon)r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{(1+\epsilon)r}
.
∎

5
A sparse simplicial filtration

Since
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
is closed and convex for every
r
,
the nerve of all (non-empty)
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
yields
a simplicial complex homotopically equivalent the
k
-fold cover at radius
r
because of the Nerve Theorem.
However, the collection of simplicial complexes obtained when varying
r
does not form a filtration
because simplices disappear from the nerve when passing
a removal radius.

Cones.

We overcome the problem of removals of lenses by adapting a construction of Cavanna et al.
[
12
]
.
The idea is to “stack-up” the lenses
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
for all radii:
the
cone
of
A
at radius
r
is

\operatorname{C}_{r}(A):=\underset{\alpha\in[0,r]}{\bigcup}\left(\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{\alpha}(A)\times\{\alpha\}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}.

We write
\operatorname{C}_{r}
for the union of
\operatorname{C}_{r}
over all
A\in\binom{P}{k}
. Figure
4
shows an example.

Figure 4:
A cone representing the evolution of a lens for
k=2.
At first the lens grows, until it is frozen. Then, the lens has static size and afterwards it disappears. At the radius where the lens disappears, it is completely covered by other lenses (which are not displayed in the figure).

Lemma 8
.

The filtrations
\operatorname{\mathcal{C}}=\{\operatorname{C}_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
and
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
are equivalent.

Proof.

In the same sense as above
\operatorname{C}_{r}
is a stacked-up version of
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\leq r
, and we can consider
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}

as a subspace of
\operatorname{C}_{r}
via the map
x\mapsto(x,r)
for
x\in\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}
. Since
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
is a filtration,
there is a strong deformation retraction
[
37
, Chap. 0]
from
\operatorname{C}_{r}
to
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}
, given by
(x,\alpha),t\mapsto(x,(1-t)\alpha+tr)
,
which naturally commutes with the canonical inclusions. The result follows.
∎

We define the nerve of the cones as the
sparse
k
th order Čech complex
,

\operatorname{S}_{r}:=\operatorname{Nrv}\left\{\operatorname{C}_{r}\mid A\in\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{P}{k}\right)\right\}.

\operatorname{S}_{r}
is a subcomplex
of the
k
th order Čech complex for every
r
because
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}
.
Moreover, if
r\leq r^{\prime}
,
\operatorname{C}_{r}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{C}_{r^{\prime}}(A)
for all
A
. Hence
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}=\{\operatorname{S}_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
is a filtration. By the Persistent Nerve Theorem
[
2
, Thm. 3.9]
and Lemmas
7
and
8
we obtain:

Lemma 9
.

The filtrations
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
and
\operatorname{\mathcal{C}}
are equivalent.
As a consequence,
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved with the
k
-fold filtration.

Discretization of the radius.

The filtration
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is challenging to compute, due to the freezing of lenses.
We elaborate on these issues in Section
7
. We now define a variant of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}

which is easier to compute and still is an
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved with the
k
-fold filtration.

Recall that the filtrations
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{C}}
and
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}

are defined based on the freezing radii of sites, which depend on a parameter
\epsilon>0
.
To obtain a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation for
\epsilon\in(0,1]
in the end, we consider the above construction of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}

with parameter
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon}{3}
, obtaining a
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})
-homotopy-interleaving
with the
k
-fold filtration.

Next, for every
r\geq 0
, let
z\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{z}\leq r<(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{z+1}

and define

D_{r}:=\operatorname{S}_{(1+\epsilon/3)^{z}}.

We call
\mathcal{D}:=\{D_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
the
discrete sparse
k
th order Čech filtration
.
It is formed by a discrete set of snapshots of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
and kept unchanged except when passing over a snapshot radius
(this is also referred to as the
Left Kan extension
of a discrete filtration
[
40
, Chap. 10]
).

Theorem 10
.

\mathcal{D}
is a
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaving of the
k
-fold filtration.

Proof.

From the definition,
D_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{S}_{r}\subseteq D_{(1+\epsilon/3)r}
,
so
\mathcal{D}
is a
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})
-(homotopy)-interleaving of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
.
Since
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is a
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})
-homotopy-interleaving of the
k
-fold filtration,
we get that
\mathcal{D}
is a
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{2}
-homotopy-interleaving of the
k
-fold filtration.
The result follows by noting that
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{2}\leq 1+\epsilon
for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.
∎

6
Size analysis

We bound the size of
\mathcal{D}
, i.e., the number of simplices it contains. Since
D_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{S}_{r}

for all
r\geq 0,
it is enough to bound the size of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
with parameter
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/3.
Let

\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A):=\bigcup_{r\geq 0}\operatorname{C}_{r}(A)

be the
cone of
A
(without dependence on a radius).
Then, the size of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
equals the size of the nerve of the cones
\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A)
, where
A
ranges over all
k
-subsets of sites.
However, the number of vertices is not necessarily
\binom{n}{k}
because many cones are empty:
this happens in particular when the smallest radius for which the balls around the sites of
A
intersect
is larger than the removal radius of the lens. In fact, our argument shows that this is the case for the vast majority
of cones. The proof for vertices extends readily to the case of
m
-wise intersections of cones, i.e. for
(m-1)
-simplices, without change and thus
we treat the general case directly.

For fixed
m\geq 1,
we derive an upper bound for the number of sets
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}

such that the cones
\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m})
intersect.
Such sets are in one-to-one correspondence to the
(m-1)
-simplices of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
, hence we refer to these sets as
(m-1)
-simplices.
Let
\sigma=\{\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m})\}
be such a
(m-1)
-simplex and the set of sites
P
be ordered according to the
k
-distance permutation.
We call a site
p_{i}

involved
in
\sigma
if
p_{i}
belongs to one of the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
.
Note that there are at most
km
sites involved in
\sigma
. We say that
\sigma
is
associated
to a site
p_{i}
if
p_{i}
is involved in
\sigma
and all other involved sites have index smaller than
i
.
Our strategy is to upper bound the number of
(m-1)
-simplices in
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
associated to an arbitrary
p_{i}
.

Fix
i\geq k+1
and
\omega_{i}:=(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)<\infty
.
Let
B
denote the ball of radius
2\omega_{i}
centered at
p_{i}
.

Lemma 11
.

If
\sigma:=\{\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m})\}
is an
(m-1)
-simplex in
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
associated to
p_{i}
, then all sites involved in
\sigma
are contained in
B
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
denote the minimal radius such that all the balls around sites involved in
\sigma
intersect. This is the radius of the minimum enclosing ball of the involved sites.
Any common intersection of the cones must happen at scale
r\geq\alpha.

On the other hand, assume wlog that
p_{i}\in A_{1}
. Since
p_{i}
has maximal index in
A_{1}
, we have that
\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{1}\right)=\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
. Hence the removal radius of
A_{1}
is equal to
\omega_{i}
and it follows that the cone of
A_{1}
is empty for all radii greater than
\omega_{i}
.
Therefore any common intersection of the cones of
\sigma
must happen at scale
r\leq\omega_{i}
.
Hence, as we assume that the cones do intersect, we must have that
\alpha\leq\omega_{i}
.
It follows that for any involved site
q
,
\operatorname{d}(q,p_{i})\leq 2\alpha\leq 2w_{i}
.
∎

Hence the involved sites of
\sigma
are close to
p_{i}
in the sense of the lemma. We can furthermore guarantee that the points of
P_{i}
are not too densely packed in
B
using the packing property of the
k
-distance permutation.

Lemma 12
.

The ball
B
contains at most

\hypertarget{link_sites-bound}{\Gamma}:=k\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{\delta}

sites of
P_{i}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}\}
, where
\delta
is the doubling dimension of
P
.

Proof.

We cover
B\cap P
by balls of radius
{\lambda_{i}}/{4}
. That can be done with at most

\zeta=\left(\dfrac{16(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)^{\delta}

balls (see
Doubling Dimension
in Section
2
). By the Packing Lemma
2
, each open ball of radius
\lambda_{i}/4
contains at most
k
sites of
P_{i}
, thus the total number of sites in
B
is at most
k\zeta
. The bound follows because
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon}{3}
and
\epsilon\leq 1
, hence

(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}\leq\frac{16}{9}<2
.
∎

Bounding the number of non-empty
m
-intersections of cones is now a matter of simple combinatorics.

Theorem 13
.

The number of
(m-1)
-simplices of
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is at most

n\cdot\Gamma^{km}=nk^{km}\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{\delta km}.

Proof.

Fix
p_{i}
with
i\geq k+1
. Every
(m-1)
-simplex associated to
p_{i}
with non-empty cone intersection has up to
km
involved sites,
which all lie in
B
by Lemma
11
. Moreover, all involved sites are in
P_{i}
and, by Lemma
12
,
there are at most
\Gamma
of those sites in
B
to choose from. It follows that there are at most
\Gamma^{km}
different choices
possible. This upper bound holds for every
p_{i}
with
i\geq k+1
, so multiplying by the number of sites
(n-k)
yields a bound of

(n-k)\Gamma^{km}
for simplices associated to
p_{i}
with
i\geq k+1
.

Finally, note that no simplex gets associated to
p_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, and the only simplex associated to
p_{k}
is the vertex defined by
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
.
Hence the total size bound is
(n-k)\Gamma^{km}+1
, and since
\Gamma\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
, the result follows.
∎

We remark that the bounds on this section are not tight and slightly better ones could be easily achieved, by keeping binomials in place or avoiding some approximations. However the improvements would be minor.

7
Computation

We now present an algorithm to construct the discrete sparse Čech filtration. As in the previous sections, let us fix a finite set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an integer
k>0

and
\epsilon\in(0,1]
. Assume that
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
has the indices ordered with respect to the
k
-distance permutation, and that we have computed the corresponding values
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
as discussed in Section
3
.
The algorithm outputs the discrete sparse
k
th order Čech filtration
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
as a list of simplices with their corresponding
critical value
, i.e., the smallest parameter value
r

for which the simplex is part of the filtration. Note that by definition of the discrete sparse Čech filtration, every critical value is of the form
(1+\epsilon/3)^{z}
for some integer
z
.
Our algorithm follows the approach and notation of Section
6
.

Friends and vertices

The first step is to find,
among
p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1}
, all sites of distance at most
2\omega_{i}
from
p_{i}
,
where
\omega_{i}=(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
(compare Lemma
11
).
We call these points
friends
of
p_{i}
.

We compute friends using the quadtreap data structure introduced in Section
2
:
we initialize the quadtreap with the first
k
sites
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
of the permutation.
For every site
p_{k+1},\ldots,p_{n}
in order, we query the quadtreap for
p_{i}
with radius
2\omega_{i}
,
obtaining a candidate list
F_{i}
of friends of
p_{i}
. We traverse the list
F_{i}
and remove
those “false friends”
x
from the list for which
d(p_{i},x)>2\omega_{i}
.
Then, we add
p_{i}
to the quadtreap.

It is straight-forward to see that the (expected) cost of one iteration is
O(\log n+|F_{i}|)
.
All points in
F_{i}
have distance at most
4\omega_{i}
from
p_{i}
by the property of the quadtreap,
and by the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
12
,
the number of sites reported for
p_{i}
is at most
k\left(\dfrac{192}{\epsilon}\right)^{\delta}=\mathcal{O}(k(1/\epsilon)^{\delta})
.
Thus we get the friends of
p_{i}

for all sites
p_{i}
in expected time

\mathcal{O}(n\log n+nk(1/\epsilon)^{\delta})
.
Note that the number of friends is bounded by
Γ
as defined in Lemma
12
.

The next step is to compute the vertices associated to
p_{i}
that enter the filtration, for each
i\geq k
.
We proceed by brute-force, just enumerating all
k
-tuples formed by
p_{i}
and
k-1
of its friends and checking for every
k
-tuple whether their cone is non-empty. The last condition is simple to check, as the cone is non-empty if and only if the radius
\alpha
of the minimum enclosing ball of the
k
sites
is at most
\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
. In this case, the critical radius of the vertex is set to
(1+\epsilon/3)^{z}
, where
z
is the smallest integer such that
(1+\epsilon/3)^{z}\geq\alpha
.
Computing
\alpha
and
z
per vertex requires
\mathcal{O}(k)
in expectation
[
20
, Chap. 4.7]
.
Hence we calculate all vertices in
\mathcal{O}((n-k)k\Gamma^{k-1})
time. We summarize:

Lemma 14
.

We can compute all friends of sites and vertices of
\mathcal{D}
in expected time

\mathcal{O}(n\log n+nk(1/\epsilon)^{\delta}+(n-k)k\Gamma^{k-1}).

Intersections of cones

To treat the case of higher-dimensional simplices, we discuss the major predicate required:
Given subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m+1}
of sites, decide whether their cones
(\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m+1}))

intersect, and if they do, compute the smallest radius
r
for which they intersect.
Answering this question is technically challenging because the cones might first intersect for a radius where one or several of the associated lenses are frozen.
Therefore, unlike for the question whether a single cone is empty or not, one cannot resolve this question in general by a simple minimal enclosing ball computation on the involved sites.

Nevertheless, that minimal enclosing ball of the involved sites yields partial information.
Let
r_{MEB}
denote the radius of the minimum enclosing ball of the sites in
A_{1}\cup\ldots\cup A_{m+1}
.
Let
\Lambda
denote the smallest freezing time among
\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{1}\right),\ldots,\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{m+1}\right)
.
There are two simple cases.

r_{MEB}\leq\Lambda
:

In this case,
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r_{MEB}}(A_{i})=\operatorname{L}_{r_{MEB}}(A_{i})
for all
i
and they intersect at radius
r_{MEB}
.
This means that
\sigma
belongs to the filtration and its critical value is the minimal value of the form
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}
that is greater or equal to
r_{MEB}
.

(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda\leq r_{MEB}
:

Here, the non-freezing lenses only intersect for a radius greater than the removal radius of one lens.
Since the freezing lenses are subsets of their non-freezing counterparts, it follows that
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A_{m+1})
do not intersect before one of them becomes empty. Hence
\sigma
does not belong to the filtration in this case.

The remaining and most challenging case is if
\Lambda<r_{MEB}\leq(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
. To manage this case, we first consider a more general geometric problem:
given balls
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}
with centers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
and radii
r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}
, decide whether
\beta_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\beta_{\ell}\neq\emptyset

(\star)
.
Assume without loss of generality that all radii
r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}
lie between
0
and
1

(otherwise, scale the instance). Now, define
\beta_{j}^{\prime}
as the ball with center
x_{j}
and radius
1-r_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq\ell
. We use the following geometric predicate to deal with this problem.

Lemma 15
.

The balls
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}
have a common intersection point if and only if there exists a ball of radius
1
that contains
\beta_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume there is a ball
B_{1}(c)
that contains each
\beta_{j}^{\prime}
. Then
d(c,x_{j})+(1-r_{j})\leq 1
and thus
d(c,x_{j})\leq r_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq\ell
. It follows that
c\in\beta_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq\ell
, implying “
\Leftarrow
”. The argument can be reversed to show the equivalence.
∎

Next, consider the following question: given
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m+1}
and a fixed radius
r
,
decide whether the corresponding cones intersect at level
r
, or equivalently, whether

\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A_{m+1})\neq\emptyset
.
We can easily reduce this problem to
(\star)
, since the intersection is simply an intersection
of (up to
k(m+1)
) balls whose radius is either
r
or
\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{i}\right)
, and we can compute this radius
easily since we know
\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{i}\right)
. We refer to this predicate as
checking the cones of
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}

at radius
r
.

Now, we handle the case of
\Lambda<r_{MEB}\leq(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
as follows: We first check the cones
at radius
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
.
If there is no intersection,
\sigma
does not belong to the filtration.
If there is an intersection,
\sigma
is added to the filtration.
To determine its critical value, let
z
be the smallest integer such that
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}\geq\Lambda
.
Check the cones at radius
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}
. If they intersect, set the critical value of
\sigma
to be
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}
.
Otherwise, set it to
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}
. This ends the description of the algorithm to determine whether
\sigma

belongs to the filtration.

We argue about the correctness: first, note that
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
is the maximum value for which no freezing lens is empty.
This implies that if the cones are intersecting at all, they intersect at level
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
.
Hence, checking the cones at
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
decides whether
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
or not.

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
, by definition of the filtration,
the critical value is necessarily an integer power of
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Note that
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}\geq(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\Lambda
, so the cones

(C_{(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}}(A_{1}),\ldots,C_{(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}}(A_{m}))

intersect, implying that the critical value is at most
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}
.
On the other hand, by the choice of
z
,
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z-1}<\Lambda<r_{MEB}
, where the last inequality
comes from our case distinction. It follows that the non-freezing lenses at radius
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z-1}

do not intersect, and neither do the freezing lenses because they are subsets of their non-freezing counterparts.
This implies that the critical value of
\sigma

is larger than
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z-1}
. Combing these two results, we obtain that the critical value
is either
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}
or
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z+1}
, and the algorithm decides this by checking the cones
at
(1+\epsilon^{\prime})^{z}
.

The above procedure requires to compute one minimum enclosing ball (to compute
r_{MEB}
), and two checks of cones at different radii,
which reduce to two instances of problem
(\star)
.
Problem
(\star)
, in turn, can be answered by computing the
minimum enclosing ball of balls

[
30
]

using Lemma
15
.
For constant dimension, the expected running time for computing the minimum enclosing ball of
\ell
balls is
\mathcal{O}\left(\ell\right)
,
the same as for standard minimum enclosing ball computations.
Hence, we obtain

Lemma 16
.

Given
\sigma=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})
, deciding whether
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
and determining its critical value
can be done in
\mathcal{O}\left(km\right)
.

We point out that we were not able to find an algorithm to compute the critical value in
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
instead of
\mathcal{D}
,
which is the reason why we introduced the latter in Section
5
.
One could approximate the smallest
r
in
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
arbitrarily closely by combining the minimum enclosing ball method described above and a binary search,
but this appear to make the approximation scheme more complicated, and the result would still only approximate
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
,
as our approach does.

Higher dimensional simplices

We now describe how to determine the higher-dimensional simplices associated to
p_{i}
in an output-sensitive fashion.
We proceed inductively by dimension, up to a maximal dimension
m_{max}
.
Fix a
(m-1)
-simplex
\sigma^{\prime}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}
associated to
p_{i}
. We compute all cofacets of
\sigma^{\prime}
in the filtration, that is, all

m
-simplices that contain
\sigma^{\prime}
and one further vertex
A_{m+1}
.
Note that we could avoid some repetitions by fixing a global order on the
k
-subsets and only check tuples
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m+1})
that are
increasing in this order, but the saving would be negligible in terms of complexity.

Since each element of
A_{m+1}
is either a friend of
p_{i}
or
p_{i}
itself,
we enumerate all
k
-subsets consisting of these sites.
Writing
A_{m+1}
for such a subset, we check whether the cones of
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},A_{m+1})

intersect, using the algorithm of the previous subsection. If yes, we add the corresponding

m
-simplex with its critical value to
\mathcal{D}
.
Note that if that happens,
A_{m+1}
is necessarily a vertex of
\mathcal{D}
,
as otherwise, the cone of
A_{m+1}
is empty
and so is the intersection, of the cones of
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m+1})
.

The number of
k
-subsets consisting of
p_{i}
’s friends is bounded by
\binom{\Gamma}{k}
, where we recall that
Γ
bounds the number of friends.
Therefore, using Lemma
16
, the expected running time spent checking for cofacets is

\mathcal{O}(mk\Gamma^{k})
per
(m-1)
-simplex of the filtration.
Doing this over all simplices of the filtration, up to dimension
m_{max}
, yields a total expected complexity of

\mathcal{O}(X\cdot(m_{max}+1)\cdot k\cdot\Gamma^{k})

for the step of computing higher dimensional simplices, where
X
is the total number of simplices in the filtration.
Recalling the bound of Lemma
14
for the complexity for computing friends and vertices, we obtain a total complexity of

\mathcal{O}\left(n\log n+\frac{nk}{\epsilon^{\delta}}+(n-k)k\Gamma^{k-1}+X\cdot(m_{max}+1)\cdot k\cdot\Gamma^{k}\right)

(3)

for computing all simplices of
\mathcal{D}
and their critical values, assuming that the
k
-distance permutation is computed.

We next show that the second and third summand in the bound are dominated by the last term. We first show a simple lower bound for
X
:

Lemma 17
.

The number of output simplices is
X\geq n-k+1
.

Proof.

For every
p_{i}
with
i\geq k
, consider the
k
-subset
A_{i}
consisting of
p_{i}
and its
(k-1)
-closest neighbors in
P_{i-1}
.
Then
A_{k}
is the
k
-subset
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
which is the unique vertex of
\mathcal{D}
that does not freeze.
For
i>k
, the distance of
p_{i}
to every site in
A_{i}
is at most
d^{k-1}(p_{i},P_{i-1})\leq d^{k}(p_{i},P_{i-1})=\lambda_{i}
.
That means that
\operatorname{L}_{\lambda_{i}}(A_{i})
is not empty.
On the other hand,
\operatorname{frz}\left(A_{i}\right)=\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)=\frac{(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\lambda_{i}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}>\lambda_{i}
, so the lens is not frozen until
\lambda_{i}
.
This implies that
A_{i}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{D}
, and since
A_{k},\ldots,A_{n}
are pairwise disjoint, the bound follows.
∎

The lemma implies immediately that the third term is dominated by the fourth. For the second term, note that
n\leq 2(n-k+1)k

by an elementary argument, hence
\frac{nk}{\epsilon^{\delta}}\leq(n-k+1)k^{2}\frac{2}{\epsilon^{\delta}}\leq Xk\Gamma
,
since
\Gamma\geq k\frac{2}{\epsilon^{\delta}}
.

Hence, (
3
) together with the algorithm from Section
3
yields the final result:

Theorem 18
.

Given a set
P
of
n
points,
k\geq 1
and
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, the discrete sparse
k
th order Čech filtration
\mathcal{D}

up to dimension
m_{max}
can be computed in time

\mathcal{O}\left(nk\log n\log\Phi+Xk^{k+1}(m_{max}+1)\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{k\delta}\right),

where
\Phi
is the spread of
P
and
X
is the total number of simplices in the filtration.

8
Approximating multicovers

We remove the restriction of
k
being a fixed parameter.
Let us write
\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}
instead of
\operatorname{L}_{r}
for the
k
-fold cover,
to emphasize the dependence on
k
. From the definition of lenses, it holds that

A\subseteq B
implies
\operatorname{L}_{r}(B)\subseteq\operatorname{L}_{r}(A)
and hence

\operatorname{L}^{(k^{\prime})}_{r^{\prime}}\subseteq\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}

whenever
k^{\prime}\geq k
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
.
The collection of
\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}
for
1\leq k\leq n
and
r\geq 0
is called
the
multicover
of a point set. For a fixed
\mu\geq 1
, if we restrict
the values of
k
between
1
and
\mu
, we call the collection
the
multicover up to
\mu
.

To define and compute a simplicial approximation of the multicover up to
\mu
, we need to extend some further definitions of Section
2
.
A collection of topological spaces

\mathcal{X}=(X_{r,k})_{r\geq 0,k\in\{1,\ldots,\mu\}}

is called a
bifiltration
(with index set
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\times\{1,\ldots,\mu\}
)
if whenever
k^{\prime}\geq k
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
,
X_{r^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\subseteq X_{r,k}
.
The multicover up to
\mu
is a bifiltration.
We call two bifiltrations
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

(homotopy) equivalent

if there is a family of maps
\{f_{r,k}:X_{r,k}\to Y_{r,k}\}_{r\geq 0,k\in\{1,\ldots,\mu\}}

that are homotopy equivalences of spaces and additionally commute with the inclusion maps
of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.
We call two bifiltrations
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved
if for every
(r,k)
, we have
Y_{r,k}\subseteq X_{r,k}\subseteq Y_{(1+\epsilon)r,k}
;
in other words, the restricted filtrations for
k
fixed are
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved.
We call
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved
if there exist bifiltrations
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

equivalent to
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
equivalent to
\mathcal{Y}
such that
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}

are
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved. We remark that this notion of
(1+\epsilon)
-(homotopy-)interleaving is stronger than
the usual definition of interleaving
[
13
]
. Moreover, it implies
that the induced
2
-parameter persistence modules of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved,
which is the common notion of closeness of two data sets in the context of multi-parameter persistence.

Given a point set
P
, this section aims
to define a simplicial bifiltration of small size that is an
(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved
with the multicover up to
\mu
of
P
.

Freezing lenses revisited.

Recall that our approach in Section
4
(for fixed
k
) yields the
filtration
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}^{(k)}
that is
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved with
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}^{(k)}
.
However,
\left\{\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}^{(k)}\right\}_{r\geq 0,k\in\{0,\ldots,\mu\}}
does
not
form a bifiltration, because
for
k^{\prime}\geq k
and
r\geq 0
,
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k^{\prime})}_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k)}_{r}
does not hold in general.
The reason is that the freezing values of
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k)}
are based on the
k
-distance permutation
and those of
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k^{\prime})}
on the
k^{\prime}
-distance permutation. There is no evident relationship
between these two permutations (even if the initial points are picked in a compatible way):
sites that appear early in the permutation for
k
can appear late in the permutation for
k^{\prime}
and vice versa,
so that lenses can freeze at arbitrary radii. We remark that the inclusion “almost” holds,
in the sense that
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k^{\prime})}_{r}\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}^{(k)}_{(1+\epsilon)r}

for
k^{\prime}\geq k
, but the technical implications of this approximation appear to be non trivial to handle.

Fortunately, there is an easy fix for this issue: we compute the
\mu
-distance permutation
(where
\mu
is the upper bound of
k
from the problem statement) and define the freezing times
of each
p_{i}
with respect to the
\lambda_{i}
obtained from this permutation.
In particular, in the definition of
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
, the value
\operatorname{frz}\left(A\right)

is independent of the cardinality of
A
and for
A\subseteq B
subsets of sites, we obtain that

\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(B)\subseteq\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}(A)
.
This yields immediately that the redefined
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}:=\left\{\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{r}^{(k)}\right\}_{r\geq 0,k\in\{0,\ldots,\mu\}}
is a bifiltration.

Moreover, the redefined
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
is still
(1+\epsilon)
-interleaved with the multicover up to
\mu
.
To see that, note that the only property of the distance permutation required to establish the sandwiching
in Section
4
was the covering property (Lemma
1
), used
in Lemma
5
. The covering property, however, is unaffected when using the
\mu
-distance
permutation instead of the
k
-distance permutations for
k\leq\mu
. The proof of the following lemma
is verbatim the same as the proof of Lemma
1
, adding the fact that
d^{k}(p,A)\leq d^{\mu}(p,A)

for
k\leq\mu
, all sites
p\in P
and all subsets
A
.

Lemma 19

(Generalized Covering)
.

Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be determined by the
\mu
-distance permutation,
and
k\leq\mu
. Let
\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}|_{P_{i}}
denote the
k
-fold cover of
P_{i}
. Then

\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}|_{P_{i}}\subseteq\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{r}\subseteq\operatorname{L}^{(k)}_{{r+\lambda_{i+1}}}|_{P_{i}}.

With this generalization, all results in Section
4
carry over using the redefined
freezing times of sites.

Cones revisited.

Recall that we constructed our simplicial approximation by “stacking up” the freezing lenses
\widetilde{\operatorname{L}}_{\alpha}(A)
with
\alpha\in[0,r]

to a cone
\operatorname{C}_{r}(A)

in one dimension higher; and considering the nerve
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
of all cones over
k
-subsets of sites.
Even with the freezing lenses redefined
as above, the resulting simplicial complexes do not form a bifiltration, since for
k\neq k^{\prime}
, already the vertex sets
of
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
and
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k^{\prime})}
are disjoint, being
k
-subsets and
k^{\prime}
-subsets of sites, respectively.

We suggest a very simple fix. For
k\leq\mu
, we simply redefine

\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}:=\operatorname{Nrv}\left\{\operatorname{C}_{r}(A)\,\,\middle|\,\,A\in\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{P}{\ell}\right),k\leq\ell\leq\mu\right\}.

In other words, we take the nerve of the cones over all subsets with at least
k
sites (up to
\mu
).
On the one hand, it is now clear that
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
is a subcomplex of
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k^{\prime})}
for
k\leq k^{\prime}
, just because
the set of cones in the former nerve is a subset of the cones in the latter nerve.
Hence, the complexes
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
form a bifiltration which we denote again by
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
.
On the other hand, the bifiltration
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is equivalent to
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
: the Persistent Nerve Theorem
[
2
]

also applies to bifiltrations and asserts that the union of the cones in the definition
of
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
is equivalent to the nerve. Moreover for
A\subseteq B
, the cone
\operatorname{C}_{r}(B)
is contained in
\operatorname{C}_{r}(A)

(because the freezing lenses are included for every
r
), thus the union of cones in
\operatorname{S}_{r}^{(k)}
does not change
from the old to the new definition.
It follows that the redefined bifiltration
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
is equivalent to
\widetilde{\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}}
,
and therefore is

(1+\epsilon)
-homotopy-interleaved with the multicover up to
\mu
. We can pass to the discretized version
\mathcal{D}

taking snapshots at every
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{z}
for all
k=1,\ldots,\mu
and using the Left Kan extension, just as in Section
5
.

Size bounds and computation revisited.

Our redefined simplicial approximation is the nerve of a much larger cover, which mixes cones of different cardinalities.
Perhaps surprisingly, the size analysis and the derived complexity bounds remain basically the same, replacing
k
with
\mu
:

Theorem 20
.

The number of
(m-1)
-simplices that appear in the simplicial bifiltration
\mathcal{D}
is at most

n\cdot\Gamma^{\mu m}=n\mu^{\mu m}\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{\delta\mu m}.

Proof.

Considering an
(m-1)
-simplex
\sigma:=\{\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m})\}
, with all
A_{i}
of cardinality
between
1
and
\mu
, we have up to
\mu m
involved sites. Associating such a simplex to the site
p_{i}

with largest index in the permutation, we have two cases: if
\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
is infinite, the involved sites
must be selected from the first
\mu
sites in the
\mu
-distance permutation, and there are at most
\mu^{\mu m}

different choices for that.
If
\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
is finite, we proceed as in Section
6
: every involved site must be in a ball
of radius
2\operatorname{frz}\left(p_{i}\right)
around
p_{i}
, and the packing property of the
\mu
-distance permutation allows us
to bound the number of sites in that call with index smaller than
i
by the same
\Gamma
as in Lemma
12
,
just with
k
replaced with
\mu
. The combinatorial argument in the proof of Theorem
13
carries over
and we obtain a total bound of

\mu^{\mu m}+(n-\mu)\cdot\Gamma^{\mu m}

where the
(n-\mu)
in the second summand comes from the fact that
(n-\mu)
sites
p
satisfy
\operatorname{frz}\left(p\right)<\infty
.
Since
\Gamma\geq\mu
, the bound follows.
∎

The computation of
\mathcal{D}
can be easily adapted to the redefined objects:
the bifiltration
\mathcal{D}
is
1
-critical
, which means that
for every simplex
\sigma
in the bifiltration, there is a unique critical value
(r_{0},k_{0})

such that
\sigma\in D_{r}^{k}
if and only if
r\geq r_{0}
and
k\leq k_{0}
.
For a collection of cones
\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{C}_{\infty}(A_{m})
, with every
A_{i}
being of
cardinality between
1
and
\mu
, the main task is to check whether the common intersection
is non-empty (in which case they form a
(m-1)
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}
),
and in this case to compute the critical value.
The intersection predicate from Section
7
carries over because it does
not depend on the number of (weighted) balls considered. The same is true for determining
the smallest value of
r_{0}
where they intersect. Writing
k_{0}
for the smallest cardinality
among the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
, it is then clear that the corresponding simplex appears
in the bifiltration exactly for all
k\leq k_{0}
(as for values
larger than
k_{0}
, one of the sets
A_{i}
is missing),
and
(r_{0},k_{0})
is the critical value. Other adaptions of how to traverse the cones
to obtain an output-sensitive bound are straight-forward and we omit the details. Finally, we arrive at

Theorem 21
.

Given a set
P
of
n
points,
m,\mu\geq 1
and
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, our algorithm computes all simplices
of dimension up to
(m-1)

of a simplicial
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of the multicover of
P
up to
\mu
in time

\mathcal{O}\left(n\mu\log n\log\Phi+X\mu^{\mu+1}m\left(\dfrac{96}{\epsilon}\right)^{\mu\delta}\right),

where
\Phi
is the spread of
P
and
X
is the total number of simplices in the filtration.

9
Conclusions

We introduced the first
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate filtration of the higher order
k
-fold filtration and provided an algorithm for computing it. If
k
and
\epsilon
are considered as constants and the input point set has constant spread, the algorithm runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
and yields a filtration of size
\mathcal{O}(n)
, which are the same favorable properties as in the well-studied case
k=1
.

There are various avenues to strengthen and generalize our results. First of all, our method has concentrated on the Euclidean case, but our approach mostly
generalizes to point sets in arbitrary metric spaces. The algorithm cannot use the quadtreap data structure anymore in this case, but there is no need for it, since
the algorithm by Har-Peled and Mendel
[
36
, Sec. 3.1]
can be adapted to the
k
-distance case in arbitrary metric spaces with little effort.
Also, the friends of
p_{i}
(Section
7
) can be computed with a slight adaptation of their techniques; we used quadtreaps mostly for the
ease of presentation. However, the computation of critical values of simplices described in Section
7
relies
on computing minimum enclosing balls of balls, for which we used an efficient algorithm for the Euclidean case.
The complexity of this step remains unspecified for a general metric space. This is common in related work; see, for instance
[
12
, Sec. 5]
.

Another natural goal is to remove the dependence on the spread. This dependence is caused by the computation of the
k
-distance permutation which is inspired by
the algorithm of
[
36
, Sec. 3.1]
. In the same paper
[
36
, Sec. 3.2–3.3]
,
they describe an approach to remove the spread from the bound (for
k=1
) using an approximate version of the greedy permutation.
While our construction of the sparsified filtration can be easily adapted to work with an approximate version of the
k
-distance permutation,
it seems less straight-forward to generalize their techniques to the
k
-distance, even in the Euclidean case.

The
k
-distance permutation relates to the
Distance to Measure
(DTM)
[
14
]
, which is the square average of the distances to the
k
nearest neighbors. The DTM has the advantage of being
robust, in terms of the Wasserstein distance, to perturbations on the sample
[
14
, Sec. 3]
. However, most of the existing methods for sparsifying filtrations obtained via the DTM
[
9
,
1
,
34
]
require a preliminary approximation by weighted distances.
Our approach might be adaptable to directly sparsify DTM filtrations.

Our techniques also yield a linear-sized approximation of the multicover bifiltration up to
\mu
.
However, the presence of an exponential factor on
\mu
in our size bounds suggests a restriction of our approach’s usability to small values of
\mu
.
The exponential factor also carries over to the expected computation time. Reducing that dependency on
\mu
is another possible line of future work.
Note that
[
18
, Prop. 5]
gives a size bound of
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{d+1}\right)
for the exact version,
but we ask whether a polynomial bound on
\mu
could be achieved without such a blow-up in the dependency on
n
.

We point out in this context that the double-nerve construction of
[
18
]
could be applied in Section
8
:
instead of taking the nerve over all
\ell
-cones with
k\leq\ell\leq\mu
at level
k
, we could as well only take the union of all nerves of the cones at level
\ell
and
\ell+1

with
k\leq\ell\leq\mu-1
. The proof of equivalence is via a mapping telescope construction, similar to
[
18
, Thm 4]
.
While this approach yields a smaller simplicial bifiltration, the complexity bounds do not change, so we omit the details.

Finally, a natural question is the practicality of our algorithm. We remark that even for
k=1
, while some work has been devoted to practical aspects of computing sparsifications
[
3
,
8
,
22
,
41
,
45
]
, the actual practical computation is still an unresolved problem. We think that the natural order for a practically efficient solution would be to first identify best practices in the simpler
k=1
case and subsequently try to adapt them to larger values of
k
. So, while we would be curious about the performance of our algorithm, such an evaluation seems to be premature at the moment.
