Quantum Dynamic Programming
Jeongrak Son, Marek Gluza, Ryuji Takagi, Nelly H. Y. Ng

Abstract
We introduce a quantum extension of dynamic programming, a fundamental computational method that efficiently solves recursive problems using memory. Our innovation lies in showing how to coherently generate recursion step unitaries by using memorized intermediate quantum states. Quantum dynamic programming achieves an exponential reduction in circuit depth for a broad class of fixed-point quantum recursions, though this comes at the cost of increased circuit width. Interestingly, the trade-off becomes more favourable when the initial state is pure. By hybridizing our approach with a conventional memoryless one, we can flexibly balance circuit depth and width to optimize performance on quantum devices with fixed hardware constraints. Finally, we showcase applications of quantum dynamic programming to several quantum recursions, including a variant of Grover’s search, quantum imaginary-time evolution, and a new protocol for obliviously preparing a quantum state in its Schmidt basis.



Introduction.
—Storing intermediate results in memory can drastically reduce computation runtime.
Computing the Fibonacci sequence provides a perfect example.
Direct calculation of the
N
th element using the recursive definition
F(n)=F(n-1)+F(n-2)
, starting with
F(0)=0
and
F(1)=1
and without storing intermediate results, requires calculating
F(N-2)
twice—once for
F(N)
and again for
F(N-1)
.
In turn, computing
F(N-2)
requires two computations of
F(N-4)
, and so on.
This repetition causes the computation time to grow exponentially with
N
.
On the other hand, if we store intermediate values of
F(n)
in memory and retrieve them whenever necessary,
F(N)
can be computed in time linear to
N
, achieving an exponential speedup.
This technique, called memoization
Michie 1968
, is a form of dynamic programming
Bellman 1952
;
Bellman 1966
that utilizes a small amount of memory to yield vastly shorter runtime by avoiding re-computations.

We introduce
quantum dynamic programming (QDP)
as a technique to utilize memoization in quantum computation.
The quantum analogue of computing the Fibonacci sequence would be solving quantum recursions, which we define as

\displaystyle|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\mapsto|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle=\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\ ,

(1)

a sequence of quantum states
\{|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\}_{n}
related by unitary operators
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
that explicitly depend on the past states
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
.
Such recursions appear in important quantum algorithms, including nested fixed-point search
Grover 2005
;
Yoder et al. 2014
and double-bracket quantum algorithms
Gluza 2024
;
Robbiati et al. 2024
;
Xiaoyue et al. 2024
;
Gluza et al. 2024
.

There are various approaches to solving Eq. (
1
), yet a uniquely quantum challenge underlies them all.
Unlike classical computing, reading out a quantum state
|{\psi}\rangle
requires many copies, and this cannot be circumvented
Gisin and Massar 1997
as dictated by the no-cloning theorem
Wootters and Zurek 1982
.
If one compiles the recursion step
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
by learning each intermediate state
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
through, e.g. tomography
Haah et al. 2016
, each step must be repeated many times before learning how to compile
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
. An alternative approach, termed
unfolding
, is analogous to classical computation without memoization.
It makes multiple queries to an operation preparing
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
in order to compile
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
.
However, since
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
is not learnt, operations preparing it must be compiled using queries to the initial step
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{0}]}
, which is assumed to be given.
This leads to the circuit depth exponential in
N
for preparing
|{\psi_{N}}\rangle
.
We expound on this scaling later in this Letter with an example.

Our approach to QDP belongs to a lineage of studies on circuits instructed by quantum states
Lloyd et al. 2014
;
Marvian and Lloyd 2016
;
Pichler et al. 2016
;
Kimmel et al. 2017
;
Kjaergaard et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2023
;
Patel and Wilde 2023a
;
Patel and Wilde 2023b
;
Rodriguez-Grasa et al. 2023
;
Go et al. 2024
;
Schoute et al. 2024
, where desired operations are implemented by injecting quantum instruction states that encode the operation, rather than by compiling circuits based on classical information.
This paradigm offers several advantages.
First, the circuit can be implemented obliviously to the quantum instructions, i.e. without learning them, while requiring significantly fewer samples compared to tomography-based learning-and-compiling methods
Kimmel et al. 2017
;
Go et al. 2024
.
Furthermore, the circuits instructed by quantum states are universal
Kimmel et al. 2017
, enabling all queries to
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{0}]}
(required in the unfolding approach) to be replaced by the injection of
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
.

The main breakthrough of our work is the following observation: instead of unfolding the recursion (i.e. compiling
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
with exponentially many queries to
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{0}]}
), we can dynamically evolve memory states from
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
to
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
in parallel and directly implement
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
by injecting these memory states.
In essence, QDP ‘folds up’ the unfolded recursion step
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
into the memory state as it evolves to
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
.
By doing so, we achieve an exponential circuit depth reduction compared to the unfolding approach, as formalized in Theorem
1
, en par with the classic example of computing Fibonacci numbers.

However, the same no-cloning challenge prevails QDP.
Many copies of each intermediate state
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
are required, necessitating circuits with greater width.
The crucial distinction from precomputation approaches
Marvian and Lloyd 2016
;
Huggins and McClean 2024
emerges here: our memory states are not given a priori, but we prepare them as a result of previous recursion steps.
This results in a trade-off, converting circuits with exponential depth into those with exponential width.
Nonetheless, QDP remains the strategically correct choice.
Any protocol must conclude within the coherence time of the quantum processor, and QDP precisely offers an explicit tool that circumvents such hard limitations—providing a concrete strategy that radically reconfigures circuit structures to leverage quantum memoization.

In this manuscript, we systematically introduce QDP for generic quantum recursions and establish its feasibility.
We exemplify the utility of QDP on known algorithms, e.g. recursive Grover’s search
Yoder et al. 2014
and quantum imaginary-time evolution
Gluza et al. 2024
. Lastly, we propose a natively dynamic algorithm for transforming a state into its Schmidt basis without learning the state itself.
With this, we establish that QDP is not only a strategic conceptual framework but also a highly applicable one.

Memory-calls in quantum recursions.
—Quantum recursion unitaries can be regarded as a mapping from a quantum state
|{\psi}\rangle
to a unitary operator
\hat{U}^{[\psi]}
.
In general,
\hat{U}^{[\psi]}=e^{if(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}
, where
f
is a function from Hermitian operators to Hermitian operators.
Then,
\hat{U}^{[\psi]}
can be approximated by

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{[\{\hat{\mathcal{N}}\},\psi]}=\hat{V}_{L}e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{L}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}\hat{V}_{L-1}\ldots\hat{V}_{1}e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}\hat{V}_{0}\ ,

(2)

where
\{\hat{\mathcal{N}}\}
is a collection of Hermitian-preserving (linear or polynomial) maps
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
, while
\hat{V}_{i}
are static unitaries independent of the instruction
|{\psi}\rangle
.
We say that a quantum recursion unitary written as Eq. (
2
) contains
L

memory-calls
of the form
e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}
.
The most prominent example is found in Grover’s algorithm
Grover 1996
;
Grover 2005
, where alternating reflections

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{[\psi]}_{L}=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{L}e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|}e^{-i\beta_{i}|{\tau}\rangle\!\langle{\tau}|}\ ,

(3)

with respect to the initial state
|{\psi}\rangle
and the target state of the search
|{\tau}\rangle
are applied to
|{\psi}\rangle
.
Eq. (
3
) contains
L
memory-calls, each
e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{i}(\psi)}
defined by
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{i}(\psi)=-\alpha_{i}|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
and a set of predetermined angles
\{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
.
Another example is the double-bracket iteration
\hat{U}^{[\psi]}=e^{s[\hat{D},|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|]}
with a fixed operator
\hat{D}
, which facilitates diagonalization
Gluza 2024
.
This corresponds to choosing
L=1
,
V_{0}=V_{1}=\mathmybb{1}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)=-is[\hat{D},|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|]
.

Unfolding quantum recursions.
— We start by introducing the standard approach in implementing quantum recursions.
Consider the Grover unitary Eq. (
3
), but applied recursively
|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle=\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}_{L}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
starting from a root state
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
.
By assumption,
e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi_{0}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{0}}|}
and
e^{-i\beta_{i}|{\tau}\rangle\!\langle{\tau}|}
are readily available.
However,
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}_{L}
involves memory-calls
e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|}
, which are not directly accessible.
Using the relation
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|=\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n-1}}|\bigl(\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}\bigr)^{\dagger}
, we observe that

\displaystyle e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|}=\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n-1}}|}\bigl(\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}-1]}_{L}\bigr)^{\dagger}\ .

(4)

We name this property
covariance
and use it to ‘unfold’ the memory-call
e^{-i\alpha_{i}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|}
into
2L+1
memory-calls to
|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle
.
This unfolding continues until each memory-call to
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
in
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}_{L}
is implemented with
(2L+1)^{n}
calls to
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
.
The nested fixed-point Grover search
Yoder et al. 2014
exactly follows this method.

To our knowledge, all existing quantum recursions are implemented through unfolding.
However, there are two severe drawbacks.
First, memory-calls must be covariant; otherwise they need to be approximated by covariant ones, via group commutators
Dawson and Nielsen 2006
as in Refs.
Gluza 2024
;
Robbiati et al. 2024
;
Xiaoyue et al. 2024
;
Gluza et al. 2024
or a general scheme described in Sec.
II.1
of
sup
.
Moreover, unfolding yields exponentially deep circuits, with the final recursion step
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{N-1}]}
dominating the depth, as it requires
L(2L+1)^{N-1}
calls to
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
.

Memory-usage queries.
—To resolve the exponential depth of unfolded circuits, QDP invokes dynamically evolving memory states to directly instruct memory-calls.
The goal is to apply the unitary operation
e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}
to a working state
|{\phi}\rangle
:

\displaystyle|{\phi}\rangle\mapsto e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}|{\phi}\rangle\ .

(5)

A crucial observation is that Eq. (
5
) can be approximately implemented using multiple copies of the (unknown) state
|{\psi}\rangle

Kimmel et al. 2017
;
Wei et al. 2023
.
Consider the channel

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\psi]}_{s}(|{\phi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|)=\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-i\hat{N}s}\left(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\otimes|{\phi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|\right)e^{i\hat{N}s}\right]\ ,

(6)

where the bipartite unitary
e^{-i\hat{N}s}
is oblivious to both the memory and working register.
Specifically,
\hat{N}
depends solely on the Hermitian-preserving map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
that characterizes the memory-call.
By choosing
\hat{N}=\Lambda^{\transp_{1}}
, the partial transpose of the Choi matrix
\Lambda
of
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
(see Supplemental Materials
sup
Section
II.2
for an explicit expression), Eq. (
6
) approximates the memory-call
e^{is\hat{\mathcal{N}}(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)}
with error
\mathcal{O}(s^{2})
.
For instance, when
\hat{\mathcal{N}}=\id
, which corresponds to density matrix exponentiation
Lloyd et al. 2014
,
\hat{N}=\mathrm{SWAP}
is used in Eq. (
6
).
We call this channel a single
memory-usage query
, as it consumes one copy of
|{\psi}\rangle
.
Each memory-usage query invokes an error scaling quadratically to the duration
s
; by setting
s=1/M
and repeating the same memory-usage queries Eq. (
6
)
M
times, we obtain the channel
(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\psi]}_{1/M})^{M}
that approximates Eq. (
5
) with total error
\mathcal{O}(s^{2}/M)
.
Therefore, the error in the memory-call approximation can be kept arbitrarily small by increasing
M
.

Exponential depth reduction with QDP.
—We define QDP as the process of making memory-usage queries on copies of
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
to approximate a recursion step towards
|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle
.
Specifically, we initialize a quantum program with
\sigma_{0}=|{\psi_{0}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{0}}|
and define the QDP iteration as

\displaystyle\sigma_{n+1}=\hat{V}_{1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{n}]}_{1/M}\right)^{M}\left(\hat{V}_{0}\sigma_{n}\hat{V}_{0}^{\dagger}\right)\hat{V}_{1}^{\dagger}\ .

(7)

Eq. (
7
) approximates a single memory-call recursion (Eq. (
2
) with
L=1
) by replacing the memory-call with
M
memory-usage queries.
In general, memory states
\sigma_{n\neq 0}
are not pure because the channel
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{n}]}_{1/M}
is not exactly unitary.
However, we show in Theorem
1
that
\sigma_{N}
can be made arbitrarily close to the desired state
|{\psi_{N}}\rangle
.
If recursions involve multiple memory-calls, QDP generalizes by replacing all memory-calls with
M
total memory-usage queries to the memory state
\sigma_{n}
.

To prepare one copy of
\sigma_{1}
, we make
M
memory-usage queries with the memory state
\sigma_{0}
.
This requires
(M+1)
root state copies (
M
in the memory register and one in the working register).
Likewise, preparing
\sigma_{2}
requires
(M+1)^{2}
copies of
\sigma_{0}
; thus, the iteration of Eq. (
7
) consumes
(M+1)^{n}
copies of the root state for
\sigma_{n}
.
Meanwhile, the circuit depth remains linear:
multiple instruction states can be prepared in parallel, resulting in a maximum depth of
nM
memory-usage queries.
An intuitive explanation can be drawn by comparing QDP to unfolding, where a memory-call to
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
is executed by making
(2L+1)^{n}
memory-calls to the root state.
In contrast, QDP folds all root state calls into the memory state
\sigma_{n}
, allowing a memory-call to be implemented with a fixed-depth circuit.

The accuracy of QDP.
—Seeking an exact solution to quantum recursions is unrealistic, as circuit compilation always entails inaccuracies.
Hence, it is necessary to ensure that each memory-call approximation in QDP is sufficiently accurate to prepare the final state
\sigma_{N}
within the same error threshold as unfolding.
Setting
M=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
in Eq. (
7
) ensures that the QDP prepared state
\sigma_{1}
approximates the exact result
|{\psi_{1}}\rangle
within an error
\|\sigma_{1}-|{\psi_{1}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{1}}|\|_{1}\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
However, subsequent steps may amplify this error, as
\sigma_{1}\mapsto\sigma_{2}
becomes instructed by
\sigma_{1}
, not
|{\psi_{1}}\rangle
.
Indeed, the triangle inequality gives
\|\sigma_{2}-|{\psi_{2}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{2}}|\|_{1}\leq\mathcal{O}(M\epsilon)
; see Sec.
II
of
sup
for the full analysis.

To address this, we identify sufficient conditions to prevent such destructive error propagation.
It turns out that if the first few steps are sufficiently accurate, then later steps benefit from stabilization properties of typical quantum recursions.
In particular, recursions with fixed-points (e.g. via the Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality in gradient descent iterations
Karimi et al. 2016
or asymptotic stability in time-discrete dynamical systems
Moore et al. 1994
) typically exhibit exponential convergence.
For quantum recursions, this implies
\||{\psi_{N}}\rangle-|{\psi_{\infty}}\rangle\|_{1}\leq\alpha^{N}\||{\psi_{0}}\rangle-|{\psi_{\infty}}\rangle\|_{1}
, for some
\alpha<1
.
However, this relies on three assumptions:
i)
a stable fixed-point
|{\psi_{\infty}}\rangle
as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
ii)
uniqueness of the fixed-point, and that
iii)
the fixed-point is sufficiently strongly attracting.
We term quantum recursions satisfying
i,ii,iii)
(or their generalization to mixed state recursions as in Sec.
III
of
sup
) as exhibiting
fast spectral convergence
.
Without
iii)
, recursions become unstable, suggesting limited physical relevance, as practically achievable protocols must withstand small perturbations.
In such cases, quantum computation would require infinite precision and resources for a successful operation.

It is worth noting that unfolding is also subject to similar stability constraints: if
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{0}]}
is not compiled exactly, subsequent recursion unitaries
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
, which rely on exponentially many applications of
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{0}]}
, become exponentially unstable.
Thus, whether using QDP or unfolding, quantum recursions must exhibit fast spectral convergence; otherwise, the computational task is ill-conditioned. When this condition is met, QDP delivers an exponential depth reduction compared to unfolding:

Theorem 1

(Exponential circuit depth reduction)
.

Suppose a quantum recursion starting from
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
satisfies fast spectral convergence. Then QDP combined with a mixedness reduction protocol yields a final state
\sigma_{N}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{N}-|{\psi_{N}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{N}}|\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,

(8)

using a circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}(N\epsilon^{-1})
and width
\epsilon^{-N}e^{\mathcal{O}(N)}
.
Here,
|{\psi_{N}}\rangle
is the exact solution to the recursion.

The technical version of this theorem and its proof is given as Theorem
12
in
sup
.
We prove this theorem by addressing two different types of error. In particular, we note that unitary errors are efficiently suppressed by fast spectral convergence, while non-unitary errors destabilize the convergence and need to be mitigated explicitly.
We employ an additional protocol
Cirac et al. 1999
;
Childs et al. 2025
to reduce
such errors, and show in
sup
Sec.
III.2
that this protocol incurs only a slightly faster exponential growth in circuit width.
Furthermore, a similar theorem can be developed for mixed state extensions, which we display in End Matters.

Implications of the depth-width trade-off: distributed quantum computing and hybrid strategy.
—Theorem
1
establishes the desired circuit depth-width trade-off.
At its core, this trade-off is valuable because it exponentially reduces computational time, which can turn a practically impossible task into a feasible one.
In addition, the capacity to realize a shorter depth circuit comes with potential advantages when combined with quantum error mitigation. In particular, it was shown that error mitigation generally requires exponential resource overhead with circuit depth
Takagi et al. 2022
;
Takagi et al. 2023
;
Tsubouchi et al. 2023
. Although there are observations indicating that error mitigation could require exponential cost also with circuit width
Temme et al. 2017
;
Quek et al. 2024
, a rigorous characterization applicable to general classes of circuits and error mitigation methods has not been established. In this sense, there is still room for some error mitigation techniques to run efficiently in large-width short-depth circuits of practical interest.
Beyond this immediate advantage, we focus on how QDP facilitates implementation.

One simple measure is the quantum circuit size, defined as the product of the maximum circuit depth and width
Yoder et al. 2016
;
Takagi et al. 2017
.
We illustrate how this measure benefits from the trade-off.
Suppose that in each recursion step,
L
non-covariant memory-calls are made, each approximated by
k

covariant
ones.
The unfolding circuit size therefore scales as
(2kL+1)^{N}
for
N
recursion steps.
Meanwhile, memory-usage queries directly implement even non-covariant memory-calls, but require
k^{\prime}
memory-usage queries to manage implementation error, i.e.
M=k^{\prime}L
memory-usage queries per recursion step.
In this scenario, the QDP circuit width scales as
(M+1)^{N}=(k^{\prime}L+1)^{N}
, which dominates the total circuit size for large
N
.
Whenever
L
is large and
k^{\prime}<2k
, QDP approximately achieves a
(2k/k^{\prime})^{N}
-fold improvement in circuit size compared to unfolding.
For instance, in the most error-tolerant case of
k=k^{\prime}=1
, QDP significantly reduces circuit size.

Beyond circuit size, QDP offers additional practical advantages: its local modularity is particularly well-suited for distributed quantum computing
Wehner et al. 2018
;
Cacciapuoti et al. 2019
;
Davarzani et al. 2020
.
By localizing and decoupling circuits
Wang et al. 2024
, QDP allows copies of
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
to be prepared independently before being injected into recursion steps
\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}
.
As the recursion progresses, the circuit width decreases exponentially as most copies are consumed as memory and traced out during memory-usage queries.
This enables greater flexibility in parallelization, since processors with shorter coherence times can be allocated for preparing
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
with smaller
n
, optimizing the use of available quantum devices.

The flexibility of QDP is reinforced by a hybrid strategy.
Realistically, quantum devices can neither execute exponentially many sequential gates nor operate on exponentially many qubits simultaneously.
A practical approach is to initiate QDP after several rounds of unfolding, distributing the exponential factor between circuit depth and width to prevent either from becoming prohibitive.
For example, one could begin with
N_{1}
unfolding steps, using a circuit of depth
e^{\mathcal{O}(N_{1})}
, nearing the device’s depth limit.
These unfolding steps are performed in parallel, maximizing circuit width to produce
M
copies of intermediate states
|{\psi_{N_{1}}}\rangle
.
For many quantum recursions, this starting strategy also conveniently steers the system into a regime where the recursion starts to exhibit fast spectral convergence.
This allows QDP to subsequently take over, executing an additional
N_{2}
recursions to attain the final state
\sigma_{N_{1}+N_{2}}
.
This hybrid strategy fully utilizes the device capacity, which is otherwise limited to producing either
|{\psi_{N_{1}}}\rangle
without QDP or
\sigma_{N_{2}}
without unfolding.

Example 1: Grover search.
—The nested fixed-point Grover search
Yoder et al. 2014
has a recursion unitary defined in Eq. (
3
), where
N
recursion steps of this algorithm prepare
|{\psi_{N}}\rangle
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert|{\psi_{N}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{N}}|-|{\tau}\rangle\!\langle{\tau}|\rVert_{1}\simeq e^{-\lvert\langle{\psi_{0}}|{\tau}\rangle\rvert(2L+1)^{N}}\ .

(9)

If the recursion is implemented through unfolding, the total circuit depth scales exponentially as
(2L+1)^{N}
.
This matches the performance of the optimal Grover search
Grover 1996
;
Brassard et al. 2002
with the same circuit depth; refer to
sup
Sec.
IV.1
for a self-contained discussion. In contrast, the QDP implementation reduces the circuit depth to
\mathcal{O}(N)
, implying an exponential runtime speedup, thanks to Eq. (
9

This speedup comes with the requirement of exponential copies of the initial state
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
.
Unfortunately, it is known that

there is no quantum advantage in sample-based Grover search algorithms, which include the QDP implementation.
Nevertheless, this example serves as a valuable demonstration of QDP’s applicability for a historically significant quantum algorithm.

Example 2: Quantum imaginary-time evolution.
—We consider quantum imaginary-time evolution in the sample-based Hamiltonian simulation scenario

, which aims at achieving the task of ground state preparation for quantum many-body systems.
Suppose that copies of
\rho\propto\hat{H}
are given, where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian with a zero ground state energy.
We also prepare copies of some pure state
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
having non-zero overlap with the ground state.
Using them, recursions of the form

\displaystyle|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\mapsto|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle=e^{s[|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|,\hat{H}]}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle,

(10)

with some duration
s
can be implemented oblivious to both
\hat{H}
and
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
; see
sup
Secs.
V.3
and
V.4
.
This unitary has been shown

Wick 1954
and yield a sequence of states
\{|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\}
whose infidelity to the ground state decays exponentially as

\rho\propto\hat{H}
, is studied in Ref.
Gluza et al. 2024
.

Example 3: Oblivious Schmidt decomposition.
—For pure bipartite states, the Schmidt decomposition
Schmidt 1907
;
Ekert and Knight 1995
fully characterizes their entanglement.
Thus we may envision a protocol that unitarily transforms any unknown
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
into a form where its Schmidt basis aligns with the computational basis:
\hat{V}_{A}\otimes\hat{V}_{B}|{\psi_{0}}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}|{k}\rangle\otimes|{k}\rangle
with
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
denoting the Schmidt spectrum.
Traditionally, prior knowledge of
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
is required to construct
\hat{V}_{A}
and
\hat{V}_{B}
.
However, QDP enables this task while completely circumventing such a need.
Given that this is a novel algorithm, we outline the key ideas here, and provide a more detailed exposition in
sup
Sec.
V.5
.
Let
\hat{D}
be a non-degenerate diagonal operator on subsystem
A
.
The recursion unitary is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\psi_{n}]}=e^{s\left[\hat{D},\Tr_{B}[|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|]\right]}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{B}\ .

(11)

This is a single memory-call type recursion with
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho_{AB})=-is[\hat{D},\Tr_{B}[\rho_{AB}]]\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{B}
that is Hermitian-preserving; hence, with copies of
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
, the recursion step
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\mapsto|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle=\hat{U}^{[\psi_{n}]}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle
can be approximated.
When
s
is small,
\{|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\}
exponentially converges to
\hat{V}_{A}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{B}|{\psi_{0}}\rangle

Helmke and Moore 1994
;
Smith 1993
allowing Theorem
1
to apply.
A similar procedure implements
\hat{V}_{B}
, completing the oblivious Schmidt decomposition.

Let us discuss the implications of oblivious Schmidt decomposition in the context of quantum information processing.
The replica method
Horodecki and Ekert 2002
extracts classical information
\lambda_{k}
but requires exponentially many swap operations.
Similarly, we expect that oblivious Schmidt decomposition may also require a long runtime to converge.
However, it not only enables sampling from the Schmidt spectrum but also provides the bipartite quantum state
\sum_{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}|{k}\rangle\otimes|{k}\rangle

coherently
in the computational basis.
This is useful, e.g. for entanglement distillation of an
unknown
state
\psi
, contrasting with standard settings
Bennett et al. 1996
that require the knowledge about the initial state for compiling local operations.
While oblivious Schmidt decomposition via QDP may not attain the optimal asymptotic rates as derived from Ref.
Matsumoto and Hayashi 2007
, it provides a constructive approach to oblivious entanglement distillation with explicit circuit implementations.

Conclusion and outlook.
—We demonstrated that QDP enables the efficient implementation of a broad class of quantum recursions involving memory-calls.
For well-behaved recursions, QDP yields accurate results with polynomial depth—an
exponential
speed-up over the previously proposed zero-memory implementations based on unfolding
Yoder et al. 2014
;
Gluza 2024
.
Additionally, QDP enables hybrid strategies of balancing circuit depth and width, making quantum recursions more feasible on real devices. By structuring quantum recursions into circuits with parallel rather than sequential operations, QDP renders them suitable as a central use-case for distributed quantum computing
Wehner et al. 2018
;
Cacciapuoti et al. 2019
;
Davarzani et al. 2020
.

The general QDP proposal assumes the preparation of many independent copies of the same quantum state, analogous to sample-based algorithms
Lloyd et al. 2014
;
Pichler et al. 2016
;
Kimmel et al. 2017
;
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Kjaergaard et al. 2022
;
Go et al. 2024
;
Schoute et al. 2024
, where copies of the quantum state replace oracle access to unitary operators.
For QDP applications involving only a marginal amount of quantum coherence, approximate cloning heuristics
Rodriguez-Grasa et al. 2023
could potentially provide exponential speed-ups without inflating the circuit width.
Nevertheless, quantitative bounds relating the coherence of input quantum states to cloning fidelity are currently unknown
Scarani et al. 2005
.
It is also unclear whether coherence could signal simulation hardness for quantum recursions, akin to how entanglement affects tensor network simulations
Vidal 2003
.
Investigating the role of coherence in QDP presents an intriguing avenue for future research.

Currently, we are unaware of quantum algorithms where QDP ensures a clear computational advantage over classical methods.
These may appear in settings robust against imperfect unitary implementations, similar to those present in diagonalizing double-bracket iterations
Helmke and Moore 1994
;
Smith 1993
;
Gluza 2024
.
Indeed, QDP allows us to add oblivious Schmidt decomposition to the quantum algorithmic toolkit.
We hope that once it will be feasible to experimentally implement memory-usage queries with high fidelity, oblivious Schmidt decomposition and QDP in general will facilitate practical state preparations that will advance our knowledge of quantum properties in materials, e.g. magnets or superconductors.

Theorem 2

Appendix B: Non-asymptotic prospects of QDP.
—Without fault-tolerance, noise in quantum hardware places limitations on the use of QDP, particularly when implementations are too small to match its asymptotic performance scaling.
However, we argue qualitatively how QDP can help mitigate noise-induced constraints.

Ref.
Robbiati et al. 2024
studied explicit gate counts preparing ground states of XXZ models using double-bracket quantum algorithms (DBQA).
It was found that for 10 qubits, a single DBQA step achieves 99.5% fidelity to the ground state with a gate cost as low as 50 CZ gates per qubit.
This can be considered as a satisfactory circuit compilation for the 10 qubit XXZ problem as it is both short and accurate.
However, for larger system sizes, the warm-start procedure used in Ref.
Robbiati et al. 2024
is expected to be insufficient due to optimization challenges
Bharti et al. 2022
;
Larocca et al. 2024
.

Theoretically, DBQA can systematically improve ground state preparation by running additional steps in such scenarios.
As exemplified in Ref.
Robbiati et al. 2024
for
L=10
qubits, if the warm-start circuit is inaccurate (i.e. initialized at a relatively high energy), additional steps gradually decrease the energy.
Since DBQA reduces energy locally, its performance is independent of system size, and in practice, CZ gate counts per qubit also remain roughly independent.
Specifically, for
L=10
qubits,
k=1
required
50
CZ gates per qubit,
k=2
required about
200
, and
k=3
demanded thousands.
Depending on the precise hardware and use of error mitigation,
k=2
steps roughly exhaust the capacities of existing quantum hardware, while
k=3
can be considered out of reach at the time of writing.

We thus expect that the transition from
k=2
to
k=3
could provide significant improvements for larger systems (e.g.
L=50
or
L=150
qubits), as available in system sizes made available by leading quantum computing companies.
However, at
k=3
the required circuit depth would likely be prohibitively high, and the implementation is bound to fail almost every time.
Instead, the
k=3
step can be implemented by running a single QDP step.
One should employ memory-usage queries for this step, which in turn must be compiled within tight runtime requirements.

For a qualitative estimate, suppose one density matrix exponentiation (DME)
Lloyd et al. 2014
memory-usage query can be implemented using 100 CZ gates per qubit.
Then the
k=3
DBQA step using
M=5
memory-usage queries halve the circuit depth compared to a standard DBQA implementation.
While the compilation cost of DME memory-usage queries has not been explicitly studied except for
L=1
qubits
Kjaergaard et al. 2022
, if it were, say,
1000
CZ gates per qubit, the same argument would apply to future scenarios, e.g. optimizing the transition from
k
to
k+1
steps for
k\approx 4
.
Note that heuristic improvements like these will remain essential even in the fault-tolerant regime, because quantum algorithms based on quantum phase estimation rely on high-quality initializations, ensuring that QDP continues to be useful in such settings.

Let us also comment on further open questions pertaining to implementation.
When running large-scale QDP, error-mitigation can be used to suppress errors and enable an unfolded implementation of the recursion until failures appear.
This suggests that practical QDP implementations may start from an advanced stage rather than from scratch, reducing the number of required QDP steps and thereby lowering the circuit width.
Furthermore, one could consider creating memory states
|{\psi_{k^{*}}}\rangle
at an intermediate step
k^{*}
using physical qubits and then encoding these states
|{\psi_{k^{*}}}\rangle
into a fault-tolerant quantum computer via heuristic (non-fault-tolerant) methods; see Refs
Buchbinder et al. 2013
;
Gluza et al. 2024
.

Finally, let us comment that increasing circuit width exposes the system to errors.
However, since intermediate states serve as quantum instructions, these errors may statistically average out, i.e.
M
memory usage queries using noisy instructions may still yield a reduction in the cost function as the ‘average’ instruction points in the ‘correct’ direction.
This robustness is made precise by quantum imaginary-time evolution (see Example 2 in the main text).
Here, in every step, energy is guaranteed to decrease by a finite amount, and maintaining this decrease only requires approximating a unitary channel whose effective Hamiltonian aligns with the steepest-descent direction.
For individual errors to overhaul the energy decrease, they would need to entirely flip the direction of the effective Hamiltonian relative to the gradient operator.
We anticipate that such a flip is unlikely, even in the presence of noisy quantum instructions.
In general, recursions with fast spectral convergence should be expected to benefit from such stabilizing effects.

I
Preliminaries and distance measures

We denote Hilbert spaces as
\mathcal{H}
and the set of quantum states having support in
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, which is a subset of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the set of generic bounded operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
.
When not specified, quantum channels are assumed to map
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
The identity operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted as
\mathmybb{1}
, whereas the identity map from
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
to
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
is denoted as
\id
.

A few different distance measures between operators and channels are utilized in this paper.
The trace norm distance between two normal operators acting on the same space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is defined to be

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\hat{A}-\hat{B}\rVert_{1}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\sqrt{(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{\dagger}(\hat{A}-\hat{B})}\right]\ .

(S1)

Between two pure states
\psi
and
\phi
, the trace norm distance can be written with respect to their overlap as
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\psi-\phi\right\rVert_{1}=\sqrt{1-\lvert\langle{\psi}|{\phi}\rangle\rvert^{2}}
.
Another measure uses the Hilbert-Schmidt norm and is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\hat{A}-\hat{B}\rVert_{2}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Tr\left[(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{\dagger}(\hat{A}-\hat{B})\right]}\ .

(S2)

The operator norm of a normal operator
\hat{A}
is defined to be
\|\hat{A}\|_{\infty}=\max\{|\lambda_{j}|\}
, where
\lambda_{j}
are eigenvalues of
\hat{A}
; likewise, the operator norm distance between two normal operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
is
\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{A}-\hat{B}\|_{\infty}
.

One can define distances between two quantum channels
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
, leveraging on operator distances.
The trace norm distance between two quantum channels
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
is defined as the maximum distance between the outputs of two channels given an identical input state, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\rm Tr}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\varrho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})}\left\lVert\Phi_{1}(\varrho)-\Phi_{2}(\varrho)\right\rVert_{1}\ .

(S3)

The diamond norm distance between
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
is a stronger measure of distance between channels, because it further optimizes over input states that can be entangled with an external reference,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\diamond}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\max_{\varrho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})}\left\lVert(\Phi_{1}\otimes\id)(\varrho)-(\Phi_{2}\otimes\id)(\varrho)\right\rVert_{1}\ .

(S4)

It is useful to note that the diamond norm distance always upper bounds the trace norm distance,
\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\diamond}\geq\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\rm Tr}
.

For both operators and channels, norms
\|\cdotB\|
with no subscript denotes that some unitary invariant norm (including trace norm, operator norm, etc.) is used.

II
Locally accurate implementations

In Supplemental Materials, we focus on quantum recursions whose initial states are in general mixed.
Then a memory-call unitary Eq. (
5

\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}(\sigma)\coloneq e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}\sigma e^{-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}\ ,

(S5)

with a memory state
\rho
, a working state
\sigma
, and a hermitian-preserving map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
.

From this, we can define quantum recursions.
Consider the simplest case with only one memory-call unitary of the form Eq. (
S5
) and trivial constant unitaries
V_{0}=V_{1}=\mathmybb{1}
when written in the form Eq. (
2

Starting from the root state
\rho_{0}
, the recursion is defined by
\rho_{n+1}=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{n}]}(\rho_{n})
; the exact implementation would lead to a sequence of states
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots

For both unfolding and QDP implementations, some memory-calls cannot be executed without error, and we need to consider the approximate implementations.

For example, consider the QDP implementation.
If we continue the recursion with approximate memory-calls, at
(n-1)
th recursion step, the instruction states available to us in the memory register are
\sigma_{n}
, which might be different from the exact intermediate state
\rho_{n}
.
Thus, the QDP implementation is natural in that it works with what it has in hand (placeholder quantum memory) and leap-frogs forward (implements the recursion step using memory-usage queries with the memoized intermediate state being a quanum instruction).
We say that
the sequence of states
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N}
is an
\epsilon
-locally accurate solution
of
N
recursion steps of Eq. (
5

k=0,\ldots N-1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{k},M]}_{\text{QDP}}({\sigma_{k}})-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{k}]}(\sigma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\sigma_{k+1}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{k}]}(\sigma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(S6)

In other words, the memory-usage queries implement at each step in Eq. (
5

\sigma_{k}
rather than
\rho_{k}
instructs the recursion step).
Exact solutions of a quantum recursion Eq. (
1

\epsilon=0
.
Note that the
\epsilon

M=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
memory-usage queries with the instruction
\sigma_{k}
at each step.

However, even when the implementation is always locally accurate, the discrepancy in the instruction state might culminate in
\Xi_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{n}-\rho_{n}\|_{1}
deviating uncontrollably, and as discussed in the main text, the recursion itself would occur unstable.
Let us discuss how this potential instability is accounted for by worst-case upper bounds using the triangle inequality.

For clarity we assume the recursion step consists of a single memory-call implemented by
M
memory-usage queries.
We then have that the ideal implementation is
\rho_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{0}]}(\rho_{0})=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0}]}(\sigma_{0})
and the practical implementation
\sigma_{1}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\sigma_{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1}\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\sigma_{0})-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0}]}(\sigma_{0})\right\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(1/M).

(S7)

We observe that the distance bound also holds in the channel level when two channels use the same quantum instruction, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0},M]}_{\text{QDP}}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{0}]}\right\|_{\Tr}=\mathcal{O}(1/M)\ .

(S8)

The QDP channel
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}
consists of
M
memory-usage queries.
For each such memory-usage query defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1}]}_{s}(\omega)=\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-i\hat{N}s}\left(\sigma_{1}\otimes\omega\right)e^{i\hat{N}s}\right]\ ,

(S9)

we have that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1}]}_{s}(\omega)=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1}]}_{s}(\omega)+\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-i\hat{N}s}\left((\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})\otimes\omega\right)e^{i\hat{N}s}\right]\ ,

(S10)

which leads to the bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1}]}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1}]}_{s}\right\|_{\Tr}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1}\|_{1}=\Xi_{1}\ .

(S11)

Here we see how the preparation error
\Xi_{1}
of
\sigma_{1}
compared to
\rho_{1}
influences the distance of the recursion step maps that define
\sigma_{2}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\sigma_{1})
and

\rho_{2}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\rho_{1})
so that
using telescoping and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}\right\|_{\Tr}\leq\frac{M}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1}]}_{1/M}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1}]}_{1/M}\right\|_{\Tr}=\mathcal{O}(M\Xi_{1})\ .

(S12)

This means that

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\sigma_{1})-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1}]}(\rho_{1})\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\sigma_{1})-\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}(\rho_{1})\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_{1},M]}_{\text{QDP}}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho_{1}]}\right\|_{\Tr}

(S13)

Analogously, we obtain

However, this exponential instability might not necessarily be the case, when there are other factors stabilizing the recursion.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\{\mathcal{N}\},\sigma_{k},M]}_{\text{BB}}(\sigma_{k})-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\{\mathcal{N}\},\sigma_{k}]}(\sigma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon\ ,

f
is Hermitian-preserving and
f(\mathmybb{1})\propto\mathmybb{1}
,
g
can also be made Hermitian-preserving and
\mathcal{N}(\mathmybb{1}^{\otimes d})\propto\mathmybb{1}
.

Proof.

Any polynomial of degree
d
can be written as
f(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}P_{s_{j}}^{(j)}
with
J
different terms, where

\displaystyle P_{s_{j}}^{(j)}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{s_{j}}A_{i}^{(j)}\rho\right)A_{0}^{(j)},

(S17)

for some operators
A_{i}^{(j)}
and
s_{j}\leq d
.
Now we define a linear map
\mathcal{N}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{d}|{v_{k}}\rangle\!\langle{w_{k}}|_{k}\right)=A^{(j)}_{s_{j}}|{v_{s_{j}}}\rangle\!\langle{w_{s_{j}}}|_{s_{j}}A^{(j)}_{s_{j}-1}\cdots A^{(j)}_{1}|{v_{1}}\rangle\!\langle{w_{1}}|_{1}A^{(j)}_{0}.

(S18)

for any matrix element
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{d}|{v_{k}}\rangle\!\langle{w_{k}}|_{k}
, where each
v_{k},w_{k}\in\{1,\cdots,\dim(\rho)\}
.
Then, it immediately gives
\mathcal{N}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}(\rho^{\otimes d})=P_{s_{j}}^{(j)}(\rho)
.
The summation of such linear maps,
\mathcal{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{N}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}
is also a linear map and yields the desired result
\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\otimes d})=f(\rho)
.

The second part of the Lemma can be shown similarly.
If
f(\mathmybb{1})\propto\mathmybb{1}
, then
\mathcal{N}(\mathmybb{1}^{\otimes d})=f(\mathmybb{1})\propto\mathmybb{1}
.
If
f
is Hermitian-preserving, we can make a decomposition where each
P_{s_{j}}^{(j)}
and thus the construction Eq. (
S18
) are also Hermitian-preserving.
∎

Combining Lemmas
3
and
4
, we can implement
e^{-if(\rho)}
with an arbitrarily small error by making queries to
e^{-i\rho^{\otimes d}t}
.
Hence, the dynamic unitary Eq. (
S14
) with
L
such memory calls can also be locally accurately implemented with error
\epsilon
by making
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
queries to
e^{-i\rho^{\otimes d}t}
.

II.2
Hermitian-preserving map exponentiation and its extension

We briefly recap the technique of Hermitian-preserving map exponentiation introduced in Ref.
Wei et al. 2023
.
The intuition behind the idea is the following:
if a map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
is not completely-positive and trace-preserving, then the operation
\sigma\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\sigma)
is not physically implementable.
However,
as long as
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
is Hermitian-preserving, the exponential
e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}
is always unitary for any
\rho
that is Hermitian, and so the unitary channel
\sigma\mapsto e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}\sigma e^{-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}
can be implemented (if one has a universal gate set). In particular, the approximation of this unitary
e^{i\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}
can be achieved given many copies of the reference state
\rho
, while the operations required for the implementation are oblivious to
\rho
.

Definition 5

(Hermitian-preserving map exponentiation
Wei et al. 2023
)
.

For a short interval
s\in\mathbb{R}
, a reference state
\rho
, and a linear Hermitian-preserving map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
, the Hermitian-preserving map exponentiation channel, acting on a state
\sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)=\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-i\hat{N}s}\left(\rho\otimes\sigma\right)e^{i\hat{N}s}\right]\ ,

(S19)

where
\hat{N}
is the partial transpose (denoted as
\transp_{1}
) of the Choi matrix corresponding to
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
, obtained by applying the map to the second subsystem of an unnormalized maximally entangled state
|{\Phi^{+}}\rangle_{12}=\sum_{j}|{j}\rangle_{1}|{j}\rangle_{2}
,

\displaystyle\hat{N}=[(\id\otimes\hat{\mathcal{N}})(\Phi^{+}_{12})]^{\transp_{1}}=\sum_{j,k}\left(|{k}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{1}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|\right)_{2}\right).

(S20)

It is useful to note that in general, the reference state
\rho
and the target state
\sigma
in Def.
5
need not be of the same dimension.
The map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
is in general a map from
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\dim(\mathcal{H})\neq\dim(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
.
In such cases, the reference state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
, while the output of the map
\mathcal{N}(\rho)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and the target state
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
Likewise, the operator
\hat{N}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\otimes\mathcal{H})
, since the maximally entangled state used to define the Choi matrix
|{\Phi^{+}}\rangle_{12}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.
The channel Eq. (
S19
) well approximates the unitary channel

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}(\sigma):=e^{is\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}(\sigma)e^{-is\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)}.

(S21)

This can be seen by Taylor expanding with respect to
s
, which yields

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sigma-is\Tr_{1}\left(\left[\hat{N},\rho\otimes\sigma\right]\right)+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})=\sigma-is\Tr_{1}\left(\left[\sum_{j,k}\left(|{k}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{1}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|\right)_{2}\right),\rho\otimes\sigma\right]\right)+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})\ .

(S22)

From the linearity of partial trace operation and commutator,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sigma-is\sum_{jk}\left[\Tr_{1}\left(|{k}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{1}\rho\otimes\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|\right)_{2}\sigma\right)-\Tr_{1}\left(\rho|{k}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{1}\otimes\sigma\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|\right)_{2}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})

(S23)

\displaystyle=\sigma-is\sum_{jk}\left[\langle{j}|\rho|{k}\rangle\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|\right),\sigma\right]+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})=\sigma-is\left[\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho),\sigma\right]+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})\ ,

where
\Tr_{1}[|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|_{1}\rho\otimes\sigma]=\Tr_{1}[\rho|{j}\rangle\!\langle{k}|_{1}\otimes\sigma]=\langle{j}|\rho|{k}\rangle\sigma
is used for the second equality.

For simplicity, we have for now omitted the fact that the magnitude of the error also depends on the map
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
. In particular, Ref.
Wei et al. 2023
proves a more rigorous bound

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\leq 8\|\hat{N}\|_{\infty}^{2}s^{2}\ ,

(S24)

whenever
\|\hat{N}\|_{\infty}s\in(0,0.8]
.
Setting
s\mapsto s/M
and repeating the channel
M
times, we can better approximate
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}
and achieve

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M}\right)^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M}\right)^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\leq 4\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\frac{s^{2}}{M}=\mathcal{O}\left(\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\frac{s^{2}}{M}\right)\ ,

(S25)

using the subadditivity
\|(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M})^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}\|\leq M\|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M}\|
.
Note that the
M
applications of
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s/M}
costs
M
copies of
\rho
.
Then Eq. (
S25
) can be recast as the following statement: the Hermitian-preserving map exponentiation unitary
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\hat{\mathcal{N}},\rho]}_{s}
can be implemented with an arbitrarily small error
\epsilon
by consuming
\mathcal{O}(\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}s^{2}/\epsilon)
copies of
\rho
.

Chronologically, the simplest case of
\hat{\mathcal{N}}=\id
, also known as density matrix exponentiation (DME)
Lloyd et al. 2014
;
Kimmel et al. 2017
, was studied prior to the more general scenario of Def.
5
.

Definition 6

(Density matrix exponentiation (DME)
Lloyd et al. 2014
)
.

For a short interval
s
and a reference state
\rho
, the density matrix exponentiation channel, acting on a state
\sigma
having the same dimension as
\rho
, is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)=\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-i\hat{S}s}\left(\rho\otimes\sigma\right)e^{i\hat{S}s}\right]\ ,

(S26)

where
\hat{S}
is the swap operator yielding
\hat{S}|{j}\rangle_{1}|{k}\rangle_{2}=|{k}\rangle_{1}|{j}\rangle_{2}
for all
j,k
.

The property of the swap operator
\hat{S}^{2}=1
facilitates the derivation of an explicit form of Eq. (
S26
):

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}\left(\sigma\right)=\cos^{2}(s)\sigma-i\sin(s)\cos(s)[\rho,\sigma]+\sin^{2}(s)\rho\ .

(S27)

Since Def.
6
is a special case of Def.
5
, it approximates the corresponding unitary channel
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\id,\rho]}_{s}
according to Eq. (
S19
), which we denote for simplicity as
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)\coloneq e^{-i\rho s}\sigma e^{i\rho s}
.
As a special instance of Eq. (
S24
) when
\|\hat{N}\|_{\infty}=\|\hat{S}\|_{\infty}=1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s/M}\right)^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s/M}\right)^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}\right\rVert_{\diamond}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{M}\right)\ .

(S28)

Now we prove the most general version of a single memory-call operation, namely exponential of polynomials.

Lemma 7

(Polynomial function exponentiation)
.

Let
f
be a Hermitian-preserving polynomial of degree
d
.
The unitary evolution
e^{if(\rho)}
can be approximated with an arbitrarily small error
\epsilon
, using

1.

\mathcal{O}(d\epsilon^{-1}F)
copies of
\rho
and

2.

a circuit oblivious to
\rho
, with depth
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}F)
, where
F
is a constant determined by the polynomial
f
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4
, a linear map
g
, such that
g(\rho^{\otimes d})=f(\rho)
for any
f
, exists.
Then we can approximate
e^{ig(\rho)}
using Hermitian-preserving map exponentiations
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{G},\rho^{\otimes d}]}_{s}
, defined in Def.
5
, where
\hat{G}
is the partial transpose of the Choi matrix of
g
.
Following Eq. (
S25
), we take
s=M^{-1}
and concatenate the channel
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{G},\rho^{\otimes d}]}_{M^{-1}}
for
M
times, which leads to the implementation error
\mathcal{O}(\lVert\hat{G}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}M^{-1})
.
Setting
M=\mathcal{O}(\lVert\hat{G}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\epsilon^{-1})
, we can achieve the approximation with arbitrarily small error
\epsilon
.
Each channel
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\hat{G},\rho^{\otimes d}]}_{s}
requires a circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{O}(d)
copies of
\rho
.
Hence,
M
concatenations of such channels cost
\mathcal{O}(d\epsilon^{-1}\lVert\hat{G}\rVert_{\infty}^{2})
copies of
\rho
and the circuit depth
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\lVert\hat{G}\rVert_{\infty}^{2})
.
∎

III
Fast spectral convergence and efficient quantum dynamic programming

We begin by illustrating a class of fixed-point iterations that admit efficient QDP implementation.
Our recursions operate on general mixed states but unitary; hence, it is natural to consider iterations with a fixed-point shared by isospectral states (states sharing the same eigenvalue spectrum).
In this section, we denote a generic quantum recursion in the form Eq. (
S14
) as
\hat{U}^{[\rho]}
.

Definition 8

(Fast spectral convergence)
.

Consider a quantum recursion
\hat{U}^{[\rho]}
that defines a fixed-point iteration

\displaystyle\rho\mapsto\hat{U}^{[\rho]}\rho\left(\hat{U}^{[\rho]}\right)^{\dagger}\ .

(S29)

This iteration
converges spectrally
, with respect to an initial state
\rho_{0}
, when there exists a fixed-point
\tau
that is attracting for all
\tilde{\rho}_{0}
satisfying
\mathrm{spec}(\tilde{\rho}_{0})=\mathrm{spec}(\rho_{0})
.
In other words, for any sequence
\{\tilde{\rho}_{0}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
obtained by the iteration, the trace distance
\delta_{n}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\tilde{\rho}_{n}\rVert_{1}<\delta_{n-1}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{n}=0
.
We say that spectral convergence is
fast
, when

\displaystyle\delta_{n+1}\leq h(\delta_{n})<\delta_{n},

(S30)

for a function
h(x)
whose derivative
h^{\prime}(x)<r
for some
0<r<1
and all relevant
x
.

Spectral convergence implies that the QDP implementation will also approach the same fixed-point, when the non-unitary error is not too large.
Furthermore, the function
h
quantifies the worst case performance of the algorithm; the distance to the fixed-point is guaranteed to be arbitrarily close to
h^{N}(\delta_{0})
after
N
iterations, where
h^{N}
indicates the
N
times composition
h\circ\cdots\circ h
.

III.1
Quantum dynamic programming with a mixed initial state

We first establish a theorem that demonstrates the efficiency of quantum dynamic programming when the initial and all the instruction states are mixed.

Theorem 9

(Spectral convergence is sufficient for efficient global convergence of QDP)
.

If a quantum recursion
\hat{U}^{[\rho]}
satisfies fast spectral convergence for some initial state
\rho_{0}
, then the QDP implementation of
N
iterations prepares a state
\rho^{\prime}_{N}
, whose distance to the fixed-point
\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{N}}\leq h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\epsilon
, by using a circuit with depth
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}\epsilon^{-1})
.

Proof.

Let us denote the unitary channel corresponding to the operator
\hat{U}^{[\rho]}
as
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}
.
The recursion is spectrally converging.
Hence, there exists a fixed-point
\tau
, such that the distance
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\rho\rVert_{1}\eqcolon\delta_{\rho}
follows

\displaystyle\delta_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}(\rho)}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}(\rho)\right\rVert_{1}\leq h(\delta_{\rho})<\delta_{\rho}\ .

(S31)

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle h(\delta+\epsilon)\leq h(\delta)+r\epsilon\ ,

(S32)

for some
r<1
and any
\delta,\epsilon
of interest from the assumption that the spectral convergence is fast.

From Lemma
7
, we are able to locally accurately implement the unitary channel
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}
by a
non-unitary
channel
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho]}
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq\eta,

(S33)

for any
\eta>0
, by making
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-1})
memory-usage queries each consuming a copy of
\rho
.
The circuit depth for this implementation is also
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-1})
.

Suppose that the sequence of states
\{\rho^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n}
is obtained from such emulation starting from
\rho^{\prime}_{0}=\rho_{0}
and thus recursively defined

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{n}=\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}\left(\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}\right)\ .

(S34)

This sequence might deviate from the desired sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
very quickly and, in general,
\mathrm{spec}\{\rho^{\prime}_{n}\}\neq\mathrm{spec}\{\tau\}
.

We additionally consider a sequence of states
\{\tilde{\rho}_{n}\}_{n}
, defined by the exact unitary recursion with an erroneous instruction state
\tilde{\rho}_{n}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}(\tilde{\rho}_{n-1})
starting from
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=\rho_{0}
. Hence,
\mathrm{spec}\{\tilde{\rho}_{n}\}=\mathrm{spec}\{\tau\}
.
Let us denote
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tilde{\rho}_{n}-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}\eqcolon\varepsilon_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{0}=0
.
We first analyze how
\varepsilon_{n}
scales.
Observe that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\rho}_{n})-\rho^{\prime}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\rho}_{n})-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{n}+\eta\ ,

(S35)

from the triangle inequality, the unitary invariance of the trace norm, and Eq. (
S33
).
Therefore,
\varepsilon_{n}
scales linearly as
\varepsilon_{n}\leq n\eta
.
The triangle inequality between
\tau
,
\rho^{\prime}_{n}
, and
\rho_{n}
yields
\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{n}}\leq\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}+\varepsilon_{n}
, which connects the quantity of our interest
\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{n}}
and the term
\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}
derived from a state
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
isospectral to the desired fixed-point
\tau
.

Next, recall that
\left\lVert e^{if(\rho)}-e^{if(\sigma)}\right\rVert\leq C^{\prime}\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert

for some constant
C^{\prime}
, from the mean value theorem for operators.
This implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho]}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\sigma]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq C\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}\

(S36)

for some constant
C
.
Then we can write

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n+1}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\rho}_{n}]}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right)\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\rho}_{n}]}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right)-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right)\right\rVert_{1}\leq h\left(\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\rho}_{n}]}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq h\left(\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}\right)+C\varepsilon_{n},

(S37)

where we use the triangle inequality for the first, the assumption Eq. (
S31
) for the second, and Eq. (
S36
) for the last inequality.
The property of
h
, Eq. (
S32
) gives

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}\leq h\left(\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n-1}}\right)+C\varepsilon_{n-1}\leq h\left(h\left(\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n-2}}\right)+C\varepsilon_{n-2}\right)+C\varepsilon_{n-1}\leq h\circ h\Big(\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{n-2}}\Big)+rC\varepsilon_{n-2}+C\varepsilon_{n-1}\ ,

(S38)

which leads to

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\rho}_{N}}\leq h^{N}\left(\delta_{\rho_{0}}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{N-n-1}(Cn\eta)=h^{N}\left(\delta_{\rho_{0}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(N\eta)\ .

(S39)

Therefore the final state
\rho^{\prime}_{N}
after
N
iterations has the distance

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{N}}=h^{N}\left(\delta_{\rho_{0}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\big(N\eta\big)\ .

(S40)

To obtain the fixed deviation
\epsilon>0
from
h^{N}(\delta_{\rho_{0}})
after
N
iterations, we can set
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon N^{-1})
, which only requires the circuit of total depth
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}\epsilon^{-1})
.
In case
\delta_{\rho_{N}}=h^{N}(\delta_{\rho_{0}})
, the exact algorithm and the QDP iteration can be made to coincide after
N
steps with an arbitrarily small deviation
\epsilon
.
∎

III.2
Quantum dynamic programming with a pure initial state

When the initial state of the recursion—and thus also the target final state of the recursion—is pure, a more efficient QDP implementation can be made, by adopting a subroutine that reduces the mixedness of instruction states.
The initial idea for this subroutine comes from Ref.
Cirac et al. 1999
, which assumed that the system of interest is a qubit.
Recently, generalizations to systems with an arbitrary dimension was proposed
Childs et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024
, albeit with a less straightforward mixedness reduction performance.
We adapt the protocol in Ref.
Childs et al. 2025
here.

Lemma 10

(Interferential mixedness reduction (IMR))
.

Let
\rho=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|{v_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{i}}|
be a
d
-dimensional density matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}
and an orthonormal basis
\{|{v_{i}}\rangle\}
.
Using an auxiliary qubit and a controlled swap operation on the two copies of
\rho
, a state
\rho^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i}|{v_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{i}}|
with
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{1+\lambda_{1}}{1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{1}
can be prepared with a probability
\frac{1}{2}(1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})
.
When the mixedness parameter
x\coloneq 1-\lambda_{1}
is small,
x^{\prime}\coloneq 1-\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{x}{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
, effectively halving the mixedness of the state
\rho
, with the success probability larger than
(1-x)
.
This procedure can be done without any prior knowledge of the state
\rho
.

Proof.

Let us denote the space each copy of
\rho
lives in as
1
and
2
.
The main idea of
Cirac et al. 1999
and
Childs et al. 2025
is to apply the projector
\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}+S_{12}}{2}
, where
\mathmybb{1}_{12}
is the identity on the total space of two copies and
S_{12}
is a swap between the first and the second copies.
This operation can be implemented by a linear combination of unitaries utilizing an additional ancilla qubit
|{0}\rangle_{A}

Long 2006
;
Childs and Wiebe 2012
, since both
\mathmybb{1}_{12}
and
S_{12}
are unitaries.
To a state
|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}\otimes\rho^{\otimes 2}
appended with ancilla, apply a unitary

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\left(H_{A}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{12}\right)\left(|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{12}+|{1}\rangle\!\langle{1}|_{A}\otimes S_{12}\right)\left(H_{A}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{12}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}+|{1}\rangle\!\langle{1}|_{A}\right)\otimes\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}+S_{12}}{2}+\left(|{1}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}+|{0}\rangle\!\langle{1}|_{A}\right)\otimes\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}-S_{12}}{2}\ ,

(S41)

by concatenating Hadamard operators
H_{A}
acting on the ancilla and a controlled swap operation, applying the swap
S_{12}
between two copies conditioned on the ancilla state
|{1}\rangle
.
After this operation, the ancilla plus system state becomes

\displaystyle U\left(|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}\otimes\rho^{\otimes 2}\right)U^{\dagger}=

\displaystyle|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}\otimes\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}+S_{12}}{2}\right)\rho^{\otimes 2}\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}+S_{12}}{2}\right)+|{1}\rangle\!\langle{1}|_{A}\otimes\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}-S_{12}}{2}\right)\rho^{\otimes 2}\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}-S_{12}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\left[|{1}\rangle\!\langle{0}|_{A}\otimes\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}-S_{12}}{2}\right)\rho^{\otimes 2}\left(\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{12}+S_{12}}{2}\right)+\mathrm{h.c.}\right]\ .

(S42)

By measuring the ancilla in
\{|{0}\rangle_{A},|{1}\rangle_{A}\}
basis, we effectively achieve (unnormalized) system states

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}[2(\rho\otimes\rho)+S_{12}(\rho\otimes\rho)+(\rho\otimes\rho)S_{12}],\quad&\text{if $|{0}\rangle_{A}$ is measured,}\\
\frac{1}{4}[2(\rho\otimes\rho)-S_{12}(\rho\otimes\rho)-(\rho\otimes\rho)S_{12}]\ ,\quad&\text{if $|{1}\rangle_{A}$ is measured,}\end{cases}

(S43)

with probability given by their traces
\frac{1}{2}(1\pm\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})
.

If the second system is traced out from Eq. (
S43
), the remaining state becomes

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}\propto\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(\rho+\rho^{2}),\quad&\text{if $|{0}\rangle_{A}$ is measured,}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\rho^{2})\ ,\quad&\text{if $|{1}\rangle_{A}$ is measured.}\end{cases}

(S44)

For our purpose, the protocol is successful when
|{0}\rangle_{A}
is measured, in which case the normalized density matrix

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\rho+\rho^{2}}{1+\Tr[\rho^{2}]}=\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{2}}|{v_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{i}}|\eqcolon\sum_{i}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}|{v_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{i}}|

(S45)

remains, and
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{1+\lambda_{1}}{1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\lambda_{1}
.
From
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}>\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}
, we obtain
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}>\lambda_{1}
whenever
\lambda_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
.

Finally, we consider the case
(1-\lambda_{1})\ll 1
.
First, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}=\frac{1-\lambda^{\prime}_{1}}{1-\lambda_{1}}=\frac{1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{2}}{(1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})(1-\lambda_{1})}=\frac{1-\lambda_{1}+\sum_{i>1}\lambda_{i}^{2}}{(1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})(1-\lambda_{1})}\leq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}+(1-\lambda_{1})^{2}}{(1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})(1-\lambda_{1})}=\frac{1+(1-\lambda_{1})}{1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}},

(S46)

where we used the Hölder’s inequality
\sum_{k>1}\lvert y_{k}z_{k}\rvert\leq(\sum_{k>1}\lvert y_{k}\rvert^{p})^{1/p}(\sum_{k>1}\lvert z_{k}\rvert^{q})^{1/q}
for vectors
y_{k}=z_{k}=\lambda_{k}
and
p=q=2
, which gives
\sum_{i>1}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq(\sum_{i>1}\lambda_{i})^{2}=(1-\lambda_{1})^{2}
.
On the other hand, by using
0\leq\sum_{i>1}\lambda_{i}^{2}
, the ratio becomes

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}\leq\frac{1+(1-\lambda_{1})}{1+\lambda_{1}^{2}}=\frac{1+x}{2-2x+x^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(x).

(S47)

Second, the success probability
\frac{1}{2}(1+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})\geq\frac{1}{2}(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})\geq\lambda_{1}=1-x
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Using IMR protocol in Lemma
10
, we can efficiently combat the accumulation of the non-unitary error
\eta
, by spending more copies after each QDP implementation of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho]}
to reduce the mixedness of the resulting state.
Furthermore, it is guaranteed that the state can be arbitrarily close to a pure state by repeating the IMR subroutine.

R
rounds of IMR protocl form a mixedness reduction subroutine that suppresses the mixedness parameter
x\coloneq 1-\lambda_{1}
under a certain threshold.

Lemma 11

(QDP mixedness reduction subroutine)
.

Let
\rho
be a density matrix with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1-x
for some mixedness parameter
x\in[0,\frac{1}{3}]
, corresponding to the eigenvector
|{v_{1}}\rangle
.
Given any maximum tolerable failure probability
q_{\mathrm{th}}\geq 0
, one can choose any parameter
\mathsf{g}\in\mathbb{R}
and prepare
M
copies of
\rho^{\prime}
, such that:

1.

\rho^{\prime}
has the largest eigenvalue
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\geq 1-\frac{x}{\mathsf{g}}
, corresponding to the same eigenvector
|{v_{1}}\rangle
.

2.

a number of
R=\mathcal{O}(\log(\mathsf{g}))
IMR rounds in Lemma
10
is used,

3.

a total of
M\times(\frac{2}{c})^{R}
copies of
\rho
is consumed,
with
c\in(0,1)
satisfying

\displaystyle c=1-x-M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{R}{q_{\rm th}}\right)}

(S48)

4.

the success probability of the entire subroutine is
q_{\rm succ}\geq 1-q_{\rm th}
.

Note that
M
must be sufficiently large to guarantee
c>0
.

Proof.

To prove this lemma, we analyze
R
sequential rounds of the IMR protocol, with initial and final states
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
.
Let us denote the intermediate states generated via the IMR protocols as
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{R}
, where
\chi_{0}=\rho
and
\chi_{R}=\rho^{\prime}
; and let
\{y_{j}\}_{j=0}^{R}
be the mixedness parameter (i.e.
1
minus the largest eigenvalue) for each
\chi_{j}
with
y_{0}=x
and
y_{R}\leq\frac{x}{\mathsf{g}}
.
Furthermore, let us denote
M_{j}
to be the number of copies of an intermediate state
\chi_{j}
generated; hence, we obtain
M_{R}=M
copies of the desired state
\rho^{\prime}
at the end, and aim to show that
M_{0}=M\times(\frac{2}{c})^{R}
suffices.

From Eq. (
S47
), the mixedness parameter after
j
rounds of the IMR protocol becomes
y_{j}\leq\frac{1+y_{j-1}}{2-2y_{j-1}+y_{j-1}^{2}}y_{j-1}
.
The final reduction
\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}=\frac{y_{R}}{y_{0}}\leq\mathsf{g}^{-1}
is achievable if
R
is sufficiently big to satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{y_{R}}{y_{0}}=\prod_{j=1}^{R}\frac{y_{j}}{y_{j-1}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{R}\frac{1+y_{j-1}}{2-2y_{j-1}+y_{j-1}^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{1+x}{2-2x+x^{2}}\right)^{R}\leq\mathsf{g}^{-1}\ .

(S49)

The second last inequality follows from two facts: i) the factor
\frac{1+y}{2-2y+y^{2}}
monotonically increases as
y
increases ii)
y_{j}\leq y_{0}
for all
j
.
Since
\log(\frac{1+x}{2-2x+x^{2}})>0
by the assumption
x\leq\frac{1}{3}
, the number of rounds
R=\mathcal{O}(\log(\mathsf{g}))
is sufficient as claimed.

The parameter
c
in the statement of the lemma can be interpreted as the ‘survival rate’ after each round of the IMR protocol.
More specifically, the
j
-th round of the protocol is successful if at least
\frac{c}{2}M_{j-1}
output states
\chi_{j}
is prepared from
M_{j-1}
copies of
\chi_{j-1}
.
If successful, we discard all the surplus output copies and set
M_{j}=\frac{c}{2}M_{j-1}
.
If not, we declare that the whole subroutine has failed.
Hence, if the entire subroutine succeeds, the final number of copies
M=M_{R}
is related to the initial number of copies as
M_{0}=(2/c)^{R}M
.

Finally, we estimate the success probability
q_{\rm succ}
given
R,M,c,x
and require it to be lower bounded with
(1-q_{\rm th})
.
The failure probability of each round will be bounded using Hoeffding’s inequality.
From Lemma
10
, the success probability of preparing one copy of
\chi_{j}
from a pair
\chi_{j-1}^{\otimes 2}
has a lower bound
p(y_{j-1})\geq 1-y_{j-1}
.
At each round,
\frac{M_{j-1}}{2}
independent trials of this IMR protocol are conducted.
The probability of more than
c\frac{M_{j-1}}{2}
attempts succeeds, i.e. the success probability of a round, is

\displaystyle q_{r}^{(j)}=1-F\left(\left\lceil\frac{cM_{j-1}}{2}-1\right\rceil;\frac{M_{j-1}}{2},p(y_{j-1})\right)\geq 1-F\left(\frac{cM_{j-1}}{2};\frac{M_{j-1}}{2},1-y_{j-1}\right)\ ,

(S50)

where
F(k;n,q)
is the cumulative binomial distribution function defined as

\displaystyle F(cn;n,p)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\binom{n}{i}p^{i}(1-p)^{n-i}\ .

(S51)

Furthermore, the Hoeffding’s inequality gives
F(cn;n,p)\leq e^{-2n(p-c)^{2}}
, and consequently sets the bound

\displaystyle q_{r}^{(j)}\geq 1-e^{-M_{j}\left(1-y_{j-1}-c\right)^{2}}\ .

(S52)

The success probability of an entire subroutine can then be bounded as

\displaystyle q_{\rm succ}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{R}q_{r}^{(j)}\geq\prod_{j=1}^{R}\left(1-e^{-M_{j}\left(1-y_{j-1}-c\right)^{2}}\right)>1-\sum_{j=1}^{R}e^{-M_{j}\left(1-y_{j-1}-c\right)^{2}}\ ,

(S53)

where the last inequality uses
\prod_{j}(1-q_{j})>1-\sum_{j}q_{j}
that holds when
q_{j}\in(0,1)
for all
j
.
Moreover, by recalling
x\geq y_{j-1}
and
M\leq M_{j}
for all
j
, the final bound

\displaystyle q_{\rm succ}>1-Re^{-M\left(1-x-c\right)^{2}}

(S54)

is obtained.
The prescribed failure threshold
q_{\rm th}
holds when

e^{-M\left(1-x-c\right)^{2}}=\frac{q_{\rm th}}{R}
,
or equivalently, when
M
is sufficiently large and Eq. (
S48
) is true.
∎

During the earlier recursions,
M
is always sufficiently large to guarantee that
c
is well separated from
0
.
For later recursions, in particular for the last recursion where only
M=1
is needed, Eq. (
S48
) can become close to zero, or even negative.
To guarantee positive and non-vanishing
c
, we allow some redundancy of the final state
\rho_{N}
and prepare
M>1
copies of it.

Now we demonstrate the advantage of using mixedness reduction subroutines for pure state QDP.

Theorem 12

(Efficient QDP for pure states)
.

Suppose that a quantum recursion
\hat{U}^{[\psi]}
satisfies fast spectral convergence for some pure initial state
\psi_{0}=|{\psi_{0}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{0}}|
.
Then for any
p_{\mathrm{th}}>0
, the QDP implementation of
N
iterations prepares a state
\rho^{\prime}_{N}
with success probability
1-p_{\rm th}
, such that the distance of
\rho^{\prime}_{N}
to the pure fixed-point is

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{N}}\leq h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\epsilon,

(S55)

and
0<\epsilon<\frac{2}{3}
.
This implementation requires
\epsilon^{-N}e^{\mathcal{O}(N)}\log(p_{\rm th}^{-1})
copies of
\psi_{0}
and a circuit with depth
\mathcal{O}(N\epsilon^{-1})
.

Proof.

The target state, i.e. the fixed-point, is again denoted as
\tau
and the distance from it as
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\rho\rVert_{1}\eqcolon\delta_{\rho}
.
Eqs. (
S31
)–(
S33
) still holds, yet the recursion starting from
\rho^{\prime}_{0}=\psi_{0}
is now defined as

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{n}=\mathrm{IMR}\circ\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}),

(S56)

where
\mathrm{IMR}
denotes the
R
rounds of the IMR subroutine as in Lemma
11
.
Suppose that
R
is sufficiently large to guarantee that
\rho_{n}
has the largest eigenvalue
1-x
with a mixedness parameter
x<\varepsilon
for some small
\varepsilon
and the corresponding eigenvector
|{\tilde{\psi}_{n}}\rangle
.

Then we can write

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\tilde{\psi}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n}-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\tilde{\psi}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}+\varepsilon=\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n}}+\varepsilon\ ,

(S57)

where triangle inequality and the assumption on the mixedness of
\rho^{\prime}_{n}
give rise to the first and the second inequality, respectively.
Then the important quantity becomes
\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n}}
, which evolves as

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}\leq h(\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n}})+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1},

(S58)

where the second inequality comes from Eq. (
S31
).
The second term can be further bounded by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}\ .

(S59)

Note that IMR subroutines do not change the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the state; hence
\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}
is the eigenvector of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of it.
The first term of the bound Eq. (
S59
) is then the mixedness parameter of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}_{n}
has the mixedness parameter smaller than
\varepsilon
and
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}
is only
\eta
different from the unitary operator
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}
, we obtain
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon+\eta
.
The second term of the bound Eq. (
S59
) can be further expanded as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}(\tilde{\psi}_{n})\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}_{n}-\tilde{\psi}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{[\tilde{\psi}_{n}]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}\leq C\varepsilon+\eta\ ,

(S60)

for some constant
C
.
The second and third inequalities follow from data processing inequality, definition of the channel distance, and Eqs. (
S33
) and (
S36
).
Combining everything,

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n+1}}\leq h(\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{n}})+(C+1)\varepsilon+2\eta\ .

(S61)

Using Eq. (
S32
), and putting
z=(C+1)\varepsilon+2\eta
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{N}}\leq h(\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{N-1}})+z\leq h(h(\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{N-2}})+z)+z\leq h^{2}(\delta_{\tilde{\psi}_{N-2}})+rz+z\leq\cdots\leq h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}r^{k}z=h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}(\eta).

(S62)

From Eq. (
S57
),

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{N}}\leq h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}(\eta).

(S63)

We can get a desired result
\delta_{\rho^{\prime}_{N}}\leq h^{N}(\delta_{0})+\epsilon
by setting
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
For simplicity, let us choose
\eta=\varepsilon
.

R
rounds of IMR need to reduce the mixedness parameter of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n-1})
to that of
\rho^{\prime}_{n}
each of which is lower bounded by
2\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon
, i.e.
\mathsf{g}=2
.
Since
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
is small,
R=\mathcal{O}(1)
suffices.
The requirement that
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
can be met by using
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
depth circuit for each
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n}]}
implementation.
Hence, the overall circuit depth for
N
recursions will be
\mathcal{O}(N\epsilon^{-1})
.

Finally, we derive the number of initial state copies needed for the algorithm.
Let us denote
\mathtt{I}_{n}
and
\mathtt{O}_{n}
to be the number of
\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}
and
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}(\rho^{\prime}_{n-1})
copies before and after
n
’th recursion step.
We require
\mathtt{I}_{N+1}\geq 1
and
\mathtt{I}_{1}
will be the number of
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
copies we need.
As the implementation of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{[\rho^{\prime}_{n-1}]}
requires
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-1})=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
copies,

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})\mathtt{O}_{n}.

(S64)

\mathtt{I}_{n+1}
and
\mathtt{O}_{n}
are related by the number of mixedness reduction rounds
R
and survival rate
c
as

\displaystyle\mathtt{O}_{n}=\left(\frac{2}{c}\right)^{R}\mathtt{I}_{n+1}.

(S65)

R=\mathcal{O}(1)
, while
c
can be determined by the success probability
q_{\rm succ}
of
R
round mixedness reduction subroutines using Eq. (
S48
) with
M=\mathtt{I}_{n+1}
.
Assume that we impose the success probability
q_{\rm succ}
to be higher than
1-q_{\rm th}
for some threshold value
q_{\rm th}
.
The probability of all
N
mixedness reduction protocols to be successful is

\displaystyle p_{\rm succ}=q_{\rm succ}^{N}\geq\left(1-q_{\rm th}\right)^{N}\geq 1-Nq_{\rm th}\ ,

(S66)

where the second inequality follows from the Taylor expansion.
Thus setting
q_{\rm th}=\frac{p_{\rm th}}{N}
ensures that
p_{\rm succ}\geq 1-p_{\rm th}
as required.

Now we fix the value of
c
.
Recalling Eq. (
S48
) and
\varepsilon<\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\frac{1}{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{N+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\log(\frac{R}{q_{\rm th}})}<\frac{1}{6}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c=\frac{1}{2},

(S67)

or
\mathtt{I}_{N+1}=\mathcal{O}(\log(p_{\rm th}^{-1}N))
implies
c=\frac{1}{2}
.
Combining everything,

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{n}=\epsilon^{-1}e^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\mathtt{I}_{n+1}\ ,

(S68)

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{1}=\epsilon^{-N}e^{\mathcal{O}(N)}\mathtt{I}_{N+1}=\epsilon^{-N}e^{\mathcal{O}(N)}\mathcal{O}(\log(p_{\rm th}))\ ,

(S69)

which concludes the proof.
∎

IV
Analysis of the nested fixed-point Grover search

In this section, we delineate the entire protocol for the QDP implementation of nested fixed-point Grover search recursions, using it as an illustrative example.
The exact recursion is analyzed in Sec.
IV.1
with the explicit formula giving the distance between the resulting state and the target state after each recursion.
QDP implementation of the recursion is carried out by combining two subroutines: approximation of the recursion unitary using DME memory-usage queries and the interferential mixedness reduction (IMR) subroutine that maintains the mixedness of the instruction states below some threshold.
The first subroutine is analysed in detail in Sec.
IV.2
.
In the rest of the section, we calculate the total cost of this implementation, namely the depth of the circuit and the number of copies needed, and show that the final state can be made arbitrarily close to the desired final state.

IV.1
Exact implementation of nested fixed-point Grover search

The analysis of the nested fixed-point Grover search, introduced in Ref.
Yoder et al. 2014
, involves Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind

\displaystyle T_{m}(x)=\begin{cases}\cos\bigl(m\arccos(x)\bigr),&\lvert x\rvert\leq 1\ ,\\
\cosh\bigl(m\arcosh(x)\bigr),&\lvert x\rvert>1\ .\end{cases}

(S70)

We observe that if
\lvert x\rvert\leq 1
then

\lvert T_{m}(x)\rvert\leq 1
and
T_{l}(T_{m}(x))=T_{lm}(x)
.
Choosing
l=m^{-1}
gives a special case

\displaystyle T_{m^{-1}}\left(T_{m}(x)\right)=T_{1}(x)=x,

(S71)

and we will also use the property that
T_{m}(1)=1
.

We begin with
n=0
, which is to specify that we are given the initial state
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
, and target state
|{\tau}\rangle
.
In the
n
th step of the iteration, we apply a composite unitary operation with
L
memory-calls

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}=\hat{E}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{\alpha_{L}}\hat{E}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{L}}\cdots\hat{E}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{\alpha_{1}}\hat{E}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{1}}

(S72)

to the iterated state
|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle
obtaining
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle=\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle
.
The composite unitary operation in Eq. (
S72
) consists of partial reflection unitaries defined as

\displaystyle\hat{E}^{[\psi]}_{s}\coloneq\mathmybb{1}-(1-e^{-is})|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\ ,

(S73)

with angle
s
, where we have adapted the notation
\psi=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
and
\tau=|{\tau}\rangle\!\langle{\tau}|
for pure states.
When
s=\pi
, we obtain the usual reflection unitary around the state
|{\psi}\rangle
.
In Eq. (
S72
), following in the steps of
Yoder et al. 2014
, we consider
L
partial reflections around both
|{\tau}\rangle
and
|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle
with angles

\displaystyle\alpha_{l}(q)=-\beta_{L-l+1}(q)=2\cot^{-1}\left(\tan\left(\frac{2\pi l}{2L+1}\right)\sqrt{1-\left[T_{1/(2L+1)}(q)\right]^{-2}}\right)\ ,

(S74)

defined with some number
q
.
The choice of the parameter
q
determines the final distance to the target state as
Yoder et al. 2014

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi]}_{L}(\psi)\left(\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi]}_{L}\right)^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{1}=qT_{2L+1}\left(T_{1/(2L+1)}(q^{-1})\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi\rVert_{1}\right)\ .

(S75)

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that the parameter
q
in Eq. (
S74
) is chosen to be
q=\delta_{n}
for the recursion unitary
\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}
at any
n
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\sech\left((2L+1)\arcsech\left(\delta_{n-1}\right)\right)\ ,\quad\forall n>0\ ,

(S76)

starting from the base
\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{0}\rVert_{1}
.
Then

\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\psi_{n}\right\rVert_{1}=\delta_{n}

for all
n
, where
\psi_{n}
is recursively defined as
|{\psi_{n}}\rangle=\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}|{\psi_{n-1}}\rangle
.

Proof.

We first observe that Eq. (
S76
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{-1}=T_{2L+1}\left(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\right)\ ,

(S77)

by recalling that
{\rm arcsech}(x)={\rm arcosh}(x^{-1})
. In particular, this also means that by applying
T_{1/(2L+1)}
to both sides, we get

\displaystyle T_{1/(2L+1)}(\delta_{n}^{-1})=T_{1/(2L+1)}\left(T_{2L+1}\left(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\right)\right)=\delta_{n-1}^{-1}.

(S78)

We may now use this identity in simplifying the trace distance of interest: starting from the general relation Eq. (
S75
) with
q=\delta_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n}\rVert_{1}=\delta_{n}T_{2L+1}\left(T_{1/(2L+1)}\bigl(\delta_{n}^{-1}\bigr)\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}\right)=\delta_{n}T_{2L+1}\left(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}\right)\ ,

(S79)

where the second equality comes from applying Eqs. (
S78
).
If the Lemma is true for step
n-1
, i.e. if
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}=\delta_{n-1}
, then Eq. (
S79
) leads to
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n}\rVert_{1}=\delta_{n}
using
T_{2L+1}(1)=1
.
Since the base case
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{0}\rVert_{1}=\delta_{0}
is true, the lemma holds by induction.
∎

The explicit form of the
\delta_{N}
, i.e. the distance to the target state after
N
iterations can also be derived.
From Eqs. (
S71
) and (
S77
),
\delta_{N}^{-1}=T_{(2L+1)^{N}}\left(\delta_{0}^{-1}\right)
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle\delta_{N}=\sech\left((2L+1)^{N}\arcosh\Bigl(\delta_{0}^{-1}\Bigr)\right)\ .

(S80)

For large
N
, Eq. (
S80
) asymptotically behaves as
\delta_{N}=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\arcosh\bigl(\delta_{0}^{-1}\bigr)(2L+1)^{N}}\right)\
.
A typical setting of a Grover search starts from the initial fidelity
F_{0}=1-\delta_{0}^{2}\ll 1
, e.g. the initial state is a superposition of all basis states.
Then the decay rate can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\arcosh\left(\delta_{0}^{-1}\right)=\arcosh((1-F_{0})^{-1/2})=\sqrt{F_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(F_{0}^{3/2})\ ,

(S81)

by Taylor expansion.
If only the leading order is taken,
\delta_{N}\sim e^{-\sqrt{F_{0}}(2L+1)^{N}}
, and thus

\displaystyle(2L+1)^{N}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\delta_{N}^{-1}\right)F_{0}^{-1/2}\right)

(S82)

is needed for the final distance
\delta_{N}
.
The circuit depth for the entire algorithm is proportional to Eq. (
S82
) due to the unfolding implementation, and it contains the characteristic Grover scaling factor
F_{0}^{-1/2}
.
If a non-recursive algorithm is applied, Eq. (
S80
) can still be used with
N=1
.
Denote the final distance as

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}=\sech\left((2L^{\prime}+1)\arcosh\left(\delta_{0}^{-1}\right)\right),

(S83)

when the alternation length of this algorithm is
L^{\prime}
. For
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{N}
, the alternation length of the non-nested algorithm should satisfy
2L^{\prime}+1=(2L+1)^{N}
, resulting in exponentially many partial reflections.
Note that Eq. (
S82
) then implies
L^{\prime}\sim F_{0}^{-1/2}
, which displays the quantum advantage compared to the classical search requiring
L^{\prime}\sim F_{0}^{-1}
.

IV.2
Robustness of dynamic programming for the Grover search

In this section, we study the nested fixed-point Grover search algorithm implemented with bounded noise arising from using a dynamic programming approach.
We establish the robustness of the algorithm against small implementation errors.

IV.2.1
Distance bounds after a DME implementation

In this subsection, we introduce the DME implementation of an iteration of the dynamic Grover search.
We first establish (through Proposition
14
and Corollary
15
) that the DME implementation still has the essential feature of the Grover search, namely all relevant states are effectively two-dimensional.
Then the main results Lemma
17
and Corollary
18
provide the upper bound of a distance after one iteration of DME implemented Grover search, starting from a mixed state.
In short, we observe that whenever the initially prepared state is close to being pure, then the distance to our Grover target state still follows a similar recursive relation as before, namely Eq. (
S99
).

The ‘dynamic’ parts of the recursion unitary
\hat{\Gamma}^{[\rho]}_{L}
are the memory-calls
\hat{E}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}
, i.e. partial reflectors around the state
\rho
.
Hence, DME (Def.
6
) is the right choice of memory-usage query to use for implementing the memory-call
\hat{E}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}
.
To recap, the DME memory-usage query can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}\left(\sigma\right)=\cos^{2}(s)\sigma-i\sin(s)\cos(s)[\rho,\sigma]+\sin^{2}(s)\rho\ ,

(S84)

with the duration
s
of our choice.
If
M
queries are made with
s=\frac{\alpha}{M}
, the implemented channel
(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma))^{M}
approximates the memory-call
\hat{E}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}
with error
\mathcal{O}(1/M)
.

There are two key considerations in the implementation of quantum instructions via DME:

1.

The implementation of a recursion step
\hat{\Gamma}^{[\psi]}_{L}
becomes non-unitary even when the instruction state
\psi=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
and the input state
\phi=|{\phi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|
are pure. To see this, consider a special case of DME protocol with pure instruction state
\psi
and input state
\phi
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\psi]}_{s}(\phi)=\cos^{2}(s)\phi+\sin^{2}(s)\psi-i\sin(s)\cos(s)\langle{\psi}|{\phi}\rangle|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|+\mathrm{h.c.}.

(S85)

We may directly compute the purity as

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left\{\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\psi]}_{s}(\phi)\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\cos^{4}(s)+\sin^{4}(s)+2\sin^{2}(s)\cos^{2}(s)\left(2\lvert\langle{\psi}|{\phi}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\lvert\langle{\psi}|{\phi}\rangle\rvert^{4}\right)

(S86)

\displaystyle=1-2\sin^{2}(s)\cos^{2}(s)\left(1-\lvert\langle{\psi}|{\phi}\rangle\rvert^{2}\right)^{2}\neq 1,

hence
\rho_{n}
, the state after
n
iterations of the Grover search recursion, is no longer pure in general.

2.

Another important property to check is whether DME maps a target state supported on a two-dimensional subspace to another (now potentially mixed) state supported on the same space.
In the exact algorithm, state vectors always stay in the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}\coloneq\mathrm{span}\left\{|{\tau}\rangle,|{\tau^{\perp}}\rangle\coloneq\frac{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle-\langle{\tau}|{\psi_{0}}\rangle|{\tau}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-|\langle{\tau}|{\psi_{0}}\rangle|^{2}}}\right\},

(S87)

defined by the initial state vector
|{\psi_{0}}\rangle
and the target state vector
|{\tau}\rangle
.
This restriction is the reason that the fixed-point Grover search of
Yoder et al. 2014
could utilize a
\mathrm{SU(2)}
representation.
To establish similar restriction for mixed states, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}\coloneq\left\{\rho\,|\,\rho:\text{density matrix},\ \mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subset\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}\right\}

(S88)

the set of density matrices having support only in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}
.
Proposition
14
then rigorously shows that all relevant density operators resulting from the DME implementation stay in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.

Proposition 14
.

Suppose two states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.
The state after a DME channel
\sigma^{\prime}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)
defined in Eq. (
S26
) is also contained in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.
In other words, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rel}}]}_{s}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rel}})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rel}}\
.

Proof.

From direct calculation,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma)|{\phi}\rangle=\cos^{2}(s)\sigma|{\phi}\rangle-i\sin(s)\cos(s)[\rho,\sigma]|{\phi}\rangle+\sin^{2}(s)\rho|{\phi}\rangle=0\ ,

(S89)

whenever
|{\phi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}^{\mathsf{C}}
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}(\sigma))\subset\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}
.
∎

Noticing that DME for dynamic Grover iterations satisfies these conditions at every step of the recursion, we can be sure that the states generated by QDP in Grover search have full support in the relevant Hilbert space representation.

Corollary 15
.

The QDP implementation of Grover search using DME generates a sequence of states
\rho_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
whenever
\rho_{0}=\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.

Proof.

From Proposition
14
, DME protocols map states in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
to
\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.
Furthermore, reflections around the target state
\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
, which is implemented by an exact unitary, not DME, also ensures that

\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\tau]}_{s}\left(\sigma\right)\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}

whenever
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.
Since a recursion step of Grover search comprises the above two operations,
the state after the iteration,
\rho_{n}
, also stays in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
.
∎

Corollary
15
enables a simple representation of intermediate states
\rho_{n}
.
Defining the rank-
2
projector onto
\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}
,
\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}=|{\tau}\rangle\!\langle{\tau}|+|{\tau^{\perp}}\rangle\!\langle{\tau^{\perp}}|
, we note that this operator is invariant under an exact Grover recursion step with respect to any state
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\phi]}_{L}\left(\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}\right)=\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel},

(S90)

due to unitarity of
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\phi]}_{L}
.
Since
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel})=2
, we can decompose any
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm rel}
in its eigenbasis

\displaystyle\rho=(1-x)|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|+x|{\psi^{\perp}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\perp}}|=(1-2x)|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|+x\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel},

(S91)

for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
by using the fact that
\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|+|{\psi^{\perp}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\perp}}|
.
We will refer to
\psi
as the pure state associated with
\rho
and
x
as the mixedness parameter.
Interestingly, the partial reflection around
\rho
with angle
s
can be rewritten using the observation that
[\psi,\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}]=0
:

\displaystyle\hat{E}^{[\rho]}_{s}=e^{-is\left[(1-2x)\psi+x\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}\right]}=e^{-isx\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}}\hat{E}^{[\psi]}_{s(1-2x)}\ ,

(S92)

which is identical to the partial reflection around the corresponding pure state
\psi
with the angle reduced by the factor
(1-2x)
, apart from the phase factor globally acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel}
.
If the mixedness parameter
x
is known, one can effectively implement the reflection around the pure state
\psi
using mixed states
\rho
by adjusting the DME duration
s\mapsto s/(1-2x)
.

Nevertheless, it is usually the case that only an upper bound, but not the exact amount of mixedness
x
is known.
The Proposition and the Lemma below assess the QDP implementation of a Grover search recursion step when the mixedness parameter
x
is unknown.
Instead of the exact partial reflection unitary channels
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{s}=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s/(1-2x)}
around its associated pure state
\psi
, we employ DME queries
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho]}_{s}
as defined in Eq. (
S84
).

Proposition 16
.

Let
\rho=(1-2x)\psi+x\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}
be a state with an associated pure state
\psi
and a mixedness parameter
x\in[0,\varepsilon]
for some
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
.
A dynamic unitary channel

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho]}_{L}=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha_{L}}\circ\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{L}}\circ\cdots\circ\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha_{1}}\circ\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{1}}

(S93)

that uses the same angles
\{\alpha_{l},\beta_{l}\}_{l}
used in
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi]}_{L}
has the distance

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi]}_{L}-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho]}_{L}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\leq L\pi\varepsilon\ ,

(S94)

from the dynamic unitary channel constructed with the associated pure state
\psi
.

Proof.

First, observe that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi]}_{L}-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho]}_{L}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha_{i}}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha_{i}}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\ ,

(S95)

from the triangle inequality and the unitary invariance of the trace norm.
The distance between
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel})}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}\left(\sigma\right)-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm rel})}\Tr\left\{\sqrt{\left(\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}\left(\sigma\right)-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}(\sigma)\right)^{2}}\right\}\ .

(S96)

Using the matrix notation

\displaystyle\psi=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\rho=\begin{bmatrix}1-x&0\\
0&x\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}p&q\\
q^{*}&1-p\end{bmatrix}\ ,

(S97)

we arrive at the explicit expression

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}(\sigma)-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}(\sigma)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}4\lvert q\rvert^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\alpha x\right)&0\\
0&4\lvert q\rvert^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\alpha x\right)\end{bmatrix}\ .

(S98)

The state
\sigma
giving the maximum distance is the one having the maximal
\lvert q\rvert
.
The condition that
\sigma\geq 0
imposes
\lvert q\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\psi]}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho]}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\rm Tr}=\lvert\sin\left(\alpha x\right)\rvert
. From
\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]
and
\lvert\alpha\varepsilon\rvert<\frac{\pi}{2}
, we bound
\lvert\sin(\alpha x)\rvert<\pi\varepsilon
and achieve Eq. (
S94
).
∎

Now we establish the main results of this subsection.
We first define

\displaystyle\delta_{n}\coloneq\left(T_{(2L+1)}\left(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\right)\right)^{-1}+\epsilon\ ,

(S99)

and use it as the parameter
q=\delta_{n}
when choosing angles Eq. (
S74
).
At the same time, we also demonstrate that
\delta_{n}
bounds the distance to the target state, after an iteration of DME implementation with error
\epsilon
, i.e. it is the counterpart to Eq. (
S76
) used for the exact iteration.
Precise statements of this result are delineated in Lemma
17
and Corollary
18
.
Further analysis on Eq. (
S99
) itself will be made in Sec.
IV.3
.

Lemma 17

(DME implementation subroutine)
.

We consider iteration
n
of the nested Grover search algorithm.
Assume that the initial state for the iteration
\rho_{n-1}=(1-2x_{n-1})\psi_{n-1}+x_{n-1}\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}
is unknown to us but satisfies the following:

•

An associated pure state
\psi_{n-1}
has the distance to the target state bounded by some number
\delta_{n-1}
, i.e.
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}\leq\delta_{n-1}
.

•

The mixedness parameter is bounded by
x_{n-1}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2L\pi}
for some number
\epsilon
.

Then, it is possible to prepare a state
\rho^{\prime}_{n}=(1-2x_{n}^{\prime})\psi_{n}+x^{\prime}\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n}-\frac{\epsilon}{4},

(S100)

where
\delta_{n}
is defined by the recurrence relation Eq. (
S99
).
The preparation requires consuming
\mathcal{O}(L^{2}\epsilon^{-1})
copies of
\rho_{n-1}
.

Proof.

The preparation is performed by implementing nested fixed-point Grover search with DME memory-usage queries.
Firstly, we choose the angles
\{\alpha_{l}(q),\beta_{l}(q)\}_{l}
for the dynamic unitary
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}
.
Following Lemma
13
, we set
q=\sech((2L+1)\arcsech(\delta_{n-1}))
, or equivalently,
q=\delta_{n}-\epsilon
.
Secondly, we denote the total number of DME queries
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{s}
used for implementing
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}
as
M
.
Since
L
memory-calls (partial reflections around
\rho_{n-1}
) are contained in
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}
, each memory-call employs
\frac{M}{L}
DME memory-usage queries.
The implemented channel reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}\coloneq\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{s_{L}}\right)^{\frac{M}{L}}\circ\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{L}}\circ\cdots\circ\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{s_{1}}\right)^{\frac{M}{L}}\circ\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\tau]}_{\beta_{1}}\ ,

(S101)

with
s_{l}=\frac{\alpha_{l}L}{M}
, the angle
\alpha_{l}
distributed equally to
\frac{M}{L}
DME queries.
The output state of the channel is

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{n}:=\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}\left(\rho_{n-1}\right)=(1-2x_{n}^{\prime})\psi_{n}+x_{n}^{\prime}\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}\ .

(S102)

We additionally define the state after an exact unitary channel
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{n}:=\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}\left(\rho_{n-1}\right)=(1-2x_{n-1})\phi_{n}+x_{n-1}\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}\ .

(S103)

Since
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}
is a unitary channel, it leaves the projector
\mathmybb{1}_{\rm rel}
intact in Eq. (
S103
), and the associated pure state

\displaystyle\phi_{n}=\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\ ,

(S104)

while the mixedness parameter for
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
is identical to that of
\rho_{n-1}
.
We prove the Lemma starting from the triangle inequality
\lVert\tau-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert\tau-\phi_{n}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\phi_{n}-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}
and upper bounding each term in the RHS.

•

Upper bound for
\lVert\tau-\phi_{n}\rVert_{1}
:
Another triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\lVert\tau-\phi_{n}\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}\ .

(S105)

The second term in the RHS is bounded by the trace norm distance of channels
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}
and
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}
.
Applying Proposition
16
and using the assumption
x_{n-1}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2L\pi}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\leq\epsilon\ .

(S106)

The other term
\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\rVert_{1}
resembles the final distance after an exact nested fixed-point Grover search in Lemma
13
.
However, we cannot directly apply Lemma
13
, since we do not know the initial distance
\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}
exactly; we only assume that it is bounded by
\delta_{n-1}
.
Hence, we must start from the more general relation Eq. (
S75
), which gives the equality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}=(\delta_{n}-\epsilon)T_{2L+1}\left(T_{1/(2L+1)}\left((\delta_{n}-\epsilon)^{-1}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\ ,

(S107)

substituting
q=\delta_{n}-\epsilon
.
Recalling that
\delta_{n}-\epsilon=(T_{(2L+1)}(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}))^{-1}
and Eq. (
S71
), we get
T_{1/(2L+1)}((\delta_{n}-\epsilon)^{-1})=\delta_{n-1}^{-1}
.
Eq. (
S107
) then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}=(\delta_{n}-\epsilon)T_{2L+1}\left(\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\ .

(S108)

Chebyshev polynomials follow
T_{2L+1}(x)\leq 1
whenever
x\in[0,1]
, and the argument of
T_{2L+1}
in the RHS,
\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\psi_{n-1}\rVert_{1}
is indeed smaller than or equal to
1
from the assumption of the Lemma.
Then
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\psi_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\rVert_{1}\leq\delta_{n}-\epsilon
follows, and we finally obtain the bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau-\phi_{n}\rVert_{1}\leq\delta_{n}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\ .

(S109)

•

Upper bound for
\lVert\phi_{n}-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}
:
Use the triangle inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\phi_{n}-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\phi_{n}-\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\rho^{\prime}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\rho_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}\ .

(S110)

The first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})-\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}(\psi_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}-\hat{\mathmybb{\Gamma}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{L}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{M}{L}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{s_{l}}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\rho_{n-1}]}_{s_{l}}\right\rVert_{\rm Tr}\ ,

(S111)

where the second inequality follows from comparing Eqs. (
S93
) and (
S101
).
Making use of the explicit expressions in Eq. (
S97

V
Double bracket iterations: quantum imaginary-time evolution and oblivious Schmidt decomposition

Two example algorithms—quantum imaginary-time evolution and oblivious schmidt decomposition—can be considered as an ordered diagonalization task: from a generic pure state to the ground state in the Hamiltonian basis in the former case and from a generic reduced state to the diagonal state in computational basis in the latter case.
In both cases, we use a recently-proposed quantum algorithm
Gluza 2024
, which we call double-bracket iterations, that adapts the continuous gradient flow for matrices for discrete gate-based quantum computing models.

V.1
Double-bracket iterations

In this section, we expound on the double-bracket iteration algorithm, which can be used for scenarios beyond quantum imaginary-time evolution or oblivious Schmidt decomposition.
A double-bracket iteration step with an instruction operator
\hat{P}_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{n+1}=e^{s_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}]}\hat{P}_{n}e^{-s_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}]}\ .

(S140)

In other words, this is a single memory-call recursion unitary, where the memory-call is defined by the linear Hermitian-preserving map
\mathcal{N}(\hat{P})=-is_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}]
.
Flow duration
s_{n}
and diagonal operator
\hat{D}
are not part of the quantum instructions and can be chosen obliviously to
\hat{P}_{n}
, or strategically with some a priori information on
\hat{P}_{n}
.

This recursion can be understood better when considering a function

\displaystyle f\left(\hat{P}\right)\coloneq\lVert\hat{P}-\hat{D}\rVert_{2}^{2}\ .

(S141)

The generator
\frac{d}{ds_{n}}\hat{P}_{n+1}=[[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}],\hat{P}_{n}]
is shown to be
-\mathrm{grad}f(\hat{P}_{n})
for some Riemannian metric defined on the manifold of matrices isospectral to
\hat{P}_{0}

Helmke and Moore 1994
.
Hence, the iteration effectively applies the gradient flow of
f
and we expect
f
to reduce over the iterations towards the minimum. This is in fact true for suitable choices of
s_{n}
and
\hat{D}
.

Lemma 23

(Theorem 4.4 in Ref.
Moore et al. 1994
)
.

When
\hat{D}
is a non-degenerate diagonal matrix such that
\hat{D}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|
with
\mu_{i}>\mu_{j}
for all
i>j
and
s_{n}=\frac{1}{4\lVert\hat{P}_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert\hat{D}\rVert_{2}}
, the recursion Eq. (
S140
) has a unique stable fixed-point
\hat{P}_{\infty}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|
, where
\lambda_{i}
are eigenvalues of
\hat{P}_{0}
arranged in a non-increasing order.
Furthermore, this fixed-point is locally exponentially stable.

In particular, the off-diagonal element of
\hat{P}_{k}
is suppressed after each step

\displaystyle(\hat{P}_{k+1})_{ij}=\left[1-\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})}{4\lVert\hat{P}_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert\hat{D}\rVert_{2}}\right](\hat{P}_{k})_{ij}\ ,

(S142)

up to the first order of
(\hat{P}_{k})_{ij}
, where the suppression factor inside the square bracket is in the range
(0,1)
.
Hence, it is possible to establish the exponential suppression of the distance.

Corollary 24
.

Let
\hat{P}_{\infty}
be a fixed-point of the double-bracket iterations starting from a matrix
\hat{P}_{0}
and let
M(\hat{P}_{0})
be a manifold of matrices isospectral to
\hat{P}_{0}
.
Then there exists
r<1
and a neighbourhood
N
of
\hat{P}_{\infty}
in the manifold
M(\hat{P}_{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{P}_{k+1}-\hat{P}_{\infty}\rVert_{2}<r\lVert\hat{P}_{k}-\hat{P}_{\infty}\rVert_{2}\ ,

(S143)

for any
\hat{P}_{k}\in N
and
\hat{P}_{k+1}
obtained from an iteration from
\hat{P}_{k}
.

V.2

We now describe the unfolding implementation of double-bracket iterations, given access to the black box queries to the evolution
e^{\pm it\hat{P}_{0}}
by the root operator
\hat{P}_{0}
, following Ref.

.
To implement the memory-call
e^{i\mathcal{N}(\hat{P})}=e^{s_{n}[\hat{D}_{n},\hat{P}]}
with queries
e^{\pm it\hat{P}_{0}}
, the group commutator approximation is used.

Lemma 25

(Group commutator,
Gluza 2024
)
.

Let
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
be Hermitian operators mapping
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}
.
For any unitarily invariant norm
\|\cdot\|
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{-i\sqrt{s}\hat{B}}e^{-i\sqrt{s}\hat{A}}e^{i\sqrt{s}\hat{B}}e^{i\sqrt{s}\hat{A}}-e^{s[\hat{A},\hat{B}]}\right\rVert\leq s^{3/2}\left(\left\lVert[\hat{A},[\hat{A},\hat{B}]\right\rVert+\left\lVert[\hat{B},[\hat{B},\hat{A}]\right\rVert\right)\ .

(S144)

Setting
\hat{A}=\hat{P}
and
\hat{B}=-\hat{D}
we obtain an locally accurate approximation to the recursion step

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{n}=\left(e^{i\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{M}}\hat{D}_{n}}e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{M}}\hat{P}_{n}}e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{M}}\hat{D}_{n}}e^{i\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{M}}\hat{P}_{n}}\right)^{M}

(S145)

that approximates
e^{s_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}]}
with error
\mathcal{O}(s_{n}^{3/2}M^{-1/2})
by making
L=2M
calls to
P_{n}
.
Instead of Eq. (
S145
), one can employ higher order product formulae
Chen et al. 2022
that can be more complicated to execute but have higher efficiency.
Typically, we apply this recursion to a state

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\frac{\hat{P}_{n}+\lambda\mathmybb{1}}{\Tr\left[\hat{P}_{n}+\lambda\mathmybb{1}\right]}\ ,

(S146)

encoding the matrix
\hat{P}_{n}
.
The convergence of the recursion
\rho_{k+1}=\hat{W}_{k}\rho_{k}\hat{W}_{k}^{\dagger}
to the diagonal state
\rho_{\infty}
has been established, despite the error arising from the group commutator approximation
Gluza 2024
.

The memory-call to
\hat{P}_{n}
needed for Eq. (
S145
) is not directly accessible to us.
However, it is covariant, i.e.
e^{-it\hat{P}_{n}}=\hat{W}_{n-1}e^{-it\hat{P}_{n-1}}\hat{W}_{n-1}^{\dagger}
.
Unfolding then follows from replacing all memory-calls in Eq. (
S145
) by the call to the root state
e^{\pm it\hat{P}_{0}}
, dressed with previous recursion steps.
The circuit depth scaling can also be calculated for the unfolding implementation.
Since
L=2M
, the depth required for performing
N
steps of the iteration would scale as
\mathcal{O}((4M+1)^{N})
.

V.3
QDP implementation for double-bracket iterations

Now we analyze the QDP implementation using states
\rho_{n}
in Eq. (
S146
) as instructions for the recursion
e^{s_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}]}
.
First, we establish the locally accurate implementation of a single recursion step, whose memory-call is of a linear Hermitian-preserving map
\mathcal{N}(\rho)=-is[\hat{D},\rho]
.
Observe that

\displaystyle e^{s_{n}[\hat{D},\hat{P}_{n}]}=e^{i\Tr[\hat{P}_{0}+\lambda\mathmybb{1}]\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho_{n})}\ .

(S147)

Henceforth, we rescale the flow duration
s_{n}\mapsto\Tr[\hat{P}_{0}+\lambda\mathmybb{1}]s_{n}
and define our recursion as
e^{i\mathcal{N}(\rho_{n})}
.

The memory-call is then implemented by memory-usage queries to Hermitian-preserving matrix exponentiation channel
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\rho_{n}]}_{r}
, given as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\rho_{n}]}_{r}(\sigma)=\Tr_{1}\left[e^{-ir\hat{N}}(\rho\otimes\sigma)e^{ir\hat{N}}\right]\ .

(S148)

The fixed operation
\mathcal{Q}=e^{-ir\hat{N}}
oblivious to
\rho_{n}
is generated by
\hat{N}=is_{n}\sum_{jk}(\mu_{k}-\mu_{j})|{kj}\rangle\!\langle{jk}|_{12}
, where
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j}
are the diagonal entries of
\hat{D}
.
Then,
M
queries to this channel with
r=1/M
gives the trace distance to the exact recursion channel

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\rho_{n}]}_{1/M}\right)^{M}-\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\rho_{n}]}\right\rVert_{\Tr}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}{M}\right)\ ,

(S149)

from Eq. (
S25
).
The flow duration is chosen to be
s_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert\hat{D}\rVert^{-1})
as in Lemma
23
, which makes the error scaling independent of the rescaling of
\hat{D}
.
Therefore, one can make the locally accurate implementation, by increasing the number of copies
M
.

Now we consider the error accumulation over multiple iterations.
Theorem
9
is applicable in this case from the exponential convergence in Corollary
24
, given that the initial state
\rho_{0}
already has a small off-diagonal part, since
h(\delta)=r\delta
for
r<1
satisfies the requirement of the theorem.
Consequently, QDP double-bracket iterations can be implemented with a circuit whose depth is at most quadratic to the number of iterations
N
.
However, we could not find a method to curb the accumulation of non-unitary error, as opposed to the Grover search example, and thus cannot achieve linear depth and exponential width.

V.4
Quantum imaginary-time evolution

Up to this point, we have focused on the diagonalization of the state
\rho_{n}
(or
\hat{P}_{n}
) as a result of double-bracket iterations.
However, Lemma
23
tells us an additional property: the fixed-point is not only diagonal in the basis of
\hat{D}
, but its eigenvalues are also rearranged in an non-increasing order.
In other words, the eigenvector of
\rho_{0}
with the highest eigenvalue will align with the eigenvector of
\hat{D}
that has the lowest eigenvalue.

Now suppose that
\rho_{0}=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
is a pure state.
Then the highest eigenvalue of it is
1
with the corresponding eigenvector
|{\psi}\rangle
.
Then, as the result of the double-bracket iterations,
\rho_{\infty}
will become an eigenvector of
\hat{D}
with the largest eigenvalue.
If we set
\hat{D}=-\hat{H}
, this means that the double-bracket iteration prepares the ground state of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.

In Ref.
Gluza 2024
, it has been found that this process is equivalent to the discretized version of quantum imaginary-time evolution.
The goal is to apply an operator similar to the usual Hamiltonian evolution
e^{-it\hat{H}}
but with an imaginary-time
t=-i\beta
.
Clearly, this is not a unitary operator, and will not result in a quantum state after the application.
Hence, we instead need to consider a unitary flow of a pure state
|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle\propto e^{-\beta H}|{\psi(0)}\rangle
starting
\beta=0
and evolving towards
\beta\to\infty
.
The corresponding differential equation is then written as

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle=-(\hat{H}-E_{\beta})|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle\propto-\hat{H}|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle\ ,

(S150)

where
E_{\beta}=\langle{\psi(\beta)}|\hat{H}|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle
.
Surprisingly, this equation is equivalent to another equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle=\left[|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle\!\langle{\psi(\beta)}|,\hat{H}\right]|{\psi(\beta)}\rangle\ ,

(S151)

which can be discretized into finite steps with size
s_{n}
as

\displaystyle|{\psi_{n+1}}\rangle=e^{s_{n}[|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|,\hat{H}]}|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\ .

(S152)

This comes from the fact that the cost function
f
in Eq. (
S141
) can be written as
f(|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|)=1+\Tr[\hat{H}^{2}]+2\langle{\psi}|\hat{H}|{\psi}\rangle
, and the double-bracket iteration, which is a gradient flow in terms of this function, is effectively giving the gradient flow in terms of the average energy of the pure state
|{\psi}\rangle
.
Note that Eq. (
S152
) is exactly in the form of Eq. (
S140
) with
\hat{D}=-\hat{H}
and
\hat{P}_{n}=|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|
.

Furthermore, Theorem 2 of Ref.
Gluza 2024
guarantees the fast spectral convergence of this algorithm.
Therefore, we can apply Thm.
12
, which ensures the efficient QDP implementation.

One potential difficulty of implementing the memory-call
e^{s_{n}[|{\psi_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{n}}|,\hat{H}]}
directly from memory-usage queries is the requirement for a prior information about the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
Hence, in the main text, we considered a sampling based Hamiltonian simulation scenario studied in Refs.
Lloyd et al. 2014
;
Kimmel et al. 2017
.
In such settings, many copies of the state
\chi\propto\hat{H}-\lambda_{0}\mathmybb{1}
are given.
Then, instead of considering the single variable function
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)=-i[\rho,\hat{H}]
as the one defining the memory-call, we can work with a two-variable function
f(\rho,\chi)=-i[\rho,\chi]
.
Such memory-usage queries can be made using the ideas from Lemma
4
.

V.5
Oblivious Schmidt decomposition

Any bi-partite pure state
\psi
has a Schmidt decomposition, which reads

\displaystyle|{\psi}\rangle_{AB}=\sum_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}|{\phi_{j}}\rangle_{A}|{\chi_{j}}\rangle_{B}\ ,

(S153)

where
\{|{\phi_{j}}\rangle_{A}\}_{j}
and
\{|{\chi_{j}}\rangle_{B}\}_{j}
form orthonormal bases for respective Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
We will refer to such bases as Schmidt bases and (squares of) coefficients
\lambda_{j}
as Schmidt coefficients.
Schmidt coefficients, in particular, provide the full information on how entangled the state is
Vidal 2000
.
Schmidt bases can be connected to computational bases
\{|{j}\rangle_{A}\}_{j}
and
\{|{j}\rangle_{B}\}_{j}
through local unitary transfornations
|{\phi_{j}}\rangle_{A}=\hat{V}_{A}^{\dagger}|{j}\rangle_{A}
and
|{\chi_{j}}\rangle_{A}=\hat{V}_{B}^{\dagger}|{j}\rangle_{B}
, for all
j
.

The oblivious Schmidt decomposition algorithm aims to access the Schmidt coefficients and Schmidt basis of a given pure state, without learning the classical description of the state first via, e.g. state tomography.
In this section, we demonstrate that this task is achievable by diagonalizing the reduced state of a given pure state
\psi
via double-bracket iteration.

We first describe how double-bracket iterations on the reduced system can be implemented with memory-usage queries using the entire bi-partite state.
Suppose that multiple copies of the instruction state
|{\psi}\rangle_{AB}
are given.
The double-bracket iteration for the Oblivious Schmidt decomposition is given as

\displaystyle e^{i\mathcal{N}(\psi_{n})}=e^{s_{n}[\hat{D},\rho_{n}^{(A)}]}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{B}\ ,

(S154)

where
\rho_{n}^{(A)}=\Tr_{B}[\psi_{n}]
.
Consider the memory-usage query

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\psi_{A_{1}B_{1}}]}_{r}(\sigma_{A_{2}B_{2}})=\Tr_{A_{1}B_{1}}\left[e^{-ir\hat{N}}(\psi_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\sigma_{A_{2}B_{2}})e^{ir\hat{N}}\right]\ ,

(S155)

where
\hat{N}=is_{n}\sum_{jk}(\mu_{k}-\mu_{j})|{kj}\rangle\!\langle{jk}|_{A_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\mathmybb{1}_{B_{1}B_{2}}
and
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j}
are the diagonal entries of
\hat{D}
.
Each query
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\psi_{A_{1}B_{1}}]}_{r}(\sigma_{A_{2}B_{2}})
consumes a copy of
\psi_{A_{1}B_{1}}
.
Furthermore, following the same calculation from the previous subsection, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{[\mathcal{N},\psi_{A_{1}B_{1}}]}_{1/M}\right)^{M}-e^{i\mathcal{N}(\psi_{n})}\right\rVert_{\Tr}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}{M}\right)\ ,

(S156)

the locally accurate implementation of the recursion unitary.

If recursions are implemented with sufficiently good precision, Theorem 1 in the main text holds and we approach the fixed-point of the recursion, which is a diagonal matrix
\rho_{\infty}^{(A)}
isospectral to the initial reduced state
\rho_{0}^{(A)}
.
In other words,
\rho_{N}^{(A)}
with some large number
N
becomes close to the desired diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\rho_{N}\simeq\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|{j}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{A}\ ,

(S157)

from which Schmidt coefficients
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}
can be extracted.
