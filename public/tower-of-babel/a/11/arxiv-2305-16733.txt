An asymptotic expansion of the empirical angular measure for bivariate extremal dependence
Stéphane Lhaut, Johan Segers

Abstract
The angular measure on the unit sphere characterizes the first-order dependence structure of the components of a random vector in extreme regions and is defined in terms of standardized margins. Its statistical recovery is an important step in learning problems involving observations far away from the center. In the common situation that the components of the vector have different distributions, the rank transformation offers a convenient and robust way of standardizing data in order to build an empirical version of the angular measure based on the most extreme observations. We provide a functional asymptotic expansion for the empirical angular measure in the bivariate case based on the theory of weak convergence in the space of bounded functions. From the expansion, not only can the known asymptotic distribution of the empirical angular measure be recovered, it also enables to find expansions and weak limits for other statistics based on the associated empirical process or its quantile version.

Introduction and background

A standard assumption on the joint upper tail of the distribution of a random vector
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is that its distribution belongs to the
maximal domain of attraction
of a multivariate extreme value distribution. This hypothesis comprises two parts:

1.

the marginal distributions of
X
belong to the maximal domains of attraction of some univariate extreme value distributions;

2.

after marginal transformation of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
through the probability integral transform or a variation thereof, the joint distribution of the transformed vector belongs to the maximal domain of attraction of a multivariate extreme value distribution with pre-specified margins.

Under the side assumption that the marginal distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of
X
are continuous, which we make in this paper, point 2 above only involves the copula of
X
in view of Sklar’s theorem. Point 2 can be imposed on its own, independently of the assumptions on the margins in point 1, and this is what we do in this paper.

Regular variation.

Our working hypothesis is that the distribution
of the standardized vector

(1)

where

[
6

Assumption 1

(Smoothness)
.

The measure
\Lambda
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a density
\lambda
which is continuous on
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.
Furthermore,

\Lambda(\{\infty\}\times[0,1])=\Lambda([0,1]\times\{\infty\})=0

Assumption

\Phi_{p}(\{0\})=\Lambda(\{\infty\}\times[0,1])=0

\lambda(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\|(x,y)\|_{p}^{-1}\varphi_{p}(\arctan(y/x)).

(8)

The proof of this identity, which is of independent interest, is given in Appendix A in the supplement.

In addition, we see that

\lambda(ax,ay)=a^{-1}\lambda(x,y),\qquad a>0,\ (x,y)\in[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}.

(9)

Assumption 2

(Vanishing bias)
.

If

denotes the density of

, the quantity

\mathcal{D}_{T}(t):=\iint_{\mathcal{L}_{T}}|tc(tu_{1},tu_{2})-\lambda(u_{1},u_{2})|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}u_{2}\quad 1\leq T<\infty,\,t>0,

2
Main results and applications

In many situations, an asymptotic analysis of a statistic based on a given stochastic process requires more than the limiting distribution of this process. It is often more convenient to describe its limiting behavior in terms of an asymptotic expansion.

A typical example comes from the fundamental goodness-of-fit problem. If one seeks to test a parametric model for the angular measure, i.e., testing if

H_{0}:\Phi_{p}\in\mathcal{M}=\{\Phi_{p,r}:r\in\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\}

is a plausible hypothesis, then a standard way to proceed is to use

T_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}:=\Delta(\widehat{\Phi}_{p},\Phi_{p,{\hat{r}_{n}}})

as a test statistic, where
\Delta
is a kind of dissimilarity measure and
\hat{r}_{n}
is an estimator of the parameter
r\in\mathcal{R}
, and to reject
H_{0}
provided
T_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
is “too large”. A way to make that statement precise is to study the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
under the null hypothesis and compare the observed statistic to a quantile of the limiting distribution. In many situations (think of Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Anderson–Darling type of statistics), the normalized test statistic
\sqrt{k}T_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
will be expressed as a continuous functional of the empirical process
\sqrt{k}(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}-\Phi_{p,{\hat{r}_{n}}})
. Therefore, an analysis to find the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
under
H_{0}
can be based on determining the asymptotic distribution of the latter process in a sufficiently rich metric space and then applying a continuous mapping theorem. To this end, note that, if
r_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
denotes the true value of the parameter under
H_{0}
, the process is naturally decomposed as

\sqrt{k}\,(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}-\Phi_{p,{\hat{r}_{n}}})=\sqrt{k}\,(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}-\Phi_{p,r_{0}})-\sqrt{k}\,(\Phi_{p,{\hat{r}_{n}}}-\Phi_{p,r_{0}}).

Asymptotic considerations regarding the first part of the decomposition can be obtained on the basis of Theorem
1
. However, those need to be obtained
jointly
with the second term to determine the asymptotic distribution of the process of interest. This is only possible if an asymptotic expansion of the first term is available, which has to be combined with assumptions on the estimator
\hat{r}_{n}
for the second term.

Theorem
2
below provides such an asymptotic expansion. In contrast to the proof of Theorem
1
in
[
4

5
]
, the proof of our result does not require a Skorokhod construction and is shown on the original probability space directly. In particular, it relies on a weak convergence result, holding in arbitrary dimension, which may be of independent interest. In the following proposition, the requirement that

is suitably measurable is to be understood as in Theorems 19.14 and 19.28 in

]
and is explained in more detail in Definition 2.3.3 and Example 2.3.4 in

\displaystyle E_{n,p}(\theta):=\sqrt{k}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\theta}\right)-\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\theta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\left\{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta-w_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{cases}\int_{x_{p}(\theta)}^{\infty}\lambda(x,y_{p}(x))\left\{y_{p}^{\prime}(x)w_{1n}(x)-w_{2n}\left(y_{p}(x)\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }p<\infty,\\
\mbox{}-w_{1n}(1)\int_{1}^{1\vee\tan\theta}\lambda(1,y)\,\mathrm{d}y-w_{2n}(1)\int_{1\vee\cot\theta}^{\infty}\lambda(x,1)\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Note the similarity with Theorem
1
. In fact, Theorem
1
is a consequence of Theorem
2
.
In the space
\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])\times\ell^{\infty}([0,\infty))^{2}
, the joint weak convergence

\left(\sqrt{k}\{\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\cdot})-\tfrac{n}{k}P(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\cdot})\},w_{1n}(\cdot)w(\cdot),w_{2n}(\cdot)w(\cdot)\right)\\
\rightsquigarrow\left(W_{\Lambda}(C_{p,\cdot}),W_{1}(\cdot)w(\cdot),W_{2}(\cdot)w(\cdot)\right),

where
w
denotes the same weight function as in Proposition
2
, can be obtained from Propositions
1
and
2
as explained in the proof of Theorem
2
. Writing
w_{jn}=w_{jn}w\frac{1}{w}
and noting that the integrals
\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\,\frac{1}{w(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x
and so on are finite, the continuous mapping theorem then yields
E_{n,p}\rightsquigarrow\alpha_{p}
in
\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
, as stated in Theorem
1
.

The quantile process of the angular probability measure.

In the goodness-of-fit problem mentioned in the beginning of this section, many statistics do not involve the original process directly, but rather its quantile version. For example, one may think of a procedure based on Wasserstein distances
[
1
]
which admits an explicit representation in terms of quantiles for measures supported on a one-dimensional space. To find the limit distribution of such a test statistic, one needs an expansion of the quantile process. In our case, since the total mass of
\Phi_{p}
is unknown, it is convenient to consider the
angular probability measure
and its empirical counterpart, with cumulative distributions functions
Q_{p}(\theta):=\Phi_{p}(\theta)/\Phi_{p}(\pi/2)
and
\widehat{Q}_{p}(\theta):=\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)/\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\pi/2)
, respectively, for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
. The asymptotic expansion of
\widehat{Q}_{p}
follows from the one of
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
in Theorem
2
and Slutsky’s lemma. The proof of the Corollary
1
is standard and is omitted for brevity. In Corollary
2
, we analyze the associated quantile function estimate
\widehat{Q}_{p}^{-1}
; a detailed proof is to be found in Appendix D in the supplement.

Corollary 1

(Asymptotic expansion of empirical angular probability measure)
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
2
, we have, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{Q}_{p}(\theta))-Q_{p}(\theta)\right)-\frac{E_{n,p}(\theta)\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\Phi_{p}(\theta)E_{n,p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})}{\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})^{2}}\right|=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1).

Corollary 2

(Asymptotic expansion of empirical angular quantile process)
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
2
and the additional requirement that
\varphi_{p}>0
on
(0,\pi/2)
, we have for any
[v,w]\subset(0,1)
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{u\in[v,w]}\Biggl|\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{Q}_{p}^{-1}(u)-Q_{p}^{-1}(u)\right)-\frac{\Phi_{p}(Q_{p}^{-1}(u))\,E_{n,p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-E_{n,p}(Q_{p}^{-1}(u))\,\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})}{\varphi_{p}(Q_{p}^{-1}(u))\,\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})}\Biggr|\\
=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1).

3
Proof of Theorem
2

The key idea behind the proof is the following decomposition that can be found in
[
4
, Equation 12]
or
[
5
, Equation 3.4]
: for each
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)-\Phi_{p}(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\right)-\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{k}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\right)-\Lambda\left(\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{k}\left(\Lambda\left(\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\right)-\Lambda\left(C_{p,\theta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=:V_{n,p}(\theta)+r_{n,p}(\theta)+Z_{n,p}(\theta),

(17)

where the set
C_{p,\theta}
is estimated by

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{p,\theta}:=\Bigl\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq\infty,0\leq

\displaystyle y\leq\tfrac{n}{k}Q_{2n}\left(\Gamma_{1n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}x\right)\tan\theta\right),

\displaystyle y\leq\tfrac{n}{k}Q_{2n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}y_{p}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{1n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}x\right)\right)\right)\Bigr\}.

(18)

The three terms in the decomposition (
17
) will be called the
stochastic term
,
bias term
, and
random set term
, respectively.

Outline of the proof.
In Section
3.1
, we state the main underlying weak convergence result needed for the proof, based on Proposition
1
. Unlike
[
4
,
5
]
, we prove our asymptotic expansion without requiring a Skorokhod construction but work with the original sample space and random variables directly. Technically, it implies that we have to work with weak convergence instead of the almost sure representations arising from the Skorokhod construction, which makes some continuity arguments a bit more delicate, but the proof becomes more direct.
In Section
3.2
, we treat each term of (
17
) separately.
In Appendix E of the supplement, it is argued that we can reduce the analysis to the case
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
, by symmetry of the problem. Hence, in the following, we only focus on that region.

3.1
Weak convergence of a tail empirical process

As in
[
5
]
, the main idea of the proof is to approximate the random sets
\hat{C}_{p,\theta}
by those in a carefully constructed VC class of sets built from a covering of the positive real line by (small) intervals on which the random perturbations of the original sets
C_{p,\theta}
are manageable.
Fix any
\Delta\in\{1,1/2,1/3,\ldots\}
and
M>1
and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Delta,M)
be the following VC class of sets, already introduced in
[
5
]
. For
m\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,1/\Delta-1\}
, define the intervals

I_{\Delta}(m,\theta):=\begin{cases}[m\Delta x_{p}(\theta),(m+1)\Delta x_{p}(\theta)]\qquad&\text{if }\theta\in(0,\pi/4],\\
[0,\infty)\qquad&\text{if }\theta=0\text{ and }m=0,\\
\emptyset\qquad&\text{if }\theta=0\text{ and }m>0.\end{cases}

(19)

and

J_{\Delta}(m):=[y_{p}(1+(2^{1/p}-1)(m+1)\Delta),y_{p}(1+(2^{1/p}-1)m\Delta)].

(20)

Set
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
to be the class of sets containing the following sets:

•

\bigcup_{m=0}^{1/\Delta-1}\left\{(x,y):x\in I_{\Delta}(m,\theta),0\leq y\leq x\tan\theta+B_{m}(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}\right\}
for some
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
and
B_{0},\ldots,B_{1/\Delta-1}\in[-1,1]
.

•

\bigcup_{m=0}^{1/\Delta-1}\left\{(x,y):x\in J_{\Delta}(m),x\geq x_{p}(\theta),0\leq y\leq y_{p}(x)(1+K_{m})\right\}
for some
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
and
K_{0},\ldots,K_{1/\Delta-1}\in[-1/2,1/2]
.

•

\{(x,y):x\leq a\}
,
\{(x,y):y\leq a\}
and
\{(x,y):x\leq a\text{ or }y\leq a\}
for some
a\in[0,M]
.

Next, define
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{s}:=\{A_{s}:A\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\}
where for
A\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
,
A_{s}:=\{(x,y):(y,x)\in A\}
. Finally, let
\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\cup\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{s}
.

As required in Proposition
1
, every set in
\mathcal{A}
is contained in
L_{M}=\{x\in[0,\infty)^{d}:\min(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\leq M\}
, provided
M>1
is picked large enough—which is not restrictive since we will consider it large later. Furthermore, the other conditions underlying Proposition
1
are easily shown to hold in view of the assumptions. In particular, the uniform regular variation condition (
10
) is satisfied thanks to the convergence in total variation in Assumption
2
. Consequently, we can conclude that (
11
) and (
12
) hold in this setting.

3.2
Asymptotic expansions of the three terms in (
17
)

The stochastic term
V_{n,p}(\theta)
.
Recall
W_{n,k}
in Proposition
1
and note that
V_{n,p}(\theta)=W_{n,k}(\hat{C}_{p,\theta})
.
Our aim is to show that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|W_{n,k}(\hat{C}_{p,\theta})-W_{n,k}(C_{p,\theta})\right|=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1).

(21)

We will consider separately
V_{n,p,1}
and
V_{n,p,2}
, where
V_{n,p,i}(\theta):=W_{n,k}(\hat{C}_{p,\theta,i})
with
\hat{C}_{p,\theta,1}:=\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\cap\{(x,y):x<x_{p}(\theta)\}
and
\hat{C}_{p,\theta,2}:=\hat{C}_{p,\theta}\cap\{(x,y):x\geq x_{p}(\theta)\}
. In particular, it would follow from the triangle inequality that the expansion (
21
) is satisfied, provided that

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|V_{n,p,i}(\theta)-W_{n,k}(C_{p,\theta,i})\right|=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1),\qquad i\in\{1,2\},

(22)

where
C_{p,\theta,1}:=C_{p,\theta}\cap\{(x,y):x<x_{p}(\theta)\}
and
C_{p,\theta,2}:=C_{p,\theta}\cap\{(x,y):x\geq x_{p}(\theta)\}
.

Let us first consider
i=1
. Recall equation (
18
) and observe that,
since
\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{jn}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)=x+w_{jn}(x)/\sqrt{k}
and
\tfrac{n}{k}Q_{jn}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)=x+v_{jn}(x)/\sqrt{k}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{n}{k}Q_{2n}\left(\Gamma_{1n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}x\right)\tan\theta\right)

\displaystyle=x\tan\theta+\frac{z_{n,\theta}(x)}{\sqrt{k}},

\displaystyle\tfrac{n}{k}Q_{2n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}y_{p}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{1n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}x\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=y_{p}(x)+\frac{s_{n,p}(x)}{\sqrt{k}},

where

\displaystyle z_{n,\theta}(x)

\displaystyle:=w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta+v_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta+\frac{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta}{\sqrt{k}}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle s_{n,p}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{k}\left\{y_{p}\left(x+\frac{w_{1n}(x)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)-y_{p}(x)\right\}+v_{2n}\left(y_{p}\left(x+\frac{w_{1n}(x)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right).

(24)

Now set, for any
m\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,1/\Delta-1\}
, with the convention that
0/0=0
,

\displaystyle V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in I_{\Delta}(m,\theta)}\frac{z_{n,\theta}(x)\wedge\left(s_{n,p}(x)+\sqrt{k}(y_{p}(x)-x\tan\theta)\right)}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}},

\displaystyle V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{x\in I_{\Delta}(m,\theta)}\frac{z_{n,\theta}(x)\wedge\left(s_{n,p}(x)+\sqrt{k}(y_{p}(x)-x\tan\theta)\right)}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}},

where the intervals
I_{\Delta}(m,\theta)
were defined in (
19
).
For fixed
\theta
, note that these intervals cover
[0,x_{p}(\theta)]
, on which
y_{p}(x)\geq x\tan\theta
. It follows that on an event whose probability tends to one,
V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{\pm}
is equal to the same expression as in its definition but with the numerator simplified to
z_{n,\theta}(x)
. In the following, we work with these simplified definitions.
Moreover, the weak convergence

\left\{\frac{|z_{n,\theta}(x)|}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}}:\theta\in[0,\pi/4],x\in[0,x_{p}(\theta)]\right\}\\
\rightsquigarrow\left\{\frac{W_{1}(x)\tan\theta-W_{2}(x\tan\theta)}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}}:\theta\in[0,\pi/4],x\in[0,x_{p}(\theta)]\right\}

(25)

holds in the Banach space of bounded functions on the indicated domain.
To see (
25
), recall the definition of
z_{n,\theta}
in (
23
) and note that for any
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
,

\frac{z_{n,\theta}(x)}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}}=\frac{w_{1n}(x)}{x^{1/16}}(\tan\theta)^{15/16}\\
+\frac{v_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta+\frac{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}{\left(x\tan\theta+\frac{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta}{\sqrt{k}}\right)^{1/4}}\left((x\tan\theta)^{3/4}+\frac{(\tan\theta)^{3/4}}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{w_{1n}(x)}{x^{1/4}}\right)^{1/4}.

Using equations (
15
) and (
16
) in Proposition
2
together with the fact that
x\tan\theta\leq 2^{1/p}
on the said domain yields (
25
).
In particular, we have

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\sup_{x\in[0,x_{p}(\theta)]}\frac{|z_{n,\theta}(x)|}{(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}}=\Oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1),\qquad n\to\infty.

(26)

Defining

M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\pm}:=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\Delta-1}\left\{(x,y):x\in I_{\Delta}(m,\theta),0\leq y\leq x\tan\theta+\frac{V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{\pm}}{\sqrt{k}}(x\tan\theta)^{1/16}\right\},

it is easy to see that for any
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]

we have
M_{\Delta,\theta}^{-}\subseteq\hat{C}_{\theta,p,1}\subseteq M_{\Delta,\theta}^{+}
and thus

V_{n,\Delta,1}^{-}(\theta)-R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta)\leq V_{n,p,1}(\theta)\leq V_{n,\Delta,1}^{+}(\theta)+R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta),

(27)

where

\displaystyle V_{n,\Delta,1}^{\pm}(\theta)

\displaystyle:={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W_{n,k}(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\pm})},

(28)

\displaystyle R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{k}\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{+}\setminus M_{\Delta,\theta}^{-})\right).

(29)

Observe that, due to Assumption
2
and the behavior of the marginal tail empirical and quantile processes,

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta)-\sqrt{k}\Lambda(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{+}\setminus M_{\Delta,\theta}^{-})\right|=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1).

Similar computations as in
[
4
, Section 3.1.1]
show that

\sqrt{k}\Lambda(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{+}\setminus M_{\Delta,\theta}^{-})\leq 16\times 2^{1/p}\sup_{y\geq 0}\lambda(1,y)\max_{m=0,\ldots,\Delta-1}(V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{+}-V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{-}),

where
\sup_{y\geq 0}\lambda(1,y)<\infty
since
\lambda
is continuous on
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
thanks to Assumption
1
and
\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}\lambda(1,y)=0
(the latter limit following from the homogeneity of
\lambda
in (
9
)).
Since
V_{m,\Delta,0}^{+}=V_{m,\Delta,0}^{-}=0
by convention for any value of
m
and
\Delta
, we deduce from (
25
) that, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{\Delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\max_{m=0,\ldots,\Delta-1}(V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{+}-V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{-})>\epsilon\right]=0.

Collecting the latter three equations, we conclude that, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{\Delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta)>\epsilon\right]=0.

(30)

Furthermore, since (
26
) implies that on an event with probability tending to one we have
M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
, it follows that for all
\epsilon,\delta>0
, we have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\max_{\sigma\in\{-,+\}}\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|W_{n,k}(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\sigma})-W_{n,k}(C_{p,\theta,1})\right|>\epsilon\right]\\
\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\max_{\sigma\in\{-,+\}}\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\rho(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\sigma},C_{p,\theta,1})\geq\delta\right]\\
+\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A},\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta}\left|W_{n,k}(A)-W_{n,k}(A^{\prime})\right|>\epsilon\right].

(31)

Standard computations lead to

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\rho(M_{\Delta,\theta}^{\pm},C_{p,\theta,1})\\
\leq\frac{16\times 2^{1/p}}{\sqrt{k}}\sup_{y\geq 0}\lambda(1,y)\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\max_{m=0,\ldots,\Delta-1}|V_{m,\Delta,\theta}^{\pm}|=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1),

so that the first limsup on the right hand side of (
31
) is zero. The second limsup can be made arbitrary close to zero as
\delta\downarrow 0
by (
12
).

Combining everything leads to (
22
) for
i=1
. Indeed, for
\epsilon>0
, we have

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|V_{n,p,1}(\theta)-W_{n,k}(C_{p,\theta,1})\right|>\epsilon\right]\\
\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\{-,+\}}\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|V_{n,\Delta,1}^{\sigma}(\theta)-W_{n,k}(C_{p,\theta,1})\right|>\epsilon/2\right]\\
+2\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}^{*}\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}R_{n,\Delta,1}(\theta)>\epsilon/2\right].

The first term on the right-hand side vanishes as
n\to\infty
, while the limsup over
n\to\infty
of the second term can be made arbitrarily small as
\Delta\rightarrow 0
.

Similar computations for the term
i=2
in (
22
) are to be found in Appendix F.

The bias term
r_{n,p}(\theta)
.

Note that all sets
\hat{C}_{p,\theta}
for
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
are contained in the set
\hat{C}_{p,\pi/4}
and, with probability tending to one, the latter is contained in the set
\{(x,y):0\leq x\wedge y\leq M\}
for
M=M(p)=2^{1/p}
. It follows that, with probability tending to one,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|r_{n,p}(\theta)\right|\leq\sqrt{k}\iint_{0\leq x\wedge y\leq M}\left|\frac{k}{n}c\left(\frac{k}{n}x,\frac{k}{n}y\right)-\lambda(x,y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k}M\iint_{0\leq u\wedge v\leq 1}\left|\frac{k}{n}Mc\left(\frac{k}{n}Mu,\frac{k}{n}Mv\right)-\lambda(u,v)\right|\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k}M\mathcal{D}_{n/k}\left(\frac{k}{n}M\right)+\sqrt{k}M\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u\wedge v\leq 1\\
u\vee v>\frac{n}{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{k}{n}Mc\left(\frac{k}{n}Mu,\frac{k}{n}Mv\right)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{k}M\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u\wedge v\leq 1\\
u\vee v>\frac{n}{k}\end{subarray}}\lambda(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v.

By the first part of Assumption
2
, the first term in the right-hand side vanishes asymptotically as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The second integral actually equals zero for any value of
n\in\mathbb{N}
since the copula density
c
vanishes outside
[0,1]^{2}
. For the last integral, assuming
n
large enough so that
n/k>1
, the domain of integration is the union of two distinct parts given by

\{(u,v):0\leq u\leq 1,v>n/k\}
and
\{(u,v):u>n/k,0\leq v\leq 1\}
. For the first part, we have

M\sqrt{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u\leq 1\\
v>n/k\end{subarray}}\lambda(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v\leq M\sqrt{k}\left(\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{k}{n})\right),

while for the second part, we have

M\sqrt{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}u>n/k\\
0\leq v\leq 1\end{subarray}}\lambda(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v\leq M\sqrt{k}\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{k}{n}).

By the second part of Assumption
2
, those two quantities converge to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The random set term
Z_{n,p}(\theta)
.
Similarly as for the stochastic term, we work on

Z_{n,p,i}(\theta)=\sqrt{k}\left(\Lambda\left(\hat{C}_{p,\theta,i}\right)-\Lambda\left(C_{p,\theta,i}\right)\right)

separately for
i=1,2
.
In particular, our aim is to show that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|Z_{n,p,1}(\theta)-\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\left\{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta-w_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1)

(32)

and, for
p<\infty
(since this is the most involved case),

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/4]}\left|Z_{n,p,2}(\theta)-\int_{x_{p}(\theta)}^{\infty}\lambda(x,y_{p}(x))\left\{y_{p}^{\prime}(x)w_{1n}(x)-w_{2n}\left(y_{p}(x)\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1).

(33)

We start with (
32
). Since, as pointed out earlier,
y_{p}(x)\geq x\tan\theta
on
[0,x_{p}(\theta)]
, we have, on an event with probability tending to one,

Z_{n,p,1}(\theta)=\sqrt{k}\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\int_{x\tan\theta}^{x\tan\theta+\tfrac{z_{n,\theta}(x)}{\sqrt{k}}}\lambda(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
z_{n,\theta}
was defined in (
23
).
Changing variables
y(z)=x\tan\theta+z/\sqrt{k}
and making use of the mean value theorem leads to

Z_{n,p,1}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda\left(x,x\tan\theta+\frac{y(x,\theta)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)z_{n,\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
y(x,\theta)
is between
0
and
z_{n,\theta}(x)
. Observing that

\displaystyle\lambda\left(x,x\tan\theta+\frac{y(x,\theta)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)z_{n,\theta}(x)-\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\left\{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta-w_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\{\lambda\left(x,x\tan\theta+\frac{y(x,\theta)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)-\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\right\}z_{n,\theta}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left[z_{n,\theta}(x)-\left\{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta-w_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta\right)\right\}\right]\lambda(x,x\tan\theta),

\int_{2^{1/p}}^{\infty}\lambda(x,y_{p}(x))\left|s_{n,p}(x)-\left\{y_{p}^{\prime}(x)w_{1n}(x)-w_{2n}\left(y_{p}(x)\right)\right\}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x=\oh_{\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}}(1),

Appendix A
Link between densities of
\Lambda
and
\Phi_{p}

For any
x,y>0
, we have

\lambda(x,y)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\Lambda\left((0,x]\times(0,y]\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\nu\left([x^{-1},\infty)\times[y^{-1},\infty)\right),

where
\nu
factorizes in polar coordinates under
T
in (
4
), i.e.,
T\#\nu=\nu_{-1}\otimes\Phi_{p}
, so that

\displaystyle\nu\left([x^{-1},\infty)\times[y^{-1},\infty)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\nu_{-1}\otimes\Phi_{p}\right)\left(\left\{T(u,v):u\geq x^{-1},v\geq y^{-1}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\nu_{-1}\otimes\Phi_{p}\right)\left(\left\{(r,\theta):\frac{r\sin\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\geq x^{-1},\frac{r\cos\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\geq y^{-1}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\frac{x\sin\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\wedge\frac{y\cos\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\right)\varphi_{p}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=x\int_{0}^{\arctan(y/x)}\frac{\sin\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\varphi_{p}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\qquad+y\int_{\arctan(y/x)}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\varphi_{p}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Differentiating this expression with respect to
x
and
y
using the Leibniz differentiation rule yields the desired formula (
8
) for
\lambda
, showing that its continuity on
(0,\infty)^{2}
is equivalent to the one of
\varphi_{p}
on
(0,\pi/2)
.

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
1

The proof is an application of Theorem 19.28 in
[
8
]
which we recall here for convenience.

Theorem
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be a random sample from a probability distribution
P
on a measurable space
(S,\mathcal{S})
. Let
P_{n}
denote the associated empirical distribution. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{f_{n,t}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,t\in T\}
be a class of measurable functions indexed by a totally bounded semimetric space
(T,\rho)
satisfying

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\rho(s,t)<\delta_{n}}P\left((f_{n,s}-f_{n,t})^{2}\right)=0,\qquad\forall\delta_{n}\downarrow 0,

and with envelope function
F_{n}
satisfying the Lindeberg condition

P\left(F_{n}^{2}\right)=\Oh(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad P\left(F_{n}^{2}\,\mathds{1}\{F_{n}>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}\right)=\oh(1),\quad\epsilon>0.

If every class
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is suitably measurable in the sense of Example 2.3.4 in
[
9
]
and if

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{Q}\int_{0}^{\delta_{n}}\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon\|F_{n}\|_{Q,2},\mathcal{F}_{n},L_{2}(Q))}\,\mathrm{d}\epsilon=0,\qquad\forall\delta_{n}\downarrow 0,

where the supremum is taken over all finitely discrete probability measures
Q
on
(S,\mathcal{S})
for which
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is not identically zero and
N
is the covering number as defined in
[
8
, page 274]
, then the sequence of empirical processes
\{G_{n}(f_{n,t}):=\sqrt{n}(P_{n}(f_{n,t})-P(f_{n,t})):t\in T\}
converges in distribution to a tight Gaussian process, provided the sequence of covariance functions
P(f_{n,s}f_{n,t})-P(f_{n,s})P(f_{n,t})
converges pointwise on
T\times T
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the class of functions
f_{n,A}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{n,A}(x):=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}\mathds{1}\left\{x\in\tfrac{k}{n}A\right\}.

These classes satisfy the measurability assumptions given the assumptions on the class
\mathcal{A}
.

Note that for
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

G_{n}\left(f_{n,A}\right)=\sqrt{k}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}A)-\tfrac{n}{k}P(\tfrac{k}{n}A)\right).

Let us verify that the technical conditions underlying Theorem 19.28 in
[
8
]
are satisfied.

We first show that the metric space
(\mathcal{A},\rho)
is totally bounded. Indeed, noting that
\Lambda(L_{M})\leq d\times M
, since each element of
\mathcal{A}
is included in
L_{M}
, we may construct the standard empirical process with respect to the probability measure
P_{M}(\cdot):=\Lambda(\cdot\cap L_{M})/\Lambda(L_{M})
on
L_{M}
,
indexed by functions
f_{A}:=\mathds{1}_{A}
for
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and use the fact that
\mathcal{A}
is a VC-class with
|f_{A}|\leq F_{M}:=\mathds{1}_{L_{M}}
pointwise for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Apply Theorem 19.14 in
[
8
]
to conclude that the considered process converges weakly in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{A})
, where we identify
\mathcal{A}
with its class of indicator functions. In view of Theorem 18.14 and Lemma 18.15 in
[
8
]
, the metric space
(\mathcal{A},\rho_{M})
with
\rho_{M}:=\rho/\Lambda(L_{M})
must be totally bounded, which also guarantees that
(\mathcal{A},\rho)
is totally bounded.

We now show that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}P\left((f_{n,A}-f_{n,A^{\prime}})^{2}\right)=0

(B.1)

whenever
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\sup_{A,A^{\prime}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}P\left((f_{n,A}-f_{n,A^{\prime}})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,A^{\prime}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}\tfrac{n}{k}P\left((\mathds{1}_{(k/n)A}-\mathds{1}_{(k/n)A^{\prime}})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{A,A^{\prime}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(A\symdif A^{\prime})\right).

Fix
\epsilon>0
. It follows from our assumptions that there exists
N_{1}(\epsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N_{1}(\epsilon)
,

\sup_{A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}\left|\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(A\symdif A^{\prime})\right)-\Lambda(A\symdif A^{\prime})\right|\leq\epsilon/2,

while, since
\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we may find
N_{2}(\epsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N_{2}(\epsilon)
,

\sup_{A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}\Lambda(A\symdif A^{\prime})\leq\epsilon/2.

It follows that for
n\geq\max\{N_{1}(\epsilon),N_{2}(\epsilon)\}
, we have

\sup_{A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\rho(A,A^{\prime})<\delta_{n}}P\left((f_{n,A}-f_{n,A^{\prime}})^{2}\right)\leq\epsilon,

which yields (
B.1
).

We now verify that the classes
\mathcal{F}_{n}
possess envelope functions
F_{n}
satisfying the Lindeberg condition. It suffices to note that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

f_{n,A}(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}\mathds{1}\left\{x\in\tfrac{k}{n}A\right\}\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}\mathds{1}\left\{x\in\tfrac{k}{n}L_{M}\right\}=:F_{n}(x).

The first part of the Lindeberg condition clearly holds since

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(F_{n}^{2}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}L_{M}\right)=\Lambda\left(L_{M}\right)\leq d\cdot M.

The second part of the Lindeberg condition is immediate since for any
\epsilon>0
, the indicator
\mathds{1}\{F_{n}>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}
is zero as soon as
1/\sqrt{k}\leq\epsilon
, which is the case for
n
large enough, since, by assumption,
k=k(n)\rightarrow\infty
.

The last condition to verify is the one associated to the uniform entropy integral. Here, since the VC dimension of any
\mathcal{F}_{n}
equals the one of
\mathcal{A}
, which is finite by assumption, we may simply apply
[
9
, Example 2.11.24]
.

Consequently, Theorem 19.28 in
[
9
]
applies and the covariance of the limiting Gaussian process is obtained by computing the limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(f_{n,A}f_{n,A^{\prime}}\right)-P\left(f_{n,A}\right)P\left(f_{n,A^{\prime}}\right)=\Lambda(A\cap A^{\prime}),

for
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, which follows from our assumption.

Finally, we show (
12
). By Example 1.5.10 in
[
9
]
the processes
W_{n,k}
are asymptotically equicontinuous in probability with respect to the standard deviation semi-metric
\rho_{2}(A,A^{\prime}):=(\EE[|W_{\Lambda}(A)-W_{\Lambda}(A^{\prime})|^{2}])^{1/2}
. Since

\EE[|W_{\Lambda}(A)-W_{\Lambda}(A^{\prime})|^{2}]=\Lambda(A)+\Lambda(A^{\prime})-2\Lambda(A\cap A^{\prime})=\Lambda(A\symdif A^{\prime})=\rho(A,A^{\prime}),

the said equicontinuity property holds with respect to
\rho
as well.

Proof of Proposition

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in T:=[0,\infty)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the class of functions
f_{n,t}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{n,t}(x):=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}\mathds{1}\left(x\leq\tfrac{k}{n}t\right)w(t)\mathds{1}\left(t\leq\tfrac{n}{k}\right).

These classes trivially satisfy the measurability assumptions underlying Theorem 19.28 in
[
8
]
since it suffices to consider positive rational coordinates
t
.
For each
t\in T
,

G_{n}(f_{n,t})=\sqrt{n}\left(P_{n}(f_{n,t})-P(f_{n,t})\right)=w_{n}(t)w(t),

where
P_{n}
is the empirical distribution of the i.i.d. sample
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
whose common law is
P
, the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
.

Our aim is to apply Theorem 19.28 in
[
8
]
. We first need to find a metric
\rho
on
T
such that the metric space
(T,\rho)
is totally bounded and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\rho(s,t)<\delta_{n}}P\left((f_{n,s}-f_{n,t})^{2}\right)=0,

(C.1)

for any
(\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
. Since the weak limit of the process is expected to be centered Gaussian process, we may choose
\rho
to be the “standard deviation metric” as in
[
8
, Lemma 18.15]
. After some computations, we find that it is given by

\rho(s,t):=\sqrt{w(t)^{2}t+w(s)^{2}s-2w(t)w(s)(s\wedge t)},\qquad s,t\in T.

With this metric, it is easily checked that (
C.1
) holds and that the metric space
(T,\rho)
is totally bounded.

We now verify that the classes
\mathcal{F}_{n}
possess envelope functions
F_{n}
satisfying the Lindeberg condition. It suffices to note that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in T
,

f_{n,t}(x)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}w\left(\tfrac{n}{k}x\right)\mathds{1}(x\leq 1)=:F_{n}(x).

We have

\displaystyle P\left(F_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{k}\int_{0}^{1}w\left(\frac{n}{k}x\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{k}\left\{\int_{0}^{k/n}w\left(\frac{n}{k}x\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{k/n}^{1}w\left(\frac{n}{k}x\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-2\delta}+\frac{1-\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\right)^{2\eta-1}}{2\eta-1}\rightarrow\frac{1}{1-2\delta}+\frac{1}{2\eta-1}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so that the first part of the Lindeberg condition holds.
To verify the second part, we note that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(F_{n}^{2}\mathds{1}(F_{n}>\epsilon\sqrt{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n}{k}\int_{0}^{1}w\left(\tfrac{n}{k}x\right)^{2}\mathds{1}\left(w\left(\tfrac{n}{k}x\right)>\epsilon\sqrt{k}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}w(y)^{2}\mathds{1}\left(w(y)>\epsilon\sqrt{k}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Since
y\in(0,\infty)\mapsto w(y)^{2}
is integrable and the integrand, which is bounded pointwise by this function, converges pointwise to zero, the dominated convergence theorem permits to conclude.

The last condition to verify is the one associated to the uniform entropy integral. We propose to use Example 2.11.24 in
[
9
]
. To this end, we need to verify that the classes
\mathcal{F}_{n}
have finite VC dimensions which can be bounded independently of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The VC dimension of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is, by definition, the VC dimension of the class of sets

\mathcal{S}_{n}:=\left\{S_{n,t}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}:t\in T\right\},

where
S_{n,t}
is the sub-graph of
f_{n,t}
given by

S_{n,t}:=\left\{(x,h):f_{n,t}(x)>h\right\}.

We compute that

S_{n,t}=\left(\left[0,\tfrac{k}{n}t\right]\times\left[0,\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}}w(t)\right]\right)\cup\left([0,\infty)\times(-\infty,0)\right).

Hence, each element of the class
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is formed from the union of two rectangles where the second rectangle does not depend on
t
. Hence, the VC dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is bounded by
4
, which corresponds to the VC dimension of the class of all rectangles on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The uniform entropy condition is then verified.

Theorem 19.28 in
[
8
]
applies and the computation of the covariance function of the limiting Gaussian process is an easy exercise.

To prove the last assertion, we need, given any
\epsilon,\eta>0
, to find
N=N(\epsilon,\eta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq N
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\frac{\left|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\right|}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]<\epsilon.

To do so, observe that for any
0<a<1
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\frac{\left|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\right|}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[a,M]}\left|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\right|>\eta a^{\delta}\right]\\
+\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,a]}\frac{|w_{n}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\frac{\eta}{2}\right]+\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,a]}\frac{|v_{n}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\frac{\eta}{2}\right].

(C.2)

In view of (
14
), we may find
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N_{1}
, the first term on the right hand side of (
C.2
) satisfies

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[a,M]}\left|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\right|>\eta a^{\delta}\right]<\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

For the second term on the right hand side of (
C.2
), note that by the first part of the proposition, there exists
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N_{2}
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,a]}\frac{|w_{n}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\frac{\eta}{2}\right]\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,a]}\frac{|W_{j}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\frac{\eta}{2}\right]+\frac{\epsilon}{6}

and we may find
a_{1}\in(0,1)
small enough such that for
0<a<a_{1}
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,a]}\frac{|W_{j}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\frac{\eta}{2}\right]<\frac{\epsilon}{6}.

For the second term on the right hand side of (
C.2
), note that for
x\in[0,(2k)^{-1}]
, we have
v_{n}(x)=-x\sqrt{k}
. Hence,

\sup_{x\in[0,(2k)^{-1}]}\frac{|v_{n}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}=\sqrt{k}\sup_{x\in[0,(2k)^{-1}]}x^{1-\delta}\leq k^{1/2-1+\delta}\to 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Therefore, we may restrict the supremum to
x\in((2k)^{-1},a]
. On that region, we have

\forall x>(2k)^{-1},\forall u\geq 0,\qquad Q_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)>u\iff\Gamma_{n}(u)<\tfrac{k}{n}x.

(C.3)

By definition of the absolute value and by the union bound, for every
\eta>0
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{|v_{n}(x)|}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]\\
\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]+\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\inf_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}<-\eta\right].

(C.4)

We will treat each of the two terms on the right-hand side of (
C.4
) separately. Recall
w_{n}(x)=\sqrt{k}\left(\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)-x\right)
for
x\geq 0
.
First, by (
C.3
), for
x>(2k)^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}>\eta

\displaystyle\iff Q_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)>\tfrac{k}{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})

\displaystyle\iff\Gamma_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right)<\tfrac{k}{n}x

\displaystyle\iff\sqrt{k}\left\{\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right)-(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right\}<-\eta x^{\delta}

\displaystyle\iff-\frac{w_{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}>\eta.

Therefore,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]=\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{-w_{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right].

Let
\gamma=\delta/(\delta+1/2)
and note that
\delta<\gamma<1/2
since
0<\delta<1/2
. We have

\frac{w_{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}=\frac{w_{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})^{\gamma}}\cdot\frac{(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})^{\gamma}}{x^{\delta}}.

Since
\delta/\gamma=\delta+1/2
, the second factor on the right-hand side satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})^{\gamma}}{x^{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\left(x\cdot x^{-\delta/\gamma}+\eta x^{\delta}\cdot x^{-\delta/\gamma}/\sqrt{k}\right)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left(x^{1-\delta-1/2}+\eta x^{-1/2}/\sqrt{k}\right)^{\gamma}\leq 2,\qquad\forall x\in((2k)^{-1},1].

We find

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{w_{n}(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{(x+\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})^{\gamma}}>\eta/2\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{z\in[0,a+\eta a^{\delta}/\sqrt{k}]}\frac{w_{n}(z)}{z^{\gamma}}>\eta/2\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{z\in[0,2a]}\frac{w_{n}(z)}{z^{\gamma}}>\eta/2\right]

for
k
sufficiently large.
As
0<\gamma<1/2
, it follows from previous considerations that we may find
N_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{2}\in(0,1)
such that for
n\geq\mathbb{N}_{3}
and
0<a<a_{2}
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{z\in[0,2a]}\frac{w_{n}(z)}{z^{\gamma}}>\eta/2\right]<\frac{\epsilon}{6}.

Let us now consider the second term in (
C.4
). By (
C.3
), for
x>(2k)^{-1}
such that also
x\geq\eta x^{\delta}\sqrt{k}
,

\displaystyle\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}\leq-\eta

\displaystyle\iff Q_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n}x)\leq\tfrac{k}{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})

\displaystyle\iff\Gamma_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right)\geq\tfrac{k}{n}x

\displaystyle\iff\sqrt{k}\left\{\tfrac{n}{k}\Gamma_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right)-(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})\right\}\geq\eta x^{\delta}

\displaystyle\iff\frac{w_{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}\geq\eta.

If
x<\eta x^{\delta}\sqrt{k}
, then the inequality
v_{n}(x)/x^{\delta}\leq-\eta
is impossible, since
Q_{n}\geq 0
and thus
v_{n}(x)\geq-x\sqrt{k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\inf_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}<-\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\inf_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}\leq-\eta\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in((2k)^{-1},a]\\
x\geq\eta x^{\delta}\sqrt{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{w_{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}\geq\eta\right].

Since
x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k}\leq x
, the weight
1/x^{\delta}
can only increase if we replace it by
1/(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})^{\delta}
. But then

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in((2k)^{-1},a]\\
x\geq\eta x^{\delta}\sqrt{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{w_{n}(x-\eta x^{\delta}/\sqrt{k})}{x^{\delta}}\leq\sup_{z\in[0,a]}\frac{w_{n}(z)}{z^{\delta}}.

Consequently, it follows again from our results on the weighted tail empirical process that we may find
N_{4}\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{3}\in(0,1)
such that for
n\geq\mathbb{N}_{4}
and
0<a<a_{3}
,

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\inf_{x\in((2k)^{-1},a]}\frac{v_{n}(x)}{x^{\delta}}<-\eta\right]<\frac{\epsilon}{6}.

Combining everything, we have for
n\geq\max\{N_{1},\ldots,N_{4}\}
and
0<a<\min\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
in (
C.2
),

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\frac{\left|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\right|}{x^{\delta}}>\eta\right]<\epsilon.

This concludes the proof.
