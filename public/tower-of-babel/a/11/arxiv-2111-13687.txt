Regularized bayesian best response learning in finite games
Sayan Mukherjee and Souvik Roy

Abstract
We introduce the notion of regularized Bayesian best response (RBBR) learning dynamic in heterogeneous population games. We obtain such a dynamic via perturbation by an arbitrary lower semicontinuous, strongly convex regularizer in Bayesian population games with finitely many strategies. We provide a sufficient condition for the existence of rest points of the RBBR learning dynamic, and hence the existence of regularized Bayesian equilibrium in Bayesian population games. These equilibria are shown to approximate the original Bayesian equilibria for vanishingly small perturbations. We also explore the fundamental properties of the RBBR learning dynamic, which includes the existence of unique continuous solutions from arbitrary initial conditions, as well as the continuity of the solution trajectories thus obtained with respect to the initial conditions. Finally, as application the the theory we introduce the notions of Bayesian potential and Bayesian negative semidefinite games and provide convergence results for such games. Keywords : Regularizer; Bayesian Strategies; Regularized Bayesian Best Response Learning; Bayesian Potential Games, Bayesian Negative Semidefinite Games. Mathematics Subject Classification : 91A07, 91A14, 91A22, 91A26, 91A27.



1. Introduction

In this paper, we seek to introduce the notion of the regularized Bayesian best response (RBBR) learning dynamic for heterogeneous populations, where the agents in the population are diverse. Unlike usual homogeneous population games, players are distributed over types taking values in a complete separable metric space. Extrapolating the notion of best response for homogeneous population games, the Bayesian best response also seems to be the plausible behavioral norm in evolutionary game theory. However, again, due to the multiplicity of the Bayesian best response map and the fact that it can change abruptly, makes the study of the map challenging exercise. Thus, the dynamic generated via the Bayesian best response map is difficult to analyze (
Gilboa and Matsui 1991
,
Hofbauer 1995
). In case of regularized best response dynamics, one can circumvent the aforementioned problem by considering a uniquely defined smoothed version of the Bayesian best response, which can then be studied via the standard theory of ordinary differential equations.

As a result, we obtain the RBBR learning dynamic, where the agents’ payoffs are subject to perturbation by an arbitrary, but fixed regularizer, and subsequently the agents best respond with respect to these perturbed payoffs. The logit dynamic (
Fudenberg and Levine 1998
) is a prototypical example of evolutionary dynamics in population games with homogeneous payoff structure, where the agents’ payoff are regularized using the well--known Shannon-Gibbs entropy. In our set--up, such a dynamic is called the logit RBBR learning dynamic. However, instead of assuming a particular form of the regularizer, RBBR learning dynamic is generated via perturbation by an arbitrary strongly convex regularizer, which includes the Tsallis entropy and the Burg entropy.

Hofbauer and Sandholm 2002
, and
Héliou et al. 2020
for further details.

We now provide some motivations behind our study. Regularized best response dynamics are one of the most important classes of dynamics in the literature of evolutionary game theory. The logit dynamic is the most common and widely studied dynamic in evolutionary games. Several authors have, since then, extended the notion of logit dynamic to the class of more general classes of perturbed best response dynamics as a means to study the social behavior of the underlying population (
Benaım and Hirsch 1999
,
Hofbauer and Hopkins 2005
,
Hofbauer and Sandholm 2002
,
Hofbauer and Sandholm 2007
).
To the best of our knowledge, regularized best response have been studied primarily in the context of homogeneous population games. This paper is an attempt to extend the notion of perturbed best response dynamics to the general class of Bayesian games. More formally, as opposed to population games with homogeneous payoff structure, we consider Bayesian population games, where the agents are distributed over types. The purpose of considering Bayesian population games is that in real life scenarios it is highly unlikely that all agents in the population are of the same type.

We now provide a brief heuristics of our model, which we borrow from
Ely and Sandholm 2005
. We consider a population where the agents are distributed over a complete separable metric space consisting of types, equipped with the usual Borel sigma-algebra, and a probability measure. Such a probability space is called the type space of the population. The state space of the RBBR learning dynamic is the space of Bayesian strategies, which are Böchner measurable maps from the type space to the space of all distributions over the strategies. However, the space of Bayesian strategies do not form a normed linear space. We therefore consider the normed linear space of integrable signed Bayesian strategies which are measurable maps from the type space onto
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Following
Ely and Sandholm 2005
, we endow this space with the strong norm, which turns it into a Banach space. It then follows from the relevant literature (
Diestel and Uhl Jr 1978
) that the space of signed Bayesian strategies is actually the Banach space of Böchner integrable functions. We then define the regularized Bayesian best response dynamic on the space of signed Bayesian strategies and subsequently study the properties of the dynamic.

Some overview of our results are as follows. First, we establish the existence of rest points of the RBBR learning dynamic, which are fixed points of the regularized Bayesian best response map, and which we call regularized Bayesian equilibria. Next, we show that these regularized equilibria which we obtain, act as approximations to the Bayesian equilibria of the original game for vanishingly small regularizations. This approximation result is somewhat non-trivial and calls for some added conditions on the regularizers apart from being strong convexity. Loosely speaking, we require the regularizer to satisfy any one of the two conditions, namely Condition C1 or Condition C2. Condition C1 ensures that the population concentrates on the strategy which provides maximum payoff as the regularization vanishes. Condition C2 requires the regularizer to satisfy some mild continuity conditions in the sense that small changes in the population state leads only to a small change in the value of the regularizer. We also show that the well-known regularizers considered in the literature of evolutionary game theory satisfies one of these conditions.
Proceeding, we show the existence of a unique continuous solution trajectory to the RBBR dynamic is continuous for every given initial condition. We further show that the solutions to the RBBR dynamic are continuous with respect to the initial states (that is, if we start from two initial states which are very close, then the respective solutions to the RBBR dynamic will also be enough close). As applications of our results, we introduce the notions of Bayesian potential and Bayesian negative semidefinite games. We bring to notice to the reader that the notion on Bayesian potential games have been considered before in
Zusai 2018
. Both these games are extensions of potential games and negative semidefinite games for homogeneous populations, respectively, for heterogeneous populations.

Unlike evolutionary games with finitely many strategies, where the state space of the underlying dynamic is finite dimensional, the space of Bayesian strategies on which the RBBR learning dynamic is defined is an infinite dimensional Banach space. As a result, the evolutionary dynamics on the space of Bayesian strategies in a non trivial extension of the existing results on homogeneous population games with finite strategies. The choice of topology on the state space plays an important role in deriving the above mentioned results. For example, the weak topology on the space of signed Bayesian strategies turns out to be the appropriate topology to prove the result related to the existence of regularized Bayesian equilibrium. Whereas, on the other hand, the strong topology (induced by the strong norm) is necessary to show existence of solution trajectories to the RBBR learning dynamic. In establishing convergence results for Bayesian potential and Bayesian negative semidefinite games requires a further exercise of characterizing (strong) norm compact and forward invariant subsets of the space of signed Bayesian strategies in view of constructing a suitable Lyapunov function and then applying relevant results from theory of dynamical systems.

As far as we know, evolution in Bayesian games have not been explored much in the literature of evolutionary game theory. Such a theory of evolution in Bayesian games was first introduced by
Ely and Sandholm 2005
in the view of studying the Bayesian best response dynamics where the agents in the population has diverse preferences. The authors did not consider regularization of the Bayesian best response. Instead, they provided sufficient conditions for the Bayesian best response correspondence to be single-valued. They showed that the equilibrium behavior of such a dynamic can be studied via the aggregation, though the disequilibrium behavior of both the dynamics are quite dissimilar. In principle, they showed that the equilibrium behavior and stability of the Bayesian best response dynamic can be fully characterized by that of the corresponding aggregation dynamic. This paper was followed by
Sandholm et al. 2007
which studies the evolutionary stability of purified equilibria of two-player normal form games under the Bayesian best response dynamic.
Staudigl 2013
considers a network evolution model where players have diverse preferences and provides a closed-form expression for the invariant distribution of the underlying co-evolutionary process for potential games.
Zusai 2018
considers evolutionary dynamics in heterogeneous populations obtained via a mean dynamic based on revision protocol functions. They show that under some regularity conditions on the revision protocol function, the evolutionary dynamic admits a unique solution trajectory along with equilibrium stability in heterogeneous potential games. Further, they show equilibrium stability in heterogeneous population games. Our approach is different from
Zusai 2018
in the sense that we focused primarily on generating the best response evolutionary dynamic obtained via regularization of the agents’ payoffs. We find this important to study mainly due to two major reasons; firstly, a vast amount of literature in evolutionary game theory devoted to perturbed best response learning, and secondly, to address and rigorously provide insights to similar questions, for eg. existence and approximation of regularized equilibria, as well as convergence of certain classes of games, namely Bayesian potential and Bayesian negative semidefinite games.

3. Regularization of Bayesian best response

In this section we seek to define the notion of regularized Bayesian best response learning dynamic, which in turn requires us to define the notion of Bayesian best response map. It is an extension of best response maps to the context of Bayesian population games. For a Bayesian strategy
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
, the expected payoff to a player of type
\displaystyle\omega
against an aggregate population state (aggregate belief)
\displaystyle\textbf{y}\in\Delta
is given by
\displaystyle\langle\textbf{y},\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)\rangle
, where
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual inner product on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Quite naturally, the
Bayesian best response correspondence
is a mapping
\displaystyle{\beta}:\Sigma\twoheadrightarrow\Sigma
such that for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
,

{\beta}[\sigma](\omega):=\arg\max_{\textbf{y}\in\Delta}\langle\textbf{y},\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)\rangle,\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\omega\in\Omega.

(1)

Note that from the definition of Bayesian population games,
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)
denotes the payoff vector a player of type
\displaystyle\omega
faces at Bayesian strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
. Thus, the best response to a player of type
\displaystyle\omega
at Bayesian strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
is any probability distribution
y
on
\displaystyle S
which maximizes the expected payoff
\displaystyle\langle\textbf{y},\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)\rangle
.

A Bayesian strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}\in\Sigma
is said to be a
Bayesian equilibrium
of the game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}\in\beta(\sigma^{\circ})
. In terms of the game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
, the above condition can equivalently be expressed in the following way:
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}
is a Bayesian equilibrium if for
\displaystyle\xi
-a.e
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega

\sigma^{\circ}_{j}(\omega)>0\implies\mathcal{G}_{j}(\sigma^{\circ},\omega)\geq\mathcal{G}_{i}(\sigma^{\circ},\omega),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }i\in S.

(2)

In principle, the above condition suggests that a Bayesian strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}
is an equilibrium provided the following condition holds: if a strategy
\displaystyle j\in S
has positive probability in the induced belief
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}(\omega)
, then the payoff vector
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)
should have a component-wise maximum at strategy

\displaystyle j
. Thus, strategies in the support of the induced belief must have maximum payoff. Let
\displaystyle\text{BE}(\mathcal{G},\xi)
denote the collection of Bayesian equilibria corresponding to the game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
.
In order to state the result on existence of Bayesian equilibrium, we require the notion of an appropriate topology on the space of Bayesian strategies, which essentially is the weak topology in this case (see Definition
A.4
).

A Bayesian population game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:\Sigma\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle{\xi}
-a.e

Suppose that the population game

As the title of the paper suggests, we now shift our focus towards the regularized version of the Bayesian best response. Recall that the Bayesian best response is a multi-valued mapping, which, under standard evolutionary dynamics, becomes difficult to analyze. To circumvent this problem, we resort to a regularized (smoothed) version of the Bayesian best response mapping in a view to obtain a unique smoothed best response which can then be analyzed using the existing theory of differential equations and dynamical systems. We begin with the definition of a regularizer.

Definition 3.2
.

A mapping
\displaystyle\textbf{v}:\Delta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is called a
regularizer
on
\displaystyle\Delta
if:

•

it is finite except possibly on the relative boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Delta
of
\displaystyle\Delta
;

•

it is lower semi-continuous on
\displaystyle\Delta
;

•

it is strongly convex on
\displaystyle\Delta^{\circ}
, in the sense that there exists
\displaystyle{\gamma}>0
such that for all
\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha\leq 1
,

\textbf{v}(\alpha\textbf{x}+(1-\alpha)\textbf{y})\leq\alpha\textbf{v}(\textbf{x})+(1-\alpha)\textbf{v}(\textbf{y})-\dfrac{{\gamma}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\|\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}\|^{2},\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\in\Delta^{\circ}.

It is well-known (see
Coucheney et al. 2015
,
Mertikopoulos and Sandholm 2016
) that regularization ensures the existence of a unique best response. We bring to notice that our definition of a regularizer is weaker as compared to the one used in the references mentioned just prior, in the sense that regularizers in
Coucheney et al. 2015
and
Mertikopoulos and Sandholm 2016
are smooth in the interior
\displaystyle\Delta^{\circ}
; whereas in our case, we only require lower semicontinuity on
\displaystyle\Delta
.

Definition 3.3

(
Regularized Bayesian best response
)
.

Given a noise parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, the regularized Bayesian best response (RBBR) is a mapping
\displaystyle{\beta}_{\epsilon}:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma
such that for every
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
,

{\beta}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega):=\arg\max_{\textbf{y}\in\Delta}\langle\textbf{y},\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)\rangle-\epsilon\textbf{v}(\textbf{y}),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\omega\in\Omega.

(4)

As we mentioned before, the purpose behind defining the regularized version of the Bayesian best response correspondence is to ensure the existence of a unique maximizer in (
4
), when the expected payoff is perturbed via the regularizer
v
, thereby making the study of the evolutionary learning dynamic amenable to the existing theory of ordinary differential equations and dynamical systems. Suppose further that the regularizer is steep, in the sense that
\displaystyle\|\nabla\textbf{v}(\textbf{x}_{n})\|\to\infty
as
\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{n}\to\partial\Delta
. It then follows that for every
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
, the best response
\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)
to type
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
lies in the relative interior
\displaystyle\Delta^{\circ}
(see
Rockafellar 1970
, Chapter 26).

Also it worthwhile to mention that the notion of deterministic and stochastically perturbed best response in homogeneous populations with finitely many strategies has been studied in several occasions such as
Benaım and Hirsch 1999
,
Hofbauer and Sandholm 2002
,
Hofbauer and Hopkins 2005
,
Hofbauer and Sandholm 2007
to name a few. In the recent past,
Mertikopoulos and Sandholm 2016
considers reinforcement learning under regularization.
In the context of homogeneous population games with a continuum of strategies, the study of perturbed best response dynamics has been generalized to logit best response in
Lahkar and Riedel 2015
and
Perkins and Leslie 2014
. Very recently, a general class of perturbed best response dynamics generated via an arbitrary perturbation function (regularizer in our case) has been considered in
Lahkar et al. 2022
. In this paper, we thus focus on extending the notion of perturbed best response to heterogeneous population games with finitely many strategies.

3.1 Some examples of regularizers

Below we provide some examples of widely used regularizers which have been extensively used in the literature of evolution and learning in games, both in the context of finite strategy games and infinite strategy homogeneous population games. They have also been used extensively in reinforcement learning and online non-convex optimization problems (see
Mertikopoulos and Sandholm 2016
,
Héliou et al. 2020
). In the case of continuum strategy games, perturbed best response dynamics using general regularizers have been recently considered in
Lahkar et al. 2022
.

Example 3.1

(
The Shannon-Gibbs entropy
)
.

The most classic and prototypical example of a regularizer is the well-known Shannon-Gibbs entropy which is defined as

\textbf{v}_{\textbf{s}}(\textbf{x}):=\sum_{j\in S}\textbf{x}_{j}\log\textbf{x}_{j},\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\textbf{x}\in\Delta.

(5)

As is well-known from the literature (see
Fudenberg and Levine 1998
), the regularized Bayesian best response map corresponding to
\displaystyle\textbf{v}_{\textbf{s}}
is such that for every
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
,

{\beta}^{\textbf{s}}_{\epsilon,j}[\sigma](\omega)=\dfrac{\exp(\epsilon^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{j}(\sigma,\omega))}{\sum_{i\in S}\exp(\epsilon^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{i}(\sigma,\omega))},\hskip 5.69054pt\text{ for all }\omega\in\Omega,\text{ and all }j\in S.

(6)

Example 3.2

(
The Tsallis entropy
)
.

A well-known generalization of the Shannon-Gibbs entropy is the so-called Tsallis entropy which is defined as

\textbf{v}_{\textbf{t}}(\textbf{x}):=(q-{q}^{2})^{-1}\sum_{j\in S}(\textbf{x}_{j}-\textbf{x}^{{q}}_{j}),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for }0<q<1,\text{ for all }\textbf{x}\in\Delta.

(7)

Notice that the Shannon-Gibbs entropy can be obtained from the Tsallis entropy by letting
\displaystyle q\to 1
. It can be shown (see for example
Coucheney et al. 2015
,
Héliou et al. 2020
) that under perturbation by
\displaystyle\textbf{v}_{\textbf{t}}
, the regularized Bayesian best response map takes the following form: for every

Consider an abstract differential equation

\displaystyle\omega\in\bar{\Omega}
, we have
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,k}_{m_{k}}(\omega)=\sigma^{\circ,k}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
for all
\displaystyle k\in S\setminus\{\textbf{i}(\omega),\textbf{j}(\omega)\}
. Therefore, in order to verify Condition C2, it is enough to bound the difference in the entropy function over the strategies
\displaystyle\textbf{i}(\omega)
and
\displaystyle\textbf{j}(\omega)
. Thus, by definition of Tsallis entropy, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\textbf{v}^{\textbf{t}}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\textbf{v}^{\textbf{t}}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q(1-q)}\Big[\underbrace{|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|}_{=:T^{k}_{1}(\omega)}+\underbrace{|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|}_{=:T^{k}_{2}(\omega)}\Big]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{1}{q(1-q)}\Big[|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|+\underbrace{|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|}_{=:T^{k}_{3}(\omega)}\Big].

(28)

We bound the terms
\displaystyle T^{k}_{1}(\omega),T^{k}_{2}(\omega)
and
\displaystyle T^{k}_{3}(\omega)
separately. Note that by the very definition of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ}_{m_{k}}
(via Definition
3.7
) that,

T^{k}_{1}(\omega)=|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|=|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|.

(29)

In order to bound the term
\displaystyle T^{k}_{2}(\omega)
, we first observe that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
can be expressed as

\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)=\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega),

(30)

where
\displaystyle\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega):=1+\frac{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}
.
Since by definition,
\displaystyle\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>1
, and
\displaystyle 0<q<1
, we must have
\displaystyle\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-1<\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-1
. Thus we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{k}_{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|(\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-1)\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq|(\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-1)\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{1-q}}.

Thus, in order to bond the term
\displaystyle T^{k}_{2}(\omega)
, we need to lower bound the term
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{1-q}
. Note that since
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
is a regularized equilibrium of the sliced game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
, we have that

\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)=\Big[\frac{\epsilon_{m_{k}}}{1-q}\Big]^{1/(1-q)}\frac{1}{(\theta^{\textbf{t}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}},\omega)-\mathcal{G}^{\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{\omega}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}))^{1/(1-q)}}

(31)

Thus, letting
\displaystyle\eta(\omega)\downarrow 0
in (
25
), we have that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). As a result, we have that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q-1}\leq\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q-1}
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
(depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). Note that since
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>0
, there exists
\displaystyle c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)>0
such that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{2}
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
(depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). This, in conjunction with the fact that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
) implies that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{2}
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). Combining all the above observations, we arrive at

T^{k}_{2}(\omega)\leq\frac{2^{1-q}}{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}(\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)).

(32)

Finally, we proceed to bound the term
\displaystyle T_{3}
. Recall that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{2}
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
(depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). Now, by an application of the mean-value theorem, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{k}_{3}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)^{q}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{q2^{1-q}}{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|.

Combining all the inequalities obtained above in (
29
), (
32
) and (
A.7.2
) we have for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\textbf{v}^{\textbf{t}}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))-\textbf{v}^{\textbf{t}}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\Big(2+\frac{2^{1-q}}{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}+\frac{q2^{1-q}}{c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}\Big)|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}+2^{1-q}(1+q)}{q(1-q)c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}\Big)|\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|.

Thus setting
\displaystyle\kappa_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega):=\frac{2c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}+2^{1-q}(1+q)}{q(1-q)c_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)^{1-q}}
, and observing the fact that
\displaystyle{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
, proves the fact that the Tsallis entropy satisfies Condition C2.

A.7.3 Burg entropy satisfies Condition C2

In this subsection, we show that Burg entropy (Example
3.2
) satisfies Condition C2. Fix
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
, distinct
\displaystyle\textbf{i},\textbf{j}:\bar{\Omega}\to S
with
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>0
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\textbf{j}(\omega)}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}},\omega)>\mathcal{G}^{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}},\omega)
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
(depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). As in the case of the proof for Tsallis entropy, it is enough to bound the Burg entropy at co-ordinates
\displaystyle\textbf{i}(\omega)
and
\displaystyle\textbf{j}(\omega)
.

We proceed similar to the case of Tsallis entropy. Note that by definition of Burg entropy, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\textbf{v}^{\textbf{b}}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))-\textbf{v}^{\textbf{b}}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{|\log\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\log{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|}_{=:U^{k}_{1}(\omega)}+\underbrace{|\log\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\log{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|}_{=:U^{k}_{2}(\omega)}.

(33)

We bound the terms
\displaystyle U^{k}_{1}(\omega),U^{k}_{2}(\omega)
separately. We first observe that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
can be expressed as

\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)=\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega),

(34)

where
\displaystyle\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega):=1+\frac{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}
.
Since
\displaystyle\log(1+x)<x
for all
\displaystyle x>0
, we have that
\displaystyle\log\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)<\frac{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}
. Now, by definition of Burg entropy, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle U^{k}_{1}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\log\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\log\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\log\big[\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega){\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\big]-\log\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\log\delta^{\textbf{i}(\omega)\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}{\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)}

Thus, similar to Tsallis entropy, we need to lower bound the term
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
. Note that since
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
is a regularized equilibrium of the sliced game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
, we have that

\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}}_{m_{k}}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{m_{k}}}{(\theta^{\textbf{b}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}},\omega)-\mathcal{G}^{\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{\omega}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}))}.

(35)

Thus, letting
\displaystyle\eta(\omega)\downarrow 0
in (
25
), we have that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). Note that since
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>0
, there exists
\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)>0
such that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{2}
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle k
(depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). This, in conjunction with the fact that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
) implies that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{j}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{2}
for all
\displaystyle k
sufficiently large (depending on
\displaystyle\omega
). Combining the above observations, we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle U^{k}_{1}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}(\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)).

(36)

We now proceed to bound the term
\displaystyle U^{k}_{2}(\omega)
. Notice that the mapping
\displaystyle x\mapsto\log x
is Lipschitz on
\displaystyle[a,\infty)
, where
\displaystyle a>0
. The fact that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
is bounded away from
\displaystyle 0
, implies that the shifted sequence of Bayesian strategies can be chosen in such a way that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
is bounded away from
\displaystyle 0
. More precisely, we can construct a shifted sequence of Bayesian strategies such that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)\geq\frac{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}{4}
. Thus, by an application of mean value theorem, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle U^{k}_{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\log\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\log\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}(\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)).

(37)

Therefore, combining (
36
) and (
A.7.3
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\textbf{v}^{\textbf{b}}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))-\textbf{v}^{\textbf{b}}(\sigma^{\circ}_{m_{k}}(\omega))|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq U^{k}_{1}(\omega)+U^{k}_{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Big(\frac{2}{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}+\frac{4}{\widehat{c}_{i}(\omega)}\Big)(\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}(\sigma^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)-\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)).

Thus, setting
\displaystyle\widehat{\kappa}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega):=\frac{6}{\widehat{c}_{\textbf{i}(\omega)}(\omega)}
and observing the fact that
\displaystyle{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)>\widetilde{\sigma}^{\circ,\textbf{i}(\omega)}_{m_{k}}(\omega)
, proves the fact that the Burg entropy satisfies Condition C2.

We use the infinite dimensional Picard-Lindeloff Theorem (see

Theorem A.4

(
Picard-Lindelöff Theorem
,
Zeidler 1986
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}
be a Banach space. Let
\displaystyle y_{0}\in\mathbb{Y},t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{Q}_{b}:=\{(t,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Y}\mid\hskip 2.84526pt|t-t_{0}|\leq a\mbox{ and }\|y-y_{0}\|\leq b\}

for fixed
\displaystyle a,b>0

\displaystyle f:\mathcal{Q}_{b}\to\mathbb{Y}
is continuous and that

\|f(t,x)-f(t,y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|\text{ for all }(t,x),(t,y)\in\mathcal{Q}_{b},\mbox{ and}

\|f(t,x)\|<M\text{ for all }(t,x)\in\mathcal{Q}_{b}

where
\displaystyle L\geq 0
and
\displaystyle M>0
are fixed. Choose
\displaystyle c
such that
\displaystyle 0<c<a
and
\displaystyle Mc<b
. Then the initial value problem

\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t)),\hskip 28.45274ptx(t_{0})=y_{0},

where
\displaystyle x:[t_{0}-c,t_{0}+c]\to\mathbb{Y}
, has exactly one continuously differentiable solution on the interval
\displaystyle[t_{0}-c,t_{0}+c]
.

Now we proceed with the proof of Theorem
4.1
. To prove Theorem
4.1
, it is by Theorem
A.4
enough to show that the mapping
\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the strong topology on
\displaystyle\Sigma

Lemma A.4
.

The mapping
\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the strong norm on
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

Proof:
To prove the lemma, we need to show that there exists a constant
\displaystyle\kappa>0
, such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)-{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\bar{\kappa}\|\sigma-\rho\|,\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\sigma,\rho\in\Sigma.

(38)

Fix
\displaystyle\sigma,\rho\in\Sigma
.
By (
4
) and the definition of strong norm, we have that

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)-{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\|{\beta}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)-{\beta}_{\epsilon}[\rho](\omega)\|{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\textbf{v}^{*}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega))-\nabla\textbf{v}^{*}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{G}(\rho,\omega))\|{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\epsilon{\gamma}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega)-\mathcal{G}(\rho,\omega)\|{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\kappa}{\epsilon{\gamma}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sigma-\rho\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

\displaystyle(\epsilon{\gamma})^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)-{\beta}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

Therefore (

\displaystyle\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathbb{V}}\leq e^{L|t-t_{0}|}\|u(0)-v(0)\|

\displaystyle t\in[t_{0}-a,t_{0}+a]

(

)

\displaystyle\{\text{avg}(f;A):f\in\mathbb{K}\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle{\mu}(A)>0

\displaystyle\Xi\subseteq\Omega

\displaystyle\xi(\Xi)=1

\displaystyle\alpha>0

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle\delta>0

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt-\int_{\Omega}\langle d\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}[\sigma+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}](\widehat{\sigma}_{0}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega).

(50)

Since the game
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is Fréchet differentiable,
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is Gâteaux differentiable too in which case, using the fact that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
satisfies Bayesian self-defeating externalities, we have the following inequality:

\int_{\Omega}\langle d{\mathcal{G}}_{\omega}(\sigma+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0})(\widehat{\sigma}_{0}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\langle D{\mathcal{G}}_{\omega}(\sigma+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0})(\widehat{\sigma}_{0}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega)\leq 0.

We now show that the second term in the RHS of (
A.13
) is positive. For this, we note that for every
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}
, the following equality holds:

\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)=\nabla\textbf{v}_{\epsilon}(\sigma(\omega)),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\omega\in\Omega.

(51)

With the above equality in hand, we have that

\int_{\Omega}\langle d\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}](\widehat{\sigma}_{0}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\xi(d\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\langle d\nabla\textbf{v}_{\epsilon}[\sigma(\omega)+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)](\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega).

(52)

By assumption of the lemma,
\displaystyle\langle d\nabla\textbf{v}_{\epsilon}[\textbf{x}+t\textbf{z}_{0}](\textbf{z}_{0}),\textbf{z}_{0}\rangle\geq 0
, for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle\textbf{x}\in\Delta
and
\displaystyle\textbf{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle\textbf{x}+t\textbf{z}_{0}\in\Delta
. Thus, noticing the fact that
\displaystyle\sigma(\omega)+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\in\Delta
,
\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{0}
for all
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
and using the equality relation in (
52
), we have that

\int_{\Omega}\langle d\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}](\widehat{\sigma}_{0}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\xi(d\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\langle d\nabla\textbf{v}_{\epsilon}[\sigma(\omega)+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)](\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega).\geq 0.

(53)

Therefore, the LHS of (
A.13
), and thus
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda(\sigma,\widehat{\sigma}_{0},t)}{\partial t}<0
. Now, let us fix a regularized Bayesian equilibrium
\displaystyle{\sigma}^{\circ}
. It then follows from standard theory of gradient of convex conjugate (see for instance
Rockafellar 1970
) that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{i}(\sigma^{\circ},\omega)
is independent of
\displaystyle i
for every
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
. In other words, given the equilibrium
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}
, we have for every
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
, a
\displaystyle c(\sigma^{\circ},\omega)
such that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{i}(\sigma^{\circ},\omega)=c(\sigma^{\circ},\omega)
for all
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle i\in S
.
As a result, we have that
\displaystyle\lambda(\sigma^{\circ},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},0)=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{\circ},\omega),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{0}\in\widehat{\Sigma}
. Now define
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}:=\sigma^{\circ}+t\widehat{\sigma}_{0}
for some non-zero
\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{0}\in\widehat{\Sigma}
such that
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}
is distinct from
\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}
. By definition, we have
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{\sigma},\omega),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega)=\lambda(\sigma^{\circ},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},t)
.Also, from the previous arguments, we have that
\displaystyle\lambda(\sigma^{\circ},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},0)=0
. This, in conjunction with the fact that
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda(\bar{\sigma},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},t)}{\partial t}<0
, implies that
\displaystyle\lambda(\bar{\sigma},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},t)<0
. Therefore, we have that
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{\sigma},\omega),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\rangle\xi(d\omega)<0
, and hence
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}
cannot be a regularized equilibrium. This completes the proof of Lemma
6.2
.
∎

A.14 Proof of Theorem
6.1

Similar to the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we need to show that the Lyapunov function
\displaystyle\psi^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}
decreases along every solution trajectory to the RBBR learning dynamic which originates in
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
. To this end let us define the following quantities:

•

\displaystyle\psi^{1}_{\epsilon}(\sigma):=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega),{\beta}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)
, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
,

•

\displaystyle\psi^{2}_{\epsilon}(\sigma):=\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}({\beta}_{\epsilon}(\sigma))
, for all
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
,

•

\displaystyle\psi^{3}_{\epsilon}(\sigma):=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\omega),\sigma(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)
, for all
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
, and

•

\displaystyle\psi^{4}_{\epsilon}(\sigma):=\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)
, for all
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
.

To prove the theorem, it is enough to show that

\dot{\psi}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)=\dot{\psi}^{1}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)+\dot{\psi}^{2}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)+\dot{\psi}^{3}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)+\dot{\psi}^{4}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\leq 0.

We now compute the derivatives of
\displaystyle\psi^{i}_{\epsilon}
,
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,4
separately. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{D}{\psi}^{1}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),{\beta}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma),\dot{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega).

Since the regularizer
\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}
is Gâteaux differentiable, we have that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{D}{\psi}^{2}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\text{d}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}({{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma))(\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma),\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}({{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma))\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)](\omega)(\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)){\xi}(d\omega).

For the third term, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{D}\psi^{3}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),\sigma(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma),\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega).

Finally for the fourth term we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{D}\psi^{4}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\text{d}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\dot{\sigma},\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)(\dot{\sigma}(\omega)){\xi}(d\omega).

Combining all the four equations above, we have after simplification that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{\psi}^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\omega)(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma),\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)](\omega)(\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)){\xi}(d\omega)-\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),\sigma(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma),\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)(\dot{\sigma}(\omega)){\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma),\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)-\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)](\omega)(\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)){\xi}(d\omega)+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)(\dot{\sigma}(\omega)){\xi}(d\omega).

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is a Bayesian negative semidefinite game and
\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{0}
, it follows from Lemma
6.1
that the first term in the RHS of the above equation is less than or equal to zero, that is,

\int_{\Omega}\langle\text{D}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}[\sigma](\dot{\sigma}),\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)\leq 0.

Using this fact and rearranging the other terms in the RHS of the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{\psi}^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma)-\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[{{\beta}}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)](\omega),\dot{{{\beta}}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}(\sigma)-\nabla^{\textbf{G}}\widetilde{\textbf{v}}_{\epsilon}[\sigma](\omega),\dot{\sigma}(\omega)\rangle{\xi}(d\omega).

It now follows from Lemma (
A.7
) that the first term in the RHS of the above inequality is
\displaystyle 0
. It also follows that the second term in the RHS of the above inequality is non-negative. As a result, we have
\displaystyle\dot{\psi}^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}\leq 0
along every solution which originates in
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
. In particular, this implies that the Lyapounov function defined in (
6
) decreases along every non–stationary solution which originates in
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
. By part (i) of Theorem
4.1
, we have that
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
is compact under the norm topology on
\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}
and
\displaystyle\dot{\psi}^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}\leq 0
with equality only at the rest points, it follows from the standard theory of dynamical systems (
Bhatia and Szegö 2006
) that the omega–limit of every solution trajectory satisfies
\displaystyle\dot{\psi}^{\alpha}_{M,\epsilon}=0
, which is the set of all rest points of the RBBR dynamic. We therefore have that every non–stationary solution arising in
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha}_{M}(\Xi)
converges to a regularized Bayesian equilibrium.

∎
