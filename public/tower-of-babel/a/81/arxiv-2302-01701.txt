Assessment of Spatio-Temporal Predictors in the Presence of Missing and Heterogeneous Data
Daniele Zambon, Cesare Alippi

Abstract
Deep learning approaches achieve outstanding predictive performance in modeling modern data, despite the increasing complexity and scale. However, evaluating the quality of predictive models becomes more challenging, as traditional statistical assumptions often no longer hold. In particular, spatio-temporal data exhibit dependencies across both time and space, often involving nonlinear dynamics, non-stationarities, and missing observations. As a result, advanced predictors such as spatio-temporal graph neural networks require novel evaluation methodologies. This paper introduces a residual correlation analysis framework designed to assess the optimality of spatio-temporal predictive neural models, particularly in scenarios with incomplete and heterogeneous data. By leveraging the principle that residual correlation indicates information not captured by the model, this framework serves as a powerful tool to identify and localize regions in space and time where model performance can be improved. A key advantage of the proposed approach is its ability to operate under minimal assumptions, enabling robust evaluation of deep learning models applied to multivariate time series, even in the presence of missing and heterogeneous data. The methodology employs tailored spatio-temporal graphs to encode sparse spatial and temporal dependencies within the data and utilizes asymptotically distribution-free summary statistics to pinpoint time intervals and spatial regions where the model underperforms. The effectiveness of the proposed residual analysis is demonstrated through validation on both synthetic and real-world scenarios involving state-of-the-art predictive models.



1
Introduction

Spatio-temporal predictive neural models fit data by leveraging the inductive biases inherent in relational information derived from the temporal and spatial dimensions of observations
(
Seo et al. 2018
;
Bai et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2019
;
Pal et al. 2021
;
Ruiz et al. 2020
;
Jin et al. 2023
)
. The spatial domain is often represented as a graph, corresponding to structures such as a pixel grid, a 3D mesh, a road map, or a brain network
(
Shuman et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Stankovic et al. 2020
)
. However, the term
spatial
should be interpreted more broadly, encompassing any functional dependencies among sensors, extending beyond correlations tied to their physical positions
(
Cini et al. 2023a
)
.
Modern spatio-temporal data, such as multivariate time series generated by sensor networks, present significant challenges, including irregular sampling, substantial missing observations, and the dynamic nature of heterogeneous sensors that can be added or removed over time
(
Alippi 2014
;
Montero-Manso and Hyndman 2021
)
.
In such contexts, predictive deep neural models may exhibit varying performance across different regions of the spatio-temporal domain, complicating the analysis of model quality and the detection of unexpected behaviors. Assessing the optimality of these predictors, particularly when leveraging advanced processing neural architectures, remains an intricate task for which no robust and effective methods currently exist.

The quality of deep predictive models is typically assessed using task-specific accuracy metrics, with the squared error being a common choice evaluating the 2-norm of the prediction
residuals
– the difference between the observed values and the model’s predictions; alternative metrics include absolute (MAE) and relative errors (MAPE)
(
Gneiting 2011
)
.
These approaches are practical and straightforward, which likely explains their widespread adoption. However, they are inherently comparative, selecting the best model based on statistically superior performance relative to others. Consequently, they provide no direct insight into a model’s optimality or specific guidance on areas that require improvement.

An alternative approach to assessing model quality focuses on analyzing the
correlations
among prediction residuals rather than their magnitude. The rationale is that correlated residuals indicate structural information not captured by the model
(
Li 2003
)
, hence suggesting room for improvement.
Over the years, various hypothesis tests have been developed to detect residual dependencies
(
Durbin and Watson 1950
;
Ljung and Box 1978
;
Box et al. 2015
)
.
Commonly known as randomness or whiteness tests, these tests assess whether residuals exhibit white noise behavior, meaning they lack correlations. However, they rely on strict assumptions, including fully available multivariate time series data, synchronous sampling, and identically distributed data.
In practice, though, incomplete and heterogeneous data are the norm,
posing significant challenges for existing methods and highlighting the need for more robust tests in such conditions
(
Zambon and Alippi 2022
)
.

1.1
Contributions

This paper presents a novel residual analysis framework for assessing the quality of models designed for spatio-temporal prediction tasks, particularly in scenarios with missing and heterogeneous data, and dynamic (spatial) relational structures.
The framework not only detects residual correlations but also pinpoints specific regions where models fail to capture the underlying data-generating process, providing deeper insights beyond traditional evaluation methods.
In particular, the paper addresses three key questions about model optimality, intended here as the absence of autocorrelations and cross-dependencies in prediction residuals.

Q1

Is the non-linear neural model optimal in terms of the absence of autocorrelations and cross-dependencies among prediction residuals?

Q2

Are there specific spatial regions (e.g., groups of time series) where predictions can be improved?

Q3

Are there specific time intervals where the model fails?

These questions are addressed by partitioning spatial and temporal regions of residuals into tailored subgraphs, followed by the computation of summary statistics, or
scores
, to quantify potential correlations. These scores measure spatio-temporal correlations by leveraging the AZ-whiteness test
(
Zambon and Alippi 2022
)
, which requires neither prior knowledge of the data distribution nor identical distributions across time series.

The key contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

A correlation scoring function that improves over the one introduced in
(
Zambon and Alippi 2022
)
, yielding scores that facilitate comparative analysis and interpretation.

•

A method to identify heterogeneous time series exposing missing data whose associated prediction residuals exhibit significant evidence of correlation.

•

A method to pinpoint time intervals where residuals display correlation.

•

A method to identify spatio-temporal regions where residual correlations are particularly prominent.

The AZ-analysis is validated using synthetic data, and its practical relevance is demonstrated in the real-world scenarios of traffic flow and energy production neural forecasting.

1.2
Significance and impact

Operating under minimal requirements, the proposed approach is versatile and broadly applicable to real-world scenarios involving deep and graph-based predictors.
Notably, it does not impose assumptions on the distribution of residuals or require data to be identically distributed.
The only prerequisite is that residuals are centered around zero – an assumption that is typically satisfied in most practical settings.

A key strength of the proposed analysis lies in its graph-based processing, which focuses on residual pairs that are more likely to exhibit correlations. This targeted approach enhances the statistical power of the method, making it more effective in detecting model shortcomings.

While the proposed residual analysis does not directly quantify the extent of potential model improvement, it complements traditional comparative evaluations based on prediction accuracy by providing an independent, metric-agnostic assessment of model quality. Experimental results reveal valuable insights from residual correlation analysis that are not captured through standard prediction error evaluations.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews related work. Section
3
introduces the edge scores, the foundation of the proposed residual analysis, and shows how to design the spatio-temporal graph – a multiplex graph that links residual observations based on spatial and temporal relationships.
Section
4
provides an overview of the AZ-whiteness test.
The paper’s novel contributions are detailed in Section
5
and validated using synthetic data in Section
6
.
Sections
7
and
8
showcase two real-world applications. Finally, conclusions are presented in Section
9
.

2
Related work

Statistical tests designed to discover correlations in temporal data date back to Durbin and Watson’s investigations
(
Durbin and Watson 1950
;
Durbin 1970
)
with variants inspecting longer-range correlations
(
Box and Pierce 1970
;
Ljung and Box 1978
)
and multivariate time series
(
Hosking 1981
;
Li et al. 2019
)
.

More general statistical tests leverage graph-based structures to connect data points. One of the earliest examples is the two-sample Friedman-Rafsky test
(
Friedman and Rafsky 1979
)
, which assesses the equality of distributions.
Building on this foundation, subsequent research has extended these methods to change-point detection tests and statistical tests applicable to high-dimensional and non-Euclidean data
(
Chen and Zhang 2013
;
Chu and Chen 2019
;
Chen and Chu 2023
)
.

Following the approach in
(
Wald and Wolfowitz 1940
;
Geary 1970
)
, which employs sign statistics to design non-parametric tests,
Zambon and Alippi 2022
introduced a whiteness test to evaluate the overall presence of spatial and/or temporal correlation in prediction residuals.

To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first one providing a comprehensive spatio-temporal analysis of correlation patterns at the node (time series), temporal, and local spatio-temporal levels.

Consider a family of predictive models

Nonnegative scalar weights

where node set

Model predictions

Define prediction residuals as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}\doteq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t,v}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t,v}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\doteq(V^{*},E^{*},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})

(6)

obtained by stacking all graphs

, for all

Formally, node set

Edge set

(8)

where

\displaystyle E_{\rm sp}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{\{u_{t},v_{t}\}:(u,v)\in E_{t},v\neq u,1\leq t\leq T\right\},

(9)

\displaystyle E_{\rm tm}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{\{v_{t},v_{t+1}\}:v\in V_{t}\cap V_{t+1},1\leq t<T\right\}.

(10)

, for all

4
AZ-whiteness test

The analysis presented in this paper leverages the AZ-whiteness test
(
Zambon and Alippi 2022
)
, a statistical test designed to detect the presence of both autocorrelation and cross-correlation.
The test is defined over hypotheses

\begin{cases}H_{0}:&\text{All pairs }(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\tau,u})\text{ are uncorrelated,}\\
H_{1}:&\text{At least a pair }(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\tau,u})\text{ is correlated,}\end{cases}

(11)

for
(t,v)\neq(\tau,u)
, and rejects the null hypothesis
H_{0}
whenever the absolute value of statistic
C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})
exceeds a threshold
\gamma
predetermined according to a user-defined significance level
\alpha

\mathbb{P}\left(\,|C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})|\geq\gamma\mid H_{0}\,\right)=\alpha.

(12)

The AZ-whiteness test statistic
C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}
is computed on the prediction residuals
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}:\forall\ t,v\}
with reference to the spatio-temporal multiplex graph
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
.
More in detail, the test statistic is defined as

C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})\doteq\frac{\lambda\widetilde{C}_{\rm sp}+(1-\lambda)\widetilde{C}_{\rm tm}}{\left(\lambda^{2}W_{{\rm sp}}+(1-\lambda)^{2}W_{{\rm tm}}\right)^{1/2}},

(13)

where parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
trades off spatial and temporal contributions

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\rm sp}

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{\{u_{t},v_{t}\}\in E_{\rm sp}}w_{\{u_{t},v_{t}\}}\;{\rm sgn}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\rm tm}

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{\{v_{t},v_{t+1}\}\in E_{\rm tm}}w_{\rm tm}\;{\rm sgn}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t+1,v}\right),

(15)

respectively.
Scalar quantities
\widetilde{C}_{\rm sp}
and
\widetilde{C}_{\rm tm}
are weighted sums assessing the spatial and temporal correlation, respectively, by accounting for positive and negative signs

{\rm sgn}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\tau,u}\right)\ \in\{-1,0,1\}

(16)

of the scalar product

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\tau,u}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{y}}r_{t,v}(i)\;r_{\tau,u}(i)

(17)

between residual vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{t,v}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\tau,u}
; sign function

{\rm sgn}(a)
equals
-1,0
or
1
depending on whether
a
is negative, null, or positive.
Scalar
w_{\rm tm}
is a single weight assigned to all temporal edges and set such that the normalization term

W_{\rm tm}\doteq|E_{\rm tm}|\cdot w_{\rm tm}^{2}
equals
W_{\rm sp}

W_{{\rm sp}}\doteq\sum_{\{u_{t},v_{t}\}\in E_{\rm sp}}w_{\{u_{t},v_{t}\}}^{2}.

(18)

5
AZ-analysis of residuals

The AZ-analysis introduced in this paper addresses questions

of subsets

of all possible pairs of residuals in

It is convenient to refer subsets

Given suitably designed subgraphs, we compute and compare correlation scores

Although subgraphs
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\}
may differ in size, we show that the associated scores

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
associated with
|C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{i})|\gg 0
(evidencing correlation),
starting from those with large
|c_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{i})|
in decreasing order.

5.2
Subgraphs as sets of edges

Note that the number of correlated pairs of residuals is, in principle,
quadratic in the number of residuals
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
in (
5
),
N\,T
, where
T
is the length of the time series and
N
the average number of available sensors.
More precisely, there are
N\,T
observed residuals – corresponding to the nodes

v_{t}\in V^{*}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
– which leads to
(N\,T)(N\,T-1)/2
(possibly) correlated node pairs. Indeed, considering all possible subsets of node pairs is unfeasible, as their number is about
2^{{N^{2}\,T^{2}/2}}
. We deal with this complexity problem in two steps.

First,
we confine the analysis to node pairs corresponding to edges in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
,
which amounts to considering all possible subgraphs of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
.
As the relational information encoded in multiplex
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
is relevant to solving the downstream task, the node pairs are those that are more likely to exhibit correlation in the associated residuals. The inspection reduces to
2^{E_{*}}
subgraphs,
i.e.
, approximately
2^{N\,\delta\,T/2}
, with
\delta
the average node degree in the graph sequence incremented by 2 to account for temporal edges.
Second,
as computing and inspecting scores for all
2^{E_{*}}
subsets is still impractical, we propose principled families
of subgraphs designed to address the three key questions
Q1
,
Q2
, and
Q3

in Sections
5.3
to
5.6
.

5.3
Determining the presence of correlation (
Q1
)

The AZ-whiteness test (
13
) with
\lambda=1/2
applied to multiplex graph
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
allows us for assessing the overall presence of correlation; here, large values of
|C_{\lambda=1/2}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})|
denote a pronounced correlation in the residuals.

Instead, the values of statistics
C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})
with
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=1
provide first insights about the presence of temporal and spatial correlations, respectively. This follows from Theorem
1
, as
C_{\lambda=0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})
and
C_{\lambda=1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})
share the same distribution under the assumption of independent residuals; therefore comparing their values indicates which – temporal or spatial – elements in the processing pipeline show margins for improvement.

For instance,
|C_{\lambda=0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})|>|C_{\lambda=1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})|
with
|C_{\lambda=0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{*})|\gg 0
would indicate temporal (auto)correlation to be more problematic than spatial correlation.
Should this be the case, the designer has to intervene on the temporal processing pipeline to improve the model,

e.g.
, by changing the width of the input window or the neural prediction architecture; we refer to
(
Gao and Ribeiro 2022
;
Cini et al. 2023a
)
for discussions about different ways of combining spatial and temporal processing in deep architectures.

6.1
Synthetic residuals

The residual signal
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
is modeled as Gaussian noise exhibiting spatial and temporal correlations within specific regions defined by the intersection of sets
A
and
B

The data is generated for 60 nodes and 400 time steps. Set

covers time steps in

time steps in
[100,300)

Starting from i.i.d. white noise

r_{t,v}=\varepsilon_{t,v}+\frac{\sum_{u\in N(v)}\varepsilon_{t,u}}{|N(v)|}+\frac{\varepsilon_{t-1,u}+\varepsilon_{t+1,u}}{2}.

(31)

Finally, for each point

Use case in energy production

In the third experiment, we investigate the task of forecasting energy production from photovoltaic plants.

8.1

We consider EngRAD dataset
(
Marisca et al. 2024

A graph representation is constructed based on the pairwise geographical distance between locations. The ttsRNN model was employed and trained to minimize the MAE on 3-hour ahead forecasts of horizontal irradiance, leveraging an input window of 24 preceding hours and related weather variables.
The residual analysis was conducted on the test set, focusing on 1-hour ahead forecasts. Further experimental details are provided in Appendices

.

8.2
Insights from the residual analysis

Figure
11
reveals a daily trend evident in both the MAE and the time scores
c_{\lambda}(t)
, attributable to the natural solar irradiation cycle. Missing observations correspond to the negligible solar irradiation of the night.

Correlation at dawn and dusk

Higher correlations observed during dawn and dusk suggest room for model improvement, while the MAE remains relatively low throughout these transitional periods. While the MAE alone is not particularly concerning during dawn and dusk, further inspection reveals higher relative prediction error (mean absolute percentage error, MAPE), reinforcing the evidence provided by
c_{\lambda}(t)
that the model’s predictions can improve in these hours of the day.

Furthermore, the residual analysis unveils another interesting insight: in certain instances where the model exhibits low MAE values, a high correlation is present, thus indicating that they can be further enhanced.
Specifically, on June 23rd and 24th, 2020, the target variable exhibited a notably high magnitude with reduced variability, coupled with a consistently lower MAE. While this suggests that the target variable is easier to forecast during these specific periods, it is likely that the training primarily focused on regions characterized by higher prediction errors.

9
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduce a novel analysis of model prediction residuals, called AZ-analysis, for spatio-temporal data that allows practitioners to identify space-time regions, such as time intervals or sets of sensors, where the model performance can be improved.
The proposed analysis complements that of the prediction error by evaluating the presence of temporal and spatial residual correlation in regions of the space-time and sheds light on aspects where the predictive model is not optimal.
The AZ-analysis is well-suited for assessing deep neural models in real-world scenarios as (i) it can operate in presence of missing heterogeneous data, (ii) it scales to large and complex datasets by exploiting graph-side information, and (iii) only requests residuals centered around zero in a few setups; no other assumption about the distribution of residuals is requested, not even of identical distribution.
The proposed framework is first validated on synthetic experiments and general comments on how to interpret the results are provided along with real-world use cases in the context of forecasting traffic flow and energy production to showcase the potential of the proposed AZ-analysis.

Appendix A
Experiment on the comparability of scores

In the experiment of Figure
3
, Section
5.1
, the values of statistic
C_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}})
and score
c_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}})
are computed for graphs
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}
of different number of edges
|E_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}}|
and Pearson correlation among residuals. Considered subgraph
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}
is given as a collection of non-incident edges. For each edge
(u,v)
, residual
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of node
v
is generated as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}=\rho\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}},

(32)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}
is the residual at node
u
. The components of vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime}
are independent samples from the standard Gaussian distribution, whereas
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}
centers the median of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}
to zero.
Note that scalar
\rho
is selected in the
[0,1]
interval and determines the Pearson correlation between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}
.
In fact, as
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}]=\mathbf{0}
,
{\rm Var}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}]=\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}]=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}
by construction,

{\rm Var}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}]=\rho^{2}{\rm Var}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}]+(1-\rho^{2}){\rm Var}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime}]=\mathbb{I}

(33)

and

\displaystyle\text{Cor}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{v}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\top}]=

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\rho\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\top}]

(35)

\displaystyle=\rho\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\top}]+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\top}]

(36)

\displaystyle=\rho\,\mathbb{I}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}^{\prime}]^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{u}]=\rho\,\mathbb{I}.

(37)

Moreover, above construction grants Assumption
A2
to hold.

Dimension

of residual vectors is set to 5.
Mean, standard deviation, and 25% and 75% percentiles are reported in Figure

and are estimated over 100 repeated simulations.

Appendix B
Additional details on models and training

For the traffic forecasting task, predictions are made from an input window of 12 time steps (1 hour), with the hour of the day and the day of the week added as covariates to all models.
For the solar irradiance prediction, forecasts are issued for 6 hours ahead with an input window of 24 hours; here, the hour of the day and hour of the year are added as covariates.
The residual analyses are conducted on the test set considering traffic forecasts at 5, 20, 40 and 60 minutes and solar irradiance predictions in the next hour for the energy production.

Baseline models uvRNN, mvRNN and ttsRNN are 1-layer RNN with gated recurrent units and the exponential linear unit as activation function. Each model has 32 hidden neurons. ttsRNN implements a simple message passing based on average aggregation on the uvRNN outputs.
Models are trained to minimize the mean absolute error (MAE) on the first 70% of data, validated on the following 10%, and tested on the remaining 20%; missing and imputed data are appropriately masked out during training. Training is performed for 200 epochs using Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.003 decreased every 50 epochs by a factor of 4. Each epoch consists of 300 batches of size 64. Early stopping is triggered after 50 epochs of no improvement on the validation set.
