A convergent numerical algorithm for α \alpha -dissipative solutions of the Hunter–Saxton equation Thanks: Research supported by the grants Waves and Nonlinear Phenomena (WaNP) and Wave Phenomena and Stability — a Shocking Combination (WaPheS) from the Research Council of Norway.
Thomas Christiansen, Katrin Grunert, Anders Nordli, Susanne Solem

Abstract
. A convergent numerical method for α \alpha -dissipative solutions of the Hunter–Saxton equation is derived. The method is based on applying a tailor-made projection operator to the initial data, and then solving exactly using the generalized method of characteristics. The projection step is the only step that introduces any approximation error. It is therefore crucial that its design ensures not only a good approximation of the initial data, but also that errors due to the energy dissipation at later times remain small. Furthermore, it is shown that the main quantity of interest, the wave profile, converges in L ∞ L^{\infty} for all t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 , while a subsequence of the energy density converges weakly for almost every time.

Primary: 65M12, 65M25; Secondary: 65M06, 35Q35

1.
Introduction

In this article, we present a numerical algorithm for
\alpha
-dissipative solutions of the Cauchy problem for the Hunter–Saxton (HS) equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)+uu_{x}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{x}^{2}(t,z)dz-\frac{1}{4}\int_{x}^{\infty}u_{x}^{2}(t,z)dz,\quad u|_{t=0}=u_{0}.

The equation was derived as an asymptotic model of the director field of a nematic liquid crystal
[
19
]
, and possesses a rich mathematical structure. We mention a few of its properties here: it is bi-Hamiltonian and admits a Lax pair
[
20
]
, it can be interpreted as a geodesic flow
[
24
,
23
]
, and it admits numerous extensions and generalizations
[
22
,
26
,
31
]
.

A lot of the interest for (
1.1
) is generated by the fact that weak solutions in general will develop singularities in finite time, and, consequently, they are not unique, see
[
9
,
13
,
19
,
21
]
. This phenomenon is known as wave breaking. In particular,
u_{x}\rightarrow-\infty
pointwise while the energy
\|u_{x}(t,\cdot)\|_{2}
remains uniformly bounded, and the solution
u
, itself, Hölder continuous. Furthermore, at wave breaking, energy concentrates on a set of measure zero. Thus the energy density is in general not absolutely continuous. To overcome this problem it is common to augment the solution with a finite, positive Radon measure
\mu
describing the energy density, see
[
9
,
13
,
18
]
. In particular,
\mu
coincides with the usual kinetic energy density
u_{x}^{2}
in regions where there is no wave breaking, thus,
d\mu_{\text{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
. The energy is then described by
F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x))
.

Weak solutions can be extended past wave breaking in various ways, see for instance
[
3
,
9
,
13
,
21
]
. The two most prominent notions in the literature are that of a dissipative solution, where one removes all the concentrated energy from the system, and that of a conservative solution, where one reinserts the concentrated energy. In this work, we consider the concept of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions, first introduced in
[
12
]
for the related Camassa–Holm equation, and in
[
13
]
for (
1.1
). Instead of removing all the concentrated energy or none of it, an
\alpha
-fraction, where
\alpha\in[0,1]
, is removed. This way, the notion of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions acts as a continuous interpolant between the two extreme cases,
\alpha=0
corresponding to conservative solutions, and
\alpha=1
corresponding to dissipative solutions. Thus, the notion of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions allows for a uniform treatment of weak solutions with nonincreasing energy. The existence of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions was established in
[
13
]
for the two-component Hunter–Saxton system which generalizes (
1.1
), and in the more general setting where
\alpha\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1))\cup\{1\}
.

A common approach for solving the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) is to use a generalized method of characteristics, see for instance
[
9
,
13
]
.
This approach is followed here. In particular, given an
\alpha
-dissipative solution, the corresponding Lagrangian coordinates are governed by a linear system of differential equations, whose right hand side admits discontinuities at fixed times. These times can be computed a priori based on the initial data. Whence, if the initial data in Lagrangian coordinates is piecewise linear, the Lagrangian coordinates will remain piecewise linear at all later times, an important property which we take advantage of in this work.

Despite receiving a considerable amount of attention from a theoretical perspective, only a few number of numerical schemes have been proposed for the HS equation. In
[
17
]
several finite difference schemes for dissipative solutions were proved to converge. Also for dissipative solutions,
[
32
]
introduces a convergent, discontinuous Galerkin method. Furthermore, in
[
30
]
a finite difference scheme on a periodic domain for a modified HS equation was derived and proven to converge towards conservative solutions. More recently, a Godunov-inspired scheme
[
25
, Chp. 12.1]
for conservative solutions based on tracking the solution along characteristics, was introduced in
[
14
]
. The scheme was proved to converge and a convergence rate prior to wave breaking was derived.

We contribute to this line of research by introducing a numerical algorithm well-suited for
\alpha
-dissipative solutions. This algorithm is based on applying a tailor-made projection operator to the initial data, and then solving exactly along characteristics. Thus, the projection step is the only step that introduces any approximation error and it is therefore of particular importance that it not only yields a good approximation of the initial data, but also ensures that additional prospective errors, due to the energy dissipation, remain small and hence do not prevent convergence.

To highlight the importance of the correct choice of the projection operator, we compare our projection operator with the one introduced in
[
14
]
.
Motivated by the fact that a piecewise linear structure of the initial data is preserved at all later times, see
[
1
,
20
]
, the numerical scheme in
[
14
]
uses a standard piecewise linear projection operator in Eulerian coordinates at every timestep. In between timesteps the numerical solution is evolved exactly along characteristics. The projection operator used treats
u
and
F
completely independently, although
u
and
\mu
are strongly connected through the absolutely continuous part. Consequently, a deviation is introduced in the sense that
d\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}\neq u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}dx
, where
\Delta x
denotes the spatial discretization parameter. Thus, as pointed out in
[
14
]
, one is no longer dealing with the Hunter–Saxton equation, but rather a reformulated version of the two-component Hunter–Saxton system
[
28
]
, which is accompanied by a density
\rho
.
Or, in other words, the projection operator maps into the Eulerian set for the two-component Hunter-Saxton equation, see
[
28
, Def. 2.2]
, for which it has been established that
\rho
has a regularizing effect. In particular, if
\rho>0
in some interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, then the solution will never experience wave breaking in the interval spanned by the characteristics emanating from
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, see
[
11
, Thm 7.1]
. While this works out neatly in the setting of conservative solutions, since no energy is lost, for
\alpha
-dissipative solutions we are highly dependent on wave breaking actually occurring in order to remove concentrated energy. Thus, more care is needed to derive a suitable projection operator. That is why we introduce a piecewise linear projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
, which in contrast to the the one in
[
14
]
, ensures that the projected initial data satisfies
d\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}=u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}dx
.

We accompany the numerical algorithm with a convergence analysis,
which shows that the limit is a weak
\alpha
-dissipative solution. In particular, we obtain that
u_{\Delta x}\rightarrow u
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for all
t\geq 0
, which is the main quantity of interest in practice, while a subsequence of the energy measure
\mu_{\Delta x}(t)
converges weakly for almost every time.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we set the stage for the Cauchy problem of (
1.1
) by defining the Eulerian set
\mathcal{D}
, the Lagrangian set
\mathcal{F}
, the mappings
L
and
M
between them and the notion of an
\alpha
-dissipative solution. Then in Section
3
we focus on deriving and motivating our choice of the projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
, before we discuss the practical implementation of our numerical algorithm. In Section
4
we conduct the convergence analysis. In the last section, Section
5
, we provide a few numerical experiments to illustrate the theoretical results and to go beyond the theory established here by investigating the convergence rate.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we set the stage for the numerical algorithm by defining the sets we are working in, as well as recalling the construction of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions by using a generalized method of characteristics, as introduced in
[
13
]
and used in
[
15
]
.

Assume that
\alpha\in[0,1]
and denote by
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
the space of positive, finite Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}
. To define the set of Eulerian coordinates, we first need to recall some important spaces from
[
13
]
and
[
15
]
.

Introduce

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

which is a Banach space when equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{E}=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Furthermore, define

\displaystyle H_{d}^{1}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle:=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad d=1,2

and introduce a partition of unity
\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}
on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., a pair of functions
\chi^{+},\chi^{-}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

•

\chi^{+}+\chi^{-}=1
,

•

0\leq\chi^{\pm}\leq 1
,

•

\text{supp}(\chi^{+})\subset(-1,\infty)
and
\text{supp}(\chi^{-})\subset(-\infty,1)
.

Then, we can define the following mappings

\displaystyle R_{1}:H_{1}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow E,

\displaystyle\qquad(\bar{f},a)\mapsto f=\bar{f}+a\chi^{+},

\displaystyle R_{2}:H_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow E,

\displaystyle\qquad(\bar{f},a,b)\mapsto f=\bar{f}+a\chi^{+}+b\chi^{-},

which are linear, continuous, and injective, see
[
4
]
. Based on those, we introduce the Banach spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
as the images of
H_{1}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
H_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, respectively, that is,

\displaystyle E_{1}:=R_{1}\left(H_{1}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\right)\quad\text{and}\quad E_{2}:=R_{2}\left(H_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\right)

and endow them with the norms

\displaystyle\|f\|_{E_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\|\bar{f}+a\chi^{+}\|_{E_{1}}=\left(\|\bar{f}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{E_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\|\bar{f}+a\chi^{+}+b\chi^{-}\|_{E_{2}}=\left(\|\bar{f}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+a^{2}+b^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By construction the spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
do not rely on the particular choice of
\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}
, cf.
[
10
]
. Furthermore, observe that the mapping
R_{1}
is also well-defined for functions in
L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}
and therefore, we set

\displaystyle E_{1}^{0}:=R_{1}\left(L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right),

and equip this space with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{E_{1}^{0}}

\displaystyle:=\|\bar{f}+a\chi^{+}\|_{E_{1}^{0}}=\left(\|\bar{f}\|_{2}^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

At last, we can define the set of Eulerian coordinates,
\mathcal{D}
, in which we also seek numerical solutions.

Definition 2.1
.

The space
\mathcal{D}
consists of all triplets
(u,\mu,\nu)
such that

(i)

u\in E_{2}
,

(ii)

\mu\leq\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
,

(iii)

\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}\leq\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
,

(iv)

d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
,

(v)

\mu\left((-\infty,\cdot)\right)\in E_{1}^{0}
,

(vi)

\nu\left((-\infty,\cdot)\right)\in E_{1}^{0}
,

(vii)

If
\alpha=1
, then
d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}=d\mu=u_{x}^{2}dx
,

(viii)

If
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then
\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}(x)\in\{1-\alpha,1\}
, and
\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}}(x)=1
if
u_{x}(x)<0
.

As
\mu
,
\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
, we can define the primitive functions
F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x))
and
G(x)=\nu((-\infty,x))
. These are bounded, increasing, left-continuous and satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}F(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}G(x)=0.

We will interchangeably use the notation
(u,F,G)
and
(u,\mu,\nu)
to refer to the same triplet in
\mathcal{D}
, since by
[
7
, Thm. 1.16]
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between
(F,G)
and
(\mu,\nu)
.

Moreover, for practical purposes, we will often restrict the initial data to belong to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}:

\displaystyle=\{(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}:\mu=\nu\}\subset\mathcal{D}.

Let
B=E_{2}\times E_{2}\times E_{1}\times E_{1}
endowed with the norm

\displaystyle\|(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4})\|_{B}:=\|f_{1}\|_{E_{2}}+\|f_{2}\|_{E_{2}}+\|f_{3}\|_{E_{1}}+\|f_{4}\|_{E_{1}},

then the set of Lagrangian coordinates,
\mathcal{F}
, is defined as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

The set
\mathcal{F}
consists of all quadruplets
X=(y,U,V,H)
with
(y-\text{{id}},U,V,H)\in B
such that

(i)

(y-\text{{id}},U,V,H)\in\big[W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})\big]^{4}
,

(ii)

y_{\xi},H_{\xi}\geq 0
and there exists
c>0
such that
y_{\xi}+H_{\xi}\geq c
holds a.e.,

(iii)

y_{\xi}V_{\xi}=U_{\xi}^{2}
a.e.,

(iv)

0\leq V_{\xi}\leq H_{\xi}
a.e.,

(v)

If
\alpha=1
, then
y_{\xi}(\xi)=0
implies that
V_{\xi}(\xi)=0
, and
y_{\xi}(\xi)>0
implies that
V_{\xi}(\xi)=H_{\xi}(\xi)
a.e.,

(vi)

If
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then there exists a function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\{1-\alpha,1\}
such that
V_{\xi}(\xi)=\kappa(\xi)H_{\xi}(\xi)
a.e., and
\kappa(\xi)=1
whenever
U_{\xi}(\xi)<0
.

In the convergence analysis, the following subsets of
\mathcal{F}
will play an important role

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{0}

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathcal{F}:H(\xi)=V(\xi)\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathcal{F}:y+H=\text{{id}}\},

and

\mathcal{F}_{0}^{0}=\mathcal{F}^{0}\cap\mathcal{F}_{0}.

To construct the
\alpha
-dissipative solution using a generalized method of characteristics means to study the time evolution in Lagrangian rather than in Eulerian coordinates, and therefore the mappings between
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
are an essential part.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
L:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{0}
be defined by
L\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)=(y,U,V,H)
, where

(2.1a)

\displaystyle y(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x+\nu((-\infty,x))<\xi\},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle U(\xi)

\displaystyle=u(y(\xi)),

(2.1c)

\displaystyle H(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi-y(\xi),

\displaystyle V(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}(y(\eta))H_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta.

Next we introduce the mapping taking us from Lagrangian to Eulerian coordinates. To this end, recall that the pushforward of a Borel measure
\lambda
by a measurable function
f
is the measure
f_{\#}\lambda
defined for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}
by

(f_{\#}\lambda)(A)=\lambda(f^{-1}(A)).

Definition 2.4
.

Define
M:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
by
M((y,U,V,H))=(u,\mu,\nu)
, where

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=U(\xi)\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }x=y(\xi),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=y_{\#}\big(V_{\xi}d\xi\big),

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=y_{\#}\big(H_{\xi}d\xi\big).

For proofs that these mappings are well-defined we refer to
[
29
, Prop. 2.1.5 and 2.1.7]
. Furthermore, it should be noted that the triplets
(u,\mu,\nu)
are mapped to quadruplets
(y,U,V,H)
and hence there cannot be a one-to-one correspondence between Eulerian and Lagrangian coordinates. However, as pointed out in
[
29
]
, one can identify equivalence classes in Lagrangian coordinates, so that each equivalence class corresponds to exactly one element in Eulerian coordinates.

Moreover, it should be pointed out that all the important information in Eulerian coordinates is encoded in the pair
(u,\mu)
, and hence contained in the triplet
(y,U,V)
in Lagrangian coordinates. In contrast, the mapping
L
relies heavily on
\nu
and hence changing
\nu
changes not only
H
, but also
(y,U,V)
.

Finally, we can turn our attention to the time evolution. The
\alpha
-dissipative solution in Lagrangian coordinates,
X(t)=(y,U,V,H)(t)
, with initial data
X(0)=X_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
, is the unique solution to the following system of differential equations

(2.3a)

\displaystyle y_{t}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=U(t,\xi),

(2.3b)

\displaystyle U_{t}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}V(t,\xi)-\frac{1}{4}V_{\infty}(t),

(2.3c)

\displaystyle V(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}V_{0,\xi}(\eta)\big(1-\alpha\chi_{\{t\geq\tau(\eta)>0\}}(\eta)\big)d\eta,

(2.3d)

\displaystyle H_{t}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=0.

Here
\tau:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is the wave breaking function given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\tau(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{2y_{0,\xi}(\xi)}{U_{0,\xi}(\xi)},&\text{if }U_{0,\xi}(\xi)<0,\\
0,&\text{if }y_{0,\xi}(\xi)=U_{0,\xi}(\xi)=0,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and
V_{\infty}(t)=\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\infty}V(t,\xi)
denotes the total Lagrangian energy at time
t
.

For a proof of the uniqueness of the solution to (
2.3
), we refer to
[
13
, Lem. 2.3]
. Furthermore, it should be pointed out that the solution operator respects equivalence classes in the following sense: If
X_{A,0}
and
X_{B,0}
belong to the same equivalence class, then also
X_{A}(t)
and
X_{B}(t)
belong to the same equivalence class for all
t\geq 0
, see
[
13
, Prop. 3.7]
.

Based on (
2.3
) we now define the solution operator
S_{t}
in Lagrangian coordinates.

Definition 2.5
.

For any
t\geq 0
and
X_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
define
S_{t}(X_{0})=X(t)
, where
X(t)
denotes the unique
\alpha
-dissipative solution to (
2.3
) with initial data
X(0)=X_{0}
.

To finally obtain the
\alpha
-dissipative solution in Eulerian coordinates, we combine the solution operator
S_{t}
with the mappings
L
and
M
as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

For any
t\geq 0
and
(u_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
the
\alpha
-dissipative solution at time
t
is given by

(u,\mu,\nu)(t)=T_{t}((u_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0}))=M\circ S_{t}\circ L((u_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})).

As we mentioned earlier
L((u,\mu,\nu))
is heavily influenced by the choice of
\nu
in the triplet
(u,\mu,\nu)
. Nevertheless, it has been shown in
[
16
, Lem. 2.13]
, that the choice of
\nu
has no influence on the solution, in the following sense. Given any two triplets of initial data
(u_{A,0},\mu_{A,0},\nu_{A,0})
and
(u_{B,0},\mu_{B,0},\nu_{B,0})
in
\mathcal{D}
such that

u_{A,0}=u_{B,0}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{A,0}=\mu_{B,0},

then

u_{A}(t)=u_{B}(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{A}(t)=\mu_{B}(t)\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

As a consequence, we restrict ourselves from now on to consider only initial data
(u_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, which means in particular that
\mu_{0}=\nu_{0}
.

Furthermore, in the case of conservative and dissipative solutions the uniqueness of weak solutions has been established in
[
9
]
and
[
6
]
, respectively, by showing that if a weak solution of the Hunter–Saxton equation satisfies certain properties, which are heavily dependent on the class of solutions one is interested in, then it can be computed using Definition
2.6
. For the remaining values of
\alpha
, i.e.,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, this is yet an open question.

3.
The numerical algorithm

This section is devoted to presenting our numerical algorithm, which combines a projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
with the solution operator
T_{t}
as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

We define the numerical solution
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})
for
t\in[0,T]
by

\displaystyle(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})(t)

\displaystyle=T_{t}\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u_{0},F_{0},G_{0})\right)=M\circ S_{t}\circ L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u_{0},F_{0},G_{0})\right),

for any
(u_{0},F_{0},G_{0})\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

As the projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
is the only part that actually introduces any error, its construction is the crucial step. In particular,
P_{\Delta x}
must preserve key properties of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions such as the total energy
\mu(\mathbb{R})
and Definition
2.1

(iv)
. Beside the construction of
P_{\Delta x}
, we will, at the end of this section, discuss the implementation of our algorithm.

3.1.
The projection operator

Any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
can be split into an absolutely continuous part
\mu_{\text{ac}}
, and a singular part
\mu_{\text{sing}}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure, see
[
27
, Prop. 9.8]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle d\mu

\displaystyle=d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}+d\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}.

Thus
F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x))
can be written as

(3.1)

F(x)=F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)+F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x),

where
F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)=\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}((-\infty,x))
and
F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x)=\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}((-\infty,x))
. Thus a projection operator acting on
F(x)
can be a combination of two projections, one for
F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)
and another one for
F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x)
. As we will see, this is how we define
P_{\Delta x}
.

To derive
P_{\Delta x}
, let
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a uniform discretization of
\mathbb{R}
, where
x_{j}=j\Delta x
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\Delta x>0
is fixed. Furthermore, set
P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)\right)=(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})
. To ensure that
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})
satisfies Definition
2.1

(iv)
, the projection operator is defined over two grid cells, i.e., over
[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]\cup[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
. Furthermore, to preserve the continuity of
(u,F_{\mathrm{ac}})
and the total energy, we require that
\left(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x,\text{ac}}\right)
coincides with
(u,F_{\mathrm{ac}})
in every other gridpoint, i.e.,

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x_{2j})

\displaystyle=u(x_{2j}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})

\displaystyle=F_{\text{ac}}(x_{2j}).

To interpolate
u_{\Delta x}
and
F_{\Delta x}
between the gridpoints
\{x_{2j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we fit two lines,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
,
such that the resulting wave profile

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}p_{1}(x),&x_{2j}\leq x<x_{2j+1},\\
p_{2}(x),&x_{2j+1}\leq x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

is continuous,
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}})
satisfies (
3.2
), and

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}(y)dy.

For an arbitrary
j\in\mathbb{Z}
these constraints then read

(3.3a)

\displaystyle p_{1}(x_{2j})

\displaystyle=u(x_{2j}),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle p_{2}(x_{2j+2})

\displaystyle=u(x_{2j+2}),

(3.3c)

\displaystyle p_{1}(x_{2j+1})

\displaystyle=p_{2}(x_{2j+1}),

(3.3d)

\displaystyle\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+1}}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}(y)\right)^{2}dy+\int_{x_{2j+1}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(p_{2}^{\prime}(y)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j}).

Introducing the operators

\displaystyle D_{+}f_{j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{f_{j+1}-f_{j}}{\Delta x},

\displaystyle Df_{2j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{D_{+}f_{2j+1}+D_{+}f_{2j}}{2}=\frac{f_{2j+2}-f_{2j}}{2\Delta x},

for any sequence
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

and solving
(
3.3a
)–(
3.3d
), we end up with

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u(x_{2j})+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j}),&x_{2j}\leq x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
u(x_{2j+2})+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j+2}),&x_{2j+1}\leq x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)^{2}\left(x-x_{2j}\right),&x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
\frac{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})+F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}{2}\mp 2Du_{2j}q_{2j}\Delta x\\
\quad+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)^{2}(x-x_{2j+1}),&x_{2j+1}<x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

where

(3.4)

\displaystyle q_{2j}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-\left(Du_{2j}\right)^{2}}.

Note that (
3.4
) is well-defined, as we have by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-\left(Du_{2j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz-\left(\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}(z)dz\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz-\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz=0.

For the singular part, we set

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle:=F(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})=F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x_{2j+2}),\quad\text{ for }x\in(x_{2j},x_{2j+2}],

where the last equality follows from (
3.1
). Hence, all the discontinuities of
F_{\Delta x,\text{sing}}(x)
are located within the countable set
\{x_{2j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Finally, introduce

F_{\Delta x}(x)=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)+F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x),

which is left-continuous. Then we can associate to
F_{\Delta x}
a positive and finite Radon measure
\mu_{\Delta x}
through

\mu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=F_{\Delta x}(x),

and, by construction,

\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}((-\infty,x))=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x),\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}((-\infty,x))=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x).

To summarize, the projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
is defined as follows.

Definition 3.2

(Projection operator)
.

We define the projection operator
P_{\Delta x}:\mathcal{D}_{0}\to\mathcal{D}_{0}
by
P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)\right)=(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})
, where

(3.5)

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u(x_{2j})+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j}),&x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
u(x_{2j+2})+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j+2}),&x_{2j+1}<x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=F_{\Delta x}(x)=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)+F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x),

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The absolutely continuous part of
F_{\Delta x}
is given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)^{2}\left(x-x_{2j}\right),&x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
\frac{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})+F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}{2}\mp 2q_{2j}Du_{2j}\Delta x\\
\quad+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)^{2}(x-x_{2j+1}),&x_{2j+1}<x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

and the singular part is defined by

(3.7)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle=F(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})=F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x_{2j+2}),\qquad x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+2}.

Finally, let
\mu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=F_{\Delta x}(x)
and
\nu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=G_{\Delta x}(x)
be the unique, finite and positive Radon measures associated with
F_{\Delta x}
and
G_{\Delta x}
, respectively.

Remark 3.3
.

The projection operator
P_{\Delta x}
in Definition
3.2
is only defined for
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
due to Definition
3.1
. In this case
\mu=\nu
and by construction
\mu_{\Delta x}=\nu_{\Delta x}
. Nevertheless,
P_{\Delta x}
can be extended to a projection operator
\tilde{P}_{\Delta x}
, which is well-defined for any
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}
as follows.
We keep
u_{\Delta x}
and
\mu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=F_{\Delta x}(x)
given by (
3.5
)–(
3.7
), but
\nu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=G_{\Delta x}(x)
needs to be adjusted:

Following the same idea as for
F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}
, we set

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle=G(x_{2j+2})-G_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})=G_{\mathrm{sing}}(x_{2j+2}),\quad\text{ for }x\in(x_{2j},x_{2j+2}],

Since we now have
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
and
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}\leq\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
, which implies that
d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}=fdx
for some
f\geq u_{x}^{2}
, we approximate the deviation
(f-u_{x}^{2})
with the integral average over two grid cells. Following these lines, we end up with

\displaystyle d\nu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(f(y)-u_{x}^{2}(y)\right)dy\right)dx

\displaystyle=\left(u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}+\left(DG_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}\right)\right)dx,

for
x\in(x_{2j},x_{2j+1})\cup(x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2})
. Computing
G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)=\nu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}((-\infty,x))
, yields
G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})=G_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})
, and

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})+F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})+\left(DG_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}\right)(x-x_{2j}),

for
x\in(x_{2j},x_{2j+2}]
. Finally, we define
\nu_{\Delta x}
implicitly by

\nu_{\Delta x}((-\infty,x))=G_{\Delta x}(x)=G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)+G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x).

Last but not least, note that there is one drawback with
\tilde{P}_{\Delta x}
. It preserves all properties in Definition
2.1
when
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
. However, in the case
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}\neq d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
, the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\mu_{\Delta x}}{d\nu_{\Delta x}}
belongs to the interval
[1-\alpha,1]
rather than the set
\{1-\alpha,1\}
, and hence the relation
\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}}(x)=1
whenever
u_{x}(x)<0
in Definition
2.1

(vi)
will not be obeyed in general. Therefore,
\tilde{P}_{\Delta x}
maps into a larger space
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\supset\mathcal{D}
. Nevertheless,
\tilde{P}_{\Delta x}
is relevant for numerical algorithms for nonconservative solutions (
\alpha\not=0
), which in a similar spirit to the one in
[
14
]
, are based on applying the projection operator after each time step
\Delta t
.

A closer look at (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) reveals that there are two possible sign choices on each interval
[x_{2j},x_{2j+2}]
. Especially on a coarse grid, it is vital to make the right choice, as the following example shows.

Example 3.4
.

Consider the tuple
(u,F,G)
, where

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0,\\
x,&0<x\leq 1,\\
2-x,&1<x\leq 2,\\
0,&2<x,\end{cases}

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0,\\
x+1-\alpha,&0<x\leq 2,\\
3-\alpha,&2<x,\end{cases}

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0,\\
x+1,&0<x\leq 2,\\
3,&2<x.\end{cases}

This models a scenario where wave breaking takes place at
x=0
.

We discretize the domain
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{5}{2}]
with
\Delta x=\frac{1}{2}
, but for convenience we use
x_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}+j\Delta x
as our gridpoints. Using (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) we find

(3.8)

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(1\mp 1\right)(x+\frac{1}{2}),&-\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm 1\right)(x-\frac{1}{2}),&0<x\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
\frac{1}{2}\mp(x-\frac{1}{2}),&\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}\pm(x-\frac{3}{2}),&1<x\leq\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-1\mp 1\right)(x-\frac{3}{2}),&\frac{3}{2}<x\leq 2,\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(-1\pm 1\right)(x-\frac{5}{2}),&2<x\leq\frac{5}{2},\end{cases}

(3.9)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}\left(1\mp 1\right)^{2}(x+\frac{1}{2}),&-\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{4}\mp\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\left(1\pm 1\right)^{2}x,&0<x\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
x,&\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 1,\\
x,&1<x\leq\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(-1\mp 1\right)^{2}(x-\frac{3}{2}),&\frac{3}{2}<x\leq 2,\\
\frac{7}{4}\pm\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\left(-1\pm 1\right)^{2}(x-2),&2<x\leq\frac{5}{2}.\end{cases}

Figure 1
.
A comparison of the tuple
(u,F,G)
from Example
3.4
with the projected data
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})
for different sign-choices for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
on a grid with
\Delta x=\frac{1}{2}
(
x
on the horizontal axis).

There are several possible ways to choose the signs in (
3.8
)–(
3.9
) and some of those are shown in Figure
1
:
u_{\Delta x,1}
is based on using minus sign over
[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
and plus over
[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
, while
u_{\Delta x,2}
is based on using plus over
[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
and minus over
[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
. By carefully choosing the signs,
u_{\Delta x}
fully overlaps with the exact solution. This choice which we denote as
u_{\Delta x,\text{opt}}
, is given by

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x,\mathrm{opt}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&-\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 0,\\
x,&0<x\leq 1,\\
2-x,&1<x\leq 2,\\
0,&2<x\leq\frac{5}{2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac},\mathrm{opt}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&-\frac{1}{2}<x\leq 0,\\
x,&0<x\leq 2,\\
2,&2<x\leq\frac{5}{2},\end{cases}

and equals
(u,F_{\mathrm{ac}})
. The singular parts are approximated by

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
1-\alpha,&-\frac{1}{2}<x,\end{cases}

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
1,&-\frac{1}{2}<x.\end{cases}

As the above example shows, choosing the best sign on each of the two grid cells in
[x_{2j},x_{2j+2}]
is vital, since it significantly affects the accuracy in the projection step and hence the whole algorithm, especially for a coarse grid. Instead of trial and error, which is never a good choice except for illustrative purposes, usually a selection criterion is imposed. In the implementation we decided to choose the sign over

[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
that minimizes the distance between
u_{\Delta x}(x_{2j+1})
and
u(x_{2j+1})
, which can be formalized as follows. Introduce
m\in\{0,1\}
such that for
x\in[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
we may write (
3.5
) as

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=u(x_{2j})+\left(Du_{2j}+\left(-1\right)^{m}q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j}).

Subtracting
u_{\Delta x}(x_{2j+1})
from
u(x_{2j+1})
, and finding the
m
that minimizes the distance can then be expressed as

\displaystyle k_{2j}:

\displaystyle=\argmin_{m\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\left|\left(D_{+}u_{2j}-Du_{2j}\right)\Delta x+\left(-1\right)^{m+1}q_{2j}\Delta x\right|\right\}.

Note that the sign over
[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
will be
(-1)^{k_{2j}+1}
.

3.2.
Numerical implementation of
T_{t}

Since
T_{t}
associates to each piecewise linear initial data, the corresponding solution at time
t
, which again is piecewise linear, our numerical implementation of
T_{t}
will yield the exact solution with the projected initial data.

Fix a discretization parameter
\Delta x>0
and an initial datum
(u,F,G)(0)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
.
Following Definition
3.1
, the numerical Lagrangian initial data is given by

(3.10)

\displaystyle X_{\Delta x}(0)

\displaystyle=(y_{\Delta x},U_{\Delta x},V_{\Delta x},H_{\Delta x})(0)=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)(0)\right),

and we therefore first focus on the numerical implementation of
L
. We will observe that each component of
X_{\Delta x}(0)
is again a piecewise linear function. Moreover, the associated Lagrangian grid is non-uniform and has possibly a larger number of gridpoints than the original Eulerian grid.

3.2.1.
Implementation of
L

To avoid any ambiguity between breakpoints of a function and points of wave breaking, we denote the former as nodes in the following.

By construction,
x+G_{\Delta x}(0,x)
is an increasing, piecewise linear function with nodes situated at the points
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and hence
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)
is again an increasing and piecewise linear function due to Definition
2.3
. Furthermore,
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)
is continuous and its nodes can be identified by finding all
\xi
which satisfy
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)=x_{j}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
as follows. By Definition
2.3

\displaystyle y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+G_{\Delta x}(0,y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi))

\displaystyle\leq y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+H_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)

\displaystyle\leq y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+G_{\Delta x}(0,y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+),

and, due to
X_{\Delta x}(0)\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
, we have

(3.11)

y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+G_{\Delta x}(0,y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi))\leq\xi\leq y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+G_{\Delta x}(0,y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)+).

Since
G_{\Delta x}(0,x)
is continuous except possibly at the points
\{x_{2j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
to every
x_{2j+1}
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, there exists a unique
\xi_{2j+1}
such that
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi_{2j+1})=x_{2j+1}
and, using (
3.11
),
\xi_{2j+1}
is given by

x_{2j+1}+G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j+1})=\xi_{2j+1}.

At the points
\{x_{2j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, the function
G_{\Delta x}(0,x)
might have a jump. Therefore, there exists a maximal interval
I_{2j}=[\xi_{2j}^{l},\xi_{2j}^{r}]
such that
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)=x_{2j}
for all
\xi\in I_{2j}
, and using once more (
3.11
),
\xi_{2j}^{l}
and
\xi_{2j}^{r}
are given by

(3.12)

x_{2j}+G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j})=\xi_{2j}^{l}\leq\xi_{2j}^{r}=x_{2j}+G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j}+).

Note that
\xi_{2j}^{l}=\xi_{2j}^{r}
if and only if
G_{\Delta x}(0,x)
has no jump at
x=x_{2j}
.

Set

(3.13)

\hat{\xi}_{3j}=\xi_{2j}^{l},\quad\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}=\xi_{2j}^{r},\quad\text{ and }\lx@nobreakspace\quad\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}=\xi_{2j+1}\quad\text{ for }j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then the nodes of
y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)
are situated at the points
\{\hat{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, as shown in Figure
2
. As mentioned earlier
y_{\Delta x}(0)
is piecewise linear and continuous, and hence also
U_{\Delta x}(0)
,
V_{\Delta x}(0)
, and
H_{\Delta x}(0)
are piecewise linear and continuous. Moreover, the nodes of
X_{\Delta x}(0)
are located at
\{\hat{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, since the nodes of
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})(0)
are situated at
x=x_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, once the nodes are identified and the values of
X_{\Delta x}(0)
at these nodes are determined using Definition
2.3
, the value of
X_{\Delta x}(0,\xi)
can be computed at any point
\xi
.

Furthermore, each two grid cells
[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}],[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
are mapped to 3 grid cells
[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}],[\hat{\xi}_{3j+1},\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}],[\hat{\xi}_{3j+2},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3}]
by
L
.
In addition, while the Eulerian discretization is uniform with size

\Delta x
, we now have

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}-\hat{\xi}_{3j}|

\displaystyle=|\xi_{2j}^{r}-\xi_{2j}^{l}|=G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j}+)-G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j}),

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}-\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}|

\displaystyle=|\xi_{2j+1}-\xi_{2j}^{r}|=\Delta x+G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j+1})-G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j}+),

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}_{3j+3}-\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}|

\displaystyle=|\xi_{2j+2}-\xi_{2j+1}|=\Delta x+G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j+2})-G_{\Delta x}(0,x_{2j+1}),

and hence the Lagrangian discretization is non-uniform.

3.2.2.
The wave breaking function

The next step is to compute the numerical wave breaking function
\tau_{\Delta x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, using (
2.4
). For
\xi\in[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}]
, we have
y_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)=U_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)=0
and hence
\tau_{\Delta x}(\xi)=0
by (
2.4
). For
\xi\in(\hat{\xi}_{3j+1},\hat{\xi}_{3j+2})
, note that
U_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)
has the same sign as
u_{\Delta x,x}(0,x)=Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}
in
(x_{2j},x_{2j+1})
. Likewise, for
\xi\in(\hat{\xi}_{3j+2},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})
,
U_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)
has the same sign as
u_{\Delta x,x}(0,x)=Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}
in
(x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2})
. Furthermore,
U_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)=u_{\Delta x,x}(0,y_{\Delta x}(0,\xi))y_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)
for all
\xi\in(\hat{\xi}_{3j+1},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})\backslash\{\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}\}
. Therefore, introducing
\tau_{3j+\frac{3}{2}}
and
\tau_{3j+\frac{5}{2}}
as

\displaystyle\tau_{3j+\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{2}{Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}},&\text{if }Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}<0,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tau_{3j+\frac{5}{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{2}{Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}},&\text{if }Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}<0,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

the wave breaking function
\tau_{\Delta x}
for
\xi\in[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})
is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\Delta x}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\xi\in[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}],\\
\tau_{3j+\frac{3}{2}},&\xi\in(\hat{\xi}_{3j+1},\hat{\xi}_{3j+2}],\\
\tau_{3j+\frac{5}{2}},&\xi\in(\hat{\xi}_{3j+2},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3}).\end{cases}

Figure
3
illustrates the relation between the slopes of
U_{{\Delta x}}(0)
and the value attained by
\tau_{{\Delta x}}
. Note that an interval where
U_{{\Delta x}}(0)
is strictly increasing leads to an interval where
\tau_{{\Delta x}}
is unbounded.

.

3.2.3.
Implementation of
S_{t}

We proceed by considering
X_{\Delta x}(t)=S_{t}(X_{\Delta x}(0))
. Introduce the function

\zeta_{\Delta x}=y_{\Delta x}-\text{{id}},

which we prefer to work with as
\zeta_{\Delta x}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in contrast to
y_{\Delta x}
, see Definition
2.2
. Furthermore, let

\hat{X}_{\Delta x}=(\zeta_{\Delta x},U_{\Delta x},V_{\Delta x},H_{\Delta x}).

It then turns out to be advantageous to compute the time evolution of
\hat{X}_{\Delta x,\xi}
rather than the one of
\hat{X}_{\Delta x}
,
due to possible drops in
V_{\Delta x,\xi}
, cf. (
2.3c
). However, this forces us to slightly change our point of view, since by differentiating (
3.10
) we find that
\hat{X}_{\Delta x,\xi}(0)
is a piecewise constant function, whose discontinuities are situated at the nodes
\hat{\xi}_{j}
, where
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, we associate to each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\hat{X}_{j,\xi}(0)=\begin{cases}\hat{X}_{\Delta x,\xi}\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{j}+\hat{\xi}_{j+1})\right),&\text{ if }\hat{\xi}_{j}\not=\hat{\xi}_{j+1},\\
(0,0,0,0),&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This sequence is evolved numerically according to,

\displaystyle\zeta_{j,t\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{j,\xi}(t),

\displaystyle U_{j,t\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}V_{j,\xi}(t),

\displaystyle V_{j,\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\alpha\chi_{\{t\geq\tau_{\Delta x}(\xi)>0\}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{j}+\hat{\xi}_{j+1})\right)\right)V_{j,\xi}(0),

(3.14)

\displaystyle H_{j,t\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=0.

The above system is obtained by differentiating (
2.3
) with respect to
\xi
. Since computing
\hat{X}_{\Delta x,\xi}(t,\cdot)
exactly, yields a piecewise constant function whose discontinuities are again located at the nodes
\{\hat{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, considering the above sequence
\{\hat{X}_{j,\xi}(t)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
does not yield any additional error. Furthermore, the exact
\hat{X}_{\Delta x,\xi}(t,\cdot)
can be read off from
\{\hat{X}_{j,\xi}(t)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Finally, we can exactly recover
\hat{X}_{\Delta x}(t)
, which is continuous with respect to
\xi
, from
\{\hat{X}_{j,\xi}(t)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Since the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{X}_{\Delta x}(t,\xi)
as
\xi\to\pm\infty
changes in accordance with (
2.3
), this must also be taken into account by our algorithm. The fact that the initial data is in the space
\mathcal{D}
combined with (
2.3c
) implies

V_{\Delta x,-\infty}(t)=0\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

where the abbreviation

f_{\pm\infty}(t)=\lim_{\xi\to\pm\infty}f(t,\xi),

has been introduced to ease the notation. Hence, for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{j-1}V_{k,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{k+1}-\hat{\xi}_{k}\right)=V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j-1})+V_{j-1,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{j}-\hat{\xi}_{j-1}\right).

From (
2.3d
) it follows that

H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})=H_{\Delta x}(0,\hat{\xi}_{j}),\quad\text{ for all }\lx@nobreakspace t\geq 0.

For
U_{\Delta x}(t,\xi)
, we have, combining (
2.3b
) and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(0)-\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\Delta x,\infty}(s)ds

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(0)-\frac{1}{4}H_{\Delta x,\infty}(0)t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{4}\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(0,\xi)\int_{\tau_{\Delta x}(\xi)}^{t}\chi_{\{s\geq\tau_{\Delta x}(\xi)>0\}}(\xi)dsd\xi

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(0)-\frac{1}{4}H_{\Delta x,\infty}(0)t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{4}\alpha\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{k,\xi}(0)\left(\hat{\xi}_{k+1}-\hat{\xi}_{k}\right)\left(t-\tau_{\Delta x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{k+1})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\chi_{\left\{s\geq\tau_{\Delta x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{k+1})\right)\right\}}(t)\chi_{\left\{j:\tau_{\Delta x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{j}+\hat{\xi}_{j+1})\right)>0\right\}}(k),

and for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle U_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(t)+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{j-1}U_{k,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{k+1}-\hat{\xi}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j-1})+U_{j-1,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{j}-\hat{\xi}_{j-1}\right).

For
\zeta_{\Delta x}(t,\xi)
, we find, using (
2.3a
) and
\zeta_{\Delta x,-\infty}(0)=0
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\Delta x,-\infty}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(s)ds

\displaystyle=U_{\Delta x,-\infty}(0)t-\frac{1}{8}\alpha H_{\Delta x,\infty}(0)t^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{8}\alpha\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{k,\xi}(0)\left(\hat{\xi}_{k+1}-\hat{\xi}_{k}\right)\left(t-\tau_{{\Delta x}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{k+1})\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\chi_{\left\{s\geq\tau_{\Delta x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{k+1})\right)\right\}}(t)\chi_{\left\{j:\tau_{\Delta x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\xi}_{j}+\hat{\xi}_{j+1})\right)>0\right\}}(k),

and for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\Delta x,-\infty}(t)+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{j-1}\zeta_{k,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{k+1}-\hat{\xi}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j-1})+\zeta_{j-1,\xi}(t)\left(\hat{\xi}_{j}-\hat{\xi}_{j-1}\right).

Recalling that
\hat{X}_{\Delta x}(t)
is piecewise linear and continuous, with nodes situated at
\hat{\xi}_{j}
with
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we can now recover
\hat{X}_{\Delta x}(t,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, and, using that
y_{\Delta x}(t)=\zeta_{\Delta x}(t)+\text{{id}}
, we end up with
X_{\Delta x}(t,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.

In practice, we have to limit the numerical approximation of the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) to initial data where
u_{x}(0)
and
\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}(0)
have compact support. For such initial data there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\zeta_{j,\xi}(0)=U_{j,\xi}(0)=V_{j,\xi}(0)=H_{j,\xi}(0)=0\quad\text{for all }|j|\geq N,

and by (
3.14
) we have

\zeta_{j,\xi}(t)=U_{j,\xi}(t)=V_{j,\xi}(t)=H_{j,\xi}(t)=0\quad\text{for all }|j|\geq N\text{ and }t\geq 0.

3.2.4.
Implementation of
M

Finally, to recover the solution in Eulerian coordinates we apply the mapping
M
to
X_{\Delta x}(t)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(u_{\Delta x},\mu_{\Delta x},\nu_{\Delta x}\right)(t)

\displaystyle=M\left(X_{\Delta x}(t)\right).

Here it is important to note that
X_{\Delta x}(t)
is piecewise linear and continuous, thus
u_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)
is also piecewise linear and continuous, while
F_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)
and
G_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)
are piecewise linear, increasing and in general only left-continuous. Furthermore, their nodes are situated at the points
\{y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Numerically, we therefore apply a piecewise linear reconstruction.

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x\in(y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j}),y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})]
.

If
x=y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})
, we have, by the definition of
M
,

u_{\Delta x}(t,x)=U_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}),\hskip 4.55254ptF_{\Delta x}(t,x)=V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}),\hskip 4.55254pt\text{and}\hskip 4.55254ptG_{\Delta x}(t,x)=H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}).

This covers also the case where
\hat{\xi}_{j}=\hat{\xi}_{j+1}
, and the case where wave breaking occurs, i.e.,
y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})=y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})
.

If
x\in(y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j}),y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}))
, observe that

F_{\Delta x}(t,y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})+)=V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j}),\quad\text{ and }\quad V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})=F_{\Delta x}(t,y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})),

and

G_{\Delta x}(t,y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})+)=H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j}),\quad\text{ and }\quad H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})=G_{\Delta x}(t,y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})).

Therefore, for
x\in(y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j}),y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}))
the linear interpolation, which coincides with
M\left(X_{\Delta x}(t)\right)
, is given by

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})-x}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}U_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})+\frac{x-y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}U_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}),

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})-x}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})+\frac{x-y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}V_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}),

(3.15)

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})-x}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})+\frac{x-y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}{D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})}H_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1}),

where
D_{+}^{\xi}y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})=y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j+1})-y_{\Delta x}(t,\hat{\xi}_{j})
. Finally, note that the Eulerian grid changes significantly with respect to time. First of all the number of grid cells is changing over time and secondly, the grid does not remain uniform.

4.
Convergence of the numerical method

In this section we prove that our family of approximations
\{\left(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x}\right)\}_{\Delta x>0}
converges to the
\alpha
-dissipative solution of (
1.1
). We start by proving convergence of the projected initial data in Eulerian coordinates. Thereafter, we show that this induces convergence, initially and at later times, towards the unique
\alpha
-dissipative solution in Lagrangian coordinates. Finally, we investigate in what sense convergence in Lagrangian coordinates carries over to Eulerian coordinates.

4.1.
Convergence of the initial data in Eulerian coordinates

Let
P_{\Delta x}
be the projection operator given by Definition
3.2
. We have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, let
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})=P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)\right)
. Then

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\Delta x,

(4.1c)

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\Delta x}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2F_{\infty}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad\text{ for }p=1,2,

(4.1d)

\displaystyle\|G-G_{\Delta x}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2G_{\infty}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad\text{ for }p=1,2,

(4.1e)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle\rightarrow F(x)\text{ for every }x\text{ at which }F\text{ is continuous},

(4.1f)

\displaystyle G_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle\rightarrow G(x)\text{ for every }x\text{ at which }G\text{ is continuous}.

Proof.

Let
x\in[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
. By (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\left|u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{x_{2j+2}-x}{2\Delta x}\left(u(x)-u(x_{2j})\right)+\frac{x-x_{2j}}{2\Delta x}\left(u(x)-u(x_{2j+2})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+q_{2j}(x-x_{2j}),

where
q_{2j}
is defined by (
3.4
). The second term is bounded by

(4.2)

\displaystyle 0\leq q_{2j}(x-x_{2j})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}}\Delta x\leq\sqrt{\frac{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}{2}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The first term can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\biggl|\frac{x_{2j+2}-x}{2\Delta x}

\displaystyle\left(u(x)-u(x_{2j})\right)+\frac{x-x_{2j}}{2\Delta x}\left(u(x)-u(x_{2j+2})\right)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x_{2j}}^{x}\left|u_{x}(z)\right|dz+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|u_{x}(z)\right|dz

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{x-x_{2j}}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{x_{2j+2}-x}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{\frac{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}{2}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}},

where we applied the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Combining (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) yields

(4.4)

\left|u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)\right|\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By a similar argument for
x\in[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
, (
4.1a
) is established.

Using (
4.4
) we get

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+1}}\left(u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)\right)^{2}dx+\int_{x_{2j+1}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)\right)^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}\left(F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x_{2j})\right)\Delta x^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}\Delta x^{2},

which yields (
4.1b
).

Next, we show (
4.1c
). For the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
-estimate, we have

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\Delta x}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}|F(x)-F_{\Delta x}(x)|dx\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(F(x_{2j+2})-F(x_{2j})\right)dx

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(F(x_{2j+2})-F(x_{2j})\right)\Delta x=2F_{\infty}\Delta x,

and for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-estimate,

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\Delta x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(F(x_{2j+2})-F(x_{2j})\right)^{2}\Delta x

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{\infty}\left(F(x_{2j+2})-F(x_{2j})\right)\Delta x=2F_{\infty}^{2}\Delta x.

To prove (
4.1e
), it suffices to show that
\mu_{\Delta x}\rightarrow\mu
vaguely as
\Delta x\to 0
, see
[
7
, Prop. 7.19]
. That is,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)d\mu_{\Delta x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)d\mu,

for all
\phi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, where
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity. Furthermore, since
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, cf.
[
7
, Prop 8.17]
, it suffices to show that (
4.5
) holds for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Combining integration by parts and (
4.1c
) with
p=1
, we find

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)d\mu_{\Delta x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)d\mu\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(x)F_{\Delta x}dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(x)Fdx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|F_{\Delta x}-F\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2F_{\infty}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\Delta x,

and consequently
\mu_{\Delta x}\lx@nobreakspace\to\lx@nobreakspace\mu
vaguely as
\Delta x\to 0
.

Since
F=G
and
F_{\Delta x}=G_{\Delta x}
by assumption, (
4.1d
) and (
4.1f
) hold.
∎

Next we establish convergence of the spatial derivative
u_{\Delta x,x}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(u,F,G)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})=P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)\right)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

We apply the Radon–Riesz theorem. Thus, we have to show that
\|u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}\rightarrow\|u_{x}\|_{2}
and
u_{\Delta x,x}\rightharpoonup u_{x}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. A direct calculation, using (
3.5
), yields

\displaystyle\|u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg(\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+1}}\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)^{2}dy+\int_{x_{2j+1}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)^{2}dy\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}dy=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(y)dy=\|u_{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

For the weak convergence, it suffices to consider test functions
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, as
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Integration by parts combined with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
4.1b
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(y)\big(u_{x}(y)-u_{\Delta x,x}(y)\big)dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(y)\left(u(y)-u_{\Delta x}(y)\right)dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{2}\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{2}\Delta x.

Thus
u_{\Delta x,x}\rightharpoonup u_{x}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as
\Delta x\to 0
.
∎

The auxiliary function
f(Z)
will be an essential part in the upcoming convergence analysis, when we want to relate convergence in Eulerian coordinates to convergence in Lagrangian coordinates. It is defined as

(4.6)

\displaystyle f(Z)(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)u_{x}^{2}(x),&x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z),\\
u_{x}^{2}(x),&x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z),\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u_{x}(x)<0\},

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u_{x}(x)\geq 0\},

and

Z:=(\text{{id}},u,1,u_{x},\mu_{\mathrm{ac}},\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}).

Recalling that
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
by Definition
2.1

(iv)
, and
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
for
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, we establish the following
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
-estimate.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(u,F,G)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x},G_{\Delta x})=P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F,G)\right)
. Then
f(Z)
,
f(Z_{\Delta x})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, and

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|f(Z)-f(Z_{\Delta x})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 8\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}.

Proof.

We only show that
f(Z)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, since the argument for
f(Z_{\Delta x})
is exactly the same. By construction
f(Z)(x)\leq u_{x}^{2}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and, since
u_{x}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows that

(4.8)

\|f(Z)\|_{1}\leq\|u_{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty},

and hence
f(Z)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

For (
4.7
), we use a splitting based on
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)
and
\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(X)
.
If
x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})
, then

\displaystyle\left|f(Z)(x)-f(Z_{\Delta x})(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left(1-\alpha\right)\left|(u_{x}+u_{\Delta x,x})(u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x})(x)\right|,

and, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
4.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})}\left|f(Z)(x)-f(Z_{\Delta x})(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\|u_{x}+u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}.

We can proceed similarly for
x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z_{\Delta x})
.

Finally, consider
x\in\left(\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})\right)\cup\left(\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z_{\Delta x})\right)
. By symmetry, it is sufficient to consider
x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})
, for which

\displaystyle\left|f(Z)(x)-f(Z_{\Delta x})(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\left|(u_{x}^{2}-u_{\Delta x,x}^{2})(x)\right|+\alpha u_{x}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\left|(u_{x}^{2}-u_{\Delta x,x}^{2})(x)\right|+\alpha u_{x}(x)\left(u_{x}(x)-u_{\Delta x,x}(x)\right),

since
u_{x}(x)\geq 0
and
u_{\Delta x,x}(x)<0
. Therefore, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

(4.10)

\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})}|f(Z)(x)-f(Z_{\Delta x})(x)|dx\leq 2\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{1,x}-u_{2,x}\|_{2}.

Combining (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) with analogous estimates for the other cases yields (
4.7
).
∎

4.2.
Convergence in Lagrangian coordinates

We start by showing convergence of the initial data in Lagrangian coordinates.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, let
X=\left(y,U,V,H\right)=L\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
and
X_{\Delta x}=(y_{\Delta x},U_{\Delta x},V_{\Delta x},H_{\Delta x})=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
, then

(4.11a)

\displaystyle\|y-y_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta x,

(4.11b)

\displaystyle\|U-U_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}},

(4.11c)

\displaystyle\|H-H_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta x.

Proof.

Given
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\xi\in[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})
. Moreover, by construction,
y
and
y_{\Delta x}
are continuous, increasing and satisfy

(4.12)

y(\hat{\xi}_{3j})=x_{2j}=y_{\Delta x}(\hat{\xi}_{3j})\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z},

as discussed in Section
3.2
. Thus

\left|y(\xi)-y_{\Delta x}(\xi)\right|\leq\left|y(\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})-y(\hat{\xi}_{3j})\right|=x_{2j+2}-x_{2j}=2\Delta x

and, as
X
and
X_{\Delta x}
belong to
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, we have shown both (
4.11a
) and (
4.11c
).

To prove (
4.11b
), note that by (
2.1a
), to any
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exist characteristic variables
\xi_{\Delta x}
and
\xi
, such that
y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x})=x=y(\xi)
, and hence

\displaystyle u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=u(y(\xi))-u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x}))

(4.13)

\displaystyle=u(y(\xi))-u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi))+u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi))-u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x})).

By (
2.1b
), we have,
u(y(\xi))=U(\xi)
and
u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi))=U_{\Delta x}(\xi)
. Therefore, by rearranging (
4.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|U(\xi)-U_{\Delta x}(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|u(x)-u_{\Delta x}(x)\right|+\left|u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x}))-u_{\Delta x}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi))\right|.

The first term is bounded by (
4.1a
), while for the second term we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, yielding

\displaystyle\left|u_{\Delta x}\left(y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x})\right)-u_{\Delta x}\left(y_{\Delta x}(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{y_{\Delta x}(\xi)}^{y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x})}u_{\Delta x,x}(z)dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}\sqrt{\left|y_{\Delta x}(\xi_{\Delta x})-y_{\Delta x}(\xi)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\sqrt{\left|y(\xi)-y_{\Delta x}(\xi)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\sqrt{\|y-y_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|U-U_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\sqrt{\|y-y_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2F_{\text{ac},\infty}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\Delta x^{\frac{1}{2}}.\qed

For the initial derivatives we have the following convergence result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Given
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, let
X=(y,U,V,H)=L\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
and
X_{\Delta x}=\left(y_{\Delta x},U_{\Delta x},V_{\Delta x},H_{\Delta x}\right)=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
, then as
{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0
,

(4.14a)

\displaystyle y_{\Delta x,\xi}

\displaystyle\rightarrow y_{\xi}\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.14b)

\displaystyle U_{\Delta x,\xi}

\displaystyle\rightarrow U_{\xi}\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.14c)

\displaystyle H_{\Delta x,\xi}

\displaystyle\rightarrow H_{\xi}\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

For a proof of
H_{\Delta x,\xi}\rightarrow H_{\xi}
and
U_{\Delta x,\xi}\rightarrow U_{\xi}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
we refer to
[
8
, Sec. 5]
.
As
X\in\mathcal{F}_{0}^{0}
and, in particular,
U_{\xi}^{2}=y_{\xi}H_{\xi}
, we have

(4.15)

H_{\xi}^{2}=H_{\xi}-U_{\xi}^{2}.

Since (
4.15
) also holds for
X_{\Delta x}
, we obtain, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}-H_{\xi}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}-H_{\xi}^{2}\|_{1}+\|U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}-U_{\xi}^{2}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}+\|H_{\xi}\|_{2}\right)\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}-H_{\xi}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\|U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}+\|U_{\xi}\|_{2}\right)\|U_{\Delta x,\xi}-U_{\xi}\|_{2},

and
H_{\Delta x,\xi}\to H_{\xi}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Lastly, (
4.14a
) follows from (
4.14c
), as
X_{\Delta x},X\in\mathcal{F}_{0}^{0}
.
∎

We proceed by introducing the stability function
g(X)
, which is the key element when showing convergence at later times in Lagrangian coordinates. In particular, it describes the loss of energy at wave breaking in a continuous way, in contrast to the actual energy density
V_{\xi}
, which drops abruptly at wave breaking.

Let

\mathbb{R}=\Omega_{d}(X)\cup\Omega_{c}(X),

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{d}\left(X\right):

\displaystyle=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:U_{\xi}(\xi)<0\right\},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\Omega_{c}(X):

\displaystyle=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:U_{\xi}(\xi)\geq 0\}.

Note that for each
\xi\in\Omega_{c}
no wave breaking occurs in the future and hence
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)
is continuous forward in time, while for
\xi\in\Omega_{d}
wave breaking occurs in the future and hence
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)
might be discontinuous forward in time.

The function
g(X)
is then defined as

(4.17)

\displaystyle g(X)(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)V_{\xi}(\xi),&\xi\in\Omega_{d}(X),\\
V_{\xi}(\xi),&\xi\in\Omega_{c}(X).\end{cases}

Note that the Eulerian counterpart of
g(X)
is the previously defined function
f(Z)
, cf. (
4.6
). The relation between the two functions is clarified in the following remark.

Remark 4.6
.

If
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\nu
is purely absolutely continuous, and
X=L((u,\mu,\nu))
, the functions (
4.17
) and (
4.6
) are related through

\displaystyle g(X)(\xi)=f(Z)(y(\xi))y_{\xi}(\xi),\text{ for a.e. }\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

The following result is the final one concerning convergence of the initial data in Lagrangian coordinates.

Proposition 4.7
.

Given
(u,\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
, let
X=(y,U,V,H)=L\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
and
X_{\Delta x}=\left(y_{\Delta x},U_{\Delta x},V_{\Delta x},H_{\Delta x}\right)=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((u,\mu,\nu)\right)
, then as
{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0
,

(4.18)

\displaystyle g(X_{\Delta x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow g(X)\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

To begin with observe that for any
X\in\mathcal{F}_{0}^{0}

\|g(X)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|V_{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|V_{\xi}\|_{1}\leq F_{\infty},

since
0\leq V_{\xi}(\xi)=H_{\xi}(\xi)\leq 1
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, which follows from (
2.1c
) and the fact that both
y
and
H
are increasing functions. We can therefore apply the Radon–Riesz theorem to establish
g(X_{\Delta x})\to g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Accordingly, we split the proof of (
4.18
) into three parts,
i)

L^{2}
-norm convergence,
ii)
weak
L^{2}
-convergence, and
iii)

L^{1}
-convergence.

i)
Verification of
\|g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}\rightarrow\|g(X)\|_{2}
. Note that since
X_{\Delta x}
and
X
belong to
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\|g(X)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-\|g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}^{2}\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{c}(X)}H^{2}_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}H_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega_{c}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}H_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha(2-\alpha)\left|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\|H_{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}-\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}^{2}\right|

(4.19a)

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha(2-\alpha)\left|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)d\xi\right|

(4.19b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha(2-\alpha)\left|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\right|,

where we used (
4.15
) in the last step.

To estimate (
4.19a
) and (
4.19b
), introduce

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:y_{\xi}(\xi)=0\}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:V_{\xi}(\xi)=1\},

and
B=y(\mathcal{S})
. Then it has been shown in the proof of
[
12
, Thm. 27]
that

(4.21)

\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=\mu|_{B^{c}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}=\mu|_{B},

where
\mu|_{B}
denotes the restriction of
\mu
to
B
, that is
\mu|_{B}(E)=\mu(E\cap B)
for any Borel set
E
. Thus

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{S})=\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}(\mathbb{R})=F_{\mathrm{sing},\infty}.

Along the same lines, by defining

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}:

\displaystyle=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:y_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)=0\}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:V_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)=1\}=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+1}\right],

we have

(4.23)

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x})=\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(\mathbb{R})=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing},\infty}=F_{\mathrm{sing},\infty}.

Since
\Omega_{d}(X)=\Omega_{d}(X)\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}
and
\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})=\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{\Delta x}
, due to Definition
2.2

(iii)
, (
4.21
) implies that

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}

\displaystyle H_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|

(4.24)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.12128pt=\left|\int_{y\left(\Omega_{d}(X)\right)}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz-\int_{y_{\Delta x}\left(\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})\right)}u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}(z)dz\right|.

Furthermore,
U_{\xi}(\xi)=u_{x}(y(\xi))y_{\xi}(\xi)
for almost every
\xi\in\Omega_{d}(X)
and likewise for
U_{\Delta x,\xi}
. Hence, we can replace in the above integrals
y\left(\Omega_{d}(X)\right)
and
y_{\Delta x}\left(\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})\right)
, by
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)
and
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})
, respectively, and end up with

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z_{\Delta x})}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz+\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{c}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})}u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}(z)dz

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z)\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(Z_{\Delta x})}\left|u_{x}^{2}(z)-u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}(z)\right|dz.

The terms on the right hand side can be estimated using the same approach as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, which yields

(4.25)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)d\xi\right|\leq 4\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}.

For (
4.19b
), we get

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|

(4.26a)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)\cap\Omega_{c}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{\Omega_{c}(X)\cap\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi

(4.26b)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)\cap\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}\left|U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)-U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)\right|d\xi.

The two terms in (
4.26a
) have a similar structure and we therefore only consider the first one. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)\cap\Omega_{c}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)\cap\Omega_{c}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\xi}(\xi)\left(U_{\xi}(\xi)-U_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)\right)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}y_{\xi}V_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{\xi}-U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{\xi}-U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{F_{\infty}}\|U_{\xi}-U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2},

since
U_{\xi}^{2}=y_{\xi}V_{\xi}
and
0\leq y_{\xi}\leq 1
.
Estimating (
4.26b
) in much the same way, yields

(4.27)

\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{d}(X)}U_{\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi-\int_{\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}U_{\Delta x,\xi}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|\leq 4\sqrt{F_{\infty}}\|U_{\xi}-U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}.

Finally, combining (
4.19
), (
4.25
) and (
4.27
), we have shown that

\displaystyle\left|\|g(X)\|_{2}^{2}-\|g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\|H_{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}-\|H_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}^{2}\right|+4\alpha(2-\alpha)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+4\alpha(2-\alpha)\sqrt{F_{\infty}}\|U_{\xi}-U_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}.

As
H_{\Delta x,\xi}\rightarrow H_{\xi}
and
U_{\Delta x,\xi}\rightarrow U_{\xi}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Lemma
4.5
and
u_{\Delta x,x}\rightarrow u_{x}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Lemma
4.2
, it holds that
\|g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}\rightarrow\|g(X)\|_{2}
.

ii)
We show that
g(X_{\Delta x})\rightharpoonup g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. To that end, we interpret
g(X_{\Delta x})
and
g(X)
as positive Radon measures with the associated functions

\displaystyle\hat{G}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}g(X)(\eta)d\eta\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{G}_{\Delta x}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}g(X_{\Delta x})(\eta)d\eta.

If we show that
\hat{G}_{\Delta x}\rightarrow\hat{G}
pointwise, then
[
7
, Prop. 7.19]
implies that
g(X_{\Delta x})\rightarrow g(X)
vaguely, and as a consequence,
g(X_{\Delta x})\rightharpoonup g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
\xi\in[\hat{\xi}_{3j},\hat{\xi}_{3j+3})
, and note that due to Definition
3.2
, Definition
2.3
, (
3.12
)–(
3.13
), and (
4.21
),

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}((-\infty,x_{2j}))

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}}V_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta=\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}}V_{\Delta x,\xi}(\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle=\mu_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}((-\infty,x_{2j})).

Thus,

\displaystyle\bigl|\hat{G}(\xi)-\hat{G}_{\Delta x}(\xi)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}}g(X)(\eta)d\eta-\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}^{c}}g(X_{\Delta x})(\eta)d\eta\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\int_{\hat{\xi}_{3j}}^{\xi}(g(X)-g(X_{\Delta x}))(\eta)d\eta\right|

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
note that after using the same argument as the one leading to (
4.2
), we obtain

\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}}g(X)(\eta)d\eta-\int_{(-\infty,\hat{\xi}_{3j}]\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}^{c}}g(X_{\Delta x})(\eta)d\eta=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{2j}}(f(Z)-f(Z_{\Delta x}))(x)dx,

and due to Lemma
4.3
,

|I_{1}|\leq\|f(Z)-f(Z_{\Delta x})\|_{1}\leq 8\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}.

The term
I_{2}
can be estimated as follows,

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]}H_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta-\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\eta)d\eta\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha\left|\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]\cap\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta-\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]\cap\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\eta)d\eta\right|.

The first term is bounded from above by
\|H-H_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\Delta x
, due to (
4.11c
). For the second term, let
x=y(\xi)
and
x_{\Delta x}=y_{\Delta x}(\xi)
, which both belong to
[x_{2j},x_{2j+2}]
. Using once more the same argument as the one leading to (
4.2
),
\nu_{\textrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}
, and

, we end up with

\displaystyle\alpha\biggl|\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]\cap\Omega_{d}(X)}H_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle-\int_{(\hat{\xi}_{3j},\xi]\cap\Omega_{d}(X_{\Delta x})}H_{\Delta x,\xi}(\eta)d\eta\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left(\int_{x_{2j}}^{x}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz+\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{\Delta x}}u_{x}^{2}(z)dz\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2(F_{\textrm{ac}}(x_{2j+2})-F_{\textrm{ac}}(x_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq 2(F_{\textrm{ac}}(x+2\Delta x)-F_{\textrm{ac}}(x-2\Delta x)),

and therefore

|I_{2}|\leq 2\Delta x+2(F_{\textrm{ac}}(x+2\Delta x)-F_{\textrm{ac}}(x-2\Delta x)).

Thus,

\displaystyle|\hat{G}(\xi)-\hat{G}_{\Delta x}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta x+8\sqrt{F_{\text{ac},\infty}}\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\quad+2(F_{\textrm{ac}}(x+2\Delta x)-F_{\textrm{ac}}(x-2\Delta x)).

By Lemma
4.2
,
u_{\Delta x,x}\rightarrow u_{x}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and, since

is continuous, also the third term tends to zero as

\hat{G}_{\Delta x}\rightarrow\hat{G}
pointwise, which gives, see
[
7
, Prop. 7.19]

(4.29)

\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(X_{\Delta x})\phi(\eta)d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(X)\phi(\eta)d\eta,\quad\text{ for all }\lx@nobreakspace\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Since
g(X)
and
g(X_{\Delta x})
belong to

and

is a dense subset of

, (
4.29
) holds for all
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
g(X_{\Delta x})\rightharpoonup g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Consequently, combining
i)
and

, the conditions of the Radon–Riesz theorem are met and
g(X_{\Delta x})\to g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

iii)
It is left to show that
g(X_{\Delta x})\rightarrow g(X)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Again we will use a splitting based on the sets

and
\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}
defined in (

) and (

), respectively.

Since
g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)=g(X)(\xi)=1
for a.e.
\xi\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}
, we have

\displaystyle\|g(X)-g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}^{c}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi

(4.30a)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi

(4.30b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}^{c}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
4.23
), (

) satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{meas}\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\Delta x}\right)}\|g(X)-g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}

The term (

Furthermore, let

, so that (

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\Delta x}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{E_{\Delta x}\cap A^{c}}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi.

For (

\int_{E_{\Delta x}\cap A}\left|g(X)(\xi)-g(X_{\Delta x})(\xi)\right|d\xi\leq\!\sqrt{2G_{\infty}}\|g(X)-g(X_{\Delta x})\|_{2}.

Combining (
4.30
), (
4.2
) (
4.33
), and (
4.34
) and recalling that
g(X_{\Delta x})\to g(X)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it remains to show that (

) tends to zero as

. We follow the proof of
[
11
, Lem. 7.3]
closely. For almost every

, we have

(4.35)

y_{\xi}(\xi)=\frac{1}{1+u_{x}^{2}(y(\xi))}\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{\Delta x,\xi}(\xi)=\frac{1}{1+u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}(y_{\Delta x}(\xi))},

Remark 4.14

5.

In this section we present three examples which highlight different challenges for the numerical algorithm. The first two examples are also considered in
[
5

14

and display solutions with multipeakon and cusp initial data, respectively.
In the first example, wave breaking happens twice; initially, and also at a later time, and at each occurrence a finite amount of energy concentrates on a set of Lebesgue measure zero. For the second example on the other hand, the wave breaking times accumulate in the interval
