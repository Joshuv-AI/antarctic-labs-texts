A Heterotic Kähler Gravity
and the Distance Conjecture

Abstract
Deformations of the heterotic superpotential give rise to a topological holomorphic theory with similarities to both Kodaira-Spencer gravity and holomorphic Chern-Simons theory. Although the action is cubic, it is only quadratic in the complex structure deformations (the Beltrami differential). Treated separately, for large fluxes, or alternatively at large distances in the background complex structure moduli space, these fields can be integrated out to obtain a new field theory in the remaining fields, which describe the complexified hermitian and gauge degrees of freedom. We investigate properties of this new holomorphic theory, and in particular connections to the swampland distance conjecture in the context of heterotic string theory. In the process, we define a new type of symplectic cohomology theory, where the background complex structure Beltrami differential plays the role of the symplectic form.

Introduction

Understanding the circumstances under which quantum field theories can be UV completed with gravity is one of the main efforts of the last decades of theoretical physics.
Mayor inspirations for consistent UV completions have been drawn from string theory, which is a UV complete theory of quantum gravity.

The swampland program

[

These limits have been studied extensively in the recent literature and further sharpened over time (see

In such setups, the respective towers could be characterized, and it was further proposed that their origin should be attributed to either light Kaluza-Klein states or light string degrees of freedom. This is known as the emergent string conjecture
[

Four-dimensional theories with

Such cases were studied in F-theory and Type II orientifolds for asymptotic limits in the Kähler and complex structure moduli spaces of the underlying CY three- and four-folds. In contrast, infinite distance limits in heterotic string theory setups are less well understood and explored. Efforts are mostly focusing on the Kähler moduli space

These new effective topological heterotic Kähler gravity theories are also interesting from a mathematical point of view. We find that the gauge structure forms a natural part of new elliptic differential complexes, which can also be used to quantize the theories and study properties like anomalies and topological invariants.

2
The Distance Conjecture

Within the swampland program, the distance conjecture
[
5
]
is rather well-established and studied, in particular since it makes strong general predictions and connects to other conjectures, such as the absence of global symmetries or the weak gravity conjecture. The distance conjecture states that when we start with an EFT in quantum gravity at a certain point
P\in\mathcal{M}
in its moduli space and move to another point
O\in\mathcal{M}
that is at infinite distance along a shortest geodesic,
d(P,O)\to\infty
, there is an infinite tower of states that becomes exponentially light. The masses of this tower scale asymptotically as

m(O)\sim m(P)e^{-\alpha\cdot d(P,O)}\,,

(2.1)

where
\alpha
is a positive, order
\mathcal{O}(1)
factor. The above behavior represents a critical breakdown of the EFT, which loses validity in this limit. One source for the tower of states are KK modes that becomes light when approaching a decompactification limit. A second source are light string states. According to the
emergent string conjecture

[
7
,
32
]
, these are the only two possibilities. The prototypical example is the compactification of a
D
-dimensional theory on a circle. This theory has a one-dimensional moduli space
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to the radius of the circle. The dimensional reduction of the radon field
R
then has the kinetic term (in Einstein frame)

S=\int\left(\frac{dR}{R}\right)^{2}+\ldots\,.

(2.2)

The field space metric is thus given by
1/R
and distances between the points
P
and
O
with radii
R_{P}
and
R_{O}
are

\displaystyle d(R_{P},R_{O})=\int_{R_{P}}^{R_{O}}\frac{1}{R}=\text{log}(R_{O}/R_{P})\,,

(2.3)

and hence
R_{O}\sim e^{d(R_{P},R_{O})}
. There is indeed already one tower we know of, which is the KK tower whose masses scale as

\displaystyle m_{n}=\frac{n}{R}\sim ne^{-d(R_{P},R_{O})}\,,

(2.4)

as expected. There does also exist a second limit, for
R_{O}\rightarrow 0
which is at infinite distance.
Field-theoretically, it is not clear what type of characteristic objects become light in this limit, but from string theory we know that the light states are the string winding modes, whose masses scale as

\displaystyle m_{\hat{n}}=\frac{\hat{n}R_{O}}{\alpha^{\prime}}\,.

(2.5)

These states become light at
R_{O}\rightarrow 0
, and correspond to the T-dual description of the
R_{O}\rightarrow\infty
limit.

This is perhaps the simplest example possible: the moduli space is only one-dimensional and both types of towers can be identified straight-forwardly. Theories in lower dimensions and with less supersymmetry substantially enrich the discussion. For example, Calabi-Yau compactifications have
\mathcal{O}(100)
-dimensional complex structure and Kähler moduli spaces. The lower amount of supersymmetry makes it also more difficult to identify the infinite towers and their behaviors.

The Heterotic Effective Action

Throughout most of this paper we will set
\alpha^{\prime}=0

3.1
The Effective Action

Let us treat the superpotential contribution
\Delta W
of (
3.2
) as a quantum theory and study its implications. For example, note that the expression is linear in
x
, so integrating out
x
simply imposes its equation of motion which puts the complex structure deformations on-shell. That is, they satisfy the Kodaira–Spencer Maurer–Cartan equation (
3.10
). More interestingly, one can ask what happens when we integrate out
\mu
? As the action is quadratic in
\mu
, there is hope of performing such an integral explicitly, leading to an effective theory for the hermitian degrees of freedom
x
and
b
. In a usual field theory, the quadratic term is given in terms of an elliptic differential operator with a finite-dimensional kernel. Hence, the operator is invertible, modulo this kernel. Our action (
3.2
) does not have a kinetic term for
\mu
. However, if
H
is invertible when acting point-wise as a matrix operator on
\mu
valued in
\Omega^{(0,1)}(T^{(1,0)})
, we can nevertheless employ the fundamental theorem of quantum field theory. Indeed, this quadratic term is a mass term for
\mu
, and for large values of
H
the fluctuations
\mu
can be integrated out. We shall see below that
H
is invertible if and only if
H
has non-vanishing Yukawa triple coupling, and so we make this requirement when we integrate out
\mu
.

When integrating out
\mu
, we treat it as a dynamical field in the path integral, with
\Delta W
as the action

\displaystyle Z=e^{-S_{\text{eff}}}=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\,e^{-\Delta W}\,,

(3.13)

which then gives rise to the effective action
S_{\text{eff}}
. Integrating out
\mu
is then a standard exercise in field theory. Some straight-forward manipulations show that the action (
3.2
) can be written as

\Delta W=i\int_{X}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)_{a\bar{c}\bar{d}}\Omega^{\bar{c}\bar{d}\bar{e}}\mu^{a}_{\bar{e}}+\mu^{a}_{\bar{d}}(H+{\partial}x)_{ab\bar{c}}\Omega^{\bar{c}\bar{d}\bar{e}}\mu_{\bar{e}}^{b}\right){\rm dvol}\>,

(3.14)

where the volume form is

{\rm dvol}=\frac{i}{|\Omega|^{2}}\Omega\wedge\bar{\Omega}\>.

(3.15)

Let us define a symmetric pairing
H+{\partial}x
on
\Omega^{(0,1)}(T^{(1,0)}X)
as

{(H+{\partial}x)_{a}}^{\bar{d}}{\,{}_{b}}^{\bar{e}}=\tfrac{2i}{|\Omega|^{2}}(H+{\partial}x)_{ab\bar{c}}\Omega^{\bar{c}\bar{d}\bar{e}}\>,

(3.16)

where we have chosen a convenient normalization, where the norm appears in the denominator so as to keep this matrix topological, i.e., metric independent. Note that the norm
|\Omega|^{2}
is constant on a Calabi-Yau. As invertibility is an open property, for small enough
x
this matrix is invertible provided
H
is invertible when viewed as a symmetric pairing on
\Omega^{(0,1)}(T^{(1,0)}X)
.

We also define an element
({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)\in\Omega^{(1,0)}(T^{(0,1)}X)
as

({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)^{\bar{e}}_{a}=\tfrac{i}{2|\Omega|^{2}}({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)_{a\bar{c}\bar{d}}\Omega^{\bar{c}\bar{d}\bar{e}}\>,

(3.17)

with a convenient normalization so that this expression also remains topological. The action (
3.14
) may then be written in a more conventional, and and manifestly topological form as

\Delta W=\int_{X}i\Omega\wedge\bar{\Omega}\left(({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)\cdot\mu+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu\cdot(H+{\partial}x)\cdot\mu\right)\>,

(3.18)

where we have suppressed the indices. To further simplify the notation, we also drop the topological measure
i\Omega\wedge\bar{\Omega}
, and simply write the topological integration as

\int_{X}i\Omega\wedge\bar{\Omega}\;\rightarrow\;\int_{X}\>.

(3.19)

If not specified otherwise, this is the measure we use when we integrate functions over
X
. Integrating top-forms is done in the normal way.

We can now solve the path integral by first completing the square, and letting

\displaystyle\mu\;\rightarrow\;\mu-(H+{\partial}x)^{-1}\cdot({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)\>.

(3.20)

This shift eliminates the linear term in
\mu
such that we can solve the Gauss integral. Note also that such a linear shift will not change the path integral measure. We then obtain the new action

\displaystyle\Delta W=\int_{X}\left(\mu\cdot(H+{\partial}x)\cdot\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)\cdot(H+{\partial}x)^{-1}\cdot({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b)\right)\>.

(3.21)

After this shift, the action is purely quadratic in

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\log\left(\det\left(H+\partial x\right)\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H+\partial x)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}\tr\left(\log(H+\partial x)\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H+\partial x)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\>,

x
. Recall that

(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)_{a}^{\bar{d}}=\tfrac{i}{2|\Omega|^{2}}\left({\bar{\partial}}x+{\partial}b\right)_{a\bar{b}\bar{c}}{\Omega}^{\bar{b}\bar{c}\bar{d}}\>.

(3.34)

The first term linear in
x
in the action (
3.33
) actually drops out: As one can show using (
3.32
), it is proportional to the inner product of
\partial x
with
\overline{\Omega(h,h)}
, where

\Omega(h,h)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_{abc}h^{a}\wedge h^{b}\wedge{\text{d}}z^{c}\>.

(3.35)

Special geometry dictates that
\Omega(h,h)
is harmonic
[
44
,
45
]
, so this inner product vanishes.

The quadratic effective action therefore becomes

S_{\text{eff}}=-\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{X}\tr\left((H^{-1}\partial x)^{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)_{b}^{\bar{a}}(H^{-1}){}_{\bar{a}}{}^{b}{}_{\bar{c}}{}^{d}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)_{d}^{\bar{c}}\>,

(3.36)

The first and second term in the effective action are of order
{\cal O}(H^{-2})
and
{\cal O}(H^{-1})
, respectively. Treating
S_{\text{eff}}
as an effective superpotential, we therefore expect two towers of light states to come down at different rates as we send
H\rightarrow\infty
. We will analyze this in further detail below.

3.2
Heterotic action including the dilaton

In the above we have neglected complications due to fluctuations of the dilaton. In string theory, the dilaton is a field and we need to take its fluctuations into account. Deforming the superpotential including the dilaton results in the action

S=\int_{X}(dx+H+db)\wedge e^{\chi}(\Omega+\Omega(\mu)+\Omega(\mu,\mu))+c\int_{X}e^{\chi}\Omega(h,h,h)\wedge\Omega\>,

(3.37)

where
\chi
denotes the complexified deformation of the dilaton, or the axio-dilaton. Note that we have now included the “constant” background term proportional to
\Omega(h,h,h)
, as this term also couples to the axio-dilaton. Using the Hodge decomposition of
\chi
, we can take
\chi
to be a total divergence, since any constant or harmonic part can be absorbed by rescaling the action. Using equation (
3.28
), we then obtain the action

\displaystyle S=\int_{X}\left(e^{\chi}(\bar{\partial}x_{a}+{\partial_{a}}b)\mu^{a}+e^{\chi}(H_{ab}+\partial x_{ab})\mu^{a}\mu^{b}+e^{\chi}\bar{\partial}b+e^{\chi}c\,|h|\bar{\Omega}\right)\wedge\Omega\>.

(3.38)

We next integrate out
\mu
in the quadratic action following the same procedure as above, which results in

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\tfrac{1}{2}\tr\log\left[e^{\chi}\left(H+\partial x\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}e^{\chi}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H+\partial x)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)+\int_{X}e^{\chi}\left(\bar{\partial}b+c\,|h|\bar{\Omega}\right)\wedge\Omega\>.

(3.39)

Note that the axio-dilaton dependence of the first term actually vanishes, since

\int_{X}\chi=0\>.

(3.40)

This is a consequence of
\chi
being a total divergence. The effective quadratic action can then be written as

S_{\rm eff}=-\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{X}\tr\left((H^{-1}\partial x)^{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)+\int_{X}\left(\chi\bar{\partial}b+\tfrac{c}{2}\,\chi^{2}\,|h|\bar{\Omega}\right)\wedge\Omega\>,

(3.41)

where again the linear term in the axio-dilaton multiplying
c|h|\bar{\Omega}
drops out since the axio-dilaton is a total derivative.

The last term in the action can be written as

\int_{X}\left(\chi\bar{\partial}b+\tfrac{c}{2}\,\chi^{2}\,|h|\bar{\Omega}\right)\wedge\Omega=\int_{X}\left(i\,\chi{\cal L}_{0}(b)+\tfrac{i\,c|h|}{2}\chi^{2}\right)\>,

(3.42)

where we are using the topological integration measure (
3.19
) and

{\cal L}_{0}(b)=\tfrac{i}{|\Omega|^{2}}(\Omega\lrcorner{\bar{\partial}}b)=\tfrac{i}{|\Omega|^{2}}\left(\tfrac{1}{3!}\Omega^{\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c}}({\bar{\partial}}b)_{\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c}}\right)\>.

(3.43)

Note that the
\chi^{2}
-term in the action, proportional to the Yukawa coupling, gives rise to a large mass-term for the axio-dilaton as
|h|\rightarrow\infty
. Just like for
\mu
, this also freezes out higher order momentum modes of the dilaton, and we can effectively integrate it out from the quadratic action. This then results in the equation of motion

{\cal L}_{0}(b)+i\,c|h|\chi=0\>.

(3.44)

Solving this for the dilaton, and inserting the result in the action gives

S_{\rm eff}=-\int_{X}\tfrac{1}{4}\tr\left((H^{-1}\partial x)^{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)(H)^{-1}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)+\tfrac{i}{2c|h|}\int_{X}{\cal L}_{0}(b)^{2}\>.

(3.45)

Note that the first term goes as
{\cal O}(H^{-2})
, the second term goes as
{\cal O}(H^{-1})
, and the third term goes as
{\cal O}(H^{-3})
. We expect a tower of light modes coming down at each of these respective rates. Comparing with the previous two towers, we see that including the axio-dilaton produced an additional tower of states that becomes light at rate of
{\cal O}(H^{-3})
.

The action (
3.45
) is difficult to analyze, especially the middle term coupling the hermitian modes given by
x
with the
b
-field. The term simplifies in backgrounds where
b=0
, or where the dilaton mass scale is far above the other fields, such that we expand the dilaton to linear instead of quadratic order. In this case, the dilaton equation of motion becomes

\bar{\partial}b=0\>,

(3.46)

which on a Calabi-Yau background means that the
(0,2)
-form
b
is
\bar{\partial}
-exact. After an appropriate field redefinition that shifts
x
by a
\partial
-exact form, the quadratic action becomes

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{4}\tr\left((H^{-1}\partial x)^{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x)_{b}^{\bar{a}}(H^{-1}){}_{\bar{a}}{}^{b}{}_{\bar{c}}{}^{d}(\bar{\partial}x)_{d}^{\bar{c}}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}{\partial}x\star_{H}{\partial}x+{\bar{\partial}}x\star^{\prime}_{H}{\bar{\partial}}x\>,

(3.47)

where the last equality serves to define “Hodge duals”
\star_{H}
and
\star^{\prime}_{H}
that are constructed using
H
instead of
g
. Note that these Hodge duals give rise to symmetric pairings. Explicitly, the Hodge duals take the form

\displaystyle\star_{H}{\partial}x

\displaystyle\quad\propto\quad{(H^{-1})_{\bar{a}}}^{b}{{\,}{}_{\bar{c}}}^{d}{(H^{-1})_{\bar{f}}}^{e}{{\,}{}_{\bar{h}}}^{j}\Omega^{\bar{l}\bar{c}\bar{f}}\Omega_{bjk}\,{\partial}x_{de\bar{l}}\,{\text{d}}z^{k\bar{a}\bar{h}}\>,

(3.48)

\displaystyle\star^{\prime}_{H}{\bar{\partial}}x

\displaystyle\quad\propto\quad(H^{-1}){}_{\bar{a}}{}^{b}{}_{\bar{c}}{}^{d}\Omega_{bef}(\bar{\partial}x)_{d}^{\bar{c}}\,{\text{d}}z^{ef\bar{a}}\>,

(3.49)

where we suppressed unimportant numerical prefactors.

The action (
3.47
) is slightly different from (
3.36
). In particular, the new action appears to have a naive gauge symmetry
\delta x=\partial\bar{\partial}f
for some function
f
, though this again turns out to be not quite accurate, which we discuss in Section
4
.

We observe that the quadratic action (
3.47
) has again two different terms of order
\mathcal{O}(H^{-2})
and
\mathcal{O}(H^{-1})
, respectively. As we send the background field
H
to infinity and approach the boundary of complex structure moduli space, we thus expect two towers of massive states coming down at different rates. In particular, the kinetic operator of the theory takes the form

\Delta_{H}={\partial}\star_{H}{\partial}+{\bar{\partial}}\star^{\prime}_{H}{\bar{\partial}}\>.

(3.50)

Note that this operator has no explicit dependence on the choice of background metric, reflecting the topological nature of the theory. However, this operator maps
(1,1)
-forms to
(2,2)
-forms. In order to discuss its eigenvalues, we need to map back to
(1,1)
-forms. This is perhaps most conveniently done by choosing a fixed background metric, taking the ordinary Hodge-dual
*
, and considering the operator

*\Delta_{H}:\;\;\Omega^{(1,1)}\rightarrow\Omega^{(1,1)}\>.

(3.51)

Note that the numerical values of the individual eigenvalues depend on the choice of fixed metric, but their overall behavior as we approach a point at large distance in complex structure moduli space is universal.

However, there is another slight complication, stemming from modes that arise from a combination of both terms in the operator. Since the two operators in
\Delta_{H}
do not commute, they cannot be diagonalized simultaneously. However, the corresponding corrections to the eigenvalues will be of higher order in the
H^{-1}
expansion, so that we still get two separate towers of modes. More concretely, by picking a Kähler metric on
X
, we can Hodge-decompose
x
as

x=x_{h}+{\partial}{\bar{\partial}}f+{\partial}^{\dagger}{\bar{\partial}}\alpha+{\bar{\partial}}^{\dagger}\left({\partial}\beta+{\partial}^{\dagger}\gamma\right)\>,

(3.52)

where
x_{h}
is harmonic. The last term appears in both parts of the action (
3.47
), while the second term only appears in the first part of the action, and it is this term that gives the leading contribution to eigenvalues of
\mathcal{O}(H^{-2})
. To illustrate why, consider a family of
2\times 2
matrices

A_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}ta&tb\\
tc&d\end{pmatrix}\>,

(3.53)

where
ta
models the part of
\Delta_{H}
acting on the space of forms of the type
{\partial}^{\dagger}{\bar{\partial}}\alpha
, while
d
models the part of
\Delta_{H}
acting on the space of forms of the type
{\bar{\partial}}^{\dagger}\left({\partial}\beta+{\partial}^{\dagger}\gamma\right)
. Here,
t
is a small parameter modeling the separation of scales. It is straight-forward to compute the eigenvalues of
A_{t}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=d+\mathcal{O}(t)

(3.54)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=ta+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})\>,

(3.55)

which illustrates the separation of scales of eigenvalues.

If we restrict ourselves for example to the tower of the heaviest modes, we can focus on the second part of (
3.47
),

\int_{X}{\bar{\partial}}x\star^{\prime}_{H}{\bar{\partial}}x\>.

(3.56)

where, as noted above,
\star^{\prime}_{H}
is a Hodge-star like operator giving rise to a symmetric pairing. Classically, we may assume without loss of generality that
x={\bar{\partial}}^{\dagger}\gamma
for a
\gamma\in{\bar{\partial}}\Omega^{(1,1)}
. Using this, the action becomes

S_{\text{eff}}=\int_{X}{\bar{\partial}}x\star^{\prime}_{H}{\bar{\partial}}x=\int_{X}\gamma\Delta\star^{\prime}_{H}\Delta\gamma\>,

(3.57)

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian. Since
\Delta
is elliptic and
\star^{\prime}_{H}
is invertible, the operator
\Delta\star^{\prime}_{H}\Delta
is elliptic. It will hence have a finite-dimensional kernel, and a discrete tower of eigenmodes with a non-zero eigenvalue that tends to zero as
H\to\infty
at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(H^{-1})
. This further translates to an exponential decay in geodesic distance using the moduli space metric as we discuss in Section
3.4
.

3.3
Integrating out the dilaton from the exact action

In Section
3.2
, we implicitly assumed that all our fields live at roughly the same energy scale, and thus we expand all the fields to at most quadratic order. If the natural energy scale of the dilaton is far below the other fields, we also need to consider higher momentum modes of the dilaton, and hence of
\chi
. We should then integrate out the axio-dilaton from the exact action (
3.39
), instead of the action at quadratic order. Since the second and third terms in the effective action (
3.39
) are linear in
e^{\chi}
, there is hope to integrate out the exponentiated dilaton from the action.

In integrating out the dilaton, we need to be careful with the Jacobian factor introduced in the path integral measure when changing variables from
\chi
to
e^{\chi}
. In position space, where
p\in X
is a point in
X
, the path integral over the dilaton is

\int{\cal D}\chi=\prod_{p}\int{\text{d}}\chi_{p}=\prod_{p}\int{\text{d}}\left(e^{\chi_{p}}\right)e^{-\chi_{p}}=\int{\cal D}\left(e^{\chi}\right)e^{-\int_{X}\chi}=\int{\cal D}\left(e^{\chi}\right)\>.

(3.58)

The last equality follows since the dilaton is taken to be a total derivative.

The factor
e^{\chi}
acts as a Lagrange multiplier in the action, which can be integrated out. We arrange the action into the form

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}\tr\log\left[\left(H+\partial x\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}e^{\chi}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H+\partial x)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)+\int_{X}e^{\chi}\left(\bar{\partial}b+c\,|h|\bar{\Omega}\right)\wedge\Omega

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tr\log\left[\left(H+\partial x\right)\right]+\int_{X}e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tr\log\left[\left(H+\partial x\right)\right]+\int_{X}e^{\chi}\left({\cal L}_{0}(x,b)+i\,c\,|h|\right)\>,

(3.59)

where the equations are taken to define
{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)
and
{\cal L}(x,b)
. Imposing the dilaton equation of motion, we get

\left[e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)\right]_{\textrm{total derivative}}=0\>.

(3.60)

On a compact space, this is equivalent to
e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)
being constant,

{\text{d}}\left(e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)\right)=0\>,

(3.61)

which gives

({\text{d}}\chi){\cal L}(x,b)+{\text{d}}{\cal L}(x,b)=0\>.

(3.62)

We can solve this equation for
\chi
to get

\chi=-\left[\log({\cal L}(x,b))\right]_{\textrm{total derivative}}=-\log({\cal L}(x,b))+\left[\log({\cal L}(x,b))\right]_{\textrm{constant}}\>,

(3.63)

where the constant part of
\log({\cal L}(x,b))
is given by

\left[\log({\cal L}(x,b))\right]_{\textrm{constant}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)}\int_{X}\log({\cal L}(x,b))\>,

(3.64)

where the norm of
\Omega
comes from the topological measure (
3.19
) in the integration. Inserting this back into the action gives

\int_{X}e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)=|\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)\exp\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)}\int_{X}\log({\cal L}(x,b))\right)\>.

(3.65)

Expanding expand the logarithm as

\log({\cal L}(x,b))=\log({\cal L}_{0}(x,b)+i\,c\,|h|)=\log(i\,c\,|h|)+\tfrac{1}{i\,c\,|h|}{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)+\tfrac{1}{2c^{2}\,|h|^{2}}{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)^{2}+\ldots\>,

(3.66)

the action becomes

\displaystyle\int_{X}e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)=i\,c\,|h||\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)\exp\Bigg(\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)}\int_{X}

\displaystyle\Big[\tfrac{1}{i\,c\,|h|}{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2c^{2}\,|h|^{2}}{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)^{2}+\ldots\Big]\Bigg)\>.

(3.67)

Further expanding the exponential, and keeping only terms which produce a quadratic ation in the remaining fields, we find

\displaystyle\int_{X}e^{\chi}{\cal L}(x,b)

\displaystyle=i\,c\,|h||\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)+\int_{X}\left[{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)+\tfrac{i}{2c\,|h|}{\cal L}_{0}(x,b)^{2}\right]+\ldots

\displaystyle=i\,c\,|h|||\Omega|^{2}{\rm Vol}(X)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{X}(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)\cdot(H)^{-1}\cdot(\bar{\partial}x+\partial b)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{i}{2c|h|}\int_{X}{\cal L}_{0}(b)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3},b^{3})\>,

(3.68)

where again

{\cal L}_{0}(b)=\tfrac{i}{|\Omega|^{2}}(\Omega\lrcorner{\bar{\partial}}b)\>.

(3.69)

We hence see that integrating out the dilaton from the full action or from the quadratic approximation results in the same action up to quadratic order. The difference enters in higher order terms. In particular, exactly integrating out the dilaton will produce higher order non-local interaction terms.

3.4
Infinite Distance Limits

Having set up the effective actions, we can discuss infinite distance limits in the moduli space and check whether a tower of states becomes exponentially light, as we would expect from the swampland distance conjecture. To discuss such limits, recall that the Kähler potential for the complex structure moduli space is given in terms of the pre-potential
\mathcal{G}(\mu_{i})
via
[
46
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=-\text{log}\left(2(\bar{\mathcal{G}}-\mathcal{G})+(\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}_{i})({\partial}_{i}\mathcal{G}+\bar{{\partial}}_{i}\bar{\mathcal{G}})\right)\,.

(3.70)

As an example, consider the mirror quintic threefold, which is a simple example with a single complex structure modulus. We take
H=uH_{0}
, where
H_{0}
is a fixed complex structure and
u
denotes the direction in complex structure moduli space for which we are taking the limit.
At large complex structure, the pre-potential for the mirror quintic becomes

\mathcal{G}(u)=\frac{5}{3!}u^{3}\>,

(3.71)

such that the Kähler potential is

\mathcal{K}=-\log\left(\frac{5}{6}(u-\bar{u})^{3}\right)\,.

(3.72)

In radial coordinates
u=e^{i\phi}\lambda
and neglecting the irrelevant
\phi
-dependent terms, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\propto-\log(\lambda^{3})\,,

(3.73)

from which we can compute the Kähler metric

K_{\lambda\lambda}=\frac{3}{\lambda^{2}}\>.

(3.74)

We then want to obtain flat coordinates
T
for the kinetic terms, i.e.

\displaystyle K_{\lambda\lambda}{\text{d}}\lambda{\text{d}}\lambda={\text{d}}T{\text{d}}T\,,

(3.75)

which is provided by

\sqrt{3}{\lambda}^{-1}{\text{d}}\lambda={\text{d}}T\,,\quad

(3.76)

from which we obtain the relation

\lambda^{-1}=e^{-\frac{T}{\sqrt{3}}}\>.

(3.77)

Recall that the elliptic operator in the action (see for example (
3.57
)) behaves like
H^{-1}\propto\lambda^{-1}
, which we interpret as the mass term for the infinite tower of internal hermitian degrees of freedom associated to the elliptic operator. For
T\rightarrow\infty
this tower becomes exponentially light as expected from the swampland distance conjecture.

The discussion was simple enough for the one-parameter case and will also apply for generic directions in
\mathcal{O}(100)
dimensional complex structure moduli spaces. However, there may be special directions in which the Yukawa couplings vanish. Note that we can infer the structure of the Yukawa couplings by the virtue of mirror symmetry
[
44
,
46
]
:
At large complex structure, the Yukawa coupling in
X_{3}
is identified with the triple intersection numbers
D_{ijk}
in the
mirror
X_{3}^{*}
, which can be evaluated as

\displaystyle D_{ijk}=\int_{X_{3}^{*}}D_{i}\cdot D_{j}\cdot D_{k}\,,

(3.78)

with
D_{i}
being a basis of integral two-forms. Indeed, the infinite distance limits in complex structure moduli space of
X_{3}
correspond to infinite distance limits in Kähler moduli spaces of the mirror
X_{3}^{*}
.

Notably, for large numbers of Kähler moduli, it has been observed that the intersection matrix indeed becomes sparse. This structure is closely related to fibration structures of the threefold as discussed in the works by Kollár, Oguiso, and Wilson
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
. Divisors
D
with
D^{3}=0
or
D^{2}\cdot D_{m}=0
, for any effective divisor
D_{m}
are associated with a
T^{2}
or K3 fibration of the CY, respectively. Infinite distance limits that send the volume of
D
to infinity can therefore be thought of as decompactification limits of the base of the fibrations
[
50
]
.

As an example, consider the bi-cubic CICY given by the configuration matrix

\displaystyle X^{*}_{3}:\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbbm{P}^{2}&3\\
\mathbbm{P}^{2}&3\end{array}\right]

with intersection numbers

\displaystyle D_{111}=D_{222}=0\,,\quad D_{112}=D_{122}=3\,.

(3.81)

The two vanishing self-intersections show the presence of two torus fibrations in
X_{3}^{*}
over a
\mathbbm{P}^{2}
base. The pre-potential for the two complex structures in the mirror bi-cubic
X_{3}
in the large complex structure limit is therefore given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu_{1}\mu_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\,.

(3.82)

The problematic limits with vanishing Yukawa couplings are those that send one of the
\mu_{i}\rightarrow\infty
and correspond to decompactifications of either
\mathbbm{P}^{2}
bases in the two
T^{2}
fibrations in the dual
X_{3}^{*}
. A general linear combination
\lambda_{1}\mu_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mu_{2}
has a non-vanishing Yukawa interaction. For example along the diagonal direction
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda
, the pre-potential is
\mathcal{G}\propto\lambda^{3}
, which behaves in the same way as in the mirror quintic example.

The connection between infinite distance limits and fibration structures in the Kähler moduli has been explored in
[
50
]
.
The three principal types of infinite distance limits are denoted by type II, III and IV
[
51
]
and precisely correspond to sending the base of a K3 (or
T^{4}
),
T^{2}
and the full threefold volume to infinite. Using our effective action, we have therefore identified the respective tower of states for the mirror dual of a type IV degeneration in the heterotic string
complex structure moduli space. It would be interesting to extend the discussion of our effective action to include degeneration limits in directions that correspond to type II and III limits in the Kähler sector of the mirror dual geometry.

4
Elliptic complexes and symplectic cohomology

In the above quadratic actions, we have pointed out putative gauge symmetries. In order to make this more rigorous, we check in this section that these structures indeed form part of elliptic complexes, implying that the corresponding cohomologies become finite-dimensional on compact geometries. This is also important when quantizing the respective actions later, since the cohomologies count the “on-shell” degrees of freedom, which ought to be finite and can (modulo potential complications from anomalies) be decoupled from the quantization procedure. Note also that the more formal BV-quantization procedure requires that the complexes involved be elliptic, which then allows one to write the (one-loop) partition function in terms of determinants of elliptic operators.

We will show that the proposed naive gauge transformations are not quite correct, and in understanding the correct gauge complex we define a new symplectic cohomology associated to the background complex structure deformation. We illustrate this for the quadratic effective action (
3.47
) that includes the dilaton. We refer to this as the “dilaton effective action”, though the dilaton does not appear explicitly in this quadratic action.

4.1
The first term of the dilaton effective action

Let us begin by considering the first term of the dilaton effective action (
3.47
),

S_{\text{eff}}=\int_{X}{\partial}x\star_{H}{\partial}x\>.

(4.1)

Here the kinetic operator in question takes the form

D={\partial}\star_{H}{\partial}\>.

(4.2)

This operator forms part of a natural differential complex

0\rightarrow\Omega^{(0,1)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}}\Omega^{(1,1)}\xrightarrow{D}\Omega^{(2,2)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}}\Omega^{(3,2)}\rightarrow 0\>.

(4.3)

To check that this complex is elliptic, we replace
{\partial}
with
\xi\in\Omega^{(1,0)}
, the holomorphic part of a real one-form,

0\rightarrow\Omega^{(0,1)}\xrightarrow{\xi}\Omega^{(1,1)}\xrightarrow{D_{\xi}}\Omega^{(2,2)}\xrightarrow{\xi}\Omega^{(3,2)}\rightarrow 0\>.

(4.4)

If the new complex is exact for all non-vanishing
\xi
, i.e., the kernel at one stage is isomorphic to the image at the previous stage, the complex is elliptic. As is, the complex (
4.3
) appears not to be elliptic, as we explain next.

It is easy to see that the complex is exact at the first stage and last stage, as these stages form part of elliptic Dolbeault complexes. Consider therefore the stage
\Omega^{(1,1)}
. Clearly
{\rm ker}(\xi)\subseteq{\rm Im}(D_{\xi})
. To show the converse, assume that
\alpha\in\Omega^{(1,1)}
sits in
{\rm ker}(D_{\xi})
. That is

D_{\xi}\alpha=\xi\star_{H}(\xi\wedge\alpha)=0\>.

(4.5)

From ellipticity of the
{\partial}
-complex, it follows that

\star_{H}(\xi\wedge\alpha)=\xi\wedge\beta

(4.6)

for some
\beta\in\Omega^{(0,2)}
. However, the space of two-forms that are not in the kernel of
\xi
is (point-wise) six-dimensional, and the operator
\star_{H}\circ\xi
is an isomorphism on this space. However, since
\beta
is a (0,2)-form, it lives in a (point-wise) three-dimensional subspace. Hence, only those
\alpha
that map into this three-dimensional subspace can satisfy equation (
4.6
). As we shall see, this subspace is related to
\alpha
being (non-)primitive in an appropriate sense. This however leaves a potential three-dimensional sub-space of
\alpha
’s which can satisfy (
4.6
), i.e. not in
\ker(\star_{H}\circ\xi)=\ker(\xi)={\rm Im}(\xi)
, implying that the complex (
4.3
) is not elliptic.

To define the primitivity notion we are interested in, we first define a new double complex
4
4

4

A similar complex appeared recently in
[
52
]
, in the context of the double copy of Chern-Simons theory and Kodaira-Spencer theory.

\hat{\Omega}^{(p,q)}:=\Omega^{(p,0)}\otimes\wedge^{q}T^{(0,1)}\>.

(4.7)

A normal form in
\Omega^{(p,q)}
can be mapped to a form in
\hat{\Omega}^{(p,3-q)}
using
\Omega^{\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c}}
. In particular, for our field we have
x\in\hat{\Omega}^{(1,2)}
. Note also that
h^{-1}\in\hat{\Omega}^{(1,1)}
, and in this sense it takes the role of a (complex) Kähler form. We use this form to define a notion of primitivity. We can then decompose

In terms of the primitive decomposition, the action becomes

can only change the form representation, but not the primitivity type of a form. Note also that forms of the same primitivity type can only pair to give a singlet in one way, which we will view as a top-form which can then be integrated in the normal way.

4.2
The second term of the dilaton effective action

Regarding the second term of the dilaton effective action (

{\bar{\partial}}_{-}:\check{\cal P}^{(p,q)}\rightarrow\check{\cal P}^{(p-1,q)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{{\bar{\partial}}_{+}{\bar{\partial}}_{-}}\check{\cal P}^{(1,2)}\xrightarrow{{\bar{\partial}}_{-}}\check{\cal P}^{(0,2)}\rightarrow 0\>,

(4.30)

Viewing

(4.31)

(4.34)

4.3
The full dilaton effective action

Let us consider the dilaton effective action (

We are interested in (elliptic) complexes in which the kinetic operator
({\partial}_{+}{\partial}_{-}+|h|\delta_{+}\delta_{-})
appears. We make an educated guesses for what these complexes look like, but proving that they are indeed elliptic is beyond the scope of the present paper.

Let us begin by considering the first part of the above action,

S(x_{p})={|h|}^{-1}\int_{X}x_{p}({\partial}_{+}{\partial}_{-}+|h|\delta_{+}\delta_{-})x_{p}\>.

(4.41)

It is clear that the gauge-transformation of
x_{p}
will include deformations by terms that are both
{\partial}_{+}

\delta_{+}

\delta x_{p}={\partial}_{+}\alpha=\delta_{+}\beta\>,

\gamma

3

\delta x_{p}={\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}\gamma\>,

(4.44)

0\rightarrow{\hat{\cal P}}^{(0,3)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(1,2)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}{\partial}_{-}+|h|\delta_{+}\delta_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(2,1)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}}{\cal P}^{(3,0)}\rightarrow 0\>.

Checking that this complex is elliptic, and thus conforming that (

x_{p}

Let us now show that
{\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}=\delta_{+}{\partial}_{-}
on the primitive forms of interest. In particular, consider

3

x_{p}

. Using equation (

\nabla_{\bar{a}}

{\bar{\partial}}

\delta x_{p}=-h^{-1}\wedge({\bar{\partial}}x_{p})\>.

(4.47)

Let

{\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}x_{p}=-\pi({\partial}{\bar{\partial}}x_{p})=\pi({\bar{\partial}}{\partial}x_{p})=\pi\left({\bar{\partial}}(h^{-1}{\partial}_{-}x_{p})\right)\>.

(4.48)

Next, note that

{\bar{\partial}}(h^{-1}{\partial}_{-}x_{p})^{\bar{b}}=-2\,\nabla_{\bar{a}}\left(-h^{[\bar{a}}{\partial}_{-}x_{p}^{\bar{b}]}\right)=\delta({\partial}_{-}x_{p})^{\bar{b}}-h^{\bar{b}}\nabla_{\bar{a}}({\partial}_{-}x_{p})^{\bar{a}}\>.

(4.49)

{\partial}_{+}\delta_{-}x_{p}=\pi\left(\delta{\partial}_{-}x_{p})\right)=\delta_{+}{\partial}_{-}x_{p}\>,

(4.50)

as desired. A similar computation shows

on the other primitive forms of interest.

The SYZ fibration and a three-dimensional toy model

According to the SYZ conjecture

As one approaches the large complex structure limit, the three-torus of the SYZ-fibration shrinks while the topological three-sphere base of the fibration grows. A light tower of states could then arise either from winding string modes of the SYZ fiber or from KK modes of the SYZ base.

Ignoring complications concerning details of the degeneration loci of the fibration, we expect a six-dimensional theory to undergo an effective dimensional reduction to the three-sphere. This local three-dimensional description has been utilized to gain valuable insights into the nature of both heterotic and M-theory compactifications, see e.g.

5.1
The model

Before we present the model, we need to define some terms. Let
\hat{\Omega}^{(p,q)}:=\Omega^{p}(S^{3},\Omega^{q}(S^{3}))

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{p!}\tfrac{1}{q!}\alpha_{a_{1}...a_{p},m_{1}...m_{q}}{\text{d}}x^{a_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\text{d}}x^{a_{p}}\otimes{\text{d}}x^{m_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\text{d}}x^{m_{q}}\>,

(5.1)

{\text{d}}x^{a_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\text{d}}x^{a_{p}}

7

7

In Appendix

\displaystyle:\;\;\hat{\Omega}^{(p,q)}\to\hat{\Omega}^{(p+1,q)}

(5.2)

\displaystyle{\text{d}}_{-}\>

\displaystyle:\;\;\hat{\Omega}^{(p,q)}\to\hat{\Omega}^{(p,q+1)}\>,

(5.3)

Inspired by the action (

S=\int_{S^{3}}\left(x_{a}{\text{d}}_{-}\mu^{a}+(H_{ab}+\nabla_{+}x_{ab})\mu^{a}\mu^{b}+\mu^{a}\nabla_{+a}b\right)\>.

(5.5)

7
Conclusions and outlook

In this work we have explored the recently proposed version of a heterotic Kodaira-Spencer like gravity
[
21
]
, derived from fluctuations of the heterotic superpotential. Treating the heterotic superpotential as an effective quantum field theory, and including mathematically motivated (non-perturbative) corrections, leads to a way to study the heterotic moduli space directly. This is non-trivial since the heterotic Bianchi identity mixes Kähler, complex structure, and bundle moduli into a combined moduli space with coupled off-shell fluctuations in the effective action.

Using this framework, we explored infinite distance limits and compared them with expectations from the swampland distance conjecture
[
5
]
: At large complex structure, we can integrate out off-shell fluctuations resulting in an effective theory for the residual degrees of freedom. We confirmed that at large distances in the complex structure moduli space, or at large background flux, we get the expected qualitative behavior of an effective tower of states becoming light at an exponential rate in geodesic distance.

The new effective actions are also interesting in their own right. In particular, the differentials governing the quadratic terms form a part of new elliptic symplectic type differential complexes, where the background complex structure Beltrami differential plays the role of the symplectic form. It would be very interesting to study the mathematical properties of these complexes further, and to investigate the quantum properties of these new effective actions, such as the one-loop partition function, anomalies, etc. In particular, the full one-loop function has been computed in
[
36
]
, where it was studied in great detail without background fluxes turned on. Turning on background fluxes and torsion complicates this analysis, and our method of partially integrating out fields in the presence of fluxes could be useful in this regard.

It should also be noted that in order to integrate out the complex structure moduli, we relied on the background complex structure having a non-vanishing Yukawa coupling. The Yukawa couplings are related to the triple intersection numbers in the mirror dual geometry, which becomes sparse in a basis of Kähler cone generators when the numbers of Kähler moduli becomes large. The vanishing entries in the intersection matrix of the mirror are related to certain fibration structures, which become more abundant with increasing number of Kähler moduli. In fact, there is a direct connection to infinite distance limits in the Kähler moduli space in which only sub-varieties of a fibration degenerate and the rate at which towers become light
[
51
,
50
]
. The specific directions in the complex structure moduli space
that we can probe in this work correspond to the most drastic decompactification limits in the mirror dual Kähler moduli spaces, denoted as type IV in the nomenclature of
[
51
]
. It would be interesting to extend our scenario to other limits and identify the respective towers that become light.

Motivated by the SYZ conjecture, we also proposed toy-models on
S^{3}
for the given effective actions. As the
T^{3}
shrinks and the
S^{3}
grows at large complex structure distance, one might expect an effective dimensional reduction of the theory. Exploiting this, we propose an ansatz for such a reduced theory. These theories have the advantage that the geometry is explicit, leading to the potential to perform explicit computations, for example of the one-loop partition functions. Such computations often lead to topological invariants that characterize the parent heterotic and Calabi-Yau geometries. Of course, the interesting topological properties of the SYZ fibration come from its degeneration loci, which can potentially be modeled as topological defects in the reduced theory, such as analogs of Wilson lines. Understanding how to model such effects and their potential computational power is an interesting future direction.

Throughout most of the paper we have set
\alpha^{\prime}=0
, effectively decoupling the gauge degrees of freedom. In Section
6
, we briefly considered turning on
\alpha^{\prime}
to include these modes. It would be interesting to study such couplings further. In doing so, one needs to take into account other non-perturbative effects, such as world-sheet instantons. These corrections introduce a complex structure dependence of the superpotential via Pfaffian prefactors of the instanton corrections. These might play an important role in our theories, especially when the tree-level Yukawa coupling vanishes. However, whether these corrections occur or are absent due to Beasley-Witten cancellation
[
75
]
also depends on the gauge sector of the theory
[
76
,
77
]
and the compactness of the instanton moduli space, which we mostly ignored in this paper. On the other hand, string theory lore suggests that couplings of such effects and the off-shell modes are suppressed, given the vanishing of world-sheet instantons at higher genus
[
78
,
79
]
. It would be interesting to extend our analysis to such cases in the future.

From a mathematical point of view, turning on the gauge degrees of freedom in the superpotential leads to a theory which effectively couples Donaldson-Thomas theory
[
26
,
25
]
to geometric degrees of freedom (the metric and complex structure). The procedure of integrating out geometric modes as outlined in this paper effectively couples Donaldson-Thomas theory to background moduli, and the remaining geometric modes. It is interesting to study how this affects the properties of Donaldson-Thomas invariants, wall-crossing phenomena, and others. On a more speculative level, the modes coming down at infinite distance in complex structure moduli space should also play a role in understanding the physics behind moduli space compactification.

Finally, the procedure of integrating out modes at large distance as outlined here can also be employed in other theories, such as heterotic
G_{2}
compactifications and M-Theory/F-theory models. In particular, the F-theory superpotential is very similar to the heterotic superpotential, and it would be very interesting to investigate similar avenues in these settings.

Using the complex structure deformation

(B.6)

(B.7)

[

Appendix C
Proving ellipticity of complexes

In this appendix we prove that (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) are elliptic. Let us repeat these complexes here for convenience

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\hat{\cal P}^{(0,2)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}}\hat{\cal P}^{(1,2)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}{\partial}_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(2,1)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(2,0)}\rightarrow 0\>,

(C.1)

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\hat{\cal P}^{(0,3)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(0,2)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(0,1)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}{\partial}_{-}}\hat{\cal P}^{(1,0)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}}\hat{\cal P}^{(2,0)}\xrightarrow{{\partial}_{+}}\hat{\cal P}^{(3,0)}\rightarrow 0\>.

(C.2)

Let us begin with the complex (
C.1
). Using the middle relation of (
4.15
) and the comment below the equation, it is easy to check that this is indeed a differential complex. To prove ellipticity, we again replace
{\partial}
with
\xi
to get the complex

(C.3)

Again, it is easy to check that this is a complex.

To show exactness at the first level, we pick
\alpha\in\hat{\cal P}^{(0,2)}
and insist that the primitive part of
\xi\wedge\alpha
vanishes. Defining
\xi_{\bar{a}}=h_{\bar{a}}^{a}\xi_{a}
, this becomes equivalent to

\xi_{(\bar{a}}\alpha_{\bar{b})}=0\>,

(C.4)

where we view
\alpha
as an ordinary
(0,1)
-form. To see that this implies
\alpha=0
, we work point-wise and pick a frame where
(\xi_{\bar{1}},\xi_{\bar{2}},\xi_{\bar{3}})=(1,0,0)
(which is possible wlog since
\xi\neq 0
).

Showing exactness at the second level is a bit more cumbersome. We pick
\beta\in\hat{\cal P}^{(1,2)}
. Being in the kernel of
\xi_{+}\xi_{-}
means that
\xi\wedge\xi_{-}\beta
is non-primitive, which is equivalent to

(C.5)

for some

. This implies that

(C.6)

But the left-hand side vanishes as
\beta_{b}^{\bar{b}\bar{c}}
is anti-symmetric in
\bar{b}\bar{c}
, which induces
\xi_{[a}\xi_{c]}=0
. It follows that

\xi\wedge h_{\bar{b}}^{a}\xi_{[a}\beta_{b]}^{\bar{b}\bar{c}}{\text{d}}z^{b}=0\>,

(C.7)

which implies

h_{\bar{b}}^{a}\xi_{[a}\beta_{b]}^{\bar{b}\bar{c}}=\tfrac{1}{2}h_{\bar{b}}^{a}\xi_{a}\beta_{b}^{\bar{b}\bar{c}}=\xi_{b}w^{\bar{c}}

(C.8)

for some

. Primitivity of
\beta
implies that

(C.9)

\beta=h^{-1}\wedge w+\tilde{\beta}\>,

. Recall also that
{\rm Im}(\xi_{+}\xi_{-})
is a sub-space of
{\rm ker}(\xi_{-})

|{\rm ker}(\xi_{-})|=3
and hence

, so they are the same space, i.e., we have exactness at the third level as well.

To show exactness at the last level, note that for

(C.11)

as long as we pick

Showing that the complex (
4.29

replaced by

(C.12)

As
\xi_{+}=\xi
on
\hat{\cal P}^{(p,0)}
, the complex is exact at the last two levels. A similar observation implies the complex is exact at the first two levels. At the third level, if

and

\xi_{+}\xi_{-}\alpha=0\>,

(C.13)

this can only happen if
\xi_{-}\alpha=0
, which implies
\alpha\in{\rm Im}(\xi_{-})
by exactness of the
\xi_{-}
-complex on
\hat{\cal P}^{(0,q)}
forms. This is similar to how one argues ellipticity of the ordinary
{\text{d}}^{\dagger}

for some appropriately chosen
