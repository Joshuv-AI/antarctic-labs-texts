Cholesky-like Preconditioner for Hodge Laplacians via Heavy Collapsible Subcomplex Thanks: Submitted to the editors on DATE.
Anton Savostianov, Francesco Tudisco, Nicola Guglielmi

Abstract
Techniques based on k k -th order Hodge Laplacian operators L k L_{k} are widely used to describe the topology as well as the governing dynamics of high-order systems modeled as simplicial complexes. In all of them, it is required to solve a number of least square problems with L k L_{k} as coefficient matrix, for example in order to compute some portions of the spectrum or integrate the dynamical system. In this work, we introduce the notion of optimal collapsible subcomplex and we present a fast combinatorial algorithm for the computation of a sparse Cholesky-like preconditioner for L k L_{k} that exploits the topological structure of the simplicial complex. The performance of the preconditioner is tested for conjugate gradient method for least square problems (CGLS) on a variety of simplicial complexes with different dimensions and edge densities. We show that, for sparse simplicial complexes, the new preconditioner reduces significantly the condition number of L k L_{k} and performs better than the standard incomplete Cholesky factorization.

Introduction

Graph models of network systems are ubiquitous throughout the sciences. However, a fast-growing line of work in modern network science has highlighted the importance of higher-order models that take directly into account multi-agent interactions rather than classical graph pairwise interactions.
Simplicial complexes are a popular higher-order generalization of graphs that allows modeling non-diadic interactions by means of a sequence of nested nodal simplicies. Given the nested structure of simplicial complexes, boundary operators
B_{k}
, mapping each simplex to its border, and the associated higher-order Laplacian operators
L_{k}
,
[
18
]
, provide a fundamental description of the topology of the system. For example, one may show the convergence of
L_{k}
in the thermodynamic limit to Hodge Laplacians on manifolds,
[
4
,
5
]
; the elements of the kernel of the higher-order Laplacian operators correspond to
k
-dimensional holes in the complex (connected components, one-dimensional holes, voids, etc.); the positive part of the spectrum of
L_{k}
can be used to measure the topological stability of the system,
[
13
]
; the corresponding eigenvectors provide information about higher-order topological features
[
8
,
11
]
and can be used to describe the underlying dynamics, e.g. in case of synchronization or higher-order diffusion,
[
9
,
28
]
.
Computing (part of) the spectrum of
L_{k}
with standard iterative methods heavily requires efficient solving a number of linear systems of the form
L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
, see e.g.
[
7
]
. Moreover, the higher-order system dynamics, for example in the case of random walks, PageRank, and opinion spreading,
[
23
,
24
]
, are primarily defined in terms of the same type of linear systems. While it was observed in
[
6
]
that for specifically structured simplicial complexes one can efficiently solve the linear system
L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
directly, e.g. when the complex can be embedded in a triangulation of a unit sphere in three dimensions, attempting to extend that approach to arbitrary complex significantly increases the complexity of the direct solver,
[
3
]
.

In order to speed up the solution of linear systems with higher-order Laplacians via iterative methods, in this work we propose a sparse and computationally efficient Choelsky-like preconditioner for
L_{k}
based on the notion of weak collapsible complex.
In the case of classical Laplacian
L_{0}
, one can build a sparse stochastic Cholesky preconditioner based on the underlying graph structure,
[
16
]
. By investigating the structure of the Schur complements forming the Cholesky factorization method, we observe that the same construction transfers to the higher-order setting only for simplicial complexes with a specific weakly collapsible topological structure
[
29
]
. As a result, we propose an algorithm that builds a fast lower-triangular preconditioner based on the optimal collapsible subcomplex. While we present the problem and the main ideas in the general setting, we will focus on the case
k=1
when designing the algorithm and testing its efficiency.

2
Simplicial complex and Homology groups

A
simplicial complex

\mathcal{K}
on the vertices
\{v_{1},v_{2}\ldots v_{n}\}
is a collection of simplices
\sigma
, sets of nodes with the property that all the subsets of
\sigma
are simplicies of
\mathcal{K}
too.
We refer to a simplex made out of
k
nodes
\sigma=[v_{i_{1}},\ldots v_{i_{k+1}}]
as being of order
k
, and write
\dim\sigma=k
; the set of all the simplices of order
k
in the complex
\mathcal{K}
is denoted by
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
. Thus,
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
are the vertices of
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
are edges between pairs of vertices,
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
triangles connecting three vertices, and so on. We let
m_{k}=|\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})|
denote the cardinality of
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
.

Each set of simplices
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})=\left\{\sigma_{1},\dots\sigma_{m_{k}}\right\}
induces a linear space of formal sums over the simplicies
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}\mid\alpha_{i}\in\mathds{R}\right\}
referred to as
chain space
; in particular,
C_{0}(\mathcal{K})
is known as the space of vertex states and
C_{1}(\mathcal{K})
as the space of edge flows. Simplices of different orders are related through the boundaries operators
\partial_{k}
mapping the simplex to its boundary; formally,
\partial_{k}:C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\mapsto C_{k-1}(\mathcal{K})
is defined through the alternating sum:

\partial_{k}[v_{1},v_{2},\ldots v_{k}]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}[v_{1},\ldots v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\ldots v_{k}]

By fixing an ordering for
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
we can fix a canonical basis for
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
and represent each boundary operator as a matrix
B_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m_{k-1}\times m_{k}}
with exactly
k
nonzero entries per each column, being either
+1
or
-1
. For these matrices, the fundamental property of topology holds:
the boundary of the boundary is zero
,
[
18
, Thm. 5.7]
:

B_{k}B_{k+1}=0

(1)

The matrix representation
B_{k}
of the boundary operator
\partial_{k}
requires fixing an ordering of the simplices in
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{V}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K})
. As it will be particularly relevant for the purpose of this work, we emphasize that we order triangles and edges as follows: triangles in
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
are oriented in such a way that the first edge (in terms of the ordering of
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
) in each triangle is positively acted upon by
B_{2}
, i.e. the first non-zero entry in each column of
B_{2}
is
+1
,
Figure

1
.

Figure 1
:
Example of the simplicial complex with ordering and orientation: nodes from
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
in orange, triangles from
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
in blue. Orientation of edges and triangles is shown by arrows; the action of
B_{2}
operator is given for both triangles.

The following definitions introduce the fundamental concepts of
k
-th homology group and
k
-th order Laplacian. See
[
18
]
e.g. for more details.

Definition 1
(Homology group and higher-order Laplacian).

Since
\im B_{k+1}\subset\ker B_{k}
, the quotient space
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\nicefrac{{\ker B_{k}}}{{\im B_{k+1}}}
, known as
k
-th homology group, is correctly defined and the following isomorphisms hold

\mathcal{H}_{k}\cong\ker B_{k}\cap\ker B_{k+1}^{\top}\cong\ker\left(B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}+B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}\right).

The matrix
L_{k}=B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}+B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}
is called the
k
-th order
graph Laplacian
; the two terms
L_{k}^{\downarrow}=B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}
and
L_{k}^{\uparrow}=B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}
are referred to as the
down-Laplacian
and the
up-Laplacian
, respectively.

The homology group
\mathcal{H}_{k}
describes the
k
-th topology of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
:
\beta_{k}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{k}=\dim\ker L_{k}
coincides exactly with the number of
k
-dimensional holes in the complex, known as the

k
-th Betti number
. In the case
k=0
, the operator
L_{0}=L_{0}^{\uparrow}
is exactly the classical graph Laplacian whose kernel corresponds to the
connected components
of the graph, while
L_{0}^{\downarrow}=0
. For
k=1
and
k=2
, the elements of
\ker L_{1}
and
\ker L_{2}
describe the simplex 1-dimensional holes and voids respectively, and are frequently used in the analysis of trajectory data,
[
23
,
1
]
.

Although more frequently found in their purely combinatorial form, the definitions of simplicial complexes, homology groups, and higher-order Laplacians admit a generalization to the weighted case. For the sake of generality, in the rest of the work, we use the following notion of weighted boundary operators (and thus weighted simplicial complexes), as considered in e.g.
[
13
]
.

Definition 2
(Weighted and normalised boundary matrices).

For
weight functions

w_{k}:\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}\cup\{0\}
, define the diagonal weight matrix
W_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m_{k}\times m_{k}}
as
(W_{k})_{ii}=\sqrt{w_{k}(\sigma_{i})}
. Then the weighting scheme for the boundary operators upholding the Hodge algebras (
1
) is given by:

B_{k}\mapsto W_{k-1}^{-1}B_{k}W_{k}

(2)

Note that, with the weighting scheme
Equation

2
, the dimensionality of the homology group is preserved,
\dim\ker L_{k}=\dim\ker\widehat{L}_{k}
,
[
13
]
as well as the fundamental property of topology
Equation

1
. For the sake of simplicity, in the following we will omit writing the weighting matrices explicitly and will write
B_{k}
to denote a generic possibly weighted boundary operator
B_{k}=W_{k-1}^{-1}B_{k}W_{k}
.

3
Linear systems

Note that
k
-th order Laplacians
L_{k}
,
k>0
, are matrices with a large number of zero entries since
m_{k}\ll m_{k-1}^{2}
asymptotically (e.g. the number of triangles
m_{2}
is always bounded by
m_{1}^{3/2}
). In particular, in analogy with the standard
k=0
case, we say that the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
and the corresponding Laplacians
L_{k}
as sparse if
m_{k}=\mathcal{O}(m_{k-1}\log m_{k-1})
, that is the number of simplices of higher order is comparable up to a constant times the number of simplices of lower order times its logarithm.
This is, for example, the case for structured simplicies such as trees for the classical Laplacian, or triangulations for
L_{1}
; similarly, one can show the existence of a sparser simplicial complex approximating the original one with the
m_{k}=\mathcal{O}(m_{k-1}\log m_{k-1})
sparsity pattern,
[
21
,
25
]
.

The spectrum of the
k
-th order Laplacian defines the
k
-th topology of the complex
\mathcal{K}
. In most applications, one is actually interested in just a few nonzero eigenvalues, possibly in the leftmost or the rightmost part of the spectrum
[
12
,
13
,
22
]
.
For large and sparse
simplicial complexes, the methods of choice for the computation of these eigenspaces are iterative eigensolvers that require solving a (possibly large) number of linear systems (least-squares problems) of the form
L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
, for suitable
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}\bot\ker L_{k}
.

The following elementary result shows that solving
L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
can be brought down to the solution of
L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
. We provide the proof for completeness.

Theorem 3
(Joint
k
-Laplacian solver).

The linear system
L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
can be reduced to a sequence of consecutive least square problems for isolated up-Laplacians. Precisely,
\boldsymbol{x}
is a solution of system (
3
),

L_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}\perp\ker L_{k}

(3)

if and only if it can be written as
\boldsymbol{x}=B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
, where:

\!\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{z}}{\argmin}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{z}-B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\right\|,\quad\!\!\boldsymbol{u}=\underset{\boldsymbol{z}}{\argmin}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{z}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|,\quad\!\!\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\underset{\boldsymbol{y}}{\argmin}\left\|L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\right\|

and
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
with
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{z}}{\argmin}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{z}-B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}\right\|
.

Proof.

For the
k
-th order Hodge Laplacian
L_{k}
, it holds (see
[
18
]
e.g.):

\mathds{R}^{m_{k-1}}=\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\overbrace{\phantom{\im B_{k}^{\top}\oplus\ker L_{k}}}^{\ker B_{k+1}^{\top}}$\hss}\im B_{k}^{\top}\oplus\underbrace{\ker L_{k}\oplus\im B_{k+1}}_{\ker B_{k}}\,.

(4)

Thus,
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}+B_{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}
for some
\boldsymbol{u}
,
\boldsymbol{v}
. Then, the system
L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
is equivalent to:

\begin{cases}L_{k}^{\downarrow}B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\\
L_{k}^{\uparrow}B_{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}=B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}B_{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\\
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\boldsymbol{f}_{2}=\boldsymbol{f}\end{cases}

where
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
are the Hodge decomposition of the right hand side
\boldsymbol{f}
, with
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\in\im B_{k}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\in\im B_{k+1}
. Then
\boldsymbol{f}=B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}+B_{k+1}\boldsymbol{z}_{2}
and, after multiplication by
B_{k}
,
B_{k}B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}\iff\min_{\boldsymbol{z}_{1}}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}-B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}\right\|
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}=\boldsymbol{f}-B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}
.

Finally, we note that equation
B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}B_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\iff(L_{k-1}^{\uparrow})^{2}\boldsymbol{u}=B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
can be solved by two consecutive LS-problems:

\min_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-B_{k}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\right\|,\qquad\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left\|L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|

which corresponds to the solution of the down part of the original system.

Theorem

3
reduces the problem of solving a linear system (least-squares problem) for the entire Laplacian matrix
L_{k}
to the problem of solving a system for the up-Laplacian
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
. Thus, in the next sections, we propose an efficient combinatorial Cholesky-like preconditioner for
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
.

4
Efficient solver for the up-Laplacian

When the up-Laplacian least-square problem
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{f}\|
is solved with standard fast iterative methods such as CGLS,
[
2
,
14
]
, the convergence rate and overall behavior of the solver highly depend on the condition number of
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
, defined as

\kappa(L_{k}^{\uparrow})=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(L_{k}^{\uparrow})}{\sigma_{\min}(L_{k}^{\uparrow})}\,,

where
\sigma_{\max}
and
\sigma_{\min}
are the largest and smallest positive singular values of
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
.

In order to reduce
\kappa(L_{k}^{\uparrow})
, we move from
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{f}\|
to the symmetrically preconditioned system

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\left(C^{+}L_{k}^{\uparrow}(C^{+})^{\top}\right)\left(C^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right)-C^{+}\boldsymbol{f}\|

where
C^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
C
, and
C
is chosen so that
(a) the transition between unconditioned and preconditioned systems is bijective,
(b) the matrix
C^{+}L_{k}^{\uparrow}(C^{+})^{\top}
is better conditioned than the initial
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
, and (c) the pseudo-inverse of the preconditioner
C
can be efficiently implemented.
In particular, a cheap
matvec
of the pseudo-inverse
C^{+}\boldsymbol{f}
is guaranteed in the case of
C
being a lower-triangular matrix. This is the case, for example, for the
Cholesky
and the
incomplete Cholesky
preconditioners
[
10
,
15
,
20
]
.

Following this line of approach, a variety of techniques have been developed for preconditioning the Laplacian matrix
L_{0}^{\uparrow}=L_{0}
. In particular, one of the most prominent approaches is based on the celebrated spectral sparsification result by Spielman and Srivastava,
[
25
]
. This result has been recently extended to the higher-order Laplacians,
[
21
]
. However, while the step from sparsification to preconditioner is well understood in the
k=0
case, when
k>0
, efficiently computing an effective preconditioner from a sparsified simplicial complex is not trivial.

In the next subsection, we review how a sparse approximate Cholesky preconditioner is computed from the spectral sparsifier in the graph case. We then highlight the difficulties for
k>0
and will devote the rest of the paper to design of an efficient approach for
k=1
.

4.1
Schur Complements and Cholesky preconditioner for
k=0

We start with a useful observation about the structure of the Gaussian elimination process to form the Cholesky factor. Let
A
be a real symmetric positive definite matrix. Then its
Schur complements

S_{i}
obtained in the process of Cholesky factorization via Gaussian elimination can be defined recursively as follows: Let
\delta_{i}
be the
i
-th canonical vector,
(\delta_{i})_{i}=1
and
(\delta_{i})_{j}=0
,
i\neq j
, then set
S_{0}=A
and for
i=1,2,3,\dots

\displaystyle

\displaystyle S_{i}=S_{i-1}-\frac{1}{\delta_{i}^{\top}S_{i-1}\delta_{i}}S_{i-1}\delta_{i}\delta_{i}^{\top}S_{i-1}^{\top}

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{i}^{\top}S_{i-1}\delta_{i}}}S_{i-1}\delta_{i}

With these definitions, the Cholesky factor
C
such that
A=CC^{\top}
is formed by the columns
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
, namely

C=[\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{c}_{2}\,\ldots\boldsymbol{c}_{m}].

Using induction, one can directly verify that
C
defined above is indeed lower triangular. Moreover, since
S_{i}-S_{i-1}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}
and
S_{m}=0
, we have
A=S_{0}=S_{0}-S_{m}=\sum(S_{i}-S_{i-1})=\sum\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}=CC^{\top}
.

The case of classical Laplacian
L_{0}
is uniquely suited for Schur complements: indeed, in the case
k=0
, each Schur complement
S_{i}
has the form
S_{i}=H_{i}+K_{i}
where both
H_{i}
and
K_{i}
are graph Laplacians on the same set of vertices.
By this key observation, one can construct a stochastic Cholesky preconditioner for
L_{0}
: each
i
th step of Gauss elimination substitutes the dense Laplacian
K_{i}
with a sparser Laplacian on a subgraph around the vertex
i
,
[
16
]
, to produce fast and sparse approximate Cholesky multiplier.
This property unfortunately holds only for
L_{0}
and does not carry over to
L_{k}
for
k>0
, as we demonstrate in the next subsection.

4.2
The structure of the Schur complements
S_{i}
for
k=1

One of the keys to the decomposition
S_{i}=H_{i}+K_{i}
for the Schur complements of
L_{0}
is the decomposition
L_{0}=\sum_{e}L_{0}(e)
, where the sum goes over all the edges of the graph and
L_{0}(e)
is the rank-1 graph Laplacian corresponding to the edge
e
,
L_{0}(e)=w_{1}(e)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}
with
\boldsymbol{e}
being the corresponding incidence column from the operator
W_{0}^{-1}B_{1}
.
One can directly show an analogous decomposition into rank-1 terms for any up-Laplacian:

Lemma 4
(Rank-1 decomposition of
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
).

For a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, the following decomposition into rank-1 up-Laplacian terms holds for any
L_{k}^{\uparrow}
:

L_{k}^{\uparrow}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})}L_{k}^{\uparrow}(t)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})}w_{k}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}

where, for each
t\in\mathcal{V}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
,
L_{k}^{\uparrow}(t)=w_{k}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}
is the rank-1 up-Laplacian for each triangle
t
such that (a)
w_{k}(t)=[W_{k}^{2}]_{tt}
is the weight of the simplex
t
, and (b)
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
is the vector obtained as the action of the boundary operator
W_{k}^{-1}B_{k}
on
t
, i.e. the
t
-th column of the matrix
W_{k}^{-1}B_{k}
.

Using
Lemma

4
, we obtain the following characterization for the first Schur complement
S_{1}
for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
:

Lemma 5
(First Schur complement
S_{1}
for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
).

For the up-Laplacian
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
, the following derivation of the first Schur complement holds:

S_{1}=\sum_{t\mid 1\notin t}w_{2}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}+\frac{1}{2\Omega_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{2}(t_{1})w_{2}(t_{2})\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\big]\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\big]^{\top}

where
\Omega_{1}
is the total weight of all triangles adjacent to the edge
1
,
\Omega_{1}=\sum_{t\mid 1\in t}w_{2}(t)
.

Proof.

Following
Equation

5
, one needs to compute the constant
\delta_{1}^{\top}S_{0}\delta_{1}
and the rank-1 matrix
S_{0}\delta_{1}\delta_{1}^{\top}S_{0}
where
S_{0}=L_{1}^{\uparrow}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{w_{1}(1)}}\mathds{1}_{1\in t}
, the indicator of the triangle
t
having the edge
1
, since
1
is necessarily the first (in the chosen order) edge in the triangle; hence,
\sum_{t}w_{2}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{w_{1}(1)}}\sum_{t\mid 1\in t}w_{2}(t)
:

\displaystyle\delta_{1}^{\top}S_{0}\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}^{\top}\left(\sum_{t}w_{2}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\right)\delta_{1}=\sum_{t}w_{2}(t)\left(\delta_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{w_{1}(1)}\sum_{t\mid 1\in t}w_{2}(t)=\frac{\Omega_{\{1\}\mid\varnothing}}{w_{1}(1)}

\displaystyle S_{0}\delta_{1}\delta_{1}^{\top}S_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{1},t_{2}}w_{2}(t_{1})w_{2}(t_{2})\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}^{\top}\delta_{1}\delta_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}=\frac{1}{w_{1}(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{2}(t_{1})w_{2}(t_{2})\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}\,.

By symmetry,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w(t_{1})w(t_{2})\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w(t_{1})w(t_{2})\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}^{\top}\right),

one can note by a straightforward arithmetic transformation that

\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}^{\top}=-\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}]\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}]^{\top}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}^{\top}

Finally,
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w(t_{1})w(t_{2})\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}^{\top}=\Omega_{1}\sum_{t\mid 1\in t}w(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}
.

The structure of the first Schur complement
S_{1}
is reminiscent of the classical graph Laplacian case as
S_{1}
is formed by the sum of two terms:

H_{1}=\sum_{t\mid 1\notin t}w_{2}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top},\qquad K_{1}=\frac{1}{2\Omega_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\mid 1\in t_{1}\\
t_{2}\mid 1\in t_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{2}(t_{1})w_{2}(t_{2})\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\big]\big[\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}\big]^{\top}.

As in the
k=0
case, the term
H_{1}
, which corresponds to the portion of the original
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
not adjacent to the edge being eliminated (edge
1
), is an up-Laplacian. The remaining term of the original
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
which consists of up-Laplacians adjacent to edge
1
, is transformed here into the matrix
K_{1}
, which we refer to as the
cyclic term
. Unfortunately, unlike the
0
-Laplacian case, it is easy to realize that the cyclic term
K_{1}
is generally not an up-Laplacian. We show this with a simple illustrative example below.

Example 6
.

Assume the simplicial complex formed by two triangles,
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})=\{1,2,3,4\}
,
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})=\{12,13,14,23,24\}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})=\{123,124\}
, adjacent by the first edge
12

. Then, vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
and
K_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&1&1&-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&1&1&-1\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
. Note that

is denser than
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
and has lost the structural balance of

:
\diag K_{1}=K_{1}\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
where
\diag L_{1}^{\uparrow}=L_{1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{1}=\left(\Omega_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{m_{2}}
.

Further computation of Schur complements
S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}\dots
unavoidably introduces a fastly growing number of similar cyclic terms.
Thus, to build an efficient preconditioner for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
, one needs to deal with the cyclic terms arising on each step

.

To this end, note that
K_{1}
is zero if and only if there is a unique triangle

adjacent to the edge

. Maintaining this property for all Schur complements is equivalent to the concept of weak collapsibility which we introduce next.

5
Collapsibility of a Simplicial Complex

In this section we borrow the terminology from
[
29
]
to introduce the concept of collapsibility and then weak collapsibility of a simplicial complex.
The simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
is
free
if it is a face of exactly one simplex
\tau=\tau(\sigma)\in\mathcal{K}
of higher order (maximal face). The
collapse

\mathcal{K}\backslash\{\sigma\}
of
\mathcal{K}
at a free simplex
\sigma
is the operation of reducing
\mathcal{K}
to
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}-\sigma-\tau
; namely, this is the operation of removing a simplex
\tau
having an accessible (not included in another simplex) face
\sigma
.
A sequence of collapses done at the simplicies
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots\}
is called a
collapsing sequence
; formally:

Definition 7
(Collapsing sequence).

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a simplicial complex. We say that
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots\}
is a
collapsing sequence
for
\mathcal{K}
if
\sigma_{1}
is free in
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}^{(1)}
and each
\sigma_{i}
,
i>1
, is free at

\mathcal{K}^{(i)}=\mathcal{K}^{(i-1)}\backslash\{\sigma_{i}\}
. The resulting complex
\mathcal{L}
obtained collapsing
\mathcal{K}
at
\Sigma
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}\backslash\Sigma
.

Note that, by definition, every collapsing sequence
\Sigma
has a corresponding sequence
\mathds{T}=\{\tau(\sigma_{1}),\tau(\sigma_{2}),\ldots\}
of maximal faces being collapsed at every step. The notion of
collapsible simplicial complex
is defined in
[
29
]
as follows

Definition 8
(Collapsible simplicial complex).

The simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is collapsible if there exists a collapsing sequence
\Sigma
such that
\mathcal{K}
collapses to a single vertex, i.e.
\mathcal{K}\backslash\Sigma=\{v\}
for some
v\in\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
.

While least square problems with collapsible simplicial complexes can be solved directly in an efficient way,
[
6
]
, collapsibility is a strong requirement for a simplicial complex. In fact, determining whether the complex is collapsible is in general
NP-complete
,
[
27
]
, even though it an be almost linear for a specific set of families of
\mathcal{K}
,
[
6
]
. Moreover, simplicial complexes are rarely collapsible, as we discuss in the following.

Next, we recall the concept of a
d
-Core,
[
26
]
:

Definition 9
(
d
-Core).

A
d
-Core is a subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
such that every simplex of dimension
d-1
belongs to at least
2

d
-simplices.

So, for example, a
2
-Core of a
2
-skeleton
\mathcal{K}
, is a subcomplex of the original complex
\mathcal{K}
such that every edge from
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
belongs to at least
2
triangles from
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
. Finally, we say that
\mathcal{K}
is
d
-collapsible if it can be collapsed only by collapses at simplices
\sigma_{i}
of order smaller than
d
, i.e.
\dim\sigma_{i}\leq d-1
. Then we have the following criterium:

Lemma 10
(
[
19
]
).

\mathcal{K}
is
d
-collapsible if and only if it does not contain any
d
-core.

The
d
-Core is the generalization of the cycle for the case of
1
-collapsibility of a classical graph, and finding a
d
-Core inside a complex
\mathcal{K}
is neither trivial nor computationally cheap. Note that a
d
-Core is in general dense, due to its definition, and does not have a prescribed structure. We illustrate simple exemplary cores in the case of

in

.

Due to the appearance of

tends to contain

-Cores as long as

is denser than the trivially collapsible case.

While collapsibility is a strong requirement, in the next section, we show that a weaker condition is enough to efficiently design a preconditioner for any “sparse enough” simplicial complex in the
2
-skeleton case. Whilst the extension to
k>2
is not hard, a careful reconsideration of necessary modifications in the proof of

5.1
Weak collapsibility

Let the complex
\mathcal{K}
be restricted up to its
2
-skeleton,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})\cup\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})\cup\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
, and assume
\mathcal{K}
is collapsible. Then a collapsing sequence
\Sigma
necessarily involves collapses at simplices
\sigma_{i}
of different orders: at edges (eliminating
edges
and
triangles
) and at vertices (eliminating
vertices
and
edges
). One can show that for a given collapsing sequence
\Sigma
there is a reordering
\tilde{\Sigma}
such that
\dim\tilde{\sigma_{i}}
in the reordered sequence are non-increasing,
[
6
, Lemma 2.5]
. Namely, if such a complex is collapsible, then there is a collapsible sequence
\Sigma=\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{0}\}
where
\Sigma_{1}
contains all the collapses at edges first and
\Sigma_{0}
is composed of collapses at vertices. Note that the partial collapse
\mathcal{K}\backslash\Sigma_{1}=\mathcal{L}
eliminates all the triangles in the complex,
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{L})=\varnothing
; otherwise, the whole sequence
\Sigma
is not collapsing
\mathcal{K}
to a single vertex. Since
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{L})=\varnothing
, the associated up-Laplacian
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})=0
.

Definition 11
(Weakly collapsible complex).

A simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
restricted to its
2
-skeleton is called
weakly collapsible
, if there exists a collapsing sequence
\Sigma_{1}
such that the simplicial complex
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}\backslash\Sigma_{1}
has no simplices of order
2
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{L})=\varnothing
and
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})=0
.

Note that while a collapsible complex is necessarily weakly collapsible, the opposite does not hold. Consider the following example in

[3,4]
or at
[2,4]
. After this, the only available collapse is at the vertex
[4]
leaving the uncollapsible

6
Preconditioning through the Subsampling of the
2
-Core

In
Section

4
we have shown that the efficient computation of the Cholesky multiplier for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
is complicated by arising cyclic terms. At the same time, the absence of the cyclic terms in the Schur complements is a characteristic of weakly collapsible complexes.
Using this observation, in this section, we develop a Cholesky-like preconditioning scheme based on an efficient Cholesky multiplier for weakly collapsible complexes.

First, we demonstrate that a weakly collapsible simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
immediately yields an exact Cholesky decomposition for its up-Laplacian:

Lemma 13
.

Assume the 2-skeleton simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is weakly collapsible through the collapsing sequence
\Sigma
. Let
\mathds{T}
be the corresponding sequence of maximal faces and let
P_{\Sigma}
and
P_{\mathds{T}}
be the permutation matrices of the two sequences, i.e. such that
\left[P_{\Sigma}\right]_{ij}=1\iff j=\sigma_{i}
, and similarly for
P_{\mathds{T}}
. Then
C=P_{\Sigma}B_{2}P_{\mathds{T}}
is an exact Cholesky mulitplier for
P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})P_{\Sigma}^{\top}
, i.e.

P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})P_{\Sigma}^{\top}=CC^{\top}
.

Proof.

Note that the sequences
\Sigma
and
\mathds{T}
(and the multiplication by the corresponding permutation matrices) impose only the reordering of
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
, respectively; after such reordering the
i
-th edge collapses the
i
-triangle. Hence, the first
(i-1)
entries of the
i
-th column of the matrix
B_{2}
(
\left[B_{2}\right]_{:,i}=\sqrt{w(t_{i})}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}
) are
0
, otherwise one of the previous edges is not free. As a result,
C
is lower-triangular and by a direct computation one has
CC^{\top}=P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})P_{\Sigma}^{\top}
.

An arbitrary simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is generally not weakly collapsible (see
Figure

2
). Specifically, weak collapsibility is a property of sparse simplicial complexes with the sparsity being measured by the number of triangles
m_{2}
(in the weakly collapsible case
m_{2}<m_{1}

since each collapse at the edge eliminates exactly one triangle); hence, the removal of triangles from
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
can potentially destroy the 2-Core structure inside
\mathcal{K}
and make the complex weakly collapsible.

With this observation, in order to find a cheap and effective preconditioner for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})
, one may search for a weakly collapsible subcomplex
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
and use its exact Cholesky multiplier
C
as a preconditioner for
\mathcal{K}
.

Specifically, such subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
should satisfy the following properties:

(1)

it has the same set of nodes and edges,
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
;

(2)

triangles in
\mathcal{L}
are subsampled,
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
;

(3)

\mathcal{L}
is weakly collapsible through some collapsing sequence
\Sigma
and corresponding sequence of maximal faces
\mathds{T}
;

(4)

\mathcal{L}
has the same 1-homology as
\mathcal{K}
, that is
\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{K})=\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{L})
;

(5)

the Cholesky multiplier
C=P_{\Sigma}B_{2}(\mathcal{L})P_{\mathds{T}}
improves the condition number of
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})
, namely
\kappa_{+}(C^{+}P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})P_{\Sigma}^{\top}(C^{+})^{\top})\ll\kappa_{+}(L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K}))
.

Let us comment on the conditions above. Conditions (1) and (2) are automatically met when a subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
is obtained from
\mathcal{K}
through the elimination of triangles. Condition (3) is a structural requirement on
\mathcal{L}
and can be guaranteed by the design of the subcomplex using the proposed
Algorithm

2
. Condition (4) guarantees that the preconditioning strategy is bijective, as we show in the next
Lemma

14
.
Finally, condition (5) asks for a better condition number and is checked numerically in
Section

7
. However, whilst one can not guarantee improvement in preconditioning quality, we can provide an explicit formula for the condition number
\kappa_{+}(C^{+}P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})P_{\Sigma}^{\top}(C^{+})^{\top})
assuming an arbitrary subcomplex forms the preconditioner
C
. We provide such a formula in the next
Section

6.1
and discuss how to use it to construct a preconditioner via heavy collapsible subcomplex.

Lemma 14
(On the conservation of the 1-homology red and condition (4)).

For any
\mathcal{L}
subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
, the following statements about 1-homology hold:

(i)

if
\mathcal{L}
satisfies conditions (1) and (2) then it can only extend the kernel, i.e. we have
\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{L})
;

(ii)

if additionally
\mathcal{L}
satisfies condition (4), then the
preconditioning scheme is bijective, in the sense that the solution to the original least square problem
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{f}\|
and the one preconditioned with the Cholesky factor of
\mathcal{L}
coincide.

Proof.

For (i) it is sufficient to note that the elimination of the triangle
t\in\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
lifts the restriction
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=0
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{K})
; hence, if
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{K})
, then
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{L})
. For (ii), note that bijection between the systems
L_{k}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}
and
\left(C^{+}L_{k}^{\uparrow}(C^{+})^{\top}\right)C^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=C^{+}\boldsymbol{f}
is guaranteed by
\ker C^{\top}=\ker L_{k}^{\uparrow}=\ker B_{k+1}^{\top}
(assuming
\boldsymbol{x}\perp\ker L_{k}^{\uparrow}
). Then, by
the spectral inheritance principle,
[
13
, Thm. 2.7]
,

\ker L_{k}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{X})=\ker L_{k}(\mathcal{X})\oplus B_{k}^{\top}\cdot\im L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}
. The second part,
B_{k}^{\top}\cdot\im L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}
, consists of the action of
B_{k}^{\top}
on non-zero related eigenvectors of
L_{k-1}^{\uparrow}
and is not dependent on
\mathcal{V}_{k+1}(\mathcal{K})
(triangles, in case
k=1
), hence remains preserved in the subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
. Since by condition (4)
\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{K})=\ker L_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, the same statement holds for up-Laplacians,
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})=\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})
. Since
C
is an exact Cholesky multiplier for
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})
,
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})=\ker C^{\top}
and
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})=\ker B_{2}^{\top}
yielding
\ker C^{\top}=\ker B_{2}^{\top}
and bijectivity.

6.1
Preconditioning quality by the Subcomplex

Note that subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
is fully defined by the subset
\mathds{T}
of subsampled triangles,
\mathds{T}\subset\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
, so
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})\cup\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})\cup\mathds{T}
). We introduce the following matrix notation corresponding to the subsampling:

Definition 15
(Subsampling matrix).

Let
\mathds{T}
be a subset of triangles,
\mathds{T}\subset\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
, then
\Pi
is a subsampling matrix if

•

\Pi
is diagonal,
\Pi\in\mathds{R}^{m_{2}\times m_{2}}
;

•

(\Pi)_{ii}=1\iff i\in\mathds{T}
; otherwise,
(\Pi)_{ii}=0
.

Assuming the subset
\mathds{T}
of subsampled triangles (or, equivalently, the subsampling matrix
\Pi
) is given, one needs only the triangle weight matrix
\widehat{W}
in order to obtain
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})
and the corresponding Cholesky multiplier
C
.

Generally speaking, (squared) weights
\widehat{W}_{2}^{2}
of sampled triangles
\mathds{T}
may differ from the original weights
W_{2}^{2}
. Let
\widehat{W}_{2}^{2}=W_{2}^{2}+\Delta W_{2}
, where
\Delta W_{2}
is still diagonal, but entries are not necessarily positive. Then one can formulate the question of the optimal weight redistribution as the optimization problem:

\min_{\Delta W_{2}}\left\|L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})-L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})\right\|=\min_{\Delta W_{2}}\left\|B_{2}\left[\Pi(W_{2}^{2}+\Delta W_{2})\Pi-W_{2}^{2}\right]B_{2}^{\top}\right\|

Here and only here, since we manipulate weights, we use the unweighted
B_{2}
matrix so one can have explicit access to the weight matrix
W_{2}
.

Lemma 16
(Optimal weight choice for the subcomplex).

Let
\mathcal{L}
be subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
with fixed corresponding subsampling matrix
\Pi
. Then the optimal weight perturbation for the subsampled triangles is

\Delta W_{2}\equiv 0,

so the best choice of weights of subcomplex is
\widehat{W}_{2}=W_{2}\Pi
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta W_{2}=\Delta W_{2}(t)
where
t
is a time parametrization; we can compute the gradient
\nabla_{\Delta W_{2}}\sigma_{1}\left(L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})-L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})\right)
through the derivative
\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{1}\left(L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})-L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{K})\right)
:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B_{2}\Pi\dot{\Delta W_{2}}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=\left\langle B_{2}\Pi\dot{\Delta W_{2}}\Pi B_{2}^{\top},\,\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(B_{2}\Pi\dot{\Delta W_{2}}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right)=

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B_{2}\Pi,\,\dot{\Delta W_{2}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla_{\Delta W_{2}}\sigma_{1},\dot{\Delta W_{2}}\right\rangle

By projecting onto the diagonal structure of the weight perturbation,

\nabla_{\diag\Delta W_{2}}\,\sigma_{1}=\diag\left(\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B_{2}\Pi\right).

Note that
\diag\left(\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B_{2}\Pi\right)_{ii}=|\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|^{2}_{i}
; then the stationary point is characterized by
\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=0\iff\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}
. The latter is impossible since
\boldsymbol{x}
is the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue; hence, since
\Pi(W_{2}^{2}+\Delta W_{2})\Pi\neq W_{2}^{2}
, the optimal perturbation is
\Delta W_{2}\equiv 0
.

We have established the optimal choice of the weights provided the subsampling matrix
\Pi
. As a result, assuming
B_{2}
is weighted, optimal
L_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{L})=B_{2}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}
. Now we proceed to characterize the quality of the preconditioning in terms of the matrix
\Pi
in
Theorem

18
, starting with a necessary technical relation between
\im B_{2}^{\top}
and
\ker\Pi
.

Remark 17
.

Knowing the optimal weight for sampled triangles from
Lemma

16
, one needs to preserve the kernel of subsampled Laplacian

\ker\left(B_{2}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\right)=\ker\left(B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}\right)

to form a correct preconditioner
C
.
Since we have
\Pi=\Pi^{2}
and
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}=\ker B_{2}^{\top}
, then
\ker\left(B_{2}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\right)=\ker\left(\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\right)
. Additionally,
\ker B_{2}^{\top}\subseteq\ker\left(\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\right)
, so
\ker\left(B_{2}\Pi B_{2}^{\top}\right)\neq\ker\left(B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}\right)\iff
there exists
\boldsymbol{y}\in\im B_{2}^{\top}
such that
B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}\neq 0
and
B_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}\in\ker\Pi
. Then in order to preserve the kernel, one needs

\im B_{2}^{\top}\cap\ker\Pi=\{0\}
.

Theorem 18
(Conditioning by the Subcomplex).

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a weakly collapsible subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
defined by the subsampling matrix
\Pi
and let
C
be a Cholesky multiplier of

defined as in

. Then
the conditioning of the symmetrically preconditioned

is given by:

where
V_{1}
forms the orthonormal basis on
\im B_{2}^{\top}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

16
,
W_{2}(\mathcal{L})=\Pi W_{2}
; then let us consider the lower-triangular preconditioner

for

; then the preconditioned matrix is given by:

\displaystyle C^{+}\left(P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}\right)(C^{+})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(P_{\Sigma}B_{2}\Pi P_{\mathds{T}}\right)^{+}\left(P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}\right)\left(\left(P_{\Sigma}B_{2}\Pi P_{\mathds{T}}\right)^{+}\right)^{\top}=

\displaystyle=P_{\mathds{T}}^{\top}\left(B_{2}\Pi\right)^{+}L_{1}^{\uparrow}\left(\left(B_{2}\Pi\right)^{+}\right)^{\top}P_{\mathds{T}}

Note that
P_{\mathds{T}}
is unitary, so

, hence the principle matrix is

Since

\kappa_{+}\left(\left(B_{2}\Pi\right)^{+}(B_{2})\right)

Let us consider the SVD-decomposition for
B_{2}=USV^{\top}

B_{2}=USV^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}&U_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}&0\\

and

is diagonal and invertible, so

(B_{2}\Pi)^{+}B_{2}=\left(SV^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}SV^{\top}=\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

By the definition of the condition number
\kappa_{+}
, one needs to compute
\sigma^{+}_{\min}
and
\sigma^{+}_{\max}

\sigma^{+}_{\min}=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\perp\ker\left(\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\right)}{\min}\frac{\left\|\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}

\sigma^{+}_{\min}

\sigma^{+}_{\max}
by changing
\min

\max

Note that
\im B_{2}^{\top}=\im V_{1}=\im V_{1}S_{1}

\ker\Pi V_{1}S_{1}\cap\im S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}=\{0\}

\ker\Pi V_{1}S_{1}=\ker\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{\top}=\ker\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}
; as a result,
\ker\left(\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\right)=\ker S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}
. Since

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker V_{1}^{\top}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x}\in\im V_{1}

\sigma^{+}_{\min}=\underset{\boldsymbol{y}}{\min}\frac{\left\|\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}S_{1}\boldsymbol{y}\right\|}{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|}\underset{\boldsymbol{z}=S_{1}\boldsymbol{y}}{=}\underset{\boldsymbol{z}}{\min}\frac{\left\|\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}\boldsymbol{z}\right\|}{\left\|S_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}\right\|}

Note that
\boldsymbol{v}=\left(S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\right)^{+}\boldsymbol{z}\iff\begin{cases}S_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{z}\\

\sigma^{+}_{\min}=\underset{\boldsymbol{v}\perp\ker V_{1}^{\top}\Pi}{\min}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|V_{1}^{\top}\Pi\boldsymbol{v}\|}

Hence
\kappa_{+}\left(C^{+}P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}(C^{+})^{\top}\right)=\kappa_{+}^{2}(V_{1}^{\top}\Pi)=\kappa_{+}^{2}(\Pi V_{1})
.

6.2
Algorithm: Preconditioner via Heavy Collapsible Subcomplex

Following
Theorem

18
, note that

C
is a perfect preconditioner for
P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}

if
\Pi=I
, since
\kappa_{+}(V_{1})=1
and we would compute the exact Cholesky decomposition. However, this is computationally prohibitive.
Thus, it is natural to try to find a sparser
\Pi
so that
\Pi V_{1}
is as close to
V_{1}
as possible. Multiplication by
\Pi
cancels rows in
V_{1}
corresponding to the eliminated triangles; at the same time, rows in
V_{1}
are scaled by the weights of the triangles since
\mathrm{span}V_{1}=W_{2}\im B_{2}^{\top}

\Pi

Based on this observation and

18

which aims to eliminate triangles of the lowest weight thus constructing what we call a heavy weakly collapsible subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
with largest possible total weight of triangles. The exact Cholesky multiplier

is cheap to compute and is used as a preconditioner for

.

The proposed

7
Numerical experiments

We present here a number of numerical experiments to validate the performance of the proposed preconditioning strategy. All the experiments are run using
julia
on Apple M1 CPU and can be reproduced with the code available at
https://github.com/COMPiLELab/HeCS

7.1
Conjugate Gradient Least Square Method

The preconditioning performance for the least square problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\perp\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}}\|L_{1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{f}\|

is measured on the
conjugate gradient least square method
(CGLS),
[
2
,
14
]
. The method requires computing
matvec
operations for the matrix
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
and its preconditioned alternatives; CGLS converges as
(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}(A)}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}(A)}+1)
and we run it until the infinity norm of the residual
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}=L_{1}^{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{f}
falls below a given threshold, i.e.
\|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
.

7.2
Shifted incomplete Cholesky preconditioner

L_{1}^{\uparrow}
is a singular matrix. Assuming
U
is an orthogonal basis of
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}
, one can move to
L_{1}^{\uparrow}\to L_{1}^{\uparrow}+\alpha UU^{\top}
which can be preconditioned by non-singular methods. Specifically, we use
C_{\alpha}=\texttt{ichol}(L_{1}^{\uparrow}+\alpha UU^{\top})
to compare with the
HeCS
preconditioner,
Figure

4
.

Calculating such a shift requires efficiently computing
\ker L_{1}^{\uparrow}
, which in principle has a complexity comparable to the original system. On the other hand, in our setting, given the spectral inheritance principle (see e.g.
[
13
, Thm. 2.7]
), an orthogonal basis
U
can be formed directly using the vectors
B_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in(\bf 1)^{\perp}
, when
\mathcal{K}
has trivial 0- and 1-homologies (i.e. it is formed by one connected component,
\ker L_{0}=\mathrm{span}\{\bf 1\}
, and has no 1-dimensional holes,
\ker L_{1}=0
).

Note that the
HeCS
preconditioner instead works without requiring any triviality of the topology of the complex.

7.3
Problem setting: Enriched triangulation as a simplicial complex

To illustrate the behaviour of the preconditioned system
C^{+}P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}(C^{+})^{\top}
, we consider a sparse simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, i.e. we assume
m_{2}=\mathcal{O}(m_{1}\ln m_{1})
. Note that the developed routine can be applied in denser cases, although one can expect a certain loss of efficiency.
To generate problem settings within this range,
\mathcal{K}
is synthesized as an enriched triangulation of
N
points on the unit square with a prescribed edge sparsity pattern
\nu
as follows:

(1)

\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
is formed by the corners of the unit square and
(N-4)
points sampled uniformly at random from
U\left([0,1]^{2}\right)
;

(2)

the Delaunay triangulation of
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
is computed; all edges and
3
-cliques of the produced graph are included in
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
respectively;

(3)

d\geq 0
edges (excluding the outer boundary) are chosen at random and eliminated from
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
; triangles adjacent to the chosen edges are eliminated from
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
. As result, produced complex
\mathcal{K}
has a non-trivial
1
-homology;

(4)

the sparsity pattern is defined as
\nu=m_{2}/q(m_{1})
where
q(m_{1})=\mathcal{O}(m_{1}\ln m_{1})
is the highest density of triangles for the sparse case;
additional edges on
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
are added to
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
alongside with new appearing
3
-cliques to reach the target
m_{2}/q(m_{1})
value. The initial sparsity pattern of the triangulation is denoted by
\nu_{\Delta}
.

7.4
Heavy subcomplex and triangle weight profile

Algorithm

2
, aims to build a heavy weakly collapsible subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
such that the total weight of triangles in
\mathcal{L}
is close to the total weight of triangles in the original complex
\mathcal{K}
. At the same time, the number of triangles in
\mathcal{L}
is limited,
m_{2}(\mathcal{L})<m_{1}(\mathcal{L})=m_{1}(\mathcal{K})
, due to the weak collapsibility, while the number of triangles in
\mathcal{K}
can go up to
q(m_{1}(\mathcal{K}))
.
Hence, the quality of the preconditioner
is determined by the triangle weight distribution
w(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{V}_{2}(\mathcal{K})
:
namely, if
w_{2}(t)
are distributed uniformly and independently, the quality of the preconditioning falls rapidly after
\nu>\nu_{\Delta}
and, at the same time, the original matrix
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
remains well-conditioned in such configurations. Instead, we observe that unbalanced weight distributions lead to ill-conditioned
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
and, thus, here we consider the following two situations:

•

the weights of triangles are random variables that are
independent
of each other and distributed as heavy-tailed Cauchy distributions or bi-modal Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma_{1})+\mathcal{N}(1/3,\sigma_{2})
. This way we generate a sufficiently large number of heavy triangles and a cluster of reducible triangles with small
w_{2}(t)
;

•

the weights of triangles are
topologically dependent
, that is
w_{2}(t)
is a function of the weights of the neighboring triangles.
A way to implement this dependence is to set
w_{2}(t)=f(w_{1}(e_{1}),w_{1}(e_{2}),w_{1}(e_{3}))
for the triangle
t=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
. Two well-known common choices of
f
are the min-rule
w_{2}(t)=\min(w_{1}(e_{1}),w_{1}(e_{2}),w_{1}(e_{3}))
,
[
13
,
17
]
, and the product rule
w_{2}(t)=w_{1}(e_{1})w_{1}(e_{2})w_{1}(e_{3})
,
[
4
,
5
]
. In this way, the edge weight profile
w_{1}(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{V}_{1}(\mathcal{K})
is transformed to an unbalanced distribution
w_{2}(t)
.

7.5
Timings

One needs to separately discuss the time cost of the computation of the preconditioning operator (as a tuplet
C
and the permutation
P_{\Sigma}
) and of the
matvec
computation for the preconditioned operator
C^{+}P_{\Sigma}L_{1}^{\uparrow}P_{\Sigma}^{\top}(C^{+})^{\top}
.

Note that
matvec
of
L_{1}^{\uparrow}
has the complexity of the number of non-zero elements,
\mathcal{O}(m_{2})
; the
HeCS
preconditioner
C
has
\leq 3m_{1}
non-zero elments and lower triangular structure, so
matvec
s of either
C^{+}
and
(C^{+})^{\top}
, as well as the permutation matrix
P_{\Sigma}
, have complexity
\mathcal{O}(m_{1})
. Hence, the complexity of each preconditioned CGLS iteration is
\mathcal{O}(m_{2}+m_{1})
, as opposed to the original
\mathcal{O}(m_{2})
; asymptotically one expects
m_{2}\gg m_{1}
, so the preconditioning scheme is efficient. On the other hand, the shifted
ichol
preconditioner
C_{\alpha}
loses the sparsity due to the shift; as a result, the application of
C_{\alpha}^{+}
costs
