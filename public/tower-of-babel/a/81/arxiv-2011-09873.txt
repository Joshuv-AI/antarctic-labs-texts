Most general theory of 3d gravity: Covariant phase space, dual diffeomorphisms, and more
Marc Geiller, Christophe Goeller, Nelson Merino

Abstract
We show that the phase space of three-dimensional gravity contains two layers of dualities: between diffeomorphisms and a notion of “dual diffeomorphisms” on the one hand, and between first order curvature and torsion on the other hand. This is most elegantly revealed and understood when studying the most general Lorentz-invariant first order theory in connection and triad variables, described by the so-called Mielke–Baekler Lagrangian. By analyzing the quasi-local symmetries of this theory in the covariant phase space formalism, we show that in each sector of the torsion/curvature duality there exists a well-defined notion of dual diffeomorphism, which furthermore follows uniquely from the Sugawara construction. Together with the usual diffeomorphisms, these duals form at finite distance, without any boundary conditions, and for any sign of the cosmological constant, a centreless double Virasoro algebra which in the flat case reduces to the BMS 3 algebra. These algebras can then be centrally-extended via the twisted Sugawara construction. This shows that the celebrated results about asymptotic symmetry algebras are actually generic features of three-dimensional gravity at any finite distance. They are however only revealed when working in first order connection and triad variables, and a priori inaccessible from Chern–Simons theory. As a bonus, we study the second order equations of motion of the Mielke–Baekler model, as well as the on-shell Lagrangian. This reveals the duality between Riemannian metric and teleparallel gravity, and a new candidate theory for three-dimensional massive gravity which we call teleparallel topologically massive gravity.

After having studied the finite distance covariant phase space of the MB model and introduced the dual charges, we conclude this work with a study of the metric and teleparallel formulations of the theory, both at the level of the equations of motion and of the Lagrangian itself. Mielke–Baekler Lagrangian

The Mielke–Baekler (MB) Lagrangian is the most general Lorentz-invariant three-form constructed with a triad one-form
e
and a connection one-form
\omega

[
57
,
56
]
. It is defined as the sum of the volume, Hilbert–Palatini, Chern–Simons and torsion Lagrangians, each with their own coupling
\sigma_{i}
, and takes the simple form

\displaystyle L_{\text{MB}}[e,\omega]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}L_{\text{V}}[e]+\sigma_{1}L_{\text{HP}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{2}L_{\text{CS}}[\omega]+\sigma_{3}L_{\text{T}}[e,\omega]

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{3}e\wedge[e\wedge e]+2\sigma_{1}e\wedge F+\sigma_{2}\omega\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}\omega+\frac{1}{3}[\omega\wedge\omega]\right)+\sigma_{3}e\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e.

(2.2)

Here and throughout this article we work in Lorentzian signature, and use an index-free differential form notation recalled in appendix
A
.

Equations of motion

The variation of the MB Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\delta L_{\text{MB}}[e,\omega]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\Big(2\sigma_{1}\delta\omega\wedge e+\sigma_{2}\delta\omega\wedge\omega+\sigma_{3}\delta e\wedge e\Big)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}+\delta e\wedge\Big(2\sigma_{1}F+2\sigma_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{0}[e\wedge e]\Big)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}+\delta\omega\wedge\Big(2\sigma_{2}F+2\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{3}[e\wedge e]\Big).

(2.5)

This reveals the symplectic potential, to which we will come back shortly, as well as the equations of motion. One can see that both equations of motion contain all possible two-forms in a “symmetrized” manner, and that likewise the symplectic potential contains all possible polarizations. This evidently results from the presence of all four terms/couplings in the MB Lagrangian.

To understand the physical meaning of the first order equations of motion, it is useful to rewrite them in the form

\displaystyle 2F+p[e\wedge e]\approx 0,\qquad\qquad 2\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+q[e\wedge e]\approx 0,

(2.6)

with

\displaystyle p\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}},\qquad\qquad q\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{0}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}}.

(2.7)

This shows that the theory has a source of Lorentzian curvature and torsion measured respectively by the parameters
p
and
q
. We use here the adjective “Lorentzian” because these are properties of the first order connection variable
\omega
. Later on we will talk about Riemannian curvature and torsion when going to second order variable by decomposing
\omega
into a reference connection and a contorsion tensor
5
5

5

In particular, as we will see below, the theory can have vanishing Lorentzian curvature but non-vanishing Riemannian curvature. It is this latter which matters when talking about the metric geometry of spacetime.
. Note that we require of course that
\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\neq 0
otherwise the theory is trivial (or more precisely admits only degenerate triads as solutions). Two useful relations which we will use in many places below are
\sigma_{0}=p\sigma_{1}+q\sigma_{3}
and
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}+q\sigma_{1}

It is now enlightening to combine the first order equations of motion into a single second order equation. For this, one can decompose the Lorentz connection as
\omega=\Gamma+k

is the torsionless Levi–Civita connection and

\displaystyle F=R+\mathrm{d}_{\Gamma}k+\frac{1}{2}[k\wedge k],\qquad\qquad\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e=[k\wedge e],

where

\displaystyle 2R\approx\Lambda[e\wedge e],\qquad\qquad\Lambda\coloneqq-\left(p+\frac{q^{2}}{4}\right).

In metric terms this means that

Note that here we have obtained a metric second order equation of motion because we have used the torsionless connection

Finally, let us observe that there are two particular cases for specific values of the couplings. For

Symmetries

We now turn to the study of the gauge symmetries of the theory. First, we have the internal Lorentz transformations, which act infinitesimally as

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{j}}_{\alpha}e=[e,\alpha],\qquad\qquad\delta^{\text{j}}_{\alpha}\omega=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\alpha,\qquad\qquad\delta^{\text{j}}_{\alpha}F=[F,\alpha],\qquad\qquad\delta^{\text{j}}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e=[\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e,\alpha].

(2.10)

Then, we have the translations acting on the triad and on the connection as

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{t}}_{\phi}e=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\phi+q[e,\phi],\qquad\qquad\delta^{\text{t}}_{\phi}\omega=p[e,\phi],

(2.11)

and on the curvature and torsion as

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{t}}_{\phi}F=p\mathrm{d}_{\omega}[e,\phi],\qquad\qquad\delta^{\text{t}}_{\phi}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e=\frac{1}{2}\big[2F+p[e\wedge e],\phi\big]+q\mathrm{d}_{\omega}[e,\phi].

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)+[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(2.13b)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\text{j}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,\omega}e+\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,e}e+\frac{1}{2}\xi\lrcorner\,\big(2\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+q[e\wedge e]\big),

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\displaystyle\Omega=-\int_{\Sigma}2\sigma_{1}\delta\omega\wedge\delta e+\sigma_{2}\delta\omega\wedge\delta\omega+\sigma_{3}\delta e\wedge\delta e.

(3.2)

We are now ready to contract this symplectic structure with the various gauge transformations introduced above. This will identify the Hamiltonian generators as well as the boundary charges. We will then compute the algebra of these charges, and present the Sugawara construction which relates diffeomorphism charges to internal gauge transformations. Along the way we will introduce the dual diffeomorphism charges.

Generators and charges

Let us consider initially that the parameters
(\alpha,\phi,\xi)
are field-independent. Contracting the gauge transformations with the symplectic structure gives

\displaystyle-\delta^{\text{j}}_{\alpha}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega=\delta\mathcal{J}(\alpha),\qquad\qquad-\delta^{\text{t}}_{\phi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega=\delta\mathcal{T}(\phi),\qquad\qquad-\delta^{\text{d}}_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega=\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi),

(3.3)

where the notation
\not{\delta}
means that, for diffeomorphisms, the field-space 1-form obtained with the contraction is not necessarily integrable, i.e. not necessarily a total variation as we will see below. By construction of the covariant phase space, the generators on the right-hand side of these expressions are the sum of a bulk piece vanishing on-shell and a boundary piece called the charge. Let us now identify these two pieces for the various gauge transformations.

For Lorentz transformations, after computing the contraction with the symplectic structure we find that the Hamiltonian generator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}2\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\alpha\wedge(\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega)+\sigma_{2}\alpha[\omega\wedge\omega]-\sigma_{3}\alpha[e\wedge e]

(3.4)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma}\alpha\big(2\sigma_{2}F+2\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{3}[e\wedge e]\big)+2\oint_{S}\alpha(\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega).

(3.5)

For the translations we find that the Hamiltonian generator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}2\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\phi\wedge(\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e)+\sigma_{1}\phi[\omega\wedge\omega]-\sigma_{0}\phi[e\wedge e]

(3.6)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma}\phi\big(2\sigma_{1}F+2\sigma_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{0}[e\wedge e]\big)+2\oint_{S}\phi(\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e),

(3.7)

where we have used the fact that
\sigma_{0}=p\sigma_{1}+q\sigma_{3}
and
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}+q\sigma_{1}
. As usual, we have written here the generators in their manifestly differentiable form, which is the first line of each expression. We have then integrated by parts to reveal the constraints (which are the spatial pullbacks of the equations of motion), and therefore the boundary charges which are left when going on-shell.

For the diffeomorphisms, we write the Lie derivative as the second line of (
2.13
) for each variable, and find

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Sigma}\Big(\sigma_{1}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+\xi\lrcorner\,F\big)+\sigma_{3}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)+[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]\big)\Big)\wedge\delta e

(3.8)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\int}+\Big(\sigma_{2}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+\xi\lrcorner\,F\big)+\sigma_{1}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)+[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]\big)\Big)\wedge\delta\omega

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\xi\lrcorner\,\Big(2\delta e\wedge(\sigma_{1}F+\sigma_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)+\delta\omega\wedge\big(2\sigma_{2}F+2\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{3}[e\wedge e]\big)\Big)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\int}-\delta\Big(2(\xi\lrcorner\,e)(\sigma_{1}F+\sigma_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)+(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\big(2\sigma_{2}F+2\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\sigma_{3}[e\wedge e]\big)\Big)

(3.11)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\int}+\oint_{S}\delta\big(2\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)e+\sigma_{2}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\omega+\sigma_{3}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)e\big)-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta.

(3.12)

We see that half of the equations of motion appear in the bulk (second and fourth terms), while for the other half the term in
\sigma_{0}
is missing. However this is consistent because of the identity
\xi\lrcorner\,\big(\delta e\wedge[e\wedge e]\big)=\delta\big((\xi\lrcorner\,e)[e\wedge e]\big)
, which means that the corresponding term can indeed be re-introduced. As usual, we see that generic diffeomorphisms are not integrable. The non-integrable piece in the bulk vanishes on-shell, or off-shell for
\xi
tangent to
\Sigma
, while for the boundary charge we need
\xi
to be tangent to
S
. If
\xi
is tangent to both
\Sigma
and
S
we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\int_{\Sigma}2\sigma_{1}\pounds_{\xi}\omega\wedge e+\sigma_{2}\pounds_{\xi}\omega\wedge\omega+\sigma_{3}\pounds_{\xi}e\wedge e.

(3.13)

Let us now go back to the case of a generic vector field
\xi
and study more closely the non-integrability of the diffeomorphisms. From now on we will focus on the boundary charges. For convenience, we will use a slight abuse of notation and write all the generators on-shell, so that they coincide with their boundary charge and we can discard the bulk once and for all.

We have seen earlier that there is a relationship, given by (
2.13
), between the diffeomorphisms and the internal gauge transformations. Since this involves field-dependent gauge transformations, which are therefore generically non-integrable, we should go back to the notation
\not{\delta}
which keeps track of this fact. With this the Lorentz and translation charges are

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\alpha)\approx 2\oint_{S}\alpha\delta(\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega),\qquad\qquad\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\phi)\approx 2\oint_{S}\phi\delta(\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e),

(3.14)

and the diffeomorphism charge is

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)\approx\oint_{S}\delta\big(2\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)e+\sigma_{2}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\omega+\sigma_{3}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)e\big)-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta.

(3.15)

Using the fact that
2(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\delta\omega=\delta\big((\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\omega\big)-\xi\lrcorner\,(\delta\omega\wedge\omega)
, and similarly for
e
, it is then straightforward to see that we have

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e),

(3.16)

which reflects (
2.13
). This shows, as expected, how the non-integrability of the diffeomorphism charge comes from the non-integrable contributions of the field field-dependent translations and Lorentz transformations.

A natural question to ask is whether the integrability of
\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)
implies separately the integrability of
\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)
and
\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)
. In the general case this statement is evidently not true, as for example taking
\xi
tangent to
S
renders
\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)
integrable but neither
\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)
nor
\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)
. We will come back to this question in section
4
in the case of more generic boundary conditions for non-tangent vector fields at finite distance.

Dual diffeomorphisms

Let us now jump a little bit ahead of ourselves, and introduce a new charge also constructed from field-dependent Lorentz transformations and translations, but with a different field-dependency as that of diffeomorphisms. We will come back to the general idea behind the identification of this charge (and its teleparallel dual) in section
4.4
. For the time being, we are free to define it, to analyse its integrability, and to consider it along with the other charges introduced so far on the covariant phase space.

More precisely, the object which we want to introduce is the
dual diffeomorphism charge
. For this, in full generality and without any boundary conditions, let us consider the quantity

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{C}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\,\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)

(3.17)

\displaystyle\phantom{:}=\oint_{S}\delta\big(2p\sigma_{2}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)e+\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\omega+p\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)e\big)-\xi\lrcorner\,\big(2p\sigma_{2}\delta\omega\wedge e+\sigma_{1}\delta\omega\wedge\omega+p\sigma_{1}\delta e\wedge e\big)

(3.18)

\displaystyle\phantom{\coloneqq\oint_{S}}+2(\sigma_{3}-p\sigma_{2})(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\delta e.

(3.19)

Several important things should be noticed about this definition.

1.

We have introduced this charge by analogy with the diffeomorphism charge (
3.16
), but swapped the fields used in the field-dependent gauge transformations. This is a first justification for the name “dual diffeomorphism”, which will be further clarified below.

2.

It is important to notice that this charge is for the moment non-integrable. However, we see that integrability is achieved without restricting the dynamical fields when
\xi
is tangent to
S
and when
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
(which means at this stage that either
q=0
or
\sigma_{1}=0
). Notice also that for the familiar Hilbert–Palatini Lagrangian, obtained for
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
, this new charge does indeed exist.

3.

To define the dual charge we have combined the field-dependent gauge transformations with a relative factor of
p
, whose role will be clear when computing the algebra below. However, it is important to point out that, instead of the definition (
3.17
), we could have defined the new charge as
\not{\delta}\mathcal{C}(\xi)=\sigma_{3}\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\sigma_{2}\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)
. It is easy to see that this quantity is
always
integrable, without the need to constrain the couplings
\sigma_{i}
of the MB model. This definition however has the disadvantage that it makes not obvious the
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
limit giving the usual Hilbert–Palatini Lagrangian, since it gives the false impression that in this case the dual charge does not exist. Moreover, we will see in a moment and explain in appendix
C.1
that, no mater which coefficients are taken to define
\mathcal{C}
, consistency of its algebra with the gauge charges requires to take
q=0
, which in turn implies
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
anyways. This is why it is coherent to simply start with the definition (
3.17
). We should keep this remark in mind because it will also apply to the other dual charge introduced below in section
3.5
.

Now, recall that for diffeomorphisms we had the relations (
2.13
) which were reflected in (
3.16
). It is then natural, given the definition of
\mathcal{C}
, to look at a transformation of the fields of the form

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{c}}_{\xi}e

\displaystyle=p\delta^{\text{j}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,e}e+\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,\omega}e

(3.20a)

\displaystyle=p[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e]+\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+q[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(3.20b)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}\omega-\xi\lrcorner\,F+p[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e]+q[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(3.20c)

\displaystyle\approx\pounds_{\xi}\omega+q[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega],

(3.20d)

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{c}}_{\xi}\omega

\displaystyle=p\delta^{\text{j}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,e}\omega+\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,\omega}\omega

(3.20e)

\displaystyle=p\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+p[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(3.20f)

\displaystyle=p\pounds_{\xi}e-p\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e)

(3.20g)

\displaystyle\approx p\pounds_{\xi}e-pq[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e],

(3.20h)

where in both cases for the last equality we have gone on-shell. We see that, up to terms proportional to
q
, the object
\mathcal{C}
which we have introduced is the charge of a transformation sending
e
to the Lie derivative of
\omega
, and vice-versa. This is yet another reason behind the name “dual diffeomorphism”. To understand what is special about the value
q=0
, for which (
3.20
) really reduces to a Lie derivative, we have to study the algebra of the charges.

Charge algebra

Let us now go back to the general case of arbitrary couplings
\sigma_{i}
. Given the Hamiltonian generator
\delta\mathcal{H}(\alpha)=-\delta_{\alpha}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega
of an integrable transformation
\delta_{\alpha}
, the Poisson bracket between two generators is given by
\big\{\mathcal{H}(\alpha),\mathcal{H}(\beta)\big\}=\delta_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}(\beta)=\delta_{\alpha}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\big(\delta\mathcal{H}(\beta)\big)=-\delta_{\alpha}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\delta_{\beta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega
. Focusing first on the Lorentz transformations and the translations we find

Notice that the bracket of translations can be rewritten as

\big\{\mathcal{T}(\phi),\mathcal{T}(\chi)\big\}=p\big\{\mathcal{J}(\phi),\mathcal{J}(\chi)\big\}+q\big\{\mathcal{J}(\phi),\mathcal{T}(\chi)\big\}
.

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}(\alpha),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}([\alpha,\phi])-2\sigma_{1}\oint_{S}\alpha\mathrm{d}\phi,

(3.21a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}(\alpha),\mathcal{J}(\beta)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}([\alpha,\beta])-2\sigma_{2}\oint_{S}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta,

(3.21b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{T}(\phi),\mathcal{T}(\chi)\big\}

\displaystyle=p\mathcal{J}([\phi,\chi])+q\mathcal{T}([\phi,\chi])-2\sigma_{3}\oint_{S}\phi\mathrm{d}\chi.

(3.21c)

These Poisson brackets form as expected an ultra-local extension of the algebra (
2.15
), and contain three classical central extensions. The central charges are simply given by three of the couplings of the MB Lagrangian, which is also a consequence of having chosen (
2.11
) as the translations. Defining
\mathcal{K}_{i}\coloneqq\mathcal{J}+k_{i}\mathcal{T}
for

and computing the new brackets, one can easily see that it is always possible to kill any two central extensions with an appropriate choice of

Following (

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\alpha)\coloneqq\mathcal{T}(\alpha)-\frac{q}{2}\mathcal{J}(\alpha),

(3.22)

which maps the algebra to

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}(\alpha),\mathcal{P}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}([\alpha,\phi])+(q\sigma_{2}-2\sigma_{1})\oint_{S}\alpha\mathrm{d}\phi,

(3.23a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}(\alpha),\mathcal{J}(\beta)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}([\alpha,\beta])-2\sigma_{2}\oint_{S}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta,

(3.23b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{P}(\phi),\mathcal{P}(\chi)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\Lambda\left(\mathcal{J}([\phi,\chi])-2\sigma_{2}\oint_{S}\phi\mathrm{d}\chi\right).

(3.23c)

As expected we recognize here the
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}
current algebra, whose global part is (
2.18
), and find once again three central extensions. In this parametrization of the algebra however, the central charges are more complicated functions of the initial couplings
\sigma_{i}
of the Lagrangian. We will see below that these central charges are actually (up to signs) the ones appearing in the asymptotic symmetry algebra (

) which is independent of boundary conditions (and is not even an algebra of diffeomorphisms).

We can now complete the algebra of Lorentz transformations and translations by including the diffeomorphisms. However, since generic diffeomorphisms are not integrable without specifying boundary conditions, we focus as explained above on vector fields
\xi
tangent to

. We then find the usual brackets

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{J}(\alpha)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\alpha),

(3.24a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{T}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi),

(3.24b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta]).

(3.24c)

The brackets (
3.21
) and (
3.24
) describe the algebra of charges of the MB model, with the only assumption being that of tangentiality of the diffeomorphisms.

Finally, in the particular case
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
, it is natural to continue the logic of extending the symmetry algebra by including as well the dual diffeomorphisms. As we are about to see, their algebra with the charges introduced so far in (
3.21
) and (
3.24
) contains an important information. Considering again tangential vector fields, the new brackets are

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{J}(\alpha)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{T}(\pounds_{\xi}\alpha),

(3.25a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi)+q\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]),

(3.25b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{C}([\xi,\zeta]),

(3.25c)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{C}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta]),

(3.25d)

for which we give detailed proofs in appendix
C.1
. In these brackets we see the appearance of a new object which requires scrutiny. Indeed, at the difference with all the other charges appearing in (
3.25
) and in the brackets above, the object
\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])
in (
3.25b
) has a field-dependent smearing. This means that it cannot actually be obtained as the charge of a field-dependent translation, since this latter would not be integrable. However, we can reverse the logic and ask under which condition
\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])
does become integrable. Writing this charge explicitly we find

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])=2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]\delta(\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e)=\oint_{S}\sigma_{1}\delta([\omega,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]\phi)+2\sigma_{3}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]\delta e,

(3.26)

where we have used the tangentiality of
\xi
to show that the first term is always integrable. We now recall that the charge
\mathcal{C}
, which leads to the above algebra, only exists when
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
, and that this condition implies either that
q=0
or
\sigma_{1}=0
. If
q=0
, we see that the translation term with field-dependent smearing in (
3.25b
) simply drops. On the other hand, if instead we have
\sigma_{1}=0
, this term survives, and we have to discuss its integrability. Without imposing boundary conditions on the fields, this can only be achieved with
\sigma_{3}=0
. This in turn trivializes (
3.26
), but most importantly implies once again that
q=0
. In summary, we see that consistency of the brackets (
3.25
)
always
implies that
q=0
. Moreover, we explain at the end of appendix (
C.1
) that it is always the condition
q=0
which is singled out, regardless of which coefficients are used to define
\mathcal{C}
in (
3.17
). Here we have chosen the definition which leads to the simplest form of the algebra.

q=0

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}m\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m},

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{C}_{n+m},

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}p(m-n)\mathcal{D}_{n+m},

(3.27h)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

The remarkable result now comes from the algebra formed by the dual diffeomorphism

\mathcal{D}_{n}

q=0

\Lambda=-p

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{C}_{n+m},

(3.28b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\pm}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathcal{C}_{\pm n}\pm\mathcal{D}_{\pm n}\right)_{\!\!p>0},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\pm}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-p}}\mathcal{C}_{\pm n}\pm\mathcal{D}_{\pm n}\right)_{\!\!p<0},

(3.29)

and in both cases find the algebra

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\pm},\mathcal{D}_{m}^{\pm}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{D}_{n+m}^{\pm},

(3.30a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{+},\mathcal{D}_{m}^{-}\big\}

(3.30b)

In the AdS case
p>0
these are two centreless Virasoro algebras. In the dS case, i.e. when
p<0
, this is the algebra of the conformal group of the complex plane, which is the infinite-dimensional enhancement of the
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}=\mathfrak{so}(3,1)
isometries.

As announced, the brackets (
3.28
) and (
3.30
) are one of the main results of this paper, namely the derivation, at finite distance and without boundary conditions, of a centreless BMS or Witt algebra depending on the sign of the cosmological constant. This result is surprising because it is obtained for a generic boundary. It shows that the BMS and Virasoro structures are not solely properties of asymptotic boundaries, but general features of the boundary algebra of quadratic charges. This also reveals an important difference between the two setups. In the asymptotic case, the BMS or Virasoro structure (and their central charge) comes entirely from the diffeomorphism
\mathcal{D}_{n}
alone, which has two independent components due to the non-tangentiality of the asymptotic vector field. Here however, the tangentiality of the vector field requires us to use the dual charge
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in addition to
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to construct the algebras, and is also responsible for the lack of central charge. It is also interesting that this finite distance construction works for any value of the cosmological constant
\Lambda=-p
, which in particular includes the less-studied dS case. Finally, we note that the algebra of
(\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})
is automatically obtained in such a way that the flat limit is well-defined. This is to be contrasted with studies of the asymptotic symmetry algebra, where the Fefferman–Graham gauge does not lead to a well-defined flat limit while the Bondi gauge does

It is important to note that finite distance Virasoro and BMS algebras were introduced before in
[
85
,
86
]
using the notion of symplectic symmetries. There it is however the algebra (
5.21
) formed by the diffeomorphisms
\mathcal{D}
alone which is brought to any finite distance
r
. We comment below (
5.18
) and in appendix
D
on the definition of the symplectic symmetries in the first order formulation.

In light of these results, a natural question is therefore that of the fate of
\mathcal{C}
in the asymptotic case and in the case of finite boundaries with non-tangential vector fields. This also evidently raises the question of the geometrical and metric interpretation of the dual diffeomorphism
\mathcal{C}
. We will come back to this in future work, but give brief comments in section
5
. Here we have established a first contact with this dual charge, and presented the simplest and natural setup in which it arises and can be understood. This understanding is deepened by the Sugawara construction, which we now turn to.

Classical Sugawara construction

We now want to construct objects which are quadratics in the basic currents
\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n}
, and in particular understand their relationship with the generators
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of diffeomorphisms and their dual. This is the essence of the Sugawara construction, which provides a concrete algebraic implementation of the relations (
3.16
) and (
3.17
). In the case of diffeomorphisms this is well-know for usual three-dimensional gravity. Here we extend this result to the case of the MB model. In addition, we give an interpretation of the dual diffeomorphism as a Sugawara quadratic generator. This is best achieved in the Fourier representation (
3.27
). The various brackets for this section are given in appendix
C.3
.

To start with, let us introduce the three quadratics

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1}\coloneqq 2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{3}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}.

(3.31)

Their brackets with
\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}
are given in (
C.60
), while the brackets of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
’s with themselves are given in (
C.61
) and (
C.62
). Matching (
C.71
) with the action of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
on
\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}
, we find a unique solution which is that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}=\frac{-1}{4(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3})}\big(\sigma_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n}-\sigma_{2}\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n}-\sigma_{3}\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n}\big).

(3.32)

Then (
C.72
) gives (
3.27f
) as expected. This is the expression of the Sugawara construction for the diffeomorphisms in the MB model.

Now, matching (
C.71
) with the action of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
on
\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}
requires to take
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
, and we then find a unique solution given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}=\frac{-1}{4(\sigma_{1}^{2}-p\sigma_{2}^{2})}\big(\sigma_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n}+p\sigma_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n}-p\sigma_{2}\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n}\big).

(3.33)

This equation is the Sugawara construction for the dual diffeomorphisms in the MB model with the extra condition
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
. Once again, note that this includes the case of usual first order three-dimensional gravity, which is recovered for
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
. The existence of this dual Sugawara construction is yet another argument for why these dual charges are natural to consider.

Finally, we can also connect these Sugawara constructions with the expression (
2.16
) for the Casimirs of the
(J,T)
algebra, which is the global part of the
(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{T})
one. Indeed, the existence of two Sugawara constructions, and therefore the existence of the dual charges, can in a sense also be understood as a consequence of the existence of two Casimirs. Identifying the terms in (
2.16
) with the quadratics in (
3.31
) shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\frac{-1}{4(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3})}\big(\sigma_{1}C_{2}-\sigma_{2}C_{1}\big),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n}\sim\frac{-1}{4(\sigma_{1}^{2}-p\sigma_{2}^{2})}\big(\sigma_{1}C_{1}-p\sigma_{2}C_{2}-pq\sigma_{2}\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n}\big).

(3.34)

Interestingly, we see here resurfacing the condition
q=0
which has been identified in the previous subsection in order for the action of
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathcal{T}
to be stable. While this condition does not appear in (
3.33
), it appears here when trying to connect the quadratic generators to the Casimirs. When
q=0
, we get as expected a (formal) linear relationship between the Casimirs of the initial algebra and the generators of diffeomorphisms and their dual.

In summary, we have shown in this section that all the known results about diffeomorphisms, internal Lorentz transformations, and translations, can be extended to the MB model. We have done so by computing the charge algebra, and related the currents to the quadratics by the Sugawara construction. In addition, we have also shown that the
(p\neq 0,q=0)
subspace of the MB model contains a dual diffeomorphism charge. This latter admits a Sugawara interpretation, and forms with the usual diffeomorphisms a finite distance centreless double Virasoro or BMS algebra. In section
4
we will see how these algebras can be centrally-extended when considering non-tangent vector fields. This can also be interpreted in terms of the so-called twisted Sugawara construction.

The twisted Sugawara construction relies on a first order linear twist of the quadratics
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
introduced above in (
3.31
). We define these twists in appendix
C.4
in terms of fixed internal vectors
\lambda^{i}
and
\mu^{i}
, and also compute all the relevant brackets between the twisted quadratics. To illustrate this construction and the result, we can focus for simplicity on the case
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
(and therefore
q=0
) of usual three-dimensional gravity. In this case, the diffeomorphisms and their dual are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}=\frac{-1}{4\sigma_{1}}\check{\mathcal{Q}}^{1}_{n},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n}=\frac{-1}{4\sigma_{1}}\big(\check{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}_{n}+p\check{Q}^{3}_{n}\big),

(3.35)

where the
\check{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}
’s are the twisted quadratics defined in (
C.75
). With this twist, the algebra of the diffeomorphisms and their dual picks up central extensions, and takes the form

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{D}_{m+n}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\lambda\cdot\mu}{\sigma_{1}}m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0},

(3.36a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{C}_{m+n}+\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}}(\lambda\cdot\lambda+p\mu\cdot\mu)m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0},

(3.36b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{C}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}p(m-n)\mathcal{D}_{m+n}+\mathrm{i}p\frac{\lambda\cdot\mu}{\sigma_{1}}m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0}.

(3.36c)

With the redefinition of generators (
3.29
) this becomes a centrally-extended double Virasoro algebra. In the flat limit
p=0
this becomes a centrally-extended BMS
3
algebra. This shows that the finite distance Virasoro and BMS algebras formed by the diffeomorphisms and their dual can be centrally-extended in the presence of a twist. In section
4
we will interpret this twist as a choice of boundary conditions on the connection and the triad.

Teleparallel dual diffeomorphisms

We have now had a first contact with the dual charges, and studied in minute details the example of the dual diffeomorphisms
\mathcal{C}
defined in the case
(p\neq 0,q=0)
. We have in particular discovered the algebra which they form with the usual diffeomorphisms
\mathcal{D}
, and related them to the Sugawara construction.

Given how the MB model elegantly unifies the Lorentzian curvature and torsion sectors of three-dimensional gravity, characterized respectively by
p
and
q
, it is natural to now ask whether there exists also a dual charge in the case
(p=0,q\neq 0)
. With a slight abuse of language we choose to call this the teleparallel sector
7
7

7

Indeed, recall that
p
and
q
are measures of the Lorentzian curvature and torsion, while the Riemannian curvature and torsion sectors of the MB model will be studied in section
6
.
. In this sector of the MB model, the dual charge is given by

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{B}(\xi)\coloneqq q\,\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega),

(3.37)

whose integrability can easily be seen to be achieved with a tangent vector and the condition
\sigma_{3}=q\sigma_{1}
. Much like what has been done above for the dual charge
\mathcal{C}
, one can explicitly check that consistency of the bracket of
\mathcal{B}(\xi)
with
\mathcal{T}(\phi)
requires that
p=0
. In this sense the charge
\mathcal{B}
is the torsion/curvature dual to the charge
\mathcal{C}
.

The action of
\mathcal{B}
on the connection and on the triad can be computed by analogy with (
3.20
). A direct calculation and a little bit of rewriting shows that

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{b}}_{\xi}e

\displaystyle=q\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,e}e+\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,\omega}e

(3.38a)

\displaystyle=q\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+q^{2}[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e]+\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+q[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(3.38b)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}\omega-\xi\lrcorner\,F+q\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+q^{2}[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e]+q[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega]

(3.38c)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}\omega+q\pounds_{\xi}e-\xi\lrcorner\,F-\frac{q}{2}\xi\lrcorner\,\big(2\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+q[e\wedge e]\big)

(3.38d)

\displaystyle\approx\pounds_{\xi}\omega+q\pounds_{\xi}e-p[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e],

(3.38e)

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{b}}_{\xi}\omega

\displaystyle=q\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,e}\omega+\delta^{\text{t}}_{\xi\lrcorner\,\omega}\omega

(3.38f)

\displaystyle=pq[e,\xi\lrcorner\,e]+p[e,\xi\lrcorner\,\omega].

(3.38g)

This result shows that when
p=0
the transformation
\mathcal{B}
does indeed have a geometrical action on the dynamical fields. At the difference with
\mathcal{C}
which was completely dualizing the diffeomorphisms by transforming
e
into the Lie derivative of
\omega
and vice-versa, we see that
\mathcal{B}
transforms
e
but not
\omega
. This therefore also shows that these duality structures on the MB space of theories are very subtle, since e.g. here we could not have intuited the form of (
3.38
) from the analogue formula for
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{C}
. This opens the question of the geometrical understanding of the dual transformations
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
, which we will explore in future work.

Using the results of appendix
C
, we can now also perform a Sugawara construction for this new dual charge. Its expression in terms of the quadratics (
3.31
) is in fact very simple, and found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}=\frac{-1}{4\sigma_{1}}\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n}.

(3.39)

Again, we can then relate this Sugawara construction to the Casimirs of the
(J,T)
algebra, and we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}\sim\frac{-1}{4\sigma_{1}}\big(C_{1}-p\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n}\big),

(3.40)

which bring up again the condition
p=0
in order to have a correspondance between the Casimirs and the quadratic generators. The other quadratic in the case
(p=0,q\neq 0)
is again given of course by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and the new algebra of diffeomorphisms and their teleparallel dual is found to be

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{D}_{m+n},

(3.41a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{B}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{B}_{m+n},

(3.41b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{B}_{n},\mathcal{B}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}q(m-n)\mathcal{B}_{m+n}.

(3.41c)

In the case
q=0
of vanishing (Lorentzian) torsion this is the centreless BMS
3
algebra, as we have found in the flat case with the generators
(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{C}_{n})
. For
q\neq 0
however, upon rescaling
\mathcal{B}_{n}
we recognize two centreless Virasoro algebras in semi-direct sum. One should recall that, although the teleparallel dual diffeomorphism
\mathcal{B}
is defined when the Lorentzian curvature
p
is vanishing, the Riemannian curvature is still non-vanishing and given by
\Lambda=-q^{2}/4
. From this point of view it is perhaps not surprising to find Virasoro algebras, although the novelty here is that they appear with a semi-direct sum. These algebras can also easily be centrally-extended with the twisted Sugawara construction using the results of appendix
C.4
.

Consistently, we then find that in the case
(p=0,q=0)
we get
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}
, so the dual diffeomorphisms agree. This can be seen both in the definitions (
3.17
) and (
3.37
) and in the Sugawara expressions (
3.33
) and (
3.39
).

Finally, let us close with a remark about the (im)possibility of extending further the duality. Given how
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{D}
are defined in terms of field-dependent gauge transformations in equations (
3.37
), (
3.17
), and (
3.16
), and in particular the fact that
\mathcal{B}
uses the translations, one can wonder if it is possible to use instead the sum of two field-dependent Lorentz transformations. One can indeed consider
\not{\delta}\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\alpha\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\beta\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)
, which is integrable for tangent vectors if
\beta\sigma_{1}=\alpha\sigma_{2}
. However, then a quick calculation along the lines of (
C.30
) reveals that this charges cannot have a stable action on
\mathcal{T}(\phi)
unless
\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}=0
, which is not authorized since it corresponds to the degenerate sector of the MB model. This therefore closes the study of the quadratic charges obtained as combinations of field-dependent gauge transformations. We can now step back a little to study the CS formulation of the MB model, and then relax the condition of tangentiality of the vector fields.

Chern–Simons formulation

We now turn to the Chern–Simons formulation of the MB model. First, we will explain the difference between the
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}
basis (
2.18
) and (
2.15
). Then we will focus on the charges and go back to the connection and triad variables in order to identify the dual charges in the case of non-tangent vector fields.

Recall that for any connection decomposed as
A=\omega^{i}J_{i}+e^{i}K_{i}
(we will keep the indices implicit below), for some generators
(J,K)
whose algebra is irrelevant for the moment, the CS Lagrangian gives

\displaystyle L_{\text{CS}}[A]

\displaystyle=A\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}A+\frac{1}{3}[A\wedge A]\right)

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\omega^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{j}\langle J_{i},J_{j}\rangle+\omega^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}e^{j}\langle J_{i},K_{j}\rangle+e^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{j}\langle K_{i},J_{j}\rangle+e^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}e^{j}\langle K_{i},K_{j}\rangle

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}+\omega^{i}\wedge\omega^{j}\wedge e^{k}\langle J_{i},[J_{j},K_{k}]\rangle+\omega^{i}\wedge e^{j}\wedge e^{k}\langle J_{i},[K_{j},K_{k}]\rangle

(4.3)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}+\frac{1}{3}\omega^{i}\wedge\omega^{j}\wedge\omega^{k}\langle J_{i},[J_{j},J_{k}]+\frac{1}{3}e^{i}\wedge e^{j}\wedge e^{k}\langle K_{i},[K_{j},K_{k}]\rangle,

(4.4)

where
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle
denotes the pairing in the algebra. This formula is always valid, as for the moment we have not used any commutators or pairings in the algebra of
(J,K)
. Let us now look at the cases
K=P
and
K=T
.

Chern–Simons formulation with
\boldsymbol{(J,P)}

Let us consider the algebra

\displaystyle[J_{i},P_{j}]={\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}P_{k},\qquad\qquad[J_{i},J_{j}]={\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}J_{k},\qquad\qquad[P_{i},P_{j}]=-\mu_{0}{\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}J_{k},

(4.5)

and the compatible pairings

\displaystyle\langle J_{i},P_{j}\rangle=\mu_{1}\eta_{ij},\qquad\qquad\langle J_{i},J_{j}\rangle=\mu_{2}\eta_{ij},\qquad\qquad\langle P_{i},P_{j}\rangle=-\mu_{0}\mu_{2}\eta_{ij}.

(4.6)

With this and
A=\omega J+eP
we find

\displaystyle L_{\text{CS}}[A]

\displaystyle=L_{\text{W}}[e,\omega]+\mu_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=2\mu_{1}e\wedge\left(F-\frac{\mu_{0}}{6}[e\wedge e]\right)+\mu_{2}\left(\omega\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}\omega+\frac{1}{3}[\omega\wedge\omega]\right)-\mu_{0}e\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e\right)+\mu_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega).\qquad

(4.8)

This is the sum of the usual Lagrangian with coupling
\mu_{1}
, and the so-called “exotic” Lagrangian with coupling
\mu_{2}
. We have denoted the sum after Witten since the two terms appeared in
[
5
]
. The Lagrangian (
4.7
) differs from the MB Lagrangian (
2.1
) in two ways:

1.

The couplings for the Lagrangians
L_{\text{CS}}[\omega]
and
L_{\text{T}}[e,\omega]
in (
4.7
) are not independent, since they both appear in the exotic Lagrangian with the common factor of
\mu_{2}
. One could think of relaxing this by introducing a new independent coupling and working with the pairing
\langle P_{i},P_{j}\rangle=\mu_{3}\eta_{ij}
, but then we would have
\langle J_{i},[P_{j},P_{k}]\rangle\neq\langle P_{k},[J_{i},P_{j}]\rangle
, so this pairing is not compatible with the algebra.

2.

The couplings for the Lagrangians
L_{\text{V}}[e]
and
L_{\text{HP}}[e,\omega]
in (
4.7
) are not independent. One could think of relaxing this by replacing
\mu_{0}\mapsto\mu_{0}/\mu_{1}
, but then the limit
\mu_{1}\rightarrow 0
would not be well-defined, neither in the algebra nor in the Lagrangian. One way to make the limit well-defined in the Lagrangian however would be to also replace
\mu_{2}\mapsto\mu_{1}\mu_{2}
, but then the Lagrangians
L_{\text{HP}}[e,\omega]
and
L_{\text{CS}}[\omega]
would not be independent.

All this comes from the fact that we actually need a fourth independent parameter in the game. This is indeed the property which distinguishes the MB model from (
4.7
).

As pointed out in
[
58
,
59
]
, a fourth coupling can be introduced consistently by considering the connection

\displaystyle A=(\omega+\mu_{3}e)J+eP.

(4.9)

As an indication, one can notice that the curvature of this connection is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}A+\frac{1}{2}[A\wedge A]=\left(F+\mu_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{3}^{2}-\mu_{0})[e\wedge e]\right)J+\Big(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\mu_{3}[e\wedge e]\Big)P.

(4.10)

Setting it to zero gives the equations

\displaystyle 2F-(\mu_{3}^{2}+\mu_{0})[e\wedge e]=0,\qquad\qquad\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e+\mu_{3}[e\wedge e]=0,

(4.11)

which therefore describe a source of Lorentzian curvature and torsion as in the MB model. The Lagrangian obtained from this connection is

\displaystyle L_{\text{CS}}[A]

\displaystyle=L_{\text{W}}[e,\omega+\mu_{3}e]+\mu_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega)

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}L_{\text{V}}[e]+\sigma_{1}L_{\text{HP}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{2}L_{\text{CS}}[\omega]+\sigma_{3}L_{\text{T}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega),

(4.13)

with the identifications

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}=\mu_{1}(3\mu_{3}^{2}-\mu_{0})+\mu_{2}\mu_{3}(\mu_{3}^{2}-3\mu_{0}),\qquad\sigma_{1}=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}\mu_{3},\qquad\sigma_{2}=\mu_{2},\qquad\sigma_{3}=2\mu_{1}\mu_{3}+\mu_{2}(\mu_{3}^{2}-\mu_{0}).

(4.14)

Notice how when going from the first to the second line in (
4.12
) the boundary term also picks up a contribution from an integration by parts. One can therefore see that the CS Lagrangian for the shifted connection (
4.9
) produces indeed the MB Lagrangian. However, the couplings of the MB Lagrangian then have the complicated relationship (
4.14
) with the parameters defining the algebra and its pairings. Moreover, one can also notice that the connection
\omega
of the MB model is not the connection used to build the composite CS connection (
4.9
), since there it is the shift
\omega+\mu_{3}e
which appears. This means that, in this particular
(J,P)
CS formulation, the MB model is not actually determined solely by the algebraic data (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), but requites also the shift of the connection.

It turns out that these two points can be improved at once if we use the
(J,T)
algebra (
2.15
) instead of
(J,P)
. First, notice that one can invert the relations (
4.14
) to find
\mu_{i}=f(\sigma_{i})
. This yields in particular
\mu_{3}=q/2
, meaning that in (
4.9
) instead of shifting the connection one can simply change the generators as
(J,P)\mapsto(J,T)
. With this change of generators the pairings (
4.6
) lead to the pairings (
4.16
) given below.

Chern–Simons formulation with
\boldsymbol{(J,T)}

A more transparent CS formulation of the MB model can be obtained by using the algebra
(J,T)
. This puts forward more clearly the role of the couplings and of the curvature and torsion parameters
(p,q)
. Let us consider the algebra

\displaystyle[J_{i},T_{j}]={\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}T_{k},\qquad\qquad[J_{i},J_{j}]={\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}J_{k},\qquad\qquad[T_{i},T_{j}]={\varepsilon_{ij}}^{k}(pJ_{k}+qT_{k}),

(4.15)

and the pairings

\displaystyle\langle J_{i},T_{j}\rangle=\sigma_{1}\eta_{ij},\qquad\qquad\langle J_{i},J_{j}\rangle=\sigma_{2}\eta_{ij},\qquad\qquad\langle T_{i},T_{j}\rangle=\sigma_{3}\eta_{ij}.

(4.16)

For the moment
p
and
q
can of course be arbitrary. With this, defining
A=\omega J+eT
leads to

\displaystyle L_{\text{CS}}[A]=(p\sigma_{1}+q\sigma_{3})L_{\text{V}}[e]+\sigma_{1}L_{\text{HP}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{2}L_{\text{CS}}[\omega]+\sigma_{3}L_{\text{T}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega),

(4.17)

where in this derivation the only condition which we have to impose on the pairings in order for the torsion Lagrangian to appear, and therefore to get this Lorentz-invariant MB form of the total Lagrangian, is

\displaystyle\langle T_{i},T_{j}\rangle=p\langle J_{i},J_{j}\rangle+q\langle J_{i},T_{j}\rangle\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}+q\sigma_{1}.

(4.18)

This relation is in fact precisely the compatibility condition

\displaystyle\langle J_{i},[T_{j},T_{k}]\rangle=\langle T_{k},[J_{i},T_{j}]\rangle.

(4.19)

The other condition, i.e. the one involving two
J
’s and one
T
, is already satisfied. We therefore have a priori 5 couplings
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},p,q)
minus this constraint, which gives 4 independent couplings as expected. We can then trade one of these couplings for
\sigma_{0}
by further imposing

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}=p\sigma_{1}+q\sigma_{3}.

(4.20)

One can then check for consistency that the parameters
p
and
q
are indeed solutions to (
4.18
) and (
4.20
), so we finally have the very simple result

\displaystyle L_{\text{CS}}[A]=L_{\text{MB}}[e,\omega]+\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}(e\wedge\omega).

(4.21)

This shows, as announced, that the MB Lagrangian is a CS theory for the connection
A=\omega J+eT
and the
(J,T)
algebra (
2.15
).

Charges in Chern–Simons theory

We now recall basic facts about gauge and diffeomorphism charges in CS theory, before going back to connection and triad variables to analyse the integrability of the diffeomorphisms. The variation of the CS Lagrangian, the potential and the symplectic structure are given respectively by

\displaystyle\delta L_{\text{CS}}[A]=2\delta A\wedge F+\mathrm{d}(\delta A\wedge A),\qquad\qquad\theta_{\text{CS}}=\delta A\wedge A,\qquad\qquad\Omega_{\text{CS}}=-\int_{\Sigma}\delta A\wedge\delta A.

(4.22)

Consistently with (
4.21
), we have that
\theta_{\text{CS}}=\theta_{\text{MB}}+\sigma_{1}\delta(e\wedge\omega)
. The generator of the CS gauge transformation
\delta_{\lambda}A=\mathrm{d}_{A}\lambda
is computed from
-\delta_{\lambda}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega=\delta\mathcal{F}(\lambda)
and given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\lambda)=-2\int_{\Sigma}\lambda F+2\oint_{S}\lambda A,\qquad\qquad\big\{\mathcal{F}(\lambda),\mathcal{F}(\mu)\big\}=\mathcal{F}([\lambda,\mu])-2\oint_{S}\lambda\mathrm{d}\mu.

(4.23)

We can now decompose
A=\omega J+eT
and
\lambda=\alpha J+\phi T
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{JT}(\alpha,\phi)\approx 2\oint_{S}\alpha(\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega)+\phi(\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e),

(4.24)

which indeed agrees with the charges (

) and (

). For diffeomorphisms we have as expected that

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Sigma}\xi\lrcorner\,(\delta A\wedge F)-\delta\big((\xi\lrcorner\,A)F\big)+2\oint_{S}(\xi\lrcorner\,A)\delta A

(4.25)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Sigma}\xi\lrcorner\,(\delta A\wedge F)-\delta\big((\xi\lrcorner\,A)F\big)+\oint_{S}\delta\big((\xi\lrcorner\,A)A\big)-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta,

(4.26)

which is in turn equal to (

In CS theory, the relation (
3.16
) giving the diffeomorphisms as field-dependent gauge transformations becomes simply
\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\not{\delta}\mathcal{F}(\xi\lrcorner\,A)
. Just as in the connection/triad formulation, generic diffeomorphisms are not integrable, and integrability can be achieved with either tangent vector fields or with specific boundary conditions on the dynamical fields. We also note very interesting recent work on the possibility of obtaining integrable diffeomorphism charges by considering
field-dependent vector fields

\mathcal{D}
and their dual
\mathcal{C}

Dual diffeomorphisms again

Let us now look at generic diffeomorphism charges, and consider an arbitrary field-independent vector field of the form
\xi=(\xi^{t},\xi^{r},\xi^{\varphi})
. With this the (a priori non-integrable) CS diffeomorphism charge is

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)=2\oint_{S}(\xi^{t}A_{t}+\xi^{r}A_{r}+\xi^{\varphi}A_{\varphi})\delta A_{\varphi}.

(4.27)

The angular
\varphi
piece is always integrable. The
(t,r)
pieces are integrable if we choose either
(A_{t},A_{r})\big|_{S}\propto A_{\varphi}
, or
(A_{t},A_{r})\big|_{S}=\text{constant}
, or some mixture of these conditions. Usually this choice is constrained by the form of the boundary conditions chosen for the variational principle. Typical boundary conditions are the chiral ones
A_{t}=\tau A_{\varphi}
, which indeed set
\theta_{\text{CS}}\big|_{\Delta}=0
on the time-like boundary. With a gauge choice it is also customary to fix
A_{r}
to be a constant
a
. With these conditions the diffeomorphism charge becomes
8
8

8

Recall that here
\tau
is a number while
a
is a Lie algebra element.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}\xi^{\tau}(A_{\varphi})^{2}+2\xi^{r}aA_{\varphi},

(4.28)

where we have introduced
\xi^{\tau}\coloneqq\tau\xi^{t}+\xi^{\varphi}
. To compute the algebra of these charges, we need two further assumptions. The first one is that
\xi
depends only on the angular variable
\varphi
. This ensures that the derivative of vector fields which appear when computing the bracket can be rewritten as the Lie bracket. The second one is that
\xi^{\tau}=\kappa\xi^{\varphi}
. This is required in order for the bracket to close. With these assumptions, we find that the bracket is

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}=-\kappa\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta])-2a^{2}\oint_{S}\xi^{r}\partial_{\varphi}\zeta^{r}.

(4.29)

This is the centrally-extended
\text{diff}(S)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)
algebra, where
r
is the Abelian direction (which is the one carrying the central extension). When the radial direction is then related to the angular one with
\xi=(\xi^{t},\xi^{r},\xi^{\varphi})=(0,\gamma\partial_{\varphi}\bar{\xi},\bar{\xi})
, this becomes the centrally-extended Virasoro algebra

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}=-\kappa\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta])+2a^{2}\gamma^{2}\oint_{S}\bar{\xi}\partial^{3}_{\varphi}\bar{\zeta}.

(4.30)

This simple and well-known result shows how the central extension of the diffeomorphism algebra arises from the non-tangentiality of the vector fields
9
9

9

Note that in appendix
B
, when presenting the covariant phase space, we have reproduced the proof of
[
88
]
that the non-integrable piece
\xi\lrcorner\,\theta
of the diffeomorphism charge (which is the piece vanishing when
\xi
is tangent) is the one responsible for the appearance of a central extension. In fact in this appendix we have extended this proof to the presence of a corner term along the lines of
[
89
,
90
,
70
]
. This is equation (
B.31
). Note however that this computation does
not
allow to recover the Virasoro or BMS central extension, because it assumes that the boundary term

is covariant. A more general formula for the central extension was given in
[
91
]
, and it would be interesting to explicitly show how this formula reproduces the Virasoro and BMS central extension.
. In this case we have to pick boundary conditions in order for the charge to be integrable, and we have chosen here the conditions leading to (
4.28
) as an example. In section
3.4
and in appendix
C.4
we have also explained how the non-tangentiality of the diffeomorphisms and the appearance of a central charge can be understood in terms of the so-called twisted Sugawara construction. Note that in (
4.30
) and in the twisted Sugawara construction, although the vector fields are not tangent, they only depend on a single parameter since the radial and angular components are related.

It is now enlightening to go back to the MB model in connection/triad variables. Denoting
a=A_{r}=(e_{r},\omega_{r})=(b,c)
, the diffeomorphism charge (
4.28
) for the MB model is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}\xi^{\tau}\big(2\sigma_{1}\omega_{\varphi}e_{\varphi}+\sigma_{2}(\omega_{\varphi})^{2}+\sigma_{3}(e_{\varphi})^{2}\big)+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{1}ce_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}b\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{2}c\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{3}be_{\varphi}\big).

(4.31)

For tangent diffeomorphisms, we have seen previously that the field-dependent Lorentz transformations and translations in (
3.16
) are
not
separately integrable. With the vector fields and boundary conditions chosen here, we find that these field-dependent charges are also non-integrable and given by

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\delta\Big(\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{2}(\omega_{\varphi})^{2}+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{1}ce_{\varphi}+\sigma_{2}c\omega_{\varphi}\big)\Big)+2\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{1}\omega_{\varphi}\delta e_{\varphi},

(4.32a)

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\delta\Big(\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{3}(e_{\varphi})^{2}+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{1}b\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{3}be_{\varphi}\big)\Big)+2\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{1}e_{\varphi}\delta\omega_{\varphi}.

(4.32b)

The first piece in each expression is integrable, but because of the cross term in
\sigma_{1}
, it is only the combination of these charges into diffeomorphisms which is integrable. The charges are therefore generically not integrable on their own.

Interestingly, the obstruction to separate integrability of the charges in (
4.32
) comes from
\sigma_{1}\neq 0
, and we can therefore make these charges integrable separately by working in the sector
\sigma_{1}=0
of the theory. This possibility exists because we are considering the MB model, which is non-trivial even when
\sigma_{1}=0
, while the charges of usual three-dimensional Hilbert–Palatini gravity would trivialize in this case. In order to find charges which are integrable in the case
\sigma_{1}\neq 0
, this then suggests to look at a natural alternative, which is to consider the “dual” type of field-dependency for the gauge charges. Instead of (
4.32
) we are then led to consider the dual charges

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\delta\Big(\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{1}(e_{\varphi})^{2}+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{2}b\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}be_{\varphi}\big)\Big)+2\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{2}e_{\varphi}\delta\omega_{\varphi},

(4.33a)

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\delta\Big(\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{1}(\omega_{\varphi})^{2}+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{3}ce_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}c\omega_{\varphi}\big)\Big)+2\xi^{\tau}\sigma_{3}\omega_{\varphi}\delta e_{\varphi}.

(4.33b)

Looking at (
4.32
) and (
4.33
), it is now evident to recognize the integrable combinations which we have introduced in the previous section. Indeed, if we put a condition on the couplings we can get new integrable charges defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq q\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\qquad\text{when}\qquad p=0,

(4.34a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\qquad\text{when}\qquad q=0,

(4.34b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\phantom{p}\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)+\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)\qquad\text{whenever}.

(4.34c)

The generator
\mathcal{D}
of diffeomorphisms is of course always integrable, while the dual charges
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
each require an extra condition. As explained in the previous section we need
p=0
and
q=0
respectively for
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
to have a consistent algebra with the rest of the charges, and furthermore we then get
p=0\Rightarrow\sigma_{3}=q\sigma_{1}
and
q=0\Rightarrow\sigma_{3}=q\sigma_{2}
.

In the case of the boundary conditions used in this section, where the vector fields are not tangent, we have the explicit expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\xi^{\tau}\big(2\sigma_{3}\omega_{\varphi}e_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}(\omega_{\varphi})^{2}+q\sigma_{3}(e_{\varphi})^{2}\big)+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{3}ce_{\varphi}+\sigma_{3}b\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}c\omega_{\varphi}+q\sigma_{3}be_{\varphi}\big),

(4.35a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\xi^{\tau}\big(2\sigma_{3}\omega_{\varphi}e_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}(\omega_{\varphi})^{2}+p\sigma_{1}(e_{\varphi})^{2}\big)+2\xi^{r}\big(\sigma_{3}ce_{\varphi}+\sigma_{3}b\omega_{\varphi}+\sigma_{1}c\omega_{\varphi}+p\sigma_{1}be_{\varphi}\big).

(4.35b)

One can compare this to (
4.31
) to see that these new generators can schematically be obtained from
\mathcal{D}
upon changing the coupling constants according to the pattern

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{B}

\longleftarrow

\longrightarrow

(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{1},p\sigma_{1})

(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})

(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{1},q\sigma_{3})

where in addition we should recall that
\sigma_{3}=q\sigma_{1}
for
\mathcal{B}
and
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
for
\mathcal{C}
. Just like the algebra of the diffeomorphisms gets centrally-extended as in (
4.29
) because of the non-tangentiality of the vector field, the brackets involving
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
will also receive central extensions. This shows how in the case of non-tangent vector fields it is still possible to define the dual charges, and moreover to obtain central extensions for the finite distance algebras
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C})
or
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{B})
. Moreover this can be understood in terms of a twisted Sugawara construction, as we explain in appendix
C.4
. We keep the detailed investigation of the central charges for future work.

Finally, an important observation following from the construction of the dual charges is that it is a priori not possible to obtain them directly from CS theory. Indeed, as we have recalled in the previous subsection, the diffeomorphisms of the MB model are obtained from its CS formulation by considering the field dependent gauge charges
\mathcal{F}(\xi\lrcorner\,A)
. It is however clear that it is not possible to consider any other field dependency, such as the ones defining
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
. CS theory does simply not allow to write down such objects. It could perhaps be possible to define dualities directly at the level of CS theory, using e.g. a connection
\tilde{A}=\omega T+eJ
or defining an operation swapping the couplings as in (
4.4
), but this is for the moment speculative and should be investigated further.

Asymptotic charges and symmetries

Now that we have unraveled the finite distance covariant phase space structure of the MB model, introduced the dual charges, and studied the charge algebra, we would like to briefly recall results about the asymptotic structure of the MB model. This paves the road for future work on the study of the dual charges in the asymptotic context.

BTZ black hole

We start by recalling some basic facts about the BTZ black hole in the MB model. In Schwarzschild coordinates, the Lorentzian line element is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-N(r)^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{1}{N(r)^{2}}\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\big(\mathrm{d}\varphi+N_{\varphi}(r)\mathrm{d}t\big)^{2},

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle N(r)^{2}=-8Gm+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}+\frac{16G^{2}j^{2}}{r^{2}},\qquad\qquad N_{\varphi}(r)=-\frac{4Gj}{r^{2}},

(5.2)

with
m
and
j
the mass and angular momentum. The horizons are located at

\displaystyle r_{\pm}^{2}=4Gm\ell^{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{j}{m\ell}\right)^{2}}\right).

(5.3)

The cosmological constant here is related to that in the solution (
2.9
) given above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}=-\Lambda=p+\frac{q^{2}}{4}.

(5.4)

The coordinates are the time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the radius
0<r<\infty
, and the angle
0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi
. With the internal metric
\eta_{ij}=\text{diag}(-1,1,1)
, an adapted set of triad components such that
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{i}_{\mu}e^{j}_{\nu}\eta_{ij}
, and the corresponding connection components

\displaystyle\omega^{i}_{\mu}=\Gamma^{i}_{\mu}-\frac{q}{2}e^{i}_{\mu},\qquad\qquad\Gamma^{i}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}g^{\alpha\beta}e^{j}_{\alpha}\nabla_{\mu}e^{k}_{\beta},

(5.5)

are given by
10
10

10

The columns and rows correspond respectively to the
i
and
\mu
indices.

\displaystyle e^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}N&0&rN_{\varphi}\\
0&1/N&0\\
0&0&r\end{pmatrix},\qquad\qquad\omega^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&r/\ell^{2}\\
0&-N_{\varphi}/N&0\\
N&0&rN_{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}-\frac{q}{2}e^{i}_{\mu}.

(5.6)

Notice how the connection has a contribution from
q
, which comes from the fact that we have decomposed it as
\omega=\Gamma+k
, where the contorsion is fixed to
k\approx-qe/2
by the torsion equation of motion in (
2.8
).

As usual, the energy and angular momentum are given by the temporal and angular components of the diffeomorphism charge for the vector field
\xi^{\mu}=(\xi^{t},0,\xi^{\varphi})
. In order to write down the charge for such a non-tangent vector field, recall that the boundary condition used on the time-like boundary is
(\theta+\delta b)\big|_{\Delta}=0
, where
b=\sigma_{1}(e\wedge\omega)
is the first order Gibbons--Hawking--York (GHY) term
11
11

11

Note that, as always, this boundary term is automatically provided by the CS formulation, since from (
4.21
) we get
\theta_{\text{CS}}=\theta_{\text{MB}}+\sigma_{1}\delta(e\wedge\omega)
.
. With this boundary condition, the non-integrable piece
-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta
for a generic diffeomorphism charge (
3.8
) becomes integrable. Indeed, as explained in the appendix leading to formula (
B.23
), where
c=0
because the first order GHY has no derivative and therefore no corner term, the diffeomorphism charge is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}Q+\sigma_{1}\xi\lrcorner\,(e\wedge\omega),

(5.7)

where the Noether charge and the extra contribution are

\displaystyle Q=2\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)e+\sigma_{2}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\omega+\sigma_{3}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)e,\qquad\qquad\xi\lrcorner\,(e\wedge\omega)\big|_{S}=-\xi^{t}\left(N^{2}-r^{2}N_{\varphi}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right).

(5.8)

We then find that the total diffeomorphism charge is finite for any value of
r
and given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\xi^{t}E+\xi^{\varphi}L,

(5.9)

with

\displaystyle\frac{E}{16\pi G}\coloneqq(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{m}{2}-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\ell^{2}}j,\qquad\qquad\frac{L}{16\pi G}\coloneqq-(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{j}{2}+\sigma_{2}m.

(5.10)

These conserved charges for the MB model were derived previously in
[
61
]
using the canonical analysis (i.e. as the boundary terms in the Hamiltonian formalism) instead of the covariant phase space. We can see here the efficiency of the latter over the former. It is interesting to note that the energy and angular momentum are in fact a linear combination of the mass and spin of the black hole, even in the case of vanishing Lorentzian torsion
q
.

We can complete this analysis with a brief discussion of the thermodynamical properties of the BTZ black hole in the MB model. In terms of the radii, the temperature, mass, and spin are given by

\displaystyle T=\frac{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}{2\pi\ell^{2}r_{+}},\qquad\qquad m=\frac{r_{+}^{2}+r_{-}^{2}}{8G\ell^{2}},\qquad\qquad j=\frac{r_{+}r_{-}}{4G\ell}.

(5.11)

With the angular velocity of the outer horizon given by
\Omega=N_{\varphi}(r_{+})
, the first law
\mathrm{d}E=T\mathrm{d}S+\Omega\mathrm{d}L
gives an entropy
[
67
]

\displaystyle S=8\sigma_{1}\pi^{2}r_{+}-4\sigma_{2}\pi^{2}\left(qr_{+}+\frac{2r_{-}}{\ell}\right).

(5.12)

Carefully keeping track of the conventions to match the first order action with the Einstein–Hilbert one, as in (
A.21
), shows that we need
\sigma_{1}=(16\pi G)^{-1}
. The first term then indeed agrees with the usual result
S=\text{perimeter}/4G
, but is modified by
\sigma_{2}
even in the absence of Lorentzian torsion
q
.

AdS spacetimes and flat limit

Having studied the fixed BTZ black hole solution, we can now study asymptotic symmetries for a family of spacetimes. We would like to do so in a way which enables to study both the AdS and flat cases by taking a flat limit. For this, we follow
[
83
]
and choose Bondi null coordinates
(u,r,\varphi)
to write the line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\left(\mathscr{M}(u,\varphi)-\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}u^{2}-2\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}r+\mathscr{N}(u,\varphi)\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\varphi+r^{2}\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2},

(5.13)

where
\ell^{2}\partial_{u}\mathscr{M}=\partial_{\varphi}\mathscr{N}
and
\partial_{u}\mathscr{N}=\partial_{\varphi}\mathscr{M}
. This is the most general solution to the Einstein field equations
R_{\mu\nu}=-\ell^{-2}g_{\mu\nu}
. With the internal metric

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(5.14)

a compatible set of triad and connection components can be chosen in the form

\displaystyle e^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\big(\mathscr{M}-r^{2}/\ell^{2}\big)/2&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
\mathscr{N}/2&0&r\end{pmatrix},\qquad\qquad\omega^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{N}/(2\ell^{2})&0&r/\ell^{2}\\
0&0&0\\
\big(\mathscr{M}-r^{2}/\ell^{2}\big)/2&1&0\end{pmatrix}-\frac{q}{2}e^{i}_{\mu}.

(5.15)

The on-shell asymptotic Killing vectors are
\xi^{\mu}=(\xi^{u},\xi^{r},\xi^{\varphi})
with

\displaystyle\xi^{u}=f,\qquad\qquad\xi^{r}=\partial^{2}_{\varphi}f-r\partial_{\varphi}g-\mathscr{N}\frac{\partial_{\varphi}f}{2r},\qquad\qquad\xi^{\varphi}=g-\frac{\partial_{\varphi}f}{r},

(5.16)

and the conditions
\partial_{r}f=0=\partial_{r}g
,
\ell^{2}\partial_{u}g=\partial_{\varphi}f
, and
\partial_{u}f=\partial_{\varphi}g
. Note that this expansion of the vector field is exact, and stops at next-to-next-to-leading order.

With the fields given above, the only non-vanishing component of the symplectic potential (
3.1
) is
\theta_{\varphi u}=\sigma_{1}\delta\mathscr{M}
. This is the component along the time-like boundary
\Delta
, so we need a boundary condition. This is again given by
(\theta+\delta b)\big|_{\Delta}=0
with
b=\sigma_{1}(e\wedge\omega)
. With this we then have

\displaystyle\xi\lrcorner\,(e\wedge\omega)\big|_{S}=\xi^{u}\left(\mathscr{M}-\frac{2r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)-\xi^{r},

(5.17)

and the full diffeomorphism charge (
5.7
) is found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}f\left((2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\mathscr{M}}{2}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\ell^{2}}\mathscr{N}\right)+g\left((2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\mathscr{N}}{2}+\sigma_{2}\mathscr{M}\right)+\mathcal{O}(r^{-1}).

(5.18)

The subleading term in this charge is exact and of order
r^{-1}
. This is to be contrasted with the corresponding charges computed when
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
in the metric formulation, in which case the
r
dependency drops completely and the charges can then be defined at any finite distance
r
(see e.g. equation (2.13) of
[
85
]
). This latter case is an example of so-called symplectic symmetries
[
85
,
86
,
92
]
. This slight discrepancy between (
5.18
) and the corresponding metric result (in the case
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
), which prevents (
5.16
) from being interpreted as symplectic symmetries (although they remain of course asymptotic ones), can be corrected if we replace the Lie derivative on the first order variables by the Kosmann derivative. The use of this derivative in first order tetrad gravity was advocated in
[
93
,
94
]
and studied in more details in
[
95
,
96
,
97
]
. It amounts to correcting the ordinary Lie derivative by a field-dependent Lorentz transformation. We explain how this is realized in appendix
D
, where we also illustrate the same result in Fefferman–Graham gauge instead of the Bondi gauge used here.

We now come back to asymptotic symmetries, and focus on the charges (
5.18
) in the limit
r\to\infty
. We are interested in their Poisson bracket. For this, we need to know how the functions
\mathscr{M}
and
\mathscr{N}
transform. This information can be found by looking at
\delta^{\text{d}}_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}
. Denoting the angular derivative
\partial_{\varphi}
by a prime, we find

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{d}}_{\xi}\mathscr{M}

\displaystyle=g\mathscr{M}^{\prime}+2\mathscr{M}g^{\prime}-2g^{\prime\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\big(2\mathscr{N}f^{\prime}+f\mathscr{N}^{\prime}\big),

(5.19a)

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{d}}_{\xi}\mathscr{N}

\displaystyle=f\mathscr{M}^{\prime}+2\mathscr{M}f^{\prime}-2f^{\prime\prime\prime}+2\mathscr{N}g^{\prime}+g\mathscr{N}^{\prime}.

(5.19b)

To compute the algebra, let us denote the null and angular components of the diffeomorphism charge by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)\coloneqq\oint_{S}f\left((2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\mathscr{M}}{2}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\ell^{2}}\mathscr{N}\right),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{L}(g)\coloneqq\oint_{S}g\left((2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\mathscr{N}}{2}+\sigma_{2}\mathscr{M}\right).

(5.20)

Note that with the identifications
\mathscr{M}=8Gm
and
\mathscr{N}=-8Gj
this agrees with the BTZ charges

and
L
which we have computed in (
5.10
), with the factor of
2\pi
coming from the angular integral. With these charges we then find the algebra

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{L}(g),\mathcal{E}(f)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{E}([g,f])+(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\oint_{S}gf^{\prime\prime\prime},

(5.21a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{L}(g_{1}),\mathcal{L}(g_{2})\big\}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}([g_{1},g_{2}])+2\sigma_{2}\oint_{S}g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime},

(5.21b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{E}(f_{1}),\mathcal{E}(f_{2})\big\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\left(\mathcal{L}([f_{1},f_{2}])-2\sigma_{2}\oint_{S}f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right),

(5.21c)

where
[f,g]=fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g
. The flat limit
\ell\to\infty
of this algebra is well-defined. It reproduces as expected a centrally-extended BMS
3
algebra, where the central charges are functions of the couplings of the MB model. Note that, up to signs, they are the same as in the algebra (
3.23
). For a finite cosmological constant, the algebra is most easily understood in Fourier modes, in terms of which it becomes

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{L}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{E}_{n+m}+(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\mathrm{i}m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0},

(5.22a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{L}_{n},\mathcal{L}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{L}_{n+m}+2\sigma_{2}\mathrm{i}m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0},

(5.22b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\big(\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{L}_{n+m}+2\sigma_{2}\mathrm{i}m^{3}\delta_{n+m,0}\big).

(5.22c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big(\ell\mathcal{E}_{\pm n}\pm\mathcal{L}_{\pm n}\big),

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n},\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(m-n)\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n+m}+\frac{c^{\pm}}{12}\mathrm{i}m^{3}\delta_{m+n,0},

(5.24a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n},\mathcal{D}^{\mp}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.24b)

which is a double Virasoro algebra with central charges

depending once again non-trivial on the couplings of the MB model.

These central charges can be used to compute the entropy using the Cardy formula

\displaystyle c^{\pm}=(2\pi)6\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\pm 2\sigma_{2}\big),\qquad\qquad D^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\ell E\pm L),

(5.25)

\displaystyle S=2\pi\left(\sqrt{\frac{c^{+}D^{+}}{6}}+\sqrt{\frac{c^{-}D^{-}}{6}}\right)=8\sigma_{1}\pi^{2}r_{+}-4\sigma_{2}\pi^{2}\left(qr_{+}+\frac{2r_{-}}{\ell}\right),

(5.26)

which matches the result derived from the first law.

In this section we have studied the asymptotic diffeomorphism charges for AdS spacetimes in Bondi gauge, and shown how the couplings of the MB model affect the central charges. The asymptotic algebras have two factors (i.e. the two Virasoros or the rotation/translation parts of BMS
3
) because the asymptotic Killing vector fields are along

\varphi

Together, these results and observations point towards an important step for future work, namely the study of the
asymptotic dual charges
. Were these charges to be non-trivial, they could extend the asymptotic symmetry algebra (

\mathcal{B}(\xi)
and/or

Metric, teleparallel, and massive sectors

We finally come to the last part of this work

1.

How to combine the two first order equations of motion into a single second order equation? What is the physical interpretation of this latter?

2.

Can we inject part or all of the equations of motion back into the initial Lagrangian? What is the physical interpretation of the newly obtained Lagrangian?

The goal of this section is to study these questions. Similar work was done in

Second order equations of motion

We start by decomposing the connection as
\omega=\varpi+k

\varpi

an arbitrary tensor

In terms of this decomposed connection, the MB Lagrangian becomes

\displaystyle L_{\text{MB}}[e,\varpi+k]

\displaystyle=L_{\text{MB}}[e,\varpi]+2\sigma_{2}k\wedge R+(2\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}k)\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}k

(6.1)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}+\sigma_{1}e\wedge[k\wedge k]+\sigma_{3}k\wedge[e\wedge e]+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{3}k\wedge[k\wedge k]+\sigma_{2}\mathrm{d}(k\wedge\varpi).

Notice that choosing

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{1}R+2\sigma_{3}\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}e+2\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}k+\sigma_{0}[e\wedge e]+\sigma_{1}[k\wedge k]+2\sigma_{3}[k\wedge e]\approx 0,

Notice that varying with respect to

\displaystyle 2R+2\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}k+[k\wedge k]+p[e\wedge e]\approx 0,\qquad\qquad 2\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}e+2[k\wedge e]+q[e\wedge e]\approx 0,

Although the initial equations of motion were quite “symmetrical”, we see that in (

[k\wedge e]=M

is an arbitrary Lie algebra-valued 2-form. In components, this is

\displaystyle{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\big(k^{j}_{\mu}e^{k}_{\nu}-k^{j}_{\nu}e^{k}_{\mu}\big)=M^{i}_{\mu\nu}.

(6.5)

When the triad is invertible, this relation can be inverted to find

\displaystyle k^{i}_{\mu}=\left(\delta^{\alpha}_{\mu}\delta^{i}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}e^{i}_{\mu}\hat{e}^{\alpha}_{j}\right){\varepsilon^{j}}_{kl}M^{k}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{e}^{\beta l}=\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jl}\left(\delta^{\alpha}_{\mu}\delta^{j}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}e_{\mu k}\hat{e}^{\alpha j}\right)M^{k}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{e}^{\beta l},

(6.6)

where

\displaystyle k\approx-\frac{q}{2}e-\mathbb{T},\qquad\qquad\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}e),\qquad\qquad[\mathbb{T}\wedge e]=\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}e,

(6.7)

where we have defined the tensor
\mathbb{T}
to be the image under the above map of the torsion of the reference connection. We can then inject this in the first equation of motion in (

\displaystyle 2R-2\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}\mathbb{T}+[\mathbb{T}\wedge\mathbb{T}]\approx\Lambda[e\wedge e].

(6.8)

This is the second order form of the equations of motion of the MB model. It encodes a duality, which is that between the metric and teleparallel formulations of gravity. When the reference connection is the torsionless Levi–Civita connection
\varpi=\Gamma

is the contorsion, we have
\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}e=\mathrm{d}_{\Gamma}e=0=\mathbb{T}

\Lambda

Second and higher order Lagrangians

Now that we have described the second order equations of motion of the MB model, we can ask which second order Lagrangian (if any) they follow from. This can be investigated by injecting the solution (
6.7
) into the original Lagrangian. In the usual case of Hilbert–Palatini gravity (i.e. for
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
), choosing the torsionless Levi–Civita connection as the reference connection leads to the second order Einstein–Hilbert action in triad variables. Choosing instead a flat reference connection leads to the teleparallel Lagrangian as in
[
79

. As we are about to see, such a manipulation in the MB model leads to a Lagrangian with drastically different properties from the initial first order one.

Let us consider again an arbitrary decomposition of the connection as
\omega=\varpi+k

\displaystyle L_{\text{h}}[e,\varpi]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\Big(L_{\text{HP}}[e,\varpi]-\Lambda L_{\text{V}}[e]-e\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}\mathbb{T}\Big)+\sigma_{2}L_{\text{CS}}[\varpi]

(6.9)

\displaystyle\phantom{\ =}-2\sigma_{2}\mathbb{T}\wedge R+\sigma_{2}\mathbb{T}\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}_{\varpi}\mathbb{T}-\frac{1}{3}[\mathbb{T}\wedge\mathbb{T}]\right)+\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{1}e\wedge\mathbb{T}+\sigma_{2}k\wedge\varpi),

(6.10)

where for simplicity we have kept

\varpi

1.

In the teleparallel sector of the theory, i.e. when the reference connection is flat (let us choose the representative

Perspectives

We have shown that the phase space of three-dimensional gravity contains two layers of rich dualities, which are elegantly revealed when considering the most general first order Lagrangian depending on the connection and triad variables. This Lagrangian is that of the Mielke–Baekler model (
2.1
), and it describes a theory with Lorentzian curvature and torsion measured by the parameters
p
and
q
respectively. This model therefore has two natural subspaces, corresponding to
(p\neq 0,q=0)
and
(p=0,q\neq 0)
, which represents a first layer of duality between Lorentzian curvature and torsion. In each of these sectors, we have exhibited a second layer of duality encoded in the existence of new integrable charges, called the dual diffeomorphisms, which arise as particular field-dependent gauge transformations, or equivalently as quadratics in the Sugawara construction. We have first studied the algebra of these dual diffeomorphisms together with the usual diffeomorphisms in the generic case of finite boundaries with no boundary conditions, in which case integrability requires tangent vector fields. In the case
(p\neq 0,q=0)
, the resulting centreless algebra consists in two commuting copies of Virasoro, and is defined even for a positive cosmological constant. In the flat limit it reduces to BMS
3
. In the case
(p=0,q\neq 0)
the two Virasoro algebras are found to be in semi-direct sum, and also reduce to BMS
3
in the flat limit. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that such algebras, which are typically thought to be properties of asymptotic boundaries, are seen to appear at finite distance for arbitrary subregions and without boundary conditions (nor the use of symplectic symmetries
[
85
,
86
]
).

This result is particularly interesting in light of recent work on asymptotic dual charges in four-dimensional gravity
[
98
,
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
,
97
]
(see also
[
103
,
104
]
for a soft theorem interpretation of these dual charges). It begs the question of whether the dual diffeomorphisms
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
which we have identified act non-trivially at infinity, and whether they can be expressed in terms of second order metric variables. Important future work will now be to understand these dual charges in the case of asymptotic boundaries, where they could further enlarge the asymptotic symmetry algebra. In particular, it would be interesting to study the dual charges using the most general flat and AdS
3
boundary conditions presented in
[
54
,
55
]
.

Coming back to finite distance and arbitrary subregions, this enlargement of the boundary symmetry algebra could have important consequences for the quantization of gravity. The program initiated in
[
70
,
72
,
71
]
aims at constructing quantum gravity from the quantization of the quasi-local algebra of charges, and from this perspective it is important to understand the nature and the physical significance of the largest available symmetry algebra. In terms of representation theory, a related question is that of the relationship between representations of the
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}
current algebras (
3.21
) or (
3.23
) and the algebra of diffeomorphisms. In principle, the current algebra seen as a universal enveloping algebra contains all possible polynomials in the currents, and therefore includes the diffeomorphisms and their dual. Understanding this at the level of representation theory is however still an open issue, especially given the fact that dual diffeomorphisms do not seem to have been discussed before.

In summary, by a detailed analysis of the covariant phase space and the boundary symmetries of the MB model, we have shown that if we consider the stable quadratics in the enveloping algebra (
3.21
), at finite distance we are automatically lead to the double Virasoro algebras (
3.30
) and (
3.41
) (and their flat BMS
3
limit) depending on which sector of the duality between
(p\neq 0,q=0)
and
(p=0,q\neq 0)
we consider. It is noteworthy that (
3.30
) is a direct sum, while (
3.41
) is a semi-direct one, and that it is therefore (
3.30
) which is the finite distance counterpart of the asymptotic result (
5.21
) (minus the central extensions).

After having presented a detailed study of the dual charges at finite distance, we have discussed in section
6
the second order equations of motion arising from the MB model as well as the on-shell Lagrangians. This represents the second order version of the duality between Lorentzian curvature and torsion, which then becomes a duality between the Riemannian metric and teleparallel formulations of gravity. In the metric formulation the dynamics of general relativity is encoded in the curvature and the geometry is torsionless, while in the teleparallel formulation the geometry is flat and the dynamics is encoded in the torsion. The MB Lagrangian has the particularity that, when rewritten on-shell, it actually becomes third order and describes a propagating massive graviton. While this was known in the metric case
[
57
,
56
]
, we have extended the result to the teleparallel dual. This has revealed a new candidate theory of massive gravity, described by the Lagrangian (
6.12
) and called TTMG for teleparallel topologically massive gravity.

We close this article with a list of possible directions for future work. It would be particularly interesting to pursue these directions in the case of the MB model, because as we have seen here this latter reveals dual structures which are hard (if not impossible) to guess when working with the usual Hilbert–Palatini or Chern–Simons Lagrangians for three-dimensional gravity.

1.

Asymptotic dual charges.
As already explained above, it is now important to study whether the dual charges presented in this work have a realization at infinity and potential analogues in second order metric variables.

2.

Holography.
Boundary symmetries in three-dimensional gravity take a particularly important physical meaning when studying holography and the boundary CFT
[
105
,
106
]
. Three-dimensional gravity being exactly soluble, one can expect it to always be dual to a CFT or an integrable model, and to admit a quasi-local holographic description as constructed in
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Some aspects of holography in the MB model were studied previously in
[
64
]
. It would be interesting to push this study further, and to investigate whether the dual gravitational charges play a role in this holographic description. In this respect, it is particularly interesting to note that we have obtained, thanks to the availability of the dual charge
\mathcal{C}
in the case
(p\neq 0,q=0)
, a double Virasoro algebra defined for all values of the cosmological constant
\Lambda=-p\neq 0
. This could play an important role in the understanding of dS holography.

3.

Boundary dynamics.
An interesting property of topological three-dimensional gravity is the possibility of writing down a boundary dynamics invariant under the boundary symmetries. Following the seminal work in AdS
3
spacetimes
[
16
,
3
]
, this has been generalized to dS and the flat case
[

,
107
,
85
]
(see also
[
108
]
). This boundary dynamics is typically obtained via a reduction from the WZNW boundary theory. It would be enlightening to perform this construction in the case of the MB model.

4.

Relation between first order and metric charges.
One intriguing property of the MB model is that a given metric geometry obeying the second order equation of motion
2R=\Lambda[e\wedge e]
can actually be obtained from several inequivalent first order equations of motion. Indeed, since
\Lambda=-(p+q^{2}/4)
, there are actually infinitely many choices of
(p,q)
leading to a given
\Lambda
. While this in itself might seem innocent, it becomes more intriguing in light of the fact that the sectors
(p\neq 0,q=0)
and
(p=0,q\neq 0)
have different dual charges with a slightly different algebraic structure (i.e. a direct vs semi-direct sum). The flat case is even more puzzling, since it can be obtained with
p=q=0
, where the dual charges
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
exist and agree, but also for
4p=-q^{2}
, where a priori there is no well-defined dual charge (see however the next point below). If the dual charges turn out to exist in the metric theory, this therefore raises the question of which version of the first order dual charges they follow from.

5.

Dual charges for all couplings.
As we have explained in section
3
, the dual charges
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}

replaced by

be made integrable on the phase space. It is the additional requirement of stability of the action of the dual charges on
\mathcal{T}(\phi)
which has singled out the consistency conditions

and
p=0
for
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively, and prevented
\mathcal{A}
from existing. One can therefore wonder if it is possible to define dual charges in the case
(p,q)\neq 0
, i.e. for arbitrary couplings of the MB model. This would however evidently require a deeper understanding of the enveloping algebra and of the “open index quadratics” generated e.g. in (
3.27j
).

6.

Coupling to point particles.
Three-dimensional gravity can easily be coupled to massive spinning point particles
[

,

One question is whether the dualities studied in this paper extend in the presence of point particles, and if so which physical meaning they acquire. Moreover, particles can also be understood in terms of boundary defects and excitations

Now that we have unraveled the structure of the bulk and boundary symmetries of the MB model, one can wonder what type of deformation plays a role in the quantum theory and the coupling to particles. This is in turn related to the next two points below.

7.

Quantum groups and deformations.
It is known since the seminal work of Witten that quantum groups appear upon quantization of three-dimensional gravity

in the flat case, and should be extended to the full MB model in order to understand the fate of the dualities in the discrete setting.

8.

Quantization.
Three-dimensional gravity admits many complementary quantization schemes
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, but it is not clear which one could be most adapted to quantizing the MB model. In first order connection and triad variables, it is particularly useful to use the techniques of canonical loop quantum gravity and spin foam models
[
120
,
9
]
. However, this has so far only been studied in the case of the usual Lagrangian obtained from (
2.1
) with
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
. Moreover, these constructions depend crucially on the signature and the sign of the cosmological constant. In the flat case the partition function is described by the Ponzano–Regge model
[
121
,
122
,
123
]
, while in Euclidean signature with positive cosmological constant it is given by the Turaev–Viro model at root of unity
[
124
,
125
]
. The latter being a quantum deformation of the former related to the passage from
(p,q)=0
to
(p\neq 0,q=0)
, it is natural to ask if there is a quantum deformation which could describe the dual case
(p=0,q\neq 0)
. A dual loop quantization of three-dimensional gravity was proposed in
[
78
]
, and shown to lead to Dijkgraaf–Witten models, but there the duality was proposed in the case
(p,q)=0
only. In full generality, the question is that of the role of the couplings of the MB model as deformation parameters in the quantum theory, and how classical dualities are lifted to the quantum theory.

9.

Extension to four- and two-dimensional gravity.
A natural question is that of the extension of the present work to four-dimensional gravity. There are several aspects to this. First, one could try to construct dual diffeomorphisms by mimicking what has been done in section
3.2
. This seems subtle because, since four-dimensional gravity is not topological, it has no analogue of relation (
2.13
). Even in four-dimensional topological BF theory, the difference of form degree between the connection
\omega
and the
B
field prevents from considering charges with field-dependent smearings where the fields
\omega
and
B
are swapped. Second, we could instead consider in the Lagrangian of four-dimensional first order gravity all possible terms which are allowed by Lorentz and diffeomorphism invariance. It has already been suggested that such terms lead to dual asymptotic charges
[
98
,
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
,
97
]
, and the systematic investigation of general results at finite distance has been initiated in
[
70
,
71
,
72
]
. In these last references it has been demonstrated that working in first order variables and adding topological terms to the Lagrangian changes drastically the physical content of the finite distance charge algebra. Finally, one could also contemplate going towards lower dimensions, and investigating the existence and the role of dual diffeomorphisms in two-dimensional gravity (which also admits a first order formulation as a BF theory
[
126
]
). A first step in this direction could be to perform a dimensional reduction from the MB model to two-dimensional spacetimes, along the lines of
[
127
,
128
]
.

10.

Teleparallel topologically massive gravity.
We have conjectured that (
6.12
) is the teleparallel dual to the third order Lagrangian (
6.11
) of topologically massive gravity (TMG), and proposed the name teleparallel topologically massive gravity (TTMG). This is very natural given the construction of section
6
and how these two Lagrangians were obtained. For (
6.12
) to earn its name, it remains however to rigorously prove that it is indeed a theory of massive gravity. For this, we should first analyse the linearized theory around a flat background and derive the equation for the propagating graviton. Then, we should perform a full non-linear analysis by counting the degrees of freedom in the Hamiltonian formalism. To our knowledge there is so far no teleparallel description of massive gravity. This could be interesting in its own right, but also for potential extensions to four-dimensional massive gravity, where theoretical model building is much more constrained. Finally, we mention that aspects of holography in TMG were studied in
[
129
]
, and could also be extended to TTMG, as well as the study of the asymptotic symmetries and charges.

11.

Extensions of the MB model.
Finally, we note that the MB model has been generalized to include e.g. higher spin fields
[

or Maxwell algebras
[
130

. The study of the covariant phase space and of the dual diffeomorphisms could be extended to these models, as well as that of the teleparallel formulation along the lines of section

Appendix A
Notations and conventions

Indices

Spacetime indices are denoted by
\mu,\nu,\ldots\in\{0,1,2\}
and spatial indices by
a,b,\ldots\in\{1,2\}
. Internal indices are denoted by
i,j,\ldots\in\{0,1,2\}
, and are lowered and raised with the
\mathfrak{so}(1,2)
metric
\eta_{ij}
. Anti-symmetrization of
n
indices is defined with a factor
1/n!
.

Levi–Civita symbol and tensor

We denote by
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho}
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho}
the densities of weight
-1
and
+1
respectively, which we define to be such that
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{012}=1
in every coordinate system. We then define the true tensor (i.e. the density of weight 0)
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\coloneqq\sqrt{|g|}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho}
, so that with our convention the tensor with upper indices is

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}=g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}g^{\rho\gamma}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}g^{\rho\gamma}\sqrt{|g|}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=g^{-1}\sqrt{|g|}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho}=\frac{\text{sgn}(g)}{\sqrt{|g|}}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho}=\frac{\text{sgn}(g)}{\sqrt{|g|}}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}.

(A.1)

For internal indices we also use the tensor
\varepsilon_{ijk}
defined by
\varepsilon_{012}=1
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon^{lmn}

\displaystyle=-\big(\delta^{l}_{i}\delta^{m}_{j}\delta^{n}_{k}-\delta^{l}_{i}\delta^{m}_{k}\delta^{n}_{j}+\delta^{m}_{i}\delta^{n}_{j}\delta^{l}_{k}-\delta^{m}_{i}\delta^{n}_{k}\delta^{l}_{j}+\delta^{n}_{i}\delta^{l}_{j}\delta^{m}_{k}-\delta^{n}_{i}\delta^{l}_{k}\delta^{m}_{j}\big),

(A.2a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon^{lmk}

\displaystyle=-\big(\delta^{l}_{i}\delta^{m}_{j}-\delta^{l}_{j}\delta^{m}_{i}\big),

(A.2b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon^{ljk}

\displaystyle=-2\delta^{l}_{i},

(A.2c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon^{ijk}

\displaystyle=-3!.

(A.2d)

With these we have that

\displaystyle|e|\coloneqq\det(e^{i}_{\mu})=\frac{1}{6}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\varepsilon_{ijk}e^{i}_{\mu}e^{j}_{\nu}e^{k}_{\rho},\qquad\qquad|e|\varepsilon^{ijk}=-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}e^{i}_{\mu}e^{j}_{\nu}e^{k}_{\rho}.

(A.3)

Differential forms

When written in terms of components, a
p
-form is given by

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{p!}P_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{p}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p}}.

(A.4)

The wedge product between a
p
-form
P
and a
q
-form
Q
has components given by

\displaystyle(P\wedge Q)_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{p+q}}=\frac{(p+q)!}{p!q!}P_{[\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{p}}Q_{\mu_{p+1}\dots\mu_{q}]}.

(A.5)

Introducing the notation
\mathrm{d}^{d}x\coloneqq\mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{d}}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}x=\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}x=\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{d}}\text{sgn}(g)\sqrt{|g|}\mathrm{d}^{d}x.

(A.6)

In particular, for
d=3
we therefore have that

\displaystyle e\wedge F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}(e\wedge F)_{\mu\nu\rho}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\rho}

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}(e\wedge F)_{\mu\nu\rho}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathrm{d}^{3}x

(A.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}\frac{(1+2)!}{1!2!}e_{[\mu}F_{\nu\rho]}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathrm{d}^{3}x

(A.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e_{\mu}F_{\nu\rho}\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathrm{d}^{3}x,

(A.10)

and

\displaystyle e\wedge[e\wedge e]=e_{\mu}[e_{\nu},e_{\rho}]\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathrm{d}^{3}x.

(A.11)

Index-free notation

In our notation there is always an implicit pairing of Lie algebra indices, and
[\cdot\,,\cdot]
denotes the Lie algebra commutator. Explicitly, the corresponding internal indices are

\displaystyle e\wedge F=\eta_{ij}e^{i}\wedge F^{j},\qquad\qquad e\wedge[e\wedge e]=\eta_{ij}e^{i}\wedge[e\wedge e]^{j}=\varepsilon_{ijk}e^{i}\wedge e^{j}\wedge e^{k}.

(A.12)

The curvature and torsion are given in index-free notation by

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{d}\omega+\frac{1}{2}[\omega\wedge\omega],\qquad\qquad\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e=\mathrm{d}\omega+[\omega\wedge e],

(A.13)

which in components translates to

\displaystyle F^{i}_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\omega^{i}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\omega^{i}_{\mu}+{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\omega^{j}_{\mu}\omega^{k}_{\nu},\qquad\qquad(\mathrm{d}_{\omega}e^{i})_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}e^{i}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}e^{i}_{\mu}+{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}(\omega^{j}_{\mu}e^{k}_{\nu}-\omega^{j}_{\nu}e^{k}_{\mu}).

(A.14)

When switching from the fundamental representation of the Lie algebra to the adjoint representation, we have introduced
\omega^{i}_{\mu}
such that
\omega^{ij}_{\mu}\eqqcolon-{\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\omega^{k}_{\mu}
, and therefore

\displaystyle F^{ij}_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\omega^{ij}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\omega^{ij}_{\mu}+\omega^{i}_{\mu k}\omega^{kj}_{\nu}-\omega^{i}_{\nu k}\omega^{kj}_{\mu}=-{\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\big(\partial_{\mu}\omega^{k}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\omega^{k}_{\mu}+{\varepsilon^{k}}_{mn}\omega^{m}_{\mu}\omega^{n}_{\nu}\big)=-{\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}F^{k}_{\mu\nu}.

(A.15)

For
(p,q,r)
-forms
(P,Q,R)
we have

\displaystyle[P\wedge Q]\wedge R=(-1)^{(p+q)r}[R\wedge P]\wedge Q,\qquad\qquad[P\wedge Q]=(-1)^{pq+1}[Q\wedge P],

(A.16)

the Leibniz rule

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\omega}[P\wedge Q]=[\mathrm{d}_{\omega}P\wedge Q]+(-1)^{p}[P\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\omega}Q],

(A.17)

the integration by parts

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\omega}P\wedge Q=\mathrm{d}(P\wedge Q)+(-1)^{p+1}P\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\omega}Q,

(A.18)

and the squared action of the covariant derivative

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\mathrm{d}_{\omega}P=[F\wedge P].

(A.19)

For two 1-forms
e
and
\omega
, and two 0-forms
\alpha
and
\beta
, we have the useful formulas

\displaystyle[\alpha,e]\wedge[\beta,\omega]+[\alpha,\omega]\wedge[\beta,e]=[\alpha,\beta][e\wedge\omega],\qquad\qquad\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\alpha\wedge\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\beta=[\alpha,\beta]F+\mathrm{d}(\alpha\mathrm{d}_{\omega}\beta).

(A.20)

Einstein–Hilbert Lagrangian

With these notations and conventions, using
\sqrt{|g|}=|e|
and
2|e|\hat{e}^{[\mu}_{i}\hat{e}^{\nu]}_{j}=\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho}\varepsilon_{ijk}e^{k}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=|e|\hat{e}^{[\mu}_{i}\hat{e}^{\nu]}_{j}\hat{e}^{\rho}_{k}\hat{e}^{\sigma}_{l}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\eta^{ik}\eta^{jl}

Appendix B
Covariant phase space

Here we review basic facts about the covariant phase space, which we use repeatedly in the main text. We first present the notations and conventions, and then the general formula for the Hamiltonian charges. We follow the logic of
[
89
,
90
,
70
]
, which is to pay attention to possible corner terms in the boundary conditions.

Notations and conventions

Consider a Lagrangian, which may contain a boundary term, and which we will write as
L+\mathrm{d}\ell
. The variation of this Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\delta(L+\mathrm{d}\ell)=\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi+\mathrm{d}(\theta+\delta\ell),

(B.1)

where
E
are the equations of motion,
\Phi
is the set of fields, and
\theta=\theta[\delta]
is the potential
(d-1,1)
-form coming from the bulk Lagrangian. Here it appears shifted by the variation of the boundary term. The symplectic current is the field-space variation of the total potential, namely

\displaystyle\delta(\theta+\delta b)=\delta\theta=\delta\theta[\delta]=\delta_{1}\theta[\delta_{2}]-\delta_{2}\theta[\delta_{1}],

(B.2)

where we have made explicit the fact that variations in field-space are anti-symmetrized, and used the fact that
\delta^{2}=0
. From the variation of the Lagrangian, one can see that
\mathrm{d}\delta\theta\approx 0
. Integrating this identity on a spacetime manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M=\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\cup\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the time-like boundary, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\delta\theta=\left(\oint_{\Sigma_{1}}-\oint_{\Sigma_{2}}+\oint_{\Delta}\right)\delta\theta\approx 0.

(B.3)

For the symplectic structure

\displaystyle\Omega\coloneqq\int_{\Sigma}\delta\theta

(B.4)

to be independent of the slice
\Sigma
, we see that we have to require the vanishing of symplectic flux through
\Delta
. In order to understand how this can be achieved in general, let us go back to the variational principle. For this latter to be well-defined in the variation of the action, the most general boundary condition which we can consider is

\displaystyle(\theta+\delta b)\big|_{\Delta}=\mathrm{d}c,

(B.5)

for some
(d-2,1)
-form
c
on the corner. Note that this is a condition on the components (say)
\theta_{t\varphi}
along
\Delta
. This is the most general boundary condition but also, consistently, the most general condition for the vanishing of symplectic flux, provided we take into account the corner term arising from
\partial\Delta=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
in the case
c\neq 0
. Indeed, in this case the conserved symplectic structure acquires a boundary term and is

\displaystyle\Omega=\int_{\Sigma}\delta\theta-\oint_{S}\delta c.

(B.6)

This is simply saying that
\theta-\mathrm{d}c
is the potential which leads to a conserved symplectic structure in the case
c\neq 0
.

Once we have defined the symplectic structure
(0,2)
-form, we can contract it with variations in field-space such as gauge transformations
\delta_{\epsilon}
. Heuristically, viewing these gauge transformations as vectors and the field-space variations as forms, the contraction is

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega=\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\big(\Omega[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]\big)=\Omega[\delta_{\epsilon},\delta]-\Omega[\delta,\delta_{\epsilon}].

(B.7)

Analogously to what happens when contracting vectors with differential forms, which lowers the spacetime form degree, one can see that the contraction of the transformation
\delta_{\epsilon}
with the
(0,2)
-form
\Omega
lowers the field-space degree, and produces a
(0,1)
-form.

Noether and Hamiltonian charges

Consider
\delta_{\epsilon}L
with
\epsilon\in(\xi,\alpha)
either a diffeomorphism
\xi
or an internal gauge transformation with parameter
\alpha
. Suppose that we have
\delta_{\epsilon}L=\mathrm{d}m
. The variation of the Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\delta L=\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi+\mathrm{d}\theta.

(B.8)

Taking the variation to be a gauge transformation, we get

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}L=\text{E}\wedge\delta_{\epsilon}\Phi+\mathrm{d}(\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\theta)=\mathrm{d}m,

(B.9)

which leads to

\displaystyle\text{E}\wedge\delta_{\epsilon}\Phi+\mathrm{d}(\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\theta-m)=0.

(B.10)

The Noether current
(d-1,0)
-form is defined as

\displaystyle J\coloneqq\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\theta-m,

(B.11)

so on-shell we have
\mathrm{d}J\approx 0
, which implies that
J\approx\mathrm{d}Q
where the
(d-2,0)
-form
Q
is the Noether charge. We also have

\displaystyle\text{E}\wedge\delta_{\epsilon}\Phi=\epsilon N-\mathrm{d}P=-\mathrm{d}P

(B.12)

by Noether’s second theorem, so we can write

\displaystyle J=P+\mathrm{d}Q.

(B.13)

The quantity
P
is therefore the off-shell part of the Noether current. We then get

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{H}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\Omega

(B.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\delta(\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\theta)-\delta_{\epsilon}\theta-\oint_{S}\delta(\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)-\delta_{\epsilon}c

(B.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\delta P+\delta m-\delta_{\epsilon}\theta+\oint_{S}\delta(Q-\delta_{\epsilon}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)+\delta_{\epsilon}c.

(B.16)

Now, consider the equality

\displaystyle 0=\delta\delta_{\epsilon}L-\delta_{\epsilon}\delta L=\mathrm{d}(\delta m-\delta_{\epsilon}\theta),

(B.17)

which holds because
\delta_{\epsilon}(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)=0
. This implies that there exists a
(d-2,1)
-form
M
such that

\displaystyle\delta m-\delta_{\epsilon}\theta=\mathrm{d}M.

(B.18)

In particular, for a diffeomorphism we have
\delta_{\epsilon}=\pounds_{\xi}
and
m=\xi\lrcorner\,L
, which implies that

\displaystyle\delta m-\delta_{\epsilon}\theta=\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)+\xi\lrcorner\,\mathrm{d}\theta-\pounds_{\xi}\theta=\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)-\mathrm{d}(\xi\lrcorner\,\theta),

(B.19)

and we get

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\delta P+\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)+\oint_{S}\delta(Q-\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)+\pounds_{\xi}c-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta

(B.20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\delta P+\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)+\oint_{S}\delta(Q-\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)+\xi\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}c-\theta)

(B.21)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\delta P+\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)+\oint_{S}\delta(Q+\xi\lrcorner\,b-\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c).

(B.22)

This gives an integrable charge

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}Q(\xi)+\xi\lrcorner\,b-\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c.

(B.23)

In the case where
b=0=c
, we have instead the usual formula

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\int_{\Sigma}\delta P+\xi\lrcorner\,(\text{E}\wedge\delta\Phi)+\oint_{S}\delta Q-\xi\lrcorner\,\theta,

(B.24)

which we use in the main text when studying arbitrary diffeomorphisms without boundary conditions.

Under the assumption that
b
is covariant, we can compute the bracket of the charges (
B.23
) along the lines of
[
88
]
, we first note that

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\big(\delta Q(\zeta)\big)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,(\zeta\lrcorner\,\delta\omega e+\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega\delta e)

(B.25)

\displaystyle=\zeta\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}\omega e+\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega\pounds_{\xi}e

(B.26)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega e)-[\pounds_{\xi},\zeta\lrcorner\,]\omega e

(B.27)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega e)-[\xi,\zeta]\lrcorner\,\omega e

(B.28)

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}Q(\zeta)-Q([\xi,\zeta]),

(B.29)

which we have here illustrated in intermediate steps with the example of the MB charge for
2\sigma_{1}=1
and
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=0
. Furthermore, the Noether charge is defined by

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\theta-\xi\lrcorner\,L=\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,(\mathrm{d}c-\delta b)-\xi\lrcorner\,L=\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\mathrm{d}c-\pounds_{\xi}b-\xi\lrcorner\,L\approx\mathrm{d}Q(\xi).

(B.30)

With this we find the Poisson bracket

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\big(\delta\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big)

(B.31)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\pounds_{\xi}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\big(\delta Q(\zeta)+\zeta\lrcorner\,\delta b-\delta(\pounds_{\zeta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)\big)

(B.32)

\displaystyle=-\oint_{S}Q([\xi,\zeta])-\pounds_{\xi}Q(\zeta)-\zeta\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}b+\pounds_{\xi}(\pounds_{\zeta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)

(B.33)

\displaystyle=-\oint_{S}Q([\xi,\zeta])-\xi\lrcorner\,\mathrm{d}Q(\zeta)-\zeta\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}b+\xi\lrcorner\,\mathrm{d}(\pounds_{\zeta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)

(B.34)

\displaystyle\approx-\oint_{S}Q([\xi,\zeta])+\xi\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\zeta}b+\xi\lrcorner\,\zeta\lrcorner\,L-\zeta\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}b+\xi\lrcorner\,\big(\mathrm{d}(\pounds_{\zeta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,c)-\pounds_{\zeta}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\mathrm{d}c\big)

(B.35)

\displaystyle=-\oint_{S}Q([\xi,\zeta])+[\xi,\zeta]\lrcorner\,b+\pounds_{\zeta}(\xi\lrcorner\,b)+\xi\lrcorner\,\zeta\lrcorner\,L-\zeta\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}b

(B.36)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta])-\oint_{S}\xi\lrcorner\,\zeta\lrcorner\,(L+\mathrm{d}b).

(B.37)

This is the result for the bracket of the integrable diffeomorphism charges (
B.23
) under the assumption that
b
is covariant. It shows how the contributions in addition to the Noether charge in the Hamiltonian charge are responsible for the appearance of a central term in the algebra. Note however that this central extension is
not
the one of e.g. the BMS or Virasoro algebra derived in section
5
, as can be checked by an explicit calculation. This is because covariance of
b
was assumed in order to derive (
B.31
). Instead, a more general formula is given in
[
91
]
. It would be interesting to apply it on an explicit example, and show that it indeed reproduces e.g. the BMS or Virasoro central charge.

to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\alpha])

\displaystyle=2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\alpha](\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e)

(C.6)

\displaystyle=2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\alpha]\sigma_{3}e

(C.7)

\displaystyle=2p\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\alpha]\sigma_{2}e

(C.8)

\displaystyle=-2p\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,e,\alpha]\sigma_{2}\omega

(C.9)

\displaystyle=-2p\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,e,\alpha](\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega)

(C.10)

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{J}([\xi\lrcorner\,e,\alpha]).

(C.11)

Similarly, for the bracket (
3.25b
) we find

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=p\big\{\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}+\big\{\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

(C.12)

\displaystyle=p\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,e,\phi])+p\mathcal{J}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])+q\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])-2\oint_{S}\big(p\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\sigma_{3}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\big)\mathrm{d}\phi

(C.13)

\displaystyle=q\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])-2p\oint_{S}\big(\sigma_{1}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\sigma_{2}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\big)\mathrm{d}\phi

(C.14)

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi)+q\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]),

(C.15)

where for the third equality we have used the tangentiality of
\xi
to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,e,\phi])

\displaystyle=2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,e,\phi](\sigma_{1}\omega+\sigma_{3}e)

(C.16)

\displaystyle=2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,e,\phi]\sigma_{1}\omega

(C.17)

\displaystyle=-2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]\sigma_{1}e

(C.18)

\displaystyle=-2\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi](\sigma_{1}e+\sigma_{2}\omega)

(C.19)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{J}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]).

(C.20)

In order to compute the bracket (
3.25c
), we write

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{D}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\big\{\mathcal{D}(\zeta),\mathcal{C}(\xi)\big\}

(C.21)

\displaystyle=-p\big\{\mathcal{D}(\zeta),\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)\big\}-\big\{\mathcal{D}(\zeta),\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\big\}

(C.22)

\displaystyle=p\mathcal{J}\big(\pounds_{\zeta}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)\big)-p\mathcal{J}\big(\xi\lrcorner\,(\pounds_{\zeta}e)\big)+\mathcal{T}\big(\pounds_{\zeta}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)\big)-\mathcal{T}\big(\xi\lrcorner\,(\pounds_{\zeta}\omega)\big)

(C.23)

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{J}([\xi,\zeta]\lrcorner\,e)-\mathcal{T}([\xi,\zeta]\lrcorner\,\omega)

(C.24)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{C}([\xi,\zeta]).

(C.25)

Finally, for the bracket (
3.25d
) we have

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{C}(\zeta)\big\}

\displaystyle=p\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{J}(\zeta\lrcorner\,e)\big\}+\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega)\big\}

(C.26)

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{T}\big(\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,e)\big)-p\mathcal{J}\big(\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega)\big)+p\mathcal{J}\big(\zeta\lrcorner\,(\delta^{\text{c}}_{\xi}e)\big)+\mathcal{T}\big(\zeta\lrcorner\,(\delta^{\text{c}}_{\xi}\omega)\big)

(C.27)

\displaystyle\approx-p\mathcal{T}\big(\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,e)\big)-p\mathcal{J}\big(\pounds_{\xi}(\zeta\lrcorner\,\omega)\big)+p\mathcal{J}\big(\zeta\lrcorner\,(\pounds_{\xi}\omega)\big)+p\mathcal{T}\big(\zeta\lrcorner\,(\pounds_{\xi}e)\big)

(C.28)

\displaystyle=-p\mathcal{D}([\xi,\zeta]).

(C.29)

In the second equality, the first two terms come from the field-independent brackets (
3.25a
) and (
3.25b
), and the last two terms come from the action of
\mathcal{C}(\xi)
on the field dependency of the argument. The third equality uses the on-shell expression (
3.20
) for the dual diffeomorphisms. We have used everywhere the fact that the cubic charges vanish identically in the case of tangent vector fields
\xi
and
\zeta
, i.e.
\mathcal{J}([\xi\lrcorner\,\Phi_{1},\zeta\lrcorner\,\Phi_{2}])=0=\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\Phi_{1},\zeta\lrcorner\,\Phi_{2}])
for any two fields
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in(e,\omega)
.

Finally, let us point out that, if we define
\mathcal{C}(\xi)
as
\mathcal{C}(\xi)=\alpha\mathcal{J}(\xi\lrcorner\,e)+\beta\mathcal{T}(\xi\lrcorner\,\omega)
, the integrability condition obtained in (
3.17
) becomes
\alpha\sigma_{2}=\beta\sigma_{3}
. The bracket (
3.25b
), on the other hand, becomes

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\phi)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi)+(\beta p-\alpha)\mathcal{J}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])+\beta q\mathcal{T}([\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi])

(C.30)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi)+2\big(\beta(p\sigma_{1}+q\sigma_{3})-\alpha\sigma_{1}\big)\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]e

(C.31)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathcal{J}(\pounds_{\xi}\phi)+2(\beta\sigma_{0}-\alpha\sigma_{1})\oint_{S}[\xi\lrcorner\,\omega,\phi]e.

(C.32)

One can therefore see that the last term can be cancelled by choosing
\alpha\sigma_{1}=\beta\sigma_{0}
. Together with the integrability condition
\alpha\sigma_{2}=\beta\sigma_{3}
, this in turn implies that
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{2}
, i.e. that
q=0
. We therefore recover consistently the condition given in the main text.

Fourier representation of the algebra

Let us first introduce the distributional currents
\mathcal{J}^{i}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}
, which are functions on
S
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\alpha)=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)\alpha_{i}(\varphi),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{T}(\phi)=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathcal{T}^{i}(\varphi)\phi_{i}(\varphi).

(C.33)

In terms of these distributional currents, the Poisson brackets in (
3.21
) become

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi),\mathcal{T}^{j}(\varphi^{\prime})\big\}

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi)\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})+2\sigma_{1}\eta^{ij}\partial_{\varphi}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}),

(C.34a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi),\mathcal{J}^{j}(\varphi^{\prime})\big\}

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\mathcal{J}^{k}(\varphi)\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})+2\sigma_{2}\eta^{ij}\partial_{\varphi}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}),

(C.34b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{T}^{i}(\varphi),\mathcal{T}^{j}(\varphi^{\prime})\big\}

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\big(p\mathcal{J}^{k}(\varphi)+q\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi)\big)\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})+2\sigma_{3}\eta^{ij}\partial_{\varphi}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}).

(C.34c)

Note the change of sign in the central terms with respect to (
3.21
). Let us now define the associated Fourier modes

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n}\coloneqq\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n}\coloneqq\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\mathcal{T}^{i}(\varphi).

(C.35)

The Fourier brackets are easily computed. For example, we have

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{j}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi^{\prime}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi^{\prime}}\big\{\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi),\mathcal{J}^{j}(\varphi^{\prime})\big\}

(C.36)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{k}(\varphi)+2\sigma_{2}\eta^{ij}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi^{\prime}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi-\mathrm{i}m\varphi^{\prime}}\partial_{\varphi}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})

(C.37)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{k}(\varphi)-2\sigma_{2}\eta^{ij}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi^{\prime}\,\partial_{\varphi}e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi-\mathrm{i}m\varphi^{\prime}}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})

(C.38)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{k}(\varphi)+2\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}n\eta^{ij}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}

(C.39)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\mathcal{J}^{k}_{n+m}+2\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}n\eta^{ij}\delta_{n+m,0}

(C.40)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{ij}}_{k}\mathcal{J}^{k}_{n+m}-2\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}m\eta^{ij}\delta_{n+m,0},

(C.41)

and similarly for the other brackets. Let us now introduce the Fourier modes

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\xi=e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}(\xi=e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}),

(C.42)

where we have to recall that integrability of
\mathcal{C}
requires tangentiality and
\sigma_{3}=p\sigma_{2}
. For the brackets of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
with the previous Fourier modes, we first compute

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{J}(\alpha)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\partial_{\varphi}\alpha_{i}(\varphi)

(C.43)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}\big(\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\big)\alpha_{i}(\varphi)

(C.44)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)\big\}\alpha_{i}(\varphi),

(C.45)

which gives

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi}\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)\big\}

(C.46)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi}\partial_{\varphi}\big(\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\big)

(C.47)

\displaystyle=-\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}

(C.48)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}m\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m},

(C.49)

and similarly for
\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}
. Finally, computing
\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}
is immediate using the commutator
[\xi,\zeta]=\xi\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta\xi^{\prime}
, and we find

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{m}\big\}=-\mathrm{i}(n-m)\mathcal{D}_{n+m}.

(C.50)

We proceed in the same way to obtain the brackets involving
\mathcal{C}
. The only subtlety is for the bracket (
3.25b
), which has a field-dependent term on the right-hand side. To evaluate this in Fourier space, we first compute

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi}\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}(\varphi)\big\}

(C.51)

\displaystyle=p\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi}\partial_{\varphi}\big(\mathcal{J}^{i}(\varphi)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\big)-q{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\omega^{j}_{\varphi}\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi)

(C.52)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}pm\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-q{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\omega^{j}_{\varphi}\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi).

(C.53)

The first term is part of the what we expect. For the second term, we use

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{i}=2(\sigma_{1}e_{\varphi}^{i}+\sigma_{2}\omega_{\varphi}^{i}),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{T}^{i}=2(\sigma_{1}\omega_{\varphi}^{i}+\sigma_{3}e_{\varphi}^{i})=2(\sigma_{1}\omega_{\varphi}^{i}+p\sigma_{2}e_{\varphi}^{i}),

(C.54)

to write

\displaystyle\omega_{\varphi}^{i}=\frac{p\sigma_{2}\mathcal{J}^{i}-\sigma_{1}\mathcal{T}^{i}}{2(p\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})},

(C.55)

which gives

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}=\mathrm{i}pm\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-\frac{pq\sigma_{2}}{2(p\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{j}(\varphi)\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi).

(C.56)

The last step of the computation is then to insert a Dirac delta in the remaining integral to obtain the Fourier expression

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}pm\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-\frac{pq\sigma_{2}}{2(p\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m)\varphi}\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})\mathcal{J}^{j}(\varphi)\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi^{\prime})

(C.57)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}pm\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-\frac{pq\sigma_{2}}{2(p\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(n+m+k)\varphi}\mathcal{J}^{j}(\varphi)\oint_{S}\mathrm{d}\varphi^{\prime}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi^{\prime}(-k)}\mathcal{T}^{k}(\varphi^{\prime})

(C.58)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}pm\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-\frac{pq\sigma_{2}}{2(p\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{k}_{-k}.

(C.59)

Details on the Sugawara construction

The brackets of the quadratics
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
with
\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}
are given by

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}m\big(\sigma_{1}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}+\sigma_{2}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m}\big),

(C.60a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}m\big(\sigma_{3}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}+\sigma_{1}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m}\big)-2q{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{k}_{-k},

(C.60b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{2},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}m\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m},

(C.60c)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{2},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}m\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m}+2p{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{k}_{-k},

(C.60d)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{3},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}m\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m},

(C.60e)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{3},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}m\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m}-2{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{k}_{-k}.

(C.60f)

For the brackets of the
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
’s with themselves we find

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{1}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{2}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{3},

(C.61a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{2},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n+m},

(C.61b)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{3},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{3}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n+m},

(C.61c)

and

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}m\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n+m}-8\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k},

(C.62a)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{3}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}m\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n+m}-8\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m+n+k}

(C.62b)

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}n\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n+m}+8\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k},

(C.62c)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{3},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}m\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-8\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}.

(C.62d)

The bracket (
C.61a
) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{1},\mathcal{Q}_{m}^{1}\big\}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\{\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\big\}

(C.63)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+k}\big\{\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\big\}+\big\{\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+k}\big\}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\big)

(C.64)

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big((-k)\big(\sigma_{3}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n-k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+k}+\sigma_{1}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n-k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+k}\big)+(m+k)\big(\sigma_{1}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+\sigma_{2}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m+n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\big)\Big)

(C.65)

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(-(n+k)\big(\sigma_{3}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}+\sigma_{1}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\big)+(m+k)\big(\sigma_{1}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+\sigma_{2}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m+n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}\big)\Big)

(C.66)

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(m+k)\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(n+k)\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}

(C.67)

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}m\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{2}+4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}n\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{3}-4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\big(\sigma_{2}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+\sigma_{3}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}\big)

(C.68)

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(m-n)\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}+4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}n\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{2}-4\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}m\mathcal{Q}_{n+m}^{3}+4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\big(\sigma_{2}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+\sigma_{3}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}\big),

(C.69)

where for the last equality we have changed the summation variable as
k\to-(n+m+k)
. This allow to switch the sign of the sum over
k
without changing the argument, and we can then add the last two lines to find the result. The same trick has been used for the brackets (
C.62c
) and (
C.62a
).

In order to find the combination of quadratics which is well-behaved and reproduces
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we define
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}\coloneqq\alpha\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n}+\beta\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n}+\gamma\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n}
. With this we find

\displaystyle\big\{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n},\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}m(\alpha\sigma_{1}+\gamma\sigma_{2})\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+n}-4\mathrm{i}m(\beta\sigma_{1}+\alpha\sigma_{2})\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m+n},

(C.71a)

\displaystyle\big\{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n},\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=-4\mathrm{i}m(\gamma\sigma_{1}+\alpha\sigma_{3})\mathcal{J}^{i}_{m+n}-4\mathrm{i}m(\beta\sigma_{3}+\alpha\sigma_{1})\mathcal{T}^{i}_{m+n}-2{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}(\alpha q-\beta p+\gamma)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n+m+k}\mathcal{T}^{k}_{-k},

(C.71b)

and

\displaystyle\big\{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n},\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\big\}=-4\mathrm{i}(m-n)\Big(

\displaystyle(\alpha^{2}\sigma_{1}+\beta\gamma\sigma_{1}+\alpha\gamma\sigma_{2}+\alpha\beta\sigma_{3})\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{n+m}

(C.72)

\displaystyle+\,

\displaystyle(2\alpha\beta\sigma_{1}+\alpha^{2}\sigma_{2}+\beta^{2}\sigma_{3})\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{n+m}

(C.73)

\displaystyle+\,

\displaystyle(2\alpha\gamma\sigma_{1}+\gamma^{2}\sigma_{2}+\alpha^{2}\sigma_{3})\mathcal{Q}^{3}_{n+m}\Big).

(C.74)

These brackets can be compared with the ones involving
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in (
3.27
) in order to identify which triple
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
give the Sugawara expression
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}
for
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. A similar calculation can be done for the teleparallel dual charge
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Twisted Sugawara construction and central extensions

The Sugawara construction can be generalized to obtain centrally-extended algebras of quadratics, such as the diffeomorphisms or their duals introduced here. This is done via the so-called twisted Sugawara construction. This corresponds in fact to a particular choice of boundary conditions such that the quadratic charges (e.g. of diffeomorphisms) are also defined for non-tangent vector fields, as was the case in e.g. (
4.28
).

The twisted construction is based on a first order linear twist of the quadratics
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
introduced in (
3.31
). More specifically, we define

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+2n\lambda^{i}\mathcal{J}_{n}^{i}+2n\mu^{i}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i},

(C.75a)

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{T}^{i}_{-k}+2n\lambda^{i}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i},

(C.75b)

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{n+k}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{-k}+2n\mu^{i}\mathcal{J}_{n}^{i},

(C.75c)

where

Appendix D
Symplectic symmetries, Kosmann derivative, and Fefferman–Graham gauge

We have seen in (
5.18
) that the first order diffeomorphism charge for the vector field (
5.16
) contains an
r^{-1}
dependency. This is fine as long as we focus on asymptotic symmetries. Without the asymptotic limit, the full diffeomorphism charge (
5.18
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)=\oint_{S}\text{finite}-\frac{\partial_{\varphi}f}{r}\left(\sigma_{2}\mathscr{M}+(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\mathscr{N}}{2}\right).

(D.1)

We therefore see that even in the case
q\sigma_{2}=0
, where the charge can also be computed in metric variables, this charge does not a priori match the corresponding metric expression. Indeed, one can see e.g. in equation (2.13) of
[
85
]
that the
r^{-1}
dependency drops there, and that the asymptotic symmetries then become so-called symplectic symmetries which can be defined at any finite distance
r
.

This discrepancy is due to the fact that we are here considering a first order formulation in terms of connection and triad variables. Clearly, we have to do so because it is the only formulation in which the general Lagrangian (
2.1
) can be written. We therefore cannot go back to a metric formulation for the general diffeomorphism charges with all independent couplings present. However, a way to resolve the tension is to use the Kosmann derivative. In three spacetime dimensions it is defined as

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{\xi}\coloneqq\pounds_{\xi}+\delta^{\text{j}}_{\lambda(\xi,e)},

(D.2)

which is the composition of the ordinary Lie derivative with a field-dependent Lorentz transformation whose parameter is

\displaystyle\lambda(\xi,e)=-\frac{1}{2}[\hat{e}\lrcorner\,\pounds_{\xi}e],\qquad\qquad\lambda^{i}(\xi,e)=-\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}\hat{e}^{\mu j}\pounds_{\xi}e^{k}_{\mu},

(D.3)

where
\hat{e}^{\mu i}=g^{\mu\nu}e^{i}_{\nu}
is the inverse triad (see
[
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
,
97
]
for the four-dimensional definition and other properties). This derivative has the property that
\mathscr{K}_{\xi}e=0
when
\xi
is Killing. In order to see this, we can compute explicitly the field-dependent Lorentz transformation

\displaystyle\delta^{\text{j}}_{\lambda}e^{i}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=[e_{\alpha},\lambda]^{i}

(D.4)

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}e^{j}_{\alpha}\lambda^{k}

(D.5)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon^{i}}_{jk}{\varepsilon^{k}}_{lm}e^{j}_{\alpha}\hat{e}^{\mu l}\pounds_{\xi}e^{m}_{\mu}

(D.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\pounds_{\xi}e^{i}_{\alpha}-\eta_{jm}e^{j}_{\alpha}\hat{e}^{\mu i}\pounds_{\xi}e^{m}_{\mu})

(D.7)

\displaystyle=-\pounds_{\xi}e^{i}_{\alpha},

(D.8)

where we have used (
A.2b
), and in the last step the fact that
\xi
is an exact Killing vector to write
\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=0=\eta_{jm}(\pounds_{\xi}e^{j}_{\mu}e^{m}_{\nu}+e^{j}_{\mu}\pounds_{\xi}e^{m}_{\nu})
.

In order to compute the charge associated with the Kosmann derivative, we can simply add to (
5.18
) the charge
\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\lambda)
of the field-dependent Lorentz transformation. This latter is found to be

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{r}\oint_{S}\partial_{\varphi}f\left(\sigma_{2}\delta\mathscr{M}+(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})\frac{\delta\mathscr{N}}{2}\right),

(D.9)

and is therefore integrable. We see that this charge cancels exactly the
r^{-1}
contribution in (
D.1
), thereby leading to an
r
-independent charge associated with the Kosmann derivative. The Bondi gauge asymptotic symmetries (
5.16
) can then be promoted to symplectic symmetries and one finds that the algebra of charges is valid at any place in the bulk. This mechanism can also be studied in Fefferman–Graham gauge.

Let us consider the solution to Einstein equations with Brown–Henneaux boundary conditions in Fefferman–Graham gauge. With coordinates
x^{\mu}=(x^{+},x^{-},r)
, this is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}\mathrm{d}r^{2}-\left(r\mathrm{d}x^{+}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r}L^{-}(x^{-})\mathrm{d}x^{-}\right)\left(r\mathrm{d}x^{-}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r}L^{+}(x^{+})\mathrm{d}x^{+}\right).

(D.10)

Working with the internal metric (
5.14
), a compatible set of triad and connection components are given by

\displaystyle e^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell^{2}L_{+}/(2r)&r&0\\
-r/2&-\ell^{2}L_{-}/r&0\\
0&0&\ell/r\end{pmatrix},\qquad\qquad\omega^{i}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell L_{+}/(2r)&r/\ell&0\\
r/(2\ell)&\ell L_{-}/r&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}-\frac{q}{2}e^{i}_{\mu}.

(D.11)

The asymptotic Killing vector field for this geometry is
\xi=(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},\xi^{r})
with

\displaystyle\xi^{\pm}=f^{\pm}(x^{\pm})+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2r^{2}}\partial_{\mp}^{2}f^{\mp}(x^{\mp})+\mathcal{O}(r^{-4}),\qquad\qquad\xi^{r}=-\frac{r}{2}\big(\partial_{+}f^{+}(x^{+})+\partial_{-}f^{-}(x^{-})\big).

(D.12)

With this, the diffeomorphism charge with the GHY boundary term is found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}Q+\sigma_{1}\xi\lrcorner\,(e\wedge\omega)

(D.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{S}\xi^{+}L^{+}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})+2\sigma_{2}\big)+\xi^{-}L^{-}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})-2\sigma_{2}\big)

(D.14)

\displaystyle=\oint_{S}\text{finite}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{4r^{2}}\Big(\partial^{2}_{-}f^{-}L^{+}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})+2\sigma_{2}\big)+\partial^{2}_{+}f^{+}L^{-}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})-2\sigma_{2}\big)\Big)+\mathcal{O}(r^{-4}),

where in these coordinates the integral is along
\varphi=(x^{+}-x^{-})/2
. We can now compute the charge of the field-dependent Lorentz transformation entering the definition of the Kosmann derivative. It is found to be

\displaystyle\not{\delta}\mathcal{J}(\lambda)=-\frac{\ell^{2}}{4r^{2}}\oint_{S}\partial^{2}_{-}f^{-}\delta L^{+}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})+2\sigma_{2}\big)+\partial^{2}_{+}f^{+}\delta L^{-}\big(\ell(2\sigma_{1}-q\sigma_{2})-2\sigma_{2}\big)+\mathcal{O}(r^{-4}),

(D.15)

and we see at order
r^{-2}
that this cancels the opposite contribution in the first order diffeomorphism charge. The same thing presumably happens at subleading order.
