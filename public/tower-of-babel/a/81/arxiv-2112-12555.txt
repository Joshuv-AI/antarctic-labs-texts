Optimal learning of high-dimensional classification problems using deep neural networks
Philipp Petersen, Felix Voigtlaender

Abstract
We study the problem of learning classification functions from noiseless training samples , under the assumption that the decision boundary is of a certain regularity. We establish universal lower bounds for this estimation problem, for general classes of continuous decision boundaries. For the class of locally Barron-regular decision boundaries, we find that the optimal estimation rates are essentially independent of the underlying dimension and can be realized by empirical risk minimization methods over a suitable class of deep neural networks. These results are based on novel estimates of the L 1 L^{1} and L ∞ L^{\infty} entropies of the class of Barron-regular functions.



Keywords:

Minimax bounds,
noiseless training data,
deep neural networks,
classification,
Barron class,
metric entropy

Introduction

In this work, we consider the estimation of functions
in the context of (binary) classification problems.
That is, we consider i.i.d. random variables

(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, where
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}

and where the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
follow an unknown joint distribution denoted by
\rho_{X,Y}
.
The task is to infer from
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
a classifier
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{0,1\}

such that the
misclassification error

\mathbb{P}(g(X)\neq Y)
is as small as possible.
It can be shown, see
[
12
, Page 2]
,
that there exists an optimal classifier
g_{*}
, i.e.,

g_{*}=\argmin_{g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{0,1\}\text{ measurable}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(g(X)\neq Y\bigr).

Setting
D_{0}\coloneqq g_{*}^{-1}(\{0\})
and
D_{1}\coloneqq g_{*}^{-1}(\{1\})
,
we observe that
g_{*}
produces a segmentation of the input space into two so-called
decision regions
.
We also call
\partial D_{0}=\overline{D_{0}}\cap\overline{D_{1}}=\partial D_{1}

the
decision boundary
of the classification problem.

In this work, we study the complexity of the learning problem associated
to the given classification problem, i.e.,
the number of observations
m
that are required to find a classifier with a small error.
More precisely, we will derive upper as well as lower bounds for this problem,
showing that this complexity is primarily influenced by the regularity of the decision boundary.
In particular,
we will identify properties of the decision boundary
under which the rate of decay of the generalization error
(with the number of training samples going to infinity)
is essentially independent of the ambient dimension.

1.1
Overview of results

We establish the following results:

1.

General Lower Bounds on Estimation Problems For Classifiers:

We start in Section
2
by making precise our notion of
classification functions satisfying a general smoothness assumption
concerning the decision boundaries.
For these classification functions, we study lower bounds for the estimation problem
in the
realizable or noiseless case
, i.e., we are given data points

\bigl(X_{i},f(X_{i})\bigr)_{i=1}^{m}
for
f
being the target classifier.

The reason for studying the case of noiseless (training) data is
that in results with noisy training data, it is sometimes hard to
understand which limits regarding the optimal learning rate result from the regularity
of the function to be learned and which limits stem from the noisy nature of the samples.
For instance, it is known that using noisy data, even for very simple problems
like estimating the mean of a (Gaussian) probability distribution,
one cannot improve on the rate
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1/2})
.
In contrast, it was recently shown that one can estimate Sobolev functions
of high smoothness with a high convergence rate, based on
noise-free random samples

[
28
]
.
See Subsection
1.3
for more details.
Of course, both of these problems concern the problem
of estimating
real-valued quantities
, whereas in a classification problem
the samples
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
are discrete-valued.
But in this setting, it is even more natural to consider noise-free samples.

The main result that we establish is Theorem
3.14
.
This result is based on classical estimation bounds by Yang and Barron

[
44
]
for the problem of
density estimation
;
the first step in our analysis is thus to translate our setting
into a density estimation problem.
The obtained result shows that if one knows
a priori
that the
decision boundary of the ground-truth classifier
f

is described by a function from a class
\mathscr{C}
,
and if the
L^{1}
metric entropy
1
1

1

As usual, the metric entropy of
\mathscr{C}
is the logarithm of the cardinality
of the smallest collection of
L^{1}
-balls of radius
\varepsilon
that covers
\mathscr{C}
.

of
\mathscr{C}

scales (up to log factors) with
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
like
\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}
,
then at least
m
i.i.d. observations
\bigl(X_{i},f(X_{i})\bigr)_{i=1}^{m}
are necessary
to learn
f
up to a squared
L^{2}
-error of order
m^{-\alpha/(\alpha+1)}
.
This hardness result applies to all possible estimation algorithms.
Our result also shows that there exists a learning algorithm
that essentially matches this lower bound.

2.

Entropy of the Barron Class:

The Barron class introduced in

[
4
,
3
,
5
]

has recently received increased interest due to the ability of neural networks
to approximate functions from this class with a rate independent of the ambient dimension.
We thus consider classification problems where the decision boundary is Barron regular.
Such classification functions were recently studied in
[
9
]
,
but the question of the
optimal
rate for learning such classification functions
was left open.
To apply the previous lower bounds,
we need to establish precise upper and lower bounds on the entropy numbers
of the class of Barron functions.
In particular, we will require these estimates for the
L^{1}
entropy.

In Proposition
4.6

and Proposition
4.4
, we establish upper bounds
on the
L^{\infty}
entropy and lower bounds on the
L^{1}
-entropy of the Barron class.

These bounds for the entropy numbers are quite sharp;
the upper and lower bounds only differ by a log factor.
To our knowledge, no sharp bounds on the
L^{\infty}
entropy numbers of the Barron class
were known up to now.

Combined with our general bounds for the learning problem of sets with regular boundaries,
these entropy bounds imply that the optimal estimation bounds for classification problems
with Barron regular decision boundaries are essentially dimension independent.
More precisely, for an optimal learning algorithm based on
m
noise-free training samples,
the squared
L^{2}
-error scales like
m^{-1/(1+\frac{2d}{2+d})}
, where the
exponent is
\approx-\frac{1}{3}
for moderately large
d
.

3.

Learning Barron Regular Boundaries by Empirical Risk Minimization Using Neural Networks:

Finally, we demonstrate in Section
5
that the lower bounds
on the estimation problem of classification problems with Barron regular decision boundary
can almost be realized by employing empirical risk minimization
using the Hinge loss over appropriate sets of deep neural networks.
This is based on combining a recent approximation result for classification functions
with Barron regular boundaries
[
9
]
with a framework
for quantifying the performance of empirical risk minimization
with the Hinge loss that was developed in
[
24
]
.
Overall,
this demonstrates that deep neural network-based learning can achieve
dimension independent learning rates of high-dimensional and discontinuous functions.

1.2
Functions with Barron boundary

As mentioned above, we study classifier functions with decision boundaries
that are (locally) functions of Barron-regularity.
The Barron class was introduced in

[
3
,
4
,
5
]

as the set of functions with the first Fourier moment being bounded by a fixed constant.
It was then demonstrated that these functions can be approximated by shallow neural networks
with sigmoidal activation functions at an approximation rate
that is independent of the underlying dimension.
Based on this result, several authors have interpreted the term

Barron regular functions
to encompass all those functions
that are well approximated by few ridge functions
and hence vary mainly along few designated directions.
This interpretation can also be turned into a definition of generalized Barron spaces,
as has been done in, e.g.,

[
14
,
13
,
15
,
16
]
.

In
[
9
]
, classifier functions with decision boundaries of Barron regularity were studied.
Assuming Barron regular decision boundaries is natural
in light of the above interpretation, since one expects the decision
to which class a certain example belongs to

locally mostly depend on a few features of the data
.

In this context, it should be mentioned that an intuitively reasonable
alternative model of high dimensional classification functions
with the properties above could be set up by considering all functions
\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\circ f
,
where
f
is a classical Barron function.
The decision boundaries of these functions correspond to the 0-level sets of Barron functions.
However, since every compactly supported
C^{\infty}
function has a finite Fourier moment,
we can find for every set of points a smooth function that takes any desired sign pattern
on these points and has a finite Fourier moment.
Rescaling this function will not change the sign pattern
but can decrease the Fourier moment to an arbitrarily small constant.
As a result, the function class introduced above will have an infinite VC-dimension
and is therefore not (PAC) learnable due to the fundamental theorem of learning,
see, e.g.
[
31
, Theorem 3.20]

or
[
37
, Theorem 6.7]
.

1.3
Noiseless measurements

In many learning scenarios, it makes an
immense difference
whether one considers
noisy or noise-free training data.
For instance, for learning a function
f\in C^{k}([0,1]^{d})
with
\|f\|_{C^{k}}\leq 1

from noisy samples of the form
S=\big((X_{i},Y_{i})\big)_{i=1}^{m}

with
X_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}U([0,1]^{d})
and
Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}

with, e.g.,
\varepsilon_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^{2})
for a fixed
\sigma>0
,
it is known (see e.g.
[
40
]
) that
the optimal rate of decay for
\mathbb{E}_{S}\|f-\widehat{f}_{m}\|_{L^{q}}
(with
q<\infty
),
where
\widehat{f}_{m}=\widehat{f}_{m}(S)
is an estimate of
f
based on the training sample
S
,
is
\mathcal{O}(m^{-k/(2k+d)})
; in particular, even for
k\gg d
, the error decays
slower than
m^{-1/2}
.
In contrast, if in the above scenario one has
\sigma=0
(i.e,
Y_{i}=f(X_{i})
),
it was recently shown
[
28
]

that one can achieve
\mathbb{E}_{S}\|f-\widehat{f}_{m}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim m^{-k/d}
,
meaning that
random points are (essentially) as good as optimally chosen deterministic
sampling points
X_{i}
and that one can achieve high convergence rates if
k\gg d
,
in stark contrast to the setting of noisy measurements.

One motivation for considering the questions addressed in the present paper
is to clarify whether a similarly stark contrast occurs for the problem of learning
a (discrete-valued) classification function instead of a (real-valued) regression function.
Intuitively, the influence of noise should be much less pronounced in this case,
since one knows that
f(X_{i})\in\{0,1\}
, so that at least any
bounded
noise
\varepsilon_{i}

with
|\varepsilon_{i}|<1/2
can be easily removed.
Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this problem has not been explicitly
considered in the literature before.
The result in the literature that is most related to the findings presented here is

[
22
]
, where minimax rates were
developed for the problem of learning piecewise smooth functions
from
noisy
measurements.
These functions are essentially of the form
f=g\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Omega}

(or finite sums of functions of this form), where
g\in C^{\beta}([0,1]^{d})

and
\Omega\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a set with boundary of regularity
\partial\Omega\in C^{\alpha}
.
Our results can thus be seen as the special case where
g\equiv 1
,
but where
noise-free
training samples are given.

1.4
Related work

1.4.1
Work regarding learning of classifier functions with smooth decision boundary

This article studies estimation by deep neural networks and is thus
part of the literature on mathematical analysis of deep learning.
Given the vast amount of literature in this area, we here
only review a very small subset of this field, namely results related to
the learning of classifier functions with smoothness conditions on the decision boundaries;
we refer to
[
35
,
17
,
7
]

for broader literature reviews.

To the best of our knowledge,
[
30
]
is the first work
that studies the complexity of classification through a smoothness assumption
on the classification regions.
These results are further developed in
[
41
]
.
The main observation is that the learnability of the associated classification functions
is determined by a specific notion of entropy of the underlying sets and a noise condition.
In
[
21
,
22
]
,
estimation of classifiers with locally Hölder continuous decision boundaries

from noisy measurements
is studied.
It is shown that in many cases, classical linear estimation methods cannot achieve
the optimal rates that deep neural networks yield.
The lower bounds are established by invoking the results
of Yang and Barron
[
44
]
.
This was the main inspiration for the analysis of lower bounds
in the noiseless case that we present in this work.
The paper
[
24
]
considers a somewhat similar problem,
albeit with a focus on a specific learning algorithm based on the Hinge loss.
For this algorithm,
[
24
]
develops estimation bounds
for various types of classification problems,
one of which involves a smoothness condition on the decision boundary.

Finally, our previous work
[
9
]
already studied estimation bounds for functions
with Barron regular boundaries.
However, in that study, a substantial gap between the upper and lower estimation bounds remained.
The goal of the present paper is to close this gap.

1.4.2
Work regarding entropy numbers for Barron-type spaces

The function class(es) nowadays called
Barron functions
or
Barron-type functions

were originally introduced in
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Already in these papers,
two different classes of functions
were considered:
One class of functions (prominently considered in
[
5
]
) are those having a
Fourier-like representation of the form

f(x)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(e^{i\langle\omega,x\rangle}-1\bigr)\,d\mu(\omega)

(1.1)

for a complex-valued measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\omega|d|\mu|(\omega)\leq C
,
where often the measure
\mu
is assumed to be absolutely continuous with respect to the
Lebesgue measure, i.e.,
d\mu(\omega)=F(\omega)\,d\omega
.
The second class of functions consists of the closed convex hull of the set

\big\{x\mapsto\gamma\cdot\phi(\langle w,x\rangle+b)\,\,\colon\,\,|\gamma|\leq C,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b\in\mathbb{R}\big\},

(1.2)

at least when
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a sigmoidal (or at least bounded) activation function;
for unbounded activation functions one has to restrict the magnitude of the parameters

w,b
suitably; see for instance
[
16
]
.

The original insights of Barron were that

i)
due to an elementary property of convex closures in Hilbert spaces,
the functions belonging to the “second Barron class” (
1.2
)
can be approximated up to
L^{2}
error of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

using shallow neural networks with
n
neurons; see
[
4
, Lemma 1]
,
and
ii)
for sigmoidal activation functions
\phi
,
the “second Barron class” (
1.2
) contains the
“first Barron class” (
1.1
).
We remark, however, that
this inclusion fails for other activation functions like the ReLU
;
see
[
9
, Section 7]
for a detailed discussion, which is partly based on
observations from
[
16
]
and
[
26
]
.

In
Section

4
of the present paper, we derive upper and lower bounds
for the (Kolmogorov metric) entropy numbers of the “first Barron class”
(
1.1
) with respect to the
L^{\infty}
metric (for upper bounds)
and the
L^{1}
metric (for lower bounds).
To the best of our knowledge, these bounds are novel results.
Similar bounds for the
L^{2}
metric (more generally, for
L^{q}
with
q<\infty
) instead
of the
L^{\infty}
metric and for quite general “Barron-type spaces”
as in (
1.2
) (instead of as in (
1.1
))
were recently obtained in
[
38
]
.
Such entropy bounds are useful for a diverse range of applications in statistical learning theory;
see for instance
[
44
,
41
]
.
Furthermore, there is a close connection between approximation results
and entropy bounds; see for instance
[
33
, Proposition 3.6]
,

[
19
, Section 5.4]
, and
[
38
, Theorem 9]
.
Precisely, approximation bounds imply upper bounds on the entropy numbers;
equivalently, lower bounds on the entropy numbers entail lower bounds for approximation.

For this reason, the existing literature regarding approximation of Barron-type
functions is also relevant for the question of entropy numbers considered here.
The original bounds by Barron
[
3
,
4
,
5
]

show that Barron functions can be approximated (in
L^{2}
) by (shallow) neural networks
at a rate that is
independent of the ambient dimension
;
namely, networks with
n
neurons can achieve
L^{2}
error
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.
Makovoz
[
29
]
showed
for a slightly smaller set
of Barron-type functions
that one can even achieve approximation error

\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2d}})\sqrt{\ln(1+n)}
in the uniform norm.
For the case where one assumes
two
finite Fourier moments
(i.e.,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\omega|^{2}\,d|\mu|(\omega)<\infty
) in (
1.1
),
it was shown in
[
27
, Theorem 2]

that networks with
n
neurons can achieve
L^{\infty}
error of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2-1/d})
.
In
[
2
]
, it was even shown that functions belonging to
the “second Barron space” (
1.2
) associated to the ReLU
can be approximated uniformly up to error
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{2}d})

using shallow ReLU networks with
n
neurons.
We note that this provides a further proof of the fact
(observed in
[
9
, Proposition 7.4]
) that the “first Barron space”
from
Equation

1.1
is
not
included in the “second Barron space”
associated to the ReLU, since the approximation rate derived in
[
2
]

beats the optimal approximation rate for the “first Barron space,”
as implied by our entropy results.

All these results showing that Barron-type functions can be approximated
by neural networks with a dimension-independent approximation rate
are usually cited as evidence that neural networks can overcome the curse of dimension.
While this is certainly true, it has been noted by Candès
[
8
, Section 7.2]

that also other representation systems—and in particular the Fourier basis—achieve
L^{2}
approximation error
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2-1/d})
for functions
from the “first Barron class” (
1.1
).
The deep results in
[
11
]
by DeVore and Temlyakov
imply (after a bit of work; see
Lemma

4.5
and
Proposition

4.4
)
that a similar result also holds for
L^{\infty}
approximation;
this is the basis for our estimate of the
L^{\infty}

entropy numbers of the Barron class.

2
Sets with regular boundaries

In this article, we are interested in upper and lower bounds
for the learning problem of
estimating classification functions
with decision boundaries that are locally of Barron regularity
.
Since there are various slightly different interpretations of the term “Barron function” in the literature,
we start by making a precise definition of this notion for the rest of this work.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
A function
f:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
is said to be of Barron class with constant
C>0
,
if there exist a measurable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
and some
c\in[-C,C]
satisfying

f(x)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1\bigr)\cdot F(\xi)\,d\xi\text{ for all }x\in[0,1]^{d}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|\cdot|F(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C.

We write
B(C)=B_{d}(C)
for the class of all such functions.

Regarding the lower bounds (i.e., hardness results) for the learning problem,
we will derive these in a more general setting
since this does not involve any additional difficulties
and more clearly exhibits those properties of the boundary class that
determine the difficulty of the learning problem.
Therefore, we will formally introduce classification functions with decision boundaries
that are locally of a
general
type of regularity;
classifiers with Barron regular decision boundaries will then be a special case of this notion.

To prepare the definition of classification functions with regular decision boundaries,
we first consider so-called
general Horizon functions
:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.
Given any function
b:[0,1]^{d-1}\to[0,1]
,
define the associated
general Horizon function
as

h_{b}:\hskip 10.00002pt[0,1]^{d}\to\{0,1\},\hskip 10.00002ptx=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\mapsto\mathds{1}_{b(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1})\leq x_{d}}.

Given a set
\varnothing\neq\mathscr{C}\subset C([0,1]^{d-1};[0,1])
,
define the set of
general horizon functions
associated to
\mathscr{C}
as

H_{\mathscr{C}}\coloneqq\left\{h_{b}\colon b\in\mathscr{C}\right\}.

Remark
.

The definition above differs slightly from alternative definitions in the literature,
such as
[
4
,
9
]
, by the additional requirement
that the functions only take values in
[0,1]
.

We will often use, for a given
h\in H_{\mathscr{C}}
,
the associated function
b\in\mathscr{C}
(called the
boundary-defining function
)
such that
h=h_{b}
.
Since in the setting of
Definition

2.2

the map
\mathscr{C}\to H_{\mathscr{C}}\colon b\mapsto h_{b}
is bijective,
this operation is well-defined.

We now define classifiers with regular decision boundaries as those functions
that are locally described by general Horizon functions.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
M,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
and let
\varnothing\neq\mathscr{C}\subset C([0,1]^{d-1};[0,1])
.
A compact set
\Omega\subset[0,1]^{d}
has
(\mathscr{C},M)
-regular decision boundary

if there exist (closed, axis-aligned, non-degenerate) rectangles
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{M}\subset[0,1]^{d}

such that
\Omega\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}Q_{i}
, where the
Q_{i}
have disjoint interiors
(i.e.,
\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{i}\cap\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{j}=\varnothing
for
i\neq j
) and such that either

\mathds{1}_{\Omega}=f_{i}\circ P_{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{or}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathds{1}_{\Omega}=1-f_{i}\circ P_{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{almost everywhere on}\hskip 10.00002ptQ_{i},

for a general horizon function
f_{i}\in H_{\mathscr{C}}
associated to
\mathscr{C}

and a
d
-dimensional permutation matrix
P_{i}
.
We write
\mathcal{R}_{\mathscr{C}}(d,M)
for the class of all such sets.

Next, we define the
set of all classifiers with
(\mathscr{C},M)
-regular decision boundary as

\mathcal{C}\ell_{\mathscr{C}}(d,M)\coloneqq\big\{\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\colon\Omega\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathscr{C}}(d,M)\big\}.

Finally,
Barron horizon functions and classifiers with Barron-regular decision boundary

are the elements of
H_{B(C)}
and
\mathcal{C}\ell_{B(C)}(d,M)
,
where
B(C)
is as introduced in
Definition

2.1
.

Remark 2.4
.

Since we will apply approximation results from
[
9
]
to indicator functions
from the set
\mathcal{C}\ell_{B(C)}(d,M)
, it is important to note that
there exists an
absolute constant

\tau\geq 1
such that

\text{if }\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{B(C)}(d,M)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{then}\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega\in\mathcal{BB}_{\tau\sqrt{d}C,M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where the class
\mathcal{BB}_{B,M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is as defined in
[
9
, Definition 3.3]
.
Since the verification of this claim is mainly technical,
we postpone it to
Appendix

A
.

3
Lower bounds for learning sets with regular boundaries

We would like to answer the general question of how efficiently one can learn
functions with
\mathscr{C}
-regular decision boundary for general function classes
\mathscr{C}
.
For this, we determine general
\inf-\sup
relations of the following form:

Let
\Lambda\coloneqq[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}
.
Denote the
set of estimators
, i.e., the set of (measurable)
maps from
\Lambda^{m}
to
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, by
\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})
.
We will establish a relation of the form

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(m)\leq\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})}\,\,\sup_{h\in H_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\big\|A\bigl((X_{i},h(X_{i}))_{i=1}^{m}\bigr)-h\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}\leq\kappa_{2}(m)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N},

(3.1)

where
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
are functions from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}

and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the uniform (Lebesgue) probability measure on
[0,1]^{d}
.

Below, we will identify
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
based on properties of
\mathscr{C}
.
We will first show that instead of taking the infimum over
A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})
,
we can study a slightly more convenient
\inf-\sup
relation where the infimum is taken
over a smaller set of estimators.
Then, we will relate the simplified relation to a density estimation problem,
for which
\inf-\sup
relations have been established in the literature,
specifically in
[
44
]
and
[
21
]
.

We remark that—within this general framework—we only demonstrate lower bounds
for the potentially simpler problem of learning
horizon
functions
compared to learning general classifiers with regular decision boundaries.
These lower bounds certainly also hold for the more challenging problem.
However, while we will observe that in many cases
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}

are asymptotically very close which indicates that the lower bounds cannot be increased,
this is not necessarily so for the estimation problem of general classifier functions.
In other words, while the derived lower bounds will essentially be optimal for
learning
horizon
functions, and while the
lower bound
also holds
for the problem of estimating general classifiers with regular decision boundaries,
it is not clear that these lower bounds are sharp in the general setting.
For classifiers with Barron-regular decision boundary, however, we will see in

Section

5
that the derived lower bound is (essentially) optimal.

3.1
The strict estimation problem

Given a class
\mathcal{H}\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
of functions that one would like to estimate
based on point samples, one can either insist that the estimator produces an element
of
\mathcal{H}
or allow more freedom, meaning that the estimator is permitted
to produce arbitrary elements of
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
.
In the following definition, we define both kinds of estimators
and the associated minimax errors.
Below, we will show that the minimax errors for both classes
coincide, up to a constant factor.

Definition 3.1
.

Denote by
L^{2}([0,1]^{d};\{0,1\})
the set of all functions
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{d})

taking values in
\{0,1\}
, i.e., indicator functions.
Let
\varnothing\neq\mathcal{H}\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{d};\{0,1\})
be arbitrary
and let
\Lambda:=[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,\mathcal{H})

the set of all measurable maps
\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{H}
.

For
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\in([0,1]^{d})^{m}
and
h:[0,1]^{d}\to\{0,1\}
, define

X^{h}:=\bigl((X_{i},h(X_{i}))\bigr)_{i=1}^{m}\!\!\in\!\Lambda^{m}
.
Finally, denoting by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}
the Lebesgue measure on
([0,1]^{d})^{m}\cong[0,1]^{dm}
,
define

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})}\,\,\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}}\big\|A(X^{h})-h\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,\mathcal{H})}\,\,\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}}\big\|A(X^{h})-h\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

The next lemma is the main technical ingredient for showing

\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H})\asymp\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,\operatorname{dist})
be a metric space and let
\varnothing\neq M\subset X
be separable.
Then for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
measurable
map
\pi_{\varepsilon}:X\to M
satisfying

\operatorname{dist}(x,\pi_{\varepsilon}(x))\leq\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(x,M)
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

Since
M\neq\varnothing
is separable, there exists a countable family
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset M

that is dense in
M
.
In particular, this implies

\operatorname{dist}(x,M)=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{dist}(x,y_{n})\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,x\in X.

(3.2)

Now, define
X_{0}:=\varnothing
and inductively

X_{n+1}^{(0)}:=\big\{x\in X\colon\operatorname{dist}(x,y_{n+1})\leq\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(x,M)\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{n+1}:=X_{n+1}^{(0)}\setminus\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{n}X_{\ell}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
By continuity of
\operatorname{dist}(\bullet,y_{n+1})
and
\operatorname{dist}(\bullet,M)
,
it follows that each set
X_{n+1}^{(0)}\subset X
is closed;
hence, each
X_{n}\subset X
is measurable.
Furthermore, it follows from
Equation

3.2
that
X=\biguplus_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
.
Overall, this shows that

\pi_{\varepsilon}:\hskip 10.00002ptX\to M,\hskip 10.00002ptx\mapsto\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}y_{n}\mathds{1}_{X_{n}}(x)

is well-defined and measurable.
Finally, given any
x\in X
we have
x\in X_{n}\subset X_{n}^{(0)}
for a unique

n\in\mathbb{N}
and hence

\operatorname{dist}(x,\pi_{\varepsilon}(x))=\operatorname{dist}(x,y_{n})\leq\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(x,M).

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

With notation as in
Definition

3.1
, we have

\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H})\leq\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})\leq 4\,\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H}).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})\supset\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,\mathcal{H})
,
we see
\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H})\leq\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})
.

Conversely, let
B\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Since
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
is separable,
Lemma

3.2
applied with
X=L^{2}([0,1]^{d})

and
M=\mathcal{H}
yields a measurable map
\pi_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}([0,1]^{d})\to\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\|f-\pi_{\varepsilon}(f)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(f,\mathcal{H})
for all
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
.

Note that
A:=\pi_{\varepsilon}\circ B\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,\mathcal{H})
.
Furthermore, using the estimate
0\leq(a-b)^{2}=a^{2}-2ab+b^{2}
, which holds for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
we see that
2\varepsilon b=2\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon}b\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon b^{2}
for
b\geq 0
,
and hence
(\varepsilon+b)^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}+2\varepsilon b+b^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)b^{2}
.
Therefore, we obtain for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
X\in([0,1]^{d})^{m}
that

\displaystyle\|A(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\|A(X^{h})-B(X^{h})\|_{L^{2}}+\|B(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(B(X^{h}),\mathcal{H})+\|B(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\varepsilon+2\,\|B(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}\big)^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 4\cdot\|B(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.3)

After taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}}
and the supremum
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
,
this shows

\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}}\|A(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 4\cdot\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{m}}\|B(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and noting that
B\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,L^{2})
was arbitrary,
we see
\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(\mathcal{H})\leq 4\,\varepsilon_{m}(\mathcal{H})
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

It follows from
Lemma

3.3
that to provide estimates
for
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
in (
3.1
)
up to an absolute multiplicative constant,
we only need to study the smaller set of estimators
\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,\mathcal{H})
.

3.2
Density estimation problems

We start by reformulating the learning problem of horizon functions as a density estimation problem.
This will be based on the following notation:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.
We consider
\Lambda=[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}
to be equipped with the measure

\lambda:=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\otimes\nu
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}

and
\nu
is the uniform probability measure on
\{0,1\}
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
the set of all measurable functions

b:[0,1]^{d-1}\to[0,1]
.

Define

\Psi:\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{R}\to[0,1],\hskip 10.00002ptx\mapsto\min\big\{1,\,\,\max\{0,\,x\}\big\}.

Then, given any measurable function
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

p[f]:\hskip 10.00002pt\Lambda\to[0,2],\hskip 10.00002pt(x,\iota)\mapsto\begin{cases}2\,\Psi(f(x)),&\text{if }\iota=1,\\
2-2\,\Psi(f(x)),&\text{if }\iota=0.\end{cases}

Finally, given any
b\in\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
,
we define
p_{b}:=p[h_{b}]
, where
h_{b}
is the Horizon function associated to
b
,
as introduced in
Definition

2.2
.
It is easy to see that

\displaystyle p_{b}(x,\iota)=\begin{cases}2\,h_{b}(x),&\text{ if }\iota=1,\\
2\,-\,2\,h_{b}(x),&\text{ if }\iota=0\end{cases}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for}\hskip 20.00003pt(x,\iota)\in\Lambda.

(3.4)

Finally, given any set
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
,
we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}\coloneqq\{p_{b}\colon b\in\mathscr{C}\}
.

The following lemma confirms that
p_{b}
is a probability density and identifies
the associated distribution:

Lemma 3.6
.

•

With notation as in
Definition

3.5
,
we have
\big\|p[f]-p[g]\big\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}\!\leq\!2\,\|f-g\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}

for all
f,g\in L^{1}([0,1]^{d})
.

•

If
A\subset[0,1]^{d}
is measurable, then
p[\mathds{1}_{A}]\,d\lambda

is the distribution of the random vector
(X,\mathds{1}_{A}(X))
, where
X\sim U([0,1]^{d})
.

Proof.

Part 1:

It is easy to see
|\Psi(x)-\Psi(y)|\leq|x-y|
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\big\|p[f]-p[g]\big\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big\|p[f](\bullet,0)-p[g](\bullet,0)\big\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}+\big\|p[f](\bullet,1)-p[g](\bullet,1)\big\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(2\,\big\|\Psi\circ g-\Psi\circ f\big\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}+2\,\big\|\Psi\circ f-\Psi\circ g\big\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\|f-g\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}.

Part 2:

Let
M\subset\Lambda=[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}
be measurable.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl((X,\mathds{1}_{A}(X))\in M\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathds{1}_{M}\bigl((x,\mathds{1}_{A}(x))\bigr)\,dx=\int_{A}\mathds{1}_{M}((x,1))\,dx+\int_{[0,1]^{d}\setminus A}\mathds{1}_{M}((x,0))\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathds{1}_{M}((x,1))\cdot p[\mathds{1}_{A}](x,1)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathds{1}_{M}((x,0))\cdot p[\mathds{1}_{A}](x,0)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\mathds{1}_{M}((x,\iota))\cdot p[\mathds{1}_{A}](x,\iota)\,d\lambda(x,\iota).\qed

Next, we establish the connection between the desired bounds in (
3.1
)
and a density estimation problem.
This density estimation problem will then be studied
in the framework introduced in
[
44
]
.

The density estimation problem associated to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}

is to find (or estimate)
p_{b}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
based on
m
i.i.d. samples

\bigl((x_{i},\iota_{i})\bigr)_{i=1}^{m}\overset{iid}{\sim}p_{b}
.
To make the connection between the density estimation problem
and the learning problem (
3.1
),
the notion of
Hellinger distance
between measurable functions

p,q:\Lambda\to[0,\infty)
is useful; it is defined as

d_{H}(p,q)\coloneqq\left(\int_{\Lambda}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q})^{2}d\lambda\right)^{1/2}.

(3.5)

This distance is related to the regular
L^{2}
norm of the underlying horizon functions as follows.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.
For measurable
b_{1},b_{2}:[0,1]^{d-1}\to[0,1]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\big\|h_{b_{1}}-h_{b_{2}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}d_{H}^{2}(p_{b_{1}},p_{b_{2}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(d_{H}(p_{b_{1}},p_{b_{2}})\bigr)^{2}.

(3.6)

Proof.

From the definition of
h_{b},p_{b}
, it follows that
h_{b}
only takes values in
\{0,1\}
,
so that
p_{b}
only takes values in
\{0,2\}
, and hence
\sqrt{p_{b}}=p_{b}/\sqrt{2}

for any measurable
b:[0,1]^{d-1}\to[0,1]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{H}^{2}(p_{b_{1}},p_{b_{2}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\left(\sqrt{p_{b_{1}}}-\sqrt{p_{b_{2}}}\right)^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Lambda}\left(p_{b_{1}}-p_{b_{2}}\right)^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\bigl(p_{b_{1}}(x,1)-p_{b_{2}}(x,1)\bigr)^{2}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{1}{4}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\bigl(p_{b_{1}}(x,0)-p_{b_{2}}(x,0)\bigr)^{2}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\big(2h_{b_{1}}(x)-2h_{b_{2}}(x)\big)^{2}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x)

(3.7)

\displaystyle=2\,\big\|h_{b_{1}}-h_{b_{2}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2},

where we used in (
3.7
) that

p_{b_{1}}(x,1)-p_{b_{2}}(x,1)=2\,h_{b_{1}}(x)-2\,h_{b_{2}}(x)
and

p_{b_{1}}(x,0)-p_{b_{2}}(x,0)=\bigl(2-2\,h_{b_{1}}(x)\bigr)-\bigl(2-2\,h_{b_{2}}(x)\bigr)=2\,h_{b_{2}}(x)-2\,h_{b_{1}}(x).\qed

Now we have the following equivalence of the learning problem and the density estimation problem.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
let
\varnothing\neq\mathscr{C}\subset\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
,
and let
\Lambda=[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}

and the measure
\lambda
on
\Lambda
as in
Definition

3.5
.
Denote by

\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}:=\bigg\{p:\Lambda\to[0,\infty)\,\,\colon\,\,p\text{ measurable and }\int_{\Lambda}p\,d\lambda=1\bigg\}

(3.8)

the set of all probability densities (with respect to the measure
\lambda
) on
\Lambda
,
which we equip with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}
.

Then,

\displaystyle\inf_{E:\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{\ast}}d_{H}^{2}\bigl(E(S),p^{*}\bigr)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sim\inf_{E:\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{\ast}}d_{H}^{2}\bigl(E(S),p^{*}\bigr)

(3.10)

\displaystyle=2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})\sim\varepsilon_{m}(H_{\mathscr{C}}).

(3.11)

Remark
.

1.

As we show in
Section

A.3
,
the topology on
\mathcal{D}
induced by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}

is identical to the topology of convergence in measure; hence, also the two
induced
\sigma
-algebras coincide.

2.

Regarding the measurability of
E:\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
,
like
\mathcal{D}
we also equip
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}\subset\mathcal{D}

with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

The estimate
\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationNonStrictError}\leq\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationStrictError}

is trivial.
The proof of the reverse estimate is essentially identical to the proof of

Lemma

3.3
and is hence omitted.
The only thing one has to note here is that the notion of convergence induced by the
Hellinger distance
d_{H}
(which is a metric) is weaker than convergence in
L^{1}(\lambda)

but stronger than convergence in measure.
Since the latter two notions of convergence are equivalent on
\mathcal{D}
(see the remark above
and
Section

A.3
), this means that the Hellinger distance
induces the same topology (and hence
\sigma
-algebra) on
\mathcal{D}

as the
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}
norm.

In particular, measurability of a function
\Phi:X\to\mathcal{D}

is independent of whether one equips
\mathcal{D}
with the
\sigma
algebra
induced by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}}
or by
d_{H}
.
Moreover, since
(L^{1}(\lambda),\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}})
is well-known to be separable
(see for instance
[
10
, Proposition 3.4.5]
),
it follows that
(\mathcal{D},\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)})

is separable, and hence so is
(\mathcal{D},d_{H})
, as required for the application of

Lemma

3.2
in the proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

The statement
\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})\sim\varepsilon_{m}(H_{\mathscr{C}})
in

Equation

3.11

follows directly from
Lemma

3.3
.
In the rest of the proof, we thus prove that

\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationStrictError}=2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})
.

For given
A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,H_{\mathscr{C}})
, we define the map
E_{A}
by

E_{A}\colon\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},\hskip 20.00003ptE_{A}(S)=p_{b[S]},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptA(S)=h_{b[S]}.

Note that
E_{A}
is well-defined, since
b
is uniquely determined by
h_{b}
;
see the discussion after
Definition

2.2
.
Now,
Lemma

3.7
shows for any

p=p_{b}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
that

d_{H}^{2}(E_{A}(S),p)=2\,\|h_{b[S]}-h_{b}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}=2\,\|A(S)-h_{b}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2},

where we note again by
Equation

3.4
that
h_{b}=p(\bullet,1)/2
.

Next, given
b\in\mathscr{C}
, note that
h_{b}=\mathds{1}_{M_{b}}

for
M_{b}=\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\colon h_{b}(x)=1\}
.
Hence, the second part of
Lemma

3.6
shows that
if
X\sim U([0,1]^{d})
, then

\bigl(X,h_{b}(X)\bigr)=\bigl(X,\mathds{1}_{M_{b}}(X)\bigr)\sim p[\mathds{1}_{M_{b}}]\,d\lambda=p[h_{b}]\,d\lambda=p_{b}\,d\lambda.

Thus, if
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p_{b}
,
then
X^{h_{b}}\sim S
, where
X^{h_{b}}
is as in Definition
3.1
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{*}}\,\,d_{H}^{2}\bigl(E_{A}(S),p^{*}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{b\in\mathscr{C}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p_{b}}\,\,\big\|A(S)-h_{b}\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{b\in\mathscr{C}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,\,\big\|A(X^{h_{b}})-h_{b}\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{h\in H_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,\,\big\|A(X^{h})-h\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}.

Therefore, we see that

\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationStrictError}\leq 2\,\sup_{h\in H_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,\,\big\|A(X^{h})-h\big\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}

for any
A\in\mathcal{A}_{m}(\Lambda,H_{\mathscr{C}})
.
Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})
(see
Definition

3.1
), we thus see
that
\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationStrictError}\leq 2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})
.

To show that also

\eqref{eq:DensityEstimationStrictError}\geq 2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})
,
we observe for a given measurable
E\colon\Lambda^{m}\!\to\!\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}

that we can define

A_{E}:\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda^{m}\to H_{\mathscr{C}},\hskip 20.00003ptA_{E}(S)=[E(S)](\bullet,1)/2,

which is then also measurable.
Then, writing
A_{E}(S)=h_{c[S]}
, we have
E(S)=p_{c[S]}
;
hence, another application of
Lemma

3.7

shows for any
h=h_{b}\in H_{\mathscr{C}}
that

2\,\|A_{E}(S)-h_{b}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}=2\,\|h_{c[S]}-h_{b}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}=d_{H}^{2}(p_{c[S]},p_{b})=d_{H}^{2}\bigl(E(S),p_{b}\bigr).

Thus, using the same arguments as above, we see

\displaystyle 2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{\mathrm{strict}}(H_{\mathscr{C}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\sup_{h\in H_{\mathscr{C}}}\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\|A_{E}(X^{h})-h\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{b\in\mathscr{C}}\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\|A_{E}(X^{h_{b}})-h_{b}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{b\in\mathscr{C}}\mathbb{E}_{X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}d_{H}^{2}\big(E(X^{h_{b}}),p_{b}\big)

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{b\in\mathscr{C}}\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p_{b}}d_{H}^{2}\big(E(S),p_{b}\big)

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{p^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}}\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{\ast}}d_{H}^{2}\big(E(S),p^{\ast}\big).

Taking the infimum over
E
then yields the result.
∎

Quite sharp bounds for the density estimation problem in terms of the Hellinger distance
have been established in
[
44
]
; we review these
in
Theorem

3.11
below.
To properly formulate these results, however, we first need to recall
the definitions of packing- and covering entropy numbers.

Definition 3.9
.

(cf.
[
44
, Definitions 1 and 2]
)
Let
\mathcal{X}\neq\varnothing
be a set and let
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty]

be a general
distance function
, meaning that
d(x,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}

and
d(x,y)>0
for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\neq y
.
Note that we do
not
assume that
d
is symmetric
or satisfies a form of the triangle inequality.

For a set
\varnothing\neq K\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
,
we call a set
N_{\varepsilon}\subset K
an
\varepsilon
-packing set in
K

if for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in N_{\varepsilon}
with
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
,
it holds that
d(x_{1},x_{2})>\varepsilon
.
We define

\mathcal{N}_{K,d}(\varepsilon):=\max\big\{|N|\,\,\colon\,\,N\text{ is an $\varepsilon$-packing set for }K\big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptM_{K,d}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\ln\big(\mathcal{N}_{K,d}(\varepsilon)\big).

We call
M_{K,d}(\varepsilon)
the
\varepsilon
-packing entropy

of
K
(with respect to
d
).

Moreover, we call a set
G_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{X}
an
\varepsilon
-net for
K
(in
\mathcal{X}
)

if for every
x\in K
there exists
y\in G_{\varepsilon}
satisfying
d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
.
We define

\mathcal{G}_{K,d}(\varepsilon):=\min\big\{|G|\,\,\colon\,\,G\subset\mathcal{X}\text{ is an $\varepsilon$-net for }K\big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptV_{K,\mathcal{X},d}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq V_{K,d}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\ln\big(\mathcal{G}_{K,d}(\varepsilon)\big).

We call
V_{K,d}(\varepsilon)
the
\varepsilon
-covering entropy

of
K
(with respect to
d
in
\mathcal{X}
).

Remark 3.10
.

If the distance
d
is in fact a metric
(i.e.,
d
is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality),
then it holds that

M_{K,d}(2\varepsilon)\leq V_{K,\mathcal{X},d}(\varepsilon)\leq M_{K,d}(\varepsilon)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\varepsilon\in(0,\infty);

see e.g.
[
44
, Page 1569]
.

Before we can properly state the results from
[
44
]
,
we need to revisit one more notion, the so-called
Kullback-Leibler divergence
,
as introduced e.g. in
[
44
, Top of Page 1569]
.
For two probability density functions
p,q\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}

(with
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
as in
Equation

3.8
),
the
Kullback-Leibler divergence of
p
from
q
is defined as

D(p\parallel q):=\int_{\Lambda}p\cdot\ln(p/q)\,d\lambda,

and the associated distance function is given by

d_{KL}(p,q):=\sqrt{D(p\parallel q)},

where we implicitly use that
D(p\parallel q)\geq 0
; this is well-known and
reviewed for completeness in
Section

A.4
.
We denote the associated covering entropy of a subset
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}

in
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
by
V_{\mathcal{P},KL}(\varepsilon)
.
Using these notions, the main result of
[
44
]
reads as follows:

Theorem 3.11
.

(slightly simplified from
[
44
]
)
Let
(\Lambda,\lambda)
be a probability space.
Let the set
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
of probability density functions
as defined in
Equation

3.8
,
and let
\varnothing\neq\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
.

Let
V,M:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be continuous from the right and non-increasing,
and with the following properties:

1.

V_{\mathcal{P},KL}(\varepsilon)\leq V(\varepsilon)
for all
\varepsilon>0
;

2.

the sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
is chosen such that

\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=V(\varepsilon_{n})/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

3.

the packing entropy
M_{\mathcal{P},H}
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to the Hellinger distance
d_{H}

satisfies

M(\varepsilon)\leq M_{\mathcal{P},H}(\varepsilon)<\infty\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\varepsilon>0;

4.

M(\varepsilon)>2\ln 2
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough;

5.

the sequence
(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)

is chosen such that
M(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})=4n\,\varepsilon_{n}^{2}+2\ln 2

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then

\frac{1}{8}\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{m}^{2}\leq\inf_{E:\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}}\,\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{\ast}}d_{H}^{2}\bigl(E(S),p^{*}\bigr)\leq 2\,\varepsilon_{m}^{2}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N}.

In fact, for the upper estimate, it suffices if only Conditions

1
and
2
are satisfied.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
[
44
, Theorems 1 and 2]

upon noting that
d_{H}
is a metric on
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
satisfying

d_{H}\leq d_{KL}
; this latter condition is verified for completeness
in
Section

A.4
.
∎

Typically, applying these results requires the densities
p\in\mathcal{P}
to never vanish
(or at least to all have the same support), since otherwise
D(p\parallel q)

might be infinite for certain
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
and hence Condition
1
of Theorem
3.11
does not hold.
For this reason, applications of the results in
[
44
]

in the literature have mainly considered estimation problems
with measurements corrupted by Gaussian noise.
In contrast, our interest is in the case of
noiseless
measurements.
Intriguingly,
[
44
, Lemma 2]
offers a way out,
as shown in (the proof of) the following proposition.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
be a set
of probability densities defined on
\Lambda=[0,1]^{d}\times\{0,1\}

(with respect to the measure
\lambda
from
Definition

3.5

and
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
as in
Equation

3.8
)
such that
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
Assume further that the packing entropy
M_{\mathcal{P},H}
of
\mathcal{P}

under the Hellinger distance satisfies for all
0<\varepsilon<1
that

\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\ln_{2}^{b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\gtrsim M_{\mathcal{P},H}(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}\big/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

for certain
\alpha\geq\beta>0
and
a,b\geq 0
.
Then, it holds for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle m^{-2\alpha/(2\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{-4\alpha(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)-2\alpha a}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{E\colon\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{P}\text{ measurable}}\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{*}}d_{H}^{2}(E(S),p^{*})

\displaystyle\lesssim m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{2(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}.

Proof.

The idea behind the proof was to some extent already outlined
on
[
44
, Page 1579]
,
but we will present the full argument here for the convenience of the reader.

We first recall from
[
44
, Lemma 2]

that for every density
g\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}

and every
\varepsilon\in(0,\sqrt{2}]
, there exists a density

\tilde{g}=\tilde{g}(g,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}

such that for all
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
with
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2

(and hence for all
f\in\mathcal{P}
)
satisfying
d_{H}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon
it holds that

d_{KL}(f,\tilde{g})\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(1+|\ln(\varepsilon)|)}.

As a result of this lemma, we have that for any
Hellinger
\varepsilon
-covering set
B_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
of
\mathcal{P}
,
the set
\widetilde{B}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{\tilde{g}(g,\varepsilon)\colon g\in B_{\varepsilon}\}

is a
c\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon))}
covering set
for
\mathcal{P}
with respect to the Kullback–Leibler distance,
where
c>1
is a suitable
absolute
constant.

Therefore, we obtain the following upper bound on the
\varepsilon
covering entropy of
\mathcal{P}

under the Kullback–Leibler distance, which we denote by
V_{\mathcal{P},KL}(\varepsilon)
:

V_{\mathcal{P},KL}\left(c\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon))}\right)\leq V_{\mathcal{P},H}(\varepsilon)\leq M_{\mathcal{P},H}(\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\ln^{b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\varepsilon\in(0,1].

We denote
\gamma(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{x^{2}\cdot(1+\ln(1/x))}
and observe that for
x<1/2

\displaystyle 2x\geq\gamma\big(x/(1+\ln(1/x))\big).

Indeed,

\displaystyle\big[\gamma(x/(1+\ln(1/x)))\big]^{2}=x^{2}\frac{1+\ln(1/x)+\ln(1+\ln(1/x))}{(1+\ln(1/x))^{2}}\leq 2x^{2}\leq(2\,x)^{2},

since
\ln(z)\leq z
for all
z\geq 1
.
The covering entropy
V_{\mathcal{P},KL}
is a non-decreasing function.
Therefore, we conclude that for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
it holds

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{P},KL}(2c\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq V_{\mathcal{P},KL}\Big(c\cdot\gamma\big(\varepsilon/(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon))\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{\mathcal{P},H}\big(\varepsilon/(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon))\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)^{1/\beta}\cdot\ln^{b}\big(2+(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon))/\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)^{1/\beta}\cdot\ln^{b}(1+1/\varepsilon)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\cdot\ln^{1/\beta+b}(1+1/\varepsilon),

where we used in (
3.12
) that for
0<\varepsilon<1/2
it holds that

1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\leq 1/\varepsilon
and hence

2+\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)/\varepsilon\leq 2+1/\varepsilon^{2}\leq(1+1/\varepsilon)^{2}.

Hence, after substituting
\varepsilon/2c
for
\varepsilon
, we have shown
that there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
C^{\ast}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
,
it holds

V_{\mathcal{P},KL}(\varepsilon)\leq C^{\ast}\cdot\big(\max\{1,\varepsilon^{-1}\}\big)^{1/\beta}\cdot\ln^{1/\beta+b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})=:V(\varepsilon),

and this estimate clearly also remains valid—after enlarging
C^{\ast}
if necessary—for
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{0}
.
Thus, by
Lemma

A.1
(which will be shown independently below),
we obtain that there exists a sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

such that
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=V(\varepsilon_{n})/n
and

\varepsilon_{n}\sim\left(\frac{\ln(2n)^{1/\beta+b}}{n}\right)^{1/(2+1/\beta)}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\beta/(2\beta+1)}[\ln(2n)]^{(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}.

(3.13)

From
Theorem

3.11
, we therefore obtain the upper bound

\begin{split}&\inf_{E\colon\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{*}}d_{H}^{2}(E(S),p^{*})\\
&\leq 2\varepsilon_{m}^{2}\sim m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)}[\ln(2m)]^{2(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}.\end{split}

(3.14)

In addition to the upper bound,
Theorem

3.11
yields a lower bound of

\inf_{E\colon\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{*}}d_{H}^{2}(E(S),p^{*})\gtrsim\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{m}^{2},

(3.15)

where
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n}
satisfies
M(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})=4n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}+2\ln 2

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Here,
M(\varepsilon)
is a (non-increasing, continuous) lower bound
on the packing entropy number
M_{\mathcal{P},H}(\varepsilon)
.
Naturally, given the hypothesis of the current proposition,
we choose
M(\varepsilon)=\bar{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

for a suitable
\bar{c}>0
.
Note that
M
is continuous and
M(\varepsilon)\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0

and
M(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to\infty
.
Hence, by the intermediate value theorem, there exists for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

some
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\in(0,\infty)
satisfying

M(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})=4n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}+2\ln 2
.
Furthermore, note that for
\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,\infty)
and

\Gamma:\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},\hskip 10.00002ptx\mapsto\bar{c}^{\alpha}\cdot x^{-\alpha}/\ln^{a\alpha}(2+x^{2\alpha})

it holds that

\displaystyle\Gamma(M(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\Gamma\big(\bar{c}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\big)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ln^{\alpha a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})/\ln^{a\alpha}\big(2+\bar{c}^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{-2}/\ln^{2\alpha a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\ln^{\alpha a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})/\ln^{a\alpha}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})=\varepsilon

where the penultimate step follows since

\bar{c}^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{-1}/\ln^{2\alpha a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

tends to infinity for
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Now we obtain that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\gtrsim\Gamma(M(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(4\,n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}+2\ln 2\right)

\displaystyle({\scriptstyle\text{by \eqref{eq:EpsilonAsymptotic} and since }\Gamma(x)\sim\Gamma(y)\text{ if }x\sim y})

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma\left(2\,n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)+1}[\ln(2n)]^{2(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}+2\ln 2\right)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(2\,n^{1/(2\beta+1)}[\ln(2n)]^{2(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}+2\ln 2\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim n^{-\alpha/(2\beta+1)}[\ln(2n)]^{-2\alpha(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)}\big/[\ln(2+2n)]^{\alpha a}

\displaystyle\gtrsim n^{-\alpha/(2\beta+1)}[\ln(2n)]^{-2\alpha(1+\beta b)/(2\beta+1)-\alpha a}.

Combining this bound with (
3.15
)
and recalling (
3.14
) completes the proof.
∎

To apply
Proposition

3.12
,
we need to find an estimate of
M_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},H}(\varepsilon)
.
The following lemma establishes a convenient relation of the packing entropy
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
(as defined in
Definition

3.5
)
under the Hellinger distance to the packing entropy of
\mathscr{C}
under the
L^{1}
norm.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
\mathscr{C}\subset C([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
.
Then the packing entropy of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
with respect to the Hellinger distance
obeys for all
\varepsilon>0
:

M_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},H}(\varepsilon)=M_{\mathscr{C},L^{1}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}/2\right),

where
M_{\mathscr{C},L^{1}}
denotes the packing entropy of
\mathscr{C}
under the
L^{1}
distance with the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, write
[k]:=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

B_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\big\{b_{\ell}\colon\ell\in[k]\big\},

be an
\varepsilon^{2}/2
-packing set of
\mathscr{C}
with respect to the
L^{1}
norm,
with
b_{\ell}\neq b_{j}
for
\ell\neq j
.
By (an obvious generalization of)
[
33
, Lemma B.1]

it holds that for
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}

\displaystyle\|h_{b}-h_{b^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|b-b^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}.

Moreover, by
Lemma

3.7
we have that
for all
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}

\|h_{b}-h_{b^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}d_{H}^{2}(p_{b},p_{b^{\prime}}).

Hence, if
\ell,j\in[k]
satisfy
\ell\neq j
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d_{H}^{2}(p_{b_{\ell}},p_{b_{j}})=\|b_{\ell}-b_{j}\|_{L^{1}}>\varepsilon^{2}/2\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and hence}\hskip 10.00002ptd_{H}(p_{b_{\ell}},p_{b_{j}})>\varepsilon.

Therefore,

N_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\big\{p_{b_{\ell}}\colon\ell\in[k]\big\},

is an
\varepsilon
-packing set for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
with cardinality
k
.
Thus, we easily see

M_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},H}(\varepsilon)\geq M_{\mathscr{C},L^{1}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}/2\right).

The inverse inequality can be obtained with the same argument
but starting with an
\varepsilon
packing set for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
with respect to
d_{H}
.
∎

Based on the lemma above, we now obtain the following bound for the density estimation
problem for the class
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}
, based on bounds for the
L^{1}
packing entropy
of the underlying set of functions
\mathscr{C}
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
\mathscr{C}\subset C([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
.
Assume further that for all
0<\varepsilon<1

\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\ln^{b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\gtrsim M_{\mathscr{C},L^{1}}(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

for certain
\alpha\geq\beta>0
and
a,b\geq 0
.
Then,

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptm^{-\alpha/(\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{-\alpha(2+\beta b)/(\beta+1)-\alpha a}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{E:\Lambda^{m}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ measurable}}\,\,\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}}}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\overset{iid}{\sim}p^{\ast}}d_{H}^{2}(E(S),p^{*})

\displaystyle\lesssim m^{-\beta/(\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{(2+\beta b)/(\beta+1)}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.13
, we have for all
0<\delta<1
that

\delta^{-1/\beta}\ln^{b}(2+\delta^{-1})\gtrsim M_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},H}(\sqrt{2\delta})\gtrsim\delta^{-1/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\delta^{-1})

and hence

\varepsilon^{-2/\beta}\ln^{b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\gtrsim M_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{C}},H}(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

for all
0<\varepsilon<\sqrt{2}
.
The result now follows by applying
Proposition

3.12
.
∎

We close this section by transferring the upper and lower bounds from above
to the problem of learning horizon functions from noiseless point evaluations.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
\mathscr{C}\subset C([0,1]^{d-1},[0,1])
.
Assume further that for all
0<\varepsilon<1

\varepsilon^{-1/\beta}\cdot\ln^{b}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})\gtrsim M_{\mathscr{C},L^{1}}(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-1/\alpha}/\ln^{a}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

for certain
\alpha\geq\beta>0
and
a,b\geq 0
.
Then, (
3.1
) holds with

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(m)

\displaystyle=C_{1}\cdot m^{-\alpha/(\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{-\alpha(2+\beta b)/(\beta+1)-\alpha a},

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}(m)

\displaystyle=C_{2}\cdot m^{-\beta/(\beta+1)}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{(2+\beta b)/(\beta+1)},

for certain
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from combining
Theorem

3.14

with
Proposition

3.8
.
∎

4
Sets with Barron-regular boundaries

4.1
Entropy bounds for the set of Barron functions

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
, we say that
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a

general Barron function with constant
C
,
if there exists a (complex-valued) measure

\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some
c\in[-C,C]
satisfying

f(x)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\,d\mu(\xi)\text{ for all }x\in[0,1]^{d}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq C.

(4.1)

Here,
|\mu|
is the total variation measure of
\mu
; see
[
34
, Section 6.1]
.
We write
\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
for the set of all such functions.

Note that the general Barron class is indeed a generalization
of the Barron class of
Definition

2.1
in the following sense.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
, then
B_{d}(C)\subset\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
.

Proof.

Let
f\in B_{d}(C)
.
Then there exists
c\in[-C,C]
and a measurable
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}

satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|\cdot|F(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C
such that for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1\bigr)\cdot F(\xi)\,d\xi=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1\bigr)\cdot(2\pi)^{d}F(2\pi\xi)\,d\xi,

where the second equality follows by change of variables from
\xi
to
2\pi\xi
.
We consider the measure
\mu_{f}\coloneqq(2\pi)^{d}F(2\pi\xi)\,d\xi
and observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|d|\mu_{f}|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|2\pi\xi|(2\pi)^{d-1}|F(2\pi\xi)|\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|(2\pi)^{-1}|F(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C/(2\pi)\leq C,

where we applied a change of variables of
\xi
to
\xi/(2\pi)
.
The final inequality demonstrates that
f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
.
∎

Next, we show that one can replace the general complex measure
\mu

in
Equation

4.1
by a measure of the form
F(\xi)\,d\xi
,
where
F\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the weight
w:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[1,\infty)
given by
w(\xi)=1+|\xi|

and the
weighted Lebesgue space

L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\bigl\{F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\,\colon\,F\text{ measurable and }\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}:=\|w\cdot F\|_{L^{1}}<\infty\bigr\}.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(d)>0
such that for every

C>0
and
f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
, there exists a function
F\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}F(\xi)\,d\xi\text{ for all }x\in[0,1]^{d}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq C_{0}\cdot C.

(4.2)

Proof.

Fix a function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and
\varphi\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of
[0,1]^{d}
,
as well as
\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset(-2,2)^{d}
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
and choose
c\in[-C,C]
and a complex measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq C
and

f(x)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\,d\mu(\xi)

for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
.
Note by
[
34
, Theorem 6.12]
that there exists
a measurable function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|\sigma|\equiv 1
and

d\mu=\sigma d|\mu|
.

Now, writing
B:=B_{1}(0):=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon|\xi|\leq 1\}
,
define
g,h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
by

g(x):=\int_{B}(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\,d\mu(\xi)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pth(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,d\mu(\xi)

and set

\widetilde{c}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\,d\mu(\xi)\in\mathbb{C}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\sigma(\xi)\,d|\mu|(\xi),

where we note because of
|\sigma(\xi)|=1\leq|\xi|
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B
that

|\widetilde{c}|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}|\xi|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq C
.

We first claim that
g
is smooth, with
|\partial^{\alpha}g(x)|\leq C_{1}\cdot C

for all
x\in[-2,2]^{d}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\alpha|\leq d+2
,
for a suitable constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(d)>0
.
Indeed, in view of
|e^{it}-1|=|e^{it}-e^{i0}|\leq|t|

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we first see that
g
is well-defined, with

|g(x)|\leq\int_{B}|e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1|\cdot|\sigma(\xi)|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\pi|x|\,|\xi|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq 2\pi C\,|x|\leq 4\pi C\,\sqrt{d}\text{ for }x\in[-2,2]^{d}.

Next, we use differentiation under the integral
(as in
[
1
, Chapter X, Theorem 3.18]
) to deduce that

g
is smooth with partial derivative
\partial^{\alpha}g

(for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
\alpha\neq 0
) given by

\partial^{\alpha}g(x)=\int_{B}(2\pi i\xi)^{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,\sigma(\xi)\,d|\mu|(\xi).

Since
|\xi^{\alpha}|\leq|\xi|^{|\alpha|}\leq|\xi|
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\setminus\{0\}

and
\xi\in B
, this implies

|\partial^{\alpha}g(x)|\leq\int_{B}(2\pi)^{|\alpha|}|\xi|\,d|\mu|(\xi)\leq(2\pi)^{d+2}C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ and }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\text{ with }|\alpha|\leq d+2.

Overall, set
f_{1}:=\varphi\cdot(c-\widetilde{c}+g)
.
Then a combination of the preceding estimates with the Leibniz rule
(see
[
18
, Equation (2.2.5)]
)
shows that
f_{1}\in C_{c}^{d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{supp}f_{1}\subset(-2,2)^{d}
and

\|\partial^{\alpha}f_{1}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C_{2}\,C
for
|\alpha|\leq d+2
, with
C_{2}=C_{2}(d)
.
By standard properties of the Fourier transform
(see e.g.
[
18
, Proposition 2.2.11]
),
this implies
\widehat{f_{1}}\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\widehat{f_{1}}\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq C_{3}\,C
,
for a suitable constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(d)>0
.

Next, define

H:\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C},\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi-\eta)\sigma(\eta)\,d|\mu|(\eta).

Then a computation using Tonelli’s theorem and the estimate

1+|\xi|\leq 1+|\xi-\eta|+|\eta|\leq|\eta|\cdot(2+|\xi-\eta|)
,
which holds for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B
, shows that

\displaystyle\|H\|_{L_{w}^{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|H(\xi)|\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi-\eta)|\,d\xi\,d|\mu|(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}|\eta|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(2+|\xi-\eta|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi-\eta)|\,d\xi\,d|\mu|(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\widehat{\varphi}\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\cdot C\leq C_{4}\cdot C<\infty,

for a suitable constant
C_{4}=C_{4}(d)>0
.
Moreover, a computation using Fubini’s theorem
and the Fourier inversion formula (that is,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}=\varphi
)
shows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi-\eta)\sigma(\eta)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi-\eta\rangle}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\eta\rangle}\,d|\mu|(\eta)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}\sigma(\eta)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\eta\rangle}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi-\eta)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi-\eta\rangle}\,d\xi\,d|\mu|(\eta)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\widehat{\varphi})(x)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\eta\rangle}\,d\mu(\eta)=\varphi(x)\cdot h(x).

Overall, setting
F:=H+\widehat{f_{1}}
, we see that
F\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq(C_{3}+C_{4})\cdot C
.
Furthermore, using the Fourier inversion formula
(which is applicable since
f_{1}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
\widehat{f_{1}}\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
), we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f_{1}}(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,d\xi=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\widehat{f_{1}}(x)=f_{1}(x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
\varphi(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]^{d}
, we thus see

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,d\xi=\varphi(x)\cdot h(x)+f_{1}(x)=h(x)+g(x)+c-\widetilde{c}=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\,d\mu(\xi)=f(x)

for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
, as claimed.
∎

4.1.1
Upper bounds for the entropy numbers

Using Proposition
4.2
, we now show that functions in the Barron space
can be represented via Fourier series with quickly decaying coefficients.
This observation was essentially already previously made in
[
8
, Section 7.2]

(for functions of the form (
4.2
)), but without a detailed proof.
For this reason, we provide a comprehensive argument.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the family
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

of functions
e_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

e_{n}:=e^{2\pi i\langle\frac{n}{2},\bullet\rangle}
.
Then for every sequence
c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
,
the series
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}e_{n}
is unconditionally convergent in
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})

with

\Big\|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}\Big\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 2^{d/2}\,\|c\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(4.3)

Furthermore, there is a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(d)>0
such that for every
C>0
,
every function
f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
has a representation

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}^{(f)}\,e_{n}\text{ for suitable }c_{n}^{(f)}\in\mathbb{C}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{satisfying}\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|n|)\,\bigl|c_{n}^{(f)}\bigr|\leq C_{0}\cdot C.

Proof.

Let
Q:=[0,1]^{d}
and
P:=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]^{d}
.
Since
\bigl(2^{-d/2}\,e_{n}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\subset L^{2}(P)

is an orthonormal basis,
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}
converges unconditionally in
L^{2}(P)

and satisfies
\big\|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}\big\|_{L^{2}(P)}\leq 2^{d/2}\,\|c\|_{\ell^{2}}

for every
c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.
Since the restriction operator
L^{2}(P)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(Q),f\mapsto f|_{Q}
has norm
1
,
this proves the first claim.

To prove the second claim, fix
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})^{d}

and
\varphi\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of
[0,1]^{d}
.
Let
C>0
and
f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
.
By
Proposition

4.2
, there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(d)>0

and a function
F\in L_{w}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq C_{1}\cdot C
and such that

f(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}F(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
.
We thus extend
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
to a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}

defined by
f(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}F(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that
f
is continuous and bounded, with

|f(x)|\leq\|F\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq C_{1}C
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Next, for any (continuous) function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, define
M_{Q}g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
by

M_{Q}g(x):=\sup_{q\in Q}|g(x+q)|\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Note for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
that

\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon M_{Q}g(x)>\lambda\bigr\}=\bigcup_{q\in Q}\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon|g(x+q)|>\lambda\bigr\}

is open; hence,
M_{Q}g
is measurable.

Define
H:=F\ast\widehat{\varphi}
.
Note that
|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)|\leq C_{2}\cdot(1+|\xi|)^{-(d+2)}

and
(1+|\xi+q|)^{-(d+1)}\leq C_{3}\cdot(1+|\xi|)^{-(d+1)}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
q\in Q
and suitable constants
C_{2},C_{3}

depending only on
d
.
Thus, using the elementary estimate

1+|\xi+q|=1+|\eta+\xi-\eta+q|\leq 1+|\eta|+|\xi-\eta+q|\leq(1+|\eta|)\cdot(1+|\xi-\eta+q|)

we see for arbitrary
q\in Q
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\displaystyle(1+|\xi+q|)\cdot|H(\xi+q)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\eta|)\cdot|F(\eta)|\cdot(1+|\xi+q-\eta|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi+q-\eta)|\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq(F_{w}\ast\zeta)(\xi)

for
F_{w}(\xi):=(1+|\xi|)\cdot|F(\xi)|

and
\zeta(\xi):=C_{2}C_{3}\cdot(1+|\xi|)^{-(d+1)}
.
Since
F_{w},\zeta\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this implies for
H_{w}(\xi):=(1+|\xi|)\cdot|H(\xi)|

that

\|M_{Q}H_{w}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|F_{w}\ast\zeta\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|\zeta\|_{L^{1}}\cdot\|F\|_{L_{w}^{1}}\leq C_{1}C_{4}\cdot C

for
C_{4}:=\|\zeta\|_{L^{1}}
.

Therefore, given any
\delta\in(0,1]
, we see using the estimates

1+|n|=1+\delta^{-1}|\delta n|\leq\delta^{-1}(1+|\delta n|)
and

(1+|\delta n|)\cdot|H(\delta n)|=H_{w}(\delta n)\leq(M_{Q}H_{w})(\delta n-q)

for
q\in[0,\delta)^{d}\subset Q
that

\begin{split}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|n|)\cdot|H(\delta n)|&\leq\delta^{-d-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,\delta)^{d}}(1+|\delta n|)\cdot|H(\delta n)|\,dq\\
&\leq\delta^{-d-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,\delta)^{d}}(M_{Q}H_{w})(\delta n-q)\,dq\\
&=\delta^{-(d+1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(M_{Q}H_{w})(x)\,dx\leq\delta^{-(d+1)}C_{1}C_{4}\cdot C.\end{split}

(4.4)

Now, note that
f_{0}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}H=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(F\ast\widehat{\varphi})

satisfies
f_{0}(x)=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}F)(x)\cdot\varphi(x)=f(x)\cdot\varphi(x)
,
thanks to the convolution theorem and the Fourier inversion theorem.
On the one hand, this implies
\operatorname{supp}f_{0}\subset P
,
and on the other hand
f_{0}(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
.
Note that,
f_{0}
is bounded and compactly supported and hence integrable;
therefore, the Fourier inversion theorem shows that
\widehat{f_{0}}=H
,
since
H
is continuous.
Furthermore, we have

\int_{P}f_{0}(x)\cdot 2^{-d/2}\,\overline{e_{n}(x)}\,dx=2^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(x)\cdot e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\frac{n}{2}\rangle}\,dx=2^{-d/2}\widehat{f_{0}}(n/2)=2^{-d/2}H(n/2)

and hence

f_{0}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}2^{-d/2}H(\frac{n}{2})2^{-d/2}e_{n}=2^{-d}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}H(\frac{n}{2})\,e_{n}

as elements of
L^{2}(P)
, since
\bigl(2^{-d/2}e_{n}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is an orthonormal basis
of
L^{2}(P)
.
Since
f=f_{0}|_{[0,1]^{d}}
, we thus see for
c_{n}:=2^{-d}\,H(\frac{n}{2})

that
f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}
as elements of
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
,
where
Equation

4.4
(with
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
) shows

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|n|)\cdot|c_{n}|\leq 2^{-d}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|n|)\,|H(\tfrac{1}{2}n)|\leq 2^{-d}2^{d+1}C_{1}C_{4}\cdot C<\infty.

In particular, the series
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}
converges uniformly.
Since
f
is continuous, this implies that the equality

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e_{n}
holds pointwise and not only in
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
.
∎

By combining the preceding result with bounds for nonlinear approximation using the
discrete Fourier basis developed in
[
11
]
,
we will obtain the following entropy bound for the class of Barron functions:

Proposition 4.4
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
, there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(d,C)>0

such that the (covering) entropy numbers of
\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)

with respect to the
L^{\infty}
-norm on
[0,1]^{d}
satisfy

M_{\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C),L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon)\leq C_{0}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\varepsilon\in(0,1).

Remark
.

We remark that a somewhat similar bound can be derived from the results in

[
8
, Section 7.2]
.
Yet,
[
8
]
only considers entropy numbers and approximation in
L^{2}
,
whereas we consider entropy numbers in
L^{\infty}
.
Similarly, the results in
[
38
]

also do not include the
L^{\infty}
case.
Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, the presented entropy bound is new.

For the proof, instead of the packing entropy numbers introduced in
Definition

3.9
,
we will find it easier to work with so-called
(external) covering entropy numbers
.
To define these, let
(\mathcal{X},\operatorname{dist})
be a metric space, and let
\varnothing\neq K\subset\mathcal{X}
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, we say that a subset
G\subset\mathcal{X}
is an
(external)
\varepsilon
-net
for
K

if
K\subset\bigcup_{x\in G}\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
, where

\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}\colon\operatorname{dist}(y,x)\leq\varepsilon\}
.
The
(external)
\varepsilon
covering entropy number

M^{\mathrm{ext}}_{K,\operatorname{dist}}(\varepsilon)\in[0,\infty]

is then defined as

M^{\mathrm{ext},X}_{K,\operatorname{dist}}(\varepsilon):=\ln\big(\min\{|G|\colon G\subset X\text{ is an external $\varepsilon$-net for $K$}\}\big).

These newly introduced entropy numbers are related to the packing entropy numbers
introduced in
Definition

3.9
via the following (well-known) inequalities:

M_{K,\operatorname{dist}}(\varepsilon)\leq M_{K,\operatorname{dist}}^{\mathrm{ext},X}(\varepsilon/2)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptM_{K,\operatorname{dist}}^{\mathrm{ext},X}(\varepsilon)\leq M_{K,\operatorname{dist}}(\varepsilon).

(4.5)

For completeness, we provide the short argument.
To prove the first inequality, let
G\subset X
be an external
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
-net for
K

and let
N\subset K
be an
\varepsilon
-packing set.
For each
x\in N\subset K
, there exists
n_{x}\in G
such that
x\in\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/2}(n_{x})
.
We claim that
\varphi:N\to G,x\mapsto n_{x}
is injective
(and hence
|N|\leq|G|
, which easily implies the claim).
To see this, note for
x,y\in N
with
n_{x}=n_{y}
that

\operatorname{dist}(x,y)\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,n_{x})+\operatorname{dist}(n_{y},y)\leq\varepsilon

and hence
x=y
, since
N
is
\varepsilon
-packing.
To prove the second inequality in
Equation

4.5
,
we can assume that
M_{K,\operatorname{dist}}(\varepsilon)<\infty
.
Thus, we can choose an
\varepsilon
-packing set
N\subset K
of maximal (finite) cardinality.
We claim that
N
is an external (in fact even internal)
\varepsilon
-net for
K
.
To see this, note that if
x\in K\setminus\bigcup_{x\in N}\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
then we have
x\notin N
, so that
\widetilde{N}:=N\cup\{x\}
would be an

\varepsilon
-packing set for
K
of strictly larger cardinality than
N
, which is impossible.

Using the relation (
4.5
), we now prove the following
entropy bound for a certain class of Fourier series, from which we will then easily
obtain
Proposition

4.4
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
, define

\mathcal{F}_{d,C}:=\bigg\{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}\,e^{2\pi i\langle n,\bullet\rangle}\hskip 10.00002pt\colon\hskip 10.00002pt(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\text{ with }\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+\|n\|_{\infty})\,|c_{n}|\leq C\bigg\}\subset L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d}).

Then there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(d,C)>0
satisfying

M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,C},L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}^{\mathrm{ext},L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon)\leq C_{0}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\varepsilon\in(0,1).

Proof.

Given a fixed
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(d,C)>0
, it is easy to see that we can without loss of generality
assume that
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
.
Moreover, it is easy to see that

M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,C},L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon)=M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,1},L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon/C)
.
Therefore, it is enough to consider the case
C=1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

For a suitable constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(d)\geq 1
to be specified below, let

N:=\lceil 3/\varepsilon\rceil,\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptn:=\bigg\lceil\Big(\frac{3^{d+1}C_{1}}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{\!1/\lambda}\,\bigg\rceil.

Furthermore, set
I_{N}:=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\colon\|k\|_{\infty}\leq 2N\}
,
let
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}=(e^{2\pi i\langle k,\bullet\rangle})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

denote the Fourier orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, and define

\Phi:\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{C}^{I_{N}}\to L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d}),\hskip 10.00002pt(c_{j})_{j\in I_{N}}\mapsto\sum_{j\in I_{N}}c_{j}\,e_{j}.

Given any subset
J\subset I_{N}
, let

\Omega_{J}:=\big\{(c_{j})_{j\in I_{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{N}}\hskip 10.00002pt\colon\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,j\in I_{N}:\,\,\,|c_{j}|\leq 2\cdot\mathds{1}_{J}(j)\big\}.

Step 1:

We show that for each
f\in\mathcal{F}_{d,1}
, there exists a subset

J\subset I_{N}
with
|J|\leq n
and some
c\in\Omega_{J}

satisfying
\|f-\Phi(c)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
.

To show this, let
f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{k}\,e_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,1}
be arbitrary
and define
g:=\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq N}c_{k}\,e_{k}
.
We then have

\|f-g\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>N}|c_{k}|\leq(1+N)^{-1}\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\geq N}(1+\|k\|_{\infty})\,|c_{k}|\leq(1+N)^{-1}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Next,
[
11
, Theorem 6.1]
shows
(for a suitable choice of
C_{1}=C_{1}(d)\geq 1
) that there exists a trigonometric polynomial
h

that is a linear combination of at most
n
of the exponential functions
e_{k}

and such that
\|g-h\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{1}\cdot n^{-\lambda}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{d+1}}
.

As usual in Fourier analysis, we identify
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})\cong L^{2}([0,1)^{d})\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})

and we denote the Fourier coefficients of
F\in L^{1}([0,1]^{d})

by
\widehat{F}(k)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle k,x\rangle}dx
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

D_{k}(x):=\sum_{\ell=-k}^{k}e^{2\pi i\ell x},\hskip 20.00003ptF_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}D_{\ell},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptV_{k}:=(1+e_{k}+e_{-k})\cdot F_{k}

denote the (one-dimensional) Dirichlet kernel, Fejér kernel, and de la Vallée Poussin kernel,
respectively.
Extend these one-dimensional kernels to the
d
-dimensional kernels
D_{k}^{d},F_{k}^{d},V_{k}^{d}

where
D_{k}^{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=D_{k}(x_{1})\cdots D_{k}(x_{d})
and similarly for the other kernels.
It is well-known (see for instance
[
32
, Pages 9 and 15]
)
that
\|F_{k}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}=1
and that
\widehat{V_{k}}(j)=1
for
|j|\leq k
.
Therefore,
\|V_{k}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\leq 3
and
\|V_{k}^{d}\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 3^{d}

and furthermore
\widehat{V_{k}^{d}}(j)=1
if
\|j\|_{\infty}\leq k
.
This easily implies that

\widehat{g}=\widehat{g}\cdot\widehat{\vphantom{V}\smash{V_{N}^{d}}}=\widehat{g\ast\vphantom{V}\smash{V_{N}^{d}}}

and hence
g=g\ast V_{N}^{d}
.
Furthermore, it is easy to see
V_{N}^{d}\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{\ell}\colon\ell\in I_{N}\}
,
and this implies (e.g. by considering the Fourier coefficients)
that
F:=h\ast V_{N}^{d}\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{\ell}\colon\ell\in I_{N}\}

is a trigonometric polynomial with at most
n
non-zero terms.
That is, there exists a subset
J\subset I_{N}

such that
|J|\leq n
and
F\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{\ell}\colon\ell\in J\}
.
Moreover,

\|g-F\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|(g-h)\ast V_{N}^{d}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|V_{N}^{d}\|_{L^{1}}\cdot\|g-h\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

and thus

\|f-F\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|f-g\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|g-F\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon.

Finally, note by definition of
\mathcal{F}_{d,1}
that
|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq 1
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
which implies that

|\widehat{F}(k)|\leq|\widehat{F}(k)-\widehat{f}(k)|+|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq\|F-f\|_{L^{1}}+1\leq 2.

Thus, we easily see that
F=\Phi(c)
for a suitable sequence
c\in\Omega_{J}
.

Step 2:

We complete the proof.

By
[
43
, Corollary 4.2.13]
, there exists a set

E\subset\overline{B}_{2}(0)\subset\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\overline{B}_{2}(0)\subset\bigcup_{z\in E}\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/(3n)}(z)
—that is,
E
is an
\frac{\varepsilon}{3n}
-net for
\overline{B}_{2}(0)
—and such that

|E|\leq\Bigl(1+\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon/(3n)}\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl(\tfrac{7n}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{2}.

For
J\subset I_{N}
with
|J|\leq n
, it is then easy to see that

E_{J}:=\big\{c\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{N}}\hskip 10.00002pt\colon\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,j\in I_{N}:\,\,c_{j}\in E\text{ if }j\in J\text{ and }c_{j}=0\text{ otherwise}\big\}\subset\Omega_{J}

satisfies
|E_{J}|\leq(7n/\varepsilon)^{2n}
and that
E_{J}
is an
\frac{\varepsilon}{3n}
-net for

\Omega_{J}
, where we consider the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm on
\Omega_{J}
.

Now, given any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{d,1}
, the preceding step produces a subset
J\subset I_{N}

with
|J|\leq n
and a sequence
c\in\Omega_{J}

such that
\|f-\Phi(c)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
.
As just seen, we can choose
c^{\prime}\in E_{J}
satisfying

\|c-c^{\prime}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3n}
and this implies

\|\Phi(c)-\Phi(c^{\prime})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sum_{j\in J}|c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}|\,\|e_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3n}|J|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

and hence
\|f-\Phi(c^{\prime})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon
.

Thus, setting
\mathscr{L}:=\{J\subset I_{N}\colon|J|\leq n\}
, we have shown that

\mathcal{F}_{d,1}\subset\bigcup_{J\in\mathscr{L}}\bigcup_{c^{\prime}\in E_{J}}\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}^{L^{\infty}}(\Phi(c^{\prime})),

and this implies

M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,1},L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon)\leq\ln\bigg(\sum_{J\in\mathscr{L}}|E_{J}|\bigg)\leq\ln\bigl(|\mathscr{L}|\cdot(7n/\varepsilon)^{2n}\bigr).

Next, note that
|I_{N}|=|\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap[-2N,2N]^{d}|\leq(1+4N)^{d}\leq(5N)^{d}
and

n\leq 1+(3^{d+1}C_{1}/\varepsilon)^{1/\lambda}\leq 2\cdot\bigl(3^{d+1}C_{1}/\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\frac{C_{2}}{d}\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}

for a suitable constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(d,C_{1},\lambda)=C_{2}(d)>0
.
Hence,
[
43
, Exercise 0.0.5]
shows

|\mathscr{L}|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\min\{n,|I_{N}|\}}\binom{|I_{N}|}{\ell}\leq\bigg(\frac{e\cdot|I_{N}|}{\min\{n,|I_{N}|\}}\bigg)^{\min\{n,|I_{N}|\}}\leq(e\cdot|I_{N}|)^{n}\leq(5eN)^{C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}}.

Therefore, and since
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,
so that
N\leq 1+\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
, we see

\ln(|\mathscr{L}|)\leq C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}\cdot\big(\ln(5e)+\ln(4)+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)\leq C_{3}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}\cdot\bigl(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)

for a suitable constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(d)>0
.
Similarly, recalling from above that
n\leq\frac{C_{2}}{d}\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}
, we see

\displaystyle\ln\big((7n/\varepsilon)^{2n}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2n\cdot\big(\ln(7n)+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}\cdot\Big(\ln(7C_{2})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1/\varepsilon)+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1\lambda}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big).

Overall, we have thus shown

M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,1},L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon)\leq\ln\big(|\mathscr{L}|\cdot(7n/\varepsilon)^{2n}\big)\leq(C_{3}+C_{4})\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/\lambda}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)

for
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
As discussed at the beginning of the proof, this proves the claim.
∎

Finally, we prove
Proposition

4.4
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.4
.

Let
C_{0}=C_{0}(d)>0
as in
Lemma

4.3
.

Lemma

4.5
provides a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(C_{0},C,d)=C_{1}(C,d)>0

such that

M_{\mathcal{F}_{d,C_{0}C},L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}^{\mathrm{ext},L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}(\varepsilon)\leq C_{1}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Thus, given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we can find an (external)
\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
-net

for
\mathcal{F}_{d,C_{0}C}
satisfying

\ln|G_{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{1}\cdot(\varepsilon/4)^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}\cdot\big(1+\ln(4/\varepsilon)\big)\leq C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}\cdot\big(1+\ln(1/\varepsilon)\big)

for a suitable constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(C,d)>0
.

Now, setting
e_{n}:=e^{2\pi i\langle\frac{n}{2},\bullet\rangle}
for

,
given

,
Lemma

4.3
shows that
we can write

for
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\subset\mathbb{C}

satisfying
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|n|)|c_{n}|\leq C_{0}\,C
.
Hence, setting

f_{1}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{2n}e^{2\pi i\langle n,\bullet\rangle}

and

f_{2}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{2n+1}e^{2\pi i\langle n,\bullet\rangle},

and

.
Thus, we see for suitable

\|f-(g_{1}+e_{1}\,g_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|f_{1}-g_{1}\|+\|e_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot\|f_{2}-g_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+1\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Overall, this shows that

\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}:=\{g+e_{1}\,h\colon g,h\in G_{\varepsilon}\}\subset L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})

4.1.2
Lower bounds for the entropy numbers

In this subsection, we complement the upper bounds on the entropy numbers of the previous subsection by lower bounds.
Since by Lemma
4.1
, we know that the general Barron class
\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
is larger than the Barron class
B_{d}(C)
of Definition
2.1
, lower bounds on the entropy of
B_{d}(C)
imply a lower bound on the entropy of
\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
. We will therefore mainly study the special class
B_{d}(C)
below. We observe that this lower bound coincides with the upper bound on the entropy of
\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)
established in Proposition
4.4
up to a logarithmic factor.

Proposition 4.6
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
C>0
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{GB}_{d}^{\ast}(C):=\bigl\{f\in\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C)\,\,\colon\,\,0\leq f\leq 1\bigr\}.

Then there exist
\varepsilon^{(0)}=\varepsilon^{(0)}(d,C)>0
and
C_{0}=C_{0}(d,C)>0

such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{(0)}]
, we have

M_{\mathcal{GB}_{d}(C),L^{1}}(\varepsilon)\geq M_{\mathcal{GB}_{d}^{\ast}(C),L^{1}}(\varepsilon)\geq M_{B_{d}(C),L^{1}}(\varepsilon)\geq C_{0}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{2+d}}=C_{0}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d})}.

Proof.

Step 1:

Fix
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}\bigl((0,1)^{d}\bigr)
with
\varphi\geq 0

and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,dx=1
; note that
\varphi
only depends on
d
.
Furthermore, fix
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\psi\geq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}

on
[0,1]^{d}
.
Again,
\psi
only depends on
d
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
for the moment and define
\Omega_{N}:=\{0,\dots,N-1\}^{d}
.
Furthermore, for
\Omega\subset\Omega_{N}
and
\theta\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}
, define

f_{N,\Omega,\theta}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\big[\theta_{\omega}\cdot\varphi\bigl(N\cdot(\bullet-\tfrac{\omega}{N})\bigr)\big]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptg_{N,\Omega,\theta}:=\psi+f_{N,\Omega,\theta}.

Note that
\|f_{N,\Omega,\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
since the supports of the functions
\varphi(N\cdot(\bullet-\frac{\omega}{N}))
,

\omega\in\Omega_{N}
are pairwise disjoint.
Since the supports are also all contained in
[0,1]^{d}
, this implies

0\leq g_{N,\Omega,\theta}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}=:C_{1}=C_{1}(d).

(4.6)

In this step, we show that there exists a constant
C_{4}=C_{4}(d,C)\geq C_{1}C>0
such that

\forall\,\Omega\subset\Omega_{N}\hskip 10.00002pt\exists\,\theta_{\Omega}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}:\hskip 20.00003pt\tfrac{C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}\cdot g_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}\in B_{d}(C).

(4.7)

To prove
Equation

4.7
,
first note that if we define the modulation
M_{\eta}g
of
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}

by
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
M_{\eta}g(x):=e^{2\pi i\langle\eta,x\rangle}g(x)
, then

\widehat{f_{N,\Omega,\theta}}=N^{-d}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\big(\theta_{\omega}\cdot M_{-\omega/N}\bigl[\widehat{\varphi}(N^{-1}\bullet)\bigr]\big).

Now, consider
\theta
as a random variable that is uniformly distributed in

\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}
.
Then, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality shows that

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\theta_{\omega}a_{\omega}\bigr|\leq\big(\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\theta_{\omega}a_{\omega}\bigr|^{2}\big)^{1/2}=(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|a_{\omega}|^{2})^{1/2}

for all
(a_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}
.
Combining these observations and using the change of variables
\eta=N^{-1}\xi

and the estimate
|\Omega|\leq|\Omega_{N}|\leq N^{d}
, we see

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot\bigl|\widehat{f}_{N,\Omega,\theta}(\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi\bigg]&=N^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(N^{-1}\xi)|\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigg|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\theta_{\omega}\cdot e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}\langle\omega,\xi\rangle}\bigg|\,d\xi\\
&\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}\cdot N^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(N^{-1}\xi)|\,d\xi\\
&\leq N^{d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|N\eta|)\cdot|\widehat{\varphi}(\eta)|\,d\eta\\
&\leq C_{2}\cdot N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}\end{split}

(4.8)

for a constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(d)>0
.
In particular, this implies the existence of
\theta_{\Omega}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|\widehat{f}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C_{2}\cdot N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}

and hence

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\cdot|\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C_{2}\,N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|)\,|\widehat{\psi}(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq C_{2}\,N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}+C_{3}\leq\frac{C_{4}}{2\pi}\,N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}

for suitable
C_{3},C_{4}>0
only depending on
d
and
C
, where we can without loss of generality
assume that
C_{4}\geq C_{1}C
.
By Fourier inversion, this implies for
c:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle g_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(x)

\displaystyle=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}-1)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi/(2\pi))d\xi,

where we applied a change of variables from
\xi
to
\xi/2\pi
.
We have by change of variables from
\xi
to
2\pi\xi
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}|\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi/(2\pi))|d\xi=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi||\widehat{g}_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}(\xi)|d\xi\leq C_{4}\,N^{1+\frac{d}{2}}.

By definition of
B_{d}(C)
, this easily implies that

\frac{C}{C_{4}}N^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}g_{N,\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}\in B_{d}(C)
,
as claimed in Equation (
4.7
).

Step 2:

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
for this step.
Define

\varphi_{N,\omega}:=\varphi\bigl(N\cdot(\bullet-\tfrac{\omega}{N})\bigr)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for}\hskip 20.00003pt\omega\in\Omega_{N}=\{0,\dots,N-1\}^{d}.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset(0,1)^{d}
, it is easy to see that
the family
(\varphi_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega_{N}}
has disjoint supports,
all contained in
[0,1]^{d}
and
\|\varphi_{\omega}\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{d})}=N^{-d}\kappa

for
\kappa:=\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}
.
Now, for
\Omega\subset\Omega_{N}
and
\theta\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}
,
define
h_{\Omega,\theta}:=\frac{C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}\cdot g_{N,\Omega,\theta}
.
Then we see for
\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega_{N}
, and
\theta\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Omega}

as well as
\widetilde{\theta}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\widetilde{\Omega}}
that

\begin{split}\big\|h_{\Omega,\theta}-h_{\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\theta}}\big\|_{L^{1}}&=\tfrac{C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}\cdot\bigg\|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\theta_{\omega}\,\varphi_{\omega}-\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Omega}}\widetilde{\theta}_{\omega}\,\varphi_{\omega}\bigg\|_{L^{1}}\\
&=\tfrac{C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}\cdot\bigg\|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\cap\widetilde{\Omega}}(\theta_{\omega}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\omega})\,\varphi_{\omega}+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}}\theta_{\omega}\,\varphi_{\omega}-\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}\widetilde{\theta}_{\omega}\,\varphi_{\omega}\bigg\|_{L^{1}}\\
&=\tfrac{\kappa C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{3}{2}d)}\cdot\bigg(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\cap\widetilde{\Omega}}|\theta_{\omega}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\omega}|+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}}|\theta_{\omega}|+\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega}|\widetilde{\theta}_{\omega}|\bigg)\\
&\geq\tfrac{\kappa C}{C_{4}}\cdot N^{-(1+\frac{3}{2}d)}\cdot|\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}|,\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}=(\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\Omega})\cup(\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega).\end{split}

(4.9)

Step 3:
Now, choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
16\mid N

and define
n:=N^{d}
and
r:=\frac{n}{16}
, as well as
k:=\frac{n}{4}
.
Note that
\ln(\frac{en}{2r})=\ln(\frac{en}{n/8})=\ln(8e)\leq 4
and hence

.
Therefore,
[
43
, Theorem 4.3.5]

shows that there exists a set
J\subset 2^{\{1,\dots,n\}}
satisfying
|J|=2^{k}

and such that
|I\Delta I^{\prime}|>r
for all
I,I^{\prime}\in J
with
I\neq I^{\prime}
,
where
I\Delta I^{\prime}=(I\setminus I^{\prime})\cup(I^{\prime}\setminus I)
denotes the symmetric difference
of
I
and
I^{\prime}
.

Let us now identify
\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\Omega_{N}=\{0,\dots,N-1\}^{d}
;
this is possible since
n=N^{d}
.
Thus, we see that there exists a set
\mathcal{I}\subset 2^{\Omega_{N}}

satisfying
|\mathcal{I}|=2^{k}=2^{N^{d}/4}
and

|\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}|>r=\frac{N^{d}}{16}

for all
\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}\in\mathcal{I}
with
\Omega\neq\widetilde{\Omega}
.
In combination with Equations (
4.9
)
and (
4.7
), this shows that

M_{N}:=\big\{h_{\Omega,\theta_{\Omega}}\colon\Omega\in\mathcal{I}\big\}\subset B_{d}(C)

is a
\delta
-packing set for
B_{d}(C)
(with respect to the metric induced
by

) for

where

.
Note furthermore that

.

), we see

Step 4:
In this step, we complete the proof.
For brevity, define

for

.
Step 3 shows for

with

and

that

.

Now, set
\varepsilon^{(0)}:=\varepsilon_{16}=C_{5}\cdot{16}^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}
.
For

there then exists

such that

.
On the one hand, this implies for

.

On the other hand, since

.

,

\mathscr{C}=B_{d-1}(C)
, with

\displaystyle\alpha=\beta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d-1}=\frac{d+1}{2(d-1)}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptb=1,\hskip 10.00002pta=0.

(4.10)

This yields the following result.

.

and
C>0
.

the associated Barron class,

Then,

\displaystyle m^{-\frac{d+1}{3d-1}}\cdot[\ln(2m)]^{-\frac{(d+1)(5d-3)}{(3d-1)(2d-2)}}

.

Proof.

The result follows directly from

by noting that (

For completeness, we define the

Definition 5.5
.

Define

\phi\hskip 10.00002pt\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pt\phi(x)\coloneqq\max\{0,1-x\}.

Let
d\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
[0,1]^{d}

For a (measurable) target concept

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\phi,\mu,h}(f)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\Big[\phi\Big(\bigl(2h(X)-1\bigr)\cdot\bigl(2f(X)-1\bigr)\Big)\Big].

For a sample

,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,S}(f_{S})=\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,S}(f).

For the sets

Theorem 5.7
.

There is a universal constant
\tau\geq 1
such that the following holds:
Let

,

,
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and

Let

and let

be the uniform

For each

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}(m)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\lceil 144\tau^{2}d^{4}M^{2}R^{2}\cdot m^{2/3}\big\rceil,

\displaystyle N(m)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\lceil M\cdot\bigl(\widetilde{N}(m)+4d+2\bigr)\big\rceil,

\displaystyle W(m)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\lceil 54\,d^{2}\cdot M\cdot\widetilde{N}(m)\big\rceil,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big\lceil\,\,5d\cdot\bigl(1+\tau\sqrt{d}R\bigr)+2\raisebox{2.56064pt}{$\sqrt{\vphantom{M_{j}}\smash{\raisebox{-3.41418pt}{$\widetilde{N}(m)$}}}$}\,\,\Big\rceil.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
S
be a training sample of size
m
;
that is,
S=\bigl(X_{i},h(X_{i})\bigr)_{i=1}^{m}
with

for

\mathbb{E}_{S}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\bigl(\bigl|H(\Phi_{m,S}(X))-h(X)\bigr|^{2}\bigr)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{S}\big[\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\bigl(H(\Phi_{m,S}(X))\neq h(X)\bigr)\big]\lesssim m^{-1/3+\kappa},

where the implied constant only depends on

.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first part of

5.4
.
To this end, note for

that

and hence

Now, note because of our convention

Hence, we see for

\operatorname{sign}(2x-1)\neq 2y-1\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathds{1}_{[0,\infty)}(2x-1)\neq y\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathds{1}_{[1/2,\infty)}(x)\neq y\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptH(x)\neq y.

Applying this to

and

, we thus see

\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\big(H(\Phi_{m,S}(X))\neq h(X)\big)=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\big(\operatorname{sign}(2\Phi_{m,S}(X)-1)\neq 2h(X)-1\big).

(5.5)

Thus, it remains to verify that the assumptions of

5.6
are satisfied
for

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\ast}:=\mathcal{NN}_{\ast}(d,N(m),W(m),B(m)),\hskip 20.00003pta_{m}:=m^{-1/3},\hskip 20.00003pt\delta_{m}^{\ast}:=m^{-1/3+\kappa},

and

for all

Now, if

.
Furthermore, note that

A.1
Explicit scaling relation for an implicit condition of Barron and Yang

Lemma A.1
.

Let
V:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be continuous and non-increasing
and such that
V(\varepsilon)\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
Then the following hold:

1.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
unique

\varepsilon_{n}\in(0,\infty)

satisfying
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=V(\varepsilon_{n})/n
.
Furthermore,
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

2.

If
V
is of the form

\displaystyle V(\varepsilon)=C\cdot\bigl(\max\{1,\varepsilon^{-1}\}\bigr)^{\alpha}\cdot\ln^{\beta}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})

(A.1)

for certain
C,\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
, then there exist

c_{\flat},c_{\sharp}>0
satisfying

c_{\flat}\cdot\big(\ln^{\beta}(2n)/n\big)^{1/(2+\alpha)}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\leq c_{\sharp}\cdot\big(\ln^{\beta}(2n)/n\big)^{1/(2+\alpha)}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(A.2)

Proof.

Part 1:

Define
W_{n}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\varepsilon\mapsto n\,\varepsilon^{2}-V(\varepsilon)
.
Then
W_{n}
is continuous and strictly increasing as the sum of a strictly increasing
and a non-decreasing function.
Furthermore, it is easy to see
W_{n}(\varepsilon)\to-\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0

and
W_{n}(\varepsilon)\geq n\,\varepsilon^{2}-V(1)
for
\varepsilon\geq 1
, and hence
W_{n}(\varepsilon)\to\infty

as
\varepsilon\to\infty
.
By the intermediate value theorem, this implies existence of
\varepsilon_{n}\in(0,\infty)

satisfying
W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})=0
, and this is equivalent to
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=V(\varepsilon_{n})/n
.
Finally,
\varepsilon_{n}
is unique since
W_{n}
is strictly increasing.

To see that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, assume towards a contradiction that there exist
c>0

and a subsequence
(\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}}\geq c

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
V
is non-increasing, this implies
V(\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}})\leq V(c)
and hence

c^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}}^{2}=V(\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}})/n_{\ell}\leq V(c)/n_{\ell}\to 0

as
\ell\to\infty
, which is the desired contradiction.

Part 2:

Note that
\varepsilon\mapsto\big(\max\{1,\varepsilon^{-1}\}\big)^{\alpha}

and
\varepsilon\mapsto\ln^{\beta}(2+\varepsilon^{-1})
are both non-increasing
and positive functions, so that
V
given by (
A.1
) is non-increasing
and continuous.
Furthermore, it is easy to see
V(\varepsilon)\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
so that the given
V
satisfies the general assumptions of the lemma.

For brevity, set
\theta:=\frac{1}{2+\alpha}>0
and define

c_{\flat}:=\min\bigl\{1,\big((\theta/2)^{\beta}\cdot C\big)^{\theta}\bigr\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptc_{\sharp}:=\max\bigl\{1,\big((2\theta)^{\beta}\cdot C\big)^{\theta}\bigr\}

as well as

\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat}:=c_{\flat}\cdot\big(\ln^{\beta}(n)/n\big)^{\theta}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp}:=c_{\sharp}\cdot\big(\ln^{\beta}(n)/n\big)^{\theta}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}.

It is not hard to see that there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(C,\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}

such that
0<\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat}\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
We will show below with
W_{n}(\varepsilon)=n\,\varepsilon^{2}-V(\varepsilon)
as in the proof of the first part
that there exists
n_{1}=n_{1}(C,\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})\leq 0\leq W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})
for
n\geq n_{1}
.
Since
W_{n}
is strictly increasing with
W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})=0
, this will then imply

\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp}
for
n\geq n_{1}
,
which easily implies validity of the target estimate (
A.2
),
possibly after adjusting
c_{\flat}
and
c_{\sharp}
.

To see
W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})\leq 0
, first note for
n\geq n_{1}

and
n_{1}=n_{1}(C,\alpha,\beta)\geq n_{0}
sufficiently large that

\ln\big((\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})^{-1}\big)=-\ln c_{\flat}-\theta\beta\ln(\ln(n))+\theta\,\ln(n)\geq\frac{\theta}{2}\ln(n).

Using this estimate and recalling that
\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat}\leq\frac{1}{2}\leq 1
,
we see

\displaystyle W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})

\displaystyle=n\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})^{2}-V(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})^{2}-C\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})^{-\alpha}\cdot\ln^{\beta}\bigl((\varepsilon_{n}^{\flat})^{-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq c_{\flat}^{2}\cdot n^{1-2\theta}\cdot\ln^{2\beta\theta}(n)-c_{\flat}^{-\alpha}C\cdot n^{\alpha\theta}\cdot\ln^{-\alpha\beta\theta}(n)\cdot(\theta/2)^{\beta}\cdot\ln^{\beta}(n)

\displaystyle=n^{\alpha\theta}\cdot\ln^{2\beta\theta}(n)\cdot c_{\flat}^{-\alpha}\cdot\big(c_{\flat}^{2+\alpha}-C\cdot(\theta/2)^{\beta}\big)\leq 0,

thanks to our choice of
\theta
and
c_{\flat}
.

On the other hand, we note for
n\geq n_{1}\geq n_{0}\geq 3\geq e

that
\ln(\ln(n))\geq\ln(\ln(e))=0
.
In combination with
c_{\sharp}\geq 1
, this shows

\ln\big((\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}\big)=-\ln(c_{\sharp})-\beta\theta\ln(\ln(n))+\theta\ln(n)\leq\theta\ln(n).

Finally, recall that
0<\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and hence

\ln\bigl(2+(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}\bigr)\leq\ln\big((\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-2}\big)=2\,\ln\bigl((\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}\bigr)

and
2+(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}\leq 2\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}

for
n\geq n_{0}
.
Therefore, we see overall for
n\geq n_{1}\geq n_{0}
that

\displaystyle W_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})

\displaystyle=n\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{2}-V(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})

\displaystyle\geq c_{\sharp}^{2}\cdot n^{1-2\theta}\cdot\ln^{2\beta\theta}(n)-2^{\beta}C\cdot(\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-\alpha}\cdot\ln^{\beta}\bigl((\varepsilon_{n}^{\sharp})^{-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq c_{\sharp}^{2}\cdot n^{\alpha\theta}\cdot\ln^{2\beta\theta}(n)-2^{\beta}\theta^{\beta}C\cdot c_{\sharp}^{-\alpha}\cdot n^{\alpha\theta}\cdot\ln^{\beta(1-\alpha\theta)}(n)

\displaystyle=c_{\sharp}^{-\alpha}\cdot n^{\alpha\theta}\ln^{2\beta\theta}(n)\cdot\big(c_{\sharp}^{2+\alpha}-(2\theta)^{\beta}C\big)\geq 0,

by our choice of
\theta
and
c_{\sharp}
.
As seen above, this completes the proof.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

Since the definition of the set

in

, Definition 3.3]

Step 1 (Barron functions as in
[
9
]
):

In
[
9
, Definition 2.1]
, given a subset

with non-empty interior and some
x_{0}\in X
and
C>0
,

F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}

where
|\xi|_{X,x_{0}}=\sup_{x\in X}|\langle\xi,x-x_{0}\rangle|
.
Now, for
X=[0,1]^{d}
and
x_{0}=0
, it is easy to see that

|\xi|_{X,x_{0}}\leq\|\xi\|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\sqrt{d}|\xi|
.
Using this estimate, it follows that

\pm B(C)\subset\mathcal{B}_{\sqrt{d}C}([0,1]^{d},0).

Step 2 (Barron approximation set):

In
[
9
, Definition 3.1]
, given
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-empty
interior and
C>0
, the
Barron approximation set

is defined.
Its precise definition is immaterial for us; we only need to know that there exists an

absolute constant

\tau>0
satisfying

\mathcal{B}_{C}(X,x_{0})\subset\mathcal{BA}_{\tau C}(X)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,x_{0}\in X;

this is shown in
[
9

.
Furthermore,
[

shows that if

.
In combination with Step 1, we thus see that

\forall\,f\in B(C)\text{ and }Q\subset[0,1]^{d}\text{ with non-empty interior}:\hskip 10.00002pt(\pm f)|_{Q}\in\mathcal{BA}_{\tau\sqrt{d}C}(Q).

Step 3 (Sets with Barron boundary as in
[
9
]
):

For

and

,
let us write

Furthermore, for

In

Step 4 (Completing the proof):

Let

be compact with

.
That is, there exist (closed, axis-aligned, non-degenerate) rectangles
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{M}\subset[0,1]^{d}

with pairwise disjoint interiors and with
\Omega\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}Q_{i}

and such that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, there exist
f_{i}\in H_{B(C)}

and a permutation matrix
P_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

\mathds{1}_{\Omega}=f_{i}\circ P_{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{or}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathds{1}_{\Omega}=1-f_{i}\circ P_{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{almost everywhere on }Q_{i}.

Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.
Since
f_{i}\in H_{B(C)}
, there exists
b:[0,1]^{d-1}\to[0,1]

satisfying
b=b_{i}\in B(C)
and
f_{i}(x)=\mathds{1}_{B(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1})\leq x_{d}}

for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
.

Given a permutation

and

define

by

and note

for permutations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{d}
.
We can then choose
\pi\in S_{d}
satisfying
P_{i}x=x^{\pi}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Set

, where

is the permutation
that swaps

and

can be seen as an element of

Let

be the associated permutation matrix

for all

.

Given

As above, we thus see

Overall, we have shown as claimed that

A.3
Equivalence of convergence in
L^{1}
and in measure on the set of probability densities

We show here that the topology of convergence in measure coincides with the topology
induced by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}
on the set
\mathcal{D}
of probability density functions.
Since both topologies are metrizable (see e.g.
[
25
, Theorem 6.7]

for convergence in measure),
it suffices to show that the convergent sequences are the same.
It is well-known (see e.g.
[
25
, Remark 6.11]
)
that convergence in
L^{1}
implies convergence in measure.
Conversely, suppose towards a contradiction that there exists a sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{D}

and
p\in\mathcal{D}
satisfying
p_{n}\to p
, but
\|p_{n}-p\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}\not\to 0
.
Passing to a subsequence, we can assume that
\|p_{n}-p\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}\geq\varepsilon

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\varepsilon>0
.
By passing to a further subsequence, we can assume that
p_{n}\to p
almost everywhere;
see e.g.
[
25
, Corollary 6.13]
.
Now,
[
23
, Lemma 1.34]
shows because of

\|p_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}=1=\|p\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}
for all
n

that
\|p_{n}-p\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}\to 0
, in contradiction to

\|p_{n}-p\|_{L^{1}(\lambda)}\geq\varepsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
\square

A.4
The relation between Kullback-Leibler and Hellinger distance

Recall the elementary estimate
1+x\leq e^{x}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,
y\leq e^{y-1}
for
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
For
y>0
, we can take the logarithm to obtain
\ln(y)\leq y-1
and hence
-\ln(y)\geq 1-y
.

Hence,

\displaystyle D(p\parallel q)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\int-\ln(\sqrt{q}/\sqrt{p})\cdot p\,d\lambda\geq 2\cdot\int p\cdot\big(1-\sqrt{q}/\sqrt{p}\big)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=2-2\int\sqrt{q}\sqrt{p}\,d\lambda,

since
\int p\,d\lambda=1
.
Finally, note because of
\int p\,d\lambda=1=\int q\,d\lambda
that

\displaystyle d_{H}^{2}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\int(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q})^{2}\,d\lambda=\int p+q-2\sqrt{p}\sqrt{q}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=2-2\int\sqrt{p}\sqrt{q}\,d\lambda.

Overall, this implies that
d_{H}^{2}(p,q)\leq D(p\parallel q)

and in particular that
D(p\parallel q)\geq 0
.
