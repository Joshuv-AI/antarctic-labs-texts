A framework for rigorous computational methods using Haar wavelets for differential equations
Guilherme Nakassima , , Marcio Gameiro

Abstract
This work presents a framework for a-posteriori error-estimating algorithms for differential equations which combines the radii polynomial approach with Haar wavelets. By using Haar wavelets, we obtain recursive structures for the matrix representations of the differential operators and quadratic nonlinearities, which can be exploited for the radii polynomial method in order to get error estimates in the L 2 L^{2} sense. This allows the method to be applicable when the system or solution is not continuous, which is a limitation of other radii-polynomial-based methods. Numerical examples show how the method is implemented in practice. Keywords: Rigorous computation, Computer-assisted proofs, Haar wavelets, Nonlinear dynamical systems. 2020 MSC: 34A34, 34L30, 65G20, 65H10, 65T60.

Introduction

Rigorous computation is an area under active development since the 1980s
[
18
]
. With an steady increase of computing power, numerical methods became viable tools for analyzing differential equations and gaining insight on structures such as invariant objects. However, standard numerical methods provide only approximations; the results are non-rigorous and cannot be used in formal proofs. They can only be used to gain insights on the true structures of the system. Moreover, some structures, such as bifurcations, may still be hidden even when using very accurate numerical methods.

Rigorous computational methods try to fill these gaps, providing mathematically valid estimates and bounds for truncation and rounding errors, and rigorously proving the existence of such hidden structures. Over the years, a number of such methods were developed, such as rigorous integration
[
29
,
8
,
17
]
, Conley index methods
[
4
,
14
]
, self-consistent bounds
[
30
,
28
]
and discretization methods
[
19
,
9
]
. A more thorough review can be seen in
[
20
]
and references therein.

Of particular interest to us are the
radii polynomials
approach
[
5
,
7
,
27
,
13
,
11
,
6
,
2
,
22
,
26
]
. These methods recast the problem of investigating the existence of structures as finding solutions to functional equations, usually expanding the solutions in terms of a basis. Then, usual numerical methods are employed to find an approximate solution to these equations. Finally, using fixed point theorems, we can guarantee the existence of a true, rigorous solution of the functional equations within certain bounds of the numerical solution. The hypotheses of the fixed point theorems are in turn proven to be satisfied with the aid of the so-called radii polynomials.

In this work we present a new radii polynomial method employing Haar wavelets. While many other bases were already employed by this approach, such as Taylor series
[
22
]
, Fourier series
[
27
]
and Chebyshev polynomials
[
13
]
, to the best of our knowledge, no attempt has been made to combine wavelet methods and rigorous computations. We also believe that this method can be can be a framework to build other methods upon, such as rigorous continuation methods and methods for partial differential equations.

Wavelets are functions that form an orthonormal basis for the
L^{2}
function space. While wavelet theory was only relatively recently formalized, their special properties — such as time and frequency localization — made them widely applicable in many fields, such as signal processing and compression algorithms. This poses an interesting case, because most of the aforementioned radii polynomial methods were proposed to work with smooth functions, while our wavelet-based radii polynomial method works in more general settings.

The radii polynomial method presented in this paper is based on the ideas of a numerical method for solving differential equations, the
Haar wavelet method
, which was first proposed in
[
3
]
. It is assumed that the highest-order derivative is expressed in terms of wavelets, and the solutions are given by the integral of the series, essentially rewriting the differential equation in its integral form. This allows the method to work with only Haar wavelets, the simplest wavelet available, and leads to a matrix representation of the integral operator. The simplicity of the Haar wavelet allows this matrix to be easily and recursively calculated. The original Haar wavelet method was further analyzed, developed and applied in several publications
[
12
,
21
,
1
,
24
,
16
,
15
]
.

In our method, we find the functional equations for the radii polynomial method using the same expansions of the Haar wavelet method. This essentially transforms the differential equation into an integral one, and allow us to use a radii polynomial theorem similar to
[
25
]
. Also, by using Haar wavelets, the integral operator and nonlinearities can be represented using infinite but recursive matrices, allowing us to make the estimates needed in the radii polynomial approach.

The work is organized in the following way. In Section
2
we introduce the Haar wavelet and its integral, and review some of their properties. In Section
3
we introduce the radii polynomial method and prove the theorems that guarantee the existence of a true solution, provided that certain estimates are satisfied. In Section
4
we study quadratic nonlinearities in order to prove estimates needed in the method. In Section
5
we present some examples illustrating the applications of the proposed method.

2
The Haar wavelets and their integral

Here we introduce the Haar wavelet and its integral, which are one of the pillars of the proposed methods.
Consider the space
L^{2}([0,1])
of the square-integrable functions
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the usual Lebesgue measure. The Haar wavelets are a family of functions
\{\phi,\psi_{j,k}\}\subset L^{2}([0,1])
defined, for
j=0,1,\ldots
and
k=0,1,\ldots,2^{j}-1
, by

\phi(t):=\begin{cases}1&,\quad 0\leq t<1\\
0&,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{,}\qquad\psi_{j,k}(t):=\begin{cases}{2^{j/2}}&,\quad\frac{k}{2^{j}}\leq t<\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}}\\[3.0pt]
-{2^{j/2}}&,\quad\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}}\leq t<\frac{k+1}{2^{j}}\\[3.0pt]
0&,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1)

The Haar wavelets form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}([0,1])
; the proof is in many standard texts in wavelet theory, see e.g.
[
10
]
. Hence any function
y(t)\in L^{2}([0,1])
can be expanded into a unique Haar wavelet series

y(t)=c_{1}\phi(t)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}c_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}(t),

where
c_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}y(t)\phi(t)\,dt
,
c_{j,k}=\int_{0}^{1}y(t)\psi_{j,k}(t)\,dt
, and the sum converges in
L^{2}([0,1])
. If we make
i=2^{j}+k+1
, then the sequence
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Notation
.

We can change between the “one-index” and “two-indices” notations, depending on which is more convenient in each case. One can be converted to the other by making, for all
i>2
,

i=2^{j}+k+1\iff\begin{aligned} j&=\lfloor\text{log}_{2}i\rfloor\\
k&=i-2^{j}-1\end{aligned}

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function. The index
i=1
is reserved for the scaling function
\phi
.

Conversely, any
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defines a unique
f(t)\in L^{2}([0,1])
by making

f(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}\psi_{i}(t)=c_{1}\phi(t)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}c_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}(t).

This means that we can define an invertible operator
\mathcal{H}:L^{2}([0,1])\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined element-wise as

(\mathcal{H}f)_{i}:=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\psi_{i}(t)\,dt.

The inverse Haar transform
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
is given by

(\mathcal{H}^{-1}\text{{c}})(t):=\text{{h}}^{T}(t)\text{{c}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}\psi_{i}(t)\quad,\quad\text{{h}}(t):=(\psi_{1}(t),\psi_{2}(t),...)

It is worth noting that the Haar transform is an isometry, due to the fact that the Haar wavelet system is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1])
.

Another interesting and useful property of the Haar wavelets is what we call the “nesting property”:

Proposition 2.1

(Nesting property)
.

Let
\psi_{j,k}
and
\psi_{m,n}
be two Haar wavelets such that
j<m
. If
\text{supp }\psi_{j,k}\cap\text{supp }\psi_{m,n}\not=\emptyset
, then either
\text{supp }\psi_{m,n}\subseteq[\frac{k}{2^{j}},\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}})
or
\text{supp }\psi_{m,n}\subseteq[\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}},\frac{k+1}{2^{j}})
.

The proof is simple and will be omitted; it consists in comparing the supports of the wavelets
\phi_{j,k}
, which are dyadic intervals of length
2^{-j}
, for different
j
.
Intuitively, it means that, if the supports of two wavelets at different resolutions overlap, then the support of the “finer” wavelet (that is, the higher-resolution one) is entirely nested within either the positive or the negative part of the “coarser” wavelet.

For this work, we are also interested in the integral of the Haar wavelets, and how the integral relates to the wavelet themselves. The integrals of the Haar wavelet family in the interval
[0,1]
are the triangular functions given by

w_{1}(t)=t\qquad\text{,}\qquad w_{j,k}(t)=\begin{cases}{2^{j/2}}\left(t-\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right)&,\quad\frac{k}{2^{j}}\leq t\leq\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}}\\[3.0pt]
{2^{j/2}}\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{j}}-t\right)&,\quad\frac{k+0.5}{2^{j}}\leq t\leq\frac{k+1}{2^{j}}\\[3.0pt]
0&,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

We also extend the one-index notation to the Haar wavelet integrals
w_{i}
.

The Haar wavelet integrals are continuous functions in
[0,1]
; thus they are square-integrable in that interval, and can be expanded in Haar wavelet series themselves:

w_{i}(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}P_{i,l}\psi_{l}(t)=P\text{{h}}^{T}(t)\ ,\ P_{i,l}:=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{l}(t)w_{i}(t)\,dt.

(3)

While
P
is expressed by an infinite matrix, there is a recursive formula to compute it. We must first define the Haar matrix of our wavelet system:

Definition 2.1
.

For a given resolution
J
, the
Haar matrix

H_{M}
of order
M=2^{J+1}
is given element-wise by

(H_{M})_{p,q}:=\psi_{p}(t_{q})

(4)

where
\displaystyle t_{q}=\frac{q-0.5}{M}
, for
q=1,\dots,M
.

The
discrete Haar transform matrix

HT_{M}
of order
M=2^{J+1}
is defined by

HT_{M}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}H_{M}

(5)

A particularly important fact is that
HT_{M}
is unitary for all
J
, and hence
H_{M}
is invertible and
(H_{M})^{-1}=\frac{1}{M}H_{M}^{T}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The infinite matrix
P
can be recursively calculated as

P_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\qquad,\qquad P_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{m}&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sqrt{m^{3}}}H_{m}\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sqrt{m^{3}}}H_{m}^{T}&0_{m\times m}\end{bmatrix}

(6)

for
m=2^{j}
and
j=0,1,2,\dots
.

The proof for this formula is in
[
3
]
, with some modifications to account for the fact that we are using normalized wavelets.

Let us prove that
P^{T}\text{{c}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\text{{c}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We first need to define some projections. Given a resolution level
J
, let
M=2^{J+1}
as before, and define the projection
\Pi_{M}
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{M}

(7)

\displaystyle\text{{c}}

\displaystyle\mapsto(c_{1},...,c_{M}).

We identify the vector
(c_{1},...,c_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with its infinite-dimensional counterpart
(c_{1},...,c_{M},0,0,...)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We also define the projection
\Pi_{\infty}\in B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
as

\Pi_{\infty}\text{{c}}:=(I-\Pi_{M})\text{{c}}.

Notation
.

Given
\text{{c}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we sometimes divide it into blocks of length
2^{n}
,
n=0,1,2,...
as

\text{{c}}=\left(\text{{c}}^{*}_{0},\text{{c}}^{*}_{1},\text{{c}}^{*}_{2},\text{{c}}^{*}_{4},...\right)^{T}\ ,\text{ where }\text{{c}}^{*}_{0}=c_{1}\text{ , }\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{n}}=\left(c_{2^{n-1}+1},c_{2^{n-1}+2},...,c_{2^{n}}\right)^{T}.

(8)

Also, given a matrix
A
, we denote
A_{m_{1}:m_{2},n_{1}:n_{2}}
the submatrix of
A
given by

A_{m_{1}:m_{2},n_{1}:n_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{m_{1},n_{1}}&\cdots&A_{m_{1},n_{2}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
A_{m_{2},n_{1}}&\cdots&A_{m_{2},n_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

If we wish to take all rows or all columns of
A
, we denote
A_{*,n_{1}:n_{2}}
and
A_{m_{1}:m_{2},*}
, respectively. Lastly, to reduce notation clutter, we denote
\text{{c}}_{M}=\Pi_{M}\text{{c}}
and
\text{{c}}_{\infty}=\Pi_{\infty}\text{{c}}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
\text{{{c}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
M=2^{J+1}
,
J=0,1,2,...
and the projections as defined before,

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{M}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(4-\frac{1}{2^{2J+2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\text{{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\quad\quad,\quad\quad\left\|\Pi_{\infty}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\|\text{{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}{2^{J+1}}

In particular, letting
J\to\infty
,
P^{T}\text{{{c}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
P^{T}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a bounded linear operator.

Proof.

From the structure of
P
in Theorem
2.1
and the block representation of
c
, we have

\left(P^{T}\text{{c}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+2}}H_{2^{j}}^{T}(\Pi_{2^{j}}\text{{c}})+\sum_{q=j+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{\frac{3q}{2}+2}}H_{2^{q}}\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{q}}

(9)

Recalling that
H_{M}=\sqrt{M}HT_{M}
and
HT_{M}
is a unitary matrix, we can bound the above term by

\displaystyle\left\|\left(P^{T}\text{{c}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{j+2}}\left\|HT_{2^{j}}^{T}(\Pi_{2^{j}}\text{{c}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}+\sum_{q=j+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{q+2}}\left\|HT_{2^{q}}\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{q}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\text{{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{j+2}}+\sum_{q=j+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{q+2}}\left\|\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{q}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\|\text{{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{j+1}}

(10)

The only term left is
\left(P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{1}
. We can bound it with

\displaystyle\left|\left(P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2}c_{1}-\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{\frac{3q}{2}+2}}H_{2^{q}}\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{q}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|c_{1}\right|+\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{q+2}}\left\|HT_{2^{q}}\text{{c}}^{*}_{2^{q}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left\|\text{{c}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(11)

Thus, the norm of
\Pi_{M}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}
can be estimated by

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{M}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left(P^{T}\text{{c}}\right)_{1}\right|^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{J}\left\|\left(P^{T}\text{{c}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\left(4-\frac{1}{2^{2J+2}}\right)\frac{\|\text{{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}{3}

Analogously, the norm of
\Pi_{\infty}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}P^{T}\text{{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\left\|\left(P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\frac{\|\text{{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}{2^{2j+2}}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\|\text{{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}{2^{2J+2}}

∎

3
The radii polynomial approach

In this section, we introduce the radii polynomial
[
5
,
7
,
27
,
13
,
11
,
6
]
approach for rigorous computation. Consider an initial value problem

\begin{cases}\dot{u}=f(u,t)\\
u(0)=u_{0}\end{cases}

(12)

and suppose we find a numerical, approximate solution
\bar{u}(t)
. Our aim is to prove the existence of a true solution
\tilde{u}(t)
in some neighborhood of
\bar{u}(t)
. This is done using the radii polynomial method.

For our work, suppose that
\dot{u}(t)\in L^{2}([0,1])
. Then, we can write
\dot{u}
and
u
using the Haar wavelet and its integral as

\dot{u}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}\psi_{i}(t)\quad,\quad u(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}w_{i}(t)+u_{0}

(13)

Substituting back into the differential equation (
12
) and taking the Haar transform,

F(\text{{c}}):=\text{{c}}-\mathcal{H}\left(f\left(t,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}w_{i}(t)+u_{0}\right)\right)=0

(14)

Thus, we have a map
F:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that finding a solution of the differential equation implies finding a zero of
F
. Conversely, due to the uniqueness of the wavelet series that represents
\dot{u}
— and consequently of the series that represents
u(t)
— finding a zero of
F
is equivalent to finding a solution to (
12
).

Now we recast the problem of finding the zeros of
F
to finding a fixed point of a map
T:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
near the numerical solution
\bar{u}
. This is done by showing that
T
is a contraction near
\bar{u}
. First, define the operator
A:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

A\text{{x}}=A_{M}\Pi_{M}\text{{x}}+\Pi_{\infty}\text{{x}},

where
A_{M}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a finite-dimensional, computational approximation for the inverse
D(\Pi_{M}F(\bar{\text{{{x}}}}))^{-1}
, with
\bar{\text{{{x}}}}
the solution numerically obtained. Then we can define the fixed-point map
T:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by

T(\text{{x}}):=\text{{x}}-AF(\text{{x}})=(\Pi_{M}-A_{M}\Pi_{M}F)(\text{{x}})+\Pi_{\infty}(\text{{x}}-F(\text{{x}}))

(15)

Its derivative, which is used for the radii polynomial method, is given by

DT(\text{{x}})=\Pi_{M}-A_{M}\Pi_{M}DF(\text{{x}})+\Pi_{\infty}(I-DF(\text{{x}}))

(16)

Remark 3.1
.

Since many of the matrices in this work are block matrices, one can compute their inverse as

\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{-1}+A^{-1}B(D-CA^{-1}B)^{-1}CA^{-1}&-A^{-1}B(D-CA^{-1}B)^{-1}\\
-(D-CA^{-1}B)^{-1}CA^{-1}&(D-CA^{-1}B)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

provided that the matrices
A
and
D-CA^{-1}B
are invertible. Calculating the inverse with this formula can be faster than directly inverting the full matrix
D(\Pi_{M}F(\bar{\text{{{x}}}}))
.

Notation
.

In order to help visualize the operators and reduce clutter in notation, we employ the following “block matrix” notation for an operator
C:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
whenever it is convenient:

C\text{{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{M}&C_{M,\infty}\\
C_{\infty,M}&C_{\infty}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{M}\text{{x}}\\
\Pi_{\infty}\text{{x}}\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

We refer to this as the
finite-infinite decomposition
.

Intuitively, the first term of (
15
) is a Newton-like map for the finite terms we computed numerically. In the second term, we hope the “tail” of
F
will contract to zero by itself — which is what happens with quadratic nonlinearities. This is all motivated by the fact that, as
M
increases, the new elements of the matrix
P_{M}
become smaller.

We now formally prove that a fixed point of
T
corresponds to a zero of
F
:

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose the map
T
as defined above in (
15
) is a contraction in some closed neighborhood of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
T
has a unique fixed point
\tilde{\text{{{c}}}}
in that neighborhood. Moreover,
\tilde{\text{{{c}}}}
is a fixed point of
T
if and only if it is a zero of
F
as defined earlier in (
14
).

Proof.

Since
T
is a contraction in a closed neighborhood of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the Banach Fixed Point Theorem guarantees that it has a fixed point
\tilde{\text{{c}}}
in the same neighborhood. Also, if
\tilde{\text{{c}}}
is a zero of
F
, then a straightforward calculation shows that it is a fixed point of
T
.

It remains to prove that the fixed point
\tilde{\text{{c}}}
is a zero of
F
. By (
15
)

T(\tilde{\text{{c}}})-\tilde{\text{{c}}}=0=A_{M}\Pi_{M}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})+\Pi_{\infty}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}}).

Since
A_{M}\Pi_{M}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})\in\Pi_{M}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{\infty}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})=0\quad,\quad A_{M}\Pi_{M}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})=0

and since
A_{M}
is invertible, then
\Pi_{M}F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})=0
as well. Thus
F(\tilde{\text{{c}}})=0
.
∎

To prove that
T
is actually a contraction near of our numerical solution
\bar{\text{{c}}}
, we use the radii polynomials. First, we define the closed neighborhood

\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r)}=\left\{\text{{y}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\|\Pi_{M}(\text{{y}}-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|\leq\omega r\text{ and }\|\Pi_{\infty}(\text{{y}}-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|\leq(1-\omega)r\right\}

(18)

in which
T
will be a contraction.
\omega\in(0,1)
is a “trade-off parameter”: we can loosen the radius in the infinite part, at the cost of tightening the radius in the finite part, and vice-versa. For the next calculations, we assume
\omega
is fixed, though in practice it is chosen later.

Next, we need bounds
Y_{M}
and
Y_{\infty}
, and polynomials
Z_{M}(r)
and
Z_{\infty}(r)
such that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{M}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq Y_{M}

(19)

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\infty}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq Y_{\infty}

(20)

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{B(r)}}\|\Pi_{M}(DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})x_{2})\|\leq Z_{M}(r)r

(21)

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{B(r)}}\|\Pi_{\infty}(DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})x_{2})\|\leq Z_{\infty}(r)r

(22)

Then, we can define the radii polynomials as

\displaystyle p_{M}(r)

\displaystyle:=Z_{M}(r)r-\omega r+Y_{M}\quad,\quad p_{\infty}(r)

\displaystyle:=Z_{\infty}(r)r-(1-\omega)r+Y_{\infty}

(23)

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the radii polynomials as defined in (
23
). If there exists an
r_{0}>0
such that
p_{M}(r_{0})<0
and
p_{\infty}(r_{0})<0
, then there exists a unique
\tilde{\text{{{c}}}}\in B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{{c}}}},r_{0})
such that
T(\tilde{\text{{{c}}}})=\tilde{\text{{{c}}}}
.

Proof.

Due to the Banach Fixed Point Theorem, we only need to prove that
T\left(\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}\right)\subseteq\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
and that
T
is a contraction when restricted to
\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
.

We first prove that
T
restricted to
\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
is a contraction. If
y_{1},y_{2}\in\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
, then by the Mean Value Theorem

\displaystyle\|T(y_{1})-T(y_{2})\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}}\|DT(x)\|\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\sup_{x_{1}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}}\|DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})\|\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}

Hence, we must show that
\|DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})\|<1
for
x_{1}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{1}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}}\|DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}}\|DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})x_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\leq\frac{1}{r_{0}}\bigg(\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}}\|\Pi_{M}DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})x_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{B(r_{0})}}\|\Pi_{\infty}DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+x_{1})x_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\leq Z_{M}(r_{0})+Z_{\infty}(r_{0})

So we have

\|T(y_{1})-T(y_{2})\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq(Z_{M}(r_{0})+Z_{\infty}(r_{0}))\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}}

But since
p_{M}(r_{0})<0
and
p_{\infty}(r_{0})<0
,

\displaystyle Z_{M}(r_{0})r_{0}+Z_{\infty}(r_{0})r_{0}-r_{0}

\displaystyle\leq(Z_{M}(r_{0})r_{0}-\omega r_{0}+Y_{M})+(Z_{\infty}(r_{0})r_{0}-(1-\omega)r_{0}+Y_{\infty})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=p_{M}(r_{0})+p_{\infty}(r_{0})<0

Thus
Z_{M}(r_{0})+Z_{\infty}(r_{0})<1
, and
T
restricted to
\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
is a contraction.

Now we must prove that
T\left(\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}\right)\subseteq\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
. If
y\in\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
, then

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{M}(T(y)-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Pi_{M}(T(y)-T(\bar{\text{{c}}}))\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|\Pi_{M}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Z_{M}(r_{0})\|y-\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}+Y_{M}\leq Z_{M}(r_{0})r_{0}+Y_{M}<\omega r_{0}

and similarly for
\Pi_{\infty}(T(y)-\bar{\text{{c}}})

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\infty}(T(y)-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Pi_{\infty}(T(y)-T(\bar{\text{{c}}}))\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|\Pi_{\infty}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Z_{\infty}(r_{0})\|y-\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}+Y_{\infty}\leq Z_{\infty}(r_{0})r_{0}+Y_{\infty}<(1-\omega)r_{0}

and hence
T(y)\in\overline{B_{\omega}(\bar{\text{{c}}},r_{0})}
.
∎

Thus, if the radii polynomial method is successful in finding an
r_{0}
, then the solution
\bar{\text{{c}}}
found by the numerical method is “close” to the wavelet coefficients of true solution
\tilde{\text{{c}}}
in the
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
sense, that is,
\|\bar{\text{{c}}}-\tilde{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq r_{0}
. Or equivalently, the numerical approximation
\bar{u}(t)=\bar{\text{{c}}}^{T}\text{{h}}(t)
is “close” to the true solution
\tilde{u}(t)
in the
L^{2}([0,1])
sense, that is,
\|\bar{u}-\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq r_{0}
.

4
Nonlinear terms

In this section we study quadratic nonlinearities in more depth. This may seem restrictive, but there are many interesting systems involving those, such as the Lorenz system. Furthermore, we believe that estimates for higher nonlinearities can be computed with similar techniques.

Consider two functions
u
and
v
such that
\dot{u},\dot{v}\in L^{2}([0,1])
. Their expansions into Haar wavelet integrals as in (
13
) are

\displaystyle u(t)=u_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}w_{i}(t)=u_{0}+\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{c}}\ ,\ v(t)=v_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}w_{i}(t)=v_{0}+\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{d}}

Thus, considering the product
u(t)v(t)
, we have

\displaystyle u(t)v(t)=u_{0}v_{0}+u_{0}\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{d}}+v_{0}\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{c}}+(W(t))(\text{{c}},\text{{d}})

where, for
t\in[0,1]
,
(W(t))(\text{{c}},\text{{d}}):=\text{{c}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{d}}
. A crucial observation is that, for any given
t\in[0,1]
,
W(t)

is a symmetric bilinear form
.

The next theorem shows that
W(t)(\text{{c}},\text{{d}})\in L^{2}([0,1])
for any pair
\text{{c}},\text{{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which allows us to calculate its Haar transform and use the radii polynomial methods developed in Section
3
. However, its proof is lengthy and will be left to
A
for clarity.

Theorem 4.1
.

The bilinear form
\left(W(\text{{{c}}},\text{{{d}}})\right)(t):=\text{{{c}}}^{T}\text{{{w}}}(t)\text{{{w}(t)}}\text{{{d}}}
is bounded in
L^{2}([0,1])
for all
\text{{{c}}},\text{{{d}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, that is, there exists
C>0
such that

\left\|W(\text{{{c}}},\text{{{d}}})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\|\text{{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{{d}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\text{ for all }\text{{{c}}},\text{{{d}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Additionally, making
\text{{{a}}}:=P^{T}\text{{{c}}}
and
\text{{{b}}}:=P^{T}\text{{{d}}}
, we can write

\text{{{c}}}^{T}\text{{{w}}}(t)\text{{{w}(t)}}\text{{{d}}}=\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\text{{{b}}}+\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)\text{{{b}}}+\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Theta(t)\text{{{b}}}

(24)

where the operator
\Omega(t)
can be recursively defined as

\Omega_{1}(t)=0\quad,\quad\Omega_{2m}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{m}(t)&\Upsilon_{m}(t)\\[7.22743pt]
0_{m\times m}&0_{m\times m}\end{bmatrix}

(25)

with
\Upsilon_{m}(t)
being a
m\times m
matrix defined element-wise as

(\Upsilon_{m})_{i,l}(t)=(H_{m})_{i,l}\psi_{m+l}(t),

and the operator
\Theta(t)
can be represented as an infinite diagonal matrix given by

\Theta(t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\psi_{1}^{2}(t)&&&\\
&\psi_{2}^{2}(t)&&\\
&&\psi_{3}^{2}(t)&\\
&&&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right]

(26)

with zeros omitted for clarity. With the terms defined as above,

\mathcal{H}(\text{{{c}}}^{T}\text{{{w}}}(t)\text{{{w}(t)}}\text{{{d}}})=\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\text{{{b}}})+\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\text{{{b}}})+\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\text{{{b}}})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\text{{{b}}})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}\text{{{a}}})\odot\text{{{b}}}

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)\text{{{b}}})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}\text{{{b}}})\odot\text{{{a}}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\text{{{a}}}^{T}\Theta(t)\text{{{b}}})

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{T}(\text{{{a}}}\odot\text{{{b}}})

where
\tilde{\Omega}^{T}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{T}
are recursively defined as

\begin{aligned} \tilde{\Omega}_{1}=0\quad,\quad\tilde{\Omega}_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Omega}_{m}&H_{m}\\
0_{m}&0_{m}\end{bmatrix}\\
\Gamma_{1}=1\quad,\quad\Gamma_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{m}&0_{m}\\
H_{m}^{T}&0_{m}\end{bmatrix}\end{aligned}\quad,\text{ for }m=2^{j}\text{ and }j=0,1,2,3,...

We now present some estimates required for the radii polynomial method. The full proof for those estimates are lengthy and left
B
. The main strategy consists in employing both the recursive block structures of the matrices from Theorems
2.1
and
4.1
and the finite-infinite decomposition from (
17
). These estimates provide tighter bounds which increase the likelihood of finding an
r_{0}
which satisfies Theorem
3.1
. We believe that similar estimates may be applied for higher-degree polynomial nonlinearities.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given
\bar{\text{{{c}}}},\bar{\text{{{d}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
M=2^{J+1}
for some
J\geq 0
, and
\bar{\text{{{a}}}}=P^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}}
,
\bar{\text{{{b}}}}=P^{T}\bar{\text{{{d}}}}
, the following estimates are valid:

i)

\Pi_{M}P^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}}=P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}}

ii)

\Pi_{M}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\bar{\text{{{b}}}})=(\tilde{\Omega}_{M}^{T}P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}})\odot(P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{{d}}}})

iii)

\Pi_{M}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}^{T}\Theta(t)\bar{\text{{{b}}}})=\Gamma^{T}_{M}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{M}\odot\bar{\text{{{b}}}}_{M})+\Gamma^{T}_{\infty,M}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{\infty}\odot\bar{\text{{{b}}}}_{\infty})
; moreover,

\left\|\Gamma^{T}_{\infty}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{\infty}\odot\bar{\text{{{b}}}}_{\infty})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\bar{\text{{{d}}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{\left(4-\sqrt{2}\right)2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
\bar{\text{{{c}}}},\bar{\text{{{d}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
M=2^{J+1}
for some
J\geq 0
, and
\bar{\text{{{a}}}}=P^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}}
,
\bar{\text{{{b}}}}=P^{T}\bar{\text{{{d}}}}
, the following estimates are valid:

i)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}P^{T}\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{J+2}}

ii)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\bar{\text{{{b}}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\bar{\text{{{d}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{2J+4}}

iii)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}^{T}\Theta(t)\bar{\text{{{b}}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{21\sqrt{7}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\bar{\text{{{d}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{3J+6}}.

Proposition 4.3
.

Given
\text{{{x}}},\text{{{y}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,

i)

\displaystyle\left\|(P_{\infty,M}^{T}\,\text{{{y}}}_{\infty})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{J+2}}

ii)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}P^{T}\right)_{M,\infty}\text{{{y}}}_{\infty}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\frac{\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{J+3}{2}}}

iii)

\displaystyle\left\|\Gamma_{\infty,M}^{T}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{\infty}\odot\Pi_{\infty}P^{T}\text{{{y}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4-\sqrt{2}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}

iv)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{M}\Pi_{M}\mathcal{H}(\text{{{x}}}^{T}P\,\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{{y}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq K_{1}\|\text{{{x}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{{y}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

v)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{M}\Pi_{M}\mathcal{H}(\text{{{x}}}^{T}P\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{{y}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq K_{2}\|\text{{{x}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{{y}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

where

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{M}\,\text{{diag}}\left(\|(P_{M}^{T})_{i,*}\|_{\ell^{2}}\right)\,\tilde{\Omega}_{M}^{T}P_{M}^{T}\right\|+\frac{\|A_{M}\tilde{\Omega}_{M}^{T}P_{M}^{T}\|}{2^{J+2}}+\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})\|A_{M}P_{M}^{T}\|}{2^{\frac{J+3}{2}}}+\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})\|A_{M}\|}{2^{\frac{3J+7}{2}}}

\displaystyle K_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{M}\Gamma_{M}^{T}\text{{diag}}(\|(P_{M}^{T})_{i,*}\|_{\ell}^{2})P_{M}^{T}\right\|+\frac{\left\|A_{M}\Gamma_{M}^{T}P_{M}^{T}\right\|}{2^{J+1}}+\frac{\left\|A_{M}\Gamma_{M}^{T}\right\|}{2^{2J+4}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\|A_{M}\|}{(4-\sqrt{2})2^{\frac{3J}{2}+4}}

Proposition 4.4
.

Given
\text{{{x}}},\text{{{y}}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the following estimates are valid:

i)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{{c}}}}^{T}\text{{{w}}}(t)\text{{{w}}}^{T}(t)\text{{{y}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq D_{1}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{{c}}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}}}\quad,\quad D_{1}=\frac{1}{8\sqrt{7}}\left(3+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}+\frac{4}{4-\sqrt{2}}\right);

ii)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\text{{{x}}}^{T}\text{{{w}}}(t)\text{{{w}}}^{T}(t)\text{{{y}}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq D_{2}\frac{\left\|\text{{{x}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+2}}\quad,\quad D_{2}=\frac{8+6\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{7}\left(4-\sqrt{2}\right)}
.

5
Examples

In this section we illustrate the implementation of our method by means of three examples: the logistic equation, the logistic equation with a discontinuous forcing term, and the Lorenz system. The method was implemented in MATLAB R2021b using the INTLAB package for interval arithmetic
[
23
]
. The files for these examples are available at
https://github.com/gknakassima/RigComp-HaarWavelet
.

5.1
Logistic equation

As a first simple example we look at the logistic equation

\dot{u}=\lambda u(1-u)\quad,\quad u(0)=u_{0}

(28)

since it has a polynomial nonlinearity and its analytical solution is given by

u(t)=\frac{u_{0}e^{\lambda t}}{1-u_{0}+u_{0}e^{\lambda t}}
. Using the expansions in (
13
), we have the functional equation equivalent to (
28
):

\text{{c}}^{T}\text{{h}}(t)-\lambda\left(\text{{c}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)+u_{0}-u_{0}^{2}-2u_{0}\text{{c}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-(W(t))(\text{{c}},\text{{c}})\right)=0.

(29)

5.1.1
Obtaining a numerical approximation

In order to obtain a finite-dimensional approximation
\bar{\text{{c}}}
of the solution, we first consider a truncated version of our matrix equation. Given a resolution level
J>0
and making
M=2^{J+1}
, we apply the projection
\Pi_{M}
to all sequences of (
29
), obtaining

\text{{c}}_{M}^{T}\text{{h}}_{M}(t)-\lambda\left(\text{{c}}_{M}^{T}\text{{w}}_{M}(t)+u_{0}-u_{0}^{2}-2u_{0}\text{{c}}_{M}^{T}\text{{w}}_{M}(t)-(W_{M}(t))(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M})\right)=0

where, for
\text{{a}}_{M},\text{{b}}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
(W_{M}(t))(\text{{a}}_{M},\text{{b}}_{M}):=\text{{a}}_{M}^{T}\text{{w}}_{M}(t)\text{{w}}_{M}^{T}(t)\text{{b}}_{M}
. Since the equation holds for all
t\in[0,1]
, we sample it at the times
t_{l}=\frac{l-0.5}{2^{J}}
for
l=1,...,M
. Recalling that

H_{M}=[\text{{h}}_{M}(t_{1}),\text{{h}}_{M}(t_{2}),...,\text{{h}}_{M}(t_{l}),...,\text{{h}}_{M}(t_{2^{J+1}})],

we can organize the time samples in matrix form as

\text{{c}}_{M}^{T}H_{M}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)\text{{c}}_{M}^{T}P_{M}H_{M}+\lambda(u_{0}^{2}-u_{0})\text{{e}}^{T}+\lambda(\text{{W}}_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M}))^{T}=0

with
e
and
\text{{W}}_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M})
being
M\times 1
vectors given by

\text{{e}}:=(1,1,...,1)^{T}\quad,\quad\big(\text{{W}}_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M})\big)_{i}:=(W_{M}(t_{i}))(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M}).

By transposing this system and multiplying by
(H_{M}^{T})^{-1}=\frac{1}{M}H_{M}
we finally arrive at the equation to solve numerically:

\text{{c}}_{M}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P_{M}^{T}\text{{c}}_{M}+\frac{\lambda(u_{0}^{2}-u_{0})}{M}H_{M}\text{{e}}+\frac{\lambda}{M}H_{M}\text{{W}}_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M})=0.

(30)

As this is a nonlinear equation, we use Newton’s method. Define
F_{M}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
as

F_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M}):=\text{{c}}_{M}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P_{M}^{T}\text{{c}}_{M}+\frac{\lambda(u_{0}^{2}-u_{0})}{M}H_{M}\text{{e}}+\frac{\lambda}{M}H_{M}\text{{W}}_{M}(\text{{c}}_{M},\text{{c}}_{M})

(31)

Thus, we apply Newton’s method by iteratively calculating

\text{{c}}^{p+1}_{M}=\text{{c}}^{p}_{M}-A_{M}(\text{{c}}^{p}_{M})F_{M}(\text{{c}}^{p}_{M})

(32)

where
A_{M}(\text{{c}}^{p}_{M})
is a numerical approximation for
\Big(DF_{M}(\text{{c}}^{p}_{M})\Big)^{-1}
and
\text{{c}}^{p}_{M}
is the result from the
p
-th iteration.

5.1.2
Estimates for the radii polynomials

Here, we provide the bounds for Theorem
3.1
. The maps
T
and
DT
for the fixed point theorem are given by (
15
) and (
16
), respectively. For this example, the map
F:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given as

F(\text{{x}}):=\lambda(u_{0}^{2}-u_{0})e_{1}+\text{{x}}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P^{T}\text{{x}}+\lambda\mathcal{H}\left(\text{{x}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{x}}\right).

(33)

and its derivative
DF(\text{{x}})
applied to
\text{{y}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given by

(DF(\text{{x}}))\text{{y}}=\text{{y}}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P^{T}\text{{y}}+2\lambda\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{y}}).

(34)

where the last equality comes from the symmetry of the bilinear form.

Before proceeding, it is worth outlining the general strategy for the estimates. We separate the operator matrices according to the finite-infinite decomposition in (
17
). Then, all the finite-dimensional parts are collected together and left for the computer to calculate, while we use the analytic estimates from Section
4
for the infinite parts.

Y_{M}

\lambda\mathcal{H}\left(\bar{\text{{c}}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)
and Proposition

,

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})

\displaystyle=-A_{M}\Pi_{M}\mathcal{H}F(\bar{\text{{c}}})

\displaystyle=-A_{M}\Big[\lambda(u_{0}^{2}-u_{0})\text{{e}}+\bar{\text{{c}}}+\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}+2\lambda\big((\tilde{\Omega}_{M}^{T}P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})\odot(P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\lambda\Gamma^{T}_{M}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{M}\odot\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{M})\Big]-\lambda A_{M}\Pi_{M}\Gamma^{T}_{\infty,M}(\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{\infty}\odot\bar{\text{{{a}}}}_{\infty}),

with

\Pi_{\infty}(T(\bar{\text{{c}}})-\bar{\text{{c}}})=\Pi_{\infty}\left(\lambda(2u_{0}-1)P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}+\lambda\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)\bar{\text{{a}}}+\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)\bar{\text{{a}}}+\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Theta(t)\bar{\text{{a}}}\right).

(36)

•

Z_{M}
: Using the finite-infinite decomposition and the fact that
\bar{\text{{c}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(DT(\bar{\text{{c}}}+\text{{x}}))\text{{y}}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{M}-A_{M}B_{1}\right)\text{{y}}_{M}-A_{M}B_{2}P_{\infty,M}^{T}\text{{y}}_{\infty}-2\lambda A_{M}\text{diag}(P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})\big(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}P^{T}\big)_{M,\infty}\text{{y}}_{\infty}

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle:=\lambda(2u_{0}-1)I_{M}+2\lambda\text{diag}\left(\bar{\text{{c}}}^{T}P_{M}\tilde{\Omega}_{M}\right)+2\lambda\text{diag}(P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})+2\lambda\Gamma_{M}^{T}\text{diag}\left(P_{M}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)

and

5.2
Logistic equation with a discontinuous forcing term

The next example is again the logistic equation, but with a discontinuous forcing term

5.3
Lorenz system

The Lorenz system is given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=\sigma(y-x)

(47)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=x(\rho-z)-y

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=xy-\beta z

where
\sigma
,
\rho
and
\beta
are positive parameters, usually taken as
\sigma=10
,
\beta=\frac{8}{3}
and
\rho=28
. This is a well-studied system, and with these parameters the system exhibits chaotic behavior with a strange attractor.

Since this is a system of equations, we must first some of our definitions in order to apply our method. First, we define the spaces
X_{s}:=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
X_{f}:=L^{2}([0,1])\times L^{2}([0,1])\times L^{2}([0,1])
, and endow them with the norms

\displaystyle\|(\text{{c}}_{1},\text{{c}}_{2},\text{{c}}_{3})\|_{X_{s}}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\|\text{{c}}_{1}\|_{\ell^{2}},\|\text{{c}}_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}},\|\text{{c}}_{3}\|_{\ell^{2}}\right\}

(48)

\displaystyle\|(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})\|_{X_{f}}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}},\|f_{2}\|_{L^{2}},\|f_{3}\|_{L^{2}}\right\}

With these norms,
X_{s}
and
X_{f}
are still Banach spaces; though they are no longer Hilbert spaces, the methods of Section
3
are still applicable. Also, an operator
A:X_{s}\to X_{s}
can be expressed using block matrix notation as

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&A_{1,2}&A_{1,3}\\
A_{2,1}&A_{2,2}&A_{2,3}\\
A_{3,1}&A_{3,2}&A_{3,3}\end{bmatrix}

where
A_{i,j}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
i,j=1,2,3
. If those are bounded,
A
is bounded and

\|A\|_{B(X_{s})}=\max_{1\leq i\leq 3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\|A_{i,j}\|_{B(\ell^{2})}.

(49)

Similar notation will be used when
A_{i,j}\in B(\mathbb{R}^{M})
. Lastly, we make a small abuse of notation and extend the notation for the operators in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such as the projections
\Pi_{M}
and
\Pi_{\infty}
to
X_{s}
by applying them element-wise:

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(\text{{c}}_{1},\text{{c}}_{2},\text{{c}}_{3})

\displaystyle:=(\Pi_{M}\text{{c}}_{1},\Pi_{M}\text{{c}}_{2},\Pi_{M}\text{{c}}_{3})

\displaystyle\Pi_{\infty}(\text{{c}}_{1},\text{{c}}_{2},\text{{c}}_{3})

\displaystyle:=(\Pi_{\infty}\text{{c}}_{1},\Pi_{\infty}\text{{c}}_{2},\Pi_{\infty}\text{{c}}_{3}).

Applying the same methods used to obtain (
29
) to each equation in (
47
), we obtain the functional equation

\displaystyle\text{{c}}_{x}^{T}\text{{h}}(t)-\sigma\text{{c}}_{y}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)+\sigma\text{{c}}_{x}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-\sigma(y_{0}-x_{0})=0

(50)

\displaystyle\text{{c}}_{y}^{T}\text{{h}}(t)+x_{0}\text{{c}}_{z}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-(\rho-z_{0})\text{{c}}_{x}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)+\text{{c}}_{y}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-(x_{0}(\rho-z_{0})-y_{0})+W(t)(\text{{c}}_{x},\text{{c}}_{z})=0

\displaystyle\text{{c}}_{z}^{T}\text{{h}}(t)-y_{0}\text{{c}}_{x}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-x_{0}\text{{c}}_{y}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)+\beta\text{{c}}_{z}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)-(x_{0}y_{0}-\beta z_{0})-W(t)(\text{{c}}_{x},\text{{c}}_{y})=0

where
\text{{c}}_{x}=\mathcal{H}(\dot{x})
,
\text{{c}}_{y}=\mathcal{H}(\dot{y})
and
\text{{c}}_{z}=\mathcal{H}(\dot{z})
.

Using the same techniques used to obtain (

6
Conclusions and future work

We developed a radii polynomial method using the Haar wavelet approach for differential equations, and illustrated the method by applying it to three differential equations. One advantage of our method over previous methods based on the radii polynomials approach is that, due to the use of the Haar wavelets, our method does not require the solutions to be smooth.

In the future we plan to develop the estimates for higher-order derivatives. While a higher-order differential equation can be transformed into a system of first-order equations, this increases the size of the matrices. Thus it might be interesting to use operators that directly represent higher-order derivatives. While some of those have already been used for usual numerical methods, we need to compute the estimates needed for our radii polynomial method.

Furthermore, we only presented the estimates needed for the radii polynomials for quadratic nonlinearities, since the main goal of this paper is to present the general method and illustrate how to compute the estimates and apply the method. In the future we plan to extend these estimates to include higher-order polynomial nonlinearities, as this would greatly expand the applicability of our method.

Lastly, we believe this method can be a basis to build other methods using similar techniques, such as continuation methods.

\left(\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right)_{i}=(\bar{\text{{c}}}^{T}P_{2^{j}}H_{2^{j}})_{i}(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{i}
. Therefore, applying the block decomposition to
\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
and some previously calculated estimates from (
2
) and (
58
),

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(H_{2^{j}}^{T}P_{2^{j}}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\odot\left(P^{T}\text{{y}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left\|\text{diag}\left((P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{j}}^{*}\right)\right\|\,\left\|\left(H_{2^{j}}^{T}P_{2^{j}}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\max_{2^{j}+1\leq i\leq 2^{j+1}}\left|\left(P^{T}\text{{y}}\right)_{i}\right|\,\left\|\left(H_{2^{j}}^{T}P_{2^{j}}^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+3}}

and thus

\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|=\sqrt{\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\left\|\left(\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{7}}\frac{\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}.

•

\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
: Since
\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}}=(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})^{T}=(P^{T}\text{{y}})^{T}\Omega(t)\bar{\text{{a}}}
, then using the estimate from (
57
),

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\text{diag}\left((P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})_{2^{j}}^{*}\right)\big(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}P^{T}\text{{y}}\big)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{2^{j}+1\leq i\leq 2^{j+1}}\left|(P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})_{i}\right|\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}\right)\frac{\left\|\text{{{y}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{j}{2}}}\leq\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}\right)\frac{\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+2}},

and the bound for
\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
becomes

\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{7}}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}\right)\frac{\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+2}}.

•

\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
: Using the block structure of
\Gamma^{T}
from (
54
) and the estimates (
58
) and (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\Gamma^{T}\left(\bar{\text{{a}}}\odot P^{T}\text{{y}}\right)\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}(H_{2^{p}})_{2^{j}+1:2^{j+1},*}\left(\bar{\text{{a}}}_{2^{p}}^{*}\odot(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}\left\|(H_{2^{p}})_{2^{j}+1:2^{j+1},*}\right\|\left\|\text{diag}(\bar{\text{{a}}}_{2^{p}}^{*})\right\|\left\|(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}\left\|H_{2^{p}}\right\|\max_{2^{p}+1\leq i\leq 2^{p+1}}\left|(P^{T}\bar{\text{{c}}})_{i}\right|\left\|(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}2^{\frac{p}{2}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{p+2}}\frac{\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{p+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4-\sqrt{2}}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+3}},

and thus

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{a}}}^{T}\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{7}(4-\sqrt{2})}\frac{\left\|\bar{\text{{c}}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}.

The result follows from summing the estimates.

ii)

We proceed in the same way as we did for
\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\text{{c}}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{y}})
, that is, separate it in three parts

\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\text{{w}}(t)\text{{w}}^{T}(t)\text{{y}})=\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})+\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})+\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})

and estimate each one.

•

\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
:
Applying the block decomposition to the expression of
\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
and the estimates from (
2
) and (
58
),

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}P^{T}\text{{x}})_{2^{j}}^{*}\odot(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left\|\text{diag}\left(P^{T}\text{{y}}\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|\left\|(\tilde{\Omega}^{T}P^{T}\text{{x}})_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{j+1}}\left(3+2\sqrt{2}\right)\frac{\|\text{{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{j}{2}+2}}=\frac{\left(3+2\sqrt{2}\right)}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+3}}\|\text{{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

and then

\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\left(3+2\sqrt{2}\right)}{\sqrt{7}}\frac{\|\text{{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}

•

\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
: Since
\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}}=(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})^{T}=\text{{y}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{x}}
, repeating the same process for the previous item, we also have

\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Omega^{T}(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\left(3+2\sqrt{2}\right)}{\sqrt{7}}\frac{\|\text{{x}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\text{{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+3}}

•

\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})
: Using the block decomposition and the block structure of
\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\Gamma^{T}\left(P^{T}\text{{x}}\odot P^{T}\text{{y}}\right)\right)_{2^{j}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}(H_{2^{p}})_{2^{j}+1:2^{j+1},*}\left((P^{T}\text{{x}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\odot(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}\left\|(H_{2^{p}})\right\|\,\left\|\text{diag}((P^{T}\text{{x}})_{2^{p}}^{*})\right\|\left\|(P^{T}\text{{y}})_{2^{p}}^{*}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=j+1}^{\infty}2^{\frac{p}{2}}\frac{\left\|\text{{x}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{p+1}}\frac{\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{p+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4-\sqrt{2}}\frac{\left\|\text{{x}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3j}{2}+2}}

and thus

\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{H}(\text{{x}}^{T}\Theta(t)P^{T}\text{{y}})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{7}\left(4-\sqrt{2}\right)}\frac{\left\|\text{{x}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\text{{y}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}}{2^{\frac{3J}{2}+2}}\\

Putting together the estimates, we have the desired result.
