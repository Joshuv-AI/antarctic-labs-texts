Geometrical aspects of entropy production in stochastic thermodynamics based on Wasserstein distance
Muka Nakazato, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
We study a relationship between optimal transport theory and stochastic thermodynamics for the Fokker-Planck equation. We show that the lower bound on the entropy production is the action measured by the path length of the L 2 L^{2} -Wasserstein distance. Because the L 2 L^{2} -Wasserstein distance is a geometric measure of optimal transport theory, our result implies a geometric interpretation of the entropy production. Based on this interpretation, we obtain a thermodynamic trade-off relation between transition time and the entropy production. This thermodynamic trade-off relation is regarded as a thermodynamic speed limit which gives a tighter bound of the entropy production. We also discuss stochastic thermodynamics for the subsystem and derive a lower bound on the partial entropy production as a generalization of the second law of information thermodynamics. Our formalism also provides a geometric picture of the optimal protocol to minimize the entropy production. We illustrate these results by the optimal stochastic heat engine and show a geometrical bound of the efficiency.

;

;
yoshimura2020information
;
gupta2020tighter

ito2018stochastic1
;
ito2020stochastic
;
dechant2018multidimensional
;
hasegawa2019uncertainty

In parallel with information geometry, optimal transport theory can explain the optimal control of thermodynamic systems and thermodynamic trade-off relations. Optimal transport theory has been used in stochastic thermodynamics to find a heat minimization protocol

This paper shows a novel relationship between optimal transport theory and stochastic thermodynamics for the Fokker-Planck equation. Based on a connection between the entropy production rate and the

II
Review on stochastic thermodynamics and optimal transport theory

II.1
Stochastic thermodynamics for Fokker-Planck equation

In this paper,
we consider the probability distribution
p_{t}({\bf x})

of a particle in a Euclid
d
-dimensional position
{\bf x}\in X(=\mathbb{R}^{d})
at time
t
. The time evolution of
p_{t}({\bf x})
is described by the following Fokker-Planck equation
for a particle driven by a potential
V_{t}(x)
with mobility
\mu
attached to a heat bath at temperature
T
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bf x})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x})),

(1)

\displaystyle{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x}):

\displaystyle=-\mu\nabla[V_{t}({\bf x})+T\ln p_{t}({\bf x})],

(2)

where
\nabla
is the del oparator, and
{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})
is a quantity called the mean local velocity. We here set the Boltzmann constant to unity
k_{\rm B}=1
. As a continuity equation, the mean local velocity
{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})
is regarded as the velocity field.
In stochastic thermodynamics
sekimoto2010stochastic
,
the internal energy
U
,
the extracted work
dW
,
the heat received from the heat bath
dQ
,
and the entropy of the system
S_{\mathrm{sys}}
at time
t
are defined as follows,

\displaystyle U:

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\ V_{t}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x}),

(3)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{sys}}:

\displaystyle=-\int d{\bf x}\ p_{t}({\bf x})\ln p_{t}({\bf x}),

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{dW}{dt}:

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\ \frac{\partial V_{t}({\bf x})}{\partial t}p_{t}({\bf x}),

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dt}:

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\ V_{t}({\bf x})\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bf x})}{\partial t}.

(6)

By definition, the heat
dQ
satisfies the first law of thermodynamics
dU/dt=dW/dt+dQ/dt
. From these definitions (
3
)-(
6
), the entropy production rate at time
t

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}:=\frac{dS_{\mathrm{sys}}}{dt}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{dQ}{dt}

(7)

is calculated as

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}\left[-\mu V_{t}({\bf x})-\mu T\ln p_{t}({\bf x})\right]\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bf x})}{\partial t}

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}\ \|{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})\|^{2}p_{t}({\bf x}),

(9)

where we used Eq. (
1
) and the normalization of the probability
(d/dt)[\int d{\bf x}p_{t}({\bf x})]=0
, and we assumed that
p_{t}({\bf x})
vanishes at infinity. The symbol
||{\bm{\nu}}_{t}||^{2}:={\bm{\nu}}_{t}\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}
indicates the square of
L^{2}
norm. Thus, the entropy production rate
\sigma_{t}
is given by the expected value of the square of the mean local velocity divided by the factor
\mu T
. The entropy production from time
t=0
to time
t=\tau
is defined as the time integral of the entropy production rate,

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle:=\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\sigma_{t}.

(10)

We can easily check the non-negativity of the entropy production
\Sigma\geq 0
, which implies the second law of thermodynamics.

II.2
Optimal transport theory and
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance

Next, we discuss the geometric measure of optimal transport called the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
Villani2008Optiaml
.
We consider the cost function
c({\bf x},{\bf y})
of transporting a single particle at the point
{\bf x}\in X
to the point
{\bf y}\in X
. We first introduce the Monge-Kantrovich minimization problem
Kantrovich2006Transport
, which quantifies a difference between two probability distributions
p({\bf x})
and
q({\bf y})
. The optimal transport cost for
c({\bf x},{\bf y})
between
p({\bf x})
and
q({\bf y})
is defined as

\displaystyle C(p,q):=\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\int d{\bf x}d{\bf y}\ c({\bf x},{\bf y})\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y}),

(11)

where the lower bound is taken over the entire set
\mathcal{P}(p,q)
of joint probability distributions
\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y})

on
X\times X
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(p,q):=\{\Pi|

\displaystyle p({\bf x})=\int d{\bf y}\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y}),

\displaystyle q({\bf y})=\int d{\bf x}\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y}),\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y})\geq 0\},

(12)

where marginal distributions of
\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y})
in the set
\mathcal{P}(p,q)
are given by
p({\bf x})
and
q({\bf y})
.
Therefore, the optimal transport cost gives a minimum value of the expected value of the cost
c({\bf x},{\bf y})
for the joint distribution
\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y})
. We call the value of
\Pi
that minimizes the expected value of the distance as the optimal transport plan
\Pi^{\ast}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\ast}({\bf x},{\bf y}):=\mathrm{argmin}_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\int d{\bf x}d{\bf y}\ c({\bf x},{\bf y})\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y}).

(13)

In general, the Monge-Kantrovich minimization problem is hard to be solved analytically. However, if we consider the
L^{2}
-norm as the optimal transport cost on the Euclidean space, the Monge-Kantrovich minimization problem can be solved with few restrictions
Villani2008Optiaml
. This optimal transport cost leads to the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance which plays an important role in this paper.

The
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}(p,q)
is introduced by the square root of the optimal transport cost for the cost function which is the square of the
L^{2}
-norm. Explicitly, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}(p,q)
between
p
and
q
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p,q)^{2}

\displaystyle:=\min_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\int d{\bf x}d{\bf y}\ \|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}\Pi({\bf x},{\bf y})

\displaystyle=C(p,q),

(14)

where
C(p,q)
is the optimal transport cost for the cost function
c({\bf x},{\bf y})=\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}
.
The
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is well defined
Villani2008Optiaml
if two probability distributions
p
and
q
satisfy

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}p({\bf x})\|{\bf x}\|^{2}<\infty,\int d{\bf y}q({\bf y})\|{\bf y}\|^{2}<\infty,

(15)

which is only an assumption for two distributions
p
and
q
to define
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. We assume this condition Eq. (
15
) throughout the paper.

Figure 1:
Schematic of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. We consider optimal transport from the probability distribution
p(\bf{x})
to the probability distribution
q(\bf{y})
. The length of the green arrow shows the optimal transportation distance
||{\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x})||
, and the square of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is given by the expected value of the square of its optimal transportation distance.

Furthermore, it is known that there exists a map
\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x})
such that
\Pi^{\ast}({\bf x},{\bf y})=p({\bf x})\delta({\bf y}-\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x}))
for the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
c({\bf x},{\bf y})=\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\delta({\bf x})
is the delta function
Villani2008Optiaml
.
This map
\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}
is called the optimal transport map from
p
to
q
.
Using the fact that the marginal distributions of
\Pi^{\ast}({\bf x},{\bf y})
are
p({\bf x})
and
q({\bf y})
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\int d{\bf y}f({\bf y})q({\bf y})

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\int d{\bf y}f({\bf y})\Pi^{\ast}({\bf x},{\bf y})

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}f(\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x}))p({\bf x})

(16)

for any differential and measurable function
f({\bf x})
. If we consider the change of variables
{\bf y}=\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x})
and
d{\bf y}=d{\bf x}|\det(\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p\rightarrow q}({\bf x}))|
, we obtain the Jacobian equation
Villani2008Optiaml

\displaystyle p({\bf x})=q(\mathcal{T}_{p\rightarrow q}({\bf x}))|\det(\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p\rightarrow q}({\bf x}))|,

(17)

where
|\det(\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p \to q}({\bf x}))|
denotes the determinant of the Jacobian matrix
\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}
at
{\bf x}
. By using the optimal transport map, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is calculated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p,q)^{2}=\int d{\bf x}\ \|{\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x})\|^{2}p({\bf x}).

(18)

Thus, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance can be regarded as the expected value of
||{\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{p\to q}({\bf x})||^{2}
(see Fig.
1
).

We briefly introduce the Benamou-Brenier formula
Benamou2000Monge
, which is related to a relation between the entropy production and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance in this paper. If dynamics of the probability
q_{t}({\bf x})
at time
t
are driven by the continuity equation with the velocity field
{\bf v}_{t}({\bf x})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}({\bf x})}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot({\bf v}_{t}({\bf x})q_{t}({\bf x})),

(19)

the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance gives the lower bound on the expected value of the square of the velocity field,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(q_{0},q_{\tau})^{2}\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\int d{\bf x}||{\bf v}_{t}({\bf x})||^{2}q_{t}({\bf x}).

(20)

where we consider the time integral from time
t=0
to time
t=\tau
. Because the velocity field of the Fokker-Planck equation is the mean local velocity, we obtain a relation between the entropy production rate and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance as discussed in the next section.

III
Entropy production and
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance

III.1
Relation between Wasserstein distance and entropy production rate

In this section, we discuss a relation between the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance and the entropy production rate, which is a main result in this paper. We set that dynamics of the probability distribution
p_{t}({\bf x})
are described by the Fokker-Planck equation (
1
).
We define the path length on the probability simplex measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance from time
t=0
to time
t=\tau
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tau}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow+0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{W}(p_{k\Delta t},p_{(k+1)\Delta t}),

(21)

where
n
is a positive integer satisfying
n\Delta t\leq\tau\leq(n+1)\Delta t
. The entropy production rate is bounded by

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\mu T}\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2},

(22)

which is a main result in this paper. This main result is consistent with the optimal transport theory for an infinitesimal time transition in Ref.
Villani2000generalization
. This equation gives a relation between the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance and the entropy production rate for the Fokker-Planck equation. In terms of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance, the quantity
(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt)^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow+0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}.

(23)

Thus, this inequality can be regarded as the Benamou-Brenier formula
Benamou2000Monge
for the short time
\tau=\Delta t
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})^{2}\leq\Delta t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\int d{\bf x}\ \|{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})\|^{2}p_{t}({\bf x})+O(\Delta t^{3}).

(24)

We next discuss the situation that the equality in Eq. (
22
) holds. We introduce a non-negative term
\sigma_{t}^{\rm rot}
defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{\rm rot}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}-\frac{1}{\mu T}\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}\geq 0,

(25)

and discuss the situation
\sigma_{t}^{\rm rot}=0
. We consider the Taylor expansion of the optimal transport map
\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x})
up to the order
\Delta t
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x})={\bf x}+\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

(26)

where
\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})
is the first order of the Taylor coefficient. From Eq. (
18
), we obtain an expression of
\left(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt\right)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\ \|\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\|^{2}p_{t}({\bf x}).

(27)

Thus, if the mean local velocity gives an optimal transport map, that is
{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})=\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})
, the equality holds and
\sigma_{t}^{\rm rot}=0
.

Next, we consider the difference between
\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})
and
{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})
. By substituting
(p_{t},p_{t+\Delta t})
into
(p,q)
, the Jacobian equation in Eq. (
17
) is given by

\displaystyle p_{t}({\bf x})=p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))|\det(\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p_t\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))|.

(28)

We calculate the Taylor expansions of the determinant up to the order
\Delta t
as follows

\displaystyle|\det(\nabla\mathcal{T}_{p_t\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))|=1+\nabla\cdot\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}).

(29)

From the Fokker-Planck equation (
1
), we also obtain

\displaystyle p_{t+\Delta t}({\bf x})=p_{t}({\bf x})-\nabla\cdot(\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x}))\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

(30)

which is the discretized version of the Fokker-Planck equation for the short time
\Delta t
.
By inserting Eqs. (
26
), (
29
) and (
30
) into Eq. (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\nabla\cdot[(\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})-\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x}))p_{t}({\bf x})]\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}).

By considering the first-order terms of
\Delta t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot[(\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})-\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x}))p_{t}({\bf x})]=0.

(31)

In case of
d=3
, this equation implies the existence of a vector potential
\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x})
because of Helmholtz’s decomposition such as

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x})=\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x})+\nabla\times\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x}).

(32)

Thus, this vector potential
\bm{A}_{t}
quantifies a difference between optimal transport plan and the time evolution of the Fokker-Planck equation from time
t
to time
t+\Delta t
. In the general case of
d\neq 3
or the case of non-Euclidean space, we may consider the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition to obtain an expression of
\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})p_{t}({\bf x})
.

To find the expression of
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
, we use the formula for the time derivative of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
Villani2008Optiaml
. The following formula

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}}{2}\right)\right|_{s=0}\!=\!-\!\int d{\bf x}({\bf x}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))\!\cdot\!{\bm{\nu}}_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))p({\bf x})

(33)

holds for any probability distribution
p({\bf x})
, where we used the notation
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{p\to p_{t}}
. The proof of this formula (
33
) is shown in Appendix A. By applying the Taylor expansion Eq. (
26
) to the formula Eq. (
33
) for
(p,p_{t+s})=(p_{t},p_{t+\Delta t+s})
, we obtain the following equation,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t},p_{t+\Delta t+s})^{2}}{2}\right)\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\int d{\bf x}[\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))]p_{t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\!\int\!d{\bf x}[\bm{a}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))\!\cdot\!{\bm{\nu}}_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{p_{t}\rightarrow p_{t+\Delta t}}({\bf x}))]p_{t}({\bf x})\!+\!\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\int d{\bf y}[\bm{a}_{1}({\bf y})\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf y})]p_{t+\Delta t}({\bf y})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\int d{\bf x}[\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})]p_{t}({\bf x})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

(34)

where we used Eq. (
16
). From the definition of the path length Eq. (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p_{t+s},p_{t})=\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}s+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}),

(35)

for small
s
.
Therefore, we also obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t},p_{t+\Delta t+s})^{2}}{2}\right)\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow+0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t+s},p_{t})-\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})}{s}\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t+\Delta t}}{dt}\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}).

(36)

By comparing Eq. (
36
) with Eq. (
34
), we obtain another expression of
\left(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt\right)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}[\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})]p_{t}({\bf x}).

(37)

We also obtain expressions of
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}[({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x}))\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})]p_{t}({\bf x}),

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}||{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})||^{2}p_{t}({\bf x}),

(39)

where we compared Eq. (
27
) with Eq. (
37
). Thus,
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
is non-negative, and zero if
||\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x})-{\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})||=0
. This value
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
quantifies the amount of a difference between the velocity field of optimal transport and the mean local velocity. In case of
d=3
,
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}[\nabla\times\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x})]\cdot\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}\frac{||\nabla\times\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x})||^{2}}{p_{t}({\bf x})}\geq 0,

(41)

which quantifies the amount of the rotation because
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
is proportional to the mean value of the square of the rotation
||\nabla\times\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x})||/p_{t}({\bf x})
.
Thus,
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
is non-negative, and zero if the rotation vanishes
||\nabla\times\bm{A}_{t}({\bf x})||=0
.

We discuss when
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
vanishes. If the mean local velocity
\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})
is given by
\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})=-\nabla\Phi_{t}
with the potential
\Phi_{t}=\mu(V_{t}(\bm{x})+T\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}))
as we assumed in Eq. (
2
), the quantity
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}[({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x}))p_{t}({\bf x})]\cdot\nabla\Phi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d{\bf x}\nabla\cdot[({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x}))p_{t}({\bf x})\Phi_{t}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d\bm{S}\cdot[({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x}))p_{t}({\bf x})\Phi_{t}]

(42)

where we used Eq. (
31
) and
\int d\bm{S}
denotes the surface integral. If the quantity
\|[({\bm{\nu}}_{t}({\bf x})-\bm{a}_{1}({\bf x}))p_{t}\Phi_{t}\|
vanishes at infinity, the quantity
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
becomes zero. The assumption that the probability
p_{t}
vanishes at infinity is physically natural. Therefore,
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
vanishes in a physically natural situation and we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu T}\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}.

(43)

The above condition
\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})=-\nabla\Phi_{t}
is based on the assumption that the force
-\nabla V_{t}(\bm{x})
is given by the gradient of the potential
V_{t}(\bm{x})
. In the section V B, we analytically show
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
for the 1D-Brownian particle trapped in the harmonic potential. If the force is given by the non-poteintial force and the mean local velocity
\bm{\nu}_{t}({\bf x})
is not given by the potential, the term
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
might not be zero. The non-poteintial force is needed to achieve nonequilibrium steady state, and the steady flow and the steady force should be cyclic because of the Schnakenberg network theory
Schnakenberg1976network
. The quantity
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
might play an important role in the steady state thermodynamics with the non-potential force
Hatano2001steady
.

III.2
Geometric lower bounds on entropy production and thermodynamic speed limits

We here discuss a lower bound on the entropy production
\Sigma:=\int dt\sigma_{t}
based on Eq. (
22
). By using Eq. (
22
), the entropy production from time
t=0
to time
t=\tau
is bounded by

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu T}\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}.

(44)

In differential geometry, the quantity
\mathcal{C}=(1/2)\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\left(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt\right)^{2}
is called the action, and Eq. (
22
) implies that the entropy production for the Fokker-Planck equation is bounded by the action measured by the path length of the Wasserstein
L^{2}
distance,

\displaystyle\Sigma\geq\frac{2\mathcal{C}}{\mu T}.

(45)

If
\sigma^{\rm rot}=0
, the entropy production is proportional to the action measured by the path length of the Wasserstein
L^{2}
distance
\Sigma=2\mathcal{C}/(\mu T)
.
Here, we consider the following Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle 2\tau\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\left(\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\right)\left(\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int^{\tau}_{0}dt\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\tau}^{2},

(46)

which gives a lower bound on the action. In information geometry, this inequality has been considered
crooks2007measuring
as a trade-off relation between time
\tau
and the action
\mathcal{C}
. By considering
\left(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt\right)^{2}
as the Fisher information of time, several variants of thermodynamic speed limits can be derived from this inequality for the Markov jump process
ito2018stochastic1
, the Fokker-Planck equation
ito2020stochastic
and the rate equation
yoshimura2020information
in stochastic thermodynamics of information geometry.
In the same way, we obtain a lower bound on the entropy production by considering the action measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance (see also Fig.
2
),

\displaystyle\Sigma\geq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\tau}^{2}}{\mu T\tau},

(47)

which is also a main result in this paper.
Because this inequality implies a trade-off relation between time and the entropy production, this result can also be regarded as a generalization of thermodynamic speed limits.
Since we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the
equality can be achieved when the probability distribution moves with a constant velocity on the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance space,
that is, when it satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\tau}}{\tau},

(48)

for any
0\leq t\leq\tau
.

Schematic of the entropy production and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. The lower bound on the entropy production is obtained from geometry of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. The entropy production
\Sigma=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sigma_{t}
is tightly bounded by the length measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
. The
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance itself
\mathcal{W}(p_{0},p_{\tau})
provides a looser bound on the entropy production.
These inequalities are generalizations of thermodynamic speed limits.

Using the fact that the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance satisfies the triangle inequality for probabilities
p
,
q
and
r

Villani2008Optiaml

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p,r)\leq\mathcal{W}(p,q)+\mathcal{W}(q,r),

(49)

we obtain the following inequality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\geq\mathcal{W}(p_{0},p_{\tau}).

(50)

from the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
.
Using Eq. (
47
) and the above inequality, we can obtain the previously known inequality in Refs.
Aurell2012Refined
;
Dechant2019Wasserstein
,

\displaystyle\Sigma\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{0},p_{\tau})^{2}}{\mu T\tau},

(51)

which is equivalent to the Benamou-Brenier formula
Benamou2000Monge
because the entropy production rate is given by the expected value of the square of the velocity field
\bm{\nu}_{t}(\bf{x})
.
Considering the above derivation,
the condition for the equality to hold is when the probability distribution changes
at a constant speed on a straight line as measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}(p_{\tau},p_{0}),

(52)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{\tau},p_{0})}{\tau}.

(53)

In this case, the entropy production is minimized with constraints
p_{0}
and
p_{\tau}
. Moreover, when the initial distribution
p_{0}
, the final distribution
p_{\tau}
,
and the time interval
\tau
are specified, the protocol to achieve this equality can be numerically obtained by the algorithm of the fluid mechanics
Benamou2000Monge
. In other words, by using this algorithm, we can construct an efficient heat engine for small systems with the minimum entropy production.

Similarly, we obtain another lower bound by applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality Eq. (
46
) and the triangle inequality Eq. (
49
). Let us consider the time interval
t_{i}=\tau(i/N)
. Because the entropy production is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\mu T}\int^{t_{i+1}}_{t_{i}}dt\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2},

(54)

another lower bound on the entropy production can be obtained in a similar way as follows

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\hat{\Sigma}(t_{i};t_{i+1}),

(55)

where
\hat{\Sigma}(t;s)
is the lower bound on the entropy production by the Benamou-Brenier formula from time
t
to time
s
,

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(t;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t},p_{s})^{2}}{\mu T(s-t)}.

(56)

Moreover, in case of
\sigma_{t}^{\rm rot}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\hat{\Sigma}(t_{i};t_{i+1}),

(57)

because the change from
p_{t_{i}}
to
p_{t_{i+1}}
is at a constant rate on a straight line as measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance in the limit
t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\tau/N\to 0
. Remarkably, a calculation of
\hat{\Sigma}(t_{i};t_{i+1})
does not require information of the joint probability distribution at time
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
, while the experimental estimation of the entropy production based on the fluctuation theorem needs information of the joint probability distribution
Ciliberto2017experiments
. It is relatively difficult to estimate the joint probability in an experiment with a small number of samples, compared to two probabilities.
This fact might be useful to estimate the entropy production in an experiment by using Eq. (
57
). This estimation of the entropy production by using Eq. (
57
) is similar to the estimation of the entropy production based on thermodynamic trade-off relations such as thermodynamic uncertainty relations
Horowitz2019quantifying
;
Manikandan2020inferring
;
otsubo2020estimation
;
Vu2020entropy
.

IV
subsystem and information thermodynamics

IV.1
Stochastic thermodynamics for subsystem

Stochastic thermodynamics for a subsystem has been discussed in terms of information thermodynamics, which explains a paradox of the Maxwell’s demon
parrondo2015thermodynamics
. In information thermodynamics, we consider a relation between the partial entropy production and information flow for the 2D Fokker-Planck equation (
58
) or the 2D Langevin equations
allahverdyan2009thermodynamic
;
horowitz2014secondlaw
;
ito2015maxwell
. In this section, we discuss a relationship between the partial entropy production and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for a subsystem.

We start with two-dimensional systems
X
and
Y
. Stochastic dynamics of two positions
x\in X(=\mathbb{R})
and
y\in Y(=\mathbb{R})
are driven by the following Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(x,y)}{\partial t}\!=

\displaystyle\!-\!\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[\nu^{X}_{t}\!(x,y)p_{t}(x,y)]\!-\!\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[\nu^{Y}_{t}\!(x,y)p_{t}(x,y)],

\displaystyle\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y):=

\displaystyle-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[V_{t}(x,y)+T\ln p_{t}(x,y)],

\displaystyle\nu^{Y}_{t}(x,y):=

\displaystyle-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[V_{t}(x,y)+T\ln p_{t}(x,y)].

(58)

We first consider the situation that the position
y
is the hidden degree of freedom and we can only observe the position
x
.
Thus, we can only measure the marginal distribution of
X
defined as

\displaystyle p^{X}_{t}(x)=\int dy\ p_{t}(x,y).

(59)

The time evolution of the marginal distribution is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{X}_{t}(x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)p^{X}_{t}(x)\right),

(60)

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int dy\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)p_{t}(x,y)}{p^{X}_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int dy\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)p^{Y|X}_{t}(y|x),

(61)

where
\bar{\nu}_{t}^{X}(x)
is the marginal mean local velocity of
X
and
p^{Y|X}_{t}(y|x):=p_{t}(x,y)/p^{X}_{t}(x)
is the conditional probability of
Y
under the condition of
X
. If we want to measure the entropy production rate for this system, we only obtain the apparent entropy production rate of
X
,

\displaystyle{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx[\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)]^{2}p^{X}_{t}(x),

(62)

which is different from the partial entropy production rate of
X
,

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{X}_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx\int dy[\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)]^{2}p_{t}(x,y).

(63)

From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{X}_{t}-{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx\frac{\left(\int dy[\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)]^{2}p_{t}(x,y)\right)\left(\int dyp_{t}(x,y)\right)}{p^{X}_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx\frac{\left(\int dy\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)p_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}}{p^{X}_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

(64)

Thus, the apparent entropy production rate
{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}
is always smaller than the partial entropy production rate
{\sigma}^{X}_{t}
. The apparent entropy production rate is equivalent to the partial entropy production when
\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)=\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)
. This condition implies that the potential force
-\partial V_{t}(x,y)/\partial x
does not depend on
y
, and the systems
X
and
Y
are statistically independent
p_{t}(x,y)=p^{X}_{t}(x)p^{Y}_{t}(y)
with
p^{Y}_{t}(y):=\int dx\ p_{t}(x,y)
.

If we define the path length of
X
from time
t=0
to time
t=\tau
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{X}_{\tau}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow+0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{k\Delta t},p^{X}_{(k+1)\Delta t}),

(65)

our result for the path length of
X
gives the apparent entropy production rate of
X
,

\displaystyle{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\mu T}\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}^{X}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{t+\Delta t},p^{X}_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}},

(66)

where
n
is a positive integer satisfying
n\Delta t\geq\tau\geq(n+1)\Delta t
.
We also obtain a lower bound on the apparent entropy production rate of
X
as follows,

\displaystyle{\bar{\Sigma}}^{X}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}

(67)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\mathcal{L}^{X}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}

(68)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{0},p^{X}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}.

(69)

Compound inequalities of Eqs. (
64
), (
68
) and (
69
) can be 446
regarded as an information-thermodynamic speed limit for the 447
subsystem.
We remark that
\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)
is given by the potential
\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)=-\partial_{x}\Phi_{t}
when
\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)=\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)
. In this case, we may obtain
{\sigma}^{X}_{t}={\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}=(d\mathcal{L}^{X}_{t}/dt)^{2}/(\mu T)
because of the same reason in case of
\sigma^{\rm tot}_{t}=0
for the total system. We also remark that
\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)
is also given by
\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x)=-\partial_{x}\bar{\Phi}^{X}_{t}
with the potential
\bar{\Phi}^{X}_{t}=\int dyp^{Y}(y)\Phi_{t}
if the systems
X
and
Y
are the statistically independent
p_{t}(x,y)=p^{X}_{t}(x)p^{Y}_{t}(y)
.

Now, we discuss a relationship between the subsystem
X
and the subsystem
Y
. We introduce the marginal mean local velocity of
Y
, the apparent entropy production rate of
Y
and the partial entropy production rate of
Y
as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{Y}_{t}(y)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\bar{\nu}^{Y}_{t}(y)p^{Y}_{t}(y)\right),

(70)

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}^{Y}_{t}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int dx\ \nu^{Y}_{t}(x,y)p_{t}(x,y)}{p^{Y}_{t}(y)},

(71)

\displaystyle{\bar{\sigma}}^{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dy[\bar{\nu}^{Y}_{t}(y)]^{2}p^{Y}_{t}(y),

(72)

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx\int dy[\nu^{Y}_{t}(x,y)]^{2}p_{t}(x,y).

(73)

We also obtain a lower bound on the apparent entropy production rate of
Y
as follows,

\displaystyle{\bar{\Sigma}}^{Y}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt{\bar{\sigma}}^{Y}_{t}

(74)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\mathcal{L}^{Y}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}

(75)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{Y}_{0},p^{Y}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T},

(76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{Y}_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow+0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{W}(p^{Y}_{k\Delta t},p^{Y}_{(k+1)\Delta t}),

(77)

where
n
is a positive integer satisfying
n\Delta t\leq\tau\leq(n+1)\Delta t
.
By definition, the entropy production rate is given by the sum of the partial entropy production rates,

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}=\sigma^{X}_{t}+\sigma^{Y}_{t}.

(78)

Because
\sigma^{X}_{t}\geq{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}
and
\sigma^{Y}_{t}\geq{\bar{\sigma}}^{Y}_{t}
, the inequality

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}-{\bar{\sigma}}^{X}_{t}-{\bar{\sigma}}^{Y}_{t}\geq 0,

(79)

is satisfied. Thus, we obtain lower bounds on the entropy production from Eqs. (
68
), (
69
), (
75
), (
76
) and (
79
),

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\mathcal{L}^{X}_{\tau})^{2}+(\mathcal{L}^{Y}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}

(80)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{0}^{X},p_{\tau}^{X})^{2}+\mathcal{W}(p_{0}^{Y},p_{\tau}^{Y})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}.

(81)

From the non-negativity of
(\mathcal{L}^{Y}_{\tau})^{2}
and
\mathcal{W}(p_{0}^{Y},p_{\tau}^{Y})
, we also obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\mathcal{L}^{X}_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau\mu T}

(82)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{0}^{X},p_{\tau}^{X})^{2}}{\tau\mu T},

(83)

as looser bounds.
This result implies that the entropy production of the total system is generally bounded by geometry of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for two subsystems
X
and
Y
.

IV.2
Information thermodynamics

We next discuss an interpretation of the above result based on information thermodynamics. In information thermodynamics, we consider the following decomposition of the partial entropy production

The partial entropy production rates of

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm sys};t}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y},

(85)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\rm bath};t}^{X}\!

\displaystyle=\!\int dx\!\int dy\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\!\ln\frac{p_{t}(x,y)}{p^{X}_{t}(x)p^{Y}_{t}(y)}\!\right)\!\right]\!\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)p_{t}(x,y),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}\!

\displaystyle=\!\int\!dx\!\int\!dy\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\!\ln\frac{p_{t}(x,y)}{p^{X}_{t}(x)p^{Y}_{t}(y)}\!\right)\!\right]\!\nu^{Y}_{t}(x,y)p_{t}(x,y),

(91)

where
\sigma_{{\rm bath};t}^{X}
(
\sigma_{{\rm bath};t}^{Y}

X
(

(

X
(

(

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\rm sys};t}^{X}

\displaystyle=\int dx\frac{\partial p^{X}_{t}(x)}{\partial t}[-\ln p^{X}_{t}(x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}S^{X}_{\rm sys},

(92)

\displaystyle S^{X}_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=\int dx[-p^{X}_{t}(x)\ln p^{X}_{t}(x)],

(93)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\rm sys};t}^{Y}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}S^{X}_{\rm sys},

(94)

\displaystyle S^{Y}_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=\int dy[-p^{Y}_{t}(y)\ln p^{Y}_{t}(y)],

(95)

where we used the partial integral and the normalization of the probability

The sum of the entropy changes of the heat bath gives the total entropy changes of the heat bathes

(96)

where we used the partial integral. The sum of information flows gives the change of the mutual information

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}

(97)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int dx\int dyp_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{p_{t}(x,y)}{p^{X}_{t}(x)p^{Y}_{t}(y)},

where we used the partial integral, the marginalization

, and the normalization of the probability
(d/dt)\int dxp_{t}^{X}(x)=0
,
(d/dt)\int dxp_{t}^{Y}(x)=0
, and
(d/dt)\int dxdyp_{t}(x,y)=0
. Additionally, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\rm sys};t}^{X}+\sigma_{{\rm sys};t}^{Y}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}=\frac{dS_{\rm sys}}{dt},

(99)

thus the sum of the partial entropy production rates gives the total entropy production rate.

The non-negativity of the partial entropy production rates gives the second laws of information thermodynamics for the subsystem
allahverdyan2009thermodynamic
;
ito2013information
;
horowitz2014thermodynamics
;
hartich2014stochastic
;
horowitz2014secondlaw
;
ito2015maxwell
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{X}_{{\rm sys};t}

\displaystyle\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X},

(100)

\displaystyle\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm sys};t}

\displaystyle\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y},

(101)

which implies that the entropy changes of the system and heat bath are bounded by information flow in the presence of the subsystem. The sum of two inequalities

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{X}_{{\rm sys};t}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X}+\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm sys};t}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}\geq 0,

(102)

gives the second law of thermodynamics for the total system

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(103)

These inequalities Eq. (
100
) and (
101
) explain a conversion between information and thermodynamic quantities in the context of the Maxwell’s demon. In the study of autonomous demon, the system
Y
can be considered as the Maxwell’s demon, and the system
X
is regraded as the system where the entropy changes
\sigma^{X}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{X}_{{\rm sys};t}
can be negative. The second laws of information thermodynamics explains the reason why the entropy changes can be negative. Because of the information flow from the demon to the system
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X}
, the entropy changes can be negative.

Based on the results Eqs. (
64
) and (
66
), we obtain tighter inequalities compared to the second law of information thermodynamics as follows

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{X}_{{\rm sys};t}

\displaystyle\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X}+\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{t+\Delta t},p^{X}_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}}\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{X},

(104)

\displaystyle\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm bath};t}+\sigma^{Y}_{{\rm sys};t}

\displaystyle\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}+\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{Y}_{t+\Delta t},p^{Y}_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}}\geq\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{Y}.

(105)

Thus, the entropy changes of the system and heat bath are tightly bounded by both information flow and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance.

We now consider the situation
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
.
Because the sum of the partial entropy production rates gives the total entropy production rate, the sum of two tighter inequalities gives non-negativity of a measure
I^{\mathcal{W}}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{I^{\mathcal{W}}}{\Delta t^{2}}\geq 0,

(106)

\displaystyle I^{\mathcal{W}}=\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})^{2}-\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{t+\Delta t},p^{X}_{t})^{2}-\mathcal{W}(p^{Y}_{t+\Delta t},p^{Y}_{t})^{2}.

(107)

The equality holds when

\displaystyle\nu^{X}_{t}(x,y)=\bar{\nu}^{X}_{t}(x),\>\>\nu^{Y}_{t}(x,y)=\bar{\nu}^{Y}_{t}(y),

(108)

because

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}_{t}+\sigma^{Y}_{t}=\sigma_{t}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t+\Delta t},p_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}},

(109)

holds in case of
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
, and we can obtain

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}_{t}=\bar{\sigma}^{X}_{t}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{X}_{t+\Delta t},p^{X}_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}},

(110)

\displaystyle\sigma^{Y}_{t}=\bar{\sigma}^{Y}_{t}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{Y}_{t+\Delta t},p^{Y}_{t})^{2}}{\mu T\Delta t^{2}},

(111)

under the condition Eq. (
108
).
The measure
I^{\mathcal{W}}
quantifies both the statistical independence and the independence of the potential, while the mutual information
I
only quantifies the statistical independence. Thus,
I^{\mathcal{W}}
could be an interesting measure of the independence between two systems when stochastic dynamics of two systems are driven by the Fokker-Planck equation. Its non-negativity is decomposed by tighter inequalities of information thermodynamics Eqs. (
104
) and (
105
).

V.1
Stochastic heat engine and geometrical bounds on efficiency

Let us consider a stochastic heat engine
Schmiedl2007stochastic
driven by the potential
V_{t}
. The cycle of a stochastic engine consists of the following four steps (see also Fig.
3
).

Figure 3:
An example of a stochastic heat engine. Because the initial state at time
t=0
is the same as the final state at time
t=t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}
, the four steps gives the cycle of a stochastic heat engine. The work
-W_{\rm h}
is extracted during time
0\leq t<t_{\rm h}
, and the work
W_{\rm c}
is done during time
t_{\rm h}\leq t<t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}
. The total amount of the work through one cycle
-W=-W_{\rm h}+W_{\rm c}>0
is extracted.

1.

Let us consider an isothermal process of varying the potential
V_{t}({\bf x})
during time
0\leq t<t_{\rm h}
at temperature
T_{\rm h}
. During this step, the probability distribution changes from
p^{\rm a}
to
p^{\rm b}
, and the entropy change of the system is given by
\Delta S:=\int d{\bf x}p^{\rm a}({\bf x})\ln p^{\rm a}({\bf x})-\int d{\bf x}p^{\rm b}({\bf x})\ln p^{\rm b}({\bf x})
.
In this step, the work is extracted
-W_{\rm h}:=\int_{0}^{t_{\rm h}}dt(dW/dt)>0
for the external system.

2.

The temperature is changed from
T_{\rm h}
to
T_{\rm c}(<T_{\rm h})
instantaneously at time
t=t_{\rm h}
. During this time, the distribution
p^{\rm b}
does not change. Therefore, the entropy of the system also did not change, and this step can be interpreted as an adiabatic process.

3.

Let us consider an isothermal process that returns the potential
V_{t_{\rm h}}({\bf x})
to
V_{0}({\bf x})=V_{t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}}({\bf x})
during time
t_{\rm h}\leq t<t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}
at temperature
T_{\rm c}
. During this step, the probability distribution changes from
p^{\rm b}
to
p^{\rm a}
, and the entropy change of the system is
-\Delta S
.
In this step, the system is assumed to be given work
W_{\rm c}:=\int_{t_{\rm h}}^{t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}}dt(dW/dt)>0
by the external system.

4.

The temperature is changed from
T_{\rm c}
to
T_{\rm h}
instantaneously at time
t=t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}
. During this time, the distribution does not change.
Therefore, the entropy of the system also did not change, and this step can be interpreted as an adiabatic process.

If we consider the harmonic potential and the initial distribution
p^{\rm a}
is Gaussian, thermodynamic quantities such as the entropy change and the work are calculated, and we can find an optimal protocol to minimize the entropy production can be obtained analytically
Schmiedl2007stochastic
. As we shown in the section V B,
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
and the entropy production rate is proportional to the action.

Here we consider a general case that the potential is not necessarily harmonic and the probability distribution at time
t
is not necessarily Gaussian.
When the time
t_{\rm h}
and
t_{\rm c}
are long enough and the potential
V_{t}({\bf x})
is a harmonic potential,
the efficiency of the heat engine becomes the Carnot efficiency asymptotically, and the heat engine can be considered as a stochastic extension of the Carnot cycle.
The extracted work of the heat engine through the one cycle is

\displaystyle-W:=W_{\rm h}-W_{\rm c}=(T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c})\Delta S-T_{\rm h}\Sigma_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c}\Sigma_{\rm c},

(112)

where
\Sigma_{\rm h}:=\int_{0}^{t_{\rm h}}dt\sigma_{t}
is entropy production in the isothermal step
1
at temperature
T_{\rm h}
and
\Sigma_{\rm c}:=\int_{t_{\rm h}}^{t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}}dt\sigma_{t}
is the entropy production in the isothermal step
3
at temperature
T_{c}
. If we assumed that the extracted work is positive
-W>0
, the condition
\Delta S\geq 0
should be needed because of the second law of thermodynamics
\Sigma_{\rm h}\geq 0
and
\Sigma_{\rm c}\geq 0
.

By using Eq. (
51
), we can obtain the following inequality for the extracted work
-W
,

\displaystyle-W

\displaystyle\leq(T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c})\Delta S-\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}{\mu t_{\rm r}},

(113)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{\rm r}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{t_{\rm h}}+\frac{1}{t_{\rm c}},

(114)

where
t_{\rm r}
is called the reduced time. When we impose the positive extracted work in the whole cycle, i.e.,
-W>0
, we obtain the following inequality for the reduced time
t_{\rm r}
from Eq.(
112
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{\rm r}}\leq\mu(T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c})\frac{\Delta S}{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}.

(115)

This inequality implies that the reduced time in the engine is generally bounded by the ratio of the square of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}
to the entropy change
\Delta S
, which are given by the initial distribution
p^{\rm a}
and the final distribution
p_{\rm b}
.

The efficiency of the heat engine
\eta
is defined as

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{-W}{T_{\rm h}\Delta S-T_{\rm h}\Sigma_{\rm h}}.

(116)

Because the second law of thermodynamics
\Sigma_{\rm h}+\Sigma_{\rm c}\geq 0
holds, we obtain the fact that the efficiency is generally bounded by the Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\rm C}

Broeck2013stochastic
,

\displaystyle\eta\leq\frac{T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c}}{T_{\rm h}}:=\eta_{\rm C}.

(117)

When we considered the situation that the entropy production is minimized as follows

\displaystyle T_{\rm c}\Sigma_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}{\mu t_{\rm c}},

(118)

\displaystyle T_{\rm h}\Sigma_{\rm h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}{\mu t_{\rm h}},

(119)

the efficiency
\eta
is given by

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c}-\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}{\mu\Delta St_{\rm r}}}{T_{\rm h}-\frac{\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}}{\mu\Delta St_{\rm h}}},

(120)

and reaches to the Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\rm C}
in the limit
t_{\rm h}\to\infty
and
t_{\rm c}\to\infty
. This fact is also discussed in Ref.
Fu2021maximal
. In the limit
t_{\rm h}\to\infty
and
t_{\rm c}\to\infty
. the square of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance plays the same role as the irreversible “action”
A_{\rm irr}
in Ref.
Schmiedl2007stochastic
.

When
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
, we obtain a geometric interpretation of the efficiency from Eq. (
25
),

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{\rm h}-T_{\rm c}-\frac{1}{\mu\Delta S}\int_{0}^{t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}}dt\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}}{T_{\rm h}-\frac{1}{\mu\Delta S}\int_{0}^{t_{\rm h}}dt\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}}.

(121)

In this case, we obtain a lower bound on the efficiency

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm C}-\frac{2\mathcal{C}}{\mu\Delta ST_{\rm h}}\leq\eta\leq\eta_{\rm C},

(122)

where
\mathcal{C}=(1/2)\int_{0}^{t_{\rm h}+t_{\rm c}}dt(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt)^{2}
is the action measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. The efficiency
\eta
can reach to the Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\rm C}
when the ratio between the action and the Shannon entropy change
\mathcal{C}/\Delta S
converges to zero. In general
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}\neq 0
and this lower bound Eq. (
122
) is generally violated, especially when the non-potential force exists. Thus, the quantity
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
might play an important role in a stochastic heat engine with the non-potential force.

V.2
Analytical calculation of geometric optimal protocol

We here discuss dynamics of a Brownian particle in a harmonic potential as an example of stochastic thermodynamics based on
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. In this case, we can show
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}=0
, and obtain the protocol of minimizing the entropy production analytically. In terms of the Langevin equation, the time evolution of the position
x(t)
at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{dx(t)}{dt}=-\mu\frac{\partial V_{t}(x)}{\partial x}+\sqrt{2\mu T}\xi(t),

(123)

with the harmonic potential

\displaystyle V_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}k_{t}(x-a_{t})^{2},

(124)

where
\xi(t)
is the Gaussian noise with the mean
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
and the variance
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
This Langevin equation corresponds to the following Fokker-Planck equation
Risken1986FokkerPlanck
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\nu_{t}(x)p_{t}(x)),

(125)

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x):

\displaystyle=-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[V_{t}(x)+T\ln p_{t}(x)].

(126)

We now assume that the probability distribution at the initial time is Gaussian. For the harmonic potential, the probability distribution at time
t
is Gaussian if the probability distribution at the initial time is Gaussian. The probability distribution
p_{t}(x)
is written as the Gaussian distribution with the mean
{\rm E}[x]_{t}
and the variance
{\rm Var}[x]_{t}
at time
t
,

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-{\rm E}[x]_{t})^{2}}{2{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}\right),

(127)

\displaystyle{\rm E}[x]_{t}

\displaystyle=\int dxxp_{t}(x),

(128)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[x]_{t}

\displaystyle=\int dxx^{2}p_{t}(x)-({\rm E}[x]_{t})^{2}.

(129)

For this Fokker-Planck equation, the time evolution of
{\rm E}[x]_{t}
and
{\rm Var}[x]_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rm E}[x]_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu k_{t}(a_{t}-{\rm E}[x]_{t}),

(130)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rm Var}[x]_{t}

\displaystyle=-2\mu\left(k_{t}{\rm Var}[x]_{t}-T\right).

(131)

Therefore, the mean local velocity
\nu_{t}(x)
is analytically calculated as

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\mu k_{t}({\rm E}[x]_{t}-a_{t})+\left(\frac{\mu T}{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}-\mu k_{t}\right)(x-{\rm E}[x]_{t}),

(132)

and the entropy production rate is also calculated as

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int dx|\nu_{t}(x)|^{2}p_{t}(x)

(133)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{T}\left\{\left(k_{t}-\frac{T}{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}\right)^{2}{\rm Var}[x]_{t}+k_{t}^{2}({\rm E}[x]_{t}-a_{t})^{2}\right\}.

(134)

The Wasserstein distance can be concretely calculated for the Gaussian distribution
Givens1984Wasserstein
;
Takatsu2012Wasserstein
. For two probability distributions

\displaystyle p^{\rm a}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm a}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-{\rm E}[x]^{\rm a})^{2}}{2{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm a}}\right),

(135)

and

\displaystyle p^{\rm b}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm b}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-{\rm E}[x]^{\rm b})^{2}}{2{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm b}}\right),

(136)

the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance can be written as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p^{\rm a},p^{\rm b})^{2}=({\rm E}[x]^{\rm a}-{\rm E}[x]^{\rm b})^{2}+\left(\!\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm a}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]^{\rm b}}\!\right)^{2}.

(137)

This
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is also known as the Fréchet distance
Dowson1982Dowson
.
Using this analytical expression of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for two Gaussian distributions, we can confirm
\sigma^{\rm rot}=0
in this case as follows

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow+0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{t},p_{t+\Delta t})^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d{\rm E}[x]_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}\left\{\frac{\left(k_{t}{\rm Var}[x]_{t}-T\right)^{2}}{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}+k_{t}^{2}({\rm E}[x]_{t}-a_{t})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mu T\sigma_{t},

(138)

where we used Eqs. (
130
) and (
131
).

We also can confirm that the entropy production
\Sigma
is minimized if Eq. (
53
) holds. The minimum value of the entropy production
\Sigma
for fixed
p_{0}
and
p_{\tau}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\left[\left(\frac{d{\rm E}[x]_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}}{dt}\right)^{2}\right]}{\mu T}

(139)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left(\int_{t=0}^{t=\tau}d{\rm E}[x]_{t}\right)^{2}+\left(\int_{t=0}^{t=\tau}d\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}\right)^{2}}{\mu T\tau}

(140)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left({\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0}\right)^{2}+\left(\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}\right)^{2}}{\mu T\tau},

(141)

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}dt(d\theta_{t}/dt)^{2}\geq(\int_{0}^{\tau}dt(d\theta_{t}/dt))^{2}
with
\theta_{t}={\rm E}[x]_{t}
and
\theta_{t}=\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}
. The minimum value can be achieved if
d\theta_{t}/dt
is constant. This condition of the minimum value can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[x]_{t}=\left(1-\frac{t}{\tau}\right){\rm E}[x]_{0}+\frac{t}{\tau}{\rm E}[x]_{\tau}

(142)

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}=\left(1-\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}+\frac{t}{\tau}\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}},

(143)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{d{\rm E}[x]_{t}}{dt}=\frac{{\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0}}{\tau},

(144)

\displaystyle\frac{d\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}}{dt}=\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\tau}.

(145)

Under this condition,
\mathcal{W}(p_{0},p_{\tau})/\tau
is calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{W}(p_{0},p_{\tau})}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\sqrt{({\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0})^{2}+\left(\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{d{\rm E}[x]_{t}}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}}{dt}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{t}}{dt},

(146)

which is the condition that the probability distribution changes at a constant rate on a straight line measured by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance Eq. (
53
). By comparing Eqs. (
144
) and (
145
) with Eqs. (
130
) and (
131
), the optimal protocol that minimizes the entropy production is given by

\displaystyle\mu k_{t}(a_{t}-{\rm E}[x]_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0}}{\tau},

(147)

\displaystyle-\mu\left(k_{t}{\rm Var}[x]_{t}-T\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\tau}.

(148)

In terms of the parameters of the harmonic potential
V_{t}(x)
, the optimal protocol that minimizes the entropy production is given by

\displaystyle k_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}-\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\mu\tau\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{t}}},

(149)

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle={\rm E}[x]_{t}+\frac{{\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0}}{k_{t}\mu\tau}.

(150)

Thus, we obtain the parameters
k_{t}
and
a_{t}
which realize such an optimal protocol in practice

\displaystyle k_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{T\tau^{2}}{\left(\tau\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}+t(\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\mu\left[\tau\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}+t(\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}})\right]},

(151)

\displaystyle a_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau{\rm E}[x]_{0}+t({\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0})}{\tau}+\frac{{\rm E}[x]_{\tau}-{\rm E}[x]_{0}}{k_{t}\mu\tau}.

(152)

If we assume that
k_{t}
is always non-negative, the following inequality

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-t\mu T}{\mu T}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}

(153)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu T}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}[x]_{0}}}.

(154)

must hold for this optimal protocol. The results implies that when the variance gets smaller, i.e.,
{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}<{\rm Var}[x]_{0}
, we can use this optimal protocol for all
\tau>0
, but when the variance gets larger, i.e.,
{\rm Var}[x]_{\tau}\geq{\rm Var}[x]_{0}
, there is a limit to the time
\tau
for the process to achieve this optimal protocol.

VI
Discussion

We discuss a geometrical feature of stochastic thermodynamics for the Fokker-Planck equation based on the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. As shown in this paper, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is strongly related to the entropy production for the Fokker-Planck equation. Thus, based on
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance, we can introduce a differential geometry of stochastic thermodynamics for the Fokker-Planck equation, closely related to the entropy production.

It might be interesting to consider a relation between the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance and the Fisher information matrix because the Fisher information matrix
gives another metric in information geometry, which is also a possible choice of differential geometry of stochastic thermodynamics. For example, the entropy production is given by the projection in information geometry
ito2018unified
. Thus, there might be a deep connection between information geometry and optimal transport by the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance. For example, the HWI inequality, the logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, and the Talagrand inequalities are considered as a trade-off relation among the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance, the relative Fisher information, and the Shannon entropy
Villani2000generalization
;
Villani2008Optiaml
. As shown in Ref.
ito2020stochastic
, we have an analogy between the entropy production rate and the Fisher information of time for the Fokker-Planck equation. This analogy is also pointed out in Ref.
Li2019Wasserstein
. Thus, we might unify two directions of researches of information geometry and the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for the Fokker-Planck equation based on the entropy production. The unification of information geometry and geometry of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance has been recently discussed
amari2018information
;
amari2019information
, and our results might provide a new direction in this topic.

If we consider thermodynamics based on information geometry, we can consider not only stochastic thermodynamics for the Fokker-Planck equation
ito2020stochastic
but also stochastic thermodynamics for the Markov jump process
ito2018stochastic1
and chemical thermodynamics for the rate equation
yoshimura2020information
. Thus, it might be interesting to seek a correspondence of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for the Markov jump process and the rate equation. Indeed, T. Van Vu and Y. Hasegawa derived a generalization of thermodynamic speed limits for the Markov jump process
Van2021geometrical
, then a thermodynamic correspondence of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance for the Markov jump process might be the distance discussed in Ref.
Van2021geometrical
. Moreover, our result is based on the setting of the overdamped Langevin equation, where the entropy production rate is given by the mean local velocity. Thus, it is interesting to generalize our result for the underdamped Langevin equation or the generalized Langevin equation for a non-Markovian process.

In a nonequilibrium steady state, the quantity
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
might play an important role. Under the existence of the non-potential force, the entropy production rate is generally decomposed into two non-negative parts, the Wasserstein part
(d\mathcal{L}_{t}/dt)^{2}/(\mu T)
and the non-potential part
\sigma^{\rm rot}_{t}
. This fact is very similar to the case of the steady state thermodynamics
Hatano2001steady
, where the entropy production is decomposed into the excess entropy production and the housekeeping heat.

Appendix A
Proof of the formula Eq. (
33
)

To obtain the formula Eq. (
33
), we introduce the map
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow s}
for the trajectory of the particle according to the Fokker-Planck equation from time
t
to time
s
. The map
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}
is given by the following differential equations for
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}({\bf x})

\displaystyle={\bm{\nu}}_{t+s}(\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}({\bf x})),

(164)

with the initial condition
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t}({\bf x}):={\bf x}
. The map
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t-s}
for
s\geq 0
is also given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t-s}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=-{\bm{\nu}}_{t-s}(\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t-s}({\bf x})).

(165)

with the initial condition
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t}({\bf x}):={\bf x}
. These differential equations correspond to the Lagrangian descriptions of the Fokker-Planck equation as a continuity equation. Because the
composite map
\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}\circ\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x})=\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))
is a non-optimal transport plan from
p
to
p_{t+s}
, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf x}\ \|{\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{t+s}({\bf x})\|^{2}p({\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq\int d{\bf x}\|{\bf x}-\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))\|^{2}p({\bf x}).

(166)

By using Eqs. (
164
) and (
166
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}}{2}\right)\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow+0}\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t})^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int d{\bf x}p({\bf x})\left[\lim_{s\rightarrow+0}\frac{\|{\bf x}-\mathcal{M}_{t\rightarrow t+s}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))\|^{2}-\|{\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x})\|^{2}}{2s}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int d{\bf x}({\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))p({\bf x}).

(167)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}}{2}\right)\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow+0}\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t+s})^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mathcal{W}(p,p_{t})^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\int d{\bf x}\!p({\bf x})\!\left[\lim_{s\rightarrow+0}\frac{\|{\bf x}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{t+s}({\bf x})\|^{2}\!-\!\|{\bf x}\!-\!\mathcal{M}_{t+s\rightarrow t}(\mathcal{T}_{t+s}({\bf x}))\|^{2}}{2s}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int d{\bf x}({\bf x}-\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))\cdot{\bm{\nu}}_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{t}({\bf x}))p({\bf x}),

(168)

because the
composite map
\mathcal{M}_{t+s\rightarrow t}\circ\mathcal{T}_{t+s}
is a non-optimal transport plan from
p
to
p_{t}
.
From Eqs. (
167
) and (
168
), we finally obtain the formula Eq. (
33
).
