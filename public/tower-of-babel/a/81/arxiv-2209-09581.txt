Unbiased time-average estimators for Markov chains
Nabil Kahalé

Abstract
We consider a time-average estimator f k f_{k} of a functional of a Markov chain. Under a coupling assumption, we show that the expectation of f k f_{k} has a limit μ \mu as the number of time-steps goes to infinity. We describe a modification of f k f_{k} that yields an unbiased estimator f ^ k \hat{f}_{k} of μ \mu . It is shown that f ^ k \hat{f}_{k} is square-integrable and has finite expected running time. Under certain conditions, f ^ k \hat{f}_{k} can be built without any precomputations, and is asymptotically at least as efficient as f k f_{k} , up to a multiplicative constant arbitrarily close to 1 1 . Our approach provides an unbiased estimator for the bias of f k f_{k} . We study applications to volatility forecasting, queues, and the simulation of high-dimensional Gaussian vectors. Our numerical experiments are consistent with our theoretical findings.



Keywords: multilevel Monte Carlo, unbiased estimator, steady-state, Markov chain, time-average estimator

E(Z^{2})=\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}\frac{E((Y_{l}-Y_{l-1})^{2})}{p_{l}}.

3.2
Construction of
\hat{f}_{k}

This subsection supposes that Assumption A1 holds and constructs
\hat{f}_{k}
along the following steps:

1.

Build a random sequence
(f_{k,l},l\geq 0)
such that
E(f_{k,l})\rightarrow\mu
as
l
goes to infinity and
f_{k,0}
is a standard time-average estimator with burn-in period
b^{\prime}(k)\in[b(k),k/2]
.

2.

Use the sequence
(f_{k,l},l\geq 0)
to construct a RMLMC estimator
Z_{k}
with
E(Z_{k})=\mu-E(f_{k,0})
.

3.

Combine
f_{k,0}
and
Z_{k}
to produce
\hat{f}_{k}
.

First, we detail Step 1. For
k\geq 1
, let
b^{\prime}(k)
be a burn-in period with
b(k)\leq b^{\prime}(k)\leq k/2
. Different choices for
b^{\prime}(k)
will be studied in Subsections
3.3
and
3.4
. For
k\geq 1
and
l\geq 0
, let

f_{k,l}:=\frac{1}{k-b^{\prime}(k)}\sum^{k-1}_{i=b^{\prime}(k)}f(X_{i,k(2^{l}-1)}).

(3.1)

In particular,

f_{k,0}=\frac{1}{k-b^{\prime}(k)}\sum^{k-1}_{i=b^{\prime}(k)}f(X_{i}).

(3.2)

As
X_{i,k(2^{l}-1)}\sim X_{i+k(2^{l}-1)}
, Theorem
2.1
implies that
E(f_{k,l})\rightarrow\mu
as
l
goes to infinity. By (
2.2
), for
0\leq l<l^{\prime}
and
i\geq 0
, the last
i+m
copies of
U_{0}
used to calculate
f_{k,l}
and
f_{k,l^{\prime}}
are the same, where
m=k(2^{l}-1)
. Thus, intuitively speaking,
f_{k,l^{\prime}}
should be close to
f_{k,l}
for large values of
l
, and increasing
b^{\prime}(k)
should make
f_{k,l^{\prime}}
closer to
f_{k,l}
even for small
l
.
For simplicity, it is assumed that the expected time to simulate
f_{k,l}
is equal to
k2^{l}
. This assumption is justified by the fact that
f_{k,l}
is calculated by generating
U_{-m},\dots,U_{k-2}
,
and using (
2.3
) to calculate
X_{-m,m},\dots,X_{k-1,m}
. Lemma
3.1
gives an upper bound on the variance of
f_{k,0}
in terms of that of
f_{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
k\geq 1
,

{\rm Var}(f_{k,0})\leq\frac{796(\overline{\nu}(0))^{2}}{k^{3/2}}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(k)-b(k)}+{\rm Var}(f_{k}).

Next, we describe Step 2. Let
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}
with
p_{l}>0
for
l\geq 0
. For
k\geq 1
, let

Z_{k}^{(b^{\prime}(k))}:=\frac{f_{k,N+1}-f_{k,N}}{p_{N}},

(3.3)

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
is an integer-valued random variable independent of
(U_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z})
such that
\Pr(N=l)=p_{l}
for
l\geq 0
. For simplicity, we will often denote
Z_{k}^{(b^{\prime}(k))}
by
Z_{k}
. Let
T_{k}
be the expected time required to simulate
Z_{k}
. Lemma
3.2
provides bounds on
T_{k}
and on the second moment of
Z_{k}
and shows that, under certain conditions,
Z_{k}
is an unbiased estimator for the negated bias
\mu-E(f_{k,0})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
T_{k}\leq 3k\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}2^{l}p_{l}
, and

kE(Z_{k}^{2})\leq 2(\overline{\nu}(\lfloor{b^{\prime}(k)}/{2}\rfloor))^{2}(\frac{1}{p_{0}}+\frac{1}{p_{1}})+\sum^{\infty}_{l=2}\frac{2^{3-l}(\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-2})-\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-1}))^{2}}{p_{l}}.

(3.4)

If the right-hand side of (
3.4
) is finite, then
Z_{k}
is square-integrable and
E(f_{k,0}+Z_{k})=\mu
.

We now detail Step 3. Given
q\in(0,1]
, let
Z^{\prime}_{k}
be a copy of
Z_{k}
independent of
f_{k,0}
and let
Q
be a binary variable independent of
(f_{k,0},Z^{\prime}_{k})
such that
\Pr(Q=1)=q
. Set

\hat{f}_{k}:=f_{k,0}+q^{-1}QZ^{\prime}_{k}.

(3.5)

In other words,
\hat{f}_{k}
is constructed by sampling
f_{k,0}
once and sampling a copy of
Z_{k}
with frequency
q
. By Lemma
3.2
, if the right-hand side of (
3.4
) is finite, then
E(\hat{f}_{k})=E(f_{k,0})+E(Z_{k})=\mu
, and
\hat{f}_{k}
is an unbiased estimator of
\mu
. When
q=1
, copies of
f_{k,0}
and of
Z_{k}
are sampled with the same frequency. When
q<1
,
Z_{k}
is sampled less often than
f_{k,0}
, which can improve the efficiency of
\hat{f}_{k}
, in the same spirit as the Multilevel Monte Carlo Method (MLMC)
[
Giles2008
]
and the randomized dimension reduction algorithm
[
kahaRandomizedDimensionReduction20
]
. Selecting the
p_{l}
’s and
q
is studied in Subsections
3.3
and
3.4
. Let
\hat{T}_{k}
be the expected time to simulate
\hat{f}_{k}
. As the expected time to simulate
f_{k,0}
is equal to
k
, we have
\hat{T}_{k}=k+qT_{k}
. Note that the estimator
Z_{k}
is interesting by itself as it provides an unbiased estimator for the bias of
f_{k}
if we set
b^{\prime}(k)=b(k)
. We now state the following assumption:

Assumption B.

There is a positive real number
w_{0}
such that
k{\rm Var}(f_{k})\geq w_{0}
for sufficiently large
k
.

When
b(k)=0
, Assumption B can be shown under certain correlation hypotheses
[
asmussenGlynn2007
, p. 99]
.

3.3
\nu
-dependent parameters

This subsection gives a construction of
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
and of
q
in terms of
\bar{\nu}
. For
l\geq 2
, set

p_{l}=\frac{\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-2})-\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-1})}{2^{l}\overline{\nu}(k)},

(3.6)

and

p_{1}=(1-\sum^{\infty}_{l=2}p_{l})/3\text{ and }p_{0}=2p_{1}.

(3.7)

Note that
p_{l}>0
for
l\geq 2
since
(\overline{\nu}(i),i\geq 0)

is a strictly decreasing sequence. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{l=2}2^{l}p_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{l=2}\frac{\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-2})-\overline{\nu}(k2^{l-1})}{\overline{\nu}(k)}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.

Hence
\sum^{\infty}_{l=2}p_{l}\leq 1/4
. Consequently,
p_{1}\geq 1/4
,
p_{0}\geq 1/2
, and
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
is a probability distribution. The
p_{l}
’s have been chosen so that the summands in the bounds on
T_{k}
and
E(Z_{k}^{2})
in Lemma
3.2
are proportional for
l\geq 2
. Lemma
3.3
shows that
Z_{k}
is an unbiased estimator of
\mu-E(f_{k,0})
and provides bounds on its second moment and expected running time. Note that the bound on
E(Z_{k}^{2})
is, up to a multiplicative constant, the square of the bound on
|E(Z_{k})|
that follows from (
2.11
) and the equality
E(Z_{k})=\mu-E(f_{k,0})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that A1 holds and that
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
are given by (
3.6
) and (
3.7
). For
k\geq 1
, we have
E(f_{k,0}+Z_{k})=\mu
,
T_{k}\leq 9k
, and

kE(Z_{k}^{2})\leq 20(\overline{\nu}(\lfloor{b^{\prime}(k)}/{2}\rfloor))^{2}.

(3.9)

Set

q=\frac{\overline{\nu}(\lfloor{b^{\prime}(k)}/{2}\rfloor)}{\overline{\nu}(0)}.

(3.10)

Section
H
gives a motivation for (
3.10
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that A1 holds, that
k\geq 1
, and that
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
and
q
are given by (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and (
3.10
). Then
\hat{f}_{k}
is square-integrable and
E(\hat{f}_{k})=\mu
. Moreover,
\hat{T}_{k}\leq k+9qk
, and

\hat{T}_{k}{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq k{\rm Var}(f_{k})+8610(\overline{\nu}(0))^{2}\max\left(q,\sqrt{\frac{b^{\prime}(k)-b(k)}{k}}\right).

(3.11)

(
3.11
) gives a bound on the work-normalized variance of
\hat{f}_{k}
in terms of the work-normalized variance of
f_{k}
. The constant
8610
is an artifact of our calculations. By setting
b^{\prime}(k)=\max(b(k),\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil/2)
, it is easy to check that the second term in the RHS of (
3.11
) goes to
0
as
k
goes to infinity. Consequently, under Assumption B, for any given
\epsilon>0
, we have

\hat{T}_{k}{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq(1+\epsilon)k{\rm Var}(f_{k})

for sufficiently large
k
.
In other words, the work-normalized variance of
\hat{f}_{k}
is at most equal to that of
f_{k}
, up to the multiplicative factor
1+\epsilon
. Thus,
\hat{f}_{k}
is asymptotically at least as efficient as
f_{k}
, as
k
goes to infinity, up to a multiplicative constant arbitrarily close to
1
.

3.4
Oblivious parameters

When the sequence
\overline{\nu}
is known or can be estimated, the choices of
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
, of
q
and of
b^{\prime}(k)
in Subsection
3.3
yield an
\hat{f}_{k}
that is asymptotically at least as efficient as
f_{k}
. Under certain assumptions and without explicit knowledge of
\bar{\nu}
, this subsection provides choices of
(p_{l},l\geq 0)
, of
q
and of
b^{\prime}(k)
so that the work-normalized variance of
\hat{f}_{k}
is at most equal to that of
f_{k}
, up to a multiplicative factor arbitrarily close to
1
. We first state the following assumption.

Assumption 3 (A3).

For
l\geq 0
,

p_{l}=\frac{1}{\theta(l)2^{l}}-\frac{1}{\theta(l+1)2^{l+1}},

(3.12)

where
\theta
is an increasing function on
[0,\infty)
, with

\theta(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]
,

\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}\frac{1}{\theta(l)}<\infty.

(3.13)

Furthermore, Assumption A1 holds and

\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\sqrt{\frac{\nu(i)\theta(\log_{2}(4i+1))}{i+1}}<\infty.

(3.14)

Observe that the
p_{l}
’s given in (
3.12
) depend only on
\theta
, and that (
3.14
) is a stronger version of
(
2.4
). Standard calculations show the following.

Example 3.1
.

Suppose that, for some positive constants
c
,
\xi
and
\delta
with
\delta<\xi-1
, Assumption A1 holds with
\nu(i)=c(i+1)^{-\xi}
for
i\geq 0
, and that the
p_{l}
’s are given by (
3.12
), with

\theta(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]
, and
\theta(x)=2^{\delta(x-1)}
for
x\geq 1
. Then Assumption A3 holds and
p_{l}
is of order
2^{-(\delta+1)l}
.

Distributions with exponentially decreasing tails have been previously used in RMLMC pricing of financial derivatives
[
GlynnRhee2015unbiased
,
kahale2020Asian
]
. In Example
3.1
, the choice of the
p_{l}
’s depends on
\xi
because of the condition
\delta<\xi-1
. Example
3.2
shows that the
p_{l}
’s can chosen without any knowledge on
\xi
.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose that, for some positive constants
c
and
\xi
with
\xi>1
, Assumption A1 holds with
\nu(i)=c(i+1)^{-\xi}
for
i\geq 0
, and that the
p_{l}
’s are given by (
3.12
), with
\theta(x)=\max(1,x)^{\delta}
for
x\geq 0
, where
\delta>1
. Then Assumption A3 holds and
p_{l}
is of order
l^{-\delta}2^{-l}
.

When Assumption A2 holds, Example
3.3
shows that the
p_{l}
’s can be chosen as in Example
3.1
without any further knowledge on
\nu
.

Example 3.3
.

Suppose that Assumption A2 holds and that the
p_{l}
’s are given by (
3.12
), with

\theta(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]
, and
\theta(x)=2^{\delta(x-1)}
for
x\geq 1
, where
\delta
is a positive constant. Then Assumption A3 holds.

Suppose now that Assumption A3 holds.
For
j\geq 0
, let

\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(j):=\sum^{\infty}_{i=j}\sqrt{\frac{\nu(i)\theta(\log_{2}(4i+1))}{i+1}}.

Assumption A3 shows that
\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(j)
is finite and goes to
0
as
j
goes to infinity, and that
\overline{\nu}(j)\leq\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(j)
for
j\geq 0
.
Lemma
3.4
shows that, under Assumption A3,
Z_{k}
is an unbiased estimator of
\mu-E(f_{k,0})
, and provides a bound on its second moment and on
T_{k}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that Assumption A3 holds. Then, for
k\geq 1
, we have
E(f_{k,0}+Z_{k})=\mu
and

kE(Z_{k}^{2})\leq 28(\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(\lfloor{b^{\prime}(k)}/{2}\rfloor))^{2}.

(3.15)

Furthermore,
T_{k}\leq 3k\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}1/\theta(l)
.

Theorem
3.3
shows that, under Assumption A3,
\hat{f}_{k}
is an unbiased estimator of
\mu
and gives bounds on its running time and variance.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that Assumption A3 holds. Then, for
k\geq 1
,
\hat{f}_{k}
is square-integrable,
E(\hat{f}_{k})=\mu
, and

k{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq k{\rm Var}(f_{k})+796(\overline{\nu}(0))^{2}\sqrt{\frac{b^{\prime}(k)-b(k)}{k}}+\frac{28}{q}(\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(\lfloor{b^{\prime}(k)}/{2}\rfloor))^{2}.

(3.16)

Moreover,
\hat{T}_{k}\leq k+3(\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}1/\theta(l))qk
.

Observe that the second (resp. last) term in the right-hand side of (
3.16
) is an increasing (resp. decreasing) function of
b^{\prime}(k)
. Likewise, the bound on the variance (resp. running time) of
\hat{f}_{k}
is a decreasing (resp. increasing) function of
q
.
Theorem
3.3
shows that setting

q=\frac{\epsilon}{3\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}1/\theta(l)},

where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, ensures that
\hat{T}_{k}\leq k(1+\epsilon)
. Furthermore, if
b^{\prime}(k)=\max(b(k),\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil/2)
, then
k{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq k{\rm Var}(f_{k})+\epsilon
for sufficiently large
k
. This is because
\overline{\nu}_{\theta}(j)
goes to
0
as
j
goes to infinity.
Then, under Assumption B, for any given
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small and
k
sufficiently large, we have

\hat{T}_{k}{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq(1+\epsilon^{\prime})k{\rm Var}(f_{k}).

Here again,
\hat{f}_{k}
is asymptotically at least as efficient as
f_{k}
, as
k
goes to infinity, up to a multiplicative factor arbitrarily close to
1
. In practice, in the absence of precise knowledge on the behavior of the chain, setting
b^{\prime}(k)=\max(b(k),\lfloor\epsilon^{\prime\prime}k\rfloor)
, where
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\in(0,1/2]
, e.g.,
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=0.1
, would make
b^{\prime}(k)
reasonably large without deleting too many observations.

Under Assumption A2, and for specific values of the
p_{l}
’s, Theorem
3.4
gives a bound on the variance of
\hat{f}_{k}
that depends explicitly on
c
and
\xi
. It also provides an improved variance bound under an additional decay assumption on
\nu
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that Assumption A2 holds, and that the
p_{l}
’s are given by (
3.12
), with
\theta(x)=\max(1,x)^{\delta}
for
x\geq 0
, where
\delta\in(1,2]
. Then, for
k\geq 1
, Assumption A3 holds,

\hat{T}_{k}\leq k(1+\frac{9q}{\delta-1}),

(3.17)

and

k{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq k{\rm Var}(f_{k})+\frac{Ac}{\xi}\sqrt{\frac{b^{\prime}(k)-b(k)}{k}}+\frac{Ac}{q\xi}\min\left(\ln^{\delta}\left(\frac{3}{\xi}\right),\frac{e^{-\xi b^{\prime}(k)/2}}{\xi}\right),

(3.18)

where
A
is an absolute constant. Moreover, if
\nu(i)\leq c/(i+1)
for
i\geq 0
, then

k{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})\leq k{\rm Var}(f_{k})+A^{\prime}c\ln^{2}\left(\frac{3}{\xi}\right)\sqrt{\frac{b^{\prime}(k)-b(k)}{k}}+\frac{A^{\prime}c}{q}\min\left(\ln^{\delta+2}\left(\frac{3}{\xi}\right),\frac{e^{-\xi b^{\prime}(k)/2}}{\xi^{2}}\right),

(3.19)

where

is an absolute constant.

The second term in the RHS of (

, while the second term in the RHS of (

. Both terms can be made arbitrarily small by setting

, with

sufficiently small. For fixed

, the last term in the RHS of (

) is uniformly bounded by a term of order

, up to a polylogarithmic factor, while the last term in the RHS of (
3.19
) is uniformly bounded by a term with a logarithmic dependence on
\xi
.
When

is proportional to

, both terms decrease exponentially with

.

Remark 3.1
.

The results of Theorem
3.4
are still valid if the constraint
\delta\in(1,2]
is replaced with
\delta\in(1,\delta_{0}]
, for any fixed

, and if

and

and the constant

in (

3.5
A stratified unbiased estimator

Given
n,k\geq 1
and
q\in(0,1)
, let

\tilde{f}_{k,n}:=\tilde{f}_{k}+\tilde{Z}_{k},

where
\tilde{f}_{k}
is the average of
n
independent copies of
f_{k,0}
and
\tilde{Z}_{k}
is the average of
\lceil nq\rceil
independent copies of
Z_{k}
. The estimator
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
is a stratified version of
\hat{f}_{k}
and has similar properties. By Lemma
3.4
, under Assumption 3,

E(\tilde{f}_{k,n})=E(f_{k,0})+E(Z_{k})=\mu.

Furthermore,

{\rm Var}(\tilde{f}_{k,n})=\frac{{\rm Var}(f_{k,0})}{n}+\frac{{\rm Var}(Z_{k})}{\lceil nq\rceil}.

On the other hand, it follows from the definition of
\hat{f}_{k}
that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(f_{k,0})+q^{-2}{\rm Var}(QZ^{\prime}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(f_{k,0})+q^{-1}E(Z_{k}^{2})-E(Z_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(f_{k,0})+q^{-1}{\rm Var}(Z_{k}).

Thus,
n{\rm Var}(\tilde{f}_{k,n})\leq{\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})
. The expected time to simulate
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
is
\tilde{T}_{k,n}=nk+\lceil nq\rceil T_{k}
. Thus
\tilde{T}_{k,n}\leq n\hat{T}_{k}+T_{k}
and
\tilde{T}_{k,n}{\rm Var}(\tilde{f}_{k,n})\leq(\hat{T}_{k}+T_{k}/n){\rm Var}(\hat{f}_{k})
. Consequently, as
n
goes to infinity, the estimator
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
is asymptotically at least as efficient as
\hat{f}_{k}
.

4.1
GARCH volatility model

In the GARCH(1,1) volatility model (see
[

, Ch. 23]
), the daily volatility
\sigma_{i}
of an index or exchange rate, calculated at the end of day

{\sigma_{i+1}}^{2}=w+\alpha{\sigma_{i}}^{2}U_{i}^{2}+\beta{\sigma_{i}}^{2},

i\geq 0

w
,
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive constants with
\alpha+\beta<1
, and
(U_{i},i\geq 0)
are independent standard Gaussian random variables. At the end of day
0

\sigma_{0}\geq 0
and a real number

, we want to estimate
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\Pr(\sigma_{i}^{2}>z)
, if such a limit exists. In this example,
F=F^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}
, with
X_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
and
g(x,u)=w+\alpha xu^{2}+\beta x
, and
f(u)={\bf 1}\{u>z\}
for
u\in\mathbb{R}
. The proof of
[
kahaRandomizedDimensionReduction20
, Proposition 9]
implies (
2.6
) with
x=0
and

\nu^{\prime}(i)=c(\alpha+\beta)^{i/2}
for
i\geq 0
, for some constant
c
. Thus,
Assumption A2 holds.

GI/G/1
queue

Consider a

Our approach can also estimate

Conclusion

Under a coupling assumption, we have established bounds on the bias, variance and mean square error of standard time-average estimators, and shown the sharpness of the variance and mean square error bounds. We have built an unbiased RMLMC estimator for the bias of a conventional time-average estimator. Combining this unbiased estimator with a conventional time-average estimator yields an unbiased estimator
\hat{f}_{k}
of
\mu
. Both unbiased estimators are square-integrable and have finite expected running time. Under certain conditions, they can be built without any precomputations. For a suitable choice of parameters, we have shown that
\hat{f}_{k}
is asymptotically at least as efficient as
f_{k}
, up to a multiplicative factor arbitrarily close to
1
. We have also constructed an efficient stratified version
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
of
\hat{f}_{k}
. Building more refined stratified versions of
\hat{f}_{k}
, such as those in
[
Vihola2018
]
, is left for future research. Our approach permits to estimate
the bias of
f_{k}
and to determine the number of time-steps needed to substantially reduce it. It can be implemented in a parallelized fashion and allows the robust construction of confidence intervals for
\mu
. We have provided examples in volatility forecasting, queues, and the simulation of high-dimensional Gaussian vectors where our approach is provably efficient, even when
f
is discontinuous. Our numerical experiments are consistent with our theoretical findings. In our experiments, the value of
q
is fixed
and
f_{k}
is about twice as efficient as
\hat{f}_{k}
and
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
when
k
is sufficiently large. As per the discussion following Theorem
3.3
, for large values of
k
, the performance of
\hat{f}_{k}
and of
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
should increase if
q
decreases. In practice, though, determining the optimal value of
q
for a given
k
may require a large amount of pre-computations. For
\tilde{f}_{k,n}
, for instance, it can be shown that the optimal value of
q
depends on the variance of
Z_{k}
, that is not easy to estimate accurately for large values of
k
.
