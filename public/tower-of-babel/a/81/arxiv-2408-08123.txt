Single-loop methods for bilevel parameter learning in inverse imaging
Ensio Suonperä, Tuomo Valkonen

Abstract
Bilevel optimisation is used in inverse imaging problems for hyperparameter learning/identification and experimental design, for instance, to find optimal regularisation parameters and forward operators. However, computationally, the process is costly. To reduce this cost, recently so-called single-loop approaches have been introduced. On each step of an outer optimisation method, they take just a single gradient step towards the solution of the inner problem. In this paper, we flexibilise the inner algorithm to include standard methods in inverse imaging. Moreover, as we have recently shown, significant performance improvements can be obtained in PDE-constrained optimisation by interweaving the steps of conventional iterative linear system solvers with the optimisation method. We now demonstrate how the adjoint equation in bilevel problems can also benefit from such interweaving. We evaluate the performance of our approach on identifying the deconvolution kernel for image deblurring, and the subsampling operator for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}\|u_{i}-z_{i}\|^{2}+\beta\|\alpha\|_{1}\quad\text{where each}\quad u_{i}\in\argmin_{u}\frac{1}{2}\|A_{\alpha}u-b_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda\mathrm{TV}(u),

However, bilevel optimisation can be very expensive. At the outset, it requires solving the inner problem
\min F_{u}
—in our domain of interest, a possibly expensive inverse problem itself—several times to find the desired optimal parameters.
Therefore, in recent years, there has been a growing interest—also from machine learning, where bilevel optimisation has relevance to adversary learning
[
35
]
—in developing “single-loop” methods, that on each step of an algorithm to solve the outer problem (
1

single step
of a conventional optimisation method towards a minimiser of the inner objective (
2

[
40

6

26

33

46

. There are also similar methods that take more than one but only a fixed (small) number of inner iterations for each outer iteration
[
30

29

18

32
]
, while
[
37

16
]
take an adaptive number of inner iterations.

In
[
28
]
, we introduced the single-loop approach to nonsmooth PDE-constrained optimisation, such as total-variation regularised electrical impedance tomography (EIT) reconstruction, avoiding solving the PDE on each step of the optimisation method, and instead taking a single step of a conventional linear system solver (Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, conjugate gradients) on each step of the optimisation method. This achieved significant speedups.

We now want to apply such single-loop approaches to the adjoint equations that also surface in bilevel optimisation.
Moreover, compared to the aforementioned works on single-loop bilevel optimisation methods, that generally use gradient descent for the inner problem, we want to use methods that are applicable to inverse problems with nonsmooth total variation regularisation. Gradient descent only applies to smooth problems. The next step from gradient descent is forward-backward splitting, which is applicable to simple total variation regularised denoising through a dual formulation. However, when both the data term and the regularisation term involve a difficult operator, neither forward-backward is applicable
[
9
]
.
A popular choice then is the primal-dual proximal splitting method (PDPS) of Chambolle and Pock
[
5
]
.
We want to use (single steps of) this method for the inner problem, yet remain flexible
towards other alternatives as well.

That said, due to inherent difficulties in bilevel optimisation, in this work we cannot yet allow for a nonsmooth inner problem.
Indeed, due to the use of the adjoint equation, we require the inner problem to be twice differentiable. Some recent works
[
32
,
34
]
avoid second-order derivatives in the algorithm through the value function reformulation, but, nevertheless, require significant second-order and other differentiability assumptions for the convergence theory.

An abstract tracking approach

We then introduce and discuss in

We continue by stating the assumptions of the abstract method in

2.1
Problem description

We slightly generalise the problem formulation (
1
), and seek to solve

\min_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}\penalty\ J(S_{u}(\alpha))+R(\alpha)\quad\text{subject to $S_{u}(\alpha)\in U$ satisfying}\quad 0=G(S_{u}(\alpha);\alpha),

(4)

where, on Hilbert spaces

and
\mathscr{A}
,
J:U\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and Fréchet differentiable,
R:\mathscr{A}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is convex, proper, and lower semicontinuous, and
G:U\times\mathscr{A}\to U
.
We call
S_{u}:\mathscr{A}\to U
the solution mapping.
We will generally assume it to be well-defined (and single-valued) on the effective domain of
R
, i.e., in
\Dom R:=\{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}\mid R(\alpha)<\infty\}
.
Of all the function, we will also be making further assumptions, including differeniability assumptions, in
Section

2.4

Taking

for sufficiently regular

, (
4
) is a necessary condition for (
1
).
If
F
is convex in
u
, this condition is also sufficient.
However, we want to allow for other optimality conditions, such as the primal-dual conditions that arise from the Frenchel–Rockafellar theorem, and that our used in the context of primal-dual methods (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
).
We therefore allow for abstract optimality conditions for the inner problem via a general
G
.

2.2
Optimality conditions and the adjoint equation

Assume that both
G
and
S_{u}
are Fréchet differentiable.
Suppose a solution
S_{u}(\alpha)
exists for all
\alpha
near
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathscr{A}
.
Then
G(S_{u}(\alpha);\alpha)=0
for all such
\alpha
, so by implicit differentiation

0=\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\nabla_{u}G(S_{u}(\alpha),\alpha)+\nabla_{\alpha}G(S_{u}(\alpha),\alpha).

That is,
p=\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)
solves for
u=S_{u}(\alpha)
the
adjoint equation

0=p\nabla_{u}G(u,\alpha)+\nabla_{\alpha}G(u,\alpha).

(5)

We introduce the corresponding solution mapping for the
adjoint variable

p
,

S_{p}(u,\alpha):=-\nabla_{\alpha}G(u;\alpha)\left(\nabla_{u}G(u;\alpha)\right)^{-1}.

(6)

We will later make assumptions that ensure that
S_{p}
is well-defined. Then

\nabla S_{u}(\alpha)=S_{p}(S_{u}(\alpha),\alpha).

Since
S_{u}:\mathscr{A}\to U
, the Fréchet derivative
S_{u}^{\prime}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{L}(\mathscr{A};U)
and the Hilbert adjoint
\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{L}(U;\mathscr{A})
for all
\alpha
. Consequently
p\in\mathbb{L}(U;\mathscr{A}).

By the sum rule for Clarke subdifferentials (denoted
\partial_{C}
) and their compatibility with convex subdifferentials and Fréchet differentiable functions
[
8
]
, we obtain

\partial_{C}(J\circ S_{u}+R)(\widehat{\alpha})=\nabla_{\alpha}(J\circ S_{u})(\widehat{\alpha})+\partial R(\widehat{\alpha})=\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\widehat{\alpha})\nabla_{u}J(S_{u}(\widehat{\alpha}))+\partial R(\widehat{\alpha}).

The Fermat principle for Clarke or Mordukhovich subdifferentials then furnishes the necessary optimality condition

0\in\nabla_{\alpha}(J\circ S_{u})(\widehat{\alpha})+\partial R(\widehat{\alpha})=\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\widehat{\alpha})\nabla_{u}J(S_{u}(\widehat{\alpha}))+\partial R(\widehat{\alpha}).

(7)

We combine the inner optimality condition
G(S_{u}(\alpha);\alpha)=0
, the adjoint equation (
5
), and the outer optimality condition (
7
) as the inclusion

\displaystyle 0\in H(\widehat{u},\widehat{p},\widehat{\alpha})

(8a)

with

\displaystyle H(u,p,\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}G(u;\alpha)\\
p\nabla_{u}G(u;\alpha)+\nabla_{\alpha}G(u;\alpha)\\
p\nabla_{u}J(u)+\partial R(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in U,p\in\mathbb{L}(U;\mathscr{A}),\alpha\in\mathscr{A}.

(8b)

This is the optimality condition that our proposed methods attempt to satisfy.

Assumption
\thedefinition

The assumption holds for smooth functions

Remark 2.1

The condition

Remark 2.2

(Existence and differentiability of the solution map)
.

The existence and differentiability of a

such that

needs to be explicitly verified.
When

, Fermat’s principle proves that
\min_{u}F(u,\alpha)
has a solution.
That in turn, follows from lower semicontinuity and coercivity.

Suppose then that

and

we have

invertible.
Then the implicit function theorem shows the existence of a unique continuously differentiable

in a neighborhood of

Such an

is also Lipschitz in a neighborhood of
\alpha

If

is finite-dimensional, a compactness argument gluing together the neighborhoods then proves the continuity and Lipschitz properties of

In

(left-invertibility of

3
Splitting methods for the inner problem

In
Section

3.1
we present a general framework for optimisation algorithms that can be used for solving the inner problem. We also give examples of algorithms following the framework. In

3.1
A general approach

Let
U
and
\mathscr{A}
be Hilbert spaces, and
G=T+W
for some

.
Then

We assume that such a solution exists for all

see
Remark

2.2
.
2
2

2

We do not here at the outset assume unique solutions, although we will later, in practise, need to impose uniqueness. In principle, for
Section

2

and a corresponding tracking inequality is enough.

We seek to move towards

with preconditioned forward-backward type algorithms that, for fixed

, solve the next iterate

be convex, proper and lower semicontinuous in their first parameter,
f
also differentiable with respect to its first parameter.
Then
Eq.

23
with
T=\nabla_{u}f
and
W=\partial_{u}g
is a necessary and sufficient optimality condition for solution of
\min_{u\in U}f(u;\alpha)+g(u;\alpha)
.
Choosing
Q=\tau^{-1}\Id,
the implicit form
Eq.

24
corresponds to the forward-backward splitting

u^{k+1}=\prox_{\tau g(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,;\alpha)}(u^{k}-\tau\nabla f(u^{k};\alpha))

(25)

Example 3.2

(Primal-dual proximal splitting, PDPS)
.

Let
X,Y
and
\mathscr{A}
be Hilbert spaces,
f_{0},e:X\times\mathscr{A}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
g:Y\times\mathscr{A}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be convex, proper, and lower semicontinuous in their first parameter,
e
with an
L
-Lipschitz gradient with respect to its first parameter.
Define
f:=f_{0}+e
, suppose
K\in\mathbb{L}(X;Y)
, and consider the problem

\min_{x}f(x,\alpha)+g(Kx;\alpha).

This can be equivalently written as the saddle point problem

\min_{x}\max_{y}f(x;\alpha)+\langle Kx,y\rangle-g^{*}(y;\alpha).

Following
[
21
,
9
, Theorem 5.11]
and writing
u=(x,y)
, this problem has primal-dual optimality conditions of the form
Eq.

23
with

T(u;\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f_{0}(x;\alpha)+K^{*}y\\
\omega^{-1}[\partial_{y}g^{*}(y;\alpha)-Kx]\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad W(u;\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}e(x;\alpha)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(26)

for any
\omega>0
.
Defining for step length parameters
\tau_{x},\tau_{y}>0
the preconditioning operator

Q:=\begin{pmatrix}\tau^{-1}_{x}\Id&-K^{*}\\
-K&\omega^{-1}\tau^{-1}_{y}\Id\end{pmatrix},

(27)

Eq.

24
then becomes an implicit form of the primal-dual proximal splitting (PDPS) method of
[
5
]
, here with an additional forward step with respect to
e
:

\begin{cases}x^{k+1}&=\prox_{\tau_{x}f_{0}(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,;\alpha)}(x^{k}-\tau_{x}(K^{*}y^{k}+\nabla_{x}e(x^{k};\alpha)),\\
y^{k+1}&=\prox_{\tau_{y}g^{*}(\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,;\alpha)}(y^{k}+\tau_{y}K((1+\omega)x^{k+1}-\omega x^{k})).\end{cases}

(28)

The method converges (for fixed
\alpha
) if
\tau_{x}L/2+\tau_{x}\tau_{y}\|K\|^{2}\leq 1
and
\omega=1
, or is chosen to appropriately reflect available strong convexity; see, e.g.,
[
9
]
.

Further algorithms fitting the general framework
Eq.

24
can be found in
[
9
,
42
]
.

3.2
Tracking estimates

We now prove inner problem tracking estimates for the above methods.
To do so, we start with a result for abstract methods of the form
Eq.

24
.
It requires introducing abstract forms of monotonicity.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
\tilde{X}\subset X.

We say that
T:X\to X
is
(\Gamma_{T}
-strongly
)
monotone
for some self-adjoint and positive semi-definite
\Gamma_{T}\in\mathbb{L}(X;X)
at
\widehat{x}\in\tilde{X}
if

\langle T(x)-T(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle\geq\|x-\widehat{x}\|_{\Gamma_{T}}^{2}\quad(x\in\tilde{X}).

(29)

If this holds for all
\widehat{x}\in\tilde{X},
we say that
T
is (
\Gamma_{T}
-strongly) monotone in
\tilde{X}.

Moreover, we say that
W:X\to X
is (
\Gamma_{W}
-strongly
)
three-point monotone
at
\widehat{x}\in\tilde{X}
with respect to a self-adjoint and positive semi-definite
\Gamma_{W},\Lambda\in\mathbb{L}(X;X)
if

\langle W(z)-W(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle\geq\|x-\widehat{x}\|_{\Gamma_{W}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\|x-z\|_{\Lambda}^{2}\quad(x,z\in\tilde{X}).

(30)

If this holds for all
\widehat{x}\in\tilde{X},
we say that
W
is (
\Gamma_{W}
-strongly) three-point monotone with respect to
\Lambda
in
\tilde{X}.

When
\Gamma_{T}=\gamma\Id,\Gamma_{P}=0
and
\Lambda=L\Id
, these operator-relative properties reduce to standard strong and three-point monotonicity, see
[
9
, Cororolary 7.2]
.

We now prove the promised tracking estimate result for general splitting methods for the inner problem.
The Lipschitz properties required from
S_{u}
, were discussed in
Remark

2.2
.

.

Let

4
Splitting methods for the adjoint

We now develop splitting methods for solving the adjoint equation based on conventional iterative splitting methods for linear systems.
In
Section

4.1
we present the overall approach and prove a perturbed contractivity property. We also provide example of splittings that satisfy the relevant conditions.
In
Section

4.2
we then prove the adjoint tracking estimate based on the perturbed contractivity property.

4.1
Operator splitting methods for linear systems

Let
U
and
\mathscr{A}
be Hilbert spaces.
We need to find
p^{k+1}\in\mathbb{L}(U;\mathscr{A})
that approximately solves the adjoint equation

p^{k+1}\nabla_{u}G(u^{k+1},\alpha^{k})+\nabla_{\alpha}G(u^{k+1},\alpha^{k})=0

(33)

to such a precision that adjoint tracking property
Section

2.4

Item

(iii)
holds.
Dropping the iteration indices for brevity, this is a linear equation of the form

\displaystyle A_{v}p=b_{v}

(34)

with

\displaystyle v=(u,\alpha),\quad A_{v}p:=p\nabla_{u}G(u,\alpha),\quad\text{and}\quad b_{v}=-\nabla_{\alpha}G(u,\alpha).

(35)

We write this section in general Hilbert spaces
V
and
P
, which in the overall setting of this paper equal
U\times\mathscr{A}
and
\mathbb{L}(U;\mathscr{A})
.
For some
v\in D\subset V
and
A_{v}\in\mathbb{L}(P;P)
, let us thus be given the splitting

A_{v}=N_{v}+M_{v}

(36)

with
N_{v}
invertible.
Given the previous iterate
p^{k}\in P
, a linear system splitting method takes a step towards solution of equation
Eq.

34
by solving
p^{k+1}
from

N_{v}p^{k+1}+M_{v}p^{k}=b_{v}.

(37)

The next lemma is adapted from
[
28
]
, where squared tracking estimates were needed for PDE-constrained optimisation. We need non-squared estimates.
The condition (
38
) is a Lipschitz condition on
A_{v}
, which for (
33
) becomes a Lipschitz condition on
\nabla_{u}G
; see (
35
).
The condition (
39
) is a standard contractivity condition on the splitting scheme; see
[
19
, Theorem 10.1.1]
.
It obviously holds for the trivial splitting
N_{v}=A_{v}
and
M_{v}=0
if
A_{v}
is positive definite. For (
33
) this again means that
\nabla_{u}G
is positive definite.
We provides examples of other splitting schemes after the lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
V
and
P
be Hilbert spaces, and
A_{v}\in\mathbb{L}(P;P)
for all
v\in D\subset V
.
Suppose
v\mapsto b_{v}:V\to P
is
L_{b}
-Lipschitz, and for some
L_{A}\geq 0
that

\|A_{v}-A_{\tilde{v}}\|\leq L_{A}\|v-\tilde{v}\|\quad(v,\tilde{v}\in D).

(38)

Let us be given a splitting
Eq.

36

with
N_{v}
invertible, and such that

\|N^{-1}_{v}M_{v}\|\leq\zeta\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{N}\|N^{-1}_{v}\|\leq 1

(39)

for some
\zeta\in[0,1)
and
\gamma_{N}>0
.
Suppose
\breve{p}
solves
N_{v}\breve{p}+M_{v}p=b_{v}
for a given
p\in P.

Then for any
v,\bar{v}\in D
and
\bar{p}\in P
satisfying

A_{\bar{v}}\bar{p}=b_{\bar{v}},

we have

\zeta\|p-\bar{p}\|\geq\|\breve{p}-\bar{p}\|-(L_{A}\|\bar{p}\|+L_{b})\gamma^{-1}_{N}\|v-\bar{v}\|.

(40)

Proof 4.2
.

Using (
37
) with
A_{\bar{v}}\bar{p}=b_{\bar{v}}
and
A_{v}\bar{p}=N_{v}\bar{p}+M_{v}\bar{p}
, we expand

\displaystyle\breve{p}-\bar{p}

\displaystyle=N^{-1}_{v}(b_{v}-M_{v}p)-\bar{p}

\displaystyle=N^{-1}_{v}[b_{v}-b_{\bar{v}}]+N^{-1}_{v}(A_{\bar{v}}-A_{v})\bar{p}-N^{-1}_{v}M_{v}(p-\bar{p}).

Taking the norm and using the triangle inequality, the Lipschitz assumption on
b
, as well as (
38
), therefore

\|\breve{p}-\bar{p}\|\leq\|N^{-1}_{v}\|(L_{A}\|\bar{p}\|+L_{b})\|\bar{v}-v\|+\|N^{-1}_{v}M_{v}\|\|p-\bar{p}\|.

Further using (
39
), we obtain (
40
).

If the parameters are not perturbed, we immediately get linear convergence:

Corollary 4.3
.

If
v=\bar{v}
in
Lemma

4.1
, then

\|\breve{p}-\bar{p}\|\leq\zeta\|p-\bar{p}\|.

We next present example slitting methods, and conditions that guarantee (
39
).

Example 4.4

(Jacobi splitting)
.

If
N_{v}
is the diagonal of a matrix
A_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we obtain Jacobi splitting.
The first part of (
39
) reduces to strict diagonal dominance, see
[
19
, §10.1]
. The second part always holds and
N_{v}
is invertible when the main diagonal of
A_{v}
has only positive entries. Then
\gamma_{N}
is the minimum of the diagonal values.

Example 4.5

(Gauss–Seidel splitting)
.

If
N_{v}
is the lower triangle and diagonal of a matrix
A_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we obtain Gauss–Seidel splitting.
The first inequality of (
39
) holds for some
\zeta\in[0,1)
when
A_{v}
is symmetric and positive definite. This follows similarly to
[
19
, proof of Theorem 10.1.2]
.
The second inequality holds for some
\gamma_{N}
when
N_{v}
is invertible, i.e., has no zeros on the main diagonal.

Example 4.6

(Identity splitting)
.

If
\gamma\Id\leq A_{v}
and
\|A_{v}\|\leq L
for
\gamma,L>0,
and

Indeed,

\|N^{-1}_{v}M_{v}\|^{2}=\sup_{x,\|x\|\leq 1}\langle(\Id-\theta A_{v})x,(\Id-\theta A_{v})x\rangle\leq 1+\theta^{2}\|A\|^{2}-2\theta\inf_{x,\|x\|\leq 1}\langle Ax,x\rangle\leq 1+\theta^{2}L^{2}-2\theta\gamma.

Example 4.7

(No splitting)
.

If
N_{v}=A_{v}
, (
39
) holds with
\zeta=0
and
\gamma_{N}
the minimal eigenvalue of
A_{v}
, assumed symmetric positive definite.

Example 4.8

(Block Gauss–Seidel)
.

Let

A_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},\,N_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}N_{11}&0\\
A_{21}&N_{22}\end{pmatrix},\,\text{ and }\,M_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}&A_{12}\\

with

and

and

invertible and bounded.
Suppose that

\|N^{-1}_{22}(M_{22}-A_{21}N^{-1}_{11}A_{12})\|^{2}+\|N^{-1}_{22}(M_{22}-A_{21}N^{-1}_{11}A_{12})\|\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|\|N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}\|\\
+\|N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}\|^{2}(1+\|N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|)(1+\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2})+\|N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|^{2}+\|N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|\|N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}\|\leq\zeta^{2}.

) holds, as we prove in

.

\|A_{22}^{-1}A_{21}A^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|^{2}+\|A^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|^{2}\leq\zeta^{2}.

(42)

This holds, in particular, when the diagonal blocks of

are invertible and large compared to the off-diagonal blocks.
Moreover, there exists
\gamma_{N}
satisfying (

) when both

and

Example 4.9
.

Let

as in

\kappa_{p}=\zeta^{-1},\quad C_{S}=\sup_{\alpha\in V_{\alpha}}\zeta^{-1}(L_{A}\|\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}+L_{b}),\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{p}=L_{\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}}.

Proof 4.11
.

By

4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta\|p^{k}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}\geq\|p^{k+1}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}-(L_{A}\|\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}+L_{b})\gamma^{-1}_{N}\|u-S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q}.

Rearranging terms and multiplying by
\zeta^{-1}
gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{p}\|p^{k+1}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}\leq\|p^{k}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}+C_{S}\|u-S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q}.

The triangle inequality and Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}
gives

\displaystyle\|p^{k}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|p^{k}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\breve{\alpha})\|_{Q^{-1}}+\|\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\breve{\alpha})-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|p^{k}-\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\breve{\alpha})\|_{Q^{-1}}+L_{\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}}\|\breve{\alpha}-\alpha\|.

Combining the previous estimates finishes the proof.

We needed to assume that the solution mapping of the inner problem is Lipschitz continuously differentiable to prove the adjoint tracking estimates. We discuss next the reasonability of this assumption. If the inner and adjoint solution mappings
S_{u}
and
S_{p}
are Lipschitz, then also
\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}
is since

\nabla_{\alpha}S_{u}(\alpha)=S_{p}(S_{u}(\alpha),\alpha).

The Lipschitz properties of
S_{u}
are discussed in

.
We next prove the Lipschitz continuity of
S_{p}
under similar assumption as in

.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
U
and
\mathscr{A}
be Hilbert spaces.
Let further

be positive definite, and
\nabla G:U\times\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{L}(U;U\times\mathscr{A})
be invertible and Lipschitz continuous with constant

in some bounded closed set
V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}.

Moreover, assume that
\|(\nabla_{u}G(u;\alpha))^{-1}\|\leq\gamma_{G}^{-1}

in
V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}
for some
\gamma_{G}>0
.
Then
S_{p}
defined in
Eq.

6
is Lipschitz in
V_{u}\times V_{\alpha},
i.e.

\|S_{p}(u_{1},\alpha_{1})-S_{p}(u_{2},\alpha_{2})\|_{Q^{-1}}\leq L_{S_{p}}(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{Q}+\|\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\|)

for
u_{1},u_{2}\in V_{u}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in V_{\alpha}
with the factor

L_{S_{p}}:=\gamma_{G}^{-1}L_{\nabla G}\Bigl(1+\gamma_{G}^{-1}\max_{(u,\alpha)\in V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}}\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u,\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}\Bigr).

Proof 4.13
.

Using the definition of
S_{p}
in (
6
), we rearrange

\displaystyle S_{p}(u_{1},\alpha_{1})-S_{p}(u_{2},\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})\right)(\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1}))^{-1}

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})\left((\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1}))^{-1}-(\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2}))^{-1}\right).

Since
Q
is positive definite, hence invertible, we have
\|pu\|_{\mathscr{A}}=\|(pQ^{-1/2})(Q^{1/2}u)\|\leq\|p\|_{Q^{-1}}\|u\|_{Q}
. This and the triangle inequality give

\displaystyle\|S_{p}(u_{1},\alpha_{1})-S_{p}(u_{2},\alpha_{2})\|_{Q^{-1}}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\|(\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1}))^{-1}\|\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})\|_{Q^{-1}}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})\|_{Q^{-1}}\|(\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1}))^{-1}-(\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2}))^{-1}\|=:E_{1}+E_{2}.

The bound
\|(\nabla_{u}G(u;\alpha))^{-1}\|\leq\gamma_{G}^{-1}
and the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla G
in
V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}
give

E_{1}\leq\gamma_{G}^{-1}L_{\nabla_{\alpha}G}\left(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{Q}+\|\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\|\right).

(44)

Towards estimating the second term on the right hand side of (

), we observe that

A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}BB^{-1}-A^{-1}AB^{-1}=A^{-1}(A-B)B^{-1}

for any invertible linear operators
A,B
.
This and Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla G
give

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})^{-1}(\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2}))\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})\|_{Q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\max_{(u,\alpha)\in V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}}\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u,\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}\right)\|\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\cdot\|\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2})^{-1}\|\|(\nabla_{u}G(u_{1};\alpha_{1})-\nabla_{u}G(u_{2};\alpha_{2}))\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{G}^{-2}L_{\nabla G}\left(\max_{(u,\alpha)\in V_{u}\times V_{\alpha}}\|\nabla_{\alpha}G(u,\alpha)\|_{Q^{-1}}\right)\left(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{Q}+\|\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\|\right).

Inserting this inequality and (

) into (

) establishes the claim since

is bounded in

by the Lipschitz continuity of

Appendix A
Three-point monotonicity

We needed the following three-point monotonicity property in
Lemma

2.10
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
X
be Banach space and let
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
be strongly convex with factor
\gamma
(in a set
A\subset X
) as well as Lipschitz differentiable with constant
L>0
(in
A
). Then for any
t>0,

\displaystyle\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma-tL}{2}\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma-tL}{2}\|z-\widehat{x}\|^{2}-\frac{L}{4t}\|x-z\|^{2}\quad(\text{for all }x,z,\widehat{x}\in A).

Proof A.2
.

The proof is adapted from
[
42
]
.
We have

\displaystyle\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\langle DF(x)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle+\langle DF(z)-DF(x),x-\widehat{x}\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),z-\widehat{x}\rangle+\langle DF(z)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-z\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I_{1}+\frac{1}{2}I_{2}.

The strong monotonicity and Lipschitz continuity of
DF
with Young’s inequality yield

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle DF(x)-DF(\widehat{x}),x-\widehat{x}\rangle+\langle DF(z)-DF(x),x-\widehat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\gamma\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}-\|DF(z)-DF(x)\|\|x-\widehat{x}\|\geq\gamma\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}-tL\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}-\frac{L}{4t}\|x-z\|^{2}.

Inserting this and an analogous estimate for
I_{2}
into
Eq.

49
establishes the claim.

Appendix B
Lipschitz selections of the solution map

We now continue from
Remark

2.2
to treat the existence and differentiability of the solution map

of the inner problem.
The next lemma is a an implicit function variant of
[

.
It relaxes the invertibility restriction on

in the standard implicit function theorem.

Lemma B.1
.

On Banach spaces
U
,
\mathscr{A}
, and

, suppose

of

Proof B.2
.

Let
A:=G_{u}(u;\alpha)
and
A^{\dagger}:=G_{u}(u;\alpha)^{\dagger}
.
Then
P:=\Id-A^{\dagger}A
is a projection into

We further define

\bar{G}:U\times\mathscr{A}\to Y\times\ker G_{u}(u;\alpha),\qquad\bar{G}(\tilde{u};\tilde{\alpha}):=(G(\tilde{u};\tilde{\alpha}),P\tilde{u})\quad\text{for all}\ (\tilde{u},\tilde{\alpha})\in U\times\mathscr{A},

Then for all
\Delta u\in U
, we have

M\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)\Delta u=A^{\dagger}A\Delta u+P\Delta u=\Delta u.

Thus

is a left-inverse of

.
Since
\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)\Delta u=(A\Delta u,P\Delta u)
for all
\Delta u\in U
, similarly, for all

, we have

\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)M(\tilde{y},\tilde{q})=(AA^{\dagger}\tilde{y}+A\tilde{q},PA^{\dagger}\tilde{y}+P\tilde{q})=(AA^{\dagger}\tilde{y},P\tilde{q})=(\tilde{y},\tilde{q}),

which shows that
M
is also the right-inverse of
\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)
on
Y\times\ker F^{\prime}(x)
.
Hence
\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)
is bijective.
By the implicit function theorem, e.g.,
[

of

\displaystyle=\bar{G}_{u}(u;\alpha)^{-1}\bar{G}_{\alpha}(u;\alpha)=M\bar{G}_{\alpha}(u;\alpha)=M(G_{\alpha}(u;\alpha),0)

\displaystyle=A^{\dagger}G_{\alpha}(u;\alpha)=G_{u}(u;\alpha)^{\dagger}G_{\alpha}(u;\alpha).

.

.

(i)

Proof B.4
.

to all

We prove here an estimate that was needed to derive the tracking inequality for block Gauss–Seidel splitting in

Lemma C.1

Proof C.2

.

Let

.

\geq\frac{\|N_{11}\|^{2}}{\|N_{11}\|^{2}+\|N_{22}\|^{2}(1+\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2})}=:\beta^{*}.

Therefore
\rho\|N^{-1}_{v}\|^{2}\leq 1
for
\rho=\beta^{*}\|N_{22}\|^{2}/(1+\beta^{*})
and we get

\rho=\frac{\beta^{*}\|N_{22}\|^{2}}{\beta^{*}+1}=\frac{\|N_{11}\|^{2}\|N_{22}\|^{2}}{2\|N_{11}\|^{2}+\|N_{22}\|^{2}(1+\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2})}\geq\frac{\|N_{11}\|^{2}\|N_{22}\|^{2}}{\left(2\|N_{11}\|+\|N_{22}\|(1+\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2})\right)^{2}}

Taking the square root, we see that
\gamma_{N}\|N^{-1}\|\leq 1
for any

\gamma_{N}\leq\|N_{11}\|\|N_{22}\|/(2\|N_{11}\|+\|N_{22}\|(1+\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2})).

Likewise we have

N^{-1}_{v}M_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}&N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\\
-N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}&-N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}+N^{-1}_{22}M_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly as in

if and only if, for some

\displaystyle\left(1+\eta^{-1}_{1}+(1+\eta^{-1}_{2})\|N^{-1}_{22}A_{21}\|^{2}\right)\|N^{-1}_{11}M_{11}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta^{2}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(1+\eta_{1})\|N^{-1}_{11}A_{12}\|^{2}+(1+\eta_{2})\|N^{-1}_{22}(M_{22}-A_{21}N^{-1}_{11}A_{12})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta^{2}.

Lemma D.1
.

Let

\prox_{\sigma R}(\tilde{a})=(\max\{0,\tilde{\alpha}_{1}\},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4})

\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
, and
\alpha_{4}
are solved from the system of linear equations

Proof D.2
.

The claim follows directly from the definition of proximal operator and the corresponding first order optimality condition

\partial_{\alpha_{j}}\left[\sigma\beta(\sum_{i=2}^{4}\alpha_{i}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{4}(\alpha_{i}-\tilde{\alpha}_{i})^{2}\right]=0\quad\text{for }j=2,3,4.

The next result on our sparsity regulariser for MRI sampling pattern weights has a lot of resemblance to simplex projection methods, see, for example,

Lemma D.3
.

For some weight vector
w\in(0,\infty)^{n}
and
\beta,M>0
, let

R(\alpha):=\beta\varphi(w^{\top}\alpha)+\delta_{[0,\infty)^{n}}(\alpha),\quad\varphi(t)=t+\delta_{(-\infty,M]}(t).

Then, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

[\prox_{\tau R}(\tilde{\alpha})]_{i}=\max(0,{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}-w_{i}\tilde{\lambda})\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{\lambda}=\max\left\{\frac{w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\top}{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}-M}{\|w_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}},\tau\beta\right\},

where
\tau>0,{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}=(\tilde{\alpha}_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
w_{\mathcal{I}}=(w_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
for
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
chosen such that

\tilde{\alpha}_{i}/w_{i}>\tilde{\lambda}\implies i\in\mathcal{I}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\alpha}_{i}/w_{i}<\tilde{\lambda}\implies i\not\in\mathcal{I}.

(55)

Moreover, such a choice exists.

Thus, due to (
55
), to calculate
\alpha
, we need to sort the vector
(\alpha_{i}/w_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and find a dividing index
j
such that
\mathcal{I}=\{j+1,\ldots,n\}
and
\tilde{\lambda}
computed based on it satisfies (
55
). If
\alpha_{i}/w_{i}=\alpha_{j}/w_{j}
and
w_{i}\neq w_{j}
for
i\neq j
, then all sorting combinations may be needed to be tried for such indices.
If
w^{\top}\alpha<M
, we can always take
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof D.4
.

The necessary and sufficient first-order optimality condition for
\alpha=\prox_{\tau R}(\tilde{\alpha})
is

0\in\alpha-{\tilde{\alpha}}+wN_{\leq M}(w^{\top}\alpha)+\tau\beta w+N_{\geq 0}(\alpha),

in other words

\displaystyle 0=\alpha-{\tilde{\alpha}}+w(\lambda+\tau\beta)+\mu\quad\text{where}

(56)

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda,\quad w^{\top}\alpha\leq M,\quad(M-w^{\top}\alpha)\lambda=0,\quad\text{and}

(57)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\leq 0,\quad\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\quad\alpha_{i}\mu_{i}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(58)

We choose

\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}-\tau\beta=\max\left\{\frac{w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\top}{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}-M}{\|w_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}}-\tau\beta,0\right\}.

For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
if
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}\geq w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}
, then
\alpha_{i}=\tilde{\alpha}_{i}-w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}\geq 0
, so (
58
) is satisfied by taking
\mu_{i}=0
, and (
56
) when
\alpha_{i}-{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}+w_{i}\lambda+\tau\beta w_{i}=0
, which indeed holds.
Likewise, if
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}<w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}
, we have
\alpha_{i}=0
, so (
58
) is satisfied for any
\mu_{i}\leq 0
, and, therefore, (
56
) when
\alpha_{i}-{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}+w_{i}\lambda+\tau\beta w_{i}\geq 0
, which again holds.

It, therefore, remains to verify (
57
).
To do so, we start by proving that

w^{\top}\alpha=w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}}-\|w_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}\tilde{\lambda},

(59)

Indeed, by the construction
\alpha_{i}=\max(0,{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}-w_{i}\tilde{\lambda})
, we have

w^{\top}\alpha=\sum_{i:{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}\geq w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}}w_{i}\tilde{\alpha}_{i}-w_{i}^{2}\tilde{\lambda}=\sum_{i:{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}>w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}}w_{i}\tilde{\alpha}_{i}-w_{i}^{2}\tilde{\lambda}.

Therefore (
59
) holds by (
55
).
Now, to prove (
57
), observe that
0\leq\lambda
by construction.
Using (
59
), and the definition of
\tilde{\lambda}
, we obtain

w^{\top}\alpha=\min\bigl(M,w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}}-\|w_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}\tau\beta\bigr).

In particular
w^{\top}\alpha\leq M
.
Moreover, if
\lambda>0
,
i.e.,
w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}}-\|w_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}\tau\beta>M
, we obtain

w^{\top}\alpha=M
, proving (
57
).

To see that (
55
) can be satisfied, let
\mathcal{J}:=\{i\mid\mu_{i}=0\}
.
Observe from (
58
) that
\mu_{i}=0
whenever
\alpha_{i}>0
.
Since
w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\alpha_{\mathcal{J}}=w^{\top}\alpha\leq M
and
w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\mu_{\mathcal{J}}
, applying
w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}
to the components of (
56
) corresponding to
\mathcal{J}
, we obtain

0\leq M-w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}+\|w_{\mathcal{J}}\|^{2}(\lambda+\tau\beta).

(60)

Due to this and the first part of
Eq.

57
,

\lambda\geq\max\left\{0,\frac{w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}-M}{\|w_{\mathcal{J}}\|^{2}}-\tau\beta\right\}.

If
w^{\top}\alpha<M
, we need
\lambda=0
, so this must hold as an equality.
If
w^{\top}\alpha=M
, the inequality in (
60
) would be an equality, so we again reach the same conclusion due to the non-negativity requirement.
It follows from
Eqs.

56
and
58
that

\alpha_{i}={\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}-w_{i}(\lambda+\tau\beta)-\mu_{i}={\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}-w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}-\mu_{i}=\max\{0,{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}-w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{J}}\},

where

\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{J}}=\lambda+\tau\beta=\max\left\{\frac{w_{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}-M}{\|w_{\mathcal{J}}\|^{2}},\tau\beta\right\}.

Finally, if
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}>w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{J}}
then
i\in\mathcal{J}
by definition. Since also
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}<w_{i}\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{J}}
implies
\mu_{i}<0
, hence
i\not\in\mathcal{J}
, it follows that we can take
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}
and
\tilde{\lambda}=\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

Lemma D.5
.

Let
g_{\varepsilon,\delta}^{*}
be defined by
Eq.

45
for some
\varepsilon,\delta>0
.
Moreover, let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{1}n_{2}}
.
Then for any
\tau>0
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{1}n_{2}\}
, we have

[\prox_{\tau g_{\varepsilon,\delta}^{*}}(v)]_{j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1+\tau\delta}v_{j},&\text{if $\|v_{j}\|<(1+\tau\delta)\alpha_{0}$}\\
\frac{2\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon(\tau^{-1}+\delta)+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}(\tau^{-1}+\delta)^{2}+4\varepsilon(\tau^{-1}\|v_{j}\|-(\tau^{-1}+\delta)\alpha_{0})}}{2\|v_{j}\|}v_{j},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof D.6
.

Let
y:=\prox_{\tau g_{\varepsilon,\delta}^{*}}(v)
.
Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{1}n_{2}\}
.
Suppose first that
\|y_{j}\|<\alpha_{0}

Using
Eq.

61
and the the definition of proximal operator, give the characterisation

y_{j}-v_{j}+\tau\delta y_{j}=0
, i.e.,

y_{j}=\frac{1}{1+\tau\delta}v_{j}

Thus

\|v_{j}\|<(1+\tau\delta)\alpha_{0}.

Suppose then that
\|y_{j}\|\geq\alpha_{0}
.
Again the partial derivative formula
Eq.

61

y_{j}-v_{j}+\tau\left(\delta y_{j}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{y_{j}}{\|y_{j}\|}(\|y_{j}\|-\alpha_{0})^{2}\right)=0.

This is to say
y_{j}=tv_{j}
for some

t\left(1+\tau\left(\delta+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{t\|v_{j}\|}(t\|v_{j}\|-\alpha_{0})^{2}\right)\right)=1,

which translates into the second order equation in

\|v_{j}\|t^{2}+(\varepsilon(\tau^{-1}+\delta)-2\alpha_{0})t+\alpha_{0}^{2}\|v_{j}\|^{-1}-\varepsilon\tau^{-1}=0.

This is solved by

t=\frac{2\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon(\tau^{-1}+\delta)\pm\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}(\tau^{-1}+\delta)^{2}+4\varepsilon(\tau^{-1}\|v_{j}\|-(\tau^{-1}+\delta)\alpha_{0})}}{2\|v_{j}\|}.
