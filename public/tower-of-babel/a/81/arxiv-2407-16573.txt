Ultimate regime of Rayleigh–Bénard turbulence: Sub-regimes and their scaling relations for 𝒩 \mathcal{N} u u vs. ℛ \mathcal{R} a a and 𝒫 \mathcal{P} r r
Olga Shishkina, Detlef Lohse

Abstract
We offer a new model for the heat transfer and the turbulence intensity in strongly driven Rayleigh–Bénard turbulence (the so-called ultimate regime), which in contrast to hitherto models is consistent with the new mathematically exact heat transfer upper bound of Choffrut et al. [J. Differential Equations 260 , 3860 (2016)] and thus enables extrapolations of the heat transfer to geo- and astrophysical flows. The model distinguishes between four subregimes of the ultimate regime and well describes the measured heat transfer in various large- ℛ \mathcal{R} a a experiments. In this new representation, which properly accounts for the Prandtl number dependence, the onset to the ultimate regime is seen in all available large- ℛ \mathcal{R} a a data sets, though at different Rayleigh numbers, as to be expected for a non-normal–nonlinear instability.



Knowing the heat and/or mass transfer in large-scale turbulent flows is of utmost importance for many questions in climate research, in geophysical or astrophysical systems, or in industrial flow systems.
Examples are thermally driven flows in the ocean, in the atmosphere
[
1
]
, or in the outer core of Earth, other planets, or stars
[
2
]
. In all these cases, very strong turbulence is achieved, due to the strong thermal driving.
For such systems, however, direct measurements under controlled conditions are not feasible, and neither are direct numerical simulations, given the many degrees of freedom of such systems, though the underlying dynamical equations (the advection-diffusion equations for the temperature and/or the mass transport, coupled to the Navier–Stokes equations) are known. Given this, in order to get an estimate for the heat and/or mass transfer in such systems, one has to rely on more controlled model systems on much smaller scale and then extrapolate towards larger systems with stronger thermal driving.

The most popular controlled model system for heat transfer is the Rayleigh–Bénard (RB) system, consisting of a container of height
L
filled with fluid, heated from below and cooled from above

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
The control parameters of this thermally driven convective flow are the Rayleigh number
\mathcal{R}
a
(the dimensionless temperature difference
\Delta
between top and bottom plates, as measure of the driving strength), the Prandtl number
\mathcal{P}
r
(the ratio between kinematic viscosity
\nu
and thermal diffusivity
\kappa
), and the aspect ratio
\Gamma

(the width
of the system divided by its height).
The main global response parameters are the Nusselt number
\mathcal{N}
u
(the dimensionless heat transfer from bottom to top) and the so-called wind Reynolds number
\mathcal{R}
e
, which quantifies the velocity of the large scale convective flow.
The key question is: How do the Nusselt and the Reynolds number depend on the control parameters,
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}(\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$},\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$},\Gamma)
and
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}(\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$},\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$},\Gamma)
?
For not too strong thermal driving (the so-called classical regime) this question can meanwhile be answered and there is
good agreement between various experiments and numerical simulations and a good understanding of the flow physics, namely in terms of the “Grossmann–Lohse-theory” or in short “GL-theory”, cf.
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, see also the reviews
[
6
,
11
]
.

This is not so for very strong thermal driving, i.e., for
the regime of
very large Rayleigh numbers, which is called the “ultimate regime” and to which, for large enough
\mathcal{R}
a
, the RB system is believed to undergo a transition of non-normal–nonlinear type
[
16
,
11
]
, as typical in strongly driven sheared wall-bounded flows
[
17
]
and as here is the case in the boundary layers.
It
is this very ultimate regime which is relevant for climate research and the above mentioned geophysical and astrophysical convective flows, due to the very strong thermal driving in these systems.
Therefore, extrapolations from the classical regime to the ultimate regime are required.
Typically, these extrapolations are
sought for as scaling laws, but
this only makes sense once there is no transition towards a different state of turbulence.
If there is such a transition, the extrapolation with a simple scaling law becomes meaningless.
But then, how to upscale the RB system and how to understand and predict the heat (and mass) flux for very large
\mathcal{R}
a
, as it occurs in geo- and astrophysical applications?

To answer these questions, various theoretical heuristic models of different degrees of rigor have been developed, based on some assumptions and speculations on the flow physics in this ultimate regime

,
27
,
28
,

. The most famous and influential one of all these models may be the one by Kraichnan
[

, who for
very large
\mathcal{R}
a
and small
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\leq 0.15

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{1/2}/(\log\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$})^{3/2}

\mathcal{R}

All these models obviously should obey the mathematically strict upper bounds for
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}(\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$},\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$})

underlying dynamical equations (heat transfer equation and Navier–Stokes equations in their Boussinesq approximation).
The well-known upper bound
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}<A\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}
was found already in the second half of the last century

[

32

Although this upper bound leads to values much higher than the experimentally measured or numerically calculated

,
it excludes the universality of the scaling relation
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{1/2}

(with any logarithmic corrections). This scaling relation was
proposed in several models for the ultimate regime

,
27
,
24

due to the upper bound
it can only hold for
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{1/2}\lesssim A
.
Moreover, in 2016 Choffrut

[

succeeded to sharpen the upper bound in a large-

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\lesssim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\gtrsim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
(all subject to logarithmic corrections).
Thus in this subregime of the ultimate regime, in which
\mathcal{P}

grows faster than
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
but slower than
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{2/3}
(that is, as
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{a}
,

), Kraichnan’s model predicts
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\gamma}

Similarly, also other models
[

,
24
]

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
for the ultimate regime, cannot hold in this subrange of the
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}-\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}
parameter space.

This discrepancy calls for revisiting the suggested scaling laws in the ultimate regime, in view of the improved and sharpened strict upper bound
[
35
]
.
To do so is the objective of this paper.
We will first suggest a new model for the heat transfer in the ultimate regime, which is based on the flow physics in the turbulent boundary layers and which respects the new mathematically strict upper bounds in the various subregimes of the ultimate regime in the
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}-\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}
parameter space.
We will then show that the available experimental and numerical data for strongly driven RB convection can be well described with our new model.

We start with the boundary layer equations for the horizontal velocity
u_{x}
and temperature
\theta
in a turbulent flow next to a rigid horizontal wall:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{x}+{{\bf u}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u_{x}+{\partial}_{x}p/\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}^{2}u_{x},

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta+{{\bf u}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}^{2}\theta

(2)

(where
t
denotes time,
x
and
z
, respectively, the horizontal and vertical coordinates,
p
the hydrodynamic pressure, and
\rho
the density)
and conduct the Reynolds decomposition of the flow components into their time-averages and fluctuations:

{\bf u}=\langle{\bf u}\rangle_{t}+{\bf u}^{\prime}
,
\theta=\langle\theta\rangle_{t}+\theta^{\prime}
.
We assume that the flow is highly turbulent, so that the convective contributions from the mean, time-averaged
\langle*\rangle_{t}
flow are negligible compared to the contributions from the Reynolds stresses, i.e.

\left|\langle{\bf u}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u_{x}\rangle_{t}\right|\ll\left|\langle{\bf u}^{\prime}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u_{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{t}\right|

and

\left|\langle{\bf u}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\theta\rangle_{t}\right|\ll\left|\langle{\bf u}^{\prime}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\theta^{\prime}\rangle_{t}\right|
, so that the following relations hold:

\langle{\bf u}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u_{x}\rangle_{t}\approx\langle{\bf u}^{\prime}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u_{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{t}={\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\langle u_{x}^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime}\rangle_{t}
,

\langle{\bf u}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\theta\rangle_{t}\approx\langle{\bf u}^{\prime}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\theta^{\prime}\rangle_{t}={\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\langle\theta^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime}\rangle_{t}
.
Using these relations, we average Eqs. (
1
), (
2
) in time and over a horizontal cross-section
S
(i.e., apply
\langle*\rangle_{t,S}
)
under further natural assumptions that the long averages in time of the temporal derivatives vanish,

\langle{\partial}_{t}u_{x}\rangle_{t,S}=0
,

\langle{\partial}_{t}\theta\rangle_{t,S}=0
,
as well as the averages in the horizontal direction of the horizontal derivatives,

\langle{\partial}^{2}_{x}u_{x}\rangle_{t,S}=0
,

\langle{\partial}^{2}_{x}\theta\rangle_{t,S}=0
,

\langle{\partial}_{x}p\rangle_{t,S}=0
,

{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\langle u_{x}^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}={\partial}_{z}\langle u_{z}^{\prime}u_{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}
,

{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\langle\theta^{\prime}{\bf u}^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}={\partial}_{z}\langle u_{z}^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}
.
With this we obtain the following reduced equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\langle u_{z}^{\prime}u_{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\partial^{2}_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t,S},

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\langle u_{z}^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\partial^{2}_{z}\langle\theta\rangle_{t,S}.

(4)

Integrating equations (
3
)–(
4
) from 0 to
z
, introducing the eddy viscosity
\nu_{\tau}
and
the eddy thermal diffusivity
\kappa_{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\langle u_{z}^{\prime}u_{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\nu_{\tau}\partial_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t,S},

(5)

\displaystyle\langle u_{z}^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}\rangle_{t,S}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\kappa_{\tau}\partial_{z}\langle\theta\rangle_{t,S},

(6)

and taking into account the vanishing fluctuations at the plate and that the Nusselt number
is defined by

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\equiv-\left.{\partial}_{z}\langle\theta\rangle_{t,S}\right|_{z=0}L/\Delta
,
we obtain

u^{2}_{\tau}\equiv\left.\nu{\partial}_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t,S}\right|_{z=0}=(\nu+\nu_{\tau}){\partial}_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t,S}

(7)

for (the square of) the friction velocity and

({\kappa\Delta}/{L})\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}=-(\kappa+\kappa_{\tau}){\partial}_{z}\langle\theta\rangle_{t,S}.

(8)

To close the system (
7
) and (
8
), we need to know the functional dependences of the eddy viscosity
\nu_{\tau}(z)
and the eddy thermal diffusivity
\kappa_{\tau}(z)
.
Near the plate, within the viscous sublayer of the thickness
z_{\tau}\equiv\nu/u_{\tau}
, both the eddy viscosity
\nu_{\tau}(z)
and the eddy thermal diffusivity
\kappa_{\tau}(z)
, behave as cubic functions of the distance from the plate,
\propto z^{3}

[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, and therefore the contribution of the eddy viscosity and eddy thermal diffusivity within the viscous sublayer is negligible.

To estimate the mean vertical profiles of
\nu_{\tau}(z)
,
\kappa_{\tau}(z)
and
\epsilon_{u}(z)
outside the viscous sublayer, we follow Landau
[
40
]
and assume that (i) the turbulent Prandtl number
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}_{\tau}\equiv\nu_{\tau}/\kappa_{\tau}
is independent of (or only weakly dependent on) the molecular Prandtl number
\mathcal{P}
r
and (ii) that the mean vertical profiles of
\nu_{\tau}(z)
,
\kappa_{\tau}(z)
, and
\epsilon_{u}(z)
, are determined exclusively by the momentum transferred by the fluid to a solid wall, i.e. the friction velocity
u_{\tau}
, and by the distance to the plate, subject to a certain Prandtl-number dependence, i.e.
z\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta}
.

These assumptions, by dimensional analysis, imply that, outside the viscous sublayer,
\nu_{\tau}(z)
,
\kappa_{\tau}(z)
, and
\epsilon_{u}(z)
should scale as

\displaystyle\nu_{\tau}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varkappa\,u_{\tau}z\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta},

(9)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\tau}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varkappa_{\theta}\,u_{\tau}z\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta},

(10)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{u}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varkappa_{\epsilon}\,u_{\tau}^{3}}{z\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta}},

(11)

with some positive constants
\varkappa
,
\varkappa_{\theta}
, and
\varkappa_{\epsilon}
.

We further propose that the turbulent diffusivities
\nu_{\tau}
and
\kappa_{\tau}
are controlled by the smallest of the two fluid characteristics of diffusion, i.e., either by
\nu
or by
\kappa
.
In other words, both,
\nu_{\tau}
and
\kappa_{\tau}
, should be proportional to
\sqrt{\left.\nu{\partial}_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t}\right|_{z=0}}
for small
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\lesssim 1
, and to
\sqrt{\left.\kappa{\partial}_{z}\langle u_{x}\rangle_{t}\right|_{z=0}}
for large

, which implies
\zeta=0
for
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\lesssim 1
and
\zeta=-1/2
for
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\gtrsim 1
.
This proposition is related to the fact that if any of the two quantities,
\nu
or
\kappa
, equals zero, convection is fully suppressed,
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}=1
, independently from which value takes the other characteristics of diffusion (
\kappa
or
\nu
, respectively).

Now, from the exact relation
\epsilon_{u}\equiv\nu\langle({\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\bf u})^{2}\rangle=\nu^{3}L^{-4}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{-2}(\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}-1)

we can derive the scaling relations, which we propose for the ultimate regime,
for both small and large

r

\displaystyle\frac{\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}_{\tau}}{\varkappa\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta}}\log\left(\frac{\varkappa}{2}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}_{\tau}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{\zeta}+1\right)

The numbers in color boxes show the scaling exponents in the relations

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{{}^{\hbox{\pagecolor{pink1}\scriptsize$\gamma_{1}$}}}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{{}^{\hbox{\pagecolor{pink3}\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\scriptsize$\gamma_{2}$}}}
,

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{{}^{\hbox{\pagecolor{blue1}\scriptsize$\gamma_{3}$}}}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{{}^{\hbox{\pagecolor{blue3}\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\scriptsize$\gamma_{4}$}}}
(subject to logarithmic corrections).
The straight lines indicate the slopes of the transitions between the neighbouring regimes,

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\eta}
, where the values of
\eta

are written next to the lines.
The dotted line indicates where the laminar kinetic boundary layer is expected to become turbulent (i.e., where
the shear Reynolds number achieves a critical value,
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}_{s}=const.
).

The transition between the scaling regimes IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
and IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
takes place at a constant
\mathcal{P}
r
, where
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}/(\log\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$})^{2}
grows slightly slower than
\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}
as
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}\rightarrow\infty
(see the horizontal line for

\eta=0

in the
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}-\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}
plane in Fig.
1
, which indicates the transition

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\eta}

between the neighbouring regimes IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
and IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
).
Another boundary for the regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
is for

\eta=-1

(marked with a blue line in Fig.
1
).
While moving along this line for an increasing
\mathcal{R}
a
and
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{-1}
, the Nusselt number remains constant, and any steeper transition slope from regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
would imply an unphysical limit
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\rightarrow 0
along that line.
The blue line in Fig.
1
indicates the slope of the transition to the regime II
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
, which has the very same scaling exponents for
\mathcal{N}
u
and
\mathcal{R}
e
as the classical regime II
ℓ
of the GL-theory
[
12
]
.

Analogously, one can conclude that the slope of the upper boundary of the regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
should not be steeper than
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}
so that along this line the Nusselt number remains constant for increasing
\mathcal{R}
a
.
However, as we will explain below, the transition from regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
has a significantly more gentle slope, namely

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\eta}
with
\eta=1/3

(marked with a pink line in the sketch of Fig.
1
).

Indeed, for the no-slip boundary conditions, each component
u
of the velocity field vanishes at the (Lipschitz) boundary of the domain, and therefore the Friedrichs inequality
\lambda_{1}\langle u^{2}\rangle\leq\langle({\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}u)^{2}\rangle
holds, where
\lambda_{1}
is the lowest (positive) eigenvalue of the Laplace operator in the considered domain with the corresponding boundary conditions, that depends only on the geometrical characteristics and has the dimension of inverse squared length
[
10
]
.
Therefore, for any RB flow

\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}^{2}\lesssim(L^{4}/\nu^{3})\epsilon_{u}=\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}\,\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{-2}(\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}-1),

(21)

where
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}^{2}
is based on the kinetic energy
\langle u^{2}\rangle
.
In regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
(for small
\mathcal{P}
r
), this relation is always fulfilled within the discussed boundaries, since (
21
) then means
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\lesssim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}
.
However, in regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
, the requirement (
21
) means
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\lesssim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
, as it follows from the combination of the relations (
17
), (
19
) with (
21
).
Therefore, regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
can exist only for
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\lesssim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
.
This is consistent with the
upper bound of Choffrut
et al.

[
35
]
, who, as mentioned above, derived that the upper bounds for the heat transport for large Prandtl numbers
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\gtrsim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
cannot exceed
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
(all up to logarithmic corrections).

While moving along the line
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
with increasing
\mathcal{R}
a
(the red line in Fig.
1
), the Nusselt number effectively scales as
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{-1/2}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/2}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
.
We assume that this transition line
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
connects regime IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
with regime III
{}^{\prime}_{\infty}
(as we call it), in which the scaling exponents are exactly the same as in the classical regime III
∞
of the GL-theory
[
12
]
, namely
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$\hskip-0.85358pt$r$}^{0}\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{1/3}
.
This result is again consistent with
the new
strict upper bound of Choffrut
et al.

[
35
]
.

The sketch in Fig.
1
summarizes the four subregimes of the ultimate regime, namely III
{}^{\prime}_{\infty}
, IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
, IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
and II
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
, which all can be interpreted as ultimate in the sense that one can approach infinite
\mathcal{R}
a
within these regimes.
All these subregimes lie to the right of the pink dotted line that indicates a constant
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$e$}_{s}
associated with the onset of a turbulent boundary layer.
In the regimes III
{}^{\prime}_{\infty}
and II
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
the scaling exponent
\gamma
in the relation
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\gamma}
is, however, smaller than 1/2.
Regimes IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
and IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
can be considered as the “true” ultimate regimes in the sense that only there
\gamma=1/2
.

The proposed model thus indeed suggests that the scaling exponent
\gamma=1/2
in the scaling relation
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{N}$\hskip-2.703pt$u$}\sim\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\gamma}
can be asymptotically achieved
within the regimes IV
{}^{\prime}_{u}
and IV
{}^{\prime}_{\ell}
, but only for almost constant Prandtl numbers.
As soon as
\mathcal{P}
r
changes as a power law of
\penalty\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$\hskip-0.85358pt$a$}^{\xi}
(with some small
|\xi|
, and here it does not matter whether
\xi
is positive or negative), one should expect an asymptotic reduction of the effective scaling exponent as
\gamma=1/2-|\xi|/2
.

We emphasize again that in this new representation, which take the
