Probing Lorentz Invariance Violation with Absorption of Astrophysical Gamma-rays by Solar Photons
Justin D. Finke, Parshad Patel

Abstract
We compute in detail the absorption optical depth for astrophysical γ \gamma -ray photons interacting with solar photons to produce electron positron pairs. This effect is greatest for γ \gamma -ray sources at small angular distances from the Sun, reaching optical depths as high as τ γ ​ γ ∼ 10 − 2 \tau_{\gamma\gamma}\sim 10^{-2} . We also calculate this effect including modifications to the absorption cross section threshold from subluminal Lorentz invariance violation (LIV). We show for the first time that subluminal LIV can lead to increases or decreases in τ γ ​ γ \tau_{\gamma\gamma} compared to the non-LIV case. We show that, at least in principle, LIV can be probed with this effect with observations of γ \gamma -ray sources near the Sun at ≳ 20 ​ TeV \gtrsim 20\ \mathrm{TeV} by HAWC or LHAASO, although a measurement will be extremely difficult due to the small size of the effect.



I
Introduction

Lorentz invariance is one of the fundamental principles of special relativity. However, the violation of Lorentz invariance has been explored in various theories beyond the Standard Model, such as string theory, brane worlds, and loop quantum gravity
(
Amelino-Camelia et al. 1998
;
Kifune 1999
;
Amelino-Camelia & Piran 2001
;
Stecker & Glashow 2001
;
Mattingly 2005
;
Christiansen et al. 2006
;
Jacobson et al. 2006
;
Ellis et al. 2008
, e.g.,)
. In the presence of Lorentz invariance violation (LIV), the normal relativistic dispersion relation for photons is modified as

\displaystyle E^{2}-p^{2}c^{2}=\pm E^{2}\left(\frac{E}{E_{\rm LIV}}\right)^{n}.

(1)

Here
E_{\rm LIV}
is the energy scale,
n
is an
integer (the order of the leading correction), and “
+
” represents
superluminal LIV, and “
-
” represents subluminal LIV. For LIV brought about by quantum gravity models, it would be natural for
E_{\rm LIV}
to be near the Planck Energy,
E_{\rm Planck}=1.2\times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{eV}
.
LIV can lead to a number of potentially observable effects (for a review see
(
Mattingly 2005
)
), including a number of astrophysically interesting effects
(
Sarkar 2002
;
Martínez-Huerta et al. 2020
;
Desai 2023
, e.g.,)
. One is that the speed of light is no longer constant, and is energy-dependent. Using this effect, time-of-flight experiments can be used with astrophysical
\gamma
-ray transients (such as gamma-ray bursts [GRBs] and blazars) to constrain LIV
(
Abdo et al. 2009
;
Vasileiou et al. 2013
;
Ellis et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. The lack of photon decay in Galactic
\gamma
-ray sources measured with HAWC has led to strong constraints on superluminal LIV
(
Albert et al. 2020
)
.

Another effect is the modification of the threshold for pair production from photon-photon interactions, i.e., the process
\gamma+\gamma\rightarrow e^{+}+e^{-}
. Subluminal LIV can be constrained with this process with
\gamma
-rays from extragalactic sources (such as blazars and GRBs), which are absorbed by ultraviolet through far-infrared photons from the extragalactic background light
(
Nikishov 1962
;
Gould & Schréder 1967a
;
Fazio & Stecker 1970
;
Franceschini et al. 2008
;
Razzaque et al. 2009
;
Finke et al. 2010
;
Kneiske & Dole 2010
;
Domínguez et al. 2011
;
Helgason & Kashlinsky 2012
;
Stecker et al. 2012
;
Scully et al. 2014
;
Khaire & Srianand 2015
;
Stecker et al. 2016
;
Franceschini & Rodighiero 2017
;
Andrews et al. 2018
;
Khaire & Srianand 2019
;
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2021
;
Finke et al. 2022
, EBL; e.g.,)
. The EBL is the integrated background light from all the stars that have existed in the observable universe, either through direct emission, or through absorption and re-radiation by dust. Both the EBL and the
\gamma
-ray spectra of extragalactic sources are rather uncertain. Nevertheless, after the discovery of photons out to 20 TeV from Mrk 501 by HEGRA
(
Aharonian et al. 1999
)
, a number of authors suggested LIV was needed to explain how the
\gamma
-rays at these high energies could avoid EBL absorption and reach Earth
(
Kifune 1999
;
Protheroe & Meyer 2000
, e.g.,)
.
Since then, studies of the
\gamma+\gamma\rightarrow e^{+}+e^{-}
process between extragalactic
\gamma
-ray sources and the EBL have been used to constrain LIV
(
Biteau & Williams 2015
;
Tavecchio & Bonnoli 2016
;
Abdalla et al. 2019
;
Dzhappuev et al. 2022
;
Baktash et al. 2022
;
Li & Ma 2023
;
Finke & Razzaque 2023
, e.g.,)
. This will continue with the greatly improved sensitivity of the Cherenkov Telescope Array
(
Abdalla et al. 2021
, CTA;)
. Constraints have also been made from the upper limits on ultra-high energy
\gamma
-ray flux measured by the Auger Observatory
(
Guedes Lang et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. In this manuscript, we explore only the effect of subluminal LIV on
\gamma\gamma
absorption.

There are (at least) two formulations of the effects of LIV on the photon-photon pair production process commonly used in the literature. One is by
Jacob & Piran 2008
. In their formulation, the standard model cross section formula is used, modifying only the threshold energy and the cross-section’s dependence on this threshold. The other is by
Fairbairn et al. 2014
. Those authors define an effective mass that is related to the LIV energy scale, and use that to define an invariant center of mass energy. The two formulations
do not
give equivalent results. A comparison of the two is given by
Tavecchio & Bonnoli 2016
.

Another possible application of the
\gamma+\gamma\rightarrow e^{+}+e^{-}
process to LIV constraints could come from the extinction of astrophysical
\gamma
-ray photons by photons from the Sun. Preliminary estimates of this process were done by
(
Loeb 2022
;
Balaji 2023
)
. Here we explore this effect in much greater detail, including its application to constraining LIV. As we will see below, subluminal LIV can make the
\gamma
-ray absorption optical depth more transparent
or more opaque
than it would be without LIV, depending on the photon distribution interacting with the
\gamma
-rays. This may allow LIV to be constrained with measurements of the
\gamma
-ray sky at energies of
\gtrsim 20
TeV, although such measurements will be difficult to make.

In Section
II
, we describe our detailed calculations of absorption of astrophysical
\gamma
-rays by solar photons, both with and without LIV included. In Section
III
we describe the potential for observing this effect and how it could be used to constrain LIV. Finally we conclude with a summary in Section
IV
.

II
Formalism

II.1
Setup

Consider an astrophysical
\gamma
-ray photon coming towards the Earth from outside the solar system. Let the angle at the Earth between the sun and the
\gamma
-ray photon be
\theta_{E}\equiv\cos^{-1}(\mu_{E})
; the angle at the Sun between the Earth and the
\gamma
-ray photon is
\theta_{S}\equiv\cos^{-1}(\mu_{S})
. The distance between the Earth and the Sun is
a
; the distance between the Sun and the incoming
\gamma
-ray photon is
x_{S}
; and the distance between the Earth and the
\gamma
-ray photon is
x_{E}
. See Fig.
1
for an illustration of the geometric setup.

The
\gamma
-ray photon will interact with solar photons, annihilating the
photons and producing electron-positron pairs (i.e.,
\gamma+\gamma\rightarrow e^{+}+e^{-}
). The
\gamma\gamma
absorption cross section is
(
Gould & Schréder 1967b
;
Brown et al. 1973
, e.g.,)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\pi r_{e}^{2}(1-\beta_{\rm cm}^{2})\Biggr[(3-\beta_{\rm cm}^{4})\ln\left(\frac{1+\beta_{\rm cm}}{1-\beta_{\rm cm}}\right)

\displaystyle-2\beta_{\rm cm}(2-\beta_{\rm cm}^{2})\Biggr]

(2)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{\rm cm}=\sqrt{1-s^{-1}}\ ;

(3)

\sqrt{s}
is the Lorentz factor of the resulting electron and positron in their center-of-momentum frame; and
r_{e}=2.817\times 10^{-13}
cm is the classical electron radius.

Figure 1:
Geometry of solar system and
\gamma
-ray heading towards Earth. Distances and angles are labeled.

The
\gamma
-ray absorption optical depth per unit distance is given by
(
Dermer & Menon 2009
, e.g.,)

\displaystyle\frac{d\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1})}{dx_{E}}

\displaystyle=\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\phi\int^{1}_{-1}d\mu(1-\cos\psi)

\displaystyle\times\int^{\infty}_{0}d\epsilon\ n(\epsilon,\Omega)\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2}\right]\ ,

(4)

where
\epsilon_{1}
is the dimensionless energy of the
\gamma
-rays;
\epsilon
is the dimensionless energy of the soft photons interacting with and absorbing the
\gamma
-rays; and
n(\epsilon,\Omega)
is the photon density of the photon field that interacts with and absorbs the
\gamma
-rays. We use the notation where
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon
represent dimensionless photon energies, i.e. photon energies in units of the electron rest energy,
m_{e}c^{2}=511\ \mathrm{keV}
. Here
\psi
is the angle between the incoming
\gamma
-ray photon and the photon from the Sun. In general, the radiation field
n(\epsilon,\Omega)
and the interaction angle
\psi
can be functions of both the polar angle (
\theta
) and the azimuthal angle (
\phi
). However, for our geometry here they are independent of
\phi
. Some trigonometric effort yields

\displaystyle\cos\psi=\mu_{S}\mu_{E}-\sqrt{1-\mu_{S}^{2}}\sqrt{1-\mu_{E}^{2}}\ .

(5)

Note that
Balaji 2023
neglect the
1-\cos\psi
term in Equation (
II.1
). This is the primary difference in our calculations and theirs.

The
\gamma
-ray comes from a source with intrinsic (i.e., unabsorbed)
\gamma
-ray flux
F_{\rm int}(\epsilon_{1})
. It will be observed by a detector on or orbiting the Earth with a
\gamma
-ray flux
F_{\rm obs}(\epsilon_{1})=\exp(-\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E}))F_{\rm int}(\epsilon_{1})
.

II.2
Monochromatic Approximation for Sun

We follow the formalism of
Boettcher & Dermer 1995
;
Dermer & Menon 2009
;
Dermer et al. 2009
;
Finke 2016
to derive formulae for the photoabsorption of astrophysical
\gamma
-rays by solar photons as a function of
\gamma
-ray photon energy (
\epsilon_{1}
) and angular distance on the sky of the source from the Sun (
\theta_{E}
). In spherical coordinates
(R_{r},\theta_{r},\phi_{r})
are the radial distance, polar angle, and azimuthal angle, respectively, for the generic radiating medium, and
\mu_{r}=\cos\theta_{r}
. In our case the radiating medium will be the Sun.

We approximate the Sun as a point source emitting monochromatic photons with dimensionless energy
\epsilon_{S}=2.7\Theta
and luminosity
L_{S}=3.846\times 10^{33}
ergs s
-1
. Here
\Theta=k_{B}T_{S}/(m_{e}c^{2})
where
k_{B}=1.380\times 10^{-16}
erg K
-1
is the Boltzmann Constant and
T_{S}=5780\ \mathrm{K}
is the effective temperature of the Sun. The center of the Sun is at the origin (see Fig.
1
). In this case, the emissivity of the photons emitted by the Sun is

\displaystyle\dot{n}(\epsilon,\Omega)=\frac{L_{S}}{m_{e}c^{2}\epsilon_{S}}\frac{\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{S})\delta(R_{r}-R_{S})}{4\pi R_{S}^{2}}\ ,

(6)

where
R_{S}=6.98\times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm}
is the radius of the Sun. The photon density at a
distance
x
from the origin is
(
Boettcher & Dermer 1995
;
Finke 2016
, e.g.,)

\displaystyle n(\epsilon,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi_{r}\int^{1}_{-1}d\mu_{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}dR_{r}\left(\frac{R_{r}}{x}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\dot{n}(\epsilon,\Omega)\delta(\phi_{r}-\phi_{S})\delta(\mu-\mu_{S})\ .

(7)

The coordinate system is chosen so that the azimuthal angle of the sun is
\phi_{S}=0
. Putting Equation (
6
) in
Equation (
II.2
) gives

\displaystyle n(\epsilon,\Omega)=\frac{L_{S}}{m_{e}c^{2}\epsilon_{S}c}\frac{1}{4\pi x^{2}}\frac{\delta(\mu-\mu_{S})}{2\pi}\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{S})\ .

(8)

We are interested in the case where the
\gamma
-ray photon interacts with
a solar photon, which occurs at
x=x_{S}
. Substituting Equation
(
8
) into Equation (
II.1
) results in

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4\pi\epsilon_{S}m_{e}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon_{S}\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2}\right]\ ,

(9)

where, from the law of cosines,

\displaystyle x_{S}^{2}=a^{2}+x_{E}^{2}-2ax_{E}\mu_{E}\

(10)

and

\displaystyle\mu_{S}=\frac{a^{2}+x_{S}^{2}-x_{E}^{2}}{2ax_{S}}\ .

(11)

If
\theta_{E}=\pi
then
\mu_{E}=-1
,
\mu_{S}=1
, and the integral in Equation
(
II.2
) can be performed analytically, giving

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E}=-1)=\frac{L_{S}}{2\pi\epsilon_{S}m_{e}c^{3}a}\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{S}\epsilon_{1})\ .

(12)

This is a factor of 2 larger than the preliminary calculation by
Loeb 2022
.

II.3
Blackbody Approximation for Sun

Now we make the more realistic assumption that, instead of emitting
monochromatically, the sun emits as a blackbody. We still assume the
Sun is a point source. In this case, the emissivity of photons emitted by the Sun
is

\displaystyle\dot{n}(\epsilon,\Omega)=\frac{L_{S}}{m_{e}c^{2}\epsilon}\frac{\delta(R_{r}-R_{S})}{4\pi R_{S}^{2}}\frac{15}{\Theta^{4}\pi^{4}}\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{\exp(\epsilon/\Theta)-1}\ .

(13)

Following the same procedure as in Section
II.2
, we get

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4m_{e}c^{3}}\frac{15}{\Theta^{4}\pi^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\epsilon\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\exp(\epsilon/\Theta)-1}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2}\right]\ .

(14)

Again, for
\theta_{E}=\pi
, the integral over
x_{E}
is analytic, so that

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E}=-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{2m_{e}c^{3}a}\frac{15}{\Theta^{4}\pi^{5}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}d\epsilon\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\exp(\epsilon/\Theta)-1}\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon\epsilon_{1})\ .

(15)

\theta_{E}\lesssim

II.4
Lorentz Invariance Violation

Subluminal LIV can modify the threshold for the
\gamma\gamma
absorption cross section, leading to an increase or decrease in
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
compared to the case without LIV. There are (at least) two ways of implementing this used in the literature, that of
Jacob & Piran 2008
and
Fairbairn et al. 2014
. The two formulations do not give identical results.

II.4.1
Jacob & Piran Formulation

Following
Jacob & Piran 2008
;
Biteau & Williams 2015
, in the presence of subluminal LIV, Equation (
II.2
) for the monochromatic approximation becomes

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4\pi\epsilon_{S}m_{e}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon_{S}\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2(1+0.25(\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{\rm LIV})^{n}\epsilon_{1}^{2})}\right]\ ,

(16)

where
\epsilon_{\rm LIV}=E_{\rm LIV}/(m_{e}c^{2})
. Similarly for the blackbody approximation, Equation (
II.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4m_{e}c^{3}}\frac{15}{\Theta^{4}\pi^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\epsilon\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\exp(\epsilon/\Theta)-1}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2(1+0.25(\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{\rm LIV})^{n}\epsilon_{1}^{2})}\right]\ .

(17)

We parameterize LIV with the parameter
\xi\equiv E_{\rm LIV}/E_{\rm Planck}
. In Figure
3
we show
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
plotted versus
E_{1}
for
\theta_{E}=1
°,
\theta_{E}=10
° and
\theta_{E}=90
° for various values of
\xi
in the linear (
n=1
) LIV case. As the plot demonstrates, there is an
increase
in the gamma-ray absorption optical depth above 10 TeV, depending on the value of
\xi
. This is because of the LIV effect modifying the argument for
\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}
. With this modification, when sampling the cross section at energies where
0.25(\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{\rm LIV})^{n}\epsilon_{1}^{2}\gg 1
, one gets a cross section equivalent to the cross section at a lower energy. As described by
Jacob & Piran 2008
: “[A]t any energy above
E^{*}
[there is] an optical depth identical to the optical depth at some energy below
E^{*}
for which the pair-production threshold is the same.” In the notation of
Jacob & Piran 2008
,
E^{*}
is the energy above which LIV effects become important for
\gamma\gamma
absorption. Thus, if the optical depth without LIV is
increasing
with energy, at energies where LIV is important, one will be sampling the optical depth at lower energies where
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
is lower, and thus one will find a
lower

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
than without LIV. However, if
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
is
decreasing
with energy, the LIV effect allows one to sample
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
at a lower energy where it is higher, and thus one will find a
higher

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
than without LIV.

and

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
versus
\theta_{E}
for various
\gamma

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}

\theta_{E}
increases, the cross section argument
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon(1-\cos\psi)/2

\theta_{E}

\epsilon_{1}\epsilon(1-\cos\psi)/2
will go below threshold for parts of the integral over
x_{E}
for smaller
\theta_{E}
. The exact results thus depend on the detailed kinematics.

II.4.2
Fairbairn
et al.
Formulation

Fairbairn et al. 2014
follow
Protheroe & Meyer 2000
in their approach to LIV. In their formulation, the absorption optical depth for astrophysical
\gamma
-rays due to interactions with solar photons is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4\pi\epsilon_{S}m_{e}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon_{S}\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2}-\frac{(\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{LIV})^{n}\epsilon_{1}^{2}}{4}\right]\

(18)

with the monochromatic approximation, and

\displaystyle\tau_{\gamma\gamma}(\epsilon_{1},\mu_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{S}}{4m_{e}c^{3}}\frac{15}{\Theta^{4}\pi^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\epsilon\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\exp(\epsilon/\Theta)-1}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{E}\frac{1-\cos\psi}{x_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}\left[\frac{\epsilon\epsilon_{1}(1-\cos\psi)}{2}-\frac{(\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{LIV})^{n}\epsilon_{1}^{2}}{4}\right]\

(19)

with the blackbody approximation.

The absorption optical depth of astrophysical
\gamma
-rays with solar photons with LIV using the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation is shown in Fig.
7
; this can be compared with the
Jacob & Piran 2008
formulation plotted in Fig.
3
. The Figure demonstrates that the calculation using the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation also can lead to an increase in the absorption optical depth relative to the calculation with no LIV, although the increase is much lower in the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation. This is also true for absorption of
\gamma
-rays by EBL photons, as demonstrated in Fig.

; here again, on the region of the plot where
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
is decreasing with
E_{1}
in the non-LIV case, the LIV can lead to an increase in
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
relative to the non-LIV case. In general, the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation predicts lower absorption optical depths than the
Jacob & Piran 2008
formulation, as discussed by
Tavecchio & Bonnoli 2016
.

, but using the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation.

III
An Experiment for LIV

In the preceding section, we have computed the absorption optical depth of
\gamma
-ray photons from the Sun, both with and without LIV effects included. These calculations make potentially observable predictions. The absorption is generally greatest at small angular distances from the Sun. This implies an experiment that could potentially detect or constrain LIV. One would need to observe a bright
\gamma
-ray source when it is at small angular distances from the Sun, where the effect is strongest; and when it is quite far from the Sun, where the absorption is minimal. The flux near the Sun would be

\displaystyle F({\rm near})

\displaystyle=F({\rm far})\exp[-\tau_{\gamma\gamma}({\rm near})]

\displaystyle\approx F({\rm far})[1-\tau_{\gamma\gamma}({\rm near})]\ ,

(20)

where
F({\rm far})
is the flux far from the Sun. The combination of these observations would give
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}({\rm near})
, the absorption optical depth near the Sun. A comparison of this with our model predictions could then constrain
\xi
. Assuming
F({\rm far})\approx F({\rm near})\approx F
for the purposes of estimating uncertainties, and
F({\rm far})
and
F({\rm near})
have the same uncertainty,
\sigma_{F}
, standard Gaussian error propagation gives the uncertainty in
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}({\rm near})
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{F}}{F}\right)\ .

(21)

In Fig.
8
the absorption optical depth is plotted versus
\xi
for
\theta_{E}=2
° for a variety of photon energies. In general, observations of photons with higher energies have the greatest ability to constrain
\xi
. Although note there is some ambiguity; a measurement of
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}=10^{-2}
at 500 TeV could indicate
\xi\approx 5
or
\xi\approx 100
. However, there are other ways to resolve these ambiguities; for instance, according to time-of-flight constraints from GRBs,
\xi\gtrsim 10

(
Vasileiou et al. 2013
)
. To constrain LIV one must be able to observe a
\gamma
-ray source near the Sun at these very high energies. Imaging Atmospheric Cherenkov Telescopes (IACTs) such as VERITAS, H.E.S.S., MAGIC, and the upcoming CTA are not able to observe during the day and thus could not observe
\gamma
-ray sources near the Sun, but could potentially observe this effect at large angles. These leaves water Cherenkov detectors, such as HAWC and LHAASO.
Fermi
-LAT could potentially measure the attenuation of
\gamma
-ray photons near the Sun, but it is not sensitive at energies relevant for constraining LIV.

Compared with constraints on LIV from the EBL, the main advantage to this method is the higher degree of certainty. With EBL absorption, neither the absorbing photon source (the EBL) nor the
\gamma
-ray source spectrum is completely known
(
Mattingly 2005
, e.g.,)
. With the experiment outlined here, the absorbing photon source (the Sun) has a known spectrum that is approximated well by a blackbody. The
\gamma
-ray source spectrum can be known from observations when the source is not near the Sun, when absorption by this effect is negligible. The disadvantage of using the Sun is that the absorption is much smaller than with the EBL; for solar photons,
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}\sim 10^{-2}
at most.

We estimate the possibility of detecting absorption of astrophysical photons by the Sun at 1 TeV by HAWC and LHAASO, without regard to constraining LIV. At
E_{1}=1
TeV and
\theta_{E}=2^{\circ}
,
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}\approx 3\times 10^{-3}
. Assuming Poisson uncertainties and a precisely known background,
\sigma_{F}/F\approx\sqrt{S+B}/S
, where
S
and
B
are the source and background count rates, respectively, collected by the detector. For HAWC and LHAASO we expect
B\gg S
, so that
\sqrt{S+B}/S\approx\sqrt{B}/S
. Using Equation (
21
),

\displaystyle\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}\left(\frac{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}{\sigma_{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}}\right)\ .

(22)

For a
3\sigma
detection,
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}/\sigma_{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}=3
.

One can search for the absorption of individual astrophysical
\gamma
-ray sources, or the
\gamma
-ray background. The background has the advantage of always being observable near the Sun. Its disadvantage is it is extremely faint. Indeed,
the extragalactic
\gamma
-ray background has not yet been detected directly (as far as the authors know) but estimates have been made by
Inoue & Tanaka 2016
;
Qu et al. 2019
;
Qu & Zeng 2022
.

The advantage of individual sources is they should have many more photons than the background. The disadvantage is they do not stay near the Sun for long, since the Sun moves across the sky by approximately 1° day
-1
. This means a point source will only be observable with
10^{\circ}
of the Sun for at most 20 days, so that for point sources it will be observed a fraction of the time
f_{t}=0.5\times(20\ {\rm days})/(365\ {\rm days})\approx 0.03
. We searched the TeVCat (
http://tevcat.uchicago.edu
;
Wakely & Horan 2008
) for astrophysical TeV sources that get near the Sun. In Table
1
we list the TeV sources with ecliptic latitude
<10
°. As of this writing, the TeVCat has not included sources from the first LHAASO catalog
(
Cao et al. 2023
)
. We included these sources in our table as well. The ecliptic latitude will be the sources’ minimum distance on the sky from the Sun. Variable sources such as blazars would not be ideal for these purposes, since one would want to compare their flux when they are near and far from the Sun. Two sources stand out as the brightest: the Crab
(
Aharonian et al. 2021
;
Cao et al. 2021a
)
and LHAASO J1825
-
1326
(
Cao et al. 2021b
)
, seen by LHAASO up to
\approx 1
PeV and 500 TeV, respectively. For point sources with photon flux
\Phi_{S}\equiv E\ dN/dE
the signal count rate can be approximated as

\displaystyle S\approx\Phi_{S}A_{\rm eff}f_{t}\Delta t.

(23)

where
\Delta t
is the length of time needed to observe the source and
A_{\rm eff}
is the effective area of the detector. For HAWC, we take the effective area from
DeYoung & HAWC Collaboration 2012
; for LHAASO we take it from
Cao et al. 2019
. Cosmic rays are a significant background for HAWC and LHAASO. The analysis with HAWC is able to reject all but a fraction
f_{r}\approx 6\times 10^{-3}
of these cosmic rays
(
DeYoung & HAWC Collaboration 2012
)
, and LHAASO can reject all but
f_{r}\approx 2\times 10^{-3}
of them
(
Aharonian et al. 2021
)
. The cosmic ray background count rate is thus

\displaystyle B\approx\Phi_{\rm CR}f_{r}A_{\rm eff}f_{t}\Delta t\Omega\ ,

(24)

where
\Phi_{\rm CR}
is the
E\ dN/dE
cosmic ray intensity and
\Omega
is the solid angle around the source. We take
\Phi_{\rm CR}
from Chapter 29 of
Tanabashi et al. 2018
. For HAWC and LHAASO, with angular resolution of about
\theta_{\rm psf}\approx 1\arcdeg
,
\Omega\approx\pi\theta_{\rm psf}^{2}\approx 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{srad}
. For a precisely known background, the number of counts above background is

\displaystyle\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+B}}\approx\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}\approx\frac{\Phi_{S}}{\sqrt{\Phi_{\rm CR}f_{r}}}\sqrt{\frac{A_{\rm eff}f_{t}\Delta t}{\Omega}}\ .

(25)

IV
Summary

In this paper:

•

We compute in detail the effect of absorption of astrophysical
\gamma
-ray sources by solar photons, a process for which preliminary calculations were made by
Loeb 2022
;
Balaji 2023
.

•

We show the absorption effect is greatest for
\gamma
-ray sources at small angular distances from sun, reaching as high as
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}\sim 10^{-2}
.
As pointed out by
Loeb 2022
, this effect causes the
\gamma
-ray background at
\gtrsim 100\ \mathrm{GeV}
to have a greater anisotropy than the cosmic microwave background from the solar system’s motion relative to the cosmic frame. Unfortunately, the EGB is
\sim 10^{6}
fainter than the CMB
(
Hauser & Dwek 2001
, e.g.,)
, making the anisotropy much more difficult to detect.

•

We make calculations for this effect that include subluminal LIV. For the first time, we show that subluminal LIV can lead to a decrease or increase in the absorption optical depth from the pair production process compared to the non-LIV case, depending on the spectrum of the absorbing photon source. This is true for both the
Jacob & Piran 2008
and
Fairbairn et al. 2014
formulation of LIV. he
Jacob & Piran 2008
formulation predicts higher values of
\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
than the
Fairbairn et al. 2014
one.

•

We show that this effect is unlikely to be observed with water Cherenkov observatories like HAWC or LHAASO. These can observe near the Sun, unlike IACTs, but the difficulties in separating
\gamma
-rays and hadrons means the needed precision will not be reached in a reasonable amount of time. However, a clever stacking analysis may lead to a possible detection of the effect in a more reasonable amount of time.
