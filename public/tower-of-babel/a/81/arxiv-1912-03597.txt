A viral propagation model with nonlinear
infection rate and free boundaries

Abstract
In this paper we put forward a viral propagation model with nonlinear infection rate and free boundaries and investigate the dynamical properties. This model is composed of two ordinary differential equations and one partial differential equation, in which the spatial range of the first equation is the whole space ℝ \mathbb{R} , and the last two equations have free boundaries. As a new mathematical model, we prove the existence, uniqueness and uniform estimates of global solution, and provide the criteria for spreading and vanishing, and long time behavior of the solution components u , v , w u,v,w . Comparing with the corresponding ordinary differential systems, the Basic Reproduction Number ℛ 0 {\cal R}_{0} plays a different role. We find that when ℛ 0 ≤ 1 {\cal R}_{0}\leq 1 , the virus cannot spread successfully; when ℛ 0 > 1 {\cal R}_{0}>1 , the successful spread of virus depends on the initial value and varying parameters. Keywords : viral propagation model, free boundaries, basic reproduction number, spreading-vanishing, long time behavior. AMS Subject Classification (2100) : 35K57, 35B40, 35R35, 92D30

Introduction

Background

In order to clarify the pathogenesis of diseases and seek
effective treatment measures, viral dynamics have been a hot research topic
(cf.
[
1
,
2
]
), which usually cannot be answered by biological
experimental methods alone but require the help of mathematical models. For this
reason, a simple model was introduced few decades ago by Nowak and Bangham

[
3
]
. See also Nowak and May
[
4
]
. The basic model of viral dynamics is the
following set of differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u^{\prime}=\theta-au-buw,\\
v^{\prime}=buw-cv,\\
w^{\prime}=kv-qw,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
u
,
v
and
w
represent the population of uninfected cells, infected cells
and viruses, respectively; uninfected cells
are produced at a constant rate
\theta
and with death rate
au
;
cv

is the death rate of infected cells; virus
particles
w
infect uninfected cells with rate
buw
, and meanwhile virus particles
are produced by infected cells with rate
kv
and have death rate
qw
. It has been shown
that if the
Basic Reproduction Number

{\cal R}_{0}={\theta kb}/(acq)<1
, then the
system returns to the uninfected state
(\theta/a,0,0)
. If
{\cal R}_{0}>1
, then the
system will converge to the unique positive equilibrium state
\big(\frac{qc}{kb},\,\frac{\theta}{c}-\frac{aq}{kb},\,\frac{\theta k}{qc}-\frac{a}{b}\big)
. This indicates that in the initial stage of infection,
if each infected cell infects less than one cell on average, then the infection cannot
spread; if each infected cell infects
{\cal R}_{0}>1
cells on average, the number of infected
cells increases and the number of uninfected cells declines.

Mathematical Model
To investigate the impact of spatial dynamics on this model, Stancevic et al.
[
5
]
extended this model to include spatially random diffusion and spatially directed chemotaxis. Invoked by their ideas, we give
the basic model assumptions as follows:

(i) A nonlinear infection rate can happen due to saturation at high virus concentration, where the infectious fraction is so high that exposure is very likely. Moreover, with the increase of the virus concentration the living environment for cells becomes worse and worse. Thus, it is reasonable for us to assume that the rate of infection for virus and the virion production rate for infected cells are both nonlinear. Here we use

f_{1}(u,w)=\theta-au-\frac{buw}{1+w},\ \ f_{2}(u,v,w)=\frac{buw}{1+w}-cv,\ \ f_{3}(v,w)=\frac{kv}{1+w}-qw

instead of the three terms in the right hand side of (
1.1
).

(ii) We assume that the major spatial dispersal comes from the moving (diffusion)
of viruses in vivo, while both the
uninfected and infected cells are immobile (do not diffuse). So we add only a
diffusion term to the differential equation of
viruses;

(iii) Since the infected cells are caused by viruses, their distribution range is
the same;

(iv) The distribution of viruses and infected cells is a local range, which is
small relative to the distribution of uninfected
cells, so we think that uninfected cells are distributed over the whole space.
Such kind of assumptions have been used in
the species invasion models (cf.
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
for example);

(v) Initially, viruses are distributed over a local range
\Omega_{0}
(the
initial habitat). They will spread from boundary to
expand their habitat as a result of the spatial dispersal freely. That is, as time

t
increases,
\Omega_{0}
will evolve
into expanding region
\Omega(t)
with expanding front
\partial\Omega(t)
. Initial
function
w_{0}(x)
, and as a result

v_{0}(x)
, will evolve into positive functions
w(t,x)
and
v(t,x)
which vanish on
the moving boundary
\partial\Omega(t)
;

(vi) For simplicity, we restrict our problem to the one dimensional case. Based on
the
deduction of free boundary
conditions
given in
[
9
]
, we have the following free boundary conditions

g^{\prime}(t)=-\mu w_{x}(t,g(t)),\ \ \ h^{\prime}(t)=-\beta w_{x}(t,h(t)).

All of these assumptions (i)-(vi) suggest the following model, which governs
the spatial and temporal evolution of
viruses and cells, as well as free boundaries:

\begin{cases}u_{t}=f_{1}(u,w),&t>0,\ -\infty<x<\infty,\\
v_{t}=f_{2}(u,v,w),&t>0,\ g(t)<x<h(t),\\
w_{t}-dw_{xx}=f_{3}(v,w),&t>0,\ g(t)<x<h(t),\\
v(t,x)=w(t,x)=0,&t>0,\ x\notin(g(t),h(t)),\\
g^{\prime}(t)=-\mu w_{x}(t,g(t)),\,\,\,h^{\prime}(t)=-\beta w_{x}(t,h(t)),&t\geq 0,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),&-\infty<x<\infty,\\
v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\ \ \ w(0,x)=w_{0}(x),&-h_{0}\leq x\leq h_{0},\\
h(0)=-g(0)=h_{0},\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
x=g(t)
and
x=h(t)
are the moving boundaries to be determined together
with
u(t,x)
,
v(t,x)
and
w(t,x)
;
d,~\theta,~a,~b,~c,~k,~q,~\mu,~\beta,~h_{0}
are positive constants.

Denote by
C^{1-}(I)
the space of Lipschitz continuous functions in
I
. We
assume that the initial functions
u_{0},v_{0},w_{0}

satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{0}\in C^{1-}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\ \ v_{0}\in C^{1-}([-h_{0},h_{0}]),\ \ w_{0}\in W^{2}_{p}((-h_{0},h_{0})),\\[1.42262pt]
v_{0}(\pm h_{0})=w_{0}(\pm h_{0})=0,\ \ w^{\prime}(-h_{0})>0,\ \ w^{\prime}(h_{0})<0,\\[1.42262pt]
u_{0}>0\ \ \text{in }\,\mathbb{R},\ \ \ v_{0},w_{0}>0\ \ \text{in}\,(-h_{0},h_{0})\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

with
p>3
. Denote by
L_{0}
and
L_{*}
the Lipschitz constant of
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
,
respectively.

Partially degenerate reaction-diffusion systems, which mean that several diffusion

Aims and Main Results
This paper concerns with the dynamics of
(
1.2
). The global existence, uniqueness, regularity and uniform estimates in time
t
of solution are first studied. Then a spreading-vanishing dichotomy is established, i.e., either

(i)
Spreading
(virus persistence): the virus successfully
infects the uninfected cells and spreads itself to the
uninfected area in the sense that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t)=-\lim_{t\to\infty}g(t)=\infty
, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}>0,\ \displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}>0.

In addition, if
\mathcal{R}_{0}+\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{0}}>b/a
, where
{\cal R}_{0}={\theta kb}/(acq)
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{u}_{\infty}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}u(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}u(t,x)\leq\bar{u}_{\infty},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{v}_{\infty}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}v(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}v(t,x)\leq\bar{v}_{\infty},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{w}_{\infty}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}w(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}w(t,x)\leq\bar{w}_{\infty}

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}
for some positive constants
\underline{u}_{\infty}
,
\bar{u}_{\infty}
,
\underline{v}_{\infty}
,
\bar{v}_{\infty}
,
\underline{w}_{\infty}
and
\bar{w}_{\infty}
. Particularly, under a stronger assumption that
b\leq 2a
, we will derive

\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,x)=u^{*},\ \ \lim_{t\to\infty}v(t,x)=v^{*},\ \ \lim_{t\to\infty}w(t,x)=w^{*}\ \ {\rm locally~uniformly~in~}\mathbb{R},

where
(u^{*},v^{*},w^{*})
is the unique positive root of (
4.12
);

or

(ii)
Vanishing
(virus dies out): the virus
w
and the
infected cells
v
will vanish in a bounded area, i.e.,

-\infty<\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}g(t)<\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t)<\infty
and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),\,h(t)])}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),\,h(t)])}=0,\ \ \lim_{t\to\infty}u=\theta/{a}\ \ \mbox{uniformly\, in }\ \mathbb{R}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t)-\lim_{t\to\infty}g(t)\leq\pi\sqrt{acd/(\theta kb-acq)}
if
{\cal R}_{0}>1
.

As for the
Basic Reproduction Number

{\cal R}_{0}=\theta kb/(acq)
, in our results, we will show that it plays a different role, comparing
with the corresponding ordinary differential systems. When
{\cal R}_{0}\leq 1
,
vanishing
always happens, that is, the virus
cannot spread successfully. On the other hand, when
{\cal R}_{0}>1
, we have a
criterion as follows: if the initial occupying
area
[-h_{0},h_{0}]
is beyond a critical size, namely
2h_{0}\geq\pi\sqrt{acd/(\theta kb-acq)}
, then
spreading
happens
regardless of the moving parameter
\mu
,
\beta
and initial population density

(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})
. While
2h_{0}<\pi\sqrt{acd/(\theta kb-acq)}
, whether
spreading
or
vanishing
happens
depends on the initial population density

(v_{0},w_{0})
and the moving parameter
\mu
and
\beta
.

For convenience, we first introduce some notations.
Denote

Theorem 2.1
.

(Local solution)
For any given

and

) has a unique local solution

.
Moreover,

\displaystyle u>0\ \ {\rm in}\ \ \Pi_{T};\ \ v,w>0\ \ {\rm in}\ \ D^{T}_{g,h};\ \ g^{\prime}(t)<0,\ \ h^{\prime}(t)>0\ \ {\rm in}\ \ [0,T],

Proof.

Invoked by the proof of

(2.1)

By
[
14

, we know that for some

) has a

Step 2:
For the function

obtained in Step 1, we consider the

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{u}_{t}=f_{1}(\tilde{u},w(t,x)),&&(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R},\\
&\tilde{u}(0;x)=u_{0}(x)>0,&&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.3)

By the standard ODE theory, (

\tilde{u}\in C^{1,1-}(\Pi_{T})

.

Now we estimate the Lipschitz constant of

.
Since it can be easily derived from (

, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}(t,x_{1})-\tilde{u}(t,x_{2})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{u}_{t}(s,x_{1})-\tilde{u}_{t}(s,x_{2}){\rm d}s+u_{0}(x_{1})-u_{0}(x_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|\tilde{u}_{t}(s,x_{1})-\tilde{u}_{t}(s,x_{2})|{\rm d}s+L_{0}|x_{1}-x_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{0}|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\int_{0}^{t}|f_{1}(\tilde{u}(s,x_{1}),w(s,x_{1}))-f_{1}(\tilde{u}(s,x_{2}),w(s,x_{2}))|{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(a+b)|\tilde{u}(s,x_{1})-\tilde{u}(s,x_{2})|{\rm d}s+(bA_{1}TM+L_{0})|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

Then noticing

. Define

Obviously,

is complete with the metric

. The above

\mathcal{F}(u)=\tilde{u}
, and

Step 3:
We are in the position to prove that

) with

, we can show

, which only depends on

, such that for any given

\displaystyle|v_{i}(t,x_{1})-v_{i}(t,x_{2})|\leq L_{v}|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

(2.4)

Denote

,
\tilde{U}=\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{2}
,

and

. Since

it follows that, for any

,

\displaystyle|\tilde{U}(t,x)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}(a+b)|\tilde{U}(s,x)|{\rm d}s+bA_{1}T\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Pi_{T})}.

By virtue of the Gronwall inequality again, it yields

(2.5)

The following arguments are devoted to an estimate of

.
Evidently,

satisfy

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &w_{i,t}-dw_{i,xx}=f_{3}(v_{i},w_{i}),&&0<t\leq T,~g_{i}(t)<x<h_{i}(t),\\
&w_{i}(t,x)=0,&&0<t\leq T,~x\notin(g_{i}(t),h_{i}(t)),\\
&w_{i}(0,x)=w_{0}(x),&&|x|\leq h_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

We straighten the boundaries and define

,

\displaystyle|V(t,x_{1}(t,y))|\leq C_{3}\left(\|g,h\|_{C^{1}([0,T])}+T\|U,\,W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Pi_{T})}\right).

Its proof will be put in the next step on account of the length. Thus we have

\displaystyle\|r\|_{C(\Delta_{T})}\leq C_{4}\left(\|g,\,h\|_{C^{1}([0,T])}+T\|U,\,W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Pi_{T})}\right).

Then it follows that

By the second estimate in (

and

are uniformly continuous in

(i)

\lambda_{1}
be the principal eigenvalue of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &d\phi_{xx}-q\phi+k\psi=\lambda\phi,&&-l<x<l,\\
&bm\phi-c\psi=\lambda\psi,&&-l<x<l,\\
&\phi(\pm l)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.3)

Then (
4.2
) has a positive solution if and only if

. Moreover, the positive solution of (
4.2
) is unique when it exists.

(ii)
To stress the dependence on

) by
(v_{l},w_{l})
. Then
(v_{l},w_{l})
is nondecreasing in

(\hat{v},\hat{w})
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}
as
l\to\infty
, where
(\hat{v},\hat{w})
is the unique positive root of

\displaystyle f_{2}(m,v,w)=0,\ \ \ f_{3}(v,w)=0.

(4.4)

Proof.

(i)
Clearly, the problem (
4.2
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &-dw_{xx}+qw=\frac{kbmw}{c(1+w)^{2}},\ \ v=\frac{bmw}{c(1+w)},\ \ &&-l<x<l,\\
&w(x)=0,&&x=\pm l.\end{aligned}\right.

For clarity of exposition, we will always use the problem (
4.2
) in later discussion.

If (
4.2
) has a positive solution
(v,w)
, it is easy to show that
q<\lambda_{1}(q)<kbm/c
, where
\lambda_{1}(q)
is the principal eigenvalue of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &-d\phi_{xx}+q\phi=\lambda\phi,&&-l<x<l,\\
&\phi(x)=0,&&x=\pm l.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

Moreover, since
\lambda_{1}(q)<{kbm}/{c}
, and the function
{bmk}/(c+x)-x
is decreasing in
x>-c
, we can show that there exists the unique
\lambda^{*}>0
such that
{bmk}/(c+\lambda^{*})-\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{1}(q)
.
Substituting this into (
4.5
), one can easily see that
\lambda^{*}
is the principal eigenvalue of (
4.3
), that is,
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{1}>0
.

If
\lambda_{1}>0
, by the standard upper and lower solution methods we can show that (
4.2
) has at least one positive solution. Thanks to the structure of nonlinear terms of (
4.2
), the uniqueness is easily derived.

(ii)  It follows from the above analysis and Corollary
3.2
that for large
l
, (
4.2
) has a unique positive solution
(v_{l},w_{l})
provided that
kbm>qc
. A comparison argument (Lemma 2.1 in
[
16
]
) shows that
(v_{l},w_{l})
is nondecreasing in
l
, and there exists
C>0
such that
v_{l},w_{l}<C
for all large
l
. Making use of the standard elliptic regularity theory, we have that
(v_{l},w_{l})\to(\tilde{v},\tilde{w})
in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
, where
(\tilde{v},\tilde{w})
is a positive solution of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &f_{2}(m,v,w)=0,&&-\infty<x<\infty,\\
&-dw_{xx}=f_{3}(v,w),&&-\infty<x<\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

Obviously,
\tilde{w}
satisfies

\displaystyle-d\tilde{w}_{xx}=\frac{kbm}{c(1+\tilde{w})^{2}}\tilde{w}-q\tilde{w},\ \ -\infty<x<\infty.

(4.6)

Since
\frac{kbm}{c(1+w)^{2}}-q
is decreasing in
w>0
, the possible positive solution of (
4.6
) is a unique positive root of
kbm=qc(1+w)^{2}
.
Thus
\tilde{w}=\hat{w}
, and consequently
\tilde{v}=\hat{v}
. The proof is finished.
∎

Theorem 5.1

Proof.

&v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),&&|x|\leq h_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Notice that
{\cal R}_{0}=\theta kb/acq\leq 1
. It follows that by simple calculations

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm dt}}\int_{g(t)}^{h(t)}\big(cw+kv\big){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{g(t)}^{h(t)}\big(cw_{t}+kv_{t}\big){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{g(t)}^{h(t)}\left[cdw_{xx}+kb\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-at}w+(k\theta b/a-qc)w\right]{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{g(t)}^{h(t)}\left(cdw_{xx}+kb\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-at}w\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-cd\gamma^{-1}(h^{\prime}(t)-g^{\prime}(t))+kbA_{3}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-at}(h(t)-g(t)).

Set

f(t)=\int_{g(t)}^{h(t)}\big(cw+kv\big){\rm d}x,\ \ \ell(t)=h(t)-g(t),\ \ \varphi(t)=kbA_{3}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-at}.

Then we have

cd\ell^{\prime}(t)\leq-\gamma f^{\prime}(t)+\gamma\varphi(t)\ell(t).

Integrating the above differential inequality from
0
to
t
yields

\ell(t)\leq\ell(0)+{\gamma}(cd)^{-1}f(0)+{\gamma}(cd)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s)\ell(s){\rm d}s.

By virtue of the Gronwall inequality,

\ell(t)\leq\big[\ell(0)+{\gamma}(cd)^{-1}f(0)\big]\exp\left\{{{\gamma}(cd)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s){\rm d}s}\right\}<\infty.

Thus,
h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}<\infty
.∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(u,v,w,g,h)
be the solution of (

This implies that if

, then
h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}=\infty
. Moreover if

h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}=\infty

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}>0,\ \ \displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}>0.

(5.1)

Proof.

Due to Theorem
4.1
and

, we see that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,\cdot)=\theta/{a}
uniformly in
\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}=0,\ ~\ ~\lim_{t\to\infty}\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{C([g(t),h(t)])}=0.

(5.2)

Arguing indirectly, if
h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}>\Lambda

T>0
such that for
any small
\varepsilon>0

\frac{kb}{c}(\frac{\theta}{a}-\varepsilon)-q>0

\displaystyle u(t,x)>{\theta}/{a}-\varepsilon,\ \ \forall\ t\geq T,\ x\in\mathbb{R};

\displaystyle h(t)-g(t)>\pi\sqrt{d}\left[kb({\theta}/{a}-\varepsilon)/c-q\right]^{-1/2}=:\Lambda_{\varepsilon},\ \ \forall\ t\geq T.

Then for any
[l_{1},l_{2}]\subseteq(g(T),h(T))
and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{t}\geq f_{2}({\theta}/{a}-\varepsilon,\,v,w),&&t>T,\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&w_{t}-dw_{xx}=f_{3}(v,w),&&t>T,\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&w(t,x)>0,&&t>T,\ x=l_{i},\ i=1,2,\\
&v(T,x)>0,\ w(T,x)>0,&&l_{1}\leq x\leq l_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.3)

Consider the following eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &d\phi_{xx}-q\phi+k\psi=\lambda\phi,&&l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&b(\theta/{a}-\varepsilon)\phi-c\psi=\lambda\psi,&&l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&\phi(l_{i})=0,&&i=1,2.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.4)

Denote the principal eigenpair of (
5.4

(\lambda_{1},(\phi,\psi))
with
\max_{x\in[l_{1},l_{2}]}|\phi(x)|=1
. It
follows from Corollary
3.2
that

\lambda_{1}>0
due to
l_{2}-l_{1}>\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
. Let

\underline{v}(x)=\delta\psi(x),\ \ \ \underline{w}(x)=\delta\phi(x)

with
\delta>0
to be determined later.

We claim that there exists
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small such that for any

, we have

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &0<f_{2}(\theta/a-\varepsilon,\,\underline{v},\underline{w}),\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&-d\underline{w}_{xx}\leq f_{3}(\underline{v},\underline{w}),\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.5)

In fact, we have

\displaystyle f_{2}(\theta/a-\varepsilon,\,\underline{v},\underline{w})=\delta\frac{b(\theta/a-\varepsilon)\phi}{1+\delta\phi}-c\delta\psi=\delta\frac{(c+\lambda_{1})\psi}{1+\delta\phi}-c\delta\psi=\delta\left(\frac{c+\lambda_{1}}{1+\delta\phi}-c\right)\psi>0

provided that
\delta>0
is small. The proof of the second inequality of (

) can
be done in a similar manner.

Furthermore, one can choose small

such that
v(T,x)\geq\delta\psi(x)
and

for

.
Then
(\underline{v},\underline{w})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\underline{v}_{t}\leq f_{2}(\theta/a-\varepsilon,\,\underline{v},\underline{w}),&&t>T,\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&\underline{w}_{t}-d\underline{w}_{xx}\leq f_{3}(\underline{v},\underline{w}),&&t>T,\ \ l_{1}<x<l_{2},\\
&\underline{v}(t,x)=0,\ \underline{w}(t,x)=0,&&t>T,\ x=l_{i},\ i=1,2,\\
&\underline{v}(T,x)\leq v(T,x),\ \underline{w}(T,x)\leq w(T,x),&&l_{1}\leq x\leq l_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

By virtue of the comparison principle,

\displaystyle v(t,x)\geq\underline{v}(x),\ \ \ w(t,x)\geq\underline{w}(x),\ \ t\geq T,\ l_{1}\leq x\leq l_{2}.

(5.6)

This is a contradiction with (
5.2
).

We now assume
h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}=\infty
and prove (
5.1
). By the comparison principle, it is easy to see that

if and only if

. Hence if we assume that one of the two limits in (

) does not hold,
we can similarly obtain
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,\cdot)=\theta/{a}
uniformly in

\mathbb{R}
. By
h_{\infty}-g_{\infty}=\infty
, we can derive an analogous contradiction as above. The proof is ended.
∎

Obviously,

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &d\phi_{xx}-q\phi+k\psi=\lambda\phi,&&-l<x<l,\\

6
Discussion

In this paper we proposed a viral propagation model with nonlinear infection rate and free boundaries and investigated the dynamical properties. This model is composed of two ordinary differential equations and one partial differential equation, in which the spatial range of the first equation is the whole space
\mathbb{R}
, and the last two equations have free boundaries. As a new mathematical model, we have proved the existence, uniqueness and uniform estimates of global solution, and provided the criteria for spreading and vanishing, and long time behavior of the solution components
u,v,w
.

Comparing with the corresponding ordinary differential systems, the
Basic Reproduction Number

{\cal R}_{0}=\theta kb/(acq)
plays a different role:

(i)

For the corresponding ordinary differential systems, by the Lyapunov function method we can prove that if
{\cal R}_{0}<1
then the infection can not spread successfully, while if
{\cal R}_{0}>1
then the infection will spread successfully. When
{\cal R}_{0}=1
the dynamical property is not clear;

(ii)

For our present model, the results indicate that when
{\cal R}_{0}\leq 1
, the virus cannot spread successfully; when
{\cal R}_{0}>1
, the successful spread of virus depends on the initial value and varying parameters. If the initial occupying
area
[-h_{0},h_{0}]
is beyond a critical size, namely
2h_{0}\geq\pi\sqrt{acd/(bk\theta-acq)}
, then
spreading
happens
regardless of the moving parameter
\mu
,
\beta
and initial population density

(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})
. While
2h_{0}<\pi\sqrt{acd/(bk\theta-acq)}
, whether
spreading
or
vanishing
happens
depends on the initial population density

(v_{0},w_{0})
and the moving parameter
\mu
and
\beta
.

From a biological point of view, our model and results seem closer to the reality. On the other hand, our model shows more complex and precise dynamical properties from a mathematical point of view.

Before ending this paper, we mention that for the corresponding Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}-d_{1}\Delta u=f_{1}(u,w),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,\\
v_{t}-d_{2}\Delta v=f_{2}(u,v,w),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,\\
w_{t}-d_{3}\Delta w=f_{3}(v,w),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\ v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\ w(0,x)=w_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

we guess that the
Basic Reproduction Number
is still
{\cal R}_{0}=\theta kb/(acq)
and it plays
the same role as in the corresponding ODEs.
