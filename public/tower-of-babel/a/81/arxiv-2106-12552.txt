Clebsch Canonization of Lie–Poisson Systems
Buddhika Jayawardana, Philip J. Morrison, Tomoki Ohsawa

Abstract
. We propose a systematic procedure called the Clebsch canonization for obtaining a canonical Hamiltonian system that is related to a given Lie–Poisson equation via a momentum map. We describe both coordinate and geometric versions of the procedure, the latter apparently for the first time. We also find another momentum map so that the pair of momentum maps constitute a dual pair under a certain condition. The dual pair gives a concrete realization of what is commonly referred to as collectivization of Lie–Poisson systems. It also implies that solving the canonized system by symplectic Runge–Kutta methods yields so-called collective Lie–Poisson integrators that preserve the coadjoint orbits and hence the Casimirs exactly. We give a couple of examples, including the Kida vortex and the heavy top on a movable base with controls, which are Lie–Poisson systems on 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 , 1 ) ∗ \mathfrak{so}(2,1)^{*} and ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔢 ​ ( 3 ) ⋉ ℝ 3 ) ∗ (\mathfrak{se}(3)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*} , respectively.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Lie–Poisson Dynamics

The formalization of mechanics by Lagrange and Hamilton evolved in the 19th century into the description of dynamical systems where the equations of motion are generated by canonical Poisson brackets, written in terms of canonical coordinates, position and momenta, with a Hamiltonian function. More modern differential geometric descriptions of Hamiltonian systems occurred well into the 20th century by, e.g.,
Mackey 1963
and
Jost 1964
, motivating present day symplectic geometry.

Less well-known is Poisson geometry.
Its origins date back to
Lie 1890
in 1890, but it was brought into modern geometric form with contributions from
Souriau 1970
and others including the seminal paper of
Weinstein 1983
.
Like the canonical Poisson brackets of symplectic geometry, noncanonical Poisson brackets of Poisson geometry are binary operations on smooth phase space functions constituting a Lie algebra realization, but explicit reference to canonical coordinates is removed and degeneracy is allowed.
A manifold with such a Poisson bracket is a generalization of the symplectic manifold called a Poisson manifold.
Noncanonical Poisson brackets, including the present day coordinate-free axioms, were present in the theoretical physics community in the mid 20th century in e.g. the works of
Dirac 1950
,
Pauli 1953
,
Martin 1959
,
Sudarshan 1964
.

A special kind of noncanonical Poisson bracket, the Lie–Poisson bracket, has explicit linear dependence on the phase space coordinates and is intimately related to a Lie algebra.
Lie–Poisson dynamics—dynamics generated by Lie–Poisson brackets—is ubiquitous as basic equations of physics.
It is this kind of dynamics that is the subject of the present paper.

An important example of Lie–Poisson dynamics is given by Euler’s equations for rigid body dynamics with a Lie–Poisson bracket based on the Lie algebra of infinitesimal rotations
[
37
]
(see also
[
62
,
61
]
).
This example often serves as inspiration for generalization and exploration of new concepts.
The Lie–Poisson bracket for the full ideal fluid including magnetohydrodynamics was given in
Morrison and Greene 1980
; see also
Vishik and Dolzhansky 1978
.
This example was followed by the Lie–Poisson formulation of the Maxwell–Vlasov system of equations in
Morrison 1980
, with a correction given in
Weinstein and Morrison 1981
and
Marsden and Weinstein 1982
and a limitation to the correction pointed out in
Morrison 1982
, which was followed up more recently in
Morrison 2013
,
Heninger and Morrison 2020
and then in
Lainz et al. 2019
.
Another example from the mid 1980s is that given in
Marsden et al. 1984a
, where the Lie–Poisson bracket for general moment closures of the kinetic hierarchy were given.
There is now a large literature with very many subsequent publications that can be found, e.g., in
Morrison 1998
,
Morrison 2006
and
Arnold and Khesin 1998
.

Given the ubiquity of the Lie–Poisson form, it is natural to inquire about its origin.
One thread extends back to the quasi-coordinate description of
Poincaré 1901
(see also
Hamel 1904
), where Euler’s equations for rigid body dynamics, and its Lagrangian counterpart—the Euler–Poincaré equation—was first formulated on a general Lie algebra.
This idea was applied to fluid dynamics in
Arnold 1966
,
Arnold 1969,
where Euler’s equations for the incompressible fluid are seen to be the Euler–Poincaré equation on the Lie algebra of a diffeomorphism group, putting the work of
Lagrange 1788
into modern language. (See
Morrison et al. 2020
for commentary.)
Although these works did not explicitly give the Lie–Poisson bracket, the equations of motion for a reduced dynamics were obtained.

The main geometrical idea behind the Lie–Poisson brackets for the dynamics of rigid body, fluids, and plasmas is now understood as a process of reduction from canonical to noncanonical Hamiltonian form as follows (see, e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 1999
): The configuration space of the systems is a Lie group
\mathsf{G}
, and the basic equation of the system is a canonical Hamiltonian system defined on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathsf{G}
.
However, the Hamiltonian has
\mathsf{G}
-symmetry, and thus one may reduce the system to the dual
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
\mathsf{G}
.
The resulting equation on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
has Lie–Poisson form.

There are also examples where the system is defined on a Lie group
\mathsf{G}
, but the symmetry of the system is broken. A well-known example is the heavy top, where the symmetry is broken by the gravity; another is the compressible fluid, where density plays a role similar to gravity for the heavy top case.
In either case, it is known that one can still recover the full symmetry by extending the configuration space to a semidirect product
\mathsf{G}\ltimes V
using a
\mathsf{G}
-representation on a vector space
V
; see, e.g.,
Marsden et al. 1984b
,
Marsden et al. 1984c
,
Holm et al. 1998
.
As a result, one again obtains a Lie–Poisson system on the dual of the semidirect product Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes V
, which is a special case of Lie–Poisson brackets based on Lie algebra extensions
[
64
]
that occur in a variety of physical systems including magnetohydrodynamics (see
Marsden and Morrison 1984
).

1.2.
Collectivization

Another class of Lie–Poisson systems arises as a result of so-called
collectivization
in the sense of
Guillemin and Sternberg 1980
(see also
Holmes and Marsden 1983
and
Guillemin and Sternberg 1990
).
Given a Poisson manifold
P
and an equivariant momentum map
\mathbf{M}\colon P\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
associated with an action of a Lie group
\mathsf{G}
on
P
, one can show that
\mathbf{M}
is a Poisson map with respect to the Poisson bracket on
P
and the Lie–Poisson bracket on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
; see, e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 1999
.
This implies the following:
Given that Hamiltonian
H\colon P\to\mathbb{R}
is
collective
in the sense that there exists
h\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
H=h\circ\mathbf{M}
, the flow
\Phi_{t}
of the Hamiltonian vector field on
P
defined by
H
and the flow
\phi_{t}
of the Lie–Poisson dynamics on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
defined by
h
are related by
\mathbf{M}
as
\mathbf{M}\circ\Phi_{t}=\phi_{t}\circ\mathbf{M}
.

The term “collective” comes from the motivating examples of
Guillemin and Sternberg 1980
,
Guillemin and Sternberg 1990
) such as the liquid drop model in nuclear physics, where one seeks a set of equations that describe aggregate motions of a number of particles “as if it were a rigid body or liquid drop”; the idea behind this dates back to
Riemann 1860
(see also
Rosensteel 1988
and
Morrison et al. 2009
).

1.3.
Clebsch Canonization and Collectivization

What we refer to as Clebsch canonization or just “
canonization
” for short is the opposite of the collectivization described above: One first has a Lie–Poisson equation on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and then constructs a cotangent bundle
T^{*}Q
and an equivariant momentum map
\mathbf{M}\colon T^{*}Q\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
so that solutions of the new
canonical
Hamiltonian dynamics on
T^{*}Q
can be mapped by
\mathbf{M}
to those of the Lie–Poisson dynamics on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

This theoretical concept is motivated by the early use of potentials for describing the velocity field of fluid mechanics: long before the introduction of the vector potential for representing a magnetic field, researchers considered various potential representations of velocity fields, the most famous of which is due to Clebsch
[
7
,
8
]
.
The connection between the Lie–Poisson brackets for fluid dynamics and the canonical Hamiltonian description in terms of the Clebsch representation was first given in
Morrison 1981
,
Morrison 1982
,
Morrison and Greene 1982
, while two-dimensional vortex dynamics was considered later in
Marsden and Weinstein 1983
.
See also
Ohsawa 2019
for the Clebsch representation of the heavy top dynamics.
A general theory for Lie–Poisson brackets, motivated by
[
46
]
was given in
Morrison 1998
and the present work places this in the geometric setting described above.

1.4.
Lie–Poisson Integrators

Compared to symplectic integrators for canonical Hamiltonian systems (see, e.g.,
Hairer et al. 2006
and
Leimkuhler and Reich 2004
), integrators for Lie–Poisson equations seem to be studied less extensively.
Some earlier works include
Ge and Marsden 1988
and
Channell and Scovel 1991
, and are based on generating functions.

Engø and Faltinsen 2001
used Lie group methods by exploiting the property that Lie–Poisson dynamics evolves on coadjoint orbits on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
More recently,
Ma and Rowley 2010
developed a variational integrator for the Lie–Poisson equation by discretizing the corresponding variational principle.
See also
Martín de Diego
for a more recent survey of Lie–Poisson integrators.

Our work gives a concrete realization of the general theory of
collective integrators
developed by
McLachlan et al. 2014
; see also
McLachlan et al. 2015
,
McLachlan et al. 2016
.
The main advantage of collective integrators is that one can construct Lie–Poisson integrators that preserve the coadjoint orbits out of existing symplectic integrators.
On the other hand, the main disadvantage is that it is not always clear how one can find a suitable cotangent bundle
T^{*}Q
and momentum map
\mathbf{M}
.

It is important that the symplectic integrator “descends”
[
39
]
to a Lie–Poisson integrator that preserves the coadjoint orbits.
One can show that this is the case with the symplectic Runge–Kutta method if, for example, one can find another momentum map
\mathbf{J}
on
T^{*}Q
so that the pair of momentum maps
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{J}
constitute a dual pair, as discussed in
[
39
, Theorem 7]
.
Existing constructions (see, e.g.,
McLachlan et al. 2014
,
McLachlan et al. 2015
,
McLachlan et al. 2016
) of such momentum maps
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{J}
are rather ad-hoc, and thus are limited to Lie–Poisson equations on relatively simple spaces such as
\mathfrak{o}(p,q,\mathbb{F})
,
\mathfrak{sp}(2k,F)
,
\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{F})
,
\mathfrak{u}(p,q)
with
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}
, and some semi-direct products.

1.5.
Main Result and Outline

We propose a systematic canonization that potentially works for a wider class of Lie–Poisson equations by constructing a momentum map
\mathbf{M}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
; hence the Lie–Poisson equation on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is “canonized” to a canonical Hamiltonian system
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\dim\mathfrak{g}
.
We first show how this works in coordinate calculations in
Section

2
.

In
Section

3
, we give a geometric interpretation of this setting.
We also find a Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
that characterizes the intrinsic symmetry of the canonized Hamiltonian system (or the
canonized system
for short).
Its action on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
gives rise to another momentum map
\mathbf{J}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}^{*}
that becomes invariants of the canonized system.
We then prove in
Theorem

3
that the momentum maps
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{J}
constitute a dual pair (in the sense of
Weinstein 1983
) under a certain condition.

Section

4
addresses the invariants of the canonized system.
For any (real) Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the momentum map
\mathbf{J}
has at least two components including an invariant associated with the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
.
Additionally, if
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, then there is another invariant associated with the Killing form.
Furthermore, we show that if the Lie–Poisson bracket on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
possesses a Casimir then there is a corresponding Noether-type invariant (momentum map) in the canonized system as well.

In
Section

5
, we first briefly review the idea of the collective integrators, and then show some numerical results.
Assuming the dual pair from
Theorem

3
, symplectic Runge–Kutta methods applied to our canonized system yields Lie–Poisson integrators that preserve the coadjoint orbits and hence the Casimirs exactly.
We demonstrate it using a couple of examples: the Kida vortex
[
23
]
(see also
Meacham et al. 1997
) and the heavy top on a movable base with a stabilizing control
[
9
]
.

2.
Clebsch Canonization

2.1.
Lie–Poisson Bracket

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an
n
-dimensional Lie algebra, and
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis for it with the structure constants
\{c_{ij}^{k}\}_{1\leq i,j,k\leq n}
, i.e.,
[E_{i},E_{j}]=c_{ij}^{k}E_{k}
; note that we use Einstein’s summation convention throughout the paper.
We may then define the dual basis
\{E_{*}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
by setting
{\left\langle E_{*}^{i},E_{j}\right\rangle}=\delta^{i}_{j}
under the standard dual pairing
{\left\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\right\rangle}\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
.

For any smooth
f\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the derivative
Df(\mu)\in\mathfrak{g}
evaluated at
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
so that, for any
\delta\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,

{\left\langle\delta\mu,Df(\mu)\right\rangle}=\left.\frac{d}{ds}f(\mu+s\delta\mu)\right|_{s=0}.

This results in the coordinate expression

Df(\mu)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\mu)\,E_{i}.

Then one defines the
(+)
-Lie–Poisson bracket (see
Section

3.3
for the
(-)
-Lie–Poisson bracket) on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as follows:
For any
f,g\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(1)

\left\{f,g\right\}_{+}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\left\langle\mu,{\left[Df(\mu),Dg(\mu)\right]}\right\rangle}=\mu_{k}c^{k}_{ij}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{j}}.

The Lie–Poisson equation for a Hamiltonian
h\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is the Hamiltonian system defined using the above Poisson bracket, i.e.,

(2a)

\dot{\mu}_{i}=\left\{\mu_{i},h\right\}_{+}=\mu_{k}c^{k}_{ij}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\mu_{j}},

or equivalently,

(2b)

\dot{\mu}=-\operatorname{ad}_{Dh(\mu)}^{*}\mu.

2.2.
Clebsch Canonization in Coordinates

The main idea of the Clebsch canonization (see
Morrison 1981
,
Morrison 1998
) is the following: Given an
n
-dimensional Lie–Poisson bracket (
2
), we would like to find a corresponding
2n
-dimensional canonical Hamiltonian system.
In other words, we would like to find a relationship between the Poisson bracket (
1
) and the canonical Poisson bracket of the form

(3)

\left\{F,G\right\}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial G}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i}},

where
F,G\colon T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Suppose that
\mu=\mu_{i}E_{*}^{i}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and
(q,p)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
are related as follows:

(4)

\mu_{i}=c^{k}_{ij}q^{j}p_{k}.

For any smooth
f,g\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, we may define
F,G\colon T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
F(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f(\mu)
where
\mu
and
(q,p)
are related as above; similarly for
G
as well.
Then, by the chain rule, we have

\frac{\partial F}{\partial q^{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial q^{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}c^{k}_{ji}p_{k},\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial p_{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}c^{i}_{jk}q^{k}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\left\{F,G\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial G}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i}}

\displaystyle=q^{l}p_{m}{\left(c^{i}_{kl}c^{m}_{ji}-c^{i}_{jl}c^{m}_{ki}\right)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{k}}

\displaystyle=q^{l}p_{m}{\left(-c^{i}_{kl}c^{m}_{ij}-c^{i}_{lj}c^{m}_{ik}\right)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{k}}

\displaystyle=q^{l}p_{m}c^{i}_{jk}c^{m}_{il}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}c^{i}_{jk}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left\{f,g\right\}_{+}(\mu),

where the fourth equality follows from the Jacobi identity for the structure constants.

Therefore, given any Lie–Poisson bracket in terms of
\mu
, one can obtain an canonized canonical bracket via (
4
).
The Hamiltonian of the canonized system will have the form
H(q,p)=h(\mu)
with
\mu
given as in (
4
).
If the resulting equations of the canonical system are solved for
t\mapsto(q(t),p(t))
, then
t\mapsto\mu(t)
constructed according to (
4
) solves the Lie–Poisson equation (
2
).

3.
Geometry of Clebsch Canonization

This section gives a geometric interpretation of the canonization presented in
Section

2.2
.
Particularly, we show that the map (
4
) is the momentum map associated with a natural
\mathfrak{g}
-action on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
.

3.1.
Left
\mathfrak{g}
-action on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}

Let
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be the cotangent bundle of
\mathfrak{g}
and define

\mathfrak{g}\times T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to T^{*}\mathfrak{g};\qquad(\xi,(q,p))\mapsto(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p)=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p).

In coordinates, we can write it as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\xi^{i}c_{ij}^{k}q^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{k}}-\xi^{i}c_{ij}^{k}p_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}

\displaystyle=\xi^{i}c_{ij}^{k}{\left(q^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{k}}-p_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\right)}.

Let us show that it is a left Lie algebra action, i.e., for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
,

[\xi,\eta]_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}=-[\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}},\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}],

where the bracket on the left-hand side is the commutator in
\mathfrak{g}
whereas the one on the right is the Jacobi–Lie bracket of vector fields on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
.
In fact, in the coordinate representation with respect to the standard basis
\{\partial/\partial q^{i},\partial/\partial p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we have,

\displaystyle D\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle={\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial q}(\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}q)\cdot\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,\,-\frac{\partial}{\partial p}(\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}p)\cdot(-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p)\right)}

\displaystyle={\left(\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,\,\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle={\left([\eta,[\xi,q]],\,\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p\right)},

where the second line follows because
q\mapsto\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}q
and
p\mapsto\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}p
are linear.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle[\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}},\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}]

\displaystyle=D\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}-D\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle={\left([\eta,[\xi,q]]-[\xi,[\eta,q]],\,\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle={\left([q,[\xi,\eta]],\,\operatorname{ad}_{[\xi,\eta]}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle={\left(-\operatorname{ad}_{[\xi,\eta]}q,\,\operatorname{ad}_{[\xi,\eta]}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle=-[\xi,\eta]_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}},

where we used the Jacobi identity of the commutator on
\mathfrak{g}
and the following dual version of it: for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
,

(5)

\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}=\operatorname{ad}_{[\xi,\eta]}^{*}.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of a Lie group
\mathsf{G}
, then we may first consider the left
\mathsf{G}
-action on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
as follows:

\Phi\colon\mathsf{G}\times T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to T^{*}\mathfrak{g};\qquad(g,(q,p))\mapsto(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}q,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}^{*}p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\Phi_{g}(q,p).

Clearly this is the cotangent lift of the adjoint action of
\mathsf{G}
on
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then its infinitesimal generator gives the above Lie algebra action:

\left.\frac{d}{ds}\Phi_{\exp(s\xi)}(q,p)\right|_{s=0}=(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p)=\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p).

3.2.
Momentum Map
\mathbf{M}^{+}

Let us find the momentum map associated with the above Lie algebra action.
For any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, define
M_{\xi}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting

X_{M_{\xi}}=\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}},

where
X_{M_{\xi}}
is the Hamiltonian vector field for
M_{\xi}
with respect to the canonical symplectic form on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
, i.e.,

X_{M_{\xi}}=\frac{\partial M_{\xi}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\frac{\partial M_{\xi}}{\partial q^{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}.

It is a straightforward calculation to find

M_{\xi}(q,p)={\left\langle p,\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q\right\rangle}=-{\left\langle\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p,\xi\right\rangle}.

The momentum map
\mathbf{M}^{+}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is then defined so that

{\left\langle\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p),\xi\right\rangle}=M_{\xi}(q,p),

which yields

(6)

\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p)=-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p.

We can obtain a coordinate expression for
\mathbf{M}^{+}
using the dual basis
\{E_{*}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as follows:

(7)

\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p)=-q^{j}c_{ji}^{k}p_{k}\,E_{*}^{i}=c_{ij}^{k}q^{j}p_{k}\,E_{*}^{i},

which is nothing but (
4
) obtained earlier.

The above momentum map is infinitesimally equivariant:
For any
\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
and any
(q,p)\in T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
,

\displaystyle T_{(q,p)}\mathbf{M}^{+}\cdot\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{ad}_{[\eta,q]}^{*}p+\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}p

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{ad}_{\eta}^{*}\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p),

where we again used the dual version (
5
) of the Jacobi identity.
The infinitesimal equivariance implies (see, e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 1999
) that
\mathbf{M}^{+}
is a Poisson map with respect to the canonical Poisson bracket (
3
) on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the
(+)
-Lie–Poisson bracket (
1
) on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, i.e., for any smooth
f,g\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(8)

\left\{f\circ\mathbf{M}^{+},g\circ\mathbf{M}^{+}\right\}=\left\{f,g\right\}_{+}\circ\mathbf{M}^{+}.

3.3.
Right Action and
(-)
-Lie–Poisson bracket

In order to find a Poisson map
\mathbf{M}^{-}
with respect to the
(-)
-Lie–Poisson bracket

(9)

\left\{f,g\right\}_{-}(\mu)=-{\left\langle\mu,{\left[Df(\mu),Dg(\mu)\right]}\right\rangle}=-\mu_{k}c^{k}_{ij}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu_{j}}

on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, one starts instead with the following right Lie algebra action:

\mathfrak{g}\times T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to T^{*}\mathfrak{g};\qquad(\xi,(q,p))\mapsto(-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p)=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p),

which satisfies
[\xi,\eta]_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}=[\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}},\eta_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}]
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of a Lie group
\mathsf{G}
, then we may consider the following right
\mathsf{G}
-action on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
:

\Phi\colon\mathsf{G}\times T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to T^{*}\mathfrak{g};\qquad(g,(q,p))\mapsto(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}q,\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{*}p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\Phi_{g}(q,p).

Then we have

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\exp(t\xi)}(q,p)\right|_{t=0}=(-\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}q,\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}p)=\xi_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p).

The associated momentum map is

(10)

\mathbf{M}^{-}(q,p)=\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p=-c_{ij}^{k}q^{j}p_{k}\,E_{*}^{i},

and satisfies, for any
f,g\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(11)

\left\{f\circ\mathbf{M}^{-},g\circ\mathbf{M}^{-}\right\}=\left\{f,g\right\}_{-}\circ\mathbf{M}^{-}.

3.4.
Clebsch Canonization

Summarizing the above arguments, we have the following special class of
symplectic realization
or
Clebsch variables
(see, e.g.,
Marsden and Weinstein 1983
):

Theorem 1

(Clebsch canonization of Lie–Poisson equations)
.

Given a smooth function
h\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, define
H\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

H(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=h\circ\mathbf{M}^{\pm}(q,p)=h{\left(\mp\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p\right)},

using
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
defined in (
6
) or (
10
), respectively.
Let
t\mapsto(q(t),p(t))
be a solution to the canonical Hamiltonian system (referred to as the canonized system)

(12)

\dot{q}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad\dot{p}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}

on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
t\mapsto\mu(t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbf{M}^{\pm}(q(t),p(t))
gives a solution to the Lie–Poisson equation

\dot{\mu}=\mp\operatorname{ad}_{Dh(\mu)}^{*}\mu,

defined in terms of the
(\pm)
-Lie–Poisson bracket, (
1
) or (
9
), respectively.

Proof.

It easily follows from the property that
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
is Poisson (see (
8
) and (
11
)) with respect to the canonical Poisson bracket on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
and the
(\pm)
-Lie–Poisson bracket on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, respectively.
∎

3.5.
Dual Pair

Moreover, there exists a dual pair of momentum maps in the sense of
Weinstein 1983
associated with the above canonization.
In order to define the other momentum map, let us first write
\mathbf{M}^{+}
from (
7
) (the case with
\mathbf{M}^{-}
is virtually the same) using its components as follows:
Writing
z=(q,p)
for short,

\mathbf{M}^{+}(z)=M_{i}(z)\,E_{*}^{i},\qquad M_{i}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=q^{T}\mathcal{C}_{i}p,

where
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the
n\times n
matrices defined in terms of the structure constants as

(\mathcal{C}_{i})_{jk}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=c_{ij}^{k}.

However, we may also write
M_{i}(z)
as a bilinear form on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
as follows:

(13)

M_{i}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{T}\mathcal{M}_{i}z,

where
\{\mathcal{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the
2n\times 2n
symmetric matrices defined as

\mathcal{M}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{C}_{i}\\
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the symplectic algebra

\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\left\{\tilde{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}\ |\ \tilde{\xi}^{T}\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{J}\tilde{\xi}=0\right\}}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbb{J}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}.

It is well known that
\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
can be identified with the set
\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})
of
2n\times 2n
real symmetric matrices equipped with the Lie bracket

[\xi,\eta]_{\mathbb{J}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\xi\mathbb{J}\eta-\eta\mathbb{J}\xi

via the following map:

\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})\to\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R});\qquad\xi\mapsto\tilde{\xi}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{J}\xi.

Now, let us define a subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
as follows:

(14)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{h}&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\left\{\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\ |\ \tilde{\sigma}^{T}\mathcal{M}_{i}+\mathcal{M}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}=0\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\right\}}\\
&\cong{\left\{\sigma\in\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})\ |\ [\sigma,\mathcal{M}_{i}]_{\mathbb{J}}=0\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\right\}}\\
&={\left\{\sigma\in\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})\ |\ (\sigma\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i})^{T}=-\sigma\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i}\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\right\}}.\end{split}

More concretely, we may write

\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{12}^{T}&\sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R}),

and see the following characterization of
\mathfrak{h}
:

(15)

\sigma\in\mathfrak{h}\iff\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{C}_{i}\sigma_{11}=-\sigma_{11}\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T},\\
\mathcal{C}_{i}\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{12}\mathcal{C}_{i},\\
\sigma_{22}\mathcal{C}_{i}=-\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T}\sigma_{22}\end{array}\right.\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

One can show that
\mathfrak{h}
is non-trivial for any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
:

Proposition 2
.

Let
\kappa
be the
n\times n
symmetric matrix defining the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
, i.e.,

\kappa(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\operatorname{ad}_{x}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{y})=\kappa_{ij}x^{i}y^{j},

or, in terms of the structure constants,
\kappa_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=c_{ik}^{l}c_{jl}^{k}
.
Then, the following elements of
\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})
are contained in
\mathfrak{h}
:

\sigma_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\varkappa\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, if
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, then

\varkappa^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\kappa^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

is also contained in
\mathfrak{h}
as well.

Proof.

See
Appendix

A
.
∎

Using the above subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
, we can construct the following dual pair in the sense of
Weinstein 1983
with some additional assumptions:

Theorem 3

(Dual pair associated with Clebsch canonization)
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})
defined in (
14
), and consider the
\mathfrak{h}
-action on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
defined by

\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{X}(T^{*}\mathfrak{g});\qquad\sigma\mapsto\sigma_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tilde{\sigma}z=\mathbb{J}\sigma z,

and let
\mathbf{J}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}^{*}
be its associated momentum map.
Then:

(i)

J_{\sigma}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\left\langle\mathbf{J}(z),\sigma\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}z^{T}\sigma z
for any
z\in T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
and any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{h}
.

(ii)

There exists an open subset
U\subset T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathbf{M}^{+}
(or
\mathbf{M}^{-}
) and
\mathbf{J}
are both submersions.

(iii)

If
U
is non-empty and
\dim\mathfrak{h}=\dim\mathfrak{g}
, then

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{h}^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{g}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{J}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{M}^{+}}

is a dual pair with respect to the standard symplectic form
\Omega
on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
(restricted to
U
), i.e.,

{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}^{\Omega}=\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\quad\forall z\in U,

and similarly with
\mathbf{M}^{-}
in place of
\mathbf{M}^{+}
, where
(\,\cdot\,)^{\Omega}
stands for the symplectically orthogonal complement.

Proof.

See
Appendix

B
.
∎

4.
Properties of Canonization

4.1.
Subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
and Momentum Map
\mathbf{J}

The dual pair constructed in
Theorem

3
implies that the momentum map
\mathbf{J}
is an invariant of the canonized system (
12
).
For example, for
\sigma_{0},\varkappa,\varkappa^{*}\in\mathfrak{h}
from
Proposition

2
, the corresponding invariants are, writing
z=(q,p)
for short,

(16)

J_{0}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=J_{\sigma_{0}}(z)=p\cdot q,

and

(17)

J_{\varkappa}(z)=q^{T}\kappa q=\kappa(q,q),\qquad J_{\varkappa^{*}}(z)=p^{T}\kappa^{-1}p=\kappa^{-1}(p,p),

where we abused the notation by using
\kappa
and
\kappa^{-1}
for both the bilinear forms and the associated matrices.

Note that, depending on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathsf{sym}(2n,\mathbb{R})
may be larger than
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\{\kappa_{0},\varkappa\}
or
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\{\kappa_{0},\varkappa,\varkappa^{*}\}
, and so there may be more invariants, as we shall see in the example presented in
Section

5.3
, where
\dim\mathfrak{h}=9
.

4.2.
Casimirs and Momentum Maps

If the Lie–Poisson bracket possesses a Casimir, then there must be a corresponding invariant for the canonical Hamiltonian system (
12
).
We would like to show that the invariant is indeed a Noether invariant (momentum map) of the canonized system:

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
f\colon\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Casimir of the Lie–Poisson bracket (
1
) or (
9
), and define
F\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
F\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f\circ\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
, i.e.,

F(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f{\left(\mp\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p\right)}.

Let us also define

\gamma\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g};\qquad\gamma(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=Df(\mathbf{M}^{\pm}(q,p))=Df{\left(\mp\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p\right)},

and consider the following
\mathbb{R}
(Lie algebra) action

\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}(T^{*}\mathfrak{g});\qquad(s,(q,p))\mapsto(\operatorname{ad}_{s\gamma(q,p)}q,-\operatorname{ad}_{s\gamma(q,p)}^{*}p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=s_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(q,p),

where
\mathfrak{X}(T^{*}\mathfrak{g})
stands for the space of vector fields on
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
.
Then the momentum map corresponding to the action is
F
.
Furthermore, the Hamiltonian
H
is infinitesimally invariant under the action, and thus
F
is an invariant of the canonized system (
12
).

Proof.

Notice first that

X_{F}(q,p)={\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial p},\,-\frac{\partial F}{\partial q}\right)}=\pm{\left(\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}q,\,-\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}p\right)},

and so, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

X_{sF}=s_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}.

This shows that
F
is the momentum map corresponding to the above symmetry.

Let us show that
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
is infinitesimally invariant under the above
\mathbb{R}
-action:
First note that, since
f
is a Casimir, its derivative
Df
satisfies
\operatorname{ad}_{Df(\mu)}^{*}\mu=0
for any
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
; see, e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 1999
.
Therefore, setting
\mu=-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p
in particular, we have

-\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p=\operatorname{ad}_{Df{\left(-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p\right)}}^{*}(-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p)=0

for any
(q,p)\in T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
.
Then, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the directional derivative of
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
along the vector field
s_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}
yields

\displaystyle s_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}[\mathbf{M}^{\pm}](q,p)

\displaystyle=\mp s{\left(\operatorname{ad}_{[\gamma(q,p),q]}^{*}p-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle=\mp s{\left(\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p-\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}p+\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle=\mp s{\left(\operatorname{ad}_{\gamma(q,p)}^{*}\operatorname{ad}_{q}^{*}p\right)}

\displaystyle=0,

where we used the dual version (
5
) of the Jacobi identity in the second equality.

This implies that the Hamiltonian
H\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=h\circ\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
is infinitesimally invariant under the
\mathbb{R}
-action as well.
That
F
is an invariant of (
12
) follows easily from either that
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
is Poisson or Noether’s Theorem (see, e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 1999
).
∎

5.
Collective Integrators via Clebsch Canonization

5.1.
Collective Lie–Poisson Integrators via Clebsch Canonization

Let
\Psi_{\Delta t}\colon T^{*}\mathfrak{g}\to T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
be an integrator with time step
\Delta t
for the canonized system (
12
).
In order for the resulting Lie–Poisson integrator to be
collective
in the sense of
McLachlan et al. 2014
,
McLachlan et al. 2015
, the method
\Psi_{\Delta t}
must “descend” to a Lie–Poisson integrator
\psi_{\Delta t}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
\psi_{\Delta t}\circ\mathbf{M}^{\pm}=\mathbf{M}^{\pm}\circ\Psi_{\Delta t}
and also that preserves coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
(and hence its Casimirs) exactly.

According to
McLachlan et al. 2014
, one of the possible realizations of collective integrators is to have a dual pair of momentum maps
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}
and
\mathbf{J}
where
\mathbf{J}
is quadratic, and use any symplectic Runge–Kutta method for
\Psi_{\Delta t}
.
Since
Theorem

3
gives the desired form of dual pair, the symplectic Runge–Kutta methods applied to our setting gives a collective integrators on
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}(U)\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

We use the Gauss–Legendre methods—a family of implicit Runge–Kutta methods based on the points of Gauss–Legendre quadrature—as the symplectic integrator
\Psi_{\Delta t}
for the canonized system (
12
).
The order of a Gauss–Legendre method is
2s
if it is based on
s
points
[
16
, Theorem 5.2]
; the simplest is of order 2 and is the Implicit Midpoint Method.
In this paper, we will use the
4^{\rm th}
order Gauss–Legendre method; see, e.g.,
Leimkuhler and Reich 2004
.

5.2.
Example 1: Kida Vortex

The Kida vortex
[
23
]
is an elliptical vortex patch of constant vorticity in a two-dimensional flow.
The equations of motion obtained by
Kida 1981
describe the time evolution of the semi-major axis
a
and semi-minor axis
b
and of the angle
\phi
of orientation of the ellipse in a steady shear background flow:

\dot{a}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}a\sin(2\phi),\qquad\dot{b}=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}b\sin(2\phi),\qquad\dot{\phi}=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{a^{2}-b^{2}}\cos(2\phi),

where
\epsilon>0
is the constant rate of strain of the background shear flow.
Defining the aspect ratio
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=b/a
, the equations reduce to

(18)

\dot{\lambda}=-\epsilon\lambda\sin(2\phi),\qquad\dot{\phi}=\frac{\lambda}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2}}\cos(2\phi).

It is then not difficult to see that the above system of equations is Hamiltonian
[
43
,
44
]
.

Furthermore,
Meacham et al. 1997
showed that (
18
) follows from a Lie–Poisson equation on
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)^{*}
obtained by projecting the Lie–Poisson structure for the 2D incompressible Euler equation onto quadratic moments of the vorticity.
Specifically, let
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
be the Lie algebra of the Lie group

\mathsf{SO}(2,1)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\ |\ R^{T}KR=K\right\}}\text{ with }K\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

A basis for
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
is given by

\{E_{1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right],E_{2}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right],E_{3}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\}
,
for which the structure constants
\{c^{k}_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j,k\leq 3}
satisfy, for any
\mu\in\mathfrak{so}(2,1)^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\mu_{k}c^{k}_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mu_{3}&\mu_{2}\\
-\mu_{3}&0&-\mu_{1}\\
-\mu_{2}&\mu_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

This is the (class A) type VIII Lie algebra of the Bianchi classification
[
11
,
69
]
.
The Casimir of the corresponding Lie–Poisson bracket (
1
) is then

(19)

f_{1}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{3}^{2},

which is essentially the area of the ellipse.

The Killing form in this case is

\kappa(x,y)=2(x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}-x_{3}y_{3}).

It is clearly non-degenerate, and thus there are two additional invariants (see (
17
)):

(20)

J_{1}(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2}\kappa(q,q)=q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}-q_{3}^{2},\qquad J_{2}(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{-1}(p,p)=p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}-p_{3}^{2}.

It is also easy to show that
\mathfrak{h}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\{\sigma_{0},\varkappa,\varkappa^{*}\}
using (
15
) (see also
Proposition

2
); hence
J_{0}
from (
16
) along with these two invariants are the components of the momentum map
\mathbf{J}
.

The variables
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
are related to the original variables
(\lambda,\phi)
as follows:

(21)

\mu_{2}=\frac{\pi}{16}{\left(\lambda-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)}\cos(2\phi),\qquad\mu_{3}=-\frac{\pi}{16}{\left(\lambda+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)}\cos(2\phi),\qquad\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{3}^{2}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{64}.

With the Hamiltonian (the “excess energy” of the elliptical vortex patch
[
42
]
)
h\colon\mathfrak{so}(2,1)^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(22)

h(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\epsilon\mu_{2}+\omega\mu_{3}-\frac{\pi}{8}\ln{\left(\frac{\pi}{8}-\mu_{3}\right)},

the Lie–Poisson equation
\dot{\mu}=-\operatorname{ad}_{Dh(\mu)}^{*}\mu
from (
2
) yields

(23)

\dot{\mu}_{1}=\omega\mu_{2}+\epsilon\mu_{3}+\frac{\pi\mu_{2}}{\pi-8\mu_{3}},\qquad\dot{\mu}_{2}=-\mu_{1}{\left(\omega+\frac{\pi}{\pi-8\mu_{3}}\right)},\qquad\dot{\mu}_{3}=\epsilon\mu_{1}.

One can then show that, using (
21
), the above Lie–Poisson equation gives rise to the original equation (
18
) of
Kida 1981
.

The map (
6
) yields (lowering the indices for
q
for simplicity),

(24)

\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p)=(q_{2}p_{3}+q_{3}p_{2},\,-q_{3}p_{1}-q_{1}p_{3},\,-q_{1}p_{2}+q_{2}p_{1}).

Following the proof (in
Appendix

B
) of
Theorem

3
, we can show that there exists an open set
U
that is dense in
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
on which
\mathbf{M}^{+}
and
\mathbf{J}
are submersions.
We also saw above that
\dim\mathfrak{h}=3=\dim\mathfrak{g}
.
Hence we have a dual pair as described in
Theorem

3
.

We then have the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(q,p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=h(\mathbf{M}^{+}(q,p))

\displaystyle=-\epsilon(q_{3}p_{1}+q_{1}p_{3})-\omega(q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1})-\frac{\pi}{8}\ln{\left(\frac{\pi}{8}+q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}\right)}.

The canonized system (
12
) is therefore

(25)

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\dot{q}_{1}=\omega q_{2}-\epsilon q_{3}+\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}+\pi/8},&\displaystyle\dot{q}_{2}=-\omega q_{1}-\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{q_{1}}{q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}+\pi/8},&\displaystyle\dot{q}_{3}=-\epsilon q_{1},\\
\displaystyle\dot{p}_{1}=\omega p_{2}+\epsilon p_{3}+\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{p_{2}}{q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}+\pi/8},&\displaystyle\dot{p}_{2}=-\omega p_{1}-\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}p_{2}-q_{2}p_{1}+\pi/8},&\displaystyle\dot{p}_{3}=\epsilon p_{1}.\end{array}

\sigma_{0}

Let us next show that
\varkappa\in\mathfrak{h}
.

\mathcal{C}_{i}\kappa=-\kappa\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T}

To that end, first recall that the Jacobi identity for the Lie bracket in

c^{m}_{il}c^{l}_{jk}+c^{m}_{jl}c^{l}_{ki}+c^{m}_{kl}c^{l}_{ij}=0.

Using this identity, we see that, for any

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}_{i}\kappa)_{jl}=(\mathcal{C}_{i})_{jk}\kappa_{kl}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{jm}(\mathcal{C}_{i})_{lm}

\displaystyle=-(\kappa\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T})_{jl}.

\mathfrak{g}

\kappa

\varkappa^{*}
is defined.

), it suffices to show
\kappa^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{i}=-\mathcal{C}_{i}^{T}\kappa^{-1}

\sigma_{T^{*}\mathfrak{g}}(z)=X_{J_{\sigma}}(z),

where
X_{J_{\sigma}}(z)
is the Hamiltonian vector field defined by
J_{\sigma}
, i.e.,

X_{J_{\sigma}}(z)=\mathbb{J}\nabla J_{\sigma}(z).

Therefore, we have
\sigma z=\nabla J_{\sigma}(z)
, and thus
J_{\sigma}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{T}\sigma z
.

For (
ii
), it suffices to show that
W(z)\subset\ker{\bf d}{J_{\sigma}}(z)
for any
z\in T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
and any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{h}
.
First recall from (
14
) that
\sigma\in\mathfrak{h}
if and only if
\sigma\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i}
is skew-symmetric for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Therefore, we see that, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

{\left\langle{\bf d}{J_{\sigma}}(z),\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i}z\right\rangle}=z^{T}\sigma\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i}z=0.

Since
W(z)
is spanned by
\{\mathbb{J}\mathcal{M}_{i}z\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we have
W(z)\subset\ker{\bf d}{J_{\sigma}}(z)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3
.

(i)

This is
Lemma

B.2
(
i
).

(ii)

In order to find the open subset
U\subset T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
, notice first that
T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}=A(z)
(see (
B.2
)) is full-rank if and only if
A(z)A(z)^{T}
is non-singular.
However,

A(z)A(z)^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{T}\mathcal{M}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
z^{T}\mathcal{M}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{M}_{1}z\dots\mathcal{M}_{n}z\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\left\|\mathcal{M}_{1}z\right\|}^{2}&\dots&z^{T}\mathcal{M}_{1}\mathcal{M}_{n}z\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
z^{T}\mathcal{M}_{n}\mathcal{M}_{1}z&\dots&{\left\|\mathcal{M}_{n}z\right\|}^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Since each entry is quadratic in
z
, the function
d_{A}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\det{\left(A(z)A(z)^{T}\right)}
is a polynomial of
z
as well.
Hence the pre-image
U_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=d_{A}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\})\subset T^{*}\mathfrak{g}
is an open set on which
T\mathbf{M}^{+}
is full-rank.
Let
\{\sigma_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{h}
, and define

B(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{T}\sigma_{0}\\
\vdots\\
z^{T}\sigma_{m-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, running the same argument with
B(z)
in place of
A(z)
, one can find
U_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=d_{B}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\})
on which
T\mathbf{J}
is full-rank.
Then
U\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=U_{1}\cap U_{2}
gives the desired open set.

(iii)

Note first that
\mathbf{M}^{+}
and
\mathbf{J}
being submersions imply that they are submersions onto open sets
\mathbf{M}^{+}(U)\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and
\mathbf{J}(U)\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, respectively.
Therefore, we have, for any
z\in U
,

\dim\!{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}=2n-\dim\!{\left(\operatorname{im}T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}=2n-\dim\mathfrak{g},

and

\displaystyle\dim\!{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\right)}=2n-\dim\!{\left(\operatorname{im}T_{z}\mathbf{J}\right)}=2n-\dim\mathfrak{h}.

Thus the assumption
\dim\mathfrak{h}=\dim\mathfrak{g}=n
implies that
\dim\!{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}=\dim\!{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\right)}=n
for any
z\in U
.
However, using
Lemma

B.1
and
Lemma

B.2
(
ii
), we have

{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}^{\Omega}\subset\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\quad\forall z\in T^{*}\mathfrak{g}.

But then
\dim{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}^{\Omega}=n=\dim\!{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\right)}
for any
z\in U
, and thus we obtain

{\left(\ker T_{z}\mathbf{M}^{+}\right)}^{\Omega}=\ker T_{z}\mathbf{J}\quad\forall z\in U.
