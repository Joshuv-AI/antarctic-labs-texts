1.
Introduction

A general motivation for studying caloric polynomials comes from their ubiquity in the theory of parabolic PDEs as finite order solutions of the heat equation. After showing that tangent functions of solutions to a parabolic PDE are homogeneous caloric polynomials (hereafter abbreviated
hcps
), one can deduce strong unique continuation principles and estimate the dimension of nodal and singular sets of solutions
[
Che98
]
. In a similar vein, it was recently shown that zero sets of hcps appear as the supports of tangent measures in non-variational free boundary problems for caloric measure
[
MP21
]
. Hcps also arise in geometric contexts such as understanding the dimension of ancient caloric functions on manifolds with polynomial growth
[
CM21
]
.

In this paper, with a goal of confirming existence of singular strata in the aforementioned free boundary regularity problem for caloric measure (see §
1.3
), we investigate basic topology of nodal domains of hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
(space
\times
time). In particular, for each ambient dimension and for each parabolic degree (see §
1.1
), we would like to determine the minimum and maximum number of possible nodal domains. This type of question has been studied extensively for spherical harmonics (see §
1.2
for a brief survey). Also, up to scaling by a constant, for each degree
d
, there is a unique hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
of degree
d
and the number of nodal domains is precisely
2\lceil d/2\rceil
(see §
2
). Thus, the problem is to determine what happens for time-dependent hcps in at least two space variables. We fully determine the minimum number of domains (with detailed constructions) and establish asymptotic bounds for the maximum number of domains.

When

for all

Theorem 1.2

(maximum number of nodal domains)
.

For all
n\geq 2
, the maximum number
M_{n,d}
of nodal domains of time-dependent homogeneous caloric polynomials in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of degree
d
is
\Theta(d^{n})
as
d\rightarrow\infty
. More precisely, for all
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
,

(1.2)

\left\lfloor\frac{d}{n}\right\rfloor^{n}\leq M_{n,d}\leq\binom{n+d}{n}.

We prove Theorem
1.2
in §
3
. The lower bound on
M_{n,d}
in (
1.2
) is based on an elementary construction using products of hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
. The upper bound follows from an indirect application of Courant’s nodal domain theorem, exploiting a connection between negative time-slices of caloric polynomials in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
and Hermite orthogonal polynomials.

Remark 1.3
.

Classical theorems in algebraic geometry imply that the maximal number of nodal domains of polynomials
p:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of degree
d
is
\Theta(d^{n+1})
as
d\rightarrow\infty
and lies between
\binom{d}{0}+\cdots+\binom{d}{n+1}
and
(d+1)^{n}(d+2)
; see
[
Sta07
, Proposition 2.4]
for the lower bound (from hyperplane arrangements) and
[
Mil64
, Theorem 3]
for the upper bound. Thus, perhaps not unsurprisingly, the behavior of hcps is distinct from the behavior of general polynomials. Finding the exact value of
M_{n,d}
appears to be a difficult problem. Except for some low degrees (
d\leq 6
), the corresponding problem for spherical harmonics in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is also open
[
Ley96
]
.

We discuss proof strategies for the main theorems and outline the paper in §
1.4
.

1.1.
Definitions and examples

Let us decode the terminology in the statements of the main theorems.
We study the heat equation
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=\{(x,t):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
\Delta\equiv\Delta_{x}=\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{i}}
. The natural notion of homogeneity in this setting is anisotropic.

Definition 1.4
.

A function
f(x,t)
is
parabolically homogeneous
of degree
d\in\mathbb{R}
if

(1.3)

\displaystyle f(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{d}f(x,t)\quad\text{for all $\lambda>0$ and $(x,t)$.}

Definition 1.5
.

A polynomial
p(x,t)
is a
homogeneous caloric polynomial (hcp) of degree
d
if
p
satisfies the heat equation and is parabolically homogeneous of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dotsc\}
. We say that
p
is
time-dependent
if
\partial_{t}p\not\equiv 0
.

Example 1.6
.

For any exponent
k\in\mathbb{N}
and multi-index

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the monomial
t^{k}x^{\alpha}
is parabolically homogeneous of degree
2k+\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=2k+\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
. In particular, if
p(x,t)
is parabolically homogeneous of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then each monomial in
p(x,t)
has the form
t^{k}x^{\alpha}
where
2k+\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=d
. Parabolic and algebraic homogeneity are distinct notions; e.g.,

(1.4)

p(x,t)=t^{2}+tx^{2}+x^{4}/12

is an hcp of degree
4
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
, but
p
is not
algebraically
homogeneous. Nevertheless, the parabolic degree and the algebraic degree of an hcp always coincide.

Remark 1.7
.

If
p
is a time-dependent hcp, then the degree of
p
is at least 2.

Definition 1.8
.

The
nodal domains
of a continuous function
u(x,t)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
are the connected components of the set
\{(x,t):u(x,t)\neq 0\}
. We let
\mathcal{N}(u)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
denote the number of nodal domains of
u
. The
nodal set
of
u
is
\{(x,t):u(x,t)=0\}
.

Remark 1.9
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}+t^{2}=1\}
. By parabolic homogeneity, if
u
is an hcp of degree
d\geq 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then
\mathcal{N}(u)
is the number of connected components of
\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:u(x,t)\neq 0\}
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Remark 1.10
.

For any
n\geq 1
, the mean value property (e.g., see
[
Eva10
, p. 50]
) implies that a non-constant solution
u:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the heat equation takes positive and negative values in any neighborhood of a zero of
u
. In particular,
m_{n,d}\geq 2
for all
n\geq 1
and
d\geq 2
.

Example 1.11
.

For any
1\leq j\leq n
, the polynomial
p(x,t)=2t+x_{j}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is an hcp of degree
2
. Moreover,
p(x,t)
has exactly two nodal domains:
\{t>x_{j}^{2}\}
and
\{t<x_{j}^{2}\}
. Thus, for all
n\geq 1
, the minimum number of nodal domains of time-dependent hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of degree 2 is
m_{n,2}=2
.

Example 1.12
.

Up to scaling by a constant multiple, for every
d\geq 1
, there exists a unique hcp
p_{d}(x,t)
of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
and
m_{1,d}=M_{1,d}=\mathcal{N}(p_{d})=2\lceil d/2\rceil
. See §
2
for the details.

Example 1.13
.

The polynomial

(1.5)

p(x,y,t)=150t(3x+y)+27x^{3}+267x^{2}y+144xy^{2}-64y^{3}

is an hcp of degree 3 in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
and
\mathcal{N}(p)=2
. This example can be found by evaluating (
5.2
) with
d=3
,
\varepsilon=1
, and
(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)=(3/5,4/5)
and multiplying by a constant to obtain a polynomial with integer coefficients. It can be checked that
\nabla p(x,y,t)=0
if and only if
(x,y,t)=(0,0,0)
.

Example 1.14
.

The polynomial

(1.6)

\begin{split}p(x,y,t)=7500t^{2}&+150t(37x^{2}-7xy+13y^{2})\\
&+192x^{4}+176x^{3}y+1623x^{2}y^{2}-351xy^{3}-108y^{4}\end{split}

is an hcp of degree 4 in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
and

. This example can be found by evaluating (

) with

,

.

The polynomial

(1.7)

p(x,y,z,t)=12t^{2}+12tx^{2}+x^{4}+y^{4}-6y^{2}z^{2}+z^{4}

.

1.2.
Comparison with spherical harmonics and Grushin spherical harmonics

Steady-state solutions of the heat equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
correspond to harmonic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Nodal geometry of homogeneous harmonic polynomials
p
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(also called
solid harmonics
) and of the so-called
spherical harmonics

p|_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
is well-studied. See
[
EJN07
,
NS09
,
Log18a
,
Log18b
]
for a short sample, including results for Laplace-Beltrami eigenfunctions on closed Riemannian manifolds beyond the sphere.

Parallel to the quantities
m_{n,d}
and
M_{n,d}
defined in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we let
\tilde{m}_{n,d}
and
\tilde{M}_{n,d}
denote the minimum and maximum number of nodal domains of hhps in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of degree
d
, respectively. In the line (
n=1
), the only harmonic functions are affine and
\tilde{m}_{1,1}=\tilde{M}_{1,1}=2
trivially. In the plane (
n=2
), since harmonic functions can be written as the real part of a complex-analytic function, the nodal sets of hhp degree
d
are rotations of
\{(x,y):\mathrm{Re}(x+iy)^{d}=0\}
and
\tilde{m}_{2,d}=\tilde{M}_{2,d}=2d
for all
d\geq 1
. The situation becomes more interesting when
n\geq 3
.
Lewy
[
Lew77
]
proved that
\tilde{m}_{3,d}=2
whenever
d\geq 1
is odd and
\tilde{m}_{3,d}=3
whenever
d\geq 2
is even. An explicit example of a degree 3 hhp with exactly two nodal domains,

(1.8)

p(x,y,z)=x^{3}-3xy^{2}+z^{3}-(3/2)(x^{2}+y^{2})z,

was found independently by Szulkin
[
Szu79
]
. Badger, Engelstein, and Toro
[
BET17
]
gave a simple construction (utilizing the explicit description of hhps in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) that shows
\tilde{m}_{n,d}=2
for all
n\geq 4
and
d\geq 1
, independent of the parity of
d
.

When
n\geq 3
, any hhp
p
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of degree
d
satisfies the equation

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}p|_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}=d(d+n-2)p|_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}},

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere. Courant’s nodal domain theorem asserts that when listed with multiplicity, the
m
-th eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a closed
C^{1}
Riemannian manifold has at most
m
nodal domains
[
CH53
,
CLMM20
]
. Since the dimension of the vector space of hhps of degree
d\geq 2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is exactly

\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}-\binom{n+d-3}{n-1}

(see
[
ABR01
, Proposition 5.8]
), the maximal number of linearly independent hhps of degree at most
d
is exactly
\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}+\binom{n+d-2}{n-1}=O(d^{n-1})
as
d\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,
\tilde{M}_{n,d}=O(d^{n-1})
as
d\rightarrow\infty
. It is known that the upper bound on
M_{n,d}
provided by Courant’s theorem is not sharp. See
[
Ley96
]
for further discussion and the (still to this day) state-of-the-art bounds on
M_{2,d}
.

In
[
LTY15
]
, Liu, Tian, and Yang study the minimum number
\tilde{m}^{G}_{2,d}
of nodal domains of
Grushin spherical harmonics
, i.e. parabolically homogenenous polynomial solutions
p(x,y,t)
of the operator
L_{G}=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}+(x^{2}+y^{2})\partial_{t}^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
. In particular, they prove that
\tilde{m}^{G}_{2,d}=2
when
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, whereas
\tilde{m}^{G}_{2,d}\geq 3
when
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
; moreover, they provide examples that show
m^{G}_{2,4}=m^{G}_{2,8}=m^{G}_{2,12}=3
. The method of proof is the perturbation technique of Lewy
op. cit.
Other than the fact that the parabolic scaling is the natural scaling for solutions of the Grushin operator and the heat operator, there does not seem to be any immediate connection between Grushin spherical harmonics and hcps. To wit, when
p(x,y,t)
is a Grushin spherical harmonic, so is
p(x,y,-t)
, whereas this strong symmetry property is not enjoyed by time-dependent solutions of the heat equation. Thus, the main results in
[
LTY15
]
cannot be used to establish Theorem
1.1
or vice-versa.

1.3.
Free boundary regularity for caloric measure

The phrase
caloric measure
refers to a family of probability measures
\omega^{X,t}_{\Omega}
that are supported on a subset of the boundary
\partial\Omega
of a space-time domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
and indexed by the points
(X,t)\in\Omega
. They arise in connection with the Dirichlet problem for the heat equation. Stochastically,
\omega^{X,t}_{\Omega}(E)
is the probability that the trace
(B(t+s),t-s)_{s\geq 0}
of a Brownian traveler
B(s)
starting at
B(t)=X
and sent into the past first intersects
\partial\Omega
inside the set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For a consolidated introduction to caloric measure, see
[
BG23
, §3]
, and for extensive background, see
[
Wat12
]
or
[
Doo01
]
. Recent progress on free boundary regularity for caloric measure was made by Mourgoglou and Puliatti
[
MP21
]
, propelling the time-dependent theory for caloric measure closer to the better developed, time-independent theory for harmonic and elliptic measure (see e.g.
[
KPT09
,
AM19
,
BET22
]
). Among other results—and setting aside certain technical assumptions related to the heat potential theory—their work leads to the following description of the asymptotic shape of the free boundary in the two-phase setting.

Theorem 1.16

(Mourgoglou-Puliatti)
.

Assume that
\Omega^{+}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{\Omega^{-}}
and
\Omega^{-}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{\Omega^{+}}
are complementary domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with a sufficiently regular (for heat potential theory), common boundary
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega^{+}=\partial\Omega^{-}
. Let
\omega^{\pm}=\omega^{X_{\pm},t_{0}}_{\Omega^{\pm}}
be caloric measures for
\Omega^{\pm}
with poles at
(X_{\pm},t_{0})\in\Omega^{\pm}
or poles at infinity. If
\omega^{\pm}
are doubling measures,
\omega^{+}\ll\omega^{-}\ll\omega^{+}
, and the Radon-Nikodym derivatives
d\omega^{-}/d\omega^{+}
and
d\omega^{+}/d\omega^{-}
are bounded continuous functions on
\partial\Omega\cap\{t\leq t_{0}\}
, then
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\Gamma_{d_{0}},
where geometric blow-ups (tangent sets)
\Sigma=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}r_{i}^{-1}(\partial\Omega-x)
of
\partial\Omega
at
x\in\Gamma_{d}
along sequence of scales
r_{i}\rightarrow 0
are zero sets of homogeneous caloric polynomials
p
of degree
d
such that
\{p>0\}
and
\{p<0\}
are connected. Cf.
[
MP21
, Theorems III, IV]
.

The main results in this paper validate Mourgoglou and Puliatti’s theory by confirming existence of time-dependent hcps with two nodal domains. Furthermore, we obtain a refined description of the free boundary in low dimensions.

Theorem 1.17
.

When
n=1
,
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}
. When
n=2
,

(1.9)

\partial\Omega=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\Gamma_{4k+1}\cup\Gamma_{4k+2}\cup\Gamma_{4k+3};

for every
d\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, the stratum
\Gamma_{d}
is nonempty for some pair of domains satisying the free boundary condition. When
n=3
, the stratum
\Gamma_{d}
can be nonempty for every
d\geq 1
.

Proof.

When
n=1
,
\Gamma_{d}=\emptyset
for all
d\geq 2
by Example
1.12
. When
n=2
,
\Gamma_{d}=\emptyset
for all
d=4k
by Theorem
1.1
. For the remaining pairs of
n
and
d
, the examples of hcps
p
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of degree
d
with
\mathcal{N}(p)=2
constructed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(see the proofs of Proposition
3.1
and Theorems
5.4
and
5.5
for details) have smooth zero sets outside any neighborhood of the origin. This fact is enough to ensure that the domains
\Omega_{p}^{+}=\{p>0\}
and
\Omega_{p}^{-}=\{p<0\}
associated to
p
satisfy the background regularity hypothesis in Theorem
1.16
. If
\omega_{p}^{\pm}
denote the caloric measures on
\Omega_{p}^{\pm}
with poles at infinity, then it is known that
\omega_{p}^{+}=\omega_{p}^{-}
and
d\omega_{p}^{-}/d\omega_{p}^{+}\equiv 1
(see
[
MP21
, §6]
). Finally, by parabolic homogeneity,
\{p=0\}
is the unique blow-up of
\partial\Omega_{p}^{\pm}=\{p=0\}
at the origin. Therefore,
\Gamma_{d}
is nonempty in
\partial\Omega^{\pm}_{p}
. ∎

1.4.
Proof strategies and outline of the paper

In Section
2
, we recall classical facts about hcps, including their connection with Hermite polynomials. We also introduce a basis of hcps of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, which we use in the constructions in Sections
3
and
5
.

In Section
3
, we first prove that
m_{n,d}=2
for
n\geq 3
and any
d\geq 1
by modifying a construction in
[
BET17
]
. Next, we build time-dependent hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with a large number of nodal domains by taking products of hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
. Finally, after showing that any nodal domain of an hcp necessarily intersects
\{t=-1\}
, we employ the proof of Courant’s nodal domain theorem on negative time slices of hcps to establish the upper bound on the number of nodal domains in Theorem
1.2
. This leaves us to determine the value of
m_{2,d}
for
d\geq 1
.

In Section
4
, we show that
m_{2,d}\geq 3
whenever
d\geq 1
satisfies
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. The main argument leverages the fact that if
p
is an hcp of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
, then the nodal set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
near the north and south poles is asymptotic to the zero set of a homogeneous harmonic polynomial in two variables, which are easy to describe and are perfectly understood.

In Section
5
, we construct examples of time-dependent hcps of degree
d\geq 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
with exactly
m_{2,d}
nodal domains. The basic strategy dates back to
[
Lew77
]
: starting with an hcp of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
whose zero set we can explicitly describe, we perturb the polynomial to produce a time-dependent hcp with the desired number of nodal domains. This style of argument requires a careful analysis of how perturbation affects the topology of nodal sets; see Lemma
5.2
for a precise statement and Section
6
for the proof of the lemma.

2.
Basic properties of homogeneous caloric polynomials

Given an hcp
p(x,t)
of degree
d
, we typically shall choose to write
p(x,t)
in the form

(2.1)

p(x,t)=t^{m}p_{m}(x)+t^{m-1}p_{m-1}(x)+\cdots+p_{0}(x)\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Each coefficient
p_{m-j}=p_{m-j}(x)
is necessarily an algebraically homogeneous polynomial of degree
d-2(m-j)
(see Example
1.6
). Moreover, applying the heat operator to
p
and collecting like powers of
t
, we obtain the relations

(2.2)

0=\Delta p_{m},\quad mp_{m}=\Delta p_{m-1},\quad\cdots,\quad(m-j)p_{m-j}=\Delta p_{m-j-1},\quad\cdots,\quad p_{1}=\Delta p_{0}.

As such, we refer to
p_{m}
as the
harmonic coefficient
of
p
and obtain that the other coefficients
p_{m-j}
are polyharmonic:
\Delta^{j+1}p_{m-j}=0
. In fact, the relations (
2.2
) and the requirement that each coefficient
p_{i}(x)
be homogeneous gives a characterization of

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{E}^{n}_{+})}
). This shows that
V
is bounded. Furthermore, since
\partial V\subset\partial U\cup\{v_{\varepsilon}=0\}\subset\{u=0\}\cup\{v_{\varepsilon}=0\}
, we have
v_{\varepsilon}\leq 0
on
\partial V
. Therefore,
v_{\varepsilon}\leq 0
throughout
V
by the maximum principle for solutions of the heat equation. This contradicts our assertion that
v_{\varepsilon}
is positive at some point of
V
. ∎

Example 3.5
.

For all
k\geq 1
, the basic hcp
p_{2k}(x,t)
of degree
2k
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
has
\mathcal{N}(p_{2k})=2k
, but
\mathcal{N}(p_{2k}|\{t=-1\})=2k+1
. See Figure
2.1
.

We are ready to present an analogue of Courant’s nodal domain theorem for negative time-slices of hcps, which gives the upper bound on
M_{n,d}
. For the original version of Courant’s theorem, see
[
CH53
, Chapter VI, §6]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If
u
is an hcp of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then
\mathcal{N}(u)\leq\binom{n+d}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
H
denote the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{-x^{2}/4}dx)
. By Lemma
2.7
, as
\alpha
ranges over all multi-indices in
\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the polynomials
p_{\alpha}(x):=p_{\alpha}(x,-1)
form an orthogonal basis for
H
. Assign
q(x):=u(x,-1)
. By Lemma
3.4
,
\mathcal{N}(u)\leq\mathcal{N}(q)
. Thus, to establish the theorem, it suffices to prove that
k:=\mathcal{N}(q)\leq\binom{n+d}{n}=\#\{p_{\alpha}(x):|\alpha|\leq d\}
. Enumerate the nodal domains of
q
by
V_{1},\dotsc,V_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We suppose for the sake of contradiction that
k>\binom{n+d}{n}
. Consider the homogeneous system of
\binom{n+d}{n}
linear equations
\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{\alpha,i}a_{i}=0
, where

c_{\alpha,i}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}q(x)\chi_{V_{i}(x)}p_{\alpha}(x)\,e^{-x^{2}/4}dx\quad\text{for all $|\alpha|\leq d$ and $1\leq i\leq k$}.

Since
k>\binom{n+d}{n}
, we can find a non-zero solution vector
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
. By definition of the linear system, the function
g
defined by
g(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}q(x)\chi_{V_{i}}(x)
is orthogonal to
p_{\alpha}
in
H
for all
|\alpha|\leq d
. On the one hand, by (
2.10
) and integration by parts,

(3.2)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert\nabla g(x)\right\rvert^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}\int_{V_{i}}\left\lvert\nabla q(x)\right\rvert^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}\int_{V_{i}}\frac{d}{2}q(x)^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx=\frac{d}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx.\end{split}

On the other hand, we can expand
g
with respect to the orthogonal basis
\{p_{\alpha}\}
for
H
, say
g(x)=\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert>d}b_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(x)

for some coefficients
b_{\alpha}
. Now, by (
2.10
) and integration by parts

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla_{x}p_{\alpha}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}p_{\beta}(x)e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\dfrac{\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert}{2}p_{\alpha}(x)p_{\beta}(x)e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\,dx=0\quad\text{for all }\alpha\neq\beta.

Thus, using (
2.10
) and integration by parts once more,

(3.3)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert\nabla g(x)\right\rvert^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx=\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert>d}b_{\alpha}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert\nabla_{x}p_{\alpha}(x)\right\rvert^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx\\
&\qquad=\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert>d}b_{\alpha}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\dfrac{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}{2}p_{\alpha}(x)^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx\geq\dfrac{d+1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)^{2}e^{-\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}/4}\;dx.\end{split}

Since
g\not\equiv 0
, (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are incompatible. Therefore,
k\leq\binom{n+d}{n}
. ∎

Note that Proposition
3.2
and Theorem
3.6
yield Theorem
1.2
.

4.
HCP in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
, Part I: lower bounds

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it remains to determine the minimum possible number of nodal domains for hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
, where the story is more complicated than in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
(see Corollary
2.5
) and in high enough spatial dimensions (see Proposition
3.1
). In this section, we aim to show that
m_{2,4k}\geq 3
for all
k\geq 1
, i.e. the number of nodal domains of an hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of degree
d\geq 4
with
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
is at least 3.

Towards our goal, we first lower bound the number of nodal domains of a continuous function in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with “alternating nodal structure” at the origin and at infinity. A
chamber of
u
in
V
is a connected component of
V\cap\{u\neq 0\}
relative to
V
. A
positive chamber
is a chamber on which
u>0
; a
negative chamber
is a chamber on which
u<0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous,
u(0)=0
, and
u
has the following nodal structure near the origin and near infinity for some integers
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}},n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 1
with
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 3
:

•

There exists
\varepsilon>0
(small) such that in
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
, the chambers of
u
are sectors based at the origin,
u
alternates signs on adjacent chambers, and
u
is positive on
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
of the chambers. (When
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}=1
, we allow either
0
or
1
negative chambers.)

•

There exists
M>\varepsilon
(large) such that in
B_{M}(0)^{c}
, the chambers of
u
are sectors extending off to infinity,
u
alternates signs on adjacent chambers, and
u
is positive on
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
of the chambers. (When
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}=1
, we allow either
0
or
1
negative chambers.)

The number of nodal domains of
u
is at least
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
.

Figure 4.1.
Parameters
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
count the number of positive chambers of
u
near the origin and near infinity, respectively. On the right, we illustrate the base case
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}=2,n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}=1
(with zero negative chambers at infinity).

Proof.

We adopt the phrase
chamber at the origin
for chambers relative to
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
and the phrase
chamber at infinity
for chambers relative to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{M}(0)
. The proof is by induction on
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
. For any fixed value of
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
, it suffices to establish either the case
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
or the case
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
, as each case follows from the other case and inversion. By the alternating hypothesis, if
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq 2
, then
u
also has
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
negative chambers at the origin; if
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 2
, then
u
also has
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
negative chambers at infinity.

For the base case, suppose that
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}=3
, say
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}=2
and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}=1
. There are two alternatives.
First alternative.
If the two positive chambers of
u
at the origin belong to different nodal domains, then
u
has at least three nodal domains, since
\{u>0\}
has at least two connected components and
\{u<0\}
is nonempty.
Second alternative.
Suppose that the two positive chambers of
u
at the origin belong to the same nodal domain. Then we can find a simple, closed curve
\gamma\subset\{u>0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
that connects two points in distinct positive chambers of
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
; see Figure
4.1
. By the Jordan curve theorem,
\gamma
disconnects the two negative chambers at the origin. Hence
u
has at least three nodal domains, since
\{u<0\}
has at least two connected components and
\{u>0\}
is nonempty. The base case holds.

For the induction step, suppose that there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that the lemma holds whenever
3\leq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}\leq m
. Assume that
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}=m+1
and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}
. We remark that this implies
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq\lceil\frac{1}{2}(m+1)\rceil\geq 2
. We must prove that
u
has at least
m+1
nodal domains. There are two cases, one easy, one harder.

Easy case.
Suppose that no two chambers at the origin belong to the same nodal domain of
u
. Then
\mathcal{N}(u)\geq 2n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq 2\lceil\frac{1}{2}(m+1)\rceil\geq m+1
(since
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq 2
).

Harder case.
Suppose that two distinct chambers
C
and
D
at the origin belong to the same nodal domain. Among all candidates, we choose
C
and
D
that have

(4.1)

\text{the least number of (positive and negative) chambers between them}.

Replacing
u
by
-u
, if needed, we may assume without loss of generality that
C
and
D
are positive chambers. By the alternating condition, the minimum number of chambers strictly between
C
and
D
is at least one and is odd. Suppose there are
2k+1
such chambers and enumerate them and
C
and
D
in order:

(4.2)

C,\ N_{1},\ P_{1},\dots,\ P_{k},\ N_{k+1},\ D,

where
N_{1},\dots,N_{k+1}
are negative and
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
are positive. (When
k=0
, the enumeration is
C,N_{1},D
.) Pause and note that the
2k+2
chambers
C
,
N_{1}
,
P_{1}
, …,
P_{k}
,
N_{k+1}
lie in
2k+2
disjoint nodal domains of
u
, otherwise we would violate (
4.1
). Also note that
k\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}-1
, when
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
is even, and
k\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}-\frac{3}{2}
when
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
is odd. (To see this, draw some examples for small values of
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}
.) Either way,
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}\geq 2k+2
.

To proceed, let
\sigma\subset\{u>0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
be a simple closed curve that connects a point in
C
to a point in
D
, passes through the origin in
\overline{C}
and
\overline{D}
, and encloses the intermediate chambers
N_{1},P_{1},\cdots,P_{k},N_{k+1}
in the bounded component
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\sigma
. Next, let
\tilde{u}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any continuous function such that
\tilde{u}|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega}=u
and
\tilde{u}>0
throughout
\Omega
. Effectively, this collapses the chambers in (
4.2
) into a single positive chamber. Since there are
k+2
positive chambers in (
4.2
), it follows that
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)-(k+1)
and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})=n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)
. Suppose to get a contradiction that
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})\leq 2
. Then

k+2=(2k+2)-(k+1)+1\leq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)-(k+1)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})\leq 2.

Hence
k=0
and
4\leq m+1=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})+1\leq 3
, which is absurd. Therefore,
3\leq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})\leq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)-1\leq m
.

By the induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{u})\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(\tilde{u})+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(\tilde{u})=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)-(k+1)
. Now, the nodal domains of
\tilde{u}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the nodal domains of
u
that intersect
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Together with the additional
2k+1
nodal domains of
u
inside
\Omega
, corresponding to the chambers
N_{1}
,
P_{1}
, …,
P_{k}
,
N_{k+1}
, we conclude that in total
\mathcal{N}(u)\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)+k\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=m+1
. This completes the induction step.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

If
u=u_{d}+f
near the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
u_{d}
is an hhp in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of degree
d\geq 1
,
f\in C^{1}
, and in polar coordinates,
|f(r,\theta)|+\left\lvert\partial_{\theta}f(r,\theta)\right\rvert=o(r^{d})
as
r\rightarrow 0
, then
u
has
2d
chambers with alternating signs in all sufficiently small neighborhoods of the origin and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)=d
.

Proof.

Up to a rotation and renormalization,
u_{d}
is given in polar coordinates by
u_{d}(r,\theta)=r^{d}\sin(d\theta)
. Thus the nodal domains of
u_{d}
consist of
2d
sectors at the origin with opening
\pi/d
with alternating signs. If we consider the function
g_{r}(\theta):\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
g_{r}(\theta)=r^{-d}u(r,\theta)
, then zero set of
g_{r}
is the same as that of
u
intersected with
\partial B_{r}(0)
. Moreover, the assumptions on
f
near the origin give us that

(4.3)

\begin{split}g_{r}(\theta)=\sin(d\theta)+e_{1}(r,\theta),\\
\partial_{\theta}g_{r}(\theta)=d\cos(d\theta)+e_{2}(r,\theta),\end{split}

where
\left\lvert e_{i}(r,\theta)\right\rvert\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow 0
for
i=1,2
. Since
\left\lvert\cos(d\theta)\right\rvert=1
on whenever
\sin(d\theta)=0
, it is straight-forward to check from (
4.3
) that the following holds. For each
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
r_{0}>0
small so that for all
0<r<r_{0}
,
g_{r}(\theta)
has exactly
2d
zeros in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
(one in each of the sectors
\{\left\lvert\theta-j\pi/d\right\rvert<\varepsilon\}
for
0\leq j\leq 2d-1
), at which
g_{r}
changes sign. If we order such zeros
\theta_{0}(r),\theta_{1}(r),\dotsc,\theta_{2d-1}(r)
, then the
\theta_{j}(r)
are continuous in
r
since
u
is a continuous function, and
\theta_{j}(r)\rightarrow j\pi/d
as
r\downarrow 0
. Recalling that the nodal set of
g_{r}
coincides with
\mathcal{N}(u)\cap\partial B_{r}(0)
, one obtains the desired conclusion.
∎

As an application of Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
, we obtain a lower bound on the number of nodal domains of an hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
whose harmonic coefficient has degree at least 2.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
p(x,y,t)
is an hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of the form (
2.1
) and
\deg p_{m}\geq 2
, then
p
has at least
2\deg p_{m}
nodal domains.

Proof.

Put
d=\deg p_{m}
. By Remark
1.9
, it suffices to prove that the nodal set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
has at least
2d
nodal domains. Since
d\geq 1
,
p(N)=p(S)=0
, where
N=(0,0,1)
and
S=(0,0,-1)
are the north and south poles of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, respectively. Let
\pi_{N}
denote the stereographic projection from the north pole of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
onto
\widehat{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then
\pi_{N}(S)=0
and
\pi_{N}(N)=\infty
. Thus, if we can show that the nodal domains of
u:=p\circ\pi_{N}^{-1}
near the origin and near infinity are homeomorphic to
2d
sectors with alternating signs, then
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)=n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=d
and
\mathcal{N}(p)=\mathcal{N}(p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}})=\mathcal{N}(u)\geq n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)+n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=2d\geq 4
by Lemma
4.1
.

The stereographic projection
\pi_{N}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{N\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\pi_{N}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{x}{1-t},\dfrac{y}{1-t}\right),\quad\pi_{N}^{-1}(X,Y)=\left(\dfrac{2X}{R^{2}+1},\dfrac{2Y}{R^{2}+1},\dfrac{R^{2}-1}{R^{2}+1}\right),

where
R=R(X,Y)=(X^{2}+Y^{2})^{1/2}
. Recalling (
2.1
), we have

(4.4)

\begin{split}u(X,Y)&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\dfrac{R^{2}-1}{R^{2}+1}\right)^{j}p_{j}\left(\dfrac{2X}{R^{2}+1},\dfrac{2Y}{R^{2}+1}\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\dfrac{R^{2}-1}{R^{2}+1}\right)^{j}\left(\dfrac{2}{R^{2}+1}\right)^{d+2(m-j)}p_{j}(X,Y)=:\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}(R)p_{j}(X,Y),\end{split}

where each term
p_{j}
is algebraically homogeneous of order
d+2(m-j)
, the lowest order term
p_{m}
is an hhp of degree
d
(see e.g. Figure
3.1
for the case
d=4
), and the coefficients
a_{j}(R)
are radial, bounded real-analytic functions of
X
and
Y
with
\lim_{R\rightarrow 0}a_{j}(R)=(-1)^{j}2^{d+2(m-j)}
. In particular,
a_{m}
does not vanish in
\{0<R\leq 1/2\}
. It follows that
f:=u/a_{m}-p_{m}
is real-analytic near
0
and in polar coordinates,
|f(R,\theta)|+\left\lvert\partial_{\theta}f(R,\theta)\right\rvert=o(R^{d})
as
R\rightarrow 0^{+}
, since each of the terms
p_{0}
, …,
p_{m-1}
is homogeneous with degree at least
d+2
. By Lemma
4.2
, we conclude that
u/a_{m}=p_{m}+f
has
2d
chambers at the origin with alternating signs. Thus, since
a_{m}
does not vanish near the origin,
u=a_{m}(u/a_{m})
also has
2d
chambers at the origin with alternating signs and
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u)=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(u/a_{m})=d
.

Finally, let
v:=p\circ\pi_{S}^{-1}
, where
\pi_{S}
denotes the stereographic projection from the south pole of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
onto
\widehat{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Repeating the argument for
u
shows that
n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(v)=d
. Therefore, because
u
and
v
are related by inversion, we have
n^{+}_{\mathrm{out}}(u)=n^{+}_{\mathrm{in}}(v)=d
.∎

Next, we present a lower bound on the number of nodal domains for a certain class of hcps of degree
4k
.

Proposition 4.4
.

If
p(x,y,t)
is an an hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of the form

(4.5)

\displaystyle p(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=t^{2k}+t^{2k-1}p_{2k-1}(x,y)+\cdots+p_{0}(x,y)

for some
k\geq 1
, then
p
has at least
3
nodal domains.

Figure 4.2.
Proof of Proposition
4.4
. If the union
\tilde{\gamma}
of the path
\gamma
and its reflection
R\circ\gamma
lie in the positivity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
, then the negativity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is

disconnected.

Proof.

By Remark
1.9
, it suffices to prove that the positivity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is disconnected or the negativity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is disconnected. Thus, if
\{p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}>0\}
is disconnected, we are done.

Suppose that the positivity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is connected. By (
4.5
),
p(0,0,1)=p(0,0,-1)=1
. Thus, the north pole
N=(0,0,1)
and south pole
S=(0,0,-1)
belong to the positivity set of
p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
. Hence there exists a simple path
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\{p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}>0\}
such that
\gamma(0)=N
and
\gamma(1)=S
. Now, when
t
is fixed,

is an even polynomial in

and

, since each coefficient
p_{i}(x,y)
in (
4.5
) has even degree. In particular, writing

, we have

is a mirrored path from

to

contained in
\{p|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}>0\}
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}
be the union of the traces of
\gamma
and its reflection

. See Figure

. By the Jordan curve theorem,
\tilde{\gamma}
separates

into two connected components. Let

be any point such that

. Then

, but

lie in different connected components of

. Therefore, the negativity set of

is disconnected. ∎

Combining the previous two results, we see that

when

.

Corollary 4.5

(A lower bound for
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
)
.

If
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
d\geq 4
, then any time-dependent hcp
p
of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
has at least
3
nodal domains. Hence

.

Proof.

Since
p
has degree

, its leading

or of the form

with

. In the first case,

by Proposition

. In the second case,
\mathcal{N}(p)\geq 2\deg p_{m}\geq 4
by Proposition

.
∎

5.
HCP in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
, Part II: constructions

In this section, we construct examples of time-dependent hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of degree

with two nodal domains when

and with three nodal domains when
d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. By Remark
1.9
, counting the nodal domains of an hcp

is equivalent to counting the nodal domains of

. With this reduction, the general strategy in constructing examples is the same in all cases (and is the strategy introduced by
[

]
and used by

5.1.
Two nodal domains when
d\equiv 2\pmod{4}

When
d=4k+2
for some
k\geq 0
, the basic hcp
p_{d}(x,t)=t^{2k+1}+c_{2k}t^{2k}x^{2}+\cdots+c_{0}x^{4k+2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
(see Definition
2.1
) satisfies

p_{d}(0,1)>0\quad\text{and}\quad p_{d}(0,-1)<0.

This simple observation will let us build hcps in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of degree
d
with two nodal domains by essentially copying Lewy’s construction of odd degree hhps in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with two nodal domains.

Theorem 5.4

(cf.
[
Lew77
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Assume
d=4k+2
for some
k\geq 0
. Let
\psi(x,y)=\mathrm{Im}((x+iy)^{d})
and let
p_{d}(x,t)
be the basic hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
. For all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.1)

u_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)\coloneqq\psi(x,y)-\varepsilon p_{d}(x,t)

is a time-dependent hcp in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
of degree
d
and
u_{\varepsilon}
has two nodal domains.

Proof.

It is clear that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a time-dependent hcp of degree
d
whenever
\varepsilon\neq 0
. To proceed, we argue that the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}
looks like the one in Figure
1.1
(right) when
\varepsilon>0
is small.
Note that the nodal set of
\psi|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
has only two singular points: the north and south pole.

We start with a description of the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
near the north pole. Define
v_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\psi(x,y)-\varepsilon p_{d}(x,1)
so that by parabolic homogeneity,

u_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)=t^{d/2}u_{\varepsilon}(x/\sqrt{t},y/\sqrt{t},1)=t^{d/2}v_{\varepsilon}(x/\sqrt{t},y/\sqrt{t})\quad\text{for all $t>0$}.

Hence the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
in each time-slice
\{t=t_{0}\}
(with
0<t_{0}<1
) agrees with the zeros of
v_{\varepsilon}
on the circle
\{x^{2}+y^{2}=(1-t_{0}^{2})/t_{0}\}
. Thus, the nodal set
u_{\varepsilon}
on a small spherical cap at the north pole is homeomorphic to the nodal set of
v_{\varepsilon}
in a small disk at the origin (see Figure
5.1
). Recall that
p_{d}(0,1)>0
. Therefore, we can use Lemma
5.1
to conclude that when
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}
in a small spherical cap at the north pole consists of
d
“southward-opening U-shaped” curves lying in every other longitudinal sector in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
of angle
\vartheta:=\frac{\pi}{d}
, starting with
\{0<\theta<\vartheta\}
and ending with
\{(2d-2)\vartheta<\theta<(2d-1)\vartheta\}
.

For all
t<0
, parabolic homogeneity instead yields

u_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)=|t|^{d/2}u_{\varepsilon}(x/\sqrt{|t|},y/\sqrt{|t|},-1)=|t|^{d/2}\big(\psi(x/\sqrt{|t|},y/\sqrt{|t|})-\varepsilon p_{d}(x/\sqrt{|t|},-1)\big).

Since
p_{d}(0,-1)<0
, we can use Lemma
5.1
to conclude that when
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}
in a small spherical cap at the south pole consists of
d
“northward-opening U-shaped” curves lying in every other longitudinal sector in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
of angle
\vartheta=\frac{\pi}{d}
, starting with
\{\vartheta<\theta<2\vartheta\}
and ending with
\{(2d-1)\vartheta<\theta<2d\vartheta\}
.

Outside of the polar regions, i.e. in the complement of the union of fixed spherical caps at the north and south pole, the nodal set of
\psi|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)
consists of
d
disjoint smooth arcs, along which
|\nabla\psi(x,y)|\geq c>0
for some constant
c
(depending on the size of the caps). Hence the same is true for
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small by the implicit function theorem.

In the end, since the chambers occupied at the north and south pole alternate, we see that when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is a single closed, smooth, Jordan curve. Therefore,
u_{\varepsilon}
has two nodal domains.∎

Lemma 6.1

Let

\begin{split}F_{\varepsilon}(x)&\leq ax^{k}+Cx^{\ell}-\varepsilon(1-Cx)\\

. Since

\begin{split}F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)\geq akx^{k-1}-Cx^{k}-C\varepsilon&=(ak-Cx)x^{k-1}-C\varepsilon\\
&\geq\tfrac{1}{2}ak\varepsilon^{(k-1)/j}-C\varepsilon\geq(\tfrac{1}{2}ak\varepsilon^{-1/(2j)}-C)\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon\end{split}

provided that

is sufficiently small depending only on

. It follows that

C^{1}
function over the
x
-axis, which would contradict the definition of
x_{\min}
. By the remarks above, there is a neighborhood
V
of
(x_{\min},y_{\min})
in which
\mathcal{N}{(G-\varepsilon F)}
coincides with a curve passing through

, which therefore must coincide with

. Hence
(x_{\min},y_{\min})\in\gamma\,\cap(U_{i_{0}}\setminus U_{i_{0}}^{\delta/2})

\mathcal{N}(G-\varepsilon F)\cap\overline{U_{i_{0}}}

U_{i_{0}}\setminus U_{i_{0}}^{\delta/2}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{N}(G-\varepsilon F)\cap(U_{i_{0}}\setminus U_{i_{0}}^{\delta/2})
has exactly one connected component, given by the curve of zeros of
G-\varepsilon F
along the trajectories
x\rightarrow(x,y_{t}(x))
. Since any connected component of
\mathcal{N}(G-\varepsilon F)\cap\overline{U_{i_{0}}}
meets this region, it follows that
\mathcal{N}(G-\varepsilon F)\cap\overline{U_{i_{0}}}
has exactly one connected component and we know that this component is a simple curve. We leave it to the reader to verify that the curve tends to
