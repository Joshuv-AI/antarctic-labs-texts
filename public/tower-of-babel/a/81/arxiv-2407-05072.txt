Tensor products of d d -fold matrix factorizations
Richie Sheng, Tim Tribone

Abstract
. Consider a pair of elements f f and g g in a commutative ring Q Q . Given a matrix factorization of f f and another of g g , the tensor product of matrix factorizations, which was first introduced by Knörrer and later generalized by Yoshino, produces a matrix factorization of the sum f + g f+g . We will study the tensor product of d d -fold matrix factorizations, with a particular emphasis on understanding when the construction has a non-trivial direct sum decomposition. As an application of our results, we construct indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay and Ulrich modules over hypersurface domains of a certain form.

1.
Introduction

Let
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
be a regular local ring,
f
a non-zero element of
\mathfrak{n}^{2}
, and
z
an indeterminate. In
[
Knö87
]
, Knörrer introduced a construction which allowed for the transfer of
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
(MCM) modules between the hypersurface rings
Q/(f)
and
Q\llbracket z\rrbracket/(f+z^{2})
. The construction, which is most easily stated in terms of matrix factorizations and is now commonly referred to as the tensor product of matrix factorizations, has had far-reaching applications. In particular, the careful analysis of the functor described by Knörrer is at the heart of their famous periodicity theorem.

Knörrer’s functor is defined as follows: given a matrix factorization
(\varphi,\psi)
of
f
, that is, a pair of
n\times n
square matrices with entries in
Q
satisfying

\varphi\psi=f\cdot I_{n}=\psi\varphi,

Knörrer defines a new matrix factorization of
f+z^{2}
given by the pair of
2n\times 2n
block matrices

(\varphi,\psi)\otimes(z,z)\coloneqq\left(\begin{pmatrix}zI_{n}&\varphi\\
\psi&-zI_{n}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}zI_{n}&\varphi\\
\psi&-zI_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Yoshino later gave a generalization in
[
Yos98
]
which produces a matrix factorization of any sum
f+g
starting from a factorization of
f
and a factorization of
g
using an analogous formula. In Yoshino’s language, the above matrix factorization can be viewed as the tensor product of the matrix factorization
(\varphi,\psi)
of
f
and of the
(1\times 1)
matrix factorization
(z,z)
of
z^{2}
. Yoshino’s generalization has found applications in homological mirror symmetry
[
Sei10
]
, knot theory
[
KR08
]
, and generally in the theory of MCM modules over hypersurface rings and complete intersection rings.

Our goal is to study a further generalization of Yoshino’s and, by extension, Knörrer’s construction. Namely, we study the tensor product of
d
-fold matrix factorizations. The techniques and ideas introduced in
[
Yos98
]
serve as a framework for this article. We lift Yoshino’s formulas, and some of their proofs, to the case of
d
-fold matrix factorizations for arbitrary
d\geq 2
. The overall theme remains the same: given a matrix factorization of an element
f
and another of
g
, the tensor product produces a matrix factorization (with the same number of factors) of the sum
f+g
.

It is worth noting that a
d
-fold version of Yoshino’s tensor product has appeared in various other forms (several of which actually predate Yoshino’s paper); see
[
Chi78
]
,
[
BHS86
]
,
[
BH89
]
,
[
HUB91
]
,
[
BES17
]
, and
[
Hop21
]
. The version we describe most closely resembles the tensor product of complexes (really of “
d
-complexes”) and therefore we feel it serves as a good candidate for a unified definition. We study some of the basic properties of the tensor product, focusing mostly on those that are relevant to our main goal: understanding its decomposability. As it turns out, the tensor product of two indecomposable matrix factorizations often has many non-trivial direct summands, so, understanding when the operation preserves indecomposability is the main theme of Section
5
. We also give one predictable situation in which the tensor product always decomposes in Section
4
.

Our main application is to the theory of
Ulrich
modules over hypersurface domains. The authors of
[
HUB91
]
showed that any hypersurface ring (and more generally, any
strict complete intersection
) has an Ulrich module using a construction essentially equivalent to the
d
-fold tensor product. In Section
6
, we reproduce their result using elementary properties of the tensor product in the case of a hypersurface domain, and because of the results of Section
5
, we are able to give conditions (and examples) for when the Ulrich modules produced are indecomposable.

2.1.
Notation and conventions

The main construction we will study is a
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
-graded analogue of the tensor product of complexes (technically of
d
-complexes
; see
[
HS21
, Section 3]
). In this setting, the usual sign convention is replaced by appropriate roots of unity. In order to give the construction in our desired generality, we will assume throughout that the ring
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]
-algebra where
\zeta
is a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity in
\mathbb{C}
. That is, we will assume that there is a (unital) ring homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]\to Q
. For instance, if
Q
is a domain containing a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity (in the usual sense), then such an algebra structure exists. We will abuse notation and write
\zeta\in Q
to denote the image of

under this homomorphism.

Due to the cyclic nature of
d
-fold matrix factorization, it will be convenient to take indices modulo
d
. Throughout, the reader should assume that subscripts and other indexing variables are to be interpreted modulo
d
unless otherwise indicated. In particular, components of a morphism will (almost always) be taken modulo
d
which we explain below.

If
\alpha:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{t}M_{j}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}N_{i}
is a morphism in an additive category, we use the notation
\alpha(i,j)
to denote the composition

M_{j}\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{t}M_{j}\xrightarrow{\,\alpha\,}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}N_{i}\twoheadrightarrow N_{i}.

We refer to
\alpha(i,j)
as the
(i,j)
-component of
\alpha
with respect to the given direct sum decompositions. In this case,
\alpha
can be represented by a matrix
\alpha=(\alpha(i,j))_{1\leq i\leq s,1\leq j\leq t}
. Furthermore, if both of the direct sum decompositions are indexed over
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
with respect to the ordering
1\prec 2\prec\cdots\prec d-1\prec d=0
, then the components of
\alpha
are naturally indexed modulo
d
as well, that is, for any
1\leq i,j\leq d
and

\alpha(i+sd,j+td)=\alpha(i,j).

The choice of ordering is simply to index the rows and columns of matrices in the usual way. In other words, as a matrix,
\alpha
is written as

(\alpha(i,j))_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(1,1)&\alpha(1,2)&\dots&\alpha(1,d)\\
\alpha(2,1)&\alpha(2,2)&\dots&\alpha(2,d)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\alpha(d,1)&\alpha(d,2)&\dots&\alpha(d,d)\end{pmatrix}.

denote the ring homomorphism defining the
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]
-algebra structure on
Q
and extend this homomorphism to
\rho:(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta])[x]\to Q[x]
by setting
\rho(x)=x
. Notice that
x^{d}-1=\rho(x^{d}-1)=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}(x-\rho(\zeta)^{i})
in
Q[x]
. Hence the coefficient of
x^{k}
on the right is zero for each
0<k<d
. By picking
z_{i}=\rho(\zeta)^{i}
,
0\leq i\leq d-1
, one can now follow the proof of
[
BES17
, Proposition 2.2]
with minor adjustments to conclude that
X\otimes Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
.
∎

The operation
(-)\otimes(-):\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)\times\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)\to\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
is functorial in both arguments. In particular, if
\alpha:X\to X^{\prime}
is a morphism in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
, then, for homogeneous
x\in X
and
y\in Y
,

(\alpha\otimes 1_{Y})(x\otimes y)=\alpha(x)\otimes y

forms a morphism
\alpha\otimes 1_{Y}:X\otimes Y\to X^{\prime}\otimes Y
in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
. Similarly, if
\beta:Y\to Y^{\prime}
is a morphism in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
, then
1_{X}\otimes\beta:X\otimes Y\to X\otimes Y^{\prime}
defines a morphism in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
.

It will often be useful to consider the components of the tensor product with respect to a given direct sum decomposition. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, we will view
\Phi_{k}
with respect to the direct sum decompositions
\Phi_{k}:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}X_{j-1}\otimes Y_{k+1-j}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}X_{i-1}\otimes Y_{k-i}
. For
1\leq i,j\leq d
, the
(i,j)
-component of
\Phi_{k}
is given by

(3.1)

\Phi_{k}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\zeta^{i-1}1_{X_{i-1}}\otimes\psi_{k+1-i}&\text{ if }j\equiv i\mod d\\
\varphi_{i}\otimes 1_{Y_{k-i}}&\text{ if }j\equiv i+1\mod d\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

Example 3.3
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk\llbracket x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}\rrbracket
where
\Bbbk
is a field containing a primitive third root of unity,
\zeta\in\Bbbk
. Consider the rank one matrix factorizations

, and

. Then
X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(x_{1}x_{2}x_{0}+y_{1}y_{2}y_{0})
is the triple of
3\times 3
matrices

(A_{1},A_{2},A_{0})=\left(\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&x_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta y_{0}&x_{2}\\
x_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}y_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}y_{2}&x_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta y_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}y_{0}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}y_{0}&x_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta y_{2}&x_{2}\\
x_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}y_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right).

If we tensor this with

, we get a rank
9
factorization of the sum
f=x_{1}x_{2}x_{0}+y_{1}y_{2}y_{0}+z_{1}z_{2}z_{0}

(X\otimes^{\zeta}Y)\otimes^{\zeta}Z\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(f)

(B_{1},B_{2},B_{0})=\left(\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}I_{3}&A_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta z_{0}I_{3}&A_{2}\\
A_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}z_{2}I_{3}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{2}I_{3}&A_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta z_{1}I_{3}&A_{2}\\
A_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}z_{0}I_{3}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}z_{0}I_{3}&A_{1}&0\\
0&\zeta z_{2}I_{3}&A_{2}\\
A_{0}&0&\zeta^{2}z_{1}I_{3}\end{pmatrix}\right)

where

and

we show that both of these matrix factorizations are indecomposable and that each of the corresponding cokernel modules are
indecomposable Ulrich modules
over the appropriate hypersurface ring.

3.1.
Properties of the tensor product

As above, we let
d\geq 2
be an integer and
Q
be a commutative
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]
-algebra where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
is a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity. We also fix elements
f,g\in Q
. Our first observation is on the symmetry of the tensor product operation. Note that since
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta^{-1}]
, we can also take the tensor product with respect to
\zeta^{-1}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
. Then there is a natural isomorphism of matrix factorizations
X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong Y\otimes^{\zeta^{-1}}X
in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
.

Proof.

Define a map
\alpha:(X\otimes^{\zeta}Y,\Phi)\to(Y\otimes^{\zeta^{-1}}X,\Phi^{\prime})
given by

\alpha(x\otimes y)=\zeta^{|x||y|}y\otimes x
for homogeneous elements
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. Then
\alpha
is invertible and satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha\Phi(x\otimes y)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{|\varphi(x)||y|}y\otimes\varphi(x)+\zeta^{|x|}(\zeta^{|x||\psi(y)|}\psi(y)\otimes x)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{(|x|-1)|y|}y\otimes\varphi(x)+\zeta^{|x|(1+|\psi(y)|)}\psi(y)\otimes x

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-|y|}\left(\zeta^{|x||y|}y\otimes\varphi(x)\right)+\zeta^{|x||y|}\psi(y)\otimes x

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}\alpha(x\otimes y)

for any homogeneous
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. This implies that
X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong Y\otimes^{\zeta^{-1}}X
in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
. The fact that
\alpha
induces a natural isomorphism (in both components) can be checked directly from the formula.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the case
d=2
, the conclusion of Proposition
3.4
amounts to the isomorphism
X\otimes Y\cong Y\otimes X

[
Yos98
, Lemma 2.1]
. However, this is not the conclusion being made in the case that
d>2
; in general, the tensor product construction with respect to a fixed root of unity is not symmetric when
d>2
.

Example 3.6
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk[x,y,z,u,v,w]
for a field
\Bbbk
containing a primitive
3
rd root of unity,
\zeta\in\Bbbk
. Consider the rank one factorizations
X=(x,y,z)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(xyz)
and
Y=(u,v,w)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(uvw)
. We claim that

X\otimes^{\zeta}Y
and
Y\otimes^{\zeta}X
are not isomorphic in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(xyz+uvw)
.

Proof.

Assume that
(X\otimes^{\zeta}Y,\Phi)\cong(Y\otimes^{\zeta}X,\Phi^{\prime})
, that is, assume that there exist invertible matrices
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
with entries in
Q
such that
\alpha_{k-1}\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{k}^{\prime}\alpha_{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
. Using the direct sum decomposition given in (
3.1
), we will consider corresponding entries on either side of the equation
\alpha_{0}\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\alpha_{1}
. We will also write
\text{c}(\alpha_{k}(i,j))
to denote the constant term of the
(i,j)
th entry of
\alpha_{k}
.

Comparing the (1,1) entries of the above equation gives us

\alpha_{0}(1,1)u+\alpha_{0}(1,3)z=\alpha_{1}(1,1)x+\alpha_{1}(2,1)u.

Equating coefficients of
u
shows that
\text{c}(\alpha_{0}(1,1))=\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(2,1))
, while the coefficients of
x
and
z
give us that
\text{c}(\alpha_{0}(1,3))=\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(1,1))=0
. Similarly, analyzing the other entries, we find that the constant term of each entry of
\alpha_{1}
must be zero except possibly the
(1,2),(2,1),
and
(3,3)
entries which must satisfy

\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(1,2))=\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(2,1))=\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(3,3)).

Repeating this analysis with the second commutative square,
\alpha_{1}\Phi_{2}=\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\alpha_{2}
, we get

\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(1,2))=\zeta\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(2,1))=\zeta^{2}\text{c}(\alpha_{1}(3,3)).

The two conclusions are only possible if each constant term is zero. Hence, all entries of
\alpha_{1}
have constant term zero contradicting that
\alpha_{1}
is invertible.
∎

Although the tensor product is not symmetric in general, Proposition
3.4
implies that the functorial properties given below, which we state only for the left component, hold for both components. In particular, the tensor product is additive in both components and associative. We omit the proofs of additivity and associativity as they are straightforward computations utilizing the corresponding properties of
Q
-modules.

Lemma 3.7
.

(i)

For any
X,X^{\prime}\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
, there is a natural isomorphism
(X\oplus X^{\prime})\otimes Y\cong(X\otimes Y)\oplus(X^{\prime}\otimes Y)
.

(ii)

For any
f,g,h\in Q
and matrix factorizations
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f),Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g),
and
Z\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(h)
, there is an isomorphism
(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\cong X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)
.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
. Then

(i)

TX\otimes Y\cong T(X\otimes Y)=X\otimes TY
;

(ii)

T^{i}X\otimes T^{-i}Y\cong X\otimes Y
for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

Proof.

First notice that the equality in
(i)
follows from the definition and
(ii)
follows from
(i)
. So, it suffices to prove the isomorphism in
(i)
.

Define a map
\alpha:(TX\otimes Y,\Phi)\to(T(X\otimes Y),\Phi^{\prime})
given by

\alpha(x\otimes y)=\zeta^{-|y|}x\otimes y
for homogeneous elements
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. For homogeneous elements
x\in TX
and
y\in Y
,
\Phi
is given by

\Phi(x\otimes y)=\varphi(x)\otimes y+\zeta^{|x|-1}x\otimes\psi(y).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha\Phi(x\otimes y)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-|y|}\varphi(x)\otimes y+\zeta^{|x|-1}\zeta^{-|\psi(y)|}x\otimes\psi(y)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-|y|}(\varphi(x)\otimes y+\zeta^{|x|}x\otimes\psi(y))

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-|y|}\Phi^{\prime}(x\otimes y)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(\alpha(x\otimes y)).

Hence
\alpha
is an invertible morphism
TX\otimes Y\to T(X\otimes Y)
.
∎

If
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is local and
f\in\mathfrak{n}
is a nonzerodivisor, the category
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
is
Frobenius exact

[
Tri22
, Section 2]
. The ring
Q
is assumed to be regular in
[
Tri22
, Section 2]
but this assumption is not needed for the Frobenius exact structure. The factorizations of the form
\mathcal{P}_{i}\coloneqq T^{i}(f,1,1,\dots,1)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, give a complete list of the indecomposable projective objects in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
. We will call a matrix factorization
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)

projective
if it is isomorphic to a direct sum of copies of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, that is, if
X\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\mathcal{P}_{i}^{s_{i}}
,
s_{i}\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is local,
f,g\in\mathfrak{n}
are nonzerodivisors, and that
\zeta\in Q
is a distinguished primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity. If
P
is a projective matrix factorization in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
, then
P\otimes Y
is a projective matrix factorization in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
for any
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
.

Proof.

The tensor product
\otimes^{\zeta}
preserves homotopy equivalences; this is proven in
[
Hop21
, Proposition 2.5.3]
. The conclusion of the lemma follows from this and the fact that an object
P\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(h)
is homotopy equivalent to zero if and only if it is projective (see
[
Hop21
, Theorem 2.8.3]
or
[
Tri22
, Proposition 2.2.15]
for more details).
∎

Recall that if
(X,\varphi)
is a matrix factorization of a nonzerodivisor
f
such that
\operatorname{rank}X=n
, then each
X_{k}
is a free
Q
-module of rank
n
. In particular, we may view
\varphi_{k}
as a square matrix with entries in
Q
. Note that if
\operatorname{rank}X=n
and
\operatorname{rank}Y=m
, then
\operatorname{rank}X\otimes Y=dnm
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
f,g\in Q
be nonzerodivisors and consider matrix factorizations
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
of rank
n
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
of rank
m
. Then
(X\otimes Y,\Phi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
satisfies
\det\Phi_{k}=(-1)^{nm(d+1)}(f+g)^{nm}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

Proof.

First, note that it suffices to compute the determinant of
\Phi_{1}
. Indeed, if
\det\Phi_{1}
can be computed for arbitrary
X
and
Y
, then one can compute
\det\Phi_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
by applying Lemma
3.8
.

Our strategy is to convert
\Phi_{1}
into a lower triangular form using (not necessarily invertible) row operations. We describe these row operations with respect to the direct sum decompostion (
3.1
). We will also utilize the following notation: If
(X,\varphi)
is a matrix factorization and
r\geq 0
is an integer, we let
\varphi_{k}^{\{r\}}=\varphi_{k}\varphi_{k+1}\cdots\varphi_{k+r-1}
for
r>0
and
\varphi_{k}^{\{0\}}=1_{X_{k-1}}
. Note that these superscripts are not taken modulo
d
. In particular,
\varphi_{k}^{\{d\}}=f\cdot 1_{X_{k-1}}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

First we scale each row of
\Phi_{1}
using the diagonal map

\alpha(i,j)=\begin{cases}1_{X_{i-1}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-i\}}&\text{ if }i=j\\
0&\text{ else}\end{cases}.

Note that
\alpha
is not an isomorphism in general. The resulting product is

\alpha\Phi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1_{X_{0}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d\}}&\varphi_{1}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-1\}}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\zeta 1_{X_{1}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-1\}}&\varphi_{2}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-2\}}&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&\zeta^{2}1_{X_{2}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-2\}}&\ddots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&&\zeta^{d-2}1_{X_{d-2}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{2\}}&\varphi_{d-1}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{1\}}\\
\varphi_{0}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{0\}}&0&0&\cdots&0&\zeta^{d-1}1_{X_{d-1}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{1\}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Next, using invertible row operations on
\alpha\Phi_{1}
, we clear above the main diagonal from right to left. We obtain the following lower triangular matrix

\beta=\begin{pmatrix}c_{d-1}(\varphi_{1}^{\{d\}}\otimes 1_{Y_{1}}+1_{X_{0}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d\}})&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
c_{d-2}\varphi_{2}^{\{d-1\}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{0\}}&\zeta 1_{X_{1}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-1\}}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
c_{d-3}\varphi_{3}^{\{d-2\}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{0\}}&0&\zeta^{2}1_{X_{2}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{d-2\}}&&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
c_{1}\varphi_{d-1}^{\{2\}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{0\}}&0&0&&\zeta^{d-2}1_{X_{d-2}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{2\}}&0\\
\varphi_{0}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{0\}}&0&0&\cdots&0&\zeta^{d-1}1_{X_{d-1}}\otimes\psi_{2}^{\{1\}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

where
c_{j}=(-1)^{j}\prod_{k=0}^{j}\zeta^{k}
,
1\leq j\leq d-1
. In particular, we have that
c_{d-1}=(-1)^{d-1}\prod_{k=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{k}=(-1)^{d-1}(-1)^{d+1}=1
. It follows that the
\beta(1,1)=(f+g)\cdot 1_{X_{0}}\otimes 1_{Y_{1}}
and therefore

\det\beta=(f+g)^{nm}\prod_{k=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{nmk}\cdot\det(\alpha)=(-1)^{nm(d+1)}(f+g)^{nm}\cdot\det(\alpha).

Since
\alpha\Phi_{1}
and
\beta
differ by an isomorphism (with determinant 1), we have

\det(\alpha)\det(\Phi_{1})=\det(\beta)=(-1)^{nm(d+1)}(f+g)^{nm}\cdot\det(\alpha).

Since
g
is a nonzerodivisor in
Q
, so is
\det(\alpha)
. The desired conclusion follows by cancelling
\det(\alpha)
.
∎

4.
Knörrer’s direct sum decomposition

An important ingredient to the classification of simple hypersurface singularities
[
BGS87
, Theorem A]
and Knörrer’s periodicity theorem
[
Knö87
, Theorem 3.1]
is a straightforward direct sum decomposition result which says that if
(\varphi,\varphi)
is a matrix factorization of an element
f
and
z
is an indeterminate, then the matrix factorization
(\varphi,\varphi)\otimes(z,z)
of
f+z^{2}
decomposes into a direct sum of the form

(\varphi,\varphi)\otimes(z,z)\cong\left(\begin{pmatrix}zI-i\varphi&0\\
0&zI+i\varphi\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}zI+i\varphi&0\\
0&zI-i\varphi\end{pmatrix}\right)

where
i
is a square root of
-1
. The key here is that both
(\varphi,\varphi)\in\operatorname{MF}^{2}(f)
and
(z,z)\in\operatorname{MF}^{2}(z^{2})
satisfy
T(\varphi,\varphi)=(\varphi,\varphi)
and
T(z,z)=(z,z)
. This was generalized by Yoshino
[
Yos98
, Lemma 3.2]
in the case
d=2
. We give a further extension of these results to the case of
d
-fold matrix factorizations for arbitrary
d\geq 2
.

Let
Q
be a commutative ring and
d\geq 2
an integer. Fix a primitive
2d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity,
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
. In this section we will assume that
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
-algebra so that
Q
contains both a
2d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity and a
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity (the images of
\omega
and
\omega^{2}
respectively). We will abuse notation and denote the image of
\omega
in
Q
by the same symbol.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
be a primitive
2d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity, and
t
be an integer such that
t+d
is even. Then
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-j^{2}+tj}\neq 0
.

Proof.

We first claim that the expression
\omega^{-j^{2}+tj}
does not depend on the representative chosen for
j
modulo
d
(this fact relies on
t+d
being even). If
j\equiv j^{\prime}\mod d
, then either
j\equiv j^{\prime}\mod 2d
, in which case we are done, or
j\equiv j^{\prime}+d\mod 2d
. In the latter case,

-j^{2}\equiv-(j^{\prime})^{2}+d^{2}\mod 2d

and
tj\equiv tj^{\prime}+td\mod 2d
. Thus,

-j^{2}+tj\equiv-(j^{\prime})^{2}+d^{2}+tj^{\prime}+td\equiv-(j^{\prime})^{2}+tj^{\prime}+d(d+t)\equiv-(j^{\prime})^{2}+tj^{\prime}\mod 2d.

Now, we claim that
(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-j^{2}+tj})(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{l^{2}-tl})=d
. Performing the change of variable
s=l-j
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-j^{2}+tj}\right)\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{l^{2}-tl}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-j^{2}+tj}\right)\left(\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{(s+j)^{2}-t(s+j)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{(s^{2}-st)+2sj}\right)

The sum
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{(s^{2}-st)+2sj}=\omega^{s^{2}-st}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{2sj}
is equal to
d
when
s=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and
0
otherwise. This proves the claim and the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
Q
-linear category, that is, an additive category such that the abelian group
\textup{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
has a
Q
-module structure for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, and such that composition of morphisms is
Q
-bilinear. In the proposition below, we will consider a pair of commuting morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
. By this we mean a pair of morphisms
A,B\in\textup{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,X)
, for some
X\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
AB=BA
. The main result of this section is an application of the following identity among matrices in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega,1/d]
-algebra. Let
A
and
B
be commuting morphisms in a
Q
-linear category
\mathcal{C}
and set
\Phi
to be the
d\times d
block morphism

\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}B&A&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\omega^{2}B&A&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&A\\
A&0&0&\cdots&\omega^{2(d-1)}B\end{pmatrix}.

Then there exist isomorphisms
\alpha_{k}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, such that

\alpha_{k-1}\Phi\alpha_{k}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A-\omega^{2k-1}B&0&\dots&0\\
0&A-\omega^{2k+1}B&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&A-\omega^{2k-3}B\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

To simplify the notation, we introduce the polynomial
\mathfrak{p}(m)=-m^{2}+dm
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, we define a map
\alpha_{k}:X^{d}\to X^{d}
, where
X
is the common domain/codomain of
A
and
B
, by the following rule: for any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the
(i,j)
-component of
\alpha_{k}
is defined to be
\alpha_{k}(i,j)=\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)}1_{X}
. As in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, this formula does not depend on the representatives chosen for
i,j,k
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, let
\Phi_{k}^{\prime}
denote the
d\times d
block diagonal morphism in the statement of the proposition. We will show that
\alpha_{k-1}\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{k}^{\prime}\alpha_{k}
by computing the
(i,j)
-component of each composition.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. The only non-zero entries of the
j
th column of
\Phi
are
\Phi(j-1,j)=A
and
\Phi(j,j)=\omega^{2(j-1)}B
. Therefore,

\displaystyle(\alpha_{k-1}\Phi)(i,j)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k-1}(i,j-1)A+\alpha_{k-1}(i,j)\omega^{2(j-1)}B

\displaystyle=\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)}A+\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k+1)+2(j-1)}B

\displaystyle=\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)}A+\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)+d+2k+2(i-1)-1}B

Similarly, the only non-zero entry in the
i
th row of
\Phi_{k}^{\prime}
is
A-\omega^{2k+2(i-1)-1}B
, so

(\Phi_{k}^{\prime}\alpha_{k})(i,j)=\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)}A-\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)+2k+2(i-1)-1}B.

Since
\omega^{d}=-1
, it follows that
(\alpha_{k-1}\Phi)(i,j)=(\Phi_{k}^{\prime}\alpha_{k})(i,j)
for all
i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

It remains to show that
\alpha_{k}
is an isomorphism for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. It suffices to prove this in the case that
\alpha_{k}
is the
d\times d
matrix with entries in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega,1/d]
given by
\alpha_{k}(i,j)=\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-i-k)}
,
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Fixing
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, we observe that
\alpha_{k}
is a
circulant matrix
, as
\alpha_{k}(i,j)
is a function of
j-i
, i.e. if
j-i=j^{\prime}-i^{\prime}
so that
\alpha_{k}(i,j)
and
\alpha_{k}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
lie on the same diagonal (modulo
d
) of
\alpha_{k}
, then
\alpha_{k}(i,j)=\alpha_{k}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
. Since
\alpha_{k}
is circulant, the determinant is given by

\displaystyle\det\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{d}(\alpha_{k}(1,1)+\alpha_{k}(1,2)\omega^{2s}+\alpha_{k}(1,3)\omega^{4s}+\dots+\alpha_{k}(1,d)\omega^{2(d-1)s})

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\omega^{\mathfrak{p}(j-1-k)+2s(j-1)}\right).

By Lemma
4.1
, each of these sums is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega,1/d]
; take
t=2+2k+2s+d
. Hence
\det\alpha_{k}
is a unit implying that
\alpha_{k}
is invertible.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Proposition
4.2
can be viewed as a matrix analogy to the polynomial factorization of
x^{d}+y^{d}
into linear factors (see Example
4.6
). Indeed, this explains the presence of the
2d^{\textit{th}}
roots of unity which allow us to unify the even and odd case into one statement. In particular, when
d
is odd, the condition that
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
-algebra is automatically satisfied by our usual assumption: if
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
is a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity, then
\omega=-\zeta
is a primitive
2d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity. Thus,
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]=\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and therefore
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]
-algebra if and only if it is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
-algebra. This is, however, not true when
d
is even. In this case, the condition that
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
-algebra is strictly stronger.

The main theorem of this section is a direct application of Proposition
4.2
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f,g\in Q
and assume that
Q
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\omega,1/d]
-algebra. Suppose
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
satisfy
TX=X
and
TY=Y
. Then there exists
Z\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
such that

X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}T^{i}Z

where the tensor product is taken with respect to the
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity
\zeta=\omega^{2}
.

Proof.

If
X=(\varphi,\varphi,\dots,\varphi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y=(\psi,\psi,\dots,\psi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
, then set
B=1\otimes\psi
and
A=\varphi\otimes 1
and apply Proposition
4.2
. The factorization
Z
is given by

Z=\left(\varphi\otimes 1-\omega(1\otimes\psi),\varphi\otimes 1-\omega^{3}(1\otimes\psi),\dots,\varphi\otimes 1-\omega^{2d-1}(1\otimes\psi)\right)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g).

∎

If the factorizations
X
and
Y
are only isomorphic to their shifts (not necessarily equal), then a similar result is possible with additional assumptions.

Corollary 4.5
.

Assume
(Q,\mathfrak{n},\Bbbk)
is a complete regular local ring with
\Bbbk
algebraically closed of characteristic not dividing
d
and fix non-zero elements
f,g\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. Suppose
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(g)
satisfy
TX\cong X
and
TY\cong Y
. Then there exists
Z\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f+g)
such that

X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}T^{i}Z

where the tensor product is taken with respect to the
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity
\zeta=\omega^{2}
.

Proof.

The additional assumptions allow us to apply
[
LT23
, Corollary 4.3]
, that is, we may assume that
X=TX
and
Y=TY
. The corollary now follows from Theorem
4.4
.
∎

In the case
d=2
, Theorem
4.4
and Corollary
4.5
recover
[
Yos98
, Lemma 3.2]
and by extension,
[
Knö87
, Proposition 2.7]
.

Example 4.6
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk[x,y]
where
\Bbbk
is a domain.

(i)

Assume that
\Bbbk
contains a primitive fourth root of unity,
i\in\Bbbk
. Consider the rank one factorizations
X=(x,x)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{2}(x^{2})
,
Y=(y,y)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{2}(y^{2})
, and
Z=(x-iy,x-i^{3}y)=(x-iy,x+iy)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})
. Then Theorem
4.4
implies that
X\otimes Y\cong Z\oplus TZ
, that is,

\left(\begin{pmatrix}y&x\\
x&-y\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}y&x\\
x&-y\end{pmatrix}\right)\cong\left(\begin{pmatrix}x-iy&0\\
0&x+iy\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}x+iy&0\\

(ii)

Assume that
\Bbbk
contains a primitive third root of unity,
\zeta\in\Bbbk
. Then
\omega=-\zeta
is a primitive
6^{\textit{th}}
root of unity. Consider the rank one factorizations
X=(x,x,x)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(x^{3})

Z=(x-\omega y,x-\omega^{3}y,x-\omega^{5}y)=(x+\zeta y,x+y,x+\zeta^{2}y)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{3}(x^{3}+y^{3}).

In this case, Theorem
4.4
implies that

X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong Z\oplus TZ\oplus T^{2}Z.

(X,\varphi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)

. Then there is an isomorphism in
\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)

(X\otimes Y)_{x}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(\zeta^{i-1}T^{1-i}Y)^{n}.

(ii)

Suppose
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
is reduced of rank
m
. Then there is an isomorphism in
\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)

(X\otimes Y)_{y}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(\zeta^{1-i}T^{1-i}X)^{m}.

Proof.

Since
(X,\varphi)
is reduced, the entries of
\varphi
live in the ideal
(x)
. This implies that
\varphi\otimes 1_{S/(x)}=0
. On the other hand, since
\psi
has entries in
S_{2}
, we may identify
\psi_{k}\otimes 1_{S/(x)}
with
\psi_{k}
and so, for any
j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, we have

(1_{X_{j}}\otimes\psi_{k})\otimes_{S}1_{S/(x)}\cong\psi_{k}^{\oplus n}.

Considering (
3.1
), we obtain an isomorphism

(X\otimes Y)_{x}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(\zeta^{i-1}T^{1-i}Y)^{n}.

The second isomorphism follows similarly by considering the isomorphism
X\otimes^{\zeta}Y\cong Y\otimes^{\zeta^{-1}}X
given in Proposition
3.4
.
∎

The powers of
\zeta
can also be omitted from these isomorphisms by Lemma
2.4
. In particular, this lemma is the
d
-fold analogue of
[
Yos98
, Lemma 2.9]
and
[
Knö87
, Lemma 2.5]
.

In order to investigate the number of indecomposable direct summands of
X\otimes Y
, we will work directly with idempotents in the endomorphism ring of
X\otimes Y
. The next lemma shows how the functors
(-)_{x}
and
(-)_{y}
interact with morphisms of this form and more generally how they interact with morphisms between tensor products.

Lemma 5.4
.

(i)

Assume
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)
is reduced of rank
n
,
Y,Y^{\prime}\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
, and
\alpha\in\textup{Hom}(X\otimes Y,X\otimes Y^{\prime})
. Then, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the
(i,j)
-component of the induced morphism

\alpha_{x}:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(\zeta^{i-1}T^{1-i}Y^{\prime})^{n}

is given by the morphism

\alpha_{x}(i,j)=(\alpha_{0}(i,j)_{x},\alpha_{1}(i,j)_{x},\dots,\alpha_{d-1}(i,j)_{x})=(\alpha_{k}(i,j)_{x})_{k=0}^{d-1}

in
\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
.

(ii)

Assume
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
is reduced of rank
m
,
X,X^{\prime}\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)
, and
\beta\in\textup{Hom}(X\otimes Y,X^{\prime}\otimes Y)
. Then, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the
(i,j)
-component of the induced morphism

\beta_{y}:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(T^{1-j}X)^{m}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(T^{1-i}X^{\prime})^{m}

is given by the morphism

\beta_{y}(i,j)=(\beta_{k}(2-i+k,2-j+k)_{y})_{k=0}^{d-1}

in
\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)
.

Proof.

The first statement follows directly from (
3.1
) and Proposition
5.3
. To see that
(ii)
holds, we introduce notation. Let
C
be a
d\times d
permutation matrix corresponding to a permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,2,\dots,d\}
. We allow
C
to act on direct sums of modules in the following way: if
H=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}H_{i}
, then we set
CH=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}H_{\sigma(i)}
and view
C
as a homomorphism
C:H\to CH
given by
(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{d})\mapsto(h_{\sigma(1)},h_{\sigma(2)},\dots,h_{\sigma(d)})
.

We will consider two such permutation matrices to justify the formula for
\beta_{y}
:

C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
1&0&\ddots&0&0\\
0&1&\ddots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&\ddots&0&1\\
0&0&\ddots&1&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

If
h:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}H_{j}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}H_{i}^{\prime}
is a homomorphism, then conjugation by
C^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, cycles the components of
h
; for any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
,

(C^{-k}hC^{k})(i,j)=h(i+k,j+k):H_{j+k}\to H_{i+k}^{\prime}.

On the other hand, noting that
B=B^{-1}
, conjugation by
B
gives

(BhB)(i,j)=h(2-i,2-j):H_{2-j}\to H_{2-i}^{\prime}.

With this set up, we write
\beta_{y}
with respect to the direct sum decomposition (
3.1
), and apply these permutations to the components of
\beta_{y}
. One can then check directly that
\beta_{y}
induces the morphism

\left(B(\beta_{0})_{y}B,BC^{-1}(\beta_{1})_{y}CB,\dots,BC^{-(d-1)}(\beta_{d-1})_{y}C^{d-1}B\right)

from
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(T^{1-j}X)^{m}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(T^{1-i}X^{\prime})^{m}
. For any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the
(i,j)
-component of this morphism is

\displaystyle\left((BC^{-k}(\beta_{k})_{y}C^{k}B)(i,j)\right)_{k=0}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\left((C^{-k}(\beta_{k})_{y}C^{k})(2-i,2-j)\right)_{k=0}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\left((\beta_{k})_{y}(2-i+k,2-j+k)\right)_{k=0}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\beta_{k}(2-i+k,2-j+k)_{y}\right)_{k=0}^{d-1}

which is the formula claimed in
(ii)
.

∎

5.1.
Reduced matrix factorizations

In this section, we will restrict our attention to tensor products of reduced matrix factorizations only.

Notation 5.5
.

In addition to the notation given in (
5.2
), we will assume that
X\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)
and
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
are indecomposable reduced matrix factorizations of
f
and
g
respectively. We denote the number of indecomposable summands (counted with multiplicity) in the direct sum decomposition of
X\otimes Y
by
\#(X\otimes Y)
. This quantity is well defined since the Krull-Schmidt Theorem holds in
\operatorname{MF}_{S}^{d}(f+g)
when
S
is complete as it is in this section (see
[
Tri22
, Section 3]
). We also set
r=\gcd(n,m)
where
n=\operatorname{rank}X
and
m=\operatorname{rank}Y
.

Our first result is a direct extension of
[
Yos98
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Theorem 5.6
.

The tensor product
X\otimes Y
has at most
dr
indecomposable summands.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{MF}_{S}^{d}(f+g)
be a summand of
X\otimes Y
. By Lemma
5.3
,
Z_{y}
is isomorphic to a summand of
\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}(T^{i}X)^{m}
(we omit the powers of
\zeta
using Lemma
2.4
). Since
T^{i}X
is indecomposable for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the Krull-Schmidt property of
\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(f)
implies that
Z_{y}\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}(T^{i}X)^{t_{i}}
for some integers
0\leq t_{i}\leq m
. Hence, the rank of
Z_{y}
, which is the same as the rank of
Z
, is
(t_{0}+\dots+t_{d-1})n
. Similarly, reduction modulo
(x)
gives us that the rank of
Z
is equal to
(s_{0}+\dots+s_{d-1})m
for some integers
0\leq s_{i}\leq n
. Together, these imply that
(t_{0}+\dots+t_{d-1})n=(s_{0}+\dots+s_{d-1})m
which must be at least
\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)=nm/r
. Since
X\otimes Y
is of rank
dnm
and we have just shown that any summand must be of rank at least
nm/r
, it follows that
\#(X\otimes Y)\leq dr
.
∎

This upper bound can be improved if the factorizations are highly non-symmetric (cf.
[
Yos98
, Theorem 3.4]
).

Theorem 5.7
.

Suppose
X\not\cong T^{i}X
and
Y\not\cong T^{j}Y
for all
i,j\neq 0
. Then
\#(X\otimes Y)\leq r
.

The proof of Theorem
5.7
will require some preparation.

5.1.1.
The radical of a Krull-Schmidt category

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
Krull-Schmidt category
, that is, an additive category such that every object decomposes into a finite sum of objects which have local endomorphism rings. Given a pair of objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, we will utilize the notion of the
radical
, denoted
\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
. This is an ideal of
\mathcal{C}
and is the category theoretic generalization of the Jacobson radical for rings. Indeed,
\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}
is the unique ideal of
\mathcal{C}
which satisfies
\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,X)=\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}(X))
, for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
, where
\operatorname{rad}(-)
on the right hand side denotes the usual Jacobson radical
[
Kra15
, Proposition 2.9]
. For our purposes, it will suffice to use the following equivalent definition of the radical.

Definition 5.8
.

[
Kra15
, Corollary 2.10]

For objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, the we define the
radical
,
\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
, to be the collection of morphisms
\alpha\in\textup{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
such that
1_{Z}-\sigma\alpha\tau
is an isomorphism for all
\sigma\in\textup{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(Y,Z)
and
\tau\in\textup{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(Z,X)
.

We recall several useful facts about
\operatorname{rad}=\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}
which follow from
[
Kra15
, Section 4]
or
[
Kra21
, Example 2.1.25]
. Note that, while working with objects in the category
\mathcal{C}
, our indices are not taken modulo
d
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
X
and
Y
be objects in a Krull-Schmidt category
\mathcal{C}
.

(i)

Suppose
X=X_{1}^{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{t}^{n_{t}}
and
Y=Y_{1}^{m_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus Y_{s}^{m_{s}}
for indecomposable objects
X_{i},Y_{j}
, and positive integers
n_{i},m_{j}
, for
1\leq i\leq t,1\leq j\leq s
. Then
\operatorname{rad}(X,Y)\cong\bigoplus_{i,j}\operatorname{rad}(X_{i}^{n_{i}},Y_{j}^{m_{j}})
.

(ii)

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are indecomposable objects such that
X\not\cong Y
. Then
\operatorname{rad}(X^{n},Y^{m})=\textup{Hom}(X^{n},Y^{m})
for any
n,m\geq 1
. ∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be indecomposable objects in a Krull-Schmidt category
\mathcal{C}
and set
X=X_{1}^{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{m}^{n_{m}}
for positive integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
. Let
e=(e(i,j))_{i,j}
be an idempotent in
\operatorname{End}(X)
, where
e(i,j):X_{j}^{n_{j}}\to X_{i}^{n_{i}}
. If
e(i,k)e(k,j)\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{j}^{n_{j}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
for all
i,j,k
where either
i\neq k
or
j\neq k
, then there exist idempotents
e_{i}\in\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
,
1\leq i\leq m
, such that
e(i,i)-e_{i}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
and
e(X)\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
, where
e(X)
denotes the direct summand of
X
given by the idempotent
e
.

Proof.

Since
e=e^{2}
, for each
1\leq i\leq m
, we have that

e(i,i)=e^{2}(i,i)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}e(i,k)e(k,i).

Since
e(i,k)e(k,i)\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
for all
i\neq k
by assumption, we have that

e(i,i)\equiv e(i,i)^{2}\mod\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}}).

Since
\mathcal{C}
is a Krull-Schmidt category, we may lift
e(i,i)
to an idempotent in
\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
, that is, there exists an idempotent
e_{i}\in\operatorname{End}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
such that
e(i,i)-e_{i}\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{i}^{n_{i}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
. Let
\epsilon=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}\in\operatorname{End}(X)
and set
\gamma=e-\epsilon
. We claim that
\gamma^{2}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X)
. To see this, first notice that for each
i
,

\gamma^{2}(i,i)=\sum_{k\neq i}e(i,k)e(k,i)+(e(i,i)-e_{i})^{2}\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{i}^{n_{i}},X_{i}^{n_{i}}).

If
i\neq j
, then

\gamma^{2}(i,j)=(e(i,i)-e_{i})e(i,j)+e(i,j)(e(j,j)-e_{j})+\sum_{k\neq i,k\neq j}e(i,k)e(k,j).

Since
\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{C}}
is an ideal in the category
\mathcal{C}
, the fact that
e(i,i)-e_{i}\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{i}^{n_{i}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
implies that
(e(i,i)-e_{i})e(i,j)\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{j}^{n_{j}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
. Similarly, we have that
e(i,j)(e(j,j)-e_{j})\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{j}^{n_{j}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
. Since the rest of the terms are in
\operatorname{rad}(X_{j}^{n_{j}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
by assumption, we have that
\gamma^{2}(i,j)\in\operatorname{rad}(X_{j}^{n_{j}},X_{i}^{n_{i}})
for all
i,j
. Lemma
5.9
(i)
then implies that
\gamma^{2}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X)
as claimed.

We can now finish the proof of the lemma. Since
\gamma^{2}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(X)
, we have that
1_{X}-\gamma^{2}=(1_{X}+\gamma)(1_{X}-\gamma)
is a unit in
\operatorname{End}(X)
. Hence, both
(1_{X}-\gamma)
and
(1_{X}+\gamma)
are units in
\operatorname{End}(X)
. Note that, since
e
and
\epsilon
are idempotents and
\gamma=e-\epsilon
, we have

(1_{X}+\gamma)\epsilon=\epsilon^{2}+\gamma\epsilon=(\epsilon+\gamma)\epsilon=e\epsilon=e(e-\gamma)=e-e\gamma=e(1_{X}-\gamma).

That is,
e
and
\epsilon
are equivalent idempotents. It follows that
e(X)\cong\epsilon(X)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}(X_{i}^{n_{i}})
.
∎

If, in addition, the indecomposable objects
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{m}
are pairwise non-isomorphic, then Lemma
5.9
(ii)
implies that the assumptions of the lemma are automatically satisfied. With this, we are able to prove the Theorem
5.7
. Our approach is a direct generalization of
[
Yos98
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
.

Suppose
\epsilon\in\operatorname{End}(X\otimes Y)
is an idempotent. Applying Lemma
5.3
(i)
, we obtain an idempotent
\epsilon_{x}\in\textup{Hom}(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y))
. By assumption, the indecomposable matrix factorizations
\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y\cong T^{1-j}Y,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, are pairwise non-isomorphic. From Lemma
5.10
, we obtain idempotents
e_{j}\in\operatorname{End}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, such that

\epsilon_{x}(j,j)-e_{j}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n})

and
\epsilon_{x}((X\otimes Y)_{x})\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n})
. Since
T^{1-j}Y
is indecomposable for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the Krull-Schmidt theorem implies

(5.1)

\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n})\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{t_{j}}

for some integers
0\leq t_{j}\leq n
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

Fix
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. We may apply Lemma
2.3
to obtain an equivalent idempotent

e_{j}\sim\begin{pmatrix}1_{t_{j}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

where
1_{t_{j}}
denotes the identity morphism on the first summand of

(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n}=(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{t_{j}}\oplus(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n-t_{j}}.

Then, since
\epsilon_{x}(j,j)-e_{j}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n})
, we use Lemma
2.2
to clear columns and rows and obtain

\epsilon_{x}(j,j)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1_{t_{j}}&0\\
0&A_{j}\end{pmatrix}

for some
A_{j}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}((\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n-t_{j}})
. Putting this together for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, and applying Lemma
2.2
again, we obtain

\epsilon_{x}\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}

where
1
is the identity morphism on
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{t_{j}}
and
B
is a morphism in

\operatorname{End}(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{n-t_{j}})

satisfying
B(j,j)=A_{j}
for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. This implies that there is an isomorphism of free
Q
-modules,
\operatorname{Im}\epsilon_{x}\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{t_{j}}\oplus\operatorname{Im}B
. However, we know that
\epsilon_{x}((X\otimes Y)_{x})\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}(\zeta^{j-1}T^{1-j}Y)^{t_{j}}
and therefore, counting ranks,
B
must be zero. Hence
B(j,j)=A_{j}=0
for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. In other words,

\epsilon_{x}(j,j)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1_{t_{j}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\sim e_{j}

for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Now, recall that the
(i,j)
-component of
\epsilon_{x}
is described by Lemma
5.4
(i)
. In particular, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
,

\epsilon_{x}(j,j)=(\epsilon_{0}(j,j)_{x},\epsilon_{1}(j,j)_{x},\dots,\epsilon_{d-1}(j,j)_{x}).

It follows that
\epsilon_{i}(j,j)_{x}\sim\begin{pmatrix}I_{nt_{j}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
,
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, where
I_{nt_{j}}
is an identity matrix of rank
nt_{j}
. This allows us to make the following rank count:

(5.2)

\operatorname{rank}_{\Bbbk}(\epsilon_{i}(j,j)\otimes_{S}\Bbbk)=\operatorname{rank}_{\Bbbk}(\epsilon_{i}(j,j)_{x}\otimes_{S}\Bbbk)=nt_{j}\text{ for each }i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}.

Applying the same steps to
\epsilon_{y}
, this time using Lemma
5.4
(ii)
, we find that, for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, there exists an integer
0\leq s_{j}\leq m
such that

\epsilon_{i}(2-j+i,2-j+i)_{y}\sim\begin{pmatrix}I_{ms_{j}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. In other words,

(5.3)

\operatorname{rank}_{\Bbbk}(\epsilon_{i}(2-j+i,2-j+i)\otimes_{S}\Bbbk)=ms_{j}\text{ for each }i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}.

Now, let
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and set
i=2j-2
. Then (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) imply that

nt_{j}=\operatorname{rank}_{\Bbbk}(\epsilon_{2j-2}(j,j)\otimes_{S}\Bbbk)=ms_{j}.

On the other hand, if
i=2j-1
, then (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) imply that

nt_{j+1}=\operatorname{rank}_{\Bbbk}(\epsilon_{2j-1}(j+1,j+1)\otimes_{S}\Bbbk)=ms_{j}.

Together, these imply that
nt_{i}=ms_{j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, and that
t\coloneqq t_{1}=t_{2}=\dots=t_{d}
and
s\coloneqq s_{1}=s_{2}=\dots=s_{d}
. This implies that the matrix factorization
e(X\otimes Y)
has rank
\sum_{j=1}^{d}nt_{j}=dnt
which is also equal to
\sum_{j=1}^{d}ms_{j}=dms
. Hence, the rank of the summand
e(X\otimes Y)
must be at least
\operatorname{lcm}(dn,dm)=dnm/r
. Since
\operatorname{rank}X\otimes Y=dnm
, and any summand must have rank at least
dnm/r
, we have
\#(X\otimes Y)\leq r
.
∎

In the following we will make repeated use of the following consequence of Nakayama’s lemma: If
h:G\to F
is homomorphism between finitely generated free
S
-modules of the same rank, then
h
is an isomorphism if and only if
h\otimes 1_{S_{1}}
is an isomorphism. The same holds for
h\otimes 1_{S_{2}}
, and in particular,
h,h\otimes 1_{S_{1}}
, and
h\otimes 1_{S_{2}}
are isomorphisms simultaneously.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{d-1},u_{0}\}
be pairwise relatively prime elements of the maximal ideal of
S_{1}
and set
X=(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{d-1},u_{0})\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{1}}^{d}(u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{d-1}u_{0})
. If
Y\in\operatorname{MF}_{S_{2}}^{d}(g)
satisfies
Y\cong TY
, then
X\otimes Y
is indecomposable.

Proof.

Notice that since
u_{i},u_{j}
are relatively prime for
i\neq j
, if
\alpha:(T^{j}X)^{m}\to(T^{i}X)^{m}
is a morphism, then
\alpha_{k}\otimes_{S_{1}}\Bbbk=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Indeed, if there exists
S_{1}
-homomorphisms satisfying
\beta u_{i}=u_{j}\gamma
, then neither of
\beta
nor
\gamma
can have unit entries.

Set
Z=X\otimes Y
and let
\epsilon\in\operatorname{End}(Z)
be an idempotent. It suffices to show that either
\epsilon=0
or
\epsilon=1_{Z}
.

We first consider reduction modulo
(y)
which gives us an idempotent
\epsilon_{y}\in\operatorname{End}(Z_{y})\cong\operatorname{End}(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}(T^{1-i}X)^{m})
. By the initial observation, for any
i\neq j
, the components of
\epsilon_{y}(i,j)
have entries only in the maximal ideal of
S_{1}
. More specifically, Lemma
5.4
(ii)
gives us that

\epsilon_{y}(i,j)=(\epsilon_{k}(2-i+k,2-j+k)_{y})_{k=0}^{d-1},

and therefore,
\epsilon_{k}(2-i+k,2-j+k)_{y}\otimes\Bbbk=0
for any

and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Letting
i\neq j
and
k
vary over

, and lifting back to

, we have

(5.4)

\epsilon_{k}(i,j)\otimes\Bbbk=0\text{ for all }i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\text{ with }i\neq j.

Next, we consider reduction modulo
(x)
. Note that since
\operatorname{rank}X=1
,
\epsilon_{x}
is an idempotent in
\operatorname{End}(Z_{x})\cong\operatorname{End}(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\zeta^{i-1}T^{1-i}Y)
. Furthermore, since
TY\cong Y
, we have that
Z_{x}
is isomorphic to a direct sum of
d
copies of
Y
. Let
\Lambda=\operatorname{End}(Y)
which is a (likely non-commutative) local ring in the sense that
\Lambda/\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
is a division ring.

By (
5.4
),
\epsilon_{k}(i,j)_{x}
is not an isomorphism for all
i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
with
i\neq j
. It follows from Lemma
5.4
(i)
that
\epsilon_{x}(i,j)
is not an isomorphism of matrix factorizations for all
i\neq j
. Since
\Lambda
is local, this implies that
\epsilon_{x}(i,j)\in\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
for all
i\neq j
, and in particular,
\epsilon_{x}(i,k)\epsilon_{x}(k,j)\in\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
for all
i,j,k
such that
i\neq k
or
j\neq k
. Hence Lemma
5.10
applies; the diagonal component
\epsilon_{x}(i,i)
is an idempotent in the division ring
\Lambda/\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. In other words, for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
,
\epsilon_{x}(i,i)
is either an automorphism of
Y
or in
\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
.

First, consider the case when
\epsilon_{x}(i,i)\in\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Since we showed that
\epsilon_{x}(i,j)\in\operatorname{rad}\Lambda
for all

above, this implies that
\epsilon_{x}\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(Z_{x})
. An idempotent in the radical must be zero and so we conclude that
\epsilon_{x}=0
in this case. It follows that
\epsilon\in\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(Z)
and, using the same reasoning, we have
\epsilon=0
.

Next, assume
\epsilon_{x}(q,q)
is an automorphism for some
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. We will prove that this implies that
\epsilon_{x}(i,i)
is an automorphism for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Since
\epsilon_{x}(q,q)
is an isomorphism, Lemma
5.4
(i)
implies that
\epsilon_{k}(q,q)_{x}
is an isomorphism for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
which in turn implies that
\epsilon_{k}(q,q)
is an isomorphism for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

Let
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. To finish the proof, it suffices to show that
\epsilon_{i}(j,j)
is an isomorphism. In this case, combined with (
5.4
), this would imply that the idempotent

is an isomorphism (as it would be a unit plus a radical element) which is only possible if it is the identity morphism on
Z
.

To show the final claim, we return for a final time to reduction modulo
(y)
. Since

has rank one, any endomorphism
\beta=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d-1})\in\operatorname{End}((T^{1-p}X)^{m}),p\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, has the property that
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=\cdots=\beta_{d-1}
. Thus, invoking Lemma

(ii)
again, we have

\epsilon_{i}(j,j)_{y}=\epsilon_{i+t}(2-j+t,2-j+t)_{y}\text{ for any }t\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}.

In particular, for

, we have
\epsilon_{i}(j,j)_{y}=\epsilon_{i+t}(q,q)_{y}
, which is an isomorphism by assumption. By Nakayama’s lemma,
\epsilon_{i}(j,j)

∎

Using similar techniques one can also show the following.

Let

Proposition 6.1

Suppose that

(i)

\operatorname{rank}X=d^{N-1}

for any

.

Proof.

For each integer
1\leq i\leq N
, let
Y_{i}=(f_{i1},f_{i2},\dots,f_{id})
be the rank one matrix factorization of the product
f_{i}\coloneqq f_{i1}f_{i2}\cdots f_{id}
with
d
factors. In other words,
Y_{i}\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq N
. Then the tensor product
X=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}
is a reduced matrix factorization of
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
of rank
d^{N-1}
. The determinant formula follows from Proposition
3.10
.
∎

A finitely generated module
M
over a local ring
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is called
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
(MCM) if
0pt_{A}M=\dim A
. We will let
\mu_{A}(M)
denote the number of elements in a minimal generating set of
M
and the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of an
A
-module will be denoted
e_{A}(M)
. If
f\in\mathfrak{m}
,
\operatorname{ord}(f)
will denote the order of
f
, that is, largest integer
d
such that
f\in m^{d}
. Recall that if
A
is a regular local ring and
f\in\mathfrak{m}
has order
d
, then the multiplicity of the ring
A/(f)
is exactly
d=\operatorname{ord}(f)
(see
[
HS06
, Example 11.2.8]
).

If
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is a Cohen-Macaulay local ring (
0pt_{Q}Q=\dim Q)
and
f\in\mathfrak{n}
is a nonzerodivisor, then every module associated to a matrix factorization
(X,\varphi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
defines an MCM
R=Q/(f)
-module. More specifically,
\operatorname{cok}(\varphi_{k}\varphi_{k+1}\cdots\varphi_{k+r-1})
is an MCM
R
-module for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and
1\leq r\leq d
; this follows directly from the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is a regular local ring,
f\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
is irreducible, and set
R=Q/(f)
. Let
d=\operatorname{ord}(f)
and write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i1}f_{i2}\cdots f_{id}
for
f_{ij}\in\mathfrak{n},N\geq 2
. Then, for each pair of integers
2\leq k\leq d,1\leq\ell\leq d-1
, there exists a maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module
M
(with no free summands) satisfying

(i)

\mu_{R}(M)=k^{N-1}

(ii)

\operatorname{rank}_{R}(M)=\ell k^{N-2}
.

In particular, the multiplicity of
M
is
e_{R}(M)=d\ell k^{N-2}
.

Proof.

Fix
2\leq k\leq d
and
1\leq\ell\leq d-1
. For each integer
1\leq i\leq N
, choose a partition of the product
f_{i}=f_{i1}f_{i2}\cdots f_{id}
into
k
products of the
f_{ij}
. Note that the index
i
is not taken modulo
d
. The choice of partition can be viewed as a rank one reduced matrix factorization of
f_{i}
with
k
factors, call it
Y_{i}\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{k}(f_{i})
. Let
(X,\varphi)\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{k}(f)
be the tensor product of
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{N}
as in the proof of Proposition
6.1
and set
M=\operatorname{cok}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{\ell}
(the choice to end at
\varphi_{1}
is not important; any composition of
\ell
maps would suffice). Then
X
has rank
k^{N-1}
by Proposition
6.1
and we have a minimal free resolution of
M
over
Q

0\to X_{\ell}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{\ell}}X_{0}\to M\to 0.

Hence, we may tensor this resolution with
R
to obtain a minimal presentation of
M
over
R
. It follows that
M
is an MCM
R
-module minimally generated by
k^{N-1}
elements.

Again by Proposition
6.1
, we have
\det\varphi_{j}=\pm f^{k^{N-2}}
, for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Hence
\det(\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{\ell})=\pm f^{\ell k^{N-2}}
. Since
f
is irreducible,
[
Eis80
, Proposition 5.6]
implies that
\operatorname{rank}_{R}(M)=\ell k^{N-2}
. Finally, since
R
is a domain, the multiplicity of
M
may be computed as
e_{R}(M)=e_{R}(R)\operatorname{rank}_{R}(M)=d\cdot\ell k^{N-2}
.
∎

The minimal number of generators of an MCM module
M
over a local ring
A
is always bounded above by the modules multiplicity. In other words,
\mu_{A}(M)\leq e_{A}(M)
. Maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules for which equality is attained are called
Ulrich
modules. In
[
Ulr84
]
, Ulrich asked if every Cohen-Macaulay local ring has an Ulrich module. After remaining open for nearly fourty years, Ulrich’s question was answered in the negative in
[
IMWZ24
]
. They give examples of Gorenstein, and even complete intersection, local domains which have no Ulrich modules. Earlier examples of non-Cohen-Macaulay local rings which have no Ulrich modules were give by Yhee in
[
Yhe23
]
. In light of these examples, the case of a hypersurface ring stands out as unique.

Corollary 6.3
.

[
HUB91
, Theorem 2.5]

Assume that
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is a regular local ring,
f\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
is irreducible, and set
R=Q/(f)
. Then there exists an Ulrich
R
-module.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
6.2
by picking
k=\operatorname{ord}(f)
and
\ell=1
.
∎

A subcategory
\mathcal{B}
of an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
is called
extension closed
if it is closed under direct summands and if for each short exact sequence
0\to L\to M\to N\to 0
, if
L,N\in\mathcal{B}
, then
M\in\mathcal{B}
as well. As it turns out, the full subcategory of Ulrich modules over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring is rarely extension closed; in dimension one this happens if and only if the ring is regular (see
[
DDD24
, Corollary 5.2.7]
). The constructions of this section induce a similar result for hypersurface domains.

Corollary 6.4
.

Assume
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
is a regular local ring,
f\in\mathfrak{n}
is irreducible, and set
R=Q/(f)
. Then the subcategory of Ulrich
R
-modules is extension closed if and only if
R
is regular.

Proof.

If
R
is regular, then every MCM module is free. Hence every Ulrich module is free and the subcategory of Ulrich modules is therefore extension closed.

Conversely, suppose that
R=Q/(f)
is not regular, that is, suppose that
f\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. Set
d=\operatorname{ord}(f)
. Since
d\geq 2
, we may apply Proposition
6.2
to obtain a reduced matrix factorization
X=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{d-1},\varphi_{0})\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
satisfying

(i)

N=\operatorname{cok}\varphi_{1}
and
L=\operatorname{cok}\varphi_{2}
are Ulrich
R
-modules, and

(ii)

M=\operatorname{cok}(\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2})
satisfies
\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{R}(M)}{e_{R}(M)}=\frac{1}{2}
.

In particular,
M
is not Ulrich. The result now follows since the commutative diagram with exact rows

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}}

induces a short exact sequence of
R
-modules
0\to L\to M\to N\to 0
. Once again, the choice of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
was not important; any pair of consecutive maps in
X
induce a short exact sequence of this form.
∎

Combining the constructions of this section and the indecomposability results of Section
5
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk\llbracket x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rrbracket
for a field
\Bbbk
,
f\in Q
be irreducible of order
e\geq 2
, and set
R=Q/(f)
. Assume further that
\Bbbk
contains a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity for all
2\leq d\leq e
. Suppose that
f
can be written as
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i1}f_{i2}\cdots f_{id}
, for
f_{ij}\in\mathfrak{(}x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
2\leq d\leq e
, such that

(i)

for each
1\leq i,j\leq N
with
i\neq j
, the power series
\{f_{i1},f_{i2},\dots,f_{id}\}
and
\{f_{j1},f_{j2},\dots,f_{jd}\}
are written in distinct sets of variables, and

(ii)

the rank one factorization
(f_{i1},f_{i2},\dots,f_{id})\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f_{i1}f_{i2}\cdots f_{id})
is strongly indecomposable for each
1\leq i\leq N
.

Then there exists an indecomposable MCM
R
-module
M
satisfying
\operatorname{rank}_{R}(M)=d^{N-2}
,
\mu_{R}(M)=d^{N-1},
and
e_{R}(M)=ed^{N-2}
. Moreover, if
d=e
, then
M
is an indecomposable Ulrich
R
-module.

Proof.

The assumptions allow us to apply Theorem
5.15
to conclude that the tensor product

X=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(f_{i1},f_{i2},\dots,f_{id})

is a strongly indecomposable matrix factorization of
f
in
\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
. If
X=(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{d-1},\Phi_{0})
, then Lemma
5.14
implies that
\operatorname{cok}\Phi_{i}
is and indecomposable MCM
R
-module for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. The rank, minimal number of generators, and multiplicity counts all follow as in the proof of Proposition
6.2
.
∎

Theorem
6.5
can be applied to any polynomial (or power series) of the form given in Example
5.17
. We give one specific instance of this below.

Example 6.6
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk\llbracket x_{ij}:1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq d\rrbracket
where
N,d\geq 2
, and
\Bbbk
is a field containing a primitive
d^{\textit{th}}
root of unity. Consider the polynomial

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i1}^{a_{i1}}x_{i2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots x_{id}^{a_{id}}\in Q,

for integers
a_{ij}\geq 1
, and assume that
f
is irreducible. Each of the rank one matrix factorizations
X_{i}=(x_{i1}^{a_{i1}},x_{i2}^{a_{i2}},\dots,x_{id}^{a_{id}})\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(x_{i1}^{a_{i1}}x_{i2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots x_{id}^{a_{id}})
,
1\leq i\leq N
, is strongly indecomposable (see Example
5.17
). Hence, Theorem
5.15
implies that
X=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\in\operatorname{MF}_{Q}^{d}(f)
is strongly indecomposable of rank
d^{N-1}
. Set
X=(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{0})
and
M_{i}=\operatorname{cok}\Phi_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

If
a_{ij}=a\geq 1
for all
i,j
, then
\operatorname{ord}(f)=da
. In this case, each module
M_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, is an indecomposable MCM
R
-module satisfying

\frac{\mu_{R}(M_{i})}{e_{R}(M_{i})}=\frac{d^{N-1}}{(da)d^{N-2}}=\frac{1}{a}.

If
a=1
, then each
M_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, is an indecomposable Ulrich module minimally generated by
d^{N-1}
elements and of rank
d^{N-2}
.

The notion of
Ulrich complexity
was introduced in
[
BES17
]
to quantify the possible ranks of Ulrich modules over hypersurface rings. Indeed, the Ulrich complexity of homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
is the smallest integer
r
such that there exists a matrix factorization
(A,B)\in\operatorname{MF}^{2}(f)
satisfying
\det(A)=f^{r}
and such that the entries of
A
are linear forms. In this case, we will write
\operatorname{uc}(f)=r
. The Ulrich complexity of several examples and classes of polynomials was computed in
[
BES17
]
but, in general, Ulrich complexity is difficult to determine. For an example of their results, consider a generic trinomial
f=x_{11}x_{12}x_{13}+x_{21}x_{22}x_{23}+x_{31}x_{32}x_{33}
(i.e.
N=3,d=3
, and
a=1
from above). They show that
\operatorname{uc}(f)=3

[
BES17
, Proposition 4.10]
. The matrix factorization of
f
constructed in Example
6.6
produces Ulrich modules which attain this minimum and are (necessarily) indecomposable.

If
f
is a homogeneous polynomial which can be written as a sum of
N
products of linear forms, then the tensor product construction gives an upper bound for Ulrich complexity;
\operatorname{uc}(f)\leq d^{N-2}

[
BES17
, Theorem 2.1]
. Interestingly, Example
6.6
produces indecomposable Ulrich modules attaining this upper bound for any generic polynomial of the form
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i1}x_{{i2}}\cdots x_{id}
. If the Ulrich complexity is strictly less than
d^{N-2}
in this case, then it seems one would need a construction more efficient than the tensor product to attain the minimum.
