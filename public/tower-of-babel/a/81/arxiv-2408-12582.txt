Convergence Properties of Iteratively Coupled Surface-Subsurface Models
Valentina Schüller, Philipp Birken, Andreas Dedner

Abstract
Surface-subsurface flow models for hydrological applications solve a coupled multiphysics problem. This usually consists of some form of the Richards and shallow water equations. A typical setup couples these two nonlinear partial differential equations in a partitioned approach via boundary conditions. Full interaction between the subsolvers is ensured by an iterative coupling procedure. This can be accelerated using relaxation. In this paper, we apply continuous and fully discrete linear analysis techniques to study an idealized, linear, 1D-0D version of a surface-subsurface model. These result in explicit expressions for the convergence factor and an optimal relaxation parameter, depending on material and discretization parameters. We test our analysis results numerically for fully nonlinear 2D-1D experiments based on existing benchmark problems. The linear analysis can explain fast convergence of iterations observed in practice for different materials and test cases, even though we are not able to capture various nonlinear effects.

Introduction

We consider a coupled surface-subsurface flow model.
This is motivated by the desire to better understand the terrestrial water cycle and to simulate effects of water management and flooding on the water table and aquifer
[
13
,
2
]
.
A common choice is to use a
d
-dimensional Richards equation for the subsurface flow and some variant of the
d-1
-dimensional shallow water equations for the surface flow.
We thus have two coupled nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs).

We follow a so-called partitioned approach.
There one uses separate solvers, with potentially different discretizations, for the two PDEs.
The models then interact by prescribing boundary conditions for each other. Examples of coupled surface-subsurface models that make use of such a strategy are given in
[
9
,
16
]
.
Specifically, the shallow water equation supplies the water height as a Dirichlet condition for the capillary head, and the Richards equation supplies a flux in the form of the vertical velocity.
The latter acts as a source term in the lower dimensional surface flux equation.
As in domain decomposition methods, this is performed in an iterative manner.

While this strategy is used successfully in practice, an analysis of this scheme is to our knowledge missing for coupled surface-subsurface flows.
Additionally, convergence can be accelerated using a relaxation step, but this is not used so far.
In this article, our goal is to provide a convergence analysis of the coupling iteration and determine optimal relaxation parameters.

Such an analysis has been performed previously for linear coupled problems, in particular for heterogeneous heat equations.
This has been done on the fully continuous level
[
10
]
, discrete in time
[
11
]
, discrete in space
[
12
]
, or fully discrete
[
17
]
.
The convergence results depend on the material parameters and, in the latter three cases, on the discretization.
The fully discrete results show that the linear analysis is able to predict the quantitative and qualitative behavior of the convergence factor for the nonlinear case.

We apply this type of linear analysis to the nonlinear problem of coupled surface-subsurface flow, where in particular for Richards’ equation material parameters vary in size by many orders of magnitude.
To this end, we consider a linearized 1D-0D model.
We then focus on the fully discrete approach from
[
17
]
, which is able to show the effect of the different mesh widths on convergence.
To get even more insight and to prove well-posedness, we also apply the continuous analysis from
[
10
]
.
Both result in explicit expressions for the convergence factor of the iterations as well as optimal relaxation parameters.

We then look at the fully nonlinear coupled 2D-1D problem and carry out numerical experiments with realistic material parameters based on existing benchmark problems.
These allow us to measure speed of convergence in practice and compare with the results predicted by our linear analysis.
The two subproblems are solved with the PDE software framework DUNE
[
7
]
and coupled using the coupling library preCICE
[
5
]
.

In

Section

The analysis of the method is presented in
Section

2.2
Surface Flow

We consider the special case where the uppermost layer of the ground is fully saturated and surface water is present. If this is not the case, the water height is zero and the surface flow model is inactive

20
]

In a first approach, we model surface flow with the one-dimensional shallow water equations (SWE) for the water height
h(t,x)

[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}h\\
hu\end{pmatrix}+\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}hu\\
hu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}s(t,x)+r(t,x)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times\Gamma,

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}h\\
hu\end{pmatrix}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}h_{0}\\

Here,

In our model problem, we do not consider additional terms due to bathymetry or friction.

In a second approach, we follow a similar implementation as large-scale coupled hydrology models such as ParFlow

The model reduces to a single equation for conservation of mass:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(hu)

\displaystyle=s(t,x)+r(t,x)\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times\Gamma,

\displaystyle h(0,x)

\displaystyle=h_{0}(x)\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

\boldsymbol{q}={(h,hu)}^{T}

Assuming homogeneous Neumann conditions at the boundary to be consistent with the subsurface model, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}(t,x)+\partial_{x}f\left(\boldsymbol{q}(t,x)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{b}(t,x)\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times\Gamma,

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f\left(\boldsymbol{q}(t,0)\right)=\partial_{x}f\left(\boldsymbol{q}(t,L_{x})\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{q}_{0}(x)\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

2.3
Interface Conditions and Coupling Iteration

Surface-subsurface models can be coupled at the interface
\Gamma
either using boundary conditions that enforce continuity of pressure and mass conservation
[
2
,
14
]
, or alternatively using a first-order exchange flux
[
24
,
18
]
.
We follow the first approach, giving for
t\in(0,T]

\displaystyle\psi_{\Gamma}(t,x)

\displaystyle=h(t,x),

(8a)

\displaystyle s(t,x)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(\psi(t,x,L_{z}))\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\eqqcolon v_{s}(t,x).

(8b)

We focus on the situation with nonzero water height.
Coupled hydrology models need to account for cases with
h=0
as well, this is not the focus of this paper.
In that case, the interface conditions (
8
) no longer hold.
1
1

1

\psi(t,0)<0
, but
h=0

Examples of how this is tackled in existing codes are given in
[
20
,
16
]
.
There exists no known analytical solution to this coupled problem
[
16
,
2
]
.

We solve the coupled problem using partitioned, sequential coupling iterations with relaxation.
Given an initial guess for the water height
h^{0}(t,x)
, compute sequentially in each iteration
k=1,2,\dots
the approximations for
\psi^{k}
followed by
\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}^{k}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta(\psi^{k})+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(\psi^{k})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times\Omega,

(9a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\left(\psi^{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma,

\displaystyle\psi^{k}(0,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\psi^{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=h^{k-1}(t,x)\quad\text{on }[0,T]\times\Gamma,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}^{k}+\partial_{x}f(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}^{k})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{b}^{k}\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times\Gamma,

(9b)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f\left(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}(t,0)\right)=\partial_{x}f\left(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}(t,L_{x})\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{q}_{0}(x)\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle s^{k}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(\psi^{k}(t,x,L_{z}))\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\quad\text{on }[0,T]\times\Gamma.

Then compute the new water height
h^{k}(t,x)
using

h^{k}(t,x)=\omega\tilde{h}^{k}+(1-\omega)h^{k-1},

(9c)

with the relaxation parameter
\omega\in(0,1]
.
The main goal of this paper is to study the convergence properties of this coupling iteration and how they relate to the choice of
\omega
.

This type of equation coupling is reminiscent of Dirichlet-Neumann waveform relaxation (DNWR) in domain decomposition or fluid-structure interaction:
The surface solver provides a Dirichlet condition for the subsurface model, while the subsurface solver sends back a (nonlinear) flux.
However, the flux enters the surface flow model as a source term, not a boundary condition, which is a fundamental difference to the classical DNWR algorithm.

Discretization of the Coupling Iterations

This section presents a fully discrete version of the coupling iteration (
9
).
We follow the method of lines, discretizing first in space, then in time.

3.1
Discretization of the Subsurface Model

For the subsurface model, we use linear continuous finite elements in space and the implicit Euler method in time.
2
2

2

Using an implicit first-order time discretization for Richards’ equation is a common choice
[
8
]
.

We solve Richards’ equation on a Cartesian grid with
M_{x}\times M_{z}
elements of size
\Delta x=L_{x}/M_{x}
and
\Delta z=L_{z}/M_{z}
.
Furthermore, we introduce uniformly spaced time steps
t^{n}
, with
\Delta t=T/N
.
Then we find approximate solutions
\psi^{n,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{V}^{h}

\psi^{k}(t^{n},\boldsymbol{x})

\mathcal{V}^{h}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega)

We use a linear nodal basis for
\psi^{n,k}
and
\phi
in our implementation and arrive at the following nonlinear system:
Given
\psi^{n-1}\in\mathcal{V}^{h}
, find
\psi^{n,k}\in\mathcal{V}^{h}
such that

\left\langle\theta\left(\psi^{n,k}\right)-\theta\left(\psi^{n-1}\right),\phi\right\rangle-\Delta t\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}(\psi^{n,k})\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\nabla\phi\right\rangle=f_{\Gamma}^{n,k}\quad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{V}^{h}.

(10)

The initial condition
\psi^{0}
is obtained by suitably approximating
\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
in
\mathcal{V}^{h}
.
The nonlinear system for
\psi^{n,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\psi^{k}(t^{n},\boldsymbol{x})
is solved using Newton’s method.

3.2
Discretization of the Surface Model

We discretize the surface model using a cell-centered finite volume scheme in space and the implicit Euler method in time.
3
3

3

Note that using the implicit Euler method for the surface flow solver is not uncommon in coupled hydrological models, cf.
[
14
]
.

Without loss of generality, we assume an equidistant mesh for the surface flow domain
\Gamma
.
This consists of
M_{x}
cells
\mathcal{C}_{l}=[x_{l-1},x_{l}]
, with
x_{l}=l\Delta x
and
l=1,\dots,M_{x}
.
We work with the balance law formulation given in
Equation

7
.
Defining

\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}_{l}^{k}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{l}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}^{k}(t,x)\,dx,

and introducing a numerical flux
F_{l}^{k}(t)
, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}^{k}_{l}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{l}}\boldsymbol{b}^{k}(t,x)\,dx-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(F_{l}^{k}(t)-F_{l-1}^{k}(t)\right).

(11)

In our tests, we use the local Lax-Friedrichs method to define the numerical flux.
Integration in time and applying the implicit Euler method yields

\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}_{l}^{n,k}=\boldsymbol{q}_{l}^{n-1}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(F_{l}^{n,k}-F_{l-1}^{n,k}\right)+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{l}}\boldsymbol{b}^{n,k}(x)\,dx.

(12)

Note that we use the same time step size
\Delta t
and horizontal grid size
\Delta x
for surface and subsurface flow.

3.3
Fully Discrete Partitioned Coupling Iteration

To finalize the discretization of the coupled problem, we need to address the interface boundary conditions (
8
).
Both solvers use the same time integration method and time step size. However, we need to provide a map from the cell-centered FV grid (surface grid) to the nodal values of the subsurface grid and vice versa.

The surface grid consists of
M_{x}
cells, centered at
\frac{\Delta x}{2},\frac{3\Delta x}{2},\dots,L_{x}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}
, whereas the subsurface grid consists of
M_{x}+1
nodes at
0,\Delta x,\dots,L_{x}
.
To map from the surface to the subsurface solver, we apply the rule that a nodal value is the average of the FV cells surrounding it:

\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k}(x_{l})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(h_{l}^{n,k-1}+h_{l+1}^{n,k-1}\right)&l=1,\dots,M_{x}-1\\
h_{1}^{n,k-1}&l=0\\
h_{M_{x}}^{n,k-1}&l=M_{x}.\end{cases}

(13)

Vice versa, the subsurface solver provides the integral of the surface flux:

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{l}}s^{n,k}(x)\,dx=\int_{x_{l-1}}^{x_{l}}\boldsymbol{v}\left(\psi^{n,k}(x,L_{z})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,dx=v_{s}^{n,k}\left(x_{l-1}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)\Delta x,

(14)

for each of the
M_{x}
cells
\mathcal{C}_{l}
.
The second equality holds since we use linear finite elements.

The partitioned, sequential coupling iteration procedure in the fully discrete case takes the following form:
Given an initial guess for the water height
h_{l}^{n,0}
, first solve
Equation

10
with the boundary condition (
13
).
Then, update the surface solver’s source term by
Equation

14
and solve
Equation

12
to obtain
\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}_{l}^{n,k}
.
Finally, apply a relaxation step to update the Dirichlet boundary condition for the next iteration:

h_{l}^{n,k}=\omega\tilde{h}_{l}^{n,k}+(1-\omega)h_{l}^{n,k-1},

(15)

with the relaxation parameter
\omega\in(0,1]
.
We terminate the iteration once the residual for the water height falls below a given tolerance:

\mathrm{res}^{n,k}\coloneqq\left\|\tilde{h}^{n,k}-h^{n,k-1}\right\|_{2}<\mathrm{TOL}.

(16)

4
Linear Analysis

In this section, we analyze a linearized, one-dimensional version of the coupling problem described in
Section

2
.
We use techniques from
[

]
and
[
17
]

4.1
One-Dimensional Idealized Model

We consider a single column of the coupled system, assuming that the convergence behavior of the iterations is driven by vertical processes.
Richards’ equation reduces to a one-dimensional PDE whereas both surface flow models reduce to the same ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the water height:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}\theta(\psi)+\partial_{z}\left(K(\psi)\partial_{z}\left(\psi(t,z)+z\right)\right)=r(t),\\
\frac{dh}{dt}=v_{s}(t).\end{gathered}

Momentum conservation in
Equation

5
trivially becomes
\dot{hu}=0
.
Since it is no longer coupled to the continuity equation, we can disregard it in the convergence analysis.
To linearize Richards’ equation, we write it in fully head-based form via

\frac{\partial\theta\left(\psi(t,z)\right)}{\partial t}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\theta(\psi)}{\partial\psi}}_{\eqqcolon c(\psi)}\frac{\partial\psi(t,z)}{\partial t},

and assume that
c,K>0
are positive constants independent of
\psi
.
4
4

4

Positivity is fulfilled in practice for
K
, cf.
Equation

2
.
However,
c(\psi)=0
in saturated regions where
\psi>0
, i.e., the equation becomes elliptic.
This degenerate nature of Richards’ equation makes it hard to study analytically and numerically
[
8
]
.

We furthermore drop the rainfall rate,
r(t)=0
, since it does not affect the convergence rate in the linear setting.
As in
Section

2.3
, we write down the partitioned coupling iteration including relaxation for this continuous model problem.

Given an initial guess
h^{0}(t)
, compute for each
k=1,2,\dots
the approximations
\psi^{k}
and
\tilde{h}^{k}
sequentially according to:

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial\psi^{k}}{\partial t}-K\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\psi^{k}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times(-L,0),

(17a)

\displaystyle\psi^{k}(0,z)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(z)\quad\text{on }[-L,0],

\displaystyle\psi^{k}(t,-L)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\psi^{k}(t,0)

\displaystyle=h^{k-1}(t)\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{h}^{k}}{dt}

\displaystyle=v_{s}^{k}(t)\quad\text{on }(0,T],

(17b)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{k}(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0}.

The boundary data for the subsurface solver in the next iteration is updated by the relaxation step:

h^{k}(t)=\omega\tilde{h}^{k}(t)+(1-\omega)h^{k-1}(t).

(17c)

Note that we use homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for Richards’ equation here, instead of no-flux boundary conditions as in the nonlinear problem.
This simplifies the fully discrete analysis and is justified if we assume that the bottom boundary is far away from the interface.

5

5

4.2
Continuous Analysis

One can determine the convergence factor of the continuous iterations, as well as an optimal relaxation parameter, with Fourier-based techniques presented in
[
10
]
.
We use the Laplace transform for time-dependent functions

\hat{f}(s)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-st}\,dt,

(18)

where
s\in\mathbb{C}
is the Laplace variable.
We can then state the sequential coupling iteration including relaxation for the Laplace-transformed system:
Given the initial guess
\psi_{\Gamma}^{0}
, compute for
k=1,2,\dots

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}-K\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\psi

\displaystyle=0,

(19a)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{k}(s,-L)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{k}(s,0)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\Gamma}^{k-1},

s\hat{h}^{k}=\hat{v}_{s}^{k}=-K\left(\partial_{z}\hat{\psi}^{k}(s,0)+1\right),

(19b)

\hat{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{k}=\omega\hat{h}^{k}+(1-\omega)\hat{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{k-1}.

(19c)

Now we study the error of each iteration with respect to the true solution of the coupled problem, introducing

\hat{e}_{1}^{k}\coloneqq\hat{h}^{k}-\hat{h},\quad\hat{e}_{2}^{k}\coloneqq\hat{\psi}^{k}-\hat{\psi},\quad\hat{\gamma}^{k}\coloneqq\omega\hat{e}_{1}^{k}+(1-\omega)\hat{\gamma}^{k-1}.

The errors
\hat{e}_{1}^{k}(s)
,
\hat{e}_{2}^{k}(s,z)
satisfy equation (
19
) with initial guess
\hat{e}_{\Gamma}^{0}
. We solve it by standard solution approaches for homogeneous linear PDEs (cf.
Appendix

A
):

\hat{e}_{1}^{k}(s)=-\hat{\gamma}^{k-1}\sqrt{\frac{cK}{s}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right),\ \hat{e}_{2}^{k}(s,z)=\hat{\gamma}^{k-1}\frac{\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}(z+L)\right)}{\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right)}.

This yields the convergence factor of the iterations in Laplace space

\rho(s,\omega)=\frac{\hat{e}_{1}^{k+1}}{\hat{e}_{1}^{k}}=\frac{\hat{e}_{2}^{k+1}}{\hat{e}_{2}^{k}}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}^{k}}{\hat{\gamma}^{k-1}}=1-\omega-\omega\sqrt{\frac{cK}{s}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right).

(20)

In particular, it holds that

\left\|\hat{e}_{2}^{k}(s,z)\right\|_{2}=\left\|\rho^{k}\hat{e}_{2}^{0}(s,z)\right\|_{2}\leq|\rho|^{k}\left\|\hat{e}_{2}^{0}(s,z)\right\|_{2}.

Parseval’s theorem relates this result in Laplace space back to the time domain:

\left\|e^{-\eta t}e_{2}^{k}(t,z)\right\|_{2}\leq|\rho|^{k}\left\|e^{-\eta t}e_{2}^{0}(t,z)\right\|_{2}.

Convergence in two iterations is obtained by requiring that
\rho(s,\omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0
, from which we obtain the optimal relaxation parameter

\omega_{\mathrm{opt}}(s)=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\frac{cK}{s}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right)}.

(21)

The result indicates that the product of hydraulic capacity and conductivity
cK
is of importance for the convergence speed.
For
\omega=1
,
\mathrm{Re}(\rho)<0
, consistent with continuous and discrete results for similar coupling iterations for the heat equation
[
10
,
17
]
.
Due to the dependence on
s
, the application of these results to a discretization of the coupling iterations (
9
) and (
17
) is not straightforward.

4.3
Discretization

Analogously to
Section

3
, we now discretize
Equation

17a
with linear finite elements in space and the implicit Euler method in time on an equidistant grid with grid size
\Delta z=L/M
. This gives the following system of ODEs for the
M-1
interior nodal unknowns
\psi_{1}(t),\dots,\psi_{M-1}(t)
:

\displaystyle c\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\left\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\right\rangle\dot{\psi}_{j}^{k}+K\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\left\langle\partial_{z}\phi_{i},\partial_{z}\phi_{j}\right\rangle\psi_{j}^{k}

\displaystyle=f_{\Gamma,i}^{k-1}

\displaystyle\ \forall i=1,\dots,M-1,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(0)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0,j}

\displaystyle\ \forall j=1,\dots,M-1.

The hat functions are denoted by
\phi_{i}(z)
and
\psi_{0,j}
are the coefficients that suitably approximate
\psi_{0}(z)
in
\mathcal{V}^{M-1}
.
The right-hand side
f_{\Gamma,i}^{k-1}
contains the Dirichlet contribution from the interface boundary condition on
\Gamma
, cf.
Equation

4
.
In matrix-vector notation, we write this as

\displaystyle M_{II}\boldsymbol{\dot{\psi}}_{I}^{k}+A_{II}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}^{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}_{\Gamma}^{k-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}^{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0},

where
M_{II}
and
A_{II}
are the mass and stiffness matrices.
The subscripts
._{I}
and
._{\Gamma}
here refer to the distinction between contributions related to the interior
\Omega
and interface
\Gamma
, respectively.
This notation borrows from the theory of domain decomposition
[
21
]
and is analogous to
[
17
]
.

The first coupling condition (
8a
) states that the capillary head at the interface
\Gamma
is equal to the water height.
We refer to the associated unknown of the Galerkin approximation as
\psi_{\Gamma}(t)=\psi_{M}(t)\approx\psi(t,0)
.
Since we are using a nodal basis,
\psi_{\Gamma}(t)\equiv h(t)
and we can define the full vector of unknowns

\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{M-1}\\
\psi_{M}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{M-1}\\
h\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}\\
\psi_{\Gamma}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

For the Dirichlet boundary condition, we obtain that

\boldsymbol{f}_{\Gamma}^{k}=-M_{I\Gamma}\dot{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{k}-A_{I\Gamma}\psi_{\Gamma}^{k},

with the vectors
M_{I\Gamma},A_{I\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1}
given by

(M_{I\Gamma})_{j}\coloneqq\left\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{M}\right\rangle,\quad(A_{I\Gamma})_{j}\coloneqq\left\langle\partial_{z}\phi_{i},\partial_{z}\phi_{M}\right\rangle,\quad j=1,\dots,M-1.

(22)

Discretizing in time with the implicit Euler method finally yields the fully discrete system of equations for linearized, one-dimensional subsurface flow:

\left(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}\right)\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}^{n,k}=-(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma}){\psi}_{\Gamma}^{n,k-1}+M_{II}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}^{n-1}+M_{I\Gamma}\psi_{\Gamma}^{n-1}.

(23)

For the second coupling condition (
8b
), we aim to obtain an expression of
v_{s}
in terms of the weak form of Richards’ equation, consistent with the FE discretization.
This can be done using Green’s first identity
6
6

6

Note that this weak reformulation of the surface flux via Green’s first identity is not possible in the nonlinear case.
, cf.
[
17
,
21
]
, giving:

\displaystyle v_{s}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\dot{\psi}_{j}\underbrace{c\left\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{M}\right\rangle}_{\eqqcolon{(M_{\Gamma})}_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\psi_{j}\underbrace{K\left\langle\partial_{z}\phi_{j},\partial_{z}\phi_{M}\right\rangle}_{\eqqcolon{(A_{\Gamma})}_{j}}-K\underbrace{\left\langle 1,\partial_{z}\phi_{M}\right\rangle}_{=1}

\displaystyle=-M_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\dot{\psi}}-A_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\psi}-K.

We can split
M_{\Gamma}
and
A_{\Gamma}
into two parts

M_{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{\Gamma I}&M_{\Gamma\Gamma}\end{bmatrix},\quad A_{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\Gamma I}&A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\end{bmatrix},

where

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma I}

\displaystyle={(M_{I\Gamma})}^{T},\

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma\Gamma}=c\left\langle\phi_{M},\phi_{M}\right\rangle,

(24)

\displaystyle A_{\Gamma I}

\displaystyle={(A_{I\Gamma})}^{T},\

\displaystyle A_{\Gamma\Gamma}=K\left\langle\partial_{z}\phi_{M},\partial_{z}\phi_{M}\right\rangle.

The semi-discrete equation for the water height (
17b
) thus becomes

\dot{\tilde{h}}^{k}=\dot{\tilde{\psi}}_{\Gamma}^{k}=-\left(M_{\Gamma I}\boldsymbol{\dot{\psi}}^{k}_{I}+M_{\Gamma\Gamma}\dot{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{k-1}+A_{\Gamma I}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{k}_{I}+A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\psi_{\Gamma}^{k-1}+K\right).

After discretization in time we are left with

\begin{split}\tilde{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{n,k}=&-(M_{\Gamma I}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma I})\boldsymbol{\psi}^{n,k}_{I}-(M_{\Gamma\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma\Gamma})\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k-1}\\
&+M_{\Gamma I}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{n-1}_{I}+(1+M_{\Gamma\Gamma})\psi_{\Gamma}^{n-1}-\Delta tK\end{split}

(25)

for the surface flow.
The resulting algorithm is given in

4.4
Fully Discrete Analysis

We now want to find the convergence rate and optimal relaxation parameter of the fully discrete
Algorithm

1
.
As in
[
17
]
, we achieve this by expressing
\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k}
in terms of
\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k-1}
,
\omega
, and values from the previous time step
t^{n-1}
.
One first solves
Equation

23
for
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{I}^{n,k}
and plugs the result into
Equation

25
:

\tilde{\psi}_{\Gamma}^{n,k}=S\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k-1}+\xi^{n-1}.

(26)

Here
\xi^{n-1}
summarizes terms which only depend on the previous time step
t^{n-1}
, while
S
is defined as
7
7

7

As also observed in
[
17
]
,
S
takes the form of a Schur complement.

S\coloneqq(M_{\Gamma I}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma I})\left(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}\right)^{-1}(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma})-(M_{\Gamma\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma\Gamma}).

Applying the relaxation step, we obtain

\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k}=\underbrace{\left(\omega S+(1-\omega)\right)}_{\eqqcolon\Sigma(\omega)}\psi_{\Gamma}^{n,k-1}+\omega\xi^{n-1}.

(27)

In this model problem, the iteration matrix
\Sigma(\omega)
is a scalar, which reduces the optimality condition to
\Sigma(\omega_{\mathrm{opt}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0
.
Therefore,

\omega_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{1-S}.

Computing
S
involves the inverse of
M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}
.
Linear finite elements with a nodal basis, along with our assumed boundary conditions, yield that this is a symmetric, tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix given by

\begin{gathered}M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&a&b\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&b&a&b\\
&&&b&a\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times(M-1)},\\
a\coloneqq\frac{2}{3}c\Delta z+\frac{2K\Delta t}{\Delta z},\\
b\coloneqq\frac{1}{6}c\Delta z-\frac{K\Delta t}{\Delta z}.\end{gathered}

The sparsity of this matrix is not preserved in its inverse, but we can follow
[
17
]
to mitigate this problem.
Since the matrix is Toeplitz,

{(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II})}^{-1}={(V\Lambda V^{T})}^{-1}=V\Lambda^{-1}V^{T}.

Here,
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M-1})^{T}
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}
and
V
is an orthogonal matrix with the right eigenvectors of
M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}
.
Both are known for Toeplitz matrices:

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{j}=a-2b\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{M}\right)\\
V_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{M}\right)}}\sin\left(\frac{ij\pi}{M}\right).\end{gathered}

(28)

Evaluating the integrals in
Equation

22
, we furthermore obtain that:

M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma}={(0,\dots,0,b)}^{T}.

Thus,

(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma})^{T}(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II})^{-1}(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma})=b^{2}\alpha,

with

\alpha\coloneqq{\left({(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II})}^{-1}\right)}_{M-1,M-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1}{V_{M-1,j}}^{2}.

Substituting
Equation

28
gives

\alpha=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{(M-1)j\pi}{M}\right)\left(a-2b\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{M}\right)\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{M}\right)}.

We can simplify this expression using the identities

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\left(\frac{(M-1)j\pi}{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{M}\right)\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2}.

Remembering that
\Delta z=L/M
, we thus obtain:

\alpha=\frac{\Delta z}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi\Delta z}{L}\right)}{\frac{a}{2}-b\cos\left(\frac{j\pi\Delta z}{L}\right)}.

(29)

Finally, we note that we can simplify the subtrahend by evaluating the integrals in
Equation

24
:

M_{\Gamma\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma\Gamma}=\frac{a}{2}.

With this we get that

S=b^{2}\alpha-\frac{a}{2},\quad\omega_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{a}{2}-b^{2}\alpha}.

(30)

The first term in
S
is positive
8
8

8

Recall that
(M_{\Gamma I}+\Delta tA_{\Gamma I})=(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma})^{T}
. If we define
\boldsymbol{x}\coloneqq(M_{I\Gamma}+\Delta tA_{I\Gamma})\neq 0
,
A\coloneqq\left(M_{II}+\Delta tA_{II}\right)^{-1}
, the first term in
S
corresponds to
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{A}^{2}>0
.
, and thus in particular
\alpha>0
.
One result of the continuous analysis was that the real part of the convergence factor
\mathrm{Re}(\rho)
is negative, which indicates that
S<0
should hold in the discrete analysis.
However, we are unable to show that this holds in general.

Implementation and Setup

To verify and test our analysis, we have implemented both the nonlinear 2D-1D coupling iteration (

Our code makes use of the DUNE Numerics Toolbox

DUNE is a general purpose open source framework for solving PDEs with grid

The models are defined using the unified form language (UFL)

6
Summary and Conclusions

This paper considers coupled surface-subsurface models which solve the Richards and shallow water equations coupled via boundary conditions.
We employ a partitioned setup where the two subsolvers are coupled with a sequential procedure, allowing for acceleration of the iterations via relaxation.
To our knowledge, this is the first time that the discrete and continuous convergence analysis techniques for coupled PDE systems have been applied to this problem.
We are contributing to the field by examining what conclusions we can draw in practice when applying existing linear analysis techniques to this highly nonlinear problem.

We derived a linearized, 1D-0D model problem, assuming that vertical processes are most relevant.
Linear analysis techniques from
[
10
,
17
]
allowed us to determine convergence factors and an optimal relaxation parameter for the fully continuous and fully discrete versions, and to show their dependence on the material properties of the subsurface, but also on the resolution in time and space. In particular, the convergence rate will be small when
c
or
K
are small. These theoretical results are confirmed numerically for the 1D-0D problem.

Nonlinear 2D-1D experiments for different realistic materials show that the convergence speed is indeed mainly driven by vertical processes and resolution in Richards’ equation.
Quantitatively, the linear analysis tends to overestimate the observed convergence rate and naturally fails to represent processes which are not part of the linear model (e.g., the effect of external source terms and spatial variability).
Also, in the drainage trench example, the convergence factor decreased linearly with respect to the time step size while the analysis predicted a square-root dependence.
Even more surprisingly, it increased when increasing the vertical resolution.

Overall the observed convergence factors do not exceed
{10}^{-3}
for the materials considered and typical simulation parameters, making acceleration using relaxation unnecessary.
The linear analysis is able to explain the small observed convergence factors, as well as how dynamically changing material properties during a simulation affect the speed of convergence.

Appendix A
Well-Posedness of the Coupled Problem

We study the linearized 1D-0D problem without iteration or relaxation:

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}-K\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\psi

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }(0,T]\times(-L,0),

(34a)

\displaystyle\psi(t,-L)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\psi(t,0)

\displaystyle=h(t)\quad\text{on }[0,T],

\displaystyle\psi(0,z)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(z)\quad\text{on }[-L,0],

\displaystyle\frac{dh}{dt}

\displaystyle=-K\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\psi(t,0)+1\right)\quad\text{on }(0,T],

(34b)

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0}.

Applying the Laplace transform (
18
), we arrive at

\displaystyle cs\hat{\psi}-K\partial_{z}^{2}\hat{\psi}

\displaystyle=0,

(35a)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(s,-L)

\displaystyle=0,

s\hat{h}=-K\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\hat{\psi}(s,0)+1\right).

(35b)

If we find a solution to this system to which the backward transform is well-defined, the problem is well-posed.
The roots to the characteristic equation for
Equation

35a
are
\lambda_{1/2}=\pm\sqrt{cs/K}
, yielding a general solution:

\hat{\psi}=A\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}z\right)+B\cosh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}z\right).

The Dirichlet boundary condition
\hat{\psi}(s,-L)=0
gives

\hat{\psi}(s,z)=\frac{B}{\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right)}\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}(z+L)\right),

and from the interface boundary condition
\hat{\psi}(s,0)=\hat{h}
we obtain
B=\hat{h}
.
Inserting this result into
Equation

35b
gives

s\hat{h}=-\hat{h}\sqrt{cKs}\coth\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right)-K,

and thus

\hat{h}=\frac{-K}{s+\sqrt{\frac{cK}{s}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\frac{cs}{K}}L\right)}.

Assuming that
c,K,L>0
, the denominator is well-defined for
s\neq 0
and thus the solution to the Laplace-transformed coupled problem has its only pole at
s=0
.
The inverse Laplace transform is therefore well-defined for
s\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, giving existence and uniqueness for
\psi(t,z)
and
h(t)
.

Appendix B
Convergence factor estimate in code

We estimate convergence according to
Equation

31
for the linearized 1D-0D coupling problem.
Here,
\|h^{k,n}\|_{2}\equiv|h^{k,n}|
and thus

\mathrm{CR}_{n}=\frac{|\tilde{h}^{k,n}-h^{k-1,n}|}{|\tilde{h}^{k-1,n}-h^{k-2,n}|}.

Recall from
Equations

26
and
27
:

\begin{gathered}\tilde{h}^{k,n}=Sh^{k-1,n}+\xi^{n-1},\\
h^{k,n}=\omega\tilde{h}^{k,n}+(1-\omega)h^{k-1,n}=\underbrace{(1-\omega+\omega S)}_{\eqqcolon\Sigma(\omega)}h^{k-1,n}+\omega\xi^{n-1}.\end{gathered}

(36)

We now define the errors at time
t^{n}
with respect to the true solution
h(t)
:

e^{k,n}\coloneqq h^{k,n}-h(t^{n}),\quad\tilde{e}^{k,n}\coloneqq\tilde{h}^{k,n}-h(t^{n})

(37)

The above iteration (
36
) can be rewritten in terms of
e^{k,n}
and
\tilde{e}^{k,n}
:

e^{k,n}+h(t^{n})=\omega(\tilde{e}^{k,n}+h(t^{n}))+(1-\omega)(e^{k-1,n}+h(t^{n})).

That is,

e^{k,n}=\omega\tilde{e}^{k,n}+(1-\omega)e^{k-1,n}.

Similarly,

\tilde{e}^{k,n}+h(t^{n})=S(\tilde{e}^{k-1,n}+h(t^{n}))+\xi^{n-1}.

Assume now that
h^{k-1,n}=h(t^{n})
.
If we have defined our coupling iteration appropriately, it must hold that
\tilde{h}^{k,n}=h(t^{n})
as well.
Thus,

h(t^{n})=Sh(t^{n})+\xi^{n-1}

and therefore

\tilde{e}^{k,n}=Se^{k-1,n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad e^{k,n}=\Sigma(\omega)e^{k-1,n}.

We can then rewrite

|\tilde{h}^{k,n}-h^{k-1,n}|=|(\tilde{e}^{k,n}+h(t^{n}))-(e^{k-1,n}+h(t^{n}))|=|(S-1)e^{k-1,n}|

and obtain the desired result (
32
)

\mathrm{CR}_{n}=\frac{|\tilde{h}^{k,n}-h^{k-1,n}|}{|\tilde{h}^{k-1,n}-h^{k-2,n}|}=\frac{|(S-1)e^{k-1,n}|}{|(S-1)e^{k-2,n}|}=\frac{|e^{k-1,n}|}{|e^{k-2,n}|}=|\Sigma(\omega)|

up to floating point precision.

Note: The same result holds if one defines the residual in terms of
both
the surface flux
v_{s}^{k,n}
and the water height, i.e.,

\mathrm{res}^{n,k}=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\hat{h}^{n,k}\\
\hat{v}_{s}^{n,k}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}h^{n,k-1}\\
v_{s}^{n,k-1}\end{pmatrix}\right\|.
