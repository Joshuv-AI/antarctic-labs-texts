Deterministic entanglement distribution on series-parallel quantum networks
Xiangyi Meng, Yulong Cui, Jianxi Gao, Shlomo Havlin, Andrei E. Ruckenstein

Abstract
The performance of distributing entanglement between two distant nodes in a large-scale quantum network (QN) of partially entangled bipartite pure states is generally benchmarked against the classical entanglement percolation (CEP) scheme. Improvements beyond CEP were only achieved by nonscalable strategies for restricted QN topologies. This paper explores and amplifies a new and more effective mapping of a QN, referred to as concurrence percolation theory (ConPT), that suggests using deterministic rather than probabilistic protocols for scalably improving on CEP across arbitrary QN topologies. More precisely, we implement ConPT via a deterministic entanglement transmission (DET) scheme that is fully analogous to resistor network analysis, with the corresponding series and parallel rules represented by deterministic entanglement swapping and concentration protocols, respectively. The main contribution of this paper is to establish a powerful mathematical framework, which is applicable to arbitrary d d -dimensional information carriers (qudits), that provides different natural optimality metrics in terms of generalized k k -concurrences (a family of fundamental entanglement measures) for different QN topology. In particular, we conclude that the introduced DET scheme (a) is optimal over the well-known nested repeater protocol for distilling entanglement from partially entangled qubits and (b) leads to higher success probabilities of obtaining a maximally entangled state than using CEP. The implementation of the DET scheme is experimentally feasible as tested on IBM’s quantum computation platform.



I
Introduction

Most quantum communication technologies find their roots in entanglement, a primordial quantum resource
Chitambar and Gour 2019
.
Yet, the nonlocality of entanglement facilitating quantum communication becomes unrealistic at large space scales where faithful quantum information is submerged in thermal incoherent noise
Weiss 1999
, making it very inefficient to directly establish point-to-point long-distance entanglement.
To overcome this limit of classical locality and indirectly distribute entanglement among arbitrary parties requires utilizing some intermediate parties as “relays” and applying to them a variety of fundamental communication protocols, including entanglement swapping
Żukowski et al. 1993
;
Bose et al. 1999
and entanglement concentration
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Morikoshi and Koashi 2001
,
as well as composite protocols that are usually recursive and more complex, such as the nested repeater protocol
Briegel et al. 1998
;
Sangouard et al. 2011
;
Buterakos et al. 2017
, etc.

Since all parties are physically separated from one another so that locality holds, only local operations and classical communication (LOCC) can be applied to the information
carriers (e.g., qubits) of different parties. Such consideration allows us to treat each party as a
node
that consists of a collection of information carriers and treat each partially entangled bipartite (multipartite) state formed by internode information carriers as a

link
(hyperlink), essentially giving rise to a network representation of the structure of locality of entanglement resources, widely known as the
quantum network
(QN)
Acín et al. 2007
.
A number of QN-based protocols have since been introduced, e.g.,
q
-swapping
Cuquet and Calsamiglia 2009
and path routing
Pirandola 2019
, combining communication protocols with network science
Biamonte et al. 2019
. In practice, basic communication protocols for small-scale QNs have been experimentally demonstrated using diamond nitrogen-vacancy centers
Kalb et al. 2017
;
Humphreys et al. 2018
;
Pompili et al. 2021
and ion traps
Riebe et al. 2008
;
Almendros et al. 2009
. It is believed that practical QNs are easier to scale than universal quantum computing platforms, since the nonlocality of information carriers only needs to be maintained within each node and not across the global QN platform. The future of a worldwide large-scale QN, namely, a “quantum Internet”
Kimble 2008
, is hence foreseeable. This, on the other hand, urgently demands a more efficient design of QN-based communication protocols that should be
scalable
with network size, be
adaptable
to different network topologies, and align with well-developed methods in complex network analysis
Albert and Barabási 2002
;
Newman et al. 2006
.

The particular task of entanglement distribution
Cirac et al. 1997
between
two
arbitrary nodes, which we coin as entanglement transmission in the QN context, is of special interest. The discovery of an exact mapping between entanglement transmission and classical bond percolation theory
Essam 1980
gives rise to a straightforward entanglement transmission scheme, called classical entanglement percolation (CEP)
Acín et al. 2007
, that greatly reduces the task of distributing a singlet (i.e., a pair of maximally entangled qubits) between two nodes to a pure percolation problem. The CEP scheme is naturally scalable and adaptable for arbitrary QN. However, immediately after it was introduced, it was realized that the CEP does not give the optimal success probability of obtaining such a singlet
Acín et al. 2007
. The problem of calculating the optimal probability was later proved to be extremely complicated even for a one-dimensional (1D) chain
Perseguers et al. 2008
;
Siomau 2016
. Even just improving this success probability for large-scale QN can only be systematically done for special network topology with a few other limitations
Perseguers et al. 2008
, making it difficult to find a better entanglement transmission scheme than CEP that is scalable and adaptable in the same satisfactory way.

This difficulty eventually leads to the questioning of the exclusivity of the classical-percolation-theory-based mapping itself: It was only recently realized that another mapping from entanglement transmission to a new statistical theory called concurrence percolation theory (ConPT)
Meng et al. 2021
;
Malik et al. 2022
exists, bearing its name due to its analogy to percolation theory yet rooted not in probability but in concurrence, a fundamental measure of bipartite entanglement
Hill and Wootters 1997
;
Gour 2005
. The ConPT predicts a lower entanglement transmission threshold than the classical percolation threshold, exhibiting a “quantum advantage” that is purely structural, independent of nontopological details. To achieve the ConPT threshold, it has been suggested that
deterministic
communication protocols that only produce outcomes with certainty (up to unitary equivalence) should be used, so that the protocols can be applied recursively without becoming involved with probabilistic distributions
Meng et al. 2021
.

Our results also suggest that

II
Quantum Network

In this paper, we focus on a pure-state version of a QN where each link is a
d^{2}
-dimensional bipartite pure state shared by the two connected nodes
Acín et al. 2007
. Such a bipartite pure state can always be written as
\sum_{\mu,\nu}\Psi_{\mu\nu}\left|\mu\nu\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, which allows matricization, i.e., allows it to be mapped to a
d\times d
matrix
\bm{\Psi}
with elements
\Psi_{\mu\nu}
,
\mu,\nu=1,2,\cdots,d
.
Left or right multiplication of
\bm{\Psi}
by a unitary matrix corresponds to a local unitary transformation performed by either of the two parties, respectively. Thus, up to unitary equivalence, the state can always be locally transformed into a diagonal form
\left|\bm{\lambda}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\left|jj\right\rangle
by a singular value decomposition, so that each link is exclusively represented by
d
positive Schmidt numbers,
\bm{\lambda}\equiv\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{d}\right)
. Additionally, note that
\bm{\lambda}
is always subject to the normalization constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}=1
. Thus, when
d=2
(qubits), each link can be represented by only one parameter
Meng et al. 2021
.

This representation by a sequence of Schmidt numbers is effective, since singular values are equipped with a built-in majorization preorder (Appendix
A
). This obviously only works for bipartite states. Tripartite states, for example, although they can be completely classified by five parameters including one phase and four moduli
Acín et al. 2000
, are not compatible with such a preorder, thus manifesting as a fundamentally difficult
“(quantum) three-body problem.”

Note that traditionally, each link in a network is also a “bipartite” pairwise notation, always connecting two nodes. Hence it seems a perfect match to study the statistics of a large number of bipartite states using a “bipartite” network theory, namely, a
“statistical theory of (quantum) two-body problems.”
In contrast, a “complex” QN consisting of multipartite entangled states as “hyperlinks” would be beyond the scope of this paper, since the difficulties coming from both quantum many-body theory and hypergraph theory are actually twofold.

II.1
Quantum Communication Protocols

We briefly review two LOCC protocols for bipartite states that are often used in quantum communication:

II.1.1
Entanglement swapping

The motivation for the entanglement swapping protocol is as follows: Suppose there are three parties, Alice–Relay–Bob (A–R–B), who share two bipartite states,
\left|\bm{\lambda}_{a}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{a,j}}\left|jj\right\rangle_{\text{AR}}
(shared between A–R) and
\left|\bm{\lambda}_{b}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{b,k}}\left|kk\right\rangle_{\text{RB}}
(shared between R–B), respectively. Since A and B do not directly share any entanglement, a swapping protocol
Bose et al. 1999
must be performed, which requires a set of quantum measurements to be conducted on R. The result, which is probabilistic in general, is an ensemble of bipartite states directly shared between A and B, entangled.

To be specific, if we matricize
\left|\bm{\lambda}_{a}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{\lambda}_{b}\right\rangle
and denote them by two diagonal matrices
\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})^{1/2}
and
\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{b})^{1/2}
, then after R performs the measurements, the resultant (unnormalized) state between A and B can be written in the matrix form,

\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}=\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})^{1/2}\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{b})^{1/2}

(1)

for outcome
\alpha
, subject to probability
p_{\alpha}=\left\|\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}\right\|_{2}^{2}
of the outcome
Perseguers et al. 2013
. The quantum measurements are encoded by a set of arbitrary matrices
\{\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\}
constrained by the completeness relation,

\sum_{\alpha}\left(X^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}\right)^{*}X^{\alpha}_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=\delta_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}},

(2)

which is sufficient for
\{\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\}
to denote a positive operator-valued measure (POVM).

II.1.2
Entanglement concentration

Given
\left|\bm{\lambda}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\left|jj\right\rangle
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, what is the maximum probability of converting it to a new state
\left|\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{x_{j}}\left|jj\right\rangle
by LOCC? Such a probability is well known
Vidal 1999
:

p_{\bm{\lambda}\to\mathbf{x}}=\min_{0\leq k<m}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda^{\downarrow}_{j}}{1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{j}}.

(3)

In particular, note that it is possible to obtain a new state
\left|\mathbf{x}\right\rangle
of a different dimension
d
if
d\leq m
, since one can pad
\mathbf{x}
by zeros and build a new sequence of
m
Schmidt numbers,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}\oplus(\overbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}^{m-d})
, which when inserted into Eq. (
3
) yields the maximum probability
p_{\bm{\lambda}\to\mathbf{x}}

Vidal 2000
.
The motivation of the entanglement concentration protocol is that, by treating multiple partially entangled bipartite states together as one tensor-product state of a much larger dimension
m
, one can convert such a tensor-product state to a bipartite state of fewer dimensions
d
but with potentially higher entanglement than each previously individual bipartite state.
A concentration protocol can be either probabilistic or deterministic, for
p_{\bm{\lambda}\to\mathbf{x}}=1
is clearly possible by Eq. (
3
).

II.2
Entanglement Transmission Schemes

A
scheme
is a (possibly infinite) number of protocols that can be collectively applied to a QN. One of the well known entanglement transmission schemes is the classical entanglement percolation (CEP) scheme for
d=2
. The CEP scheme consists of two steps
Acín et al. 2007
: (i) Use the concentration protocol to convert each link to a singlet, the probability of which is given by Eq. (
3
). (ii) Find a path of links connecting two arbitrary nodes A and B that have all been successfully converted to singlets, and apply the swapping protocol to them one by one. The final state between A and B is guaranteed to be a singlet by Eq. (
1
). The probability of finding such a path can be considered as a measure of the entanglement transmission ability between A and B, determined by the network topology. This probability, however, is known to be nonoptimal, since for some special topology, e.g., a honeycomb with double links between every two connected nodes, adding a “preprocessing” step of entanglement swapping to the CEP scheme may change the network topology to a triangular type and potentially increase the probability of establishing a singlet between A and B
Acín et al. 2007
.

A deterministic entanglement transmission (DET) scheme, on the other hand, does not use generic probabilistic protocols to increase the singlet conversion probability, but uses deterministic protocols to produce a single highly entangled state between A and B (demonstrated in Fig.
1
). Note that after performing each deterministic protocol, the new system is still an intact QN, i.e., a pure state as a whole, not a probabilistic ensemble. This makes DET purely “nonclassical” (without mixing with probability measures) and easily scalable with network size.

The specific DET scheme we introduce, which utilizes only deterministic swapping and concentration protocols, can be applied to arbitrary series-parallel QN.

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{z})\succ\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y})\otimes\mathbf{z})
. Thus we must have
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y})\otimes\mathbf{z})
since
\mathcal{P}
is permutation invariant. The same is also true for
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{z}))
. Hence together we have
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y})\otimes\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{z}))=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{z})
.

Implementability by LOCC.—

The definition [Eq. (
11
)] immediately implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})\oplus(\overbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}^{m-d})\succ\bm{\lambda}.

(18)

Thus, any new bipartite state of Schmidt numbers
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})

can be produced deterministically (i.e.,
p_{\bm{\lambda}\to\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})}=1
) from a bipartite state of Schmidt numbers
\bm{\lambda}
by the concentration protocol [Eq. (
3
)]. However, the full LOCC construction is complicated, and only a sketch was originally given in Ref.
Nielsen 1999
.
An example of the full construction of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})
is given in Sec.
V
.

The choice of
\mathbf{x}
such that
p_{\bm{\lambda}\to\mathbf{x}}=1
is not unique. The motivation behind our special choice of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})
[Eq. (
10
)] is that it denotes the unique state that contains the maximum entanglement to be concentrated deterministically from
\bm{\lambda}
after reducing the dimension. This is made manifest by Theorem
2
in Sec.
IV
.

IV
Optimality

We have shown that the series and parallel rules in Table
2
can be implemented by the entanglement swapping and concentration protocols.
Naturally, we are interested in how good the DET scheme built by these rules is. Here, we proceed to study the optimality of these rules. As we will see, the degree of optimality of our series and parallel rules actually varies for different series-parallel network topologies.

When discussing the optimality of the series and parallel rules, we will compare our specific rules with generic probabilistic protocols. We will show that, to some degree, the deterministic outcomes by the series and parallel rules are still the best, even when we
relax the requirement of determinacy
and consider the average of general probabilistic ensembles of outcomes.
Since an entanglement-efficient protocol that yields generic probabilistic outcomes would necessarily require more storage and more sophisticated algorithms to keep track of every possible outcome, the complexity of the full scheme would inevitably scale with the QN size, suggesting that a DET scheme is more practical for entanglement transmission over a large-scale QN.

Concurrence monotones.—
It is necessary to introduce a proper entanglement measure on the Schmidt numbers
\bm{\lambda}
. Although
\bm{\lambda}
only admits a preorder, the entanglement measure as a real function admits a strict total order, which can be used to quantify the amount of entanglement. We will adopt a special family of entanglement monotones, called the concurrence monotones
Gour 2005
,
C_{k}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,d
(see Appendix
B
for more details).
We will show that the series and parallel rules optimize the task of entanglement transmission for series-parallel QNs when measured by the average or worst-case
k
-concurrence, or
G
-concurrence (another name for
C_{d}
), depending on the network topology.

IV.1
Some lemmas

We first prove some lemmas for the swapping and concentration functions,
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
.

Lemma 1
.

(Convexity)
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha},\mathbf{z})
is convex, i.e.,

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha},\mathbf{z})\succ\mathcal{S}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha},\mathbf{z}),\quad p_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}.

Proof.

Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=d\times\mathrm{eig}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow})\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\text{diag}(\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow})\mathbf{V}),

(19)

where “
\mathrm{eig}
” denotes the eigenvalues, arranged in descending order. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle d\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathrm{eig}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}_{\alpha})\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\text{diag}(\mathbf{z}^{\downarrow})\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha},\mathbf{z})

by Lidskii’s theorem on eigenvalues (Theorem III.4.1 of Ref.
Bhatia 1997
). ∎

Lemma 2
.

(Determinant preserving)

\det(\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=d^{d}\det(\mathbf{x})\det(\mathbf{y}),

where
\det(\mathbf{x})\equiv\det(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{x}))=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{d}
.

Proof.

By Eq. (
19
), we directly have

\det(\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=d^{d}\det(\mathbf{x})\left|\det(\mathbf{V})\right|^{2}\det(\mathbf{y})=d^{d}\det(\mathbf{x})\det(\mathbf{y})
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

(Duality)
Let
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. Then,

\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{z})^{\downarrow}=d^{d-2}\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{x}),\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{y})),

where
\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\left(\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{1},\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{2},\cdots,\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{d}\right)
.

Proof.

By Eq. (
19
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{x}),\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\times\mathrm{eig}(\text{diag}(\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}))\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\text{diag}(\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow}))\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\times\mathrm{eig}(\mathrm{adj}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}))\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{V})\mathrm{adj}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow}))\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\times\mathrm{eig}(\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\text{diag}(\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow})\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\times\mathrm{adj}(\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\text{diag}(\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow})\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow})))^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\times\mathrm{adj}(\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})/d)^{\downarrow}=d^{2-d}\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{z})^{\downarrow},

where we have used several properties of the adjugate of a matrix
\bm{\Psi}
, e.g.,
\mathrm{adj}(\bm{\Psi})=\det(\bm{\Psi})\bm{\Psi}^{-1}
and
\mathrm{adj}(c\bm{\Psi})=c^{d-1}\mathrm{adj}(\bm{\Psi})
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

(Majorization extremity)
Let
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. If
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\oplus(\overbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}^{m-d})\succ\mathbf{x}
, then
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\succ\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

We will prove this lemma by induction. Suppose there exists
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{l}>x^{\downarrow}_{l}
for some
l
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{k}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j},\quad\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1;

(20)

we would like to prove

\sum_{j=1}^{l}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}.

(21)

Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}+z^{\prime\downarrow}_{l}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}}{d+1-l}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}}{d+1-l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\left(d+1-l\right)^{-1}{\sum_{j=l}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j},

(22)

where the first inequality holds because the maximum is never less than the mean and the second inequality holds because
\mbox{tr}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}))=\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})

and
1-\left(d+1-l\right)^{-1}\geq 0
. Hence Eq. (
21
) is proved.

Finally, choose the minimum
l
that satisfies
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{l}>x^{\downarrow}_{l}
. In other words,
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}=x^{\downarrow}_{j}
for
j<l
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}z^{\prime\downarrow}_{j},\quad\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1,\qquad

which completes the induction. ∎

Lemma 5
.

(Convexity)
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})
is convex, i.e.,

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\succ\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}),\quad p_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Proof.

Similar to Lemma
4
, we will prove this lemma by induction. Suppose there exists
\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{l}>\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\right]^{\downarrow}_{l}
for some
l
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]^{\downarrow}_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{j},

(23)

\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1
; we can prove

\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]^{\downarrow}_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{j}

(24)

the same way as in Eq. (
IV.1
).
Now, choose the minimum
l
that satisfies
\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{l}>\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\right]^{\downarrow}_{l}
. In other words,
\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{j}=\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\right]^{\downarrow}_{j}
for
j<l
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\mathcal{P}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\right]^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}^{\downarrow}\right]^{\downarrow}_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\right]^{\downarrow}_{j},

\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1
, which completes the induction.
∎

Lemma 6
.

(A sum-product mixing majorization inequality)
Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
. If

\ln\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}+\ln\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow}\succ_{w}\ln\mathbf{z}^{\downarrow},

then

\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})+\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\succ_{w}\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z}).

Proof.

To begin with, w.l.o.g. we will just assume
m=d+1
, which means that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
is only one dimension less than
\mathbf{x}
. Indeed, for any
m>d
, the concentration process [Eq. (
10
)] can be equally constructed by applying such a one-dimension-less
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathbf{x}
recursively
m-d
times. Thus, if our statement holds for
m=d+1
, then it holds for general
m
too.

First, we would like to prove the following inequality:

\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{j}\right)\geq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}.

(25)

By the definition of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
[Eq. (
12
)], we have

\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}z^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)\left[{\sum_{j=l}^{d+1}z^{\downarrow}_{j}}/{\left(d+1-l\right)}\right]^{d+1-l},

where
l
separates
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})
and satisfies
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}=z^{\downarrow}_{j}
for
j<l
and
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}>z^{\downarrow}_{j}
for
l\leq j\leq d
.

Now we take advantage of a special reverse AM–GM inequality which we will introduce and prove in Appendix
D
. Let
\Delta=z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}
,
\varepsilon_{j}=z^{\downarrow}_{d+1-j}
, and
E_{k}=\Delta+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,d+1-l
. We can see that
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon_{k+1}\leq{E_{k}}/k
,
\forall k
[implied by Eq. (
10
)]. Thus, using Corollary
D.3
,

\displaystyle\left[{\sum_{j=l}^{d+1}z^{\downarrow}_{j}}/{\left(d+1-l\right)}\right]^{d+1-l}

\displaystyle\leq\exp({z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}/z^{\downarrow}_{d}})\prod_{j=l}^{d}z^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{{z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}}/{z^{\downarrow}_{d}}}\right)^{2}\prod_{j=l}^{d}z^{\downarrow}_{j},\quad\quad

where the second inequality holds since
{z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}\leq z^{\downarrow}_{d}}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}\leq\left(1+\sqrt{{z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}}/{z^{\downarrow}_{d}}}\right)^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}z^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(z^{\downarrow}_{d}+z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}+2\sqrt{z^{\downarrow}_{d}z^{\downarrow}_{d+1}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}z^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(x^{\downarrow}_{d}y^{\downarrow}_{d}+x^{\downarrow}_{d+1}y^{\downarrow}_{d+1}+2\sqrt{x^{\downarrow}_{d}y^{\downarrow}_{d}x^{\downarrow}_{d+1}y^{\downarrow}_{d+1}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}x^{\downarrow}_{j}y^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(x^{\downarrow}_{d}+x^{\downarrow}_{d+1}\right)\left(y^{\downarrow}_{d}+y^{\downarrow}_{d+1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}x^{\downarrow}_{j}y^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{j}\right),

where in the third step we use Theorem
C.2
, in the fourth step we use the usual AM–GM inequality, and in the last step we use the definition of the concentration process [Eq. (
10
)]. Hence Eq. (
25
) is proved.

Now we will complete the proof by induction. Suppose there exists
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{l}>z^{\downarrow}_{l}
for some
l
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j},

(26)

\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1
, we would like to prove

\sum_{j=1}^{l}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}.

(27)

The proof structure is the same as in Eq. (
IV.1
), while the only difference is that, for the second inequality in Eq. (
IV.1
) to hold, we need to show that

\mbox{tr}(\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}))+\mbox{tr}(\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}))\geq\mbox{tr}(\ln\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z}))
.
This has been proved by Eq. (
25
). Hence Eq. (
27
) is proved.

Finally, choose the minimum
l
that satisfies
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{l}>z^{\downarrow}_{l}
. In other words,
\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}=z^{\downarrow}_{j}
for
j<l
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})\right]_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln z^{\downarrow}_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln x^{\downarrow}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln y^{\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ln\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{j},\forall k=1,\cdots,l-1\qquad

which completes the induction.
∎

IV.2
Simple Series

For simple series network topology [Fig.
2
], the swapping protocol is responsible for entanglement transmission. What is the maximum of the final average
k
-concurrence [from Eq. (
1
)]

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C^{\alpha}_{k}=\sum_{\alpha}\left\|\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}\right\|_{2}^{2}C_{k}(\sigma^{2}({\left\|\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}\right\|_{2}^{-1}}{\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}}))

between A and B that can be obtained by the swapping protocol? How does it compare with the deterministic series rule? This is answered by the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Given a simple series QN,
compared with generic entanglement swapping protocols of probabilistic outcomes,
the series rule (Table
2
) produces the optimal average
G
-concurrence between A and B.

Proof.

It suffices to investigate two links
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
in series. The proof can be easily generalized to
n
links.

Let
\{\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\}
denote a set of quantum measurements as used in the entanglement swapping protocol [Eq. (
1
)]. In general, given probability
p_{\alpha}
and the corresponding
k
-concurrence
C^{\alpha}_{k}
of outcome
\alpha
, the maximum average
k
-concurrence
\max_{\{\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\}}\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C^{\alpha}_{k}
is intractable, and the corresponding optimal
\{\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\}
should implicitly depend on
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
. However, this is not the case for
G
-concurrence. To see this,
let
k=d
; then,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C^{\alpha}_{d}=d\sum_{\alpha}\left|\det(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha})\right|^{{2}/{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\sum_{\alpha}\det(\bm{\lambda}_{a})^{1/d}\left|\det(\mathbf{X}^{\alpha})\right|^{2/d}\det(\bm{\lambda}_{b})^{1/d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{-1}C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{b})\sum_{\alpha}\left|\det(\mathbf{X}^{\alpha})\right|^{2/d}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{-2}C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{b})\sum_{\alpha}\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\alpha\dagger}\mathbf{X}^{\alpha})=C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{b}),\qquad

(28)

where the AM–GM inequality is used in the second-to-last step and Eq. (
2
) is used in the last step. Equation (
IV.2
) indicates that the final average
G
-concurrence will never be greater than the product of the
G
-concurrences of
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
.

Note that this proof was originally given by Gour in Ref.
Gour 2005
. The validity of the proof comes from the unique feature of the multiplicity of determinants, which is not applicable to other
k
-concurrence monotones
Gour 2005
. A summary of the use of determinant-based entanglement measures compared with other probabilistic measures for certain QN topologies can be found in Ref.
Perseguers et al. 2008
.

It remains to be shown that the equality in Eq. (
IV.2
) holds for
\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})
. By Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle C_{d}(\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}))=d\det(\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}))^{1/d}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{2}\det(\bm{\lambda}_{a})^{1/d}\det(\bm{\lambda}_{b})^{1/d}=C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})C_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{b}).

Thus,

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C_{d}^{\alpha}\leq C_{d}(\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})),

(30)

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark.—
Note that Eq. (
30
) says nothing about the majorization preorder. In fact, usually there is

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\sigma^{2}(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}/\left\|\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}\right\|_{2})^{\downarrow}=\sum_{\alpha}\sigma^{2}(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha})^{\downarrow}\nsucc\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}).

(31)

Would the inequality in Eq. (
31
) hold, one could prove Eq. (
30
) for not only
k=d
but
k<d
as well. Unfortunately, this is not true.

IV.3
Simple Parallel

For simple parallel network topology [Fig.
2
], the concentration protocol is responsible for entanglement transmission. Similarly, we ask:
What is the maximum of the final average
k
-concurrence
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C^{\alpha}_{k}
between A and B that can be obtained by the concentration protocol? How does it compare with the deterministic parallel rule? The answer is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Given a simple parallel QN,
compared with generic entanglement concentration protocols of probabilistic outcomes,
the parallel rule (Table
2
) produces the optimal average
k
-concurrence between A and B for
k=1,2,\cdots,d
.

Proof.

Again, it suffices to look at two links
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
in parallel. The proof can be easily generalized to
n
links.

If we can obtain an ensemble of outcomes
\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}^{\prime}
, each with probability
p_{\alpha}
, from
\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}
, then, by the fundamental limit of LOCC
Vidal 2000
,

\left(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}^{\prime\downarrow}\oplus(\overbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}^{d^{2}-d})\right)\succ\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}.

Hence, by Lemma
4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}^{\prime\downarrow}\succ\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}),

(32)

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}^{\prime})\leq C_{k}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}^{\prime\downarrow})\leq C_{k}(\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}))\quad

for
k=1,2,\cdots,d
.
∎

Remark.—
Note that unlike Eq. (
31
) for the series rule, Eq. (
32
) does hold. Therefore the optimality of the parallel rule is for all
k
-concurrence, not just
G
-concurrence.
This implies that
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b})
always contains the maximum allowable entanglement that can be concentrated from
\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}
on average.

IV.4
Parallel-then-Series

For parallel-then-series network topology [Fig.
2
], both the swapping and concentration protocols are responsible for entanglement transmission. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Given a parallel-then-series QN,
compared with generic entanglement swapping and concentration protocols of probabilistic outcomes,
the series and parallel rules (Table
2
) produce the optimal average
G
-concurrence between A and B.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the theorem for four links:
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
are connected in parallel,
\bm{\lambda}_{c}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{d}
are connected in parallel, and then the two parallel groups are connected in series.
Now, let
\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}=\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b})^{1/2}\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d})^{1/2}
, as used in the entanglement swapping protocol [Eq. (
1
)]. The difference, however, is that
\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}
is not a
d\times d
matrix, but a
d^{2}\times d^{2}
matrix. This is because we are considering a generic swapping protocol, which must be applied to the full
d^{2}
dimensions. Then, the final
d
-dimensional outcome
\alpha
will be given by
\mathcal{P}(\sigma^{2}(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha}))
.

By the Gelfand-Naimark theorem on singular values (Theorem III.4.5 of Ref.
Bhatia 1997
), we have

\ln\sigma^{2}(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha})\prec\ln\left(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}\right)^{\downarrow}+\ln\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{X}^{\alpha})+\ln\left(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d}\right)^{\downarrow}.

(33)

Thus, by Lemma
6
we have, in particular,

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{P}(\sigma^{2}(\bm{\Psi}^{\alpha})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{-2}C_{d}(\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}))C_{d}(\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d}))\sum_{\alpha}\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\alpha\dagger}\mathbf{X}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle C_{d}(\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}))C_{d}(\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d})),\qquad

(34)

where the AM–GM inequality is used in the second step, Eq. (

\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d})

Remark.—
At first glimpse, it seems that we would have more degrees of freedom to design a swapping protocol directly on the full
d^{2}
-dimensional
\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d}

-concurrence. Interestingly though, Theorem

says that the best strategy is to first reduce them to
d

\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b})
and
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d})
and then apply a swapping protocol on them. Our result implies a strong practical convenience in entanglement transmission and that it is in fact unnecessary to store all
d^{m}

m
parallel links. We can safely concentrate and produce a
d

m
parallel links and then swap it with other links connected in series. The final

Note particularly that the nested repeater protocol
Briegel et al. 1998

\bm{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{b}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{c}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{d}
using two independent swapping protocols on

and

d^{2}

G
-concurrence.

Series-then-Parallel

IV.6
Series-Parallel

Finally, for arbitrary series-parallel network topology [Fig.

Theorem 4

(For

compared with generic entanglement swapping and concentration protocols of probabilistic outcomes,

-concurrence between A and B.

Proof.

From Theorems
1

2

\alpha

\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}

Remark.—
This special optimality on the worst-case

IV.7

After obtaining a partially entangled state as the DET outcome, it is always possible to further convert it to a maximally entangled state
\bm{\lambda}_{\max}=\left(1/d,1/d,\cdots,1/d\right)

Quantum Circuit Implementation

In this section, we demonstrate the experimental feasibility of the DET scheme using IBM’s quantum computing platform
Qiskit

Treinish et al. 2023
, showing how to design quantum circuits for the corresponding protocols on qubits (
d=2
).
The performance of the circuits is numerically tested on a 5-qubit noisy simulator with its parameters exactly matching the realistic scenario of IBM’s quantum hardware.

Compared with Bell-state-based schemes, the partially-entangled-state-based DET scheme has notable pros and cons: On the one hand, all inputs in DET are only partially entangled and likely easier to generate, and hence the fidelity will be, in general, higher than using Bell states; on the other hand, the circuit parameters explicitly depend on the inputs, and thus the initial states must be properly estimated by, e.g., heralding
Humphreys et al. 2018
or tomography
Bantysh et al. 2021
before executing the scheme. Since we have shown that any obtained partially entangled qubits can be converted into a singlet for Bell-state-based tasks with higher conversion probability than CEP,
we believe that our demonstration of DET, representing a thorough study of general operations on partially entangled states, is not only a proof of principle but also practically useful.

Series Rule

The circuit that implements the swapping function
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
resembles the common design of the Bell-state-based swapping protocol
Żukowski et al. 1993

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(+\left|\uparrow\uparrow\right\rangle+\left|\uparrow\downarrow\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow\uparrow\right\rangle-\left|\downarrow\downarrow\right\rangle\right)\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(+\left|\uparrow\uparrow\right\rangle-\left|\uparrow\downarrow\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow\uparrow\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow\downarrow\right\rangle\right)\\

\frac{1}{2}\left(+\left|\uparrow\uparrow\right\rangle-\left|\uparrow\downarrow\right\rangle-\left|\downarrow\uparrow\right\rangle-\left|\downarrow\downarrow\right\rangle\right),\\
\end{cases}

which can be implemented by a controlled-not (CX) gate
plus two Hadamard gates.
Here, we use the convention
\left|\uparrow\right\rangle
and
\left|\downarrow\right\rangle
to denote a physical qubit’s two states, keeping the physical convention distinguishable from the logical convention of the Schmidt numbers.
According to the Relay’s measurement results, Alice (A) and Bob (B) choose whether to apply a Z gate to their own qubits, originally prepared as partially entangled with the Relay’s qubits given by
\bm{\lambda}_{a}=\left(\lambda_{a,1},\lambda_{a,2}\right)
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}=\left(\lambda_{b,1},\lambda_{b,2}\right)
respectively, now resulting in a new state
\sqrt{\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{b,1}}\left|\uparrow\uparrow\right\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{b,2}}\left|\uparrow\downarrow\right\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,1}}\left|\downarrow\uparrow\right\rangle-\sqrt{\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,2}}\left|\downarrow\downarrow\right\rangle
deterministically shared by A and B. The next step is for A and B to apply local unitary transformations
U^{\dagger}
and
V^{\dagger}
to transform the new deterministic state into a diagonal form with Schmidt numbers
\bm{\lambda}=\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})=\left({\frac{1+\sqrt{1-16\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,1}\lambda_{b,2}}}{2}},{\frac{1-\sqrt{1-16\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,1}\lambda_{b,2}}}{2}}\right)
, given by the singular value decomposition (SVD),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{b,1}}&\sqrt{\lambda_{a,1}\lambda_{b,2}}\\
\sqrt{\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,1}}&-\sqrt{\lambda_{a,2}\lambda_{b,2}}\end{pmatrix}=U\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda})^{1/2}V^{\dagger}.

The full circuit diagram is shown in Fig.

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{11},\lambda_{12}+\lambda_{21}+\lambda_{22},0,0\right).

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{11},\lambda_{12},\lambda_{21},\lambda_{22}\right)\overset{\text{T}}{\longrightarrow}\left(1/2,\lambda_{12},\lambda_{21},\lambda_{11}+\lambda_{22}-1/2\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\overset{\text{T}}{\longrightarrow}\left(1/2,\lambda_{11}+\lambda_{12}+\lambda_{22}-1/2,\lambda_{21},0\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\overset{\text{T}}{\longrightarrow}\left(1/2,\lambda_{11}+\lambda_{12}+\lambda_{21}+\lambda_{22}-1/2,0,0\right).

Discussion

What makes the formulation of deterministic entanglement transmission (DET) scalable with network size and adaptable to different (at least series-parallel) network topologies is the use of deterministic quantum communication protocols
Meng et al. 2021
. One may wonder if the specific DET scheme based on the series and parallel rules (Table
2
) that we have introduced is the only possible scheme using deterministic protocols. The answer is negative:
For example, the matrix
\mathbf{V}
in the recipe of
\mathcal{S}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})
[Eq. (
4
)] that makes it deterministic is not unique if
\mathbf{V}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})
can generally depend on
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
. When properly chosen, the new swapping function can even produce a better
k
-concurrence (
k\neq d
) than our proposed swapping function
Gour 2005
. Moreover, if quantum catalysts
Jonathan and Plenio 1999
are allowed, then it is also possible to have a new concentration function
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\lambda})
that is deterministic without satisfying the majorization relation [Eq. (
18
)]. These new functions can be used to build a different set of series and parallel rules.

What remains is the question of whether we can also find deterministic protocols that are scalable for non-series-parallel QN. The complexity of answering this lies in the fact that multipartite protocols
Perseguers et al. 2010
and multilink-based QN routing
Pirandola 2019
may have to be used for producing deterministic outcomes. The existence of such deterministic protocols can help us achieve entanglement transmission for a more general QN.

We also find it interesting to apply our scheme to an
infinite
series-parallel QN. We expect that percolation-like criticality in terms of
\bm{\lambda}
of each link can be observed for entanglement transmission in the thermodynamic limit. However, note that to each QN link we have assigned not a single number but a sequence of Schmidt numbers which have more than one degree of freedom when
d>2
. This prohibits us from establishing an exact one-to-one mapping from
\bm{\lambda}
to a real temperature-like parameter. It is unknown how criticality behaves in such case.

Appendix A
Basic Notations

We briefly recall some basic notions in matrix analysis and majorization theory
Bhatia 1997
:

Let
\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Note that some notations for matrices can also be defined for
\mathbf{x}
. Given the diagonal matrix of
\mathbf{x}
,

\text{diag}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&\ldots\\
0&x_{2}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

we can simply define the trace of
\mathbf{x}
as
\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\mbox{tr}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}
, the determinant of
\mathbf{x}
as
\det(\mathbf{x})\equiv\det(\text{diag}(\mathbf{x}))=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
, and
the adjugate of
\mathbf{x}
as
\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\left(\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{1},\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{2},\cdots,\det(\mathbf{x})/x_{n}\right)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}
be the sequences given by rearranging the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
in decreasing order and increasing order, respectively. In other words,
x^{\downarrow}_{1},x^{\downarrow}_{2},\cdots,x^{\downarrow}_{n}
as the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}
satisfy
x^{\downarrow}_{1}\geq x^{\downarrow}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x^{\downarrow}_{n}
. Similarly, for
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}
there is
x^{\uparrow}_{1}\leq x^{\uparrow}_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x^{\uparrow}_{n}
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. We say that
\mathbf{x}
is
weakly submajorized
by
\mathbf{y}
, or
\mathbf{x}\prec_{w}\mathbf{y}

Bhatia 1997
, if

\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{j},\quad\forall k=1,\cdots,n.

(39)

In particular, if
\mathbf{x}
is weakly submajorized by
\mathbf{y}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{n}x^{\downarrow}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y^{\downarrow}_{j},\text{ or, }\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{x})=\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{y}),

(40)

then we say that
\mathbf{x}
is
majorized
by
\mathbf{y}
, or
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}

Bhatia 1997
.

For example, when
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{x})=1
, we always have
\left(1/n,\cdots,1/n\right)\prec\mathbf{x}\prec\left(1,0,\cdots,0\right)
.
On the other hand, given two arbitrary
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
with
\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{x})=\mbox{tr}(\mathbf{y})=1
, we may have both
\mathbf{x}\nprec\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}\nsucc\mathbf{y}
. Thus, the majorization relation is not a total order. It is not a partial order either, because
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{y}\succ\mathbf{x}
do not necessarily imply
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
, since they may differ by a permutation
Bhatia 1997
. The majorization relation is only a
preorder
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.

In this paper, our attention is mostly focused on
d^{2}
-dimensional normalized states with
d
Schmidt numbers
\bm{\lambda}
satisfying
\mbox{tr}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv 1
. We exclusively use the symbol
\bm{\lambda}
whenever we implicitly know that it has a
trace of unity
and
d-1
degrees of freedom. For general vectors, we use the symbols
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\cdots\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
instead.

Appendix B
The
k
-Concurrence Family of Entanglement Monotones

Given a
\left(d\times d\right)
-dimensional bipartite pure state, up to unitary equivalence,
\left|\bm{\lambda}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\left|jj\right\rangle
, there are
d
concurrence monotones, as developed in Ref.
Gour 2005
,

C_{k}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv\left[{S_{k}(\bm{\lambda})}/{S_{k}(\left(\frac{1}{d},\frac{1}{d},\cdots,\frac{1}{d}\right))}\right]^{1/k},\quad\mbox{tr}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv 1,

(41)

k=1,2,\cdots,d
,
where
S_{k}(\bm{\lambda})
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial
Bhatia 1997
, e.g.,

\displaystyle S_{0}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle S_{1}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq d}\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle S_{2}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j},

\displaystyle S_{3}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{1\leq i<j<k\leq d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k},

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle S_{d}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{d}.

Note that
C_{k}
is named
k
-concurrence
since the whole monotone family is nothing but a generalization of
C_{2}(\bm{\lambda})
, the “concurrence” which is more commonly referred to in the context of quantum information theory. Another special concurrence of the family is
C_{d}
(i.e.,
k=d
), the
G
-concurrence
, as it stands for the
geometric
mean of the Schmidt numbers
Gour 2005
. The importance of
G
-concurrence can be understood from the main text.

All
k
-concurrences have the following properties
Gour 2005
.

•

Permutation invariance
[
\bm{\lambda}\to\text{perm}(\bm{\lambda})
]:

C_{k}(\bm{\lambda})=C_{k}(\text{perm}(\bm{\lambda})).

(42)

•

Unit measure:

\displaystyle 0\leq C_{k}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1,\text{ and }

\displaystyle C_{k}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\text{ if }\bm{\lambda}=\left(\frac{1}{d},\frac{1}{d},\cdots,\frac{1}{d}\right)

(43)

•

Isotone:

C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{a})\leq C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{b})\text{ if }\bm{\lambda}_{a}\succ\bm{\lambda}_{b}.

(44)

•

Concavity:

C_{k}(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha})\geq\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}),\quad p_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(45)

In particular, if
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=1
, then
p_{\alpha}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}
can be explained as a probabilistic ensemble. The
average

k
-concurrence is then defined as the RHS of Eq. (
45
),
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha})
, and the
worst-case

k
-concurrence is defined as
\min_{\alpha}C_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha})
, accordingly.

Appendix C
Some Useful Theorems

Theorem C.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
be convex, i.e.,

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}F(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})\succ F(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha})
,
p_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. If, for all
\mathbf{x}
,

\displaystyle F(\text{perm}(\mathbf{x}))=F(\mathbf{x}),

(46)

then
F
is isotone, i.e.,
F(\mathbf{x})\succ F(\mathbf{y})
if
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{y}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{y}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
. By Theorem II.1.10 of Ref.
Bhatia 1997
there exist a set of
m\times m
-dimensional permutation matrices
\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2},\cdots
and a set of
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=1
such that
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}
. Thus

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{y})=F(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x})\prec\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}F(\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}F(\mathbf{x}),

i.e.,
F(\mathbf{x})\succ F(\mathbf{y})
.
∎

Theorem C.2
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. If
\ln\mathbf{x}\succ_{w}\ln\mathbf{y}
, then

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}x^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)\sum_{j=l}^{k}\left(x^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)^{s}\geq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}y^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)\sum_{j=l}^{k}\left(y^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)^{s},

(48)

\forall k=1,2,\cdots,n
and
\forall l=1,2,\cdots,k
, given that
0\leq s\leq k-l+1
.

Proof.

Let
c=\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}\left({y^{\downarrow}_{j}}/{x^{\downarrow}_{j}}\right)\leq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\prod_{j=l}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{j}\geq c\prod_{j=l}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{j}\geq c^{\left(k-l+1\right)/{s}}\prod_{j=l}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{j}=\prod_{j=l}^{k}\left(c^{1/s}y^{\downarrow}_{j}\right),\qquad

(49)

\forall k=l,l+1,\cdots,n
. Therefore, by Example II.3.5(v) of Ref.
Bhatia 1997
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=l}^{k}\left(x^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)^{s}\geq c\sum_{j=l}^{k}\left(y^{\downarrow}_{j}\right)^{s},

(50)

which holds because
f(e^{t})=\left(e^{t}\right)^{s}
is convex and monotone increasing in
t

Bhatia 1997
. ∎

Appendix D
A Reverse AM–GM Inequality

We will prove the following inequality.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\Delta\geq 0
. Let
E_{k}=\Delta+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,n
. If
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon_{k+1}\leq{E_{k}}/k
,
\forall k
, then

\left(\frac{E_{n}}{n}\right)^{n}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\varepsilon_{n-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{n}\right)^{n}

(51)

with equality if and only if
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n-1}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{1}
.

Proof.

To start with, note that

{E_{k}}/k={\varepsilon_{k}/k+E_{k-1}}/k\leq E_{k-1}/\left(k-1\right)
. Therefore the following inequality holds:

\frac{E_{n}}{n}\leq\frac{E_{n-1}}{n-1}\leq\cdots\leq\frac{E_{1}}{1}.

(52)

Now we will prove Eq. (
51
) by induction. Suppose

\left(\frac{E_{j-1}}{j-1}\right)^{j-1}\leq\varepsilon_{j-1}\varepsilon_{j-2}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j-1}\right)^{j-1};

(53)

we would like to prove

\left(\frac{E_{j}}{j}\right)^{j}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j}\right)^{j}.

(54)

To do so, define
f_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j-1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{1})
as the right-hand side minus the left-hand side of Eq. (
54
). Taking the derivative of
f_{j}
w.r.t.
\varepsilon_{k}
for
k=2,\cdots,j
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial\varepsilon_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j}\right)^{j}-\left(\frac{E_{j}}{j}\right)^{j-1}

(55)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j}\right)^{j}-\left(\frac{E_{j-1}}{j-1}\right)^{j-1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j-1}\right)^{j-1}-\left(\frac{E_{j-1}}{j-1}\right)^{j-1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f_{j-1}\geq 0

with equality if and only if
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j-1}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{1}
.
Thus, fixing
\varepsilon_{1}
and noticing that
\varepsilon_{2}
is constrained by
\varepsilon_{1}
only, we conclude that, since
{\partial f_{j}}/{\partial\varepsilon_{2}}\geq 0
,
f_{j}
takes the minimum if and only if
\varepsilon_{2}\geq\varepsilon_{1}
actually takes the equality; next, fixing
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{1}
and noticing that
\varepsilon_{3}
is constrained by
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
only, we conclude that
f_{j}
takes the minimum if and only if
\varepsilon_{3}\geq\varepsilon_{2}
actually takes the equality; ….

Taken together, we conclude that
f_{j}
takes the minimum if and only if
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j-1}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{1}
, which put back into
f_{j}
yields
f_{j}(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{1})=0
. Hence
f_{j}\geq 0
.
The induction is thus completed given
f_{1}=0
.
∎

We note that, despite being a reversed inequality of the AM–GM type, Theorem
D.1
is tight. This is because
\Delta
can take any nonnegative value. The two independent constraints
\varepsilon_{k+1}\geq\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}\leq{E_{k}}/k
in Theorem
D.1
together imply that the deviations between the coordinates of
\bm{\varepsilon}
cannot be too large, which are controlled by
\Delta
. In particular, if
\Delta=0
, then Eq. (
51
) just becomes the reverse of the usual AM–GM inequality. However,
\Delta=0
also requires
\varepsilon_{k+1}=\varepsilon_{k}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{1}
given the constraints. Therefore Eq. (
51
) will not be violated since it can only take the equality.

Corollary D.2
.

(Same prerequisites as in Theorem
D.1
)

\left(\frac{E_{j}}{j}\right)^{j}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}\left(1+\frac{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}}{j}\right)^{j},\quad j=1,2,\cdots,n

(56)

with equality if and only if
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j-1}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{1}
.

Proof.

This has been proved in the proof of Theorem
D.1
, which is a special case (
j=n
) of this Corollary.
∎

Corollary D.3
.

(Same prerequisites as in Theorem
D.1
)

\left(\frac{E_{j}}{j}\right)^{j}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}\cdots\varepsilon_{1}e^{\Delta/\varepsilon_{1}},\quad j=1,2,\cdots,n

.

This is because
