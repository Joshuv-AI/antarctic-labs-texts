Flexible Accuracy for Differential Privacy
Aman Bansal, Rahul Chunduru, Deepesh Data, Manoj Prabhakaran

Abstract
Differential Privacy (DP) has become a gold standard in privacy-preserving data analysis. While it provides one of the most rigorous notions of privacy, there are many settings where its applicability is limited. Our main contribution is in augmenting differential privacy with Flexible Accuracy , which allows small distortions in the input (e.g., dropping outliers) before measuring accuracy of the output, allowing one to extend DP mechanisms to high-sensitivity functions. We present mechanisms that can help in achieving this notion for functions that had no meaningful differentially private mechanisms previously. In particular, we illustrate an application to differentially private histograms, which in turn yields mechanisms for revealing the support of a dataset or the extremal values in the data. Analyses of our constructions exploit new versatile composition theorems that facilitate modular design. All the above extensions use our new definitional framework, which is in terms of “lossy Wasserstein distance” – a 2-parameter error measure for distributions. This may be of independent interest.

Introduction

In the era of big data, privacy has been a major concern, to the point that
recent legislative moves, like General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in
the European Union, have mandated various measures for ensuring privacy. Further, in
the face of a global pandemic that has prompted governments to collect and
share individual-level information for epidemiological purposes, debates on
privacy-utility trade-offs have been brought to sharper relief.
Against this backdrop, mathematical theories of privacy are of great
importance.
Differential Privacy
[
DMNS06
]
is by far the most impactful
mathematical framework today for privacy in statistical databases. It has
seen large scale adoption in theory and practice, including machine learning
applications and large scale commercial implementations (e.g.,

[
ACG+16
,
BCS15
,
BCSZ18
,
EPK14
,
DPT17
]
).

In this work, we make foundational contributions to the area of Differential
Privacy (DP), extending its applicability. Our main contribution is the notion of
Flexible Accuracy
–
a new framework for measuring the
accuracy
of a mechanism (while
retaining the DP framework unaltered for quantifying privacy). This lets us
develop new DP mechanisms with non-trivial provable (and empirically
demonstrable) accuracy guarantees in settings involving high-sensitivity functions.

Motivating Flexible Accuracy (FA).

Consider querying a database consisting of integer valued observations –
say, ages of patients who recovered from a certain disease – for the
maximum value. For the sake of privacy, one may wish to apply a DP
mechanism, rather than output the maximum in the data itself. Two possible
datasets which differ in only one patient are considered neighbors and a DP
mechanism needs to make the outputs on these two samples indistinguishable
from each other. However, the function in question is
highly
sensitive
-- two neighboring datasets can have their maxima differ by as
much as the entire range of possible ages
1
1

1

In fact,
all datasets
with low maximum values have high
sensitivity
locally
, by considering a neighboring dataset with a
single additional data item with a large value.

– and, as we shall see in our empirical evaluations in
Section
7
, the various kinds of mechanisms in the literature
[
MT07
,
BNS19
,
Vad17
,
DL09
,
NRS07
,
BNS16
]
do not provide a satisfactory solution.

The difficulty in solving this problem is related to another issue.
Consider the problem of reporting a
histogram
(again,
say, of patients’ ages). Here a standard DP mechanism, of adding a zero-mean
Laplace noise to each bar of the histogram is indeed reasonable, as the
histogram function has low sensitivity in each bar. Now, note that

maximum can be computed as a function of the histogram
. However, even
though the histogram mechanism was sufficiently accurate in the standard
sense, the maximum computed from its output is no longer accurate! This is
because when a non-zero count is added to a large-valued item which
originally has a count of 0, the maximum can increase arbitrarily.

Flexible Accuracy (FA) is a relaxed notion of accuracy that lets us address both
of the above issues. In particular, it not only enables new DP mechanisms
for maximum, but also allows one to derive the mechanism from a new DP
mechanism for histograms. We provide a general
composition theorem
that
enables such transfer of accuracy guarantees that is not applicable to
conventional accuracy measures.

The high-level idea of Flexible Accuracy is to allow for some

distortion of the input
when measuring accuracy. We shall require
distortion to be defined using a
quasi-metric
over the input space (a
quasi-metric is akin to a metric, but is not required to be symmetric). A
good example of distortion is
dropping a few items
from the dataset;
note that in this case,
adding
a data item is
not
considered low distortion.
Referring back to the example of reporting maximum, given a dataset with a
single elderly patient and many young patients, flexible accuracy with
respect to this distortion allows a mechanism for maximum to report the
maximum age of the younger group.
2
2

2

Of course, it is not obvious what should determine which items
should be dropped and with what probability. This will be the subject of
our new mechanisms.

Flexible accuracy needs to account for errors that can be attributed to
distortion of the input (input error), as well as to inaccuracies in the
output (output error). To be able to exploit input distortion while retaining privacy, we allow
input distortion to be randomized. A side-effect of this is that our
measure of output accuracy needs to allow the “correct output” to be
randomized (i.e., defined by a distribution), even if we are interested in
only deterministic functions. To generalize the conventional

probabilistically approximately correct
(PAC) guarantees to this
setting, we introduce a natural, but new quantity called
lossy

\infty
-Wasserstein distance
.
Our final definition of flexible accuracy is a 3-parameter quantity, with
one parameter accounting for input distortion, and 2 parameters used for
output error measured using lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance.

1.1
Our Contributions

Our contributions are in three parts:

•

Definitions:
We present a conceptual
enhancement to the framework of DP –
flexible accuracy
– which considers error after allowing for a small
distortion of the input
; see
Definition
3
.
To account for randomized distortion (and more generally, to be able to

introduce and use a new measure called

-Wasserstein distance
(see

),

Distance). This also generalizes several existing notions, such as the PAC guarantee, the notion of total variation distance, etc.

•

Composition Theorems:

We present a composition theorem for flexible accuracy (see
Theorem
1
),
which gives an FA guarantee for a composed mechanism from those of the constituent ones. This involves identifying new quantities including
distortion sensitivity
(see
Definition
4
)
and
error sensitivity
(see
Definition
5
).
To be able to use such composed mechanisms for DP, we rely on the well-known post-processing theorem of DP,
as well as a new pre-processing theorem (see
Theorem
2
).

•

Mechanisms:
We give a DP mechanism with FA guarantee for releasing a sanitized histogram (called the
Shifted-Truncated Laplace
mechanism; see
Algorithm
1
and

),
which, via our composition theorems, yield DP mechanisms with FA

(see

).
These functions include several high-sensitivity functions, such as maximum

We present an

none were available till now), our mechanisms compare favorably with
the others in terms of accuracy (flexible and otherwise) empirically
as well.

1.2
The Surprising Power of Flexible Accuracy

Consider a sequence of
n+1
neighboring histograms, such that the first in the sequence has all
its
n
elements in the first bar, and the last one has all elements in the last
bar, and the first and the last bars are far away from each other.
In any reasonably accurate (flexible or not) mechanism for a histogram-based statistic like max, the answers for these two extremes must be very different with
probability almost 1. So, intuitively, there should be some pair of neighbors in this
sequence for which the
answers should be significantly different with probability at least
1/n
.
This seems to preclude obtaining
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for a small constant

\epsilon
with
\delta\ll 1/n
. Remarkably, this intuition turns out to be
wrong! By carefully calibrating the probability of the responses (while
also making sure that the responses can be attributed to only dropping a few
items – as permitted by flexible accuracy), our mechanism
can obtain the following guarantee for the max function (see
Corollary
1
):

Informal result for max:
Our flexibly-accurate mechanism for max over a bounded range achieves
\left(\epsilon,\epsilon e^{-\Omega(\epsilon\alpha n)}\right)
-DP while incurring an arbitrarily small output error after dropping only
\alpha n
elements.

The above result gives a trade-off between the privacy guarantee and number of elements dropped. For example:

(i)
By choosing
\epsilon=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}
and
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, our mechanism is
(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}},e^{-\Omega(n^{1/4})})
-DP while dropping only
O(\sqrt{n})
elements.

(ii)
By choosing
\epsilon
to be a small constant (say,
0.1
) and say,
\alpha=\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}
, our mechanism is
(0.1,n^{-\Omega(\log n)})
-DP while dropping only
O(\log^{2}n)
elements.
See
Section
5.2.3
for several other parameter choices that are of interest.

Significance of the New Mechanisms.

Traditional DP literature has largely
not addressed functions like the maximum function,
f_{\mathrm{max}}
. This is in part due to the very high
sensitivity of such functions: When the database has entries from
[0,B]
,
the sensitivity of
f_{\mathrm{max}}
is
B
.
3
3

3

The sensitivity of a real-valued function
f:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined by
\Delta_{f}:=\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})|
. In the case of
f_{\mathrm{max}}
, there are neighboring databases
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, where the first database
\mathbf{x}
has all the inputs as
0
and the second database has
n-1
inputs as
0
but one input is
B
, so,
\Delta_{f_{\mathrm{max}}}=B
.

The same holds for other functions like a “thresholded maximum”
\max_{k}

which outputs the maximum value that appears at least
k
times in the
database. Despite being natural functions about the shape of the data, no DP
mechanisms have been offered in the literature for these functions.
With FA,
for the first time, we provide DP mechanisms for such
functions, with meaningful worst-case accuracy guarantees
. We
emphasize that we retain the
standard
definition of

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, and achieve strong parameters for it (see above).
Further, the additional dimension of inaccuracy that we allow – namely,
input distortion – is in line with what applications like (robust) Machine
Learning often anticipate and tolerate.

We also remark that,
on specific data distributions
, some of the
existing DP mechanisms may already enable empirical FA guarantees (see

Section
7
where such guarantees are compared). But crucially,
such guarantees are not always available in the worst-case, and even on data
distributions where they do exist, they were not identified previously.

Lossy Wasserstein Distance

Central to the formalization of all the results in this work is a new notion of distance between
distributions over a metric space, that we call
lossy Wasserstein
distance
. Lossy Wasserstein distance generalizes the notion of Wasserstein
distance
[
Vil08
]
, or Earth Mover Distance,
which is the minimum cost of transporting probability mass (“earth”) of one
distribution to make it match the other. Loss refers to the fact that some
of the mass is allowed to be lost during this transportation. We shall
use the “infinity
norm” version, where the cost paid is the maximum distance any mass is
transported.

Formally, consider a metric space with ground set

\Omega
, and metric
\mathfrak{d}
, where Wasserstein distance can be defined. For
example, one may consider
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and the metric
\mathfrak{d}
being an

\ell_{p}
-metric.
For
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and distributions
P,Q
over the metric space

(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
,
4
4

4

We will use upper case letters (
P,Q,X,Y
, etc.) to denote random variables (r.v.), as well as the probability distributions associated with them. Sometimes, we will also denote the probability distribution associated with a r.v.
X
by
\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X}}
.
we define
\Phi^{\gamma}(P,Q)
, the set of
\gamma
-lossy couplings of
P

and
Q
, as consisting of joint distributions
\phi

over
\Omega^{2}
with marginals
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
such that

\Delta(\phi_{1},P)+\Delta(\phi_{2},Q)\leq\gamma
, where

\Delta(P,Q):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|{P}(\omega)-{Q}(\omega)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega
denotes the total variation distance between
P
and
Q
.
Note that
\Phi^{0}(P,Q)
consists of joint distributions with marginals exactly equal to
P
and
Q
.

Definition 1

(
\gamma
-Lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein Distance)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two distributions over a metric space

(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
.
For
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the
\gamma
-lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance between
P
and
Q
is defined as:

W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)=\inf_{\phi\in\Phi^{\gamma}(P,Q)}\sup_{(x,y)\leftarrow\phi}\mathfrak{d}(x,y).

(1)

For simplicity, we write
W^{\infty}(p,q)
to denote
W_{0}^{\infty}(p,q)
.
We remark that while our definition of
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}
uses a worst case notion of distance
(as signified by
\infty
), there is an analogous average case definition,
that may be of independent interest. We define this in

Appendix
A.2
.

2.1
Lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein Distance Generalizes Some Existing Notions

Now we show that the Lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance generalizes the guarantee of
being “Probably Approximately Correct” (PAC) and also the definition of total variation distance, as shown below.

•

Generalizing the PAC guarantee:
The PAC guarantee states that
a randomized quantity
G
is, except with some small probability
\gamma
,
within an approximation radius
\beta
of a desired
deterministic

quantity
f
: i.e.,
\Pr_{g\leftarrow G}[\mathfrak{d}(f,g)>\beta]\leq\gamma
. For example, when
G
takes values over
{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathfrak{d}
can be the standard different metric over
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{d}(f,g)=|f-g|
.
Representing
f
by a point distribution
F_{f}
, this can
be equivalently written as
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(F_{f},G)\leq\beta
, where the underlying metric is
\mathfrak{d}
;
see
Lemma
9
in
Appendix
A.3
for a proof of this.

•

Generalizing the total variation distance:
It also
generalizes the total variation distance
\Delta(P,Q)
between two distributions, since

W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)=0
iff
\Delta(P,Q)\leq\gamma
; see
Lemma
10
in
Appendix
A.3
for a proof of this.

2.2
Triangle Inequality for Lossy Wasserstein Distance

The Lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance satisfies the following triangle inequality.

Lemma 1
.

For distributions
P
,
Q
, and
R
over a metric space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
and for all

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle W_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(P,R)

\displaystyle\leq W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(P,Q)+W_{\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(Q,R).

(2)

We can easily prove
Lemma
1
for the special case when
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
using standard tools from
[
Vil08
]
; see
Lemma
7
in
Appendix
A.1
for a proof. However, proving
Lemma
1
in its full generality requires a significantly more involved proof, which we present in
Section
6.1
.

Flexible Accuracy

The high-level idea of flexible accuracy is to
allow for some
distortion of the input
before measuring accuracy.
We would like to define “natural” distortions of a database, that are meaningful for the
function in question. For many functions, removing a few data points (say, outliers) would be a natural distortion, while for others, perturbing the data points (or a combination of both) is more natural. Note that
adding
new entries – even just one – is often not a reasonable
distortion. Therefore, distortion is generally defined not using a metric over
databases, but a
quasi-metric
(which is not required
to be symmetric).
5
5

5

A function
\mathsf{\partial}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
is called a quasi-metric, if for every
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
, we have
(i)

\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
and
(ii)

\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
.

3.1
Measure of Distortion

We shall use quasi-metrics with range
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
to define a measure of distortion,
where
\infty
indicates that one database cannot be distorted into another one.
As we shall need distortion measure between two distributions in our accuracy guarantees and also in the definitions of distortion and error sensitivities, it will be useful to extend the distortion measure to distributions. This can be done in same way as
W^{\infty}
, but with respect to a quasi-metric rather than a metric.

Definition 2

(Measure of Distortion)
.

A
measure of distortion
on a set
\mathcal{X}
is a function
\mathsf{\partial}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
which forms a quasi-metric over
\mathcal{X}
.
We also define
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}
as the extension to
\mathsf{\partial}
to distribution, which maps a pair
of distributions
P,Q
over
\mathcal{X}
to a real number as

{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(P,Q):=\inf_{\phi\in\Phi^{0}(P,Q)}\sup_{(x,y)\leftarrow\phi}\mathsf{\partial}(x,y).

If
P
is a point distribution with all its mass on a point
x
, we denote

{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(P,Q)
as
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(x,Q)
, which can be simplified as
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(x,Q)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{support}(Q)}\mathsf{\partial}(x,x^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, if both
P
and
Q
are point distributions on
x
and
y
, respectively, then
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(P,Q)=\mathsf{\partial}(x,y)
, and we will write
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(P,Q)
simply by
\mathsf{\partial}(x,y)
.

It is easy to verify that if
\mathsf{\partial}
is a quasi-metric, so is
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}
. We prove this in
Lemma
12
in
Appendix
I
.

Examples of measures of distortion.

We formally define three measures of distortion:
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}
for dropping elements,
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}
for perturbing/moving elements, and
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}
for a combination of dropping and moving elements.
These are defined when each element in
\mathcal{X}
is a finite multiset over a ground set
\mathcal{G}
. Formally,

\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is a function
\mathbf{x}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
(where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all non-negative integers, including zero) that outputs the multiplicity
of each element of
\mathcal{G}
in
\mathbf{x}
. We denote the
s

\mathbf{x}
by
|\mathbf{x}|:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{x}(i)
.

1.

Dropping elements:

For finite
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, we define
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}
, a measure of distortion for dropping elements, as follows:

\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}\frac{\sum_{g\in G}\mathbf{x}(g)-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(g)}{\sum_{g\in G}\mathbf{x}(g)}&\text{ if }\forall g\in\mathcal{G},\mathbf{x}(g)\geq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(g),\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

That is,

, we define

\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}W^{\infty}(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}|},\frac{\mathbf{y}}{|\mathbf{y}|})&\text{ if }|\mathbf{x}|=|\mathbf{y}|,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

where
\frac{\mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}|}
(similarly,
\frac{\mathbf{y}}{|\mathbf{y}|}
) is treated as a probability vector of size

, indexed by the elements of
\mathcal{G}
; the

.
We show in that

3.

Both dropping and moving elements:
For finite
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}

\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}

(5)

We show in

13
in

that
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}
is a quasi-metric.

3.2
Defining Flexible Accuracy

Informally, flexible accuracy with a distortion bound
\alpha
guarantees
that on an input
\mathbf{x}
, a mechanism shall produce an output that corresponds to

f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
for some
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\alpha
. In addition to
such input distortion, we may allow the output to be also probably
approximately correct, with an approximation error parameter
\beta
and an
error probability parameter
\gamma
. Formally, the probabilistic
approximation guarantee of the output is given as a bound of
\beta
on a

\gamma
-lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance.

Definition 3

(
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-accuracy)
.

Let
\mathsf{\partial}
be a measure of distortion on a set
\mathcal{X}
and
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a randomized function
such that
\mathcal{Y}
admits a metric. A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is said to
be
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-accurate for
f
with respect to
\mathsf{\partial}
, if

\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\inf_{X^{\prime}:{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(\mathbf{x},\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X^{\prime}}})\leq\alpha}W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}),f(X^{\prime}))\right)\leq\beta.

(6)

In other words, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a random variable
X^{\prime}
satisfying
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(\mathbf{x},\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X^{\prime}}})\leq\alpha
(i.e.,
\mathsf{\partial}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\alpha
for all

Flexible accuracy generalizes existing accuracy definitions.

It should be noted that flexible accuracy is not a completely disparate notion but a more generalized form of the standard accuracy guarantees. In particular:

•

As mentioned in
Section

•

Blum et al.
[

introduced

measure accuracy with respect to a “perturbed” function. While adequate

As we show later, flexible accuracy lets us develop DP mechanisms for highly

4
Composition Theorems

It is often convenient to design a mechanism as the function composition
of two mechanisms,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}

4.1
Flexible Accuracy Under Composition

In order to give our composition theorem for flexible accuracy, we need to define two new sensitivity notions:
distortion sensitivity
for a function and
error sensitivity
for a mechanism.
We give motivation behind each of these sensitivity notions when defining them in their respective subsections below.

4.1.1
Distortion Sensitivity

When we compose two flexibly accurate mechanisms
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
for
f_{1}:A\to B
and
f_{2}:B\to C
, respectively, to obtain the flexible accuracy guarantee of
M_{2}\circ M_{1}
for
f_{2}\circ f_{1}:A\to C
, we would like to attribute all the distortion made in
A
and
B
(for measuring the output error of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, respectively) to the distortion in
A
. This requires transferring the input distortion from
B
back into
A
, and the notion of distortion sensitivity allows us to quantify this.
Informally, distortion sensitivity of a function
f
(denoted by
\sigma_{f}
) captures the amount of distortion required in the domain of
f
to capture a certain amount of distortion in the codomain of
f
. We formalize this intuition below.

Definition 4

(Distortion sensitivity)
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a randomized function where
B
admits Wasserstein distances.
Let
\mathsf{\partial}_{1},\mathsf{\partial}_{2}
be measures of distortion on
A,B
, respectively.
Then, the
distortion-sensitivity
of
f
w.r.t.
(\mathsf{\partial}_{1},\mathsf{\partial}_{2})
is defined as the function

\sigma_{f}:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
given by

\sigma_{f}(\alpha)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,Y:\\
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{2}(f(x),\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{Y}})\leq\alpha\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}X:\\
f(X)=Y\end{subarray}}{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X}})

(7)

where
x\in A
, and the random variables
X
and
Y
are distributed over
A
and
B
,
respectively.
Above, infimum over an empty set is defined to be
\infty
.

See
Figure
1(b)
on page
1(b)
for an illustration of distortion sensitivity using a pebbling game.

Distortion sensitivity at
\alpha=0
.

It is easy to verify that for any randomized function
f
, we have
\sigma_{f}(0)=0
. We will use this for deriving flexible accuracy guarantees for any histogram-based-statistic in
Section
5.2
.

Distortion sensitivity of deterministic bijective functions:

When
f:A\to B
is a deterministic and bijective map, then for every
x,Y
such that
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{2}(f(x),\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{Y}})\leq\alpha
, there is only one choice of
X
for which
f(X)=Y
holds, that is
X=f^{-1}(Y)
.
Since for any point
x\in A
and distribution
P
over
A
, we have
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{1}(x,P)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{support}(P)}\mathsf{\partial}_{1}(x,x^{\prime})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma_{f}(\alpha)\quad=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,Y:\\
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{2}(f(x),\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{Y}})\leq\alpha\end{subarray}}{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}_{1}(x,\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{f^{-1}(Y)}})\quad=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in A,y\in B:\\
\mathsf{\partial}_{2}(f(x),y)\leq\alpha\end{subarray}}\mathsf{\partial}_{1}(x,f^{-1}(y)).

(8)

In particular, if
f:A\to A
is an identity function and
\mathsf{\partial}_{1}=\mathsf{\partial}_{2}
, then we we have
\sigma_{f}(\alpha)\leq\alpha
. Many of our mechanisms in this paper for which we derive flexible accuracy guarantees are given for the identity function over the space of histograms; see, for example, the result for our basic histogram mechanism (
Theorem
3
), the bucking mechanism (see
Claim
4
), and their composition (
Theorem
4
), etc.

Distortion sensitivity of the histogram function w.r.t.
(\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}},\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}})
:

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{k}\}
denote a finite set.
The histogram function
f_{\mathrm{hist}}
takes an unordered dataset
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
(where each
x_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
and the ordering of
x_{i}

k
-tuple
(\mathbf{x}(g_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{x}(g_{k}))
where
\mathbf{x}(g_{i}):=|\{j:x_{j}=g_{i}\}|
denotes the multiplicity of
g_{i}
in
\mathbf{x}
. Note that
f_{\mathrm{hist}}
is a deterministic bijective function. It is easy to verify (from (

)) that
\sigma_{f_{\mathrm{hist}}}(\alpha)\leq\alpha
w.r.t.
(\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}},\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}})
. Later on, it will be convenient for us to represent a histogram over a finite set

as a map

in the dataset.

4.1.2
Error Sensitivity

Suppose we want to compose an
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\gamma_{1})
-accurate mechanism
M_{1}
for
f_{1}:A\to B
with another flexibly accurate mechanism
M_{2}
for
f_{2}:B\to C
to obtain flexible accuracy guarantee of the composed mechanism
M_{2}\circ M_{1}
for
f_{2}\circ f_{1}:A\to C
.
For this, on any input
x\in A
, first we measure the output error of
M_{1}
on input
x
in terms of
W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(M_{1}(x),f_{1}(X^{\prime}))
, where
X^{\prime}
is an
\alpha_{1}
-distortion of the
same

x
on which we run the mechanism
M_{1}
; see (
6
).
Now, for composition, we need to run
M_{2}
on
M_{1}(x)
and distort
f_{1}(X^{\prime})
to obtain another r.v.
Y
, and the output error of the composed mechanism is given by
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(M_{2}(M_{1}(x)),f_{2}(Y))
. The problem here is that since the input (distribution)
f(X^{\prime})
that we distort is
not the same
as the input (distribution)
M_{1}(x)
that we run
M_{2}
on, we cannot directly obtain the output error guarantee of the composed mechanism from that of
M_{2}
. Therefore, we need a way to generalize the measure of accuracy (output error) of a flexible accurate mechanism when the input (distribution) to the mechanism is not the same as the input (distribution) that we distort, but they are at a bounded distance from each other (as measured in terms on the lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein distance). The notion of error sensitivity formalizes this intuition. Informally, it captures the sensitivity of the output error of a flexibly accurate mechanism in such situations.

Definition 5

(Error sensitivity)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:B\to C
be any mechanism for a function
f:B\to C
,
where both
B
and
C
have associated Wasserstein distances.
Let
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}
be a measure of distortion on
B
. Then, for
\alpha_{2},\gamma_{2}\geq 0
, the error-sensitivity
\tau_{\mathcal{M},f}^{\alpha_{2},\gamma_{2}}:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
of
\mathcal{M}
w.r.t.
f
is defined as:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{M},f}^{\alpha_{2},\gamma_{2}}(\beta_{1},\gamma_{1})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,X^{\prime}:\\
W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X}},\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X^{\prime}}})\leq\beta_{1}\end{subarray}}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y:\\
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(X^{\prime},Y)\leq\alpha_{2}\end{subarray}}W_{\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}(X),f(Y)).

(9)

In other words, if
\tau_{\mathcal{M},f}^{\alpha_{2},\gamma_{2}}(\beta_{1},\gamma_{1})=\beta_{2}
, then for distributions
X,X^{\prime}
over
A
such that
W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X}},\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X^{\prime}}})\leq\beta_{1}
, one can
\alpha_{2}
-distort
X^{\prime}
to
Y
in such a way that
W_{\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}(X),f(Y))\leq\beta_{2}
.

.

As mentioned earlier, the notion of error sensitivity generalizes the definition of flexible accuracy. In other words, if a mechanism

-accurate, then

4.2
Differential Privacy Under Composition

First we formally define the notion of differential privacy.

Differential Privacy.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote a universe of possible “databases” with a
symmetric neighborhood relation
\sim
. In typical applications, two
databases
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are considered neighbors if one is obtained from the
other by removing the data corresponding to a single “individual.”
A
mechanism

\mathcal{M}
over
\mathcal{X}
is an algorithm which takes
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
as
input and samples an output from an output space
\mathcal{Y}
, according to some
distribution. We shall denote this distribution by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})
.

Definition 6

(Differential Privacy
[
DMNS06
,
DKM+06
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private (DP), if for all neighboring databases
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

and all measurable subsets
S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]+\delta
.

A simple but very useful result in differential privacy is the “post-processing” theorem for DP (see
[
DR14
, Proposition 2.1]
), which states that if
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, then for any mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the composed mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}
would remain
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
We prove a “pre-processing” theorem for differential privacy, which can be viewed as complementing the “post-processing” theorem for DP.
Our pre-processing theorem for DP states that if
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is private,
then so would
\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}
be (i.e.,
pre-processing does not hurt privacy), provided that
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is well-behaved.
The following notion of being well-behaved suffices for our purposes.

Definition 7

(Neighborhood preserving Mechanism)
.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}:A\rightarrow B
is
neighborhood preserving
w.r.t. neighborhood relations
\sim_{A}
over
A
and
\sim_{B}
over
B
, if for all

x,y\in A
s.t.
x\sim_{A}y
, there exists a pair of jointly distributed
random variables
(X,Y)
s.t.
\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X}}=\mathcal{M}(x)
,
\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{Y}}=\mathcal{M}(y)
, and

\Pr[X\sim_{B}Y]=1
.

The following theorem states our pre-processing theorem for DP, which we prove in
Appendix
E
.

Theorem 2

(Differential Privacy Composition)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}:A\to B
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}:B\to C
be any two mechanisms.
If
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is neighborhood-preserving w.r.t. neighborhood relations
\sim_{A}
and
\sim_{B}
over
A
and
B
, respectively,
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{B}
,
then
\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}:A\to C
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{A}
.

It is important to note here is that we are not releasing the output of the neighboring-preserving mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{1}
; we only release the output of
\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

Looking ahead, we will require
Theorem
2
to establish the DP guarantee of our bucketed-histogram mechanism (
Algorithm
3
) which is obtained by pre-processing our
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP histogram mechanism (
Algorithm
1
) with the neighborhood-preserving bucketing mechanism (

).

5.1
A Private Mechanism for Releasing Histograms with Flexible Accuracy

Before describing our new mechanism for releasing histograms with flexible accuracy, let us consider a simpler Boolean task of privately reporting whether a given set is empty or not. Deriving a solution to this simpler problem will pave a way towards our new histogram mechanism.

Private mechanism for determining whether a given set is empty or not.

For this, the only input distortion we are allowed is to drop some elements – i.e., we cannot
report an empty set as non-empty. Since we seek to limit the extent of
distortion, let us add a constraint that if a set has
q
or more elements,
then with probability 1 (or very close to 1) we should report the set as
being non-empty. Let
p_{k}
denote the probability that a set of size
k\in[0,q]
is
reported as being non-empty, so that
p_{0}=0
and
p_{q}=1
.

For our scheme to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential private, we require

\displaystyle p_{k}\leq p_{k+1}e^{\epsilon}+\delta,

\displaystyle\qquad p_{k+1}\leq p_{k}e^{\epsilon}+\delta,

\displaystyle(1-p_{k})\leq(1-p_{k+1})e^{\epsilon}+\delta,

\displaystyle\qquad(1-p_{k+1})\leq(1-p_{k})e^{\epsilon}+\delta,

for
0\leq k<q
, with boundary conditions

p_{0}=0
and
p_{q}=1
.
We are interested in simultaneously reducing
\epsilon
and
\delta

subject to the above constraints.
The pareto-optimal
(\epsilon,\delta)
turn out to be given by

\delta\left(\frac{e^{(q/2)\epsilon}-1}{e^{\epsilon}-1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}
, with corresponding
values of
p_{k}
being given by

\displaystyle p_{k}=\delta\left(\frac{e^{{k}\epsilon}-1}{e^{\epsilon}-1}\right)\text{ for }k\leq\nicefrac{{q}}{{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{k}=1-p_{q-k}\text{ for }k\geq\nicefrac{{q}}{{2}}.

(11)

The condition
\delta\left(\frac{e^{(q/2)\epsilon}-1}{e^{\epsilon}-1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}
implies that we can achieve
(\epsilon,\epsilon e^{-\Omega(\epsilon q)})
-differential privacy.
In particular, we may choose
\epsilon=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\right)
, and

\displaystyle\delta=O\left(\frac{e^{-\sqrt{q}/2}}{\sqrt{q}}\right)
, providing a
useful privacy guarantee when
q
is sufficiently large.

.

Towards a private mechanism for histograms.

To generalize this Boolean mechanism to a full-fledged histogram mechanism, we reinterpret it. In a histogram mechanism,
where again, the distortion allowed in the input is to only drop elements,
we can add a
negative noise
to the count in each “bar” of the
histogram. (If the reduced count is negative, we report it as 0.) We seek a noise function such that the probability of the
reported count being 0 (when the actual count is
k\in[0,q]
) is the same as
that of the above mechanism reporting that a set of size
k
is empty.
That is, the probability of adding a noise
\nu\leq-k
should be
1-p_{k}
.
That is, if the noise distribution is given by the density function
\sigma
,
we require that

\displaystyle\int_{-q}^{-k}\sigma(t)\cdot dt=1-p_{k}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma(t)=0\text{ for }t\not\in[-q,0].

Substituting the expression for
p_{k}
from (
11
), and then differentiating this identity with respect to
k
,
we obtain the following expression for
\sigma(t)
:

\sigma(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\epsilon q/2}}\mathrm{Lap}(t\mid-\frac{q}{2},\frac{1}{\epsilon}),&\text{ if }t\in[-q,0],\\
0,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(12)

where
\mathrm{Lap}
is the Laplace noise distribution with mean
-\frac{q}{2}
and
scale parameter
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}
.
7
7

7

The Laplace distribution over
{\mathbb{R}}
, with
scaling parameter

b>0
and mean
\mu
, is defined by the density function
\mathrm{Lap}(x|\mu,b):=\frac{1}{2b}e^{\frac{-|x-\mu|}{b}}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We denote a random variable that is distributed according to the Laplace distribution with the scaling parameter
b
and mean 0 by
\mathrm{Lap}(b)
.

We call
\sigma(t)
the shifted-truncated Laplace distribution, which is equal to the (normalized) Laplace distribution with mean
-\frac{q}{2}
and scale parameter
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
when
t\in[-q,0]
, and equal to zero when
t\notin[-q,0]
.

Datasets can be abstractly represented by multi-sets, and each element in the multi-set belongs to a ground set
\mathcal{G}

Formally, a multi-set

f_{\mathrm{supp}}

5.2.3
Choosing the Parameters

As mentioned in
Remark
3
for

3

4
,
5
, and Corollaries

,

.
For concreteness, in the following, we illustrate the privacy accuracy trade-off by choosing parameters for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\alpha,\beta,[0,B)}
mechanism in

; the same result applies to Theorems

,

, and

If we choose
\epsilon=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau n}}
and
\tau

, then by dropping only

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau n}},\frac{e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{\tau n})}}{\sqrt{\tau n}}\right)

is a small constant (say,

\alpha n=O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})

(\epsilon,\epsilon e^{-\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon})})

By setting

6.1
Proof of
Lemma
1
– Triangle Inequality for
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}

In this section we prove
Lemma
1

The following lemma is crucial to proving
Lemma
1
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
P
and
Q
be any two distributions over a metric space

(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
. If
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)=\beta
, then for all
\gamma_{1}\in[0,\gamma]
, there exist distributions
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
s.t.
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{1}
,
\Delta(Q,Q^{\prime})\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}
, and
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\beta
.

Proof:

Let
P
and
Q
be any two distributions over a metric space

(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
. Let us assume that the optimal
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)
(=
\beta
) is obtained at the joint distribution
\phi_{opt}
. Let the first and the second marginal distributions of
\phi_{opt}
be
P_{opt}
and
Q_{opt}
, respectively. Let
\Delta(P,P_{opt})=\gamma_{opt}
, which implies that
\Delta(Q,Q_{opt})\leq\gamma-\gamma_{opt}
.
Define a function
R_{opt}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
as
R_{opt}(\omega):={P_{opt}}(\omega)-{P}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Clearly,
\int_{\Omega}R_{opt}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega=0
and
\int_{\Omega}|R_{opt}(\omega)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega=2\gamma_{opt}
.

In the discussion below, we shall take a general
\gamma_{1}\in[0,\gamma_{opt})
and construct distributions
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
s.t.
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{1}
,
\Delta(Q,Q^{\prime})\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}
, and
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\beta
, as required in the conclusion of
Lemma
3
.
We can show a similar result for the other case also when
\gamma_{1}\in(\gamma_{opt},\gamma]
(by swapping the roles of
P
and
Q
in the above as well as in the argument below). This will complete the proof of
Lemma
3
.

Define a function
R^{\prime}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
as
R^{\prime}(\omega):=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{opt}}R_{opt}(\omega)
.
For any
\omega\in\Omega
, let
P^{\prime}(\omega)={P}(\omega)+R^{\prime}(\omega)
.
After substituting the value of
R_{opt}(\omega)={P_{opt}}(\omega)-{P}(\omega)
, we get
P^{\prime}(\omega)=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{opt}}{P_{opt}}(\omega)+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{opt}}\right){P}(\omega)
. Since
P^{\prime}
is a convex combination of two distributions, it is also a valid distribution.
It is easy to see that
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})=\gamma_{1}
. Define a joint distribution
\phi^{\prime}
as follow: for every
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
, define

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\phi_{opt}(x,y)\frac{{P^{\prime}}(x)}{{P_{opt}}(x)}&\text{if }{P_{opt}}(x)>0\\
{P^{\prime}}(x)\delta(x-y)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function.
It follows from the definition that
\int_{\Omega}\phi^{\prime}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y={P^{\prime}}(x)
, i.e., the first marginal of
\phi^{\prime}
is
{P^{\prime}}(\cdot)
. This also implies that
\phi^{\prime}
is a valid joint distribution because

(i)

\phi^{\prime}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
, and

(ii)

\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\phi^{\prime}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega}{P^{\prime}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
.

Let the second marginal of
\phi^{\prime}
be
Q^{\prime}
. We show in
Claim
8
in
Appendix
A.1
that
\Delta(Q,Q^{\prime})\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}
.

The only thing left to prove is to show that
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\beta
for the above constructed
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
.
First we show
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\geq\beta
and then show
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\leq\beta
.

•

Showing
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\geq\beta
:
This follows from the following claim, which we prove in
Appendix
A.1
.

Claim 1
.

For distributions
P
and
Q
over a metric space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
and
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)\quad=\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{P},\hat{Q}:\\
\Delta(P,\hat{P})+\Delta(Q,\hat{Q})\leq\gamma\end{subarray}}W^{\infty}(\hat{P},\hat{Q}).

(15)

Now, since
P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}
satisfy
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})+\Delta(Q,Q^{\prime})\leq\gamma
, we have
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)\leq W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
. Since
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)=\beta
, we have shown that
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\geq\beta
.

•

Showing
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\leq\beta
:

For the sake of contradiction, let us assume that
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})>\beta
.
Then there is a pair
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
such that
\phi^{\prime}(x,y)>0
and
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)>\beta
.
This implies that
\phi_{opt}(x,y)=0
, because, otherwise, we would have
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)>\beta
,
which contradicts our hypothesis that
W_{\gamma}^{\infty}(P,Q)=\beta
.
So, we know that
\phi^{\prime}(x,y)>0
and
\phi_{opt}(x,y)=0
. From the definition of
\phi^{\prime}
, this is only possible if
{P_{opt}}(x)=0
and
{P^{\prime}}(x)\delta(x-y)>0
. This can happen only if
x=y
, but this implies
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)=0\leq\beta
, which is a contradiction. Hence
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\leq\beta
.

This completes the proof of
Lemma
3
.

\Box

Now we are ready to prove
Lemma
1
.
Let
W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(P,Q)=\beta_{1}
and
W_{\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(Q,R)=\beta_{2}
.
It follows from
Lemma
3
that there exists a distribution
P^{\prime}
such that
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{1}
and
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},Q)=\beta_{1}
. Similarly, there exists a distribution
R^{\prime}
such that
\Delta(R,R^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{2}
and
W^{\infty}(Q,R^{\prime})=\beta_{2}
.
Using these, we have from
Lemma
7
that
W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},R^{\prime})\leq\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}
.

Now, the result follows from the following set of inequalities.

\displaystyle W_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(P,R)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(d)}}}{{=}}\hskip-14.22636pt\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{P},\hat{R}:\\
\Delta(P,\hat{P})+\Delta(R,\hat{R})\leq\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\end{subarray}}\hskip-14.22636ptW^{\infty}(\hat{P},\hat{R})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(e)}}}{{\leq}}\ W^{\infty}(P^{\prime},R^{\prime})\ \leq\ \beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\ =\ W_{\gamma_{1}}^{\infty}(P,Q)+W_{\gamma_{2}}^{\infty}(Q,R),

where (d) follows from
Claim
1

and (e) follows because
P^{\prime},R^{\prime}
satisfy
\Delta(P,P^{\prime})+\Delta(R,R^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
.

This concludes the proof of
Lemma
1
.

6.3
Proof of
Theorem
3
– Truncated Laplace Mechanism for Histograms

First we prove the flexible accuracy part, which is easy, and then we will move on to proving the privacy part, which is more involved than the existing privacy analysis of differentially-private histogram mechanisms.
We also note that the requirement of
|\mathrm{support}(\mathbf{x})|\leq t
is only needed the accuracy result.

Flexible accuracy.

Note that the noise added by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
in each bar of the histogram is bounded by
-q=-\tau|\mathbf{x}|
, which can lead to a drop of at most
\tau
fraction of total number of elements from each bar. Combined with the fact that
|\mathrm{support}(\mathbf{x})|\leq t
, the fraction of the maximum fraction of elements that can be dropped is
\tau t
. Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
is
(\tau t,0,0)
-accurate.

Differential privacy.

Our proof of the privacy part of
Theorem
3
depends on the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

For any
\nu\geq 0,\epsilon>0
and on inputs
\mathbf{x}
s.t.
|\mathbf{x}|\geq\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\ln\left(1+\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}}{e^{\epsilon(\nu+\frac{\tau}{2})}-1}\right)
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
is

\left((1+\nu)\epsilon,\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\nicefrac{{\epsilon q}}{{2}}}-1)}\right)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{\mathrm{hist}}
, where
q=\tau|\mathbf{x}|
.

Proof:

We shall in fact prove that a mechanism that outputs
\hat{}\mathbf{y}
with
\hat{}\mathbf{y}(i):=\mathbf{x}(i)+z_{i}
(without rounding, and without replacing negative values with 0)
is already differentially private as desired. Then, since the actual mechanism is a
post-processing of this mechanism, it will also be differentially
private with the same parameters.

Let
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
be two neighbouring histograms.
For simplicity, for every
i\in\mathcal{G}
, define
x_{i}:=\mathbf{x}(i)
and
x_{i}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(i)
.
Since
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, there exists an

i^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
|x_{i^{*}}-x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}|=1
and that
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}
for every
i\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\{i^{*}\}
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
x_{i^{*}}=x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}+1
, which implies
|\mathbf{x}|=|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|+1=n+1
.
Let
q=\tau(n+1)
and
q^{\prime}=\tau n
.
For simplicity of notation, we will denote
\mathrm{support}(\mathbf{y})
by
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{y}}
for any
\mathbf{y}\in\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\}
.

In order to prove the lemma, for every subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, we need to show that

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]\leq e^{(1+\nu)\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S]+\delta,

(24)

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S]\leq e^{(1+\nu)\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]+\delta,

(25)

where
\delta=\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\epsilon\nicefrac{{q}}{{2}}}-1)}
.
We only prove (
24
); (
25
) can be shown similarly.

Fix an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
adds independent noise to each bar of the histogram according to
\pi_{q}(z)
, we have that for every
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, we have
\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})}}(\mathbf{s})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}}}\pi_{q}(s_{i}-x_{i})
where
s_{i}=\mathbf{s}(i)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S]

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\big[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}}}\pi_{q}(s_{i}-x_{i})\big]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s},

(26)

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\big[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}}\pi_{q^{\prime}}(s_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\big]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.

(27)

Now, using the fact that
\forall k\neq i^{*},x_{k}=x_{k}^{\prime}
and
x_{i^{*}}=x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}+1
, we partition
S
into three disjoint sets:

1.

S_{0}:=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}:s_{i^{*}}-x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}<-q^{\prime}\}\cup\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}:0<s_{i^{*}}-x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}\}
.

2.

S_{1}:=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}:-q^{\prime}\leq s_{i^{*}}-x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}<-q^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\}
.

3.

S_{2}:=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}:-q^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\leq s_{i^{*}}-x_{i^{*}}^{\prime}\leq 0)\}
.

The proof of (
24
) is a simple corollary of the following two claims, which we prove in
Appendix
F
.

Claim 2
.

\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S_{0}\cup S_{2}]\leq e^{(1+\nu)\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S_{0}\cup S_{2}]
, provided
n\geq\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\ln\left(1+\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}}{e^{\epsilon(\nu+\frac{\tau}{2})}-1}\right)
.

Claim 3
.

\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S_{1}]\leq\delta
, where
\delta=\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\nicefrac{{\epsilon q}}{{2}}}-1)}
.

The above two claims together imply (
24
) as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S_{0}\cup S_{2}]+\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in S_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{(1+\nu)\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S_{0}\cup S_{2}]+\delta

\displaystyle\leq e^{(1+\nu)\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x})\in S]+\delta.

(Since
S_{0}\cup S_{2}\subseteq S
)

This completes the proof of
Lemma
5
.

\Box

In
Lemma
5
,
\nu
is a free variable. By taking
\nu=0
, we get the following result in
Corollary
3
. We can also get different guarantees by restricting to
\nu>0
; see
Remark
6
below for this.

Corollary 3
.

For any
\epsilon,\tau,\mathbf{x}
such that
\tau|\mathbf{x}|\epsilon\geq 2
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
is

\left(\epsilon,\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\nicefrac{{\epsilon q}}{{2}}}-1)}\right)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{\mathrm{hist}}
, where
q=\tau|\mathbf{x}|
.

Proof:

Substituting
\nu=0
in
Lemma
5
gives that when
\mathbf{x}
satisfies
|\mathbf{x}|\geq\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\ln\left(1+\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}}{e^{\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}-1}\right)
, we have that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
is
\left(\epsilon,\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\nicefrac{{\epsilon n\tau}}{{2}}}-1)}\right)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{\mathrm{hist}}
. Now, the corollary follows because

\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\geq\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\ln\left(1+e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}\right)=\frac{2}{\epsilon\tau}\ln\left(1+\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}}{e^{\frac{\epsilon\tau}{2}}-1}\right)
,
where the first inequality uses
x\geq\ln(1+x)
for
x>0
.

\Box

Remark 6
.

We show in
Lemma
11
in
Appendix
F
that by restricting

5
to
\nu>0
, we can get a weaker condition than what we have in
Corollary
3
with a slight increase in the privacy parameter

. In particular, we show that for all

such that

-DP w.r.t.

. We can take

Now the privacy part of
Theorem
3
follows because
q=\tau|\mathbf{x}|
and
\tau|\mathbf{x}|\epsilon\geq 2
(note that
\tau|\mathbf{x}|\epsilon
is typically a much bigger number than

as it scales with the size of the dataset), which implies that
\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1}{2(e^{\nicefrac{{\epsilon q}}{{2}}}-1)}=\epsilon e^{-\Omega(\epsilon\tau|\mathbf{x}|)}
. Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}
is
(\epsilon,\epsilon e^{-\Omega(\epsilon\tau|\mathbf{x}|)})
-DP.

This completes the proof of

.

6.4
Proof of
Theorem
4
– Bucketed Truncated Laplace Mechanism

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BucHist}}^{\alpha,\beta,[0,B)}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,[0,B)}\circ\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}
, with

.
We will use

to show the DP guarantee and

Differential privacy.

. This follows because adding/removing any one element changes the output of bucketing by at most one element; hence, neighbors remain neighbors after bucketing. Now, since
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}

, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,[0,B)}

\sim_{\mathrm{hist}}

that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BucHist}}^{\alpha,\beta,[0,B)}
is also differentially private w.r.t.
\sim_{\mathrm{hist}}
with the same parameters.

Flexible accuracy.

First we show in the following claim that the flexible accuracy guarantee of the bucketing mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}(\mathbf{x})
, and we prove it in
Appendix
G
.

Claim 4
.

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}
is
\left(0,\frac{w}{2},0\right)
-accurate for the identity function

over
\mathcal{H}_{[0,B)}
w.r.t the metric
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{hist}}
.

Note that when we apply

3
to compute the flexible accuracy parameters of the composed mechanism

and the error sensitivity of

\bullet

Distortion sensitivity of

:

over

, we have (as noted in the first example in

\bullet

Error sensitivity of

:

Note that the bucketing mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}:\mathcal{H}_{[0,B)}\to\mathcal{H}_{[0,B)}
is a deterministic map, and is
(0,\beta,0)
-accurate (see

) for computing the identity function

, where

. As mentioned in the first bullet after the statement of

, this implies that when computing the error sensitivity of

(which is required for calculating the output error

of the composed mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,[0,B)}\circ\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{buc}}^{w,[0,B)}
), we only need to take supremum in (
9
) over point distributions
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{hist}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\beta
, where
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{hist}}(,)
is the metric that we use over
\mathcal{H}_{[0,B)}
. In other words, in order to compute the error sensitivity of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,[0,B)}
, we only need to bound
\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\\
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathrm{hist}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\beta}\ \inf_{Y:\\
{\widehat{}\mathsf{\partial}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},Y)\leq\alpha}W^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{STLap}}^{\tau,\epsilon,\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{Y}})

Lemma 6
.

For any

7.2
Description of the Compared Mechanisms

Exponential Mechanism.

The Exponential Mechanism
[
MT07
]
can be tailored for an
abstract utility function. We consider the negative of the error as the utility of a response
y
on input histogram
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
q(\mathbf{x},y)=-\mathrm{err}(\mathbf{x},y)=-|\max(\mathbf{x})-y|
.
However, for both
\max_{k}
and
\mathrm{mode}
, error has high sensitivity – changing
a single element in the histogram can change the error by as much as the
number of bars in the histogram. Since the mechanism produces an output
r

with probability proportional to
e^{\frac{\epsilon q(\mathbf{x},r)}{2\Delta_{\mathrm{err}}}}
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{err}}
is the sensitivity of
\mathrm{err}(\cdot,\cdot)
, having a
large sensitivity has the effect of moving the output distribution close to
a uniform distribution. This is reflected in the performance of this
mechanism in all our plots.

Propose-Test-Release Mechanism (PTR).

We consider the commonly used form of the PTR mechanism of Dwork and Lei

[
DL09
]
, namely, “releasing stable values” (see
[
Vad17
, Section 3.3]
). On input
\mathbf{x}
, the
mechanism either releases the correct result
f(\mathbf{x})
or refuses to do so (replacing it with a random output value), depending on whether the radius of the neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
where it remains constant is sufficiently large (after adding some noise). For computing a function
f
and a setting of parameter
\beta=0
and privacy parameters
\epsilon,\delta
, the mechanism calculates this radius for an input
\mathbf{x}
as,

r=d(\mathbf{x},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\text{LS}_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})>0\})+\text{Lap}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}})
, where
d(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{S})
is the minimum Hamming distance between
\mathbf{x}
and any point in the set
\mathcal{S}
and
\text{LS}_{f}(\mathbf{y}):=\max\{|f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{z})|:\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbf{z}\}
is local sensitivity of function
f
at
\mathbf{y}
.
If this radius
r
is greater than
\nicefrac{{\ln{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}})}}}{{\epsilon}}
, the
mechanism will output the exact answer
f(\mathbf{x})
, otherwise it outputs a random value from the domain.
For the functions we consider, this radius of stable region can be computed efficiently and is typically small or even zero for input distributions considered which is reflected in our plots.

Smooth-sensitivity Mechanism (SS).

This mechanism, due to Nissim et al.
[
NRS07
]
, uses
the smooth sensitivity of a function
f
, defined as
SS_{f}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\max\{LS_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})e^{-\epsilon d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}\}
, where
LS_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
denotes the
local sensitivity
of
f
at
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, and
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Hamming distance. Given an input histogram
\mathbf{x}
, the mechanism adds noise
O(SS_{f}^{\beta}(\mathbf{x})/\alpha)
to
f(x)
for appropriate values of
\alpha
and
\beta
to obtain
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
For functions like
\max_{k}
and
\mathrm{mode}
, like sensitivity, local sensitvity (and hence smooth sensitivity) also tends to
be large on many histograms, which leads this mechanism to add a large
noise.

Stability-Based Sanitized Histogram Mechanism.

This mechanism was proposed by Bun et al.
[
BNS19
]
(also see
[
Vad17
, Theorem 3.5]
) for
releasing histograms with provable worst-case guarantees. However, these
guarantees are in terms of the errors in the individual bar heights of the
histogram, and does not necessarily translate to the histogram based functions, as we
consider. Nevertheless, this mechanism provides a potential candidate for
a mechanism for any histogram based statistic.

For each bar of the histogram, the mechanism adds Laplace noise to the bar
height, and the resulting value is reported only if it is more than a
threshold, and
otherwise a
0
is reported. By treating empty bars differently, this
mechanism achieves comparable flexible accuracy as our mechanism in the case
of
\max
. However, this does not generalize to
\max_{k}
. In particular, in
the example in (4) in
Figure
4
, by adding (possibly positive)
noise to histogram bars of height lower than
k
, the mechanism is very
likely to find a bar which is much further to the right than the point where
the bar heights cross
k
.

Choosing-Based Histogram Mechanism.

Beimel et al.
[
BNS16
]
presented a mechanism SanPoints for
producing a sanitized histogram, with formal PAC-guarantees for the height
of each bar of the histogram. The mechanism involves iteratively choosing
bars from the histogram, without replacement, and adding some noise
to the bar heights. The bars are chosen according to a DP mechanism for picking the tallest bar
(which in turn uses the exponential mechanism).

For the functions we consider, SanPoints yields mixed results, but is
dominated by BNS and our new mechanism.

A.2
Average Version of Lossy Wasserstein Distance

Our definition of
W_{\theta}^{\infty}
uses a worst case notion of distance.
Many of the results using this notion have analogues using an average
case version. We formally present this definition below, as it may be of interest
elsewhere.

Definition 9

(
\theta
-Lossy Average Wasserstein Distance)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two probability distributions over a metric space

(\Omega,\mathfrak{d})
, and let
\theta\in[0,1]
.
The
\theta
-lossy average Wasserstein distance
between
P
and
Q
is defined as:

W_{{\theta}}(P,Q)=\inf_{\phi\in\Phi^{\theta}(P,Q)}\E_{(x,y)\leftarrow\phi}[\mathfrak{d}(x,y)].

(30)

The following lemma relates lossy average Wasserstein and lossy
\infty
-Wasserstein
distances.

Lemma 8
.

For any two distributions
P,Q
, and
0\leq\beta^{\prime}<\beta\leq 1
,

W_{{\beta}}(P,Q)\leq W_{\beta}^{\infty}(P,Q)\leq\frac{W_{{\beta^{\prime}}}(P,Q)}{(\beta-\beta^{\prime})}.

Proof:

Clearly from the definitions,
W_{{\beta}}(P,Q)\leq W_{\beta}^{\infty}(P,Q)
.

Suppose
W_{{\beta^{\prime}}}(P,Q)=\gamma
and
\phi\in\Phi^{\beta^{\prime}}(P,Q)
is an optimal
coupling that realizes this. Then, in
\phi
, the total mass that is
transported more than a distance
\gamma^{\prime}
is at most
\gamma/\gamma^{\prime}
and
the total mass that is lost is at most
\beta^{\prime}
. By choosing to simply not
transport this mass at all, one loses
\beta^{\prime}+\gamma/\gamma^{\prime}
mass, but no
mass is transported more than a distance
\gamma^{\prime}
. Choosing

\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma/(\beta-\beta^{\prime})
this upper bound on loss is
\beta
, and
hence this modified coupling shows that
W_{\beta}^{\infty}(P,Q)\leq\gamma^{\prime}
.

Theorem

(Restating
Theorem
2
)
.

Let

and
\mathcal{M}_{2}:B\to C
be any two mechanisms.

\mathcal{M}_{1}

\sim_{A}

\sim_{B}

B

-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{B}

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
\sim_{A}

For simplicity, we consider the case when
B

generalized to the continuous setting.

Since the mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is neighborhood preserving, for
x,x^{\prime}\in A

s.t.
x_{1}\sim_{A}x_{2}
, there exists a pair of jointly distributed random

(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy of the mechanism

\mathcal{M}_{2}
, for all subsets

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{M}_{2}(x_{1})\in S)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr(\mathcal{M}_{2}(x_{2})\in S)+\delta.

Thus, if
x\sim_{A}x^{\prime}
, then for any subset

, we have,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{1}(x))\in S]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}}\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X_{1}}}(x_{1})\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{2}(x_{1})\in S]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},x_{2})}\mathfrak{p}_{{}_{X_{1},X_{2}}}(x_{1},x_{2})\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{2}(x_{1})\in S]

\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{1})=a;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y})=b;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s}_{2})=c;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{z})=d,

which implies
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=a+\eta b
and
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=c+\eta d
.
We need to show that
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\leq(a+c)+\eta(b+d)
.

Using
Lemma
13
with
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y})=b
and
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s}_{2})=c
, we get that there is a
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=c
and
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},s_{2})\leq b
. This gives the following:

\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{1})=a;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=c;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{s}_{2})\leq b;\ \mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{z})=d.

Now we prove that
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\leq(a+c)+\eta(b+d)
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drmv}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\eta\cdot\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})+\eta\cdot\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{1})+\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})+\eta\cdot\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{z})

(
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}
is a quasi-metric)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{1})+\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{drop}}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})+\eta\cdot(\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{s}_{2})+\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}(\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{z}))

(
\mathsf{\partial}_{\mathrm{move}}
is a metric)

\displaystyle\leq(a+c)+\eta(b+d).

This concludes the proof of
